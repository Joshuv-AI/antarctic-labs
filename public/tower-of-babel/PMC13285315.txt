Interpol review of forensic toxicology, 2023–2025
Authors: Stephen Chi-yuen Ip, Chun-wa Chan, Chun-wo Chan, Anlea Chu, Wai-wah Chung, Janesse Wing-sze Hui, Ka-Wai Ku, Wing-sze Lee, Kam-moon Lo, Ka-chun Poon, Wing-cheong Wong, Bobbie Kwok-keung Cheung, Jack Yuk-ki Cheng
Published: 2026

Introduction

The period from 2022 to 2025 has seen significant advancements and ongoing challenges in forensic toxicology. As the world emerged from the COVID-19 pandemic, forensic scientists have continued to navigate the evolving landscape of drug use and distribution. The rise of new psychoactive substances (NPS) poses a pressing concern, as these substances continually adapting to evade detection and regulation. In response, forensic toxicologists have leveraged advancements in instrumentation, methodology, and quality assurance to detect and analyze these substances with greater accuracy and reliability. The increasing use of alternative specimens and general screening techniques for unknown substances has also improved the ability to identify drug use in various contexts, including driving under the influence, workplace testing and court-ordered assessments.

This review paper summarizes key developments and challenges in forensic toxicology from January 2022 to March 2025, focusing on quality assurance, driving under the influence, workplace and court-ordered drug testing, drugs of forensic interest, and advances in toxicological analysis. Also, it introduces a new chapter on the ‘Application of Artificial Intelligence (AI) in Forensic Toxicology’, highlighting how AI is transforming the field. By providing a comprehensive overview of these topics, the review aims to deepen the understanding of forensic toxicologists and support their efforts in improving the detection, analysis, and interpretation of toxicological results in both legal and clinical settings.

Quality assurance

Method validation

The identification of analytes is a critical aspect in forensic toxicology and requires robust validation of analytical methods. In 2023, the Organization of Scientific Area Committees (OSAC) for Forensic Science, under the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST), published a relevant standard [1] outlining the minimum criteria for analyte identification, excluding alcohols, routine volatiles, carbon monoxide, cyanide, and metals. This standard employs a point-based system. Furthermore, updates regarding qualitative method development and validation are planned for the Standard Practices for Method Validation in Forensic Toxicology [2], as highlighted in a webinar [3] by the Forensic Toxicology Subcommittee of OSAC.

The role of the spike and recovery (SAR) method in the identification and quantification of analytes within sample matrices has been extensively reviewed by Thakur et al. [4]. As a key parameter in analytical method validation, SAR holds significant importance in method development. Sánchez-Sellero et al. [5] reported excellent recovery rates (100.84–121.01%) using a sonication-assisted dispersive liquid–liquid microextraction procedure to measure zolpidem in human plasma. This method was successfully applied to ten postmortem samples and proposed for routine toxicology analyses.

In a separate study, Ferrari Júnior and Caldas [6] validated the use of QuEChERS (Quick, Easy, Cheap, Effective, Rugged, and Safe) for recovering 79 NPS and other drugs from blood and urine samples. Employing ultra-high liquid performance chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (UHPLC–MS/MS), their method achieved limits of quantification ranging from 0.4 to 16 ng/mL in both postmortem and antemortem samples. Additionally, Cox et al. [7] demonstrated effectiveness of QuEChERS combined with liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS-MS) in quantifying 34 fentanyl compounds, metabolites and analogs with in liver specimens. Between 2011 and 2021, Wille et al. [8] conducted a comprehensive review of 26 high-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS)-based methods for qualitative systematic toxicological analysis. Their review encompassed both targeted and untargeted data acquisition approaches, the use of HRMS reference databases and libraries, and current guidelines for HRMS data interpretation, with a specific emphasis on identification criteria. The authors provided recommendations for method development, compound identification, validation experiments, and the precise determination of measurement uncertainty, taking into account samples matrices, analytical platforms, numbers of analytes, and the employed mass spectral reference databases and libraries. In a more recent study, Becam et al. [9] validated simple method utilizing QuEChERS salts and acetonitrile combined with liquid chromatography-high-resolution mass spectrometry (LC-HRMS). This method was designed for non-targeted screening and quantification of 29 compounds relevant to clinical and forensic toxicology applications.

The application of ambient ionization mass spectrometry (AIMS) techniques for analyzing of new psychoactive substances in biological matrices and drug seizures was reviewed by Boronat Ena et al. [10]. Their review highlighted a lack of comprehensive guidelines specific to AIMS. Consequently, validation in the reviewed studies was conducted with reference to the criteria from the US Food and Drug Administration's (FDA) Bioanalytical Method Validation or the recommendations of the Scientific Working Group for the Analysis of Seized Drugs (SWGDRUG). In another study, Tegner et al. [11] developed and validated a method for quantifying EtG and EtS using UPLC-MS-MS, following guidelines from the Scientific Working Group for Forensic Toxicology (SWGTOX). Their research, involving the blood of 20 volunteers after ethanol ingestion and 99 fatal road traffic injury cases, demonstrated concordance between blood alcohol concentrations and EtG/EtS findings in 98.6% of positive and 96.3% of negative cases.

Dried blood spots (DBS) have emerged as a valuable tool in toxicological analyses due to their stability for analytes and convenience in sample storage. Massano et al. [12] developed and validated a method for detecting 132 NPS and synthetic opioids, including fentanyl, in DBS. This method utilized ultra-high-pressure liquid-chromatography (UHPLC) coupled with time-of-flight high resolution mass spectrometry (TOF-HRMS) using a 0.5 mL of methanol/acetonitrile (3:1 v/v) extract. It was verified using previously tested positive venous whole blood samples. Additionally, Su et al. [13] developed an analytical system for quantifying drugs and poisons, including methamphetamine, lidocaine, ketamine, fentanyl, and diazepam, in DBS using ultra-performance liquid chromatography coupled with tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC-MS/MS). Their study examined storage, preparation and analytical conditions, demonstrating that these drugs remained stable in DBS for up to 60 days at room temperature. The results showed high quantitative consistency between DBS and whole-blood samples. Moreover, Guo et al. [14] established and validated a screening method for 425 drugs and poisons using DBS stored at room temperature. While most drugs, except ambroxol, zopiclone, carbofuran, chlorpyrifos, and valproic acid, remained detectable after 3-5 years, drugs from unknown volumes of bloodstains collected at crime scenes were only estimable in concentration, as demonstrated by valine and leucine analysis [15].

Quality control and accreditation

The use of suitable matrix reference materials (RM) is required for quality control. It is suggested that drugs spiked in drug-free hair samples from healthy volunteers (soaked in water and methanol, 1:1 v/v) [16] and freeze-dried human urine matrix RM [17] are excellent tools for daily quality control, as these gave results closest to those of natural matrix reference materials.

Realizing the need for measures of performance in the forensic arena, a regional forensic laboratories accreditation center is being set up by the Arab Accreditation Cooperation [18]. The Forensic Laboratory-Arabian Gate platform would be developed preliminarily, followed by the Arab Forensic Laboratories Accreditation Center following the requirements specified in the ISO/IEC 17011 standard.

Uncertainty of measurement

The consideration of measurement uncertainty (MU) using gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) and UHPLC-MS/MS was explored by Holman et al. [19]. Their study showed that a two-sample approach, involving consecutive sample collection at intervals of ≥20 min, for retrograde extrapolation of Δ9-tetrahydrocannabinol (Δ9-THC) concentration in blood is not recommended. This conclusion was drawn after examining 548 drug-impaired driving cases from apprehended drivers in Norway between 2000 and 2020.

Zancanaro et al. [20] established a decision rule based on measurement uncertainties using a validated method for cannabinoid analysis in whole blood from drug-impaired driving cases. This method incorporates online extraction using turbulent flow chromatography and LC-High-Resolution, Accurate-Mass (HRAM)-Orbitrap-MS. It offers a simple, rapid, highly specific, and sensitive approach for the determination of Δ9-THC, cannabidiol (CBD), cannabinol (CBN), 11-hydroxy-Δ9-tetrahydrocannabinol (11-OH-THC), and 11-nor-9-carboxy-Δ9-tetrahydrocannabinol (Δ9-THC-COOH) in 50 μL whole blood samples.

Kim et al. [21] conducted quantitative determination of amphetamine and 4-hydroxyamphetamine in urine using a dilute-and-shoot approach combined with LC-MS/MS. Measurement uncertainty was evaluated using both the Guide to the Expression of Uncertainty in Measurement (GUM) and the Monte Carlo method (MCM), with the latter considered to be a more practical approach for assessing MU.

Result interpretation

Dinis-Oliveira [22], in a review article, discussed the complexities of utilizing ethanol blood analysis to determine the state of influence in drink-driving cases. Several artefacts and pitfalls were identified, including considerations for antemortem cases, such as the type of sample used (breath or blood), and for postmortem cases, factors like the site of blood collection, storage conditions, microbial ethanol production, and postmortem redistribution (PMR). Additional factors include the condition of the body, the postmortem interval, the analysis of nonvolatile/nonoxidative metabolites, and comparisons with alternative samples such as vitreous humour or urine. A similar perspective was shared by Stephenson et al. [23], who highlighted the challenges in interpreting postmortem drug levels in routine forensic toxicological analyses. These challenges involve determining the cause, mechanism, and manner of death, as well as accounting for PMR, diffusion, drug reservoirs, site-to-site variability in drug concentrations, bacterial activity, and the pharmacological and metabolic properties of drugs.

Two studies specifically demonstrated the use of the cardiac-to-peripheral blood (C/P) drug concentration ratio as an indicator of PMR. One study focused on cocaine and its metabolites [24], while the other examined amphetamines and benzodiazepines [25]. Both studies analyzed postmortem cases from the Netherlands between 2010 and 2020. Regarding the interpretation of postmortem results from hair analysis, Benhalima et al. [26] discussed the pitfalls and artefacts, predominantly stemming from contamination during perimortem or postmortem periods. It was proposed that nail analysis could provide complementary information to enhance the reliability of positive hair analysis results.

Driving under influence

Detection of alcohol

The alcohol level in blood/breath is a critical information in the case of driving under the influence of alcohol. Relevant studies with respect to the instrumentation, cases studies and the stability of the sample are summarized below.

Blood alcohol concentration (BAC) detection

A wearable blood alcohol content monitoring device that provides accurate, noninvasive, and real-time measurements is highly desirable. Despite several wearable blood alcohol content devices are available on the market, there is still room for improvement in accuracy. The main challenge lies in measuring transcutaneous alcohol and correlating it to BAC, as the accuracy is affected by factors such as skin thickness and alcohol pharmacokinetics. To address this, Jalal et al. [27] developed a detailed transcutaneous alcohol diffusion model by using laboratory-built BAC monitoring devices based on the proton exchange membrane fuel cell (PEMFC) and real-time captured data from the wrists of eight volunteers. Their results demonstrated that the experimental data collected was best fit to an exponential linear function.

Daglioglu et al. [28] developed methods for detecting phosphatidylethanol (PEth) in dried blood samples from drivers involved in traffic accidents, showing that there is a positive correlation between BAC and PEth concentration when driving under the influence of ethanol.

Breath alcohol concentration (BrAC) detection

Ethanol, commonly found in e-cigarette liquids (e-liquids), poses the risk of producing false positives during ethanol breath tests if vaped before or during testing. Both preliminary breath tests (PBTs) and evidentiary breath tests (EBTs) detect ethanol in exhaled breath, while standardized field sobriety tests (SFSTs) are typically used to assess impairment. Holt et al. [29] investigated the mechanics of vaping ethanol using e-cigarettes and the impact of vaping ethanol on SFSTs and breath tests utilized by law enforcement officers. Their findings emphasized the importance of a waiting period before conducting breath tests, though such a wait is not always required by jurisdiction or practiced in all settings, such as workplace testing.

Ethanol stability study

Olds [30] examined the stability of ethanol in blood samples preserved in sampling tubes with 0.25% nominal sodium fluoride (NaF). A retrospective analysis of initial and reanalyzed BAC data from antemortem blood, showed that initial BACs ranged from 0.025 to 0.394 g/100 mL and reanalyzed BACs ranged from 0.021 to 0.393 g/100 mL. The comparison indicated that the BAC values remained consistent even after nearly 4 years of refrigerated storage. The study also showed that ethanol increases (n = 6) were within the uncertainty of measurement (UM) of the initial analysis, i.e. statistically insignificant. In contrast, four of the ethanol losses (n = 29) exceeded the UM, which is comparable to or less than those observed in studies using 1% NaF preservative under varying temperature and storage durations.

Another similar study by Phung et al. [31], which involved the largest sample size of 1228 blind quality control evaluations carried out from 2015 to 2023, aimed to determine if long-term refrigerated storage produced results that aligned closely with ethanol concentrations at the time of blood draw. The authors showed that all 316 ethanol-negative samples remained negative, while after 42-758 days, the ethanol concentrations of the 936 positive samples gradually declined over time, with a statistically significant difference observed. With a measurement uncertainty of 9.4% (k = 3), there was no apparent correlation between the change in ethanol and the theoretical target concentration. As the interval between the analysis of the two blood specimens increased, ethanol loss significantly increased (P < 0.001).

Kosecki et al. [32] investigated ethanol stability in antemortem blood stored without the NaF preservative. The authors collected antemortem blood from ethanol-free individuals into tubes containing only the anticoagulant potassium EDTA and spiked with ethanol to approximately either 0.08 or 0.15 mg/100 mL. Dual-FID headspace gas chromatography analysis showed that the mean decreases in ethanol levels in the unpreserved antemortem blood were comparable in magnitude to those observed in antemortem blood with NaF after one year of refrigerated storage. Notably, no increase of ethanol levels was detected in unpreserved samples, despite the absence of NaF.

In another study, Kosecki et al. [33] examined the possibility of ethanol formation in blood stored in unstoppered sampling tubes due to environmental contamination by Candida Abicans resulting from ambient air drawn into the evacuated blood collection tube. Blood sampling tubes without preservative were left unstoppered at room temperature for 24 or 48 h in various locations and stored under different conditions (∼4, ∼22°C or 37°C). Headspace gas chromatography analysis showed no ethanol in blood samples under various storage conditions, regardless of storage time, temperature, or preservative concentration.

Olds et al. [34] reviewed the importance of various preanalytical factors affecting ethanol concentration in blood after collection and before analysis or reanalysis after various periods of storage. This review of the scientific literature found no evidence that BACs increased after sample collection and before analysis, provided the samples were stored with NaF preservative. Instead, in most of the studies, BACs decreased during storage depending on time, storage temperature, the amount of NaF preservative, and whether the tube was previously opened. Ethanol loss was independent on the type of evacuated tubes used, whether glass or plastic. Urine alcohol concentrations remained stable over various storage periods, although in cases of glycosuria and urinary tract and/or Candida infections, the addition of NaF (1% w/v) was necessary to prevent post-sampling ethanol synthesis.

The analysis of BAC provided crucial information for both clinical condition of acute alcohol intoxication (AAI) and criminal liability of driving under the influence (DUI). Ialongo [35] conducted a systematic review analyzing preanalytical factors affecting BAC. The review aimed to identify optimal practices for sample collection, transportation, handling, and preparation, while also exploring whether separate processes are necessary for diagnostic and forensic BAC testing. One of the key findings of the review is that no literature evidence supports the necessity to differentiate preanalytical procedures for AAI and DUI, except for the traceability throughout the chain of custody. Adhering to preanalytical guidelines from official bodies, such as European Federation of Clinical Chemistry and Laboratory Medicine, ensures both diagnostic accuracy and forensic defensibility of BAC results.

Detection of drug

Identification and quantification of different drugs in various matrices always pose a challenge in the case of driving under the influence of drugs (DUID). The following is a review of different aspects concerning drug examination in DUID cases.

Simultaneous examination of multiple analytes in different matrices

Blood and urine specimens

Rodda et al. [36] developed a streamlined LC-MS/MS method using protein precipitation and filtration extraction for consolidating analyses of drug-facilitated crime (DFC), postmortem investigations, and DUID testing. The method targeted 57 drug and metabolite analytes, which eluted in under 6 min, in blood (quantitatively) and urine (qualitatively). These included gamma-hydroxybutyrate (GHB) precursors (1), hallucinogens (3), muscle relaxants (3), anticonvulsants (7), antidepressants (20), antihistamines (5), antipsychotics (11), antihypertensives and alpha-adrenergics (3), analgesics and anesthetics (3), and miscellaneous compounds (1). Limit of detection (LOD) for each analyte was set to meet the stringent sensitivity requirements for DFC, and were suitable for postmortem and other forensic casework, including DUID. Comprehensive validation following guidelines from the American National Standards Institute/Academy Standards Board (ASB/ANSI) was performed. Applicability studies analyzed 72 proficiency test blood and urine samples, along with 9206 unique blood and urines samples from 5192 authentic forensic cases, resulting in 11,961 positive analytes.

Nie et al. [37] developed a method utilizing high-performance liquid chromatography coupled with Orbitrap mass spectrometry for the simultaneous analysis of new psychoactive substances (NPS). The method, which including 42 fentanyl analogs and 104 synthetic cannabinoids, also detects commonly encountered drugs such as opiates, cocaine, cannabinoids, stimulants, sedatives and tranquilizers in blood, urine and hair samples. The most common drugs found in the samples were methamphetamine, 6-acetylmorphine, and ketamine.

Hsu et al. [38] established a rapid analytical platform for NPSs using paper-loaded direct analysis in real time triple quadrupole mass spectrometry (pDART-QqQ-MS). The method offers the advantage of analyzing a single sample within 30 s and required only a small volume of the specimen. It can detect 21 NPSs, including ketamine, methoxetamine, norketamine, deschloroketamine, mephedrone, 4-MPD, MEAP, CMC, methylone, ephylone, eutylone, 3,4-MDPHP, dibutylone MDPV, α-PVP, 4-chloro-α-PVP, amphetamine, methamphetamine, 6-acetylmorphine, MDA, MDMA, DMA, PMEA, PMA, and PMMA in urine at concentration ranging from 20 to 75 ng per milliliter.

Ayala and Kerrigan [39] developed a comprehensive screening procedure utilizing supported liquid extraction coupled with liquid chromatography-quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometry (LC-QTOF-MS) using All Ions data acquisition for identifying over 200 drugs, including cannabinoids and NPS, in whole blood. The procedure, validated as per ANSI/ASB guidelines, successfully identified all compounds of interest at recommended cutoff levels for drug-impaired driving investigations. Cannabinoids, such as Δ9-THC-COOH, along with fentanyl analogs, buprenorphine, novel synthetic opioids, and synthetic cannabinoids, were detected at low to sub-nanogram/milliliter concentrations using both positive and negative electrospray ionization methods.

Oral fluid specimen

Coulter et al. [40] and Sarris et al. [41] developed and validated rapid, straightforward methods for qualitative screening of over 30 drugs in oral fluid using LC-MS-MS. These drugs included Δ9-THC, amphetamine, methamphetamine, 3,4-methylenedioxymethamphetamine, 3,4-methylenedioxyamphetamine, cocaine, benzoylecgonine, cocaethylene, carisoprodol, meprobamate, zolpidem, alprazolam, clonazepam, 7-aminoclonazepam, diazepam, nordiazepam, lorazepam, oxazepam, temazepam, codeine, morphine, 6-acetylmorphine, buprenorphine, fentanyl, hydrocodone, hydromorphone, oxycodone, oxymorphone, methadone, tramadol, and phencyclidine. Both methods adhered to ANSI/ASB standards for forensic toxicology and were successfully applied to proficiency specimens and routine samples, demonstrating reliability in detecting drugs for DUID investigations.

Hair

Barone et al. [42] developed an ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography mass spectrometry (UHPLC-MS) for the identifying and quantifying 127 NPS, including phenethylamines, arylcyclohexylamines, synthetic opioids, tryptamines, synthetic cannabinoids, synthetic cathinones, and designer benzodiazepines, as well as 15 drugs of abuse in hair samples. The method, fully validated according to European Medicines Agency (EMA) guidelines, assessed selectivity, linearity, accuracy, precision, limit of quantification (LOQ), LOD, matrix effects, and recovery. To demonstrate its applicability, the method was tested on 22 authentic hair samples collected for forensic purposes.

Kutzler et al. [43] developed a qualitative method for detecting synthetic cannabinoids (SCs) in abstinence control programs for driver's license regranting, adhering to the guidelines set by the German Society of Toxicological and Forensic Chemistry (GTFCh). Hair samples (n = 5097) collected from various regions in Germany between March 2020 and March 2021 were analyzed for drugs of abuse and 107 SCs using LC–MS/MS, with positive samples further analyzed using LC-QTOF/MS to identify unknown SC analogs. This method revealed 181 detections of SCs across 44 different compounds. The most prevalent compounds detected were 5F-MDMB-PICA and MDMB-4en-PINACA. The study highlighted the necessity for routine SC screening in abstinence control programs to monitor emerging SC analogs and prevent the substituted use of cannabis with SCs.

Drugs of interest

Cannabinoids

Given the presence of products in the market containing delta-8-tetrahydrocannabinol (Δ8-THC), it is highly desirable to develop a method that can distinguish it from Δ9-THC, the major active ingredient of cannabis. To address this, Mantinieks et al. [44] developed a multi-analyte LC-MS/MS method capable of separating Δ9-THC and Δ8-THC, as well as MDMA, cocaine, methamphetamine and their metabolites.

The global rise of semi-synthetic cannabinoids, such as hexahydrocannabinol (HHC), has been significant over the past few years. HHC first emerged in the USA drug market around mid-2021, later appearing elsewhere in infused hemp flowers, vape products, and edibles. HHC is a hydro-derivative of cannabinol with THC-like pharmacological properties and can be synthesized from CBD. In a comprehensive review, Ujváry [45] examined the current state of knowledge on HHC and closely related semi-synthetic cannabinoids, summarizing their chemical properties, pharmacology, and potential therapeutic applications. An LC-MS-MS method was developed and validated by Kronstrand et al. [46] for the quantitative analysis of the two commonly encountered diastereomers, 9R-HHC and 9S-HHC, and their metabolites. Blood samples from DUID cases that screened positive for cannabis and confirmed negative for THC were reanalyzed for HHC. The method demonstrated good precision (<10% for both inter- and intra-day) and low bias (<6%), with an LLOQ of 0.2 ng/mL for the parent drugs and 2.0 ng/mL for the carboxylated metabolites. Reanalysis confirmed HHC in 32 out of 145 cases, with 9R-HHC-COOH identified as the major metabolite in blood. Additionally, Zancanaro et al. [20] developed a rapid and precise online extraction method using turbulent flow chromatography and LC-HRAM-Orbitrap-MS to detect THC, CBD, CBN, 11-OH-THC, and Δ9-THC-COOH in 50 μL whole blood samples. This method was tested on 7620 DUID samples over time, yielding 641 positive results.

New psychoactive substances (NPS)

Over the past decade, forensic toxicologists have faced challenges due to the rise of NPS. Clandestine labs modify chemical structures of existing drugs to evade regulations, leading to unregulated compounds that have unknown toxicities and potencies. Traditional analytical methods are often inadequate due to the lack of reference standards and rapid metabolism of NPS. Fortunately, improved LC-MS instrumentation, especially LC-MS/MS with electrospray ionization (ESI) and diagnostic fragment ions, provides an effective tool for untargeted screening analysis using growing mass spectra databases. Matey et al. [47] conducted a comprehensive review in the identification of NPS and their metabolites using non-targeted detection with high-resolution mass spectrometry through diagnosing fragment ions/neutral loss analysis. Cieri et al. [48] assessed the effectiveness of Enzyme Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA) for detecting NPS across five subclasses: novel synthetic opioids, fentanyl analogs, stimulants, benzodiazepines, and hallucinogens, using whole blood samples. The study evaluated cross-reactivity with commercially available ELISA kits and found varying degrees of cross-reactivity for different NPS. The results underscore the challenges in accurately detecting NPS and the need for improved analytical methods.

Phenibut is a psychotropic drug structurally similar to the neurotransmitter γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA) and acts as an agonist of the GABAB receptor. Dziadosz et al. [49] recently reported its detection in a DUID case involving self-reported intake of a recommended/recreational dose. The authors utilized an LC-MS/MS-based multitarget screening method for identification and a separate LC-MS/MS method for quantification. Given the rarity of data concerning phenibut blood concentrations, the information from this case can be useful for future identification of positive phenibut samples.

Other drugs of interest

Nielsen et al. [50] investigated the origin of drugs consumed among drivers who tested positive for amphetamine in eastern Denmark. The authors applied a simple and efficient analytical workflow using chiral UHPLC-MS-MS to separate amphetamine enantiomers, supplementing routine quantification of common illicit and medical drugs in 5248 forensic DUID cases over a 2-year period. Similarly, Bauer et al. [51] utilized a chiral UPLC-MS/MS method to analyze the enantiomeric profiles of amphetamine and methamphetamine in Iceland, examining blood samples from suspected DUID cases and seized drugs from 2021 to 2022. The study revealed that DUID blood samples positive for amphetamine (n = 564) had a median concentration of 180 ng/mL and a median enantiomeric fraction (EFR) of 0.54, whereas methamphetamine-positive samples (n = 236) showed a median concentration of 185 ng/mL and a median EFR of 0.23.

Additives in blood sampling tubes

Olds and Naquin [52] aimed to determine whether there was a statistical difference in blood alcohol samples stored in 6 mL and 10 mL tubes with 0.25% and 1% sodium fluoride, respectively. Considering variables such as time, storage temperature, fill volume, and concentration, paired t-tests indicated no significant difference in BAC between the two tube sizes. However, Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) testing revealed that the two tube sizes produced different results at 0.08 g/100 ml.

In his subsequent work, Olds [53] conducted a study focused on fill volume and storage duration with the samples at 0.08 g/100mL, while increasing the sample population to n = 8. The two tube types yielded equivalent concentrations under most conditions studied. Differences between the two tube types were found using paired t-tests for the high-volume samples on Days 7 and 30; ANOVA yielded the same results but also identified one additional statistical difference for the Day 30 low-volume samples. However, the differences observed between tube sizes fall within standard deviation ranges established for the analytical method's precision profile, indicating that the statistical differences are not analytically significant.

Roadside testing

Driving under the influence of cannabis is on the rise globally, making it one of the most common substances involved in impaired driving cases after alcohol. Harper et al. [54] reported on the first comprehensive Oral Fluid (OF) drug testing program in the U.S., which includes roadside screening devices and LC-MS-MS confirmation methods. The roadside devices demonstrated over 80% accuracy for targeted drugs, and the LC-MS-MS methods met 86% of the National Safety Council-recommended cutoffs. Over five years, THC and cocaine were found in 90% and 97% of samples, respectively, compared to 75% and 44% in blood samples.

Detection of Δ9-THC in OF has been utilized to identify recent cannabis use. Scherer et al. [55] evaluated the sensitivities, specificities, and efficiencies of various OF devices for detecting cannabinoids at the roadside, including AquilaScan®, Draeger DrugTest®, WipeAlyser Reader®, and Druglizer®. The study demonstrated varying performance among the devices, with Draeger DrugTest® and WipeAlyser Reader® showing the best results.

Wennberg et al. [56] conducted a review to evaluate roadside screening tests for cannabis use, analyzing data from six databases and grey literature sources. The review focused on the sensitivity and specificity of Δ9-THC detection across 101 studies. Oral fluid tests demonstrated higher specificity but lower sensitivity compared to urine tests, and showed higher sensitivity and comparable specificity to observational tests when assessed against both blood and oral fluid laboratory tests. The Draeger DrugTest® 5000 and Alere DDS 2® Mobile Test System were identified as the most promising instruments.

Drug level interpretation

Alcohol-impaired driving (AID)

Barry et al. [57] analyzed the prevalence, frequency, and rates of AID among adults in the USA for the years 2014, 2016, and 2018. Between 2014 and 2018, AID reporting among adults saw slight fluctuations, peaking in 2016, with a significant majority being men and many engaging in binge drinking. Dempsey et al. [58] further investigated the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on blood ethanol levels in postmortem and driving while intoxicated (DWI) cases analyzed by the Dallas County Southwestern Institute of Forensic Sciences from 2019 to 2021. The study found that while ethanol concentrations were higher in DWI cases than in postmortem cases, the ethanol positivity rate for DWI cases decreased during Q2 2020 due to the pandemic but returned to pre-pandemic levels by the end of 2020, with postmortem ethanol positivity remaining stable at around 20%.

In addition to these temporal trends, other studies have examined the acute effects of alcohol on various driving-related activities. Zube et al. [59] found that e-scooter driving performance significantly worsens with alcohol consumption, with marked deterioration observed at BACs below 1.10 g/kg. Performance dropped to about 60% of initial levels even at low BACs of 0.21−0.60 g/kg, highlighting the negative effects of alcohol on e-scooter driving. Besides, Pérez-Mañá et al. [60] investigated the interaction between energy drinks and alcohol on driving skills and perceptions. Their findings indicate that mixing energy drinks with alcohol may lead consumers to drive under the influence, as energy drinks can mask alcohol's negative effects on driving abilities.

Isopropanol (IPA) intoxication, which is relatively rare compared to other types of alcohol intoxication, can severely impair driving due to its effects on the central nervous system. Kirkton and Limoges [61] described a traffic incident involving intentional IPA intoxication. Despite a low preliminary breath test result, further tests and forensic blood analysis revealed IPA ingestion and an interferent-detected error, highlighting the need for thorough evaluations in IPA-related impairment cases.

Driving under the influence of drugs (DUID)

Single-drug impairment

Ongoing investigations have been focused on understanding how drug intake affects driving impairment, such as cocaine [62], MDMA [62], ketamine [63], fluorodeshloroketamine (FDCK) [64], bromazolam [65], N2O [66] and gabapentin [67]. Heide et al. [62] investigated the prevalence and blood concentrations of cocaine and MDMA in drugged drivers from 2000 to 2022. Cocaine was detected in 2331 cases and MDMA in 2569 cases, with median concentrations of 0.09 mg/L for 377 mono cases of cocaine and 0.19 mg/L for 85 mono cases of MDMA, and clinically, 54% of cocaine drivers and 38% of MDMA drivers were found to be impaired. Lucuta et al. [63] evaluated eight DUID cases involving ketamine from two institutes of legal medicine between January 2021 and January 2023. The study provides additional data on psychomotor impairments observed in ketamine-related DUID cases. Besides, Cheng et al. [64] reported the seizure of FDCK, a ketamine analog, as a street drug, and its detection in several drug-driving cases in Hong Kong. The authors noted that FDCK seizures resembled ketamine in physical appearance, suggesting that users might mistakenly misuse it as ketamine. Bierly et al. [65] reported the detection of bromazolam in 98 impaired driving cases from samples reported between January 2021 and December 2023, with the earliest detection from September 2020. Based on its prevalence and demonstrated impairing effects, the authors suggested that bromazolam should be included in the scope of impaired driving testing as long as it continues to be prevalent in the drug supply.

Nitrous oxide (N2O), commonly known as laughing gas, has gained attention for its potential misuse in driving cases. In light of this, Lindholm et al. [66] conducted a three-year (2020−2022) retrospective study on N2O involvement in traffic cases in Eastern Denmark. Out of 62 blood samples, 52 confirmed recent N2O intake, with 26 cases involving other driving-impairing drugs and 3 cases involving alcohol. This study shows that detection of N2O in whole blood samples is possible by headspace (HS)-GC-MS with a minimum of sample preparation. Besides, Bierly and Chan-Hosokawa [67] explored the role of gabapentin in drugged driving investigations by reviewing approximately 108,000 gabapentin-positive DUID cases between January 2020 and December 2022.

Polydrug impairment

The growing threat of multi-drugs-impaired driving is becoming a critical concern for public safety and road traffic regulations. Favretto et al. [68] reported the prevalence and concentrations of sedative-hypnotic drugs, such as benzodiazepines and zolpidem, in the blood of drivers involved in road traffic crashes in the Padova region of Italy. Frequent associations with alcohol, cocaine, and other benzodiazepines were noted, confirming the abuse potential of these medications. Kuhlman et al. [69] further investigated the prevalence of buprenorphine in DUID cases in Southwestern Virginia from 2017 to 2019. Polydrug use involving buprenorphine was common, with benzodiazepines, amphetamines, and cannabinoids being the most frequently confirmed drugs in DUID screenings, and only 10% of cases not involving other confirmed drugs.

Zopiclone is a widely utilized hypnotic agent, frequently detected in individuals apprehended for driving under the influence. Strand et al. [70] analyzed two consecutive whole blood samples from drivers suspected of DUI in Norway between 2003 and 2021. Zopiclone was detected in 2 to 3% of drivers’ blood samples, often in supra-therapeutic concentrations and polydrug cases, with an estimated half-life of approximately 3.1 to 3.8 h. Similarly, Papsun et al. [71] documented a rising trend in designer benzodiazepine (DBZD) use in DUI cases from 2017 to 2021. Out of 805 blood samples, 1145 DBZDs were detected, with the most frequently found being etizolam/alpha-hydroxyetizolam (60%) and flualprazolam (18%).

Currie et al. [72] conducted a retrospective data analysis of suspected impaired driving cases between 2021 and 2022. The evaluation of blood and urine samples by the Center of Forensic Sciences revealed the presence of cocaine and/or benzoylecgonine, providing data on concentrations in suspected impaired drivers, prior cocaine use, and the drug's effects on driving. Beirness et al. [73] also examined the results of toxicological tests performed on blood and urine samples collected from suspected drug-impaired drivers in Ontario from 2008 to 2019. Over a 12-year period, 5388 samples revealed that cannabis (52.8%), cocaine (44.3%), and methamphetamine (24.8%) were the most frequently detected substances in drivers, with more than one substance found in 80% of cases. Institóris et al. [74] further examined the prevalence and patterns of psychoactive substance use among suspected DUID drivers in Hungary from 2016 to 2018. Of the 2260 drug-positive drivers, 1553 (69%) were classified as impaired, 38 to 50% involved multiple drugs, with a noted decrease in stimulant NPS use and an increase in synthetic cannabinoids use compared to 2014−15.

Chan-Hosokawa and Bierly [75] conducted an 11-year study on fentanyl-positive DUID cases from 2010 to 2020 and identified fentanyl as the most frequently detected opioid, often in combination with stimulants. In another study, Harper et al. [76] evaluated the prevalence and concentrations of fentanyl in blood and oral fluid, as well as traffic fatalities, in DUID cases across three regions—Alabama, Orange County, CA, and Houston, TX—from 2017 to 2022. The study found significant increases in fentanyl DUID cases during the COVID-19 pandemic, with a 4-fold rise in Alabama and a 14-fold rise in Orange County from 2017 to 2022, and a 2-fold increase in Houston from 2019 to 2022. Over 90% of fentanyl cases involved polydrug use, with methamphetamine, THC, and alprazolam being the most common additional substances. The study suggests that using oral fluid as an alternative or supplemental specimen to blood could be a promising approach for detecting fentanyl in DUID cases.

DUID vs postmortem (PM) cases

Ongoing research have highlighted the complexities of interpreting drug concentrations in impaired driving and PM cases, revealing significant overlaps and challenges. Hermansen and Christoffersen [77] analyzed whole-blood results from 7088 DUID cases from 2015 to 2019 and forensic autopsies of 220 drug addicts conducted between 2015 and 2020. Their study concluded that within the DUID group, opioids like heroin/morphine and methadone, as well as stimulants such as amphetamine and MDMA, frequently appeared in concentrations exceeding reported lower comatose-fatal levels, whereas cocaine levels were generally below these thresholds.

Dempsey et al. [78] also analyzed blood concentrations of various drugs − fentanyl, oxycodone, hydrocodone, clonazepam, THC, and methamphetamine − in both impaired driving and postmortem cases in Houston, TX, from 2014 to 2020. They found that drug levels in impaired driving cases frequently overlap with toxic levels observed postmortem, complicating the interpretation of results. Similarly, Havro et al. [79] analyzed fentanyl concentrations in DUID and PM cases in Orange County, CA, over a two-year period and reported significant overlap in fentanyl levels between DUID and PM cases.

Lastly, Kacinko et al. [80] reviewed the prevalence and concentrations of xylazine over 2.5 years from 2019 to 2021 in DUID and medico-legal death investigation (MDI) cases, along with other drugs detected in combination with xylazine. Out of over 170,000 xylazine cases, 97% were classified as MDI, with overlapping concentrations in living and deceased individuals, complicating the interpretation of the drug's role without additional case information.

Cannabinoids

Over the past 25 years, marijuana laws in the USA have evolved, with California beginning to legalize medicinal marijuana in 1996 and progressively relaxing regulations. Meneses and Mata [81] studied the prevalence of five cannabinoids, including THC, 11-OH-THC, Δ9-THC-COOH, CBN, and CBD in Orange County, CA, from 2016 to 2019. Following the legalization of recreational marijuana, there was an increase in THC, Δ9-THC-COOH, and 11-OH-THC in postmortem and major crime cases from 2016 to 2017, with a slight decrease in DUID cases. By 2018, after marijuana shops could sell to anyone over 21, all five cannabinoids showed an increase in both DUID and postmortem cases.

Favretto et al. [82] also analyzed cannabinoid concentrations in blood samples of DUI offenders involved in road traffic accidents or apprehended by police in the University Hospital of Padova, Italy from 2017 to 2021, noting that the time elapsed between the time of incident and sampling could affect the assessment of impairment. Willeman et al. [83] further reported recent trends for drivers testing positive for illicit drugs in oral fluid from 2018 to 2021 in France, and they noted that cannabis was the most prevalent drug of abuse identified.

Gjerde et al. [84] investigated polydrug use among apprehended crash-involved drivers with THC concentrations above the legal limit in Norway during 2013−2020. The study concluded that cannabis might significantly contribute to impairment since most THC-positive drivers also tested positive for other psychoactive substances, while those with high THC concentrations less frequently had high concentrations of other substances.

NPS and synthetic cathinones

The increasing prevalence of NPS and synthetic cathinones in DUID cases has led to extensive research aimed at understanding their impact and prevalence. Mohr et al. [85] reviewed publications in PubMed (National Center for Biotechnology Information, U.S. National Library of Medicine, Bethesda, MD) and Google Scholar (Google, Inc., Mountain View, CA) associated with NPS from 2017 to 2020, reporting 1319 cases that included DUID incidents as well as overdose fatalities and clinical treatments. The study identified over 60 NPS with confirmed adverse events, including synthetic cannabinoids, stimulants, hallucinogens, benzodiazepines, and opioids, with 50 of these substances being reported for the first time during this period. Pieprzyca et al. [86] examined 28 cases of mixed poisonings involving synthetic cathinones and other drugs, with α-pyrrolidinohexiophenone and N-ethylpentedrone detected in blood samples. The findings revealed that the concurrent use of synthetic cathinones with other substances significantly increases toxicity, leading to severe health consequences, including multiple organ failure and death.

Simultaneous alcohol- and drug impairment in driving

The concurrent use of cannabis and alcohol has been the subject of extensive research, particularly regarding its impact on driving performance and behavior. Simmons et al. [87] conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis, demonstrating that the simultaneous consumption of these two substances markedly impairs driving abilities and elevates the risk of vehicular accidents. Fares et al. [88,89] also concluded that combining alcohol and cannabis has potential additive effects on driving skills, including weaving and reaction time, as well as on cognition and subjective experiences.

Besides, the interaction between alcohol and methamphetamine (MA) has also been a focus of research. Hayley et al. [90] conducted a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled study, which demonstrated that the acute co-administration of these substances significantly alters gaze behavior during driving, indicating marked cognitive and motor impairments.

Substantial research has explored the impacts of alcohol and various other substance combinations on driving performance and behavior, shedding light on the significant risks involved. Aitken et al. [91] reported that legally permissible levels of alprazolam (1 mg) and alcohol (0.04% BAC), alone and in combination, impaired driving performance, which can be indexed by ocular activity. Simonsen et al. [92] examined the presence of psychoactive drugs and alcohol in blood from apprehended DUI drivers in Denmark in a five-year period from 2015 to 2019, and they reported that alcohol was the most prevalent substance in impaired drivers, followed by THC and central nervous system stimulants, i.e. mostly amphetamine and cocaine. The authors also noted that DUID was evenly spread over the week while drink-driving peaked on weekends.

Cieri et al. [93] studied drug use in suspected impaired drivers from 2017 to 2020, demonstrating that ∆-9 THC (∼50% of the cases), ethanol, amphetamines/methamphetamines, fentanyl, and alprazolam were the most commonly abused substances. ∆-9 THC showed an increase in positivity rates, while ethanol was the most common substance overall. The combination of ⊗-9 THC and ethanol was frequently detected. Significant increases in average concentrations of fentanyl, methamphetamine, and ∆-9 THC were noted, as well as in the maximum concentrations of amphetamine, methamphetamine, and fentanyl. Conversely, decreases were observed in the concentrations of certain central nervous system depressants, particularly prescription benzodiazepines and narcotic analgesics.

Hjelmeland et al. [94] investigated the relationship between blood concentrations of ethanol, benzodiazepines, amphetamines, and THC, and driver impairment using a clinical test of impairment across 15,514 individual mono-drug cases. They found a strong correlation between ethanol levels and impairment (Spearman's rho = 0.548, p < 0.001), a moderate correlation for benzodiazepines (Spearman's rho = 0.377, p < 0.001), and weak correlations for amphetamines (Spearman's rho = 0.078, p < 0.001) and THC (Spearman's rho = 0.100, p < 0.001). Similarly, Blandino et al. [95] reviewed the correlation between blood concentrations of the most commonly detected psychoactive substances including ethanol, amphetamines, cannabis, opioids, and cocaine, and driving performance; however, they found no unequivocal evidence demonstrating a clear dose/concentration-dependent impairment for these substances. Gjerde and Frost [96] assessed the role of alcohol and drugs in driver fatalities in Norway between 2011 and 2020. They found that while alcohol was the most commonly detected substance, its prevalence had decreased compared to the previous decade. Conversely, the prevalence of other substance groups remained unchanged.

Meanwhile, the COVID-19 pandemic brought about changes in behavior. Marrone et al. [97] conducted a comparative analysis of toxicological test results for alcohol and drug use among drivers stopped at roadside checks before (1 January 2018 to 8 March 2020) and after (9 March 2020 to 31 December 2021) the COVID-19 lockdown. Their findings revealed that 20.7% of subjects had blood alcohol levels over the legal limit, 3.9% tested positive for cocaine, and 5.4% for cannabis. During the COVID-19 period, there was a significant increase in mean blood alcohol levels, with greater alcohol consumption and cannabis use more prevalent among younger individuals, often linked with cocaine use.

Legislation & enforcement approaches

Effectiveness of policies and legislation

The implementation and impact of zero-tolerance laws on drug driving have been extensively studied in recent years. Souza et al. [98] examined the impact of Brazil's zero-tolerance drinking and driving law (Law 12,760, enacted in December 2012) on hospital admissions for road traffic injuries using data from 2008 to 2019. Their findings suggested that the law potentially prevented 440,599 hospitalizations due to land transport accidents between 2012 and 2019. Similarly, Hosseinichimeh et al. [99] analyzed factors influencing alcohol-impaired driving fatalities at the state level using a dataset of 50 states from 1985 to 2019. Their study suggested that states with stricter laws and better enforcement significantly reduce alcohol-related crash fatalities. Bui et al. [100] conducted a pilot study to measure short-term changes in alcohol consumption in Hanoi Vietnam during 15 December 2018 to 14 January 2019 and 15 December 2019 to 14 January 2020 following the implementation of harsher DUI laws. They found no immediate, significant reduction in alcohol consumption at the population level, indicating the need for long-term monitoring to assess the law's impact. Collectively, these studies underscore the importance of stringent enforcement and timely intervention in mitigating the adverse effects of impaired driving.

Several other studies have evaluated the impact of various policies and legislative measures on impaired driving. Chan and Tsai [101] used interrupted time-series analysis to study the impact of policy reforms from 2007 to 2020 on breath test refusal rates in Taiwan. The study concluded that strengthening penalties for breath test refusal, if less severe than DUI penalties, could reduce refusal rates and enhance DUI deterrence. Portillo et al. [102] employed a difference-in-differences strategy to study the impact of lowering Utah's legal BAC for driving from 0.08 to 0.05 g/dL in 2019, comparing with neighboring states as controls. Their findings indicated a temporary decrease in total accidents, mainly for property damage-only incidents, but insurance claims data suggested no corresponding fall in claims or payouts, implying under-reporting for property damage-only incidents as the cause.

Scholz et al. [103] surveyed 1024 US police and sheriff agencies in 2019 to assess alcohol-impaired driving enforcement. The study found that 68% of agencies prioritized alcohol-impaired driving enforcement, with 93% using strategies like saturation patrols. The authors noted that agencies that prioritized alcohol-impaired driving enforcement were more likely to use sobriety checkpoints and saturation patrols, conduct enforcement of open container laws and train field officers in identifying driving impairment. Additionally, O'Hara et al. [104] reviewed 21 studies on the impact of enforcement dosage and visibility on drink-driving outcomes, such as crashes and fatalities, following PRISMA guidelines. The study found that while sustained enforcement generally reduced drink driving and related harms, only two studies tested an optimal dosage, resulting in no consistent evidence for an enforcement dosage threshold due to insufficient well-designed studies.

Zamba and Rossheim [105] further examined how minimum jail sentences in state DUI laws relate to self-reported drunk driving in the US using 2020 BRFSS data. They found a marginally significant higher rate [p = 0.07] of drunk-driving episodes in states without minimum jail sentences compared to those with such sentences for first-time DUIs. In addition, Jung et al. [106] examined the effects of two 2018 acts in Korea—one increasing penalties for fatal drunk driving incidents and another lowering the blood alcohol concentration limit for driver's licenses—on DUI rates and clinical outcomes. They found that these acts were associated with reduced in-hospital mortality for patients with severe road traffic injuries.

Lastly, Jones [107] reviewed DUI and DUID enforcement in Denmark, Finland, Norway, and Sweden, suggesting that treatment and rehabilitation programs could be more beneficial than conventional penalties for offenders. Collectively, these studies underscore the critical role of robust legal frameworks in enhancing road safety.

Detection, measurement, and monitoring of impairment

Recent research has explored various methodologies and challenges associated with the detection and measurement of impairment, as well as the monitoring of impaired driving prevalence. Singh [108] highlighted the critical importance of accurate breathalyzer readings in law enforcement, emphasizing the influence of various factors on their reliability. Szala et al. [109] examined the uncertainties in measuring alcohol concentration in exhaled breath to determine sobriety, noting that results are influenced by various factors, including suspect information. They emphasized that caution is essential, as misjudging these uncertainties can lead to erroneous conclusions, and recognizing them enables accurate interpretation of sobriety test results. Cittadini et al. [110] further highlighted the challenges in accurately assessing impairment due to delays in arrival at the emergency department or in blood sampling, which often leads to the underestimation of blood alcohol concentration in road accidents.

Jones [111] highlighted the discrepancies in concentration units used for reporting blood- and breath-alcohol concentrations for legal purposes across different countries, which is essential for international comparisons. Joye et al. [112] investigated the prevalence of psychoactive substances among drivers through roadside controls with local police in Western Switzerland over two periods (P1: 2006-2008, P2: 2017-2020). The study found a 10.5% positivity rate among 1605 voluntary drivers who passed preliminary checks, highlighting the importance of systematic biofluid sampling and emphasizing the need for better prevention and deterrence measures to reduce drug-related accidents.

Poetto et al. [113] detailed the launch of a mobile drug-testing lab by Forensic Lab Service S. r.l. in 2019. During 88 road safety services in Italy, 9% of 2082 samples were tested positive in both screening and confirmatory tests, with THC (72%) and cocaine (41%) being the most common drugs detected. The study found a higher prevalence of drug-positive drivers compared to previous surveys, emphasizing the need for enhanced control measures to improve road safety. Liut et al. [114] assessed the performance and reliability of two oral fluid and two urine on-site immunochemical tests for detecting drugs of abuse in DUID cases in Germany. The study found that while these tests provided reliable detection for certain drugs, the involvement of trained police officers to identify DUID suspects through signs of recent use and impairment remains essential. Wennberg et al. [56] reviewed the effectiveness of roadside screening tests for cannabis use against lab tests for cannabinoids in blood or oral fluid. The study identified oral fluid tests as the most promising method for detecting blood THC levels exceeding legal limits. It found urine tests generally inadvisable and highlighted the need for sensitivity improvements in observational tests.

In addition to these findings, innovative approaches and technological advancements are being explored to enhance the detection and prevention of impaired driving. Adamowicz and Ziora [15] developed a method to estimate drug concentrations from bloodstains at crime scenes, like hit-and-runs, where traditional samples are unavailable. They found that it is possible to estimate drug levels from bloodstains of unknown volume, and their protocol was tested successfully against whole blood results. However, this method has significant limitations, and results should be treated as cautious estimates. Farooq et al. [115] introduced “DrunkChain,” a blockchain-based IoT system designed to prevent drunk driving-related accidents by monitoring drivers’ blood alcohol concentration (BAC) and vehicle stability, securely transmitting data to a central police account via blockchain to ensure data integrity and mitigate tampering risks. Freni et al. [116] analyzed EtG levels in hair to assess alcohol consumption among individuals charged with DUI in the Italian Province of Pavia from 2015 to 2019. The study found that incorporating EtG hair tests in the driving license reissuing process helped reduce alcohol misuse, with drinking behavior influenced by age, sex, and district. Collectively, these studies underscore the necessity for reliable detection and monitoring systems to effectively manage impaired driving.

Back-calculation and hip-flask defense

Saskőy et al. [117] reviewed and proposed updates to the UKIAFT guidelines on retrograde extrapolations. The study addressed the need for scientific consensus on back calculations in the UK, highlighting current guidelines and uncertainties, and recommended using a 99.7% coverage for estimating alcohol pharmacokinetics, adopting a 2-h time limit, adjusting the slowest elimination rate, adopting the OSAC draft guidelines value, and reporting back calculation results as a range.

Workplace and court-ordered drug testing

To maintain a safe and productive work environment, addressing drug abuse has become crucial, and workplace drug testing plays a vital role in this effort. Implementing these tests not only helps to prevent incidents related to drug abuse but also promotes a culture of accountability within the workplace. Furthermore, court-ordered drug testing gives individuals a chance to fulfil their legal obligations and adopt a healthier, drug-free lifestyle. However, workplace drug testing faces several challenges, including urine validity, drug detection, and the interpretation of drug test results. The following sections will explore these key issues in more detail.

Samples validity of different matrices

Urine and hair are the most used samples for drug testing in workplaces and court-ordered drug testing, although other biological fluids can also be used. Positive test results often carry serious legal and economic consequences. As a result, various strategies for sample manipulation and adulteration are employed to evade a positive result.

Urine

Typical strategies for manipulating and adulterating urine samples include dilution, substitution, and the addition of adulterants. To address these issues, new methods have been developed to improve the detection of established urine validity markers and to screen for new adulteration markers. Despite recent advancements in detecting manipulation, it continues to pose a challenge in both clinical and forensic toxicology. Therefore, there is an ongoing need for simple, reliable, specific, and objective techniques, particularly for identifying synthetic urine.

Wissenbach et al. [118] reviewed the current strategies for manipulating urine and their detecting adulterated urine samples, as well as recent research advancements and their applications. The author remarked that the risk of urine adulteration, whether through dilution, chemical alteration, or substitution, can be mitigated through supervised urine collection, temperature control, and visual inspections. Additionally, specimen validity, such as measuring creatinine levels and checking for oxidative substances, are essential in confirming sample validity. The study suggests that screening for multiple endogenous biomolecules yields a more reliable method for detecting synthetic urine than relying on a single endogenous biomarker, which could be easily added to synthetic urine specimens.

Vikingsson et al. [119] conducted market research identifying three types of adulterants and 32 synthetic urine products. Their study revealed that the adulterants included iodate or nitrite, both of which are easily detected through specimen validity testing. Iodate-containing adulterant resulted in negative and invalid results in immunoassays, whereas nitrite-containing adulterant did not affect immunoassay results but reduced analyte concentrations during confirmatory testing. Additionally, the authors suggested that alternative specimen validity testing methods could detect the presence of uric acid, caffeine, and magnesium in most synthetic urine samples.

Musile et al. [120] developed and validated a new three-dimensional origami paper-based device that utilizes traditional colorimetric reagents, such as picric acid and 3,5-dinitrobenzoic acid, for on-site colorimetric determination of urine creatinine, serving as a test for sample integrity. This device, with a detection limit of 0.02 g/L, was employed to qualitatively screen for urine dilution in 48 samples. The colorimetric results can be quickly recorded and analyzed using smartphone cameras and related software. This method offers a rapid and low-cost analytical solution suitable for the on-site assessment of urine tampering.

Franke et al. [121] characterized synthetic urine products in the Austrian and German markets using enzymatic detection of uric acid, the Axiom Test, and LC-MS-MS analysis. The findings indicated that LC-MS-MS analysis is the most reliable method for detecting synthetic urine. The researchers also suggested that combining endogenous biomolecules with synthetic urine markers would enhance detection accuracy.

Hair

Wissenbach et al. [122] conducted a comprehensive review of recent advancements in the field of hair sample manipulation testing. Similar to urine testing, strategies for manipulating hair tests commonly aim to reduce drug concentrations to values below cut-off levels, employing methods such as forced washout effects or adulteration. However, distinguishing between routine cosmetic hair treatments and deliberate efforts to evade positive drug tests remains a significant challenge in clinical and forensic toxicology. Therefore, the accurate identification of cosmetic hair treatments is crucial for proper interpretation of hair analysis results. The review also discussed novel techniques and specific biomarkers that aid detecting adulteration or cosmetic treatments in hair samples.

Ramiez Fernandez et al. [123] developed a UPLC-MS-MS method for the analysis of doxylamine and its metabolite, doxylamine-N-oxide, in hair samples. The study examined the incorporation of these substances into the hair of both regular and occasional doxylamine users. Additionally, it assessed the effects of permanent oxidative dyeing on the concentrations of the target analytes in hair.

Mestria et al. [124] examined various online cleansing treatments available, which utilize common chemicals and cosmetic products. It is claimed that such treatments can effectively reduce drug concentrations in hair samples. The study found that these treatments were successful in lowering analyte concentrations. Additionally, hair treated with these methods showed no visible signs of damage or color loss after washing, making it challenging to distinguish between treated and untreated hair. This could potentially lead to false negative results when applying cut-off levels.

Shima et al. [125] investigated the impact of different hair dyeing processes on the distribution patterns of zolpidem and methoxyphenamine in hair. The study analyzed drug distributions under three dyeing conditions using 2-mm segmental analysis with LC-MS/MS. The results showed that semi-permanent coloring, which does not involve bleaching, has minimal effect on the distribution patterns of these drugs. In contrast, permanent coloring that includes bleaching significantly alters the distribution shape, with changes varying based on the number of treatments and subsequent exposure to water, such as from daily hair washing.

Bertges et al. [126] investigated the potential to manipulate tetrahydrocannabinol hair analysis results using various commercially available products, including those sold online, which claim to reduce drug concentrations in hair. The study revealed that visual inspections could not detect changes caused by these treatments. Moreover, the products successfully reduced THC concentrations in hair to non-detectable levels. As a result, the researchers recommend complementing hair sample analyses with urine tests and advise caution when interpreting quantitative THC concentrations in hair.

Methodology and instrumentation

Urine

Dos Santos et al. [127] reported a dilute-and-shoot method for the quantitative analysis of 95 substances, including drugs of abuse and their metabolites, in urine samples using LC-MS-MS. This method was applied in cases of suspected intoxication, both in vivo and postmortem, to verify toxicological findings within the Brazilian population.

Wong et al. [128] developed a qualitative screening method for detecting abused drugs in urine using LC-MS-MS. This innovative method employs a dual-mode extraction column to effectively clean up the urine matrix. It covers a wide range of abused drugs, including methamphetamine, methylenedioxymethamphetamine, and ketamine. This method is utilized in forensic toxicology and the analysis of drugs involved in sexual assault and other criminal activities.

Diekhans et al. [129] presented a comprehensive LC-MS-MS method for identifying over 250 novel synthetic opioids and xylazine in the urine samples. This method includes a protein precipitation step followed by size-exclusion filtration. To demonstrate its applicability, the researchers retroactively analyzed over 600 cases of fatal accidental overdoses in United States, revealing that nearly 10% of these cases involved a new novel synthetic opioid.

Schackmuth and Kerigan [130] developed a method using LC-MS/MS to identify fentanyl and its related substances in urine. This method was used to quantify authentic urine samples and screen for additional biomarkers associated with fentanyl use. The analysis successfully identified fentanyl, five analogs, and two metabolites in human urine samples. In addition to quantitative analyses, an LC-QTOF-MS assay was utilized to detect other substances and potential biomarkers for fentanyl.

Sørensen et al. [131] validated an LC-MS-MS method that enhances sensitivity through anion exchange clean-up of extracts, coupled with anion-supported reverse-phase chromatography, for determining GHB at endogenous concentration levels in urine and oral fluid. Additionally, this study examined the stability of GHB under various storage conditions and measured endogenous GHB concentrations in samples collected under controlled circumstances.

Sim et al. [132] developed an automated online micro-solid-phase extraction system combined with LC-MS/MS to detect metabolites of CBD, Δ8-THC, and Δ9-THC in urine samples. The study targeted specific metabolites including 7-carboxycannabidiol, 11-nor-9-carboxy-Δ8-tetrahydrocannabinol, Δ9-THC-COOH, and 11-nor-9-carboxy-Δ9-tetrahydrocannabinol-glucuronide. The study began by ensuring a clear separation between the isomers 11-nor-9-carboxy-Δ8-tetrahydrocannabinol and Δ9-THC-COOH through optimized LC-MS-MS conditions. Subsequently, an automated online micro-solid-phase extraction system was employed for routine processing.

Cifuentes Girard et al. [133] investigated the use of differential mobility spectrometry, in conjunction with LC-MS to analyze drugs of abuse in urine. The study targeted a range of substances, including cocaine, ecgonine methyl ester, cocaethylene, benzoylecgonine, norcocaine, tramadol, O-desmethyl-cis-tramadol, N-desmethyl-cis-tramadol, Δ9-THC, Δ9-CBD, 11-OH-THC, Δ9-THC-COOH, and its glucuronide. The study aimed to assess the potential of differential ion mobility as an additional separation dimension to reduce the analysis time of liquid chromatography while maintaining good quantitative performance for the detection of drugs of abuse.

Patton et al. [134] developed an LC-MS-MS method to detect 11 cannabinoid metabolites extracted from urine. This technique was applied to analyze randomly collected urine specimens from military workplaces that were screened positive with cannabinoid immunoassay. The study identified Δ8-THC and Δ9-THC as the most frequently observed analytes. Additionally, Δ10-tetrahydrocannabinol (Δ10-THC) was found in 77 specimens. Cross-reactivity studies for Δ10-THC using the immunoassay demonstrated cross-reactivity comparable to the cut-off for Δ9-THC, providing further confidence in the reported prevalence and detection patterns.

Nie et al. [37] developed a screening method for detecting common NPS and the nonmedical use of prescription drugs. This method employs HPLC combined with Orbitrap MS to analyze blood, urine, and hair samples. It is particularly effective for identifying drugs like fentanyl and synthetic cannabinoids, which produce various variants. The method also includes procedures for sample pretreatment, the establishment of a database, and identification criteria, providing valuable technical support for drug abuse management.

Hair

Hehet et al. [135] developed an LC–MS3 method for detecting trace amounts of Δ9-THC-COOH in hair samples. This method also enabled the detection of derivatized 11-OH-THC, serving as an additional marker for cannabis use. The preparation of hair samples involved digesting the hair matrix under alkaline conditions, followed by an optimized liquid-liquid extraction procedure. The authors noted that liquid-liquid extraction is more effective than solid-phase extraction due to its time-saving steps and more satisfactory results. The introduction of MS3 in this method improved analytical detection by improving sensitivity and selectivity.

Wen et al. [136] developed a UHPLC coupled with HRMS method for the qualitative and quantitative analysis of seven barbiturates in hair samples. This method employed two distinct UHPLC conditions to identify barbiturates and the structural isomers amobarbital and pentobarbital. The method was subsequently applied to a real case involving drug-facilitated sexual assault, where hair samples were collected three months after the assault, and the proximal segments were analyzed. The method successfully measured the concentration of barbiturates in human hair following a single-dose exposure.

Wei et al. [137] reported the development of hair reference materials suitable for quantifying various fentanyl analogs, including norfentanyl, acetylfentanyl, para-fluorofentanyl, isobutyryl fentanyl, fentanyl, thiofentanyl, 4-fluoroisobutyryl fentanyl, ocfentanil, and tetrahydrofuran fentanyl. These reference materials were created by soaking hair samples in a solution of water and dimethyl sulfoxide containing the target analytes. The study established an analytical method utilizing both GC-MS-MS and LC-MS-MS techniques for measuring trace amounts of fentanyl in human hair. The GC-MS-MS method eliminates the need for derivatization, allowing for direct quantitative analysis. In contrast, the LC-MS-MS method is highly sensitive and features a low limit of quantification. These methods can be used for the certification of fentanyl certified reference substances.

Xie et al. [138] developed a quantitative method utilizing UHPLC combined with tandem mass spectrometry for targeted screening of etomidate and its metabolite, etomidate acid, in hair samples. They analyzed 105 hair samples collected by police from individuals suspected of drug abuse. The findings indicated that etomidate was the primary substance identified in the hair samples, rather than its metabolite. Furthermore, etomidate abuse was particularly prevalent among males aged 21 to 35.

Oral fluid

Bergström et al. [139] developed a high-throughput LC HRMS method for analyzing drugs of abuse in oral fluid. This method covers a panel of 71 substances, including traditional drugs of abuse, prescription narcotics, and new psychoactive substances. It has been implemented in over 18,000 clinical routine oral fluid samples collected from addiction clinics in West Sweden. Compared to the standard practice of supervised urine sampling, which can be uncomfortable for patients, oral fluid collection is considered a more patient-friendly method.

Zheng et al. [140] developed an LC-MS method for detecting 58 drugs of abuse in oral fluid samples. The method demonstrated versatility by utilizing two different oral fluid sampling kits from Greiner Bio-One and Immunalysis.

Cabarcos-Fernández et al. [141] developed a method for analyzing oral fluid that can simultaneously detect several alkaloids found in coca leaves, as well as a cocaine metabolite and cocaine itself. This process involves two methods of sample collection, followed by solid-phase extraction to concentrate the samples, and detection using GC-MS. In some South American countries, consuming coca leaves is a traditional practice, which can result in positive tests for cocaine use in biological sample analyses. This method could be effectively applied in workplace testing and programs aimed at identifying driving under the influence in Latin American countries, as it can distinguish between legal and illegal consumption by identifying specific markers.

Suraev et al. [142] evaluated the performance of two commonly used point-of-collection testing devices for detecting Δ9-THC in patients’ oral fluid at various time intervals following a single oral dose of oil containing Δ9-THC and CBD. The effectiveness of these devices was compared to LC-MS-MS.

Other matrices

The use of alternative matrices is an actively developing area in forensic toxicology. Samples other than blood and urine can provide additional information about drug exposure and offer analytical advantages. de Campos et al. [143] reviewed recent literature on the application of alternative matrices, including sweat, meconium, breast milk, and vitreous humour. The review compares the characteristics, advantages (such as sample collection, extraction, and detection windows), and limitations (including limited drug incorporation, challenges in correlating drug concentrations to effects, and the need for more sensitive analyses) of these alternative matrices. Their applications in studying both traditional drugs of abuse and NPS are also discussed. Chen et al. [144] investigated the use of exhaled breath aerosols as an alternative method for monitoring drug abuse. Due to the limited availability of positive samples of exhaled breath aerosols, the study developed and validated a simulated device that generates aerosols similar to human exhaled breath. The performance of this device was assessed by comparing the results obtained from UHPLC paired with triple quadrupole tandem mass spectrometry, using caffeine as the target drug. The effectiveness of the device was further tested with several other commonly abused drugs.

Drug level interpretation

Urine

Immunoassays are commonly utilized for rapid urine drug screening in workplace drug testing. However, these screenings can sometimes yield false-positive results and typically require confirmation through MS. In this context, Pope et al. [145] examined over 12,000 urine samples to investigate cases where drugs other than amphetamines might have caused false positives in amphetamine immunoassay screenings. The study identified various drugs and common medications that exhibit cross-reactivity in immunoassay tests, leading to these false-positive results. Sholler et al. [146] conducted a study to characterize the urinary pharmacokinetics profiles of various cannabinoids, including Δ9-THC-COOH, Δ9-THC, Δ8-THC, 11-hydroxy-Δ9-tetrahydrocannabinol (11-OH-THC), Δ9-tetrahydrocannabivarin (THCV), 11-nor-Δ9-tetrahydrocannabivarin-9-carboxylic acid (THCV-COOH), CBD, CBN, and 8,11-dihydroxytetrahydrocannabinol (8,11-diOH-Δ9-THC). These cannabinoids were administered in both oral and vaporized forms. The study provided detailed information on urinary pharmacokinetics, including maximum concentrations and the time taken to reach those maximum concentrations. Sholler et al. [147] conducted a study to evaluate the urinary pharmacokinetics of CBD, Δ9-THC-COOH, and their metabolites. This was done after administering a single dose of either vaporized or orally ingested cannabidiol products (across three formulations) or vaporized cannabidiol-dominant cannabis with a very low amount of Δ9-THC. The study aimed to fill knowledge gaps regarding how acute administration of CBD affects the outcomes of urine cannabis drug testing. Deville et al. [148] conducted a review of urinalysis results for cannabinoids from their toxicology laboratory, highlighting the challenges in interpreting these data. They analyzed over 1000 urine specimens submitted for cannabinoid testing and found that only 15% tested positive for CBD. Most of the samples tested positive for CBD were also associated with high concentrations of Δ9-THC-COOH, indicating that there were only a few CBD consumers in their population, with the majority primarily using Δ9-THC.

CBD can potentially convert into Δ9-THC in acidic environments, raising concerns about this conversion occurring when exposed to gastric fluid after consumption. Research suggests that this conversion requires a surfactant due to the limited water solubility of Δ9-THC. Water-compatible nanoemulsions of CBD are already available on the market. Hart et al. [149] conducted a toxicology study using synthetic gastric fluid to investigate whether water-soluble CBD converts into Δ9-THC. Their findings suggest that typical consumption of water-soluble CBD products is unlikely to result in positive urine drug tests for Δ9-THC.

Reisfield et al. [150] conducted a study on urinary opiate profiles resulting from the daily consumption of food products containing presumed codeine-predominant poppy seeds, commonly bought from supermarkets. Ten participants consumed these food products for breakfast over 10 consecutive days. The results showed no observable trend in increasing urinary opiate concentrations (including codeine, morphine, hydrocodone and hydromorphone) among the participants, as they recorded opiate-negative results at all time points during the study. Broussard et al. [151] indicated that consuming pastries containing poppy seeds, such as bagels, muffins, cakes, and kolaches, can lead to codeine-positive results on urine drug tests for at least 24 h after consumption. Therefore, it is advised that individuals refrain from consuming any poppy seed products for several days, especially before undergoing pain management testing.

Wilson et al. [152] reviewed current guidelines regarding urinary codeine concentrations. They noted that codeine levels exceeding the cut-off value, along with a morphine-to-codeine ratio of less than 2, are typically indicative of codeine use and can help rule out poppy seed ingestion. However, recent studies have questioned these guidelines by showing that high codeine concentrations and low morphine-to-codeine ratios can occur in various food products as well. To investigate this further, the researchers recruited anonymous volunteers to consume different food items containing poppy seeds. The findings suggest that the established guidelines for interpreting urine codeine concentrations may no longer be valid for ruling out poppy seed ingestion. Therefore, clinicians are recommended to interpret these results with caution. Additionally, patients are advised to avoid poppy seed products for a few days prior to testing.

Zamparripa et al. [153] conducted a study on the pharmacokinetics of various commercially available hemp-derived topical products high in CBD and low in Δ9-THC, including creams, lotions, balms, gels, and patches. All the CBD products contained ≤0.3% Δ9-THC, making them federally legal in the United States. The pharmacokinetic outcomes, including blood, urine, and oral fluid samples, were assessed using LC-MS-MS to quantify the concentrations of CBD, Δ9-THC, and their primary metabolites in each biological matrix. The study found that no urine samples tested positive for cannabis based on current U.S. federal workplace drug testing criteria. However, nine participants showed positive for Δ9-THC in oral fluid. This study provides initial data on the toxicological effects of hemp-derived topical CBD products available on the market.

Morphine and 6-acetylmorphine have been key markers for confirming heroin use. However, the heroin supply in the USA has evolved, and it is now often mixed with illicit fentanyl. In light of this change, Vikingsson et al. [154] suggested that fentanyl could serve as an alternative marker for illegal opioid use. Their study analyzed 504 morphine-positive urine samples collected from workplace drug testing, quantifying various substances, including opiates, 6-diacetylmorphine, fentanyl, and fentanyl analogs. They found that fentanyl was more prevalent than 6-acetylmorphine in these samples with para-fluoro fentanyl being the only fentanyl analog present. These findings underline fentanyl's potential as a significant marker for identifying illegal drug use.

Hair

Ji et al. [155] developed an LC-MS-MS method to identify and quantify 88 drugs, including withdrawal medications and new types of drugs, in hair samples from over 1000 drug addicts undergoing rehabilitation in China. The study found traditional illicit drugs like opioids and methamphetamine to be predominant, alongside sedative-hypnotic and psychotherapeutic drugs such as tramadol and dextromethorphan. These findings highlight potential mental health issues, including insomnia, depression and psychosis, likely as a result of the long-term effects of drug use and withdrawal. The presence of methadone, tramadol, and dextromethorphan in some samples indicates a shift toward alternative drug use during the COVID-19 pandemic when access to traditional drugs was limited.

Madry et al. [156] investigated the presence of minor metabolites such as norcocaine and cocaethylene in seized cocaine and hair samples to prevent external contamination. Their study highlighted the need for testing these metabolites to avoid false interpretations of cocaine-positive hair results, as cocaine metabolites can also appear as degradation products in street cocaine. Using an established decision model to assess metabolic ratios, they effectively differentiated between actual cocaine use and external contamination, enhancing the accuracy of hair test results.

Ramirez Fernández et al. [157] investigated the impact of varying degrees of passive cocaine exposure on hair analysis results. Using UPLC-MS-MS method, they analyzed hair samples for multiple cocaine metabolites. The study involved professionals exposed to cocaine powder in diverse environments, from staff handling small amounts in labs to individuals dealing with large quantities of seized cocaine dismantling extraction facilities. The author also evaluated current guidelines and literature on this topic, underscoring the need for accurate interpretation of hair analysis results in the context of passive exposure.

Larabi et al. [158] examined timeline for ketamine to become undetectable in hair following short-term treatment for major depressive disorder. Analyzing hair samples using LC-MS-MS, they estimated the elimination half-life of ketamine in hair to be 0.88 months, suggesting that undetectable concentrations approximately 7 months post-treatment. The study also reviewed existing literature, noting that the time frame for negative hair results after discontinuation varies by substance, with examples including 3 months for heroin, 3-4 months for cocaine and tramadol, 6-7 months for amphetamines and Δ9-THC-COOH. This research provides valuable insights into interpreting ketamine concentration levels in hair.

Infant exposure to drug abuse is a significant global issue. To address this, Clara et al. [159] assessed drug exposure in infants under one year using hair analysis to detect substances such as cocaine, cannabinoids, opiates, methadone, amphetamines and ketamine. The authors emphasized the need for comprehensive assessments, considering both clinical symptoms and family history, to accurately identify and interpret drug exposure. Additionally, Cestonaro et al. [160] conducted a retrospective study on suspected prenatal and infant drug exposure, analyzing toxicological results of children's hair and comparing them with their mothers to determine exposure routes, which included intrauterine transmission, breastfeeding, accidental intake, passive inhalation, and intentional administration. Both studies underscore the complexities of interpreting hair analysis results in infants.

Vaiano et al. [161] conducted an observational study on analyzing Δ9-THC and Δ9-THC-COOH concentrations in 126 hair samples, focusing on the influences of age, gender, consumption habits, and hair characteristics. The findings indicated a decrease in cannabinoid levels with age, notably lower concentrations in individuals over 40. Long-term cannabis use (over 10 years) correlated with higher cannabinoid levels in hair. Furthermore, bleached hair exhibited significantly lower levels of Δ9-THC and Δ9-THC-COOH.

Xu et al. [162] developed a segmental hair analysis method utilizing UHPLC-MS-MS to detect and quantify five synthetic cannabinoids, including 5F-MDMB-PICA, 5F-ADB, 4F-MDMB-BICA, MDMB-4en-PINACA, and 4F-MDMB-BUTINACA, in 1 cm hair segments from eight cases of synthetic cannabinoids abuse. All cases showed the presence of synthetic cannabinoids, with concentrations ranging from 1.5 to 632.9 pg/mg.

Yang et al. [163] developed an LC-MS-MS method to study synthetic cannabinoids abuse in East China, focusing on 65 previously identified substances. Over 600 hair samples from forensic cases were qualitatively analyzed, with 80 positive samples further examined quantitatively. The researchers identified ADB-BUTINACA and MDMB-4-en-PINACA as the most frequently detected substances, and explored the demographics of those primarily involved in synthetic cannabinoids abuse.

Niebel et al. [164] determined the prevalence of conventional and NPS in families with known or suspected use of drugs. Hair samples from parents and children were tested for methadone, heroin, cocaine, amphetamines, ecstasy, cannabinoids, benzodiazepines, therapeutic opioids, alcohol markers, and NPS, including 33 cathinones, 10 phenylethylamines, 5 piperazines, 2 tryptamines, 9 designer benzodiazepines, 4 synthetic opioids, and 4 ketamine analogs. The authors also examined the relationships between family members’ drug use and the detected substances.

Carelli et al. [165] reviewed literature on analytical methods for synthetic opioids and NPS in hair. They summarized frequently analyzed compounds, their concentrations, metabolites, and proposed future research areas in hair analysis for clinical and forensic applications. The review highlighted that methanol, typically diluted with acid, is commonly used for extracting these substances. To enhance sensitivity, extraction is often followed by purification steps, such as solid-phase or liquid-liquid extraction. Chromatographic techniques with mass spectrometry, particularly triple quadrupole systems, are most effective for keratin matrix in hair. Among synthetic compounds, hydromorphone, hydrocodone, tramadol, and fentanyl derivatives like furanyl fentanyl, sufentanil, and butyryl fentanyl are frequently detected.

Blood

Bergeria et al. [166] investigated the pharmacokinetics of Δ9-THC and its metabolites in the blood following vaporized and oral CBD product use. Using immunoassay screening and LC-MS-MS confirmatory tests, the authors found THC in the blood of participants using vaporized CBD-dominant cannabis, but not in those using vaporized or oral CBD-only products. The study demonstrated significant variability in CBD pharmacokinetics and suggested that CBD alone is unlikely to convert into Δ9-THC or result in positive THC drug tests.

Prevalence of illicit drugs use in workplace

The use of illicit drugs in the workplace remains a persistent concern. Hence, various countries around the world have implemented drug testing protocols in professional settings. Feltmann et al. [167] examined the prevalence of positive drug tests among adult employees in Sweden over a 25-year period (1994−2019). Since 2007, the rate of positive sample has increased for cannabis and decreased for benzodiazepines. The positive rate of opiates tests remained stable, but rates for amphetamine and cocaine rose between 2013 and 2019.

Calvetti et al. [168] analyzed 847 hair samples for driving relicensing and drug withdrawal monitoring to study the prevalence of NPS. Results showed 56 positive samples for ketamine, 35 for norketamine, 6 for fentanyl, 3 each for norfentanyl, 4-ANPP, MDMB-4en-PINACA, 2 each for N,N-DMT and 5-chloro AB-PINACA, 1 each for α-PHP and methcathinone.

Vikingsson et al. [154] quantified the level of opiates, 6-acetylmorphine, fentanyl and fentanyl analogs in 504 morphine-positive urine specimens from workplace drug testing using LC-MS-MS. They found that almost half (43%) of specimens had morphine concentrations below 4000 ng/mL, indicating the need for additional markers to differentiate illicit drugs use. Fentanyl was co-detected in 22% of specimens, compared to 12% for 6-acetylmorphine. The results highlight the prevalence of fentanyl use among employees and underscore the importance of fentanyl and norfentanyl as markers of illicit drug use.

The prevalence of CBD, Δ9-and Δ8-THC and metabolites in workplace drug testing urine specimens were also investigated by Vikingsson et al. [169,170]. Specimens from workplace drug testing estimate the prevalence and concentrations of 11 cannabinoids and cannabinoid metabolites in regulated and non-regulated workplace urine testing specimens. Using a 1 ng/mL cutoff, 8.3% of non-regulated specimens and 5.0% of regulated specimens were positive for Δ9-THC and/or metabolites. Of those specimens, 99.3% were positive for Δ9-THC-COOH, and 64% had concentrations below 15 ng/mL. Furthermore, Δ8-THC-COOH was identified in 76 (1.3%) specimens. The selectivity and sensitivity of the Δ9-THC-COOH immunoassay was also evaluated. Although 93% agreement was observed between confirmatory LC–MS-MS (15 ng/mL cutoff) and immunoassay (50 ng/mL cutoff), a false-negative specimen (66 ng/mL Δ9-THC-COOH) was identified.

Drug abuse during the period of pregnancy is a serious health risk for both mothers and newborn infants. Clara et al. [159] performed an observational retrospective study analyzing hair samples from infants under 1 year of age between 2018 and 2022. Among 102 samples, 44.7% from infants with clinically suspicious symptoms and 67.2% other cases (e.g., maternal drug history, at-risk environment, and suspected maltreatment) tested positive for drugs of abuse. The substance most commonly detected were cocaine, followed by opiates. Similarly, Bertaso et al. [171] assessed in utero drug exposure through hair testing in 51 suspected cases. Drug presence was confirmed in 92% of cases, with over 50% showing multiple substance classes. The most frequently detected substances were cocaine, followed by opiates, methadone and cannabinoids.

Palamar et al. [172] reported five cases of BZO-4en-POXIZID exposure among nightclub attendees in New York City, detected through hair testing as part of an epidemiological study monitoring drugs use including NPS. The study found unreported exposure to BZO-4en-POXIZID, likely as an adulterant in other drugs. Weaver et al. [173] identified work-related exacerbation of preexisting asthma in a cannabis cultivation workplace. An employee experienced worsening work-associated respiratory symptom, culminating in a fatal asthma attack. The report, based on an occupational safety and health administration inspection, recognizes cannabis production work as a potential causative factor.

International guideline and policy

During their 2022 board meeting in Malaga, Society of Hair Testing (SoHT) launched a consensus on general recommendations for hair testing [174]. The consensus addresses various aspects of hair tests, including sampling, sample continuity, storage, segmentation, washing, pre-analytical steps and analytical techniques, quality control and considerations for interpretation. In December 2023, the US Health and Human Services Department published Mandatory Guidelines [175] for Federal Workplace Testing Programs. These guidelines establish an annual process for publishing the authorized drug testing panel, revise the definition of a substituted specimen, clarify oral fluid collection procedures, and update the confirmatory test cutoff for morphine.

In January 2023, U.S. Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC) launched a report [176] on a comprehensive benchmarking analysis focusing on policy analysis and benchmarking metrics comparison among different countries and regions. The report covers international and national policies relevant to drug and alcohol testing in the nuclear industry, including national drug and alcohol policy, workplace drug and alcohol testing, fitness for duty regulations for nuclear power plants. The analysis addresses the use of various testing methods (blood, breath, urine, hair, and oral fluid), drug and metabolite cutoff values, point-of-collection testing devices, roadside testing methodologies, innovations in drug testing, and the availability of data. This information provides the NRC with international comparative insights on the technical basis for such programs and methods, emphasizing health and safety for both worker and public. The Division of Workplace Programs of Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration U.S. published the Guidance [177] for Using the 2023 Federal Custody and Control Form. The guidance details requirements for the chain of custody (CoC) form and the tamper-evident labels/seals on the specimen containers.

Workplace and court order testing approach

De Jong et al. [178] studied oral fluid and exhaled breath as alternatives for heroin and methadone monitoring. Samples were screened for opiates, methadone and other drugs using immunoassay and LC-MS-MS. The findings indicated that these methods are suitable alternatives for heroin and methadone monitoring. De Jong et al. [179] further conducted a prospective pilot study to evaluate the use of an impaction-based breath sampling device, Breath Explor®, as an alternative to traditional urine-based drug monitoring. Breath samples were analyzed using a validated 32-component LC-MS-MS method. This innovative approach offers advantages, such as non-invasiveness, reduced risk of adulteration, and the ability to perform repeated automated sampling and confirmation testing. These findings suggest that breath-based substance monitoring could complement or replace traditional urine-based methods in clinical practice.

Pollard et al. [180] designed a non-invasive latent fingerprint (LFP) drugs screening tool with rapid result turnaround for the MDMA detection using surface plasmon resonance (SPR) and lateral flow immunoassay (LFA) technology. A high-affinity antibody binding pair was identified, making the pair suitable for LFA. The resulting LFP screening tool is sensitive and requires no sample preparation.

Drug facilitated crime (DFC)

Analytes and matrices of concern

Drug-facilitated crime (DFC) encompassed a range of offenses, with drug facilitated sexual assault (DFSA) being a particularly critical concern. This issue primarily affected vulnerable populations, such as minors and individuals in nightlife environments. Between 2022 and 2024, substantial advancements were made in research and forensic methodologies, illuminating the complexities surrounding DFC, especially DFSA, and highlighting the need for precise definitions and effective detection strategies. This chapter explored the analytes and matrices relevant to DFC incidents, focusing on contemporary studies that underscore the necessity for enhanced forensic techniques, victim support systems, and informed legal frameworks.

The impact of DFSA remained pronounced among at-risk groups. A notable contribution to this discourse was the study by Rial et al. [181], which addressed the terminological ambiguities complicating the understanding of “chemical submission” and “chemical vulnerability”. This highlighted the critical need for clear definitions to improve research and legal contexts.

Substances commonly involved in DFSA

Recent investigations conducted comprehensive reviews of substances involved in DFSA. AlOtaibi et al. [182] performed a systematic review that examined the use of volatile substances, particularly chloroform and aromatic hydrocarbons, in DFSA. They found that chloroform was the primary substance in several cases, often accessed by offenders with chemistry backgrounds. The review highlighted the need for stricter regulations on these substances and called for further research due to the underreported nature of such crimes. Complementarily, Volonnino et al. [183] analyzed the increasing role of Z-drugs in DFSA cases, indicating significant challenges in detection and calling for advancements in forensic toxicology. Their findings revealed that the incapacitating effects of Z-drugs rendered victims particularly vulnerable.

Analytical challenges

DFSA presented significant analytical challenges in forensic science, particularly with the emergence of novel psychoactive substances like synthetic cathinones. Drevin et al. [184] reported a case on N-ethyl-pentedrone (NEPD) by LC-HRMS. The literature review found 16 DFSA cases linked to synthetic cathinones, with methylone being the most frequently reported due to its entactogenic effects. The authors emphasized that while synthetic cathinones are commonly associated with chemsex, their use in DFSA cases remains rare, and NEPD's pharmacological effects are not well understood.

Forensic methodologies and detection techniques

Innovative detection methods proved essential for addressing these issues. Teoh et al. [185] explored vortex-assisted dispersive liquid-liquid microextraction-gas chromatography (VADLLME-GC) and demonstrated its effectiveness in identifying residual drugs even after beverages have dried, which is particularly important for forensic analysis in DFSA cases. Additionally, advances in multi-class drug analysis using LC-MS/MS allow for the simultaneous detection of various substances, enhancing forensic capabilities [36].

Challenges in current detection practices

Evaluations of commercially available drug detection coasters by Germain et al. [186] revealed their inadequacy in reliably screening beverages for GHB and ketamine, substances frequently associated with DFSA. This underscored the need for more effective detection methods in both public and private settings. Furthermore, research advocating for systematic toxicological analyses, such as a study conducted in Paris by Liautard et al. [187], calls for improved data sharing among law enforcement to effectively address these incidents.

Victim support and mental health considerations

The paper by Van Dijck and Van Assche [188] highlighted that victim support and mental health considerations are critical in addressing the aftermath of DFSA. The psychological impact on survivors has led to long-term issues such as PTSD, anxiety, and depression, making access to mental health resources essential. Integrating toxicological analyses into forensic investigations not only helps establish evidence but also validates victims' experiences, empowering them in their recovery. A comprehensive approach that combines forensic support with mental health care remains vital for improving outcomes for DFSA victims.

Recent research findings

Poyatos et al. [189] investigated the acute effects of methylone compared to MDMA, revealing that both substances induced significant cardiovascular and pleasurable effects, including euphoria and increased empathy. Notably, methylone exhibited a faster onset and shorter duration of effects than MDMA, suggesting a comparable abuse potential. These findings contributed to the understanding of the pharmacological profiles of these substances and their implications for human health. The study of zolpidem metabolism by Yamaguchi et al. [190] revealed novel metabolites that could enhance forensic investigations related to DFSA. Retrospective analyses of sexual assault cases in Santiago de Compostela by Cabarcos-Fernández et al. [191] revealed significant patterns, particularly the correlation between alcohol consumption and victimization, emphasizing the need for strategic interventions. Moreover, a rapid method for the detection and quantification of GHB in hair using UPLC-MS/MS was developed by Blanco-Ces et al. [192]. This method allowed analysis of small hair samples (10 mg) and yielded results in approximately 1.5 h, enhancing efficiency for forensic applications. The validation process demonstrated precision, selectivity, and sensitivity, which are critical for distinguishing between endogenous and exogenous GHB levels in forensic investigations. Dos Santos et al. [127] developed and validated a dilute-and-shoot LC–MS-MS method for analyzing 95 illicit drugs and medicines in urine samples. Optimized through multivariate studies, this method achieved a total chromatographic run time of 7.5 min, making it a rapid and efficient screening tool for clinical and forensic cases. Following ANSI/ASB Standard 036 guidelines, the method achieved a high detection rate of 90.9% for at least one substance in analyzed samples, showcasing its applicability in toxicological investigations. Lastly, a case series by Arndt et al. [193] examined acetone findings in suspected DFSA cases from Harris County, Texas. The study found that acetone was present more frequently in DFSA cases compared to other human performance cases, suggesting its potential as a biomarker for acute stress experienced during such assaults. These findings indicated a need for further research to understand the physiological impact of acetone and its relevance as a trauma marker in forensic toxicology.

Emerging trends and future directions in DFSA detection and forensic science

The rise of designer benzodiazepines added further challenges for detection and forensic analysis, as these substances could be difficult to identify with standard drug tests [194]. The authors discussed the pharmacological characteristics of these drugs, their detection challenges, and implications for forensic science and public health. The study underscored the urgent need for improved analytical techniques to identify designer benzodiazepines in biological samples, enhancing the ability to address and prosecute DFSA cases effectively. Recent reviews emphasized the complexity of DFSA and its significant impact on victims. For example, Fields et al. [195] discussed the implications of impaired trauma memory in victims of DFSA, suggesting that mental health support must be integrated into responses to these cases to improve recovery outcomes. Edwards et al. [196] presented a case report detailing the use of tetrahydrozoline in a DFSA, highlighting the diverse substances that can facilitate such assaults and emphasizing the importance of comprehensive awareness among medical and legal professionals. Prego-Meleiro et al. [197] highlighted the unique vulnerabilities of young individuals in nightlife contexts regarding DFSA, noting that a significant percentage of both young women and men have experienced such assaults, often involving opportunistic tactics that take advantage of incapacitation due to alcohol consumption.

Chauhan and Sharma [198] focused on developing presumptive tests for identifying Z-drugs, demonstrating the need for rapid screening tools in forensic investigations. Melchior et al. [199] pointed out the detection of scopolamine in DFSA cases, emphasizing the variety of drugs that can be used in these crimes. Zhai et al. [200] contributed to the understanding of dexmedetomidine in drug-facilitated cases, showcasing the importance of comprehensive toxicological analyses to identify lesser-known substances. Madry et al. [156] evaluated cocaine and its metabolites across various forensic samples, underscoring the complexity of drug interactions in DFSA contexts. Skov et al. [201] discussed automated hydrolysis techniques to enhance the systematic analysis of toxicological samples, further advancing forensic methodologies. Almofti et al. [202] examined both conventional and unconventional forensic specimens, advocating for a broad approach to evidence collection in DFSA investigations. Avram et al. [203] validated hands-free analytical urine testing technology, which could revolutionize drug testing procedures in real-time scenarios. Lastly, Rubicondo et al. [204] introduced a new multi-analyte LC–MS-MS screening method capable of detecting 120 new psychoactive substances, providing forensic scientists with enhanced tools for investigation.

Conclusion and call to action

Overall, continued research and interdisciplinary approaches remained vital for enhancing the understanding and response to DFC, ultimately aiming to protect potential victims and hold perpetrators accountable. The evolving landscape of DFSA, particularly from 2022 to 2024, underscored the critical importance of understanding the substances involved and their implications for victims. Recent research revealed innovative detection methods and highlighted gaps in current practices, such as the inadequacies of commercial drug detection tools. Findings advocated for improved training for law enforcement and healthcare professionals, as well as enhanced interdisciplinary collaboration to effectively address DFSA. As we move forward, a sustained commitment to research, public education, and advocacy remains essential to combat the pervasive issue of drug-facilitated sexual assault.

Specific drugs of forensic interests

New psychological substances (NPS)

By the end of 2024, the United Nations Office on Drugs and Crime (UNODC) reported a total of 1265 individual NPS through its Early Warning Advisory (EWA) [205]. Concurrently, the European Union Drugs Agency (EUDA) monitored 1000 NPS via the EU Early Warning System (EU EWS) [206]. In recent years, the emergence of individual NPS has notably declined, with just 48, 45, and 15 substances reported to UNODC EWA for the first time in 2022, 2023 and 2024, respectively [207]. Despite this decline, a shift in the distribution of NPS types was observed. In 2022, synthetic cannabinoids dominated the landscape, constituting 48% of reported cases, followed by stimulants (mostly synthetic cathinones) (19%) and synthetic opioids (13%). By 2023, the distribution of these categories became more balanced, but by 2024, synthetic opioids (47%) and synthetic cannabinoids (27%) emerged as the most prevalent NPS reported to the UNODC EWA [207]. Notably, benzodiazepine-type substances predominated among toxicology cases reported to UNODC Toxi-Portal during May 2021 to 2023 [[208], [209], [210]].

Novel benzodiazepines

Benzodiazepines, classified as sedatives and hypnotics effect group have gained attention in the context of NPS. As of 8 February 2025, the UNODC was monitoring 35 benzodiazepines, [205,207], with the most recent addition reported in 2022 [207]. Benzodiazepine-type substances accounted for 52% of the 191 NPS detected in 133 postmortem (PM) cases reported to the UNODC from December 2021 to May 2023 [209]. The rise of fatalities associated with novel benzodiazepines, especially bromazolam, has prompted global concern [[211], [212], [213], [214], [215], [216], [217], [218], [219], [220], [221], [222], [223], [224], [225], [226], [227], [228], [229], [230]].

Bromazolam was first critically reviewed at the 45th WHO Expert Committee on Drug Dependence (ECDD) meeting in 2022 [231], and again at the 46th WHO ECDD meeting in 2023 [232,233], leading to its international control at the 67th Commission on Narcotic Drugs in the same year [234]. In 2023, bromazolam was the most frequently identified benzodiazepine-type NPS in a total of 605 p.m. cases analyzed by UNODC (n = 260) [210], with concentration ranging from 0.016 to 5800 ng/mL [210]. In the United States, bromazolam was detected in more than 250 toxicological cases between 2019 and 2022 [211], with its prevalence increasing from less than 10% in early 2022 [235] to 62.4% by the end of 2024 [236,237].

The Center for Forensic Science Research & Education (CFSRE) [211] reported that the concentrations of bromazolam in PM blood (n = 236) ranged from 2.1 to 670 ng/mL. In a study [216] on coronial investigations in the England and Wales, bromazolam was quantified in 96 cases between September 2021 and November 2022, with PM blood concentrations ranging from <1 to 425 μg/L. Additionally, a study in British Columbia detected bromazolam in 41 p.m. cases in 2021 and the blood concentrations of bromazolam ranged from 0.5 to 319.3 ng/mL [220]. Drummer et al. [219] described the situation in Victoria, Australia from 2018 to 2022 in a report published in November 2024, noting that bromazolam was detected in 15 out of 133 death cases.

Novel benzodiazepines, including bromazolam, were frequently reported in combination with other psychoactive substances [[210], [211], [212], [213], [214], [215], [216], [217], [218], [219], [220], [221], [222], [223], [224], [225], [226], [227], [228], [229], [230], [231], [232], [233]]. The phenomenon known as ‘benzo-dope’, where synthetic opioids are mixed with new benzodiazepines, emerged in 2022 [211,238,239]. These combinations have been linked to an increased risk of opioid overdose death [220,238]. Among the 605 p.m. cases reported to the UNODC EWA Tox-portal in 2023, fentanyl was detected in 62% of cases where bromazolam was also identified, and in 56% of cases with clonazolam [210]. In the 2023 UNODC PM cases where NPS were deemed causal to the fatality (n = 21), combined consumptions of bromazolam with other opioids or stimulants was evidenced in 76% of the cases [210].

As of June 2022, co-detection with fentanyl was observed in over 75% of bromazepam-positive samples analyzed by CFSRE in the United States [211]. In a Canadian study performed by Mérette et al. [220], bromazolam was found to co-occur with fentanyl in most PM cases, with low concentrations of bromazolam detected. The authors believed that this pattern may indicate an enhancement of opioid effects. In contrast, a trend of benzodiazepines co-occurring with fentanyl was not observed in the Europe. Hikin et al. [216] did not detect fentanyl in any of the bromazolam cases examined in England and Wales. Other substances detected alongside bromazolam included cocaine and various central nervous system depressants.

Synthetic opioids

As of 9 February 2025, the UNODC reported monitoring 143 synthetic opioids [205,207], with fentanyl derivatives being the most commonly identified. In response to global control measures aimed at reducing the availability of fentanyl, new synthetic opioids particularly nitazenes, have emerged. From 2022 to 2024, 15 of the 23 synthetic opioids reported to the UNODC Early Warning Advisory (EWA) for the first time were nitazene derivatives [207]. Notably, in 2024, six out of seven new synthetic opioids were nitazene derivatives, making the first time fentanyl derivatives were absent from the list since their emergence in 2012 [207]. In the European drug market, one novel opioid was identified in 2022 and seven in 2023 [238,239]. Among the seven synthetic opioids formally notified to the EU EWS in 2023, six were highly potent nitazenes. The CFSRE year-in-reviews indicated that synthetic opioids remained the largest subclass of newly discovered NPS in the US from 2022 to 2024 [[240], [241], [242]]. This substance group also represented the largest subclass identified in forensic samples by the CFSRE's NPS Discovery program over the past three years.

Four novel synthetic opioids, namely N-pyrrolidino protonitazene (protonitazepyne) [243], N-pyrrolidino metonitazene (metonitazepyne) [244], etonitazepipne (N-piperidinyl etonitazene) [245] and N-desethyl-isotonitazene [246] were critically reviewed at the 47th WHO ECDD meeting in 2024 [247]. Butonitazene [248], another novel benzimidazole-derived synthetic opioid, was reviewed at the 46th ECDD in 2023 [232] and subsequently placed under international control at the 67th Commission on Narcotic Drugs that year [234].

Between December 2021 and May 2023, synthetic opioids accounted for 17% of the 133 p.m. cases reported to UNODC EWA, with protonitazene being the most frequently reported nitazene (n = 16, or 12%) [209]. Compared to previous reporting periods, there was a notable increase in PM cases involving nitazene derivatives, while cases involving fentanyl analogs decreased. By 2023, the prevalence of nitazenes continued to rise. Among the PM cases reported to UNODC, eight individual nitazenes, primarily metonitazene, protnitazene and N-desethyl isotonitazene, accounted for 58% (n = 80) of the cases [210].

The prevalence of novel synthetic opioids and their disposition in the human body are currently being explored by academic researchers and relevant authorities. Data on the concentrations of these new opioids in biological specimens are being collected. Adamowicz et al. [249] reviewed the concentrations in fatal and nonfatal cases involving 37 novel synthetic opioids. In a clinical study conducted in Australia from July 2020 to February 2024 [250], protonitazene was the most commonly detected nitazene, accounting for 72% (n = 23) of relevant presentations, with blood concentrations ranging from 0.7 to 15 mg/mL.

Vandeputte et al. [251] detected N-Piperidinyl etonitazene (etonitazepipne) in serum and urine at levels of 1.21 ng/mL and 0.51 ng/mL, respectively. Pucci et al. [252] reported the detection of N-desethyl isotonitazene, which potentially a metabolite of isotonitazene or a drug on its own, in polydrug users admitted to hospital in Birmingham, with blood concentrations ranging from 0.59 to 5.48 μg/mL and urine concentration from 0.51 to 91.53 μg/mL.

In a study involving casework in New York City, Pardi et al. [253] detected isotonitazene in 10 p.m. cases between 2021 and 2022, with femoral blood concentrations ranging from 0.11 to 12 ng/mL. Metronitazene was detected in 4 p.m. cases in 2022, with femoral blood concentrations ranging from 0.10 to 1.5 ng/mL.

Synthetic and semi-synthetic cannabinoids

As of 9 February 2025, the UNODC was monitoring 367 synthetic opioids [205,207]. Although 40 substances were added, the number of newly reported synthetic cannabinoids decreased from 2022 to 2024 [207]. The EU data reflected a similar trend [238,239]. In 2022, 24 new cannabinoids were reported for the first time to the EU EWS, while only 9 new cannabinoids were firstly reported in 2023. According to data collected by the US CFSRE NPS Discovery program, 20 synthetic cannabinoids were discovered from 2022 to 2024 [240], constituting 34% of the total of 58 new NPS reported during the period. MDMB-4en-PINACA, ABD-BUTINACA and HHC were among the top five most frequently reported synthetic cannabinoids to the UNODC in the past 3 years.

MDMB-4en-PINACA was the most frequently reported synthetic cannabinoids in PM cases analyzed by the UNODC EWA Tox-portal in 2023 [210]. It was also the most commonly identified synthetic cannabinoids in the US from Q1 2022 to Q4 2024 [237]. MDMB-4en-PINACA was scheduled for international control in 2021 and was covered in the last Interpol of Toxicology published in 2023 [254]. ADB-BUTINACA, first reported in 2019 [207], was critically reviewed by the WHO ECDD at its 45th meeting in 2022 [255,256] and was scheduled for international control in 2023 [207,257].

Synthetic cannabinoids are often highly potent, raising significant concerns, particularly regarding the adulteration of cannabis and cannabis edibles [238,239]. Cannabis users may unknowingly be exposed to synthetic cannabinoids. The general public are also at risk of accidental exposure as adulterated cannabis edibles can be mistaken for regular food items, especially by children. Since 2019, at least five countries have reported detections of synthetic cannabinoids in cannabis gummies. HHC, which has drawn attention from the WHO and EUDA in recent years, exemplifies this issue. First identified in Europe in May 2022, it quickly spread to over 70% of the EU Member States within just eight months. In response to the potential public health and social risks, the European Monitoring Center for Drugs and Drug Addiction (EMCDDA) published a technical report in 2023 to raise global awareness of the issue [258]. HHC was also critically reviewed at the 47th WHO ECDD meeting in 2024 [247,259].

Synthetic cathinones

Synthetic cathinones have emerged as replacements for stimulants like amphetamine in certain regions in the world [238,239]. As of 9 February 2025, the UNODC was monitoring 220 synthetic cathinones, with 16 substances reported for the first time in the past 3 years [207]. In 2022, large quantities of cathinones, including 3-CMC and 3-MMC, continued to be seized in Europe [238,239]. The EU EWS noted an increase in reports of synthetic cathinones mis-sold as MDMA or used to adulterate MDMA in 2022 and 2023. Numerous cases of synthetic cathinone intoxication were reported during this period [[259], [260], [261], [262], [263], [264]].

Dipentylone and pentylone were the most commonly identified synthetic cathinones in PM cases analyzed by the UNODC EWA in 2023. Dipentylone was also notably present in US toxicology data from Q1 2022 to Q4 2024 [240]. The substance was critically reviewed at the 46th WHO ECDD meeting in 2023 [232,265] and was placed under international control by the 67th Commission on Narcotic Drugs in March 2024 [234].

3-MMC [3,231]-CMC [232,266] were critically reviewed at the 45th and 46th ECDDC meeting, respectively. Risk assessments for 3-MMC [3,267]-CMC [268] were conducted by an extended EMCDDA Scientific Committee, with reports published in 2022. Both substances were placed under international control in 2023 [208,258] and 2024 [234], respectively.

Other substances of interest

The detection of exposure to chemical warfare agents (CWAs) has attracted significant interest. Otsuka et al. [269,270] successfully detected hydrolysis products of nerve agents in urine and serum. Herbal medicine poisoning remains an important area of research. Huang et al. [271] published the first case report describing the detection of aconitine in a decedent following long-term treatment with herbal medicine derived from aconitum species. Okuda et al. [272] measured levels of oleandrin in serum in a case of suicide involving boiled oleander leaf extract. Zhai et al. [273] authored a review on various detection methods of oleandrin in biological samples.

Behonick et al. [274] reported postmortem blood concentrations in cases of mitragynine intoxication, while Ma et al. [275] studied postmortem distribution and redistribution of paraquat, a common herbicide, and its metabolites in fatal poisoning cases. A forensic examination by Chłopaś-Konowałek et al. [276] investigated a case of insecticide poisoning that resulted in the death of 48 birds, identifying bendiocarb in the liver, stomach, beak contents and the bait found near the deceased birds.

Advances in toxicological analysis

Liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry/mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS)

LC-MS/MS has become essential in pharmaceutical and toxicology studies, providing comprehensive analysis for the identification and quantification of analytes in various biological matrices. Its application in the screening of legal and illicit drugs is particularly crucial for supporting law enforcement efforts. D'Ovidio et al. [277] reviewed the applications of LC-MS/MS in therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) and the analysis of illicit drugs. Merone et al. [278] reported a rapid screening method capable of identifying 739 drugs in blood and urine. This method exploited the advantages of MS/MS, including polarity switching to detect both positive and negative ions, as well as characteristic ion fragmentation in multiple reaction monitoring (MRM), allowing for straightforward and minimal sample manipulation.

NPS abuse has become a global phenomenon and poses a significant challenge to current drug surveillance systems. Rubicondo et al. [204] presented and validated an LC-MS/MS method for the simultaneous detection of 120 NPSs, 43 benzodiazepines/antidepressants, and 6 opioids in hair samples. Czarny et al. [279] developed a method to determine 465 psychoactive substances using liquid-liquid extraction followed by LC-MS/MS in just 30 min. This validated method enabled rapid examination and was applied in routine analysis of urine samples from drivers. Other researchers have also reported analyses of synthetic cathinones [280], synthetic opioids and xylazine [129] using LC-MS/MS.

Farley et al. [281] reported a fully validated LC-MS/MS method for assessing 127 drugs implicated in DUID cases. This research group further presented multi-class testing for drugs involved in drug-facilitated crimes, DUID and postmortem investigations [36]. With these two published methods, over 180 drugs and metabolites, including hallucinogens, antidepressants, antihistamines, antipsychotics, antihypertensives and miscellaneous substances, can be identified in blood and urine. Furthermore, Ji et al. [282] utilized the LC-MS/MS technique to quantify 88 psychotropic drugs, including withdrawal medicines and new-type drugs, in hair samples from drug abstainers. This method identified relapse in some addicts, reflecting emerging trends in the local drug epidemic.

Liquid chromatography-quadrupole time of flight mass spectrometry/mass spectrometry (LC-QTOF-MS/MS)

Ayala and Kerrigan [39] reported a whole blood screening method using LC-QTOF-MS/MS in conjunction with an optimized supported liquid extraction procedure for over 200 compounds, including NPS and drugs used in DUID tests. This method can simultaneously identify Δ9-THC-COOH, which typically requires separate screening, alongside other common drugs of abuse and NPS. Meanwhile, Goh et al. [283] developed a qualitative LC-QTOF-MS/MS method targeting 94 NPS, including synthetic cannabinoids, cathinones, and their metabolites in urine.

Ultra-high performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry/mass spectrometry (UPLC-MS/MS)

The emergence of UPLC-MS/MS has brought significant advancements in chemical analysis of samples, enhancing resolution, sensitivity and speed to meet the growing demands of various industries. Barone et al. [284] optimized a UPLC-MS/MS method for screening multiple analytes in blood within 15 min after a simple protein precipitation extraction. This method was applied to 85 forensic cases, detecting 68 psychoactive drugs, including antidepressants, benzodiazepines, and neuroleptics in postmortem blood. Similarly, Konecki et al. [285] reported a UPLC-MS/MS method capable of determining 55 psychotropic drugs and metabolites in a single run of 5.7 min.

Nielsen et al. [50] developed a rapid UPLC-MS/MS method using a chiral Phenomenex LUX® AMP column to analyze the composition of (R)- and (S)-amphetamine isomers in blood samples, enabling the distinction between illicit and medical amphetamine use. Jin et al. [286] created a UPLC-MS/MS method combined with a modified pretreatment technique to measure the concentrations of nine cardiovascular drugs in plasma samples. Liu et al. [287] employed UPLC-MS/MS with a cryogenic grinding preparation method to detect 106 NPS and other controlled drugs in nail samples. This method was successfully applied to real case samples, aiding in monitoring the drug market in China.

Sample preparation and extraction

Sample preparation is a critical step in toxicological analysis. An optimized method is necessary to enhance sensitivity in instrumental analysis by retaining the analyte of interest while minimizing matrix interference. Microextraction techniques have been developed to analyze complex matrices with varying chemical properties, all without compromising workflow simplicity. D'Ovidio et al. [288] summarized the principles and applications of various microextraction techniques in forensic and biological contexts, highlighting their advantages and limitations. Greco et al. [289] presented an overview of sample preparation techniques used across different complex biological matrices.

Solid-phase microextraction (SPME)

SPME has been widely used across various fields, including bioanalytical, clinical, environmental, forensic and pharmacological applications, demonstrating its effectiveness with complex biological matrices. Sevgen et al. [290] conducted a comprehensive review on the application of SPME for bioanalytical applications, focusing on novel extractive phases and instrumental coupling strategies. Owczarzy et al. [291] introduced and summarized trends and pharmacological applications of different SPME coatings.

Magnetic solid-phase extraction (m-SPE)

m-SPE is a dispersive solid-phase extraction technique that involves the addition of magnetic sorbent to a sample solution, followed by the elution of adsorbed analytes with appropriate solvents. Hwang et al. [292] developed a method for screening of 40 NPS in human plasma using carboxylic acid functionalized multi-walled carbon nanotubes sorbent coupled with LC-QTOF. Similarly, modifications to the magnetic nanomaterial enabled the development of detection methods for cannabinoids [293] and other illicit drugs [294]. Alanazi et al. [295] developed a method for the quantification of anti-cancer drug methotrexate in plasma by coupling magnetic molecularly imprinted SPME with a ratiometric fluorometric assay.

Other microextraction based techniques

Electromembrane extraction (EME) is a liquid-phase microextraction technique that employs an electric field to transfer target analytes from a sample to an acceptor phase through a hydrophobic membrane containing a supported organic solvent [296]. Schüller et al. [297] reported a method using EME coupled with UPLC-MS/MS to quantify 16 tryptamine analogs, a class of NPS, in whole blood. The same group later employed a similar strategy to develop a quantification method for synthetic opioid analogs, such as nitazene [298], and synthetic cathinones [299] in human whole blood. EME is also effective for extracting conventional drugs of abuse; for instance, it has been used to extract and quantify 12 opioids from oral fluids [300]. Furthermore, Schüller et al. [301] validated methods for determining and quantifying drugs of abuse, therapeutic drugs and their metabolites in hair.

QuEChERS

The quick, easy, cheap, effective, rugged, and safe (QuEChERS) method was originally designed for cost-effective analysis of pesticide residues in food samples, but its application has expanded to include pharmaceutical compounds and veterinary drugs. This method typically requires minimal solvent and involves two main steps 1) extraction followed by salting out and 2) dispersive solid-phase extraction. Veiga-del-Baño [302] presented an overview of QuEChERS applications published from 2003 to 2023, highlighting its characteristics, modifications and trends over the years. Zheng et al. [303] reported and validated a modified QuEChERS method coupled with HPLC-MS/MS for the determination of 6 immunosuppressants in blood. Cox et al. [7] reported an LC-MS/MS method which used QuEChERS extraction to quantify 34 fentanyl analogs successfully applying it to postmortem liver specimens. In 2022, Lehotay [304] proposed a revised method called QuEChERSER (QuEChERS + Efficient and Robust), which involved comminuting a smaller sample size and demonstrated a 5% lower overall relative standard deviation compared to traditional QuEChERS, broadening the scope of sample types. This “mega-method” was effective in determining pesticides, veterinary drugs and environmental contaminants [305]. Jin and coworkers [286] also adopted QuEChERS pretreatment method to simultaneously measure the concentrations of nine cardiovascular drugs in human plasma.

Disposable pipette extraction (DPX)

DPX, developed by Dr. William Brewer in 2003, is a solid-phase extraction technique in which the sorbent is loosely dispersed within a pipette tip. This design facilitates quick and dynamic contact between the aspirated analyte from the sample and the solid phase. DPX is a simple, fast, and efficient technique that requires minimal sample volume, making it suitable for a wide range of applications, particularly in forensic analysis.

Kakuda et al. [306] reported the use of DPX coupled with ultra-efficient liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry for the determination of cortisol and cortisone in urine and saliva samples from patients with Parkinson's disease. Comelli et al. [307] developed a fully optimized DPX method for the determination of free monomethyl phthalate, monobutyl phthalate, monobenzyl phthalate, and monoethylhexyl phthalate in urine samples. In addition, DPX has been employed for determining a heterogeneous group of 40 drugs of abuse and their metabolites in urine by liquid chromatography coupled with tandem mass spectrometry [308]. Beyond urine and saliva matrices, DPX has also been successfully used for extracting psychoactive drugs from sweat samples [309,310].

Screening of drugs in dried blood spots

Dried blood spot (DBS) is a micro-sampling technique that collects a small amount of capillary blood, which is then blotted and dried on specialized filter paper. This minimally invasive approach offers several advantages over traditional venous blood sampling, including easier handling, reduced biohazard risk, facilitated automation and improved analyte stability. However, variations in hematocrit (HCT) value, which can be affected by factors such as age and sex, pose a significant challenge in analyte quantification.

Bossi et al. [311] summarized commercially available micro-sampling devices that address variations in HCT spot size and blood volume. Meikopoulos et al. [312] utilized a quantitative DBS device to develop a UPLC-MS/MS method for determining 26 drugs of abuse in DBS samples. Abady et al. [313] optimized a protein precipitation method for the simultaneous quantification of eight cardiovascular drugs in DBS. González-Rubio et al. [314] developed a thermal decomposition, amalgamation and atomic absorption spectrometry (TDA-AAS) method, requiring no sample preparation, for the direct analysis of mercury in capillary blood. They compared this method with Inductively Coupled Plasma Mass Spectrometry (ICP-MS) analysis and found no significant differences in the results. Wikström et al. [315] reported a method for determining lithium concentrations in capillary blood in clinical settings. Stöth et al. [316] adopted the DBS strategy on urine samples, presenting an LC-QTOF method coupled with a fully automated extraction system for screening over 65 drugs in dried urine spots.

Interpretation of toxicological results and alternative specimens

Toxicological analysis plays a crucial role in forensic investigations, particularly in cases involving substance abuse or poisoning. Traditionally, blood and urine have been the primary specimens used for testing. However, alternative specimens such as dried blood spots, thoracic cavity fluid, and brain homogenates are gaining traction due to their practicality and ease of use. The exploration of these alternative specimens is vital, especially when traditional blood samples are compromised or unavailable, as they can provide additional insights into toxicological assessments. Continued research into the reliability and applicability of these alternative matrices is essential for enhancing the accuracy of toxicological interpretations in forensic cases.

Interpretation of toxicological results

The interpretation of toxicological results is intricate and hinges on a thorough understanding of how substances interact within the body. Various factors, including the time elapsed since exposure, the individual's metabolic rate, and the type of specimen utilized, can significantly influence the results. For instance, although blood is often the preferred specimen, the concentration of substances contained in blood may change over time, complicating interpretations when samples are collected long after the incident.

The literature emphasized the importance of context in interpreting toxicological findings. For example, the source of detected substances, such as morphine, varied significantly, stemming from illicit drug use, dietary sources, or prescribed medications. A recent study by Arbouche et al. [317] on insulin overdose illustrated the complexities involved in postmortem toxicology, highlighting that even therapeutic concentrations of insulin could complicate the determination of overdose in forensic cases. Additionally, novel approaches to characterize individual drug metabolism, as discussed by Jackson et al. [318], emphasized the need for integrating traditional and innovative methods to enhance the understanding of drug metabolism in diverse populations.

Further insights came from the postmortem chiral analysis of MDMA and MDA in human blood and hair by Bickel et al. [319], which revealed enantiomer ratios correlated with survival times, emphasizing the complexities of interpreting drug concentrations in forensic contexts. Research by Yu et al. [320] on FDA-approved drugs from 2022 highlighted enzyme- and transporter-mediated drug interactions, underlining the importance of understanding these interactions within the context of drug metabolism and potential toxicological implications. Moreover, pharmacokinetic drug-drug interactions associated with FDA-approved medications in 2021, characterized by Yu et al. [321], emphasized their mechanisms and clinical implications for patient safety.

The comprehensive review by Stielow et al. [322] provided insights into drug bioavailability's critical role in pharmacological therapy efficacy. The combination of electrochemistry and microfluidic technology in drug metabolism studies by Grint et al. [323] offered promising methods for mimicking metabolic processes, enabling faster analysis of drug interactions and metabolite formation. Additionally, Shrewsbury [324] explored the pharmacokinetics of drugs delivered to the upper nasal space, emphasizing the unique absorption and distribution characteristics that could influence therapeutic efficacy, particularly for central nervous system-targeted therapies, Thus, a comprehensive understanding of case circumstances, combined with robust analytical techniques, was essential for accurate toxicological interpretation.

The paper by Fairman et al. [325] discussed the application of PBPK models in forensic science, emphasizing their potential to improve the interpretation of toxicological data. The authors highlighted that “PBPK models can be applied to establish concentration-time profiles of the drug(s) in blood and different tissues in humans to resolve such limitations,” particularly in the context of postmortem redistribution phenomena. They also noted the existing gaps and uncertainties in the application of these models within forensic investigations.

The paper by Romańczuk et al. [326] discussed the challenges posed by the detection of chloromethcathinone isomers in postmortem specimens, emphasizing the need for careful interpretation of toxicological results in cases involving synthetic cathinones.

Bloodstains from crime scenes

The study by Adamowicz and Ziora [15] investigated the potential of using bloodstains from crime scenes to estimate drug concentrations at the time of injury. Their findings indicated that drug concentrations, namely amphetamine and amino acids, could be quantified in bloodstains of unknown volume, which was particularly advantageous in hit-and-run cases where traditional blood samples might not have been available. This approach underscored the significance of alternative specimens in forensic toxicology.

Alternative specimens

Dried blood spots (DBS)

Dried blood spots (DBS) presented a convenient alternative for toxicological analysis, allowing for easy collection, storage, and transport. Research showed that drug concentrations in DBS often correlated well with those in whole blood, though variability in blood volume should have been considered. In the article by Skogvold et al. [327], DBS sampling was highlighted as a simple, minimally invasive method for at-home blood collection, enhancing global access to clinical diagnostics. By utilizing liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS), DBS enabled stable sample transport and analysis for various health conditions, including newborn screening and disease monitoring, thereby offering cost-effective and accessible solutions to reduce health inequalities.

Thoracic cavity fluid (TCF)

Thoracic cavity fluid emerged as a viable alternative for measuring carboxyhaemoglobin (COHb) levels. Studies demonstrated a strong correlation between COHb levels in TCF and blood, making TCF a reliable specimen when blood was unavailable. Research by Muleya et al. [328] found TCF to be effective for COHb analysis, particularly in cases where blood samples might have been compromised.

Brain homogenates

Brain tissue provided valuable insights into drug concentrations, especially for central nervous system stimulants. Studies by Behnke et al. [329] suggested that stimulant drugs in brain homogenates could remain stable for extended periods when stored correctly. For example, cocaine and amphetamines were shown to maintain stability for up to 90 days when frozen.

Stomach wall tissue

The use of alternative specimens was particularly relevant in cases of advanced putrefaction, where traditional samples might have been compromised or absent. For instance, the analysis of stomach wall tissue by Al-Asmari et al. [330] was shown to aid in the detection of opioids and their metabolites, offering a complementary approach to understanding drug-related fatalities. However, interpreting results from these specimens posed challenges due to factors such as matrix effects, degradation of analytes, and the need for validated analytical methods.

Challenges in postmortem toxicology

The interpretation of postmortem toxicology results was fraught with challenges due to various pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic factors that influenced drug levels after death. Factors such as postmortem redistribution, site-to-site variability, and the presence of underlying conditions complicated the assessment of toxicological findings, as suggested by Stephenson et al. [23]. Despite these difficulties, the information derived from postmortem toxicology remained crucial for determining the cause and manner of death.

The exploration of alternative specimens for toxicological analysis continued to gain traction. For instance, urine and tissue samples could provide additional insights when traditional blood samples were compromised or unavailable. Continued research by Zughaibi et al. [331] into the reliability and applicability of these alternative matrices was essential for enhancing the accuracy of interpretations.

Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF)

The study by Wachholz et al. [332] examined the correlation between drug concentrations in CSF and blood in postmortem cases. The authors found significant correlations for many analytes, suggesting that CSF can be a valuable alternative specimen for toxicological analysis, particularly when blood samples were unavailable. This research underscored the necessity for further studies to enhance the understanding of pharmacokinetic processes and the effects of postmortem redistribution on drug concentrations.

CSF also offered promising insights into the central nervous system's exposure to xenobiotics, which might not have been reflected in blood or urine. However, as suggested by Czarniak et al. [333], interpreting CSF results could be challenging due to a limited database of reference values and various influencing factors, including the timing of collection and individual physiological differences.

Result interpretation complication by novel designer benzodiazepines, drug-drug interactions as well as drugs distribution in ethanol and water

A technical note by Rackow et al. [334] evaluated false positive results for amphetamines in urine drug screening, identifying m-chlorophenylpiperazine and solriamfetol as culprits in the Thermo Scientific DRI Amphetamines immunoassay. Trazodone showed no cross-reactivity, while aripiprazole and atomoxetine did not impact the assay results, underscoring the importance of confirmatory testing for accurate patient care.

Additionally, Reber et al. [335] addressed the interference of novel designer benzodiazepines with standard toxicological analyses, further complicating result interpretation. Dagnew et al. [336] explored potential drug-drug interactions among psychiatric patients, underscoring the need for comprehensive assessments in clinical settings. Jones and Tilson [337] provided recommendations for interpreting clinical laboratory results in a legal context, emphasizing the importance of accurate data in forensic investigations. Lastly, Shang et al. [338] discussed drug disposition in cholestasis, adding another layer of complexity to toxicological evaluations.

Advantages of alternative biological specimens

Alternative biological specimens offered several advantages, including non-invasiveness, ease of collection, and the ability to reflect long-term exposure. For instance, hair could provide a historical record of exposure, while saliva might be useful for assessing acute exposure levels. The systematic review by Nguyen et al. [339] examined how physicochemical properties affect drug excretion into saliva, revealing that many commonly used drugs are suitable for saliva-based therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM). The authors concluded that “further research on drugs potentially suitable for saliva-based TDM will aid in adopting this person-centred TDM approach to improve patient outcomes.”

Computational toxicology

The review by Jia et al. [340] on advancing computational toxicology through interpretable machine learning underscored the importance of developing models that elucidate toxicity mechanisms, thereby enhancing the interpretation of toxicological data. The work by Rosano et al. [341] on the confirmation of cannabinoids in forensic toxicology casework using isomer-selective UPLC–MS-MS analysis demonstrated the need for updated methods to accurately identify and quantify cannabinoid isomers in urine, which was crucial for forensic investigations.

Toxicogenomics and artificial intelligence (AI)

Research by Singh et al. [342] discussed how toxicogenomics, combined with AI, could enhance personalized medicine and toxicology by analyzing large datasets to predict treatment outcomes and assess risks. The paper by Garneau et al. [343] focused on postmortem redistribution patterns of methadone in chronic users and emphasized the need to analyze multiple blood sources for accurate toxicological interpretation. Integrating toxicogenomic data with AI could have enhanced the analysis of complex toxicological cases, potentially improving the understanding of drug metabolism and toxicity in chronic users.

Central/peripheral blood (C/P) ratios and drug behavior

Emaus et al. [24] investigated the relationship between various factors and the C/P ratios of cocaine and its metabolites, providing insights into the complexities involved in interpreting toxicological results. Tascon et al. [344] examined the postmortem behavior of Δ9- THC and its metabolites, emphasizing the need for careful consideration of postmortem redistribution and its impact on drug concentration measurements in forensic toxicology.

Lastly, de Groot et al. [25] explored the relationship between various factors and the C/P ratios of amphetamines and benzodiazepines, further complicating result interpretation. Deng et al. [345] illustrated the challenges of interpreting toxicological results, demonstrating that colchicine can remain in the bloodstream for several days post-ingestion, leading to fatal outcomes. This case underscores the importance of understanding delayed elimination and accumulation in toxicological assessments.

Case studies

GHB cut-off values

A recent case report by Loos et al. [346] emphasized the challenges in establishing cut-off values for GHB. It recommended a cut-off of 30 mg/L for urine samples to differentiate between endogenous and exogenous levels, while noting that ante-mortem values of 2 to 10 mg/L could lead to false negatives. The authors highlighted the importance of proper storage and analysis methods to ensure accurate toxicological interpretations.

Study on citalopram and escitalopram

Additionally, a study by Rygaard et al. [347] investigated the concentrations of citalopram and escitalopram in postmortem hair segments from deceased individuals with mental illnesss. The study found citalopram concentrations ranging from 0.12 to 67 ng/mg and escitalopram from 0.027 to 7.0 ng/mg, providing valuable reference values for forensic investigations regarding drug intake and tolerance.

Human tissue distribution of caspofungin

Moreover, a study by Marx et al. [348] examined the human tissue distribution of caspofungin, an echinocandin antifungal. The study found the highest concentrations in the liver, followed by the spleen, kidney and lung, with lower levels in the brain. These findings suggested that caspofungin penetrated various tissues effectively, except for the brain, which might have had implications for treating invasive candidiasis.

Drug interactions of THC and CBD

Furthermore, a review by Herdegen and Cascorbi [349] discussed the drug interactions of THC and CBD in cannabinoid drugs. It highlighted that THC and CBD could inhibit cytochrome P450 enzymes, leading to potential drug interactions, particularly at higher doses. The clinical significance of these interactions varied and required careful monitoring in patients using cannabinoid medications.

Conclusion

Integrating alternative specimens like CSF into toxicological analysis offered valuable insights but also presented unique challenges in interpretation. As forensic toxicology continued to evolve, developing standardized procedures for the collection and analysis of these specimens was essential. Ongoing research and collaboration within the scientific community would enhance the reliability and accuracy of toxicological interpretations, ultimately improving outcomes in forensic investigations.
