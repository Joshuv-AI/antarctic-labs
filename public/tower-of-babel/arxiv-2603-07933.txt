Condition-Triggered Cryptographic Asset Control via Dormant Authorization Paths
Jian Sheng Wang

Abstract
Control of encrypted digital assets is traditionally equated with permanent possession of private keys—a model that fundamentally precludes regulatory supervision, conditional delegation, and legally compliant transfer at the cryptographic layer. Existing remedies, including multi-signature schemes, threshold signature protocols, smart-contract-based enforcement, and custodial delegation, require either persistent key exposure, on-chain state mutation, or trusted intermediaries, and therefore fail to provide immediate, revocable, and condition-dependent control purely through cryptographic means. We introduce Condition-Triggered Dormant Authorization Paths ( CT-DAP ), a cryptographic asset control method built on destructible authorization factors . The method is parameterized by an abstract root-derivable framework satisfying three properties: deterministic key derivation from a single root entity, context-isolated control capability generation, and authorization-bound revocation. Under CT-DAP , asset control rights are modeled as dormant authorization paths composed of user-held credentials and one or more administrative authorization factors held by independent custodians. A control right remains cryptographically inactive until all required authorization factors are simultaneously available. Upon verification of predefined external conditions—such as explicit user consent, legally recognized inheritance events, or time-based triggers—the corresponding administrative authorization factor is released, activating the associated control path. Revocation is achieved by destroying authorization factors, rendering the corresponding control path permanently unusable without altering the underlying cryptographic root. We formalize the threat model, define security games for unauthorized control resistance, path isolation, and stateless revocation, and provide proofs under standard assumptions (AEAD security—IND-CPA and INT-CTXT—of AES-GCM-SIV, PRF security of HKDF in the random-oracle model, memory-hardness of Argon2id, and collision resistance of SHA-256). We instantiate the framework using the Atomic Cryptographic Entity Generative Framework (ACE-GF) and evaluate performance on commodity hardware, demonstrating sub-second activation latency with configurable security–performance trade-offs. Keywords: Cryptographic Asset Control; Dormant Authorization Path; Destructible Authorization Factor; Conditional Activation; Stateless Revocation; Key Derivation; ACE-GF

Introduction

1.1
Background and Motivation

Control of encrypted digital assets is a foundational problem in modern
cryptographic systems. In prevailing designs, control rights are
implicitly equated with permanent possession of cryptographic secrets,
typically private keys. While this model provides simplicity and strong
autonomy, it fundamentally limits the expressiveness of asset control in
real-world scenarios that require conditional delegation, regulatory
supervision, inheritance, or legally enforceable transfer of rights.

In particular, existing cryptographic asset systems lack native mechanisms
for representing control rights that are
temporarily inactive
,

conditionally enabled
, or
revocable without state migration
.
As a result, control transitions are commonly implemented through ad hoc
constructions—such as trusted custodians, contractual agreements
external to the cryptographic system, or on-chain smart contracts—that
introduce additional attack surfaces and operational complexity.

These limitations are increasingly problematic as encrypted assets are
integrated into regulated financial systems, estate planning, and
multi-party organizational governance, where control must be exercised not
only securely, but also conditionally and compliantly.

1.2
Limitations of Existing Approaches

Several classes of solutions have been proposed to address aspects of
conditional or shared control; each suffers from structural limitations.

Permanent Key Possession.

Systems that rely on a single controlling private key provide no intrinsic
mechanism for conditional access or delegation. Any transfer of control
requires direct disclosure or replacement of the controlling secret,
resulting in irreversible loss of exclusivity and making revocation
impractical
[
5
,
6
]
.

Multi-Signature and Threshold Schemes.

Multi-signature constructions
[
26
]
and threshold
signature protocols (e.g., GG18
[
11
]
, GG20
[
12
]
,
FROST
[
13
]
) distribute control across multiple keys and parties
but require persistent availability of all participating keys. Changes in
control conditions necessitate reconfiguration of signing policies or
migration of assets, introducing operational overhead. Moreover, these
schemes conflate control enforcement with participation requirements,
making revocation and conditional delegation stateful and
inflexible
[
14
]
.

Secret Sharing.

Shamir’s secret sharing
[
10
]
and its proactive
variants
[
15
]
can distribute a master secret among
multiple parties with a threshold reconstruction policy. However,
reconstructing the secret reveals it to the reconstructing party,
and the scheme does not natively support conditional activation
tied to external events or stateless revocation of individual shares.

Smart-Contract-Based Enforcement.

Smart contracts enable programmable control logic, including conditional
execution, but at the cost of on-chain state mutation, code immutability
risks, and dependency on external data
sources
[
17
,
18
]
. Conditional triggers
implemented via contracts are vulnerable to oracle
manipulation
[
8

ambiguity, and often fail to align cryptographic enforcement with
off-chain legal authorization.

Attribute-Based and Policy-Based Encryption.

Ciphertext-policy attribute-based encryption
(CP-ABE)
[
19
]
and key-policy ABE
(KP-ABE)
[
20
]
enable fine-grained access control
policies embedded in ciphertexts or keys. While these schemes support
conditional decryption, they are designed for data confidentiality
rather than cryptographic asset control, and do not address revocable
control paths or stateless revocation without re-encryption.

Time-Lock Cryptography.

Time-lock puzzles
[
22
]
and verifiable delay
functions (VDFs)
[
23
]
enable time-conditioned
release of secrets. However, they support only temporal conditions
(not arbitrary external events), and revocation after puzzle generation
is impossible.

Custodial Models.

Custodial solutions externalize control to trusted intermediaries,
sacrificing cryptographic autonomy and introducing centralized trust
assumptions. While custodians may enforce legal or regulatory
conditions, cryptographic control is no longer directly tied to the
asset holder or beneficiaries
[
25
]
.

Social Recovery.

Social recovery wallets
[
24
]
allow
designated guardians to assist in key recovery, but conflate recovery
with ongoing control and do not support dormant, conditionally activated
control paths.

Collectively, these approaches conflate
control
with either

key possession
,
contract state
, or
institutional
trust
, and do not provide a unified cryptographic abstraction for
conditional and revocable asset control.

1.3
Problem Statement

The core problem addressed in this work is:

How can control of encrypted digital assets be conditionally enabled,
revoked, and transferred in a manner that is cryptographically
enforceable, legally auditable, and does not require persistent key
exposure, asset migration, or on-chain state modification?

A satisfactory solution must satisfy several requirements simultaneously:

•

Cryptographic Enforceability:
Unauthorized parties must
be unable to exercise control regardless of off-chain agreements.

•

Conditional Activation:
Control rights should remain
inactive until predefined external conditions are satisfied.

•

Immediate Revocation:
Control must be disableable
without rotating keys or modifying asset ownership structures.

•

Statelessness:
Control transitions should not require
maintaining mutable global state.

•

Legal Compatibility:
Cryptographic control should align
with legally recognized authorization and delegation processes.

Existing systems satisfy at most a subset of these requirements, and often
only by introducing additional trusted components.

1.4
Core Insight

This paper is based on a key conceptual insight:

Control of encrypted assets need not be transferred; it can be
activated.

Rather than equating control with possession of cryptographic secrets, we
model control rights as
authorization paths
that exist prior to
activation. These authorization paths remain cryptographically dormant
until all required authorization factors are present. Activation of
control is achieved not by reassigning keys or modifying assets, but by
enabling a pre-established authorization path under verifiable conditions.

Revocation is performed by destroying or invalidating authorization
factors, rendering the corresponding control path permanently unusable
without altering the underlying cryptographic root or asset identity.

1.5
Proposed Approach

We propose
Condition-Triggered Dormant Authorization Paths

(
CT-DAP
), a cryptographic asset control method based on

destructible authorization factors
. The method is parameterized
by an abstract
root-derivable framework
(
Definition 3.1
)
satisfying three properties: deterministic derivation from a single root
entity, context-isolated key generation, and authorization-bound
revocation via destructible credential components.

Under
CT-DAP
, each control right is represented as a dormant
authorization path composed of:

•

One or more user-held authorization credentials, and

•

One or more administrative authorization factors held by
independent custodians.

A control right becomes effective only when all required authorization
factors are simultaneously available. Administrative authorization
factors may be conditionally released upon verification of external
events, such as explicit user consent, legally recognized inheritance
conditions, or time-based triggers.

Related Work

This section surveys the landscape of cryptographic asset control,
positioning our work relative to established and emerging approaches.

2.1
Single-Key and Hierarchical Deterministic Wallets

Traditional cryptocurrency wallets equate control with possession of a
single private key or a hierarchically derived key tree
(BIP-32
[
5
]
, BIP-39
[
6
]
, BIP-44
[
7
]
).
These systems offer simplicity and full user autonomy but provide no
native support for conditional activation, delegation, or revocation.
Any control transition requires direct key transfer or asset migration,
both of which are irreversible and operationally costly.

2.2
Multi-Signature and Threshold Signature Schemes

Multi-signature wallets
[
26
]
require
m
-of-
n

signatures to authorize a transaction. Threshold signature schemes
(TSS)—including GG18
[
11
]
, GG20
[
12
]
, and
FROST
[
13
]
—eliminate the need for on-chain multi-sig scripts
by distributing key generation and signing across
n
parties with a
threshold
t
.

While TSS reduces on-chain footprint, both approaches require
persistent availability of all participating key shares, lack native
support for dormant (conditionally inactive) control rights, and
require resharing or migration when control conditions change.
Proactive secret sharing
[
15
]
can
refresh shares periodically, but does not address conditional
activation tied to external events.

2.3
Secret Sharing and Social Recovery

Shamir’s secret sharing
[
10
]
enables
(t,n)
-threshold
reconstruction of a master secret. Social recovery
wallets
[
24
]
apply this principle by
designating guardians who can collectively recover access.

These schemes conflate
recovery
with
ongoing control
:
once the secret is reconstructed, the reconstructing party gains full
and irrevocable access. There is no mechanism for conditional
activation that preserves dormancy, nor for revoking a specific
guardian’s capability without redistributing shares.

2.4
Attribute-Based and Policy-Based Access Control

Ciphertext-policy ABE (CP-ABE)
[
19
]
and
key-policy ABE (KP-ABE)
[
20
]
embed fine-grained
access policies into the encryption layer. While conceptually related
to our notion of conditional activation, ABE schemes are designed for

data confidentiality
(who can decrypt a ciphertext) rather than

asset control
(who can derive a signing key or exercise
cryptographic authority over an asset). Furthermore, revocation in ABE
typically requires re-encryption or key update
mechanisms
[
21
]
, which conflict with our
statelessness requirement.

2.5
Smart-Contract-Based Enforcement

Smart contracts
[
17
,
18
]
enable
programmable, on-chain enforcement of control policies, including
multi-sig, time-locks, and conditional transfers. However,
contract-based control depends on on-chain state mutation, is vulnerable
to oracle manipulation
[
8
]
, and ties control
semantics to a specific blockchain’s execution model. Control
transitions require explicit asset migration (e.g., transferring tokens
to a new contract address), which is neither stateless nor
chain-agnostic.

2.6
Time-Lock Cryptography and Verifiable Delay Functions

Time-lock puzzles
[
22
]
and verifiable delay
functions (VDFs)
[
23
]
provide time-conditioned secret
release. While useful for temporal conditions, they are limited to
a single trigger type (elapsed time), cannot incorporate arbitrary
external events (e.g., legal recognition of inheritance), and are
irrevocable once the puzzle is generated. Our method supports
arbitrary condition types and immediate revocation.

2.7
MPC-Based Custody Solutions

Institutional custody platforms (e.g.,
[
27
]
) use
multi-party computation (MPC) to distribute signing authority without
reconstructing the private key. While MPC custody preserves
non-disclosure of the full key, it requires persistent online
participation of MPC nodes, and policy changes require re-keying.
Our approach achieves conditional control through dormant paths that
can be activated or revoked without any online coordination among
authorization factor holders.

System Model

This section defines the abstract system model, trust assumptions, and
formal syntax for the
CT-DAP
method.

3.1
Entities and Roles

The system involves the following entities:

•

Asset Owner (
\mathcal{O}
):

The entity that holds the cryptographic root and establishes
authorization paths.
\mathcal{O}
possesses the user-held
credential
\mathsf{UserCred}
and the sealed artifact
\mathsf{SA}
.

•

Custodian (
\mathcal{C}_{i}
):

An independent entity that holds and conditionally releases a
single administrative authorization factor
\mathsf{AdminFactor}_{i}
.
Custodians cannot unilaterally exercise asset control.

•

Beneficiary (
\mathcal{B}_{j}
):

An entity designated to receive control capability upon
activation of authorization path
\mathcal{AP}_{j}
. A beneficiary may
also act as the asset owner in self-authorization scenarios.

•

Condition Verifier (
\mathcal{V}
):

An entity (or protocol) that verifies whether a predefined
external condition is satisfied and provides a
cryptographically signed attestation to the custodian.
The verifier may be a trusted third party (e.g., notary,
regulatory body) or a decentralized oracle
network
[
8
]
.

3.2
Trust Model and Assumptions

We formalize the trust assumptions that underpin the security of
CT-DAP
.

Assumption 3.1

(Honest-but-Curious Custodians)
.

Each custodian
\mathcal{C}_{i}
is assumed to faithfully hold and
conditionally release
\mathsf{AdminFactor}_{i}
according to predefined release
conditions. However, custodians may attempt to learn information
about other authorization factors or the root secret from the
information available to them. Custodians do not collude with
each other or with unauthorized parties unless explicitly modeled.

Assumption 3.2

(Bounded Adversary)
.

The adversary
\mathcal{A}
is a probabilistic polynomial-time (PPT)
algorithm that may:

(i)

Compromise up to
t<n
custodians for a given path with

n
authorization factors (obtaining their
\mathsf{AdminFactor}_{i}
values);

(ii)

Obtain the sealed artifact
\mathsf{SA}
(e.g., by compromising
storage);

(iii)

Observe activated control keys from
other
paths

\mathcal{AP}_{k}
(
k\neq j
);

(iv)

Interact with the condition verifier.

The adversary
cannot
simultaneously obtain
all

components of
\mathsf{Cred}_{\mathrm{composite}}
for a target path.

Assumption 3.3

(Condition Verifier Integrity)
.

The condition verifier
\mathcal{V}
correctly evaluates the
predefined condition and provides attestations only when the
condition is genuinely satisfied. For deployments requiring
stronger guarantees,
\mathcal{V}
may be instantiated as a
decentralized oracle with multi-source aggregation to mitigate
single-point-of-failure risks (see
subsection 6.2
).

Assumption 3.4

(Cryptographic Hardness)
.

We assume:

(i)

The sealing mechanism (e.g., AES-256-GCM-SIV) provides
both IND-CPA security (ciphertext indistinguishability) and
INT-CTXT security (ciphertext integrity / authentication):
decryption with an incorrect key returns
\bot
with
overwhelming probability
[
3
]
;

(ii)

HKDF-SHA256
[
4
]
is a pseudorandom function (PRF)
in the random oracle model;

(iii)

Argon2id
[
2
]
provides memory-hard key derivation
with resistance to offline brute-force attacks;

(iv)

The hash function
H
used for path identifiers (e.g.,
SHA-256) is collision-resistant: for any PPT adversary
\mathcal{A}
,

\Pr[\mathcal{A}\text{ finds }x\neq x^{\prime}\text{ s.t.\ }H(x)=H(x^{\prime})]\leq\mathsf{negl}(\lambda)
.

3.3
Root-Derivable Framework (Abstract Interface)

CT-DAP
is parameterized by a
root-derivable framework

satisfying the following interface:

Definition 3.1

(Root-Derivable Framework)
.

A root-derivable framework
\Pi
is a tuple of PPT algorithms

(\mathsf{Setup},\mathsf{Seal},\mathsf{Unseal},\mathsf{Derive})

such that:

•

\mathsf{Setup}(1^{\lambda})\to(\mathsf{REV},\mathsf{params})
:
Generates a 256-bit Root Entropy Value and public parameters.

•

\mathsf{Seal}(\mathsf{params},\mathsf{Cred}_{\mathrm{composite}},\mathsf{REV})\to\mathsf{SA}
:
Encrypts
\mathsf{REV}
under the composite credential, producing
a sealed artifact.

•

\mathsf{Unseal}(\mathsf{params},\mathsf{SA},\mathsf{Cred}_{\mathrm{composite}})\to\mathsf{REV}\cup\{\bot\}
:
Reconstructs
\mathsf{REV}
if and only if the correct composite
credential is provided; otherwise returns
\bot
.

•

\mathsf{Derive}(\mathsf{REV},\mathsf{Ctx})\to\mathsf{Key}_{\mathcal{AP}}
:
Deterministically derives a context-isolated control key from

\mathsf{REV}
and a context tuple
\mathsf{Ctx}
.

Definition 3.2

(Required Security Properties)
.

The framework
\Pi
must satisfy:

P1

Sealing Indistinguishability:

For any two roots
\mathsf{REV}_{0},\mathsf{REV}_{1}
of equal length, no PPT
adversary without
\mathsf{Cred}_{\mathrm{composite}}
can distinguish
the two sealed artifacts

\mathsf{Seal}(\mathsf{params},\mathsf{Cred}_{\mathrm{composite}},\mathsf{REV}_{b}),\quad b\in\{0,1\}

with non-negligible advantage.

P2

Unsealing Correctness:

\mathsf{Unseal}(\mathsf{params},\mathsf{SA},\mathsf{Cred}^{\prime})=\bot
for all

\mathsf{Cred}^{\prime}\neq\mathsf{Cred}_{\mathrm{composite}}
.

P3

Context Isolation:

For distinct contexts
\mathsf{Ctx}_{j}\neq\mathsf{Ctx}_{k}
, the values

\mathsf{Derive}(\mathsf{REV},\mathsf{Ctx}_{j})
and

\mathsf{Derive}(\mathsf{REV},\mathsf{Ctx}_{k})
are computationally
independent: no PPT adversary given one can
distinguish the other from uniform with non-negligible
advantage.

3.4
Formal Syntax

Definition 3.3

(
CT-DAP
Syntax)
.

The
CT-DAP

•

\mathsf{PathSetup}(\Pi,1^{\lambda},\mathsf{UserCred},\{\mathsf{AdminFactor}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n})\to(\mathsf{SA}_{\mathrm{root}},s,\mathsf{params})

Establishes the root entity, generates a random salt

•

\mathsf{Activate}(\Pi,\mathsf{SA},\mathsf{UserCred},\{\mathsf{AdminFactor}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n},\mathsf{Ctx}_{j})\to\mathsf{Key}_{\mathcal{AP}_{j}}\cup\{\bot\}
:
Reconstructs
\mathsf{REV}
via
\mathsf{Unseal}
, derives

\mathsf{Key}_{\mathcal{AP}_{j}}
, zeroizes
\mathsf{REV}
from memory, and returns the

•

\mathsf{Revoke}(i)\to\{\mathsf{revoked}\}
:
Destroys
\mathsf{AdminFactor}_{i}

permanently

.

Definition 3.4

(Authorization Path)
.

The
j

Algorithm 1

\mathsf{PathSetup}

6
Conditional Triggering and Activation

Administrative authorization factors are not persistently available.
Their release is conditioned on verification of predefined external
events.

6.1
Trigger Conditions

Supported condition types include, but are not limited to:

•

Explicit User Consent:
Direct authorization by the
asset owner (e.g., signed request).

•

Legal Events:
Legally recognized inheritance or
succession events, verified by a certified entity (e.g., notary,
probate court).

•

Temporal Conditions:
Time-based or jurisdiction
deadlines, verifiable via trusted timestamps or on-chain
time-locks.

•

Regulatory Actions:
Suspension or compliance-driven
release by a supervisory authority.

6.2
Condition Verification

Condition verification may be instantiated via two complementary
mechanisms:

Trusted Third-Party (TTP) Verifiers.

For legally binding conditions, certified entities (e.g., notaries,
regulatory bodies) act as verifiers, providing cryptographically
signed condition-fulfillment proofs to custodians.

Decentralized Oracles.

For transparent, tamper-resistant conditions (e.g., time locks,
cross-chain events), decentralized oracle
networks
[
8
]
can replace TTPs, providing
condition verification without single points of failure. Oracle
manipulation risks are mitigated via multi-source data aggregation.

7
Security Analysis

This section provides formal security analysis of
CT-DAP
. We first
specify the canonical encoding used by
\mathsf{Derive}
, then state
three auxiliary lemmas that capture the individual cryptographic
advantages, and finally prove the main security theorems by composing
these lemmas with explicit advantage bounds.

7.1
Canonical Context Encoding for
\mathsf{Derive}

To ensure unambiguous domain separation across implementations, we
require a
canonical
byte-level encoding of the context tuple
passed to HKDF. Specifically, for a context

\mathsf{Ctx}_{j}=(\mathsf{AlgID},\,\mathsf{Domain},\,\mathsf{Index})
,
the
\mathsf{Derive}
function computes:

\displaystyle\mathsf{PRK}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{HKDF\text{-}Extract}(\mathsf{salt}=\texttt{"CT-DAP-v1"},\;\mathsf{IKM}=\mathsf{REV}_{\mathrm{root}})

(3)

\displaystyle\mathsf{Key}_{\mathcal{AP}_{j}}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{HKDF\text{-}Expand}(\mathsf{PRK},\;\mathsf{info}=\mathsf{Encode}(\mathsf{Ctx}_{j}),\;L)

(4)

where the info string is the length-prefixed concatenation:

\mathsf{Encode}(\mathsf{Ctx}_{j})=\langle|\mathsf{AlgID}|\rangle_{2}\,\|\,\mathsf{AlgID}\,\|\,\langle|\mathsf{Domain}|\rangle_{2}\,\|\,\mathsf{Domain}\,\|\,\langle\mathsf{Index}\rangle_{4}

with
\langle\cdot\rangle_{k}
denoting big-endian encoding into
k

bytes. The fixed-width length prefixes ensure that no two distinct
tuples
(\mathsf{AlgID},\mathsf{Domain},\mathsf{Index})\neq(\mathsf{AlgID}^{\prime},\mathsf{Domain}^{\prime},\mathsf{Index}^{\prime})
produce the
same info string, preventing domain-separation collisions. The static
salt
"CT-DAP-v1"
binds the derivation to this protocol
version; future versions increment the version tag.

Lemma 7.1

(Sealing Indistinguishability)
.

For any two roots

We reduce to the IND-CPA security of AES-256-GCM-SIV.

Lemma 7.2

(Credential Brute-Force Resistance)
.

Let the composite credential be

\mathsf{Cred}_{\mathrm{composite}}=\mathsf{Argon2id}\!\bigl(\mathsf{UserCred}\!\,\|\,\!\mathsf{AdminFactor}_{1}\!\,\|\,\!\cdots\!\,\|\,\!\mathsf{AdminFactor}_{n};\;s;\;m,t,p\bigr)

where at least one component has min-entropy
\geq\kappa
bits
and
s
is a 128-bit per-path random salt. For any adversary

\mathcal{A}
with at-most-
Q
queries to an Argon2id evaluation oracle
(each costing
\geq m
bytes of memory):

\mathrm{Adv}_{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathrm{MH}}(\lambda)\;\leq\;\frac{Q}{2^{\kappa}}

The per-path salt ensures that work performed against target

(s_{j},\mathsf{SA}_{j})
provides no non-trivial advantage against a
distinct target
(s_{k},\mathsf{SA}_{k})
with
s_{k}\neq s_{j}
: the
adversary cannot amortize queries across targets and must
restart with an independent budget of
Q
evaluations per
target.

Proof.

We model Argon2id as a random oracle
\mathcal{O}
that, on
each fresh query
(x,s,m,t,p)
, returns a uniformly random
output in
\{0,1\}^{256}
.

Single-target bound.

The adversary
\mathcal{A}
knows at most
n-1
of the
n+1

credential components
\{\mathsf{UserCred},\mathsf{AdminFactor}_{1},\ldots,\mathsf{AdminFactor}_{n}\}
. The
missing component has min-entropy
\geq\kappa
, so each
query to
\mathcal{O}
with a guessed value matches the true

\mathsf{Cred}_{\mathrm{composite}}
with probability at most

2^{-\kappa}
. Over
Q
queries, a union bound gives

\Pr[\exists\,i\leq Q:\mathcal{O}(x_{i},s,m,t,p)=\mathsf{Cred}_{\mathrm{composite}}]\;\leq\;\frac{Q}{2^{\kappa}}

Memory-hardness cost.

Each Argon2id evaluation requires
\geq m
bytes of working
memory
[
2
,
16
]
. An adversary with total memory

M
can execute at most
\lfloor M/m\rfloor
parallel
evaluations per time step, bounding the achievable query rate
and making large
Q
resource-prohibitive.

Cross-target independence.

The random salt
s_{j}
is drawn independently for each path.
Since
\mathcal{O}
is modelled as a random oracle, queries
with salt
s_{j}
and salt
s_{k}\neq s_{j}
produce independent
outputs. Hence work performed against target
(s_{j},\mathsf{SA}_{j})

yields no non-trivial advantage against
(s_{k},\mathsf{SA}_{k})
: the
adversary must spend an independent budget of
Q
evaluations
per target.
∎

Remark 7.1

(On the random oracle model for Argon2id)
.

The proof above models Argon2id as a random oracle, which is a
standard idealization shared by the Argon2 specification
itself
[
2
]
and RFC 9106
[
16
]
. In practice,
Argon2id is
not
a random oracle; its security rests on

memory-hardness
—the property that any algorithm
computing the function faster than the honest evaluator must use
comparable memory. Formalizing memory-hardness in the standard
model requires graph-pebbling-based
frameworks
[
29
]
, which yield asymptotically
weaker but more realistic bounds of the form

\mathrm{cost}(\mathcal{A})\geq\Omega(m\cdot t)
per evaluation.

Our ROM-based bound
Q/2^{\kappa}
should therefore be
interpreted as an
upper bound on the online query
advantage
, complemented by the economic argument that each
query costs
\geq m
bytes of memory. A full standard-model
analysis of Argon2id’s memory-hardness is an independent
research problem beyond the scope of this work; we refer the
reader to Alwen and Blocki
[
29
]
for the
state-of-the-art treatment.

Lemma 7.3

(PRF Security of
\mathsf{Derive}
)
.

Let
\mathsf{Derive}
be instantiated as HKDF-SHA256 per

subsection 7.1
. For any PPT
\mathcal{A}

q

\mathrm{Adv}_{\mathcal{A},\,\mathrm{HKDF}}^{\mathrm{PRF}}(\lambda)\;\leq\;q\cdot\mathrm{Adv}_{\mathrm{HMAC\text{-}SHA256}}^{\mathrm{PRF}}(\lambda)

\mathsf{negl}(\lambda)

[
4
]
.

Proof.

We reduce to the PRF security of HMAC-SHA256.

Structure of HKDF.

HKDF consists of two phases
[
4
]
:

\mathsf{PRK}=\mathrm{HKDF\text{-}Extract}(\mathsf{salt},\mathsf{IKM})
and

\mathsf{OKM}=\mathrm{HKDF\text{-}Expand}(\mathsf{PRK},\mathsf{info},L)
.
For a fixed
\mathsf{PRK}
,
\mathrm{HKDF\text{-}Expand}
is
an iterated application of
\mathrm{HMAC\text{-}SHA256}
keyed
by
\mathsf{PRK}
. It is therefore a PRF from the info space
to
\{0,1\}^{L}
, with advantage bounded by the PRF advantage of
HMAC-SHA256.

Hybrid argument.

Consider a sequence of
q+1
hybrid games

H_{0},H_{1},\ldots,H_{q}
. In
H_{0}
, all
q
outputs

are computed honestly via

H_{i}
, the first
i
outputs are replaced by
independent uniform random strings, while the remaining

q-i
are computed honestly. In
H_{q}
, all outputs are
uniformly random.

The canonical encoding (
subsection 7.1
) ensures
that distinct contexts
\mathsf{Ctx}_{j}\neq\mathsf{Ctx}_{k}
produce distinct
info strings
\mathsf{Encode}(\mathsf{Ctx}_{j})\neq\mathsf{Encode}(\mathsf{Ctx}_{k})
. Therefore each transition

H_{i-1}\to H_{i}
replaces one PRF output on a fresh input
with a random string, which is indistinguishable by

\mathrm{Adv}_{\mathrm{HMAC\text{-}SHA256}}^{\mathrm{PRF}}
.
Summing over
q
transitions:

\mathrm{Adv}_{\mathcal{A},\,\mathrm{HKDF}}^{\mathrm{PRF}}(\lambda)\;\leq\;q\cdot\mathrm{Adv}_{\mathrm{HMAC\text{-}SHA256}}^{\mathrm{PRF}}(\lambda)

In the random oracle model for SHA-256, the right-hand side is

\mathsf{negl}(\lambda)
for polynomial
q
.
∎

for distinct contexts

\mathsf{Ctx}_{j}\neq\mathsf{Ctx}_{k}
using the canonical encoding of

subsection 7.1
.

2.

The adversary receives
\mathsf{Key}_{\mathcal{AP}_{j}}
(i.e., path
j
is
compromised or activated).

3.

The challenger samples
b\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\$}}{{\leftarrow}}\{0,1\}
. If
b=0
,
it sends
\mathsf{Key}_{\mathcal{AP}_{k}}
; if
b=1
, it sends a uniformly random
key
K^{*}
of the same length.

4.

\mathcal{A}
outputs a guess
b^{\prime}
.

5.

\mathcal{A}
wins if
b^{\prime}=b
.

Theorem 7.5

(Cross-Path Isolation)
.

Under
Assumption 3.4
(ii), for any PPT adversary
\mathcal{A}
:

\bigl|\Pr[b^{\prime}=b]-\tfrac{1}{2}\bigr|\;\leq\;\mathrm{Adv}^{\mathrm{PRF}}_{\mathrm{HKDF}}(\lambda)\;\leq\;\mathsf{negl}(\lambda)

Proof.

Let
\mathsf{PRK}=\mathrm{HKDF\text{-}Extract}(\texttt{"CT-DAP-v1"},\mathsf{REV}_{\mathrm{root}})
. Then:

\displaystyle\mathsf{Key}_{\mathcal{AP}_{j}}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{HKDF\text{-}Expand}(\mathsf{PRK},\,\mathsf{Encode}(\mathsf{Ctx}_{j}),\,L)

\displaystyle\mathsf{Key}_{\mathcal{AP}_{k}}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{HKDF\text{-}Expand}(\mathsf{PRK},\,\mathsf{Encode}(\mathsf{Ctx}_{k}),\,L)

Since
\mathsf{Ctx}_{j}\neq\mathsf{Ctx}_{k}
and
\mathsf{Encode}
is injective
(
subsection 7.1
), the two info strings are distinct.

Suppose
\mathcal{A}
distinguishes
\mathsf{Key}_{\mathcal{AP}_{k}}
from random given

\mathsf{Key}_{\mathcal{AP}_{j}}
with advantage
\epsilon
. We construct a PRF
distinguisher
\mathcal{D}
with oracle access to either

F_{\mathsf{PRK}}(\cdot)
or a truly random function
R(\cdot)
.

\mathcal{D}
queries
\mathsf{Encode}(\mathsf{Ctx}_{j})
and

\mathsf{Encode}(\mathsf{Ctx}_{k})
, forwards the first response to
\mathcal{A}

as
\mathsf{Key}_{\mathcal{AP}_{j}}
, and uses
\mathcal{A}
’s distinguishing output on the
second response. This yields

\mathrm{Adv}_{\mathcal{D}}^{\mathrm{PRF}}\geq\epsilon
,
contradicting
Lemma 7.3
.
∎

7.5
Trigger-Restrictiveness and Release Transcript Binding

A key concern is that an adversary might replay or confuse release
transcripts to trick a custodian into releasing
\mathsf{AdminFactor}_{i}
under
conditions that were not genuinely satisfied. We formalize the
required binding between the verifier’s attestation and the
activation inputs.

Definition 7.3

(Release Transcript)
.

For each factor release, the condition verifier
\mathcal{V}

produces a signed attestation:

\mathsf{Rel}_{j,i}=\mathsf{Sign}\bigl(\mathsf{sk}_{\mathcal{V}},\;(\mathsf{PathID}_{j}\,\|\,\mathsf{ConditionHash}\,\|\,\mathsf{Timestamp}\,\|\,\mathsf{Nonce})\bigr)

where
\mathsf{PathID}_{j}=H(\mathsf{SA}_{\mathrm{root}}\,\|\,\mathsf{Ctx}_{j})

binds the attestation to a specific sealed artifact and path
context, and
\mathsf{Nonce}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\$}}{{\leftarrow}}\{0,1\}^{128}
is a
single-use value.

Definition 7.4

(Trigger-Restrictiveness Game)
.

The adversary
\mathcal{A}
wins

\mathsf{Game}_{\mathrm{TR}}^{\mathcal{A}}(\lambda)
if it can produce
a valid release transcript
\mathsf{Rel}^{*}_{j,i}
that causes
custodian
\mathcal{C}_{i}
to release
\mathsf{AdminFactor}_{i}
for path
j
, when
the condition for path
j
has not been satisfied.

Theorem 7.6

(Trigger-Restrictiveness)
.

Under the EUF-CMA security of the verifier’s signature scheme,
the collision resistance of
H
(
Assumption 3.4
(iv)
),
and
Assumption 3.3
, for any PPT adversary
\mathcal{A}
that
issues at most
q_{\mathrm{rel}}=\mathrm{poly}(\lambda)

release queries against a nonce space of size

N=2^{\lambda}
:

\Pr\bigl[\mathsf{Game}_{\mathrm{TR}}^{\mathcal{A}}(\lambda)=1\bigr]\;\leq\;\underbrace{\mathrm{Adv}^{\mathrm{EUF\text{-}CMA}}_{\mathsf{Sig}}(\lambda)}_{\text{forgery}}\;+\;\underbrace{\mathrm{Adv}^{\mathrm{CR}}_{H}(\lambda)}_{\text{cross-path collision}}\;+\;\underbrace{\frac{q_{\mathrm{rel}}^{2}}{2^{\lambda+1}}}_{\text{nonce collision}}

The first two terms are
\mathsf{negl}(\lambda)
by assumption.
For the third term, since
q_{\mathrm{rel}}=\mathrm{poly}(\lambda)
:

\frac{q_{\mathrm{rel}}^{2}}{2^{\lambda+1}}\;\leq\;\frac{\mathrm{poly}(\lambda)^{2}}{2^{\lambda+1}}\;=\;\mathsf{negl}(\lambda)

so the entire bound is negligible.
The three terms correspond to distinct attack strategies:

•

Forgery:
producing a valid signature without the
verifier’s cooperation.

•

Cross-path replay:
finding a hash collision

H(\mathsf{SA}_{\mathrm{root}}\,\|\,\mathsf{Ctx}_{j})=H(\mathsf{SA}_{\mathrm{root}}\,\|\,\mathsf{Ctx}_{k})
for
j\neq k

to replay an attestation across paths.

•

Temporal replay:
reusing a nonce accepted by the
custodian’s local de-duplication state, bounded by the
birthday bound over the nonce space.

Proof.

The custodian
\mathcal{C}_{i}
verifies
\mathsf{Rel}_{j,i}
by
checking:
(a) the signature under
\mathsf{pk}_{\mathcal{V}}
,
(b)
\mathsf{PathID}_{j}
matches the locally stored

H(\mathsf{SA}_{\mathrm{root}}\,\|\,\mathsf{Ctx}_{j})
, and
(c) the nonce has not been previously accepted.
We bound the probability that
\mathcal{A}
bypasses each check.

Forgery resistance (check a):

Producing a valid
\mathsf{Rel}^{*}_{j,i}
without the verifier’s
cooperation requires forging a signature under

\mathsf{sk}_{\mathcal{V}}
, contributing

\mathrm{Adv}^{\mathrm{EUF\text{-}CMA}}_{\mathsf{Sig}}
.

Cross-path replay (check b):

A valid attestation
\mathsf{Rel}_{k,i}
for path
k
contains

\mathsf{PathID}_{k}=H(\mathsf{SA}_{\mathrm{root}}\,\|\,\mathsf{Ctx}_{k})
.
Since
\mathsf{Ctx}_{j}\neq\mathsf{Ctx}_{k}
implies
\mathsf{PathID}_{j}\neq\mathsf{PathID}_{k}
except with probability

\mathrm{Adv}^{\mathrm{CR}}_{H}
(by

Assumption 3.4
(iv)
), the custodian’s check (b)
rejects the replayed transcript.

Temporal replay (check c):

The custodian maintains a local set of accepted nonces. A
nonce collision among the
q_{\mathrm{rel}}
release transcripts
occurs with probability at most
q_{\mathrm{rel}}^{2}/2N
by
the birthday bound. Absent a collision, each legitimate nonce is
accepted at most once, so replay of a previously used attestation
is deterministically rejected.

A union bound over the three failure events yields the stated
inequality.
∎

7.6
Stateless Revocation

Definition 7.5

(Stateless Revocation Property)
.

We say
CT-DAP
provides
stateless revocation
if, after

\mathsf{Revoke}(i)
is executed for some
\mathsf{AdminFactor}_{i}
:

(i)

No PPT adversary can reconstruct

\mathsf{Cred}_{\mathrm{composite}}
(and hence
\mathsf{REV}_{\mathrm{root}}

or
\mathsf{Key}_{\mathcal{AP}_{j}}
) for any path depending on
\mathsf{AdminFactor}_{i}
.

(ii)

The revocation does not require any state update, broadcast,
or coordination beyond the custodian performing the destruction.

(iii)

Paths that do not depend on
\mathsf{AdminFactor}_{i}
remain unaffected.

Theorem 7.7

(Stateless Revocation)
.

Under
Assumption 3.4
, destruction of
\mathsf{AdminFactor}_{i}
ensures:

\textsf{ACE-GF}.\mathsf{Unseal}(\mathsf{params},\mathsf{SA}_{\mathrm{root}},\mathsf{Cred}_{\mathrm{partial}})=\bot\quad\forall\,\mathsf{Cred}_{\mathrm{partial}}\neq\mathsf{Cred}_{\mathrm{composite}}

Proof.

After destruction of
\mathsf{AdminFactor}_{i}
, the adversary can construct at most

\mathsf{Cred}^{\prime}=\mathsf{Argon2id}\!\bigl(\mathsf{UserCred}\!\,\|\,\!\mathsf{AdminFactor}_{1}\!\,\|\,\!\cdots\!\,\|\,\!\mathsf{AdminFactor}^{\prime}_{i}\!\,\|\,\!\cdots\!\,\|\,\!\mathsf{AdminFactor}_{n};\;s;\;m,t,p\bigr)

where
\mathsf{AdminFactor}^{\prime}_{i}\neq\mathsf{AdminFactor}_{i}
.

By the correctness of AES-256-GCM-SIV authenticated encryption,
decryption with an incorrect key yields
\bot
(authentication
tag verification fails deterministically). Since
\mathsf{Cred}^{\prime}\neq\mathsf{Cred}_{\mathrm{composite}}
, the derived decryption key differs,
and
\mathsf{Unseal}
returns
\bot
.

The revocation is stateless because it requires only local
destruction of
\mathsf{AdminFactor}_{i}
by
\mathcal{C}_{i}
—no revocation lists,
state broadcasts, or re-keying are necessary. Independence of
other paths follows from context isolation (
Theorem 7.5
).
∎

7.7
Activation Correctness

Proposition 7.8

(Activation Correctness)
.

If all credential components are correctly provided, then

\mathsf{Activate}
returns the correct control key with
overwhelming probability:

\Pr\bigl[\mathsf{Activate}(\Pi,\mathsf{SA},\mathsf{UserCred},\{\mathsf{AdminFactor}_{i}\},\mathsf{Ctx}_{j})\neq\mathsf{Key}_{\mathcal{AP}_{j}}\bigr]\;\leq\;p_{\mathrm{unseal}}+p_{\mathrm{derive}}

where
p_{\mathrm{unseal}}
and
p_{\mathrm{derive}}
are the
failure probabilities of the underlying primitives.

Proof.

We decompose the failure event

E=\{\mathsf{Activate}\neq\mathsf{Key}_{\mathcal{AP}_{j}}\}
into two disjoint
sub-events:

E_{1}
: Unseal failure despite correct credential.

Given the correct
\mathsf{Cred}_{\mathrm{composite}}
,

\mathsf{Unseal}
decrypts
\mathsf{SA}_{\mathrm{root}}
using
AES-256-GCM-SIV. By the correctness of the AEAD scheme,
decryption with the correct key succeeds deterministically
(AES-GCM-SIV is a deterministic AEAD with no decryption error
for correctly formed ciphertexts
[
3
]
). Thus

p_{\mathrm{unseal}}=0
under the assumption that

\mathsf{SA}_{\mathrm{root}}
is not corrupted in storage. If storage
integrity is not assumed,
p_{\mathrm{unseal}}
is bounded by
the probability of undetected bit-flip (caught by the GCM-SIV
authentication tag with probability
\leq 2^{-128}
).

E_{2}
: Derive failure despite correct REV.

\mathsf{Derive}
is a deterministic function
(HKDF-SHA256)—given
\mathsf{REV}_{\mathrm{root}}
and
\mathsf{Ctx}_{j}
, it
always produces the same
\mathsf{Key}_{\mathcal{AP}_{j}}
. Thus

p_{\mathrm{derive}}=0
.

Combining:
\Pr[E]\leq 0+0=0
under perfect storage, or

\Pr[E]\leq 2^{-128}
under storage bit-flip model.
∎

Custodial solutions delegate control to trusted intermediaries.

9
Illustrative Scenarios

To demonstrate the generality of
CT-DAP
, this section presents
scenarios across different operational and legal contexts.

9.1
Scenario A: Conditional Inheritance

An asset owner establishes multiple dormant authorization paths
corresponding to designated beneficiaries. Each path includes a
user-held credential and an administrative authorization factor held
by an independent custodian (e.g., an estate attorney).

During the owner’s lifetime, all inheritance-related paths remain
cryptographically inactive. Upon verification of a legally recognized
inheritance event, the custodian releases the corresponding
authorization factor. This activates the path, enabling the
beneficiary to exercise cryptographic control.

No asset migration or reassignment occurs; control is enabled solely
through path activation.

9.2
Scenario B: Regulated or Supervised Control

An organization operates assets subject to regulatory oversight.
Control rights require both an internal credential and an
authorization factor held by a supervisory entity.

Under normal conditions, the supervisory factor remains available.
In the event of regulatory intervention, the supervisory factor is
destroyed, immediately disabling control without modifying asset
ownership or cryptographic identity.

9.3
Scenario C: Delegated and Time-Bound Control

An asset owner delegates control to a third party for a limited
duration. A dormant authorization path is established with a
time-conditioned authorization factor.

The factor is released only when temporal conditions are satisfied.
Upon expiration, the factor is destroyed, rendering the path
permanently inactive—without requiring continuous trust or post-hoc
revocation procedures.

9.4
Scenario D: Multi-Party Governance

A DAO or corporate treasury requires
m
-of-
n
approval for asset
operations. Each of
n
governance members holds an authorization
factor. The path is activated only when
m
factors are
simultaneously released, with the remaining
n-m
factors remaining
withheld.

Unlike traditional multi-sig, individual governance paths can be
independently revoked by destroying a single factor, without requiring
resharing or on-chain reconfiguration.

10
Discussion

10.1
Practical Deployment Considerations

10.1.1
Custodian Trust and Incentive Alignment

The system relies on custodians to faithfully hold and conditionally
release
\mathsf{AdminFactor}_{i}
. Practical deployment should include:

•

Legal agreements binding custodians to predefined release
conditions.

•

Multi-custodian sharding of
\mathsf{AdminFactor}_{i}
(e.g., threshold
cryptography for factor release) to mitigate single custodian
failure.

•

Auditable logs of factor release/destruction operations,
enabled by
ACE-GF
’s compatibility with trusted execution
environments (TEEs)
[
9
]
.

10.1.2
Condition Verification Mechanisms

The method’s effectiveness depends on accurate verification of
external trigger conditions. For legally binding conditions, TTP
verifiers (e.g., notaries, regulatory bodies) provide
cryptographically signed attestations. For transparent,
tamper-resistant conditions, decentralized oracle
networks
[
8
]
can replace TTPs.

10.1.3
Interoperability with Existing Asset Systems

CT-DAP
is agnostic to underlying asset types due to
ACE-GF
’s
context-isolated derivation:

•

Blockchain-based assets:

\mathsf{Key}_{\mathcal{AP}}
can be mapped to standard account addresses (e.g.,
Secp256k1 for Bitcoin/Ethereum) via
ACE-GF
’s legacy key
embedding mechanism.

•

Centralized digital assets:

\mathsf{Key}_{\mathcal{AP}}
can serve as an API authentication token, with asset
providers enforcing cryptographic control via signature
verification.

10.1.4
Legacy Migration and Address Preservation

The system supports a “legacy-anchored” mode that imports
entropy from existing BIP-39 mnemonics to produce a
legacy-anchored
\mathsf{REV}_{\mathrm{root}}
. Using standard
BIP-32/SLIP-10 derivation paths, the method ensures deterministic
address parity with the original wallet, enabling users to wrap
existing assets into the
CT-DAP
framework without on-chain
migration.

10.2
Fault Tolerance via Parallel Authorization Paths

A key concern with destructible factors is permanent asset lockout
due to accidental loss.
CT-DAP
mitigates this by supporting

parallel, redundant authorization paths
.

Formally, an asset owner can establish
m
distinct authorization
paths:

S_{\mathcal{AP}}=\{\mathcal{AP}_{1},\mathcal{AP}_{2},\ldots,\mathcal{AP}_{m}\}

where each
\mathcal{AP}_{j}
is defined by a unique credential combination

\mathsf{Cred}_{\mathrm{composite},j}
and context
\mathsf{Ctx}_{j}
.

This architecture provides:

1.

Redundant Recovery:

A primary path
\mathcal{AP}_{\mathrm{primary}}
for daily operations
and emergency recovery paths
\mathcal{AP}_{\mathrm{recovery}}
.
Destruction of a factor in
\mathcal{AP}_{\mathrm{primary}}
does not
affect
\mathcal{AP}_{\mathrm{recovery}}
.

2.

Cryptographic Isolation:

By
Theorem 7.5
, compromise of one path does not leak
information about other paths.

3.

Policy-Based Availability:

Different paths can require varying security levels (e.g.,
a “Succession Path” with more factors than a “User Consent
Path”).

10.3
Limitations

10.3.1
Credential Entropy Dependence

The security of
\mathsf{Cred}_{\mathrm{composite}}
inherits
ACE-GF
’s
reliance on credential entropy. Low-entropy
\mathsf{UserCred}
or
\mathsf{AdminFactor}_{i}

increases vulnerability to offline brute-force attacks.
Deployment must enforce minimum entropy requirements (e.g., 128-bit
min-entropy for
\mathsf{UserCred}
) and leverage Argon2id’s memory-hard
properties.

10.3.2
Side-Channel Attack Vulnerabilities

While
ACE-GF
ensures
\mathsf{REV}_{\mathrm{root}}
is ephemeral in memory,
unsealing and derivation operations remain susceptible to side-channel
attacks (e.g., cold-boot attacks, timing analysis) in untrusted
environments. This can be mitigated by executing critical operations
within TEEs (e.g., Intel SGX, ARM TrustZone)
[
9
]
.

10.3.3
Irreversible Revocation Risks

Revocation via
\mathsf{AdminFactor}_{i}
destruction is permanent. Practical systems
should implement secure backup mechanisms for
\mathsf{AdminFactor}_{i}
(e.g.,
encrypted sharding) and recovery paths as described in

subsection 10.2
.

10.3.4
Condition Verifier Trust

The method’s security depends on the integrity of condition verifiers
(
Assumption 3.3
). A compromised verifier can trigger premature
factor release. Multi-verifier attestation and threshold verification
schemes can mitigate this risk but add operational complexity.

10.4
Future Research Directions

1.

Decentralized Condition Verification:

Explore DAO-based condition verification using community-governed
smart contracts, with zero-knowledge proofs for privacy
preservation.

2.

Post-Quantum Optimization:

Optimize derivation efficiency for PQC algorithms (e.g.,
ML-DSA, ML-KEM), including tailored HKDF parameters and
validation of context isolation under quantum threats.

3.

Cross-Chain and Multi-Asset Control:

Standardize context tuples across blockchains and enable
interoperable
\mathsf{AdminFactor}_{i}
release via cross-chain messaging
protocols (e.g., IBC).

4.

Dynamic Authorization Paths:

Support dynamic modification of
\mathsf{AdminFactor}_{i}
sets (e.g.,
adding/removing custodians) without re-deriving

\mathsf{REV}_{\mathrm{root}}
or migrating assets.

5.

Formal Verification:

Apply automated verification tools (e.g., ProVerif, Tamarin)
to formally verify the protocol against a broader class of
adversarial behaviors.

12
Conclusion

This paper introduced
Condition-Triggered Dormant Authorization
Paths
(
CT-DAP
), a cryptographic asset control method based on
destructible authorization factors. By modeling control rights as
dormant authorization paths that are
activated
rather than

transferred
,
CT-DAP
decouples asset identity from
condition-dependent control.

We formalized the system model with explicit trust assumptions and
algorithm specifications, and proved three core security properties—
unauthorized control resistance, cross-path isolation, and stateless
revocation—under standard cryptographic assumptions. We instantiated
the method using the ACE-GF framework and demonstrated its applicability
across regulated custody, inheritance planning, delegated control, and
multi-party governance.

More broadly, this work demonstrates that cryptographic asset control
need not be limited to static key possession models.
Condition-triggered activation of dormant authorization paths offers a
general abstraction for expressing legally compliant and
cryptographically enforced control semantics across diverse operational
and regulatory environments.
