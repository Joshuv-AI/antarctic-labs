FDX1 Depletion Activates CD8 + T Cell Antitumor Immunity by Promoting DMBT1 Secretion in Cuproptosis of Colorectal Cancer
Authors: Jieqiong Wu, Zaixi Lian, Mingzhou Li, Xingtu Qin, Xiaoyu Lei, Lihua Yang, Lin Bing, Qiuyue Li, Lingyan Zhou, Zhiyang Ma, Xuemei Xu, Jinghao Huang, Zilong Chen, Lijie Chen, Lingjie Zhang, Zhiyuan Xiao, Xiangyu Jian, Xiaoqin Zhang, Yanqing Ding, Yaping Ye, Li Liang, Hongli Jiao, Shuyang Wang

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCuproptosis, a copper‐dependent form of regulated cell death, has been documented in colorectal cancer (CRC), yet its impact on the tumor immune microenvironment remains incompletely understood. Here, we demonstrate that elesclomol‐induced cuproptosis potently activates innate and adaptive antitumor immunity, markedly enhancing CD8+ T cell cytotoxicity while attenuating exhaustion. Mechanistically, FDX1, which correlates inversely with CD8+ T cell infiltration and predicts poor prognosis, functions as a core regulator of CD8+ T cell functionality during cuproptosis. FDX1 promotes CRC proliferation and metastasis while suppressing CD8+ T cell cytokine production. We further show that FDX1 binds DICER1 to reduce DMBT1 mRNA stability, with FDX1 and DMBT1 expression exhibiting significant negative correlation in CRC tissues. Our findings establish the FDX1–DICER1–DMBT1 axis as a critical regulator of CRC progression and immune evasion, suggesting that therapeutic targeting of this pathway could enhance antitumor immunity and overcome resistance to existing immunotherapies.Keywords: CD8+ T cell, colorectal cancer, cuproptosis, DMBT1, FDX1

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) ranks as the third most prevalent malignancy worldwide [1]. Despite advances in multimodal therapies, the prognosis for advanced CRC remains dismal. Approximately 20% of patients present with distant metastases at diagnosis, while 40% of those with initially localized disease experience relapse after curative resection. The 5‐year survival rate for metastatic CRC is below 20%, underscoring the urgent need to develop robust prognostic tools and precision‐targeted therapies to improve outcomes.

Cuproptosis is a recently identified form of copper‐induced cell death, mediated by endoplasmic reticulum (ER) toxicity triggered by excessive copper ions [2, 3]. This process critically depends on the mitochondrial reductase ferredoxin 1 (FDX1) [4, 5, 6, 7]. In gastric cancer, copper stress induces lactylation of METTL16 at lysine‐229, thereby promoting m6A modification of FDX1 mRNA and enhancing cuproptosis [8]. Similarly, ferroptosis inducers (e.g., sorafenib and erastin) suppress the degradation of FDX1 protein and deplete glutathione as a key copper chelator by inhibiting cystine uptake, thereby sensitizing hepatocellular carcinoma cells to copper‐triggered cell death [9]. These studies highlight cuproptosis induction as a promising anticancer strategy, yet its function in CRC remains poorly characterized [10]. The tumor microenvironment (TME) plays a pivotal role in CRC initiation and progression. Mitochondria, which regulate cellular bioenergetics, biosynthesis, and signaling, represent an attractive therapeutic target [11]. As a mitochondrial copper‐dependent regulated cell death mechanism, cuproptosis is distinct from apoptosis and may offer novel therapeutic potential for CRC. However, its impact on prognosis and TME remodeling remains unclear. A systematic investigation of cuproptosis‐mediated immune infiltration is essential for elucidating CRC pathogenesis and predicting immunotherapeutic response.

Here, through integrated bioinformatics analysis and experimental validation, we demonstrate that dysregulation of cuproptosis‐related molecules (e.g., FDX1, DLAT, and DLD) correlates with poor survival in CRC patients. Notably, FDX1 is up‐regulated in CRC tissues compared to normal counterparts and promotes invasive behavior. Our prior in vivo studies revealed that cuproptosis induction in MC38 subcutaneous xenograft models (C57 mice) significantly decelerates tumor growth. Flow cytometry analysis further indicated enhanced CD8+ T cell cytotoxicity and reduced exhaustion. Nevertheless, the mechanistic interplay between cuproptosis in CRC cells and immune microenvironment modulation remains elusive. This study aims to delineate the effects and underlying mechanism of cuproptosis on the TME, thereby providing a theoretical foundation for future CRC immunotherapy trials.

Materials and Methods

Mice

C57BL/6 and BALB/c mouse strains were procured from the Guangdong Medical Laboratory Animal Center. OT‐1 mice were obtained from Shanghai Model Organisms. All experimental animals, aged between 4 and 7 weeks, were maintained in specific pathogen‐free (SPF) facilities at Southern Medical University. All animal experiments were performed in compliance with the ethical guidelines and protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Southern Medical University in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines.

Cell Culture

Two colorectal cancer cell lines were employed: human RKO cells (ATCC CRL‐2577) and murine MC38 cells. In accordance with ATCC recommendations, both lines were maintained in DMEM (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (HyClone, Logan, UT), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin at 37°C with 5% CO2. The stable cell lines were established as previously described [12].

Vectors Construction and Retroviral Infection

The FDX1 truncated fragments were generated and constructed as previously described [13]. The primers used are shown in Table S1. Retroviral production and infection were performed as previously described [14]. Stable cell lines of FDX1 overexpression or silencing were selected with 3 μg/mL puromycin in medium for 7 days, and stable cell lines of DMBT1 silencing were selected with 500 μg/mL G418 in medium for 21 days.

Co‐Immunoprecipitation (Co‐IP) and Mass Spectrometry Analysis

Lysates from RKO cells transfected with FLAG‐tagged FDX1 or control vector were prepared using lysis buffer (150 mM NaCl, 10 mM HEPES, pH 7.4, 1% NP‐40). The cell lysates were incubated with FLAG affinity agarose (Sigma‐Aldrich) for 12 h at 4°C. The beads were washed 5 times with immunoprecipitation washing buffer (10 mM HEPES, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% NP‐40, pH 7.4). The proteins were collected with elution buffer, which was pooled and concentrated in a 10‐kDa MW cut‐off filter unit (Millipore) to 30 μL in volume. Proteins were separated by SDS polyacrylamide gels and stained with Coomassie blue. Specific bands were excised for MS analysis.

Chromatin Isolation by RNA Purification (CHIRP)

Probe Design and Cell Cross‐linking: A tiling array of biotinylated antisense DNA oligonucleotides complementary to the full‐length sequence of DMBT1 mRNA was synthesized. Probes were split into two independent pools (“Odd” and “Even”) to eliminate non‐specific background signals, while non‐targeting LacZ probes served as the negative control. RKO cells were fixed with 1% glutaraldehyde in PBS at room temperature for 10 min to preserve native RNA‐protein interactions. The cross‐linking reaction was quenched by adding 1.25 M glycine to a final concentration of 125 mM for 5 min. Cells were washed twice with ice‐cold PBS, collected by scraping, and pelleted at 2500 g for 5 min before freezing at −80°C. The probes' sequences are summarized in Table S1.

Cell Lysis and Sonication: Cell pellets were resuspended in ChIRP Lysis Buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.0, 10 mM EDTA, 1% SDS) supplemented with protease, PMSF, and Superase‐In RNase inhibitors. The cell lysate was sonicated at 4°C using a focused ultrasonicator until chromatin and high‐molecular‐weight complexes were sufficiently disrupted to maximize protein accessibility. The lysate was cleared via centrifugation at 16,000 g for 10 min at 4°C, and a 1% aliquot of the supernatant was reserved as the Input control.

Hybridization and RNP Capture: The cleared lysate was diluted with 2 volumes of ChIRP Hybridization Buffer (750 mM NaCl, 1% SDS, 50 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.0, 1 mM EDTA, 15% formamide). Fractions were incubated separately with 100 pmol of Odd, Even, or LacZ control probe pools at 37°C for 4 h under constant rotation. Subsequently, pre‐washed Streptavidin C1 magnetic beads (100 μL per 100 pmol of probes) were added to capture the biotinylated probe‐DMBT1‐protein complexes during a 30‐min incubation at 37°C. Beads were separated magnetically and washed five times with ChIRP Wash Buffer (2× SSC, 0.5% SDS, 1 mM PMSF) at 37°C to remove non‐specific protein binders.

Protein Elution and Identification: To isolate the enriched DMBT1 mRNA‐binding proteins, the washed beads were resuspended in Proteinase K Buffer without Proteinase K, supplemented with RNase A (0.1 mg/mL) and RNase H, and incubated at 37°C for 30 min to digest the RNA scaffold and gently release bound proteins. Released proteins were eluted by boiling in SDS‐PAGE loading buffer and subjected to formal cross‐link reversal. The specific interactome was subsequently analyzed via Liquid Chromatography‐Mass Spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) for de novo RBP discovery or validated via Western Blotting using antibodies against candidate target proteins. Proteins enriched significantly in both Odd and Even pools compared to the LacZ control were defined as high‐confidence DMBT1 mRNA‐binding proteins.

Co‐Immunoprecipitation (Co‐IP) Assays

Cell lysates were prepared from the indicated cells in lysis buffer (150 mM NaCl, 10 mM HEPES, pH 7.4, 1% NP‐40). For endogenous immunoprecipitation, lysates were incubated with anti‐FDX1 or anti‐DICER1 antibodies together with protein G‐conjugated agarose beads. Alternatively, for Flag‐tagged protein pull‐down, lysates were incubated with Flag affinity agarose (Sigma–Aldrich). All incubations were performed at 4°C overnight. The beads were then washed six times with IP wash buffer (150 mM NaCl, 10 mM HEPES, pH 7.4, 0.1% NP‐40). Bound proteins were eluted with 1 M glycine (pH 3.0), and the eluates were immediately neutralized, mixed with sample buffer, denatured, and subjected to Western blot analysis.

RNA Isolation, Reverse Transcription (RT), and Real‐Time PCR

Total RNA extraction, RT‐PCR, and real‐time PCR were performed as previously described [12]. Primers' Sequences are summarized in Table S1.

ELISA

DMBT1 concentrations in cell‐culture supernatants were quantified using a human‐specific DMBT1 ELISA kit (ELK Bio, cat. # ELK3416‐96T) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Each experiment was performed in triplicate and repeated three times.

Cellular Proliferation and Colony Formation Assays

For proliferation assays, 3 × 103 cells per well were seeded in 96‐well plates. After 24 h, 20 μL MTT (5 mg/mL in PBS) was added and incubated at 37°C for 4 h. The medium was aspirated, 150 μL DMSO was added, and plates were shaken at 37°C for 10 min. Absorbance at 570 nm was measured on an 800TS microplate reader (BioTek, USA). For clonogenic assays, 1 × 103 cells per well were plated in 6‐well plates and cultured for 14 days. Colonies were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, stained with 0.5% crystal violet for 30 min, rinsed, and air‐dried. Colonies containing ≥ 50 cells were counted manually.

Cell Migration and Wound Healing Assays

Cell migration was assessed in 24‐well Transwell inserts (8.0 μm pore size, Corning, NY). A total of 1 × 105 cells in medium containing 1% FBS were seeded in the upper chamber, and medium with 10% FBS served as a chemoattractant in the lower chamber. After 24 h, cells on the underside were fixed, stained with 0.1% crystal violet, and counted in ten random 200× fields per insert. Data are expressed as mean ± SEM from three independent experiments. For wound‐healing assays, 1 × 106 cells per well were cultured to 80% confluence in 6‐well plates. After 24 h of serum starvation, linear scratches were generated with a sterile 10 μL pipette tip. Images were acquired at 0, 12, 24, 36, and 48 h under an inverted microscope, and gap closure was quantified using ImageJ. Each experiment was performed in triplicate.

Cell Counting Kit‐8 (CCK‐8) Assay

Cell proliferation in the indicated groups was assessed using the CCK‐8 assay (Glpbio, GK10001) according to the manufacturer's instructions. After the cells adhered to the culture plates, 10 μL of CCK‐8 solution and 90 μL of DMEM medium (total volume 100 μL) were added to each well, and the cells were cultured for an additional 2 h. The absorbance (OD value) of each well was measured daily for 7 consecutive days.

T‐Cell Co‐Culture Assay

FDX1‐silenced FDX1‐overexpressing or FDX1 and DMBT1‐both silenced MC38‐OVA cells (1 × 104 per well) were seeded in 96‐well plates. OT‐1 CD8+ T cells were isolated from spleens of OT‐1 mice using the EasySep Mouse CD8+ T Cell Isolation Kit (Stemcell, 19853A) and co‐cultured with tumor cells at an effector: target ratio of 10:1 in complete medium supplemented with 20 IU/mL IL‐2. FDX1‐overexpressing MC38‐OVA cells were supplemented with recombinant mouse DMBT1 protein (Bioss, bs‐42083P). After 20 h, brefeldin A (5 μg/mL) was added for an additional 4 h to block cytokine secretion. Cells were then harvested, washed, and surface‐stained with anti‐CD8α‐PE for 30 min on ice. Following fixation and permeabilization, intracellular staining was performed using anti‐perforin‐APC and anti‐granzyme B‐FITC before flow cytometric analysis.

Flow Cytometry Analysis

To investigate immune cell infiltration, we first isolated tumor‐infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs). Tumor tissues were dissected, cut into small pieces, and digested in RPMI 1640 medium containing DNase I (100 U/mL, Sigma, D5025), collagenase VI (210 U/mL, Thermo, 17104019), and hyaluronidase (0.5 mg/mL, Sigma, H3506) for 30 min at 37°C. The dissociated cells were passed through a 70 μm strainer, and the supernatant was collected and centrifuged at 1000× g for 10 min. The cell pellet was then resuspended and subjected to density gradient centrifugation using 40% and 70% Percoll. The interphase containing the TILs was harvested and centrifuged at 1000× g for 5 min. The isolated TILs were incubated with the indicated antibodies, then resuspended and analyzed by flow cytometry.

RNA‐Sequencing Analysis

Total RNA was extracted from RKO cells stably expressing empty RKO‐shNC or RKO‐shFDX1 using trizol reagent (Invitrogen) and treated with DNase I to remove genomic DNA. Strand‐specific libraries were prepared with the NEBNext Ultra RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB, USA) following the manufacturer's protocol and indexed to allow multiplexing. Paired‐end 150‐bp reads were generated on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 (Illumina, USA) under the control of Illumina Real‐Time Analysis software. Library construction and sequencing were performed by GenePharma (Shanghai, China). Raw reads were filtered (FastQC v0.11.9) and aligned to GRCh38 using STAR v2.7.10a. Gene‐level expression was quantified as FPKM with featureCounts v2.0.3. Differential expression between groups was determined with DESeq2 (v1.34.0); genes with an adjusted p < 0.05 and |log2 fold change| ≥ 1 were considered significant.

RNA Stability Assay

Transcription was arrested with actinomycin D (5 μg/mL, Sigma‐Aldrich) at the indicated time points (0, 20, 40 min, 1, 2, 3, 4 h). Total RNA was harvested at each interval, and DMBT1 mRNA levels were quantified by qRT‐PCR as described above.

Western Blot

Western blot was performed as previously described [15] using anti‐FDX1 (Proteintech), anti‐DMBT1 (Proteintech), and anti‐DICER1 (Abcam). A mouse monoclonal anti‐α‐Tubulin antibody (Sigma‐Aldrich) and anti‐GAPDH antibody (Proteintech) were used as internal controls.

Immunofluorescence Staining

Immunofluorescence staining of cultured cells was performed as previously described [13]. Briefly, the cells were incubated with primary antibodies against DMBT1. The slides were incubated with FITC‐conjugated goat anti‐rabbit IgG (Jackson Immuno Research Laboratories). The nuclei were stained with DAPI. Images were captured with a confocal laser‐scanning microscope (Olympus FV1000). Data were processed using Adobe Photoshop 7.0 software.

Multiplex Immunofluorescence (mIHC)

Multiplexed immunofluorescence was performed with the Opal 4‐color Manual IHC kit (PerkinElmer, NEL810001KT) per the manufacturer's protocol. Sequential primary antibodies were applied overnight at 4°C: anti‐CD8 followed by anti‐FDX1. After each step, slides were incubated with HRP‐conjugated secondary antibodies and developed with tyramide signal amplification (TSA). Intervening microwave antigen retrieval was performed between TSA cycles. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI for 30 min. Slides were imaged on an LSM 880 confocal microscope (Zeiss); spectral unmixing and signal quantification were performed with ZEN software. Mean fluorescence intensity was calculated as total integrated fluorescence divided by the area of interest.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

IHC was performed as described previously [12]. Briefly, the slides were placed in 10 mM sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0) for antigen retrieval. Endogenous peroxidases were inactivated by 3% hydrogen peroxide (H2O2). Non‐specific signals were blocked using goat serum. Tumor samples were stained with anti‐FDX1, anti‐CD8, and anti‐DMBT1 at 4°C. After overnight incubation, the slides were washed and incubated with secondary antibody (HRP‐polymers) for 30 min at room temperature. After three washes, the slides were stained with DAB substrate (ZSbio). The slides were counterstained with hematoxylin and mounted with mounting medium. The staining intensity of sections was scored in a blinded manner by two independent pathologists.

Tumor Formation and Drug Treatment in Mice

C57BL/6 and BALB/c female mice, 4–5 weeks old and weighing 17–20 g, were obtained from the Laboratory Animal Center of Southern Medical University (Guangzhou, China). To test whether cuproptosis induction affects CRC growth, MC38 or CT26 cells (5 × 105 per mouse) were implanted subcutaneously into the dorsal flank on day 0. Tumor‐bearing mice were randomized 7 days later, when palpable tumors were evident, to receive intraperitoneal injections of vehicle (2% DMSO, 30% PEG300, 5% Tween 80) or elesclomol (80 mg kg−1 formulated in 30% PEG300 + 5% Tween 80) every 48 h. Tumor dimensions were measured with digital calipers, and volumes were calculated as length × width2 × 0.5. Mice were euthanized when tumors reached 2000 mm3 or showed ulceration/necrosis. Excised tumors were photographed, weighed, and divided into three portions: one snap‐frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C for molecular analyses, one dissociated for flow cytometry, and one fixed in 10% neutral‐buffered formalin for immunohistochemistry and H&E staining.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 13.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Two‐tailed Student's t‐test or chi‐square test (χ 2 test) were used to analyze differences among variables. p Value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Elesclomol‐Induced Cuproptosis Remodels the Immune Microenvironment and Enhances Antitumor Immunity in CRC

To evaluate the therapeutic potential of cuproptosis in CRC, we established subcutaneous MC38 and CT26 tumor models in C57BL/6 mice and BALB/c mice. Once tumors reached 100 mm3, mice were treated with the cuproptosis inducer elesclomol. Treatment with elesclomol resulted in significant inhibition of tumor growth in both MC38 (Figure 1A–C) and CT26 (Figure 1D–F) models, as evidenced by reduced tumor volumes and final tumor weights at endpoint measurements.

Comprehensive immune‐microenvironmental profiling by multiparameter flow cytometry revealed substantial alterations in tumor‐infiltrating leukocyte populations following elesclomol treatment. In the MC38 model, elesclomol significantly decreased macrophage abundance while increasing dendritic‐cell (DC) numbers; neutrophils, monocytic (M‐) and polymorphonuclear (PMN‐) Myeloid‐derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), and NK cells remained statistically unchanged (Figure 1G). Interestingly, the CT26 model exhibited a similar decrease in macrophages but demonstrated more extensive immune modulation, including a significant increases in DCs, neutrophils, M‐ and PMN‐MDSCs (Figure 1G). The conserved inverse correlation between macrophage depletion and DCs accumulation across both tumor models suggests a fundamental mechanism of innate immune reprogramming induced by cuproptosis.

Analysis of adaptive immune responses demonstrated consistent enhancement of CD8+ T cells infiltration in both models (MC38 and CT26), accompanied by significant reductions in CD4+ T cells (Figures 1G and S1A). B lymphocyte numbers remained unaffected in either model (Figure 1G). Functional characterization revealed that elesclomol treatment enhanced the cytotoxic potential of tumor‐infiltrating CD8+ T cells, as evidenced by increased perforin and granzyme B production, while simultaneously reducing expression of exhaustion markers programmed cell death protein 1 (PD‐1) and T‐cell Immunoglobulin and Mucin‐domain containing molecule 3 (Tim‐3) (Figure 1H,I). Immunohistochemical validation confirmed these findings, showing significantly reduced tumor cell proliferation (Ki67) and enhanced CD8+ T‐cell infiltration in elesclomol‐treated tumors (Figure S1B). These findings collectively demonstrate that pharmacological induction of cuproptosis through elesclomol treatment induces comprehensive remodeling of the tumor immune microenvironment, characterized by depletion of immunosuppressive macrophages, expansion of antigen‐presenting dendritic cells, and preferential activation of cytotoxic CD8+ T cell responses. The observed enhancement of CD8+ T cell effector function, coupled with reduced exhaustion marker expression, suggests that cuproptosis induction may represent a promising strategy to overcome immunosuppression in colorectal cancer.

FDX1 Is a Critical Mediator of Elesclomol‐Induced Immune Microenvironment Remodeling in CRC

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying elesclomol‐mediated remodeling of the CRC immune microenvironment, we first quantified the expression of ten cuproptosis‐related genes (FDX1, LIAS, LIPT1, DLD, DLAT, PDHA1, PDHB, MTF1, GLS, and CDKN2A) via RT‐qPCR in tumor samples from our initial cohort. Notably, all ten genes exhibited significant downregulation following elesclomol treatment, with FDX1 demonstrating the most substantial reduction (Figure 2A). Consistent with mRNA expression patterns, Western blot and immunohistochemical analyses confirmed a marked decrease in FDX1 protein levels (Figure 2B,C).

Given the pronounced suppression of FDX1 upon elesclomol exposure, we next investigated whether FDX1 is functionally required for elesclomol‐mediated immune microenvironment modulation. We generated MC38 cell lines with stable FDX1 overexpression (MC38‐FDX1) or knockdown using two independent shRNAs (MC38‐shFDX1#1 and MC38‐shFDX1#2) (Figure 3D,E). To evaluate the functional consequences of FDX1 perturbation in vivo, we subcutaneously implanted MC38‐scramble or MC38‐shFDX1 cells (5 × 105 cells/mouse) into C57BL/6 mice and administered elesclomol or vehicle treatment once tumors reached 100 mm3. Strikingly, FDX1 knockdown alone significantly attenuated tumor growth, and, critically, the anti‐tumor effect of elesclomol was abrogated in FDX1‐deficient tumors (Figure 3F,G). These findings collectively establish FDX1 as an essential mediator determinant of elesclomol‐induced cuproptosis and its consequent anti‐tumor efficacy.

FDX1 Is Upregulated in CRC and Inversely Correlates With CD8 + T Cell Infiltration

To systematically evaluate FDX1 expression in CRC, we first analyzed publicly available transcriptomic datasets. Interrogation of the TCGA‐CRC cohort demonstrated that FDX1 mRNA levels were markedly elevated in tumor tissues compared with adjacent normal mucosa (Figure 3A). Prognostic analysis of the GSE12945 dataset, stratified by median FDX1 expression, revealed that patients with low FDX1 expression exhibited significantly prolonged overall survival (log‐rank p = 0.041; Figure 3B), suggesting a potential role for FDX1 in CRC progression.

To explore the relationship between FDX1 expression and the tumor immune microenvironment, we performed EPIC deconvolution analysis of the TCGA‐CRC RNA‐seq data. Intriguingly, FDX1 expression showed a significant inverse correlation with intratumoral CD8+ T‐cell infiltration (Figure 3C), implying a possible immunosuppressive effect of FDX1.

In an AOM/DSS‐driven CRC mouse model, immunohistochemical staining revealed that FDX1‐low tumors exhibited substantially higher CD8+ T‐cell infiltration compared to FDX1‐high tumors (Figure 3D). We extended these observations to human specimens, in which immunohistochemistry (IHC) and multiplex immunofluorescence confirmed that FDX1 protein was markedly upregulated in CRC tissues relative to adjacent normal tissues (Figure 3E). Moreover, high FDX1 expression was associated with reduced CD8+ T‐cell density (Figure 3F). To further validate this association, we analyzed a tissue microarray (TMA) of 100 CRC resections and semi‐quantitatively scored FDX1 and CD8 expression. Consistent with prior results, FDX1‐high tumors harbored significantly fewer CD8+ T cells (Figure 3G). Clinicopathological correlation analysis revealed no significant association between FDX1 levels and age, sex, tumor differentiation grade, or TNM stage (Table S2, all p > 0.05). Nevertheless, Kaplan–Meier analysis of this cohort demonstrated that high FDX1 expression predicted worse prognosis (log‐rank p = 0.025; Figure 3H), reinforcing its potential as an independent prognostic marker in CRC.

FDX1 Promotes the CRC Cells' Proliferation, Metastasis, and CD8 + T Cells Exhaustion

To define the oncogenic role of FDX1 in CRC, we established isogenic RKO cell models with either FDX1 overexpression (RKO‐FDX1) or knockdown (RKO‐shFDX1#1/#2), validated by qPCR and immunoblotting (Figure S2A,B). CCK8 and colony‐formation assays revealed that FDX1 overexpression accelerated proliferation in both RKO‐FDX1 and MC38 lines (Figure 4A), whereas FDX1 depletion reduced growth (Figure 4B), with concordant results in clonogenic assays (Figure S2C,D).

Metastatic potential was assessed through wound‐healing and transwell assays. FDX1‐overexpressing cells exhibited accelerated scratch closure and increased matrigel invasion (Figure 4C,D), while FDX1 knockdown impaired both processes (Figure S2E), indicating FDX1's dual role in enhancing CRC motility and invasiveness.

Notably, co‐culture experiments using OVA‐activated CD8+ T cells from OT‐I mice and FDX1‐modulated MC38‐OVA cells revealed that FDX1 overexpression suppressed CD8+ T‐cell function, evidenced by reduced perforin and granzyme B production alongside elevated PD‐1 and Tim‐3 expression (Figure 4E,F). In contrast, FDX1 knockdown yielded the opposite phenotype (Figure 4E,F), establishing tumor‐intrinsic FDX1 as a regulator of CD8+ T‐cell exhaustion.

FDX1 Binds DICER1 to Destabilize DMBT1 mRNA and Inhibit DMBT1 Secretion

To systematically identify downstream pathways and target genes regulated by FDX1, we performed RNA sequencing (RNA‐seq) in RKO cells stably expressing either FDX1‐targeting shRNA (RKO‐shFDX1) or non‐targeting control shRNA (RKO‐shNC). Differential expression analysis (|log2(FC)| > 1, FDR ≤ 0.05) identified 64 high‐confidence genes, including 21 downregulated and 43 upregulated transcripts upon FDX1 knockdown (Table S3). Hierarchical clustering of the top 20 differentially expressed genes revealed distinct expression patterns (Figure 5A), while volcano plot analysis highlighted DMBT1 (Deleted in Malignant Brain Tumors 1) as the most prominently upregulated gene following FDX1 knockdown (Figure 5B). To validate these findings, we performed qRT‐PCR in four isogenic RKO cell lines: RKO‐vector, RKO‐FDX1, RKO‐shNC, and RKO‐shFDX1. Among the top five upregulated and downregulated candidates, only DMBT1 and LAIR2 exhibited reciprocal and consistent expression changes in response to both FDX1 overexpression and knockdown (Figure 5C), strongly implicating DMBT1 as a direct downstream target of FDX1.

We next assessed whether FDX1 regulates DMBT1 at the protein level. Western blot analysis confirmed that FDX1 overexpression markedly reduced intracellular DMBT1, whereas FDX1 knockdown increased its abundance (Figure 5D). Concordantly, ELISA of conditioned media demonstrated that FDX1 suppresses DMBT1 secretion, with reduced levels in RKO‐FDX1 cells (Figure 5E). Immunofluorescence microscopy further corroborated the FDX1‐dependent downregulation of intracellular DMBT1 (Figure 5F), collectively indicating that FDX1 negatively regulates DMBT1 at both transcriptional and translational levels.

To elucidate the mechanistic basis of FDX1‐mediated DMBT1 suppression, we performed co‐immunoprecipitation coupled with mass spectrometry (Co‐IP/MS) in RKO cells, which revealed that endogenous FDX1 interacts with multiple RNA‐binding proteins involved in RNA metabolism (Table S4, Figure 5G). Consistent with this finding, actinomycin D chase assays demonstrated that FDX1 accelerates DMBT1 mRNA decay, indicating that FDX1 post‐transcriptionally destabilizes DMBT1 transcripts (Figure 5H). To identify proteins that regulate DMBT1 mRNA stability, we performed mass spectrometry analysis using the Chromatin Isolation by RNA Purification (ChIRP) technique (Table S5). By intersecting the DMBT1 mRNA‐binding proteins with the FDX1‐associated protein interactome, we identified DICER1, a protein closely associated with RNA stability (Figure 5I). ChIRP‐Western blot further confirmed the interaction between DMBT1 mRNA and DICER1 (Figure 5J). This interaction of FDX1 and DICER1 was further confirmed using Co‐IP, which showed that DICER1 was a potent FDX1‐interacting protein (Figure 5K). DICER1 plays an important regulatory role in mRNA stability by directly degrading target mRNAs, regulating miRNA balance, and maintaining genomic stability. Its dysfunction may lead to an imbalance in mRNA stability, thereby affecting cellular gene expression and physiological functions [16]. The above results suggest that FDX1 promotes the degradation of DMBT1 mRNA through binding with DICER1.

FDX1 Promotes the CRC Cells' Proliferation, Metastasis and CD8 + T Cells' Exhaustion by Regulating DMBT1

Previous studies have reported that DMBT1 tissue expression may serve as an efficient prognostic marker for colorectal cancer (CRC) [17]. We hypothesized that DMBT1 might function as a key downstream effector of FDX1 in promoting CRC progression. To test this hypothesis, DMBT1 was knocked down in FDX1‐knockdown cells (Figure 6A). CCK‐8 and plate colony formation assays showed that silencing DMBT1 significantly attenuated the anti‐proliferative effects of FDX1 knockdown on CRC cells in vitro (Figures 6B and S3A). Similarly, wound‐healing and transwell assays demonstrated that DMBT1 silencing markedly reversed the suppressive effects of FDX1 knockdown on CRC cell migration and invasion (Figures 6C,D and S3B,C). Notably, co‐cultured experiments using OVA‐activated CD8+ T cells from OT‐I mice with FDX1 or DMBT1 modulated MC38‐OVA cells revealed DMBT1 silencing markedly reversed the suppressive effects of FDX1 knockdown on CD8+ T‐cell function, evidenced by reduced Perforin and granzyme B production alongside elevated PD‐1 and Tim‐3 expression (Figure 6E). Administer recombinant mouse DMBT1 protein markedly reversed the suppressive effects of FDX1 overexpressing on CD8+ T‐cell function (Figure S3D). Moreover, A subcutaneous tumor model confirmed that knockdown of DMBT1 could restore the inhibitory effect of FDX1 knockdown on tumors (Figure 6F) and its effect on CD8+ T cell function (Figure 6G).

FDX1‐DMBT1 Axis Modulates CD8 + T Cell Infiltration in CRC

To characterize DMBT1 expression in CRC, we analyzed the GSE18105 and GSE21510 datasets, which revealed a significant downregulation of DMBT1 mRNA in tissues relative to adjacent normal mucosa (Figure 7A). Further stratification of the TCGA‐CRC cohort by median DMBT1 expression demonstrated that patients with low‐DMBT1 levels had significantly worse overall survival than those with high‐DMBT1 expression (log‐rank p = 0.032; Figure 7B), suggesting a potential tumor‐suppressive role for DMBT1 in CRC.

To investigate the regulatory interplay among FDX1, DMBT1, and CD8+ T cell infiltration, we stratified our colorectal cancer tissue cohort based on FDX1 expression. Intriguingly, FDX1‐high tumors exhibited significantly reduced DMBT1 levels and diminished CD8+ T cell infiltration, whereas FDX1‐low tumors displayed elevated DMBT1 expression and increased CD8+ T cell presence (Table S6, Figure 7C). These findings establish an inverse correlation between FDX1 abundance and both DMBT1 expression and cytotoxic T‐cell infiltration in CRC (Figure 7D).

Notably, our study demonstrates that FDX1, which is upregulated in CRC and associated with poor prognosis (Figure 7E), modulates the tumor immune microenvironment during cuproptosis by targeting DMBT1. Mechanistically, FDX1‐mediated suppression of DMBT1 significantly promotes CD8+ T cells' activation while inhibiting T‐cell exhaustion, ultimately restraining CRC progression.

Discussion

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is a major global health burden, ranking third in incidence and cancer‐related mortality worldwide. Approximately 9% of all annual cancer deaths are attributable to CRC, underscoring its significant impact on public health [18]. While immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have transformed cancer treatment, monotherapies targeting PD‐1 or CTLA‐4 yield limited clinical benefits in CRC. Thus, identifying molecular determinants of ICI response and developing predictive biomarkers remains an urgent priority.

Cuproptosis, a novel form of copper‐induced cell death dependent on mitochondrial respiration, was first characterized by Tsvetkov et al. [2]. This process involves copper binding to lipoylated enzymes in the tricarboxylic acid cycle, leading to toxic protein aggregation and loss of iron–sulfur cluster proteins. Growing evidence implicates cuproptosis in tumorigenesis and progression across multiple malignancies. Computational studies further suggest that cuproptosis‐related gene signatures can predict prognosis and reflect distinct TME features in malignancies such as hepatocellular carcinoma and gastric adenocarcinoma. For instance, low cuproptosis scores are associated with enriched cancer‐associated fibroblasts [6], while immune infiltration patterns vary across cancer types. These findings highlight the therapeutic potential of targeting copper homeostasis using chelators or ionophores in combination with chemotherapy or immunotherapy to overcome treatment resistance. In this study, we utilized the MC38 and CT26 subcutaneous tumor models to induce cuproptosis with elesclomol (ES) and characterized subsequent changes in the TME. ES acts as a copper ionophore, facilitating copper entry into cells where it is reduced by ferredoxin 1 (FDX1), ultimately triggering cell death. ES treatment significantly inhibited tumor growth and altered immune cell composition: reducing macrophage infiltration while enhancing dendritic and CD8+ T cell accumulation. These findings suggest that ES‐induced cuproptosis activates both innate and adaptive anti‐tumor immunity, with a notable enhancement of CD8+ T cell cytotoxicity and reduced exhaustion.

Elevated intracellular copper promotes PD‐L1 upregulation via STAT3‐mediated transcription or by inhibiting ubiquitin‐proteasome system (UPS)‐dependent PD‐L1 degradation, thereby impairing anti‐tumor immunity and facilitating immune escape. Conversely, copper chelators enhance the infiltration of intratumoral CD8+ T cells and natural killer cells and inhibit tumor growth. In a murine model of hepatocellular carcinoma, tumor‐derived exosomes carrying LOXL4‐containing drive induce PD‐L1 expression in macrophages through a cuproptosis‐related STAT3 signaling mechanism. Copper chelation disrupts this signaling cascade, reinstates CD8+ T cell cytotoxicity, and attenuates tumor progression [19]. Therefore, targeting copper‐dependent LOXL activation emerges as a promising therapeutic approach to augment immune‐checkpoint inhibition and mitigate immunosuppression in the tumor microenvironment. Among cuproptosis‐related genes, FDX1 exhibited the most pronounced changes in expression following ES treatment. Further bioinformatic analysis of TCGA‐CRC data combined with tissue microarray immunohistochemistry revealed FDX1 overexpression in CRC tissues, where it correlated negatively with CD8+ T cell infiltration and predicted poor patient prognosis. Functional assays—including CCK‐8, colony formation, Transwell, and wound healing—confirmed that FDX1 promotes CRC proliferation and metastasis. Moreover, co‐culture experiments indicated that FDX1 suppresses CD8+ T cell cytokine production and promotes exhaustion, reinforcing its role as a potential biomarker and therapeutic target.

To elucidate the mechanism by which FDX1 modulates the TME, we performed RNA sequencing in FDX1‐silenced RKO cells. This analysis identified DMBT1 (Deleted in Malignant Brain Tumor 1) as the most significantly up‐regulated gene. DMBT1, a secreted glycoprotein involved in mucosal immunity and epithelial differentiation, is frequently epigenetically silenced or homozygously deleted in gastrointestinal cancers, supporting its role as a tumor suppressor [20, 21, 22, 23]. Hypoxia, nitric oxide, and pro‐inflammatory stimuli (IL‐22, LPS, TNF‐α) all induce DMBT1 expression [24, 25, 26, 27, 28]. Subsequent validation via qRT‐PCR, immunoblot, ELISA, and immunofluorescence confirmed that FDX1 negatively regulates DMBT1 expression. By intersecting the DMBT1 mRNA‐binding proteins with the FDX1‐associated protein interactome, we identified DICER1, a protein closely associated with RNA stability. DICER1 plays an important regulatory role in mRNA stability by directly degrading target mRNAs, regulating miRNA balance, and maintaining genomic stability. Its dysfunction may lead to an imbalance in mRNA stability, thereby affecting cellular gene expression and physiological functions [16]. In addition, pathway analysis suggested involvement of the MAPK/ERK cascade, which is known to suppress DMBT1 transcription [29, 30], although further investigation is required to establish this link definitively.

In summary, our results demonstrate that FDX1, a key regulator of cuproptosis, drives immunosuppression in CRC by downregulating DMBT1. This axis contributes to CD8+ T cell dysfunction and supports a pro‐tumorigenic microenvironment. The overexpression of FDX1 in CRC and its association with poor clinical outcomes position the FDX1–DMBT1 pathway as a promising therapeutic target. Modulating this axis may enhance antitumor immunity and overcome resistance to existing immunotherapies.
