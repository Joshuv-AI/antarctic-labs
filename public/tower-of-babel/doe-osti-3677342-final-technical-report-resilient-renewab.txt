 Executive Summary

The Resilient Renewable Energy to Diminish Disaster Impact (REDDI) Communities
program, carried out by the North Carolina Clean Energy Technology Center
(NCCETC), was designed to enable local communities, emergency management
agencies, local organizations, and local governments to make informed decisions for
the deployment of solar photovoltaics (PV) and energy storage to improve community
resilience. The project developed a system to guide communities through the integration
of resilience measures into their local energy systems. This system included metrics of
the value of resilience, a playbook for communities, and a series of technical assistance
workshops.

By conducting broad stakeholder engagement with county and municipal governments,
NCCETC built a baseline of understanding of communities’ needs, interest,
understanding, and attitudes towards resilience. For some communities, resilience
planning was front of mind given the prevalence of outages and vulnerable households.
For others, emergency managers were more likely to want to fix problems after they
arise, rather than try to prevent them. Some communities wanted to participate in
resilience planning but didn’t have the resources to engage. With 100 counties and
many local governments in North Carolina with all kinds of needs, feedback on
resilience planning was bound to be varied.

Incorporating this feedback and looking into actual metrics of resilience led NCCETC to
work with a professor at NCSU, Benjamin Rachunok and his team. The metrics work
focused on collecting data for poorly performing circuits in North Carolina, studying
existing metrics and frameworks such as System Average Interruption Duration Index
(SAIDI), Customer Average Interruption Duration Index (CAIDI), Value of Lost Load
(VoLL), and Interruption Cost Estimator (ICE). Putting these together, NCCETC
develops a metrics framework and a calculator to determine the household,
infrastructure, and county-level costs of outages, and by extension, the benefits of
resilience.

After conducting multiple stakeholder engagement sessions, workshops, and technical
assistance activities, and utilizing other outreach strategies, NCCETC prepared a
community resilience playbook. The purpose of the playbook is to provide a set of best
practices for communities and emergency managers in North Carolina, though the
lessons learned can be applied across the country. This playbook delivers general
applications of resilient energy systems for community energy resilience needs and
experiences. It offers solutions and strategies that communities can implement to meet
their resilience needs, and it contains lessons learned from real-world examples of
community resilience projects throughout the state. This can help inform program
design and long-term planning for community emergency preparedness.

This report will describe the actions taken by NCCETC and its partners at NCSU and
the Smart Electric Power Alliance (SEPA) to engage with North Carolina communities to
develop resilience metrics and the community playbook.

 Background

North Carolina in Context

Many of the benefits described in this guide are resilience and/or resilience services in
the context of energy. The U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) defines energy resilience
as the ability of communities, buildings, and the grid to withstand and recover from
power outages or remain operational during grid-scale events.1 Specifically, energy
resilience focuses on the hours to days immediately before and following an outage and
how to mitigate negative impacts to communities. This differs from reliability, which is
the ability of the grid to remain operational through day-to-day events and challenges.

Much of the resilience conversation in North Carolina has been shaped by power
outages and other crises that result directly from extreme weather, especially
hurricanes. Hurricanes have affected and will continue to affect almost all regions of
North Carolina. Two exemplary storms to consider are Hurricane Florence in 2018 and
Hurricane Helene in 2024. These two storms created devastating impacts in both
eastern and western North Carolina, respectively.

Due to Hurricane Helene, western North Carolina experienced heavy rainfall and 1,000-
year flooding events in several counties. According to the North Carolina Governor and
the Office of State Budget and Management's assessments,2 recovery costs total $59.6
billion, and the storm damage is estimated to have impacted 4.6 million people in 39
counties, or 40% of the state's population.

Hurricane Florence struck North Carolina in September 2018,3 bringing dangerous
storm surge, record rainfall, and widespread flooding. Total damages amounted to $17
billion, more than triple the cost of Hurricane Matthew in 2016. FEMA ultimately
designated 44 counties for federal disaster assistance, an area home to nearly 4 million
North Carolinians, again about 40% of the state’s population.

National Resilience Frameworks
In 2025, the National Association of Regulatory Utility Commissioners (NARUC)
convened experts to develop a resilience framework for state and local policymakers.4
The principles of that framework make up the basis of this project, including the metrics
report and community playbook. The framework provides a series of considerations,

 Page 5 of 29

factors, and decision points for state and local policymakers as they work to develop
and implement resilience measures in their communities. The framework is designed to
be a best-practices and decision-making guide, emphasizing key questions for
policymakers and emergency planners, data that needs to be collected, stakeholders to
engage, and interventions to consider.

The Federal Emergency Management Agency’s (FEMA) National Disaster Recovery
Framework (NDRF) was updated in December 2024.5 It sets a series of pre-disaster
and post-disaster best practices, which include resilience planning and implementing
resilience measures. Additionally, the framework discusses the roles of Federal, State,
local, and Tribal governments in the event of an emergency or disaster. Specifically,
while states often act as pass-through agencies for federal dollars, and often set
statewide plans and frameworks, it is local governments that end up doing most of the
recovery and resilience implementation alongside NGO, business, and community
organization partners. Resilience planning is an incredibly important part of this mix:
according to FEMA, every dollar spent on resilience and mitigation can save up to six
dollars on recovery.6

North Carolina Resilience Resources
The State of North Carolina encourages resilience planning at the local level, assisting
local governments with creating Hazard Mitigation Plans every five years.7 In December
2024, the North Carolina Office of Recovery and Resiliency released the North Carolina
Resilient Communities Planning Guide8 to provide local governments with the tools to
plan for resilience in their communities. The REDDI Community Resilience Playbook
builds on this resource by directly providing best practices and decision-making
guidance for installing microgrids.9 The state also hosts a Resilience Exchange hub,
connecting community leaders to state-level resources for resilience.10

On the utility side, planning tends to focus on energy reliability; in addition, this planning
is done at the system-wide level, instead of at the local or community level. North
Carolina has two investor-owned utilities in the state: Dominion Energy and Duke
Energy. Dominion Energy’s North Carolina territory is relatively small, so it tends to align
any of its North Carolina actions with requirements in its Virginia territory. In Virginia,
Dominion files a mandatory Grid Transformation Plan,11 largely focusing on reliability
rather than resilience.12 Dominion also has a program to improve resilience at U.S.
military bases, though none of the currently participating bases are located in North
Carolina.13

Similar to Dominion, Duke Energy’s resilience planning is more reliability planning, with
a focus on transmission and distribution grid infrastructure including solutions like poles-
and-wires upgrades, vegetation management, and communication technologies.14 Duke
also has a strategy for climate resilience and adaptation.15

Recent North Carolina efforts to implement resilience measures include the State
Energy Office’s creation of a $5 million program to install up to 24 stationary microgrids
in counties impacted by hurricane Helene, with two mobile “beehive” microgrids that can

be deployed at locations in need to lend out solar panels and batteries.16 Duke Energy
and the State Energy Office are partnering on a $57 million grant from DOE to
reconductor the Lee-Milburnie line, improving resilience by preventing sag and other
physical failure modes.17

Resilience Metrics
Many metrics currently in use measure reliability, not resilience. In this project,
NCCETC and NCSU researchers adapt reliability metrics for use in resilience
measurements. CAIDI can be replaced with the number or percentage of critical
services without power, SAIDI with the average number or percentage of critical
customers experiencing an outage, and SAIFI with the number of critical customer-
hours of outages or the amount of kWh load not served.18 These changes can also help
focus the metrics on communities and households. The Electric Power Research
Institute has also developed a roadmap identifying gaps in knowledge for valuing
resilience.19

Econometrics studies of reliability often rely on benefit-cost analysis (BCA). However,
BCA is unreliable for resilience metrics, as BCA often misses broader societal, non-
economic, and hard-to-monetize benefits and costs. Placing a greater emphasis on
avoided costs to the customer, community, and utility can improve the economics of
resilience measures.20

There are several tools and calculators for quantifying the value of avoided power
interruptions or estimating the economic impacts of power outages, including the
Interruption Cost Estimate Calculator Tool,21 NLR’s Customer Damage Function
Calculator Tool,22 FEMA’s Benefit-Cost Analysis Toolkit,23 and LBNL’s Power Outage
Economics Tool Pilot,24 although none of these tools coalesce around an agreed-upon
quantification for the societal benefits that these systems can provide. There are also
tools and products that have aimed to correct this imbalance between economic benefit
and societal benefit in resilience valuation. Notably, those are EPRI’s Value of
Resilience Working Group and Research Roadmap,25 NLR’s and Converge Strategies’
Value of Resilience for Distributed Energy Resources,26 and NARUC’s, NASEO’s, and
Converge Strategies’ Valuing Resilience for Microgrids.27

Additional frameworks provide toolkits to address community resilience, such as the
Analysis of Resilience of Communities to Disasters (ARC-D) toolkit.28 A resilience
framework proposed by resilience planning researchers in Australia29 defines
community resilience as cooperation and contribution of customers and other
community stakeholders.

Defining Critical Infrastructure
FEMA provides examples of critical facilities,30 such as hospitals, fire stations, police
stations, storage of critical records, and similar facilities; FEMA also provides definitions
of “Community Lifelines”31 and has a Community Lifelines Toolkit32 that helps
communities stabilize after a disaster.

 Page 7 of 29

North Carolina’s definition of critical infrastructure is stated in the NC Infrastructure
Protection Act33 as a “communication infrastructure system, cybersecurity system,
electric grid, hazardous waste treatment system, or water treatment facility.” The North
Carolina Department of Revenue34 provides examples of critical infrastructure, including
communications networks, electric generation, transmission and distribution systems,
natural gas transmission and distribution systems, water pipelines, and related support
facilities.

There have been limited attempts to prioritize programming to build and support
disaster-resilient critical infrastructure. Duke Energy has proposed critical community
customer batteries35 to support critical services such as hospitals, police stations, and
evacuation shelters.

The North Carolina Department of Public Safety maintains a database of hazard
mitigation plans at the county and regional level,36 many of which identify critical
facilities. Several counties have filed hazard mitigation plans including Buncombe and
Madison,37 Wake,38 and the Southeastern region of NC (Brunswick, New Hanover,
Onslow, and Pender).39

 Project Objectives

The Resilient REDDI Communities project aims to enable local communities, through
the leadership of emergency management and local organizations, to make informed
decisions for deployment of energy generation and energy storage projects to improve
resilience. Through research, modeling, and community engagement, the project team
will develop and disseminate a system that will guide communities through the steps of
incorporating resilience into community planning.

The major resources developed as part of this system will be a metrics report that
discusses a variety of resilience factors: what state officials can do, statewide
resources, notable examples of resilience planning, and estimates of the economic
value of resilience; and a playbook that details strategies and best practices for
communities that are considering resilience measures such as solar and storage,
microgrids, backup generators, or other interventions.

The four major elements of this project are stakeholder engagement and data collection,
community resilience metrics, a community playbook, and technical assistance. Using
these tools, the project produces a comprehensive review of resilience metrics,
providing a report and resource for communities to understand resilience frameworks,
interventions, measures, and valuation; a playbook of best practices for resilience-
interested communities; a stakeholder cohort to whom the project can disseminate
findings and reports; and a technical assistance agenda to provide interested
communities and stakeholders with additional information beyond the lifetime of the
project.

Initial Resilient REDDI Communities Workshop

On January 19, 2024, the project team hosted a stakeholder roundtable on the Resilient
Renewable Energy to Diminish Disaster Impacts on Communities (Resilient REDDI
Communities) project, focusing on enhancing community resilience through renewable
energy initiatives, particularly in disaster-prone areas. The goal of the roundtable was to
assess stakeholder interest in the development of a replicable playbook for consistent
valuation of energy resilience in communities. It also provided an opportunity for
stakeholders to review the proposed metrics for valuing investments, assess the
benefits, and discuss examples of technical assistance and collaboration opportunities.
Attendees represented a range of organizations, including utility companies, local
government agencies, state agencies, and grassroots organizations in North Carolina.
NCCETC invited participants to share their perspectives on resilience metrics and their
experiences implementing resilience systems within their organizations or communities.
The key takeaways from that discussion fall into the following four categories:

 resilience metrics and a playbook aimed at assisting communities,

 energy systems and justifying investments.

 usefulness and challenges associated with each metric category.

 challenges and priorities of each community.

 shared insights into the challenges faced in recovering critical
 infrastructure quickly after outages and serving marginalized communities
 with legacy grid infrastructure.

Cooperative and Municipal Utility Virtual Roundtable

On May 31, 2024, the North Carolina Clean Energy Technology Center and the Smart
Electric Power Alliance hosted a roundtable dialogue with Cooperative and Municipal
utilities in North Carolina. The roundtable discussion focused on what utilities are doing
and can do to enhance community resilience through resilient renewable energy
initiatives, particularly in disaster-prone areas. The goal of the roundtable was to inform
utilities about the project, have an open discussion among participants about how grid
resiliency is defined and addressed by their utility, and to present the utilities with
partnership opportunities.

                                        Page 11 of 29

The project team introduced the REDDI project, as well as example resilience metrics and
GIS maps that will help identify critical facilities in distressed areas that could use
technical assistance. Examples of microgrids at a bus depot and a school were shown,
and an overview of the modeling tools used to evaluate resilient energy systems was given,
as well as an analysis of how these systems could provide value by reducing coincident
peak demands for a utility.

The meeting then opened for discussion among the utilities about how they define,
measure, and address grid resiliency. One participant shared that once a year their utility
looks at the 5-10 worst performing feeders and invests in the improvement of those
feeders. Another participant shared that their utility is looking at hardening substation
foundations and converting miles of copper to aluminum. Both utilities mentioned that
there is a balance when making improvements to the system because they don’t want to
overwhelm their communities with rising rates, but they want to provide them with reliable
power. There is a challenge for underserved communities because they have the same
expectation for resiliency, but they are often in rural areas where there is more exposure to
the elements and more equipment that could fail, and these are the communities that
rising costs would affect the most. In order not to raise customer costs, the utilities are
consistently engaged in applying for grant funding to complete these projects to upgrade
their systems.

In regard to critical loads and potential resilient solar and battery storage microgrid
locations, one participant shared that all member loads are important, but there are loads
that are more critical than others to prioritize for restoration of power, such as loads that
serve the public good, areas that serve a lot of public traffic, and places with agricultural
operations. Another participant mentioned that they have a school that would be a good
target for a microgrid because it is used as an emergency shelter, and another participant
just finished a microgrid project and is familiar with microgrids. The utilities mentioned that
they were very engaged in their communities and are always looking for opportunities to
improve their service to them and their quality of life. The utilities that shared insights
seemed open to sharing data and accepting help for technical assistance, grant writing,
and anything to help resilience to their communities.

The NCCETC team closed out the meeting by presenting the utilities with partnership
opportunities, information about the types of data that they could share with the REDDI
project team, and options for federal funding. The team also shared the next steps in the
project, the project timeline, and their contact information for future questions and
involvement.

Task 2: Resilience Metrics

Establishment of Resilience Metrics

The project team compiled resources and conducted a review of possible resilience
metrics. The team began by looking at economic methods of quantifying the direct
impacts of power outages:
 ● Value of Lost Load (VoLL): the economic cost to customers of unserved
 electricity, and the willingness-to-pay to avoid outages or willingness to accept
 outages. VoLL can be calculated via surveys to understand customer attitudes,
 revealed preference experiments, and general economic production calculations
 ● Customer Damage Functions or Interruption Cost Estimators: utility-level outage
 costs based on duration and customer class; often based on VoLL

                                        Page 13 of 29

 ● Affordability-based metrics: these require the extension of VoLL to additional
 categories, such as income loss, infrastructure loss, and other household metrics
 and expenditures

Based on these methods, the project team established a tiered approach to resilience
indices:
 ● Tier 1: Direct Impacts/Households - how does a hazard impact something of
 value? Computed using VoLL, with existing measures and on-the-ground data
 collection.
 ● Tier 2: Service Disruption/Infrastructure - if the “something of value” is
 infrastructure, how is the customer impacted? Computed via weighted penalties
 for proximity to essential services.
 ● Tier 3: Impact of Loss of Service/Systemic Impacts - if tier-2 impacts interact,
 what happens to the customer? Computed via standard vulnerability measures at
 the county or tract level.

The resilience index is then the sum of the tiered indices. Then, emergency managers
can customize this calculation based on the characteristics of resilience interventions.

GIS Dataset Development of Community Resilience Data

The resilience metrics tools compile zip code-, tract-, and county-level data, including
electricity-dependent medical needs, household makeup, physical building quality,
digital connectivity, vehicle ownership/mobility, caregiving status, and disability status in
order to gain a geospatial understanding of vulnerability and resilience. For each of the
index tiers above, this allows emergency managers to aggregate household
vulnerability to power outages, quantify access to essential services based on average
distance to services for each household, and determine the value of certain resilience
interventions to the system based on a propensity for consumers to utilize them.

Econometric Modeling

Following data collection, creation of tiers, and development of the metrics calculations,,
the project team developed a tool that emergency managers can use to compute these
factors. The tool is attached to this package.

Framework Development

Based on all of this data, the team prepared a unified framework for estimating outage
vulnerability, resilience benefits, and cost/benefit.
 ● Tier 1: Household Impacts
 ○ Uses public data to estimate which homes are at greatest risk during
 outages.
 ○ Factors include electricity-dependent medical needs, demographics,
 income, and mobility.

                                        Page 14 of 29

 ○ Results show where households are most likely to struggle when power
 fails.
 ● Tier 2: Community Access to Essential Services
 ○ Places with worse access to critical services face greater burdens when
 outages occur.
 ○ Measure proximity to schools, groceries, hospitals, and emergency
 services.
 ○ Poor access compounds household vulnerability during disruptions.
 ● Tier 3: Matching Resources to Needs
 ○ Using NLR ResStock data, estimate typical household energy needs.
 ○ This reveals which communities require more energy to maintain basic

 ○ High-need areas may benefit from larger storage or generation
 investments.
 ● Tier 4: Outage Costs
 ○ Estimate per-capita and community-level outage costs using DOE’s ICE
 2.0 model.
 ○ Costs represent economic losses from unserved energy for both residents
 and businesses.
 ○ This helps prioritize where reliability investments deliver the highest
 benefit.

Task 3: Energy Resilience Playbook

Develop Best Practices and Model Policies and Programs

A major component of the playbook is developing best practices, guidelines, and
replicable and scalable models for communities. As resilience planners consider
interventions, they might consider the following steps, opportunities, challenges, and
data collection before making any decisions or deploying any solutions:
 1. Identify household and community vulnerabilities, including specific concerns or
 weaknesses
 2. Identify critical facilities (hospitals, public safety infrastructure, etc.)
 a. Proximity to vulnerable households, and quantity of vulnerable households
 served
 b. Location(s) of services and non-government "essential" services
 3. Identify existing backup generation or resilient solutions
 4. Investigate partnership with utilities
 5. Compare site(s) loads under blue sky and emergency conditions
 6. Model resilience needs
 a. Model outage durations and locations
 b. Model generation, demand response, storage options
 7. Determine relative costs of system(s)
 a. Is there a cost sharing opportunity with local business or utility?

                                     Page 15 of 29

 b. Are there state or federal grants available for resilience implementation?
 8. Determine resilience application(s) to be funded
 a. Seek out grants, public funding, loans, etc.
 9. Publish a request for proposals for qualified companies to construct the project.

Technological and Community Perspective

When planning for resilience needs, officials should consider the backup power needs
and current supplies for their public safety infrastructure: emergency communications
and operations centers, fire stations, police stations, and other first-responders. Officials
should also consider proximity of these services to residents, with a special focus on
vulnerable residents. There is also room for public-private partnerships and cross-
functional resilience planning: businesses like restaurants or grocery stores or
community centers that are able to remain operational can save owners money by
avoiding loss of product while providing resources to the community. Officials can help
facilitate and support these partnerships before disaster strikes.

Traditional infrastructure investments, such as hardening of grid components like poles
and wires, must be weighed against less common investments, such as distributed
resources for household energy supply. The economics of DERs for resilience make
these solutions more viable in recent years. For instance, battery energy projects can
help critical services and households remain operational through grid-level disruptions.
Distributed resources also avoid the cost of transmission and distribution buildout or
upgrades.

Resilience helps communities overcome disasters. Everyone relies on power to operate
medical equipment, preserve medications and food, and keep homes habitable. Local
governments must keep communications and emergency services operational. In the
case of widespread disaster impacts, shelters and aid distribution points need power for
occupant safety and communications.

Local governments and utilities can leverage distributed, resilient systems not only to
avoid costly infrastructure or costly outages, but to bolster normal grid operations as
well.

 Significant Accomplishments and Conclusions
Resilience Metrics and Valuation
Upon incorporation of household, tract, and county level data and development of the
resilience tiers and resilience metrics framework, the team developed a tool that can
calculate the value of resilience for a particular community, given certain initial
conditions. This is based on outage data, value of lost load calculations, average
distance of residents to essential services, and other data.
Using the tool, North Carolina counties can be sorted into 6 groups, ranked by
vulnerability, access to essential services, and level of energy needs. The tool also
computes costs and benefits of resilience. For instance, in Lumberton County, NC, the
SAIDI index is 1030 and the CAIDI index is 202, with 924 customers each year
experiencing outages. This leads to a total cost of $113,389 per year.
There were some roadblocks and pivots in preparing the resilience metrics. Some data,
such as private or protected data on medically vulnerable households, is hard to acquire
and analyze. As a result, certain GIS applications were not possible. Additionally, while
the resilience metrics can estimate the cost of outages, it is harder to estimate the
benefits of resilience. This tool assumes the benefit as the avoided cost of outages if
resilience metrics were implemented, but further study would incorporate the total cost
of the resilience metrics to get a more accurate benefit-cost analysis.

Community Resilience Playbook
NCCETC and SEPA incorporated learnings from outreach activities including
stakeholder engagement sessions, workshops, and technical assistance opportunities
to produce a playbook to guide communities and emergency managers through
resilience decision making, including strategies and tools for supporting community
resilience in the face of natural disasters. While the content focuses on North Carolina,
it is intended that the lessons in the playbook be scalable and replicable across the
United States.
The playbook also aims to discuss practical solutions that strengthen emergency
preparedness and response. The playbook expands upon North Carolina’s Disaster
Recovery Framework, led by NC Emergency Management (NCEM), to focus on how to
integrate resilience planning for both storm-related outages and cyberattacks into its
disaster recovery planning. The guide identifies decision-making processes backed by
criteria and tools, outlines specific implementation steps for resilience projects, and
highlights best practices through real-world examples. Furthermore, it emphasizes the
identification and prioritization of critical facilities as a means to better map and
understand the needs of disadvantaged communities.

Conclusions
Overall, the REDDI project elucidated a number of key takeaways about resilience
interventions, community resilience planning, and technical assistance:

There are differing attitudes towards resilience across North Carolina, likely across the
country, and even within counties and communities. While some local governments are
interested in resilience planning, others are skeptical of the value, and still others lack
the resources to even begin to think about this kind of long-term planning.

There are myriad metrics that planners and policymakers can utilize to quantify the
vulnerability of communities, the costs of outages and disasters, and the relative benefit
of resilience planning. These metrics have varying usefulness. At the household level,
understanding customer-specific details is important, such as average outage time per
household, any electricity dependency, and average distance to critical resources. At
the county or community level, understanding the duration and frequency of outages on
a full distribution circuit, the average distance of multiple community members to critical
infrastructure, and the relative propensity for residents to use that infrastructure are
important to paint a picture of dependency on and need for these critical resources.
Together, this helps communities determine whether community-wide or household-
specific resilience measures are beneficial. Putting these calculations together,
emergency managers can understand their community’s needs and begin to make
decisions.

With ongoing technical assistance activities, growing and improving relationships with
communities, a tool to compute community vulnerability and costs, and an existing
cohort of communities interested in resilience planning, closeout of this project will not
be the end of the research and conversation in North Carolina. In the next section,
NCCETC lays out its plans to disseminate these resources, continue to work with
communities, and develop new projects to implement resilience plans and deploy
technologies for improved household and community resilience.

 Path Forward

The Resilient REDDI Communities project sought to provide metrics, tools, playbooks,
and frameworks to assist communities and emergency managers in decision making for
resilience planning. The project resulted in a community vulnerability and benefit
calculator, a playbook for resilience decision-making, and a cohort or collective of
communities interested in resilience planning.

These tools are designed to facilitate resilience planning and estimate the value of
resilience interventions. The project team envisions three future directions to share and
make use of these tools.

First, the metrics tool and the playbook will be published on the NC Clean Energy
Technology Center website, and will be made freely available to the public for use in
resilience planning.

Second, the NC Clean Energy Technology Center will continue to leverage the
community cohort and technical assistance partnerships to create municipal resilience
plans for interested North Carolina towns and cities. While there are federal and state
frameworks for disaster recovery, and many local governments are responsible for
implementing those in partnership with states, many local governments do not have
their own resilience frameworks. Furthermore, depending on local government
structure, both counties and municipalities may need to have resilience frameworks and
resilience plans, to best serve their constituents. If there is interest, NCCETC would like
to continue using the metrics tool and playbook to design resilience plans for
communities.

Third, the tools available allow communities to estimate both the cost of outages and
the benefits of resilience interventions. To that end, NCCETC would like to begin
research on the value of distributed solar and storage, specifically for medically
vulnerable households. A major concern during outages is whether households that rely
on medical equipment will be able to continue operating their equipment or will be able
to reach a medical facility. Resilient energy sources, like solar with storage, can help
alleviate this particularly vulnerability, saving households time, money, and life.

As a final note, one continued challenge with community engagement and technical
assistance was the lack of resources within counties and local governments to engage
with the REDDI project. While counties often expressed interest in resilience planning
and were able to attend a small number of meetings, lack of staffing, funding, time, and
other resources prevented further engagement. A consideration for future work in
resilience planning is funding and staffing for local governments that want to take these
efforts seriously.

Executive Summary
This report evaluates the feasibility and benefits of implementing and integrating a battery energy
storage system with existing on-site generation assets to create a dispatchable microgrid at the
City of Asheville’s Broadway Public Safety Station (BPSS). The proposed microgrid aims to
enhance the facility’s resiliency, reduce energy costs, and integrate renewable energy sources to
support critical operations during outages. The study includes technical modeling, cost analysis,
and recommendations for system design and implementation.

Project Overview
The BPSS currently relies on grid electricity, a 67 kW solar PV array, and a 200 kW diesel
generator to meet its energy needs. The proposed microgrid would incorporate a 250 kWh
Battery Energy Storage System (BESS) for backup power, a 20 kWh BESS for life safety loads,
and advanced controls to optimize energy usage. The system is designed to operate in both grid-
connected and islanded modes, ensuring uninterrupted power for critical loads during outages.

Key Findings
Resiliency Improvements. The proposed microgrid upgrades will enhance resiliency at the
BPSS by enabling the islanding of the entire facility. In addition, simulations show that integrating
the BESS with solar PV and the generator significantly extends outage coverage. Specifically,
the proposed microgrid will increase outage coverage by at least 3-5 days (37.5% to 62.5%
increase).
Cost and Financial Viability. Under the current Medium General Service (MGS) rate structure,
annual savings are limited to $2,403, resulting in a benefit-cost ratio of 0.17. Switching to a Time-
of-Use (TOU) rate structure increases annual savings to $10,624, improving the benefit-cost ratio
to 0.78. Further revenue may be achievable through the participation of Duke Energy’s Demand
Response Automation Program, which would increase the benefit-cost ratio to 0.95.
Non-Monetary Benefits. The microgrid enhances operational resilience, environmental
sustainability, and energy independence. It supports the City of Asheville’s sustainability goals by
increasing renewable energy utilization during outages and helping enable greater renewable
generation deployment broadly.
Technical Recommendations. This report provides recommended upgrades to the BPSS
electrical system. The recommended design maintains the current configuration of the facility’s
electrical system with a few key modifications to enhance functionality and compliance. The
existing generator will be disconnected from the life safety load panels and tied directly into the
main distribution panel to back up all site loads. A new ~20 kWh BESS will replace the generator
for life safety loads, ensuring code compliance, while a ~250 kWh BESS will be added as a
microgrid asset, also connected to the main distribution panel. Addressable breakers will be
installed for the main breaker, diesel generator, and BESSs to enable remote actuation by the
microgrid controller.

Conclusion and Next Steps
The proposed microgrid offers significant resiliency and sustainability benefits for the BPSS, with
near breakeven financial viability achievable under a TOU utility rate structure combined with
potential demand response program incentives currently available through Duke Energy. TRC
recommends initiating discussions with the utility to confirm TOU rate structure terms, feasibility

1 Microgrid Components
A microgrid is a localized group of electricity sources and loads which operate connected to and
synchronous with the local utility grid. It is also able to disconnect from the utility grid and function
autonomously without grid support, also known as “island mode.” Each microgrid system is unique,
but TRC illustrates the core microgrid components in Figure 1:

 Figure 1. Microgrid Components
The Controllable Load element represents the end-users that consume electricity from the grid. At
the City of Asheville site, this includes the Broadway Public Service Station. These individual systems
are integrated into a centralized Microgrid Controls Center, which functions as the primary hub for
managing energy generation and consumption within the microgrid. This microgrid system ensures
seamless operations by dynamically balancing power supply and demand.
During non-autonomous operations (when the microgrid is connected to the utility grid), the
microgrid can either draw power from or sell power to the utility grid, depending on the site’s energy
requirements. In contrast, during autonomous operations (when the microgrid operates in "island
mode"), the microgrid relies on on-site energy sources to meet demand.
The primary on-site energy sources include:
  Generators: These encompass diverse energy systems such as combined heat and power
 (CHP) plants, nuclear reactors, or local generators. Generators are highly flexible, capable
 of being activated, deactivated, or scaled to precisely match energy demand without
 producing excess power.
  Solar Photovoltaic (PV) Power: Solar PV systems, along with other intermittent sources like
 wind, can generate variable energy levels. To address fluctuations, excess energy can be
 stored for later use.
  Energy Storage Systems: Technologies such as batteries, electrochemical cells, and
 mechanical storage systems store surplus energy from intermittent sources. These
 systems supply power during periods of insufficient generation, ensuring consistent energy
 availability.

2.3 BPSS Electrical System
2.3.1 Existing Electrical System
The BPSS facility is provided with 800A, 480V three-phase power through a single utility meter

backfeeding power from the PV system to the grid does not typically occur.
All facility loads are fed via subpanels that come off the main panel. Two of these subpanels
have automatic transfer switches between them and the main panel, allowing these circuits to be
automatically switched onto the onsite diesel generator in the event of the loss of grid power. The
circuit that supports life safety loads also has a manual transfer switch for a portable generator to
be hooked up to that circuit specifically.

3 Resiliency Modeling
3.1 Outage Simulations: Solar + BESS Only
TRC collaborated with researchers from North Carolina State University to evaluate the
Broadway Public Safety Station’s electrical demand and outage response capabilities. Using the
web-based microgrid modeling tool Xendee, NC State simulated outage scenarios at the BPSS
site to determine optimal sizing for various battery energy storage systems (BESS) that could
support the City of Asheville’s resiliency initiatives. These simulations were based on the annual
average peak electric usage day, identified as occurring between June 21 and June 27, 2024.
Three outage durations—one day, three days, and seven days—were analyzed, with BESS
sizing tailored to outage length, load demand, and available solar resources.
In addition to the Xendee simulations, TRC conducted independent calculations using a
proprietary model that incorporates hourly utility load and solar output data across the entire
year. This broader scope allowed TRC to account for seasonal variations, such as reduced solar
output during winter outages, which were not considered in the Xendee model. Consequently,
discrepancies in recommended BESS sizes were observed between the two approaches. TRC’s
report includes both sets of results to provide a comprehensive analysis of BESS sizing options
for the BPSS microgrid.

3.1.1 Modeling Parameters
BESS sizing is primarily driven by the interaction of 1) electric load; 2) outage duration; and 3)
generation. The design implications of these factors are described in the following sections.
Electric Load. Microgrids can be designed to provide power to an entire site, or for a smaller
subset of loads considered critical to a facility’s operations. For this study, the load scenarios
considered were 50 percent and 100 percent, representing either critical load coverage or full
facility coverage.
Outage Duration. The duration of an outage a microgrid is designed to cover has a significant
impact on the required size of the BESS. This section evaluates BESS sizes required to provide
resiliency for one, three, and seven-day outages.

3.1.5 Results of Solar + BESS Modeling Simulations
Based on these simulations, TRC determined that solar production plays a critical role in
influencing BESS sizing for this site. The analysis indicates that reduced solar production
significantly increases BESS size requirements, with sizing increasing by up to 1 MWh for three-
and seven-day outages when solar output is reduced to 50% or eliminated entirely.
TRC also identified notable differences between the results of the Xendee simulations and TRC’s
proprietary model. These discrepancies arise from Xendee’s focus on a specific week in June for
its analysis, whereas TRC’s model evaluates seasonal variations across the entire year. The
largest differences were observed in high solar production scenarios, highlighting the importance
of accounting for seasonal variability in solar PV output when determining BESS sizing.
Additionally, TRC observed that solar PV currently offsets only about 25% of the site’s average
energy consumption. As a result, covering more than 25% of the site’s load for extended
durations using solar PV alone would necessitate a significantly larger BESS.

As part of this study, TRC developed a High-Level BESS Controls Assessment Narrative, which

the BPSS. It focuses on the controls architecture, communication protocols, and operational
strategies necessary to optimize energy usage, enhance resiliency, and maintain critical loads

infrastructure, including the solar PV array and diesel generator, while ensuring seamless

Key Components and Integration
  BESS Configuration: The proposal includes a 250kWh BESS for backup power and a
 20kWh BESS dedicated to life safety loads. These systems will operate alongside the
 existing 67kW solar PV array and 200kW diesel generator to ensure reliable power
 during outages. The diesel generator could also be used in load shedding applications to
 lessen then dependency on utility provided power and provide some operating cost
 savings.
  Microgrid Controller: The SEL-3350 Real-Time Automation Controller (RTAC) is
 designated as the Microgrid Controller, responsible for managing operational modes
 such as grid-paralleling and islanding, while dynamically balancing loads and generation.

Communication and Monitoring
  Protocols: The system will utilize Modbus or DNP3 communication protocols for data
 exchange between components, with options for serial or TCP/IP communication
 mediums. Existing equipment including the PV controller and backup diesel generator
 have Modbus communication capabilities that will be utilized to pull alarm/metering data
 from those units and provide controls where capable.
  Human Machine Interface (HMI): The SEL-3350 RTAC will host an HMI for real-time
 monitoring, control, and fault management. If integrated with the local area network at the
 BPSS, the HMI will be accessible remotely through a web browser interface, providing
 operators with critical system insights and controls.

Operational Considerations
  Failure Modes: Preliminary safeguards are proposed to monitor system conditions and
 prevent hazardous situations, with detailed design to be developed in subsequent
 phases.
  Load Prioritization: The system will prioritize critical and life safety loads during outages,
 ensuring uninterrupted operation of essential services.
  Load Shedding and Cost Saving Schemes: The system will be able to react to user
 input from the HMI or signals from the utility serving main power to the facility in order to
 activate load shedding and cost saving schemes to help limit costs in peak times. Note
 that a communications path to the utility, which would require additional equipment and
 cooperation with the utility, would need to be established in order to react automatically.

6 Specification Support
BESS Technology Considerations
There are various forms of energy storage currently available on the market, these include:
  Lithium-ion batteries
  Lead-acid batteries
  Flow batteries
  Flywheels
  Ultracapacitors
For this project, a battery energy storage system based on lithium-ion batteries is likely to be the
best choice and this is what is generally assumed will be used. This is because other options
either are not as commercially mature or are not able to provide similar or better cost,
performance, or energy density. When soliciting bids, however, it may be prudent to not explicitly
require that the selected winner be using a lithium-ion product, in case someone is able to
produce a competitive option using a different technology.
Within the realm of lithium-ion batteries, there are various subtypes. Most large-scale commercial
BESS products on the market now use lithium iron phosphate (LFP) chemistry as this tends to
provide the best balance of safety, energy density, and cost. Some products use Nickel
Manganese Cobalt (NMC) instead, an option which offers greater energy density but has
declined in popularity for large applications due to its greater volatility, which makes thermal
runaway events more likely and more dangerous. If an NMC product is used, extra care and
scrutiny must be taken with regards to fire safety aspects of the system and site design.

BESS Vendor Considerations
BESS products are not swappable commodities like AA batteries are – each vendor’s product is
different and will have different requirements for testing and integration as well as ongoing
maintenance and operations. Because of this, it will be important to select a BESS product
relatively early on in the design process as the selected product will inform other aspects of the
design. It will then be important to work with the vendor throughout the design process to ensure
their system is properly integrated.
TRC does not endorse the use of any particular BESS vendor’s products. However, TRC has
worked extensively with Tier 1 BESS vendors and suppliers and are prepared to provide input on
the selection of one as part of the design process.

8 Microgrid Benefits
Modern society’s dependence on continual, reliable, and sustainable power is routinely made
clear by social, economic, and environmental disruptions such as extreme weather events,
emerging digital threats, and public health emergencies. These events highlight the need for
adaptive and innovative approaches for planning, analyzing, and implementing new technologies
and practices. Fortunately, the evolving roles of utilities and customers, new business models,
and industry-wide technical advancements provide the City of Asheville with the opportunity to
lead by example.
While the benefits and applications of microgrids are widely recognized, they are not necessarily
directly quantifiable, consistently valued, or equally distributed to stakeholders and the broader
public. Nevertheless, characterizing and presenting these benefits will remain necessary for
ensuring that this project, and others like it, are adequately considered and appropriately
implemented. This study outlines the monetary and non-monetary benefits of implementing a
microgrid at the BPSS.

8.1 Monetary Benefit Cost Analysis
8.1.1 Electric Bill Savings
The primary mechanism for generating savings is through the operation of the BESS to reduce
demand charges. North Carolina State University utilized the Xendee platform to estimate the
potential electric bill savings of a 250 kWh BESS under the Broadway Public Safety Station’s
current Medium General Service (MGS) rate structure, as well as the MGS Time-of-Use (TOU)
rate structure. Table 6 presents the savings for both scenarios.
The analysis conducted by NC State indicates that switching to the MGS-TOU rate without a
battery would increase the utility bill by 14%. However, incorporating a 250 kWh BESS under the
MGS-TOU rate reduces the bill by 38%, resulting in a net reduction of 29%. Given the significant
savings potential, TRC recommends that Asheville initiate discussions with Duke Energy to
confirm the feasibility and implications of switching rate structures, as this evaluation falls outside
the scope of this preliminary study.

Introduction
Purpose of the Playbook
This playbook delivers general applications of resilient energy systems for community energy
resilience needs and experiences. For the purpose of this playbook, "community" will refer to
not only individual municipalities or districts but also to (1) regional authorities conducting
emergency planning and (2) groups of stakeholders with common interests working in a
common corridor or region to enhance energy resilience and related infrastructure. This
playbook offers solutions and strategies that communities can implement to meet their resilience
needs. It contains lessons learned from real-world examples of community resilience projects
throughout the state. This can help inform program design and long-term planning for
community emergency preparedness.

Much of this playbook refers to "critical facilities" which includes emergency shelters and local or
state emergency operations centers, as well as traditional critical infrastructure such as poles
and wires for communications and electric transmission and distribution. The critical facilities are
intentionally broad and can be defined by the community, local or state authorities, or using
federal standards. Depending on the community's needs, other services or infrastructure can
become critical to the safety of the community. Some communities will have broader or slightly
different definitions of critical facilities; coastal or mountain communities on major evacuation
routes may consider refueling stations "critical" to their own community and evacuees, as
exemplified below. Communities may have municipal complexes which are critical as they
house emergency communications, rescue, and medical services. Communities with large
numbers of medically vulnerable residents may find that their shelters need to be able to
support electrical medical devices and refrigerate medicines to best serve their people.

Many of the benefits described in this guide are resilience and/or resilience services in the
context of energy. The U.S. Department of Energy describes energy resilience as the ability of
communities, buildings, and the grid to withstand and recover from power outages.1
Specifically, energy resilience focuses on the hours to days immediately following an outage
and how to mitigate negative impacts to communities. This is distinct from the concept of
reliability which is an overall statistical analysis of grid performance (i.e. outage frequency and
duration) over longer time periods.

Microgrids and battery storage systems allow critical facilities to continue operations in the event
of a disaster or other power interruption or to restore power more quickly to critical sections of
the grid after an outage has occurred. Resilience services can refer to a business model for
microgrid and backup power systems to provide power, capacity, or other services to utilities
and the local grid during normal operating conditions, and provide resilience to customers
during grid power outages.

Understanding Disaster Impacts
Hurricanes can affect almost all regions of North Carolina. Two exemplary storms to consider
are Hurricane Florence in 2018 and Hurricane Helene in 2024. These two storms created
devastating impacts in eastern and western North Carolina, respectively. The state's Office of
State Budget and Management surveyed the aftermath and created estimates for immediate
impacts from the storms and the overall recovery costs.

Hurricane Helene made landfall in the Southeastern US in September 2024, bringing heavy
rains to states along the east coast. Portions of western North Carolina experienced heavy
rainfall and 1,000-year flooding events in several counties. According to the North Carolina
Governor and the Office of State Budget and Management's preliminary damage and needs
assessment,2 Hurricane Helene amounted to $59.6 billion in recovery costs and is estimated to
have impacted 4.6 million people in 39 counties - 40% of the state's population.

Though the western region of North Carolina had experienced hurricanes and natural disasters
in the past, Hurricane Helene was exceptional in the scope and scale of disaster impact. The
largest impacts were to physical access to the region, impairing travel within and between
communities and for outside disaster response and aid efforts. The mountainous nature of the
region and the flooding isolated many individuals and communities.

Communications infrastructure was also greatly impacted, with wind and flood-related damages
as well as lack of grid power and limitations on fuel and replacement parts moving into the
region, especially in the immediate aftermath of Hurricane Helene. The elevation changes
throughout the region can make communications unreliable or patchy if some, but not all,
communications relays and infrastructure sites are restored.

Hurricane Florence struck North Carolina in September 20183, bringing dangerous storm surge,
record rainfall, and widespread flooding. Preliminary estimates put total damages at $17 billion,
more than triple the cost of Hurricane Matthew in 2016. FEMA ultimately designated 44 counties
for federal disaster assistance, an area home to nearly 4 million North Carolinians, again about
40% of the state’s population.

Communities in eastern and southeastern North Carolina suffered the greatest losses, with
widespread destruction to homes, businesses, agricultural operations, and local infrastructure.
More than a million residents experienced power outages, forcing tens of thousands to seek
safety in emergency shelters.

Prioritizing Critical Community Facilities
Taking a systematic approach to identifying and considering critical
facilities for resilience projects enables efficient development of
energy resilience throughout a community.

A prioritization methodology using available data can help decision-
makers determine a plan of action for deploying resilience projects
at critical facilities as funding becomes available. The priority level
for funding resilience projects is dependent upon locally-understood
threats to resilience. Generally, factors of resilience can be grouped
into three categories: exposure to and preparation for external risks,
ability to adapt to changing conditions, and the rate of return to
baseline operations.17 Facilities that face high exposure to external
risks, suffer significant impacts during those events, and take a
longer amount of time to recover ought to be prioritized for resilience
projects.18

Higher priority facilities for resilience projects might be those that provide critical services, as
defined by the community and/or local leaders, and those interconnected to other systems or
facilities providing community services. Energy, water and wastewater, communications, and
transportation systems are necessary for the operations of nearly every critical facilities sector;
therefore, facilities that make up those systems take priority. Emergency response services and
facilities like 911 call centers, police and fire stations, emergency operations centers, and
hospitals must also retain reliable power to support public safety and crisis mitigation. If
communities have additional time and resources to expand this list of prioritized facilities, they
might also consider adding other facilities whose operational continuity is vital to social and
economic functionality.19

Once a list of critical facilities and their locations has been identified, they can be further
prioritized based on their exposure to hazards. Exposure to external risks can be quantified by
assessing the frequency and severity of natural hazard events in a given area. Facilities
connected to isolated or distant portions of the electric grid, or those in locations especially
vulnerable to natural disasters, benefit from on-site resilience services.

Additionally, the ability of a community or facility to adapt to the changing conditions created by
natural hazards is dependent upon the state of its infrastructure and dependencies of its people.
Facilities that do not have system redundancy to maintain operations in the event of failure or

Resilience Strategies and Technology Solutions

Decision-making process
The process for implementing community resilience projects can be complex, but the first step is
identifying the need for energy resilience. If there is a need for energy resilience, the scope and
breadth of the needed resilience services must be determined by analyzing the opportunities
available in the community, the local electric grid, and the needs of the population.

Partnerships with a community's electric utility or utilities can provide benefits to the community
while meeting utility needs. Local utilities can gain benefits from some resilience projects,
creating the opportunity for partnerships. Several case studies in this playbook explore
partnerships between utilities, electric suppliers, and private operations.

A short checklist for resilience projects might look something like:
 1. Identify vulnerabilities
 2. Identify critical facilities
 a. Proximity to vulnerable households
 b. Location(s) of services and non-government "essential" services
 3. Identify existing backup generation or resilient solutions
 4. Investigate partnership with utility
 5. Compare site(s) loads under blue sky and emergency conditions
 6. Model resilience needs
 a. Model outage durations and locations
 b. Model generation, demand response, storage options
 7. Determine relative costs (e.g. RFI) of system(s)
 a. Is there a cost sharing opportunity with local business or utility?
 8. Determine resilience application(s) to be funded
 a. Seek out grants, public funding, loans, etc
 9. Publish a request for proposals for qualified companies to construct the project.

Technologies/Tools
When considering resilience needs, utilities and emergency managers often have several
traditional and non-traditional resilience solutions (NTS) available to them. How those solutions
are differentiated and the process for selecting the appropriate solution vary by utility and
emergency responder, but ultimately, the goals are the same: to enhance system resilience.

Traditional resilience investments, such as poles, wires, undergrounding, and grid hardening,
must be weighed against non-traditional investments, such as grid and customer scale energy
storage and solar photovoltaic (PV) projects and microgrids. Exploring non-traditional resilience
investments within the utility business model, such as DER projects, is becoming increasingly
viable and is coming in all shapes and sizes. Microgrids specifically, can range in size from
supporting a single customer to providing backup for an entire community. These are promising
technologies that other utilities can incorporate to enhance grid resilience while achieving
carbon reduction and energy storage mandates. For instance, non-traditional approaches to
improving resilience through battery energy projects can improve infrastructure’s ability to
withstand disruptive events while also contributing to a cleaner, more equitable energy future for
their customers and utility. Those storage systems can also defer costly traditional transmission
and distribution upgrades, keeping costs low for customers.

Project Implementation and Best Practices

Guidance for implementing resilience projects
Implementing resilience assets can overcome challenges that communities face when disasters
inevitably strike. Community members, especially vulnerable groups, rely on power to operate
medical equipment, preserve medications and food, and keep their homes habitable. Local
governments must keep communications and emergency services operational. In the case of
widespread disaster impacts, shelters and aid distribution points need power for occupant safety
and communications. Relationships with community organizations can be leveraged to
determine the types of services needed and suggest resilience system locations to best benefit
the communities that they serve.

Local governments can use resilience assets like batteries and solar panels in partnership with
their utility, as seen in examples below, to defray some of the costs of installation by partnering
with their local utilities. A utility can leverage the various assets of the resilience system during
normal grid operations while maintaining the ability of the systems to provide emergency power
to one or more buildings in the event of a grid power interruption. Additional resilience projects
will depend on the existing conditions within local emergency management facilities as well as
community needs.

When planning for resilience needs, officials should consider the backup power needs and
current supplies for their emergency communications and operations center(s), fire stations,
police stations, and other first-response-capable facilities. Then, the proximity of these facilities
should be compared to places that are (or might be) used as shelters or aid distribution points
for affected citizens. For example, a municipal complex or several emergency/first
response/shelter facilities located in close proximity, a microgrid may be able to enhance the
services for all facilities by pooling backup power, renewables, and energy storage resources.

In the case of shelters and evacuation planning, there is an opportunity for public-private
partnerships, again depending on nearby resources. Grocery or convenience stores near
emergency shelters that are served by a microgrid can save store owners money by limiting the
loss of supply while also providing displaced people with a way to procure more medications,
food, and convenience items. In the case of evacuation routes, fueling stations located near
emergency response or operations centers could be linked in a microgrid to keep
communications up and allow evacuees a place to refuel and potentially reprovision in the event
of a power outage.

The NC Clean Energy Technology Center used several tools in the technical assistance
process for the design, analysis, and comparison of various resilience strategies and potential
projects in several localities throughout North Carolina. Though these tools were used for
locations in North Carolina, these tools, along with others, are applicable to all projects and
resilience program evaluations.

REopt21
REopt is a free tool that optimizes the size, costs, and operations of onsite energy systems to
help meet various goals set by the user. REopt can recommend combinations of renewable,
battery, and generator power to reduce the site’s energy costs enough to produce value for the
site operator above and beyond the cost of the system itself. REopt can also configure systems
that meet requirements to serve various outage durations. REopt combines data about energy
requirements, utility bills, financial parameters, and local energy policies with user goals.

Helioscope22
Helioscope is a web-based software tool used to model solar PV systems performance based
on site location. The user can define details about panels and system components, tilt angle,
etc. Once the user completes a design, Helioscope can generate reports on annual and monthly
production, system losses, and technology specifications. This tool requires a paid subscription
after a free trial, and is typically used to visualize and size a solar PV system. The results can
be input into REopt, or used to validate REopt recommendations for the project site.

System Advisor Model (SAM)23
SAM is a free desktop application that is used to analyze the feasibility and economic viability of
energy technologies. SAM can model renewable energy systems, including solar PV and
battery, from rooftop to utility scale. Financial models can be completed for behind the meter
residential and commercial projects, for power purchase agreement (PPA) projects, and for third
party ownership projects. The user selects the desired type of system and then inputs data like
an appropriate weather profile for the area, system specifications, costs, incentives, electricity
rates, etc. to obtain information like the annual and monthly production, hourly output, capacity
factor, levelized cost of energy (LCOE), net present value (NPV), payback, and revenue.

systems and allows building owners, installers, and manufacturers to develop estimates of the
performance of a potential PV system. The user selects an address, confirms coordinates to be
used, inputs system information, dictates a perimeter for the system, and the calculator
produces results for the system. The results for PV systems include the annual and monthly

Practical information for implementation
When considering the addition of solar and/or batteries for a community energy resiliency
project, there are some practical things to keep in mind. In the planning phase, it is important to
determine how a project will shift a building’s utility rate schedule, as providers often reclassify
facilities once renewable energy assets are added. In territories such as Duke Energy in North
Carolina, adding solar or batteries could transition a customer to a Time-of-Use (TOU) rate.
While this can increase a site’s baseline bill, it also offers the greatest opportunity for savings if
the battery is used to offset power during peak price windows. Furthermore, while some facilities
may continue under legacy net metering programs if they added solar before a certain date,
Duke Energy requires a move to TOU schedules for new solar installations if the customer
wants to retain the Renewable Energy Credits (RECs). Engaging with the utility provider early in
the process is best practice to confirm which rules apply to the selected site and how they will
impact your community’s long-term return on investment.

Regarding the equipment itself, insights from a battery energy storage system (BESS) feasibility
study conducted by TRC Engineers, Inc. and the NC Clean Energy Technology Center for the
City of Asheville suggest that procurement should remain flexible yet strategic. For the City of
Asheville’s Broadway Public Safety Station, which already included a 62 kW DC solar array and
a 200 kW diesel generator, TRC Engineers made specific recommendations for adding batteries
to the station. When adding batteries for a community resilience project, there are several types
of batteries on the market, such as lithium-ion batteries, lead-acid batteries, and flow batteries.
While lithium-ion is currently the industry standard due to its commercial maturity and cost-
effectiveness, communities might consider not requiring lithium-ion batteries when writing bid
solicitations to allow for emerging technologies that may offer competitive cost and
performance. Additionally, BESS products are all different and require deep integration with the
facility's infrastructure. Consequently, a battery vendor should be selected early in the design
phase because it will inform other aspects of the design and ensure the system is properly
integrated. If the BESS is intended to be used as a microgrid, a microgrid controller will be
needed. This controller is used to help the building work in parallel with the grid, island itself
from the grid during an emergency, or perform load shedding. It is recommended the community
ensure the procurement specifications require an industrial-grade controller that is built to last,
has no moving parts to break, and includes strong cybersecurity protections to keep your local
grid safe.

By carefully aligning utility rate strategies with early vendor engagement and robust control
technology, communities can ensure their resiliency projects provide both financial stability and
dependable power when it is needed most.

Real-world Examples

Duke Energy Carolinas - Hot Springs microgrid25
In 2017, Duke Energy filed for a Certificate of Public Convenience and Necessity (CPCN) with
the North Carolina Public Utilities Commission (NC PUC) to obtain regulatory approval for a
front-of-the-meter microgrid in Hot Springs, North Carolina. Following a two-year regulatory
review, the project was eventually approved in 2019 and commissioned in January 2023. While
Duke considered more traditional grid investments, such as building a second feeder or
hardening infrastructure, the microgrid was deemed the most cost-effective option for improving
resilience.

The project features a 4.4 MW lithium-ion BESS and a 2 MW AC solar PV system (see above)
and was designed to provide backup power, reduce emissions, and enhance community
resilience. On September 27th, 2024, those objectives were put to the test when Hurricane
Helene swept through the southeast, dropping 14 inches of rain, causing flash flooding,
landslides, and road closures. The storm cut off power to the town of Hot Springs, isolating it at
the end of a ten-mile power line serviced by Duke. The Hot Springs microgrid kicked on and
supplied power to the community and its 520 residents for nearly six full days during the event,
proving its benefits. The project highlighted the potential for non-traditional solutions, such as
microgrids, to support grid resilience with reduced emissions, faster recovery times, and greater
cost-effectiveness.

The Hot Springs microgrid project was a lesson in flexibility and adaptability. The town
strategically reduced loads to extend the microgrid's capability beyond the typical 4-6 hours of
continuous power, covering the town for six consecutive days. Duke Energy’s proactive
communication efforts kept the town energized and informed throughout the crisis.

Conclusion
Incorporating energy resilience into communities requires a proactive and coordinated planning
approach that convenes emergency managers, utility planners, regulators, and local community
members. This community resilience playbook offers a practical approach to doing just that
while highlighting valuable real-world examples of communities that have successfully improved
resilience for their community members.

Communities located far from electric substations (e.g., at the end of long transmission lines,
like Hot Springs or Ocracoke Island) can find that locally sited storage, firm generation, and
demand response can improve the quality of their electric service and lessen or eliminate the
impacts of some grid outages. By siting energy resources near the community load centers, the
community can benefit from resilience services during disasters and a serving utility can benefit
from lower throughput on their transmission infrastructure. Both utilities and communities benefit
from reducing power outage frequency and duration from microgrids.

Emergency managers and local government officials should convene key stakeholders including
commercial and residential community members and organizations along with utilities to
determine the most critical facilities and mitigate known vulnerabilities that can be addressed by
energy resilience projects. The stakeholders can also help determine priorities and operation
strategies for resilience hubs during normal grid conditions and during and immediately after
power outages.

Local governments themselves, or through technical assistance partnerships, can use the tools
listed within this document to determine a site's energy needs, potential energy resource
production, and design strategies to operate and utilize resilience assets. This analysis can
include the use of existing facilities (e.g. back up generation at a municipal building) to support
disaster operations and to maximize the benefits through renewable generation and/or energy
storage.

By quantifying community needs and solutions, emergency managers, community members,
and partners can size resilience projects to best fit community needs. Further, they can analyze
costs and benefits to ensure that community energy resilience services are cost effective.

 Introduction
This report is an addendum to the Community Resilience Playbook, providing information on
metrics for energy resilience. One of the biggest hurdles to planning for energy resilience is the
lack of metrics to help assess proposed investments. This report is meant to bridge this gap in
knowledge by providing quantitative metrics, based on qualitative characteristics, to assist
decisionmakers in their review of energy resilience projects for their community.

This report covers three major areas:
 - Resilience planning
 - Resilience metrics
 - Stakeholder engagement

The resilience planning section focuses on existing policies and frameworks regarding
government and utility efforts to develop resilience frameworks and use those frameworks to
implement resilience projects. The resilience metrics section lays out the quantification of the
value of resilience for the purposes of encouraging future planning and investment. The
stakeholder engagement section reviews how decisionmakers can interact with key players in
their jurisdiction to ensure the needs of their communities are effectively incorporated into
resilience planning, frameworks, and projects.

 Current Landscape of Resilience Planning

Emergency Management and Disaster Response Frameworks

NARUC’s Resilience Framework1

In 2025, the National Association of Regulatory Utility Commissioners (NARUC) convened
experts to develop a resilience framework for state and local policymakers. The principles of
that framework make up the basis of this project, including the metrics report and community
playbook. The framework provides a series of considerations, factors, and decision points for
state and local policymakers as they work to develop and implement resilience measures in
their communities. Those considerations are summarized here.

Setting Goals
Resilience decision-makers should set a vision or destination for their efforts. Starting from
existing laws, statutes, policies, and goals, decision-makers should consider what they would
like to accomplish and how. These resilience outcomes could include creating or updating
system requirements or policy mandates, establishing cost-effectiveness standards, achieving
some level of consumer or economic benefit, or reducing or setting a standard for environmental
impact. The activities to achieve these outcomes, i.e., the resilience measures themselves,
could include increasing situational awareness, hardening infrastructure, deploying new
technologies to mitigate risk and boost recovery, establishing or updating operational criteria,
reducing or regulating emissions, or updating regulatory and market structures to promote
resilience.

Use Cases
Resilience planners should also consider when and why to apply this framework. It can be
important to consider resilience measures when undertaking a number of efforts, such as
developing new policy to set long-term, actionable resilience guidelines; when engaging in long-
term planning, implementing resilience measures can ensure stability in a community and
longevity of plans and investments; providing grants for community solutions to energy and
infrastructure challenges should consider resilience measures so that communities have access
to more than one-time, immediate solutions; and utility programs with a customer-facing angle
should also consider resilience measures to help more customers bounce back after outages
and other hazardous events.

Definitions
It’s important that resilience decision-makers are all singing from the same song book. That
means having a unified understanding of resilience, reliability, and other terms of art.
 1. Resilience: the ability to prepare for threats and hazards, adapt to changing conditions,
 and withstand and recover rapidly from disruptions. Resilience is focused on severe but
 unlikely events, above average impacts, and recovery from and minimization of these
 events and impacts.
 2. Reliability: the ability of the system to respond to normal operating conditions.

The NARUC framework defines many more terms of art that resilience planners, emergency
managers, and policymakers should understand, including the kinds of resilience measures,
levels of event severity, resilience priority zones such as disadvantaged communities and high
risk areas, and resilience service levels that describe how efficient and effective a resilience
measure is.

Process Leadership and Participation
Any resilience framework must consider both the universe of decision-makers and the universe
of community members to engage and educate. In North Carolina, the relevant decision-makers
would include the NC Utilities Commission, the NC State Energy Office, Duke Energy Progress,
Duke Energy Carolinas, ElectriCities of NC, the NC Electric Membership Corporation, the NC
Department of Public Safety and NC Emergency Management, local military branches at Fort
Bragg, the NC Department of Environmental Quality, and more.

Stakeholders and community groups can be engaged via workshops, formal regulatory
proceedings, research projects, and interagency working groups. In North Carolina, these
stakeholders might include local governments and local councils of governments, consumer
advocates, environmental organizations and advocates, and others.

Design Questions
When preparing a resilience plan or resilience measures, decision-makers have a wide range of
factors to consider, and there are some that might slip through the cracks. When considering
resilience metrics, it’s important to understand the availability of existing metrics, the gaps in
data, and what might be needed to fill those gaps. Resilience valuation depends on event
severity, priority zones, and the cost-benefit analysis of various interventions. And, it’s crucial to
understand the existing regulatory frameworks and processes so that resilience decisions can
be made effectively either within existing mechanisms or by creating new ones. This metrics
report considers these factors in its breakdown of resilience metrics and valuation.

Implementation
Finally, before implementing, resilience decision-makers should consider what final decisions
need to be made, what actions are required, when and how will resilience measures be
updated, and how will information be shared with the public. Some of these notes are
considered in the resilience playbook that accompanies this report.

NC State agencies and their roles
There are multiple national frameworks and guides for state and local responses to emergency
management. The Federal Emergency Management Agency’s National Disaster Recovery
Framework was updated in December 2024.2 It sets a series of pre-disaster and post-disaster
best practices, which include resilience planning and implementing resilience measures.
Additionally, the framework discusses the roles of Federal, State, local, and Tribal governments
in the event of an emergency or disaster.

In particular, State government acts as the pass-through for federal support to local
governments, NGOs, businesses, and other disaster recovery partners. States also establish
and implement disaster recovery frameworks and resilience frameworks that complement
federal frameworks, and provide guidelines for local governments and other recovery partners.

Local governments are often responsible for the direct provision of recovery resources, as they
are closest to the broader population. By coordinating with NGOs, businesses, community
organizations, and others, local governments can directly support resilience implementation
before disasters and recovery after disasters. Additionally, local governments may encounter
capacity constraints; coordination with private sector partners in addition to State and Federal
entities can help alleviate these difficulties and increase the supply of aid. Resilience planning
can help build capacity before a disaster, or alleviate the need for recovery resources.
According to FEMA, every dollar spent on resilience and mitigation can save up to six dollars on
recovery.3

In North Carolina, the primary agency responsible for disaster response is NC Emergency
Management (NCEM), within the Department of Public Safety. NCEM leads hazard mitigation
efforts for the state, including providing grants for disaster recovery, supporting work to mitigate
flood risk and build resilient communities, and creating statewide hazard mitigation plans that
local governments can use. NCEM also provides a range of data, metrics, mapping, and
analysis tools to help others in the state understand and mitigate hazard risk.

In recent years, other North Carolina entities have taken significant actions to prepare
communities for hazards and power outages. The State Energy Office has created a $5 million
program to create up to 24 stationary microgrids in counties impacted by hurricane Helene, with
two mobile “beehive” microgrids that can be deployed at locations in need to lend out solar
panels and batteries.4 Additionally, Duke Energy and the State Energy Office received a $57
million grant to reconductor the Lee-Milburnie line to improve resilience by preventing sag and

Energy Resilience in Utility and State Planning
In North Carolina, integrating energy resilience into planning generally takes two forms: planning
by governments and planning by utilities. However, using the term “energy resilience” for this is
vague – government plans tend to focus on general community resilience, while utility plans
tend to focus on energy reliability. “Resilience” and “reliability” differ in that while reliability refers
to a system’s ability to maintain operations, resilience refers to the ability to come back online
after a failure or outage. This report will mostly discuss resilience.

The State of North Carolina encourages resilience planning at the local level, assisting local
governments with creating Hazard Mitigation Plans every five years.6 These plans are meant to
guide local governments and communities to prepare for and recover after natural disasters;
they also allow them to apply for federal disaster funding. With a focus on disaster recovery, the
plans normally estimate the number of critical facilities, including critical energy facilities, that
could be affected by a potential disaster, and how they could be affected. When it comes to
recommendations for energy resilience, the plans focus on energy efficiency improvements,
infrastructure improvements, or fossil fuel-based generators, if mentioned at all.

In December 2024, the North Carolina Office of Recovery and Resiliency released the North
Carolina Resilient Communities Planning Guide7 to provide local governments with the tools to
plan for resilience in their communities. The guide mentions microgrids as a way to ensure
resilience during extreme events, but doesn’t provide key information on how to acquire a
microgrid or make the decision to install one, as the Community Resilience Playbook does.8

The state also hosts a Resilience Exchange hub, connecting community leaders to state-level
resources for resilience.9 The hub helps local leaders understand what resilience hazards they
might face, outlines best practices from around the country to combat hazards, and provides
information on possible funding sources. Many of the energy-related best practices focus on
reliability interventions like energy efficiency upgrades or grid hardening, rather than resilience.

Some reliability metrics can be “translated” into resilience metrics. CAIDI can be replaced with
the number or percentage of critical services without power, SAIDI with the average number or
percentage of critical customers experiencing an outage, and SAIFI with the number of critical
customer-hours of outages or the amount of kWh load not served.15 This also shifts the focus of
the metrics from grid-level to community-level, giving decision-makers a better understanding of
their local characteristics.

Many metrics with a focus on economic values – like cost of recovery, avoided outage cost, or
cost of grid damages – are grid-level, looking at how an outage affects the economics of the
utility. To transition economic metrics to community-level, decision-makers can look at business
interruption costs, cost of losing assets and perishables, or cost to deploy emergency services.

One of the more complex issues with community-level metrics is that key aspects of community
performance are difficult to measure. Important characteristics like trust in leadership, level of
communication to residents, or support from stakeholders cannot be easily quantified for BCAs,
even if decision-makers have an understanding of these characteristics within their community.
Therefore, qualitative aspects have to be proxied through other quantitative aspects.

Others have worked on developing resilience metrics. The Electric Power Research Institute
developed a roadmap identifying gaps in knowledge for valuing resilience,16 while the National
Association of Regulatory Utility Commissioners has its own resilience framework for state-level
utility regulators.17 Other sections discuss these frameworks more in-depth, while also providing
quantitative, community-level, resilience metrics for decisionmakers.

Existing Justifications for Resilience Investments
Utility investments, included in general rate cases, often need a thorough record of evidence to
justify their inclusion in the customer rate base. In these circumstances, BCAs, along with
compelling qualitative evidence that is often case-specific, can be used to justify resilience
investments. As noted above, many utility proceedings acknowledge that quantifying and
measuring many of the benefits and costs of resilience measures can be difficult. However,
proceedings can still provide a record for existing justifications.

 Resilience Metrics as a Part of Resilience
 Planning
Resilience planning is important because it protects communities and infrastructure and
provides human, societal benefits. Some studies of resilience are also interested in quantifying
this value and these benefits. By taking into account existing reliability frameworks, the
likelihood of outages and failures, the cost of disruption, and the ability of a system to resist or
bounce back from failure, researchers can begin to extract a quantifiable value of resilience.
This measurement can be useful in resilience planning and for investing in physical resilience
projects, demonstrating the “return on investment” in terms of infrastructure and quality of life.

State-Level Requirements for Resilience Metrics
Across utilities and regulators in the United States, there is still no consensus on the way to
measure resilience, however, more and more utilities and regulators are accepting the
fundamental definition of resilience as the ability to withstand and recover quickly from impacts
to the grid. Although definitions of “resilience” are still left to be defined at the state level, there is
a clear understanding of the four elements of resilience planning, which are mitigation,
preparedness, response, and recovery.

The top five states with the most regulatory actions on resilience metrics are Washington,
Texas, New York, Connecticut, California, and Massachusetts. Other states, such as Maryland,
the Carolinas, Michigan, Florida, Minnesota, Colorado, etc., also have regulatory dockets that
encourage the development of resiliency metrics for utilities in their states. Overall, more states
mandate resilience metrics than not; there are currently only 10 states without resilience metrics
identified in any dockets or legislation.

Some commonalities between states implementing resilience metrics are linked with
performance-based metrics, defining what each metric is, and how it ties into the overall goals
and objectives of the resilience investments. Examples of state implementation are listed out in
the appendix.

Community-wide resilience requires proactive planning and investment across the entire energy
system throughout the community, rather than reactive projects that patch problems after an
energy resilience failure occurs. Those seeking funding for proactive resilience investments –
local governments, state governments, or utilities – can justify their efforts by referencing their
necessity as part of long-term, system-wide resilience planning. Additionally, enumerating all of
the cascading benefits that come from strengthening resilience will strengthen their case.
Leveraging a system-wide approach to resilience rather than siloed initiatives delivers benefits
more broadly and can help secure funding for an entire portfolio of projects instead of one at a
time.

In 2022, the North Carolina State Energy Office partnered with SEPA to assess energy
resilience across the state and prioritize areas for resilience investment.24 The project team built
and followed a framework throughout the project that has since been applied to similar
evaluations in other states. This framework establishes five steps:

 1. Evaluate baseline energy reliability: assess existing reliability metrics across the
 system and identify areas with frequent outages or chronic service challenges.
 2. Assess external threats: quantify and qualify geographic vulnerabilities and hazards
 that consider remote, end-of-the-line locations or areas with recurring hazards.
 3. Identify community needs: leverage established indices of social vulnerability as well
 as anecdotal utility information that points to which communities experience the greatest
 social and economic impact as a result of outages.
 4. Evaluate existing energy resilience technologies: compare non-wires alternatives
 like microgrids with traditional investments, like line upgrades or generators, for cost-
 effectiveness and feasibility.
 5. Model and customize energy resilience solutions: generate ideas/models of energy
 resilience solutions, like microgrids, based on resilience needs, sizing constraints, cost
 preferences, and carbon emissions goals.

SEPA facilitated the collaboration between the North Carolina State Energy Office and other
stakeholders to develop and receive feedback on the framework. Emergency managers can
incorporate this nationally applicable framework into their planning processes to better prepare
for and recover from future energy grid challenges.

Solutions to Fix BCA Gaps
BCAs, by their nature, often have to use direct quantitative data to reach a conclusion, but this
often leaves out other aspects of a project that either cannot be easily quantified or have indirect
impacts on cost. Resilience projects in particular tend to provide additional benefits that aren’t
always caught in traditional BCAs. From a technical perspective, placing a greater emphasis on
the avoided costs could improve the BCA of a project – instead of upgrading existing
transmission and distribution infrastructure (which would take a significant amount of time and
money, and could still not prevent 100% of all outages), a resilience microgrid could achieve
similar results over a shorter construction period and at a lower cost.

There are also the community and social benefits of resilience that aren’t caught by traditional
analyses, as a microgrid can provide other benefits like improving emergency management
operations, ensuring continued standard government operations during an emergency, or
optimizing resource allocation. If a community has a microgrid to provide electricity – and
therefore still has access to emergency shelters, space heating and cooling, refrigeration, and
other electric-based services – then local governments can pivot resources to other areas

Metrics for Community Energy Needs and Access in North
Carolina
Narrowing down to the local level, we have developed a county-level analysis of community
energy needs in North Carolina. This analysis takes three different aspects of energy use –
essential energy needs, access to critical services, and social vulnerability – to group counties
into similar types, allowing decisionmakers to evaluate their resilience investments against their
resilience needs.

Essential energy needs utilizes public data to estimate which households are at greatest risk
during an outage, based on factors like electricity-dependent medical needs, demographics,
income, and mobility. This provides information on where households are most likely to struggle
during a power outage; the higher the energy needs, the greater the impact of an outage.

Access to critical services measures distances to essential services like schools, groceries,
hospital, and emergency services, based on public GIS data. Areas with poor access to these
services will have greater impacts from an outage, and can compound an individual household’s
vulnerability during an outage.

Social vulnerability captures the extent to which a community is likely to experience
disproportionate impacts from an outage and face greater barriers to preparing for, responding
to, and recovering from disruptions. Counties with higher social vulnerability values generally
have fewer resources and reduced adaptive capacity, meaning the same outage can produce
larger health, safety, and economic consequences than in lower-vulnerability areas.

Typology Analysis:
To support portfolio-scale resilience planning and microgrid siting, we developed a county-
level typology of 1) essential energy needs, 2) access to critical services, and 3) social
vulnerability for counties in North Carolina. The goal of this analysis is to group counties with
similar multi-dimensional profiles so that resilience investments can be evaluated and justified
on a systemwide basis rather than as isolated projects. Methodologically, the work adapts the
contrastive community network framework previously used to study disaster recovery and
transformation, but implements a simplified unsupervised clustering approach tailored to the
metrics and spatial scale of this project.25

First, we quantify essential household energy needs using modeled electricity demand from
NLR’s ResStock platform. ResStock provides end-use–specific residential electricity
consumption estimates that account for building characteristics, climate, and technology mix.
For each county in North Carolina, we aggregate household-level model outputs to construct
a single “essential energy needs” metric that includes electricity used for space heating, space
cooling, refrigeration, and one-half of total plug loads. This definition focuses on services
necessary to maintain life, health, and basic habitability during grid disruptions, excluding
purely discretionary or easily deferable uses. The resulting county-level metric represents the
average essential electricity requirement per household and provides an energy-specific
complement to traditional reliability indices such as SAIDI and SAIFI.

Second, we quantify access to essential community services as an average driving distance
from residents to key facilities. For each county, we identify locations of grocery stores,
hospitals, and public parks and compute the shortest-path driving distance along the road
network from residential population centers to the nearest facility in each category. These
distances are then averaged to produce county-level indicators of spatial access to food,
health care, and recreational green space. Longer distances indicate communities that are
more isolated from critical services under normal operating conditions, and are likely to face
more severe functional disruptions when power outages coincide with transportation or
communication challenges.

Third, we incorporate social vulnerability using the Centers for Disease Control and
Prevention’s Social Vulnerability Index (SVI). The SVI aggregates census-based indicators
into four domains—(i) socioeconomic status (e.g., poverty, unemployment, income,
education), (ii) household composition and disability (e.g., age, disability status, single-parent
households), (iii) minority status and language, and (iv) housing type and transportation (e.g.,
multi-unit structures, crowding, vehicle access). For each county, we extract the overall SVI
score and its component domain scores to represent the proportion of residents who may
have limited capacity to prepare for, cope with, and recover from disruptions. Higher SVI
values denote greater vulnerability, which is particularly relevant when assessing distributional
equity in the benefits of resilience investments.

 18

 To enable joint analysis of these heterogeneous metrics, we normalize all variables to
 percentile ranks across counties. For each metric, we compute the percentile of a county
 relative to the empirical distribution of that metric across all North Carolina counties. This
 transformation yields dimensionless scores on [0,1], removes differences in physical units or
 scale, and facilitates interpretation of “high” or “low” values across metrics. Where necessary,
 we reverse-code variables so that higher percentile values consistently correspond either to
 higher essential energy needs, greater distance to services, or greater social vulnerability.
 The result is a standardized feature vector for each county that jointly characterizes its
 essential energy burden, spatial access to services, and vulnerability profile.

 We then apply unsupervised clustering using k-means to these normalized feature vectors to
 identify groups of counties with similar joint profiles. In k-means, each county is represented
 as a point in a multidimensional space whose axes are the normalized metrics; the algorithm
 partitions the counties into k clusters by minimizing the within-cluster sum of squared
 distances between counties and their assigned cluster centroids. This approach aligns with
 the core idea of contrastive community methods: counties are compared and grouped based
 on their full portfolio of risk factors rather than any single metric, so that the meaning of a
 county’s classification is explicitly relational to its peers. The number of clusters k is selected
 using the silhouette score, which measures how similar a county is to other counties in its
 assigned cluster compared with counties in other clusters.

 In parallel with characterizing counties based on energy needs, access, and vulnerability, we
 also estimate the economic costs of power outages to contextualize the potential benefits of
 resilience investments. Using the Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory’s Interruption Cost
 Estimate (ICE) Calculator, we calculate expected outage costs for each county based on the
 estimated number of residential and commercial customers and representative outage
 durations derived from statewide reliability indices such as SAIDI and SAIFI. The ICE
 Calculator provides sector-specific cost functions that translate outage duration and frequency
 into lost economic value, accounting for direct and indirect costs of service interruptions. By
 combining these functions with county-level customer counts, we generate annualized
 estimates of total outage costs under current reliability conditions. These estimates serve two
 purposes: first, they establish a financial baseline against which the benefits of resilience
 interventions (e.g., microgrids) can be compared, and second, they highlight how outage
 impacts vary geographically, revealing where resilience investments may yield the greatest
 economic return per dollar spent.

New Justifications for Resilience Investments
Along with new metrics to improve BCAs, there are also regulatory mechanisms and program
designs that could be implemented to provide additional justifications for investing in resilience
projects, including alternative ratemaking or utility program models.

Alternative Ratemaking Mechanisms
Under a traditional ratemaking structure, utilities receive revenue based on the amount of
electricity they sell and earn a return based on capital investments. Alternative ratemaking
mechanisms can instead base utility revenue on utility performance to meet certain goals.
Alternative structures include decoupling revenue from electricity sales, providing incentives or
penalties based on achievement of certain goals, and other mechanisms. Alternative
ratemaking, when implemented properly, can shift a utility’s motivation away from selling the
maximum amount of electricity or building the maximum amount of capital-funded facilities and
towards specific performance goals. For example, North Carolina regulators implemented
performance metrics26 regarding worst performing circuits for Duke Energy. Duke must file
annual reports27 on its worst performing circuits in the state, then take steps to improve their
performance, after which the utility can receive an incentive for exceeding the stated goals.

Performance incentives tied to traditional utility metrics for resilience and reliability – like CAIDI,
SAIDI, and SAIFI – can encourage a utility to improve their electric grid by providing an
incentive for “better” results and a penalty for “worse” results. These incentives can also be
tailored to fit certain circumstances (like extreme weather versus standard operations) or
locations (like areas that have a disproportionately higher amount of outages), thereby
increasing the motivation for a utility to fix specific resilience issues.

Performance incentives can also be tied to actual deployment of capital investments, or
associated cost recovery. By providing a monetary incentive for microgrid or resilience projects
to service areas with significant resilience issues, regulators could ensure effective utility
deployment of resilience investments while also providing a pathway for the hardest-hit
locations to receive resilience benefits.

customer expectations and access to DERs, utility prioritization in T&D deferrals, and developer
interest in diversified portfolios that enable customer acquisitions. For EaaS models, a customer

 Stakeholder Engagement
The usefulness and implementation of products like policy frameworks or technical solutions
hinges on the inclusion of those most affected by natural disasters in their development.28 A
deep understanding of community-specific resilience factors is crucial to measuring baseline
resilience posture, quantifying needs, and prioritizing facilities and communities for investment.
While state and federal frameworks often rely on broad categorizations, resilience efforts thrive
when informed by the granular, on-the-ground insights offered by community members, local
institutions, emergency managers, and infrastructure providers. This inclusive approach not only
strengthens planning and evaluation, but also fosters trust, improves outcomes, and ensures
that resilience efforts truly reflect and address the priorities of the communities they aim to
serve.

Geography, history, population dynamics, economic posture, and resource access impact a
community’s resilience. State and federal frameworks often group vulnerabilities into broad
categories, but effective community resilience planning, evaluations and project deployment
depends on retrieving the granular detail behind these community priorities. On-the-ground
implementers of resilience initiatives and community preservation like community centers,
utilities, local and state governments, emergency managers, and first responders play a key role
in defining resilience needs (such as historical and economic stressors, and compounded risks
and patterns from extreme weather) for a specific community.

Community engagement helps define resilience needs by supplementing decision-makers’
understanding of the factors that shape community resilience. It provides anecdotal and
qualitative data not captured by existing metrics or quantitative sources, shapes future data
collection and monitoring, and refines definitions of “disadvantaged” or vulnerable populations.

The Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency - established by Congress in 2018 to
manage risks for digital and physical critical infrastructure - recommends including service
providers such as energy, communications, transportation, and water and wastewater sectors,
along with community representatives who can identify critical infrastructure considered
essential to daily community functions.29

Effective stakeholder engagement combines structured methods like surveys and
questionnaires with informal approaches such as interviews and focus groups. Engagement
starts by identifying existing contacts and asking for recommendations on additional participants

To develop stakeholder-informed and data-driven metrics, practitioners should first set clear
goals and objectives. Then they should draft preliminary metrics for stakeholders to review and
offer feedback on through focus groups, surveys, or one-on one meetings to tailor engagement
methods to stakeholder preferences and to create a comfortable space for sharing input.
Promoting the project broadly encourages self-identification and comment submission from
interested parties. Throughout the process teams should collect and integrate stakeholder
feedback into project development, acknowledge contributions in reports and presentations, and
remain mindful of engagement timing to avoid burdening participants.

The Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency advises that while not all stakeholders
need to participate in every phase, planners should prioritize engagement when their expertise
is most relevant and valuable. Highlighting benefits like faster recovery from power outages,
energy cost savings, improved hazard mitigation, and alignment with community priorities can
encourage stakeholder participation, particularly from private sector stakeholders.30

Successful resilience planning implementation and investments rely on strong partnerships
between utilities, communities, developers, and regulators who are deliberate on clear
communication, collaboration, and understanding of shared goals.

 ➢ Utilities can play a central role through public-private partnerships, innovative rate
 designs, and/or special tariffs for critical customers.
 ➢ Community members offer essential local knowledge about risks, needs, and priorities
 that may not be apparent from the outside.
 ➢ Developers contribute by integrating on-site energy solutions like microgrids to provide
 community resilience, while also leveraging available tax incentives and reducing energy
 costs for customers and utilities.
 ➢ Regulators and government officials support implementation by participating in
 proceedings/dockets, shaping policy, and influencing investment decisions.

Together, these partners can align actions, resources, and decision-making to advance effective
resilience investments.

 Conclusion
By integrating new characteristics into BCAs, a decisionmaker can have a better understanding
of the true value of a resilience microgrid system and therefore make a more informed decision
on whether to pursue a project. Issues like lack of access to critical services, loss of electric-
based medical services, or overburdended government resources can all be partially mitigated
with resilience microgrids, but traditional metrics and BCA models do not always capture those
benefits. It is critical to assess the community and societal benefits of resilience, and not just the
direct economic or grid impacts, in order to understand the true value of these systems and
ensure that investments provide a holistic boon to residents.

To put this information to action, the Community Resilience Playbook provides a step-by-step
process for decisionmakers to consider their concerns and options when it comes to
community-level energy resilience, while the Community Analysis Calculator provides a
mathematical starting point for decisionmakers to learn what types of problems their community
faces when it comes to energy resilience. For additional information or questions, contact the
NC Clean Energy Technology center at nccleantech@ncsu.edu.

Connecticut In 2022, the Connecticut Public Utilities Regulatory Authority adopted
 reliability and resilience frameworks for Eversource and United
Docket No. 17-12- Illuminating, meant to improve system performance during standard
03RE08 operation, minor emergencies, and major emergencies; prioritize
 delivering increased resilience; maximize benefits by and return-on-

 annual reports outlining their actions during the past year under the

 The Resilience framework focused on mitigation, preparedness,
 response, and recovery. Utility resilience plans must include the
 identification of vulnerabilities and zone selection criteria, and the
 selection of mitigation measures via a cost-effective methodology. The
 framework classifies internal lineworker resources as a type of resilience
 measure, particularly in relation to storm response. Utilities must also
 evaluate, measure, and verify any chosen solutions, including benefit-
 costs analyses, rate impact studies, measurement and data collection,
 and verification of solution effectiveness.

 In 2023, during Baltimore Gas and Electric’s rate case, the Maryland
 Public Service Commission deemed that certain costs in the utility’s

New York Orange & Rockland’s Climate Change Resilience Plan highlights Sandia
 National Laboratories “Resilience Metrics for the Electric Power System:
Case No. 21-E- A Performance-Based Approach” report, which lays out a Resilience
0074 Analysis Process (RAP) for performance-based resilience metrics. The
 RAP framework includes the following elements: defining resilience

 goals, defining consequence & resilience metrics, characterizing

 Orange & Rockland believed that rather than the typical standardized
 metrics like SAIDI and SAIFI, RAP provides a framework for customizing
 metrics specific to the state or utility jurisdiction. The RAP framework is
 meant to quantify the effects of grid disruptions, either as direct
 consequences like interruption costs or indirect consequences like the
 amount of population without power. The utility also suggested other
 resilience performance measures like major outage events, major event

Oregon As part of its 2024 rejection of Portland General Electric’s Clean Energy
 Plan, the Oregon Public Utility Commission argued that there was not
Docket No. LC 80 sufficient data to support the plan and that the plan was inconsistent with
 the state’s emission targets. In addition, there were concerns about their
 modeling of future emissions, a lack of focus on renewable energy and
 an increased reliance on fossil fuels, as well as a poor assessment on
 community benefits and resilience within the plan.

 The Commission ordered the utility to develop informational and portfolio
 community benefit indicators and provide baseline metrics prior to its
 next Clean Energy Plan filing. The Commission wanted a clear
 quantification of benefits, which included community-based resilience
 benefits as well as system benefits. While no clear metrics were decided
 on in this docket, the Commission demanded that the assessment and
 evaluation of such measures be more clearly defined.

Texas During the 2023 legislative session and in response to Winter Storm Uri,
 the Texas Legislature established requirements for utilities to file
H.B. 2555 (2023) transmission & distribution resiliency plans with the Public Utility
 Commission of Texas. Eligible measures for resilience include hardening
 and modernizing transmission & distribution facilities; undergrounding
 certain distribution lines; lightning or flood mitigation measures; IT,
 cybersecurity, and physical security measures; vegetation management;
 or wildfire mitigation and response.

 For each measure, a utility must provide a metric or criteria for
 evaluating the effectiveness of the measure during emergency events,
 including qualitative metrics. A utility must also distinguish these
 measures from other programs, like storm hardening or standard
 vegetation management, and explain how the resilience measures work
 in conjunction with other programs.
