Dissipativity Analysis of Nonlinear Systems: A Linear–Radial Kernel-based Approach
Xiuzhen Ye, Wentao Tang

Abstract
Estimating the dissipativity of nonlinear systems from empirical data is useful for the analysis and control of nonlinear systems, especially when an accurate model is unavailable. Based on a Koopman operator model of the nonlinear system on a reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS), the storage function and supply rate functions are expressed as kernel quadratic forms , through which the dissipative inequality is expressed as a linear operator inequality . The RKHS is specified by a linear–radial kernel , which inherently encode the information of equilibrium point, thus ensuring that all functions in the RKHS are “locally at least linear” around the origin and that kernel quadratic forms are “locally at least quadratic”, which expressively generalize conventional quadratic forms (including sum-of-squares polynomials). Based on the kernel matrices of the sampled data, the dissipativity estimation can be posed as a finite-dimensional convex optimization problem, and a statistical learning bound can be derived on the kernel quadratic form for the probabilistic approximate correctness of dissipativity estimation.

Linear–Radial Kernel and its RKHS

\omega_{k}\in\mathring{H}^{s}(\mathbb{X}):x\mapsto w_{k}^{\top}x
.

Remark 2

Suppose that
s(y,u)=h(x,u)^{\top}u-\epsilon|y|^{2}
(
d_{y}=d_{u}
).
If
h_{k}\in\mathring{H}^{s}(\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{U})=\mathcal{H}_{\mathring{\varkappa}}(\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{U})
for all
k=1,\dots,d_{y}
, then, via
u_{k}=\langle e_{d_{x}+k},\,\mathring{\varphi}_{x,u}\rangle
, we obtain

\textstyle s(y,u)=\sum_{k=1}^{d_{y}}\langle h_{k},\,\mathring{\varphi}_{x,u}\rangle\langle e_{d_{x}+k},\,\mathring{\varphi}_{x,u}\rangle-\epsilon|\langle h_{k},\,\mathring{\varphi}_{x,u}\rangle|^{2}.

Then by letting

\textstyle S=\sum_{k=1}^{d_{y}}\left(\frac{1}{2}h_{k}\times e_{d_{x}+k}+\frac{1}{2}e_{d_{x}+k}\times h_{k}-\epsilon h_{k}\times h_{k}\right),

we reach at
s(y,u)=\langle\mathring{\varphi}_{x,u},\,S\mathring{\varphi}_{x,u}\rangle
.

As seen from the examples, when the supply or storage function can be written as a linear combination of squares or products of
\mathring{H}^{s}
-type functions, then it is a kernel quadratic form specified by a
finite-rank
adjoint operator.
The class of finite-rank adjoint operators, however, cannot be complete. For completion, we endow the adjoint operators with a norm.
We say that
P
on Hilbert space
\mathcal{G}
belongs to the Hilbert–Schmidt class
B_{2}(\mathcal{G})
if under any orthonormal basis
\{p_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
, it can be written as
P=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{j}p_{j}\times p_{j}
with
(\lambda_{j})_{j=1}^{\infty}\in\ell^{2}
. In this case, we define the Hilbert–Schmidt norm
\|P\|_{B_{2}}=\left(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}|\lambda_{j}|^{2}\right)^{1/2}
.
The space
B_{2}(\mathcal{G})
is then a complete, actually Hilbert, space, in which the subspace of finite-rank operators is dense
[
12
]
.

III-B

Dissipative Inequality

We recall that the adjoint Koopman operator
K^{*}
satisfies the relation in Corollary
2
. Hence, if the storage function
v
is a kernel quadratic form specified by a positive semidefinite self-adjoint operator
P
(i.e.,
\langle g,\,Pg\rangle\geq 0
for all
g\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathring{\kappa}}(\mathbb{X})
, then
v(x)=\langle\mathring{\phi}_{x},\,P\mathring{\phi}_{x}\rangle
and hence

\displaystyle v(f(x,u))

\displaystyle=\langle\mathring{\phi}_{f(x,u)},\,P\mathring{\phi}_{f(x,u)}\rangle=\langle K\mathring{\varphi}_{(x,u)},\,PK\mathring{\varphi}_{(x,u)}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\mathring{\varphi}_{(x,u)},\,K^{*}PK\mathring{\varphi}_{(x,u)}\rangle.

To convert
v(x)
, which is a kernel quadratic form on
\mathcal{H}_{\kappa}(\mathbb{X})
, to a kernel quadratic form on
\mathcal{H}_{\mathring{\varkappa}}(\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{U})
, we need a linear operator that maps
\mathring{\varphi}_{(x,u)}
to
\mathring{\phi}_{x}
.
To this end, define
E:\mathring{H}^{s}(\mathbb{X})\rightarrow\mathring{H}^{s}(\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{U})
,
g\mapsto Eg
, where
(Eg)(x,u)=g(x)
. In other words,
E
is the embedding of state-dependent functions into state- and input-dependent functions.

Lemma 4

Under Assumption
1
,
E\in B(\mathring{H}^{s}(\mathbb{X}),\mathring{H}^{s}(\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{U}))
, and its adjoint operator
E^{*}\in B(\mathring{H}^{s}(\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{U}),\mathring{H}^{s}(\mathbb{X}))
satisfies
E^{*}\mathring{\varphi}_{(x,u)}=\mathring{\phi}_{x}
for all
(x,u)\in\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{U}
.

With the conclusion of the above lemma, we easily obtain

v(x)=\langle E^{\ast}\mathring{\varphi}_{(x,u)},\,PE^{\ast}\mathring{\varphi}_{(x,u)}\rangle=\langle\mathring{\varphi}_{(x,u)},\,EPE^{\ast}\mathring{\varphi}_{(x,u)}\rangle.

If, in addition, the supply rate function
s(h(x,u),u)
can also be written as a kernel quadratic form
\langle\mathring{\varphi}_{(x,u)},\,S\mathring{\varphi}_{(x,u)}\rangle
with some self-adjoint operator
S
(for which Remark
2
gives a typical example), then the dissipative inequality (
2
) becomes the following linear operator inequality, imposed on all state–input pairs in
\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{U}
.

Theorem 1
(Linear operator inequality for dissipativity)

Suppose that Assumption
1
hold. For the system (
1
) to be dissipative with respect to a supply rate
s(y,u)=\langle\mathring{\varphi}_{(x,u)},\,S\mathring{\varphi}_{(x,u)}\rangle
, it is sufficient that there exists a positive semidefinite operator
P\in B(\mathcal{H}_{\mathring{\kappa}}(\mathbb{X}),\mathcal{H}_{\mathring{\kappa}}(\mathbb{X}))
such that
\forall(x,u)\in\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{U}
,

\langle\mathring{\varphi}_{(x,u)},\,\left(KPK^{*}-EPE^{*}-S\right)\mathring{\varphi}_{(x,u)}\rangle\leq 0.

(5)

In this case, the storage function can be chosen as
v(x)=\langle\mathring{\phi}_{x},\,P\mathring{\phi}_{x}\rangle
.
Moreover, if
S
is Hilbert–Schmidt, then as long as a positive semidefinite operator solution exists for
P
, a Hilbert–Schmidt solution for
P
exists.

Proof:

The first half of the theorem is obvious. For the latter half of the claim, we note that if
S
is finite-rank, say
S=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\omega_{i}\times\omega_{i}
, then any solution
P
to (
7
), when replaced by
\Pi P\Pi^{\ast}
where
\Pi
is the projection onto
\mathrm{span}\{K^{\ast}\omega_{i},E^{\ast}\omega_{i}\}_{i=1}^{r}
, is still a solution to (
7
) and is finite-rank.
The case where
S
is Hilbert–Schmidt can then be proved following the denseness of finite-rank operators.
∎

Remark 3

While the sufficiency of (
7
) is obvious, the necessity requires that the storage function
v
be indeed a kernel quadratic form.
Following the theory of dissipative systems
[
14
]
, it is possible to construct an “maximum available storage function” by

\textstyle v^{*}(x)=\sup_{(x_{0},u_{0},\cdots,x_{k})\in\mathbb{T}(x)}\sum_{t=0}^{k-1}s(h(x_{t},u_{t}),u_{t}),

where
\mathbb{T}(x)
is the collection of trajectories on which
x_{0}=x
is steered to
x_{k}=0
by inputs
u_{0},\cdots,u_{k-1}\in\mathbb{U}
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
If for all
x
,
\mathbb{T}(x)\neq\emptyset
, then
v^{\ast}
is nonnegative, bounded, and satisfies the dissipative inequality.
For
s
in a kernel quadratic form, the spectral decomposition
S=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\omega_{i}^{2}
with
\omega_{i}\in\mathring{H}_{s}(\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{U})
gives

\textstyle v^{\ast}(x)=\sup_{\mathbb{T}(x)}\sum_{t=0}^{k-1}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\omega_{i}(x_{t},u_{t})^{2}.

Each
\omega_{i}
, due to Assumption
1
, as a function of
x
must belong to
\mathring{H}^{s}(\mathbb{X})
. Therefore, each trajectory
\tau\in\mathbb{T}(x)
determines a sum of squares of
\mathring{H}^{s}(\mathbb{X})
-type functions of
x
, namely a kernel quadratic form, say
\langle\mathring{\phi}_{x},\,P_{\tau}\mathring{\phi}_{x}\rangle
. However, the point-wise supremum over
\tau\in\mathbb{T}(x)
may not be guaranteed to yield a kernel quadratic form.
At this point, the conditions that ensure
v^{\ast}\in\mathring{H}^{s}(\mathbb{X})
remains an interesting open problem worth future studies.

Remark 4

We wrote the inequality (
5
) as a
pointwise inequality
at all
\mathring{\varphi}_{(x,u)}
, instead of a negative semidefiniteness constraint
KPK^{*}-EPE^{*}-S\preceq 0
. The latter is a sufficient condition, which, however, can be too strong.
After all, we are concerned with dissipative inequality as an inequality over all
(x,u)
-pairs. Their canonical features
\mathring{\varphi}_{(x,u)}
only occupy a
(d_{x}+d_{u})
-dimensional compact manifold in the infinite-dimensional RKHS.

III-C

Finite-Rank Approximation from Data

The verification of linear operator inequality (
5
) at all
(x,u)
-pairs, however, is tractable only in a finite data-driven approximate setting. That is, we collect a sample
\mathbb{S}=\{(x_{i},u_{i},x_{i}^{+})\}_{i=0}^{n-1}
, where
x_{i}^{+}=f(x_{i},u_{i})
for all
i\in\mathbb{I}_{n}
, and consider (
5
) as
n
inequality constraints on
P
and
S
.
In this setting,
P
and
S
should both be approximated as finite-rank operators, which allow matrix representations and convert (
5
) into data-based linear matrix inequalities. We will discuss such approximations and analyze the resulting statistical error bounds in the next section.

Let the finite-rank approximations of
S
and a corresponding approximation of
P
be given by:

\textstyle\hat{S}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\theta^{S}_{ij}\mathring{\varphi}_{(x_{i},u_{i})}\times\mathring{\varphi}_{(x_{j},u_{j})},\,\hat{P}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\theta^{P}_{ij}\mathring{\phi}_{x_{i}}\times\mathring{\phi}_{x_{j}}.

(6)

Denote the matrices of coefficients
\Theta_{S}=[\theta^{S}_{ij}]_{i,j=1}^{n}
and
\Theta_{P}
, and kernel matrices
G_{xx}=[\mathring{\kappa}(x_{i},x_{j})]_{i,j=1}^{n}
,
G_{xx^{+}}=[\mathring{\kappa}(x_{i},x_{j}^{+})]_{i,j=1}^{n}
, and
G_{xu}=[\mathring{\varkappa}((x_{i},u_{i}),(x_{j},u_{j}))]_{i,j=1}^{n}
.
We can verify that for each
k\in\mathbb{I}_{n}
,

\langle\mathring{\varphi}_{(x_{k},u_{k})},\,K\hat{P}K^{*}\mathring{\varphi}_{(x_{k},u_{k})}\rangle=[G_{xx^{+}}^{\top}\Theta_{P}G_{xx^{+}}]_{kk}

\langle\mathring{\varphi}_{(x_{k},u_{k})},\,E\hat{P}E^{*}\mathring{\varphi}_{(x_{k},u_{k})}\rangle=[G_{xx}^{\top}\Theta_{P}G_{xx}]_{kk},

\langle\mathring{\varphi}_{(x_{k},u_{k})},\,\hat{S}\mathring{\varphi}_{(x_{k},u_{k})}\rangle=[G_{xu}^{\top}\Theta_{S}G_{xu}]_{kk}.

Now the inequality of interest (
5
) is approximated as the following linear matrix inequality on
\Theta_{P}
and
\Theta_{S}
:

\mathrm{diag}\left(G_{xx^{+}}^{\top}\Theta_{P}G_{xx^{+}}-G_{xx}^{\top}\Theta_{P}G_{xx}-G_{xu}^{\top}\Theta_{S}G_{xu}\right)\leq 0.

(7)

Naturally, finite-rank approximation results in an error in dissipativity estimation.
Suppose that
S
is also estimated by
\hat{S}
, we solve a finite-rank operator
\hat{P}
that satisfies (
7
) and let
\hat{v}(x)=\langle\mathring{\phi}_{x},\,\hat{P}\mathring{\phi}_{x}\rangle
.
When evaluating the actual dissipation:

\displaystyle w(x,u)

\displaystyle=\hat{v}(f(x,u))-\hat{v}(x)-s(h(x,u),u)

(8)

\displaystyle=\langle\mathring{\varphi}_{(x,u)},\,\underbrace{(K\hat{P}K^{\ast}-E\hat{P}E^{\ast}-S)}_{=:W}\mathring{\varphi}_{(x,u)}\rangle

it may not be non-positive, although on the sample points, replacing
W
by
\hat{W}=K\hat{P}K^{\ast}-E\hat{P}E^{\ast}-\hat{S}
guarantees non-positivity. The following operator error bound is obvious.

Lemma 5

Suppose that
S
is Hilbert–Schmidt with estimation error
\|S-\hat{S}\|_{B_{2}}\leq\epsilon_{S}
, and that
\hat{P}
is a finite-rank operator satisfying (
7
). Then
\|W-\hat{W}\|_{B_{2}}\leq\epsilon_{S}
.

The operator norm error in
W
turns out to give a resulting bound on the square-scaled violation of dissipativity.

Corollary 4

Under the conditions of Lemma
5
, we have

w(x_{i},u_{i})/|(x_{i},u_{i})|^{2}\leq\epsilon_{S}\rho(0),\kern 5.0pti\in\mathbb{I}_{n}\text{ with }|(x_{i},u_{i})|\neq 0.

That is, on the sample, the violation of dissipativity is bounded by at most a quadratic function of
(x,u)
.

Proof:

Since for all
i\in\mathbb{I}_{n}
,
\langle\mathring{\varphi}_{(x_{i},u_{i})},\,\hat{W}\mathring{\varphi}_{(x_{i},u_{i})}\rangle\leq 0
holds, we have

\langle\mathring{\varphi}_{(x_{i},u_{i})},\,W\mathring{\varphi}_{(x_{i},u_{i})}\rangle\leq\langle\mathring{\varphi}_{(x_{i},u_{i})},\,(W-\hat{W})\mathring{\varphi}_{(x_{i},u_{i})}\rangle\leq\|W-\hat{W}\|_{B_{2}}\|\mathring{\varphi}_{(x_{i},u_{i})}\|^{2}
, where, due to (
4
), we get
\|\mathring{\varphi}_{(x_{i},u_{i})}\|^{2}=|(x_{i},u_{i})|^{2}\rho(0)
.
∎

Remark 5

If the system is indeed dissipative with respect to supply rate
s(y,u)=\langle\mathring{\varphi}_{(x,u)},\,S\mathring{\varphi}_{(x,u)}\rangle
, then (
7
) is feasible if
\hat{S}
is equal to the projection of
S
onto
\mathrm{span}\{\mathring{\varphi}_{(x_{i},u_{i})}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, denoted as
S_{n}
. However, when
\hat{S}
is estimated with an operator error
\epsilon_{S}
from
S
, i.e., a maximum error of
\epsilon_{S}
from
S_{n}
, (
7
) may be infeasible. Nevertheless, feasibility can be restored if we relax (
7
) to

\displaystyle\left[G_{xx^{+}}^{\top}\Theta_{P}G_{xx^{+}}-G_{xx}^{\top}\Theta_{P}G_{xx}-G_{xu}^{\top}\Theta_{S}G_{xu}\right]_{ii}\leq

(9)

\displaystyle\quad\epsilon_{S}\|\mathring{\varphi}_{(x_{i},u_{i})}\|^{2}=\epsilon_{S}\rho(0)|(x_{i},u_{i})|^{2},\kern 5.0pt\forall i\in\mathbb{I}_{n}.

After this, the bound guaranteed in Corollary
4
becomes
w(x_{i},u_{i})/|(x_{i},u_{i})|^{2}\leq 2\epsilon_{S}\rho(0)
.

Remark 6

While Koopman operator
K
and the embedding operator
E
appear in the operator formulation,
they do not need to be explicitly estimated from data
, as the dataset
\mathbb{S}
already covers their behavior in the recorded snapshots.
This not only simplifies the computation but also the eschews from Koopman operator learning, where one must calculate
G_{xx}^{-1}
(or its regularized version) as an approximate inverse of a “sampling operator”. The theoretical analysis of Koopman operator learning typically involves spectral theory or notions of interpolation spaces; see Kostic et al.
[
11
]
.

IV
DATA-DRIVEN ESTIMATION OF DISSIPATIVITY AND GENERALIZATION ERROR

Now we study how
\hat{S}
is estimated from the data sample and how the resulting
\epsilon_{S}
depends on the sample size, based on which
\hat{P}
is solved from (
7
) or its relaxed version (
9
).

Assumption 2

The supply rate
s(h(x,u),u)
can be written as
\sum_{j=1}^{m}q_{j}^{(0)}(x,u)q_{j}^{(1)}(x,u)
where
q_{j}^{(0)},q_{j}^{(1)}\in\mathring{H}^{s}(\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{U})=\mathcal{H}_{\mathring{\varkappa}}(\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{U})
for all
j=1,\dots,m
, where
m<\infty
.
7
7

7

As seen in Remark
2
, such a finite-rank assumption is appropriate and covers most practical case. If one considers a general Hilbert–Schmidt case, i.e.,
s
contains an infinite number of products of
H^{s}
functions, a finite-term truncation would be needed, and the truncation error will need to be accounted for in
\epsilon_{S}
.

Now obviously we have the true
S
operator expressed as

\textstyle S=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{1}{2}(q_{j}^{(0)}\times q_{j}^{(1)}+q_{j}^{(1)}\times q_{j}^{(0)}).

IV-A

Estimation of
S
by Kernel Ridge Regression

The approximation of functions
q_{j}^{(0)},q_{j}^{(1)}
(
j\in\mathbb{I}_{m}
) on the dataset
\{(x_{i},u_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
, with their function values at these
n
points, boils down to a kernel-based regression problem.
The typical formulation is a
kernel ridge regression
(KRR) one, where we seek approximations of the form

\textstyle\hat{q}_{j}^{(0)}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\eta_{ji}^{(0)}\mathring{\varphi}_{(x_{i},u_{i})},

where the coefficients
\eta_{jk}^{(0)}
are determined by solving the following convex optimization problem:

\textstyle\min\,\sum_{k=1}^{n}\|\langle\hat{q}_{j}^{(0)},\,\mathring{\varphi}_{(x_{k},u_{k})}\rangle-q_{j}^{(0)}(x_{k},u_{k})\|^{2}+\gamma\|\hat{q}_{j}^{(0)}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathring{\varkappa}}}^{2}.

The same formulation is repeated for
q_{j}^{(1)}
and for all
j\in\mathbb{I}_{m}
. The closed-form solutions for the vector of coefficients are

\eta_{j}^{(\ell)}=(G_{xu}+\beta I_{n})^{-1}\left[q_{j}^{(\ell)}(x_{k},u_{k})\right]_{k=1}^{n},\kern 5.0ptj\in\mathbb{I}_{m},\ell\in\mathbb{I}_{2}.

(10)

The resulting estimation of operator
S
is then written as

\textstyle\hat{S}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{1}{2}(\hat{q}_{j}^{(0)}\times\hat{q}_{j}^{(1)}+\hat{q}_{j}^{(1)}\times\hat{q}_{j}^{(0)})

which can then be expressed as
\hat{S}=\sum_{i,i^{\prime}=1}^{n}\theta^{S}_{i,i^{\prime}}\mathring{\varphi}_{(x_{i},u_{i})}\times\mathring{\varphi}_{(x_{i^{\prime}},u_{i^{\prime}})}
, where
\theta^{S}_{ii^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{m}(\eta_{ji}^{(0)}\eta_{ji^{\prime}}^{(1)}+\eta_{ji}^{(1)}\eta_{ji^{\prime}}^{(0)})
.
More compactly, denote
\mathrm{H}^{(\ell)}=[\eta_{ji}^{(\ell)}]\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
, then

\textstyle\Theta_{S}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathrm{H}^{(0)\top}\mathrm{H}^{(1)}+\mathrm{H}^{(1)\top}\mathrm{H}^{(0)}\right).

(11)

As we can see,
\beta>0
is a regularization parameter that plays the role of ensuring numerical stability and preventing overfitting. Its choice can be tuned by cross-validation. The following conclusion from Smale & Zhou
[
21
, Cor. 3]
gives the approximation error of KRR on RKHS, which we only specialize in our context.

Lemma 6

Suppose that
\{(x_{i},u_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}\in\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{U}
are drawn from the uniform distribution. Then there exists a constant
c>0
, such that for all
j\in\mathbb{I}_{m}
and
\ell\in\mathbb{I}_{2}
, we have

\|q_{j}^{(\ell)}-\hat{q}_{j}^{(\ell)}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathring{\varkappa}}}\leq cn^{-1/3}\log(2/\delta)

with probability
1-\delta
over random sampling.

Corollary 5

Under the condition of Lemma
6
and Assumption
2
, there exists a constant
c>0
such that

\|S-\hat{S}\|_{B_{2}}\leq cn^{-1/3}\log(2m/\delta)

with probability
1-\delta
over random sampling.

Combining Corollaries
4
and
5
, with probability
1-\delta
, we have
w(x_{i},u_{i})/|(x_{i},u_{i})|^{2}\leq cn^{-1/3}\log(2m/\delta)
for all
i\in\mathbb{I}_{n}
with
|(x_{i},u_{i})|\neq 0
, where
c>0
is a constant.

IV-B

Generalization Error Bound

At the end, we should generalize the scaled violation of dissipativity to all points
(x,u)\in\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{U}
.
With
W
being a self-adjoint Hilbert–Schmidt operator, the kernel quadratic form that it specifies, if scaled by
|(x,u)|
near the origin, or
|(x,u)|^{2}
away from the origin, should be a function with nice continuity property. Hence, when the data sample is rich, the bound in Corollary
4
can be extended from the sample points onto the whole region “without much inflation”.

Lemma 7

Suppose that Assumption
1
hold and that
W
is a self-adjoint and Hilbert–Schmidt operator on
\mathcal{H}_{\mathring{\varkappa}}(\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{U})
. Let
\overline{w}_{1}(x,u)=w(x,u)/|(x,u)|
and
\overline{w}_{2}(x,u)=w(x,u)/|(x,u)|^{2}
, and
r>0
small enough such that
\mathbb{B}_{r}:=\{(x,u)\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}+d_{u}}:|(x,u)|<r\}\subset\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{U}
.
Then
\overline{w}_{1}\in C^{\alpha}(\mathbb{B}_{r},\mathbb{R})
, the class of continuous functions with Hölder exponent
\alpha
, with
\alpha=1\wedge(s-(d_{x}+d_{u})/2)
, while
\overline{w}_{2}\in C^{\alpha}((\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{U})\backslash\mathbb{B}_{r},\mathbb{R})
.
The modulus of Hölder continuity can be chosen as
\propto r^{-1}
.

Proof:

Due to the Sobolev embedding theorem,
H^{s}\hookrightarrow C^{\alpha}
. We claim that any function of the form

\textstyle\omega(z)=\frac{1}{|z|}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{d}z_{k}\omega_{k}(z)\right)^{2},

where all
\omega_{1},\cdots,\omega_{k}\in H^{s}(\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{U})
, is
C^{\alpha}
at the origin.
This can be shown by the Lipschitz continuity of the
z_{k}z_{l}/|z|
term and the Hölder continuity of the product term
\omega_{k}\omega_{l}
.

Now
W
is Hilbert–Schmidt,
w(x,u)=\langle\mathring{\varphi}_{(x,u)},\,W\mathring{\varphi}_{(x,u)}\rangle
can be written as
\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{d_{x}+d_{u}}z_{k}\omega_{ik}(z)\right)^{2}
, with the squared
H^{s}
-norms of
\omega_{ik}
summable. Hence,
\overline{w}_{1}\in C^{\alpha}
in
\mathbb{B}_{r}
.
Since
\mathbb{X}
and
\mathbb{U}
are bounded, outside of
\mathbb{B}_{r}
,
1/|z|^{2}
is Lipschitz continuous. By elementary calculation similar to the proof for
\overline{w}_{1}
, it can be shown that
\overline{w}_{2}
remains in
H^{s}
, and hence
C^{\alpha}
.
∎

Theorem 2
(Generalized error of dissipativity violation)

Suppose that Assumptions
1
and
2
hold. Then
\exists c>0
, such that at large
n
, with probability
1-\delta
, we have

\textstyle\frac{w(x,u)}{|(x,u)|^{2}\vee|(x,u)|}\leq c\left(\left(\frac{\log(n/\delta)}{n}\right)^{\alpha^{*}}+\frac{1}{n^{1/3}}\log\frac{4m}{\delta}\right)

(12)

for all
(x,u)\in\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{U}
, where
\alpha^{*}=\frac{1}{d_{x}+d_{u}}\wedge(\frac{s}{d_{x}+d_{u}}-\frac{1}{2})
.

Proof:

Denote
\eta=\sup_{(x,u)\in\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{U}}\min_{i\in\mathbb{I}_{n}}|(x,u)-(x_{i},u_{i})|
as the fill distance of the sample.
Let
r>\eta
be such that
\mathbb{B}_{r+\eta}\subset\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{U}
. For any
(x,u)
, if
|(x,u)|<r
then there exists a sample point
(x_{i},u_{i})\in\mathbb{B}_{r+\eta}
with distance not exceeding
\eta
, and hence due to the Hölder continuity of
\overline{w}_{1}
,
w(x,u)/|(x,u)|\leq\epsilon_{S}\rho(0)+c_{1}\eta^{\alpha}
; if
|(x,u)|\geq r
then there exists a
(x_{i},u_{i})
with
|(x_{i},u_{i})|\geq r-\eta
, and hence due to the Hölder continuity of
\overline{w}_{1}
,
w(x,u)/|(x,u)|^{2}\leq\epsilon_{S}\rho(0)+c_{2}\eta^{\alpha}/(r-\eta)
. Here
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
are constants. In both cases, the right-hand-side error bound is bounded by a linear combination of
\eta^{\alpha}
and
\epsilon_{S}
.
Since
\eta\leq c_{\eta}(\log(n/\delta)/n)^{\alpha}
with probability
1-\delta/2
for some
c_{\eta}>0

[
30
]
, and
\epsilon_{S}
was given in Lemma
5
, we obtain the desired conclusion.
∎

To validate the proposed data-driven dissipativity estimation, we evaluate its performance with three case studies.
In all experiments, the storage function is solved under constraint (
7
) with a minimization objective of
\mathrm{tr}(G_{xx}^{\top}PG_{xx})
(sum of the storage values at sample points), using
cvxpy
.

V-A

Case 1: Polynomial System with Analytical Storage

To show the precision of the proposed method, we consider a synthetic polynomial system:

\textstyle x_{1}^{+}=\frac{1}{2}\left(x_{2}+x_{1}^{2}\right),\kern 5.0ptx_{2}^{+}=u-\frac{1}{4}\left(x_{2}+x_{1}^{2}\right)^{2},\kern 5.0pty=x_{1}

and aim to find a storage function that verifies the rate
s(y,u)=\frac{1}{4}u^{2}-y^{2}
(which implies
\ell^{2}
-gain). A ground-truth storage function that fits this given
s
, and actually satisfies exactly
v(x^{+})-v(x)=s(y,u)
, is the quartic polynomial:

\textstyle v^{*}(x)=x_{1}^{2}+\frac{1}{4}(x_{2}+x_{1}^{2})^{2}.

We sample
8
trajectories, each with a length of
10
time steps, initial state randomly located in
\mathcal{X}=[-2,2]\times[-2,2]
and inputs randomly sampled from
u_{t}\in[-1,1]
. We examine (i) whether
v_{\ast}
can be learned precisely from data under the specified
s
with the proposed approach, (ii) if
v(x)
is restricted to be a simple quadratic form
v(x)=x^{\top}Mx
, whether finite
\ell^{2}
-gain property can still be verified from data, and (iii) if we assume an “oracle lifting”
\psi(x)=(x_{1},x_{2}+x_{1}^{2})
and restrict
v(x)=\psi(x)^{\top}M\psi(x)
, whether the estimated
v
appear close to the ground truth.

The ground-truth
v^{\ast}
and the estimates from three experiments in order are shown in Fig.
1
.
It can be claimed that our linear–radial kernel-based approach precisely learned the storage function (depicted as
\hat{v}_{1}(x)
), close to the case as if we had an “oracle” that
v
is a quadratic form of
\psi(x)=(x_{1},x_{2}+x_{1}^{2})
(depicted as
\hat{v}_{3}(x)
).
In contrast, the storage function restricted to a simple quadratic form (
\hat{v}_{2}(x)

The foregoing results are for the case
\beta=1/4
.
In addition, numerical simulations verify there is no feasible storage function if we set
\beta<1/4

Case 2: Inverted Pendulum

Consider an inverted pendulum with continuous-time dynamics, discretized by a Runge–Kutta 4th-order method with sampling interval
\Delta t=0.05
:

\dot{x}_{1}=x_{2},\kern 5.0pt\dot{x}_{2}=\sin x_{1}-\gamma(x_{2})+u,\kern 5.0pty_{1}=x_{1},\kern 5.0pty_{2}=x_{2},

where
x_{1}
is the angle,
x_{2}
is the angular velocity, and
u
is the external torque.
The friction
\gamma(x_{2})
is assumed to be unknown, but of sectorial nonlinearity:
x_{2}\gamma(x_{2})\geq ax_{2}^{2}
for some
a>0
.
We consider supply rate of the type
s(x,u)=\beta\sin^{2}y_{1}+y_{2}u
, and aim to find the lowest
\beta
under which there still exists a feasible storage function dissipative with respect to
s
.
When choosing this form of
s
, we view
y_{2}u
as the power provided by the external torque and
\sin^{2}y_{1}
as a term associated with the instability of the system.
To understand the theoretical lower bound on
\beta
, consider a candidate storage
v(x)=1-\cos x_{1}+\frac{1}{2}x_{2}^{2}
, for which

\displaystyle\dot{v}(x)

\displaystyle=2x_{2}\sin x_{1}-x_{2}\gamma(x_{2})+x_{2}u

\displaystyle\leq 2x_{2}\sin x_{1}-ax_{2}^{2}+x_{2}u\leq(1/a)\sin^{2}x_{1}+x_{2}u.

Hence, all
\beta\geq 1/a
are valid.
When generating the simulation data, we use
\gamma(x_{2})=x_{2}(1+x_{2}\tanh x_{2})/6
, which verifies
a=1/6
.

:
Storage function estimate (left) and dissipativity satisfaction check (right) for Case 2 under
\beta=6
.

We sample
75
snapshots, with the state uniformly sampled from
\mathcal{X}=[-\pi,\pi]\times[-2,2]
and the input
u\in[-0.5,0.5]
. Setting
\beta=6
, dissipativity learning returns a feasible storage function.
The evaluation of the dissipative inequality on
500
new snapshots sampled from the same distribution validates the result, with few violations (
4.2\%
).
The learned
v
profile, as plotted in Fig.
2
, however, is very different from the “physically intuitive” storage
v(x)=1-\cos x_{1}+\frac{1}{2}x_{2}^{2}
(plotted in the gray shadow), which is the mechanical energy of the “non-inverted pendulum” (scaled by
\Delta t
).
This shows that the learned storage function is
drastically less conservative
.

Case 3: Bioreactor

Finally, we consider a bioreactor
[
17
, Problem II.23]
:

\textstyle\dot{x}_{1}=(\mu(x_{2})-1-u)x_{1},\text{ where }\mu(x_{2})=\frac{1+x_{2}}{1+0.7x_{2}+0.05x_{2}^{2}},

\textstyle\dot{x}_{2}=(1+u)(20-x_{2})-20(1+x_{1})\mu(x_{2}),\kern 5.0pty=x_{2}.

The two states represent scaled deviations from steady-state biomass and substrate concentrations, and the
\mu
function represents the biomass growth rate. The input
u
is a dilution rate. Here, physical intuition is not enough to readily give us a guess on the storage or supply rate function.
We investigate whether the system is
(Q,S,R)
-dissipative with respect to any supply rate in the form of
s(y,u)=qy^{2}+yu
for some
q<0
, and we seek to find a choice of
q
so that the violation of dissipativity,
w(x,u)
, is empirically small.

In the case study, we sample
100
snapshots over
\mathbb{X}=[-0.5,0.5]\times[-0.5,0.5]
and
\mathbb{U}=[-0.25,0.25]
. A feasible storage function found and the dissipativity validation results are shown in Fig.
3
, for
q=2.0
.
We discover an empirical storage function with nontrivial profile. The violation to the dissipative inequality
w(x,u)
, which is positive on
5.6\%
of the sample points as plotted in Fig.
3
, is bounded apparently by
|(x,u)|^{2}
away from the origin. This observation confirms the conclusion of Theorem
2
.

VI
CONCLUSIONS

A data-driven dissipativity estimation method for nonlinear systems, based on operator theory and approximated with data, is presented.
Building on the tool of linear–radial kernels, the dissipative inequality is estimated under linear matrix inequality constraints, which ensures the dissipativity on the sample points in a bounded region and can be generalized to all points therein, with bounded violation of dissipativity (scaled by distance or squared distance to the equilibrium point) that vanishes at large data limit.
The learning of dissipativity can be used in multiple potential ways, e.g., to characterize plant-model mismatch in robust control problems, to certify stability, and to design dissipativity-based controllers in a model-free approach.

An existing limitation of this work is the dependence on state data, which may not be accessible when the states are not measured fully.
This is in fact a common issue in Koopman operator-related works. Overcoming it would require data-driven state observer synthesis and even
operator modeling directly from input–output data
. These important problems are worth examination by researchers.
