Hypofractionated stereotactic radiation therapy for optic nerve sheath meningiomas: A single-center experience and preliminary results
Authors: YuanYou Yang, FengXia Luo, Chao Wang, Gang Ren, Yi Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPrimary optic nerve sheath meningioma (pONSM) causes progressive vision loss. Surgery carries high morbidity (~ 94% visual impairment), and conventional fractionated radiotherapy (CFRT; 50.4–54 Gy) requires an extended course. Given the low α/β ratio of meningiomas (~ 3.76 Gy), we investigated hypofractionated stereotactic radiotherapy (hypo-FSRT; 40 Gy/10 fx) as an alternative.MethodsThis retrospective STROBE-compliant study (2016–2023) included imaging-confirmed pONSM patients (PS 0–2) unsuitable for or refusing surgery/CFRT. Hypo-FSRT delivered via gamma-ray system with thermoplastic immobilization. PTV = GTV + 3 mm, prescribed to 60% isodose. OAR constraints: optic chiasm/retina/pituitary Dmax ≤ 25 Gy. Visual acuity assessed by Snellen; regression defined as ≥ 1 mm reduction on consecutive MRIs. Statistics: paired t-tests, chi-square, logistic regression (OR, 95% CI), Kaplan-Meier with log-rank; p < 0.05 significant. Progression: any increase in axial/sagittal diameter on ≥ 2 consecutive MRIs. Local control: no progression at last follow-up. Baseline visual impairment duration categorized as acute (≤ 2 months onset-to-RT) or chronic (> 2 months).Results43 patients were included (median follow-up 59 months). Overall vision preservation was 30.2% (13/43). Preservation rates by baseline status: normal 40% (4/10), mild loss 63.6% (7/11), moderate loss 12.5% (1/8), severe loss 7.1% (1/14). Age (OR = 4.21) and mild baseline loss (OR = 7.89) predicted preservation. Tumor control was 100%, regression 76.7% (higher in females: 85.3% vs. 44.4%, p = 0.020). Acute toxicity: reversible ocular pain (16.3%), eyelid edema (13.9%); late retinal hemorrhage (7.0%). No secondary malignancies. Time to blindness was shorter with moderate/severe vs. normal/mild baseline loss (p < 0.001). In post-hoc analysis, acute visual loss (≤ 2 months) showed higher preservation than chronic loss (> 2 months) (62.5% [5/8] vs. 22.9% [8/35]; OR = 5.36). All acute cases were in normal/mild baseline subgroup (preservation: 75.0% and 80.0%, respectively).ConclusionHypo-FSRT yielded 100% tumor control and 76.7% regression, but lower vision preservation (30.2%) than CFRT, particularly with moderate/severe baseline loss. Acute impairment (≤ 2 months) predicts better preservation. Low preservation with normal baseline vision necessitates individualized planning balancing tumor control, vision, and cosmesis. CFRT preferred for vision preservation; optimal fractionation needs prospective validation.

Introduction

Primary optic nerve sheath meningioma (pONSM) originates from the arachnoid meningeal epithelial cell layer and accounts for 1–2% of all meningiomas [1–2]. Compression of the optic nerve affects its vascular supply, eventually leading to visual loss [3]. Surgical resection has historically been associated with poor functional outcomes, including a 25% risk of recurrence and postoperative visual impairment in approximately 94% of cases [4]. Current management prioritizes vision preservation via radiotherapy over surgical resection due to high postoperative morbidity [4–5]. Conventional fractionated radiotherapy (CFRT; 50.4–54 Gy in 28–30 fractions) is standard, but hypofractionated regimens are theoretically advantageous given meningiomas’ low α/β ratio (α/β = 3.76 Gy based on Vernimmen et al. [6]). Hypofractionated radiotherapy has demonstrated efficacy and is recommended in current NCCN guidelines for tumors with low α/β ratios (e.g., prostate and breast cancer). Therefore, hypofractionated stereotactic radiotherapy (hypo-FSRT) is theoretically advantageous for ONSM. This study evaluates hypo-FSRT (40 Gy/10 fractions) as an alternative approach in terms of efficacy, safety, and visual outcomes.

Methods

Study design and patient selection

This retrospective cohort study followed Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines. From October 2016 to September 2023, pONSM patients who met the eligibility criteria were treated with hypo-FSRT. Inclusion criteria: (1) Imaging-confirmed pONSM; (2) Unsuitability for or refusal of surgery; (3) Performance status (PS) 0–2; (4) Refusal of CFRT due to treatment duration. Additional clinical data collection: The duration of baseline visual impairment was defined as the time from the onset of visual loss symptoms to the initiation of radiotherapy, extracted from original disease course records. Patients were categorized into two groups: acute visual impairment (≤ 2 months) and chronic visual impairment (> 2 months), to analyze its impact on visual preservation outcomes. Exclusion criteria: previous history of orbital radiotherapy; Severe hepatic/renal dysfunction (due to potential impaired radiation tolerance); Other contraindications to radiotherapy included inability to cooperate with radiotherapy and pregnancy. Informed consent was obtained from patients or patients’ guardian before treatment. The collection and evaluation of protected patient health information complied with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA). Informed consent for publishing potentially identifiable images was obtained from patients’ guardians and documented. This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from our institutional ethics committee (Approval No.: IRB-AF-37-03-3).

Radiation treatment

All patients were treated with hypo-FSRT using gamma-ray treatment system (manufactured by Shenzhen Haibo Technology Co., Ltd.) based on principles similar to the Gamma Knife (Elekta AB, Stockholm, Sweden). The head was immobilized using a thermoplastic mask, and CT simulation was performed with 2-mm slice thickness following the administration of iodinated contrast medium. Pre-treatment contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans were fused with CT simulation images to assist in the delineation of target volume and organs at risk (OAR).

The gross tumor volume (GTV) was the tumor seen on the imaging, expanded isotropically by 3 mm to form the planning target volume (PTV). The median prescribed isodose curve was 60% (55%-75%) with a dose of 40 Gy in 10 fractions over two weeks. EQD2 for tumor control (α/β = 3.76) was 53.89 Gy, which was close to EQD2 (48.65–52.13 Gy) for conventional fractionation (50.4–54 Gy).

OAR dose constraints were adapted from Liu et al. [7], who treated ONSM with 1–2 fractions using Sweden Elekta’s gamma knife. Due to tumor encasement, optic nerve within GTV was not constrained to ensure target coverage, and the surrounding normal tissues outside the PTV were constrained to receive ≤ 10 Gy where feasible. Based on the biological equivalent dose (BED) formula and assuming an α/β ratio of 2–3 Gy for cranial nerves [8], the BED of 10 Gy in a single fraction is comparable to that of 25 Gy delivered in 10 fractions. Therefore, we require that the maximum dose (Dmax) of the OAR should be ≤ 25 Gy, which is as follows: the Dmax for the optic nerve (beyond GTV), optic chiasm, retina, and pituitary gland was constrained to ≤ 25 Gy. When proximity of the ipsilateral retina or optic chiasm to the tumor precluded meeting the constraints, priority was given to respecting the OAR dose limits over target volume coverage, considering the benign nature of pONSM. The Dmax of the lens was <5 Gy.

Main outcome measures

Visual acuity was assessed via Snellen charts—standardized tools developed by Dutch ophthalmologist Hermann Snellen in 1862 to measure distance visual clarity/sharpness. These charts contain rows of letters (optotypes) that decrease in size progressively. Visual acuity was graded per International Classification of Diseases (ICD-11) criteria: ≥0.8 (normal, Class 0), 0.32–0.79 (mild loss, Class 1), > 0.1 and < 0.32 (moderate loss, Class 2), ≤ 0.1 (severe loss, Class 3). Vision preservation was defined as stable or improved visual acuity.

Complete blood counts were performed weekly during radiotherapy to monitor for acute hematologic toxicity. After treatment, pituitary function tests, contrast-enhanced orbital MRI, visual acuity assessment, and proptosis measurements were conducted every 6 months. Changes in visual field were documented solely based on patients’ subjective reports.

We referred to the tumor assessment method of Koc et al. [9], Tumor progression was defined as any increase in D1 (axial diameter) or D2 (sagittal diameter) in at least 2 consecutive MRI scans; stable disease was considered if both dimensions remained stable, and regression was considered in case of any decrease in either parameter (≥ 1 mm change for clinical significance). All responses other than progression were classified as local control (LC). Tumor classification adhered to the criteria established by Schick et al. [10]. Type I lesions are confined purely to the orbit; Type Ia refers to tumors restricted to a flat extension around the optic nerve; Type Ib presents as a large bulbiform mass growing concentrically around the optic nerve with marked proptosis; and Type Ic exhibits exophytic growth on the optic nerve. Type II lesions are located intraorbitally and extend through the optic canal or superior orbital fissure; Type IIa specifically grows through the optic canal, while Type IIb involves the orbital apex and superior orbital fissure, possibly with cavernous sinus infiltration. Type III ONSMs are intraorbital with significant intracranial extension: Type IIIa extends to the chiasm, and Type IIIb involves the chiasm up to the contralateral optic nerve and the planum sphenoidale, with intracranial growth appearing either diffuse grasslike or nodular.

Statistical analysis

Continuous data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Paired t-tests were used to analyze changes in visual acuity, proptosis, and tumor size before and after treatment. Chi-square tests compared categorical variables between groups (including vision preservation rates by baseline visual impairment duration). Binary logistic regression was used to identify independent factors influencing vision preservation and tumor regression outcomes; odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) were calculated to quantify effect sizes. Given the small sample size (n = 43) and limited vision preservation events (n = 13), we only included clinically meaningful variables (age, baseline visual acuity, baseline visual impairment duration, sex, tumor volume) in the regression model to avoid overfitting. Time-to-event analysis (e.g., time to blindness) was performed using the Kaplan-Meier method, with group comparisons via the log-rank test. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0 software (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 43 patients were enrolled. The median follow-up time was 59 months, with a follow-up cutoff date of December 31, 2024. The baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of the cohort are summarized in Table 1. Based on disease course records, the duration of baseline visual impairment (time from visual loss onset to radiotherapy initiation) was acute (≤ 2 months) in 8 patients (18.6%) and chronic (> 2 months) in 35 patients (81.4%) (Table 1). Dose-volume histogram (DVH) data were available for a subset of 11 patients (due to departmental equipment upgrades). In this subgroup, the mean maximum dose (Dmax) to the affected optic nerve was 24.82 ± 3.87 Gy (median, 24.64 Gy; range, 2.64–40.00 Gy), and the mean dose (Dmean) was 16.01 ± 3.32 Gy (median, 16.32 Gy; range, 0.56–36.56 Gy).

Kaplan-Meier analysis for time to blindness showed significantly worse visual prognosis for patients with moderate or severe baseline visual loss compared to those with normal/mild loss (Fig. 1), with detailed preservation rates over time as follows: in the favorable group (normal/mild baseline vision), rates were 90.5% at 12 months, 71.4% at 24 months, and 66.3% at 42 months, with a mean time to blindness of 65.27 months (95% CI, 51.97 to 78.58); in the unfavorable group (moderate/severe baseline vision), rates were 40.9% at 12 months, 18.2% at 24 months, and 4.4% at 41 months, with a mean time to blindness of 16.04 months (95% CI, 10.15 to 21.94). The difference between groups was statistically significant (Log-rank χ² = 22.844, p < 0.001).

Imaging characteristics and tumor regression outcome

Following treatment, significant reductions were observed in both axial (D1) and sagittal (D2) tumor dimensions (for both, p < 0.001). Tumor control rate reached 100%, with no patients showing radiographic progression (an increase in D1/D2 in 2 consecutive MRIs) during the follow-up period. The tumor regression rate was 76.7% (33/43), with median time to regression of 13 months. Radiographic regression patterns, rates, and the time to regression are detailed in Table 5. Discussion

Different radiotherapy techniques for ONSM show no significant differences in tumor control prognosis [11, 12]. Reported tumor control rates include: approximately 90% for two-dimensional radiotherapy (2DRT), 96.1% for three-dimensional conformal radiotherapy (3DCRT), 97.8% for fractionated stereotactic radiotherapy, 97.1% for intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT), 96.3% for proton beam therapy (PBT) and 90.6% for stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS) [13]. Furdova et al. [2] recommend conventional fractionated radiotherapy (CFRT) with a dose of 50.4 Gy in 28 fractions or 54 Gy in 30 fractions. In the case of radiosurgery, hypo-FSRT is recommended, and single-dose treatment is reserved only for patients with complete vision loss. In our study, hypo-FSRT (40 Gy/10 fractions) achieved excellent tumor control (100%), with vision preservation in a subset of patients. We found that younger age and worse baseline visual acuity were associated with vision loss, and female sex predicted higher tumor regression.

CFRT regimens (total dose 45–59.4 Gy) for ONSM are associated with visual acuity preservation rates of approximately 90% [14–16]. The meta-analysis by Yash et al. [17] included 20 studies involving 444 ONSM patients. Patients received a median dose of 50.4 Gy (range 45–59.4 Gy) in 25–33 fractions, with a median follow-up of 51.15 months. The tumor control rate was 99.8% (95% CI, 98.3%-100%), and the proportion of patients with stable or improved visual acuity was 89.7% (95% CI, 86.2%-92.4%). Tang et al. [18] reported a vision preservation rate of only 37.2% in 43 ONSM patients treated with CFRT (median follow-up 54.1 months), a tumor control rate of 92%, and a tumor regression rate of 16.3%. Koc et al. [9] treated 36 ONSM patients with hypo-FSRT using a median total dose of 25 Gy (range, 20–25 Gy) and a median fraction size of 5 Gy (range, 4–7.5 Gy). With a median follow-up of 106 months, the vision preservation rate was also high at 89%, and the tumor control rate was 89%. In the present study, our hypo-FSRT regimen (higher biological dose) achieved a 30.2% vision preservation rate, 100% tumor control rate, and 76.7% tumor regression rate. These findings indicate that both CFRT and hypo-FSRT can preserve vision and control tumors, but our hypo-FSRT regimen was more effective for tumor regression yet less effective for vision preservation than most CFRT reports [14–17] and Koc et al.’s hypo-FSRT schedule [9].

A particularly concerning finding is the vision preservation rate of only 40% (4/10) in patients with normal baseline visual acuity, which is substantially lower than the 80–90% rates typically reported with CFRT [14–17] and largely accounts for the low overall vision preservation rate in this study. Several factors may explain this observation. First, the equivalent dose in 2-Gy fractions (EQD2) delivered to the optic nerve (assuming α/β = 2 Gy [19]) in our regimen was 60 Gy, which exceeds both the EQD2 range of CFRT regimens (42.75–56.43 Gy) and the EQD2 of the hypo-FSRT schedule reported by Koc et al. [9] (43.75 Gy). This elevated biologically effective dose may cause subclinical radiation-induced damage to the normal optic nerve even in the absence of pre-existing impairment. Second, the highly focused irradiation from the gamma-ray system resulted in high central doses (up to 40 Gy) to the optic nerve within the GTV, exceeding the tolerance threshold proposed by Timmerman [20] for 10-fraction schedules and potentially contributing to radiation-induced optic neuropathy (RION). Third, the small sample size of this subgroup (n = 10) may introduce selection bias. These findings highlight that hypo-FSRT should be used with extreme caution in patients with normal baseline vision, and CFRT remains the preferred treatment for this subgroup to maximize vision preservation.

The radiation tolerance of the optic pathway is a key determinant of visual outcomes after hypofractionated radiotherapy. Li et al. [21], in a large cohort study of 514 patients with skull base tumors treated with proton/photon radiotherapy, reported that the cumulative incidence of RION was only 1% in patients receiving < 59 Gy (RBE) to the optic pathway but increased to 5.8% in those receiving ≥ 60 Gy (RBE); older age and female sex were identified as independent risk factors for RION. Our findings are consistent with this dose-response relationship: the EQD2 delivered to the optic nerve in our regimen (60 Gy) approaches the 60 Gy (RBE) threshold identified by Li et al., which may explain the relatively high rate of visual deterioration in our cohort. Notably, while Li et al. found female sex to be a strong risk factor for RION (SHR = 16.3, p = 0.007), we observed no significant association between sex and vision preservation (p = 0.559), although female sex did predict higher tumor regression. This discrepancy may reflect differences in tumor types and radiotherapy modalities between the two studies and warrants further investigation.

A key novel finding from our analysis is that the duration of baseline visual impairment—defined as the time from visual loss onset to radiotherapy initiation—is an independent predictor of vision preservation. Patients with acute visual impairment (≤ 2 months) had a significantly higher vision preservation rate (62.5%, 5/8) than those with chronic impairment (> 2 months, 22.9%, 8/35; p = 0.028, OR = 5.36). Stratified analysis confirmed that all acute cases were in the normal/mild baseline vision subgroup, with preservation rates of 75.0%-80.0%, significantly higher than the corresponding chronic subgroup rates (16.7% and 50.0%). This aligns with the clinical insight that acute visual loss is often associated with early-stage tumors and minimal irreversible optic nerve damage, whereas chronic loss reflects long-standing compression and axonal degeneration that is less responsive to radiotherapy. These findings have important clinical implications: for patients with acute visual impairment (< 2 months), CFRT should be prioritized to leverage its gentler effect on the optic nerve; for those with chronic visual loss who prioritize tumor regression and cosmetic improvement over vision preservation, hypo-FSRT may represent a reasonable alternative.

Our study found baseline visual acuity strongly predicted vision preservation outcomes. The highest vision preservation rate (63.63%) was observed in patients with mild baseline vision loss, compared with 12.5% in the group with moderate vision loss and 7.14% in the group with severe vision loss. Furthermore, mild baseline vision loss was a significant predictor of vision preservation (p = 0.014), and normal baseline vision showed a trend towards significance (p = 0.060). Tang et al. [18] found that severe visual loss before treatment was significantly associated with reduced improvement in visual acuity after treatment (OR = 4.73, 95% CI 10.106–21.15, p = 0.04). Pintea et al. [22] reported a mean pre-treatment visual acuity of 0.52 in the improvement group vs. 0.153 in the non-improvement group, with no improvement in patients with pre-treatment acuity < 0.4. Thus, patients with better pre-treatment vision are more likely to preserve vision, while those with worse baseline vision have lower preservation rates. For patients with poor pre-treatment vision, if cosmetic outcomes are not the priority, surgical feasibility should be thoroughly evaluated, as both surgery and radiotherapy carry a risk of complete visual loss.

Age emerged as a significant factor, with patients aged ≥ 39 years showing significantly higher vision preservation rates than younger patients (45.8% vs. 10.5%, p = 0.030). However, the literature on age effects is inconsistent: Eckert et al. [23] found that age > 48.3 years predicted vision loss (p = 0.01) in their analysis of 26 ONSM cases. Further investigation is needed to clarify the relationship between age and visual outcomes. Regarding tumor characteristics, we found no significant association between vision preservation and tumor volume, location, or sex (p = 0.524, p = 0.811, and p = 0.559, respectively), although Yash et al. [17] reported a significant correlation between GTV size and visual outcomes (p = 0.014) in their meta-analysis, and Pintea et al. [22] found that patients with combined intra-orbital and intra-cranial lesions had poorer functional prognosis.

The high tumor regression rate observed in our study (76.7%) translated into measurable clinical benefit, with significant reduction in proptosis (median 2 mm, p < 0.001) and improved cosmesis—an outcome particularly valued by patients. A novel finding was that female sex independently predicted higher tumor regression (85.3% vs. 44.4% in males; OR = 6.92, p = 0.029). While most literature focuses on tumor control rates (~ 100%) with limited analysis of factors affecting regression, Koc et al. [9] reported a 64% regression rate but did not identify predictors. This sex-related difference in tumor regression has rarely been reported and warrants further investigation in larger cohorts.

Kaplan-Meier analysis revealed the profound impact of baseline vision on long-term outcomes: the mean time to blindness was 65.3 months in patients with normal/mild baseline vision versus only 16.0 months in those with moderate/severe baseline loss, with preservation rates plummeting to 4.4% by 41 months in the latter group. This underscores the rapid risk of blindness for patients with compromised vision at treatment initiation and highlights the importance of early intervention.

Acute toxicities in our study were mild and resolved rapidly with steroid management. The incidence of late retinal hemorrhage (7.0%) was comparable to previous reports [17]. Notably, all three patients who developed retinal hemorrhage had pre-treatment visual acuities of 0.1–0.3; two experienced visual stability for 1–3 years before sudden vision loss following hemorrhage, while one initially improved from 0.2 to 0.6 before deteriorating. Minimizing the risk of retinal hemorrhage remains an important consideration in treatment planning.

This study has several limitations. First, diagnosis was based solely on imaging without pathological confirmation, although biopsy is generally considered unnecessary given the high risk of vision loss [24]. Second, and most critically, dose-volume histogram (DVH) data were available for only 11 of 43 patients due to departmental equipment upgrades. This incompleteness precluded robust dose-toxicity correlation analysis, prevented reliable determination of a safe dose threshold, and limited our ability to establish dose-response relationships for visual outcomes. Third, employing a single dose-fractionation regimen for all patients precluded exploration of optimal fractionation schemes. Fourth, prescribing to the 55%-75% isodose line resulted in maximum doses ranging from 53.33 to 72.72 Gy within the target volume; when such high-dose points coincided with the optic nerve, they likely exceeded tolerance thresholds and contributed to RION. As noted in the Methods, no specific dose constraint was applied to the optic nerve within the GTV. Fifth, the lack of objective perimetry limited visual field assessment. Sixth, while the sub-analysis of baseline visual impairment duration yielded statistically significant results, the acute group sample size was small (n = 8), and no acute cases were observed in moderate/severe baseline VA subgroups—a distribution reflecting the natural history of pONSM rather than selection bias. These findings require validation in larger multicenter cohorts. Seventh, the binary logistic regression model was limited by the small sample size and few outcome events, which may affect the generalizability of effect estimates. Future studies with complete dosimetry, optimized fractionation, prospective objective metrics, and larger sample sizes are needed.

Conclusion

Hypo-FSRT (40 Gy/10 fractions) achieves excellent tumor control and high regression rates in primary optic nerve sheath meningiomas, offering significant cosmetic benefit. However, the risk of vision deterioration is substantial, particularly in patients with normal baseline vision, for whom preservation rates may be unexpectedly low. The duration of visual symptoms is a key predictor of functional outcome, supporting the value of early intervention. Conventional fractionated radiotherapy remains the preferred treatment for vision preservation, especially in patients with good baseline acuity or acute visual loss. Hypo-FSRT may be considered for those prioritizing tumor shrinkage and cosmesis, particularly when vision is already severely compromised. Individualized treatment planning and thorough patient counseling are essential, and optimal fractionation requires further prospective study.
