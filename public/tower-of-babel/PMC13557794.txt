Hypoglycemia and altered bile acid metabolism are linked to latent iron deficiency in young Japanese women
Authors: Sena Ishikawa, Rika Miyazawa, Fumika Yokoyama, Daisuke Sawamura, Sandra Iuliano-Burns, Kei-Ichi Nakajima, Satoshi Ishizuka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIron deficiency anemia (IDA) remains a prevalent health issue, especially among Japanese women, and may be influenced by dietary patterns and lipid metabolism. This study aimed to investigate the association between circulating bile acid (BA) concentrations and iron-related biomarkers in young Japanese women. Blood biochemical and anthropometric data were collected from 33 healthy young women aged 20. The participants can be divided into two groups based on the levels of total-iron binding capacity (TIBC), although their hemoglobin and ferritin levels were in the normal ranges. When participants were divided based on TIBC, the low TIBC group showed significantly higher levels of 12-hydroxylated, non-12-hydroxylated, and total BAs, as well as significantly lower blood glucose levels compared to the normal group (p<0.05). Positive correlations were observed between TIBC and specific BA species, such as glycolithocholic acid (ρ = 0.3993, p<0.0288), cholic acid (ρ = 0.4767, p<0.0077), and taurocholic acid (ρ = 0.4423, p<0.0144). Additionally, transferrin levels were positively correlated with glycolithocholic acid (ρ = 0.3649, p<0.0474). These findings suggest that TIBC may serve as an early indicator of hypoglycemia, reflecting preclinical metabolic disturbance, in addition to being a latent IDA marker.Keywords: 12-hydroxylated bile acids, hypoglycemia, iron deficiency anemia, total iron-binding capacity

Introduction

Anemia is evident in approximately one-third of the world’s population, mostly due to iron deficiency.(1) According to the Global Burden of Disease Study, the global prevalence of anemia was 24.3% in 2021. A study utilizing data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey reported that 38.6% of nonpregnant women aged 12–21 years in the United States were iron deficient.(2) In Japan, iron deficiency anemia (IDA) is also recognized as a major public health concern. Approximately 17% of Japanese women are reported to suffer from IDA,(3,4) while the prevalence of latent iron deficiency in this group is estimated to range from 30 to 50%.(4,5) IDA can result in symptoms such as fatigue, cognitive impairment, and reduced work productivity in adults.(1) Although IDA is a global issue, susceptibility to the condition and the effectiveness of treatment are thought to vary among different ethnic groups. For example, studies have shown that Black women with anemia tend to have lower mean hemoglobin levels compared to White (Caucasian) women, even after iron status is improved.(6)

In Japan, IDA is diagnosed based on the following three criteria: serum ferritin <12 ‍ng/dl, hemoglobin <12 ‍g/dl, and total iron binding capacity (TIBC) ≥360 ‍μg/dl.(7) Among these indicators, serum ferritin is considered the most sensitive IDA marker, as it closely reflects the amount of stored iron in the body. TIBC represents whole iron binding capacity in the serum transferrin.(8) One of the main contributing factors to the high prevalence of IDA in Japan appears to be dietary habits.(7) The proportion of anemic women in Japan has increased between 1996 and 2003, and dietary iron intake significantly declined from 13.4 ‍mg/day in 1975 to 8.2 ‍mg/day in 2001, and further to 7.4 ‍mg/day in 2012.(5) According to the 2018 and 2019 National Health and Nutrition Surveys, the average iron intake of adult women was 7.5 ± 3.0 ‍mg/day, falling short of the estimated average requirement of 8.5–9.0 ‍mg/day for menstruating women aged 15–69 years.(9) The risk of developing IDA may also involve broader nutritional factors affecting iron absorption and metabolism. A study examining the relationship between iron deficiency and dietary pattern in 159 young Japanese women revealed a negative correlation between dietary fat intake and hemoglobin level in underweight.(4) In rodent models, high-fat diets inhibit iron absorption and induce abnormal iron metabolism.(10,11) These findings suggest that macronutrient compositions influence the development of IDA.

Bile acids (BAs) are synthesized in the liver from cholesterol and facilitate the digestion and absorption of dietary lipids through their amphipathic properties.(12) BAs represent one of the major excretory forms of cholesterol, as they are consistently detected in feces.(13) BAs can be categorized into 12-hydroxylated (12OH) BAs, such as cholic acid (CA), which possess a hydroxyl group at the C-12 position of the steroid nucleus, and non-12OH (n12OH) BAs, which lack this modification. In a study using a rat model, we previously demonstrated that 12OH BAs are selectively increased in bile-pancreatic fluid in response to a high-fat diet.(14) To experimentally replicate this BA profile in rats, we administered CA, a primary 12OH BA, via dietary supplementation.(15,16) A dietary concentration of 0.5 ‍g CA/kg diet would not interfere with 7α-dehydroxylation of BAs in the large intestine, thereby enabling the reproduction of physiological BA environment especially in the gut.(15) The rats fed the CA-supplemented diet exhibited various symptoms commonly observed in noncommunicable diseases, including hepatic lipid accumulation, impaired gut barrier function, and alteration in gut microbiota composition.(15,16) In addition, a significant reduction in hepatic iron concentration was observed in CA-fed rats.(17) It is important to note that CA-loaded rats did not develop obesity, a hepatic inflammation, or fibrosis. However, when lipopolysaccharide was administered to induce acute inflammation, a marked elevation in plasma alanine aminotransferase (ALT) level was observed.(16) These findings suggest that an increased levels of enterohepatic 12OH BA may sensitize the liver to subsequent inflammatory insults.

Given that alterations in BA and iron levels are thought to occur prior to clinical onset of noncommunicable diseases, it is plausible that changes in BA metabolism and iron homeostasis may arise simultaneously during the early stages of diseases development. Iron deficiency may happen in individuals with sufficient energy status depending on the diet. Since iron deficiency is especially common in young women, we investigated whether iron-related biomarkers link to circulating BA profiles in routine health check-ups.

Materials and Methods

Participants

The study was conducted according to the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Research Faculty of Agriculture at Hokkaido University (approval number: 2023-004). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants involved in the study. Participants were non-pregnant women students (n = 39) aged 20 years who attended an annual health check-up at Fuji Women’s University in Ishikari in 2023. Anthropometric data was collected, and blood samples drawn by licensed nurses trained in standard venipuncture methods. Blood parameters other than BAs, transferrin, lipocalin-2, SARS-CoV-2, and insulin were measured by a commercial laboratory service (Daiichi Kishimoto Clinical Laboratories, Sapporo, Japan). Whole blood was used to measure hemoglobin and hematocrit. Routine biochemical parameters, BAs, and other parameters were measured after separating sera as listed in Table 1. A TIBC value of ≥360 ‍μg/dl was considered indicative of IDA.(7) TIBC was calculated from sum of the serum iron and unbound iron-binding capacity (UIBC) after measuring them using JCA-BM (BioMajesty) 8060 (JEOL Ltd., Akishima, Japan) with Quick Auto Neo Fe and Quick Auto Neo UIBC (Shino-Test Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). Participants were divided into two groups based on their TIBC values: a normal group (TIBC <360 ‍μg/dl) and a latent IDA group (TIBC ≥360 ‍μg/dl) in the present study. Various clinical parameters were then compared between groups and analyzed for correlations. Six participants were excluded due to withdrawal of consent (n = 1) or incomplete data (n = 5) therefore data from the remaining 33 participants was included in analyses.

BA analysis

BAs in the sera were determined as previously described with some minor modifications.(17) In brief, the sera were dried using vacuum oven (VOS-300SD; Eyela, Tokyo Rikakikai Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) for 48 ‍h. Nordeoxycholic acid (23-nor-5β-cholanic acid-3α,12α-diol) was added to each sample as an internal standard, followed by ethanol extraction. The extracts were dissolved in methanol and subjected to solid-phase extraction using HLB 1 cc (10 ‍mg) Extraction Cartridges (Nippon Waters K.K., Tokyo, Japan) conditioned with methanol and 10 ‍mM of ammonium acetate. BAs were measured using an ultra-high-performance liquid chromatograph Ultimate 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific K.K., Tokyo, Japan) equipped with an ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 1.7 ‍μm column (Nihon Waters K.K.) and Q Exactive Plus Hybrid Quadrupole-Orbitrap Mass Spectrometer (MS) (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The BAs measured in this study were listed in Supplemental Table 1*. The column oven and the autosampler were maintained at 40°C and 15°C, respectively. The solvent flow rate was 400 ‍μl/min, and the sample injection volume was 3 ‍μl. The gradient program was as follows: 5% solvent B from 0 to 5 ‍min, linear ramp to 15% solvent B over 10 ‍min, linear ramp to 25% solvent B over 5 ‍min, and linear ramp to 75% solvent B over 2 ‍min. Solvent B was maintained at 75% for 1 ‍min and was then decreased linearly to 5% over 1 ‍min. Solvent B was maintained at 5% for 3 ‍min. The column eluent was then analyzed via MS. Mass spectrometry was performed using an Orbitrap MS equipped with an electrospray ionization probe in the negative-ion mode. Full scan MS spectral data (from m/z 200 to 700) were acquired using an Orbitrap analyzer after accumulation to achieve a target value of 1 × 106 in the linear ion trap. The resolution of the Orbitrap system was set to r = 17,500. Standard mass spectrometric conditions for all experiments were as follows: spray voltage, 3.8 kV; sheath gas flow rate, 35 ‍L/h; aux gas flow rate, 10 ‍L/h; heated capillary temperature, 250°C; aux gas heater temperature, 450°C; s-lens RF level, 100%.

Other parameters

The serum concentrations of transferrin, lipocalin-2, and ferritin were measured using a Transferrin ELISA Kit (ICL, Inc., Portland, OR), Lipocalin-2/NGAL picokine ELISA kit (Funakoshi Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan), and ferritin ELISA Kit (Cusabio, Houston, TX), respectively. Insulin concentration was measured using an INS ELISA kit (Proteintech Group, Inc., Tokyo, Japan).

Statistical analysis

All data are expressed as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs). The Kruskal–Wallis test was used for comparison between the groups. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (ρ) was used to find the relationship among the data. All the statistical evaluations were carried out using JMP (JMP Statistical Discovery LLC, Cary, NC). A probability less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

None of the participants in this study exhibited abnormal blood parameters as shown in Table 1. Fifteen participants had TIBC within the normal range (<360 ‍μg/dl), while 18 participants were classified as latent IDA based on TIBC levels (≥360 ‍μg/dl). In contrast, 31 participants were not diagnosed with IDA based on both hemoglobin and ferritin levels. High levels of serum BA parameters such as 12OH BAs, n12OH BAs, and total BAs was observed in the high TIBC participants (Fig. 1A). In the iron-related parameters, significantly lower level of ferritin was observed, while a trend toward increase were observed in transferrin levels in the high TIBC participants. No significant difference was observed in lipocalin-2 level between the groups. The serum concentrations of 12OH BAs negatively correlated ferritin levels, whereas the correlation was not significant between n12OH BAs and ferritin (Fig. 1B). Notably, serum glucose level was significantly lower in the high TIBC participants accompanied by a trend toward increase in insulin concentration (Fig. 1C). No difference was observed in HbA1c percentage between the groups.

The glucose level negatively correlated with the 12OH/n12OH proportion (Fig. 2), while no direct association was found between the iron transport-related factors and blood glucose levels. Discussion

In this study, blood tests conducted on young healthy Japanese women revealed that participants with latent IDA (TIBC ≥360 ‍μg/dl) exhibited elevated serum concentration of BA, especially 12OH BAs. A decrease in serum glucose was observed, along with a trend toward increased serum insulin and transferrin concentrations. These observations indicate that TIBC may serve as a potential marker for mild and/or early-stage IDA.

A study in rhesus monkeys reported that inducing iron deficiency in newborns, which have high demand for iron, resulted in increased blood levels of both 12OH and n12OH BAs.(18) Similarly, a study using neonate pigs also showed that although no difference was observed in ALT level or liver histology under iron deficiency, hepatic expression of Cyp7a1, the rate-limiting enzyme in BA synthesis, was increased accompanied by elevated serum levels of CA, chenodeoxycholic acid, and ursocholic acid.(19) In young rats, a significant increase in serum glycocholic acid was observed in those fed an iron-deficient diet containing approximately one-third of the normal iron content.(20) These studies suggest peripheral BA concentration increases in response to iron deficiency regardless of animal species. One possible mechanism is an upregulation of Cyp7a1 expression under iron-deficient condition. Meanwhile, in our previous study using rats, dietary CA supplementation led to a significant reduction in hepatic iron levels.(17) This finding suggests that fluctuation of BA environment outside the blood stream may also affect the systemic iron metabolism. In the present study, a positive correlation was observed between the levels of transferrin and glycolithocholic acid (Table 2). The relative abundance of gut bacteria belonging to the phyla Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes in the gut microbiota positively correlated plasma glycolithocholic acid concentration.(21) Likewise, some of the BAs including glycolithocholic acid, CA, and taurocholic acid, positively correlated TIBC and UIBC (Table 2). These observations suggest relationships between BA metabolism in the gut microbes and iron-related parameters.

Participants with TIBC ≥360 ‍μg/dl exhibited hypoglycemia with a trend toward increased insulin levels, although no significant difference was observed in HbA1c levels (Fig. 1C). These findings suggest that increased insulin secretion under iron-deficient conditions may induce hypoglycemia. Notably, blood glucose levels were found to be associated not only with transferrin, but also with BAs (Fig. 2). Regarding the relationship between BA and glucose metabolism, the involvement of 12OH BAs in insulin resistance has been shown in humans.(22) In our previous rat studies, we found that 12OH BAs, which are elevated following high-fat diet or high-fat/high-sucrose diets, are linked to increased energy intake and impaired glucose tolerance.(14,23) To date, no studies reported a relationship between iron deficiency and hypoglycemia in individuals with relatively low energy intake. We hypothesize that BA metabolism plays a key role in this association. BAs are involved in sterol excretion, a process that is generally enhanced under conditions of energy surplus. Therefore, adequate energy intake may be a prerequisite for increasing BA synthesis. In contrast, iron deficiency appears to be driven by a separate factor. In other words, even when energy intake is sufficient, an inadequate dietary intake of iron may lead to increased TIBC. Taken together, these observations suggest that the combination of sufficient energy intake and marginal iron availability could result in hypoglycemia mediated by elevated insulin levels. Participants with TIBC ≥360 ‍μg/dl likely have adequate energy intake but insufficient iron consumption. If this assumption is correct, iron supplementation alone may be sufficient to alleviate hypoglycemic symptoms in this population. This study found that elevated TIBC levels in young women are associated with hypoglycemia; however, whether this relates to hypoglycemia as a clear clinical symptom requires further verification. In addition, the causality of these associations should be carefully verified in future studies.

Only 2 participants had hemoglobin and ferritin levels below the reference values and the remaining participants could not clearly be classified as anemic. Other indicators of IDA include transferrin saturation and mean corpuscular volume.(24) In the present study, transferrin saturation showed a positive correlation or trend toward correlation with TIBC and mean corpuscular volume (results not shown). However, no significant associations were found between transferrin saturation and BAs or blood glucose levels. TIBC represents the quantity of transferrin, and its potential to bind iron.(8) It serves as an index of the iron-binding capacity of the blood excluding cellular components and is thought to be influenced by the amount of protein and energy obtained from the diet. In contrast, transferrin saturation merely represents the proportion of transferrin that is saturated with iron, and it is difficult to infer dietary energy intake from this value. Considering that BA concentrations may reflect the sufficiency of energy intake, it is reasonable that TIBC, among iron-related parameters, correlates BA levels.

Lipocalin-2, a protein involved in iron binding in the blood stream, has been shown to be upregulated in the liver in response to CA loading in experimental animals when hepatic iron levels decrease,(17) but no involvement was observed in the relationship between BA profiles and glucose-related parameters in the present study.

A limitation of the present study is the restricted number of samples available for BA analysis, due to limitation in sample processing capacity. Additionally, the study population consisted solely of women, all of whom were 20 years old, making it impossible to account for the effect of sex and age. As this study did not include a dietary survey, the actual balance of dietary energy intake remains unclear. This study focused on young women with low BMI, and it remains unclear whether the relationship observed here between TIBC and BAs would be seen in young women with different BMI levels. Furthermore, while the effects of menstruation should be considered in women, the present study did not conduct a detailed evaluation of this factor. Although latent iron deficiency is influenced by nutritional status, this study did not assess nutritional status. Furthermore, the presence of infections or other diseases may also contribute to the variations in the parameters presented here. However, this study did not conduct a detailed examination of these factors. These considerations should be taken into account when interpreting the data from this study.

In conclusion, this study investigated young Japanese women and found that the participants with latent IDA, as indicated by elevated TIBC levels, exhibited increased plasma transferrin and BAs concentrations along with symptoms of hypoglycemia. This study identified relationships between TIBC, BA metabolism, and blood glucose levels. However, it was a limited cross-sectional study conducted under specific conditions, and it cannot be ruled out that these relationships may vary depending on factors such as sex, age, and nutritional environment. Cohort studies are necessary to clarify the precise influence of these factors on a larger sample size in the future.
