Maximin Learning of Individualized Treatment Effect on Multi-Domain Outcomes
Yuying Lu ∗, Wenbo Fei, Yuanjia Wang, Molei Liu

Abstract
Precision mental health requires treatment decisions that account for heterogeneous symptoms reflecting multiple clinical domains. However, existing methods for estimating individualized treatment effects (ITE) rely on a single summary outcome or a specific set of observed symptoms or measures, which are sensitive to symptom selection and limit generalizability to unmeasured yet clinically relevant domains. We propose DRIFT, a new maximin framework for estimating robust ITEs from high-dimensional item-level data by leveraging latent factor representations and adversarial learning. DRIFT learns latent constructs via generalized factor analysis, then constructs an anchored on-target uncertainty set that extrapolates beyond the observed measures to approximate the broader hyper-population of potential outcomes. By optimizing worst-case performance over this uncertainty set, DRIFT yields ITEs that are robust to underrepresented or unmeasured domains. We further show that DRIFT is invariant to admissible reparameterizations of the latent factors and admits a closed-form maximin solution, with theoretical guarantees for identification and convergence. In analyses of a randomized controlled trial for major depressive disorder (EMBARC), DRIFT demonstrates superior performance and improved generalizability to external multi-domain outcomes, including side effects and self-reported symptoms not used during training.



Keywords:
Precision medicine; Adversarial learning; Item response data; Latent factor model; Mental disorders.

1
Introduction

1.1
Background

Mental disorders pose challenges for precision medicine due to symptom heterogeneity and variability in patient responses. Unlike physical illnesses, often defined by clear biomarkers and objective measurements, the diagnosis of mental disorders relies on a broad spectrum of subjective symptoms, encompassing mental, cognitive, and somatic factors
(
Insel and Cuthbert, 2015
)
. For instance, Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) is diagnosed based on nine core symptoms, which can manifest in over 200 distinct combinations. These diverse symptom profiles, often complicated by comorbidities, make the development of precise therapeutic strategies challenging and contribute to the low success rate of first-line treatments, with less than 50% of patients achieving remission
(
Gartlehner et al., 2011
;
McGrath et al., 2020
)
.

One barrier to improving treatment outcomes is the standard practice of relying on a single clinical summary to guide treatment decision-making. In mental health, treatment effects often vary across different symptom dimensions, sometimes presenting opposite effects on distinct domains
(
Maj et al., 2020
)
. For example, Selective Serotonin Reuptake Inhibitors (SSRIs) effectively alleviate core depressive symptoms but are also associated with side effects such as sleep disturbances
(
Ferguson, 2001
)
. Similarly, while antipsychotic medications may reduce hallucinations in schizophrenia, they frequently cause metabolic side effects, including significant weight gain and increased diabetes risk
(
Ballard and Zarate Jr, 2020
)
.
Antipsychotic medications, which may reduce hallucinations and delusions in schizophrenia, frequently cause metabolic side effects, including significant weight gain and increased diabetes risk
(
Tschoner et al., 2007
;
Marino et al., 2015
)
.
Relying on a single summary outcome for treatment decision-making may improve one symptom domain but harm another. Thus, it is essential to obtain reliable, generalizable estimates of the treatment effect across multiple outcome domains and latent factors to enable individualized, effective care in mental health.

1.2
Related Literature

Statistical methodology for estimating Individualized Treatment Effects (ITE) has advanced significantly with the development of machine learning approaches such as causal trees
(
Athey and Imbens, 2016
)
, causal forests
(
Wager and Athey, 2018
)
, and meta-learners
(
Künzel et al., 2019
;
Nie and Wager, 2021
;
Curth and Van der Schaar, 2021
;
Kennedy, 2023a
)
. However, these methods predominantly focus on a single scalar outcome, which is insufficient for the multifaceted nature of mental disorders.

While multivariate regression can incorporate multiple observed outcomes,
high-dimensional and subjective symptom data in psychiatry are more effectively modeled using latent variable models and factor analysis
(
Moustaki and Knott, 2000
;
Muthén, 2002
)
. In this context, Item Response Theory (IRT) and generalized factor models are widely used to identify underlying constructs from categorical symptoms
(
Embretson and Steven, 2000
;
Reise and Waller, 2009
)
, with recent advances extending these to high-dimensional settings via joint maximum likelihood estimation
(
Chen et al., 2019
;
Zhang et al., 2020
)
.

To inform treatment decision-making with multi-domain outcomes, existing approaches often aggregate sub-domain outcomes into a single global measure using fixed weights. For instance, recent works have proposed preference-sensitive aggregation
(
Butler et al., 2018
;
Luckett et al., 2021
)
or composite outcomes
(
Chen et al., 2020
;
Chen et al., 2021
)
to learn individualized treatment rules. Also,
Nwankwo et al., 2024
proposed a reduced-rank multi-objective policy learning method leveraging low-dimensional representations of the outcomes. However, these approaches assume a fixed aggregation function, thereby ignoring unobserved or infrequently observed outcomes that could yield a “worst-case” ITE. This is a critical limitation in risk-sensitive clinical settings, calling for more generalizable methods
(
Cinelli et al., 2025
)
.

This challenge relates to the growing literature on maximin effects and distributionally robust learning (DROL), which seeks to optimize performance under the worst-case shift in data distribution
(
Meinshausen and Bühlmann, 2015
;
Sagawa et al., 2019
;
Wang et al., 2023
, e.g.)
. While such adversarial learning frameworks have been applied to ITE and policy learning to address heterogeneity across data sources
(
Shi et al., 2018
;
Zhang et al., 2024
)
, they have not been adapted to balance trade-offs across latent symptom domains. These methods optimize over the convex hull of multiple observed outcomes, which is often overly conservative and, more importantly, problematic if the measured symptoms are a “biased” subset of the full symptom spectrum. By restricting optimization to observed items, this strategy fails to generalize to unmeasured but relevant domains that exist within the full “hyper-population” of symptoms (see Section
2.5
).

1.3
Our Contribution

To address the limitations of using a single outcome for treatment optimization in mental health, we propose the multi-Domain Robust estimation of Individualized and Factorized Treatment effect (DRIFT) framework. This approach leverages generalized factor analysis to identify latent disease constructs from multi-dimensional symptom data and constructs an “on-target” set of clinically plausible outcomes anchored by a Global Evaluation Outcome (GEO). By optimizing the worst-case predictive performance across this set, DRIFT ensures that the estimated treatment effects are stable and balanced across diverse symptom domains. Our main contributions can be summarized as follows.

Novel Framework:

We propose a maximin learning framework that extrapolates the GEO-anchored uncertainty set in latent space to account for unmeasured or underrepresented clinical symptoms. Unlike standard group maximin regression methods deterministically restricted to the empirical convex hull of observed symptom outcomes, this GEO-guided extrapolation renders the solution distributionally robust to variability in observed symptom coverage. It also yields a more generalizable ITE estimate by recovering the full spectrum of potential symptoms, thereby protecting DRIFT against failures within clinical domains underrepresented in the observed data.

Convenient Estimation Procedures with Theoretical Guarantees:
We provide a computationally efficient, three-step algorithm for identifying the optimal treatment effect estimator. We establish that our maximin problem admits a closed-form solution and prove the consistency of the resulting estimator. Crucially, we show that our estimator is not affected by the inherent non-identifiability of latent factor models, remaining invariant to their rotational indeterminacy.

Enhanced Clinical Generalizability in Real World:
We demonstrate the practical utility of DRIFT through an analysis of the EMBARC
(
Trivedi et al., 2016
)
randomized controlled trial for Major Depressive Disorder (MDD). Our results show that DRIFT achieves superior generalizability to external multi-domain outcomes (e.g., side effects and self-reported symptoms) not used during model training, highlighting its potential to prevent the neglect of critical clinical factors.

2
Conceptual Framework

2.1
Problem formulation

Let
\mathbf{X}=(X_{1},\cdots,X_{p})
denote baseline covariates and treatment effect moderators of the outcomes to be introduced next. Suppose
K
latent factors underlie a subject’s overall mental health status. Let
\mathbf{Z}^{(a)}=(Z_{1}^{(a)},\ldots,Z_{K}^{(a)})
denote the potential outcomes of these latent factors after receiving treatment
A=a\in\{0,1\}
, where
Z_{k}^{(a)}\in\mathbb{R}
,
k=1,\ldots,K
represents the
k
th latent variable. Let
\mathbf{Y}^{(a)}=(Y_{1}^{(a)},\ldots,Y_{J}^{(a)})
denote the potential outcomes of the observed multi-domain symptom measures after receiving treatment
a
, where
Y_{j}^{(a)}
represents the
j
th symptom and it can be discrete or continuous. While the latent factors are not explicitly observed, each measured symptom,
e.g., item responses from the Hamilton Rating Scale for Depression (HAM-D), is a partial manifestation of the subject’s underlying latent states, e.g., depression severity, general anxiety level, and psychotic propensity. Typically, the total number of observed outcomes
J
is much larger than the number of latent factors
K
.

We assume that (C.1)
measurement conditional independence
:
\mathbf{Y}^{(a)}
is independent of
(A,{\mathbf{X}})
conditional on
\mathbf{Z}^{(a)}
; and
(C.2)
measurement invariance
: the conditional distributions of
\mathbf{Y}^{(1)}\mid\mathbf{Z}^{(1)}
and
\mathbf{Y}^{(0)}\mid\mathbf{Z}^{(0)}
are the same. (C.1) implies that baseline covariates
\mathbf{X}
and treatment
A
affect the observed outcomes
\mathbf{Y}
only through the latent factors in
\mathbf{Z}
.
(C.1) and (C.2) are commonly used on item response data from clinical studies of mental health
(
Harman, 1976
;
Lohr, 2002
;
Chen et al., 2021
)
. Additionally, we assume
(C.3)
randomized treatment assignment
:
A\perp({\mathbf{X}},\mathbf{Z}^{(0)},\mathbf{Z}^{(1)},\mathbf{Y}^{(0)},\mathbf{Y}^{(1)})
;
and (C.4)
stable unit treatment value assumption (SUTVA)
:
\mathbf{Y}=\mathbf{Y}^{(a)}
and

\mathbf{Z}=\mathbf{Z}^{(a)}
when
A=a
. (C.3) could be replaced by the weaker no unmeasured confounding assumption
({\bf Z}^{(a)},{\bf Y}^{(a)})\perp A\mid{\bf X}
in observational studies (see Section
3.4
). We introduce the following generalized factor model where
Y^{(a)}_{1},\ldots,Y_{J}^{(a)}
are conditionally independent given
\mathbf{Z}^{(a)}
and satisfy that for
a\in\{0,1\}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Y^{(a)}_{j}|\mathbf{Z}^{(a)}=\mathbf{z}]=g^{-1}_{j}(\mathbf{z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j}-\zeta_{j}).

(1)

Here, each
Y^{(a)}_{j}
is associated with a linear function of the latent factors
\mathbf{Z}^{(a)}
through the coefficient
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j}{\in\mathbb{R}^{K}}
, the intercept
\zeta_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
and some known link function
g_{j}(\cdot)
that monotonically increases. Also, assume the potential outcomes of the latent factors
\mathbf{Z}^{(a)}
follow that:

\displaystyle\mathbf{Z}^{(a)}=\boldsymbol{f}^{(a)}(\mathbf{X})+\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{(a)},

(2)

where the vector function
\boldsymbol{f}^{(a)}=(f_{1}^{(a)},\ldots,f_{K}^{(a)})
encodes the treatment response models of
\mathbf{Z}^{(a)}
, and
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{(a)}
are random components being mean-zero and independent of
\mathbf{X}
.

2.2
Factorized outcomes and ITE

We define the class of latent factor representations as
{\mathcal{H}}:=\{{\mathbf{z}}\mapsto\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\top}\mathbf{z}-\zeta\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma}\in\mathbb{R}^{K},\zeta\in\mathbb{R}\}
, which contains all linear functions of the
K
-dimensional latent factors. For any observed clinical symptom
Y
, we define its best latent representation in
{\mathcal{H}}
, denoted by
\phi_{Y}({\mathbf{z}})
, as the minimizer of the population expected loss:

\phi_{Y}({\mathbf{z}}):=\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{Y}^{\top}{\mathbf{z}}-\zeta_{Y}=\mathop{\mbox{arg min}}_{\phi\in{\mathcal{H}}}\ \mathbb{E}\big[\ell_{Y}(Y,\phi({\mathbf{Z}}))\big].

The loss function
\ell_{Y}(y,u)
is assumed to be convex with respect to
u
and specified based on the type of outcomes: for continuous
Y
, we use the squared error
\ell_{Y}(y,u)=(y-u)^{2}
; for binary
Y
, we use the negative log-likelihood
\ell_{Y}(y,u)=-yu+\log(1+\exp(u))
. We further provide the choice of the link and loss functions for ordinal item responses with multiple categories in Appendix. Under assumptions (C.1) through (C.4) and the factor model (
1
) for each observed item
Y_{j}
, these choices ensure that
\phi_{Y_{j}}({\mathbf{z}})=g_{j}(\mathbb{E}[Y_{j}\mid{\mathbf{Z}}={\mathbf{z}}])=\mathbf{z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j}-\zeta_{j}
, with the link
g_{j}(u)=u
for continuous
Y_{j}
and
g_{j}(u)=\log\{u/(1-u)\}
for binary
Y_{j}
.

To quantify the deviation of any candidate linear representation
\phi({\mathbf{z}})
from a specific outcome
Y
, we define the excess loss as

d_{Y}(\phi)=\mathbb{E}\left[\ell_{Y}(Y,\phi({\mathbf{Z}}))\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\ell_{Y}(Y,\phi_{Y}({\mathbf{Z}}))\right].

(3)

This non-negative metric serves as a distance measure and will be used to construct a set of clinically plausible outcomes to be defined later. For binary
Y
,
d_{Y}(\phi)
is equivalent to the expectation of the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence between the Bernoulli distributions with success probabilities

{\rm expit}(\phi_{Y}({\mathbf{Z}}))
and
{\rm expit}(\phi({\mathbf{Z}}))
where
{\rm expit}(u)=e^{u}/(1+e^{u})
.

In application fields like psychiatry and psychology, the ITE on any factorized outcome
\phi({\mathbf{Z}})
for a subject with baseline covariates
{\mathbf{x}}
is typically defined as:

\displaystyle\tau_{\phi}(\mathbf{x}):

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\phi(\mathbf{Z}^{(1)})-\phi(\mathbf{Z}^{(0)})\mid\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x}\right]=\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\top}\Big(\boldsymbol{f}^{(1)}(\mathbf{x})-\boldsymbol{f}^{(0)}(\mathbf{x})\Big),

(4)

with
\boldsymbol{f}^{(a)}({\mathbf{x}})=\mathbb{E}\big[{\mathbf{Z}}^{(a)}\mid\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x}\big]
. In this work, we consider a linear model for the treatment effect
{\boldsymbol{f}}^{(a)}({\mathbf{X}})={\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{(a)}{\mathbf{X}},\ {\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{(a)}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times p}
. The framework readily extends to nonlinear models.

To ensure that the weights in model (
4
) are clinically interpretable, we leverage a one-dimensional Global Evaluation Outcome (GEO), denoted by
O
, that captures the overall effectiveness of an intervention. For example, the Clinical Global Impression (CGI) Scale is routinely collected in mental health studies
(
Busner and Targum, 2007
)
, used as the GEO to assess overall improvement of a patient. When
O
is observed or well-defined by domain knowledge, we define the factorized GEO representation as:

\phi_{\text{GEO}}({\mathbf{z}}):=\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{\text{GEO}}^{\top}\mathbf{z}-\zeta_{\text{GEO}}=\mathop{\mbox{arg min}}_{\phi\in{\mathcal{H}}}\ \mathbb{E}\left[\ell_{O}\big(O,\phi({\mathbf{Z}})\big)\right],

(5)

and the corresponding ITE function as
\tau_{\phi_{\text{GEO}}}({\mathbf{x}})=\mathbb{E}\big[\phi_{\text{GEO}}(\mathbf{Z}^{(1)})-\phi_{\text{GEO}}(\mathbf{Z}^{(0)})\mid\mathbf{X}={\mathbf{x}}\big]
. In the absence of such a GEO, we propose a minimax strategy to obtain
\phi_{\text{GEO}}
as the “center” of the observed responses
Y_{j}
’s (see Section
3.2
).

2.3
On-target set of factorized outcomes

In precision mental health, accounting for symptoms and outcomes across multiple domains is crucial to guide treatment decisions. Overlooking an important domain can lead to suboptimal or ineffective decisions. Although the GEO-oriented factorized representation
\phi_{\text{GEO}}
and ITE function
\tau_{\boldsymbol{\phi}_{\text{GEO}}}
are clinically informative, they remain tied to specific observed outcomes, thus lack generalizability to capture the full spectrum of multi-domain outcomes in mental disorders. Therefore, it is essential to maintain a robust and balanced characterization of the ITE across a diverse array of outcomes represented by latent factors
\mathbf{Z}
. For this purpose, we introduce a key concept called the
on-target
set of representation:

\displaystyle\Phi(\delta)=\left\{\phi\mid d_{O}(\phi)\leq\delta,~\phi\in{\mathcal{H}}\right\},

(6)

where
d_{O}(\cdot)
denotes the excess loss for the GEO response as defined in (
3
) (replacing
Y
with
O
), and
\delta\geq 0
is a radius parameter controlling the allowable deviation in representing GEO among the candidate representations included in
\Phi(\delta)
. Each factorized outcome
\phi(\mathbf{Z})
, with
\phi\in\Phi(\delta)
, constitutes a clinically meaningful aggregation of the underlying latent factors, anchored by
\phi_{\text{GEO}}({\mathbf{Z}})
yet distinct from it. A small
\delta
restricts consideration to outcomes very similar to
\phi_{\text{GEO}}({\mathbf{Z}})
while a larger
\delta
allows a broader range of factorized outcomes. Correspondingly, we define the set of aggregation weights associated with
\phi\in\Phi(\delta)
as:

\displaystyle\Gamma(\delta)=\left\{\boldsymbol{\gamma}\ \Big|\ \min_{\zeta\in\mathbb{R}}\{d_{O}(\phi)\mid\phi:{\mathbf{z}}\mapsto\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\top}{\mathbf{z}}-\zeta\}\leq\delta,~\boldsymbol{\gamma}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}\right\}.

(7)

When
\delta=0
, the uncertainty set collapses to a single clinical anchor (GEO), simplifying the approach to a classical estimator that considers a fixed weight
\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{\text{{GEO}}}
(Figure
1(a)
). As
\delta
increases, the set expands to a latent neighborhood surrounding the GEO. Clinically, this expanded “on-target” set captures a comprehensive spectrum of relevant outcomes that may be unmeasured or underrepresented in the observed
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{J}
, such as individual symptoms reported in HAM-D or burden of side effects (Figure
1(b)
). Further geometric interpretations of GEO-anchored extrapolation are provided in Section
2.5
and our real-world study.

Z_{1},Z_{2}
).

(orange arrow)is the GEO aggregation weight, serving as the clinical anchor.

, encompassing weights close to

.

2.4
Maximin framework

We now introduce a novel maximin framework for estimating ITE that achieves stable, balanced predictive performance across a range of symptom representations in
\Phi(\delta)
. This enables a generalizable evaluation of treatment effects and more reliable treatment recommendations. For any factorized representation
\phi({\mathbf{z}})=\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\top}\mathbf{z}-\zeta
and a learner
\tau(\mathbf{x})
belonging to some prespecified model space
\mathcal{F}
, we introduce the reward function

\displaystyle{R}_{\phi}\left(\tau\right):=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\big(\phi({\mathbf{Z}}^{(1)})-\phi({\mathbf{Z}}^{(0)})\big)^{2}-\big(\phi({\mathbf{Z}}^{(1)})-\phi({\mathbf{Z}}^{(0)})-\tau(\mathbf{X})\big)^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\big(\tau_{\phi}(\mathbf{X})\big)^{2}-\big(\tau_{\phi}(\mathbf{X})-\tau(\mathbf{X})\big)^{2}\Big].

This quantity measures how much of the variation in the ITE function
\tau_{\phi}(\mathbf{x})
(defined in (
4
)) is captured by the candidate estimator
\tau(\mathbf{x})
. A higher value of
{R}_{\phi}\left(\tau\right)
indicates that
\tau(\mathbf{x})
better predicts ITE on the factorized outcome
\phi({\mathbf{Z}})
. To obtain a robust characterization of the ITE over all on-target factorized representations in
\Phi(\delta)
defined in (
6
),
we propose a maximin optimization to obtain the estimator that maximizes the worst-case reward
{R}_{\phi}\left(\tau\right)
:

\displaystyle\tau^{*}:=\mathop{\mbox{arg max}}_{\tau\in\mathcal{F}}\min_{\phi\in\Phi(\delta)}{R}_{\phi}\left(\tau\right),

(8)

which we call the multi-Domain Robust estimator of the Individualized and Factorized Treatment effect (DRIFT). This formulation is an adversarial learning problem that pursues a balance (Nash equilibrium) between the ITE function
\tau
and the representation
\phi
. At this equilibrium, the solution
\tau^{*}
achieves a stable prediction performance over all representations in
\Phi(\delta)
in the sense that it maximizes the worst-case ITE prediction performance. We will also demonstrate in Proposition
1
that optimizing the worst-case reward
{R}_{\phi}\left(\tau\right)
leads to the optimal individualized treatment regime for the outcome
\phi({\mathbf{Z}})
under certain conditions.

Leveraging Sion’s Minimax Theorem
(
Sion, 1958
)
, Theorem
1
establishes that problem (
8
) has an explicit-form solution formed as a weighted combination of the latent-factor-wise ITE functions
\big\{\tau_{k}:k\in[K]\big\}:=\big\{f_{k}^{(1)}-f_{k}^{(0)}:k\in[K]\big\}
.

Theorem 1
(Identification)

Suppose the function class
\mathcal{F}
is convex with
\tau_{k}\in\mathcal{F}
for
k\in[K]
, then
\tau^{*}
defined in (
8
) is identified as:

\displaystyle\tau^{*}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\gamma^{*}_{k}\tau_{k}(\mathbf{x})\quad\text{with}\quad\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*}=\mathop{\mbox{arg min}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}\in\Gamma(\delta)}\ \boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\top}{\boldsymbol{\Xi}}\boldsymbol{\gamma},

(9)

where
\Xi_{k,l}=\mathbb{E}[\tau_{k}(\mathbf{X})\tau_{l}(\mathbf{X})]
for
k,l\in[K]
.

Theorem
1
implies a straightforward way to compute the maximin ITE function
\tau^{*}
. In particular, when the ITE function
\tau_{k}
for each latent factor
Z_{k}
is available, one only needs to solve the quadratic programming problem in (
9
) to obtain the optimal adversarial weights
\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*}
. The final estimator is then formed by linearly aggregating
\tau_{k}
’s. This result shares a similar spirit with the group maximin regression
(
Meinshausen and Bühlmann, 2015
)
aiming to combine models from multiple sources through adversarial learning, with its solution being some convex combination of the source-specific models.

Interestingly, the strategy of linearly aggregating informative latent factors has been widely used to address multi-domain outcomes in areas such as mental health
(
Qiu et al., 2018
)
. An advantage of such linear ITE functions is that they provide a straightforward interpretation, with the linear weights quantifying the relative contributions of the latent factors to the aggregated model. Also, it facilitates easy implementation of decision rules in clinical practice.

Remark 1

In our framework, it is essential to define the on-target set
\Phi(\delta)
in (
8
) using the low-dimensional representation
\mathbf{Z}
instead of applying linear combinations directly to the original outcomes
\boldsymbol{Y}
. First, the original outcomes may have different distributional types and scales, whereas the GEO-guided on-target factorized outcome subspace
\{\phi({\mathbf{Z}})\mid\phi\in\Phi(\delta)\}
is free of these issues because it is invariant to any scaling or rotation of
\mathbf{Z}
(Proposition
2
). Second, using
\mathbf{Z}
effectively overcomes the high-dimensionality and measurement noise of
\boldsymbol{Y}
.

2.5
Geometric interpretation of GEO-anchored extrapolation

In clinical practice, the full spectrum of potential symptoms constitutes a “hyper-population” of outcomes, whereas any given study collects only a subset determined by its design. As a concrete example, in the clinical trial of major depressive disorder (MDD; Section
6
), mania-related symptoms (e.g., feeling unusually euphoric or energized, talking more than usual, having a flood of exciting ideas) are not represented in the Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAM-D), a primary outcome in many MDD studies. As a result, ITE estimators based only on observed outcomes may overlook underrepresented domains and be sensitive to the particular symptoms included in the study.
Interestingly, DRIFT overcomes these limitations through the GEO-anchored extrapolation strategy. The geometric interpretation involves two main aspects:

The GEO-anchored set
\Gamma(\delta)
reduces algorithmic sensitivity:

Group maximin methods
(
Meinshausen and Bühlmann, 2015
)
were originally developed for regression on a common outcome across multiple data sources. In analogy to this strategy, under our setting, the “observed-only” maximin approach (
Obs Maximin
) constrains its uncertainty set to the convex hull of aggregation weights for all the observed factorized outcomes (pink triangle in Figure
1(a)
). It yields an estimator
\gamma_{\text{obs}}
that could be highly sensitive to a subset of outcomes from the hyper-population measured in some specific study. In contrast,
DRIFT
optimizes over a GEO-anchored robust region
\Gamma(\delta)
that recovers the “hyper-population” of outcomes. As illustrated in Figure
1(a)
, introducing unobserved weights (e.g.,
\alpha_{4}
and
\alpha_{5}
) can significantly reshape the convex hull of the observed outcomes’ representation while only mildly perturbing
\Gamma(\delta)
, yielding a more stable solution
\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{\text{DRIFT}}
.

Extrapolation enhances generalizability:
Confined to the convex hull of observed outcomes, the observed-only method generalizes poorly to underrepresented or unmeasured clinical outcomes. For instance, if observed symptoms preferentially load on latent factor
Z_{2}
over
Z_{1}
(Figure
1(a)
), the resulting ITE estimators overlook treatment effects operating through
Z_{1}
. Consequently, it fails to detect beneficial effects on unmeasured symptoms or adverse side effects hidden within underrepresented latent dimensions.
DRIFT
mitigates these failures by extrapolating to the larger and more comprehensive “hyper-population”, thereby accounting for plausible outcomes in underrepresented domains. This yields a more generalizable characterization of treatment effects. We validate this advantage empirically in the HAM-D study (Section
6
), where the observed-only method suffers from failures in the underrepresented mania domain, whereas
DRIFT
maintains robustness.

We also note that defining the uncertainty set via the excess loss
d_{O}
ensures that our ITE estimator is invariant to the rotational indeterminacy of the latent factors
{\mathbf{Z}}
. The uncertainty set of the
Obs Maximin
method is defined strictly on the coordinates of estimated loadings of the observed items, which may require more assumptions, e.g. the varimax criterion
(
Kaiser, 1958
)
, to ensure proper interpretation and identification. Free from this issue, DRIFT remains well-defined even when the “true” underlying
{\mathbf{Z}}
is not identifiable (see Proposition
2
).

3
Estimation Method

3.1
Generalized factor analysis

Suppose that for subject
i\in\{1,\dots,N\}
, we observe baseline covariates
{{\mathbf{x}}_{i}}=(x_{i1},\dots,x_{ip})\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, received treatment
a_{i}\in\{0,1\}
and multi-domain outcomes
{\mathbf{y}}_{i}=(y_{i1},\dots,y_{iJ})\in\mathbb{R}^{J}
. Denote the unobserved latent factors as
{\mathbf{z}}_{i}=(z_{i1},\dots,z_{iK})\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
. In (
10
), we introduce the constrained joint maximum likelihood estimation (CJMLE) approach
(
Chen et al., 2019
)
:

\displaystyle\big\{\widehat{{\mathbf{z}}}_{i},\widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{j},\widehat{\zeta}_{j}:i\in[N],j\in[J]\big\}=

\displaystyle\ \mathop{\mbox{arg min}}_{{\mathbf{z}}_{i},{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{j},{\zeta}_{j}}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\ell_{Y_{j}}(y_{ij},\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j}^{\top}{\mathbf{z}}_{i}-{\zeta}_{j}),

\displaystyle\quad s.t.\quad{1+\|{\mathbf{z}}_{i}\|^{2}}\leq C^{2},\ \

\displaystyle{\zeta_{j}^{2}+\|\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j}\|^{2}}\leq C^{2},\quad\forall i\in[N],j\in[J],

(10)

where
\{\ell_{Y_{j}}(\cdot,\cdot):j\in[J]\}
are loss functions for the observed outcomes
Y_{j}
’s as defined in Section
2.2
and
C>0
is a pre-specified regularization parameter, set as
5\sqrt{K}
in our numerical studies. In practice, the latent space dimension
K
can be chosen based on expert knowledge or information criterion. To optimize (
10
), we adopt the alternating minimization procedure described in Appendix, with the constrained optimization problem handled by projected gradient descent.

With the observed
({\mathbf{x}}_{i},a_{i})
and the latent factors
\widehat{{\mathbf{z}}}_{i}
estimated by (
10
), we then learn
\widehat{\boldsymbol{f}}^{(a)}=(\widehat{f}_{1}^{(a)},\dots,\widehat{f}_{K}^{(a)})~(a\in\{0,1\})
as estimates of the treatment response functions defined in (
2
). This is a standard regression problem, where both traditional methods (e.g., linear regression) and machine learning methods (i.e., neural networks) can be used. Because the latent
\widehat{{\mathbf{z}}}_{i}
’s are only identifiable up to an invertible rotation and a constant shift,
\widehat{\boldsymbol{f}}^{(a)}
(and, accordingly,
\widehat{\tau}_{k}=\widehat{f}_{k}^{(1)}-\widehat{f}_{k}^{(0)}
) can be consistent with
{\boldsymbol{f}}^{(a)}
in (
2
) (and
\tau_{k}=f_{k}^{(1)}-f_{k}^{(0)}
) up to corresponding reparameterizations. Consequently, the empirical estimators
\widehat{{\mathbf{z}}}_{i}
and
\widehat{\tau}_{k}
can be directly used to solve the downstream maximin problem (see Proposition
2
).

3.2
Constructing the on-target set

Construction of the on-target set
\Gamma(\delta)
requires specifying the GEO representation
\phi_{\text{GEO}}\in{\mathcal{H}}
and selecting the radius parameter
\delta
. The representation
\phi_{\text{GEO}}
serves as the “center” of
\Gamma(\delta)
. Let
o_{i}
denote the GEO of subject
i
. When the GEO is available in the data, we solve the empirical version of (
5
) to obtain
\widehat{\phi}_{\text{GEO}}({\mathbf{z}})=\widehat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{\text{GEO}}^{\top}{\mathbf{z}}-\widehat{\zeta}_{\text{GEO}}
as an estimate of
{\phi}_{\text{GEO}}({\mathbf{z}})
.

When GEO is unobserved, we propose to learn
\widehat{\phi}_{\text{GEO}}
as the center of the observed outcomes’ representations
\big\{\widehat{\phi}_{Y_{j}}({\mathbf{z}})=\widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{j}^{\top}{\mathbf{z}}-\widehat{\zeta}_{j}:j\in[J]\big\}
. Specifically, we solve the minimax problem:

\widehat{\phi}_{\text{GEO}}=\mathop{\mbox{arg min}}_{\phi\in{\mathcal{H}}}\ \max_{j\in[J]}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\big(\phi(\widehat{{\mathbf{z}}}_{i})-\widehat{\phi}_{Y_{j}}(\widehat{{\mathbf{z}}}_{i})\big)^{2},

(11)

which minimizes the largest distance to the factors of all observed outcomes to find their center. For this problem, one can again invoke Sion’s Minimax Theorem
(
Sion, 1958
)
to derive an explicit-form solution (see Theorem
1
). Note that problem (
11
) coincides with the group min-max regret framework proposed by
Mo et al., 2024
. However, an important difference is that we use the solution of (
11
) as an anchor, or center, for constructing the on-target uncertainty set rather than using it as the final estimator.

The radius parameter
\delta
determines the size of the uncertainty set for the maximin problem (
8
). If
\delta
is too small, the on-target set may become overly restrictive, limiting the generalizability of DRIFT to those outcomes nearly identical to the GEO. Conversely, if
\delta
is too large, the set may include outcomes that are not clinically meaningful or relevant, leading to overly conservative solutions. To achieve a balanced trade-off, we recommend setting the radius parameter as

\delta^{\dagger}=\min\Big\{\max_{j\in[J]}\widehat{d}_{O}(\widehat{\phi}_{Y_{j}}),~0.95\min_{\zeta\in\mathbb{R}}\widehat{d}_{O}(\zeta)\Big\},
where

\widehat{d}_{O}(\phi):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left[\ell_{O}(o_{i},\phi(\widehat{\mathbf{z}}_{i}))-\ell_{O}(o_{i},\widehat{\phi}_{\text{GEO}}(\widehat{\mathbf{z}}_{i}))\right]

denotes the empirical excess loss operator of the GEO
O
as defined in (
3
). Note that even without the observation of
O
, one can still compute
\widehat{d}_{O}
with
\widehat{\phi}_{\text{GEO}}
obtained by (
11
) because
O
can be replaced by its conditional expectation specified by
\widehat{\phi}_{\text{GEO}}({\mathbf{Z}})
under the generalized factor model. This threshold
\delta^{\dagger}
is selected to be large enough to encompass the observed factorized outcomes, thereby addressing the limited observed symptom coverage illustrated in Section
2.5
. In addition,
\delta^{\dagger}
is bounded by
0.95\min_{\zeta\in\mathbb{R}}\widehat{d}_{O}(\zeta)
to ensure that our robust estimator does not degenerate to a null model. The on-target set for aggregation weights of the factorized outcomes is constructed as
\widehat{\Gamma}(\delta^{\dagger})
, where
\widehat{\Gamma}(\delta)
denotes the empirical on-target set obtained from (
7
) by replacing
d_{O}
with its empirical estimate
\widehat{d}_{O}
.

3.3
Maximin estimation

We now combine the components from the previous sections to compute the DRIFT estimator. Our goal is to empirically estimate the maximin-optimal ITE function
\tau^{*}
defined in (
8
), which achieves robust performance on the data-driven on-target set. Following Theorem

, we solve the quadratic optimization problem:

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}=\mathop{\mbox{arg min}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}\in\widehat{\Gamma}(\delta)}\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\top}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Xi}}\boldsymbol{\gamma},\ \text{ where }\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Xi}}=(\widehat{\Xi}_{k,l})_{K\times K},~\widehat{\Xi}_{k,l}=N^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\widehat{\tau}_{k}\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}\right)\widehat{\tau}_{l}\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}\right),

(12)

and derive the final estimator for
\tau^{*}
as
\widehat{\tau}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\widehat{\gamma}_{k}\widehat{\tau}_{k}(\mathbf{x})
. In practice, the radius parameter
\delta
used in (
12
) is recommended as
\delta^{\dagger}
introduced in Section
3.2
. The complete procedure for obtaining the DRIFT estimator is outlined in Algorithm

.

The DRIFT estimator also yields an individualized treatment rule (ITR),

, this decision rule corresponds to the optimal regime under the maximin criterion for centered Gaussian covariates

Suppose

The optimal ITR characterized in Proposition

3.4
Observational Studies

In real-world applications, treatment assignments are often non-randomized. To properly adjust for confounding and estimate treatment effects in such observational settings, one could employ a two-stage doubly robust procedure (DR-Learner) adapted from
(
Kennedy, 2023b
)
. After obtaining latent estimators
\{\widehat{{\mathbf{z}}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
in Section
3.1
, we randomly divided the set of
N
observations
\mathcal{D}:=\{{\mathbf{x}}_{i},a_{i},\widehat{{\mathbf{z}}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
evenly into two disjoint sets
\mathcal{D}_{1},\mathcal{D}_{2}
with an equal size
[N/2]
. Then we apply the DR-Learner method as follows:

Stage 1: Nuisance training:
Construct estimates of two nuisance functions using
\mathcal{D}_{1}
: (i)
\widehat{s}
of the propensity scores
s
, where
s({\mathbf{x}})=\mathbb{P}(A=1\mid{\mathbf{X}}={\mathbf{x}})
; (ii)
(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{f}}}^{(0)},\widehat{{\boldsymbol{f}}}^{(1)})
of the latent outcome regression functions
({\boldsymbol{f}}^{(0)},{\boldsymbol{f}}^{(1)})
.

Stage 2: Pseudo-outcome regression:
Construct the latent pseudo-outcome:

\widehat{\varphi}({\mathbf{Z}})=\frac{A-\widehat{s}({\mathbf{X}})}{\widehat{s}({\mathbf{X}})[1-\widehat{s}({\mathbf{X}})]}\big\{{\mathbf{Z}}-{\boldsymbol{f}}^{(A)}({\mathbf{X}})\big\}+\widehat{\boldsymbol{f}}^{(1)}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{f}}^{(0)}

(13)

and regress it on covariates
{\mathbf{X}}
in the holdout sample
\mathcal{D}_{2}
, yielding
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{dr}({\mathbf{x}})=\widehat{\mathbb{E}}[\widehat{\varphi}({\mathbf{Z}})\mid{\mathbf{X}}={\mathbf{x}}]
. The
k
-th element
\widehat{{\tau}}_{dr,k}({\mathbf{x}})
is then used as the factor-wise treatment effect estimation, which serves as the input of (
12
) for computing the final maximin estimator.

4
Theoretical Properties

Let
\|\mathbf{M}\|_{F}=\big\{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{J}M_{ij}^{2}\big\}^{1/2}
be the Frobenius norm of a matrix
\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times J}
and denote the smallest and largest eigenvalues of a squared matrix
{\boldsymbol{\Xi}}
as
\lambda_{\min}({\boldsymbol{\Xi}}),\lambda_{\max}({\boldsymbol{\Xi}})
, respectively.
We use
\|\boldsymbol{u}\|_{2}
to denote the
\ell_{2}
-norm of vector
\boldsymbol{u}
. For positive sequences
a(n),b(n)
, use
a(n)\lesssim b(n)
when there exists some universal constant
c\geq 0
such that
a(n)\leq cb(n)
for all
n\geq 1
, and
a(n)\lesssim_{\mathrm{P}}b(n)
for
a(n)\lesssim b(n)
with the probability approaching one.

4.1
Identifiability

Before proceeding, we address the identification issue caused by the latent factor
\mathbf{Z}
in the generalized factor model (
1
). Note that model (
1
) is invariant under any affine transformations of
\mathbf{Z}
. Consequently, the unobserved
{\mathbf{Z}}
is identifiable only up to an
admissible reparameterization

\tilde{\mathbf{Z}}=\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Z}+\boldsymbol{\mu}
, where
\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times K}
is invertible and
\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
. Despite the non-identifiability of
{\mathbf{Z}}
, the core components of our framework remain intrinsic to the data and invariant to the choice of latent representation. Specifically, it is straightforward to verify that for any admissible reparameterization
\tilde{\mathbf{Z}}
, the induced optimal factorized outcome for any given response
Y
, including GEO, is identical to
\phi_{Y}(\mathbf{Z})
obtained with the original
\mathbf{Z}
. This invariance of the optimal factorized outcomes guarantees that the proposed maximin ITE estimator is well-defined, as formally stated in Proposition
2
.

Proposition 2
(Invariance of DRIFT to Latent Reparameterization)

Let
\tilde{\mathbf{Z}}=\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Z}+\boldsymbol{\mu}
be any admissible reparameterization of
\mathbf{Z}
. For any
\phi\in{\mathcal{H}}
, any response
Y
and any radius
\delta\geq 0
, we define the induced quantities as
\tilde{R}_{\phi},\tilde{\phi}_{Y},\tilde{d}_{Y}
and
\tilde{\Phi}(\delta)
by replacing
{\mathbf{Z}}
with
\tilde{\mathbf{Z}}
in their respective definitions. The resulting DRIFT function
\tilde{\tau}^{*}:=\mathop{\mbox{arg max}}_{\tau\in{\mathcal{F}}}\ \min_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}\in\tilde{\Gamma}(\delta)}\tilde{R}_{\phi}(\tau)
is identical to the original
\tau^{*}
in (
8
) with the same radius
\delta
. Notably, setting
\delta=0
establishes the invariance of the ITE for the GEO.

4.2
Estimation Consistency

We now establish the convergence of the ITE estimator proposed in
Section
3.3
under an asymptotic regime in which both the
number of subjects
N
and the number of items
J
diverge (
N,J\to\infty
),
while the latent dimension
K
remains fixed. We further assume that the
treatment and control sample sizes
N_{1}
and
N_{0}
satisfy

\min\{N_{1},N_{0}\}\asymp N
. Let
\mathbf{U}^{*}=[\mathbf{z}_{1}\cdots\mathbf{z}_{N}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times K}
denote the matrix of true latent factors, and
\mathbf{W}^{*}=[\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1}\cdots\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{J}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{J\times K}
represent the loading matrix, with the intercept vector
\boldsymbol{\zeta}^{*}=(\zeta_{1},\dots,\zeta_{J})\in\mathbb{R}^{J}
. Denote
(\widehat{\mathbf{U}},\widehat{\mathbf{W}},\widehat{\boldsymbol{\zeta}})
as the corresponding solution to the empirical problem (
10
).

Suppose the treatment effect model (
2
) admits a linear form:
{\boldsymbol{f}}^{(a)}({\mathbf{X}})={\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{(a)}{\mathbf{X}},\ {\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{(a)}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times p}
for
a=0,1
. Let
\mathbf{B}^{*}=[\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}\cdots\boldsymbol{\beta}_{K}]:=({\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{(1)}-{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{(0)})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times K}
denote the matrix of the true factor-wise linear effect parameters. Then, the factor-wise ITE function is given by
\tau_{k}({\mathbf{x}})=f_{k}^{(1)}({\mathbf{x}})-f_{k}^{(0)}({\mathbf{x}})=\boldsymbol{\beta}_{k}^{\top}{\mathbf{x}}
, for
k\in[K]
. We let
\widehat{\mathbf{B}}=[\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{1}\cdots\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{K}]\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times K}
denote the estimator of
{\mathbf{B}}^{*}
obtained by linearly regressing the recovered latent outcomes
\widehat{{\mathbf{U}}}
on the covariates. Although this analysis primarily addresses the linear case, extensions to non-linear ITE models are feasible. We rely on some regularity and non-singularity assumptions to guarantee the convergence of
\widehat{\mathbf{U}}
,
\widehat{\mathbf{W}}
and
\widehat{\mathbf{B}}
, up to some invertible rotation and scaling.

Assumption 1

The generalized factor model is correctly specified in problem (
10
), i.e., the true model parameters and latent factors satisfy that

\sqrt{1+\|\mathbf{z}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}}\leq C,\ \forall i\in[N]
and
\sqrt{\zeta_{j}^{2}+\|\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}}\leq C,\ \forall j\in[J],
where
C
is the boundary of the feasible set used in (
10
).

Assumption 2

There exist constants
L,B>0
such that for all
j\in[J]
and
y
in the domain of
Y_{j}
, (i) Strong convexity and smoothness:

B^{-1}\leq{\partial^{2}\ell_{Y_{j}}(y,\phi)}/{\partial\phi^{2}}\leq B,

\forall\phi\in[-C^{2},C^{2}]
; (ii) Lipschitz continuity:
|{\partial\ell_{Y_{j}}(y,\phi)}/{\partial\phi}|\leq L,\forall\phi\in[-C^{2},C^{2}]
.

Assumption 3

For any response
Y\in\{Y_{1},\cdots,Y_{J},O\}
and
\phi\in[-C^{2},C^{2}]
, the centered loss process
\mathcal{L}_{Y}(\phi):=\ell_{Y}(Y,\phi)-\mathbb{E}[\ell_{Y}(Y,\phi)]
satisfies either (i) or (ii):

(i)

Boundedness:
|\mathcal{L}_{Y}(\phi)|\leq B^{\prime}
for some constant
B^{\prime}>0
;

(ii)

Sub-exponential: There exist
\nu,\alpha>0
such that
\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda\mathcal{L}_{Y}(\phi)}]\leq e^{\lambda^{2}\nu^{2}/2}
for all
|\lambda|<1/\alpha
.

Assumption 4

There exist some constant
\sigma>0
such that for
a\in\{0,1\}
,
\lambda_{\min}\big({\mathbf{Z}}^{(a)}({\mathbf{Z}}^{(a)})^{\top}\big)\geq\sigma^{-1}
,
\lambda_{\min}\big(J^{-1}\mathbf{W}^{*\top}\mathbf{W}^{*}\big)\geq\sigma^{-1}
,
\sigma^{-1}\leq\lambda_{\min}({\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})\leq\lambda_{\max}({\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})\leq\sigma
,
\lambda_{\max}\big(\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{(a)}(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{(a)})^{\top}]\big)\leq\sigma
, where
{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}:=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top}]
denotes the second-moment of the covariates
\mathbf{X}
and
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{(a)}
is the noise term in model (
2
).

Assumption
1
guarantees that the estimator from the constrained optimization problem (
10
) is well-specified. Assumption
2
imposes regularity conditions on the ordinal loss, ensuring it is well-behaved for optimization. Meanwhile, Assumption
3
controls the noise level in the observed responses. Collectively, these three assumptions ensure that the estimated factorized outcome matrix,
\widehat{\mathbf{M}}=\widehat{\mathbf{U}}\widehat{\mathbf{W}}^{\top}-\mathbf{1}_{N}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}^{\top}
, is consistent with the true matrix,
\mathbf{M}^{*}=\mathbf{U}^{*}\mathbf{W}^{*\top}-\mathbf{1}_{N}\boldsymbol{\zeta}^{*\top}
, in terms of the Frobenius norm. Assumption
4
includes a non-degeneracy condition requiring strictly positive lower bounds on the eigenvalues of the latent covariance and weight matrices. This prevents the latent factor model from collapsing and guarantees that each loading weight retains sufficient information as the dimension
J
of the items grows. Also, Assumption 4 requires non-singular covariates
{\mathbf{X}}
and bounded noise variance, both of which are standard conditions for linear regression.

Based on these assumptions, Lemma
1
establishes that
\widehat{\mathbf{U}}
,
\widehat{\mathbf{W}}
and
\widehat{{\mathbf{B}}}
consistently recover the true latent factors, the weighting matrix of the generalized factor model (
1
), and the factor-wise linear model coefficients. This recovery holds in a standard sense, up to an invertible scaled rotation.

Lemma 1

Let
\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{c}=(\mathbf{I}_{N}-\mathbf{P}_{\mathbf{1}})\widehat{\mathbf{U}}
and
\mathbf{U}^{*}_{c}=(\mathbf{I}_{N}-\mathbf{P}_{\mathbf{1}})\mathbf{U}^{*}
denote the centered latent factor matrices, where
\mathbf{P}_{\mathbf{1}}=\frac{1}{N}\mathbf{1}_{N}\mathbf{1}_{N}^{\top}
.
Under Assumptions
1
-
4
, when
N,J\rightarrow\infty
, we have

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{Q}\in\mathcal{O}_{K}}\frac{1}{J}\|\widehat{\mathbf{W}}\mathbf{Q}-\mathbf{W}^{*}\|_{F}^{2}+\min_{\mathbf{Q}\in\mathcal{O}_{K}}\frac{1}{N}\|\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{c}\mathbf{Q}-\mathbf{U}_{c}^{*}\|_{F}^{2}+\min_{\mathbf{Q}\in{\mathcal{O}}_{K}}\|\widehat{{\mathbf{B}}}\mathbf{Q}-{\mathbf{B}}^{*}\|_{F}^{2}\lesssim_{\mathrm{P}}\sqrt{\frac{N+J}{NJ}},

where
\mathcal{O}_{K}=\{\mathbf{Q}\mid\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times K},\det\mathbf{Q}\neq 0\}
.

Assumption 5

It is satisfied that
\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{\Xi})>0
where
{\Xi}_{k,l}:=\mathbb{E}[\tau_{k}({\mathbf{X}})\tau_{l}({\mathbf{X}})]
for
k,l\in[K].

While
\mathbf{\Xi}
defined in (
9
) is inherently positive semi-definite, Assumption
5
requires strict positive definiteness to control the uncertainty introduced by estimating
{\boldsymbol{\Xi}}
, and hence bound the error of the solution to our maximin problem. Ridge regularization could be potentially used to enforce the well-posedness of
{\boldsymbol{\Xi}}
in the presence of the singularity
(
Guo, 2024
)
. In Theorem
2
, we establish the convergence of the proposed DRIFT estimator.

Theorem 2

Suppose Assumptions
1
-
5
hold. For a given radius
\delta>0
, let
\tau^{*}({\mathbf{x}})=\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*\top}{\mathbf{x}}
be the true maximin ITE over the uncertainty set
\Gamma(\delta)
, and
\widehat{\tau}({\mathbf{x}})=\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\top}{\mathbf{x}}
be the estimator constructed using
\widehat{\mathbf{U}}
over the uncertainty set
\widehat{\Gamma}(\delta)
. Then we have:

\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*}\|_{2}\lesssim_{\mathrm{P}}\sqrt{\tilde{E}}\cdot\max\Big\{\sqrt{\tilde{E}},\frac{1}{\sqrt{\delta}}\Big\},\text{as }N,J\to\infty,

where
\tilde{E}=(\frac{N+J}{NJ})^{1/4}+(\frac{1}{N})^{1/2}.

Theorem
2
shows that the convergence rate of our empirical estimator
\widehat{\tau}({\mathbf{x}})
can be decomposed into two components: the first term captures the estimation error of the linear coefficient matrix
{\mathbf{B}}^{*}
. The second term accounts for the additional uncertainty in estimating the optimal aggregation weights
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
, arising from the empirical approximation of
{\boldsymbol{\Xi}}
and the on-target set
\Gamma(\delta)
.
This result distinguishes our theory from previous work on maximin regression (e.g.,
Meinshausen and Bühlmann, 2015
;
Guo, 2024
) by resolving the challenge posed by estimating the on-target set
\Gamma(\delta)
. Our explicit control of the estimation error for this set is essential for the consistency of the maximin ITE estimator. Moreover, this theoretical guarantee is not limited to linear parametric models. When more flexible machine learning methods are used to fit factorized outcomes in high-dimensional settings, similar convergence results can also be obtained
(
Wang et al., 2023
)
.

\{\mathring{Y}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}

\widehat{\tau}(\mathbf{x}_{i})

\mathring{\tau}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{i})

For each setting, we repeat the simulation

6
Application to EMBARC

6.1
Background of the Study

We applied
DRIFT
to a randomized controlled trial of major depressive disorder (MDD), EMBARC
(
Trivedi et al., 2016
)
, to assess its real-world performance.
Participants were randomly assigned to receive either sertraline (
A=1
) or placebo (
A=0
).
The Clinical Global Improvement (CGI) scale assessed at the end of treatment was used as the GEO to guide the construction of the on-target factorized outcome set.
Individual items in the Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAM-D)
(
Hamilton, 1960
)
, a clinician-administered depression assessment, were used as the manifest outcomes
\mathbf{Y}
to learn latent factors
\mathbf{Z}
.
To enhance learning efficiency and maintain sample balance, items with few endorsements and inconsistent collection procedures were excluded
(
van Borkulo et al., 2015
;
Chekroud et al., 2017
)
, and sparse categories were combined.
Electroencephalogram (EEG) measures of brain activity related to antidepressant response were also incorporated to improve the estimation performance on the latent factors
(
Yang et al., 2024
)
.
Because the excess loss for constructing the on-target set is defined for binary GEO, including representations of continuous EEG outcomes would lead to scale incompatibility. Therefore, we construct the on-target set using only HAM-D items and use EEG measures only to improve latent factor estimation.
Given that prior studies identified 2–4 latent factors for depression
(
Van Dorn et al., 2016
;
van Loo et al., 2012
;
Sunderland et al., 2013
;
Chen et al., 2021
)
, we fit the factor model with 4 latent domains.
Covariates
\mathbf{X}
include age, sex, Flanker Interference Accuracy score (indicating cognitive control), and inferred baseline latent domains.
After excluding participants with missing measures, 168 subjects were included in the analysis, of whom 75 received sertraline.

We compared our method with three alternatives:
Original GEO
,
Factorized GEO
and
Obs Maximin
(see Section
5
). Because
Obs Maximin
suffers from latent factor non-identifiability, we applied an oblique promax rotation
(
Hendrickson and White, 1964
)
to the loading matrix and used the rotated structure as the factor estimates for fair comparison. It also improves clinical interpretability by allowing each rotated factor to better reflect distinct, clinically meaningful domains. To assess generalizability, we assessed the prediction accuracy (ACC in Section
5
) of each method on external outcomes not used in training. These external outcomes include (i) the self-reported 17-item Concise Associated Symptom Tracking (CAST) scale, a validated instrument for quantifying depression-related symptoms by probing experiences such as anxiety, sleep disturbances, restlessness, and shifts in mood and energy
(
Minhajuddin et al., 2020
)
, and (ii) the self-rated Frequency, Intensity, and Burden of Side Effects Rating (FIBSER) scale, which measures medication side effects
(
Wisniewski et al., 2006
)
. Because these outcomes differ clinically from the training responses, performance on them reflects out-of-distribution generalizability.

7
Discussion

The proposed DRIFT framework offers a principled approach to learning robust ITE across multiple outcome domains, with broad potential applications in precision medicine. Several extensions could further enhance its utility.
First, replacing the explained-variance reward function with an absolute-deviance criterion may reduce conservative estimates for small
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
values and improve robustness to outliers. Second, the current linear treatment response model for latent factors could be relaxed to incorporate nonlinear CATE models such as spline regressions, or causal forests
(
Wager and Athey, 2018
)
. Finally, addressing missing data in the items is an important extension. Multiple imputation or matrix completion
(
Davenport et al., 2014
;
Chen et al., 2019
)
can be incorporated.

Another key extension is to directly derive individualized treatment rules (ITRs), using policy learning or value function optimization
(
Chen et al., 2021
)
to guide treatment strategies. Finally, expanding the framework to handle multiple treatments, including combination therapies or sequential, multiple assignment randomized trials (SMART)
(
Lei et al., 2012
)
, would offer the ability to estimate dynamic treatment regimens.
