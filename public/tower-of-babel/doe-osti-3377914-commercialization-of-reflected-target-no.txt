 PROJECT SCOPE AND OBJECTIVE
 In a concentrated solar thermal (CST) plant, the solar field composed of heliostats is one of the largest
 drivers of CST plant cost [1]. Heliostat optics directly determine efficiency, and thus the necessary size,
 of the field. This makes mirror optics a primary driver of CST plant efficiency. The U.S. Heliostat
 Consortium (HelioCon) roadmap [2] identified the lack of metrology tools for opto-mechanical error
 measurement available for commercial heliostat production as a Tier 1 gap (most important). Collecting
 these measurements in the laboratory or warehouse using conventional methods requires large, often
 costly setups, or time-consuming, lower-resolution photogrammetry. Under HelioCon, the National
 Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR) began developing a new optical measurement system called Reflected
 Target Non-Intrusive Assessment (ReTNA), which performs deflectometry with printed targets instead of
 the projectors and screens used in conventional fringe methods. ReTNA’s differences compared to
 conventional methods, described below, make it well suited to address this gap and be an additional tool
 for commercial heliostat developers, complementing conventional measurement systems. This section
 describes ReTNA’s status prior to the project, the project objectives, and its benefits to the mission of the
 U.S. Department of Energy.

 Prior to this project, ReTNA was used to effectively measure optical surfaces within a predetermined
 uncertainty target in a laboratory environment. This project focuses on turning this research software and
 methodology into a commercial tool for partners in the CST industry. It fills an industry measurement
 need for a flexible, low-cost way to measure surfaces at a higher resolution than photogrammetry but a
 lower resolution than traditional deflectometry systems, generating a useful and complementary
 measurement to both. ReTNA is designed to use simple equipment: a small, thin printed target; consumer
 cameras; and a Raspberry Pi controller. This equipment is generally lower cost than the components used
 in traditional fringe deflectometry systems, which typically include machine vision cameras, high-quality
 projectors and large rigid screens. The total cost depends on the specifics of the system. Excluding the
 screen and target, the other hardware used in ReTNA (cameras and control system) is approximately one
 third the cost of the recommended equipment for a SOFAST fringe system (camera, projector, control
 PC), largely due to the additional cost of the projector [3]. Internal system installation estimates at the
 laboratory have indicated the material and installation cost of the screen far outweighs that of the other
 system components. ReTNA operation using multiple images and a smaller, movable target, are the
 primary drivers of system affordability.

 Often, for industry installations, measurement speed, space constraint around the assembly area, and
 lighting requirements are larger concerns than cost. ReTNA can address these concerns. Unlike traditional
 fringe deflectometry, it can generate results from a single image and in bright or changing lighting,
 making it useful for use as an in-line quality control measurement. The printed targets are also well suited
 to stitching results from multiple images, allowing for versatile measurement with smaller, movable
 targets. It is designed to use photogrammetry to locate the target, mirror, and camera, removing the need
 to careful positioning during setup and precise calibration. All these characteristics make it adaptable for
 testing new heliostat designs at varied orientations.

 The objective of this work is to adapt and demonstrate NLR’s ReTNA software in a commercial
 environment. This work will be focused on three primary objectives:
 • Adapt system layout, measurement procedure, and software for commercial deployment.
 • Design, install, and validate a commercial ReTNA system, streamline setup and data collection,
 with continuous input from industry.
 • Conduct industry and market analysis to determine the best path forward for a commercial
 ReTNA system.

 Benefit to the Mission of the U.S. Department of Energy

 3

 Development of needed measurement and quality assurance tools advances the U.S. Department of
 Energy’s mission of increasing energy resilience, capacity, and affordability in the United States.
 Solar thermal is a growing industry worldwide [4], [5]. In the United States, concentrated solar is of
 particular interest for high-energy industrial process heat applications, from low temperature (e.g.,
 industrial food and beverage processing, forestry products, mining) to very high temperature (steel,
 concrete)[6]. U.S. solar resources are most plentiful throughout the western and southwestern United
 States[7], well suited to provide heat to industries such as concrete and aggregate production, mining,
 and some lower-temperature industries. U.S. power needs are also increasing [8], [9], and more
 resilient technologies that integrate energy storage can assist with the changing grid and electricity
 loads. For use in both power generation and industrial heat, system reliability is essential for lowering
 the risk and cost of solar thermal and has been a challenge in some past installations. ReTNA offers a
 simple, low-cost option for verifying heliostat optics. It has applications in both R&D and large-scale
 production in the solar thermal industry and can contribute to efforts to boost U.S. energy supply and
 enhance reliability.

 In the R&D space, our surveys with heliostat developers highlighted a need for a simple, adaptable,
 and effective optical measurement tool that will allow for validation and broader innovation in solar
 concentrator design. For example, at Solar Dynamics’ facility over the course of this project, several
 iterations of ReTNA were developed to measure a fully assembled heliostat at two different
 orientations. This was essential for their measurement needs. Measuring a heliostat face up on the
 assembly jig allowed them to quickly adjust the jig. Measuring at 30 degrees’ elevation allowed them
 to estimate the assembled heliostat’s performance. ReTNA was also adapted to measure a blank
 mirror on a separate bonding jig and to estimate the error imparted on the mirror by bonding ribs to
 the back. All these different measurements were completed with the same low-cost components, in
 ambient warehouse lighting. This enabled them to quickly understand and improve their heliostat
 design and could not have been completed with a traditional deflectometry system at this location.
 Staff at Solar Dynamics were able to measure their prototype heliostat with ReTNA to make small
 design changes and quickly adapt the measurement system to different layouts to better characterize
 their design heliostat.

 The work completed for this project is also applicable to mass production, supporting DOE’s mission
 to boost domestic energy and manufacturing production. A fast, low-cost, quality assurance system is
 essential to expanding and de-risking U.S. solar collector production. Internal reports have shown
 errors in heliostats in the field. The cause of this is not always clear because of limited measurements
 of heliostats over time, but several groups have worked on investigating this issue [10]. One possible
 cause is error during the heliostat manufacture and assembly process. Typically, not every heliostat is
 measured on the assembly line. If this could be done quickly and inexpensively with a system like
 ReTNA, a precise understanding of each heliostat could be catalogued, ensuring the field performs as
 expected, and later providing information on heliostat performance over time. ReTNA has several
 benefits over traditional deflectometry systems for this application:
 1. Accurate measurement in the ambient and dynamic lighting of a manufacturing facility.
 2. 2D surface measurement from a single image, enabling fast in-line measurement.
 3. Light, modular targets and a small footprint. Unlike a projector screen, targets can be
 modular, discontinuous, and not flat. This makes target mounting easier (done by one
 technician on a lift at an NLR installation). The modular targets are also well suited for
 multicamera measurement stitching. This combined with their weight makes it easier to
 implement a vertical setup, in which the mirror is measured face up, reducing the footprint by
 about half, and removing additional steps required to reorient mirrors on the assembly line. If
 the heliostat is traveling down an assembly line, a small ReTNA archway could stitch images

 4

 to assess the entire mirror, similar to how the 21-m2 SunLite heliostat was measured with a
               4.5-m2 target.
 These benefits make ReTNA particularly valuable for smaller solar heat for industrial processes
 (SHIP) deployments because it can provide quality assurance measurements during production,
 assembly, and installation, where existing systems could be cost prohibitive for smaller heliostat
 production quantities. Improving reliability, capacity, and lowering the cost of heat production
 benefits both U.S. industry and the DOE’s mission.

 Milestone 1.1.1: Python photogrammetry

 Milestone 1.1.1 was to develop a Python-based photogrammetry tool that could be included in the
 ReTNA codebase and used instead of the PhotoModeler software to calibrate a new ReTNA setup. To
 complete this milestone, the Python tool had to demonstrate coherence with PhotoModeler. The purpose
 of this milestone was to make ReTNA self-contained so that it could be used by others without requiring
 purchase of additional software. As the testing scope expanded, this work was delayed and deprioritized.
 Luckily, during the course of this project, Sandia National Laboratories released their OpenCSP software
 [14]. This is a fully open-source tool for concentrated solar thermal software development, released under
 an Apache 2.0 license and therefore available for use in private and commercial projects. Some

 9

 development in OpenCSP’s “scene_reconstruction” code could be leveraged to reduce the new
 development necessary for the Python photogrammetry milestone. NLR’s team modified this code for
 ReTNA calibration purposes. To compare the commercial PhotoModeler software currently used to
 calibrate ReTNA, a simple set of 19 test images was used. These images, like the one pictured in Figure
 4, contain 75 fiducial targets from a variety of angles, and some of which are placed on books at a
 different height. These are the images used for PhotoModeler camera calibration. Because every image
 contains every target, this is one of the simplest possible test cases to compare the solved 3D position of
 each target point.

 Figure 4: One of the PhotoModeler camera calibration images used in the PhotoModeler-Python
 comparison.

 PhotoModeler was used to identify the points, which were passed into a custom Python code based on the
 OpenCSP scene reconstruction code. A common scale and reference frame were set for both
 measurements, and both tools solved for the 3D location of each point. After correcting several issues, the
 results agreed. Figure 5 shows the cartesian distances (magnitude) between solved 3D point locations in
 Python and PhotoModeler. Figure 6 shows the distribution of these results.

 10

 Figure 5: The distance between solved 3D point locations in Python and PhotoModeler.

 The sinusoidal behavior of these results is an effect of the marker numbering system and positioning. The
 x-axis shows marker number, in order. Each sheet contains five consecutive markers, (1-5, 6-10, etc.)
 through 75. Sheets are arranged in order, left to right, top to bottom, so larger residual differences in
 Figure 5 correspond to markers further from the center of the pattern, and generally further from the
 center of the images. This effect is likely due to small differences in the camera lens distortion
 formulation, or reprojection algorithms used by the two different tools. Python-PhotoModeler differences
 are small in this study, but should be examined in larger photogrammetry models to ensure these stay
 small.

 Figure 6: The distribution of distances between solved 3D point locations in Python and PhotoModeler.

 We can estimate the accuracy of measured points using the single tailed formulation for 95% confidence
 interval (CI):

                                                  𝐶𝐼95% = 𝜇 + 1.645𝜎

 11

 This equation allows us to estimate that two points solved with Python and PhotoModeler will be solved
 to within 0.245 mm, with 95% certainty. This meets the milestone target of having 90% of points within 1
 mm; however, this test must still be repeated in the future on a more complex scene involving a test
 mirror.

 One challenge in comparing photogrammetry tools is aligning different formulations for camera
 calibration. In initial comparisons, errors converting these formulations led to several centimeters of
 difference in the solved 3D point locations. This was corrected in the final comparison by removing lens
 distortion from all points before processing to get a one-to-one comparison of only the photogrammetry
 solving algorithms. In future tests, the best strategy would be to calibrate the cameras separately for
 Python and PhotoModeler photogrammetry, using the OpenCV camera calibration method [15], and the
 PhotoModeler camera calibration method, respectively [16].

 For near-term ReTNA usage, both systems will be used and compared, until researchers are confident that
 they will continue to produce the same results.

 Milestone 1.2.1: BoM and system design

 Another project objective was to demonstrate the low cost of a ReTNA installation by examining the bill
 of materials for a commercial layout. A target system equipment cost of <$10,000 was selected for
 ReTNA after discussion with commercial partners and project technical management based on the
 ReTNA value proposition and logistical considerations, such as project budget. Although estimating the
 cost of a traditional deflectometry system is difficult, we expect its cost to be well above this. One simple
 fringe deflectometry system was budgeted at the lab for approximately double this cost in materials and
 leveraged existing laboratory structures.

 This milestone was met over the course of the project. The initial system design was quoted at $9,877.
 Further system cost reductions changed the final system cost to $5,781. A simple bill of materials is
 shown in Figure 7. This equipment was used for all measurements at SolarTAC.

 Figure 7: Bill of materials for installed ReTNA R&D layout. The original planned costs (above) were reduced
 by switching the camera and lenses to less expensive versions, which were used during testing.

 More significantly, for deployment in both the laboratory and commercial facilities, the nonmaterial costs
 were found to be a bigger driver of system adoption. These factors include:

 • The permanent, non-movable footprint of the system, given space constraints in the laboratory,
 R&D facility, and assembly line.

 • Ease of adapting the system to different orientations, to measure a heliostat or mirror components
 at different orientations (face up, vertical, at 30-degrees elevation, etc.).

 • Ability to measure in ambient or changing lighting.

 This finding is included in the Lessons Learned section and is captured in future ReTNA value

 Milestone 2.1.1: ReTNA measurement comparison

 The objective of Milestone 2.1.1 was to demonstrate the validity of ReTNA through repeated
 measurement, and comparison with a secondary deflectometry system. ReTNA measurements were taken
 at Solar Dynamics’ facility between January 2025 and February 2026. These measurements show the
 effects of underlying structure of the heliostat, such as small surface deviations at the locations of
 mounting points and supporting ribs. Correlation between the mechanical structure in the mirror surface
 shape should be observable in all ReTNA measurements and provides an initial qualitative verification
 that the measurement is capturing real and expected surface deviations.

 ReTNA repeatability and sensitivity was studied extensively on the mobile SolarTAC ReTNA
 setup and stationary setups constructed at Heliogen and NLR’s OTF. Measurement uncertainty
 in the slope error root mean square was determined to be 0.22 mrad with 95% confidence [13].
 Root mean square uncertainty detailed under a variety of conditions, including shifting and
 rotating the sample, lighting changes, system recalibration, moving the target, and so on, and
 will be described in a forthcoming journal paper. Characterizing repeatability and uncertainty in
 the SolarTAC setup is more difficult, given the recent completion of the system, and more time
 required to collect measurements (stitched results from ~40 images). Because of the longer
 image collection process, only a few repeated full heliostat scans were collected. This is not
 enough for statistical comparison of the full heliostat root mean square but does give some
 indication of the measurement repeatability. In total, we have two sets of repeatability
 measurements. In both comparisons, the root mean square values for slope error in x, y, and
 magnitude each varied by ≤0.06 mrad.

 For pointwise uncertainty, two ReTNA measurements can be interpolated onto a regular grid
 and subtracted. Figure 8 shows two surface slope measurements collected on full-scale layout 2,
 with no heliostat adjustments made in between. For each measurement (A and B), the left
 column shows the raw surface error results in the x-direction, y-direction, and a total surface
 error magnitude at each point. These results are then interpolated onto regular grids in the
 middle column of Figure 8, and the two measurements are subtracted. The subtracted value at
 each grid point is shown on the right, on the same scale. For x and y slope error plots, red
 indicates positive slope error and blue indicates negative. For the slope error magnitude, the
 lowest value on the scale is zero. This measurement was collected on a commercial prototype
 heliostat during adjustment, so quantitative scales are not included at this time.

 13

 Figure 8: Repeated measurements of the SunLite heliostat, which are subtracted to assess measurement
 uncertainty.

 The distribution of slope error magnitude differences in Figure 8 has an average absolute value
 of 0.32 mrad. The standard deviation of the distribution of differences is 0.42 mrad. There are
 likely several causes of this difference. First, some amount of smoothing is necessary to
 interpolate unstructured data onto a regular grid. This is currently done using a bivariant spline
 method in Python’s SciPy package and necessitates different automatically selected smoothing
 parameters for different cases, which can lead to slight differences. Positioning measured points
 on the heliostat surface is another source of uncertainty. Because there is some periodic error in
 the mirror, small misalignments of the entire surface (shifting the entire result in x and y), can
 lead to larger differences in subtracted data. Precise surface alignment has been less of a focus
 in the past because this does not matter when considering the root mean square measurement,
 but this is an area we are devoting more time to in the future. Currently, the system solves the
 camera position independently in each image. In the future, especially for a commercial system
 where positions of the camera, target, and heliostat are well controlled, additional information
 could be added to the software to decrease this uncertainty.

 Unfortunately, comparison data were not available from the other measurement system planned
 at SolarTAC, largely because of events outside the control of project participants. This has

 14

 made it difficult to directly validate this specific ReTNA layout against another deflection-
 based system that measures surface angle. We have seen good qualitative agreement with
 photogrammetry measurement collected by Solar Dynamics, but detailed comparison between
 these different types of results is difficult. Several efforts are currently underway to verify
 ReTNA against other measurement systems under other projects. Under a recent effort in the
 joint 3rd Party Metrology project between NLR and Sandia, ReTNA results were compared with
 a high-precision, well-known optical surface.

 ReTNA System Technical Improvements and Achievements
 In addition to the planned project milestones, several technical improvements to the ReTNA
 measurement system hardware, software, and procedures were necessary for deployment of the
 system in commercial environments. The most significant of these are listed below:
 • Mobile data collection, necessary for the gantry crane setup at SolarTAC.
 Development for this project enabled the truck-mounted setup used at Ivanpah and lift-
 mounted setup used at the NLR solar furnace.
 • Replacing heliostat corner reference points with a ground reference frame located
 with computer vision fiducial targets. This proved necessary at SolarTAC to assess a
 heliostat larger than the camera’s field of view. This system uses targets on the ground
 or on the mirror surface to locate the camera pose in each image.
 • Improved stitching, which proved to be necessary for the setup at SolarTAC. Initial
 stitching methods resulted in discontinuities in the mirror surface. A new algorithm is
 used to align data based on an interpolation and k-nearest-neighbor (KNN) technique,
 which will be described in a future presentation or publication.
 • Automated image capture. Images are captured and transferred wirelessly using a
 Raspberry Pi. This was necessary at SolarTAC because images had to be captured on
 two cameras, and they are out of reach of system operators.
 • Improved software workflow, definitions, and reference frame alignment with
 industry. Being able to adapt ReTNA results to industry reference frame, having clear
 definitions for ReTNA’s native reference frames and conventions, and creating a clean
 software workflow is essential to allowing industry users to run the measurement
 system, understand the results, and use them to improve industrial processes.
 Effectively working with industry was a major effort and area of improvement for
 ReTNA over the course of this project.

 Generated Technical Data and Reports
 This project generated primarily internal testing documents (test plans, procedures, BoMs, code

 commercial partners, and improved and modified based on feedback. Developments and improvements to
 the ReTNA measurement system, supported by this project and others, were captured in several published
 and pending reports and conference presentations over the course of this project [13], [17], [18], [19], [20],

 This section details the advancements of ReTNA towards commercial usage. The Market
 Advancements section provides an overview of how ReTNA has become more suited for
 commercial use over the course of this project. The Commercialization Achievements and

 15

 Success Stories section lists technology demonstrations, presentations, and patents that were
 completed for this project.

 Market Advancements
 ReTNA has several valuable use cases in the CST industry. Primarily, it provides a method for fast
 measurement in a variety of orientations with low-cost components. The system can use a small,
 discontinuous target with less strict flatness requirements than alternative systems and is less sensitive
 to lighting levels and changes. This makes it a useful and flexible system for heliostat assessment
 during R&D at a variety of orientations, for simple quality control measurement on an assembly line,
 and for in situ outdoor heliostat measurement in factory lighting.

 Prior to this project, ReTNA had only been used in the laboratory and for single, time-consuming
 measurements at external facilities. Work undertaken over the course of this project has moved
 ReTNA significantly closer to a commercial measurement system. These commercial demonstration
 campaigns have been driven by interest from U.S. heliostat developers, manufacturers, and operators
 and have greatly improved ReTNA viability in the heliostat market. Some of the advancements made
 during this project that improved ReTNA’s market viability include:
 • Development and demonstration of a mobile (moving but coupled camera and target)
 measurement system, increasing system flexibility.
 • Demonstration of measurement with multiple cameras.
 • Demonstration of measurement with a much smaller target with improved information
 density, and improvement of stitching algorithms to combine data from 30+ images.
 • Automation of image capture using a wireless Raspberry Pi controller.
 • Demonstration on a real heliostat manufacturing line, and extensive sensitivity study of the
 installed system.
 • Demonstration of several installations and layouts at an active heliostat R&D facility and
 examination of the resulting data with heliostat engineers.
 • Under another project, but leveraging development completed under this TCF project,
 demonstration of truck-mounted system at an operating CSP plant, and discussion of data
 with plant personnel.

 Unfortunately, the U.S. heliostat market is small, with little large-scale heliostat manufacturing
 planned in the near-term. The project team is continuing to investigate opportunities for heliostats, as
 well as in manufacturing lines for other precise, specular surfaces, like windows, windshields, and
 large glass panels.

 LOOKING AHEAD: LESSONS LEARNED AND PATH FORWARD
 This section details the next steps for the ReTNA measurement system. The Lessons Learned
 subsection lists important considerations for future commercial deployment and measurement
 comparison with ReTNA. The Path Forward subsection describes the near-term next steps for
 ReTNA, and potential avenues for continued development and usage.

 Lessons Learned
 This work led to several important lessons learned that will be carefully considered in
 future projects and ReTNA development work.
 • The extent of tool development needed to get methodology and software from the
 laboratory into commercial applications was significant. Commercial users have
 their own specific use cases, needs for tool outputs, and constraints, which leads to
 new and unique layouts that must be validated. Testing timelines are also
 constrained by internal hurdles (time required to set up an agreement with partners)
 and commercial partners’ timelines (when a heliostat is available for test during
 R&D, production timelines for manufacturing quality assurance). In the future, it
 would have been useful to plan for multiple rounds of testing from the start. The
 team is thankful for DOE leadership’s flexibility, allowing for no-cost time
 extensions that let us match ReTNA testing with commercial partners’ schedules.
 • Pointwise ReTNA validation is challenging, especially with the mobile layout.
 Although root mean square results on the full mirror surface are consistent, the
 positional uncertainty of measurement points (the x-y position on the mirror surface
 where the measurement is taking place) makes it difficult to accurately align data
 for pointwise comparison. For example, shifting observed sinusoidal error on the
 mirror surface by even a small amount will result in a large difference at a given

 17

 grid point. This is less important for most practical uses up the tool (the entire
 dataset is shifted together) but makes comparing repeated measurements difficult.
 • System cost is still a valid metric, but system versatility is often of higher value to
 commercial users. ReTNA’s ability to use smaller, less flat targets, and to take
 measurements from a single image in bright lighting was usually more of a selling
 point than the low cost of components. The low component cost was still valuable
 and enabled us to do demonstrations more easily, but this is an important lesson for
 future iterations of this tool.
 • We gained important insights from industry surveys. Stakeholders at each level
 (mirror manufacturer, heliostat developer, heliostat manufacturer, field operator)
 were interviewed:
 o A survey with a mirror manufacturer provided useful information on when
 they typically collect measurements. They indicated online measurement is
 likely not needed during individual flat panel manufacture. Curved mirrors
 are removed from the line for intermittent QC measurement. ReTNA could
 improve this process with a single image online system, but it may be
 difficult on highly curved mirrors (troughs, secondary concentrators, etc.).
 ReTNA seems most useful for measurement of focused facets if they’re
 focused online and of fully assembled heliostats.
 o As mentioned in the previous bullet point, ReTNA’s versatility was a more
 important selling point than equipment costs for R&D and manufacturing.
 Both surveyed potential users in this area recognized ReTNA’s value.
 o An operating CSP field manager was interested in ReTNA results, but the
 scale of ReTNA measurement is more useful for identifying
 underperformance trends (e.g., all heliostats experiencing a similar failure)
 and lessons for future development than diagnosing individual heliostats.

 Path Forward
 For the next few months, NLR will continue to support Solar Dynamics’ use of the ReTNA
 measurement system installed at SolarTAC, as Solar Dynamics LLC makes final
 adjustments and measurements to their SunLite heliostat prototype. Solar Dynamics and
 NLR technology transfer personnel have met several times to discuss potential licensing
 options for longer-term use of this system. Solar Dynamics LLC is also considering several
 options to incorporate ReTNA into larger-scale manufacture of the SunLite heliostat. NLR
 will continue to work with Solar Dynamics LLC to make sure this system meets time, cost,
 and space requirements for incorporation into the SunLite assembly line.

 Heliogen also expressed interest in the measurement system early in the project but
 unfortunately closed the heliostat manufacturing branch of their company in May 2025.
 NLR has also met with another concentrated solar mirror manufacturer and continues to
 investigate additional opportunities for this measurement system.

 ReTNA will continue to actively support heliostat measurement at NLR. It is currently
 being used in the OTF building to assess new composite facet constructions, at the high-flux
 solar furnace to assess measure and tune the optics, and at the outdoor heliostat testbed at
 Flatirons Campus to assess heliostat performance. Work completed for this TCF project has
 been essential for ReTNA’s deployment in these different locations and layouts.
