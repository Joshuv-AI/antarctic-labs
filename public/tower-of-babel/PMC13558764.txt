Synthesis and characterization of selenium-containing sodium phosphate glasses with enhanced physical and radiation shielding performance
Authors: M. A. Madshal, Abdullah F. Al Naim, Mohammed Al-Yaari, Norah Alsairy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In this study, ternary selenium sodium phosphate glasses with the molar composition 50P2O5–(50 − x)Na2O–xSe, where x = 0, 0.5, 1, 1.5, 10, 20, and 30 mol%, were fabricated using the melt-quenching technique. The structural, morphological, optical, physical, and gamma-ray shielding properties of the prepared glasses were investigated using X-ray diffraction (XRD), Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), scanning electron microscopy (SEM), UV–visible spectroscopy, and theoretical shielding calculations. XRD analysis confirmed the amorphous nature of the synthesized glasses, while FTIR analysis revealed selenium-induced modifications in the phosphate network, indicating structural rearrangement with increasing Se content. UV–visible spectroscopy showed characteristic absorption bands centered at 470 and 650 nm, associated with Se-related electronic transitions. Increasing Se concentration reduced the indirect optical band gap from 3.380 to 2.890 eV and the direct band gap from 4.091 to 3.750 eV, accompanied by an increase in the refractive index from 2.302 to 2.427, reflecting enhanced electronic polarizability. The density increased from 2.350 to 2.600 g/cm³, whereas the molar volume decreased from 43.388 to 41.144 cm³/mol, suggesting a more compact glass network. Gamma-ray shielding parameters calculated using Phy-X/PSD over the 0.015–15 MeV range showed a clear improvement with Se addition. At 0.015 MeV, the mass attenuation coefficient (MAC) increased from 5.686 to 27.578 cm²/g, while the linear attenuation coefficient (LAC) increased from 13.362 to 71.702 cm⁻¹. In parallel, the half-value layer (HVL), tenth-value layer (TVL), and mean free path (MFP) decreased with increasing Se content, confirming enhanced attenuation efficiency. Among the investigated compositions, the 30 mol% Se-containing glass exhibited the best shielding performance. These findings indicate that Se-rich sodium phosphate glasses are promising multifunctional materials for photonic applications and low-energy gamma-ray radiation shielding.

Introduction

Phosphate-based oxide glasses constitute an important class of vitreous materials in which phosphorus pentoxide (P2O5) acts as the primary glass former1. Their structure consists of interconnected PO4 tetrahedral units linked by P–O–P bridging oxygen (BO) atoms, forming a polymer-like network that is fundamentally distinct from the SiO4-based framework of conventional silicate glasses2. The structural units of phosphate glasses are conveniently described using the Qn notation, where n denotes the number of BO atoms per tetrahedron. Accordingly, vitreous P2O5 consists entirely of Q3 units3,4. The addition of modifier oxides disrupts the phosphate network by converting BOs into NBOs, thereby reducing network connectivity. According to the [O]/[P] ratio, phosphate glasses are classified into Q⁰–Q³ structures, including orthophosphate, pyrophosphate, metaphosphate, and ultraphosphate, respectively1,2,4; when [O]/[P] reaches 3 through modifier oxide addition, metaphosphate glasses mainly composed of Q² units are formed4,5. Owing to their structural nature, phosphate glasses exhibit several distinctive physical and chemical characteristics, including low melting and glass transition temperatures, reduced viscosity, and relatively high thermal expansion coefficients compared with those of silicate and borate glass systems6. From a chemical standpoint, phosphate-based glasses generally display higher solubility and reactivity in aqueous environments. Although this behavior limits their applicability in load-bearing structural components, it provides important advantages for bioactive materials and controlled-degradation systems7. Moreover, the chemical durability and structural stability of phosphate glasses can be effectively tailored through compositional modification, particularly by incorporating alkali, alkaline-earth, and transition-metal oxides such as Fe2O3, CaO, Na2O, MgO, and ZnO8,9. As a result of their favorable optical properties, high ionic mobility, and compositional flexibility, phosphate-based glasses have attracted significant attention for a wide range of advanced technological applications, including laser materials, solid-state electrochemical devices10, ion-conducting electrolytes11, bioresorbable implants12, and radioactive waste immobilization13. Among these systems, sodium phosphate glasses are of particular technological interest due to their low melting temperatures, high Na+ ionic conductivity, and tunable optical and physical properties11. Considerable efforts have therefore been devoted to enhancing their thermal, chemical, optical, and radiation-shielding performance through compositional engineering. In this context, the incorporation of selected dopants or substituent species provides an effective route for modifying the phosphate network, controlling the concentration of non-bridging oxygens (NBOs), and tailoring the density, optical band gap14, polarizability, and radiation-interaction behavior of the glass matrix15. Several dopants have been investigated to enhance the structural stability and functional response of phosphate-based glasses. For instance, Kalahe et al. 16 demonstrated that incorporating Fe2O3 into binary sodium phosphate glasses significantly enhances network connectivity by promoting the formation of P–O–Fe cross-linkages, thereby reducing the NBO content and improving chemical durability. Similarly, Nabhan et al. 17 reported that substituting Na2O with divalent metal oxides increases the glass transition and softening temperatures, indicating enhanced network cross-linking and improved thermal and chemical stability. Kumar et al. 18 showed that CuO incorporation into P₂O₅–Na₂O glasses increases density and network polymerization, as evidenced by the presence of Q² metaphosphate units, while also modifying the optical band gap and refractive index, making these glasses suitable for laser and nonlinear optical applications. In parallel with these structural and optical developments, phosphate-based glasses have recently attracted increasing attention as photon-shielding materials. Although borate19, silicate, and borosilicate glasses20 have been widely explored for radiation-shielding applications because of their structural stability, ease of fabrication, and compositional flexibility, phosphate glasses offer additional advantages, including compositional tunability and the ability to accommodate various modifying oxides. Almuqrin et al. 21 investigated the influence of Tb2O3 content in the La2O3–P2O5 glass system and found that substituting La₂O₃ with Tb₂O₃ enhanced the shielding properties of the phosphate glasses. Walid Dridi et al. 22 studied the effect of MnO doping on the radiation-shielding properties of Na₂O–P₂O₅ glasses and concluded that MnO addition improved their shielding efficiency. Among chalcogen elements, selenium has received particular attention due to its semiconducting nature, multiple oxidation states, and strong interaction with electromagnetic radiation in the ultraviolet and visible regions23. Selenium-containing glasses have been reported to exhibit enhanced optical absorption, modified band-gap energies, and improved radiation-interaction characteristics, rendering them promising candidates for optical filtering and photoprotective applications24,25. Recently, Alsaif et al. 26 demonstrated that increasing the selenium oxide content in lead phosphate glasses results in higher density, reduced molar volume, enhanced network depolymerization, and significant band-gap narrowing, highlighting their potential for optical applications. Despite these advances, most previous studies have focused primarily on selenium introduced in oxide form, such as SeO2. In contrast, investigations on the incorporation of elemental selenium into sodium phosphate glass matrices remain limited. To the best of our knowledge, the influence of elemental Se on the structural connectivity, optical behavior, physical properties, and radiation-shielding performance of sodium phosphate glasses has not yet been systematically explored. Therefore, the present work investigates Se-containing sodium phosphate glasses to clarify the relationships among Se content, glass network modification, optical band structure, and photon attenuation efficiency, thereby establishing a selenium-containing sodium phosphate glass system for multifunctional optical and radiation-shielding applications.

Experimental details

Glass preparation

The glass samples were synthesized by the melt-quenching technique using high-purity precursor materials (≥ 99%). Ammonium dihydrogen phosphate (NH4H2PO4, PIOCHEM, 99.5% Purity) and sodium carbonate (Na2CO3, PIOCHEM, 99% Purity) were used as sources of P2O5 and Na2O, respectively, while elemental selenium (Se, DIACHEM, 99% Purity) was employed as received. The base glass composition was designed as (50 − x) mol% Na2O–50 mol% P2O5, with selenium introduced at varying levels (x = 0–30 mol%) via partial substitution of Na2O. Appropriate batch quantities corresponding to the desired glass compositions (listed in Table 1) were accurately weighed using an electronic analytical balance (± 0.0001 g). Before melting, NH4H2PO4 was thermally treated at 350 °C for 30 min to eliminate ammonia and ensure complete conversion to phosphate species (Scheme 1). The remaining raw materials were then added and thoroughly mixed. The prepared batches were melted at 900 °C for 30 min, a temperature sufficient to achieve homogeneous melts across all selenium concentrations. During melting, intermittent manual stirring was applied to enhance compositional uniformity. The molten glass was then quenched by casting it onto a preheated aluminum plate to obtain the glass samples. The prepared NaSeP glass samples exhibited a systematic color variation from colorless to red and dark brown with increasing selenium content, reflecting composition-induced changes in the electronic structure of the glass network (Fig. 1).

X-ray diffraction (XRD) spectroscopy

X-ray diffraction (XRD) measurements were performed using a Rigaku SmartLab diffractometer to characterize the structural properties of the prepared glass samples. The analysis was performed to confirm the amorphous state and to identify any structural changes induced by Se incorporation into the glass matrix.

SEM microscopy

The surface morphology was examined using a JEOL JSM-6510 scanning electron microscope at 30 kV accelerating voltage, 1000× magnification, and 10 mm working distance in SEI mode under high vacuum.

Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy

Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy (Bruker INVENIO S) was employed to analyze the vibrational characteristics and functional groups in the glass network. Spectra were recorded over the 400–4000 cm⁻1 wavenumber range in order to evaluate the effect of Se addition on the local structural units and bonding configurations within the glass matrix.

Results and discussion

XRD analysis

The X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of the undoped sodium phosphate glass (0NaSeP) and the sample containing 30 mol% Se substituted at the expense of Na2O (30NaSeP) are presented in Fig. 2. The undoped glass exhibits a broad diffuse halo centered around 2θ ≈ 25–35°, which is characteristic of an amorphous phosphate network lacking long-range structural order. The absence of sharp diffraction peaks confirms the formation of a fully vitreous structure. Upon incorporation of 30 mol% selenium, the XRD profile preserves its predominantly amorphous nature, as no distinct crystalline reflections are detected within the instrumental resolution. However, a noticeable modification in the halo intensity and shape is observed. The slight enhancement and reshaping of the broad band suggest a change in the medium-range structural organization rather than crystallization. This observation suggests that Se incorporation induces structural rearrangement within the phosphate network. Structurally, Na2O acts as a classical network modifier in phosphate glasses, generating non-bridging oxygens (NBOs) by depolymerizing P–O–P linkages. Replacing Na2O with selenium reduces the concentration of modifier cations (Na⁺), potentially decreasing the overall NBO population and altering phosphate chain connectivity. The observed modification in the diffuse halo can therefore be attributed to changes in the short- and medium-range order arising from the partial reconstruction of the phosphate network.

SEM analysis

The scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images further support the structural evolution inferred from XRD analysis. Figure 3 shows SEM micrographs of the 0NaSeP and 30NaSeP glasses, selected as representative samples. The undoped sample (0NaSeP, Fig. 3a) exhibits a relatively compact and homogeneous morphology, consistent with a uniform glassy matrix. In contrast, the Se-containing glass (30NaSeP, Fig. 3b) shows noticeable microstructural heterogeneity, including irregular granular features and localized agglomerate-like regions. These morphological changes indicate increased surface heterogeneity with increasing Se content, without providing direct evidence for crystalline phase formation. Overall, the substitution of 30 mol% Na₂O by selenium preserves the amorphous nature of the glass while inducing noticeable changes in network connectivity and microstructural uniformity. These structural changes are expected to influence the physical properties, particularly density, molar volume, and optical behavior, mainly due to the reduced Na₂O modifier content and Se-induced modifications in the phosphate glass network.

FTIR interpretation

Figure 4 depicts the FTIR spectra of the sodium phosphate base glass alongside those of selenium-containing glasses. The spectra are arranged to enable a direct comparison of the structural modifications arising from the progressive substitution of Na2O with Se metal. As indicated in Fig. 4, spectral changes were observed with increasing Se content, indicating Se-induced modifications in the glass network. The FTIR results are consistent with previously reported studies40,41 and confirm a pronounced depolymerization of the phosphate network upon the introduction of 50 mol% Na2O. The vibrational bands observed in the 450–1500 cm− 1 region are primarily associated with the phosphate glass network. Phosphate glasses are known to form a three-dimensional network composed of PO₄³⁻ tetrahedral units interconnected through bridging oxygen (BO) atoms. The recorded absorption features are broad and consist of overlapping strong and weak bands with distinct shoulders, reflecting the structural disorder inherent to the glassy state. Accordingly, the spectra were deconvoluted with high accuracy (Fig. 5a) and analyzed over selected spectral regions, each corresponding to specific vibrational modes related to phosphate structural units, including chain and terminal groups. The yielding residuals were below 0.02, as shown in Fig. 5b. Based on spectral inspection, the main absorption features include a broad band centered near 470 cm⁻1, doublet bands in the ranges 650–800 cm⁻1 and 800–1050 cm⁻1, a band at approximately 1151 cm⁻1, a weak band near 1198 cm⁻1, and a strong, broad band centered around 1265 cm⁻1. The overlap of absorption bands complicates accurate band assignment. The bands observed at approximately 750 and 873 cm⁻1 are attributed to the asymmetric and symmetric stretching vibrations of P–O–P bridging oxygen bonds associated with Q² phosphate tetrahedra42. The addition of modifier oxides disrupts the primary phosphate network by occupying interstitial sites, leading to the formation of terminal oxygen species, incorporated as non-bridging oxygens (NBOs). In this context, the absorption band near 470 cm⁻1 is associated with vibrations involving Na⁺ cations. The band observed around 984 cm⁻1 is assigned to the asymmetric stretching vibration of P–O–P (v(P-O-P)as) bonds in Q3 phosphate tetrahedra43. The prominent bands at approximately 1265 and 1198 cm⁻1 correspond to the asymmetric v(PO₂)as and symmetric v(PO₂)s stretching modes of two non-bridging oxygen atoms bonded to phosphorus in Q2 units, respectively. Additionally, the small band near 1093 cm⁻1 is attributed to the symmetric stretching vibration v(PO₂)s 43. Table 3 lists the positions and assignments of the FTIR absorption bands.

UV–Vis spectroscopy

Figure 6 presents the UV–Vis absorption spectra for ternary Na₂O–P₂O₅–Se glasses with varying selenium contents in the wavelength range 190–1100 nm. The absorption spectra show a strong absorption band in the ultraviolet region (200–400 nm) for all selenium-containing glasses, with the absorption intensity increasing with increasing Se content. The absorption edges are broad in all investigated samples, indicating their glassy nature37, which is in good agreement with XRD and SEM results. At higher selenium concentrations (20 and 30 mol%), the absorption edge shifts toward longer wavelengths, indicating a pronounced UV cut-off near 400 nm. This red shift suggests enhanced UV-shielding capability in Se-rich phosphate glasses and may be associated with Se-related electronic transitions and composition-induced modifications in the phosphate glass network. At higher Se contents, particularly 20 and 30 mol%, a broad absorption band appears in the visible region (400–700 nm), which can be attributed to additional Se-related absorption features and changes in the local electronic structure of the glass. Consequently, the 30NaSeP glass exhibits the strongest UV–visible absorption among the investigated samples, indicating improved UV-shielding performance. Owing to their strong ultraviolet absorption and dark amber coloration, Se-rich phosphate glasses may be promising candidates for photoprotective applications. Physical properties

The physical parameters of the glass network, including density, molar volume, packing density, free volume, oxygen packing density, oxygen molar volume, and cross-link density, were evaluated to investigate the effect of selenium incorporation. Density is a sensitive indicator of structural modifications in glass systems, as it strongly depends on ionic size and atomic weight50. Accordingly, these parameters were determined and are discussed in detail. Figure 10a illustrates the variation of density and molar volume as a function of Se content for the prepared phosphate glass samples. The density increases continuously from 2.350 to 2.600 g/cm3 with increasing Se concentration, whereas the molar volume decreases from 43.388 to 41.144 cm3/mol. This opposing trend indicates that the increase in density is mainly driven by the rise in average molar mass resulting from the progressive replacement of Na2O by heavier Se species, along with structural rearrangements within the phosphate glass network. Therefore, the observed density enhancement is associated with mass increase and network contraction rather than volumetric expansion. Further structural information can be obtained from the packing density and free volume variations, as shown in Fig. 10b. The packing density decreases from 0.543 to 0.486. In contrast, the free volume increases from 19.827 to 21.169 cm3/mol as the Se content increases. Although the molar volume decreases with increasing Se content, the decrease in packing density and increase in free volume indicate local structural relaxation and redistribution of unoccupied space within the glass network. These trends can coexist because the increase in density is accompanied by Se-induced electronic modifications that enhance UV absorption, since the density increases from 2.350 to 2.600 g/cm³ upon incorporation of heavier Se species. Moreover, Se-induced electronic modifications, including localized electronic states and UV-absorbing centers, play a key role in improving shielding performance. The same structural tendency is confirmed by the oxygen packing density and oxygen molar volume results presented in Fig. 11. The oxygen packing density decreases from 0.691 to 0.656 mol/L. In contrast, the oxygen molar volume increases from 14.462 to 15.238 cm3/mol as the Se content increases. This behavior reflects a reduction in the compactness of the oxygen sub-network, which can be attributed to the progressive substitution of Na2O by Se and the consequent modification of the local oxygen environment in the phosphate network.

Radiation shielding properties

Radiation shielding properties describe a material’s ability to attenuate harmful ionizing radiation, thereby reducing the intensity of transmitted photons. The interaction of electromagnetic radiation, such as X-rays and gamma rays, with matter occurs through specific mechanisms that depend on both the incident photon energy and the intrinsic properties of the absorbing material. The principal mechanisms governing gamma-ray interactions with matter are the photoelectric effect, Compton scattering, and pair production47,51. Among various shielding materials, glass systems have attracted considerable attention as effective radiation shielding media owing to their optical transparency, structural stability, ease of fabrication, and favorable attenuation characteristics against X-ray and gamma-ray photons. These advantages enable their potential application in medical diagnostics, radiotherapy facilities, and nuclear technology. The radiation attenuation performance of shielding materials can be quantitatively evaluated using several key parameters, including the mass attenuation coefficient (MAC), linear attenuation coefficient (LAC), half-value layer (HVL), tenth-value layer (TVL), mean free path (MFP), and equivalent atomic number (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{Z}_{\mathrm{eq}}$$\end{document}). These parameters provide comprehensive insight into photon–matter interactions and can be theoretically calculated to assess shielding efficiency. In this work, the Phy-X/PSD software52 was used to compute the shielding parameters of the prepared glass samples. The calculated mass attenuation coefficient (MAC) and linear attenuation coefficient (LAC) values are summarized in Tables 5 and 6, respectively. Their dependence on photon energy and selenium (Se) concentration is illustrated in Fig. 12a, b. At 0.015 MeV, the MAC values increased in the following order: 5.686, 6.069, 6.451, 6.833, 13.222, 20.516, and 27.578 (cm2/g) for 0NaSeP, 0.5NaSeP, 1NaSeP, 1.5NaSeP, 10NaSeP, 20NaSeP, and 30NaSeP, respectively. For comparison, at the same energy, the influence of MnO on MAC values in Na2O-P2O5 glasses varied from 5.686 to 14.277 cm2/g, as reported by Walid Dridi et al.22. Moreover, for bismuth borate glasses doped with Yb2O3, MAC values changed from 23.76 to 27.82 cm2/g 53. Furthermore, MAC values of Lithium boro-phosphate-tellurite glasses varied from 4.979 to 25.666 cm2/g as reported by As¸kın et al.54. Similarly, LAC values increase with increasing Se content, indicating an enhancement in the glass matrix’s radiation attenuation capability. For example, at 0.015 MeV, LAC values increase from 13.362 cm− 1 for the 0NaSeP glass to 71.702 cm− 1 for the 30NaSeP glass. This improvement is attributed to the higher atomic number and electron density introduced by selenium incorporation. In the low-energy (photoelectric) region, gamma-ray interactions are dominated by the photoelectric effect47,55, as the photon energy is insufficient to initiate pair production and is efficiently absorbed by tightly bound electrons, particularly in high-Z materials. In the intermediate energy range, Compton scattering becomes the predominant interaction mechanism due to the rapid decrease in photoelectric absorption probability with increasing photon energy. In contrast, the energy threshold for pair production has not yet been reached51. At high gamma-ray energies, pair production dominates the interaction process, as the photon energy exceeds the threshold required for electron–positron pair creation, rendering the photoelectric effect negligible51. The HVL and TVL values, which are critical indicators of shielding thickness requirements, were evaluated over the photon energy range of 0.1–15 MeV for different Se concentrations and are shown in Fig. 12c, d. Both HVL and TVL exhibit a decreasing trend with increasing selenium content. Notably, glass samples with the highest Se concentration exhibit the lowest HVL and TVL values, confirming their superior radiation shielding efficiency. For example, HVL at 0.2 MeV decreased from 2.393 to 1.976 cm, while TVL at 0.2 MeV decreased from 7.950 to 6.564 cm. Furthermore, Fig. 12e shows the variation of MFP as a function of Se concentration across the same energy range. A progressive reduction in MFP with increasing selenium content is observed, further corroborating the enhanced attenuation performance of the prepared glass samples. MFP at 0.2 MeV dropped from 3.453 to 2.851 cm. Additionally, the equivalent atomic number (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{Z}_{\mathrm{eq}}$$\end{document}), which represents the effective atomic behavior of a composite material in photon–matter interactions, increases with increasing selenium incorporation (Fig. 12f), thereby contributing to enhanced shielding performance. This increase is attributed to the substitution of low-Z Na2O by higher-Z selenium, which increases the overall atomic number and electron density of the glass matrix, thereby enhancing the probability of photon–electron interactions.

Overall, the results demonstrate a clear correlation among the structural, physical, optical, and radiation-shielding properties of the Se-containing sodium phosphate glasses. Partial replacement of Na2O with Se modifies the phosphate network, as evidenced by FTIR analysis, while XRD and SEM confirm that the amorphous nature is preserved. Increasing Se content increases density and reduces molar volume, indicating structural rearrangement of the glass matrix. These changes are accompanied by lower optical band gaps and a higher refractive index, reflecting enhanced electronic polarizability and the formation of localized states. Moreover, substituting low-Z Na2O with higher-Z Se improves photon attenuation, resulting in higher MAC and LAC values and lower HVL, TVL, and MFP. Accordingly, the 30NaSeP glass shows the best overall performance and is a promising candidate for photonic, optical filtering, high-refractive-index, and radiation-shielding applications.

Conclusion

This study investigated the effect of elemental selenium substitution for Na₂O in ternary Na₂O–P₂O₅–Se glasses with the nominal composition (50-x)Na2O-50P2O5-xSe, where x = 0, 0.5, 1, 1.5, 10, 20, and 30 mol%. The glass samples were successfully synthesized using the melt-quenching technique and characterized using different analytical methods. XRD analysis confirmed the amorphous nature of the prepared glasses. At the same time, FTIR spectroscopy revealed modifications in the vibrational modes and structural units of the phosphate glass network with increasing Se content. The physical properties showed that the density increased from 2.350 to 2.600 g/cm− 3, whereas the molar volume decreased from 43.388 to 41.144 cm− 3/mol, indicating Se-induced structural rearrangement in the glass matrix. The packing density decreased from 0.543 to 0.486, while the free volume increased from 19.827 to 21.169 cm3/mol. In addition, the oxygen packing density decreased from 0.691 to 0.656 mol/L, whereas the oxygen molar volume increased from 14.462 to 15.238 cm3/mol. Optical analysis showed that both indirect and direct optical band gaps decreased with increasing Se concentration. The indirect band gap decreased from 3.380 eV for the Se-free glass to 2.890 eV for the 30NaSeP sample, while the direct band gap also decreased from 4.091 eV to 3.750 eV. The radiation shielding results revealed that the mass attenuation coefficient (MAC) and linear attenuation coefficient (LAC) increased with increasing selenium content, mainly due to the replacement of low-Z Na2O by higher-Z Se. At 0.015 MeV, MAC increased from 5.686 to 27.578 cm2.g−1, while LAC increased from 13.362 to 71.702 cm− 1. Conversely, the half-value layer (HVL), the tenth-value layer (TVL), and the mean free path (MFP) decreased with increasing Se concentration, confirming the improved photon attenuation efficiency of the prepared glasses. At 0.2 MeV, HVL decreased from 2.393 to 1.976 cm, TVL decreased from 7.950 to 6.564 cm, and MFP decreased from 3.453 to 2.851 cm. Among all compositions, the 30NaSeP sample exhibited the greatest enhancement in optical and radiation-shielding performance, highlighting its potential for advanced photonic and radiation protection applications.
