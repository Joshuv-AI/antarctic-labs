Postoperative cerebral infarction after major thoracic and abdominopelvic surgery: a retrospective single-centre cohort study of incidence, risk factors, and outcomes
Authors: Xian Zhang, Yan Li, Qiang Li, Quan Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPostoperative cerebral infarction (POCI) is an uncommon but potentially fatal complication of major non-cardiac surgery and is associated with higher in-hospital mortality, intensive care unit (ICU) admission, and subsequent mortality. The contribution of preoperative composite inflammatory and coagulation indices, including the systemic immune-inflammation index (SII), to POCI risk stratification has not been well characterized in Korean thoracic and abdominopelvic surgical cohorts.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study using the INformative Surgical Patient dataset for Innovative Research Environment (INSPIRE). Of 15,225 eligible patients who underwent elective or emergency thoracic or abdominopelvic surgery, 15,138 met inclusion criteria. The primary outcome was confirmed POCI. Independent associations with POCI were assessed by multivariable logistic regression (adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% confidence intervals). Three machine learning classifiers were trained and evaluated by five-fold stratified cross-validation. Survival was compared using a pre-specified fixed-endpoint analysis (1-year all-cause mortality). Kaplan-Meier estimation was provided as supporting description.ResultsPOCI was confirmed in 277 of 15,138 patients (1.83%; 95% CI: 1.63–2.06%). In multivariable logistic regression, the factors independently associated with POCI were advanced age (aOR: 1.65 per 10-year increment; 95% CI: 1.46–1.88), hypertension (aOR: 5.92; 95% CI: 4.56–7.68), atrial fibrillation (aOR: 4.21; 95% CI: 2.60–6.79), chronic kidney disease (aOR: 2.47; 95% CI: 1.56–3.90), and peripheral arterial disease (aOR: 2.45; 95% CI: 1.27–4.72) (all p < 0.01). In unadjusted descriptive comparisons, POCI patients had higher preoperative neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR; median 3.1 vs. 2.5; p < 0.001), systemic immune-inflammation index (SII; 694.4 vs. 577.5; p = 0.006), and fibrinogen-to-albumin ratio (FAR; 85.5 vs. 76.3; p < 0.001). However, none of these indices was independently associated with POCI after adjustment (e.g. NLR aOR: 1.21; 95% CI: 0.93–1.58; p = 0.155). No consistent pattern of greater intra-operative hypotension was observed. In-hospital mortality (5.4% vs. 1.6%; OR: 3.56; 95% CI: 2.10–6.03) and ICU admission (42.6% vs. 22.8%; OR: 2.52; 95% CI: 1.98–3.20) were higher in the POCI group (both p < 0.001). One-year all-cause mortality was 14.8% in the POCI group versus 5.8% in controls (OR: 2.83; 95% CI: 2.02–3.96; p < 0.001), and the Kaplan-Meier log-rank comparison was non-significant (p = 0.25).ConclusionsPOCI following major thoracic and abdominopelvic surgery occurred in 1.83% of this cohort and was associated with substantially higher in-hospital mortality and higher 1-year all-cause mortality. These findings provide a population-specific incidence benchmark and support structured preoperative cardiovascular risk review, rather than reliance on inflammatory indices or machine-learning scores, whose positive predictive values are limited in this low-prevalence setting.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12871-026-04061-x.

Introduction

Approximately 300 million adults undergo non-cardiac surgical procedures each year worldwide, and major perioperative complications occur in an estimated 7–15% of these individuals [1]. Among the spectrum of recognized complications, postoperative cerebral infarction (POCI) occupies a particularly concerning position owing to its high case-fatality rate, the frequency of permanent neurological disability among survivors, and its considerable contribution to prolonged hospital stays and downstream health-care costs [2, 3]. The insidious clinical presentation of postoperative stroke, frequently masked by residual anesthetic effects, analgesic sedation, or the patient’s inability to communicate, compounds the problem by delaying diagnosis and the initiation of evidence-based management.

Estimates of POCI incidence in non-cardiac, non-neurological surgery derived from large national administrative datasets have ranged from 0.1% to 1.9%.(2, 4, 5) The true burden is likely underestimated because covert cerebral infarctions, detectable on diffusion-weighted magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) but clinically silent, occur in approximately 7% of patients undergoing non-cardiac surgery [4]. The NeuroVISION prospective cohort demonstrated that covert stroke is at least 14 times more frequent than overt stroke after non-cardiac surgery, and that its presence independently predicts a composite of stroke, transient ischemic attack, and delirium within 30 days [5]. Overt POCI carries an in-hospital mortality of 5–26% across published series and leaves 25–45% of survivors with moderate or severe disability.

Established risk factors for POCI include older age, hypertension, pre-existing cerebrovascular disease, atrial fibrillation (AF), chronic kidney disease, and elevated ASA physical status [2, 6, 7]. AF is of particular mechanistic interest: new-onset perioperative AF precipitates cardioembolic events, and epidemiological evidence suggests that patients who develop AF in the perioperative setting face a significantly elevated risk of ischemic stroke for at least 12 months following surgery [8, 9]. The 2020 Society for Neuroscience in Anesthesiology and Critical Care (SNACC) guidelines emphasize perioperative hemodynamic stability, bridging anticoagulation decisions, and structured neurological screening as the principal modifiable targets, yet no validated instrument exists for stratifying POCI risk specifically in thoracic or abdominopelvic surgical populations [7].

A growing body of evidence has linked circulating inflammatory and coagulation indices, calculable from routinely collected blood parameters, to incident cerebrovascular events. Elevated neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), reflecting a shift in immune balance towards a pro-inflammatory state, has been associated with stroke severity, hemorrhagic transformation, and adverse functional outcomes in acute ischemic stroke [10, 11]. The fibrinogen-to-albumin ratio (FAR), a composite marker integrating the pro-thrombotic potential of fibrinogen with the anti-inflammatory and oncotic properties of albumin, has been identified as an independent predictor of early neurological deterioration following intravenous thrombolysis and as a prognostic marker in large-artery atherosclerosis stroke [12, 13]. The systemic immune-inflammation index (SII), derived from neutrophil, platelet, and lymphocyte counts, was recently reported to predict perioperative ischemic stroke in older patients undergoing non-cardiac surgery with superior discriminatory performance to NLR or platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR) alone [14]. However, the joint preoperative evaluation of these composite indices within a large surgical cohort remains limited.

The INformative Surgical Patient dataset for Innovative Research Environment (INSPIRE) is a publicly available perioperative research database containing records from approximately 130,000 surgical admissions to Seoul National University Hospital. It includes time-series vital signs, longitudinal laboratory results, medication administration records, and post-discharge outcomes [1]. Its single-center Korean origin and multispecialty surgical scope provide an opportunity to characterize POCI incidence, risk factor profiles, and clinical consequences in a population that has been less represented in existing perioperative-stroke literature [15, 16]. The present study therefore had two primary aims: (i) to determine POCI incidence across four major surgical categories (esophageal, lung, gastrointestinal, and hepatobiliary surgery); and (ii) to identify independent, adjusted associations between preoperative clinical variables and POCI using multivariable logistic regression. The composite inflammatory indices, machine-learning discrimination, perioperative hemodynamic summaries, and fixed-endpoint clinical outcomes were also evaluated in secondary analyses.

Methods

Data source and ethics

This retrospective cohort study used INSPIRE version 1.3 (PhysioNet DOI https://doi.org/10.13026/4hm3-0x86), a publicly available perioperative dataset released by Seoul National University Hospital (SNUH) that extends the originally published 2011–2020 release through December 2021, under Institutional Review Board approval (IRB No. H-2210-078-1368) and Institutional Data Review Board authorization for public release following de-identification [1]. Because INSPIRE is an openly available, fully anonymized secondary dataset, no additional ethical approval or individual informed consent was required for this analysis. INSPIRE encompasses records from approximately 50% of all surgical admissions to SNUH between January 2011 and December 2021, incorporating patient demographics, ASA classification, procedure codes, intraoperative vital signs recorded at one-minute intervals, all laboratory results from six months before admission to six months after discharge, drug administration data, and post-discharge survival outcomes linked to the national death registry.

Study population

The eligible population comprised all adult patients (aged ≥ 18 years) undergoing elective or emergency thoracic or abdominopelvic surgery within the INSPIRE dataset, with a primary procedure classified as esophageal, lung, gastrointestinal, or hepatobiliary surgery. Surgeries designated as “other” were retained only to account for the denominator in the overall incidence estimate and were excluded from comparative surgical-subgroup analyses given diagnostic heterogeneity. Patients were included in the primary analysis cohort according to a pre-specified algorithm that excluded: (a) patients with an ICD-10-coded cerebral infarction diagnosis predating the index surgery or recorded within 48 h of admission, classified as pre-existing or peri-admission (“uncertain”) non-postoperative stroke (n = 87; defined as a cerebral-infarction code recorded before the index surgery or within 48 h of admission, where pre- versus postoperative timing could not be unambiguously established from the available timestamps); and (b) patients with incomplete ascertainment of the primary outcome variable. The final primary analysis cohort comprised 15,138 patients. The complete patient selection algorithm, including ICD-10 code-based filtering, temporal criteria for distinguishing pre-existing from new postoperative stroke, the pre-existing versus uncertain breakdown within the 87 excluded patients, and the resulting cohort sizes at each stage, is illustrated in Supplementary Figure S1.

Definition of postoperative cerebral infarction

The primary outcome, POCI, was defined as a new diagnosis of cerebral infarction (ICD-10 codes I63.0-I63.9) first recorded during or following the index surgical admission, excluding cases where the diagnosis was present on admission or temporally inconsistent with a postoperative event as identified by the INSPIRE curation algorithm. This definition encompasses both clinically overt strokes and those incidentally coded during the admission, but does not capture covert infarctions diagnosed exclusively on imaging without a clinical diagnosis code, consistent with the ICD-based outcome ascertainment used in comparator administrative-dataset studies [2, 6, 16].

Variables and composite indices

Pre-specified exposures included: age (modelled as a continuous term, expressed per 10-year increment, in the multivariable analysis; the ≥ 65-year dichotomy was retained only for descriptive tabulation to avoid the nested, collinear ≥ 65/≥75 categories used previously), sex, ASA physical status (≥ 3 as binary), emergency surgery, and a panel of comorbidities derived from ICD-10 diagnosis codes, including hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease, atrial fibrillation, coronary artery disease, heart failure, and peripheral arterial disease. A composite vascular comorbidity variable (any of the above vascular conditions) was also derived. A previously included “cardioembolic-prone profile” composite (atrial fibrillation, valvular disease, or heart failure) was removed from the analysis because it overlapped with atrial fibrillation and heart failure entered as individual predictors and incorporated a valvular-disease component not tabulated separately. Laboratory values were extracted from the first preoperative measurement within 30 days preceding surgery and the first available postoperative value. Perioperative hemodynamic variables included baseline mean arterial pressure (MAP), last preoperative MAP, end-of-operation MAP, and intraoperative vasopressor exposure.

Four composite inflammatory and coagulation indices were computed from available laboratory data: NLR = absolute neutrophil count ÷ absolute lymphocyte count; SII = neutrophil count × platelet count ÷ lymphocyte count [14]; FAR = fibrinogen (mg/dL) ÷ albumin (g/dL) [12, 13]; and CRP-to-albumin ratio (CAR) = CRP (mg/dL) ÷ albumin (g/dL). Delta (Δ) values for each index were computed as first postoperative minus preoperative value and are reported only as secondary descriptive analyses. Hemoglobin, creatinine, platelet count, fibrinogen, albumin, and CRP were included as individual laboratory variables.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are reported as median (interquartile range, IQR) and compared between groups using two-sided Mann-Whitney U tests. Categorical variables are reported as counts and percentages and compared using two-sided Fisher’s exact test or Pearson χ² test as appropriate. Unadjusted ORs and their 95% confidence intervals for binary exposures were computed from 2 × 2 contingency tables with Haldane-Anscombe 0.5-cell continuity correction applied where any cell contained zero observations; confidence intervals were constructed using the Wilson score method. POCI incidence and 95% confidence intervals for each surgical category were likewise estimated using the Wilson method. All p values are two-sided; p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Three machine-learning classifiers were evaluated as supporting prediction analyses: L2-regularised logistic regression (regularization parameter C = 0.05), random forest (300 decision trees, maximum depth 5), and gradient boosting (200 trees, maximum depth 3). All models were trained and validated using five-fold stratified cross-validation to account for the low POCI prevalence (1.83%). Missing preoperative laboratory values were imputed using feature-level medians, and continuous features were standardized before model fitting. Data completeness is summarized in Supplementary Table S1. Fibrinogen and FAR were excluded from the primary binary odds-ratio analysis and from the primary machine-learning feature matrix because FAR was missing in 27.8% of patients. Model performance was summarized using area under the ROC curve (AUC-ROC), area under the precision-recall curve (AUC-PR), Brier score, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value (Supplementary Figure S2).

Survival time was defined from hospital admission to all-cause death as recorded by national death-registry linkage (maximum observed follow-up 12.1 years). Patients alive at the end of their admission were censored at hospital discharge, reflecting the structure of INSPIRE in which long-duration follow-up data are available only for patients in whom a subsequent death event was registered. Because patients alive at discharge are censored at hospital discharge (median 10 days) while long-duration follow-up is available almost exclusively for patients in whom a registry death was subsequently linked, the censoring in INSPIRE is informative and violates the non-informative-censoring assumption underlying both the Kaplan-Meier estimator and the Cox proportional-hazards model. We therefore pre-specified a fixed-endpoint analysis, 1-year all-cause mortality compared by Fisher’s exact test, as the primary survival comparison. Kaplan-Meier curves are presented in the Supplementary Material for descriptive purposes only, with between-group difference assessed by the log-rank test. The Cox model was not fitted because the same informative-censoring violation would bias the hazard ratio. To identify independent preoperative predictors of POCI, a multivariable logistic regression was fitted incorporating age as a continuous term expressed per 10-year increment, sex, ASA physical status ≥ 3, emergency surgery, individual binary comorbidities, and log-transformed preoperative NLR and SII. Missing laboratory values for modelled variables were imputed by feature-level medians, and continuous predictors were standardised before model fitting. Adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% bootstrap confidence intervals (1,000 resamples) were reported. All analyses were conducted in Python 3.12 using scipy (v1.11), scikit-learn (v1.3), numpy (v1.24), and pandas (v2.0).

Reporting

This study is reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for cohort studies.

Results

Study cohort and overview

Of the 15,225 patients in the INSPIRE database who underwent elective or emergency thoracic or abdominopelvic surgery at Seoul National University Hospital between January 2011 and December 2021, 15,138 met the eligibility criteria for the primary analysis after the exclusion of 87 individuals with pre-existing or diagnostically uncertain non-postoperative cerebral infarction (Fig. 1A). Postoperative cerebral infarction (POCI) was confirmed in 277 of these patients, corresponding to an overall incidence of 1.83% (95% CI: 1.63–2.06%).

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics

Demographic and surgical profile

Complete baseline characteristics for both groups are presented in Table 1. POCI patients were substantially older than controls (median age 70.0 years, IQR 65.0–75.0 vs. 60.0 years, IQR 55.0–70.0; p < 0.001), with 80.9% aged ≥ 65 years and 41.2% aged ≥ 75 years, compared with 46.4% and 17.1% in the control group, respectively. Male sex was more prevalent among POCI patients (71.1% vs. 60.5%; p < 0.001). The POCI group also had a higher proportion of ASA physical status grade ≥ 3 (28.9% vs. 8.7%; p < 0.001), greater median number of co-existing diagnoses (8.0, IQR 5.0–13.0 vs. 3.0, IQR 2.0–5.0; p < 0.001), and a higher rate of emergency procedures (11.6% vs. 7.2%; p = 0.009).

Comorbidity profile

The POCI group demonstrated a markedly higher burden of cardiovascular and cardiometabolic comorbidities across all domains examined. Hypertension was present in 45.1% of POCI patients versus 8.1% of controls (OR 9.36, 95% CI: 7.33–11.94; p < 0.001). Atrial fibrillation was identified in 10.1% of POCI patients compared with 1.0% of controls (OR 11.80, 95% CI: 7.74–17.98; p < 0.001), and chronic kidney disease was present in 9.7% versus 1.9% (OR 5.67, 95% CI: 3.76–8.55; p < 0.001). Coronary artery disease (10.8% vs. 3.2%; OR 3.73, 95% CI: 2.53–5.49), heart failure (2.9% vs. 0.3%; OR 10.74, 95% CI: 5.10-22.63), and peripheral arterial disease (4.7% vs. 0.7%; OR 7.01, 95% CI: 3.93–12.50) were all significantly more prevalent in the POCI group (all p < 0.001). In aggregate, at least one vascular comorbidity was present in 62.1% of POCI patients versus 16.6% of controls (OR 8.23, 95% CI: 6.43–10.52; p < 0.001).

Preoperative and postoperative laboratory values

Conventional biochemical indices

Preoperative and postoperative laboratory values are presented in Table 2. POCI patients had lower preoperative albumin (median 3.7 g/dL, IQR 3.1-4.0 vs. 3.9 g/dL, IQR 3.5–4.1; p < 0.001) and hemoglobin (12.2 g/dL, IQR 10.7–13.7 vs. 12.9 g/dL, IQR 11.6–13.7; p < 0.001), and higher creatinine (0.9 mg/dL, IQR 0.7–1.2 vs. 0.8 mg/dL, IQR 0.7–0.9; p < 0.001) and fibrinogen (327.0 mg/dL, IQR 276.0-376.0 vs. 301.0 mg/dL, IQR 249.0-357.0; p < 0.001). Composite inflammatory and coagulation indices

Among the novel composite indices, the preoperative NLR was higher in the POCI group (median 3.1, IQR 1.9–6.6 vs. 2.5, IQR 1.6–5.2; p < 0.001), as was the systemic immune-inflammation index (SII: 694.4, IQR 380.7–1414.0 vs. 577.5, IQR 338.2–1129.0; p = 0.006). The fibrinogen-to-albumin ratio (FAR) was also elevated preoperatively in the POCI group (85.5, IQR 71.5-116.8 vs. 76.3, IQR 64.1–96.4; p < 0.001), although this comparison is descriptive. By contrast, the CRP-to-albumin ratio (CAR) did not differ significantly between groups (median 0.3 vs. 0.4; p = 0.743). These distribution comparisons are illustrated in Fig. 2.

Perioperative hemodynamic parameters

Perioperative hemodynamic summaries are presented in Table 3; Fig. 3. Baseline MAP, end-of-operation MAP, and relative MAP decrease were similar between groups (all p > 0.50). Vasopressor use was less frequent in the POCI group (30.0% vs. 36.6%; p = 0.023), but this secondary observation is likely confounded by surgical profile, anesthetic approach, and case complexity. Overall, the available hemodynamic summaries did not show a consistent pattern of greater hypotension among patients who developed POCI.

Univariate risk-factor analysis

Unadjusted ORs for all pre-specified binary risk factors are displayed as a forest plot in Fig. 4 and tabulated in Table 4. Among the comorbidities examined, atrial fibrillation carried the largest effect estimate (OR 11.80; 95% CI: 7.74–17.98), followed by heart failure (OR 10.74; 95% CI: 5.10-22.63), hypertension (OR 9.36; 95% CI: 7.33–11.94), peripheral arterial disease (OR 7.01; 95% CI: 3.93–12.50), and chronic kidney disease (OR 5.67; 95% CI: 3.76–8.55). Among demographic and surgical variables, age ≥ 65 years yielded an OR of 4.84 (95% CI: 3.58–6.53; p < 0.001), ASA grade ≥ 3 an OR of 4.30 (95% CI: 3.30–5.60; p < 0.001), and emergency surgery an OR of 1.71 (95% CI: 1.18–2.48; p = 0.009). Unadjusted odds ratios estimated in the complete-case cohort (patients with non-missing values across all machine-learning predictor variables; n = 10,694; POCI = 213) were consistent in direction and magnitude with those from the full imputed cohort (Supplementary Table S2).

Multivariable risk-factor analysis

In multivariable logistic regression mutually adjusting for all pre-specified comorbidities, age (per 10-year increment), sex, ASA physical status ≥ 3, emergency surgery, and log-transformed preoperative NLR and SII (n = 15,138), six variables were independently associated with POCI (Table 4). For specific estimates, they were hypertension (aOR 5.92; 95% CI: 4.56–7.68; p < 0.001), atrial fibrillation (aOR 4.21; 95% CI: 2.60–6.79; p < 0.001), chronic kidney disease (aOR 2.47; 95% CI: 1.56–3.90; p < 0.001), peripheral arterial disease (aOR 2.45; 95% CI: 1.27–4.72; p = 0.008), age (aOR 1.65 per 10-year increment; 95% CI: 1.46–1.88; p < 0.001), and emergency surgery (aOR 1.57; 95% CI: 1.04–2.37; p = 0.030). Coronary artery disease (aOR 1.27; 95% CI: 0.82–1.97; p = 0.284), heart failure (aOR 1.29; 95% CI: 0.50–3.32; p = 0.591), and diabetes mellitus (aOR 1.21; 95% CI: 0.80–1.84; p = 0.369) were not independently associated with POCI after adjustment. Neither log-transformed NLR (aOR 1.21 per SD; 95% CI: 0.93–1.58; p = 0.155) nor SII (aOR 0.94 per SD; 95% CI: 0.71–1.23; p = 0.641) was independently associated with POCI after adjustment for the comorbidity profile. Variance-inflation factors for all predictors were below 5 (Supplementary Table S3), indicating acceptable collinearity.

Machine learning-based prediction of POCI

The machine-learning analysis is summarized in Fig. 5. L2-regularised logistic regression achieved the highest AUC-ROC (0.819), followed by random forest (0.806) and gradient boosting (0.801) (Fig. 5A). However, precision-recall performance was limited: the AUC-PR values were 0.118, 0.081, and 0.089 for logistic regression, random forest, and gradient boosting, respectively, against a baseline event prevalence of 1.83%. The low positive predictive values (6.0-16.7%) indicate limited screening utility despite acceptable AUC-ROC values.

Clinical outcomes

Clinical outcomes are detailed in Table 5 and illustrated in Fig. 6. In-hospital mortality was 5.4% in the POCI group versus 1.6% in controls (OR: 3.56, 95% CI: 2.10–6.03; p < 0.001). ICU admission rates were 42.6% versus 22.8% (OR: 2.52, 95% CI: 1.98–3.20; p < 0.001), and a prolonged hospital stay exceeding 14 days was recorded in 39.0% of POCI patients compared with 24.7% of controls (OR: 1.95, 95% CI: 1.53–2.48; p < 0.001). Hospital LOS was longer in the POCI group (median 12.0 days, IQR 9.0–22.0 vs. 10.0 days, IQR 8.0–13.0; p < 0.001). Among patients admitted to the ICU, ICU LOS was also greater in the POCI group (median 1.1 days, IQR 0.9–3.2 vs. 1.0 days, IQR 0.8–1.9; p = 0.005). All-cause mortality recorded during the observation period was 49.1% in the POCI group and 26.4% in controls (OR: 2.68, 95% CI: 2.12–3.41; p < 0.001). One-year all-cause mortality, the pre-specified survival endpoint (Sect. 3.10), was 14.8% in the POCI group versus 5.8% in controls (OR: 2.83; 95% CI: 2.02–3.96; p < 0.001).

One-year mortality and survival analysis

In view of the informative censoring structure of INSPIRE, the primary survival comparison was a pre-specified fixed-endpoint analysis. One-year all-cause mortality was 14.8% in the POCI group (41 of 277) versus 5.8% in controls (868 of 14,861), corresponding to an OR of 2.83 (95% CI: 2.02–3.96; p < 0.001). Thirty-day mortality was too infrequent in this predominantly elective cohort to support a stable comparison (0% vs. 0.3%), so the 1-year endpoint was adopted as the primary survival measure. For descriptive purposes, Kaplan-Meier curves and the corresponding log-rank test are provided in Supplementary Figure S5.

Supporting perioperative biomarker analyses

Pre-to-postoperative trajectories for six key biomarkers are illustrated in Supplementary Figure S6. Both groups exhibited the expected postoperative changes, including reductions in albumin and hemoglobin and increases in creatinine. None of the evaluated delta values reached statistical significance. Accordingly, perioperative biomarker changes were treated as secondary exploratory observations and were not considered central results.

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort of 15,138 patients who underwent major thoracic or abdominopelvic surgery at a large academic center in South Korea, POCI occurred in 277 individuals, yielding an overall incidence of 1.83% (95% CI: 1.63–2.06%). This estimate is at the upper end of the 0.1–1.9% range reported in studies of non-cardiac, non-neurological surgery based on Western administrative databases [2, 6, 17]. But it is consistent with the higher rates observed in series restricted to thoracic and abdominal procedures and in studies reporting outcomes from older cohorts with higher vascular comorbidity burdens [7, 16]. Several factors may account for this distribution in the present data: the mean age of the POCI group was 70 years and 80.9% were aged ≥ 65 years, consistent with the strong age-dependency of perioperative stroke risk. The INSPIRE cohort also has a higher proportion of oncological and upper gastrointestinal resections than many general surgical registries, which contributes a patient profile with elevated pre-existing cardiovascular risk and physiological reserve limitations.

The strongest unadjusted comorbidity signal for POCI was AF. New-onset AF in the peri-operative context is of particular concern. Prior registry data indicate that patients who develop AF during a non-cardiac surgical admission face approximately a threefold increase in stroke risk over the subsequent 12 months compared with patients in sinus rhythm [8], and that the risk of thromboembolism attributable to peri-operative AF may persist even after the arrhythmia has resolved [9]. The high prevalence of pre-existing AF (10.1%) in the POCI group, more than ten times that in controls, underscores the need for structured preoperative cardiovascular review and individualized perioperative anticoagulation planning in patients undergoing major abdominal and thoracic procedures, consistent with the 2020 SNACC guidance for patients at high risk of perioperative stroke [7] and the 2024 multisociety guideline for perioperative cardiovascular management before non-cardiac surgery [18].

Multivariable adjustment provided an important qualification to the univariate comorbidity findings [18]. After mutual adjustment for age and other covariates, hypertension (aOR 5.92), atrial fibrillation (aOR 4.21), chronic kidney disease (aOR 2.47), peripheral arterial disease (aOR 2.45), advancing age (aOR 1.65 per decade), and emergency surgery (aOR 1.57) remained independently associated with POCI, whereas coronary artery disease, heart failure, and diabetes attenuated to non-significance. Advancing age, hypertension, and atrial fibrillation are the most readily identifiable preoperative flags, while chronic kidney disease and peripheral arterial disease identify a broader vascular-risk phenotype [19]. Because these estimates derive from an observational design, they should be interpreted as associations rather than causal effects. NLR, SII, and FAR were higher in POCI patients than controls in unadjusted comparisons. However, neither NLR nor SII was independently associated with POCI after adjustment, and FAR was excluded from adjusted models because it was missing in 27.8% of patients. These indices therefore did not add demonstrable predictive value beyond established risk factors in this cohort and should be interpreted cautiously [10–14]. Prospective validation with complete laboratory ascertainment would be required before these indices could be recommended for perioperative POCI risk stratification.

The hemodynamic findings were also secondary and should be interpreted cautiously. Baseline MAP, end-of-operation MAP, relative MAP decrease, and vasopressor exposure did not show a consistent pattern of greater hypotension in POCI patients. The lower observed vasopressor use in the POCI group (30.0% vs. 36.6%) is most plausibly explained by confounding from surgical profile, anesthetic approach, operation duration, and case complexity rather than by a protective effect. Because the analysis was limited to coarse MAP summaries and vasopressor exposure, it cannot exclude associations involving cumulative time or area under MAP thresholds, postoperative blood-pressure variability, or cerebral perfusion surrogates.

The machine-learning analysis was retained as a compact supporting analysis. Although L2-regularised logistic regression achieved an AUC-ROC of 0.819, the AUC-PR was only 0.118 and the positive predictive value remained low. Tree-based models did not improve practical utility; in particular, gradient boosting produced near-complete specificity at the expense of clinically unacceptable sensitivity. These results indicate that machine-learning scores should not be used as standalone screening tools in this low-prevalence setting. The adjusted clinical model and the vascular comorbidity-count gradient provide a simpler and more interpretable approach for focusing postoperative neurological vigilance on patients with higher baseline vascular risk.

The surgical category analysis revealed meaningful heterogeneity across the four predefined surgical groups. Hepatobiliary surgery carried the lowest POCI rate (0.71%; OR 0.36 relative to the rest of the cohort), whereas lung and gastrointestinal procedures had numerically higher rates (1.91% and 1.98%, respectively) without statistically significant deviation from the overall cohort incidence. The low POCI rate in hepatobiliary surgery may reflect patient selection, lower baseline vascular comorbidity burden among elective hepatobiliary candidates at this tertiary center, and procedure-specific differences in perioperative physiology. Esophageal procedures, despite being among the most physiologically demanding operations in the cohort, had an intermediate POCI incidence (1.39%), possibly reflecting the comparatively lower vascular comorbidity burden in patients undergoing oncological esophagectomy at this center, although unmeasured confounders including surgical approach and tumor-related pro-coagulant states cannot be excluded.

POCI was also associated with worse short- and longer-term outcomes. In-hospital mortality, ICU admission, prolonged hospital stay, and all-cause mortality recorded during the observation period were all more frequent in the POCI group. One-year all-cause mortality was significantly higher in the POCI group (14.8% vs. 5.8%; OR 2.83; p < 0.001). The Kaplan-Meier log-rank comparison, by contrast, was non-significant (p = 0.25) because of informative censoring. We therefore base the survival conclusion on the fixed-endpoint analysis rather than on the survival curves. The excess 1-year mortality is consistent with POCI being a marker of severe systemic vascular disease and perioperative vulnerability, although the observational design precludes causal inference. It should be acknowledged that the censoring structure of INSPIRE, in which patients alive at discharge are censored at the time of hospital discharge rather than at a formal administrative censoring date, limits interpretation of the Kaplan-Meier curves as population-level actuarial estimates.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, POCI ascertainment was based on ICD-10 diagnostic coding rather than neuroimaging adjudication, which may have led to under-coding of mild events and occasional misclassification of pre-existing silent infarcts. Second, the observational design precludes causal interpretation, and residual confounding by unmeasured variables such as preoperative anticoagulation status, antiplatelet therapy, anesthetic technique, intraoperative blood loss, and surgical approach remains possible. Third, INSPIRE is a single-centre tertiary-hospital dataset from South Korea, which limits generalizability to community hospitals and to broader Korean, Asian, or non-Asian surgical populations. Fourth, informative censoring precludes definitive long-term actuarial survival estimation. Therefore, the fixed 1-year endpoint is more interpretable than the supplementary Kaplan-Meier curves. Fifth, covert cerebral infarctions were not captured because brain MRI data were unavailable. Sixth, the machine-learning models lacked external validation and had limited screening utility because of severe class imbalance, low AUC-PR, and low positive predictive value. Seventh, fibrinogen and FAR were missing in more than 20% of patients, limiting inference for fibrinogen-derived indices. Finally, inflammatory indices, perioperative hemodynamics, and machine-learning were secondary supporting analyses around the primary aims of incidence estimation and adjusted risk-factor identification.

Conclusions

POCI following major thoracic and abdominopelvic surgery occurred at an overall rate of 1.83% in this single-center cohort and was associated with higher in-hospital mortality, more frequent ICU admission, and higher 1-year all-cause mortality. After multivariable adjustment, advancing age, hypertension, atrial fibrillation, chronic kidney disease, peripheral arterial disease, and emergency surgery were independently associated with POCI, with age, hypertension, and atrial fibrillation the most clinically tractable preoperative flags. The composite inflammatory indices (NLR, SII, FAR) differed between groups in unadjusted comparisons but were not independently associated with POCI after adjustment. Likewise, although a logistic-regression model reached an AUC-ROC of 0.819, its low positive predictive value in this low-prevalence setting limits its standalone screening utility. These results provide a population-specific incidence benchmark and support structured preoperative cardiovascular risk review, with prospective external validation required before any composite-index or machine-learning tool could be recommended for clinical use.

Supplementary Material 5: Supplementary Figure S5. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis (descriptive). (A) Kaplan-Meier overall survival curves for the POCI (gold, n = 277) and control (teal, n = 14,861) groups. Survival time is defined as the interval from hospital admission to all-cause death (national death-registry linkage), expressed in months. Patients alive at last observation are censored at hospital discharge. Shaded bands represent 95% confidence intervals derived from the Nelson-Aalen cumulative hazard estimator. Tick marks indicate censored observations. The at-risk table beneath the plot shows the number of patients with follow-up time exceeding each threshold. The annotated p value is from the log-rank test. Owing to the censoring structure of INSPIRE, the curves are descriptive and should not be interpreted as population-level actuarial survival estimates. (B) Kaplan-Meier survival curves stratified by surgical category within the POCI subgroup.

Supplementary Material 6: Supplementary Figure S6. Perioperative biomarker trajectories. Line graphs (mean ± standard error) illustrating the change from the preoperative to the first postoperative measurement for six biomarkers, albumin, CRP, fibrinogen, hemoglobin, creatinine, and NLR, in a random subsample of controls (teal, up to n = 800) and all POCI patients (gold, n = 277). CRP and NLR are displayed on a log(1 + x) scale. Annotated p values in each panel correspond to the between-group comparison of the delta value (postoperative minus preoperative) by Mann-Whitney U test. CRP, C-reactive protein; NLR, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio.

Supplementary Material 7: Supplementary Figure S7. C-reactive-protein-to-albumin ratio (CAR) dynamics and ΔCAR-ΔNLR relationship. (A, B) Violin plots overlaid with box-and-whisker plots comparing preoperative (A) and postoperative (B) CAR distributions between control (teal) and POCI (gold) patients, displayed on a log(1 + x) scale. Significance brackets are from Mann-Whitney U tests; annotated p values correspond to these comparisons. (C) Scatter plot of log(1 + ΔNLR) against log(1 + ΔCAR) for a random subsample of controls (teal, up to n = 600) and all POCI patients (gold), where Δ denotes the postoperative minus preoperative change. CAR, CRP-to-albumin ratio; NLR, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio.
