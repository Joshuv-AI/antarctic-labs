Looking Back at Key Events |

The Breakdown of the Yamoussoukro Peace Process

National Patriotic Front of Liberia (NPFL) leader Taylor--whom most observers blame
for the failure of the old Yamoussoukro peace process--appears convinced that he was
justified in not implementing that accord because other signatories broke their
commitments. The accord, signed in late 1991 at Yamoussoukro, Ivory Coast by Taylor,
a‘host of regional leaders, and the Secretaries General of the OAU and the Economic
Community of West African States (ECOWAS), called for a cease-fire, disarmament of
the I‘iberian combatants, and elections, all within a six-month timeframe.

around the same time, Taylor arranged to sell off excess weapons to

finance a presidential campaign, suggesting that at least initially he planned to implement
the accord in the hope of gaining power through the electoral process.

indicates, however, that his willingness to follow through waned as he became

increasingly suspicious of the motives of ECOMOG, which was charged with overseeing
the accord.

The deployment and role of Senegalese troops in ECOMOG became a particular point of
contention. Taylor believed--with some justification,

-that he had agreed to a deal whereby Senegal's

troop deployment would be matched by a Nigerian drawdown.
Taylor was angry when he realized the Senegalese deployment was an
augmentation of the ECOMOG force.

Taylor's doubts about ECOMOG's impartiality as a peacekeeper intensified when the
Senegalese ECOMOG troops, deployed along the border with Sierra Leone as a buffer
force, failed to block an attack against the NPFL in July 1992 by ULIMO, a rival
Liberian rebel group that was then based in Sierra Leone. During the same period,
Taylor discovered that ECOMOG was providing logistic assistance and weapons to all
the main anti-Taylor Liberian factions, including ULIMO, remnants of former President
Doe's Army (AFL), and military elements attached to the Interim Government in
Monrovia.

The Resumption of Hostilities

While most observers point to Taylor's assault on Monrovia on 15 October 1992 as the
beginning of renewed hostilities after the failed Yamoussoukro peace process, Taylor
believes ULIMO--backed by ECOMOG--violated the cease-fire first when it captured

most of western Liberia from NPFL forces in mid-August.

Taylor--already bitter over ECOMOG's failure to block ULIMO's march into ©
iberia--viewed the regional force's artillery support for ULIMO fighters battling the
NPEL on the outskirts of the capital in early September as confirmation that he had little
choice but to pursue a military option. In addition, Taylor saw ECOMOG's enforcement
of a naval blockade against his territory and Nigerian ECOMOG Commander Olurin's
decision to implement a 45-mile defensive perimeter around Monrovia as a virtual
declaration of war, | Finally, Taylor claimed that his

move to detain and disarm ECOMOG soldiers in September 1992--the key event that led .

Nigeria to oppose Taylor directly--was designed to preempt ECOMOG's expected move
against him.

The Yamoussoukro Agreement...

The Yamoussoukro agreement specified the following conditions as prerequisites for
peace:

-- All parties should be willing to encamp and disarm.
-- The neutrality of ECOMOG should be recognized by all.

-- BCOMOG should secure all entry points into Liberia.

{
The agreement also set forth as actions items the following tasks:

-- Elimination of unspecified “external threats" to allow encampment and
disarmament.

-- The securing of key ports and border crossings into Liberia by patrols and
static guards.

-- Establishment of guard forces for key installations.

-- Efforts to recover
hidden weapons, but only if reasonable cause was demonstrated. The “reasonable
cause" caveat came at Taylor's insistence; he feared arbitrary security sweeps.

-- Provision of security for faction leaders.

-- Initiation of the encampment and disarmament phase on 15 November 1991, to be
completed by 14 January 1992, followed by elections in mid-1992.

...and the Cotonou Accord

The peace accord signed in Cotonou, Benin in late July affirms the Yamoussoukro
agreement as the basis for a peace plan and set forth separate military and political
agendas. The military accord called for:

-- A cease-fire in place.

-- Monitoring by a 250-man UN monitoring force.

-- An augmentation of the current ECOMOG force with four battalions of additional
African troops from countries outside the West African region, with disarmament to
take place only after the new troops arrive.

-- Disarmament and encampment of fighters as specified by the Yamoussoukro
agreement.

-- Patrolling of borders and entry points by ECOMOG.

The political part of the accord calls for:

-- A single transition government with three branches, executive, legislative, and
judicial.

-- A five-man transition council with a mandate limited to managing day to day
business and arranging elections; the council is to include one representative
from each of the warring factions and two nonpartisan “eminent Liberians."

-- Presidential and legislative elections to be held seven months from the signing of
the agreement, in February 1994. Officials that serve in the transition government
will be ineligible to contest the elections.

-- A major UN role in monitoring the election.

Taylor's Assessment of Key Actors
Nigeria and ECOMOG: Enemy Number One

Taylor thus views ECOMOG as a protagonist in the Liberian conflict, not a peacekeeper,
and he sees Nigeria as his principal enemy. he has cited
ECOMOG's bombing of civilian targets behind rebel lines, support for other armed
factions, and blocking of humanitarian assistance from Taylor-held territory as evidence
of ECOMOG's partisanship. Taylor claims that the bombings of the Firestone Hospital
and the museum at Tubman Farm, in particular, indicate ECOMOG's willingness to
destroy Liberia to gain a military victory. Moreover, Taylor is bitter over what he sees as
ECOMOG's selective adherence to UN mandates--enforcing UN sanctions against NPFL
territory at the cost of great hardship for civilians as well as NPFL fighters in Taylorland,
while continuing to arm and supply anti-Taylor factions in defiance of a UN Security
Council resolution. As a result, he is adamant that the current Nigerian-dominated
ECOMOG is unfit to serve as a peacekeeping force and is insisting on a major
reconfiguration of ECOMOG with outside forces to monitor and implement the latest
peace accord.

Taylor rejects Nigerian President Babangida's claims that he is bankrolling ECOMOG to
bring democratic transition to Liberia and is deeply suspicious of Babangida's intentions.
He contends that Babangida is primarily concerned with personal financial investments
in Liberia and might be more amenable to a negotiated solution if he were assured his
property would be protected, 1 As of June, Taylor
reportedly planned to meet with the Nigerian leader and to reassure him that the NPFL
posed no threat to his personal interests, but ongoing turmoil in Nigeria resulting from
the annulment of the presidential election in June apparently has convinced Taylor to
back away from personal contact with Babangida.

Taylor apparently also believes, however, that the Nigerian authorities are constantly
plotting against him; he suspects that Lagos, in addition
to supporting ULIMO and the AFL, is training a force of anti-Taylor Liberians under
former rival rebel leader Prince Johnson--a claim we have no evidence to support. His
security fears were no doubt reinforced by ECOMOG's apparent attempt--

--to bomb his convoy as he transited Ivory Coast in early June enroute

to a UN-sponsored meeting in Ouagadougou. More recently, Taylor's concern with his
security led him to refuse an invitation to attend the signing ceremony in Benin for the
new peace accord, which may create problems if Taylor interprets ambiguous portions of
the accords differently than the other signatories.

Liberian Factions: The Pecking Order

Despite his recent military setbacks, Taylor still appears convinced--correctly, we
believe--that his is the largest, most capable Liberian fighting force.2 He has publicly
called both ULIMO and the AFL ineffectual, and has cited as evidence the looting,
undisciplined behavior, and ethnic favoritism that characterizes both groups. He believes

that both ULIMO and the AFL are totally dependent on ECOMOG logistic support, and
that the Liberian Interim Government (IGNU) is under ECOMOG control,

. He has maintained that the conflict ultimately would have to be

resolved by Liberians, and apparently approves of the new peace accord because it was
negotiated by Liberians and its provisions will lead to a decline in Nigerian influence.

Key Goals and Objectives — (b)(3)
Gaining Power

Taylor remains committed to gaining the presidency, but flexible on tactics for achieving
this goal. he recognizes that recent battlefield reverses at
ECOMOG's hands and the cumulative effects of ECOMOG-led sanctions have hurt his .
political standing. Taylor, however, views his drop in popularity as only temporary and
expects to use the peace process outlined in the Cotonou accord to rebuild his political
base to ensure that he wins the election.3 He appears convinced that he has the ability to

unify opposing factions in Liberia and cites as proof his previous success in
reincorporating Prince Johnson's splinter INPFL rebel group into the NPFL.

he maintains contacts with a wide variety of Liberians;

in late April and early May, for example, he was engaged in secret negotiations with
ULIMO and IGNU officials, although the particulars are unclear.

Taylor also remains bent on keeping his military options open in case he loses ground on
the political front. | Taylor was surprised by Nigeria's

however, even when suffering tactical defeats at ECOMOG's hands, that the regional

Taking Advantage of the Cease-fire

Taylor welcomes the cease-fire as a chance to regain the political initiative, improve
economic conditions in his area of control, and rest his battle-weary troops.
the rebel leader will insist that sanctions against his territory be

removed now that a cease-fire is in effect because he desperately needs the revenues
generated by timber and other exports to run his political campaign.

Taylor prob has stockpiled $30 million worth of timber to be sold
‘as soon as Sanctions are lifted. suggest that he hopes also to
improve the quality of life in rebel-held territory--and his own popularity--by increasing
commerce and humanitarian aid flows. Finally, he reportedly is planning to use the
cease-fire to rest and resupply his troops to preserve his military option.

Dilution of Nigeria's Influence

One of Taylor's priorities has been to see the forces of his Nigerian foes removed from
Liberia or their numbers and influence there diminished.
that he would have preferred to replace the Nigerians in ECOMOG with non-African
troops, whom he views as intrinsically less partisan, but was pleased with the
compromise in the peace accord that called for the addition to ECOMOG of 3,000-4,000
new troops from non-West African countries, in effect diluting the Nigerian presence.
Taylor, however, is likely to refuse to disarm to any Nigerian troops, and probably will
demand that the 10,000 Nigerian troops be restricted to the Monrovia area, a stance that
may complicate implementation of the accord.

Implications for the Peace Process

Taylor, in the near

term, is likely to cooperate in implementing the peace plan because he believes he still
holds a winning hand and that the peace process now offers his best bet for gaining the
presidency. Despite some significant concessions in the accord--especially his agreement
to dissolve the administration he has set up in "Taylorland" once a new transition
government is in place--the rebel leader almost certainly believes he is better placed than
