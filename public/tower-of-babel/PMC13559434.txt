Prognostic role of triglyceride-glucose index and triglyceride-glucose body mass index in patients undergoing coronary artery bypass grafting: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Dan Chen, Xinqi Wei, Huimin Sun, Jie Meng, Yan Huang, Xiaolan Lu, Yang Gao, Zhiwei Xu, Yan Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe prognostic role of triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index and triglyceride-glucose body mass index (TyG-BMI) in patients undergoing coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) remains unclear. This meta-analysis aimed to clarify prognostic value of TyG index and TyG-BMI among patients undergoing CABG.MethodsSeveral databases were searched up to May 20, 2026. Primary prognostic outcomes included major adverse cardiovascular event (MACE) and major adverse cardiovascular and cerebrovascular event (MACCE). Secondary outcomes included all-cause mortality, cardiac mortality, nonfatal myocardial infarction (MI), nonfatal stroke, acute kidney injury (AKI), atrial fibrillation (AF), symptomatic graft failure (SGF) and cerebral infarction.ResultsFifteen studies with 16,185 cases were enrolled. The results demonstrated that elevated TyG index was related to increased risk of MACE [odds ratio (OR) =1.91, P<0.001] and MACCE (OR =1.67, P<0.001). Subgroup analysis indicated more significant association between TyG index and risk of MACE (OR =4.27, P=0.001) and MACCE (OR =1.71, P<0.001) among diabetic patients. Besides, TyG index was associated with the risk of all-cause mortality [hazard ratio (HR) =1.37, P=0.02], cardiac mortality (HR =2.59, P=0.001), nonfatal MI (OR =2.02, P=0.04), nonfatal stroke (OR =2.00, P<0.001), AF (OR =1.55, P<0.001), SGF (OR =2.987, P=0.003) and cerebral infarction (OR =1.93, P<0.001). Furthermore, elevated TyG-BMI was related to increased incidence of AKI (OR =1.03, P<0.001) among diabetic patients.ConclusionsPreoperative elevated TyG index was associated with worse clinical outcomes such as increased incidence of MACE and MACCE in patients undergoing CABG, especially among diabetes mellitus (DM) patients. Besides, higher TyG-BMI index indicated increased risk of AKI in diabetic patients.

Highlight box

Key findings

• Elevated preoperative triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index was significantly associated with increased risks of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs), major adverse cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events (MACCE), all-cause mortality, cardiac mortality, nonfatal myocardial infarction, nonfatal stroke, atrial fibrillation, symptomatic graft failure, and cerebral infarction in patients undergoing coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG).

• The associations between TyG index and adverse outcomes were more pronounced among patients with diabetes mellitus.

• Limited evidence from a single study suggested a possible association between a higher TyG-body mass index index and postoperative acute kidney injury in diabetic patients.

What is known and what is new?

• The TyG index is a simple and reliable surrogate marker of insulin resistance and has been associated with adverse cardiovascular outcomes in various populations. However, its prognostic value in patients undergoing CABG has remained uncertain, and no previous meta-analysis has comprehensively synthesized the available evidence.

• This study is the first meta-analysis to evaluate the prognostic significance of TyG-related indices in CABG patients, demonstrating consistent associations between elevated preoperative TyG index and multiple adverse postoperative outcomes.

What are the implications, and what should change now?

• Preoperative TyG index may serve as an inexpensive and readily available biomarker for identifying CABG patients at increased metabolic and cardiovascular risk.

• Patients with elevated TyG index, particularly those with diabetes, may warrant closer perioperative monitoring and optimization of metabolic risk factors.

• Large prospective studies are needed to validate these findings and determine whether TyG-guided risk assessment can improve postoperative outcomes.

Introduction

Coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) remains the gold standard revascularization strategy for patients with complex coronary artery disease (CAD), particularly those with left main coronary artery stenosis ≥50%, three-vessel disease, diabetes mellitus (DM), or impaired left ventricular function, for whom current European and American guidelines assign a Class I recommendation for CABG over percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) in appropriate clinical settings (1,2). Worldwide, more than 800,000 CABG procedures are performed annually, making it one of the most frequently conducted cardiac surgeries (3). Despite continuous improvements in surgical techniques, anesthesia, and perioperative management, adverse outcomes remain substantial. Contemporary registries report a 30-day mortality rate of approximately 1–3% for isolated CABG, increasing markedly in high-risk populations (4). Long-term follow-up data from the SYNTAXES study demonstrated that 5-year all-cause mortality reached 9.2% after CABG in patients with three-vessel or left main disease, and major adverse cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events (MACCE) occurred in more than 20% of patients (5). Risk stratification tools such as the EuroSCORE II and the Society of Thoracic Surgeons (STS) score are widely used to estimate perioperative mortality (6,7); however, these models mainly predict short-term surgical mortality and rely heavily on demographic and procedural variables. They incompletely account for metabolic disturbances, insulin resistance (IR), and residual cardiovascular risk, which are increasingly recognized as important determinants of long-term outcomes after revascularization. Consequently, accurate prediction of postoperative clinical outcomes, particularly long-term adverse cardiovascular events, remains challenging, highlighting the need for simple and effective biomarkers to refine risk stratification in patients undergoing CABG.

The triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index, calculated as ln [fasting triglycerides (mg/dL) × fasting plasma glucose (mg/dL)/2], has emerged as a reliable and practical surrogate marker of IR, demonstrating strong correlation with the hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp test, the reference standard for IR assessment (8). Growing evidence indicates that elevated TyG index is independently associated with incident cardiovascular disease, subclinical atherosclerosis, and cardiovascular mortality. In some specific cohorts, higher TyG index was related to increased risk of cardiovascular events and all-cause mortality (9,10). A meta-analysis including 41 studies further demonstrated that individuals in the highest TyG category had higher risk of cardiovascular events [odds ratio (OR) =1.94] compared with those in the lowest category (11). The triglyceride-glucose body mass index (TyG-BMI), calculated as TyG × body mass index (BMI), integrates adiposity into the assessment of IR and has shown enhanced predictive performance for metabolic syndrome, type 2 diabetes, and heart failure (12). Given the central role of IR in endothelial dysfunction, inflammation, graft atherosclerosis, and atrial remodeling, TyG-related indices may have particular relevance in CABG patients. However, although recent observational studies have explored the association between TyG index and adverse outcomes after CABG, their results are inconsistent, and the prognostic significance of TyG-BMI remains largely unclear in this surgical population. Therefore, we conducted the present meta-analysis to comprehensively evaluate the prognostic value of preoperative TyG index and TyG-BMI index in patients undergoing CABG. We present this article in accordance with the PRISMA reporting checklist (13) (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-1498/rc).

Methods

Literature search

PubMed, Web of Science and Chinese National Knowledge Infrastructure databases were searched up to May 20, 2026 with following terms: triglyceride-glucose index, TyG index, triglyceride glucose-body mass index, TyG-BMI index, coronary artery bypass grafting and CABG. Specific search strategy in PubMed was as follows: ((((triglyceride-glucose index) OR (TyG index)) OR (triglyceride glucose-body mass index)) OR (TyG-BMI index)) AND ((coronary artery bypass grafting) OR (CABG)) (Appendix 1). Twenty-four, 26 and 27 records were retrieved from the PubMed, Web of Science and CNKI databases, respectively.

Study selection process

Studies meeting these criteria were included: (I) studies investigated the prognostic role of TyG index or (and) TyG-BMI index among patients undergoing CABG; (II) TyG index was calculated with the formula: TyG = ln (triglycerides (mg/dL) × glucose (mg/dL)/2) (14) and TyG-BMI index was calculated with the formula: TyG-BMI = TyG × BMI (12); (III) data about the clinical outcomes were provided; (IV) available full texts; (V) studies published in English or Chinese.

Studies meeting these criteria were excluded: (I) conference abstracts, case reports, letters, editorials, and animal trials; (II) insufficient, overlapped or duplicated data.

All retrieved records were imported into EndNote (Version 21.0, Clarivate Analytics) for duplicate removal. The titles, abstracts, and full texts were then independently screened by two reviewers according to the predefined eligibility criteria.

Data collection

We collected these data from the studies: study ID (including the first author and year), country, sample size, observational indicator including the TyG index and TyG-BMI index, cutoff value of indicators, combination of DM, endpoint, source of OR and hazard ratio (HR), OR, HR and 95% confidence interval (CI). All TyG-related indices included in this meta-analysis were calculated based on preoperative measurements as reported in the original studies. When both unadjusted and multivariable-adjusted effect estimates were available, the multivariable-adjusted estimates were preferentially extracted for the pooled analyses.

In our meta-analysis, primary prognostic outcomes included major adverse cardiovascular event (MACE) and MACCE. Secondary outcomes included all-cause mortality, cardiac mortality, nonfatal myocardial infarction (MI), nonfatal stroke, acute kidney injury (AKI), atrial fibrillation (AF), symptomatic graft failure (SGF) and cerebral infarction.

MACE generally referred to a composite of MACEs, including outcomes such as all-cause mortality, MI, repeat revascularization, or cardiac death, whereas MACCE additionally included cerebrovascular events such as stroke. Because the exact definitions varied slightly across the included studies, outcome classification followed the definitions reported in the original studies (15,16).

Quality assessment

Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) was used to evaluate the quality and studies with NOS scores ≥6 were regarded as high-quality studies (17).

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted through STATA version 15.0 software. Heterogeneity between studies was evaluated by Q test and I2 statistic. When significant heterogeneity was observed, represented as I2>50% and/or P<0.10, the random effects model was applied; otherwise, the fixed effects model was applied (18). ORs with 95% CIs were combined to evaluate the association of TyG index and TyG-BMI index with incidence of MACE, MACCE, nonfatal MI, nonfatal stroke, AKI, AF, SGF and cerebral infarction. HRs with 95% CIs were combined to assess the association of TyG index with all-cause mortality and cardiac mortality. Subgroup analysis for the MACE and MACCE based on the combination of DM was further performed. Sensitivity analysis for the MACE was conducted to detect sources of heterogeneity and assess the stability of the overall results. Besides, Begg’s funnel plot and Egger’s test for the MACE were conducted to detect publication bias (19,20). When significant publication bias was detected, the trim-and-fill method was further applied (21).

Association of preoperative TyG index with clinical outcomes among patients undergoing CABG

Fourteen studies explored prognostic role of TyG index in patients who underwent the CABG. As for primary outcomes, pooled results manifested that elevated TyG index was significantly related to increased incidence of MACE (OR =1.91, 95% CI: 1.46–2.50, P<0.001; I2=66.3%, P=0.007) (Figure 2) and MACCE (OR =1.67, 95% CI: 1.32–2.11, P<0.001; I2=32.4%, P=0.22) (Figure 3). Subgroup analyses based on the DM indicated more significant association of TyG index with increased incidence of MACE (OR =4.27, 95% CI: 1.87–9.75, P=0.001) (Figure S1A) and MACCE (OR =1.71, 95% CI: 1.28–2.30, P<0.001) (Figure S1B) among diabetic patients and also statistical trend about the relationship between TyG index and incidence of MACE (OR =4.05, P=0.09) and MACCE (OR =1.19, P=0.40) in non-diabetic patients (Table 2).

As for the secondary outcomes, TyG index was related to the incidence of all-cause mortality (HR =1.37, 95% CI: 1.04–1.81, P=0.02) (Figure S2A), cardiac mortality (HR =2.59, 95% CI: 1.45–4.62, P=0.001), nonfatal MI (OR =2.02, 95% CI: 1.04–3.92, P=0.04) (Figure S2B), nonfatal stroke (OR =2.00, 95% CI: 1.36–2.93, P<0.001) (Figure S2C), AF (OR =1.55, 95% CI: 1.22–1.98, P<0.001) (Figure S2D), SGF (OR =2.987, 95% CI: 1.440–6.195, P=0.003) and cerebral infarction (OR =1.93, 95% CI: 1.41–2.63, P<0.001). Association of preoperative TyG-BMI index with clinical outcomes among patients undergoing CABG

Only one study explored the prognostic value of TyG-BMI index in patients undergoing CABG, which indicated that elevated TyG-BMI was associated with higher incidence of AKI (OR =1.03, 95% CI: 1.02–1.06, P<0.001) among diabetic patients.

Publication bias

According to Begg’s funnel plot (Figure 5A) and Egger’s test (P=0.001), significant publication bias was detected. Therefore, the trim-and-fill method was applied and three potentially unpublished studies were found (Figure 5B). However, these three studies did not cause an impact on conclusion (fixed OR =1.55, 95% CI: 1.39–1.75, P<0.001; random OR =1.61, 95% CI: 1.18–2.21, P=0.003), which also verified the stability of our findings.

Discussion

In this meta-analysis including 16,185 patients undergoing CABG, we demonstrated that an elevated preoperative TyG index was significantly associated with increased incidences of MACE, MACCE, all-cause mortality, cardiac mortality, nonfatal MI, nonfatal stroke, AF, SGF, and cerebral infarction. Notably, the prognostic value of TyG index was more pronounced among patients with DM. Furthermore, limited available evidence from a single study suggested that elevated TyG-BMI index was independently associated with a higher incidence of postoperative AKI in diabetic patients. These findings suggest that TyG-related indices, as simple and inexpensive metabolic markers, are associated with adverse clinical outcomes after CABG and may serve as potential biomarkers for identifying patients with higher metabolic risk. However, whether incorporation of TyG-related indices into established surgical risk models can improve risk prediction requires further investigation. It should be noted that several secondary outcomes, including cardiac mortality, SGF, cerebral infarction, and TyG-BMI-related AKI, were based on a very limited number of studies. Therefore, these findings should be considered exploratory and interpreted with caution until confirmed by larger prospective studies.

The close association between TyG index and adverse cardiovascular outcomes may be explained by its robust reflection of IR, a key driver of atherosclerosis and cardiovascular remodeling. The TyG index has been validated as a reliable surrogate marker of IR compared with the hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp test (8). IR promotes endothelial dysfunction, oxidative stress, vascular inflammation, and prothrombotic states, all of which accelerate graft atherosclerosis and adverse cardiovascular events (35). Experimental and clinical data indicate that IR impairs nitric oxide bioavailability and increases vascular smooth muscle proliferation, thereby contributing to progression of CAD and vein graft failure (11). Moreover, IR is strongly associated with atrial structural remodeling and increased susceptibility to AF (36). The stronger association observed in diabetic patients may be attributed to the coexistence of chronic hyperglycemia, advanced glycation end-product accumulation, microvascular dysfunction, and heightened systemic inflammation, which synergistically exacerbate postoperative cardiovascular risk (37). Regarding AKI, IR and obesity-related metabolic dysfunction contribute to renal microvascular injury, glomerular hyperfiltration, and activation of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (38). The TyG-BMI index integrates both IR and adiposity, and obesity itself is an established risk factor for postoperative AKI due to increased oxidative stress, inflammation, and altered renal hemodynamics (37). Therefore, the strong association between TyG-BMI and AKI in diabetic CABG patients may reflect the combined detrimental effects of metabolic dysregulation and excess adiposity on perioperative renal vulnerability.

From a clinical perspective, TyG and TyG-BMI indices have several practical advantages. They are derived from routinely measured fasting triglyceride, glucose, and BMI values, requiring no additional cost or specialized testing. Preoperative identification of patients with elevated TyG-related indices may facilitate more intensive metabolic optimization, including stricter glycemic control, lipid-lowering therapy, weight management, and perioperative insulin protocols. Evidence suggests that improved perioperative glycemic control is associated with reduced mortality and complications after cardiac surgery (39). Furthermore, patients identified as high metabolic risk may benefit from closer hemodynamic monitoring, renal protective strategies, and more aggressive secondary prevention after CABG. Integration of TyG index into existing risk models such as EuroSCORE II or STS score may improve long-term risk prediction, as metabolic risk is not fully captured by traditional surgical variables (6). Future prospective studies are warranted to determine whether TyG-guided interventions can translate into improved postoperative outcomes.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, all included studies were retrospective cohort studies and most study were from China, which are inherently subject to selection bias and residual confounding despite multivariable adjustment. Second, substantial clinical and methodological heterogeneity existed across the included studies, including differences in diabetic status, TyG cutoff values, outcome definitions, follow-up duration, patient characteristics, and adjustment strategies, which may have influenced the pooled estimates. Third, although subgroup analyses according to diabetic status were performed, more detailed subgroup analyses or meta-regression based on other important study characteristics (e.g., follow-up duration, TyG categorization method, study location, sample size, and adjustment level) could not be conducted because of the limited number of eligible studies and insufficient reporting of relevant data. Fourth, most studies were conducted in Asian populations, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to other ethnic groups. In addition, publication bias was assessed only for MACE because most other outcomes were reported by too few studies to permit reliable evaluation. Therefore, the possibility of publication bias for these outcomes cannot be completely excluded. Finally, differences in the reporting and modeling of TyG-related indices across the included studies, including the use of adjusted or unadjusted effect estimates and continuous or categorical variables, together with the limited evidence regarding TyG-BMI index, may have introduced additional heterogeneity. For the AKI outcome, only two studies were available, resulting in considerable heterogeneity and imprecise estimates. Therefore, potential sources of heterogeneity could not be explored, and this finding should be interpreted cautiously. Thus, further large-scale prospective studies with standardized reporting and analytical methods are warranted to validate our findings.

Conclusions

In conclusion, elevated preoperative TyG index is significantly associated with worse clinical outcomes, including increased risks of MACE and MACCE, in patients undergoing CABG, particularly among those with DM. In addition, limited evidence suggests that a higher TyG-BMI index may be associated with an increased incidence of postoperative AKI in diabetic patients. These findings highlight the potential value of TyG-related indices as simple and practical biomarkers associated with adverse outcomes in CABG populations. Prospective studies are required to confirm these associations and to explore whether targeted metabolic interventions based on TyG-related indices can improve clinical outcomes.
