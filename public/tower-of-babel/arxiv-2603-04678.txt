Optimizing Language Models for Crosslingual Knowledge Consistency
Tianyu Liu, Jirui Qi, Mrinmaya Sachan, Ryan Cotterell, Raquel Fernández, Arianna Bisazza

Abstract
Large language models are known to often exhibit inconsistent knowledge. This is particularly problematic in multilingual scenarios, where models are likely to be asked similar questions in different languages, and inconsistent responses can undermine their reliability. In this work, we show that this issue can be mitigated using reinforcement learning with a structured reward function, which leads to an optimal policy with consistent crosslingual responses. We introduce D irect C onsistency O ptimization (DCO), a DPO-inspired method that requires no explicit reward model and is derived directly from the LLM itself. Comprehensive experiments show that DCO significantly improves crosslingual consistency across diverse LLMs and outperforms existing methods when training with samples of multiple languages, while complementing DPO when gold labels are available. Extra experiments demonstrate the effectiveness of DCO in bilingual settings, significant out-of-domain generalizability, and controllable alignment via direction hyperparameters. Taken together, these results establish DCO as a robust and efficient solution for improving knowledge consistency across languages in multilingual LLMs. All code, training scripts, and evaluation benchmarks are released at https://github.com/Betswish/ConsistencyRL .

Introduction

As multilingual capabilities become a standard feature of modern large language models (LLMs)
(
27
;
20
;
5
)
, ensuring crosslingual consistency (CLC) has become increasingly critical.
Ideally, an LLM should provide consistent answers question regardless of the language in which it is asked.
However, this is far from guaranteed:
prior work
(
13
;
22
;
30
)
has demonstrated that LLMs often produce conflicting responses across languages, as illustrated in
Figure

1
(top).
Such inconsistencies can confuse users with diverse language backgrounds and undermine trust in multilingual systems.

To address this challenge, we aim to improve CLC by Reinforcement Learning (RL), inspired by principles of alignment with human preferences.
Existing post-training algorithms for aligning with human preferences, such as proximal policy optimization
(
25
, PPO;)
and direct preference optimization
(
24
, DPO;)
, rely on reward functions defined over pairs of responses, often modeled using the Bradley–Terry framework.
However, CLC involves connecting multiple languages and requires a different approach to reward design and optimization.

To this end, we propose a new reward function that promotes CLC by leveraging the likelihoods assigned by a model to the same answer expressed in different languages.
Specifically, to align two languages,
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}L}_{1}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}L}_{2}
, we define the reward for
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}L}_{1}
based on the log-likelihood of answers generated when prompted in
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}L}_{2}
, and vice versa.
This leads to a policy expressed as a
product of experts

(
12
)
:

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pi^{\star}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{y}}^{i}}\mid{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}^{i}})=\frac{1}{Z}\prod_{j}\Big({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pi_{\textsc{ref}}}\big({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tau}^{j}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{y}}^{i}})\mid{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tau}^{j}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}^{i}})\big)\Big)^{w_{ij}},

where
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pi_{\textsc{ref}}}
is the base multilingual LLM,
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tau}^{i}
translates a prompt or response to
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}L}_{i}
,
w_{ij}
are controllable parameters, and
Z
is a normalization term.
By controlling
w_{ij}
, the optimal policy
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pi^{\star}}
can be theoretically guaranteed to be consistent while preserving the model’s overall performance.
Building on this foundation, we propose
direct consistency optimization
(DCO), an efficient algorithm that adapts a DPO-like procedure to our proposed objective without explicit reward models.
We theoretically prove that DCO can bypass the online sampling step in RL, yet still arrive at the same optimal policy as the original RL formulation.

As illustrated in
Figure

1
(middle), training involves parallel prompts and completions across languages, and the optimization encourages consistent distributions over completions in both languages, shown in
Figure

1
(bottom).

We evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed reward and optimization algorithm with nine LLMs across three datasets, covering 26 languages.
Experimental results demonstrate that DCO significantly improves crosslingual consistency while maintaining, and often improving, answer accuracy in the post-trained languages.

In summary, our contributions are as follows:

•

We propose a reward function tailored for CLC and introduce DCO, an algorithm that solves the RL objective, with theoretical guarantees of improved CLC and preserved task performance.

•

We empirically validate DCO on advanced LLMs across diverse benchmarks.

•

We provide extensive analyses, including comparisons with other alignment techniques, cross-domain generalization, and control over language preference.

Related Work

Crosslingual knowledge consistency is a crucial property for multilingual LLMs.

Measuring crosslingual consistency.

Several studies have explored methods to assess the consistency of knowledge in multilingual LLMs.

35
and
1
evaluate consistency by measuring the agreement of top-1 generated answers to the same question posed in different languages, whereas
13
compute the average overlapping ratio of correct predictions across languages.
To assess CLC more comprehensively and to disentangle it from accuracy,
22
introduce the RankC metric, based on a weighted average of the overlapping ratio of top-1 to top-
N
ranked candidates.
These studies reveal significant crosslingual knowledge inconsistencies in a wide range of LLMs.

Improving crosslingual consistency.

A number of recent studies attempt to improve CLC by applying vector interventions on the hidden representations of LLMs
(
18
;
29
;
17
)
.
While promising and insightful, these interpretability-based methods are typically tested on small datasets and specific models, making them challenging to scale to broader applications.

Closer to our work,
30
proposes CALM, which improves CLC using RL.
Their approach first selects a target answer by majority voting based on the model’s completions across multiple languages.
Then, they use DPO to increase the likelihood of the target majority answer across languages.
However, CALM requires more than two languages, restricting its usage in practical bilingual scenarios.
Moreover, it does not necessarily benefit from adding more languages as majority voting can become unreliable when multiple low-resource languages are included.

Preliminaries

Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback.

Before applying RL,
supervised fine-tuning
(SFT) is often applied to
a pre-trained language model
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pi_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{\theta}}}}
on a dataset
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}_{\textsc{sft}}}
containing annotated examples for downstream tasks to minimize the loss
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{L}}^{\textsc{ml}}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{\theta}})=-\E_{({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{y}})\sim{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}_{\textsc{sft}}}}\Bigl[\log{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pi_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{\theta}}}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{y}}\mid{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}})\Bigr]
.
The resulting fine-tuned model is denoted by
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pi_{\textsc{sft}}}
. However, it may not always reflect human preferences.
This misalignment arises because the maximum likelihood estimation objective does not differentiate between major and minor errors in the model’s responses.
To address this, a
reward optimization
step is introduced.
Assuming the availability of a reward model
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}r}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{y}})
, which is trained on a
dataset with human feedback
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}_{\textsc{hf}}}
, the RLHF objective aims to maximize the expected reward of the model’s outputs.
A KL divergence regularization term is added to the objective
(
26
)
to prevent reward hacking
(
2
)
and ensure the model does not deviate excessively from
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pi_{\textsc{sft}}}
. The ultimate target is to obtain a
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pi_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{\theta}}}}
:

\displaystyle\max_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pi_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{\theta}}}}}

\displaystyle\E_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}\sim{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{y}}\sim{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pi_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{\theta}}}}(\cdot|{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}})}\Big[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}r}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{y}})\Big]

(1)

\displaystyle-\beta\cdot{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\textsc{kl}}\big[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pi_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{\theta}}}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{y}}\mid{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}})\,\|\,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pi_{\textsc{sft}}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{y}}\mid{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}})\big],

where
\beta
is a hyperparameter controlling the adherence to
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pi_{\textsc{sft}}}
.
This objective is typically optimized using algorithms such as PPO or other actor–critic methods
(
19
;
8
)
. As proposed by
24
, the optimal
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pi^{\star}}

can be expressed in the closed form:

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pi^{\star}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{y}}\mid{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}})=\frac{1}{Z({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}})}\,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pi_{\textsc{sft}}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{y}}\mid{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}})\exp\Big(\frac{1}{\beta}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}r}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{y}})\Big).

(2)

where
Z({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}})
is the partition function ensuring normalization.

Direct Preference Optimization.

24
observe that by rearranging the terms in
Equation

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}r}

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}r}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{y}})=\beta\log\frac{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pi^{\star}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{y}}\mid{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}})}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pi_{\textsc{ref}}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{y}}\mid{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}})}+\beta\log Z({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}),

(3)

where

To avoid training reward models,

4
Optimizing Crosslingual Consistency

In this section, we formulate the consistency alignment problem as an RL task, define the reward function, and derive the optimal policy that ensures consistent responses across languages.

4.1
Defining Crosslingual Consistency

Throughout this paper, we use superscripts to denote the language of a prompt
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}
or a response
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{y}}
.
For example,
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}^{1}}
represents a prompt in language
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}L}_{1}
,
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{y}}^{2}}
represents a response in language
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}L}_{2}
.
We define two prompt–response pairs
({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}^{1}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{y}}^{1}})
and
({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}^{2}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{y}}^{2}})
in languages
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}L}_{1}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}L}_{2}
as
equivalent
, denoted by
({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}^{1}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{y}}^{1}})\sim({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}^{2}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{y}}^{2}})
, if they can be mapped to each other via translational mappings
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tau}^{1}\colon{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}L}_{2}\to{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}L}_{1}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tau}^{2}\colon{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}L}_{1}\to{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}L}_{2}
.
1

1

We assume the existence of such translational mappings
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tau}^{1},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tau}^{2}

(

For simplicity, we denote
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tau}^{1}

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}L}_{2}

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tau}^{2}
.
For example, this implies
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}^{2}}={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tau}^{2}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}^{1}})

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{y}}^{1}}={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tau}^{1}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{y}}^{2}})

We formalize

as the property that the relative preference between any pair of responses remains

\displaystyle\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\displaystyle\bm{x}^{1}})\!\!\iff\!\!

(5)

\displaystyle\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\displaystyle\pi^{\star}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{y}}^{2}_{w}}\mid{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}^{2}})\geq{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pi^{\star}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{y}}^{2}_{l}}\mid{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}^{2}}).

In other words, given a prompt

4.2
Solving the Constrained RL Problem

In this section, we will design a reward function
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}r_{\textsc{align}}}
which enforces crosslingual consistency.
Suppose we already have
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}r_{\textsc{align}}}

\displaystyle\max_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pi_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{\theta}}}}}

\displaystyle\E_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}\sim{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{y}}\sim{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pi_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{\theta}}}}(\cdot\mid{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}})}\Big[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}r_{\textsc{align}}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{y}}))\Big]

(6)

\displaystyle-\beta\cdot{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\textsc{kl}}\big[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pi_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{\theta}}}}(\cdot\mid{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}})\|{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pi_{\textsc{ref}}}(\cdot\mid{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}})\big],

where

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pi^{\star}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{y}}\mid{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}})}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pi_{\textsc{ref}}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{y}}\mid{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}})\exp\Big(\frac{1}{\beta}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}r_{\textsc{align}}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{y}})\Big),

Lemma 1
.

If
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}_{1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}_{2}=\beta^{2}
, the optimal policy
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pi^{\star}}
defined by
Equation

7
is consistent across
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}L}_{1}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}L}_{2}

Proof sketch.

When
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}_{1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}_{2}=\beta^{2}
, raising both sides of
Equation

9a
to the power of
\frac{\beta}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}_{1}}
gives us:

\displaystyle\Big({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pi^{\star}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{y}}^{1}}\mid{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}^{1}})\Big)^{\nicefrac{{\beta}}{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}_{1}}}}\equiv\frac{Z({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tau}^{2}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}^{1}}))}{Z^{\nicefrac{{\beta}}{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}_{1}}}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}^{1}})}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pi^{\star}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tau}^{2}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{y}}^{1}})\mid{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tau}^{2}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}^{1}})).

Since the function

.

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pi^{\star}}

and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}L}_{2}

D.1

Remark 1
.

Optimizing the objective in
Equation

6
yields a policy
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pi^{\star}}
that balances the original policy
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pi_{\textsc{ref}}}
across
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}L}_{1}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}L}_{2}
.

Lemma

1
specifies the relationship
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}_{1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}_{2}=\beta^{2}
to ensure consistency.
Here,
\beta
controls the overall deviation of
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pi^{\star}}
from
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pi_{\textsc{ref}}}
.
While
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}_{1}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}_{2}
determine the relative alignment strength for
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}L}_{1}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}L}_{2}
.
For instance, a smaller
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}_{1}
biases
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pi^{\star}}
closer to
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pi_{\textsc{ref}}}
in
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}L}_{1}
.

Generalizing to
N
languages.

Our method naturally extends to align
N
languages.
For
N
languages, we introduce
(N^{2}-N)
hyperparameters
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}_{ij}
, where
i,j\in\{1,2,\dots,N\}
and
i\neq j
, to control the alignment strength between
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}L}_{i}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}L}_{j}
.
The reward function is
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}r_{\textsc{align}}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{y}})=\sum_{j=1}^{N}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}_{ij}\log{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pi_{\textsc{ref}}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tau}^{j}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{y}})\mid{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tau}^{j}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}))\text{ when }{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{y}}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}L}_{i}
.

The detailed derivation, the mathematical form of the optimal policy, and the constraints on
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}_{ij}
to ensure consistency are provided in
Appendix

E
.
This formulation ensures that the policy
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pi^{\star}}
aligns preferences across all
N
languages while maintaining flexibility through the hyperparameters
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}_{ij}
.

4.3
Direct Consistency Optimization

In principle, there could be diverse ways to implement
Equation

6
.
Here, we propose Direct Consistency Optimization (DCO) as an efficient algorithm tailored to our consistency objective.
DCO is inspired by DPO, which bypasses the reward modeling and constrained RL phase.
It leverages a dataset of parallel prompt–response pairs, eliminating the need for online sampling and translator usage.

The Objective Function.

The core idea of DPO is to use a change of variables to express the human preference alignment loss directly as a function of the policy
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pi_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{\theta}}}}
.
In
Equation

8
, we have described the exact form of
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}r_{\textsc{align}}}
that we need.
Our goal is to design an objective function that will lead to an optimal
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\widehat{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}r}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{\theta}}}}}
that is the same as
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}r_{\textsc{align}}}
, and thus leads to policy
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pi_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{\theta}}}}
that is the same as
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pi^{\star}}
.
In principle, any objective function with an optimal solution of
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}r_{\textsc{align}}}
can be used. Here, we adopt an objective that mirrors the DPO framework, leveraging the Bradley–Terry preference model to align reward differences with expected values.
Specifically, we train
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\widehat{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}r}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{\theta}}}}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{y}}_{w}})-{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\widehat{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}r}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{\theta}}}}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{y}}_{l}})
to match
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}r_{\textsc{align}}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{y}}_{w}})-{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}r_{\textsc{align}}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{y}}_{l}})
.
Through this modeling choice, we avoid computing the intractable normalization term
Z({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}})
in
Equation

3
.

Let
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}_{\parallel}}
denote a dataset of parallel prompt–response pairs, from which we sample tuples
({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}^{1}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{y}}^{1}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}^{2}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{y}}^{2}})
, where
({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}^{1}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{y}}^{1}})\sim({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}^{2}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{y}}^{2}})
.
The responses are
randomly
paired into
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{y}}^{1}_{w}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{y}}^{1}_{l}}
, meaning we do not assume
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{y}}^{1}_{w}}
is inherently better than
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{y}}^{1}_{l}}
.
We define the following loss function to train
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\widehat{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}r}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{\theta}}}}}
to match
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}r_{\textsc{align}}}
:

\displaystyle d^{1}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{\theta}}}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{\tiny def}}}{{=}}}\Big({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\widehat{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}r}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{\theta}}}}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}^{1}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{y}}^{1}_{w}})-{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\widehat{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}r}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{\theta}}}}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}^{1}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{y}}^{1}_{l}})\Big)

(10)

\displaystyle d^{2}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{\theta}}}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{\tiny def}}}{{=}}}\Big({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\widehat{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}r}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{\theta}}}}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}^{2}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{y}}^{2}_{w}})-{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\widehat{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}r}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{\theta}}}}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}^{2}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{y}}^{2}_{l}})\Big)

Lemma 2

6.6
Discussion

Where do the accuracy gains come from?

In general, when a language model performs poorly on a task, it tends to have a
high-entropy distribution
over the candidate answers.
In contrast, a low-entropy one that is skewed toward an incorrect answer is less common.
Thus, the experts in
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pi^{\star}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{y}}\mid{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}})=\frac{1}{Z}\prod_{j}\Big({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pi_{\textsc{ref}}}\big({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tau}^{j}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{y}})\mid{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tau}^{j}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}})\big)\Big)^{w_{j}}
are complementary to each other.
Specifically, a low-entropy expert only contributes minimally to the final distribution, allowing the ensemble to rely more on high-confidence predictions from other experts.
As a case study, we verify this assumption using
Qwen-2.5-14B
on the MMMLU dataset, where the average entropy of the answer distribution on the questions that are correctly answered is
0.41\pm 0.41
, while for incorrectly answered questions, it is significantly higher at
0.98\pm 0.33
.
The accuracy of the theoretical optimal policy
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pi^{\star}}
on MMMLU using
Qwen-2.5-14B
is
77.0
, surpassing the accuracy of the base policy in individual languages.

Beyond crosslingual consistency.

While this work focuses on
crosslingual
knowledge consistency, the training objective of DCO is not limited to this task and can naturally be extended to other forms of consistency.
For instance, by aligning the output distributions over candidate answers of paraphrased prompts, the model could be encouraged to respond consistently regardless of surface variations. Such extensions are promising for future works.

7
Conclusion

This paper proposes a novel structured reward function to improve crosslingual consistency in multilingual LLMs and introduces an efficient method, DCO.

Through comprehensive experiments, we demonstrate that DCO consistently improves CLC across a variety of models and datasets.
Compared to existing methods, DCO delivers robust performance gains and complements DPO when gold labels are available, producing the strongest overall knowledge alignment in a joint
N
-languages training setting.
In bilingual settings, DCO also enhances CLC effectively,
raising accuracy in non-English languages while maintaining accuracy in English.
We further show the generalizability of DCO across domains, with gains observed even when testing on subjects that differ from the training ones.
The analysis of direction-controlling weights demonstrates how practitioners can steer alignment toward specific languages according to deployment requirements.

Looking ahead, we believe the structured reward underlying DCO has potential for application beyond crosslingual knowledge consistency, for example,
improving self-consistency across paraphrases
(
34
)
or consistency across modalities.
As a computationally efficient algorithm, DCO provides a practical path toward building powerful multilingual LLMs that are not only accurate but also reliable and equitable across languages.

Appendix B
Background: Modeling Human Preferences with Bradley-Terry Model

An important component for post-training LLMs is reward models that align with human preferences
(
37
;
31
;
21
)
.
To construct a human feedback dataset
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}_{\textsc{hf}}}
, humans are shown two (or more) responses to a prompt
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}
and are asked to select the response they prefer.

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}_{\textsc{hf}}}
is denoted as a collection of triples
({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{y}}_{w}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{y}}_{l}})
, where
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{y}}_{w}}
is preferred over
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{y}}_{l}}
by a human.

To train a reward function
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}r}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{\phi}}}}
on
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}_{\textsc{hf}}}
, it is common to assume that human preference can be modeled by a Bradley–Terry model
(
4
)
,

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbb{P}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}r}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{\phi}}}}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{y}}_{w}}\succ{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{y}}_{l}})\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{\tiny def}}}{{=}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\sigma}\big({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}r}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{\phi}}}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{y}}_{w}})-{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}r}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{\phi}}}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{y}}_{l}})\big),

(12)

where
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\sigma}(x)\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{\tiny def}}}{{=}}}\frac{1}{1+\exp(-x)}
is the sigmoid function.
The reward model
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}r}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{\phi}}}}
, parameterized by
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{\phi}}
, is trained to minimize the following negative log-likelihood loss:

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{L}}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}r}}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{\phi}})=-\E_{({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{y}}_{w}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{y}}_{l}})\sim{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}_{\textsc{hf}}}}\Big[\log{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbb{P}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}r}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{\phi}}}}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{y}}_{w}}\succ{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{y}}_{l}})\Big].

(13)

Intuitively, the reward function should assign higher rewards to the responses that are preferred by humans.
Then, the reward function is then plugged into
Equation

1
for policy optimization.

Appendix C
Discussion on the Definition of Crosslingual Consistency

One might attempt to use a stricter definition of consistency, such as requiring exact probability matches
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pi^{\star}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{y}}^{1}_{w}}\mid{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}^{1}})={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pi^{\star}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{y}}^{2}_{w}}\mid{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}^{2}})
.
Previous work has shown that even semantically identical sentences in different languages can have likelihoods that differ significantly due to lexical, semantic, and tokenization differences
(
15
)
.
For instance, in
Gemma3-12B-it
, using a temperature of
1
,
\pi({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\text{`` Paris''}}\mid{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\text{``The capital of France is''}})=0.8991
, while
\pi({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\text{`` Paris''}}\mid{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\text{``Die Hauptstadt Frankreichs ist''}})=0.1283
.
Thus, enforcing exact probability matches would ignore these inherent differences and lead to suboptimal alignment.
Instead, we adopt a softer order-based consistency constraint.

Appendix E
Generalization to
N
Languages

We generalize
Section

4
to aligning
N
languages.
The reward function for alignment is generalized to

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}r_{\textsc{align}}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{y}})=\begin{cases}\sum_{j=1}^{N}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}_{ij}\log{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pi_{\textsc{ref}}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tau}^{j}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{y}})\mid{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tau}^{j}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}))&\text{when }{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{y}}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}L}_{i},\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(14)

We set
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}_{ii}=0
for
i\in\{1,2,\cdots,N\}
.
Thus, there are
(N^{2}-N)
hyperparameters in total.

The optimal policy is

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pi^{\star}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{y}}^{i}}\mid{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}^{i}})=\frac{1}{Z({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}^{i}})}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pi_{\textsc{ref}}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{y}}^{i}}\mid{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}^{i}})\prod_{j\in\{1,2,\cdots,N\}\setminus\{i\}}\Big({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pi_{\textsc{ref}}}\big({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tau}^{j}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{y}}^{i}})\mid{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tau}^{j}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}^{i}})\big)\Big)^{\nicefrac{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}_{j}}}{{\beta}}}.

(15)

We define the following matrix

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Gamma}\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{\tiny def}}}{{=}}}\begin{pmatrix}1&\nicefrac{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}_{12}}}{{\beta}}&\cdots&\nicefrac{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}_{1N}}}{{\beta}}\\
\nicefrac{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}_{21}}}{{\beta}}&1&\cdots&\nicefrac{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}_{2N}}}{{\beta}}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
\nicefrac{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}_{N1}}}{{\beta}}&\nicefrac{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}_{22}}}{{\beta}}&\cdots&1\end{pmatrix}

(16)

We give a sufficient condition on
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Gamma}
that leads to consistent policies.

Lemma 3
.

When
\text{rank}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Gamma})=1
,
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pi^{\star}}
is consistent across
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}L}_{1},\cdots,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}L}_{N}
.

Proof.

When
\text{rank}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Gamma})=1
, each row of
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Gamma}
can be represented as a multiple of the first row.
I.e., there exists
(N-1)
numbers
k_{2},\cdots,k_{N}
such that

\begin{pmatrix}1&\nicefrac{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}_{12}}}{{\beta}}&\cdots&\nicefrac{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}_{1N}}}{{\beta}}\end{pmatrix}=k_{i}\begin{pmatrix}\nicefrac{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}_{i2}}}{{\beta}}&\nicefrac{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}_{i2}}}{{\beta}}&\cdots&\nicefrac{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma}_{iN}}}{{\beta}}\end{pmatrix}.

(17)

Then,
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pi^{\star}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{y}}^{1}}\mid{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}^{1}})=\frac{(Z({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}^{i}}))^{k_{i}}}{Z({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}^{1}})}\big({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pi^{\star}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{y}}^{i}}\mid{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}^{i}})\big)^{k_{i}}
, which indicates
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pi^{\star}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{y}}^{i}}\mid{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}^{i}})
is consistent with
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pi^{\star}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{y}}^{1}}\mid{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}^{1}})
for
i\in\{2,\cdots,N\}
.
Therefore, every pair of languages
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}L}_{i},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}L}_{j}
is consistent.
∎

to match ground truth preference labels.
The optimal
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\widehat{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}r}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{\theta}}}}}
in this case can take
unbounded
values and lacks a closed-form expression.
By contrast, in
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}_{\parallel}}

is not necessarily the better response carrying the gold answer, which benefits real-world applications.
Minimizing

ensures that the Bradley–Terry model
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbb{P}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\widehat{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}r}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{\theta}}}}}}
matches the distribution
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\sigma}\big(\log\frac{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pi_{\textsc{ref}}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{y}}^{2}_{w}}\mid{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}^{2}})}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pi_{\textsc{ref}}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{y}}^{2}_{l}}\mid{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}^{2}})}\big)

Appendix I
Details of RankC: Ranking-based Crosslingual Consistency

RankC

(
22
)
is a ranking-based consistency metric for assessing crosslingual knowledge consistency independently of probing accuracy.

Given a parallel query set:

Q^{1}=\{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}^{1}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{|Q|},\quad Q^{2}=\{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}^{2}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{|Q|},

where each
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}^{1}_{i}
in
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}L}_{1}
corresponds to
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}^{2}_{i}
in
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}L}_{2}
.
For the
i
-th query, assuming there are
N_{i}
candidate answers
\{c_{i,j}\}_{j=1}^{N_{i}}
, the model assigns a likelihood to each of the candidates.
Let the candidates in each language be sorted by descending likelihood:

c^{1}_{i,1},c^{1}_{i,2},\dots,c^{1}_{i,N_{i}}\quad\text{(in }{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}L}_{1}\text{)}
and

c^{2}_{i,1},c^{2}_{i,2},\dots,c^{2}_{i,N_{i}}\quad\text{(in }{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}L}_{2}\text{)}
.

Then, the ‘precision at
j
’ (denoted
P@j
) is defined as the proportion of overlap among the top-
j
candidates in both languages:

P@j=\frac{1}{j}\;\bigl|\{c^{1}_{i,1},\dots,c^{1}_{i,j}\}\cap\{c^{2}_{i,1},\dots,c^{2}_{i,j}\}\bigr|
.
A ranking-based weight
w_{j}=\frac{\exp(N_{i}-j)}{\sum_{k=1}^{N_{i}}\exp(N_{i}-k)}
is multiplied to each
P@j
, so that agreements at smaller
j
(i.e., top of the list) are rewarded more.
Given these, the consistency score for that query pair is:

\mathrm{consist}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}^{1}_{i},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}^{2}_{i})=\sum_{j=1}^{N_{i}}w_{j}\cdot P@j
.

Finally, the overall RankC between
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}L}_{1}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}L}_{2}
is the average consistency score over all query pairs:

\mathrm{RankC}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}L}_{1},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}L}_{2})=\frac{1}{|Q|}\sum_{i=1}^{|Q|}\mathrm{consist}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}^{1}_{i},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}^{2}_{i})
.

Appendix K
Guidance of Direction Controlling for Real-World Applications

Direction–controlling hyperparameters matter: by adjusting
(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})
, we control the transfer strength for each side, thus deciding the direction of optimizing knowledge consistency. The low-resource languages Swahili and Yoruba were selected in our experiments for the best visibility of the effect; yet the results should not be misinterpreted as ‘always set a large
\gamma
for
en
.’ In fact, the principle is more general: anchor on the high-quality, high-priority language, which is
en
in our study, but could be French or any other well-trained language, according to the specific requirements of the downstream LLM application. When both languages are of comparable quality, or when policy requires reciprocity, a symmetric schedule like 1:1 is expected to be optimal. In practice,
(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})
can also be selected empirically against a small validation set. The ‘ratio of changed answers’ (i.e.
Figure

2
(right) &
Figure

3
(right)) is useful:
if the intended stable language exhibits excessive changes, or the language expected to shift shows little movement, increase
\gamma_{1}
, and by construction,
\gamma_{2}
will decrease correspondingly (
\gamma_{2}=1/\gamma_{1}
).

Note that DCO is capable of improving CLC under any direction parameter setups, as demonstrated in
Figure

2
&
Figure

3
. This empirical adjustment further yields gains in accuracy for both sides.
