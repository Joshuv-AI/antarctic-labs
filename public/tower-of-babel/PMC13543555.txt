HIF-1α/NREP axis promotes hypertrophic scar fibroblast proliferation and collagen deposition via activation of the TGF-β1/Smad pathway
Authors: Baiye Chen, Chaofan Lin, Xueming Wang, Weiwen Zhu, Jiaqi Zhang, Liu Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHypertrophic scar (HS) is a fibroproliferative skin disorder characterized by excessive fibroblast proliferation and collagen overproduction during aberrant wound healing. Although hypoxia is a well-recognized driver of HS formation, the molecular mechanisms linking hypoxic signaling to fibroblast activation remain incompletely understood.MethodsIn this study, the expression of hypoxia-inducible factor 1α (HIF-1α), neuronal regeneration–related protein (NREP), and phosphorylated small mother against decapentaplegic family member 2/3 (p-Smad2/3) was examined in HS and matched normal tissues from patients. Hypertrophic scar fibroblasts (HSFBs) were cultured under hypoxic or normoxic conditions, followed by genetic manipulation of HIF-1α or NREP to assess their effects on fibroblast proliferation and collagen synthesis. The direct transcriptional regulation of NREP by HIF-1α was confirmed using dual-luciferase reporter and ChIP assays.ResultsCompared with normal tissues, HS tissues exhibited markedly elevated expression of HIF-1α, NREP, and phosphorylated Smad2/3. Hypoxia significantly increased HIF-1α and NREP levels in HSFBs, promoting fibroblast proliferation and collagen deposition, whereas knockdown of either HIF-1α or NREP abrogated these effects. Mechanistically, HIF-1α directly bound to the NREP promoter to enhance its transcription, and NREP overexpression partially rescued the suppressive effects of HIF-1α knockdown. Furthermore, HIF-1α-induced NREP upregulation activated the TGF-β1/Smad signaling cascade, while inhibition of this cascade counteracted the effect of NREP overexpression on the changes caused by HIF-1α silencing.ConclusionOur findings identify a novel HIF-1α/NREP/TGF-β1–Smad axis that drives fibroblast hyperproliferation and excessive collagen accumulation in HS. Targeting this signaling pathway may offer a promising therapeutic strategy for preventing or treating HS formation.Graphical Abstract Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13062-026-00855-8.

Introduction

Hypertrophic scar (HS) is a fibroproliferative skin disorder that frequently develops following deep burns, trauma, or extensive surgical wounds [1, 2]. The pathological hallmark of HS is the excessive proliferation of hypertrophic scar fibroblasts (HSFBs), which leads to aberrant collagen synthesis and excessive extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition [3, 4]. These cellular and molecular events result in scar thickening, stiffness, and cosmetic disfigurement, severely affecting patients’ quality of life. Therefore, elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying HSFB proliferation and collagen accumulation is critical for developing targeted interventions to prevent or treat HS formation.

Hypoxia is a well-recognized feature of wound healing and scar tissue formation. During the proliferative phase of wound repair, the imbalance between oxygen supply and demand leads to localized hypoxia, which activates hypoxia-inducible factor-1 alpha (HIF-1α), a master transcriptional regulator of cellular adaptation to low oxygen tension [5–7]. HIF-1α orchestrates a variety of biological processes, including angiogenesis, metabolism, inflammation, and fibrosis, through the transcriptional regulation of downstream target genes [8, 9]. In HS tissues and HSFBs, HIF-1α expression is markedly elevated [10], and its knockdown has been shown to attenuate collagen accumulation and improve scar architecture in vivo [11]. Moreover, recent studies have demonstrated that mesoporous silica nanoparticles loaded with resveratrol alleviate hypoxia-induced autophagy and fibroblast survival through the ROS-mediated p38-MAPK/HIF-1α/p53 signaling pathway [12]. Although HIF-1α has been implicated in both scar formation and remodeling [13], the precise mechanisms of formation and remodeling are unclear.

Neuronal regeneration-related protein (NREP), also known as P311, is an epigenetically regulated protein initially identified for its role in neural regeneration [14]. Accumulating evidence has revealed that NREP is also involved in blood pressure regulation, renal fibrosis, glioblastoma invasion, and cutaneous scar formation [15]. NREP promotes fibroblast activation and proliferation, while its genetic deletion markedly reduces collagen deposition and neovascularization in cutaneous [16, 17]. Notably, HIF-1α has been reported to transcriptionally activate NREP in breast cancer cells, facilitating tumor growth and metastasis [18]. These findings suggest that NREP may serve as a key downstream effector of HIF-1α in mediating fibroblast hyperactivation under hypoxic conditions. We therefore hypothesize that in HSFBs, HIF-1α transcriptionally upregulates NREP expression to promote profibrotic behavior and ECM deposition.

Transforming growth factor-β1 (TGF-β1) is a multifunctional cytokine and one of the central regulators of fibrosis [19, 20]. Mechanistically, NREP has been shown to interact with eIF6 to facilitate Sp1 recruitment to the TGF-β1 promoter via H2A.Z inhibition, thereby enhancing TGF-β1 transcription and promoting myofibroblast differentiation [21, 22]. The downstream TGF-β1/small mother against decapentaplegic (Smad) signaling pathway plays a pivotal role in HS pathogenesis by stimulating fibroblast proliferation and collagen synthesis [14, 15]. Conversely, inhibition of this pathway with agents such as loureirin B or botulinum toxin type A has been reported to attenuate fibroblast activation and reduce collagen deposition in HS models [23, 24]. Interestingly, HIF-1α has also been shown to activate TGF-β/Smad signaling in keloid fibroblasts [25], suggesting possible crosstalk between hypoxia signaling and profibrotic cascades.

Based on these findings, we propose that HIF-1α may promote HS progression by transcriptionally activating NREP, which in turn amplifies TGF-β/Smad signaling, thereby enhancing fibroblast proliferation and collagen deposition. To validate this hypothesis, we analyzed scar and adjacent normal skin (NS) tissues from HS patients and performed a series of in vitro assays to elucidate the molecular mechanisms by which the HIF-1α/NREP axis contributes to HS pathogenesis.

Materials and methods

Study subjects

A total of 113 patients with HS who underwent scar excision at the Department of Plastic Surgery of Fujian Children’s Hospital and the Department of Plastic Surgery of Fujian Provincial Maternity and Child Health Hospital between January 2021 and January 2024 were screened for eligibility. Based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, 32 patients were enrolled in this study. The cohort included 13 males and 19 females, with an age range of 5 to 49 years. The duration of scarring ranged from 8 to 44 months (mean: 23.34 ± 9.66 months). The Vancouver Scar Scale (VSS) scores ranged from 7 to 11, with an average of 8.97 ± 0.97.

Paired scar tissue and adjacent NS tissue samples were collected intraoperatively for analysis. All procedures involving human participants were reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Fujian Children’s Hospital (2025ETKLRK11025), and were conducted in compliance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal guardians prior to inclusion.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Patients with burn-induced scars accompanied by typical HS symptoms, such as pruritus or pain; (2) Presence of thickened, erythematous, and elevated scars with clearly defined boundaries, histopathologically confirmed as HS [26]; (3) Availability of complete clinical records and willingness to undergo postoperative follow-up.

Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Patients with life-threatening illness, severe infections, autoimmune diseases, psychiatric disorders, or major organ dysfunction; (2) Pregnant or lactating women; (3) Scar duration of less than 6 months.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Collected tissues were fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 5-μm thickness. Sections were deparaffinized in xylene, rehydrated through graded ethanol, and subjected to heat-induced antigen retrieval in citrate buffer (pH 6.0). Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched using 3% H2O2 (10011218, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) for 15 min at room temperature.

After blocking with 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) (A602440-0050, Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) for 15 min, sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: HIF-1α (ab51608, 1:100, Abcam, Cambridge, UK); NREP (PA5-68426, 1:20, Thermofisher; Waltham, MA, USA); p-Smad2/3 (#8828, 1:200, CST, Danvers, MA, USA).

After washing, sections were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (ab205718, 1:5000, Abcam) for 1 h at 37 °C. Color development was achieved using 3, 3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB) substrate (DAB-1031, Fuzhou Maixin Biotechnology Development Co., Ltd., Fuzhou, China), followed by hematoxylin counterstaining (H8070, Solarbio, Shanghai, China).

Stained sections were visualized and imaged using an Olympus BX53 microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). The staining intensity and positive area were semi-quantitatively assessed by two independent blinded pathologists using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA) to evaluate protein expression levels.

Cell culture and treatments

The human primary HSFB cell line was obtained from Zhejiang Meisen Cell Technology Co., Ltd. (CTCC-185-HUM, Meisen CTCC, Zhejiang, China). Cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM; Gibco, Carlsbad, CA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. The medium was replaced every 2 - 3 days, and cells were passaged when they reached 80–90% confluence.

Plasmid construction and cell transfection

Based on sequences available from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database, overexpression plasmids for HIF-1α (oe-HIF-1α), NREP (oe-NREP), and TGF-β1 (oe-TGF-β1), as well as small interfering RNA (siRNA) constructs targeting HIF-1α (si-HIF-1α), NREP (si-NREP), and TGF-β1 (si-TGF-β1) were established (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China). Three siRNA sequences were tested for each gene, and the sequence with the highest knockdown efficiency was selected for subsequent experiments. Corresponding negative control plasmids (oe-NC, si-NC) were also constructed.

HSFBs were transfected using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA; Cat. No. 11,668–019) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. After 24 h of transfection, cells were either maintained under normoxic conditions or subjected to hypoxia induction. Experiments were performed 48 h post-induction.

Hypoxia induction

To establish an in vitro hypoxia model, HSFBs (1 × 106 cells per well) were seeded in 6-well plates and cultured in a tri-gas incubator (Midi 40, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) under hypoxic conditions (5% O2, 5% CO2, 90% N2) for 48 h. Cells cultured under normoxic conditions (21% O2) served as controls [27].

The experimental groups were defined as follows: Nor (normoxic culture), Hy (hypoxic culture), Hy + si-NC (transfection with si-NC under hypoxia), Hy + si-NREP (transfection with si-NREP under hypoxia), Hy + si-HIF-1α (transfection with si-HIF-1α under hypoxia), Hy + si-HIF-1α + oe-NC (transfection with si-HIF-1α and oe-NC under hypoxia), Hy + si-HIF-1α + oe-NREP (transfection with si-HIF-1α and oe-NREP under hypoxia), Hy + si-TGF-β1 (transfection with si-TGF-β1 under hypoxia), Hy + si-HIF-1α + oe-TGF-β1 (transfection with si-HIF-1α and oe-TGF-β1 under hypoxia), and Hy + si-HIF-1α + oe-NREP + SB-431542 group (transfected with si-HIF-1α and oe-NREP, then treated with 10 μM TGF-β1/Smad inhibitor SB-431542 [SF7890, Beyotime, Shanghai, China] for 2 h, followed by hypoxia induction).

All in vitro experiments were independently repeated at least three times, with each experiment containing three technical replicates.

Cell viability assay (CCK-8)

Cell viability was evaluated using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Beyotime; Cat. No. C0039). Following treatment, 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well and incubated for 2 h at 37 °C in the dark. The optical density (OD) at 450 nm was measured using a Varioskan™ LUX multimode microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

Cell proliferation assay (EdU)

Cell proliferation was assessed using the EdU Imaging Kit (40276ES60, Yeasen Biotechnology [Shanghai] Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). When HSFBs reached ~80% confluence, cells were incubated with 10 μM EdU for 2 h at 37 °C, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature, and permeabilized using 0.5% Triton X-100 for 20 min.

Subsequently, cells were incubated with the Click-iT reaction cocktail for 30 min in the dark, followed by nuclear counterstaining with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI). Fluorescence images were captured under an Olympus IX53 fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). The percentage of EdU-positive cells was quantified using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from HSFBs using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA purity and concentration were determined spectrophotometrically (NanoDrop 2000, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Equal amounts of RNA were reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using the PrimeScript RT reagent kit (Takara, Dalian, China).

Quantitative qPCR amplification was conducted using the SYBR Premix Ex TaqTM II (Takara) on an ABI7900HT Real-Time PCR system (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA). The thermal cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 10 s, annealing at 60 °C for 20 s, and extension at 72 °C for 34 s.

β-actin was used as the internal reference. Relative gene expression levels were quantified using the 2-ΔΔCT method, where

ΔCTsample = CTtarget gene - CTreference;

ΔΔCT = ΔCTexperimental - ΔCTcontrol;

Relative expression = 2-ΔΔCT.

Western blot analysis

Total cellular proteins were extracted using RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime; Cat. No. P0013B) containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentrations were quantified with a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay kit (Beyotime, Cat. No. P0011).

Equal amounts of protein (20 µg per lane) were separated by SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred onto 0.45 µm polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA). Membranes were blocked for 1 h at room temperature with 5% skimmed milk in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween-20 (TBST), and then incubated overnight at 4 °C overnight with the following primary antibodies: HIF-1α (ab51608, 1:1000, Abcam), NREP (PA5-68426, 1:500, Thermo Fisher Scientific), TGF-β1 (ab215715, 1:1000, Abcam), Smad2/3 (#3102, 1:1000, CST), p-Smad2/3 (#8828, 1:1000, CST), and β-actin (ab8227, 1:2000, Abcam).

After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP- conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (ab205718, 1:5000, Abcam) for l h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The concentrations of collagen type-I (COL-I) and collagen type-III (COL-III) in HSFBs were determined using human ELISA kits from Cusabio (Wuhan, Hubei, China): COL-I: Cat. No. CSB-E08082h, and COL-III: Cat. No. CSB-E04799h.

All experimental steps were performed strictly according to the manufacturer’s protocols.

Dual-luciferase reporter gene assay

Potential binding sites between HIF-1α and the NREP promoter were predicted using the JASPAR database (https://jaspar.elixir.no/). To experimentally validate this prediction, a dual-luciferase reporter gene assay was performed following the manufacturer’s protocol for the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay Kit (KGAF040, KeyGEN BioTECH).

Briefly, the promoter region of NREP containing the putative HIF-1α binding sequence was amplified and inserted into a pGL3-basic vector (BR014, Fenghui Biotech, Changsha, China) to generate the wild-type (NREP-WT) reporter construct. A mutant (NREP-MUT) construct was similarly generated by site-directed mutagenesis of the predicted HIF-1α binding site.

HSFBs were co-transfected with oe-HIF-1α or oe-NC (negative control) together with either NREP-WT or NREP-MUT plasmids using Lipofectamine™ 2000 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). After 48 h, cell lysates were harvested, and both firefly and Renilla luciferase activities were measured using a Tecan Infinite® M200 PRO microplate reader (Tecan, Männedorf, Switzerland). Relative luciferase activity was calculated as the ratio of firefly to Renilla luciferase luminescence.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay

A ChIP assay was performed as previously described [18], using a ChIP Assay Kit (Wanlei Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) to verify the direct interaction between HIF-1α and the NREP promoter.

In brief, HSFBs were cross-linked with 1% formaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature to fix protein-DNA complexes. Cells were lysed and sonicated 12 times for 10 s each with 30 s intervals on ice to generate DNA fragments.

The chromatin supernatants were incubated with anti-HIF-1α antibody (20960–1-AP, Proteintech, Wuhan, China) or normal rabbit IgG (negative control). Subsequently, all chromatin samples were incubated with 60 μL protein A/G magnetic beads at 4°C with rotation for 1-2 h to enrich the protein-DNA complexes. Following purification, the precipitated DNA was recovered and analyzed by PCR to verify the target DNA fragments.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed and visualized using GraphPad Prism 9.5 software (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA). Data distribution and homogeneity of variance were assessed prior to analysis. Quantitative results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) from at least three independent experiments.

Comparisons between two paired samples were performed using a paired t-test, and comparisons between two unpaired groups using an unpaired t-test. For multi-group comparisons, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was applied. A two-tailed p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

To validate these observations in vitro, a hypoxia model of HS fibroblasts was established by culturing cells under 5% O₂ for 48 h. RT-qPCR and Western blot analyses revealed significant upregulation of both HIF-1α and NREP at the mRNA and protein levels under hypoxic conditions relative to normoxia (both p < 0.01, Fig. 1B, C).

Functionally, CCK-8 assay results demonstrated that hypoxia significantly increased HSFB proliferation compared to normoxic controls (p < 0.01, Fig. 1D). Consistent with this, EdU incorporation assays showed a higher proportion of EdU-positive nuclei in hypoxia-treated HSFBs (p < 0.01, Fig. 1E). Furthermore, ELISA results indicated elevated secretion of collagen proteins COL-I and COL-III in HSFBs exposed to hypoxia (p < 0.01, Fig. 1F).

Collectively, these data demonstrate that hypoxia stimulation upregulates HIF-1α and NREP expression, thereby promoting HSFB proliferation and collagen deposition, both hallmark features of HS formation.

Knockdown of HIF-1α or NREP suppresses HSFB proliferation and collagen synthesis

Three siRNAs were designed for both HIF-1α and NREP, and their silencing efficiencies were compared under normoxic conditions. RT-qPCR and Western blot analyses confirmed that si-HIF-1α-2 and si-NREP-1 achieved the most potent knockdown effects (p < 0.01, Supplementary Fig. 1), and thus were used in subsequent assays. Following transfection with si-HIF-1α or si-NREP, HSFBs were cultured under hypoxic conditions. Functional assays revealed that either HIF-1α or NREP knockdown significantly inhibited cell viability (CCK-8 assay, p < 0.05, Fig. 2E) and cell proliferation activity (EdU-positive cells, p < 0.01, Fig. 2F). These findings collectively indicate that HIF-1α and NREP are essential mediators of HSFB proliferation and collagen synthesis, supporting their cooperative role in HS development.

Bioinformatic analysis using the JASPAR database predicted several putative HIF-1α binding motifs within the NREP promoter region (Fig. 3C). To verify this prediction, dual-luciferase reporter assays were performed in HSFBs. HIF-1α overexpression significantly increased the luciferase activity of the NREP-WT construct but had no effect on the NREP-MUT construct lacking the predicted binding site (p < 0.01, Fig. 3D).

Moreover, ChIP assays confirmed direct physical interaction between HIF-1α and the NREP promoter. Specifically, HIF-1α enrichment in the NREP promoter region was markedly elevated under hypoxic conditions, whereas HIF-1α silencing markedly diminished this enrichment (p < 0.05, Fig. 3E).

Together, these results demonstrate that HIF-1α directly binds to the NREP promoter to enhance its transcription, thereby upregulating NREP expression in HS fibroblasts.

Discussion

The present study identified a previously uncharacterized regulatory mechanism in HS pathogenesis, in which HIF-1α transcriptionally activates NREP, leading to TGF-β1/Smad pathway activation and subsequent fibroblast proliferation and collagen overproduction. Both in vivo and in vitro findings demonstrate that HIF-1α and NREP are markedly upregulated in HS tissues and hypoxia-exposed HSFBs. Mechanistically, HIF-1α directly binds to the promoter region of NREP, enhancing its transcription and expression. Functionally, HIF-1α/NREP activation promotes HSFB proliferation and collagen deposition, whereas knockdown of either gene markedly attenuates these effects. These findings provide novel insights into the molecular cascade that drives aberrant fibroblast activation in HS and suggest that targeting the HIF-1α/NREP/TGF-β1 axis represents a promising therapeutic strategy to suppress fibroproliferative remodeling.

Consistent with our data, HIF-1α upregulation has been widely implicated in pathological scar formation. Clinical studies have shown that increased HIF-1α expression is burn-related HS tissues, particularly in upper-limb lesions, and similar upregulation in dermal fibroblasts [13]. Besides, NREP is preferentially expressed in dermal fibroblast-like cells derived from HS tissues [29]. Hypoxia, a hallmark of the wound microenvironment, is known to induce fibroblast proliferation, metabolic reprogramming, and collagen synthesis, all central processes in scar formation [3, 30, 31]. The current study confirmed that hypoxia significantly enhanced HSFB proliferation and collagen deposition, whereas HIF-1α knockdown suppressed these effects, consistent with previous reports demonstrating that HIF-1α activation promotes fibroblast hyperproliferation and extracellular matrix accumulation [13, 23, 27, 32].

Parallel to HIF-1α, NREP also emerged as a key mediator of fibroblast activation. NREP was found to be highly expressed in HS-derived fibroblast-like cells, and its overexpression in dermal fibroblasts promotes cell proliferation and upregulates fibrogenic markers such as alpha-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) and COL-I [29]. These proteins are canonical indicators of myofibroblast differentiation and excessive matrix deposition [33–35]. Our findings expand upon these earlier observations by demonstrating that NREP functions downstream of HIF-1α and amplifies hypoxia-driven fibrotic activation through TGF-β1/Smad activation.

Mounting evidence indicates that HIF-1α serves as a master regulator of cellular adaptation to hypoxia, primarily through transcriptional upregulation of downstream genes that support metabolic and structural remodeling under low-oxygen conditions [36, 37]. In the present study, we demonstrated that HIF-1α directly binds to the promoter region of NREP, thereby enhancing its transcriptional activity. This finding aligns with prior observations that inhibition of HIF-1α reduces NREP mRNA levels in murine epidermal stem cells under hypoxic conditions [38]. Similarly, Ruan et al. provided compelling evidence via luciferase reporter and ChIP assays that HIF-1α interacts directly with the NREP promoter, leading to elevated NREP transcription and expression [18]. Extending these findings to the HS context, our data showed that ectopic overexpression of NREP partially rescued the inhibitory effects of HIF-1α knockdown on fibroblast proliferation and collagen deposition. These results collectively suggest that the HIF-1α/NREP axis is a key mediator of hypoxia-driven fibrotic activation and may represent a promising therapeutic target for controlling fibroblast overactivity in HS.

To further dissect the downstream signaling mechanisms, we identified that HIF-1α upregulates NREP, which subsequently promotes activation of the TGF-β1/Smad pathway, resulting in enhanced HSFB proliferation and collagen accumulation. This mechanistic cascade is consistent with reports in other pathological contexts: HIF-1α has been shown to function as an upstream activator of the TGF-β1/Smad3 pathway, where elevated HIF-1α levels increase both TGF-β1 and Smad3 expression in breast cancer cells [39], while silencing HIF-1α suppresses this pathway [40]. In addition, NREP silencing has been demonstrated to reduce TGF-β1 and phosphorylated Smad3 levels in ATDC5 chondrocytes [41], supporting the notion that NREP positively regulates the TGF-β1/Smad signaling axis.

The TGF-β1/Smad pathway plays a pivotal role in HS formation and progression. Its activation promotes fibroblast proliferation and excessive collagen matrix synthesis, which are hallmarks of hypertrophic scarring [28, 42]. Recent studies have further demonstrated the involvement of the TGF-β1/Smad2/3 pathway in the regulation of skin fibrosis. For instance, this pathway activation induces hyperproliferation of dermal fibroblasts and myofibroblast transition, while its inhibition partly contributes to the role of acetoacetate in rescuing skin fibrosis [43]. Notably, mast cell chymase can activate this pathway to accelerate HSFB proliferation and collagen production [42], whereas pharmacological or genetic inhibition of TGF-β1/Smad signaling markedly reduces fibroblast contractility, scar elevation, and collagen density, restoring a more organized collagen fiber architecture [44]. To corroborate the pathological role of this pathway in HS, our findings additionally validated that suppression of the TGF-β1/Smad pathway using SB-431542 mitigated the pathological phenotypes induced by HIF-1α/NREP activation in HSFBs. Taken together, our data support a mechanistic model in which HIF-1α transcriptionally activates NREP to enhance TGF-β1/Smad signaling, thereby amplifying fibroblast proliferation and collagen deposition, the core drivers of HS formation.

In summary, the present study reveals that the HIF-1α/NREP signaling axis promotes HSFB proliferation and collagen deposition primarily through activation of the TGF-β1/Smad pathway. These findings uncover a novel molecular mechanism underlying the aberrant fibroblast activation and excessive extracellular matrix accumulation characteristic of hypertrophic scarring. By establishing a direct transcriptional link between HIF-1α and NREP, this work extends current understanding of hypoxia-mediated fibrotic regulation and identifies a potential therapeutic target for the prevention or treatment of HS.

Despite these advances, several limitations warrant consideration. First, although our data demonstrate activation of the HIF-1α/NREP/TGF-β1 axis in HS tissues compared with adjacent NS, we did not include other scar types, such as normotrophic or atrophic scars, for comparison. Consequently, the upregulation observed in HS may primarily reflect an enhanced wound repair response relative to uninjured tissue. Future studies incorporating comparative analyses across different scar phenotypes are needed to determine whether this axis represents a specific biomarker for hypertrophic scarring or a broader marker of fibroproliferative remodeling. Second, the mechanistic interplay between HIF-1α/NREP and the TGF-β1/Smad pathway, particularly the specific mechanisms by which HIF-1α/NREP regulates the TGF-β1/Smad pathway, remains incompletely defined. While our in vitro findings indicate that HIF-1α transcriptionally upregulates NREP to potentiate TGF-β1/Smad activation based on functional rescue experiments, further investigation is required to delineate the transcriptional regulation of the TGF-β1 promoter by NREP. Thus, the direct interaction between NREP and the TGF-β1 promoter might be a focus for future research. Finally, the absence of in vivo validation experiments constitutes a notable limitation. Establishing animal models of hypertrophic scarring that recapitulate human fibroproliferative features will be essential to confirm the therapeutic potential of targeting the HIF-1α/NREP axis in scar attenuation. Beyond the limitations above, it is worth noting that skin fibrosis, a contributor to excessive scarring, can also be influenced by metabolic disorders. For example, enhanced homocysteine metabolism upon folate supplementation has been shown as a protective mechanism against skin fibrosis [45], highlighting the complexity of fibrotic regulation. Future work integrating in vivo gene modulation, pharmacologic inhibition, and transcriptomic profiling will help an in-depth understanding of associated mechanisms and accelerate the development of novel antifibrotic strategies for clinical application.
