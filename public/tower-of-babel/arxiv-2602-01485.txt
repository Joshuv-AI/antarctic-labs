Predicting and improving test-time scaling laws via reward tail-guided search


Abstract
Test-time scaling has emerged as a critical avenue for enhancing the reasoning capabilities of Large Language Models (LLMs). Though the straight-forward “best-of- N N ” (BoN) strategy has already demonstrated significant improvements in performance, it lacks principled guidance on the choice of N N , budget allocation, and multi-stage decision-making, thereby leaving substantial room for optimization. While many works have explored such optimization, rigorous theoretical guarantees remain limited. In this work, we propose new methodologies to predict and improve scaling properties via tail-guided search. By estimating the tail distribution of rewards, our method predicts the scaling law of LLMs without the need for exhaustive evaluations. Leveraging this prediction tool, we introduce Scaling-Law Guided (SLG) Search, a new test-time algorithm that dynamically allocates compute to identify and exploit intermediate states with the highest predicted potential. We theoretically prove that SLG achieves vanishing regret compared to perfect-information oracles, and achieves expected rewards that would otherwise require a polynomially larger compute budget required when using BoN. Empirically, we validate our framework across different LLMs and reward models, confirming that tail-guided allocation consistently achieves higher reward yields than Best-of- N N under identical compute budgets. Our code is available at https://github.com/PotatoJnny/Scaling-Law-Guided-search .

Introduction

The past years have seen remarkable advancements in the capabilities of Large Language Models (LLMs), particularly in complex reasoning tasks
(
28
;
5
)
. A key driver of these improvements has been the adoption of test-time scaling strategies, which leverage the stochastic nature of LLM outputs to enhance performance without necessitating further model training
(
33
;
20
;
4
)
. Among these strategies, the “best-of-
N
” (BoN) approach has emerged as a simple yet effective method. By generating multiple outputs and selecting the one with the highest reward according to a predefined metric, BoN has demonstrated significant performance gains across various tasks
(
19
;
7
)
.

Despite its empirical success, the application of BoN strategies still faces several challenges. On the one hand, while it is known that increasing the number of samples
N
generally leads to better performance, the precise relationship between
N
and expected reward remains poorly understood. This gap in understanding hinders the ability to make informed decisions about resource allocation, especially in scenarios where computational budgets are constrained. On the other hand, the BoN strategy does not account for the potential benefits of adaptive sampling, where intermediate outputs could inform subsequent sampling decisions.

The answers to these challenges lie in the ability to predict scaling laws governing the performance of BoN strategies, and to develop algorithms that can effectively leverage these predictions. For example, in training-time settings, explicit scaling laws have been established, linking model, data and compute budget to performance metrics
(
15
;
12
)
. These laws are used to guide resource allocation and model design, and have been theoretically justified under various setups
21
;
3
. By way of contrast, analogous scaling laws for test-time strategies like BoN remain underexplored, both empirically and theoretically.

In this work, we address these gaps by introducing a novel framework for predicting test-time scaling laws of BoN via tail extrapolation of reward distributions. Building on this predictive capability, we further propose Scaling-Law Guided (SLG) Search, an adaptive algorithm that dynamically allocates computational resources based on predicted rewards. Our contribution includes

•

A new method for predicting the best-of-
N
scaling behavior with only
m\ll N
samples, by modeling the tail distribution of rewards and extrapolating to larger
N
.

•

A scaling-law guided (SLG) search algorithm that utilizes predicted scaling laws to adaptively allocate sampling budgets in two reasoning tasks. We theoretically show that SLG achieves vanishing regret compared to perfect-information oracles, and attains expected rewards that would otherwise require polynomially larger compute budgets under BoN.

•

Our empirical evaluation across various LLMs and reward models demonstrates that our tail-guided allocation consistently outperforms BoN under identical compute budgets.

Test-time scaling laws.

Recent research has established inference-time compute as a scalable dimension of model performance, distinct from pre-training parameters.
Empirical studies have demonstrated that quality of final outputs improves as test-time budget increases

Regarding the factors governing these laws,

Subsequent theoretical works have sought to explain these phenomena, typically by modeling the relationship between solution coverage and budget

Our work contributes to this theoretical lineage by constructing a more accurate statistical model based on distributional tail property to explain and predict test-time scaling laws.

Test-Time Search Algorithms.

Existing search strategies range from iterative refinement to complex structured exploration. Classical techniques like self-refinement
(
27
;
14
)
improve reasoning loops without relying on external signals.
More recent approaches integrate explicit reward models to guide this search, utilizing uncertainty-aware verification
(
38
)
or generative feedback (“PRMs that think”)
(

)

At the most complex end of the spectrum, methods like Tree of Thoughts (ToT)
(

(

(

However, these methods typically rely on dense supervision from trained Process Reward Models (PRMs) or estimate state values using the empirical mean of lookahead rollouts which lacks theoretical guarantees.
In contrast, we propose a novel search algorithm that leverages predicted scaling laws to guide resource allocation, offering a framework with provable performance guarantees.

Problem setup

We formulate the test-time alignment problem as a decision-making task under a fixed computational budget.

Generation process and states.

We model the generation process as a trajectory
x=s_{0}\to s_{1}\to\dots\to s_{k}\to y
, where
x
is the initial prompt,
s_{i}
represents an
intermediate state
(e.g., a reasoning step, partial plan, or draft segment), and
y
is the final terminal response.
From any given state
s
, the language model follows a stochastic policy
\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot|s)
to generate the subsequent state or terminate at a response
y
.
Our objective is to identify the generation trajectory that yields the highest reward response
y^{*}
subject to a total computational budget.

Reward distribution and value function.

We assume access to a reward function
r(y,x)
that evaluates the quality of a complete response
y
.
For any intermediate state
s
(including the prompt
x
), the stochastic policy induces a distribution over potential future outcomes. Let
Y_{s}
denote a random final response obtained by rolling out the policy
\pi_{\text{ref}}
from state
s
until termination.
We denote the distribution of its reward as
R_{s}=r(Y_{s},x)\sim F_{s}
, and refer to
F_{s}
as the
reward distribution
associated with state
s
.

To quantify the potential of a state
s
, we define the
value function

V_{N}(s)
as the expected maximum reward achievable by dedicating a sampling budget of
N
to that state:

V_{N}(s):=\mathbb{E}\Big[\max_{1\leqslant i\leqslant N}R_{s}^{(i)}\Big],\quad\text{where }R_{s}^{(i)}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}F_{s}.

(1)

Intuitively,
V_{N}(s)
measures the “scaling potential" of the state
s
: it is the expected best outcome if we were to dedicate a budget of
N
to exploring it.

Prediction and search objectives.

We can formulate the test-time alignment task under a compute budget using the above framework. We consider the following two problems.

(1) The prediction problem.
Given a state
s
and a target budget
N
, our first objective is to estimate the scaling behavior for state
s
using only a small amount of
m
samples. Concretely, we seek to construct an estimator
\widehat{V}_{N}(s)
that approximates
the value function
V_{N}(s)
using only
m\ll N
i.i.d. reward samples
\mathcal{D}=\{r_{1},r_{2},\dots,r_{m}\}
from the underlying distribution
F_{s}
.

(2) The search problem.
Given an initial prompt
x
and a total budget
N
, our second objective is to design an algorithm that optimizes the expected reward of the maximal reward response generated within the budget. In particular, within one unit of budget, an algorithm
\mathcal{A}
can sample from any states already generated (including the initial prompt). The decision of which state to sample next can depend on all previously observed states and their sampled rewards. The expected reward of algorithm
\mathcal{A}
under budget
N
is defined as

V_{N}(\mathcal{A};x):=\mathbb{E}\Big[\max_{y\in\mathcal{Y}_{\text{out}}}r(y,x)\Big],

(2)

where
\mathcal{Y}_{\text{out}}
is the set of final responses generated by
\mathcal{A}
given the initial prompt
x
and budget
N
.
The goal is to find an algorithm
\mathcal{A}
that maximizes
V_{N}(\mathcal{A};x)
. When it is clear from context, we will abbreviate
V_{N}(\mathcal{A};x)
as
V_{N}(\mathcal{A})
.

For the rest of the paper, we first address the prediction problem in
Section

3
, and then leverage the resulting estimator to design a
resource-efficient search algorithm in
Section

4
.

3
Predicting scaling laws via tail extrapolation

Based on above setup, we now present our method for predicting the scaling behavior of
V_{N}(s)
for large
N
using only
m\ll N
samples. The key insight is that the behavior of the maximum reward is primarily influenced by the tail of the reward distribution. Therefore, accurately modeling the tail allows us to extrapolate
V_{N}(s)
to larger
N
.

In this section, we first introduce the tail assumption underlying our approach, supported by empirical evidence. We then describe a method to estimate the tail parameters from observed samples, and finally present theoretical guarantees on the accuracy of our scaling law predictions.

3.1
Tail assumption and empirical validation

As the prediction problem of test-time scaling laws requires estimating reward values beyond the observed sample range, it necessitates modeling assumptions on the reward distribution’s upper tail behavior.

An empirical observation:

We start by empirically analyzing the reward distributions obtained from LLM-generated responses. By collecting the reward distributions for various prompts and models, we observe consistent patterns in their tail behavior. In
Figure

1
, we present the histograms of reward samples (
N=5000
) from two representative examples.
It can be observed that though the main body of the reward distribution is often skewed, the tail aligns closely with a truncated Gaussian distribution. The goodness-of-fit analysis and the Q-Q plots also support this observation, which is provided in Appendix
A
.

N=5000

(Left)
The standard density histograms show that the distribution body may contain irregularities or deviations from a perfect bell curve.

(Right)

Motivated by these findings, we consider the following Gaussian tail assumption.

Assumption 1

(Gaussian tail)
.

Let
F_{s}
be the cumulative distribution function of the reward distribution at state
s
, and
r_{\alpha}:=F_{s}^{-1}(1-\alpha)
be the threshold for a tail probability
\alpha\in(0,1)
.
We assume that for all
r\geqslant r_{\alpha}
, the reward density coincides with a Gaussian probability density function:

p_{s}(r)=\phi(r;\mu,\sigma^{2}).

(3)

where
\phi(\cdot;\mu,\sigma^{2})
denotes the PDF of a normal distribution with mean
\mu
and variance
\sigma^{2}
.
Furthermore, we assume the second moment bound
\mathbb{E}[R_{s}^{2}]\leqslant C_{R}
.

Under Assumption
1
, the full reward density
p_{s}(r)
can be viewed as a mixture:

p_{s}(r)=\begin{cases}p_{\text{body}}(r)&\text{if }r<r_{\alpha}\\
\phi(r;\mu,\sigma^{2})&\text{if }r\geqslant r_{\alpha}\end{cases}

and we impose no constraints on the distribution’s body, allowing the majority of samples to follow an arbitrary
p_{\text{body}}
.

Under Assumption
1
, the behavior of
V_{N}(s)
for large
N
is governed entirely by the tail parameters
\mu
and
\sigma
. We now present a method to estimate these parameters and provide theoretical guarantees for the estimation error.

3.2
Statistical estimation of scaling laws

We employ the Method of Moments (MoM) on the tail statistics.
Let
\phi
and
\Phi
denote the PDF and CDF of the standard normal distribution, respectively.
Standard results for the truncated normal distribution link the observed tail moments to the generative parameters:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[R\mid R\geqslant R_{\alpha}]

\displaystyle=\mu+\sigma\lambda(z_{\alpha}),

(4a)

\displaystyle\text{Var}[R\mid R\geqslant R_{\alpha}]

\displaystyle=\sigma^{2}\left(1+z_{\alpha}\lambda(z_{\alpha})-\lambda(z_{\alpha})^{2}\right),

(4b)

where
z_{\alpha}=\Phi^{-1}(1-\alpha)
and
\lambda(z)=\phi(z)/(1-\Phi(z))
is the inverse Mills ratio.
By inverting these equations, we derive estimates for
\mu
and
\sigma
based on the empirical tail mean and variance, and thus
predict
V_{N}(s)
via extrapolation. The complete procedure is detailed in Algorithm
1
.

\hat{\sigma}=\sqrt{\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{\text{tail}}/{\delta(z)}},\quad\hat{\mu}=\hat{\mu}_{\text{tail}}-\hat{\sigma}\lambda(z)

3.2.1
Application to resource allocation

Let us illustrate one application of our scaling law estimator
\hat{V}_{N}(s)
in adaptive resource allocation of computational budget across multiple prompts.
30
explores this direction by estimating scaling laws using kernel density estimation (KDE) and demonstrates empirical improvements over uniform allocation. However, it is well known that KDE methods do not extrapolate well beyond the observed sample range (see e.g.
34
). In contrast, our tail-based method provides robust theoretical guarantees for extrapolation, making it more suitable for high-budget predictions.

Specifically, we consider a total budget of
n_{\text{total}}
to be allocated across
K
prompts
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{K}
. The goal is to maximize the weighted sum of expected maximum rewards across all prompts, for a given set of weights
\{w_{i}\}_{i=1}^{K}
.

\displaystyle\max_{\{n_{i}\}_{i=1}^{K}}\sum_{i=1}^{K}w_{i}V_{n_{i}}(x_{i})\quad\text{s.t.}\sum_{i=1}^{K}n_{i}\leqslant n_{\text{total}},~n_{i}\in\mathbb{N}_{+}.

(6)

Using our scaling law estimator
\hat{V}_{n}(x_{i})
, we can first allocate a small pilot budget
n_{\text{pilot}}\ll n_{\text{total}}
to estimate the scaling laws for each prompt, each using
n_{\text{pilot}}/K
samples. We can then solve the above optimization problem (
6
) by replacing
V_{n_{i}}(x_{i})
with the estimated values
\hat{V}_{n_{i}}(x_{i})
. Note that under our tail assumption,
\hat{V}_{n_{i}}(x_{i})
is a concave function in
n_{i}
. By relaxing the integer constraint, the resulting problem becomes a convex optimization that can be solved efficiently using standard methods. Finally, we round the solution to obtain integer allocations.

Scaling-Law Guided search for test-time alignment

Having established a method to predict the scaling potential of a single state in Section
3
, we now address the search problem defined in Section
2
.
Specifically, we focus on the two-stage generation setting, where the model first generates an intermediate state
s
from the prompt
x
, and then produces a final response
y
based on
s
. As defined in Section
2
,
the state
s
can be interpreted as a chain of thought, a partial response, or one mathematical step.

We recall the search problem from Section
2
, where we are given a total budget
N
to allocate between generating new intermediate states
s
from the prompt
x
and sampling final responses
y
from these states.
The objective is to maximize the expected maximum reward of the final responses generated within this budget.
To design an efficient search strategy,
we leverage the scaling law prediction derived in Section
3
. We begin by extending the local tail assumption to the global state space.

Assumption 2
.

We assume Assumption
1
holds for all candidate states
s
with uniform constants
\alpha
and
C_{R}
. Furthermore, we assume that the tail parameters satisfy
\sigma(s)\in[\sigma_{\min},\sigma_{\max}]
almost surely for some constants
0<\sigma_{\min}\leqslant\sigma_{\max}<\infty

, and the latent means
\mu(s)
have a finite second moment
\mathbb{E}[\mu(s)^{2}]\leqslant M_{\mu}
.

Since the intermediate states

are generated by a stochastic LLM policy conditioned on the prompt

, their associated reward parameters
\mu(s)

are inherently random variables. Assumption
2
imposes mild regularity conditions on this induced distribution: the bounds on
\sigma(s)

\mu(s)
ensures the distribution of state qualities remains well-behaved.

4.1
Scaling-Law Guided search algorithm

Leveraging the scaling law derived in Section
3
, we propose an algorithm that dynamically allocates the budget
N
between exploration (finding good states) and exploitation (resampling the best state), as outlined in Algorithm

Specifically, the algorithm uses an estimation budget

It is important to note that the SLG algorithm is highly parallelizable – the exploration phase can be executed concurrently across all

4.2
Theoretical results

Recall that the performance of a search algorithm
\mathcal{A}
is measured by the expected reward defined in
Equation (
2
), which we restate here for clarity:

\displaystyle V_{N}(\mathcal{A}):=\mathbb{E}\Big[\max_{y\in\mathcal{Y}_{\text{out}}}r(y,x)\Big]

where the set
\mathcal{Y}_{\text{out}}
consists of final responses generated by
\mathcal{A}
using budget

.

To contextualize the performance of our SLG algorithm, we introduce two reference strategies for comparison.

Reference strategies.

First, we consider a theoretical performance ceiling that operates with perfect knowledge:

Definition 1

(Full-information oracle
\mathcal{A}^{*}
)
.

The oracle
\mathcal{A}^{*}
first samples a pool of
N

\mathcal{S}_{N}=\{s_{1},\dots,s_{N}\}
from the prompt
x
. It then identifies the optimal state

using perfect distributional information and dedicates a budget of
N
to sampling from it.

In other words, the oracle
\mathcal{A}^{*}
chooses the best state among
N

\mathcal{A}^{*}
is unattainable in practice as it assumes zero-cost evaluation of the tail parameters. Replicating this strategy blindly would require a budget of at least
2N
(generating
N

N

Second, we define the standard Best-of-

Definition 2

(Best-of-

)
.

The Best-of-
N

(equivalent to sampling from a random mixture of states) without any intermediate selection.

We will use the oracle
\mathcal{A}^{*}
to bound the

(Section

4.2.1
Regret Analysis

We now establish the theoretical guarantees of the SLG algorithm. We first derive a lower bound on the performance of SLG compared to the full-information oracle
\mathcal{A}^{*}
under a slightly decreased budget.

Theorem 2
.

Under Assumption
2
, consider the SLG algorithm configured with estimation sample size
m
and search width
K=\lfloor N/2m\rfloor
, consuming a total budget of
N
.
Then, for
N\geqslant 4(m+1)
and
m\geqslant c_{0}\log N
for some constant

, we have:

V_{N}(\mathcal{A}_{\text{SLG}})\geqslant V_{\lfloor N/2m\rfloor}(\mathcal{A}^{*})-c\frac{\log N}{\sqrt{m}}

where
c
is a constant depending on model parameters

(\alpha,\sigma_{\min}
,
\sigma_{\max},C_{R},M_{\mu})
.

See Appendix
D
for the proof of Theorem
2
.

Vanishing Regret.

By setting
m\asymp(\log N)^{\alpha}
with
\alpha>2
, SLG achieves the same performance as
\mathcal{A}^{*}
with a slightly smaller budget
\mathcal{O}(N/(\log N)^{\alpha})
, while the estimation error term vanishes as
\mathcal{O}((\log N)^{1-\alpha/2})
.
Under practical settings, this budget reduction is negligible.
For light-tailed rewards, the oracle
\mathcal{A}^{*}
typically satisfies a scaling law of the form
V_{N}(\mathcal{A}^{*})\approx C(\log N)^{\gamma}
for some
\gamma\in(0,1)
.
1
1

1

For instance, the BoN baseline satisfies this with
\gamma=1/2
, as shown in Section
3
.

Consequently, the performance loss due to the logarithmic budget reduction scales as:

\displaystyle V_{N}(\mathcal{A}^{*})-V_{\frac{N}{\log^{\alpha}N}}(\mathcal{A}^{*})\asymp\frac{\log\log N}{\log^{1-\gamma}N},

which diminishes as
N
increases. This implies that the gap between SLG and
\mathcal{A}^{*}
operating at the
same
budget
N
also vanishes. Specifically, by choosing
m\asymp(\log N)^{3}
, we have:

V_{N}(\mathcal{A}^{*})-V_{N}(\mathcal{A}_{\text{SLG}})\leqslant\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log\log N}{\log^{1-\gamma}N}\right).

(7)

We provide the formal statement and proof of this result under mild regularity conditions in Appendix

4.2.2
Comparison to Best-of-N baseline

To explicitly quantify the performance gain of the SLG algorithm, we apply our general framework to a concrete Gaussian example.
This instantiation allows us to derive sharp, non-asymptotic bounds on the advantage gap over the Best-of-N baseline (Definition
2
).

Suppose the reward distributions for all intermediate states
are Gaussian
p_{s}(r)=\phi(r;\mu(s),\sigma_{1})
with a fixed noise level
\sigma_{1}>0
, and the latent mean parameters are drawn from a Gaussian distribution
\mu(s)\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{0},\sigma_{0}^{2})

6
Discussion

In this work, we established a principled framework for test-time scaling that bridges the gap between statistical prediction and algorithmic search.
We first introduced a methodology to predict the scaling laws of Best-of-
N
performance via tail extrapolation, allowing us to estimate the “scaling potential” of intermediate states without exhaustive sampling.
Building on this predictive capability, we designed the Scaling-Law Guided (SLG) search, an algorithm that utilizes these forecasts to dynamically allocate compute resources.
Both our theoretical analysis and empirical results confirm that this tail-guided approach acts as a resource amplifier, consistently achieving higher rewards than the Best-of-
N
baseline under identical compute budgets.

We view this work as a first step toward a rigorous theory of test-time scaling. Promising directions for future research include: (1) generalizing our framework to recursive settings like MCTS to guide pruning; (2) applying these principles to broader agentic workflows such as code generation; and (3) incorporating the error structure of imperfect reward models into scaling-law predictions.

C.2
Bias Error

We can establish a lemma bounding the approximation bias
|V_{N}(s)-M(N)|

differs from the Gaussian distribution in the "body" of the distribution (

Lemma 4
.

Under Assumption
1
, the difference between the true expected maximum
V_{N}(s)
and the Gaussian expected maximum
M(N)
satisfies:

|V_{N}(s)-M(N)|\leqslant c(1-\alpha/2)^{N},

(9)

where
c
is a finite constant depending only on
(\alpha,\sigma,C_{R})
.

Proof of Lemma
4
.

Let
F(r)
be the CDF of the true reward distribution and
G(r)=\Phi(r;\mu,\sigma^{2})
be the CDF of the Gaussian approximation. And we have:

V_{N}(s)-M(N)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(G(r)^{N}-F(r)^{N})dr.

By Assumption
1
, the CDFs are identical for
r\geqslant r_{\alpha/2}

F(r)=G(r),\quad\forall r\geqslant r_{\alpha/2},

which implies that the integral over
[r_{\alpha/2},\infty)
is zero. Therefore, we only need to consider the integral over
(-\infty,r_{\alpha/2}]

|V_{N}(s)-M(N)|=\left|\int_{-\infty}^{r_{\alpha/2}}(G(r)^{N}-F(r)^{N})dr\right|\leqslant\int_{-\infty}^{r_{\alpha/2}}|G(r)^{N}-F(r)^{N}|dr.

(10)

For
r\leqslant r_{\alpha/2}
, both CDFs are bounded by
1-\alpha/2
. Then we can further bound the integral:

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{r_{\alpha/2}}|G(r)^{N}-F(r)^{N}|dr

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{-\infty}^{r_{\alpha/2}}(G(r)^{N}+F(r)^{N})dr

\displaystyle\leqslant(1-\alpha/2)^{N-1}\int_{-\infty}^{r_{\alpha/2}}(G(r)+F(r))dr.

Let
c=\int_{-\infty}^{r_{\alpha/2}}(G(r)+F(r))dr
. By the change of variable
u=r-\mu
, we observe that the integral depends only on the centered distributions and the centered threshold
r_{\alpha/2}-\mu
.
Thus,

is independent of the location parameter
\mu
. Finally, Assumption
1
guarantees finite second moments (
\mathbb{E}[R^{2}]<C_{R}
),
which implies
\mathbb{E}[|R|]<\infty
and ensures the integral converges to a finite constant which only depends on
C_{R}
and
\sigma
. This completes the proof.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[M\cdot\mathbb{I}(E)]

\mathcal{A}^{*}
operating at the same budget

We first introduce the necessary regularity conditions on the latent parameter distributions.

Assumption 3

For random state

(\mu(s),\sigma(s))

1.

\mu(s)

The expected maximum of the
\mu(s)

And we have the following corollary of Theorem
2

Corollary 2

for sufficiently large

by choosing the parameters

m\asymp(\log N)^{3}

\displaystyle V_{N}(\mathcal{A}^{*})-V_{N}(\mathcal{A}_{\text{SLG}})\leqslant\frac{c\log\log N}{(\log N)^{\min\{\beta,1-\gamma,1/2\}}}.

depending on the model parameters
(\alpha,\sigma_{\min},\sigma_{\max},C_{R},M_{\mu})

While Assumption

posits independence between
\mu(s)
and
\sigma(s)
for analytical simplicity, this condition can be relaxed.
As shown in the proof provided in next section,
\mathcal{A}_{\text{SLG}}
predominantly selects states with variance near

(Condition 3) holds

Conditions (2) and (3) are mild regularity conditions and are satisfied by many standard distributions. For instance, if the means
\mu(s)
follow a Gaussian distribution and the variances
\sigma(s)
are uniformly distributed, then the regularity conditions hold with
\alpha=1
,
\gamma=1/2
, and any
\beta>0
.

We provide the proof of Corollary
2
in next section.

E.1
Proof of Corollary
2

To help us evaluate the difference between
\mathcal{A}_{\text{SLG}}
with exploration width
K
and the full-information oracle
\mathcal{A}^{*}
, we first define an intermediate
oracle algorithm
\hat{\mathcal{A}}
that knows the true expected maximal reward
V_{N}(s)
for each candidate state
s
but only selects among the
K
candidates sampled beforehand.

Definition 3
.

The Oracle
\hat{\mathcal{A}}
first samples a pool of
K
candidate states
\mathcal{S}_{K}=\{s_{1},\dots,s_{K}\}
from the prompt
x
. It then identifies the optimal state
s^{*}=\operatorname*{argmax}_{s\in\mathcal{S}_{K}}V_{N}(s)
using perfect parameter information and dedicates the entire budget
N
to sampling from it.

Then we can compare the performance of
\mathcal{A}_{\text{SLG}}
with
\hat{\mathcal{A}}
first, which is shown in the following lemma.

Lemma 6
.

Under Assumptions
2
,
let
N
be the total sampling budget and
m
be the estimation sample size.
When we choose the sample size scales as
m=c_{0}\left(\frac{N}{K}\right)^{2/3}\log(N)
for some constant
c_{0}>0
,
the performance gap between
\mathcal{A}_{\text{SLG}}
and the oracle
\hat{\mathcal{A}}
satisfies:

V_{N}(\hat{\mathcal{A}})-V_{N}(\mathcal{A}_{\text{SLG}})\leqslant C\left(\frac{K}{N}\right)^{1/3}\sqrt{\log N}

(11)

where
C
is a constant depending on
(\sigma_{min},\sigma_{max},\alpha,C_{R},M_{\mu})
.

Proof of Lemma
6
.

We apply the same coupling argument as in the proof of Theorem
2
.
Let
\xi=(s_{1},s_{2},\dots)
be an infinite sequence of states sampled from the parameter prior distribution,
and we condition on a fixed realization of
\tau
.

Conditioned on
\xi
, we know that the states sampled by Scaling-Law Guided(SLG) algorithm and the oracle algorithm
\hat{\mathcal{A}}
are the same, and we denote

s^{*}
as the optimal state with the highest true expected maximal reward among the
K
candidates, i.e.,
s^{*}=\arg\max_{i\in[K]}V_{N}(s_{i})
,
and
\hat{s}
as the state selected by SLG algorithm based on the estimated expected maximal reward, i.e.,
\hat{s}=\arg\max_{i\in[K]}\hat{V}_{N}(s_{i})
.

Then we can decompose the performance gap between SLG and the oracle algorithm
\hat{\mathcal{A}}
as follows:

\displaystyle V_{N}(\hat{\mathcal{A}})-V_{N}(\mathcal{A}_{\text{SLG}})

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbb{E}[V_{N}(s^{*})-V_{N-Km}(\hat{s})]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[V_{N}(s^{*})-V_{N}(\hat{s})]+\mathbb{E}[V_{N}(\hat{s})-V_{N-Km}(\hat{s})]

where the inequality holds because conditioned on
\tau
, the expected
reward of SLG algorithm is lower bounded by the expected maximal reward of the selected state
\hat{s}
with
N-Km
samples.

For the first term, using the same technique as in the proof of Theorem
2
and by applying Lemma
5
, we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[V_{N}(s^{*})-V_{N}(\hat{s})]\leqslant C_{1}\sqrt{\frac{\log N\log K}{m}}

for some constant
C_{1}
depending on
(\sigma_{min},\sigma_{max},\alpha,C_{R},M_{\mu})
.

For the second term, we analyze the regret caused by the reduced sample size. Using the scaling law property, we have:

\displaystyle V_{N}(\hat{s})-V_{N-Km}(\hat{s})

\displaystyle=\sigma(\hat{s})(E(N)-E(N-Km))

\displaystyle\leqslant\sigma_{max}(E(N)-E(N-Km))

where
E(N)
is the expected maximum of
N
standard normal variables.
Since the above inequality holds for any fixed
\tau
, we can take expectation over
\tau
on both sides, and since
E(n)-E(m)\leqslant 2(\sqrt{\log n}-\sqrt{\log m})
for any
n>m\geqslant 3
, we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[V_{N}(\hat{s})-V_{N-Km}(\hat{s})\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\sigma_{max}(\sqrt{\log N}-\sqrt{\log(N-Km)})

\displaystyle\leqslant\sigma_{max}\frac{Km}{(N-Km)\sqrt{\log(N-Km)}}

And considering that
N-Km\geqslant\frac{N}{2}
, there exists a constant
C_{2}
depending on
\sigma_{max}
such that:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[V_{N}(\hat{s})-V_{N-Km}(\hat{s})\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant C_{2}\frac{K}{N\sqrt{\log N}}m

Combining the two parts, the total performance gap between
\hat{\mathcal{A}}
and
\mathcal{A}_{\text{SLG}}
is bounded by:

\displaystyle V_{N}(\hat{\mathcal{A}})-V_{N}(\mathcal{A}_{\text{SLG}})

\displaystyle\leqslant C_{1}\sqrt{\frac{\log N\log K}{m}}+C_{2}\frac{Km}{N\sqrt{\log N}}

To minimize the upper bound, we choose the sample size
m
to balance the order of the two terms, which suggests setting
m=c_{0}(N/K)^{2/3}\log N
for some constant
c_{0}>0
.
Substituting this choice of
m
back into the inequality yields:

\displaystyle V_{N}(\hat{\mathcal{A}})-V_{N}(\mathcal{A}_{\text{SLG}})

\displaystyle\leqslant C\left(\frac{K}{N}\right)^{1/3}(\log N)^{1/2}

(12)

where
C
is a universal constant depending on the problem parameters. This completes the proof.

∎

And then it suffices to compare the performance of
\hat{\mathcal{A}}
with the full-information oracle
\mathcal{A}^{*}
, which is shown in the following lemma.

Lemma 7
.

Under Assumption
2
and Assumption
3

, we can bound the performance gap between the full-information oracle
\mathcal{A}^{*}
and the restricted oracle
\hat{\mathcal{A}}
with
K\asymp N(\log N)^{-3}
as:

\displaystyle V_{N}(\mathcal{A}^{*})-V_{N}(\hat{\mathcal{A}})\leqslant C\frac{\log\log N}{(\log N)^{\min\{\beta,1-\gamma\}}}.

where
C
is a constant depending on
(\sigma_{min},\sigma_{max},\alpha,C_{R},M_{\mu},C_{\mu},\mu_{0},\beta,\gamma)
.

Proof of Lemma
7
.

Let
\mathcal{S}_{N}
denote the set of states sampled by the oracle strategy
\mathcal{A}^{*}
. We upper bound the performance of
\mathcal{A}^{*}
as follows:

\displaystyle V_{N}(\mathcal{A}^{*})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{s\in\mathcal{S}_{N}}V_{N}(s)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{s\in\mathcal{S}_{N}}\left(\mu(s)+\sigma(s)E(N)\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{s\in\mathcal{S}_{N}}\mu(s)\right]+E(N)\sigma_{\max}

\displaystyle\leqslant\mu_{0}+C_{\mu}(\log N)^{\gamma}+E(N)\sigma_{\max}+C(\log N)^{-\beta},

where
E(N)
is the expected maximum of
N
standard normal variables, and the last inequality follows from Assumption
3
.

To establish a lower bound for
\hat{\mathcal{A}}
, we consider a weaker oracle algorithm
\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{\delta}
for some
\delta\in(0,1)
, constructed as follows:

•

\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{\delta}
draws
K
states
\{s_{1},\dots,s_{K}\}
from the prior. It has access to the true values
\{V_{N}(s_{i})\}_{i=1}^{K}
.

•

Define the candidate set
\mathcal{S}_{\delta}=\{s_{i}\mid\sigma(s_{i})\geqslant\sigma_{\max}-\delta,\,i\in[K]\}
.
\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{\delta}
selects the state
s^{*}
that maximizes the value within
\mathcal{S}_{\delta}
, i.e.,
s^{*}=\arg\max_{s\in\mathcal{S}_{\delta}}V_{N}(s)
. If
\mathcal{S}_{\delta}
is empty, an arbitrary state is selected.

•

The algorithm samples
N
times from
s^{*}
and outputs the best response.

Since the search space of
\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{\delta}
is a subset of the space explored by
\hat{\mathcal{A}}
(with additional constraints),
we have
V_{N}(\hat{\mathcal{A}})\geqslant V_{N}(\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{\delta})
. Thus, it suffices to lower bound the performance of
\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{\delta}
.

By Assumption
3
, the probability that a sampled state has variance at least
\sigma_{\max}-\delta
is lower bounded by
\delta^{\alpha}
. This implies
\mathbb{E}[|\mathcal{S}_{\delta}|]\geqslant\delta^{\alpha}K
. Define the event
E=\{|\mathcal{S}_{\delta}|\geqslant\frac{1}{2}\delta^{\alpha}K\}
. By a standard Chernoff bound, we have:

\mathbb{P}(E)\geqslant 1-\exp(-C^{\prime}\delta^{\alpha}K),

for some constant
C^{\prime}>0
.

Since
\mu
and
\sigma
are independent under the prior, conditioning on
E
(which depends only on
\sigma
) does not alter the distribution of
\mu
for states in
\mathcal{S}_{\delta}
. We invoke the mean scaling assumption with effective sample size
|\mathcal{S}_{\delta}|
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{s\in\mathcal{S}_{\delta}}\mu(s)\mid E\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant\mu_{0}+C_{\mu}\left(\log(\delta^{\alpha}K/2)\right)^{\gamma}-C\left(\log(\delta^{\alpha}K/2)\right)^{-\beta}.

Conditioned on
E
, every state in
\mathcal{S}_{\delta}
satisfies
\sigma(s)\geqslant\sigma_{\max}-\delta
. We lower bound the expected value:

\displaystyle V_{N}(\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{\delta})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[R_{N}(\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{\delta})]\geqslant\mathbb{E}[R_{N}(\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{\delta})\mid E]\mathbb{P}(E)

\displaystyle\geqslant\left(\mu_{0}+C_{\mu}(\log(\delta^{\alpha}K/2))^{\gamma}-C(\log(\delta^{\alpha}K/2))^{-\beta}+E(N)(\sigma_{\max}-\delta)\right)\left(1-\exp(-C^{\prime}\delta^{\alpha}K)\right).

We set
\delta=(\log N)^{-2}
. Recalling that
K=C_{K}N(\log N)^{-3}
, the failure probability
\exp(-C^{\prime}\delta^{\alpha}K)
decays polynomially in
N
(specifically as
O(N^{-c})
for some
c>0
), which is negligible compared to polylogarithmic terms.

Let
P=\frac{N}{\delta^{\alpha}K/2}
. We have
\log P=O(\log\log N)
and
\log(\delta^{\alpha}K/2)=\log N-\log P
. For sufficiently large
N
, using the inequality
(1-x)^{\gamma}\geqslant 1-x
for
x\in[0,1)
and
\gamma<1
, the lower bound simplifies to:

\displaystyle V_{N}(\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{\delta})

\displaystyle\geqslant\mu_{0}+C_{\mu}(\log N)^{\gamma}\left(1-\frac{\log P}{\log N}\right)^{\gamma}-C^{\prime\prime}(\log N)^{-\beta}+E(N)\sigma_{\max}-z_{N}\delta-O(N^{-c})

\displaystyle\geqslant\mu_{0}+C_{\mu}(\log N)^{\gamma}\left(1-\frac{\log P}{\log N}\right)-C^{\prime\prime}(\log N)^{-\beta}+E(N)\sigma_{\max}-\frac{z_{N}}{(\log N)^{2}}-O(N^{-c}).

Subtracting this from the upper bound of
V_{N}(\mathcal{A}^{*})
, we obtain:

\displaystyle V_{N}(\mathcal{A}^{*})-V_{N}(\hat{\mathcal{A}})

\displaystyle\leqslant V_{N}(\mathcal{A}^{*})-V_{N}(\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{\delta})

\displaystyle\leqslant C_{\mu}(\log N)^{\gamma-1}\log P+\frac{E(N)}{(\log N)^{2}}+C(\log N)^{-\beta}+O(N^{-c}).

Using the facts that
\log P=O(\log\log N)
and
E(N)\leqslant\sqrt{2\log N}
, the gap is dominated by:

\displaystyle V_{N}(\mathcal{A}^{*})-V_{N}(\hat{\mathcal{A}})

\displaystyle\leqslant O\left(\frac{\log\log N}{(\log N)^{1-\gamma}}\right)+O\left(\frac{1}{(\log N)^{3/2}}\right)+O\left(\frac{1}{(\log N)^{\beta}}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant C\frac{\log\log N}{(\log N)^{\min\{\beta,1-\gamma\}}},

for some constant
C>0
.
∎

Then we are ready to prove Corollary
2
.

Proof of Corollary
2
.

We first decompose the performance gap between
\mathcal{A}^{*}
and Scaling-Law Guided (SLG) algorithm as follows:

\displaystyle V_{N}(\mathcal{A}^{*})-V_{N}(\mathcal{A}_{\text{SLG}})

\displaystyle=V_{N}(\mathcal{A}^{*})-V_{N}(\hat{\mathcal{A}})+V_{N}(\hat{\mathcal{A}})-V_{N}(\mathcal{A}_{\text{SLG}})

Since the choice of
K,m
satisfies the conditions in Lemma
6
, we know by Equation (
12
) that:

\displaystyle V_{N}(\hat{\mathcal{A}})-V_{N}(\mathcal{A}_{\text{SLG}})

\displaystyle\leqslant C\left(\frac{K}{N}\right)^{1/3}(\log N)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leqslant C_{1}\frac{1}{(\log N)^{1/2}}

for some constant
C_{1}>0
. And by Lemma
7
, we have:

\displaystyle V_{N}(\mathcal{A}^{*})-V_{N}(\hat{\mathcal{A}})

\displaystyle\leqslant C_{2}\frac{\log\log N}{(\log N)^{\min\{\beta,1-\gamma\}}}

for some constant
C_{2}>0
. Combining the two parts, we conclude that:

\displaystyle V_{N}(\mathcal{A}^{*})-V_{N}(\mathcal{A}_{\text{SLG}})

\displaystyle\leqslant C_{2}\frac{\log\log N}{(\log N)^{\min\{\beta,1-\gamma\}}}+C_{1}\frac{1}{(\log N)^{1/2}}

\displaystyle\leqslant C\frac{\log\log N}{(\log N)^{\min\{\beta,1-\gamma,1/2\}}}

for some constant
C>0
depending on the problem parameters. This completes the proof.

∎

F.1
Proof of Proposition
1

We analyze the performance gap between SLG and Best-of-N algorithms under the homogeneous noise setting as stated in Proposition
1
.

Proof of Proposition
1
.

We first analyze the performance of our algorithm SLG and the Best-of-N algorithm under the homogeneous noise setting.

If we denote
S_{\text{SLG}}
as the set of states selected by SLG algorithm
(where we know
|S_{\text{SLG}}|=K
),
and
\hat{s}=\arg\max_{s\in S_{\text{SLG}}}\hat{V}_{N}(s)
as the candidate with the highest estimated value among the selected states, then since
the variance
\sigma(s)=\sigma_{1}
for all
s
, we have:

\displaystyle\hat{s}=\arg\max_{s\in S_{\text{SLG}}}\hat{V}_{N}(s)

\displaystyle=\arg\max_{s\in S_{\text{SLG}}}\hat{\mu}(s)

and we can lower bound the expected performance of SLG algorithm as follows:

\displaystyle V_{N}(\mathcal{A}_{\text{SLG}})

\displaystyle\geqslant\mathbb{E}[V_{N-Km}(\hat{s})]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\mu(\hat{s})+\sigma_{1}E(N-Km)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\mu(\hat{s})]+\sigma_{1}E(N-Km)

Because we know
\hat{\mu}(s)\mid\mu(s)\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu(s),\sigma_{1}^{2}/m)
and
\mu(s)\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{0},\sigma_{0}^{2})
, by the properties of Gaussian distribution, we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mu(\hat{s})]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\mu}}\left[\mathbb{E}[\mu(\hat{s})\mid\hat{\mu}(\hat{s})]\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\mu}}\left[\mu_{0}+\frac{\sigma_{0}^{2}}{\sigma_{0}^{2}+\sigma_{1}^{2}/m}(\hat{\mu}(\hat{s})-\mu_{0})\right]

\displaystyle=\mu_{0}+\frac{\sigma_{0}^{2}}{\sigma_{0}^{2}+\sigma_{1}^{2}/m}\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\mu}}[\hat{\mu}(\hat{s})-\mu_{0}]

\displaystyle=\mu_{0}+\frac{\sigma_{0}^{2}}{\sigma_{0}^{2}+\sigma_{1}^{2}/m}\sqrt{\sigma_{0}^{2}+\sigma_{1}^{2}/m}E(K)

where the last equality follows from the fact that
\hat{\mu}(s)\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{0},\sigma_{0}^{2}+\sigma_{1}^{2}/m)
for all
s
and
\hat{s}
is the maximizer among
K
i.i.d. samples.
If we denote
t=\frac{\sigma_{1}}{\sigma_{0}}
, then we can simplify the above expression as:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mu(\hat{s})]

\displaystyle=\mu_{0}+\frac{\sigma_{0}}{\sqrt{1+t^{2}/m}}E(K)

which leads to the following lower bound for SLG algorithm:

\displaystyle V_{N}(\mathcal{A}_{\text{SLG}})

\displaystyle\geqslant\mu_{0}+\sigma_{0}\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+t^{2}/m}}E(K)+tE(N-Km)\right]

For the Best-of-N algorithm, since the reward distribution of the prompt
x
is
\mathcal{N}(\mu_{0},\sigma_{0}^{2}+\sigma_{1}^{2})
, we have:

\displaystyle V_{N}(\mathcal{A}_{\text{BoN}})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\max_{R_{1},\dots,R_{N}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}(\mu_{0},\sigma_{0}^{2}+\sigma_{1}^{2})}R_{i}]=\mu_{0}+\sigma_{0}\sqrt{1+t^{2}}E(N)

So the performance gap between the two algorithms can be lower bounded as:

\displaystyle V_{N}(\mathcal{A}_{\text{SLG}})-V_{N}(\mathcal{A}_{\text{BoN}})

\displaystyle\geqslant\sigma_{0}\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+t^{2}/m}}E(K)+tE(N-Km)-\sqrt{1+t^{2}}E(N)\right].

(13)

To proceed, we define a surrogate function to approximate the gap, and then derive a lower bound for this surrogate. Let:

\displaystyle G(t,N)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+t^{2}/m}}\sqrt{2\log K}+t\sqrt{2\log(N-Km)}-\sqrt{1+t^{2}}\sqrt{2\log N}.

By choosing
m=t^{2}\log N
and
K=\frac{N}{(1+t)m}=\frac{N}{(1+t)t^{2}\log N}
, we have:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+t^{2}/m}}\sqrt{2\log K}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+1/\log N}}\sqrt{2\log N-2\log\log N-2\log(t^{2}(1+t))}

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+1/\log N}}\sqrt{2\log N}\left(1-\frac{\log\log N+\log(t^{2}(1+t))}{\log N}\right)

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+1/\log N}}\sqrt{2\log N}-C_{1}\frac{\log\log N}{\sqrt{\log N}}

for some constant
C_{1}>0
depending on
t
. Similarly, we have:

\displaystyle t\sqrt{2\log(N-Km)}

\displaystyle=t\sqrt{2\log N-2\log(1+1/t)}

\displaystyle\geqslant t\sqrt{2\log N}\left(1-\frac{\log(1+1/t)}{\log N}\right)

\displaystyle\geqslant t\sqrt{2\log N}-C_{2}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\log N}}

for some constant
C_{2}>0
depending on
t
. Combining these parts yields:

\displaystyle G(t,N)

\displaystyle\geqslant\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+1/\log N}}+t-\sqrt{1+t^{2}}\right)\sqrt{2\log N}-C\frac{\log\log N}{\sqrt{\log N}}

for some constant
C>0
depending on
t
. When
N\geqslant\exp(1+1/t)
, we observe:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+1/\log N}}+t-\sqrt{1+t^{2}}

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\frac{t}{t+1}}}+t-\sqrt{1+t^{2}}

\displaystyle\geqslant\left(1-\frac{1}{2}\frac{t}{t+1}\right)+t-\sqrt{1+t^{2}}

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{t}{t+1}-\frac{1}{2}\frac{t}{t+1}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{t}{t+1},

where the last inequality follows from the fact that
t+1-\sqrt{1+t^{2}}\geqslant\frac{t}{t+1}
for all
t>0
. Therefore:

\displaystyle G(t,N)

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{1}{2}\frac{t}{t+1}\sqrt{2\log N}-C\frac{\log\log N}{\sqrt{\log N}}

for
N\geqslant\exp(1+1/t)
.

It suffices to analyze the approximation error between the original gap and the surrogate function, which arises from approximating
E(N)
by
\sqrt{2\log N}
. Using the known bound:

\displaystyle\left|E(N)-\sqrt{2\log N}\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant C_{3}\frac{\log\log N}{\sqrt{\log N}}

for some constant
C_{3}>0
and all
N\geqslant 3
, we have:

\displaystyle\quad\left|V_{N}(\mathcal{A}_{\text{SLG}})-V_{N}(\mathcal{A}_{\text{BoN}})-\sigma_{0}G(t,N)\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\sigma_{0}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+t^{2}/m}}\left|E(K)-\sqrt{2\log K}\right|+t\left|E(N-Km)-\sqrt{2\log(N-Km)}\right|+\sqrt{1+t^{2}}\left|E(N)-\sqrt{2\log N}\right|\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sigma_{0}\left(C_{3}\frac{\log\log K}{\sqrt{\log K}}+tC_{3}\frac{\log\log(N-Km)}{\sqrt{\log(N-Km)}}+\sqrt{1+t^{2}}C_{3}\frac{\log\log N}{\sqrt{\log N}}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant C_{4}\frac{\log\log N}{\sqrt{\log N}}

for some constant
C_{4}>0
depending on
t
.

Combining the above results, we observe that the lower bound consists of a dominant term of order
\sqrt{\log N}
and a negative error term of order
\frac{\log\log N}{\sqrt{\log N}}
.
Since
\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\log\log N}{\log N}=0
, there exists a threshold
N_{0}
(dependent on
t
) such that for all
N\geqslant N_{0}
, the error term is at most half of the dominant term.
Therefore, for
N\geqslant N_{0}
:

\displaystyle V_{N}(\mathcal{A}_{\text{SLG}})-V_{N}(\mathcal{A}_{\text{BoN}})

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{t}{4(t+1)}\sigma_{0}\sqrt{2\log N}

This completes the proof.
∎

Proof of Corollary

We now prove Corollary

by translating the performance gap into an equivalent budget amplification.

Proof of Corollary

.

We aim to find a constant
\gamma>0
such that for

(as defined in Proposition

V_{N}(\mathcal{A}_{\text{SLG}})\geqslant V_{N^{1+\gamma}}(\mathcal{A}_{\text{BoN}})
holds.

From Proposition

V_{N}(\mathcal{A}_{\text{SLG}})\geqslant V_{N}(\mathcal{A}_{\text{BoN}})+\frac{t}{4(t+1)}\sigma_{0}\sqrt{2\log N}.

(14)

Now, consider the Best-of-
N
baseline operating with an amplified budget
N^{1+\gamma}
. Its expected value is given by:

V_{N^{1+\gamma}}(\mathcal{A}_{\text{BoN}})=\mu_{0}+\sigma_{0}\sqrt{1+t^{2}}E(N^{1+\gamma}).

The difference in value between the amplified baseline and the standard baseline is:

\displaystyle\Delta V_{\text{BoN}}

\displaystyle=V_{N^{1+\gamma}}(\mathcal{A}_{\text{BoN}})-V_{N}(\mathcal{A}_{\text{BoN}})

\displaystyle=\sigma_{0}\sqrt{1+t^{2}}\left(E(N^{1+\gamma})-E(N)\right).

Since we know
\mathbb{E}(n)-E(m)\leqslant 2(\sqrt{\log n}-\sqrt{\log m})

\displaystyle E(N^{1+\gamma})-E(N)

\displaystyle\leqslant 2(\sqrt{\log(N^{1+\gamma})}-\sqrt{\log N})

\displaystyle=2(\sqrt{1+\gamma}-1)\sqrt{\log N}.

Substituting this back into the expression for
\Delta V_{\text{BoN}}
, we have:

\Delta V_{\text{BoN}}\leqslant 2\sigma_{0}\sqrt{1+t^{2}}(\sqrt{1+\gamma}-1)\sqrt{\log N}\leqslant\sigma_{0}\sqrt{1+t^{2}}\gamma\sqrt{\log N}.

And when we choose
\gamma=\frac{\sqrt{2}t}{4(t+1)\sqrt{1+t^{2}}}
, we have:

\displaystyle V_{N}(\mathcal{A}_{\text{SLG}})

\displaystyle\geqslant V_{N^{1+\gamma}}(\mathcal{A}_{\text{BoN}})-\Delta V_{\text{BoN}}+\frac{t}{4(t+1)}\sigma_{0}\sqrt{2\log N}

\displaystyle\geqslant V_{N^{1+\gamma}}(\mathcal{A}_{\text{BoN}})-\sigma_{0}\sqrt{1+t^{2}}\gamma\sqrt{\log N}+\frac{t}{4(t+1)}\sigma_{0}\sqrt{2\log N}

\displaystyle=V_{N^{1+\gamma}}(\mathcal{A}_{\text{BoN}})

This completes the proof.

∎
