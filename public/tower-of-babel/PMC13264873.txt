Estimated plasma volume for predicting sepsis-associated ARDS risk: a multicentre retrospective cohort study
Authors: Lina Zhao, Lei Zong, Wei Wei, Yuehao Shen, Haiying Liu, Xuguang Li, Dongxue Huang, Daihua Yu, Fei Yang, Yunying Wang, Xiaopeng Shi, Yun Li, Yi Wang, Keliang Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSepsis-associated acute respiratory distress syndrome (SA-ARDS) is a lethal complication demanding early predictors. This study assessed estimated plasma volume status (ePVS) for predicting SA-ARDS development.MethodsThis multicentre cohort study analysed 3854 adult patients with sepsis 3.0 from China. The primary outcome was the incidence of SA-ARDS. Multivariate logistic regression, propensity score matching (PSM) and inverse probability weighting (IPW) were used to assess associations between ePVS and SA-ARDS.ResultsElevated ePVS was independently associated with SA-ARDS incidence across all models (multivariate OR: 1.56, 95% CI 1.50 to 1.63; PSM OR: 1.72, 1.63 to 1.81; IPW OR: 1.58, 1.49 to 1.69; p<0.001). Receiver operating characteristic analysis demonstrated a strong discriminative ability of ePVS for SA-ARDS incidence (area under the curve (AUC): 0.772, 95% CI 0.757 to 0.788). SA-ARDS patients exhibited higher mortality (31.8% vs 23.1%, p<0.001), mechanical ventilation (MV) use (82.3% vs 48.4%, p<0.001) and continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) requirements (7.7% vs 4.7%, p<0.001). Generalised additive model analysis revealed a significant linear association between ePVS >8.0 dL/g and SA-ARDS mortality (p<0.001). Although ePVS >8.0 dL/g alone showed limited predictive value for in-hospital mortality in SA-ARDS patients (AUC: 0.549), its combination with acute physiology and chronic health evaluation II significantly improved discrimination (AUC: 0.823). This synergistic effect persisted even after excluding early deaths (≤72 hours), reinforcing ePVS’s complementary role alongside established severity scores in mortality risk stratification. Stratified analysis revealed ePVS levels strongly correlated with acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) severity (p<0.001), particularly distinguishing mild from moderate cases. Patients with pulmonary infections exhibited higher ePVS than non-pulmonary sources (p<0.001).ConclusionsThese findings highlight ePVS is strongly associated with ARDS onset, disease severity and mortality risk, supporting its utility as a prognostic marker in risk stratification.Keywords: ARDS, Critical Care, Patient Outcome Assessment

Introduction

Sepsis, defined as a life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection, remains a leading cause of mortality in intensive care units (ICUs), with contemporary studies reporting mortality rates exceeding 25% despite advancements in critical care management.1 2 Among its severe complications, Sepsis-associated acute respiratory distress syndrome (SA-ARDS) significantly worsens clinical outcomes, elevating mortality rates to over 40%.3 4 Acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) is characterised by acute hypoxaemic respiratory failure, diffuse alveolar damage and heightened systemic inflammation, often triggered by dysregulated cytokine release and endothelial injury.5 Effective fluid management and haemodynamic monitoring are pivotal in mitigating organ dysfunction in these patients.6 However, balancing adequate tissue perfusion with the risks of fluid overload—a key contributor to pulmonary oedema and impaired oxygenation—remains a critical challenge.7 8 Traditional haemodynamic monitoring tools, such as pulmonary artery catheters and stroke volume variation, are limited by invasiveness, cost and dependency on operator expertise,9,12 underscoring the need for non-invasive, accessible biomarkers to guide clinical decisions.

Estimated plasma volume status (ePVS), a biomarker derived from haemoglobin and haematocrit using the Duarte formula,13 has emerged as a promising indicator of fluid balance and prognosis in cardiovascular14,16 and infectious diseases.17,19 Recent studies highlight its utility in predicting outcomes in heart failure, where elevated ePVS correlates with fluid overload and mortality.15 16 In sepsis, ePVS has been shown to reflect cumulative fluid administration and predict adverse outcomes, offering a pragmatic tool for risk stratification.20 21 Notably, ePVS has also demonstrated prognostic value in COVID-19-related ARDS, suggesting broader applicability in critical care settings.22 Despite these advancements, its role in SA-ARDS—a population with distinct pathophysiological challenges, including systemic inflammation and microcirculatory dysfunction—remains unexplored.

This multicentre retrospective cohort study aimed to investigate the predictive value of ePVS for SA-ARDS occurrence and outcomes. We hypothesised that elevated ePVS would independently correlate with ARDS incidence, disease severity and mortality. Our findings aim to bridge a critical knowledge gap, offering a novel, cost-effective biomarker for early intervention and personalised care in SA-ARDS.

Materials and methods

Data sources

The data for this multicentre retrospective study were collected from three Grade-A tertiary hospitals in China between January 2020 and February 2025. All study protocols received ethical approval from the respective institutional review boards: Tianjin Medical University General Hospital (IRB No. 2025-YX-147-01), Henan Provincial People’s Hospital (IRB No. 202550) and Chifeng Municipal Hospital (IRB No. CK2023082).

Patient and public involvement

As this was a retrospective study using anonymised clinical data, patient and public involvement in study design or conduct was not applicable. All data were collected through routine clinical practice without direct patient participation.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Adult patients (≥18 years) diagnosed with sepsis (Sepsis-3 criteria)1 and ARDS (2024 New Global Diagnostic Criteria4 with complete haemoglobin/haematocrit data) were included. Our database prospectively collected ARDS cases using the Berlin Definition23 during routine clinical screening, with subsequent retrospective reclassification to align with the 2024 Global Criteria. This reclassification process carefully mapped key Berlin components (hypoxaemia, radiographic findings and exclusion of cardiac failure) to the updated framework. In this study, time zero was defined as ICU admission with concurrent sepsis diagnosis. Incident SA-ARDS was ascertained through daily screening during the first 7 days of ICU stay. Patients who met ARDS criteria at ICU admission were excluded from the primary analysis to focus on the predictive value of ePVS for incident SA-ARDS. To enhance transparency, we have included an online supplemental eTable 1 summarising how each 2024 diagnostic component was operationalised in our cohort. These additions demonstrate our methodological rigour in applying contemporary diagnostic standards.

We excluded patients with ICU length of stay <24 hours, congestive heart failure, structural lung abnormalities (eg, atelectasis, pleural effusion), patients with active haemorrhage or clinically significant bleeding, admitted to the ICU with a simultaneous diagnosis of sepsis with ARDS, or missing critical baseline data (eg, laboratory values, clinical outcomes). This study specifically focused on patients who developed ARDS after sepsis diagnosis (incident SA-ARDS), excluding those with pre-existing ARDS at ICU admission. This distinction was essential to examine ePVS as a predictive marker for subsequent ARDS development in sepsis patients rather than as an associative marker of established ARDS. The exclusion preserved the critical temporal sequence needed to assess whether early ePVS changes could forecast later SA-ARDS onset.

Data extraction

We selected the clinical characteristics of the target patients on the first day of ICU admission as the primary research indicators. Patients’ baseline characteristics included age, gender and comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes, chronic liver disease, chronic kidney disease, coronary artery disease and cerebrovascular disease). Systematic measurements of oxygen exposure parameters were performed every 8 hours, and ePVS parameters were assessed once daily during the first 7 days of ICU admission. The respiratory-related indicators extracted from hospitalised patients included respiratory rate (RR), peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO2), partial pressure of carbon dioxide in arterial blood (PaCO2), partial pressure of oxygen in arterial blood (PaO2), fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2), oxygenation index (PaO2/FiO2) and peripheral oxygen saturation to fraction of inspired oxygen ratio (SpO2/FiO2). The laboratory parameters included white blood cell count (WBC), red blood cell count (RBC), platelet count (PLT), neutrophil percentage (Neut%), C-reactive protein (CRP), haematocrit (Hct), haemoglobin (Hb), fibrinogen (FIB), international normalised ratio (INR), prothrombin time (PT), glucose (Glu) and lactates (Lac). Clinical interventions included continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT), mechanical ventilation (MV) and the use of vasopressors. In this retrospective cohort, 90-day all-cause mortality was systematically verified through standardised telephone interviews at pre-specified intervals. Additionally, we collected data on patients’ length of hospital stay, as well as the sequential organ failure assessment (SOFA) score and acute physiology and chronic health evaluation II (APACHE II) score within the first 24 hours of admission.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed variables were expressed as mean±SD and analysed with Student’s t-tests, while non-normally distributed variables were reported as median (IQR) and compared via Mann-Whitney U tests. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies (%) and analysed using Pearson’s χ² or Fisher’s exact tests, as appropriate. Multivariable logistic regression models were constructed to evaluate the independent association between ePVS and SA-ARDS incidence, adjusting for age, sex, comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes) and laboratory parameters (WBC, CRP). Results were reported as ORs with 95% CIs.

To address potential confounding, propensity score matching (PSM) was performed in a 1:1 ratio using nearest-neighbour matching with a calliper width of 0.2 SD of the logit propensity score. Propensity scores were derived from logistic regression models incorporating age, sex, comorbidities and baseline inflammatory markers. Standardised mean differences (SMDs) <0.1 indicated adequate balance between matched cohorts. Inverse probability weighting (IPW) was further applied to validate findings, with weights calculated from propensity scores.

Predictive performance of ePVS was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, with area under the curve (AUC) comparisons performed via DeLong’s test. Kaplan-Meier survival curves and log-rank tests were used to assess mortality differences between ePVS-stratified groups. A generalised additive model (GAM) with smoothing splines was employed to explore linear relationships between ePVS and mortality. We employed restricted cubic splines (RCS) regression analysis to examine the non-linear relationship between ePVS and the incidence of SA-ARDS. Sensitivity analyses included subgroup assessments by age, sex and ARDS severity. Missing data (<25% per variable) were handled using multiple imputation with chained equations (MICE).24 Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p<0.05. Analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.4.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Results

Study participants

A total of 4555 patients with sepsis were included. After excluding those with structural lung abnormalities (n=154), congestive heart failure (n=238), age <18 years (n=23), hospital stay <24 hours (n=65), missing data (n=13), active haemorrhage or clinically significant bleeding (n=96), and admitted to the ICU with a simultaneous diagnosis of sepsis with ARDS (n=112), 3854 patients were included in the final analysis. These patients were stratified into non-SA-ARDS (n=2045) and SA-ARDS (n=1809) cohorts, illustrating the study’s systematic screening process (online supplemental eFigure 1).

Baseline characteristics of SA-ARDS patients

The baseline characteristics, respiratory-related indicators and laboratory parameters of SA-ARDS and non-SA-ARDS patients were compared between the original cohort (n=2045 non-SA-ARDS vs n=1809 SA-ARDS) and the propensity-matched cohort (n=1440 each), as detailed in table 1 and online supplemental eTable 2. In the original cohort, SA-ARDS patients were observed to have a significantly higher median age (67.00 vs 65.00 years, p<0.001) and a higher proportion of males (60.9% vs 57.1%, p=0.018), with similar trends retained after matching. Respiratory parameters, including lower SpO2 (97.00% vs 98.00%, p<0.001), elevated PaCO2 (36.90 vs 34.20 mm Hg, p<0.001), reduced PaO2/FiO2 (208.60 vs 389.03 mm Hg, p<0.001) and increased ePVS (7.74 vs 6.01 dL/g, p<0.001), were found to differ significantly between groups. Lower haematocrit (28.20% vs 35.50%, p<0.001) and haemoglobin levels (9.30 vs 10.90 g/L, p<0.001), alongside higher fibrinogen (3.58 vs 3.34 g/L, p<0.001), INR (1.18 vs 1.15, p<0.001) and CRP (12.70 vs 11.80 mg/L, p<0.001), were identified in SA-ARDS patients. Online supplemental eTable 2 revealed Klebsiella (9.6% vs 3.9%, p<0.001) and Acinetobacter baumannii prevalence (8.0% vs 3.0%, p<0.001) to be more frequent in SA-ARDS patients, with comorbidities such as hypertension disease (52.0% vs 41.4%, p<0.001) and coronary artery disease (47.2% vs 31.2%, p<0.001) being more commonly associated.

The outcomes of SA-ARDS patients

The outcomes of SA-ARDS and non-SA-ARDS patients were compared between the original cohort (n=2045 non-SA-ARDS vs n=1809 SA-ARDS) and the propensity-matched cohort (n=1440 each), as outlined in table 2. In the original cohort, SA-ARDS patients were observed to require MV more frequently (82.3% vs 48.4%, p<0.001), with similar trends maintained post-matching (89.2% vs 44.6%, p<0.001). Vasopressor use (31.8% vs 20.3%, p<0.001) and CRRT (7.7% vs 4.7%, p<0.001) were identified as more common interventions in SA-ARDS patients, with these differences persisting in the matched cohort (31.0% vs 19.0% and 7.4% vs 4.2%, respectively, p<0.001). Hospital mortality was reported to be significantly higher in SA-ARDS patients (31.8% vs 23.1%, p<0.001), with consistent findings after matching (31.0% vs 23.2%, p<0.001). Non-respiratory failure SOFA scores were found to be lower in SA-ARDS patients (p<0.001), while SOFA and APACHE II scores showed no significant intergroup differences (p≥0.05).

Association between ePVS and SA-ARDS

The association between ePVS and SA-ARDS was evaluated using multivariate logistic regression, PSM and IPW, as summarised in figure 1A,C. In this observational study, elevated ePVS showed a consistent and graded association with SA-ARDS incidence across all adjusted models (multivariate OR: 1.56, 95% CI 1.50 to 1.63; PSM OR: 1.72, 1.63 to 1.81; IPW OR: 1.58, 1.49 to 1.69; p<0.001). The forest plot (figure 1C) derived from multivariate logistic regression further supported these findings, demonstrating that ePVS exhibited a strong association with SA-ARDS (OR: 2.37, 95% CI 2.08 to 2.73, p<0.001). The variables matched in PSM, including abdominal cavity infections, Klebsiella pneumoniae, glucose levels, lactate and inflammatory markers (WBC, CRP), were illustrated in the SMD plot (figure 1B).

Predictive performance of ePVS for SA-ARDS incidence

The predictive performance of ePVS for SA-ARDS incidence was evaluated through specificity and sensitivity analyses, as detailed in figure 2. For SA-ARDS incidence prediction (figure 2A), three models were constructed: Model 1 (ePVS alone), Model 2 (ePVS + PaCO2 + WBC + fibrinogen) and Model 3 (Model 2+vasopressor + coronary artery disease). The AUC values were reported as Model 1 0.772 (95% CI 0.757 to 0.788), Model 2 0.789 (0.775 to 0.804) and Model 3 0.795 (0.781 to 0.809), respectively, with specificity ranging from 0.731 to 0.783 and sensitivity from 0.689 to 0.747 (p<0.001 for all models). The calibration curve demonstrated excellent agreement between predicted and observed probabilities of SA-ARDS occurrence, with the bias-corrected curve closely aligning with the ideal reference line. The corrected calibration slope of 0.892 and intercept of 0.032 indicated minimal overfitting and no significant systematic bias, suggesting the model reliably predicts SA-ARDS risk across the probability spectrum. The slight deviation between apparent and bias-corrected estimates (slope: 0.914 vs 0.892) reflected modest over-optimism in the uncorrected model, which was effectively addressed through calibration adjustment. These results confirm the model’s robust predictive accuracy for clinical application (figure 2B). The RCS analysis revealed a clear dose-response relationship between ePVS levels and SA-ARDS incidence, with progressively higher risk observed as ePVS increased. The curve demonstrated a gradual upward trend, particularly noticeable beyond moderate ePVS values, suggesting that elevated ePVS serves as a significant risk predictor for SA-ARDS development. The non-linear association remained consistent across the measured range, reinforcing the clinical relevance of monitoring ePVS levels for risk stratification (online supplemental eFigure 2).

Prognostic stratification of ePVS in SA-ARDS

The relationship between ePVS and SA-ARDS prognosis was stratified using GAM, Kaplan-Meier survival analysis and severity-based comparisons, as shown in figure 3. A linear association between ePVS levels and in-hospital mortality was observed in the GAM analysis (figure 3A), with a threshold at approximately 8.0 dL/g, accompanied by a progressive increase in mortality risk with elevated ePVS levels. Based on GAM results, patients were categorised into low-ePVS and high-ePVS groups, and Kaplan-Meier survival curves (figure 3B) revealed significantly higher cumulative mortality in the high-ePVS group compared with the low-ePVS group (p=0.006). The multivariate analysis demonstrated that lower ePVS levels (OR: 0.49, 95% CI 0.37 to 0.63, p<0.001) and higher APACHE II score (OR: 1.47, 95% CI 1.40 to 1.54, p<0.001) were significantly associated with patient prognosis (figure 3C) (online supplemental eTable 3). For prognosis assessment (figure 3D), three models were compared: Model a (ePVS alone, AUC=0.549), Model b (APACHE II score alone, AUC: 0.811) and Model c (ePVS+APACHE II, AUC: 0.823), with specificity (0.572–0.974) and sensitivity (0.526–0.585) further validated by ROC curves.

Stratified analysis of clinical outcomes by key prognostic variables

Further stratification by ARDS severity (mild, moderate, severe) demonstrated that ePVS levels were strongly associated with disease severity (online supplemental eFigure 3), especially between mild and moderate groups (p<0.001). Patients with pulmonary infection exhibited significantly higher ePVS scores than non-infected individuals (p<0.001), indicating a strong association between pulmonary infection and ePVS (online supplemental eFigure 4).

After excluding patients who died within 72 hours of ICU admission, lower ePVS burden was significantly associated with reduced mortality risk (OR: 0.48, 95% CI 0.37 to 0.63, p<0.001), suggesting a protective effect. In contrast, higher APACHE II (OR: 1.49, 95% CI 1.42 to 1.56, p<0.001) and SOFA scores (OR: 1.07, 95% CI 1.02 to 1.12, p=0.006) were independent predictors of increased mortality, while age and gender showed no significant association (p>0.05) (online supplemental eFigure 5A). The ROC analysis demonstrated that ePVS alone exhibited limited discriminative ability (AUC: 0.549, 95% CI 0.523 to 0.574), with modest sensitivity (52.5%) and specificity (57.2%). In contrast, APACHE II showed strong predictive performance (AUC: 0.818, 95% CI 0.793 to 0.843), achieving high specificity (97.4%) and positive predictive value (PPV) (90.6%). The combined model (ePVS+APACHE II) further improved discrimination (AUC: 0.829, 95% CI 0.805 to 0.853), balancing sensitivity (59.3%) and specificity (96.7%) while maintaining a high PPV (88.5%) (online supplemental eFigure 5B).

Based on the previously established cut-off of ePVS>5.9 in mortality risk stratification,25 our study further validated its association with increased mortality in SA-ARDS patients (OR: 1.53, 95% CI 1.03 to 2.30, p=0.037) (online supplemental eFigure 6A). Although ePVS alone demonstrated limited discriminative ability (AUC: 0.51, 95% CI 0.49 to 0.53), this threshold effectively identified a high-risk subgroup with elevated mortality. In contrast, the APACHE II score exhibited superior predictive performance (AUC: 0.81, 95% CI 0.79 to 0.84; OR: 1.45, 95% CI 1.39 to 1.53, p<0.001). The combined model incorporating both ePVS and APACHE II did not significantly improve discrimination (AUC: 0.81, 95% CI 0.79 to 0.84) (online supplemental eFigure 6B), suggesting that ePVS >5.9 provides supplementary rather than independent prognostic value. These findings indicate that while ePVS may serve as a useful adjunct biomarker for risk stratification in SA-ARDS, its clinical utility beyond established scoring systems remains limited.

Discussion

Our multicentre retrospective cohort study demonstrates that ePVS serves as a robust predictor for both the development and adverse prognosis of SA-ARDS. First, ePVS exhibited strong independent associations with SA-ARDS incidence across multivariate logistic regression, PSM and IPW models. Second, higher ePVS levels were linearly correlated with increased mortality risk and disease severity. These findings collectively highlight the dual clinical utility of ePVS in early risk stratification and prognostication for SA-ARDS patients.

Our results establish ePVS as a novel biomarker for predicting SA-ARDS onset. Elevated ePVS (OR: 2.37, 95% CI 2.08 to 2.73) independently predicted SA-ARDS incidence with strong discriminative accuracy (AUC: 0.772), aligning with prior studies in COVID-19 ARDS22 and heart failure.15 Although albumin is a critical determinant of oncotic pressure, our analysis revealed comparable albumin levels between SA-ARDS and non-SA-ARDS groups, notably, our stratified analysis revealed significantly higher ePVS levels in patients with pulmonary infections compared with non-pulmonary sources suggesting that hypoproteinaemia may not be a primary driver of the observed ePVS-ARDS association in this cohort. This finding aligns with recent studies demonstrating that sepsis-induced endothelial injury and capillary leak may overwhelm oncotic pressure gradients in ARDS pathogenesis.21 26 Elevated ePVS likely signifies interstitial oedema and impaired microcirculation, exacerbating pulmonary fluid accumulation and hypoxaemia.5 25 We acknowledge that our dataset lacks direct measurements of cumulative fluid balance, diuretic use, vasopressor dosing or transfusions. Thus, the association between ePVS and fluid overload should be interpreted cautiously, as ePVS reflects a composite of haemodynamic and microvascular disturbances rather than a direct measure of fluid status. Nevertheless, our validation across 3854 patients underscores its clinical relevance as a readily available laboratory biomarker. Future studies incorporating detailed fluid balance and endothelial injury markers (eg, angiopoietin-2)27 could further clarify these relationships.

Beyond prediction, our study reveals ePVS’s prognostic significance. The linear mortality association (GAM p<0.001) and 31.8% vs 23.1% mortality difference between SA-ARDS and non-SA-ARDS cohorts emphasise its clinical relevance. These findings extend ePVS’s established role in cardiovascular diseases15 16 to critical care. Niedermeyer et al similarly reported ePVS >5.9 dL/g as predictive of ARDS mortality,25 while our Kaplan-Meier analysis showed higher 90-day in-hospital mortality in high-ePVS patients (p=0.006). Mechanistically, while our data show an association between elevated ePVS and worse outcomes, further research is needed to determine whether plasma volume expansion contributes causally to organ failure or merely reflects underlying pathophysiology through impaired oxygen delivery5 and lymphatic drainage.27 Although ePVS alone showed limited mortality prediction (AUC: 0.549), its integration with APACHE II scores improved prognostic accuracy (AUC: 0.823), supporting multimodal biomarker approaches.28 29 Importantly, ePVS’s simplicity—requiring only routine laboratory parameters—makes it accessible in resource-limited settings, contrasting with complex tools like pulmonary artery catheters.9 We recommend serial ePVS measurements during ICU stays to dynamically assess therapeutic responses and refine risk stratification.

While our study established ePVS as an important prognostic marker for SA-ARDS outcomes, several limitations inherent to the retrospective design should be acknowledged. First, we lacked data on key therapeutic variables known to influence ARDS prognosis, including MV parameters (positive end-expiratory pressure levels, tidal volume settings), pharmacological interventions (corticosteroid use) and advanced respiratory support strategies (prone positioning). These unmeasured confounders may have impacted the observed associations between ePVS and clinical outcomes. Second, the reliance on single-timepoint haemoglobin/haematocrit measurements may not fully capture dynamic plasma volume changes influenced by active bleeding, haemolysis or fluid resuscitation, potentially affecting ePVS accuracy in clinical scenarios with acute blood loss or transfusion requirements. Third, the calculation does not account for sodium-mediated plasma osmolarity changes, which are clinically significant as hyponatraemia may exacerbate oedema formation while hypernatraemia could mask true volume status. Fourth, additionally, while ePVS reflects relative volume changes, it cannot quantify absolute volume deficits that would directly guide fluid replacement strategies at the bedside. Fifth, our ePVS calculation using the Duarte formula has inherent limitations. Since ePVS=100 – haematocrit, it may overestimate plasma volume in patients with severe anaemia (haemoglobin <7 g/dL) where low haematocrit reflects reduced red cell mass rather than true volume expansion. Additionally, the formula does not account for osmolarity changes or active bleeding. While we excluded patients with haemorrhage, this limitation should be considered when interpreting results in anaemic populations. Finally, while baseline albumin levels were comparable between groups (32.8 vs 32.5 g/L, p=0.120), our study did not directly account for osmotic or oncotic pressure dynamics that may influence fluid balance. The ePVS calculation, while clinically practical, does not incorporate variables such as fluid resuscitation strategies, capillary permeability, or electrolyte imbalances that could affect plasma volume estimation. Future studies measuring colloid osmotic pressure or serial albumin trends may better characterise these relationships in SA-ARDS. The retrospective design also limited our ability to systematically track concurrent factors like diuretic use, albumin administration or detailed fluid balance records that might modify the ePVS-ARDS relationship. Future prospective studies incorporating serial Hb/Hct measurements, osmolarity parameters and standardised volume assessments would help validate ePVS as a dynamic monitoring tool and clarify its clinical utility across diverse critical care populations. Future prospective studies should incorporate standardised fluid tracking to validate ePVS as a dynamic marker of fluid responsiveness. Finally, while our multicentre cohort enhances generalisability, external validation in diverse populations remains necessary. Future prospective studies should investigate ePVS-guided fluid protocols and integrate endothelial injury biomarkers (eg, angiopoietin-2)27 to optimise predictive models.

Conclusions

In this large multicentre study, elevated ePVS emerged as a significant risk indicator of SA-ARDS onset, severity and mortality. Its calculation from routine haemoglobin and haematocrit measurements positions ePVS as a practical, cost-effective tool for early risk stratification in critically ill patients. These findings support the use of ePVS as a practical predictive and prognostic marker for both SA-ARDS occurrence and subsequent clinical outcomes, though prospective studies are needed to validate its clinical utility. Prospective trials are needed to validate its role in dynamic monitoring and refining prognostic evaluation.
