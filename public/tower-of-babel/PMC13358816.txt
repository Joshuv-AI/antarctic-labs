Mechanical thrombectomy in intermediate-high risk pulmonary embolism: progress, promise, and remaining uncertainty
Authors: Orly Ran Sapir, Stanislav Henkin
Published: 2026

Introduction

Intermediate-high risk pulmonary embolism (PE) represents one of the most clinically challenging and debated areas in cardiovascular medicine. These patients are, by definition, hemodynamically stable, yet they carry objective evidence of right ventricular (RV) dysfunction and myocardial injury, a combination that places them at elevated risk for clinical deterioration. The stakes are high, with mortality ranging from 3 to 15% in this group compared with approximately 1% with low risk PE and exceeding 25% in those presenting with hemodynamic instability due to acute PE (1,2). Even among those who survive the acute episode, residual disease imposes a significant long-term burden. Up to 50% of survivors experience persistent dyspnea and/or functional impairment despite 3 months of therapeutic anticoagulation, coined post-PE syndrome and reflecting persistent pulmonary vascular impairment due to unresolved thromboembolic obstruction (3,4).

Anticoagulation is the foundation of PE treatment and is highly effective at stopping clot propagation and preventing recurrence. It does not, however, directly dissolve existing thrombus or rapidly unload the RV, and in a condition where hemodynamic deterioration can unfold over hours, that distinction matters. Systemic thrombolysis offers rapid clot dissolution and has been shown to reduce the rate of hemodynamic decompensation however, this benefit is offset by a substantial increase in major bleeding, including hemorrhagic stroke (2% vs. 0.2%), as demonstrated in the PEITHO trial (5). As a result, clinicians managing intermediate-high risk PE face a difficult dilemma: intervene early and accept procedural or bleeding risk or manage conservatively and accept the risk of potential deterioration.

Catheter-based therapies have emerged as an attempt to navigate this therapeutic gap. The appeal is straightforward: achieve more rapid hemodynamic improvement while limiting systemic exposure to fibrinolytics. Over the past decade, multiple studies have demonstrated improvements in right ventricle/left ventricle (RV/LV) ratio and physiological parameters using catheter-directed thrombolysis and mechanical thrombectomy (6-11). Most of these studies, however, lacked a randomized comparison against anticoagulation alone, the very therapy these approaches are meant to supplement or replace.

The STORM-PE trial was designed to address that gap directly. It is the first randomized controlled trial to compare mechanical thrombectomy plus anticoagulation head-to-head against anticoagulation alone in intermediate-high risk PE, and its results are a key step forward for the field.

Key findings and clinical implications

STORM-PE is a multicenter, international randomized controlled trial enrolling 100 patients with intermediate-high risk PE across 22 sites in the United States, Canada, New Zealand, and Poland. Patients were randomized 1:1 to computer-assisted vacuum thrombectomy (CAVT) using the Lightning Flash 16F catheter system (Penumbra, Inc.) plus anticoagulation, versus anticoagulation alone. The primary endpoint was the change in RV/LV ratio at 48 hours, measured by repeat computed tomography pulmonary angiography (CTPA) and adjudicated by a blinded independent imaging core laboratory. Secondary endpoints included a composite of major adverse events within 7 days and a range of additional physiological parameters. Longer-term outcomes including functional status, quality of life, and the 6-minute walk test are being collected through 90 days and will be reported separately (12).

CAVT led to a greater reduction in RV/LV diameter at 48 hours compared with anticoagulation alone (0.52±0.37 vs. 0.24±0.40), reflecting more effective early RV unloading. The between-group difference was 0.27 [95% confidence interval (CI): 0.12 to 0.43; P<0.001]. Notably, 39.1% of patients in the CAVT arm achieved RV/LV ratio normalization (≤1.0) at 48 hours versus only 13.5% in the anticoagulation arm (P=0.005). This three-fold difference in normalization rate, and the overall magnitude of RV/LV improvement with CAVT, represent the largest reported reductions among mechanical thrombectomy trials measured by CTPA (12).

The benefit of thrombectomy was consistently reflected across multiple physiological parameters, supporting a broad and coherent physiological effect. In addition to RV/LV improvement, patients undergoing thrombectomy demonstrated greater reductions in pulmonary artery obstruction (refined modified Miller score reduction of 42.1% vs. 15.6%, P≤0.001), faster resolution of tachycardia (100% vs. 56.5% of patients with baseline tachycardia resolved at 48 hours, P=0.006), greater reduction in supplemental oxygen requirements (P=0.003), and improved National Early Warning Score 2 (NEWS2, a bedside score reflecting overall physiologic stability; P=0.034) (12).

Procedural outcomes were favorable. Mechanical thrombectomy was technically successful in all 47 patients (100%), with a median thrombectomy time of 25 minutes and no access-site complications. Real-time intraprocedural hemodynamics confirmed the physiological impact, with systolic pulmonary arterial pressure falling from 50.0 to 39.4 mmHg (P≤0.001) and mean pulmonary arterial pressure from 30.9 to 23.0 mmHg (P<0.001) during the procedure (12).

Safety outcomes were reassuring. Only 6 of 100 patients experienced a major adverse event within 7 days, including 2 (4.3%) in the CAVT arm and 4 (7.5%) in the anticoagulation arm (P=0.681). Two PE-related deaths occurred in the CAVT arm, both independently adjudicated as unrelated to the procedure or device, most probably underscoring the severity of the underlying disease rather than procedural harm. Not surprisingly, the CAVT arm showed a greater decline in hemoglobin (2.5±1.44 vs. 1.6±1.22 g/dL; P=0.001), which is expected in the setting of a percutaneous invasive procedure. While this is reasonable, it should be acknowledged, as these patients are often already hemodynamically vulnerable.

Taken together, when imaging, hemodynamics, and clinical parameters all point in the same direction, they form a convincing narrative that is difficult to ignore. However, hospital length of stay and intensive care unit (ICU) utilization were similar between the two groups. While this does not diminish the early physiological signal, it raises the possibility that improved RV function on imaging may not translate into essential clinical outcomes.

Interpreting the signal: RV function, clinical outcomes, and the evolving evidence base

The physiological signal observed in STORM-PE is compelling, yet its clinical meaning warrants careful interpretation. The RV/LV ratio is a validated prognostic marker in acute PE and a commonly used endpoint in interventional trials (13). Its reduction reflects genuine reversal of RV pressure overload, but what it cannot tell us on its own is whether that early improvement translates into fewer deaths, less hemodynamic collapse, or better functional recovery. STORM-PE was not designed or powered to answer those questions, and with only six major adverse events across both arms, the clinical endpoints remain underdetermined. The 90-day follow-up data will be important, especially in trying to understand whether CAVT reduces the likelihood of developing post-PE syndrome.

To appreciate where STORM-PE sits, it is worth tracing how the field arrived at this point. The management of intermediate-high risk PE has evolved through a succession of trials.

Surgical embolectomy was the first reperfusion strategy, with early case series dating to the 1960s. It remains reserved for extreme cases, typically massive PE with refractory hemodynamic compromise, where catheter-based approaches are not feasible. Perioperative mortality in historical series ranged from 1 to 15%, and the requirement for cardiopulmonary bypass and specialized surgical teams limits its broad applicability (7).

The PEITHO trial established the central therapeutic tradeoff. Systemic thrombolysis reduced the composite of hemodynamic decompensation and death (3.2% vs. 5.6%; P=0.015) but caused a five-fold increase in major bleeding (6.3% vs. 1.2%) and a ten-fold increase in hemorrhagic stroke (2.0% vs. 0.2%) (5). The benefit was real; so was the harm.

Catheter-directed thrombolysis emerged to capture the hemodynamic benefits of fibrinolysis with lower bleeding risk. The ULTIMA, SEATTLE II and OPTALYSE-PE all demonstrated that ultrasound-facilitated with different fibrinolytic regimens can achieve meaningful RV/LV reductions with low rates of major bleeding and no intracranial hemorrhage (6,8,10).

Thrombolysis-free mechanical thrombectomy offered a different approach entirely. The FLARE showed an RV/LV reduction of 0.38 (standard deviation not reported) with major adverse event rate of 3.8%, and the EXTRACT-PE showed a reduction of 0.43±0.26 with major adverse events rate of 1.7%, both with no intracranial hemorrhage. PEERLESS compared large-bore mechanical thrombectomy versus catheter-directed thrombolysis and demonstrated superiority of thrombectomy on a hierarchical composite endpoint, prioritizing mortality, bleeding, clinical deterioration, and ICU utilization in that order. The benefit was driven by fewer clinical deteriorations (1.8% versus 5.4%; P=0.04) and less ICU use (19.3% versus 64.5% with stays >24 hours; P<0.001), while mortality and major bleeding were similar between strategies (9,11,14).

STORM-PE fills that gap. Its RV/LV reduction of 0.52 with CAVT is the largest reported in any mechanical thrombectomy trial measured by CTPA. Although cross-trial comparisons must always be interpreted cautiously given differences in patient populations and study designs (12).

The consistency of RV/LV improvements across different mechanical thrombectomy devices raises the possibility that these benefits reflect a class effect of thrombus aspiration, though whether device-specific differences in design and mechanism translate into meaningful outcome differences remains unknown.

The recently published HI-PEITHO trial provides important context for interpreting what that RV improvement means clinically. Enrolling intermediate-risk PE patients, ultrasound-facilitated catheter-directed fibrinolysis reduced the composite of PE-related death, cardiorespiratory decompensation, or recurrent PE within 7 days from 10.3% to 4.0% (RR 0.39; 95% CI: 0.20 to 0.77; P=0.005), with no intracranial hemorrhage and similar major bleeding rates through 30 days between arms; it is important to note that the primary outcome was driven by a reduction in cardiorespiratory decompensation rather than a reduction in mortality (15).

HI-PEITHO does what prior catheter-directed trials could not: in patients selected not just by RV dysfunction and troponin elevation but by active cardiorespiratory distress, it demonstrates a reduction in essential clinical outcomes rather than imaging surrogates alone, with an acceptable safety profile. It is the first randomized trial of any catheter-directed intervention to show a statistically significant reduction in clinical decompensation compared with anticoagulation alone, and in doing so provides the clinical outcome anchor that the RV/LV improvement seen in STORM-PE has been lacking (15). Read side by side, these two trials make a mutually reinforcing argument that early intervention in carefully selected intermediate-high risk patients with acute PE may offer benefits that extend well beyond the imaging window. The field is moving, and the weight of evidence is accumulating.

Clinical implications: patient selection and timing

STORM-PE does not support routine use of mechanical thrombectomy in all patients with intermediate-high risk PE. This is a heterogeneous population, many patients recover with anticoagulation alone, and the central challenge is identifying, in real time, which patients are on a trajectory toward deterioration. Clinical factors that may favor thrombectomy include significant RV dysfunction, worsening despite anticoagulation, persistent hypoxemia, and contraindications to systemic thrombolysis. Patients who are improving with anticoagulation alone or carry significant comorbidities that increase procedural risk may be better served by careful observation and escalation only if needed.

The ESC/EAPCI consensus statement on percutaneous interventions in PE offers a practical framework for navigating this uncertainty. Rather than a single threshold for intervention, the panel proposed three clinical scenarios of treatment failure: lack of improvement after 24 to 48 hours of therapeutic anticoagulation, overt hemodynamic deterioration, and a life-threatening scenario requiring emergency reperfusion and cardiovascular support. The panel acknowledged, however, that defining treatment failure in this population remains an important area of uncertainty (16). The 2026 AHA/ACC guidelines rise to this challenge directly. By moving away from the broad intermediate-high risk category and introducing a more granular classification, they formally distinguish between patients with transient hemodynamic compromise responsive to fluids (Category D1) and those in normotensive shock with signs of end-organ hypoperfusion despite preserved blood pressure (Category D2), the latter representing patients who appear stable but are already on a trajectory toward collapse. Mechanical thrombectomy carries a Class 2b recommendation in both categories, with the explicit guidance that escalation decisions should be driven by hemodynamic trajectory and RV functional progression rather than anatomy alone (3). Among available prognostic tools, Pulmonary Embolism Severity Score Index (PESI) has demonstrated the strongest discriminative performance for short-term adverse outcomes and is embedded in the new guideline framework for initial risk categorization and patient triage, while NEWS-2 offers complementary value as an inpatient monitoring tool to identify those who may be deteriorating and warrant escalation from anticoagulation alone toward catheter-based intervention (17). Timing matters. STORM-PE initiated CAVT within 24 hours of index CTPA, reflecting an early intervention philosophy. Whether that window is optimal, or whether later intervention in patients who fail to improve would produce similar results, is not yet known. What the trial does reinforce is the value of a structured, multidisciplinary approach. All STORM-PE sites operated with pulmonary embolism response teams (PERTs), and their role in rapid, individualized decision-making likely contributed to the trial’s favorable outcomes. PERTs integrate clinical, imaging, laboratory, and hemodynamic data to facilitate timely decisions and are increasingly recognized as a best-practice framework for managing complex PE presentations, a role now formalized with a Class 1 recommendation in the 2026 AHA/ACC guidelines (3).

Future directions

The next phase of research must focus on bridging the gap between surrogate physiological endpoints and meaningful clinical outcomes. Larger trials powered for essential endpoints such as mortality, hemodynamic collapse, and long-term complications are essential. PEERLESS II and PE-TRACT will contribute important evidence, and the 90-day follow-up data from STORM-PE will be a critical first test of whether the physiological signal observed translates into durable patient-level benefit. Second, refinement of patient selection strategies will be critical. Future work should aim to identify subgroups most likely to benefit through development of risk scores incorporating biomarkers, imaging parameters, and clinical characteristics.

Finally, cost-effective analyses will be needed. The procedural costs of mechanical thrombectomy must be weighed against potential reductions in complications, repeat hospitalizations, and long-term disease burden. That would be essential for broad adoption, particularly in health systems with constrained resources.

Conclusions

STORM-PE is a genuinely important trial. It provides the first randomized evidence that mechanical thrombectomy with CAVT produces superior RV decompression compared with anticoagulation alone in intermediate-high risk PE, and it does so without an increase in major adverse events. The technical success rate, procedure times, and safety profile all speak to the maturity of this approach.

At the same time, early physiological improvement is necessary but not sufficient evidence for broad adoption. The impact of mechanical thrombectomy on mortality, functional outcomes, and long-term complications including post-PE syndrome remains to be defined. HI-PEITHO has shown that catheter-based intervention can prevent clinical deterioration in this population, and the convergence of these two trials around a shared biological rationale is encouraging. What is now needed is confirmation that the degree of RV recovery achieved with mechanical thrombectomy translates into the same downstream clinical protection.

For now, mechanical thrombectomy should be viewed as a selective but evidence-supported strategy, applied thoughtfully in carefully chosen patients, particularly those with significant RV dysfunction, or early signs of clinical deterioration, and delivered in centers with the expertise and infrastructure to use it well. The field is moving in a clear direction, and STORM-PE has meaningfully advanced it.
