Few-Shot Contrastive Adaptation for Audio Abuse Detection in Low-Resource Indic Languages
Aditya Narayan Sankaran, Reza Farahbakhsh, Noel Crespi

Abstract
Abusive speech detection is becoming increasingly important as social media shifts towards voice-based interaction, particularly in multilingual and low-resource settings. Most current systems rely on automatic speech recognition (ASR) followed by text-based hate speech classification, but this pipeline is vulnerable to transcription errors and discards prosodic information carried in speech. We investigate whether Contrastive Language-Audio Pre-training (CLAP) can support abusive speech detection directly from audio. Using the ADIMA dataset ( 9 ) , we evaluate CLAP-based representations under few-shot supervised contrastive adaptation in cross-lingual and leave-one-language-out settings, with zero-shot prompting included as an auxiliary analysis. Our results show that CLAP yields strong cross-lingual audio representations across ten Indic languages, and that lightweight projection-only adaptation achieves competitive performance with respect to fully supervised systems trained on complete training data. However, the benefits of few-shot adaptation are language-dependent and not monotonic with shot size. These findings suggest that contrastive audio-text models provide a promising basis for cross-lingual audio abuse detection in low-resource settings, while also indicating that transfer remains incomplete and language-specific in important ways.



1
Introduction

Spoken content has become a primary mode of interaction across short-form video platforms, voice notes, live audio rooms, podcasts, and streaming services. Harmful language expressed through speech is therefore an increasingly important moderation problem. In many online settings, abuse conveyed through audio can be more impulsive, emotionally charged, and less self-monitored than text
25
, making it both socially consequential and technically difficult to detect. Despite major advances in text-based hate speech and toxicity detection, direct audio abuse detection remains comparatively underexplored
27
, especially in multilingual and low-resource settings where speech is often preferred over writing.

This challenge is particularly acute in linguistically diverse regions such as South Asia, where users routinely communicate in regional languages, dialects, and code-mixed speech
7
. These forms of communication are often underrepresented in large annotated corpora and pre-trained models, limiting the effectiveness of systems developed primarily for high-resource languages such as English. At the same time, abusive spoken content can spread rapidly across platforms, contributing to harassment, misinformation, and targeted hate. Building robust audio-native abuse detection systems is therefore both a technical challenge and a societal necessity.

A common strategy for abusive audio detection is to first transcribe speech using automatic speech recognition (ASR) and then apply text-based hate speech classifiers
27
. Although this pipeline benefits from progress in natural language processing, it also introduces several important limitations. First, ASR systems for low-resource languages often exhibit high error rates, particularly for informal, colloquial, code-mixed, or profanity-rich speech typical of social media. Such errors can obscure abusive intent, distort offensive expressions, or remove contextual cues required for accurate classification
4
. Second, abuse in speech is not purely lexical. Paralinguistic and prosodic properties such as pitch, emphasis, rhythm, stress, and emotional intensity may strongly influence how an utterance is perceived
29
. These cues are largely lost when the signal is reduced to text. Third, ASR-based pipelines compound errors across stages, increasing both computational cost and the risk of downstream failure
30
. These limitations motivate models that operate directly on audio representations rather than relying entirely on transcription.

Recent advances in contrastive learning offer a promising alternative for cross-modal representation learning. Contrastive approaches learn embeddings by pulling semantically aligned examples closer together while pushing mismatched examples apart. In vision-language research, such objectives have produced highly transferable representations with strong zero-shot capability
32
;
19
. Contrastive Language–Audio Pre-training (CLAP)
6
extends this paradigm to the audio-text setting by jointly learning aligned audio and text embeddings in a shared semantic space. This makes it possible to compare an audio clip directly with natural-language prompts representing candidate labels. For abusive speech detection, prompts such as
"This audio contains hate speech"
and
"This audio does not contain hate speech"
can therefore act as class prototypes.

CLAP is especially attractive in multilingual settings because its cross-modal training may encourage language-agnostic representations. Rather than training separate supervised systems for each language, a single pre-trained audio-text model can potentially transfer across languages with limited adaptation. Moreover, these representations can be refined under few-shot conditions. Even a small number of labelled examples per class per language may improve the geometry of the embedding space, and supervised contrastive objectives can explicitly bring same-class examples closer while pushing apart examples from different classes. Lightweight adaptation through projection heads or selective fine-tuning therefore offers a practical compromise between large-scale pre-training and task-specific learning.

In this work, we investigate whether CLAP-based representations can provide an effective and scalable framework for multilingual audio abuse detection. Our main focus is on few-shot supervised contrastive adaptation and downstream classification under monolingual, cross-lingual, and leave-one-language-out transfer settings, while zero-shot prompting is treated as an auxiliary evaluation setting. Our study is guided by the following research questions:

•

RQ1:
How effective are CLAP-based representations for abusive speech detection directly from audio across multiple Indic languages?

•

RQ2:
To what extent does few-shot contrastive adaptation improve performance over using frozen pre-trained CLAP embeddings in low-resource language settings?

•

RQ3:
How well do CLAP embeddings transfer across languages, and does excluding the target language from training (leave-one-language-out) substantially degrade performance?

•

RQ4:
Does lightweight projection-only adaptation offer a more favourable trade-off between adaptation cost and downstream performance than deeper encoder fine-tuning in few-shot regimes?

In this work, there is no direct comparison against a few-shot supervised fine-tuning baseline trained with full task data, as such results are available from
9
and serve as an upper-bound reference rather than a direct competitor. Our primary interest is in understanding the behaviour of contrastive audio-text representations under varying degrees of cross-lingual transfer and adaptation, rather than in maximising absolute performance on ADIMA.

2
Literature Review

Research on hate, abuse, and toxicity detection has been dominated by text-based approaches, but the growth of voice-based social media has exposed the limitations of text-only moderation
14
;
10
;
15
. Recent work has therefore begun to focus on direct speech-based detection, motivated by ASR brittleness in noisy, multilingual, and code-mixed conditions, as well as by latency and privacy concerns. This section reviews datasets, modelling trends, and the specific challenges of multilingual and low-resource audio abuse detection.

2.1
Datasets for Audio Hate and Abuse Detection

Labelled resources for audio hate speech detection remain limited in both size and linguistic coverage. Among the most important benchmarks is
ADIMA

9
, which contains 11,775 audio clips spanning ten Indic languages and supports monolingual, multilingual, and cross-lingual evaluation. ADIMA is especially important because it highlights the limitations of ASR-based pipelines in profanity-rich and code-mixed speech.

Several other datasets extend the space in different ways.
DeToxy

8
and
MuTox

5
increase multilingual coverage and compare ASR cascades with end-to-end audio models, often showing that direct audio-based systems are competitive with or stronger than transcription-based approaches. Smaller language-specific datasets, including work on Malayalam and Tamil, remain useful for targeted studies but are limited in scale
18
. More recent benchmarks such as
SynHate

22
extend evaluation to synthetic and deepfake audio, while studies using text-to-speech and prosodic analysis examine bias, explainability, and the role of non-lexical cues
29
. Spoken toxicity resources continue to emerge for other languages as well, such as
ToxicTone
for Mandarin audio
13
. Taken together, these datasets reveal substantial heterogeneity in annotation schemes, language balance, recording conditions, and task formulation.

2.2
Methodological Approaches

Early audio abuse detection systems relied on handcrafted acoustic descriptors such as MFCCs, spectral statistics, and prosodic cues combined with traditional classifiers
23
;
2
. While these methods can be effective in controlled settings, they typically transfer poorly across languages and domains. More recent work increasingly uses pre-trained speech encoders such as Wav2Vec 2.0, XLS-R, and Whisper, either as fixed feature extractors or as fine-tuned backbones. On datasets such as ADIMA and MuTox, these audio-native representations can match or exceed ASR-based cascades, which remain vulnerable to transcription errors and additional latency
27
.

Multimodal approaches combine acoustic and textual signals in an effort to capture both lexical and paralinguistic information. Frameworks such as
MADA
report gains over unimodal baselines
27
, and related work shows the value of explicit speech-text alignment for multilingual toxicity classification
12
. Other studies show that prosodic-only or acoustic-only cues can remain discriminative for abusive speech, even without transcripts
28
. Shared tasks such as DravidianLangTech@NAACL 2025
3
further underscore the practical need for multimodal and multilingual moderation systems. Parallel work also addresses robustness and interpretability, including hateful segment localisation and evaluation under synthetic or adversarial perturbations
1
;
22
;
21
.

2.3
Few-Shot Learning and Low-Resource Challenges

Low-resource audio abuse detection remains difficult because labelled data is scarce, annotation is expensive, and the notion of abuse is often culturally sensitive. Performance is further affected by variability in speakers, accents, recording conditions, and code-mixing practices
9
;
27
. Few-shot approaches remain relatively underexplored in this area, although recent work on cross-lingual meta-learning over ADIMA shows promising adaptation behaviour together with clear degradation in very low-shot regimes
26
.

In multilingual settings, transfer is complicated by dialectal variation, language family differences, script differences, and culturally specific notions of offensiveness. Synthetic data generation and cross-lingual augmentation offer one route to scale, but they also raise questions about fairness, authenticity, and cultural validity
17
;
16
;
24
. Promising directions therefore include contrastive few-shot adaptation, parameter-efficient fine-tuning of large audio encoders, multimodal few-shot learning, and stricter language- and speaker-disjoint evaluation protocols.

3
Methodology

3.1
Problem Formulation

We study abusive speech detection from audio under
zero-shot
and
few-shot
conditions. Given an audio segment
x\in\mathcal{X}
and a binary label
y\in\{0,1\}
indicating non-abusive or abusive speech, the goal is to learn a classifier
f:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
that generalises across languages
\ell\in\mathcal{L}
. Zero-shot inference uses no task-specific labelled audio, whereas few-shot learning assumes access to
k
labelled examples per class per language drawn from the training split. We cast the problem as one of aligning audio signals with label semantics in a shared contrastive embedding space.

3.2
CLIP and CLAP

CLIP
20
aligns image and text embeddings using a contrastive objective, while CLAP
6
replaces the image encoder with an audio encoder and learns joint audio-text representations. For a batch of
N
aligned audio-text pairs
(a_{i},t_{i})
, with normalised embeddings
z^{a}_{i}
and
z^{t}_{i}
, CLAP typically optimises a symmetric InfoNCE loss
31
:

\mathcal{L}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathcal{L}_{a\rightarrow t}+\mathcal{L}_{t\rightarrow a}\right),

(1)

\mathcal{L}_{a\rightarrow t}=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log\frac{\exp(\mathrm{sim}(z^{a}_{i},z^{t}_{i})/\tau)}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\exp(\mathrm{sim}(z^{a}_{i},z^{t}_{j})/\tau)},

(2)

with
\mathcal{L}_{t\rightarrow a}
defined analogously. Here,
\mathrm{sim}(\cdot,\cdot)
is cosine similarity and
\tau
is a temperature parameter. Because label descriptions can be encoded as text, this framework naturally supports zero-shot classification.

3.3
Audio Representation with CLAP

We use a pre-trained CLAP audio encoder to extract
d
-dimensional audio embeddings, with
d=512
in our implementation. Audio is resampled and padded or truncated to a fixed duration before encoding:

z^{a}=\frac{f_{\mathrm{audio}}(x)}{\lVert f_{\mathrm{audio}}(x)\rVert_{2}}.

(3)

These embeddings are used in three ways: for zero-shot prompt similarity, for few-shot supervised contrastive adaptation, and as features for downstream supervised classifiers.

3.4
Zero-shot Prompting

For zero-shot classification, we define two prompts
p_{0}
and
p_{1}
representing the non-abusive and abusive classes, respectively, for example:

“This audio contains hate speech”
and
“This audio does not contain hate speech”
. We compute normalised text embeddings:

z^{t}_{c}=\frac{f_{\mathrm{text}}(p_{c})}{\lVert f_{\mathrm{text}}(p_{c})\rVert_{2}},

(4)

and predict the label using prompt similarity:

\hat{y}=\arg\max_{c\in\{0,1\}}\ \mathrm{sim}(z^{a},z^{t}_{c}).

(5)

3.5
Few-Shot Contrastive Adaptation

To adapt CLAP to abusive speech detection in low-resource conditions, we construct support sets with
k
examples per class per language from the training data. These few-shot examples are used to refine the embedding space through supervised contrastive learning and then we consider two adaptation strategies. In
Projection-only adaptation
, the CLAP encoder is frozen and only a lightweight projection head is trained. In
Projection+fine-tuning
, the projection head is trained jointly with the final audio encoder blocks.

Given projected embeddings
u_{i}=g(z^{a}_{i})
, we optimise the supervised contrastive objective, based on
11
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{sup}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in I}\frac{-1}{|P(i)|}\sum_{p\in P(i)}\log

(6)

\displaystyle\frac{\exp\left(\mathrm{sim}(u_{i},u_{p})/\tau\right)}{\sum_{a\in A(i)}\exp\left(\mathrm{sim}(u_{i},u_{a})/\tau\right)},

where
P(i)
denotes the set of positive examples sharing the same label as
i
, and
A(i)
denotes all other samples in the batch. This loss encourages embeddings from the same class to form tighter clusters while increasing separation between classes. After adaptation, the resulting embeddings are used for downstream classification.

3.6
Downstream Classifiers

We evaluate two downstream classifiers on frozen and adapted embeddings: a Support Vector Machine (SVM) and a scikit-learn ANN-MLP. These models allow us to assess whether abuse-related information is linearly or non-linearly separable in the CLAP embedding space, and whether few-shot contrastive adaptation improves downstream decision boundaries.

Classifier selection

For each language and evaluation setting, we train both the SVM and the ANN-MLP on the adapted or frozen CLAP embeddings and select the higher macro-F1 result. This per-language selection is reported consistently across all tables and is not varied between shot sizes for a given language. We adopt this strategy because no single downstream classifier is uniformly strongest across all languages and transfer settings, a pattern consistent with prior work on embedding-based classification in low-resource multilingual settings. The SVM with an RBF kernel tends to outperform the ANN-MLP on languages with tighter cluster structure in the embedding space, while the ANN-MLP is marginally stronger in cases where class boundaries are non-linear and more diffusesed.

Representations and Models

Embeddings:
We use normalised CLAP audio embeddings as defined in Eq.
3
.

Zero-shot prompting:
We evaluate prompt-based similarity using the two label prompts described in Eq.

Few-shot adaptation:

Experimental Setups

We evaluate the proposed framework along two axes:

and

•

•

Frozen embeddings:

•

4.4
Implementation and Metrics

We implement CLAP feature extraction and adaptation in PyTorch and HuggingFacem using the Supervised Contrastive Loss

9
reported Macro-F1, we use Macro-F1 as the main comparison metric in this paper. We evaluate 0-, 1-, 5-, 10-, 25-, and 50-shot settings for supervised contrastive adaptation. Full hyper-parameter settings are provided in Appendix

Overall, the results show that CLAP provides a strong basis for cross-lingual abusive speech detection across all ten Indic languages. It is important to note that the ADIMA benchmark results reported by
9
reflect fully supervised systems trained on the complete per-language training sets, whereas our CLAP-based framework operates in a few-shot regime, using at most 50 labelled examples per class per language. The ADIMA figures therefore represent an upper-bound reference under full supervision rather than a directly comparable few-shot baseline. Under this framing, the degree to which our approach closes the gap to full supervision is the relevant measure of performance.

The strongest result under the proposed framework is obtained for Punjabi, where projection-only adaptation reaches 83.65 macro-F1 at 5-shot, marginally surpassing the fully supervised ADIMA benchmark of 83.40. Projection-only adaptation also performs strongly for Haryanvi (80.23 at 25-shot), Odia (79.67 at 25-shot), and Malayalam (78.18 at 50-shot), suggesting that CLAP-based representations transfer effectively to these low-resource Indic languages with very limited supervision.

For languages where a gap to the fully supervised ADIMA system remains — Bengali (76.34 vs. 79.10), Hindi (77.76 vs. 80.70), Kannada (76.67 vs. 78.40), and Tamil (72.50 vs. 75.20) — this gap should be interpreted in the context of the substantially different supervision regime. The key finding is not that CLAP uniformly exceeds full supervision, but that projection-only adaptation with as few as 5–25 labelled examples per class achieves results within 2–4 macro-F1 points of systems trained on the full dataset for the majority of languages. This represents a practically meaningful result for low-resource settings where full annotation is infeasible.

5.2
Leave-One-Language-Out Analysis

To better understand the role of target-language supervision, we compare the standard cross-lingual setting with a multilingual leave-one-language-out (LOLO) setup. In the cross-lingual setting, the target language is included in the multilingual training data, whereas in LOLO it is fully excluded and used only for testing. This comparison provides a stronger test of transfer: if performance remains stable under LOLO, the model is likely capturing language-agnostic cues of abusive speech rather than relying mainly on target-language-specific supervision.

Figures
2
and
3
present this comparison from two complementary perspectives. Figure
2
shows the language-wise difference between LOLO and cross-lingual macro-F1, while Figure
3
shows the mean macro-F1 across shot sizes. Across both adaptation settings, a consistent pattern emerges:
LOLO remains competitive with cross-lingual evaluation, but does not outperform it on average
. The mean performance curves in Figures

and
3(b)
show that cross-lingual training retains a small but persistent overall advantage, indicating that direct exposure to the target language still provides useful information.

At the same time, the language-wise comparison reveals a more nuanced picture. As shown in Figures
2(a)
and
2(b)
, LOLO outperforms cross-lingual for a subset of languages, most clearly for
Tamil
in both settings. Smaller LOLO gains are also observed for
Kannada
and
Bhojpuri
, and for
Punjabi
under projection+fine-tuning. These cases suggest that multilingual transfer can generalise well even when the target language is entirely absent from training. One plausible explanation is that the shared multilingual CLAP representation already captures some abuse-related acoustic cues in a form that transfers across languages.

However, LOLO does not produce consistent improvements across the full benchmark. In both adaptation settings, cross-lingual remains clearly stronger for languages such as
Haryanvi
and
Gujarati
, and the margins in favour of cross-lingual are often larger than the margins in favour of LOLO. This indicates that some languages benefit more strongly from target-language exposure, even in a multilingual training regime. The learned representation therefore appears to combine both transferable cross-lingual structure and language-specific information, with the relative importance of each varying across languages.

The shot-wise curves further show that the LOLO effect is
small and unstable rather than monotonic
. The gap between LOLO and cross-lingual fluctuates across shot sizes rather than widening or narrowing consistently. This suggests that the effect of excluding the target language is not determined solely by supervision volume, but also by the interaction between language identity, adaptation regime, and the structure of the pre-trained embedding space. Put differently, target-language exclusion is not uniformly harmful, but neither does it systematically improve multilingual transfer.

We therefore interpret LOLO not as a stronger alternative to cross-lingual training, but as a
transfer analysis
that quantifies how much the system depends on target-language supervision. The fact that LOLO remains close to cross-lingual performance in both settings supports the claim that the model learns partially language-agnostic representations of abusive speech. At the same time, the persistent average advantage of cross-lingual training indicates that transfer is meaningful but incomplete. A future study, in association with linguists and para-linguists with respect to language families, can reveal hidden associations, which could potentially be a follow-up work.

6
Conclusion

This paper investigated whether CLAP-based audio-text representations can support multilingual abusive speech detection directly from audio, particularly in low-resource and cross-lingual settings. Across the ADIMA benchmark, the results show that CLAP provides a strong representation space for abusive speech detection, with projection-only adaptation achieving the best overall cross-lingual average macro-F1 of
77.18
. At the language level, the strongest result was obtained for
Punjabi
, where projection-only adaptation reached
83.65
macro-F1 at
5-shot
, slightly surpassing the reported ADIMA benchmark of
83.40
.

The results also show that the benefits of few-shot contrastive adaptation are not uniform across languages. Some languages, such as
Gujarati
, achieved their best performance in the
0-shot
setting, whereas others, such as
Haryanvi
and
Odia
, benefited most from
25-shot
adaptation. Across the two adaptation strategies, projection-only adaptation generally proved more effective and stable than projection+fine-tuning, indicating that much of the useful task-specific information can be recovered through a lightweight projection space without extensive encoder updating.

Finally, the leave-one-language-out analysis showed that the model learns meaningful cross-lingual structure: LOLO remained close to standard cross-lingual performance, with average macro-F1 values of
76.99
for projection-only adaptation and
76.92
for projection+fine-tuning, compared with
77.18
and
76.97
in the corresponding cross-lingual settings. Overall, these findings suggest that contrastive audio-text models are a promising basis for multilingual audio abuse detection, while also highlighting that transfer remains language-dependent and incomplete. Future work should explore stronger prompt design, parameter-efficient adaptation, multimodal fusion, and deeper analysis of language-specific transfer behaviour.

Limitations

Our findings should be interpreted in light of several limitations. First, the experiments are conducted on a single multilingual benchmark, ADIMA, so the results may not generalise directly to other datasets, recording conditions, or non-Indic languages. Second, although CLAP is motivated partly by its zero-shot capability, the strongest results in this paper come from few-shot adaptation and downstream classification; zero-shot prompting is therefore only an auxiliary evaluation setting. Third, the few-shot results may be sensitive to support-set composition and optimisation choices, particularly in very low-shot regimes (1-shot and 5-shot). We fix random seeds for reproducibility and report results across six shot sizes (
k\in\{0,1,5,10,25,50\}
) rather than relying on a single condition, which partially mitigates this concern by revealing whether performance trends are consistent across data regimes. Nonetheless, we do not report variance across multiple random seeds, and results at individual shot sizes for individual languages should be interpreted with appropriate caution. Future work should include multi-seed evaluation to quantify support-set sampling variance, particularly for the 1-shot condition where instability is most likely. Fourth, we evaluate only two downstream classifiers, SVM and ANN-MLP, and compare only two lightweight adaptation strategies, so the conclusions do not extend to all possible fine-tuning or parameter-efficient methods. Finally, we do not conduct a dedicated fairness analysis across speakers, dialects, or culturally specific abusive expressions.

Ethics Statement

This study does not involve any personal or public data pointing to an individual or a group of individuals and thus does not break any ethical guidelines.

A.2
Hyper-parameters and Training Details

All experiments use a pre-trained CLAP
1
1

1

https://hf.co/laion/clap-htsat-unfused
audio encoder as the base representation model. Audio inputs are resampled to the target sampling rate required by the encoder and padded or truncated to a fixed maximum duration before feature extraction. For few-shot experiments, support sets are constructed by sampling
k
examples per class per language from the training split, with
k\in\{0,1,5,10,25,50\}
. The
k{=}0
setting corresponds to using frozen pre-trained CLAP embeddings without task-specific adaptation. We trained the model with 50 epochs with a 1e-4 Learning rate with
AdamW
optimizer with default warm up initialization. We trained the model on the Apple MPS back-end with the PyTorch metal library.

We study two adaptation variants. In the
projection-only
setting, the CLAP encoder is frozen and only a lightweight projection head is trained using supervised contrastive learning. In the
projection+fine-tuning
setting, the projection head is trained jointly with the final audio encoder blocks. The projection-only setup serves as the default adaptation strategy in the main experiments, whereas projection+fine-tuning is included to test whether limited encoder adaptation yields additional gains.

For supervised contrastive learning, we optimise a temperature-scaled supervised contrastive objective over mini-batches drawn from the support set. We used supervised contrastive loss
11
as the loss function for projection training. The main experiments evaluate multiple shot sizes rather than a single low-resource condition, since performance is sensitive to both support-set size and optimisation choices. In general, projection-only adaptation is more stable under higher learning rates, whereas projection+fine-tuning requires a smaller encoder learning rate to avoid disrupting the pre-trained representation space. Empirically, projection-only adaptation proved more consistent across languages, while deeper fine-tuning yielded only modest gains for a smaller subset of languages.

After adaptation, embeddings are extracted for all train and test samples and used as input to downstream classifiers. In the final experimental setup, we use two downstream models: a
Support Vector Machine (SVM)
and a
scikit-learn ANN-MLP
. These two models were retained because they consistently produced the strongest results across languages and transfer settings. The SVM provides a strong non-linear margin-based classifier for the CLAP embedding space, while the ANN-MLP offers a lightweight neural alternative that can capture non-linear structure without requiring end-to-end retraining of the audio encoder.

Model selection is based primarily on macro-F1, with accuracy used as a tie-breaker where necessary. Since the strongest-performing model varies across languages and evaluation settings, the appendix tables report the best language-wise configuration rather than assuming that a single downstream classifier is uniformly optimal across the benchmark.

Random seeds are fixed for few-shot sampling, representation adaptation, and downstream model training wherever applicable. Because few-shot support sets are small, results can vary with both shot size and optimisation settings. This is one reason why the main paper reports results across multiple shot values rather than relying on a single few-shot condition.

Additional Cross-Lingual and LOLO Visualisations

To complement the main results, we provide additional visual summaries of the cross-lingual and leave-one-language-out (LOLO) analyses in Figures
4
,
5
, and
6
. These plots are intended to reduce table fatigue and make the language-wise trends easier to compare visually.

Figure
4
presents the best cross-lingual macro-F1 scores by language for the ADIMA benchmark and for our two adaptation settings: projection-only adaptation and projection + partial fine-tuning. The plot makes clear that the proposed CLAP-based framework is competitive across all ten languages, with especially strong results for Punjabi, Haryanvi, and Odia. In particular, projection-only slightly surpasses the reported ADIMA benchmark for Punjabi (83.65 vs. 83.40), while remaining close to the benchmark for several other languages. At the same time, the figure also highlights that the strongest system is language-dependent: ADIMA remains better for Bengali, Hindi, Kannada, and Tamil, indicating that cross-lingual transfer quality is uneven across languages.

Figure
5
directly compares LOLO and cross-lingual macro-F1 across languages for both adaptation settings. This visualisation reinforces the discussion in Section
5.2
: LOLO remains highly competitive, but it does not consistently outperform the standard cross-lingual setting. For some languages, especially Tamil, and to a lesser extent Kannada and Bhojpuri, LOLO yields slightly stronger results, suggesting that multilingual transfer can remain effective even when the target language is excluded from training. However, for other languages such as Gujarati and Haryanvi, cross-lingual performance remains clearly higher, indicating that target-language exposure still provides useful information.

Finally, Figure
6
summarises the average macro-F1 across evaluation setups. The mean scores are all tightly clustered, with Cross-A achieving the highest overall average (77.18), followed by LOLO-A (76.99), Cross-B (76.97), and LOLO-B (76.92). This confirms that the differences between LOLO and cross-lingual settings are relatively small overall, even though the language-wise plots reveal more substantial local variation. Taken together, these figures support two main observations: first, CLAP-based representations transfer reasonably well across languages; second, target-language exclusion does not cause catastrophic degradation, but cross-lingual training retains a slight overall advantage.
