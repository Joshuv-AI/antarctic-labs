Latent trajectories of PTSD over 12 months following road trauma and their association with recovery trajectories
Authors: Somayeh Momenyan, Steven Taylor, Herbert Chan, John A. Taylor, John A. Staples, Devin R. Harris, Jeffrey R. Brubacher
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) is one of the most common psychological consequences following road trauma (RT), leading to physical and psychological impairments.Objectives: This study aims to identify distinct PTSD trajectories following RT and examine their association with recovery outcomes.Methods: We analyzed data from a prospective cohort of 1042 survivors recruited from Canadian emergency departments between July 2018 and March 2020. PTSD symptoms were assessed using the PTSD Checklist–Specific version (PCL-S) at 2, 4, 6, and 12 months post-injury. Health-related quality of life (HRQoL) was measured using the Short Form and the 5-level EuroQol instrument at baseline and follow-up. Self-reported recovery outcomes, including full recovery and return to work and daily activities, were also evaluated at these intervals.Results: Three distinct trajectories were identified for PTSD measures: Chronic, Delayed-Recovery, and Resilient. Participants in the Chronic and Delayed-Recovery trajectories were identified as high-risk groups. These individuals were characterized by high pre-injury HRQoL, followed by a sharp decline thereafter. They exhibit the most significant and persistent deficits in full recovery, return to work, and return to usual daily activities compared to the Resilient class. Patients in these classes are also more likely to be female and exhibit more pre-injury somatic symptoms, pain catastrophizing, psychological distress, no expectations for a fast recovery, higher injury severity, and greater injury pain.Conclusions: This study demonstrates the heterogeneity of PTSD symptom trajectories and emphasizes the importance of addressing PTSD early in the recovery process. The strong link between high-risk trajectories, pain catastrophizing, and greater injury pain suggests that early interventions should target the mutually reinforcing relationship between PTSD and chronic pain.

Introduction

Road trauma (RT) injuries are among the most prevalent traumatic events (Blevins et al., 2015). There are about 150,000 RT injuries annually in Canada, over 1,900 fatalities and around 9,000 serious injuries (Canadian Motor Vehicle Traffic Collision Statistics, 2022). Advancements in emergency medical care have reduced mortality from RT injuries, shifting the burden toward disability (Rissanen et al., 2020). Non-fatal injuries often lead to significant health, social, and economic challenges (Connelly & Supangan, 2006). A considerable proportion of individuals who survive severe injuries develop psychiatric conditions, particularly anxiety disorders, mood disorders, and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) (Ehlers et al., 1998). PTSD is one of the most common psychological consequences for RT survivors, leading to physical and psychological impairments (Heron-Delaney et al., 2013). PTSD symptoms include reexperiencing the traumatic event, avoidance and emotional numbing, and heightened arousal (Association AP, 2000).

While the psychological impact of traumatic events, such as PTSD, is often overlooked in favour of addressing physical injuries, research has shown that PTSD is associated with negative outcomes, including reduced life and job satisfaction, functional impairment, and overall lower physical health (Jackson et al., 2016; Lange et al., 2021). Furthermore, PTSD increases the risk of experiencing persistent symptoms and slower recovery after trauma (Scheenen et al., 2017; Stulemeijer et al., 2008). PTSD is also frequently associated with co-occurring psychiatric conditions, such as depression and anxiety (Stoll et al., 2000). Trauma patients often experience a significant decline in health-related quality of life (HRQoL) following major injuries (Momenyan et al., 2024). In injury research, HRQoL is a key outcome measure as it provides standardized insights into recovery patterns, encompassing physical, psychological, and functional well-being from the patient’s perspective (Kvarme et al., 2009). People with PTSD following RT injury experience a significantly lower quality of life compared to those without PTSD (Wang et al., 2005). Additionally, PTSD is associated with substantial healthcare costs and resource use (Stanicic et al., 2024).

Most RT studies have focused on patients with severe injuries (Holbrook et al., 1998; Holbrook & Hoyt, 2004; Ringburg et al., 2011), but there is evidence that even minor injuries can have long-term health consequences (Murgatroyd et al., 2016). For instance, approximately 20% of people who sustained minor to moderate severity RT injury had not returned to paid work two years post-injury (Gopinath et al., 2015). Additionally, many RT survivors are young, and the lasting effects of their injuries can significantly disrupt daily activities. Therefore, further studies on RT survivors are required to provide empirical evidence across a broader range of injury severities. In particular, a more comprehensive understanding of how PTSD influences HRQoL and social and occupational functioning across different levels of RT injury severity is essential for informing clinical care and recovery planning.

Understanding of PTSD has advanced substantially through the use of trajectory-based methods such as Latent Growth Mixture Modeling (LGMM), which identifies unobserved subgroups with similar symptom patterns over time. A review of 54 studies by Galatzer-Levy et al. (Galatzer-Levy et al., 2018) identified four consistent PTSD trajectories: resilience, recovery, chronicity, and delayed onset, helping to distinguish individuals who recover naturally from those needing targeted intervention. Despite the extensive literature on PTSD following RT injury, the application of LGMM and other trajectory-based clustering methods in this population remains limited. The review by Galatzer-Levy et al. found that only a small number of trajectory-based studies included RT patients as a proportion of their samples, and to our knowledge only one published study has applied clustering methods specifically to identify PTSD trajectories following RT (Pozzato et al., 2020). This highlights the need for further trajectory-based research in RT populations using validated, symptom-level measures to better characterize the trajectories of PTSD symptoms and identify individuals at risk for chronic outcomes.

The first aim of this study was to identify distinct PTSD trajectories over 12-months of follow-up using LGMM in RT survivors with predominantly minor injuries who visited three participating emergency departments (EDs) in British Columbia (BC), Canada. The second aim was to examine trajectories of 12-month recovery outcomes, including HRQoL and self-reported recovery outcomes within each identified PTSD trajectory. The third aim was to compare economic costs, including healthcare and productivity costs, across identified PTSD trajectories. We also examined whether patients’ characteristics distinguish these trajectories.

Methods

Participants and procedures

This longitudinal cohort study enrolled RT survivors from three EDs in BC: Vancouver General Hospital in Vancouver, Royal Columbian Hospital in New Westminster, and Kelowna General Hospital in Kelowna. Participants were recruited between July 2018 and March 2020 and followed for 12 months after their injury. Eligible participants included RT survivors from all injury severity levels and five different road user categories (cyclists, pedestrians, motorcyclists, and motor vehicle drivers and passengers) who were at least 16 years old and presented at the ED within 24 h of a collision involving at least one motorized vehicle. 75% of participants were discharged directly from the ED and were not admitted to hospital, reflecting the inclusion of a broad spectrum of injury severity. Exclusions were made for non-residents of BC and those who died within 30 days of the injury. Data were collected at baseline and at 2, 4, 6, and 12 months following RT injury through interviews and medical record reviews. At baseline, research assistants conducted in-person interviews during ED visits or hospital stays, while a portion of interviews was carried out over the phone. Most interviews took place within one week of injury, over 98% were completed within two weeks, and all were finalized within 35 days, including participants with severe injuries who were initially unable to respond. Follow-up data were collected according to participant preference, using telephone calls, online surveys, self-administered paper questionnaires, or in-person interviews. For non-English speakers, interviews were conducted in Cantonese, French, Korean, Mandarin, Punjabi, and Vietnamese. The study received approval from the University of British Columbia’s research ethics board (certificate H18-00284). All participants gave informed consent, either in writing or verbally. For minors aged 16–18, both parental or guardian approval and the participant’s own assent were required. The study’s methodology has been described in detail elsewhere (Shum et al., 2021).

Measures

PTSD at 2, 4, 6, and 12 months post-injury was evaluated using the PTSD Checklist – Specific version (PCL-S) (Weathers et al., 1993), a DSM-IV–based 17-item self-report instrument designed to evaluate PTSD symptoms experienced over the past month. The questions are framed around ‘stressful experiences from the past’ and can be categorized into three disorder dimensions: reexperiencing, avoidance, and hyper-arousal. Responses are rated on a 5-point Likert scale, and the total score is calculated by summing the responses (range: 17–85), with higher scores indicating more severe PTSD symptoms. The PCL-S has demonstrated strong internal consistency and good convergent validity with clinician-administered PTSD measures (Weathers et al., 1993).

HRQoL was assessed at baseline and at 2, 4, 6, and 12 months post-injury using the Short Form 12 (SF-12) survey (Ware, 1993) and the 5-level EuroQol instrument (EQ-5D-5L) (Cheung et al., 2009). The baseline SF-12 measured HRQoL in the four weeks preceding the injury, while the EQ-5D-5L assessed HRQoL on the day before the injury. The SF-12 is a 12-item survey designed to assess health across eight dimensions: general health, physical role and functioning, social functioning, bodily pain, mental health, vitality, and emotional role. From these dimensions, the Physical Component Summary (PCS) and Mental Component Summary (MCS) scores were derived, (norm-based scoring; mean = 50, SD = 10) with higher scores indicating better physical and mental well-being, respectively. The SF-12 has demonstrated good internal consistency and validity for both PCS and MCS scales (Ware et al., 1996). The EQ-5D-5L measures health across five dimensions: mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression. It includes a descriptive questionnaire (EQ-5D) and a visual analog scale (EQ-VAS). Summary scores are calculated using the Canadian value (Xie et al., 2016). The EQ-5D-5L has demonstrated good construct validity and test–retest reliability across a broad range of populations and settings (Feng et al., 2021). Self-reported recovery outcomes at 2, 4, 6, and 12 months post-injury were assessed using questions on recovery (‘Have you fully recovered from the accident?’), return to work (‘Have you returned to work since the accident?’), and return to daily activities (‘Have you resumed your usual daily activities as before the accident?’).

Economic costs, including healthcare and productivity costs over one year following RT, were collected and have been previously described (Momenyan et al., 2025). Healthcare utilization data were obtained from Population Data BC (PopulationdataBC, 2023). Productivity losses were measured at 2, 4, 6, and 12 months post-injury using the iPCQ, a validated instrument designed to measure productivity losses, including absenteeism and reduced productivity at work (Bouwmans et al., 2015). All costs were adjusted for inflation to 2024 CAD dollars using the Statistics Canada Consumer Price Index (Statistics Canada, 2023).

At baseline, RT survivors’ characteristics were collected across four domains: sociodemographic, psychological, medical, and injury-related. Sociodemographic variables included age, sex, employment, education, ethnicity, living situation, duration of residence in Canada, and alcohol or cannabis use. Pre-injury psychological factors included somatic symptoms (PHQ-15) (Kocalevent et al., 2013), pain catastrophizing (Pain Catastrophizing Scale) (Sullivan et al., 1995), and psychological distress (PHQ-4) (Kroenke et al., 2009), all assessed for the two weeks prior to injury, with higher scores indicating greater symptom severity. The PHQ-15 (range: 0–30), PHQ-4 (range: 0–12), and Pain Catastrophizing Scale (range: 0–52) are well-validated instruments with good internal consistency (Kroenke et al., 2002; Kroenke et al., 2009; MJl, 1995). We also assessed immediate post-injury recovery expectations. Medical factors included number comorbidities, pre-injury complaints in the injured body area(s), and medication use. Pre-existing comorbidities reported by participants included conditions such as eye disease, arthritis, diabetes, respiratory disorders, heart disease, hypertension, stroke, epilepsy, kidney disease, psychiatric disorders, and any other conditions reported during the interview. Injury-related factors included the type of road user, the Injury Severity Score (ISS) (Barnard et al., 2013), pain resulting from the injury, and injury location. Pain was assessed using a Visual Analog Scale (VAS) (Todd et al., 1996). The VAS is a widely used and validated measure of pain intensity with good reliability (Hawker et al., 2011).

Statistical analysis

Data cleaning and statistical analyses were conducted using R (version 4.0.5) and Mplus (version 7.4). Descriptive statistics were used to to describe the participants at baseline, as well as their PTSD symptoms, recovery outcomes and cost-related outcomes. The analysis proceeded in three steps: first, a LGMM was applied to identify distinct classes of patients with different PTSD trajectory patterns during follow-up. Second, within each identified PTSD class, we estimated the trajectories of change in recovery outcomes throughout the follow-up period. Third, differences in cost-related outcomes and participant characteristics at baseline across identified trajectories of PTSD were assessed.

In LGMM, trajectory classes are identified by regressing a latent categorical variable (class) on latent growth parameters (e.g. intercept and slope), which capture patterns from repeated measures across time. To determine the optimal number of classes, a sequence of LGMMs ranging from 2 to 5 classes was compared using several criteria: Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), sample size-adjusted Bayesian Information Criterion (ABIC), the adjusted Lo-Mendell-Rubin likelihood ratio (ALMR LR) test, entropy, posterior probabilities, class sizes, and clinical interpretability (Nylund et al., 2007). Smaller AIC, BIC, and ABIC values reflect a better fit of the model. The ALMR LR test evaluates the k-class model against the (k-1)-class model, with a significant p-value indicating that the k-class model is favoured. Entropy, ranging from 0 to 1, measures the accuracy of classification, and values of 0.80 or greater are considered indicative of strong class separation (Clark 2010). Individuals were assigned to classes based on their highest posterior probabilities. An average posterior probability above 0.7 is recommended for reliable classification (Nagin & Nagin, 2005). After assignment, it is essential to assess class sizes and interpretability. Models were rejected if they lacked clinical interpretability or contained classes representing less than 1% of the total sample. For trajectory analyses to be more stable and precise, only participants with at least two PTSD measurements over time were included in the LGMM.

Within each identified PTSD trajectory class, we examined the trajectories of recovery outcomes including HRQoL, self-reported full recovery, and return to work and daily activities. These trajectories of recovery outcomes were estimated using generalized additive mixed model (GAMM) with a smoothing function of time and an interaction between time and PTSD class. The GAMM also adjusted for age, sex, ISS, injury pain, comorbidities number, somatic symptoms, pain catastrophizing, and psychological distress. The within-individual correlation was accounted for by random effects. For the return-to-work analysis, individuals without paid employment were excluded.

Differences in baseline characteristics and cost outcomes across PTSD trajectories were examined using Chi-square (or Fisher’s exact) tests for categorical variables and Kruskal–Wallis tests for continuous variables. Post-hoc comparisons were performed using Bonferroni-corrected pairwise comparisons, conducted with either the Chi-square test (or Fisher's exact test) or Mann-Whitney U test.

Missing data

LGMM handled missing outcome data using full-information maximum likelihood under a missing-at-random (MAR) assumption. Baseline characteristics differed significantly across follow-up completion groups, indicating non-random attrition (Supplementary Table 1). However, because missingness was associated with observed variables and little unmeasured information was available, the MAR assumption was considered reasonable. For self-reported recovery questions, if participants answered ‘Yes’ at a given time point but missed subsequent follow-up interviews, their positive response was carried forward to later time points rather than being treated as missing data. On the other hand, if a participant responded ‘No’ at a given time point and then missed subsequent follow-ups, they were classified as lost to follow-up. In GAMM, the Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) method was used to impute missing values for adjusted covariates. All baseline variables were considered in the imputation model, either as predictors or imputed variables.

Patterns of PTSD across time

Table 3 presents the fit criteria for linear and quadratic LGMMs, varying from two to five classes for PTSD. According to fit criteria, a quadratic three-class model was optimal. As seen from the parameter estimates in Table 4, the quadratic parameters were significant for most trajectories. Each class was labelled to reflect the overall shape of the 12-month trajectory. The three-class model for PTSD trajectories is illustrated in Figure 2. The Chronic PTSD class (n = 174, 16.7%) represents participants who started with high PTSD symptoms at 2 months and showed a gradual increase over time. This class includes individuals with persistent or worsening PTSD symptoms throughout the follow-up period. The Delayed-Recovery PTSD class (n = 30, 2.9%) includes participants who started with high PTSD scores at 2 months, which peaked at the 4-month follow-up before decreasing sharply over the next 6 months. This class represents individuals whose PTSD symptoms initially worsened but then recovered over time. The Resilient PTSD class (n = 838, 80.4%) indicates a PTSD trajectory that started low and remains consistently low throughout the follow-up. It represents individuals with low and stable PTSD symptoms, indicating resilience. Discussion

This study investigated the trajectories of PTSD among RT survivors and found significant variability in symptom experiences. Using a LGMM, we identified three distinct nonlinear PTSD trajectories: Chronic (16.7%), characterized by persistently severe symptoms and high vulnerability to poor recovery following the injury; Delayed-Recovery (2.9%), marked by initially high symptom levels followed by gradual improvement; and Resilient (80.4%), defined by consistently low symptom levels and minimal impact from the injury. These findings indicate that while the majority of road trauma survivors adapt well to their circumstances and are unlikely to require additional therapeutic intervention, a clinically meaningful minority follow more burdensome symptom courses that may benefit from targeted support. It is important to acknowledge, however, that we did not systematically capture psychological or pharmacological treatment received during the follow-up period. This is particularly relevant for interpreting the Delayed-Recovery trajectory, where symptom improvement emerged only after six months and may partly reflect the effects of intervention received during this window rather than spontaneous natural recovery. Conversely, the persistence of symptoms in the Chronic class may have been reinforced by the absence of timely treatment. Without treatment data, we are unable to disentangle the natural course of PTSD from treatment-related change, which limits causal interpretation of the trajectories and should be considered when comparing our findings with those from randomized trials where treatment exposure is measured.

The three-trajectory solution identified in the present study aligns with patterns reported across diverse trauma populations. The foundational review by Galatzer-Levy et al. synthesising 54 studies identified four recurring trajectories, resilience, recovery, chronicity, and delayed onset, with the delayed onset trajectory appearing least frequently across studies (Galatzer-Levy et al., 2018). Our findings are consistent with this framework, though we did not observe a delayed onset class. Similarly, Chen et al. identified three analogous trajectories, Resilience, Recovery, and Moderate-chronic, among intensive care patients assessed between one and four months post-trauma in China (Chen et al., 2024), and Ravn et al. reported three comparable classes, Resilient, Recovering, and Chronic, over six months following whiplash injury (Ravn et al., 2019), supporting the robustness of this three-class structure across different trauma types and cultural settings.

Some studies have identified additional trajectory classes beyond those observed here. Osenbach et al., studying traumatic injury survivors over 12 months, reported a fourth Relapsing/Remitting class characterised by moderate, fluctuating symptoms (Osenbach et al., 2014). The absence of this class in our data is likely attributable to methodological differences: our cohort was recruited from the ED and encompassed a wide range of injury severities, producing a large Resilient class, whereas Osenbach's sample comprised seriously injured patients enrolled in a randomized clinical trial where treatment status meaningfully influenced trajectory membership. Similarly, a study of Australian RT survivors identified four trajectories over 12 months, including a Worsening class not observed in our data (Pozzato et al., 2020). As noted by Galatzer-Levy et al., worsening trajectories are less consistently observed across studies and tend to emerge in cohorts with repeated or cumulative stressor exposure (Galatzer-Levy et al., 2018), which may explain their absence in our single-incident RT sample. Zuiden et al. also identified four trajectories, Resilient, Recovery, Chronic, and Delayed Onset, over one year following suspected serious injury (van Zuiden et al., 2022). Collectively, these comparisons highlight that while the core trajectory types are broadly replicable, variation in clinical context, recruitment setting, treatment exposure, and methodology meaningfully shapes trajectory solutions.

Our second aim was to estimate the trajectories of HRQoL and self-reported recovery within each PTSD class. Functional ability, including the ability to return to work and daily activities, is a key measure of health, as its loss leads to a decline in independence (Kelley-Quon et al., 2010). As our results showed, the greatest loss in HRQoL scores across all three classes occurred within the first two months of follow-up, with the most significant decline observed in the Chronic class, followed by Delayed-Recovery and Resilient. Additionally, HRQoL scores did not return to pre-injury levels at the 12-month follow-up in any class. This trend aligns with prior studies indicating that the most substantial decrease in HRQoL occurs soon after an injury, with many patients failing to regain their pre-injury HRQoL by the final follow-up (Littleton et al., 2014). Regarding self-reported recovery outcomes, the most significant impairment was observed in the Chronic class, followed by the Delayed-Recovery and Resilient classes. Consistent with our findings, a systematic review by Lin et al. showed a negative association between PTSD and return to work (Lin et al., 2016). Additionally, Lin reported return to work rates of only 31% to 63% following an occupational injury. Another study by Chu et al. found that about 10% of injured workers were unable to resume work even six years post-injury (Chu et al., 2019). Severe psychological symptoms within the first year following the injury were identified as a major risk factor for failing to return to work six years later. Our results indicate that patients in both the Chronic and Delayed-Recovery trajectories incur higher healthcare and productivity loss costs compared to those in the Resilient class. These findings align with the results of Chan et al., who demonstrated that healthcare costs were significantly higher for RT patients with PTSD than for those without PTSD (Chan et al., 2003). Similarly, Switzer et al. found that individuals with PTSD utilized more outpatient and inpatient services and were prescribed a greater variety of psychotropic medications than those without PTSD (Schwarz & Kowalski, 1992).

We also aimed to identify the characteristics of participants in high-risk groups following RT injury. These high-risk groups include individuals in the ‘Chronic’ and ‘Delayed-Recovery’ trajectories, comprising 20% of the total participants. Patients in these groups are more likely to be female and exhibit more pre-injury somatic symptoms, pain catastrophizing, psychological distress, no expectations for a fast recovery, higher ISS, and greater injury-related pain. The results of the present study were consistent with previous research, which also reported that men, compared to women, are more likely to be in the lowest PTSD scores group (Orcutt et al., 2004). This may be due to the fact that females tend to be less satisfied with their overall health status (Rissanen et al., 2017), experience greater fear and helplessness (Iteke et al., 2011) and perceive higher levels of threat (Olff et al., 2007). The association between a history of psychological disorders and PTSD has been extensively documented in the literature (Chossegros et al., 2011). However, it is important to acknowledge that elevated baseline psychological distress in the Chronic or Delayed-Recovery trajectories may partly reflect pre-existing PTSD or prior trauma history rather than solely representing a risk factor for new-onset PTSD following the index injury. Pain catastrophizing is hypothesized to be associated with a greater risk of PTSD following injury, as individuals who catastrophize pain often exaggerate pain-related fear, feel helpless in managing it, and ruminate on their discomfort. Consistent with previous research (Sullivan et al., 2011), our results supported this hypothesis. Ravn et al. found that initial higher pain after a whiplash injury was associated with an increased probability of being in the recovering and chronic trajectories (Ravn et al., 2019). Patients with severe pain may be more likely to appraise their situation negatively, potentially increasing the risk of PTSD.

The pronounced differences between the trajectory groups highlight the importance of early identification of individuals at heightened risk for poor recovery, enabling the delivery of targeted interventions aimed at improving long-term outcomes and supporting return to work and daily functioning. Additionally, since individuals in the Delayed-Recovery trajectory began to show improvement only after six months, providing interventions within the first six months post-injury may help accelerate recovery for some RT survivors. These findings also underscore the need for long-term rehabilitation strategies, as even those in the Resilient class did not achieve full recovery within one year, emphasizing that sustained support may be essential for optimizing post-injury health and quality of life.

Strengths and Limitations: The key strengths of this study include its prospective design, which spans five waves over a 12-month period, and its use of validated scales for measurements. Additionally, to enhance generalizability, we did not impose restrictions based on injury severity or language. However, the study also has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First the patient characteristics captured in our study were not comprehensive. Future studies should consider additional factors that were not collected in our study, such as social support, litigation status, peritraumatic dissociation, and perceived threat to life (Ozer et al., 2003). Furthermore, we did not capture or track key events that occurred during the 12-month follow-up period, such as the receipt of psychological or pharmacological treatment, changes in social support over time, or occurrence of additional stressors (e.g. litigation-related adversity). These unmeasured factors may have influenced symptom trajectories and should be considered when interpreting observed PTSD patterns. Second, participants were not formally screened for pre-existing PTSD prior to enrolment, and PTSD symptoms were measured without restricting attribution to the index RT event. It is therefore possible that some individuals in the Chronic or Delayed-Recovery trajectories were experiencing persistent symptoms related to prior traumatic experiences rather than solely the index injury. The elevated psychological distress scores observed among Chronic and Delayed-Recovery classes members at baseline are consistent with this possibility, as pre-existing psychological vulnerability may have contributed to their elevated symptom profiles. Third, PTSD symptoms were assessed using the PCL-S, a DSM-IV–based self-report instrument, rather than a clinician-administered diagnostic interview. Although the PCL-S has strong psychometric properties, differences between DSM-IV and DSM-5 diagnostic criteria, including the addition of negative alterations in cognition and mood, may limit direct comparability with more recent studies using DSM-5–based measures, and may have influenced trajectory classification. In addition, while a range of PCL-S cutoff scores (Barnard et al., 2013; Bouwmans et al., 2015; Chen et al., 2024; Clark 2010; Hawker et al., 2011; Kocalevent et al., 2013; Kroenke et al., 2002; Kroenke et al., 2009; Kroenke et al., 2009; MJl, 1995; Nagin & Nagin, 2005; Nylund et al., 2007; PopulationdataBC, 2023; Statistics Canada, 2023; Sullivan et al., 1995; Todd et al., 1996) has been used to indicate probable PTSD (Bressler et al., 2018), the present study focused on symptom trajectories rather than diagnostic classification. Accordingly, participants assigned to the Chronic and Delayed-Recovery classes may not all meet full diagnostic criteria for PTSD; rather, these groups likely reflect individuals with persistently elevated or fluctuating symptom severity over time. This distinction is important for interpretation, as the findings capture longitudinal patterns of symptom burden rather than PTSD status based on diagnostic criteria. Fourth, recall bias may have affected self-reported pre-injury HRQoL, although baseline interviews were conducted within 7 days of injury for most participants. Fifth, selection bias is also possible, as older individuals, those with severe pain, and survivors who did not seek hospital care may be underrepresented. Finally, despite appropriate MAR-based methods, the 18% loss to follow-up remains a limitation. Thus, our findings should be interpreted with caution, particularly regarding the generalizability of results to the specific sub-population that was lost.

Conclusions

This prospective study underscores the heterogeneity of PTSD symptom trajectories over 12 months following RT injury. We identified three distinct classes, with 16.7% of participants following a ‘Chronic’ trajectory, indicating that a significant subset of patients does not recover from PTSD symptoms within a year post-injury. Patients in the Chronic trajectory had persistently low HRQoL and higher healthcare costs. Most individuals in this group did not report full recovery by the end of the first-year post-injury. Thus, directly addressing PTSD symptoms may be a crucial part of the recovery process. This group also exhibited higher levels of pre-injury psychosocial disorders and physical pain at the time of hospital admission. Consequently, early interventions targeting pain management and psychosocial disorders may help mitigate PTSD symptoms and improve long-term recovery outcomes following RT injury.
