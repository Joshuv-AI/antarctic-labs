Beckwith–Wiedemann spectrum exhibiting a 46,XY karyotype caused by genome-wide paternal uniparental heterodisomy: a case report
Authors: Satoshi Hara, Tomohiko Sato, Osamu Shimokawa, Ritsuko Pooh, Yayoi Watanabe, Musashi Kubiura-Ichimaru, Hidenobu Soejima
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundsPatients with genome-wide paternal uniparental disomy (GWpUPD) usually exhibit clinical features of Beckwith–Wiedemann syndrome (BWS) and a 46,XX karyotype, with all chromosomes showing isodisomy. To date, male patients with GWpUPD and a complete 46,XY karyotype, specifically involving heterodisomy, have not been described.ResultsWe report a male infant exhibiting classical BWS clinical features. DNA methylation analyses showed paternal-specific methylation across multiple imprinted loci, suggesting GWpUPD. Genetic analysis of autosomes and sex chromosomes indicated two distinct paternal genomes in peripheral blood leukocytes, whereas a normal biparental genome was detected in other tissues under chimeric conditions. These findings indicated that the patient had genome-wide paternal uniparental heterodisomy (GWpUPhD). The SNP array revealed the presence of one copy of the X chromosome and one copy of the Y chromosome, the patient is a chimera composed of 46,XY biparental cells (with maternal X) and 46,XY GWpUPhD cells (with paternal X).ConclusionsThis is the first report of a male patient with a GWpUPhD chimera. We propose a potential mechanism of GWpUPhD formation. Our findings expand the molecular spectrum of GWpUPD and provide valuable insights into its pathogenesis in chimeric conditions. Introduction

Uniparental disomy (UPD) occurs when both copies of a chromosome are derived from one parent. If the chromosome contains imprinted genes, imprinting disorders may arise due to the aberrant expression of these genes. Conversely, if pathogenic variants in recessive or tumor suppressor genes are present, there is an increased risk of autosomal recessive disorders or tumor development [1]. UPD can be classified into two types: isodisomy (UPiD), in which two identical copies of a chromosome are inherited from the same parent, and heterodisomy (UPhD), in which two different homologous chromosomes are inherited from the same parent.

Beckwith–Wiedemann syndrome (BWS; OMIM#130650) is an imprinting disorder characterized by diverse phenotypes and an increased risk of embryonal tumors. Due to phenotypic variability, the BWS spectrum (BWSp) scoring system is recommended for diagnosis [2]. Patients with a total score of ≥ 4 can be diagnosed with classical BWS without molecular testing, whereas patients with a score of ≥ 2 and positive molecular test results are diagnosed with atypical BWS or isolated lateralized overgrowth. BWS is caused by (epi)genetic alterations at chromosome 11p15.5, which contains two imprinting control regions: H19/IGF2:IG-DMR (ICR1) and KCNQ1OT1:TSS-DMR (ICR2) [2]. Paternal UPD of 11p15.5 (upd [11] pat) is a major cause of BWS and is detected in approximately 20% of patients. Among these, approximately 8% show paternal UPD affecting all chromosomes (genome-wide paternal uniparental disomy; GWpUPD) [3]. Most patients with GWpUPD exhibit clinical features of BWS and have a 46,XX karyotype with genome-wide paternal uniparental isodisomy (GWpUPiD) [4–11]. Although one male patient with 46,XX/46,XY chimerism has been reported [12], male GWpUPD patients with a complete 46,XY karyotype have not yet been described.

Here, we report a male patient with clinical features of BWS and a complete 46,XY karyotype, exhibiting two distinct paternal genomes under chimeric conditions, a phenomenon referred to as genome-wide paternal uniparental heterodisomy (GWpUPhD). To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of a GWpUPhD chimera in a patient with a 46,XY karyotype. We propose a potential mechanism underlying the development of GWpUPhD chimeras.

Materials and methods

DNA preparation

Genomic DNA (gDNA) was extracted from peripheral blood leukocytes (PBL), oral swabs, and fingernail/toenail samples of the patient using a DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (QIAGEN GmbH, Hilden, Germany). gDNA was also extracted from PBL of the parents.

DNA polymorphism analyses

Polymorphic analyses were performed using short tandem repeat (STR) markers on chromosomes 11 and X with our in-house primer sets and the GlobalFiler PCR Amplification Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). PCR products were separated using SeqStudio and analyzed using GeneMapper software (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA).

Bisulfite pyrosequencing

One microgram of gDNA was subjected to sodium bisulfite conversion using the EZ Bisulfite Kit (Zymo Research, Irvine, CA, USA). PCR products were analyzed by pyrosequencing using the PyroMark Q24 system (QIAGEN GmbH), as previously described [13]. The gDNA extracted from PBL of control children (n = 24) was used as a control. The average of two independent analyses was used as the methylation index (MI, %). The corrected MI of each DMR in the samples was calculated based on the observed MI values of the controls.

Multiplex ligation-dependent probe amplification (MLPA) and methylation-specific MLPA (MS-MLPA)

For copy number variation and DNA methylation analyses of imprinted loci, MLPA and MS-MLPA were performed using the SALSA MLPA Probemix (ME034 and ME030; MRC Holland, Amsterdam, Netherlands) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

SNP microarray and data analyses

Trio-based SNP microarray analysis was performed using a CytoScan 750 K Array (Affymetrix, Santa Clara, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Data were visualized using Chromosome Analysis Suite (ChAS v4.5; Affymetrix). Chimeric ratios were calculated using ChAS and Rawcopy software (http://rawcopy.org).

Results

Case presentation

The male infant was the first-born child of non-consanguineous, healthy Japanese parents and was born following spontaneous conception at 38 weeks and 1 day of gestation. His birth parameters were as follows: length; 48.5 cm (+ 0.2 standard deviation [SD]), weight; 2,828 g (+ 0.02 SD), and occipitofrontal circumference; 31.5 cm (–1.12 SD). At 5 years and 2 months of age, his weight and length were 19.1 kg (+ 0.4 SD) and 109.5 cm (+ 0.4 SD), respectively. No developmental psychomotor abnormalities were observed. The following BWS-related clinical features were observed: right-sided upper- and lower-limb-dominant lateralized overgrowth (2 points), umbilical hernia (1 point), transient neonatal hypoglycemia (1 point), nevus flammeus on the forehead (1 point), bilateral kidney enlargement (1 point), and polyhydramnios and placentomegaly (1 point). The placental weight exceeded the 90th percentile and showed no pathological abnormalities. The patient’s total BWSp score was 7; therefore, he was diagnosed with classical BWS (Table S1). Multiple retroperitoneal masses were found during the fetal period, and intramuscular and subcutaneous masses were identified in the right femur at 18 months of age; however, these masses spontaneously regressed. A right spermatic cord lipoma was present and showed progressive enlargement at five years of age. Until approximately nine months of age, the patient had low ACTH levels with relatively elevated cortisol levels, suggesting adrenal Cushing syndrome; however, both ACTH and cortisol levels spontaneously normalized. Chromosomal analysis using PBL showed a normal karyotype (46,XY[20]), and high-resolution banding at 11p revealed no abnormalities (Figure S1).

Molecular studies

To investigate BWS-related molecular features in the patient, we performed genetic and epigenetic analyses using gDNA from PBL of the patient and his parents. Bisulfite pyrosequencing of ICR1 and ICR2 revealed MIs of 93.5% and 5.7%, respectively (Fig. 1A). MLPA showed that all imprinted loci harbored two normal copies, whereas MS-MLPA exhibited paternal-specific methylation patterns (Fig. 1B, S2, and S3). Bisulfite pyrosequencing analysis further confirmed paternal-specific methylation patterns across 16 imprinted DMRs (Figure S4), suggesting GWpUPD. STR analyses of chromosome 11 revealed two distinct paternal alleles with nearly identical peak heights, whereas no maternal alleles were detected (Fig. 1C). Genome-wide STR analysis identified two paternal alleles with similar peak heights in the patient’s PBL, whereas an additional maternal allele was present in oral swabs and fingernail samples (Fig. 1D and S5). These results strongly suggested that the patient had GWpUPhD, characterized by a chimeric state consisting of GWpUPhD cells and normal biparental cells. In addition, the proportion of GWpUPhD cells appeared to be higher in PBL than in other tissues.

Because the patient with a 46,XY karyotype had two distinct paternal alleles on the autosomes, we further analyzed the parental origin of the patient’s sex chromosomes. In the patient’s PBL, STR markers from the Y chromosome showed only paternal alleles, as expected, whereas those from the X chromosome also exclusively exhibited paternal alleles, with no maternal peaks detected (Fig. 2A). STR analysis of the sex chromosomes in oral swabs and nail samples showed both parental alleles derived from the paternal X chromosome (Xp) and maternal X chromosome (Xm) were detected in oral swabs and fingernails; however, only the maternal allele was detected in toenails (Fig. 2A and S6). Furthermore, SNP microarray analysis showed two copies of autosomes and one copy each of the X and Y chromosomes in both the patient’s PBL and oral mucosa, indicating a complete 46,XY karyotype without a detectable 46,XX cell population (Fig. 2B). A Mendelian inheritance error analysis also revealed variants not inherited from the mother across chromosomes 1–22 and X (Figure S7). Taken together, these results suggested that the patient exhibited a chimeric condition consisting of normal biparental cells carrying Xm (46,XmY) and GWpUPhD cells carrying Xp (46,XpY).

Next, we analyzed chimeric ratios in several tissues in addition to the patient’s PBL. STR analyses of oral swabs and fingernail samples suggested that the oral mucosa and fingernails contained GWpUPhD cells at a lower chimeric ratio than PBL. However, toenail samples showed approximately one maternal allele and one paternal allele, suggesting that toenails consisted almost exclusively of normal biparental cells and that the chimeric ratio was extremely low (Figure S5). To determine precise chimeric ratios in each tissue, MI-based chimeric ratios at nine gametic DMRs were calculated using MIs obtained from the patient’s PBL and oral swabs. Consistent with the STR analyses, the average chimeric ratios across nine gametic DMRs were 85.7% in PBL, 41.1% in the right oral swab, and 46.2% in the left oral swab (Table S2). We did not analyze the DNA methylation status of nails because of their high variability and lack of reproducibility. In addition, analysis of the chimeric ratio for the X chromosome in oral mucosa revealed 37% using ChAS and 40% using Rawcopy, both consistent with the MI-based chimeric ratios (Figure S8). Taken together, these results indicated that the chimeric ratio in the patient’s PBL was higher than that in other tissues.

Discussion

The most common mechanism for GWpUPD in individuals with a 46,XX karyotype involves failure of maternal genome replication in the zygote, followed by endoreplication of the paternal genome in one daughter cell after the first cell division [14]. This results in GWpUPiD in one daughter cell, whereas the other retains a biparental genome. Recently, Sheppard et al. reported a male case of a 46,XX/46,XY chimera with GWpUPD. They proposed several mechanisms by which GWpUPD chimeras with a 46,XY karyotype may arise: (i) Dispermy – fertilization of an oocyte by two spermatozoa, one carrying an X chromosome and the other a Y chromosome, followed by asymmetric division into one biparental daughter cell (46,XmY) and one haploid daughter cell (23,Xp). Endoreplication of the paternal genome in the haploid daughter cell results in GWpUPD (46,XpXp) (Fig. 2C). (ii) Fertilization involving an anuclear polar body – an oocyte and an anuclear polar body are fertilized separately by a Y-bearing sperm and an X-bearing sperm, respectively. The zygote derived from the oocyte (carrying the maternal Xm genome) and the paternal Y genome develops into normal biparental cells (46,XmY). In contrast, the zygote derived from the anuclear polar body and the X-bearing sperm undergoes endoreplication of the paternal genome, leading to GWpUPD [7]. In all reported mechanisms, the resulting GWpUPD is characterized by complete GWpUPiD (46,XpXp).

In contrast, the patient in the present study exhibited GWpUPhD and carried two distinct haploid genomes derived from his father (46,XpY). Because this condition cannot be explained by the abovementioned mechanisms, we propose the following mechanism: fertilization of an oocyte by two spermatozoa (an X-bearing sperm and a Y-bearing sperm), followed by endoreplication of the paternal genome containing the Y chromosome, resulting in a tetraploid zygote. The zygote subsequently divided into biparental daughter cells (46,XmY) and paternal uniparental heterodisomic daughter cells (46,XpY), ultimately forming a chimera (Fig. 2D). The patient was born following natural conception, consistent with previous reports of GWpUPiD [4, 7, 15]. This observation suggests that the proposed model represents a physiological phenomenon that occurs independently of assisted reproductive technology.

It is important to identify GWpUPD among patients with upd [11] pat because of the risks of miscarriage, preterm delivery, and tumor development [1]. Although the prenatal and postnatal masses regressed spontaneously, the right spermatic cord lipoma exhibited progressive enlargement and required careful monitoring. Moreover, the potential impact of varying chimeric ratios across tissues and cell lineages warrants further investigation. Additionally, XX/XY chimerism is associated with an increased risk of germ cell tumors [16]. Nevertheless, because this patient had GWpUPhD with a 46,XY karyotype, the risks of gonadal malignancy and autosomal recessive diseases are likely lower than those reported by Sheppard et al. for 46,XX/46,XY chimeric cases [7]. In addition, GWpUPD cases require consideration of the clinical manifestations of other imprinting disorders [1, 5]. Paternal-biased DNA methylation patterns at other imprinted loci suggest that the patient may develop clinical features associated with other imprinting disorders, such as Kagami-Ogata syndrome (upd [14] pat) and Angelman syndrome (upd [15] pat). However, no clinical features of other imprinting disorders were observed in this patient. These results are consistent with previous observations that many patients with GWpUPD exhibit BWS features and are likely less affected by other imprint loci [1, 4].

Previous studies have reported a higher proportion of GWpUPD cells in mesoderm-derived tissues, particularly PBLs, compared with ectoderm-derived tissues such as the buccal mucosa and nails [17, 18]. Based on these observations, it has been proposed that mesoderm-derived tissues, particularly the hematopoietic lineage, may be more permissive to the proliferation and differentiation of cells with profound epigenetic abnormalities, such as GWpUPD. The marked tissue-specific differences observed in our patient suggest that GWpUPhD may similarly exhibit tissue-specific distribution patterns. Additionally, the differing chimeric ratios observed between the fingernails and toenails in the patient suggest that an uneven distribution of GWpUPhD and biparental cells occurred during limb bud formation. Although the MI-based chimeric ratio in the left oral swab was higher than that in the right (p = 0.0499, Wilcoxon rank-sum test), the patient showed dominant lateralized overgrowth on the right side. This discrepancy is likely attributable to differences in the distribution of GWpUPhD cells between the oral mucosa and limb tissues.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this is the first report of a male patient with a GWpUPhD chimera consisting of 46,XpY and 46,XmY cell populations. Our findings provide valuable insights into the pathogenesis of GWpUPD in chimeric conditions. In addition, the possibility of future clinical manifestations unique to GWpUPhD chimeras with 46,XpY, which have not been observed in cases with 46,XpXp, should be considered.
