Hyper-Operations and Extension of Scalars from 𝔽 1 \mathbb{F}_{1} to ℤ \mathbb{Z}
Luqiao Xu

Abstract
. The additive structure of 𝔽 1 \mathbb{F}_{1} -modules (in the sense of Segal’s Γ \Gamma -sets) differs fundamentally from that of abelian groups: addition is encoded through a family of n n -ary hyper-operations that are multivalued and do not satisfy classical associativity. We establish a law of generalized associativity showing that, despite this failure of strict associativity, all n n -ary sums are controlled by successive binary operations. This enables us to construct an extension of scalars functor − ⊗ 𝔽 1 ℤ : 𝔽 1 𝐌𝐨𝐝 → 𝐀𝐛 -\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\mathbb{Z}:\mathbb{F}_{1}\mathbf{Mod}\to\mathbf{Ab} that universally strictifies the hyper-additive structure of 𝔽 1 \mathbb{F}_{1} -modules into classical abelian group addition. We prove this functor is left adjoint to the Eilenberg-MacLane functor H : 𝐀𝐛 → 𝔽 1 ​ 𝐌𝐨𝐝 H:\mathbf{Ab}\to\mathbb{F}_{1}\mathbf{Mod} . Extending to the multiplicative setting, we obtain an adjunction − ⊗ 𝔽 1 ℤ : 𝔽 1 𝐀𝐥𝐠 ⇆ 𝐂𝐑𝐢𝐧𝐠 : H -\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\mathbb{Z}:\mathbb{F}_{1}\mathbf{Alg}\leftrightarrows\mathbf{CRing}:H between commutative 𝔽 1 \mathbb{F}_{1} -algebras and commutative rings. This recovers Deitmar’s monoid ring construction for spherical monoid algebras and provides a base change mechanism needed for absolute algebraic geometry.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Motivation: Hyperfields and the field with one element

The notion of a “field with one element,” denoted
\mathbb{F}_{1}
, originates in Tits’ work on spherical buildings, where certain combinatorial formulas suggested the existence of geometric objects “over
\mathbb{F}_{1}
”. While
\mathbb{F}_{1}
cannot exist as a classical field, various frameworks have been proposed to give it mathematical meaning, including Deitmar’s monoid schemes
[
6
]
, Toën-Vaquié’s relative schemes
[
10
]
, Lorscheid’s blueprints
[
9
]
, Connes-Consani’s
\mathbb{S}
-algebras
[
3
,
4
]
, and Borger’s
\lambda
-rings
[
2
]
.

One particular challenge in developing
\mathbb{F}_{1}
-geometry is understanding its “additive” structure. Deitmar’s approach, by considering monoids, effectively neglects the existence of addition altogether, whereas Lorscheid’s blueprints involve partially defined addition, incorporating the monoid case. In this paper, we propose the model of
\mathbb{F}_{1}
-modules as
\Gamma
-sets
, following Connes-Consani’s framework
[
3
]
. This allows for even more possibilities, namely multiply defined addition, or hyper-operation.

In hyperstructure theory
[
8
,
5
]
, the notion of binary operation is generalized to a
hyper-operation
: a map
\oplus:H\times H\to\mathcal{P}(H)
assigning to each pair of elements not a unique sum, but a
set
of possible sums. This accounts for the possibility of an empty sum, the classic one-element sum, or a multiply defined sum, and is naturally linked to
\mathbb{F}_{1}
-geometry.

However, classical hyperfields have certain limitations: they capture only
binary
hyper-operation and impose axioms (existence of additive inverses, distributivity, associativity) that don’t extend naturally beyond fields. To overcome this deficiency, we study
n
-ary
hyper-operations in the most general sense, and deal with the problem of associativity in
\mathbb{F}_{1}
-modules.

1.2.
Main results

Our first main result establishes the coherence property that controls all hyper-operations:

Theorem A

(Law of Generalized Associativity)
.

Let
X
be an
\mathbb{F}_{1}
-module, let
I=\{1,\ldots,n\}
, and let
\{I_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
be a partition of
I
. For any collection of subsets
\{A_{i}\}_{i\in I}
of
X(1_{+})
, we have

\bigoplus_{i\in I}A_{i}\subseteq\bigoplus_{j=1}^{m}\left(\bigoplus_{i\in I_{j}}A_{i}\right).

This shows that
n
-ary sums can be “computed” through successive operations over any partition. Although strict equality does not hold, the inclusion reveals that all information about
n
-ary sums is
controlled by
lower-order operations. In particular, all
n
-ary sums are controlled by successive binary operations, for example

a\oplus b\oplus c\subseteq(a\oplus b)\oplus c

Exploiting the Law of Generalized Associativity, we construct the extension of scalars functor:

Theorem B

(Extension of scalars for modules)
.

There exists a functor

-\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\mathbb{Z}:\mathbb{F}_{1}\mathbf{Mod}\to\mathbf{Ab}

left adjoint to the Eilenberg-MacLane functor
H:\mathbf{Ab}\to\mathbb{F}_{1}\mathbf{Mod}
. For any
\mathbb{F}_{1}
-module
X
and abelian group
M
, we have

\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbb{F}_{1}\mathbf{Mod}}(X,HM)\cong\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbf{Ab}}(X\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\mathbb{Z},M).

Explicitly, we construct
X\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\mathbb{Z}
as a quotient of the free abelian group
\mathbb{Z}[X(1_{+})]
by relations encoding the binary hyper-additive structure. By Theorem
A
, imposing relations for binary sums automatically ensures compatibility with all higher
n
-ary sums. The adjunction provides a universal “strictification” of hyper-addition into classical addition.

Extending to the multiplicative setting:

Theorem C

(Extension of scalars for algebras)
.

The functor
-\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\mathbb{Z}
extends to commutative
\mathbb{F}_{1}
-algebras, yielding an adjunction

-\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\mathbb{Z}:\mathbb{F}_{1}\mathbf{Alg}\leftrightarrows\mathbf{CRing}:H.

This construction gives the following satisfying results:

•

For a commutative ring
R
:
(HR)\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\mathbb{Z}\cong R

•

For a pointed monoid
M
:
(\mathbb{S}M)\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\mathbb{Z}\cong\mathbb{Z}[M]
(integral monoid ring)

In particular, we have
\mathbb{F}_{1}\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\mathbb{Z}\cong\mathbb{Z}
.

1.3.
Relation to existing work

Our construction relates to several existing approaches to
\mathbb{F}_{1}
-geometry:

Deitmar’s monoid schemes
[
6
]
.
Deitmar defines
\mathbb{F}_{1}
-modules as (pointed) commutative monoids and constructs the extension of scalars functor via the integral monoid ring construction
M\mapsto\mathbb{Z}[M]
. Our functor generalizes this: for spherical monoid algebras
\mathbb{S}M
, we recover
(\mathbb{S}M)\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\mathbb{Z}\cong\mathbb{Z}[M]
, while for more general
\mathbb{F}_{1}
-modules this yields results utilizing the inherent additive structure.

Borger’s
\lambda
-rings
[
2
]
.
Borger interprets
\mathbb{F}_{1}
-algebras as
\lambda
-rings, with the Witt vector functor playing a central role, and the extension of scalars functor interpreted as the forgetful functor. We discuss the relationship in detail in Section
5.6
.

Connes-Consani’s absolute geometry
[
3
,
4
]
.
Our work provides algebraic foundations for their geometric program. They introduce
\Gamma
-sets as
\mathbb{F}_{1}
-modules but do not construct extension of scalars explicitly. Our results provide the base change mechanism needed for their theory of absolute affine schemes.

Hyperstructure theory
[
5
,
9
]
.
Classical hyperfields use binary hyper-operations with axioms adapted from field theory. Our framework allows
n
-ary hyper-operations for all
n
and reveals that the relevant coherence condition is generalized associativity rather than axioms like existence of additive inverses. This connects
\mathbb{F}_{1}
-geometry to the broader theory of hyperstructures.

Beardsley’s plasmas
[
1
]
.
Beardsley and Nakamura propose plasmas (weakly unital commutative hypermagmas) to encode the hypersum structure in
\mathbb{F}_{1}
-modules, establishing an adjunction that captures information on the first two levels. Our approach could be seen as a further generalization: objects equipped with compatible families of
n
-ary hyper-operations satisfying generalized associativity. This suggests a hierarchy of increasingly structured models for
\mathbb{F}_{1}
-geometry.

2.
Preliminaries:
\Gamma
-sets and
\mathbb{F}_{1}
-modules

In this section, we review the theory of Segal’s
\Gamma
-sets following
[
7
]
and
[

\Gamma

2.1.
The category
\Gamma^{\mathrm{op}}

Definition 2.1
.

The category
\Gamma^{\mathrm{op}}
has:

•

Objects: Pointed finite sets
n_{+}:=\{0,1,\ldots,n\}
for each integer
n\geq 0
, where
0
is the basepoint.

•

Morphisms: Pointed maps (i.e., maps preserving the basepoint
0
).

Equivalently,
\Gamma^{\mathrm{op}}
is the skeleton of the category of finite pointed sets.

Definition 2.2
.

A
\Gamma
-set
is a pointed functor
F:\Gamma^{\mathrm{op}}\to\mathbf{Set}_{*}
from
\Gamma^{\mathrm{op}}
to the category of pointed sets. The pointed set
F(n_{+})
is called the
n
-th level
of
F
. We denote by
\Gamma\mathbf{Set}_{*}
the category of
\Gamma
-sets, whose morphisms are natural transformations.

Remark 2.3
.

In the language of Segal
[
7
]
,
\Gamma
-sets are
\Gamma
-spaces with discrete topology, where
\Gamma
-spaces provide a model for connective spectra. In the language of Connes-Consani
[
3
]
,
\Gamma
-sets are
\mathbb{F}_{1}
-modules (or denoted as
\mathbb{S}
-modules), emphasizing their role as the fundamental objects in
\mathbb{F}_{1}
-geometry. This connection to homotopy theory is one reason
\Gamma
-sets provide a natural framework for absolute algebraic geometry.

2.3.
The spherical monoid functor

While the Eilenberg-MacLane functor embeds algebraic structures such as commutative monoids into
\Gamma
-sets, we also need a “free” construction that builds
\Gamma
-sets from pointed sets. This is provided by the spherical monoid functor, which produces
\Gamma
-sets with minimal additive structure.

Definition 2.7
.

Let
X
be a pointed set with basepoint
*
. The
spherical monoid object

\mathbb{S}X
is the
\Gamma
-set defined by:

\mathbb{S}X(n_{+}):=X\wedge n_{+}

where
\wedge
denotes the smash product of pointed sets. For a morphism
f\in\mathrm{Hom}_{\Gamma^{\mathrm{op}}}(n_{+},k_{+})
, we define:

\mathbb{S}X(f):=\mathrm{id}_{X}\wedge f:X\wedge n_{+}\to X\wedge k_{+}.

Proposition 2.8

(
[
3
]
, Proposition 2.2)
.

The assignment
X\mapsto\mathbb{S}X
defines a fully faithful functor

\mathbb{S}:\mathbf{Set}_{*}\to\Gamma\mathbf{Set}_{*}

that is left adjoint to the evaluation functor
-(1_{+}):\Gamma\mathbf{Set}_{*}\to\mathbf{Set}_{*}
sending a
\Gamma
-set to its first level.

Proof.

For a morphism
f:X\to Y
of pointed sets, we define
\mathbb{S}f:\mathbb{S}X\to\mathbb{S}Y
levelwise by
f\wedge\mathrm{id}_{n_{+}}
. The adjunction

\mathrm{Hom}_{\Gamma\mathbf{Set}_{*}}(\mathbb{S}X,F)\cong\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbf{Set}_{*}}(X,F(1_{+}))

follows from the universal property of the smash product and the Yoneda lemma.
∎

The

3.
Hyper-operations in
\mathbb{F}_{1}
-modules

In this section, we analyze the additive structure encoded in
\Gamma
-sets. Unlike classical abelian groups, where addition is a binary operation

with a unique output, the additive data of a
\Gamma
-set is encoded through a family of
n
-ary

n
-ary sums, establish that strict associativity fails in general, and prove the law of generalized associativity that nonetheless controls all higher sums.

, where
*
is the basepoint.

These are consistent with Definition
3.7
since
p_{1,1}
and
s_{1}
are both the identity morphism, and
X(0_{+})=\{*\}
has only the basepoint. When the level
n
is clear from context, we write
p_{i}
instead of
p_{i,n}
and
s
instead of
s_{n}
to simplify notation.

For subsets
A_{1},\ldots,A_{n}\subseteq X(1_{+})
, we extend the definition by

\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}:=\bigcup_{a_{i}\in A_{i}}\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}.

For the binary sum, we write
a\oplus b
for brevity.

3.3.
Examples

We now present several examples that illustrate the behavior of
n
-ary sums and reveal the spectrum from classical strict addition to maximally degenerate hyper-addition.

Example 3.9

(Eilenberg-MacLane objects)
.

Let
M
be a commutative monoid and consider
HM
from Definition
2.4
. For any collection
\{a_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\subseteq M=HM(1_{+})
, there is a
unique
element
z\in HM(n_{+})
exhibiting their sum, namely the
n
-tuple
z=(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n})
.

Indeed, we have:

\displaystyle HM(p_{i})(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})

\displaystyle=a_{i}\quad\text{for each }i=1,2,\ldots,n

\displaystyle HM(s)(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\quad\text{(the sum in }M\text{)}

Therefore,

\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\right\},

a singleton set containing the usual sum in
M
. This shows that our definition recovers classical addition when the
\Gamma
-set has the form
HM
. In this sense, Eilenberg-MacLane objects represent the case where hyper-addition is “strict”—there is exactly one way to sum any collection of elements.

Example 3.10

(The field with one element)
.

For
\mathbb{F}_{1}=\mathbb{S}\{0,1\}
from Example
2.9
, we have
\mathbb{F}_{1}(n_{+})=n_{+}=\{0,1,\ldots,n\}
. Let us compute
1\oplus 1
in
\mathbb{F}_{1}
.

Elements of
\mathbb{F}_{1}(2_{+})=\{0,1,2\}
are potential exhibits for the sum. We need
z\in\mathbb{F}_{1}(2_{+})
such that:

•

\mathbb{F}_{1}(p_{1})(z)=1

•

\mathbb{F}_{1}(p_{2})(z)=1

However, no element of
\mathbb{F}_{1}(2_{+})
exhibits the sum of
1
and
1
, thus we have

1\oplus 1=\emptyset.

Indeed:

•

For
1\oplus 0
: the element
z=1\in\mathbb{F}_{1}(2_{+})
satisfies
p_{1}(1)=1
,
p_{2}(1)=0
, and
s(1)=1
, so
1\oplus 0=\{1\}
.

•

For
0\oplus 1
: the element
z=2\in\mathbb{F}_{1}(2_{+})
satisfies
p_{1}(2)=0
,
p_{2}(2)=1
, and
s(2)=1
, so
0\oplus 1=\{1\}
.

•

For
0\oplus 0
: the element
z=0\in\mathbb{F}_{1}(2_{+})
satisfies
p_{1}(0)=0
,
p_{2}(0)=0
, and
s(0)=0
, so
0\oplus 0=\{0\}
.

Yet there is no “fourth” element in
\mathbb{F}_{1}(2_{+})
that exhibits
1\oplus 1
. This reflects the purely multiplicative nature of
\mathbb{F}_{1}
: there is “no way” to add two nonzero elements, justifying the term “hyperfield with one element” from Example
3.5
.

Example 3.11

(Spherical monoid objects)
.

Let
X
be a pointed set with at least two elements and consider
\mathbb{S}X
from Definition
2.7
. Recall that
\mathbb{S}X(n_{+})=X\wedge n_{+}
, whose elements can be written as pairs
(x,i)
where
x\in X\setminus\{*\}
and
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, with a basepoint adjoined.

For
z=(x,j)\in\mathbb{S}X(n_{+})
, we have

\mathbb{S}X(p_{i})(x,j)=\begin{cases}x&\text{if }i=j\\
*&\text{if }i\neq j\end{cases}

Therefore,
z=(x,j)
exhibits the sum of
\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\}
only if:

•

a_{j}=x\neq*

•

a_{i}=*
for all
i\neq j

In this case,
\mathbb{S}X(s_{n})(x,j)=x
, so

\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}=\begin{cases}\{a_{j}\}&\text{if exactly one }a_{j}\neq*\text{ and all others are }*\\
\emptyset&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

In particular, for nonzero
a,b\in X
, we have
a\oplus b=\emptyset
, generalizing Example
3.10
. The only nontrivial sums are those involving the basepoint:
a\oplus*=\{a\}
and
*\oplus*=\{*\}
. This reflects the purely multiplicative nature of spherical monoid objects: they have no additive rules except those forced by the basepoint axioms.

The following lemma shows that morphisms of
\Gamma
-sets respect
n
-ary sums, though not necessarily strictly (the image of a sum may be contained in, but not equal to, the sum of images):

Lemma 3.12
.

Let
f:X\to Y
be a morphism of
\Gamma
-sets, and let
\{A_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
be a finite collection of subsets of
X(1_{+})
. Then

f\left(\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}\right)\subseteq\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n}f(A_{i}).

In particular, for elements
a,b\in X(1_{+})
, we have
f(a\oplus b)\subseteq f(a)\oplus f(b)
.

Proof.

It suffices to show that if
c\in\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}
for
a_{i}\in A_{i}
, then
f(c)\in\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n}f(a_{i})
.

Suppose
c\in\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}
. Then there exists
z\in X(n_{+})
exhibiting
c
as the sum of
\{a_{i}\}
:

X(p_{i})(z)=a_{i}\quad\text{for all }i,\quad\text{and}\quad X(s_{n})(z)=c.

Consider
f(z)\in Y(n_{+})
. By functoriality of
f
(i.e., naturality of the natural transformation), we have:

Y(p_{i})(f(z))=f(X(p_{i})(z))=f(a_{i})

and

Y(s_{n})(f(z))=f(X(s_{n})(z))=f(c).

Therefore,
f(z)
exhibits
f(c)
as the sum of
\{f(a_{i})\}
, which means
f(c)\in\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n}f(a_{i})
.
∎

Remark 3.13
.

The inclusion in Lemma
3.12
can be strict. For example, consider a morphism
f:\mathbb{S}M\to HR
from a spherical monoid algebra to an Eilenberg-MacLane algebra, and take nonzero
a,b\in M
. Then
a\oplus b=\emptyset
in
\mathbb{S}M
by Example
3.11
, so

f(a\oplus b)=f(\emptyset)=\emptyset\subsetneq\{f(a)+_{R}f(b)\}=f(a)\oplus f(b).

This shows that morphisms preserve hyper-additive structure, but do not necessarily reflect it: the target may have more sums than the source.

3.4.
The failure of strict associativity

Now we consider the natural question: can
n
-ary sums be computed using iterated binary sums? In classical algebra, associativity ensures that

a_{1}+a_{2}+a_{3}=(a_{1}+a_{2})+a_{3}=a_{1}+(a_{2}+a_{3}).

For
\Gamma
-sets, we have three potentially different sets:

•

The ternary sum:
a_{1}\oplus a_{2}\oplus a_{3}

•

The left-associated binary sum:
(a_{1}\oplus a_{2})\oplus a_{3}

•

The right-associated binary sum:
a_{1}\oplus(a_{2}\oplus a_{3})

One might hope these are always equal. However, this turns out to be false in general, as the next counter-example shows. While equality fails, we will show in Section
3.5
that a weaker
inclusion
holds, and this suffices for our construction of extension of scalars functor.

Example 3.14

(Failure of strict associativity)
.

Consider the quotient
\Gamma
-set
H\mathbb{Z}/H(3\mathbb{Z})
, obtained by collapsing the sub-
\Gamma
-set
H(3\mathbb{Z})\subseteq H\mathbb{Z}
to the basepoint. (Quotients of
\Gamma
-sets are formed levelwise:
(H\mathbb{Z}/H(3\mathbb{Z}))(n_{+})=H\mathbb{Z}(n_{+})/H(3\mathbb{Z})(n_{+})=\mathbb{Z}^{\oplus n}/3\mathbb{Z}^{\oplus n}
.) Let
[n]
denote the image of
n\in\mathbb{Z}
in the quotient.

In
H\mathbb{Z}/H(3\mathbb{Z})
, we compute the ternary sum:

[1]\oplus[2]\oplus[2]=\{[5]\}.

This is because in
H\mathbb{Z}
, the element
(1,2,2)\in\mathbb{Z}^{\oplus 3}
exhibits the sum
1+2+2=5
, and this is the unique element exhibiting this combination. In the quotient,
([1],[2],[2])
still uniquely exhibits the sum
[5]
.

On the other hand, consider the iterated binary sum:

\displaystyle([1]\oplus[2])\oplus[2]

\displaystyle=[3]\oplus[2]

\displaystyle=[0]\oplus[2]\quad\text{(since }[3]=[0]\text{ in the quotient)}

\displaystyle=\{[3n+2]:n\in\mathbb{Z}\}

\displaystyle=\{\ldots,[2],[5],[8],[11],\ldots\}

The equality
[0]\oplus[2]=\{[3n+2]:n\in\mathbb{Z}\}
holds because for any
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, the element
([3n],[2])\in H\mathbb{Z}/H(3\mathbb{Z})(2_{+})
exhibits the sum
[3n+2]
.

Therefore:

[1]\oplus[2]\oplus[2]=\{[5]\}\subsetneq\{\ldots,[2],[5],[8],\ldots\}=([1]\oplus[2])\oplus[2].

The ternary sum is
strictly contained in
the iterated binary sum, but they are not equal.

Remark 3.15
.

Despite this failure, we still have the inclusion

[1]\oplus[2]\oplus[2]\subseteq([1]\oplus[2])\oplus[2].

We shall prove that this type of inclusion
always
holds:

a_{1}\oplus a_{2}\oplus a_{3}\subseteq(a_{1}\oplus a_{2})\oplus a_{3}

and similarly

a_{1}\oplus a_{2}\oplus a_{3}\subseteq a_{1}\oplus(a_{2}\oplus a_{3})

Moreover, it holds for any way of parenthesizing an
n
-ary sum. This weaker property, which we call
generalized associativity
, is sufficient for our purposes.

3.5.
The law of generalized associativity

We now establish the central technical result of this section, which shows that while strict associativity fails, an inclusion property holds.

Theorem 3.16

(Law of Generalized Associativity, Theorem
A
)
.

Let
I=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
be a finite index set, and let
\mathcal{P}=\{I_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
be a partition of
I
with
n_{j}:=|I_{j}|
and
n=\sum_{j=1}^{m}n_{j}
. Let
X
be a
\Gamma
-set and let
\{A_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
be a family of subsets of
X(1_{+})
. Then

\bigoplus_{i\in I}A_{i}\subseteq\bigoplus_{j=1}^{m}\left(\bigoplus_{i\in I_{j}}A_{i}\right).

Remark 3.17
.

This theorem states that the
n
-ary sum can be “computed” by first taking sums within the parts of a partition, then summing the results. While this does not give equality (as Example
3.14
shows), the inclusion reveals that all information about
n
-ary sums is
controlled by
lower-order operations. In particular, taking
m=2
repeatedly shows that all
n
-ary sums are controlled by successive binary operations.

Proof.

Let
a\in\bigoplus_{i\in I}A_{i}
. Then there exist elements
a_{i}\in A_{i}
for all
i\in I
such that
a\in\bigoplus_{i\in I}a_{i}
. By definition, there exists
z\in X(n_{+})
such that:

X(s_{n})(z)=a\quad\text{and}\quad X(p_{i,n})(z)=a_{i}\quad\text{for all }i\in I.

Our goal is to show that
a\in\bigoplus_{j=1}^{m}\left(\bigoplus_{i\in I_{j}}a_{i}\right)
.

Let
f\in\mathrm{Hom}_{\Gamma^{\mathrm{op}}}(n_{+},m_{+})
be the morphism determined by the partition
\mathcal{P}
, defined by:

f(i)=\begin{cases}j&\text{if }i\in I_{j}\\
0&\text{if }i=0\end{cases}

Set
y:=X(f)(z)\in X(m_{+})
. We claim that
y
exhibits
a
as the sum of the
m
intermediate sums.

Indeed, we have:

X(s_{m})(y)=X(s_{m}\circ f)(z)=X(s_{n})(z)=a

where the second equality holds because
s_{m}\circ f=s_{n}
(both morphisms send all nonzero elements of
n_{+}
to
1\in 1_{+}
).

Now we show
X(p_{j,m})(y)\in\bigoplus_{i\in I_{j}}a_{i}
for each
j
. Fix
j\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
. We must construct an element
y_{j}\in X(n_{j})
that exhibits
X(p_{j,m})(y)
as the sum of
\{a_{i}\}_{i\in I_{j}}
.

Let
\phi_{j}:I_{j}\to\{1,\ldots,n_{j}\}
be an order-preserving bijection. Define
\tilde{\phi_{j}}\in\mathrm{Hom}_{\Gamma^{\mathrm{op}}}(n_{+},(n_{j})_{+})
by:

\tilde{\phi_{j}}(t)=\begin{cases}\phi_{j}(t)&\text{if }t\in I_{j}\\
0&\text{if }t\notin I_{j}\end{cases}

Set
y_{j}:=X(\tilde{\phi_{j}})(z)\in X(n_{j})
.

We verify that
y_{j}
exhibits the desired sum. First:

X(s_{n_{j}})(y_{j})=X(s_{n_{j}}\circ\tilde{\phi_{j}})(z).

Observe that both
s_{n_{j}}\circ\tilde{\phi_{j}}
and
p_{j,m}\circ f
send elements in
I_{j}
to
1
and all other elements to
0
.

Therefore:

X(s_{n_{j}})(y_{j})=X(s_{n_{j}}\circ\tilde{\phi_{j}})(z)=X(p_{j,m}\circ f)(z)=X(p_{j,m})(X(f)(z))=X(p_{j,m})(y).

Second, for any
\ell\in\{1,\ldots,n_{j}\}
, set
i=\phi_{j}^{-1}(\ell)\in I_{j}
. Then:

X(p_{\ell,n_{j}})(y_{j})=X(p_{\ell,n_{j}}\circ\tilde{\phi_{j}})(z)=X(p_{i,n})(z)=a_{i}\in A_{i}.

Therefore,
y_{j}
exhibits
X(p_{j,m})(y)
as the sum of
\{a_{i}\}_{i\in I_{j}}
, which means:

X(p_{j,m})(y)\in\bigoplus_{i\in I_{j}}a_{i}.

Since this holds for all
j\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
, we conclude that
y
exhibits
a
as the sum of the intermediate sums:

a=X(s_{m})(y)\in\bigoplus_{j=1}^{m}\left(\bigoplus_{i\in I_{j}}a_{i}\right).\qed

The following corollary makes explicit the case of iterated binary sums:

Corollary 3.18
.

Let
X
be a
\Gamma
-set, and let
\{a_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
be a finite collection of elements in
X(1_{+})
. Then

\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\subseteq(a_{1}\oplus(a_{2}\oplus(\cdots\oplus(a_{n-1}\oplus a_{n})\cdots))).

More generally, for any way of parenthesizing the expression
a_{1}\oplus a_{2}\oplus\cdots\oplus a_{n}
using binary operations, the
n
-ary sum is contained in the result.

Proof.

Apply Theorem
3.16
repeatedly with appropriate binary partitions. For the specific right-nested parenthesization, use partitions
I=\{1\}\cup\{2,\ldots,n\}
, then
\{2\}\cup\{3,\ldots,n\}
, and so on.
∎

Example 3.19
.

Let us verify Corollary
3.18
explicitly for
n=3
. Apply Theorem
3.16
with the partition
\mathcal{P}=\{\{1\},\{2,3\}\}
of
I=\{1,2,3\}
. This gives:

a_{1}\oplus a_{2}\oplus a_{3}\subseteq a_{1}\oplus(a_{2}\oplus a_{3})

directly.

Explicitly, if
c\in a_{1}\oplus a_{2}\oplus a_{3}
, then there exists
z\in X(3_{+})
such that:

\displaystyle X(p_{1})(z)

\displaystyle=a_{1}

\displaystyle X(p_{2})(z)

\displaystyle=a_{2}

\displaystyle X(p_{3})(z)

\displaystyle=a_{3}

\displaystyle X(s_{3})(z)

\displaystyle=c

The partition morphism
f:3_{+}\to 2_{+}
sends
f(1)=1
and
f(2)=f(3)=2
(and
f(0)=0
).

Setting
y=X(f)(z)\in X(2_{+})
, we have:

\displaystyle X(p_{1})(y)

\displaystyle=X(p_{1}\circ f)(z)=X(p_{1})(z)=a_{1}

\displaystyle X(p_{2})(y)

\displaystyle=X(p_{2}\circ f)(z)=X(s_{2}\circ\tilde{\phi})(z)\quad\text{where }\tilde{\phi}(2)=1,\tilde{\phi}(3)=2

\displaystyle\in a_{2}\oplus a_{3}

\displaystyle X(s_{2})(y)

\displaystyle=X(s_{2}\circ f)(z)=X(s_{3})(z)=c

Therefore
y
exhibits
c
as an element of
a_{1}\oplus(a_{2}\oplus a_{3})
, confirming the inclusion.

3.6.
Interpretation as higher hyperstructures

Having established the law of generalized associativity, we can now articulate the precise sense in which
\mathbb{F}_{1}
-modules generalize classical additive structures. This provides conceptual context for the extension of scalars construction in the next section.

Remark 3.20
.

An
\mathbb{F}_{1}
-module
X
can be viewed as consisting of:

(i)

A pointed set
X(1_{+})
(the “underlying set”)

(ii)

A family of
n
-ary
hyper-operations

\bigoplus:X(1_{+})^{n}\to\mathcal{P}(X(1_{+}))
for each
n\geq 2

(iii)

Satisfying the law of
generalized
associativity (Theorem
3.16
)

In contrast, a
\mathbb{Z}
-module (abelian group)
M
consists of:

(i)

A pointed set
M
(the “underlying set”)

(ii)

A family of
n
-ary
operations

+:M^{n}\to M
for each
n\geq 2
(all reducible to binary
+
)

(iii)

Satisfying
classical
associativity:
(a+b)+c=a+(b+c)

In the category of abelian groups, all
n
-ary operations reduce to iterated binary operations by associativity, and the associativity law reduces to the 3-element equation
(a+b)+c=a+(b+c)
.

The
\Gamma
-set framework makes explicit the “higher additive structures” that are implicit (and trivial) in classical algebra. From this perspective:

•

The Eilenberg-MacLane functor
H:\mathbf{Ab}\to\mathbb{F}_{1}\mathbf{Mod}
can be seen as a
forgetful functor
, forgeting that the hyper-operations are strict (have unique results).

•

The natural question is: does there exist a
left adjoint
in the opposite direction? That is, can we universally “strictify” a family of
n
-ary hyper-operations satisfying generalized associativity into a single binary operation?

The answer is yes, and this left adjoint is precisely the extension of scalars functor
-\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\mathbb{Z}
.

4.
Extension of scalars for
\mathbb{F}_{1}
-modules

Having established the law of generalized associativity in the previous section, we are now ready to construct the extension of scalars functor
-\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\mathbb{Z}:\mathbb{F}_{1}\mathbf{Mod}\to\mathbf{Ab}
that associates to each
\mathbb{F}_{1}
-module an abelian group.

The key challenge is to compress the infinite family of
n
-ary hyper-operations in a
\Gamma
-set
X
into a single binary operation in an abelian group. The generalized associativity law (Theorem
3.16
) is crucial: it shows that all
n
-ary sums are controlled by successive binary operations, reducing the problem to understanding the binary structure encoded at the second level
X(2_{+})
.

Our construction proceeds in four steps:

(1)

Characterize morphisms to Eilenberg-MacLane objects (Section
4.1
). We show that morphisms
X\to HM
correspond to “pointed additive maps”
g:X(1_{+})\to M
satisfying two simple conditions.

(2)

Define the extension of scalars (Section
4.2
). We construct
X\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\mathbb{Z}
as a quotient of the free abelian group on
X(1_{+})
by relations encoding binary hyper-sums.

(3)

Verify the universal property (Section
4.3
). We prove that morphisms
X\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\mathbb{Z}\to M
correspond bijectively to morphisms
X\to HM
, establishing the adjunction.

(4)

Compute examples (Section
4.4
). We compute
X\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\mathbb{Z}
for several important classes of
\mathbb{F}_{1}
-modules.

4.1.
Characterization of maps to Eilenberg-MacLane objects

To construct
X\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\mathbb{Z}
as a universal object, we first need to understand what morphisms
out of
it should look like. By the proposed adjunction, morphisms
X\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\mathbb{Z}\to M
should correspond to morphisms
X\to HM
of
\Gamma
-sets. The following lemma characterizes such morphisms in elementary terms, showing they are completely determined by their behavior on the first level
X(1_{+})
.

We begin with a technical lemma:

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
g:X(1_{+})\to M
be a pointed additive map. Then for any
n\geq 2
and any
c\in\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}
, we have

g(c)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}g(a_{i})

where the right side is the sum in the commutative monoid
M
.

Proof.

By Corollary
3.18
, we have

c\in\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\subseteq(a_{1}\oplus(a_{2}\oplus(\cdots\oplus(a_{n-1}\oplus a_{n})\cdots))).

We proceed by induction. For
n=2
, this is the additive property. Suppose the claim holds for
n-1
. If
c\in\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}
, then by Corollary
3.18
with partition
\{1\}\cup\{2,\ldots,n\}
, there exists
d\in\bigoplus_{i=2}^{n}a_{i}
such that
c\in a_{1}\oplus d
. By the inductive hypothesis,
g(d)=\sum_{i=2}^{n}g(a_{i})
. By the additive property,

g(c)=g(a_{1})+g(d)=g(a_{1})+\sum_{i=2}^{n}g(a_{i})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}g(a_{i}).\qed

Now we present the key lemma:

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
X
be a
\Gamma
-set, let
M
be a commutative monoid written additively, and let
f:X\to HM
be a morphism of
\Gamma
-sets. Then
f
is equivalent to the data of a map
g:X(1_{+})\to M
satisfying:

(i)

(Pointed)

g(*)=0\in M
, where
*
is the basepoint of
X(1_{+})
.

(ii)

(Additive)
If
c\in a\oplus b
, then
g(c)=g(a)+g(b)
in
M
.

Conversely, any map
g:X(1_{+})\to M
satisfying these two conditions extends uniquely to a morphism of
\Gamma
-sets
f:X\to HM
such that
f|_{1_{+}}=g
.

Remark 4.3
.

We call a map
g:X(1_{+})\to M
satisfying conditions (i) and (ii) a
pointed additive map
. The lemma says that pointed additive maps are in bijection with morphisms of
\Gamma
-sets
X\to HM
.

Proof of Lemma
4.2
.

Let
f:X\to HM
be a morphism of
\Gamma
-sets. Define
g:=f|_{1_{+}}:X(1_{+})\to M
to be the restriction of
f
to the first level. We verify that
g
satisfies the required properties.

Pointed property:
Since
f
is a natural transformation of pointed functors, it must map basepoints to basepoints at each level. In particular,
f
maps the basepoint of
X(1_{+})
to the basepoint of
HM(1_{+})=M
, which is
0
. Thus
g(*)=f(*)=0
.

Additive property:
Suppose
c\in a\oplus b
. By Lemma
3.12
, we have

g(c)=f(c)\in f(a\oplus b)\subseteq f(a)\oplus f(b).

But
f(a),f(b),f(c)\in HM(1_{+})=M
, and in an Eilenberg-MacLane object
HM
, the binary sum is strict (Example
3.9
):

f(a)\oplus f(b)=\{f(a)+f(b)\}

where
+
is the monoid operation in
M
. Therefore
g(c)=f(c)=f(a)+f(b)=g(a)+g(b)
.

Uniqueness of
f
:
We claim that
f
is completely determined by
g=f|_{1_{+}}
.

Let
n\geq 0
and consider any
x\in X(n_{+})
. We will show that
f|_{n_{+}}(x)\in HM(n_{+})=M^{\oplus n}
is uniquely determined by
g
.

For each
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, the naturality of
f
with respect to the projection
p_{i}:n_{+}\to 1_{+}
gives a commutative diagram:
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X(n_{+})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces HM(n_{+})=M^{\oplus n}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X(1_{+})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces HM(1_{+})=M}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X(p_{i})}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f|_{n_{+}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces HM(p_{i})}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f|_{1_{+}}=g}

Tracing through the diagram, the
i
-th component of
f|_{n_{+}}(x)\in M^{\oplus n}
is:

\text{(i-th component of }f|_{n_{+}}(x))=HM(p_{i})(f|_{n_{+}}(x))=f|_{1_{+}}(X(p_{i})(x))=g(X(p_{i})(x)).

Since an element of
M^{\oplus n}
is uniquely determined by its
n
components (via the projections
p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}
), we conclude:

f|_{n_{+}}(x)=(g(X(p_{1})(x)),g(X(p_{2})(x)),\ldots,g(X(p_{n})(x))).

Therefore,
f
is uniquely determined by
g
at every level.

Now we show the other direction. Given a pointed additive map
g:X(1_{+})\to M
, we construct a morphism of
\Gamma
-sets
f:X\to HM
by defining it levelwise using the formula derived above:

\displaystyle f|_{n_{+}}:X(n_{+})

\displaystyle\to HM(n_{+})=M^{\oplus n}

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle\mapsto(g(X(p_{1})(x)),g(X(p_{2})(x)),\ldots,g(X(p_{n})(x))).

We must verify that this defines a morphism of
\Gamma
-sets, i.e., a natural transformation of pointed functors.

Pointed property:
For the basepoint
*\in X(n_{+})
, we have
X(p_{i})(*)=*
for all
i
(since
X
is a pointed functor and
p_{i}
is a pointed map). Therefore:

f|_{n_{+}}(*)=(g(*),g(*),\ldots,g(*))=(0,0,\ldots,0)

which is the basepoint of
HM(n_{+})=M^{\oplus n}
. Thus
f
preserves basepoints at each level.

Naturality:
For any morphism
\gamma\in\mathrm{Hom}_{\Gamma^{\mathrm{op}}}(n_{+},m_{+})
, we need to verify commutativity of the diagram:
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X(n_{+})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces HM(n_{+})=M^{\oplus n}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X(m_{+})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces HM(m_{+})=M^{\oplus m}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X(\gamma)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f|_{n_{+}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces HM(\gamma)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f|_{m_{+}}}

For any
x\in X(n_{+})
and coordinate
j\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
, we compare the
j
-th component along both paths.

Bottom-left path:

\displaystyle\text{(j-th component of }f|_{m_{+}}(X(\gamma)(x)))

\displaystyle=g(X(p_{j})(X(\gamma)(x)))

\displaystyle=g(X(p_{j}\circ\gamma)(x))

Top-right path:

\displaystyle\text{(j-th component of }HM(\gamma)(f|_{n_{+}}(x)))

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in\gamma^{-1}(j)}\text{(i-th component of }f|_{n_{+}}(x))

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in\gamma^{-1}(j)}g(X(p_{i})(x))

where the first equality uses the definition of
HM(\gamma)
from Definition
2.4
.

To show these are equal, observe that

X(p_{j}\circ\gamma)(x)\in\bigoplus_{i\in\gamma^{-1}(j)}X(p_{i})(x).

This holds because
p_{j}\circ\gamma
sends elements in
\gamma^{-1}(j)
to
1
and all other elements to
0
. We can factor
p_{j}\circ\gamma=s_{r}\circ\gamma^{\prime}
where
r:=|\gamma^{-1}(j)|
and
\gamma^{\prime}:n_{+}\to r_{+}
sends elements in
\gamma^{-1}(j)
bijectively to
\{1,\ldots,r\}
and everything else to
0
. Then:

\displaystyle X(p_{j}\circ\gamma)(x)

\displaystyle=X(s_{r}\circ\gamma^{\prime})(x)

\displaystyle=X(s_{r})(X(\gamma^{\prime})(x))

\displaystyle\in\bigoplus_{\ell=1}^{r}X(p_{\ell}\circ\gamma^{\prime})(x)

\displaystyle=\bigoplus_{i\in\gamma^{-1}(j)}X(p_{i})(x).

By Lemma
4.1
, since
g
is a pointed additive map:

g(X(p_{j}\circ\gamma)(x))=\sum_{i\in\gamma^{-1}(j)}g(X(p_{i})(x)),

establishing commutativity of the diagram. Therefore
f
is a morphism of
\Gamma
-sets, and by construction
f|_{1_{+}}=g
.
∎

4.2.
Definition of extension of scalars

With Lemma
4.2
in hand, we can now define the extension of scalars for
\mathbb{F}_{1}
-modules. The insight from Lemma
4.1
is that we only need to impose relations for binary hyper-sums, despite the presence of infinitely many
n
-ary operations.

Definition 4.4
.

Let
X
be an
\mathbb{F}_{1}
-module (i.e., a
\Gamma
-set). We define its
extension of scalars to
\mathbb{Z}
by

X\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\mathbb{Z}:=\mathbb{Z}[X(1_{+})]/\mathcal{R}

where:

•

\mathbb{Z}[X(1_{+})]
is the
free abelian group
generated by the set
X(1_{+})
.

•

\mathcal{R}
is the subgroup generated by the following relations:

(i)

(Basepoint relation)

[*]=0
, where
*
is the basepoint of
X(1_{+})
and
0
is the identity of the abelian group.

(ii)

(Additivity relations)

[a]+[b]=[c]
for all
a,b,c\in X(1_{+})
with
c\in a\oplus b
.

Here
[a]
denotes the generator of
\mathbb{Z}[X(1_{+})]
corresponding to
a\in X(1_{+})
. We write
[a]
also for its image in the quotient
X\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\mathbb{Z}
when no confusion arises.

Equivalently,
X\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\mathbb{Z}
can be described as the abelian group with:

•

Generators:
One generator
[a]
for each
a\in X(1_{+})\setminus\{*\}

•

Relations:

[a]+[b]=[c]
whenever
c\in a\oplus b

The basepoint relation
[*]=0
allows us to ignore the basepoint in the generating set.

Remark 4.5
.

X\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\mathbb{Z}
is the “most general” abelian group in which the hyper-addition of
X
becomes a strict binary operation. The relations imposed are precisely those required to ensure that any pointed additive map
g:X(1_{+})\to M
(in the sense of Lemma
4.2
) factors through the quotient.

Before proving the main theorem, we establish that the extension of scalars is functorial:

Lemma 4.6
.

The assignment
X\mapsto X\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\mathbb{Z}
extends to a functor

-\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\mathbb{Z}:\mathbb{F}_{1}\mathbf{Mod}\to\mathbf{Ab}.

Proof.

Let
\varphi:X\to Y
be a morphism of
\mathbb{F}_{1}
-modules. The map
\varphi|_{1_{+}}:X(1_{+})\to Y(1_{+})
is a pointed map, so it extends uniquely to a group homomorphism on the free abelian groups:

\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}[\varphi]:\mathbb{Z}[X(1_{+})]

\displaystyle\to\mathbb{Z}[Y(1_{+})]

\displaystyle\sum_{i}n_{i}\cdot[a_{i}]

\displaystyle\mapsto\sum_{i}n_{i}\cdot[\varphi(a_{i})]

We claim that
\mathbb{Z}[\varphi]
descends to a homomorphism on the quotients, i.e., that
\mathbb{Z}[\varphi]
maps
\mathcal{R}_{X}
into
\mathcal{R}_{Y}
. It suffices to check that
\mathbb{Z}[\varphi]
vanishes on the generators of
\mathcal{R}_{X}
.

Basepoint relation:
Since
\varphi
is a pointed map,
\varphi(*_{X})=*_{Y}
. Therefore:

\mathbb{Z}[\varphi]([*_{X}])=[\varphi(*_{X})]=[*_{Y}]=0

in
Y\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\mathbb{Z}
.

Additivity relations:
Suppose
c\in a\oplus b
in
X
. By Lemma
3.12
,
\varphi(c)\in\varphi(a)\oplus\varphi(b)
in
Y
. Therefore, the relation
[\varphi(a)]+[\varphi(b)]=[\varphi(c)]
holds in
Y\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\mathbb{Z}
. Thus:

\mathbb{Z}[\varphi]([a]+[b]-[c])=[\varphi(a)]+[\varphi(b)]-[\varphi(c)]=0

in
Y\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\mathbb{Z}
.

Therefore,
\mathbb{Z}[\varphi]
induces a well-defined homomorphism:

\varphi\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\mathbb{Z}:X\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\mathbb{Z}\to Y\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\mathbb{Z}.

Preservation of identity follows because
\mathbb{Z}[\mathrm{id}_{X}]=\mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{Z}[X(1_{+})]}
, which clearly descends to
\mathrm{id}_{X\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\mathbb{Z}}
. Preservation of composition follows from
\mathbb{Z}[\psi\circ\varphi]=\mathbb{Z}[\psi]\circ\mathbb{Z}[\varphi]
and the uniqueness of the induced maps on quotients.
∎

4.3.
The adjunction

We now establish the main result of this section, showing that extension of scalars is left adjoint to the Eilenberg-MacLane functor:

Theorem 4.7

(Theorem
B
)
.

The extension of scalars functor
-\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\mathbb{Z}:\mathbb{F}_{1}\mathbf{Mod}\to\mathbf{Ab}
is left adjoint to the Eilenberg-MacLane functor
H:\mathbf{Ab}\to\mathbb{F}_{1}\mathbf{Mod}
. That is, there is a natural bijection

\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbb{F}_{1}\mathbf{Mod}}(X,HM)\cong\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbf{Ab}}(X\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\mathbb{Z},M)

for all
\mathbb{F}_{1}
-modules
X
and abelian groups
M
.

Proof.

Consider the canonical map

\displaystyle\iota:X(1_{+})

\displaystyle\to X\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\mathbb{Z}

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle\mapsto[a]

where
[a]
denotes the image in the quotient.

By construction of the quotient,
\iota
is a pointed additive map in the sense of Lemma
4.2
:

•

Pointed:

\iota(*)=[*]=0
by the basepoint relation.

•

Additive:
If
c\in a\oplus b
, then by the additivity relations,
[c]=[a]+[b]
in
X\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\mathbb{Z}
. Thus
\iota(c)=[c]=[a]+[b]=\iota(a)+\iota(b)
.

This gives a commutative diagram:
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X(1_{+})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\mathbb{Z}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces M}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\iota}

where any pointed additive map from
X(1_{+})
to
M
factors uniquely through
\iota
, as we show below.

Define
\Phi
as follows:

\Phi:\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbb{F}_{1}\mathbf{Mod}}(X,HM)\to\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbf{Ab}}(X\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\mathbb{Z},M)

Given
f:X\to HM
, let
g:=f|_{1_{+}}:X(1_{+})\to M
be its restriction to the first level. By Lemma
4.2
,
g
is a pointed additive map.

Extend
g
linearly to
\mathbb{Z}[X(1_{+})]
:

\displaystyle\tilde{h}:\mathbb{Z}[X(1_{+})]

\displaystyle\to M

\displaystyle\sum_{a\in X(1_{+})}n_{a}\cdot[a]

\displaystyle\mapsto\sum_{a\in X(1_{+})}n_{a}\cdot g(a)

We verify that
\tilde{h}
vanishes on the relations
\mathcal{R}
:

•

Basepoint:

\tilde{h}([*])=g(*)=0
since
g
is pointed.

•

Additivity:
If
c\in a\oplus b
, then
g(c)=g(a)+g(b)
since
g
is additive. Thus:

\tilde{h}([a]+[b]-[c])=g(a)+g(b)-g(c)=0.

Therefore,
\tilde{h}
descends to a unique group homomorphism
h:X\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\mathbb{Z}\to M
making the following diagram commute:
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X(1_{+})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\mathbb{Z}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces M}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\iota}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces g}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\exists!h}

We define
\Phi(f):=h
.

Now given
h:X\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\mathbb{Z}\to M
, define
g:=h\circ\iota:X(1_{+})\to M
. Then the composition
g
is still a pointed additive map, which by lemma
4.2
extends uniquely to a morphism
f:X\to HM
. This defines the inverse map

\Psi:\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbf{Ab}}(X\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\mathbb{Z},M)\to\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbb{F}_{1}\mathbf{Mod}}(X,HM)

It is straightforward to verify that
\Phi
and
\Psi
are inverses, and naturality in
X
and
M
follows from the functoriality established in Lemma
4.6
and the naturality of the characterization in Lemma
4.2
. Therefore, we have established the adjunction:

\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbb{F}_{1}\mathbf{Mod}}(X,HM)\cong\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbf{Ab}}(X\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\mathbb{Z},M)

∎

4.4.
Examples

We now compute
X\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\mathbb{Z}
for several important classes of
\mathbb{F}_{1}
-modules, illustrating the range of behavior.

Example 4.8

(Extension of
\mathbb{F}_{1}
)
.

For the base
\mathbb{F}_{1}
-module
\mathbb{F}_{1}
itself, we have

\mathbb{F}_{1}\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\mathbb{Z}\cong\mathbb{Z}.

Indeed,
\mathbb{F}_{1}(1_{+})=\{0,1\}
and the only nontrivial hyper-sums (from Example
3.10
) are:

\displaystyle 0\oplus 0

\displaystyle=\{0\}

\displaystyle 0\oplus 1

\displaystyle=\{1\}

\displaystyle 1\oplus 0

\displaystyle=\{1\}

\displaystyle 1\oplus 1

\displaystyle=\emptyset

The free abelian group
\mathbb{Z}[\{0,1\}]
has generators
[0]
and
[1]
. The relations are:

•

Basepoint:

[0]=0

•

Additivity:
From
0\oplus 1=\{1\}
, we get
[0]+[1]=[1]
, which simplifies to
[1]=[1]
(no new relation), and the same holds for
1\oplus 0=\{1\}
. Since
1\oplus 1=\emptyset
, there is no relation involving
[1]+[1]
.

The basepoint relation identifies
[0]
with the zero element, and there are no other nontrivial relations involving
[1]
. Therefore:

\mathbb{F}_{1}\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\mathbb{Z}=\langle[1]\rangle\cong\mathbb{Z}

where
[1]
freely generates the integers. This confirms that extending scalars from
\mathbb{F}_{1}
to
\mathbb{Z}
produces
\mathbb{Z}
itself, as expected.

Example 4.9

(Spherical monoid objects)
.

More generally, let
Y
be a pointed set with basepoint
*
and at least one non-basepoint element. Then

(\mathbb{S}Y)\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\mathbb{Z}\cong\mathbb{Z}[Y]

where the right side denotes the free abelian group generated by the non-basepoint elements of
Y
.

This follows from Example
3.11
: in
\mathbb{S}Y
, the only nontrivial sums are those involving the basepoint:

\displaystyle a\oplus*

\displaystyle=\{a\}

\displaystyle*\oplus a

\displaystyle=\{a\}

\displaystyle*\oplus*

\displaystyle=\{*\}

\displaystyle a\oplus b

\displaystyle=\emptyset\quad\text{for nonzero }a,b\in Y

The additivity relations only impose
[a]+[*]=[a]
and
[*]+[*]=[*]
. Combined with the basepoint relation
[*]=0
, these give no additional constraints beyond identifying
*
with
0
. For nonzero
a,b\in Y
, since
a\oplus b=\emptyset
, there is no relation between
[a]
and
[b]
.

Therefore, the elements
\{[a]:a\in Y\setminus\{*\}\}
generate a free abelian group:

(\mathbb{S}Y)\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\mathbb{Z}\cong\mathbb{Z}[Y\setminus\{*\}].

In particular, taking
Y=\{0,1\}
recovers Example
4.8
.

Example 4.10

(Eilenberg-MacLane objects)
.

For an abelian group
M
, we have

(HM)\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\mathbb{Z}\cong M.

This follows because in
HM
, every
n
-ary sum has a unique representative:
a_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus a_{n}=\{a_{1}+_{M}\cdots+_{M}a_{n}\}
(Example
3.9
). Therefore, the additivity relations in Definition
4.4
impose:

[a]+[b]=[a+_{M}b]

for all
a,b\in M
. This means that every formal linear combination in
\mathbb{Z}[M]
can be “computed” using the group structure of
M
, and the quotient
\mathbb{Z}[M]/\mathcal{R}
is isomorphic to
M
itself. The group homomorphism
M\to(HM)\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\mathbb{Z}
sending
a\mapsto[a]
is an isomorphism.

This shows that the extension of scalars functor is the identity on Eilenberg-MacLane objects of abelian groups, confirming that the adjunction
(-\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\mathbb{Z})\dashv H
provides a reflection of
\mathbb{F}_{1}\mathbf{Mod}
onto
\mathbf{Ab}
.

More generally, if
M
is a commutative monoid (not necessarily a group), then

(HM)\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\mathbb{Z}\cong M^{\mathrm{gp}}

where
M^{\mathrm{gp}}
denotes the
group completion
(the Grothendieck group) of
M
—the universal abelian group receiving a monoid homomorphism from
M
. This can be constructed as:

M^{\mathrm{gp}}=\mathbb{Z}[M]/\langle[a]+[b]=[a+_{M}b]:a,b\in M\rangle

where
+_{M}
denotes the monoid operation in
M
. The same calculation as above shows that our construction recovers this universal property.

Example 4.11

(Quotients of Eilenberg-MacLane objects)
.

Consider the quotient
X=H\mathbb{Z}/H(n\mathbb{Z})
for some
n\geq 2
. Then

X\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\mathbb{Z}\cong\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}.

To see this, note that for any
[a]\in\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
, we have:

[0]\oplus[a]=\{[kn+a],k\in\mathbb{Z}\}

where
(kn,a)\in X(2_{+})
exhibits the desired sum.

Therefore, the additivity relations and basepoint relation in
X\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\mathbb{Z}
imposes:

[0]+[a]=[kn+a],\forall k\in\mathbb{Z}

Since
[0]=0
by the basepoint relation, this gives
[a]=[kn+a]
for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Combined with the fact that
[1]
generates the group, we conclude:

X\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\mathbb{Z}\cong\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}

where the isomorphism sends
[1]\mapsto\overline{1}\in\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
.

5.
Extension of scalars for
\mathbb{F}_{1}
-algebras

In the previous section, we constructed the extension of scalars functor for
\mathbb{F}_{1}
-modules, which strictifies the hyper-additive structure into classical abelian group addition. For applications to algebraic geometry, we need more than just abelian groups—we need
rings
with both addition and multiplication. This requires extending our construction to
\mathbb{F}_{1}
-algebras, which incorporate multiplicative structure alongside the additive hyper-structure.

Since an
\mathbb{F}_{1}
-algebra
A
is a
\Gamma
-set with additional multiplicative structure making it a monoid object in the symmetric monoidal category
(\Gamma\mathbf{Set}_{*},\wedge,\mathbb{F}_{1})
, the underlying
\Gamma
-set determines an abelian group
A\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\mathbb{Z}
by the construction of Section
4
, and the multiplicative structure of
A
induces a compatible ring structure on this abelian group. The result is a functor
-\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\mathbb{Z}:\mathbb{F}_{1}\mathbf{Alg}\to\mathbf{CRing}
left adjoint to the Eilenberg-MacLane embedding of rings into
\mathbb{F}_{1}
-algebras.

5.1.
\mathbb{F}_{1}
-algebras as monoid objects

Just as commutative rings can be defined as commutative monoid objects in the symmetric monoidal category
(\mathbf{Ab},\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}},\mathbb{Z})
, we define commutative
\mathbb{F}_{1}
-algebras as commutative monoid objects in
(\Gamma\mathbf{Set}_{*},\wedge,\mathbb{F}_{1})
. This perspective makes clear the parallel between classical algebraic geometry over
\mathbb{Z}
and absolute algebraic geometry over
\mathbb{F}_{1}
.

Definition 5.1
.

A
commutative
\mathbb{F}_{1}
-algebra
is a commutative monoid object in the symmetric monoidal category
(\Gamma\mathbf{Set}_{*},\wedge,\mathbb{F}_{1})
.

Explicitly, a commutative
\mathbb{F}_{1}
-algebra consists of:

•

A
\Gamma
-set
A

•

A
multiplication morphism

\mu:A\wedge A\to A
in
\Gamma\mathbf{Set}_{*}

•

A
unit morphism

\eta:\mathbb{F}_{1}\to A

satisfying the usual associativity and unit axioms for monoids, as well as commutativity (compatibility with the symmetry isomorphism
\tau:A\wedge A\to A\wedge A
).

A
morphism of commutative
\mathbb{F}_{1}
-algebras

\varphi:A\to B
is a morphism of the underlying
\Gamma
-sets that preserves the multiplication and unit.

We denote by
\mathbb{F}_{1}\mathbf{Alg}
the category of commutative
\mathbb{F}_{1}
-algebras.

Convention 5.2
.

Following the standard conventions of algebraic geometry, we use the term
\mathbb{F}_{1}
-algebra
to mean
commutative
\mathbb{F}_{1}
-algebra
throughout this paper, unless explicitly stated otherwise.

Remark 5.3
.

By the universal property of the smash product (Theorem
2.10
), a multiplication morphism
\mu:A\wedge A\to A
is equivalent to a collection of basepoint-preserving maps

\mu_{m,n}:A(m_{+})\wedge A(n_{+})\to A(m_{+}\wedge n_{+})

natural in both arguments. At level
1
, we obtain

\mu_{1,1}:A(1_{+})\times A(1_{+})\to A(1_{+})

which endows
A(1_{+})
with the structure of a pointed commutative monoid (where the basepoint is the absorbing element
0
).

This gives a forgetful functor:

-(1_{+}):\mathbb{F}_{1}\mathbf{Alg}\to\mathbf{CMonoid}_{*}

sending an
\mathbb{F}_{1}
-algebra to its underlying pointed commutative monoid. This functor is right adjoint to the spherical monoid functor described below (
[
3
]
, Proposition
3.3
).

Example 5.4

(Spherical monoid algebras)
.

Let
(M,\cdot,1)
be a pointed commutative monoid, meaning a commutative monoid with a distinguished element
0_{M}
satisfying
0_{M}\cdot x=0_{M}
for all
x\in M
. The spherical monoid object
\mathbb{S}M
from Definition
2.7
admits a natural
\mathbb{F}_{1}
-algebra structure.

The multiplication
\mu:\mathbb{S}M\wedge\mathbb{S}M\to\mathbb{S}M
is defined using the monoid operation on
M
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{S}M(m_{+})\wedge\mathbb{S}M(n_{+})

\displaystyle=(M\wedge m_{+})\wedge(M\wedge n_{+})

\displaystyle\cong M\wedge M\wedge(m_{+}\wedge n_{+})

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\cdot_{M}\wedge\mathrm{id}}M\wedge(m_{+}\wedge n_{+})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{S}M(m_{+}\wedge n_{+})

where
\cdot_{M}:M\wedge M\to M
is induced by the monoid operation on
M
(sending
(x,y)
to
x\cdot y
and the basepoint to
0_{M}
).

The unit morphism
\eta:\mathbb{F}_{1}\to\mathbb{S}M
is determined by the multiplicative unit:
\eta_{1_{+}}(1)=1_{M}\in M=\mathbb{S}M(1_{+})
.

This assignment
M\mapsto\mathbb{S}M
defines a fully faithful functor

\mathbb{S}:\mathbf{CMonoid}_{*}\to\mathbb{F}_{1}\mathbf{Alg}

from pointed commutative monoids to
\mathbb{F}_{1}
-algebras, providing the “free”
\mathbb{F}_{1}
-algebra construction on a pointed commutative monoid
[
3
]
.

Example 5.5

(Eilenberg-MacLane algebras)
.

Let
R
be a commutative semiring (a set with commutative operations
+
and
\cdot
satisfying ring axioms, but without requiring additive inverses). The Eilenberg-MacLane object
HR
from Definition
2.4
admits a natural
\mathbb{F}_{1}
-algebra structure.

The multiplication is defined levelwise by:

\displaystyle HR(m_{+})\times HR(n_{+})

\displaystyle\to HR((mn)_{+})

\displaystyle((x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}),(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}))

\displaystyle\mapsto(x_{i}\cdot y_{j})_{i\in\{1,\ldots,m\},j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}}

where the output is indexed by pairs
(i,j)
and we identify
m_{+}\wedge n_{+}\cong(mn)_{+}
.

The unit morphism
\eta:\mathbb{F}_{1}\to HR
is determined by
\eta_{1_{+}}(1)=1_{R}\in R=HR(1_{+})
.

This assignment
R\mapsto HR
defines a fully faithful functor

H:\mathbf{CSemiring}\to\mathbb{F}_{1}\mathbf{Alg}

from commutative semirings to
\mathbb{F}_{1}
-algebras
[
3
]
.

5.2.
Characterization of algebra maps to Eilenberg-MacLane algebras

We now establish the analogue of Lemma
4.2
for
\mathbb{F}_{1}
-algebras, adding the natural multiplicativity conditions:

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
A
be an
\mathbb{F}_{1}
-algebra, let
R
be a commutative ring, and let
f:A\to HR
be a morphism of
\mathbb{F}_{1}
-algebras. Then
f
is equivalent to the data of a map
g:A(1_{+})\to R
satisfying:

(i)

(Pointed)

g(*)=0\in R
.

(ii)

(Additive)
If
c\in a\oplus b
, then
g(c)=g(a)+g(b)
in
R
.

(iii)

(Unit)

g(1_{A})=1_{R}
, where
1_{A}
is the multiplicative unit in
A(1_{+})
.

(iv)

(Multiplicative)

g(a\cdot b)=g(a)\cdot_{R}g(b)
for all
a,b\in A(1_{+})
.

Conversely, any map
g:A(1_{+})\to R
satisfying these four conditions extends uniquely to a morphism of
\mathbb{F}_{1}
-algebras
f:A\to HR
such that
f|_{1_{+}}=g
.

Proof.

Let
f:A\to HR
be a morphism of
\mathbb{F}_{1}
-algebras. Define
g:=f|_{1_{+}}:A(1_{+})\to R
.

By Lemma
4.2
, since
f
is a morphism of the underlying
\Gamma
-sets, we know that
g
is pointed and additive. We verify the additional conditions:

Unit property:
Since
f
preserves the unit morphism, we have:

g(1_{A})=f|_{1_{+}}(1_{A})=1_{HR}=1_{R}.

Multiplicative property:
Since
f
preserves multiplication, for any
a,b\in A(1_{+})
:

\displaystyle g(a\cdot_{A}b)

\displaystyle=f|_{1_{+}}(\mu_{A}(a,b))

\displaystyle=\mu_{HR}(f|_{1_{+}}(a),f|_{1_{+}}(b))

\displaystyle=g(a)\cdot_{R}g(b)

where
\mu_{A}
and
\mu_{HR}
denote the multiplications in
A
and
HR
respectively, and the second equality uses that
f
is a morphism of
\mathbb{F}_{1}
-algebras.

By Lemma
4.2
,
f
is completely determined by
g=f|_{1_{+}}
.

Now we prove the other direction. Given a map
g:A(1_{+})\to R
satisfying all four conditions, Lemma
4.2
provides a unique morphism of
\Gamma
-sets
f:A\to HR
with
f|_{1_{+}}=g
. We verify that
f
is a morphism of
\mathbb{F}_{1}
-algebras.

Unit preservation:
We have

f(1_{A})=f|_{1_{+}}(1_{A})=g(1_{A})=1_{R}=1_{HR}.

Multiplication preservation:
We must show that for any
a\in A(m_{+})
and
b\in A(n_{+})
:

f(\mu_{A}(a,b))=\mu_{HR}(f(a),f(b))

where we suppress the levels in the notation for
\mu
.

By the construction in Lemma
4.2
, for any
x\in A(k_{+})
:

f|_{k_{+}}(x)=(g(A(p_{1})(x)),\ldots,g(A(p_{k})(x)))\in R^{\oplus k}=HR(k_{+}).

The product
\mu_{A}(a,b)\in A(m_{+}\wedge n_{+})\cong A((mn)_{+})
satisfies, for any
(i,j)\in\{1,\ldots,m\}\times\{1,\ldots,n\}
:

A(p_{i,j})(\mu_{A}(a,b))=A(p_{i})(a)\cdot A(p_{j})(b)

where
p_{i,j}:(mn)_{+}\to 1_{+}
is the projection onto the
(i,j)
-th coordinate, and the product on the right is at level
1
in
A
.

Therefore, the
(i,j)
-th component of
f(\mu_{A}(a,b))
is:

\displaystyle g(A(p_{i,j})(\mu_{A}(a,b)))

\displaystyle=g(A(p_{i})(a)\cdot A(p_{j})(b))

\displaystyle=g(A(p_{i})(a))\cdot_{R}g(A(p_{j})(b))\quad\text{(since }g\text{ is multiplicative)}

On the other hand, the
(i,j)
-th component of
\mu_{HR}(f(a),f(b))
is:

(f(a))_{i}\cdot_{R}(f(b))_{j}=g(A(p_{i})(a))\cdot_{R}g(A(p_{j})(b)).

Since these agree for all
(i,j)
, we have
f(\mu_{A}(a,b))=\mu_{HR}(f(a),f(b))
, establishing that
f
preserves multiplication.
∎

5.3.
Extension of scalars for
\mathbb{F}_{1}
-algebras

We now define the extension of scalars for
\mathbb{F}_{1}
-algebras by incorporating the multiplicative structure:

Definition 5.7
.

Let
A
be an
\mathbb{F}_{1}
-algebra. We define its
extension of scalars to
\mathbb{Z}
by

A\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\mathbb{Z}:=\mathbb{Z}[A(1_{+})]/\mathcal{R}

where:

•

\mathbb{Z}[A(1_{+})]
is the
monoid ring
of the pointed commutative monoid
A(1_{+})
. As an abelian group, this is the free abelian group on
A(1_{+})
(as in Definition
4.4
). The multiplication is defined by:

[a]\cdot[b]:=[a\cdot_{A}b]

extended bilinearly, where
\cdot_{A}
denotes the monoid operation on
A(1_{+})
from the
\mathbb{F}_{1}
-algebra structure. The multiplicative unit is
[1_{A}]
.

•

\mathcal{R}
is the
ideal
(not just subgroup) generated by the same relations as in Definition
4.4
:

(i)

(Basepoint relation)

[*]=0
.

(ii)

(Additivity relations)

[a]+[b]=[c]
for all
a,b,c\in A(1_{+})
with
c\in a\oplus b
.

The quotient inherits a commutative ring structure from
\mathbb{Z}[A(1_{+})]
.

Lemma 5.8
.

The assignment
A\mapsto A\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\mathbb{Z}
extends to a functor

-\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\mathbb{Z}:\mathbb{F}_{1}\mathbf{Alg}\to\mathbf{CRing}.

Proof.

Let
\varphi:A\to B
be a morphism of
\mathbb{F}_{1}
-algebras. By Lemma
4.6
, the induced map
\varphi|_{1_{+}}:A(1_{+})\to B(1_{+})
extends to a group homomorphism
\mathbb{Z}[\varphi]:\mathbb{Z}[A(1_{+})]\to\mathbb{Z}[B(1_{+})]
that descends to the quotient.

We verify that
\mathbb{Z}[\varphi]
is also a ring homomorphism. Since
\varphi
preserves multiplication in the
\mathbb{F}_{1}
-algebras, we have for
a,b\in A(1_{+})
:

\mathbb{Z}[\varphi]([a]\cdot[b])=\mathbb{Z}[\varphi]([a\cdot_{A}b])=[\varphi(a\cdot_{A}b)]=[\varphi(a)\cdot_{B}\varphi(b)]=[\varphi(a)]\cdot[\varphi(b)]=\mathbb{Z}[\varphi]([a])\cdot\mathbb{Z}[\varphi]([b]).

Similarly, since
\varphi
preserves units:

\mathbb{Z}[\varphi]([1_{A}])=[\varphi(1_{A})]=[1_{B}].

Therefore,
\mathbb{Z}[\varphi]
descends to a ring homomorphism:

\varphi\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\mathbb{Z}:A\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\mathbb{Z}\to B\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\mathbb{Z}.

Functoriality follows from the corresponding property in Lemma
4.6
.
∎

5.4.
The adjunction for
\mathbb{F}_{1}
-algebras

We now establish the main result, showing that extension of scalars for algebras is left adjoint to the Eilenberg-MacLane functor:

Theorem 5.9

(Theorem
C
)
.

The extension of scalars functor
-\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\mathbb{Z}:\mathbb{F}_{1}\mathbf{Alg}\to\mathbf{CRing}
is left adjoint to the Eilenberg-MacLane functor
H:\mathbf{CRing}\to\mathbb{F}_{1}\mathbf{Alg}
. That is, there is a natural bijection

\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbb{F}_{1}\mathbf{Alg}}(A,HR)\cong\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbf{CRing}}(A\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\mathbb{Z},R)

for all
\mathbb{F}_{1}
-algebras
A
and commutative rings
R
.

Proof.

The proof follows the same structure as Theorem
4.7
, with modifications to account for the multiplicative structure.

The canonical map

\displaystyle\iota:A(1_{+})

\displaystyle\to A\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\mathbb{Z}

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle\mapsto[a]

is now a pointed additive multiplicative unital map (satisfying all four conditions of Lemma
5.6
):

•

Pointed:

\iota(*)=[*]=0
by the basepoint relation.

•

Additive:
If
c\in a\oplus b
, then
\iota(c)=[c]=[a]+[b]=\iota(a)+\iota(b)
by the additivity relations.

•

Unit:

\iota(1_{A})=[1_{A}]=1
in
A\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\mathbb{Z}
(the multiplicative identity in the ring).

•

Multiplicative:

\iota(a\cdot b)=[a\cdot b]=[a]\cdot[b]=\iota(a)\cdot\iota(b)
by the ring structure on the monoid ring.

This gives a commutative diagram:
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A(1_{+})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\mathbb{Z}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces R}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\iota}

where any pointed additive multiplicative unital map from
A(1_{+})
to
R
factors uniquely through
\iota
, as we show below.

Define
\Phi
as follows:

\Phi:\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbb{F}_{1}\mathbf{Alg}}(A,HR)\to\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbf{CRing}}(A\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\mathbb{Z},R)

Given
f:A\to HR
, let
g:=f|_{1_{+}}:A(1_{+})\to R
. By Lemma
5.6
,
g
is a pointed additive multiplicative unital map.

The ring homomorphism:

\displaystyle\tilde{h}:\mathbb{Z}[A(1_{+})]

\displaystyle\to R

\displaystyle\sum_{a\in A(1_{+})}n_{a}\cdot[a]

\displaystyle\mapsto\sum_{a\in A(1_{+})}n_{a}\cdot g(a)

preserves both addition and multiplication (since
g
is a monoid homomorphism), and vanishes on the ideal
\mathcal{R}
by the same argument as in Theorem
4.7
.

Therefore,
\tilde{h}
descends to a unique ring homomorphism
h:A\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\mathbb{Z}\to R
such that
h\circ\iota=g
. We define
\Phi(f):=h
.

On the other direction, define:

\Psi:\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbf{CRing}}(A\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\mathbb{Z},R)\to\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbb{F}_{1}\mathbf{Alg}}(A,HR)

Given
h:A\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\mathbb{Z}\to R
, define
g:=h\circ\iota:A(1_{+})\to R
. Then the composition
g
is still a pointed additive multiplicative unital map, which by Lemma
5.6
extends uniquely to a morphism of
\mathbb{F}_{1}
-algebras
f:A\to HR
with
f|_{1_{+}}=g
. We define
\Psi(h):=f
.

The verification that
\Phi
and
\Psi
are inverses, and the proof of naturality of the correspondence, follow the same arguments as in Theorem
4.7
.
∎

5.5.
Examples

Example 5.10

(Extension of
\mathbb{F}_{1}
as an algebra)
.

As an
\mathbb{F}_{1}
-algebra (not just as an
\mathbb{F}_{1}
-module), we have

\mathbb{F}_{1}\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\mathbb{Z}\cong\mathbb{Z}

as a commutative ring.

The underlying
\Gamma
-set gives
\mathbb{Z}
as an abelian group (Example
4.8
). The multiplicative structure comes from
\mathbb{F}_{1}(1_{+})=\{0,1\}
with the usual multiplication (
0\cdot x=0
,
1\cdot 1=1
). The monoid ring
\mathbb{Z}[\{0,1\}]
with the basepoint relation
[0]=0
gives:

\mathbb{F}_{1}\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\mathbb{Z}=\mathbb{Z}[1]\cong\mathbb{Z}

where
[1]
generates
\mathbb{Z}
both additively and multiplicatively (as
1_{\mathbb{Z}}
).

This confirms that extending scalars from
\mathbb{F}_{1}
to
\mathbb{Z}
produces
\mathbb{Z}
itself as a ring, serving as the base ring for classical algebraic geometry.

Example 5.11

(Spherical monoid algebras)
.

Let
M
be a pointed commutative monoid. Then

(\mathbb{S}M)\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\mathbb{Z}\cong\mathbb{Z}[M]

is the integral monoid ring of
M
with the basepoint removed.

In particular, when
M
is a monoid without a distinguished absorbing element, we may first freely adjoin one to make it pointed, then take the monoid ring. This recovers Deitmar’s construction:

M\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{1}(Deitmar)}\mathbb{Z}\cong\mathbb{Z}[M].

This shows that our construction generalizes Deitmar’s base change functor: for spherical monoid algebras (which encode purely multiplicative structure), extension of scalars recovers the classical monoid ring construction.

Example 5.12

(Eilenberg-MacLane algebras)
.

For a commutative ring
R
, we have

(HR)\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\mathbb{Z}\cong R.

This follows because in
HR
, every
n
-ary sum has a unique representative:
a_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus a_{n}=\{a_{1}+_{R}\cdots+_{R}a_{n}\}
. Therefore, the additivity relations in Definition
5.7
impose:

[a]+[b]=[a+_{R}b]

for all
a,b\in R
. Combined with the multiplicative structure
[a]\cdot[b]=[a\cdot_{R}b]
from the monoid ring, the quotient
\mathbb{Z}[R]/\mathcal{R}
is isomorphic to
R
itself. The semiring homomorphism
R\to(HR)\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\mathbb{Z}
sending
a\mapsto[a]
is an isomorphism.

This shows that the extension of scalars functor is the identity on Eilenberg-MacLane algebras of commutative rings, confirming that the adjunction
(-\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\mathbb{Z})\dashv H
provides a reflection of
\mathbb{F}_{1}\mathbf{Alg}
onto
\mathbf{CRing}
.

More generally, if
R
is a commutative semiring (not necessarily a ring), then

(HR)\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\mathbb{Z}\cong R^{\mathrm{gp}}

where
R^{\mathrm{gp}}
denotes the
group completion
or
ring completion
of
R
—the universal commutative ring receiving a semiring homomorphism from
R
. This can be constructed as:

R^{\mathrm{gp}}=\mathbb{Z}[R]/\langle[a]+[b]=[a+_{R}b],\,[a]\cdot[b]=[a\cdot_{R}b],\,[0_{R}]=0,\,[1_{R}]=1\rangle.

The same calculation as above shows that our construction recovers this universal property.

Example 5.13

(Quotients of Eilenberg-MacLane algebras)
.

Consider the quotient
X=H\mathbb{Z}/H(n\mathbb{Z})
as an
\mathbb{F}_{1}
-algebra (with ring structure from
\mathbb{Z}
). Then:

X\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\mathbb{Z}\cong\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}

as a commutative ring, by the same calculation as Example
4.11
, where now the ring structure is preserved through the quotient.

This flexibility, combined with the extension of scalars mechanism established in this paper, provides an appropriate framework for developing geometric theories over

.

6.
Geometric interpretation and future directions

Recall from classical algebraic geometry that for any commutative ring
R
, the base change functor
-\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}R
induces a functor between affine schemes:

\mathbf{AffSch}_{\mathbb{Z}}\to\mathbf{AffSch}_{R}

given by
\mathrm{Spec}A\mapsto\mathrm{Spec}(A\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}R)
.

Our construction provides an analogous functor for absolute algebraic geometry. In forthcoming work
[
11
]
, we develop a theory of affine schemes over
\mathbb{F}_{1}
(or absolute affine schemes) by defining, for a commutative
\mathbb{F}_{1}
-algebra
A
, a suitable prime spectrum

\mathrm{Spec}A:=(|\mathrm{Spec}A|,\mathcal{O}_{A})

such that there is an anti-equivalence of categories:

\mathrm{Spec}:\mathbb{F}_{1}\mathbf{Alg}^{\mathrm{op}}\xrightarrow{\sim}\mathbf{AffSch}_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}.

The adjunction established in Theorem
5.9
:

-\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\mathbb{Z}:\mathbb{F}_{1}\mathbf{Alg}\leftrightarrows\mathbf{CRing}:H

hence provides a base change mechanism for affine schemes over
\mathbb{F}_{1}
by contravariance of
\mathrm{Spec}
.

This construction provides the algebraic foundations for the Connes-Consani program of absolute algebraic geometry
[
3
,
4
]
. While they introduce
\Gamma
-sets as
\mathbb{F}_{1}
-modules and develop the geometric framework, an explicit extension of scalars functor is not provided. Our results fill this gap: the law of generalized associativity (Theorem
3.16
) explains why the hyper-additive structure of
\Gamma
-sets can be strictified despite lacking classical associativity, and the adjunction
(-\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\mathbb{Z})\dashv H
(Theorems
4.7
and
5.9
) provides a base change mechanism.

We hope that this work provides useful foundations for further developments in absolute algebraic geometry and its applications.
