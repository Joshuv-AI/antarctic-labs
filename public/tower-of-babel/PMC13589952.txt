Dual-modal tumor-selective benzofuran tracer integrating real-time imaging and anticancer effects
Authors: Ho Jin Han, Min Su Yim, Gwi Ja Hwang, Junyeol Han, Sumin Kim, Yerim Choi, Min Ju Kim, Kyeong-Ryoon Lee, Junhye Kwon, Uisup Shin, Eun Young Shin, Jeong Kyu Bang, Eun Kyoung Ryu, Bo Yeon Kim, Nak-Kyun Soung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Microtubule-targeting agents remain a cornerstone of cancer therapy; however, few compounds can simultaneously visualize intratumoral distribution and correlate pharmacodynamics with therapeutic response. Here, we describe BF-04, a Benzofuran-derived microtubule destabilizer that circumvents multidrug resistance and integrates anticancer efficacy with dual-modality imaging. Chemical conjugation with the Cyanine 5 fluorescent dye (Cy5) and Zirconium-89 (89Zr) provided high-contrast, real-time fluorescence and Positron emission tomography (PET) signals without the need for antibody or nanoparticle carriers, enabling direct monitoring of drug distribution and retention in tumors. Importantly, BF-04 exhibited tumor-selective accumulation independent of labeling, confirming a scaffold-driven targeting mechanism with strong translational potential for image-guided therapy. Mechanistically, BF-04 induced microtubule depolymerization, mitotic arrest, and apoptosis across multiple cancer cell lines and patient-derived organoids (PDOs). In vivo studies demonstrated selective tumor uptake and robust growth suppression with minimal systemic toxicity. Collectively, these findings establish BF-04 as a promising theranostic platform that combines potent microtubule disruption with real-time imaging to facilitate precision cancer therapy.

Introduction

Microtubules are polymers composed of α- and β-tubulin heterodimers. They undergo rapid, reversible assembly and disassembly, a process known as microtubule dynamics. This continual remodeling enables the formation of the mitotic spindle during cell division and supports broader roles in cell cycle progression, intracellular transport, and maintenance of cell shape and polarity1, 2, 3.

In most differentiated adult tissues, normal somatic cells are in a quiescent state (G0), dividing infrequently and thus rarely undergoing the rapid microtubule remodeling required for mitosis4. In contrast, most aggressive tumors maintain a high growth rate and frequently enter mitosis. Consequently, they rely heavily on microtubules, which must undergo continuous reorganization to form the mitotic spindle and are therefore recognized as key targets of anticancer agents5. Accordingly, microtubule-stabilizing agents, such as taxanes, and destabilizing agents, such as vinca alkaloids, have been widely used in cancer therapy to disrupt mitotic progression by interfering with microtubule dynamics6.

However, many of these microtubule-targeting agents are substrates of the ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporter p-glycoprotein (P-gp). P-gp actively exports a broad range of chemotherapeutic agents out of cells, thereby reducing their intracellular concentrations7. This efflux mechanism plays a crucial role in the development of multidrug resistance (MDR), ultimately compromising the clinical efficacy of microtubule-targeting agents8. To overcome this limitation, we previously reported microtubule depolymerizing agent STK899704, which effectively bypasses multidrug resistance. However, due to its rapid systemic clearance, it failed to demonstrate meaningful efficacy in xenograft models9. To improve its drug-like properties, we synthesized a series of derivatives in which the naphthofuran core was replaced with a benzofuran moiety. This optimization ultimately led to the identification of BF-04, a novel compound that exhibits increased anticancer activity in both in vitro and in vivo settings.

While the development of novel therapeutic agents remains a central focus in cancer treatment, early detection is equally critical for improving clinical outcomes10. Accordingly, considerable efforts have been devoted to advancing molecular imaging technologies capable of visualizing tumor biology in situ with high specificity and sensitivity11. Among them, positron emission tomography (PET) has emerged as a powerful non-invasive modality that enables quantitative assessment of tumor metabolism and specific molecular activity in vivo12. A representative PET tracer, 18F-fluoro-2-deoxy-d-glucose (18F-FDG), introduced in the 1970s, evaluates glucose metabolism to localize tumors and has been widely used in clinical oncology13. However, its short half-life (110 min) and glycolysis-based uptake often result in non-specific background signals, limiting its applicability for extended or delayed imaging windows14. To address these limitations, long-lived radio nuclides have been developed. The clinical utility of zirconium-89 (89Zr) was first demonstrated in 2006, using an antibody-conjugated 89Zr to detect lymph node metastases in head and neck cancer15. It has since been under active investigation for molecular imaging applications16. It has been validated for high-contrast imaging at delayed time points, leading to its widespread use in immuno-PET, particularly for tracking monoclonal antibodies and related therapeutics17. Nevertheless, full-length antibodies exhibit slow tumor penetration, limited target specificity, and delayed imaging protocols. In contrast, small molecules offer faster pharmacokinetics and deeper tumor penetration than antibodies18, 19, 20, 21, 22.

In this study, we first evaluated the Cyanine 5 fluorescent dye (Cy5)-conjugated BF-04 (Cy5-BF-04) as a preclinical tumor tracer. Near-infrared fluorophores such as Cy5 are widely used in preclinical optical imaging because they afford low background autofluorescence and high tissue penetration23. We performed experiments with BF-04 conjugated with Cy5 and 89Zr, respectively, to confirm tubulin engagement and in vivo tumor targeting. We conjugated Cy5 to BF-04 to confirm tubulin engagement and tumor targeting in vivo, subsequently attached a deferoxamine (DFO) chelator for stable 89Zr radiolabeling. The resulting labeled derivatives enable real-time optical tracking of drug distribution and high-sensitivity PET visualization over multiple days. Collectively, these developments position BF-04 as a next-generation microtubule-destabilizing agent that functions not only as an anticancer agent but also as an image-guided tool for cancer visualization22.

Experimental section

Chemical synthesis

All chemicals were of reagent grade or higher and were obtained from commercial suppliers without further purification. The synthetic procedures for BF-04, Cy5-BF-04, DFO-BF-04, and Biotin-BF-04 are described in the Supporting Information

Patient-derived colon cancer organoid culture

Colon tumor specimens were processed for organoid culture using Hans Clevers’ protocol24. Tissues were washed with HBSS, minced, and digested at 37 °C for 30 min in a solution containing 1.5 mg/mL Collagenase II (Worthington Biochemical Corporation, Lakewood, NJ, USA) and 20 μg/mL Hyaluronidase (Sigma–Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). The digested samples were filtered through a 100 μm strainer, mixed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing 10% (v/v) calf serum, and centrifuged at 1200 rpm for 3 min.

Pellets were mixed with Matrigel (Corning Life Sciences, Corning, NY, USA), seeded into a 24-well plate, and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. After solidification, 500 μL of growth medium (DMEM/F12 with B27 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), N-acetyl cysteine (Tocris Bioscience, Bristol, UK), hEGF (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific), Noggin (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific), A83-01 (Tocris Bioscience), Gastrin (Sigma–Aldrich), and Y-27632 (Tocris Bioscience) was added and refreshed every 3 days.

For passaging, organoids were dissociated using a gentle cell dissociation buffer, centrifuged at 600×g for 5 min at 4 °C, and resuspended in fresh Matrigel for replating.

Organoids #2, #6, #10, #11, #13, #14, and #17 were established in-house, while others were obtained from the Korea Institute of Radiological and Medical Sciences. This study followed BRISQ guidelines. Tissue samples were collected during surgery and cultured under organoid culture conditions within 1–2 h at 4 °C.

Cell culture

HeLa (ATCC, CCL-2, USA), MCF-7 (KRIBB, H007, Korea), and K-562 (KCLB, 10,243, Korea) cell lines were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA), KRIBB, and Korea Cell Line Bank (KCLB, Seoul, Korea). The MCF-7/ADR and K-562/ADR cell lines were kindly provided by the Laboratory Animal Resource & Research Center at the Korea Research Institute of Bioscience and Biotechnology (KRIBB, Cheongju, Korea). Cells were cultured at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 in culture medium supplemented with 100 U/mL penicillin, 100 U/mL streptomycin, and 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Cells were routinely tested for mycoplasma contamination every six months. All cell lines were confirmed to be free of mycoplasma contamination throughout the experimental period (Supporting Information Table S1).

Western blot

HeLa cells were seeded in 6-well plates and cultured for 24 h prior to compound treatment. Cells were treated with BF-04 (0, 0.1, 0.25, 0.5, and 1 μmol/L) or colchicine (25 and 50 nmol/L) for either 24 or 48 h. DMSO was used as a vehicle control. After treatment, cells were lysed in TBSN buffer (50 mmol/L Tris–HCl (pH 8.0), 120 mmol/L NaCl, 5 mmol/L EGTA, 1.5 mmol/L EDTA, 0.5% NP-40) containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Lysates were centrifuged to remove debris, and the supernatant was collected and mixed with 2 × SDS sample buffer. Proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto a Polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA).

Membranes were blocked in 5% (w/v) skim milk in TBST (50 mmol/L Tris–HCl (pH 8.0), 120 mmol/L NaCl, 5 mmol/L EGTA, 1.5 mmol/L EDTA, 0.01% Tween-20) for 1 h at room temperature, then incubated with primary antibodies. After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies and developed using an Enhanced Chemiluminescence kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). A list of antibodies used is provided in the Supporting Information Table S2.

Cancer organoid viability assay

Organoids were seeded in 96-well plates at 10 μL/well and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min to solidify the Matrigel. After the Matrigel had solidified, 100 μL of organoid growth medium was added to each well. Organoids were then treated with BF-04 at concentrations of 0, 0.25, 0.5, and 1 μmol/L. Dimethylsulfoxide (DMSO) was used as a vehicle control. After 7 days of incubation, organoid viability was assessed using the EZ-cytox cell viability assay solution (LPS solution, Daejeon, Korea). Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader, and viability was normalized to the DMSO-treated control group. Representative images were captured using a Zeiss microscope (Carl Zeiss, Jena, Germany). All patient-derived organoids tested negative for mycoplasma contamination throughout the study (Supporting Information Table S3).

Immunofluorescence staining

Organoids #4 and #8 were seeded in 8-well chamber slides and incubated at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 until the Matrigel solidified. For apoptosis analysis, chamber slide organoids were treated with BF-04 at 0, 0.25, 0.5, or 1 μmol/L for 4 days, fixed in 4% (v/v) paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.1% (v/v) Triton X-100, blocked overnight at 4 °C in 1% (w/v) BSA, incubated for 3 h with primary antibodies, followed by 1 h with fluorescence-conjugated secondary antibodies. DNA was stained with 0.1 μg/mL Hoechst 33,342 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). For Cy5 tracing images, organoids or spheroids were treated with 1 μmol/L of Cy5 only or Cy5-BF-04 for 1 or 24 h. Organoids were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.5% (v/v) NP-40, and blocked for 2 h in 5% BSA, and stained with Hoechst 33,342.

For tumor tissues, samples were fixed overnight in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned into 6 μm slices using a microtome. Sections were mounted onto glass slides, deparaffinized, and subjected to antigen retrieval by boiling in antigen retrieval buffer (10 mmol/L Tris-base, 1 mmol/L EDTA, 0.05% (v/v) Tween-20, pH 9.0) for 20 min. After three washes with PBS, sections were blocked with BSA and incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-cleaved Caspase-3 antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA). After additional PBS washes, the slides were incubated with Texas Red-conjugated secondary antibodies, and the nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst 33,342 (0.1 μg/mL).

Flow cytometric analysis

HeLa cells were seeded in 6-well plates and incubated at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 for 24 h. Cells were then treated with BF-04 (0, 0.1, 0.25, 0.5, and 1 μmol/L) or colchicine (25 and 50 nmol/L) for 24 or 48 h. DMSO was used as a vehicle control.

For apoptosis detection, 48 h after treatment, cells were harvested and stained with APC-conjugated Annexin V and propidium iodide (PI) using the Annexin V apoptosis detection kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Flow cytometric analysis was performed using a CytoFLEX system (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA). For cell cycle phase analysis, 24 h after treatment, the cells were collected, washed with PBS, and fixed in 70% (v/v) ethanol at −20 °C. After fixation, cells were washed with PBS and stained with PI. DNA content was analyzed by flow cytometry using the CytoFLEX system. All data were quantified using FlowJo software (FlowJo LLC, Ashland, OR, USA).

α/β-Tubulin polymerization/depolymerization assay

Tubulin polymerization/depolymerization assays were performed according to the manufacturer's protocol (Cytoskeleton Inc., Denver, CO, USA). Briefly, 200 μL of purified tubulin protein was resuspended in 420 μL of tubulin polymerization buffer (80 mmol/L PIPES, pH 6.9, 2 mmol/L MgCl2, 0.5 mmol/L EDTA, 3.75 % glycerol, and 1 mmol/L GTP) to a final concentration of 5 mg/mL, on ice. Then, 100 μL of the reaction mixture was transferred to each well of a 96-well plate containing 10 μL of BF-04, paclitaxel, colchicine, or DMSO (control). All compounds were tested at a final concentration of 20 μmol/L. The samples were gently mixed, and tubulin polymerization was monitored by measuring the increase in turbidity at 340 nm at 37 °C in real time.

Molecular docking with DiffDock

Molecular docking of BF-04 with tubulin dimer proteins was performed using the DiffDock algorithm implemented via the Neurosnap platform (Neurosnap Inc., Wilmington, DE, USA). The three-dimensional structure of the tubulin dimer was retrieved from the Protein Data Bank. The top-ranked binding poses generated by DiffDock were selected and further analyzed. Two-dimensional interaction diagrams between BF-04 and the tubulin dimer were generated using PoseView (University of Hamburg, Hamburg, Germany).

Bio-layer interferometry assay (BLItz)

Bio-layer interferometry was performed using a BLItz instrument (Sartorius, Fremont, CA, USA) with streptavidin-coated biosensor tips. The tips were pre-wetted in incubation buffer (10 mmol/L HEPES, pH 7.4, 1% BSA, 0.05% Tween-20) for 10 min. After equilibration, the tips were transferred to a solution containing 10 μmol/L biotin-BF-04 in incubation buffer and incubated for 180 s to allow ligand binding. The sensors were then washed in incubation buffer for 60 s. Following ligand loading, the tips were incubated with tubulin dimer solutions (0.1, 0.25, 0.5, or 1 μmol/L) for 180 s to measure the association phase. The sensors were then transferred into fresh incubation buffer for 180 s to monitor dissociation. The equilibrium dissociation constant (KD) was calculated using BLItz Pro v1.1 software (Sartorius).

Cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA)

HeLa cells were treated with 20 μmol/L BF-04 or DMSO for 1 h at 37 °C. Cells were washed twice with PBS, detached with trypsin-EDTA, and resuspended in PBS containing a protease inhibitor cocktail. The suspension was aliquoted equally into PCR tubes and heated for 3 min at 58–61 °C, then immediately cooled on ice (4 °C) for 3 min. Cells were lysed in TBSN buffer containing protease inhibitors for 30 min on ice. Insoluble debris was removed by centrifugation at 20,000×g for 30 min at 4 °C. Supernatants were mixed with SDS sample buffer, resolved by SDS-PAGE, and transferred to PVDF membranes. Tubulin stability was evaluated by Western blot analysis using an anti-α-tubulin antibody, with GAPDH serving as a loading control. Band intensities were quantified relative to those in DMSO using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Xenograft mouse model

Male BALB/c nude mice (nu/nu, 5 weeks of age) were purchased from NARA Biotech Animal Center (NARA-Biotech, Seoul, Korea) or the Central Laboratory Animal Center (Central Lab. Animal Inc., Seoul, Korea). For the antitumor efficacy study, HeLa cells (5 × 106 cells) were subcutaneously injected into the right subscapular region. When the average tumor volume reached approximately 40 mm3, the mice were randomly divided into three groups (n = 5 per group). BF-04 (25 mg/kg), BF-07 (25 mg/kg), or vehicle control was administered intraperitoneally once daily for 15 consecutive days. Tumor volume and body weight were measured twice a week during the treatment period. One day after the final injection, the mice were sacrificed. Tumor tissues were collected and fixed overnight at 4 °C in 10% neutral buffered formalin for histological analysis.

For the tumor tracer imaging study, HeLa cells or organoids #4, #8, and #14 were subcutaneously injected into the right foreleg or thigh region of each mouse. When the tumor volume reached approximately 100 mm3, the tumor-bearing xenograft mice were used for in vivo imaging studies.

Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) assay

Xenograft tumor tissue was deparaffinized and rehydrated, and slides were incubated in 0.85% (w/v) NaCl in PBS at room temperature for 5 min. Next, samples were incubated in a 20 μg/mL protease K solution (Sigma–Aldrich) for 5 min. Slides were then washed and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS for 10 min.

Slides were incubated with 50 μL of DNA labeling solution (20% TdT reaction buffer, 1.5% TdT enzyme, 16% BrdUTP, 62.5% H2O) for 1 h at 37 °C. After washing, sections were incubated with anti-BrdU solution for 30 min. Total DNA was stained with Hoechst 33,342.

Live cell imaging

HeLa cells expressing Green Fluorescent Protein (GFP)-α-tubulin were seeded into 2-well chambered Lab Tek cover glasses (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and cultured overnight at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. Immediately before imaging, cells were treated with either 1 μmol/L Cy5-only or 1 μmol/L Cy5-BF-04. Time-lapse fluorescence images were acquired using a Zeiss confocal microscope (Carl Zeiss) equipped with an environmental control chamber set to 37 °C and 5% CO2. Images were collected at 2-min intervals starting immediately after compound addition. Image acquisition and analysis were performed using Zen software (Carl Zeiss).

In vivo fluorescence imaging of tumor uptake

To confirm tumor uptake of the fluorescent probe, Cy5-conjugated BF-04 was administered intravenously (IV) via the tail vein to xenograft mice (5 mg/kg body weight in 100 μL). Fluorescence images were acquired using the In Vivo Imaging System Spectrum (IVIS; PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA) at pre-injection and at 6, 24, 30, and 48 h post-injection, using a 640 nm excitation and 700 nm emission filter set (spectral unmixing performed at 680, 700, and 720 nm; binning factor = 8).

At 24 and 48 h post-injection, mice were euthanized, and major organs (heart, lung, liver, spleen, and kidney), along with tumors, were collected. Ex vivo fluorescence images were acquired using the same IVIS settings as those used for in vivo imaging. Fluorescence intensities of tumors and organs were quantified using Living Image software (PerkinElmer). For quantitative analysis, tumor-associated signals were quantified by delineating a primary region of interest (ROI) that encompassed the entire fluorescent lesion and measuring the mean fluorescence intensity of the entire tumor ROI. All data were automatically corrected using Living Image software to ensure objective, background-subtracted quantification. Fluorescence levels are reported as photons per second per square centimeter per steradian (p/s/cm2/sr).

Cancer cell uptake assay of 89Zr-DFO-BF-04

To examine the cellular uptake of 89Zr-DFO-BF-04, HeLa cells (1 × 105 cells/well in 12-well) were incubated with 0.03 MBq of 89Zr-DFO-BF-04 for 1, 2, 4, and 6 h. After incubation, cells were rinsed five times with PBS and harvested using 0.1 N NaOH. The radioactivity of the cell lysate was counted using a gamma counter (Wizard2, PerkinElmer). For the blocking study to verify the specificity of 89Zr-DFO-BF-04 uptake into HeLa cells, the cells were incubated with 0.03 MBq of 89Zr-DFO-BF-04 and free form of BF-04 (0.5 μmol/L) for 1, 2, 4, and 6 h, and then treated as described above.

MicroPET and biodistribution study of 89Zr-DFO-BF-04

89Zr-DFO-BF-04 was intravenously injected via the tail vein into tumor-bearing xenograft mice (5 mg/kg, 1.11 MBq/200 μL). PET images were acquired for 20 min at 1, 24, 48, 72, and 96 h post-injection using a microPET scanner (Siemens Medical Solutions, Knoxville, TN, USA). All images were analyzed using Inveon Acquisition Workplace software (Siemens Medical Solutions). Tumor uptake was quantified using 3-dimensional (3D) volumetric ROIs across coronal, sagittal, and axial slices, with signal-to-background ratios (SBR) calculated relative to the contralateral muscle, and radioactivity uptake was quantified and expressed as the percentage of the injected dose per gram of tissue (%ID/g). Automatic background correction was performed using the built-in algorithms of the IAW software to provide objective measurements.

For ex vivo biodistribution analysis, mice injected with 89Zr-DFO-BF-04 (5 mg/kg, 0.66 MBq/200 μL) were sacrificed after the in vivo PET scan. Blood, major organs (heart, lung, liver, spleen, and kidney), and tumor tissues were promptly harvested. Radioactivity in the collected tissues was measured using a gamma counter (PerkinElmer), and the results were expressed as a percentage of the injected dose per gram (%ID/g).

Statistical analysis

All data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) from at least three independent experiments. Data were analyzed using Student's t-test with GraphPad Prism 6.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). A P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All experiments were independently repeated at least three times.

Results

Identification and validation of BF-04 as a lead anticancer candidate

Previously, we reported on a naphthofuran-based microtubule-targeting anticancer agent, STK-899704. However, due to its unfavorable pharmacokinetic properties, in vivo evaluation using xenograft models was not possible. To overcome this limitation and enhance antitumor activity, we synthesized 16 novel analogues based on a benzofuran scaffold. Cytotoxicity screening using the MTT assay in HeLa cells identified the top three candidates with potent anti-proliferation activity (Supporting Information Table S4). SwissADME analysis revealed that BF-04 exhibited the most favorable drug-like ADME profile among the candidates25 (Supporting Information Fig. S1 and Supporting Information Table S5). Subsequent in vivo pharmacokinetic profiling confirmed that BF-04 exhibited superior exposure compared to the other candidates (Supporting Information Fig. S2 and Supporting Information Table S6). In a pilot xenograft study using three mice per compound, BF-04 demonstrated the strongest anti-tumor efficacy and was thus selected as the lead compound for further investigation (Supporting Information Fig. S3).

To further evaluate its anticancer potential, BF-04 was tested against 20 cancer cell lines of diverse tissue origins, yielding a submicromolar IC50 while exhibiting low toxicity in normal cells (Supporting Information Table S7). Additionally, its efficacy against multidrug resistance was assessed using paired drug-sensitive and resistant cell lines, including K562 and K562-ADR, as well as MCF-7 and MCF-7-ADR. BF-04 maintained submicromolar IC50 values across four lines, with resistance indices (RIs) of 0.53 and 0.76, indicating preserved sensitivity in MDR cells. In contrast, colchicine, a known P-gp substrate used as a control, exhibited high levels of resistance, with RI values of 30.2 and 37.6 in MDR cells compared to sensitive cells. These results suggest that BF-04 effectively bypasses multidrug resistance (Supporting Information Table S8 and Supporting Information Fig. S4).

BF-04 induces apoptosis in cancer cells and cancer organoids

Given that many clinically approved anticancer agents exert their effects by inducing apoptosis, we investigated whether BF-04 triggers apoptosis in cancer cells26. HeLa cells were treated with various doses of BF-04 and analyzed by Annexin V/PI flow cytometry. Apoptotic cells were detected at 0.25 μmol/L, and the death population increased in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 1C). To validate these results, we performed immunoblotting for key proteins associated with apoptosis. Cleavage of PARP and Caspase-3, Caspase-7, and Caspase-9 was observed at 0.25 μmol/L and increased with higher doses (Fig. 1D).

To confirm these findings in a 3D model that more closely mimics the tumor microenvironment, PDOs were treated with BF-04 for 4 days and stained for cleaved Caspase-3. All treated organoids displayed enhanced apoptotic signal (Fig. 1E).

Collectively, these data demonstrate that BF-04 exerts its anticancer activity by activating the apoptotic pathway.

Consistent with this observation, immunoblotting revealed increased expression of PLK1, Aurora kinase B, and phosphorylated Histone H3 at 0.25 μmol/L, with further elevation at higher concentrations (Fig. 2B). Given the observed mitotic arrest, we investigated whether it was associated with changes in microtubule dynamics. To this end, we assessed the activity of BF-04 using the tubulin polymerization/depolymerization assay (Fig. 2C and D). The results indicated that BF-04 exhibited a polymerization pattern closely resembling that of colchicine, a well-known destabilizing agent, suggesting that BF-04 functions as a microtubule destabilizing agent (Fig. 2C and D).

Based on these findings, we next investigated whether BF-04 directly interacts with tubulin. To assess target engagement, molecular docking with DiffDock predicted that BF-04 binds at the interface between α- and β-tubulin subunits (Fig. 2E). Binding affinity was measured using Bio-Layer Interferometry with biotin-conjugated BF-04, resulting in a dissociation constant (KD) of 0.245 μmol/L (Fig. 2E). To further validate the direct interaction between BF-04 and tubulin in a cellular context, we performed a cellular thermal shift assay. This assay evaluates target binding by measuring changes in protein thermal stability upon compound treatment. HeLa cells were treated with BF-04 or DMSO and then exposed to a temperature gradient. In the DMSO-treated group, tubulin was largely degraded at 61 °C, whereas BF-04-treated cells retained a substantial amount of intact tubulin at the same temperature. These findings provide additional cellular evidence that BF-04 directly interacts with tubulin (Fig. 2F).

BF-04 suppresses tumor growth in vivo

Building on the in vitro findings, we next assessed whether BF-04 exhibits antitumor efficacy in vivo. After subcutaneous injection of HeLa cells into mice to establish tumors, vehicle, BF-04, or BF-07 was administered intraperitoneally at 25 mg/kg daily for 15 days. Consistent with previous pilot studies, BF-07 treatment resulted in approximately 40% inhibition of tumor growth compared with the vehicle group. Notably, BF-04 exhibited a more pronounced antitumor effect, demonstrating 68% tumor suppression in vivo (Fig. 3A–C). To further elucidate the mechanism underlying this antitumor effect, we performed immunostaining of tumor sections for phospho-histone H3, which indicated that the BF-04 and BF-07 injection groups exhibited increased mitotic arrest (Fig. 3D). Novel microtubule-targeting agents are often associated with systemic toxicity, which can limit their application27. To evaluate the toxicological properties of BF-04, we first assessed its cytotoxicity in normal cells, which revealed minimal toxicity (Table S7). Consistently, no significant body weight loss was observed in xenograft models (Fig. 3F). To further investigate the in vivo safety of BF-04, ICR mice were administered BF-04 at doses of 0, 12.5, 25, 50, and 100 mg/kg daily for 10 days. Behavioral assessments revealed no notable changes in locomotion, speed, or general activity (Supporting Information Fig. S7A–S7C). Body weights also remained stable across all groups, indicating that BF-04 did not induce significant systemic toxicity at the tested doses (Fig. S7D). We performed H&E staining of the liver, a primary organ for drug metabolism and a key site of potential hepatotoxicity, and examined the spleen to evaluate hematologic and immune-related alterations. Using the same mice employed in the behavioral assessment (Fig. S7A), liver and spleen tissues were collected and subjected to histopathological analysis. No apparent hepatocellular injury was observed at doses up to 50 mg/kg, corresponding to the dosing regimen used in efficacy studies and up to a two-fold higher dose. At 100 mg/kg, focal inflammatory cell infiltration along the hepatic capsule/serosal surface (serositis-like change) was noted, while hepatic parenchymal architecture remained largely preserved. Spleen histology showed no clear treatment-related differences compared with the control across the tested dose range (Fig. S7E).

Tumor-selective accumulation of free-form BF-04 in HeLa and PDO xenograft models

A competition assay in HeLa cells was conducted to confirm that free-form BF-04 could selectively target tumors independently of labeling. Treatment of HeLa cells with 89Zr-DFO-BF-04 showed clear cellular labeling, which was reduced upon co-treatment with free-form BF-04, indicating effective blocking and confirming that tumor targeting was mediated by BF-04 (Fig. 6C). To investigate in vivo tumor-selective accumulation, BF-04 was administered intravenously to HeLa xenograft mice, and the mice were sacrificed at 3, 6, and 12 h post-injection for quantitative mass analysis. While plasma BF-04 levels were below the lower limit of quantification (LLOQ; 3 ng/mL) starting at 3 h post-injection, clear retention was detected in tumor tissues, with measured concentrations of 39, 21.9, and 13 ng/mL at 3, 6, and 12 h, respectively. These results demonstrate the tumor-selective accumulation of free-form BF-04 over time (Fig. 6D and Supporting Information Table S10).

To assess clinical applicability, PDO xenograft models were used to evaluate whether BF-04 could trace tumors in a clinically relevant model. Following IV administration of BF-04, mice were sacrificed 3 h post-injection, and the concentrations of BF-04 in tumor tissue and plasma were quantified by mass spectrometry analysis. Consistent with the findings in the HeLa xenograft model, BF-04 exhibited markedly higher concentrations in tumor tissues (i.e., 46.2 ± 56.5 ng/mL, 11.4 ± 3.9 ng/mL, and 14.6 ± 9.8 ng/mL in #4, #8, and #14, respectively) compared to plasma (i.e., 3.5 ± 3.6 ng/mL, 1.2 ± 0.9 ng/mL, and 1.7 ± 1.7 ng/mL in #4, #8, and #14, respectively) at 3 h post-dose in all three PDO xenograft models (Supporting Information Table S11). These results demonstrate that free (unconjugated) BF-04 exhibits tumor-selective accumulation in preclinical models and suggest its potential for clinical application as a tumor-targeting imaging tracer.

Discussion

In this study, we identified BF-04 as a novel benzofuran-based microtubule-destabilizing agent with dual functionality, serving as both an anticancer therapeutic and a tumor-targeting tracer. BF-04 demonstrated submicromolar cytotoxicity across diverse cancer cell lines and PDOs, resulting in significant inhibition of tumor growth in HeLa xenograft models.

By conjugating BF-04 with Cy5 or 89Zr, we achieved high contrast optical and PET imaging with prolonged tumor uptake. Importantly, free-form BF-04 also exhibited selective tumor accumulation, confirming that tumor-targeting capability depends on the scaffold rather than on the attached imaging label.

The biodistribution of BF-04 varies by label: Cy5 conjugates undergo renal clearance, whereas 89Zr conjugates follow hepatobiliary clearance pathways. Despite these differences, the compound consistently exhibited persistent and selective tumor accumulation across all formats, supporting its potential as a flexible theranostic platform for diverse imaging and treatment strategies in oncology.

Recent studies using 89Zr-labeled docetaxel-encapsulated nanoparticles have demonstrated the potential of nanomedicine32. These nanoparticle-based approaches represent a significant advancement, combining therapeutic action with visualization of delivery kinetics within the tumor microenvironment and supporting the precision medicine paradigm. While the use of nanoparticles offers distinct advantages, such as protecting payloads and facilitating controlled release, their reliance on passive enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effects, as well as the need for extended systemic circulation to achieve tumor accumulation, can pose practical limitations, particularly in the context of heterogeneous tumor vascularity and stromal architecture33. In contrast, 89Zr-labeled BF-04 enables direct tumor targeting and immediate visualization of intratumoral drug distribution, achieving a progressively increasing tumor-to-blood ratio without requiring an EPR-based delivery mechanism. Notably, BF-04 bypasses multidrug resistance, thereby enabling both imaging and treatment of resistant tumors. This property expands the utility of this agent as a microtubule-targeting agent capable of overcoming a critical barrier in contemporary cancer therapy, while also serving as a tracer to identify MDR tumors and monitor treatment response. By integrating microtubule-targeted therapy with real-time, label-integrated imaging into a single scaffold, BF-04 offers a next-generation theragnostic approach. This direct targeting strategy complements the advances made in nanomedicine while offering an alternative platform for rapid, precise, and resistance-overcoming drug delivery in precision oncology.

Although preclinical pharmacokinetics and imaging results with BF-04 are promising, translating them to clinical settings may be challenging due to interpatient variability in tumor perfusion, microenvironmental factors, and drug resistance mechanisms, all of which require further evaluation. Our use of PDOs represents an initial step toward exploring clinical relevance; however, these models only partially reflect patient heterogeneity, and further validation in expanded clinical trials will be necessary.

Additionally, hepatobiliary clearance of the 89Zr-DFO-BF-04 may limit its sensitivity in detecting early liver cancer. Future work should investigate alternative radioisotopes in combination with appropriate chelators, such as 64Cu- or 68Ga-chelator conjugates, to modulate biodistribution and potentially expand the applicability of BF-04 to liver and abdominal cancers34,35.

In summary, BF-04 extends the role of microtubule-destabilizing agents beyond traditional therapeutic use, establishing a versatile theragnostic platform capable of real-time, image-guided dosing and precision microtubule-targeted therapy. Flexible labeling strategies and effective bypass of MDR mechanisms make BF-04 hold substantial promise for improving outcomes across diverse cancer types within precision oncology.
