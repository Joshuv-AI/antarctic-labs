1 Introduction
U.S. Forest Service (USFS) wildfire base camps (also referred to as incident command posts, or
ICPs) use portable power for electrical needs, including heating, cooling, powering devices,
cooking, heating water for showering, and more. The “main street” of ICPs typically includes
yurts and/or trailers, from which logistics staff work during the incident. These yurts and trailers
include heating and cooling systems, computer equipment, lighting, and other plug loads. They
are conventionally powered by portable diesel generators. Hybrid portable power systems
consisting of a combination of solar photovoltaics (PV), battery energy storage systems (BESS),
and/or backup diesel generators have been used in recent years and were piloted by the National
Technology and Development Program (NTDP) on incidents in Fall 2024 as part of NTDP’s
Portable Power Project.

The Incident Energy Systems Model was developed as part of this NTDP project as a tool for
USFS contracting officer engineers and NTDP engineers to analyze hybrid portable power
systems for yurts and/or trailers in base camps. In addition, a separate cost-benefit analysis was
conducted estimating fuel cost savings of hybrid portable power systems for command trailers,
resulting in a cost-benefit analysis Excel tool and associated report, “Hybrid Powered Command
Trailers Cost-Benefit Analysis Tool: User Guide and Examples” (Powell 2025). Although the
Excel tools are not currently publicly available, the insights gained from their development and
initial use, as described in these reports, could be applied to other scenarios involving portable
power systems.

1.1 Model Overview and Intended Use
The Incident Energy Systems Model (referred to as “the model”) is an Excel spreadsheet tool
that estimates (1) the electric power demands (loads) of yurts and/or trailers at a specific incident
over a “typical” (day of the median month over the past several years) 24-hour period and (2) the
power output of portable power systems serving those loads. The user inputs the types of yurts
and/or trailers (referred to as “areas”), the heating, ventilating, air-conditioning (HVAC) units
serving those areas, and the estimated non-HVAC plug loads as high, medium, or low. The user
also inputs details on the portable power systems, including type, and the areas served by those
systems.

Portable power systems can be hybrid and contain solar photovoltaics (PV), BESS, and/or fossil
fuel, including diesel generators. 1 The model outputs an estimated hourly profile of the load and
power output of the portable power systems serving the load. The intent of the tool is for the user
to get an idea of the expected loads and where the power systems can and cannot meet the loads.
It is important to note that actual power loads and outputs will vary based on real-world weather
conditions, equipment powered, system setup, and other factors. The model shows results for

1.2 User Guide
The “Instructions” sheet within the model Excel workbook describes how to use the tool and
what each subsequent sheet contains. There are three yellow input sheets that contain cells the
user must input values for, and three green outputs sheets that contain results. The “Outputs—
TOTAL” sheet contains most of the information the user will want to analyze. Instructions for
how to fill out the input sheets and analyze the output sheets are included in later sections of this
report.

Throughout the model, cells are color coded to indicate whether they are inputs or calculations,
as shown in Figure 1. Orange indicates the user must select from a list of drop-down options.
Yellow indicates an open response input. Light red indicates a cell that has a default value but
can be overridden by the user. Dark grey indicates not applicable, and cells will autopopulate
with this color through conditional formatting when certain criteria are met. Finally, light green
indicates a calculation completed by the model. Green cells should not be modified and could be
locked in future versions of the tool to avoid accidental changes.

 Figure 1. Color-coded key for the Incident Energy Systems Model

1.2.1 Inputs
There are three input sheets that must be filled out by the user, referring to areas and loads,
weather and irradiance, and portable power systems.

1.2.1.1 “Inputs—Areas & Loads” Sheet
The user will fill out the orange, yellow, and light red input cells with information about each
powered area (yurt or trailer) of interest and HVAC units serving those spaces. Each column is
one area. If the user is unsure what size of yurt to select (e.g., type 1, 2, etc.), there are examples
of different sizes of yurts in the “Yurt Type Examples” sheet.

All values must be entered for the units specified. Note that 1 ton = 12,000 British thermal units
per hour (Btu/hr). For example, if the nameplate cooling capacity is 3.5 tons, the user should
report it as 3.5 * 12,000 = 42,000 Btu/hr. For plug loads, the user should select the general
demand of non-HVAC plug loads as either low (mostly lighting only), medium (e.g., laptops,
printers, or lights), or high (e.g., several coffee pots or space heaters). This selection factors into
the electricity demand assumptions.

 2

Users do not need to enter anything below the solid black line in row 44 in this sheet, unless they
are interested in modifying the sensitivity analysis.

1.2.1.2 “Inputs—Portable Power Systems” Sheet
Each column on the “Inputs—Portable Power Systems” sheet is for one portable power system,
which can include solar PV, a BESS, and/or a fossil fuel generator. Examples of one system
include (1) solar PV + BESS, (2) solar PV + BESS + backup diesel generator, or (3) BESS only.
User should enter “0” for the system capacity or size for PV, BESS, or generator if those are not
components of the portable power system. In addition, in the orange cells, the user must indicate
which areas are powered by each system. Each area is assumed to be powered by only one
system (i.e., a single yurt or trailer cannot be powered by multiple portable power systems,
although one power system can contain multiple components). However, one system can power
multiple areas. The user indicates which areas are powered by selecting “Yes” from the drop-
down menu; otherwise, the user leaves it blank. This step is crucial to establishing the
load/demand profiles.

1.2.1.3 "Inputs—Weather & Irradiance" Sheet
The user must enter the date to be modeled, nearest city, latitude and longitude, time zone, and
weather conditions into column B, rows 11–16. The user must then enter the hourly outdoor air
temperature into column B, beginning in row 21, either from weather forecasts for an ICP or
from the National Solar Radiation Database (NSRDB) for a “typical meteorological year”
(TMY) day. A TMY day in the NSRDB includes weather data from an actual historical day in
the median (“judged most typical by the TMY algorithm,” Wilcox and Marion 2008) month
between 1998 and 2023 (National Renewable Energy Laboratory n.d.). The user must also enter
the indoor temperature set point schedule for the areas into column C (e.g., 68°F from 5 a.m. to 9
p.m. and none from 10 p.m. to 4 a.m.). Only one set point schedule can be set for all areas
evaluated by the model.

In addition, the user must download the direct horizontal, direct normal, and global horizontal
solar irradiance and solar zenith angle data for the location of interest from the NSRDB for a
TMY. The analysis will be performed for one user-selected day of the year, referred to as the
“day of interest.” From these data, the user should find the specific day and month for the day of
interest, and copy and paste the 24-hour data into the model in columns D through G.

The current version of the tool does not incorporate cloud cover data. As a simplified method to
address cloud cover, the user can input how weather conditions are expected to deviate from the
TMY day—either no change, more sun/clearer, or more clouds/rain/smoke. However, the TMY
day may or may not represent average conditions for that day. More important, the user can run
the model with different days of interest within a few weeks of their actual day of interest and
compare the results.

The methodology for accounting for cloud cover could be improved in future versions of the
tool. For example, the “clearsky” direct, diffuse, and global horizontal irradiance from NSRDB
could be compared to the actual irradiance. In addition, the “total sky cover” and “opaque sky
cover” from TMY datasets (Wilcox and Marion 2008) could be brought into the model.

1.2.2 Outputs
There are also three output sheets, which focus on electrical loads, portable power system power
output, and total results.

1.2.2.1 "Outputs—Loads" Sheet
This sheet is view-only and is optional to view. It shows hourly HVAC + plug load demands for
each area, based on the heating/cooling requirements, modeled heat transfer of that specific
trailer or yurt, and typical plug loads for other equipment. It may be useful for a user to view if
the user is interested in the composition of loads from HVAC versus plug loads, amount of heat
transfer from incoming radiation versus conductive heat transfer, or whether heating or cooling is
occurring on an hourly basis for each system.

1.2.2.2 "Outputs—Power" Sheet
This sheet is view-only and is optional to view. It has estimated hourly power production for
each portable power system and power demands for the areas those systems are serving. It may
be helpful to a user to view for information about the solar power production calculations, timing
of solar curtailment, or the battery or generator hourly outputs for each system.

1.2.2.3 “Outputs—TOTAL” Sheet
This sheet is view-only and pulls in information from the “Loads and Power” output sheets and
has all the information most users will want to analyze, including the following in both tabular
and visual forms:

 • Estimated daily load (kilowatt-hours, kWh) and peak load (kilowatts, kW) of powered
 areas over the modeled day (columns E and F)
 • Expected daily energy output (kWh) by solar, battery, and fossil fuel generator
 components of portable power systems over the modeled day (columns G, I, and J)
 • Estimated diesel generator fuel use in a baseline case in which only diesel generators are
 used over the modeled day, and associated fuel cost savings (columns M–Q)
 • High-level checks (True/False output), including whether the inverter capacity is greater
 than the peak load, whether the portable power system is big enough to meet the load of
 the assigned areas, whether the heating and cooling loads can be met by the inputted
 HVAC system, and whether the fossil fuel generator runs less than 5 hours 2 on the
 modeled day (columns R–X).
See the “Instructions” sheet of the model and notes in the individual sheets for more information
on how to use the model.

2 Methodology
The methodology for key components of the Incident Energy Systems Model is outlined in this
section. These include the load, portable power systems, solar power production, and outputs
compared to a baseline scenario.

2.1 Load
The power demands for each area comprise (1) heating and cooling loads from HVAC
equipment and (2) equipment plug loads.

2.1.1 Heating and Cooling Loads
Heating and cooling demands (Q) for the yurts or trailers are estimated by calculating the
approximate heat transfer using the following equations. Conductive heat transfer, incoming
radiation from the sun, and heat emitted by occupants were considered. We did not include
convective heat transfer (i.e., the impacts of wind and air flow), outgoing radiation (radiation
emitted by the yurt or trailer), and the impact of plug loads on heat gain in this version of the
model, but these could be incorporated in future versions. The conductive heating and cooling
demands are calculated by:

For both heating and cooling, the conductive heat transfer of the space is estimated through the
walls, roof, and floor. Conductive heat transfer uses assumed R-values for the walls, floor, and
roof based on the material type and the difference between the space set point and the outdoor air
temperature, using the following formula. An R-value is a rating that reflects how insulative a
material is and is measured in North America using units of sq ft * °F * hr/Btu. The higher the R-
value, the more insulative the material, resulting in less conductive heat transfer and reduced
heating and cooling requirements (increased energy efficiency). The difference in temperature
was assumed to be the same for the walls, roof, and floor. Future work could model the surface
temperature of the ground rather than the ambient air temperature, particularly because logistics
staff have reported that heating/cooling demands vary based on the surface a yurt is placed on
(asphalt, grass, gravel).

2.1.2 Equipment Plug Loads
Non-HVAC loads, otherwise known as equipment plug loads, were estimated by evaluating
meter data collected at actual fire base camps during the NTDP Portable Power Project in 2024.
The average hourly power for non-HVAC yurt plug loads over 24 hours was used as the
“medium” case. One standard deviation above and below were considered the “low” and “high”
cases. These profiles are included in the “Plug Loads” sheet.

2.2 Portable Power System
From a high level, the model uses the following logic for hybrid portable power systems:

 6

 1. Solar PV (if available) meets the demand first, followed by batteries (if available), and
 finally, a fossil fuel generator.
 2. The BESS is fully charged at the beginning of the 24-hour period and will not drain
 below 20% (the latter percentage can be changed by the user in the inputs).
 3. Any excess solar after meeting the hourly demand goes toward charging the battery and
 is curtailed if the battery is already 100% charged.
 4. A backup fossil fuel generator (if available) meets any remaining load the solar and
 BESS could not meet.
In practice, fossil fuel generators are also used to charge BESS. This can be beneficial to reduce
generator operation during designated quiet nighttime hours and to improve generator efficiency
through operating the generator at its most efficient loading, rather than at inefficient low
loading. Future version of the model could incorporate generators charging batteries into the
operational regime of hybrid portable power systems. In addition, the model currently only
verifies that the inverter is large enough through a True/False check on the “Outputs—TOTAL”
sheet and does not limit power passing through it. Future iterations of the model could
incorporate the inverter size into the power calculations.

2.3 Solar Power Production
Expected solar PV energy production is calculated directly from solar irradiance data. Note that
existing models such as PVWatts®3 output hourly expected solar photovoltaic production for
systems at specific locations from a few user inputs; however, certain inputs such as temperature
data cannot be changed in the PVWatts online version. Therefore, this tool estimates solar energy
production directly for greatest flexibility. Future versions of the model could integrate with
PVWatts for greater accuracy, if desired.

Hourly solar energy production is determined through calculating the solar irradiance for the
particular location striking the PV array, based on (1) user inputs for different irradiance
components and solar zenith angle from the NSRDB for the location and day of interest and (2)
PV array system specifications, including tilt angle, orientation, rated capacity, and panel
efficiency. All PV arrays are assumed to be fixed-tilt and unshaded. The formula used to
estimate hourly energy production follows:

2.4 Outputs—Comparing to Baseline
The “Outputs—TOTAL” sheet displays estimated loads (columns E–F) and energy production
(columns G–L) over the modeled day, in addition to comparing to a baseline case in which only
diesel generators are used to power the loads (columns M–Q). The model assumes that, in the
baseline case, a diesel generator 1.5 times the size of the peak load is used to power the loads,
rounded to the next-closest size of generator, which typically come in 25-kW increments. The
size was estimated based on 1.5 times the size of the peak load, as diesel generators are currently
typically oversized in fire base camps to account for unanticipated loads. Approximate fuel
consumption in gallons per hour for different-size generators is used based on specification
sheets and field/vendor experience. A flat $5.00/gallon fee is assumed. This methodology could
be improved in future work to more accurately approximate the size and quantity of diesel
generators used in the baseline (current status quo) case. In addition, basing generator sizing on
peak load may underestimate generator size and thus fuel consumption because larger generators
consume more fuel even when they serve smaller loads. A separate cost-benefit analysis was
conducted estimating fuel cost savings of hybrid portable power systems powering command
trailers, resulting in a cost-benefit analysis Excel tool and associated report, “Hybrid Powered
Command Trailers Cost-Benefit Analysis Tool: User Guide and Examples” (Powell 2025).

3.2 Hybrid Ground-Mount Solar Power System in Arizona
This ground-mount power system was piloted near Payson, Arizona, on the West Preacher Fire
in September 2024 and included a temporary ground-mounted solar PV array, a mobile trailer
with rooftop PV and BESS inside the trailer, and a backup diesel generator (Figure 8). The
hybrid system powered six type 1 command trailers: two efficient trailers with added insulation
and four standard trailers.

 BESS, and inverters (left), ground-mount solar PV array (middle), and backup diesel generator
 (right). Photos from NTDP.
The hybrid power system specifications were included on the “Inputs—Portable Power Systems”
sheet. Because the model accepts only one tilt angle for a single solar PV system, only the
ground-mounted solar PV array (19.2-kW rated capacity at 30-degree tilt) was included and not
the rooftop solar array (2 kW). The battery capacity was entered as 104 kWh with a 45-kW
inverter. Finally, the backup diesel generator was rated as 36 kW.

The temperature and irradiance data were entered into the “Inputs—Weather & Irradiance” sheet
for an example “typical” TMY day of September 20 at a latitude, longitude location near Payson,
Arizona. The indoor set point was set to 68°F from 5 a.m. to 10 p.m., and no set point outside of
that time.

The modeled results showed the hybrid system would be able to power the six trailers with
batteries and solar for most of the day, and then the diesel generator would be required to
supplement for 2 hours at the end of the day (Figure 9). The modeled results also show the

battery state of charge would reach the minimum allowable 20% at the end of the day and
therefore would need to be recharged via a diesel generator or supplemental solar-powered
system or swapped out with a fully charged BESS unit for the next day. However, the real-world
pilot results showed less power consumption, with 4 hours of diesel generator use and the BESS

consumption and could be validated further. The model could be overestimating power

or the non-HVAC plug loads being lower than modeled because of less equipment being plugged
in, among other potential factors.

4 Sensitivity Analysis
There are several key variables that impact the results. These include but are not limited to:

 • Load (power demands)
 • HVAC unit capacity and efficiency
 • Emissivity of the yurt or trailer (for calculating solar heat gain for cooling load)
 • Location (latitude, longitude)
 • Time of year
 • Solar irradiance and cloud cover
 • Outdoor temperature and space set point
 • R-values of yurt and trailer walls, roof, and floor
 • Solar PV capacity and tilt angle
 • BESS capacity
 • Scale of plug loads.
For the two examples outlined previously, modeled HVAC loads comprise between 50% and
80% of the total load for a trailer. In addition, HVAC unit power spikes during startup.
Therefore, HVAC loads are a significant demand for the portable power system.

In the current version of the model, the majority of the cooling load is from solar heat gain
(incoming radiation from the sun) as compared to conductive heat transfer. Therefore, solar
irradiance and yurt or trailer emissivity have a greater impact on cooling load than the
temperature difference. This methodology could be validated and further refined in future work.

A sensitivity analysis was conducted for a few inputs affecting the load profile for the two
example scenarios, including occupancy, SEER value, size of non-HVAC plug loads, and
material emissivity. It revealed that assumed occupancy had little impact on total daily load (less
than 1 kWh difference when occupancy increased or decreased by 50%) and SEER rating had a
greater impact on load for the solar command trailers scenario, which used high-efficiency heat
pumps, as shown in Figure 10. This demonstrates that a high-efficiency HVAC unit can have a
large impact on the load. The size of non-HVAC plug loads increased/decreased total daily
loads, but not drastically, because the available profiles (low, medium, high) vary by less than 1
kWh. Emissivity had a moderate impact on daily load, but this is primarily because the cooling
load is capped at the HVAC unit capacity and the maximum capacity was reached at higher
emissivity values.

5 Conclusion
The model is an approximation, and real-world systems will differ. Future work to improve the
model could include improvements to the heating and cooling demand methodology, solar
irradiance and cloud cover TMY methodology, and baseline number of diesel generators, as
described previously. In addition, the model could be expanded to analyze multiple days or
weeks, and the battery state of charge at the beginning of the day could be modified to be less
than 100%. However, the model can be used to compare general trends and order of magnitudes.
The shape of the 24-hour load profile, in particular, can inform further analysis and discussion
with potential vendors to ensure their systems are able to meet loads on incidents.
