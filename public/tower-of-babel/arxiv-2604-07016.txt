Predictive Representations for Skill Transfer in Reinforcement Learning
Ruben Vereecken, Luke Dickens, Alessandra Russo

Abstract
A key challenge in scaling up Reinforcement Learning is generalizing learned behaviour. Without the ability to carry forward acquired knowledge an agent is doomed to learn each task from scratch. In this paper we develop a new formalism for transfer by virtue of state abstraction. Based on task-independent, compact observations (outcomes) of the environment, we introduce Outcome-Predictive State Representations (OPSRs), agent-centered and task-independent abstractions that are made up of predictions of outcomes. We show formally and empirically that they have the potential for optimal but limited transfer, then overcome this trade-off by introducing OPSR-based skills , i.e. abstract actions (based on options) that can be reused between tasks as a result of state abstraction. In a series of empirical studies, we learn OPSR-based skills from demonstrations and show how they speed up learning considerably in entirely new and unseen tasks without any pre-processing. We believe that the framework introduced in this work is a promising step towards transfer in RL in general, and towards transfer through combining state and action abstraction specifically.



Research conducted: September 2018 – June 2021.

This manuscript represents the work as of June 2021.

Introduction

One core feature humans seem to share
is the tendency to think of a task
in terms of subtasks;
the act of navigating through a door
may require taking out a key,
putting it to a lock and
turning a handle
before moving through the doorway.
However, at no point while reading this would you think
of the minute motor actions involved.
There appear to be levels of abstraction,
higher-level more abstract actions such as

unlocking a door
,
described in terms of

taking out a key
,

putting it in the lock
etc,
while intermediate level actions such as

taking out a key

might decompose into
lower-level more primitive actions such as muscle contractions.
It is this phenomenon that gave rise to

hierarchical reinforcement learning
(HRL),
a popular area of present-day research
concerned with the decomposition of tasks
and learning of subtasks.

Not only are humans adept at decomposing tasks,
there is also the notion that we can reuse abstract behavior
across a variety of settings
where subtasks can be said to reoccur.
The act of unlocking a door is, intuitively,
mostly independent of which door is being unlocked
or the shape of the key,
and the differences can be thought of as variations on a common theme.
In this paper, we refer to abstract actions in this context as
skills
,
and suggest that this reusability of behavior,
i.e. this
transfer
of skills,
is the core advantage of action abstraction.
This notion it not new
and others have discussed action abstraction
as a basis for transfer in RL

(
50
;
15
)
,
yet there is one obstacle standing in the way of
skill transfer:

a behavior learnt in one particular task’s state space
can not readily be used in a different one.
There is a fundamental need for
state abstraction
.
The skill of opening a door is independent
of the color of the door or the shape of the key;
it acts on the abstract concepts
key
and
door

and ignores specifics.
In a recent opinion piece,

51

advocates for
joint state and action abstraction

(
51
)
.
In this paper
we show
that state and action abstraction
naturally go hand in hand.

This paper presents a new formalism
for thinking about how to transfer
learned behavior from one MDP to another
(Section
2
).
This formalizes the concept of

state abstraction

and
both
describes how to transfer a policy
between MDPs
by means of the
transfer cover
,
i.e. the overlap between state abstractions,
and the impact on performance.
Based on

outcomes

(Section
3.1
),
environmental features
that are reward-relevant
and task-agnostic,
we introduce

outcome equivalent state abstractions

which can be constructed from

predictions of outcomes

(Section
3.2
).
We define

plannable
MDPs as MDPs where
reactive, state-dependent policies,
can not outperform
non-reactive
plans
,
and prove that outcome equivalent state abstractions
allow optimal transfer between plannable MDPs
(Section
3.3
).

To overcome the limiting factor of transfer cover,
we introduce the more practical

Outcome-Predictive State Representations

(OPSR)
and integrate these ideas with action abstraction
in the form of
skills

(Section
4
).
We show how these skills can be learned in
a fully automated way
such that combined,
they achieve full transfer between tasks
by virtue
of the OPSR state abstraction.
In a collection of empirical studies,
we both learn these skills
from demonstrations
and show that they speed up learning considerably
in entirely new and unseen tasks
with zero pre-processing
(Section
5
).
Section
6

describes the work on state abstractions and

We conclude that the framework introduced in this work

7

General Framework

This section
builds the theoretical framework
of transfer based on state abstraction
that we use as a foundation
to build and assess a new
type of state abstraction
in Section
3
.
We develop formalisms for comparing state abstractions,

2.1
Markov Decision Processes and Problems

Reinforcement Learning is concerned with
sequential decision-making
in an environment
which is perceived through states and rewards.
It is conventional
to describe a Reinforcement Learning task
as a Markov Decision Process.

Definition 2.1

(Markov Decision Process)
.

A Markov Decision Process (MDP)
is defined as a 4-tuple

{M}\mathrel{\doteq}\langle\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},p,r\rangle
,
where
\mathcal{S}
is the discrete set of states
and
\mathcal{A}
the set of actions.
The MDP dynamics are described by the two functions

p
and
r
:

•

We write
the probability to transition between states
s,s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}

following action
a\in\mathcal{A}

as

p(s^{\prime}|s,a)\mathrel{\doteq}\Pr(S_{t+1}=s^{\prime}|S_{t}=s,A_{t}=a).

This function is called the
transition dynamics
of the MDP.

•

We write the expected reward
for a transition
(s,a,s^{\prime})\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{S}

as

r(s,a,s^{\prime})\mathrel{\doteq}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[R_{t+1}\mid S_{t}=s,A_{t}=a,S_{t+1}=s^{\prime}}}\right].

We will also rely on
the notion of expected reward for an action
in a given state:

r(s,a)\mathrel{\doteq}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[R_{t+1}\mid S_{t}=s,A_{t}=a}}\right].

Both functions can be referred to as the
reward dynamics
of the MDP
and which one is actually in use
can be inferred from the context.

The goal in Reinforcement Learning is then
to find an optimal behaviour in an MDP.
A behavior is represented by a policy

\pi(a|s)=\Pr(A_{t}=a|S_{t}=s)
.
Optimality is defined in terms of

expected long-term accumulated reward
.
To properly define this,
we need to instantiate a
Markov Decision Problem.
A Markov Decision Problem
is simply a Markov Decision Process
extended with a

discount factor

\gamma
,
so that we can define
the long-term accumulated reward
as a discounted sum of rewards.

G_{t}\mathrel{\doteq}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\gamma^{k-1}R_{t+k}.

(1)

We can now define the

value
of a policy for a given state
as the expected accumulated reward
from that state.

v_{\pi}(s)\mathrel{\doteq}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[G_{t}\mid S_{t}=s}}\right]

(2)

Where necessary
to avoid ambiguity,
we include the MDP
{M}

in the value function and instead write

v_{\pi}(s;{M})
.
The task in Reinforcement Learning now consists of
finding a policy
\pi

that maximizes the quantity
(
2
)
for some state
s
,
which is usually a fixed start state.

One last note on notation.
We sometimes rely on partially applied functions,
i.e.
functions where some of the parameters are given and some are left out.
For example,

\pi(\cdot|s)

is the policy for state
s
.
This allows us to say
for example
that a policy
has the same distribution over actions
for two different states
s
and
s^{\prime}
,
denoted
\pi(\cdot|s)=\pi(\cdot|s^{\prime})
.
We often rely on comparing functions
in this fashion.
For another example,

v_{\pi}(\cdot;{M})\geq v_{\pi^{\prime}}(\cdot;{M})

denotes that the value function
of
\pi

is greater or equal than that of
\pi^{\prime}

for all states in its domain,
that being some state set
\mathcal{S}
.
If the MDP
{M}
is clear from context,
this can again be simplified to

v_{\pi}\geq v_{\pi^{\prime}}
.
Such comparisons are only defined when
the functions are defined over the same domain.

2.2
Representation and Abstraction

2.2.1
State representation

Representations
– and in particular, state representations –
are inextricably linked with
problem descriptions in contemporary literature.
For our purposes,
it is important to define explicitly what a representation is
in order to critically compare different representation approaches
and to develop our own.
A representation

stands in

for the real thing.
Put another way,
it is a proxy for the raw, real-world thing that it represents.
It follows that a
state
representation
is a description for states,
standing in for the underlying
environment’s configurations.
Whether manually defined
or somehow learned,
state representations
help enumerate large or continuous state sets.
Not only that,
state representations
can also provide a common language
to describe tasks that we might think of as related.
For example,
we can represent
the states of all Gridworlds
as coordinate tuples
(see Example
2.1
).

Definition 2.2

(state representation)
.

A
state representation

\rho

is a function
mapping from a set of

raw states

\mathcal{X}

to a state representation set
\mathcal{S}_{\rho}

\rho:\mathcal{X}\mapsto{\mathcal{S}_{\rho}}

We term

{\mathcal{S}_{\rho}}

the
state set
associated with
\rho
.
Given a raw state

x\in\mathcal{X}
,
we term
s\mathrel{\doteq}\rho(x)

the representation of
x
,
overloading the meaning of the term
representation

to mean both the function and a raw state’s image.
Where the relation with the representation
\rho
is clear,
we drop the subscript from

\mathcal{S}_{\rho}

and simply write
\mathcal{S}
.

Since we cannot access
the raw states
\mathcal{X}

of the true environment being modelled,
state representations are always used
when defining a MDP,
albeit usually implicitly.
It is also possible
to define further state representations,
possibly based on the existing ones.

It has become common to
make use of
the notion of structure of a state set,
for example
by employing a similar policy
in states that
look similar
.
To facilitate this,

state spaces

can be used in place of
state sets.
A state space is simply
a state set with added structure
(such as a metric).
The type of space
is dependent on the problem at hand
and is often assumed implicitly.
Metric spaces are common
as they employ a distance metric
which can be used to measure the distance between states.
For example,
if raw Gridworld states
were reprsented by coordinate tuples,
neighboring states would be closer
than non-neighboring states
under the Euclidean metric on the coordinate space.

Definition 2.3

(state space)
.

Given a state representation

\rho:\mathcal{X}\mapsto{\mathcal{S}}
,
if
\mathcal{S}

is a
space
,
i.e. a set with some added structure,
then
we term it the
state space
associated with
\rho

and denote it with a non-caligraphic

S
.

A state representation
can be as simple
as a scheme for enumerating states
such that each has a unique identifier
which one can use to point at
(to represent)
that state.
We refer to
a rudimentary representation
that uniquely identifies each state
as a
ground representation
.

Definition 2.4

(ground representation)
.

A state representation

\rho_{0}:\mathcal{X}\mapsto{\mathcal{S}_{\rho_{0}}}

is a

ground representation

if and only if

\rho_{0}
is injective,
that is,
raw states that are distinct
have distinct representations.

Example 2.1
.

Take the simple Gridworld in
Figure
1
.
There are 5 states
corresponding to the location of the agent.
In order to refer to these states
we can employ a representation.
A simple enumeration is feasible:
we can number the states
in ascending order
according to nearness to the goal location,
with
s_{1}
denoting the state depicted in
Figure
1

(with the agent located in the upper left cell)
and
s_{5}
denoting the state where the agent

This is a valid ground representation,

A simple Gridworld labelled to construct a representation.

We have already established that multiple

Since the agent’s locations are conveniently arranged in a grid,
we could label each state with
a
(x,y)

Employing the numbering

we can identify the following 5 states:

(1,1),(1,2),(2,2),(3,2),(3,1)

–

We can summarise its signature as

\rho_{0}:\mathcal{S}\mapsto\{1,2,3\}\times\{1,2\}
.
Some caution should be exercised with this description though:
the codomain of
\rho_{0}
does not span the entire image.
That is, the state

Likewise we could have written

Using the Euclidean metric for example,

(1,1)
is closer to

(1,3)

(2,3)

It is far from obvious however
that states that are close together

(2,3)

Many representations with distinct images
are ground representations,
yet they all fulfill the same purpose of uniquely labelling states.
For instance,
we could have easily devised a different numbering scheme
in the above example.

Isomorphism

neatly captures this notion of similarity
and allows us to focus only on meaningful differences between representations.

Definition 2.5

(isomorphic ground state representations)
.

Given two ground state representations

\rho^{\alpha}:\mathcal{X}\mapsto\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}

and

\rho^{\beta}:\mathcal{X}\mapsto\mathcal{S}_{\beta}
,
if there exists a bijection
between

\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}

and

\mathcal{S}_{\beta}

(i.e. an isomorphism),
then we say that

\rho_{\alpha}

and

\rho_{\beta}

are

isomorphic

or

equal up to an isomorphism
:

\rho^{\alpha}\mathrel{\cong}\rho^{\beta}

It follows that every bijective ground representation
is isomorphic with the
identity state representation.

2.2.2
State Abstraction

Abstractions themselves are representations of a sort.
Yet
abstractions are more than that:
they aim to focus on just the

relevant information

and ignore the irrelevant.
In the context of transfer,
good abstractions should focus
on relevant commonalities between tasks.
We see abstractions throughout this paper
as a molding of distinctions,
masking those that are less relevant.
To remove all that distinguishes two things
is to abstract away from them
and equate them as a result.

A state abstraction is a special form of state representation
which is potentially non-injective:
it supports
the mapping of distinct raw states onto the same abstract state.

Definition 2.6

(state abstraction)
.

Given an MDP

{M}=\langle\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},p,r\rangle
,
a

state abstraction

\phi:~\mathcal{S}~\mapsto~\Phi

maps from a state representation set
\mathcal{S}

to an abstract state set
\Phi
.
We term

\phi(s)\in\Phi

the
abstract state

corresponding to the

ground state

s\in\mathcal{S}
.
The inverse abstraction

\phi^{-1}:\Phi\mapsto\mathbb{P}{(\mathcal{S})}

maps an abstract state

\phi(s)\in\Phi

onto the set of corresponding ground states

\set{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}{\phi(s^{\prime})=\phi(s)}

and where

\mathbb{P}(X)
denotes the powerset of
X
.

Since there are many possible state abstractions for any given state set,
it is useful to again rely on the notion of isomorphism
between state abstractions to describe state abstractions
that are the same in every relevant aspect.

Definition 2.7

(isomorphic state abstractions)
.

Let

{\phi_{\alpha}}:\mathcal{S}\mapsto{\Phi_{\alpha}}

and

{\phi_{\beta}}:\mathcal{S}\mapsto{\Phi_{\beta}}

be two state abstractions.
We say that

{\phi_{\alpha}}

and

{\phi_{\beta}}

are

isomorphic

or

equal up to an isomorphism
,
denoted

{\phi_{\alpha}}\mathrel{\cong}{\phi_{\beta}}
,
if all sets of ground states
that correspond to the abstract states
of
{\phi_{\alpha}}

are the same for
{\phi_{\beta}}
.
That is,
if

\Set{{\phi_{\alpha}^{-1}}(\bar{\phi}_{\alpha})}{\bar{\phi}_{\alpha}\in{\Phi_{\alpha}}}=\Set{{\phi_{\beta}^{-1}}(\bar{\phi}_{\beta})}{\bar{\phi}_{\beta}\in{\Phi_{\beta}}}
.

2.2.3
Loss of the Markov Property.

It is tempting to see a state abstraction
as inducing a new decision process
based on the abstract state set.
This can however be far from straightforward.
It is possible
that this
abstract decision process

no longer maintains the Markov property.

Example 2.2
.

(a)

(b)

Figure 2
:
On the left hand side is depicted
an MDP
with 5 states and the action set
\{a,b\}
.
Arcs between states denote
deterministic transitions following the labelled action,
receiving the annotated reward
r
.
An abstraction is shown as grouping together states.
The resulting abstract states are depicted on the right.

Figure
2(a)

depicts an MDP to which a state abstraction
is applied.
This simple state abstraction
\phi

2a
and
2b
together:

\phi(2a)=\phi(2b)=\bar{\phi}_{2}
.
The other states remain ungrouped under the abstraction,
i.e.,
each is mapped to a different abstract state.
Figure
2(b)
shows the resulting abstract
states,
along with transitions labelled with actions
in an attempt at
fidelity to the original MDP.
There is a problem describing the
resulting
abstract

MDP however:
it is impossible to express
the transition probabilities from
\bar{\phi}_{2}

unambiguously
in such a way that it accurately reflects the transition
probabilities of the ground MDP.
The result of an action
from
\bar{\phi}_{2}

depends on the action
that was taken before (at
\bar{\phi}_{1}
).
This is because
\bar{\phi}_{2}

is the abstraction of the ground states

2a
and
2b
.
It is
no longer possible to distinguish between

2a
and
2b
,
yet they lead into different states:

3
and
4
respectively,
with abstract states

\bar{\phi}_{3}
and
\bar{\phi}_{4}
respectively.
As a result,
it is not clear whether a certain action
from
\bar{\phi}_{2}
will lead to

\bar{\phi}_{3}
or
\bar{\phi}_{4}
.
Not without knowing what preceded.
In short, the Markov property is violated.

Consider for example

p(\bar{\phi}_{3}\mid\bar{\phi}_{2},a)

which has two candidates.
The resulting state of action
a

in
\bar{\phi}_{2}

is either
\bar{\phi}_{3}

or
\bar{\phi}_{4}
(in which case it is not
\bar{\phi}_{3}
).
The candidates depend on different histories over actions:

\displaystyle\Pr(\bar{\Phi}_{t+1}=\bar{\phi}_{3}\mid\bar{\Phi}_{t}=\bar{\phi}_{2},A_{t}=a,\bar{\Phi}_{t-1}=\bar{\phi}_{1},A_{t-1}=a)=1

\displaystyle\neq\;

\displaystyle\Pr(\bar{\Phi}_{t+1}=\bar{\phi}_{3}\mid\bar{\Phi}_{t}=\bar{\phi}_{2},A_{t}=a,\bar{\Phi}_{t-1}=\bar{\phi}_{1},A_{t-1}=b)=0

That is,
in order to know
the outcome of action

from

\bar{\phi}_{2}
,
it is necessary to know
which action
was taken from state
\bar{\phi}_{1}
.
Similarly,
it has also become impossible to describe the expected reward function

r(\bar{\phi}_{2},a)
:

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[R_{t+1}\mid\bar{\Phi}_{t}=\bar{\phi}_{2},A_{t}=a,\bar{\Phi}_{t-1}=\bar{\phi}_{1},A_{t-1}=a}}\right]=0

\displaystyle\neq\;

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[R_{t+1}\mid\bar{\Phi}_{t}=\bar{\phi}_{2},A_{t}=a,\bar{\Phi}_{t-1}=\bar{\phi}_{1},A_{t-1}=b}}\right]=1

Both transition and reward dynamics
have become dependent on the history,
making it impossible to construct
an abstract Markov Decision Process
from this state abstraction
that is faithful to the original MDP.
∎

As the example shows,
a state abstraction does not necessarily lead to an obvious choice
for an abstract MDP.
Instead, it
results in a range of possible abstract MDPs,
each a candidate that we can learn a policy for.
We will delve into ways of assessing different state abstractions
and abstract MDPs after we define some basic machinery.

Definition 2.8

(abstract MDP)
.

Given an MDP

{M}=\langle\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},p,r\rangle

and state abstraction

\phi:\mathcal{S}\mapsto\Phi
,
we say that

{M}_{\phi}=\langle\Phi,\mathcal{A},p_{\phi},r_{\phi}\rangle

is an

abstract MDP

for
{M}
and
\phi

if and only if
there exists a function

w_{\phi}:\mathcal{S}\mapsto[0,1]

such that for all

\bar{\phi},\bar{\phi}^{\prime}\in\Phi
,
it holds that

\sum_{s\in\bar{\phi}}w_{\phi}(s)=1
,
and

\displaystyle p_{\phi}(\bar{\phi}^{\prime}|\bar{\phi},a)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\in{\phi^{-1}}(\bar{\phi})}\sum_{s^{\prime}\in{\phi^{-1}}(\bar{\phi}^{\prime})}w_{\phi}(s)p(s^{\prime}|s,a)

(3)

\displaystyle r_{\phi}(\bar{\phi},a)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\in{\phi^{-1}}(\bar{\phi})}w_{\phi}(s)r(s,a)

(4)

By reuse of notation we may write the set of abstract MDPs
for
{M}
and
\phi

as
\phi({M})
, such that

{M}_{\phi}\in\phi({M})

and

\phi^{-1}({M}_{\phi})={M}
.

As a result of the freedom permitted by
w_{\phi}(s)
,
many abstract MDPs are possible
for a particular MDP and state abstraction.
The abstract transition and reward dynamics
are constructed as weighted averages over the
original transition and reward dynamics.
The weighting
w_{\phi}(s)

is the mechanism through which a new valid MDP
is created,
albeit only an approximation of the original.
The cost of state abstraction
is a loss in fidelity to the original MDP.
In this paper we will discuss particular classes
of state abstractions
that result in abstract MDPs
where the freedom permitted by
w_{\phi}

does not play a role and
that are particularly useful for
transfer.

2.2.4
Comparing Abstractions

State abstractions operating on the same state set
can be compared to one another.
The first state abstraction property that allows comparison
is
coarseness

(or conversely
fineness
),
where one state abstraction is finer than another
if it makes more distinctions between states.
The following definition of
coarseness

is adapted from
the work of

57

to take into account isomorphism.

Definition 2.9

(coarseness)
.

Let

\phi_{1}:\mathcal{S}\mapsto\Phi

and

\phi_{2}:\mathcal{S}\mapsto\Phi^{\prime}

be two state abstractions.
We say that

\phi_{1}
is
finer
than

\phi_{2}
,
or equivalently that

\phi_{2}
is
coarser
than

\phi_{1}
,
written
\phi_{1}\succeq\phi 2
,
if
all states that are equivalent under
\phi_{1}

are also equivalent under
\phi_{2}
.
Formally:

\phi_{1}\succeq\phi_{2}\quad\text{iff}\quad\forall s,s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}:\phi_{1}(s)=\phi_{1}(s^{\prime})\Rightarrow\phi_{2}(s)=\phi_{2}(s^{\prime})

We say
strictly finer
(
\succ
)
or

strictly coarser
(
\prec
)
if in addition

\phi_{1}
and
\phi_{2}

are not isomorphic.
We also refer to the

coarseness

of an abstraction
as its

expressivity
;
an abstraction that is

finer

is

more expressive
.

The

coarser
relation
imposes a partial ordering
over a set of state abstractions

\Theta_{M}

for an MDP
{M}
.
If there is no state abstraction

\phi^{\prime}\in\Theta_{M}

that is
finer
(coarser)
than
\phi\in\Theta_{M}
,
we say that
\phi
is a

maximally fine
(maximally coarse) state abstraction in
\Theta_{M}
.
If a state abstraction

\phi\in\Theta_{M}

is
finer
(coarser)
than all other state abstractions

\Theta_{M}
,
we say that

\phi

is

finest
(coarsest).
The finest abstraction
for
\Theta_{M}

is isomorphic with the identity state abstraction.
The coarsest abstraction
for
\Theta_{M}
,
on the other hand,
maps every state
onto a single abstract state,
equating all states and
making no distinctions.
It follows
that there is only one isomorphically distinct
finest and coarsest abstraction
for any MDP.

2.3
Transfer Through State Abstraction

The power of state abstractions
becomes apparent when multiple ground states
are mapped to the same abstract state.
When a behavior is learned for a single abstract state,
it can apply automatically for
all ground states for this abstract state.
It now becomes useful
to see a state abstraction
as inducing an equivalence relation,
where two states are equivalent
when they
share an abstraction.
An equivalence relation

E\subseteq\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{S}

is a binary relation
that is reflexive, symmetric and transitive.
If
a state pair

(s,s^{\prime})

is in
E
,
we say that

s
and
s^{\prime}
are equivalent.
We employ the shorthand

E(s,s^{\prime})

to denote

(s,s^{\prime})\in E
.
The view of
state abstractions as
inducing equivalence relations
is due to

39
,
the language and what follows is our own.

Definition 2.10

(abstraction-induced equivalence relation)
.

A state abstraction

\phi

induces an equivalence relation

E_{\phi}

over a state set.
If
E_{\phi}(s,s^{\prime})

we write
s\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\phi}}{{=}}s^{\prime}
.
For states
s,s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}

this relation is defined as

s\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\phi}}{{=}}s^{\prime}\iff\phi(s)=\phi(s^{\prime})

(5)

If
s\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\phi}}{{=}}s^{\prime}

we say that

s
is equivalent to
s^{\prime}
under the abstraction
\phi
.

An equivalence relation in turn
results in a partitioning of the state set.
States that
share the same abstraction
belong to the same

equivalence class
.
For an equivalence relation induced by
a state abstraction
\phi
,
we can denote the equivalence class
of a ground state
s

under
\phi

as

[s]_{\phi}\mathrel{\doteq}\Set{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}{s\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\phi}}{{=}}s^{\prime}}
.
For convenience of notation
we overload
\phi(s)
,
so far used to denote an abstract state,
to also denote the equivalence class of
s
under
\phi
,
i.e.

\phi(s)\mathrel{\doteq}[s]_{\phi}
.

An equivalence relation

E
on
\mathcal{S}

induces a
partition

P=\Set{[s_{1}],\dots,[s_{m}]}
.
For an abstraction-induced equivalence relation
we can write the partition as

\Phi=\Set{\bar{\phi}}{\bar{\phi}\mathrel{\doteq}\phi(s)\;\forall s\in\mathcal{S}}
.
In addition,
we have

\bigcup_{\bar{\phi}}=\mathcal{S}

and

\bigcap_{\bar{\phi}}=\varnothing
.
It follows that
if there is at least one
equivalence class with more than one member,
i.e. if two states share an abstraction,
then
the partition will be smaller than the original state set.

2.3.1
Single-Task Transfer.

The view of a state abstraction
as inducing an equivalence relation
between ground states
is useful
because it gives us a way to transfer behavior
learned for an abstract state
to all ground states
in the corresponding equivalence class.
Where before
behavior had to be learned for each ground state separately,
now we only need to learn a policy
for the abstract states.
Since the abstract MDP has fewer states to contend with,
it can be easier to learn than the ground MDP.

If we have an MDP

{M}

and a state abstraction
\phi

which induces the abstract MDP
{M}_{\phi}
,
then we can learn a policy

\pi_{\phi}:\mathcal{S}_{\phi}\mapsto\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{A})

for this abstract MDP.
To transfer
this
abstract policy

to the ground task,
we make use of
a process called

policy derivation
.

Definition 2.11

(derived policy)
.

Let

{M}=\langle\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},p,r\rangle

be an MDP,
let

\phi:\mathcal{S}\mapsto\Phi

a state abstraction,
and
let

{M}_{\phi}=\langle\Phi,\mathcal{A},p_{\phi},r_{\phi}\rangle

be
an abstract MDP constructed
from
{M}
and
\phi
.
Given an abstract policy

\pi_{\phi}:\Phi\mapsto\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{A})
,
we define
the
policy derived from

\pi_{\phi}
as

\delta:\mathcal{S}\mapsto\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{A})
s.t.

\forall s\in\mathcal{S},a\in\mathcal{A}
:

\delta(a\mid s)=\pi_{\phi}(a\mid\phi(s))

Note that we use the symbol
\delta

to denote a derived policy
rather than the customary
\pi

to suggest that it is derived
from an abstract policy.

Let

\Pi_{\phi}

be the set of all abstract policies

\pi_{\phi}:\Phi\mapsto\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{A})
.
We write the

set of derived policies

for state abstraction
\phi

and MDP
{M}

as

\Pi_{\delta}(\phi,{M})\mathrel{\doteq}\Set{\delta}{\delta(s,a)=\pi_{\phi}(\phi(s),a)\quad\forall\pi_{\phi}\in\Pi_{\phi}}

If
{M}
is clear from the context,
we also refer to
\Pi_{\delta}(\phi,{M})

as

the derived policies of
\phi
.

Policy derivation gives us a simple construction
to translate an abstract policy to a policy for a ground MDP.
It is interesting to investigate
what manner of ground policies can be derived
given a particular state abstraction,
even before considering any abstract MDPs.
As we shall see shortly,
state abstraction is restrictive.
Only the most trivial of state abstractions
allow access to the whole set
of ground policies by means of derivation.
This means that different state abstractions
will result in different sets of derived policies.
Some sets of derived policies
might not even include the optimal policy
because of restriction by the state abstraction.
We start with one straightforward result
that describes how state abstraction
limits possible derived policies.
After that we will consider
whether this limitation is harmful
to the end goal:
attaining good policies.

When a state abstraction
assigns two ground states to the same abstract state,
any abstract policy can no longer distinguish between those two states
as it only sees the combined abstract state.
As a result, any derived policy
is also unable to distinguish between these two states,
effectively decreasing
controllability

or
control granularity
.
Increased control is only possible
by distinguishing between states.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let

{M}=\langle\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},p,r\rangle

be an MDP
and let

\phi:\mathcal{S}\mapsto\Phi

be a state abstraction
that maps two states

s,t\in\mathcal{S}

to the same equivalence class
\bar{\phi}
.
Then all derived policies for
\phi

must have the same distributions over actions
for both
s
and
t
.
Formally,

\forall s,t\in\mathcal{S}:

\phi(s)=\phi(t)\;\Leftrightarrow\;\forall\delta\in\Pi_{\delta}(\phi,{M}):\delta(\cdot\mid s)=\delta(\cdot\mid t)

(6)

Proof
.

Since different state abstractions
result in different restrictions on possible derived policies,
the level of control or

control granularity

is a useful property to characterise
and compare between state abstractions.

Definition 2.12

(control granularity)
.

Let

{M}=\langle\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},p,r\rangle

be an MDP
and let

\phi:\mathcal{S}\mapsto\Phi

and

\phi^{\prime}:\mathcal{S}\mapsto\Phi^{\prime}

be two state abstractions.
We say
that
\phi

is

more control granular

than
\phi^{\prime}

if all derived policies contained in
\Pi_{\delta}(\phi^{\prime},{M})

are also contained in

\Pi_{\delta}(\phi^{\prime},{M})
.
Formally:

\Pi_{\delta}(\phi^{\prime},{M})\subseteq\Pi_{\delta}(\phi,{M})\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\forall\delta^{\prime}\in\Pi_{\delta}(\phi^{\prime},{M}):\exists\delta\in\Pi_{\delta}(\phi,{M}):\delta=\delta^{\prime}

If in addition
we have that

\Pi_{\delta}(\phi,{M})\neq\Pi_{\delta}(\phi^{\prime},{M})
,
we say that

\phi

is

strictly more control granular

than
\phi^{\prime}
.

State abstraction control granularity
sounds a lot like state abstraction fineness or coarseness.
It turns out both properties
are different sides of the same coin.
Coarser state abstractions give up more control;
finer state abstractions have more control,
are more control granular
.
The reason we introduced the secondary notion of control granularity
is that it is more amenable to the context of control
that we are faced with.

Proposition 2.2

(control granularity–coarseness trade-off)
.

Let

{M}=\langle\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},p,r\rangle

be an MDP
and let

\phi:\mathcal{S}\mapsto\Phi

and

\phi^{\prime}:\mathcal{S}\mapsto\Phi^{\prime}

be two state abstractions.
Then

\phi

is

finer

than
\phi

if and only if

\phi

is more control granular than
\phi^{\prime}
.
Conversely,
if there exists a derived policy for
\phi

that cannot be derived for
\phi^{\prime}

then
\phi

must be finer,
meaning
that there must be two states

s,t\in\mathcal{S}

that are mapped to different abstract states
by
\phi

but to a single abstract state
by
\phi^{\prime}
.

Formally:

\begin{gathered}\phi\succeq\phi^{\prime}\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\Pi_{\delta}(\phi,{M})\supseteq\Pi_{\delta}(\phi^{\prime},{M})\end{gathered}

(7)

For the proof see Appendix A on page
Proof
.

Potential for transfer
through state abstraction
and policy derivation
does not necessarily mean
that it is beneficial
–
the resulting derived policy
is not necessarily a good one.
For example,
a policy derived from an optimal
abstract policy
is not necessarily optimal in the ground MDP.
Now that we know that the set of derived policies

\Pi_{\delta}(\phi,{M})

is a subset of the set
of all ground policies,
it pays to look at the value functions
of those derived policies.
This will tell us
what solution qualities
are excluded by a state abstraction,
impossible to be attained
regardless of the learning algorithm
that might eventually be used,
constrained as it must be by
the control granularity permitted by the state abstraction.
What this will come down to eventually
is the question of how
a state abstraction’s
potential for transfer
and
the potential solution quality permitted by that state abstraction
are related.

Consider the value functions
associated with the derived policies

\Pi_{\delta}(\phi,\mathcal{A})
.
These value functions
constitute a partial order without the guaranteed existence of
a greatest element
as there can be multiple policies that are optimal for the set of derived policies.
Still, this set of value functions can be useful
as we can compare sets of value functions of derived policies
between state abstractions.
If one state abstraction allows a derived policy
that trumps all derived policies of another state abstraction,
then that state abstraction
is potentially better.

Definition 2.13

(transfer value)
.

Let

{M}=\langle\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},p,r,\gamma\rangle

be an MDP
and let

\phi:\mathcal{S}\mapsto\Phi

and

\phi^{\prime}:\mathcal{S}\mapsto\Phi^{\prime}

be two state abstractions.
We say that

\phi

has
greater transfer value for
{M}

than

\phi^{\prime}
,
denoted

v_{\phi}({M})\geq v_{\phi^{\prime}}({M})
,
if
there exists a derived policy for

\phi

with a value function greater than or equal
all derived policies’ value functions
for
\phi^{\prime}
.
Formally:

v_{\phi}({M})\geq v_{\phi^{\prime}}({M})\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\exists\delta\in\Pi_{\delta}(\phi,{M}):\forall\delta^{\prime}\in\Pi_{\delta}(\phi^{\prime},{M}):v_{\delta}(\cdot;{M})\geq v_{\delta^{\prime}}(\cdot;{M})

We say that

\phi
has
strictly greater transfer value

than
\phi^{\prime}
,
written

v_{\phi}({M})>v_{\phi}^{\prime}({M})
,
if there is a derived policy in
\Pi_{\delta}(\phi,{M})

that has a strictly greater value function
than any derived policy in
\Pi_{\delta}(\phi^{\prime},{M})
.

We should add that
comparing transfer values
can be conservative
for some applications
as the above definition
requires value dominance at all states.
In practice
there is often a set of start states for which
a higher value is far more important than for other states.
In those scenarios,
transfer values
could be compared for a subset of states
rather than for all states.
In this paper we limit ourselves to comparing
values for all states but limiting this comparison to a subset of states
is a straightforward extension.

The set of all possible ground policies
may contain multiple optimal policies
but they will all have the same greatest value function.
The same is not necessarily true for the set of derived policies.
Because policy derivation restricts the space of available policies,
the optimal policy might no longer be available
which will be reflected in the transfer value of
that state abstraction.
Instead of a single optimal derived policy,
it is possible to have
multiple derived policies
with maximal value,
all suboptimal,
all with value functions that are incomparable
(neither greater nor less).
Since transfer value is defined in terms of value functions,
it is also possible
for one state abstraction’s
transfer value
to not be comparable to that of another state abstraction.

In effect,
we now have two partial orderings over
state abstractions:
coarseness and control granularity on one side and transfer value on the other.
The two sides are of course related.
To describe how they relate,
it is instructive
to understand when one state abstraction
has
a higher transfer value than
another.
It is only possible for a state abstraction
to have a strictly higher transfer value than another
if it allows for more control where the other does not (and for that improved control to support a greater value at one or more states).
If two states are grouped together under an abstraction
that an optimal policy would distinguish between,
the value of the best available policy under this abstraction suffers
and this is reflected in the transfer value of this abstraction.
This leads to the following Theorem.

Theorem 2.3

(transfer value–control trade-off)
.

Let

\phi:\mathcal{S}\mapsto\Phi

and

\phi^{\prime}:\mathcal{S}\mapsto\Phi^{\prime}

be two state abstractions
such that

\phi

has

strictly higher transfer value than

\phi^{\prime}

for some MDP

{M}\mathrel{\doteq}\langle\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},p,r\rangle,\gamma
.
Then there must
exist two states

s,t\in\mathcal{S}

such that

\phi^{\prime}
does not distinguish between them
while
\phi
does,
and there exists a derived policy for

\phi

that takes advantage of this distinction
by controlling
s
and
t
differently
in a way that no derived policy for
\phi^{\prime}
can do.
By giving up representational capacity
and increasing potential for transfer,

\phi^{\prime}
sacrifices transfer value.
Formally:

\displaystyle v_{\phi}({M})>v_{\phi^{\prime}}({M})

\displaystyle\Downarrow

\displaystyle\exists s,t\in\mathcal{S}:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &1.\;\phi(s)\neq\phi(t)\\
&2.\;\phi^{\prime}(s)=\phi^{\prime}(t)\\
&3.\;\exists\delta\in\Pi_{\delta}(\phi,{M}):\delta(\cdot\mid s)\neq\delta(\cdot\mid t)\\
&4.\;\forall\delta^{\prime}\in\Pi_{\delta}(\phi^{\prime},{M}):\delta^{\prime}(\cdot\mid s)=\delta^{\prime}(\cdot\mid t)\end{aligned}

Note that
\phi
need not be

finer

than
\phi^{\prime}
,
so there can still exist two states

\bar{s},\bar{t}\in\mathcal{S}

where

\phi(\bar{s})=\phi(\bar{t})

and

\phi^{\prime}(\bar{s})\neq\phi^{\prime}(\bar{t})

(to contrast with the consequent above).
This result just means that there is at least one
pair of states where the opposite is true.

As a direct result of
Theorem
2.3

we also have the following corollary.
While it is weaker than the theorem
it does enjoy some brevity.

Corollary 2.4
.

Let

\phi:\mathcal{S}\mapsto\Phi

and

\phi^{\prime}:\mathcal{S}\mapsto\Phi^{\prime}

be two state abstractions
such that

\phi

has

strictly higher transfer value than

\phi^{\prime}

for some MDP
{M}
.
Then

\phi^{\prime}
can both be
no finer

than
\phi

and
no more control granular

than
\phi
.
Formally:

v_{\phi}({M})>v_{\phi^{\prime}}({M})\Longrightarrow\phi^{\prime}\not\succeq\phi\;\wedge\;\Pi_{\delta}(\phi,{M})\not\subseteq\Pi_{\delta}(\phi^{\prime},{M})

(8)

The following also holds.
If
\phi
is coarser
and less control granular than
\phi^{\prime}
,
it is impossible for

\phi

to have a higher transfer value than

\phi^{\prime}
.
Formally:

\phi^{\prime}\succeq\phi\;\wedge\;\Pi_{\delta}(\phi,{M})\subseteq\Pi_{\delta}(\phi^{\prime},{M})\Longrightarrow v_{\phi}({M})\not>v_{\phi^{\prime}}({M})

.

We have established a general trade-off
between transfer value on one hand
and coarseness or control granularity
on the other hand.
However,
it’s not as simple
as every coarsening resulting
in decreased transfer value and vice versa.
It is sometimes possible to coarsen a state abstraction
without sacrificing transfer value.
For example,
the identity state abstraction
has maximal transfer value,
yet there can be state abstractions
with the same maximal transfer value
yet that are coarser
that the identity state abstraction.
This presents an interesting opportunity,
where potential for transfer can be increased
with no drawback.

Definition 2.14

(minimal control state abstraction)
.

A state abstraction

\phi:\mathcal{S}\mapsto\Phi

is a

minimal control state abstraction

for an MDP

{M}=\langle\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},p,r,\gamma\rangle

if
\phi

has
maximal transfer value

and there exists
no state abstraction

coarser than
\phi

with higher transfer
value than
\phi
.

In other words,
there is no coarsening
a
minimal control state abstraction

without negatively impacting transfer value
because that would prevent the optimal policy from being derived.
Still,
there is the potential for
some
increased coarseness
compared to the identity state abstraction
where distinguishing between
states does not impact the solution quality.

2.3.2
Multi-Task Transfer.

Now that we have considered transfer within a single task,
a natural extension would be to consider two tasks

{M}_{\alpha}=\langle\mathcal{S}_{\alpha},\mathcal{A},p_{\alpha},r_{\alpha}\rangle

and

{M}_{\beta}=\langle\mathcal{S}_{\beta},\mathcal{A},p_{\beta},r_{\beta}\rangle

with a shared action set
\mathcal{A}
.
We can also consider two state abstractions

\phi_{\alpha}:\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}\mapsto\Phi_{\alpha}

and

\phi_{\beta}:\mathcal{S}_{\beta}\mapsto\Phi_{\beta}

such that

\Phi_{\alpha}\cup\Phi_{\beta}\subseteq\Phi
.
While the images of

\phi_{\alpha}

and

\phi_{\beta}
,
i.e.

\Phi_{\alpha}

and

\Phi_{\beta}
,
are not necessarily the same,
they might have a non-empty intersection.
Both state abstractions
define equivalence relations on their respective domains,
as discussed in the previous single-task setting.
On top of that
we can also consider
an equivalence relation
relating states from the original sets

\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}

and

\mathcal{S}_{\beta}

wherever they share an abstraction.
We term this equivalence relation

E_{\phi_{\alpha},\phi_{\beta}}

over

{\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}}\cup{\mathcal{S}_{\beta}}

the equivalence relation imposed by
{\phi_{\alpha}}
and
{\phi_{\beta}}

and,
if we take

\phi(s)
to be

\phi_{\alpha}(s)
if
s\in\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}

and
\phi_{\beta}(s)
otherwise,
define it
for states

s,s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}\cup\mathcal{S}_{\beta}

as:

E_{\phi_{\alpha},\phi_{\beta}}(s,s^{\prime})\iff\phi(s)=\phi(s^{\prime})

(10)

This is a generalization of the single-task setting
in Eq. (
5
).
If we now learn a policy

\pi_{\phi_{\alpha}}:\mathcal{S}_{\phi_{\alpha}}\mapsto~\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{A})

for
the abstract MDP
{M}_{\phi_{\alpha}}
,
it can be readily transferred to the ground task

{M}_{\alpha}

using policy derivation.
Moreover,
if

\Phi_{\alpha}

and

\Phi_{\beta}
intersect,
there will be states

s_{\alpha}\in\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}

and

s_{\beta}\in\mathcal{S}_{\beta}

so that

\phi(s_{\alpha})=\phi(s_{\beta})
.
For these states

s_{\beta}
,
it is possible to again
construct a policy
by setting

\pi_{\beta}(\cdot|s_{\beta})=\pi_{\phi_{\alpha}}(\cdot|{\phi_{\beta}}(s_{\beta}))
.
This may not cover
the whole state set

\mathcal{S}_{\beta}
—a policy

\pi_{\beta}

so constructed
would not necessarily constitute a full solution
to
{M}_{\beta}
—but it could still improve
learning in
{M}_{\beta}

by bootstrapping with a partial solution.
In this setting we refer to

{M}_{\beta}
as the
target task
,
and to
{M}_{\alpha}
as the
source task
.
The part of the target task’s state set
that is covered by the overlap in state abstractions
we refer to as the
transfer cover
.

Definition 2.15

(transfer cover)
.

Let

\phi_{\alpha}:\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}\mapsto\Phi_{\alpha}

and

\phi_{\beta}:\mathcal{S}_{\beta}\mapsto\Phi_{\beta}

be two state abstractions.
We refer to the set of
all ground states

s_{\beta}\in\mathcal{S}_{\beta}

with an abstraction in
the intersection
of the two abstractions

\Phi_{\alpha}\cap\Phi_{\beta}

as

the transfer cover of
\phi_{\beta}

given

\phi_{\alpha}

and denote it as

\displaystyle\phi^{-1}_{\beta}(\Phi_{\alpha})

\displaystyle\mathrel{\doteq}\Set{s\in\phi^{-1}_{\beta}(\bar{\phi})}{\bar{\phi}\in\Phi_{\alpha}}

This also implies a partial ordering
over pairs of state abstractions.
Let

\phi_{\beta}^{\prime}:\mathcal{S}_{\beta}\mapsto\Phi_{\beta}^{\prime}

be another state abstraction.
Given

\phi_{\alpha}
,

\phi_{\beta}

has greater transfer cover
than

\phi_{\beta}^{\prime}

if the transfer cover of
\phi_{\beta}^{\prime}

is a subset of that of
\phi_{\beta}
.
That is, if:

{\phi_{\beta}^{\prime}}^{-1}(\Phi_{\alpha})\subseteq{\phi_{\beta}}^{-1}(\Phi_{\alpha})

Now that we have identified the overlap between state abstractions
as an opportunity for transfer,
we can revisit
policy derivation

for the multi-task setting.

Definition 2.16

(partially derived policy)
.

Let

{M}_{\alpha}=\langle\mathcal{S}_{\alpha},\mathcal{A},p_{\alpha},r_{\alpha}\rangle

and

{M}_{\beta}=\langle\mathcal{S}_{\beta},\mathcal{A},p_{\beta},r_{\beta}\rangle

be two MDPs,
let

\phi_{\alpha}:\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}\mapsto\Phi_{\alpha}

and

\phi_{\beta}:\mathcal{S}_{\beta}\mapsto\Phi_{\beta}

be two state abstractions
such that

\Phi_{\alpha}\cup\Phi_{\beta}\subseteq
,
and let

\pi_{\phi_{\alpha}}:\Phi_{\alpha}\mapsto\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{A})

be an abstract policy.
A policy

\delta a\beta:\mathcal{S}_{\beta}\mapsto\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{A})

is

partially derived

if it holds that

\forall s_{\beta}\in\phi_{\beta}^{-1}(\Phi_{\alpha}):

\delta a\beta(a|s_{\beta})=\pi_{\phi_{\alpha}}(a|{\phi_{\beta}}(s_{\beta}))

Let

\Pi_{\phi_{\alpha}}

be the set of all abstract policies

\pi_{\phi_{\alpha}}:\Phi_{\alpha}\mapsto\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{A})
.
We write the

set of partially derived policies
from state abstraction

\phi_{\alpha}

for state abstraction
\phi_{\beta}

and MDP
{M}_{\beta}

as

\Pi_{\delta}(\phi_{\beta},{M}_{\beta};\phi_{\alpha})\mathrel{\doteq}\Set{\delta a\beta\in\Pi_{\delta}(\phi_{\beta},M)}{\exists\pi_{\phi_{\alpha}}\in\Pi_{\phi_{\alpha}}:\forall s_{\beta}\in\phi^{-1}_{\beta}(\Phi_{\alpha}):\delta a\beta(\cdot|s_{\beta})=\pi_{\phi_{\alpha}}(\cdot|\phi_{\beta}(s_{\beta}))}

We can also consider the subset of partially derived policies
for a single abstract policy.
We write the

set of partially derived policies from
abstract policy

\pi_{\phi_{\alpha}}

for state abstraction
{\phi_{\beta}}
and MDP
{{M}_{\beta}}
as

\Pi_{\delta}(\phi_{\beta},{M}_{\beta};\pi_{\phi_{\alpha}})\mathrel{\doteq}\Set{\delta a\beta\in\Pi_{\delta}(\phi_{\beta},M)}{\forall s_{\beta}\in\phi^{-1}_{\beta}(\Phi_{\alpha}):\delta a\beta(\cdot|s_{\beta})=\pi_{\phi_{\alpha}}(\cdot|\phi_{\beta}(s_{\beta}))}

It is straightforward to see that
single-task

derived policies

form a special case
of cross-task

partially derived policies

by setting

{M}_{\alpha}={M}_{\beta}

and

\phi_{\alpha}=\phi_{\beta}
.
The single-task setting is in effect a special case
of the multi-task setting
with full overlap between the
abstract codomains
resulting in the partially derived policy
making up a complete policy.

Whenever

{\Phi_{\beta}}\not\subseteq{\Phi_{\alpha}}
,
that is,
when
\phi_{\beta}

does not have maximal transfer cover
given
\phi_{\alpha}
,
the above partially derived policy
construction does not constitute
a full policy
for all states in
\Phi_{\beta}
,
hence the

partial

in the name.
Since the region of the state space
described by the transfer cover
is the only place where transfer can occur,
we should want it to be as great as possible.
At the same time,
there exists an interesting interplay
between transfer cover
and concepts we have discussed before in the single-task setting:
control granularity and transfer value.
We will now re-visit
these concepts for the multi-task setting,
taking into account their interactions with transfer cover.

Definition 2.17

(conditional coarseness)
.

Let

\phi_{\alpha}:\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}\mapsto\Phi_{\alpha}
,

\phi_{\beta}:\mathcal{S}_{\beta}\mapsto\Phi_{\beta}
,

\phi_{\beta}^{\prime}:\mathcal{S}_{\beta}\mapsto\Phi^{\prime}_{\beta}

be state abstractions
such that

\Phi_{\alpha}\cup\Phi_{\beta}\cup\Phi^{\prime}_{\beta}
.
We say that

\phi_{\beta}

is
finer
given

\phi_{\alpha}

than

\phi_{\beta}^{\prime}
,
denoted

\phi_{\beta}\underset{\phi_{\alpha}}{\succeq}\phi_{\beta}^{\prime}
,
if all states in the transfer cover of
\phi_{\beta}
given
\phi_{\alpha}

that are equivalent under
\phi_{\beta}

are also in the transfer cover of
\phi_{\beta}^{\prime}
given
\phi_{\alpha}

and are also equivalent under
\phi_{\beta}^{\prime}
.
Formally:

\begin{gathered}\phi_{\beta}\underset{\phi_{\alpha}}{\succeq}\phi_{\beta}^{\prime}\\
\Updownarrow\\
\forall s,t\in\mathcal{S}_{\beta}:\phi_{\beta}(s)=\phi_{\beta}(t)\wedge s,t\in\phi_{\beta}^{-1}(\Phi_{\alpha})\;\Longrightarrow\;\phi_{\beta}^{\prime}(s)=\phi_{\beta}^{\prime}(t)\wedge s,t\in{\phi_{\beta}^{\prime}}^{-1}(\Phi_{\alpha})\end{gathered}

If, on the other hand,
it holds that

\phi_{\beta}\underset{\phi_{\alpha}}{\preceq}\phi_{\beta}^{\prime}
(shorthand for
\phi^{\prime}_{\beta}\underset{\phi_{\alpha}}{\succeq}\phi_{\beta}
),
we say that
\phi_{\beta}
is

coarser given
\phi_{\alpha}
than
\phi_{\beta}^{\prime}
.
If, in addition,

\phi_{\beta}
is not isomorphic to
\phi_{\beta}^{\prime}
,
we say
that
\phi_{\beta}
is
strictly finer

(resp.
strictly coarser
)
than
\phi_{\beta}^{\prime}
.

The difference with the single state abstraction version
we used before
is that this definition of coarseness
takes into account
the transfer cover.
It is possible for one state abstraction to be strictly finer than another
without being strictly finer with respect to
a given state abstraction
\phi_{\alpha}
,
simply because only states in the transfer cover are relevant
for
conditional coarseness
.
In effect,
in our transfer setting,
part of the abstract state set is left outside of consideration.
Specifically, the part of
\Phi_{\beta}
that does not overlap with

\Phi_{\alpha}
,
i.e.
\Phi_{\beta}-\Phi_{\alpha}
.

Since transfer cover requires exact equality
between abstract states in
\Phi_{\alpha}
and
\Phi_{\beta}

and coarseness includes equality under isomorphism,
the interplay between transfer cover and coarseness
can be non-obvious.
It is for example possible
for a state abstraction to have different transfer cover
but the same level of conditional coarseness,
by means of a simple
relabelling of abstract states.
This relabelling is a bijection,
i.e. an isomorphism,
and preserves conditional coarseness.

In each setting,

as
\bar{\psi}_{2}
,

While conditional coarseness is preserved,
transfer cover can be affected as transfer cover requires exact equality.
From this perspective
transfer cover is a more stringent property,
one that is necessary for transfer.
This also means that there is no straightforward relation
between a change in coarseness and a change in transfer cover.
Just like in the single-task setting however,
there does exist
a direct relation between conditional coarseness and
and

conditional control granularity
.

Definition 2.18

(conditional control granularity)
.

Let

\phi_{\alpha}:\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}\mapsto\Phi_{\alpha}
,

\phi_{\beta}:\mathcal{S}_{\beta}\mapsto\Phi_{\beta}
,
and

\phi_{\beta}^{\prime}:\mathcal{S}_{\beta}\mapsto\Phi_{\beta}^{\prime}

be state abstractions
such that

\Phi_{\alpha}\cup\Phi_{\beta}\cup\Phi^{\prime}_{\beta}
,
and
let

{M}_{\beta}=\langle\mathcal{S}_{\beta},\mathcal{A},p_{\beta},r_{\beta}\rangle

be an MDP.
We say
that
\phi_{\beta}

is

more control granular
for
{M}_{\beta}

given
\phi_{\alpha}

than
\phi_{\beta}^{\prime}

if all derived policies contained in

\Pi_{\delta}(\phi_{\beta}^{\prime},{M}_{\beta};\phi_{\alpha})

are also contained in

\Pi_{\delta}(\phi_{\beta}^{\prime},{M}_{\beta};\phi_{\alpha})
.

\Pi_{\delta}(\phi_{\beta}^{\prime},{M};\phi_{\alpha})\subseteq\Pi_{\delta}(\phi_{\beta},{M};\phi_{\alpha})\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\forall\delta^{\prime}\in\Pi_{\delta}(\phi_{\beta}^{\prime},{M};\phi_{\alpha}):\exists\delta\in\Pi_{\delta}(\phi_{\beta},{M};\phi_{\alpha}):\delta=\delta^{\prime}

If in addition

\Pi_{\delta}(\phi_{\beta}^{\prime},{M};\phi_{\alpha})\neq\Pi_{\delta}(\phi_{\beta},{M};\phi_{\alpha})
,
we say that

\phi_{\beta}

is

strictly more control granular

than
\phi_{\beta}^{\prime}
given
\phi_{\alpha}
.

Proposition 2.5

(control granularity–coarseness trade-off)
.

Let

\phi_{\alpha}:\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}\mapsto\Phi_{\alpha}
,

\phi_{\beta}:\mathcal{S}_{\beta}\mapsto\Phi_{\beta}
,
and

\phi_{\beta}^{\prime}:\mathcal{S}_{\beta}\mapsto\Phi^{\prime}_{\beta}

be state abstractions
such that

\Phi_{\alpha}\cup\Phi_{\beta}\cup\Phi^{\prime}_{\beta}

and
let

{M}_{\beta}=\langle\mathcal{S}_{\beta},\mathcal{A},p_{\beta},r_{\beta}\rangle

be an MDP.
Then

\phi_{\beta}

is
finer given
\phi_{\alpha}

than
\phi_{\beta}^{\prime}

if and only if

\phi_{\beta}

is more
control granular
given
{M}_{\beta}
and
\phi_{\alpha}

than
\phi_{\beta}^{\prime}
.
Formally:

\phi_{\beta}\underset{\phi_{\alpha}}{\succeq}\phi_{\beta}^{\prime}\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\Pi_{\delta}(\phi_{\beta},{M}_{\beta};\phi_{\alpha})\supseteq\Pi_{\delta}(\phi_{\beta}^{\prime},{M}_{\beta};\phi_{\alpha})

The proof is a straightforward extension of the
proof of the
single-task state abstraction Proposition
2.2
.
We omit it here for the sake of brevity.

Just like in the single-task setting,
state abstractions
can impact attainable value functions
in the multi-task setting.
We can compare attainable value functions
between two state abstractions for an MDP,
conditional on another state abstraction
(based on transfer cover).

Definition 2.19

(conditional transfer value)
.

Let

\phi_{\alpha}:\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}\mapsto\Phi_{\alpha}
,

\phi_{\beta}:\mathcal{S}_{\beta}\mapsto\Phi_{\beta}
,

\phi_{\beta}^{\prime}:\mathcal{S}_{\beta}\mapsto\Phi^{\prime}_{\beta}

be state abstractions
such that

\Phi_{\alpha}\cup\Phi_{\beta}\cup\Phi^{\prime}_{\beta}

and
let

{M}_{\beta}=\langle\mathcal{S}_{\beta},\mathcal{A},p_{\beta},r_{\beta},\gamma\rangle

be an MDP.
We say that

\phi_{\beta}

has
greater transfer value for
{M}_{\beta}
given
\phi_{\alpha}

than

\phi_{\beta^{\prime}}
,
denoted

v_{\phi_{\beta}}({M}_{\beta};\phi_{\alpha})>v_{\phi_{\beta}^{\prime}}({M}_{\beta};\phi_{\alpha})
,
if
there exists a derived policy for

\phi_{\beta}

with a value function greater than or equal to
all derived policies’ value functions
for
\phi_{\beta}^{\prime}
:

\begin{gathered}v_{\phi_{\beta}}({M}_{\beta};\phi_{\alpha})\geq v_{\phi_{\beta}^{\prime}}({M}_{\beta};\phi_{\alpha})\\
\Updownarrow\\
\exists\delta_{\beta}\in\Pi_{\delta}(\phi_{\beta},{M}_{\beta};\phi_{\alpha}):\forall\delta_{\beta}^{\prime}\in\Pi_{\delta}(\phi_{\beta}^{\prime},{M}_{\beta};\phi_{\alpha}):v_{\delta_{\beta}}(\cdot;{M}_{\beta})\geq v_{\delta_{\beta}^{\prime}}(\cdot;{M}_{\beta})\end{gathered}

If the above inequality is strict,
we say that

\phi_{\beta}
has
strictly greater transfer value

than
\phi_{\beta}^{\prime}
.

As is the case in the single-task setting,
so too in the multi-task setting are
control granularity and coarseness on one side
and transfer value on the other side
related.
This time it takes into account the transfer cover.

Theorem 2.6

(transfer value–control trade-off)
.

Let

\phi_{\alpha}:\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}\mapsto\Phi_{\alpha}
,

\phi_{\beta}:\mathcal{S}_{\beta}\mapsto\Phi_{\beta}
,
and

\phi_{\beta}^{\prime}:\mathcal{S}_{\beta}\mapsto\Phi^{\prime}_{\beta}

be state abstractions
such that

\Phi_{\alpha}\cup\Phi_{\beta}\cup\Phi^{\prime}_{\beta}

and such that

\phi_{\beta}

has

strictly higher transfer value given
\phi_{\alpha}

for an MDP

{M}_{\beta}=\langle\mathcal{S}_{\beta},\mathcal{A},p_{\beta},r_{\beta}\rangle

than

\phi_{\beta}^{\prime}
.
Then there must
exist two states
in the transfer cover of

\phi_{\beta}^{\prime}
given
\phi_{\alpha}

such that

\phi_{\beta}^{\prime}
does not distinguish between them
while either
\phi_{\beta}
does,
or not both states are in the transfer cover of
\phi_{\beta}
.
In addition,
there exists a partially derived policy for

\phi_{\beta}

that takes advantage of this
by controlling
s
and
t
differently
in a way that no partially derived policy of
\phi_{\beta}^{\prime}
can do.
Formally:

\displaystyle v_{\phi_{\beta}}({M}_{\beta};\phi_{\alpha})>v_{\phi_{\beta}^{\prime}}({M}_{\beta};\phi_{\alpha})

\displaystyle\Downarrow

\displaystyle\exists s,t\in\mathcal{S}_{\beta}:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &1.\;\phi_{\beta}(s)\neq\phi_{\beta}(t)\vee s,t\not\in{\phi_{\beta}}^{-1}(\Phi_{\alpha})\\
&2.\;\phi_{\beta}^{\prime}(s)=\phi_{\beta}^{\prime}(t)\wedge s,t\in{\phi_{\beta}^{\prime}}^{-1}(\Phi_{\alpha})\\
&3.\;\exists\delta_{\beta}\in\Pi_{\delta}(\phi_{\beta},{M}_{\beta};\phi_{\alpha}):\delta_{\beta}(\cdot\mid s)\neq\delta_{\beta}(\cdot\mid t)\\
&4.\;\forall\delta_{\beta^{\prime}}\in\Pi_{\delta}(\phi_{\beta}^{\prime},{M}_{\beta};\phi_{\alpha}):\delta_{\beta^{\prime}}(\cdot\mid s)=\delta_{\beta^{\prime}}(\cdot\mid t)\end{aligned}

The proof of this theorem proceeds exactly like
that of
Theorem
2.3

with the added limitation imposed by transfer cover.
Analogous to the single task setting we derive the following corollary.

Corollary 2.7
.

Let

\phi_{\alpha}:\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}\mapsto\Phi_{\alpha}
,

\phi_{\beta}:\mathcal{S}_{\beta}\mapsto\Phi_{\beta}
,
and

\phi_{\beta}^{\prime}:\mathcal{S}_{\beta}\mapsto\Phi_{\beta}

be state abstractions
such that

\Phi_{\alpha}\cup\Phi_{\beta}

and such that

\phi_{\beta}

has

strictly higher transfer value given
\phi_{\alpha}

for an MDP

{M}_{\beta}=\langle\mathcal{S}_{\beta},\mathcal{A},p_{\beta},r_{\beta}\rangle

than

\phi_{\beta}^{\prime}
.
Then

\phi_{\beta}^{\prime}
can both be
no finer given
\phi_{\alpha}

than
\phi_{\beta}

and
no more control granular given
\phi_{\alpha}

than
\phi_{\beta}
.
Formally:

v_{\phi_{\beta}}({M}_{\beta};\phi_{\alpha})>v_{\phi_{\beta}^{\prime}}({M}_{\beta};\phi_{\alpha})\Longrightarrow\phi_{\beta}^{\prime}\not\underset{\phi_{\alpha}}{\succeq}\phi_{\beta}\;\wedge\;\Pi_{\delta}(\phi_{\beta},{M}_{\beta};\phi_{\alpha})\not\subseteq\Pi_{\delta}(\phi_{\beta}^{\prime},{M}_{\beta};\phi_{\alpha})

(11)

The following also holds.
If
\phi_{\beta}
is coarser
and less control granular given
\phi_{\alpha}

than
\phi_{\beta}^{\prime}
,
it is impossible for

\phi_{\beta}

to have a higher transfer value
given
\phi_{\alpha}

than

\phi_{\beta}^{\prime}
.
Formally:

\phi_{\beta}^{\prime}\underset{\phi_{\alpha}}{\succeq}\phi_{\beta}\;\wedge\;\Pi_{\delta}(\phi_{\beta},{M}_{\beta};\phi_{\alpha})\subseteq\Pi_{\delta}(\phi_{\beta}^{\prime},{M}_{\beta};\phi_{\alpha})\Longrightarrow v_{\phi_{\beta}}({M}_{\beta};\phi_{\alpha})\not>v_{\phi_{\beta}^{\prime}}({M}_{\beta};\phi_{\alpha})

(12)

Ideally we’d have maximal transfer cover
and maximal transfer value.
Maximal transfer cover
means that a partially derived policy is

complete
:
behavior is transferred
for every single target state.
Nothing more needs to be learned for the target task.
Maximum transfer value
in addition means
that it is possible for
a derived policy to be optimal everywhere.
This is no guarantee that it will be
but it does guarantee that the optimal policy is not excluded
by means of policy derivation.
Having both maximal transfer cover and transfer value
is an ideal situation,
though impossible in all but the most trivial cases.
For some intuition behind this,
consider when transfer cover
is highest.
If one task is a subtask of another
then behavior might be transferred readily
and beneficially to the subtask.
In less trivial settings
where this is not the case,
there simply are no source states
from which we could substitute the behavior for every single target state.
It is always possible to arbitrarily increase transfer cover
by increasing overlap between the abstract state sets

\Phi_{\alpha}

and
\Phi_{\beta}
,
arbitrarily equating states across tasks.
However, this overlap is not necessarily beneficial,
as would be reflected in the transfer value.
In general, there is no free lunch;
there is no way for a given transfer setting
to maximise both transfer cover and transfer value.
That summarised,
we end this section with a definition of those state abstractions
that do in fact not sacrifice on transfer value
while still being as coarse as possible.

Definition 2.20

(minimal control state abstraction)
.

A state abstraction

\phi_{\beta}:\mathcal{S}_{\beta}\mapsto\Phi_{\beta}

in a
minimal control state abstraction

for an MDP

{M}_{\beta}=\langle\mathcal{S}_{\beta},\mathcal{A},p_{\beta},r_{\beta}\rangle

given a state abstraction

\phi_{\alpha}:\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}\mapsto\Phi_{\alpha}

if
\phi_{\beta}

has
maximal transfer value

given
\phi_{\alpha}

and there exists no state abstraction coarser given
\phi_{\alpha}

than
\phi_{\beta}

that has higher transfer value for
{{M}_{\beta}}

than
\phi_{\beta}
.

As in the single task setting,
a
minimal control state abstraction

cannot be coarsened without negatively impacting transfer value.
Since multi-task coarsening can result from increasing transfer cover,
this means that a minimal control state abstraction
can also not increase its transfer cover
without affecting transfer value.

state abstractions
and the opportunities for transfer
that they provide.
What we have not gone into detail about
is how to actually arrive at such state abstractions.
It is this topic that forms
the heart of
Section
3

and this paper as a whole.
Before we get there however,
we will briefly introduce the basic setting of

hierarchical reinforcement learning
,
which will be the framework in which we will leverage
our newly developed and assessed state abstractions.

2.4
Skills as Options and Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning

Now that we have discussed state abstraction
at length,
it is time we discuss its counterpart:

action abstraction
.
A complete discussion
building on the state abstractions we develop in the rest of this paper
can be found in
Section
4
.
This section will be limited
to laying the foundations.

In this paper we
rely on the well-known formalism of

options

(
82
)
,
useful for its versatility and lack of assumptions.
Options can be seen as macro-actions
that encapsulate a particular behavior.
In particular,
we focus on options that
describe

local

and

reusable

behavior.
To capture these notions
we refer to such options
as
skills
,
as skills in common parlance
describe a very specific
(and repeatedly applicable) behavior.

To formalize our notion of
skills
,
we extend the traditional
definition of

options

with a
state set that the option operates on.

Definition 2.21

(option)
.

An option

\omega

is a tuple

\langle\mathcal{S}_{\omega},\pi_{\omega},\beta_{\omega}\rangle
,
with

\mathcal{S}_{\omega}

the option’s

domain
,
and

\displaystyle\pi_{\omega}:\mathcal{S}_{\omega}\mapsto\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{A})

\displaystyle\beta_{\omega}:\mathcal{S}_{\omega}\mapsto\mathcal{P}(\{0,1\})

the option control policy and termination policy respectively.

Given an MDP

{M}

with action
set

\mathcal{A}

and an option set

\Omega=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\omega\mid\omega=\langle\mathcal{S}_{\omega},\pi_{\omega},\beta_{\omega}\rangle}}\right\}
,
we construct
an option-enabled MDP
(also known as a Semi-MDP;
(
82
)
)

{M}_{\Omega}
with action set

\mathcal{A}\cup\Omega
.
Intuitively,
the agent expands its action set with the set of options
and can use them interchangeably,
making no distinction between primitive action (
a\in\mathcal{A}
)
or option.
Upon invocation,
an option’s control policy
selects primitive actions
until a
1
is sampled from its termination policy
indicating termination.
Upon termination,
control is passed back to the agent,
for whom only a single step has passed.

There is a form of action abstraction at play here.
While multiple primitive steps may have been taken
by the option,
to the agent it appears as only one action.
This action abstraction leads to a hierarchy,
with the option-enabled agent
initiating options on the higher level
and the option operating on the lower level.
This view of options inducing a hierarchy
has led to the term

hierarchical reinforcement learning

(HRL)

(
14
)
.

A skill is defined for a particular state set
– its domain.
When its domain is chosen to be task-independent
the skill itself becomes usable across tasks.
The hard part is of course to find such a task-independent domain.
Skills go hand in hand with state abstraction,
the latter providing the abstract state set for a skill to operate over.
We will return to combining
action abstraction and state abstraction
in Section
4

when we define options over our state abstractions
that we introduce in Section
3.2
.

3
State Abstractions for Transfer

Whereas Section
2
dealt mainly with
state abstractions and different ways to describe and compare them,
this section deals mainly with
the construction of useful state abstractions.
‘Useful’, as discussed in the previous section,
generally means high transfer cover
and high transfer value,
but that too will receive more nuance in this section
as we explore how a state abstraction construction
impacts transfer value.
In particular,
in this section we propose
a new method for state abstraction.
Based on predictions of
outcomes
,
i.e. common characteristics between tasks,
(Section
3.1
)
outcome equivalent state abstractions
come with strong guarantees
of optimality
when used with a particular class of MDPs
(Section
3.2
).
We finish with an illustration
of what our outcome-based state abstraction affords us
in Section
3.4
,
showing that outcome equivalent state abstractions
can be coarsened to increase opportunity for transfer
while incurring a cost in optimality.
This neatly leads into Section
4

which illustrates that this cost can be overcome,
meaning that you
can

have your cake and eat it too.

3.1
Outcomes as Reward Signals

So far we have not formally
defined the notion of a

domain
of related tasks.
We want to be able to describe tasks
that on the surface have something in common,
where transitions across tasks
follow
the same rules
.
In order to do so we first need a way to describe transitions.
We start off with
remarkable transitions
:
transitions that are especially
meaningful
to the task at hand.
It will then be possible
to describe transitions that are remarkable
in the same way across tasks
—
a step closer to describing related tasks.

We first introduce
the notion of
an
outcome
.
Our notion of
outcomes

is inspired by the
homonymous
outcomes

of
79

that describe differences in state,
tracing back to outcomes in planning

(
20
)
,
and more recently
by the reward decomposition
of
13
.

A transition can be described in terms of multiple outcomes,
each denoting the occurrence of something remarkable.
In particular,
remarkable here is taken to mean
‘relevant to reward’:
the occurrence of an outcome
signals the presence of reward.
Or, from a different perspective,
a transition with no outcomes
is a transition with no reward.

Just like rewards can be randomly distributed for a given transition,
so are outcomes.
Where we use the random variable

R_{t+1}
to describe reward for a transition,
we use

\Sigma_{t+1}

to do the same for the outcome distribution.
The difference is that while

R_{t+1}
ranges over real numbers,

\Sigma_{t+1}

describes a distribution over vectors of outcomes.
The motivation for this is that multiple things may be relevant for reward,
each in a different way.
We rarely use
R_{t+1}
directly
and usually use the expected reward for a transition

(s,a,s^{\prime})
instead,
denoted
r(s,a,s^{\prime})
.
In the same vein we use

\sigma(s,a,s^{\prime})

to denote the expected outcome
for that transition.
In addition,
for simplicity we assume
that the expected outcome is itself an outcome.
We will discuss later
how this outcome function can be obtained.

Definition 3.1

(outcome)
.

The random variable describing

outcomes
at time
{t+1}

is denoted
\Sigma_{t+1}

and is defined over the
outcome domain

\Sigma
.
Given a transition
(s,a,s^{\prime})\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{S}
,
its

expected outcomes

are given by

\sigma(s,a,s^{\prime})~\in~\Sigma
,
with

\sigma(s,a,s^{\prime})

defined as

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[\Sigma_{t+1}\mid S_{t}=s,A_{t}=a,S_{t+1}=s^{\prime}]
.
The

outcome domain

\Sigma

is
defined as

\Sigma\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Finally,
the expected reward
of a transition

(s,a,s^{\prime})\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{S}

can be decomposed as
a product of the expected outcomes of that transition
and a task-specific

reward weights vector

w_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

which describes how
different outcomes affect the reward
for that task:

r(s,a,s^{\prime})=\sigma(s,a,s^{\prime})^{\top}w_{r},

(13)

If this holds for all transitions we can also write

r=\sigma^{\top}w_{r}

which we define to only hold when the above equality
holds for all transitions in the domain
\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{S}
.
Since we do not work with MDPs that have randomly distributed reward
given an exact transition
(s,a,s^{\prime})
,
we instead refer to

expected outcomes

as
outcomes
.

An outcome’s presence is indicated
by a non-zero element in

\sigma(s,a,s^{\prime})
.
Similarly,
a zero element means the outcome did not occur.
If all elements are
0

it means the transition is not

remarkable

in any way,
i.e. it is not relevant for reward.

Example 3.1
.

Figure 5
:
Two gridworld tasks with different layouts
that belong to the same domain.

For example,
Figure
5

describes two Gridworlds.
In both tasks
transitioning onto a green tile
yields a positive reward
of
10
and terminates the episode.
Transitioning onto a red tile
incurs a negative reward
depending on the task at hand:

-10

in the first task
and
-1

in the second task.
Both tasks seem to be composed of the same

elements
;
red and green tiles
are present in both tasks
and both incur rewards,
albeit that the specific amount differs.
These elements are part of the language
of the domain that both tasks belong to
and outcomes are our tool
to describe them.

To continue the example,
we can describe the outcomes
for both tasks
as a binary-valued vector

\sigma(s,a,s^{\prime})=(\texttt{green}(s^{\prime}),\texttt{red}(s^{\prime}))^{\top},

where

\texttt{green}(s)

and

\texttt{red}(s)

take on a value of
1

only if
the agent in state
s

occupies a tile
of the corresponding colour.
That is,
there are two possible outcomes
and they describe where the agent ends up
after a transition.
In this example,
we have

d=2

because there are two outcomes
corresponding to the two different colors.
The reward weights vector for the first task
is

w_{r_{\alpha}}=(10,-10)^{\top}

and for the second task we have

w_{r_{\beta}}=(10,-1)^{\top}
.
The difference between the reward weights
for both tasks
highlights the aforementioned difference in rewards;
the second task penalizes transitioning onto red cells
less heavily.

Transitions
with rewards
are not the only remarkable transitions
in this example.
Both tasks
contain impassable walls
and attempted movement in the direction of a wall will fail
in both tasks.
This, just like transitioning onto red or green cells,
is
also a kind of transition
that exists in both tasks
and is part of the

elements

that make up the domain.
It may therefore be useful to expand the
outcome function

\sigma

to include other outcomes that are not relevant
to reward.
This irrelevance will show itself
in corresponding elements of
w_{r}

which are set to
0
.
As a result,
the outcome function
\sigma

can always be expanded
while maintaining correctness.
We will revisit this idea
of
non-necessary outcomes

later.
∎

To summarize the above,
outcomes allow
describing reward functions of different tasks
in terms of invariant
commonalities.
These commonalities make up the language
of what we have so far informally referred to as

domains
.
We formalize a domain
for the purposes of our work as follows.

Definition 3.2

(domain)
.

A domain

\mathcal{D}

is a set of tasks
with the same

outcome domain

\Sigma
:

\mathcal{D}\mathrel{\doteq}\{{M}|{M}=\langle\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},p,r\rangle\ \text{with}\ r(s,a,s^{\prime})=\sigma(s,a,s^{\prime})^{\top}w_{r}\}

with

\sigma:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{S}\mapsto\Sigma

and
w_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

specific to each task

{M}
.

We can now rely on outcomes
to describe domains
in terms of domain-invariant commonalities.
However, they only help us to describe transitions
relevant for the one-step reward function.
Many transitions are unremarkable
and have no non-zero outcomes associated with them.
To overcome this
lack of descriptive power
we can talk about a sequence of outcomes
allowing us to go beyond a single time step:
specifically,
a sequence of ouctomes
given a sequence of actions
and a starting state.
Since such an outcome sequence is conditional
on future behavior (an action sequence),
this gives us a way to describe
the future of a state in terms of outcomes.
We formalize the following.

Definition 3.3

(outcome sequence–test)
.

Let

{M}=\langle\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},p,r\rangle

be an MDP
and
let

\sigma(s,a,S^{\prime})

be the random variable describing the distribution over outcomes

For a state

{\langle\bbsigma\rangle}(s_{t},\langle a\rangle)\mathrel{\doteq}(\sigma(s_{t},a_{1},S_{t+1}),\dots,\sigma(S_{t+n-1},a_{n},S_{t+n}))

(14)

Since the future states

and so is the resulting outcome sequence.
Its distribution describes possible outcome sequences given an action sequence.
To stress that it is a random variable,
we use the blackboard character
\bbsigma

Note that an

is effectively an
outcome sequence

that is conditioned on a state and action sequence.
The probability that it takes on a value

p_{\sigma}((\sigma_{1},...,\sigma_{n})\mid s_{t},\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle a}}\right\rangle_{)}\mathrel{\doteq}\Pr_{p}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\langle\bbsigma\rangle}(s_{t},\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle a}}\right\rangle_{)}=(\sigma_{1},...,\sigma_{n})}}\right)

Its computation can be expressed with the help of the shorthand

p_{\sigma}(\sigma_{i}\mid s,a,s^{\prime})\mathrel{\doteq}\Pr(\Sigma_{t+1}=\sigma_{i}\mid S_{t}=s,A_{t}=a,S_{t+1}=s^{\prime}))
,
as:

\displaystyle\begin{split}p_{\sigma}((\sigma_{1},...,\sigma_{n})\mid s_{t},\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle a}}\right\rangle_{)}&=\sum_{s_{t+1}\in\mathcal{S}}p(s_{t+1}\mid s_{t},a_{1})p_{\sigma}(\sigma_{1}\mid s_{t},a_{1},s_{t+1})\\
&\qquad\qquad\dots\sum_{s_{t+n}\in\mathcal{S}}p(s_{t+n}\mid s_{t+n-1},a_{n})p_{\sigma}(\sigma_{n}\mid s_{t+n-1},a_{n},s_{t+n})\end{split}

\displaystyle\begin{split}&=\sum_{s_{t+1},...,s_{t+n}\in\mathcal{S}^{n}}p(s_{t+1}\mid s_{t},a_{1})\dots p(s_{t+n}\mid s_{t+n-1},a_{n})\\
&\qquad\qquad p_{\sigma}(\sigma_{1}\mid s_{t},a_{1},s_{t+1})\dots p_{\sigma}(\sigma_{n}\mid s_{t+n-1},a_{n},s_{t+n})\end{split}

3.2
Outcome-Based State Abstractions That Transfer

This section
develops state abstractions
based on our new notion of

outcomes
.
We will see that these state abstractions
lend themselves well for transfer,
though not all is straightforward.
By the end of this section we will have developed
a firm understanding of
outcome-based state abstractions
and their implications for transfer value,
as well as their applicability.

To start constructing new state abstractions,
consider first how outcome sequence–tests relate between states.
In particular,
when an outcome sequence–test
is identically distributed
for two different states and given action sequence,
we know that these two states
share the same distribution
over possible futures
for that particular action sequence
and
for all relevant purposes
– that is, for reward.
For a collection of outcome sequence tests to all be identical for two different states, indicates that those states share the same distribution over relevant futures for any action sequence in the collection of tests.
In fact, we often only care about
expected
reward
and need only focus on expected outcomes
and expected outcome sequences rather than the full distributions.

Definition 3.4

(expected outcome sequence)
.

Let

{M}=\langle\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},p,r\rangle

be an MDP
with outcome function

\sigma
.
The

expected outcome sequence

for state
s\in\mathcal{S}

and action sequence

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle a}}\right\rangle_{=}(a_{1}\dots a_{n})\in\mathcal{A}^{n}
,
denoted

{\langle\sigma\rangle}(s,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle a}}\right\rangle_{)}
,
is defined as the expected value of
the outcome sequence–test

{\langle\bbsigma\rangle}(s,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle a}}\right\rangle_{)}
.
Formally:

\displaystyle{\langle\sigma\rangle}(s,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle a}}\right\rangle_{)}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\doteq}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{p_{\sigma}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\langle\bbsigma\rangle}(s,a)}}\right]

(15)

For a continuous domain
\Sigma
,
this can be expressed as
the following n-dimensional volume differential:

\displaystyle{\langle\sigma\rangle}(s,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle a}}\right\rangle_{)}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\doteq}\int_{\Sigma}p_{\sigma}((\sigma_{1},...,\sigma_{n})\mid s_{t},\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle a}}\right\rangle_{)}(\sigma_{1},...,\sigma_{n})d^{n}(\sigma_{1},...,\sigma_{n})

(16)

Since the outcome sequence–test
{\langle\bbsigma\rangle}(s,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle a}}\right\rangle_{)}

is a random variable describing a distribution over sequences,
the
expected outcome sequence

{\langle\sigma\rangle}(s,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle a}}\right\rangle_{)}

is simply a sequence.
Note the difference in notation to highlight this difference in nature.

As stated before,
the interesting thing about expected outcome sequences
is that states can be described in an
entirely task-agnostic way
yet in a way that is sufficiently rich.
Two states
can now be equated based on
their expected outcome sequences,
despite being from different tasks
with entirely different transition and reward dynamics, and will respond in the same way (up to expectation) as one another in response to the action sequences included in the collection of expected outcome sequences. If the collection of outcome sequences includes all possible sequences of actions from the states, then equality of the expected outcome sequences corresponds to identical, action dependent, reward relevant futures for the pair of states.

Definition 3.5

(outcome equivalence)
.

Given two
MDPs

{M}_{\alpha}=\langle\mathcal{S}_{\alpha},\mathcal{A},p_{\alpha},r_{\alpha}\rangle

{M}_{\beta}=\langle\mathcal{S}_{\beta},\mathcal{A},p_{\beta},r_{\beta}\rangle

two states
s_{\alpha}\in{\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}}

and
s_{\beta}\in{\mathcal{S}_{\beta}}

if and only if for every possible sequence of actions

\langle a\rangle=a_{1},\dots,a_{n}

That is, if and only if

\forall n\in\mathbb{N},\;\forall\langle a\rangle\in\mathcal{A}^{n},\forall s_{\alpha}\in{\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}},s_{\beta}\in{\mathcal{S}_{\beta}}

\displaystyle{\langle\sigma\rangle_{\alpha}}(s_{\alpha},\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle a}}\right\rangle_{)}={\langle\sigma\rangle_{\beta}}(s_{\beta},\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle a}}\right\rangle_{)}

(17)

\displaystyle\Updownarrow

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{p_{\alpha}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\langle\bbsigma\rangle}_{\alpha}(s_{\alpha},\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle a}}\right\rangle_{)}}}\right]=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{p_{\beta}}[{\langle\bbsigma\rangle}_{\beta}(s_{\beta},\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle a}}\right\rangle_{)}]

to compare expected outcome sequences

The method for this bridging of tasks

Definition 3.6

{M}=\langle\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},p,r\rangle

.

.

.

Since outcome equivalence is based on equality between sequences

∎

An outcome equivalent state abstraction
is based on
infinite action sequences
which makes it impractical for any real problem.
Nonetheless,
we continue with the proposed construction
because of its theoretical merit,
which we will discuss shortly.
Later in this paper
in Section

we will consider again finite action sequences
to make outcome equivalent state abstractions more tractable.
Even with a limit on the length of the action sequences in consideration
however,
having access to future expected rewards
is not a small assumption.
Because the main goal of this paper is to investigate
the usefulness of outcome equivalent state abstractions,
we continue under the assumption that we have access
to expected outcomes.
We resume this discussion
on how to actually attain such a state abstraction
in practice
in Section

.

Aside from single-task state abstractions,
we can also extend the notion of outcome equivalence to apply to
relations
that span multiple tasks.
This is a particularly useful view for transfer.
On one side there is the inter ground task view:
states from different tasks with the same expected outcome sequences
are outcome equivalent,
despite being from different tasks.
Outcome equivalence relates states
in a meaningful way
that before had nothing obviously in common.
The other view is between an abstract task
and ground tasks.
An abstract state from an abstract MDP
is outcome equivalent
with its ground states
and all states from other MDPs
that are outcome equivalent with its ground states.
This is a powerful property:
MDP abstraction based on outcome equivalent state abstractions
preserves outcome equivalence.
We will see later why this is such an interesting property.
In short though,
in particular kinds of MDPs
an optimal policy for an abstract MDP
based on outcome equivalent state abstractions
will also result in optimal derived policies,
which is a great guarantee to have for transfer.

We formalize the findings for the two views of inter ground tasks
and abstract to ground tasks.

Outcome equivalent construction

For a given MDP
there are many outcome equivalent state abstractions,
the identity abstraction being the finest of them.
There is one straightforward way to construct
the coarsest outcome equivalent state abstraction,
which we will from here on refer to as the

minimal outcome equivalent state abstraction
,
which maps together all states that have the same expected outcome sequences.
This construction assigns to each state
a labelled set of expected outcome sequences
as that state’s abstraction.
The labels of this set are the action sequences,
the elements corresponding to those labels
are the expected outcome sequences.
Algorithm
1

lists this simple iteration.

Algorithm 1

minimal outcome equivalent state abstraction construction

input

MDP
{M}=\langle\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},p,r\rangle

initialize
\phi:\mathcal{S}\mapsto\varnothing

for

do

\phi(s)\leftarrow\langle\sigma\rangle(s,\mathcal{A}^{\infty})

Given state abstractions
that are constructed by labelling states
with their outcome sequence tests
(according to Algorithm
1
),
an equivalence relation that
equates these states across
tasks naturally follows.
This equivalence relation
is again outcome equivalent
(Proposition
3.4
).
This construction is part of what makes
outcome equivalence such a useful property:
a task-specific construction that is agnostic of other tasks
still results in a meaningful equivalence relation across tasks,
one that equates states with the same future outcomes.

3.3
Transfer Optimality

Given an outcome equivalent state abstraction,
we need to be able to construct an abstract MDP
that we can then solve.
Many constructions are possible
as each choice for the abstract
transition and reward dynamics

p_{\phi}
and
r_{\phi}

results in a different abstract MDP.
However,
not all abstract MDPs are created equally.
While the preceding propositions have shown that some properties
are not affected by the choice of abstract MDP
when using an outcome equivalent abstraction,
this does not go for all properties.
Eventually
we want to learn an policy for the abstract MDP
and translate it to the ground MDP.
Ideally, a good abstract policy leads to a good ground policy.
What follows is an example
to illustrate the problems we face
when employing an outcome equivalent state abstraction.
This will naturally lead to a new definition
of when transfer is really
optimal
.

Summary

To summarize this section,
there are two fundamental properties that make
outcome equivalent state abstractions useful:

1.

Outcome equivalence relations
are constructed in an inherently
bottom-up
fashion
(Algorithm
1
).
For each MDP
an outcome equivalent state abstraction
can be created separately
from the ground up
without having to consider other MDPs.
Thanks to outcome equivalence,
these state abstractions naturally lead to an outcome equivalence relation
between MDPs.
This makes it very easy to consider
new MDPs without having to start from scratch
as one just needs a new state abstraction for that MDP.

2.

Outcome equivalence relations
guarantee
transfer optimality

for the entire transfer cover
when the ground MDPs are
plannable
.

While outcome equivalence relations are versatile
with strong guarantees,
these guarantees break down in the face
of highly stochastic,
non-plannable MDPs.
Still,
in practical problems this may not prove that
much of a problem,
especially
when we introduce our hierarchical framework
in Section
4
.
Outcome equivalent state abstractions
shine especially
in this setting.
The question of how
to actually compute the expected outcomes
necessary for the construction
of outcome equivalent state abstractions
is an important one that has come up
earlier in this section
but one that is left to the discussion
in Section
7
.

.

Many realistic tasks have some structure to them,

As discussed before,

4.1
Subtasks as Skills

We have conceptually sketched
how a task can be seen as being made up of

and how those subtasks can each contribute

for a target task.
We now dig a little deeper to understand
how these subtasks can be used in practice.

We approach our
subtasks with

).
Formally,
skills are
just options.
We have opted for the term

to add semantics
to the usual notion of an option;
in our eyes,
a skill represents
local behavior

that is
reusable
.
First,
the
local-ness
of behavior
goes hand in hand with using coarse

if a state abstraction
captures only

it will be coarser
than a state abstraction
that captures more global information.
Subtasks should require only
local information.
Second,
we focus on subtasks that are repeated across tasks,

This corresponds to preferring reusable options
rather than options that are useful in only

Consider again the definition
we introduced previously

This time however,
we choose

to be abstract
so that a state abstraction
maps into an option’s domain.
The hierarchy now has options
operating over an abstract state set
on the lower level
and an option-enabled agent
initiating options
(themselves abstract actions)
on the higher level.
This may be contrary to intuition
demanding that abstract states are used on a ‘higher’ level
–
indeed here it is the lower level
(the option)
which employs state abstraction.

as governed by the option’s termination policy.
Finally,
a subtask presents a task
which the option’s policy presents the solution to.
One thing remains:
it is as of yet unclear how the start of a subtask is encoded.
Some approaches enrich an option with an

initiation policy

which dictates when an option can be started
(when a subtask begins).
We employ the more common approach
of giving an option-enabled agent control over when to start an option.
This control puts the onus on the option-enabled agent
as the option is now available everywhere
but not necessarily universally useful.

It is now worth returning
to the question of
what complexity
this approach adds.
Consider a set of options
that each encode a solution to a smallest
conceivable subtask,
one which only concerns itself with a single state
and so only consists of a single step.
Realistically
there would be as many
different options
as there would be unique outcome-equivalent state abstractions
for one-step tasks,
a number that is combinatorial
in the number of actions.
The option-enabled agent now has control
over all primitive actions,
plus this combinatorial amount of options.
Its action set has increased greatly.
Since the agent will still need to learn
a policy to use each of these actions effectively,
it has gained effectively nothing
–
instead its task has only gotten more complicated.
At the other extreme
is the task consisting of a single subtask
with little to no potential for transfer.
There is need for a balancing act:
the ideal number of subtasks
and their sizes
should provide a benefit that outweighs
the cost of the added complexity.
There is a trade-off between a larger number of
shorter options and fewer longer options.
Short options can transfer behaviors to a large number of
states of a target task (high coverage),
but the higher level agent must still learn when to initiate each option.
A longer option transfers more behavioral information,
but is not as widely applicable.
This trade-off poses a key problem that we will delve
into in Section
5
.

So far we have left unanswered
the question of how subtasks or options
are specified.
We do not want to take the approach
of manually specifying subtasks
as that would require a large amount of
knowledge and engineering
without a guarantee to be helpful.
This is a question in two complementary parts:
state abstraction and action abstraction.
That is,
each option both
relies on a state abstraction
and represents
an abstract action
through
its control and termination policy.
These then make up the two interdependent great variables of this approach:
which state abstractions and which action abstractions.
We will solve for both in our experiments.

In what is to follow we describe our approach to this problem,
starting with
how we can uncover options
(or indeed – action abstractions)
when given a set of state abstractions.
We finish with our construction
of coarse outcome equivalent state abstractions.

4.2
Option Discovery

In order to find a set of suitable options
we park for the moment the problem
of how to find suitable state abstractions.
We assume for now that
we have access to state abstractions
and that they are
both useful
and suitably coarse
to allow for transfer to happen.
The options that we would like to find
are those that are as repeatedly useful across tasks as possible.
Ideally,
we want to find behaviors
for subtasks
that are common
in a domain
—
reusable skills
.
This would make it all the more likely
that an option is useful
in a new and unseen task of the same domain.

At this point we should clarify our setting.
We assume that we have access to
a small subset of source tasks of a domain
such as in
Figure
11
,
except
the target tasks are unknown beforehand.
Ideally these source tasks are representative for the domain,
for we aim to find options that
are as useful as possible in all of these tasks.
Recall that it is only possible to consider
options that operate in different tasks
thanks to a useful state abstraction,
one that is coarse
and one that ideally equates states
that benefit from the same policy,
i.e. one with a high transfer value such as an outcome equivalent state abstraction.

There are a few ways in which option discovery could be approached.
For example,
imagine an agent learning how to navigate
multiple tasks
while continuously updating an option set
that helps replicate its learned behavior.
A certain pattern of action
might arise as the agent is learning:
a natural candidate for an option.
This can be seen as a form of online compression
of the learned behavior,
constantly bootstrapping the agent’s learning
and improving replicability of behavior.
We do not adopt this

online
approach
but we do let it guide us.
We still see option discovery as a compression
of learned behavior,
with one option explaining as much of the demonstrated behavior as possible,
but instead of an online approach
we take a more stratified
and offline approach.
As option discovery is not the main topic of this paper,
we describe our version and
rephrasing of the work of

27

in detail in
Appendix
B
.
We summarize it here.

Our approach uses a collection of demonstration traces

\{\tau_{i}\}^{N}_{i=1}
,
where each trace (state-action-reward sequence)
is derived experience
from policies trained independently on one of
a collection of
J

source
(training) tasks

{M}_{j}=\langle\mathcal{S}_{j},\mathcal{A},p_{j},r_{j},\gamma\rangle
.
Although these traces are derived from a non-hierarchical agent,
we then model them as if they were derived from a single hierarchical agent
acting across all
J
tasks.
This hierarchical agent has access to
K
options

\Omega_{K}=\{\omega_{k}\}^{K}_{k=1}
,
each with its own abstraction
\phi_{k}

1
1

1

Although each option can have its own abstraction,
in our experiments we use the same abstract space for all policies,
suck that
\Phi_{k}=\Phi
.
,
and for each task
{M}_{j}

a separate high-level policy

\pi_{j}:\mathcal{S}_{j}\mapsto\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{A}\cup\Omega_{K})
.
Equivalently,
this can be seen as
J
hierarchical agents
sharing options
\Omega_{K}
.
Given a parameterization

\theta_{j}

for the control and termination policy of each option
\omega_{j}
,
the discovery mechanism seeks to find the maximum likelihood
of high-level policies

\{\pi_{j}\}^{J}_{j=1}

and option parameters

\{\theta_{k}\}^{K}_{k=1}

to explain traces

\{\tau_{i}\}^{N}_{i=1}

as if they were generated by the hierarchical agent,
i.e.

\argmax_{\{\theta_{k}\}_{k},\{\pi_{j}\}_{j}}\prod_{i}^{N}\Pr(\tau_{i}\mid K,\{\theta_{k}\}_{k},\{\pi_{j}\}_{j})

We always pick the state abstraction
to be strictly coarser
than the ground state representation,
ensuring opportunity for transfer
so that each option can be useful in multiple tasks,
as discussed previously.
As a consequence,
each of the resulting options
may not be able to reconstruct
the demonstrated trajectories exactly,
so that an optimal solution
would typically only be attained when options are combined.
This is exactly what we want:
a task made up of subtasks,
with each subtask reoccurring in many tasks.
We discuss the exact state abstraction
used in the next section.

4.3
Implementing Coarse Outcome Equivalent State Abstractions

The example in
Section
3.4

showed that

truncating

the action sequences employed
in the construction of
outcome equivalent state abstractions
naturally made for coarser state abstractions.
Consider shorter action sequences
and expected outcome sequences
as
resulting in short-sightedness,
leaving local information only.
Truncated outcome-equivalent state abstractions
are perfectly suited
for our options:
each option
has access to a local window of information,
ignoring global and unnecessary detail,
ensuring that states across tasks
that have an identical local window
(the same abstract state)
have access to the same behavior.

In this section
we describe how exactly we construct these truncated
outcome-equivalent state abstractions
for our experiments.
We assume for the experiments
that we have
access to an oracle
which gives perfect information
about future outcomes.
In practice,
outcomes would not be known without some prior exploration
or access to the model,
but for clarity we omit this complicated from our empirical studies.
This omission allows us to assess the
benefits of our approach in isolation,
without in addition needing to learn state abstractions.
We discuss the learning of outcomes in section
6
.

Consider again the expected outcome sequence, Equation (
14
),
constructed from
an action sequence

\langle a\rangle=(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
.
It contains
the expected outcome
for each transition

(S_{t+k-1},a_{k},S_{t+k})

following the actions

(a_{1},...,a_{k-1})

up to that point (repeated here for convenience):

\langle\sigma\rangle(s_{t},\langle a\rangle)\mathrel{\doteq}(\sigma(s_{t},a_{1},S_{t+1}),\dots,\sigma(S_{t+n-1},a_{n},S_{t+n}))

Given that
\Sigma\subseteq\mathbb{R}
,
this is a sequence of real values.
To construct a
vector

of outcomes,
we simply stack
the individual entries.
We employ boldface to denote vectors
(to contrast with the notation for a sequence of expected outcomes).

\langle\bm{\sigma}\rangle(s_{t},\langle a\rangle)\mathrel{\doteq}\begin{bmatrix}\sigma(s_{t},a_{1},S_{t+1})\\
\vdots\\
\sigma(S_{t+n-1},a_{n},S_{t+n})\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{nd}

(23)

The vector containing
all expected outcomes
for action sequences of length
n

is constructed by stacking
the individual expected outcome vectors.
So for action sequences

\langle a\rangle_{i}\in\mathcal{A}^{n}

and
|\mathcal{A}^{n}|=N
:

\langle\bm{\sigma}\rangle(s_{t},\mathcal{A}^{n})\mathrel{\doteq}\begin{bmatrix}\langle\bm{\sigma}\rangle(s_{t},\langle a\rangle_{1})\\
\vdots\\
\langle\bm{\sigma}\rangle(s_{t},\langle a\rangle_{N})\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{Nnd}

(24)

We can now
define a state abstraction
based on this vector construction.

Definition 4.1

(Outcome-Predictive State Representation)
.

Given an MDP

{M}=\langle\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},p,r\rangle
,
and outcome function

\sigma:\mathcal{S}\mapsto\Sigma
,
we define the
Outcome-Predictive State Representation
(OPSR)
of length
k

as the vector of all expected outcome sequences
of length
k
.
It is a function

\phi_{\sigma,k}:\mathcal{S}\mapsto\Phi_{\sigma,k}
:

\bm{\phi}_{\sigma,k}(s)\mathrel{\doteq}\langle\bm{\sigma}\rangle(s,\mathcal{A}^{k})

(25)

We refer to
k
as the
horizon
.

Since an OPSR
is parameterized
by the
horizon

k
,
it is natural to consider
the family of functions
arrived at by varying the
horizon.
For a given state set
\mathcal{S}

and outcome function
\sigma:\mathcal{S}\mapsto\Sigma
,
we get the set

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\phi_{\sigma,k}:\mathcal{S}\mapsto\Phi_{\sigma,k}\mid k\in\mathbb{N}}}\right\}.

(26)

This set is totally ordered
under the

finer
and
coarser
relations.
That is,
for
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

\phi_{\sigma,k+1}

will be finer than

\phi_{\sigma,k}
.
If

\phi_{\sigma,k+1}

is
not strictly finer than

\phi_{\sigma,k}
,
we term

\phi_{\sigma,k}

the
finest OPSR

for
{M}
and
\sigma
.

This section

In particular,
we will evaluate
and compare

with focus on one aspect:

.
We consider transfer to be beneficial

This means that for our experiments
we set aside the issue of

That said,

We evaluate our methods

The Lightworld domain was originally introduced by

49
,
who proposed

:
manually engineered abstract state spaces
amenable to transfer.
We compare our general method directly
agent-space–based options.
Finally,
we inspect the potential
for transfer
between different subdomains.

5.1
Experimental Structure

The previous section
already discussed
our Outcome-Predictive State Representation

and our option discovery process
that builds on this
method of state abstraction.
In particular,
we constrain all options for a particular experiment

\Phi_{\sigma,k}
.

the number of options,
the OPSR horizon

,

An experiment is organized as a

First,

From the demonstrated trajectories,

).

this is simply for demonstration purposes
and our method is by no means limited to

where a set of source tasks is available.
The procedure is in fact designed
to closely imitate

5.2.1
Types of Agent

We use two types of reinforcement learning agents:
a regular agent that has access to primitive actions only,
referred to as the

,
and an

5.2.2
State Representation

The base state representation
is a fully Markov state representation
where each state is encoded separately:
a one-hot representation
which we will denote

.
This is used by the option-less agent
as well as the high-level policy of the hierarchical agent.

The option’s state representation

This leaves us to define

Using

\sigma(s,a,s^{\prime})=(\Delta\texttt{wood},\Delta\texttt{stone},\Delta\texttt{handle},\Delta\texttt{head},\Delta\texttt{hammer})

\displaystyle\pi_{\omega}(s)

5.2.7
Option Trace Inspection

We have shown that our options
help speed up learning,
yet it is unclear how exactly they are helping.
The outcome-based abstraction is complex and hard to interpret
and an option’s behavior is learned entirely from scratch.
In order to gain some insight in how exactly options helped,
we ran option discovery
once

4

with

.
We then want to investigate how this set of learned options
tends to be used
across multiple runs
of learning an option-enabled policy.
In particular,
we want to investigate whether these options implemented generic

or whether they implemented more specific behavior
for a particular subtask
that the option-enabled agent could rely on at the right time.
In order to test this,
we trained an option-enabled agent
to completion
on the Craftworld task
depicted in

,
resulting in one final, ideally optimal trace.
We repeated this a number of times

For our investigation of option use

We extracted the stories for all final trajectories,
then randomly selected

The dominant story for this set of options
was

(\texttt{stone},\texttt{head},\texttt{wood},\texttt{handle},\texttt{hammer})
,
i.e.
crafting the hammer head first,
then the handle from scratch.

For each trajectory,
we looked at which option was in use at which time step.

5.3
The Lightworld Domain

The Lightworld domain was conceived by
49

who tailored it to demonstrate
their theory of

agent-space
representations.
We treat it here because of the similarity
with our work.
An
agent-space
representation
is quite loosely defined
as an abstraction that captures local information
centered on the agent.
An agent-space abstraction
should be engineered to be coarser than a fully Markov representation
for the same reason we employ coarse OPSRs:
to enable reusable behavior across a task.
In fact,
agent-spaces shine when the space is the same across tasks
so behavior can be reused across tasks.
From this description
outcome-predictive representations
are themselves
agent-space representations,
as they are by definition centered on the agent;
expected outcome sequences are based on action sequences
that always start from the agent’s current state.
Since agent-spaces are fully engineered,
predictive representations can be seen as a generalisation
that allows for a more principled
and even automatic construction
of agent-spaces.

A Lightworld task exists of
a number of rooms in succession.
Exiting the final room means success.
Aside from doors
a room can contain keys and locks.
Some rooms do not contain keys,
in which case the lock can be opened without them.
Keys are picked up by navigating over them
and employing a

pickup
action.
Locks require an

unlock
action.

Figure 17

We investigate discovered options

Evaluation: Transfer Between Subdomains

Figure 19

The Lightworld domain consists of tasks
made up of 2 to 5 rooms.
We could say that all tasks with the same number of rooms
belong to the same

subdomain
.
This raises the question:
how well does transfer work between different subdomains?
That is,
given demonstrated trajectories from one subdomain,
how well do the options learned from these trajectories
perform on tasks from another subdomain?

We have decided to look into this question
using options learned from tasks with 2 rooms,
evaluated on tasks with 3 to 5 rooms,
(
extra-subdomain
transfer).
This is more than simple transfer between subdomains;
a task with more rooms is decidedly more complicated
than its room-deficient cousins,
as can be seen from the results of the previous experiment
in Figure
18
.
This allows us to investigate
whether options learned in simpler tasks
perform in more complicated tasks.

Figure
19

shows the performances of

extra-
against
intra-subdomain
transfer.
The latter refers to the case where options are learned from tasks
with the same number of rooms.
It is clear that extra-subdomain transfer works:
options learned in simpler tasks are useful in more complicated tasks,
despite never having encountered one before.
What is more,
options learned from a simpler subdomain almost match the performance
of options learned within the same, more complicated subdomain,
as can be seen from comparing extra-subdomain with intra-subdomain transfer boxplots.

These results point at the interesting possibility of
curriculum learning
.
To solve a complicated task where one has access to simpler ones,
one could learn options in progressively harder tasks
in a
curriculum
fashion,
each set of options making the next subdomain easier to tackle.

Discussion of Related Work

6.1
Transfer Through State Abstraction

Transfer Setting

Transfer is hard
and generally not very well understood
in the context of reinforcement learning.
One indicator of this
is the lack of a common and concise language
for describing a transfer setting,
for example in terms of the allowed differences
between tasks.
Some papers
allow differences only in the reward dynamics
(e.g. goal location;

(
13
)
),
others only
changes in what might be referred to as

layout

while keeping
the (loosely termed)
dynamics

the same,
and others again
only slight changes in
the
dynamics

(e.g. actuator strength;

(
99
;
10
;
54
)
).
All of the above examples
are cast under the same nomer of

transfer

and it can be a considerable
undertaking to figure out the transfer
setting in a particular work.
We preface with this
because our area of transfer is a fairly general one,
with OPSRs working across
different transition dynamics, layout,
and even reward dynamics.

Underlying Transfer Method

All transfer
is by definition
the result of some mapping,
implicit or otherwise.
This paper has focused on transfer
through state abstraction,
where the mapping
is an equivalence relation
resulting from states sharing an abstraction
or between abstraction and ground states.
This mapping can be relaxed
–
many papers
achieve partial transfer
through similarity (rather than equivalence)
based on some
(often implicit) measure
(see also discussion in Section
2.2
).
We found that many papers that we will discuss shortly
rely on state abstraction
(or representation)
to achieve transfer
so we will not discuss
in detail
less common transfer
approaches based on other types of mapping
(e.g. the task mappings of
(
86
)
).
We also largely focus on transfer in model-free RL
rather than model-based RL

(
88
;
95
;
70
)
.

State Abstraction per se

Knowledge transfer in reinforcement learning
through state abstraction
is not a particularly new notion

(
7
)
,
relying on a long tradition of abstraction in planning

(
6
)
.
State abstraction tends to be seen
as state aggregation

(
57
)
,
as the removal of distinguishing features,
two states appearing the same as a result.
This traditional view still stands,
and can for example be found
in the more recent work of

5

on robot manipulation

(
5
)

and

3

on lifelong learning

(
3
;
1
)
.
Finally,

36

describe different types of abstraction
and stress that abstraction
is not merely about reducing data,
but also about focusing on relevant information

(
36
)
.

Agent-Space and Deictic Representations

Our state abstractions
that describe possible behaviors
hark back to the

deictic
(roughly meaning
“centered on the agent”
) representations
of
4
,
who realized
that
ego-centric
state representations
capture general concepts

(
4
)
.

49

picked up this idea again
and termed their
own agent-centric
state abstractions

agent-space representations

(
49
)
.
While agent-spaces are useful for transfer,
their problem lies with their manual engineering

(
50
)
.
Problems aside,
task-agnostic and sufficiently coarse,
agent-spaces perfectly capture what it takes to transfer beneficially.
It is for this reason that we think that so much related work
has implicitly opted for agent-space–like representations
that they ultimally owe their ability
for transfer to

(
21
;
33
)
.
Others have attempted to learn
shared representations
which inevitably must have commonalities
with agent-spaces

(
76
;
98
)
.

Successor Representations

While perhaps not its own category of representation,
successor representations
(originally by
(
28
)
)
deserve their own discussion
for the frenzy of related work that they sparked.
In the paper
that blew new life into the idea,

13

introduced

successor features
,
representations
(themselves learned on top
of a given agent-space–like representation)
that enabled transfer
between tasks with the same transition dynamics
but different reward dynamics
by learning a value function
independent of the task’s reward weighting

(
13
;
12
)
.
Since then,
the framework has been
insightfully compared to model-based RL

(
56
)
,
had its applicability for transfer extended

(
60
)
,
and been extended with options

(
74
;
11
)
.

Relational Representations

A traditional idea
that has recently gained more traction again
is that

relational representations

are required for generalisation

(
31
)
.
It has even been used to achieve
transferable options

(
26
)
.

42

a

is elementary for generalisation
in experiments not unlike our own.
This premise is for example explored
by

(
101
)

who implemented it in a deep learning context,
though the authors note that it is not yet fully understood
why relational representations afford generalisation.
Also relying on graph networks,

37

learned generalized policies
for relational MDPs,
expressing their hopes
for the future of relational representations.
We posit that all relational representations
are predictive in nature
and suspect that
they can be translated into our framework of outcomes.
In the example of the Box World of
(

)

where the agent needs to open a sequence of boxes with corresponding keys,
a high weight between a key and a box corresponds
to the
outcome
of the chest opening
after picking up the key
and walking up to the box.

Predictive Representations

There have been multiple avenues of work
pursuing predictive representations in RL.

on Predictive State Representations in

(
58
;

)

Based on the idea of

(

)
,
they represented a state

Though different from our setting

and even showed that there was a trade-off
in the expressivity of the representation

PSRs have even been extended with options

(

and combined with relational knowledge

(

)

PSRs found new life

In an originally different avenue of work,

Finally,

6.2
Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning

HRL per se

The main goal
currently under investigation in hierarchical reinforcement learning,
i.e. RL relying on action abstraction,
is to make longer and more complex tasks more tractable
by virtue of task decomposition
(be it through options, skills, abstract actions, macros, etc)

(
92
)
.
One way HRL delivers on this is
targeted exploration

(
44
;
45
)
.
Another way is more consistent, targeted action
that aims to reach subgoals,
an endeavour that has sparked a series of work
in
subgoal
discovery

(
59
;
68
;
40
;
46
;
92
)

and even

subgoal direction
discovery

(
90
;
66
)
.
While abstract actions
are usually combined temporally,
some work also exists on

composite actions
,
leading to a different kind of hierarchy

(
69
;
87
)
.

State Abstraction in HRL

One part of any HRL approach
is the state space that the abstract action operates over.
It is possible to simply use the original state space,
the same one the high-level agent uses

(
65
)
.
Alternatively,
end-to-end approaches
that learn both high-level
and low-level agents (abstract actions)
can simultaneously learn representations for
the abstract actions to operate over

(
9
;
90
)
.
These option-specific representations
can be seen as state abstractions.
In contrast with previous work,

66

learn the low-level representation from unsupervised objectives

(
66
)

This representation transfers to different tasks,
although this representation transfer does not result
in policy transfer.
Of special interest to us,
this work also investigates
sub-optimality of a representation
when used in a hierarchical setting.
Other approaches to
low-level state representations
include
grounding in language

(
43
)

and relying on concealed information

(
91
)
.

Transfer in HRL

Finally
there is the holy grail,
a long-standing dream for HRL:
reusing abstract actions in other tasks

(
15
)
.
Or,
in the common case where state abstraction is used
as the underlying method:
combining state abstraction and action abstraction

(
51
)
.
Most of the HRL approaches discussed so far
relied on task-specific representations
and as such did not transfer to new tasks.
The insight that state representation is the limiting factor
led to the early proposal
of
agent-space–based options

by

49

(
49
;
50
)
,
though their agent-spaces (task-agnostic representations)
were engineered
and their options partially pre-defined.
Recent approaches try to learn both a
task-agnostic state abstraction
and the transferable abstract action on top of that.
One such a line of enquiry
focuses on
object-centric representations

(
89
;
100
)

which are again reminiscent of agent-spaces.

64

achieve transfer of options by
manually introducing partial observability
(and therefore state aliasing through coarsening)
in state abstractions.
Others attempt to learn a suitable state representation
from a source domain
which tends to need adaptation in a target task

(
35
;
69
;
72
)
.

Outcome-predictive state representations
and the theory behind them
give an answer to the question
of what makes a state representation useful for transfer,
taking away the need to rely on agent-space–like engineering.
What’s more,
combined with options
they also give an answer
to the big question of how to
make HRL work for transfer
as we have demonstrated.
Finally,
work on learning transferable state representations for HRL
is very recent and we believe that
the insights around OPSRs
can guide this area of research in the right direction.

6.3
A Cognitive Perspective and Motivation

93

argue that much of complicated animal behavior
emerges from a complicated
interplay between animal and environment,
where often this behavior is attributed only
to the animal’s
“intelligence”
,
its cognitive functions.
This phenomenon of over-attribution
has been termed

causal spread

by

93
.
In this paper
we heed the caution of
causal spread

and advocate for a shift away from the
traditional agent that simply interfaces with the world
in favor
of an agent that is fully embedded in the world
(an
embodied, embedded mind
,
(
23
)
).
In the traditional cartesian view
the agent perceives its environment,
reconstructs an internal representation,
and only then leverages this representation for action

(
94
)
.
In contrast
we advocate for perception
that is heavily geared towards
(and guided by)
action primarily,
where perceiving an object for example
does not happen
in terms of objective properties
but rather in terms
of how to negotiate that object.

22

discusses a robot
which employs a map
that is encoded in terms of percepts and actions.
The map is prescriptive,
it describes locations in terms of which movements
would get the robot to different locations
and the robot has no planning module outside the map
– the map is itself the controller.

22

coined the term

action-oriented representations

to describe representations of this nature

(
22
, p. 47-51)
.
An action-oriented representation of an object,
instead of describing the object,
immediately encodes how to negotiate the object.
Closely related is
Gibson’s notion of
affordances
,
which are possible interactions
an organism can detect in its environment
relative to its own skills

(
38
)
.
In later work,

24

describes perception as fundamentally

predictive

(
24
)
,
delivering action-oriented representations
that,
once modulated,
prescribe how to act and respond
–
that is, representations of

affordances

(
25
)
.

This would be great:
both predictions
and affordances have been thought to be great basis for generalizability
because…

In our example of unlocking a door,
an action-oriented representation of a lock
is nothing so descriptive as

a brass rectangle with a keyhole
,
but perhaps as

fitting a key

–
the key itself
as
unlocking the door

or as
fitting the lock
.
Now,
how can an agent know in advance that a sequence of primitive actions
will lead to a complicated behavior such as opening a lock?
It needs to be capable of postulating goals.
Rather than the agent imagining complicated future states,
we follow the caution of causal spread
(of over-attributing intelligence to an agent),
and instead
we think it is the environment which signals
to the agent
when something has succeeded,
signals what is relevant to the agent’s subsequent behavior
and its goals.
When turning a key in its lock
we do not model
the ‘openness’ of the door,
be it as a percept or otherwise,
instead we simply anticipate
a click of the lock
or a jolt of the door.
Anticipating select signals
is far cheaper than complex internal modelling
and,
perhaps more importantly,
constitutes a kind of universal knowledge.
All locks click or jolt when opened,
their shapes or sizes matter not.

To formalize this
we introduce
outcomes
,
cues derived from transitions in the environment
that are important to the agent.
This is not to be taken too literally:
the environment does not actively signal the agent.
Instead the agent perceives outcomes
in transitions of the environment
because they are relevant to the agent.
Example outcomes are

“the key slides in the lock”

or

“the lock clicks open”
.
Outcomes are derived from the agent’s perception of these events.
They tend to signal the enabling of future behavior:
the key being in the lock allows turning the key,
the lock clicking allows one to successfully turn the door handle.
Importantly,
outcomes form a domain-wide language
which allows us to talk about state transitions
in a way that is completely independent of the underlying
task-specific state.
They capture what is really important
for a particular goal
and ignore what is extraneous.
They form the base of our abstractions.

To achieve an action-oriented representation
like that of

22

we represent states in terms of
the future outcomes they afford.
That is,
we represent states in terms of predictions of what outcomes follow,
following different short term behaviors.
To take our example,
there are a few
combinations of muscular movements
that would successfully
enter a key into a lock,
quite a few more than would result in missing the lock
and perhaps some that end in scratching the door.
Put together,
the representation of a key becomes the union
of all these predicted outcomes
following different behaviors.
We term this representation
an

Outcome-Predictive State Representation
(OPSR).
It ticks all the boxes
of the previous narrative:
an action-oriented, predictive representation
which captures affordances.

In this paper, we both argue and demonstrate that it is possible to leverage this representation
to learn transferable skills.
Two sets of locks and keys may visually look very different,
yet look very similar if we look at them in terms of predicted outcomes.
It is this aggregation
of distinct situations
under a shared representations
that makes OPSR
an abstraction.
This combined state and action abstraction

(
51
)

is key to this paper.

7
Conclusion and Future Work

This paper contains both theoretical and practical contributions
on transfer in Reinforcement Learning.
We first introduce a strong theoretical framework
based on state abstractions
that describes
the quality of a transferred policy
(
transfer value
)
in both single-
and multi-task settings.
Based on these insights
we propose a new state abstraction
based on predictions of
reward-relevant changes in the environment
(outcomes),
termed
Outcome-Predictive State Representation
(OPSR).
Since outcomes describe transitions
in a task-agnostic manner,
OPSRs equally form a task-agnostic state description
which naturally leads to transfer.
We show that OPSRs are particularly useful
because of their bottom-up construction
which automatically leads to

transfer optimality

in a class of MDPs which we term

plannable
.

Next,
we demonstrate how predicting fewer outcomes
coarsens the state abstraction,
leading to state aliasing and
increased opportunity for transfer.
While coarsening normally incurs a penalty in
transfer value
,
subtasks can offer the best of both worlds.
We introduce OPSR-based skills
that transfer between tasks
as a means to leverage
both state abstraction and action abstraction
to achieve transfer.
In a series of experiments
conducted with
a new library for transfer research
(VGDL 2.0),
we show that OPSR-based options
can be fully learned in one set of tasks
and subsequently speed up learning in
new and unseen tasks
with zero processing.

The practical investigation
in this paper
is a proof of concept
of the usefulness of OPSRs
that relies on two assumptions:
both
outcomes

and their predictions are available to the agent.
The next step left for future work
is to learn both of these.
The work in related areas
(as discussed in Section
6
)
and the observation that
outcomes are far more parsimonious goal representations
than fully concrete future states
make us confident in the feasability of this step.
In addition,
human brains appear to heavily limit predictions
according to needs and available actions

(
52
)
;
such limitation would greatly improve tractability.
Another extension is to realize
an agent that learns OPSR-based skills
in an online fashion
rather than offline after episodes.
This would allow the options
to not only help with transfer but also
solving complex tasks.
Finally,
it would be interesting to consider multiple levels
in the hierarchy of skills.
Instead of sequences of actions after which outcomes are predicted
we would have sequences of options,
resulting in a high-level OPSR
that enables transfer at the higher level
in addition to the lower level.

s
and
t
.
Formally,

\forall s,t\in\mathcal{S}:

\phi(s)=\phi(t)\;\Leftrightarrow\;\forall\delta\in\Pi_{\delta}(\phi,{M}):\delta(\cdot\mid s)=\delta(\cdot\mid t)

(32)

Proof

(
2.2
)
.

Let

{M}=\langle\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},p,r\rangle

be an MDP
and let

\phi:\mathcal{S}\mapsto\Phi

and

\phi^{\prime}:\mathcal{S}\mapsto\Phi^{\prime}

be two state abstractions.
We prove
(
7
)
by proving its inverse instead:

\begin{gathered}\phi^{\prime}\not\succeq\phi\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\Pi_{\delta}(\phi^{\prime},{M})\not\supseteq\Pi_{\delta}(\phi,{M})\end{gathered}

(33)

Or, more explictitly:

\begin{gathered}\exists\delta\in\Pi_{\delta}(\phi,{M}):\delta\notin\Pi_{\delta}(\phi^{\prime},{M})\\
\Updownarrow\\
\exists s,t\in\mathcal{S}:\phi(s)\neq\phi(t)\wedge\phi^{\prime}(s)=\phi^{\prime}(t)\end{gathered}

(34)

We proceed by proving both directions of the bidirectional
separately by contradiction.

(\bm{\Rightarrow})

It is given that

\exists\delta\in\Pi_{\delta}(\phi,{M}):\delta\notin\Pi_{\delta}(\phi^{\prime},{M})

(35)

which can be expanded to

\exists\delta\in\Pi_{\delta}(\phi,{M}):\forall\delta^{\prime}\in\Pi_{\delta}(\phi^{\prime},{M}):\exists s\in\mathcal{S}:\delta(\cdot\mid s)\neq\delta^{\prime}(\cdot\mid s)

(36)

We prove the forward implication
by contradiction.
Assume that

\nexists s,t\in\mathcal{S}:\phi(s)\neq\phi(t)\wedge\phi^{\prime}(s)=\phi^{\prime}(t)

(37)

This can be rewritten as

\forall s,t\in\mathcal{S}:\phi^{\prime}(s)=\phi^{\prime}(t)\Rightarrow\phi(s)=\phi(t)

(38)

The contrapositive yields a more useful perspective:

\forall s,t\in\mathcal{S}:\phi(s)\neq\phi(t)\Rightarrow\phi^{\prime}(s)\neq\phi^{\prime}(t)

(39)

In other words,
for every two states

s,t\in\mathcal{S}

that
\phi

maps to different abstract states,

\phi^{\prime}
will also map them to different states.
Every distinction that
\phi
makes,

\phi^{\prime}
also makes.
This means that for every
abstract policy

\pi_{\phi}
,
there exists
an equivalent
abstract policy

\pi_{\phi^{\prime}}

such that:

\pi_{\phi}(\cdot\mid s)=\pi_{\phi^{\prime}}(\cdot\mid s)\;\wedge\;\pi_{\phi}(\cdot\mid t)=\pi_{\phi^{\prime}}(\cdot\mid t)

(40)

By definition of derived policies,
for every derived policy

\delta_{\phi}\in\Pi_{\delta}(\phi,{M})
,
there is a matching derived policy

\delta_{\phi^{\prime}}\in\Pi_{\delta}(\phi^{\prime},{M})

such that

\delta_{\phi}(\cdot\mid s)=\delta_{\phi^{\prime}}(\cdot\mid s)\;\wedge\;\delta_{\phi}(\cdot\mid t)=\delta_{\phi^{\prime}}(\cdot\mid t)

(41)

This is in contradiction with the assumption
(
36
)
that there exists at least one
derived policy

\delta_{\phi}\in\Pi_{\delta}(\phi,{M})

that does not have an equivalent
derived policy

\delta_{\phi^{\prime}}\in\Pi_{\delta}(\phi^{\prime},{M})
.
The contradiction proves the forward implication.

(\bm{\Leftarrow})

It is given that

\begin{gathered}\exists s,t\in\mathcal{S}:\phi(s)\neq\phi(t)\wedge\phi^{\prime}(s)=\phi^{\prime}(t)\end{gathered}

(42)

To prove the reverse implication,
we proceed by contradiction.
Assume that

\forall\delta\in\Pi_{\delta}(\phi,{M}):\exists\delta^{\prime}\in\Pi_{\delta}(\phi^{\prime},{M}):\forall s\in\mathcal{S}:\delta(\cdot\mid s)=\delta^{\prime}(\cdot\mid s)

(43)

In other words,
for each derived policy

\delta_{\phi}\in\Pi_{\delta}(\phi,{M})

there exists an equivalent
derived policy

\delta_{\phi^{\prime}}\in\Pi_{\delta}(\phi^{\prime},{M})
.

From
(
42
)
we know that there
exists
an abstract policy
which assigns different distributions over actions
to abstract states

\phi(s)

and

\phi(t)
:

\pi_{\phi}(\cdot\mid\phi(s))\neq\pi_{\phi}(\cdot\mid\phi(t))
.
To contrast,
because

\phi^{\prime}(s)=\phi^{\prime}(t)

there can be no

\pi_{\phi^{\prime}}

such that

\pi_{\phi^{\prime}}(\cdot\mid\phi^{\prime}(s))\neq\pi_{\phi^{\prime}}(\cdot\mid\phi^{\prime}(t))
.
By the definition of derived policies,
we have

\displaystyle\exists\delta\in\Pi_{\delta}(\phi,{M})

\displaystyle:\delta(\cdot\mid s)\neq\delta(\cdot\mid t)

(44)

\displaystyle\forall\delta^{\prime}\in\Pi_{\delta}(\phi^{\prime},{M})

\displaystyle:\delta^{\prime}(\cdot\mid s)=\delta^{\prime}(\cdot\mid t)

(45)

That is,
it is possible
that

\delta^{\prime}(\cdot\mid s)=\delta(\cdot\mid s)

or

\delta^{\prime}(\cdot\mid t)=\delta(\cdot\mid t)
,
but never both.
This contradicts the assumption
in
(
43
),
proving the reverse implication
and completing the proof for the biconditional in
(
7
).

∎

Theorem
2.3
.

Let

\phi:\mathcal{S}\mapsto\Phi

and

\phi^{\prime}:\mathcal{S}\mapsto\Phi^{\prime}

be two state abstractions
such that

\phi

has

strictly higher transfer value than

\phi^{\prime}

for some MDP

{M}\mathrel{\doteq}\langle\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},p,r\rangle,\gamma
.
Then there must
exist two states

s,t\in\mathcal{S}

such that

\phi^{\prime}
does not distinguish between them
while
\phi
does,
and there exists a derived policy for

\phi

that takes advantage of this distinction
by controlling
s
and
t
differently
in a way that no derived policy for
\phi^{\prime}
can do.
By giving up representational capacity
and increasing potential for transfer,

\phi^{\prime}
sacrifices transfer value.
Formally:

\displaystyle v_{\phi}({M})>v_{\phi^{\prime}}({M})

\displaystyle\Downarrow

\displaystyle\exists s,t\in\mathcal{S}:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &1.\;\phi(s)\neq\phi(t)\\
&2.\;\phi^{\prime}(s)=\phi^{\prime}(t)\\
&3.\;\exists\delta\in\Pi_{\delta}(\phi,{M}):\delta(\cdot\mid s)\neq\delta(\cdot\mid t)\\
&4.\;\forall\delta^{\prime}\in\Pi_{\delta}(\phi^{\prime},{M}):\delta^{\prime}(\cdot\mid s)=\delta^{\prime}(\cdot\mid t)\end{aligned}

Proof

(
2.3
)
.

Let

{M}=\langle\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},p,r\rangle

be an MDP
and

\phi:\mathcal{S}\mapsto\Phi

and

\phi^{\prime}:\mathcal{S}\mapsto\Phi^{\prime}

be two state abstractions.
In addition we have:

\exists\delta\in\Pi_{\delta}(\phi,{M}):\forall\delta^{\prime}\in\Pi_{\delta}(\phi^{\prime},{M}):v_{\delta}>v_{\delta^{\prime}}

(46)

For simplicity we will assume deterministic policies in this proof.
Instead of distributions over actions,
a policy
\pi:\mathcal{S}\mapsto\mathcal{A}
simple maps a state to an action.
The extension to stochastic policies is straightforward.

First,
we show that there must exist a state
s\in\mathcal{S}

for which there is a derived policy for
\phi

that takes a different action from every derived policy for
\phi^{\prime}
.
By (
46
)
and the definition for inequality of value functions
we have

\exists\delta\in\Pi_{\delta}(\phi,{M}):\forall\delta^{\prime}\in\Pi_{\delta}(\phi^{\prime},{M}):\exists s\in\mathcal{S}:

v_{\delta}(s)>v_{\delta^{\prime}}(s)\iff\\
r(s,\delta(s))+\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{s^{\prime}\sim p(s,\delta(s))}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\gamma v_{\delta}(s^{\prime})}}\right]>r(s,\delta^{\prime}(s))+\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{s^{\prime}\sim p(s,\delta^{\prime}(s))}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\gamma v_{\delta^{\prime}}(s^{\prime})}}\right]\qquad

(47)

If
\delta(s)=\delta^{\prime}(s)
,
the inequality in (
47
)
can only hold if

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{s^{\prime}\sim p(s,\delta(s))}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\gamma v_{\delta}(s^{\prime})}}\right]>\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{s^{\prime}\sim p(s,\delta^{\prime}(s))}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\gamma v_{\delta^{\prime}}(s^{\prime})}}\right]

(48)

Hence, either the actions at
s
are different or inequality
(
48
) also holds. The inequality
(
48
)
can only hold if there is some other state

s^{\dagger}

for which the inequality in (
47
)
holds
by means of
\delta(s^{\dagger})\neq\delta^{\prime}(s^{\dagger})

or that there is some other state,
s^{\dagger\dagger}
, reachable in one step from
s^{\dagger}
, for which inequality (
47
) also holds.
By a process of induction, if Equation (
47
) holds for
s
there must exist some state, reachable from
s
in a finite number of steps under
\delta
(and
\delta^{\prime}
) for which the actions under these two policies differ.
In short,
there exists a state
s

where
\delta(s)\neq\delta^{\prime}(s)
.
We have:

\begin{gathered}\exists\delta\in\Pi_{\delta}(\phi,{M}):\forall\delta^{\prime}\in\Pi_{\delta}(\phi^{\prime},{M}):\exists s\in\mathcal{S}:\delta(s)\neq\delta^{\prime}(s)\\
\Updownarrow\\
\exists\delta\in\Pi_{\delta}(\phi,{M}):\nexists\delta^{\prime}\in\Pi_{\delta}(\phi^{\prime},{M}):\forall s\in\mathcal{S}:\delta(s)=\delta^{\prime}(s)\\
\Updownarrow\\
\exists\delta\in\Pi_{\delta}(\phi,{M}):\nexists\delta^{\prime}\in\Pi_{\delta}(\phi^{\prime},{M}):\delta=\delta^{\prime}\\
\Updownarrow\\
\exists\delta\in\Pi_{\delta}(\phi,{M}):\delta\notin\Pi_{\delta}(\phi^{\prime},{M})\end{gathered}

(49)

By
Proposition
2.2

we now have that
there do exists at least two states

s,t\in\mathcal{S}

such that

\phi(s)\neq\phi(t)\wedge\phi^{\prime}(s)=\phi^{\prime}(t)

(50)

This proves implications (1) and (2) of the theorem.
By repeated invocation of
Proposition
2.1
,
implications (3) and (4) follow naturally
from implications (1) and (2) respectively.

.

Theorem
3.1
.

Let

{M}=\langle\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},p,r\rangle

be an MDP
with outcome function

\sigma
.
The

expected outcome sequence

for state
s\in\mathcal{S}

and action sequence

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle a}}\right\rangle_{=}(a_{1}\dots a_{n})\in\mathcal{A}^{n}

can also be written as the sequence of
expected outcomes after action sequences

(a_{1}\dots a_{i})
for

i
ranging from
1
to
n
.
Formally:

{\langle\sigma\rangle}(s,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle a}}\right\rangle_{)}=\big(\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{p}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sigma(S_{t},A_{t},S_{t+1})\mid S_{t}=s,A_{t}=a_{1}}}\right],\dots,\\
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{p}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sigma(S_{t+n-1},A_{t+n-1},S_{t+n})\mid S_{t}=s,A_{t}=a_{1},\dots,A_{t+n-1}=a_{n}}}\right]\big)

(54)

Proof

(
3.1
)
.

Since
\sigma(s,a,s^{\prime})
is defined as an expectation,
we have:

\sigma(s,a,s^{\prime})=\int_{\Sigma}p_{\sigma}(\sigma_{i}\mid s,a,s^{\prime})(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{n})d(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{n})

(55)

Since
c(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{n})=(c\sigma_{1},\dots,c\sigma_{n})

and the integral of sequences in
Eq. (
16
)
is a sequence of integrals, we can look at each element of the resulting
sequence in turn.
Consider the element in the resulting sequence at position

i
, with
1\leq i\leq n
:

\int_{\Sigma}p_{\sigma}((\sigma_{1},...,\sigma_{n})\mid s_{t},\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle a}}\right\rangle_{)}\sigma_{i}d^{n}(\sigma_{1},...,\sigma_{n})

(56)

Writing
p_{\sigma}((\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{n})\mid s_{t},\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle a}}\right\rangle_{)}
in full, this becomes:

\begin{split}\int_{\Sigma}\sum_{s_{t+1},...,s_{t+n}\in\mathcal{S}^{n}}p(s_{t+1}\mid s_{t},a_{1})\dots p(s_{t+n}\mid s_{t+n-1},a_{n})\\
\quad p_{\sigma}(\sigma_{1}\mid s_{t},a_{1},s_{t+1})\dots&p_{\sigma}(\sigma_{n}\mid s_{t+n-1},a_{n},s_{t+n})\sigma_{i}d^{n}(\sigma_{1},...,\sigma_{n})\end{split}

Since
p_{\sigma}

is a probability density function
conditioned on a transition
(s,a,s^{\prime})
,
we have

\int_{\Sigma}p_{\sigma}(\sigma_{j}\mid s,a,s^{\prime})d\sigma_{j}=1
.
This allows for further simplification:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\sum_{s_{t+1},...,s_{t+n}\in\mathcal{S}^{n}}p(s_{t+1}\mid s_{t},a_{1})\dots p(s_{t+n}\mid s_{t+n-1},a_{n})\int_{\Sigma}p_{\sigma}(\sigma_{i}\mid s_{t+i-1},a_{i},s_{t+i})\\
\int_{\Sigma}p_{\sigma}(\sigma_{1}\mid s_{t},a_{1},s_{t+1})\dots p_{\sigma}(\sigma_{i-1}\mid s_{t+i-2},a_{i-1},s_{t+i-1})\\
p_{\sigma}(\sigma_{i+1}\mid s_{t+i},a_{i+1},s_{t+i+1})p_{\sigma}(\sigma_{n}\mid s_{t+n-1},a_{n},s_{t+n})\sigma_{i}d^{n}(\sigma_{1},...,\sigma_{n})\end{split}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s_{t+1},...,s_{t+n}\in\mathcal{S}^{n}}p(s_{t+1}\mid s_{t},a_{1})\dots p(s_{t+n}\mid s_{t+n-1},a_{n})\int_{\Sigma}p_{\sigma}(\sigma_{i}\mid s_{t+i-1},a_{i},s_{t+i})\sigma_{i}d\sigma_{i}

Combining this with
Eq. (
55
)
and the fact that for a given start state
s
and action
a
,
all transition probabilities must sum up to
1
,
i.e.
\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}p(s^{\prime}\mid s,a)=1
,
this further simplifies to:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&=\sum_{s_{t+1},...,s_{t+n}\in\mathcal{S}^{n}}p(s_{t+1}\mid s_{t},a_{1})\dots p(s_{t+n}\mid s_{t+n-1},a_{n})\sigma(s_{t+i-1},a_{i},s_{t+i})\end{split}

\displaystyle\begin{split}&=\sum_{s_{t+1},...,s_{t+i}\in\mathcal{S}^{i}}p(s_{t+1}\mid s_{t},a_{1})\dots p(s_{t+i}\mid s_{t+i-1},a_{i})\sigma(s_{t+i-1},a_{i},s_{t+i})\\
&\qquad\sum_{s_{t+i+1},...,s_{t+n}\in\mathcal{S}^{n-i}}p(s_{t+i+1}\mid s_{t+i},a_{i+1})\dots p(s_{t+n}\mid s_{t+n-1},a_{n})\end{split}

\displaystyle\begin{split}&=\sum_{s_{t+1},...,s_{t+i}\in\mathcal{S}^{i}}p(s_{t+1}\mid s_{t},a_{1})\dots p(s_{t+i}\mid s_{t+i-1},a_{i})\sigma(s_{t+i-1},a_{i},s_{t+i})\end{split}

This is exactly the expected value of the outcomes
starting from state
s_{t}

after actions
a_{1},\dots,a_{i}
,
i.e.

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{p}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sigma(S_{t+i-1},A_{t+i},S_{t+i})\mid S_{t}=s_{t},A_{t}=a_{1},\dots,A_{t+i}=a_{i}}}\right]
.
Putting this together for all

i
such that
1\leq i\leq n

results in the sequence of expectations
in
Eq. (
54
),
the object of this proof.

Theorem
3.5
.

Let

{M}_{\alpha}=\langle\mathcal{S}_{\alpha},\mathcal{A},p_{\alpha},r_{\alpha},\gamma\rangle

and

{M}_{\beta}=\langle\mathcal{S}_{\beta},\mathcal{A},p_{\beta},r_{\beta},\gamma\rangle

be two MDPs
with respective outcome functions

\sigma_{\alpha}
and
\sigma_{\beta}

and reward weightings

w_{r_{\alpha}}
and
w_{r_{\beta}}

such that

r_{\alpha}=\sigma_{\alpha}^{\top}w_{r_{\alpha}}

and

r_{\beta}=\sigma_{\beta}^{\top}w_{r_{\beta}}
,
and
let

\phi_{\alpha}:\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}\mapsto\Phi_{\alpha}

and

\phi_{\beta}:\mathcal{S}_{\beta}\mapsto\Phi_{\beta}

be two

outcome equivalent
state abstractions.
Then all abstract states
in
{\Phi_{\alpha}}

are outcome equivalent with their ground states
with respect to
any abstract MDP

{M}_{\phi_{\alpha}}=\langle{\Phi_{\alpha}},\mathcal{A},p_{\phi_{\alpha}},r_{\phi_{\beta}},\gamma\rangle

created from
{{M}_{\alpha}}
and
{\phi_{\alpha}}

with outcome function

\sigma_{\phi_{\alpha}}

and reward weighting

w_{r_{\phi_{\alpha}}}

such that

r_{\phi_{\alpha}}=\sigma_{\phi_{\alpha}}^{\top}w_{r_{\phi_{\alpha}}}
,
given that the reward weighting
for that abstract MDP is the same
as those of the ground MDPs,
i.e.

w_{r_{\phi_{\alpha}}}=w_{r_{\beta}}=w_{r_{\alpha}}
.
Formally,

\forall\bar{\phi}\in{\Phi_{\alpha}},\forall s_{\beta}\in{\phi_{\beta}}^{-1}(\bar{\phi})
:

{\langle\sigma\rangle}_{\phi_{\alpha}}(\bar{\phi},\mathcal{A}^{\infty})={\langle\sigma\rangle_{\beta}}(s_{\beta},\mathcal{A}^{\infty})

(57)

Proof

(
3.5
)
.

For this proof, we assume for simplicity
that the abstract state set
{\Phi_{\alpha}}
is finite.
We proceed by a kind of
backward
induction,
as will become clearer in the induction hypothesis.
For the hypothesis we will assume that the ‘next’ abstract states
are outcome equivalent with their ground states.

Base case

Since this is a proof by backward induction,
we start at the end.
The base case is a final absorbing state

\bar{\phi}\in{\Phi_{\alpha}}
.
All ground states
s_{\beta}\in\bar{\phi}

must also be final.
Just like there can be no reward past the
final state,
there can be no outcomes past the final state either.
Since both
\bar{\phi}

and all
s_{\beta}\in\bar{\phi}

are final states,
they must be outcome equivalent.

Induction hypothesis

We assume that all ‘next’ abstract states

\bar{\phi}_{t+1}\in{\Phi_{\alpha}}

are outcome equivalent with their ground states.
Let
\bar{\Phi}_{t}
be the random variable describing the abstract state
at time
t
.
Then it holds that for all states

s_{t+1}\in{\phi_{\beta}^{-1}}(\bar{\phi}_{t+1})
:

\forall n\in\mathbb{N}_{1}
:

\forall(a_{2},\dots,a_{n})\in\mathcal{A}^{n-1}
:

\begin{split}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{p_{\phi_{\alpha}}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sigma(\bar{\Phi}_{t+n-1},A_{t+n},\bar{\Phi}_{t+n})\mid\bar{\Phi}_{t+1}=\bar{\phi}_{t+1},A_{t+2}=a_{2},\dots,A_{t+n}=a_{n}}}\right]\\
=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{p_{\beta}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sigma(S_{t+n-1},A_{t+n-1},S_{t+n})\mid S_{t+1}=s_{t+1},A_{t+2}=a_{2},\dots,A_{t+n}=a_{n}}}\right]\end{split}

(58)

Inductive step

We now take a step ‘backward’ and consider
the abstract state

\bar{\phi}_{t}
,
direct predecessor to states

\bar{\phi}_{t+1}
.
We need to show that
\bar{\phi}_{t}
is outcome equivalent with its ground states.

First we consider the single-step expected outcomes separately
for any single action
a\in\mathcal{A}
.
From the construction of the abstract reward dynamics

r_{\phi_{\alpha}}

we can derive two identities.
The first is based on the definition of
the expected outcomes.

\displaystyle r_{\phi_{\alpha}}(\bar{\phi}_{t},a)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bar{\phi}_{t+1}\in{\Phi_{\alpha}}}p_{\phi_{\alpha}}(\bar{\phi}_{t+1}\mid\bar{\phi},a)\sigma(\bar{\phi}_{t},a,\bar{\phi}_{t+1})^{\top}w_{r_{\phi_{\alpha}}}

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{p_{\phi_{\alpha}}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sigma(\bar{\Phi}_{t},A_{t},\bar{\Phi}_{t+1})\mid\bar{\Phi}_{t}=\bar{\phi}_{t},A_{t}=a}}\right]^{\top}w_{r_{\phi_{\alpha}}}

(59)

The second is based on the construction
of
r_{\phi_{\alpha}}

from ground reward dynamics
r_{\alpha}

with the help of a weighting function

w_{\phi_{\alpha}}:\mathcal{S}\mapsto[0,1]
(see definition abstract MDP),
which disappears
because all ground states
s_{\alpha}\in{\phi_{\alpha}^{-1}}(\bar{\phi}_{t})

are outcome equivalent and therefore have
the same expected
outcome for any action
a
.

\displaystyle r_{\phi_{\alpha}}(\bar{\phi}_{t},a)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s_{\alpha}\in{\phi_{\alpha}^{-1}}(\bar{\phi}_{t})}\sum_{s_{\alpha}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}}}w_{{\phi_{\alpha}}}(s_{\alpha})p_{\alpha}(s_{\alpha}^{\prime}\mid s_{\alpha},a)\sigma_{\alpha}(s_{\alpha},a,s_{\alpha}^{\prime})^{\top}w_{r_{\alpha}}
