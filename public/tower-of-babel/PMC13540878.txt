Analysis of the impact of altitude changes on human physiological indicators and response capabilities
Authors: Jia Haijiang, Wang Xiaodong, Hu Bin, Chen Mengyao, Ming Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHypoxic environments represent a core stress factor affecting human physiological functions and cognitive performance under high-altitude exposure. This study utilized an environmental experiment chamber to replicate varying altitude conditions, tracking and recording parameters such as blood pressure, Blood oxygen saturation, heart rate, and cognitive response capabilities in test subjects, while evaluating the interrelationships and mutual influences among these indicators. Results demonstrated that young populations exhibit stronger adaptability to high-altitude hypoxia; no significant linear correlation was observed between age and reaction time, although age-related differences were noted in auditory response time. Blood oxygen saturation showed no significant correlation with reaction time, suggesting that physiological compensations can maintain response speed. Heart rate exhibited a weak positive correlation with systolic blood pressure (r = 0.378), with synergistic changes enhancing reaction accuracy by 12%. Discrimination reaction time significantly prolonged when Blood oxygen saturation < 85% and heart rate > 110 beats per minute. A fatigue early warning method based on key physiological indicators and reaction time data, along with corresponding threshold values, was proposed. This provides actionable quantitative evidence for real-time monitoring and risk assessment of fatigue status in high-altitude work environments, facilitating scientifically guided rest scheduling and workload regulation to improve safety standards and operational efficiency in high-altitude operations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-56813-x.Keywords: High altitude, Hypoxia, Cognitive function, Reaction time, SpO2, FatigueSubject terms: Neuroscience, Physiology

Introduction

High-altitude environments exert extensive impacts on human physiological functions and cognitive abilities due to decreased atmospheric pressure and oxygen partial pressure. Compared to low-altitude airports, high-altitude airports face greater urgency in risk management of human factors, as their unique low-pressure, hypoxic, and cold environments significantly increase error rates among staff, thereby exacerbating operational safety risks. Studies indicate that the hypobaric and hypoxic environment at an altitude of 3,600 m can induce a series of physiological responses, including adaptations in pulmonary ventilation, hemodynamics, and cardiovascular systems1. Blood oxygen saturation (SpO2) and alveolar oxygen partial pressure (PaO2), as key indicators for assessing altitude sickness, exhibit dynamic changes closely correlated with the degree of hypoxia and adaptive status. Medical research by Ye Chaoliang et al. confirmed that normal arterial blood oxygen saturation ranges between 93% and 98%, with values below 93% classified as hypoxemia. When SpO2 drops to 85%, it may lead to impaired cognitive concentration and reduced muscle fine motor coordination; at 75%, misjudgment and muscle dysfunction occur; and at 60%, loss of consciousness may ensue2. Wei Yi’s study on physical fitness testing in military and police academies revealed that performance in rapid-response and coordination tasks (e.g., 4 × 10-meter sprint) significantly declines under hypoxic conditions (P < 0.001), indicating specific interference with temporal control of complex movements3. Hypoxic environments impair neuromuscular function through multiple mechanisms, including reduced motor neuron firing frequency, decreased muscle fiber contraction velocity, and disrupted sensory feedback integration, ultimately manifesting as prolonged reaction times and reduced movement precision.

From a physiological perspective, hypoxia exerts multidimensional effects on the human body. Beyond the nervous system, individuals transitioning from lowland to high-altitude environments often experience increased heart rate, blood pressure fluctuations, and decreased blood oxygen saturation. When blood oxygen saturation falls below 92%, the body exhibits significant hypoxic responses4–6. The decline in blood oxygen saturation can trigger reflexive reactions such as increased respiratory rate and elevated maximum expiratory volume to mitigate the downward trend7. This process further accelerates heart rate in high-altitude individuals, induces sympathetic nervous system excitation, and subsequently impairs cognitive function—representing just one potential factor contributing to cognitive impairment in high-altitude environments8,9. During the initial phase of high-altitude exposure, hypoxic conditions primarily manifest as diminished auditory and visual perception, reduced attention span and attentional shift capacity, impaired short-term memory, as well as decreased complex reasoning ability and cognitive flexibility10,11.

The effects of hypoxic conditions in high-altitude environments on human brain function encompass multiple aspects, including emotional regulation, reflex activities, perceptual and memory capacities, as well as motor coordination and accuracy. In high-altitude settings, higher elevations correlate with more pronounced low atmospheric pressure and hypoxia, leading to significant declines in both cognitive and physical performance. Hypoxic environments impair cognitive abilities, emotional states, and working memory12,13, increasing operational vulnerability and posing risks to flight safety. However, systematic quantitative analyses linking overall physiological indicators with response capabilities remain scarce. With the continuous growth of air traffic volume, the relationship between controller fatigue and air traffic safety has become increasingly critical. The U.S. Aviation Safety Report indicates that 21% of flight safety incidents are attributed to controller fatigue14. The International Civil Aviation Organization (ICAO) further notes that fatigue, induced by irregular work schedules or excessive workload, compromises controllers ‘attention, memory, and decision-making abilities during cognitive processes, thereby increasing the likelihood of accidents15. Fatigue-induced cognitive impairment is a primary cause of aviation safety incidents; thus, investigating controllers’ cognitive performance under fatigue conditions holds significant practical implications for enhancing air traffic safety16. This study focuses on airport tower controllers to examine the evolution of physiological indicators and response capabilities across different altitudes, aiming to identify key metrics for fatigue detection.

Experimental design

This study simulated environments at three altitude levels: 114 m, 2950 m, and 3650 m. Cognitive performance tests (including auditory reaction time, visual reaction time, and discrimination reaction time) and flight control task simulations were conducted under each altitude condition. Blood pressure, oxygen saturation, and heart rate were continuously monitored and automatically recorded throughout the experiment. The altitude elevation protocol was implemented as follows: ascending from 114 m to 2950 m took approximately 12–20 min, while the ascent from 2950 m to 3650 m required about 7 min. Each altitude-specific test lasted approximately 1 h, with total in-cabin exposure duration averaging 3.5 h per participant.The altitude elevation protocol is designed based on a well-established low-pressure chamber exposure experiment paradigm and is widely employed in hypoxic physiology research. The altitude selection considers typical airport elevations (Ningxia Airport: 2,950 m; Lhasa Airport: 3,650 m).

1) Test Conditions: The experiment simulated the actual operational environment of a plateau airport, with air traffic controllers serving as key test subjects. The experimental setup utilized an emergency rescue training simulation system capable of replicating extreme environmental conditions including high-altitude environments, cold climates, and confined spaces. It accurately simulated temperature ranges from-20 °C to 70 °C, covering both extreme cold and scorching heat scenarios. The system recreated hypoxic conditions at an altitude of 5,300 m (atmospheric pressure ranging from 50 kPa to 101.3 kPa) while precisely maintaining oxygen concentration between 13% and 21%. It supported multi-environmental coupled testing including extreme temperatures, oxygen deprivation, wind/snow conditions, and solar radiation exposure to evaluate life safety, operational efficiency, and adaptability under extreme conditions. Test Equipment: During experiments, wrist-worn pulse oximeters collected heart rate (HR), blood oxygen saturation (SpO2), and blood pressure data, with each device outputting HR and SpO2 readings per second. An audio-visual reaction time tester was employed to measure simple auditory reaction time, simple visual reaction time, and audio-visual discrimination reaction time. The system featured data storage, average value calculation display, and error feedback functions, providing professional and precise data support for testing.

Analysis of key physiological indicators

The plateau environment is characterized by low atmospheric pressure and low oxygen partial pressure, which can trigger a series of physiological compensatory responses in humans, including adaptive adjustments in the cardiovascular and respiratory systems. Blood pressure, blood oxygen saturation, and heart rate serve as critical indicators reflecting the body’s adaptability to high-altitude conditions.The gradual decline in SpO₂ across different altitude gradients can be explained by the hypoxic ventilation response (HVR) theory, which describes a physiological feedback loop triggered by arterial hypoxemia. As altitude increases, alveolar oxygen partial pressure decreases, and peripheral chemoreceptors stimulate respiratory drive to maintain adequate oxygenation17,18.By comparing physiological parameter changes at different altitudes, this study analyzes the impact of altitude gradients on human physiological functions and establishes standardized data quality control procedures and outlier handling protocols, providing methodological references for related research.

In this study, the Kruskal-Wallis H-test was employed for statistical analysis. After merging observed data across all groups, the values were uniformly ranked by numerical magnitude, with equal ranks corrected using average rank order. The rank sum for each group was then calculated to construct the H-statistic, which tested whether the overall medians were equal across groups. When data contained a high number of ties, the H-statistic was adjusted to enhance the accuracy of the test results. All statistical tests were conducted as two-tailed tests with an alpha level of 0.05. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for differences between groups. If the overall test indicated significant differences, the Dunn test was further applied for pairwise post-hoc comparisons to identify specific groups exhibiting differences, thereby controlling for Type I errors in multiple comparisons.

5) Adaptation time requirements: A longer duration is required to establish effective physiological adaptation at an altitude of 3,650 m; significant individual variability exists, with the greatest response differences observed at this altitude, which may involve genetic and physiological factors.

Correlation analysis

The Pearson correlation coefficient is used to quantify the strength of the linear relationship between variables.

The Pearson correlation coefficient (denoted as r) is a statistical measure of the degree of linear correlation between two continuous variables, with a value range of [− 1, 1].

r = 1: Perfect positive correlation (the variables exhibit identical trends);

r = − 1: Completely negative correlation (the variable trends are entirely opposite);

r = 0: No linear correlation (no significant linear relationship between variables).

This analysis employed the Pearson correlation coefficient as the core methodology, combined with statistical significance testing and multidimensional visualization, to comprehensively quantify the linear associations among blood oxygen saturation, heart rate, and blood pressure, while revealing the impact patterns of altitude and experimental interventions on these correlations.

As can be clearly seen from Table 6. Systolic blood pressure and diastolic blood pressure exhibit a highly positive correlation (r = 0.721, p < 0.001), with highly consistent trends in their changes, making them core indicators for blood pressure assessment. Heart rate shows a weak positive correlation with blood pressure (r = 0.266), where increased heart rate is accompanied by elevated blood pressure, consistent with the physiological regulation of cardiovascular systems.

Correlation discussion

Relationship between age and blood pressure, blood oxygen saturation, and heart rate at different altitudes

For the 20–25 age group, individuals demonstrated enhanced physiological adaptability at an altitude of 3,650 m, specifically manifested through faster blood oxygen saturation recovery rates (a 0.9% rebound after Experiment 2) and relatively smaller heart rate variability (standard deviation < 15 beats/min). In contrast, individuals over 30 years old exhibited poorer blood oxygen saturation stability (40% of cases showed > 5% fluctuation range) and more pronounced systolic blood pressure elevation (8–10 mmHg higher than the youth group), suggesting the need for specialized health management strategies for high-altitude work in this population. Heart rate data further revealed a clear upward trend with altitude increase [from (79.5 ± X) beats/min at 114 m to (95.6 ± X) beats/min at 3,650 m], which continues the aforementioned pattern and corroborates the impact of age factors on cardiac compensatory function.

The age-stratified differences in SpO₂ stability and heart rate response observed in Table 12 are consistent with prior research on age and altitude acclimatization. Muza et al.19 demonstrated that older adults exhibit reduced ventilatory chemosensitivity and slower physiological adaptation to hypobaric hypoxia, leading to greater SpO₂ variability during acute exposure. Similarly, Erzurum et al.20 found age-related declines in cardiovascular adaptive responses to chronic hypoxia, which may explain the more pronounced heart rate elevations observed in older participants in this study. Relationship between age, education level, and reaction time

Age and Reaction Time: The analysis revealed no significant linear correlation between age and any of the three reaction times. However, there were significant differences in auditory reaction time across age groups (p = 0.0226), with the 25–29-year-old age group demonstrating the optimal reaction time performance.

Educational background and reaction time: Educational background had no significant effect on any of the three reaction times (p > 0.05). However, the auditory reaction time showed a near-significant difference between educational groups (p = 0.0542).

Main influencing factors: Reaction time is primarily affected by altitude, while the influence of age and educational background is relatively limited.

Practical significance: In the selection process of plateau workers, age and educational background should not be used as primary indicators for assessing reaction time. Instead, the focus should be on monitoring changes in reaction time under altitude conditions of 3,650 m.

Based on reaction stability, the test subjects were classified into the following three categories:

High stability type (3 individuals): Numbers 3, 7, and 12. These individuals exhibit a reaction time fluctuation amplitude of less than 15% across various altitude conditions, with no more than one erroneous response, demonstrating strong adaptability.

Moderately stable type (12 individuals): Numbers 1, 2, 4, 5, 6, 8, 9, 10,11,13,14,15. The reaction time fluctuation range was between 15% and 30%, with 1 to 3 erroneous responses, indicating moderate adaptability.

Low stability type (4 individuals): Codes 16,17,18,19. Reaction time fluctuation amplitude exceeds 30%, with no fewer than 3 incorrect responses, indicating relatively weak adaptability.

The core influencing factors of individual differences include: Altitude adaptation capacity – High-stability individuals exhibit only a 5% to 10% increase in reaction time at 3,650 m altitude compared to 114 m elevation, demonstrating faster adaptation rates; Cognitive and motor coordination – Low-stability individuals generally show poor synchronization between discriminative responses and motor responses, with correlation coefficients below 0.1; Attention maintenance – Medium-stability individuals experience an increase of 1–2 erroneous responses during the later testing phase (at 3,650 m altitude), indicating attention fatigue effects.

Relationship between physiologically important indicators and reaction time

Based on existing experimental data, blood oxygen saturation exhibited a significant downward trend with increasing altitude (from 94.8% to 91.2%), but no significant correlation was observed between oxygen saturation and reaction time. At an altitude of 2,950 m, a significant positive correlation was found between heart rate and discrimination reaction time (r = 0.553), suggesting that increased heart rate may be accompanied by a decline in cognitive processing speed. Systolic blood pressure showed weak negative correlations with both light response time and discrimination reaction time, though these correlations did not reach statistical significance. Overall, physiological indicators demonstrated certain associations with reaction capabilities, but the correlations remained relatively weak.The relationship between elevated heart rate (physiological arousal) and prolonged discrimination reaction time observed in this study aligns with the Yerkes-Dodson Law (1908) and its contemporary extensions. This theory posits an inverted U-shaped relationship between arousal and cognitive performance: moderate arousal improves efficiency, while excessive arousal impairs attentional resources and decision-making speed21. The observed reaction time degradation at high altitude likely reflects a shift toward the “over-arousal” end of this continuum. The specific data can be obtained from Table 13.

Fatigue warning

Analysis of physiological indicators and reaction times using curve methodology revealed that Blood oxygen saturation is the most outstanding independent early warning indicator, with an AUC of 0.971 (nearly perfect); sensitivity = 88.6%, specificity = 100%; statistical significance: p = 0.004. Heart rate serves as an effective auxiliary independent indicator, with an AUC of 0.682, sensitivity = 62.9%, specificity = 68.4%, and statistical significance: p = 0.038 (*). Blood pressure indicators demonstrate poorer early warning efficacy, with systolic blood pressure showing an AUC of 0.466 and diastolic blood pressure lacking statistical significance due to significant individual variability influenced by factors such as age and underlying diseases. Reaction time indicators are unsuitable as independent early warning metrics; all reaction time indicators exhibited AUC values < 0.5 (below the random level), with auditory reaction time AUC = 0.465 and visual reaction time AUC = 0.372, demonstrating substantial individual differences and being severely affected by training level and attention factors, all of which showed no statistical significance (p > 0.05). Warning Threshold Standards.

1) Single-parameter alert system, utilizing only blood oxygen saturation for early warning. Warning protocol: SpO₂ <95% → yellow alert; SpO₂ <87% → red alert.

2) Dual-parameter combined early warning system: simultaneous monitoring of blood oxygen saturation (SpO₂) and heart rate (HR). SpO₂ normal + HR normal → normal; SpO₂ mildly decreased + HR normal → mild early warning; SpO₂ significantly decreased + HR elevated → moderate/severe early warning.

3) Multi - index comprehensive early warning system employing a composite scoring model: Considering that this research encompasses three distinct reaction time indicators, namely auditory response time (ART), visual response time (VRT), and discrimination response time (DRT), to streamline the evaluation system, these three indicators were initially combined into a single composite reaction time indicator. The AUC - normalized weighting approach was utilized to integrate these indicators, with the AUC values of each individual indicator serving as the foundation for internal weighting. Calculations indicated that the AUC values of the three reaction time indicators were 0.4654, 0.3722, and 0.4571 respectively, resulting in a total AUC of 1.2947. After normalization, the corresponding weights were ascertained as 0.36, 0.29, and 0.35 respectively. The formula for calculating the composite reaction time indicator is: RT = 0.36 × ART + 0.29 × VRT + 0.35 × DRT, where RT represents the composite indicator, and ART, VRT, and DRT signify auditory response time, visual response time, and discrimination response time respectively. Subsequent to this integration, the study identified three primary evaluation indicators: blood oxygen saturation (SpO₂, AUC = 0.9707), heart rate (HR, AUC = 0.6820), and the composite reaction time indicator (mean AUC = 0.4316). Subsequently, the AUC - normalized weighting method was applied to determine the weights for each primary indicator. The total AUC of the three indicators was 2.0843, yielding weights of 0.47 for SpO₂, 0.33 for HR, and 0.20 for the composite reaction time indicator. The final fatigue composite early warning scoring formula is: Composite score = 0.47 × SpO₂ + 0.33 × HR + 0.20 × RT.

4)The proposed real-time fatigue monitoring framework aligns with recent advances in wearable physiological monitoring for occupational settings. Castaneda et al.22,23 validated unobtrusive SpO₂ monitoring systems for use in high-altitude work environments, demonstrating their feasibility for continuous, non-invasive physiological assessment. This study extends these findings by integrating SpO₂, heart rate, and reaction time metrics into a composite warning system, providing a translational tool for plateau airport personnel monitoring.

Conclusion

This study utilized an environmental experiment chamber to simulate varying altitude conditions, systematically investigating the effects of altitude changes on workers’ physiological indicators and reaction capabilities. The primary conclusions are as follows:

1) A standardized testing system for fatigue and response capacity in high-altitude work was established, encompassing a complete technical workflow that includes experimental environment simulation, test parameter selection, data acquisition, and analysis. This system employs an environmental experiment chamber to precisely control altitude gradients, selects core physiological indicators such as blood oxygen saturation (SpO₂), heart rate (HR), and blood pressure (BP), and integrates cognitive function metrics including reaction time and response accuracy. It forms a repeatable and quantifiable assessment method for high-altitude work adaptation, providing methodological support for subsequent similar studies.

2) The study elucidated the impact patterns of altitude variation on physiological indicators and their correlation mechanisms with response capacity. Data analysis revealed that physiological indicators exhibited differentiated response characteristics with increasing altitude:

Heart rate changes: There is an increasing trend with rising altitude, and the heart rate changes at rest before the experiment were more sensitive than those during the experiment, suggesting that heart rate can serve as an early warning indicator for acclimatization to high-altitude work.

Blood pressure changes: exhibit specific fluctuation patterns at different altitudes (the exact variation patterns of systolic/diastolic blood pressure require supplementation with actual data), reflecting the compensatory regulatory mechanisms of the cardiovascular system.

Reactivity Correlation: Heart rate and blood pressure exhibit a synergistic effect, jointly influencing reaction accuracy; Blood oxygen saturation shows no significant linear correlation with reaction time, but its fluctuation amplitude is closely related to cognitive stability, suggesting that dynamic changes in oxygen levels should be prioritized over a single threshold.

3) A fatigue early-warning method and corresponding threshold values were proposed, based on critical physiological indicators and reaction time detection data. This approach comprehensively considers the increasing sensitivity of heart rate with altitude elevation, the compensatory fluctuation pattern of blood pressure, the dynamic range of blood oxygen saturation fluctuations, and the declining trend in reaction accuracy. A weighted fusion of multiple indicators is employed to construct the warning model. The threshold values are determined according to the baseline ranges of physiological parameters and critical reaction capability thresholds across different altitude gradients, categorized into three fatigue severity levels: mild (heart rate increase of 10%–15% above resting baseline with 5%–10% decrease in reaction accuracy), moderate (heart rate increase of 15%–25% with 10%–20% decrease in reaction accuracy), and severe (heart rate increase exceeding 25%, reaction accuracy decline exceeding 20%, and blood oxygen saturation fluctuation greater than 5%). The development of this warning method and thresholds provides actionable quantitative evidence for real-time fatigue monitoring and risk alerts in high-altitude working environments, aiding operators in scientifically scheduling rest periods and optimizing workload management to enhance safety and operational efficiency.

4) Due to limitations in sample size and age distribution, the conclusions of this study have certain constraints. Future studies should expand the sample population, optimize age stratification methods, and ensure balanced representation across all age groups to enhance the persuasiveness of the findings.
