Direct Construction of Magnetic and Electrical Two-Dimensional Radical Covalent Organic Frameworks
Authors: Rajendra Prasad Paitandi, Madhurima Giri, Ryosuke Koyae, Masaki Nobuoka, Yusuke Tsutsui, Yoshiaki Shuku, Yun Hee Koo, Zhuowei Li, Yuta Sakurai, Katsuaki Suzuki, Javier López-Cabrelles, Shuhei Furukawa, Hironori Kaji, Masayuki Suda, Samrat Ghosh, Shu Seki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Radicals arranged in a two-dimensional (2D) hexagonal network can offer various exotic magnetic, electronic, and optical properties that find application in electronics/spintronics. However, direct synthesis remains challenging due to the scarcity of stable, symmetry-matched radical building blocks. Here, we report the bottom-up synthesis of hexagonal 2D radical covalent organic frameworks (RCOFs) with unpaired electrons at the nodes of the frameworks. A planar verdazyl radical amine (V-NH2) undergoes Schiff-base condensation with aldehydes to afford highly crystalline hexagonal RCOFs (VTPT and VPMT). The spin density was precisely controlled through the selection of building blocks with modulated spin-spin distances. The EPR and SQUID measurements confirmed a high spin concentration with antiferromagnetic interactions at low temperature, which is further tuned by interlayer interactions. Thin films of VTPT exhibited preferential in-plane orientation with enhanced photoconductivity, attributed to improved π-conjugation. These findings establish a direct route to RCOFs and underscore their potential as pseudo 1-dimensional antiferromagnetic materials.

Introduction

Stable radical polymers have attracted significant attention in the field of magnetic materials, organic electronics/spintronics, catalysis, and energy storage/conversion processes1–5. Compared to radical polymers, radical covalent organic frameworks (RCOFs) have additional advantages of porosity and long-range ordered structure, which are beneficial for efficient charge and spin transport as polarons and magnons, respectively6–8. Due to their permanent porosity and robust stability, COFs have also been utilised in the field of gas adsorption and separation, sensing, catalysis, optoelectronics, and energy storage6–10. The spin-spin interactions of the radical units in RCOFs provide an additional dimension to tune their property while keeping magnetic field-active and spin transport properties11,12. It enables notable optical, electronic, and magnetic functionalities relevant to spintronic applications. Additionally, RCOFs with a high density of spin centres are found suitable for applications as a polarizing agent in dynamic nuclear polarization-nuclear magnetic resonance (DNP-NMR) and organic rechargeable batteries13,14. However, developing such materials (RCOFs) directly from radical building blocks continues to present a synthetic challenge, stemming from the scarcity of appropriate building blocks with adequate symmetry, planarity, and chemical stability; their tendency to undergo oligomerizations to form stable closed-shell configurations. To prevent oligomerization of the radical unit, bulky substituents are typically introduced near the radical centre, which disrupts the planarity and symmetry of the building blocks. Such distortions hinder the formation of crystalline 2D COFs by interfering with the stacking and alignment required for extended crystalline sheet structures15. There are two ways to incorporate the radical unit within the framework: a direct route using a radical building block as a node/linker, or a post-synthetic modification route. The former route is limited due to a lack of suitable symmetrical and stable radical precursor molecules required for COF synthesis. Notably, these radical centres remain localized at the side chain of the framework and do not contribute to the extended π-conjugated backbone of the COF, consequently, no significant magnetic communication between the radical centers is observed, even at low temperatures (2 K)13. The latter route requires chemical oxidation or chemical transformation reactions to introduce radical centres into the framework, which often compromises porosity and long-range order16–25.

Moreover, the scope of the direct or post-synthetic modification route to synthesize RCOFs is often limited to a few building blocks, namely, polychlorotriphenylmethyl (PTM) moiety or TEMPO units, owing to their appropriate molecular symmetry and persistent stability16–24. For example, Jiang et al. first introduced radical moiety into the alkyne-appended porphyrin COF framework via a post-synthetic transformation reaction with an azide-substituted TEMPO unit using a click reaction16. Recently, Lu et al. developed a radical cationic COF using a post-synthetic modification method and used it as a sulfur host and cathode material for Li-S ion batteries26. Despite these findings, most of the reported post-synthetically modified 2D RCOFs generally suffer from lower surface area as well as poor crystallinity: essential for efficient spin and charge transport processes16–19. Note that the heterogeneous nature of post-synthetic modification reactions inherently makes it difficult to control/maximize the radical concentrations (spin density) within the framework. Meanwhile, most post-synthetically developed RCOFs are sensitive to oxygen and moisture, exhibiting limited stability, which impedes wide-range practical applications19. Achieving high crystallinity and porosity in post-synthetic modified RCOFs is rare.

It is envisioned that the direct synthesis of RCOFs from a suitable building block may overcome these problems. In this regard, verdazyl, a class of π-delocalized heterocyclic radicals with ambipolar redox properties, has attracted our attention owing to its unique magnetic properties and stability27–30. It has been employed as a redox-active component in symmetrical flow batteries and in molecular magnets31. Notably, Boudouris and co-workers recently developed a verdazyl-based nonconjugated radical polymer as an exceptional spin transport medium, featuring high spin mixing conductance and a long spin diffusion length1. Symmetrically substituted planar verdazyl molecules are the promising building blocks for RCOFs, as they can promote efficient stacking and crystallinity while enabling stronger intermolecular magnetic exchange interactions at low temperatures. The planar geometry of verdazyl is also beneficial for achieving crystalline conjugated frameworks with highly efficient transport pathways; features that are not attainable with conventional bulky radical building blocks that disrupt conjugation and intermolecular interactions32,33. Keeping these points in mind, we have selected the C3 symmetry planar 6-oxoverdazyl amine moiety as a new building block for creating 2D π-conjugated lattices. The ambipolar redox properties of verdazyl are secured to lead the pathways via the charge delocalization through tight π − π stacking34,35. The spins located at nodes (verdazyl) of the 2D lattice can be unidirectionally aligned across the crystalline framework, which may also induce a unique magnetic phase of the spins at low temperature via a magnetic exchange interaction. Moreover, highly oriented, crystalline thin films of RCOFs become ideal platforms for magneto-electronic devices.

Results

Design principle and COF synthesis

In topology-guided COF synthesis, the design of highly symmetric molecular building blocks is essential, as symmetry dictates stacking arrangements, pore architecture, and the formation of extended ordered frameworks (Fig. 1). It is established that the planarity of the building blocks directly affects the crystallinity and charge transport properties of the polymeric materials36–38. Although verdazyl is a well-established stable organic radical, its application in COF synthesis has not been explored, mainly due to the scarcity of suitably stable and symmetrical verdazyl-based precursors39. Theoretical calculations suggest that the conversion of the non-radical sp³-hybridized tetrazane amine (4,4,4-amino-1,3,5-tris(4-biphenylyl)−6-oxotetrazane; T-NH₂) into the radical sp²-hybridized verdazyl amine (4,4,4-amino-1,3,5- tris(4-biphenylyl)−6-oxoverdazyl; V-NH₂) results in a pronounced change in molecular geometry, reduction of the dihedral angle from 65.0° to 7.0° (Supplementary Fig. S1). This transformation enhances planarity while introducing radical character and magnetic properties. It was anticipated that the incorporation of these two distinct building blocks into polymeric frameworks would yield materials with markedly different crystallinity, porosity, and magnetic behaviour. Guided by these theoretical predictions, we designed and synthesized two magnetically contrasting hexagonal polymeric materials—VTPT (paramagnetic) and TTPT (diamagnetic)—which have comparable pore sizes and linkage motifs but have different crystallinity. Both frameworks (VTPT and TTPT) were constructed via Schiff-base condensation of the C₃-symmetric amine precursors V-NH₂ and T-NH₂, respectively with the C₃-symmetric aldehyde 1,3,5-tris(4-formylphenyl)benzene (TPT). To validate the direct synthesis approach, another framework (VPMT) was also synthesized with a higher radical density in the unit cell by combining V-NH₂ with C2-symmetric aldehyde, namely para-dimethoxy-terephthaldehyde (PMT). After careful screening of the reaction conditions, condensation reactions were performed under solvothermal conditions at 90 °C for 3 days, in the solvent mixture of mesitylene/1,4-dioxane for VTPT and VPMT and o-dichlorobenzene/n-butanol at 120 °C for 3 days for TTPT, in the presence of 6 M acetic acid (Fig. 2).Fig. 1General overview of RCOFs.The schematic illustration shows the synthesis routes and characteristics of various reported RCOFs and their comparison with our strategy.Fig. 2Design and synthesis route.Synthesis procedure and chemical structures of the RCOFs (VTPT and VPMT) and non-radical polymer (TTPT).

Herein, we found that the symmetric and planar radical precursor V-NH₂ promotes the formation of highly crystalline and porous RCOFs (VTPT and VPMT) with an extended π–π conjugated network when combined with TPT and PMT aldehydes, affording isolated yields of 75% and 80%, respectively. In contrast, the non-planar tetrazane analogue T-NH₂, yielded a diamagnetic porous polymer (TTPT) upon reaction with TPT under strictly anaerobic conditions (sealed tube with multiple freeze–pump–thaw cycles), giving an isolated yield of 88%. Interestingly, when the reaction was performed in a normal pressure tube (partially anaerobic conditions) in a solvent mixture of o-dichlorobenzene/n-butanol at 120 °C, a weak crystalline framework (denoted as TTPT-O) was obtained. This crystallinity can be attributed to the partial in situ oxidation of the non-planar T-NH₂ to the planar verdazyl amine V-NH₂, which favors stacking interactions during framework assembly at elevated temperature (120 °C)40. In contrast, post-synthetic oxidation of the TTPT polymer under an oxygen atmosphere did not improve the crystallinity of the material, highlighting the importance of in situ transformation of the building block during framework growth. Notably, VTPT COF is formed in two distinct forms: polycrystalline powders in solution and oriented thin films in one pot when quartz substrates are put inside (almost vertically) into the above solvent mixtures at a lower concentration of the building blocks. RCOFs (VTPT and VPMT) and polymeric analogues TTPT were obtained as dark green and pale-yellow powders, respectively. These were insoluble in common organic solvents such as acetone, tetrahydrofuran, N-dimethylformamide, alcohols, and dichloromethane. The deep green color of RCOFs (VTPT and VPMT) indicates the presence of a high density of radical moieties within the framework, even after their synthesis under harsh conditions. The crystallinity and porosity of both RCOFs (VTPT and VPMT) and the polymer (TTPT) were confirmed by a combination of powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD), transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and gas sorption studies, as discussed below.

Structural characterization

The successful conversion of the building blocks into imine-linked polymeric material was confirmed by Fourier transform infrared analysis (FT-IR), solid-state 13C cross-polarisation/magic angle spinning (CP/MAS) NMR spectroscopy. The disappearance of the free –NH2 stretching band at around 3365 cm−1 for V-NH2 and 3350 cm−1 for T-NH2, as well as the relative disappearance of aldehyde stretching bands ~1700 cm−1 compared to TPT (for VTPT and VPMT) and ~1669 cm−1 PMT (for VPMT), reveals the complete consumption of starting materials. The appearance of the characteristic imine band at 1623 cm−1 for VTPT and VPMT and at 1622 cm−1 for TTPT further validates the imine linkage in these materials (Supplementary Fig. S2). On the other hand, carbonyl (−C = O) stretching bands of the original amine building blocks also shifted to a higher frequency, further indicating an increase in the π − π interaction between the COF layers/network formation36,37. The 13C CP/MAS NMR spectra of RCOFs were not adequately obtained owing to the paramagnetic nature. For the TTPT polymer, the characteristic −CH carbon resonance originating from the imine group was observed at approximately 150 ppm, which was significantly broadened, probably due to the partial oxidation of the T-NH₂ building block of the framework to V-NH₂ during high-temperature synthesis (Supplementary Fig. S3). The characteristic non-quaternary carbonyl and sp3 (−CH) carbon atom signals of the T-NH₂ building block of the framework were also observed at around 145 ppm and around 10 − 20 ppm, respectively. The triazine ring carbon atom of the TPT building block appeared at 170 ppm. Thus, solid-state NMR and FT-IR results indicate the successful formation of the TTPT polymer.

Solid-state 1H DNP–NMR measurements were performed on the VTPT and TTPT to evaluate their potential as polarizing agents for DNP. Despite possessing higher radical concentration, the VTPT polymer exhibited no detectable signal enhancement, while the TTPT polymer with a low radical content (0.5 % per tetrazane unit, vide infra) showed a 1.6-fold increase in signal intensity (Supplementary Fig. S4). Partial oxidation of the TTPT polymer upon exposure to air yielded a radical-containing polymeric framework (denoted as TTPT-O), which likely accounts for the observed DNP enhancement. These results indicate that a moderate concentration of radical centres within the framework is essential for achieving the optimum DNP performance, consistent with trends reported for other polymeric paramagnetic materials13.

The crystalline structures of VTPT and VPMT were determined by PXRD analysis and computational simulations. VTPT exhibited distinct sharp diffractions at 2θ = 3.32°, 5.63°, 6.40°, and 8.30°, which were assigned to the (100), (110), (020), and (120) crystal planes, respectively (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. S5). Similarly, VPMT exhibited sharp diffractions at 2θ = 2.16°, 3.55°, and 4.32°, assigned to the (100), (110), and (200), planes, respectively (Fig. 3b). The presence of multiple higher-order reflections confirmed the high degree of long-range order structure for VTPT and VPMT. Moreover, the broad peaks at 2θ ∼24° − 26° for VTPT (2θ = 24.5°) and VPMT (2θ = 25.2°) manifested by the (001) plane reflection, demonstrating the π − π interaction between the COF layers (Supplementary Fig. S5). Here, VTPT displayed a shorter interlayer distance (3.50 Å), than VPMT (interlayer distance: 3.65 Å), which may be due to the presence of the bulky methoxy group in the latter, hampering the face-to-face stacking interaction. In contrast, a non-radical building block (T-NH₂) produced an amorphous polymer under strictly anaerobic conditions. However, under partially anaerobic conditions, in situ oxidation of a small fraction of T-NH₂ to V-NH₂ occurred, yielding a polymeric framework (TTPT-O) with reduced crystallinity (Supplementary Fig. S8). The latter displayed relatively broad diffraction at 2θ = 3.16° and 5.56°, corresponding to the (100) and (110) planes, respectively. It is noteworthy that the reaction of T-NH₂ in complete aerobic conditions does not lead to any further improvement of crystallinity. To find the possible crystal structure of VTPT and VPMT, a variety of interlayer stacking modes (AA/AB/ABC) were simulated and then compared to the experimental PXRD pattern (Supplementary Fig. S6). The slightly slipped AA mode provides the best fit with the experimental PXRD patterns from the stacking in VTPT (Fig. 3c, d). The porosity of these materials was examined by nitrogen sorption measurements at 77 K. The N2 isotherm of RCOFs (VTPT and VPMT) at low relative pressures (P/P0 < 0.1) displayed a characteristic type IV isotherm, indicating their mesoporous structure (Fig. 5a). The Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) surface areas were found to be 1890 and 1719 m2/g for VTPT and VPMT, respectively, using the BETSI method (Supplementary Fig. S13-S14)42. The pore volumes were calculated to be 1.41 and 1.72 cm3/g for VTPT and VPMT, respectively. On the other hand, the BET surface area of TTPT was evaluated as 110 m2/g, with a pore volume of 0.15 cm3/g. The BET surface area of these RCOFs is greatly higher than that of most previously reported RCOFs (Supplementary Tab. S1). The higher specific surface area of RCOFs arises from the higher symmetry and planarity of the verdazyl amine building block, which promotes the ordered framework stacking compared to the non-planar T-NH₂ analogue. Notably, the BET surface area of the partially oxidized TTPT-O was higher than its non-oxidised analogue (TTPT) owing to the higher degree of crystallinity of the former (Supplementary Fig. S15). The non-local density functional theory (NLDFT) was used to compute the pore size distributions of TTPT, VTPT, and VPMT, which were centred at 1.9, 2.1, and 3.5 nm, respectively (Supplementary Fig. S17). The smaller pore size of TTPT than VTPT is attributed to the non-planar tetrazane amine (T-NH₂) building block (sp3 hybridized), which occupies less lateral space during stacking compared to the planar verdazyl amine units (sp2 hybridized). The abundant nitrogen atoms (triazine, verdazyl/tetrazane core) at the surface of the frameworks encourage us to measure the CO2 uptake capacity of these materials at 195 K and at 1 bar (Fig. 5b)43–45. Compared to the non-radical polymeric material (TTPT), RCOFs (VTPT and VPMT) showed greatly enhanced CO2 adsorption capacities with values of 433 cm3/g (for VTPT) and 725 cm3/g (for VPMT), respectively. The high CO2 adsorption capacities of these RCOFs may be due to the synergistic effect of stronger quadrupole-quadrupole interactions among nitrogen and radical centres with CO2 molecules, along with the higher porosity of the RCOFs (Supplementary Tab. S2)44,45. The thermal stability of COFs was determined by thermogravimetric analysis (TGA). The detailed chemical structure of the frameworks is verified by full X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) of the precursor and the COFs. For tetrazane amine (T-NH2), the deconvoluted high-resolution N1s spectra revealed two major peaks at 399.4 and 400.5 eV, corresponding to the amine nitrogen and tetrazane ring nitrogen atoms. After transformation to verdazyl amine (V-NH2), the peak corresponding to tetrazane N is shifted to 401.1 eV, while the amine N peak remains at 399.4 eV, indicating stability of the ring N atoms after radical formation. For the VTPT, a small positive shift of the N1s peaks associated with verdazyl nodes is observed along with the appearance of a peak at 398.8 eV (Supplementary Fig. S20). This observed peak is assigned as the imine nitrogen of the aldimine bonds and triazine ring46,47. Similarly, TTPT exhibits the same peak as tetrazane nodes, along with a peak that arises from the overlap of imine and triazine, thus indicating successful formation of the frameworks linked by imine bonds through the Schiff base condensation reaction.

Field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM) was used to examine the morphology of the COFs after evaporating an ethanol suspension of the COFs on a silicon wafer. As shown in the image (Supplementary Fig. S21), VTPT displayed both nanorod and sheet-like structures, whereas VPMT and TTPT showed nanorod-type structures. The formation of a sheet structure indicates a higher stacking order via π–π interactions in VTPT, which is also observed in PXRD studies. The TEM images of VTPT and VPMT showed clear lattice fringes at approximately 2.6 and 2.8 nm, respectively, which match well with the (100) and (200) facets of the simulated model, further demonstrating the higher crystallinity of RCOFs (Fig. 5c-d). Fast Fourier transform (FFT) patterns of the TEM images of VTPT and VPMT evidence the hexagonal order structure in the systems (Supplementary Fig. S23). Although lattice fringes are observed in discrete sections of TTPT (approximately 2.4 nm), it appeared slightly wavier (poor π–π stacking) than VTPT, indicating lattice defects and the presence of disorder within the TTPT frameworks. Thus, the planarity of the building blocks plays an important role in the ultimate crystallinity of the framework motifs. SEM and TEM images of VTPT film further supported a continuous and uniform morphology composed of nanosheets (Supplementary Fig. S22). The surface roughness and thickness of the VTPT film were measured by atomic force microscopy (AFM) and surface profiler, revealing the uniformity of the film with an average thickness of ~ 180 nm (Fig. 4c).

Electronic study

The electronic structure of these materials was studied using diffuse reflectance UV−vis spectroscopy (UV-DRS). These RCOFs powder (VTPT and VPMT) appear deep green in color and exhibit strong visible-light absorption, with an absorption tail extending beyond 800 nm (Fig. 6a). An obvious red shift of ~300 nm in the optical absorption edge was observed from TTPT to VTPT, and the calculated optical band gaps from Tauc plots are 2.7 (for TTPT), 1.6 (for VTPT), and 1.45 eV (for VPMT) (Fig. 6b). VTPT film shows clear signature of a low-energy absorption band at 650-700 nm associated with the electronic transition from the singly occupied molecular orbital (SOMO) of verdazyl radical (Fig. 4a). The red-shifted absorption bands of VTPT than TTPT suggested a more planar structure and extended π-conjugation of the RCOFs induced by the sp2 hybridized planar building blocks (V-NH2), further supported by the theoretical analyses of the unit cell structures of the representative materials41. Compared to the tetrazane analogue, the unit cell of VTPT exhibits a lower singly unoccupied molecular orbital (SUMO) energy level relative to the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) energy level of TTPT. In both TTPT and VTPT, the highest occupied molecular orbitals (HOMO/SOMO) were localized on the tetrazane and verdazyl units, respectively, whereas the LUMO reside on the triazine ring, establishing the donor–acceptor character of these frameworks (Supplementary Fig. S24). The band gap energy of these materials was also calculated from the cyclic voltammetry, which is consistent with the UV-Vis study. Electron Paramagnetic Resonance (EPR) spectroscopy of VTPT and VPMT reveals a strong, single Lorentzian line centred at a g-value of 2.0033, with spin densities of 5.5 and 7.0 × 1020 spins g−1, which are 89% and 94%, respectively, against the predicted density of verdazyl nodes in the unit cell (Fig. 6c). The sharpness of the signal of VPMT over VTPT indicates more spin-spin interactions in the former, owing to the presence of higher radical centres within the frameworks. On the other hand, tetrazone polymer (TTPT) showed a very weak signal with an identical g-value of 2.0033 but a significantly reduced spin density of 3.0 × 1018 spins g−1. Moreover, an obvious enhancement of spin density was observed for TTPT-O (1.5 × 1019 spins g−1) with a spin density of ~2 % against tetrazane nodes, which is likely attributable to the aerobic oxidation of the framework during synthesis at high temperature (120 °C) (Supplementary Fig. S26). Moreover, the EPR intensities of monomer and RCOFs remain unchanged even after keeping the samples in open air for more than 3 months, indicating the stability of the unpaired electron in the framework (Supplementary Fig. S27). The spin density distribution on the repeating unit of VTPT showed that the unpaired electrons are partially delocalized into verdazyl nodes and nearby phenyl rings attached to the node, thus enabling spin and charge mobility (vide supra) (Supplementary Fig. S28).

Here, the transformation of the building blocks from tetrazane to verdazyl promotes the π-delocalized features contributing to the charge transport pathways in the materials. To understand the impact of crystallinity and different π-delocalisation on charge transport in TTPT, VTPT, and VPMT, we have employed time-resolved microwave conductivity (FP-TRMC) measurements. It measures the intrinsic photoconductivity ϕΣμ of photo-injected charge carriers in the materials, where ϕ is the charge carrier generation quantum yield and Σμ is the sum of mobilities of electrons and holes49–51. Upon excitation at 355 nm, clear conductivity transients are observed of these materials (Fig. 6d). Among these, the planar VTPT (5.1 × 10–5 cm2 V–1s–1) and VPMT (4.1 × 10–5 cm2 V–1s–1) showed higher photoconductivity than the non-planar TTPT (1.7 × 10–5 cm2 V–1s–1) possibly due to the high spin density, low band gap and superior crystallinity. It should be noted that the film of VTPT showed significant enhancement of photoconductivity (8.2 × 10–5 cm2 V–1s–1) relative to the powders under similar conditions, owing to the anisotropic nature of the film in the face-on orientation mode.

It is anticipated that the lower oxidation potential and higher porosity of RCOFs may favour the incorporation of guest molecules (I2) within the framework than the TTPT polymer, which may further enhance the charge carrier generation yield and photoconductivity52. In particular, the photoconductivity of the doped COFs is 3–4 times greater than that of the undoped COFs (Fig. 6e and Supplementary Fig. S29). Following doping, the ϕΣμ value in TTPT, VTPT, and VPMT increased from 1.7, 5.1, and 4.1 × 10–5 cm2 V–1s–1 (undoped) to 5.0, 15, and 8.6 × 10–5 cm2V–1s–1 (I2 doped), respectively. The doping to VTPT film was more efficient with an increase of ϕΣμ from 8.2 (undoped) to 38 × 10–5 cm2V–1s–1 (I2 doped). The charge transfer from the COF to the iodine is also verified from EPR spectra (Supplementary Fig. S30-S31). In the case of TTPT, a clear increase in the EPR signal after doping, indicated the generation of radical cations. Although the photoconductivity of VTPT increases significantly after doping, the EPR signal gradually decreases with time, which suggests the formation of closed-shell cations as the major charge carriers in this system. These results demonstrate that in RCOFs, the radical unit couples to the framework and enhances electronic conductivity via cation hopping mediated by the highly dense radicals in VTPT. The Direct-Current (DC) mode electrical conductivity was also measured in the VTPT film thanks to its uniform, highly crystalline film-forming nature across different substrates. A linear response of current with voltage was observed even in DC mode, indicating ohmic conduction behaviour with enough concentration of charge carriers in undoped VTPT. Electrical conductivity was further enhanced by two orders of magnitude upon doping with I2, achieving a value of 5.0 × 10−4 S/m, which is higher than most 2D polymeric materials (Fig. 6f and Supplementary Tab. S3)53,54.

Magnetic properties

To examine the role of intra/interlayer spin-spin distances on the magnetic properties of these materials, temperature-dependent magnetic susceptibility measurements were conducted using superconducting quantum interference device (SQUID) magnetometry over the temperature range 2–300 K (Fig. 7)12,19,55–59. The non-radical polymer (TTPT) is diamagnetic in nature. Despite having a lower number of radical centres within the frameworks, VTPT (1 radical/unit cell) showed stronger antiferromagnetic interactions than VPMT (2 radicals/unit cell) at low temperature, which is probably due to the stronger interactions in the former. The temperature dependence of molar magnetic susceptibility (χm) for RCOFs is shown in Fig. 7a, d. The χpT vs T plot in the high temperature range (100 − 300 K) fitted by the Curie-Weiss law (χm = C/(T − θ)), where C is the Curie constant, θ is the Weiss temperature (Fig. 7b, e). The best fit of the χpT vs T plot yielded C = 0.34 emu mol−1 K, and θ = −30 K (for VTPT) and C = 0.34 emu mol−1 K, and θ = −10 K (for VPMT), respectively. The lower value of θ for VTPT than that of VPMT suggests a stronger antiferromagnetic interaction between spins and demonstrates the predominance of interlayer stacking interactions (Fig. 7b, e). However, at lower temperatures (below 35 K), the molar magnetic susceptibility of VPMT drops more sharply than VTPT, indicating stronger interactions owing to the higher spin density of the former. Furthermore, we measured magnetization as a function of magnetic field at various temperatures, which showed a typical increase and then approached saturation at low temperatures (Fig. 7c, f). At 2 K, the magnetic moments of these RCOFs reached 2.37 emu g−1 (VTPT) and 3.98 emu g−1 (VPMT) at 50,000 Oe, which are significantly higher than those of the most reported magnetic porous organic polymers22. Discussion

In summary, we have established a novel design strategy for the synthesis of radical-based semiconducting COFs by employing radical building blocks and have successfully realized their formation in both polycrystalline powders and thin films. The resulting RCOFs exhibit enhanced crystallinity, high porosity, and notable chemical stability. Our findings demonstrate that planar radical building blocks facilitate stacking interactions, thereby improving crystallinity, structural stability, and photoconductivity. The developed RCOFs retain the spin density of the building blocks and exhibit an antiferromagnetic phase transition at low temperatures. Apparently, an interlayer (out-of-plane) magnetic interaction dominates over intralayer (in-plane) ones, directing the overall magnetic interactions of these materials at low temperature. We expect that our effort will not only lead to the direct synthesis of RCOFs but also broaden the scope of their potential applications. In particular, the unique integration of magnetic and electronic properties renders verdazyl-based RCOFs promising candidates for future research and technological development. This work introduces an important design principle to tune the spin density in π-conjugated covalent organic framework systems, which may serve as benchmark materials for the development of novel magnetic materials (metal-free quantum magnetism and spintronic technologies) in the future.

Methods

Synthesis of TTPT polymer

T-NH2 (24.1 mg, 0.4 mM, 1 equiv) and TPT (16.0 mg, 0.4 mM, 1 equiv) were taken in a 10 mL ampoule, followed by the addition of o-dichlorobenzene (2 mL) and n-butanol (2 mL). The reaction mixture was homogenized by sonication for 30 minutes, and then aqueous acetic acid (0.4 mL, 6 M) was added. The ampoule was degassed by three freeze-pump-thaw cycles and sealed by flame. The ampoule was kept at 120 °C for 3 days in an oven. After cooling to room temperature, the yellow precipitate was collected by filtration and washed with anhydrous N,N-dimethylacetamide (DMAC), tetrahydrofuran (THF), and acetone. To remove the monomer completely, the precipitate was washed several times with anhydrous THF and acetone and centrifuged until the supernatant became colorless. Finally, the precipitate was dried at 90 °C under reduced pressure.

Synthesis of VTPT COF Powder

V-NH2 (24.0 mg, 0.4 mM, 1 equiv) and TPT (16.0 mg, 0.4 mM, 1 equiv) were taken in a 10 mL ampoule, followed by the addition of mesitylene (2 mL) and 1,4-dioxane (2 mL). The reaction mixture was homogenized by sonication, and aqueous acetic acid (0.4 mL, 6 M) was added. The ampoule was degassed by three freeze-pump-thaw cycles and sealed by flame. The ampoule was kept at 90 °C for 3 days in an oven. After cooling to room temperature, the dark green precipitate was collected by filtration and washed with anhydrous N,N-dimethylacetamide (DMAC), tetrahydrofuran (THF), and acetone. To remove the monomer completely, the precipitate was washed several times with anhydrous THF and acetone and centrifuged until the supernatant became colorless and non-fluorescent. Finally, the precipitate was dried at 70 °C under reduced pressure.

Synthesis of VPMT COF Powder

VPMT COF powder is synthesized in the same process as VTPT powder using PMT aldehyde (16.0 mg, 0.4 mM, 1.5 equiv) instead of TPT aldehyde (1 equiv).

Synthesis of VTPT COF Film

VTPT COF films is prepared at the same conditions as powder, with a lower concentration of the starting material. For COF film synthesis, V-NH2 (12.0 mg, 0.2 mM, 1 equiv) and TPT (8.0 mg, 0.2 mM, 1 equiv) were taken in a 20 mL BIOTAGE reaction tube, followed by the addition of mesitylene (3 mL) and 1,4-dioxane (3 mL). The quartz substrate was placed almost vertically within the tube and (0.6 mL, 6 M), of aqueous acetic acid was added to the reaction mixture (without sonication). The tube was sealed after purging with nitrogen and kept at 90 °C for 3 days in an oven. Along with the progress of the reaction, a COF film formed on the substrate and along the walls of the tube. The dark green film was carefully removed from the solvent mixture and washed multiple times by submerging it into anhydrous tetrahydrofuran (THF), and acetone and then finally dried in open air.
