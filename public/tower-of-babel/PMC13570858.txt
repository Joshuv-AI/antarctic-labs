Immunogenicity, Tolerability, and Safety of BA.1-Adapted BNT162b2 Vaccine in 18- to 55-Year-Olds Previously Vaccinated with BNT162b2 or Who Were COVID-19 Vaccine-Naive
Authors: David Fitz-Patrick, Omair Sahgal, Leon F. Fouché, Essack Mitha, Mookho Malahleha, Juleen Gayed, Georgina Keep, Ying Zhang, Kayvon Modjarrad, Todd Belanger, Xia Xu, David Cooper, Federico J. Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionVariant-adapted COVID-19 vaccines can induce more robust immune responses against closely matched circulating variants than original vaccines.MethodsWithin a phase 3 master study, monovalent Omicron BA.1-adapted BNT162b2 (BNT162b2-BA.1) was evaluated in healthy BNT162b2-experienced (cohorts 1−2) or COVID-19 vaccine-naive (cohort 3) 18 to 55 year-olds. Primary immunogenicity endpoints in cohort 1 were geometric mean ratios (GMRs) of Omicron BA.1 neutralizing titers 1 month after BNT162b2-BA.1 to those after one BNT162b2 dose and differences in percentages of participants with Omicron BA.1 seroresponse between BNT162b2-BA.1 and BNT162b2. In cohort 2, primary immunogenicity objectives (participants without evidence of previous SARS-CoV-2 infection) were to demonstrate superiority (GMR two-sided 95% CI lower limit > 1) with respect to neutralizing titer levels and noninferiority with respect to seroresponse difference (difference two-sided 95% CI lower limit > − 5%) for BNT162b2-BA.1 versus BNT162b2. In cohort 3, primary immunogenicity objectives were to demonstrate superiority with respect to neutralizing titer levels and noninferiority with respect to seroresponse after two BNT162b2-BA.1 doses compared with participants receiving BNT162b2 in an efficacy trial. Reactogenicity and adverse event frequencies were determined.ResultsA total of 1471 participants were included. In cohort 1 (BNT162b2-experienced), GMRs compared with one BNT162b2 dose were 2.87 and 2.64 after one or two BNT162b2-BA.1 doses, respectively, and differences in percentages of participants with seroresponse between BNT162b2-BA.1 and BNT162b2 were 29.0% and 21.0% for one or two BNT162b2-BA.1 doses. In cohort 2 (BNT162b2-experienced), one BNT162b2-BA.1 dose met prespecified superiority and noninferiority criteria with respect to GMR and difference in percentage of participants achieving seroresponse, respectively, compared with BNT162b2. In cohort 3 (COVID-19 vaccine-naive), superiority with respect to GMR and noninferiority with respect to difference in percentage of participants achieving seroresponse of BNT162b2-BA.1 to BNT162b2 were met. BNT162b2-BA.1 and BNT162b2 safety and tolerability profiles were similar.ConclusionsOmicron BA.1-adapted COVID-19 vaccines can broaden immune responses against Omicron BA.1 SARS-CoV-2 variants.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov identifier, NCT04955626.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s40121-026-01409-1.

Introduction

Vaccination remains a critical tool for the prevention of severe illness and death from COVID-19 [1]. The original COVID-19 BNT162b2 vaccine encoded the ancestral SARS-CoV-2 Wuhan-Hu-1 strain spike glycoprotein [2]. The pivotal phase 2/3 clinical trial initiated in the first year of the COVID-19 pandemic, in which the ancestral strain was circulating, demonstrated 95.0%‒100% efficacy of BNT162b2 administered as an initial two-dose series in individuals ≥ 12 years of age [3, 4]. The original monovalent BNT162b2 vaccine was effective across the Alpha, Beta, and Delta SARS-CoV-2 variants, which emerged between approximately September and November 2020, and were characterized as having relatively low immune escape [5–9]. However, effectiveness against Omicron variant B.1.1.529 (hereafter referred to as Omicron BA.1), which emerged in November 2021, was reduced [9–12]. Although additional vaccine doses were able to mitigate some immune escape, effectiveness against Omicron BA.1 waned faster than observed for earlier variants, including waning protection from severe disease [11–15]. Omicron BA.1 showed greater transmissibility, especially among pediatric populations, compared with earlier variants [16]. Although Omicron BA.1 was generally associated with milder disease compared with the previous Delta variant, those with certain medical or immunocompromising conditions, older adults, and children < 5 years of age remained at risk for both moderate and severe COVID-19 outcomes [17–19].

Consistent with regulatory agency recommendations from 2022, manufacturers began developing updated COVID-19 vaccines more closely matching the first emerging Omicron sublineages [20, 21]. A monovalent Omicron BA.1 variant-adapted BNT162b2 vaccine (as reported here but never widely clinically implemented), as well as a bivalent variant-adapted BNT162b2 vaccine encoding both the SARS-CoV-2 ancestral strain and Omicron BA.1 strains, were initially assessed. As earlier reported from a phase 3 master study of different formulations and dose levels of booster approaches for monovalent and bivalent variant-adapted BNT162b2 vaccines, the monovalent and bivalent Omicron BA.1-adapted BNT162b2 vaccine (hereafter BNT162b2-BA.1 and bivalent BNT162b2-BA.1, respectively) demonstrated superiority compared with the original BNT162b2 vaccine in adults > 55 years of age who had previously received three doses of the original BNT162b2 vaccine with respect to neutralizing activity and noninferiority with respect to seroresponse against Omicron BA.1 [22]. Furthermore, the bivalent BNT162b2-BA.1 vaccine also demonstrated noninferiority to BNT162b2 in the evaluation of neutralizing activity against the ancestral strain [22].

To contribute to the body of evidence for variant-adapted COVID-19 vaccines, herein we report the immunogenicity, tolerability, and safety of the monovalent BNT162b2-BA.1 vaccine from this phase 3 master study in participants 18–55 years of age who had previously received BNT162b2 vaccination or who were COVID-19 vaccine-naive. At the time the study was conducted, individuals had a varied COVID-19 vaccination history; this heterogeneity allowed a rich and robust evaluation of boosting strategies for the monovalent BNT162b2-BA.1 vaccine vs. original BNT162b2.

Methods

Study Design

This randomized substudy (NCT04955626), which was part of a phase 3 master study of the BNT162b2 booster approaches, evaluated the immunogenicity, tolerability, and safety of monovalent BNT162b2-BA.1 across three cohorts of healthy participants who were 18–55 years of age and had previously received the BNT162b2 vaccine (cohorts 1 and 2) or who were COVID-19 vaccine-naive (cohort 3) but had not had a previous clinical or microbiologic diagnosis of COVID-19. Participants were assessed for their baseline SARS-CoV-2 status using N-binding antibody or nucleic acid amplification testing of nasal (midturbinate) swabs. Both the BNT162b2-BA.1 and BNT162b2 vaccines were administered in this study, and all study vaccines were administered intramuscularly into the deltoid muscle.

Participants in cohort 1 were COVID-19 vaccine experienced, having previously received two doses of BNT162b2, with the last dose occurring 90–240 days before enrollment (Fig. 1; Supplementary Table S1). Participants in cohort 2 were also COVID-19 vaccine experienced, having previously received three doses of BNT162b2, with the third dose administered 90–180 days before randomization (Fig. 1; Supplementary Table S1) [4, 23]. Participants in cohort 2 were randomized 1:1 to receive a single dose of BNT162b2-BA.1 or BNT162b2 and were offered an optional additional dose of BNT162b2-BA.1 at 3 months after the first dose.

Participants in cohort 3, who were COVID-19 vaccine-naive, received two doses of BNT162b2-BA.1 given 3 weeks apart and a third dose of BNT162b2 given approximately 5 months later if the participant consented (Fig. 1; Supplementary Table S1).

Eligibility criteria, randomization, and blinding for all cohorts are further described in the Supplementary Material. This study was conducted in accordance with the protocol and consensus ethical principles derived from international guidelines, including the Declaration of Helsinki Council and those from the Council for International Organizations of Medical Sciences, applicable International Council for Harmonisation of Technical Requirements for Pharmaceuticals for Human Use good clinical practice guidelines, and other applicable laws and regulations, including privacy laws. All participants provided written informed consent before enrollment.

Assessments, Objectives, and Endpoints

Immunogenicity

The blood sampling for immunogenicity analyses against Omicron subvariant BA.1 and the ancestral strain was based on 50% neutralizing titers (interpolated from the reciprocal of the two assay dilutions encompassing the 50% reductions in fluorescent viral foci) against SARS-CoV-2 that were measured using a validated recombinant SARS-CoV-2 neutralization assay at the time points outlined in Fig. 1 [22, 24]. These assessments included neutralizing geometric mean titers (GMTs) at various time points and percentages of participants achieving seroresponse (≥ 4-fold increase in neutralizing titers from before the first study vaccine dose; if predose measurements were below the lower limit of quantitation [LLOQ], then a postvaccination measure of ≥ 4 × LLOQ was used) after vaccination.

The primary immunogenicity objectives for cohort 1 were to descriptively compare immune responses after one or two doses of BNT162b2-BA.1 with one dose of BNT162b2 in BNT162b2-experienced participants who had evidence of previous SARS-CoV-2 infection at baseline. Primary immunogenicity endpoints in cohort 1 included the geometric mean ratios (GMRs) of Omicron BA.1 neutralizing titers 1 month after one dose and 1 month after two doses of BNT162b2-BA.1 to those 1 month after one dose of BNT162b2. Additional primary immunogenicity endpoints in cohort 1 were the differences in percentages of participants with seroresponse to Omicron BA.1 between BNT162b2-BA.1 (at 1 month after one dose and 1 month after two doses) and BNT162b2 (at 1 month after one dose).

In cohort 2, the primary immunogenicity objectives were to demonstrate superiority with respect to neutralizing titer levels against Omicron BA.1 and noninferiority with respect to percentage of participants achieving seroresponse after one dose of BNT162b2-BA.1 compared with one dose of BNT162b2 in BNT162b2-experienced participants without evidence of previous SARS-CoV-2 infection at baseline (i.e., no evidence of previous SARS-CoV-2 infection up to 1 month after the first study vaccination). Primary immunogenicity endpoints in cohort 2 were the GMR of Omicron BA.1 neutralizing titers 1 month after one dose of BNT162b2-BA.1 to those 1 month after one dose of BNT162b2 and the difference in percentages of participants with seroresponse to Omicron BA.1 between BNT162b2-BA.1 and BNT162b2 at the same time point.

In cohort 3, the primary immunogenicity objectives were to demonstrate superiority with respect to neutralizing titer levels against Omicron BA.1 and noninferiority with respect to seroresponse after two doses of BNT162b2-BA.1 in COVID-19 vaccine-naive participants when compared with participants who received BNT162b2 in the pivotal phase 2/3 efficacy trial (NCT04368728) [4]. Primary immunogenicity endpoints in cohort 3, which included participants regardless of previous SARS-CoV-2 infection, were the GMR of Omicron BA.1 neutralizing titers 1 month after the second dose of BNT162b2-BA.1 to 1 month after the second dose of BNT162b2 and the difference in percentages of participants with seroresponse to Omicron BA.1 at 1 month after the second dose of BNT162b2-BA.1 and 1 month after the second dose of BNT162b2.

Secondary immunogenicity objectives were to demonstrate the “super” superiority of the neutralizing Omicron BA.1 immune response at 1 month after a single dose of BNT162b2-BA.1 compared with BNT162b2 in BNT162b2-experienced participants (cohort 2) or after two doses of BNT162b2-BA.1 compared with a historical control (i.e., a random group of COVID-19 vaccine-naive participants who received BNT162b2 in the pivotal phase 2/3 efficacy trial [4]; cohort 3). Secondary immunogenicity endpoints were the GMR assessments as described for the primary immunogenicity endpoints in cohorts 2 and 3.

Exploratory immunogenicity endpoints reported here in BNT162b2-experienced participants and COVID-19 vaccine-naive participants overall (all cohorts) and by baseline SARS-CoV-2 status (cohorts 1 and 2 only) included neutralizing GMTs for the Omicron BA.1 and ancestral strains. In cohort 1, these were measured before vaccination and 1 month after the first and second dose. In cohort 2, these were measured before vaccination; 1 month and 3 months after the first dose; and 1, 3, and 6 months after the last dose. In cohort 3, these were measured before vaccination, 1 month and 6 months after the second dose, and 1 and 6 months after the third dose. Exploratory endpoints also included geometric mean fold rises (GMFRs) from before vaccination to the subsequent time point and the percentages of participants with seroresponse at each postvaccination time point.

Safety

The primary safety objectives were to describe the safety and tolerability profile of BNT162b2-BA.1 and BNT162b2 in BNT162b2-experienced participants and COVID-19 vaccine-naive participants.

Safety endpoints included the percentage of participants with local reactions and systemic events as recorded by participants for 7 days after each vaccination using an electronic diary. Local reactions included pain, redness, and swelling at the injection site. Systemic events included fever, vomiting, diarrhea, headache, fatigue/tiredness, chills, new or worsened muscle pain, and new or worsened joint pain. Severity gradings of local reactions and systemic events are provided in Supplementary Table S2.

Safety endpoints also included the percentage of participants experiencing adverse events (AEs) through 1 month after the last study vaccination and serious AEs (SAEs) through 6 months after the last vaccination or throughout the study. Additional assessments of potential cases of myocarditis and pericarditis, which were protocol-specified AEs of special interest (AESIs), were performed through 1 month after each study vaccination. AEs and SAEs were categorized according to the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (version 26.0) terms.

Participants also underwent active surveillance for SARS-CoV-2 infection. Description of surveillance for SARS-CoV-2 infection and definitions of COVID-19-related cases and COVID-19-related severe cases are provided in the Supplementary Material. Samples of SARS-CoV-2 from confirmed cases of COVID-19 were sequenced as described previously [25].

Statistics

Immunogenicity

Analysis populations are defined in Supplementary Table S3. All immunogenicity statistical analyses were based on the evaluable immunogenicity population. Each cohort had a sentinel subset of the first 30 participants per group for potential separate exploratory immunogenicity assessment. After exclusion of the sentinel subsets, approximately 100 BNT162b2-experienced participants per vaccine group within cohort 1 with evidence of a previous SARS-CoV-2 infection were randomly selected for immunogenicity assessment (i.e., cohort 1 immunogenicity subset); approximately 175 participants per vaccine group were randomly selected from cohort 2 (BNT162b2-experienced) for primary and secondary immunogenicity assessments (i.e., cohort 2 primary immunogenicity subset); and for cohort 3 (COVID-19 vaccine-naive) the remaining approximately 175 participants were used for immunogenicity assessment (i.e., cohort 3 immunogenicity subset). For COVID-19 vaccine-naive participants, 175 participants (18–55 years of age with a similar percentage of those with evidence of previous SARS-CoV-2 infection as in the expanded set of cohort 3) from the pivotal phase 2/3 efficacy trial who had received two doses of BNT162b2 30 μg were randomly selected in the analysis as a comparator [4]. Additionally for cohort 2, after exclusion of the sentinel subsets, the full expanded immunogenicity subset was used for descriptive immunogenicity assessments (i.e., cohort 2 expanded set), and a sample of 100 participants from each vaccine group (comprising all participants with evidence of previous SARS-CoV-2 infection, and the remainder randomly selected from participants without evidence of SARS-CoV-2 infection up to 1 month after the first study vaccination) was selected for exploratory immunogenicity assessments (i.e., cohort 2 exploratory immunogenicity subset).

For immunogenicity endpoints, the GMR was calculated as the mean of the difference of logarithmically transformed assay results and exponentiating the mean. Two-sided 95% CIs were obtained using the Student t distribution for the mean difference of the logarithmically transformed assay results and exponentiating the CIs. Superiority based on GMR was declared if the lower limit of the two-sided 95% CI for the GMR was > 1. The secondary objectives of “super” superiority were evaluated using a 1.5-fold margin for GMR; “super” superiority for GMR was established if the lower limit of the two-sided 95% CI for the GMR was > 1.5.

Noninferiority based on seroresponse was declared if the lower limit of the two-sided 95% CI for the difference in percentages of participants with seroresponse was greater than –5%. The exact 95% CI for the percentages of participants with seroresponse for each vaccine group was computed using the Clopper–Pearson method. The difference in percentages and the associated two-sided 95% CI using the Miettinen and Nurminen method were calculated.

The GMTs were calculated as the means of assay results after logarithmic transformation and then exponentiating the means to express results on the original scale. Two-sided 95% CIs were obtained by taking logarithmic transformations of assay results, calculating the 95% CI using the Student t distribution, and then exponentiating the CIs. The GMFRs were calculated as the mean of the difference of logarithmically transformed assay results (later time point minus earlier time point) and exponentiating the mean. The associated two-sided 95% CIs were obtained by constructing CIs using the Student t distribution for the mean difference on the logarithm scale and exponentiating the CIs.

Multiplicity adjustments for immunogenicity endpoints are described in the Supplementary Material.

Safety

Local reactions, systemic events, AEs, and SAEs were assessed in the safety population (Supplementary Table S3). Descriptive statistics were provided for each endpoint for each vaccine group, including counts and percentages of participants with the indicated endpoint.

Results

Participants

This study was conducted at 38 sites in the United States and South Africa between January 2022 and May 2023, with a total of 1471 participants across all cohorts. In cohort 1, which was conducted in the United States and South Africa, 619 BNT162b2 vaccine-experienced participants were randomized 1:1:1 to receive either one dose of BNT162b2-BA.1, two doses of BNT162b2-BA.1, or one dose of BNT162b2 (Supplementary Figure S1). Overall, 99.7% of participants in cohort 1 received the study vaccination. In cohort 2, which was conducted in the United States, 640 BNT162b2 vaccine-experienced participants received a first study dose of either BNT162b2-BA.1 or BNT162b2 (Supplementary Figure S2). Overall, 70.8% of participants in cohort 2 received the optional second study dose of BNT162b2-BA.1. In cohort 3, which was conducted in the United States and South Africa, all 210 assigned COVID-19 vaccine-naive participants received the first BNT162b2-BA.1 dose, with 94.3% and 81.9% receiving the second BNT162b2-BA.1 and third BNT162b2 doses, respectively (Supplementary Figure S3). The most common reasons for study withdrawal across all cohorts were withdrawal by the participants and lost to follow-up.

Demographic characteristics in BNT162b2-experienced participants (cohorts 1 and 2) were generally similar across vaccine groups (Table 1). In cohort 1, the median age at first study vaccination was 39 years, the median time from the previous BNT162b2 dose to the first study vaccination was 6.1 months, and 88.2% of participants had evidence of previous SARS-CoV-2 infection at baseline. In cohort 2, the median age at first study vaccination was 43 years, the median time from the previous dose of BNT162b2 to the first study vaccination was 3.9 months, and 17.2% of participants had evidence of previous SARS-CoV-2 infection at baseline. BNT162b2-Vaccine Experienced (Cohorts 1 and 2)

The percentages of participants in cohort 2 who were without evidence of previous SARS-CoV-2 infection at baseline and achieving seroresponse to Omicron BA.1 were 62.3% in the BNT162b2-BA.1 group and 39.3% in the BNT162b2 group 1 month after a single dose, a difference of 23.0% (two-sided 95% CI, 11.1%, 34.3%; Fig. 2b). As the lower limit of the two-sided 95% CI for the difference in the percentage of participants achieving seroresponse was greater than the prespecified –5% margin, noninferiority of the seroresponse of BNT162b2-BA.1 to BNT162b2 for Omicron BA.1 was achieved.

Findings in participants in cohort 2 who were without evidence of previous SARS-CoV-2 infection at baseline from the full expanded set were consistent with results from the primary immunogenicity subset. For Omicron BA.1, the GMR for the BNT162b2-BA.1 group to the BNT162b2 group was 1.96 (two-sided 95% CI, 1.62, 2.37). The difference in percentages of participants who achieved seroresponse to Omicron BA.1 between the BNT162b2-BA.1 and BNT162b2 groups was 21.4% (two-sided 95% CI, 12.0%, 30.4%).

In an exploratory assessment in BNT162b2-experienced participants in cohort 2, neutralizing GMTs to Omicron BA.1 among participants with or without evidence of previous SARS-CoV-2 infection at baseline were higher in participants with evidence of previous SARS-CoV-2 infection compared with those without in both vaccine groups and across all time points (Supplementary Figure S5A; Supplementary Figure S6A). GMTs to Omicron BA.1 at 1 month after a single dose of BNT162b2-BA.1 or BNT162b2 were similar among participants with evidence of previous SARS-CoV-2 infection at baseline, with a modest decline after 3 months (Supplementary Figure S6A). Among participants in cohort 2 who had evidence of previous SARS-CoV-2 infection at baseline, GMTs to Omicron BA.1 at 1 month after the second dose were higher compared with the GMTs before this dose, with a more substantial increase in the BNT162b2 group. Thereafter, a gradual decline in Omicron BA.1 GMTs at 3 and 6 months after the second dose was observed in both vaccine groups and Omicron BA.1 GMTs were generally similar at 6 months after the second dose in both the BNT162b2-BA.1 and BNT162b2 groups.

One month after the optional second dose of BNT162b2-BA.1 in participants in cohort 2 (who had received either BNT162b2-BA.1 or BNT162b2 as the first dose), GMTs to Omicron BA.1 and percentages achieving seroresponse in the BNT162b2 group among participants with evidence of previous SARS-CoV-2 infection at baseline were substantially increased versus those 1 month after the first dose and were greater than those in the BNT162b2-BA.1 group (who received BNT162b2-BA.1 at both doses; Supplementary Figures S6−S7). A gradual decline in GMTs and, consequently, percentages of participants achieving seroresponse were observed at 3 and 6 months after this second dose with BNT162b2-BA.1 in both participants with and participants without evidence of SARS-CoV-2 infection at baseline. However, GMTs remained above baseline for all groups.

Among BNT162b2 vaccine-experienced participants in cohort 2, GMFRs were lower for the ancestral strain than for the Omicron BA.1 strain across most time points and vaccine groups; the exception was for the first dose of BNT162b2, for which GMFRs were similar between the strains (Supplementary Figure S5).

Among BNT162b2-experienced participants in cohort 2 who received BNT162b2-BA.1 or BNT162b2, neutralizing immune responses against the ancestral strain were higher in participants who were baseline SARS-CoV-2 positive compared with those who were baseline SARS-CoV-2 negative in both vaccine groups across all time points (Supplementary Figure S6). The percentages of participants achieving seroresponse against the ancestral strain were numerically lower than against the Omicron BA.1 strain across all time points and regardless of baseline SARS-CoV-2 status and vaccine group (Supplementary Figure S7).

Additionally, a similar percentage of COVID-19 vaccine-naive participants with or without evidence of previous SARS-CoV-2 infection who received two doses of BNT162b2-BA.1 or BNT162b2 achieved a seroresponse to Omicron BA.1 at 1 month after the second dose (Fig. 3b). Therefore, noninferiority of BNT162b2-BA.1 to BNT162b2 was achieved as the lower limit of the two-sided 95% CI was greater than − 5%.

Across all COVID-19 vaccine-naive participants, observed GMTs for Omicron BA.1 were substantially higher 1 month after the second dose of BNT162b2-BA.1 compared with those 1 month after the second dose of BNT162b2 (Supplementary Figure S8a). A decline of GMTs was observed in the BNT162b2-BA.1 group with lower values at 6 months compared with 1 month after the second vaccination. In participants who received a third dose of BNT162b2 (after two doses of BNT162b2-BA.1), GMTs 1 month after the third dose increased to similar levels as those observed 1 month after the second dose of BNT162b2-BA.1, followed by a decline at 6 months after the third dose. GMTs were similar 6 months after the second and third doses and substantially greater than baseline values.

Across all COVID-19 vaccine-naive participants, observed GMTs for the ancestral strain were substantially lower 1 month after the second dose of BNT162b2-BA.1 compared with those 1 month after the second dose of BNT162b2 (Supplementary Figure S8b). The observed GMFRs against the ancestral strain were lower in the BNT162b2-BA.1 group compared with the BNT162b2 group at 1 month after the second dose (7.8 vs. 48.7).

Safety

Most local reactions were mild or moderate in severity (Fig. 4). Among BNT162b2-experienced participants (cohorts 1 and 2) and COVID-19 vaccine-naive participants (cohort 3), pain at the injection site was the most common prompted local reaction across BNT162b2-BA.1 and BNT162b2 groups. Systemic events were also predominantly mild or moderate in severity (Fig. 5). The most common prompted systemic events across vaccine groups and doses were fatigue and headache among BNT162b2-experienced (cohorts 1 and 2) and COVID-19 vaccine-naive participants (cohort 3). No grade 4 local reactions or systemic events were reported. Among BNT162b2-experienced participants (cohorts 1 and 2), the frequency of AEs from the first vaccination through 1 month after the last vaccination ranged from 2.3%–6.1% among BNT162b2-BA.1 recipients and from 2.0%−3.7% among BNT162b2 recipients (Supplementary Tables S7−S8). Corresponding related AEs within 1 month of the last vaccination ranged from 0–2.5% among BNT162b2-BA.1 recipients and 0.5%–1.5% among BNT162b2 recipients. There were no AEs leading to withdrawal. Frequencies of SAEs throughout the study were generally similar between all groups (0.9%−1.6%); all were assessed as not related to study vaccination by the investigator.

Overall, 6.7% of COVID-19 vaccine-naive participants (cohort 3) experienced an AE from the first BNT162b2-BA.1 dose through 1 month after the second dose, 0.5% experienced an AE assessed as related to study vaccination by the investigator, and 0.5% experienced an AE leading to study withdrawal (Supplementary Table S9). Throughout the study, 3.8% of participants reported SAEs, none of which were assessed as related to study vaccination.

No cases of myocarditis or pericarditis (protocol-specified AESIs), or anaphylaxis or Bell’s palsy were reported. Lymphadenopathy occurred in six BNT162b2-experienced participants (cohort 2) who received BNT162b2-BA.1; all were assessed as related to vaccination and resolved within 2–8 days.

In cohort 1, severe AEs reported from the first study vaccination to 6 months after the last study vaccination included dilated cardiomyopathy in a participant in the two-dose BNT162b2-BA.1 group, which was assessed as not related to study intervention by the investigator. Life-threatening cardiac disorders reported in the two-dose BNT162b2-BA.1 group included acute myocardial infarction, congestive cardiac failure, and dilated cardiomyopathy (1 participant reported both congestive cardiac failure and dilated cardiomyopathy, while another participant reported acute myocardial infarction); all were assessed as unrelated to study vaccine.

Cases of confirmed COVID-19 after study vaccination were reported in 3.4%−5.3% and 20.6%−25.2% of BNT162b2-experienced participants in cohorts 1 and 2, respectively (Supplementary Table S10). This discrepancy observed between cohort 1 and cohort 2 is likely attributable to marked differences in baseline SARS-CoV-2 status (88.2% vs. 17.2% had evidence of previous SARS-CoV-2 infection at baseline) and geographic distribution, with the majority of cohort 1 participants recruited from South Africa and cohort 2 participants recruited only from the United States (Table 1). There was one reported case of severe COVID-19 in a participant (cohort 1) who received two doses of BNT162b2-BA.1. The participant did not require hospitalization and there was no diagnosis of significant renal, hepatic, or neurologic dysfunction. The participant was noted to have an oxygen saturation of 93% on room air. Confirmed cases of COVID-19 occurred in 2.9% of participants who had not previously received COVID-19 vaccination after the study vaccination (cohort 3). Among the determinate sequencing results for COVID-19 cases, all identified cases were of the Omicron lineage.

Discussion

In this study of adults 18–55 years of age conducted between January 2022 and May 2023, the variant-adapted monovalent BNT162b2-BA.1 vaccine induced robust immune responses against Omicron BA.1 in both BNT162b2 vaccine-experienced and COVID-19 vaccine-naive participants. When administered as a single dose among BNT162b2-experienced participants, BNT162b2-BA.1 met the prespecified criteria for superiority with respect to GMR and noninferiority with respect to seroresponse compared with BNT162b2. Although the immune response declined over time after the first and second study doses, there was a persistence of immune response through 6 months after the last dose of BNT162b2-BA.1. In COVID-19 vaccine-naive participants, BNT162b2-BA.1 administered as a two-dose series met the prespecified criteria for superiority and “super” superiority.

Among COVID-19 vaccine-naive participants, it was noted that most participants had evidence of previous SARS-CoV-2 infection at baseline. A comparison of the immune response of BNT162b2-BA.1 against Omicron BA.1 and the ancestral strain suggests that the neutralizing immune response to the primary series in COVID-19 vaccine-naive individuals elicited a more substantial neutralizing antibody response against Omicron BA.1. This is expected and a lower neutralizing antibody response against the ancestral strain was not considered clinically or epidemiologically relevant.

The spread of Omicron BA.1 was facilitated by immune escape because of mutations that reduced affinity for BNT162b2-induced antibodies against the S protein N-terminal and receptor-binding domains, enhanced infectivity compared with the ancestral strain and earlier variants, and waning immunity [16]. Clinical trial data, including those from the current study, were used to support the authorization of variant-adapted vaccines by regulatory authorities [22, 26]. Consequently, the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved and authorized bivalent variant-adapted Omicron BA.4/BA.5 BNT162b2 for the 2022/2023 winter season in the United States, which the FDA considered a closer match to contemporaneously circulating strains; in Europe, the European Medicines Authority approved and authorized bivalent variant-adapted Omicron BA.1 BNT162b2 [27]. Bivalent BA.1 or BA.4/BA.5 vaccines reduced rates of COVID-19related hospitalizations based on real-world studies from 2022 and 2023 [28, 29].

With the continued evolution of SARS-CoV-2 and emergence of variants of concern, several lineage-specific monovalent vaccines were developed, including monovalent XBB.1.5-adapted BNT162b2, which was designed to match the circulating strain for the 2023/2024 season [30, 31]. The component directed against the ancestral strain was removed from this and subsequent formulations because of concerns over immune imprinting skewing the immune response to the ancestral strain [32, 33]. Omicron JN.1 and KP.2 were the predominant circulating strains in the United States ahead of the 2023/2024 winter season, leading to FDA approval and authorization for use of monovalent JN.1-adapted and KP.2-adapted COVID-19 vaccines; based on updated epidemiology, KP.2-adapted COVID-19 vaccines were preferentially recommended by the FDA for use in all persons ≥ 6 months of age for the winter 2024/2025 season [34–36].

The tolerability and safety profiles of BNT162b2-BA.1 and BNT162b2 across all vaccination groups were consistent with results previously reported in clinical studies and the known safety profile of BNT162b2 [22, 23, 37, 38]. Additionally, no events of myocarditis or pericarditis were reported.

A limitation of the study was the lack of a longer follow-up to assess the duration of immune response, particularly hindering interpretation of cohort 1 results. Each cohort had predominant enrollment of participants of either Black (cohorts 1 and 3) or White (cohort 2) race. Additionally, among COVID-19 vaccine-naive participants, a historical rather than a contemporaneous comparator was used in the immunogenicity assessments and the numbers of participants who were without evidence of previous SARS-CoV-2 infection at baseline were too few to sufficiently evaluate the variant-adapted primary series in this participant subgroup. Finally, this was a generally low-risk population group; further investigation into a more at-risk group may be clinically significant.

Conclusions

In conclusion, in participants 18–55 years of age, BNT162b2-BA.1 elicited a robust immune response against Omicron BA.1 in BNT162b2-experienced participants and as a two-dose series in participants who had not previously received a COVID-19 vaccine. Previous studies of the BNT162b2‑BA.1 variant‑adapted vaccine were conducted in older populations, assessed higher doses, or investigated bivalent formulations incorporating the original BNT162b2 vaccine [22, 39]. BNT162b2-BA.1, when administered as a primary series, and as additional doses in COVID-19 vaccine-experienced individuals, was safe and with a tolerability profile consistent with that previously observed for this age group. These results highlight that variant-adapted vaccines can be developed and updated to broaden immune responses against Omicron BA.1 SARS-CoV-2 variants.
