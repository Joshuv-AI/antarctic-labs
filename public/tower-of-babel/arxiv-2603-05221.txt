Reachability in VASS Extended with Integer Counters
Clotilde Bizière, Wojciech Czerwiński, Roland Guttenberg, Jérôme Leroux, Vincent Michielini, Łukasz Orlikowski, Antoni Puch, Henry Sinclair-Banks

Abstract
We consider a variant of VASS extended with integer counters, denoted VASS+ ℤ \mathbb{Z} . These are automata equipped with ℕ \mathbb{N} - and ℤ \mathbb{Z} -counters; the ℕ \mathbb{N} -counters are required to remain nonnegative and the ℤ \mathbb{Z} -counters do not have this restriction. We study the complexity of the reachability problem for VASS+ ℤ \mathbb{Z} when the number of ℕ \mathbb{N} counters is fixed. We show that reachability is NP -complete in 1 1 -VASS+ ℤ \mathbb{Z} (i.e. when there is only one ℕ \mathbb{N} -counter) regardless of unary or binary encoding. For d ≥ 2 d\geq 2 , using a KLMST-based algorithm, we prove that reachability in d d -VASS+ ℤ \mathbb{Z} lies in the complexity class ℱ d + 2 \mathcal{F}_{d+2} . Our upper bound improves on the naively obtained Ackermannian complexity by simulating the ℤ \mathbb{Z} -counters with ℕ \mathbb{N} -counters. To complement our upper bounds, we show that extending VASS with integer counters significantly lowers the number of ℕ \mathbb{N} -counters needed to exhibit hardness. We prove that reachability in unary 2 2 -VASS+ ℤ \mathbb{Z} is PSpace -hard; without ℤ \mathbb{Z} -counters this lower bound is only known in dimension 5. We also prove that reachability in unary 3 3 -VASS+ ℤ \mathbb{Z} is Tower -hard. Without ℤ \mathbb{Z} -counters, reachability in 3-VASS has elementary complexity and Tower -hardness is only known in dimension 8.

Introduction

Vector Addition Systems with States (VASS) together with Petri nets, an equivalent model, are established computational models of concurrency with a variety of applications in computer science
[
28
]
.
VASS can be equivalently seen as automata equipped with nonnegative counters.
These counters can be incremented and decremented, but importantly, they cannot be tested for equality with zero.
The central problem for VASS is the reachability problem: from a given source configuration (state together with counter values), is there a run to the a given target configuration?

Research into VASS reachability has long and rich history.
The problem was first considered in the seventies; in 1976, reachability was shown to be
ExpSpace
-hard by Lipton
[
24
]
.
Decidability of reachability was only proved later by Mayr in 1981
[
25
]
.
Mayr’s algorithm was subsequently simplified and reformulated by Kosaraju
[
19
]
and Lambert
[
20
]
.
After the names of the three inventors, and after Sacerdote and Tenney who provided partial results in 1977
[
30
]
, the main algorithm used to decide reachability in VASS goes by the acronym “KLMST”.
There has since been plenty of effort devoted to establishing complexity of reachability; it was only recently determined to be
Ackermann
-complete
[
22
,
23
,
9
]
.

Despite this breakthrough, many questions concerning reachability problems in VASS and related models are far from being understood.
A prominent yet puzzling example is the complexity of the reachability problem in
d
-dimensional VASS (aka.
d
-VASS) for fixed
d\in\mathbb{N}
.
The complexity has only been settled for dimensions
1
and
2

[
33
,
17
,
2
]
.
Already for dimension
3
, despite very recent progress, there is a wide complexity gap: with unary encoding, the problem is
NP
-hard
[
4
]
and in
2-ExpSpace

[
7
]
.
Even though an elementary algorithm is not known for reachability in
4
-VASS, the best-known non-elementary lower bound requires 8 dimensions
[
10
]
.
Moreover, in higher dimensions
d\in\mathbb{N}
the gap is huge: reachability in
d
-VASS is known to be
\mathcal{F}_{(d-3)/2}
-hard
[
6
]
and in
\mathcal{F}_{d}

[
15
]
; here
\mathcal{F}_{d}
is the
d
-th complexity class defined by the hierarchy of fast growing functions (for details, see
[
31
]
).

In this paper, we consider VASS extended with integer counters.
This model can be seen as automaton with counters that must remain nonnegative (
\mathbb{N}
-counters) and counters without this restriction (
\mathbb{Z}
-counters).
We use
d
-VASS+
k\mathbb{Z}
to refer to a VASS which has
d

\mathbb{N}
-counters and
k

\mathbb{Z}
-counters.
We also use
d
-VASS+
\mathbb{Z}
to refer to the scenario in which the number of
\mathbb{Z}
-counters is not specified.

The reachability problem for VASS extended with integer counters in which both
d
and
k
are not specified can be easily shown to be
Ackermann
-complete.
Indeed, every integer counter can be simulated by the difference of two nonnegative counters.
This means that the additional integer counters do not offer any expressive power.
However, reachability in
d
-VASS+
\mathbb{Z}
, for fixed
d\in\mathbb{N}
, turns out to be an intriguing, nontrivial problem.

Our main motivation to study reachability in
d
-VASS+
\mathbb{Z}
is two-fold.
First,
d
-VASS+
\mathbb{Z}
are a natural and basic model which we wish to examine in detail.
Even though the model of
d
-VASS+
\mathbb{Z}
has been informally discussed many times by the community, it had not yet been systematically investigated.
Adding integer counters immediately yields surprising observations: even though the coverability problem for VASS is
ExpSpace
-complete
[
24
,
27
]
, adding just one integer counter makes coverability as hard as reachability (i.e.
Ackermann
-complete).
This is because one additional integer counter can be used to store minus the sum of all of the nonnegative counters.
The second motivation is that understanding fixed dimension VASS appears to be highly nontrivial
and requires novel approaches.
We anticipate that studying this closely related model may be fruitful for finding new techniques that will also be applicable for fixed dimension VASS.

Additionally, we have observed an application of reachability in
d
-VASS+
\mathbb{Z}
, it can be reduced to the following problem.
Given two labelled
d
-VASS
\mathcal{V}_{1}
and
\mathcal{V}_{2}
, decide whether there exists a word
w_{1}
accepted by
\mathcal{V}_{1}
and a word
w_{2}
accepted by
\mathcal{V}_{2}
with the same Parikh image.
To sketch the reduction, first consider running
\mathcal{V}_{1}
while guessing
w_{1}
and store its Parikh image in some
\mathbb{Z}
-counters, zero-test the
\mathbb{N}
counters using some
\mathbb{Z}
counters, then run
\mathcal{V}_{2}
while guessing
w_{2}
and subtract its Parikh image from the same
\mathbb{Z}
-counters; if the
\mathbb{Z}
-counters end with zero value, then the Parikh images were equal.

Our contributions

In this paper we present four main contributions regarding the reachability problem for
d
-VASS+
\mathbb{Z}
.

Dimension one

In
Section

3
, we study
1
-VASS+
\mathbb{Z}
; we prove that reachability is
NP
-complete, regardless of whether unary or binary encoding is used.
The main result here is the
NP
upper bound (Theorem
1
).
Our result generalises the
NP
upper bound for binary 1-VASS
[
17
]
and interestingly uses a novel approach.

Dimension two

In
Section

4.1
, we show that the reachability problem for unary
2
-VASS+
\mathbb{Z}
is
PSpace
-hard (
Theorem

10
).
At a glance, this result may not seem particularly impressive because reachability in unary VASS with only integer counters is
NP
-hard.
However, it turned out to most challenging and it required new techniques.
The first is a newly developed, intricate construction to produce doubly-exponential counter values (Lemma
12
).
The second is a new flavour of a hard reachability problems in counter automata (Lemma
8
).
The third is a novel way of simulating counter automata with VASS provided with a large counter value; we believe this last technique will have applications in the future.

Dimension three

In
Section

4.2
, we prove that reachability in unary
3
-VASS+
\mathbb{Z}
is
Tower
-hard (
Theorem

15
).
The situation here is the opposite compared to dimension two: the techniques are somewhat standard, but the result is rather strong.
Regardless, it is striking to see that reachability in
d
-VASS+
\mathbb{Z}
is non-elementary for such a small
d
.
In particular, this case proves that extending VASS with integer counters makes the problem strictly harder as reachability for
3
-VASS has elementary complexity
[
7
]
.

Higher dimensions.

In
Section

5
, we study
d
-VASS+
\mathbb{Z}
for an arbitrary fixed dimension
d\in\mathbb{N}
.
We show that reachability in
d
-VASS+
\mathbb{Z}
is in
\mathcal{F}_{d+2}
(
Theorem

17
).
Notice that this is slightly higher than the best-known
\mathcal{F}_{d}
upper bound for reachability in
d
-VASS
[
15
]
.
Our approach is KLMST-based and also uses some of the ideas introduced in
[
15
]
.
In order to deal with
\mathbb{Z}
-counters without the complexity increasing much, we use a new vector space which manages the possible deviations of the
\mathbb{Z}
-counters.
Our construction is almost optimal in the following sense: any KLMST-based approach cannot attain an upper bound below
\mathcal{F}_{d+1}
(see
Section

6
for more details).

Vector Addition Systems with States

A
d
-dimensional Vector Addition System with States (
d
-VASS) is a non-deterministic finite automaton with
d
nonnegative integer counters.
Formally, VASS consists of a finite set of
states

Q
and finite set of
transitions

T\subseteq Q\times\mathbb{Z}^{d}\times Q
.
For a transition
t=(p,\mathbf{v},q)\in T
, we say that the transition has
effect

\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, which we denote by
\mathbf{eff}(t)
.
A
configuration
of a
d
-VASS consists of the current state
p\in Q
and the current counter values
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
; denoted as
p(\mathbf{u})\in Q\times\mathbb{N}^{d}
.
A transition

can be

if
\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}\geq\mathbf{0}
.
We write
p(\mathbf{u})\xrightarrow{t}q(\mathbf{u+v})
.
A

is a sequence of configurations
(q_{0}(\mathbf{u}_{0}),q_{1}(\mathbf{u}_{1}),\ldots,q_{\ell}(\mathbf{u}_{\ell}))
such that, for every
i\in[\ell]
,
(q_{i-1},\mathbf{u}_{i}-\mathbf{u}_{i-1},q_{i})\in T
is a transition.
A
path
is a sequence of transitions, suppose
\rho=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{\ell})
where
t_{i}=(q_{i-1},\mathbf{u}_{i}-\mathbf{u}_{i-1},q_{i})
, then we can write
q_{0}(\mathbf{u}_{0})\xrightarrow{\rho}q_{\ell}(\mathbf{u}_{\ell})
.
We may also write
q_{0}(\mathbf{u}_{0})\xrightarrow{\rho}_{\mathcal{V}}q_{\ell}(\mathbf{u}_{\ell})
to specify that the run occurs in
\mathcal{V}
.
We also extend the definition of effect to paths: the effect of a path is the sum of the effect of its transitions
\mathbf{eff}(\rho)=\mathbf{eff}(t_{1})+\mathbf{eff}(t_{2})+\ldots+\mathbf{eff}(t_{n})
.

We consider a variant of VASS, called

\pi_{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathbf{v})=(\mathbf{v}[d+1],\ldots,\mathbf{v}[d+k])\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}

The definitions of effects, paths, and runs remain unchanged for

-VASS+
k\mathbb{Z}
.
Though, to be clear, a transition

-VASS+

.
We consider the situation in which both

We denote the size of VASS

.

Fast-growing Functions and Complexity Classes

We define here a hierarchy of
fast-growing functions
and their corresponding complexity classes.
Concretely, for each
k\geq 1
we define function
F_{k}:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
and the complexity class
\mathcal{F}_{k}
which corresponds with the
k
-th level in the Grzegorczyk Hierarchy
[
31
, Subsections 2.3 and 4.1]
.

Let
F_{1}(n)\coloneqq 2n
and, for
k>1
, let
F_{k}(n)\coloneqq\underbrace{F_{k-1}\circ\cdots\circ F_{k-1}}_{n\ \text{times}}(1)
.

Notice that
F_{2}(n)=2^{n}
and
F_{3}(n)=\textup{Tower}(n)
. Using the functions
F_{k}
, we define corresponding classes
\mathcal{F}_{i}
as problems solvable in deterministic time
F_{i}(f(n))
where
f:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
is a function computable in deterministic time
F_{i-1}^{m}(n)
for some fixed
m\in\mathbb{N}
; concretely we define
\mathcal{F}_{k}\coloneqq\bigcup_{f\in\textsc{Fun}_{k-1}}\mathrm{DTIME}(F_{k}(f(n)))
where
\textsc{Fun}_{k}\coloneqq\bigcup_{m\in\mathbb{N}}\mathrm{FDTIME}(F_{k}^{m}(n))
.

Intuitively, problems in
\mathcal{F}_{k}
are those that can be solved in time
F_{k}(n)
under “easier-than”
\mathcal{F}_{k}
-reductions.
For example the class
\mathcal{F}_{3}
, known as
Tower
, contains all the problems,
which can be solved in the deterministic
\text{Tower}(n)
-time, but also for example those, which can be solved in time
\text{Tower}(2^{n})
(because
\text{Tower}(2^{n})=\text{Tower}\circ F_{2}
).
Notice, that there are many known variants of the definitions of the fast-growing function hierarchy. However, the classes
\mathcal{F}_{k}
are robust with respect to minor modifications to the definition of the hierarchy of fast-growing functions, see
[
31
, Section 4]
.

-VASS+

; these are VASS with a single natural valued counter and

Our main result is that reachability in binary

-VASS+
\mathbb{Z}
is in

.
This extends the best known results: reachability in binary 1-VASS is in

[
17
]
and reachability in binary integer VASS (with a variable number of counters) is in

.
The latter follows from the fact that integer VASS can efficiently be converted into instances of integer linear programming which can be decided in

.

Theorem 1
.

Reachability in binary

-VASS+
\mathbb{Z}
is in

.

Our main technical contribution of this section is
Lemma

4
.
Here we show that reachability in
1
-VASS+
\mathbb{Z}
is captured by
linear path schemes
; namely reachability can always be witnessed by a run of the form
\rho_{0}\sigma_{1}^{*}\rho_{1}\cdots\rho_{\ell-1}\sigma_{\ell}^{*}\rho_{\ell}
.
Here,
\rho_{0},\rho_{1},\ldots,\rho_{\ell}
are paths that connect disjoint cycles
\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{\ell}
(which each may be taken multiple times).
So long as the underlying paths and cycles are short, and the number of times each cycle is taken is at most exponential, then such a run can be specified with only polynomially many bits.
This is what allows us to prove that reachability in
1
-VASS+
\mathbb{Z}
is in

.

Before detailing

4
, we must prove that reachability in binary

-VASS+
\mathbb{Z}
can be witnessed by exponential length runs.

Lemma 2
.

Let
\mathcal{V}
be a
1
-VASS+
k\mathbb{Z}
and let

be two states.
If

, then there exists a run

such that
\lvert\tau\rvert\leq(nk)^{Ck}
, where

is the size of
\mathcal{V}
encoded in unary and

\mathcal{V}
,

, and

.

We will now sketch the ideas required to prove

We depend on
[
1
, Lemma 15]
as a core tool.
Roughly speaking,
[
1
, Lemma 15]
states that there is a polynomial semilinear description of the Parikh image of the language recognised by a given one-counter automaton (which is encoded in unary).
A One-Counter Automaton (OCA) is a finite automaton with a single
\mathbb{N}
-counter which can be incremented, decremented, and zero-tested.
The transitions are labelled by letters from an alphabet
\Sigma
.
A word
w\in\Sigma^{*}
is accepted by an OCA if there exists a run from the initial state (with zero on the counter) to a final state (also with zero on the counter).
The Parikh image is a function
\psi:\Sigma^{*}\to\mathbb{N}^{\Sigma}
that maps a word to the vector whose entries contain the number of times each letter occurs in the word.
The function is also defined over languages,
\psi(L)\coloneqq\{\psi(w):w\in L\}
.

More precisely,
[
1
, Lemma 15]
states that the Parikh image of the language of an
n
-state OCA,
\psi(L(\mathcal{A}))
, can be expressed as the union of linear sets such that

(i)

the number of linear sets in the union is at most
C^{\prime}n^{20}
,

(ii)

the norm of the base vectors does not exceed
C^{\prime}n^{20}
,

(iii)

the number of periods in each linear set is at most
C^{\prime}n^{20}
, and

(iv)

the norm of the period vectors does not exceed
C^{\prime}n^{20}
.

Here,
C^{\prime}
is some fixed constant.

In order to use
[
1
, Lemma 15]
, we need to convert a
1
-VASS+
\mathbb{Z}

\mathcal{V}
into an OCA
\mathcal{A}
and be able to use
\psi(L(\mathcal{A}))
to identify runs from
s(\mathbf{0})
to
t(\mathbf{0})
in
\mathcal{V}
.
For this, we use the one
\mathbb{N}
-counter of
\mathcal{A}
to track the value of the
\mathbb{N}
-counter in
\mathcal{V}
and we will use the letters that
\mathcal{A}
reads to track the updates that the
\mathbb{Z}
-counters receive in
\mathcal{V}
.
Precisely, suppose that
\mathcal{V}
is a
1
-VASS+
k\mathbb{Z}
, then
\Sigma=\{a_{1},b_{1},\ldots,a_{k},b_{k}\}
.
For each

, the letter ‘

’ will be read whenever the
i
-th
\mathbb{Z}
-counter is incremented (i.e. if the

-th
\mathbb{Z}
-counter is increased by five, then five ‘

’s are read) and the letter ‘

’ will be read whenever the

-th

-counter is decremented.
The number of states in

is exactly the size of

The corresponding algorithm is an extension of a reachability algorithm for VASS named KLMST after its inventors Kosaraju, Lambert, Mayer, Sacerdote, and Tenney

We start this section by call the algorithm for VASS reachability, explaining the differences to our version along the way, followed by formal proofs whenever an aspect of correctness significantly differs from the original algorithm.

KLMST considers a slight extension of the VASS reachability problem, whose input are still triplets
(\mathbf{s},\mathcal{V},\mathbf{t})
, but now
\mathcal{V}
is a “generalised VASS”.
Again, we wish to decide whether
\mathbf{s}
can reach
\mathbf{t}
in
\mathcal{V}
.
The main structure of KLMST is a standard recursive algorithm: one first checks whether the current query
(\mathbf{s},\mathcal{V},\mathbf{t})
is from a special class of base cases, usually called
perfect
. If it is, then the algorithm answers “yes” (reachability holds).
Otherwise,
(\mathbf{s},\mathcal{V},\mathbf{t})
is decomposed into finitely many queries
(\mathbf{s}_{i},\mathcal{V}_{i},\mathbf{t}_{i})
, which are smaller w.r.t. a well-founded rank, such that
\mathbf{t}
is reachable from
\mathbf{s}
in
\mathcal{V}
if and only if for some
i
,
\mathbf{s}_{i}
can reach
\mathbf{t}_{i}
in
\mathcal{V}_{i}
.

Then, KLMST proceeds to solve these queries by recursion.
To provide a KLMST-like algorithm for reachability in
d
-VASS+
\mathbb{Z}
, we have to define an analogues generalised VASS, their runs, rank, and perfectness before proving the following lemmas.

Lemma 18
.

If
(\mathbf{s},\mathcal{V},\mathbf{t})
is a perfect query, then
\mathbf{s}\xrightarrow{*}_{\mathcal{V}}\mathbf{t}
.

Lemma 19
.

There is an elementary-time algorithm that decides whether a given query
(\mathbf{s},\mathcal{V},\mathbf{t})
is perfect, and if not outputs finitely many queries
(\mathbf{s}_{i},\mathcal{V}_{i},\mathbf{t}_{i})
such that
\mathbf{rank}(\mathcal{V}_{i})<\mathbf{rank}(\mathcal{V})
and
\mathbf{s}\xrightarrow{*}_{\mathcal{V}}\mathbf{t}
if and only if for some
i
,
\mathbf{s}_{i}\xrightarrow{*}_{\mathcal{V}}\mathbf{t}_{i}
.

If only the least important coordinate of the rank decreases, then all queries
(\mathbf{s}_{i},\mathcal{V}_{i},\mathbf{t}_{i})
have polynomial size w.r.t. the size of
(\mathbf{s},\mathcal{V},\mathbf{t})
.

Assuming these two lemmas, the above-sketched recursive algorithm clearly terminates thanks to König’s Lemma, and gives a correct answer.

5.1
The Complexity of the Algorithm

Regarding complexity, in
[
13
]
an analysis using so-called “controlled bad sequences” was invented.
In our scenario, we will be using this analysis on the sequence
(\mathbf{rank}(\mathcal{V}_{0}),\mathbf{rank}(\mathcal{V}_{1}),\dots)
, where
\mathcal{V}_{0}
is the VASS of the initial query, and
\mathcal{V}_{i+1}
is one of the VASS outputted by

19

Since the rank will be a vector in
\mathbb{N}^{d+1}
, and a single decomposition step takes elementary time, one is able to obtain an
\mathcal{F}_{d+3}
upper bound with the usual bad sequence analysis.
However, we can improve the analysis using the fact that if only the least important component decreases, then we only face polynomial blowup.

We first provide the necessary definition.

Definition 20
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}
and let
f_{0},f_{1}\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
be monotone functions with
f_{0}(x),f_{1}(x)\geq x
for all

Let

be a sequence in
\mathbb{N}^{d}

be the induced sequence of bits where

\mathbf{x}_{k}
is greater than
\mathbf{x}_{k+1}
on any of the first

\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots
is called
(f_{1},f_{0},m)
-controlled
if
|\mathbf{x}_{k}|\leq f_{b_{k}}(f_{b_{k-1}}(\dots(f_{b_{0}}(m))\dots))
, for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

This definition exactly describes the situation of Lemma
19
that, in general, we have an elementary blowup, but if only the least important coordinate decreases, then we only face polynomial blowup.
Usually, one considers the case

and hence the following proposition on

5.2
The necessary definitions for KLMST

We start with the definition of generalised VASS.
We remark that in the literature, there are many different variations of this structure, each with some differences: marked graph transition sequences in
[
20
]
, KLM sequences in
[
22
,
15
]
,
Semil
-VASS in
[
16
]
, and the terminology “generalised VASS” was first used in
[
19
]
.

Definition 23
.

A
generalised VASS
with
d

\mathbb{N}
-counters and
k

\mathbb{Z}
-counters extends
d
-VASS+
k\mathbb{Z}
as follows.
Transitions
e

leaving an SCC
are
additionally
labelled with an
\omega
-configuration
test_{e}\in(\mathbb{N}\cup\{\omega\})^{d}
.
The semantics of such a transition
e
are defined as follows: for
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
and
\mathbf{z}_{1},\mathbf{z}_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}
: We have
p(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}_{1})\xrightarrow{e}q(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z}_{2})
if and only if
e
is a transition from
p
to
q
,
(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z}_{2})=(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}_{1})+\mathbf{eff}(e)
, and
\mathbf{x}[j]=test_{e}[j]
, for all
j
such that
test_{e}[j]\neq\omega
.

Generalised VASSs are encoded in
binary
.

In a generalised VASS, a transition
e
leaving an SCC also zero-tests the
\mathbb{N}
-counters.
As already mentioned in Section
4
, it is easy to simulate these zero tests using
\mathbb{Z}
-counters, hence generalised
d
-VASS+
\mathbb{Z}
are equivalent to
d
-VASS+
\mathbb{Z}
, but zero tests are sometimes convenient during the decomposition.

Rank

We proceed to define the rank of a generalised VASS
\mathcal{V}
which is a slight extension of the rank in
[
22
]
.
It is based upon the complexity of cycles, in particular any transitions
e
leaving an SCC do not influence the rank. The definition is as follows: For every state
q\in Q
, we let
\mathbf{V}(\mathcal{V},q)
be the vector space generated by the effects of cycles of
V
on state
q
.
Recall that
\pi_{\mathbb{N}}(\mathbf{v})
is the projection of a vector
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}\times\mathbb{Z}^{d}
to the vector
(\mathbf{v}[1],\ldots,\mathbf{v}[d])
that is the
\mathbb{N}
-counters
We define the
\mathbb{N}
-part of the rank
,
\mathbf{rank}_{\mathbb{N}}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
, as

\mathbf{rank}_{\mathbb{N}}[i]\coloneqq|\{q\in Q:\dim(\pi_{\mathbb{N}}(\mathbf{V}(\mathcal{V},q)))=i\}|,\text{ for }i\in[d].

In other words,
\mathbf{rank}_{\mathbb{N}}[i]
is the number of states
q\in Q
whose cycle space
\mathbf{V}(\mathcal{V},q)
has has dimension
i
when projected to
\mathbb{N}
-counters.
If the dimension of
\pi_{\mathbb{N}}(\mathbf{V}(\mathcal{V},q))
is
0
, this state does not influence the rank, thus
\mathbf{rank}_{\mathbb{N}}
has
d
components. So far, this is the same as the rank for VASS (without additionally
\mathbb{Z}
counters) introduced in
[
22
]
; the main difference is that our rank has the following extra component.
We define the
\mathbb{Z}
-vector space
\mathbf{V}_{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathcal{V})
as

\mathbf{V}_{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathcal{V}):=\sum_{q\in Q}\mathbf{V}(\mathcal{V},q)\cap(\{0^{d}\}\times\mathbb{Q}^{k}),

i.e. we consider all elements of
\mathbf{V}(\mathcal{V},q)
with effect
0
on the
\mathbb{N}
-counters, and take their Minkowski sum. Intuitively speaking, this characterises the complexity of the behaviour of the
\mathbb{Z}
-counters.
Accordingly, we define the rank of a generalised VASS as
\mathbf{rank}\coloneqq(\dim(\mathbf{V}_{\mathbb{Z}}(V)),\mathbf{rank}_{\mathbb{N}})
.
Ranks are compared lexicographically with
\dim(\mathbf{V}_{\mathbb{Z}}(V))
being the first component, and
\mathbf{rank}_{\mathbb{N}}[j]
has higher precedence than
\mathbf{rank}_{\mathbb{N}}[i]
, for all
j>i
.

Perfectness

In the following, we always assume that the given generalised VASS
\mathcal{V}
is a sequence of SCCs.
This can easily be guaranteed by splitting
\mathcal{V}
into one VASS
\mathcal{V}_{\pi}
, for every sequence
\pi
of SCCs in
\mathcal{V}
.
Observe that, while this decomposition takes exponential time (there may be exponentially many simple paths), any
\mathcal{V}_{\pi}
created fulfills
\textup{size}(\mathcal{V}_{\pi})\leq\textup{size}(\mathcal{V})
.

The basis of perfectness is an integer linear program (ILP) for approximating reachability.
We remark, for a reader familiar with KLMST for
d
-VASS (without additional
\mathbb{Z}
-counters)
[
22
,
15
,
16
]
, that except for using a different ILP, perfectness will remain the same.

ILP Definition

Let
\mathcal{V}=(\mathcal{V}_{0}e_{1}\mathcal{V}_{1}\dots e_{s}\mathcal{V}_{s})
be a generalised VASS, and let
\mathbf{s}=(\mathbf{s}_{\mathbb{N}},\mathbf{s}_{\mathbb{Z}})\in\mathbb{N}^{d}\times\mathbb{Z}^{k}
,
\mathbf{t}=(\mathbf{t}_{\mathbb{N}},\mathbf{t}_{\mathbb{Z}})
be two configurations.
Let
E_{0},\dots,E_{s}
be the transitions of the
\mathcal{V}_{0},\dots,\mathcal{V}_{s}
, respectively.
As always,
d
denotes the number of
\mathbb{N}
-counters and
k
denotes the number of
\mathbb{Z}
-counters.
We will use
i
as an index to refer to the current SCC, and
j
to refer to a particular counter.

Then the ILP has
2sd+\sum_{i=0}^{s}|E_{i}|
variables. The first set of
2sd
variables are
2s
vectors
\mathbf{x}_{i}=(x_{i,j})_{j}\in\mathbb{N}^{d},i\in[s]
and
\mathbf{y}_{i}=(y_{i,j})_{j}\in\mathbb{N}^{d},i\in[s-1]
, which form the counter values when the run enters and respectively leaves each SCC.
We set
\mathbf{x}_{0}:=\mathbf{s}_{\mathbb{N}}
and
\mathbf{y}_{s}:=\mathbf{t}_{\mathbb{N}}
.
Finally, we also have a variable
x_{e}
, for every transition
e\in E_{i}
in an SCC
\mathcal{V}_{i}
, these variables count how many times the corresponding transition
e
is used.

The ILP has three sets of equations.
The first are the Kirchhoff equations, which govern graph reachability.
Formally, for all
i\in[s]
and
q\in Q_{i}
, we add the equation

\sum_{e\in in(q)}x_{e}-\sum_{e\in out(q)}x_{e}=\delta_{q,q_{i,\text{in}}}-\delta_{q,q_{i,\text{out}}}.

This requires
q
to be entered as often as it is left (except, we adjust this equation by
\pm 1
for the initial and target states).

The second set of equations are effect equations.
For
\mathbb{Z}
-counters we only require a global effect equation:
\mathbf{t}_{\mathbb{Z}}-\mathbf{s}_{\mathbb{Z}}=\sum_{i=1}^{s}\mathbf{eff}(e_{i})+\sum_{i=0}^{s}\sum_{e\in E_{i}}\mathbf{eff}_{\mathbb{Z}}(e)\cdot x_{e}
.
In other words, taking transitions as often as prescribed by
x_{e}
, and the transitions
e_{i}
connecting SCCs exactly once, produces the desired effect on the
\mathbb{Z}
counters,
\mathbf{t}_{\mathbb{Z}}-\mathbf{s}_{\mathbb{Z}}
.

For
\mathbb{N}
-counters, we have an effect equation for all
i\in[s]
.
We require
\mathbf{y}_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{i}=\sum_{e\in E_{i}}\mathbf{eff}_{\mathbb{N}}(e)\cdot x_{e}
.
This states that the effects on
\mathbb{N}
-counters should match the difference between the values
\mathbf{y}_{i}
and
\mathbf{x}_{i}
which are specified for leaving and entering the given SCC.

The final set of equations require the transitions
e_{i}
between SCCs
\mathcal{V}_{i-1}
and
\mathcal{V}_{i}
is to be taken correctly.
We add the equation
\mathbf{x}_{i}=\mathbf{y}_{i-1}+\mathbf{eff}_{\mathbb{N}}(e_{i})
and
y_{i-1,j}=test_{e_{i}}[j]
, for every
j
such that
test_{v}[j]\neq\omega
.

We can now turn back to the definition of perfectness. First we define when the ILP is perfect.

Definition 24
.

Let
(\mathbf{s},\mathcal{V},\mathbf{t})
be a query.
Then
ILP(\mathbf{s},\mathcal{V},\mathbf{t})

perfect
if

1.

ILP(\mathbf{s},\mathcal{V},\mathbf{t})
has a solution;

2.

there is a homogeneous solution
\mathbf{h}
s.t.
\mathbf{h}[x_{e}]>0
, for every transition
e
; and

3.

for every
i\in[s]
, if
test_{e_{i}}[j]=\omega
for some
j
, then variable
y_{i-1,j}
is unbounded in the ILP (i.e. if the
j
-th counter is not zero-tested when entering an SCC
\mathcal{V}_{i}
, then the
j
-th counter is unbounded at this point in the run).

Condition (1) is as expected, since any run
\rho
of
\mathcal{V}
gives rise to a solution to the ILP and hence, we certainly require an ILP solution.
Condition (2) essentially states that every transition can be taken arbitrarily often, which is a typical and crucial aspect of perfectness in KLMST.
Condition (3) guarantees that if the
j
-th counter is bounded when leaving of some SCC
\mathcal{V}_{i}
, then we know the value of this counter (at this point, on any run).

The second half of perfectness now requires that
test_{e_{i}}+\mathbf{eff}_{\mathbb{N}}(e_{i})
, the
\omega
-configuration reached when entering
\mathcal{V}_{i}
, is pumpable (inside of
\mathcal{V}_{i}
) in a certain sense that is formalised in the following definition.
The definition refers to “rigid counters”.
We will not define formally here as they are a side detail in the overall scheme, but the value of a rigid counter is completed determined by the control state.
For the sake of understanding, the reader should think that no rigid counters are present.

Definition 25
.

Let
(\mathbf{s},\mathcal{V},\mathbf{t})
be a query. Let
q_{i,\text{in}},q_{i,\text{out}}
be the states for entering and leaving the SCC
\mathcal{V}_{i}
, respectively.

We call
\mathcal{V}

forward-pumpable
if, for every SCC
\mathcal{V}_{i}
, there exists a cycle
\mathbf{up}_{i}
at
q_{i,\text{in}}
which is enabled at
test_{e_{i}}+\mathbf{eff}_{\mathbb{N}}(e_{i})
and has a (strictly) positive effect on all
\mathbb{N}
-counters
j
such that
test_{e_{i}}[j]\neq\omega
(and
j
is not rigid in
\mathcal{V}_{i}
).

Similarly, we call
\mathcal{V}

backward-pumpable
if, for every SCC
\mathcal{V}_{i}
, there exists a cycle
\mathbf{dwn}_{i}
on
q_{i,\text{out}}
which is
enabled at
test_{e_{i+1}}
and has (strictly) negative effect on all
\mathbb{N}
-counters
j
such that
test_{e_{i+1}}[j]\neq\omega
(and
j
is not rigid in
\mathcal{V}_{i}
).

Finally,
(\mathbf{s},\mathcal{V},\mathbf{t})
is
perfect
if it is forward-pumpable, backward-pumpable, and ILP
(\mathbf{s},\mathcal{V},\mathbf{t})
is perfect.

The proof that perfectness implies a run from
\mathbf{s}
to
\mathbf{t}
in
\mathcal{V}
(i.e. the proof of Lemma
18
), is essentially the same as for prior versions.
This is because the
\mathbb{Z}
-counters are automatically handled by the ILP and cannot otherwise disturb the validity of the run.
We hence only include a quick proof sketch in
Appendix

C
.

5.3
Deciding Perfectness and the Decomposition

Now that we have set up the definitions, the next step is to decide perfectness and decompose in when some condition of perfectness is failed.
The procedures for checking perfectness and decomposing are the heart of KLMST.

Thankfully for our purposes, most of the checks and decompositions are the same as they are for VASS.
The decrease of
\mathbf{rank}_{\mathbb{N}}(V)
, which was used as the rank also in
[
22
,
15
,
16
]
, can simply be copied to almost all decompositions.
There is, however, one place where we perform a major update:
\mathbf{V}_{\mathbb{Z}}
.
We may also skip over another detail; namely the rigid counters.
The difference between “cleaning” and “refining” steps is that cleaning decomposition steps need not decrease the rank; this is handled in
[
22
,
15
,
16
]
.

One new building block is the following lemma.
We use
Rel(\mathcal{V})
to denote the reachability relation of
\mathcal{V}
, and say that
\mathcal{V}
is
0
-dimensional
if
\dim(\pi_{\mathbb{N}}(\mathbf{V}(\mathcal{V},q)))=0
, for every state
q\in Q
.

Lemma 26
.

There is an elementary-time algorithm which, given a
0
-dimensional VASS+
\mathbb{Z}

\mathcal{V}
whose unary encoding is exponentially large but uses a linear number of
\mathbb{Z}
-counters
k
, produces finitely many
0
-dimensional VASS+
\mathbb{Z}

\mathcal{V}_{j}
such that every
\mathcal{V}_{j}
has polynomial size w.r.t. the size of
\mathcal{V}
and
Rel(\mathcal{V})=\bigcup_{j}Rel(\mathcal{V}_{j})
.

The proof of Lemma
26
is similar (in fact a simpler version of)
Lemma

4
.
A proof of Lemma
26
can be found on Appendix
C
.

We now provide an overview of the various decomposition steps.

Condition (1)

Checking the existence of a solution to ILP
(\mathbf{s},\mathcal{V},\mathbf{t})
is in
NP
(see, for example, book
[
32
]
).
Clearly if the ILP has no solution, then there is no run from
\mathbf{s}
to
\mathbf{t}
in
\mathcal{V}
, so the empty decomposition is returned (reachability does not hold).

Condition (2)

This is the main change compared to VASS (without additional integer counters), its explanation is detailed later.

Condition (3)

We can check in
NP
whether a variable is bounded, and for bounded variables we can, in exponential time, compute the set of possible values, since every minimal solution to an ILP has polynomial size
[
32
]
.
For every one of the possibly exponentially many different values
b
of a variable
y_{i,j}
, we create a separate generalised VASS
\mathcal{V}_{b}
where we set
test_{e_{i}}[j]=b
at the exit of SCC
\mathcal{V}_{i}
.
This ensures that
y_{i,j}
now has a unique solution.
Symmetrically, we create generalised VASS for every possible value of
x_{i,j}
.

Pumpability

Observe that this property only cares for
\mathbb{N}
-counters.
The conditions only state the effect on
\mathbb{N}
-counters, and being enabled does not depend on the values of the
\mathbb{Z}
-counters.
Hence, we can equivalently check this condition in the projected VASS
\pi_{\mathbb{N}}(\mathcal{V})
, where
\pi_{\mathbb{N}}
removes the
\mathbb{Z}
-counters to obtain a
d
-VASS.
Without integer counter, we can therefore apply
[
22
, Lemma 4.13]
without modification: there is a
j
such that the
j
-th counter is bounded by
\textup{size}_{\text{u}}(\mathcal{V})^{O(d^{d})}
, and that the decomposition simply stores the
j
-th counter in the control states (which makes it rigid).

One new difficulty is the case in which
\mathcal{V}
is
1
-dimensional, i.e. if only the least important part of the rank decreases, then we have to create a decomposition which contains potentially many generalised VASS, but each of polynomial size.
As is stated in
[
22
, Lemma 4.13]
, decomposing a
1
-dimensional SCC
\mathcal{V}
leads to a
0
dimensional VASS
\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
, i.e. in our case a
0
-dimensional VASS+
\mathbb{Z}

\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
. However,
\textup{size}_{\text{u}}(\mathcal{V}^{\prime})\leq 2^{\textup{size}_{\text{b}}(\mathcal{V}^{\prime})}
is exponential since
\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
in encoded in binary. Therefore also
\textup{size}_{\text{u}}(V)^{O(d^{d})}
is at most exponential for fixed
d
.
By Lemma
26
, we can replace
\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
by a finite set of polynomial size
\mathcal{V}_{j}
as required.

Rigid counters

Regarding rigid counters (the aspect we did not explain), the corresponding decomposition of
[
22
]
only deletes parts of the given VASS, hence again we retain polynomial size.

The decomposition step induced by condition (2)

Checking whether there exists a homogeneous solution that assigns a positive value can once again be checked in
NP
.
Importantly, an ILP has a homogeneous solution assigning value
\mathbf{h}[x_{e}]>0
if and only if the corresponding variable
x_{e}
is unbounded.
Hence if condition (2) does not hold, then the variable
x_{e}
is bounded.
The decomposition here accordingly removes the transition
e
from an SCC using the following strategy.
If
x_{e}\leq b
, then we create
b+1
copies of
(Q_{i},E_{i}\setminus\{e\})
and connect in series using
e
.

There is an important difference in our version: originally in
[
19
]
, only single transitions were removed at once.
This, however, leads to a very long decomposition sequence (already for VASS without additional integer counters).
This was subsequently improved in
[
22
]
to simultaneously removing all bounded transitions
e\in E_{i}
in some SCC
\mathcal{V}_{i}
.
This lead to an Ackermannian upper bound.
Again, our main improvement is in this step: we remove every bounded transition
e\in E_{i}
in
every SCC

\mathcal{V}_{i}
simultaneously; we do not limit ourselves to decomposing a single SCC in a particular step.

Formally, let
\mathcal{V}=\mathcal{V}_{0}e_{1}\mathcal{V}_{1}\dots e_{s}\mathcal{V}_{s}
be the current generalised VASS.
We will compute, for every
i\in[s]
, a decomposition
Decomp(\mathcal{V}_{i})
, which is a set of generalised VASS, and return

Decomp(\mathcal{V})=\{\mathcal{V}_{0}^{\prime}e_{1}\mathcal{V}_{1}^{\prime}\dots e_{s}\mathcal{V}_{s}^{\prime}:\mathcal{V}_{i}^{\prime}\in Decomp(\mathcal{V}_{i})\}.

In other words, for every
i
, we choose a VASS from
Decomp(\mathcal{V}_{i})
and connect them with transitions
e_{i}
as in
\mathcal{V}
.

We define
Decomp(V_{i})
in the same way as in
[
22
]
by removing
all
bounded transitions from this SCC and choosing an order to apply them in.
Let
\mathcal{V}_{i}=(Q_{i},E_{i})
, and let
E_{i}^{\prime}\subseteq E_{i}
be the subset of bounded transitions.
For every minimal ILP solution
\mathbf{s}
, and every word
w\in(E_{i}^{\prime})^{\ast}
with Parikh image
\psi(w)=\mathbf{s}[E_{i}^{\prime}]
, we create a VASS
V_{i,\mathbf{s},w}\in Decomp(\mathcal{V}_{i})
as a repetition of
\mathcal{V}_{\text{local}}=(Q_{i},E_{i}\setminus E_{i}^{\prime})
:

V_{i,\mathbf{s},w}:=V_{\text{local}}\,w_{1}\,V_{\text{local}}\,\cdots\,w_{|w|}\,V_{\text{local}},

where
w=w_{1}\cdots w_{|w|}
.

However, the decomposition requires another step; we promised that if the rank decreases only in the least significant location, then the blowup is polynomial.
In Lemma
27
, we will show that the rank decreases only in the least important coordinate if and only if every
\mathcal{V}_{i}
of dimension at least 2 remained unchanged, and
1
-dimensional
\mathcal{V}_{i}
is either unchanged or split into
0
-dimensional
\mathcal{V}_{i,\mathbf{s},w}\in Decomp(\mathcal{V}_{i})
.
Although solutions to ILPs have polynomial size when written in binary,
\mathcal{V}_{i,\mathbf{s},w}
may consist of exponentially many SCCs so it may have exponential size in unary.
Hence, as a last step, we check whether every
\mathcal{V}_{i,\mathbf{s},w}
dimension
0
, and if so apply Lemma
26
to decompose it into polynomial size VASS+
\mathbb{Z}
.

It is easy to see that this decomposition preserves the reachability relation, it remains to show that the rank decreases.

Lemma 27
.

The Decomposition for condition (2) decreases the rank.
Moreover, if only the least significant coordinate decreases, then all
\mathcal{V}_{i}
of dimension at least 2 remain unchanged, and every
1
-dimensional
\mathcal{V}_{i}
either remains unchanged, or is split into
0
-dimensional SCCs.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{V}^{\prime}\in Decomp(\mathcal{V})
.
We will show that
\mathbf{rank}(\mathcal{V}^{\prime})<\mathbf{rank}(\mathcal{V})
.

Case 1

\dim(\mathbf{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathcal{V}^{\prime}))<\dim(\mathbf{V}_{\mathbb{Z}}(V))
.
Since this dimension is the most important component of the rank, the rank has decreased.

Case 2

\dim(\mathbf{V}_{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathcal{V}^{\prime}))=\dim(\mathbf{V}_{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathcal{V}))
remains the same. Clearly the new VASS
\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
may only have fewer cycle effects than
\mathcal{V}
since
\mathbf{V}_{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathcal{V}^{\prime})\subseteq\mathbf{V}_{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathcal{V})
.
These statements imply
\mathbf{V}_{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathcal{V}^{\prime})=\mathbf{V}_{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathcal{V})
.

We claim the following is true for every SCC
\mathcal{V}_{i}
of
\mathcal{V}
.
Let
\mathcal{V}_{i}^{\prime}\in Decomp(\mathcal{V}_{i})
be the graph obtained from
\mathcal{V}_{i}
, let
\mathbf{V}_{\mathbb{N}}(\mathcal{V}_{i})
be the cycle space of
\mathcal{V}_{i}
(this only depends on the SCC, and hence is well-defined) and let
q
be some state of
\mathcal{V}_{i}^{\prime}
.
If
\dim(\pi_{\mathbb{N}}(\mathbf{V}_{\mathbb{N}}(q,\mathcal{V}_{i}^{\prime})))=\dim(\pi_{\mathbb{N}}(\mathbf{V}_{\mathbb{N}}(q,\mathcal{V}_{i})))
, then all transitions of
\mathcal{V}_{i}
were contained in a homogeneous solution
\mathbf{h}
.
That is to say:
Decomp(\mathcal{V}_{i})=\{\mathcal{V}_{i}\}
was not decomposed.

First, observe that this claim implies a rank decrease; since some transition was bounded, at least one SCC was decomposed.
Let
d^{\prime}
be the maximal dimension such that some SCC of dimension
d^{\prime}
was decomposed. By the claim, whenever an SCC is decomposed,
all
resulting states have lower dimension, hence
