B‐Cell Depletion Relative to Tumor Necrosis Factor Inhibition Extends the Duration of Viral Shedding in COVID‐19 Among Autoimmune Disease Patients: A Matched Prospective Cohort Study
Authors: Liya Sisay Getachew, Xiaosong Wang, Naomi J Patel, Zachary S Wallace, Lauren A O'Keeffe, Madison Negron, Jiaqi Wang, Grace Qian, Alene A Saavedra, Kevin T Mueller, Natalie Davis, Yijia Li, Brooke M Leeman, Manish C Choudhary, Sebastien Haneuse, Luke Benz, Julie Boucau, Gregory E Edelstein, Owen T Glover, Yumeko Kawano, Rinki Deo, Zahra Reynolds, Karry Su, Chase B Mandell, Zoie Smith‐Sparks, Eliza Passell, Mamadou Oury Barry, Dessie Tien, Sarah P Hammond, Gaurav D Gaiha, Jacob E Lemieux, Amy K Barczak, Jonathan Z Li, Mark J Siedner, Jeffrey A Sparks
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo investigate SARS‐CoV‐2 viral shedding duration in autoimmune patients using B cell depleting (BCD) therapy or tumor necrosis factor inhibitors (TNFi) and immunocompetent comparators.MethodsWe conducted a matched cohort analysis among participants in POSITIVES, a prospective study enrolling outpatients with acute COVID‐19 within five days of diagnosis. Anterior nasal swabs were self‐collected thrice weekly over two weeks and then weekly until two negative polymerase chain reaction (PCR) results. We compared autoimmune cases on BCD or TNFi at baseline with immunocompetent comparators, matched using a propensity score based on age, sex, race and ethnicity, number of COVID‐19 vaccinations, calendar time, and antiviral use. Survival methods with Cox proportional hazards modeling was used to compare the time to undetectable PCR.ResultsWe enrolled 16 BCD users and 30 TNFi users, each matched to 33 and 53 immunocompetent comparators, respectively. The most common autoimmune disease was rheumatoid arthritis (31% among BCD and 53% among TNFi). About 60% used an antiviral, and the mean number of COVID‐19 vaccinations was 4.3. At the time of infection, BCD users had significantly longer viral shedding duration than comparators (median 15 vs 7 days; hazard ratio [HR] 0.43, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.22–0.86; P = 0.017). The median time to undetectable viral load was similar between TNFi users and comparators (10 vs 8 days; HR 0.77, 95% CI 0.48–1.23; P = 0.27).ConclusionBCD users had eight days longer viral shedding duration than immunocompetent comparators; TNFi users and comparators had similar viral shedding duration. These findings inform clinical and public health strategies for autoimmune patients with COVID‐19.

INTRODUCTION

Individuals with autoimmune diseases have been shown to be at a higher risk of infection with SARS‐CoV‐2. 1 , 2 The risk for acute and postacute severe COVID‐19 outcomes such as hospitalization and death is also higher in these individuals. 1 This elevated risk may be in part due to their immunosuppressive medications. 3 , 4 Medications such as B cell depleting (BCD) therapies and tumor necrosis factor inhibitors (TNFi) have an impact on the immune response against SARS‐CoV‐2. 2 , 5 Previous studies showed that autoimmune patients on BCD are at risk for poor COVID‐19 outcomes and prolonged viral shedding, whereas TNFi poses no additional risk or may have potential advantages. 2 , 5 , 6 , 7 However, most previous studies investigating COVID‐19 outcomes of immunosuppressed people taking these medications were retrospective.

Several studies have reported an increased risk of adverse acute COVID‐19 outcomes and prolonged SARS‐CoV‐2 viral shedding in patients treated with BCD such as rituximab (RTX). 5 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 A retrospective US cohort study that included data from over 22,000 patients showed that baseline RTX use was associated with higher odds of severe COVID‐19 outcomes. 5 Another large nationwide Korean retrospective cohort study found that RTX treatment was associated with an increased risk of COVID‐19–related hospitalization and intensive care unit admission. 8 A seminal case report showed prolonged SARS‐CoV‐2 viral shedding over five months and intrahost viral evolution in a patient with antiphospholipid syndrome severely immunosuppressed from RTX and other medications. 12 , 13 Since then, several case series 14 , 15 and a prospective study 16 have shown similar findings in people with severe immunosuppression due to chemotherapy, stem cell transplantation, or BCD. However, reported cases may be outliers, and it is unclear how these findings relate to people on BCD for autoimmune conditions.

In contrast, treatment with TNFi has generally been associated with a lower risk of adverse COVID‐19 outcomes, 17 according to data from several large international autoimmune disease COVID‐19 registries. 2 , 9 This along with preclinical data led to the rationale that TNFi may treat acute inflammatory manifestations of COVID‐19 in the general population. 18 Early in the pandemic and before available vaccines, the ACTIV‐1 placebo‐controlled clinical trial investigated the TNFi infliximab, among other immunosuppressant medications, as a possible treatment for people with hospitalized COVID‐19 pneumonia. 19 Although the primary outcome of time to recovery was not different from the placebo in the trial, the infliximab group had lower all‐cause mortality than the placebo group (odds ratio 0.59, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.39–0.90) in a secondary analysis. 19 Thus, TNFi are commonly used immunosuppressive medications that are relatively less likely to have detrimental effects on viral persistence and may even have beneficial effects. However, there has been less of a focus on outpatient COVID‐19 outcomes among people on TNFi, particularly in a contemporary cohort.

Therefore, we aimed to prospectively investigate the duration of SARS‐CoV‐2 viral shedding in people with autoimmune diseases receiving BCD or TNFi. We hypothesized that those receiving BCD would experience prolonged viral shedding compared to immunocompetent comparators. Conversely, we hypothesized no difference in duration of viral shedding for autoimmune patients on TNFi compared to immunocompetent comparators.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study population and design

We conducted a matched prospective cohort study among participants in POSITIVES, a prospective study enrolling outpatients with acute COVID‐19 infection within five days of diagnosis in the greater Boston, MA, area from 2021 to December 2024 for this analysis. Details on the POSITIVES study have been reported before. 16 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 Potential participants were identified by querying electronic health records for new positive SARS‐CoV‐2 laboratory test, antiviral prescription, physician referral, and self‐referral. The Mass General Brigham Institutional Review Board approved the study, and all participants provided informed consent. Patients and the public were not involved in the study design or interpretation.

Sample size

For sample size and power calculations, the mean time to negative viral polymerase chain reaction (PCR) for Omicron variants in healthy people was six days (SD 3) in a previous study. 23 Assuming the same SD and matching each immunosuppressed individual to at least two immunocompetent controls, we had 80% power to detect a difference of four days in duration of viral shedding with 10 immunosuppressed people in each group. To account for loss to follow‐up and potential effects of novel variants, antivirals, monoclonal antibodies, and other treatments on viral shedding in this population, we planned to enroll at least 15 immunosuppressed cases in each arm (along with 30 immunocompetent comparators) to ensure adequate power.

TNFi and BCD cases and immunocompetent comparators

In this analysis, we investigated people who were receiving BCD or TNFi for an autoimmune disease indication at COVID‐19 onset. We excluded people with active cancer requiring immunotherapy or chemotherapy or were inpatient at the time of enrollment. We performed manual medical record review to confirm autoimmune disease status and exposure to BCD (within six months of previous dose) or TNFi (within one month of previous dose) by board‐certified physicians. Concomitant immunosuppression and glucocorticoids were allowed, but patients with active cancer were excluded. We extracted the specific types of autoimmune disease and concomitant immunosuppression as well as glucocorticoid use and dose in prednisone equivalent milligrams. SARS‐CoV‐2 spike antibody, CD19, or CD20 blood levels were not available at study entry.

For each autoimmune case, immunocompetent comparators with acute COVID‐19 were matched (about 1:2) using propensity scores based on age, sex, race, ethnicity, number of prior COVID‐19 vaccinations at the time of infection, calendar time of infection, and use of antivirals to treat COVID‐19. All potential immunocompetent comparators were verified to have no immunocompromising condition or immunosuppressive medication by manual medical record review by board‐certified physicians.

SARS‐CoV‐2 viral load

All participants were required to have a documented positive SARS‐CoV‐2 test within five days of enrollment. Anterior nasal swabs were self‐collected at home three times weekly over two weeks and then weekly until two negative quantitative PCR results.

SARS‐CoV‐2 viral RNA was quantified as described previously. 23 Briefly, virions were pelleted from nasal swab fluid by centrifugation at 21,000g for two hours at 4°C. TRIzol‐LS Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was added to the pellet, vortexed, and incubated on ice for 10 minutes. Chloroform was added, and the solution was vortexed before centrifugation at 21,000g for 15 minutes at 4°C. RNA was isolated from the aqueous layer by isopropanol precipitation and eluted in diethyl pyrocarbonate–treated water (Thermo Fisher Scientific). SARS‐CoV‐2 RNA copies were quantified with an in‐house viral load assay using the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention 2019‐nCoV_N1 primer and probe set (Integrated DNA Technologies).

SARS‐CoV‐2 viral RNA clearance was defined as SARS‐CoV‐2 viral RNA from nasal swabs reached below the lower quantifiable (<40 copies/mL) threshold. In participants who had viral rebound after initial viral clearance, we used the time point when viral RNA first became unquantifiable.

SARS‐CoV‐2 viral culture

Viral culture was performed as previously reported. 23 Vero‐E6 cells (the American Type Culture Collection) maintained in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM; Corning) supplemented with Hepes (Corning), 1× penicillin (100 IU/mL) or streptomycin (100 μg/mL) (Corning), 1× glutamine (GlutaMAX, Thermo Fisher Scientific), and 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; MilliporeSigma) were plated 16 to 20 hours before infection. Each sample consisting of nasal swab fluid was thawed on ice and filtered through a Spin‐X 0.45‐μm filter (Corning) at 10,000g for 5 minutes. Before infection, the medium was changed to DMEM supplemented with Hepes, 1× antibiotic or antimycotic (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 1× glutamine, 2% FBS, and polybrene (5 μg/mL; Santa Cruz Biotechnologies). Each filtered sample was then used to inoculate Vero‐E6 cells by spinfection (2,000g for 1 hour at 37°C). The plates were observed at seven days after infection using a light microscope to check for cytopathogenic effect (CPE), and a median tissue culture infectious dose (TCID50) was calculated for each sample.

For culturable virus clearance, we chose the first time point when there was no CPE effect and where all subsequent samples did not have culturable viruses (if there were subsequent samples collected).

Statistical analysis

We first matched each autoimmune case to about two immunocompetent comparators, defined as people with no immunosuppressing medical conditions and no use of immunosuppressing agents. We used nearest‐neighbor propensity score matching without caliper length that included age, sex, race, ethnicity, number of prior COVID‐19 vaccinations, calendar time, and use of antivirals. We then selected matched pairs of immunocompetent individuals in a 2:1 ratio to the immunosuppressed cases.

We then compared each autoimmune case group with their comparators using survival methods for the outcomes of undetectable viral load as well as for negative viral culture. We plotted Kaplan–Meier curves and reported the 25th, 50th, and 75th percentiles from survival curves as median and interquartile range (IQR) for each group. We also directly compared each immunosuppressed group with each other for each outcome addressed. We then fit unadjusted Cox proportional hazards models with time to negative PCR or culture as the outcome of interest and immunosuppression versus immunocompetent status as the predictor interest. For those with lengthy viral shedding (>30 days), we reported clinical characteristics and also performed sequencing on all available samples to detect drug resistance mutations in the spike and recombinant receptor binding domain (RbRd) regions, as previously described. 16

Finally, we modeled viral load decay trajectories with linear mixed‐effects models for each group with one knot at day 7. As described previously, we compared viral decay trajectories for TNFi and BCD user groups with their matched immunocompetent comparators as well as directly to each other.

The proportional hazards assumption was examined using Schoenfeld residuals and was met in all analyses. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 and R software.

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics

As of December 2024, we identified 16 BCD and 30 TNFi users for autoimmune conditions at the time of acute COVID‐19 onset that enrolled in POSITIVES. These were matched to 30 and 53 immunocompetent comparators, respectively (Table 1). The mean age of the participants was about 51 years. About 75% of the analyzed participants were women, and about 80% reported White race.

The most common systemic autoimmune rheumatic disease type was rheumatoid arthritis (RA; 31% among BCD users and 53% in the TNFi group). RTX was the BCD therapy for all cases in that group. The most common concomitant immunosuppressive medication used was methotrexate (31% in BCD and 43% in TNFi), followed by systemic glucocorticoids (25% in BCD and 27% in TNFi).

The mean number of prior COVID‐19 vaccinations was 4.2 among BCD users and 4.3 among TNFi users; the immunocompetent comparators had similar numbers of vaccinations. A total of six individuals were unvaccinated, including two autoimmune cases, one from each case group. Most of the participants received antiviral treatment for COVID‐19, including 69% of BCD users and 77% of TNFi users.

Time to undetectable viral load

BCD users had a significantly longer median time to undetectable viral load (15 days, IQR 7–23) compared with their comparators (7 days, IQR 3–13; hazard ratio [HR] 0.43, 95% CI 0.22–0.86, P = 0.017; Figure 1). The median time to undetectable viral load was similar between TNFi users (10 days, IQR 6–13) and their comparators (8 days, IQR 4–12; HR 0.77, 95% CI 0.48–1.23, P = 0.27). When directly comparing the immunosuppressed groups, the BCD users had about five days longer viral duration than TNFi users that was statistically significant (HR 0.50, 95% CI 0.25–1.00; P = 0.0499).

There were two BCD users that had viral persistence lasting over 30 days. The first was a 66‐year‐old man with antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody–associated vasculitis on RTX at acute COVID‐19 onset who developed persistent sinusitis. The second was a 65‐year‐old woman with RA on RTX and methotrexate at the time of COVID‐19. Neither had significant comorbidities, and both were noted clinically to test persistently positive for SARS‐CoV‐2. We tested both participants for drug resistance mutations in the spike and RdRd regions and found none in any sample.

Time to negative viral culture is shown in Supplemental Figure 1. Although there were no statistical differences, the direction and magnitude were similar to the primary analysis that investigated viral load. For example, BCD had an HR for negative viral culture of 0.59 (95% CI 0.31–1.15, P = 0.12) compared to their immunocompetent comparators.

DISCUSSION

In this matched prospective cohort study that used home‐based serial sample collection of nasal swabs in people with acute COVID‐19, we found that BCD users for autoimmune diseases had about eight days longer duration of SARS‐CoV‐2 viral shedding than immunocompetent comparators. Reassuringly, only a small portion of BCD users had a lengthy (ie, >30 days) duration of viral shedding. In contrast, people with autoimmune disease using TNFi had a similar duration of viral shedding as immunocompetent comparators, suggesting minimal impact of this medication on SARS‐CoV‐2 viral kinetics. These findings provide data to patients, clinicians, and public health authorities about the effect of BCD on prolonged SARS‐CoV‐2 duration in a contemporary cohort.

Immunosuppressive medications such as BCD and TNFi have been shown to have varying impact on patients with autoimmune disease with COVID‐19. BCD has been consistently associated with poor COVID‐19 outcomes that include risk of hospitalization 5 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 24 , 25 , 26 or death as well as breakthrough infection, 27 repeat infection, 28 and long COVID. 29 BCD therapies have been previously reported to delay SARS‐CoV‐2 viral clearance for up to six months in various case reports and case series since the start of the COVID‐19 pandemic, often among people with severe immunosuppression and cancer, often hospitalized. 12 , 30 , 31 , 32 In our analysis, outpatients receiving BCD experienced significantly prolonged viral shedding when compared to both people without immunosuppression and those receiving TNFi. Our finding supports results from previous studies that have found prolonged viral shedding in patients receiving BCD. A case report of a patient with RA receiving RTX showed a significantly prolonged viral shedding, which resulted in a positive COVID‐19 PCR test persisting five months after the initial infection. 33 Unlike most previous studies, ours was an unselected population with autoimmune disease who experienced outpatient COVID‐19 and had available matched comparators. BCD is known to severely blunt vaccine humoral response, 34 which may be a mechanism for our findings.

Delayed viral shedding among immunosuppressed patients may be a reservoir for circulating SARS‐CoV‐2 and produce new viral variants. A seminal early case report before available vaccination among a patient with antiphospholipid syndrome with severe immunosuppression including BCD was hospitalized for COVID‐19 had prolonged SARS‐CoV‐2 for about five months. 12 There was evidence for in‐host viral evolution, with mutations similar to the Delta and Omicron SARS‐CoV‐2 variants. 12 , 13 Another case report of a patient with follicular lymphoma who received RTX experienced recurrent relapse of symptoms with PCR results remaining positive for up to 189 days. 35 Similar findings were found in a case report about a patient receiving combinations of immunosuppressive medication which notably included B cell‐directed antibody treatment with SARS‐CoV‐2 infection for at least 119 days. 36 A previous POSTIVES study examined severe immunosuppression, including BCD and cancer therapies, and showed delayed SARS‐CoV‐2 clearance and evidence for in‐host evolution compared to immunocompetent comparators. 16 Our study extends those findings by focusing on patients with autoimmune diseases and including TNFi as another comparison group. Of note, duration of culturable virus was also numerically longer in the BCD group, though this was not statistically significant likely due to the low sample size. However, there were two BCD participants who shed virus >30 days and neither had evidence of in‐host viral evolution. Thus, these findings provide more data to patients and clinicians regarding the effect of BCD on SARS‐CoV‐2 viral kinetics. Future studies should investigate whether specific actions should be taken that include longer duration of antivirals or longer periods of isolation for people with autoimmune diseases receiving BCD.

In contrast to BCD, TNFi had less impact on viral clearance in our study. Although TNFi may be associated with increased risk of viral infections, 37 this medication class is generally associated with favorable clinical outcomes for COVID‐19, often with better outcomes than alternative immunosuppressive medications. 1 , 2 , 6 , 9 , 38 The ACTIV‐1 clinical trial investigated infliximab as a treatment for patients with COVID‐19 pneumonia. 19 Although the primary outcome of time to recovery was not met, secondary analyses suggested a potentially lower mortality rate, 19 also providing reassurance to lack of detrimental effect on viral outcomes for this drug class. To our knowledge, this study is the first to investigate baseline use of TNFi for autoimmune conditions and its effects on SARS‐CoV‐2 viral kinetics. Our study did not show any association between TNFi use and prolonged viral shedding compared to immunocompetent comparators, which may be reassuring for patients and clinicians regarding this commonly used immunosuppressant drug class.

BCD therapies are monoclonal antibodies that target the CD20 protein that is expressed on B lymphocytes. 39 This target is critical in the regulation of B cell cycle and differentiation. By binding to this antigen, BCDs activate various pathways that lead to the depletion of B cells, thereby interfering with the larger humoral immunity. In addition, because B cells are responsible for the production of Igs, their depletion impairs the ability to mount an adequate humoral response to COVID‐19 contributing to continued viral shedding in these patients and reduced COVID‐19 vaccine responsiveness. 34 , 40 This may explain why, even though our participants were matched on the number of prior COVID‐19 vaccinations, BCD users may have had a limited antibody response which likely contributed to the longer viral shedding in this group. BCD also affects T cell responses as they play a key role in T cell priming and activation. B cells act as antigen‐presenting cells and are also a key player in CD8+ T cell regulation and survival. 41 , 42 Given the important role of cytotoxic T cells in SARS‐CoV‐2 clearance, BCD has a significant impact, which likely contributes to the observed delay in viral clearance. Conversely, TNFi seem to have less of an important role in viral clearance, supported by studies showing minimal impact on vaccine immunogenicity. 34

Our study has several important strengths. Both immunosuppressed and immunocompetent consecutive patients were recruited and followed prospectively. In addition, we collected serial samples through frequent home collection and performed quantitative PCR and viral culture on all the samples. This increased our chances of capturing changes in viral load as they occurred providing a better characterization of viral kinetics.

Several limitations should also be noted. All participants experienced acute COVID‐19 in the outpatient setting, so our findings may not be generalizable to more severe cases requiring hospitalization. However, outpatient COVID‐19 is the most common clinical scenario. Although we included antiviral use in the propensity score for matching, residual confounding may still be present related to timing and differential effect of the antiviral therapy. Participants were recruited from a single geographic area, had to have a documented positive laboratory test, and healthier people may have been more likely to participate, which may also affect generalizability. The nasal samples were self‐collected which may have introduced variability and inaccuracy, although this is unlikely to differ systematically by immunocompromised status. Our sample size was small. However, we were still able to detect statistically significant associations between groups. Variants of SARS‐CoV‐2 have evolved since the study period during which Omicron was the major variant. We adjusted for calendar time as a surrogate for variant, but this may still limit our ability to generalize to other circulating variants. We only investigated two classes of immunosuppressive medications and only analyzed patients with autoimmune diseases which may make it difficult to extrapolate findings to other populations (eg, patient with malignancies) and classes of immunosuppressive medications. Given limited sample size, we were unable to perform subgroup analyses investigating the impact of concomitant immunosuppression. In addition, glucocorticoid dosing was extracted manually from the electronic health record and may be subject to variability, which should be considered in interpretation of the findings. The majority of participants had systemic rheumatic diseases, so it may not extrapolate to all patients with autoimmune diseases. All participants on BCD therapy were receiving RTX, so it is unclear whether other BCD therapies such as CD19 inhibition or CAR T cell therapy might have similar findings. Participants on TNFi may have discontinued their medication during acute infection, which may not be possible for patients receiving BCD given the long half‐life of RTX and which could be another mechanism for our findings. Finally, CD19/CD20 measurements were unavailable, and we were unable to account for time since last BCD infusion or cumulative BCD exposure due to low sample size, both of which may influence the degree of BCD and contribute to differences within the BCD group.

In conclusion, patients with autoimmune diseases on BCD therapies had longer SARS‐CoV‐2 viral shedding when compared to both immunocompetent comparators and patients receiving TNFi. In contrast, individuals receiving TNFi had a similar rate and duration of viral shedding when compared to their immunocompetent comparators. In this study, none of the participants receiving TNFi and two of those on BCD experienced lengthy viral shedding which may be reassuring. These results have significant public health implications as prolonged viral replication increases the possibility of intrahost viral evolution, which may contribute to the emergence of resistant strains and new variants. Further studies are needed to determine whether treatment with BCDs is associated with intrahost viral evolution and to assess the impact of prolonged viral shedding on clinical outcomes.
