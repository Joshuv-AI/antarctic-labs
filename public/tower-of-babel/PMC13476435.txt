Association of income inequality and aggregate health indicators in Japan: analyses of prefecture-level panel data
Authors: Hitoshi Murakam, Azusa Iwamoto, Ayako Masu, Miho Sodeno, Rei Haruyama, Sumiyo Okawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEvidence indicates that income inequality is associated with poor health outcomes. In Japan, while the relationship between income inequality and self-rated health has been explored, the association between income inequality and aggregate health indicators remains insufficiently examined. This study seeks to add evidence on the association between income inequality and population health status in the Japanese context by investigating the relationship between the Gini coefficient and aggregate health indicators across 47 prefectures in 1999, 2004, 2009, 2014, and 2019. Associations between the Gini coefficient and health indicators, adjusted for average per capita income, population density, and year dummies, were assessed using pooled ordinary least squares (OLS), fixed-effects, and random-effects regression models. We treated year as a time variable and prefecture as the unit variable in the fixed- and random-effects regression models. In the pooled OLS analysis, we disregarded this panel structure and treated all 235 observations as independent cases. A significant association between the Gini coefficient and the infant mortality rate (b = 7.93, p = 0.008) was observed in the OLS model, which was tested as the most appropriate. The result was consistent with that derived from an alternative model using log-transformed income figures, indicating the robustness of the finding. Although the findings suggest the importance of income inequality for further improvements in Japan’s infant mortality rate, more conclusive evidence requires additional studies incorporating multilevel frameworks, time-lag analyses, and a broader range of healthcare-related factors.Keywords: Gini coefficient, Income inequality, Mortality, Japan, Panel data analysis, Partial correlationSubject terms: Diseases, Health care, Medical research, Risk factors

Introduction

The health status of the population is determined not only by the performance of health and medical services, but also largely by various social determinants, including social position, education, occupation, and income1. A study conducted in the United States attributed 15% of premature deaths to social circumstances and 40% to behavioral patterns2. However, health-related behaviors are also known to be largely shaped by social factors3. In Canada, it was estimated that the impact of the social and economic environment on the health status of the population was 50%4.

Among the social determinants of health, income inequality, aside from income level, is recognized as being associated with poorer health outcomes across populations. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses have demonstrated the adverse effects of income inequality, most commonly measured by the Gini coefficient, on infant and child mortality5, COVID-19 mortality6, and mental health, including depression7,8.

In Japan, an increase in income inequality was observed from 1984 to 2019, primarily driven by population aging, as the proportion of elderly households, who tend to exhibit higher levels of inequality, continued to rise9. A comparison of household income distribution in Japan with that of other member states of Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD), based on 1999–2002 data, revealed that Japan’s income distribution was more unequal. The ratio of incomes at the top and bottom ends of the distribution—measured by the upper bound of the ninth decile relative to that of the first decile—was roughly 30% higher than the OECD average. Likewise, the proportion of equivalised household disposable income received by the highest quintile was nearly six times that of the lowest quintile, exceeding the OECD average by about 20%10.

Several Japanese studies using multilevel analysis have identified an association between area-based income inequality and self-rated health11,12. Another study suggested that relative deprivation—defined as the perception or feeling of being worse off compared to others—may serve as a mediator of this association13. Conversely, a cross-sectional study found that individual income, rather than prefectural income inequality, was associated with self-rated health14. However, the association and correlation between income inequality and aggregate health indicators across prefectures remain insufficiently examined.

Building on the above background, the objective of this study was to examine the association between the income Gini coefficient and aggregate health indicators—including the age-adjusted mortality rate, age-adjusted premature (0–74 years) mortality rate, stillbirth rate, perinatal mortality rate, early neonatal mortality rate, and infant mortality rate—across Japan’s 47 prefectures over five time points of 1999, 2004, 2009, 2014, and 2019, using prefecture-level panel data. By assessing these associations using rigorous models, this study seeks to contribute evidence on the role of social determinants of health—particularly income inequality—in shaping population health in the Japanese context, and to provide insights for health and social policy formulation.

Data and methods

Data

Aggregate health indicators encompassing the premature mortality rate, stillbirth rate, perinatal mortality rate, early neonatal mortality rate, infant mortality rate, and maternal mortality ratio across Japan’s 47 prefectures were obtained from the Annual Report of Vital Statistics (ARVS) published by the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare (MHLW), Japan. Population and death data by prefecture, sex, and five-year age groups, used to calculate the age-adjusted mortality rate and age-adjusted premature mortality rate, were obtained from the Population Estimates published by the Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications (MIC) and from ARVS published by the MHLW, Japan, respectively. The Gini coefficients for annual household income at the prefectural level were obtained from the National Survey of Family Income and Expenditure (NSFIE), conducted every 5 years by the MIC, Japan. Two covariates—per capita income and population density—were obtained from the Annual Report on Prefectural Accounts published by the Cabinet Office of Japan and from the System of Social and Demographic Statistics published by the Statistics Bureau, MIC, Japan, respectively.

Aggregate health indicators (dependent variables)

Our dependent variables were the age-adjusted mortality rate, the age-adjusted premature (0–74 years) mortality rate, the stillbirth rate, the perinatal mortality rate, the early neonatal mortality rate, and the infant mortality rate across Japan’s 47 prefectures for the years 1999, 2004, 2009, 2014, and 2019. These years were selected because the Gini coefficient, our independent variable, was available only for those time points.

The age-adjusted mortality rate was defined as the mortality rate per 100,000 population that would be observed if the 47 prefectures in 1999, 2004, 2009, 2014, and 2019 had the same age and sex structure as a standard population, namely the 2020 Japanese national population. It was calculated by applying age-specific death rates in 5-year age intervals for both sexes for each prefecture in those years to the 2020 population, which served as the standard population based on the national census of that year, stratified by sex. The age-adjusted premature mortality rate was defined as the number of deaths per 100,000 population aged 0–74 years, following the OECD definition, and was age-adjusted with sex stratification in the same manner as the age-adjusted mortality rate. In both cases, the oldest age stratum was defined as 80 years and over, as this was the highest age category available in the sex-stratified population data for 1999 and 2004, although the latter year provided a more detailed breakdown of this category.

Definitions of the other indicators followed those used in the ARVS published by the MHLW, Japan. The stillbirth rate is defined as the number of stillborn fetuses at or after 12 completed weeks of gestation (the fourth month of pregnancy) per 1,000 total births. The perinatal mortality rate refers to the number of stillbirths at ≥ 22 weeks of gestation and early neonatal deaths per 1,000 total births, defined as the sum of live births and stillbirths at ≥ 22 weeks of gestation. The early neonatal mortality rate refers to the number of deaths during the first 7 days of life per 1,000 live births. The infant mortality rate refers to the number of deaths before the age of 1 year per 1,000 live births.

The child mortality indicators—including the stillbirth rate, perinatal mortality rate, early neonatal mortality rate, and infant mortality rate—were included because existing evidence generally indicates a stronger correlation and association between income inequality and child health outcomes than with adult health outcomes15–18.

Income inequality indicator (independent variable)

Our independent variable was the income Gini coefficient for Japan’s 47 prefectures in the years 1999, 2004, 2009, 2014, and 2019. A Gini coefficient of 0 indicates perfect equality, where all households in a given area have the same income, whereas a coefficient of 1 indicates perfect inequality, where a single household holds all the income.

Covariates

As covariates, we included average per capita income and population density for each prefecture in 1999, 2004, 2009, 2014, and 2019. We also included year as a set of dummy variables. Average income represents the economic status of each prefecture, while the year variables capture overall temporal trends.

Regression analyses

The associations between the Gini coefficient and the health indicators, adjusted for average per capita income, population density, and year (dummy variables), were assessed using pooled ordinary least squares (OLS), fixed-effects, and random-effects generalized least squares (GLS) regression models. Figure 1 presents the directed acyclic graph (DAG) used for causal inference on the relationship between income inequality and population health status, incorporating potential confounders. By applying the backdoor criterion, average income per capita, population density, and year are identified as the minimal adjustment set, as adjusting for these variables blocks all backdoor paths to the dependent variable—population health status, as measured by aggregate health indicators—and none of them are descendants of the exposure, namely income inequality as indicated by the Gini coefficient. Conversely, medical resource density—typically measured by the number of hospital beds or physicians per population—lacks plausible evidence of influencing income inequality. This is particularly the case in the Japanese context, where universal health insurance coverage has been achieved and a nationally standardized fee schedule applies to both public and private healthcare facilities.

In the panel data analysis using fixed-effects and random-effects models, the years were used as the time variable and the prefectures as the individual identification variable. The OLS model can be written as:

where yit is the aggregate health indicators of prefecture i in the year t; x is the Gini coefficient; 0 is the intercept (constant term), representing the baseline level of the outcome when all independent variables are set to zero; β are the regression coefficients for the independent variable (Gini coefficient) and covariates (the average per capita income and the number of hospital beds per 100,000 population); γ are the effect of each covariate; and εit is an error term indicating everything affecting yit not captured by xit.

The fixed-effects model can be written as:

where yit is the aggregate health indicators of prefecture i in the year t; β are the slope regression coefficients within prefecture effects for the independent variable and covariates; αi is an unobserved, time-invariant prefectural characteristics; and εit is an error term indicating everything affecting yit not captured by xit.

Lastly, the random-effects model can be written as:

where yit is the aggregate health indicators of prefecture i in the year t; 0 is the intercept (constant term), representing the baseline level of the outcome when all independent variables are set to zero; β are the slope regression coefficients within prefecture effects for the independent variable and covariates; ui is the prefecture-specific random effect; and εit is an error term indicating everything affecting yit not captured by xit.

For standard errors (SEs), robust SEs clustered at the individual prefecture level were used. To determine the most appropriate model, the F-test, Hausman test, and Breusch-Pagan test were conducted. The F-test evaluates the null hypothesis that the pooled OLS model is more suitable than the fixed-effects model. The Hausman test evaluates the null hypothesis that the random-effects model is more suitable than the fixed-effects model. The Breusch-Pagan test evaluates the null hypothesis that the pooled OLS model is more suitable than the random-effects model. We rejected the null hypotheses at p < 0.05. Through these tests, we can select the most “appropriate” model among pooled OLS, fixed-effects, and random-effects models that provides the most valid and reliable estimates, given the structure of the data and the underlying assumptions about unobserved heterogeneity. With respect to these assumptions, pooled OLS assumes no unobserved heterogeneity; the fixed-effects model allows unobserved effects to be correlated with the explanatory variables; and the random-effects model assumes that unobserved effects are uncorrelated with the explanatory variables.

The R2 values generally indicate the proportion of variation in the outcome variables (namely, the aggregate health indicators) explained by the model, which includes the Gini coefficient and three covariates—average per capita income, population density, and year (dummy variables). The Overall R2 in our panel data analysis indicates how well the model explains total variation, combining variation within each prefecture over time and variation between prefectures. The Within R2 indicates how well the model explains variation within each prefecture over time, while the Between R2 indicates how well the model explains differences between prefectures.

All analyses were conducted using StataNow version 19.5 (BE) (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA).

Results of regression analyses

Table 2 presents the results of the regression analyses using pooled OLS, fixed-effects, and random-effects models. A significant association was observed only between the Gini coefficient and the infant mortality rate (b = 7.93, p = 0.008). The regression model for the infant mortality rate showed a relatively high overall R² of 0.56, indicating that the model, which includes the Gini coefficient and three covariates—average per capita income, population density, and year (dummy variables)—explains a substantial proportion of the variation in the outcome variable, the infant mortality rate. The regression coefficients indicate that a 0.01 increase in the Gini coefficient, which ranges from 0 to 1, is associated with an increase of 0.079 infant deaths per 1,000 live births.

Notably, the F-tests rejected the null hypothesis of no individual effects in most model specifications, except for the infant mortality rate model, indicating the presence of significant unobserved prefecture-specific heterogeneity. The Hausman test, conversely, did not reject the null hypothesis in most model specifications, except for the stillbirth rate, suggesting no systematic evidence that unobserved prefecture-specific effects are correlated with the regressors (i.e., the Gini coefficient and three covariates: average per capita income, population density, and year dummies).

It is also noteworthy that the Breusch–Pagan tests rejected the null hypothesis of no panel effects in most model specifications, except for the infant mortality rate model, indicating the presence of significant unobserved prefecture-specific heterogeneity in most cases. Taken together, these results suggest that, in most model specifications, there is significant unobserved heterogeneity across the 47 prefectures; however, these effects do not appear to be systematically correlated with the explanatory variables.

Discussion

This study demonstrated that income inequality was significantly associated with infant mortality at the prefectural level in Japan, after adjusting for income level, population density, and year as dummy variables. The two covariates, namely the income and population density, were also important predictors of stillbirth rates. The relatively high overall R² of 0.56 for the infant mortality rate model indicates that the model, which includes the Gini coefficient and three covariates—average per capita income, population density, and year (dummy variables)—explains a substantial proportion of the variation in infant mortality.

This study has several limitations. First, as an ecological study, it cannot establish associations between income inequality and mortality at the individual level. Second, although various risk factors at the individual, community, and societal levels contribute to mortality across different age groups, as discussed in this section, our analysis adjusted only for income level, population density, and year (included as a dummy variable). The use of an ecological design based on prefecture-level data inherently limits the ability to control for risk factors at the individual or small-area level.

Third, there may be additional prefecture-level covariates that should have been included. For example, with regard to cohabitation patterns—proxied in this study by population density—the level of urbanization may act as a confounder. However, given that Japan’s overall share of the urban population is 92.1%, according to the most recent estimate by the World Bank based on its definition19, the extent of variability may be limited. In addition, within Japan, the proportion of the population living in Densely Inhabited Districts (DIDs) is available only for census years. Although both fixed-effects and random-effects models account for time-invariant factors, the final model selected for infant mortality—based on the results of three specification tests—was the pooled OLS model. Nevertheless, the random-effects model, which assumes that these factors are uncorrelated with the regressors and was judged to be more appropriate than the fixed-effects model by the Hausman test, revealed a significant association between the Gini coefficient and the infant mortality rate.

The association and correlation between income inequality and health outcomes have been studied using global and regional country-level data. Dorling et al. demonstrated that mortality was correlated with income inequality across 124 countries worldwide15. Torre and Myrskylä showed that the Gini coefficient was associated with male and female mortality up to age 15 in 21 high-income countries16. Pickett et al. revealed that the UNICEF index of child well-being was negatively correlated with income inequality among 21 high-income countries17. Ray and Linden found that increasing income inequality was associated with higher infant mortality rates across 194 countries18. Overall, the existing evidence generally indicates a stronger correlation and association between income inequality and child health outcomes than with adult health outcomes. This is concordant with our infant mortality results.

Kondo et al. conducted a meta-analysis of multilevel cohort studies to estimate the pooled relative risk of income inequality on subsequent mortality, as well as a meta-analysis of multilevel cross-sectional studies to estimate the pooled odds ratio (OR) for poor self-rated health per 0.05-unit increase in the Gini coefficient20. They reported a pooled relative risk of 1.08 (95% CI: 1.06–1.10) and a pooled OR of 1.04 (95% CI: 1.02–1.06), respectively, indicating a positive association between income inequality and both mortality and poor self-rated health. The authors argue that the use of multilevel data—incorporating both individual income and the distribution of income across the area units in which individuals reside—is essential for testing the contextual effects of income inequality. This highlights a potential limitation of our study, which attempts to account for absolute income levels using average per capita income at the prefectural level.

Although we have measured both income inequality and health outcomes in the same year cross-sectionally, there is a scholarly debate over whether income inequality has an instantaneous effect on health status, or whether it takes time to manifest negative health effects. Both Subramanian and Kawachi (2004), as well as Blakely et al. (2000), have demonstrated that income inequality measured up to 15 years previously was more strongly associated with self-rated health than contemporaneous income inequality21,22. Kondo et al. (2011) also found that threshold, period, and lag effects were independent of the area unit used to evaluate inequality23. These findings clearly indicate the need for further studies using income inequality metrics with a time lag, for example, 15 years prior to the measurement of health outcomes. In contrast, Pickett and Wilkinson (2014), in their causal review, argued that the substantial body of cross-sectional studies conducted over several decades linking income inequality to health and violence suggests a temporal relationship24. Because neither income distribution nor health outcomes remains constant over time, the repeated observation of cross-sectional associations between them provides indirect evidence that they tend to change in tandem.

The significant association between the Gini coefficient and infant mortality rate (IMR) demonstrated by this study is supported by a body of literature. Spencer (2004) carried out a systematic review of the literature to investigate how income inequality and variations in macro-level social policies are related to infant mortality and low birthweight in high-income countries and subnational regions. While acknowledging methodological constraints in both the review and the underlying studies, the findings indicated a statistically significant relationship between higher income inequality—along with other markers of less redistributive social policy—and increased IMR25. Olson et al. (2010) used U.S. state-level data (2000–2004) to examine links between income, poverty, and inequality with IMR. They found a positive association between the Gini coefficient and infant mortality, which remained significant after adjusting for income. The effect of income weakened as poverty declined, while the influence of inequality increased, indicating that both factors are important determinants of infant health outcomes26.

Macinko et al. (2004) likewise supported a link between income inequality, while highlighting that this relationship is markedly weakened when health system characteristics are taken into account. Their study analyzed data from 19 OECD countries over the period 1970–1996 using Pearson correlation and fixed-effects multivariate regression. Results from both cross-sectional (year-specific) and longitudinal analyses indicated that the Theil index of wage inequality (based on sectoral wage information) was positively and significantly associated with infant mortality, even after controlling for GDP per capita. However, adjusting for factors such as healthcare financing systems and the number of physicians per 1,000 population substantially attenuated this association27. Siddiqi et al. (2015) also demonstrated the association between income inequality and IMR, controlling for U.S. state-level poverty rates, median incomes, and the proportion of high school graduates, and increasing association over 1990–2007 using fixed- and random-effects regressions. Fixed-effects and random-effects models suggested that income inequality was inversely associated with IMR (b =- 0.07, SE (0.01)) and (b = -0.05, SE (0.01)), respectively. As time increased, the effect of income inequality had an increasingly positive association with total IMR (b = 0.009, SE (0.002)28.

Conversely, there is literature that provides counter- or mixed evidence. Avendano (2012) tested the impact of income inequality on infant mortality using data of 34 OECD countries with a fixed-effects model that exploits the evolution of income inequality over a 38-year period, controlling for all time-invariant differences across countries. As a result, income inequality was not associated with infant mortality (RR = 1.00, 0.98, 1.01)29. Ehntholt et al. (2020) applied multilevel models to examine whether income inequality was associated with infant and neonatal mortality across U.S. counties and states, with county-level factors assessed as potential mediators. Adjusted analyses showed that income inequality at the county level—but not at the state level—was associated with higher odds of infant mortality (OR 1.14, 95% CI 1.10–1.18) and neonatal mortality (OR 1.17, 95% CI 1.12–1.23)30.

Notably, two studies proposed a potential mechanism underlying the association between income inequality and IMR, as well as its policy implications, respectively. Mayer and Sarin (2005) analyzed U.S. state-level data and found that greater economic inequality is associated with higher neonatal mortality, even after adjusting for maternal and state factors. Inequality was linked to increased economic segregation, which raises infant death risk, while also being associated with higher healthcare spending that reduces mortality. However, the adverse effect of segregation outweighs the benefits of increased spending, resulting in a net increase in infant deaths, particularly in the neonatal period31. Navarro et al. (2006) conducted a cross-national analysis and identified a significant association between income inequality, measured by the Theil index, and IMR. They also explored the role of political traditions, finding that countries with more redistributive policies tend to have better population health outcomes, including lower IMR, suggesting important policy implications for addressing inequality32.

None of these articles, however, addresses why income inequality is more strongly associated with child mortality than with adult mortality, as suggested by references 15–17 above. A potential explanation is that child survival depends heavily on material and environmental conditions shaped by the distribution of resources at the societal level, whereas adult mortality is more strongly influenced by long-term behavioral, occupational, and healthcare-related factors, which attenuate the observable association with inequality.

As shown in the Results section, our findings indicate that a 0.01 increase in the Gini coefficient (ranging from 0 to 1) is associated with an increase of 0.079 infant deaths per 1,000 live births. Based on this estimate, reducing infant mortality by 1 per 1,000 live births in a Japanese prefecture would require a reduction in the Gini coefficient of 0.127.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study found that income inequality, as measured by the Gini coefficient, was significantly associated with the infant mortality rate at the prefectural level in Japan after adjusting for income level, population density, and year dummies in panel data analyses of 47 prefectures for the years 1999, 2004, 2009, 2014, and 2019. These findings suggest that addressing social determinants of health, including income inequality, alongside improvements in medical interventions and the efficiency of health systems, may contribute to further reductions in Japan’s already low infant mortality rate. However, more conclusive evidence requires additional studies, potentially incorporating individual-level income within a multilevel framework, conducting time-lag analyses to examine the delayed effects of income inequality on health outcomes, and assessing the attenuation of the association between income inequality and the infant mortality rate by healthcare-related factors.
