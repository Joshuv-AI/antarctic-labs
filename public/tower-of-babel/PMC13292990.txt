GPAM mediates mitochondrial dysfunction and the progression of alcoholic liver disease through lipid remodeling
Authors: Zibin Zhan, Xuewen Liu, Zehua Li, Xueyan Qiao, Shuo Li, Yu Gong, Luping Yang, Yi Gao, Xianfeng Xia, Kunhao Bai, Fanhong Zeng, Jun Weng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Lipid metabolic disorders and mitochondrial dysfunction are key and core pathological processes that contribute to the progression of alcoholic liver disease (ALD). However, the effects of lipid metabolism disorders on mitochondrial dysfunction in patients with ALD remain unknown. Here, we demonstrated that glycerol-3-phosphateacyltransferase (GPAM) expression was down-regulated in patients with ALD and associated with ALD progression. Dysregulation of GPAM, a triglyceride synthetase, reshaped lipid metabolism by increasing the levels of toxic lipids such as ceramide and lysophosphatidylcholine and reducing the levels of mitochondrial structural lipids such as cardiolipin. These changes resulted in abnormal mitochondrial dynamics, impaired mitophagy, and dysfunctional mitochondrial respiration, which induced activation of the cGAS-STING pathway. This activation resulted in the accumulation of inflammatory infiltrates, triggering the ALD process in mice. However, the reexpression of GPAM in vivo and in vitro and in hepatic organoids alleviated the development of ALD. This study aimed to determine whether GPAM is a potential therapeutic agent and assess the close relationship between lipid metabolism disorders and mitochondrial dysfunction in patients with ALD.

INTRODUCTION

Alcoholic liver disease (ALD) is a leading cause of chronic liver disease worldwide (1, 2) and substantially increases the risk of end-stage liver disease, cardiovascular diseases, and hepatocellular carcinoma (3–5). ALD encompasses a spectrum of conditions, including alcoholic hepatitis, steatosis, and fibrosis and is characterized by abnormal lipid accumulation in hepatocytes (6, 7). Alcohol consumption accounts for ~2.6 million deaths worldwide each year (8–10); alcohol consumption in China also increased from 7.1 to 11.2 liters yearly per capita and is expected to continue to increase through 2030 (11). There is now no US Food and Drug Administration–approved pharmacotherapy specifically for ALD (1, 12). Thus, further elucidation of the pathogenesis of ALD and treatment approaches is necessary to alleviate the global clinical burden associated with this disease.

Alcohol-induced dysfunction of lipid metabolism is a core and persistent pathological process that contributes to the accumulation of toxic lipids, a reduction in structural phospholipids, hepatic steatosis, and ALD progression (13–15). Ethanol (EtOH) exposure directly increases triglyceride (TG) accumulation in the liver (15), which provides substrates for the generation of toxic bioactive lipids, such as ceramide (Cer) and lysophosphatidylcholine (LPC) (16), resulting in hepatic lipotoxicity in patients with ALD. Alcohol exposure also promotes Cer production, which leads to lipotoxicity (17, 18). Moreover, studies have confirmed that hepatic structural phosphatidylcholine (PC) levels and the PC/phosphatidylethanolamine (PE) ratio are decreased in patients with ALD and animal models (18, 19). Furthermore, EtOH has been shown to inhibit mitochondrial SIRT1 biosynthesis, thereby up-regulating the expression of lipogenic genes (20, 21), indicating that mitochondria play a key and direct role in mediating lipid metabolism disorders in ALD. Structurally, both the inner and outer membranes of mitochondria contain multiple lipid metabolic enzymes (22) associated with ALD. Additionally, mitochondria contain various phospholipids, including cardiolipin (CL), PC, and PE (22, 23), that regulate mitochondrial dynamics, respiratory chain activity, and mitophagy, which closely link mitochondria and lipid metabolism (24) during ALD progression. Previous studies have shown that CL deficiency results in the loss or disorganization of mitochondrial structure (25), leading to inhibited mitophagy and dysfunctional mitochondrial respiration. However, the mechanism by which lipid metabolic disorders affect mitochondrial function in ALD remains largely unknown.

Previous studies have shown that mutations in key lipid metabolism–related genes, such as glycerol-3-phosphateacyltransferase (GPAM) and apolipoprotein E, are associated with lipid metabolic alterations and liver injury in patients with fatty liver disease (FLD) (26). Specifically, GPAM is localized to the mitochondrial outer membrane and mediates the first step of de novo glycerolipid synthesis (27, 28). Studies involving animal models and patient samples have confirmed that elevated-GPAM expression is associated with increased severity of metabolic dysfunction–associated FLD (MAFLD) through the enhancement of de novo TG synthesis (29, 30). However, hepatic GPAM knockout induces mitochondrial fission and contributes to an imbalance in mitochondrial dynamics (31). Collectively, these findings indicate that GPAM plays important but controversial roles in the regulation of lipid metabolism and mitochondrial function. In patients with ALD, a loss of GPAM function has been reported, which is related to the severity of ALD (32). Thus, GPAM may play different roles and exhibit different trends in patients with ALD. In this study, we performed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and showed that GPAM expression was down-regulated in patients with ALD and in animal models of ALD. We also found that down-regulation of GPAM expression was positively associated with the prognosis of patients with ALD in both patients and animal models. Mechanistically, upon alcohol exposure, the down-regulated expression of GPAM led not only to a reprogrammed lipid metabolism, which notably resulted in decreased CL production, increased levels of toxic lipids (Cer and LPC), and excess TG that further promoted the generation of toxic lipids through the provision of substrates, but also to impaired mitochondrial dynamics, mitophagy, and mitochondrial respiratory function and to activation of the cGAS-STING-TBK1 inflammatory pathway both in vivo and in vitro. In contrast, GPAM overexpression reprogrammed lipid metabolism, especially CL synthesis, to protect mitochondrial function and ameliorate ALD progression. Furthermore, CL supplementation alleviated GPAM down-regulation–induced mitochondrial injury after EtOH exposure both in vivo and in vitro. Additionally, through studies of alcohol-treated organoids, GPAM was demonstrated to be a potential therapeutic target for ALD. In conclusion, our results indicated that a decrease in GPAM expression can alter lipid metabolism and mitochondrial function, thus promoting ALD progression in humans and in mouse models.

RESULTS

Hepatic GPAM expression is reduced in patients with ALD and is associated with ALD progression

scRNA-seq of ALD liver tissues (45,426 cells) revealed 11 major cell types, including hepatocytes, endothelial cells, macrophages, and immune cells (Fig. 1A and fig. S1, A and B). Hepatocytes were further divided into 10 subclusters (Fig. 1B and fig. S1, C and D), among which clusters 1, 5, and 7 were enriched in ALD samples (fig. S1E). Cluster 1 displayed high expression of cholangiocyte markers (KRT19, SOX9, and EPCAM) and loss of hepatocyte markers (HNF4A, ASS1, and ALDOB) (33), indicating GPAM was associated with hepatocytes identity loss and ductular reaction (fig. S1, F and G). Among the lipid metabolism–related genes, GPAM expression was uniquely down-regulated in hepatocyte cluster 1, whereas expression of most others was unchanged or up-regulated (Fig. 1D and fig. S2A). Integration of scRNA-seq, proteomic (34), and lipid-related gene data revealed GPAM as a potential lipid regulatory gene in ALD (Fig. 1C). GPAM was highly expressed in the albumin+ (ALB+) hepatocytes under normal conditions but markedly decreased in the ALD samples, especially in cluster 1 (Fig. 1D and fig. S2, B and C). GPAM expression progressively decreased with disease severity in patient samples and Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) datasets and inversely correlated with CK18 levels (Fig. 1, E to H; and fig. S2, D to F). In contrast, the GPAM level was elevated in the metabolic dysfunction–associated steatohepatitis (MASH) model but unchanged in the acute liver failure and fibrosis models (fig. S3). Collectively, these data revealed that an ALD-specific reduction in hepatic GPAM expression is closely associated with disease progression.

Alcohol exposure induces a decrease in GPAM expression, mitochondrial dysfunction, and morphological abnormalities

The expression of GPAM, the key enzyme that catalyzes glycerolipid synthesis on the mitochondrial outer membrane (28), was markedly down-regulated in hepatocytes from ALD samples. Gene Ontology (GO) and gene set enrichment analysis revealed that decreased GPAM expression was associated with disrupted mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) (Fig. 2A and fig. S4A). The expression of OXPHOS complexes I, III, and IV was significantly reduced (fig. S4, B and C), accompanied by enrichment of apoptosis- and ferroptosis-related genes (BCL2, BAX), and GPX4) but not pyroptosis or cuproptosis markers (fig. S4, D to G), suggesting the occurrence of mitochondria-driven cell death (35). Consistent with these findings, the expression of genes related to OXPHOS (NDUFB9, SDHB, UQCRC2, MTCO1, and ATP5A1) was down-regulated in both the ALD GEO and the scRNA-seq datasets (Fig. 2, B and C, and fig. S4H), which aligns with previous findings (36). After investigating the effects of alcohol concentration gradients on GPAM and mitochondrial structure, 100 mM EtOH was selected as the optimal concentration for establishing alcohol-induced injury models in cell models with mitochondrial injury and GPAM down-regulation (Fig. 2, D and E; and figs. S4I, S5, and S6, A to D) (37, 38). Immunofluorescence staining and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed mitochondrial fragmentation and cristae loss after EtOH treatment (Fig. 2, F and G, and fig. S6E), with decreased mitochondrial length (fig. S6F). Furthermore, EtOH treatment inhibited mitochondrial respiratory chain complex expression and was positively associated with down-regulation of GPAM expression (Fig. 2, H and I, and fig. S6G). Collectively, these findings suggested a potential connection between GPAM and mitochondrial function.

Chronic alcohol exposure impairs mitochondrial function and GPAM expression in an ALD mouse model

To gain further insight into the pathological process of ALD, we subjected 8-week-old C57BL/6 mice to either a control liquid diet or a 30-day EtOH liquid diet in accordance with the Gao binge model protocol (Fig. 3, A and B) (39). As expected, the mice fed the EtOH diet exhibited substantial increases in liver-to-body weight ratios and plasma levels of aspartate transaminase (AST), alanine transaminase (ALT), and TG (Fig. 3C). Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and Oil Red O staining demonstrated that consumption of the EtOH diet resulted in hepatic steatosis in these mice (Fig. 3, D and E). Western blot (WB), quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR), and immunohistochemical (IHC) analyses confirmed a decrease in GPAM expression in hepatocytes from these mice, which is consistent with the findings in patients with ALD (Fig. 3, D, F, and G). TEM revealed substantial mitochondrial injury, including mitochondrial swelling and partial loss of cristae (Fig. 3, H to J). Additionally, the expression levels of mitochondrial respiratory chain complexes decreased, indicating impaired mitochondrial function in response to EtOH consumption (Fig. 3K). The chronic EtOH diet also significantly reduced the basal respiratory capacity, maximum respiratory capacity, and adenosine 5′-triphosphate (ATP)–linked respiratory capacity (Fig.3, L and M). Together, our results showed that chronic alcohol exposure inhibits mitochondrial respiratory function and GPAM expression in a mouse model.

GPAM participates in mitochondrial OXPHOS regulation in ALD in vivo and in vitro

To further investigate the effect of GPAM on mitochondrial function in ALD, we generated GPAM-knockdown and GPAM-overexpressing in vitro (Fig. 4, A and C; and fig. S7, J and L). We observed that knocking down GPAM expression in vitro reduced the expression of OXPHOS proteins. Alcohol exposure further exacerbated this reduction (Fig. 4B and fig. S7K). Conversely, GPAM overexpression alone did not significantly influence the expression of OXPHOS proteins. However, in the presence of EtOH, GPAM overexpression reversed the alcohol-induced decrease in OXPHOS protein expression (Fig. 4D, fig. S7M). Furthermore, we measured the oxygen consumption rate (OCR) in vitro and found that, compared with control cells, GPAM-overexpressing cells displayed better mitochondrial respiratory function under EtOH exposure. Specifically, GPAM-overexpressing cells exhibited greater basal respiratory capacity, maximum respiratory capacity, and ATP-linked respiratory capacity (Fig. 4, E and F). To further confirm the regulatory effects of GPAM on mitochondrial OXPHOS, we administered GPAM-overexpressing adeno-associated virus serotype 8 (AAV8-TBG-GPAM-Flag) to C57BL/6J mice and then fed them either a control or an EtOH-supplemented liquid diet (Fig. 4G and fig. S7A), and the development of ALD was investigated. GPAM-overexpressing mice in both the control and EtOH diet groups maintained high GPAM mRNA and protein levels (fig. S7, B and C; and Fig. 4, H and I). After excluding the possible effects of GPAM overexpression on liver function and mitochondrial function (fig. S7, D to I) in the mice fed the control diet, we observed that GPAM overexpression ameliorated liver injury and decreased serum TG levels in the livers of the EtOH diet–fed mice (Fig. 4, J to L). These mice exhibited less hepatic lipid accumulation than the control mice did, as confirmed by H&E and Oil Red O staining (Fig. 4L). Furthermore, TEM analysis demonstrated that GPAM overexpression in EtOH-fed mice partially restored mitochondrial length and improved mitochondrial cristae integrity compared with EtOH-fed mice without GPAM overexpression (Fig. 4, M to O). Additionally, GPAM overexpression attenuated the EtOH-induced down-regulation of mitochondrial respiration complex proteins in mice (Fig. 4P and fig. S7N). These results suggested that GPAM is involved in alcohol-induced mitochondrial OXPHOS dysfunction. In addition, we further examined the GPAM function in ALD mice models and confirmed that the hepatocyte-specific deletion of GPAM further aggravates the progression of ALD and that the restoration of GPAM expression ameliorates hepatic injury and steatosis and improves ALD-associated changes (figs. S8 and S9).

Alcohol-induced reduction in GPAM expression disrupts mitophagy both in vivo and in vitro

GO enrichment analysis revealed that low GPAM expression was strongly associated with the down-regulation expression of factors associated with mitophagy-related pathways in ALD hepatocytes (Fig. 5A and fig. S10, A and B). PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitophagy, a key mitochondrial quality control mechanism under stress, was markedly suppressed in patients with ALD, as indicated by reduced PINK1 and Parkin expression and increased p62 and LC3-II expression (Fig. 5, B to D). Similar inhibition of mitophagy was observed in EtOH-treated hepatocytes (fig. S10, D to F). In vitro, GPAM knockdown further decreased PINK1 and Parkin expression, whereas GPAM overexpression restored these expression levels (Fig. 5, E and F). To definitively validate autophagic flux, we found that chronic alcohol exposure impaired autophagosome-lysosome fusion, resulting in blocked autophagic flux, a phenotype that was aggravated by GPAM depletion but effectively rescued by GPAM overexpression (fig. S10G). Functional assays using mito-Keima and mRFP-GFP-LC3B reporters revealed that EtOH significantly decreased mitophagy flux, an effect exacerbated by GPAM knockdown and rescued by GPAM overexpression (Fig. 5, G and I, and fig. S10C). Immunofluorescence staining revealed reduced colocalization of Parkin and translocase of outer mitochondrial membrane 20 (TOMM20) in EtOH-treated cells models, which was reversed by GPAM overexpression (Fig. 5, H and J). In vivo, EtOH-fed mice presented decreased PINK1 and Parkin expression and increased p62 expression, confirming the inhibition of mitophagy. AAV8-mediated hepatic GPAM overexpression reversed these effects and restored mitophagy (Fig. 5, K to M). Collectively, these findings demonstrated that alcohol can inhibit mitophagy and that GPAM can partially rescue the impairment of mitophagy in vivo and in vitro.

Reduced GPAM expression reprograms hepatocyte lipid metabolism to disrupt mitochondrial dynamics in ALD

GPAM is the rate-limiting enzyme of lipid metabolism. Recent studies have confirmed that GPAM can promote MAFLD progression by increasing de novo TG synthesis (29). To determine its role in ALD, GO analysis of human scRNA-seq data was used, which revealed marked alterations in lipid metabolic pathways, including phospholipid biosynthesis and fatty acid metabolism (fig. S11A). Lipidomic profiling in ALD models and cell models (fig. S11, B to F) revealed that GPAM overexpression decreased LPC, TG, and Cer levels but increased CL and sphingomyelin (SM) levels, particularly mature CL (MLCL) and SM(d41:1) (Fig. 6, A to E). Specifically, LPC species such as LPC(16:0), LPC(18:0), LPC(20:4), and LPC(22:6), which mediate the inflammatory response (40–44), decreased upon GPAM overexpression, whereas SM(d41:1), which decreased in patients with ALD (45), was restored by GPAM overexpression (fig. S11F). Conversely, alcohol exposure increased the abundance of Cer species (C16:0 to C24:0), but these levels were suppressed by GPAM overexpression (Fig. 6E). The expression of lipid metabolism enzymes was also reprogrammed: GPAM overexpression reversed alcohol-induced changes in LPCAT3 (46), PLA2G5/6, and sphingomyelin synthase 2 (SGMS2) and the down-regulated expression of Cer synthase 1/5/6 (CERS1/5/6) (Fig. 6F and fig. S11I) (47). Similar results were also observed for the scRNA-seq and GEO bulk-seq data of patients with ALD (fig. S11, G and H). Moreover, GPAM restoration up-regulated CL synthase (CRLS1) activity and significantly increased hepatic PG levels, a upstream lipid intermediates of CL (fig. S12, A to C) to increase the mitochondrial CL content, as shown by the results of the 10-N-nonyl-acridine orange (NAO) staining (Fig. 6G). CLs are fundamental molecules that bind to DRP1 and OPA1 to mediate mitochondrial dynamics (25); thus, we focused on the connection between lipid metabolism and mitochondrial dynamics in ALD. Functionally, GPAM knockdown led to reduced OPA1 and MFN1/2 expression, elevated DRP1 expression, and excessive mitochondrial fission under EtOH exposure, all of which were reversed by GPAM overexpression (Fig. 6, J and K; and figs. S12D and S13, A to E). CL supplementation in vivo restored mitochondrial morphology and reduced hepatic injury and steatosis (Fig. 6, L to O; and fig. S13, F and G). Furthermore, we evaluated lipid peroxidation in ALD model mice by measuring the content of 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE), a product of reactive oxygen species–induced lipid peroxidation. We found that GPAM overexpression decreased the 4-HNE content in the mice fed an EtOH diet (fig. S13I). In conclusion, the results of these experiments suggested that GPAM reprograms lipid metabolism to affect mitochondrial dynamics and function in hepatocytes during ALD progression.

Hepatic GPAM overexpression inhibits cGAS-STING-TBK1 pathway activation to alleviate ALD-related inflammation

Structural damage to mitochondria directly induces mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) leakage and activates the cGAS-STING-TBK1 pathway to mediate the inflammatory response (48–50). JC-1 staining revealed that EtOH exposure disrupted the mitochondrial membrane potential, and this effect was aggravated by GPAM knockdown but rescued by GPAM overexpression (fig. S14, A and C). TOMM20 and mtDNA staining in ALD livers confirmed increased mitochondrial injury and the presence of cytosolic mtDNA (Fig. 7A). Consistent with these findings, the expression of cGAS, STING, TBK1, and IRF3 was up-regulated in patients with ALD and enriched in hepatocyte clusters 1 and 9 (Fig. 7D and fig. S14, E to H) (49). In vitro and in vivo assays demonstrated that GPAM knockdown exacerbated mtDNA leakage, whereas GPAM overexpression suppressed cGAS-STING-TBK1 activation and reduced immune infiltration (Fig. 7, B, C, and E; and fig. S14, B and D). Neutrophil and monocyte infiltration is a characteristic of ALD pathology and can be induced by cGAS-STING-TBK1 pathway activation (51). Notably, GPAM overexpression diminished the accumulation of neutrophils, CD4+/CD8+ T cells, and CD68+ macrophages but restored the accumulation of CD206+ macrophages (Fig. 6F and fig. S14, I and J). CellPhoneDB analysis revealed increased hepatocyte-immune interactions mediated by interleukin-16 (IL-16; Fig. 7G). IHC staining and RNA sequencing confirmed the increase in IL-16 expression during ALD progression, which was markedly reduced by GPAM overexpression (Fig. 7, I and J; and fig. S14, K and L). We further examined inflammatory cytokines in ALD model mice and found that GPAM overexpression significantly decreased the levels of IL-16 and other proinflammatory cytokines [including IL-1a, IL-13, IL-16, IL-17, CXCL9, CXCL12, macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF), and tumor necrosis factor–α (TNF-α)], which were abnormally increased in humans with ALD (Fig. 7H). Therefore, these data suggested that GPAM can attenuate alcohol-induced mtDNA release, cGAS-STING-TBK1 pathway activation, and the secretion of the core proinflammatory cytokine IL-16 to alleviate ALD-associated inflammation.

GPAM is a potential therapeutic target for ALD

To elucidate the therapeutic potential of GPAM for ALD, we used human liver organoids generated from induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) to effectively model ALD through differentiation (52, 53). The process for liver organoid establishment is shown in Fig. 8A. After 14 days of differentiation, the organoids displayed normal hepatocyte morphology and function, as indicated by the increase in the hepatic markers ALB, HNF1A, and HNF4A; normal bile secretion; and EtOH-metabolizing enzymes (Fig. 8, B and C, and fig. S15A). We then exposed the organoids to increasing concentrations of EtOH to establish an organoid model of ALD. This exposure caused progressive lipid accumulation and impaired cell proliferation in hepatic organoids, as confirmed by an increase in boron dipyrromethene (BODIPY)-positive lipids and a decrease in the number of Ki67+ cells, and we found that hepatic organoids treated with 100 mM alcohol for 48 hours exhibited the most significant reduction in proliferation activity and the greatest lipid droplet accumulation; thus, we chose this concentration for subsequent analysis (fig. S15, B to E). WB analysis confirmed that GPAM expression was significantly decreased in the organoids after exposure to EtOH (Fig. 8D). To determine the therapeutic potential of GPAM, we established hepatic organoids in which GPAM was either knocked down or overexpressed following EtOH exposure. Immunofluorescence (IF) staining revealed greater lipid accumulation and reduced cell proliferation in the GPAM-knockdown organoids than in the GPAM-overexpressing organoids after EtOH exposure (Fig. 8, E to G). In addition, our organoids were also treated with CL, and an improvement in mitochondrial morphology and a decrease in the BODIPY-positive area following EtOH exposure were observed (Fig. 8, H to I). CL supplementation alleviated the disruption of the mitochondrial membrane potential in the organoids (Fig. 8, J and K). Furthermore, we selected pioglitazone as a potential therapeutic therapy to up-regulate GPAM expression and found that pioglitazone markedly attenuated hepatic steatosis and reversed mitochondrial dysfunction through JC-1 assays in hepatic organoids and in vitro (fig. S16, A to L). In conclusion, these results demonstrated that the molecular mechanism by which GPAM functions in a cellular model of ALD also extends to human hepatic organoids.

DISCUSSION

In the present study, we found that GPAM expression was specifically down-regulated both in vivo and in vitro and was associated with ALD progression. Decreased hepatic GPAM expression led to the reprogramming of lipid metabolism by increasing toxic lipid production and inhibiting mitochondrial lipid synthesis, which resulted in alcohol-induced mitochondrial dysfunction and cGAS-STING-TBK1 pathway activation, thus exacerbating inflammation and ALD progression. Reexpressing hepatic GPAM reprogrammed lipid metabolism, particularly CL metabolism, and protected mitochondrial function, ameliorating liver injury caused by chronic alcohol consumption in in vivo models, in cellular models, and in hepatic organoids. Collectively, these findings demonstrated that GPAM could be a therapeutic target for ALD.

GPAM is a transmembrane protein localized in the mitochondrial outer membrane that primarily metabolizes glycerol 3-phosphate and fatty acyl–coenzyme A to synthesize lysophosphatidic acid (28, 54). Variants at the GPAM locus, including loss-of-function variants, have been identified as independent genetic risk factors for increased hepatic fat content and FLD (26, 32), especially MASH. In animal models, hepatic deletion of GPAM directly reduces TG synthesis (29) and fatty acid oxidation (55) and protects against diet-induced hepatic steatosis and insulin resistance (56). However, a genome-wide association study revealed that 12 missense and 3 protein-truncating mutations (loss-of-function mutations) in GPAM are associated with an increased risk of ALD progression (32), in contrast to the role of GPAM in MASH. Consistent with these findings, we observed abnormal hepatic down-regulation of GPAM expression in response to chronic EtOH exposure in vivo in animal models, in vitro in cells, and in human samples. In MAFLD, the induction of de novo lipogenesis (DNL) is the central mechanism driving hepatic lipid accumulation and disease progression (57, 58). The intake of a high-calorie diet contributes to glucose accumulation, and abnormal activation of DNL (59) promotes glucose-to-lipid conversion, resulting in the up-regulation of the expression of lipogenic genes such as GPAM and in the promotion of the accumulation of toxic lipids and hepatic lipotoxicity to exacerbate MAFLD progression. However, chronic alcohol consumption directly promotes lipolysis in adipose tissue (60), leading to increased hepatic free fatty acid (FFA) uptake (61) and subsequent inhibition of lipogenic genes such as GPAM. These phenomena potentially lead to reduced phospholipid generation and mitochondrial dysfunction, which exacerbate hepatic steatosis and liver injury in patients with ALD.

Mitochondrial dysfunction has long been considered a key contributor to the progression of ALD (62, 63). The development of ALD is associated with the dysregulation of mitochondrial dynamics, the impairment of mitophagy, and the dysfunction of OXPHOS (20, 62). In this study, we demonstrated that chronic EtOH exposure induced abnormal mitochondrial fission, inhibited mitochondrial fusion, and impaired mitophagy, subsequently reducing the expression of OXPHOS-related proteins and causing mitochondrial dysfunction. This dysfunction further decreases the β-oxidation of FFAs (64) and inhibits SIRT1 expression, which promotes the expression of lipogenic enzymes (21), resulting in hepatic steatosis. Therefore, liver-specific mitochondrial gene therapy is a promising approach for alleviating ALD. We demonstrated that the overexpression of GPAM alleviated alcohol-induced liver damage by restoring the homeostasis of mitochondrial dynamics, promoting mitophagy, and increasing OXPHOS activity. Previous studies have shown that GPAM can mediate mitochondrial fragmentation by regulating phospholipid generation (31). Through scRNA-seq analysis of ALD samples and studies in animal models, we have shown that GPAM may be associated with phospholipids and that these lipids form the basis of mitochondrial structure.

Lipid metabolic dysfunction is the central pathological process in ALD and is characterized by excess toxic lipid production and a shortage of structural lipids in hepatocytes. Excessive accumulation of toxic lipids (Cer, FFAs, and sterol lipids) tends to induce lipotoxicity and mediate cellular dysfunction, which are major contributors to the progression of ALD (13, 14). Although a previous study has confirmed that TGs tend to be protective lipids for cells (65), excess hepatic TG accumulation also becomes a source of substrates for the production of other toxic biolipids, such as LPC and Cer (16), which further aggravate ALD progression. Our lipidomic results confirmed that chronic alcohol exposure could increase unsaturated TGs, which could be oxidized to promote hepatic steatosis and inflammation (66), but these effects were alleviated when GPAM was overexpressed. In addition, our study revealed that abnormal LPC accumulation was associated with ALD. LPC has been shown to induce caspase activation and oxidative stress, resulting in apoptosis in patients with MAFLD (67, 68). Specifically, LPC species such as LPC(16:0), LPC(18:0), LPC(18:1), and LPC(20:4) can mediate the release of inflammatory cytokines (41–43, 46). Our lipidomic data revealed that GPAM overexpression inhibited the production of these LPC species in vivo and in vitro, leading to decreased inflammatory cell infiltration. LPC is primarily produced by the cleavage of PC by phospholipase A2 (PLA2) (46), and we found that GPAM can decrease LPC production by down-regulating the expression of PLA2 (specifically, PLA2G5 and PLA2G6). Altered sphingolipids, such as Cer and SM, also play important roles in ALD pathogenesis. The abundance of Cer, especially Cer(20:0) and Cer(24:0), has been shown to be significantly elevated in ALD (17), and our lipidomic data confirmed these findings. Additionally, the absence of SM was noted in the lipid profiles of patients with ALD and our lipidomic results (45). Cer impairs mitochondrial OXPHOS by inhibiting complex III activation and reduced form of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate oxidase activity, as well as by inducing oxidative stress and inhibiting fatty acid β-oxidation (69, 70). Cer is generated from de novo synthesis through the activation of Cer synthases (CERS1–6) (71) and the degradation of SM through SGMS2 and Smpd3 (72). Therefore, we investigated the gene expression of the above enzymes and found that hepatic GPAM overexpression decreased Smpd3 and CERS5/6 mRNA levels to decrease SM degradation and the de novo synthesis of Cer but accelerated the conversion of Cer to SM by promoting SGMS1/2 expression to reduce hepatic Cer levels and increase SM levels, thus protecting mitochondrial function and alleviating liver injury in patients with ALD. Structural lipid disorders are another feature of ALD-related lipid metabolic disorders. Mitochondria are composed of multiple kinds of structural lipids, including PCs, PEs, and CL (22, 23). CL, especially MLCL, is a mitochondrial-specific structural lipid in the mitochondrial membrane that plays a key role in mitochondrial function and structural integrity (73). A previous study demonstrated that hepatic mitochondrial dysfunction occurs when the CL content is decreased by 35% (74, 75). CL is synthesized mainly by CRLS1, and CRLS1 knockdown has been shown to significantly decrease imbalances in mitophagy and mitochondrial dynamics (76). In our study, we observed that CL, especially mature CL, significantly decreased under EtOH exposure in vivo and in vitro and that GPAM overexpression increased CRLS1 expression to promote CL biosynthesis, thus exerting protective effects on mitochondrial dynamics and function. Notably, exogenously added CL can ameliorate mitochondrial respiratory dysfunction. Furthermore, supplementation with CL to replace CL loss due to down-regulated expression of GPAM in vitro, in vivo, and liver organoids alleviated alcohol-induced mitochondrial damage and mitochondrial dynamics imbalance. In summary, we showed that GPAM reprogrammed dysregulated lipid metabolism in ALD by degrading toxic lipids (LPC, TG, Cer, and SM) and increasing the production of CL to maintain mitochondrial function during ALD.

Lipid metabolic disorders and subsequent mitochondrial dysfunction can result in mtDNA release, which, in turn, activates cGAS-STING-TBK1 signaling (48) to induce immune cell infiltration, ultimately leading to inflammation. We demonstrated that communication between hepatocytes and immune cells in patients with ALD resulted in the infiltration of neutrophils and macrophages; however, hepatic GPAM overexpression reversed these phenomena. Moreover, we showed that mechanistically, GPAM overexpression attenuated the release of proinflammatory cytokines, including IL-1a, IL-13, IL-16, IL-17, CXCL9, CXCL12, M-CSF, and TNF-α, among which IL-16 was the most obvious cytokine. Therefore, we propose that GPAM can protect mitochondrial function, reduce activation of the cGAS-STING-TBK1 pathway, and decrease IL-16 secretion to alleviate inflammation in patients with ALD.

In conclusion, our study revealed that the down-regulation of the expression of hepatic GPAM plays a vital role in the induction of lipid metabolism disorders, mitochondrial damage, and inflammatory responses during the progression of ALD. Therefore, targeting mitochondrial GPAM could represent a therapeutic approach for ALD.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Human liver samples

Human liver samples were acquired from healthy donor individuals (n = 12) and patients diagnosed with alcohol liver disease (n = 14) who underwent a liver biopsy at the Department of Hepatobiliary Surgery, Zhujiang Hospital of the Southern Medical University from 1 June 2023 to 31 August 2024. All the patients enrolled in the present study were excluded from drug or toxin injury and hepatitis virus infection. All tissues collections had been approved and supervised by Institutional Review Boards of Zhujiang Hospital of Southern Medical University (2022-KY-041-02), and written consent was obtained from all participants. The basic clinical characteristics and histological assessments are detailed in table S1.

Cell culture and treatment

Murine primary hepatocytes were cultured in the HepatoZYME-SFM (17705021, Gibco), and AML12 cell line was cultured in the Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM)/F-12 medium (L310KJ, BasalMedia) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; BC-SE-FBS01, Biochannel), dexamethasone (D829854, Macklin) at 50 μg/ml, and 1% insulin-transferrin-selenium (ITS) liquid medium supplement (S450J7, BasalMedia). All cells cultured in a 5% CO2 incubator. For EtOH treatment, cells were incubated with complete cell culture medium containing 100 mM EtOH for 48 hours, and the culture medium was replaced every 24 hours to prevent alcohol concentration reduction due to evaporation. For CL treatment, the concentration of CL used for the in vitro treatment was 10 μg/ml.

Knockdown or overexpression of target genes

For knockdown or overexpression of GPAM, short hairpin RNA (shRNA) or forced expression vectors were constructed as described previously [13]. Three shRNA sequences used in this study were as follows: 5′-CGACGAAGGCUCGAUGAAA-3′ for shGPAM-1, 5′-GAACAGCAGUAGAGUACAA-3′ for shGPAM-2, and 5′-GGUCAACUUGAGAUGGUUA-3′ for shGPAM-3. The shRNAs containing specific sequences targeting the human GPAM mRNA sequence were cloned into the pSilencer 3.1-H1 puro vector (Ambion, Austin, TX, USA). A control shRNA was also cloned into the same vector and used as a silencing negative control. For overexpression, ADH and CYP2E1, GPAM overexpression adenovirus (GPAMOE), and an empty vector adenovirus (NC) were constructed by OBiO Technology (Shanghai, China). Transfection was performed using Lipo8000 Transfection Reagent (C0533, Beyotime Biotechnology), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Stable cell lines transfected with shRNAs or forced expression vectors were generated after selection with Puromycin Dihydrochloride (ST551, Beyotime Biotechnology) and used for in vitro mechanistic investigations or in vivo studies.

Animal models

Animal experiments were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Zhujiang Hospital, Southern Medical University (LAEC-2022-236). All animals received humane care, according to the criteria outlined in the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals prepared by the National Academy of Sciences and published by the National Institutes of Health. They were housed in pathogen-free conditions with a 12-hour light/dark cycle, and temperature was maintained at 22° to 24°C and humidity at 40 to 70%. National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism (NIAAA) model was used as previously described with some modifications. Eight-week-old male mice were fed with the Lieber-DeCarli EtOH diets (Lieber, Dyets) containing 5% alcohol (EtOH fed) or Lieber-DeCarli Regular control diet (Lieber-C, Dyets) (pair fed) for 4 weeks. Food intakes was measured daily, and the amount of food given to the pair-fed mice was adjusted to match that consumed by the alcohol-fed mice the previous day. Nine hours before tissue collection, the EtOH-fed mice were administrated with a dose of EtOH at 5 g/kg, and the pair-fed mice received an isocaloric maltose dextrin. For CL treatment, male C57BL/6J mice were treated intraperitoneally with CL (100 μg per mouse) every day before intragastric administration. To induce liver-specific GPAM overexpression, a liver-specific gene vector AAV8 was used, and the recombinant GPAM-encoding AAV8 (AAV8-TBG-GPAM-3xFlag-tWPA) and control vector (AAV8-TBG-MCS-3xFlag-tWPA) were generated by OBIO Company. AAV8-TBG-GPAM-3xFlag-tWPA [2 × 1011 vector genomes per milliliter (v.g.)] was injected into mice through the tail vein. After 3 weeks of injection, all mice begin treatment followed the above protocol. For the collection of tissue samples, mice were anesthetized with inhalational isoflurane.

Hepatocyte differentiation/organoid generation

Hepatic differentiation was performed using previously described protocols with modifications. For definitive endoderm differentiation, iPSCs were seeded and cultured on six-well plates with Matrigel-precoated under iPSC medium for 3 days. Then, the medium was exchanged with RPMI 1640 (L210KJ, BasalMedia) supplemented with 1× B-27 without insulin (S445J7, BasalMedia) and human activin A [100 ng/ml; HY-P70311, MedChemExpress (MCE)] and incubated for 5 days. After 5 days, the cells were harvested as single cells and seeded into Matrigel embeds (354248, Corning). The medium was then exchanged with RPMI 1640 supplemented with B-27 (S440J7, BasalMedia), basic fibroblast growth factor (10 ng/ml), and human BMP-4 (20 ng/ml; HY-P7007, MCE) for 4 days to induce hepatic endoderm spheroid (HES) differentiation. For hepatic organoid, HES was harvested using dispase (1 mg/ml) and dissociated into single cells. Then, cells were subsequently seeded in a six-well ultralow attachment plate (3471, Corning) at a density of 7.5 × 105 cells per well with medium containing HepatoZYME-SFM without epidermal growth factor, diluted with 1:1 Endothelial Cell Growth Medium-2 (CC-3162, Lonza) supplemented with 2.5% FBS, 100 nM dexamethasone, oncostatin M (OSM) (20 ng/ml; HY-P70465, MCE), and hepatocyte growth factor (HGF) (10 ng/ml; HY-P70627, MCE) for 12 days. The medium was changed every 3 days. To make in vitro ALD model using hepatic organoid, 100 mM EtOH was added into the culture medium for 48 hours with medium refreshed every day. To limit EtOH evaporation, parafilm was used to seal the ultralow attachment plate. The control group without EtOH was treated in the same manner.

Visualization of functional bile canaliculi

All organoids used for bile canaliculi visualization underwent standard preparation, which included a single wash with Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (DPBS) containing calcium and magnesium (DPBS Ca/Mg) (14040133, Gibco). This was followed by staining with Hoechst 33342 in HepatoZYME-SFM (17705021, Gibco) for 30 min at 37°C. For snapshot imaging of the bile canaliculi, organoids were incubated in HepatoZYME-SFM medium containing 1 mM CDFDA (F1303, Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 2 hours at 37°C, washed twice with DPBS Ca/Mg, and then transferred into a well of a m-slide Angiogenesis glass-bottom plate (Ibidi, Bayern, Germany) containing HepatoZYME-SFM. For time-lapse imaging, organoids were continuously maintained in HepatoZYME-SFM medium containing 1 mM CDFDA.

mRNA isolation and reverse transcription qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from liver tissues and cells using TRIzol reagent (AG21102, Accurate Biology) following to the manufacturer’s instructions and was quantified with a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (NanoDrop One, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Reverse transcription of isolated RNA was performed using the Evo M-MLV RT Kit with gDNA Clean for qPCR (AG11711, Accurate Biology). Real-time qPCR was conducted using on the Real-Time PCR System using 2× SYBR Green Pro Taq HS Premix IV (AG11746, Accurate Biology). The mRNA abundance of the target genes was normalized to glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH; human) or GAPDH (mouse). The primers used in this study are listed in table S2.

RNA sequencing

Purified mRNA was fragmented into small pieces using fragment buffer at appropriate temperature. First-strand cDNA was then generated using random hexamer-primed reverse transcription, followed by second-strand cDNA synthesis. Subsequently, A-Tailing Mix and RNA Index Adapters were added, and the mixture was incubated to facilitate end repair. The cDNA fragments obtained from previous step were amplified by PCR, and the products were purified using Ampure XP Beads and then dissolved in EB solution. The products were validated on the Agilent Technologies 2100 bioanalyzer for quality control. The double-stranded PCR products from previous step were heated denatured and circularized by the splint oligo sequence to get the final library. The single-strand circle DNA was formatted as the final library. The final library was amplified with phi29 to create DNA nanoball (DNB), each containing more than 300 copies of one molecular. DNBs were loaded into the patterned nanoarray, and paired-end 150 base reads were generated on MGIseq2000 platform (BGI-Shenzhen, China). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were detected with DEGseq, which is based on the Poisson distribution. Genes that exhibited more than 1.5-fold change and the corrected P value of 0.05 or less were defined as DEGs. With the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes annotation result, we classified DEGs according to the official classification and also performed pathway functional enrichment using phyper, a function of R.

WB analysis

Protein from cells and liver tissues were extracted by the radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer (P0013B, Beyotime Biotechnology) supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail (C0001, TargetMol) and Phosphatase Inhibitor Cocktail I (C0002, TargetMol). The concentration of total protein was quantified using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (HRX0121, Heruibio) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Equal quantities of the protein were separated by 10% SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis gels and then transferred to 0.45-μm polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (IPVH00010; Millipore; Billerica, MA, USA). PVDF membranes with proteins were being blocked with 5% skim milk in tris-buffered saline/Tween 20 for 2 hours at room temperature and then incubated with the target primary antibodies overnight at 4°C, followed by horseradish peroxidase (HRP)–conjugated secondary antibodies for 2 hours at room temperature. Signals were then visualized using Super ECL Detection Reagent ECL (36208ES60, Yeasen Biotechnology) on a ChemiDoc MP Imaging System (Bio-Rad; Hercules, CA, USA). ACTIN was used as a loading control. The antibodies used are listed in table S3.

Histological and IHC staining

Liver tissue samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight and embedded in paraffin. Paraffin-embedded liver sections were used H&E staining (hematoxylin: G1004, Servicebio, Wuhan, China; and eosin: BA-4024, Baso, Zhuhai, China) to visualize histological features. OCT-embedded liver sections were evaluated hepatic steatosis using a Modified Oil Red O Staining Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, C0158S). To detect target gene expression, paraffin-embedded liver sections were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C, followed by incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 2 hours at room temperature. The positive areas were visualized after DAB staining (ZLI-9018, Zhongshan Biotech, Beijing, China). Histological and IHC images were captured using a 3D histech (Pannoramic MIDI II; 3D-HISTECH, Tokyo, Japan). The primary antibodies used are listed in table S3.

Biochemical analysis

The serum concentrations of alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) were measured using an ADVIA 2400Chemistry System Analyzer (Siemens, Tarrytown, NY, USA). The serum concentrations of TG, CHO, HDL, and LDL were measured using an automatic clinical chemistry analyzer (Wuhan Servicebio Technology) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The serum concentration of vLDL was measured using a Mouse vLDL ELISA kit (F9186) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Immunofluorescence staining

For coverslip staining, cells were fixed with Immunol Staining Fix Solution (P0098, Beyotime Biotechnology) for 15 min at room temperature and then were permeabilized with Enhanced Immunostaining Permeabilization Buffer (P0097, Beyotime Biotechnology) for 10 min at room temperature. After blocking with QuickBlock Blocking Buffer for Immunol Staining (P0260, Beyotime Biotechnology) for 20 min at room temperature, the cells were incubated with primary antibody at 4°C overnight. For double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) staining, after blocking, the cells were incubated with dsDNA (CBL186, Sigma-Aldrich) at 4°C overnight. Subsequently, the cells were labeled with fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies. Nuclei were stained using Hoechst 33342 (S19116, Yuanye Biotechnology). Images were acquired with a confocal laser scanning microscope (AX NIS-Elements 5.4, Nikon, Germany), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. All antibodies and tracker used in this study are listed in table S3. For the tissues staining, the paraffin sections were deparaffinized and subjected to antigen retrieval by steaming with sodium citrate buffer and then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C, followed by incubated with fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies. Nuclei were stained using 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI). Images were acquired with a confocal laser scanning microscope (AX NIS-Elements 5.4, Nikon, Germany), according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Mitochondria isolation

The mitochondria used in this work were freshly isolated from HepG2, AML12, and primary mouse hepatocyte (PMH) cells following the protocol of the mitochondria isolation kit (C3601, Beyotime Biotechnology). First, the cells were washed three times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), detached from the culture flask with trypsin-EDTA solution (TE2004Y, TBD), and centrifuged at 300g for 3 min. Second, the supernatant was removed, and the collected cells were resuspended with 1 ml of cell lysis reagent (C3601-1, Beyotime Biotechnology) and placed in an ice bath for 10 min. Third, the lysed cells were homogenized with a glass homogenizer for 30 cycles. Fourth, the homogenized cells were centrifuged at 1000g for 10 min at 4°C. Then, the supernatant was resuspended with 1 ml of cell lysis reagent and then centrifuged at 11,000g for 10 min at 4°C again for more purity. Last, carefully remove the supernatant. The precipitate is the isolated cell mitochondria. Mitochondria storage reagent (C3601-3, Beyotime Biotechnology) was used to suspend the isolated mitochondria at the required concentration for further experiments. The mitochondria isolated from 2 × 107 cells and suspended in 10 ml of mitochondria storing reagent.

OCR measurement

The process was performed according to manufacturer’s protocol. The results of OCR were normalized by the cells number. Briefly, 20,000 primary hepatocytes were seeded on cell culture microplate (103793-100-1, Agilent Technologies) overnight in DMEM, 10% FBS, and penicillin/streptomycin. Ten thousand HepG2 cells were plated on cell culture microplate (103793-100-1, Agilent Technologies) overnight. OCRs were measured in a XF-96 Extracellular Flux Analyzers (Agilent, USA) in cells suspended in XF medium. XF medium consists of Seahorse XF DMEM Medium (103575, Agilent Technologies), 10 mM glucose (103577, Agilent Technologies), 2 mM glutamine (103579, Agilent Technologies), and 1 mM sodium pyruvate (103578, Agilent Technologies). OCR measurements were according to the guidelines provided by the Seahorse XF Cell Mito Stress Test kit (103015, Agilent Technologies). OCRs were obtained under basal conditions and following the addition of oligomycin (1.5 μM), carbonyl cyanide p-trifluoromethoxyphenylhydrazone (1.0 μM), and rotenone (0.5 nM) and antimycin A (0.5 μM).

CellPhoneDB analysis

We applied CellPhoneDB (version 2.1.4) to analyze potential ligand-receptor interactions between hepatocytes and immune cells, based on significant enrichment of reciprocal expression of ligands and receptors among annotated cell populations. P values for the interactions were calculated by randomly permuting cluster labels to generate a null distribution, and P values of <0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry–based lipidomics of ALD liver and cells

Lipids were extracted from liver samples using previously reported protocols In short, 25-mg liver samples were weighted with 800 ul of prechilled solution of dichloromethane/methanol (3:1) spiked in lipid internal standard mix (SPLASH LIPIDOMIX Mass Spec Standard, Avanti, USA), and 10 μl of SPLASH internal standard solution and two small steel balls were then added. The mixture was ground using a TissueLyser at 50 Hz for 10 min through ultrasonic treatment. After centrifuge the mixture at 25,000 rpm for 15 min at 4°C, 600 μl of the supernatant was lyophilizates and reconstituted with 200 μl of reconstitution fluid. Then, the mixture was vortexed for 1 min through ultrasonic treatment with water and centrifuged at 25,000 rpm for 15 min at 4°C. At last, the supernatant was moved into glass vials for liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC-MS) analysis. Each sample (20 μl) was mixed to evaluate the repeatability and stability of LC-MS analysis. CSH C18 column (1.7 μm, 2.1 mm by 100 mm; Waters, USA) was used to separate lipids, and then a Q Exactive mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was used to qualify and quantify lipids. Mobile phase A consisted of 10 mM ammonium formate with 0.1% formic acid in 60% acetonitrile aqueous solution, and mobile phase B consisted of 10 mM ammonium formate with 0.1% formic acid in 10% acetonitrile aqueous solution and 90% isopropanol. The following gradient was used for elution: 0 to 2 min, 40 to 43% B solution; 2 to 2.1 min, 43 to 50% B solution; 2.1 to 7 min, 50 to 54% B solution; 7 to 7.1 min, 54 to 70% B solution; and 7.1 to 13 min, 70 to 99% B solution with a flow rate of 0.35 ml/min. The mass spectrometric settings for positive/negative ionization modes were as follows: spray voltage, 3.8/−3.2 kV; aux gas heater temperature, 350°C; and capillary temperature, 320°C. The full scan range was 200 to 2000 mass/charge ratio with a resolution of 70,000, and the automatic gain control (AGC) target for MS acquisitions was set to 3e6 with a maximum ion injection time of 100 ms. The top three ions were selected for subsequent MS/MS fragmentation with a maximum ion injection time of 50 ms and resolution of 17,500, and the AGC was 1 × 105. The collision energy was set to 15, 30, and 45 eV step by step. Quality control (QC) samples were injected after every 10 sample injections followed previous description for system quality control. The results were identified and quantified by LipidSearch 4.1 SP2 software (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The peak extraction was set to 5 parts per million (ppm) to deviate the mass. The filter was set as follows: M-score was set as 5.0; c-score was set as 2.0; and the identification level was selected as “A,” “B,” “C,” and “D.” The processing parameters for product ion search include precursor and product tolerance as 5 ppm; adduct ions of positive mode as [M+H]+, [M+NH4]+, and [M+Na]+; and adduct ions of negative mode as [M−H]− and [M−2H]−, and [M−HCOO]−. Data scaling and normalization were further processed using meta.

Electron microscopy

Electron microscopy was used to observe the ultrastructure of mitochondria. Samples were dehydrated using acetonitrile and graded EtOH and then embedded in epoxy resin (EMbed-812, SPI, USA) and polymerized at 60°C for 48 hours. Hitachi HT7800 Electron Microscope (Hitachi, Japan) was used to capture the images.

Mitochondrial morphology analysis

The mitochondrial morphology of cells and organoids was evaluated using confocal immunofluorescence microscopy (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan) with MitoTracker Red CMXROS (C1035, Beyotime Biotechnology) and Mito-Tracker Deep Red FM (C1032, Beyotime Biotechnology) as described previously. ImageJ software [National Institutes of Health (NIH), Bethesda, MD, USA] was used for analysis of mitochondrial length and critase number through Feret’s diameter.

Mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm)

The mitochondrial transmembrane potential was analyzed using a mitochondrial membrane potential detection kit (JC-1) (HY-K0601, MCE) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells were washed with ice-cold PBS and then stained with 2 μM JC-1 for 20 min at 37°C. After being washed with PBS for three times, the cells were examined by fluorescence microscopy. For organoids, the JC-1 staining time was extended to 1.5 hours at 37°C. All other procedures are as described above. Nuclei were stained with Hoechst 33342 for 10 min at room temperature, and the organoids were analyzed by fluorescence confocal microscopy. Results are presented as relative aggregate-to-monomer (red/green) fluorescence intensity ratio.

Detection of CL level

CL content was determined using CL-specific fluorescent dye NAO staining. For detecting CL in vivo, we extracted PMHs from mice treated with intraperitoneal CL administration using a two-step hepatocyte isolation method. Then, the PMHs were incubated with 1 μM NAO for 20 min. For detecting CL in vitro, the target cells were washed several times and then were incubated with 1 μM NAO for 20 min. Fluorescence intensity was measured using fluorescence confocal microscopy (Nikon, Japan). Data were analyzed by ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Statistical analysis

All data in this study are expressed as the means ± SEM from at least three independent experiments. Statistical analysis was performed using two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or Student’s t test. GraphPad Prism 9 software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA) was used for these statistical analyses. A P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
