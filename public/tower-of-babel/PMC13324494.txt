Management of antimicrobial therapy in culture-negative post-neurosurgical meningitis caused by augmented renal clearance
Authors: Hai Zeng, Xinrong Ou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAugmented renal clearance (ARC) is a well-recognized driver of subtherapeutic antibiotic concentrations in critically ill patients; however, its occurrence in elective neurosurgical populations remains unrecognized. Conventional serum creatinine and conventional intensive care unit (ICU)-based screening tools may fail to identify ARC, leading to antimicrobial failure.Case presentationA 51-year-old previously healthy woman underwent elective resection of a right cerebellar meningioma. Postoperatively, she developed culture-negative meningitis, with clinical and cerebrospinal fluid findings consistent with bacterial infection. Despite guideline-based vancomycin dosing (escalated to 18.5 mg/kg/dose), serial therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) revealed persistently subtherapeutic trough concentrations (7.21 and 8.41 µg/mL; target 15–20 µg/mL). Serum creatinine levels remained normal (0.43 mg/dL); calculated creatinine clearance (Cockcroft–Gault) confirmed ARC (131.99 mL/min/1.73 m2), corroborated by 8-h urine collection (135.31 mL/min/1.73 m2). Conventional ICU-based ARC screening tools (ARC and ARCTIC scores) erroneously classified the patient as low risk. TDM-guided meropenem dose intensification (from 2 g every 8 h to 2.0 g every 4 h) and switching to linezolid (non-renally cleared, good CNS penetration) achieved therapeutic targets. A multimodal composite endpoint guided safe antibiotic cessation on postoperative day 23. The patient recovered fully without recurrence.ConclusionsIn culture-negative postoperative meningitis refractory to guideline-based therapy, ARC should be suspected as the underlying cause, specifically for renally eliminated medications. Calculated or estimated creatinine clearance values may underestimate true renal clearance in patients with ARC; therefore, proactive creatinine clearance measurement using timed urine collection is essential. Additionally, TDM-guided dosing and a multimodal composite endpoint are critical for optimal management.

Introduction

Augmented renal clearance (ARC; creatinine clearance [CrCl] ≥130 mL/min/1.73 m²) is a well-known driver of subtherapeutic antibiotic concentrations in critically ill patients. [1], [2]. From a pharmacokinetic(PK) perspective, ARC significantly increases the renal elimination of hydrophilic antibiotics such as β-lactams and glycopeptides, necessitating dose adjustments to maintain therapeutic concentrations [6], [7]. However, at present，its relevance in elective surgical patients remains poorly characterized at this stage. Existing ARC screening tools (ARC and ARCTIC scores) [3], [4] were developed and validated exclusively in intensive care unit (ICU) and trauma cohorts; thus, it remains unknown whether they can accurately identify ARC risk in patients undergoing major elective procedures. Beyond antimicrobials, ARC also affects a broader spectrum of renally eliminated medications, including antiseizure drugs (e.g., levetiracetam) and certain anticoagulants (e.g., dabigatran), for which subtherapeutic concentrations have been reported in patients with ARC [14].

Herein, we present a case of culture‑negative meningitis after elective neurosurgery in which initial guideline-based therapy failed, leading to the identification of ARC as the driver of antimicrobial failure. Using therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) to guide antibiotic optimization, we observed a superinfection (oral candidiasis) due to prolonged broad- spectrum therapy, and finally achieved safe cessation through a multimodal composite endpoint. Although several large observational studies have established the prevalence and general associations of ARC in critically ill and surgical populations [11], [12], [13], they predominantly focus on ICU patients and lack the granular PK detail and practical management guidance required for individual patient care. Critically, ARC occurs in postoperative patients without ICU admission [11], yet its recognition in elective neurosurgical populations remains poor. This case report provides unique, complementary value by offering complete serial TDM data for two antimicrobials with measured CrCl validation, proposing a novel multimodal composite endpoint for safe antibiotic cessation in culture-negative postoperative central nervous system (CNS) infections and exposing a critical diagnostic pitfall (normal serum creatinine and low‑risk screening scores do not exclude ARC in patients undergoing elective neurosurgery).

(3) TDM-guided antimicrobial optimization Following multidisciplinary discussion, vancomycin was discontinued. Therapy was transitioned to linezolid (0.6 g q12h) and meropenem (2 g q8h, administered as a 2–4-h extended infusion). Subsequent meropenem TDM revealed subtherapeutic trough concentrations (1.54 µg/mL; target 8–16 µg/mL for CNS infections). Structured antibiotic cessation using a multimodal composite endpoint

By POD 23, systemic inflammatory markers normalized (CRP 0.32 mg/L, PCT <0.05 ng/mL). Repeat lumbar puncture demonstrated a shift to mononuclear predominance (85% mononuclear cells), with declining lactate and adenosine deaminase levels. All microbiological tests remained negative. Following the proposed multimodal composite endpoint (Table 1) — incorporating resolution of systemic inflammation, CSF evolution to a non-bacterial pattern, persistently negative microbiology, neurological improvement, and emergence of superinfection (oral candidiasis)—antibiotics were safely discontinued on POD 23. After discontinuation, no recurrence of infection was observed during 13-day hospital follow-up. Discussion

The key clue was failure of guideline‑dosed vancomycin (renally cleared) [6] to achieve therapeutic levels, prompting CrCl calculation. Notably, ARC is a relevant clinical consideration only for medications that undergo renal clearance; ARC has minimal impact in drugs with non‑renal clearance pathways (e.g., linezolid). ARC should be suspected in any postoperative patient with unexplained poor response, regardless of serum creatinine. When culture‑negative meningitis fails to respond to standard therapy, ARC must be considered as a primary differential.(2)TDM-guided dose optimizationIn our case study, TDM showed that vancomycin did not reach the target level despite dose increases reaching the maximum recommended dose per current guidelines (15–20 mg/kg/dose) [8]. In contrast, linezolid is a lipophilic oxazolidinone with non‑renal clearance. This pharmacological property makes linezolid a rational choice in ARC patients. Additionally, linezolid achieves excellent CNS penetration, making it suitable for central nervous system infections [6]. Possible pathogen based on antibiotic response sequence

The sequence of antibiotic changes provides clues about the possible pathogen. Our patient showed no improvement after switching from ceftriaxone to vancomycin monotherapy. In contrast, improvement occurred after adding meropenem and linezolid, a regimen that covers gram-negative bacilli (Enterobacteriaceae and Pseudomonas), gram-positive cocci (MRSA and streptococci), and anaerobes. A plausible explanation is that the causative organism was a gram-negative bacterium (e.g., E. coli, Klebsiella, or Enterobacter) susceptible to ceftriaxone but resistant to vancomycin, which has no gram-negative activity. The initial response to ceftriaxone was incomplete possibly because of inadequate CNS penetration or subtherapeutic concentrations; the infection recurred following the discontinuation of ceftriaxone. Clinical resolution occurred following the subsequent addition of meropenem (which has excellent CNS penetration and covers most gram-negative pathogens) and linezolid (covering gram-positive bacteria). Alternatively, the infection could have been caused by a mixed flora including gram-positive and gram-negative organisms.（5）A pragmatic framework for culture-negative infections

When microbiological confirmation is unavailable, a common challenge in clinical practice, especially in under resourced regions [10]. We propose as a hypothesis-generating approach a structured framework (Fig. 1): (1) Screening: For any postoperative CNS infection with a poor response, proactively calculate CrCl using a timed urine collection (e.g., 8-hour or 24-hour collection), irrespective of the normal serum creatinine level. Clinicians should not wait for therapeutic failure before screening for ARC. Established clinical identifiers—including young age, male sex, trauma, or neurological illness—are independently strong predictors of ARC and should prompt early assessment. In patients with these risk factors, a timed urine collection for CrCl measurement should be performed upfront, followed by TDM once steady-state concentrations are reached. (2) Confirmation and Optimization: If ARC is confirmed, initiate or intensify TDM to guide aggressive dose optimization. Limitations

This single case cannot establish causality; however, the temporal relationship and TDM data strongly support ARC as the primary mechanism. The proposed framework requires prospective validation.

We acknowledge that current guidelines recommend area under the concentration-time curve (AUC)-guided vancomycin monitoring as the preferred method, targeting an AUC₀₋₂₄/MIC ratio of 400–600. However, this patient was managed in a public tertiary hospital in China where laboratory infrastructure for AUC-guided dosing (requiring two serum level measurements and Bayesian software or repeated sampling) was not available at the time of care. Trough-based monitoring (target 15–20 µg/mL) remains widely accepted as a practical alternative in resource-limited settings. Where feasible, AUC-guided dosing would be preferable.

Conclusion

In culture‑negative post‑neurosurgical meningitis refractory to antimicrobial therapy, when guideline‑based treatment fails, clinicians should suspect ARC as an underlying cause, specifically for renally eliminated medications. In such cases, proactive CrCl calculation and TDM‑guided antimicrobial optimization are essential. Prolonged broad‑spectrum therapy has a risk of superinfection, which can serve as a safety signal for antibiotic cessation. The proposed multimodal composite endpoint provides a practical, stepwise framework for safe antibiotic de‑escalation. This case report offers a replicable management pathway for neurosurgeons, emphasizing that the identification of ARC as an important cause can prevent antimicrobial failure and reduce unnecessary antibiotic exposure.
