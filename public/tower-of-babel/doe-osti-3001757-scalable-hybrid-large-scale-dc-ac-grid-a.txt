 ABSTRACT

The goals of the project included the identification and evaluation of a voltage source converter
multiterminal high-voltage direct current (VSC-MTdc) system architecture suitable for a high amount of
power transfer through long transmission lines. To achieve these goals, the proposed project team
developed the following:

(i) A VSC high-voltage direct current converter topology/arrangement in a station suitable for higher
power transfer over longer distances at higher voltages (approximately 800 kV, 20 GW).

(ii) A control and protection system in the VSC-MTdc system with a higher power rated station for
steady-state operation, dc and ac fault ride-through, and maintenance in a certain station. This includes the
hierarchical control and protection system needed at different timescales from milliseconds to minutes for
the first time.

MAJOR AC COMPLISHMENTS

All milestones have been completed on time. A new high-power, very-high-voltage MTdc system
architecture is proposed, and the hierarchical control and protection system for this architecture is being
researched. These developments have led to multiple invention disclosures. The studies in this phase used
the models and tools developed in the first phase of the project.

From the assessment, the project team has submitted or completed multiple journal articles, conference
papers, and presentations.

 1. MTDC SYSTEM ARCHITECTURE

1.1 HARDWARE DESCRIPTION

As per the studies based on the capital expenditure model, four locations that require a large power
transfer are identified in the Eastern Interconnection (EI) power network of the United States. The details
are presented in Figure 1.1-1 and Table 1.1-I. The power requirement at each location is between 4 and 20
GW, and the power is to be transferred over hundreds of miles, as shown in

Table 1.1-II.

To address the problem of large power transfer over long distances, a new high-power, multiterminal
high-voltage direct current (MTdc) system architecture (Figure 1.1-2) is designed.

  Figure 1.1-1. HVdc terminals in the US EI network. (Abbreviations: CHI = Chicago, Illinois; DET = Detroit,
 Michigan; FD = Fort Dodge, Iowa; GI = Grand Island, Nebraska.)

Each station is formed using series- and parallel-connected valve groups (VGs) rated for 2 GW and 400
kV dc voltage. A VG consists of a half-bridge modular multilevel converter. Each arm of the VG contains
200 series-connected half-bridge submodules, each rated for 2 kV. The submodule capacitance is 28 mF,
which corresponds to an energy storge of 33.3 kJ/MVA. The VG is connected to the ac grid through a
220/220 kV star (grid side)/delta (VG side) transformer. The leakage inductance of the transformer is
15%, and the arm inductance is 12% of 2 MVA base. On the ac side, based on the power requirements
and capacity constraints, the VGs may need to be connected to different points of interconnection (POIs)
in the ac network. To maintain symmetry, the VGs connected in a particular position in positive and
negative portions of the MTdc networks are connected to the same POI, as shown in Figure 1.1-3. A 16
GW station is chosen as an example. At each POI, the ac network is represented by an ideal voltage
source or by a detailed ac system.

1.2.1 Converter control

The block diagram of the implemented control for each VG is shown in Figure 1.2-2. Reference
commands are provided for the ac real power, reactive power, and dc voltage, as well as the sum of the
submodule voltages of the VG. The internal stored energy of the VG is controlled by regulating the q
component of the ac current, IqX*. The dc voltage is controlled through the active current component,
IqY*. The reactive power reference is converted to an equivalent current reference, Id*. Internal dq current
loops control the ac current to their respective references. The circulating current controller maintains the
second harmonic component of arm currents at zero.

To enable parallel operation of VGs, two additional loops marked by the shaded region in Figure 1.1-2
are added. The controller marked as “Parallel Connection” avoids 120 Hz current oscillations between the
parallel-connected VGs by controlling the nonzero frequency common-mode arm current to zero.

1.2.2 Station control

Series connection station controller: Unequal power sharing between the series-connected VGs is
observed when the OHL at +400 kV is absent (because of dc fault or maintenance of OHL). To correct
unequal load sharing, an additional controller named “Series Connection” (see Figure 1.2-2) is included.
As shown in Figure 1.2-2, the ac power reference of the series-connected VGs is adjusted based on the
difference between the dc voltages of the VGs. The Substation Controller is executed at a time step of 1
ms. To avoid interaction with the other control loops, the integral time constant of the series controller is
chosen to be 10 times larger and its proportional gain to be equal to that of the ac power controller.
Whereas the dq current control and circulating current controllers are implemented at 1 kHz band width,
the band width of the energy controller is set to be 100 Hz. The dc voltage and ac power loops are
designed to be much slower, with bandwidths of 10 Hz and 0.2 Hz, respectively.

Voltage controller: This control scheme was developed to control POI bus voltages by regulating reactive
power of the station within available reactive power capabilities. To achieve this, the POI voltage control

(Figure 1.2-3).

1.2.3 Dc breaker coordination

As mentioned previously, to mitigate the dc fault current, a dcB is placed at each end of the OHL
sections. In this report, no detailed dc fault detection algorithm is implemented. Instead, the dcB is opened
after 3 ms of fault inception. The dcB open command is communicated with the dcB connected at the
remote end of the OHL (and this strategy is termed communication-based coordination between dcBs).
Because the OHL faults are temporary in nature, the faulted section of the OHL can be restarted if the
fault is temporary and is cleared. In this report, the primary–secondary restart approach is applied with
communication-based coordination between the dcBs. The restart operation is detailed in a separate
section in this report.

1.2.4 MTdc system control and protection coordination

All the VGs in the MTdc system are provided with active power references. Based on the error in active
power, the dc voltage reference is adjusted through a proportional controller (droop controller). The error
in active power is not strictly controlled to zero. Hence, the power transferred between different stations
might partially deviate from the respective reference power commands. The total power loss of the MTdc
system is shared among all the VGs. As shown in Error! Reference source not found., the droop gain
can be adjusted based on inputs (e.g., gains and deadband) from a higher-level control. A generalized
MTdc power flow solver is also needed to help optimize droop parameters in normal operations.

In case of a dc fault, the dcB open command is communicated to the connected VGs’ controller. For the
long OHL, in this report the communication delay is considered 7 ms for 800 kV OHL faults and 5 ms for
400 kV OHL faults. Once the VG controller receives the signal from dcB, the power reference of the VGs
is altered. Based on the availability of redundant OHL lines, the power reference remains unchanged or
reduced to half of the prefault value. These constraints are used to optimize the droop parameters and
power commands post-fault.

1.2.4.1 Generalized MTdc power flow solver

One of the gaps in power system modeling tools used to study large MTdc systems is that no power flow
solver is available for planning and operation studies of a voltage source converter (VSC)-based MTdc
grid. This prevents engineers from not only performing steady-state full network and contingency studies
but also obtaining steady-state solutions that can help initialize dynamic simulations.

To help bridge this gap, the project team has developed a power flow algorithm that is general and can
represent an MTdc network with converters of various configurations [6], including the one used in this
work (Figure 1.1-2). In this configuration, a station can have multiple parallel- and series-connected VGs
to increase the station rating, and the dc network can have more than one dc voltage level per pole, as in
traditional dc.

 7

The MTdc generalized power flow model can represent asymmetrical operational conditions in the MTdc
system. The power flow model explicitly represents the positive-pole, negative-pole, and neutral circuits
(composed of VGs, buses, and lines) so that outages of any of these elements can be modeled. With
representation of all these elements, the algorithm can find the steady-state system condition in the MTdc
network for balanced, full network operation, as well as unbalanced conditions with element outages in
any of the MTdc circuits.

The generalized power flow solution depends not only on the parameters of the lines and the injections
between dc and ac systems but also on the control settings of the converter controls for dc voltage and
power injections between the ac and dc systems. The power flow algorithm is not limited by the number
of VGs per station, dc buses, dc lines, VG control parameters, and dc network topologies.
The steady-state solution obtained with the developed algorithms has been validated against detailed
electromagnetic transient (EMT) models for both normal and contingency conditions with very high
accuracy; the power flow steady-state solution closely matches the final steady-state conditions obtained

1.2.4.3 Post-fault optimization of droop settings

When an outage occurs on the MTdc system of a dc line or VG, the power schedules and droop control
gains need to be rescheduled quickly. If the system keeps the same power reference as that of the pre-
contingency conditions, the system might be led to instability and/or significant dc voltage/current
violations. In addition to optimizing droop coefficients for dc voltage control like in normal conditions, it
is important to perform fast rescheduling to find new power reference for all VGs.

An optimization algorithm for fast active power rescheduling is developed. The objective of the algorithm
is to minimize the total deviation of the new active power reference at POIs from the pre-contingency
power reference—as well as the total deviation between the actual and new power references—while
satisfying all operating limits.

The optimization algorithms use the MTdc power flow model that can represent asymmetrical operational
conditions in the MTdc system. The power flow model explicitly represents the positive- and negative-
pole circuits (VGs, buses, and lines) so that outages of any of these elements can be modeled.

1.2.5.2 PCM implementation with additional equivalent MTdc operational constraints

Geographical decomposition

To address the computational and representational challenges of running EI-scale dc optimal power flow
(DCOPF)with MTdc integration, a three-step workflow was created, as illustrated in Figure 1.2-7.

PLEXOS is applied to solve the DCOPF for the EI dc/ac system, incorporating VSC line and converter
station capacity limits, as well as practical power flow ramping constraints and phase-angle damping
control limits. However, PLEXOS uses a lossless DCOPF model, which diverges from the detailed MTdc
model. Additionally, solving an EI-scale DCOPF presents tractability issues, and the centralized system
dispatch approach conflicts with the decentralized operation of independent system operators (ISOs). To
address these challenges, the three-step strategy in Figure 1.2-7 has been developed to improve the
alignment and efficiency of the modeling approach.

 12

 Figure 1.2-7. Three-step strategy for DCOPF in a dc/ac system.
 (Abbreviation: MIP = mixed integer programming.)

The first step involves using PLEXOS to solve the entire EI DCOPF with a lossless, simplified MTdc
PCM and linear programming. In the second step, the power flow at dc/ac POIs is fixed, and the detailed
MTdc model is solved while considering losses, ensuring greater accuracy in system representation. In the
final step, the MTdc architecture power flow from Step 2, along with the updated POI dc/ac flow
exchange, is fixed. A series of decomposed mixed integer subprograms (sub-MIPs) are solved in parallel
for each ISO. In each subproblem, the generators of the focused ISO are freely optimized via unit
commitment and economic dispatch, using mixed integer programming. For non-focused ISOs,
generators will follow the fixed profiles established in Step 1. The detailed formulation of the strategy is
in the Appendix.

To ensure the tractability of the DCOPF problem in the EI-scale power grid, the following solutions are
adopted:

  Apply the three-stage geographical and temporal decomposition method to the EI system and use
 high-performance computing (HPC) for system parallel computation.

  Solve the vulnerable days PCM with down-selected transmission line constraints (only consider
 230 kV and above transmission lines). This type of modification still yields a high memory
 request in HPC.

  Solve the PCM with 230 kV and above AC lines with power flow >90% of the line loading (can
 be solved with standard memory request in HPC).

  Iteratively add the 230 kV and above lines that violated power flow limits in the line modeling
 list and solve the problem.

1.2.5.3 dc-to-ac power flow conversion bridge development

The next step in the process is to convert the PLEXOS dc power flow results to ac power flow. PLEXOS
uses the dc power flow model, which is a simplified and linear approximation, although using it to obtain
a good initial condition in dynamic simulations is challenging. To achieve the conversion and create a
robust conversion bridge for the EI system, the dc-to-ac power flow conversion tool (Figure 1.2-8) is
used. The workflow presented in the figure facilitates the conversion of vulnerable operation hours from
dc to ac power flow.

 Figure 1.2-8. dc-to-ac bridge flowchart. (Abbreviation: PSS/E = Power System Simulator for Engineering.)

The first step is to identify a solved ac power flow by systematically adjusting the case, such as adding
temporary fictitious generators with zero active power injections and unlimited reactive power capacity or
reducing the system loading. Once a solved ac power flow solution is found, the tool gradually reverses
these adjustments by solving a series of power flow cases. It does this by either modifying or maintaining
certain specified conditions of the power flow problems, such as the voltage set point of the generator bus
and the loading level of the load bus. The entire procedure is automated, ultimately resulting in an AC
power flow solution.

In this section, the results pertaining to the maintenance of VG+H2 in the GI station are presented.

2.6.2.3 dc fault study

As mentioned previously, in this study the +800 kV, −800 kV, +400 kV, −400 kV and the neutral OHLs
are constructed on different tower structures. Hence, either positive-pole-to-ground or negative-pole-to-
ground faults are the only possible dc fault scenarios. The positive-pole-to-negative-pole fault is not
possible and is therefore not considered in this work.

Temporary and permanent faults

OHL line-to-ground faults typically occur because of brush fires and insulation breakdowns. In most
cases, they can clear in a short time. The power flow through the line can be restored after the fault is
cleared. In the event of a permanent fault, the line has to be permanently disconnected for repair.
Therefore, identifying the difference between temporary and permanent faults is important.

A fault occurs at t = 0 s, and it can be detected in multiple ways. Using advanced detection algorithms, the
fault is assumed to be detected within 3 ms. In this work, the following steps are performed to handle dc
faults (also summarized in Error! Reference source not found.):

 30

 Once a dcB detects a fault, it communicates the fault to the dcB connected at the other end of the
 transmission line. Based on the OHL length, the communication delay is assumed to be 7 ms for
 800 kV faults and 5 ms for 400 kV faults (owing to the respective lengths of the lines).

 After the fault is detected, the dcB is opened. First, the load commutation switch (LCS) and
 ultrafast disconnector (UFD) are opened, and these actions divert the fault current through MB
 modules.

 After 3 ms (for safe opening of UFD), the MB modules are opened in a stepped manner. In this
 report, for an 800 kV dcB (containing 10 of 80 kV series-connected MB modules), the first five
 MB modules are opened concurrently. After a delay of 200 µs, the remaining five modules are
 opened. For a 400 kV DCB (containing 10 of the 40 kV series-connected MB modules), the first
 five MB modules are opened concurrently. After a delay of 200 µs, the remaining five modules
 are opened. This sequence can be altered, but it does not significantly affect the performance of
 the MTdc system. Once all the MB modules are opened, the current gets diverted into the
 arresters, and the fault current drops to zero. Up to this point, the two dcBs connected on either
 end behave identically.

 While making a restart attempt, one of the two dcBs is designated as the master dcB, which is
 responsible for the restart. The slave will wait for the primary dcB’s command on whether to
 close or permanently open.

 After dcB opening, a waiting period of 200 ms is considered for fault deionization.

 After 200 ms, five of the MB modules are closed concurrently.

 Then with a 200 µs time lag, the remaining five modules are also closed. If the fault is temporary,
 the current does not rise after the MB modules are closed. Hence, the power flow through the
 OHL can be reestablished.

 If the fault is permanent, after closing the MB modules, the fault current again increases. Thus,
 after closing the MB modules, the current is monitored for 20 ms.

 After a 20 ms wait, the fault can be assumed to be cleared if the current stays below 2 kA (0.4
 p.u.). The UFD and LCS are closed, and the OHL is ready to transfer power. A current that is
 more than 2 kA means the fault persists, and the MB modules are once again opened.

 If the fault is cleared, the master dcB communicates the decision to close the breaker to the slave
 dcB (with a delay of 7 ms/5 ms), which is subsequently closed. The UFD and LCS are not closed
 until it is confirmed that the fault is cleared. This minimizes the number of operations of the
 mechanical switch UFD and increases its lifespan.

 The wait time of 20 ms is essential to avoid false detection of permanent faults. After the fault is
 cleared, a current that charges the capacitance of the dc OHL could exist. Further, as the OHL
 connected in parallel to the faulted line is carrying rated current, momentary oscillating current
 could occur between the parallel lines. The 20 ms delay is needed for such transient currents to
 die down.

 The dc fault signal is communicated to the corresponding converter stations. A communication
 time delay of 7 ms/5 ms is assumed. Upon detection of a dc fault—until the fault is determined to

 31

 be temporary or permanent—the power transfer command of the VGs connected with the faulty
 line is reduced by 2 GW, if there are no redundant parallel OHLs for handling the power.

  In case of a temporary fault, the slave dcB remains in wait mode. The master dcB will make three
 restart attempts, with 200 ms deionization time between each operation. If the fault persists, the
 mechanical disconnectors on both the primary and secondary side dcBs will be opened
 permanently.

2.6.4 System-level controller and test results

This section analyzes the redispatch from the system (both ac and dc in the slower timescale) once the
fault occurs. The ac redispatch analysis aims to evaluate the system’s response to a contingency at a
specific POI with a load increment of 4 GW. This assessment focuses on a critical time stamp
corresponding to the network’s annual peak demand, identified as July 7 at 16:00.

The first step involves identifying which generators in the PLEXOS EI database should be designated for
automatic generation control (AGC). Because of the absence of detailed start-up profiles in the PLEXOS
database and the limited availability of public data, the classification approach outlined by the US Energy
Information Administration (EIA) is adopted. This classification is important for accurately modeling
generator responsiveness during the AC redispatch simulation, ensuring that the system’s dynamic
behavior under contingency scenarios is correctly represented.

According to the EIA data presented in Figure 2.6-13, approximately 25% of US power plants can start
up within an hour, with varying capabilities based on technology. Conventional hydroelectric and pumped
storage turbines can achieve around 80% of their operating capacity from a cold shutdown, whereas
petroleum liquids and natural gas combustion turbines (CTs) can reach approximately 25% within the
same time frame. These values derived from EIA data will serve as the basis for approximating the start
time of relevant generator categories in the PLEXOS model during the simulation.

  Figure 2.6-13. Minimum time required for different generators to reach operating capacity starting from
 shutdown, based on EIA data.

An initial simulation is performed using PLEXOS under normal operating conditions for the chosen time
stamp, corresponding to the network’s annual peak demand. This simulation allows the research team to
extract the set points of all generators in the database at this critical time. Based on the values of these set
points, the team next modifies each generator’s specific properties in the PLEXOS database to correctly
represent the initial state of the network for the ac redispatch simulation.

 41

The ac redispatch simulation assumes a redispatch period of 15 min, which requires an additional
classification of the generators to appropriately adjust their operational properties. More specifically, the
team programmatically modifies the maximum capacity, minimum stable level, units, initial units
generating, and initial generation properties in PLEXOS based on the generator type and operational
status at the specific time stamp. The goal is to ensure that generators with nonzero generation values at
the time stamp of interest are adjusted differently compared with those that have zero output, reflecting
their potential contribution to redispatch within the 15 min timeframe. A visual representation of this
classification is shown in Figure 2.6-14.

 Figure 2.6-14. Classification of PLEXOS generators for ac redispatch simulation.

For generators categorized according to the EIA data, their status at the time stamp of interest is critical
for determining the appropriate adjustments. More specifically, for generators with a set point of zero at
this time stamp, the scaling factors provided by EIA are applied (i.e., 25% or 80% of operating capacity)
depending on the generator type. However, accounting for the simulation’s 15 min duration is crucial. If
the scaled value, calculated as the EIA factor multiplied by the generator’s operating capacity, is less than
the generator’s minimum stable level property value, the generator is excluded from the simulation
(shown in red in Figure 2.6-14). On the other hand, if the scaled value exceeds the minimum stable level
property value, the generator remains active in the new simulation. The retained generators’ maximum
capacity property is then updated to reflect the scaled value, which represents the capacity realistically
attainable within the 15 min interval.

For generators within the selected categories that had nonzero generation set points at the time stamp of
interest, additional properties need to be set. If the generation set point equals the generator’s maximum
capacity, it can only ramp down during the AC redispatch simulation. To model this, the generator’s
maximum ramp-down capability is calculated based on its max ramp-up property value (defined in
MW/min) and the 15 min simulation duration. If the resulting value is larger than the generator’s
minimum stable level, this adjusted ramp-down limit is set as the new minimum stable level property.
Otherwise, the generator’s minimum stable level property remains unchanged. Similarly, for generators
with generation set points equal to their minimum stable level, adjustments are made to their maximum

 42

capacity property respectively, ensuring that the generator’s ramping-up capability aligns with the
simulation duration.

For generators with set points equal to neither maximum capacity nor minimum stable level, both
properties are adjusted in accordance with their ramping capabilities. This ensures consistency between
the operational properties and the generator’s realistic performance within the 15 min simulation window.
Additionally, for all generators with nonzero set points at the time stamp of interest, starting the ac
redispatch simulation with these generators exactly at their initial set point is crucial. To achieve this in
PLEXOS, the initial generation property is set equal to the generator’s generation value at the time of the
contingency, and the initial units’ generating property is updated to reflect the corresponding number of
generating units.

Finally, for the generators in the rest of the categories, all property values discussed previously are
modified. The initial generation property is set to the generation set point value at the time stamp of
interest, and the initial units’ generating property is set equal to the corresponding number of generating
units. Because these generators have slow start profiles, none of them are included in the 15 min ac
redispatch simulation, which in PLEXOS is achieved by setting their commitment property equal to zero.

Preliminary results from the ac redispatch simulation for the specific time stamp of interest are presented
in Figure 2.6-15 and Figure 2.6-16, in which percentage changes in generation are plotted by generator
type and by zone, respectively.

The largest value for the increase in generation is observed for the pumped hydro plants. The total
generation is tripled across all zones, mainly driven by their ability to quickly ramp up from a state of

(initial time stamp), leaving limited space for further adjustment.

Significant increases in generation are also observed for oil and natural gas CTs. These include a 150%
increase for waste oil CT plants, a 100% increase for distillate fuel oil CT plants, and a 20% increase for

units to address the contingency.

 5. IMPACT

In this project design, operation and control of a large HVdc substation (16 GW) is established. Further, a
four-terminal MTdc system involving the proposed HVdc substation is designed, and its operation during
steady state and faults is tested through simulations. This project provides a pathway for future MTdc
systems based on VSC technology. Because the design can support transfer of up to 20 GW/station of
power over a distance of up to thousands of miles, this project will be a benchmark design for developing
a large continental-level electric power transmission system, which can enhance the reliability and
resilience of the US electrical grid.

The power flow and dynamic tools developed in this project are also being leveraged in different projects
supported by other US Department of Energy offices.

 6. FUTURE WORK AND CONCLUSION

The large-scale dc-ac grid analysis completed in this project did not discuss interactions between HVdc
stations and inverters. This has been observed recently in the field during some of the reliability events.
One of the future activities of interest will therefore be to evaluate the reliability of future power grids
with an understanding of the interaction between HVdc stations and inverters (from both loads and
sources).

As mentioned previously, the MTdc system has multiple HVdc substations/terminals. The grounding of
the MTdc system will have a significant effect on the dc fault current flow and electrical ratings of the
equipment. In this report, the system is grounded at only one location (i.e., GI). In the future, the optimum
grounding location and its effect on the system design can be studied.

Finally, new use cases such as integration of data centers and connectivity of new generation to load
centers using HVdc, as well as integration of offshore oil wells to onshore grids (including industry
projects that are happening today), should be explored for the United States.

 APPENDIX B. GEOGRAPHIC DECOMPOSITION METHODOLOGY
To reduce the computational burden and complexity of performing day-ahead and real-time simulations
for the contiguous US power grid, geographic and temporal decomposition methods are leveraged.
Running simulations for the same large-scale network is also required to successfully define new
operational constraints, which ensure that the high-voltage direct current lines operate within limits, while
supporting future system needs.

Geographic decomposition is performed in three stages. This section presents an analysis of a short
description of what each stage achieves as well as the changes that are made programmatically by the
developed script.

B.1 Stage A

The goal of Stage A is to perform a relaxation of the system-wide unit commitment (UC) problem. A less
complex linear problem, constrained by network active power flow limits, is solved at this stage. As a
result, the solution requires less computational time to be computed compared with that of the
corresponding mixed integer program. It provides economic dispatch decisions for each generator, which
can consequently be used to also calculate interregional interchange flows between the different zones, as
visualized in Figure A-1.

 Figure B-1. US power network split into 11 zones.

 A-2

To run the simplified UC, the changes that are made programmatically to the PLEXOS database file
(.xml) are the following: (1) A new model, scenario, and horizon object are added to the database’s object
list. (2) These are then linked together by adding the corresponding memberships. (3) For all the
conventional thermal generators that match the type the user has specified, the start-up and shutdown
costs and the ramp-up and ramp-down rates are all set to zero for the Stage A scenario. (4) For the same
generators, the heat rate function is linearized by taking the average value of the quadratic heat rate. (5)
Finally, the “offset at start property” for the generators that have start fuel is set to zero, and all the
reserve-related properties are disabled.

Changes to the simulation parameters are also made in the attributes of the database. To reduce the

B.2 Stage B

The goal of Stage B is to solve a series of N decomposed mixed integer subprograms, one for each
disjoint subregion. Each subproblem is a UC/economic dispatch (ED) for the specific region and can be
solved in parallel. The split into subregions is also advantageous because it results in a decrease of the
overall size of the integer solution space and reduces the total computation time.

As visualized in Figure A-2, the zones can be characterized as focus and nonfocus. The generators in the
focus zone can freely optimize their UC and ED, meaning that the same parameters as the ones that
existed in the original (input) database are assigned. The generators in the nonfocus zone follow fixed
profiles that are obtained from the output of Stage A. The same linearization used in Stage A is applied
for all the generators in the nonfocus region. The necessary number of scenarios, models, and horizons is
created so that every zone becomes a focus zone for only one scenario.

B.3 Stage C

After all the N decomposed UC/ED simulations are completed in Stage B, a system wide redispatch is run
on those results in a single physical solution.

The simulations for each region, represented by an individual scenario, can then be parallelized using
high-performance computing before the reconciliation of the system operator behavior, where a final
system-wide redispatch simulation is run.
