Dysregulated differentiation kinetics underlie the essential role of DNA damage repair in placental development
Authors: Shanshan Yi, Mingzhu Wang, Qianshu Zhu, Yanxin Huang, Jing Xiao, Ming Zong, Wenju Liu, Xingyi Zhou, Jincan He, Mian Wang, Kexin He, Xiaochen Kou, Yanhong Zhao, Hong Wang, Rui Gao, Shaorong Gao, Guang Yang, Jiayu Chen, Cizhong Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe placenta orchestrates maternal–fetal exchanges, and its dysfunction compromises pregnancy outcomes. Somatic cell nuclear transfer (SCNT) placentas offer a model to investigate such dysfunction. However, SCNT placentas exhibit severe pathological features that remain poorly understood. Using single-nucleus multi-omics profiling, we uncover defective differentiation programs in SCNT placentas, including the persistent multipotency of arrested trophoblast precursors and an aberrant differentiation trajectory in the junctional zone. In addition, SCNT placentas demonstrate impaired VEGF signaling, which subsequently compromises labyrinthine vascularization. Mechanistically, we identify reprogramming-induced DNA damage as a core driver of these defects. Furthermore, we trace this genomic instability to the loss of donor cell-inherited H3K27me3 protection, an epigenetic deficiency that correlates with specific DNA damage-associated regions. Consistently, enhancing DNA damage repair pathways restores proper trophoblast differentiation kinetics and vascular transport capacity. Collectively, our study reveals that genomic instability acts as a barrier to placental development, providing a molecular framework to understand compromised placental function.Subject terms: Differentiation, Reprogramming, Cell lineage

Introduction

The placenta is a transient yet highly sophisticated organ that establishes vital maternal-fetal connections for oxygen and nutrient transfer while simultaneously functioning as an essential protective barrier against pathogenic and toxic threats1. Dysfunction of the placenta underlies a spectrum of pregnancy-related disorders, including fetal growth restriction, preeclampsia, preterm birth, miscarriage, and stillbirth2,3. Despite its central role, modeling human placental pathologies remains challenging due to the organ’s developmental complexity, limited temporal accessibility, and ethical constraints. Somatic cell nuclear transfer (SCNT), or cloning, is the sole method capable of fully reprogramming a differentiated somatic cell into a totipotent state to produce viable offspring, underscoring its importance for both reproductive and therapeutic applications4,5. Unlike conventional genetic or pharmacological models, where mid- to late-gestational lethality precludes the systematic assessment of placental physiology6, SCNT placentas progress to advanced gestational stages while retaining distinct pathological features across species7,8. This unique “observable and intervenable” system permits longitudinal phenotypic characterization, mechanistic dissection, and therapeutic validation at the maternal–fetal interface, providing a practical platform to interrogate placental dysfunction and nuclear reprogramming barriers.

Previous investigations into the causes of cloning failure revealed that replacing trophectoderm (TE) of cloned embryo with in vitro fertilization (IVF)-derived tetraploid counterparts effectively resolved placental hyperplasia and achieved 15.7% live births, providing direct evidence that trophoblast lineage defects critically limit cloning efficiency9. TE gives rise to the junctional zone (JZ) and labyrinth (Lab), which together with the maternal decidua, comprise three distinct regions of the mature mouse placenta10. The labyrinth facilitates maternal–fetal nutrient, gas, and waste exchange through a branched network formed by interdigitating fetal capillaries and maternal blood sinuses. This network is structurally supported by mononuclear sinusoidal trophoblast giant cells (S-TGC) surrounding maternal sinuses, and two layers of syncytiotrophoblasts (SynTI and SynTII) in contact with fetal vessels11,12. Cloned placentas have been reported to exhibit poor vascularization and a generalized hypoxic state13, while the mechanisms remain unclear. Outside the labyrinth lies the junctional zone, also known as the spongiotrophoblast layer, the primary endocrine compartment secreting hormones and growth factors essential for pregnancy maintenance. It comprises spongiotrophoblasts (SpT) mainly responsible for endocrine function and glycogen trophoblasts (GlyT) for glycogen storage and mobilization to support fetal growth14,15. Cloned placentas are initially underdeveloped during early gestation but progress to hyperplasia from mid-gestation, representing a hallmark phenotype of cloned animals. Previous studies have shown that SCNT placental hyperplasia predominantly occurs in the junctional zone16,17, yet the cellular and molecular basis remain poorly understood.

Epigenetic modulation has been widely studied as a strategy to ameliorate placental hyperplasia. Reprogramming of somatic nuclei toward totipotency entails dramatic chromatin remodeling, yet this process is often incomplete, resulting in abnormal epigenetic landscapes and subsequent defects in embryonic and placental development. Epigenetic restoration by Dnmt3a/3b knockdown or treatment with chromatin remodeling agents such as H3K9me3 demethylases and histone deacetylase inhibitors (HDACi) has been shown to improve SCNT reprogramming efficiency and rescue placental dysfunction18,19. Alternatively, haplotype-specific knockout of multiple H3K27me3-imprinted genes has successfully rescued the placental phenotype16,20–22, albeit at the cost of compromised genomic integrity and increased pathogenic risks. Emerging evidence reveals that DNA damage alters the localization of chromatin-modifying factors and disrupts histone modification landscapes such as H3K27me3, H3K27ac and H3K56ac23, highlighting a critical link between genome instability and epigenetic regulation. In SCNT embryos, DNA damage accumulates throughout preimplantation development due to the dual pressures of replication stress and reprogramming stress24,25. DNA damage could activate cell cycle checkpoints26,27, thereby exacerbating reprogramming failure and frequently resulting in developmental arrest. Recently, we showed that mitigating DNA damage by promoting homologous recombination (HR) repair through enhanced RAD51 and BRCA1 function improves SCNT efficiency25. Unlike epigenetic modulation, DNA damage repair restores genome stability and corrects chromosome segregation errors, highlighting DNA repair enhancement as a promising strategy for improving SCNT outcomes.

To investigate how incomplete reprogramming impacts placental development and drives hyperplasia, a recent study profiled the cellular composition of embryonic day (E) 9.5 SCNT placentas at single-nucleus resolution28. While it delineated differences in cell types, transcriptional profiles, and cell–cell interactions between SCNT and IVF placentas, it did not address the underlying mechanisms or developmental dynamics. Here, we employed single-nucleus RNA and ATAC-seq multi-omics at the E12.5, E14.5 and E18.5 to capture temporal dysfunctions in SCNT placentas. We found that SCNT placentas exhibited persistent retention of multipotent trophoblast precursors and sustained cell cycle arrest, leading to delayed lineage maturation. These stalled precursors subsequently underwent an aberrant lineage detour from SpT precursors to GlyT, leading to glycogen overload and endocrine insufficiency in the junctional zone. Concurrently, defective labyrinthine vascularization and ensuing hypoxia exacerbated metabolic dysfunction through impaired crosstalk between SynTII and endothelial cells. Mechanistically, inherited DNA damage from the donor nucleus emerged as a key driver of these abnormalities. Remarkably, pharmacological enhancement of homologous recombination repair mitigated DNA damage, restored normal trophoblast differentiation, and re-established vascular and metabolic homeostasis. Taken together, our work provides a comprehensive single-nucleus dissection of developmental barriers in SCNT placentas, identifying genomic instability as a principal barrier of placental dysfunction and outlining a feasible strategy to improve cloning outcomes.

Results

SCNT placentas exhibit trophoblast composition shifts

To dissect cloned placental defects, we established an experimental system using PWK (paternal) × C57BL/6N (maternal) hybrid strains for SNP-based allelic resolution, with the ICR strain as the surrogate mother. Multimodal single-nucleus (sn)RNA-seq and snATAC-seq were performed on naturally fertilized (WT) and SCNT placentas at critical developmental stages (E12.5, 14.5, and 18.5) (Fig. 1A and Supplementary Data 1, 2). SCNT-derived placentas exhibited marked hyperplasia, evidenced primarily by increased organ weight21,23,25,29–31 (Supplementary Fig. 1A). Histological examination localized this enlargement in the junctional zone (JZ), which progressively invaded the labyrinthine layer (Lab)16,17,32 (Fig. 1B and Supplementary Fig. 1B). This resulted in a disproportionate JZ-to-Lab area ratio, reflecting a fundamental disruption of spatial architecture and lineage differentiation. After excluding doublets and low-quality cells, we obtained a median of 3157 detected genes per cell across the retained 31,566 cells from the six samples (Supplementary Data 2). Using canonical marker genes, we identified six major cell populations through integrated RNA and ATAC modalities33–35 (Fig. 1C, D, Supplementary Fig. 1C–E and Supplementary Data 3). Strong concordance between modalities confirmed the robustness of our data36 (Fig. 1E). SNP-based allelic analysis showed surrogate maternal origin (~0% PWK) in decidual stroma and blood cells (mainly immune cells and erythrocytes), versus fetal origin (~50% PWK) in other lineages (Fig. 1F and Supplementary Fig. 1F). Together, these analyses established a high-resolution multi-omics atlas for systematic analysis of SCNT placental defects.

Our single-nucleus atlas highlighted that trophoblasts constitute the principal cellular component in both WT and SCNT placentas (Fig. 1G and Supplementary Fig. 1G). Strikingly, unlike the progressive trophoblast reduction observed during normal placental development, SCNT placentas maintained gradually elevated trophoblast proportions throughout gestation, most notably at E18.5 (66.9% vs. 87.0%) (Fig. 1G, Supplementary Fig. 1E and Supplementary Data 4). Since trophoblasts constitute more than 80% of placental cells (Fig. 1G and Supplementary Fig. 1G), we further subclassified them into 13 distinct subtypes11,34,37–39 (Figs. 1H, I, Supplementary Fig. 1H and 1I and Supplementary Data 3), including eight precursor populations: labyrinth trophoblast progenitors (LaTP and LaTP2), junctional zone progenitors (JZP1 and JZP2), SynTI precursor, SynTII precursor, S-TGC precursor, and SpT precursor, along with five terminally differentiated subtypes: SynTII, SynTI, S-TGC, SpT, and GlyT (Fig. 1H). In addition, precursor populations were characterized by high expression of Mki67 and Gjb3, indicative of early proliferating trophoblast states (Supplementary Fig. 1J). Investigation into cell proportions revealed two dimensions of differences in cell compositions between SCNT and WT placentas. Firstly, we observed a progressive decline in labyrinthine populations in SCNT placentas and marked expansion of junctional zone cells (Fig. 1J) compared with WT placentas, which was consistent with the expanded JZ we observed (Fig. 1B). This imbalance was particularly evident in the GlyT population, which continuously expanded to comprise 55.2% of total trophoblasts by E18.5 in SCNT placentas, compared with a dramatic decline to 14.8% in WT placentas, suggesting its central role in placental hyperplasia. In parallel, JZ progenitors remained elevated while SpT differentiation was reduced, indicating impaired differentiation kinetics in the junctional zone. LaTP, LaTP2, and other precursor populations accumulated in the Lab, accompanied by reduced SynTI, SynTII, and S-TGC cells. Notably, the loss of SynTI at E18.5 was particularly pronounced (Fig. 1J and Supplementary Data 4). In addition to imbalanced cell populations across locations (JZ vs. Lab), we also observed higher percentages of trophoblast precursor cells in SCNT placentas at E12.5 and E14.5. The differences rapidly diminished along development and disappeared at E18.5. In contrast, the differentiated cells were more enriched in WT placentas. This suggested relatively delayed precursor differentiation in SCNT placentas (Fig. 1K, L and Supplementary Fig. 1K).

To ensure the reproducibility of these temporal dynamics, we performed additional multi-omics on WT and SCNT placentas at E14.5 as biological replicates because SCNT placental overweight emerged at E14.5 (Supplementary Data 1), the proportions of the major cell types prominently diverged starting at E14.5 in SCNT placentas (Fig. 1G). We obtained more than 5300 cells for each sample and ~2500 genes per cell for the two snRNA-seq datasets. As expected, the biological replicates showed reproducible composition of the major cell types and the expansion of trophoblasts in SCNT placentas (Supplementary Fig. 2A), and recapitulated the composition of trophoblast subtypes, demonstrating that trophoblast precursor cells were aberrantly increased in SCNT placentas (Supplementary Fig. 2B–E). This confirmed the reproducibility of our single-nucleus multi-omics data.

We further performed a statistical assessment of cellular composition shifts using different methods for analyzing cell-type abundance in single-cell and single-nucleus omics data, including scProportionTest, the ratio of observed to expected (Ro/e) method40–42, and scCODA43. Overall, our initial observations that precursor cells were enriched while differentiated cells were depleted in SCNT placentas were supported statistically, though there were minor discrepancies across algorithms (Fig. 1M, N, Supplementary Fig. 2F and Supplementary Data 5). This suggested that these cell type abundance changes were true biological shifts, rather than sampling effects.

In conclusion, our single-nucleus multi-omics atlas revealed abnormal trophoblast composition shifts and imbalanced lineage allocation, which underlies structural and functional defects during SCNT placental development.

SCNT trophoblast precursors are trapped in multipotent state

Trophoblast precursor cells could almost complete the differentiation process in WT placentas at E14.5 (Fig. 1K), coinciding with structural maturation44,45. In contrast, SCNT placentas retained abundant precursors at E14.5, despite their differentiation being completed until E18.5, prompting us to investigate the cellular basis of their retention.

To further characterize the developmental state of these retained precursors, we quantified differentiation potency along a defined hierarchy using the CytoTRACE2 algorithm46, ranking the cells from totipotent to pluripotent, oligopotent, multipotent, and unipotent. As expected, the precursor cells exhibited markedly higher differentiation potential than the differentiated cells (Fig. 2A, B). Notably, every subtype of precursor cells displayed higher developmental potential in SCNT placentas, indicating a more immature status (Fig. 2A, B). Furthermore, precursor cells in SCNT placentas were predominantly in a multipotent state; in contrast, those in WT placentas were largely unipotent (Fig. 2C). Unlike the precursors, the majority of the differentiated cells were committed to terminal fates in both WT and SCNT placentas (Fig. 2A–C). Moreover, a substantial proportion of trophoblasts were in a multipotent state at E12.5 (42.6%) and E14.5 (16.5%) in SCNT placentas compared with only 0.5% and 0.3% in WT placentas, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 3A). Together, these findings indicate that precursor cells exhibit multipotency state persistence, differentiation failure, and delayed placental maturation.

Given the antagonistic relationship between self‑renewal capacity and differentiation status47, we analyzed cell-cycle stages in SCNT and WT trophoblasts. The results showed that cells with higher potency scores predominantly resided in the active S and G2M phases of the cell cycle (Supplementary Fig. 3B and 3C). Consistent with their terminally differentiated states, WT placentas exhibited a sharp decline in the percentage of trophoblasts in S and G2M phases, from 48.3% (E12.5) to 4.4% (E14.5). In contrast, SCNT placentas retained a high percentage of trophoblasts in the S and G2M phases, from 78.3% (E12.5) to 48.6% (E14.5) (Fig. 2D, E and Supplementary Fig. 2G). These findings indicated that the differentiation lag in SCNT trophoblast precursors was likely due to the precursors being stuck in the S and G2M phases until E14.5.

To understand the molecular basis of differentiation retardation, we identified differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in each of the eight precursor populations between SCNT and WT placentas (Supplementary Data 6). Functional annotation of the pooled upregulated DEGs across the precursor subtypes revealed significant enrichment in cell cycle phase transition, double-strand break repair, response to DNA damage, and DNA damage checkpoint signaling (Fig. 2F). Notably, key homologous recombination (HR) genes Brca1, Rad51, Mre11a and Rbbp8/CtIP were significantly upregulated in SCNT precursors (Fig. 2G and Supplementary Data 6)48–50, suggesting a compensatory mechanism that reinforces cell‑cycle checkpoint control to prevent cells with DNA damage from entering mitosis51. Taken together, we demonstrate that SCNT placental precursor cells retain high multipotent potential and exhibit impaired differentiation commitment, accompanied by cell cycle arrest mediated by DNA damage checkpoints. This dysregulation may underlie the developmental delay and functional immaturity observed in SCNT placentas.

Distorted JZ cell differentiation and metabolic homeostasis

To further elucidate how delayed differentiation and sustained multipotency reshape trophoblast lineage topology, we performed computation-based developmental trajectories inference (pseudotime and RNA velocity) analyses. In the labyrinth, LaTP cells developed into SynTII, while LaTP2 gave rise to SynTI and S-TGC lineages, with no divergence observed between WT and SCNT placentas (Fig. 3A). In contrast, the junctional zone lineages exhibited striking abnormalities in SCNT placentas. In WT placentas, JZP1 and JZP2 normally differentiated into GlyT, consistent with the previous studies34,52. However, SCNT placentas displayed an aberrant branch trajectory in which SpT precursors bypassed the JZP state and directly differentiated into GlyT (Fig. 3A and Supplementary Fig. 4A, B). Concordant with the pseudotime analysis results, RNA velocity analysis suggested this aberrant differentiation trajectory from SpT precursors to GlyT in SCNT placentas (Supplementary Fig. 4C). Moreover, we performed the same analyses for the labyrinthine and the junctional zones separately and obtained consistent results (Fig. 3B and Supplementary Figs. 2H, 4D, E). Thus, GlyT in SCNT placentas may arise from both normal and aberrant trajectories, resulting in its aberrant expansion. Together, these findings provided a major cellular basis for placental hyperplasia, although validation experiments using lineage tracing or in vitro differentiation assays are warranted. In addition to this aberrant trajectory, we also observed a severe cellular imbalance, with cell composition markedly skewed toward GlyT following SCNT (Fig. 3C; Supplementary Fig. 2I), a potential consequence of this lineage-commitment disorder. Concordantly, we also observed significant reductions in the expression of the JZ markers Tpbpa and Flt1 (encoding VEGFR1), both of which were most highly expressed in SpT cells, in SCNT placentas53,54; these reductions were further confirmed by immunohistochemical staining (Fig. 3D, E, Supplementary Figs. 2J, 4F, G and Supplementary Data 6). To probe the underlying cause of the differentiation defects, we identified 133 SCNT-specific trajectory genes temporally regulating junctional zone development (Fig. 3F and Supplementary Data 7). Gene ontology (GO) analysis of these genes revealed enrichment in cell cycle regulation and DNA damage repair, providing potential molecular evidence that mitotic dysregulation underlies the abnormal developmental trajectory in cloned placentas (Fig. 3F, G). Together, these findings revealed potential lineage commitment disorders in SCNT placentas, which are likely linked to cellular stress55.

GlyT cells are glycogen-rich trophoblasts specialized for storing and metabolizing glycogen. Previous studies have shown that their numbers increase from E12.5 to E16.5 but decline markedly by E18.5, a process thought to enable the utilization of stored glycogen to meet the maximal energy demands of fetal development52,56. This developmental pattern was recapitulated in WT placentas in our study. However, SCNT placentas exhibited sustained GlyT accumulation until E18.5 (Fig. 3C), indicating impaired glycogen-to-glucose mobilization. Gene set variation analysis (GSVA) revealed elevated glycogen synthesis activity in GlyT cells from SCNT placentas (Fig. 3H). Concurrently, we observed an increasing trend in the expression of key biosynthetic enzymes (Gbe1, Ugp2, Hk1, and Pgm1) in SCNT GlyT cells, with Gbe1 showing statistical significance57 (Fig. 3I, Supplementary Fig. 2K and Supplementary Data 6). In contrast, the expression of the glucose transporter Slc2a1 was downregulated58 (Supplementary Figs. 2K, 4H and Supplementary Data 6). The combined effect of excessive glycogen synthesis and impaired glucose transport resulted in a profound metabolic bottleneck that limited efficient glycogen utilization. Periodic acid–Schiff (PAS) staining further confirmed massive glycogen overaccumulation in SCNT placentas (Fig. 3J), suggesting a disruption in energy supply homeostasis.

In parallel, we assessed endocrine function in the junctional zone. Placental lactogens/prolactin (PL/PRL) family hormones, such as Prl3b1 and Prl8a9, are predominantly secreted by SpT cells and play essential roles in maintaining maternal physiological adaptations during pregnancy59. These genes were markedly downregulated, accompanied by reduced activity of the lactation signaling pathway (Fig. 3K, L, Supplementary Figs. 2L, 4I and Supplementary Data 6). This impaired hormonal expression was consistent with the loss of SpT and reflects disrupted endocrine output. In conclusion, SCNT placentas exhibit disrupted metabolic and endocrine functions in the junctional zone due to aberrant lineage allocation, underlying placental insufficiency and impaired fetal development.

Impaired angiogenesis compromises labyrinthine transport

The labyrinth is the principal site of maternal–fetal exchange, characterized by dense fetal vascular networks established during mid to late gestation, which are essential for supporting fetal growth60. Given the disrupted composition of trophoblast subpopulations observed in SCNT placentas, we next examined whether these alterations impair labyrinthine vascular development. Immunohistochemical staining for the endothelial cell markers CD31 (PECAM1) and CD34 showed a substantial reduction of vascular density in the labyrinth of SCNT placentas61 (Fig. 4A and Supplementary Fig. 5A). Consistently, cellular composition analysis revealed that endothelial cells and pericytes, two key cellular components of blood vessels, were reduced by 81.1% and 69.8%, respectively, in SCNT placentas at E18.5 (Fig. 4B, Supplementary Fig. 2M and Supplementary Data 4). This aligns with the disrupted vascular architecture observed in SCNT placentas and points to impaired labyrinthine angiogenesis. Molecular profiling further confirmed downregulation of key endothelial markers: Pecam1 (mediating cell adhesion and vascular integrity), Plvap (regulating vascular permeability), Eng (involved in TGF-β-mediated angiogenesis), and Flt1 (functioning in blood vessel morphogenesis)62,63 (Fig. 4C, Supplementary Fig. 2N and Supplementary Data 6). In addition to reduced abundance, endothelial cells and pericytes also exhibited impaired cellular maturation in SCNT placentas, mirroring trophoblast precursors. These cells accumulated in the S/G2M phase, showed elevated Mki67 expression, and downregulated the terminal differentiation marker Cdkn1c (Figs. 4C, D and Supplementary Fig. 5B). In contrast, non-SCNT maternal decidual cells did not display such abnormalities and served as negative controls (Fig. 4D). These results revealed dysregulated angiogenesis in SCNT placentas, which is likely caused by reduced endothelial cell numbers and impaired maturation.

In the labyrinth, the maternal blood sinusoids and fetal vasculature are separated by a three-layer structure (sequentially S-TGCs, SynTI, and SynTII from maternal to fetal side), with SynTII directly contacting endothelial cells12 (Fig. 4E). Since endothelial cells and pericytes were dysregulated in SCNT placentas, we focused on cell-cell interactions to identify upstream regulatory signals. Remarkably, the interaction strengths between endothelial cells or pericytes and S-TGC, SynTI, and SynTII were markedly reduced in SCNT placentas (Fig. 4F), with most signaling pathways being more active in WT placentas (Fig. 4G). Among these, vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) signaling64, a key regulator of angiogenesis, was specifically weakened in SynTII–endothelial communication, as evidenced by diminished activity of the corresponding ligand–receptor pairs (Fig. 4H, I and Supplementary Figs. 2O, P, 5C). In line with this, the expression of Flt1 in endothelial cells and Vegfa in SynTII was significantly downregulated (Fig. 4C, J, Supplementary Fig. 2N, Q and Supplementary Data 6), highlighting the VEGFA–VEGFR1 (FLT1) axis as a central mechanism underlying vascular malformations65. In addition, LAMININ signaling, a critical extracellular matrix (ECM) pathway involved in cell adhesion, migration, and morphogenesis66, was also significantly impaired in SCNT placentas, with most ligand–receptor interactions markedly reduced (Supplementary Fig. 5C–E). This disruption likely contributes to the loose labyrinthine architecture, scattered vasculature, and increased intercellular distances (Fig. 4A and Supplementary Fig. 5F), which further diminish effective cell communication (Fig. 4F). Intriguingly, the expression of hypoxia-inducible factor 1-alpha (Hif1a) was significantly increased in SCNT trophoblast cells, along with upregulation of hypoxia response activity. Meanwhile, we observed elevated glycolytic activity, which might serve as a compensatory adaptation to impaired vascular network and hypoxic microenvironment (Fig. 4K, L, Supplementary Fig. 5G, H and Supplementary Data 6).

Collectively, disrupted VEGF-mediated SynTII–endothelial crosstalk and defective endothelial maturation converge to impair angiogenesis, thereby compromising placental oxygen and nutrient exchange, which could in turn contribute to fetal growth restriction in SCNT conceptuses. Moreover, impaired placental circulation may amplify glucose deprivation caused by GlyT hyperaccumulation, reinforcing a self-perpetuating cycle of metabolic insufficiency.

DNA damage and H3K27me3 loss underpin placental dysfunction

We next sought to investigate the molecular basis underlying the disrupted trophoblast subtype composition, which led to defects in differentiation, metabolism, endocrine signaling, and vascular development. To identify molecular defects conserved across lineages, we identified DEGs of each trophoblast subtype between SCNT and WT placentas and retained 1865 DEGs that were upregulated in at least 50% of subtypes (≥ 7 out of 13) in SCNT placentas. GO analysis of these genes revealed significant enrichment for DNA damage- and DNA repair-related pathways (Fig. 5A), in line with the pathways implicated in abnormal developmental events described above (Figs. 2F, 3G). Concordantly, SCNT trophoblasts showed significantly elevated activity of response to DNA damage stimulus, particularly within precursor populations (Fig. 5B, C). Immunohistochemical staining of γH2AX, a well-established marker of DNA double-strand breaks67, confirmed widespread DNA damage in SCNT placentas, which was largely absent in WT controls (Fig. 5D). Intriguingly, this damage was not acquired de novo post-implantation, but instead accumulated during preimplantation development, as evidenced by a 3.5-fold increase in γH2AX foci in trophectoderm (TE) cells of SCNT blastocysts relative to WT, with TE identity confirmed by CDX2 immunostaining (Fig. 5E). Moreover, TE cells derived from cumulus cell (CC) nuclear donors exhibited significantly more DNA damage foci than those from embryonic stem cell (ESC) nuclear donors, underscoring the dependence of DNA damage on the genomic stability of the donor cell type24.

DNA damage sites often exhibit an open chromatin conformation to allow the recruitment of DNA repair machinery68. Consistently, we identified a set of significantly increased accessible regions in SCNT trophoblast precursor subtypes (Fig. 5F and Supplementary Fig. 6A, B), which were further merged as DNA damage-associated differentially accessible regions (DDARs) (Supplementary Data 8). Considering SCNT placentas undergo incomplete reprogramming18, we further compared the chromatin states at DDARs between nuclear donor cumulus cells and oocytes. The results showed that DDARs exhibited significantly higher chromatin accessibility and enrichment of the active mark H3K4me3 in cumulus cells than in oocytes (Fig. 5G, H), whereas no such differences were observed in negative control regions. Notably, the pattern of the repressive mark H3K27me3 at DDARs was opposite, with significantly lower signals in cumulus cells (Fig. 5I). Strikingly, H3K27me3 signals remained depleted at DDARs in SCNT extraembryonic ectoderm (ExE), the embryonic compartment that gives rise to the placenta, compared with WT ExE (Fig. 5J). In contrast, the other repressive mark, H3K9me3, did not show this pattern and was genome-wide lower in cumulus cells than in oocytes (Supplementary Fig. 6C).

To investigate whether H3K27me3 depletion at DDARs is specifically associated with loss of imprinting (LOI), we classified known imprinted genes into H3K27me3- and canonical DNA methylation-dependent18,20,22. Strikingly, DDARs-annotated upregulated genes selectively captured 83.3% (5/6) of the core H3K27me3-dependent imprinted genes (Gab1, Smoc1, Sfmbt2, Slc38a4, and Jade1), exhibiting concordant biallelic upregulation (Supplementary Fig. 6D–F and Supplementary Data 6). Conversely, overlap with canonical DNA methylation-dependent imprinted genes was negligible. This highlights targeted H3K27me3-mediated LOI as a key epigenetic feature underlying DDAR-associated placental dysfunction.

Consistent with the LOI of repressive H3K27me3 modifications, we confirmed increased chromatin accessibility at the Sfmbt2 promoter region in SCNT trophoblast precursors, tracing back to elevated accessibility and H3K4me3 in donor cumulus cells and persistent loss of H3K27me3 through ExE stages (Fig. 5K). To establish a causal link between H3K27me3 deficiency and DNA damage, we utilized EED226 (10 μM) to inhibit PRC2 activity, thereby mimicking H3K27me3 depletion in wild-type embryos. This treatment-induced H3K27me3 loss compromised the blastocyst formation rate (90.4% in WT vs. 67.9% in EED226-treated, Supplementary Fig. 6G and Supplementary Data 9), accompanied by abnormal lineage cell counts and a severe ICM/TE ratio imbalance (Supplementary Fig. 6H). Crucially, EED226-treated blastocysts recapitulated the elevated DNA damage signals characteristic of SCNT embryos, directly demonstrating that H3K27me3 is essential for maintaining genomic stability (Supplementary Fig. 6H). The resulting persistent genomic instability further compromised the implantation and development rates observed in post-implantation embryos (Supplementary Fig. 6I and Supplementary Data 9). Together, these results suggest that DNA damage in SCNT placentas is likely inherited from the donor-cell epigenetic barriers. Specifically, H3K27me3, rather than H3K9me3, serves as the dominant repressive mark that safeguards genome integrity at DDARs loci. Its absence leads to chromatin decompaction, rendering naked DNA vulnerable to reprogramming-induced stress and resulting in widespread damage.

Enhanced DNA damage repair ameliorates placental defects

Given the early onset of reprogramming-induced DNA damage in SCNT placentas, we sought to enhance DNA repair as a strategy to restore placental function. Building on our previous work showing that improved homologous recombination (HR) repair promotes SCNT embryo development25, we applied an optimized culture medium supplemented with farrerol (F-SCNT) to repair SCNT reprogramming-induced DNA damage. This treatment achieved a 39.2% reduction in DNA damage foci within trophectoderm cells (Supplementary Fig. 7A) and a widespread decrease in γH2AX signals in placentas (Fig. 6A). Morphologically, the defective SCNT placental architecture was also greatly restored, yielding a clearer demarcation between the junctional and labyrinthine zones without distortion of the boundary and more compact tissue organization (Fig. 6B and Supplementary Fig. 7B). In parallel, placental hyperplasia in SCNT was also significantly attenuated (Supplementary Fig. 7C). This demonstrated that farrerol treatment could improve SCNT placental development. To further elucidate the mechanism, we used etoposide to introduce DNA damage in wild-type embryos. As expected, etoposide-induced DNA damage recapitulated major SCNT-associated early developmental defects in vitro, including morula-to-blastocyst transition obstruction, NANOG+ and CDX2+ cell count reduction, TE/ICM ratio imbalance, and implantation failure, all of which were rescued by farrerol treatment (Supplementary Fig. 7D–G and Supplementary Data 9).

To delineate the molecular basis underlying the restoration of SCNT placental dysfunctions, we performed single-nucleus multi-omics sequencing of E12.5 farrerol-treated SCNT placentas, a stage at which trophoblast precursors remained abundant. The trophoblast cells were clustered into 13 subtypes as described above (Supplementary Fig. 7H). Comparative analysis with untreated E12.5 SCNT placentas confirmed that farrerol treatment markedly attenuated the cellular response to DNA damage, particularly in precursor populations (Fig. 6C and Supplementary Fig. 7I). Consistently, chromatin accessibility at DDARs was significantly reduced posttreatment, indicating effective mitigation of DNA damage (Fig. 6D). Since persistent DNA damage disrupts differentiation69,70, a defect also evident in SCNT trophoblasts, we next examined whether restored repair facilitated lineage progression. Intriguingly, the proportion of multipotent cells was substantially decreased, alongside significantly lower developmental potential scores, especially in precursor subtypes (Fig. 6E, F and Supplementary Fig. 7J, K). These findings indicated that the timely resolution of reprogramming-induced DNA damage promotes trophoblast lineage commitment.

Moreover, the aberrant differentiation of SpT precursors to GlyT cells did not occur in SCNT placentas following DNA repair (Figs. 6G and 3B). As a result, the proportion of SpT cells was restored from 14.9% to 25.8%, while GlyT cells decreased from 85.1% to 74.2% (Fig. 6H). Consistently, the expression of SpT-enriched proteins TPBPA and FLT1 was increased in the junctional zone, suggesting the restoration of the SpT lineage (Supplementary Fig. 7L–O and Supplementary Data 6). Functionally, GlyT cells displayed marked downregulation of glycogen synthesis gene Gbe1 (Supplementary Fig. 7P and Supplementary Data 6), and PAS staining confirmed less glycogen deposition, indicating a shift toward glycogen mobilization to fuel fetal growth (Fig. 6I). In parallel, SpT endocrine function was also restored, as reflected by the increased expression of prolactin family genes (Supplementary Fig. 7Q and Supplementary Data 6). Together, reprogramming-induced DNA damage triggered aberrant lineage conversion in SCNT placentas, which was rescued upon DNA repair, along with restoration of glycogen metabolism and endocrine homeostasis in the junctional zone.

In the labyrinth, enhanced DNA repair markedly improved placental function by strengthening SynTII–endothelial crosstalk (Fig. 6J). Molecular analysis further revealed upregulated VEGF signaling between SynTII and endothelial cells through VEGFA–VEGFR1 interactions (Fig. 6K, L, Supplementary Fig. 7O, R and Supplementary Data 6). Immunohistochemical staining confirmed vascular restoration, with increased expression and broader distribution of fetal vascular markers CD31 and CD34 (Fig. 6M and Supplementary Fig. 7S). Hypoxia response activity was also significantly reduced (Fig. 6N). These structural and molecular improvements collectively enhanced maternal–fetal exchange, alleviated placental hypoxia, and contributed to the restored developmental homeostasis.

Collectively, enhanced DNA damage repair greatly restored defective SCNT placental architecture, corrected the aberrant differentiation of SpT precursors into GlyT cells, and improved placental vasculature. Hence, placental functions at the fetal–maternal interface, including metabolic and endocrine functions and nutrient exchange, were greatly rescued. These validated reprogramming-induced DNA damage as a tractable driver of SCNT placental pathology.

Discussion

Our study uses single-nucleus multi-omics to systematically dissect the underlying mechanisms of placental abnormalities in SCNT placentas, focusing on cellular composition, lineage dynamics, cell cycle states, and chromatin accessibility. We found that impaired exit from the multipotent state, along with an elevated DNA damage response, leads to lineage disorganization in the junctional zone and failed vascularization in the labyrinth layer, resulting in a hyperplastic but inefficient phenotype (Fig. 7). Mechanistically, this phenotype stems from a failure of precursor cells to exit multipotency and initiate terminal differentiation, contributing to a global developmental delay in SCNT placentas. Such a developmental clock delay parallels potency stalling observed in 2-cell arrest caused by disrupted maternal-to-zygotic transition71,72 and Bend3-deficient embryos with persistent pluripotency and chromatin dysregulation in the extraembryonic lineage73, highlighting the potency-to-lineage commitment bottleneck as a measurable and targetable window in perturbed or stress-induced development.

Aberrant trophoblast differentiation and impaired vascular signaling underlie developmental defects in SCNT placentas.

A schematic model summarizing the abnormal phenotypes and molecular mechanisms of SCNT placentas compared with WT placentas. Specifically, it illustrates an impaired exit from the multipotent state, along with an elevated DNA damage response, leads to lineage disorganization (aberrant differentiation toward GlyT) in the junctional zone and failed vascularization (disruption of VEGFA–VEGFR1 signaling) in the labyrinth layer.

Interestingly, placenta hyperplasia in SCNT is phenotypically mirrored in human gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM)74,75, despite substantial biological differences between mouse and human placentation and the distinct pathogenic mechanisms (epigenetic inheritance failure in SCNT vs. metabolic environmental stress in GDM). Nonetheless, transcriptomic analysis revealed a striking biological convergence. Fundamentally distinct triggers, such as artificial reprogramming in SCNT and metabolic disorders in GDM, elicit similar compensatory phenotypes. Specifically, both conditions exhibit trophoblast overexpansion (Supplementary Fig. 8A–F). Consistent with this shared feature, in human GDM placentas, trophoblast subtypes, particularly villous cytotrophoblasts (VCT), which serve as progenitors of syncytiotrophoblasts (SCT) and extravillous trophoblasts (EVT)76, exhibit multipotency arrest (Supplementary Fig. 8G–J). Alongside these cellular stalling events, GDM placentas specifically display enriched transcriptomic signatures for vascular development, hypoxia response, and damage (Supplementary Fig. 8K). However, while these transcriptomic-based results are appealing, the extent to which our conclusions derived from SCNT models could be applied to human GDM and subsequent therapeutic development still warrants further studies.

From the perspective of lineage progression, multipotent precursors trapped in S/G2M not only fail conventional differentiation but also show an increased risk of deviating into alternative fates. In the junctional zone, despite experimental validations such as in vitro differentiation assays or lineage-tracing experiments being required, our computational models suggest a previously unrecognized putative aberrant trajectory where SpT precursors were diverted to GlyT cells under sustained checkpoint activation and replication stress, converging with the preserved normal junctional zone progenitor (JZP) to GlyT trajectory (Fig. 3A, B). We propose that this overflow likely contributes to GlyT overexpansion and SpT depletion, thereby disturbing intra-zone balance within the junctional zone. Such fate diversion reflects a stress-induced developmental detour, in which prolonged differentiation blockade compels precursor cells to deviate from their canonical trajectories and adopt an alternative path as a compensatory response, highlighting how checkpoint activation can unveil latent plasticity under perturbed conditions55. Consistent with such a perturbed environment, the SCNT placenta intrinsically exhibits a broader cellular stress landscape, where previous ultrastructural analyses have revealed pronounced dilatation of the rough endoplasmic reticulum (ER) in SpT cells77. Therefore, the aberrant developmental detour we observed in SpT precursors is likely driven by a synergistic and multi-faceted stress response that encompasses both this established ER stress and the cell cycle stress identified herein. This is supported by recent studies indicating that crosstalk between replication stress-induced cell cycle checkpoints and ER stress jointly coordinates cell fate under adverse conditions78,79. Rather than contradicting developmental directionality, this reflects latent developmental plasticity that becomes unmasked under stress and manifests as structural and functional dysregulation at the tissue level.

As a transient yet critical organ, the placenta depends on tightly coupled structural and functional coordination to maintain pregnancy homeostasis. The junctional zone manages endocrine signaling and glycogen storage, while the labyrinth supports material exchange and vascular development through coordinated interlayer signaling. Although we missed the JZP to SpT transition due to late sampling, which requires earlier time points to reconstruct the trajectory34, our data showed that SpT precursor to GlyT misdirection transforms the junctional zone into a metabolic sink, characterized by enhanced glycogen synthesis but impaired mobilization in SCNT placentas. Concurrently, the labyrinth faces a transport bottleneck marked by sparse vasculature, immature barriers, and widespread hypoxia, driven by weakened VEGFA–VEGFR1 signaling between SynTII and endothelial cells (Fig. 4). The membrane-bound isoform of FLT1 in endothelial cells promotes fetal vascularization65,80, and its downregulation impairs angiogenesis in the labyrinth. Notably, FLT1 also exists in a soluble form produced via alternative splicing, which functions to inhibit excessive maternal vascular invasion81. Its reduced expression in the junctional zone may lead to uncontrolled spiral artery remodeling and destabilize the maternal–fetal interface in SCNT placentas.

Although the placenta exhibits a degree of intrinsic tolerance or compensatory characteristics by expanding cell number and tissue volume to buffer reduced unit-level function, this strategy has clear limitations. The hyperplastic but inefficient SCNT placenta reflects the failure of this compensatory mechanism. Importantly, these defects do not result from genetic mutations but rather from early epigenetic dysregulation and genome instability. Incomplete restoration of the repressive histone mark H3K27me3 during reprogramming, compounded by replication stress, leads to the accumulation of DNA damage in open chromatin regions (Fig. 5). Crucially, our functional validation links this genomic instability to the inherited absence of H3K27me3 at DDARs in donor cumulus cells, extending our prior pre-implantation findings25 to reveal its long-term disruption of placental development. This damage persists throughout the extraembryonic differentiation stages, triggering a cascade of checkpoint activation and developmental delay. Similar patterns appear in human IVF embryos, where karyotypic abnormalities during rapid cleavage and mosaicism frequently localize to the trophectoderm82,83. Even with a normal fetal karyotype, placental abnormalities can lead to pregnancy failure or fetal growth restriction; yet clinical focus remains on fetal genetics and often overlooks the placenta as a pathology site. We propose that chromatin state and genome stability in placental development should be treated as a second axis of viability.

Accordingly, our study provides three key innovations at both methodological and mechanistic levels: the construction of a complete SCNT causal chain from chromatin state to cell cycle, lineage, and organ function in SCNT placentas; the identification of a stress-induced SpT precursor to GlyT overflow path; and the demonstration that HR repair enhancement can partially rescue lineage imbalance and vascular dysfunction, supporting the reversibility of the genome integrity–placental function axis (Fig. 7). These findings open several avenues for future exploration: (1) combining DNA repair enhancement with targeted histone modifications strategies to improve reprogramming and differentiation; (2) dissecting how maternal inflammation, hypoxia, and metabolism intersect with early epigenetic instability to shape placental compensatory thresholds; (3) investigating immune and trophoblast interactions at maternal–fetal interfaces such as the decidua and spiral arteries; and (4) translating key indicators (e.g., DNA damage load, H3K27me3 profiles, VEGF signaling) into real-time quality control tools for assisted reproduction and obstetric care. SCNT placentas thus serve not only as a platform to optimize cloning and assisted reproduction outcomes, but also as a powerful system for decoding and intervening in human placental pathologies.

Methods

Ethical statement

All animal experimental procedures were approved by the Biological Research Ethics Committee of Tongji University and conducted in strict accordance with the institutional Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. All mice were euthanized via cervical dislocation.

Mice

Specific pathogen-free (SPF) grade mice, including C57BL/6N, PWK/PhJ, DBA2, BDF1, BPF1, and ICR mice, were housed in the animal facility at Tongji University, Shanghai, China. Mice were housed in individually ventilated cages at an ambient temperature of 23–27 °C and humidity of 30 to 45% with a 12 h light and 12 h dark light cycle. The BDF1 hybrid mice were obtained from mating female 6-week-old C57BL/6N mice with male 6-week-old DBA2 mice. The BPF1 hybrid mice were obtained from mating 6-week-old female C57BL/6N mice with male 6-week-old PWK/PhJ mice. For embryo transfer, 7-week-old female ICR mice were served as recipients.

SCNT and embryos transfer

In the SCNT experiments performed here, BDF1 (female C57BL/6N × male DBA2) female mice were used as oocyte donors, cumulus cells from BPF1 (female C57BL/6N × male PWK/PhJ) female mice and mESCs of the same background were used as nuclear donors. BDF1 mice were superovulated by injecting 6 IU of pregnant mare serum gonadotropin (PMSG) (San-Sheng Pharmaceutical, S160106), followed 48 h later by injecting 6 IU of human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) (Sansheng Pharmaceutical, B151104). 14 h after hCG injection, MII oocytes were collected from the fallopian tubes and vesicated in Chatot-Ziomek-Bavister (CZB) medium containing 5 µg/mL cytochalasin B (CB). Enucleation was performed by a Piezo-driven pipette (Prime Tech) with an Olympus inverted microscope. For nuclear transfer, cumulus cells were injected into enucleated oocytes. The reconstructed oocytes were then incubated in CZB medium for 1 h before activation. Activation was performed by incubation in Ca2+-free CZB medium containing 1 mM SrCl2 and 5 µg/mL CB for 6 h, and then the oocytes were thoroughly washed and cultured in G-1 PLUS medium (Vitrolife, 10128) at 37 °C under 5% CO219. Small molecule treatments were performed as with 50 nM farrerol (Sigma-Aldrich, SML1389) for 16 h, starting after activation25.

To obtain naturally fertilized (NF) zygote embryos, the super ovulated C57BL/6N female mice were mated with PWK/PhJ male mice. Then, the zygotes were collected from the oviducts at 20 h after hCG injection and were cultured in G-1 PLUS medium. For in vitro fertilization (IVF), sperm was collected from the cauda epididymis of adult PWK/PhJ male mice and incubated in G-IVF PLUS medium (Vitrolife, 10136) for 30 min at 37 °C under 5% CO2 to allow sperm swim-up. The zona pellucida of each C57BL/6N MII oocyte was punched to make a small hole using a Piezo-drill micromanipulator. Oocytes were then placed in the prepared sperm suspension in G-IVF PLUS medium. At 4 hours post-fertilization (h.p.f), these embryos were washed and cultured in G-IVF PLUS medium. Fertilized oocytes that had developed to the blastocyst stage were collected for immunofluorescence staining. NF and IVF were designated as wild type (WT). To induce DNA damage in NF embryos, 8-cell embryos were exposed to 0.5 μM Etoposide (Sigma-Aldrich, E1383) for 3 h. For rescue experiments, 50 nM farrerol was added to the culture medium subsequent to Etoposide treatment and maintained until the blastocyst. In addition, to inhibit H3K27me3, 10 μM EED226 (Selleck Chemicals, S8496) was supplemented in the culture medium from the zygote through to the blastocyst.

Placental developmental data for WT, SCNT, and farrerol-treated groups are provided in Supplementary Data 1.

Placenta collection

The WT and SCNT embryos were transplanted into the uterus of pseudopregnant recipients at the stage of 2-cell development, and the placental samples were collected at the appropriate day. Pregnant ICR mice at E12.5, E14.5 and E18.5 were humanely sacrificed, and their placentas were dissected. Placentas were washed with PBS to remove blood and blotted dry with absorbent paper. The treated placentas were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored to a liquid nitrogen tank. Samples to be sent are placed on dry ice for transportation. Placental tissue was thoroughly washed in ice-cold PBS to eliminate blood contaminants and precisely weighed. The tissue was then carefully minced into ~ 1 mm³ fragments using sterile scissors before enzymatic digestion in pre-warmed DMEM/F12 medium (Gibco, 11320082) containing 0.5 mg/mL collagenase IV (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 17104019) and 0.2 mg/mL DNase I (Roche, 10104159001). The digestion was performed at 37 °C for 20 min in a shaking water bath with gentle manual inversion every 10 min.

Histological characterization and Quantitative analysis

Tissue samples were fixed with fixing solution (Servicebio, G1101) at room temperature for 6 h. Subsequently, the samples were embedded in paraffin and sliced into 5μm sections. Paraffin sections were used for hematoxylin-eosin (H&E), immunohistochemistry (IHC) and periodic acid-schiff (PAS) staining according to the relevant kit instructions (Servicebio). After staining, cover slips were sealed and photographed using a scanning machine, and images were viewed and produced using case viewer software. For IHC, γH2AX (Cell Signaling Technology, 9718S), CD31 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, PA5-16301), CD34 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA1-10202), VEGFR1 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 361100), and TPBPA (Abcam, ab320823) were performed. To better visualize and compare the spatial distribution and relative optical density of the DAB signal in IHC images, we employed a gradient map visualization technique using Caseviewer software (3DHISTECH Ltd.). In this digital pseudocolor transformation, the intensity of the brown DAB (3,3’-diaminobenzidine) signal was quantitatively converted into a color-coded heatmap. Within these maps, warmer colors represent areas of high staining intensity, intermediate colors represent moderate intensity, and cooler colors indicate low intensity.

Histological assessment was conducted via SaiViewer software (Servicebio). Key parameters, including total area, positive cell counts, the percentage of positive cells, and mean staining intensity, were statistically evaluated across three biological replicates.

In vitro 3D culture of mouse embryos

E3.5 mouse blastocysts were used to in vitro 3D culture. Firstly, blastocysts were incubated with 0.5% Pronase E (Sigma-Aldrich, P8811) to remove the zona pellucida. After washing, individual blastocysts were transferred into 50 μl droplets of IVC1 medium in a 35 mm petri dish (Corning, 351007), which was subsequently covered with mineral oil (Sigma-Aldrich, M8410). To establish a 3D environment for post-implantation development, 25% Matrigel (v/v; Coring, 356234) was microinjected into the pre-warmed IVC1 droplets using a glass capillary to embed the embryos. The dishes were then incubated at 37 °C under 5% CO2 for 2 days. By the end of this period (approximately E5.0–E5.25), embryos typically exhibited 3D rosette or early lumen structures. The IVC1 medium was then aspirated and replaced with equilibrated IVC2 medium for an additional 2 days of culture. By day 4 of in vitro culture, embryos reached the early egg cylinder stage (E5.5), at which point they were harvested for immunofluorescence staining84.

The IVC1 medium consisted of CMRL 1066 basic medium (Gibco, 11530037) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Millipore, ES-009B), 1 mM sodium pyruvate (Invitrogen, 11360070), 1:100 diluted N2 supplement (Invitrogen, 17502048), 1:200 diluted B27 supplement (Invitrogen, 17504044), and 100 units mL-1 penicillin-streptomycin (Invitrogen, 15140122). The IVC2 medium shared the same formulation as IVC1, except that the FBS concentration was increased to 20%.

Immunofluorescence staining

For the immunofluorescence assay, embryos were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Servicebio, G1101) for 20 min, washed twice with PBS, and permeabilized using 0.2% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich, X100) in PBS for 10 min at room temperature. Embryos were washed three times with cold PBS and blocked with 2.5% BSA for 1 h at room temperature. Embryos were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies diluted in PBS containing 2.5% BSA (MP Biomedicals, 219989680). After washing, embryos were incubated with appropriate fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. After counterstaining nuclei with DAPI (Beyotime Biotechnology, C1002), embryos were mounted for imaging. Confocal images were acquired using either a Zeiss LSM 880 or an Olympus FV4000 laser-scanning confocal microscope.

The primary antibodies used included: γH2AX (Cell Signaling Technology, 9718S), CDX2 (BioGenex, MU392A-5UC), NANOG (Cell Signaling Technology, 4903S), NANOG (R&D Systems, AF1997-SP), H3K27me3 (Diagenode, C15410195), OCT4 (Abcam, ab181557), TFAP2C (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-12762), GATA6 (R&D Systems, AF1700-SP). Secondary antibodies were used as follows: Donkey anti-Mouse 488 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A32766), Donkey anti-Rabbit 488 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A21206), Donkey anti-Mouse 594 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A21203), Donkey anti-Rabbit 594 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A32754), Donkey anti-Goat 647 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A21447).

ULI-NChIP-seq library construction and sequencing

The extraembryonic ectoderm (ExE) was precisely isolated using forceps under 40 × magnification on a stereomicroscope. Isolated ExE fragments were incubated in pre-warmed Ca2+ -free CZB medium at 37 °C/5% CO2 for 20 minutes, followed by gentle mechanical dissociation into single cells using a pipette with a diameter of 40–60 μm. For ultra-low-input native chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ULI-NChIP-seq), 500 trophoblast cells were used per immunoprecipitation reaction, with two independent biological replicates performed. Prior to chromatin preparation, isolated cells were washed three times in ice-cold PBS containing 0.5% bovine serum albumin (BSA-PBS) to minimize sample contamination. Chromatin immunoprecipitation was using 1 μg anti-H3K27me3 (Diagenode, C15410195) antibodies per reaction85. Sequencing libraries were constructed using the KAPA Hyper Prep Kit (Roche, KK8504) with PCR amplification, following the manufacturer’s instructions. Paired-end 150-bp sequencing was performed on Illumina NovaSeq 6000 at Nanjing Jiangbei New Area Biopharmaceutical Public Service Platform Co., Ltd.

Nuclei preparation for single-nucleus multiome sequencing

For scMultiome-seq, cell nuclei were isolated following the 10X Genomics Demonstrated Protocol for Single Cell Multiome Sequencing. Briefly, the cell suspension was washed once in PBS/0.04% BSA, and 200,000 cells were pelleted (300 × g for 5 min), resuspended in 100 µl of freshly prepared lysis buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.4, 10 mM NaCl, 3 mM MgCl2, 0.1% Tween-20 (Sigma-Aldrich, P9416), 0.1% NP-40 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 28324), 0.01% Digitonin (Invitrogen, BN2006), and 1% BSA). After washing lysates, the nuclei were pelleted and resuspended in 30 µl of 1 × Nuclei Buffer (10X Genomics). Successful preparation of intact, isolated nuclei was confirmed through visual inspection in a phase-contrast microscopy, and nuclei concentration was assessed with a Luna FX7 Automated Cell Counter (Logos Biosystems) before subsequently proceeding with RNA-seq and ATAC-seq.

10X single-nucleus multiome library preparation, sequencing and analysis

Multiome (RNA + ATAC) sequencing from the same single nuclei was performed on the 10 x Genomics Chromium Next GEM platform using fresh-frozen tissue-derived nuclei (> 10,000 viable nuclei) co-encapsulated with hydrogel beads to simultaneously capture mRNA via oligo-dT primers and chromatin accessibility through Tn5 transposase-mediated tagmentation. For the ATAC library, transposed DNA fragments flanked by P5 and P7 adapters were sequenced using Read 1 N and Read 2 N, along with the i7 index for library demultiplexing and the i5 index for 10x barcode retrieval. The gene expression (GEX) library contained P5 and P7-anchored cDNA inserts, where TruSeq Read 1 sequenced the 16 bp 10X barcode and 12 bp unique molecular identifier (UMI), while TruSeq Read 2 sequenced the cDNA insert, with dual 10 bp sample indexes via i5 and i7 for GEX library demultiplexing. Paired-end sequencing was performed using an Illumina NovaSeq XPlus platform for GEX and ATAC. All procedures followed the manufacturer’s standard protocols. To ensure consistency with SNPsplit’s reference genome, we constructed mm10 PWK_PhJ N-masked genome reference index using the CellRanger-ARC (v2.0.2) mkref command. Raw sequencing data were aligned to the PWK_PhJ N-masked reference genome, followed by barcode assignment and UMI quantification via the CellRanger-ARC count command.

snRNA-seq analysis

Single-nucleus RNA sequencing data were processed using the Seurat (v5.1.0) R package86. A gene-cell count matrix was imported to create a Seurat object, with raw counts stored in the RNA assay. Six samples were merged into a unified Seurat object for joint analysis. Quality control was performed by calculating mitochondrial gene percentages by the PercentageFeatureSet(object, pattern = ‘^mt-’) function and retaining cells with mitochondrial counts < 5%, the number of unique genes detected per cell (nFeature_RNA) > 200, and total UMIs (nCount_RNA) between 1000 and 25,000 to exclude low-quality cells. DoubletFinder (v2.0.4)87 was applied per sample to mitigate library preparation biases and remove potential doublets. Gene expression counts were normalized via the LogNormalize method of the NormalizeData function, followed by identification of the top 2000 highly variable genes using the FindVariableFeatures function. Data scaling centered gene expression to a mean of 0 and a variance of 1 using the ScaleData function. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on scaled data using the RunPCA function, and batch effects between samples were corrected using the Harmony (v1.2.0)88 on PCA embeddings, generating a harmonized reduction (“harmony_rna”). Nonlinear dimensionality reduction was achieved via Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) using the RunUMAP function for visualization. After the independent pre-processing of snRNA and snATAC-seq data, the approach to integrate two modalities is referred to as ‘Multimodal Analysis’ later on. Cell clusters were annotated based on snRNA-seq data using two approaches: initial classification with SingleR (v2.4.1)89 against a reference transcriptome dataset34 (https://figshare.com/projects/Single_nuclei_RNA-seq_of_mouse_placental_labyrinth_development/92354), followed by manual refinement using the DotPlot and FeaturePlot visualizations of canonical markers and top 20 cluster-specific differentially expressed genes (DEGs) identified by the FindAllMarkers(only.pos = TRUE, min.pct = 0.25, logfc.threshold = 0.25) function.

For WT vs. SCNT comparisons, DEGs were identified by the FindMarkers (min.pct = 0.25, logfc.threshold = 0.25) function and filtered at FDR < 0.05. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of DEGs was performed using the enrichGO function in the clusterProfiler package (v4.10.1)90. The analysis focused on Biological Process (BP) categories. P-values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) method, and the significance cutoff q-value was set to 0.05. Data from the three developmental stages (E12.5, E14.5, and E18.5) were pooled to conduct SCNT vs. WT comparisons within each cell type, including visualizations (e.g., violin-box plots and boxplots), DEG identification, and GO enrichment analyses. Cell cycle phases (G1, G2M and S) were assigned to each cell using the CellCycleScoring function based on G2M and S-phase markers provided by Seurat, followed by sub-classification of G0 (exit cell cycle) from original G1 cells via signature scoring using the AddModuleScore function for G0 (Cdkn1a, Cdkn1b, Cdkn2a, Cdkn2b)91,92 and G1 (Mki67, Ccnd1, Ccnd2, Ccnd3, Cdk4, Cdk6) markers93,94.

snATAC-seq analysis

Single-nucleus ATAC-seq data were processed using Seurat (v5.1.0)86 and Signac (v1.12.0)95. The cell-peak count matrix was stored in the ‘ATAC’ assay of a Seurat object. Genome annotations were built using BSgenome.Mmusculus.UCSC.mm10 and EnsDb.Mmusculus.v79 R packages, and peaks overlapping non-standard chromosomes or genomic blacklist regions were excluded. The nucleosomeSignal function was used to calculate the nucleosome signal that defined as the ratio of fragments between mononucleosome (147-294 bp) and nucleosome-free (< 147 bp) fragments. Transcriptional start site (TSS) enrichment score was calculated by the TSSEnrichment function, which defined as the ATAC-seq targeting score based on the ratio of fragments centered at the TSS to fragments in TSS-flanking regions. In order to keep high-quality cells, cells with 2000 <nCount_ATAC < 100000, nucleosome_signal score < 2 and TSS.enrichment score > 1 were retained for the following analysis. Term frequency-inverse document frequency (TF-IDF) normalization using the RunTFIDF function was applied to adjust for sequencing depth and give higher values to rare peaks. Singular value decomposition (SVD) was performed on the TF-IDF matrix using features selected by the FindTopFeatures(min.cutoff = ‘q0’) function, generating latent semantic indexing (LSI) embeddings using the RunSVD function. The first LSI component, which exhibited a strong correlation with sequencing depth as quantified by the DepthCor function, was excluded to minimize technical bias. Batch effects were corrected using the Harmony (v1.2.0)88 on LSI embeddings, producing a harmonized reduction (‘harmony_atac’). UMAP visualization using the RunUMAP function was applied to the integrated dataset. To obtain a more accurate set of peaks, cell type-specific peaks were re-identified using MACS2 with the CallPeaks(group.by = ’celltype_label’) function and stored in a new ‘peaks’ assay. Differentially accessible peaks (DARs) between WT and SCNT were identified via logistic regression using the function FindMarkers(only.pos = TRUE, test.use = ‘LR’, latent.vars = ‘nCount_peaks’). Final DARs were further filtered based on thresholds: p-value < 0.05, |log2FC| > 0.25, and fractional detection > 0.1 in at least one group.

Multimodal analysis

Given the simultaneous measurement of RNA and ATAC data within the same nuclei, we employed the Weighted Nearest Neighbor (WNN) algorithm to integrate these multimodal datasets and to determine cell state36. The WNN workflow requires independent preprocessing and dimensional reduction of RNA and ATAC assays. We constructed a WNN graph to identify closest multimodal cell neighbors using a weighted combination of RNA and ATAC modalities, where cell-specific modality weights were computed via the FindMultiModalNeighbors(reduction.list = list(“harmony_rna”, “harmony_atac”), dims.list = list(1:50, 2:50)) function in Seurat. UMAP visualization was generated using the RunUMAP(nn.name = “weighted.nn”, reduction.name = “wnn.umap”) function based on a weighted combination of multi-modalities. Cell clustering was performed based on the WNN graph with the FindClusters(graph.name = ‘wsnn’, algorithm = 3) function at a range of resolutions from 0.2 to 1. Downstream analyses utilized the WNN-derived UMAP due to its clearest separation of cell states. Gene activity scores were predicted from ATAC data using the GeneActivity function, followed by anchor identification and cell type label transfer from RNA to ATAC assays using the FindTransferAnchors and TransferData functions, respectively. Finally, RNA-ATAC correlation was assessed based on the concordance of transferred cell type labels.

Differential abundance analysis of cell types

To comprehensively evaluate the dynamic changes in cell type compositions between SCNT and WT placentas across different embryonic stages (E12.5, E14.5, and E18.5), we employed three complementary statistical approaches at both the main cell type and trophoblast subtype levels.

We utilized the scProportionTest (v0.0.0.9) R package to perform permutation testing on the single-nucleus proportion data40. For each individual embryonic stage and the integrated dataset, we calculated the log2 fold difference (log2FD) in relative cell proportions between SCNT and WT samples. To prevent infinite values arising from cell types with zero counts in either condition, an adjusted log2FD was computed by adding a pseudocount of 0.001 to the cell fractions of both groups. Statistical significance was determined by generating 95% bootstrap confidence intervals utilizing 10,000 permutations. Cell types with a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 were considered to have a significantly altered proportion.

To assess the specific condition preference of each cell type, we calculated the ratio of observed to expected cell numbers (Ro/e) based on the Chi-square test41,42. The observed cell counts were divided by the expected cell counts under the assumption of random distribution. Ro/e ratio > 1 indicates an enrichment or preference of a given cell type in a specific condition, whereas Ro/e ratio < 1 indicates relative depletion.

To robustly account for inter-sample variability and the compositional nature of single-nucleus data, wherein an increase in one cell type intrinsically forces a decrease in the relative frequencies of others, we applied the scCODA (v0.1.9) Python framework to the E14.5 dataset containing biological replicates43. The count data was modeled using a Bayesian Dirichlet-Multinomial model with the formula set to the biological condition (SCNT vs. WT). Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) inference was performed using Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC) sampling with 20,000 total iterations, a burn-in of 5000, 10 leapfrog steps, and a step size of 0.01. To ensure robust identification of perturbed populations and prevent bias from a single reference, we iteratively performed the compositional analysis using every cell type as the reference. Statistically credible changes in cell populations were determined based on the posterior inclusion probability, with the expected false discovery rate (FDR) controlled at 0.4.

Split allelic genome

Split allelic genome was done by SNPsplit (v0.6.0)96. SNP annotation files were downloaded from the Mouse Genomes Project (https://www.mousegenomes.org/), and the mouse reference genome was downloaded from Ensembl (https://ftp.ensembl.org/pub/release-98/fasta/mus_musculus/). The SNPsplit_genome_preparation command was performed to generate the PWK_PhJ N-masked reference genome, where PWK_PhJ strain SNPs were masked by the ambiguity nucleobase N. Only chromosomes 1-19, X, Y and M were retained in the N-masked genome. Hisat2 and bowtie2 index were created based on the custom PWK_PhJ N-masked reference genome. To assign parent-of-origin genomes for individual cells based on SNPs, we first extracted a BAM file of filtered cell barcodes from the CellRanger-ARC output files (atac_possorted_bam.bam and gex_possorted_bam.bam) using Sinto (v0.10.0) (https://github.com/timoast/sinto). The RNA and ATAC BAM files were converted to FASTQ format using samtools (v1.18)97, followed by alignment to the PWK_PhJ N-masked reference genome with Hisat2 (v2.2.1)98 for RNA sequences and Bowtie2 (v2.5.3)99 for ATAC sequences, respectively. The generated SAM files were subsequently demultiplexed into per-cell SAM files using single-nucleus barcodes, which were then compressed into BAM format for downstream analysis. Next, we run the SNPsplit command to split the maternal and paternal allele genome on the resulting BAM files of each single cell. Finally, the allele ratio of each cell was referred to the ratio of reads mapped to each parental genome and the PWK ratio was defined as the paternal allele ratio.

Pseudotime analysis

Single-nucleus developmental trajectories were reconstructed using complementary approaches implemented in Monocle2 (v2.30.0) and Monocle3 (v1.3.4) R packages100,101. For Monocle2, raw count matrix from the Seurat object was formatted into a CellDataSet object with phenotypic metadata and gene annotations. After estimating size factors and dispersions, genes expressed in ≥ 10 cells were retained. To prioritize trajectory-informative features, differentially expressed genes (q-value < 0.01) across annotated cell types were identified using the differentialGeneTest function, and the top 2000 ranked genes were selected as ordering features. DDRTree dimensionality reduction was applied to embed cells into a low-dimensional manifold, followed by pseudotemporal ordering to assign cells a pseudotime value based on geodesic distance from the trajectory root. For Monocle3, processed RNA counts and metadata were similarly converted into a CellDataSet object. Preprocessing included normalization, PCA dimensionality reduction (50 PCs), and UMAP embeddings using preprocessed coordinates from Seurat’s WNN integration to ensure alignment with multimodal clustering results. Cells were partitioned into transcriptional neighborhoods via graph-based clustering. The trajectory graph was learned using the learn_graph function, which models cell-state transitions based on ‘wnn.umap’ embeddings. Pseudotime ordering was initiated by manually specifying root nodes corresponding to progenitor cell populations (LaTP and JZP1), and pseudotime values were assigned based on geodesic distances from root nodes using the order_cells function. Both frameworks were rigorously validated to ensure consistency in branch topology and pseudotime trajectory.

RNA velocity analysis

RNA velocity analysis was performed to infer transcriptional dynamics using the velocyto (v0.17.17)102 command and scvelo (v0.3.3) Python package103. Raw spliced and unspliced mRNA counts were generated by velocyto from aligned BAM files, producing a loom file. Cell embeddings, cluster annotations, and cell type labels from the Seurat object were imported to ensure consistent cellular metadata. Preprocessing in scVelo included filtering genes detected in fewer than 20 cells and retaining the top 2000 highly variable genes using the scv.pp.filter_and_normalize(min_shared_counts = 30, n_top_genes = 2000) function. Data were normalized and log-transformed, followed by moment calculation using the scv.pp.moments (n_pcs = 30, n_neighbors = 30) function to estimate first- and second-order moments for stochastic modeling. RNA velocity vectors were computed using the “stochastic” mode using the scv.tl.velocity function, which accounts for transcriptional bursting and splicing kinetics. Velocity coherence was validated by embedding the vectors into the WNN UMAP and visualizing streamlines using scv.pl.velocity_embedding and scv.pl.velocity_embedding_stream functions respectively to assess directional consistency with cell state transitions. Proportions of spliced/unspliced transcripts using the scv.pl.proportions function were quantified to evaluate technical biases. All analyses utilized default parameters unless specified, with velocity-inferred trajectories cross-referenced against pseudotime and cell type hierarchies to ensure biological interpretability.

Cell–cell communication analysis

The CellChat2 (v2.1.2)104 R package was implemented to systematically infer cell-cell communication networks from snRNA-seq data. The mouse-specific ligand-receptor interaction database (CellChatDB.mouse) was employed as the reference database. Preprocessing included identification of overexpressed genes and interaction pairs, followed by expression value correction via projection onto a protein-protein interaction network (PPI.mouse). Cell-cell communication probabilities were computed at ligand-receptor pairs and signaling pathway-centric networks levels using the computeCommunProb(type = “triMean”, raw.use = TRUE, population.size = FALSE) and computeCommunProbPathway function, respectively. Interactions with fewer than 10 cells in any group were filtered to ensure robustness. Aggregated communication networks and centrality metrics were generated to quantify interaction strength and signaling activity. Comparative analyses between WT and SCNT were performed by merging CellChat objects and evaluating differential interaction counts and weights using the compareInteractions function. The netVisual_diffInteraction and netVisual_heatmap functions visualized the differential number and strength of cell-cell interaction networks from the target cell cluster to other clusters. The rankNet function compared the overall information flow of each signaling pathway. The netVisual_bubble function charted upregulated and down-regulated signaling ligand-receptor pairs between two interacted clusters. All computations were parallelized (future::plan(“multicore”)) to optimize runtime efficiency.

CytoTRACE2

CytoTRACE2 (v1.0.0)46 R package was conducted to infer cellular differentiation potential based on single-nucleus transcriptional profiles. Raw count matrices from the Seurat objects were processed through the cytotrace2(object, is_seurat = TRUE, slot_type = “counts”, species = ‘mouse’) function. CytoTRACE2 generates three algorithmically derived metrics: Potency score (CytoTRACE2_Score), a continuous value ranging from 0 (differentiated) to 1 (stem cell state) representing absolute developmental potential of each cell; Relative order (CytoTRACE2_Relative), a relative ordering of cells by developmental potential; Potency category (CytoTRACE2_Potency), a categorical framework stratifying cells hierarchically based on potency score—from multilineage totipotent and pluripotent cells, to lineage-restricted oligopotent, multipotent and unipotent cells and finally differentiated cells. Informative visualizations based on prior cell type annotations and the three predicted values were generated using the plotData function. To maintain spatial consistency with prior analyses, UMAP embeddings generated by CytoTRACE2 were aligned to precomputed ‘wnn.umap’ coordinates from the Seurat analysis by directly overwriting UMAP coordinates in plot data. Potency score distributions across phenotypes and treatments were compared using boxplots and violin-box plots, with statistical significance evaluated via unpaired two-tailed Wilcoxon rank-sum tests.

Gene set analysis

Signature scores for the functional pathways GOBP_lactation, GOBP_cellular_response_to_hypoxia, REACTOME_glycolysis, and GOBP_cellular_response_to_DNA_damage_stimulus were calculated using AUCell (v1.24.0)105 for each cell. Gene rankings were generated from normalized data via the AUCell_buildRankings function. Area Under the Curve (AUC) scores were subsequently calculated using AUCell_calcAUC, setting the aucMaxRank parameter to the top 10% of detected genes in the dataset. The AUC scores indicate gene set activity per cell.

Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) (v1.50.5)106 was performed using the REACTOME_glycogen_synthesis gene set. All gene sets were obtained from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB v2023.1)107.

Human GDM placenta scRNA-seq data analysis

Single-cell RNA sequencing data from human placenta tissue (GSE173193)108 were analyzed using the Seurat R package (v5.1.0)86. Briefly, raw count matrices were imported, retaining genes expressed in at least three cells and cells with a minimum of 200 unique features. Initial quality control filtered out low-quality cells or doublets by keeping only those with unique feature counts between 200 and 6,000 and a mitochondrial transcript percentage < 10%. The post-QC dataset was normalized via log transformation with (scale factor = 10,000), and the top 2000 highly variable features were selected for the following linear data scaling and PCA analysis. The Harmony algorithm was then executed on the top 30 principal components to correct batch effects. Subsequently, unsupervised clustering (resolution = 0.5) was then performed on the shared nearest neighbor graph constructed from the first 30 Harmony dimensions, following which clusters were annotated using canonical markers.

To characterize shifts in placental cellular composition between the control and GDM states, the scProportionTest and Ro/e analyses were performed using the identical parameters mentioned above. The differentiation state and developmental potency of the core trophoblast lineages were modeled using the CytoTRACE2 framework (see technical details in the corresponding section).

Pathological transcriptional alterations within the trophoblast compartment were evaluated through differential expression analysis. Differentially expressed genes between the GDM and control states were identified using a Wilcoxon rank-sum test via the FindMarkers function, applying a threshold of an absolute log-fold change > 0.25 and presence in at least 25% of cells in either group. P-values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate correction with a significance threshold of 0.05. Upregulated genes in GDM trophoblasts were subsequently subjected to GOBP enrichment analysis using the clusterProfiler package with the org.Hs.eg.db database as reference.

ULI-NChIP-seq analysis

Adapters and low-quality sequences were trimmed using trim_galore (v0.6.10) (https://github.com/FelixKrueger/TrimGalore) and then mapped to mm10 using Bowtie2 (v2.5.3)99 with parameters: --no-mixed --no-discordant --no-unal. Mapped reads with high confidence were further sorted, indexed and transferred to bam format by samtools (v1.18)97. Duplicates were removed using sambamba (v1.0.1)109. In addition, histone modification signal levels were calculated with a 50-bp window and normalized to uniquely mapped fragments using bamCompare from deeptools (v3.5.4)110.

Statistics and reproducibility

All experimental results were successfully reproduced across multiple independent experiments. For comparisons, potential confounding variables, particularly mouse strain and age, were strictly controlled. Biological samples were harvested from at least three mice per group to minimize individual variance. Sample sizes were determined based on established standards in the field, and no statistical methods were used to predetermine sample sizes. No data were excluded from the analyses. No randomization or blinding was applied during the experiments or outcome assessments. Detailed statistical parameters, including specific tests, exact sample sizes, replicates, and error bar definitions, are specified in the respective figure legends and the Source Data file. Statistical analyses and graphing were performed using GraphPad Prism (v8.4.3) for all experimental datasets.

The single-nucleus multi-omics and ExE H3K27me3 ULI-NChIP-seq data generated in this study have been deposited in the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database under accession number GSE305963. Several publicly available datasets were used for comparative analysis. The scRNA-seq data for human gestational diabetes mellitus placentas was obtained from GSE173193108 [https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE173193]. The DNase-seq data for cumulus cells and GV oocytes were obtained from GSE110851111 [https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE110851] and GSE9260522 [https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE92605], respectively. The ATAC-seq data for GV and MII oocytes are available under GSE116854112 [https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE116854]. The ChIP-seq data for histone modifications in cumulus cells were retrieved from GSE262040113 [https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE262040] (H3K27me3 and H3K4me3) and GSE195762114 [https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE195762] (H3K9me3). The corresponding MII oocytes ChIP-seq data were obtained from GSE7395285 [https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE73952] (H3K27me3 and H3K4me3) and GSE97778115 [https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE97778] (H3K9me3). Source data are provided in this paper.
