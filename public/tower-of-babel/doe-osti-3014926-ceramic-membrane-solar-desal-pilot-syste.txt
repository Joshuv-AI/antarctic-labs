 Executive Summary of CRADA Work:

 This work is conducted in support of the American Made Challenges Solar Prize. The current
 scope addresses the Design portion of the overall prize competition. This team, led by the
 University of Connecticut, will design a solar powered, pilot scale, ceramic-based membrane
 distillation system that can operate on high salinity waters and propose that design for
 construction in the Test phase of the Prize competition.

 Summary of Research Results:

 Task 1 – Module design and prototype.

 Based on ongoing research at UConn, UConn will design multi-element modules that use single
 channeled ceramic membranes. UConn will have ceramic membranes manufactured that UConn
 will incorporate into multi-element modules for Vacuum Membrane Distillation (VMD) testing
 on UConn’s existing test rig. The ceramic membranes will be manufactured by Fraunhofer IKTS
 in Germany, shipped to the U.S., and installed into modules at UConn. As a risk mitigation
 strategy, in the event that UConn has trouble with the ceramic membranes from Fraunhofer
 IKTS, UConn will consider an alternative, for example, through ALSYS, located in
 Massachusetts. ALSYS carries a ceramic membrane option that would serve as a functional
 alternative to the Inopor® membrane from Fraunhofer IKTS.

 Task 2 – Module testing under VMD conditions.

 The new multi-element module will be tested by UConn on UConn’s existing test rig to assess
 flux and salt rejection performance. This rig can measure flux continuously, which is unique for
 VMD systems. UConn will evaluate multi-element module performance over a range of
 temperature (from 60-100°C), salinity (32,000 – 200,000 ppm NaCl), and downstream pressure
 (50-200 mbar). UConn will evaluate the thermal energy demand for the multi-element module
 and extrapolate to assess the thermal energy demand for systems over a range of capacities (0.1-
    100 m3/day).

 Task 3 – System Design.

 Task 3.1 Solar Thermal Collector System Design Based on UConn’s multi-element module
 performance and operating parameters, NREL will design and model relevant Solar Thermal
 Collector Systems with NREL’s System Advisor Model (SAM). NREL will iterate with
 UConn’s supplier Artic Solar, or other solar collector supplier designated by UConn, on the solar
 thermal collector type and the solar thermal collector system configuration based on balance of
 plant requirements and overall system costs. NREL will use parameters from the solar collector
 supplier, or representative generic values if none are provided, to design and model Solar
 Thermal Collector Systems. The design will include the option of thermal energy storage.

 Task 3.2 Pilot System Design NREL will support UConn in UConn’s designing of a pilot of the
 Solar Thermal Collector System. The pilot system is expected to provide valuable insight into
 challenges UConn may face when scaling up. With input from UConn, NREL will incorporate
 the Solar Thermal Collector System from Task 3.1 into a model of the pilot system design
 incorporating UConn’s multi-element modules and the solar thermal collector system from Task
 3.1. This effort will involve component selection of the vacuum compression system, water and
 energy balance modelling, Gained Output Ratio (“GOR”) calculations, and heat exchanger
 2

 requirements. UConn intends to target the design of a pilot system that can remove 1 m3/day of
 distillate water from brine. If UConn determines this capacity is too expensive when the bill of
 materials (BOM) is developed, they may have to further scale down. With input from UConn,
 NREL will run the model until a final version of the pilot system is agreed upon by NREL and
 UConn.

 Task 3.3 Systems Design Drawings Review

 UConn will generate design drawings of the pilot system. NREL will review the technical
 content of the pilot system design drawings.

 Task 3.4 Techno-Economic Assessment (TEA)

 UConn and NREL will identify target applications and the appropriate scale for the applications.
 NREL will provide the required capital cost and performance inputs for the TEA based on their
 pilot system model.

 Task 3.5 Review of Final Proposal

 UConn will develop a proposal for construction of a pilot system. NREL will review the final
 proposal and provide comments as appropriate.

 Executive Summary

 1. Overall Project Objectives

 NREL is part of the University of Connecticut’s (UCONN) Membrane Distillation with Ceramic
 Membranes team within the Solar Desalination Prize competition. Under this award, NREL was
 tasked with determining the size, cost, and performance of a solar-thermal system that can
 provide at least 90% of the thermal energy required to drive the membrane distillation (MD)
 system. Two thermal capacities were considered: 142-kW and 1400-kW. A performance model
 of Artic Solar’s XCPC was developed in NREL’s System Advisor Model (SAM) Solar Water
 Heating (SWH) software. Parametric studies to identify the optimal collector field and tank size,
 along with exploring several operation profiles are presented. The estimated cost of the solar
 thermal components is included in NREL’s study, while UCONN specified the MD system
 requirements.

 NREL’s participation via this CRADA is covered under a voucher as part of the competition
 rules. The information in this report was shared with the UCONN team for use in their full
 system performance and cost model.

 2.2. Proposed System

 In the current work, solar collectors transfer solar energy from the solar-field heat transfer fluid
 (HTF, in this case treated water), to the thermal energy storage tank through heat exchanger
 HEX1. Pressurized water is used as a storage medium. The UCONN membrane distillation
 process is coupled to the solar system through heat exchanger (HEX2) where incoming brine
 from the condenser is preheated and fed to the MD process. A simple schematic of the proposed
 solar-thermal system is shown in the Figure 7.

 The work includes simulating the system operation over a time span of one year to accommodate
 seasonal variation in temperature and solar irradiation and their subsequent effects on the amount
 and temperature of hot liquid that can be produced by the solar field. The solar data are typical
 meteorological year (TMY) data drawn from the NSRDB. A constraint applied to the designs
 was the solar thermal system to supply at least 90% of the MD heat load (solar fraction >0.9).

 3.6. Conclusions and Recommendations (142-kWth Pilot Scale)

 • Base Case system size = 200 XCPC collectors + 40 m3 storage tank
 • Extending MD system operating hours can be accomplished while maintaining a solar
 fraction > 0.90 by increasing tank volume.
 o Annual permeate production is unchanged (smaller MD unit runs for more hours)
 o Number of solar collectors is unchanged (same total thermal requirement)
 • Increasing solar unit operating hours by 33%, decreases MD system capacity by 33% and
 increases solar unit CAPEX by 9% (larger tank).
 • Increasing solar unit operating hours by 52%, decreases MD system capacity by 52% and
 increases solar unit CAPEX by 17% (larger tank).
 • Depending on the relative costs of the solar-thermal and MD systems, these changes may
 be advantageous.

 5.6. Conclusions and Recommendations (1400-kWth Scale)

 • Storage tank and collector field are of comparable cost
 o Temperature difference across the storage tank is 5.7 °C, resulting in a very large
 tank
 • Consider reducing approach temperature on HEX2 to increase the tank ΔT, resulting in
 smaller tank but larger heat exchanger
 • Consider latent heat thermal energy storage to achieve a higher energy storage density

 7. Appendix - Cost Estimation

 7.6. Heat Exchangers

 The solar system includes two heat exchangers: HEX 1 (between Collectors and Tank) and HEX
 2 (between Tank and MD system). The costing tool EconExpert2 was utilized to estimate the cost
 of the heat exchangers. Chemical Engineering Plant Cost Index (CEPCI) utilized in the costing is
 606.9 (corresponding to December 2020).

 Sizing was conducted at three estimated values for heat transfer coefficients (U_high, U_mid,
 U_low) resulting in three different design sizes for each heat exchanger. For each design, three
 material types were considered from the options offered within EconExpert: stainless steel (SS),
 nickel, titanium. “Nickel” was selected as surrogate for salt-resistant SS, e.g., Zeron-100.
