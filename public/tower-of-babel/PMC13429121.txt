Cellular Immunity Profile in B-Cell Non-Hodgkin Lymphoma Patients Receiving Rituximab-Based Chemotherapy
Authors: Lugyanti Sukrisman, Suzy Maria, Robert Sinto, Renata Tamara, Samuel Pratama
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeRituximab-based chemotherapy improves outcomes in B-cell non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL), but may exacerbate treatment-related immunosuppression, increasing the risk of opportunistic infections. Infection prophylaxis is not routinely implemented in Indonesia. This study aimed to characterize cellular immunity changes associated with rituximab-based chemotherapy and compare them with non-rituximab chemotherapy.Patients and MethodsThis prospective cohort study was conducted at two tertiary hospitals between March 2024 and October 2025. Adult patients with biopsy-confirmed B-cell NHL were enrolled in the rituximab-containing or non-rituximab chemotherapy groups. The absolute lymphocyte count, CD4 and CD8 counts, and their relative percentages were measured before treatment and after the third chemotherapy cycle. Comparative and intragroup analyses were performed using the appropriate statistical tests.ResultsFrom 115 recruited patients, 71 completed the post-cycle-3 assessment (58 receiving rituximab-based chemotherapy, 13 receiving chemotherapy without rituximab). Baseline immune parameters were comparable between the groups. After chemotherapy, the case group demonstrated significantly lower absolute CD4 count (median 231.5 vs 423.0/µL; p=0.014), higher relative CD8 percentage (47.1% vs 34.9%; p=0.004), and a lower CD4/CD8 ratio (0.7 vs 0.9; p=0.014) compared with controls. Intragroup analysis showed significant reductions in absolute lymphocyte, CD4, and CD8 counts in the rituximab group, whereas the control group demonstrated decreases only in lymphocyte and CD8 counts, with CD4 counts largely preserved.ConclusionRituximab-based chemotherapy was associated with greater suppression of cellular immunity, particularly CD4 depletion and CD4/CD8 ratio reduction, than chemotherapy alone. These findings support routine immune monitoring and provide immunological evidence that may help guide future prophylaxis strategies in rituximab-treated NHL patients, especially in settings with variable prophylaxis practices.Keywords: non-Hodgkin lymphoma, rituximab, cellular immunity, CD4, CD8, CD4/CD8 ratio, chemotherapy Introduction

Background

Non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL) comprises a heterogeneous group of lymphoid malignancies with diverse biological behaviors ranging from indolent to highly aggressive subtypes. NHL can be further classified based on the type of lymphocyte involved, which includes B-cell, T-cell, or Natural Killer (NK)-cell NHL. Diffuse Large B-Cell Lymphoma (DLBCL) is the most common form of B-cell NHL and is characterized by rapid progression and substantial variation in treatment response.1–4 In contrast, indolent lymphomas (such as follicular lymphoma and mantle cell lymphoma) exhibit slower disease progression and are often managed using less intensive therapeutic approaches.5

Immune dysregulation plays a critical role in lymphoma pathogenesis. Impaired cellular immunity, particularly involving CD4+ and CD8+ T-cell subsets, contributes not only to tumor progression but also to increased susceptibility to serious infections. CD4+ T cells coordinate adaptive immune responses against intracellular pathogens and support antitumor immunity, whereas CD8 T+ cells mediate cytotoxic immune responses against malignant and infected cells. Consequently, alterations in these cellular immunity parameters may reflect treatment-related immune dysfunction and help identify patients at increased risk of opportunistic infections.6 Low absolute lymphocyte count (ALC) and reduced CD4 T-cell levels are recognized as poor prognostic factors in DLBCL, reflecting both disease burden and treatment-related immunosuppression.7,8 The CD4/CD8 ratio has been reported to provide clinically relevant markers of treatment-related cellular immune dysfunction and to predict treatment response also prognosis of cancer patients.9,10

The introduction of rituximab, an anti-CD20 monoclonal antibody, in combination with CHOP chemotherapy (R-CHOP), has markedly improved outcomes for B-cell malignancies.11 However, rituximab may exacerbate chemotherapy-related immunosuppression by depleting B cells and impairing cellular immune function.12 A small subset of CD20-expression CD4+ T cells has been identified, suggesting that rituximab may contribute to direct depletion of helper T cells. Indirectly, depletion of normal B cells impairs antigen presentation, cytokine production, and B cell – T cell interactions that are essential for maintaining CD4+ T cell activation.13,14 Several studies have reported a higher incidence of opportunistic infections (including Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PCP)) among patients receiving rituximab-containing regimens.15 PCP incidence in this population may reach 6–15%, and related mortality remains substantial.16 Even so, there is still no uniform guideline that mandates infection prophylaxis to be given specifically for NHL patients receiving rituximab.17–19

Although previous studies have demonstrated immunosuppression following rituximab therapy, most have focused on B-cell depletion, infection incidence, or heterogeneous hematologic malignancies.20 Prospective studies specifically comparing longitudinal changes in cellular immunity between rituximab-based and non-rituximab chemotherapy among patients with B-cell NHL remain limited. Consequently, the magnitude of treatment-related CD4 and CD8 alterations attributable to rituximab beyond chemotherapy alone has not been well characterized, particularly in resource-limited settings where infection prophylaxis is not routinely standardized.

In Indonesia, the implementation of routine infection prophylaxis (such as cotrimoxazole for PCP or antiviral agents) in patients with lymphoma undergoing chemotherapy has not been consistent. Prophylaxis is often provided selectively, rather than systematically, and decisions are typically made on a case-by-case basis in the absence of robust local data describing treatment-related changes in cellular immunity. This variability in practice underscores a significant clinical gap: the absence of objective immune profile data to determine which patients may benefit most from prophylaxis during rituximab-based therapy. Given this context, it is essential to evaluate how rituximab-containing regimens affect the immune profiles of patients with NHL. Such data may help clinicians to better stratify infection risk, optimize prophylaxis strategies, and improve supportive care.

This study aimed to characterize cellular immune changes before and after the third chemotherapy cycle among NHL patients receiving rituximab-based immunochemotherapy compared to those receiving chemotherapy without rituximab. The findings of this study are expected to provide locally relevant evidence to inform infection risk assessment and support more rational and consistent prophylaxis practices, especially in Indonesia.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This prospective cohort study was conducted at Cipto Mangunkusumo National General Hospital and Dharmais National Cancer Hospital from March 2024 to October 2025. Patients receiving rituximab-containing chemotherapy were included in the case group, whereas those receiving chemotherapy without rituximab were included in the control group. Treatment allocation was not randomized because this study was designed as a prospective observational cohort reflecting routine clinical practice. The decision regarding the therapy program (i.e, choice of chemotherapy regimen, dosing, and infection prophylaxis) was determined by the attending medical oncologists at the respective hospitals based on lymphoma histological subtype, disease characteristics, previous treatment history, patient fitness, and local reimbursement policies. Consequently, differences in lymphoma subtype and chemotherapy regimen between the two groups reflect real-world clinical practice rather than investigator-directed treatment allocation. As this was an observational cohort study, neither participants nor treating physicians were blinded to treatment allocation.

Blood samples were collected from all consenting patients within one week prior to the first cycle of chemotherapy (pre-chemotherapy). Another blood sample was collected from all patients two weeks after the third cycle of chemotherapy, considering the hematologic recovery period (post-chemotherapy). The parameters evaluated were complete blood count, differential count, Lactate Dehydrogenase (LDH) level, absolute CD4 and CD8 counts, and relative CD4 and CD8 percentages. Peripheral blood CD4 and CD8 T-cell counts were measured using flow cytometry performed in the Clinical Pathology Laboratory of Cipto Mangunkusumo National General Hospital according to the manufacturer’s standardized protocol. Absolute CD4 and CD8 counts and their corresponding percentages were obtained from whole blood specimens collected in EDTA tubes and analyzed within the recommended processing time as part of routine flow cytometry procedures in the clinical laboratory of Dharmais National Cancer Hospital. Laboratory personnel performing flow cytometric analyses were blinded to study group whenever applicable.

The results pre- and post-chemotherapy were compared and statistically analyzed between the case and control groups. Baseline information, which included age, sex, comorbidity, diagnosis, Ann Arbor staging, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) grading, International Prognostic Index (IPI) score, Ki-67 percentage, chemotherapy regimen administered, and prophylaxis given, was collected from the electronic medical records. The results were reported in accordance with the STROBE checklist for cohort studies.

Participants and Inclusion Criteria

Adult patients (aged 18 years or above) who were diagnosed with B-cell NHL and were being treated at Cipto Mangunkusumo National General Hospital or Dharmais National Cancer Hospital during the study period were eligible to participate in this study. The diagnosis of NHL was confirmed by biopsy and immunohistochemical examinations performed by certified pathologists. Treatment-naive and-relapsed cases of B-cell NHL were included in this study. Relapsed cases were defined as patients who had previously achieved documented complete remission and subsequently experienced a return of disease. Patients with primary central nervous system lymphoma, primary bone marrow lymphoma, Burkitt lymphoma, T-cell or NK-cell lymphoma, Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) infection, or tuberculosis treatment were excluded from this study. Subjects were recruited using consecutive sampling.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM, USA). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize baseline patient characteristics. Categorical variables are expressed as frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables are reported as means with standard deviations or medians with interquartile ranges, depending on the data distribution. Normality of data distribution was determined using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, with p < 0.05 indicating abnormal distribution. Appropriate comparative analysis was used to determine the statistical significance between pre- and post-chemotherapy, as well as between the case and control groups. The Unpaired T test or Mann–Whitney U-test was used for two independent sample sets, while the paired T test or Wilcoxon test was used for two dependent sample sets. A P value of < 0.05 was an indicator of statistically significant differences.

Results

Patient Recruitment

A total of 115 patients with Non-Hodgkin Lymphoma (NHL) were consecutively recruited from two centers (Figure 1). During follow-up, 44 patients were classified as dropouts due to death (n = 30), loss to follow-up (n = 9), or voluntary withdrawal from the study (n=5). Among these dropouts, 29 patients had already received at least one chemotherapy cycle (23 in the rituximab group and 6 in the control group), while 15 patients discontinued chemotherapy. The final analytic cohort consisted of 71 patients who completed the immunological assessment after the third chemotherapy cycle, comprising 58 patients in the case group and 13 patients in the control group.

Patient recruitment and study flow.

Baseline Characteristics

Baseline characteristics were evaluated for the 71 patients who completed three cycles of chemotherapy and were included in the final analysis (Table 1). The mean age and sex distributions were comparable between groups. The ages of participants in the case group ranged from 19 to 81 years, and from 23 to 69 years for participants in the control group. The prevalence of major comorbidities, including hypertension, diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease, heart failure, and prior tuberculosis, did not differ significantly between groups. DLBCL was the predominant subtype in the rituximab group, whereas the control group had a higher proportion of follicular and marginal zone lymphomas. Disease stage, ECOG performance status, and IPI scores were broadly similar between groups, although stage IV disease was more frequent in the control group. Prior chemotherapy exposure was documented in 9 patients in the rituximab group and 3 patients in the control group. The majority of patients received R-CHOP (i.e, combination of Rituximab, Cyclophosphamide, Doxorubicin, Vincristine, and Prednisone), whereas CHOP-based regimens predominated in the control group. Prophylactic cotrimoxazole and acyclovir were administered to 77.6% of the rituximab group and none of the control group patients. In addition, the case group had a higher median Ki67 proliferation index and serum LDH level than the control group.

Immune Status Before Chemotherapy

The pre-chemotherapy cellular immune parameters were comparable between the case and control groups (Table 2). There were no significant differences in absolute lymphocyte count (median 1.6 vs 2.1 ×103/µL, p=0.349), absolute CD4 count (median 428.0 vs 427.0/µL, p = 0.582), or relative CD4 percentage (mean 29.0% vs 28.1%, p = 0.792). Similarly, absolute CD8 count (median 408.0 vs 486.0/µL, p = 0.523), relative CD8 percentage (mean 33.3% vs 29.1%, p = 0.256), and the CD4/CD8 ratio (median 0.8 vs 0.9, p = 0.513) did not differ significantly between groups. Overall, the baseline cellular immunity profiles were balanced before chemotherapy (Figure 2).

Immune Status After Chemotherapy

After the third chemotherapy cycle, several cellular immunity parameters differed significantly between groups (Table 3). The case group showed a markedly lower absolute CD4 count than the control group (median 231.5 vs 423.0/µL, p = 0.014). The relative CD8 percentage was significantly higher in the case group (mean 47.1% vs 34.9%, p = 0.004), and the CD4/CD8 ratio was correspondingly lower (median 0.7 vs 0.9, p = 0.014). Absolute lymphocyte count and absolute CD8 count did not differ significantly between the groups (p = 0.214 and p = 0.970, respectively), while the difference in relative CD4 percentage approached but did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.066). Scatter plots for these comparisons are shown in Figure 3.

Intragroup Analysis: Pre- vs Post-Chemotherapy

After the third chemotherapy cycle, the case group demonstrated a significant decline in multiple cellular immunity parameters (Table 4). The absolute lymphocyte count decreased substantially (median change −0.7 ×103/µL, p < 0.0001), accompanied by a significant reduction in the absolute CD4 count (median change −180.5/µL, p < 0.0001). The relative CD4 percentage showed no significant change (p = 0.206). The absolute CD8 count decreased significantly (median change: −78.0/µL, p = 0.046), while the relative CD8 percentage increased markedly (p < 0.0001). The CD4/CD8 ratio also decreased significantly after chemotherapy (p = 0.001).

Discussion

This study provides a detailed characterization of cellular immunity changes in patients with B-cell NHL receiving rituximab-based chemotherapy compared with those receiving chemotherapy without rituximab. Prior to treatment, both groups demonstrated comparable immune profiles, suggesting that the baseline cellular immunity was not a confounding factor. However, after three cycles of chemotherapy, clear differences emerged, particularly in the parameters reflecting T-cell–mediated immunity.

Comparison of pre- and post-chemotherapy immune parameters showed that both groups experienced reductions in absolute lymphocyte and CD8 counts, consistent with the expected effects of cytotoxic chemotherapy.22 In contrast, a reduction in absolute CD4 count was observed only in the rituximab group. This differential pattern supports the interpretation that CD4 depletion identified in this study primarily reflects a rituximab-related effect rather than the nonspecific lymphotoxic effects of chemotherapy. The addition of rituximab to cytotoxic chemotherapy is known to induce deeper and more sustained immunosuppression through several mechanisms.11 Although rituximab primarily targets CD20-positive B cells, its impact on CD4 T cells may occur through several indirect and direct mechanisms as mentioned in the Introduction.13,14 Together, these mechanisms provide a biological basis for the preferential CD4 decline observed in the rituximab cohort. This specific decrease in CD4 counts observed in the rituximab cohort aligns with the immune shifts previously associated with higher susceptibility to opportunistic infections, including Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia.15,16,23,24 In addition, this T-cell depleting effect can also be observed in other clinical settings, such as rheumatoid arthritis and multiple sclerosis patients receiving rituximab-based treatment regimens.25–27

The apparent increase in the CD4 and CD8 relative percentages, despite reductions in absolute counts, should not be interpreted as evidence of true immune recovery. Following chemotherapy, depletion of multiple lymphocyte populations (particularly B cells and other non-T-cell subsets such as NK cells) can reduce the overall lymphocyte pool, thereby increasing the relative proportion of remaining T-cell subsets.13 This effect would be expected to be more pronounced in the rituximab group, where profound B-cell depletion is expected and may be compounded by post-chemotherapy lymphopenia. Consequently, the increase in relative CD4 and CD8 percentages should be interpreted as a redistribution phenomenon rather than evidence of functional immune restoration. Therefore, absolute lymphocyte counts remain more informative than relative percentages when assessing treatment-related cellular immunosuppression. As reported in previous studies, lower absolute lymphocyte count after chemotherapy has been correlated with poorer prognosis in lymphoma patients.14

Furthermore, our study showed a significant decrease in the CD4/CD8 ratio in the rituximab-treated group, with no significant changes in the control group. The CD4/CD8 ratio reflects the balance between helper and cytotoxic T-cell populations, with shifts in its value may signify treatment-related immune suppression or recovery.28 In contrast to conditions such as HIV infection, where a low CD4/CD8 ratio is typically driven by expansion of CD8 cells due to chronic immune activation, the decline observed in this study appears to result primarily from selective CD4 depletion rather than CD8 expansion.29–31 This iatrogenic pattern of CD4 loss may indicate a treatment-induced state of impaired helper T-cell–mediated immunity, which is clinically relevant because reduced CD4 levels are closely linked to susceptibility to opportunistic infections.23,24

Additionally, the CD4/CD8 ratio may offer prognostic value by capturing treatment-related immune dysregulation.32 A low CD4/CD8 ratio was shown to increase the risk of Kaposi sarcoma and NHL in HIV-infected cohorts.33,34 A higher CD4/CD8 ratio was associated with greater treatment response and overall survival in gastric cancer, mantle cell lymphoma, primary central nervous system lymphomas, and Acquired Immune Deficiency Syndrome (AIDS)-related lymphomas.9,10,35,36 Although our study did not evaluate clinical outcomes, the consistently lower CD4/CD8 ratio observed in rituximab-treated patients is consistent with previous evidence, suggesting that this parameter may have prognostic relevance in hematologic malignancies. Thus, while our findings do not establish a prognostic value, they support the rationale for incorporating CD4/CD8 ratio monitoring into post-treatment immune assessment, particularly for identifying rituximab-based chemotherapy in patients who may benefit from closer surveillance or prophylactic measures.

However, these findings should be interpreted with caution because treatment allocation was not randomized, and the rituximab group consisted predominantly of patients with DLBCL, whereas the control group mainly comprised indolent lymphoma subtypes. DLBCL itself has been associated with immune dysregulation through alterations in the tumor microenvironment, impaired T-cell function, and cytokine-mediated immunosuppression, which may have contributed to the observed differences independently of rituximab exposure.37 Therefore, although rituximab likely plays an important role in treatment-related CD4 depletion, the independent contribution of lymphoma biology cannot be fully distinguished within the present study design.

Another potential concern is the presence of three patients aged > 75 years in the rituximab group, while the control group did not include individuals in this age range. Advanced age is associated with immunosenescence, including reduced naïve T-cell production and altered CD4/CD8 dynamics.38 However, in these older patients, the median pre-chemotherapy CD4 and CD8 counts were 411/µL and 375/µL, respectively, which changed to 221/µL and 1038/µL after the third chemotherapy cycle. The magnitude and direction of the immune changes in these individuals were broadly consistent with the overall pattern observed in the rituximab cohort. Given the very small number of such patients and the comparable baseline immune parameters between the treatment groups, it is unlikely that age distribution alone explains the post-treatment differences observed.

These findings have several practical clinical implications. Although international guidelines acknowledge the risk of opportunistic infections in rituximab recipients, they do not consistently mandate prophylaxis for all NHL patients receiving rituximab-based regimens.17–19 In Indonesia, as reflected in this cohort, prophylaxis practices remain highly variable and largely physician-driven. Our data suggest that patients receiving rituximab experience a deeper CD4 suppression by the third cycle of therapy, a period during which opportunistic infections occur. Although infection outcomes and the effectiveness of prophylactic strategies were not evaluated in this study, the observed decline in CD4 counts following rituximab-based chemotherapy provides biological evidence of impaired cellular immunity. Therefore, a more consistent implementation of routine immune monitoring during treatment and prophylactic measures (such as cotrimoxazole for Pneumocystis jirovecii and antiviral prophylaxis) may be warranted, especially in settings where supportive care resources are limited and early recognition of infections may be delayed.

This study has several strengths, including prospective data collection, standardized timing of immune measurements, and comparison with the control group. Although previous studies have demonstrated rituximab-associated immunosuppression, prospective data evaluating longitudinal changes in cellular immunity among patients with B-cell NHL remain limited, particularly in low- and middle-income countries where infection prophylaxis is not routinely standardized. Therefore, the principal contribution of the present study lies in providing real-world regional evidence describing treatment-related CD4, CD8, and CD4/CD8 ratio changes in Indonesian patients receiving lymphoma therapy.

Nevertheless, several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. The sample size of the control group was smaller than planned, which might have reduced the statistical power of the analyses. Multivariable adjustment was not performed to avoid unstable model estimates, considering the relatively limited sample size. Nevertheless, it reflected real-world treatment patterns in which most patients received rituximab-based regimen. The heterogeneity of lymphoma subtypes and prophylaxis practices may also contribute to variability in immune outcomes. Because treatment allocation reflected routine clinical practice, chemotherapy regimens differed between groups according to lymphoma subtype and clinical indication. The relatively high dropout rate may have introduced an attrition bias and reduced the statistical power of several subgroup comparisons. This limits the generalizability of our findings and should be considered when interpreting the results. Although the planned sample size could not be achieved because of slower-than-expected recruitment and participant attrition, all eligible patients were consecutively enrolled during the study period to maximize statistical precision. Additionally, immune recovery beyond the third cycle was not assessed; therefore, long-term immunological consequences could not be inferred from this study.

Despite these limitations, this study provides important real-world evidence showing that rituximab-based chemotherapy induces more pronounced CD4 depletion and CD4/CD8 ratio reduction than chemotherapy alone. Considering its association with impaired cell-mediated immunity and increased susceptibility to opportunistic infections, the observed reduction in CD4 counts provides biological rationale for closer immune monitoring, a more systematic risk assessment, and future studies on prophylactic strategies for infection prevention in patients with NHL receiving rituximab. Future studies with more homogeneous lymphoma subtypes or multivariable adjustments evaluating immune recovery across subsequent cycles, correlating immune suppression with infection and clinical outcomes, also the effectiveness of prophylaxis strategies would further enhance clinical decision making in our setting.

Conclusion

Rituximab-based chemotherapy was associated with a substantially greater suppression of cellular immunity than chemotherapy without rituximab, reflected by significant declines in absolute lymphocyte and CD4 counts, as well as a reduced CD4/CD8 ratio by the third cycle of treatment. These findings highlight the immunosuppressive effect of rituximab-containing regimens, support the importance of routine immune monitoring, and provide immunological evidence that may help guide future prophylaxis strategies and studies in patients with NHL receiving immunochemotherapy.
