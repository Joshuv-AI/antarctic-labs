Bayesian change-plane regression
Yuki Ohnishi, Fan Li

Abstract
Change-plane regression identifies subpopulations through an interpretable linear threshold rule, but likelihood-based inference for the hard-threshold boundary is nonregular: objectives are non-smooth, the boundary is weakly identified under no heterogeneity, and standard large-sample approximations are fragile. We develop a new Bayesian inferential framework based on a probit-gated working likelihood—a computationally regular surrogate that is deliberately misspecified for any fixed smoothing scale. For fixed smoothing, posterior summaries are therefore interpreted for a well-defined smoothed pseudo-true target; inference for the hard-threshold target is recovered only in a vanishing-smoothing regime, where approximation bias is governed by a boundary-margin condition on the covariate distribution. The resulting theory adapts misspecified Bernstein–von Mises arguments to Bayesian change-plane regression and makes explicit the triangular-array trade-off created by sending the smoothing scale to zero: sharper gates worsen the derivative bounds needed for Gaussian approximation, while approximation bias decreases according to the local amount of covariate mass near the boundary. Building on the resulting joint posterior, we further propose a decision-theoretic reporting protocol that separates evidence for clinically meaningful heterogeneity from the reporting of a subgroup boundary, with boundary uncertainty propagated to the covariate level through posterior membership probabilities. Simulations show favorable accuracy and uncertainty quantification of our new methods relative to the frequentist counterpart, and an application to a randomized lifestyle-intervention trial further demonstrates the utility of Bayesian change-plane regression in understanding treatment effect heterogeneity.



Keywords: Change-plane regression; Bayesian inference; Slice sampling; Subgroup identification; Posterior contraction

1
Introduction

Clinical trials are typically powered to detect average treatment effects in the full study population. However, treatment response is often heterogeneous among patients; a new treatment may be effective, or more effective than standard care, only in a specific subpopulation. Therefore, regulatory decisions in clinical trials increasingly require methods that can detect and quantify heterogeneous treatment effects while preserving interpretability and valid inference for protocol-specified endpoints and subgroups
(
4
, ICH E20, )
. Leveraging patient-specific covariates, subgroup analysis aims to identify patients with enhanced treatment effects, narrow the target population for a therapy, and guide future study designs. Conventional subgroup analyses often test one variable at a time or rely only on interaction terms in simple parametric models. These strategies tend to be underpowered, prone to spurious positives, and ignore that multiple individual attributes can jointly shape risk and response
(
31
)
.

Change-plane regression models address this challenge by defining subgroup membership through a linear rule,
\mathbbm{1}\{Z^{\top}\theta\geq 0\}
, and allowing the treatment effect to differ across the resulting two regimes. This yields a clinically communicable decision rule—a single score
Z^{\top}\theta
with a cutoff at zero—and a parsimonious summary of treatment effect heterogeneity. Despite its intuitive appeal, inference for the hard-threshold boundary parameter is intrinsically nonregular. Likelihoods and the associated optimization objectives are non-smooth in
\theta
, such that identification breaks down under no heterogeneity, and standard large-sample approximations can fail or be difficult to calibrate
(
5
;
14
;
33
)
. These challenges are only exacerbated by multimodality and sensitivity to tuning and initialization in finite samples
(
19
)
.

A common workaround to these estimation challenges proceeds by replacing the hard indicator with a smooth counterpart to regain differentiability and facilitate optimization
(
20
;
34
;
6
;
32
;
21
)
.
While smoothing improves numerical stability, it also changes the inferential target: for any fixed smoothing level, the resulting model generally does not perfectly coincide with the original hard-threshold data-generating mechanism, and the associated estimates can depend materially on tuning choices (bandwidth, temperature, or penalties). Moreover, uncertainty quantification after selecting a boundary is delicate: two-stage procedures that treat the estimated boundary as fixed can understate uncertainty in the estimated subgroup effects, especially near the null where the boundary is weakly identified.

In this article, we contribute a fully Bayesian framework for change-plane regression, and our novel contributions are several fold. First, we define a Bayesian change-plane regression model itself. The central inferential object is a joint posterior for a regularized, smoothing-indexed change-plane rule. At any fixed smoothing scale, this posterior targets the Kullback–Leibler pseudo-true parameter of the probit-gated working model, not the discontinuous hard-threshold parameter itself; the hard-threshold target is approached through a vanishing-smoothing regime. This distinction is important for interpretation: smoothing is not treated as a literal data-generating assumption or as an automatically selected nuisance parameter, but as a transparent regularization level that will be reported. To implement posterior inference, we develop a sampling algorithm that combines a latent-variable augmentation yielding conditional Gaussian blocks within a Gibbs sampler and a tuning-free great-circle elliptical slice update for the boundary direction.
Second, we establish the large-sample theory of the misspecified probit-gated posterior. To our knowledge, this is the first asymptotic treatment of posterior inference for Bayesian change-plane regression. The fixed-smoothing results deliberately build on standard misspecified-posterior theory, especially the Bernstein–von Mises framework of
17
; the change-plane-specific contribution is to identify what must be added when the smooth surrogate is used to approximate a nonregular hard threshold. In the vanishing-smoothing regime the likelihood forms a triangular array: derivatives of the probit gate become increasingly large as smoothing decreases, while the pseudo-true parameter drifts toward the hard-threshold target at a rate determined by the local boundary geometry. The resulting margin analysis yields a posterior contraction rate consisting of ordinary parametric fluctuation plus geometry-dependent smoothing bias, and gives an explicit feasibility calculation for polynomial smoothing schedules. Under the sufficient derivative-envelope conditions used for the Gaussian approximation, this calculation shows that removing the deterministic centering shift requires high-margin geometry. This theory clarifies when hard-threshold interpretation is asymptotically justified and when finite-sample posterior summaries should instead be read as inference for the smoothed target.
Third, under the Bayesian change-plane regression framework, we formalize a decision-theoretic reporting protocol for interpretable subgroup results under weak identification. A subgroup boundary is reported only when the posterior assigns sufficiently high probability to a prespecified clinically meaningful heterogeneity event, with boundary uncertainty propagated to the covariate level through posterior hard-membership probabilities. Evidence for heterogeneity, effect-size credible summaries, and subgroup membership uncertainty all arise from the same joint posterior, providing a coherent alternative to the separate and differently calibrated procedures required in frequentist workflows. Finally, we evaluate the proposed Bayesian change-plane regression through extensive simulation studies spanning correctly specified and misspecified baselines and both low- and high-dimensional effect-modifier settings, demonstrating favorable accuracy and uncertainty quantification for the treatment-effect contrast and the boundary direction relative to a frequentist counterpart. We further illustrate the framework through an application to the PREMIER clinical trial.

Bayesian Inference for Change-Plane Regression

Suppose we observe i.i.d. data
(Y_{i},W_{i},X_{i},Z_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
and the outcome is assumed to be generated from the following hard-threshold data-generating process (DGP).

Y_{i}=W_{i}^{\top}\beta_{0}+X_{i}^{\top}\gamma_{0}\,\mathbbm{1}\{Z_{i}^{\top}\theta_{0}\geq 0\}+\varepsilon_{i},

(1)

where
\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{i}\mid W_{i},X_{i},Z_{i}]=0
and
\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{i}^{2}\mid W_{i},X_{i},Z_{i}]=\sigma_{0}^{2}\in(0,\infty)
with
\|\theta_{0}\|_{2}=1
.
Without loss of generality, we impose the sign convention
\theta_{0,1}\geq 0
for identifiability and assume the plane parameter
\theta
lives in
\mathbb{S}^{q-1}=\{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{q}:\|\theta\|_{2}=1\}
and

\mathbb{S}^{q-1}_{+}=\{\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{q-1}:\theta_{1}\geq 0\}
,
so that
\theta_{0}\in\mathbb{S}^{q-1}_{+}
.
We work on the noncompact parameter space
\Theta=\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathbb{R}^{r}\times\mathbb{S}^{q-1}_{+}\times(0,\infty),
where
\beta_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
,
\gamma_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
,
\theta\in S^{q-1}_{+}
and
\sigma^{2}\in(0,\infty)
.
Although we write (
1
) as

Y=W^{\top}\beta_{0}+X^{\top}\gamma_{0}\,\mathbbm{1}\{Z^{\top}\theta_{0}\geq 0\}+\varepsilon
,
we do not intend to restrict the effect of
X
to be zero outside the subgroup.
Our notation allows
W
and
X
to overlap: in particular,
W
may include
X
(and other functions of
X
).
This practice is harmless when the baseline model is a strictly parametric, but with a highly adaptive nuisance model it may allow
\mu
to explain treatment effect heterogeneity through implicit interactions with
X
.
In that case the change-plane term becomes partially redundant and posterior learning of
(\gamma,\theta)
can be unstable.
To preserve interpretability of the learned boundary, we may restrict
\mu
to exclude treatment–covariate interactions (e.g., omit
X
from
W
, or impose
\mu(W,X)=\mu_{0}(W)+\delta X
).
In a parametric model with
X
included in
W
, the coefficients in
\beta_{0}
capture the global (main) effect of
X
that applies to all units,
while
\gamma_{0}
captures the additional effect of
X
within the subgroup defined by
Z^{\top}\theta_{0}\geq 0
.
For example, with a binary treatment
X\in\{0,1\}
and

W=(1,\text{baseline covariates},X)^{\top}
so that
\mathbb{E}(Y\mid W,X,Z)=W^{\top}\beta_{0}+\gamma_{0}X\,\mathbbm{1}\{Z^{\top}\theta_{0}\geq 0\}
,
which is equivalent to a model with an overall treatment effect
\delta
(contained in
\beta_{0}
) and a subgroup-specific increment
\gamma_{0}
: the treatment effect is
\delta
outside the subgroup and
\delta+\gamma_{0}
inside the subgroup.
This parameterization aligns with standard practice in effect-modification analyses and avoids interpreting
\gamma_{0}
as the entire treatment effect rather than an incremental subgroup contrast.
Also, the baseline component in (
1
) may be specified nonparametrically as a safeguard against model misspecification. A canonical choice for this component in the Bayesian paradigm is Bayesian additive regression trees (BART)
(
9
)
. Throughout, we restrict attention to the parametric specification
\mu(W_{i})=W_{i}\beta_{0}
. The semiparametric extension is discussed in Supplementary Material Section
A
.

For inference we adopt a Bayesian working-likelihood construction based on a smooth probit gate.
Let
\Phi
and
\phi
denote the standard normal cumulative distribution function and the associated probability density function.
Fix a smoothing scale
\tau>0
and, for
\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{q-1}_{+}
, define
\pi_{\theta,\tau}(z)=\Phi(z^{\top}\theta/\tau)\in(0,1)
.
For
\eta=(\beta,\gamma,\theta,\sigma^{2})\in\Theta
, introduce latent variables

T_{i}=Z_{i}^{\top}\theta+e_{i}
with
e_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\tau^{2})
, and
D_{i}=\mathbbm{1}\{T_{i}\geq 0\}
, and specify the conditional model

\displaystyle Y_{i}\mid(D_{i},W_{i},X_{i},Z_{i})\sim\mathcal{N}\big(W_{i}^{\top}\beta+X_{i}^{\top}\gamma D_{i},\sigma^{2}\big),\quad D_{i}\mid(Z_{i},\theta)\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(\pi_{\theta,\tau}(Z_{i})).

Equivalently, conditional on
(W_{i},X_{i},Z_{i})
, the induced working marginal likelihood for
Y_{i}
becomes the two-component Gaussian mixture

p_{\eta,\tau}(y\mid w,x,z)=(1-\pi_{\theta,\tau}(z))\,\varphi_{\sigma}(y-w^{\top}\beta)+\pi_{\theta,\tau}(z)\,\varphi_{\sigma}(y-w^{\top}\beta-x^{\top}\gamma),

(2)

where
\varphi_{\sigma}(u)=(2\pi\sigma^{2})^{-1/2}\exp(-u^2/(2\sigma^2))
.
The induced working conditional mean is
\mu_{\eta,\tau}(w,x,z)=\mathbb{E}_{\eta,\tau}[Y\mid W=w,X=x,Z=z]=w^{\top}\beta+x^{\top}\gamma\,\pi_{\theta,\tau}(z),

which replaces the hard indicator in (
1
) by the smooth gate
\pi_{\theta,\tau}(z)
.

We treat
\tau>0
as a user-chosen smoothing scale that defines the probit gate

\pi_{\theta,\tau}(z)=\Phi(z^{\top}\theta/\tau)
and thus the working likelihood in (
2
) used for computation and uncertainty quantification.
For fixed
\tau
, posterior inference under the working model targets the Kullback–Leibler minimizer
\eta^{\star}(\tau)
, not the discontinuous hard-threshold parameter
\eta_{0}=(\beta_{0},\gamma_{0},\theta_{0},\sigma_{0}^{2})
in (
1
).
The hard-threshold model supplies the motivating scientific ideal and the limiting target for the asymptotic approximation, but fixed-
\tau
posterior summaries should be interpreted as summaries of the
\tau
-smoothed pseudo-true rule.
Accordingly, we do not estimate
\tau
from the data; instead, we treat it as an explicit regularization level whose effect is reported through sensitivity analysis (Section
3.3
).
Section
3.2
studies a vanishing-smoothing regime
\tau=\tau_{n}\downarrow 0
under which posterior uncertainty under the working model can transfer to inference for the hard-threshold target under additional margin and rate conditions.

2.1
Posterior inference

We first describe an MCMC algorithm for sampling from the posterior distribution of
\eta=(\beta,\gamma,\theta,\sigma^{2})
. Recall that for
\eta=(\beta,\gamma,\theta,\sigma^{2})
with
\sigma^{2}>0
, we introduce latent variables
T_{i}=Z_{i}^{\top}\theta+e_{i}
, where
e_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\tau^{2})
, and set
D_{i}=\mathbbm{1}\{T_{i}\geq 0\}
; we further specify the conditional model
Y_{i}\mid D_{i},W_{i},X_{i},Z_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}\big(W_{i}^{\top}\beta+X_{i}^{\top}\gamma D_{i},\sigma^{2}\big)
and
D_{i}\mid Z_{i},\theta\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(\Phi(z^{\top}\theta/\tau))
.
Because
\tau
indexes the working likelihood rather than the hard-threshold target, we treat
\tau>0
as a fixed regularization level (Section
2
).
This data-augmentation scheme yields a Gibbs sampler that alternates between updating the model parameters
\eta
and the latent augmentation variables
\{D_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
(equivalently
\{T_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
). Under conjugate priors,
(\beta,\gamma,\sigma^{2})
have standard Gaussian and inverse-gamma full conditionals given the latent variables (Supplementary Material Section
A
). The nonstandard component is the boundary direction
\theta
, which enters the complete likelihood through
T_{i}\mid Z_{i},\theta\sim\mathcal{N}(Z_{i}^{\top}\theta,\tau^{2})
and is constrained to have unit Euclidean norm (with
\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{q-1}_{+}
enforcing identifiability up to sign).

Since a conjugate update for
\theta
is unavailable, and the standard Metropolis-Hastings algorithm suffers from slow mixing, we adopt a great-circle elliptical slice sampler
(
23
)
under the uniform prior on
\mathbb{S}^{q-1}_{+}
. The update exploits the representation

p(\nu\mid\mathcal{D}_{n},\cdot)\propto\phi(\nu),L(\nu/|\nu|_{2}),

where
\phi(\cdot)
is the
\mathcal{N}(0,I_{q})
density and
L(\theta)
is the likelihood of
\theta
under the working model. The induced density on
\nu\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
yields the desired posterior on directions after normalization and takes the form of a “Gaussian prior
\times
likelihood,” for which elliptical slice sampling is purpose-built. Given the current state
\theta
, an auxiliary Gaussian draw projected onto the tangent space defines a great circle
\theta(\varphi)=\theta\cos\varphi+u\sin\varphi
; slice sampling over
\varphi
preserves both the unit-sphere constraint and the hemisphere convention. The algorithm iterates the following steps.

1.

Draw an auxiliary Gaussian vector
\nu\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{q})
and project it onto the tangent space at
\theta
,
\nu_{\perp}=\nu-(\nu^{\top}\theta)\theta
,
u=\nu_{\perp}/\|\nu_{\perp}\|_{2}
,
so that
u\in\mathbb{S}^{q-1}
and
u^{\top}\theta=0
.

2.

Define the log-likelihood
\ell_{T}(v):=-(2\tau^{2})^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(T_{i}-Z_{i}^{\top}v)^{2}
for
v\in\mathbb{S}^{q-1}
, and draw a slice level
\log y=\ell_{T}(\theta)+\log U
,
U\sim\mathrm{Unif}(0,1)
.

3.

Draw an initial angle
\varphi\sim\mathrm{Unif}(-\pi,\pi)
and set a bracket
(\varphi_{\min},\varphi_{\max})=(-\pi,\pi)
. Propose
\theta^{\mathrm{prop}}(\varphi)=\theta\cos\varphi+u\sin\varphi
,
which satisfies
\|\theta^{\mathrm{prop}}(\varphi)\|_{2}=1
by construction.

4.

If
(\theta^{\mathrm{prop}}(\varphi))_{1}<0
, reject the proposal and shrink the bracket to exclude
\varphi
.
Otherwise, if
\ell_{T}(\theta^{\mathrm{prop}}(\varphi))\geq\log y
, accept
\theta^{\mathrm{prop}}(\varphi)
as the new value of
\theta
.
If neither condition holds, shrink the bracket to exclude
\varphi
.
In all rejection cases, set
(\varphi_{\min},\varphi_{\max})=(\varphi,\varphi_{\max})
if
\varphi<0
and
(\varphi_{\min},\varphi_{\max})=(\varphi_{\min},\varphi)
otherwise, draw a new
\varphi\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\varphi_{\min},\varphi_{\max})
.

This update preserves the uniform prior on
\mathbb{S}^{q-1}_{+}
and leaves the posterior
p(\theta\mid\mathcal{D}_{n},\cdot)
invariant. It requires no tuning, it always accepts once a point on the slice within the hemisphere is found, and it preserves the unit norm of
\theta
by construction of the great circle.

2.2
Extensions to handle high-dimensional effect modifiers

In many applications, the subgroup-defining covariate vector
Z_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
contains a mix of true effect modifiers and irrelevant or highly correlated features. Estimating the change-plane direction
\theta
without regularization can therefore dilute the boundary signal, inflate posterior uncertainty, and yield unstable subgroup rules when
q
is moderate or large relative to
n
. To enable automatic subgroup detection while preserving the interpretable linear score
Z_{i}^{\top}\theta
, we endow the boundary direction with a global–local shrinkage prior of horseshoe type
(
2
)
.
We introduce an unconstrained coefficient vector
\nu\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
and place a horseshoe prior on its coordinates:
\nu_{j}\mid\lambda_{j}^{\mathrm{loc}},\lambda^{\mathrm{glob}}\sim\mathcal{N}\!\big(0,(\lambda^{\mathrm{glob}})^{2}(\lambda_{j}^{\mathrm{loc}})^{2}\big)
,
\lambda^{\mathrm{glob}}\sim\mathcal{C}^{+}(0,1)
and
\lambda_{j}^{\mathrm{loc}}\sim\mathcal{C}^{+}(0,1)
for
j=1,\dots,q
,
where
\mathcal{C}^{+}(0,1)
denotes the standard half-Cauchy distribution.
We then define the boundary direction by normalizing,
\theta=\theta(\nu):=\nu/\|\nu\|_{2},

and enforce the hemisphere identifiability convention via the deterministic mapping
\theta(\nu)\leftarrow\mathrm{sign}(\nu_{1})\,\theta(\nu)
with
\mathrm{sign}(0):=1
,
so that
\theta(\nu)\in\mathbb{S}^{q-1}_{+}
by construction.
This reparameterization preserves the scientific interpretation of the subgroup score
Z^{\top}\theta
while allowing the horseshoe to shrink many coordinates of
\nu
(and hence
\theta
) toward zero.
An immediate consequence of the normalization
\theta=\nu/\|\nu\|_{2}
is that the likelihood is invariant under
\nu\mapsto c\nu
for any
c>0
, so the posterior on
\theta
is determined by the relative magnitudes of the local scales
\{\lambda_{j}^{\mathrm{loc}}\}
alone; the global scale
\lambda^{\mathrm{glob}}
cancels through normalization and acts purely as a computational nuisance parameter that helps mix the horseshoe augmentation. Sparsity in the boundary direction is therefore driven entirely by the local horseshoe components, which is the desired qualitative behavior.
We implement the half-Cauchy scales using an auxiliary-variable representation. For example, each local scale admits
(\lambda_{j}^{\mathrm{loc}})^{2}\mid\xi_{j}\sim\mathrm{IG}\!\left(1/2,\ 1/\xi_{j}\right)
,
\xi_{j}\sim\mathrm{IG}\!\left(1/2,\ 1\right),

and similarly for
(\lambda^{\mathrm{glob}})^{2}
with its own auxiliary variable.
These augmentations yield closed-form inverse-gamma updates for the global and local scales conditional on
\nu
.

Under the probit-gated data augmentation, the boundary parameter enters the complete likelihood through the latent Gaussian layer
T_{i}\mid(Z_{i},\theta)\sim\mathcal{N}(Z_{i}^{\top}\theta,\tau^{2})
,
and in the horseshoe parameterization this becomes
T_{i}\mid(Z_{i},\nu)\sim\mathcal{N}\!\big(Z_{i}^{\top}\theta(\nu),\tau^{2}\big)
, where
\theta(\nu)=\mathrm{sign}(\nu_{1})\,\nu/\|\nu\|_{2}
.
Conditional on the scale parameters, the prior for
\nu
is Gaussian,
\nu\mid\lambda^{\mathrm{glob}},\lambda^{\mathrm{loc}}\sim\mathcal{N}\!\big(0,\ \Sigma_{\lambda}\big)
with
\Sigma_{\lambda}=(\lambda^{\mathrm{glob}})^{2}\mathrm{diag}\big((\lambda^{\mathrm{loc}}_{1})^{2},\dots,(\lambda^{\mathrm{loc}}_{q})^{2}\big)
.
However, because the likelihood depends on
\nu
only through the normalized direction
\theta(\nu)
, there is no conjugate Gaussian full conditional for
\nu
.
Instead, conditional on
(T,Z,\lambda^{\mathrm{glob}},\lambda^{\mathrm{loc}})
, the target density for
\nu
takes the form
p(\nu\mid\cdot)\propto\ \mathcal{N}(\nu;0,\Sigma_{\lambda})\exp\!\left\{-\frac{1}{2\tau^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(T_{i}-Z_{i}^{\top}\theta(\nu)\big)^{2}\right\},

which is precisely a “Gaussian prior
\times
likelihood” in
\nu
, well-suited for elliptical slice sampling.
This strategy retains the computational advantages of the latent Gaussian augmentation while incorporating sparsity adaptively through the horseshoe scales.
Posterior summaries of the subgroup rule are based on the induced posterior of
\theta=\theta(\nu)
.
Coordinates that are persistently shrunk toward zero contribute negligibly to the score
Z^{\top}\theta
and may be interpreted as unimportant effect modifiers for subgroup formation.

3
Bayesian Asymptotic Theory

In this section we study asymptotic properties of the proposed Bayesian procedure.
In the theoretical development below, we impose signal-strength conditions that rule out the weak-identification regime with
\gamma\approx 0
(where the likelihood is nearly invariant in
\theta
).
In particular, we establish posterior contraction and a Bernstein-von Mises (BvM) theorem under model misspecification. The working model is misspecified in the sense that, for a fixed
\tau>0
, the model class of soft-threshold regressions indexed by
\eta\in\Theta
does not contain the hard-threshold DGP in (
1
). Consequently, the posterior concentrates around the pseudo-true parameter, the Kullback-Leibler minimizer, rather than the data-generating parameter. At fixed
\tau
, the proof follows the general misspecified-posterior strategy of
17
, with complementary finite-sample misspecified-BvM perspectives in
24
: prior thickness near the pseudo-true parameter, exponentially consistent tests, and a local asymptotic normality (LAN) expansion of the log-likelihood ratio.

The additional work specific to change-plane regression enters when the smooth surrogate is connected back to the discontinuous target. Letting
\tau=\tau_{n}\downarrow 0
creates a triangular array of misspecified likelihoods whose regularity constants deteriorate with
n
: derivatives of
\Phi(Z^{\top}\theta/\tau_{n})
carry powers of
\tau_{n}^{-1}
, while the pseudo-true path
\eta^{\star}(\tau_{n})
moves toward the hard-threshold parameter only at a rate controlled by the amount of covariate mass near the boundary. The theory below isolates these two forces by combining the sieve scale used to control the noncompact parameter space with the inverse smoothing scale, and by introducing a boundary-margin exponent
\alpha
for the local covariate geometry. This yields the rate
n^{-1/2}+\tau_{n}^{\alpha}
and explicit polynomial-schedule constraints for removing the centering shift at the hard-threshold target.
To keep the main theoretical narrative focused, we separate the assumptions into several layers. The main manuscript retains only the conditions that are special to the change-plane problem and needed to interpret the bias–variance tradeoff governed by
\tau
and the margin exponent
\alpha
. Standard local regularity, prior-positivity, sieve/tightness, and LAN-remainder conditions are collected in Supplementary Material Section
B.1
. The theorem statements below point back to that section where the full technical machinery is needed.

3.1
Fixing smoothing scale
\tau

Let
P_{0}
denote the true joint law of
(Y,W,X,Z)
under (
1
), and let
P_{0}^{WXZ}
denote its marginal law for
(W,X,Z)
.
For each
(\eta,\tau)
, define the working joint law
P_{\eta,\tau}
by keeping the same marginal distribution of
(W,X,Z)
as under
P_{0}
and replacing only the conditional distribution of
Y
given
(W,X,Z)
by (
2
).
Let
\ell_{\tau}(\eta;O)=\log p_{\eta,\tau}(Y\mid W,X,Z)
for
O=(Y,W,X,Z)
and define the population risk
K_{\tau}(\eta)=\mathbb{E}_{P_{0}}\big[-\ell_{\tau}(\eta;O)\big]
.
A pseudo-true parameter at smoothing level
\tau
is any minimizer
\eta^{\star}(\tau)\in\arg\min_{\eta\in\Theta}K_{\tau}(\eta)
.
Fix
\tau>0
and select one minimizer, denoted
\eta^{\star}=\eta^{\star}(\tau)
. Write
\eta^{\star}=(\beta^{\star},\gamma^{\star},\theta^{\star},\sigma^{2\star})
for its components.
The risk
K_{\tau}(\eta)
is assumed to be finite and continuous on
\Theta
, and
\eta^{\star}
is an isolated minimizer in the sense that, for every
\rho>0
,
\inf_{\eta\in\Theta:\|\eta-\eta^{\star}\|\geq\rho}\big(K_{\tau}(\eta)-K_{\tau}(\eta^{\star})\big)>0
.
We also assume that
\theta^{\star}
lies in the interior of
\mathbb{S}^{q-1}_{+}
, with
\theta_{1}^{\star}>0
, and admits a
C^{2}
local chart: there exist a point
\vartheta^{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{q-1}
, an open neighborhood
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{q-1}
of
\vartheta^{\star}
, an open neighborhood
V
of
\theta^{\star}
in
\mathbb{S}^{q-1}_{+}
, and a
C^{2}
map
\theta(\cdot):U\to V
with
\theta(\vartheta^{\star})=\theta^{\star}
.
In these local coordinates, write
\tilde{\eta}=(\beta,\gamma,\vartheta,\sigma^{2})
and
\tilde{\eta}^{\star}=(\beta^{\star},\gamma^{\star},\vartheta^{\star},\sigma^{2\star})
, and set
\tilde{\ell}_{\tau}(\tilde{\eta};O)=\log p_{\eta(\tilde{\eta}),\tau}(Y\mid W,X,Z)
. The log working likelihood
\tilde{\ell}_{\tau}
is twice continuously differentiable on
\mathbb{B}_{\delta}(\tilde{\eta}^{\star})
for some
\delta>0
.

Let
\Pi
be a prior on
\Theta
and define the (fixed-prior) posterior under the working likelihood:

\Pi^{(\tau)}(A\mid\mathcal{D}_{n})=\frac{\int_{A}\prod_{i=1}^{n}p_{\eta,\tau}(Y_{i}\mid W_{i},X_{i},Z_{i})\,\Pi(d\eta)}{\int_{\Theta}\prod_{i=1}^{n}p_{\eta,\tau}(Y_{i}\mid W_{i},X_{i},Z_{i})\,\Pi(d\eta)}.

Beyond this local-coordinate setup, the remaining ingredients are standard for misspecified BvM arguments on noncompact spaces: positive curvature of the working risk around the pseudo-true parameter, local prior thickness, sub-Gaussian tail control for the design/noise process, and sieve/tightness conditions that transfer the compact-sieve posterior back to the fixed prior on
\Theta
. Because
\Theta=\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathbb{R}^{r}\times\mathbb{S}^{q-1}_{+}\times(0,\infty)
is noncompact, contraction and testing arguments are carried out on a growing
sieve

\Theta_{n}\subset\Theta
, namely the compact set obtained by bounding the Euclidean blocks by a radius
S_{n}\to\infty
and keeping
\sigma^{2}
in a compact subinterval of
(0,\infty)
; results on the sieve are then transferred back to the fixed prior on
\Theta
via prior-tail control. These conditions are explicitly stated in Supplementary Material Section
B.1
.

Remark 1

(Posterior shape versus frequentist coverage under misspecification)
.

Theorem
1
delivers the asymptotic
posterior shape
: conditional on the data, the rescaled posterior for
h=\sqrt{n}(\tilde{\eta}-\tilde{\eta}^{\star})
is approximately
\mathcal{N}(I_{\tau}^{-1}\Delta_{n},\,I_{\tau}^{-1})
. It does not, by itself, assert exact frequentist coverage under misspecification. The frequentist sampling distribution of the posterior mean is governed by the sandwich covariance,

\sqrt{n}\big(\hat{\tilde{\eta}}-\tilde{\eta}^{\star}\big)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{P_{0}}\mathcal{N}\big(0,\ I_{\tau}^{-1}V_{\tau}I_{\tau}^{-1}\big),\qquad V_{\tau}:=\mathrm{Var}_{P_{0}}\!\big[\nabla\tilde{\ell}_{\tau}(\tilde{\eta}^{\star};O)\big],

so standard credible sets centered at the posterior mean have exact first-order frequentist coverage only under the information equality
V_{\tau}=I_{\tau}(\tilde{\eta}^{\star})
; otherwise they may be over- or under-covered
(
17
;
22
)
. If frequentist calibration under misspecification is required, the Gaussian posterior shape can be combined with a plug-in sandwich correction or a weighted-bootstrap/bagged posterior construction
(
11
;
30
)
. In this paper we report raw posterior credible summaries as Bayesian uncertainty and assess their frequentist behavior empirically in the simulation studies.

Remark 2

(Nonregular hard-threshold rates versus regularized Bayesian inference)
.

Hard-threshold change-plane models are intrinsically nonregular because the boundary parameter enters through a discontinuous indicator.
Accordingly, in strong-signal regimes and under favorable covariate geometry, point estimators of the boundary component, e.g.,
\theta
, can exhibit nonstandard convergence behavior, potentially faster than
the regular
n^{-1/2}
rate, while other components typically remain
n^{-1/2}
-regular
(
13
, e.g.,)
.

Our goal, however, is joint and stable uncertainty quantification for
(\beta,\gamma,\theta,\sigma^{2})
under a computationally tractable posterior, rather than an optimal pointwise rate for
\hat{\theta}
alone. Hard-threshold
M
-estimators attain sharper marginal rates for
\theta
—cube-root
n^{-1/3}
under generic boundary geometry
(
15
;
18
)
and
n^{-1}
under stronger signal/design conditions—but with non-Gaussian limit laws that do not directly deliver joint credible sets, Wald-type intervals, or a tunable bias–regularity trade-off. Smoothed frequentist surrogates recover
n^{-1/2}
asymptotic normality at the cost of bandwidth tuning
(
26
)
, and analogous non-regular posterior rates for Bayesian change-point problems are studied in
7
. The
n^{-1/2}+\tau_{n}^{\alpha}
rate in Theorem
2
is the price of a Gaussian joint posterior for all four components of
\eta
, which in turn supports the coherent uncertainty propagation underlying the reporting protocol in Section
4
.

3.3
Role and practical choice of the smoothing scale
\tau

After scaling the boundary covariates
Z
(e.g., centering and standardizing each coordinate),

\tau
controls the size of the “gray zone” around the boundary
z^{\top}\theta=0

under the probit gate
\pi_{\theta,\tau}(z)=\Phi(z^{\top}\theta/\tau)
.
In particular,
\pi_{\theta,\tau}(z)
increases from
0.05
to
0.95
as
z^{\top}\theta
moves from

-1.645\tau
to
1.645\tau
, so the
5\%
–
95\%
transition occurs over a score window of width
3.29\tau
.

The vanishing-
\tau_{n}
regime is useful for understanding the bias–regularity trade-off, but it is not a plug-in rule for selecting
\tau
from a single dataset.
In applications we recommend treating
\tau
as a fixed finite-sample regularization parameter and reporting sensitivity over a prespecified grid of plausible values.
After standardizing
Z
, it is often most interpretable to calibrate that grid through the implied gray-zone width
3.29\tau
on the score scale.
For example, one may examine a small grid such as
\tau\in\{0.01,\,0.035,\,0.1\}
, which spans sharper, intermediate, and smoother transitions without claiming that one value is asymptotically optimal.
A very small
\tau
can lead to multimodality and slow mixing (reflecting the underlying nonregular hard-threshold geometry),
while very large
\tau
can over-smooth and attenuate subgroup contrast.
We therefore emphasize posterior summaries that are stable across
\tau
values with acceptable MCMC diagnostics and explicitly qualify conclusions that depend on the amount of smoothing.
When the report/no-report decision or the membership summaries change materially across the grid, the analysis should be presented as evidence about a regularized,
\tau
-indexed subgroup rule.

4
A Framework for Decision-Theoretic Reporting

A core feature of change-plane models is that the boundary direction
\theta
is learned only through treatment effect heterogeneity.
In the model (
1
),
if the heterogeneity term vanishes (e.g.,
\gamma_{0}=0
when
X
encodes treatment contrast), then the likelihood is invariant to
\theta
:
the induced fit does not depend on how the sample is partitioned.
Consequently,
\theta
is not identified under the no-heterogeneity null, and when
\|\gamma_{0}\|
is small the likelihood in
\theta
is typically flat and may admit many near-equivalent directions. In many frequentist literatures
(
13
, e.g., )
(and our asymptotic claims in Section
3
), one typically imposes signal-strength conditions that rule out the weak-identification regime with
\gamma\approx 0
or reports the treatment heterogeneity in a two-step “test-then-report” procedure
(
5
, e.g., )
.
In finite samples, however, this manifests as a diffuse posterior for
\theta
that is driven primarily by geometric constraints and prior regularization rather than data evidence.
Accordingly, point summaries of
\theta
should not be interpreted as a clinically meaningful subgroup rule unless the data support heterogeneity.
This is precisely the setting that motivates the practical weak-identification discussion and reporting protocol below. Specifically, we recommend a
decision-theoretic reporting protocol
that separates (i) evidence for clinically meaningful heterogeneity from
(ii) summarization of a subgroup boundary.
The protocol can be stated decision-theoretically, yielding an explicit rule for when subgroup boundaries should be reported. This is achieved by the following steps.

Step 1: Specify the heterogeneity estimand and a clinical threshold.

Let
\Delta(\gamma)
denote a one-dimensional, decision-relevant heterogeneity contrast.
In the common binary-treatment case,
X=A\in\{0,1\}
and
\Delta(\gamma)=\gamma
.
For multi-dimensional
X
, one may take
\Delta(\gamma)=x_{\rm clin}^{\top}\gamma
for a pre-specified treatment contrast
x_{\rm clin}

(e.g., the active-versus-control contrast), or alternatively
\Delta(\gamma)=\|\gamma\|_{2}
if overall heterogeneity magnitude is of interest.
Fix a clinically meaningful threshold
\delta>0
(e.g., a minimal clinically important difference) and define the event
H_{\delta}=\{|\Delta(\gamma)|\geq\delta\}
. These values should be chosen based on substantive domain-knowledge.

Step 2: A Bayes decision rule for whether to report a subgroup boundary.

Consider two reporting actions:

a_{0}:\ \text{``do not report a subgroup boundary''},\quad a_{1}:\ \text{``report a subgroup boundary''}.

(3)

Let
c_{\rm FP}>0
be the loss for reporting a subgroup boundary when heterogeneity is not clinically meaningful
(i.e., a false-positive subgroup claim), and
c_{\rm FN}>0
be the loss for not reporting a boundary when
H_{\delta}
holds
(i.e., a missed clinically meaningful heterogeneity).
Under the 0-1 loss
L(a_{1},\gamma)=c_{\rm FP}\,\mathbbm{1}\{H_{\delta}^{c}\}
,
L(a_{0},\gamma)=c_{\rm FN}\,\mathbbm{1}\{H_{\delta}\}
,
the Bayes action minimizes posterior expected loss and is therefore:

\text{choose }a_{1}\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \Pi(H_{\delta}\mid\mathcal{D}_{n})\ >\ \frac{c_{\rm FP}}{c_{\rm FP}+c_{\rm FN}}\ :=\ p_{\mathrm{report}}.

Equivalently, the boundary is reported only when the posterior probability of clinically meaningful heterogeneity exceeds a
pre-specified threshold determined by the relative cost of false subgroup discovery versus missed heterogeneity.
This rule makes explicit the principle that
\theta
is decision-relevant only insofar as heterogeneity is supported.

This decision rule specializes a line of Bayesian subgroup-identification work to the change-plane setting; closely related decision-theoretic formulations of when to declare a subgroup include the zero-enriched Polya-urn approach of
28
and the credible-subgroup methodology of
25
. Elicitation of
(c_{\rm FP},c_{\rm FN})
is itself a substantive exercise: in confirmatory drug-development contexts regulators typically penalize false subgroup claims more heavily than missed heterogeneity (because the all-comers analysis remains the fall-back), suggesting
p_{\mathrm{report}}\in[0.85,0.95]
as a reasonable default range; exploratory biomarker-discovery analyses may justify lower thresholds. We use
p_{\mathrm{report}}=0.9
as a baseline and report sensitivity to
(\delta,p_{\mathrm{report}})
in Section
6.3
.

Step 3(a): What to report when
a_{0}
is selected (no boundary report).

If
a_{0}
is selected, we recommend reporting:
(i) posterior summaries for
\Delta(\gamma)
(e.g.,
\Pi(\Delta(\gamma)>\delta\mid\mathcal{D}_{n})
and a credible interval for
\Delta(\gamma)
);
and (ii) an explicit statement that
\theta
is weakly identified under near-null heterogeneity. In this regime, any numerical summary of
\theta
reflects prior regularization rather than empirical evidence, and should not be interpreted as a data-supported subgroup boundary rule.

Step 3(b): What to report when
a_{1}
is selected (boundary report).

If
a_{1}
is selected, we recommend reporting the subgroup rule together with uncertainty measures that reflect boundary non-identifiability and finite-sample dispersion.

Boundary point summary.

Because
\theta
is constrained to the sphere,we recommend a decision-theoretic point summary that respects the geometry.
Let
M=\mathbb{E}[\theta\theta^{\top}\mid\mathcal{D}_{n}]
be the posterior second-moment matrix, approximated by MCMC draws.
Represent the boundary direction by its rank-one projection
P_{\theta}=\theta\theta^{\top}
and consider squared Frobenius loss

L(\theta,\Theta)=\|P_{\theta}-P_{\Theta}\|_{F}^{2}
.
The corresponding Bayes estimator, which we refer to as the
posterior principal direction
, is:

\hat{\theta}\in\arg\max_{\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{q-1}_{+}}\ \theta^{\top}M\theta,

(4)

which, by the Rayleigh–Ritz theorem, is a leading eigenvector of
M
, with sign chosen to satisfy the hemisphere convention
\hat{\theta}_{1}\geq 0
.
This yields a stable directional summary even when the componentwise posterior mean does not lie on a sphere.

The Frobenius-projection estimator (
4
) is a natural extrinsic summary on
\mathbb{S}^{q-1}_{+}
but not the only one. Alternatives include the intrinsic Fréchet mean under the geodesic distance
(
1
)
and the radial projection of the componentwise posterior mean. Under a unimodal posterior on the hemisphere, these summaries asymptotically coincide. They can diverge near the identifiability boundary
\gamma\approx 0
, where the posterior on
\theta
is diffuse, and no single summary is informative. Step 3(a) accordingly recommends not reporting
\hat{\theta}
in this regime.

Boundary stability diagnostic.

A simple concentration index is
\lambda_{\max}(M)\in[1/q,1]
.
Values near
1
indicate a concentrated posterior over directions, while values near
1/q
indicate near-uniform dispersion.
We recommend reporting
\lambda_{\max}(M)
alongside
\hat{\theta}
to communicate whether the boundary is sharply learned or only weakly identified.

Subgroup membership uncertainty.

For any covariate vector
z
, define the posterior hard-membership probability for the scientific subgroup rule by
q(z):=\Pi(z^{\top}\theta\geq 0\mid\mathcal{D}_{n})
,
estimated by Monte Carlo as
q(z)\approx M^{-1}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathbbm{1}\{z^{\top}\theta^{(m)}\geq 0\}
.
We write
\bar{q}:=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}q(Z_{i})
for its sample mean over the observed covariates.
Reporting
q(z)
, or its distribution over the observed sample, conveys uncertainty in subgroup assignment without forcing a brittle hard classification.

Effect reporting.

Finally, report posterior summaries of the subgroup-specific incremental effect
\Delta(\gamma)
(and, if desired, the implied effect inside/outside the subgroup when
X
is included in
W
),
together with posterior probabilities of clinically meaningful benefit within the subgroup and uncertainty in membership.
This yields a coherent summary in which (i) evidence for heterogeneity is established first and (ii) boundary interpretation is conditioned on that evidence.
This reporting strategy is illustrated in Section
6
.

4.1
Why the frequentist analogue is hard to calibrate.

An analogous “test-then-report” strategy can be articulated frequentistically (e.g., test for heterogeneity and, if significant, report an estimated boundary
(
5
, e.g.,)
),
but in hard-threshold change-plane problems this approach is hard to calibrate because it couples two nonstandard steps:
(i) inference on
\gamma
in the presence of an estimated, potentially weakly identified boundary, and
(ii) inference on
\theta
after implicitly selecting a boundary and a subgroup.
Naively treating the estimated boundary as fixed after a pretest can lead to anti-conservative uncertainty quantification (a post-selection effect as discussed in
14
),
while bootstrap or Wald-type approximations can be unreliable under weak identification and nonregularity of hard thresholds.
More broadly, classical error-rate control does not directly encode clinically meaningful thresholds
\delta
or asymmetric costs of false subgroup discovery versus missed heterogeneity, which makes this protocol comparatively difficult to operationalize with standard frequentist workflows
In contrast, the Bayesian posterior provides a coherent probability statement
\Pi(H_{\delta}\mid\mathcal{D}_{n})
and a direct expected-loss rule,
while automatically propagating boundary uncertainty into effect and membership summaries through joint posterior sampling.

6
Empirical Data Example

6.1
Motivating application

The dataset for our empirical analysis comes from the PREMIER randomized clinical trial
(
29
)
, a multi-site study of lifestyle modification for blood pressure control among generally healthy adults with above-optimal blood pressure through stage 1 hypertension (
N=810
). Participants were not taking antihypertensive medication at baseline and were randomized to “Advice Only,” a multicomponent lifestyle intervention, or the same intervention augmented with counseling to adopt a structured dietary component. In this analysis, we combine the two active intervention groups as the treatment arm. The primary outcome is change in systolic blood pressure from baseline to 6 months, measured using standardized assessments by masked staff. Because race, age, baseline hypertension status, and sex are clinically relevant candidate effect modifiers in PREMIER, the trial provides a natural setting for detecting whether intervention benefit is concentrated in a data-learned subgroup defined by combinations of baseline profiles.

6.2
Primary analysis results

We analyze the PREMIER trial, following the reporting protocol in Section
4
.
We jointly estimate the treatment effect of lifestyle recommendations on the systolic blood pressure and identify the subgroup of people for whom the intervention is effective. Following
29
, we include self-reported race, sex, hypertension status, and age as the baseline covariates
W_{i}
and
Z_{i}
, and we also include the two-way interactions between them.
We use the same model classes considered in Section
5.2
(parametric and BART), specifying the baseline function

both parametrically and nonparametrically and sparsity-aware prior for the change-plane parameters; see Supplementary Material Section

We evaluate a prespecified finite-sample sensitivity grid

This intermediate choice corresponds to a gray-zone width of about

(

7
Conclusion

We developed a Bayesian framework for change-plane regression that delivers interpretable regularized subgroup boundaries together with principled uncertainty quantification. A smooth working likelihood replaces the brittle hard-threshold likelihood, restoring regularity for posterior simulation while making the fixed-
\tau
inferential target explicit: posterior summaries concentrate around a smoothed pseudo-true parameter, and hard-threshold interpretation requires the additional vanishing-smoothing conditions characterized in the theory. The resulting bias–regularity trade-off is governed by the geometry of the covariate distribution near the boundary. Together with the decision-theoretic reporting protocol, the framework yields a coherent workflow for deciding when a subgroup boundary should be reported and for summarizing it with uncertainty once clinically meaningful heterogeneity is supported by the data. Finally, the framework is modular rather than tied to the Gaussian parametric baseline emphasized here. The same augmentation device can be combined with semiparametric baselines, sparsity priors for high-dimensional effect modifiers, and outcome-specific likelihoods for generalized linear
(
10
)
, survival
(
33
)
, or other structured settings
(
34
, e.g.,)
. Extending the present theory to those broader models while preserving the interpretability of the reporting protocol is a natural next step.

Appendix A
Semiparametric Extension and Posterior Inference

A.1
Semiparametric baseline for robustness

A key advantage of our Bayesian change-plane framework over standard frequentist approaches is its adaptability to more advanced settings.
A practically important extension is semiparametric specification that relaxes the linear baseline
W^{\top}\beta
in (
1
) to an unknown regression surface
\mu_{0}(W)
. This generalization is motivated by the fact that misspecification of the baseline mean can distort subgroup detection, because the gate
\mathbbm{1}\{Z^{\top}\theta_{0}\geq 0\}
is identified through contrasts in
Y
that must be separated from systematic variation explained by
W
. Concretely, we consider the hard-threshold DGP:
Y=\mu_{0}(W)+X^{\top}\gamma_{0}\,\mathbbm{1}\{Z^{\top}\theta_{0}\geq 0\}+\varepsilon,

where
\|\theta_{0}\|_{2}=1
and
\mu_{0}
is an unknown function on the support of
W
, and
\varepsilon
is mean-zero noise as in Section
2
.
This is a widely adopted form of the change-plane regression model considered in the literature
(
5
;
12
, e.g., )
.
To retain computational tractability while preserving the interpretation of the change-plane component, we adopt the same data-augmentation strategy and posit the working model

Y\mid(D,W,X,Z)\sim\mathcal{N}\big(\mu(W)+X^{\top}\gamma D,\sigma^{2}\big),\quad D\mid Z\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}\big(\pi_{\theta}(Z)\big),

(5)

where
\mu(\cdot)
is endowed with a flexible prior. Under (
5
),
\mu(W)
absorbs complex main effects of baseline covariates, while the treatment effect heterogeneity is still summarized by the low-dimensional regression
X^{\top}\gamma
and the subgroup partition induced by
\theta
.
A promising specification for
\mu(W)
is a Bayesian additive regression trees (BART) prior
(
9
)
.
For illustration, suppose we use a generic nonparametric prior that yields a tractable conditional update
\mu\mid\cdot
. Conditional on
(D,\gamma,\sigma^{2})
, the model reduces to a nonparametric regression of residualized outcomes
Y_{i}^{\dagger}:=Y_{i}-X_{i}^{\top}\gamma D_{i}

on
W_{i}
with Gaussian noise, so posterior computation can proceed by alternating: (a) an update of
\mu
given
\{(Y_{i}^{\dagger},W_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
; (b) a conjugate Gaussian update for
\gamma
given
(Y-\mu(W),X,D,\sigma^{2})
under a normal prior; (c) an inverse-gamma update (or other chosen prior update) for
\sigma^{2}
; and (d) the same hemispherical update for
\theta
as in Section
2.1
.
Under the BART specification of
\mu
, the residual variance
\sigma^{2}
in (
5
) is the same object as the residual variance maintained by BART internally; step (c) is then the BART scaled-inverse-
\chi^{2}
update of
3
, and a single source of truth for
\sigma^{2}
must be enforced at the implementation level to avoid double-counting.
See Supplementary Material Section
A
for details.

This semiparametric extension preserves the main advantages of our approach: the subgroup boundary remains interpretable through the linear score
Z_{i}^{\top}\theta
, the heterogeneous effect is summarized by
X_{i}\gamma
, and the nonparametric component
\mu(W)
offers robustness to baseline misspecification. Moreover, because posterior inference is joint, uncertainty in
\mu(W)
is automatically propagated into posterior summaries for
(\theta,\gamma)
, yielding coherent credible intervals and posterior probabilities for subgroup effects even when the baseline mean is complex.

We continue to use the probit-gated data augmentation as a working inferential engine. Fix a smoothing scale
\tau>0
and define
\pi_{\theta,\tau}(z)=\Phi(z^{\top}\theta/\tau)
. The working semiparametric model is
T_{i}\mid(Z_{i},\theta)\sim\mathcal{N}(Z_{i}^{\top}\theta,\tau^{2})
,
D_{i}=\mathbbm{1}\{T_{i}\geq 0\}
, and
Y_{i}\mid(D_{i},W_{i},X_{i},\mu,\gamma,\sigma^{2})\sim\mathcal{N}\big(\mu(W_{i})+X_{i}^{\top}\gamma\,D_{i},\sigma^{2}\big)
,
with
\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{q-1}_{+}
and
\sigma^{2}>0
. Conditional on
(W_{i},X_{i},Z_{i})
, integrating out
T_{i}
yields the mixture representation
p(y\mid w,x,z)=(1-\pi_{\theta,\tau}(z))\,\varphi_{\sigma}(y-\mu(w))+\pi_{\theta,\tau}(z)\,\varphi_{\sigma}(y-\mu(w)-x^{\top}\gamma)
,
where
\varphi_{\sigma}(u)=(2\pi\sigma^{2})^{-1/2}\exp\{-u^{2}/(2\sigma^{2})\}
.

A.2
Generic posterior sampling scheme

Posterior computation alternates between the latent gate variables
(D,T)
, the parametric block
(\gamma,\sigma^{2},\theta)
, and the nonparametric baseline
\mu
. Write
\mu_{i}=\mu(W_{i})
and define the residualized outcomes
Y_{i}^{\dagger}:=Y_{i}-X_{i}^{\top}\gamma\,D_{i}
.
A single MCMC iteration proceeds as follows.

1.

Update
D_{i}
given
(\mu,\gamma,\sigma^{2},\theta)
.

For each
i
, the full conditional is Bernoulli with success probability

\Pr(D_i=1\mid\cdot)=\frac{\pi_{\theta,\tau}(Z_{i})\,\varphi_{\sigma}\!\big(Y_{i}-\mu_{i}-X_{i}^{\top}\gamma\big)}{(1-\pi_{\theta,\tau}(Z_{i}))\,\varphi_{\sigma}\!\big(Y_{i}-\mu_{i}\big)+\pi_{\theta,\tau}(Z_{i})\,\varphi_{\sigma}\!\big(Y_{i}-\mu_{i}-X_{i}^{\top}\gamma\big)}.

2.

Update
T_{i}
given
(D_{i},\theta)
.

For each
i
, sample

\displaystyle T_{i}\mid(D_{i}=1,\theta,Z_{i})\sim\mathcal{TN}(Z_{i}^{\top}\theta,\tau^{2},0,\infty),\quad T_{i}\mid(D_{i}=0,\theta,Z_{i})\sim\mathcal{TN}(Z_{i}^{\top}\theta,\tau^{2},-\infty,0),

where
\mathcal{TN}(m,s,l,u)
denotes the truncated normal distribution with the local parameter
m
, the scale parameter
s
, the lower and upper bound
l
,
u
.

3.

Update
\gamma
given
(D,\mu,\sigma^{2})
.

Let
\widetilde{X}_{i}:=D_{i}X_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
and define the working regression
Y_{i}-\mu_{i}=\widetilde{X}_{i}^{\top}\gamma+\varepsilon_{i}
,
\varepsilon_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
.
Under a Gaussian prior
\gamma\sim\mathcal{N}(m_{\gamma},V_{\gamma})
, the full conditional is
\gamma\mid\cdot\sim\mathcal{N}(m_{\gamma\mid\cdot},V_{\gamma\mid\cdot})
with

\displaystyle V_{\gamma\mid\cdot}=\Big(V_{\gamma}^{-1}+\sigma^{-2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widetilde{X}_{i}\widetilde{X}_{i}^{\top}\Big)^{-1},m_{\gamma\mid\cdot}=V_{\gamma\mid\cdot}\Big(V_{\gamma}^{-1}m_{\gamma}+\sigma^{-2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widetilde{X}_{i}(Y_{i}-\mu_{i})\Big).

We fix
m_{\gamma}=0.0

V_{\gamma}=100.0
throughout.

4.

Update
\sigma^{2}
given
(D,\mu,\gamma)
.

With an inverse-gamma prior
\sigma^{2}\sim\mathrm{InvGamma}(\sigma_{a},\sigma_{b})
, the full conditional is

\sigma^{2}\mid\cdot\sim\mathrm{InvGamma}\!\left(\sigma_{a}+\frac{n}{2},\ \sigma_{b}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(Y_{i}-\mu_{i}-\widetilde{X}_{i}^{\top}\gamma\big)^{2}\right).

We fix
\sigma_{a}=2.0
,
\sigma_{b}=1.0
throughout.

5.

Update
\theta
given
T
.

This step is unchanged by the semiparametric baseline. Conditional on
T
, the relevant likelihood term is Gaussian,
T_{i}\mid(Z_{i},\theta)\sim\mathcal{N}(Z_{i}^{\top}\theta,\tau^{2})
,
with
\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{q-1}_{+}
. Under the uniform prior on
\mathbb{S}^{q-1}_{+}
(or the sparsity-inducing alternatives in Section
2.2
), we update
\theta
using the great-circle elliptical slice sampler described in Section
2.1
, with log target proportional to
-\frac{1}{2\tau^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(T_{i}-Z_{i}^{\top}\theta)^{2}
,
and with proposals restricted to the hemisphere via truncation of the log target outside
\mathbb{S}^{q-1}_{+}
.

6.

Update
\mu
given
(Y^{\dagger},W,\sigma^{2})
.

Given
(D,\gamma)
, the model reduces to a nonparametric Gaussian regression of
Y^{\dagger}
on
W
,
Y_{i}^{\dagger}=\mu(W_{i})+\varepsilon_{i}
,
\varepsilon_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
,
so the
\mu
-update depends only on the chosen prior for
\mu(\cdot)
.

A.3
BART

A.3.1
Semiparametric specifications

BART represents the baseline mean as a sum of
m
regression trees,
\mu(W)=\sum_{t=1}^{m}g(W;\mathcal{T}_{t},\mathcal{M}_{t})
,
where each tree
\mathcal{T}_{t}
defines a partition of the predictor space and
\mathcal{M}_{t}
collects terminal-node means. Given
(\mathcal{T}_{t},\mathcal{M}_{t})_{t=1}^{m}
, the baseline vector is
f_{i}=\mu(W_{i})=\sum_{t=1}^{m}g(W_{i};\mathcal{T}_{t},\mathcal{M}_{t})
, and
Y_{i}^{\dagger}\mid(\mu,\sigma^{2})\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu(W_{i}),\sigma^{2})
.

A.3.2
Posterior inference

The BART update uses the standard Bayesian backfitting strategy, conditional on
(Y^{\dagger},W,\sigma^{2})
:

1.

For
t=1,\dots,m
, form the partial residuals
R_{i}^{(t)}=Y_{i}^{\dagger}-\sum_{\ell\neq t}g(W_{i};\mathcal{T}_{\ell},\mathcal{M}_{\ell})
.

2.

Update the tree structure
\mathcal{T}_{t}
.

Propose a local modification of
\mathcal{T}_{t}
(e.g., grow or prune a terminal node) and accept or reject the move by a Metropolis–Hastings step based on the integrated likelihood obtained by analytically integrating out terminal-node means under their Gaussian prior.

3.

Update terminal-node means
\mathcal{M}_{t}
.

Conditional on
\mathcal{T}_{t}
, each terminal node
b
collects observations
\{i:W_{i}\in b\}
. Under a Gaussian leaf prior
\mu_{b}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{\mu}^{2})
and Gaussian noise variance
\sigma^{2}
, the full conditional is

\displaystyle\mu_{b}\mid\cdot

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}(m_{b\mid\cdot},V_{b\mid\cdot}),\qquad V_{b\mid\cdot}=\Big(\sigma_{\mu}^{-2}+\frac{n_{b}}{\sigma^{2}}\Big)^{-1},\qquad m_{b\mid\cdot}=V_{b\mid\cdot}\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\sum_{i:W_{i}\in b}R_{i}^{(t)},

where
n_{b}
is the number of observations in node
b
.

4.

After updating
(\mathcal{T}_{t},\mathcal{M}_{t})
, refresh the fitted values of tree
t
and proceed to
t+1
.

After all trees are updated, set
\mu(W_{i})=\sum_{t=1}^{m}g(W_{i};\mathcal{T}_{t},\mathcal{M}_{t})
and return to the generic sampler steps for
(D,T,\gamma,\sigma^{2},\theta)
.

A.3.3
Prior specifications

The BART prior is characterized by: (i) the number of trees
m
; (ii) a depth-penalizing splitting rule
\Pr(\text{split at depth }d)=\alpha_{\mathrm{tree}}(1+d)^{-\beta_{\mathrm{tree}}}
, which favors shallow trees and regularizes
\mu(\cdot)
; (iii) a choice of splitting variable and cutpoint distribution (typically uniform among available predictors and observed cutpoints); and (iv) a Gaussian prior on leaf means,
\mu_{b}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{\mu}^{2})
. Following standard scaling heuristics,
\sigma_{\mu}
is chosen as
\sigma_{\mu}=c/(k\sqrt{m})
after putting
Y^{\dagger}
on an interpretable scale, where
k>0
controls shrinkage and
c
is proportional to a marginal scale of
Y^{\dagger}
(e.g., its empirical standard deviation). These defaults yield a weakly informative prior that keeps individual trees small while allowing the ensemble to represent complex regression surfaces. We fix
(m,\alpha_{\mathrm{tree}},\beta_{\mathrm{tree}},k,c)=(200,0.95,2.0,2.0,1.0)
in our simulations and analysis for stable mixing.

Appendix B
Sieve prior and sieve asymptotics

To facilitate theoretical development of our Bayesian model on a general noncompact parameter space
\Theta
in the main manuscript, we introduce here an increasing sequence of compact sieve sets to obtain uniform empirical-process bounds and sieve-uniform tests without imposing global boundedness.
Let
B_{\beta,n}\uparrow\infty
,
B_{\gamma,n}\uparrow\infty
,
\underline{\sigma}_{n}^{2}\downarrow 0
, and
\overline{\sigma}_{n}^{2}\uparrow\infty
be deterministic sequences with

0<\underline{\sigma}_{n}^{2}\leq\overline{\sigma}_{n}^{2}<\infty
for each
n
and define
\Theta_{n}=\Big\{(\beta,\gamma,\theta,\sigma^{2}):\|\beta\|\leq B_{\beta,n},\ \|\gamma\|\leq B_{\gamma,n},\ \theta\in\mathbb{S}^{q-1}_{+},\ \underline{\sigma}_{n}^{2}\leq\sigma^{2}\leq\overline{\sigma}_{n}^{2}\Big\}
.
Let
\underline{\sigma}_{n}=\sqrt{\underline{\sigma}_{n}^{2}}
and
\overline{\sigma}_{n}=\sqrt{\overline{\sigma}_{n}^{2}}
, so that

\underline{\sigma}_{n}\downarrow 0
,
\overline{\sigma}_{n}\uparrow\infty
, and
\underline{\sigma}_{n}\leq\sigma\leq\overline{\sigma}_{n}
is equivalent to

\underline{\sigma}_{n}^{2}\leq\sigma^{2}\leq\overline{\sigma}_{n}^{2}
.
Each
\Theta_{n}
is compact and
\Theta_{n}\uparrow\Theta
.
Let

S_{n}=\max\Big\{\underline{\sigma}_{n}^{-1},\ \overline{\sigma}_{n},\ B_{\beta,n}\underline{\sigma}_{n}^{-1},\ B_{\gamma,n}\underline{\sigma}_{n}^{-1}\Big\}
, and
L_{n}=\max\{S_{n},\tau_{n}^{-1}\}

To facilitate sieve-uniform arguments, define the truncated sieve prior and the corresponding sieve posterior
(
27
;
8
, e.g., )

\Pi_{n}(A)=\frac{\Pi(A\cap\Theta_{n})}{\Pi(\Theta_{n})},\qquad\Pi_{n}^{(\tau)}(A\mid\mathcal{D}_{n})=\frac{\int_{\Theta_{n}\cap A}\exp\{R_{n,\tau}(\eta)\}\,\Pi_{n}(d\eta)}{\int_{\Theta_{n}}\exp\{R_{n,\tau}(\eta)\}\,\Pi_{n}(d\eta)}.

Working on
\Theta_{n}
is convenient because it localizes the parameter space, making it substantially easier to verify the uniform regularity conditions needed for the testing and LAN arguments, and to control remainder terms in the likelihood expansion. We first establish posterior contraction and a BvM theorem for the sieve posterior. These results parallel some prior frequentist results that formulated and analyzed the model on a compact parameter space from the outset
(
21
, e.g., )
.
Our asymptotic results in Section
3
are derived by transfering the results from the sieve posterior to the fixed-prior posterior on the general, noncompact space
\Theta
. This is done by showing that the fixed-prior posterior assigns asymptotically negligible mass to
\Theta\setminus\Theta_{n}
(under suitable tail conditions on
\Pi
), so that truncation of the prior has no asymptotic effect on the posterior law in the local neighborhoods relevant for contraction and the BvM limit.

Lemma 1

(Existence of a pseudo-true parameter)
.

Fix
\tau>0
and write
K_{\tau}(\eta)=\mathbb{E}_{P_{0}}[-\ell_{\tau}(\eta;O)]
.
Assume that
K_{\tau}(\eta)
is finite and continuous on
\Theta
, and that it is coercive with respect to
T(\eta)=\|\beta\|+\|\gamma\|+\sigma+\sigma^{-1}
,
\sigma=\sqrt{\sigma^{2}}
,
in the sense that
\inf_{\eta:\,T(\eta)\geq t}K_{\tau}(\eta)\uparrow\infty
as
t\uparrow\infty
.
Then there exists
\eta^{\star}\in\Theta
such that
K_{\tau}(\eta^{\star})=\inf_{\eta\in\Theta}K_{\tau}(\eta)
.

Proof.

Let
K_{\inf}=\inf_{\eta\in\Theta}K_{\tau}(\eta)\in\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}
.
Since
K_{\tau}
is finite everywhere,
K_{\inf}>-\infty
.
Choose a sequence
(\eta_{m})_{m\geq 1}\subset\Theta
such that
K_{\tau}(\eta_{m})\downarrow K_{\inf}
.

Fix
c>K_{\inf}
and consider the sublevel set
\mathcal{L}_{c}=\{\eta\in\Theta:K_{\tau}(\eta)\leq c\}
.
By coercivity, there exists
t<\infty
such that
\mathcal{L}_{c}\subset\{\eta:T(\eta)\leq t\}
.
The set
\{\eta:T(\eta)\leq t\}
is compact in
\Theta
because:
(i)
\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{q-1}_{+}
is compact;
(ii)
T(\eta)\leq t
implies
\|\beta\|\leq t
and
\|\gamma\|\leq t
;
(iii)
T(\eta)\leq t
implies
\sigma+\sigma^{-1}\leq t
, hence
\sigma\in[\underline{\sigma}(t),\overline{\sigma}(t)]
for some

0<\underline{\sigma}(t)\leq\overline{\sigma}(t)<\infty
, so
\sigma^{2}
is bounded away from
0
and
\infty
.
Therefore
\mathcal{L}_{c}
is a closed subset of a compact set, hence compact.

For
m
large enough,
\eta_{m}\in\mathcal{L}_{c}
, so by compactness there exists a convergent subsequence

\eta_{m_{k}}\to\eta^{\star}\in\mathcal{L}_{c}
.
By continuity of
K_{\tau}
,
K_{\tau}(\eta^{\star})=\lim_{k\to\infty}K_{\tau}(\eta_{m_{k}})=K_{\inf}
.
∎

Lemma 2

(Uniform
L^{2}
envelopes and Lipschitz modulus on sieves)
.

Assume Assumption
2
(v)
and fix
\tau>0
.
For each
n
, let
\Theta_{n}
be as in Section
2
and fix
\eta^{\star}
.
Define, for
O=(Y,W,X,Z)
,

r_{\eta}(O)=\log\frac{p_{\eta,\tau}(Y\mid W,X,Z)}{p_{\eta^{\star},\tau}(Y\mid W,X,Z)}.

Then, for each fixed
n
:

(i)

There exists a nonnegative random variable
F_{n}(O)
such that
\sup_{\eta\in\Theta_{n}}|r_{\eta}(O)|\leq F_{n}(O)
a.s., with
\mathbb{E}_{P_{0}}[F_{n}(O)^{2}]<\infty
and
\sup_{\eta\in\Theta_{n}}\mathbb{E}_{P_{\eta,\tau}}[F_{n}(O)^{2}]<\infty
.

(ii)

There exists a nonnegative random variable
G_{n}(O)
such that for all
\eta,\eta^{\prime}\in\Theta_{n}
,
|r_{\eta}(O)-r_{\eta^{\prime}}(O)|\leq\|\eta-\eta^{\prime}\|\,G_{n}(O)
a.s.,
and
\mathbb{E}_{P_{0}}[G_{n}(O)^{2}]<\infty
,
\sup_{\eta\in\Theta_{n}}\mathbb{E}_{P_{\eta,\tau}}[G_{n}(O)^{2}]<\infty
.

Proof.

Fix
n
and define the sieve scale
S_{n}=\max\{\underline{\sigma}_{n}^{-1},\overline{\sigma}_{n},B_{\beta,n}\underline{\sigma}_{n}^{-1},B_{\gamma,n}\underline{\sigma}_{n}^{-1}\}
.
All constants below may depend on
(S_{n},\tau,\kappa)
, but not on
\eta\in\Theta_{n}
or on
O
.

Step 0: moment bounds under
P_{0}
and uniformly over
\{P_{\eta,\tau}:\eta\in\Theta_{n}\}
.

By Assumption
2
(v)
,
(W,X,Z)
is sub-Gaussian, so
\|W\|,\|X\|,\|Z\|
have finite moments of all orders.
Also,
\varepsilon
is unconditionally sub-Gaussian since

\mathbb{E}e^{t\varepsilon}=\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}(e^{t\varepsilon}\mid W,X,Z)\big]\leq e^{\kappa^{2}t^{2}/2}.

Hence, under
P_{0}
, the response
Y
in (
1
) has finite moments of all orders.

Under
P_{\eta,\tau}
with
\eta\in\Theta_{n}
, conditional on
(W,X,Z)
,
Y
has the two-component Gaussian mixture law in
(
2
) with component means
m_{0}=W^{\top}\beta
and
m_{1}=W^{\top}\beta+X^{\top}\gamma
, and variance
\sigma^{2}\in[\underline{\sigma}_{n}^{2},\overline{\sigma}_{n}^{2}]
.
Therefore, for each integer
m\geq 1
there exists
C_{m}<\infty
such that

\sup_{\eta\in\Theta_{n}}\mathbb{E}_{P_{\eta,\tau}}\big[|Y|^{m}\mid W,X,Z\big]\leq C_{m}\big(1+|m_{0}|^{m}+|m_{1}|^{m}+\overline{\sigma}_{n}^{m}\big).

Since
\|\beta\|\leq B_{\beta,n}
and
\|\gamma\|\leq B_{\gamma,n}
on
\Theta_{n}
, we have

|m_{0}|\leq B_{\beta,n}\|W\|
and
|m_{1}|\leq B_{\beta,n}\|W\|+B_{\gamma,n}\|X\|
,
so integrating over the common marginal
P_{0}^{WXZ}
yields

\sup_{\eta\in\Theta_{n}}\mathbb{E}_{P_{\eta,\tau}}|Y|^{m}<\infty
for
m\geq 1
.

Step 1: a uniform envelope for
\sup_{\eta\in\Theta_{n}}|\log p_{\eta,\tau}|
.

Write
p_{\eta,\tau}=(1-\pi)f_{0}+\pi f_{1}
where
\pi=\pi_{\theta}(Z)=\Phi(Z^{\top}\theta/\tau)
and

f_{0}=\varphi_{\sigma}(Y-m_{0}),\qquad f_{1}=\varphi_{\sigma}(Y-m_{1}),\qquad m_{0}=W^{\top}\beta,\qquad m_{1}=W^{\top}\beta+X^{\top}\gamma.

Since
a\vee b\leq a+b\leq 2(a\vee b)
for
a,b>0
,

\max\{\log((1-\pi)f_0),\log(\pi f_1)\}\leq\log p_{\eta,\tau}\leq\max\{\log((1-\pi)f_0),\log(\pi f_1)\}+\log 2.

Thus it suffices to bound
\log f_{k}
and
\log\pi
,
\log(1-\pi)
.

For
k\in\{0,1\}
,

\log f_{k}=-\tfrac{1}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^2)-\frac{(Y-m_{k})^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}},

so using
\sigma^{2}\in[\underline{\sigma}_{n}^{2},\overline{\sigma}_{n}^{2}]
,

|\log f_{k}|\leq C\Big(1+\log\overline{\sigma}_{n}^{2}+\underline{\sigma}_{n}^{-2}(Y-m_{k})^{2}\Big).

Moreover, uniformly on
\Theta_{n}
,

(Y-m_{k})^{2}\leq C\big(Y^{2}+B_{\beta,n}^{2}\|W\|^{2}+B_{\gamma,n}^{2}\|X\|^{2}\big).

For the probit terms, there exist
c_{0},c_{1}<\infty
such that for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
,

-\log\Phi(t)\leq c_{0}+c_{1}t^{2},\qquad-\log(1-\Phi(t))\leq c_{0}+c_{1}t^{2}.

With
t=Z^{\top}\theta/\tau
and
\|\theta\|_{2}=1
, we have
t^{2}\leq\|Z\|^{2}/\tau^{2}
, hence

\sup_{\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{q-1}_{+}}\max\{-\log\pi_{\theta}(Z),-\log(1-\pi_\theta(Z))\}\leq C_{\tau}(1+\|Z\|^{2}).

Combining these displays yields a constant
C<\infty
such that

\sup_{\eta\in\Theta_{n}}|\log p_{\eta,\tau}(Y\mid W,X,Z)|\leq C\Big(1+\log\overline{\sigma}_{n}^{2}+\underline{\sigma}_{n}^{-2}\big(Y^{2}+B_{\beta,n}^{2}\|W\|^{2}+B_{\gamma,n}^{2}\|X\|^{2}\big)+\|Z\|^{2}\Big)=:\bar{F}_{n}(O).

Since
r_{\eta}=\log p_{\eta,\tau}-\log p_{\eta^{\star},\tau}
and
\eta^{\star}\in\Theta_{n}
for all large
n
,

\sup_{\eta\in\Theta_{n}}|r_{\eta}(O)|\leq 2\bar{F}_{n}(O).

Set
F_{n}(O)=2\bar{F}_{n}(O)
. Step 0 implies
\mathbb{E}_{P_{0}}[F_{n}(O)^{2}]<\infty
and

\sup_{\eta\in\Theta_{n}}\mathbb{E}_{P_{\eta,\tau}}[F_{n}(O)^{2}]<\infty
, proving (i).

Step 2: a uniform Lipschitz modulus for
r_{\eta}
on
\Theta_{n}
.

Define the convex enlargement

\bar{\Theta}_{n}=\Big\{(\beta,\gamma,\theta,\sigma^{2}):\|\beta\|\leq B_{\beta,n},\ \|\gamma\|\leq B_{\gamma,n},\ \|\theta\|_{2}\leq 1,\ \theta_{1}\geq 0,\ \underline{\sigma}_{n}^{2}\leq\sigma^{2}\leq\overline{\sigma}_{n}^{2}\Big\}.

Then
\bar{\Theta}_{n}
is compact and convex, and
\Theta_{n}\subset\bar{\Theta}_{n}
.
For fixed
O
,
\xi\mapsto\log p_{\xi,\tau}(Y\mid W,X,Z)
is continuously differentiable on
\bar{\Theta}_{n}
(because
\tau>0
is fixed),
so for any
\eta,\eta^{\prime}\in\Theta_{n}
, the mean-value theorem yields

|\log p_{\eta,\tau}-\log p_{\eta^{\prime},\tau}|\leq\|\eta-\eta^{\prime}\|\sup_{\xi\in\bar{\Theta}_{n}}\|\nabla_{\eta}\log p_{\xi,\tau}(Y\mid W,X,Z)\|.

Define

G_{n}(O)=\sup_{\xi\in\bar{\Theta}_{n}}\|\nabla_{\eta}\log p_{\xi,\tau}(Y\mid W,X,Z)\|.

Then
|r_{\eta}-r_{\eta^{\prime}}|\leq\|\eta-\eta^{\prime}\|G_{n}
a.s.

It remains to bound
G_{n}
in
L^{2}
under
P_{0}
and uniformly under
P_{\eta,\tau}
for
\eta\in\Theta_{n}
.
Let
\xi=(\beta,\gamma,\theta,\sigma^{2})\in\bar{\Theta}_{n}
, set
t=Z^{\top}\theta/\tau
, and let
\omega_{\xi}=\omega_{\xi}(Y,W,X,Z)\in[0,1]
be the responsibility.
Differentiation gives

\nabla_{\beta}\log p_{\xi,\tau}=\frac{Y-W^{\top}\beta-\omega_{\xi}X^{\top}\gamma}{\sigma^{2}}W,\qquad\nabla_{\gamma}\log p_{\xi,\tau}=\frac{\omega_{\xi}(Y-W^{\top}\beta-X^{\top}\gamma)}{\sigma^{2}}X,

so using
\sigma^{2}\geq\underline{\sigma}_{n}^{2}
and
\|\beta\|\leq B_{\beta,n}
,
\|\gamma\|\leq B_{\gamma,n}
,

\|\nabla_{\beta}\log p_{\xi,\tau}\|\leq C\underline{\sigma}_{n}^{-2}\big(|Y|+B_{\beta,n}\|W\|+B_{\gamma,n}\|X\|\big)\|W\|,

\|\nabla_{\gamma}\log p_{\xi,\tau}\|\leq C\underline{\sigma}_{n}^{-2}\big(|Y|+B_{\beta,n}\|W\|+B_{\gamma,n}\|X\|\big)\|X\|.

For the gate, note that
\nabla_{\theta}\log p_{\xi,\tau}=(\partial_{t}\log p_{\xi,\tau})\,Z/\tau
and

\partial_{t}\log p_{\xi,\tau}=\phi(t)\frac{\omega_{\xi}-\Phi(t)}{\Phi(t)(1-\Phi(t))}.

Using
|\omega_{\xi}-\Phi(t)|\leq 1
and standard Mills ratio bounds, there exists
C<\infty
such that

\frac{\phi(t)}{\Phi(t)(1-\Phi(t))}\leq C(1+|t|),\qquad t\in\mathbb{R}.

Therefore
|\partial_{t}\log p_{\xi,\tau}|\leq C(1+|t|)
.
Since
\|\theta\|_{2}\leq 1
on
\bar{\Theta}_{n}
,
|t|\leq\|Z\|/\tau
, so

\|\nabla_{\theta}\log p_{\xi,\tau}\|\leq\frac{1}{\tau}|\partial_{t}\log p_{\xi,\tau}|\,\|Z\|\leq C_{\tau}(\|Z\|+\|Z\|^{2}).

For
\sigma^{2}
,

\partial_{\sigma^{2}}\log p_{\xi,\tau}=-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}+\frac{(1-\omega_{\xi})(Y-m_{0})^{2}+\omega_{\xi}(Y-m_{1})^{2}}{2\sigma^{4}},

hence, using
\sigma^{2}\geq\underline{\sigma}_{n}^{2}
and the same bound on
(Y-m_{k})^{2}
as in Step 1,

|\partial_{\sigma^{2}}\log p_{\xi,\tau}|\leq C\Big(\underline{\sigma}_{n}^{-2}+\underline{\sigma}_{n}^{-4}\big(Y^{2}+B_{\beta,n}^{2}\|W\|^{2}+B_{\gamma,n}^{2}\|X\|^{2}\big)\Big).

Combining these bounds shows that
G_{n}(O)
is dominated by a polynomial in
|Y|,\|W\|,\|X\|,\|Z\|
of degree at most
2
,
with coefficients depending only on
(S_{n},\tau)
.
Step 0 implies that this polynomial has finite fourth moment under
P_{0}
and uniformly over
\{P_{\eta,\tau}:\eta\in\Theta_{n}\}
, so

\mathbb{E}_{P_{0}}[G_{n}(O)^{2}]<\infty,\qquad\sup_{\eta\in\Theta_{n}}\mathbb{E}_{P_{\eta,\tau}}[G_{n}(O)^{2}]<\infty.

This proves (ii).
∎

Lemma 3

(Local
L^{2}
envelope for score and local

Assume Assumption
2
(v)
and fix
\tau>0
.
There exist
\delta>0
and nonnegative random variables

with

\mathbb{E}_{P_{0}}[F_{1}(O)^{2}]<\infty

\delta>0
so that the closed ball

(M_{0},\underline{\sigma}_{0}^{2},\overline{\sigma}_{0}^{2})

\theta

\sup_{\|\tilde{\eta}-\tilde{\eta}^{\star}\|\leq\delta}\|\nabla^{2}\tilde{\ell}_{\tau}(\tilde{\eta};O)\|\leq P_{2}(|Y|,\|W\|,\|X\|,\|Z\|)=:F_{2}(O),

\tilde{\eta}=\tilde{\eta}^{\star}
.

(ii)

\bar{\Delta}_{K}:=\inf_{\eta:\ \|\eta-\eta^{\star}\|\geq\delta_{\eta}}\{K_{\tau}(\eta)-K_{\tau}(\eta^{\star})\}>0,

\delta_{0}(\varepsilon):=\inf_{\eta:\ \|\eta-\eta^{\star}\|\geq\varepsilon}\{K_{\tau}(\eta)-K_{\tau}(\eta^{\star})\}\geq\min\{\underline{c}_{K}\varepsilon^{2},\bar{\Delta}_{K}\}>0.

(ii)

(i)
,

The bound for
\delta_{0}(\varepsilon)
follows exactly as in the original argument by splitting into

\{\varepsilon\leq\|\eta-\eta^{\star}\|\leq\delta_{\eta}\}
and
\{\|\eta-\eta^{\star}\|\geq\delta_{\eta}\}
and taking the infimum.
∎

Lemma 5

(Exponentially consistent tests on sieves)
.

Assume Assumption
2
. Fix
\varepsilon>0
and define

\Theta_{n,\varepsilon}=\{\eta\in\Theta_{n}:\|\eta-\eta^{\star}\|\geq\varepsilon\}
.
Let
\delta_{0}(\varepsilon)
be as in Lemma
4
and set
b=\delta_{0}(\varepsilon)/4
.
Define

T_{n}=\sup_{\eta\in\Theta_{n,\varepsilon}}R_{n}(\eta),\qquad\varphi_{n}=\mathbbm{1}\{T_{n}\geq-nb\}.

Then there exist constants
c_{1},c_{2}>0
such that, for all sufficiently large
n
,

\mathbb{E}_{P_{0}}\varphi_{n}\leq\exp\{-c_{1}n/S_{n}^{4}\},\qquad\sup_{\eta\in\Theta_{n,\varepsilon}}\mathbb{E}_{P_{\eta,\tau}}(1-\varphi_{n})\leq\exp\{-c_{2}n/S_{n}^{4}\},

where
S_{n}=\max\{\underline{\sigma}_{n}^{-1},\overline{\sigma}_{n},B_{\beta,n}\underline{\sigma}_{n}^{-1},B_{\gamma,n}\underline{\sigma}_{n}^{-1}\}
.

Proof.

All constants below may depend on
(\varepsilon,\tau,p,r,q,\kappa)
.

Step 1: fixed-parameter Bernstein bounds.

Fix
n
and
\eta\in\Theta_{n}
.
By Lemma
2
(i),
|r_{\eta}(O)|\leq F_{n}(O)
with
\mathbb{E}_{P_{0}}[F_{n}(O)^{2}]<\infty
.
Under Assumption
2
(v)
, the random variables that enter
F_{n}(O)
(polynomials in
|Y|,\|W\|,\|X\|,\|Z\|
)
are sub-exponential, hence
r_{\eta}(O)-\mathbb{E}_{P_{0}}r_{\eta}(O)
is sub-exponential under
P_{0}
with
\psi_{1}
-norm bounded by
CS_{n}^{2}
,
uniformly over
\eta\in\Theta_{n}
.
Therefore, Bernstein’s inequality yields

P_{0}\Big(R_{n}(\eta)-\mathbb{E}_{P_{0}}R_{n}(\eta)\geq nb\Big)\leq 2\exp\{-cnb^{2}/S_{n}^{4}\}.

Since
\mathbb{E}_{P_{0}}R_{n}(\eta)=n\mathbb{E}_{P_{0}}r_{\eta}(O)=-n(K_{\tau}(\eta)-K_{\tau}(\eta^{\star}))\leq-n\delta_{0}(\varepsilon)
for
\eta\in\Theta_{n,\varepsilon}
,

P_{0}\big(R_{n}(\eta)\geq-2nb\big)\leq 2\exp\{-cn/S_{n}^{4}\}.

Similarly, under
P_{\eta,\tau}
,
r_{\eta}(O)-\mathbb{E}_{P_{\eta,\tau}}r_{\eta}(O)
is sub-exponential with
\psi_{1}
-norm
\leq CS_{n}^{2}
uniformly on
\Theta_{n}
.
Moreover,

\mathbb{E}_{P_{\eta,\tau}}r_{\eta}(O)=\mathbb{E}_{P_{\eta,\tau}}\!\left[\log\frac{p_{\eta,\tau}(Y\mid W,X,Z)}{p_{\eta^{\star},\tau}(Y\mid W,X,Z)}\right]=\mathrm{KL}(P_{\eta,\tau},P_{\eta^{\star},\tau})\geq 0.

Therefore,

\displaystyle P_{\eta,\tau}\big(R_{n}(\eta)\leq-nb\big)

\displaystyle\leq P_{\eta,\tau}\big(R_{n}(\eta)-\mathbb{E}_{P_{\eta,\tau}}R_{n}(\eta)\leq-nb\big)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\{-cn/S_{n}^{4}\}.

Step 2: type II error.

For any
\eta\in\Theta_{n,\varepsilon}
,

\{1-\varphi_{n}=1\}=\{T_{n}<-nb\}\subseteq\{R_{n}(\eta)<-nb\},

so

\sup_{\eta\in\Theta_{n,\varepsilon}}\mathbb{E}_{P_{\eta,\tau}}(1-\varphi_{n})\leq\sup_{\eta\in\Theta_{n,\varepsilon}}P_{\eta,\tau}(R_{n}(\eta)\leq-nb)\leq 2\exp\{-cn/S_{n}^{4}\}.

Step 3: type I error via covering and Lipschitz control.

Let
G_{n}(O)
be the Lipschitz modulus from Lemma
2
(ii), so that

|r_{\eta}(O)-r_{\eta^{\prime}}(O)|\leq\|\eta-\eta^{\prime}\|G_{n}(O)
on
\Theta_{n}
.
Let
m_{G,n}=\mathbb{E}_{P_{0}}G_{n}(O)<\infty
and set
\rho_{n}=b/(4m_{G,n})
.
Let
\{\eta_{1,n},\dots,\eta_{N_{n},n}\}
be a
\rho_{n}
-net of
\Theta_{n,\varepsilon}
.

For any
\eta\in\Theta_{n,\varepsilon}
, choose
j
with
\|\eta-\eta_{j,n}\|\leq\rho_{n}
and sum the Lipschitz bound to obtain

R_{n}(\eta)\leq R_{n}(\eta_{j,n})+\rho_{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}G_{n}(O_{i}),

hence

T_{n}\leq\max_{1\leq j\leq N_{n}}R_{n}(\eta_{j,n})+\rho_{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}G_{n}(O_{i}).

Define
E_{n}=\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}G_{n}(O_{i})\leq 2m_{G,n}\}
.
On
E_{n}
,
\rho_{n}\sum_{i}G_{n}(O_{i})\leq nb/2
, so

\{T_{n}\geq-nb\}\cap E_{n}\subseteq\left\{\max_{1\leq j\leq N_{n}}R_{n}(\eta_{j,n})\geq-\tfrac{3}{2}nb\right\}.

Therefore,

P_{0}(\varphi_{n}=1)\leq P_{0}\left(\max_{1\leq j\leq N_{n}}R_{n}(\eta_{j,n})\geq-\tfrac{3}{2}nb\right)+P_{0}(E_{n}^{c}).

By Step 1, uniformly in
j
,

P_{0}\left(R_{n}(\eta_{j,n})\geq-\tfrac{3}{2}nb\right)\leq 2\exp\{-cn/S_{n}^{4}\},

so a union bound gives

P_{0}\left(\max_{1\leq j\leq N_{n}}R_{n}(\eta_{j,n})\geq-\tfrac{3}{2}nb\right)\leq 2N_{n}\exp\{-cn/S_{n}^{4}\}.

A volumetric bound implies
\log N_{n}\lesssim d\log(S_n/\rho_n)
.
Moreover, Lemma
2
(ii) implies
m_{G,n}
grows at most polynomially in
S_{n}
(for fixed
\tau
),
so
\rho_{n}\geq cS_{n}^{-a}
for some
a<\infty
and all large
n
, hence
\log N_{n}\lesssim\log S_{n}
.
Assumption
2
(vi)
implies
\log N_{n}=o(n/S_{n}^{4})
, so
N_{n}\exp\{-cn/S_{n}^{4}\}\to 0
.

Finally,
G_{n}(O)
is sub-exponential under
P_{0}
with
\|G_{n}\|_{\psi_{1}}\lesssim S_{n}^{2}

(it is dominated by a polynomial in
|Y|,\|W\|,\|X\|,\|Z\|
with coefficients polynomial in
S_{n}
, and the inputs are sub-Gaussian).
Thus Bernstein’s inequality yields, for some
c>0
and all large
n
,

P_{0}(E_{n}^{c})=P_{0}\Big(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}G_{n}(O_{i})>2m_{G,n}\Big)\leq 2\exp\{-cn/S_{n}^{4}\}.

Combining bounds gives
\mathbb{E}_{P_{0}}\varphi_{n}\leq\exp\{-c_{1}n/S_{n}^{4}\}
for large
n
after adjusting constants.
∎

Lemma 6

(Local denominator lower bound)
.

Assume Assumption
2
(v)
and Assumption
2
(i)
, and fix
\tau>0
.
For every
\delta>0
there exist
\varepsilon>0
and constants
c,C>0
such that, for all sufficiently large
n

P_{0}\Big(\inf_{\eta\in\Theta_{n}:\ \|\eta-\eta^{\star}\|\leq\varepsilon}R_{n}(\eta)\geq-n\delta\Big)\geq 1-Ce^{-cn}.

In particular,

P_{0}\Big(\inf_{\eta\in\Theta_{n}:\ \|\eta-\eta^{\star}\|\leq\varepsilon}R_{n}(\eta)\geq-n\delta\Big)\to 1.

Fix
\delta>0
. By continuity of
K_{\tau}
at
\eta^{\star}
(Assumption
2
(i)

\varepsilon>0
such that

\sup_{\|\eta-\eta^{\star}\|\leq\varepsilon}\big(K_{\tau}(\eta)-K_{\tau}(\eta^{\star})\big)\leq\delta/4.

\|\eta-\eta^{\star}\|\leq\varepsilon

\mathbb{E}_{P_{0}}[r_{\eta}(O)]=-(K_{\tau}(\eta)-K_{\tau}(\eta^{\star}))\geq-\delta/4.

\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon}=\{\eta:\|\eta-\eta^{\star}\|\leq\varepsilon\}
.
As in Lemma
2
(ii), there exists a random variable
G(O)
such that

|r_{\eta}(O)-r_{\eta^{\prime}}(O)|\leq\|\eta-\eta^{\prime}\|G(O)\quad\text{a.s.},\qquad\forall\eta,\eta^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon},

and
G(O)
is sub-exponential under
P_{0}
(it is dominated by a polynomial in
|Y|,\|W\|,\|X\|,\|Z\|
, with
\tau
fixed).
Let
m_{G}=\mathbb{E}_{P_{0}}G(O)\in(0,\infty)
and set
\rho=\delta/(8m_{G})
.

Choose a finite
\rho
-net

of
\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon}
(here
N<\infty
does not depend on
n

For any
\eta
with
\|\eta-\eta_{j}\|\leq\rho

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{\eta}(O_{i})\geq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{\eta_{j}}(O_{i})-\rho\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}G(O_{i}).

Define
E_{n}=\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}G(O_{i})\leq 2m_{G}\}

On

\inf_{\eta\in\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{\eta}(O_{i})\geq\min_{1\leq j\leq N}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{\eta_{j}}(O_{i})-\delta/4.

P_{0}\left(\inf_{\eta\in\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{\eta}(O_{i})<-\delta\right)\leq C^{\prime}e^{-cn}.

Equivalently,
P_{0}(\inf_{\eta\in\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon}}R_{n}(\eta)\geq-n\delta)\geq 1-C^{\prime}e^{-cn}

For all large
n
,
\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon}\subset\Theta_{n}

∎

Lemma 7

(Uniform local asymptotic normality)
.

Assume Assumptions
2
. Define

\Delta_{n}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nabla\tilde{\ell}_{\tau}(\tilde{\eta}^{\star};O_{i}),

for
O_{i}=(Y_{i},W_{i},X_{i},Z_{i})
.
Then
\Delta_{n}\Rightarrow\mathcal{N}(0,J_{\tau}(\tilde{\eta}^{\star}))
under
P_{0}
.
Moreover, for every fixed
M<\infty
,

\sup_{\|h\|\leq M}\bigg|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big(\tilde{\ell}_{\tau}(\tilde{\eta}^{\star}+h/\sqrt{n};O_{i})-\tilde{\ell}_{\tau}(\tilde{\eta}^{\star};O_{i})\Big)-\Big(h^{\top}\Delta_{n}-\tfrac{1}{2}h^{\top}I_{\tau}(\tilde{\eta}^{\star})h\Big)\bigg|=o_{P_{0}}(1).

Proof.

We prove the lemma in several steps.

Step 1: CLT for
\Delta_{n}
.

Because
\tilde{\eta}^{\star}
minimizes
K_{\tau}(\tilde{\eta})=\mathbb{E}_{P_{0}}[-\tilde{\ell}_{\tau}(\tilde{\eta};O)]
and
K_{\tau}
is differentiable at
\tilde{\eta}^{\star}
,

\mathbb{E}_{P_{0}}[\nabla\tilde{\ell}_{\tau}(\tilde{\eta}^{\star};O)]=0.

Lemma
3
implies

\mathbb{E}_{P_{0}}\|\nabla\tilde{\ell}_{\tau}(\tilde{\eta}^{\star};O)\|^{2}<\infty
.
Thus the multivariate CLT gives

\Delta_{n}\Rightarrow\mathcal{N}(0,J_{\tau}(\tilde{\eta}^{\star})).

Step 2: uniform quadratic expansion.

Fix
M<\infty
and set
\tilde{\eta}_{n,h}=\tilde{\eta}^{\star}+h/\sqrt{n}
.
For each
i
, Taylor’s theorem gives

\tilde{\ell}_{\tau}(\tilde{\eta}_{n,h};O_{i})-\tilde{\ell}_{\tau}(\tilde{\eta}^{\star};O_{i})=\nabla\tilde{\ell}_{\tau}(\tilde{\eta}^{\star};O_{i})^{\top}\frac{h}{\sqrt{n}}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{h^{\top}\nabla^{2}\tilde{\ell}_{\tau}(\tilde{\eta}^{\star}+s_{i,n}(h)(\tilde{\eta}_{n,h}-\tilde{\eta}^{\star});O_{i})h}{n}

for some
s_{i,n}(h)\in(0,1)
.
Summing over
i
and subtracting
h^{\top}\Delta_{n}-\tfrac{1}{2}h^{\top}I_{\tau}(\tilde{\eta}^{\star})h
yields

\frac{1}{2}h^{\top}\Big(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nabla^{2}\tilde{\ell}_{\tau}(\tilde{\eta}^{\star};O_{i})-\mathbb{E}_{P_{0}}\nabla^{2}\tilde{\ell}_{\tau}(\tilde{\eta}^{\star};O)\Big)h+\frac{1}{2}h^{\top}\Big(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i,n}(h)\Big)h,

where

\Delta_{i,n}(h)=\nabla^{2}\tilde{\ell}_{\tau}(\tilde{\eta}^{\star}+s_{i,n}(h)(\tilde{\eta}_{n,h}-\tilde{\eta}^{\star});O_{i})-\nabla^{2}\tilde{\ell}_{\tau}(\tilde{\eta}^{\star};O_{i}).

For the first term, Lemma
3
gives an
L^{1}
envelope for
\|\nabla^{2}\tilde{\ell}_{\tau}(\tilde{\eta}^{\star};O)\|
, so the LLN implies it is
o_{P_{0}}(1)
uniformly over
\|h\|\leq M
.

For the second term, define, for
r>0
,

H_{r}(O)=\sup_{\|\tilde{\eta}-\tilde{\eta}^{\star}\|\leq r}\|\nabla^{2}\tilde{\ell}_{\tau}(\tilde{\eta};O)-\nabla^{2}\tilde{\ell}_{\tau}(\tilde{\eta}^{\star};O)\|.

For each fixed
O
,
H_{r}(O)\to 0
as
r\downarrow 0
by continuity of
\tilde{\eta}\mapsto\nabla^{2}\tilde{\ell}_{\tau}(\tilde{\eta};O)
(Assumption
2
(iii)
).
For
r\leq\delta
(with
\delta
from Lemma
3
),

H_{r}(O)\leq 2\sup_{\|\tilde{\eta}-\tilde{\eta}^{\star}\|\leq\delta}\|\nabla^{2}\tilde{\ell}_{\tau}(\tilde{\eta};O)\|\leq 2F_{2}(O),

and
\mathbb{E}_{P_{0}}[F_{2}(O)]<\infty
.
Thus
\mathbb{E}_{P_{0}}[H_{r}(O)]\to 0
by dominated convergence.

Let
r_{n}=M/\sqrt{n}
. Since
\|s_{i,n}(h)(\tilde{\eta}_{n,h}-\tilde{\eta}^{\star})\|\leq r_{n}
whenever
\|h\|\leq M
,

\sup_{\|h\|\leq M}\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i,n}(h)\right\|\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}H_{r_{n}}(O_{i}).

By Markov’s inequality,

\mathbb{E}_{P_{0}}\Big[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}H_{r_{n}}(O_{i})\Big]=\mathbb{E}_{P_{0}}[H_{r_{n}}(O)]\to 0,

so
(1/n)\sum_{i}H_{r_{n}}(O_{i})=o_{P_{0}}(1)
.
This proves the stated uniform LAN expansion.
∎

Lemma 9

(TV bound for sieve posterior)
.

Assume Assumption
2
together with Assumption
5
.
For any measurable
A\subset\Theta
,
\Pi_{n}^{(\tau)}(A\mid\mathcal{D}_{n})=\Pi^{(\tau)}(A\mid\mathcal{D}_{n},\eta\in\Theta_{n})=\Pi^{(\tau)}(A\cap\Theta_{n}\mid\mathcal{D}_{n})/\Pi^{(\tau)}(\Theta_{n}\mid\mathcal{D}_{n})

The identity follows by Bayes’ formula and

and let
Q_{n}=Q(\cdot\mid\Theta_{n})

P_{0}
-probability.

A_{m}=\Theta_{m}\setminus\Theta_{m-1}

\varphi_{n,m}

5

A_{n}^{\delta}=\Big\{\inf_{\|\eta-\eta^{\star}\|\leq\varepsilon}R_{n}(\eta)\geq-n\delta\Big\}\quad\text{satisfies}\quad P_{0}(A_{n}^{\delta})\geq 1-Ce^{-cn}.

(iv)
.

by Assumption
5
(i), the right-hand side converges to
0
for any fixed
\delta
.
Together with
P_{0}((A_{n}^{\delta})^{c})\to 0
and Step 1, this implies

\Pi^{(\tau)}(\Theta_{n}^{c}\mid\mathcal{D}_{n})\to 0
in
P_{0}
-probability.

Step 2: local Laplace approximation on

Fix

\Theta_{n}

\Theta_{n}

\Pi_{n,M}

h

\{\|h\|\leq M\}

Step 3: tail control in

Fix

P_{0}

On this event, Lemma

Since
\lambda_{\min}(I_{\tau}(\tilde{\eta}^{\star}))>0

and a standard comparison of numerator and denominator integrals yields

\lim_{M\to\infty}\ \limsup_{n\to\infty}P_{0}\Big(\Pi_{n}^{(\tau)}(\|h\|>M\mid\mathcal{D}_{n})>\epsilon\Big)=0.

The BvM conclusion implies that, for any sequence

because Gaussian tails vanish as

Lemma 10

(Gate approximation rate)
.

For
\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{q-1}_{+}
define
U_{\theta}=Z^{\top}\theta
and

g_{\tau}(u)=\Big|\mathbbm{1}\{u\geq 0\}-\Phi(u/\tau)\Big|=\Phi(-|u|/\tau)\in[0,1],\qquad\Delta_{\tau}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{P_{0}}\big[g_{\tau}(U_{\theta})\big].

Under Assumption
3
(i)
, for every compact
\mathcal{N}\subset\mathcal{N}_{0}

there exist constants
C<\infty
and
\tau_{1}>0
such that for all
\tau\in(0,\tau_{1}]
,

\sup_{\theta\in\mathcal{N}}\Delta_{\tau}(\theta)\leq C\tau^{\alpha},

where
\alpha
is the margin exponent in Assumption
3
(i)
.

Proof.

Fix
\theta\in\mathcal{N}
. Using
\Phi(-a)=\int_{a}^{\infty}\phi(s)\,ds
for
a\geq 0
and Fubini,

\Delta_{\tau}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}\big[\Phi(-|U_{\theta}|/\tau)\big]=\mathbb{E}\Big[\int_{|U_{\theta}|/\tau}^{\infty}\phi(s)\,ds\Big]=\int_{0}^{\infty}P_{0}\big(|U_{\theta}|\leq\tau s\big)\,\phi(s)\,ds.

Let
s_{0}=t_{0}/\tau
. Split the integral at
s_{0}
:

\Delta_{\tau}(\theta)=\int_{0}^{s_{0}}P_{0}(|U_{\theta}|\leq\tau s)\phi(s)\,ds+\int_{s_{0}}^{\infty}P_{0}(|U_{\theta}|\leq\tau s)\phi(s)\,ds.

For the first term, Assumption
3
(i)
yields

P_{0}(|U_{\theta}|\leq\tau s)\leq C_{U}(\tau s)^{\alpha}\quad\text{for }s\leq s_{0},

hence

\int_{0}^{s_{0}}P_{0}(|U_{\theta}|\leq\tau s)\phi(s)\,ds\leq C_{U}\tau^{\alpha}\int_{0}^{\infty}s^{\alpha}\phi(s)\,ds\lesssim\tau^{\alpha}.

For the second term, use
P_{0}(|U_{\theta}|\leq\tau s)\leq 1
and Gaussian tail decay:

\int_{s_{0}}^{\infty}P_{0}(|U_{\theta}|\leq\tau s)\phi(s)\,ds\leq\int_{t_{0}/\tau}^{\infty}\phi(s)\,ds=\Phi(-t_{0}/\tau)=o(\tau^{\alpha}).

Uniformity over
\theta\in\mathcal{N}
follows because the margin constants are uniform over
\mathcal{N}_{0}

and
\mathcal{N}\subset\mathcal{N}_{0}
is compact.
∎

Lemma 11

(Conditional score identities under gate mismatch)
.

Fix
\tau>0
and condition on
(W,X,Z)
. Set
d_{0}=\mathbbm{1}\{U_{0}\geq 0\}
,
\pi_{\tau}=\Phi(U_{0}/\tau)
,
\Delta_{0}=X^{\top}\gamma_{0}
,
\mu_{0}=W^{\top}\beta_{0}
,
\mu_{1}=\mu_{0}+\Delta_{0}
,
f_{j}(y)=\varphi_{\sigma_{0}}(y-\mu_{j})
for
j\in\{0,1\}
,

p_{\tau}(y)=(1-\pi_{\tau})f_{0}(y)+\pi_{\tau}f_{1}(y),\qquad\omega_{\tau}(y)=\frac{\pi_{\tau}f_{1}(y)}{p_{\tau}(y)}.

At
\eta_{0}
, the observed-data score blocks satisfy

\nabla_{\beta}\tilde{\ell}_{\tau}(\tilde{\eta}_{0};O)=\frac{W}{\sigma_{0}^{2}}\Big\{Y-\mu_{0}-\omega_{\tau}(Y)\Delta_{0}\Big\},

\nabla_{\gamma}\tilde{\ell}_{\tau}(\tilde{\eta}_{0};O)=\frac{X}{\sigma_{0}^{2}}\,\omega_{\tau}(Y)\Big\{Y-\mu_{1}\Big\},

\partial_{\sigma^{2}}\tilde{\ell}_{\tau}(\tilde{\eta}_{0};O)=-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{0}^{2}}+\frac{(1-\omega_{\tau}(Y))(Y-\mu_{0})^{2}+\omega_{\tau}(Y)(Y-\mu_{1})^{2}}{2\sigma_{0}^{4}},

and, with
t=U_{0}/\tau
,

\nabla_{\theta}\ell_{\tau}(\eta_{0};O)=\frac{Z}{\tau}\,\phi(t)\,\frac{\omega_{\tau}(Y)-\pi_{\tau}}{\pi_{\tau}(1-\pi_{\tau})}.

Define

J_{\tau}(W,X,Z)=\int\frac{f_{0}(y)f_{1}(y)}{p_{\tau}(y)}\,dy,\qquad 0\leq J_{\tau}(W,X,Z)\leq\min\!\big\{\pi_{\tau}^{-1},(1-\pi_{\tau})^{-1}\big\}.

Then under the conditional hard-threshold law
Y\mid W,X,Z\sim f_{d_{0}}
,

\mathbb{E}\big[d_{0}-\omega_{\tau}(Y)\mid W,X,Z\big]=(d_{0}-\pi_{\tau})\,J_{\tau}(W,X,Z),

\mathbb{E}\big[\omega_{\tau}(Y)-\pi_{\tau}\mid W,X,Z\big]=(d_{0}-\pi_{\tau})\,\{1-J_{\tau}(W,X,Z)\}.

In particular,
J_{\tau}(W,X,Z)\leq 2
whenever
d_{0}\in\{0,1\}
.

Proof.

Differentiate the working mixture density
p_{\tau}(y)
at
\eta_{0}
. Writing

r_{0}=y-\mu_{0}
and
r_{1}=y-\mu_{1}
,

\nabla_{\beta}p_{\tau}(y)=\frac{W}{\sigma_{0}^{2}}\Big\{(1-\pi_{\tau})f_{0}(y)r_{0}+\pi_{\tau}f_{1}(y)r_{1}\Big\},

\nabla_{\gamma}p_{\tau}(y)=\frac{X}{\sigma_{0}^{2}}\,\pi_{\tau}f_{1}(y)r_{1},

\partial_{\sigma^{2}}p_{\tau}(y)=p_{\tau}(y)\Bigg[-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{0}^{2}}+\frac{(1-\omega_{\tau}(y))r_{0}^{2}+\omega_{\tau}(y)r_{1}^{2}}{2\sigma_{0}^{4}}\Bigg].

Dividing by
p_{\tau}(y)
yields the first three displayed score formulas. For the gate parameter,

\partial_{t}p_{\tau}(y)=\phi(t)\{f_{1}(y)-f_{0}(y)\}=p_{\tau}(y)\,\phi(t)\,\frac{\omega_{\tau}(y)-\pi_{\tau}}{\pi_{\tau}(1-\pi_{\tau})},

and
\nabla_{\theta}t=Z/\tau
, which gives the
\theta
-score formula.

For the conditional identities, first note that
J_{\tau}(W,X,Z)\geq 0
, and because
p_{\tau}(y)\geq\pi_{\tau}f_{1}(y)
and
p_{\tau}(y)\geq(1-\pi_{\tau})f_{0}(y)
,

J_{\tau}(W,X,Z)\leq\int\frac{f_{0}(y)}{\pi_{\tau}}\,dy=\pi_{\tau}^{-1},\qquad J_{\tau}(W,X,Z)\leq\int\frac{f_{1}(y)}{1-\pi_{\tau}}\,dy=(1-\pi_{\tau})^{-1}.

On
\{d_{0}=1\}
,
U_{0}\geq 0
implies
\pi_{\tau}=\Phi(U_{0}/\tau)\geq 1/2
, so
J_{\tau}\leq 2
; symmetrically on
\{d_{0}=0\}
,
J_{\tau}\leq 2
. If
d_{0}=1
, then

\mathbb{E}\big[1-\omega_{\tau}(Y)\mid W,X,Z\big]=\int\frac{(1-\pi_{\tau})f_{0}(y)}{p_{\tau}(y)}\,f_{1}(y)\,dy=(1-\pi_{\tau})J_{\tau}(W,X,Z).

If
d_{0}=0
, then

\mathbb{E}\big[\omega_{\tau}(Y)\mid W,X,Z\big]=\int\frac{\pi_{\tau}f_{1}(y)}{p_{\tau}(y)}\,f_{0}(y)\,dy=\pi_{\tau}J_{\tau}(W,X,Z).

These two displays combine to

\mathbb{E}\big[d_{0}-\omega_{\tau}(Y)\mid W,X,Z\big]=(d_{0}-\pi_{\tau})J_{\tau}(W,X,Z).

Since
\mathbb{E}[\omega_{\tau}(Y)-\pi_{\tau}\mid W,X,Z]=d_{0}-\pi_{\tau}-\mathbb{E}[d_{0}-\omega_{\tau}(Y)\mid W,X,Z]
, the second identity follows immediately.
∎

Lemma 12

(Score bias rate at the hard-threshold parameter)
.

Assume Assumption
3
.
Let
S_{\tau}(\tilde{\eta})=\nabla_{\tilde{\eta}}K_{\tau}(\eta(\tilde{\eta}))
.
Then there exist constants
C<\infty
and
\tau_{1}>0
such that for all
\tau\in(0,\tau_{1}]
,

\|S_{\tau}(\tilde{\eta}_{0})\|\leq C\tau^{\alpha}.

Proof.

Write
\eta_{0}=(\beta_{0},\gamma_{0},\theta_{0},\sigma_{0}^{2})
and
U_{0}=Z^{\top}\theta_{0}
.
Under
P_{0}
,

Y=W^{\top}\beta_{0}+X^{\top}\gamma_{0}\,\mathbbm{1}\{U_{0}\geq 0\}+\varepsilon,\qquad\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon\mid W,X,Z]=0.

At
\eta_{0}
, the working conditional mean equals

m_{\tau}(W,X,Z)=W^{\top}\beta_{0}+X^{\top}\gamma_{0}\,\Phi(U_{0}/\tau),

so the residual decomposes as

Y-m_{\tau}=\varepsilon+(X^{\top}\gamma_{0})\Big(\mathbbm{1}\{U_{0}\geq 0\}-\Phi(U_{0}/\tau)\Big).

Denote the gate discrepancy by

\delta_{\tau}(U_{0})=\mathbbm{1}\{U_{0}\geq 0\}-\Phi(U_{0}/\tau),

so
|\delta_{\tau}(U_{0})|\leq g_{\tau}(U_{0})
. Also write

d_{0}=\mathbbm{1}\{U_{0}\geq 0\},\qquad\pi_{\tau}=\Phi(U_{0}/\tau),\qquad\Delta_{0}=X^{\top}\gamma_{0}.

Because
K_{\tau}(\eta)=\mathbb{E}_{P_{0}}[-\ell_{\tau}(\eta;O)]
and
\tilde{\eta}\mapsto\ell_{\tau}(\eta(\tilde{\eta});O)
is differentiable on
\mathbb{B}_{\delta}(\tilde{\eta}_{0})

(Assumption
3
(iii)
),

S_{\tau}(\tilde{\eta}_{0})=-\mathbb{E}_{P_{0}}\big[\nabla_{\tilde{\eta}}\tilde{\ell}_{\tau}(\tilde{\eta}_{0};O)\big],\qquad\tilde{\ell}_{\tau}(\tilde{\eta};O)=\log p_{\eta(\tilde{\eta}),\tau}(Y\mid W,X,Z).

Thus it suffices to bound
\big\|\mathbb{E}_{P_{0}}[\nabla_{\tilde{\eta}}\tilde{\ell}_{\tau}(\tilde{\eta}_{0};O)]\big\|
.

Step 1: explicit conditional score calculations.

Apply Lemma
11
. For the
\beta
block,

\nabla_{\beta}\tilde{\ell}_{\tau}(\tilde{\eta}_{0};O)=\frac{W}{\sigma_{0}^{2}}\Big\{\varepsilon+\Delta_{0}\big(d_{0}-\omega_{\tau}(Y)\big)\Big\}.

Since
\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon\mid W,X,Z]=0
and

\mathbb{E}[d_{0}-\omega_{\tau}(Y)\mid W,X,Z]=\delta_{\tau}(U_{0})J_{\tau}(W,X,Z)
,

\mathbb{E}[\nabla_{\beta}\tilde{\ell}_{\tau}(\tilde{\eta}_{0};O)\mid W,X,Z]=\frac{W\,\Delta_{0}}{\sigma_{0}^{2}}\,\delta_{\tau}(U_{0})\,J_{\tau}(W,X,Z),

\big\|\mathbb{E}[\nabla_{\beta}\tilde{\ell}_{\tau}(\tilde{\eta}_{0};O)\mid W,X,Z]\big\|\leq C\,\|W\|\,\|X\|\,|\delta_{\tau}(U_{0})|,

because Lemma
11
gives
J_{\tau}(W,X,Z)\leq 2
on
\{d_{0}\in\{0,1\}\}
and
|\Delta_{0}|\leq\|\gamma_{0}\|\,\|X\|
.

For the
\gamma
block, Lemma
11
gives

\nabla_{\gamma}\tilde{\ell}_{\tau}(\tilde{\eta}_{0};O)=\frac{X}{\sigma_{0}^{2}}\,\omega_{\tau}(Y)\Big\{\varepsilon-(1-d_{0})\Delta_{0}\Big\}.

If
d_{0}=1
, then
\pi_{\tau}\geq 1/2
and

1-\omega_{\tau}(Y)=\frac{(1-\pi_{\tau})f_{0}(Y)}{p_{\tau}(Y)}\leq\frac{1-\pi_{\tau}}{\pi_{\tau}}\frac{f_{0}(Y)}{f_{1}(Y)}\leq 2(1-\pi_{\tau})\frac{f_{0}(Y)}{f_{1}(Y)}.

Since
\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon\mid W,X,Z,d_{0}=1]=0
,

\big\|\mathbb{E}[\nabla_{\gamma}\tilde{\ell}_{\tau}(\tilde{\eta}_{0};O)\mid W,X,Z,d_{0}=1]\big\|=\frac{\|X\|}{\sigma_{0}^{2}}\Big|\mathbb{E}\big[(\omega_{\tau}(Y)-1)\varepsilon\mid W,X,Z,d_{0}=1\big]\Big|

\leq\frac{\|X\|}{\sigma_{0}^{2}}\mathbb{E}\big[(1-\omega_{\tau}(Y))|\varepsilon|\mid W,X,Z,d_{0}=1\big]\leq C\,\|X\|\,(1+|\Delta_{0}|)\,(1-\pi_{\tau}).

If
d_{0}=0
, then
\pi_{\tau}\leq 1/2
and

\omega_{\tau}(Y)=\frac{\pi_{\tau}f_{1}(Y)}{p_{\tau}(Y)}\leq\frac{\pi_{\tau}}{1-\pi_{\tau}}\frac{f_{1}(Y)}{f_{0}(Y)}\leq 2\pi_{\tau}\frac{f_{1}(Y)}{f_{0}(Y)},

so

\big\|\mathbb{E}[\nabla_{\gamma}\tilde{\ell}_{\tau}(\tilde{\eta}_{0};O)\mid W,X,Z,d_{0}=0]\big\|\leq\frac{\|X\|}{\sigma_{0}^{2}}\mathbb{E}\big[\omega_{\tau}(Y)\,|\varepsilon-\Delta_{0}|\mid W,X,Z,d_{0}=0\big]\leq C\,\|X\|\,(1+|\Delta_{0}|)\,\pi_{\tau}.

Combining the two cases and using
|\Delta_{0}|\leq\|\gamma_{0}\|\,\|X\|
,

\big\|\mathbb{E}[\nabla_{\gamma}\tilde{\ell}_{\tau}(\tilde{\eta}_{0};O)\mid W,X,Z]\big\|\leq C\,(1+\|X\|^{2})\,|\delta_{\tau}(U_{0})|.

For the
\sigma^{2}
block, write
r_{0}=Y-W^{\top}\beta_{0}
and
r_{1}=Y-W^{\top}\beta_{0}-X^{\top}\gamma_{0}
.
If
d_{0}=1
, then
r_{1}=\varepsilon
and
\mathbb{E}[r_{1}^{2}\mid W,X,Z,d_{0}=1]=\sigma_{0}^{2}
, so

\mathbb{E}[\partial_{\sigma^{2}}\tilde{\ell}_{\tau}(\tilde{\eta}_{0};O)\mid W,X,Z,d_{0}=1]=\frac{1}{2\sigma_{0}^{4}}\mathbb{E}\big[(1-\omega_{\tau}(Y))(r_{0}^{2}-r_{1}^{2})\mid W,X,Z,d_{0}=1\big].

Because
r_{0}^{2}-r_{1}^{2}=(\varepsilon+\Delta_{0})^{2}-\varepsilon^{2}=2\Delta_{0}\varepsilon+\Delta_{0}^{2}
,

\big|\mathbb{E}[\partial_{\sigma^{2}}\tilde{\ell}_{\tau}(\tilde{\eta}_{0};O)\mid W,X,Z,d_{0}=1]\big|\leq C\,(1+\Delta_{0}^{2})\,(1-\pi_{\tau}).

If
d_{0}=0
, then
r_{0}=\varepsilon
and
\mathbb{E}[r_{0}^{2}\mid W,X,Z,d_{0}=0]=\sigma_{0}^{2}
, hence

\mathbb{E}[\partial_{\sigma^{2}}\tilde{\ell}_{\tau}(\tilde{\eta}_{0};O)\mid W,X,Z,d_{0}=0]=\frac{1}{2\sigma_{0}^{4}}\mathbb{E}\big[\omega_{\tau}(Y)(r_{1}^{2}-r_{0}^{2})\mid W,X,Z,d_{0}=0\big],

with
r_{1}^{2}-r_{0}^{2}=(\varepsilon-\Delta_{0})^{2}-\varepsilon^{2}=-2\Delta_{0}\varepsilon+\Delta_{0}^{2}

\big|\mathbb{E}[\partial_{\sigma^{2}}\tilde{\ell}_{\tau}(\tilde{\eta}_{0};O)\mid W,X,Z]\big|\leq C\,(1+\|X\|^{2})\,|\delta_{\tau}(U_{0})|.

For the
\vartheta
block, Lemma
11
yields

\mathbb{E}[\nabla_{\theta}\ell_{\tau}(\eta_{0};O)\mid W,X,Z]=\frac{Z}{\tau}\,\phi(U_{0}/\tau)\,\frac{\mathbb{E}[\omega_{\tau}(Y)-\pi_{\tau}\mid W,X,Z]}{\pi_{\tau}(1-\pi_{\tau})}

\frac{\phi(t)}{\Phi(t)\{1-\Phi(t)\}}\leq C(1+|t|)\qquad\forall t\in\mathbb{R},

hence

\big\|\mathbb{E}[\nabla_{\theta}\ell_{\tau}(\eta_{0};O)\mid W,X,Z]\big\|\leq C\,\|Z\|\,(1+|U_{0}|/\tau)\,|\delta_{\tau}(U_{0})|.

Because
\nabla_{\vartheta}\tilde{\ell}_{\tau}=(\nabla_{\theta}\ell_{\tau})\,\nabla_{\vartheta}\theta
and the chart Jacobian is bounded by Assumption
3
(ii)
,

\big\|\mathbb{E}[\nabla_{\vartheta}\tilde{\ell}_{\tau}(\tilde{\eta}_{0};O)\mid W,X,Z]\big\|\leq C\,\|Z\|\,(1+|U_{0}|/\tau)\,|\delta_{\tau}(U_{0})|.

Step 2: absorb the factor
(1+|U_{0}|/\tau)
into a slightly larger gate error.

Define
\rho(s)=\frac{(1+s)\Phi(-s)}{\Phi(-s/2)}
for
s\geq 0
.
Then
\rho
is continuous on
[0,\infty)
and
\rho(s)\to 0
as
s\to\infty
, so
C_{\rho}:=\sup_{s\geq 0}\rho(s)<\infty
.
Hence

(1+s)\Phi(-s)\leq C_{\rho}\,\Phi(-s/2)\qquad\forall s\geq 0.

With
s=|u|/\tau
and
g_{\tau}(u)=\Phi(-|u|/\tau)
, this gives

(1+|u|/\tau)\,g_{\tau}(u)\leq C_{\rho}\,g_{2\tau}(u)\qquad\forall u\in\mathbb{R}.

Therefore

\big\|\mathbb{E}[\nabla_{\vartheta}\tilde{\ell}_{\tau}(\tilde{\eta}_{0};O)\mid W,X,Z]\big\|\leq C\,\|Z\|\,g_{2\tau}(U_{0}).

Step 3: integrate using near-boundary conditional moments and the margin condition.

Combining the block bounds and using
|\delta_{\tau}(U_{0})|\leq g_{\tau}(U_{0})\leq g_{2\tau}(U_{0})
, we obtain for some constant
C<\infty
,

\|S_{\tau}(\tilde{\eta}_{0})\|\leq C\,\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big(1+\|W\|\,\|X\|+\|X\|^{2}+\|Z\|\Big)\,g_{2\tau}(U_{0})\Big].

By Assumption
3
,
(W,X,Z)
have finite moments of order at least four under
P_{0}
, so the displayed polynomial factor is integrable.
Split the expectation into
\{|U_{0}|\leq t_{0}\}
and
\{|U_{0}|>t_{0}\}
.
On
\{|U_{0}|>t_{0}\}
,
g_{2\tau}(U_{0})\leq\Phi(-t_{0}/(2\tau))=o(\tau^{\alpha})
, hence this contribution is
o(\tau^{\alpha})
by Cauchy–Schwarz.

On
\{|U_{0}|\leq t_{0}\}

(i)
and Cauchy–Schwarz to bound the conditional expectation of

uniformly in

. Thus, for some

Applying Lemma
10
at
\theta=\theta_{0}
yields
\mathbb{E}[g_{2\tau}(U_{0})]\lesssim\tau^{\alpha}
. Combining the two regions gives
\|S_{\tau}(\tilde{\eta}_{0})\|\leq C\tau^{\alpha}
for
\tau
small enough.
∎

Then there exist
c_{1},c_{2}>0
such that for all large
n
,

\mathbb{E}_{P_{0}}\varphi_{n}\leq\exp\{-c_{1}n/L_{n}^{4}\},\qquad\sup_{\eta\in\Theta_{n,\varepsilon}}\mathbb{E}_{P_{\eta,\tau_{n}}}(1-\varphi_{n})\leq\exp\{-c_{2}n/L_{n}^{4}\}.

Moreover, for every
\delta>0
there exist constants
c_{\rm rad},c,C>0
such that,
with
\varepsilon^{\prime}_{n}(\delta)=c_{\rm rad}\,\delta^{1/2}\,L_{n}^{-2}
, for all sufficiently large
n
,

P_{0}\Big(\inf_{\eta\in\Theta_{n}:\ \|\eta-\eta_{n}^{\star}\|\leq\varepsilon^{\prime}_{n}(\delta)}R_{n,\tau_{n}}(\eta)\geq-n\delta\Big)\geq 1-C\exp\{-c\,n/L_{n}^{4}\}.

Proof.

Repeat the proofs of Lemmas
5
and
6
with
\tau
replaced by
\tau_{n}
,
\eta^{\star}
by
\eta_{n}^{\star}
, and the fixed sieve scale
S_{n}
by the effective scale
L_{n}=S_{n}\vee\tau_{n}^{-1}
. The only substantive changes are that the score envelopes now scale as
L_{n}^{2}
because the
\theta
block contributes a factor
1/\tau_{n}
, the separation constant becomes
\delta(\varepsilon)=\min\{c_{K}\varepsilon^{2},\Delta_{K}\}
from Lemma
13
, and the denominator ball shrinks to
\varepsilon_{n}^{\prime}(\delta)=c_{\rm rad}\delta^{1/2}L_{n}^{-2}
using Assumption
3
(vi)
. The same entropy calculation then gives
\log N_{n}\lesssim\log L_{n}
, so Assumption
3
(vii)
yields tail bounds of order
\exp\{-cn/L_{n}^{4}\}
for both the tests and the denominator event.

Lemma 15

(Uniform local asymptotic normality under
\tau_{n}
)
.

Assume Assumption
3
and Assumption
4
. Assume also
L_{n}^{8}/n\to 0
.
Define

The proof is the fixed-
\tau
LAN argument with two triangular-array substitutions. First, the empirical Hessian fluctuation is controlled by the

envelope in Assumption

4

L_{n}^{8}/n\to 0

as in Lemma
7
.
