LDRD PROJECT TITLE: Multiphoton diagnostics utilizing entangled

PROJECT TEAM MEMBERS: Christopher J. Kliewer, David W. Chandler

ABSTRACT: Laser-based nonlinear optical spectroscopy approaches have enabled the direct
sensing of important chemistry in nearly every fundamental or applied field of science. Yet in
many applications, increased detectivity is needed to unravel fundamental mechanisms. One
possibility is the use of quantum entanglement to increase detection cross-sections, but it is
unproven that enhancement from quantum light extends to practical intensities for chemical
sensing. In this report, we investigate the quantum advantage for second harmonic generation
nonlinear optical mixing. We observe quantum enhanced mixing probabilities for entangled
photon pairs from 50 to 400 times greater than the classical nonlinear mixing probability, and we
quantify this advantage over a range of photon flux from a 10-nanosecond entangled-pair pulsed
laser source. The total second harmonic generation output over this flux ranged from 80 to 20
nW respectively.

INTRODUCTION AND EXECUTIVE SUMMARY OF RESULTS: In 2006, Lee
and Goodson reported several orders of enhancement in the nonlinear optical spectroscopy cross
section for two-photon absorption using quantum-correlated photons generated through
spontaneous parametric downconversion.1 A later work in 2017 repeated this experimental
observation, reporting several orders of enhancement in photon absorption rate in a two-photon
absorption process.2 However, the veracity of these reports has been questioned in the
literature,3 and a recent quantum calculation predicted that the overall increase in two-photon
absorption in the high photon flux regime by entangled photons generated through spontaneous
parametric down-conversion is limited to a factor of 3,4 whereas the dramatic increases in
nonlinear cross section using entangled photons previously reported could only take place at
such low incident photon counts that the generated fluorescence should have been undetectable 3
at least when solely considering the expected quantum enhancement from using time-frequency
entangled photon pairs. Clearly there is debate in the literature on the enhancement caused by
the single-photon entangled state created through spontaneous parametric downconversion.

In recent simulations at Sandia National Laboratories (Daniel Soh), the enhancement factor for
multiphoton nonlinear mixing processes such as coherent Raman scattering and sum-frequency
generation were calculated in a photon flux regime typical of practical nonlinear optical
experiments for a range of squeezing parameter achievable with a quantum entangled light
source. These results for coherent Raman scattering are shown in Slide 3 of the Addendum
where with reasonably achievable amounts of two-mode squeezing, significant enhancement
(orders of magnitude) is expected to coherent Raman signal generation efficiency. In this case,
only the pump and Stokes fields were assumed to be derived from the entangled output of the
light source.

DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF RESEARCH AND DEVELOPMENT AND
METHODOLOGY: We generated frequency entangled photon pairs through spontaneous
parametric downconversion (SPDC) in a MgO doped periodically poled lithium niobate (PPLN)
crystal (Covesion, MSHG1064, 10 mm length), with experimental layout shown on Slide 6 of
the addendum. The wide phase matching bandwidth of the PPLN yielded > 100 nm of
bandwidth at 1064 nm, even though the narrowband 532 nm source was << 1 nm in bandwidth.
Because the generated photons are frequency entangled, the energies of each photon of the
entangled pair must add up to the energy of the source photon. At each stage of the experiment
(prior to the first SPDC PPLN, between PPLN-1 and PPLN-2, and after PPLN-2) relevant short-
pass and long-pass filters (Semrock, RazorEdge) were used in duplicate to fully prevent any seed
photons from the previous stage to contaminate the results. This was verified by adding the
second, duplicate, spectral filter (which should further suppress any leakage photons by another
factor of ~106) and verifying no change to the mixing efficiency downstream of those optics.
Fiber-coupled Ocean Optics spectrometers were used to record the spectra after the SPDC stage
and following the SHG stage. A narrowband notch filter (Semrock NF03-532E-25) with ~17 nm
FWHM stop-band surrounding 532 nm was used to block the strong central frequency spike
shown on Slide 7 of the addendum to measure the classical light mixing pedestal.

The ratio of the integrated intensity of the broad SHG pedestal to the integrated intensity under
the sharp 532 nm SHG spectral line that arises from the mixing of entangled photons yields the
quantum advantage directly, which we recorded for a range of measured pump and SHG
intensities.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION: In order to deduce the ratio of quantum enhanced
nonlinear mixing to classical mixing, our aim was to create a SPDC bandwidth much broader
than the laser source. For degenerate SPDC (532 nm -> 1064 nm + 1064 nm photons), the phase
mismatch, Δk, as a function of frequency detuning Δω from the degenerate frequency ω0 is
governed by Eq. 1.
                       Δk(Δω) = k(ω0+Δω) + k(ω0–Δω) – kp + 2π/Λ         (1)
In the case of a MgO:PPLN crystal, the nonlinear interaction is of “type-0” (e -> e + e). Because
of this, all input and output fields are e-polarized. The signal and idler thus share the same
dispersion curve k(ω). The linear group velocity mismatch term cancels exactly by symmetry.
Expanding to next order leaves only the group-velocity dispersion (GVD) term:
                                     Δk(Δω) ≈ k″(ω0) · Δω2

For this reason, degenerate SPDC has a much broader phase matching spectrum than non-
degenerate SPDC, which is instead limited by the much larger liner group-velocity mismatch.
For the 10 mm-long MgO:PPLN used in these experiments, the phase matched bandwidth at
1064 nm is > 40 nm, more than 5 order of magnitude broader than our pump laser source. We
exploit this unique feature to incisively probe the quantum advantage directly for SHG.
Classical SHG will sample photons randomly from this broad bandwidth to generate a broadband
SHG signal. Quantum-enhanced SHG will only mix entangled pairs of photons, whose energies
will sum up to the narrow bandwidth of our source laser, 5 orders of magnitude lower in
frequency bandwidth. Thus, at each signal photon flux, we have a simultaneous measure of the
classical and quantum nonlinear mixing, with isolatable signals, under identical illumination
conditions.
Because of the limited dynamic range of our fiber-coupled spectrometers, we used a notch filter
to block the narrow central portion of the signal to isolate signal from the classical broadband
SHG. Additionally, the bright intensity of the narrowband quantum SHG required a calibrated
neutral density (ND) optical filter to be placed in the beam path. Thus, the only changes between
recording the spectrum to evaluate the quantum SHG intensity and the classical SHG intensity
were after the final mixing stage, and included adding or removing a single ND filter and the
narrowband notch filter. These changes have no impact on the light entering the final mixing
stage, thus the quantum/classical mixing ratio is truly evaluated under identical conditions.

Figure 1. Measured classical SHG signal with notch filter to block the narrowband (<< 1 nm) quantum SHG signal.
The data is fitted to a Gaussian to determine the full area of under the curve, as shown, omitting from the fit those
points at the center that are affected by the notch filter.

Because the notch filter necessarily removed a portion of the middle of the classical mixing
spectrum, we fitted a Gaussian to the signal, with a fitting mask that omitted points affected by
the notch filter. An example is shown in Figure 1. By rotating the notch filter in place (changing
the angle between the laser beam propagation and the surface normal of the optic), one can tune
the exact cut-off frequency. This additionally verified that the portion of the classical SHG

pedestal blocked by the optical notch followed an essentially Gaussian profile. We then
integrated the area under this Gaussian to determine the classical SHG total mixing intensity.
Similarly, we integrate under the bright narrowband quantum+classical SHG shown in Slide 7
right panel. Subtracting the ND corrected classical amplitude from this integrated signal count
leaves only the quantum SHG intensity. We report the ratio of these two quantities as the
directly measured quantum advantage for SHG, and then compare this across a range of SHG
signal photon flux. For instance, at a SHG signal flux of 2 nJ/pulse in our 10 nanosecond laser
pulse we measure a quantum advantage of ~400. By a signal flux of 8 nJ/pulse the quantum
advantage is still ~50, as shown in Figure 2.

The results of this project provide a clear quantified measurement of the quantum advantage for
nonlinear optical mixing (SHG) with entangled photons over a range of photon flux. The signals
for classical and quantum mixing were easily separable because of dramatically different
frequency bandwidths. We are sending these results to our theoretical collaborator, Professor
Daniel Soh, University of Arizona, to compare against a numerical model for nonlinear optical
mixing with quantum light. These benchmark numbers will directly assess our capability to
model these interactions correctly, which will form the basis for justification of future proposals

that may attempt to use quantum enhanced light for practical purposes such as surface sum-
frequency generation detection of intermediates with greater sensitivity. Once this comparison is
finished, we intend to send these results to an archival journal such as Physical Review Letters to
provide this valuable benchmark to the larger community. Repeated calls from DOE/BES,
DOE/BER, DTRA, and other natinoal agencies have sought new ideas in quantum-enhanced
diagnostics. Building on the foundation of this work, we will seek to continue by submitting a
proposal to these calls with justifiable understanding of the quantified quantum advantage that
may be achieved.

CONCLUSION:
This work establishes a clear and experimentally grounded benchmark for the quantum
advantage achievable in nonlinear optical mixing using entangled photons, addressing a central
controversy in the literature with a direct, flux-dependent measurement of second harmonic
generation enhancement. By designing an experiment in which classical and quantum-enhanced
SHG could be observed simultaneously under identical illumination conditions and then
separated spectrally because of their dramatically different bandwidths, we were able to quantify
the true mixing advantage with unusual clarity and rigor. The results show that entangled photon
pairs can drive SHG with probabilities approximately 50 to 400 times greater than the
corresponding classical nonlinear mixing probability across a practically relevant range of
photon flux, with total SHG outputs spanning roughly 20 to 80 nW from a 10 ns pulsed source.
These measurements demonstrate that quantum enhancement is not confined to vanishingly
small signal levels, but persists into a regime where detection is experimentally practical, thereby
strengthening the case for quantum light as a viable tool for chemical and spectroscopic sensing.
Just as importantly, the methodology developed here provides a broadly useful framework for
evaluating quantum advantage in other nonlinear optical processes, since it isolates the relevant
observables without changing the input conditions to the final mixing stage. As a result, the
present study delivers not only a significant scientific finding, but also an incisive metrology
approach for future benchmarking of quantum-enhanced light–matter interactions. The
immediate next step is comparison with numerical modeling being developed in collaboration
with Professor Daniel Soh, which will test whether current theory can accurately reproduce the
measured enhancement factors and their dependence on flux. That comparison will be essential
for establishing predictive design rules for future quantum-enhanced diagnostics. Looking ahead,
these results position Sandia to pursue high-impact applications in areas such as surface sum-
frequency generation, coherent Raman sensing, and other nonlinear spectroscopies where
improved detectivity could reveal weak intermediates or transient species that remain
inaccessible with classical illumination. More broadly, the findings support a compelling path
forward for proposals to DOE, NNSA, BES, BER, DTRA, and related programs focused on
quantum-enabled sensing, while also motivating archival publication and continued development
of practical quantum-light sources for real-world measurement challenges.
