The evolving treatment landscape of restless legs syndrome
Authors: Maria P Mogavero, Giuseppe Lanza, Pasquale Tondo, Oliviero Bruni, Raffaele Ferri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRestless legs syndrome (RLS) is a chronic sensorimotor disorder with a substantial impact on sleep, quality of life, and long-term neurological health. Because RLS has long required longitudinal clinical management in many patients, the major recent change is not the recognition of chronicity itself, but the reordering of treatment priorities as long-term evidence and clinical experience have clarified the risks of dopaminergic augmentation. This review synthesizes the progression of treatment paradigms from 2015 to 2026, focusing on how increasing recognition of augmentation has reshaped clinical decision-making. Historically, dopaminergic agents dominated RLS treatment because of robust short-term efficacy; however, their long-term risk-benefit profile has been reconsidered as augmentation became recognized as a major treatment-related complication. This led to a rebalancing of treatment strategies, with greater emphasis on iron metabolism and non-dopaminergic therapies. Iron deficiency is now considered a core pathophysiological and therapeutic target, with both oral and intravenous supplementation playing a key role even in the absence of overt anemia. Contemporary guidelines, culminating in the 2025 American Academy of Sleep Medicine recommendations, formalize this shift by endorsing α2δ ligands (gabapentin, pregabalin, and gabapentin enacarbil) and intravenous iron as first-line treatments, while discouraging routine use of dopamine agonists due to long-term risks. The modern approach is therefore augmentation-aware, iron-conscious, and phenotype-oriented, incorporating structured pathways for refractory disease that may include opioids and emerging non-dopaminergic or device-based therapies. Despite these advances, however, important gaps remain, including limited evidence for augmentation management, lack of disease-specific pharmacotherapies, and insufficient data in special populations such as children and pregnant individuals. Future directions will likely depend on mechanism-based drug development, improved biomarkers, and precision medicine approaches. Overall, the evolution of RLS treatment reflects a broader transition toward sustainable, individualized care in chronic neurological disorders.Keywords: α2δ ligands, augmentation, dopamine agonists, iron deficiency, long-term disease management, restless legs syndrome, treatment refinement

Introduction

Restless legs syndrome (RLS) is a neurological sensorimotor disorder characterized by an urge to move the legs, usually accompanied by unpleasant sensations, that begins or worsens during rest, is relieved at least transiently by movement, and shows a clear evening or nocturnal predominance.1,2 First described by Thomas Willis in the seventeenth century 3 and later established as a modern clinical entity by Karl-Axel Ekbom 4 in the mid-twentieth century, RLS long remained underrecognized despite its marked impact on sleep and quality of life. The development of standardized diagnostic criteria by the International Restless Legs Syndrome Study Group 5 and subsequent refinements 2 helped transform it from a historically overlooked condition into a well-defined neurological disorder and laid the foundation for the therapeutic evolution discussed in this review.

Beyond its diagnostic definition, RLS is clinically important because its circadian pattern directly interferes with sleep initiation and maintenance, often leading to chronic sleep restriction, daytime fatigue, impaired concentration, mood disturbance, and substantial reduction in quality of life. 1 Symptoms may fluctuate over time, but in many patients the disorder becomes a long-term management problem requiring repeated reassessment of severity, comorbidity, iron status, and treatment response. 6 This clinical burden helps explain why changes in therapeutic guidance have had consequences well beyond simple drug preference and why the field has progressively moved toward strategies that prioritize durability, tolerability, and avoidance of iatrogenic worsening.1,2,5

Over the last decade, treatment guidance for RLS has undergone a major reordering of therapeutic priorities. The central issue was not the emergence of long-term management as a new goal, since RLS has long been managed as a chronic condition in many patients. Rather, the answer to the long-standing question of which treatments remain safest and most effective over time has changed as evidence accumulated regarding dopaminergic augmentation. Recent guidance therefore places greater weight on augmentation risk, durability, iron status, and clinical phenotype than on short-term symptom reduction alone.

The inflection point was augmentation, a paradoxical, treatment-induced worsening of RLS in which symptoms tend to appear earlier in the day, intensify, occur more rapidly at rest, and may spread to other body parts despite ongoing dopaminergic therapy; first described with carbidopa/levodopa, it later became recognized as the major long-term complication shaping modern treatment strategies.5,7 –9 The 2016 International RLS Study Group (IRLSSG)/European RLS Study Group (EURLSSG)/RLS Foundation task-force paper 9 explicitly framed augmentation as the central long-term complication of dopaminergic therapy and already proposed α2δ ligands as reasonable initial therapy, together with iron correction and strict limitation of dopaminergic exposure. The same paper states that α2δ ligands “may be considered for initial RLS/WED treatment” because they are effective and have little risk of augmentation, whereas dopaminergic therapy should be kept at the lowest possible dose and only started when symptoms clearly impair quality of life.

The 2021 updated algorithm 10 turned that concern into a practical management framework. It explicitly stated that much had changed since the 2013 algorithm, organized care around intermittent, chronic persistent, and refractory RLS, and crystallized a hierarchy that many clinicians had already started to adopt: assess iron in all patients, use α2δ ligands first line for chronic persistent RLS unless contraindicated, reserve dopamine agonists as second-line options, and use low-dose opioids for selected refractory cases. In other words, the algorithm era did not merely add new drugs; it changed sequencing logic.

The 2025 American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM) guideline, 11 supported by a systematic review/meta-analysis, 12 completed this evolution by formal evidence grading. It strongly recommended gabapentin, gabapentin enacarbil, pregabalin, and intravenous (IV) ferric carboxymaltose, while suggesting against the standard use of levodopa, pramipexole, rotigotine, and ropinirole in adults because long-term harms, especially augmentation, materially alter their risk-benefit balance. The same guideline also widened the therapeutic horizon by conditionally supporting extended-release oxycodone/other opioids, dipyridamole, and bilateral high-frequency peroneal nerve stimulation in selected settings. This update reflects a substantial reordering of treatment priorities.

Taken together, the recent literature shows that the evolution of RLS treatment guidelines is best understood not as a simple replacement of one drug class by another, but as a transition toward phenotype-aware, iron-conscious, augmentation-averse, long-term care. That broader framework will be important for the next sections of this review, because the current state of the art is less about any single “best drug” than about choosing the least destabilizing pathway for the individual patient.

The dopamine era under pressure

The first half of the last decade was still, in practical terms, the late dopamine era of RLS management. Dopamine agonists remained central because they had the strongest trial-based efficacy record, were familiar to clinicians, and had regulatory approvals built on relatively short-term symptom reduction. Reviews from 2015 still described dopaminergic drugs as the most commonly prescribed therapies, even while openly acknowledging that long-term treatment was frequently complicated by loss of efficacy, dose escalation, and augmentation.13,14

This tension is especially visible in the 2016 American Academy of Neurology (AAN) practice guideline summary. 15 That document still largely reflected the evidence hierarchy created by randomized trials of relatively limited duration: strong evidence supported pramipexole, rotigotine, cabergoline, and gabapentin enacarbil; moderate evidence supported ropinirole, pregabalin, and IV ferric carboxymaltose; and levodopa remained a consideration in selected cases. In historical terms, this guideline captured the end of a period in which treatment recommendations were driven mainly by short-term efficacy, before long-term harms had been fully integrated into the ranking of therapies.

At the same time, however, the conceptual foundations of that approach were already being challenged. The 2016 IRLSSG/EURLSSG/RLS Foundation task-force paper on first-line treatment and augmentation prevention/treatment 9 marked a major turning point. It explicitly states that α2δ ligands may be considered for initial treatment because they are effective and have little risk of augmentation, whereas dopaminergic therapy, if chosen, should be kept as low as possible and used only when symptoms clearly affect quality of life. The same article framed augmentation as the major long-term management problem and emphasized that even low-dose dopaminergic therapy can still produce it.

What put the dopamine era “under pressure” was not the discovery that dopamine agonists failed in the short term, but the recognition that they could become self-destabilizing over time. The 2016 task force 9 summarized evidence suggesting that augmentation risk rises with treatment duration: rates were generally below 10% in short-term studies, roughly 30% in studies lasting 2–3 years, and as high as 42%–68% in some very long-term series; it also cited a community-based estimate of about 8% per year for augmentation and/or dose escalation among dopaminergically treated patients. The task force further noted that levodopa appears to have the highest risk and that shorter-acting agonists may be worse than longer-acting agents, though the latter point was not fully settled. 9

A second source of pressure was methodological. Much of the original enthusiasm for dopamine agonists came from pivotal trials that were too short to capture augmentation adequately. Later guidance explicitly recognized this mismatch. The 2025 AASM guideline 11 and its supporting review 12 state that augmentation usually emerges over months to years, that the initial American Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-registration trials were too short to demonstrate it, and that dose escalation in routine practice can intensify the problem nonlinearly. That later critique helps explain why the mid-2010s literature looks transitional: the field was still relying on an evidence base that favored agents whose most important harms were incompletely visible at the time of approval.

Thus, this phase should be understood as a period in which dopamine agonists remained prominent because of their immediate symptomatic efficacy, while accumulating field experience from long-term follow-up increasingly exposed the limits of a dopamine-first model. The key clinical issue was not that any medication class “won,” but that chronic dopaminergic use brought to light the widespread problem of augmentation. The most important gaps at this stage were the lack of controlled long-term comparative data, the absence of practical validated tools for early recognition of augmentation, and the limited incorporation of iron biology and non-dopaminergic strategies into mainstream treatment sequencing.9,13,14 These unresolved issues set the stage for the next phase, in which treatment guidance began to shift from dopamine efficacy to dopamine stewardship.

Rebalancing treatment with iron, α2δ ligands, and refractoriness

Between 2016 and 2021, RLS guidance entered a transitional but highly consequential phase: treatment strategy began to move away from a simple dopamine-centered model and toward a broader framework built on iron biology, α2δ ligands, and structured management of augmentation and refractoriness. The 2016 IRLSSG/EURLSSG/RLS Foundation task-force paper 9 was pivotal here. It did not yet eliminate dopamine agonists from routine care, but it clearly changed the direction of travel: α2δ ligands were presented as reasonable initial therapy because they were effective and carried little risk of augmentation; dopaminergic exposure, if used, should be kept as low as possible; iron deficiency should be corrected; and in severe augmentation, an opioid could be considered. This paper explicitly framed the reduction of “dopaminergic load” as a preventive principle and linked low iron stores to augmentation risk. 16

The next major step was the 2018 IRLSSG iron treatment guideline, 17 which effectively elevated iron from an adjunctive measure to a core therapeutic axis. Its message was not merely that iron deficiency should be corrected in general medicine terms, but that iron assessment in RLS requires RLS-specific thresholds and treatment logic. The guideline concluded that ferric carboxymaltose 1000 mg is effective in adults with moderate-to-severe RLS and serum ferritin <300 μg/L, and could be used as a first-line treatment in adults, while oral iron was considered possibly effective in those with serum ferritin ⩽75 μg/L. This position was supported by randomized trial evidence showing the benefit of single-dose IV ferric carboxymaltose in RLS with nonanemic iron deficiency. 18 By separating iron into its own dedicated guideline, the field signaled that iron status was no longer a background laboratory issue but a treatment determinant in its own right.

In parallel, the 2018 revised evidence-based review 19 helped formalize a more pluralistic pharmacologic view of RLS. Rather than treating dopamine agonists as the automatic default, the review sat within a growing literature that increasingly considered dopamine agonists, α2δ ligands, and iron as competing or complementary strategies whose value depended on long-term tolerability, augmentation risk, and patient phenotype. This was the phase in which the field began to move from “What works?” to “What works without destabilizing the disease over time?” Even where older efficacy data for dopamine agonists remained valid, they were now being interpreted through a more cautious long-term lens.

That rebalancing became much more operational in the 2021 updated algorithm. 10 This document explicitly organized management into intermittent RLS, chronic persistent RLS, refractory RLS, special circumstances, and alternative/investigational therapies. Most importantly, it explained the choice of an α2δ ligand as first-line therapy for chronic persistent RLS, with dopamine agonists moved to second-line status. It also made iron management more concrete, recommending oral iron when ferritin is ⩽75 μg/L with transferrin saturation <45%, and consideration of IV iron in selected settings, including ferritin <100 μg/L when a more rapid response is needed or oral absorption/tolerance is problematic. The same algorithm defined refractory RLS as RLS unresponsive to tolerable monotherapy because of reduced efficacy, augmentation, or adverse effects, and recommended reassessing iron stores, correcting exacerbating factors, considering combination therapy, and, in selected cases, opioid monotherapy.

By the early 2020s, this new framework had also normalized the idea that refractory and augmented RLS require a distinct management philosophy, not simply more of the same medication. The 2022 review on severe refractory and augmented RLS 20 reflected that maturation: it treated augmentation as a central chronic-management problem, emphasized careful withdrawal or tapering of dopaminergic drugs, re-evaluation of iron status and exacerbating factors, and supported the use of opioids in appropriately selected severe cases rather than treating them as a last-resort taboo. This is conceptually important, because it shows that the field was no longer merely adding options to dopamine therapy; it was building an alternative care pathway for patients in whom dopamine-based treatment had failed, backfired, or become unsustainable.

Overall, this was the period in which RLS treatment guidance became recognizably modern. Iron was promoted from supportive measure to therapeutic pillar; α2δ ligands moved toward the front of the treatment sequence; and refractory disease began to be managed with explicit algorithms rather than improvised rescue strategies. The major remaining limitation, however, was that this rebalancing was still largely driven by expert synthesis and evolving consensus, rather than by a single definitive guideline that fully reordered the hierarchy of standard treatments. That final step came with the next phase.

The 2025 turning point

The 2025 AASM clinical practice guideline, 11 together with its accompanying systematic review and meta-analysis, 12 represents the most explicit evidence-based reordering of treatment priorities in the modern treatment history of RLS. Unlike earlier reviews and algorithms, which had already begun to shift practice away from routine dopamine-first prescribing, the 2025 document formally re-ranked therapies using a GRADE-based framework and explicitly updated the previous 2012 AASM practice parameter. 21 In that sense, 2025 was not simply another incremental revision: it was the point at which the field’s evolving concerns about augmentation, durability, and long-term safety were converted into a new evidence-graded treatment hierarchy.

The clearest sign of this shift was the pattern of recommendations in adults with RLS. The guideline gives strong recommendations for gabapentin, gabapentin enacarbil, pregabalin, and IV ferric carboxymaltose, while levodopa, pramipexole, transdermal rotigotine, and ropinirole receive conditional recommendations against standard use. The same table also gives conditional support to IV low-molecular-weight iron dextran, IV ferumoxytol, ferrous sulfate, dipyridamole, extended-release oxycodone and other opioids, and bilateral high-frequency peroneal nerve stimulation. This is the practical core of the 2025 transition: the center of gravity moved away from dopamine agonists and toward α2δ ligands and iron-based strategies, with selected non-dopaminergic options for refractory disease.

What made this a true turning point was not that dopamine agonists suddenly stopped working. Drugs such as pramipexole, rotigotine, and ropinirole still showed clinically meaningful improvement in important outcomes; however, they were judged less favorably because augmentation of RLS symptoms materially altered their long-term risk-benefit profile. The note attached to the 2025 table is especially important: these dopaminergic agents may still be used in patients who place a higher value on short-term symptom reduction and a lower value on long-term adverse effects, particularly augmentation. That caveat is crucial because it shows the 2025 guideline did not deny efficacy; rather, it changed the weighting of outcomes by treating augmentation as a load-bearing endpoint in treatment selection.

This was also the moment when the earlier strands of the literature converged. The 2018 iron guideline 17 had already elevated iron therapy into a central therapeutic axis, and the 2021 updated algorithm 10 had already placed α2δ ligands first line for chronic persistent RLS while moving dopamine agonists to second line. What the 2025 AASM document did was to codify that evolving practice model within a formal guideline structure. In other words, 2025 did not invent the new paradigm from scratch; it ratified and consolidated a transition that had been developing across the previous decade. Importantly, subsequent international review under the auspices of the World Sleep Society 22 indicated that, although the 2025 AASM guideline received broad global support, its implementation is not uniform across countries because of differences in drug availability, regulatory approval, specialist access, and local treatment traditions; accordingly, its worldwide applicability should be viewed as context-dependent rather than automatic.

The 2025 guideline also broadened the therapeutic landscape in a way that is relevant for future directions. Extended-release oxycodone and other opioids received conditional support for moderate-to-severe RLS, especially in clinically difficult cases, with explicit discussion of their role in managing dopamine agonist-related augmentation and facilitating dopaminergic tapering. The same section conditionally supported dipyridamole, reflecting renewed interest in adenosinergic mechanisms, 23 and bilateral high-frequency peroneal nerve stimulation, signaling that nonpharmacologic/device-based therapy had entered guideline-level consideration rather than remaining purely experimental. This expansion matters because it shows that the field’s response to the limits of dopaminergic therapy was not merely substitution with α2δ ligands, but the opening of several mechanistically and clinically distinct pathways.

Thus, 2025 represents the point at which RLS guidance became fully augmentation-aware, iron-centered, and non-dopamine-default. The essential historical change was not the abandonment of dopaminergic therapy altogether, but the end of its status as the unquestioned routine starting point for chronic management. After 2025, any narrative review of treatment guidelines has to treat that reordering as the central event around which the current state of the art is organized.

State of the art in 2026

In 2026, the practical state of the art in RLS treatment is no longer “start with a dopamine agonist and escalate if needed,” but rather a stepwise, augmentation-averse strategy centered on three principles: first, remove aggravating factors; second, assess and correct iron status early; third, when chronic pharmacologic treatment is needed, use gabapentinoids/α2δ ligands first, reserving dopamine agonists for more selective use. This summary is consistent across the 2025 AASM guideline, 11 its supporting systematic review, 12 the 2021 updated algorithm, 10 and the recent JAMA review. 24

The first step in current care is to identify reversible contributors. Recent reviews emphasize stopping or minimizing medications that can worsen RLS, especially serotonergic antidepressants, dopamine antagonists, and centrally acting H1 antihistamines, and addressing coexisting sleep disorders and lifestyle factors. Iron evaluation is now considered mandatory in routine assessment, even when the patient is not frankly anemic. The 2021 algorithm recommends a full iron panel in all patients, while the 2026 JAMA review states that iron supplementation should be considered when serum ferritin is ⩽100 ng/mL or transferrin saturation is <20%, noting that some data support treatment even with ferritin values below 300 ng/mL in selected settings. 24

In current practice, iron therapy is not ancillary but one of the main therapeutic pillars. The 2018 IRLSSG iron guideline 17 already supported ferric carboxymaltose 1000 mg as effective in adults with moderate-to-severe RLS and ferritin <300 μg/L, and the 2025 AASM guideline gave strong support to IV ferric carboxymaltose, with conditional support for low-molecular-weight iron dextran, ferumoxytol, and oral ferrous sulfate. This means that, in 2025–2026, iron is not merely a supplement for deficiency but a central therapeutic axis with potential disease-modifying implications, particularly in symptomatic patients with low or low-normal iron indices or refractory disease.

For patients who need ongoing pharmacologic therapy, the current default is an α2δ ligand/gabapentinoids: gabapentin, pregabalin, or gabapentin enacarbil. The 2021 algorithm 10 explicitly places these agents first line for chronic persistent RLS unless contraindicated, and the 2025 AASM guideline 12 gives strong recommendations to gabapentin, gabapentin enacarbil, and pregabalin. The 2026 JAMA review 24 likewise states that, if medication is indicated, gabapentinoids are first-line treatment. In the United States, gabapentin enacarbil has an FDA indication for moderate-to-severe primary RLS in adults, whereas gabapentin and pregabalin are commonly used on an off-label basis despite their prominent position in guidelines.

Dopamine agonists have not disappeared, but they are no longer the preferred routine starting point for chronic treatment. Current guidance treats pramipexole, ropinirole, and rotigotine as effective but strategically disadvantaged by their long-term risk of augmentation and by concerns such as impulse control disorders. The 2025 AASM guideline 12 therefore issued conditional recommendations against their standard use, while still allowing that some patients may reasonably choose them when they value short-term symptom relief more highly than long-term risk. The 2026 JAMA review 24 goes even further in practical language, stating that dopamine agonists are no longer recommended as first-line medications because augmentation has an estimated annual incidence of about 7%–10%. At the same time, these agents remain FDA-indicated in the United States for moderate-to-severe primary RLS: pramipexole, ropinirole, and rotigotine all retain labeled indications, so the modern shift is not regulatory disappearance but clinical de-prioritization.

Although current algorithms clearly favor α2δ ligands for chronic persistent RLS, treatment selection remains phenotype-dependent rather than purely hierarchical. In practice, α2δ ligands are particularly attractive when insomnia, pain, anxiety, or prior augmentation are prominent concerns, whereas dopamine agonists may still be considered in selected patients with very severe motor symptoms, troublesome periodic limb movements, or a previous good dopaminergic response, provided that the risks of augmentation and impulse control disorders are carefully weighed. Modern management is therefore best understood as individualized sequencing within an augmentation-aware framework, rather than rigid application of a single universal first-choice drug.10,24

From an international perspective, however, the practical applicability of this hierarchy remains uneven. As introduced above, a WSS-led global review found broad support for the AASM framework but also identified important barriers to implementation outside the United States, particularly limited access to IV iron formulations and gabapentin enacarbil, regulatory restrictions, and regional disagreement regarding the avoidance of dopaminergic agents. 22

For refractory RLS, including patients with failed first-line therapy or established augmentation, current practice is more structured than in prior eras. The 2021 updated algorithm 10 defines refractory disease explicitly and recommends reassessing iron, correcting exacerbating factors, and considering combination therapy or opioids in selected cases. The 2025 AASM guideline 12 gives conditional support to extended-release oxycodone and other opioids, and emphasizes that low-dose opioids are often necessary in dopamine agonist-related augmentation and can facilitate tapering and discontinuation of dopaminergic therapy. The contemporary view is therefore that opioids are not casual treatment, but neither are they an exceptional taboo; rather, they are a legitimate option in carefully screened, severe, refractory, or augmented RLS. Studies on large patient cohorts with long-term follow-up demonstrate their efficacy with good tolerability even in the long term.25,26

A final feature of the current landscape is the emergence of additional non-dopaminergic pathways. The 2025 AASM guideline 12 gives conditional recommendations to dipyridamole, reflecting interest in adenosinergic mechanisms, and to bilateral high-frequency peroneal nerve stimulation, a noninvasive device-based option supported by short-term sham-controlled studies. 27 Primary sham-controlled evidence for this approach came from the TOMAC randomized clinical trial in medication-refractory RLS28,29 and some authors are beginning to evaluate the use of TOMAC as a well-tolerated and effective non-pharmacological treatment for RLS, both as adjunctive therapy and as monotherapy. 30

These approaches are not yet central enough to define standard first-line care, but they show that the field is moving beyond the old dopamine-versus-gabapentinoid binary toward a broader, mechanistically more diverse therapeutic portfolio. This mechanistic broadening is biologically plausible, as recent transcriptomic work in RLS identified dysregulation not only in dopaminergic-related processes but also in cholinergic synapses, cAMP signaling, inflammatory/immune pathways including IL-17, NF-kB and NLR signaling, and neurodegeneration-related mechanisms such as mitophagy and ferroptosis.31,32 These findings could partially explain the distinct biological modulation compared with other disorders characterized by dopaminergic dysfunction, such as Parkinson’s disease, in which the associated iron metabolism differs, leading to different therapeutic implications.33,34

Taken together, the state of the art in 2026 can be summarized as follows: evaluate iron in everyone, correct iron more proactively than before, prefer α2δ ligands for chronic persistent RLS, reserve dopamine agonists for selected situations, and treat refractory/augmented disease with a structured pathway that may include opioids and newer non-dopaminergic options. The main unresolved issue is not what the current hierarchy is, which has become fairly clear, but how to optimize it across different phenotypes and special populations, thus, also considering the etiopathogenetic aspects of the disorder, and given that drugs such as dopamine agonists can modulate biological pathways in different ways. 35

Gaps, unmet needs, and future pathways

Despite the major conceptual progress reflected in the 2025 guideline, 12 the field still has several important blind spots. The first is that augmentation remains the central unresolved clinical problem. It was the main reason dopamine agonists were downgraded in the AASM guideline, 12 yet clinicians still face limited high-quality evidence on how best to taper, switch, sequence, or combine therapies once augmentation has emerged. Augmentation management is explicitly identified as an “important challenge” and notes that this represents a substantial change in recommended care because dopamine agonists remain widely prescribed. Recent augmentation-focused reviews make the same point: practice is ahead of trial evidence, especially for withdrawal strategies and transition pathways to α2δ ligands, opioids, or combination regimens.11,20,36

From a future perspective, non-pharmacological treatments will also need to be further explored, many of which are currently under investigation, such as the role of physical exercise, 37 acupuncture or yoga. 38

A second major gap is that RLS still lacks truly disease-specific pharmacotherapy. Among the therapies strongly recommended in the 2025 guideline, treatment development has largely proceeded by adaptation from other fields rather than by a clear understanding of RLS biology. New reviews published after the guideline echo this concern and argue that current treatment remains constrained by the limitations of repurposed agents, especially in severely affected or comorbid patients.11,39,40

That leads directly to the third unmet need: mechanism-based treatment development. This need is underscored by recent transcriptomic and proteomic evidence showing that RLS is characterized by closely interconnected dysregulated pathways spanning inflammation/immunity, neurotransmission, neurodegeneration, iron-related mechanisms, synaptic plasticity, axon guidance, mitophagy, and ferroptosis, thereby supporting a shift from empiric drug repurposing toward mechanism-based therapeutic development.31,41 The 2025 guideline’s future-directions section 12 states that advances may stall without a better understanding of RLS pathobiology and specifically highlights the need to clarify how iron and dopamine interact with other systems, including glutamatergic, GABAergic, endogenous opioid, melanocortin, and histaminergic pathways, pathways involved in RLS according to recent research.42,43 This direction is reinforced by recent genetics and mechanistic papers. The 2024 large genome-wide meta-analysis reported that RLS genetics 44 now yields insights into disease biology and risk prediction, while a 2025 perspective on the adenosine hypothesis argues that hypoadenosinergic mechanisms may offer a unifying framework linking iron deficiency, dopaminergic dysregulation, and hyperexcitability, with possible therapeutic implications beyond the traditional dopamine-versus-gabapentinoid model. 23 In practical terms, the most plausible future pathway is a shift from empiric drug repurposing toward targeted, biology-informed therapy, especially around adenosinergic and other non-dopaminergic systems.

Another potentially relevant non-dopaminergic pathway is CGRP signaling. Recent proteomic evidence supports altered CGRP-related biology in RLS, 45 and preliminary clinical observations in patients with comorbid migraine and RLS suggest that CGRP monoclonal antibodies may improve RLS symptoms in selected phenotypes. 46 However, these findings are still exploratory and require further confirmation to support CGRP-directed therapy as a general treatment strategy for RLS.

A fourth weakness is the persistent lack of robust evidence in special populations, especially children/adolescents and pregnancy/lactation. In pediatrics, evidence is insufficient for most adult drugs and that no recommendations can be made for many treatments because of the paucity of dedicated studies; recent pediatric reviews similarly describe a complex landscape with limited high-quality trials and continued reliance on iron-based and individualized strategies. 47 Pregnancy remains even more constrained: the 2015 consensus guideline is still a foundational reference, 48 and contemporary management remains centered on nonpharmacologic measures and iron, with only a few drugs cautiously considered when symptoms are truly refractory. These are not minor subgroups, so future progress will require dedicated trials and registries rather than extrapolation from adult nonpregnant populations.

A fifth and more methodological gap is that the field still lacks sufficiently mature biomarkers and outcome frameworks. Even for the 2025 guideline, 12 defining minimum clinically important differences and interpreting older trials was difficult because of inconsistent or non-validated scales. This matters because the next generation of RLS studies will likely need more than symptom scores alone: the field needs markers that predict iron responsiveness, augmentation risk, disease subtype, and perhaps opioid or device responsiveness, as well as outcomes that capture durability, sleep quality, daytime functioning, and treatment burden over years rather than weeks. Candidate biomarker directions are now emerging from genetics, neurophysiology, and imaging research, but they are not yet ready to guide routine treatment decisions.11,44

A related unmet need is better trial design. Future therapeutic studies should move beyond short-duration efficacy comparisons and instead incorporate longer follow-up, standardized definitions of augmentation, consistent iron phenotyping, and clinically meaningful patient-centered outcomes that capture sleep quality, daytime functioning, tolerability, and treatment burden over time. Head-to-head and strategy trials comparing treatment sequences, switch protocols, cross-titration approaches, combination therapy, and augmentation management in real-world populations will be particularly important if the field is to move from expert-driven algorithms toward more robust precision-based care.12,19,20,36

Finally, several future pathways already appear visible. One is augmentation-focused pragmatic trials, directly comparing tapering, cross-titration, iron repletion, opioids, and combination therapy in real-world patients.25,26 Another is expansion of device and neuromodulation approaches: 30 the 2025 guideline 12 conditionally supported bilateral high-frequency peroneal nerve stimulation, and a 2026 systematic review 49 suggests that neuromodulation for treatment-refractory RLS is now moving from proof-of-concept toward a broader mechanistic and interventional agenda. A third is precision phenotyping, integrating family history, iron profile, comorbidities, augmentation history, and possibly genetics or electrophysiology to guide initial treatment choice instead of using a single pathway for all patients. Altogether, the next phase of RLS therapeutics will likely depend less on finding one new “best drug” and more on building a stratified, mechanism-linked, long-term treatment model that is safer, more durable, and more tailored to specific patient profiles. Future progress will likely depend on integrating mechanistic insights with pragmatic, phenotype-driven clinical strategies.

Conclusion

Over the last decade, the evolution of RLS treatment guidance has been less a sequence of isolated updates than a progressive reordering of therapeutic priorities within an already chronic disease-management framework. The field has moved from a dopamine-first model, largely supported by short-term efficacy data, toward a more augmentation-aware strategy in which prevention of augmentation, systematic assessment and correction of iron status, and more selective use of dopaminergic drugs have become central principles. The 2016 task-force guidance on augmentation prevention and first-line therapy 9 initiated this rebalancing, the 2021 updated algorithm 10 translated it into practical bedside sequencing, and the 2025 AASM guideline with its companion systematic review11,12 formally consolidated the shift by strongly favoring α2δ ligands/gabapentinoids and iron-based strategies while downgrading routine dopamine-agonist use because of augmentation risk. This represents a continued evolution in guidance rather than a sudden rupture, and treatment for a chronic lifelong disorder should primarily be judged by durability and long-term safety, not just initial symptom suppression.

The current take-home message is therefore straightforward: modern RLS management should be iron-conscious, augmentation-aware, and deliberately non-dopamine-default. In practice, this means assessing iron in all patients, favoring α2δ ligands for chronic persistent disease unless contraindicated, reserving dopamine agonists for carefully selected situations, and using structured pathways for refractory or augmented RLS that may include opioids and newer non-dopaminergic approaches.10,11,17,24 However, recent WSS review suggests that this framework should not be interpreted as uniformly transferable across all healthcare systems, since implementation remains constrained in many regions by regulatory, economic, and therapeutic-access factors. 22 As such, the degree of adoption of this framework varies across healthcare systems. At the same time, the literature makes clear that important needs remain unresolved: augmentation still lacks robust comparative management trials, pediatric and pregnancy data remain sparse, and the field still depends heavily on repurposed rather than mechanism-specific therapies. The next phase of progress will likely depend on biology-informed treatment development, pragmatic augmentation trials, and more precise phenotyping, so that future guidelines can move beyond hierarchy shifts and toward truly individualized long-term care.
