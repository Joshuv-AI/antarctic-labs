Effectiveness of a personalised 6-month programme on physical frailty in older patients treated for bladder or kidney cancer – FRAGECO program – multicenter, controlled, randomized study protocol
Authors: Evolène Fayolle, Amandine Baudot, Anne-Françoise Chanelière-Sauvant, Emilie Favier, Florianne Rambaud, Fatah Torche, Thomas Celarier, David Hupin

Background

Cancer incidence increases with age, and in France, more than 62% of all new cases diagnosed in 2017 occurred in individuals aged 65 years and older [1]. The mean age at diagnosis is approximately 70 years for bladder cancer [2], and 65 years for kidney cancer [3], with surgery remaining the primary treatment approach [4].

For older patients, the French National Cancer Institute (INCa) recommends a preoperative frailty assessment and, when needed, referral to oncogeriatric care [5]. Oncogeriatric coordination units (UCOGs) offer comprehensive geriatric evaluations and multidisciplinary decision-making for patients aged 75 years and over, but no personalized pre- or postoperative care pathway currently exists to address frailty and preserve autonomy.

Frailty and its related manifestations, including fatigue, loss of strength and endurance, reduced mobility and undernutrition, are frequent and disabling in older cancer patients [6]. Cancer-related fatigue, often accompanied by up to a 30% decline in cardiorespiratory capacity, accelerates functional decline and loss of independence [6]. Undernutrition affects more than one-third of older patients and is associated with increased postoperative mortality, treatment toxicity, and reduced functional capacity [7–10]. Together with comorbidities, polypharmacy, and cognitive or balance impairments [10, 11], these factors promote sedentary behavior and postoperative complications, which affect 35–50% of elderly surgical patients [9, 12].

Frailty, defined by Fried’s criteria, is common among older adults with urological cancers, affecting approximately 40% of patients, with an additional 35% considered pre-frail [13, 14]. The frailty cycle describes how age-related factors and comorbidities lead to sarcopenia and reduced muscle strength and aerobic capacity, resulting in impaired walking performance, a core manifestation of physical frailty. Walking performance is therefore considered an integrative marker of frailty, reflecting the cumulative impact of multisystem dysfunction, and a strong predictor of adverse outcomes in older adults [13, 15, 16].

The six-minute walk test (6MWT) provides a standardized assessment of walking capacity and global functional status and is widely used to assess functional decline in older populations [17]. Accordingly, the 6MWT was selected as the primary outcome as a proxy measure of physical frailty, while a phenotype-based frailty measure (Fried criteria) is included as a key secondary outcome.

Although frailty becomes more prevalent with age, it remains a potentially reversible condition. Physical activity (PA) is one of the most effective strategies to counteract frailty [10], improving strength, cardiorespiratory fitness, gait speed [18], and reducing cancer-related fatigue [19]. PA practiced after surgery is also associated with reduced recurrence and improved quality of life [6]. Patient education further supports autonomy, self-management and long-term healthy behaviors [20].

Recent evidence supports multimodal prehabilitation programs combining PA, nutrition, and relaxation. In bladder cancer patients (mean age 69.7 years), such programs improved 6MWT performance four weeks post-surgery, although benefits were not maintained beyond eight weeks [21], highlighting the need for sustained and individualized interventions.

To fill this gap, we propose the FRAGECO program, a comprehensive multimodal pathway combining adapted physical activity (APA) and patient education before and after surgery, and evaluated in comparison with a control group receiving usual care. The objective is to reduce frailty, preserve autonomy, and maintain quality of life in older adults with bladder or kidney cancer. To promote long-term adherence, patients will be referred to Auvergne Rhône-Alpes Devices for Adapted Physical Activity Practice (AURA DAPAPs), facilitating continuity of training and maximizing long-term benefits [22].

Methods and analysis

This is a randomized, controlled trial using a mixed-methods approach, combining quantitative and qualitative methodologies. This trial is registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT07073703) and was registered on 10 July 2025, prior to the first participant enrolment. Recruitment began on 3 December 2025.

Study setting and recruitment

Patients will be recruited by clinicians from the oncogeriatric units of Saint-Etienne University Hospital and the Loire Private Hospital, France. All patients aged 65 years and older diagnosed with bladder or kidney cancer and scheduled for surgery will be identified during multidisciplinary tumor board meetings. Eligible patients will be informed about the study during a follow-up consultation. The investigator will present the study objectives, provide an information leaflet, and obtain written informed consent from those who agree to participate. Participants are free to withdraw from the study at any time without any consequence to their medical care. Participants will be excluded from the study if the planned surgery is not performed (due to treatment modification) or if surgical complications occur that make continuation in the study impossible.

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board (n°24.04790.000316#1, CPP Est III, France) and the trial is sponsored by Saint-Etienne University Hospital.

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria: patients must be aged ≥ 65 years, have a diagnosis of bladder or kidney cancer, be scheduled for surgery, have no severe cognitive impairment that would prevent understanding of the protocol, be affiliated with the French social security system, and provide informed consent.

Exclusion criteria: patients will be excluded if they have previously undergone the same type of surgery, present major comorbidities contraindicating PA (e.g., severe cardiac, respiratory, or disabling musculoskeletal conditions), are under legal guardianship or deprived of liberty, or are already engaged in a private prehabilitation program prescribed by a physiotherapist.

Participants in the control group will receive usual care at each center, which includes comprehensive geriatric assessment prior to surgery and standard postoperative management. Routine referrals to physiotherapy, rehabilitation programs, or community PA initiatives may occur when clinically indicated; such referrals will be documented prospectively in the case report form to allow appropriate interpretation of between-group differences.

Intervention group: FRAGECO program

Patients in the intervention arm will follow the FRAGECO program: a six-month personalized care pathway combining APA and patient education, in addition to standard care (Fig. 1).

The program begins with a comprehensive multidisciplinary preoperative assessment carried out by an oncogeriatrician and an APA specialist. The evaluation covers functional, nutritional, and psychological domains, as well as an initial educational assessment to tailor the intervention to each patient’s needs. Based on this evaluation, an individualized six-month plan is developed, including APA sessions, patient education, and referral to the AURA DAPAP.

APA component

The APA program consists of two supervised one-month exercise periods:

Between Phase 1 and Phase 2, patients undergo surgery, followed, if required, by a rehabilitation-center stay, which typically lasts up to approximately one month depending on clinical needs. Phase 2 begins for all patients approximately one month after surgery, following discharge from acute care and any rehabilitation-center stay.

Each phase includes two 60-minute sessions per week, led by trained APA teacher. Each session is tracked in a logbook for each participant.

The program is individualized, progressive, and multimodal, integrating endurance, strength, and balance exercises using stationary bikes, treadmills, elastic bands, and light weights. Exercise intensity will be gradually increased and monitored using the modified Borg scale (0–10), with a target perceived exertion (RPE) between 3 and 7. Sessions are conducted face-to-face in small groups to promote motivation and peer support.

Intervention fidelity and adherence will be monitored using a standardized core program, documented in a participant logbook. This logbook contains the planned exercises, intensity, and duration for each session and records what is actually performed. Participants will be considered adherent if they attend at least 75% of planned sessions. Missed sessions may be rescheduled within the same week, but no additional sessions will be added beyond the planned two sessions per week. APA teachers follow the same core program across. Any deviations or adaptations made to accommodate a participant’s capacities (reduced intensity, modified exercises) are systematically recorded in the logbook. Regular review of logbooks by the coordinating team ensures consistent delivery and allows monitoring of intervention fidelity.

Adverse events (AEs) during the APA intervention are predefined to include, but are not limited to, falls, musculoskeletal injuries, and cardiopulmonary symptoms (chest pain, dyspnea, palpitations). Each exercise session is supervised by a APA teacher. Participants are continuously monitored for any signs of discomfort, abnormal vital signs, or other AEs. Standardized observation checklists are used to record participant status throughout the session. Perceived exertion is tracked for each participant to ensure safety. If a participant experiences an AE, the APA teacher will immediately stop the session or reduce the intensity based on the type and severity of the event. All AEs, adaptations, decisions, and participant responses are recorded in the session logbook. Serious adverse events are reported immediately to the study coordinating center and, when required, to the ethics committee, in line with institutional guidelines. Minor events are documented and reviewed during weekly team meetings to adjust the intervention safely.

Patient education component

The patient education module also consists of two one-month cycles, scheduled before and after surgery. Topics are tailored to each patient’s needs and interests, identified during the initial and third evaluations. Themes may include PA, nutrition, fatigue management, sleep, treatment adherence, and cognition. Within each theme, content is tailored to the patient’s individual needs and priorities, identified during baseline and mid-cycle evaluations. Standardized written materials (booklets/PDFs) are provided for all topics.

Each educational session lasts 60 min and is delivered by a multidisciplinary team (nurses, dietitians, sports medicine physicians, APA teachers). Education is provided in a hybrid format, combining in-person sessions at Saint-Étienne University Hospital with remote sessions to facilitate accessibility and adherence.

Attendance, content delivered, and any adaptations are recorded in a session logbook to ensure consistency across participants. This structured approach, combining standardized and tailored elements, ensures reproducibility while meeting individual patient needs.

Transition to long-term activity – AURA DAPAP referral

At the end of the phase 2, patients will be referred to the AURA DAPAP near their home. These community-based facilities provide supervised APA sessions once per week, supporting long-term adherence and the maintenance of regular PA beyond the study period.

AEs will be collected systematically in both the intervention and control groups throughout the study period, regardless of their presumed relationship to the intervention. This approach ensures comparable safety monitoring across study arms. All AEs and serious adverse events (SAEs) will be recorded in the case report forms with details on onset, severity, outcome, and suspected relationship to the intervention or study procedures. Events will be reviewed regularly by the study investigators. The principal investigator will oversee safety monitoring and ensure that SAEs are reported to the sponsor and relevant regulatory authorities according to applicable timelines.

Assessment measures

As part of the study protocol, patients in the control group will undergo additional follow-up assessments alongside their usual care. These follow-ups conducted at baseline and at 1, 2, 3 months, and at the final endpoint at 6months, will mirror the assessment schedule of the intervention group (Fig. 2). Participants will be informed in advance of all scheduled study visits and provided with a detailed information sheet outlining the study procedures.

All interviews will be recorded, transcribed verbatim, and analyzed using thematic content analysis following the six-step process described by Braun and Clarke [24]: data familiarization, initial code generation, organization of codes into themes, review and refinement of themes, and final definition and illustration of results. To ensure credibility, transcripts will be double-coded independently by two researchers trained in qualitative methods, with discrepancies resolved through discussion. An audit trail will be maintained for all coding and analysis decisions.

Data management and analysis will be conducted with Dedoose software (Version 10.0.35) (SocioCultural Research Consultants, Los Angeles, CA), a platform dedicated to the organization, analysis, and visualization of qualitative and mixed-methods research data.

Sample size

Based on various studies [25–27] we have assumed a minimum clinically important difference (MCID) of 30 m [21–23] for the 6MWT in this population, with a standard deviation of 45 m. With a two-sided alpha risk of 5% and a power of 95%, 48 participants per group would be required to demonstrate the efficacy of the intervention. Considering the frailty of the population and potential interruptions due to surgery or rehabilitation-center stay, we anticipated an attrition rate of 10%, leading to a target sample size of 53 participants per group, for a total of 106 participants.

For sensitivity analyses, a per-protocol analysis will also be performed, excluding participants who complete less than 50% of the planned APA sessions.

Randomisation and data collection

The randomisation list will be a computer-generated pseudo-random sequence, prepared prior to the start of the study by a statistician independent of the study team. Randomisation will be stratified by study center to account for potential site-specific differences. Details of block sizes and sequence restrictions are stored in a separate, secure document, inaccessible to investigators responsible for participant enrolment or intervention delivery.

To further minimize predictability at the site level, the intervention providers are informed of group allocation only after randomisation, and only for participants assigned to the intervention group. Control group assignments are not revealed to intervention providers.

Allocation concealment is maintained until the moment of randomisation. Randomisation is triggered centrally within the REDCap platform by the inclusion assessor during the V0 visit (before intervention), after eligibility has been confirmed and all baseline questionnaires and physical assessments have been completed. This ensures that recruiting staff cannot access or predict upcoming allocations.

All procedures for randomisation and allocation concealment are consistent with CONSORT 2010 recommendations for randomized trials, ensuring methodological rigor and transparency in reporting.

Data analysis

The study population will first be described overall and by randomized group (intervention vs. control).

The primary analysis will follow the intention-to-treat principle, including all randomized participants in the groups to which they were allocated, regardless of adherence to the intervention.

Given the longitudinal design with repeated measurements over time and the anticipated attrition in this older surgical population, the primary outcome (6-minute walk test at 6 months) and other repeated continuous outcomes will be analyzed using linear mixed-effects models. These models will include fixed effects for group, time, and group-by-time interaction, with a random intercept for participant to account for within-subject correlation. The baseline value of the outcome will be included in the model to improve precision and account for any residual imbalance. Study centrer may also be included as a fixed effect, consistent with the stratified randomisation.

The primary estimand is the adjusted between-group difference in 6MWT at 6 months, obtained from the group-by-time interaction in the mixed-effects model including baseline value as a covariate, and reported with 95% confidence intervals. This modelling approach allows inclusion of all available data without requiring complete-case observations.

Missing values for the 6MWT and other repeated outcomes will be handled under the missing-at-random (MAR) assumption using linear mixed-effects models, which appropriately accommodate intermittently missing observations. Reasons for missingness will be systematically documented (e.g., medical complications, withdrawal, loss to follow-up, death) to characterize missing data mechanisms. Deaths occurring before outcome assessment will be reported descriptively and considered in sensitivity analyses. To assess robustness to departures from the MAR assumption, we will conduct complete-case analyses, pattern-mixture models, and worst-case scenario analyses for the primary outcome. These analyses will explore the potential impact of missing data related to dropout, complications, or mortality.

Secondary outcomes will be analyzed using similar modelling approaches adapted to the nature of each outcome. Given the exploratory nature of secondary endpoints, no formal adjustment for multiplicity will be applied; these results will therefore be interpreted cautiously as hypothesis-generating.

Estimates will be presented with 95% confidence intervals and corresponding two-sided p-values, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Discussion

The FRAGECO programme represents an innovative approach to the management of older adults with cancer. This is particularly relevant in the current context, as cancer prevalence continues to rise in France and older people remain the most affected. Surgery is the most common treatment for bladder and kidney cancer, yet older adults are often affected to postoperative complications that compromise quality of life and autonomy. The challenge is therefore to develop strategies that limit the adverse effects of cancer and its treatment, while considering age-related vulnerabilities such as frailty.

The originality of this approach lies in the integration of both prehabilitation and rehabilitation within a single, continuous care pathway, specifically designed for older adults undergoing surgery for bladder or kidney cancer, populations highly susceptible to functional decline. Unlike conventional models, where postoperative recovery is often managed outside the structured cancer pathway, this integrated approach aims to reduce frailty, optimize functional recovery, and close a critical gap current oncogeriatric management.

A recent study showed that two weekly 60-minute sessions of moderate-intensity PA significantly reduced frailty in older adults (mean age 84.9) identified as frail [28]. Guided by these findings, the FRAGECO programme adopts a comparable training frequency and intensity to ensure both safety and clinical relevance.

Building on this exercise-based foundation, the integration of patient education seeks to strengthen patient engagement, self-management, and long-term adherence. Recent evidence suggests that combining empowering education with exercise yields significant improvements in frailty among frail and pre-frail older adults [29]. The inclusion of patient education is therefore intended not only to support short-term participation but also to promote lasting behavioural change. To ensure care continuity and sustained PA, patients are referred to the DAPAP at the end of the FRAGECO program. This transition supports long-term adherence, bridges the gap between hospital-based interventions and community care, and promotes durable lifestyle changes.

Another key innovation is the assessment of the programmes’s implementation. This will allow the identification of barriers and facilitators to integrating personalized APA and patient education within oncogeriatric care. It will provide valuable insights into contextual, professional and organizational factors influencing feasibility, acceptability, and sustainability, information essential for scaling and optimizing similar interventions.

A foreseeable challenge lies in both patient recruitment and maintaining adherence after surgery, as postoperative fatigue, complications or treatment-related side effects may reduce participation. Previous studies have identified comorbidities, physical symptoms, functional limitations, anxiety, and unfamiliarity with APA as common barriers during the perioperative period [30].

The results of this study could support the implementation of APA and patient education within oncogeriatric units, and more widely in oncology, where such interventions remain insufficiently developed and may provide a scalable model for routine integration of PA in cancer care pathways.
