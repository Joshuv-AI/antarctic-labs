Counterexamples to the Corsten–Frankl conjecture on diameter-Ramsey simplices Journal: European Journal of Combinatorics
Yaping Mao

Abstract
Corsten and Frankl conjectured that a simplex is diameter-Ramsey if and only if its circumcenter lies in its convex hull. We disprove this conjecture in every dimension d ≥ 3 d\geq 3 . The main tool is a sufficient criterion based on a higher-order deficit decomposition: if the squared deficits D 2 − ‖ p i − p j ‖ 2 D^{2}-\|p_{i}-p_{j}\|^{2} admit a nonnegative decomposition over subsets of the vertex set, with total mass at most D 2 D^{2} , then the simplex is diameter-Ramsey. The pairwise deficit criterion of Frankl–Pach–Reiher–Rödl is recovered as a special case. As an application, for every d ≥ 3 d\geq 3 we construct a diameter-Ramsey d d -simplex whose circumcenter lies outside its convex hull. A particularly simple family has squared edge lengths ‖ p 1 − p 2 ‖ 2 = ‖ p 1 − p j ‖ 2 = 7 ​ ( 4 ≤ j ≤ d + 1 ) \|p_{1}-p_{2}\|^{2}=\|p_{1}-p_{j}\|^{2}=7~(4\leq j\leq d+1) , ‖ p 1 − p 3 ‖ 2 = 4 \|p_{1}-p_{3}\|^{2}=4 , ‖ p i − p j ‖ 2 = 4 ​ ( 2 ≤ i < j ≤ d + 1 ) \|p_{i}-p_{j}\|^{2}=4~(2\leq i<j\leq d+1) .

1
Introduction

Euclidean Ramsey theory considers
the problems that
for any positive integer
r
and any configuration
K
,
does there exist an integer
n_{0}
such that
for any
n\geq n_{0}
and any
r
-coloring of
\mathbb{E}^{n}
,
there exists a monochromatic configuration congruent to
K
?
Erdős, Graham, Montgomery, Rothschild, Spencer, and
Straus
EGMRSS73
in 1973 first
investigated this problem.
We refer the reader to survey papers
GrahamTressler
,
GrahamOldNew
,
GrahamRecentTrend
.

For a positive integer
m
, write
[m]:=\{1,\dots,m\}
. A finite set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
is called
diameter-Ramsey
if, for every positive integer
q
, there are an integer
N
and a finite set
R\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
such that

\diam(R)=\diam(A)

and every
q
-coloring of
R
contains a monochromatic subset congruent to
A
. We write
R\to(A)_{q}
for this Ramsey property.

Throughout the paper, by a
simplex
we mean its vertex set. Thus an
(n-1)
-simplex is a set

A=\{p_{1},\dots,p_{n}\}

of
n
affinely independent points in a Euclidean space. Its circumcenter is the center of the unique sphere through its vertices.

Frankl, Pach, Reiher, and Rödl introduced diameter-Ramsey sets and proved, among other things, that regular simplices are diameter-Ramsey, that Cartesian products of diameter-Ramsey sets are again diameter-Ramsey, and that a pairwise deficit condition suffices for simplices to be diameter-Ramsey
FPRR
. Corsten and Frankl later proved that every finite spherical set with circumradius strictly larger than
\diam(A)/\sqrt{2}
is not diameter-Ramsey
CF
. Motivated by this, they
CF
proposed the following conjecture.

Conjecture 1.1
.

A simplex is diameter-Ramsey if and only if its circumcenter lies in its convex hull.

The purpose of this paper is to show that this conjecture fails in every dimension
d\geq 3
.

Our main structural observation is a sufficient criterion that may be viewed as a
higher-order deficit decomposition
. Let

A=\{p_{1},\dots,p_{n}\}

be a simplex with diameter
D
, and define its pairwise deficits by

\delta_{ij}:=D^{2}-\|p_{i}-p_{j}\|^{2}\qquad(1\leq i<j\leq n).

We show that if the deficits
\delta_{ij}
admit a suitable nonnegative decomposition over subsets of
[n]
, with total mass at most
D^{2}
, then
A
is diameter-Ramsey. The criterion from
[
FPRR
, Section 4]
is recovered when only two-element subsets are used.

2
Basic lemmas

We begin with two standard facts and include short proofs for completeness.

Lemma 2.1
.

Every regular simplex is diameter-Ramsey.

Proof.

Let
\Delta_{k}
be a regular
k
-simplex of side length
\ell
, and fix a positive integer
q
. Consider a regular simplex
V
with
qk+1
vertices and side length
\ell
. Then
\diam(V)=\ell=\diam(\Delta_{k})
. In every
q
-coloring of
V
, some color appears on at least
k+1
vertices. Any
k+1
vertices of a regular simplex form a regular
k
-simplex of side length
\ell
, so
V
contains a monochromatic copy of
\Delta_{k}
. Hence
\Delta_{k}
is diameter-Ramsey.
∎

Lemma 2.2
.

If
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
Y\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
are diameter-Ramsey, then their Cartesian product

X\times Y:=\{(x,y):x\in X,\ y\in Y\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m+n},

with the Euclidean product metric, is diameter-Ramsey. Moreover,

\diam(X\times Y)^{2}=\diam(X)^{2}+\diam(Y)^{2}.

Proof.

Fix a positive integer
q
. Since
X
is diameter-Ramsey, there is a finite set
S
such that

\diam(S)=\diam(X)\qquad\text{and}\qquad S\to(X)_{q}.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be the finite set of all pairs
(X^{\prime},c)
where
X^{\prime}\subseteq S
,
X^{\prime}\cong X
, and
c\in[q]
. Put
M:=|\mathcal{P}|
.

Since
Y
is diameter-Ramsey, there is a finite set
T
such that

\diam(T)=\diam(Y)\qquad\text{and}\qquad T\to(Y)_{M}.

We claim that
S\times T
witnesses that
X\times Y
is diameter-Ramsey for
q
colors.

Let
\chi:S\times T\to[q]
be a
q
-coloring. For each
y\in T
, the coloring

x\longmapsto\chi(x,y)\qquad(x\in S)

contains a monochromatic copy
X_{y}\subseteq S
congruent to
X
. Choose one such copy and denote its color by
c_{y}
. Thus each
y\in T
receives the auxiliary color

\phi(y):=(X_{y},c_{y})\in\mathcal{P}.

Since
T\to(Y)_{M}
, there exists a subset
Y^{\prime}\subseteq T
congruent to
Y
on which
\phi
is constant. Hence there are a fixed copy
X_{*}\subseteq S
congruent to
X
and a fixed color
c_{*}
such that
X_{y}=X_{*}
and
c_{y}=c_{*}
for all
y\in Y^{\prime}
. Therefore every point of
X_{*}\times Y^{\prime}
has color
c_{*}
, and
X_{*}\times Y^{\prime}
is a monochromatic copy of
X\times Y
.

The formula for the diameter follows from the product metric:

\diam(S\times T)^{2}=\diam(S)^{2}+\diam(T)^{2}=\diam(X)^{2}+\diam(Y)^{2}.

Therefore
X\times Y
is diameter-Ramsey.
∎

By induction, Lemma
2.2
applies to every finite Cartesian product of diameter-Ramsey sets.

3
A higher-order deficit criterion

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
A=\{p_{1},\dots,p_{n}\}
be an
(n-1)
-simplex with
n\geq 2
, and let

D:=\diam(A).

Relabel the vertices so that
\|p_{1}-p_{2}\|=D
. Let

\mathcal{B}:=\{B\subseteq[n]:|B|\geq 2\ \text{and}\ \{1,2\}\nsubseteq B\}.

Suppose that there are nonnegative real numbers
\alpha_{B}
, indexed by
B\in\mathcal{B}
, such that

D^{2}-\|p_{i}-p_{j}\|^{2}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}B\in\mathcal{B}\\
\{i,j\}\subseteq B\end{subarray}}\alpha_{B}\qquad(1\leq i<j\leq n),

(1)

and

\sum_{B\in\mathcal{B}}\alpha_{B}\leq D^{2}.

(2)

Then
A
is diameter-Ramsey.

Proof.

Define the reserve mass

\alpha_{0}:=D^{2}-\sum_{B\in\mathcal{B}}\alpha_{B}\geq 0.

We shall realize
A
inside a finite Cartesian product of regular simplices whose diameter is
D
.

If
\alpha_{0}>0
, let
S_{0}
be a regular
(n-1)
-simplex of side length
\sqrt{\alpha_{0}}
with distinct vertices

v_{1}^{(0)},\dots,v_{n}^{(0)}.

If
\alpha_{0}=0
, let
S_{0}
be a singleton and put
v_{1}^{(0)}=\cdots=v_{n}^{(0)}
equal to its unique point.

For each
B\in\mathcal{B}
with
\alpha_{B}>0
, let
S_{B}
be a regular simplex of side length
\sqrt{\alpha_{B}}
with vertex set

\{u_{B}\}\cup\{v_{i}^{(B)}:i\notin B\}.

This is possible because the displayed set has
n-|B|+1
vertices. Define

q_{i}^{(B)}:=\begin{cases}u_{B},&i\in B,\\[2.84526pt]
v_{i}^{(B)},&i\notin B,\end{cases}\qquad(i\in[n]),

and set
q_{i}^{(0)}:=v_{i}^{(0)}
.

Now put

\mathcal{R}:=S_{0}\times\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}B\in\mathcal{B}\\
\alpha_{B}>0\end{subarray}}S_{B}

and

q_{i}:=\bigl(q_{i}^{(0)},(q_{i}^{(B)})_{B}\bigr)\in\mathcal{R}\qquad(i\in[n]).

Fix
1\leq i<j\leq n
. In the factor
S_{B}
, the points
q_{i}^{(B)}
and
q_{j}^{(B)}
coincide if and only if
\{i,j\}\subseteq B
; otherwise they are distinct vertices of a regular simplex and are at squared distance
\alpha_{B}
. Therefore

\displaystyle\|q_{i}-q_{j}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{0}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}B\in\mathcal{B}\\
\alpha_{B}>0\\
\{i,j\}\nsubseteq B\end{subarray}}\alpha_{B}

\displaystyle=D^{2}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}B\in\mathcal{B}\\
\{i,j\}\subseteq B\end{subarray}}\alpha_{B}

\displaystyle=\|p_{i}-p_{j}\|^{2},

where the last equality is (
1
). Thus
\{q_{1},\dots,q_{n}\}
is congruent to
A
.

Moreover, no set
B\in\mathcal{B}
contains both
1
and
2
. Hence
q_{1}
and
q_{2}
are distinct in every nontrivial factor, and

\|q_{1}-q_{2}\|^{2}=\alpha_{0}+\sum_{B\in\mathcal{B}}\alpha_{B}=D^{2}.

The same sum is the squared diameter of the product
\mathcal{R}
, so

\diam(\mathcal{R})=D.

By Lemma
2.1
, every nontrivial factor of
\mathcal{R}
is diameter-Ramsey, and a singleton is trivially diameter-Ramsey. Repeated use of Lemma
2.2
shows that
\mathcal{R}
is diameter-Ramsey. Therefore, for every positive integer
q
, there exists a finite set
W_{q}
such that

\diam(W_{q})=\diam(\mathcal{R})=D\qquad\text{and}\qquad W_{q}\to(\mathcal{R})_{q}.

Since
\mathcal{R}
contains the subset
\{q_{1},\dots,q_{n}\}
congruent to
A
, every monochromatic copy of
\mathcal{R}
contains a monochromatic copy of
A
. Hence
W_{q}\to(A)_{q}
for every
q
, and
A
is diameter-Ramsey.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

If only two-element subsets are used in Theorem
3.1
, namely if

\alpha_{\{i,j\}}=D^{2}-\|p_{i}-p_{j}\|^{2}\qquad((i,j)\neq(1,2)),

and all other coefficients are zero, then (
2
) becomes

\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\bigl(D^{2}-\|p_{i}-p_{j}\|^{2}\bigr)\leq D^{2},

because the
(1,2)
-term is zero. This is exactly the sufficient condition proved by Frankl–Pach–Reiher–Rödl in
[
FPRR
, Section 4]
.

4
A counterexample family

We now specialize the construction in the proof of Theorem
3.1
to a simple three-parameter family. The first parameter is denoted by
s
to avoid confusing it with the number of colors in the Ramsey property.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
d\geq 3
and let
s,t,u>0
. Then there exists a diameter-Ramsey
d
-simplex

A_{d}(s,t,u)=\{p_{1},\dots,p_{d+1}\}

with squared edge lengths

\displaystyle\|p_{1}-p_{2}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\|p_{1}-p_{j}\|^{2}=s+t+u

\displaystyle(4\leq j\leq d+1),

(3)

\displaystyle\|p_{1}-p_{3}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=s+u,

(4)

\displaystyle\|p_{i}-p_{j}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=s+t

\displaystyle(2\leq i<j\leq d+1).

(5)

Proof.

Run the construction from the proof of Theorem
3.1
with
n=d+1
and

D^{2}:=s+t+u.

Choose the only nonzero higher-order coefficients to be

\alpha_{\{1,3\}}=t,\qquad\alpha_{\{2,3,\dots,d+1\}}=u.

The total non-reserve mass is
t+u\leq s+t+u=D^{2}
, so the reserve mass is
\alpha_{0}=s
.

The construction gives points
q_{1},\dots,q_{d+1}
in a Cartesian product of regular simplices such that

D^{2}-\|q_{1}-q_{3}\|^{2}=t,\qquad D^{2}-\|q_{i}-q_{j}\|^{2}=u\quad(2\leq i<j\leq d+1),

and all remaining deficits are zero. Equivalently, the squared distances among the
q_{i}
are precisely those listed in (
3
)–(
5
). The product
\mathcal{R}
used in this construction has diameter
D
and is diameter-Ramsey; since it contains
\{q_{1},\dots,q_{d+1}\}
, the final argument in the proof of Theorem
3.1
shows that this set is diameter-Ramsey.

It remains only to check that these points form a
d
-simplex. Since
\alpha_{0}=s>0
, the projection onto the
S_{0}
-factor maps
q_{1},\dots,q_{d+1}
to the vertices of a regular
d
-simplex. Any affine dependence among
q_{1},\dots,q_{d+1}
would project to an affine dependence among those
d+1
regular-simplex vertices. Hence no nontrivial affine dependence exists, and the points form a
d
-simplex. We denote this simplex by
A_{d}(s,t,u)
.
∎

Next we locate the circumcenter.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
d\geq 3
, and let
A_{d}(s,t,u)
be the simplex from Proposition
4.1
. Write the circumcenter in barycentric coordinates as

O=\lambda_{1}p_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{d+1}p_{d+1}.

Then

\lambda_{2}=\lambda_{4}=\cdots=\lambda_{d+1},

and, more precisely,

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{(s+t)(s+du)}{\Delta_{d}},

\displaystyle\lambda_{2}=\lambda_{4}=\cdots=\lambda_{d+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{(s+2t)(s+u)}{\Delta_{d}},

\displaystyle\lambda_{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{s^{2}+st+su-(d-2)tu}{\Delta_{d}},

where

\Delta_{d}:=(d+1)s^{2}+2d(st+su+tu).

Consequently, the circumcenter lies outside
\conv(A_{d}(s,t,u))
whenever

(d-2)tu>s(s+t+u).

(6)

Proof.

The distance data in (
3
)–(
5
) are invariant under every permutation of the indices
2,4,\dots,d+1
. The labelled configurations before and after such a permutation have the same distance matrix; by rigidity of Euclidean simplices, the permutation is induced by an isometry of the affine span. This isometry fixes the circumcenter, and the barycentric representation in a simplex is unique. Hence the barycentric coordinates corresponding to the indices
2,4,\dots,d+1
are equal. Write

\lambda_{1}=a,\qquad\lambda_{3}=c,\qquad\lambda_{2}=\lambda_{4}=\cdots=\lambda_{d+1}=b.

Let
\rho
be the circumradius and translate the circumcenter to the origin. Then
\|p_{i}\|=\rho
for every
i
, and

\|p_{i}-p_{j}\|^{2}=2\rho^{2}-2\langle p_{i},p_{j}\rangle.

Since
\sum_{j}\lambda_{j}p_{j}=0
and
\sum_{j}\lambda_{j}=1
, we obtain

\sum_{j=1}^{d+1}\lambda_{j}\|p_{i}-p_{j}\|^{2}=2\rho^{2}\qquad(1\leq i\leq d+1).

(7)

Using (
3
)–(
5
), the cases
i=1
,
i=3
, and a representative case
i=2
among the indices
2,4,\dots,d+1
of (
7
) give

\displaystyle(s+u)c+(d-1)(s+t+u)b

\displaystyle=2\rho^{2},

(8)

\displaystyle(s+u)a+(d-1)(s+t)b

\displaystyle=2\rho^{2},

(9)

\displaystyle(s+t+u)a+(s+t)c+(d-2)(s+t)b

\displaystyle=2\rho^{2}.

(10)

The normalization condition is

a+c+(d-1)b=1.

(11)

Subtracting (
8
) from (
9
) and subtracting (
9
) from (
10
) reduce the system to

(s+u)(a-c)=(d-1)ub,\qquad(s+t)(b-c)=ta,\qquad a+c+(d-1)b=1.

Solving these three linear equations gives

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle=\frac{(s+t)(s+du)}{\Delta_{d}},

\displaystyle b

\displaystyle=\frac{(s+2t)(s+u)}{\Delta_{d}},

\displaystyle c

\displaystyle=\frac{s^{2}+st+su-(d-2)tu}{\Delta_{d}},

with

\Delta_{d}=(d+1)s^{2}+2d(st+su+tu).

This proves the displayed formulae.

Because
s,t,u>0
, the denominator
\Delta_{d}
is positive, and the factorizations of the numerators of
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
show that they are positive. Thus the only barycentric coordinate that can be negative is
\lambda_{3}
. A point belongs to the convex hull of a simplex if and only if all of its barycentric coordinates are nonnegative. Therefore the circumcenter lies outside
\conv(A_{d}(s,t,u))
exactly when
\lambda_{3}<0
, which is equivalent to (
6
).
∎

Combining the preceding two propositions yields the desired counterexamples.

Theorem 4.3
.

For every integer
d\geq 3
, there exists a diameter-Ramsey
d
-simplex whose circumcenter lies outside its convex hull.

Proof.

Take
s=1
and
t=u=3
. By Proposition
4.1
, there exists a diameter-Ramsey
d
-simplex with squared edge lengths

\displaystyle\|p_{1}-p_{2}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\|p_{1}-p_{j}\|^{2}=7

\displaystyle(4\leq j\leq d+1),

\displaystyle\|p_{1}-p_{3}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=4,

\displaystyle\|p_{i}-p_{j}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=4

\displaystyle(2\leq i<j\leq d+1).

For this simplex, Proposition
4.2
gives

\lambda_{3}=\frac{1+3+3-(d-2)\cdot 9}{(d+1)+2d(3+3+9)}=\frac{25-9d}{31d+1}<0\qquad(d\geq 3).

Thus the circumcenter lies outside the convex hull of the simplex.
∎

Corollary 4.4
.

For every integer
d\geq 3
, there exists a diameter-Ramsey
d
-simplex that is not covered by the pairwise deficit criterion from
[
FPRR
, Section 4]
.

Proof.

For the simplex in Theorem
4.3
, we have
D^{2}=7
,

D^{2}-\|p_{1}-p_{3}\|^{2}=3,\qquad D^{2}-\|p_{i}-p_{j}\|^{2}=3\quad(2\leq i<j\leq d+1),

and all other pairwise deficits are zero. Therefore

\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq d+1}\bigl(D^{2}-\|p_{i}-p_{j}\|^{2}\bigr)=3+3\binom{d}{2}>7=D^{2}\qquad(d\geq 3).

Hence the pairwise criterion stated in Remark
3.2
does not apply.
∎

Example 4.5

(A concrete tetrahedron)
.

For
d=3
, the above construction gives a tetrahedron with squared edge lengths

\displaystyle\|p_{1}-p_{2}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\|p_{1}-p_{4}\|^{2}=7,

\displaystyle\|p_{1}-p_{3}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\|p_{2}-p_{3}\|^{2}=\|p_{2}-p_{4}\|^{2}=\|p_{3}-p_{4}\|^{2}=4.

Its circumcenter has barycentric coordinates

\left(\frac{20}{47},\frac{14}{47},-\frac{1}{47},\frac{14}{47}\right),

so it lies outside the tetrahedron.

One explicit realization is

p_{2}=(0,0,0),\qquad p_{3}=(2,0,0),\qquad p_{4}=(1,\sqrt{3},0),

p_{1}=\left(\frac{7}{4},\frac{\sqrt{3}}{12},\sqrt{\frac{47}{12}}\right).

The circumcenter is

O=\left(1,\frac{\sqrt{3}}{3},\frac{10\sqrt{141}}{141}\right).

Its barycentric coordinate at
p_{3}
is negative, so
O
lies across the face

\conv\{p_{1},p_{2},p_{4}\}.

5
Final remarks

The Corsten–Frankl conjecture therefore fails in every dimension
d\geq 3
. In particular, the position of the circumcenter alone does not characterize diameter-Ramsey simplices. Theorem
3.1
suggests that higher-order deficit decompositions may be a useful tool for studying diameter-Ramsey simplices.

The argument here does not address the planar case
d=2
.
