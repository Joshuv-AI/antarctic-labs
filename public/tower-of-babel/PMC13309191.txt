The path to securing a resource-intensive physician job in Canada: lived experiences of decision-makers and trainees
Authors: Kameela Miriam Alibhai, Elger Baraku, Isabelle Raîche, Michael Kim, Nada Gawad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Securing meaningful employment as a physician is anxiety provoking, particularly in resource-intensive specialties, and newly certified specialists continue to report difficulty obtaining employment despite proposed solutions. In this study, we aimed to explore the lived experiences of decision-makers and trainees navigating job-seeking and placement in resource-intensive specialties in Canada and identify suggestions for improvement.Methods:In this qualitative interview study, guided by phenomenology, we recruited trainees, recent graduates, program directors, and division chiefs from resource-intensive specialties across Canada, using purposive sampling. Participants completed semi-structured interviews, which we transcribed and inductively coded in duplicate using NVivo 12. We iteratively discussed the preliminary codes and grouped them into categories to identify overarching themes.Results:We interviewed 20 physicians across 15 specialties. Most described the hiring process as nebulous and difficult to navigate, citing challenges related to transparency, equity, and access. We identified 4 key themes: the hiring process is difficult to navigate and poorly defined, hiring decisions rely heavily on perceived fit, bias and inequity persist, and perspectives differ across participant groups. Participants suggested multilevel changes, such as creating national hiring guidelines to promote transparency and implementing structured career-planning initiatives for trainees.Interpretation:Our study highlights factors that affect the hiring process for resource-intensive specialties in Canada and demonstrates misaligned expectations among participants that may create additional employment challenges. Changes at the individual, institutional, and national levels should be considered to promote transparency and reconcile divergent perspectives.

The process of securing meaningful employment is anxiety provoking for most residency graduates.1,2 The Royal College of Physicians and Surgeons of Canada (RCPSC) published an employment study in 2019, which reported that a concerning number of new specialists face challenges securing jobs.3 These challenges are more prevalent in resource-intensive fields, where fewer than 10% of practising physicians work in private offices or clinics, such as critical care medicine and certain surgical specialties.3 Surveys across multiple resource-intensive specialties have shown that 22% to 34% of respondents consider themselves underemployed, whether by obtaining temporary employment as a locum, working at multiple centres where they have temporary hospital privileges, or pursuing fellowship training, in large part owing to limited job opportunities. 1,4,5 This is not a new problem, with decades’ worth of data demonstrating employment challenges across multiple specialties across Canada.3,5

Barriers that affect specialist employment are multifactorial and variable. The most common barrier, cited by 80% of newly certified specialist respondents in the RCPSC’s employment study, was the perceived lack of full-time job opportunities.3 Other barriers include poor access to job listings, desire to stay near family, need for spousal employment, and lack of available health care resources, such as funding or operating room time.3,4 To ameliorate their chances of securing employment, newly certified specialists try to establish a network to increase visibility as a job seeker, pursue additional training, and stay geographically flexible in where they seek employment.3 Even when prospective candidates implement these suggestions, however, they continue to face challenges in obtaining employment, which suggests that additional unnamed barriers may affect this process.6 Furthermore, the role that training programs and institutions seeking specialists play in the hiring process remains unknown.

We sought to explore the lived experience of Canadian specialist physicians regarding job-seeking and placement. This study had 2 objectives: to explore the job-hiring process for resource-intensive specialties in Canada through the lens of multiple groups, and to develop a set of suggestions to help navigate the hiring process.

Methods

Study design

In this qualitative study, grounded in an interpretivist paradigm, we conducted semi-structured interviews with 4 groups who recounted their experiences with job-seeking and placement. Interpretative phenomenological analysis (IPA) was the guiding methodology as it enables a detailed examination of participants’ lived experiences and the meaning they ascribe to them,7 with the goal of uncovering new insights about a specific phenomenon, which in this case is the job-hiring process.8–10 One of IPA’s defining features is its use of double hermeneutics, in which researchers actively interpret participants’ meaning-making through the lens of their own pre-understandings.7 This dual interpretive process enriches the analysis by acknowledging the researcher as a co-constructor of meaning and fostering deeper insight into how participants experience job-seeking and placement.11,12

Study setting

This study took place between 2021 and 2022. Participants who currently or previously trained or worked at the University of Ottawa or the University of Alberta were recruited. The University of Ottawa accepts approximately 210 new residents across all residency programs annually and is affiliated with 3 academic hospitals.13 Similarly, the University of Alberta accepts about 200 new residents annually and is affiliated with 4 academic hospitals. 14 Both are medium-sized academic institutions located in urban cities in Ontario and Alberta, respectively, with more than 70 Royal College–accredited specialty and 30 subspecialty training programs. We selected these 2 institutions as they are large enough to accommodate resource-intensive careers and also have a good variety of both academic and community employment opportunities. Additionally, the 2 universities are located in 2 different Canadian provinces with different population densities and demographics, which allowed us to capture potential geographic differences.

Study participants

We recruited participants from 4 groups: current residency or fellowship trainees, recent graduates, program directors, and division chiefs. Only current trainees within 2 years of completing their program were eligible. Additionally, only division chiefs affiliated with the University of Alberta or the University of Ottawa were eligible, to ensure they had sufficient experience interacting with residency training programs. This was done to capture perspectives from both employment-seeking physicians and leaders involved in training and selecting employees. We recruited participants exclusively from resource-intensive specialties, which we defined as any specialty where fewer than 10% of physicians work in private offices or clinics, based on the Canadian Medical Association Specialty Profiles.15 A private office or clinic includes any combination of a free-standing laboratory or diagnostic clinic, free-standing walk-in clinic, private office or clinic, research unit, or office. Accordingly, participants from 15 specialties were eligible for inclusion: anatomic pathology, anesthesiology, cardiovascular surgery, critical care medicine, emergency medicine, general surgery, geriatric medicine, hematology, medical microbiology and infectious diseases, medical oncology, nephrology, neurosurgery, nuclear medicine, radiation oncology, and urology.

We used purposive sampling to recruit participants. Four study authors (K.A., I.R., M.K., and N.G.) accessed publicly available lists of residents, program directors, and division chiefs from different specialties across both the University of Ottawa and the University of Alberta. All study authors also identified individuals from within their professional networks who had previously trained at the 2 institutions. Recruitment was guided by diversity of specialty, size of residency program, level of training (i.e., current trainees versus recent graduates), and years in leadership (i.e., as program director or division chief).

Data collection

We developed 4 participant-specific interview guides, consisting of between 16 and 25 questions, a priori and used them for each interview (Appendix 1, available at www.cmaj.ca/lookup/doi/10.1503/cmaj.251882/tab-related-content). We developed the guides iteratively using sensitizing concepts identified during literature review and in discussion with the study team. The first 5 questions collected standardized demographic information (e.g., age, gender, years in role). Questions used neutral language and included nonleading probes to allow for further inquiry. All interview guides were reviewed by 3 authors with graduate degrees in medical education and extensive experience in qualitative methodology (N.G., M.K., and I.R.). The guides were then piloted with a nonparticipant; minor wording changes were made for clarity before final review by the study team.

Semistructured, one-on-one interviews were conducted by 2 study authors (N.G. and K.A.) between 2021 and 2022. No participant had any pre-existing relationship with their interviewer. Participants did not receive any honoraria. Data collection continued until interpretive sufficiency was reached. To establish sufficiency, the research team met regularly during data collection to engage in reflexive discussions about the interview content. We analyzed transcripts iteratively until no new codes were generated. Given the diversity of participants recruited, the absence of new codes across interviews suggested that interpretive sufficiency had been reached.

We conducted all interviews over Zoom and audio-recorded them. The interviews were transcribed using a transcription tool (https://otter.ai/), with explicit participant consent obtained for third-party processing.16 Transcripts were manually reviewed for accuracy and deidentified by 1 of 2 study authors who had no pre-existing relationship with participants (K.A. and E.B.). All data were securely stored, and audio recordings were deleted after transcription and verification were completed. We implemented these procedures to ensure confidentiality and data security.

Data analysis

The analysis process was iterative and involved the entire research team. First, 2 independent coders (E.B. and N.G.) familiarized themselves with the first 5 transcripts by completing line-by-line reading. Salient quotes were highlighted, and reflexive memos were added as comments in NVivo (version 12, QSR International). Next, coding was conducted independently and in duplicate by the same 2 coders in NVivo. Codes were generated from participants’ own language where possible, to preserve meaning. The 2 analysts compared preliminary codes from the first 5 transcripts, discussed differences, and refined code definitions to create an agreed-upon working codebook. This codebook was iteratively updated as new concepts were identified in new transcripts. After all transcripts had been coded, the coders and a third author (K.A.) iteratively reviewed the coded data, clustered related codes, and collapsed them into higher-order categories. These categories were then organized into themes that captured participants’ lived experience with the hiring process. We directly asked participants how the hiring process could be more easily navigated, and used their responses to this question, or any unprompted suggestions, to generate a list of suggestions to improve job-hiring in Canada. The entire research team engaged in interpretive discussions to ensure the categories and themes reflected both the convergence and divergence of perspectives.

To ensure completeness in the reporting of this study, we followed the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research guideline.17

Team composition and reflexivity

Given the influence of the researchers’ pre-understanding in the analysis process, it is necessary to review the authors’ positionality. The senior author (N.G.) was a trauma and acute care surgery fellow at the time of data collection and was a newly hired staff surgeon during data analysis. She brought extensive experience in qualitative research and experience as a recent residency graduate. The first and second authors (K.A. and E.B.) were senior and junior medical students, respectively, at the time of data collection and transitioned to junior residents during analysis. Both had experience conducting cognitive interviews. As medical trainees without pre-existing relationships with participants, they were uniquely positioned as insider–outsiders. The third and fourth authors (I.R. and M.K.), academic general surgeons at the University of Ottawa and the University of Alberta, respectively, brought extensive experience in qualitative research. They offered seasoned perspectives as early and midcareer surgeon–scientists, respectively. The diversity in the authors’ stages of medical training and practice likely enriched the development of data analysis.

Ethics approval

Ethics approval was obtained from the University of Alberta Ethics Board (No. 00112929).

Results

We interviewed 20 physicians (Table 1). Interview duration ranged from 24 to 64 minutes. Twenty-five percent (n = 5) were current trainees and 30% (n = 6) were recent graduates who had completed training less than 1 year previously at the time of the interview. Half of the recent graduates had secured permanent employment. Of the remaining participants, 20% (n = 4) were program directors and 25% (n = 5) were division chiefs. Fifteen different medical (n = 9) and surgical (n = 6) specialties were represented, with no more than 2 participants from the same specialty. At the time of the study, participants were affiliated with a variety of hospitals across North America, with some having trained in foreign countries.

Participants drew upon their experiences from the various institutions at which they had trained, worked, or applied for work, and offered detailed accounts of the hiring process for resource-intensive specialties in Canada. They highlighted significant challenges related to transparency, equity, and access. The analysis identified 4 key themes: the process is difficult to navigate and poorly defined, hiring decisions rely heavily on perceived fit, bias and inequity persist, and perspectives differ across groups. Given these findings, we outline stage-specific suggestions to improve the hiring process, informed by participant insights.

A difficult-to-navigate and poorly defined process

Most participants described the job-hiring process as nebulous and difficult to navigate. Current trainees and recent graduates provided examples including a lack of a centralized system for job postings, little to no instruction on how to navigate the process, and a discrepancy between what is advertised and what actually occurs. A small minority of participants described the process as easy, straightforward, or both. These participants explained this was because of the abundance of jobs available in their specialty.

Despite the complexity of the process, participants identified traits that make navigating job-hiring easier, including being extroverted, organized, and proactive. Although many participants identified the same successful traits, being able to genuinely embody them was challenging for certain participants. The characteristics identified by current trainees and recent graduates were similar to those that division chiefs sought in successful applications. Supporting quotes can be found in Table 2.

Balancing fit with transparency

Among all 4 groups of participants, there was a mutual understanding that being both clinically excellent and hard-working is required but not sufficient to obtain a job. Instead, an applicant’s “fit” was frequently cited as an important factor in hiring decisions. An individual with a good fit was described as someone who had strong interpersonal relations, who meshed with the culture of the institution, and whose clinical and academic goals were aligned with those of their future practice partners.

Despite job postings that may describe a fitting applicant, participants described a lack of transparency. Participants noted that job postings are often a “sham” because the hiring institution already has a candidate in mind and therefore described the job selection process as “pretending to be transparent” (Program Director 02) and “a dishonest procedure” (Recent Graduate RG07). Whereas current trainees and recent graduates expressed the desire for greater transparency, program directors and division chiefs expressed negative consequences of compromising fit because of the risks associated with hiring the wrong person. Bias in the hiring process

When asked generic questions about the challenges that applicants face during the hiring process, many participants spontaneously cited issues related to equity, diversity, and inclusion (EDI). Table 4 contains illustrative quotes to capture this theme. More than one-quarter of participants had personally experienced or witnessed individuals being treated unfairly in the hiring process because of their gender, sexual orientation, or religious beliefs. Concerns related to EDI were spontaneously raised by members of the dominant group (i.e., self-identifying male, White) and minority (self-identifying female, or racialized) participants.

Influence of mentorship on the hiring process

Nearly all participants mentioned mentorship, which was described as both formal and informal. Program directors and division chiefs generally portrayed mentorship as informal and organically developed, often arising through shared interests or casual connections. In contrast, current trainees and recent graduates expressed a desire for more formal, intentional mentorship — particularly in the later stages of training — and specifically focused on navigating the job market. What distinguished successful versus less successful employment outcomes was whether mentorship evolved into sponsorship — when mentors took active steps to advance their mentees’ career prospects. Participants who successfully obtained employment after training — or the program directors and division chiefs who were involved in facilitating employment — described mentors who were deeply invested in their mentees’ trajectory. These mentors took initiative in asking about and planning their mentee’s future career, made networking calls, and explicitly shared key tips to successfully obtain employment. Conversely, participants with less successful outcomes described mentors who offered general guidance about the process, without a clear sense of accountability for the mentee’s trajectory. Perceived strategies for success also differed across groups. Current trainees and unsuccessful applicants viewed the process as driven by luck and informal networks, whereas successful applicants emphasized proactivity and networking. Program directors associated success with strong residency performance and the ability to “pass the 5-year interview” that is residency training; division chiefs underscored the importance of early relationship-building with decision-makers and clarity about one’s desired practice.

Perspectives also differed on when to prepare for the job search. Current trainees and unsuccessful applicants felt this should begin late in residency, whereas successful applicants, program directors, and division chiefs suggested starting as early as postgraduate year 1 and 2. Participants also described varied ways of learning about the hiring process — from reliance on mentors and word of mouth to self-directed learning, informal mentorship, or formal teaching within residency programs. Strategies for improvement

Participants identified strategies at the individual, training, institutional, and national levels that may be implemented to improve the hiring process (Appendix 2, available at www.cmaj.ca/lookup/doi/10.1503/cmaj.251882/tab-related-content).

At the individual level, trainees should think about their career goals as early as possible, network, and contact leaders at the institutions where they are interested in working. Additionally, trainees should find mentors who can provide advice and facilitate networking opportunities. Although participants did not agree on the optimal time to start these tasks, applicants should consider preparing at least 2 to 3 years ahead.

At the training-program level, career-planning initiatives should be organized. Current trainees and recent graduates stated that even when the training program addressed these topics, it felt like a performative endeavour that did not provide substantial or actionable guidance. Therefore, current trainees and recent graduates asked that training programs consider hosting formal sessions on navigating the job-hiring process and advice to develop a strong application portfolio.

Participants voiced the need for hiring institutions to produce an accessible list of upcoming jobs once a need is established and to clearly outline the types of candidates the institution is seeking. One division chief suggested that if a prospective candidate has already been identified, that should be stated in the job posting. Participants also described wanting more clarity about the administrative process once applications are submitted.

At the national level, participants recognized a role for advocacy from resident associations and specialty societies. This may include hosting province-wide job-hiring information sessions and creating national best-practice guidelines for job-hiring. Supporting quotes are included in Table 8.

Interpretation

This study sheds light on the complex and often opaque nature of the hiring process for physicians applying to resource-intensive jobs in Canada. Participants identified barriers including insufficient guidance during training, discrepancies between advertised job criteria and actual criteria used in hiring decisions, and a lack of centralized resources to learn about job information. Although proactive and networked candidates were more likely to succeed, the emphasis on “fit” in hiring decisions introduced an additional layer of complexity. These findings highlight the opportunity for improvement at the individual, program, institutional, and national levels to promote transparency and equitable access to employment opportunities among physicians.

The discrepancy between advertised and actual hiring practices was repeatedly identified among different participants in our study. Participants frequently described job postings as formalities, with hiring institutions having already identified their preferred candidates. This aligns with research on hiring practices in academia, where candidates are often identified through informal networks before the job posting is widely circulated, with bureaucratic inefficiencies limiting broader candidate access.18 Previous studies have also found inconsistencies between the formal criteria outlined in the job description and the criteria applied by the selections committee,18,19 with weight placed on subjective characteristics, such as enthusiasm or determination.20 Despite evidence of these discrepancies, certain program directors and division chiefs expressed caution about changing the existing hiring process, owing to risk of compromising on “fit.” This reluctance to change the hiring process exemplifies the concept of institutional inertia, defined as organizational resistance to change driven by established routines, bureaucratic complexity, and lack of incentive to remain innovative.21 Institutional inertia has been identified as a barrier to implementing equitable hiring processes in employment settings.22 Institutional preference for current hiring practices may contribute to applicant frustration and unintentionally result in inequitable and noninclusive physician hiring.23,24

The concept of “fit” also relates to a lack of diversity in hiring and was identified as a contentious factor in hiring decisions. Although participants emphasized its importance for ensuring team cohesion and long-term professional compatibility, the vagueness of “fit” often muddied the selection criteria. This concern aligns with organizational and sociological research demonstrating that “fit” can enable affinity bias, whereby decision-makers favour candidates who resemble existing members of the organization.25–28 Studies across professional labour markets have also shown that “organizational fit” can function as a cultural gatekeeping mechanism, allowing recruiters to rationalize exclusionary boundaries and perpetuate homogeneity.29,30 Moreover, when fit is applied in an unstructured way, it has been shown to amplify interviewer bias, and to frame judgments as job-relevant even when they are not.31 Our findings, along with previous research in this area, suggest that reliance on “fit” risks undermining EDI efforts. Specifically, fit may disadvantage candidates from diverse or underrepresented backgrounds, perpetuate homogeneity, and limit the pool of innovative perspectives and skills.29,32

Beyond fit, our study revealed stark differences in how different participant groups perceive the hiring process. Although program directors and division chiefs often thought that either they, or the residency curriculum, provided adequate preparation for trainees to participate in the job hunt, current trainees and recent graduates described feeling unprepared and unsupported. This discordance highlights that trainees seek more structured guidance, whereas faculty think that trainees are either independently accessing resources or benefiting from informal mentor interactions.

Future research should explore the impact of structured career-planning interventions and mentorship programs on hiring outcomes among resource-intensive specialty trainees. Additionally, national hiring guidelines and centralized job databases have not yet been developed. Establishing and evaluating the effectiveness of these interventions may improve the job-hiring process for newly certified specialists. Finally, structured approaches that integrate EDI principles — such as standardized evaluation during interviews, formation of EDI hiring subcommittees, and implicit bias training for committee members — should be considered to balance the subjective elements of fit with objective assessments.33–35

Limitations

First, although our qualitative approach provided rich insights into the hiring process for resource-intensive specialties, our findings are based on a sample of participants from just 2 Canadian academic institutions, which may limit transferability to other institutions, such as community hospitals or international health care systems. In particular, the perspectives of those hiring resource-intensive specialists at nonacademic hospitals were not obtained in this study. Given the theory behind phenomenology, generalizability was not an intended outcome; instead, our aim was to offer an in-depth exploration of participants’ lived experiences related to job-seeking and placement. Second, in the context of the COVID-19 pandemic, all interviews were conducted virtually using Zoom; therefore, the interviewers may not have appreciated various nonverbal cues. Finally, although we explored participants’ lived experiences of job-hiring, examining their long-term career trajectories was beyond our scope. Evaluating factors such as job satisfaction, retention, and professional development (i.e., promotions and research productivity) would help to identify issues that persist beyond the initial job-hiring phase.

Conclusion

The job-hiring process for resource-intensive specialties in Canada remains complex and nebulous for trainees and decision-makers, which poses challenges to specialists seeking employment. Our findings highlight numerous issues, including a lack of transparency, limited career-planning support, and the subjectivity of hiring decisions, which may prioritize “fit” over objective qualifications. Although proactive networking and early preparation may facilitate success, the absence of structured learning leaves many applicants unprepared for the job search. Addressing the challenges cited by participants requires a multilevel approach, including integrating formal career-planning curricula into residency; establishing transparent, best-practice hiring guidelines; and developing centralized job repositories.
