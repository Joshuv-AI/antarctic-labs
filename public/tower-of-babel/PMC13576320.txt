Root resorption in Class II malocclusion treatment with various methods
Authors: Fundagül Bilgiç Zortuk, Eyüp Burak Küçük, Çağrı Yeşildağ
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTo determine the apical root resorption (ARR) associated with three different orthodontic treatment methods for Class II malocclusion.MethodsARR of the upper and lower incisors was evaluated in patients treated with Forsus (FG; n = 20), headgear (CHG; n = 20), and fixed orthodontic appliances involving extraction of the upper first premolars (EG; n = 20). A total of 480 teeth were evaluated using panoramic radiographs obtained at T1 (after active treatment) and T2 (during retention). ARR was assessed using a scoring system ranging from 0 to 4. ANOVA, the Kruskal–Wallis test, and the chi-square test were used for statistical analysis.ResultsARR of the lower right lateral incisors in the FG was greater than that in the CHG at T1 (P = 0.031). In the EG, greater ARR was observed in the upper right lateral incisors at T1 (P = 0.037) and in the upper left central incisors at T2 (P = 0.038) compared with the FG. The resorption scores of the incisors did not change between T1 and T2 in any group, except for the lower left central incisors.ConclusionsCorrection of Class II malocclusions using Forsus, headgear, or extraction of the maxillary first premolars followed by fixed appliance treatment results in varying degrees of ARR after active treatment.Trial registrationRetrospective observational study. This study evaluated data from the years 2015 to 2024.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-026-09281-8.

Introduction

Apical root resorption (ARR) develops as a consequence of sterile inflammation induced by orthodontic forces, initially affecting the superficial root cementum and, in more severe cases, progressing to involve the underlying dentin. It is an undesirable complication of orthodontic treatment with fixed appliances and leads to a reduction in root length in the apical region [1–3]. Fortunately, severe root resorption resulting in tooth loss or the termination of orthodontic treatment is infrequently observed [4]. However, general dental practitioners often believe that ARR can be prevented and that orthodontists are responsible for root resorption occurring during orthodontic treatment [5]. Therefore, orthodontists need to identify the factors associated with ARR and minimize its occurrence as much as possible [6, 7].

Numerous studies investigating ARR have been reported in the literature [8, 9]. However, factors such as appliance type, the duration and magnitude of orthodontic forces, patient-related characteristics (including age, sex, genetic background, history of trauma, systemic health, type of malocclusion, and root morphology), the amount of tooth movement, and other variables have contributed to conflicting findings regarding root resorption [10–12]. Orthodontic treatment for Class II or Class III malocclusions may pose a greater risk of ARR than treatment for Class I malocclusions because of the increased severity of these malocclusions [13]. A systematic review reported that mild to moderate ARR in both the maxillary and mandibular teeth is frequently observed following non-surgical treatment of Class II Division 1 malocclusions [14]. Various treatment modalities, including distalization techniques using intraoral or extraoral appliances, functional appliances, premolar extraction protocols, and intramaxillary elastics combined with fixed orthodontic appliances, may be used to manage different types of Class II malocclusions [15, 16]. Because the prevention of orthodontically induced root resorption is crucial for maintaining dental health, understanding the relationship between different appliance types, treatment protocols, and the risk of ARR may help reduce the occurrence of treatment-related ARR and facilitate the selection of the most appropriate treatment approach [12].

This study aimed to compare the degree and prevalence of ARR among patients treated with a fixed functional appliance (Forsus), headgear treatment, or fixed orthodontic treatment involving first premolar extractions, both immediately after treatment and during the retention period. The null hypothesis (H0) was that no significant differences would exist in the degree of ARR among the treatment groups following active orthodontic treatment and during the retention period. The alternative hypothesis (H1) was that different orthodontic treatment protocols would result in different levels of ARR during active treatment and/or the retention period.

Material and method

Two-dimensional panoramic and cephalometric radiographs of patients treated with a Forsus Fatigue Resistant Device (FRD), cervical headgear, or fixed appliances with extraction of two maxillary premolars at the Department of Orthodontics, Hatay Mustafa Kemal University, between 2015 and 2024 were evaluated in this study. This retrospective cohort study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Hatay Mustafa Kemal University (Project No. 2024/19) and was conducted in accordance with the STROBE guidelines.

A pilot study including 10 patients in each group (Forsus group [FG], cervical headgear group [CHG], and extraction group [EG]) was performed for the power analysis. Calculations based on the resorption score of the lower right lateral incisor revealed a partial eta-squared value of 0.155, corresponding to an effect size of 0.428. Based on these values, with a statistical power of 80% and a Type I error rate of 0.05, the minimum required sample size was calculated as 19 patients per group, resulting in a total sample of 57 patients. To maintain the statistical power determined by the analysis, 60 patients were ultimately included in the study, with 20 patients allocated to each group. Sample size calculations were performed using a one-way ANOVA in Microsoft Excel and G*Power version 3.1.9.4.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: Class II Division 1 malocclusion with a bilateral molar relationship of at least one-half Class II, permanent dentition, an overjet greater than 3 mm, high-quality lateral cephalometric and panoramic radiographs, and the absence of dental agenesis, supernumerary teeth, transverse discrepancies, or systemic diseases.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: previous orthodontic treatment; endodontic treatment of the incisors; a history of dental trauma; the presence of supernumerary or impacted teeth; nail biting or other oral habits; tooth size or shape anomalies; dental agenesis; and the presence of ARR in the incisors before orthodontic treatment.

The selected patients were categorized into three groups. The Forsus group (FG) consisted of 20 patients (9 boys and 11 girls) with a mean initial age of 14.92 ± 1.49 years (Table 1) who underwent orthodontic treatment with fixed appliances in combination with a Forsus FRD (3 M Unitek, Monrovia, Calif.) for a mean treatment duration of 2.97 ± 0.56 years (Table 2). The Forsus FRD was removed after an overcorrected Class I occlusion had been achieved, with a mean duration of use of 5.6 ± 1.8 months.

The cervical headgear group (CHG) consisted of 20 patients (6 boys and 14 girls) with a mean initial age of 13.44 ± 1.47 years (Table 1) who were treated with cervical headgear followed by fixed appliances for a mean treatment duration of 3.01 ± 0.64 years (Table 2). The cervical headgear was worn for 14–16 h per day until a Class I molar relationship was achieved and delivered an average force of 450 g per side.

The extraction group (EG) consisted of patients who underwent camouflage treatment for Class II malocclusion involving the bilateral extraction of the maxillary first premolars. This group comprised 20 patients (10 boys and 10 girls) with a mean initial age of 14.96 ± 1.86 years (Table 1) and a mean treatment duration of 2.64 ± 0.49 years (Table 2). The orthodontic mechanics used in all groups included standard 0.022-inch slot straight-wire brackets.

For this study, patients whose digital radiographs obtained at pretreatment (T0), posttreatment (T1), and the retention period (T2) met the inclusion criteria were selected through a review of the archives of the Department of Orthodontics. The following variables were assessed on lateral cephalometric radiographs using NemoCeph NX 2021 software for Windows (Nemotec, Madrid, Spain): initial overjet, overbite, SNA0, SNB0, ANB0 and upper and lower incisor inclinations (Mx1.PP0, Md1.NB0) (Table 1); treatment-induced changes in overjet and overbite; and changes in incisor inclination between T0, T1, and T2 (Table 2). All T0 cephalometric radiographs were traced blindly.

Lateral cephalometric and panoramic radiographs were obtained using a Vatech PAX-I SC digital panoramic and cephalometric imaging system (Gyeonggi, South Korea). During image acquisition, patients were positioned with the Frankfort horizontal plane oriented parallel to the floor and the teeth in centric occlusion to ensure standardized radiographic conditions.

The degree of apical root resorption (ARR) in the maxillary and mandibular incisors (eight teeth per participant) was blindly assessed by an orthodontist (Ç.Y.) at T1 (after removal of labial fixed appliances) and T2 (during the retention period) using panoramic radiographs. The scoring system proposed by Malmgren et al. [17], ranging from 0 to 4, was used to evaluate ARR (Score 0: no resorption; Score 1: mild resorption, with the root maintaining its normal length but showing irregular contours; Score 2: moderate resorption, characterized by minor root resorption and an apex with a nearly straight contour; Score 3: severe resorption, with approximately one-third of the root length lost; Score 4: extreme resorption, with more than one-third of the root length lost) (Fig. 1).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) when they showed normal distribution and as median and interquartile range (Q1–Q3) when they did not meet the normality assumption. Because Malmgren ARR scores are ordinal variables, they were also reported as median (Q1–Q3). Categorical variables were presented as frequency and percentage.

Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Baseline demographic, clinical, and cephalometric characteristics were compared among the three treatment groups using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for normally distributed variables and the Kruskal–Wallis test for non-normally distributed variables. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test where appropriate.

Because apical root resorption (ARR) was assessed using the Malmgren scoring system, which represents an ordinal outcome ranging from 0 to 4, ARR-related outcomes were analyzed using non-parametric methods. Intergroup comparisons of ARR scores at post-treatment (T1) and retention (T2) were performed using the Kruskal–Wallis test. When significant overall differences were identified, pairwise comparisons were conducted using the Mann–Whitney U test with Bonferroni correction. Since three pairwise group comparisons were performed (FG vs. CHG, FG vs. EG, and CHG vs. EG), the adjusted significance threshold was set at p < 0.017 (0.05/3). No pairwise comparison was interpreted as significant unless it met this adjusted threshold.

Within-group changes in ARR scores between T1 and T2 were evaluated using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Differences in the distribution of ARR severity categories among treatment groups were assessed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

To account for potential confounding caused by baseline differences among treatment groups, multivariable ordinal logistic regression analyses were additionally performed. For each patient, the maximum ARR score observed among the eight evaluated incisors was used as the dependent variable. Treatment group was entered as the primary explanatory variable. Baseline variables that differed significantly among groups and were clinically relevant to ARR development, including age at T0, ANB angle, overbite, mandibular incisor inclination (Md1.NB), and severity of molar relationship, were included as covariates. Results were reported as regression coefficients (β), odds ratios (ORs), 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs), and p-values. These analyses were used to determine whether treatment modality remained independently associated with ARR severity after adjustment for baseline differences.

Statistical significance was accepted at p < 0.05 unless otherwise specified. Intra-examiner reliability was assessed by re-evaluating panoramic radiographs from 15 randomly selected patients after a 30-day interval. Reliability was quantified using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) with 95% confidence intervals. ICC values ranging from 0.92 to 1.00 indicated excellent intra-examiner agreement.

Results

A total of 480 teeth from 60 patients were examined at posttreatment (T1) and during the retention period (T2). Baseline assessment indicated that the treatment groups were not entirely homogeneous with respect to their initial clinical characteristics (Table 1). Specifically, participants in the CHG were significantly younger than those in the FG and EG, with mean baseline ages of 13.44 ± 1.47 years, 14.92 ± 1.49 years, and 14.96 ± 1.86 years, respectively (P = 0.005). Furthermore, the severity of the molar relationship differed significantly among the groups; the prevalence of a half-Class II molar relationship was 90% in the CHG, compared with 45% in the FG and 55% in the EG (P = 0.008).

Additional baseline differences were observed for overbite (P = 0.034), mandibular incisor inclination relative to the NB line (Md1.NB; P = 0.032), and the ANB angle (P = 0.040), further supporting the presence of initial clinical variability among the study groups. In contrast, no significant between-group differences were detected in sex distribution, overjet, maxillary incisor inclination relative to the palatal plane (Mx1.PP), SNA angle, or SNB angle (all P > 0.05) (Table 1). Given these baseline imbalances, the potential confounding effects of initial group differences were further accounted for in subsequent analyses using multivariable statistical modeling.

Evaluation of the treatment and retention periods revealed that patients in the CHG remained significantly younger than those in the FG and EG at both T1 and T2 (Table 2). At T1, the mean age was 16.44 ± 1.45 years in the CHG group, compared with 17.91 ± 1.56 years in the FG and 17.62 ± 2.00 years in the EG (P = 0.024). A similar pattern was observed at T2, with mean ages of 19.74 ± 1.93 years, 22.37 ± 2.39 years, and 21.55 ± 2.71 years in the CHG, FG, and EG, respectively (P = 0.004).

In contrast, neither active treatment duration nor retention duration differed significantly among the groups (P = 0.770 and P = 0.140, respectively), indicating comparable observation periods across treatment modalities.

Regarding treatment-related changes, overjet change during the T2–T1 interval differed significantly among the groups (P = 0.042). Likewise, overbite change during the active treatment phase demonstrated a significant between-group difference, with the FG exhibiting the greatest reduction (− 1.6 mm), compared with decreases of − 0.2 mm in the CHG and − 0.8 mm in the EG (P = 0.014). Conversely, changes in maxillary incisor inclination relative to the palatal plane (Mx1.PP) and mandibular incisor inclination relative to the NB line (Md1.NB) did not differ significantly among the groups (all P > 0.05) (Table 2).

Comparison of ARR scores among the treatment groups revealed that significant differences were confined to a limited number of teeth and were predominantly observed within the lower range of ARR severity scores (Table 3). At the post-treatment assessment, the ARR score for the maxillary right lateral incisors was 1 (1–2) in the EG and 1 (0–1) in the FG, and this difference was statistically significant (P = 0.037). During the same period, a statistically significant difference was also observed among the groups for the mandibular right lateral incisors (P = 0.031). The ARR score was 1 (1–1) in the FG group, 0 (0–1) in the CHG group, and 1 (0–1) in the EG group.

Discussion

Orthopaedic forces may lead to ARR, a frequent iatrogenic complication in orthodontic treatment [1]. The deterioration of root structure, which is minimal in most cases, varies between patients regardless of the type of appliance or the magnitude of orthodontic force applied. Resorption exceeds one-third of the initial root length in only a small percentage of cases [4].

The results regarding ARR in the treatment of Class II malocclusion have been contradictory [18–20]. In this retrospective study, we compared ARR scores in the upper and lower incisors under three treatment protocols—Forsus Fatigue Resistant Device (FRD), cervical headgear, and extraction of two maxillary premolars with fixed orthodontic treatment—to correct Class II malocclusion.

In this study, the scoring system of Malmgren et al. [17] was employed due to its reliability and widespread use in research evaluating ARR [6, 20, 21]. Most previous studies on Class II treatment have examined ARR in the incisor teeth [13, 21–23]. Severe ARR as a consequence of orthodontic treatment is a rare occurrence in the literature [4]. The Malmgren score distributions corroborated these findings, indicating that ARR was generally mild, with score 1 representing the most prevalent category in all groups, while severe root resorption (scores 3–4) was uncommon (Table 5).

The Forsus FRD is a fixed functional appliance that leads to unfavorable labial tipping of the incisors [23]. Previous studies have reported that the lower incisors, following the upper central and lateral incisors, are often affected by ARR after treatment with the Forsus FRD [21, 23, 24]. The results of the present study revealed that ARR in the lower right lateral incisors was significantly greater in FG than in CHG at T1. These results are consistent with the findings of previous studies.

Kaya et al. [6] observed a greater amount of ARR in all assessed teeth in cases involving premolar extractions. The authors reported that the ARR occurring in the lower incisors was similar between premolar extraction and non-extraction cases [6]. Sameshima and Sinclair [4] observed greater resorption in the upper incisors in cases involving premolar extractions compared with non-extraction cases. Shahrure et al. [25] also reported severe ARR in the upper incisors following orthodontic treatment. The present findings revealed that resorption in the upper right lateral incisors was significantly greater in the EG than in the FG at T1. Similarly, resorption in the upper left central incisors was significantly greater in the EG than in the FG at T2. These results are consistent with the findings of Sameshima and Sinclair [4] and Kaya et al. [6]. Intrusion into the cortical plate, associated with contact between the root apices and the palatal cortical bone during incisor retraction and torque control, may lead to increased compressive forces on the apical cementum, thereby initiating biological processes that contribute to external apical root resorption in the EG.

In previous investigations, it has been reported that the overjet and overbite correction could be correlated with a greater risk of resorption and this was also confirmed in our study [1, 4, 26]. In this study, the amount of overjet (T2-T1) were higher in EG than in the FG (Table 2). These factors may have contributed to the greater number of teeth with ARR in EG than in FG and CHG at T2.

In the present study, the ANB angle at T1 was lower in the EG. Variations in the ANB angle may affect the extent of root resorption, as greater distalization or retraction may be required in these cases. Nevertheless, the greater overjet reduction (T2–T1) observed in the EG may explain the higher number of teeth with resorption compared with the other groups.

The force magnitude applied by cervical headgear ranges from 200 to 400 g [16]. Because the force generated by headgear is distributed across the entire maxillary dentition, it is generally not associated with excessive root resorption [27]. In the CHG, ARR was predominantly mild (score 1) at T1, whereas the absence of resorption (score 0) was the most frequently observed finding at T2 (48.7% and 42.5%, respectively) (Table 4). Previous studies have suggested that this degree of resorption is clinically acceptable [3, 13]. In the present study, mild resorption was observed in 48.7% of the 160 teeth evaluated in the CHG at T1, decreasing to 40.0% at T2. The observed decrease was associated with an increase in resorption severity, as the frequencies of moderate, severe, and extreme ARR were higher in the CHG group at T2. The short-term retention assessment represents a limitation of the present study, as it may not have been sufficient to evaluate the potential repair of moderate and severe resorption. A longer follow-up period may reveal improvement even in teeth with higher resorption scores; however, it is also possible that moderate and severe resorption persists despite the cessation of active orthodontic forces (Table 4).

At T1, the number of teeth with no resorption (score 0) was lowest in the EG group. This finding suggests that ARR was more prevalent in extraction treatment cases. Furthermore, the numbers of teeth with resorption scores of 2, 3, and 4 were also higher in the EG group than in the other groups at T1. Therefore, the findings of the present study indicate that the frequency of teeth exhibiting ARR was greater in the extraction treatment group than in either of the two non-extraction treatment groups (Table 5).

Most importantly, in the multivariable ordinal logistic regression analysis, treatment modality was not independently associated with ARR severity, whereas baseline age emerged as the sole significant predictor of the maximum ARR score (Table 6). The intermittent force application and the younger age of the patients in the CHG may explain the lower prevalence of resorption observed in this group compared with the other treatment groups.

Copeland et al. [28] reported that ARR associated with orthodontic treatment ceases to progress after the active treatment period. Remington et al. [29] found that ARR most commonly occurred in the upper incisors during active orthodontic treatment and that no significant changes were observed during long-term follow-up, except for remodeling of the roughened and sharp root apices. Evidence has also indicated that post-retention ARR is not always associated with significant ARR that occurred during the active phase of treatment [28]. Instead, it is more commonly related to factors such as traumatic occlusion and the use of retainers that exert continuous force. In the present study, Table 4 shows a decrease in the frequency of score 1 and an increase in the frequency of score 2 at T2. Similarly, in the CHG, the frequency of score 1 decreased, whereas the frequencies of scores 2, 3, and 4 increased at T2 (Fig. 2).

A previous study reported signs of remodeling in three out of four resorptive areas that had developed during orthodontic treatment following 8 weeks of retention with a passive appliance [30]. Similarly, the present study demonstrated that mild resorption (score 1) detected in the lower left central incisors of the FG at the end of active treatment improved to score 0 during the retention period, suggesting remodeling of the affected root surfaces. Furthermore, in the extraction group, the frequencies of scores 3 and 4 decreased at T2, whereas the frequency of score 2 increased. This shift toward lower resorption scores may reflect remodeling of previously resorbed root surfaces.

Although statistically significant differences were identified (p < 0.05), these findings were primarily attributable to shifts between adjacent resorption scores (Scores 1 and 2). The lack of severe resorption (Scores 3–4) in the study sample limits the clinical relevance of these differences. Moreover, the observed changes were restricted to mild and moderate levels of resorption and did not reach a severity likely to compromise long-term tooth prognosis.

In the present study, high-quality panoramic radiographs were utilized to evaluate incisor root resorption. Accordingly, the findings should be interpreted as reflecting relative differences in resorption rather than absolute values. In contemporary orthodontic literature, the use of two-dimensional panoramic radiography is widely considered a methodological limitation, whereas three-dimensional imaging techniques, particularly cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT), are recognized as providing more accurate and reliable assessments. However, despite its superior sensitivity in depicting root morphology, CBCT cannot replace conventional radiography in routine clinical practice due to increased radiation exposure and cost. Conversely, Stramotas et al. reported that metric measurements on panoramic radiographs obtained at a consistent angulation, with the occlusal plane tilted no more than 10 degrees, were sufficiently reliable for estimating apical root changes over time [31].

Differences in clinical severity (ANB angle, molar relationship) and patient age between the groups may be considered limitations of the present study. Another limitation is the relatively short retention period. Longer post-treatment follow-up may provide a more comprehensive understanding of Class II treatment mechanics in relation to ARR.
