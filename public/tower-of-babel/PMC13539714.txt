PET/CT Radiomics-based assessment of tumor heterogeneity for predicting immunotherapy outcomes in advanced lung squamous cell carcinoma: a retrospective prognostic modeling study
Authors: Rui Zhang, Yan Li, Furui Duan, Ximei Wang, Minghui Zhang, Ping Li, Dalong Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAccurately predicting progression-free survival (PFS) in patients with advanced lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC) receiving immunotherapy remains a clinical challenge. Radiomics has emerged as a promising non-invasive approach; however, its application in this specific patient population remains relatively limited.MethodsA total of 129 patients were retrospectively enrolled. Radiomics features were extracted from baseline positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT) images. Feature reproducibility was evaluated using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC), and only features with ICC > 0.75, indicating good reproducibility, were retained. A three-step feature selection strategy, including univariate Cox regression, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO), and multivariate Cox modeling, was applied to construct the radiomics signature. Model performance was evaluated using time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis, Kaplan–Meier survival analysis, and bootstrap resampling. Additionally, a 3D convolutional neural network (CNN) model was explored for comparative analysis.ResultsThe final model achieved a 2-year AUC of 0.673. The relatively stable AUC values across time points (1-year: 0.670; 2-year: 0.673; 3-year: 0.651) suggest that the model primarily captures baseline risk stratification rather than strong time-dependent variation. Patients in the high-risk group exhibited a significantly increased risk of progression compared with those in the low-risk group (HR = 4.36, 95% CI: 2.70–7.04, P < 0.001). The radiomics signature remained an independent predictor of PFS in multivariate analysis. In comparison, the 3D CNN model demonstrated inferior predictive performance compared with the radiomics-based model.ConclusionThe PET/CT-based radiomics model enables non-invasive risk stratification of patients with advanced LUSC receiving immunotherapy and may provide complementary information for clinical decision-making.Keywords: PET/CT, Lung squamous cell carcinoma, Radiomics, Immunotherapy, Tumor heterogeneity

Introduction

Lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide [1]. LUSC represents a major histological subtype and is associated with poor prognosis [2]. In recent years, immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), particularly programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) and programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) inhibitors, have significantly improved clinical outcomes in patients with advanced non-small cell lung cancer, including LUSC [3, 4]. However, treatment responses to immunotherapy remain highly heterogeneous, and reliable biomarkers for predicting PFS are still lacking.

18F-FDG PET/CT is widely used for tumor staging and response assessment in lung cancer [5, 6]. However, conventional interpretation relies on visual or semi-quantitative metrics, which may be confounded by inflammatory or immune-related uptake, particularly in patients receiving immunotherapy. Radiomics has emerged as a promising approach for extracting high-throughput quantitative features from medical images, enabling the characterization of tumor heterogeneity beyond visual assessment. By capturing subtle spatial and textural patterns, radiomics may provide additional prognostic information and improve risk stratification in oncology [7–9]. Previous studies have demonstrated the potential of radiomics in predicting treatment response and survival outcomes in lung cancer [10, 11]. However, most existing studies have focused on heterogeneous patient populations or early-stage disease. Given that LUSC exhibits distinct metabolic profiles and immune microenvironment characteristics compared with lung adenocarcinoma, findings derived from mixed NSCLC populations may not be directly applicable. Consequently, radiomics-based studies specifically focusing on advanced LUSC undergoing immunotherapy remain scarce [12].

Recent reviews have highlighted the growing role of radiomics in precision oncology and immunotherapy outcome prediction, emphasizing its potential as a non-invasive biomarker for treatment stratification while also underscoring the need for large-scale validation studies [6, 9].

In this retrospective study, we aimed to develop and internally validate a PET/CT-based multi-modality radiomics model for predicting progression-free survival in patients with advanced lung squamous cell carcinoma undergoing immunotherapy. In addition, we explored the comparative performance of conventional radiomics and deep learning approaches and assessed the incremental value of integrating imaging and clinical information.

Materials and methods

Study population and design

This retrospective single-center study initially screened 166 patients with histologically confirmed LUSC who received ICI-based treatment between September 2017 and June 2025 at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Harbin Medical University. The Medical Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Harbin Medical University approved this retrospective study (No. YJSKY2025-100). After excluding 37 patients for reasons detailed in Fig. 1 and 129 patients were included in the final analysis. The patient selection process is illustrated in Fig. 1. Following patient inclusion, the overall study workflow is presented in Fig. 2. Inclusion criteria were: (1) Histologically confirmed LUSC; (2) Confirmed advanced-stage disease (Stage IIIB or IV); (3) ICI treatment (PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors) as first-line or subsequent therapy; (4) Baseline 18F-FDG PET/CT within 4 weeks prior to treatment; (5) Measurable disease per RECIST v1.1 [13]; (6) Adequate clinical follow-up. Exclusion criteria: (1) Prior systemic therapy that could confound baseline imaging assessment; patients were included only if baseline PET/CT was performed before initiation of the analyzed ICI regimen; (2) Concurrent active malignancy within 5 years; (3) Suboptimal PET/CT image quality; (4) Missing essential clinical data; (5) Corticosteroids within 4 weeks of ICI initiation. This retrospective study was approved by the institutional review board and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The requirement for written informed consent was waived.

PET/CT imaging protocol

Patients underwent PET/CT imaging using a Siemens Biograph 64 mCT scanner. All patients fasted for more than 6 h prior to examination, and blood glucose levels were confirmed to be below 11 mmol/L before administration of 18F-FDG. Subsequently, 18F-FDG was administered intravenously at a dose of 0.11 mCi/kg, with a pH range of 5–7 and radioactive purity exceeding 95%.

Image acquisition was performed 45–60 min after tracer injection. Whole-body PET/CT scans were obtained from the vertex to the proximal lower limbs. CT scanning was performed first, with parameters including a tube voltage of 120 kV and automatic tube current modulation using CARE Dose technology. PET acquisition followed CT scanning using a continuous bed motion mode at a speed of 1.6 mm/s.

PET images were reconstructed using a TrueD algorithm with CT-based attenuation correction. The PET reconstruction matrix was 200 × 200 with a voxel size of 4.07 × 4.07 × 3 mm, while CT images were reconstructed with a matrix size of 512 × 512 and a voxel size of 0.78 × 0.78 × 0.7 mm. Final PET images were corrected for attenuation using CT data and reconstructed using the TrueX + TOF reconstruction algorithm.

Tumor segmentation and feature extraction

Tumor segmentation was independently performed by two radiologists (with 10 and 8 years of experience) using semi-automated methods, and discrepancies were resolved by consensus. PET volumes of interest (VOIs) were defined using a standardized uptake value (SUV) threshold of 2.5 with manual correction, while CT boundaries were delineated using standardized window settings (window width: 400 Hounsfield units [HU]; window level: 40 HU). The intersection of PET and CT VOIs was defined as the final analysis volume. The intersection of PET and CT VOIs was selected to ensure that radiomics features were extracted from regions simultaneously demonstrating increased metabolic activity and anatomically identifiable tumor tissue, thereby reducing potential inclusion of inflammatory uptake, necrotic regions, atelectatic tissue, and segmentation inconsistencies.

To assess feature reproducibility, interobserver agreement was evaluated using ICC based on segmentations performed by the two radiologists. Quantitative radiomics features were extracted using PyRadiomics (version 3.9) following the Image Biomarker Standardization Initiative (IBSI) guidelines [14]. Feature categories included shape (n = 14), first-order statistics (n = 18), gray-level co-occurrence matrix (GLCM, n = 24), gray-level run length matrix (GLRLM, n = 16), gray-level size zone matrix (GLSZM, n = 16), and gray-level dependence matrix (GLDM, n = 14), derived from original images and filtered images, including wavelet decompositions and Laplacian of Gaussian filters with five sigma values.

A total of 3,264 radiomics features were initially extracted (1,632 from CT and 1,632 from PET). After reproducibility filtering using an ICC threshold ≥ 0.75, 1,652 robust features (851 CT-derived and 801 PET-derived) were retained for further analysis. All features were subsequently standardized using z-score normalization.

Clinical variables and missing data handling

Comprehensive clinical data were collected from electronic medical records, including demographic characteristics, smoking history, tumor markers, inflammatory biomarkers, PET-derived metabolic parameters, and treatment details.

Tumor markers included carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), neuron-specific enolase (NSE), cytokeratin 19 fragment (CYFRA21-1), and squamous cell carcinoma antigen (SCC). Inflammatory biomarkers comprised lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), and lymphocyte-to-monocyte ratio (LMR). PET-derived metabolic parameters included maximum standardized uptake value (SUVmax), mean standardized uptake value (SUVmean), metabolic tumor volume (MTV, defined as the metabolically active tumor volume above the predefined SUV threshold), and total lesion glycolysis (TLG, calculated as MTV × SUVmean). Laboratory measurements were obtained within 7 days prior to treatment initiation.

All key clinical variables required for survival analysis were complete, and patients with missing essential data were excluded. Occasional missing values in non-essential continuous variables were minimal and were imputed using median values.

Feature selection and model construction

To prevent data leakage, all feature selection procedures, including univariate Cox screening and LASSO selection, were strictly performed within each training fold of cross-validation, with no information from the validation folds used at any stage. Radiomics features and clinical variables were jointly entered into the LASSO-Cox regression framework; clinical variables were pre-screened based on clinical relevance and univariate Cox analysis (P < 0.10), and multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factor (VIF), with highly correlated variables excluded. The regularization process simultaneously performed feature selection across both imaging and clinical domains, yielding a parsimonious multi-modality signature.

The workflow consisted of three steps. First, univariate Cox regression screening was performed, and features with P < 0.10 were retained to avoid excluding potentially relevant predictors at an early stage, as subsequent LASSO regression would further perform variable shrinkage and selection [15, 16]. Second, LASSO-Cox regression with 5-fold cross-validation was applied to identify the optimal regularization parameter (λ_min). Third, the radiomics risk score (Rad-score) was calculated as a linear combination of selected features weighted by their coefficients: Rad-score = Σ(Feature_i × Coefficient_i). Patients were subsequently stratified into low- and high-risk groups based on the median Rad-score.

Model validation

Internal validation was performed using bootstrap resampling with 1,000 iterations to obtain robust performance estimates. In each iteration, patients were randomly sampled with replacement, and the complete modeling pipeline was applied to the resampled dataset. Model performance was evaluated using the bootstrap samples and corresponding out-of-bag observations to estimate optimism-corrected performance. The mean concordance index (C-index) with 95% confidence intervals was reported [17].

Bootstrap validation was used for final performance estimation of the selected LASSO-Cox model, while cross-validation was applied separately for hyperparameter tuning and model comparison.

Deep learning analysis (supplementary)

A 3D convolutional neural network (CNN) model was implemented as an exploratory analysis to assess the potential of deep learning in capturing complex imaging patterns. Given the limited sample size, the model was intentionally designed to be lightweight, and its results are presented for comparative purposes only. PET/CT images were resampled to 32 × 32 × 64 voxels, separately min-max normalized, and stacked as dual-channel input. The architecture comprised three convolutional blocks (16→32→64 filters, 3 × 3 × 3 kernels) with max-pooling and dropout (0.2), followed by global average pooling and a fully connected layer (32→1). Training used 5-fold stratified cross-validation, Adam optimizer (learning rate = 0.001), and early stopping (patience = 15).

Statistical analysis

Survival analysis was conducted using Kaplan-Meier estimators with log-rank test comparisons. Hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% CIs were estimated by Cox proportional hazards models. Model discrimination was evaluated by C-index and time-dependent ROC curves at 1-, 2-, and 3-year timepoints. AUC differences between models were assessed by the DeLong test [18], with P-values adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Bonferroni method. Calibration curves and DCA evaluated calibration and clinical utility, respectively [19]. The proportional hazards assumption of the final Cox model was assessed using Schoenfeld residual tests. All analyses were performed in R (v4.4.3) using the packages: glmnet, survival, timeROC, rms, ggplot2, and ggDCA.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 129 patients with histologically confirmed advanced LUSC were included (Fig. 1; Table 1). The median age was 63 years (range: 42–78), with male predominance (85.3%, n = 110) and 78.3% current or former smokers (n = 101). All patients had confirmed advanced-stage disease per the 8th edition TNM staging system: Stage IIIB (31.8%, n = 41) and Stage IV (68.2%, n = 88). Combination chemoimmunotherapy was administered in 91.5% of patients (n = 118). The median follow-up duration was 14.2 months (range: 3.2–36.8), and the overall median PFS was 9.0 months (95% CI: 8.5–10.1).

Final model performance

The final predictive signature consisted of 14 radiomics and clinical features (Table 2). The multi-modality predictive model yielded moderate but clinically meaningful discriminative ability (bootstrap C-index: 0.738, 95% CI: 0.682–0.791). Time-dependent AUC values were 0.670 at 1 year, 0.673 at 2 years, and 0.651 at 3 years (Fig. 3). The relatively stable AUC values across the 1- and 2-year time points suggest that the model primarily captures baseline risk stratification rather than strong time-dependent variation in hazard. The association between radiomics risk scores and PFS was illustrated in Fig. 4. The relative contribution of radiomics and clinical features within the final model is presented in Fig. 5. The 3-year AUC should be interpreted with caution due to the relatively limited follow-up duration.

The final model included 14 predictors with 87 observed progression events, corresponding to an events-per-variable ratio of 6.2. Although this value is below the traditional threshold of 10, recent methodological studies have shown that penalized regression approaches such as LASSO can maintain acceptable stability and reduce overfitting risk in high-dimensional settings with lower EPV values [20, 21]. Furthermore, fold-specific feature selection and bootstrap validation were implemented to further mitigate optimism bias. The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated for the final Cox model using Schoenfeld residual tests, and no statistically significant violations were observed (global test P > 0.05).

Risk stratification and survival analysis

Patients were stratified into low-risk (n = 64) and high-risk (n = 65) groups based on the median radiomics risk score. Kaplan–Meier analysis demonstrated significantly different progression-free survival between the two groups (log-rank P = 3.53 × 10⁻¹²; Fig. 6). Consistent with these findings, representative PET/CT images from the low-risk and high-risk groups (Fig. 7) revealed distinct imaging phenotypes corresponding to radiomics-based risk stratification. The low-risk group showed longer median PFS (12.5 months, 95% CI: 10.8–14.2) compared with the high-risk group (8.0 months, 95% CI: 7.4–8.5). The radiomics risk score was an independent prognostic factor in multivariate Cox regression (adjusted HR = 4.36, 95% CI: 2.70–7.04, P < 0.001).

The multi-modality model demonstrated numerically higher performance than the single-modality and clinical-only models (Table 3), with consistently higher AUC and C-index values. Model robustness and algorithm comparison

Across 5-fold cross-validation, the LASSO-Cox model achieved a mean 1-year AUC of 0.817 (SD: 0.112), comparable to RSF (0.831) and CoxBoost (0.816) (Fig. 9A). In RSF analysis, key radiomics features identified by LASSO-Cox were also ranked highly, supporting consistency across modeling strategies. The 3D CNN demonstrated lower performance (AUC = 0.643, SD: 0.176). Ridge-Cox analysis yielded higher apparent AUC values compared with LASSO-Cox, reflecting differences in regularization strategies (Fig. 9B). Discussion

This study aimed to develop and validate a multi-modality PET/CT radiomics model for progression-free survival prediction in patients with advanced lung squamous cell carcinoma undergoing immunotherapy. The final 14-feature LASSO-Cox model demonstrated moderate discriminative performance (bootstrap C-index = 0.738) and enabled significant risk stratification between low- and high-risk patients (log-rank P = 3.53 × 10⁻¹²; adjusted HR = 4.36, 95% CI: 2.70–7.04, P < 0.001), with acceptable calibration [8, 22]. Importantly, the integration of PET metabolic and CT morphological information with clinical biomarkers demonstrated improved discrimination compared with several comparator models, suggesting a potential complementary value of multi-parametric imaging in characterizing tumor behavior under immunotherapy. However, the improvement over the clinical-only model did not reach statistical significance (DeLong test, P = 0.067), and therefore the incremental value of radiomics should be interpreted cautiously pending validation in larger cohorts.

From a biological perspective, radiomics-derived signatures may reflect underlying tumor heterogeneity, including variations in cellular density, metabolic activity, and tumor microenvironment. The observed association between texture-based radiomics features and survival outcomes is consistent with the concept that imaging phenotypes may serve as non-invasive surrogates of tumor aggressiveness and immune-related heterogeneity [23]. These findings suggest that PET/CT radiomics may serve as a non-invasive surrogate of tumor heterogeneity associated with immunotherapy response [24, 25].

When compared with previous studies, the predictive performance observed in this work is consistent with reported ranges for PET/CT radiomics-based immunotherapy outcome prediction. The moderate predictive performance observed in this study may reflect the relatively homogeneous disease stage and treatment setting, as well as the stringent internal validation strategy employed. Notably, this study focuses specifically on advanced LUSC, a relatively underrepresented subgroup, potentially yielding more conservative but clinically realistic performance estimates. Prior studies have reported AUC values ranging from approximately 0.60 to 0.89 in various thoracic malignancies [10, 11], although differences in patient populations, endpoints, and modeling strategies limit direct comparisons.

Methodologically, the LASSO-Cox model was selected as the primary approach due to its balance between predictive performance, model sparsity, and clinical interpretability, as well as its suitability for high-dimensional radiomics data in the context of limited sample size [15, 16]. Although the events-per-variable (EPV) ratio was below the conventional threshold of 10, recent methodological studies have suggested that penalized regression approaches such as LASSO can maintain acceptable model stability in high-dimensional settings with lower EPV values [20, 21]. Sensitivity analyses using alternative algorithms, including random survival forests, ridge regression, and deep learning-based approaches, demonstrated broadly consistent predictive trends and supported the robustness of the selected radiomics signature [26, 27]. The relatively lower performance of the 3D CNN model likely reflects the limited sample size and the high dimensionality of volumetric imaging data, which remain common challenges for deep-learning applications in small imaging cohorts. Although ridge-based models achieved slightly higher apparent predictive performance, LASSO was ultimately selected because it provides greater sparsity and interpretability, both of which are important for potential clinical implementation. These findings highlight the importance of balancing predictive performance with model transparency and clinical applicability.

From a clinical perspective, the proposed radiomics signature may function as a complementary tool for risk stratification in patients undergoing immunotherapy, potentially assisting in identifying individuals at higher risk of early disease progression. However, its current role should be considered supportive rather than directive in treatment decision-making, pending external validation.

This study has several limitations. First, the retrospective single-center design and relatively modest sample size may limit generalizability despite internal cross-validation. Second, although bootstrap validation was performed, external validation in independent multi-center cohorts is lacking, which may limit the generalizability and transportability of the proposed model. Third, molecular biomarkers such as PD-L1 expression and tumor mutational burden were not available, limiting multi-omics integration. Finally, peritumoral regions were not included, although they may provide additional prognostic information in future studies. Moreover, the use of PFS as the primary endpoint may be confounded by pseudoprogression, a phenomenon well-documented in immunotherapy [28]. Radiomics features capturing metabolic heterogeneity may partly reflect inflammatory or immune-related responses rather than true tumor progression. Future studies incorporating biopsy-confirmed progression or immune-specific response criteria (such as iRECIST) would help validate the biological specificity of the identified radiomics signature [29].

Future work should focus on external validation across multi-institutional cohorts and integration of multi-omics biomarkers, including genomic and circulating tumor markers, to enhance predictive performance. Additionally, incorporating peritumoral radiomics features [30] and developing prospective radiomics-guided clinical trials will be essential steps toward clinical translation.

Conclusion

This study developed a multi-modality PET/CT radiomics model that enables clinically meaningful risk stratification for progression-free survival among patients with advanced lung squamous cell carcinoma undergoing immunotherapy. The proposed LASSO-Cox model provides a parsimonious and interpretable framework with potential utility for pre-treatment risk assessment and treatment stratification.

External validation in independent multi-center cohorts is warranted to further confirm its generalizability and clinical applicability.
