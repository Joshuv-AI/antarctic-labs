Acaricidal efficacy of medicinal plants against Rhipicephalus (Boophilus) microplus in Africa: A systematic review
Authors: M Tawana, T Sako, N Koza, T Ramatla, S Mbizeni, M Yawa, O Thekisoe, N Nyangiwe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe widespread misuse of synthetic acaricides for controlling and preventing tick infestations and tick-borne pathogens has accelerated the development of acaricidal resistance in various tick species. Rhipicephalus (Boophilus) microplus is a major cattle tick, and resistant strains to synthetic acaricides have now spread globally. The growing resistance to synthetic acaricides has fostered the search for eco-friendly alternatives, particularly medicinal plant extracts, for controlling R. (B.) microplus in cattle. This is the first systematic review to consolidate evidence on medicinal plants acaricidal activities specifically within the African context. Following PRISMA guidelines, this systematic review synthesizes original studies in English conducted between 2000 and 2025 that evaluate the acaricidal activity of medicinal plants against R. (B.) microplus in Africa. A total of 2,046 studies were retrieved after an extensive and comprehensive search using AJOL, CAB Abstract with Full Text, Google Scholar, PubMed, Scopus, ScienceDirect, SpringerLink, and Web of Science. After screening titles, abstracts, and full texts for eligibility, only 20 studies met the strict inclusion criteria. Acaricidal activity was reported in plant extracts from 26 different plant families, with Asteraceae (n = 7) and Lamiaceae (n = 7) being the most utilized, followed by Fabaceae (n = 5). The efficacy of medicinal plant-based acaricidal activity against R. (B.) microplus revealed overall, 40% of studies demonstrated high tick mortality, 35% reported moderate efficacy, and 25% showed low efficacy. Analysis of the data highlights a lack of published scientific research in several African countries on the use of medicinal plants for controlling R. (B.) microplus. Therefore, there is a need for more studies across the African continent to support the development of effective plant-based acaricidal products for tick control.Keywords: R. (B.) microplus, Acaricidal activity, Medicinal plants, Efficacy, Africa

Introduction

Ticks are categorized into three groups according to feeding life cycles including single or one host, two host and three host ticks (Fletcher, 2003). They undergo four phases throughout their lifespan including egg, larvae, nymphs and adult phase (Blouin et al., 2021). In one host tick after egg hatching phase, it prefers to undergo all four phases in one host until the adult tick phase jumps off the preferred host (Ashraf et al., 2024). Nonetheless, the growth rate of ticks during non-parasitic phase relies primarily on abiotic factors, particularly temperature which can either shorten this phase during hot temperatures or extend it during cold temperatures (Sales et al., 2024). The cattle tick Rhipicephalus (Boophilus) microplus is a one host tick widely spread in warm climate regions globally and responsible to cause serious threats to livestock production all over tropical and subtropical regions, including African continent, due to its role as a pathogen transmitting vector such as babesiosis and anaplasmosis (Dominguez-Garcia et al., 2010; Jongejan & Uilenberg, 2004; Nardoni et al., 2018). It is estimated that several billions of US dollars are lost every year to management and control ticks and tick-borne disease globally (Jongejan & Uilenberg, 2004). Usually for the management of ticks and tick-borne pathogens farmers and veterinarians solely depend on commercial acaricidal drugs such as macrocyclic lactones, organophosphate and pyrethroids their continuous use has resulted in widespread of acaricidal resistance, environmental pollution, and public health dangers to people and animals (Banumathi et al., 2017; Githaka et al., 2022). In Africa, tick-borne pathogens are a disproportionate burden and have a major impact on communal and small-scale farming systems. In Tanzania, livestock farming has suffered severe financial losses as a result of TBP, Kivaria et al. (2006) reported the financial losses caused by TBP in Tanzania at approximately at 364 million USD in damages. This decline encompassed bovine mortality approximately at 1.3 million livestock units or 7.34 percent of the stock. The principal Ixodidae tick taxa impeding livestock development in West Africa are classified under the taxonomic groups Amblyomma spp., Hyalomma spp., Rhipicephalus spp., and R. (Boophilus) spp. (Jongejan, 1999). Within these taxa, R. microplus was the most investigated over the past twenty years after its establishment in Côte d’Ivoire (Madder et al., 2007) and Benin (Madder et al., 2012) between 2002 and 2004. The additional tick taxa most investigated in West Africa is Amblyomma variegatum, along with its TBPs, particularly Ehrlichia ruminantium (Some et al., 2023). In response to these challenges, the use of medicinal plants extracts and oils has emerged as a promising alternative solution to the problems. Medicinal plants are rich in bioactive compounds including tannins, alkaloids, flavonoids, and essential oils. These botanical extracts show acaricidal, repellent and oviposition-inhibiting activities (Bravo-Ramos et al., 2021; Rosado-Aguilar et al., 2017). Indigenous veterinary knowledge across African societies has consistently documented the therapeutic effectiveness of botanical species such as Azadirachta indica, Calpurnia aurea, and Cissus quadrangularis in the management of tick infestation (Wellington et al., 2017; Fouché et al., 2017). Research has validated the efficacy of medicinal plants through in vitro assays such as larval packet tests and adult immersion tests which has confirmed their potential acaricidal activities, with some extracts achieving mortality rates comparable to commercial acaricides (Maddikontla et al., 2023). Moreover, plant-based acaricides offer ecological benefits, including rapid biodegradability, possess minimum toxicity to non-target animals, and less chances to develop resistance due to their complex phytochemical properties (Borges et al., 2011; Adenubi et al., 2018). These properties are in support with the role of sustainable agriculture and “One Health” concept (Fouché et al., 2019), making them more applicable for communal farming systems in Africa. While substantial investigations on plant-based acaricidal compound have been conducted in Latin America (Guzman et al., 2022; Sosa-Rueda et al., 2023; Zaldivar et al., 2024) and Asia (Banzragchgarav et al., 2024; Bisht et al., 2025; Udayan et al., 2020), African studies remain comparatively scarce.

Currently, there is no systematic review evaluating the acaricidal efficacy of medicinal plants against Rhipicephalus (Boophilus) microplus in Africa. This study aims to synthesize the existing literature, focusing on its relevance and application to African livestock systems, thereby addressing this significant knowledge gap.

Materials and methods

Study design and search strategy

Following Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) guidelines, we systematically searched electronic databases to identify, screen, and assess the eligibility of articles for inclusion in this review (Page et al., 2021, Ramatla et al., 2022). A systematic review was conducted through a comprehensive search of the AJOL, CAB Abstract with Full Text, Google Scholar, PubMed, Scopus, ScienceDirect, SpringerLink, and Web of Science databases, using relevant Keywords: "Rhipicephalus microplus" AND ("medicinal plants" OR "plant extracts") AND (acaricidal OR efficacy) AND Africa, to search for cross-sectional articles reporting acaricidal activities of medicinal plant extracts against cattle tick R. (B). microplus in Africa from January 2000 until July 2025. Primary search terms and their combinations were employed using Boolean operators, which were tailored to the specific syntax and functional requirements of each database. Detailed retrieval terms and search strategies are provided in the Supplementary Table S1. The last search took place on 15 July 2025.

Study selection and inclusion criteria

Two independent reviewers examined the titles and abstracts of the chosen studies to determine if they met the inclusion criteria. Studies were included if they fulfilled the following criteria: 1) the study had to be an original; 2) the exposure should be related to acaricidal effect (including: i. Mortality, ii. Repellency; iii. Oviposition inhibition; iv. Egg hatch inhibition) of natural plants; 3) the outcome should be the acaricidal effects of these plants against R. microplus and 4) the studies should be written in English. Conference abstracts, review articles and non-empirical studies (such as personal accounts) were excluded. Any questions regarding article inclusion were then forwarded to the third author for resolution. To make sure inclusion criteria were consistently followed, a third reviewer independently examined a subset (5%) of the study titles and abstracts for eligibility.

Outcome measures

The outcome measures of this systematic review were mortality rate, repellence, egg laying and hatching inhibitor of R. (B). microplus following the exposure to medicinal plant extracts. The efficacy outcomes were categorized into three levels based on percentage reduction in cattle tick populations relative to controls: high efficacy (≥70%), moderate efficacy (40 – 69%), and low efficacy (<40%). Each study was assigned to one of these categories and the portion of studies in each category was calculated.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Using a standardized data extraction form, researchers gathered the following data from included articles: 1) Authors, 2) year of study, 3) country, 4) region, 5) plant species, 6) plant family, 7) plant part used, 8) method of extraction, 9) tick stage, 10) mode of action, 11) solvent used, 12) type of bio-assay and 13) lethal dose. These data were then organized into a customized Microsoft Excel template. To accurately represent the effectiveness of these medicinal plants, the findings were further summarized into a set of relevant categories.

The quality of the included research was evaluated using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Tools for cross-sectional studies (Munn et al., 2020). Following this, a meticulous quality assessment of the selected sources was conducted, using both the Checklist for Analytical Cross-sectional Studies and the Checklist for Qualitative Research. The appraisal tool was assessed in four categories: “yes”, “no”, “unclear” and “not applicable”. The checklist for cross-sectional studies has eight criteria, while the one for qualitative studies had ten criteria. An article was included if it met ‘yes’ for at least five criteria. One study met ‘yes’ for eight out of eight questions. The second author confirmed the quality of the review and assessment process through critical dialogue to ensure the eligibility of the literature included in the review. This quality assessment aimed to evaluate the methodological rigor and potential bias in the studies. The findings from this assessment were considered when interpreting the results (Supplementary Table S2)

Results

The extensive search yielded a comprehensive dataset consisting of 2046 studies published between 2000 and 2025, providing a solid foundation for analysis. After the removal of 570 duplicate records, 1474 studies were screened based on their titles and abstracts, resulting in the exclusion of 1372. Among the 111 full texts assessed for eligibility, 91 were excluded for various reasons. Ultimately, 20 full-text studies met the inclusion criteria and were selected for data extraction (Fig. 1). Methodological quality was evaluated using the JBI Critical Appraisal Checklist (scores: 1 to 8). Study inclusion: All 20 papers included in this review had a minimum score of 50%. As a result, all included studies scored 5 or higher, which is considered an acceptable baseline quality (Moola et al., 2015).

Discussion

The escalating resistance to synthetic acaricidal drugs has emerged as a significant challenge within Africa. Considering this growing prevalence of acaricidal resistance, it is imperative to expedite scientific inquiry aimed at the identification and development of novel, environmentally sustainable acaricidal compounds. Unfortunately, this review reveals that advancement in the identification of novel, environmentally sustainable acaricidal drugs within Africa remains limited. While a substantial number of plant taxa have exhibited pronounced acaricidal efficacy under in vitro conditions, only a smaller subset has been subjected to in vivo evaluation to date. This underscores the imperative for both foundational and clinical research across the African continent.

This review identified research publications originating from eleven (11) African nations, with the majority produced in Benin and South Africa. The findings indicate that these two countries serve as principal contributors to scientific inquiry on acaricidal drug discovery and development, a role that is arguably justified given their substantial burden of tick related challenges. Madder et al. (2012) reported that R. microplus was the most investigated over the past twenty years after its establishment Benin was between 2002 and 2004. Notably, the co-leading of South Africa with Benin in terms of research output is unsurprising, as it may be explained by South Africa’s comparatively advanced technological and research infrastructure, in contrast to other nations that yielded only minimal contributions, often limited to a single publication. The African continent bears the greatest global burden of tick-borne Pathogens and acaricidal resistance, conditions frequently associated with udder damage, compromised hide quality, and ultimately livestock mortality. The distribution of the blue tick (Rhipicephalus microplus) across Africa is heterogeneous, with disproportionately higher concentrations observed in both East and West Africa. This pattern suggests that scientific inquiry into the development of environmentally sustainable acaricidal drugs is closely linked to the distribution of ticks and tick-borne pathogens, as well as to national policies governing their management. Consequently, there is a pressing need for policy driven research initiatives aimed at advancing novel acaricidal therapeutics across African regions.

A total of thirty-nine (39) plant species representing twenty-five (25) families were documented as exhibiting activities ranging from repellence to mortality against ticks. The most extensively studied families were Astreraceae, Lamiaceae, and Fabaceae; however, the families containing the most bioactive taxa included Lamiaceae, Asteraceae, Poaceae, and Meliaceae. These findings indicate that greater research emphasis should be directed toward species within these families for the development of environmentally sustainable acaricidal drugs. In addition, the plant species most frequently investigated were Ocimum gratissimum, Hyptis suaveolens and Calpurnia aurea. Notably, Ocimum gratisimum, Hyptis suaveolens, Chromolaena odorata, Cymbopogon citratus and Azadirachta indica were the only taxa to consistently demonstrate complete acaricidal mortality across studies. Surprisingly, none of these species have yet undergone in vivo evaluation, underscoring a critical research gap. Accordingly, further in vivo investigations of these plants are strongly recommended.

In this study, extraction solvents including ethanol, hydrodistillation, and dichloromethane were shown to exert acaricidal effects across multiple developmental stages of Rhipicephalus (Boophilus) microplus. Reported LC50 values were 0.95 mg/ml for eggs, 12.5 mg/ml for larvae, and 50 mg/ml for adults, which represent the minimum thresholds required for qualifying plant extracts in acaricidal drug screening (Aboagye et al., 2021; Achi et al., 2022; Salifou et al., 2020). Although ethanol was the most efficacious method yielding the lowest LC50 value. Nevertheless, it has been recommended that highly polar solvents such as water, methanol and ethanol be restricted to traditional preparations (Willcox, 2011).

With respect to compound screening methodologies, nearly half of the studies did not undertake chemical characterization of their bioactive constituents. Among those that did, Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) was the most frequently employed analytical technique. The predominance of GC-MS can be attributed to its status as the benchmark method for profiling volatile and semi-volatile plant metabolites, as it provides both retention time data and mass spectral information essential for structural elucidation (Al-Rubaye et al., 2017; Lisec et al., 2006; Thamer and Thamer, 2023; Willie et al., 2020).

This review revealed that, although various plant parts have been utilized, leaves emerged as the most frequently employed component in acaricidal drugs investigations. Comparable findings have been reported in studies from the global systematic review in plant acaricides against dog ticks, where leaves were consistently identified as the predominant plant part used in ethnobotanical acaricidal formulations (Molaba et al., 2025). The preference for leaves is rationalized by their easiness for collection, storage and processing, as well as their central role in photosynthesis and their abundance of bioactive phytochemicals (Amjad et al., 2017; Bibi et al., 2015; Faruque et al., 2019; Kayani et al., 2014; Vijayakumar et al., 2015). Moreover, leaf harvesting is comparatively less detrimental to plant growth and development than the removal of other plant parts (Alalwan et al., 2019; Kebebew, 2017).

The management of Rhipicephalus (Boophilus) microplus in Africa through the application of synthetic acaricides constitutes a significant “One Health” concern due to its interrelated consequences for animal health, human wellbeing and environmental integrity. The “One Health” approach underscores the interdependence of these domains, rendering pesticide contamination a transboundary issue (Destoumieux-Garzón et al., 2018; Bronzwaer et al., 2021). Persistent compounds such as DDT, organochlorines, and pyrethroids have been extensively identified in African rivers, groundwater, and even treated drinking water, frequently at concentrations equivalent to or surpassing those documented in industrialized economies (K’oreje et al., 2019; Okoye et al., 2022; Temima Jemal et al., 2023; Makgoba et al., 2024). Traditional drinking water treatment processes, including coagulation, filtration, and chlorination remove only a portion of these pollutants, permitting residual traces to remain in domestic supplies (K’oreje et al., 2022; Efikrie et al., 2020). For instance, in Ethiopia, conventional treatment eliminated up to 97% of certain pesticides, yet residual concentrations still exceeded established safety thresholds 0.1 µL (per pesticide) and 0.5 µL (sum of pesticides) in finished water (Jemal et al., 2023; Navarro et al., 2024; de Oliveira et al., 2023). Household interventions such as boiling and solar disinfection proved inadequate against highly persistent compounds like DDT (Jemal et al., 2023). These difficulties are further aggravated in African watersheds dominated by small rivers and wastewater discharges, which exhibit limited dilution and restricted self-purification capacity, thereby facilitating the accumulation of acaricides and other contaminants (K’oreje et al., 2018; K’oreje et al., 2019). Consequently, intensive reliance on synthetic acaricides fosters not only resistance in R. (B.) microplus but also prolonged contamination of aquatic ecosystems, potable water and the human food chain, ultimately compromising “One Health” objectives. Plant derived acaricides represent a more sustainable alternative consistent with One Health principles, as they diminish environmental persistence, chemical exposure and resistance pressure. Numerous botanical extracts and essential oils have demonstrated potent acaricidal and repellent properties against R. (B.) microplus, while being biodegradable and less likely to pollute meat, milk and water compared with synthetic formulations (Banumathi et al., 2017; Hoffmann et al., 2022; Nalak et al., 2022; Wellington et al., 2017). Moreover, many acaricidal plants are accessible and compatible with integrated pest management and organic livestock systems, rendering them economically viable and culturally appropriate for African smallholder farmers (Malak et al., 2022; Silnie et al., 2021). Given that African drinking water treatment systems frequently fail to eliminate pesticide residues entirely, substituting persistent synthetic acaricides with plant-based alternatives at the source constitutes a vital preventive measure for safeguarding water quality and public health (K’oreje et al., 2019; Jemal et al., 2023). Integrating medicinal plant acaricides into tick control programs can simultaneously enhance cattle productivity, protect human health and conserve ecosystems, thereby exemplifying the “One Health” paradigm for sustainable livestock production in Africa (Hoffmann et al., 2022). Policy mainstreaming and regulatory endorsement are indispensable for scaling up plant based acaricides within national livestock initiatives.

Limitation

Several key limitations have been characterized in the current systematic review. There is a bias regarding location because most of the studies have been carried out in Benin and South Africa and no studies were recorded from other 42 African countries, limiting their ability to be generalized across different agroecological regions in Africa. Another limitation associated with the methodology used in the selected articles includes variability in bioassay methods, extraction methods, and solvents. The majority of the studies relied on in vitro bioassays, which makes the findings less applicable in the practical field. Moreover, there is lack of complete phytochemical analysis in almost half of the studies analysed, thus limiting the understanding of the mechanisms involved in this area. It should be noted that there is publication bias in this review because only studies written in English language were considered.

Conclusions

Many plants in Africa serve as a potential source of acaricidal drugs against R. (B). microplus. Thirty-nine (39) plant species from 25 families demonstrated acaricidal activity, with Ocimum gratissimum and Azadirachta indica showing the highest efficacy. These plants have demonstrated promising results in in vitro studies; however further in vivo studies are required to confirm their effectiveness in real world livestock systems. At the same time, phytochemical analysis is necessary to isolate and characterize the bioactive compounds responsible for acaricidal activity in their purified form. This review highlights the phytochemistry, safety and efficacy of medicinal plants for the control and prevention of R. (B). microplus in Africa. Nevertheless, further investigations remain vital. These investigations should include dosage form development, clarification of mechanisms of action, assessment of the efficacy of individual and synergistic bioactive compounds, clinical trials and their comprehensive toxicity profiling.
