An autonomous AI agent for knowledge and data cooperation in ED clinical decision support
Authors: Peiyuan Lai, Zhenwei Huang, Xinhui Huang, Danyuan Xu, Cheng Li, Zenghui Wang, Huantao Cai, Xing Li, Jin Wu, Changdong Wang, Qingyun Dai, Li Li, Tao Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMedical knowledge accumulation and clinical practice form a closed loop, yet enabling effective cooperation between the two elements, namely autonomously distilling updated knowledge from dynamic data to guide practice, remains challenging, especially in the emergency department (ED). To overcome this, we developed an autonomous AI agent that integrates established medical knowledge graphs with dynamic clinical data into a hybrid graph of over 800,000 nodes. Using large language models (LLMs) for knowledge extraction and semantic mapping, the system dynamically selects the most relevant graph to power specialized tools for ED recognition, prediction, and decision-making. The agent achieves average improvements over state-of-the-art baselines of 23.13% in ED triage, 13.05% in drug–drug interaction detection, 1.58% in readmission prediction, and 5.47% in medication recommendation, demonstrating superior performance across all task categories. This demonstrates an effective framework for synergizing established medical knowledge and dynamic clinical data in emergency care.Subject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Health care, Mathematics and computing, Medical research

Introduction

Medical knowledge accumulates gradually through empirical observation, innovation, and dynamic clinical practice1. Foundational breakthroughs—from Koch’s establishment of germ theory2,3 to Watson and Crick’s discovery of the DNA double helix4—have systematically reshaped medical understanding. Concurrently, clinical practice relies on established knowledge to guide decisions, which in turn generates dynamic data that not only validates but also disruptively advances this knowledge base5. This is exemplified by the landmark randomized aldactone evaluation study (RALES), where spironolactone unexpectedly reduced heart failure mortality by 30%, leading to guideline updates and transformed care for millions6,7.

However, distilling and updating medical knowledge from dynamic clinical data remains challenging due to several factors. First, data fragmentation and heterogeneity—such as inconsistent electronic health record (EHR) documentation across hospitals—can severely limit model generalizability, as demonstrated in multi-center diabetes studies8. Second, real-world evidence sometimes contradicts randomized trials, illustrated by antiarrhythmic drugs that suppressed arrhythmias yet increased mortality in long-term use9. Third, the rapid generation of clinical data often outpaces guideline updates, delaying the adoption of effective treatments, as seen with dexamethasone in COVID-1910. Moreover, established knowledge itself can lag behind new evidence, exemplified when hormone replacement therapy was found to increase cardiovascular risks11,12. These challenges are particularly acute in emergency departments, where physicians confront highly complex conditions under severe diagnostic and therapeutic pressure. In such settings, the study of the cooperation between established medical knowledge and dynamic clinical data faces heightened demands. Thus, medical knowledge updating confronts three major challenges: data fragmentation and heterogeneity, evidence conflict, and knowledge lag.

Existing research has explored two primary approaches to linking medical knowledge with clinical data. Some studies leverage AI to extract medical knowledge from dynamic data, such as OHDSI’s standardized common data model13, TREWS’ real-time sepsis alerts14, and Shang et al.’s EHR-based knowledge graph for collaborative diagnosis15. Others integrate knowledge directly into models, as demonstrated by Sirocchi et al.’s knowledge-informed diabetes prediction16 and Futterman et al.’s guideline-based system reducing adverse events17. Although prior studies have explored linking medical knowledge with clinical data, few have addressed these challenges simultaneously within a unified agent framework. In particular, the need to choose between established medical knowledge and dynamic clinical data depending on the specific clinical task poses an additional challenge to the adaptability of traditional methods.

Recent advances in AI, particularly large language models (LLMs), offer new pathways for integrating medical knowledge with clinical data. Fine-tuned LLMs demonstrate strong clinical knowledge encoding capabilities18, while graph-based models improve disease prediction by combining data with domain knowledge19. Further, structured knowledge graphs built from medical texts20 and knowledge-guided patient stratification tools21 highlight the potential of these technologies. These studies highlight the potential of AI, LLMs, and knowledge graph techniques to enhance clinical decision support. Yet, they primarily focus on either knowledge extraction or disease prediction, without addressing the systematic bidirectional collaboration between established medical knowledge and dynamic clinical data—a capability essential for real-world, high-stakes settings such as emergency medicine.

To address these challenges, we developed and evaluated a novel autonomous AI agent for cooperation between established medical kn owledge and dynamic clinical d ata in emergency departments (KnDAgent, with pronunciation [nO’deIdZ@nt]) as shown in Fig. 1. Specifically, we integrated an established medical knowledge graph (EMKG) with a dynamic clinical data graph (DCDG) through fusion training to provide diverse clinical decision support in emergency departments, including recognition, prediction, and decision-making tasks. The KnDAgent system incorporates several components: (i) a knowledge extraction tool based on LLMs, which derives graph-style triples from established knowledge bases and dynamic clinical data; (ii) a data mapping tool for semantic alignment and node identification; (iii) a graph selection tool, which strategically selects the most appropriate graph, i.e., EMKG, DCDG, or a hybrid graph (HG); and (iv) an ED clinical decision support selector, which invokes task-specific modules such as recognition, prediction, or decision-making tools. By aligning the choice of graph and processing tool with the requirements of each emergency decision task, the AI agent delivers targeted decision support. Apart from the methodology contribution, another significant contribution of this work lies in constructing an EMKG comprising 33,740 nodes and 1,041,724 edges, a DCDG comprising 782,372 nodes and 5,847,941 edges, and an HG integrating 813,191 nodes and 6,889,665 edges. Through these resources, the proposed LLM-based agent achieves seamless fusion of established medical knowledge and dynamic clinical data. Across a range of emergency decision tasks, the KnDAgent system consistently demonstrated state-of-the-art (SOAT) performance.

In recognition tasks, we used emergency triage as a workflow scheduling engine and drug–drug interaction (DDI) detection as a safety sentinel. On a randomly sampled 20% subset of 98,719 real-world emergency cases, KnDAgent achieved an accuracy of 90.25%, outperforming the SOTA baselines by achieving average 23.13% improvement. For DDI detection across more than 300 drug pairs, the model reached 79.18% accuracy, achieving average 13.05% improvement over the SOTA baselines. In prediction tasks, we evaluated hospital readmission risk as a risk-radar scenario: on 67,686 cases (20% random sample), KnDAgent achieved 66.43% accuracy, exceeding the SOTA baselines by average 1.58% improvement. In decision-making tasks, we tested medication recommendation, the most complex setting. On 92,211 cases (20% random sample), KnDAgent achieved a Top-3 match accuracy of 83.52% with physicians’ prescriptions, improving by average 5.47% over SOTA AI baselines. Collectively, these results confirm a key finding of this study, i.e., established medical knowledge can effectively guide clinical practice, while dynamic clinical data enable continuous knowledge refinement. This synergy improves the accuracy of emergency decision-making and provides a robust foundation for deploying AI-driven medical knowledge updating and decision support in clinical care.

Results

We developed three complementary graphs: an EMKG, a DCDG, and a hybrid graph integrating the above two graphs, as shown in Fig. 2. The EMKG was derived from authoritative clinical guidelines and standardized ontologies, encompassing 33,740 entities, 47 relation types, and 1,041,724 edges, and capturing core diagnostic pathways and DDIs. The DCDG was constructed from 195,161 emergency department cases at Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hospital, integrating demographic profiles, physician orders, laboratory results, imaging reports, and longitudinal disease-course records. This graph comprised 782,372 entities, 28 relation types, and 5,847,941 edges, thereby representing the temporal dynamics of patient trajectories. By applying entity alignment, bidirectional completion, and LLM-based relation inference, we merged the two sources into a hybrid graph with 813,191 entities, 75 relation types, and 6,889,665 edges, uniting the authority of established medical knowledge with the real-time specificity of clinical data.

Use case 1 and 2: recognition capability

To evaluate the system’s recognition capabilities, we tested its recognition ability to identify key clinical information from patients’ current medical profiles and provide accurate judgments, e.g., assigning a patient’s symptoms to the appropriate emergency triage level or detecting potential DDIs. We conducted experiments across four datasets, assessing performance in emergency triage and DDI detection, representing real-time risk stratification and medication safety evaluation, respectively. These tasks are clinically important because they enable rapid, reliable decision support during the early stages of care.

To establish a comprehensive comparison for all recognition tasks, we evaluated KnDAgent against two major categories of baseline methods, namely graph-free approaches and graph-based ones. The graph-free approaches covered both of the traditional approaches such as LightGBM23, Logistic Regression24, Random Forest25, XGBoost26 and MLP27, as well as general-purpose LLMs such as GPT-5 (GPT hereafter)28 and DeepSeek-R1 (DeepSeek hereafter)29. The graph-based approaches were implemented by replacing the graph selected by KnDAgent with widely used public biomedical knowledge graphs. For the emergency triage task, we employed three clinical knowledge graphs—PrimeKG30, iKraph31 and ChEMBL32; Whereas for the DDI task, this category was further extended to include the general biomedical knowledge graph cBioPortal33, along with two DDI-specific molecular and pharmacological knowledge sources, namely, HuRI34 and BioSNAP-KG22. Notice that although both BioSNAP-KG and BioSNAP-DS were derived from22, they are different because the former is a comprehensive knowledge graph while the latter contains only DDI edges. As shown in Table 1, the ED-Triage dataset was derived from 98,719 emergency department visits at Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hospital. The original labels exhibited substantial class imbalance: level 4, 84.00% (82,917 cases); level 3, 12.23% (12,075 cases); level 2, 3.28% (3,245 cases); and level 1, 0.49% (482 cases). The dataset is called ED-Triage. As shown in Table 1, DDI detection task was performed on the public dataset, namely BioSNAP-DS (137 drugs, 9316 pairs, 5937 positive), with separate training and test sets for model evaluation.

As shown in Figs. 3 and 4, the KnDAgent system selected HG as the tool graph for both recognition tasks. For triage, the KnDAgent system achieved an accuracy of 0.9025 and an F1-score of 0.63, outperforming baseline models that relied on public knowledge graphs or graph-free AI approaches, indicating superior capability of capturing real-time patient risk signals. Compared with the graph-free LLM baselines, GPT and DeepSeek yielded substantially lower accuracy and F1-scores, indicating that general-purpose language models struggle to capture structured triage patterns without the support of graph-derived clinical context. In terms of statistical significance, KnDAgent showed clear and consistent advantages over most graph-free and graph-based baselines. For example, its F1-score (0.63) exceeded the strongest baseline by a substantial bootstrap-estimated margin, with a 95% confidence interval that did not overlap with those of PrimeKG, iKraph or ChEMBL (all adjusted p < 0.01). Improvements in accuracy and recall were similarly stable. Performance relative to the strongest tree-based models (e.g., LightGBM and XGBoost) was numerically higher (ACC) and directionally consistent but did not reach statistical significance across all metrics after multiple-comparison correction, indicating that these models form a competitive upper baseline. Full confidence-interval estimates and paired-bootstrap significance tests are reported in the supplementary materials (Supplementary Tables S1–S10).

For DDI detection, HG enabled better detection of rare interactions by integrating established medical knowledge and dynamic clinical data. While KnDAgent achieved robust DDI detection performance, it was not uniformly superior across all benchmarks. As detailed in Fig. 4 and Supplementary Tables S11–S23, although KnDAgent attained a markedly higher accuracy on the BioSNAP-DS dataset, its precision, recall, and F1-score were generally comparable to, rather than exceeding, those of the BioSNAP-KG baseline. This indicates that our KnDAgent’s primary advantage on this benchmark lies in its overall correctness (accuracy), while its discriminative capability on positive interactions, as captured by F1-score, is competitive. These findings suggest that the magnitude of KnDAgent’s advantage is context-dependent, varying with dataset characteristics. Notably, KnDAgent’s improvements were statistically significant, with non-overlapping bootstrap confidence intervals in F1-score relative to most baseline models (all adjusted p < 0.01). In contrast, purely text-based or graph-free approaches such as GPT and DeepSeek underperformed markedly, reflecting the inherent difficulty of modeling molecular and pharmacological interaction patterns without structured representations. These results collectively demonstrate that KnDAgent delivers strong and stable DDI detection performance, with its principal advantage lying in accuracy, while other metrics show competitive yet closely matched outcomes. Detailed bootstrap distributions and hypothesis-testing outcomes are provided in the supplementary materials (Supplementary Tables S11–S23).

Clinical case analyses further illustrate these advantages. Traditional triage guidelines classify “shortness of breath” as a level 3 indicator. However, when HG incorporated both established knowledge and real-time vital signs, it identified that patients with concurrent hypoxemia had a substantially increased risk of progressing to critical illness within 30 min. Consequently, the KnDAgent system with HG upgraded such cases to level 2 or 1 during triage, providing clinicians with crucial lead time for intervention. Similar insights were observed in DDI detection. Through dynamic mining of real-world prescriptions and adverse events, some drug combinations labeled as low-risk in conventional guidelines were found to significantly increase antiplatelet therapy failure, e.g., the combination of clopidogrel and omeprazole. This interaction was previously unrecorded in standard drug interaction databases. By integrating this signal, the KnDAgent system enabled timely alerts during medication decision-making, reducing the incidence of adverse drug events.

Use case 3: prediction capability

To evaluate the KnDAgent system’s prediction performance, we considered hospital readmission as a representative task, in which the system forecasts a patient’s future status—e.g., whether readmission will occur within 7 or 30 days-based on current clinical information. Real-world EHR data from the emergency department of Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hospital were used. As shown in Table 1, the 7-day readmission dataset (7d-RA for short) contained 67,686 cases (30,082 non-readmitted, 37,604 readmitted; readmission rate 55.56%, class imbalance ratio 1.25), and the 30-day readmission dataset (30d-RA for short) contained 85,773 cases (38,065 non-readmitted, 47,708 readmitted; readmission rate 55.62%), both showing a slight positive-class skew. To ensure methodological consistency with the recognition tasks, the baselines used in the prediction task followed the same two-category structure: graph-free models—including LightGBM, Logistic Regression, Random Forest, XGBoost, MLP, GPT and DeepSeek—and graph-based models implemented by replacing KnDAgent’s selected graph with publicly available knowledge graphs. Similar to the emergency triage task, PrimeKG, iKraph and ChEMBL were used as the core graph-based baselines. In addition, for the readmission prediction task, we incorporated one more clinically oriented biomedical graph, namely, cBioPortal.

For this task, the KnDAgent system selected the HG as the tool graph, leveraging its advantage in capturing infrequent but clinically critical readmission risk features. As shown in Fig. 5, for 7-day prediction, the KnDAgent system achieved accuracy 0.6643 and AUC 0.7229; for 30-day prediction, accuracy and AUC were 0.6588 and 0.7091, respectively. These results outperform both public knowledge graph-based and graph-free AI baselines across multiple metrics, demonstrating superior clinical utility in high-risk patient prediction. Compared to public knowledge graphs, the system also achieved higher F1-scores—0.7142 for 7-day and 0.7096 for 30-day predictions—exceeding PrimeKG and iKraph, and outperforming ChEMBL and cBioPortal by a larger margin. Notably, recall was 0.755 for 7-day and 0.7298 for 30-day predictions, surpassing all public knowledge graphs, highlighting the hybrid graph’s unique ability to identify infrequent but clinically critical readmission risk factors. Statistically, KnDAgent achieved consistent gains across both 7-day and 30-day readmission tasks. Its F1-scores (0.7142 for 7-day and 0.7096 for 30-day) were significantly higher than all graph-free baselines and most graph-based baselines (bootstrap tests, all adjusted p < 0.01). The performance gaps relative to ChEMBL and cBioPortal were particularly robust, with non-overlapping confidence intervals across recall, F1-score and AUC. Differences relative to PrimeKG and iKraph were directionally positive but did not remain statistically significant after multiple-comparison correction, reflecting their stronger baseline performance. Complete confidence-interval summaries are provided in the supplementary materials (Supplementary Table S24–S34 for 7-day and Tables S35–S45 for 30-day).

The clinical significance of this performance is clear. In the current chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) management guidelines, “smoking history” is recognized as an important readmission risk factor, but “recent weight loss” is not considered, causing some patients’ risks to be underestimated. Through autonomous analysis of emergency EHR data, DCDG identified the combination of smoking history and > 5% weight loss over the past three months as significantly associated with 30-day readmission—a relationship not present in the established knowledge. Incorporating this signal into HG enabled the system to identify high-risk patients at their first emergency admission, allowing early follow-up and intervention, thereby substantially reducing short-term readmission rates. These results not only validate the prediction advantage of fusing established medical knowledge with dynamic clinical data, but also demonstrate the system’s ability to discover and operationalize new clinical knowledge from real-world data.

Use case 4: decision-making capability

To evaluate the KnDAgent’s decision-making performance, we considered medication recommendation as a representative task, in which the system provides precise, safe, and clinically rational prescriptions early during emergency admission. As shown in Table 1, the MedRec dataset comprised 92,211 real-world emergency cases from Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hospital, including only the 70 most frequently used emergency medications. Each record contained demographic information, primary diagnoses, comorbidities, prior medication history, laboratory and imaging results, and physician orders, providing a comprehensive clinical context for medication recommendation. To maintain methodological consistency, the medication recommendation task used the same set of baselines as the readmission prediction task, with graph-free approaches (LightGBM, Logistic Regression, Random Forest, XGBoost, MLP, GPT and DeepSeek) and graph-based approaches (PrimeKG, iKraph, ChEMBL and cBioPortal).

For this task, KnDAgent selected the EMKG as the primary tool graph, leveraging its authoritative guidance on drug knowledge and safety. As shown in Fig. 6, KnDAgent achieved the highest Top-3 accuracy (0.8326) and Top-3 F1-score (0.8407). Compared with public knowledge graphs, it outperformed PrimeKG (F1 = 0.8161) and iKraph (F1 = 0.8253), and notably exceeded ChEMBL (F1 = 0.6909) by nearly 15 percentage points. While overall performance was comparable to cBioPortal, KnDAgent achieved higher recall, identifying more potential high-risk cases. Compared with graph-free AI baselines, KnDAgent consistently led across metrics: Logistic Regression and MLP yielded F1-scores of 0.8176 and 0.8213, respectively, whereas KnDAgent reached 0.8407, demonstrating that a knowledge-guided decision framework enhances reliability and clinical adaptability in complex tasks. From a statistical perspective, KnDAgent achieved the highest Top-3 F1-score (0.8407) among all methods. The improvement over ChEMBL was large and robust (F1-score difference = 0.1498, 95% CI: 0.1201–0.1795, Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted p < 0.001), and the gains over classical graph-free baselines such as LightGBM, Logistic Regression, Random Forest and XGBoost were likewise statistically significant for Top-3 F1-score and AUC (all adjusted p < 0.01). In contrast, differences relative to MLP, PrimeKG, and iKraph were numerically favorable but did not remain statistically significant after multiple-comparison correction, indicating that KnDAgent’s advantage over these stronger graph-based baselines is more modest. Full bootstrap confidence intervals and hypothesis-testing results are reported in the supplementary materials (Supplementary Tables S46–S56).

Clinical case analyses further illustrate the KnDAgent system’s value. For instance, established guidelines recommend combined aspirin and clopidogrel therapy for patients with acute myocardial infarction and diabetes. However, dynamic analysis of EHR data (DCDG) revealed that in patients with concurrent chronic kidney disease, this combination substantially increased bleeding risk. Replacing clopidogrel with ticagrelor maintained antiplatelet efficacy while reducing bleeding events. Incorporating this observation into HG allowed KnDAgent to proactively avoid high-risk medication combinations and recommend safer alternatives for specific patient populations, demonstrating its capacity to integrate dynamic clinical insights with established medical knowledge for individualized decision support.

Mutual enhancement of established medical knowledge and dynamic clinical data

To assess the mutual enhancement between established medical knowledge and dynamic clinical data, we examined two directions: (i) the effect of dynamic clinical data on enhancing EMKG, and (ii) the effect of established medical knowledge on augmenting DCDG, as shown in Fig. 7. Results demonstrate that relying on a single knowledge source without an intelligent agent has clear limitations. For instance, in 7-day readmission prediction (7d-RA), EMKG alone achieved an F1-score of 0.7083, while DCDG reached 0.7040, yet both exhibited complementary weaknesses: EMKG, though authoritative, lacked sensitivity to real-time risk signals, whereas DCDG could detect novel features but was unstable under sparse conditions. When the agent integrated the two into the HG, the F1-score was improved to 0.7142. A similar trend was observed in DDI detection: EMKG achieved F1 = 0.5853 on BioSNAP-DS, DCDG improved it to 0.6440, but both fell short of clinical requirements; under the KnDAgent with HG, F1-score rose to 0.7644. For emergency triage, EMKG and DCDG each emphasized different dimensions, yet HG reached F1 = 0.9025, achieving optimal performance. In medication recommendation, EMKG excelled in precision and F1-score, demonstrating superior overall balance. These results indicate that the agent does not simply stack knowledge sources; rather, it dynamically selects and integrates complementary information, mitigating the limitations of any single graph.

Clinical cases further illustrate the KnDAgent’s value. In drug safety management, EMKG alone could not detect the high-risk combination of clopidogrel and omeprazole, while DCDG could discover the risk from adverse event data but lacked interpretability and generalizability. By integrating both, HG provided clear alerts during medication recommendation, reducing risk. Similarly, in readmission prediction, the DCDG-derived feature “recent weight loss > 5%” was unstable in small samples, whereas EMKG contained stable priors such as “diabetes with coronary heart disease” but lacked dynamic sensitivity. Through the KnDAgent’s selective fusion, HG maintained prediction stability while uncovering new associations, e.g., prior coronary heart disease and elevated admission glucose were significantly linked to 30-day readmission, enhancing clinical applicability.

In summary, single-source graphs capture only partial knowledge dimensions and are insufficient for complex clinical scenarios. The KnDAgent’s integration of dynamic clinical data with established medical knowledge enables rapid updating and structured prior complementarity, yielding superior performance and robustness across tasks. This not only strengthens prediction and decision-making capabilities in emergency care but also lays the foundation for a sustainably evolving medical knowledge system and clinical decision support framework.

Hyperparameter sensitivity analysis

To systematically assess the robustness of KnDAgent across different clinical scenarios, we conducted a unified hyperparameter sensitivity analysis for all four tasks. This analysis examined how variations in key architectural and optimization parameters affected model performance, thereby evaluating whether KnDAgent’s gains were attributable to stable underlying design rather than narrow hyperparameter tuning. Across tasks, the analysis revealed a generally stable performance landscape, with most parameters inducing only minor fluctuations, and only a few settings—primarily learning_rate in certain tasks—showing stronger influence. The following paragraphs summarize task-specific patterns.

For the emergency triage task, the hyperparameter sensitivity analysis (Fig. 8a) showed that model performance was highly stable across a broad range of configurations. Variations in learning_rate (which controls the step size during gradient updates), num_leaves (the maximum number of leaf nodes in each decision tree), n_estimators (the total number of boosting iterations) and max_depth (the maximum depth allowed per tree) produced only mild fluctuations in ACC (generally within 1–2%), indicating that the selected configuration lies within a wide and robust performance plateau. This stability further suggests that KnDAgent’s strong triage recognition capability is driven predominantly by the underlying graph representations rather than by fine-grained hyperparameter tuning.

For DDI detection, the hyperparameter sensitivity analysis revealed a more heterogeneous pattern. As shown in Fig. 8b, we report ACC, Recall, Precision, F1-score, and AUC across variations in three critical hyperparameters, i.e., learning_rate, dropout_rate and hidden_layer_structure. Among the examined parameters, the learning_rate exerted the strongest influence on model performance, where exhibited moderate fluctuations when the step size was either too small or too large. This reflects the sharper decision boundaries intrinsic to the DDI detection task, which make optimization more sensitive to step-size selection. In contrast, variations in dropout_rate (which randomly deactivates neurons for regularization) and hidden_layer_structure (the width of each MLP layer) had comparatively mild effects, with performance changes generally remaining within a narrow 1 ~ 2% range, indicating good architectural robustness outside of learning_rate extremes.

For the hospital readmission prediction task, the hyperparameter sensitivity analysis demonstrated consistently stable behavior across all examined configurations. As shown in Fig. 8c, d, across perturbations in learning_rate, dropout_rate and hidden_layer_structure, performance fluctuations generally remained within 1–2%, confirming that KnDAgent’s predictive accuracy lies within a broad and robust plateau and is not overly dependent on precise hyperparameter choices.

For the medication recommendation task, hyperparameter sensitivity analysis (Fig. 8e) focused on learning_rate, num_leaves, n_estimators and max_depth. Among these, learning_rate exerted the most noticeable influence, producing more pronounced fluctuations in Top-3 ACC. The remaining parameters induced only mild changes—generally within a 1–2% range—indicating that tree-structure-related hyperparameters contribute little instability. Despite the stronger effect of learning_rate, no configuration led to abrupt performance degradation, suggesting that the HG’s structured drug-disease-interaction representations provide a stabilizing effect during optimization.

Discussion

The updating of medical knowledge has long posed a formidable challenge. New knowledge emerges continuously, yet existing knowledge must be repeatedly tested, revised, and at times overturned in practice. This process, while ensuring reliability, is inherently slow. In recent years, much research has focused on mining dynamic clinical data, particularly EHRs, to support clinical decision support systems35. Advances have been reported in health event prediction36, risk prediction37, and medicine recommendation38. However, these efforts largely neglect the integration of existing medical knowledge. To address the inherent trade-off between efficiency (e.g., rapid extraction of knowledge from EHRs) and reliability (e.g., the lengthy cycles of prospective clinical validation), we propose an intelligent co-update framework that couples existing knowledge bases with dynamic clinical data. Implemented through an adaptive agent capable of selecting the most suitable knowledge graph for different tasks, our approach offers a new paradigm for computer-assisted medical knowledge updating.

Although clinical guidelines and formalized knowledge are universally accessible, leading hospitals gain their reputation largely from accumulated experience and the breadth of their case data. In practice, physicians frequently revisit similar historical cases during consultations, subjecting them to repeated analysis and validation, thereby enhancing the effectiveness of clinical decision-making. This highlights the central role of experiential knowledge in medicine. In this study, we demonstrate that by employing a hybrid graph framework to integrate and contrast EMKGs, our approach consistently achieves superior performance across diverse tasks, particularly in the complex and wide-ranging context of emergency medicine. Beyond its technical contributions, this framework offers a new pathway for the inheritance of case-based medical knowledge and the systematic sharing of clinical experience.

Our experimental results reveal a nuanced pattern. In classical domains, such as DDI detection, existing knowledge graphs deliver the strongest performance. By contrast, in complex and dynamic scenarios—such as emergency triage and hospital readmission—the integration of experiential knowledge(e.g., DCDG) with formalized medical knowledge(e.g., EMKG) proves essential. This underscores the value of intelligent technologies that can automate the selection of knowledge sources and decision-support tools.

Because the relative importance of knowledge and experience varies across tasks, we introduce intelligent agents capable of adaptively selecting the most appropriate graph for each scenario. Recent studies demonstrate that agents can integrate large-scale knowledge bases, real-time data streams and environmental feedback to achieve goal-directed tasks without continuous human intervention39. Applications range from designing novel SARS-CoV-2 nanobodies40 to precision oncology prototypes that combine multimodal inputs—clinical notes, genomic profiles and imaging summaries—to recommend diagnostics and therapies in clinical workflows41. Despite these advances, most current AI agents remain confined to narrow applications, limiting their transferability to the broader and more heterogeneous challenges of general medicine. We therefore propose a new direction: leveraging AI agents to fuse and selectively deploy both established medical knowledge and dynamic clinical data, offering a promising strategy for accelerating knowledge renewal and enhancing clinical decision support.

Although LLMs have become a central tool in many studies owing to their powerful reasoning and predictive capabilities, KnDAgent differs fundamentally from end-to-end LLMs that rely solely on textual pattern learning. Specifically, our framework explicitly integrates a hybrid graph and employs graph based retrieval and reasoning tools. This design enables the model to effectively capture topological information—such as DDI networks, disease hierarchies, and the complementary structure between clinical data and medical knowledge—that is often only implicitly or incompletely represented in free text. The explicit graph structure provides a relational inductive bias, enhancing generalization under limited samples and improving reasoning over relational dependencies. Therefore, for structure sensitive tasks such as clinical decision support, a graph augmented agent design offers clear advantages over fully end-to-end LLM-based approaches; in other words, graph based reasoning serves as an effective complement to fully end-to-end LLM-based methods.

In KnDAgent, all the tasks, including recognition, prediction, and decision-making can be traced back to specific subgraphs and path based reasoning steps, providing a foundation for interpretability. Future work could further leverage the analytical capabilities of LLMs by incorporating traceable subgraph information and key contributing nodes (e.g., drugs, symptoms, or disease nodes that most strongly influence the downstream tasks), as well as by using rule-based constraints derived from the graph structure to guide LLM prompting. In this manner, clinician facing explanations can be generated, such as the graph paths traversed, a summary of key node contributions, and rule-based summaries grounded in the subgraph structure. This design holds promise for substantially enhancing the interpretability of the model, enabling clinicians to understand and audit the rationale behind model decisions. Moreover, KnDAgent employs a structured graph tool, where the finite set of nodes and relations imposes effective boundary constraints on model reasoning. Compared with fully end-to-end LLMs, this feature significantly reduces the risk of hallucination, enhances the safety of inference and prediction, and provides structured traceability information for regulatory purposes.

While our framework addresses the interplay between knowledge and experiential data through automated selection of agents and graph-based tools, considerable scope remains for advancing the underlying graphs, strategies and reasoning mechanisms. At the graph-construction stage, the scale of knowledge nodes and the complexity of their inferential relations continue to challenge current tools, limiting efficient large-scale integration. At the reasoning stage, the availability of high-performance computation and the background knowledge of LLMs could be harnessed to extend inference, thereby further validating and refining system performance. With respect to agent strategy, our current implementation—like most existing studies—relies on relatively simple LLM-driven policies. Future work should explore more sophisticated strategies tailored to task contexts and data characteristics, including hybrid designs that integrate data mining, knowledge reasoning and reinforcement learning. Such advances may prove crucial for realizing the full potential of intelligent agents in clinical knowledge integration and decision support. It should also be noted that the interpretability of our model remains an area for further enhancement, particularly given its reliance on the inherent explainability of the underlying tools themselves.

In this study, the dynamic clinical data were derived from a single hospital, and the healthcare system was confined to China, without incorporating multi-center data from different regions or countries (e.g., Europe or the United States). This single center, single region setting constitutes a major limitation of the present work. Differences in data collection standards, coding systems (e.g., variations in ICD versions), clinical pathways, and medical culture across healthcare systems may affect the generalizability of our framework to other institutions or systems. Validation on multi-center data, particularly across regions and healthcare systems, would likely reveal more meaningful insights and further test the robustness and transferability of the model. When data conditions permit, future work could leverage the automated data preprocessing capabilities of LLMs to mitigate the challenges posed by cross-institutional heterogeneity. Specifically, LLMs could be used to: (1) identify and map heterogeneous coding systems (e.g., for diagnoses, medications, and laboratory tests) across different hospitals; (2) extract standardized clinical events from unstructured or semi structured text; and (3) assist in constructing cross-institution alignment graphs. In this way, the barriers to multi-center data integration could be lowered, facilitating the application and validation of our graph based agent across a broader range of healthcare settings. Besides, a genuine knowledge distillation framework could be introduced into our model in future work. For example, a teacher model trained on dynamic clinical data could be used to guide a student model in learning the essential structures of established medical knowledge. Conversely, another teacher model trained on established medical knowledge could guide the student model in extracting clinically meaningful patterns from dynamic clinical data. Such a bidirectional distillation paradigm may further enhance the complementary fusion of the two knowledge sources.

Methods

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hospital, Sun Yat-sen University (approval no. SYSKY-2026-320-01). The requirement for informed consent was waived by the committee as the research involved only the analysis of anonymized data on symptoms and treatment regimens, without utilizing any additional identifiable patient information.

Overall framework

To address the challenge of insufficient collaboration between established medical knowledge and dynamic clinical data in the emergency department, we propose and evaluate an AI agent termed the KnDAgent system. The core objective of the KnDAgent system is to automatically select the most appropriate graph and clinical decision support tool according to the task, thereby providing flexible and efficient decision support for complex and rapidly changing ED scenarios.

The overall workflow of the KnDAgent system can be summarized as follows. As shown in Fig. 1b, first, both established medical knowledge bases and EMR data are standardized and preprocessed. A knowledge extraction tool based on LLMs is then used to perform semantic parsing and triple extraction, converting heterogeneous information into a unified graph representation. Next, a data mapping tool is designed for semantic alignment and node ID assignment. Based on this process, the system constructs three types of graphs through semantic reasoning: EMKG, constructed from authoritative guidelines and medical knowledge; DCDG, representing longitudinal patient trajectories and indicators; and Hybrid Graph (HG), formed by integrating the previous two graphs. This design ensures the stability of authoritative knowledge while incorporating the dynamic nature of clinical data, thereby providing consistent and scalable knowledge representation and reasoning support for diverse tasks in the ED.

Building on these graph foundations, the KnDAgent system employs the Task-Graph-Tool execution chain module. Specifically, the Graph Selector dynamically selects the most suitable graph (EMKG, DCDG, or HG) based on input task information, after which the ED-CDS Selector identifies the corresponding decision support tool, forming a complete Task-Graph-Tool chain. Finally, the execution module calls the selected tools to complete the task. Throughout this process, DeepSeek R1 functions as the core LLM reasoning engine, responsible for strategy generation, tool selection, and process scheduling, thereby achieving a closed-loop pipeline from task input to decision output.

Graph construction

The medical knowledge underpinning this study was curated from authoritative knowledge bases and structured into two principal domains: drugs and diseases.

For drug entities, we used the institutional pharmacy drug list as the primary source, where original entries frequently included trade names, dosage forms, specifications, or colloquial labels. To ensure consistency, we performed name normalization by mapping all drugs to their Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) standardized identifiers, while eliminating redundant descriptors such as brand names or dosage specifications. For instance, “amlodipine tablets” and “amlodipine” were consolidated into a single entity. Each drug entity was annotated with clinical attributes, including indications, contraindications, DDIs, dosage, and safety considerations. Knowledge extraction was performed using an LLM-driven graph construction framework in combination with rule-based templates. Through carefully designed prompt engineering, LLM outputs were strictly constrained to predefined authoritative literature and medical knowledge bases, thereby preventing hallucinations and ensuring reliability. Graph construction was fully automated and scale-controlled: for each central node, relation templates were systematically traversed to extract tail entities, which were then standardized and represented as entity-relation-entity triples. This process generated a drug knowledge graph Gdrug=(Edrug,Rdrug) with 125 entities and 35,167 triples.

For disease entities, a parallel strategy was applied. All disease terms were standardized using the ICD-10 classification system, with uniform mapping to third-level codes and removal of non-standard abbreviations or misspellings to harmonize cross-source inconsistencies. Synonym merging and semantic unification were performed using LLM-based extraction with manual validation, ensuring that semantically equivalent entities were consolidated under a single standard. For example, “hypertension” and “primary hypertension” were unified under the same ICD-10 code. Each disease entity, anchored to its ICD-10 identifier, was enriched with multidimensional relations, including typical symptoms, diagnostic methods, therapeutic regimens, complications, and susceptible populations. Using the same extraction pipeline as for drug entities, we constructed a disease knowledge graph Gdisease=(Edisease,Rdisease) with 284 entities and 14,505 triples, with all nodes and edges retaining their original source information to support traceability.

After constructing the disease and drug knowledge graphs, entity alignment and relation enrichment were performed based on core connecting points such as treatment, indications, and contraindications. Specifically, therapeutic agents in the disease graph were mapped bidirectionally to corresponding drug entities in the medication graph, and additional relationships from the drug graph—such as interactions and pharmacological mechanisms—were incorporated to supplement the disease graph’s treatment information. Conversely, relationships describing complications and pathophysiology in the disease graph were integrated into the drug graph’s indication/contraindication context, achieving bidirectional enhancement and multi-dimensional connectivity. This process yielded a EMKG encompassing diseases, medications, and associated clinical elements.

Formally, each disease node d∈D is connected via a set of relations r∈R to corresponding tail entities e∈E, forming structured triples (d, r, e). For example, the node “Acute Myocardial Infarction” (AMI) links to aspirin through the triple (AMI, treatment, aspirin), which in turn inherits cross-domain relations from the drug graph, such as (aspirin, drug–drug interaction, warfarin), enabling propagation and enrichment of clinical knowledge across domains.

Finally, we implemented knowledge reasoning within EMKG using an LLM-driven chain-of-relation inference framework. As shown in Fig. 2, in this approach, existing relations in the graph were grouped in pairs, enabling LLM to infer extended relational pathways that connect diseases, symptoms, drugs, and their interactions. For instance, given a patient diagnosed with acute myocardial infarction (AMI), EMKG can traverse from the knowledge chain (AMI, treatment, aspirin) to (aspirin, interaction, warfarin), thereby deriving the clinically relevant insight that aspirin-warfarin interactions should be carefully monitored during anticoagulant therapy for AMI. This form of reasoning is both authoritative and inherently interpretable, offering robust and durable support for clinical decision-making. Unlike static rule invocation, EMKG inference leverages multi-relational knowledge chains to deliver cross-domain semantic augmentation, thereby enriching disease diagnosis and medication recommendation with greater knowledge completeness and deeper inferential capacity.

DCDG was derived from real-world longitudinal EMRs of 195,161 patients seen in the emergency department during 2022-2023. The dataset includes both structured and unstructured information. Structured data comprise demographics, diagnosis codes, vital signs, laboratory tests (LOINC-coded), imaging summaries, and prescription records, whereas unstructured data include progress notes, imaging reports, discharge summaries, and surgical records. These dynamic records capture real-time patient status and disease progression, which are critical for time-sensitive clinical reasoning tasks.

Prior to graph construction, the dynamic data underwent systematic preprocessing and standardization. Each patient’s data were indexed using Patient ID and Visit ID to maintain longitudinal traceability and visit-level aggregation. Structured data were cleaned by removing invalid or duplicate entries, with imaging records deduplicated according to Patient ID + Visit ID + examination time and laboratory results aggregated by test item with abnormal values automatically flagged against reference ranges. Unstructured text was processed using LLM-assisted extraction with predefined summary templates, capturing key elements such as medical history, symptoms, diagnoses, treatments, test types, and results, converting free-text narratives into structured, standardized fields.

DCDG was indexed along both patient and encounter, with each clinical event precisely annotated by timestamp. Within a single encounter, structured data (demographics, diagnoses, vital signs, laboratory results, imaging summaries, physician orders) and unstructured data (progress notes, imaging reports, discharge summaries) were integrated into a unified context. Each patient record was formalized as a quadruple (p, v, e, t), where p denotes the patient ID, v denotes the visit ID, e represents the event node (e.g., symptom, laboratory test, medication), and t indicates the timestamp. Triple construction followed an event-driven extraction strategy, where LLMs identified clinical events, including diseases, symptoms, laboratory results, treatments, and procedures, from the contextual record and organized them into entity-relation-entity triples.

All extracted event nodes were standardized and mapped to authoritative medical coding systems (ICD-10, ATC, LOINC), ensuring semantic interoperability with EMKG. Each event retained its timestamp attribute, enabling temporal reconstruction and disease trajectory analysis. For example, a disease node may be associated with laboratory fluctuations or therapeutic adjustments across different time intervals, supporting longitudinal monitoring of disease progression and treatment response.

To enhance completeness and accuracy, a multi-source completion mechanism was employed: missing laboratory reference ranges were supplemented from laboratory standards, and absent medication dosages were inferred from physician orders or pharmacopeia records. Based on the current dataset, the resulting DCDG comprised 782,372 nodes, 28 relation types, and 5,847,941 edges, capturing the temporally resolved clinical information of patients during emergency encounters. This high-resolution representation provides a critical substrate for subsequent static-dynamic fusion and downstream tasks requiring individualized knowledge support.

As shown in Fig. 2, in addition to the graph reasoning approach exemplified by EMKG, DCDG leverages LLM-based inference to uncover latent relationships among clinical entities. For instance, when a relational chain simultaneously links “abnormal laboratory results” and “drug administration” within DCDG, the model can infer a potential hidden risk association between the two, which is then incorporated into the graph as a novel triplet. This process enables DCDG not only to preserve the authenticity of the original clinical records but also to dynamically expand the edge set of the graph through semantic reasoning, thereby enriching the clinical context available for downstream tasks. For example, when a patient admitted to the emergency department presents with “elevated serum creatinine” and subsequently receives “aspirin” within 24 h, DCDG can trace the temporal chain (patient, laboratoryresult, elevatedcreatinine) → (patient, medication, aspirin) and infer the dynamic risk that “aspirin may increase bleeding risk in the context of renal dysfunction.” Such reasoning allows the timely identification of individualized risk patterns not yet captured in existing knowledge bases, thereby complementing the limitations of established medical knowledge. Compared with the authority and stability of EMKG, the strength of DCDG lies in its real-time sensitivity, which links evolving disease trajectories with potential risk factors to support rapid prediction and risk alerting in emergency care.

To enable synergy between existing medical knowledge and dynamic clinical data, we integrated EMKG and DCDG by merging identical lexical entities and remapping their identifiers, followed by unified inference on the fused graph. Importantly, the original expressions were preserved as alias attributes, allowing decision tasks to retrieve entities using a broader set of keywords and thereby generate more comprehensive representations.

The resulting HG integrates the complementary strengths of static and dynamic knowledge: EMKG provides stable long-term disease-symptom-treatment-mechanism linkages, while DCDG introduces temporally resolved patient-specific events such as laboratory fluctuations, imaging changes, and medication adjustments. Cross-layer connections enable multi-hop reasoning, for example: Patient → High blood pressure (DCDG) → Hypertension (EMKG) → First-line therapy → Losartan. This fusion allows downstream tasks to leverage both established medical rules and real-time patient data for enhanced decision support. With LLM-driven semantic reasoning, the HG continuously evolves alongside accumulating clinical data, offering scalability and forward-looking adaptability.

Task-graph-tool execution chain

The Task-Graph-Tool execution chain is the core mechanism by which the agent achieves autonomous task scheduling and tool invocation. When task information is obtained from the current query, a memory search is first conducted. If the task has been executed before, the previously stored Task-Graph-Tool execution chain will be reused; otherwise, a new Task-Graph-Tool execution chain will be generated for this new task and saved for subsequent use.

The generation process proceeds as follows: the task retriever identifies emergency-related knowledge by using a retrieval-augmented generation approach over the ED knowledge base. Specifically, the language model first queries the knowledge base based on the input task name and returns the top-k most relevant knowledge items. This process is achieved by computing the semantic similarity between the task name and each knowledge block in the ED knowledge base, where the similarity score helps identify the information most likely to be useful for the given query. After acquiring task-related information, it is passed to the Graph Selector, which determines the most appropriate graph for the task. This step is implemented by inputting both the task-related information and the descriptions of all graphs in the Graph Learning Library into the LLM, which then performs reasoning and returns the most suitable graph (EMKG, DCDG, or HG). Similarly, once the graph has been selected, the task-related information, selected graph information, and tool information from the Decision Support Tool Library are provided as context to the LLM, which then reasons and selects the most suitable decision support tool for the task.

The core objective of the execution phase is to ensure that the Task-Graph-Tool execution chain can be successfully operated. During this phase, the LLM functions as a scheduling engine, sequentially invoking the following tools: the data mapping tool to complete semantic alignment and ID standardization; the graph tool to retrieve task-related subgraphs and generate embedding representations; and the intelligent processing tools to perform downstream tasks such as recognition, prediction, or decision-making.

Throughout the execution process, the LLM continuously monitors the results of tool invocations, recording intermediate outputs and final results, thereby guaranteeing both interpretability and stability of the execution chain. All tools are invoked via DeepSeek R1 (through API calls), ensuring unified policy generation and tool scheduling with high efficiency.

Case studies

The capabilities of the KnDAgent system were validated through three representative categories of downstream tasks: recognition (emergency triage and DDI detection), prediction (readmission prediction), and decision-making (prescription generation).

The emergency triage task integrates patients’ vital signs with graph-based entity embeddings to predict triage levels. In this task, the inputs to the KnDAgent system consist of multi-source clinical information, including vital signs (such as systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, heart rate, respiratory rate, temperature, and oxygen saturation), chief complaints (such as chest pain, dyspnea, dizziness), and demographic characteristics (age and sex). These raw inputs are first processed by the data mapping tool, which performs missing value imputation, numerical normalization, and entity recognition for textual inputs, while mapping symptoms to standardized medical terminology, thereby generating normalized vector representations.

Subsequently, the task retriever queries the ED knowledge base to locate task entries related to “emergency triage”. By computing semantic similarity, knowledge blocks highly relevant to triage are selected as task-extended descriptions, providing contextual support for subsequent graph selection. At this stage, the KnDAgent system invokes the Graph Selector, which integrates task requirements with extended knowledge and ultimately selects the HG. This choice is motivated by the fact that triage relies not only on dynamic clinical data such as current vital signs and symptoms, but also on authoritative standards for diagnostic and classification criteria. By aligning and complementing DCDG and EMKG, HG enables the KnDAgent system to bridge patient-derived EMR information with guideline-based standards, thereby producing a more comprehensive and reliable representation of triage risk.

After graph selection is completed, the Decision Support Tool Selector receives the task description, HG information, and tool library information. Through LLM-based reasoning, the most appropriate downstream tool is determined to be the LightGBM classifier. LightGBM is particularly suitable for handling combined structured features and graph embeddings, offering both efficiency and accuracy in clinical triage scenarios. This constitutes the Task-Graph-Tool execution chain for the emergency triage task.

During execution, the KnDAgent system strictly follows the chain: it first standardizes and encodes the patient inputs, then retrieves subgraphs from HG that are relevant to the patient’s symptoms and vital signs to generate embeddings. These numerical features and subgraph embeddings are concatenated into a unified feature vector, which is subsequently input into the LightGBM classifier to output the triage level.

The DDI detection task aims to classify the risk of DDIs based on graph-embedded representations of drug pairs. In this task, the inputs to the KnDAgent system are drug pairs extracted from patient prescriptions. The data mapping tool restores drug IDs to their actual names, performs semantic alignment, and assigns standardized IDs, thereby converting the input into normalized drug-pair representations.Subsequently, the task retriever identifies task descriptions related to “drug–drug interaction” in the ED knowledge base and provides them as extended information. The KnDAgent system then invokes the Graph Selector, which integrates task requirements with the extended knowledge and ultimately selects the HG. This choice is motivated by the fact that DDI detection depends not only on authoritative knowledge of drug interactions from EMKG but also on real-world dynamic medication records from DCDG, enabling the discovery of potential risk combinations that are not covered by established guidelines. In this context, HG provides complementary authority and sensitivity, making DDI detection both more comprehensive and responsive.

After graph selection, the Decision Support Tool Selector integrates the extended task information, HG-derived subgraph representations, and tool library, and identifies the multilayer perceptron (MLP) classifier as the optimal downstream tool. The MLP is well-suited for handling high-dimensional dense vectors and is effective for concatenation and interaction modeling of drug-pair embeddings. This forms the Task-Graph-Tool execution chain for the DDI detection task. During execution, the standardized drug pairs and their graph representations are concatenated into contextual embeddings and input into the MLP classifier for conflict prediction. The final output indicates whether a given drug pair carries a potential interaction risk. By combining guideline-based knowledge with real-world prescribing patterns, this execution chain enables the KnDAgent system to uncover interaction risks not covered in traditional databases, thereby substantially enhancing the safety of clinical medication.

The readmission prediction task aims to evaluate short-term readmission risk by combining patients’ prior medical history, chief complaints, and examination records, leveraging knowledge graph-enhanced semantic representations. In this task, the inputs to the KnDAgent system include major diagnostic results, clinical course notes, and key laboratory test indicators.

The data mapping tool processes diagnostic results, clinical course notes, and examination summaries from patients’ EMRs through tokenization, entity standardization, and synonym normalization, thereby generating a standardized feature set.The task retriever searches the ED knowledge base for task descriptions related to “readmission prediction” and provides them as extended information to the Graph Selector. Since readmission prediction requires both long-term authoritative knowledge and the longitudinal clinical trajectories of relevant patients, the KnDAgent system selects the HG. HG captures low-frequency but critical features from established knowledge while simultaneously reflecting real-time changes from dynamic data, thereby ensuring comprehensive coverage in risk prediction.After graph selection, the Decision Support Tool Selector integrates the extended task information with HG-derived subgraph representations and selects LightGBM as the downstream tool. LightGBM is advantageous for handling large-scale, high-dimensional, and sparse features, providing efficient and robust performance in clinical prediction scenarios.

During execution, patients’ diagnostic, clinical course, and laboratory data are mapped and concatenated with subgraph embeddings extracted from HG, which are then fed into the LightGBM model for prediction. The output is the probability of patient readmission within a specified time window (e.g., 7 or 30 days).

The medication recommendation task aims to generate personalized medication suggestions by integrating patients’ clinical symptoms, examination reports, and structured information from the knowledge graph. In this task, the inputs to the KnDAgent system include demographic features (age, sex), clinical symptoms, and laboratory test results.

The data mapping tool maps symptom entities to ICD-10 codes, normalizes laboratory results, and constructs demographic feature vectors, thereby producing a complete patient input representation. The task retriever locates task entries related to “medication recommendation” in the ED knowledge base and provides them as extended descriptions to the Graph Selector. Since this task relies heavily on authoritative guidelines to ensure the rationality and safety of recommendations, the agent selects the EMKG. EMKG provides systematic rules for medication indications, contraindications, and interactions, ensuring that the recommendations are consistent with clinical standards.After graph selection, the Decision Support Tool Selector integrates the extended task information with EMKG-derived subgraph representations and selects a LightGBM multi-class classifier as the recommendation tool. This model is capable of producing probability distributions over candidate medications under multi-class output scenarios, based on the distribution of input features. The result forms the Task-Graph-Tool chain for prescription recommendation.

During execution, the patient’s input information is standardized and encoded, concatenated with EMKG-derived subgraph embeddings, and then input into the multi-class classifier to output probability distributions of candidate medications. Finally, the KnDAgent system ensures that the recommendations are authoritative, rational, and individualized by following the execution chain.

Evaluation metrics

All downstream tasks were evaluated under a unified framework to ensure comparability of results across different task types. For recognition and prediction tasks, the primary evaluation metrics included accuracy (ACC), macro-averaged precision (Precision), macro-averaged recall (Recall), macro-averaged F1-score (F1-score) and AUC, providing a comprehensive assessment of the model’s classification ability and generalization performance across categories. For decision-making tasks, clinical feasibility and robustness of recommendations were emphasized. Evaluation was conducted using Top-3 ACC, Precision, Recall, F1-score and AUC, where a prediction was considered correct if the true class appeared within the top three recommended results.

By employing this multi-metric evaluation, a consistent quantitative standard was established across task types. This enabled systematic assessment of the agent’s overall performance—from risk recognition and outcome prediction to prescription recommendation—in the emergency department setting.

Model and training hyperparameters

To ensure that all graph resources provided coherent and semantically rich representations before being used by KnDAgent, we pretrained all knowledge graphs using a SACN-based graph representation learning framework42. During this stage, node embeddings were initialized with a dimensionality of 32 and refined through graph convolutional layers with intermediate embedding sizes of 48. The model was optimized using AdamW43 with a learning_rate of 0.001, weight_decay of 0.0005, and a batch_size of 128. An input_dropout_rate of 0.1 and a model-level dropout_rate of 0.1 were applied to enhance regularization.

For graph pretraining, we employed a binary cross-entropy loss (BCELoss) defined over node-node relationships. For each node, its supervision signal was constructed as a binary adjacency vector indicating whether an edge exists between that node and every other node in the graph (1 for connected, 0 for unconnected). To improve robustness to noise in large-scale biomedical graphs, we applied label smoothing with label_smoothing_epsilon = 0.2, replacing the hard adjacency labels with a convex combination of the original target and a uniform prior over all nodes. This smoothing prevents overconfident predictions on ambiguous or sparsely observed relations. The SACN encoder employed 200 convolutional channels with a kernel_size of 3 to capture multi-scale structural and relational patterns within the graph. Pretraining was conducted for 20 epochs with early stopping based on reconstruction performance, enabling the graph encoders to produce stable and clinically meaningful representations that integrate both high-level medical semantics and fine-grained relational dependencies.

For the downstream tasks, all models were trained using task-appropriate cross-entropy objectives. For multiclass problems such as emergency triage and medication recommendation, we employed a standard multiclass cross-entropy loss. For binary predictions, such as DDI classification and 7-day/30-day readmission, we used a weighted binary cross-entropy loss to correct for class imbalance. The weighting scheme for readmission followed the empirical positive-negative ratio to ensure balanced gradient contributions from minority classes.

In the downstream tasks, we adopted task-specific hyperparameters tailored to the characteristics of each clinical scenario. For the emergency triage task, we used a configuration with max_depth set to 5, num_leaves to 100 and n_estimators to 100, while increasing max_depth to 20 to accommodate higher-order feature interactions. For the DDI tasks, models were trained with a learning_rate of 0.001, a dropout_rate of 0.3 and hidden_layer_structure of [512, 256, 128], a structure that was also applied to the 7-day and 30-day readmission prediction tasks to maintain architectural consistency across patient-level prediction problems. In the medication recommendation task, we used a learning_rate of 0.001, num_leaves of 63, n_estimators of 200 and max_depth of 20, enabling the model to capture complex relationships among medications, diagnoses and laboratory findings. These task-specific configurations ensured that each clinical problem was optimized under the most appropriate inductive bias, while preserving methodological comparability within the overall framework.

Implementation details

Throughout training, we assessed model performance on the validation set and computed the validation AUC score after each epoch. The model checkpoint with the highest validation AUC score was saved and subsequently tested on the testing set. Our model implementation was carried out using PyTorch44.

It should be noted that the input content was strictly aligned across all baselines. For emergency triage, the input is the patient’s symptom description, with the task to assign a triage level from 1 to 4. For DDI detection, the input is a drug pair (drug names). For hospital readmission, the input is the patient’s EHR data, predicting 7-day or 30-day readmission. For medication recommendation, the input is the same EHR data, and the model recommends medications. For GPT and DeepSeek, we provided task descriptions combined with the same textual information (e.g., symptom descriptions or EHR summaries). However, it is important to note that the pure LLM baselines did not have access to the full dynamic clinical data in a structured graph form. Besides, We designed the prompts carefully and iteratively refined them with LLM assistance, but we cannot guarantee that we achieved optimal performance for the pure LLM baselines. Nevertheless, given that the tasks are straightforward and well-defined prediction problems, the impact of prompt variations is expected to be negligible.

Statistics and reproducibility

For all downstream tasks, we performed a unified statistical evaluation to ensure that the reported improvements were both stable and clinically meaningful. Confidence intervals were estimated using non-parametric bootstrap resampling, where 10,000 resamples were used for emergency triage, readmission prediction and medication recommendation, and 100 resamples for the BioSNAP dataset due to its smaller sample size. For each resample, we computed the metric differences between KnDAgent and every baseline model, enabling the construction of paired-difference distributions for all metrics. Statistical significance was assessed using a two-sided paired bootstrap test, with p-values derived from the proportion of resamples whose signs contradicted the observed mean difference, followed by Benjamini-Hochberg correction for multiple comparisons. Cohen’s d was additionally computed to quantify standardized effect sizes. To provide a clear visual representation of statistical uncertainty, we also plotted error bars based on the bootstrap-derived confidence intervals for KnDAgent and all baseline models across every metric. These procedures ensure that our evaluation reflects not only point estimates but also the stability and reliability of model performance under resampling variability. Note that confidence intervals used for paired model comparisons were computed on the distribution of paired differences, whereas the error bars shown for individual models in the figures were obtained by bootstrapping each model’s metric independently. This distinction ensures that significance testing and visualization are both performed under the appropriate statistical assumptions.
