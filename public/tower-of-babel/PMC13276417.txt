Construction and validation of a preimplantation kinship identification model using ultra-low-depth whole genome sequencing
Authors: Jian Lu, Jie Lin, Rong Hu, Feng Wu, Yi Li, Haisheng Huang, Shijie Wang, Miao Tian, Yiming Qi, Xin Zhao, Yangyi Chen, Jing Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryErrors during in vitro fertilization (IVF) workflows pose significant risks; yet, existing quality control measures remain insufficient. Herein, we developed a preimplantation full-sibling relationship identification (PFSRI) model using ultra-low-depth whole genome sequencing (uldWGS) data from 789 biopsied embryos (average depth, 0.02×). The model predicts embryonic relationships via the heterozygosity-based relationship ratio (HetRR). In double-blind testing, PFSRI successfully distinguished full-sibling embryos from different ovarian stimulation cycles within the same family and identified resequenced samples. At optimized thresholds (unique reads [URs] ≥ 800 K and co-detected SNPs [CoSNPs] ≥ 800), the model achieved 100% accuracy in discriminating full-sibling, unrelated, and identical relationships, with performance unaffected by chromosomal status. The PFSRI model provides a cost-effective and robust tool for precise embryo relationship identification, serving as a valuable quality control instrument for IVF laboratories.Subject areas: genomics, bioinformatics, machine learning

Introduction

As women age, their fertility progressively declines, accompanied by a significant increase in the rates of spontaneous abortion and aneuploid embryos.1 In this context, assisted reproductive technology (ART) has emerged as an effective treatment for infertility. To minimize the transfer of aneuploid embryos during ART cycles, preimplantation genetic testing for aneuploidy (PGT-A) has been employed for advanced maternal age, recurrent implantation failure, severe male factors, and couples with a normal karyotype experiencing recurrent miscarriages.2 However, ART involves a complex treatment process that encompasses multiple critical steps, including oocyte retrieval, fertilization, embryo culture, and embryo transfer. Each of these stages requires precise execution, thereby increasing the potential for human errors that could result in embryo loss or, more severely, the misidentification of embryos or gametes.3,4,5,6 Potential problems can include, but are not limited to: incorrect sperm being used for fertilization, implantation of non-target embryos into the recipient, errors in genetic testing results, and malfunctions during cryopreservation.6 According to a recent report by the Human Fertilisation and Embryology Authority (HFEA), the incidence of adverse events during approximately 72,000 in vitro fertilization (IVF) treatment cycles is less than 1%, including clinical errors, near misses, and management and laboratory mistakes.7 Furthermore, a retrospective analysis of an electronic database of nonconformities from a large IVF laboratory showed that in 36,654 IVF treatment cycles involving 181,899 individual laboratory procedures, the overall incidence rate of moderate (negative impact on one cycle but not resulting in a loss of cycle) and significant nonconformities (loss of the cycle or most gametes or embryos) was 0.045% per procedure and 0.23% per cycle.8

To date, a variety of risk management and error mitigation strategies have been developed to support IVF laboratories in enhancing the traceability of gametes and embryos, improving quality control processes, and preventing adverse events. The European Society of Human Reproduction and Embryology Special Interest Group (ESHRE SIG) strongly recommends implementing a double-check mechanism for critical operational steps, such as verification by a second individual or the use of electronic identification technologies like barcode scanning or radio-frequency identification (RFID).9 Furthermore, establishing a comprehensive quality management system allows for the timely identification of potential errors and the implementation of effective corrective measures through regular audits, data analysis, and process optimization, thereby enhancing overall operational standards and service quality.10 In the context of a comprehensive quality management framework, the application of artificial intelligence (AI) technology brings new possibilities to IVF laboratories. AI can track embryos at multiple critical time points, enhancing the efficiency and reliability of data management while providing robust technical support for laboratory quality control.11,12

Despite the active efforts made by embryology laboratories to enhance sample management and improve the traceability of treatment cycles, the effectiveness of current measures remains insufficient. Based on a retrospective analysis of PGT-A data from our center (Figure 1), this study developed a simple yet accurate preimplantation kinship determination model called PFSRI. The PFSRI model is capable of classifying embryo kinship into three distinct categories full siblings, unrelated, and identity. This functionality not only enables the early warnings for potential human errors during the IVF workflow but also facilitates experimental traceback and validation for embryos with abnormal relatedness.

Results

Study design using clinical PGT-A data

Using ultra-low-depth whole-genome sequencing (uldWGS) data (average depth, 0.021 ± 0.007) from 789 biopsied embryos obtained from Guangdong Women and Children Hospital, we developed the PFSRI model to precisely predict the relationships between preimplantation embryos, including full siblings, unrelated, and identical relationship (Figure 2). We obtained target-single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) from publicly available databases and extracted the heterozygosity-based relationship ratio (HetRR) feature based on the heterozygosity rates of co-detected SNPs (CoSNPs) between biopsied embryo samples. These HetRR features, reflecting the kinship between two samples under identification, were then utilized for the development and training of the PFSRI model to predict preimplantation kinship.

PFSRI model prediction results

Under a blind testing condition without prior family information, researchers classified samples based on family codes. If the family code was the same, the samples were expected to originate from the same family; if different, they were categorized as unrelated samples. The kinship predictions from the PFSRI model were subsequently compared against the presumptive classifications based on family codes.

The PFSRI model identified embryo S0822_E01 as being in a full-sibling relationship with all five embryos from family S0851 (Figure 3A). Similarly, embryo S0868_E01 was predicted to be a full sibling of all three embryos in family S0829 (Figure 3B). Consistent with these findings, embryo S1205_E01 was also classified as a full sibling of the three embryos from family S1163 (Figure 3C), and embryo S2083_E01 was determined to be a full sibling of both embryos in family S2063 (Figure 3D). However, these samples had been categorized as unrelated based on the initial presumptive classification framework. Upon further verification with clinical doctors, it was confirmed that the S0822_E01, S0868_E01, S1205_E01, and S2083_E01 were in fact derived from the second ovarian stimulation cycles of family S0851, S0829, S1163, and S2063, respectively (Tables S3, S4, S5, and S6).

Furthermore, the PFSRI model identified embryos E02 and E03 from family S2003 as identical relationships, which was confirmed by the finding that E03 represents a resequencing replicate of E02 (Figure 3E; Table S7). In family S0838, the model predicted E03–E05 and E07–E09 as two distinct sets of identical samples, consistent with subsequent validation showing that E04 and E05 were resequencing replicates of E03, and E08 and E09 were replicates of E07 (Figure 3F; Table S8). However, the prediction of identity between E06 and E09 from family S0838 was incorrect, as these originated from distinct embryos (Table S8). Notably, E06 (UR = 398 K) and E09 (UR = 516 K) exhibited a low number of co-detected SNPs (CoSNPs = 546), likely due to insufficient sequencing depth and genomic coverage, which may have compromised the model’s ability to differentiate these non-identical samples (Table S2).In the S0847 family, E03 and E04 were predicted as identical, a result confirmed upon review as E04 being a resequencing replicate of E03 (Figure 3G; Table S9). Collectively, these results demonstrate the robust discriminative performance of the PFSRI model in kinship classification, while highlighting its dependence on adequate sequencing data quality for optimal accuracy.

Performance of the PFSRI model

Based on the verification by clinical doctors, the presumptive relationships were corrected. As shown in Figure 4, the performance of the PFSRI model in the training cohort is as follows: full-sibling (AUC = 0.994, Figure 4A), identical (AUC = 1.000, Figure 4B), and unrelated (AUC = 0.996, Figure 4C); and in the testing cohort: full-sibling (AUC = 0.992, Figure 4A), identical (AUC = 1.000, Figure 4B), and unrelated (AUC = 0.996, Figure 4C). These results demonstrate that the model exhibits excellent discriminative ability across both cohorts, with AUC values approaching or reaching 1.0, validating the high accuracy and robustness of the HetRR-based PFSRI model for preimplantation kinship identification.

Model performance across threshold groups

To further enhance the reliability of kinship prediction, we stratified the training and testing cohorts into six incremental threshold groups (A–F) by setting different thresholds for unique reads (URs) and co-detected SNPs (CoSNPs). Figure 5 depicts performance metrics across all threshold groups in both the training and testing cohorts. In the training cohort, the predictive performance for full-sibling and unrelated relationships reached optimum when UR ≥ 800 K and CoSNP ≥ 800, achieving 100% in precision, recall, and F1 score. Meanwhile, identical relationships attained stable and completely accurate prediction at a lower threshold (UR ≥ 600 K and CoSNP ≥ 600). Consistent with the training cohort, the testing cohort also demonstrated perfect classification performance (100% across all three metrics) for full-sibling, unrelated, and identical relationships at the threshold of UR ≥ 800 K and CoSNP ≥ 800. Confusion matrix analysis (Figure 6) confirmed flawless classification of all three kinship types at UR ≥ 800 K and CoSNPs ≥ 800 in both the training and testing sets.

These findings reveal that under uldWGS, UR ≥ 800 K and CoSNP ≥ 800 support precise discrimination among full-sibling, unrelated, and identical relationships. Following rigorous cost-accuracy evaluation, UR ≥ 800 K and CoSNPs ≥ 800 emerged as the optimal parameter thresholds for PFSRI implementation, providing a reliable quantitative basis for preimplantation kinship testing.

The HetRR distributions for three kinship relationships

In the cohort dataset where UR ≥ 800 K and CoSNPs ≥800, the median HetRR values for identical, full-sibling, and unrelated relationships were 0.798 (interquartile range [IQR], 0.766–0.827), 1.272 (IQR, 1.241–1.305), and 1.683 (IQR, 1.656–1.709), respectively. The distributions of HetRR for identical, full-sibling, and unrelated relationships predicted by the PFSRI model exhibit significant differences (p < 0.0001), with no overlap between any categories (Figure 7C). This finding not only validates the effectiveness and robustness of the HetRR feature in distinguishing kinship relationships among embryos but also further demonstrates the high reliability of this method.

Aneuploidy do not affect the prediction results of the PFSRI model

To evaluate the impact of chromosomal aneuploidy on kinship prediction, we extracted PGT-A results of 577 embryo biopsy samples from the cohort dataset with UR ≥ 800 K and CoSNPs ≥ 800. Aneuploidy was detected in 315 samples (55%) (Figure 7A). Whole chromosome aneuploidies constituted the predominant abnormality type, with chromosome 22 monosomy and trisomy representing the most common numerical chromosomal abnormalities (Figure 7B).

Both samples used for kinship identification that were euploid are denoted as euploid-euploid sample pairs. Within the full-sibling cohort, the HetRR values did not differ significantly between euploid-euploid pairs and those with at least one aneuploid embryo (Figure 7D). Consistent findings were observed in unrelated and identical relationship cohort (Figure 7D). These results indicate that embryonic chromosomal copy number abnormalities do not interfere with the accuracy of kinship prediction based on HetRR features, validating the robustness of the PFSRI model under complex chromosomal backgrounds.

Discussion

Under normal circumstances, embryos from the same family should inherit half of their genetic material from the father and the other half from the mother, thereby exhibiting a full-sibling relationship. If embryos from the same family display non-full-sibling kinship, this indicates potential procedural errors during embryo in vitro culture, biopsy, or other handling steps. Leveraging uldWGS data from PGT-A, this study successfully established the PFSRI model, which accurately predicts preimplantation embryo kinship, including full siblings, unrelated and identical relationships. This groundbreaking achievement provides vital technical support for the field of ART and effectively mitigates the risks associated with errors prior to embryo implantation.

While human errors during the IVF process are rare, they can occur and may result in highly consequential outcomes. To reduce errors in the IVF process, various strategies have been proposed and implemented, including double-check systems, barcode identification, RFID, and AI-based embryo tracking and patient specimen recognition systems.9,12,13 These measures have enhanced the accuracy and reliability of operations to a certain extent, helping to mitigate the risks of human error and technical failures. However, they are still unable to completely eliminate all types of errors and may, in some cases, increase additional time costs, labor demands, and economic burdens.14 While electronic identification technologies are advanced, their application may introduce potential risks of harm to embryos or gametes.15 In summary, while existing interventions can reduce the risk of embryo or gamete mix-ups to a low level, they cannot completely eliminate it and may introduce additional costs or potential harm. The PFSRI model developed in this study achieves stable and effective discrimination among full-sibling, unrelated, and identical sample relationships with AUC values approaching or reaching 1.0, offering a reliable solution for specimen identification and kinship verification in IVF workflows.

In PGT, whole-genome amplification (WGA) is widely used to amplify single-cell or picogram-level DNA to nanogram or microgram levels to meet clinical testing requirements. However, the WGA process may introduce biases such as allele dropout and amplification preference, as well as sequence errors, leading to a decline in DNA quality.16,17,18 Consequently, the reliability of kinship analysis based on single-cell WGA faces significant challenges. A study demonstrated that genotyping technology based on 52 SNP and 15 short tandem repeat (STR) loci successfully achieved parent-child duo and parent-mother-father trio identification at the single-cell level after WGA, with 100% sensitivity and 100% specificity.19 Another study developed a real-time quantitative PCR (qPCR)-based trophectoderm DNA fingerprinting method, which evaluates the similarity of WGA cells from human blastocyst trophectoderm biopsies through analysis of 40 SNP alleles. Results showed that cell samples with self relationships exhibited an average similarity of 95.1 ± 5.9%, sibling samples averaged 57.2 ± 5.9% similarity, and the accuracy rate for assigning relationships was 100% for cell lines and embryos.20 Collectively, these findings validate the feasibility of kinship analysis using single-cell WGA. Nevertheless, such conventional detection strategies exhibit several critical limitations: they require additional experimental workflows that increase cost and turnaround time; they typically necessitate the availability of parental samples for accurate phasing; and rely on a limited panel of discrete loci (dozens of STR or SNP markers) that are highly susceptible to allelic dropout and stutter artifacts induced by amplification bias, thereby substantially undermining analytical robustness.

In contrast, the PFSRI model leverages hundreds to thousands of genome-wide heterozygous sites, where the aggregated statistical power across numerous loci compensates for ultra-low sequencing depth to ensure robust sensitivity and specificity in embryonic kinship classification. Unlike previous methods that require additional experiments for SNP or STR capture,19,20,21,22 the PFSRI model leverages existing uldWGS data generated during routine PGT-A, thereby incurring no additional experimental cost and integrating seamlessly into existing workflows. This represents a low-cost, highly accurate technology for detecting full-sibling relationships. Specifically, we have established that chromosome aneuploidy does not interfere with HetRR-based kinship prediction accuracy, enhancing confidence in the technique’s reliability. Additionally, there is no need to use parental information, as full-sibling relationships between embryos can be precisely distinguished without incorporating parental informative SNPs.

Furthermore, the PFSRI model can serve as an important tool for validation and traceback. For example, when inconsistencies arise between fetal sex or PGT results and those obtained prior to embryo transfer, the uldWGS data of the fetus (obtained from chorionic villi, amniotic fluid, or umbilical cord blood) can be compared with the pre-transfer uldWGS data: if the relationship is identical, it confirms that the fetus is the intended transferred embryo; if the relationship is full-sibling, it suggests the possibility of transferring another sample from the same family or spontaneous pregnancy of the mother; if unrelated is detected, it raises suspicion of transferring an embryo from a different family. It is reported that the incidence of twin pregnancies after single blastocyst transfer ranges from 0% to 13.2%, potentially caused by embryo splitting or concurrent natural conception.23 Studies suggest that 10%–35% of twins born following single-embryo transfer during IVF are the result of spontaneous conception.24 In such cases, undetected embryo implantation may occur, leading to the birth of affected children. The PFSRI model allows for comparison between the uldWGS data of twins and transfer embryos: If both twins show identity with the pre-implantation embryo, they can be determined to be monozygotic twins; if one is a full-sibling, it indicates spontaneous conception, and prenatal diagnosis should be performed to exclude risks of genetic diseases.

To summarize, this research has introduced a new and highly cost-efficient PFSRI model, which leverages uldWGS data to accurately assess full-sibling, unrelated, and identical relationships among embryos. The PFSRI model not only serves as a robust tool to mitigate pre-implantation procedural errors but also enables experimental traceback analysis and validation of potential post-transplantation discrepancies. Notably, this approach is based on uldWGS data, offering significant advantages in technical scalability, cost-effectiveness, and ease of clinical implementation.

Limitations of the study

This study successfully established and validated a robust PFSRI model based on uldWGS, achieving high accuracy in distinguishing full-sibling, unrelated, and identical embryonic relationships under complex chromosomal backgrounds. Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged for further optimization and clinical application. First, all embryonic samples were derived from a single ART center, and the model was developed and validated using a homogeneous cohort with consistent WGA and sequencing protocols. While this approach minimized technical variability during proof-of-concept development, the generalizability of the PFSRI model to samples processed with alternative WGA methods or different sequencing platforms remains to be established. Consequently, future multi-center studies employing diverse WGA methods will be valuable to further validate the generalizability of the model framework. Second, the optimal thresholds (UR ≥ 800 K; CoSNP ≥ 800) have not yet undergone multicenter validation. Although the mathematical basis of the PFSRI model is theoretically generalizable and inter-platform divergence is expected to be limited, definitive validation of generalizability will require future large-scale multicenter studies incorporating diverse PGT-A protocols. Third, a limitation of this study is the population specificity of the selected SNP markers. Nevertheless, given that the selection of target SNP markers primarily affects the expected value of HetRR, we anticipate that its impact across different populations is limited, although this remains to be validated.

Despite these limitations, the PFSRI model demonstrates significant advantages in preimplantation kinship classification. It serves not only as a reliable, cost-effective early warning system for embryo mix-ups but also as a critical tool for verification and retrospective analysis. These capabilities substantially enhance its clinical utility. In summary, our work provides a transformative solution for ensuring embryo identity and enhancing safety in assisted reproductive technologies.

Study participants

This study analyzed data comprised 789 embryos from 218 families who underwent in vitro fertilization (IVF) cycles and preimplantation genetic testing for aneuploidy (PGT-A) at the Guangdong Women and Children Hospital between January 2020 and November 2021. All participants or their legal guardians provided written informed consent.

Method details

Data collection

This study retrospectively analyzed raw data of PGT-A from 789 embryos derived from 218 families. Blastocyst trophectoderm (TE) biopsy was performed on day 5 for 94.95% of these embryos, most of which exhibited fair morphological quality, with a mean sequencing depth of 0.021 ± 0.007 across all samples (Table S1). ALL TE biopsy samples were subjected to whole genome amplification (WGA) using the WGA method (Jabrehoo, Beijing, China), and sequencing was performed on the Illumina MiSeqDx platform.

Construction of the cohort dataset

Under blinded conditions, kinship classification for each sample pair was initially determined based on their family codes and sample IDs. Samples with consistent family codes were presumptively assigned to the same family and classified as full-sibling relationships; those with discordant family codes were considered unrelated, reflecting embryos derived from different families; and pairs matching in both family and sample identifiers were designated as identical individuals. The kinship predictions generated by the PFSRI model were subsequently compared against both the presumptive classifications and the clinically validated results.

A total of 789 clinical biopsied embryo samples were included in this study. Pairwise kinship analyses were performed for all sample combinations, yielding 311,655 pairwise kinship determination datasets. These datasets were organized into a cohort dataset and underwent category balancing. Of these, 70% of the pairwise kinship data were randomly selected as the training cohort, while the remaining 30% were used as the testing cohort. The training cohort consisted of 218,158 pairwise kinship identifications, including 1,152 pairs with full-sibling relationships, 216,454 pairs with unrelated relationships, and 552 pairs with identical relationships. The testing cohort contained 494 full-sibling, 92,766 unrelated, and 237 identical pairs. The entire process of the cohort dataset is shown in Figure 1.

Alignment and calling SNPs

The uldWGS data of the research samples were initially processed using the trimmomatic to remove adapter sequences, reads with high proportions of N bases, and low-quality reads.28 Subsequently, the preprocessed data were aligned to the human reference genome hg19 using bwa-mem,25 and the alignment results were sorted by chromosome position while removing PCR duplicates using samtools,26 resulting in reads uniquely mapped to the reference genome. To identify high-quality single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), we employed the samtools mpileup command with stringent filtering parameters. Specifically, we excluded sites with base quality scores below 20 and mapping quality scores below 20, ensuring the retention of only high-confidence variants for subsequent analysis.

A set of SNPs was selected from the dbSNP database (build 151; https://hgdownload.soe.ucsc.edu/goldenPath/hg19/database/snp151Common.txt.gz). To increase the number of eligible target SNPs, the minor allele frequency threshold was relaxed from the commonly used greater than 0.30 to greater than 0.25.29 In addition, the target SNPs were distributed across the 22 autosomal chromosomes. Reads containing these target SNPs were then randomly selected from the results of calling SNPs (each extracted read must cover these SNP loci at least once), and it was ensured that the genomic spacing between these SNP loci was at least 50 base pairs to construct a reads dataset for predicting kinship.

Evaluation of the PFSRI model

The PFSRI model was constructed using the HetRR feature value and the GNB algorithm. To evaluate the performance of the PFSRI model, 5-fold cross-validation was employed as a resampling method on the training cohort. In each iteration, 4-folds served as the training subset, while the remaining fold was used for parameter tuning. The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) was calculated for both the training and test cohorts. To determine the optimal quality control cutoff, we evaluated the training and testing cohorts across a range of threshold values. Both training cohort (n = 218,158) and testing cohort (n = 93,497) was divided into six threshold-increasing groups based on unique reads (UR) and co-detected SNPs (CoSNPs): (A) UR ≥ 200 K, CoSNPs ≥ 200; (B) UR ≥ 400 K, CoSNPs ≥ 400; (C) UR ≥ 600 K, CoSNPs ≥ 600; (D) UR ≥ 800 K, CoSNPs ≥800; (E) UR ≥ 1000 K, CoSNPs ≥ 1000; (F) UR ≥ 1200 K, CoSNPs ≥ 1200. For each of the six threshold groups (A-F), the predictive performance of the model was evaluated by calculating precision, recall, and F1 score for three kinship categories: full-sibling, unrelated, and identical relationship. Precision is defined as the proportion of true positive predictions among all positive predictions. Recall is defined as the proportion of true positive predictions among all actual positives. F1 is the harmonic mean of precision and recall. Among all threshold groups (A–F) evaluated in both the training and testing cohorts, the final model was selected at the lowest data threshold that achieved stable and optimal performance. The distribution of HetRR values among the three kinship categories (full-sibling, unrelated, and identical relationship) was examined to further validate the final model’s classification accuracy and discriminatory power.

Impact of aneuploidy on PFSRI model performance

To assess the potential impact of PGT-A results on embryonic kinship prediction, we analyzed the ploidy status (euploid vs. aneuploid) of embryos included in the final PFSRI model dataset. Here, ‘aneuploid’ encompassed both whole and segmental chromosome abnormalities, including not only whole and segmental chromosomal gain or loss but also whole and segmental mosaic gain or loss.31 Among the three kinship groups (full-sibling, unrelated, identical), the HetRR values of euploid-euploid, euploid-aneuploid, and aneuploid-aneuploid sample pairs were statistically analyzed and compared within each group, respectively.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R, Python, and GraphPad Prism. Comparisons among multiple groups were conducted using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05. The degrees of significance are denoted in the figures and figure legends as follows: ∗∗∗∗ indicates p < 0.0001 and “ns” denotes non-significant differences. In Figures 7C and 7Ds, HetRR values are presented as median with 25th and 75th percentiles. Model performance was evaluated using precision, recall, F1 score, accuracy, and AUC.
