Effect of Titrated Dexmedetomidine versus Esmolol on Postoperative Myocardial Injury in High-Risk Patients Undergoing Laparoscopic Colorectal Cancer Surgery: A Randomized Controlled Trial
Authors: Yuanqiang Li, Wenting Jiang, Jingchen Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeLaparoscopic pneumoperitoneum induces tachycardia and hemodynamic instability, key drivers of myocardial injury after noncardiac surgery (MINS). This randomized controlled trial evaluated whether a proactive hemodynamic management protocol based on titrated dexmedetomidine or esmolol could reduce the incidence of MINS in high-risk patients undergoing laparoscopic colorectal cancer surgery compared with conventional management.MethodsA total of 270 high-risk patients undergoing elective laparoscopic colorectal cancer surgery were randomly assigned to conventional management (Group C), dexmedetomidine infusion (Group D), or esmolol infusion (Group E). Infusions in the two active groups were titrated to maintain an intraoperative heart rate between 50 and 70 beats/min. The primary outcome was the incidence of MINS within 48 hours postoperatively.ResultsIn the primary ITT analysis, the incidence of MINS was lower in Group D (7.8%, 7/90) than in Group C (20.0%, 18/90), although this difference did not reach the Bonferroni-adjusted significance threshold (P = 0.018; adjusted α = 0.0167). The reduction in Group E compared with Group C (11.1% vs 20.0%) was not statistically significant (P = 0.121). In the prespecified PP sensitivity analysis, Group D had a significantly lower MINS incidence than Group C (6.9% vs 20.7%, P = 0.008). Patients receiving dexmedetomidine exhibited more stable intraoperative hemodynamics and lower postoperative lactate levels, without an increased incidence of hypotension.ConclusionIn high-risk patients undergoing laparoscopic colorectal cancer surgery, titrated dexmedetomidine showed a potential trend toward reduced MINS incidence compared with conventional management, with a more pronounced signal in the PP analysis. This cardioprotective signal may be associated with improved intraoperative hemodynamic stability, although the evidence is exploratory.

Introduction

Myocardial injury after noncardiac surgery (MINS) is a common and serious complication, strongly associated with increased short- and long-term mortality.1,2 Its predominant pathophysiology is myocardial oxygen supply-demand mismatch (type 2 myocardial infarction), usually related to perioperative hemodynamic stress, particularly tachycardia and blood pressure fluctuation.3,4 Laparoscopic surgery, despite its minimally invasive nature, induces significant hemodynamic alterations. Pneumoperitoneum increases intra-abdominal pressure, activating the sympathetic nervous system and leading to tachycardia, hypertension, and increased systemic vascular resistance.5 This state elevates myocardial oxygen demand while potentially compromising coronary perfusion, creating a substrate for ischemia, particularly in patients with limited coronary reserve.3,6,7 The reported incidence of MINS in high-risk patients undergoing major abdominal surgery can exceed 20%.8

Proactive management of these hemodynamic disturbances is therefore a rational preventive strategy.3,4 Heart rate control is a cornerstone, as tachycardia is a major determinant of myocardial oxygen consumption and has been independently associated with MINS in a large prospective cohort study.9 However, focusing on heart rate alone may be insufficient. Hemodynamic instability and pronounced blood pressure fluctuations can further reduce coronary perfusion pressure and increase ventricular wall stress, thereby contributing to the supply-demand imbalance.3,9 An ideal intervention would concurrently address both heart rate control and hemodynamic stability.

Dexmedetomidine, a highly selective central alpha-2 adrenergic agonist, offers a unique pharmacological profile for this purpose. It reduces sympathetic outflow at the central level, producing a titratable decrease in heart rate and a stabilizing effect on blood pressure, while also possessing analgesic and anti-inflammatory properties that may further attenuate the perioperative stress response.10 Recent experimental evidence further indicates that dexmedetomidine reduces heart rate in a dose-dependent manner through both central vagal activation and direct suppression of sinoatrial node pacemaker cell automaticity, supporting the feasibility of titratable heart rate control.11 Clinical studies have demonstrated that perioperative dexmedetomidine can reduce myocardial enzyme release, improve myocardial ischemia, and reduce the risk of postoperative cardiac complications in high-risk patients.12–14 Esmolol, in contrast, is an ultra-short-acting, peripherally acting beta-1 selective blocker that provides rapid and primarily cardioselective heart rate control.15 A meta-analysis indicates that intravenous esmolol is effective in preventing perioperative myocardial ischemia during non-cardiac surgery, although its administration requires careful titration to avoid hypotension.16

While both agents can ameliorate pneumoperitoneum-induced tachycardia and attenuate adverse hemodynamic responses during laparoscopic surgery,17,18 whether their differential mechanisms, namely central sympatholysis versus peripheral beta-1 blockade, translate into a distinct capacity to promote the comprehensive hemodynamic stability believed to be critical for MINS prevention remains unproven.

We therefore conducted this randomized controlled trial to evaluate whether a protocol based on titrated dexmedetomidine or esmolol, compared with conventional management, could reduce the incidence of MINS in high-risk patients undergoing laparoscopic colorectal cancer surgery.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

This single-center, prospective, randomized controlled trial was conducted with approval from the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Guangxi Medical University (approval No. 2021-KY-E-293) and registered at the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2100054099; registered 08 December 2021), in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the CONSORT guidelines. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before randomization. Enrollment of eligible patients occurred between December 2021 and November 2022.

Inclusion criteria were high-risk patients undergoing elective laparoscopic colorectal cancer surgery, defined as age ≥65 years, or age ≥45 years with a history of coronary artery disease, peripheral arterial disease, or stroke.1,19 We excluded patients meeting any of the following criteria: (1) preoperative comorbidities of pulmonary embolism, atrial fibrillation, cardioversion, or acute/chronic heart failure; (2) severe preoperative hypertension (systolic blood pressure (SBP) ≥180 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure (DBP)≥110 mmHg) or severe hypotension (SBP <90 mmHg); (3) long-term beta-blocker use with preoperative HR <60 beats/min; (4) cardiac conduction abnormalities, including sinus bradycardia, sinoatrial node dysfunction, atrioventricular block greater than first-degree, or sick sinus syndrome; (5) bronchial asthma or severe chronic obstructive pulmonary disease; (6) concurrent immune disorders, active infection, sepsis, severe renal impairment, or hepatic insufficiency (Child-Pugh class C or higher); (7) preoperative neoadjuvant chemotherapy or immunotherapy; (8) elevated preoperative high-sensitivity cardiac troponin T (hs-cTnT) levels (≥65 ng/L); (9) planned radical Miles procedure for rectal cancer.

Randomization and Blinding

Patients were randomly allocated in a 1:1:1 ratio to the dexmedetomidine group (Group D), the esmolol group (Group E), or the conventional management group (Group C) using a computer-generated sequence. Assignments were concealed in sealed, opaque, sequentially numbered envelopes. Owing to the distinct interventional protocols, the attending anesthesiologists and post-anesthesia care unit (PACU) nurses were not blinded to group assignment. However, patients and outcome assessors (responsible for MINS adjudication and postoperative data collection) remained blinded throughout the study period. Additionally, the statistician conducted all analyses blinded to group allocation until completion of the final analysis.

Anesthetic Management and Intervention

Standard monitoring was instituted upon arrival in the operating room, including pulse oximetry (SpO2), electrocardiography, non-invasive and invasive arterial blood pressure, and bispectral index (BIS). All patients underwent total intravenous anesthesia. Induction was performed with sufentanil (0.4–0.6 μg/kg), etomidate (0.2–0.3 mg/kg), and cisatracurium (0.2–0.3 mg/kg). Ultrasound-guided bilateral transversus abdominis plane and rectus sheath blocks were executed with 60 mL of 0.25% ropivacaine. Anesthesia was maintained via target-controlled infusion of propofol (1–4 μg/mL) and remifentanil (1–5 ng/mL), with cisatracurium (0.1–0.2 mg/kg/h) for muscle relaxation. BIS values were maintained between 45 and 55, and core temperature was kept ≥36°C using forced-air warming. Intraoperative fluid management followed our institutional enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) protocol using goal-directed fluid therapy. Crystalloid infusion was guided by pulse pressure variation (PPV), with a target PPV maintained below 13%. Pneumoperitoneum pressure was uniformly maintained at 12–14 mmHg. Postoperative analgesia was provided via a patient-controlled intravenous analgesia pump containing sufentanil (2 μg/kg) and tropisetron (10 mg) in 100 mL of normal saline, with a 2 mL/h background infusion, a 0.5 mL bolus dose, and a 15-minute lockout interval.

Following anesthesia induction, patients in the dexmedetomidine group received a continuous infusion starting at 0.4 μg/kg/h, while the esmolol group began at 10 μg/kg/min. Infusion rates in both groups were titrated to maintain an intraoperative HR between 50 and 70 beats/min until surgery concluded. Infusion rates were reassessed and adjusted every 5 to 10 minutes throughout the surgical procedure, or immediately upon any sustained deviation of HR outside the 50–70 beats/min target range. This target range was designed to maintain HR well below the threshold (>100 beats/min) associated with increased risk of myocardial injury after noncardiac surgery (MINS),9 while also below the 70 beats/min threshold linked to elevated risk of postoperative myocardial injury and death,20 yet above 50 beats/min to avoid clinically significant bradycardia. The conventional management group received routine hemodynamic care targeting an HR of 50–80 beats/min, with rescue esmolol used only for tachycardia.

Intraoperative Conventional Hemodynamic Management

For HR deviations, initial assessment included Electrocardiogram (ECG) and arterial blood gas analysis to exclude arrhythmias, electrolyte imbalances, or hypercapnia. Bradycardia (HR <50 beats/min) was treated with intravenous atropine (5–10 μg/kg), and tachycardia (HR >100 beats/min) with an esmolol bolus (0.2–1.0 mg/kg). This approach in the control group contrasted with the continuous, target-driven infusion strategy used in the esmolol group. Intraoperative blood pressure was maintained within 80–110% of the preoperative baseline value and an SBP of 100–160 mmHg using vasoactive agents as needed.9,21

Outcome Measurements

The primary outcome was the incidence of MINS within 48 hours postoperatively. Serum hs-cTnT levels were assessed preoperatively (T0) and on postoperative days 1 and 2 (POD1, POD2) using the Roche Cobas e601 electrochemiluminescence immunoassay, with a 99th percentile upper reference limit of 14 ng/L. MINS was defined based on established prognostic thresholds: an elevated postoperative hs-cTnT level (20–65 ng/L) with an absolute increase ≥5 ng/L from baseline, or a postoperative level exceeding 65 ng/L.3 Diagnoses of MINS excluded other non-ischemic causes of troponin elevation, such as sepsis, pulmonary embolism, atrial fibrillation, or renal failure.

Secondary outcomes encompassed intraoperative hemodynamic variables (HR and BP) measured at predefined timepoints: pre-induction (T1), post-intubation (T2), pre-pneumoperitoneum (T3), 1, 5, 15, 30, 45, 60 minutes after pneumoperitoneum (T4-T9), 5 minutes post-desufflation (T10), and end of surgery (T11); rate-pressure product (RPP); serial lactate and glucose levels (T1, T9, T11). Additional evaluated parameters included consumption of anesthetics, vasoactive drugs, post-anesthesia recovery profiles; hematological biomarkers [platelet count, D-dimer, fibrinogen, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP)] pre- and post-operation; postoperative pain scores (VAS) within 48 hours; HR and BP at 2, 4, 8, 12, 16, 20, 24, and 48 hours post-surgery; adverse cardiovascular events during surgery and postoperative hospitalization; and major adverse cardiovascular events (myocardial infarction, cardiac arrest, heart failure, cerebral infarction), coronary revascularization procedures, and all-cause mortality assessed at 30 days postoperatively via telephone follow-up and electronic medical record review. All secondary outcomes were exploratory in nature.

Sample Size Calculation

Sample size was calculated using PASS 15.0 (NCSS LLC). Based on an anticipated MINS incidence of 25% in the control group (derived from previous hs-cTnT studies)1,3,8 and pilot data showing incidences of 8% and 13% in the dexmedetomidine and esmolol groups, respectively, a total of 270 patients (90 per group) were required to achieve 80% power at a two-sided α of 0.05 to detect an overall difference in MINS incidence among the three groups, allowing for a 10% dropout rate.

Statistical Analysis

All analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The primary analysis was based on the intention-to-treat (ITT) population, which included all 270 randomized patients analyzed in their originally assigned groups. A prespecified sensitivity analysis was performed using the per-protocol (PP) population, defined as patients who completed the scheduled laparoscopic surgery without any pre-specified protocol deviation (conversion to laparotomy, massive subcutaneous emphysema, or postoperative re-laparotomy) and who provided all required postoperative troponin data. The PP population comprised 257 patients (D group, n=87; E group, n=83; C group, n=87).

The primary outcome (MINS incidence) was compared among groups using the chi-square test. Odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals were calculated from 2×2 contingency tables for pairwise comparisons. Two pre-specified primary comparisons were defined: dexmedetomidine vs control and esmolol vs control. A Bonferroni correction was applied to control the family-wise type I error rate, with an adjusted significance threshold of P <0.0167 (0.05/3), accounting for the two primary comparisons and the exploratory dexmedetomidine vs esmolol comparison.

Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances was assessed using Levene’s test. When both assumptions were met, one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s HSD post-hoc test was used. When the normality assumption was violated, the Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn’s post-hoc test was applied. Normally distributed data are presented as mean ± SD; non-normally distributed data are expressed as median (IQR). Categorical variables are reported as numbers (%) and compared using the chi-square or Fisher’s exact test.

For repeated measures data (intraoperative hemodynamics, lactate, glucose, postoperative hemodynamics, and pain scores), linear mixed-effects models (LMMs) were used to account for within-patient correlation. Models included fixed effects for group, time, and group × time interaction, with a random intercept for each patient. An autoregressive (AR1) covariance structure was specified for the repeated measurements. Model results are reported as Type III tests of fixed effects with F statistics and P values. When the group × time interaction was significant, post-hoc pairwise comparisons were performed at individual time points.

Given the exploratory nature of secondary analyses, a two-sided P <0.05 was considered significant for all secondary outcomes, without additional multiplicity adjustment.

Handling of Missing Data

For the primary outcome, troponin data were complete for all 270 randomized patients, as postoperative hs-cTnT monitoring was part of institutional standard care for high-risk patients undergoing major abdominal surgery. For secondary outcomes, 11 patients who underwent conversion to laparotomy had non-random missing data for intraoperative (post-conversion) and postoperative secondary endpoints. Two additional patients (massive subcutaneous emphysema, n=1; postoperative re-laparotomy, n=1) were excluded from secondary outcome analyses in accordance with pre-specified protocol deviation criteria. Given that these data were missing not at random and were directly attributable to protocol deviations, no imputation was performed. Secondary outcome analyses were based on the PP population (n=257).

Results

Patient Characteristics and Study Flow

A total of 294 high-risk patients scheduled for laparoscopic colorectal cancer surgery were assessed for eligibility. Of these, 270 were successfully randomized into three groups of 90 patients each. After excluding patients with pre-specified protocol deviations (conversion to laparotomy, n=11; massive subcutaneous emphysema, n=1; postoperative re-laparotomy, n=1), 257 patients were included in the PP analysis (D group, n=87; E group, n=83; C group, n=87), as detailed in the participant flow diagram (Figure 1). All included patients remained hospitalized until at least postoperative day 2 (POD2), ensuring the successful collection of all scheduled blood samples for hs-cTnT measurement. Primary and Secondary Outcomes

Primary Outcome

In the primary ITT analysis (n=270), the overall incidence of MINS was 13.0% (35/270). The incidence was lower in Group D compared with Group C (7.8% vs. 20.0%; odds ratio [OR], 0.337; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.133–0.854; P = 0.018), although this pairwise comparison did not meet the prespecified Bonferroni-corrected significance threshold (P < 0.0167). The absolute risk difference was −12.2 percentage points (95% CI, −22.2 to −2.3). No significant differences were observed among the three groups in postoperative hematological biomarkers, including platelet count, D-dimer, fibrinogen, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP), at any postoperative time point (Supplementary Table S3). Discussion

This randomized controlled trial evaluated the comparative efficacy of two proactive hemodynamic management strategies for MINS prevention in high-risk patients undergoing laparoscopic colorectal cancer surgery. In the primary ITT analysis, dexmedetomidine was associated with a lower incidence of MINS compared with conventional management, although the difference did not meet the Bonferroni-corrected significance threshold. In the PP sensitivity analysis, the reduction in MINS with dexmedetomidine was statistically significant. Esmolol did not show a significant reduction in either analysis. These findings suggest that dexmedetomidine may be associated with a numerically lower incidence of MINS compared with conventional management; however, this signal did not reach the prespecified significance threshold in the primary ITT analysis. The PP sensitivity analysis provides additional exploratory support but should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of a treatment effect.

The differential results between the dexmedetomidine and esmolol groups, despite comparable heart rate control, warrant careful consideration. Two factors likely contribute to this disparity. First, the observed effect size for esmolol was smaller than that for dexmedetomidine, and our sample size was powered to detect an overall difference among the three groups rather than each pairwise contrast. The resulting insufficient statistical power may have precluded the detection of a smaller but potentially clinically meaningful benefit of esmolol. Second, and more importantly, the two agents differ fundamentally in their pharmacological mechanisms. Dexmedetomidine acts centrally as an alpha-2 adrenergic agonist, reducing sympathetic tone and norepinephrine release.22 This central sympatholytic effect can more comprehensively attenuate the neuroendocrine stress response, thereby achieving a controllable reduction in heart rate while simultaneously stabilizing blood pressure.23 Esmolol, as a cardioselective beta-1 adrenergic receptor antagonist, primarily exerts its effect peripherally.15 It effectively lowers heart rate but does not directly attenuate the central sympathetic outflow triggered by surgical stress.23,24 Consequently, while heart rate is controlled, other manifestations of the stress response, such as vasoconstriction and blood pressure lability, may persist. The absolute risk differences further illustrate this distinction: the 95% confidence interval for D vs C (−22.2 to −2.3 percentage points) excluded zero, suggesting a more consistent signal, whereas the interval for E vs C (−19.4 to 1.6 percentage points) included zero, reflecting greater uncertainty about the magnitude and direction of effect. Although these estimates are exploratory in the context of the primary ITT result, they provide complementary information about the potential clinical relevance of the observed trends. A plausible explanation for the observed signal is that dexmedetomidine may promote integrated hemodynamic stability, which extends beyond isolated parameter control to describe the coordinated, low-variability stabilization of both heart rate and blood pressure. By simultaneously attenuating tachycardia and mitigating hemodynamic oscillations, it addresses the principal threats to myocardial oxygen supply-demand balance in a synergistic manner during acute surgical stress. Our data support this distinction: the dexmedetomidine group not only achieved the target heart rate but also exhibited a more stable blood pressure trajectory during pneumoperitoneum, without an increase in clinically significant hypotensive events.

The exploratory comparison between dexmedetomidine and esmolol showed no significant difference in MINS incidence. Given that the sample size was not powered for this specific comparison, this result should be interpreted with caution and should not be interpreted as evidence of equivalence between the two active regimens.

The metabolic effects observed provide exploratory evidence consistent with this interpretation. Intraoperative hyperlactatemia is a marker of inadequate tissue perfusion from oxygen supply-demand imbalance, and has been associated with a higher incidence of MINS.25,26 A recent study also showed that hyperglycemia induced by intraoperative stress is associated with an increased risk of MINS.27 Although the overall group × time interaction for lactate did not reach statistical significance, lactate levels at the end of surgery were significantly lower in the dexmedetomidine group compared with the control group, even within the normal range, suggesting a possible improvement in global tissue perfusion and oxygen supply-demand balance, consistent with its hemodynamic stabilizing action. Similarly, the attenuated glycemic response and reduced requirements for remifentanil and propofol in this group may reflect a blunted surgical stress response and are consistent with the multimodal, anesthetic-sparing properties of dexmedetomidine.28–30

A key strength of our design is the use of a titrated, goal-directed approach to maintain a heart rate of 50 to 70 beats/min, rather than administering fixed drug doses. This range was selected based on evidence linking the avoidance of perioperative tachycardia with improved cardiovascular outcomes.3,9,20,31,32 Titration is essential because fixed doses may be ineffective during periods of high surgical stress or cause excessive bradycardia and hypotension during low-stimulus phases. Our protocol ensured the intervention was dynamically matched to individual patient needs while prioritizing patient safety.

To investigate whether the cardioprotective effect of dexmedetomidine involves sustained modulation of key postoperative processes linked to MINS, such as systemic inflammation, coagulation, hemodynamics, and pain, we compared corresponding biomarkers, vital signs, and pain scores.3,33–35 Under standardized postoperative management, we observed no significant intergroup differences. This consistent absence of differences argues against a major mediating role for these postoperative factors in the observed reduction of MINS. These exploratory findings suggest that the intraoperative period, particularly the hemodynamic stability observed with dexmedetomidine, may play an important role in the observed cardioprotective signal.

The differential results between the PP and ITT analyses provide further clinical insight. The significant reduction in MINS observed in the PP population reflects the protocol’s efficacy when the surgical procedure remains within the laparoscopic paradigm, which the protocol was designed to address. The attenuation of this effect in the ITT analysis, which included all randomized patients irrespective of protocol deviations, including conversion to open surgery, suggests that the physiological disruption caused by conversion to laparotomy introduces competing risks that dilute the measurable effect. This pattern is consistent with observations in other surgical trials, where PP analysis may provide complementary information about biological efficacy when protocol non-compliance involves a fundamental alteration in the treatment context.36

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these results. First, this was a single-center study, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other institutions with different perioperative practices. Second, secondary outcome analyses were based on the PP population, which excluded 13 patients with protocol deviations or non-random missing data. While clinically justified, this approach may introduce selection bias, as the excluded patients had a more complicated intraoperative course. Therefore, the results of secondary outcome analyses should be interpreted with caution. Third, the sample size was calculated to detect an overall difference in MINS incidence among the three groups, and the study was not powered to detect all pairwise contrasts. Consequently, the comparisons of esmolol versus control and the exploratory dexmedetomidine versus esmolol comparison may have lacked sufficient statistical power. Fourth, the 48-hour postoperative troponin monitoring window may have missed delayed myocardial injury events, and the 30-day follow-up period precludes assessment of long-term outcomes. Larger multicenter trials with extended follow-up are needed to determine whether the observed cardioprotective signal translates into improved survival or reduced major adverse cardiac events.

Conclusion

In high-risk patients undergoing laparoscopic colorectal cancer surgery, titrated dexmedetomidine was associated with a numerically lower incidence of MINS compared with conventional management in the primary ITT analysis. However, this difference did not reach the prespecified Bonferroni-corrected threshold. The reduction was statistically significant only in the exploratory PP sensitivity analysis. This signal was not observed with esmolol. The findings suggest that dexmedetomidine may have a potential role in intraoperative hemodynamic optimization in this patient population, but the evidence is not definitive and requires confirmation in larger, adequately powered multicenter trials.
