Mitigation of transient beam loading in a compact high duty cycle injector via hardware upgrade and multi-objective optimization
Authors: Hao Hu, Yu’ang Xia, Yan Wang, Yifeng Zeng, Kaifeng Liu, Tongning Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCompact normal-conducting photoinjectors, such as the Independently Tunable Cells (ITC) injector, are critical for high-power terahertz free electron laser (FEL) sources. However, under high duty cycle operation, the transient beam loading effect significantly degrades the uniformity of electron beam energy and phase within the macropulse, thereby compromising the FEL radiation performance. Here, we propose a strategy to mitigate these transient effects and enhance the macropulse beam quality of the ITC injector for the HUST THz-FEL facility. The proposed hardware upgrade decouples the RF power feeds for the standing wave (SW) cavity and the traveling wave (TW) linac to enable independent timing control, and introduces an adjustable coupling coefficient for the SW cavity. Furthermore, the Non-dominated Sorting Genetic Algorithm III (NSGA-III) is employed to perform multi-objective optimization on four key variables: beam injection time, RF phase, coupling coefficient, and the inter-structure RF delay. Simulation results indicate that single-variable optimization is insufficient to balance energy and phase stability. In contrast, global multi-objective optimization reveals that increasing the coupling coefficient effectively reduces the cavity filling time, while independent RF timing synchronizes the accelerating fields with the beam transient. The optimized configuration achieves a stable macropulse energy of approximately 14 MeV with minimized energy spread and phase variation. Consequently, the combination of independent RF feeding, adjustable coupling, and global optimization effectively compensates for transient beam loading effects. This approach markedly improves the macropulse uniformity of the ITC injector in simulation, offering a potential pathway for similar compact injectors.Subject terms: Engineering, Physics

Introduction

Free Electron Lasers (FELs) are distinguished as the only high-power light sources capable of providing tunable coverage across the entire electromagnetic spectrum1. Particularly in frequency bands that are inaccessible to conventional sources—ranging from Terahertz (THz) waves and soft X-rays to hard X-rays—FELs can generate radiation with both high peak and high average power. Consequently, they have been widely adopted in fundamental research and various industrial applications2,3. In recent years, short-wavelength FELs have provided new pathways for overcoming critical technological bottlenecks, such as those found in advanced lithography4,5. Simultaneously, long-wavelength FELs have gradually expanded from military applications to civil sectors, including large-scale security screening and long-distance communications6,7. To meet the significant demands of civil industry and national defense, the generation of high photon flux FEL radiation imposes increasingly stringent requirements on the power parameters of the driving electron beams.

Among the nearly 100 FEL facilities currently in operation or under construction worldwide, the driving accelerators for high-power radiation primarily follow two technological routes: superconducting and normal-conducting (room-temperature) structures. While superconducting structures offer superior beam quality and high radiation power8–11, they are associated with system complexity and high construction and operational costs. Consequently, normal-conducting structures continue to constitute a significant proportion of existing facilities. These include short-wavelength FELs such as SwissFEL (Switzerland), PAL-XFEL (Korea), SACLA (Japan), and SDUV-FEL (China)5,12–15, as well as long-wavelength facilities like FELIX (Netherlands), NovoFEL (Russia), and FELiChEM and HUST THz-FEL (China)16–19. Notably, the HUST THz-FEL facility employs an Independently Tunable Cells (ITC) injector. This design circumvents the need for complex, expensive photocathode guns and bulky thermionic gun-plus-bunching systems, striking a balance between beam quality and structural compactness. It operates with a macropulse repetition rate of up to 50 Hz, where the micropulse repetition rate is synchronized with the RF frequency19.

From an economic perspective, upgrading the power of existing normal-conducting FEL injectors is a highly suitable approach for civil applications. With advancements in high-power microwave sources and ultra-pure copper processing technologies20,21, enhancing injector beam power by increasing the RF duty cycle has become feasible. However, operating normal-conducting RF injectors at high duty cycles presents new challenges22. High-repetition-rate operation introduces significant thermal loads and other engineering issues that can compromise stability and, in severe cases, threaten equipment safety. Furthermore, the prolonged transient beam loading effect in long-pulse normal-conducting accelerating structures results in performance non-uniformity among bunches within a macropulse, thereby reducing the effective pulse duration. In the specific case of the HUST THz-FEL, given the compact nature of the ITC injector beamline and the scarcity of adjustable parameters, it is necessary to introduce new system-level control variables to guarantee the overall performance of the driving beam.

Based on the HUST THz-FEL experimental platform, this paper elucidates a strategy for optimizing the macropulse performance of the ITC injector. We explore and propose a cost-effective improvement scheme, aiming to provide a robust technical foundation for power upgrades and high-power applications in similar normal-conducting injectors.

Macropulse beam optimization of the current ITC injector

Overview of the injector system

The HUST THz-FEL is a long-wavelength free-electron laser (FEL) experimental platform jointly developed by Huazhong University of Science and Technology (HUST) and the University of Science and Technology of China (USTC). Aimed at civil and commercial applications, the facility focuses on researching key technologies for compact, high-power THz sources. Its ultimate objective is to generate radiation in the 1–10 THz frequency range, achieving a peak power of up to 1 MW and an average power of up to 10 W.

Figure 1 illustrates the layout of the HUST THz-FEL facility. A 2.6 MeV, picosecond-scale electron beam generated by the microwave electron gun is accelerated by a normal conducting constant-gradient traveling wave (TW) linac. The beam is subsequently transported via a quadrupole triplet to a deflection section composed of two dipole magnets and achromatic quadrupoles which can also be used to measure the emittance23. Finally, the beam enters the undulator and is diverted to the beam dump by a downstream dipole magnet. Preliminary experimental results demonstrate that the injector outputs an electron beam with an energy of 13.7 MeV, an energy spread of 0.41%, and a normalized emittance of 8.8 mm·mrad, satisfying the design specifications24.

Schematic layout of the HUST THz-FEL facility19.

As depicted in Fig. 1, the ITC injector comprises two standing wave (SW) cavities (Cell 1 and Cell 2) and a TW LINAC. In the current configuration, the ITC and the LINAC share a common RF power source. Correspondingly, to achieve independent regulation of the accelerating field amplitude and phase, two sets of phase shifters and attenuators are installed at the RF feed waveguides of Cell 2 and the LINAC, supporting maximum input powers of 3.56 MW and 20 MW, respectively. For potential future upgrades, the installation of a new, independent power source for the ITC is being considered to enable variable power injection timing. To monitor the beam status effectively, three Fast Current Transformers (FCTs) are installed along the beamline for real-time detection of the beam waveform and intensity; these are positioned upstream and downstream of the TW linac, and at the entrance of the beam dump. The entire facility features a highly compact design, with the overall layout confined within a 3 m × 3 m area.

Analysis of injector improvements and their impact on macropulse beam performance

Rationale for injector upgrades

Designed to provide high-quality electron beams for civil and commercial FEL facilities, the ITC injector must strictly adhere to the core requirements of compactness and cost-effectiveness. Consequently, any future facility upgrades should aim to minimize both economic costs and the extent of modification workload.

Previous studies26 indicate that the electron beam emitted from the pre-injector exhibits a transverse focusing trend, where the focal point coincides with the position of minimum emittance. Crucially, longitudinal characteristics such as energy spread and bunch length do not deteriorate significantly at this location. Therefore, this specific position is selected as the interface point for the TW linac and focusing solenoids. Furthermore, given that the waveguide transmission line is already equipped with adjustable attenuators and phase shifters, it is advisable to maintain the existing assembly positions of the beamline components to preserve the integrity of the original design layout.

Further analysis of Eq. (1) suggests that adjusting the coupling coefficient significantly influences macropulse stability, while employing a split RF power feeding scheme enhances tuning flexibility. It is noteworthy that neither of these proposed modifications requires altering the existing spatial layout of the beamline or waveguide components.

In summary, this study proposes a strategy that preserves the original spatial configuration. The upgrade involves converting the fixed-coupling SW structure to an adjustable-coupling mechanism and implementing independent RF power injection for the SW and TW structures. This approach aims to achieve highly flexible macropulse regulation and performance optimization at minimum cost.

Impact analysis of adjustable standing wave cavity coupling

The standing wave (SW) cavity is designed to boost the electron beam energy from hundreds of keV to approximately 2.6 MeV. To achieve this energy gain, a peak input power of 3.56 MW is required. This high-power RF is magnetically coupled into the cavity through a coupling iris on the cavity wall. There are two primary methods for adjusting the coupling coefficient: modifying the dimensions of the iris or introducing a coupling tuning post. Previous research27 indicates that, for the same tuning range, modifying the iris requires higher machining precision and induces significant shifts in the resonant frequency and other RF parameters. Consequently, the HUST-FEL injector adopts the post scheme. Figure 4a illustrates the structural model of the coupler, while Fig. 4b presents the S11 reflection coefficient curves corresponding to different coupling coefficients. The minor resonant frequency shifts observed during coupling adjustment can be compensated by the cavity tuners installed on the cavity wall. This approach offers greater flexibility and operational ease while minimizing perturbations to other cavity parameters.

(a) Model of the adjustable coupler; (b) S-parameters corresponding to different coupling coefficients.

However, while increasing the coupling coefficient shortens the transient filling process of the SW cavity, it inevitably reduces the maximum accelerating voltage, thereby lowering the output beam energy. Nevertheless, the proposed system improvement—splitting the RF power feeds for the SW and TW structures—allows for effective compensation. By independently increasing the input power to the SW cavity, the energy deficit caused by the increased coupling coefficient can be fully offset.

Combining observations from Fig. 6a,b, the configuration with exhibits better uniformity in both energy and phase.

The observed improvement in energy consistency is fundamentally due to a slope compensation mechanism. When Δ t > 0, the RF power injection into the SW cavity is delayed relative to the TW linac. Consequently, when the electron beam macropulse traverses the SW cavity, the field is still in its initial build-up phase, exhibiting a positive temporal slope (increasing voltage). In contrast, within the structure, the accelerating field experienced by the beam exhibits a negative slope (decreasing voltage) due to the dominant transient beam loading effect. By optimizing Δ t, the rising edge of the SW field effectively compensates for the drooping field in the TW linac. This mutual compensation flattens the net energy gain profile across the macropulse, thereby enhancing the overall energy stability.

The advantage of the four-variable collaborative optimization can be attributed to a “pulse shaping” mechanism. While determines the filling rate (slope) of the cavity voltage, the timing variables () control the synchronization of this rising edge with the beam transient. By simultaneously optimizing these parameters, the system can tailor the temporal profile of the accelerating field to perfectly mirror the inverse of the beam loading transient. Furthermore, provides the necessary degree of freedom to compensate for phase slippage caused by velocity modulation. This multi-dimensional coupling allows for a robust balance between energy flatness and phase stability that is difficult to achieve through single-parameter tuning alone.

As evident in Fig. 9, the Pareto front solutions are densely concentrated. In the context of engineering commissioning, a high density of solutions implies high operational robustness, meaning that slight deviations in initial parameter settings will still likely yield a solution within the optimal range. Conversely, sparse regions in the Pareto set (which were more prevalent in previous low-dimensional optimizations) correspond to “knife-edge” operating points where the system performance is highly sensitive to parameter deviations, making them unsuitable for stable operation.

In conclusion, implementing independent control over the power sources for the SW cavity and LINAC, combined with adjustable coupling, effectively resolves the issue of large σ (W) and under high duty cycle operation. This approach achieves a synchronized optimization of both energy and phase consistency, providing a robust theoretical and technical basis for the facility upgrade.

Conclusion

To address the significant transient beam loading effects observed in the HUST THz-FEL ITC injector under high duty cycle operation, this study proposes a system improvement scheme combining independent RF power feeding with an adjustable coupling coefficient for the standing wave (SW) cavity. Based on this upgraded hardware configuration, a multi-variable collaborative optimization model was established using the Non-dominated Sorting Genetic Algorithm III (NSGA-III). This model systematically investigates the influence mechanisms of the coupling coefficient, RF injection timing, and phase on macropulse beam quality.

Our analysis reveals that single-variable regulation is insufficient to balance the trade-off between energy gain and macropulse uniformity. In contrast, global collaborative optimization significantly improves the convergence and density of the Pareto solution set. Physically, this enhancement is attributed to a slope compensation mechanism: by optimizing the delay between the SW and TW RF pulses ( vs. ) and adjusting the SW filling rate via the coupling coefficient (), the rising edge of the SW field effectively counteracts the transient field droop caused by beam loading in the TW linac.

Virtual experiments demonstrate that the optimized system maintains a stable energy output of approximately 14.5 MeV while reducing intra-macropulse energy inconsistency to approximately 1.5–2.0% and phase variation to approximately 1.9–2.5, representing a 30–50% improvement over the baseline configuration.

Beyond the beam dynamics optimization, successfully transitioning this proposed scheme to physical operation necessitates addressing several key hardware considerations. First, the introduction of a movable coupling tuner increases the risk of RF field leakage near the tuner region; this can be effectively managed by employing choke-mode structures or optimized tuner geometries, as detailed in our prior analysis27. Second, the suppression of multipacting during long RF pulses is a critical operational concern, which is currently being systematically evaluated through 3D particle-in-cell (PIC) simulations. Finally, it is worth noting that all proposed upgrades are designed to strictly preserve the original spatial layout of the beamline and waveguides, which will significantly limit modification complexity and hardware costs during actual implementation.

Consequently, this work demonstrates the effectiveness of the global parameter optimization strategy in mitigating transient instabilities in compact normal-conducting injectors. The resulting robust operating parameters provide a valuable reference for the future engineering commissioning of the proposed injector upgrade. Finally, it should be noted that the proposed hardware modifications, including the adjustable coupler and independent RF power feeds, remain at the design stage; experimental bench tests and beam commissioning will be required to verify the simulated performance.
