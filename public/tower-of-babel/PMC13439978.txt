A nursing care model with a skill mix in the intensive care unit: a qualitative study of ICU nurses’ and care assistants’ experiences
Authors: Atreyu van Esch, Dewi Stalpers, Susanne M. Maassen, Monique van Dijk, Margo M. C. van Mol
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNursing care models that incorporate a skill mix, including care assistants, are increasingly introduced in intensive care units (ICUs) to address workforce challenges. Understanding staff experiences with the early implementation of such models is essential to support effective collaboration and safe care delivery. This study aimed to explore the ICU nurses’ and care assistants’ experiences with a newly implemented skill mix, focusing on collaboration, role allocation, and perceptions of care delivery.MethodsAn exploratory qualitative study was conducted using four focus group meetings with 33 ICU nurses and nine care assistants working in two adult ICUs of a large tertiary hospital in the Netherlands (August to December 2023). Data were audiotape-recorded, transcribed verbatim, independently coded by two researchers, and analysed using thematic content analysis.FindingsThree major themes were identified: (1) Navigating change and adopting roles, including clear and continuous communication, realistic expectations, and guidance through team managers; (2) Collaboration and team dynamics, encompassing team spirit, recognition to bridge a knowledge gap, opportunities for professional growth, and added value in essential care; (3) Balancing responsibilities in delivering safe care, highlighting tensions related to task allocation and accountability during early implementation.ConclusionThe findings of this early phase evaluation illustrate tensions experienced by ICU nurses and care assistants when adapting to new role distributions within a nursing care model including skill mix. Clear and continuous communication, aligned expectations, and practical support tools—such as guidelines or task cards—facilitate collaboration and support ICU teams during the implementation of such models.Trial registrationNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-026-05170-1.

Background

Nurse shortages in intensive care units (ICUs) are a global challenge [1, 2]. Contributing factors include excessive workload, burnout, moral distress, and nurses’ inability to provide the level of care they consider necessary [3–6]. As a result, healthcare organisations are increasingly exploring alternative models of care delivery.

Traditionally, ICU nurses are responsible for all aspects of patient care, ranging from essential nursing care to highly specialised clinical tasks. Essential nursing care, often referred to as the ‘Fundamentals of Care’, encompasses physical, psychosocial, and relational care activities required to maintain patients’ comfort, dignity, safety, and well-being. These elements are integral to holistic nursing practice [7, 8]. In the ICU, essential care includes personal hygiene, comfort care, positioning, nutrition support, and communication [9]. At the same time, ICU care is delivered in an increasingly complex and technologically advanced environment that requires specialised expertise and continuous clinical oversight. In the context of persistent nursing shortages, integrating care assistants into ICU teams may help alleviate workload pressures by taking responsibility for clearly defined components of essential nursing care [10, 11]. This approach aligns with ‘skill mix’ in an integrated care team. Although skill mix has been used inconsistently in the literature to describe various aspects of nurse staffing, including qualification levels, grade mix, educational preparation, clinical experience, nurse-to-patient ratios, and nursing care hours [12, 13], in this study skill mix addresses the collaboration of healthcare professionals with different competencies and roles who share responsibility for patient care [14].

Across Europe, ICU nurses and care assistants differ in educational preparation, competencies, and scope of practice [15, 16]. Although not without criticism, the European Qualifications Framework (EQF) focusses on learning outcomes formulated according to the knowledge, skills, and general competences at each education level [17, 18]. In general, ICU nurses are vocationally or bachelor educated (EQF levels 4 and 6, respectively), while care assistants are educated at EQF level 3. Integrating these professionals within one team combines different competencies and educational backgrounds, creating a skill mix model of care.

The introduction of care assistants into the ICU nursing team, where essential care activities are performed under the supervision of ICU nurses, is not a new model of care [19, 20]. Previous studies have reported mixed experiences with this approach, including challenges related to collaboration, task delegation, role delineation, professional identity, and continuity of care [20–24]. Care assistants require time and guidance to develop the contextual understanding needed to deliver care in the highly specialised ICU setting.

In the Netherlands, however, this model is not yet explored in its value. Care assistants traditionally support nursing care in non-ICU settings; however, their introduction into ICU teams is relatively recent and varies across hospitals.

Existing literature predominantly focuses on staffing indicators or patient outcomes, while less attention has been paid to how ICU nurses and care assistants experience working within integrated teams. As a result, there is limited understanding of how a skill mix model is experienced by ICU nurses and care assistants. The early implementation of a skill mix model may particularly influence collaboration, role delineation, and perceptions of care delivery as ICU nurses and care assistants adapt to new responsibilities and working relationships. A thorough exploration of both ICU nurses’ and care assistants’ perspectives is warranted to bridge a knowledge gap during the early implementation of a skill mix nursing care model.

Aim

This study aimed to explore ICU nurses’ and care assistants’ experiences with a newly implemented skill mix nursing care model, focusing on collaboration, role allocation, and perceptions of care delivery.

Methods

Design

An exploratory qualitative design was adopted for this study. As the study aimed to gain an in-depth understanding of ICU nurses’ and care assistants’ experiences with a newly implemented skill mix model, no specific theoretical framework was applied a priori. Focus group interviews were used for data collection, as these facilitate dynamic interaction between participants and enable the exploration of shared experiences, attitudes, and perceptions regarding an integrated team structure. This approach is particularly suited to early-stage research where knowledge and understanding are limited [25, 26]. The study adhered to The Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) [27] (Supplemental File 1).

Setting and participants

The study was conducted from August to December 2023 in two ICUs of a large tertiary hospital in the Netherlands, including a mixed 10-bed medical–surgical ICU and a 16-bed cardiothoracic ICU for adults. During this period, a total of 119 ICU nurses and 13 care assistants, were employed across both ICUs. Eligible participants were ICU employees (> 18 years) working as ICU nurses or care assistants, who were able to participate in a focus group discussion, had at least one month of ICU work experience, and had experience collaborating in a skill-mix team with care assistants. Employees who were absent due to sickness or maternity leave during the study period were excluded.

Context

A steering group, including two nurse managers, one human resources advisor, one quality advisor, and three ICU nurses, prepared the introduction of care assistants into the ICU nursing team. Several sessions were organised to discuss the permitted tasks, onboarding, and training of the care assistants, as well as overall support, communication, and monitoring during the project. ICU nurses were informed through team meetings, newsletters, and posters, which detailed the scope of the care assistants’ tasks, the training process, and their integration into the ICU team.

All care assistants were newly hired and completed a structured two-week onboarding and training period which included mastering basic care tasks for both low-complexity non-intubated and high-complexity intubated ICU patients. This also covered nutritional care, logistical support, assistance with medical procedures, administrative tasks, and responses to acute situations (e.g., intubations and resuscitations). Care assistants had specific ‘task cards’, written cards explaining all legal permitted tasks (Supplemental file 2). These task cards specified the scope of practice for care assistants as decided by the ICU management team. They were not authorised to administer medication or perform clinical assessments. Care assistants were scheduled into eight-hour day and evening shifts. During day shifts, two care assistants were present in each team of five ICU nurses, together taking care of maximal nine patients. In the evening shift, one care assistant was assigned to four ICU nurses who were responsible for maximal nine patients. Daily team huddles provided feedback and improvement points, while managers monitored psychosocial wellbeing and supported staff under stress.

Procedures and data gathering

Qualitative data were collected three to four months after care assistants joined the ICU teams. Four mixed focus groups consisting of ICU nurses and care assistants were hold to support joint reflection on collaboration and role negotiation and to avoid discussion of one professional group in the absence of the other. Convenience sampling was used; ICU nurses and care assistants who were present in the unit at the time of the focus group interviews were permitted to participate in the focus group meetings. No eligible participants declined participation at the time of recruitment. Participant gender and professional group (ICU nurse or care assistant) were recorded as participant characteristics. In the background, to support participants during the focus group meetings if necessary, a nurse manager who served as a familiar contact person for care assistants during the implementation process was present for logistical support only. The nurse manager did not facilitate or participate in the discussions and was not involved in the study design, data collection, analysis, or interpretation. Although intended to provide practical support and reassurance, we acknowledge that the presence of a person in a managerial role may have influenced participants’ willingness to speak openly. Participants received comprehensive information on the study’s objectives, the duration of interviews, their anticipated contributions, and the researchers’ backgrounds.

To minimise content bias, all semi-structured interviews were facilitated by three researchers (MvM, ICU nurse and holding a PhD in psychology; SM, holding a PhD in nursing; and AvE, ICU nurse and holding a Master’s degree in epidemiology) who were not involved in decisions or introductions concerning the care assistants, nor in participants’ clinical supervision, ICU care, or team interactions. Two facilitators (MvM and SM) had expertise in qualitative interview techniques, enabling them to highlight participants’ reflections on topics such as implementation, interprofessional collaboration, and clarity of task demarcation. An interview guide, informed by relevant literature and the researchers’ expertise, was specifically developed to facilitate in-depth discussion of both positive experiences and challenges associated with working with the new skill mix (Box 1, Supplemental file 3). The interview guide informed data collection but was not used as an analytical framework. Although the interview guide included questions related to collaboration, role allocation, and care delivery, these topics were not used as predefined themes during analysis. An observer (AvE) noted non-verbal gestures and interactions between the participants. During the focus group interviews, the moderators actively encouraged balanced participation by inviting both ICU nurses and care assistants to share their views. Particular attention was paid to ensuring that care assistants, being in the minority, were explicitly invited to contribute their experiences. At the start of each session, ground rules were clearly communicated to create a respectful and safe atmosphere, and to minimise any feelings of intimidation. Both positive and negative experiences were welcomed, and the group dynamics were closely observed throughout the interviews. These measures were intended to reduce potential hierarchical influences associated with the mixed-professional composition of the focus groups and to facilitate open discussion among all participants. All focus groups were held in-person during daytime working hours and lasted 30 to 50 min. An Auronic Digital Voice Recorder was used for audio recording. The recordings were transcribed verbatim and destroyed thereafter to ensure proper privacy regulations were followed.

Data analysis

A thematic content analysis approach was used for data analysis [28]. This method was chosen to structure the data systematically and to provide new insights into this developing care model, improve researcher understanding, and inform practical strategies [29]. The analysis was conducted in three main stages.

First, the interview transcripts were read multiple times by two researchers (AvE and MvM) to become familiar with the data and gain a holistic understanding of the content. Second, meaningful units were identified, coded, and grouped into categories based on recurring patterns and pre-defined topics relevant to the research aim. One researcher (AvE) coded the transcripts line by line using AtlasTi© software. Codes were generated inductively from participants’ accounts and iteratively grouped into categories and themes that reflected recurring patterns across participants’ experiences. The coding process involved condensing key statements into shorter formulations and abstracting these into conceptual labels. Third, the codes were clustered into subthemes and overarching themes that captured central aspects of participants’ experiences. These thematic groupings were reviewed and discussed within the research team to ensure they adequately represented the data. Coding decisions and thematic structures were refined in discussion with a third researcher (DS), enhancing the credibility of the interpretation. Data saturation was reached after four focus group interviews, as no new relevant information emerged in the final session.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Medical Ethics Committee of Erasmus MC (MEC-2023-0551), which determined that the study did not fall under the scope of the Dutch Medical Research Involving Human Subjects Act and granted a waiver. The study adhered to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki (64th WMA General Assembly, Fortaleza, Brazil, October 2013). Both oral and written informed consent were obtained from all participants. The collected data were processed in a strictly anonymous manner to ensure the participants’ privacy.

Rigour

To ensure methodological rigour, we applied the four dimensions of trustworthiness, which include credibility, transferability, dependability, and confirmability [30, 31]. A respectful and open relationship was established with participants, and efforts were made to foster a safe interview environment in which both positive and negative experiences could be shared. Throughout the interviews, the moderators maintained a neutral stance and used probing questions, repetition, summarising, and informal member checking to elicit and validate in-depth responses. Non-verbal observations were documented, and peer debriefings were held after each interview to reflect on dynamics and emerging findings. Transferability was supported by clear inclusion and exclusion criteria and by detailed descriptions of the study setting and participant characteristics, enabling contextual comparison. Dependability was ensured through a consistent research process guided by a predefined topic list, which remained unchanged during the study. All methodological steps were transparently documented and discussed within the research team. Confirmability was supported by involving at least two independent researchers in each phase of the analysis. Coding and theme development were performed independently, and discrepancies were resolved through discussion with a third researcher. This collaborative approach helped to minimise bias and maintain analytic transparency.

Findings

The four focus group interviews included 42 participants, ranging from 9 to 12 participants per focus group (Table 1). Three focus groups were mixed, including ICU nurses and care assistants. However, no care assistants were present in focus group 3 due to unforeseen last-minute absence of the care assistants who had intended to participate. Rescheduling was considered but was not feasible within the planned data collection period because of staff availability and roster constraints. We therefore proceeded with the focus group as scheduled.

We identified three overarching themes, comprising a total of nine subthemes, that reflected the inherently complex process of introducing a skill mix model in the ICU: (1) Navigating change and adopting roles; (2) Collaboration and team dynamics; and (3) Balancing responsibilities in delivering safe care (Fig. 1). Although presented as separate themes, the findings illustrate interconnected aspects of the implementation process. Experiences related to communication and role adoption shaped collaboration between ICU nurses and care assistants, while collaboration subsequently influenced perceptions of responsibility, accountability, and safe care delivery. All quotes indicate their subthemes and reflect the opinion of one participant (Tables 2 and 3, and 4).

Theme 1: Navigating change and adopting roles

Across both professional groups, participants described uncertainty during the early phase of implementation. However, ICU nurses primarily emphasised the need for clear task boundaries and oversight, whereas care assistants focused more on guidance and learning opportunities while adapting to their new role and workplace. Participants frequently linked communication about the implementation process to their understanding of role expectations and responsibilities. These differing perspectives reflected the distinct positions of ICU nurses and care assistants within the implementation process and shaped how they experienced the introduction of the skill mix model. This theme was divided into three subthemes.

Clear and continuous communication

ICU nurses had mixed views on communication regarding the pilot project. Some felt well informed (quote 1), while others found the communication lacking despite the provision of informative team meetings, emails, and repeated ICU newsletters (quote 2). Nurses also expressed the need for clearer communication from the nurse managers (quote 3). In contrast, care assistants generally viewed the communication positively, feeling well informed about the onboarding process through a personal approach and regular email updates (quote 4).

Realistic expectations

ICU nurses recognised the importance of onboarding and providing emotional support to care assistants, but some reported that they were not the best suited for these tasks, which increased their workload (quote 5). Guiding care assistants was challenging. Some ICU nurses felt responsible for this task, noting that the time and energy spent on training reduced their focus on patient care, which they considered to be a suboptimal situation (quotes 6–7). Others assumed that this investment would eventually enable more efficient collaboration (quote 8). ICU nurses also emphasised their own need for realistic expectations, acknowledging that adjusting to a new domain would require individual learning pathways among the care assistants (quote 9).

Care assistants felt integrated into the team but faced moments of uncertainty and described their experience as a ‘culture shock’ (quote 10). They needed time to adapt to the ICU environment and patient population, highlighting initial confusion about the roles and expectations of both sides (quotes 11–12). They struggled with not contributing in acute situations (quote 13) and emphasised their ongoing learning process, varying adaptability, and need for guidance (quote 14). While willing to take over tasks, care assistants understood that building trust with ICU nurses is essential.

Guidance through the team managers

Some ICU nurses faced uncertainty about the roles and responsibilities of the care assistants. The nurses expressed that management could have better clarified what tasks care assistants were able and permitted to do independently, thus improving collaboration in the new integrated team (quote 15). In contrast, end-of-shift evaluation meetings with management were well received, because such meetings were focused on accepting the skill mix and possibilities rather than repeatedly discussing concerns (quote 16). The frequency of evaluation meetings was deemed sufficient by ICU nurses and care assistants to guide role adaptation (quotes 17–18).

Theme 2: collaboration and team dynamics

Both ICU nurses and care assistants highlighted the importance of teamwork and mutual respect, yet their perspectives differed in emphasis: ICU nurses often reflected on trust and responsibility within the team, while care assistants emphasised recognition, inclusion, and being acknowledged as valuable team members. Building on the experiences described in theme 1, participants’ perceptions of communication and role adoption during implementation shaped day-to-day collaboration within the integrated team. This theme is divided into four subthemes.

Team spirit

At the time of the pilot, ICU nurses and care assistants did not yet view themselves as one cohesive team, though they saw potential for this to develop (quotes 1–2). Key areas for enhancement included communication and managing expectations, while their initial difficulties were considered to be typical of those when forming a new team (quotes 3–4).

ICU nurses recognised that integration and collaboration could take time and considered this factor to be a normal challenge when onboarding new members (quote 3). Some nurses were positive about the collaboration and assumed that mutual investment would yield positive outcomes (quotes 5–6). However, others noted that care assistants sometimes showed a passive attitude, potentially due to insufficient onboarding, unclear task specifications, and uncertainty about mutual expectations (quote 7). As time progressed, ICU nurses gained a clearer sense of care assistants’ competences and increasingly worked as a team in essential tasks such as patient turning and offering essential care tasks (quote 8).

Care assistants felt welcomed and appreciated the personal guidance from ICU nurses (quote 9). They generally viewed the collaboration positively (quote 6), despite differences in attitude and openness (quote 4). Despite initial uncertainty about their expectations, care assistants reported that collaboration improved over time as they spent more time in the unit (quote 6).

Recognition to bridge a knowledge gap

ICU nurses noted a knowledge gap among care assistants when working in the ICU. Nurses reported a potentially misleading first impression of the patient population, with a focus on long-stay and regular ward patients. The nurses observed that care assistants still struggled to recognise critical situations such as acute respiratory issues (quote 10). However, ICU nurses also questioned whether it is realistic to expect care assistants to possess the necessary knowledge and expertise to care for ICU patients (quote 11). Care assistants acknowledged their specific knowledge in caring, which was limited when it came to medication and the complexity of ventilated patients (quote 12). The nurses recognised the ICU as a distinctly different environment and emphasised the need for time to adjust (quote 13).

Opportunities for professional growth

All care assistants shared that their career switch to an ICU environment was driven by an intrinsic desire for professional growth and new challenges, not because of staff shortages or a high workload in previous jobs (quote 14). They expressed a commitment to lifelong learning, career advancement, and pursuing excellence in their field (quote 15). Care assistants experienced satisfaction in the complexity of ICU work and greatly admired the work of ICU nurses (quote 16). Meanwhile, some ICU nurses expressed a willingness to support the learning processes of care assistants (quote 17).

Added value in essential care

The integration of care assistants was perceived as beneficial, especially for patient mobilisation and essential care tasks (quote 18). Although building relationships in such teams requires time and effort, this approach could help address ICU staff shortages in the long term (quote 19). However, ICU nurses were ambivalent regarding the added value of minimising their own tasks in providing care (quotes 20–21). The nurses felt responsible for the technical devices and advanced medical treatment provided in the ICU and preferred to retain control over all nursing procedures (quote 20). Nonetheless, the nurses anticipated that care assistants would retain their motivation until they could significantly contribute and add value (quote 22). Care assistants themselves emphasised their perceived added value in providing essential nursing care. They described feeling competent and comfortable performing basic care tasks for less complex patients. While they experienced that their contribution was often appreciated, they also recognised that not all ICU nurses felt equally comfortable delegating these tasks (quote 23).

Theme 3: Balancing responsibilities in delivering safe care

Building on participants’ experiences of collaboration and role allocation, theme 3 continues to reflect how responsibilities and accountability for patient care were perceived within the integrated team. While both groups shared concerns about maintaining safe care, ICU nurses more frequently expressed responsibility-related tensions and accountability for patient outcomes, whereas care assistants described navigating their scope of practice and knowing when to seek support from ICU nurses. This theme is divided into two subthemes.

Tensions related to task allocation

Clear task specifications were needed to enhance collaboration and task distribution between ICU nurses and care assistants (quotes 1–2). ICU nurses preferred care assistants to focus on less complex patients, reserving the care of highly complex cases for qualified ICU nurses (quote 3). Overall, it appeared important for care assistants to clearly define and assert their roles (quote 4).

Care assistants reported difficulties in declining tasks they were not authorised to perform (Table 2, quote 12) and expressed a need for clearer guidelines. They suggested using task cards (quote 5). Additionally, care assistants could not perform their regular tasks, such as inserting nasogastric tubes, performing bladder catheterisations, administering medications, and giving injections (quote 6–7).

ICU nurses expressed concerns about responsibilities, fearing that a skill mix could pose safety risks since care assistants lack the same level of independent authority and may not adequately detect or respond to clinical issues in a timely manner (quotes 8–9). Nurses emphasised the need to retain their clinical judgment and ultimate responsibility (quotes 8–9). Care assistants, in turn, emphasised that they did not wish to take responsibility for ICU patient care, suggesting that this domain should remain with the ICU nurses (quotes 10–11). Finding a balance, in letting go and taking responsibility was recognised in both ICU nurses and care assistants.

Accountability during early implementation

Several ICU nurses expressed concerns about maintaining responsibility, especially for highly complex patients. These nurses noted a shift towards task-oriented care rather than a holistic approach (quote 12) and feared that delegating tasks might compromise quality of care, particularly in the high-tech and demanding ICU environment (quote 13). Nurses worried about potential miscommunications and losing their line of sight on patient care when tasks were divided among different caregivers (quote 13). ICU patients, they argued, deserve an ICU nurse who maintains a close overview of all aspects of care. Nurses emphasised the importance of remaining involved in the comprehensive care of the patient (quote 9). Similarly, a team with too many different professions and levels was perceived negatively by some of the ICU nurses. However, others noted that the involvement of care assistants with less complex ICU patients could be a valuable addition (quote 3).

Some of the care assistants, meanwhile, highlighted their supportive role in recovery-focused and essential care, particularly with less complex patients (Table 3, quote 18). They emphasised their role in managing anxious or restless patients, helping to calm them and prevent issues such as the removal of endotracheal tubes (quote 14), thereby contributing to overall care safety.

Discussion

This study explored ICU nurses’ and care assistants’ experiences with a newly implemented skill mix model in two Dutch ICUs. The findings reinforce existing literature by illustrating that skill mix implementation in the ICU is a complex and evolving process, shaped by changing roles, team dynamics, and shared responsibility for patient care, while requiring continuous alignment between professionals’ competencies, responsibilities, and the demands of critical care practice [32, 33].

The introduction of skill mix was accompanied by uncertainty and a learning curve in defining and refining the responsibilities of ICU nurses and care assistants. Team forming is a continuous process of exploring roles, letting go of established patterns, and striving for positive outcomes [34]. Consistent with theories of transition and team development, participants described challenges related to changing roles, responsibilities, and ways of working [35, 36]. Some ICU nurses expressed concerns that dividing care tasks could lead to more task-oriented rather than holistic care, reflected as balancing responsibilities in delivering safe care. They also voiced fear of losing oversight and responsibility for managing complex patients. These concerns coexisted with generally positive evaluations of collaboration and guidance, illustrating the tension inherent in the early phases of implementation. Some ICU nurses related these concerns to their experiences during the COVID-19 pandemic, a period in which care fragmentation was perceived as more pronounced [37, 38].

Together, these findings underscore the importance of clear communication, managerial support, and shared expectations during periods of role transition and team development. Differences between ICU nurses and care assistants were most apparent in perceptions of communication, role clarity, and responsibility. These differences likely reflect their distinct starting positions within the implementation process and may partly explain the tensions observed during the early phase of skill mix implementation.

Beyond practical challenges related to task allocation and responsibility, introducing a skill mix model prompted reflection on professional identity and long-established nursing roles. ICU nurses reported difficulties delegating tasks to care assistants, partly due to concerns about losing oversight of complex patient care in this acute setting. This finding is consistent with previous studies showing that ICU nurses perceive themselves as responsible for the full spectrum of patient care activities [11, 39, 40]. To navigate a change in this process would require a new staffing culture, including a complete redesign of nursing roles responsive to patient needs and essential care [41]. However, staff could be resistant to adopting skill mix models due to their traditional way of working [41]. It is therefore important to not only acknowledge ICU nurses’ knowledge and competencies but also understand their professional identity [42]. ICU nursing entails more than simply delivering the right care at the right time. As suggested by our findings, ICU nurses need time to recognise the added value of care assistants, build trust in new ways of working, and adapt to evolving role boundaries.

While task delegation and more efficient task distribution are intended to reduce workload and improve the efficiency of ICU care, holistic nursing care remains a fundamental professional value for ICU nurses [43, 44]. This does not necessarily imply that all care activities must be performed by a single individual; rather, it entails maintaining clinical oversight, continuity, and responsibility for the patient as a whole. When roles, responsibilities, and communication pathways are clearly defined, skill mix can support rather than undermine holistic care, whereas ambiguity may reinforce perceptions of fragmented, task-oriented practice [43].

Implementing a new model of care with integrated teams in the ICU required adaptation and flexibility, realistic expectations, and the guidance of management. In particular, participants reported mixed experiences with project communication mediated through team managers. Similar to a previous study, nurses did not always feel adequately informed and reported an increased workload from supervising care assistants, which impacted their focus on patient care [11]. Guiding the old (safe) situation to the new (and uncertain) one is an important responsibility of the manager [45].

Care assistants contribute meaningfully to essential patient care [46]. In our study, ICU nurses and care assistants highlighted their role in direct patient care, particularly in communication, comfort, and rehabilitation activities. Their involvement in defined aspects of essential care enabled ICU nurses to devote more attention to complex clinical tasks while ensuring that patients’ fundamental care needs remained visible and addressed. This finding aligns with previous studies showing that care assistants support timely nursing interventions and enhance patient experiences [19]. To maximise these benefits, care assistants need a clearly defined scope of practice, delineated responsibilities, and structured collaboration with ICU nurses [47, 48]. Within such a complementary role, they strengthen ICU teams without replacing the expertise and accountability of ICU nurses [49, 50]. Clear role boundaries support patient safety, effective teamwork, and high-quality care.

Practical implications

Introducing a skill mix nursing care model in the ICU is a complex process that requires sufficient time for adaptation during early implementation. Our findings suggest that successful implementation depends on realistic expectations, structured onboarding, tailored education, and supervised practice. Importantly, ICU nurses and care assistants may require different forms of support during this process. While ICU nurses may need support in adapting to changing responsibilities, delegation, and maintaining oversight of complex patient care, care assistants benefit from clear guidance, opportunities for professional development, and sufficient time to gain confidence within the ICU environment.

Clear role delineation, supported by practical tools such as defined scopes of practice and task cards, can enhance confidence in delegation and teamwork. Particular attention should be paid to clarifying which activities can be performed independently by care assistants and when support from ICU nurses should be sought. In addition, care assistants require ongoing development of ICU-specific knowledge and contextual understanding, making continued supervision and active involvement of ICU nurses in onboarding and training essential.

Skill mix models may be particularly valuable in supporting fundamental nursing care activities, such as mobilisation, comfort care, communication, and other essential care tasks, allowing ICU nurses to focus on patients with higher levels of complexity. Successful implementation further requires supportive leadership, continuous communication, and opportunities to build trust between team members. Although implementation should be tailored to local staffing models, patient populations, and organisational contexts, our findings suggest that skill mix models may offer a promising strategy for addressing ongoing ICU workforce shortages. Care assistants should therefore be positioned as complementary members of the ICU team rather than as substitutes for ICU nurses.

Strengths and limitations

The strength of this study lies in its broad exploration of experiences among both ICU nurses and care assistants. In particular, reported practices for optimal onboarding and professional growth of new staff contribute to strategies for reducing ICU workforce shortages.

However, our study also has limitations that should be acknowledged. First this research is based on data from a single-centre study. Therefore, transferability of the findings should be assessed with caution. Contextual differences—such as team culture, staffing models, and institutional policies—should be considered when applying these findings. Second, the data collection was conducted in an early phase of implementing the skill mix model. As a results, collaborative relationships may not have been fully established, and experiences may reflect a period of transition and uncertainty. Third, due to the characteristics of using a qualitative design with focus groups, the most prominent opinions may have been voiced during the meetings, potentially resulting in an overrepresentation of certain experiences. In addition, the mixed-professional composition of the focus groups may have influenced participants’ willingness to express critical views. Although efforts were made to create a safe environment and encourage balanced participation, socially desirable responses may have occurred, particularly given the inclusion of both ICU nurses and care assistants within the same discussion groups. Fourth, a nurse manager was present during the focus group meetings in a non-participatory role. Although this was intended to provide practical support and reassurance during the early implementation phase, the presence of an individual in a managerial position may have influenced participants’ responses through perceived hierarchical relationships or social desirability bias. Fifth, detailed participant characteristics, such as professional title and length of work experience, were not systematically collected. This represents a missed source of potentially relevant information, as variation in perspectives related to seniority or professional role may not have been fully captured. Future studies should consider incorporating such characteristics to enhance the representativeness and persuasiveness of their findings. Finally, future quantitative studies should examine measurable outcomes of integrated ICU teams, such as the impact of care assistants on patient safety (e.g., near misses, medication errors), nursing-sensitive indicators (e.g., missed care), and staff-related outcomes (e.g., job satisfaction, sickness absence, perceived workload).

Conclusion

The introduction of a skill mix model including ICU nurses and care assistants required teams to adapt to new role distributions and ways of working during the early implementation phase. While care assistants contributed to essential patient care and supported ICU nurses in managing workload, the transition also raised questions about role boundaries, responsibility, and maintaining a holistic approach to care. Our findings suggest that successful implementation of skill mix in the ICU depends on clear communication, aligned expectations, practical support tools, and sufficient time for teams to adapt to new roles. Opportunities for knowledge-sharing and the development of trusting working relationships may further facilitate collaboration while supporting safe, person-centred care.
