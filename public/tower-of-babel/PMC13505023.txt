Prevalence of intimate partner violence (IPV) among women with mental illness in the tertiary hospital setting
Authors: Anitha Jeyagurunathan, Edimansyah Abdin, Muhammad Iskandar Shah Mohmad Khalid, Lee Yun Ting, Rajeswari Sambasivam, Zhang Yunjue, Kang Xinyi, Lilian Mark Fong Eng, Tommy Chan Chun Ting, Sutapa Basu, Nisha Chandwani, Steve Yin Lam Lee, Mythily Subramaniam

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIntimate partner violence (IPV) is a major public health concern. It is one of the most common forms of interpersonal violence, with approximately 35% of women experiencing physical and sexual violence by an intimate partner at some point in their lives. Women with mental illness are a vulnerable risk group for IPV. The current study aims to assess the proportion of women with mental illness who experienced IPV, and to examine the association of socio-demographic variables, quality of life, and clinical correlates of women with mental illness experiencing IPV in Singapore.Methods255 participants with a primary diagnosis of schizophrenia and related psychoses or depression disorders were recruited. Data on IPV were assessed on the World Health Organisation Violence Against Women (WHOVAW) scale, consisting of three domains- psychological, physical, and sexual IPV. Psychopathology was measured using the Brief Psychiatric Rating Scale-18 items (BPRS), and the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 items (PHQ-9). The World Health Organization Quality of Life-BREF (WHOQOL-BREF) was used to assess subjective well-being. Data on socio-demographic characteristics were also obtained. Multivariable logistic regressions were used for analysis.ResultsThe mean age of the participants (n = 255) was 35.1 ± 11.9 years. Approximately half of the sample were diagnosed with schizophrenia (n = 128, 50.2%). The proportion of women with mental illness who experienced IPV was 50.2%. Women with higher scores on the positive symptoms’ subscale in BPRS were more likely to experience overall violence against women (VAW) (OR = 1.03, P = 0.02), psychological (OR = 1.03, P = 0.03) and physical violence (OR = 1.04, P < 0.0001) compared to those with lower scores. Women who experienced psychological violence had significantly lower quality of life (QOL) in the social relationship domain (ꞵ=-2.42, P = 0.05) and environmental domain (ꞵ=-2.46, P = 0.01) compared to those who did not experience psychological violence, whereas women who experienced physical abuse were significantly associated with lower QOL in the physical domain (ꞵ=-1.91, P = 0.05) as compared to those not experiencing physical abuse.Discussion and conclusionThe study highlights the high proportion of women with mental illness who experience IPV. Overall VAW scores, psychological and physical IPV were strongly associated with socio demographic variables and clinical risk factors. Exposure to violence also adversely affects multiple domains of QOL. The high proportion of IPV among this group of patients is concerning and mental health professionals should actively identify IPV and implement holistic interventions to ensure care of women with mental illness.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Women, Intimate partner violence, Abuse, Depression, Schizophrenia, Outpatients

Background

Intimate partner violence (IPV) is a violation of an individual’s human rights which is recognized as a global public health issue [1]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), IPV is defined as: any behaviour within an intimate relationship that causes physical, psychological, or sexual harm, including physical aggression, sexual coercion, psychological abuse and controlling behaviours [2]. IPV affects around 641 million women globally [3], or one in three women which is approximately 35% of women experiencing physical and sexual violence by an intimate partner at some point in their lives [2, 4]. Countries in Africa and Southeast Asia have been reported to have the highest lifetime prevalence of IPV with rates of 36.6% and 37.7% respectively [5, 6].

Research has shown that women with severe mental illness experience higher rates of violence than women in the general population [7–10]. Experiences of IPV can impact many aspects of women’s well-being, including social cohesion and connectedness, economic security, physical and mental health, and political aspirations [2]. Globally, studies from high, middle and low-income countries document that IPV can lead to various mental health consequences. A meta-analysis showed that women exposed to IPV were at 2–3 times higher risk for major depressive disorder and 1.5-2 times higher risk for elevated depressive symptoms and postpartum depression [11]. Another meta-analysis observed higher odds of lifetime diagnosis of anxiety, depression, eating disorders, posttraumatic stress and sleep disorders among those who had experienced sexual abuse [12].

An intimate relationship involves physical and emotional intimacy. When violence occurs within such relationships, it significantly impacts quality of life; the standard of health, comfort and happiness experienced in day-to-day life [13]. Various research studies have shown that women who experienced IPV from male partners reported lower quality of life across multiple domains, including physical health, social relationships, environment and psychological wellbeing [13–16]) Research consistently demonstrates that women with severe mental illness experience significantly higher rates of violence compared to women in the general population. Globally, in the general population, 35% of women aged 15 years and above have experienced IPV in their lifetime [5, 6]. However, evidence shows that women with mental illness face significantly higher risk, often exceeding 50% in the clinical populations [17]. The possible reasons could be that their mental illness affects their cognitive and emotional functioning, such as reduced risk perception and low self-esteem, which may limit women’s ability to recognise or exit abusive relationship [18, 19]. In addition, women with mental illness are more likely to experience social isolation and stigma, which are particularly relevant in the Singapore context where mental health stigma remains a barrier to help-seeking [20]. Furthermore, dependence on intimate partners for financial or emotional support can reinforce power imbalances and increase the risk of ongoing abuse [21]. National reports from the Ministry of Social and Family Development Singapore [22] also identify vulnerable women, including those with psychosocial difficulties, as being at a greater risk of family violence. Importantly, the relationship between IPV and mental illness is bidirectional, as exposure to violence increases the likelihood of depression, anxiety etc., thereby perpetuating a cycle of victimisation [4, 7]. Overall, these interacting factors explain why IPV prevalence is markedly higher among women with mental illness compared to the general female population. IPV against women is a pressing issue in Singapore like the rest of the world. Recent data indicates a concerning rise in IPV in Singapore. Earlier data from the International Violence Against Women Survey reported that approximately 1 in 10 (9.2%) women in Singapore had experienced at least one incidence of violence (physical and /or sexual violence in their adult lifetime [23]. According to the Ministry of Social and Family Development, 2008 new IPV cases were reported in 2023, marking a 15% increase from 1,741 in 2022 [24]. Additionally, during the COVID-19 pandemic, a study found elevated rates of IPV among Singaporean women, with 19.8% reporting verbal abuse, 13.5% physical violence, 14.7% pressured sex, and 15.2% reported forced sex [25]. However, while studies conducted elsewhere have indicated that women with psychiatric illness are at greater risk of IPV, little is known about the clinical profile, lifetime exposure and its forms in the psychiatric settings in Singapore. Given the current gap in the literature, this study aims to examine the proportion of women with mental illness who experience IPV in the clinical settings. Therefore, the aims of the study are: (1) to estimate the proportion of women with mental illness reporting IPV, and (2) to examine the association of socio-demographic variables, quality of life and clinical correlates of women with mental illness experiencing IPV.

Methods

Participants

The present study was conducted among outpatients who were seeking treatment at a tertiary psychiatry hospital, in Singapore, and its satellite clinics. A total of 255 female participants were recruited for this study from March 2024 to February 2025. Participants were recruited using convenience sampling: attending clinicians or other mental healthcare professionals (e.g., case managers, researchers) referred interested patients to the study team. The inclusion criteria included: (1) being a Singapore citizen or Permanent Resident aged 21 to 65 years; (2) having a clinical diagnosis of schizophrenia and related psychoses or a depressive disorder; (3) able to speak and comprehend English, Chinese, Malay, or Tamil. The exclusion criteria were those with cognitive impairment or intellectual disability.

Procedures

Researchers on the team exercised caution during recruitment and approached only women who were alone and not accompanied by a partner. After obtaining written informed consent, all participants were given a resource brochure containing details of available helplines. Thereafter, they completed a series of self-administered questionnaires using a pencil and paper format and were interviewed in a secluded place to ensure confidentiality of their information. This study was approved by the National Healthcare Group Domain Specific Review Board (DSRB) and the IMH Institutional Research Review Committee (IRRC) in Singapore (2023/00650). All participants provided written informed consent prior to their enrolment and appropriate measures were taken to maintain confidentiality of their data.

Instruments

World health organization violence against women (WHOVAW)

The WHOVAW instrument was developed for use in the World Health Organization (WHO) multi-country study on women’s health and IPV [26, 27]. The questionnaire comprises 13 items measuring the three basic forms of IPV (i.e., psychological, physical, and sexual violence), with questions in these domains rated either ‘yes’ or ‘no’. Psychological violence subscale comprises of 4 items (score range :0–4), physical violence subscale consists of 6 items (score range 0–6), and sexual violence subscale consists of 3 items (score range: 0–3). The categorical variables are computed as: any psychological violence: score of > 1 on psychological subscale, any physical violence: score > 1 on physical subscale, any sexual violence: score > 1 on sexual subscale and any IPV (overall): total score > 1. Proportions are reported by category (psychological, physical, sexual) and overall VAW are used to estimate proportions of women experiencing any violence which is standard in epidemiological reporting and allows comparability across studies and countries [26, 28]. It is a cross-culturally appropriate tool that has been used among individuals with various psychiatric disorders [7, 28, 29]. The instrument demonstrated good internal consistency (overall Cronbach’s alpha = 0.89). The Cronbach's alpha coefficients for the psychological, physical, and sexual violence subscales were 0.84, 0.87, and 0.73, respectively.

Patient health questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9)

The PHQ-9 uses 9 items to measure for the presence and severity of depressive symptoms [30]. Each item is rated on a scale from 0 (not at all) to 3 (nearly every day), with a higher total score indicating greater self-reported symptoms of depression. Based on prior studies, a cutoff score of less than 10 is commonly used as minimal/mild symptoms of depression and a cutoff score of 10 or more is commonly used to indicate as moderate/severe symptoms of depression [31, 32]. A PHQ-9 score of more than 10 has also been shown to have sensitivity of 88% and a specificity of 88% for identifying depression [33]. The PHQ has been widely used to measure the severity of depressive symptoms among psychiatric patients in Singapore [34–36] and has shown good reliability and validity for use in local primary care settings [37, 38]. The internal consistency reliability for PHQ-9 (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.92) was high in the current study.

World health organization quality of life-BREF (WHOQOL-BREF)

The WHOQOL-BREF questionnaire was used to assess subjective personal health and well-being over the past 2 weeks using a 5-point Likert scale [39]. The brief version of WHOQOL-100 consisting of 26 items examines four domains: physical health, psychological health, social relationships, and environment. Domain scores are scaled in a positive direction (i.e., higher scores denote higher QOL), and the mean score of items within each domain is used to calculate the domain score. Mean scores are then multiplied by 4 in order to make domain scores comparable with the scores used in the WHOQOL-100, and subsequently transformed to a 0–100 scale. This scale is a well-validated patient-centred measure suited for use in the clinical and research settings [40–42]. The scale was also previously validated in Singapore [43, 44] and used amongst local psychiatric outpatients [45–47]. The internal consistency reliability for WHOQOL-BREF (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.95) was high in the current study.

Brief psychiatric rating scale (BPRS)

The 18-item BPRS [48] is a standard instrument commonly used to evaluate the severity of psychiatric symptoms in clinical settings [49]. The tool was administered by researchers who had achieved high levels of inter-rater reliability with an experienced rater after receiving training. In this current study the inter-rater agreement of BPRS among the study team members was 0.82. The BPRS includes five subscales: (a) affect – anxiety, guilt, depression, somatic, (b) positive symptoms – thought content, conceptual disorganization, hallucinatory behaviour, grandiosity, (c) negative symptoms – blunted affect, emotional withdrawal, motor retardation, (d) resistance – hostility, uncooperativeness, suspiciousness, and (e) activation – excitement, tension, mannerisms-posturing. Each item is rated on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (not present) to 7 (extremely severe) Items are summed to a total score that ranges from 18 to 126, with a higher score representing more severe symptomatology. The scale has been used in clinical studies to evaluate symptoms in patients with depression and schizophrenia or related psychosis in tertiary psychiatric settings in Singapore [50, 51]. The internal consistency reliability for BPRS-18 (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.77) was high in the current study.

Sociodemographic data including age, ethnicity, marital status, highest educational attainment, employment status, smoking and alcohol history, and clinical diagnosis, were obtained through a semi-structured questionnaire.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS, version 29.0. The socio-demographic and clinical information of the study sample, and the proportion of participants experiencing IPV was reported using descriptive statistics. Mean, standard deviations, and range of scores were calculated for continuous variables while frequencies and, percentages were calculated for categorical variables. The association between IPV variables and sociodemographic and clinical characteristics were examined using a multivariable logistic regression, with socio-demographic and clinical variables as independent variables and IPV variables (including VAW total, psychological, physical and sexual domains) as dependent variables. Multiple linear regressions were conducted to examine the association between IPV and quality of life (QOL), adjusting for socio-demographic factors, with IPV variables as the main predictors and QOL domains as dependent variables. Statistically significant differences were evaluated at p < 0.05, using two-sided tests for correlation analyses.

Discussion

The present study found that the overall proportion of women with mental illness experiencing intimate partner violence in Singapore was 50.2%, indicating that one in two women in this population had experienced some form of violence. This prevalence aligns with findings from Western countries, where rates of IPV among women with severe mental illness ranges from 40% to 60% [8, 18, 52]. Studies from the United Kingdom, reported that half of women with mental disorders experienced at least one form of violence in their lifetime, suggesting that mental illness substantially increases the risk compared to the general population [18]. Comparable findings have been reported in the United States and Europe, where women with psychotic or mood disorders are 2 to 3 times more likely to experience IPV compared to the general population [10, 53, 54]. Studies from Asian countries also report a high but variable prevalence of IPV among women with mental illness, though cultural norms, stigma, and underreporting may obscure true figures. For instance, research in India found that 42% of women with severe mental disorders had experienced physical or sexual violence [55], while studies in Hong Kong and Malaysia reported lifetime IPV exposure rates of 56% and 52% respectively, among women with psychiatric conditions [56, 57]. These findings suggest that IPV among women with mental illness in Singapore is consistent with global and regional estimates, underscoring its significance as a global public health issue. Multiple logistic regression analyses in this study identified several socio-demographic and clinical factors associated with IPV. The findings of this study revealed significant association between marital status, ethnicity, and clinical variables with experience of violence against women with mental illness. Participants who were married were significantly more likely to experience overall VAW and psychological violence compared to those who were single, highlighting the role of intimate relationships in exposure to violence [10, 52]. Women who were separated/divorced/widowed also had significantly higher odds of experiencing overall, psychological and physical violence. Evidence from Asian studies suggests that relationship dissolution is often preceded by severe abuse, meaning that women who leave relationships may have higher cumulative exposure to violence compared to women those who are single [58]. Mainly the violence, particularly psychological abuse and physical abuse, may continue after separation through ongoing contact related to caregiving responsibilities, financial arrangements, or shared social networks which is common in Asian family contexts [59, 60]. Furthermore, studies emphasize that mental health vulnerabilities-such as impaired coping, emotional dysregulation, or reduced capacity to mobilize protective strategies-can increase susceptibility to repeated victimization [59]. These findings highlight the need for targeted screening and support for women with mental illness during and after relationship transitions. Ethnic differences were also observed, with women of Indian ethnicity being significantly less likely to experience physical violence compared to those of Chinese ethnicity. This is consistent with previous findings suggesting that cultural norms, help-seeking behaviours, strong community ties, and collectivist coping practices among Indian women in Southeast Asia may offer protective effects against violence [60–63]. However, cultural stigma surrounding disclosure of violence may also contribute to underreporting within certain ethnic groups [64–66]. Such findings underscore the need for culturally sensitive screening tools and intervention frameworks that account for ethnic and cultural variations in reporting and experience of violence. Further qualitative work is needed to understand how cultural norms and stigma surrounding mental illness and family honour influence disclosure or violence among minority ethnic groups. From the clinical perspective, the study found that women with clinical depression were significantly more likely to experience sexual violence, consistent with evidence that depression increases vulnerability to victimization and may be both a cause and consequences of violence [7, 67]. This association aligns with prior studies showing that depressive symptoms- such as low self-esteem, guilt, and helplessness-can increase vulnerability to sexual coercion and victimization [17, 52]. The current study results showed that women presenting with positive psychotic symptoms-such as hallucinations or delusion had higher odds of experiencing overall, psychological and physical violence. However, some caution is warranted when interpreting self-reported IPV among individuals with schizophrenia disorders. Symptoms such as delusions, hallucinations, and impaired reality testing may influence the perception and reporting of events, raising the possibility of misinterpretation or misclassification of IPV experiences in some cases [28, 68]. For instance, reports of violence among women with schizophrenia could, in certain situations, be shaped by psychopathology-related distortions. Despite this concern, it is important not to dismiss these reports, as substantial evidence demonstrates that individuals with severe mental illness are at genuinely increased risk of experiencing violence, including IPV [7, 17, 52]. To address this complexity, future research should incorporate strategies to improve the validity of IPV measurement in populations with psychotic disorders. These may include clinician-administered assessments, triangulation with multiple informants where appropriate, and longitudinal designs to assess consistency of reporting over time. The present study also examined the impact of IPV on quality of life (QOL) among women with mental illness. The findings indicated that psychological violence was significantly associated with lower QOL in the social relationships and environmental domains, whereas physical violence was significantly associated with lower QOL in the physical health domain. These findings underscore the pervasive impact of IPV on multiple facets of life among women with psychiatric disorders, aligning with previous research demonstrating that exposure to violence adversely affects both physical and psychosocial well-being [4, 18]. Psychological violence often characterized by emotional manipulation, coercion, or threats, can disrupt social support networks and impair interpersonal relationships, which may explain the observed reduction in the social relationships’ domain of QOL [10, 17]. Similarly, the environmental QOL domain, encompassing factors such as safety, financial resources, and access to social service, may be negatively impacted due to fear, stigma, or restricted autonomy associated with abusive experiences [7, 13, 15]. Physical violence was linked with lower QOL in the physical domain, reflecting the direct somatic consequences of violence, including injuries, chronic pain, and functional impairment, which are well-documented in both Western and Asian contexts [15, 67, 69]. Collectively, these findings higlight the multidimensional burden of IPV on women with mental illness and emphasize the importance of integrating trauma-informed case and targeted psychosocial interventions to mitigate the negative effects of violence on quality of life.

Limitation

Several limitations should be noted. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences regarding the relationships between IPV, clinical characteristics and quality of life. Second, data were collected through self-report, which may be subject to recall bias and despite being reassured of confidentiality participants may have been reluctant to share their experiences of IPV, leading to an under-reporting of the prevalence. Third, the study sample was drawn from a tertiary care outpatient psychiatric setting in Singapore, and focusses on women with mental illness, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other clinical or community populations. Fourth, the study did not examine the severity, frequency, or timing of violence which could influence both risk factors and outcomes. Fifth, although multiple socio-demographic and clinical variables were examined, other potential confounders such as family support, or community-level factors were not examined and may contribute to the risk of IPV. Finally, the key limitation of this study is the lack of a qualitative component, such as in-depth interviews or focus groups discussions, which could have provided richer, more nuanced insights into the participants lived experiences of IPV. Future studies are therefore planned to incorporate a qualitative component. This mixed-methods approach will enable a more comprehensive understanding of IPV by capturing detailed personal narratives and contextual factors, ultimately contributing to more informed interventions, polices and support systems.

Conclusion

IPV is highly prevalent among women with mental illness and is associated with significant socio-demographic and clinical risk factors, including marital status, ethnicity, depression and positive psychotic symptoms. Exposure to violence also adversely affects multiple domains of quality of life. Taken together, the findings emphasize that IPV among women with mental illness in Singapore is comparable to both Western and Asian countries, highlighting the need for integrated mental health and violence-prevention services. These findings underscore the need for integrated, culturally sensitive, and trauma-informed interventions that combine mental health care with social and legal support. Early identification, routine screening for violence in psychiatric settings, and targeted support can help prevent violence, mitigate its negative consequences, and improve the overall well-being of women with mental illness.
