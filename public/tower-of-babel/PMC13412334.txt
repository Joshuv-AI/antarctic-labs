Tracking the big picture during wartime: A national trauma registry perspective
Authors: Teva Amir, Tomer Talmy, Irina Radomislensky, Adi Givon, Gal Khanchin, Inbal Dym, Danielle Akler, Harel Gershgoren, Pavel Eidelman, Arthur Shapiro, Avi Benov, Arnon Afek, Israel Trauma Group (ITG), Sami Gendler, Ramat Gan, Israel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Trauma registries are a core component of modern trauma systems for surveillance, quality improvement, and research. During armed conflicts, the need for timely, population-level data increases; however, the functioning of national trauma registries during active war has been sparsely described. This study describes the performance of Israel’s National Trauma Registry (INTR) during the 2023–2025 Swords of Iron War, and reports key wartime injury epidemiology alongside the central operational processes implemented to support both civilian and military health care systems.METHODS:We conducted a retrospective analysis of all hospitalized civilian and military casualties with war-associated injuries recorded in the INTR between October 7, 2023, and July 31, 2025. The primary analysis focused on descriptive epidemiology and clinical outcomes, while a secondary descriptive component addressed operational adaptations of the national trauma registry during the conflict.RESULTS:A total of 4,317 war-related trauma casualties were recorded, including 3,071 military personnel (71.1%) and 1,246 civilians (28.9%). Admissions peaked during the initial 48 to 72 hours of the war. Military casualties more frequently sustained penetrating injuries, with a higher proportion of critical injury (ISS ≥25) compared with civilians (10.8% vs. 5.8%). Serious thoracic, abdominal, facial, and upper-extremity injuries (Abbreviated Injury Scale≥3) were more common among military personnel; civilians exhibited a higher burden of lower-extremity trauma. More than half of all casualties underwent operative intervention, one-fifth of military casualties required intensive care, and overall in-hospital mortality remained low (~2%).CONCLUSIONS:INTR maintained continuous, high-quality surveillance of casualties throughout the Swords of Iron War. Through targeted operational and methodological adaptations, the registry enabled real-time characterization of evolving wartime injury patterns, system burden, and outcomes, while supporting clinical and policy decisions under extreme conditions. These findings highlight the critical role of national trauma registries for trauma system management during armed conflicts. (J Trauma Acute Care Surg. 2026;101: S122–S130. Traumatic injuries represent a major global cause of death and long-term disabilities and impose a significant and sustained burden on health systems, workforce productivity, and broader societal functioning. In high-income countries, the annual incidence of traumatic injury remains substantial, with ~1,000 to 2,000 cases per 100,000 population reported each year,1 making it the leading cause of death in the working population.2

Trauma registries are structured, systematic information systems designed to collect, organize, and analyze data on patients sustaining traumatic injuries. As an essential component of modern trauma systems,3 they provide the information infrastructure needed to monitor injury trends, evaluate policy decisions, improve quality of care, monitor clinical outcomes, and support rigorous epidemiological research.4–7

Armed conflicts generate complex patterns of injury that challenge trauma systems at every level of care. In such settings, timely access to reliable data becomes essential. However, very little is known about how a national registry functions during an active war, when the demand for rapid, high-fidelity information abruptly increases, routine data collection processes are disrupted by security, workload, and resource constraints, and system-wide adaptability becomes critical. Israel’s National Trauma Registry (INTR) during the 2023–2025 conflict provides a unique opportunity to address this gap.8

Understanding both the epidemiology of wartime injuries and the operational performance of the registry itself is crucial for improving preparedness and optimizing the medical response in future mass-casualty or conflict scenarios.

This study, therefore, aims to (1) characterize the epidemiological and clinical features of casualties recorded in the INTR during the war; and (2) describe the unique adaptations and real-time contributions of the registry to national policy, resource allocation, and trauma system management during the conflict.

METHODS

Study Population

This retrospective study utilized data from the INTR.9 The study population included all military and civilian casualties with war-related injuries documented in the registry during the Swords of Iron War (October 7, 2023, to July 31, 2025). This conflict began with a large-scale attack against Israel and evolved into a prolonged multifront war involving the Israel Defense Forces (IDF) and several armed organizations and state actors, including Hamas in Gaza, Hezbollah in Lebanon, and Iran-aligned groups operating from Yemen, Syria, and Iraq. This time frame encompassed the entire spectrum of available wartime data at the time of writing, including the unprecedented, high-intensity mass-casualty events of October 7, 2023, and subsequent phases of the conflict.10 Casualties injured within Israeli territory and across broader theaters of conflict were included. Noncombat injuries, including road traffic accidents, falls, and trauma related to criminal activity or interpersonal violence, were excluded unless they occurred in a war-related context, such as instances of running to bomb shelters. For patients who underwent interhospital transfer, registry data were abstracted exclusively from the final receiving hospital.

For temporal comparisons of system-level injury burden, a separate ancillary analysis was performed using the full INTR population, including both war-related and non–war-related trauma admissions. Under routine registry criteria, trauma-related hospitalizations are defined as admissions occurring within 72 hours of injury. During the war, this interval was temporarily extended to 30 days to improve the capture of delayed hospitalizations associated with operational conditions and interhospital transfers. A prewar baseline period was defined as January 2021 through September 2023, representing routine trauma system activity outside the realms of a high-intensity conflict.

This study is reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement (SDC 1, Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/TA/F487) and was approved by the Sheba Medical Center Institutional Review Board (SMC 5138-18).

Israel National Trauma Registry

The INTR is managed by the National Center for Trauma and Emergency Medicine Research in the Gertner Institute for Epidemiology and Public Health Policy Research at the Sheba Medical Center. The Registry collects and aggregates data from all seven Level I trauma centers and 17 Level II centers in Israel, covering 24 of the country’s 26 designated trauma centers.9

Data are entered into the system retrospectively by trauma registrars at each hospital. The registry captures all trauma casualties admitted to the hospital or transferred to another facility following initial evaluation and management. It does not include casualties who die at the scene, during transport, those discharged directly from the ED or injuries resulting from poisoning, drowning, or suffocation. Patient-level data are completed by registrars after the patient has completed the full course of hospitalization.

Logical tests are built in the data entry platform to assure data quality. Electronic files are transferred weekly to the institute, where additional quality-assurance procedures are performed before data analysis. Periodically, logical consistency audits are generated and sent back to each hospital.

Variables

Demographic, emergency department, and hospital data extracted from the INTR included patient demographics, mechanism of injury, injured body regions, vital signs, and Glasgow Coma Scale score upon hospital arrival, Injury Severity Score (ISS), and Abbreviated Injury Scale (AIS) by anatomic region.11 Serious injury was defined as an AIS score of ≥3 in any body region. The ISS was categorized into four severity groups: 1 to 8, 9 to 14, 16 to 24, and ≥25.10 Emergency department procedures and operations performed during hospitalization were also assessed.

Morbidity outcomes were assessed based on discharge destination, specifically transfer to rehabilitation facilities following hospitalization.

For analyses of the system-level burden, a monthly caseload variable was constructed. Severe injury burden was defined as the monthly number of entries with an ISS ≥16.

Statistical Analysis

Categorical variables were analyzed using the χ² test or Fisher exact test, as appropriate, while continuous variables were analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U test. Adjustment for multiple testing across the four ISS categories was applied using the Bonferroni correction (α = 0.05/4 = 0.0125). For all analyses, a p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, unless otherwise specified. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS software, version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC).

RESULTS

Between October 2023 and July 2025, a total of 4,317 hospitalized military and civilian casualties related to combat and war-associated incidents were recorded. Monthly registry entries peaked sharply in October 2023, with 907 casualties recorded, including 465 military personnel and 442 civilians (Fig. 1). This initial peak was followed by persistently high entry volumes in November and December 2023, predominantly comprising military casualties. Throughout 2024, monthly registry entries fluctuated substantially. A second peak was observed in October 2024, when 466 entries were recorded. During 2025, monthly entry volumes were generally lower. A third peak occurred in June 2025, during which civilian casualties outnumbered military personnel (258 vs. 50).

Penetrating trauma was substantially more common among military casualties than civilians (77.3% vs. 35.9%, p<0.001). Explosive mechanisms were the dominant cause of injury among soldiers (63.8%). In contrast, civilians experienced a more heterogeneous pattern of injury mechanisms. Aeromedical evacuation was used far more frequently for military casualties than civilians (55.1% vs. 4.8%, p<0.001).

Critically injured casualties (ISS ≥25) were significantly more common among military personnel compared with civilians (10.8% vs. 5.8%; absolute difference +5.0%, p < 0.001).

Physiological Status on Emergency Department Arrival

Civilians more frequently presented with hypotension (systolic blood pressure <90 mm Hg; 8.9% vs. 5.0%, p<0.001). Military casualties more commonly exhibited tachycardia (pulse ≥130 bpm; 4.2% vs. 3.6%, p=0.019), hypoxemia (oxygen saturation <90%; 2.4% vs. 1.6%, p<0.001), and severe impairment of consciousness (Glasgow Coma Scale 3–8; 4.8% vs. 3.5%, p=0.011).

Emergency Department Care and Operative Management

Military casualties were significantly more likely to be admitted via the trauma bay than civilians (66.1% vs. 26.2%, p<0.001). Advanced emergency department interventions were more frequently required among military casualties (all p≤0.034).

Overall rates of operative management did not differ between groups (53.1% in civilians vs. 52.2% in military personnel, p=0.63). However, the distribution of surgical procedures by domain differed significantly. Overall, the pattern of surgical interventions corresponded to the anatomic distribution of severe injuries observed in each group, with higher rates of torso-related procedures among military casualties and a predominance of musculoskeletal procedures among civilians.

Clinical In-hospital Outcomes

Clinical outcomes are presented in Table 2. Military casualties were significantly more likely to require intensive care unit (ICU) admission than civilians (20.6% vs. 11.0%, p<0.001). Among patients admitted to the ICU, civilians had a longer median ICU length of stay [4 d (IQR: 1–13) vs. 3 d (IQR: 1–9), p=0.012].

DISCUSSION

This study presents a national registry–based analysis of hospitalized trauma casualties during the Swords of Iron War, the longest and one of the most casualty-intensive conflicts in Israel’s history. Over a 22-month period, the INTR documented more than 4,300 war-related hospitalizations, reflecting a sustained and system-wide trauma burden.

In historical perspective, the cumulative number of hospitalized casualties during the current conflict exceeds that reported in most prior major Israeli wars and military operations, including the Six-Day War (1967; ~2,500 hospitalized casualties),12 the First Lebanon War (1982; 1,950 injured soldiers reported during the early phase of the campaign),13 the Second Lebanon War (2006; 845 hospitalized soldiers),14 and Operation Protective Edge (2014; 730 hospitalized casualties, including both soldiers and civilians).14,15 In the context of the growing emphasis on large-scale combat operations,16 this conflict—representing one of the largest sustained wartime trauma burdens captured within a modern, integrated trauma system in the past decade—provides a unique real-world opportunity to inform preparedness, system design, and training requirements for future high-intensity conflicts.

Temporal fluctuations in registry entries closely mirrored distinct operational phases of the conflict. The initial surge reflected a short-duration, high-intensity mass-casualty event, resulting in an unprecedented volume of hospitalized casualties during the opening phase of the war.10 This was followed by a prolonged period of sustained ground maneuver operations, during which registry volumes increased again and reached levels comparable to earlier high-intensity periods. A subsequent, discrete escalation involving long-range strike capability against the civilian home front was associated with a renewed increase in civilian hospitalizations. Together, these patterns illustrate how changes in operational focus, intensity, and theater were rapidly and consistently reflected in national trauma registry activity.

The INTR and Operational Adaptations During the War

Under routine conditions, national trauma registries function primarily as retrospective surveillance and quality-improvement tools.17 During the Swords of Iron War, however, the INTR assumed an expanded role, providing near real-time, population-level visibility of hospitalized war-related trauma involving both soldiers and civilians, thereby enabling health care leaders and national decision-makers to monitor evolving injury patterns and system burden and to adapt clinical and operational responses as the conflict progressed.

This shift was enabled by key operational adaptations: sustained data collection under wartime conditions, integration of medical students to support data entry in high-volume centers, and prioritization of rapid documentation of war-related cases.

Conceptual adaptations were embedded within the coding structure, including dual coding of casualties injured while falling en route to protected spaces, reflecting both the war-related context and the immediate injury mechanism. These cases were classified under “other mechanisms” and accounted for nearly one-quarter of all registry entries (n = 1,052; 24.4%), while constituting the majority of civilian injuries. Among civilian casualties, this category largely captured injuries that occurred within the broader context of wartime disruption, including events such as falls while running to bomb shelters, road traffic accidents during periods of heightened alert, and other blunt trauma associated with civilian behavioral responses to ongoing hostilities. Inclusion criteria were broadened by extending the postinjury hospitalization window to 1 month, and registry policy was modified to include repeat emergency department visits, acknowledging altered care pathways and early discharge practices during mass-casualty periods. Registry leadership adopted a forward-looking approach, incorporating new variables during the course of the conflict in anticipation of their relevance for future research, in response to emerging changes in injury characteristics and patterns of care. These included, for example, calcium levels, which became particularly relevant following the broad introduction of prehospital whole blood transfusion in the military setting.

A distinguishing feature of the registry is its cross-referencing with the IDF Trauma Registry (IDF-TR), which captures military prehospital and point-of-injury data, thereby enabling linkage between prehospital military data and in-hospital outcomes while preserving the independence of the two databases.18,19 Data verification was intensified, with cross-referencing with the IDF-TR shifted from an annual to a monthly process.

Collectively, these adaptations transformed the registry from a predominantly retrospective surveillance tool into an active platform supporting operational decision-making during wartime.

Early Monitoring of Trauma System Performance

Registry-based analyses conducted during the first months of the war already demonstrated that the conflict was characterized by a higher burden of severe and critical injury, with the proportion of casualties with ISS ≥16 increasing from 13.7% in 2014 to 18.6% in 2023.15 Despite this increase in injury severity, contemporaneous assessments of combat casualty outcomes reported a lower overall case fatality rate, ~6.5% to 7%, compared with ~9% in prior conflicts,20 suggesting preservation—and potentially improvement—of trauma system performance under substantially more demanding conditions. Howard and colleagues demonstrated that combat casualty survival is typically assessed retrospectively, years after conflict, once datasets mature. In their analysis, survival among critically injured casualties (ISS 25–75) increased substantially over the course of the conflicts, from 2.2% to 39.9% in Afghanistan and from 8.9% to 32.9% in Iraq.21 Peleg et al.22 demonstrated a consistent decline in in-hospital mortality among severely and critically injured patients following the implementation of a comprehensive trauma system, after adjustment for patient characteristics and injury severity, supporting the concept that continuous surveillance and feedback are integral components of effective trauma system performance.

Leveraging Data to Guide Policy in Wartime

Identification of Unique Injury Patterns

Among hospitalized military casualties, the present analysis demonstrates a distinct anatomic injury profile characterized by a high proportion of penetrating and blast-related trauma. As early as December 2024, registry-based analyses comparing the initial phase of the Swords of Iron War with Operation Protective Edge (2014) identified that ~78% of hospitalized combat casualties sustained penetrating injuries in both conflicts, while firearm-related injuries were more frequent in 2023 (19.6% vs. 14.5%).15 Increased rates of torso and upper-extremity injuries, traumatic amputations, exploratory laparotomy, and vascular surgery were also observed. Notably, these early findings closely mirror the patterns identified in the current comprehensive cohort.

These injury characteristics align with data from US and UK military trauma registries during the Iraq and Afghanistan conflicts. In analyses from the UK Joint Theatre Trauma Registry, explosive mechanisms predominated, reaching ~80% at peak periods, and extremities constituted the most commonly injured body region.23 Registry-based US data further demonstrate that among hospitalized combat casualties, serious injuries (AIS ≥3) most commonly involved the extremities (20.9%), followed by the head and neck (11.2%), chest (9.7%), and abdomen (6.0%).24 The relative anatomic distribution observed in the present cohort is broadly comparable.

Civilian casualties exhibited a distinct anatomic injury profile, characterized by a disproportionately high burden of serious extremity trauma. In the present cohort, serious lower-extremity injuries (AIS ≥3) occurred in 26.5% of civilians, compared with 14.3% among military casualties, and were accompanied by a higher requirement for musculoskeletal surgical intervention (39.6% vs. 25.0%, respectively). This extremity-dominant civilian pattern is directionally consistent with a recent large systematic review of civilian injuries in armed conflicts spanning 1973–2023, which reported that ~50% of civilian war–related injuries, of any severity, involved the extremities, compared with 26% involving the head and 18% involving the chest.25

The high proportion of penetrating and blast-related torso injuries among military casualties highlights the continued importance of training general surgeons in damage-control surgery, vascular repair, and management of complex thoracoabdominal trauma. Similarly, the substantial burden of extremity injuries, particularly among civilian casualties, emphasizes the importance of orthopedic and limb-salvage capabilities during prolonged conflicts involving civilian populations.

Although the injury mechanisms and demographic profile observed in the current conflict, particularly the substantial involvement of civilians, may resemble those reported from the Russia-Ukraine war, meaningful quantitative comparison is limited by the absence of a formal trauma registry in Ukraine. Scoping reviews of the medical literature from the war in Ukraine further underscore these limitations, noting the absence of registry-based datasets, the inability to quantify injury severity, outcomes, or potentially preventable mortality, and notably the complete lack of published studies reporting data on Russian casualties.26–28

Revision of Clinical Practice Guidelines

In the current cohort of 3,071 hospitalized military casualties, a substantial injury burden was observed: 663 patients (21.6%) sustained severe or critical injuries (ISS ≥16), including 329 critically injured casualties (10.8%, ISS ≥25). Injury mechanisms were dominated by high-energy trauma, with 1,959 casualties (63.8%) injured by explosions and an additional 788 (25.7%) sustaining firearm-related injuries, such that nearly 90% of military casualties were exposed to high-energy mechanisms. Together, these findings underscore the operational challenge of accurate early triage in a setting characterized by mass-casualty incidents and predominantly high-energy injury patterns. During the conflict, validation of the prehospital triage tool was performed in over 2,000 military casualties and demonstrated high sensitivity (93.9%) and a low undertriage rate (3.8%). Review of the 18 undertriaged cases showed a disproportionate contribution of traumatic brain injury—often without overt physiological compromise—and selected fragment-related injuries with subtle early presentation. Early identification of these patterns allowed targeted feedback and focused training for frontline providers, particularly regarding occult head injury and blast mechanisms, underscoring the registry’s role as a real-time learning system during active conflict.[manuscript under review]

Among military casualties in the INTR cohort analyzed in this study, severe thoracic injury (AIS ≥3) was documented in 386/3,071 patients (12.6%). In-hospital emergency department management reflected this burden, with chest tube insertion performed in 110/3,071 cases (3.6%). Talmy et al.29 reported in a registry-based analysis of prehospital chest decompression procedures derived from the IDF-TR (2010–2023) that among 224 needle thoracostomy and chest decompression interventions, objective resolution of clinical signs consistent with tension pneumothorax was documented in only 3 cases (1.3%). A focused IDF mortality review of 49 selected military fatalities found only one case in which tension pneumothorax was considered a possible contributor to death.[manuscript under review] Integration of these findings and the current literature was performed by a national expert committee (February 2024), which subsequently revised the local Combat Casualty Care Clinical Practice Guidelines, most notably recommending cessation of routine needle thoracostomy use and substantial narrowing of indications for chest tube insertion, with clearly defined physiological criteria.

Data-driven Evacuation and Resource Allocation

As shown in Figure 4, the opening month of the war was marked by an extreme and abrupt hospital surge, with 907 hospitalized casualties and an exceptionally high interhospital transfer rate (~33.5%), reflecting early system strain and the need for rapid load redistribution. In the months that followed, the absolute number of transfers declined and stabilized, even during periods of high admission volume (eg, December 2023: 581 admissions, 51 transfers), suggesting system adaptation rather than persistent insufficiency. The distribution of admissions between Level I and regional trauma centers further reflects context-driven evacuation policy. In October 2024 and June 2025, a relatively higher proportion of casualties were hospitalized in regional centers (October 2024: 149/466; June 2025: 167/308), corresponding to periods of substantial injury burden in civilian population centers, during which evacuation patterns favored geographically proximal facilities and differed from military-dominant phases.

However, despite the high demand for surgical interventions, Epstein et al.30 demonstrated that during wartime surge conditions, the arrival of 1 to 6 casualties per hour to Level I trauma centers was not associated with increased mortality, ICU utilization, or delays to operative intervention, underscoring the capacity of a well-prepared trauma system to absorb substantial surges without compromising outcomes. Collectively, these findings illustrate how timely, registry-linked data can guide evacuation policy, resource allocation, and hospital designation decisions in real time during war.

Limitations

This study is based on retrospective analysis of registry data, which inherently limits causal inference. Although the INTR, as a registry, is prospectively populated using standardized definitions and rigorous quality assurance procedures, the data were not collected to address the specific research questions of this study. Accordingly, residual confounding by unmeasured factors such as tactical circumstances at the point of injury, use of personal protective equipment, or prehospital clinical decision-making cannot be excluded. The INTR captures only hospitalized casualties. Consequently, two ends of the injury severity spectrum are not represented: patients with minor injuries discharged directly from the emergency department, and casualties who died at the scene or during evacuation before hospital arrival. The findings, therefore, reflect the epidemiology and outcomes of hospitalized war-related trauma rather than the full burden of conflict-related injury, and in-hospital mortality likely underestimates overall lethality. Finally, the results reflect the experience of a single national trauma system operating within a unique civilian-military integrated framework, which may limit generalizability to other health care systems or conflict settings.

CONCLUSIONS

Israel’s National Trauma Registry maintained continuous, high-quality surveillance of casualties throughout the Swords of Iron War. Through targeted operational and methodological adaptations, the registry enabled real-time characterization of evolving wartime injury patterns, system burden, and outcomes, while supporting clinical and policy decision-making under extreme conditions. These findings highlight the critical role of national trauma registries as adaptive, data-driven tools for trauma system management, with important implications for preparedness and resilience in future modern armed conflicts.
