Caregivers’ perspectives on family meetings and preparedness for imminent death: A qualitative descriptive study in a palliative care setting
Authors: Nadia A. Shahda, Mohammed F. Abosoudah, Maha A. Aldosari, Abdulhadi M. Alqahtani, Mustafa Zakkar, Ibrahim Antoun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Family caregivers often feel unprepared for the final phase of life, which is associated with distress and adverse bereavement outcomes. Structured family meetings are recommended, yet evidence from Middle-Eastern settings is limited.Aim:To explore how routine family meetings influence caregiver preparedness and communication when patient is judged to be approaching death in Saudi palliative care service.Design:Qualitative descriptive study using semi-structured interviews. Data were analysed using grounded theory analytic techniques.Setting/participants:A tertiary palliative care unit in Saudi Arabia. Seven family caregivers of imminently dying patients with cancer were interviewed in Arabic within 2–5 days after attending a protocol-mandated family meeting.Results:Six themes were identified to describe the meetings’ impact. Psychological relief: clear prognostic information reduced uncertainty and anxiety. Emotional burden: anticipatory grief was substantial despite valuing honesty. Communication style: direct, compassionate disclosure and stepwise foreshadowing aided understanding and acceptance. Religious framing: alignment with Islamic beliefs provided meaning and guided spiritually oriented bedside care. Caregiver support, including multiple relatives who distributed the emotional load, improved intra-family communication. Practical care guidance: specific advice on recognising signs of dying and interacting with unresponsive patients increased caregiving confidence and reduced crises.Conclusions:Caregivers in a middle eastern setting found routine, empathetic, and culturally sensitive family meetings helpful in supporting informational, emotional, spiritual, and practical needs during end-of-life. They valued clear communication, culturally sensitive religious framing, and practical guidance about dying. These findings suggest that structured family meetings can support caregivers and highlight areas for further research and development in family-centred end-of-life care.

Introduction

Caring for a family member with advanced cancer is emotionally and physically demanding, and family caregivers frequently experience high levels of stress, anxiety, and depression in the final phase of illness. 1 A consistent predictor of this distress is a lack of preparedness for the patient’s death. Caregivers who feel unprepared are at increased risk of complicated grief and poor psychological outcomes after bereavement. At the same time, those who are adequately informed and supported are more likely to cope adaptively. 2 Family meetings are a widely used communication strategy in palliative care and are recommended as part of family-centred care for patients with serious illness. 3 In general, a family meeting is a structured conversation among healthcare professionals, patients when possible, and family members to discuss the patient’s condition, goals of care, treatment options, and expectations for the future. These meetings can serve multiple functions, including facilitating shared decision making, clarifying medical information, addressing emotional and spiritual concerns, supporting family coping, and coordinating care plans. 4

Communication at the end of life is therefore recognised as a cornerstone of palliative care. 5 Structured family meetings have been developed as a systematic way to address these gaps. In such meetings, members of the healthcare team meet with relatives to discuss the patient’s condition, anticipated trajectory, and care plans. Studies in Europe, North America, and East Asia have shown that family meetings can reduce caregiver anxiety, facilitate shared decision-making, and improve satisfaction with care.6,7

In Saudi Arabia and other Middle Eastern countries, end-of-life communication and caregiving experiences are strongly shaped by cultural and religious norms. 8 End-of-life communication and caregiving experiences are also shaped by cultural and religious values. In many Middle Eastern and Islamic societies, family involvement in healthcare decision-making is central, and discussions about serious illness and death often occur within a collective family framework. Religious beliefs frequently influence how illness, suffering, and death are interpreted, with many families framing end-of-life events within the context of divine will and spiritual meaning. These cultural and religious factors may shape both how clinicians communicate information and how families understand and act on it. Understanding this context is therefore important when examining the role of family meetings and caregiver preparedness in palliative care settings. Despite these cultural considerations, little empirical research has examined how family meetings function in the Middle Eastern context. To date, most evidence on their impact comes from Western or East Asian healthcare systems, which may differ in family structures, expectations, and spiritual frameworks. Understanding how caregivers experience family meetings in this context is important for informing culturally sensitive palliative care practice.8,9 This qualitative study explores how structured family meetings affect the readiness of family caregivers of imminently dying cancer patients in a Saudi palliative care unit. We focus on the psychological, emotional, spiritual, and practical dimensions of preparedness, as well as communication practices that shape caregivers’ experiences. The findings aim to inform best practices for implementing family meetings in culturally diverse palliative care settings.

Methods

Design

We used a qualitative descriptive design to explore caregivers’ experiences and perceptions of structured family meetings. Data were collected through semi-structured interviews and analysed using grounded theory analytic techniques as described by Strauss and Corbin. Two researchers independently conducted open coding to identify initial concepts, followed by axial coding to examine relationships between codes using constant comparison. Selective coding was then used to integrate categories and develop overarching themes describing caregiver preparedness and communication experiences.

Setting

The study was conducted in the palliative care unit of a tertiary care hospital. The service provides interdisciplinary care for patients with advanced cancer and routinely conducts structured family meetings when patients are assessed as approaching the terminal phase. When the interdisciplinary team identifies clinical indicators of imminent death, the Imminent Death Protocol is activated. The Imminent Death Protocol stipulates that a structured family meeting be conducted as standard practice, ideally within 24 h of activation. In this setting, family meetings are typically led by a palliative medicine physician, reflecting common communication practices in this clinical environment. Other members of the interdisciplinary team, including nurses and, when appropriate, social workers or spiritual care providers, may also participate to address psychosocial and spiritual concerns.

The meeting aims to provide clear information about the patient’s clinical condition and expected trajectory; review goals of care and resuscitation status; offer anticipatory guidance on common signs of dying; discuss symptom management plans; and provide guidance on bedside communication, visiting, and available spiritual support.

Population

The study population consisted of family caregivers of patients with advanced cancer who had been assessed by the clinical team as approaching the end of life.

Inclusion criteria: caregivers aged 18 years or older attendance at a structured family meeting regarding the patient’s end-of-life care and the ability to participate in an interview in Arabic. All participants identified as Muslim. All Caregivers were excluded if they were unable to provide informed consent or if the clinical team deemed participation inappropriate due to severe emotional distress.

Sample

A purposive sampling approach was used to identify participants who had recently attended a family meeting. Recruitment and analysis proceeded iteratively. Interviews were analysed as they were completed, and subsequent participants were selected to increase variation in patient characteristics and age. The study aimed for thematic saturation, defined as the point at which no new codes or conceptual insights emerged from additional interviews. After seven interviews, the research team determined that thematic saturation had been reached.

Recruitment

Potential participants were identified by members of the palliative care team after a family meeting. Caregivers were informed about the study and asked whether they were willing to speak with the research team. Those who expressed interest received verbal and written information about the study. Participants who agreed to take part provided written informed consent prior to the interview.

Data collection

Data were collected through semi-structured interviews conducted within 1–2 days after the family meeting. This timing ensured that experiences were recent while allowing participants a short period for initial emotional processing.

Interviews were conducted in Arabic by two bilingual researchers experienced in qualitative interviewing who were not involved in the patients’ clinical care. Both interviewers had prior experience conducting research interviews in sensitive healthcare contexts. The transcripts were analysed in Arabic to preserve linguistic nuance. Quotations included in the manuscript were translated into English by the bilingual research team and cross-checked against the original Arabic transcripts by another bilingual researcher to ensure accuracy and preservation of meaning. Using two interviewers helped create a supportive environment and allowed for additional probing and clarification during discussions. An interview guide with open-ended questions and follow-up prompts was used to explore participants’ perceptions of the meeting, emotional responses, communication practices within the family, and preparedness for the patient’s final phase of life. Only one caregiver per patient was interviewed for this study. When multiple relatives attended the family meeting, the caregiver most closely involved in the patient’s care or decision-making was invited to participate. All interviews were conducted individually rather than as group interviews to allow participants to speak freely about their experiences.

Interviews lasted approximately 30–45 min and were audio recorded with permission. Field notes were also taken to capture contextual observations such as emotional responses or emphasis placed on particular topics. Recordings were transcribed verbatim in Arabic. The transcripts were analysed in Arabic to preserve linguistic nuance, and translated extracts used in the manuscript were cross-checked against the original transcripts to ensure accuracy.

Data analysis

We used grounded theory techniques, as described by Corbin and Strauss, to analyse caregivers’ perceptions of how family meetings related to their preparedness for the patient’s final phase of life. 10 Our process included open, axial, and selective coding with constant comparison. Codes were grouped into broader conceptual categories relating to psychological, emotional, communicative, religious, social, and practical aspects of preparedness.

During selective coding, these categories were integrated into overarching themes that describe caregivers’ experiences and the mechanisms by which family meetings influenced preparedness near the end of life. We adhered to COREQ guidelines in reporting the research. 11

Ethical issues

The study was approved by the hospital’s IRB (No. 21-191, 06/05/2021). All participants gave informed consent, including permission for audio recording and anonymous quotes.

Theme 1: Psychological relief and gaining clarity

Nearly all caregivers experienced psychological relief after the family meeting, describing feelings of calm or mental peace from understanding the situation and what to expect. Before, many felt anxious and uninformed about ‘imminent death’. The meeting clarified the patient’s condition, dispelling uncertainty. A daughter said it lifted a weight by answering her questions about her mother’s condition and signs of nearing end, helping her feel more at ease and prepared. Despite sadness, she felt psychologically relieved, understanding everything the doctor explained. A granddaughter shared that knowing her grandmother was likely approaching death brought grim certainty but also readiness, allowing her to plan and pray, replacing fear with awareness of specific signs. Caregivers found the meeting ‘important’ and ‘useful’, feeling more in control and less helpless because they now knew what to expect. The meeting reduced the stress of uncertainty; one said it provided relief to understand her father’s state and what was coming. Honest information in a supportive setting helped alleviate anxiety, often worse than knowing the truth. Many valued having their questions answered, gaining reassurance from direct communication with the doctor. One son noted that knowing everything made them feel calmer and assured that they did all they could, with remaining concerns in God’s hands. Psychological relief wasn’t happiness, caregivers still felt sadness, but it reduced anxiety and fear. Many expressed relief at no longer being in the dark and feeling more mentally prepared. Overall, well-conducted family meetings offer comfort through knowledge, even in emotional situations, helping caregivers feel more equipped to face impending loss.

Theme 2: Emotional burden and distress

The theme of emotional burden reflects the intense sorrow, shock, or stress caregivers face when confronting their family member’s imminent death. Almost all participants, even those relieved by the truth, experienced emotional turmoil from the discussion. This duality shows information can reduce uncertainty but also make loss more real, triggering grief. Some caregivers saw prognosis news as a heavy blow; a granddaughter recounted her outward calm during a virtual meeting, but inwardly she was anguished. Visiting her grandmother afterwards, she was overwhelmed by her deterioration, hyperventilating and feeling shock-induced heart palpitations. This underscores that no prep fully shields caregivers from pain at the end of life. Another daughter accepted the news intellectually, but emotionally, it was hard, she described heartbreak and sleepless nights, knowing her mother’s limited time. This shows knowledge spurs anticipatory grief, starting early but adding emotional burden. Caregivers also faced stress as they delivered bad news to others, felt emotionally drained, and struggled with supporting family members. None regretted knowing the truth; the pain stemmed from the content, not the communication. They valued honesty despite the emotional pain and felt it helped them accept the situation.

Theme 3: Communication style and honesty of the discussion

The physician and healthcare team’s communication during family meetings was vital, emphasising honesty, clarity, empathy, and the delivery of bad news. Participants valued direct, compassionate communication, which greatly influenced their ability to understand and cope.

Caregivers described doctors as “very clear and respectful." One daughter praised the doctor for being straightforward and kind, noting his honest but gentle approach. She also said his words and demeanour were appropriate and not upsetting. The physician used compassionate, honest language, balancing empathy with full disclosure. For example, instead of bluntly saying ‘your mother is dying’, they might say ‘signs suggest she’s nearing end, though only God knows when’ – being truthful but gentle. Caregivers appreciated this empathy, which softened the impact.

One granddaughter emphasised the importance of honesty, stating she wanted full details and preferred communicating directly with doctors to avoid surprises. She believed transparency helped her family prepare, unlike others who might prefer less detail out of hope or denial. This shows differing preferences for information within families, but she valued honesty and transparency. Many participants appreciated that the physician did not withhold the truth about the prognosis. One interview question asked if being told ‘the end is near’ was helpful. A participant said, ‘I wanted to know that. I didn’t hesitate to attend because I wanted to know everything. . . We should know this to be prepared and not be shocked. Even if I know, I can’t stop it; it’s God’s decree. But knowing helps us face it’. This shows that truthful, appropriate communication can be empowering. Caregivers didn’t see discussing death as giving up or insensitive; they found it helpful.

Another aspect of communication was gradual preparation. A daughter, who had multiple interactions with the palliative team, noted that before the family meeting, the team provided preliminary signals about her mother’s decline. She said the palliative care doctor had discussed similar cases where treatment was no longer effective, and the oncologist had explained the cancer was spreading and untreatable. By the time of the formal meeting, nothing was a surprise, as it was all foreshadowed. She believed stepwise disclosure was crucial: preparing us over time prevented shock if told suddenly her mother was near death. Because they were honest along the way, the meeting was more of a confirmation and discussion. One caregiver said the doctor mentioned, ‘the knowledge of exact timing is with God, but we see signs that suggest it may be soon’, acknowledging religious beliefs while giving a prognosis. This was respectful. Caregivers appreciated that the team encouraged questions and answered thoroughly. Satisfaction was high, reflecting structured meetings covering key topics, unlike rushed hallway chats. The Communication Style theme emphasises that honest, clear, and empathetic communication is vital for effective family meetings. Caregivers’ positive responses were linked to doctors’ truthfulness, fostering dialogue, and respect for cultural sensitivities.

Theme 4: Religious framing and faith-based coping

The ‘Religious Framing’ theme highlights how religious beliefs influence family meetings and caregiver interpretations, especially in Saudi Arabia’s Islamic context. Both healthcare providers and families often invoked faith, framing discussions around God’s will, prayers, and religious duties. Participants frequently used phrases like ‘Alhamdulillah’ and ‘This is God’s will’, reflecting spiritual acceptance that helped them cope with bad news. For example, a daughter said her family accepted God’s plan, saying, ‘We are satisfied with what God has ordained’. Despite sadness, she repeatedly praised God, showing reliance on faith to process the prognosis as part of a divine plan.

Family physicians incorporated religious elements appropriately during meetings. One granddaughter recalled a doctor saying, ‘Ultimately, the timing is in God’s hands, but from a medical perspective, we believe the time is near’. She appreciated this acknowledgement of God’s control, which aligned with her beliefs. Another caregiver mentioned that after explaining medical facts, the doctor encouraged the family to ‘be with the patient, pray, and remind her of God’. This emphasises practical religious practices for the dying.

Several caregivers engaged in religious actions after the meeting. For example, a daughter said, ‘The doctor told me she can hear and feel, so we should speak good words, remind her of God, and recite the Quran and prayers’. The caregiver followed this advice: ‘I drew closer, talked to her, reminded her of God, recited the Shahada in her ear, and read the Quran and verses of peace’. This illustrates how religious framing was associated with concrete actions that provided spiritual care and helped her mother’s comfort and dignity.

Another participant noted that the meeting reinforced their family’s belief that her death was God’s will, based on signs and religious faith.

Religious framing provided comfort, with some viewing the end as natural and up to God, easing guilt and anger. However, tension existed between hope and acceptance, with some families reluctant to hear the full truth to maintain hope for miracles. One granddaughter balanced hope and realism, recognising God’s power but accepting medical facts. The family and healthcare team navigated this delicate balance, with the team emphasising that outcomes are ultimately God’s will, which helped them accept the likely death while holding onto faith.

Theme 5: Caregiver support and family involvement

The Caregiver Support theme highlights how family meetings impact support dynamics for the primary caregiver and the wider family in end-of-life care. Participants reflected on who attended, how information was shared, and how families rallied (or could have). It was clear that involving more family members spreads emotional burden and boosts preparedness, while only one person attending can be overwhelming. Family meetings often increased communication and shared caregiving tasks. Several caregivers attended alone due to restrictions or choice. One daughter said, ‘I was the one who attended because I’m closest to my mom’, but felt it would be better if more family members were present to share responsibility and support the primary caregiver. Others agreed that hearing the news together ensures everyone is on the same page. During COVID, a granddaughter attended alone and carefully informed her family afterwards, describing her approach as calm and gradual. She acknowledged that hearing the prognosis was painful but valued honesty, though she wished someone had supported her emotionally, feeling the burden was lonely but necessary for accuracy. Most participants favoured shared attendance, believing it offers benefits like shared understanding, faster decision-making, emotional solidarity, and support for the primary caregiver. One said, ‘Honestly, when the whole family gathers with the doctor and knows the situation, I feel that’s best’.

Shared understanding ensures everyone hears the same message directly from the physician, reducing miscommunication or denial.

Collective decision-making helps reach consensus quickly, especially for choices such as a do-not-resuscitate (DNR) order or hospice care. Hearing news together allows family members to comfort each other immediately, such as hugging or crying together. It also supports the main caregiver, who feels less isolated; as one caregiver noted, ‘When the family meets together, they can then better cooperate with the caregiver and give moral support’. The first daughter, for example, found that after informing others, her family became more supportive and better understood her role, even offering her mental support. Not all family involvement is positive; some members may not handle the truth well, either refusing to accept or becoming angry. One participant said, ‘I’m enough by myself, but I wished someone had been there just for me to not be alone with the shock’. She didn’t want information filtered but acknowledged the emotional impact.

The theme emphasises caregiver support beyond meetings. Families often mobilise afterwards; for example, a caregiver told her siblings, and they visited the patient together, ensuring someone was always present. Relatives also offered help, inspired by the meeting’s urgency. Some primary caregivers act as protectors or intermediaries. One granddaughter said she gradually prepared distant relatives, supporting them emotionally and temporarily taking on healthcare roles, highlighting the heavy burden of primary caregivers.

Theme 6: Practical care guidance and preparedness for caregiving

Caregivers were advised to speak gently, maintain a soothing environment, and engage in spiritual comfort. For instance, a doctor told a daughter her unconscious mother could still hear, so she was instructed to speak kindly and avoid negative words. The daughter followed this, reassuring her mother and preventing distress. She said, ‘The doctor said she can hear and feel, so the family should only speak kind words to her’, and she reminded everyone, ‘talk to Mom, tell her we’re here, don’t say anything upsetting’. This knowledge empowered her to create a peaceful last moment for her mother, showing how meeting guidance translated into meaningful action.

Caregivers learned signs of impending death, like cold extremities, unconsciousness, and breathing changes, which helped them understand what was happening and stay calm. For example, when they saw these signs, they knew to focus on comfort rather than aggressive treatments. They received guidance on which interventions were no longer appropriate, such as DNR orders or discontinuing medications, and focussed on comfort measures, such as IV hydration, rather than force-feeding or unnecessary procedures. Caregivers responded positively to practical advice. One said, ‘The meeting helped us understand her condition and recent developments, so we knew what to expect’. Another added, ‘Without this meeting, I wouldn’t have known her condition. I became more prepared. We knew her time was near, and the doctor explained what might happen, so we got ready’. ‘Being ready’ included family visits, arranging religious rites, and preparing for the moment, such as knowing who to call first after death or how to keep her comfortable. Some participants said the meeting prompted them to get affairs in order. While not explicitly stated in the transcripts, it is common for doctors or social workers to encourage families to consider arrangements such as funeral planning. One caregiver mentioned ‘funeral planning and finances’ as part of ‘practical preparedness’. Even if not explicitly stated, practical discussions, such as where the patient should spend their final days or funeral logistics, likely occurred and were influenced by the meeting. One granddaughter shared how a doctor’s advice on engaging with her family member helped her feel connected and empowered, providing comfort. Practical care guidance was described by caregivers as valuable because it provided knowledge about signs of dying, care practices, and what to expect, which appeared to increase their sense of confidence and preparedness. One caregiver summarised: ‘The meeting was good; it taught me how to deal with her and what she needs and doesn’t need’.

Discussion

Key findings

This qualitative study explored caregivers’ perspectives on how structured family meetings related to their preparedness and communication practices as a patient approached the end of life. Six interrelated themes emerged from caregiver interviews: psychological relief, emotional burden, communication style, religious framing, caregiver support, and practical care guidance. 12 Caregivers reported that structured family meetings reduced uncertainty and were perceived to improve understanding of the dying process. At the same time, the meetings triggered anticipatory grief and emotional distress, reflecting the inherent difficulty of confronting imminent death. Participants consistently valued clear, honest, and compassionate communication from clinicians. Religious framing played an important role in helping families interpret the situation and cope with the anticipated loss. 13 Practical guidance about signs of dying and how to interact with the patient increased caregivers’ confidence and helped them prepare for the final phase of life. These findings are consistent with previous studies showing that structured family meetings can improve information clarity and support caregivers’ coping during the final phase of life. Prior research in Western and East Asian palliative care settings has highlighted the importance of honest communication, anticipatory guidance, and emotional support in reducing uncertainty for families. 3 Our results extend these observations by illustrating how caregivers in this cultural context described the value of religious framing and family-centred communication practices when preparing for an impending death.

What this study adds

This study contributes qualitative evidence on family meetings in a Middle Eastern palliative care context. Most existing research on end-of-life family meetings has been conducted in Western or East Asian healthcare systems. Our findings highlight how cultural and religious factors shape caregiver experiences and responses to communication about dying. In particular, Islamic beliefs and practices influenced how families interpreted medical information and engaged in bedside care. 14 The findings also highlight the importance of including multiple family members in meetings, when possible, to distribute the emotional burden and improve shared understanding. In addition, caregivers valued specific practical guidance about the dying process, suggesting that structured meetings can enhance both emotional preparedness and caregiving competence. 15

Strengths and limitations of the study

This study has several strengths. It provides in-depth qualitative insights into caregivers’ experiences shortly after family meetings, allowing participants to reflect on their immediate emotional and informational responses. Interviews were conducted in the participants’ native language and analysed in Arabic prior to translation, thereby helping preserve cultural nuance and meaning. The use of grounded theory analytic techniques, along with independent coding by two researchers, strengthened the rigour of the analysis.

This study’s limitations must be considered when interpreting results. The small sample size (seven caregivers) from a single Saudi Arabian institution may not reflect broader experiences, particularly regarding access, among those who refused or lacked family-to-family meetings. There could be selection bias, as participants might have positive views, although some critical feedback was captured. Interviews conducted soon after meetings capture immediate reactions and lack long-term data on grief or care satisfaction; a longitudinal study could address this. Although the findings cannot be generalised beyond this setting, they offer transferable insights that may inform communication practices in culturally similar palliative care environments. All interviews were in Arabic and translated into English, risking subtle meaning loss despite careful translation.

Conclusion

This study suggests that caregivers experienced structured family meetings were helpful in supporting informational, emotional, spiritual, and practical preparedness during the final phase of life. Caregivers appreciated clear, compassionate communication, culturally sensitive religious framing, and practical guidance on end-of-life care. Challenges included the emotional burden, the need for broader family inclusion, and the need for ongoing support.

We recommend family meetings as standard in palliative care, delivered with empathy and cultural sensitivity, involving key relatives. Practical instructions and follow-up should be provided, with interdisciplinary collaboration encouraged. These measures can make family meetings essential in family-centred end-of-life care, helping caregivers feel informed, supported, and prepared during this difficult time.
