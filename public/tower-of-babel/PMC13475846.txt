Lower airway changes in a neonate with cystic lesions: Anesthestic considerations
Authors: Sneha Ann, T. Krishna Prasad, G. Dilip Kumar
Published: 2026

Sir,

Congenital pulmonary airway malformation (CPAM) is a rare developmental lung anomaly presenting with respiratory distress in newborns, mimicking other congenital thoracic lesions. Pediatric anesthesia poses challenges due to difficult lung isolation, risk of rupture, and cardiovascular collapse. In this case, controlled ventilation through single-lumen tube enabled safe lobectomy. The lung separation maneuvers in pediatric patients are difficult due to no availability of appropriate size double-lumen endotracheal tube, we managed to provide general anesthesia and controlled ventilation using single-lumen tube to provide a good operating condition for right upper lobectomy.

We present a case of respiratory distress in a 4-year-old female with CPAM, managed surgically in a resource-constrained setting. The child underwent thoracotomy with resection of the right upper lobe, part of the middle lobe, and partly the lower lobe. The patient presented with fever, cough, hemoptysis, and chest pain. Imaging revealed pulmonary abscesses in the right upper, apical, and posterior lobes. She had no prior history of recurrent respiratory infections or neonatal distress. Born at term through normal vaginal delivery, birth weight was 2.7 kg, with no intensive care unit (ICU) admission. Echocardiography showed normal intracardiac anatomy and good left ventricle function. On auscultation, air entry was reduced in the right supraclavicular area. Computerized tomography scan demonstrated multilocular cystic lesions involving two lobes, consistent with CPAM [Figure 1]. In the holding area, IV access was secured, and midazolam (0.5–0.8 mg) was administered. Baseline vitals were stable. Induction included fentanyl (30–40 mcg), propofol (50 mg), and atracurium (8 mg).

Cystic (large) lesion in the upper zone and showing serious lower airway changes

She was intubated with a cuffed 4.5 endotracheal tube. A femoral central venous line was placed. Controlled ventilation was maintained with tidal volume 7–8 mL/kg, respiratory rate 24–26/min, and sevoflurane in O2/air mixture. Fentanyl infusion and intermittent atracurium doses were used. Caudal epidural catheter provided postoperative analgesia. Intraoperative blood loss exceeded 40% of total blood volume, requiring transfusion. Ventilation was intermittently withheld during bronchial resection. A chest drain was inserted, and 100–150 mL packed cells were transfused. Surgery lasted 2.5 h, followed by smooth extubation once protective reflexes returned. The patient was transferred to pediatric ICU, requiring nasal continuous positive pressure ventilation for 2 h, then oxygen via nasal cannula. Morphine infusion provided analgesia. Chest physiotherapy was initiated for lung reexpansion. The complications included air leaks and pneumothorax, managed with chest drainage. By the following day, respiratory function stabilized, and the child was transferred to high-dependence unit. The chest drain was removed after 24 h.

Lung isolation in children under 8 years poses significant challenges due to the lack of appropriately sized double-lumen tubes and bronchial blockers.[12] This increases the risk of spillage of infective material into the healthy lung. In this case, prone and Trendelenburg positioning facilitated postural drainage, minimizing contamination and maintaining oxygenation.[34] Avoidance of nitrous oxide prevented hyperinflation of cysts and mediastinal shift. Postoperative ventilatory complications in similar cases may require high-frequency oscillation or extracorporeal membrane oxygenation, as pulmonary hypertension remains a major risk factor.
