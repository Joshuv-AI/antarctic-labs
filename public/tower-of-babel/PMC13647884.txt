Vitrification-Controlled
Curing of Epoxidized Linseed
Oil Resins: Kinetics, TTT Analysis, and Thermomechanical Performance
with Anhydride and Amine Curing Agents

Cristiane da Silva Fonseca, Maurício Carpe Diem Ferreira Xavier, Otávio Bianchi, Sandro Campos Amico, Cesar Liberato Petzhold

Abstract

Epoxidized linseed
oil (ELO) is a promising biobased
alternative
to petrochemical epoxy precursors, with a curing behavior strongly
influenced by the chemistry of the curing agent and the resulting
network architecture. In this study, ELO was synthesized via the Shi
epoxidation approach and subsequently cured with either methyltetrahydrophthalic
anhydride (ELO-AD) or a fatty amine agent (ELO-AM). Although epoxy–amine
reactions exhibit lower activation energies (19–26 kJ·mol–1) than the epoxy–anhydride system (40–45
kJ·mol–1), the overall curing efficiency is
governed by vitrification and diffusion constraints rather than intrinsic
chemical reactivity. The anhydride-cured system forms a dense network
with higher glass transition temperature (T
g = 46–94 °C), higher cross-link density (υ = 1.19
× 103 mol·m–3), and gel content
(97.95 ± 0.16%) together with rapid viscosity growth (k
η up to 0.83 min–1 at
140 °C), resulting in shorter characteristic times in time–temperature–transformation
(TTT) diagrams. The amine-cured system forms a more flexible, lower T
g (−10 to 19 °C) network with lower
υ (4.77 × 102 mol·m–3), and gel content (79.87 ± 4.45%), consistent with a less interconnected
netword and greater diffusion limitations at advanced conversion.
These structural and kinetic differences translate directly into macroscopic
performance. ELO-AD exhibits superior Young’s modulus and strength
(2.0 GPa and ≈ 46 MPa, respectively), whereas ELO-AM shows
enhanced ductility (strain at break ≈ 26%) and higher thermal
stability (DTG peak ≈ 485 °C). Overall, this study establishes
quantitative structure–process–property relationships
for ELO-based epoxy systems, with practical guidelines for selecting
curing agents and processing windows for sustainable thermoset resins.

Introduction

Epoxy resins constitute a cornerstone
of modern thermosetting materials,
owing to their ability to form densely cross-linked networks with
high mechanical strength, thermal stability, and chemical resistance.
These attributes originate from the high reactivity of epoxide groups
combined with the structural rigidity typically imparted by aromatic
backbones and multifunctional curing agents. Consequently, epoxy systems
have been extensively employed in high-performance applications, including
fiber-reinforced composites. Nevertheless, the overwhelming dominance
of petrochemical-based formulations, particularly those derived from
diglycidyl ether of bisphenol A (DGEBA), has raised significant environmental
and health concerns, including toxicity, endocrine disruption, and
environmental persistence.
,

Biobased epoxy
resins have emerged as alternative materials to
reduce dependence on fossil resources and expand the design space
of epoxy networks beyond the constraints imposed by rigid aromatic
structures. Renewable feedstocks such as lignin, tannins, cardanol,
terpenes, furans, and vegetable oils have been investigated as epoxy
precursors, each with particular chemical architectures and reactivity
patterns.
−

Vegetable oils are particularly attractive due to their high
availability,
low toxicity, biodegradability, and intrinsic multifunctionality.
They are molecularly characterized by a triglyceride composed of long-chain
fatty acids that are predominantly aliphatic and often highly unsaturated.
Epoxidation of carbon–carbon double bonds converts these intrinsically
flexible molecules into multifunctional epoxy precursors. In contrast
to conventional aromatic epoxy monomers, epoxidized vegetable oils
combine pronounced segmental mobility with sterically constrained
epoxy functionalities randomly distributed along extended aliphatic
chains This structural duality governs curing kinetics and network
development, often resulting in lower glass transition temperature
(T
g), broader curing exotherm, and increased
susceptibility to diffusion-controlled regimes during cross-linking.
,

Within this class of materials, linseed oil (LO) extracted
from Linum usitatissimum stands out as an especially
attractive
epoxy precursor due to its high α-linolenic acid content, which
provides a high degree of unsaturation and enables efficient epoxidation,
yielding high epoxy functionality per molecule. The spatial distribution
of double bonds along the fatty acid chains reduces steric hindrance
during chemical modification, favoring the selective formation of
oxirane rings.
,
 While these features make epoxidized
linseed oil (ELO) a versatile platform for the development of biobased
epoxy networks, they also introduce molecular heterogeneity and higher
backbone flexibility compared to conventional DGEBA systems.

The curing of ELO-based resins is more complex and cannot be described
by a single, uniform reaction pathway. The combination of a flexible
aliphatic backbone and a heterogeneous distribution of epoxy groups
leads to multistep curing mechanisms, in which chemical reaction kinetics,
segmental mobility, and diffusion processes are strongly coupled.
Under these conditions, the nature of the curing agent becomes decisive
in governing network formation. Anhydrides, acids, and amines differ
not only in their intrinsic reactivity toward epoxy groups but also
in the stiffness, functionality, and secondary interactions they introduce
into the evolving network, thereby modulating cross-link density,
vitrification, and final thermomechanical properties.
,

In epoxy–anhydride systems initiated by tertiary amines,
curing typically proceeds via an anionic ring-opening mechanism, involving
the formation of alkoxide and carboxylate intermediates that propagate
via competing esterification and etherification reactions. These pathways
are generally autocatalytic and exhibit strong temperature dependence,
leading to a rapid acceleration of network formation once a critical
concentration of reactive species and sufficient thermal energy are
reached.
,
 In contrast, epoxy–amine systems
are governed by the nucleophilic attack of amine to epoxide rings,
yielding hydroxyl groups and secondary or tertiary amines. In biobased
epoxy systems from vegetable oils, this is frequently accompanied
by aminolysis reactions, in which amines react with ester groups within
the triglyceride backbone, generating amide linkages and altering
both the effective cross-link density and the topology of the developing
network.
,

The coexistence of these distinct
and sometimes competing reaction
pathways poses a challenge in the design of sustainable epoxy thermosets.
High cross-link densities are associated with enhanced thermal stability
and mechanical strength, but excessive rigidity can restrict molecular
mobility, narrow processing windows, and reduce toughness. Conversely,
curing agents that yield greater chain flexibility enhance deformability
and energy dissipation, but often at the expense of stiffness and
thermal resistance. A mechanistic understanding of how the curing
agent chemistry governs the transition from chemically controlled
kinetics to diffusion-limited regimes, as well as the onset of vitrification
is essential for the design of biobased epoxy networks.

Todorovic
et al. (2021) demonstrated that ELO network formation
involves complex, multistep mechanisms influenced by the steric hindrance
of epoxy groups near the triglyceride core and by secondary reactions.
They showed that curing agent selection strongly affects thermomechanical
performance, with T
g ranging from 12 to
145 °C and storage moduli (E′) from 10
to 1940 MPa, depending on the chemical rigidity of the curing agent. Similarly, Menager et al. (2020) examined the
curing of ELO with biobased dicarboxylic acids of different chain
lengths (succinic, suberic, and sebacic acids) and demonstrated a
direct correlation between curing agent structure, curing kinetics,
and final properties. Short-chain succinic acid promoted higher reaction
rates, greater cross-link density, and superior thermomechanical performance
(T
g ≈ 20 °C; E′ ≈ 1.7 GPa), whereas longer-chain suberic and sebacic
acids yielded more flexible networks with lower cross-link density
(T
g ≈ 4–6 °C; E′ ≈ 1.1–1.4 GPa). These findings underscore how curing agent chemistry enables
precise tuning of stiffness, thermal stability, and elasticity in
ELO-based epoxy systems.

Since epoxy resins derived from renewable
precursors, such as LO,
typically exhibit complex, multistep curing mechanisms that depend
strongly on the nature of the curing agent,
−

 a rigorous
kinetic analysis is essential to understand these processes. In this
context, advanced approaches combining isoconversional methods applied
to nonisothermal and isothermal differential scanning calorimetry
(DSC) data with spectroscopic monitoring by Fourier-transform infrared
spectroscopy (FTIR) allow reaction profiles to be resolved without
assuming predefined kinetic models. The
construction of time–temperature–transformation (TTT)
diagrams further enables the identification of distinct kinetic regimes
and the simultaneous correlation of temperature, time, vitrification,
and diffusion-controlled limitations, offering insights that cannot
be captured by kinetic parameters alone. By revealing how curing-agent
chemistry governs vitrification behavior, network architecture, and
thermomechanical performance, this approach establishes TTT diagrams
as a key tool for process control and rational design of high-performance,
sustainable epoxy thermosets tailored to specific application and
processing requirements.

This study investigates the curing
behavior of ELO using two chemically
distinct curing systems: methyltetrahydrophthalic anhydride activated
with benzyltriethylammonium chloride and a biobased aliphatic diamine.
By combining isothermal and nonisothermal kinetic analyses with complementary
characterization techniques, DSC, attenuated-total-reflectance FTIR
(ATR-FTIR) spectroscopy, isoconversional methods, rheological monitoring,
dynamic mechanical analysis, and TTT diagrams, this study aims at
bringing a mechanistic and quantitative comparison of curing pathways,
kinetic regimes, and structure–property relationships in these
ELO-based epoxy networks.

Synthesis of ELO

The LO was epoxidized using the dimethyldioxirane
intermediate route (Shi epoxidation), as described in the literature
for vegetable oils. After a series of
optimization experiments on the reaction parameters, the epoxidation
of LO was carried out in a Büchiglasuster BMD 300 pilot reactor,
with a capacity of two liters, equipped with an agitation system and
temperature control. For the reaction, LO (24 g, 0.03 mol) was dissolved
in acetone (800 mL, 632 g), and the mixture was transferred to the
reactor along with sodium bicarbonate (43 g, 0.51 mol). Then, an aqueous
solution of KHSO5·0.5KHSO4·0.5K2SO4 known by its commercial name Oxone (0.46 mol·L–1) was slowly added to the reaction, which was kept
under constant stirring (500 rpm) and temperature control (not exceeding
40 °C). After approximately 5 h, when the reaction was complete,
the mixture was transferred to a separatory funnel. The aqueous phase
was then extracted with ethyl acetate and treated with small portions
of a saturated sodium chloride solution to prevent emulsion formation.
After extracting the organic phase, anhydrous magnesium sulfate was
added to remove any remaining water. The organic solvent was then
evaporated using a rotary evaporator for approximately 40 min (40
°C, 240 mbar) and subsequently placed in a reduced-pressure system
for 8 h (room temperature, >10 mbar) to completely remove the solvent.

To determine the conversion degree of the unsaturated bonds to
epoxy groups, the sample was analyzed by 1H NMR on a Bruker
Avance 400 (9.39 T) using deuterated chloroform (CDCl3)
as the solvent. Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy with attenuated
total reflectance (ATR-FTIR) was also used to characterize ELO. The
FTIR spectra were obtained on a Perkin-Elmer Spectrum 1000 instrument,
in the range of 4000–500 cm–1, with 32 scans
and a resolution of 4 cm–1.

In the kinetic modeling,
the curing
process was described using
a sequential reaction scheme. In the
first stage, curing was modeled as a first-order reaction, representing
the initiation step in which reactive species are generated and the
reaction rate is primarily temperature-dependent, with no significant
autocatalysis. In the second stage, the main propagation of the epoxy
network was described using the Kamal–Sourour autocatalytic
model, which accounts for the acceleration of epoxy ring-opening reactions
promoted by hydroxyl anion formed during curing. This stage dominates
at intermediate degrees of conversion and is responsible for the main
heat release observed in DSC. At high degrees of conversion, the formation
of a cross-linked network restricts molecular mobility and leads to
vitrification, limiting the reaction rate to diffusion rather than
chemical reactivity. To capture this effect, diffusion control was
incorporated via a Rabinowitch-type formulation that couples the intrinsic
chemical reaction rate with a diffusion-controlled rate constant,
enabling a smooth transition from chemically controlled to diffusion-controlled
kinetics and preventing overestimation of reaction rates at high conversion.
The diffusion-control parameters were simultaneously optimized with
the Kamal–Sourour kinetic parameters through a global fitting
procedure using both isothermal and nonisothermal DSC data. This global
approach provides a consistent description of the curing kinetics
over the entire conversion range and yields a single parameter set
constrained by multiple experimental conditions, which was subsequently
used to construct the TTT diagrams.

 All kinetic parameters were determined
by global fitting of isothermal and nonisothermal DSC data using NETZSCH
Kinetics Neo software.

Results and Discussion

Synthesis
of ELO

The synthesis of ELO was adapted from
the procedures reported by Setien et al., applying their methodology
to LO with specific modifications. The
chemical structure of ELO was characterized by 1H NMR and
FTIR spectroscopy. In the 1H NMR spectra (a), the signal at 4.33 ppm
corresponds to the methylene protons and the signal at 5.30 ppm to
the methinic proton of the triacylglycerol backbone. The disappearance
of signals in the 5.44 ppm region, attributed to the vinylic protons
of unsaturated fatty acids, together with the appearance of signals
at 2.86 and 3.04 ppm, assigned to epoxy protons, indicates the conversion
of CC double bonds into oxirane rings. From that, an average
epoxidation degree of 5.54 epoxy groups per ELO molecule was determined,
corresponding to a average conversion yield of 92%, indicating high
reaction selectivity.

The FTIR spectrum of ELO (b) further supports the successful epoxidation.
Characteristic
absorption bands associated with unsaturated bonds at 3014 cm–1 (C–H), 1656 cm–1 (−CC−), and 718 cm–1 (H–CC–Hcis) are absent after epoxidation, confirming the consumption
of double bonds, in agreement with previous reports. In addition, the appearance of the band at 830 cm–1, assigned to the −C–O–C– stretching
vibration of epoxy groups, provides clear evidence for the formation
of oxirane functionalities in the ELO structure.

Adjustment
of the Resin Formulations

DSC was first
used to evaluate curing parameters (T
p, α, and T
g) of formulations prepared
with both curing agents ( and ). The ELO-AD systems exhibited two exothermic peaks, whereas
ELO-AM showed multiple overlapping peaks, indicating distinct curing
mechanisms. For anhydride-cured formulations, decreasing the anhydride-to-epoxy
ratio increased T
g from 10 to 35 °C
(b). The ELO-AD
(1:0.8) formulation showed the highest T
g, T
p, and conversion (α = 0.73),
indicating a higher cross-link density, whereas the ELO-AD (1:1.5)
formulation exhibited lower T
g and conversion
(α = 0.66), likely due to unreacted epoxy groups. Reduced performance
at stoichiometric conditions has been attributed to steric hindrance
of epoxy groups near the triglyceride core, requiring a slight excess
of epoxy for effective network formation.
,

In contrast, the amine-cured
formulations displayed
only minor
variations in T
g (−21 to −19
°C), with a slight decrease at excess amine content (−22
°C for 1:1.5), consistent with lower conversion (α = 0.55). T
p values remained nearly constant (224–226
°C), indicating similar reaction onset across compositions, while
final conversion and network structure varied with curing agent content. Formulations with the highest T
g and α were selected for kinetic analysis to compare
the curing mechanisms.

Curing Reactions

FTIR spectra of the
ELO-AD immediately after mixing
and throughout the curing are shown in . An increase in the broad absorption band
at 3600–3300 cm–1, attributed to −O–H

ring opening. Concurrently, the growth
of aliphatic −C–H stretching bands in the 3000–2850
cm–1 region reflects network formation through epoxy–anhydride

The gradual reduction of the anhydride carbonyl
bands at 1860 and
1780 cm–1, accompanied by an increase in the ester
carbonyl band at 1728 cm–1 and in the −C–O
stretching bands at 1157 and 1148 cm–1, confirms
that cross-linking proceeds predominantly via esterification, with
a secondary contribution from etherification reactions. In parallel, the progressive decrease of the
epoxy-related bands (−C–O–C−) at 915 and
830 cm–1 further evidence epoxy ring-opening reactions
during curing.

The epoxy–anhydride
polymerization proceeds via an anionic
ring-opening mechanism, as widely reported in the literature. In the present system, curing is initiated by
the in situ generation of a tertiary amine resulting
from thermal activation of the initiator contained in the curing agent,
producing triethylamine and benzyl chloride. At early stages of curing, zwitterionic active species are generated
through nucleophilic interactions involving the tertiary amine (). Specifically,
nucleophilic attack of the amine on the epoxy ring leads to the formation
of alkoxide species (Reaction I), while interaction with the anhydride
carbonyl generates carboxylate species (Reaction III). These intermediates subsequently propagate the
network through esterification reactions between alkoxides and anhydrides
(Reaction II) and between carboxylates and epoxy groups (Reaction
IV).

Both epoxy and anhydride
groups therefore participate
throughout
the curing process, occurring concurrently, as corroborated by FTIR
analysis. However, the reaction between alkoxide species and anhydrides
is kinetically favored over the carboxylate–epoxy reaction
due to the higher electrophilicity of the anhydride, higher nucleophilicity
of alkoxide, and the greater steric hindrance of the epoxy groups.

in ester-related absorption bands and the emergence of characteristic
amide bands, consistent with previous reports.

Curing Kinetics by DSC and Thermal Analysis

The curing
kinetics of ELO-AD and ELO-AM formulations were investigated by nonisothermal
DSC (). For
both formulations, T
p increased with increasing
heating rate, reflecting heat-transfer effects and reduced chain mobility
during network formation. ELO-AD exhibited lower T
p values (142–188 °C; a) and higher reaction enthalpies (ΔH = 297–345 J·g–1) than ELO-AM
(T
p = 169–239 °C; ΔH = 178–242 J·g–1; c), indicating faster
curing kinetics and a higher extent of cross-linking for the anhydride-cured
system.

Both systems displayed broad and asymmetric DSC
peaks, indicative
of overlapping thermal events and multistep curing behavior arising
from differences in epoxy accessibility and reactivity (Costa, Gerbase
and Petzhold, 2011; Xavier et al., 2024). Peak deconvolution using
independent Gaussian functions (R
2 >
0.99)
revealed distinct curing stages (b,d). For ELO-AD, the first peak (T
p ≈ 86 °C) is attributed to reaction
initiation promoted by tertiary amine species, while subsequent peaks
at approximately 157 and 181 °C correspond to the main cross-linking
stages, consistent with epoxy–anhydride systems initiated by
tertiary amines. In contrast, ELO-AM
exhibited higher initiation and propagation temperatures, with peaks
at ∼130, ∼164, and ∼228 °C, reflecting the
coexistence of multiple reaction pathways characteristic of epoxy–amine
systems. These features corroborate the
multistep cross-linking mechanisms inferred from FTIR analysis. The
degree of conversion at the peak heat flow (αp) differed
significantly between the systems, reaching ∼0.65 for ELO-AD
and ∼0.54 for ELO-AM, indicating that the maximum reaction
rate occurs at more advanced conversion for the anhydride system.
This behavior is characteristic of a more effective contribution of
autocatalytic curing, whereas the lower αp of ELO-AM
reflects a more gradual network development and reduced thermal sensitivity. Minor variations in T
g with heating rate were observed in the second heating scan for both
systems, likely due to incomplete equilibration at higher heating
rates. The data of both formulations

Considering
that both curing systems exhibit complex multistage
reaction behavior, as evidenced by the nonisothermal DSC curves shown
in , a more
detailed kinetic interpretation is required. The ELO resin contains
epoxy groups with varying degrees of accessibility due to steric constraints
and molecular architecture. This heterogeneity promotes sequential
reactivity, in which epoxy groups located at more exposed regions
of the molecule react preferentially at early stages, while sterically
hindered or internally located epoxy groups participate predominantly
at later stages of the curing process.

The nature of the curing
agents further modulates this effect.
Molecules with larger hydrodynamic volumes, such as the amine used
as the AM curing agent, are expected to exhibit slower diffusion and
reduced access to reactive sites, particularly as network formation
progresses and molecular mobility decreases. To quantitatively assess
these effects, hydrodynamic volumes were estimated from three-dimensional
molecular conformations generated using RDKit with the ETKDG algorithm,
followed by force-field optimization and calculation of van der Waals
molecular volume and shape descriptors. An equivalent hydrodynamic
radius was then obtained by applying a shape correction based on molecular
asphericity. The resulting hydrodynamic volumes were 0.23 nm3 for the anhydride curing agent, 1.53 nm3 for the ELO
resin, and 1.50 nm3 for the amine curing agent. These values
indicate that, despite differences in chemical functionality, both
curing agents interact with the ELO resin under diffusion-influenced
conditions, leading to overlapping and sequential reaction events
rather than a single, well-defined curing step.

Based on these
considerations, the curing kinetics of the epoxy
systems cured with anhydride and amine curing agents were analyzed
by fitting different kinetic models directly to the DSC heat flow
signal. The evaluated models included a two-step, sequentially ordered
Kamal–Sourour model with diffusion control coupled with the
Williams–Landel–Ferry (WLF) (A → B → C),
a single-step Kamal–Sourour model, and an nth-order single-step
model. The quality of the fits was assessed using the coefficient
of determination (R2), the sum of deviation squares, the
mean residual, and the number of degrees of freedom.

For the
anhydride-cured system, the two-step Kamal–Sourour
model provided an excellent description of the DSC signal, yielding
an R2 of 0.990, a sum of deviation squares of 4.537, and
a mean residual of 0.022, based on 4135 degrees of freedom. The single-step nth-order model yielded a slightly inferior fit (R
2 = 0.988), with a higher sum of squared deviations
(5.556) and a mean residual of 0.024, despite a comparable number
of degrees of freedom (4145). The single-step Kamal–Sourour
model achieved a marginally higher R

(0.991) and a lower sum of deviation squares (4.183), with a mean
residual of 0.021 and 4142 degrees of freedom. Although its numerical
performance is comparable to that of the two-step approach, the latter
was selected for further analysis because it explicitly represents
sequential reaction stages and diffusion-controlled effects, which
are consistent with the heterogeneous accessibility of epoxy groups
in the ELO resin.

A similar trend was observed for the amine-cured
system. The two-step
Kamal–Sourour model again exhibited the best overall performance,
with an R

The differences
in curing behavior between the anhydride- and amine-cured
ELO systems arise from fundamental molecular factors related to reaction
mechanism, molecular mobility, and network architecture. The ELO resin
contains epoxy groups with heterogeneous steric accessibility along
its bulky aliphatic backbone, inherently favoring sequential rather
than concerted reactions. In the anhydride-cured system, epoxy–anhydride
ring-opening and esterification require higher activation energies
and progressively form an ester-rich cross-linked network. In contrast,
epoxy–amine reactions proceed with lower activation barriers
and generate C–N linkages and secondary hydroxyl groups. As
curing progresses, network formation increasingly restricts the accessibility
of residual epoxy groups, favoring diffusion-controlled behavior and
competing reactions such as aminolysis. These molecular-level differences
account for the distinct curing kinetics observed experimentally and
provide the basis for the subsequent analysis of the TTT diagrams.

In contrast, epoxy–amine reactions proceed with lower activation

groups. Although chemically faster, this results in a more compliant
network with a lower T

limitations dominate at advanced conversion, restricting access to
sterically hindered epoxy groups favoring the aminolysis reaction.
Thus, the amine-cured system becomes mobility-limited earlier, whereas
the anhydride-cured system remains chemically controlled over a broader
conversion range. These molecular-level differences directly underpin
the distinct curing kinetics observed experimentally and set the basis
for the subsequent analysis of the TTT diagrams.

Conclusions

This study provides clear experimental
evidence that the curing
performance of ELO systems is governed by network structure and diffusion
constraints rather than intrinsic chemical reactivity alone. Although
the amine-cured system exhibits lower activation energies (19–26
kJ·mol–1), its low T
g (−10 to 19 °C), reduced cross-link density (υ
= 4.77 × 102 mol·m–3), lower
gel content (79.87 ± 4.45%), and higher swelling ratio (1.69)
indicate a less interconnected network and greater diffusion limitations
at advanced conversion, resulting in extended characteristic times
in the TTT diagrams.

In contrast, the anhydride-cured system
develops a denser network,
evidenced by its higher T
g (46–94
°C), cross-link density (υ = 1.19 × 103 mol·m–3), and gel content (97.95 ± 0.16%),
together with rapid viscosity growth (k
η up to 0.83 min–1 at 140 °C), resulting in
shorter TTT times at processing-relevant temperatures. These kinetic
and structural differences translate directly into superior mechanical
performance, with a Young’s modulus of ∼2.0 GPa and
tensile strength of ∼46 MPa, compared with ∼1.7 and
∼0.2 MPa for the amine-cured system.

The consistent agreement
among kinetic modeling, rheology, DMA,
