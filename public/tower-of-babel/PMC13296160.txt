Participatory approaches for tailoring implementation strategies in healthcare settings: a scoping review of contemporary literature (2019-2024)
Authors: Laura Krusch, Sara Söling, Christine Aumann, Aline Dick, Trutz Bommhardt, Nicola Gabriela Dymek, Juliane Köberlein-Neu, Silke Kuske
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundParticipatory approaches are crucial for identifying the determinants of successful implementation and informing the development of implementation strategies. However, systematic knowledge regarding the nature of participatory approaches for tailoring implementation strategies in healthcare remains limited. Therefore, we explored and mapped participatory approaches used in tailoring implementation strategies, characterized them, and conducted an exploratory analysis of their reported links to implementation outcomes.MethodsA scoping review of contemporary literature was conducted using MEDLINE via PubMed, PsycINFO and CINAHL via EBSCOhost that included literature published between January 2019 and December 2023. The search was updated for 2024. Backward and forward citation tracking was applied for studies that included labeled participatory approaches and quantitative outcomes. Two raters independently screened the literature. An adapted Intervention Component Analysis (ICA) was conducted as an exploratory approach to analyze reported characteristics.ResultsBased on 24,240 citations, 129 studies were identified. Among them, 71 studies reported labeled participatory approaches, summarized in seven main categories: co-approaches, design, research, engagement, mapping, knowledge translation and other approaches. Nearly two-thirds were common approaches such as co-design, while slightly more than one-third were specific, such as the Person-Based Approach. More than half of these were implementation-specific, e.g., implementation mapping. Among these approaches, the linking of determinants to implementation strategies was based primarily on interest-holder theory (experience-based). Empirically measured quantitative outcomes were identified in 12 studies, six of which were eligible for ICA. All six studies reported positive findings of participatory approaches applied to the tailoring of implementation strategies. Among them, three studies reported results for each tailoring step and linked them to the participatory approach used. Three studies reported significant quantitative outcomes. However, no method showed a notable link to the success of participatory approaches for tailoring implementation strategies.ConclusionThis scoping review identified diverse participatory approaches that were applied for tailoring implementation strategies in healthcare. The emergence of implementation-specific participatory approaches highlights the need to consider specialized methods in implementation science. The lack of rigorously evaluated studies across all tailoring phases emphasizes the need for further research to facilitate a comprehensive understanding of effective participatory approaches for tailoring implementation strategies.Trial RegistrationProtocol registered on Open Science Framework (24 April 2024, https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/RQKTP).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s43058-026-00939-6.

Background

Healthcare systems continually adapt to emerging technologies to improve patient care. However, integrating these into existing structures poses numerous challenges for healthcare organizations [1]. Therefore, feasible approaches that correspond closely to implementation contexts are needed to provide effective support [2] and to inform the process for systematically introducing evidence-based interventions in practice [3]. In this context, participatory approaches can be crucial to identify implementation determinants and to inform the selection and development of implementation strategies or to provide policy developers with a better understanding of implementation, end-user needs and their capacities [4].

Participatory approaches cover a wide range of different types of participation. They differ in what is meant by “participation”, whose participation is desired, and the types and extent of involvement [5]. Despite a lack of evidence on which approach is most effective, it is generally recognized that involving experts as well as end-users in the design and implementation process of an intervention can enhance the effectiveness [4]. The continuous involvement of all interest-holders [6] affected by the implementation of a new technology is also necessary and effective for increasing (technology) acceptance and accelerating its spread [7–10]. Participatory approaches are particularly valuable in this tailoring process because they help identify the factors critical to successful implementation and provide policy developers with a better understanding of end-user needs [4]. Various methods (e.g., contextual inquiry vs. rapid crowd sourcing) highlight collaboration with interest-holders and participatory design [4]. (Definitions, see Additional file 1).

Despite the recognized importance of participatory approaches in tailoring implementation, several critical gaps remain in our understanding. First, while individual studies [4, 7, 9] have demonstrated the value of interest-holder involvement, there is limited systematic knowledge about which specific components of participatory approaches are most effective in different healthcare contexts. Second, the mechanisms through which participatory approaches influence implementation outcomes remain poorly understood. It is unclear whether their effectiveness stems from improved interest-holder buy-in, better identification of contextual barriers, enhanced strategy appropriateness, or other factors. Third, the diversity of participatory methods used across studies makes it difficult for researchers and practitioners to select the most appropriate approach for their specific implementation challenges.

Implementation strategies that are oriented towards implementation determinants and flexibly adapted to the respective context are decisive factors for the successful implementation of innovations [11, 12]. Tailored implementation, which is defined as “a prospective process involving systematic identification of determinants of practices within a local context, selection of implementation strategies appropriate to those determinants, the integration of these strategies into local work structures, and the actual application, evaluation, and potential further adaptation of the tailored implementation strategies (…).” [13]. It involves multiple steps: 1) identifying and understanding implementation determinants within the local context; 2) prioritizing determinants; 3) identifying change methods to address the identified determinants; 4) developing or selecting strategies that utilize these methods to address the identified determinants [14–18]; 5) operationalizing the implementation strategies; and 6) executing these strategies [18–20]. The process of linking implementation determinants to implementation strategies can be based on a formal theory, e.g., normalization process theory [21], or on an interest-holder theory, e.g., experience-based. Without a clear understanding of the characteristics and mechanisms of participatory approaches reported with positive outcomes, organizations may invest resources in participatory activities that do not optimally support their implementation goals. Such strategies may lead to inconsistent use of participatory approaches and potentially undermine implementation success. Therefore, a comprehensive synthesis of existing evidence is needed to identify the characteristics of participatory approaches that contribute to successful tailored implementation.

While recent reviews have also examined interest-holder involvement in implementation science in broad terms [22], indication-specific [23], with a focus on initial tailoring and the effectiveness of tailoring [24] or with the aim to understand mechanisms of ongoing tailoring [25, 26], our scoping review of contemporary literature extends beyond such studies by addressing participatory approaches for tailoring implementation strategies.

Therefore, we aimed to: 1) systematically map and characterize the nature and diversity of participatory approaches applied to tailor implementation strategies in healthcare; 2) describe their characteristics; and 3) where data permitted, conduct an exploratory analysis of how these approaches are linked to implementation outcomes.

Methods

This scoping review was conducted in accordance with the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) guidelines [27] and the PRISMA Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) [28] (see Additional file 2). Data extraction was carried out according to the Cochrane Guidelines [29] and the JBI guidelines [27]. The review protocol is registered on the Open Science Framework (date of registration: 24 April 2024, 10.17605/OSF.IO/RQKTP).

Review design

We conducted a scoping review of contemporary literature (2019-2024) to systematically map the nature and extent of participatory approaches used in tailoring implementation strategies in healthcare settings. Our approach involved two distinct analytical phases: 1) a comprehensive mapping of all studies reporting participatory approaches in tailored implementation and 2) an Intervention Component Analysis (ICA) for a subset of studies that met specific methodological criteria for analyzing reported characteristics [30].

Data sources and search strategy

Initially, a comprehensive search of the relevant literature published between January 2019 and December 2023 was conducted using MEDLINE via PubMed, PsycINFO, and CINAHL via EBSCOhost. The search period was chosen to capture contemporary developments in participatory approaches for tailored implementation, a rapidly evolving field. This timeframe aligns with 1) the formalization of 'implementation science’ as a MeSH term in PubMed (2019), signaling increased recognition and indexing precision; 2) the emergence of implementation-specific participatory frameworks referenced in recent methodological literature (e.g. implementation mapping [31]); and 3) the proliferation of studies explicitly addressing tailored implementation following foundational methodological publications [14]. While we acknowledge that earlier literature on participatory methods and intervention adaptation exists, our focus on explicitly labeled and described participatory approaches for tailoring implementation strategies in healthcare required a contemporary search period where such work was systematically indexed and reported. An update for 2024 was conducted on January 28, 2025. Furthermore, we applied forward and backward citation tracking for all included studies with labeled participatory approaches (see inclusion and exclusion criteria) and empirically measured quantitative outcomes.

The search strategy was designed based on our research aim using keywords and Boolean operators following the PICo framework (population, phenomenon of interest, context) [32]. The search involved three main concept areas: 1) participatory approaches and interest-holder involvement, 2) tailored implementation strategies, and 3) healthcare settings. The text and index terms contained in the titles and abstracts of the retrieved papers were iteratively refined to enable more comprehensive searches. The search strategy included both controlled vocabulary and keywords. The authors developed the search strategy iteratively through feedback loops within the research team by using the Peer Review of Electronic Search Strategies (PRESS) checklist [33]. The detailed search strategies for each database are provided in the appendices (see Additional file 3).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Literature published in English or German between January 2019 and December 2024 was included. Publications had to contain an empirically based and/or applied participatory approach for tailoring implementation strategies. For inclusion, publications additionally had to include a description of the tailoring process. Participatory tailoring of implementation strategies with and/or by the participants, and/or based on participants’ feedback on the tailored strategies, had to be described in detail. This description is equivalent to at least level 2 participation, as defined by Goodman & Sanders Thompson (2017) [9] and Vaughn & Jaquez 2020 [34]. At this level, interest-holders are, e.g., invited to provide perspectives and feedback on a research process or findings, which are then incorporated into the research process. This is an interactive process in which direct feedback is collected on certain aspects of a project [9, 34]. To precisely capture participatory approaches for this review, we distinguished between “labeled” and “unlabeled” participatory approaches in studies. Labeled participatory approaches were those that were explicitly named or identified by the study authors using specific terminology (e.g., “co-design”). Unlabeled approaches included general descriptions of interest-holder involvement or participatory activities without specific methodological labels. This distinction allowed us to examine both established participatory frameworks and emergent or context-specific approaches. We further distinguished between common (nonspecific general participatory approaches) and specific participatory approaches, e.g., implementation- or study-specific or developed. Studies that only characterized implementation strategies (e.g., using Proctor et al. (2013) [35]) or that merely evaluated the effectiveness of an implementation strategy, without providing a detailed description of the tailoring process, were excluded. In accordance with the context-specific nature of our research objective, which pertains to the healthcare setting, settings such as education and social services were excluded. All types of studies, reviews, and expert reports (excluding study protocols, abstracts, and poster contributions) were included if they provided a clear description or definition of the participatory approach for tailoring implementation strategies. Furthermore, publications had to include intervention (health care intervention) implementation, which had to be provided by healthcare providers. Protocols or other implementation objects as well as interventions provided by those other than healthcare providers, e.g., teachers, were excluded because of the specificity of our research aim. Additional file 4 provides a summary of the abovementioned eligibility criteria.

For the ICA, additional criteria were applied: a labeled participatory approach with a clear description; empirically measured quantitative outcomes (all types of outcomes, e.g., patient, service and/or implementation outcomes) related to the intervention, implementation strategy, or participatory approach; a study design suitable for drawing conclusions about participation effects (e.g., pre-post study, group comparisons); and author reflections on participatory approaches.

Study selection

The articles retrieved from each database were first imported into Citavi 7 and subsequently into Rayyan [36]. Within Rayyan, duplicate articles were identified and removed, both automatically and manually. Rayyan is a free web and mobile application that is designed to facilitate the screening process for researchers working on literature review projects [36]. We pretested the publication selection process using predefined eligibility criteria. Three reviewers independently screened 400 records by title and abstract in a two-stage process. The first 50 records were reviewed and discussed in a team meeting, resulting in a refinement of the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Subsequently, 350 additional records were screened to ensure the consistency and reliability of the selection process. Disagreements were discussed and resolved within the research team. No further refinement was needed.

The screening process was conducted by independent double screening using Rayyan. All records were split in half, and each record was independently screened by two reviewers. Titles and abstracts were then rated as ‘include’, ‘unclear’ or ‘exclude’. For records categorized as ‘include’ or ‘unclear’, the full text was also screened independently by the reviewers for final eligibility. If any discrepancies were identified, they were discussed within the team and resolved by consensus or by a third reviewer. Publications emanating from larger projects, which may themselves comprise several associated publications [24], were all reviewed and decided upon. In the case of full texts that were not freely available and for which interlibrary loan was not possible, the authors were contacted with a request to send the full texts. A reminder was sent after one week. If no response was received, the article was excluded. Reasons for the exclusion of full-text articles were documented.

Data extraction

Data were extracted by one reviewer and independently checked for accuracy by a second reviewer. Disagreements were resolved by discussion until consensus was reached. Data extraction was performed according to the research question and by using the described methods [27, 29]. For the initial overview, the following data were extracted into a table template using Word: first author and year of publication, study design and methods, participants and sample size, setting and country of origin, and study objective (see Table 2). To conduct a detailed analysis of participatory approaches, the following data were extracted into different table templates using Excel: name/label or description of the participatory method for tailored implementation strategies, characteristics and dimensions of participatory methods, descriptions of tailoring steps and phases addressed, basis for linking implementation determinants and strategies (formal theory or interest-holder theory (experience-based)), reported outcomes of implementation success, methods and instruments used in each tailoring phase, level of participation achieved, and author reflections on the methods used (see Additional file 5–7, Tables 1, 2 and 3). Conceptual matrices are developed to address the level of participation and tailoring phases (see Additional file 1). The data extraction framework was continuously refined through an iterative process to capture emerging themes and characteristics relevant to our research questions. Data analysis

A two-phase exploratory analytical approach was applied: Phase 1—Comprehensive Mapping: We conducted a descriptive analysis of all included studies, calculating frequencies and percentages to provide an overview of the participatory approaches used in tailored implementation. Studies were categorized by the type of participatory approach, methods used, tailoring phases addressed, and participatory level reached.Phase 2—ICA: An adapted ICA [30] was conducted as an exploratory analytical approach to descriptively analyze and characterize participatory approaches in studies reporting quantitative implementation outcomes. Given the anticipated small number and heterogeneity of eligible studies, this analysis was designed to map reported components and outcomes rather than to establish causal relationships or definitive success factors.

Phase 2—ICA: An adapted ICA [30] was conducted as an exploratory analytical approach to descriptively analyze and characterize participatory approaches in studies reporting quantitative implementation outcomes. Given the anticipated small number and heterogeneity of eligible studies, this analysis was designed to map reported components and outcomes rather than to establish causal relationships or definitive success factors.

The ICA process involved two steps and was conducted as a team approach: 1) an eligibility assessment and narrative analysis were conducted. Two researchers independently assessed all included studies for ICA eligibility using the additional criteria specified above. We then conducted a narrative analysis of the effects of participatory approaches for tailoring implementation strategies, describing outcome differences and analyzing the characteristics of participatory approaches applied. We also analyzed assumed mechanisms of action introduced by author reflections, including the specific methods and instruments used. 2) Reported characteristics were elaborated building on the results of the first step. Therefore, we summarized the findings and identified characteristics of participatory approaches that appear supportive for tailored implementation. Data analysis was performed by aggregating thematically similar results and by describing thematically different results [111]. Disagreements were resolved through team discussion until a consensus was reached.

Quality considerations included multiple reviewer involvement at each stage, systematic documentation of decisions, and regular team meetings to ensure consistency in approach and interpretation.

Results

A total of 24,240 records were identified from the scientific databases (see Fig. 1). In line with our inclusion criteria, a total of 129 studies (87 first search, 42 update) were included. Of these, 71 studies contained labeled participatory approaches (see Additional file 5). Among these 71 studies, 12 [37–39, 41–45, 47–50] reported empirically measured quantitative outcomes. After forward and backward citation tracking of these 12 studies, four related publications [13, 40, 46, 51] were also included. Finally, six studies [44, 45, 47–50] were found eligible for ICA, including three of the four publications [13, 46, 51] from forward and backward citation tracking. All the ICA-eligible studies reported positive effects of participatory approaches for tailoring implementation strategies. Three of these studies and one related publication [45, 49–51] stand out because they reported results for each tailoring step of the implementation process and linked them to the participatory approach. Considering the results of the first search and the update regarding the study characteristics (label of the participatory approach for tailoring implementation strategies, definition of the participatory approach, no reported outcomes, no empirically measured outcomes and empirically measured outcomes), the first search showed lower proportions for four out of five characteristics than did the update. Landscape of participatory approaches

Among 129 included studies, 71 (55%) labeled participatory approaches (see Table 1). These approaches were summarized into seven main categories: co-approaches (n=5), design (n=6), research (n=5), engagement (n=5), mapping (n=3), and knowledge translation approaches (n=2). Additionally, a diverse group of ‘other approaches’ (n=10) included both established frameworks and novel context-specific methods. The most frequently reported participatory approaches within the studies were design approaches (n=6), followed by co-approaches, engagement and research approaches (n=5 each).

A key finding was the distinction between common participatory approaches, specific approaches, and implementation-specific approaches. Among the 71 labeled approaches, nearly two thirds were common approaches (65%), slightly more than one third (35%) were specific approaches, and nearly more than half of these (56%) were developed for or adapted to implementation contexts. Examples of implementation-specific approaches included Implementation Mapping [52–55], the Basel Approach for Contextual Analysis (BANANA) [56], and the Implementation-Stakeholder Engagement Model (I-STEM) [57], which demonstrate how participatory principles are adapted to address specific phases of the implementation process.

Of the 12 final included studies with labeled participatory approaches and empirically measured quantitative outcomes, three studies each could be assigned to the categories of ‘co-approaches’ [37, 48, 49], ‘design’ [41, 42, 47] and ‘other approaches’ [39, 45, 50]. Two studies could be assigned to the ‘research’ [38, 43] category, and one study could be assigned to the ‘engagement” [44] category. Among the final studies suitable for the ICA (n=6), two studies each could be assigned to the ‘co-approaches’ [48, 49] and the ‘other approaches’ [45, 50] categories, and one study each could be assigned to the ‘design’ [47] and ‘engagement’ [44] categories.

Methodological characteristics of the final 12 studies

Among the 12 included studies (see Table 2) with a labeled participatory approach and empirically measured quantitative results, only two study designs were observed in several studies, namely, implementation studies (design not further defined) [39, 42] and quality improvement studies [44, 47]. All other study designs were identified only once: randomized feasibility trial [37], feasibility study [49], implementation process study (design not further labeled) [38], implementation evaluation alongside clinical trial [41], prospective process evaluation [43], process evaluation alongside a pragmatic cluster randomized controlled trial [45], multicenter stepped-sedge cluster randomized trial [50], and pilot study [48].

Most of the studies (see Table 2) used combined research methods such as surveys combined with interviews, focus groups or observations [38, 39, 41, 43, 45, 47, 48, 50]. The other studies combined other methods, such as interviews and evaluations of electronic health records [37].

The process of linking implementation determinants to implementation strategies in the 12 studies was primarily based on interest-holder theory (experience-based) (11 of 12 studies) as opposed to formal theory (1 of 12 studies) (see Table 2).

The primary objectives of the studies were the description and/or evaluation of the implementation process [38, 39, 41, 42, 45, 47]: feasibility/effectiveness assessment [49, 50], development of implementation strategies [37, 43, 48], and patient safety [44]. Samples and settings varied considerably, representing diverse healthcare contexts: among patients [37, 39, 43, 44], facility managers [43], organizational representatives [43], healthcare professionals [38, 41–43, 45], and various other interest-holder groups [42–44, 47, 48, 50]. Settings ranged from primary care [37, 41, 42, 48] to rehabilitation [38, 39, 49], routine mental health care organizations [50], and specialized services [43–45, 47], with studies conducted across multiple countries including the USA [37, 41, 42, 47, 48], the Netherlands [39, 43], Sweden [38], Australia [49], and Iran [44]. Two studies were conducted in several countries [45, 50].

Participatory methods and instruments across different tailoring phases

Several methods from the final 12 studies could be assigned to ten methodology categories (meetings, group discussions, interviews, surveys, audits, team/partnership, sessions, documentation/notes, workshops, and other methods) (see Table 3; see Additional file 6). The most frequently mentioned methods were those assigned to the ‘meetings’ and ‘surveys’ categories, followed by ‘interviews’ and ‘group discussions’. Other, more specific methods were, e.g., design thinking [37] and training [41, 47, 50]. No phase-specific methods were reported along the participatory approaches in any phase of the tailoring process (see Table 3). However, the methods were used differently according to the aim of each phase. The number of studies that reported methods was similar throughout the phases.

For phases 1–4 of the tailoring process [14–18], two studies [43, 45] did not include phase-specific reporting of the methods used. In phase 5, all the studies reported on the methods used. In phase 6, three studies [37, 42, 45] did not report phase-specific results for the methods used. Two studies also reported methods that could not be assigned to any phase, such as monthly workgroup meetings [49], attendance of sessions, and analysis of written summaries of meetings, and notes of communication [45]. Regarding the instruments, most studies lacked phase-specific reporting and reported a survey questionnaire. The other category included journey maps [41] and checklists [43, 47]. The studies that were suitable for the ICA showed a heterogeneity of the participatory methods used.

With respect to the different levels of participation: 1) inform, 2) consult, 3) involve, 4) collaborate and 5) empower [34], eight studies [37–39, 41, 43, 48–50] reached level 4 as the highest level while also incorporating lower levels. In the remaining studies [42, 44, 45, 47], level 3 was the highest level achieved, with lower levels also being addressed. This finding indicated that most studies achieved meaningful interest-holder engagement in decision-making processes, although opportunities existed for deeper participatory approaches that transfer greater decision-making authority to interest-holders.

Considering the studies eligible for ICA, three studies [44, 45, 47] addressed level 3 as the highest level of participation, while the other three [48–50] addressed level 4.

Results of the Intervention Component Analysis

Six of the final 12 studies fit the criteria for ICA (see Additional file 7), indicating high heterogeneity among the participatory approaches and methods. All eligible studies for ICA reported positive effects of using participatory approaches for tailoring implementation strategies (see summary author reflections, Additional file 7). Five of the six studies included general participatory approaches such as patient engagement [44], a human-centered, participatory design approach [47], co-production [48], experience-based co-design [49] and a participatory action approach [45]. One study included normalization process theory [50] as an implementation-specific participatory approach. Three of the six ICA studies [47, 48, 50] reported a significant positive effect.

Among the ICA studies, three [45, 49, 51] stand out because they show results for each tailoring step of the implementation process, demonstrating the comprehensive application of participatory approaches across all the phases performed and establishing clear links to the participatory approaches. These studies provide valuable models for systematic participatory implementation and show how interest-holder involvement can be maintained throughout the entire tailoring process.

However, based on the current findings, we cannot identify a specific participatory approach that consistently influences the success of the participatory approach in tailoring implementation strategies in healthcare settings.

Discussion

This scoping review highlights the nature and characteristics of participatory approaches for tailoring implementation strategies in healthcare. Our findings reveal the diversity and the potential of participatory approaches and highlights important distinctions from broader interest-holder involvement research for tailoring implementation strategies.

Growing field of participatory approaches in tailoring implementation strategies

We identified 129 studies, with 71 employing labeled participatory approaches following our defintions. The findings demonstrate a substantial and increasing interest in participatory methods specifically for tailoring implementation strategies and suggests an increasing recognition that effective implementation requires context-sensitive, interest-holder-responsive approaches. However, only 10% of innovation projects in healthcare practice incorporate implementation science knowledge explicitly [113].

We identified a variety of participatory approaches throughout the phases of implementation tailoring and distinguished between common and specific approaches. All the studies suitable for ICA reported positive effects of using participatory approaches for tailoring implementation strategies. However, given the small number and heterogeneity of studies, these findings should be interpreted as exploratory observations rather than definitive evidence of effectiveness. The search update identified proportionally more studies addressing key participatory characteristics, which suggests that growth in this field is accelerating. Our findings complement and extend recent examinations of interest-holder involvement in implementation science. While Arnahoutova et al. (2025) [22] reported that 89% of implementation studies broadly report some form of interest-holder involvement, our scoping review focuses specifically on participatory approaches within the tailored implementation process. The evidence gap map approach of the former study [22] examined interest-holder involvement as a general implementation strategy, whereas our work presents participatory approaches as mechanisms for (context-specific) adaptation. Whereas Sridhar et al. (2023) [23] focused on implementation strategy mapping methods (including participatory features), e.g., concept mapping in children’s mental health, our research focuses on participatory approaches applied to the tailoring of implementation strategies in healthcare settings. Although there are formal theories that link implementation determinants and strategies, such as normalization process theory [21], our findings indicate that the link is mostly based on interest-holder theories (experience-based).

Diversity of participatory approaches

Slightly fewer than two-thirds of the 71 studies with labeled participatory approaches reported common participatory approaches; the others reported specific participatory approaches, among which more than half were related to implementation research. Design approaches were most common, followed by co-, engagement, and research approaches. This distribution suggests a balance between established research methodologies and design thinking principles, alongside emerging specialized approaches.

While 27 common participatory approaches have been identified in the literature [34], only six of our identified approaches could be associated with these common approaches; these included user-centered design, CBPR, participatory action research design, action research approach, participatory research approach and PAR. While most ICA studies use common approaches, our results also suggest the relevance of implementation-specific approaches, such as implementation mapping [52–55], the BANANA [56], and the I-STEM [57]. This development toward implementation-specific approaches addresses gaps identified in the broader implementation science literature, where participatory principles are recognized as important but specific guidance for tailored implementation has been limited [2, 12]. Common approaches are most often used in studies with positive quantitative outcomes. Other commonly used approaches in healthcare innovation, such as Living Labs [114], were not prominently reported in our review. This deficit likely reflects our specific inclusion criterion, which explicitly focused on tailoring of implementation strategies. Living Labs and similar innovation-focused methods incorporate participatory principles, which are primarily used for co-creating new products, services, or interventions rather than for adapting implementation strategies for existing evidence-based practices. This distinction underscores the distinct purposes of participatory approaches serve across the innovation-to-implementation continuum. Future research should investigate whether the implementation-specific approaches or other established theories, models and frameworks mentioned in the literature are equally suitable and effective.

Methodological heterogeneity and participation levels

Heterogeneity was observed in the participatory methods used. Regarding the different participation levels [34], most studies reached the second highest level (collaborate). This finding aligns with the emphasis of meaningful interest-holder engagement in the participatory research literature [34], although the absence of studies achieving the highest level (empower) suggests opportunities for deeper participatory approaches in implementation contexts. Our analysis revealed that no phase-specific methods were used in the participatory approaches in any phase of the tailoring process. However, the methods were adapted based on each phase’s aim. Further research is needed to investigate whether certain methods are more suitable for certain phases and certain participation levels and to determine the criteria for decision-making corresponding to the participatory approach, including the level of participation. The primary goal of participatory approaches is to directly involve essential interest-holders in collaboratively shaping tools, products, and processes using various methods. Common methods and instruments include field observations, interviews, focus groups, and traditional user-centered practices, such as feedback on mock-ups or prototypes throughout the design phase [4]. In our work, we found that participatory methods were used throughout the entire tailoring process. In contrast, a systematic review [115] on patient and public involvement revealed that despite the recognition of early involvement, a key factor in preventing unnecessary resource expenditure and minimizing rejection rates for innovations, patients were often involved only in the final, passive stages of an innovation process, such as usability testing, in which context their potential to proactively influence change was severely restricted. We also found that specific methods and instruments were used.

Characteristics of participatory approaches include open, participatory, and democratic data collection and analysis, enabling communities and interest-holders to shape the work and contribute to decision-making. This makes the research findings practical, relevant, and meaningful to interest-holders, as their needs and perspectives are considered. Another characteristic lies in efforts to establish a culture of open communication and collaboration among all interest-holders, which can help overcome obstacles, support joint problem-solving, and facilitate the co-development of strategies [34], which is consistent with our findings. However, we went beyond this by identifying specific approaches. Participation varied in intensity. The participatory research offers a relevant conceptual perspective, particularly Goodman & Sanders Thompson’s (2017) [9] categorization of 1.) nonparticipation, 2.) symbolic participation and 3.) engaged participation. Participation enables partnerships between science and practice to efficiently combine knowledge from these two worlds [116]. Interest-holder involvement can enhance the credibility, legitimacy and impact of findings; support social change; and generate meaningful insights [34]. While most studies showed high participation levels, these were related to the study aims and target groups and can therefore vary.

Evidence gaps and evaluation challenges

Despite these positive findings, our analysis revealed critical evidence gaps, similar to those identified in broader interest-holder involvement research. Like Arnahoutova et al. [22], we found limited systematic evaluation of participatory processes. Only six of the 12 studies met the ICA requirements, and the variety of participatory approaches, methods and instruments led to no clear picture. However, positive effects of using participatory approaches for tailoring implementation strategies were reported in all the ICA-eligible studies, suggesting that different approaches can be suitable for different contexts. Three ICA studies [45, 49, 51] evaluated all the implementation steps and linked them to participatory approaches. However, no particular method seems to be linked to the success of tailoring. This point shows that flexible, context-oriented implementation strategies are key for implementation [11]. While decision-making complexity is a known challenge, participatory methods enhance the research relevance and community adoption [34]. This was also observed in our scoping review, particularly in studies with sustained interest-holder engagement across multiple implementation phases.

The selection of implementation strategies can be influenced by a variety of other factors, e.g., the environment, context, and needs [4]. Despite their importance, practical, feasible, and quality-tested systematic approaches to strategy selection remain scarce [2, 12]. Our review addresses this gap by identifying emerging implementation-specific participatory approaches, although further rigorous evaluation of the effectiveness of these approaches is needed.

Strengths and limitations

First, owing our focus on participatory approaches in tailoring implementation strategies, our research differs from typical “participatory research” as a research-to-action methodology that emphasizes active involvement throughout the complete research process [34]. Second, our search was limited to publications from 2019–2024 to focus on contemporary developments in this rapidly evolving field. While this approach enabled systematic mapping of recent, implementation-specific participatory approaches, it may have excluded relevant earlier work. Earlier foundational studies using participatory principles predate the specific focus on tailored implementation strategies. Future comprehensive reviews spanning longer timeframes could provide historical perspectives on the evolution of participatory approaches in implementation contexts. However, our review results might indicate that our topic became more common in recent years, supporting our temporal focus. Third, our requirement for the explicit labeling of participatory approaches may have led to the exclusion of studies that use participatory principles without naming them and potentially underestimated the prevalence of such studies. Fourth, we used an adapted ICA team approach because of the low number of studies that ultimately met the ICA criteria. All eligible studies reported positive effects, perhaps because of the high participation levels achieved. Nevertheless, publication bias cannot be excluded. Fifth, our ICA was necessarily descriptive and exploratory rather than analytical in the traditional sense, as the evidence base did not support causal inferences about which components drive successful outcomes. Thus, publication bias toward positive results cannot be excluded. Sixth, some study-related methodological limitations must be acknowledged. Only six of 129 studies met the ICA eligibility criteria, limiting the statistical power and generalizability. The heterogeneity of these studies precluded robust comparative analysis or identification of consistent success patterns. Therefore, our ICA findings should be understood as hypothesis-generating observations that map the current landscape rather than as definitive evidence of what works. Future research with larger sample sizes, standardized approaches, and comparative designs is essential to move from description to explanatory findings. Finally, grey literature was not considered because of the large number of total results, which was associated with the high degree of sensitivity of our search strategy and the varying quality of the literature.

Practical implications and directions for future research

Our findings have practical implications for researchers and healthcare organizations.

For researchers, the diversity of effective approaches suggests the importance of selecting context-appropriate methods over rigidly applying a specific approach. The limited number of studies with rigorous outcome evaluation highlights the need to plan systematic evaluations of (specific) participatory processes from project inception. For healthcare organizations, emerging implementation-specific approaches provide novel instruments for those seeking to adapt implementation strategies.

Future research priorities include: 1) systematic evaluation of (implementation-specific) participatory approaches across diverse contexts—only 6 of the 129 studies were eligible for ICA; 2) comparative studies of different participatory approaches to identify optimal methods for specific implementation contexts; 3) the development of approaches that maintain meaningful interest-holder engagement throughout all tailoring phases; 4) the economic evaluation of different participatory approaches to inform resource allocation decisions; and 5) long-term follow-up studies to assess whether participatory approaches lead to more sustainable implementation outcomes.

Conclusion

This scoping review revealed a wide variety of participatory approaches, methods and instruments for tailoring implementation strategies in healthcare. While both general and implementation-specific approaches emerged, the relatively high number of specific approaches suggests an increasing recognition of the need for specialized participatory frameworks in implementation contexts. However, the low number of studies showing measurable effects across all tailoring phases confirms the need for further research to strengthen the evidence base. Our review complements the literature on interest-holder involvement in implementation science by providing specific insights into participatory approaches for tailored implementation, thereby contributing to the evidence base that supports these approaches as a mechanism in the tailoring process rather than as general implementation strategies.
