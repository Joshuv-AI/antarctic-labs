Using stable isotopes in water vapor to study the interdependence of clouds,
 atmospheric aerosols, and precipitation processes

 Final Report for SC0023350

 Joseph Galewsky

 Department of Earth and Planetary Sciences

 University of New Mexico

 Albuquerque, NM, 871031

 galewsky@unm.edu

The core of this project focused on two complementary field studies that deployed water
vapor isotope measurements during major ARM campaigns to address fundamental
questions about boundary layer mixing, aerosol-cloud-precipitation interactions, and the
environmental controls on isotopic composition in coastal environments.

TRACER-iso: Quantifying Sea Breeze Mixing Processes

During the Tracking Aerosol Convection Interactions Experiment (TRACER) in La Porte,
Texas (June-September 2022), we deployed a Picarro L2130 water vapor isotope analyzer
to trace mixing processes in sea breeze circulations. TRACER's central objective was to
understand how Houston's varying aerosol loading affects convective cloud development,
with sea breeze circulations playing a critical role in transporting the urban aerosol plume
and triggering afternoon convection. Our isotope measurements provided a novel tracer for
quantifying the vertical and horizontal mixing that controls how aerosol-laden marine air
interacts with the continental boundary layer.

Using data from our colleagues at Brookhaven National Labs, we identified 46 sea breeze
events using multi-platform observations (GOES-16 satellite imagery, NEXRAD radar, and
ARM surface meteorology) and applied kernel-based change point detection to precisely
determine frontal passage times. The isotopic measurements revealed systematic shifts in
δD, δ¹⁸O, and deuterium excess during sea breeze onset, with composite analyses showing
characteristic temporal evolution of the isotopic composition relative to frontal passages.
These observations demonstrated that the cool, moist marine air mass maintains a distinct
isotopic signature as it penetrates inland, allowing us to quantify the degree of mixing with
the overlying continental air.

The novel contribution lies in using water vapor isotopes as a quantitative constraint on
mixing processes that govern aerosol transport. Traditional meteorological measurements
reveal the presence of the sea breeze front but provide limited information about the extent

of vertical mixing between the marine inflow layer and the air aloft. Our isotopic tracers
showed that the marine air undergoes substantial but incomplete mixing as it moves
inland, with implications for how aerosols are vertically redistributed before convective
initiation. We developed an isotope-enabled transilient matrix model that explicitly
represents non-local mixing and maintains conservation of isotopic ratios. This model
framework can be applied to other coastal ARM sites and provides a rigorous test of how
well boundary layer schemes in larger models represent mixing processes relevant to
aerosol transport.

EPCAPE: Information Theory Analysis of Coastal Boundary Layer Isotopes

During the Eastern Pacific Cloud Aerosol Precipitation Experiment (EPCAPE) at Scripps
Pier in La Jolla, California, we measured water vapor isotopes continuously and applied
information-theoretic methods to quantify environmental controls on deuterium excess.
EPCAPE focused on marine stratocumulus clouds and their interactions with aerosols in a
region where both clean marine and polluted continental air masses occur. Our work
addressed a fundamental question: what environmental factors control the isotopic
composition of boundary layer water vapor, and how does this vary between marine and
continental regimes?

We applied Shannon entropy and mutual information metrics to determine which
environmental variables contain predictive information about deuterium excess. This
approach goes beyond traditional regression by quantifying absolute limits of predictability
and identifying synergistic versus redundant relationships between variables. We found
that relative humidity with respect to sea surface temperature (RHS) is the dominant
predictor in marine conditions, explaining 27% of deuterium excess entropy, with strong
synergy when combined with SST (36% total explained variance). In continental
conditions, the system becomes less predictable (only 20% explained variance with optimal
predictors), and wind direction emerges as a critical variable, reflecting the importance of
air mass source regions.

The novel contribution is demonstrating that heteroscedasticity in isotope-environment
relationships is not statistical noise but rather a meaningful signal indicating regime shifts
in controlling processes. This has direct implications for interpreting water isotope
measurements in aerosol studies: the isotopic composition of water vapor depends not
only on local thermodynamic conditions but also on the history of air mass transport and
mixing. In marine conditions with strong ocean-atmosphere coupling, local SST and RH
control deuterium excess through surface evaporation, directly relevant to understanding
how aerosol loading modifies cloud droplet formation in the same thermodynamic
environment. In continental conditions, the weaker predictability reflects complex upwind

moisture sources and mixing histories, indicating that isotopic measurements can help
diagnose whether observed aerosol-cloud interactions occur in well-mixed local conditions
or in air masses with complex histories.

Integration and Broader Impacts

Both projects demonstrate that water vapor isotope measurements provide unique
constraints on boundary layer processes critical to aerosol-cloud-precipitation interactions.
The TRACER work showed how isotopes quantify mixing in sea breeze circulations that
control aerosol transport and convective initiation. The EPCAPE work revealed
fundamental regime-dependent controls on isotopic composition that reflect the same
environmental factors (mixing depth, stability, source region) that govern aerosol
distributions. The information-theoretic framework developed for EPCAPE is transferable
to other ARM sites and provides a rigorous method for quantifying which environmental
variables contain meaningful information about boundary layer processes.

We produced a comprehensive isotope-enabled modeling framework (the transilient
matrix model with explicit isotopologue tracking) that can be applied at other ARM sites
and incorporated into larger-scale models. The model maintains strict conservation
properties and properly represents the nonlinear mixing relationships inherent in isotope
physics. We also established that information theory provides quantitative measures of
predictability limits, revealing when and why certain environmental variables fail to explain
isotopic variability—a critical insight for both measurement strategy and model evaluation.

These projects delivered high-quality datasets archived at ARM data repositories,
developed novel analytical frameworks, and demonstrated the value of isotope
measurements as quantitative tracers of processes controlling aerosol-cloud-precipitation
interactions in coastal environments.
