Attitudes and beliefs regarding colorectal cancer screening among African American patients of community health centers in North-Central Florida and stakeholder perspectives: a qualitative study
Authors: John S Luque, Sabrina L Dickey, Deloria R Jackson, Onyx S Franklin, Matthew A Vargas, Ryan Mohorne, Gebre-Egziabher Kiros, Askal Ali, Temple Robinson, Bessie Duncan, Jeanne’ Freeman, Jaamela Howell-Murray, Clement K Gwede
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundColorectal cancer (CRC) is one of the leading causes of cancer mortality in the US with African Americans continuing to experience CRC health disparities due to numerous factors. While colonoscopy remains the most utilized test for CRC screening, stool-blood tests are cost-effective and offered by community health centers (CHC) serving un- and underinsured patients. Qualitative research has identified multilevel barriers to CRC screening and can potentially inform the development of tailored interventions to promote stool-based screening.MethodsIn this qualitative study, we explored the CHC context for delivering CRC screening services and patient education by interviewing six stakeholders of two north Florida CHCs and conducted six focus groups among African American patients of the two CHCs who had recently completed a CRC screening. CHC stakeholders were identified for interviews by their organizational roles delivering CRC screening services and focus group participants were recruited from our previous CRC screening behavioral clinical trial study group participant pool.ResultsCHC stakeholders assessed their organizations’ delivery of CRC screening services, which included stool-based tests and colonoscopy referrals, and discussed trends in the screening rates achieved by their organizations. CHC stakeholders spoke about their patient education and outreach within the community and offered ideas about how community-based CRC screening education could be improved. CHCs highly valued the community-academic partnership, identifying numerous mutually beneficial aspects and embraced the ongoing partnership as a way to increase CRC screening adherence among their patients. The focus group discussions can be grouped into three main themes. The first theme is healthcare attitudes and relationships with providers. This theme covered perceptions of seeking healthcare and communication and trust with providers. The second theme is CRC knowledge and prevention. This theme covered patients’ knowledge about CRC, what symptoms to monitor, knowing their family history of cancer, and the different screening tests. The third theme is technological and systematic issues in healthcare. These discussions focused on health insurance and accessing the patient portal.ConclusionsThis study demonstrated the value of community-engaged qualitative research on CRC screening delivery and completion in partnership with CHC stakeholders.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28346-5.Keywords: Colorectal cancer screening, Focus groups, Screening, Cancer prevention, African Americans

Background

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most common cancer when ranked among the leading causes of cancer-related mortality in the United States (US) [1]. Compared to other racial and ethnic populations in the US, African Americans continue to experience cancer health disparities for CRC incidence, mortality, and late-stage CRC diagnosis [2]. These differences in CRC outcomes have been attributed to access-to-care issues, treatment disparities, and social determinants of health [2, 3]. Because African Americans face disproportionate CRC rates, recommended guidelines from some professional organizations had been for African Americans to begin screening at age 45, before the guidelines were changed for all average-risk individuals to begin screening at that age because of the upward trend of early-onset CRC [4, 5].

CRC is a preventable disease for individuals who adhere to screening guidelines. The most utilized test for screening in the US is the colonoscopy, an invasive test wherein precancerous polyps can be removed during the screening examination. Non-invasive methods include several stool-based tests including the fecal immunochemical test (FIT) and stool DNA test. These are noninvasive, at-home tests that detect hidden blood in the stool and require annual screening or longer intervals depending on the test, and these tests are cost-effective and are recommended [6, 7]. Intervention research to increase rates of stool-based screening in African American populations has been conducted in clinical and community settings with mixed results [8].

Qualitative research has been used to explore perceptions, knowledge, and lived experiences related to colorectal cancer screening with members of African American communities in clinical and non-clinical settings to identify patient-centered barriers and inform culturally tailored interventions to improve screening uptake [9, 10]. Community health centers (CHC) offer an opportunity for under- and uninsured populations to access stool-based screening; however, rates of adherence nationally were reported at approximately 44% [11]. One focus group study conducted in CHCs in central Florida aimed to understand motivations and impediments to screening and to guide development of educational materials [12]. The study revealed limited CRC knowledge, fear of screening procedures, financial concerns, and lack of provider recommendations, alongside preferences for clear, engaging resources supporting stool-based screening such as the FIT [12]. Similarly, another study conducted in Los Angeles with African American patients recruited from a Veteran’s Administration health system used qualitative methods to examine attitudes and beliefs surrounding CRC screening in order to inform intervention development, and identified themes including perceived invasiveness of colonoscopy, fear of diagnosis, misinformation, and cost-related barriers [13]. A community-engaged research study in north-central Florida used focus groups to inform the development of a CRC screening educational intervention, and African Americans in the study provided feedback on culturally tailored CRC educational materials [14]. The focus groups assessed knowledge, attitudes, and beliefs about CRC screening, explored persistent misconceptions, discussed medical mistrust, and received patient input for testing motivational text messages that were one component of the community health advisor intervention [14, 15].

Other focus group studies have also used community-engaged research approaches. In a church-based study in Kentucky, focus group discussions with African Americans explored the barriers and facilitators to stool-based screening. Negative findings included low awareness, perceived test invasiveness, and negative healthcare experiences, and positive findings highlighted churches, community leaders, and healthcare providers as critical facilitators of engagement [16]. More recent approaches to collecting qualitative data have used virtual focus groups. A recent study conducted in New York City employed bilingual virtual focus groups with simultaneous translation to refine stool-based CRC screening educational messaging and address health disparities among Black immigrant and non-immigrant populations [17]. This study reported participants identified structural barriers to CRC screening such as language access and healthcare navigation challenges while emphasizing the need for culturally sensitive materials [17]. Collectively, these qualitative studies demonstrate how community-engaged research approaches can identify multilevel barriers to CRC screening and support the development of culturally tailored interventions, particularly those promoting stool-based testing for African Americans.

The aim of this qualitative study was to better understand the CHC context for implementing CRC screening education interventions among African Americans not up to date with screening in north-central Florida. This aim was accomplished through qualitative interviews with CHC stakeholders (leaders and staff of the CHCs) and focus group discussions with CHC patients who had completed CRC screening, most in the context of participating in our previous behavioral clinical trial. For context, the focus group participants were recruited from the pool of a previous clinical trial group which consisted of 93 African American study participants (55% female and 45% male), with an average age of 55 years, and with 62% of the sample reporting some college education or higher [15]. To be participants in the clinical trial, participants had to satisfy the following inclusion criteria: (1) 45–64 years of age; (2) identify as African American; (3) have a working cell phone; (4) be a Florida resident; (5) be non-adherent with CRC screening guidelines (i.e., no stool-based tests > 9 months, no colonoscopy within 9 years, and no flexible sigmoidoscopy within 4 years); and (6) no personal history of CRC, precancerous CRC polyps, or inflammatory bowel disease [15]. This study reports the analysis of these stakeholder interviews and focus group discussions.

Methods

Overview

This qualitative study employed focus groups with patients and individual interviews with CHC stakeholders from two different CHCs to collect data on patients’ CRC screening experiences and stakeholder’s perceptions of their respective CRC screening programs. This IRB-approved study was conducted concurrently with our quantitative survey study monitoring CRC screening in a cohort of CHC patients [18]. Our qualitative project aim was to describe the CHC context for implementing CRC screening education interventions among African American CHC patients in North Florida. There were two research objectives of the qualitative research. The first research objective was to identify the facilitators and challenges for CHCs to implement stool-based screening programs and to identify the obstacles they encountered in referring patients to follow-up colonoscopy when indicated. We asked CHC stakeholders to describe their screening implementation strategies and best practices. The second research objective was to understand how the university-community partnership functioned in the research project to facilitate the implementation of successful CRC screening programs. The university-community partnership began in 2019 between the university-based academic researchers and two key representatives from each of the CHCs. The partnership was maintained through quarterly video conference meetings as well as in-person meetings between our research project staff and CHC staff. The research grants budgeted for consultant fees to be paid to each CHC for their time and effort in the partnership. For the stakeholder interviews, the discussion questions asked about the major challenges for CHCs to implement stool-based screening for their patients. These two research objectives were addressed in our qualitative interviews with patients about their CRC screening experiences and in our interviews with CHC stakeholders about their CRC screening programs and partnership with our university research.

Focus group guide

We developed a focus group discussion guide for use with African American CHC patients specifically for this study. Questions in the guide covered barriers and facilitators to healthcare access, community assets, cancer education and screening needs, current screening practices, access to follow-up care, and attitudes and beliefs toward CRC screening. We also developed a semi-structured interview guide to interview community stakeholders. For the stakeholder interviews with community organizations, CHC leaders (e.g., CEOs and medical directors) and clinical staff responded to questions covering expectations to partner with researchers, roles for community partners in research, mechanisms for working together, and methods to measure accomplishments [19]. The interview instrument was comprised of four sections: (1) organization characteristics; (2) perceived priorities and community needs; (3) CRC screening capacity and goals; and (4) qualitative evaluation of the expectations of the community partnership. Both the focus group discussion guide and the semi-structured interview guide are available as Additional file 1: Questionnaires. All research participants received a $30 gift card incentive.

Recruitment

The study team recruited African American CHC patients who had participated in our quantitative survey study as a follow-up survey to a previous clinical trial and had received CRC screening in the last year to attend one of six focus groups. We recruited three focus groups with patients from each of the two CHCs. Our aim was to recruit six to eight participants per focus group according to recommended practice [20]. All focus groups were held in person at a health department conference room. We also virtually interviewed six CHC stakeholders about their CRC screening programs.

Data collection

The stakeholder interviews were administered via the Zoom video conferencing platform and recorded. The interviews were conducted by the lead investigator with assistance from the project coordinator. The leaders of each CHC were interviewed first, followed by the other CHC stakeholders. Each interview was completed virtually by taking notes on the questionnaire. The interviews lasted approximately 30 to 40 min. The focus groups with CHC patients were conducted in person, digitally recorded, and professionally transcribed verbatim.

Data analysis

Data from the CHC stakeholder interviews were pooled and summarized to identify similarities and differences in responses. Responses to the open-ended stakeholder questions were analyzed and pooled by question domain to assess the content and identify common and unique partner needs. The focus groups transcripts were imported into MAXQDA (Marburg, Germany) qualitative data analysis software, coded by two study investigators (JL and SD), and then reviewed by the research team in biweekly meetings. The two coders coded the six focus group transcripts independently using the codebook created by the first coder. Four new codes were created by the second coder and the first coder needed to return to code the six transcripts with the new codes. Qualitative data were analyzed by the primary coder using a content analysis technique, which identifies emergent themes, or trends, from the interview transcript and a codebook was created. The primary coder has background and training in computer-assisted qualitative data analysis and has worked with MAXQDA software for multiple research projects to create codebooks and analyze qualitative data. Analysis of data includes a “mechanical” stage (organizing and dividing the data into a useful scheme), and an “interpretive” stage (identifying criteria for organizing the thematic codes) [21]. The objective of this procedure is to corroborate interpretations and minimize the effect of any subjective interpretation of the data. Following the deductive coding approach using the codebook, the two coders met to resolve discrepancies identified using the intercoder agreement tool in MAXQDA. Following the meetings to review codes with lower intercoder agreement, the average intercoder agreement for all codes reached a level greater than 85%.

Results

Stakeholder interviews

For the CHC stakeholder interviews, there were six participants, two representatives from one CHC partner and four from our other CHC partner. Directors of both CHCs participated and other participants included quality assurance personnel and clinical staff. We prioritized interviewing CHC colleagues who had key roles in the partnership and regularly participated in quarterly meetings. Participants described the services that their organization provides to patients and the wider community, discussed the state of CRC screening services they provide, and articulated their views about the research partnership in terms of their experiences and their thoughts on how the academic-community partnership could be further developed and maintained.

We first asked CHC partners to check off what were the main services they provided from a long list of services we had in our questionnaire. One partner stated that the three main services they provide are primary care, dental care, and obstetrician-gynecologist (OBGYN) care, while our other partner named primary care, dental care and HIV/AIDS care as their main services. CHC partners’ patient populations are characterized by many socio-economic hardships as evidenced by a wide range of supporting services they provide. In addition to medical services and referrals, behavioral health care and cancer screening, CHC partners provide case management, health education, legal services, outreach/mobile health care, social support, housing assistance, advocacy and training. Partners said that the top issues in the community concerning cancer included lack of follow-through with cancer screening and lack of access to specialty care providers, especially for uninsured patients. Specific types of cancer identified as top concerns included CRC, and cancers of the breast, prostate, cervix, lung, blood, and lymphatic system.

Regarding barriers among their patients and wider community to cancer education and awareness, CHC colleagues identified low health literacy, lack of cancer education resources, and apathy. Perspectives on how cancer education and outreach could be improved included increasing CHCs’ presence in the community and utilizing cancer testimonies from peers/survivors in the community. Partners were asked to rate from 1 (poor) to 5 (exemplary) their patients’ awareness about USPSTF CRC screening recommendations. On this topic, one CHC assigned a rating of 2.5 and the other a rating of 3, with both CHCs citing low health literacy as the primary factor. CHCs also rated, from 1 to 5, their organization’s “track record in providing patients with a CRC stool kit,” and both partners assigned a rating of 4 to their organization’s performance ensuring patients the opportunity to complete the CRC stool test. Interview participants cited numerous reasons for rating themselves highly in providing the CRC screening kit, which included tracking patients’ CRC screening in their electronic health records systems, educating providers, and utilizing multiple options to deliver the screening kit, namely, in-person and by mail; and in some cases, administering the screening in the clinic. CHC partners noted improving trends regarding their CRC screening rates. One partner stated they have increased CRC screening rates over the past three years to 49% of their patients, surpassing their goal of 45%, and noted that they plan to update their goal. The other partner set a goal of 40% of their patients being up to date with CRC screening and reported their current screening completion rate to be 23%, and they indicated to us that their rates had been improving. As to how their organizations could improve CRC screening completion rates in their patient populations, interviewees said that having dedicated staff, expanding patient education, including utilizing a colon teaching model, and doing outreach through the CHC’s mobile unit could all be effective. One partner thought that using social media to explain “in a fun way why it’s important to get this done” could be an effective outreach strategy.

Interviewees provided favorable assessments of the community research partnership. CHC representatives said the study team provided avenues to keep them engaged with their patient populations, kept them informed on current research and said the partnership enhanced credibility within the community. Communication between the study team and CHCs was commended. Increasing collaboration such as holding community educational meetings or “town halls” was suggested as a way of strengthening the community-academic partnership. Thoughts on how to improve community research capacities included increasing collaboration by establishing a liaison between partners, involving the research team in on-site activities, developing a residency program and considering a “citizen scientist approach” to better understand community needs and how to best serve the community. When using the term “citizen scientist” the stakeholders were referring to training they might receive from university mini-courses and outreach with community members on community-engaged research and research ethics. In looking forward to what the partnership could accomplish in three years, both partners hoped that the partnership would positively impact CRC screening rates.

Focus group discussions

Demographics

There were six focus groups with a total of 26 participants. Focus groups 1 and 2 had six participants each. Focus groups 3, 4, and 5 each had four participants each. Focus group 6 only had two participants due to last-minute cancellations. The focus groups were held between April 2024 and June 2025. Participants ranged between 46 and 65 years of age, with an average age of 57 years. There were eight male participants and 18 female participants. Seven of the participants were married, 13 were single, three were divorced, and three were widowed. The sample were educated, with 18 participants (69%) having completed high school or higher education. Most participants reported they had healthcare coverage (81%) and had a regular doctor (92%). Many participants reported living in a home (65%), compared to an apartment (27%) or a trailer (8%). Participants were asked to list the date and location of their most recent colonoscopy, and 14 participants listed a recent date and the location, which was predominantly the major public hospital. In addition, 21 participants listed the date and location of their most recent stool test, and most listed the CHC where they were a patient. Five of the participants lived in a neighboring county to Leon County (Table 1).

Healthcare seeking

Participants described healthcare seeking behaviors that require persistence, self-advocacy, and ongoing engagement with the medical system. Several participants expressed concern that the quality of care they received depended heavily on their insurance coverage. One individual noted that when her insurance changed, so did the level of attention and responsiveness she experienced from her providers, suggesting that insurance status can shape access to thorough and timely care. Participants also emphasized the need to be proactive in managing their own healthcare. For example, one participant named Yolanda explained, “staying on top of their doctors” to ensure they were not overlooked or treated as just another number, reiterating a belief that patients must advocate for themselves to receive high quality attention (Focus Group 2). Others highlighted the necessity of keeping providers informed, such as regularly updating medication lists, which help clinicians deliver safer and more accurate care. Taken together, these reflections show that patients view healthcare seeking as an active process, influenced by insurance barriers, provider responsiveness, and the patient’s own willingness to push for needed information and follow-up.

Healthcare provider communication

Across focus group discussions, participants highlighted both the value and the limitations of healthcare provider communication. Some individuals described taking an active role in preparing for appointments, gathering pamphlets, conducting personal research, and documenting questions to ensure meaningful dialogue with their providers and specialists. For example, one participant made the point, “I make sure I dictate everything I need to dictate down where that they would know before I get to the back of the room, in the room with them” (Focus Group 4). This preparation reflects a desire for collaborative communication and the need to compensate for gaps in the information they receive. At the same time, several participants expressed concern that providers do not always explain medical issues thoroughly or emphasize the importance of routine exams and preventative care, suggesting a perceived lack of depth or attentiveness in clinician–patient interactions. Others described instances in which providers did communicate about additional health concerns, such as weight related risks during procedures, illustrating how conversations can broaden beyond the immediate issue to address overall health. Together, these discussions on this topic revealed that while some patients engage proactively to enhance communication, many feel that healthcare providers need to offer clearer, more comprehensive explanations to support informed, confident decision-making.

Trust/relationship with healthcare providers

Across the focus group discussions, participants strongly emphasized the importance of trust and long-term relationships with their healthcare providers. Several participants described staying with the same doctor for years, sometimes decades because of familiarity and continuity, making them feel understood, supported, and confident in the care they received. For example, a female participant named Maria said, “well, I trust my doctor. I’ve been with him over 20 years, so I trust him, you know? He’s real strict with diet and all that, but I trust him, his opinion, and I research stuff on Google when I don’t know, like meds and stuff, and if it’s something to do with the colon or something of that sort. He gave me good advice” (Focus Group 4). This trust was reinforced by providers who communicated clearly, explained procedures thoroughly, and consistently invited questions to ensure patient understanding. Participants also expressed discomfort with frequently switching doctors, noting that it disrupted continuity of care, created confusion around medical histories and medications, and made it harder to build a reliable therapeutic relationship. Overall, the discussions under this code illustrate that trust and stable, long-standing relationships with healthcare providers are vital to patients’ sense of comfort, confidence, and satisfaction in their care.

Fear of doctors

While many participants reported good communication with medical providers, participants also described fear as a barrier to seeking medical care, particularly for cancer screenings such as colonoscopies and prostate exams. This fear was often rooted in cultural experiences and community narratives, especially among African Americans, who may associate medical visits with discomfort, vulnerability, or invasive procedures. For some participants, these fears stemmed from earlier stages of life, shaped by misconceptions or a sense of mistrust, yet it was acknowledged that overcoming these fears is essential for long-term health and survival.

Other participants emphasized that the fear is not necessarily of doctors themselves, but of the potential bad news that medical visits might reveal. More specifically, some individuals may avoid screenings simply to avoid hearing a serious diagnosis, illustrating how fear of the unknown can outweigh the perceived benefits of early detection. One participant opined, “I said some people are scared to go get colon cancer screening – go and get checked, ‘cause they don’t want to hear no bad news” (Focus Group 3). Additionally, several participants highlighted gendered dimensions of fear, observing that men are reluctant to seek care. One participant explained many men prefer not to know if something is wrong, which can delay diagnosis and treatment.

Together, these perspectives show how emotional and psychological barriers such as fear of procedures, fear of bad news, and fear rooted in cultural context shape healthcare seeking behaviors. They highlight the need for supportive communication, community education, and reassurance to help individuals understand that screenings are tools for prevention and longevity rather than sources of harm.

Cancer illness communication

Within the discussions about cancer and illness communication, participants highlighted how conversations with both family members and healthcare providers shaped their understanding of cancer risk and screening. One participant named Nikki described relying on these conversations to interpret her own risk, drawing on family histories of polyps and symptoms to make sense of what screening results might mean for them personally. She had expressed concern about the possibility of missed warning signs or errors in care, emphasizing the importance of clear, proactive communication from healthcare providers about screening timelines and results. She commented, “I just don’t want it to be an oversight because of somebody dropped the ball and then I’m sitting up in here and having issues and then it’ll be too late” (Focus Group 2). This participant’s narrative reflects how individuals use dialogue with loved ones and clinicians to navigate uncertainty, monitor their health, and advocate for timely, accurate cancer screening.

Knowledge about cancer

Throughout the focus group discussions, participants expressed a wide range of beliefs about how cancer develops, reflecting both biological understanding and culturally shaped explanations. Some participants described cancer in clinical terms, emphasizing how it spreads and leads to deterioration once it reaches an advanced stage. Others attributed cancer to spiritual or moral causes, such as sin or not “living your life right,” demonstrating how personal or religious frameworks can shape interpretations of illness. Many participants also identified lifestyle factors such as diet, tobacco use, alcohol consumption, or being overweight as contributors to cancer risk, aligning more closely with established public health messaging. In addition, several individuals voiced concerns about environmental exposures, particularly contaminants in food, pesticides on vegetables, or genetically modified ingredients, as possible drivers of cancer. Together, these perspectives illustrate a diverse mix of scientific, religious, and environmental explanations that influence participants’ understanding of cancer, shaping how they think about risk, prevention, and personal vulnerability.

Family history of cancer

Participants frequently described extensive family histories of cancer, highlighting how personal loss and generational patterns shape their understanding of risk and motivate screening behavior. Many participants shared stories of parents, siblings, and grandparents who had died from various cancers including colon, stomach, ovarian, breast, and prostate cancer, which emphasizes the emotional weight and immediacy of these experiences. These losses often served as powerful reminders that cancer “runs in the family,” prompting individuals to take screening more seriously or begin it earlier than recommended.

Participants also described how witnessing a loved one’s diagnosis, sometimes discovered too late for treatment, triggered family awareness and led relatives to seek screening together. For example, one participant described family members who had died from cancer by stating, “And then, I think my granddaddy died, 25 years ago, I think he had colon cancer. So, that’s very strong in our family, on both sides” (Focus Group 1). Others spoke about actively monitoring their own health because of a strong family history, with some needing more frequent screening due to increased risk. Overall, these accounts illustrate how family experiences with cancer create a sense of vulnerability, urgency, and responsibility, reinforcing the importance of early detection and influencing personal decisions about CRC screening.

CRC symptoms

Across these conversations, participants identified a range of symptoms they associated with CRC. Many of the symptoms mentioned were gastrointestinal in nature, including diarrhea, constipation, dark or bloody stools, rectal bleeding, and persistent abdominal discomfort. Some participants described pain or difficulty during bowel movements as signs that something more serious might be occurring. Other symptoms referred to broader health issues, such as immune system problems or the impact of smoking, as potentially related to cancer risk. Several stories indicated how symptoms can be misunderstood or overlooked by both patients and providers. One participant named Paris described how her mother experienced a hardened abdomen and repeatedly sought care, “going back and forth to the doctor,” only to be misdiagnosed until she insisted on further evaluation further highlighting the danger of attributing symptoms to benign causes (Focus Group 6). Overall, the discussions illustrated that while patients can identify some key CRC symptoms, there is also confusion, under-recognition, and frustration tied to symptom interpretation, reinforcing the need for clearer education and more attentive clinical evaluation.

Knowledge about CRC prevention

Participants expressed a variety of beliefs about CRC prevention, emphasizing both lifestyle practices and the critical role of early detection. Several participants identified healthy behavior such as maintaining a balanced diet, exercising regularly, and reducing consumption of fried foods, greasy foods, and red meat as important preventive steps. For example, one participant explained, “Stay away from red meat, stay away from steaks and red meat” (Focus Group 1). Participants also discussed the challenges and nuances of dietary guidance, noting that while fruits and vegetables are often recommended, certain health conditions may limit what people can safely eat. Alongside lifestyle changes, early detection emerged as a central and repeatedly emphasized theme. Participants stressed the importance of not delaying appointments, getting evaluated promptly when something feels wrong, and adhering to recommended screening schedules. Some felt strongly that screening should begin earlier than age 45, citing rising rates of cancer in younger individuals and personal or family experiences that heightened their sense of vulnerability. Others highlighted the preventive benefits of colonoscopy itself, specifically the ability to remove polyps before they progress to cancer. Collectively, these perspectives reflect a broad understanding of CRC prevention that integrates personal responsibility, health education, and timely medical intervention, while also emphasizing ongoing concerns about whether current screening guidelines adequately address average risk individuals.

Recommendations for CRC screening

Within the focus group discussions participants offered clear recommendations for CRC screening, emphasizing both the need for earlier screening and the common tendency to delay it despite knowing its importance. Several individuals felt strongly that screening should begin at younger ages, especially when there is a family history of polyps or cancer. One participant held the view that screening should occur earlier, “I think it need to be younger, ‘cause even for my brother who had quite a few colonoscopies, you know, I know if I’m in my – well, he’s just a couple years younger than me.” (Focus Group 2). Personal experiences, such as witnessing a sibling undergo colonoscopies at a young age, highlighted how early issues can go unnoticed or unspoken within families, reinforcing the belief that screening guidelines may not adequately reflect actual risk. In addition, participants acknowledged how easy it is to postpone screening, citing shifting recommended ages, lack of symptoms, and fear related to the procedure or anesthesia. These delays illustrate how practical barriers and emotional hesitations can overshadow intentions to follow recommended guidelines. Despite this, participants were united in recognizing the lifesaving potential of early detection. One participant captured the overarching message succinctly: screening and early detection can save lives. Taken together, these reflections highlight a strong belief in the necessity of earlier, proactive CRC screening and the importance of clear messaging and support to encourage timely participation.

CRC screening (colonoscopies and stool tests)

Participants discussed their experiences, challenges, and preferences related to CRC screening, reflecting a wide range of familiarity and comfort with both colonoscopies and stool-based tests. For some, family history played a strong motivating role. Yolanda described how her parents’ and brother’s colonoscopies, along with her father’s discovery of longstanding polyps prompted her to seek her own screening, reinforcing how witnessing a loved one’s diagnosis or preventive care can influence personal decisions (Focus Group 2). Yet even for those who were motivated, insurance barriers sometimes complicated access. For instance, another participant struggled to obtain a referral for a colonoscopy, despite recommendations from her mother’s physician, because her own provider insisted on completing a stool test first.

Awareness of screening options varied considerably. While colonoscopies were familiar to most, stool tests like the Cologuard were less well known. Yet one participant offered the opinion, “I’d rather do Cologuard than a colonoscopy” (Focus Group 2). One participant noted that she had heard of colon cancer but not of the stool test, highlighting gaps in knowledge that may affect screening uptake. Participants also expressed mixed feelings about colonoscopy preparation, often describing it as burdensome or unpleasant. Several participants detailed the discomfort of drinking large volumes of prep solution and fasting, while another participant shared that not following instructions led to rescheduling his procedure. Others reported never ever having undergone a colonoscopy and relying exclusively on annual stool tests.

Participants also weighed the advantages and limitations of different screening methods. Some preferred the noninvasive stool test but recognized that Cologuard may not detect everything and may not replace a colonoscopy when symptoms or risk factors are present. At the conclusion of the focus groups, the researchers explained that the participants should consult with their doctor about diagnostic testing if symptoms were present. Others appreciated the reassurance of annual stool testing, as one participant stated that she looked forward to receiving her kit each year for peace of mind. However, logistical issues such as delays in receiving results occasionally undermine confidence in the process. However, positive experiences existed, with one participant noting that her Cologuard testing was consistent and well-coordinated, “I do the Cologuard every year. And they had the date right and everything - Oh, you’ve got to wait two more weeks. And we’ll send it out in two weeks” (Focus Group 3). Overall, these perspectives reflect diverse experiences with CRC screening, highlighting the influence of family history, insurance coverage, awareness, test preparation burdens, and the perceived reliability and convenience of different screening methods.

Spread the word about CRC screening

Across these focus groups, participants emphasized the need for more widespread, accessible, and consistent messaging about CRC screening. Many felt that colon cancer is discussed far less than other cancers, such as breast cancer, creating a gap in public awareness. Participants recommended expanding communication beyond clinical settings and making information more visible in everyday environments. Suggestions included more frequent public service announcements on radio and television, increased signage in doctors’ offices, and broader use of online videos or DVDs to deliver clear, engaging content. One participant explained, “Like seeing the doctor’s office or somewhere. You know, just like constant hearing, on the radio, public service announcements. TV” (Focus Group 6). Others noted that effective outreach may require incentives such as gift cards, to draw people into educational events. Participants also highlighted structural barriers, particularly transportation challenges, and recommended distributing flyers or booklets at transit hubs to reach individuals who rely on buses or have difficulty attending appointments. Collectively, these reflections express a strong desire for diverse, accessible, and persistent outreach strategies to help “spread the word” and ensure that more people are informed about the importance of CRC screening.

Health insurance

Participants highlighted how health insurance plays a powerful and often limiting role in their ability to seek care, access screening, and manage chronic conditions. Many described stark differences between insurance plans, noting that some required significant out-of-pocket costs while others covered transportation, appointments, and procedures with little to no expense. These comparisons shaped participants’ choices and loyalty to certain plans, as well as their frustration with others that created financial strain.

Participants also discussed how race and insurance status intersect, noting that African Americans are often stereotyped as lacking adequate coverage. This assumption, they explained, can influence how they are treated from the moment they walk into a clinic, contributing to feelings of bias and inequity in healthcare settings. One participant noted, “When you walk into a doctor’s office, the first thing they say - Do you have any insurance? - you’re not even giving me a chance. You don’t even know me and you’re already stereotyping me” (Focus Group 2). Concerns about fairness extended to screening access. For example, several individuals emphasized that people without insurance, especially older adults, still deserve opportunities to be tested for cancer, and that more programs are needed to support those who cannot afford it. The complexity of navigating insurance systems was another recurring issue. Some participants described losing coverage unexpectedly, disrupting their ability to attend appointments or obtain medications for serious health conditions.

Others shared stories of finally securing insurance and, for the first time, being able to receive recommended CRC screening tests. Still, even with coverage, costs could remain prohibitive; moreover, some had to switch plans entirely to make colonoscopy affordable. For example, Leon explained, “Some insurances I had, they weren’t covered. Or they covered it, but it was too expensive to do. So, I had to go get a whole other insurance for them to cover it and lower the cost of it. ‘Cause the first time I had it I had insurance, and I had to pay almost $600 just to get a colonoscopy” (Focus Group 3).

Overall, these discussions made clear that insurance is not just a background factor, but a central determinant of whether participants can access preventative care, receive timely treatment, and maintain ongoing relationships with their providers. The discussions revealed a strong desire for more equitable coverage options, clearer pathways to low-cost screening, and healthcare experiences that do not depend so heavily on the type of insurance a person carries.

Patient portal

Discussions about the patient portal occurred in two of the focus groups. Participants discussed both the benefits and the initial challenges of using the patient portal as part of their healthcare experience. Some described a learning curve, noting that it took time to become comfortable with digital communication because they preferred traditional, face-to-face interactions. However, once they began using the portal, they recognized its value and the convenience of sending questions, uploading documents or photos, and receiving timely responses from their care team. Other participants emphasized the portal’s usefulness in consolidating health information, such as lab results, immunization records, and documentation from different providers and clinics. Together, these discussions showed that while patients may be hesitant at first, the patient portal ultimately enhances access to information, supports faster communication, and offers a centralized space to manage aspects of their healthcare.

Faith, God, and healing

Participants frequently described Christian faith as a central source of comfort, strength, and healing, influencing how they understood illness and approached their health. Several individuals emphasized relying on God more than their medical treatments. For example, one participant explained that she entrusted her pain to God rather than to medication, expressing a deep belief that divine healing was more powerful than anything a doctor could offer. Another participant shared a similar sentiment, recalling advice from his mother and grandmother that taking God with you alleviates fear, particularly in moments of health uncertainty. One participant spoke of trust in God to stay healthy, “My cholesterol had got up a little bit, so I’m now taking cholesterol drugs – to lower my cholesterol … but I mostly trust God to keep me healthy, and I speak the word over my life. So, I don’t accept no sickness, and everything that I did came back good, like the mammogram, all that, because I prayed to God, and I speak the word with my life” (Focus Group 4).

Christian faith also played a significant role when participants or their loved ones experienced serious illnesses. One participant spoke emotionally about her brother’s recovery from recurring cancer, giving thanks and praise to God for his improved health and describing his progress as evidence of divine intervention. Others connected health and spirituality more broadly. One participant suggested that sickness can be linked to sin or unresolved emotional burdens, highlighting a belief that spiritual wellbeing is intertwined with physical health. She also described her own health practices as guided by her Christian faith, noting that although she takes certain medications, she primarily trusts God to sustain her health and uses prayer and scripture as part of her personal healing process. Collectively, these reflections illustrate the profound role of Christian faith in shaping how participants interpret illness, cope with pain, support loved ones, and engage with the healthcare system.

Discussion

This qualitative study adds to the literature on African American CHC patients’ knowledge, attitudes, and beliefs about CRC and CRC screening, and the experiences that shaped their views on accessing healthcare. In addition, by collecting the CHC stakeholder perspective, the study provides context for both patient and provider concerns around CRC screening. CHC stakeholders highlighted challenges with encouraging their patients to access screening but also lauded their own efforts to increase screening rates as part of their quality improvement efforts. Researchers asked focus group participants to elaborate on their experiences with CRC screening, barriers and benefits of screening, knowledge around cancer and CRC, and communication with healthcare providers. The results suggested that patients preferred the stool-based tests to colonoscopy; however, some patients also reported challenges in receiving a colonoscopy due to barriers such as insurance coverage, discomfort with preparation or incorrect preparation for the exam, which is similar to past research [12, 13].

The focus group participants valued the CHC they attended as their medical home. They also developed strong communication and relationships with their healthcare providers. While their relationships and communication with their doctors were generally positive, several participants also reported a fear of doctors when it comes to screening tests out of the worry about receiving a positive screening test. One theme that runs throughout the focus group discussions is fear and worry around a cancer diagnosis. These emotions may be connected to some participant’s belief that screening should start at an earlier age than 45 or 50 years old per patient guideline recommendations. However, most participants were unaware of the change in guidelines or did not know the recommended age to begin screening, a finding common in other studies [17]. Because the lay population may not completely understand the rationale for how screening guidelines are generated based on the available data and cost/benefit analysis, the statements that screening should occur at earlier ages may be tied to anecdotal evidence of a friend or family member who was diagnosed at an earlier age. In addition, family history plays a role, since those participants with a family history may exhibit higher levels of perceived susceptibility to the disease [22]. Another factor discussed that could mitigate fear and worry was the importance of Christian faith and how faith in God could serve as a source of comfort, strength, and healing in the face of illness,

Toward the end of the focus groups, the researchers introduced a tailored CRC education brochure with photos of local community members. Participants discussed that there were not many discussions happening in the community about CRC and there needed to be more information in the community accessible in public places and healthcare facilities. Having educational materials that are culturally appropriate and contain patient testimonials can be more effective to elicit behavior change [8, 12].

There were some limitations to the research as a qualitative focus group study. Since the study was qualitative, the study sample was small and the findings are not generalizable since the data are only representative of the individuals interviewed, yet the findings are transferable in the sense that focus group discussions would likely be similar in other CHC patient contexts in the US South. The sample was very representative of the types of patients these two CHCs serve. The focus groups also served as educational boosters or reinforcement for the importance of regular CRC screening for a group of participants who had participated in a previous educational intervention that was part of the larger study.

Conclusions

This study has demonstrated the value of a qualitative approach for engaging CHC patients and stakeholders in discussions on the importance of CRC screening and disease prevention. Through a community-engaged approach, the researchers recruited CHC patients who had participated in a previous behavioral clinical trial to gather their perspectives on their experiences of CRC screening and identify some of the challenges of obtaining follow-up colonoscopies. Because of a shortage of gastroenterology practices willing or able to cover the costs of the colonoscopy procedure for uninsured patients, some patients needed to wait until their health insurance could cover the costs, since paying out of pocket could translate into a bill for thousands of dollars. Ongoing healthcare policy efforts for Medicaid expansion in Florida, which would also possibly expand CRC screening coverage, could help to cover the gap in coverage for some of these patients. The types of insights gathered in the qualitative research provided rich description of the challenges this group of patients experience in seeking healthcare. The concurrent quantitative survey research indicated that patients with higher scores on a doctors’ mistrust scale were less likely to be adherent to stool-based testing; however, the qualitative focus group study identified a range of opinions and factors that impacted patients’ ability to receive CRC screening [18]. Future research aims to continue to partner with these CHCs to identify educational opportunities to reduce barriers and increase opportunities for CRC screening with these patients.
