The impact of T cell exhaustion in hematopoietic stem cell transplantation
Authors: Yue Li, Huidong Guo, Yikai Zhang, Jie Chen, Liye Zhong, Xiaojun Huang, Yangqiu Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
T cell exhaustion is a hallmark of failure to control cancer and chronic infection. In hematological malignancies, T cell exhaustion not only relates to disease progression but also impacts the outcome of hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT), which may be due to a low graft-versus-leukemia (GVL) effect and a low anti-virus infection capacity. However, a deeper mechanistic understanding of the impact of T cell exhaustion in HSCT remains to be characterized. In this review, we summarize the characteristics of T cell exhaustion, functional alterations in T cells post-transplantation, the role of T cell exhaustion in post-transplant complications, and related therapeutic strategies.

Introduction

T cells are essential for immunoregulation, antimicrobial defense, and anticancer immunity [1]. Cytotoxic CD8+ effector T cells (Teff) eliminate pathogens through cytokine secretion and direct cytotoxic activity against infected cells, while long-lived memory CD8+ T cells enhance protection against reinfection with both subsets being critical for maintaining organismal homeostasis [2]. In chronic infections, Teff cells directly suppress pathogen replication, whereas in autoimmune disorders, regulatory T cells (Tregs) maintain immune homeostasis by suppressing excessive inflammatory responses [3]. However, prolonged antigen exposure or persistent inflammatory stimulation can induce T cell exhaustion, characterized by sustained upregulation of inhibitory receptors (e.g., Programmed Cell Death Protein 1 (PD-1) and T cell Immunoglobulin and Mucin-Domain Containing-3 (TIM-3)), progressive loss of effector function (e.g., IFN-γ secretion and cytotoxic activity), and impaired memory differentiation potential [4]. This functional impairment directly compromises the immune system’s capacity to control disease. In solid tumors, exhausted tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) lose their cytotoxic activity, facilitating tumor immune escape [5]. Increasing data have also shown a high percentage of exhausted CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in leukemia patients, which is associated with poor clinical outcome [6, 7]. There is direct evidence that donor lymphocyte infusion (DLI) can help leukemia patients who relapse after hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) (Some experts suggest that it should be referred to as: hematopoietic cell transplantation (HCT)) [8] achieve complete remission, and the underlying mechanism is the DLI-mediated reversal of exhausted T cell (Tex) status and restoration of the GVL effect in relapse patients. Additionally, blockage of inhibitory receptors, which is called immune checkpoint blockade (ICB), can restore T cell function and enhance anti-tumor responses [9].

T cell exhaustion significantly limits the efficacy of immunotherapies such as chimeric antigen receptor T cell (CAR-T) therapy and is associated with relapse in leukemia patients after HSCT [10, 11]. Following allogeneic-HSCT (allo-HSCT), persistent host allo-antigens on non-hematopoietic host cells lead to prolonged exposure of donor T cells, which promotes the differentiation of donor T cells into exhausted T (Tex) cells, consequently reducing their attack on host tissues and lowering the risk of graft-versus-host disease (GVHD) [12, 13]. Similarly, in patients with relapsed acute myeloid leukemia (AML) post-transplantation, bone marrow CD8+ T cells highly express exhaustion markers such as PD-1, CTLA-4, TIM-3, LAG-3, 2B4, KLRG1, and GITR. Overall, Tex cells impact post-transplant immune reconstitution and promote tumor recurrence [14, 15].

T cell exhaustion

Under conditions of chronic antigen stimulation, such as chronic infection, cancer, and autoimmune disease, T cells progressively lose their normal immune function. This state, characterized by diminished proliferative capacity, weakened effector functions, and increased expression of inhibitory receptors, is termed T cell exhaustion [16]. Initially, researchers used a murine model of chronic lymphocytic choriomeningitis virus (LCMV) infection and found that when these mice are infected with LCMV and persistently exposed to viral antigens, virus-specific T cells gradually lose their effector function, leading to the definition of this Tex population [17, 18]. T cell exhaustion represents a distinct stage in T cell differentiation, rendering the cells incapable of providing effective immune protection. The primary manifestations of T cell exhaustion include reduced cytotoxicity, decreased cytokine production, and impaired antigen-specific proliferative capacity [19]. Importantly, Tex function is not completely abolished during the exhausted phase; rather, they exhibit progressive impairment in phenotype and function, resulting in diminished activity and functional insufficiency within immune responses [20]. For example, this functional downregulation can be partially reversed by administering immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) [10, 21, 22].

The features of Texs are characterized in several aspects: (1) Sustained high expression of inhibitory receptors: The most prominent characteristic of Texs is high co-expression of multiple inhibitory receptors (immune checkpoint molecules), which form an “inhibitory receptor repertoire.” Key receptors include [23]: (1) PD-1, which binds to its ligands PD-L1/PD-L2 and transmits inhibitory signals that suppress T cell proliferation, cytokine production, and cytotoxicity [24], (2) TIM-3, which interacts with its ligand galectin-9 to induce apoptosis in helper T cells (Th1/Th17), dampening immune responses and thereby promoting tumor cell proliferation and survival [25], (3) LAG-3, which mediates T cell suppression primarily through interaction with major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class II molecule [26], (4) CTLA-4, which competitively binds to CD80/CD86 and attenuates co-stimulatory signals limiting T cell activation and expansion [27], (5) TIGIT, which binds to its ligands CD155 (PVR) and CD112 (PVRL2/nectin-2) and disrupts costimulatory CD226 signaling, directly delivering inhibitory signals suppressing T and NK cell cytotoxicity and cytokine production [28], and (6) BTLA, which interacts with its ligand herpesvirus entry mediator (HVEM) to recruit the phosphatases SHP-1/SHP-2 to attenuate T cell receptor signaling and proliferation, thereby maintaining lymphocyte tolerance and limiting immunopathology [29]. (2) Functional Impairment: This is characterized by a progressive and hierarchical loss of polyfunctionality. Early exhaustion involves the loss of IL-2 production and proliferative capacity, followed by loss of TNF-α, and ultimately downregulation of IFN-γ and cytotoxicity markers (e.g., CD107a, perforin, and granzyme B (GrzB)) [30, 31]. Furthermore, Texs enter a state of “metabolic gridlock” following multiple rounds of antigen stimulation, characterized by simultaneous impairment of glycolysis and mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation, reduced ATP production, and dysregulation of key metabolic sensing pathways, such as mTOR/AMPK. These alterations further constrain their proliferative potential and their capacity to restore effector functions [32–36]. (3) Metabolic Reprogramming: metabolic reprogramming is primarily characterized by mitochondrial dysfunction, excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation, and alterations in glucose metabolism, among others. For instance, sustained antigen stimulation combined with hypoxia synergistically induces mitochondrial dysfunction and pathological ROS buildup in T cells, thereby driving irreversible exhaustion mainly through the Blimp-1–PGC-1α regulatory axis [34, 37, 38]. (4) Epigenetic Memory: sustained antigen stimulation induces stable “epigenetic scarring” or “epigenetic memory,” exemplified by irreversible demethylation of the PD-1 promoter region. Regulated by key chromatin modifiers such as DNMT3A and TET2, this epigenetic fixation prevents Texs from regaining long-term memory potential even after antigen clearance or ICB, thereby limiting the durability of adoptive cell therapies such as CAR-T. However, in the specific context of hematological malignancies such as multiple myeloma (MM), a decoupling of the epigenetic landscape from functional loss has been observed. Recent studies have indicated that T cells exhibiting chromatin accessibility profiles characteristic of terminal exhaustion (e.g., accessible Tox and Havcr2 loci) can surprisingly retain potent cytotoxicity and cytokine production provided that accessibility to critical transcription factor motifs, such as BATF, is maintained. These findings suggest that the epigenetic memory of exhaustion is context-dependent and may harbor exploitable plasticity within specific tumor microenvironments [20, 39, 40].

Further studies reveal that the T cell exhaustion state is not a homogeneous endpoint but rather a dynamically evolving continuum. Based on functional heterogeneity and molecular marker differences, exhausted CD8+ T cells can be finely categorized into four distinct differentiation stages, uncovering a hierarchical transition from “reservoir potential” to “functional paralysis”: (1) Progenitor 1 Texs (TexProg1): Identified by the markers Ly108+ (TCF1+) and CD69+, this population represents a quiescent, lymphoid tissue-resident, non-proliferative progenitor cell subset. Characterized by high TCF-1 expression, these cells slowly divide and exhibit superior persistence. These cells participate in progenitor biology and display an antiviral defense signature; (2) Progenitor 2 Exhausted T (TexProg2) Cells: Identified by the markers Ly108+ (TCF1+) and CD69−, this population represents a highly proliferative, activated progenitor subset with robust persistence. The gene expression profile of these cells is primarily associated with cell cycle progression and cellular motility. (3) Intermediate Texs (TexInt): Identified by the markers Ly108− (TCF1−) and CD69−, this population represents an intermediate differentiation state. These cells possess residual effector activity and migratory capacity but lack TCF-1 expression. The transcriptional signature of these cells includes cytotoxic effector-related genes, such as granzymes and perforin. (4) Terminal Texs (TexTerm): Identified by the markers Ly108− (TCF1−) and CD69+, this population represents the terminal exhausted subset. These cells express high levels of inhibitory receptors (e.g., PD-1, TIM-3, and LAG-3) and are non-cycling. The primary function of these cells appears to be immune suppression. TexTerm cells exhibit the highest TOX protein expression. Although the cells retain some residual cytotoxic potential, they are incapable of further proliferation and consequently exhibit poor responsiveness to therapeutic intervention [41, 42].

T cell exhaustion occurring post-HSCT

Allo-HSCT is an effective therapeutic modality for hematologic malignancies and immunodeficiency disorders. However, the therapeutic efficacy of allo-HSCT and associated complications, such as GVHD and disease relapse, are closely linked to the functional status of donor-derived T cells [43]. Following transplantation, recipients undergo immune reconstitution during which T cell reconstitution exhibits highly dynamic and complex characteristics (Table 1). Mechanistically, these dynamic characteristics are fundamentally shaped by the dual pathways of immune recovery: the primarily thymus-independent and the delayed thymus-dependent central pathway. The latter generates de novo naïve T cells contributing to the durable restoration of a non-exhausted T cell pool [44].

The stage at which T cells accumulate during differentiation varies depending on the type of transplant. In syngeneic transplantation, T cells do not encounter allo-antigens and consequently maintain a normal phenotype. However, in allo-HSCT, donor-derived T cells may undergo exhaustion following transplantation, differentiating into terminal Tex cells [12]. It is noteworthy that compared with allo-HSCT, autologous-HSCT (auto-HSCT) exhibits a higher proportion of effector memory T cells (TEM) in its graft, along with elevated expression of inhibitory receptors (e.g., PD-1, CTLA-4, and TIM-3) and a weaker capacity for IFN-γ secretion. Mechanistically, this state can be attributed to the microenvironmental composition of the graft. Specifically, in peripheral blood stem cell (PBSC) grafts in Auto-HSCT, there is a significant number of monocytic myeloid-derived suppressor cells (M-MDSCs). These cells highly express ligands such as PD-L1 and CD86 and show a negative correlation with the expression of inhibitory receptors on T cells. These cells promote T cell exhaustion by suppressing T cell activation, and blocking the PD-1 pathway can partially reverse this inhibitory effect [63].

After transplantation, T cells in the exhausted state exhibit significant temporal dynamics. While PD-1 expression is generally negatively correlated with time post-transplantation, CD4+ and CD8+ T cells display distinct patterns. High PD-1 expression in CD8+ T cells is typically confined to the first 1–3 months post-transplantation, whereas in CD4+ T cells, it can persist for over two years. Thus, it is essential to clarify the boundary between “exhaustion” and “activation.” PD-1 is also a marker of acute T cell activation [64–66]. Acute activation is generally transient and accompanied by preserved or enhanced effector functions, whereas exhaustion reflects persistent antigenic stimulation and is typically characterized by the co-expression of multiple inhibitory receptors, such as PD-1, together with TIM-3, LAG-3, TIGIT, 2B4, or CD160, along with impaired proliferation, reduced cytokine production, and diminished cytotoxicity. Therefore, PD-1 single-positive T cells after transplantation might not be regarded as terminally exhausted without additional phenotypic and functional evidence [67–69]. Studies have found that long-term PD-1 single-positive CD4+ T cells after transplantation lack co-expression of 2B4 or CD160, which may reflect a state of sustained activation driven by GVHD rather than terminal exhaustion (Fig. 1A) [59]. In contrast, truly Texs typically co-express multiple inhibitory receptors (e.g., co-expression of PD-1 and TIM-3), a phenotype that is more prominent in the terminal effector memory RA T cell (TEMRA) subset within the bone marrow microenvironment [70].

Patients with different primary diseases exhibit heterogeneous exhaustion characteristics after transplantation, all of which point to poor prognosis. For instance, in MM patients undergoing auto-HSCT, a surge in PD-1+/TIM-3+ cells can be observed early after transplantation, suggesting that the reinfused T cells themselves are the primary source of the exhausted phenotype (Fig. 1C) [71]. In autoimmune diseases such as multiple sclerosis (MS), the chronic inflammatory environment drives premature T cell aging and exhaustion [72]. In patients with severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID), CD8+ T cells coexpress multiple inhibitory receptors and activation markers, and this abnormal phenotype is directly associated with an increased risk of infection and relapse [73].

The most critical impact of T cell exhaustion on HSCT efficacy is weakening the GVL effect and leading to disease relapse. Studies indicate that after allo-HSCT, approximately 90% of patients harbor tumor-associated antigen (TAA)-specific T cells, but these cells are often in a deeply exhausted state, i.e., high expression of PD-1 and TIM-3. In patients with relapse in particular, stem-like memory T cells (TSCM) prematurely accumulate exhaustion characteristics (Fig. 1B) [46]. Mechanistic studies further confirm that leukemia cells not only exhibit inefficient antigen presentation but also induce GVL-T cells to enter a deeply exhausted state characterized by PD-1+TOX+EOMES+ expression， accompanied by impaired mitochondrial function and reduced IFN-γ production, ultimately leading to GVL failure and disease relapse [12, 74].

T cell exhaustion and HSCT complications

Graft failure

Graft failure (GF), a severe complication following allo-HSCT [75], is closely associated with T cell dysfunction. Bone marrow biopsies reveal significant expansion of CD8+ TEM exhibiting an “activated-exhausted paradoxical state” in GF patients. Phenotypically, these cells overexpress the activation marker CD95 and downregulate CD127 (IL-7Rα) while simultaneously exhibiting coordinated upregulation of the exhaustion-associated molecules CD57, PD-1/CD279, LAG-3/CD223, and TIM-3/CD366. Although these T cells retain partial activation capacity, their terminal differentiation potential and cytotoxic function are significantly impaired [45]. Overall, GF may be driven by a maladaptive cycle of excessive immune activation and compensatory functional T cell exhaustion.

Disease relapse

The functional state of T cells after allo-HSCT, particularly the exhausted phenotype, may act as a double-edged sword, influencing clinical outcome. Low T cell cytotoxicity due to exhaustion mechanisms may help mitigate tissue damage associated with GVHD [47, 48], but it can also impair the immune system’s surveillance capacity against residual tumor cells, thereby increasing the risk of disease relapse.

Compared to patients in long-term remission after allo-HSCT, relapsed patients exhibit an accumulation of exhausted TSCM and TCM cells in the bone marrow. These cells display increased expression of IC proteins (e.g., PD-1, CTLA-4, and TIM-3), diminished cytokine production, reduced degranulation capacity, and weakened proliferative potential. Notably, the landscape of exhaustion in early relapse extends beyond conventional CD4+ and CD8+ T cells. A recent study identified a significant expansion of TIM3-high double-negative T (DNT) cells in the bone marrow of early relapse patients. These DNT cells exhibit a profound “functional paralysis” characterized by the downregulation of cytotoxic granules and effector genes like CD226 (DNAM1) [76]. This suggests that relapse-associated immune dysfunction involves not only conventional CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, but also unconventional T cell subsets. Therapeutically, infusion of functional allogeneic DNTs may help compensate for this impaired antileukemic immunity. A Phase I trial has demonstrated that prophylactic infusion of healthy allogeneic DNTs can successfully prevent relapse in high-risk AML patients by activating recipient CD8+ T cells and enhancing the indirect GVL effect without inducing GVHD [77].

As we know, since the 1990 s of the last century, DLI has been an important treatment used to restore the GVL effect after CML post-transplant relapse, and it has also been used to prevent or treat acute leukemia relapse [78–81]. Beyond these clinical observations, mechanistic studies suggest that DLI may restore GVL in part by reversing T cell exhaustion within the bone marrow immune microenvironment. This concept was supported in the clinical study of DLI for relapsed AML. The study found that bone marrow CD4 + and CD8 + T cells exhibited increased PD-1/Tim-3 co-expression and functional impairment, while reversal of T cell exhaustion after the first DLI was associated with persistent complete remission [6, 82].

Overall, T cell exhaustion represents a critical immunosuppressive axis in the relapse-associated tumor microenvironment, indicating that reversing T cell exhaustion may offer a promising therapeutic strategy to prevent disease recurrence (Table 2) [15, 46].

GVHD

For decades, T cell exhaustion has been regarded as the primary cause of failure in eliminating tumors or chronic viral infection. However, in the context of allo-HSCT, emerging evidence suggests that exhaustion serves as an essential negative feedback mechanism, preventing alloreactive T cells from inflicting catastrophic damage on host tissues [85]. Recent fundamental studies have identified several key molecular pathways driving this “protective exhaustion.” Mohammadpour et al. identified donor T cell-expressed galectin-3 (Gal-3) as a critical upstream regulator that promotes NFAT nuclear translocation without activating AP-1, thereby driving T cells into an exhausted state; notably, Gal-3 deficiency leads to aberrant donor T cell proliferation and lethal GVHD [50]. Similarly, Li et al. demonstrated in STAT3-deficient models that loss of STAT3 enhances downstream PD-1 signaling and downregulates the glutathione (GSH)/Myc metabolic axis. This process results in mitochondrial dysfunction and the subsequent apoptosis or anergy of T cells infiltrating target organs, effectively preventing GVHD [51]. This protective exhaustion is regulated not only by cell-intrinsic pathways but also by precise extrinsic modulation within the microenvironment. For instance, IL-35-producing regulatory B cells (i35-Bregs) have been shown to secrete exosomes that contain membrane-bound IL-35. These exosomes “coat” bystander alloreactive T cells, inducing significant upregulation of PD-1 and LAG-3 through a mechanism of “infectious tolerance” that mitigates GVHD by driving pathogenic clones into anergy or exhaustion [52]. While IL-39 drives chronic GVHD (cGVHD) by enhancing T and B cell pathogenicity through STAT1/3 signaling and exacerbating thymic damage, thereby antagonizing immune tolerance [86]. Furthermore, research into renal GVHD indicates that donor T cells are activated and express exhaustion markers (e.g., PD-1 and Tim-3) within the kidney microenvironment, a state that limits collateral damage to renal tubules and endothelial cells. Collectively, these findings suggest that inducing and maintaining “terminal exhaustion” within target organs is a cornerstone of post-transplant tissue tolerance [49].

While calcineurin inhibitors (CNIs) such as cyclosporine A and tacrolimus remain the gold standard for GVHD prophylaxis, their long-term use often impedes durable immune tolerance, often leading to GVHD recurrence upon withdrawal. Recent studies have consistently highlighted that while CNIs reduce cytokine production by inhibiting the NFAT pathway (thereby masking acute symptoms), they simultaneously arrest the differentiation of T cells into terminal exhaustion. Under CNI pressure, alloreactive T cells are trapped in a “transitional exhaustion” or “TCM” state (TCF-1hiTOXlow), retaining potent self-renewal capacity and pathogenic potential. Once CNIs are tapered, these latent clones rapidly expand, triggering severe chronic GVHD [12, 54]. In contrast, post-transplant cyclophosphamide (PTCy) physically depletes rapidly proliferating Teffs while sparing Tregs and non-alloreactive TSCM [53]. Low-dose PTCy strategies, in particular, appear to more precisely quell GVHD while preserving a therapeutic window for the specific activation of TSCM via IL-18 agonists to enhance GVL effects without exacerbating GVHD [53, 55]. However, studies have also indicated that PTCy acts as a ‘double-edged sword.’ While it successfully suppresses GVHD by increasing Tregs and inducing an inhibitory T cell phenotype (characterized by high expression of PD-1, TIGIT, etc.), it simultaneously weakens the GVL effect, which is attributed to the reduced expression of cytotoxic molecules and impaired cytokine production capacity in T cells, resulting in an increased risk of disease relapse [87].

The observation that some patients maintain potent GVL effects in the absence of GVHD despite widespread exhaustion suggests fundamental differences between the T cells mediating these two responses in terms of their spatial distribution and differentiation states. (1) Anatomic segregation: This model emphasizes that T cell fate after allo-HSCT is not uniform throughout the body but is shaped by compartment-specific signals within distinct anatomical sites. The “anatomic segregation” model proposed by Ni et al. suggests that following early CD4+ T cell depletion, donor CD8+ T cells infiltrating GVHD target organs (e.g., gut and liver) encounter high levels of host tissue-expressed PD-L1, leading to rapid exhaustion or apoptosis. Conversely, CD8+ T cells within lymphoid tissues (e.g., spleen and lymph nodes) are shielded from these inhibitory signals and receive costimulation via APC-expressed CD80, allowing them to proliferate and maintain functional anti-leukemic activity [57]. Therefore, the same donor T cell response may be attenuated in GVHD target organs while remaining functionally competent in lymphoid or marrow compartments, providing a mechanistic basis for the partial uncoupling of GVHD from GVL. (2) Tissue residency vs. systemic circulation: Sacirbegovic et al. further confirmed via parabiosis experiments that established GVHD is primarily maintained by local self-renewal of the tissue-resident TCF-1+ subpopulation rather than the continuous influx of effectors from the circulation [56]. On a molecular level, the persistence of GVHD is driven by a “stem cell pool” of TCF−1hi precursor-exhausted T cells (TPEX) that are highly dependent on the long isoform of TCF − 1 (p45). Crucially, deletion of this isoform abrogates GVHD without compromising GVL activity in lymphoid and marrow compartments, revealing a distinct molecular rift between the two processes and providing a specific target for precise intervention [58].

Given these mechanisms, the indiscriminate use of broad-spectrum ICIs for post-transplant relapse carries substantial risks and necessitates strategies tailored to T cell phenotype and the depth of exhaustion. Previous studies have provided an early clinical example of CTLA-4 blockade after allo-HSCT. In 28 patients with post-transplant relapse, ipilimumab induced responses in some patients, especially those with extramedullary myeloid relapse, and these responses were linked to CD8+ T cell infiltration at tumor sites. However, GVHD and immune-related toxicities also occurred [88]. Similarly, anti-PD-1 therapy produced a high response rate in relapsed lymphoma after allo-HSCT, but with substantial GVHD risk. Among 31 patients, the overall response rate was 77%, whereas 55% developed treatment-emergent GVHD, including cases of rapid, refractory, and fatal GVHD [89]. A recent Phase Ib trial demonstrated that while pembrolizumab (anti-PD-1) elicited a ~ 30% response rate in AML/MDS relapse, 37.5% of patients developed severe (Grade 3–4) and often steroid-refractory GVHD [60]. This clinical risk is mechanistically supported by Simonetta et al., who observed that T cells express high, dynamic levels of PD-1 early post-transplant, suggesting that ill-timed checkpoint blockade can dismantle the fragile balance of tolerance [59]. Overall, checkpoint blockade can restore GVT/GVL activity but must be used with caution. To navigate this “immunologic knife’s edge,” Shapiro and Soiffer emphasized that mixed donor chimerism, rather than tumor PD-L1 expression, serves as a superior biomarker for predicting therapeutic benefit from ICIs [90]. To widen the safety window on this “knife’s edge,” novel engineering approaches are being developed: Tian et al. designed αp-T cells that utilize a synergistic IFN-α/PD-L1 loop to actively drive alloreactive clones into terminal exhaustion [61], while CAR-Vδ1 T cells can provide potent anti-tumor activity without inducing GVHD or CRS, even in the absence of checkpoint blockade, due to their MHC-independent recognition and low-exhaustion profile [62, 91]. These advancements represent a paradigm shift from global immune inhibition to precise immunophenotypic sculpting in post-transplant therapy.

Viral and fungal infection

Chronic or acute infections represent significant disruptors of immune reconstitution post-HSCT, profoundly impacting post-transplant immune homeostasis by directly inducing antigen-specific T cell exhaustion or indirectly impairing thymic function.

Cytomegalovirus (CMV) reactivation exerts a dual influence on shaping the post-HSCT T cell repertoire. After HSCT, CMV-specific CD8+ T cell (CMV-CTLs) rapid reconstitution is critical against CMV infection [92]. These cells exhibit significantly lower expression of exhaustion markers (e.g., PD-1 and TIM-3) and superior IFN-γ and TNF-α secretion capacity compared to TAA WT1-specific T cells (WT1-CTLs), which were thought to be typical GVL effectors. However, the frequency of CMV-CTLs gradually declines over time， accompanied by a dynamic reduction in PD-1, CTLA-4, and TIM-3 expression [93]. For example, Özdemir et al. showed that CMV-specific CD8+ T cells could be detected after allo-HSCT, but their cytokine-producing capacity was impaired, especially in patients with aGVHD or receiving corticosteroids [94]. Notably, patients experiencing CMV reactivation display even lower PD-1 and TIM-3 expression on their CMV-CTLs and a differentiation bias towards the functionally superior Eomes⁺ T-bet⁺ effector memory subset within CD8⁺ T cells compared to patients without CMV relapse, suggesting that CMV infection may optimize Teff function through selective pressure [93]. Conversely, other studies indicate that CMV reactivation concurrently impairs thymic output, manifested by reduced numbers of recent thymic emigrants (RTEs) and naïve T cells alongside decreased Treg proportions and early elevation of exhaustion markers (e.g., PD-1) on CD4⁺/CD8⁺ T cells [95]. The cost of this immune remodeling is a significant reduction in TCR-β repertoire diversity: CMV reactivation drives the oligoclonal expansion (> 50-fold) of virus-specific CD8+ TEM, leading to the formation of “clonal holes” (i.e., deficits in specific V-J combinations) within the TCR-β repertoire concurrent with a persistent inversion of the CD4+/CD8+ ratio, indicating global immune lineage defects [96]. This process reveals a fundamental conflict between virus-specific immune responses and overall immune homeostasis: while CMV-specific T cell expansion provides short-term protection, it occurs at the expense of TCR diversity, potentially leading to a long-term decline in immune surveillance capability. Clinical cases further demonstrate that chronic CMV infection (e.g., CMV enteritis) can induce high PD-1/PD-L1 expression on CMV-CTLs. Blocking the PD-1/PD-L1 pathway significantly restores their expansion capacity, underscoring the reversibility of the exhausted phenotype [97]. Moreover, adoptive transfer of CMV-specific T cells in patients with refractory CMV infection after haplo-HSCT promoted CMV clearance in most patients and was accompanied by improved CMV-specific T cell expansion, cytokine production, and proliferation, and reduced PD-1 expression [98]. In addition, clinical data also linked CMV disease and severe aGVHD with increased PD-1 expression on CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, suggesting that CMV-related inflammation and GVHD may together promote a PD-1-high dysfunctional T cell state after HSCT [99].

Epstein–Barr virus (EBV) reactivation is another critical complication after allo-HSCT and may progress to EBV-associated lymphoproliferative disease (EBV-LPD) when EBV-specific T cell immunity is insufficient. Studies showed that assessing EBV-specific T cell recovery, together with EBV DNA load, better identifies patients at risk for EBV-LPD; patients with concurrent expansion of CD3+ T cells and EBV-specific memory CD8+ T cells can control EBV reactivation without immediate rituximab treatment [100]. More recently, another study found that poor day-30 T cell reconstitution, particularly reduced CD8+ and CD4+CD45RO+ memory T cell recovery, predicted EBV reactivation, whereas PD-1+ T cell proportions were not significantly different between good and poor immune reconstitution groups [101]. These findings suggest that EBV control after HSCT is more closely associated with early virus-specific T cell recovery than with PD-1-defined T cell exhaustion alone. Furthermore, CMV/EBV co-reactivation after allo-HSCT was associated with higher viral pneumonitis, lower 1-year OS and leukemia-free survival, and poorer day-30 CD4+CD25+ T cell recovery; aGVHD was also more frequent in patients with viral reactivation than in those without reactivation [102]. In a pediatric HSCT cohort, further showed that delayed CD4+ T cell reconstitution predicted AdV, EBV, and HHV-6 reactivation, and timely CD4+ recovery mitigated viral reactivation-related adverse outcomes [103]. Together, these data indicate that insufficient early T cell recovery, especially delayed CD4+ T cell reconstitution, is a key driver of herpesvirus reactivation and related complications after HSCT.

Human Herpesvirus 6 (HHV-6) viremia may further impair post-HSCT T cell recovery. Very high HHV-6 viremia (> 105 copies/mL) was associated with impaired late CD4+ and CD8+ T cell reconstitution, particularly in naïve CD4+ T cell recovery, while antiviral treatment improved the probability of T cell reconstitution [104]. HHV-6B may influence post-HSCT immune function and GVHD development, providing a rationale for future studies to explore whether exhaustion-related T cell dysfunction contributes to this association.

In addition, experimental LCMV models provide mechanistic evidence for the link between viral infection, thymic injury, and T cell exhaustion. LCMV infection exacerbates thymic atrophy and T cell exhaustion via a CD8+ T cell-dependent Type I interferon (IFN-I) and STAT2 signaling pathway. In an LCMV-CI13-infected mouse model, exhaustion (PD-1+ Tim-3+) of virus-specific CD8+ T cells leads to a transient loss of their ability to kill thymocytes. However, PD-1 blockade can restore this cytotoxicity and disrupt the thymic structure again. Although HSCT can reconstitute the T cell repertoire when performed in the context of such an infection, impaired thymic negative selection results in increased autoreactive T cell clones, elevating the risk of autoimmunity [105].

Clinical evidence is limited, but a recent case report provides proof-of-concept for checkpoint blockade in severe post-transplant viral infection. When conventional antiviral therapies proved ineffective, the administration of the PD-1 inhibitor (sintilimab) successfully cleared the adenovirus and concomitant EBV. It was further substantiated that the potential to reverse T cell exhaustion through ICB can restore virus-specific immune responses in the management of severe post-transplant viral infections [106].

Beyond viral infection, invasive fungal disease is also an important clinical complication after HSCT. In a prospective study of 1858 allo-HSCT recipients, the 1-year invasive fungal disease incidence of 8.8%. Grade II–IV aGVHD and extensive cGVHD were major risk factors for late fungal infection, and fungal disease was associated with poorer 1-year survival [107]. aGVHD and prolonged corticosteroid exposure increased the risk of IFI and mold infections. In turn, invasive fungal disease (IFD) or mold infection markedly increased non-relapse mortality, especially in patients with acute or chronic GVHD. These findings highlight a clear clinical vicious cycle among GVHD, steroid-driven immunosuppression, and fungal infection [108]. Overall, infection after HSCT should be viewed as a dynamic regulator of T cell fate rather than a simple trigger of T cell exhaustion. Persistent antigen stimulation and inflammatory stress may drive exhaustion-like dysfunction, whereas infection-induced T cell activation may also support antiviral immunity and GVL. Therefore, T cell exhaustion in this setting should be defined by functional impairment and immune context, not by checkpoint marker expression alone.

Targeted intervention strategies for post-transplant T cell exhaustion

Differential efficacy of ICIs

ICIs are central to reversing T cell exhaustion, yet their efficacy depends on both the stage of exhaustion and the chosen target. In allo-HSCT mouse models, PD-1 blockade confers limited functional recovery once T cells have progressed to the Tex state, indicating that intervention must occur before the exhaustion program becomes irreversible [12]. ICIs are used to reverse T cell exhaustion under two main conditions. First, for patients who carry driver mutations such as FLT3-ITD and c-KIT-D816V that can markedly up-regulate TIM-3 ligands galectin-9 and CEACAM1 on leukemic stem cells (LSCs), AML blasts with low ligand expression are intrinsically resistant to anti-TIM-3 therapy. In this case, ICIs are used after HSCT. Second, ICIs are used for the treatment of patients with relapse after HSCT. Mechanistically, TIM-3 blockade reprograms CD8⁺ T cell metabolism (enhanced glycolytic capacity) and transcriptional state (reduced PD-1⁺TIM-3⁺ exhausted subset), thereby potentiating GVL activity. Single-cell profiling further demonstrates that TIM-3 inhibition expands stem-like progenitor exhausted CD8⁺ T cells and augments effector T cell cytotoxicity, establishing a durable antitumor response. Crucially, unlike PD-1 or CTLA-4 blockade, anti-TIM-3 therapy does not exacerbate aGVHD, highlighting a distinctive safety profile. TIM-3 blockade with sabatolimab successfully reprogrammed the exhausted metabolic and transcriptional state of bone marrow T cells from relapsed patients [76]. In preclinical models, sabatolimab markedly reduces the burden of FLT3-ITD-mutant AML and prolongs survival. Moreover, FLT3 inhibitors can downregulate galectin-9 and TIM-3 via pathway inhibition, providing a mechanistic rationale for combination regimens. Building on these findings, the phase I/II trial NCT04623216 has preliminarily confirmed the safety and efficacy of TIM-3 targeting in post-transplant relapsed AML patients, positioning TIM-3 as a promising therapeutic node for optimizing the GVL/GVHD balance [109].

Combination therapies

In exploring the synergistic potential of combination treatment strategies, Jia et al. used a humanized GVL model (transplanting patient-derived AML cells and donor PBMCs into MHC-deficient NSG mice) to reveal a mechanism in which AML blunts the GVL effect by inducing T cell exhaustion (upregulation coexpression of PD-1/TIGIT/TIM-3) and enhancing apoptosis. Significantly, combined blockade of the PD-1, TIGIT, and TIM-3 pathways markedly reduced the AML burden and restored cytotoxic function in CD8+ T cells, as evidenced by increased GrzB expression and IFN-γ secretion [110]. Similarly, in MM patients, post-auto-HSCT, activated CD8⁺ T cells (CD28+ DNAM-1+) within mobilized peripheral blood stem cells highly express TIGIT, indicating functional constraint by inhibitory signaling. Preclinical studies have demonstrated that an FcγR-binding anti-TIGIT antibody (αTIGIT) synergizes with lenalidomide to inhibit myeloma progression, concomitantly reducing the proportion of exhausted TIM-3+ CD8+ T cells in the bone marrow while expanding polyclonal Teff cells, upregulating IFN-γ, GrzB, and perforin, and promoting TCM accumulation. In contrast to the clinical failures observed with PD-1 inhibitors combined with immunomodulatory drugs (IMiDs) (e.g., the KEYNOTE-183/185 trials), this combination did not increase toxicity in preclinical models with efficacy dependent on bone marrow niche-specific immune remodeling. A clinical trial (NCT05289492) evaluating the Fc-enabled TIGIT antibody EOS-448 combined with the next-generation IMiD iberdomide has been initiated to validate its clinical translation potential in relapsed/refractory MM patients, although the primary resistance observed in some mice necessitates further investigation into mechanisms of tumor antigen presentation and the metabolic microenvironment [111]. Furthermore, a CD40 agonist combined with an antigen vaccine (DEC-H60) significantly enhanced the cross-presentation capacity of donor DCs, promoting the expansion of H60-specific T cells and reducing PD-1 and TIM-3 expression. Compared with PD-1 monotherapy blockade, early post-transplant administration of the CD40 agonist FGK45 more effectively enhanced the GVL effect, suggesting that targeting the antigen presentation microenvironment represents a novel approach for reversing exhaustion [74].

Novel immunomodulatory approaches

Within the realm of immunosuppressive drugs, the reprogramming effects of cyclosporine (CSP) and other immunomodulators on T cell exhaustion after allo-HSCT are increasingly being revealed. In a murine allo-HSCT model, CSP treatment significantly inhibited the differentiation of terminally exhausted T (Tex: PD-1⁺ TIGIT⁺ TOXhigh) cells while inducing the expansion of effector-like transitory Texs (transitory Tex-CNI: PD-1⁺ TIGIT⁻ Ly6C⁺). These transitional T cells exhibit characteristics of low exhaustion (TOXlow and TCF-1⁻), high proliferative activity (Ki67⁺), and strong cytotoxicity (GZMB⁺). Mechanistically, CSP inhibits TOX expression by blocking NFAT signaling, thereby preventing T cell differentiation into a terminal exhaustion phenotype. Notably, adoptive transfer of transitory Tex-CNI to secondary recipients induced cGVHD. Simultaneously, these cells retained sensitivity to PD-1 blockade therapy, as αPD-L1 significantly enhanced their proliferation and GVL activity. These findings reveal that CNIs establish a dynamic equilibrium between GVHD and GVL effects by modulating T cell exhaustion plasticity: the persistence of transitory Tex-CNI exacerbates cGVHD risk, while its functional reversibility provides a therapeutic window for combination therapy PD-1 blockade to selectively enhance the GVL effect [12]. Recent research further discovered that timed administration of interleukin-2 cytokine complexes (IL-2Cx) in a murine allogeneic bone marrow transplant model differentially regulates Treg and Teff: short-term IL-2Cx25 therapy not only prevented GVHD but also enhanced the GVL effect by reducing the expression of exhaustion markers (PD-1/Eomes) on CD8⁺ T cells, whereas prolonged treatment caused an immunosuppressive imbalance due to excessive Treg expansion. These data suggest that reducing T cell exhaustion may correlate with enhanced GVL effects, potentially independent of heightened donor T cell alloreactivity, which may be mediated through alternative mechanisms [112]. This discovery complements the earlier mechanism of CNI-induced transitory Texs [12], jointly supporting the core role of the “exhaustion reversal - function preservation” strategy in optimizing transplant outcomes. Recent clinical data suggest that post-HSCT AML may also appear a noncanonical T cell dysfunctional state beyond classical exhaustion. In a phase I/II trial of WT1-specific TCR-engineered donor CD8+ T cells for active AML after allo-HSCT, TTCR−C4 infusion was well tolerated but showed limited clinical efficacy, with persisting cells progressively acquiring an NK-like/Temra phenotype and reduced IFN-γ production rather than a classical PD-1/TIM-3/TIGIT-dominant exhaustion profile [113].

Beyond cell-intrinsic mechanisms, microenvironmental factors, such as the gut microbiota, can indirectly influence relapse risk by modulating T cell exhaustion phenotypes. Clinical studies have consistently demonstrated that reduced intestinal microbiota diversity around engraftment is associated with adverse outcomes after allo-HSCT. An initial study of 80 recipients showed that lower diversity at engraftment was linked to inferior 3-year overall survival and higher transplant-related mortality, and this association was subsequently confirmed in a multicenter cohort of 1,362 recipients, in which greater peri-engraftment diversity was associated with a lower risk of overall, transplant-related, and GVHD-related mortality [114, 115]. Beyond overall microbial diversity, specific gut microbial configurations may further shape relapse risk and post-transplant T-cell immunity. Enterotypes associated with high relapse risk (enterotype 1/4) significantly correlate with upregulated TOX expression in CD4⁺ Th1 cells, a co-inhibitory phenotype (TIGIT⁺ PD-1⁺ CTLA-4⁺), and an Eomes⁺ T-bet⁺ exhausted subset within CD8⁺ T cells. Conversely, enterotype 2/3 is enriched for activated/cytotoxic T cells (2B4⁺ KLRG1⁺ GrzB⁺). Critically, Bacteroides fragilis was further revealed to specifically drive the expansion of TIGIT⁺ PD-1⁺ TOX⁺-exhausted subsets within central memory CD4⁺ Th1/Th2 and CD8⁺ T cells [116]. These findings suggest that certain gut microbiota patterns may be associated with a T cell exhaustion phenotype and a higher risk of relapse after allo-HSCT. Although current clinical evidence remains limited, the relationship between gut microbiota and T cell exhaustion deserves further study and may provide a new approsch to improve T cell function and reduce relapse after transplantation.

Overall, T cell exhaustion is associated not only with cell-intrinsic factors (such as high expression of inhibitory receptors and dysregulated transcription factor activity), but it is also closely linked to the cellular microenvironment [117, 118]. Within the tumor or chronic infection microenvironment, metabolites such as lactate and adenosine directly exacerbate exhaustion by suppressing mitochondrial function and impairing the glycolytic pathway in T cells [119]. Modulating the immune microenvironment to ameliorate these immunosuppressive conditions can provide a more favorable survival milieu for T cells, thereby alleviating or reversing T cell exhaustion (Fig. 2).

Summary and outlook

T cell exhaustion, a pivotal pathological mechanism in immune responses, plays a complex role in immune reconstitution and complications following HSCT. Chronic antigen exposure or inflammatory microenvironments drive a progressive decline in T cell function, manifesting as sustained expression of inhibitory receptors (e.g., PD-1, TIM-3), loss of effector function, and metabolic/epigenetic reprogramming. In allo-HSCT, exhaustion of donor T cells, while potentially mitigating GVHD risk, also impairs the GVL effect, contributing to disease relapse. The dynamic differentiation of Texs (e.g., progenitor vs. terminal exhaustion subsets) further influences their functional reversibility, where early intervention can partially reverse exhaustion, and the terminal stage proves to be refractory due to solidified epigenetic imprinting. Furthermore, post-HSCT complications (e.g., infections and GF) are closely linked to T cell exhaustion through mechanisms involving multiple factors, including IC imbalance, metabolic suppression, and microenvironmental remodeling. While current intervention strategies (e.g., ICB, metabolic modulation, and combination therapies) show promise, their efficacy is constrained by the stage of exhaustion, target selection, and tissue specificity. Future research must delve deeper into the mechanisms underpinning T cell exhaustion and develop precision-targeted interventions tailored to the specific differentiation stages and subsets of Texs. Concurrently, greater attention focusing on the impact of microenvironmental factors (e.g., gut microbiota, metabolites) on T cell exhaustion and exploration of modulatory approaches targeting these microenvironments is needed. The ultimate goal is to achieve precise modulation of T cell exhaustion, thereby enhancing the efficacy and safety of HSCT.
