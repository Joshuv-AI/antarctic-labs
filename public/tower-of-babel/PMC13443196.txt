HRS-7535, an oral small-molecule GLP-1 receptor agonist, in Chinese adults with obesity without diabetes: a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled phase 2 trial
Authors: Weijun Gu, Lihui Zhang, Ling Li, Dong Wei, Qifu Li, Yicen Zong, Zi Ye, Chang Shu, Tingyan Zhong, Yiming Mu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
HRS-7535 is an orally active small-molecule glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonist that showed weight-loss potential in a phase 1 study. In this completed multicenter, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled phase 2 trial conducted at 29 centers in China, we evaluated the efficacy and safety of HRS-7535 in adults with obesity without diabetes. 235 participants with a body mass index of 28.0–40.0 kg/m² were randomized a 1:1:1:1:1 ratio to once-daily oral HRS-7535 at target doses of 30 mg (n = 48), 60 mg (n = 47), 120 mg (n = 46), or 180 mg (n = 48), or placebo (n = 46); all randomized participants were included in the analyses. Primary endpoint was percentage change in body weight from baseline to Week 26. At Week 26, least-squares (LS) mean percentage changes in body weight were −2.99% with 30 mg, −7.09% with 60 mg, −6.17% with 120 mg, and −9.36% with 180 mg, versus −2.50% with placebo. Corresponding placebo-adjusted LS mean differences were −0.49%, −4.60%, −3.67%, and −6.87% (P = 0.7104, 0.0006, 0.0062, and <0.0001, respectively). Gastrointestinal adverse events were the most common, were predominantly mild to moderate, and occurred more frequently during dose escalation. Overall, once-daily oral HRS-7535 at doses of 60 mg or higher produced clinically meaningful weight loss and was generally well tolerated.Trial registration: ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT06250946

Introduction

Obesity is a chronic, relapsing disease that affects more than one billion people worldwide and is associated with insulin resistance, hypertension, dyslipidaemia, cardiovascular disease, and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), ultimately reducing life expectancy1–3. Lifestyle modification, primarily caloric restriction and increased physical activity, remains first-line therapy; however, sustained long-term weight loss is often difficult to achieve because compensatory neuroendocrine mechanisms promote weight regain4–6. Consequently, clinical guidelines recommend adjunctive pharmacotherapy for individuals with obesity, or for those who are overweight with obesity-related comorbidities, when lifestyle measures alone are insufficient.

Among available anti-obesity medications, glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs) provide substantial and durable weight-loss efficacy and improve glycaemic measures with a low risk of hypoglycemic7,8. However, most GLP-1RAs are injectables, which may limit treatment initiation and long-term adherence and create practical barriers related to storage and handling. Oral semaglutide is was the first oral GLP-1RA to become available, but it requires fasting administration and specific dosing instructions, which may reduce convenience and affect real-world persistence and effectiveness7,9,10. These limitations have driven interest in orally active small-molecule GLP-1RAs that may combine robust efficacy with simpler administration. The first oral small-molecule GLP-1RA to report proof-of-concept in obesity is orforglipron; in a 36-week phase 2 trial, once-daily dosing produced mean weight reductions of 9.4–14.7% compared with 2.3% with placebo11.

HRS-7535 is an orally bioavailable small-molecule GLP-1RA. In preclinical studies, HRS-7535 showed favorable oral exposure and pharmacodynamic effects consistent with glucose-dependent insulin secretion, appetite suppression, and weight reduction. In a first-in-human trial, HRS-7535 showed an acceptable safety profile and pharmacokinetics compatible with once-daily dosing, together with reductions in glucose and body weight12. HRS-7535 is being developed for T2DM, obesity, and diabetic kidney disease.

In this work, we report a multicenter, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled phase 2 trial evaluating once-daily oral HRS-7535 in Chinese adults with obesity without diabetes who have not achieved adequate weight loss with lifestyle intervention alone. We show that HRS-7535 at doses ≥60 mg leads to clinically meaningful reductions in body weight at Week 26, with weight loss sustained through Week 36 at the 180 mg dose. HRS-7535 is generally well tolerated, with gastrointestinal adverse events being the most common and a safety profile consistent with the GLP-1RA class.

Results

Participant disposition and baseline characteristics

Between 23 February and 18 March 2024, 339 Chinese adults were screened and 235 eligible participants were randomized to receive once-daily HRS-7535 at target doses of 30 mg (n = 48), 60 mg (n = 47), 120 mg (n = 46), or 180 mg (n = 48), or placebo (n = 46) (Fig. 1). Overall, 31 participants (13.2%) discontinued the assigned study treatment, all during the 26-week core treatment period; the most common reason was participant withdrawal (22/235, 9.4%), followed by adverse events (5/235, 2.1%). In total, 31 participants discontinued the trial prematurely, most commonly due to participant withdrawal (25/235, 10.6%), followed by investigator decision (3/235, 1.3%). All randomized participants (n = 235) were included in the ITT population; all received at least one dose of study treatment and were included in the safety population. Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were broadly similar across treatment groups (Table 1). A numerically higher mean baseline body weight was observed in the HRS-7535 60 mg group. Overall, the mean age was 33.5 years, and 114 participants (48.5%) were female (self-reported). Efficacy

At Week 26, the least-squares (LS) mean percent change in body weight from baseline was –2.99% (95% CI, –4.84 to –1.14) with HRS-7535 30 mg, –7.09% (95% CI, –8.95 to –5.24) with 60 mg, –6.17% (95% CI, –8.03 to –4.31) with 120 mg, and –9.36% (95% CI, –11.21 to –7.52) with 180 mg, versus –2.50% (95% CI, –4.34 to –0.65) with placebo. HRS-7535 at doses ≥60 mg showed statistically significant superiority over placebo, with LS mean differences of –4.60% (95% CI, –7.21 to –1.98; P = 0.0006) for 60 mg, –3.67% (95% CI, –6.29 to –1.05; P = 0.0062) for 120 mg, and –6.87% (95% CI, –9.48 to –4.26; P < 0.0001) for 180 mg (Table 2). These findings were consistent across two sensitivity analyses: a mixed-effects model for repeated measures (MMRM) with copy-reference multiple imputation for missing data after intercurrent events, and an MMRM excluding baseline BMI category; At Week 36, the LS mean percent change in body weight from baseline was –2.44% (95% CI, –4.62 to –0.27) with HRS-7535 30 mg, –5.36% (95% CI, –7.51 to –3.22) with 60 mg, –5.36% (95% CI, –7.51 to –3.21) with 120 mg, and –9.46% (95% CI, –11.61 to –7.32) with 180 mg, compared with –1.39% (95% CI, –3.52 to 0.74) with placebo. HRS-7535 at doses ≥60 mg again showed superiority over placebo, with LS mean differences of –3.98% (95% CI, –7.00 to –0.96; P = 0.0101) for 60 mg, –3.97% (95% CI, –7.00 to –0.95; P = 0.0103) for 120 mg, and –8.08% (95% CI, –11.10 to –5.05; P < 0.0001) for 180 mg. These results indicate sustained reductions in body weight at both Week 26 and Week 36, with the largest effect observed in the 180 mg group (Table 3; Fig. 2A). Sex-stratified analyses showed a consistent pattern of greater relative weight loss among female than male participants at both Week 26 and Week 36. In the prespecified subgroup analysis at Week 26, the LS mean percent change in body weight with HRS-7535 180 mg was –10.18% in women versus –3.94% in men (Table S2). A similar pattern was observed in the post hoc analysis at Week 36, with LS mean percent changes of –12.76% in women and –6.41% in men (Fig. S2). Collectively, these findings suggest that, in this trial, female participants tended to achieve larger relative weight reductions with HRS-7535, particularly in the 180 mg group.

At Week 26, the LS mean weight loss was −8.40 kg (95% CI, −10.02 to −6.77) with HRS-7535 180 mg compared with −2.20 kg (95% CI, −3.82 to −0.57) with placebo. All HRS-7535 doses ≥60 mg produced greater weight loss than placebo, with the largest placebo-adjusted difference observed with 180 mg (−6.20 kg; 95% CI, −8.50 to −3.90) (Table 2; Fig. 2B). Weight loss was sustained through Week 36, and the separation from placebo increased further in the 180 mg group (placebo-adjusted difference, −7.41 kg; 95% CI, −10.11 to −4.71) (Table 3).

HRS-7535 was also associated with sustained reductions in BMI and waist circumference through Weeks 26 and 36. Among participants receiving doses ≥60 mg, the placebo-adjusted LS mean change in BMI ranged from −1.20 to −2.25 kg/m² at Week 26 and from −1.31 to −2.67 kg/m² at Week 36. Corresponding placebo-adjusted LS mean changes in waist circumference ranged from −2.57 to −4.43 cm at Week 26 and from −2.14 to −4.92 cm at Week 36 (Table 2 and Table 3; Fig. S3).

Metabolic parameters improved in parallel. The placebo-adjusted LS mean change from baseline in HbA1c ranged from −0.19 to −0.23% in the HRS-7535 groups at doses ≥30 mg at Week 26 and from −0.09 to −0.18% at Week 36. For doses ≥60 mg, the corresponding placebo-adjusted LS mean change in fasting plasma glucose was −0.22 to −0.37 mmol/L at Week 26 and −0.05 to −0.22 mmol/L at Week 36 (Table S3). Across all HRS-7535 dose groups, changes in systolic and diastolic blood pressure were small and similar to those with placebo (Table S4). Changes in total cholesterol, LDL-C, HDL-C, and triglycerides were also comparable with placebo, suggesting no appreciable effect of HRS-7535 on lipid parameters over 36 weeks (Table S5).

Safety

During the double-blind treatment period, the proportions of participants with adherence within 80–120% were 97.9%, 100%, 97.8%, and 97.9% in the HRS-7535 30, 60, 120, and 180 mg groups, respectively, and 99.4% in the placebo group. AEs were reported in 93.5%–97.9% of participants receiving HRS-7535 (43/46–47/48) and in 91.3% of those receiving placebo (42/46) (Table 6). Gastrointestinal AEs were the most commonly reported events, occurring in 37.5–64.6% of HRS-7535–treated participants (18/48–31/48) compared with 15.2% in the placebo group (7/46) (Table S6). Dose step-backs during titration were uncommon and occurred only in the HRS-7535 120 mg and 180 mg groups. No participant in the placebo, 30 mg, or 60 mg group required a dose step-back. Overall, 8 of 235 randomized participants (3.4%) experienced a dose step-back, all attributable to gastrointestinal adverse events. In the 120 mg group, 2 participants required step-backs during titration and subsequently re-escalated, with 1 participant reaching and maintaining the 120 mg target dose. In the 180 mg group, 6 participants required step-backs at different titration stages; most stabilized after dose reduction, whereas 1 participant discontinued treatment because of persistent intolerance despite dose reduction (Table S7).

Nausea was reported in 16.7–58.3% of participants receiving HRS-7535 (8/48–28/48) compared with 4.3% with placebo (2/46), vomiting in 18.8–43.8% (9/48–21/48) compared with 2.2% (1/46), and diarrhea in 18.8–26.1% (9/48–12/46) compared with 8.7% (4/46). Nausea and vomiting were more frequent in the 120 mg and 180 mg groups, which followed longer and more intensive dose-escalation schedules that may have contributed to the higher event rates.

Five participants (2.1%; 5/235) discontinued HRS-7535 because of AEs, four of which were considered treatment-related: one participant with anxiety in the 30 mg group, and three participants in the 180 mg group (one with tremor and palpitations, one with nausea, vomiting, and decreased appetite, and one with dyspepsia and decreased appetite) (Table S8). Serious AEs occurred in 2.1–6.3% (1/48–3/48) of participants treated with HRS-7535 and in 2.2% (1/46) of participants treated with placebo; none were considered related to the study drug. One death due to hemorrhagic shock following a physical altercation was reported and was deemed unrelated to the study treatment (Table S9). Overall, the safety profile of HRS-7535 was consistent with that of established GLP-1RAs.

During the 36-week treatment period, four prespecified AESIs were reported (4/235, 1.7%), all in participants receiving HRS-7535; no AESIs occurred in the placebo group (Table 6). Transient elevations of amylase or lipase >3× the upper limit of normal (ULN) were observed in one participant (2.2%) in the 120 mg group and two participants (4.2%) in the 180 mg group, and none progressed to acute pancreatitis. One participant (2.1%) in the 30 mg group had a transient post-dose serum calcitonin elevation (≥50 ng/L and >50% increase from baseline) that did not require dose modification. All documented hypoglycaemic events in the HRS-7535 groups were classified as Level 1, Level 2, or possible symptomatic hypoglycemic; no severe (Level 3) episodes occurred. Such events were reported in 3/48 (6.3%) participants in the 30 mg group, 2/47 (4.3%) in the 60 mg group, 9/46 (19.6%) in the 120 mg group, and 5/48 (10.4%) in the 180 mg group, with none in the placebo group (Table S10). No participant met Hy’s Law criteria, defined as concurrent elevations in ALT or AST > 3×ULN and total bilirubin >2×ULN (Table S11). After 36 weeks of treatment, mean serum amylase and lipase showed small increases from baseline in the HRS-7535 groups (Table S12). Other laboratory parameters, vital signs (blood pressure and pulse rate), physical examination findings, and ECG results showed no clinically meaningful differences among treatment groups (Tables S13).

Discussion

This phase 2 study demonstrates that once-daily oral HRS-7535 results in clinically meaningful weight loss in Chinese adults with obesity without diabetes. At Week 26, all doses of HRS-7535 ≥ 60 mg showed significantly greater weight reduction compared to placebo, with the 180 mg group achieving the largest absolute (–8.40 kg) and relative (–9.36%) reductions. Notably, the 180 mg dose continued to show sustained weight loss through Week 36, with no evidence of plateauing. A post hoc analysis excluding participants with missing post-baseline pharmacokinetic (PK) exposure data revealed even greater reductions in the 180 mg group, with weight loss reaching –12.08% and –14.98% from baseline at Week 36, further supporting the efficacy of HRS-7535.

While a greater weight loss was observed with higher doses, the weight loss curves for the 60 mg and 120 mg doses were closely aligned. At Week 26, both the 60 mg and 120 mg groups showed signs of approaching a plateau, while the 180 mg group continued to exhibit sustained weight loss. This pattern, where higher doses maintain weight loss effects for a longer period and lower doses show earlier signs of plateauing, is consistent with the pharmacodynamic properties of GLP-1RAs, both peptide-based and small molecule. For instance, in a phase 2 dose-ranging study of semaglutide (0.4 mg/day), the highest dose group sustained weight loss throughout 52 weeks, suggesting that the time to reach the lowest weight point correlates with the drug dose13. Similarly, in the phase 2 trial of the oral small-molecule GLP-1RA orforglipron, the 12 mg group showed weight loss plateauing after 26 weeks, while higher doses (24 mg and 36 mg) continued to demonstrate a more pronounced weight loss trend11. These results align with our findings in the 180 mg group, where weight loss continued through Week 36, suggesting that higher doses sustain sufficient receptor activation to effectively counteract metabolic adaptation mechanisms during long-term treatment. Therefore, the differences in weight loss trajectories across the dosing groups in our study reflect the direct impact of drug exposure on the time required to establish a new metabolic equilibrium.

Two baseline factors may explain the transient advantage observed in the 60 mg group at Week 26: (i) women in the 60 mg cohort entered with a slightly higher mean BMI than those in the 120 mg cohort, and (ii) a greater proportion of participants in the 120 mg cohort experienced post-baseline pharmacokinetic (PK) values below the lower limit of quantification (BLQ), which likely resulted in lower actual drug exposure. In the male subgroup, where baseline BMI was similar between the two dose groups, the weight loss was also similar. However, in the female subgroup, the 60 mg group had a higher baseline BMI, which is generally associated with greater weight loss. This may have artificially boosted the weight loss effect in the 60 mg group, partially offsetting the expected advantage of the 120 mg dose. After adjusting for these factors in the post hoc analysis, the expected dose-response relationship between the 60 mg and 120 mg doses became more apparent.

Multiple randomized controlled trials and real-world studies consistently demonstrate that women experience greater weight loss than men when using GLP-1RAs. For instance, in the phase 3 trial of the oral small-molecule GLP-1RA orforglipron (Attain-1)14, women receiving 36 mg of orforglipron achieved a weight loss of 12.3% over 72 weeks, compared to 9.2% in men, with a similar pattern observed at lower doses. Similarly, in the SURMOUNT-5 trial15, women receiving the maximum tolerated dose (MTD, 15 mg) of tirzepatide administered once weekly for 72 weeks experienced an average weight loss of 23.8%, while men lost 17.8%. In contrast, in the semaglutide MTD (2.4 mg) group, women lost 18.0% of their body weight, while men lost 11.0%. In both tirzepatide and semaglutide groups, women demonstrated significantly greater weight loss than men. Furthermore, in a real-world study in Italy involving 7847 patients with type 2 diabetes16, although the reductions in HbA1c were similar between men and women, women lost an average of 1.1 kg more weight, with a higher proportion of women achieving ≥5% or ≥10% weight loss. This difference was independent of drug type, dose, and baseline characteristics, further supporting the presence of a “female advantage” in the efficacy of GLP-1RAs, suggesting a consistent class-wide effect favoring enhanced weight loss in women. The observed sex difference may partly reflect sex-related differences in fat distribution and body composition, and is also supported by an ovariectomy-based animal study suggesting that estrogen deficiency alters lipolysis in white adipose tissue and attenuates GLP-1RA–mediated metabolic regulation17.

Although injectable liraglutide (3.0 mg) and semaglutide (2.4 mg) have demonstrated greater average weight reductions in Western populations, their real-world use is constrained by injection-related burdens and the need for cold-chain storage18,19. Oral semaglutide, while eliminating injections, requires strict fasting and fluid intake conditions and results in only modest weight loss at the currently approved doses 7,9. In contrast, HRS-7535 (60–180 mg) achieved weight loss of 6.17 to 9.36% (absolute weight loss of –5.40 to –8.04 kg) at Week 26, without any dosing restrictions. This was achieved despite a lower baseline BMI (mean ~32 kg/m²) and a lower proportion of female participants in this Chinese cohort. The 180 mg dose resulted in a least-squares mean weight loss of –9.36% at Week 26, which is comparable to the –8.6% weight loss observed with orforglipron 12 mg in a heavier Western population (mean baseline weight ~105–109 kg)11, highlighting the competitive efficacy of HRS-7535 on a weight-adjusted basis.

By Week 26, 54.2% of participants receiving HRS-7535 180 mg achieved ≥ 5% weight loss, and 41.7% achieved ≥ 10%, compared to 23.9% and 6.5%, respectively, in the placebo group. However, these comparisons with other trials should be interpreted with caution, given differences in ethnicity, baseline weight and BMI, the lower proportion of female participants, and the exclusion of individuals with class III obesity (BMI > 40 kg/m²) in this Chinese cohort. Notably, weight loss in the HRS-7535 180 mg group continued to increase through Week 36, suggesting that further reductions may be attainable with extended treatment duration. In addition to weight loss, HRS-7535 at doses ≥ 60 mg also led to improvements in glycemic parameters, including reductions in fasting plasma glucose and HbA1c, which are consistent with the dual metabolic benefits observed with other GLP-1RAs9,19–27.

Overall, the safety and tolerability profile of HRS-7535 was consistent with that reported for the GLP-1 RA class7,10,11,28–32. Gastrointestinal (GI) adverse events were the most common treatment-emergent events and were predominantly mild to moderate. In the 120 mg and 180 mg groups, most GI events occurred during dose escalation, with peaks aligning with titration steps and generally diminished with continued treatment, which is an expected pattern for GLP-1–based therapies7,10,11,28–32. Consistent with the more rapid escalation schedule used for the 180 mg regimen (30 mg for 4 weeks followed by monthly 30 mg increments), rates of nausea (58.3%) and vomiting (43.8%) were higher than those observed with placebo (4.3% and 2.2%, respectively), while diarrhea occurred in 18.8% versus 8.7%.

Although the overall proportion of participants reporting any adverse event was high, discontinuations due to adverse events were uncommon across HRS-7535 groups (2.1–6.3%). In addition, treatment adherence was high across groups, and dose step-backs during titration were infrequent and largely related to GI intolerance, with most participants able to stabilize and continue treatment after dose adjustment. In this context, the tolerability profile compares favorably with discontinuation rates reported for liraglutide 3.0 mg in SCALE (9.6%)19 and for orforglipron (12–36 mg; 10–21%)11.

Events of special interest for GLP-1 RAs were carefully monitored. Cholelithiasis, a recognized risk with GLP-1 RAs9,19–27,33, was not observed; however, the modest sample size and 36-week duration limit inference for less frequent outcomes. Hypoglycemic was infrequent and comparable to other GLP-1 RAs30,32,34. Small increases in pulse and heart rate have been described previously for HRS-7535 and other GLP-1 RAs12,30,35, but no clinically meaningful changes were observed in this trial. At Week 36, amylase and lipase increased slightly versus placebo across HRS-7535 groups, consistent with class experience; transient elevations >3 × ULN occurred in a small number of participants and were not accompanied by clinical pancreatitis. Given prior transaminase findings reported with lotiglipron36, liver function was closely monitored; no participant met Hy’s Law criteria, and no clinically meaningful trends were observed in hepatic enzymes, calcitonin, vital signs, ECG parameters, or other laboratory measures. Collectively, these data indicate no new safety signals in this phase 2 study and support further evaluation of HRS-7535 in larger and longer phase 3 trials.

This trial has several limitations. First, the study population was ethnically homogeneous (Chinese adults), which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other racial and ethnic groups; extrapolation to broader global populations will require larger, longer-duration phase 3 trials with more diverse enrollment. Second, participants had a relatively low baseline body weight compared with cohorts in many global obesity trials, which may also affect the comparability of absolute weight-loss magnitudes across studies, although this profile is representative of adults with obesity in China. Third, as a phase 2 study with modest sample sizes per group, chance baseline imbalances may occur despite randomization. Mean baseline body weight was numerically higher in the 60 mg group than in the other groups; however, baseline body weight (and the baseline value of the endpoint) was included in the prespecified primary MMRM, which helps account for such differences.

In conclusion, once-daily oral HRS-7535 produced clinically meaningful weight loss in Chinese adults with obesity without diabetes, with significant placebo-adjusted reductions observed for doses ≥60 mg and effects maintained through Week 36. The safety and tolerability profile was consistent with the GLP-1RA class, with gastrointestinal adverse events occurring predominantly during dose escalation and generally manageable with continued treatment. Among the evaluated doses, the 180 mg group showed the largest weight reduction and evidence of continued weight loss through 36 weeks. As an oral, injection-free therapy that does not require strict fasting, HRS-7535 warrants further evaluation in larger and longer-term studies to confirm durability, optimize dosing, and define its benefit–risk profile for long-term obesity management.

Methods

Study design and participants

The study was conducted at 29 centers in China in accordance with local laws, the International Council for Harmonization (ICH) Good Clinical Practice guidelines, and the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol and informed consent form were approved by the ethics committee/institutional review board at each participating site (Table S15), including: Chinese PLA General Hospital; Peking University Third Hospital; The Fourth Hospital of Harbin Medical University; Jilin Province FAW General Hospital; Shengjing Hospital Affiliated to China Medical University; The Second Hospital of Hebei Medical University; Shanxi Provincial People’s Hospital; The First Affiliated Hospital of Xi’an Medical University; The Third People’s Hospital of Hefei; Nanyang Central Hospital; Zhongda Hospital, Southeast University; Tongji Hospital, Tongji University; Affiliated Hangzhou First People’s Hospital, Westlake University School of Medicine; Huangshi Central Hospital; The Third Xiangya Hospital of Central South University; Jiangxi Provincial People’s Hospital; Huizhou Central People’s Hospital; Chengdu Second People’s Hospital; The First Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University; The First Affiliated Hospital of Kunming Medical University; The First People’s Hospital of Lianyungang; Lianyungang Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine; The First Affiliated Hospital of Ningbo University; Xi’an No. 3 Hospital; The First People’s Hospital of Zhangjiagang; The First Affiliated Hospital of Shaoyang University; Deyang People’s Hospital; Guangyuan Central Hospital; and Suzhou Municipal Hospital. All participants provided written informed consent before any study procedures. Participants were enrolled between 23 February and 18 March 2024. The study was preregistered on ClinicalTrials.gov in February 2024 (NCT06250946). Study treatment was provided free of charge, and participants received transportation reimbursement.

This was a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled phase 2 trial comprising five sequential periods: screening (<2 weeks), a 2-week lead-in, a 26-week randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled core treatment period, a 10-week extension period, and a 2-week follow-up. Eligible participants were 18–65 years of age with a body mass index (BMI) of 28.0–40.0 kg/m² who had undertaken diet and exercise management for at least 3 months before screening and had experienced <5% change in body weight during the preceding 3 months. Key exclusion criteria were fasting plasma glucose (FPG) ≥ 7.0 mmol/L (126 mg/dL) or glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) ≥ 6.5%; uncontrolled hypertension (systolic blood pressure [SBP] ≥ 160 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure [DBP] ≥ 100 mmHg); endocrine disorders known to affect body weight (e.g., Cushing’s syndrome, hypo- or hyperthyroidism); a history of diabetes; a personal or family history of medullary thyroid carcinoma or multiple endocrine neoplasia type 2; a history of pancreatitis or symptomatic gallstones; and use of anti-obesity medications (e.g., orlistat or GLP‑1RAs) within 2 months before screening or prior bariatric surgery. Detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria can be found in the protocol.

Randomization and study procedures

Following screening, eligible participants were randomized in a 1:1:1:1:1 ratio to receive placebo or HRS-7535 at one of four target doses (30 mg, 60 mg, 120 mg, or 180 mg), administered once daily for 36 weeks. Randomization was stratified by sex and baseline BMI (<32.5 vs ≥32.5 kg/m²) and implemented centrally via an interactive web response system (IWRS). Treatment was assigned using unique kit numbers to maintain allocation concealment. In each active-treatment group, dosing commenced at 15 mg or 30 mg and was gradually escalated to the assigned target dose over up to 20 weeks. HRS-7535 and placebo tablets were indistinguishable in appearance, packaging, and labeling. Participants, investigators, study-site personnel, the sponsor team involved in trial conduct, and data analysts remained blinded to treatment assignment from randomization until database lock. Access to the randomization code was restricted to authorized personnel responsible for drug supply management or IWRS administration who were not involved in efficacy or safety assessments.

All participants, including those receiving placebo, followed a standardized lifestyle modification program that began during screening and continued throughout the 36-week treatment period. The program included individualized dietary counseling and physical activity guidance. Participants were instructed to reduce daily caloric intake by approximately 500 kcal from baseline and to engage in at least 150 min per week of moderate-intensity exercise (e.g., brisk walking or stair climbing). To support adherence, participants maintained weekly exercise logs and completed 3-day food diaries before each scheduled study visit. These records were reviewed at each counseling session to monitor adherence and to guide individualized adjustments to diet and physical activity recommendations, as needed.

Outcomes and assessments

The primary endpoint was the percentage change in body weight from baseline to Week 26. Prespecified secondary endpoints included the percentage change in body weight from baseline to Week 36; the proportions of participants achieving ≥5%, ≥10%, and ≥15% weight loss at Weeks 26 and 36; and changes from baseline to Weeks 26 and 36 in absolute body weight, waist circumference, body mass index (BMI), systolic and diastolic blood pressure, lipid parameters (total cholesterol [TC], triglycerides [TG], low-density lipoprotein cholesterol [LDL-C], and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol [HDL-C]), and glycaemic/metabolic measures (glycated hemoglobin [HbA1c], fasting plasma glucose [FPG], fasting serum insulin, and fasting C-peptide).

Prespecified subgroup analyses of the primary endpoint were conducted by sex (male vs female) and baseline BMI category (<32.5 vs ≥32.5 kg/m²). Additional exploratory analyses were performed, including a post hoc analysis excluding participants with missing post-baseline pharmacokinetic exposure data to assess exposure–response consistency; all subgroup and post hoc findings are reported as exploratory.

Safety assessments included monitoring of adverse events, vital signs, clinical laboratory tests, physical examinations, 12-lead electrocardiograms, and participant-reported outcomes. Participants who experienced symptoms suggestive of hypoglycemic or had a documented blood glucose level <3.9 mmol/L (<70 mg/dL) were instructed to record the episode in a dedicated hypoglycemic log (definitions are provided in Table S14).

Pharmacokinetic endpoints included plasma concentrations of HRS-7535; pharmacokinetic results are not presented in this manuscript and will be reported separately.

Statistical analysis

Efficacy was assessed in the intention-to-treat (ITT) population, defined as all randomized participants. Safety was evaluated in the safety population, defined as all participants who received at least one dose of study treatment.

A sample size of 180 participants (36 per group: placebo and HRS-7535 at 30, 60, 120, or 180 mg) provided 85% power to detect a 5-percentage-point difference in percentage change in body weight from baseline to Week 26 between the optimal HRS-7535 dose and placebo, assuming a standard deviation of 7% and a two-sided α of 0.05. Allowing for an anticipated 20% dropout rate, the planned enrollment was increased to 45 participants per group (total, 225 participants).

The primary endpoint was analyzed using a mixed-effects model for repeated measures (MMRM) with restricted maximum likelihood estimation. The model included fixed effects for treatment group, visit, sex, baseline BMI category (<32.5 vs ≥32.5 kg/m²), baseline body weight, and the treatment-by-visit interaction. Participants were treated as random effects, and an unstructured covariance matrix was specified for within-participant errors. Least-squares (LS) means and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated for each treatment group, together with LS mean differences versus placebo and corresponding two-sided P values.

The family-wise type I error rate for the primary endpoint across dose groups was controlled at a two-sided α = 0.05 using a prespecified hierarchical testing procedure. Superiority versus placebo was tested sequentially, starting with the 180 mg dose group and proceeding in descending order to the 30 mg dose group; testing stopped at the first dose level that did not achieve statistical significance. Secondary endpoints were analyzed without multiplicity adjustment, and nominal two-sided P values are reported. The primary estimand used a hypothetical strategy in which efficacy data collected after an intercurrent event were excluded from the primary analysis. Intercurrent events were defined as premature discontinuation of study treatment or initiation of prohibited anti-obesity medication before Week 26. Missing data, whether due to intercurrent events or other reasons, were assumed to be missing at random. To assess robustness under alternative missing-data assumptions, percent change in body weight from baseline to Week 26 was also analyzed in two sensitivity models: (1) an MMRM under a treatment-policy estimand using copy-reference multiple imputation (30 imputations) for post–intercurrent-event missing data (missing not at random), with results combined using Rubin’s rules; and (2) an MMRM excluding baseline BMI category under the hypothetical estimand, assuming missing at random.

Continuous secondary efficacy endpoints were analyzed using the same MMRM framework as the primary endpoint. For categorical responder outcomes at Weeks 26 and 36 (≥5%, ≥10%, or ≥15% weight loss), non-responder imputation was applied such that participants without a body weight measurement at the relevant visit were classified as non-responders. Treatment effects versus placebo were evaluated using logistic regression models with treatment group, sex, and baseline BMI category as fixed effects and baseline body weight as a covariate. Two-sided 95% confidence intervals for responder rates were calculated using the Clopper–Pearson method. Additional post hoc analyses were performed in the ITT population excluding participants with (1) ≥ 2 consecutive post-baseline pharmacokinetic (PK) values below the lower limit of quantification (BLQ) or (2) ≥ 1 post-baseline PK value BLQ. We also conducted a post hoc sex-stratified analysis of percentage change from baseline in body weight through Week 36. All analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). The statistical methodology is detailed in the Statistical Analysis Plan.
