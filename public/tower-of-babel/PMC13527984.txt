Bovine lactoferrin induces apoptosis and modulates the transcriptomic landscape of HeLa cervical cancer cells
Authors: Yu Zhao, Rui Liu, Mengtian Li, Mingzhuang Feng, Yuting Bao, Guoxin Chen, Haixia Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cervical cancer (CC) remains a leading malignancy among women worldwide. Current management strategies, including surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and prevention, often cause significant adverse effects and demonstrate limited efficacy in advanced disease. Consequently, identifying novel therapeutic strategies and drug targets, particularly low-toxicity, high-specificity natural agents-constitutes a critical research priority. Bovine lactoferrin (bLF), a multifunctional iron-binding glycoprotein, demonstrates therapeutic potential, though its precise molecular mechanisms in cervical cancer are incompletely understood. This study therefore examined the biochemical and transcriptomic alterations in bLF-treated cervical cancer cells to elucidate its mode of action. bLF suppressed HeLa cell viability in a dose-dependent manner and concurrently promoted apoptosis while inhibiting proliferation and migration. The bLF-induced apoptosis appears to arise from a synergistic multi-mechanistic interplay, potentially involving increased intracellular ROS levels, downregulated mitochondrial membrane potential, or modulated expression of autophagy-related proteins to activate autophagic pathways. RNA sequencing identified substantial transcriptomic changes in cancer-associated pathways, notably affecting cell cycle progression, protein transport, apoptotic processes, and the negative regulation of apoptosis. These results substantiate the potent anticancer activity of bLF and highlight its promise as a therapeutic candidate derived from a natural product.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13062-026-00859-4.

Introduction

Cervical cancer, or uterine cervical cancer, primarily results from persistent infection with human papillomavirus (HPV). It represents the second most common malignancy in women worldwide, following breast cancer [1]. Global statistics from 2022 reported roughly 660,000 new cases, comprising 3.3% of all female cancers and confirming its status as the second most frequent cancer specific to women. Current treatments-such as surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and preventive approaches exhibit significant clinical limitations and adverse effects. This situation underscores the urgent need for alternative cervical cancer therapies.

Apoptosis is a tightly regulated form of programmed cell death essential for tissue homeostasis, removal of damaged cells, and suppression of tumor development. Research indicates that disruption of apoptotic pathways in cancer can cause aberrant cell death, facilitating tumor initiation, progression, and metastasis. Investigating the molecular regulation of apoptosis in cervical cancer thus advances understanding of its pathology and establishes a theoretical basis for new treatment approaches. Natural compounds are increasingly studied for cancer prevention and therapy owing to their low toxicity and diverse biological activities. Lactoferrin (LF), which includes human (hLF) and bovine (bLF) forms, is a multifunctional bioactive protein with potential for treating various diseases, including viral infections and cancer [2]. In antiviral research, LF has emerged as a promising candidate for managing HPV and other viral infections. Several studies have shown that LF inhibits HPV by interfering with viral binding and entry into host cells [3–5]. Using ex vivo HeLa cultures to model HPV infection, researchers co-incubated HPV virus-like particles (VLPs) or host cells with different LF concentrations and assessed viral binding and uptake. These experiments demonstrated that LF blocks HPV VLP entry and attachment to host cells in a dose-dependent manner, with stronger inhibition at higher concentrations [3]. Therefore, LF holds clinical potential for cancer treatment. Although bLF is known to exert anti-cancer effects in HeLa cells, the mechanism remains poorly understood, particularly at the transcriptomic level. This study aims to clarify this mechanism and provide evidence for bLF’s anti-cancer action in HeLa cells.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and reagents

HeLa cells and human embryonic kidney 293 (HEK-293) cells were obtained from Lanzhou Minhai Bioengineering cell bank (Lanzhou, China). Both cell lines were cultured in DMEM/HIGH GLUCOSE medium (Lanzhou Minhai Bioengineering Co., Lanzhou, China) supplemented with 10% newborn bovine serum (NBS, Lanzhou Minhai Bioengineering Co., Lanzhou, China). The cells were maintained at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ atmosphere. Bovine lactoferrin (bLF) was obtained from MacKlin Inc. (Shanghai, China).

Assessing the cytotoxicity of bLF

Cytotoxicity was assessed using the CCK-8 assay (New Cell &Molecular Biotech Co., Ltd). Cells were exposed to bLF at concentrations ranging from 0 to 2.5 mg/mL (0, 0.3, 0.5, 1.0, 1.5, and 2.0 mg/mL) for 24 h, followed by the addition of 20 µL of CCK-8 solution to each well. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured with a microplate reader (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA).

Wound healing assay

Cells were seeded in 6-well plates and cultured until reaching approximately 70% confluence. After wounding the monolayer with 200 µL pipette tips, the cell were treated with bLF at concentrations ranging from 0 to 2.0 mg/mL (0, 0.3, 0.5, 1.0, 1.5, and 2.0 mg/mL). Following a 1×PBS wash to remove detached cells, the cultures were maintained at 37℃ for 12 and 24 h. Microscopic images of the wound margins were taken at both time points to accurately assess the rate of closure.

Transwell assay

When the cells reached 50%–60% confluence, the medium was replaced with serum-free medium, and the cells were starved for 24 h. After starvation, the cells were digested and resuspended in fresh culture medium. A 200 µL cell suspension (1 × 10⁴ cells per well) was seeded into the upper chamber, while the lower chamber received medium containing 10% NBS and 0, 0.5, or 1.0 mg/mL bLF. The chambers were incubated at 37℃ with 5% CO₂ for 24 h. Migrated cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 30 min and then stained with crystal violet for 20 min. After removing the chamber, excess liquid was carefully blotted with a cotton swab, and images were captured using an inverted microscope under standardized parameters.

Hoechst 33,342 staining

Hoechst 33,342 staining (Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) was used to examine cell morphology following a 24-hour treatment with bLF at concentrations of 0, 0.3, 0.5, 1.0, 1.5, and 2.0 mg/mL. The stained cells were visualized with an Olympus fluorescence microscope (Olympus Corporation, Japan).

ROS assay

Intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels were measured after cells were treated with bLF (0, 0.5, and 1.0 mg/mL) for 24 h. The cells were then loaded with probes and incubated at 37℃ in 5% CO₂ for 20 min to permit adequate probe-cell contact. After incubation, the cells were imaged using an Olympus fluorescence microscope (Olympus Corporation, Japan).

Mitochondrial membrane potential assay

A mitochondrial membrane potential and apoptosis detection kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai) was used for analysis. Cells treated with bLF (0, 0.5, and 1.0 mg/mL) for 24 h were incubated with the Mito-Tracker Red CMXRos probe. The cells were subsequently visualized with an Olympus fluorescence microscope (Olympus Corporation, Japan).

JC-1 staining assay

The JC-1 mitochondrial membrane potential detection kit (Dalian Meilun Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) was employed. After a 24-hour treatment with bLF (0, 0.5, and 1.0 mg/mL), the cells were stained with freshly prepared JC-1 solution under light-protected conditions for 20 min. Fluorescence microscopy (Olympus Corporation, Japan) was then used for observation and analysis.

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) was performed with a BGI Optimal series two-module mRNA library construction kit (BGI-Shenzhen, China), and PE100/PE150 sequencing was subsequently conducted on G400/T7/T10 platforms employing combined probe anchor polymerization (cPAS). Functional annotation of differentially expressed genes and pathway enrichment analysis were carried out using GO and KEGG databases.

Overexpression and SiRNA transfection

To overexpress the SYP gene, SYP cDNA was amplified and cloned into the pcDNA3.1(+) vector. SYP-targeting siRNAs were also designed to produce cervical cancer cells featuring either SYP overexpression or knockdown. HeLa cells underwent transient transfection with Lipofectamine™ 3000 (Thermo Fisher) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After 48 h, SYP overexpression and knockdown efficiency were verified by western blotting prior to subsequent experiments.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was isolated using Trizol reagent (TaKara Company), and cDNA was synthesized with an extraction kit (Hunan Aierui Bioengineering Co., Ltd.). Amplification was performed by SYBR Green real-time quantitative PCR on an ABI PRISM 7500 system (Applied Biosystems, Carlsbad, CA). Relative gene expression levels were quantified via the 2−ΔΔCT method, using GAPDH as the internal control. Primer sequences are listed in Table 1.

Western blot assay

Protein lysates were prepared in RIPA buffer (Solarbio) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Total protein concentrations were determined with a BCA Protein Assay Kit. Proteins were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore), which were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies. The primary antibodies used included: SNAIL1 (#13099-1-AP, 1:1000, Proteintech, China), E-cadherin (#20874-1-AP, 1:1000, Proteintech, China), RPH3AL (#15297-1-AP, 1:1000, Proteintech, China), SYP (#R25834, 1:500, Zhengneng Bio, China), β-tubulin (#TA503129, 1:1000, OriGene), and GAPDH (#ET1601-4, 1:1000, HUABIO). Membranes were subsequently incubated with the corresponding species-specific secondary antibody at room temperature for 50 min. Immunoblots were visualized using ECL Blotting Substrate (Dalian Meilun Biotechnology Co., Ltd.). GAPDH served as the loading control, and band intensities were quantified with ImageJ software.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed with GraphPad Prism 8.0. Comparisons among more than two groups utilized one-way or two-way ANOVA, whereas Student’s t-test was applied for pairwise comparisons. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation from at least three independent replicates. A p-value below 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Asterisks denote significance levels as follows: *** p < 0.001, ** p < 0.01 and * p < 0.05.

Results

Numerous studies have demonstrated that LF exhibits antiviral activity by preventing HPV binding to host cells [3–5, 6–8]. Using HeLa cervical cancer cells as a model system, the inhibitory effects of bLF and its underlying mechanisms were investigated to further elucidate its anticancer potential. CCK-8 assays showed that increasing bLF concentrations reduced HeLa cell viability in a dose-dependent manner, while HEK-293 cell viability remained unaffected, confirming bLF’s selective cytotoxicity (Fig. 1A). Higher concentrations of bLF induced morphological changes and promoted apoptosis (Fig. 1B). Nuclear morphology was evaluated using Hoechst 33,258 staining, revealing a significant increase in apoptotic cells with characteristic nuclear condensation after bLF treatment (Fig. 1C). Scratch wound and Transwell assays demonstrated that bLF dose-dependently inhibited cell migration compared to untreated controls (Fig. 2A-C). Snail critically regulates epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) by suppressing epithelial cell adhesion molecules such as E-cadherin, thereby disrupting intercellular junctions and enhancing cellular migration and invasion. The reduced E-cadherin expression associated with EMT serves as a key indicator of tumor cell metastatic potential. Gene and protein expression analyses revealed decreased MMP-2 and Snail levels alongside elevated E-cadherin expression, findings subsequently confirmed through protein quantification (Fig. 2D-F). To investigate the mechanism by which bLF induces apoptosis in HeLa cells, preliminary research found that increasing bLF doses upregulated the expression of the pro-apoptotic proteins P53, Bax, and Caspase-3. Concurrently, the expression of the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2 was downregulated. Excessive autophagy can induce apoptosis by damaging cellular components, depleting energy, and activating apoptotic pathways. Therefore, a preliminary study titled “Regulation of HeLa cell proliferation and apoptosis by bovine lactoferrin” found that the LC3-II/LC3-I ratio rose significantly with increasing bLF concentration, reflecting enhanced autophagosome formation. Concurrent decreases in p62 and increases in Beclin-1 further indicated that bLF activates autophagy. The addition of 3-MA subsequently inhibited the bLF-induced changes in autophagy markers within HeLa cells. This autophagy was further shown to promote bLF-induced apoptosis, demonstrating that the autophagic process actively facilitates bLF-mediated cell death [9]. In summary, autophagy regulates bLF-induced apoptosis in HeLa cells, while the execution of apoptosis depends on precise molecular signaling and exhibits close crosstalk with autophagy.

Apoptosis represents a genetically regulated form of programmed cell death that occurs through orderly molecular mechanisms under physiological or pathological conditions, primarily via mitochondrial and death receptor pathways. Reactive oxygen species (ROS) also function as a signaling hub that regulates both autophagy and apoptosis. To elucidate the apoptotic mechanism of bLF in HeLa cells, a reactive oxygen species (ROS) assay was performed, demonstrating dose-dependent ROS accumulation and indicating that oxidative stress contributes to bLF-induced apoptosis (Fig. 3A). Mitochondrial damage is a key marker of early apoptosis, primarily manifested by the loss of mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP). Increasing bLF concentrations decreased red fluorescence intensity and increased green fluorescence in HeLa cells, indicating that bLF promotes MMP dissipation (Fig. 3B). JC-1 staining further confirmed that bLF induced mitochondrial membrane potential changes in a concentration-dependent manner, thereby promoting apoptosis (Fig. 3C). These collective findings demonstrated that bLF induces apoptosis through coordinated multi-pathway mechanisms.

To investigate the effect of bLF on HeLa cell apoptosis more systematically, RNA sequencing and bioinformatics analysis were performed on bLF-treated cells. Compared with untreated cells, 2558 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified, including 1394 up-regulated and 1164 down-regulated genes (Fig. 4A). GO enrichment analysis revealed 1088 significantly enriched genes in cellular component terms, with many DEGs localized to the cytoplasm and nucleus, suggesting their involvement in cytoplasmic metabolic processes and nuclear transcriptional regulation. DEGs were significantly associated with biological processes such as cell cycle, protein transport, apoptotic process, and negative regulation of apoptosis, including genes like Synaptophysin (SYP) and AKT serine/threonine kinase 2 (AKT2) (Fig. 4B). KEGG pathway analysis indicated enrichment of DEGs in major pathways including nucleocytoplasmic transport, mitophagy, and the AMPK signaling pathway, implying substantial effects on cellular metabolism and immune responses under these experimental conditions (Fig. 4C). To determine whether bLF-induced apoptosis in HeLa cells is mediated by specific genes, the most significantly upregulated and downregulated genes were selected for validation at both the gene and protein levels. The mRNA and protein expression of SYP was significantly up-regulated, whereas Rabphilin-3 A-like protein (RPH3AL) showed significant down-regulation, consistent with the transcriptomic DEG screening results (Fig. 4D-E). Studies have demonstrated a relationship between changes in SYP protein expression and cellular apoptosis. For example, in a chronic unpredictable mild stress (CUMS) model of depression in rats, hippocampal SYP protein levels decreased significantly, while cleaved caspase-3 was upregulated and Bcl-2 expression was downregulated [10]. These findings suggest that reduced SYP expression may be linked to increased apoptotic activity. In Alzheimer’s disease research, activation of autophagy markedly increased neuronal SYP protein levels, implying that autophagy may modulate SYP expression or function through an unknown mechanism. Based on these observations, the SYP gene was overexpressed and its effect on apoptosis in HeLa cells was evaluated. Transfection with either the pcDNA3.1-SYP recombinant plasmid or an empty vector showed that both SYP mRNA and protein levels were significantly elevated in the pcDNA3.1-SYP group, confirming successful SYP overexpression (Fig. 4F-G). The impact of SYP overexpression on the clonogenic ability of HeLa cells was then examined, revealing a significant reduction in colony formation, which was further inhibited by the addition of bLF (Fig. 4H). To investigate the underlying mechanisms, apoptosis- and autophagy-related pathways were assessed following SYP overexpression. Overexpression of SYP led to a marked increase in the pro-apoptotic protein Bax and a decrease in the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2, indicating promotion of apoptosis. Concurrently, elevated expression of the autophagy-related proteins ATG5 and Beclin1, along with an increased LC3-II/LC3-I ratio, suggested that SYP overexpression also induces autophagy (Fig. 4I). SYP knockdown was then performed using three specific siRNAs (SYP-siRNA#1–3) and a negative control (SYP-siRNA#NC). Transfection with SYP-siRNA significantly reduced both SYP mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 4J), though it did not affect HeLa cell viability (Fig. 4K). Knockdown of SYP resulted in decreased expression of Bax, ATG5, Beclin1, and the LC3-II/LC3-I ratio, implying that SYP participates in regulating both apoptosis and autophagy in HeLa cells via these pathways (Fig. 4L). Overall, these results provide important insights into the molecular mechanisms through which bLF influences apoptosis and autophagy.

Discussion

Lactoferrin’s anticancer properties have been documented across multiple malignancies [11–12]. However, its mechanism of action in cervical cancer remains incompletely understood. Recent studies highlight the context-dependent nature of lactoferrin-induced cell death: bLF primarily triggers apoptosis and autophagy in HeLa cells by upregulating p53 and cleaved caspase-3 while downregulating Bcl-2, yet it follows distinct pathways in other cell types [9, 13]. This cell-type specificity underscores the need to investigate bLF’s mechanisms specifically in cervical cancer. In this study, bLF treatment was observed to suppress proliferation, migration, and viability in HeLa cells (Figs. 1 and 2). Apoptosis is essential for tissue homeostasis and tumor suppression, regulated by NF-κB and MAPK pathways, as well as intrinsic mitochondrial and extrinsic death receptor mechanisms [14]. Tumor progression is facilitated by evasion of apoptosis. The data suggest that bLF participates in an “apoptosis- autophagy” regulatory network in HeLa cells, as evidenced by elevated intracellular ROS levels, increased apoptosis, disrupted mitochondrial membrane potential, and enhanced autophagy (Fig. 3) [9].

Transcriptomic screening identified synaptophysin (SYP) as a differentially expressed gene; its overexpression promoted both apoptosis and autophagy, whereas knockdown inhibited these processes. Although SYP is recognized as a neuroendocrine marker in prostate cancer [15], and related family members such as SYPL1 have been implicated in pancreatic cancer progression [16], its role in cervical cancer and regulation by lactoferrin have not been reported previously. These findings suggest that SYP contributes to bLF’s antitumor effects.

Functional enrichment analysis revealed that differentially expressed genes are primarily involved in metabolic processes, cell cycle regulation, protein transport, and apoptosis. KEGG pathway analysis further showed that bLF significantly affects nucleocytoplasmic transport, mitophagy, and the AMPK signaling pathway (Fig. 4). AMPK acts as a cellular energy sensor coordinating autophagy and apoptosis via shared signaling nodes [17–18], with its activation linked to mitophagy during mitochondrial dysfunction [19]. These findings support the hypothesis that bLF engages AMPK-mediated energy stress responses. At the molecular level, bLF treatment increased E-cadherin and decreased Snail protein expression, correlating with reduced cell migration. Concurrently, changes in Bcl-2 family proteins (increased Bax and p53, decreased Bcl-2) accompanied mitochondrial dysfunction and impaired redox metabolism. Excessive autophagy induced by bLF may further facilitate apoptosis, warranting additional investigation.

The observation that bLF modulates the AMPK pathway aligns with reports from other cancer models [17]. However, the relationship between lactoferrin and ROS is complex: while this study observed increased intracellular ROS, lactoferrin’s iron-chelating properties have been shown to reduce ROS production in certain contexts [20]. This paradox may reflect dose-dependent effects or differences in cellular iron homeostasis. Additionally, compounds like Gboxin have demonstrated simultaneous autophagy, apoptosis, and ferroptosis in cervical cancer cells, supported by in vivo validation [21], whereas this study focused on in vitro characterization.

This study has several limitations. All experiments were conducted in vitro using a single cervical cancer cell line (HeLa), which may not fully capture the heterogeneity of the tumor microenvironment or physiological conditions in vivo. The absence of in vivo validation (e.g., xenograft or syngeneic tumor models) limits assessment of pharmacokinetics, systemic toxicity, and true antitumor efficacy of bLF, although prior in vivo studies in breast cancer models support its translational potential [22, 23]. Moreover, while transcriptomic screening identified SYP as a candidate mediator, direct molecular interactions between bLF and SYP or the precise mechanisms by which SYP regulates autophagy and apoptosis remain unclear. Therefore, the proposed “apoptosis–autophagy” network should be viewed as a working hypothesis, requiring further exploration with genetic rescue models and in vivo studies.

Despite these limitations, this study identifies SYP as a novel contributor to bLF’s anticancer activity in cervical cancer and lays the groundwork for mechanistic follow-up and in vivo validation.

Conclusion

In summary, this study reveals the potential mechanisms and application value of lactoferrin in HeLa cell proliferation. Preliminary in vitro experiments investigated the molecular basis for bLF-mediated inhibition of HeLa cell growth. The results demonstrate that bLF suppresses HeLa cell viability, and alters cellular morphology. Higher bLF concentrations further diminished cell motility and downregulated migration-associated marker proteins, indicating multiple inhibitory mechanisms. bLF not only induces apoptosis in HeLa cells but also activates autophagy by modulating autophagy-related proteins. Co-treatment with the autophagy inhibitor 3-MA showed that bLF-induced apoptosis is autophagy-dependent. RNA sequencing and bioinformatics analysis identified differentially expressed genes in bLF-treated HeLa cells. Overexpression of SYP enhanced apoptosis and autophagy, whereas SYP knockdown attenuated these effects. These findings collectively support bLF as a promising natural anticancer agent. Although limited to in vitro studies, this work provides mechanistic insights into bLF’s anti-cervical cancer activity and uncovers novel transcriptomic targets.
