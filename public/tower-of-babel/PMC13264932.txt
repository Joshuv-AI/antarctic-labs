Effect of Aerobic exercise on depression symptoms and quality of life in Patients with major depressive disorder: a systematic review Protocol
Authors: Saad Bin Muaythir, Bashir Bello, Nombeko Mshunqane, Enos Ramano, Nontembiso Magida
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionMajor depressive disorder (MDD) is a prevalent mental illness characterised by persistent sadness, loss of interest in activities and cognitive impairment. While pharmacological and psychotherapeutic treatments remain the standard for MDD management, non-pharmacological interventions, such as aerobic exercise, have gained attention for their potential benefits in reducing depressive symptoms and improving quality of life. Although several studies have explored the effectiveness of aerobic exercise in managing MDD, there is still no comprehensive synthesis of the existing evidence. This study aims to synthesise existing evidence on the effects of aerobic exercise interventions in the management of individuals diagnosed with MDD.MethodsThe systematic review will be conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Protocol (PRISMA-P) guidelines. A comprehensive search will be conducted across Cochrane, Medline, PEDro, CINAHL, Scopus, Web of Science and BioMed Central databases. Search terms will be developed using the Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome and Study design (PICOS) framework, incorporating keywords and Medical Subject Headings related to ‘Major Depressive Disorder’, ‘Aerobic Exercise’, ‘Depression’, and ‘Quality of Life’. Only intervention studies, including randomised controlled trials, quasi-experimental and pre–post intervention studies, will be included involving adults aged 18 years or older diagnosed with MDD according to standardised criteria (eg, Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition and International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems -10 (ICD-10)). For included intervention studies, the comparator will be standard care, placebo or no-exercise control groups. The primary outcome is change in depressive symptoms, and secondary outcomes include quality of life, anxiety and stress-related biomarkers. Three independent reviewers will screen studies, extract data using Covidence software (Veritas Health Innovation in partnership with Cochrane) and assess study quality using the updated Cochrane Risk of Bias 2.0 (Rob-2) tool alongside the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach. If feasible, a meta-analysis will be conducted using RevMan V.5.4 (Cochrane's Review Manager), with effect sizes determined by mean differences, standardised mean differences or ORs, depending on the outcome type. This study is currently at the proposal stage, with article searches expected to begin in November 2025 and data extraction anticipated to be completed by January 2026.Ethics and disseminationNo ethical approval is required as this review uses existing published data. Findings will be disseminated through a peer-reviewed journal and presented at academic conferences.PROSPERO registrationCRD420251151897.

Introduction

Depression is one of the leading causes of years lived with disability globally, ranking second among all non-fatal health conditions in the 2019 Global Burden of Disease Study.1 These disorders are characterised by clinically significant disruptions in behaviour, emotional regulation and cognitive processes, often leading to distress and impaired daily functioning.2 According to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition, Text Revision (DSM-5-TR), psychiatric disorders include schizophrenia spectrum disorders, bipolar disorder and major depressive disorder (MDD).3 MDD is a chronic and recurrent mental illness characterised by persistent low mood, diminished interest in activities, low energy levels, feelings of guilt, low self-esteem, sleep and appetite disturbances and impaired concentration. These symptoms significantly impair social and occupational functioning.46

Globally, MDD has a point prevalence of approximately 13.3% among adults7 and over 10% among children.8 It is a leading cause of disability-adjusted life years and is projected to become the leading cause of disability by 2030.9 Furthermore, MDD is associated with a substantial decline in health-related quality of life.5 10 Given its high prevalence and socioeconomic impact, effective management strategies are essential to mitigate its burden. The management of MDD traditionally involves a combination of pharmacotherapy and psychotherapy as first-line treatments, as recommended by the American Psychiatric Association Practice Guideline for the treatment of MDD.11 12 However, there is increasing recognition of non-pharmacological interventions, particularly exercise-based therapies, in improving mental health outcomes. Among these, aerobic exercise has gained significant attention as a complementary approach to managing MDD symptoms.13

Aerobic or cardiovascular exercise includes activities such as walking, jogging and cycling, which engage large muscle groups and enhance cardiovascular endurance and overall fitness.14 15 While public health guidelines, such as those from the WHO, recommend 75–150 min/week of moderate-to-vigorous aerobic activity for general health benefits,16 this dosage is not specifically derived from evidence on MDD. Clinical trials in MDD have used a wide range of exercise prescriptions, and there is currently no consensus on an optimal dose for reducing depressive symptoms. Nevertheless, randomised controlled trials consistently demonstrate that aerobic exercise produces significant antidepressant effects across varying intensities and durations.17

Mechanistically, aerobic exercise is thought to alleviate depression by increasing endorphins, modulating neurotransmitters such as serotonin and dopamine, and reducing inflammation.141820

Several studies have examined the effects of aerobic exercise on depressive symptoms and quality of life.2125 While many of these studies report positive outcomes, they often involve mixed interventions, such as combining aerobic exercise with pharmacotherapy or psychotherapy or focus on general depression rather than MDD specifically. Additionally, a previous systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrated the effectiveness of aerobic exercise in reducing depression and improving quality of life, but it was conducted in healthy adults rather than individuals diagnosed with MDD.24 Given these limitations in the existing evidence, there remains a critical need for a systematic review that focuses explicitly on aerobic exercise interventions tailored for individuals with MDD.

Recent systematic reviews have examined the effects of exercise interventions in adults with MDD, including some meta-analyses that specifically explore cognitive outcomes.26 However, much of this literature combines aerobic exercise with other modalities, resulting in considerable clinical heterogeneity and limiting the ability to isolate the specific effects of aerobic exercise. This distinction is important because aerobic exercise is associated with distinct neurobiological mechanisms, including increased brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) expression and enhanced cerebral perfusion, which may differentially influence cognitive and emotional outcomes. Focusing exclusively on aerobic exercise, therefore, enables a clearer evaluation of its therapeutic effects and provides a stronger basis for developing targeted, evidence-based exercise recommendations for individuals with MDD. Although this evidence underscores the potential benefits of aerobic exercise for areas like cognitive function, these reviews did not assess patient-centred outcomes such as quality of life or reductions in symptoms. Currently, there is no systematic review that has thoroughly compiled the effects of aerobic exercise on both depressive symptoms and quality of life in adults with MDD. Consequently, this protocol aims to fill a significant gap in existing research by outlining a rigorous and transparent method for evaluating these outcomes. Besides the previously mentioned points, aerobic exercise deserves particular attention in adults with MDD because it stands out as one of the few non-drug treatments that have clear physiological and psychological mechanisms relevant to the fundamental characteristics of MDD. Evidence suggests that exercise is associated with several neurobiological mechanisms relevant to MDD, including increased BDNF,27 modulation of inflammatory processes28 and improved regulation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis and stress response.29 While these mechanisms have largely been examined in the context of exercise or physical activity more broadly rather than specific modalities, aerobic exercise represents the most commonly prescribed and extensively studied form of exercise in clinical populations. As such, it is likely to be a key contributor to these observed effects. Focusing specifically on aerobic exercise therefore enables a more precise evaluation of its therapeutic role, while reducing the clinical heterogeneity introduced by multimodal or combined exercise interventions. This solid mechanistic foundation strongly supports the need to investigate aerobic exercise. The analysis conducted by Morres et al30 concentrated mainly on mental health service contexts and failed to evaluate patient-centred outcomes like quality of life, nor did it incorporate the substantial amount of new evidence that has come to light since 2018, which includes more recent large-scale trials and meta-analytic studies. Furthermore, earlier reviews often combined various exercise modalities, complicating the ability to determine the specific effects of aerobic exercise alone. Our review overcomes these shortcomings of Yan et al1 by concentrating solely on aerobic exercise interventions,2 including only adults diagnosed with clinically significant MDD, and assessing both depressive symptoms and quality of life as the main outcomes. This results in a more clinically applicable synthesis to aid in the formulation of guidelines and support patient-centred decision-making. This study aims to synthesise current evidence, evaluate the quality of existing research and provide clinical insights on the role of aerobic exercise in managing MDD.

Objectives

This study aims to systematically review and synthesise existing evidence on the effects of aerobic exercise interventions in the management of individuals diagnosed with MDD, with a specific focus on their impact on depressive symptom severity and quality of life.

Methods

Study design

Only intervention studies, including randomised controlled trials and other controlled intervention designs (eg, non-randomised controlled trials or quasi-experimental studies), will be included. Studies must be written in English and available in full text. Studies will not be limited by publication year. Eligible studies must investigate the effectiveness of aerobic interventions in managing MDD and use randomised controlled or quasi-experimental designs. This systematic review will adhere to standard reporting guidelines as described by Souto et al31 and follow the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Protocol (PRISMA-P) methodology. The study protocol is registered with PROSPERO (CRD420251151897) to ensure methodological transparency. The study is scheduled to start the literature search in November 2025, with data extraction to be completed by January 2026, aligning with the publication timelines.

Search strategy

An information specialist will assist in developing a comprehensive search strategy that combines Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and free-text keywords to ensure both sensitivity and specificity in identifying relevant studies. The following is the search strategy pilot used from PubMed database: (‘Major Depressive Disorder’ OR ‘Depression’ OR ‘Depressive Disorders’) AND (‘Aerobic Exercise’ OR ‘Physical Activity’ OR ‘Exercise Therapy’) AND (‘Intervention’ OR ‘Programme’ OR ‘Programme’ OR ‘Therapy’ OR ‘Treatment’) AND (‘Effectiveness’ OR ‘Efficacy’ OR ‘Impact’) AND (‘Depressive Symptoms’ OR ‘Anxiety Scale’ OR ‘Mental Health Outcomes’). Boolean operators ‘AND’ or ‘OR’ will be applied to refine search results. Search queries will be customised for each database, integrating MeSH terms where applicable. Electronic searches will be conducted in multiple databases, including PubMed, Embase, CINAHL, PsycINFO and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials. In addition, grey literature sources such as clinical trial registries, conference proceedings and dissertations will be searched to capture unpublished or ongoing studies. A systematic record of the search process, including search strategies, databases searched and search dates, will be maintained using the Covidence software to ensure transparency and reproducibility.

Patient and public involvement

None. Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct or reporting of this research protocol.

Eligibility criteria

This systematic review will include studies that meet the following criteria, structured according to the PICOS framework: the ‘Population’, ‘Intervention’, ‘Comparison’, ‘Outcomes’ and ‘Study Characteristics’.32

Population and diagnostic criteria

Adults aged 18 years and older diagnosed with MDD using standardised criteria from either the DSM-5 or ICD-10 will be included. The DSM-533 defines MDD as the presence of at least five out of nine possible symptoms, such as low mood, loss of interest or pleasure, fatigue, feelings of guilt and impaired concentration, lasting for a minimum of 2 weeks. The ICD-10, developed by the WHO,34 identifies MDD under codes F32 and F33, based on core symptoms, such as depressed mood, reduced energy and loss of interest.

Ensuring MDD-specific data

These classification systems ensure consistency across studies and support the generalisability of the review. For studies involving mixed psychiatric diagnoses (eg, MDD with bipolar disorder), only data specific to participants with MDD will be extracted. This will be done using subgroup analyses, stratified results or outcomes explicitly reported for MDD participants. Studies that do not provide separable MDD-specific data will be excluded from the review.

Intervention

The intervention will include studies that examine different modalities of aerobic exercise, such as running, swimming, walking or cycling. Only studies that meet the eligibility criteria for study design will be considered, ensuring consistency across the methods section. Studies with combined interventions, such as aerobic exercise plus cognitive-behavioural therapy, will be included only if the effect of aerobic exercise is reported separately, allowing the review to assess its independent contribution to outcomes.

Comparator

The comparator will be the standard care, placebo or no exercise intervention only.

Outcome

The outcome will be studies reporting changes in depressive symptoms using standardised depression assessment tools, such as the Beck Depression Inventory and the Hamilton Depression Rating Scale. Secondary outcomes may include improvements in quality of life (eg, 36-Item Short Form Survey), reduction in anxiety, or changes in stress biomarkers.

Study selection

Two reviewers (SBA and NM) will independently screen titles and abstracts in Covidence, followed by full-text review against eligibility criteria. A third reviewer (BB) will resolve disputes. A PRISMA flow diagram will document the process, detailing the studies screened, included and excluded, along with the reasons for exclusion.

Risk of bias and quality assessment

Each included study will be assessed for risk of bias using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2.0 (RoB 2) tool for randomised controlled trials and the Risk Of Bias In Non-randomized Studies of Interventions-I tool for non-randomised studies.35 The following domains will be evaluated: selection bias (randomisation and allocation concealment), performance bias (blinding of participants and personnel), detection bias (blinding of outcome assessment), attrition bias (incomplete outcome data), reporting bias (selective reporting) and other biases (study design-related concerns). Each domain will be rated as low, high or unclear risk of bias. Three reviewers (NoM, ER and BB) will conduct assessments, resolving disagreements via discussion. Reporting bias will be assessed by comparing study protocols (when available) with published reports to detect selective outcome reporting. Additionally, when at least 10 studies are included in a meta-analysis, funnel plots and statistical tests (eg, Egger’s test) will be used to evaluate potential publication bias across studies. The Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach will evaluate evidence quality across outcomes.36 Evidence will be assessed across the standard GRADE domains: risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision and publication bias. Downgrading will follow established GRADE criteria, with −1 applied for serious concerns and −2 for very serious concerns in any domain. High-bias studies may be excluded from meta-analysis or analysed separately, using sensitivity analyses, and these decisions will inform the GRADE rating for each outcome. This approach ensures that the evidence quality assessment is transparent, consistent and reproducible.

Data extraction

Data extraction will be conducted using Covidence software to ensure precision and consistency. The extracted data will include: study characteristics (author(s), publication year, study title, study design, sample size, participant demographics (eg, age and sex), follow-up duration and study setting); intervention details (type, frequency, intensity, duration and mode of delivery of aerobic exercise interventions); comparator characteristics; description of the control group (eg, standard care, placebo and no exercise intervention); outcome measures will include primary and secondary outcomes, including depressive symptoms (eg, Beck Depression Inventory and Hamilton Depression Rating Scale) and quality of life measures. Pilot testing of the data extraction form will be conducted using a subset of studies to assess accuracy and feasibility. The three reviewers (SBA, NM and BB) will extract data, with the other two reviewers (NoM and ER) adjudicating discrepancies. Unresolved cases will be excluded from meta-analysis if key variables (eg, effect sizes) are absent.

Data synthesis

Data synthesis will integrate both narrative and quantitative methods, with a meta-analysis conducted as the primary objective where data availability allows. A narrative synthesis will use thematic analysis to code study findings, aiming to identify recurring patterns related to symptom reduction, improvements in quality of life and enhancements in overall well-being.

For the meta-analysis, a random-effects model will be used in RevMan 5.4 to accommodate the anticipated heterogeneity among studies.37 Effect measures will comprise:

Mean differences (MD): applied to continuous outcomes (eg, Beck Depression Inventory scores) measured on the same scale, calculated as the post-intervention difference between intervention and control groups.

Mean differences (MD): applied to continuous outcomes (eg, Beck Depression Inventory scores) measured on the same scale, calculated as the post-intervention difference between intervention and control groups.

Standardised mean differences (SMD): used when outcomes are assessed with different scales (eg, Hamilton Depression Rating Scale vs Patient Health Questionnaire-9), with effect sizes standardised using Cohen’s d.

Standardised mean differences (SMD): used when outcomes are assessed with different scales (eg, Hamilton Depression Rating Scale vs Patient Health Questionnaire-9), with effect sizes standardised using Cohen’s d.

ORs: applied to binary outcomes (eg, remission rates), derived from reported dichotomous data.

ORs: applied to binary outcomes (eg, remission rates), derived from reported dichotomous data.

Heterogeneity will be evaluated using the I² statistic and χ2 tests, with an I² value greater than 50% considered indicative of substantial heterogeneity.38 Sources of heterogeneity, such as intervention duration and type of comparator, will be investigated through subgroup analyses (comparing short-term vs long-term interventions) and sensitivity analyses that exclude studies with a high risk of bias. Publication bias will be assessed using funnel plots and Egger’s test, provided that 10 or more studies are included.39 If a meta-analysis is not feasible due to substantial heterogeneity or limited data, a structured qualitative synthesis will be undertaken to summarise the findings by outcome domain, using tables and narrative descriptions to support the results. Statistical significance will be defined at α=0.05, and 95% CIs will be reported for all effect estimates.

Where sufficient data are available, sensitivity analyses will be conducted by excluding studies at high risk of bias to examine their impact on the overall effect estimates. Subgroup analyses will be performed based on relevant study or participant characteristics, such as type of aerobic exercise, intervention duration and baseline severity of MDD, to explore potential sources of heterogeneity.

Ethics and dissemination

Ethical approval is not required, as this review is based solely on previously published data. The findings will be submitted to a peer-reviewed journal in the fields of mental health or physiotherapy by late 2025, with the aim of informing evidence-based management of MDD. Any amendments to the protocol will be recorded with dates and justifications, and will be reported in the final publication.

Discussion

This protocol for a systematic review and meta-analysis aims to assess the effects of aerobic exercise interventions in the management of individuals diagnosed with MDD, with a specific focus on their impact on depressive symptom severity and quality of life. This review aims to synthesise the current evidence and provide a detailed insight into the potential advantages and disadvantages of using aerobic exercise as an adjunctive intervention. The expected outcomes aim to support clinicians, researchers and policymakers in evaluating the integration of aerobic exercise into the treatment of MDD, potentially providing a cost-effective, readily available and non-pharmacological means to enhance patient outcomes. Furthermore, this protocol emphasises the intended methodological rigour for the review, including clear eligibility criteria, a thorough literature search and standardised procedures for data collection and analysis, all with the goal of minimising bias and improving reproducibility. Nonetheless, expected challenges, such as inconsistencies in intervention protocols, measures of outcomes and the quality of studies, may limit the applicability of the findings. These issues will be addressed through subgroup analyses and sensitivity analyses when applicable. Ultimately, this review will add to the body of evidence regarding non-pharmacological treatments for MDD and may inform future research, clinical practices and policy formulation in the realm of mental healthcare.
