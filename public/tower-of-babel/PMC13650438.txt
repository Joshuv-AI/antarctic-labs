Comparative Analysis of Respirator Fit Factors Using Korean Test Panels and Standardized Korean Headforms
Authors: Hyekyung Seo, Hoyeong Jang, Sungho Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFit testing is essential to evaluate the effectiveness of respiratory protective equipment for ensuring occupational safety. The Republic of Korea currently lacks a standardized fit test panel that reflects domestic facial characteristics, although international guidelines emphasize its establishment. This study aimed to validate a Korean fit test panel developed according to international standards and to examine the consistency of fit test results between human participants and headforms with comparable facial dimensions.MethodsThe panel was developed using facial anthropometric data from 11,429 adults in the Size Republic of Korea database based on ISO 16976-2:2022(E). Fifty participants representing the panel categories underwent quantitative fit testing with various respirator models. Fit test pass rates of human participants and headforms were evaluated against the U.S. National Personal Protective Technology Laboratory ( 2015) criterion of 64–76% to determine the panel’s applicability.ResultsParticipants with medium-to-large faces achieved significantly higher fit factors with large respirators (p < 0.05), whereas those with small faces exhibited lower fit for small-sized models. Significant differences were observed across respirator size categories (p < 0.05), with full-face types showing higher pass rates than half-face types. Human fit tests exceeded the National Personal Protective Technology Laboratory criterion (76–100%), supporting the panel’s international applicability. In contrast, headform-based testing produced lower fit factors, particularly for small respirators. Intraclass correlation coefficient analysis revealed poor agreement (<16%) between human and headform results.ConclusionThe Korean fit test panel appears to represent population-specific facial dimensions and shows potential for respirator evaluation under the tested conditions. However, differences between participants and headform outcomes indicate the need for improved headform fidelity to enhance test reliability.Keywords: Fit test panel, Headform, ISO16976-2, Respiratory Protective Equipment (RPE), Validate

Introduction

The effectiveness of respiratory protective equipment (RPE) worn by workers is largely determined by how well the device fits the user’s face. A proper fit ensures adequate protection and is typically verified through fit testing, which serves as a critical process in both respirator selection and safe usage assessment. While many countries including the United States, Canada, and Japan have implemented fit testing as a legal requirement [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5]], the Republic of Korea currently recommends fit testing as a voluntary practice [[6], [7], [8]]. Furthermore, standardized protocols regarding fit testing equipment and procedures remain underdeveloped in the Republic of Korea, and there is a notable absence of test panels equivalent to international standards.

The earliest test panel was the bivariate panel developed by the U.S. Air Force through anthropometric surveys conducted at the Los Alamos National Laboratory [9,10]. This was followed by the principal component analysis (PCA) panel developed by Zhuang et al [11,12] and subsequent test panels designed specifically for full-face and half-face respirators [[13], [14], [15]]. Similarly, China has developed its own bivariate and PCA-based panels [[16], [17], [18]]. Recently, the Republic of Korea has developed its own anthropometric test panel using methods outlined in the ISO 16976-2:2022(E) standard [[19], [20], [21], [22]], and research is ongoing to evaluate its validity for use as a national standard [23].

Fit testing methods are generally divided into qualitative fit testing, which relies on the wearer’s sensory detection of test agents, and quantitative fit testing, which uses numerical data to assess seal integrity. Quantitative fit testing is considered more objective and is increasingly preferred in occupational settings, though it requires specialized equipment and trained personnel [24]. It is also crucial to consider respirator sizing relative to the user’s facial dimensions, particularly when evaluating mismatches between wearer anthropometry and respirator design. Therefore, anthropometric test panels must be validated for proper application and reliability.

In the Republic of Korea, early research by Han [25] emphasized the need for test panels by analyzing the relationship between fit factors and facial dimensions. However, a lack of comprehensive anthropometric data on the Korean respirator-using population delayed panel development. With the recent establishment of a Korean-specific test panel, there is now a need to validate its applicability by comparing fit test results obtained using human participants selected based on the panel with those obtained using the newly developed Korean headform. Despite their practical utility, the consistency and validity of fit factor results between human-panel-based testing and headform-based evaluation have not been thoroughly investigated. This study aims to compare pass rates and fit factor distributions obtained from both methods and to analyze their correlation. The findings are expected to provide foundational evidence for the potential standardization of the Korean test panel and headform in domestic respirator certification and evaluation processes.

Methods

Development of the Korean test panel

A Korean facial anthropometric test panel was constructed based on facial dimension data collected from the Seventh and Eighth Size Korea surveys, which included a total of 11,429 individuals aged 15 to 69 years. The panel was developed following the guidelines outlined in ISO 16976-2:2022(E) by the International Organization for Standardization, which provides a standardized framework for constructing representative test panels based on population-specific anthropometric data.

The vertical axis (Y-axis) of the test panel was defined using face length measurements. The boundary range was determined from the mean face length of Korean males (118.6 mm) plus two standard deviations (SDs), and the mean face length of Korean females (111.9 mm) minus two SDs, resulting in a vertical panel range of 97.9 mm to 134.6 mm. The horizontal axis (X-axis) was determined based on face width, using the same two-SD approach, yielding a range of 120.8 mm to 158.2 mm. To ensure that the test panel captured at least 95% of the population distribution, individuals whose facial dimensions fell outside the defined range were excluded. As a result, 10,985 individuals (96.1%) were successfully included in the Korean test panel, while 444 individuals (3.9%) were excluded due to outlier measurements (Table 1).

Participants

According to the international standard (ISO 16976-2:2022(E)), the representative facial dimensions of 10,985 Koreans are categorized into a 25-participant test panel. These 25 facial types represent the facial length and width distribution of the Korean population, covering approximately 96% of the population within 10 proportional cells. In this study, participants were recruited to match the facial dimensions of the 25-participant Korean test panel, and a total of 50 participants were enrolled to improve proportional representation across the Korean test panel cells. Although only 50 participants were included, their facial dimensions were distributed proportionally across the Korean facial panel categories, supporting representativeness for the intended fit test evaluation. All participants fell within the facial length and width ranges defined by the Korean anthropometric test panel (Table 2). Participants were recruited through advertisements within the research institution, and all individuals provided written informed consent prior to participation. Personal information was protected in accordance with ethical research standards. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the institutional review board under approval number SHIRB-202111-HR-142-01.

Headforms

The development of Korean headforms based on facial shape analysis was conducted using data from11,429 individuals obtained in the Seventh (2015) and Eighth (2021) Size Republic of Korea surveys [[26], [27], [28]]. In accordance with the international standard (ISO 16976-2:2022(E)), Korean headforms were developed by calculating eigenvalues for 45 facial measurements and identifying the principal factors through PCA, based on cumulative proportion. Two principal components (PC1 and PC2) were selected. A scatter plot of facial shapes was constructed using the derived principal component scores, and elliptical modeling was applied to represent the distribution by face size. Based on this, the size categories of the Korean headforms were determined (Appendix 1). The test headform device equipped with a Korean facial model is a patented (10-2023-0145234, 2023) respirator testing apparatus developed in the Republic of Korea. It simulates human breathing patterns according to specific movements, thereby reflecting dynamic conditions during fit factor evaluation.

Fit test

The fit test followed the National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health (NIOSH) protocol outlined in the U.S. federal regulations (Occupational Safety and Health Administration, 29 CFR 1910; 42 CFR 84.63(d)). Testing was conducted using the PortaCount Respirator Fit Tester Model 8048 (TSI Incorporated, Shoreview, MN, USA) with three size types (small, medium, and large) of elastomeric full-face and elastomeric half-face respirators (EFRs and EHRs), respectively. Only a limited number of respirator models were evaluated, and powered air-purifying respirators (PAPRs) and respirators representing a broader range of manufacturers and seal designs were not included. The test panel validation was compared with the NIOSH National Personal Protective Technology Laboratory (NPPTL) study results [[29], [30], [31]]. Previous research suggests that if the pass rate difference between human participants and headforms is approximately 20%, headform-based fit evaluation may hold practical potential [33,34]. Therefore, when the fit test pass rate exceeds 64–76%, the headform is considered suitable for use as a Korean standard [[30], [31], [32]].

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software version 20.0 (International Business Machines Corporation, IBM, New York, USA). Fit factors were expressed as geometric means (GMs) and geometric SDs (GSDs). The significance level was set at p < 0.05. Potential variables, including sex, age, and facial dimensions, which may affect the fit factor, were evaluated using subgroup analysis. To compare the fit factor values between human test panel participants and headforms, t tests were conducted. An analysis of variance followed by post hoc tests was conducted to assess differences in fit test pass rates according to face size. The agreement and reliability of fit test pass rates between the two groups were evaluated using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) and Bland–Altman plot analysis. The ICCs were used to assess the degree of correlation and consistency, while the Bland–Altman plots were utilized to visualize the agreement and identify potential systematic biases between human and headform fit test results.

Headforms and Korean facial dimensions

The headforms classified through PCA correspond to the Korean test panel category. The mean face lengths were 124.1 mm for the large size, 114.2 mm for the medium size, and 107.4 mm for the small size. The corresponding face widths were 148.8 mm, 138.1 mm, and 127.7 mm, respectively (Appendix 1). The mean face lengths of the participants were 128.4 mm for the large size, 118.1 mm for the medium size, and 106.1 mm for the small size. The corresponding face widths were 149.6 mm, 138.3 mm, and 130.5 mm, respectively (Table 1), which were comparable to those of the headform sizes. The participants and headforms were distributed within predefined test panel categories and are therefore considered representative of typical Korean facial characteristics (Fig. 1).

Fit testing results based on headform size showed significant differences according to respirator size; however, the measured fit factors were consistently lower than those obtained from human participants (Table 5). The large headform achieved the highest fit factor when tested with the medium-sized full-face respirator, while the medium headform showed the lowest fit factor when tested with the large-sized full-face respirator. The small-sized half-face respirator yielded low fit factors across all headforms. These findings indicate that fit factors measured using headforms with the EHR were substantially lower than those measured on human participants.

Verification of the Korean test panel

To validate the test panel, fit test pass rates were compared between human participants and headforms. Based on the NPPTL criteria [[30], [31], [32], [33]], the validity of the test panel was verified. The fit test pass rates among the participants exceeded the NPPTL criterion (64–76%), thereby verifying the validity of the Korean test panel. The fit test pass rates observed in human participants were higher than those obtained using headforms. In contrast, the pass rates for fit tests conducted with headforms were significantly lower than those obtained from human participants (Fig. 2). In particular, EHRs tested with small headforms showed lower pass rates across all respirator sizes. Overall, small EHRs consistently exhibited lower pass rates regardless of the headform type used for fit testing (Fig. 2). Therefore, the fit test pass rates for human participants were reliably high, whereas those for headforms were comparatively low. The Korean test panel demonstrated utility in evaluating human fit performance; however, headform-based assessments require further refinement due to their relatively low fit accuracy.

Previous studies [34] have indicated that headform-based fit assessments may have practical potential when the pass rate difference between human participants and headforms is approximately 20%. In particular, fit test results for EHRs showed differences in pass rates between human participants and headforms (Fig. 2). It was necessary to examine whether these variations were due to random error. Subgroup analysis of participants’ face size effects on EHRs’ fit performance found that fit test pass rates had wide CIs, but observed differences were neither statistically significant nor heterogeneity was evident (Fig. 3). Although the subgroup analysis did not demonstrate statistically significant heterogeneity by face-size strata, the consistently low pass rates observed for small EHR models in both human participants and headforms suggest that further evaluation of the tested small-sized respirator products is warranted. Therefore, while the test panel pass rates for human participants met the validation criteria, confirming their suitability, the headforms did not, particularly showing substantially lower pass rates in small headform and EHR tests that precluded validation.

Fit factor reliability analysis using ICC and Bland–Altman methods

The reliability of fit testing was assessed by comparing the fit factors obtained from Korean test panels and corresponding headforms. Statistical agreement was evaluated using the ICC and Bland–Altman plots. CI analysis was conducted for ICC estimation; however, the observed 95% CIs were wide, indicating instability rather than precise estimation of the observed poor agreement (Table 6). Accordingly, the poor agreement findings should be interpreted cautiously because the wide ICC CIs (EFR: -0.605 to 0.811; EHR: -0.345 to 0.428) indicate unstable agreement estimates despite the sample-size consideration for ICC estimation [35] (Table 6). Fit factor evaluations using the test panel and headforms showed approximately 15% agreement for the full-facepiece respirator, with no statistically significant differences between the two methods, indicating a low rather than moderate level of consistency (Table 6). However, this agreement was not observed for the half-facepiece respirators.

This pattern suggests that one method may have overestimated (or underestimated) the results compared to the other (Fig. 4B). Discussion

This study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of the newly developed Korean fit test panel based on ISO 16976-2:2022(E) by comparing the fit test performance of human participants and standardized Korean headforms. The results provide important insights into the applicability of the Korean panel and the current limitations of headform-based fit evaluation.

The Korean test panel, derived from anthropometric data of 11,429 individuals, showed high representativeness of the general population and demonstrated a high fit test pass rate, particularly when using large-sized full-face respirators. This study is not an exploratory research aimed at establishing new national standards from the raw data of 11,429 individuals. Instead, it is a validation study designed to verify the statistical effectiveness of a 25-participant Korean test panel that was already constructed following the ISO 16976-2:2022(E) methodology. The 25-participant panel was derived from the Size Republic of Korea database (n = 11,429) to represent more than 96% of the Korean population. Because this panel is already a statistically optimized distillation of the 11,429 data points, the large-scale population characteristics are inherently reflected within the 25-person matrix. To evaluate the validity of this 25-person panel with higher reliability than the minimum ISO requirements, we recruited 50 participants—double the size of the standard panel. This approach ensured that each cell (size category) within the facial dimension matrix was tested with multiple participants, providing broader coverage to evaluate whether the panel accurately represents the target population’s distribution Therefore, we believe that 50 targeted participants are both appropriate and robust for the specific purpose of validating an ISO-compliant test panel. However, inferential comparisons across respirator sizes and models remain limited because no formal a priori power analysis was performed for these comparisons.

Potential confounding variables such as gender, age, and face size, known to influence respirator fit performance, were analyzed for the EFR and EHR. Table 3, Table 4 revealed no statistically significant differences or heterogeneity. Subgroup analysis showed that the GMR for EFR exhibited no heterogeneity (I2 = 0%), indicating no significant variation across face sizes. I2 = 0% signifies that observed variations in fit test results were attributable to random error rather than systematic differences between subgroups. Although the GMR for medium versus small faces was 1.39 (indicating higher fit factors for medium faces), this difference was not due to true heterogeneity but rather chance, as confirmed by I2 = 0%. I2 values ≥ 75% typically indicate substantial heterogeneity. GMR represents the ratio of GMs between subgroups. GMR = 1 indicates no difference; GMR >1 suggests the numerator subgroup has higher mean fit factors; and GMR <1 indicates lower values. Table 4 demonstrates that small faces consistently yielded lower fit factors than large or medium faces across both EFR and EHR, with medium faces performing better than large faces. Subgroup analysis revealed no statistically significant GMR differences, as confirmed by CIs encompassing unity, indicating observed variations were likely due to chance rather than systematic face size effects.

In the subgroup analysis by gender, EFR (GMR: 1.06, 95% CI: 0.94–1.19) and EHR (GMR: 1.02, 95% CI: 0.78–1.35) both included one within their CIs and were not statistically significant, indicating that a consistent gender effect on fit factor was not observed across subgroups. These findings suggest that appropriately matching facial dimensions with respirator size can directly enhance EFR fit performance. Age did not significantly influence respirator fit performance in this study, whereas the overall gender difference observed for EHR was not consistently supported in subgroup analysis. For gender, all GMRs around 1.0–1.06 with 95% CI crossing one (EFR: 0.94-1.19, p = 0.948), confirming no meaningful difference. Similarly, age group comparisons show GMRs of 1.08–1.09 with 95% CI including one (p > 0.6), suggesting these factors do not modify fit efficacy.

This aligns with previous studies [31,33], which emphasized the importance of size-matched respirator selection to maximize protection. Participants with medium-to-large facial sizes achieved significantly higher fit factors, while small-faced individuals consistently failed to achieve an adequate seal with small respirators. Because the Korean test panel was developed from a nationally representative anthropometric database and the present participants were proportionally recruited across the predefined panel cells, these findings are not sufficient to indicate a deficiency of the Korean test panel itself. Rather, the consistently low performance of the small-sized respirators observed in both human participants and headforms suggests that the tested small-sized respirator models currently available in the market may not be sufficiently optimized for Korean small-faced users. User-related donning factors may have contributed to some variability in human testing, but they are unlikely to fully explain the parallel low performance observed across both human and headform evaluations.

The best performance occurs with size-matched pairs (medium face/medium respirator; small face/small) significantly [30,31]. Significant advantages were observed with matched/oversized respirators but extreme mismatches (small face/large respirators) show variability. This indicates superior EFR fit primarily benefits medium faces over small ones, with no consistent superiority across all large-face comparisons (Table 4). Prioritize medium/large faces with corresponding respirators for optimal protection in Korean cohorts. Size-matched respirators yield highest fit factors for both EFR and EHR, with participants achieving better performance than headforms. Participants exhibited substantially higher fit factors than headforms across all face size and respirator combinations (p < 0.005), with up to 10-fold differences (large face/large EFR: 14,961.7 vs. 12,991.5) (Table 5).

According to the radar charts in Fig. 2, headforms exhibited markedly lower fit test pass rates than human participants during EHR testing (Fig. 2C and D). This difference arises from the human participant’s facial flexibility and skin adaptability, confirming that headforms underestimate real-world protection performance. In this study, headforms were limited in reproducing human motion or accurately reflecting breathing volume errors. This disparity confirms that headform testing underestimates real-world seal integrity, highlighting the necessity of human participant validation for accurate respirator fit assessment in anthropometrically diverse populations). Headforms exhibit much lower GMs overall, underscoring participant testing relevance. This demonstrates superior real-world fit performance compared to standardized headforms.

Headform-based fit test results showed significantly lower pass rates and fit factors than those obtained from human participants, particularly when using half-face respirators. The low ICC values and Bland–Altman analysis confirmed that the agreement between headform and human results was poor for both respirator types, although full-face respirators showed relatively smaller bias than half-face types (ICC = 0.03, Cronbach α = 0.08). The extremely low Cronbach α value indicates poor internal consistency among repeated headform measurements, statistically confirming that headform fit stability was substantially inferior to human participants due to inadequate replication of facial adaptability and seal maintenance. This may be attributed to the static nature of headforms, which does not account for facial muscle movement, skin deformation, or proper donning behavior—all critical elements that influence real-world respirator performance. Future headform development should incorporate soft-tissue simulation using silicone/rubber skin layers with variable elasticity (durometer: 10-30 Shore A), dynamic actuation with motorized jaw/cheek movement (±5mm range) to mimic donning/speaking micro-movements, and multilayer construction featuring a rigid core with deformable surface (2–3mm thickness) matching human skin compliance. These technical enhancements would address the current limitations revealed by low ICC and Cronbach α values, substantially improving measurement repeatability and standardization of Korean test panels for half-face respirator assessment. In other words, the PCA-based headform developed using Korean anthropometric data only limitedly simulated the dynamic properties of biological tissues, failing to sufficiently replicate actual human facial movements [19,36]. Consequently, the correlation between fit test results from human participants and headform testing was less than 16%, indicating low agreement.

To achieve full standardization in the Korean context, enhancements such as the use of soft skin materials or robotic actuation should be incorporated into headform design to improve its ability to simulate human fit behaviors more accurately. According to Bergman et al [33], fit tests using headforms made with human-like skin materials showed partial correlation with human participant results. Moreover, the Correlation of Respirator Fit Measured on Human Subjects and a Static Advanced Headform (2015) study reported that although fit factors measured on headforms were significantly lower overall, two test exercises, normal breathing and deep breathing, showed strong predictive agreement with human fit results (R2 = 0.95). These findings highlight the need to improve the Korean headform validated in this study to better reflect such performance aspects.

Nevertheless, the findings of this study support the necessity of a standardized respirator fit testing framework in the Republic of Korea. Based on the study by Zhuang et al [31], who suggested that a test panel pass rate between 65% and 74% is adequate for panel variability, the Korean test panel exhibiting pass rates exceeding 90% suggests potential applicability for domestic use under the tested conditions. However, to be integrated into regulatory evaluation processes, further phased validation across additional respirator types, manufacturers, and wearer populations is required. Other nations, such as China [14,16,37] and Malaysia [38], have developed bivariate panels based on their populations’ facial lengths and widths. Moreover, the United States and Japan have adopted robotic headform systems (NBC Headform System; i-bodi Technology Ltd., Buckingham, United Kingdom) and JIS T8157 [39], respectively, for certifying various types of respirators in accordance with ISO/TS 16976-2 [19]. These international trends suggest that the Republic of Korea should likewise pursue the development and standardization of its own fit testing tools.

In summary, the Korean test panel based on anthropometric data demonstrated promising applicability and reliability for human-based RPE evaluation under the tested conditions and appears to represent the facial characteristics of the Korean population. The participant-based Korean test panel may serve as an objective framework for fit testing. Currently, the Republic of Korea relies on imported headforms from Europe and other countries for certification and performance testing, which do not accurately represent Korean facial features. Although the Korean headform reflects Korean facial dimensions, it is not yet adequate for use in performance or certification testing of RPE. Therefore, headform-based fit testing requires further refinement, and improvements in the design and material of the Korean headform are essential to enhance its representativeness and validity.

Although 25 participants representing the Korean test panel were recruited, males were predominantly represented in the large-face category, whereas most participants in the small-face category were female. Although subgroup analysis attributed the observed sex differences to chance, we cannot conclusively state that sex had no influence on pass rates in the small-face category. In addition, because no participants had facial hair or long hair that interfered with testing, the potential effects of these characteristics could not be evaluated; physiological factors such as body mass index were also not considered. Subsequent research should ensure a balanced sex distribution across face-size categories and evaluate these potential factors.

Based on the present findings, it is essential to explore headform improvement strategies and continuously assess the feasibility of broader standardization in the Republic of Korea. Aligning with international efforts, the use of nationally representative test panels and headforms must be strengthened through continued research aimed at improving the biomechanical fidelity of headforms to better match human test results and diversifying testing conditions [40]. This study represents the first attempt to conduct fit tests on both human participants and headforms using a Korean test panel developed in accordance with international standards. Furthermore, this study is meaningful in that it provides preliminary evidence relevant to the standardization procedure. Future studies are needed to expand this work by evaluating various types of respiratory protective devices, including powered air-purifying respirators, and respirators representing a broader range of manufacturers and seal designs. These efforts will support the continued development and validation of a science-based fit testing approach in the Republic of Korea.

Conclusion

Currently, the Republic of Korea relies on imported headforms from Europe and other countries for certification and performance testing, which do not accurately represent Korean facial features. The Korean test panel, developed using national anthropometric data, appears to represent the facial characteristics of the Korean population and showed applicability in fit testing under the conditions evaluated in this study. However, while the Korean headform reflects Korean facial dimensions, it is not yet adequate for use in performance or certification testing of RPE.

Although 25 participants representative of the Korean test panel were recruited, males predominantly exhibited large faces while most small faces were females. While subgroup analysis attributed gender differences to chance, we cannot conclusively state that gender exerted no influence on small-face pass rates. Based on the present findings, it is essential to explore headform improvement strategies and continuously assess the feasibility of broader standardization in the Republic of Korea. Subsequent research should ensure gender balance across face size categories. Additionally, although no participants had facial hair or long hair that interfered with testing, the lack of consideration for physiological factors such as body mass index represents a limitation requiring attention in future research. Therefore, the Korean headform must be further improved through future studies focused on enhancing its biomechanical fidelity to better match human test results. This study represents the first attempt to conduct fit tests on both human participants and headforms using a Korean test panel developed in accordance with international standards. Furthermore, this study is meaningful in that it provides preliminary evidence relevant to the standardization procedure. Future studies are needed to expand this work by evaluating various types of respiratory protective devices including powered air-purifying respirators and respirators representing a broader range of manufacturers and seal designs.
