Recent advances in the bioanalysis of acylcarnitines: Methodologies, challenges, and clinical perspectives
Authors: Jiaxin Zheng, Wanting Chen, Fengxia Qin, Pei Zhang, Fengguo Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcylcarnitines (ACs) are a diverse class of fatty acid esters of L-carnitine that serve as critical mediators in energy homeostasis and mitochondrial function. Beyond their classical role in newborn screening for inborn errors of metabolism, ACs have emerged as promising biomarkers for complex pathologies, including cardiovascular diseases, diabetes, and drug-induced toxicities. However, the accurate quantification and comprehensive profiling of ACs in biological matrices remain analytically challenging due to their broad polarity range, vast concentration disparities, and the presence of isomers. This review provides a comprehensive overview of the current bioanalytical strategies for ACs, covering sample preparation techniques and detection platforms, with a focus on liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry. Special attention is given to the differentiation of isomers. Finally, we discuss the clinical applications of ACs profiling and highlight future perspectives, including the integration of ion mobility spectrometry, automated high-throughput workflows, spatial and single-cell metabolomics, and AI-driven analytics, to pave the way for precision medicine.Keywords: Acylcarnitines, Sample pretreatment, Mass spectrometry, Clinical diagnosis, Metabolic disease

Introduction

Acylcarnitines (ACs) are a class of metabolites formed by the esterification of L-carnitine with fatty acids (FAs) [1]. According to the number of carbon atoms in the acyl group, ACs are classified into short-chain acylcarnitines (<6 carbons, SCACs), medium-chain acylcarnitines (6–12 carbons, MCACs), long-chain acylcarnitines (13–20 carbons, LCACs), and very-long-chain acylcarnitines (>20 carbons, VLCACs). In addition, they can also be classified by FAs saturation into saturated ACs (n = 0), monounsaturated ACs (n = 1), and polyunsaturated ACs (n > 1) [2]. ACs are essential metabolic intermediates that facilitate mitochondrial and peroxisomal fatty acid oxidation (FAO), serving as a principal cellular energy source [3]. Because the inner mitochondrial membrane is impermeable to long-chain acyl-CoAs, ACs serve as obligate transport intermediates via the carnitine shuttle [4]. Concurrently, peroxisomes perform preliminary oxidation of complex lipids (e.g., very-long-chain and branched-chain FAs), converting them into SCACs and MCACs for subsequent complete mitochondrial oxidation [5]. During metabolic stress accompanied by abnormal acyl-CoA accumulation, carnitine buffers this toxicity by forming ACs to liberate free CoA [6].

AC metabolism has important clinical significance. Profiling of ACs has become an important approach for diagnosing inborn errors of metabolism, such as FAO disorders [7] and organic acidemias (OA) [8], and is widely used in newborn screening (NBS) and prenatal diagnosis [9]. In acquired or complex chronic diseases, including obesity, type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) [10], cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) [2], Alzheimer's disease (AD) [11], Parkinson's disease [12], inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) [13], and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) [14], abnormal AC profiles not only help to deepen our understanding of the metabolic and molecular mechanisms underlying disease onset and progression, but also provide a metabolomic basis for early diagnosis and precision treatment. Despite their high clinical value, comprehensive profiling of ACs remains challenging due to several factors: the complexity of biological sample matrices [15], the broad polarity range of ACs, the difficulty in separating their isomers and the lack of commercial reference standards. These limitations hinder the qualitative identification, accurate quantification, and further clinical translation of ACs [16]. Nevertheless, comprehensive reviews on AC bioanalysis remain notably lacking.

In this review, we systematically summarize recent advancements in AC bioanalysis, including sample preparation strategies and advanced detection platforms, with a primary focus on liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC–MS). Particular attention is given to isomer discrimination and the standardization of quantitative methods. Finally, we also discuss the clinical application prospects of ACs analysis, including the integration of ion mobility spectrometry (IMS) and automated high-throughput workflows to advance precision medicine.

Blood samples. They are mainly divided into plasma/serum samples and dried blood spot (DBS) samples. The levels of ACs in plasma and serum can reflect systemic AC metabolism, highlighting their importance in clinical testing. DBS have become one of the standard sample types for NBS of inherited metabolic disorders (IMDs) because of their advantages, including ease of sample collection, minimal blood volume requirement, good stability, and convenience in storage and transportation. Currently, tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) based DBS analysis has been widely applied for the early screening of disorders such as FAO defects, OA, and amino acid metabolism abnormalities [17].

Tissue samples. ACs in tissue samples directly indicate local FAO and mitochondrial function. Although invasive biopsies limit their clinical application primarily to research, tissue analysis offers critical mechanistic insights. For instance, comprehensive AC analysis in HCC tissues has revealed carnitine palmitoyltransferase 2 (CPT2) downregulation as a pivotal driver of carnitine shuttle dysregulation, thereby unveiling novel therapeutic targets [18].

Urine samples. Because urine serves as the primary excretion route for highly polar SCACs and free carnitine. For instance, in OA like glutaric acidemia type 1 (GA-1), markedly elevated urinary glutarylcarnitine acts as a definitive diagnostic biomarker [19]. Furthermore, urinary profiling is critical for diagnosing primary carnitine deficiency (PCD). Driven by defects in the Organic Cation Carnitine Transporter 2 transporter that impair renal reabsorption, PCD exhibits a paradoxical metabolic signature: severe systemic carnitine depletion concurrent with elevated urinary carnitine and ACs [20]. This distinctive urinary AC profile enables reliable differentiation of PCD from secondary carnitine deficiency, in which systemic carnitine depletion results from drug-carnitine conjugation followed by renal elimination. Such discrimination facilitates accurate diagnosis and guides targeted therapeutic management [21].

Fibroblasts. For example, both carnitine CPT2 deficiency and very long-chain Acyl-CoA dehydrogenase deficiency can show elevated LCACs in blood, and AC analysis in fibroblasts may be required for differential diagnosis [22]. When results are borderline, fibroblast-based testing provides a more reliable basis for diagnosis [23].

Other samples. ACs in bile or bile spots are utilized in postmortem investigations of sudden unexplained death due to their superior stability against putrefaction compared to blood [24]. Measurement of ACs in amniotic fluid is an important component of prenatal diagnosis. Fetal metabolites are excreted into amniotic fluid, and analysis of ACs can identify FAO disorders and OA before birth, guiding early interventions and monitoring maternal risks [25].

Sample preparation methods

In AC bioanalysis, selecting an appropriate sample preparation strategy is critical to mitigate matrix effects, enrich trace analytes, and ensure analytical sensitivity. The extraction strategy depends on sample characteristics and analytical objectives. For biological samples, pretreatment is required because of matrix effects. First, proteins, lipids, inorganic salts, and other matrix components should be removed to reduce analytical interference and protect sensitive instruments [26]. Second, target analytes can be enriched to increase their concentrations and improve method sensitivity. Finally, pretreatment can inactivate metabolic enzymes and stabilize samples. The main strategies for AC extraction from biological samples include direct dilution, liquid–liquid extraction, protein precipitation, solid-phase extraction and chemical derivatization (Fig. 1).

Direct dilution

Dilution is the simplest sample pretreatment technique. In this approach, the sample is diluted with an instrument-compatible solvent and then directly injected, making the procedure straightforward and convenient [27]. It has been reported that urine samples diluted 5-20 fold with ultrapure water or mobile phase, followed by centrifugation and direct injection of the supernatant, can be used to detect urinary ACs [28]. While rapid, the lack of analyte enrichment inherently limits its sensitivity for low-abundance ACs. In addition, residual proteins and salts in the sample may also interfere with the analytical results.

Liquid–liquid extraction

Liquid-liquid extraction (LLE) separates analytes based on their different solubilities in two immiscible or slightly miscible liquids, transferring the target compounds from one phase to the other. Common extraction solvents include diethyl ether, chloroform, n-butanol, ethyl acetate, and n-hexane [29]. In extracting ACs from biological samples, LLE can retain highly polar components such as inorganic salts, urea, and sugars in the aqueous phase while transferring lipophilic LCACs into the organic phase, thereby reducing ion suppression in MS and improving the signal-to-noise ratio [30]. LCACs are relatively nonpolar and present at low concentrations; after extraction into an organic solvent, evaporating the solvent under nitrogen enriches the analytes, facilitates subsequent detection, and supports downstream derivatization. Organic solvents can also denature proteins, enabling their removal and better protecting chromatographic columns. Wancewicz et al. employed biphasic extraction methods, such as the Bligh and Dyer and Matyash protocols, as well as three-phase liquid extraction, to achieve the most comprehensive possible extraction of metabolites with different polarities from cardiac tissue [31]. This strategy can effectively address the distribution behavior of ACs, in which SCACs preferentially partition into the polar phase, whereas LCACs more readily distribute into the nonpolar phase, thereby enabling broader metabolome coverage. Additionally, it was reported that supplementing the extraction solvent with trace trifluoroacetic acid enhances both extraction efficiency and AC protonation, leading to significantly amplified MS signals [32].

However, LLE has several limitations. When processing plasma, serum, or tissue homogenates, proteins and lipids can form emulsions after vigorous mixing, this often requires prolonged high-speed centrifugation to break, markedly increasing pretreatment time. In addition, LLE consumes large amounts of organic solvents, many of which are toxic. Finally, LLE favors hydrophobic molecules such as LCACs and provides poorer recovery for more polar analytes [33].

Protein precipitation

Protein precipitation (PPT) is the simplest and fastest pretreatment method for biological samples, and it is the most widely used in bioanalysis [34]. PPT removes proteins from plasma, serum, or tissue homogenates by inducing protein denaturation and precipitation through physical or chemical means. The most commonly used PPT solvents are organic solvents such as acetonitrile or methanol, which disrupt the hydration layer on protein surfaces to induce precipitation. Strong acids such as trichloroacetic acid or perchloric acid are also used; by altering pH and protein charge, they rapidly drive protein aggregation [35].

PPT is fast and low-cost. However, it cannot effectively remove lipids or salts, which may cause substantial matrix effects during MS analysis, particularly ion suppression in ESI, thereby reducing ionization efficiency, sensitivity, and quantitative accuracy. Therefore, it is suitable for preliminary screening and high-throughput testing. Methanol is commonly used in NBS, where it directly extracts DBS after being spiked with internal standards. It solubilizes carnitine while leaving hemoglobin bound to the filter paper [36].

Solid-phase extraction

Solid-phase extraction (SPE) is a sample extraction and separation technique that achieves analyte isolation, enrichment, and purification based on differences in analyte distribution between a solid phase and a liquid phase. SPE can be categorized into reversed-phase SPE, normal-phase SPE, and ion-exchange SPE [37]. SPE offers several advantages: it removes proteins and salts that interfere with MS detection, improves signal quality and reduces background, enables sample concentration for downstream analysis, and is suitable for batch processing of multiple samples [38]. Nicholas B. Vera et al. extracted ACs using SPE with methanol pretreatment, followed by sample loading, methanol washing, and elution with a methanol/water/acetic acid solution before LC–MS analysis [39]. Moreover, the use of ion-exchange SPE to separate carnitine and ACs, followed by derivatization of ACs prior to instrumental analysis, is also a commonly employed sample pretreatment method. Because both carnitine and ACs contain a positively charged quaternary ammonium group, ion-exchange SPE with negatively charged cation-exchange sorbents can extract all positively charged ACs. Compared with conventional SPE, ion-exchange SPE can recover ACs across all chain lengths and is less affected by AC polarity [40]. SPE can enrich analytes and remove lipids and salts, whereas it is slower and requires more expensive cartridges, making it better suited for precise or trace-level analysis. In MS-based analysis, SPE is often preferred to better protect instruments and obtain more accurate quantitative results.

Chemical derivatization

ACs are amphiphilic molecules containing a quaternary ammonium group and possess a broad range of polarities. Highly polar SCACs and MCACs often exhibit poor retention on conventional reversed-phase chromatographic columns. To enhance mass spectrometric detection sensitivity and improve chromatographic separation among homologous compounds, derivatization has become a critical step in sample pretreatment. Early strategies primarily relied on butanol esterification (e.g., reacting at 60°C for 20 min); however, such elevated temperatures can induce AC degradation, thereby compromising analytical accuracy [41]. Alternatively, utilizing acetyl chloride and 1-butanol to generate butyl esters effectively increases molecular hydrophobicity, improves sensitivity, and enhances peak shape [42]. However, derivatization efficiency may vary across ACs with different chain lengths and functional groups, potentially introducing quantitative bias.

Recently, novel derivatization reagents have significantly advanced AC analysis, and the derivatization principles are shown in Fig. 2. Notable examples include pentafluorophenzoyl trifluoromethanesulfonate for the simultaneous quantification of 66 ACs [43], 4-bromomethyl biphenyl for achieving sub-femtomolar limits of detection (e.g., 4.5 fM) [44], and 3-nitrophenylhydrazine for generating isotope-labeled derivatives to minimize matrix effects in complex systems [45]. In our previous work, we established a derivatization method for ACs using 5-dimethylamino-naphthalene-1-sulfonyl piperazine (Dns-PP). First, 2-(7-Azabenzotriazol-1-yl)-N, N, N′, N′-tetramethyluronium hexafluorophosphate was added and the reaction to convert carboxylic acids into activated intermediates that are more susceptible to nucleophilic attack. Subsequently, Dns-PP was added to undergo substitution and coupling with the activated carboxyl group, generating stable amide- or ester-type derivatives. This strategy increased the hydrophobicity of AC molecules, enhanced the retention of SCACs and highly polar ACs on octadecyl-bonded silica (C18) columns, and improved peak shape. In addition, introducing the dansyl moiety enhanced analyte signals in positive ESI mode, thereby improving separation and detection sensitivity while reducing matrix interference [46]. Furthermore, our group performed structural modification of the compound, and developed an LC–MS/MS method based on N2, N2, N4, N4-Tetramethyl-6-(4-(piperazin-1-ylsulfonyl) phenyl)-1,3,5-triazine-2,4-diamine (Tmt-PP) derivatization quantitative analysis of multi-components by a single marker strategy for AC quantification. Despite these analytical benefits, chemical derivatization presents inherent limitations. Derivatization procedures typically involve multiple steps, which increase sample preparation time and potential sources of human error, making them less suitable for high-throughput analysis. Additionally, incomplete or excessive reactions may generate impurity peaks and introduce quantification errors. Ultimately, the persistent reliance on scarce and expensive commercial standards continues to constrain broader clinical applications and methodological development [48].

To overcome these deficiencies, chemical derivatization is typically integrated with other pretreatment techniques like PPT, LLE, or SPE, rather than being used as a standalone procedure. The combination of PPT and derivatization is the most widely adopted strategy for high-throughput analysis. Initial protein removal using solvents like acetonitrile ensures a cleaner matrix, while subsequent derivatization enhances the chromatographic retention and MS response of polar ACs, achieving limits of detection (LOD) as low as 0.01–0.27 ng/mL [49,50]. For more complex matrices or structural characterization, LLE or SPE are often employed prior to derivatization to provide superior enrichment and purification. For instance, LLE (e.g., the Folch method) has been coupled with Paternò–Büchi (PB) photochemical derivatization to enable precise structural identification of AC isomers in plasma [51]. Similarly, SPE-derivatization workflows, particularly those using mixed-mode cation-exchange cartridges, offer even higher selectivity. This integrated approach has successfully enabled the accurate quantification of over 60 AC species in urine and serum by significantly reducing ion suppression from salts and phospholipids [52,53]. Ultimately, these hybrid strategies balance sample cleanup, analyte enrichment, and signal enhancement, making them indispensable for both targeted clinical diagnostics and comprehensive untargeted metabolomics (Table 2).

Automation and high-throughput processing

Given the large-scale screening demands in clinical settings, automated sample preparation is essential. Traditional pretreatment methods, including PPT, LLE, and SPE have limitations in automation and high-throughput applications. At present, one of the most mature approaches is PPT in 96/384-well plates [70]. This method is fast, low-cost, and suitable for large-scale screening. In NBS, automated DBS punching combined with in-plate extraction is commonly used. To achieve both high throughput and superior sample cleanup, online SPE–LC–MS/MS systems are increasingly adopted, allowing direct sample loading and rapid continuous analysis [71]. Recently, electroextraction has emerged as a novel, solvent-minimized alternative. By utilizing an electric field to drive the directional migration of charged molecules across a membrane, this technique achieves simultaneous cleanup and enrichment. Due to the positive charge of ACs, electroextraction shows great promise for automated, high-recovery profiling of ACs from urine [72]. However, the technique remains largely research-based and requires further validation before widespread routine application.

Analytical methodologies

ACs characterized by a wide polarity range and the presence of numerous positional and structural isomers, which poses significant chromatographic separation challenging. Modern clinical screening and metabolomic studies demand both high sensitivity and high throughput for AC measurement. This section systematically reviews analytical methods for AC detection, with a focus on LC–MS-based techniques, and summarizes several emerging methods with promising applications, with the aim of providing useful insights for future AC analysis.

Separation techniques

Liquid chromatography

Liquid chromatography (LC) is one of the most commonly used separation techniques for ACs. Common stationary phases for LC analysis include bare silica, amino, diol, and amide columns, and the mobile phase contains a high proportion of organic solvent with a small amount of aqueous buffer, typically 3%–40% water. A water-enriched layer forms on the column surface, and separation occurs through differences in partitioning between the organic mobile phase and the water layer, combined with electrostatic and hydrogen-bonding interactions [73]. Hydrophilic interaction chromatography (HILIC) uses a polar stationary phase and a mobile phase containing a high proportion of organic solvent. HILIC is mainly used to separate highly polar, hydrophilic compounds such as amino acids, organic acids, nucleosides, and SCACs [74]. Reversed-phase liquid chromatography (RPLC) uses a nonpolar stationary phase and a polar mobile phase. The most common stationary phase is C18 [75]. While RPLC is inherently well-suited for retaining hydrophobic MCACs and LCACs [76]. Meikopoulos et al. developed an optimized LC–MS/MS strategy to broaden its coverage. By employing a high-acetonitrile mobile phase combined with an acidic, volatile ammonium salt buffer, this gradient method achieves the early elution of polar SCACs, followed by the sequential elution of LCACs within a reasonable run time. Coupled with multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) in positive ESI, this approach allows the simultaneous detection of 13 distinct ACs, spanning all chain lengths (SCACs, MCACs, and LCACs) [77]. Moreover, utilizing an amide-based HILIC stationary phase allows direct interaction with the positively charged quaternary ammonium group of ACs, ensuring their robust retention. This method typically employs a mobile phase consisting of 5 mM ammonium acetate in a water-acetonitrile system at pH 3 [78]. By employing gradient elution, this approach achieves the rapid and simultaneous separation of 22 ACs within 20 min. However, this non-derivatization approach often suffers from suboptimal ionization efficiency for certain compounds and makes it difficult to separate isomers. To address these issues, Chen et al. employed dansylhydrazine derivatization to label the carboxyl groups of ACs, combined with LC–MS/MS for analyte detection. This strategy improved ionization efficiency, enhanced MS sensitivity, and enabled the chromatographic separation of AC isomers [79]. The integration of PB derivatization with MS/MS in MRM mode enables the precise identification of branched-chain isomers and carbon–carbon double-bond positional isomers of ACs [51].

Gas chromatography

As gas chromatography (GC) is primarily limited to volatile and thermally stable analytes, the analysis of ACs, contained groups (hydroxyl, carboxyl, ester, and amide functional groups) increasing molecular polarity, is inherently challenging [80]. To enable GC elution, they must be converted into neutral, volatile, and thermally more stable derivatives. One established strategy involves converting the quaternary ammonium group into a neutral form, followed by derivatization with N, N-diisopropylethylamine to promote a cyclization reaction that yields acyloxy lactones. This modification improves volatility, reduces peak tailing, and enhances analytical sensitivity [81]. Dennis Seal's team established a GC–MS method for AC detection, in which ACs are derivatized to acyloxy lactones and subsequently analyzed by GC–MS to achieve qualitative and quantitative determination [82]. However, the complex workflow of GC analysis for ACs, derivatization and de-quaternization, significantly hinders the routine clinical adoption of GC-based AC profiling [83].

Capillary electrophoresis

Capillary electrophoresis (CE) separates analytes based on their differential electrophoretic mobilities, determined by charge, size, and molecular shape under an applied electric field [84]. In the analysis of ACs, CE offers superior resolution, accuracy and reliability for isomeric AC species. By applying a 25 kV electric field, distinct electrophoretic zones are created within the capillary achieving effective separation. Utilizing an uncoated fused-silica capillary under strongly acidic conditions effectively suppresses the electroosmotic flow. As a result, the migration order and resolution of ACs depended primarily on the inherent mobilities of the cations, thereby improving the discrimination of isomeric ACs [85]. Furthermore, CE–time of flight mass spectrometer (TOF)–MS fingerprint analysis has been successfully employed to address the insufficient retention of highly polar SCACs encountered with conventional liquid chromatography and improved the separation efficiency of structurally similar metabolites, providing enhanced accuracy of AC detection in complex biological samples [86]. For clinical applications, CE–MS is currently more suitable for enhancing specificity or confirmation of certain analytes in NBS; its widespread use in routine first-tier screening still requires further standardization and clinical validation.

Ion mobility spectrometry

Ion mobility spectrometry (IMS) separates gas-phase ions based on their differential velocities as they navigate an electric field against a buffer gas such as nitrogen or helium. This mobility is governed by the ion's size, shape, and charge state, resulting from frictional collisions with gas molecules. Unlike conventional chromatographic methods, IMS enables structural characterization by measuring characteristic the collision cross section (CCS) [87]. Major IMS configurations include drift tube, traveling wave, trapped, and field asymmetric IMS (FAIMS, also known as differential mobility spectrometry, DMS) [88]. IMS is often coupled with MS, improving selectivity without substantially increasing total analysis time.

The prevalence of AC isomers with identical and similar MS/MS spectra necessitates advanced gas-phase resolution. Integrating DMS with flow injection analysis (FIA) facilitates rapid ion filtering prior to MS detection. This technique enhances the selectivity of AC analysis in complex matrices. Compared with HPLC, it provides better retention and separation of SCACs, together with higher separation efficiency, minimal matrix interference, and reduced sample consumption. Therefore, it represents a valuable complementary technique for the comprehensive analysis of ACs [89]. Lapthorn et al. developed a method in which FIA continuously introduces the sample into the ion source, followed by DMS separation under an asymmetric high electric field. Due to differences in ion mobility between high- and low-field conditions, combined with variations in AC chain length, degree of unsaturation, and functional composition, distinct net displacements of ions lead to effective separation of AC isomers [90].

Overall, IMS/DMS generally exhibits the highest analytical throughput owing to its rapid gas-phase separation capability and short analysis time [39,89], whereas LC-MS achieves a good balance among sensitivity, selectivity, and applicability [91,92]. In contrast, before the widespread adoption of LC-MS/MS, GC-MS was the principal analytical approach [81,93]. CE-MS offers advantages such as high separation efficiency and low sample consumption [94,95].

Detection platforms

Mass spectrometry

Mass spectrometry (MS) strategies for AC profiling are broadly categorized into targeted and untargeted approaches. Targeted MS, typically employing MRM on triple quadrupole instruments, preselects and monitors specific ion transitions. While offering superior sensitivity, reproducibility, and absolute quantitation via isotope-labeled internal standards, its coverage is inherently limited to predefined metabolites [96]. Conversely, untargeted high-resolution MS (HRMS), such as TOF, Orbitrap, and Fourier transform ion cyclotron resonance acquires comprehensive full-scan data, excelling in novel biomarker discovery despite requiring complex data processing and targeted validation [97].

Clinically, targeted MS serves as the gold standard for first-tier NBS, diagnosing IMDs, and evaluating mitochondrial function and FAO. In clinical applications, especially NBS, FIA coupled with electrospray ionization–tandem mass spectrometry (ESI–MS/MS) is often used for targeted AC analysis [98]. Although FIA provides rapid turnaround times, its lack of chromatographic separation exacerbates matrix effects, ion suppression, and isomeric interferences. In such cases, targeted LC–MS/MS is typically employed as a second-tier confirmation to ensure diagnostic accuracy [99]. Driving novel biomarker discovery and mechanistic exploration, LC–MS/MS-based untargeted metabolomics utilizing TOF full-scan acquisition has been employed to profile serum and plasma from offspring of T2DM patients. Following rigorous structural annotation, specific MCACs were identified as significantly dysregulated in lipid-induced glucose intolerance, highlighting their utility as predictive biomarkers [100].

Therefore, targeted and untargeted MS play highly complementary roles in comprehensive AC profiling. While targeted platforms fulfill the clinical demands for high-throughput absolute quantification, untargeted LC–HRMS expands analytical boundaries by utilizing data-dependent acquisition or data-independent acquisition modes. These advanced acquisition strategies maximize broad signal capture for mechanistic studies, though their semi-quantitative nature inherently necessitates downstream targeted MRM for absolute structural and quantitative validation.

Other techniques

Although MS remains the gold standard for comprehensive AC profiling, non-MS approaches offer viable alternatives when cost, portability, or simplicity are prioritized. One of common non-MS detection methods is ultraviolet–visible (UV–Vis) detection. Because AC molecules exhibit almost no significant UV absorption, optical detection necessitates chemical derivatization or indirect monitoring. For UV–vis analysis, pre-column derivatization with reagents such as pentafluorophenacyl trifluoromethanesulfonate enables UV detection at 240 nm and 260 nm [53]. Alternatively, indirect UV detection leverages a strongly UV-absorbing electrophoretic buffer, where migrating AC ions transiently decrease the detector signal to enable quantification [101]. However, UV-based detection only provides absorbance intensity information and cannot yield molecular-weight or fragmentation data, which limits its confirmatory capability in metabolomic studies.

Fluorescence-based detection methods offer a highly sensitive alternative for AC analysis. Pre-column derivatization with fluorophores such as 2-(2,3-naphthylamino) ethyl trifluoromethanesulfonate as a fluorescent derivatization reagent, which increase the detection sensitivity of ACs by approximately 500-fold. This approach enables quantification of LCACs down to 0.3 nmol/mL using inexpensive reagents and relies on widely available instrumentation [102]. Similarly, labeling with 1-aminoanthracene and 1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl) carbodiimide hydrochloride yields highly fluorescent derivatives that achieve significantly greater sensitivity than UV detection [103]. However, despite these analytical advantages, fluorescence detection reflects only emission intensity. It fundamentally lacks the molecular weight, structural, and fragment information required to distinguish among AC isomers.

The radioenzymatic assay was a classical approach for measuring carnitine and ACs prior to the widespread adoption of MS. This technique typically employs specific enzymes, such as carnitine acetyltransferase or CPT, to catalyze reactions between the carnitine or ACs in a sample and a radiolabeled substrate. By quantifying the radioactivity of the reaction product, the concentrations of ACs or free carnitine in the sample can be determined [104]. While this method benefits from simple instrumentation, high accuracy, and minimal background interference due to stringent enzyme specificity, its modern utility is severely restricted. The reliance on radioactive isotopes mandates strict safety and regulatory requirements. Moreover, this approach cannot distinguish ACs of different chain lengths or structural isomers [105].

In conclusion, non-MS methods (UV-Vis, fluorescence, and radioenzymatic assays) offer low-cost, portable alternatives for AC analysis but lack either structural information or isomer discrimination, compromising the confirmatory power of MS.

Applications of AC detection

Biomarkers for clinical diagnosis

ACs profiling was initially used primarily for the diagnosis of IMDs, including FAO, OA, and defects in carnitine transport or the carnitine cycle. Combined with DBS from neonatal heel-prick blood and LC–MS/MS technology [106], the identification of characteristic metabolic patterns has been widely applied in NBS. Zhi et al. conducted a retrospective study to identify biomarkers for PCD in NBS. Analyzing 72 confirmed cases and validating their findings in approximately 80,000 samples, they found that acetylcarnitine (C2), butyrylcarnitine (C4), and isovalerylcarnitine (C5) exhibited strong discriminatory power (AUC: C4 99.79%, C2 98.72%, C5 98.62%). Furthermore, the combination of C4 and C5 achieved 100% sensitivity at a cutoff of 0.181 μmol/L, suggesting that C4+C5 can serve as an auxiliary quantitative indicator to reduce false-positive results and improve diagnostic efficiency in NBS for PCD [107]. Furthermore, ACs also have been recognized as important metabolic indicators of mitochondrial dysfunction and impaired energy homeostasis, and have been widely used for the diagnosis and monitoring of acquired diseases, particularly T2DM and insulin resistance (IR). In a study of 3313 non-diabetic Asian adults followed for 8.4 years, 314 developed diabetes. Elevated C4 and C16-OH acylcarnitines were associated with increased diabetes risk, whereas C8-DC, glycine, and serine correlated with decreased risk. The prediction model achieved an AUC of 0.846, indicating good overall performance, though sensitivity/specificity were unreported. These findings suggested ACs are significantly associated with T2DM risk in Asians and may add incremental predictive value to current models [108]. Overall, the clinical application of AC profiling has expanded from screening for rare IMDs in newborns to the diagnosis and disease assessment of common complex adult diseases, thereby providing a new metabolic biomarker basis for early detection, precise stratification, and dynamic monitoring of disease. Overview of AC applications in clinical diagnosis and disease mechanisms.

Clinical diagnosis using ACs requires not only their detection but also reliable, stable, and reproducible quantification at concentrations near clinically relevant decision thresholds. A robust diagnostic method must achieve a limit of quantification (LOQ) below the clinical cutoff values or the lower limits of reference intervals for disease-related ACs. Additionally, the method must meet predefined accuracy and precision criteria at the LOQ level. Maeda et al. reported an HPLC-MS/MS method for quantifying ACs in serum and urine, with LOQs of 0.1 μmol/L for methylmalonylcarnitine and 0.05 μmol/L for other ACs (intra- and inter-day CVs <9.2%, recoveries >90%) [119]. Moreover, Parsa Esmati developed an FI-MS/MS method applied to the measurement of ACs in patients with T2DM, achieving LOQs of 0.05–5 μmol/L, inter-batch precision <12.3%, and bias <10.2%, thereby meeting clinical requirements [42]. In acquired diseases, Meikopoulos et al. developed a HILIC-MS/MS method with LOQs of 78.1 ng/mL (C2), 2.4 ng/mL (C3, C18:1, C18:2), and 1.2 ng/mL (C4–C18), suitable for studies on T2DM [77].

Overall, ACs can serve as biomarkers for the clinical diagnosis of IMDs and other metabolic diseases. Developing methods that achieve acceptable accuracy, precision, and reproducibility is essential for their further application.

Investigating mechanisms underlying diseases and identifying therapeutic targets

Accumulating metabolomic evidence suggests that ACs are not merely passive byproducts of mitochondrial dysfunction but active mediators in disease initiation and progression. Their dynamic changes map perturbed metabolic networks and identify vulnerabilities in the carnitine shuttle, thus offering potential therapeutic targets for multisystem diseases (Table 3). In cancer studies, breast cancer cells (MCF-7 and MDA-MB-231) treated with 100 μM resveratrol showed a 2 to 13-fold increase in LCAC levels, indicating dose-dependent mitochondrial reprogramming [120]. Similarly, in gallbladder cancer, lncBCL2L11 disrupts the THOC6/THOC5 interaction and promotes THOC5 degradation, leading to AC accumulation that drives malignant progression. Conversely, high AC levels stabilize lncBCL2L11 via m6A methylation, forming a positive feedback loop that promotes metastasis. This axis represents a potential therapeutic target [121]. Moreover, in metabolic diseases, a retrospective study of 50 patients with diabetic cardiomyopathy (DCM) found that multiple MCACs and LCACs were positively associated with DCM risk. Treatment of H9c2 cardiomyocytes with myristoylcarnitine induced lipid accumulation, inhibited the AMPK/ACC/CPT1 pathway. This led to reduced fatty acid β-oxidation capacity, further accumulation of lipid intermediates, and the development of myocardial lipotoxicity, thereby promoting cardiac energy metabolism disorders, lipotoxic injury, and structural remodeling, ultimately driving the progression of DCM, which suggesting targeting the AMPK pathway may offer therapeutic potential [122].

In summary, precise quantification of ACs offers a novel approach for biomarker discovery and investigation of disease mechanisms. However, not all reported findings have been successfully replicated in independent cohorts, and effect sizes vary considerably depending on disease type, population characteristics, analytical platform, and study design. This observed heterogeneity in the literature needs to be carefully evaluated and balanced.

Challenges and future perspectives

In summary, despite the indispensable value of AC bioanalysis in NBS, metabolic diseases, and cancers, several analytical bottlenecks remain unresolved. Major challenges involve polarity coverage, isomer resolution, matrix interference, and standardization gaps. To tackle these obstacles, future research should embrace multidimensional strategies, such as IMS integration, automation and microfluidics, spatial and single-cell metabolomics, and AI-driven analytics.

First, integrating HRMS and IMS is a key future direction for AC bioanalysis. IMS provides orthogonal CCS information to conventional m/z, helping distinguish AC isomers and compensating for LC-MS/MS limitations [123]. Thus, LC–IMS–HRMS is expected to become an important tool for accurate structural characterization and unknown AC identification. However, its application in AC analysis still faces limitations. For some IMS platforms, CCS determination relies on calibrants and calibration curves, so results may be affected by calibration standards, experimental conditions, and inter-platform differences [124]. Moreover, IMS adds complexity to experimental design and data interpretation [125]. Although LC-IMS-HRMS offers clear advantages in structural resolution, isomer differentiation, and unknown compound discovery, it incurs higher instrument costs and data complexity. Therefore, in the short term, LC-IMS-HRMS is more likely to serve as a complementary validation platform to conventional LC-MS/MS, improving confidence in AC structural annotation and abnormal metabolite discovery.

Next, microfluidic chip technology, also known as “lab-on-a-chip” technology, enables precise manipulation of minute fluid volumes within microscale channels, integrating sample processing, reaction, separation, detection, and data analysis onto a single chip for rapid, low-consumption, automated, and high-throughput analysis [126]. This technology can improve clinical AC analysis efficiency. Conventional LC-MS/MS for AC detection involves multi-step sample pretreatment (PPT, centrifugation, transfer, derivatization), which is time-consuming and error-prone. In contrast, automated microfluidics simplifies sample processing, reduces sample/reagent volumes, minimizes manual handling, lowers contamination risks, improves reproducibility, and facilitates automation [127]. However, ACs encompass numerous structurally similar variants with differing polarities and chain lengths. In contrast, clinical quantification demands high accuracy, wide linear range, effective control of matrix effects, and stable quality control, all of which necessitate systematic validation. Furthermore, challenges such as large-scale standardized chip manufacturing, regulatory approval, and seamless integration with existing hospital workflows and information systems remain unresolved [128].

Moreover, revealing new features of local AC metabolism necessitates spatial and single-cell metabolomics. Conventional targeted LC–MS/MS offers high sensitivity and reproducibility but only reflects overall metabolic status, failing to resolve local or cell-type-specific abnormalities [129]. Spatial metabolomics, such as MALDI–MSI, enables in situ localization of analytes in complex tissues [130], while single-cell metabolomics can uncover intercellular metabolic differences, providing higher-resolution insights for disease mechanisms, biomarker discovery, and precision medicine [131]. Although current MALDI–MSI and single-cell approaches face challenges such as limited isomer resolution, poor absolute quantification, low analyte abundance, and complex sample preparation [132], these emerging technologies serve as a complement to current analytical methods.

Beyond the aforementioned directions, artificial intelligence (AI)-driven data analysis is poised to become an important future direction for AC bioanalysis. Notably, machine and deep learning algorithms can automate analytical workflows, including signal extraction and pattern recognition [133]. Compared with conventional LC–MS/MS, AI can automatically extract features from large-scale datasets, identify potential patterns, and build predictive models [134]. However, AI-driven analysis faces several challenges. First, a data bottleneck exists: high-quality, standardized datasets are scarce, limiting AI optimization and application. Second, poor model interpretability hinders regulatory acceptance, and general-purpose models often lack adaptability to specialized scenarios [135]. Given these limitations, in the short term, AI will primarily assist in peak processing, quality control, and anomaly detection, while in the long term, it may become a key component of clinical AC profile interpretation in precision medicine after sufficient validation and standardization.

In summary, ACs are key mediators of energy metabolism and promising biomarkers for complex diseases, including cardiovascular disorders and diabetes. This review summarizes current bioanalytical strategies for ACs, covering sample preparation, analytical techniques, and detection platforms, along with their clinical applications and future perspectives. These advances aim to overcome analytical challenges and facilitate the integration of AC profiling into precision medicine.
