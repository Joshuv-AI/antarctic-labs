Robust identification of impact force acting on composite structures via a single FBG sensor using variational mode decomposition
Authors: Jian Huang, Xiaoming Yang, Quanxin Jiang, Li Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIdentifying impact force on composite structures is crucial for structural integrity assessment, yet it remains a formidable challenge under operational conditions where impact-induced vibrations are corrupted by ambient noise and other dynamic loads. To address this limitation, this paper develops a robust two-stage identification framework that requires only a single Fiber Bragg Grating (FBG) sensor. The core innovation of the proposed methodology lies in leveraging Variational Mode Decomposition (VMD) to decouple the complex, noisy strain signal into its constituent modal strain responses, effectively isolating the impact’s signature from operational interference. From these purified modal responses, the uncorrected modal constant vector is extracted to serve as a unique fingerprint for impact localization over a grid of predefined potential impact points. The location is accurately determined by evaluating the cosine similarity between this vector and a pre-computed modal matrix of potential impact sites. Subsequently, with the location identified, the impact force’s time history is reconstructed using a sparse deconvolution model, which is efficiently solved by the Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers (ADMM). Experimental validation on a composite plate with simplified airfoil geometry confirms the method’s efficacy. The results demonstrate a localization success rate across the ten potential impact points exceeding 90% even under simulated operational conditions (harmonic and white noise excitation) and accurate force reconstruction, demonstrating the framework’s promising robustness, efficiency, and practical applicability for in-service structural health monitoring (SHM).Keywords: Impact force identification, Composite structure, Variational mode decomposition, FBG, Structural health monitoringSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Composite materials have been widely used in the manufacture of critical structural components within aviation, marine, and energy industries because of their exceptional mechanical properties, including high specific strength and specific stiffness1,2. However, during maintenance or operational phases, these composite structures are susceptible to external impact loads, resulting in internal damage that is undetectable through visual inspection3,4. Conventional approaches employ non-destructive testing (NDT) techniques, such as ultrasonic detection and infrared thermography, to assess impact-induced damage offline. However, these approaches are often time-consuming and financially prohibitive. Consequently, identifying external impact forces and leveraging their characteristics to evaluate structural integrity has emerged as a viable alternative. Two primary methodologies exist for impact force identification (ImFoId) on composite structures: direct measurement and indirect measurement. In practical engineering applications, direct force measurement is frequently infeasible due to challenges associated with sensor placement at targeted locations. Hence, indirect identification of impact loads via structural vibration responses (e.g., strain, acceleration) has emerged as the predominant methodology over the past three decades5,6.

Identifying impact force through structural vibration response is a typical inverse problem, which can be solved in both time and frequency domains. The most refined approach for ascertaining impact forces within the time domain involves the utilization of the triangulation technique. This technique employs an intricately arranged network of sensors to capture the Time of Arrival (TOA) delays of elastic waves at designated sensor locations. Upon acquiring the TOA delays, and by integrating them with the relative distances between the sensors, one can efficiently calculate the coordinates of the impact source7–9. Despite the principle of triangulation technique is straightforward and easy to implement, however it demands an exceedingly high sampling rate, significantly elevating the sampling costs of the system10. Moreover, the triangulation technique is predicated upon a foundational understanding of the propagation velocity of elastic waves within the given structure. In the context of composite structures, the inherent anisotropy and wave dispersion present considerable challenges in precisely determining both the speed and trajectory of elastic wave propagation, potentially resulting in erroneous localization results11. In order to overcome the shortcomings of triangulation techniques, ImFoId methods using the complete vibration response of the structure have been developed12,13.

The identification of impact force through complete vibration response is an ill-posed inverse problem characterized by a rank-deficient transfer function matrix exhibiting low condition number, rendering it both numerically unstable and computationally prohibitive14. Among conventional inverse solution strategies, pseudoinverse-based methodologies and least squares optimization have demonstrated limited efficacy due to their inability to address the inherent ill-conditioning and noise sensitivity of impact response data15. To address these limitations, regularization frameworks have emerged as dominant approaches for impact force identification (ImFoId), with Tikhonov regularization (l2-norm penalty) and sparse regularization (l1-norm penalty) being prominently applied across time and frequency domains to stabilize the solution process16,17. These techniques effectively mitigate the ill-posedness by introducing controlled bias through norm-based penalty terms, thereby enabling robust estimation of impact load characteristics. However, the implementation of regularization techniques-based ImFoId methods remain significant challenges in real world applications. Firstly, the dimensionality of the transfer matrix between impact forces and structural responses escalates significantly with enlarged structural dimensions, leading to an excessively time-consuming deconvolution process. Secondly, to ameliorate the rank deficiency of the transfer function matrix, regularization techniques necessitate a dense array of sensors on structure, which imposes a significant increase in both the sampling cost and wiring complexity for the ImFoId system. These interrelated challenges underscore the need for hybrid methodologies that balance solution accuracy with computational efficiency, potentially through model order reduction techniques or physics-informed machine learning frameworks18.

In consideration of the disparities in vibration modes among various potential impact locations, the discriminating modal constant vector can be served as a signature for impact localization19–21. Once the impact location is determined, the time history of the impact force can be rapidly reconstructed through either basis function fitting or regularization methods22. Generally, this approach only requires merely an accelerometer or a strain gauge to achieve the global ImFoId of the structure, and its computational efficiency is significantly higher than that of the simultaneous impact force identification method that depends on regularization techniques23,24. However, all the existing reports conduct ImFoId on the assumption that the structure is only subjected to impact loads25. When the structure is in an operational state, it is often exposed to different kinds of dynamic loads, which makes the frequency components of its vibration response signals extremely complex. As a result, accurately extracting the discriminating modal constant vector from the structural vibration response under actual operating conditions remains a challenging task.

Although the single-sensor approach proposed by Goutaudier et al24. demonstrates high efficiency, its performance under significant operational noise has yet to be verified. Similarly, the method in22 is primarily concerned with efficiency under ideal conditions. To address these shortcomings, the critical challenge addressed in this paper is maintaining identification accuracy when impact signals are contaminated by other dynamic loads. To improve the accuracy of ImFoId results under operational conditions, a robust ImFoId method based on variational mode decomposition (VMD) is proposed in this paper. The core innovation lies in integrating VMD as a physics-guided preprocessing step and the requirement of only a single low sampling rate fiber Bragg grating (FBG) sensor for implementation. By selectively extracting the modal strain responses corresponding to the structure’s natural frequencies, VMD effectively serves as an advanced modal filter that isolates the impact signature from operational interference. The reason VMD is selected is that it outperforms other methods, such as empirical mode decomposition (EMD) and wavelet transform, in decomposing complex structural response signals, especially in the context of structural health monitoring (SHM) tasks26. Specifically, once an impact event is detected, the VMD is employed to determine the modal strain responses of the structure. Subsequently, the uncorrected modal constant vector is extracted from the modal responses via the envelope fitting technique. By evaluating the collinearity between the uncorrected modal constant vector and all the columns of the system modal matrix, the location of the impact among a set of potential locations can be rapidly identified. Based on the localization result, a sparse deconvolution model for reconstructing the time history of the impact force is established and solved using the alternating direction method of multipliers (ADMM). This capability enables the robust application of the modal constant vector method using only a single sensor, representing a significant step toward practical in-service SHM applications. To validate the performance and robustness of the proposed method, some experimental verifications are carried out on a laminated composite plate with a simplified airfoil geometry under simulated operational conditions.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: Section "Theoretical background" introduces the theoretical background, including the sensing principle of FBG, the forward transfer model and VMD. Section "Impact force identification" investigates the proposed ImFoId framework, and a procedure is described for identifying the impact from the vibration measurements of a single FBG sensor. Section "Experimental validation" presents the experimental validations, which is used as a reference to demonstrate and validate the effectiveness of the proposed method. Finally, Section "Conclusion" concludes the paper.

Overall flowchart of proposed ImFoId methodology

The overall flowchart of proposed ImFoId methodology is summarized in Fig. 2. As can be seen from the picture, the proposed ImFoId methodology can be divided into off-line preparation phase and on-line identification phase. In the off-line preparation phase, the modal parameters of the target structure are firstly obtained by EMA or FEA, and the number of required modes is then determined by satisfying the column full rank condition of matrix D. Note that the measurement point should avoid the mode node of the first Nm modes. The acquisition and processing of impact response data are carried out in the on-line identification phase. Once the impact response data is acquired, the first Nm order modal strain response is obtained by VMD, and the uncorrected mode constant vector is then extracted by fitting the envelope curve. As the uncorrected mode constant vector Γ is obtained, impact localization can be realized by evaluating the location of the column vector in D with the highest collinearity to Γ. Following the results of impact localization and modal analysis, the transfer matrix H is then determined and the sparse regularization model can be established. Through solving the inverse problem by Eqs. (23)-(26), the time history of the impact force can be reconstructed.

Overall flowchart of the proposed ImFoId methodology.

While the online identification relies on a single FBG, the method’s efficacy is predicated on a comprehensive offline calibration phase. This calibration burden is a key consideration for practical implementation. For larger or more complex structures, this would necessitate more extensive experimental modal analysis or a high-fidelity finite element model. Therefore, the proposed framework is most readily applicable to critical structural components where such detailed characterization is feasible and warranted as part of the design or maintenance cycle.

Experimental validation

The ImFoId methodology presented in previous section can theoretically work utilizing a single FBG sensor with low sampling rate. In this section, a laminated composite plate with simplified airfoil geometry is employed to examine the feasibility of the proposed ImFoId methodology. In the experiments, impacts were applied with different heads of a hand-held impact hammer COINV INV9311 (rubber and nylon). The impact load history data and strain response data were simultaneously recorded by NI DAQ card and Gator High-Speed FBG interrogator (1–19.2kHz), respectively. The sampling frequency was set as 5 kHz, and the duration of the measurement was maintained at 1 second. The datum and proposed ImFoId methodology were run on a PC with an AMD Ryzen 5 5500 Processor.

Impact force identification under operational conditions

Most of the existing literatures have primarily focused on the ImFoId in an idealized environment, whereas investigations into ImFoId under operational conditions remain scarce. This study incorporates the operational conditions by employing an exciter to simulate external excitation, and examines the robustness of proposed method in identifying the impact force under operational conditions. The external excitation signal utilized in this study is a composite of a sinusoidal wave and Gaussian white noise, with point #2 randomly selected as the research object. Prior to testing, a sinusoidal wave with an amplitude of 1.3 mm and a frequency of 150 Hz was mixed with Gaussian white noise at a signal-to-noise ratio of 10 dB to serve as the excitation signal, which was then input into the control system of the signal generator. Subsequently, a power amplifier was employed to amplify the excitation signal, enabling the exciter to function effectively. Fig. 10 presents the external excitation signal (Fig. 10a), the strain response of the measuring point (Fig. 10b), and the time-frequency spectrum (Fig. 10c) of the strain response. As can be seen from Fig. 10c, even under external excitation, harmonic components centered around the natural frequency of the plate are still clearly discernible in the vibration response, and these harmonic components will be extracted as the basis for impact location.

To quantitatively demonstrate the necessity of the VMD stage, a baseline comparison was conducted. The localization procedure was repeated on the same noisy signals from the operational condition tests, but without applying VMD. The uncorrected modal constants were extracted by directly fitting the raw signal using band-pass filters centered at the natural frequencies. The resulting localization success rate dropped to 42.61%, with many cases showing ambiguous results where the cosine similarity for the true location was not uniquely the highest. This comparison confirms that the VMD-based modal filtering is the key component enabling the high accuracy and robustness of the proposed framework.

Figure 13 shows the reconstruction results of the impact force acting on point 2 under different parameter settings of external loads. In this study, the first six orders of modal parameters of the plate were employed to establish the transfer matrix H, with the dimensions of 1000 ×1000. Under operational conditions, higher-order modes can be excited and contribute to the overall response. Therefore, the first six modes were used to construct the transfer matrix H to capture a fuller dynamic range of the structure, improving the model’s ability to distinguish the impact response from the external excitation noise. Due to the existence of external excitation, it can be seen that the RE and PRE of the reconstruction results are higher than those in the ideal case. Discussions

The purpose of this subsection is to propose improvements of the ImFoId method presented in this paper. As can be observed from Table 1, there is a positive correlation between the hardness of the hammer and the success rate of impact localization on ten potential impact points. Specifically, the harder the hammer, the higher the success rate. To elaborate on this phenomenon more comprehensively, the spectrums of impact forces produced by different types of hammer tips are plotted, as depicted in Fig. 15. From Fig. 15, it is evident that the amplitude of the ideal impact force remains constant across the entire frequency domain. In contrast, the amplitudes of the impact forces generated by the nylon tip and the rubber tip decline as the frequency rises. Nevertheless, when compared to the rubber tip, the amplitude of the impact force generated by the nylon tip decays at a slower pace in the low-frequency band, being more similar to the ideal impact force. Consequently, the success rate of impact localization under the excitation of the nylon hammer is higher. The modeling error stemming from assuming the real impact force as an ideal impulse is one of the primary causes of inaccurate localization results.

Spectrums of different impact forces.

Figure 16 illustrates the results of impact localization at point #2 using four uncorrected modal constants (the first, third, fourth, and fifth orders). A comparison with Fig. 11 reveals that increasing the number of uncorrected modal constants enhances the contrast in cosine similarity between point #2 and other potential impact points, leading to more distinct impact localization results. Consequently, irrespective of the hammer tip used to generate the impact force, appropriately augmenting the number of uncalibrated modal constants improves the accuracy of impact localization. Nevertheless, extracting higher-order uncorrected modal constants typically necessitates a higher sampling rate, which may substantially increase computational and sampling costs.

Conclusion

This paper presents and validates a robust impact identification methodology for composite structures, uniquely requiring only a single FBG sensor and demonstrating efficacy under simulated operational conditions. The core innovation is a two-stage framework: initially, variational mode decomposition (VMD) acts as a modal filter to isolate impact-induced strain responses from noisy operational signals. From these purified responses, an "uncorrected modal constant vector" is extracted as a unique signature for localization through collinearity assessment. Subsequently, a sparse regularization model, efficiently solved by ADMM, reconstructs the impact force’s time history based on the identified location. Experimental validation on a composite airfoil plate demonstrated the framework’s potential for robust performance, achieving over 90% localization success across ten potential impact points even amidst significant external harmonic and stochastic excitations. While reconstruction accuracy showed some sensitivity to external noise intensity, the PRE of the reconstructed force was captured with an average precision of 22.29%, which is generally considered engineering-acceptable. It should be acknowledged that the validation was conducted on a single composite plate with a fixed sensor location and a specific set of simulated operational loads, while a detailed sensitivity analysis of key parameters is not carried out in this study, we recognize its importance for further validation. Future work should focus on extending this framework to more complex structures, considering various sensor placements, and testing against a wider range of operational noise profiles, and conducting such sensitivity analyses to fully establish its generalizability and limitations.
