Impact of Household Environmental Conditions on Residents’ Health in the Dammam Metropolitan Area, Saudi Arabia: A Cross-Sectional Study
Authors: Maryam Somali, Mohammed Al-Warthan, Mahmoud Berekaa, Mohammed Aljassim, Manna Alwadei, Abdulmalik Alsaif
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPoor indoor air quality (IAQ) has been linked to nonspecific health complaints, known as sick building syndrome (SBS). This cross-sectional study explored the association between common factors affecting IAQ and the prevalence of SBS among adult residents in the Dammam Metropolitan Area (DMA), Saudi Arabia. Data on SBS symptoms and related factors, including demographic information, housing characteristics, and environmental conditions were collected from 726 residents using a structured questionnaire. Multivariate logistic regression models were used to examine the associations between these factors and SBS. The overall prevalence of SBS in this study was 48.2%, with skin symptoms the most frequently reported, followed by general, respiratory, and eye symptoms. Factors associated with SBS included pet ownership, mold, cockroaches, passive smoking, tile flooring, home renovations, and accumulated dust. Although the cross-sectional design and the reliance on self-reported data may present limitations, these findings highlight a potential health risk in Saudi homes. Preventive measures are needed to improve IAQ in household environments, thereby reducing the risk of SBS and other related health effects.Keywords: sick building syndrome, indoor air quality, insect infestation, mold, pets: dust

1. Introduction

Prolonged exposure to poor indoor air quality (IAQ) has been linked to a wide range of nonspecific health complaints, which are collectively referred to as sick building syndrome (SBS).1,2 This health condition is characterized by nonspecific symptoms that typically lessen or disappear once individuals leave the affected building, indicating a direct relationship between IAQ and human health.3,4 Poor IAQ arises from multiple factors, including inadequate ventilation, chemical pollutants (e.g., volatile organic compounds (VOCs), particulate matter (PM), carbon monoxide (CO), and biological contaminants (e.g., bacteria, fungi, dust mites).5-9 According to the World Health Organization, poor IAQ contributes to 3.8 million deaths each year worldwide. 10 Hence, IAQ is a subject of ongoing research.

Sources of indoor pollutants vary depending on many factors such as building characteristics, occupant activities, climate, and socioeconomic status. One of the major sources of indoor pollutants that has been thoroughly investigated is tobacco smoke from various sources, including cigarettes, shisha, and vapes. It is recognized as one of the most significant and harmful indoor air pollutants, even for passive smokers, due to its complex chemical composition.11-13 The impact of passive smoking has been linked to several negative health outcomes, such as lung cancer and respiratory and cardiovascular diseases.14-16 Furniture, pets, insect infestations, and mold growth also affect IAQ, leading to the occurrence of SBS. On one hand, new furniture, including carpets, can emit VOCs, such as formaldehyde, while older furniture may trap dust and allergens, exacerbating eye, skin, and respiratory irritation.17-19 On the other hand, pets introduce allergens, such as dander, feathers, and saliva, which are associated with eye, skin, and nasal irritation.20-22 Insect infestations, particularly cockroaches, release allergenic proteins and microbial particles that can provoke allergic responses and respiratory issues,23,24 adding another layer to poor IAQ and occupant discomfort. Mold growth also plays a significant role, as damp indoor environments promote the release of spores, bioaerosols, and microbial VOCs, which are associated with allergic reactions and respiratory symptoms.25-27 Hence, multifactorial SBS results from combined chemical, biological, and ventilation-related exposures that compromise IAQ and negatively affect the health of household residents.

Numerous studies in Saudi Arabia (SA) and the Gulf Cooperation Council countries have highlighted that burning incense (e.g., oud and bakhoor) and heavy reliance on air-conditioning (AC) systems significantly impact IAQ. Burning incense, a culturally common practice in the region, is associated with the emission of large quantities of fine PM, heavy metals, and VOCs,28,29 which can exacerbate respiratory symptoms.30-32 In addition, due to the region’s extremely hot climate, households rely heavily on continuous AC systems, which contribute to dust accumulation in AC filters, including microorganisms and toxic chemicals that pose potential health risks following long-term exposure.33-35 Driven by the synergy of frequent sandstorm events and rapid industrialization, dust exposure in SA poses a critical environmental health threat.36,37 The accumulation of interior dust within households serves as a persistent reservoir for PM, toxic chemicals, and biological contaminants, reinforcing the strong linkage between outdoor air quality and IAQ.38-40 PM from these sources contributes to significant acute and chronic respiratory and cardiovascular morbidity.41-44

As noted, the majority of studies conducted in SA on this issue have focused on isolated pollutants and specific population groups or were conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic, limiting their generalizability to typical household environments.45-51 Therefore, this study aims to investigate the association between household environmental conditions—including building characteristics, cultural practices, biological and chemical contaminants—and the prevalence of SBS among adult residents in the Dammam Metropolitan Area.

2. Materials and Methods

2.1. Study Design and Settings

A cross-sectional study was conducted between May and September 2025 in the densely populated Dammam Metropolitan Area (DMA), which is located along the Arabian Gulf coast in the Eastern Province of SA (Figure 1). With a population of 2 million people living in a combined area of approximately 3,900 km2, DMA comprises three principal cities: Dammam (the provincial capital), Al-Khobar, and Dhahran. The DMA hosts the majority of the Eastern Province’s regional administrative institutions and serves as a strategic hub for Saudi Arabia’s oil and gas industry.52,53

A city-based stratified sampling approach was used, where the population of the DMA was divided into three strata corresponding to the three major cities (Dammam, Al-Khobar, and Dhahran), and a sample size per stratum was determined proportionally to its actual size in the overall DMA population.

2.3. Data Collection

The data were collected from participants via an online questionnaire survey using Google Forms, which was distributed through social media platforms (e.g., WhatsApp, Telegram, LinkedIn, and Twitter) using convenience sampling. All adult residents (≥18 years old) of the DMA who used social media platforms and understood the questionnaire contents were eligible to participate in this study. Participation was voluntary and anonymous. No personally identifiable information was collected, and participants were free to withdraw from the survey at any time. Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Institutional Review Board at Imam Abdulrahman Bin Faisal University (IRB No: PGS-2025-03-0317).

The three-part survey questionnaire was based on previous studies56-59 and modified to better reflect the cultural context, lifestyle, housing designs, and features specific to the DMA. The first section gathered demographic information (e.g., age, gender, education, city of residence, smoking habits, and health status), while the second section asked about the household’s characteristics and environmental conditions (e.g., building type and age, AC, floor material, dampness and mold issues, pets, and use of scented products and pesticides). Finally, the third section included items related to 16 symptoms associated with SBS, which were grouped into four categories: (a) general symptoms (e.g., nausea or vomiting, headache, tiredness, fatigue or drowsiness, nervousness, difficulty concentrating, dizziness or lightheadedness, and feeling depressed), (b) respiratory symptoms (e.g., stuffy or runny nose, cough, sneezing, sore or dry throat, wheezing, and shortness of breath), (c) skin symptoms (e.g., dry or flushed skin, scaling/itching scalp or ears), and (d) eye irritation symptoms (e.g., dry, itching, or irritated eyes and tired or strained eyes). Participants were asked whether they frequently experienced any of these symptoms while at home over the past six months. To determine whether the symptoms were SBS-related, participants were also asked whether these symptoms improved or disappeared when they were away from home.56,60,61 Participants were classified as having SBS if they reported two or more SBS-related symptoms.4,62,63

2.4. Data Analysis

The data were entered and analyzed using SPSS software (version 24). Descriptive statistics, including frequencies and percentages, were used to summarize the variables. Subsequently, multivariate logistic regression was performed to analyze home conditions to identify potential predictors of SBS after adjusting for potential confounders, such as age, gender, smoking status, history of asthma or eczema, and city of residence. Adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported, and a p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

3. Results

3.1. Demographic Characteristics

The majority (61.0%) of the 726 participants lived in Dammam city. Their age distribution indicated a generally young sample, with 56.4% younger than 40 years old. The participants were predominantly married (68.6%), and women comprised a slight majority (55.5%). A substantial proportion held a college degree and were employed (60.9%). Only 17.1% reported being smokers, and 20.1% reported a history of asthma or eczema (Table 1).

3.2. Household Conditions

The majority of DMA residents (87.4%) lived in villas or apartments, and 62.6% had lived in their homes for 5 years or more. About 39.8% lived near a main road or highway. Most were homeowners (70.4%), and nearly half of the residences were over 10 years old. Almost all participants (97.8%) lived with at least one other person.

Regarding household conditions, about one-fourth reported having pets at home (24.7%), and more than half reported the presence of cockroaches (54.0%). Pesticide use was common but generally infrequent, with most participants using it rarely or occasionally (about 69%). Water damage was reported by 21.3% of the participants, and 23.4% reported visible mold. Around one-third indicated exposure to passive smoking at home (31.0%).

Considering the use of AC in indoor environments, 69.0% used split-type AC, while others used central AC (18.6%) or window AC units (12.4%). AC maintenance was typically performed once or twice a year. Home renovations within the past year were reported by 32.2% of the participants, and dust accumulation was described as moderate by about half. Incense burning was common (33.1%), and nearly one-fifth (19.6%) used perfumes or other scented products at home. Gas (54.5%) or electric stoves (45.5%) were mainly used for cooking, and most kitchens were equipped with exhaust fans (92.0%). Bedrooms commonly had tile floors (62.8%), and most participants cleaned their bedrooms at least eight times per month (Table 2).

3.3. Prevalence of SBS

The data analysis results from the study participants (n=726) indicated that the overall prevalence of SBS was 48.2% among those who reported experiencing two or more symptoms. As shown in Figure 2, skin symptoms were the most prevalent (52.1%), particularly skin dryness and itching of the scalp or ears. General symptoms were commonly reported (47.7%), especially tiredness or fatigue, difficulty concentrating, nervousness, and feeling depressed. Respiratory symptoms affected 41.6% of participants, with sneezing, a stuffy or runny nose, and a sore or dry throat being the most frequent. Eye symptoms were also reported (38.0%), including strained or tired eyes as well as itching and irritation.

3.4. Factors Associated With SBS

A logistic regression was conducted to assess the relationship between demographic characteristics, household conditions, and the likelihood of experiencing SBS symptoms. As shown in Table 3, women were more likely than men to report eye, respiratory, and general symptoms. Participants aged 21-29 years old had a higher risk of reporting eye and respiratory symptoms compared with the other age groups. Smokers showed slightly higher odds of skin and respiratory symptoms, although these associations were not statistically significant. Individuals with a history of asthma or eczema were consistently more likely to report symptoms across all categories. In addition, Dammam residents were more likely to experience skin, eye, and general symptoms compared to Dhahran residents.

Exposure to passive smoking showed significant associations across all symptom types, including skin, eye, respiratory, and general symptoms. Recent home renovations (within the last 12 months) were similarly associated with higher odds of skin, respiratory, and general symptoms.

Dust accumulation also showed a strong pattern. High dust levels were significantly associated with skin, eye, and respiratory symptoms. Moderate dust accumulation showed similar but slightly weaker associations with skin, eye, and respiratory symptoms. The type of bedroom flooring, on the other hand, showed an influential effect on SBS symptoms. Participants who reported having tile flooring in their bedroom had a higher risk of skin symptoms compared to those having fully carpeted bedrooms.

4. Discussion

This study is one of the few that have assessed the relationship between SBS and environmental conditions in Saudi households. Several sources of indoor air pollutants were found to be significantly associated with the prevalence of SBS symptoms among adult residents in the DMA, which suggests that SBS is a common public health concern in SA and highlights the need for prompt and targeted interventions.

The reported prevalence of SBS in this study (48.2%) is relatively high compared to studies from Japan (3.7%), 64 Ethiopia (21.7%), 56 Sweden (23%), 65 United Arab Emirates (30%), 55 and Somalia (41%), 66 while lower than the rates reported in Iran (56.4%), 67 Mexico (91%), 68 and Jordan (93%). 69 These cross-country differences may be explained by variations in climate, ventilation practices, housing design, and cultural behaviors that influence IAQ and time spent indoors.

Gender demographics influenced the distribution of SBS among DMA residents; for instance, female participants were more likely to develop SBS symptoms than male participants. Previous studies have also shown that this influential demographic characteristic is consistently linked to a higher prevalence of SBS.66,70,71 These gender differences can be attributed to a combination of social and biological influences. For example, women often experience different social roles and life circumstances compared to men, which may increase their exposure to indoor environments and associated psychosocial stressors.72-74 Biological factors, including hormonal fluctuations, menstrual-related discomfort, and greater sensitivity to chemical exposures75,76 may further contribute to women’s heightened susceptibility to SBS.

Moreover, SBS prevalence was higher among adults under 30, with skin and respiratory symptoms were the most frequently reported. This aligns with other studies suggesting that younger age increases susceptibility to SBS,62,77,78 potentially influenced by differences in lifestyle, awareness, and physiological tolerance to certain environmental exposures. Nowadays young adults, as those in SA, have a lifestyle pattern characterized by prolonged time spent indoors engaging in sedentary activities,79,80 which may increase their exposure to indoor air pollutants. In addition, they might have elevated concern about environmental exposures, which makes them in general perceive and report symptoms more frequently than older adults.81,82 Also, compared with older adults, they may exhibit greater physiological sensitivity to such exposures.83,84 Additionally, the study analysis showed that residents with a history of asthma or eczema exhibited a higher likelihood of developing SBS symptoms, which is consistent with previous studies showing that individuals with asthma, eczema, or other atopic conditions are more susceptible to SBS.68,85-89 Residents in Dammam were found to be at higher risk of SBS than those in Al-Khobar and Dhahran. Although not directly examined in this study, differences in socioeconomic status across these cities may be an important factor contributing to the observed disparities in SBS prevalence. Socioeconomic determinants, including income, education, employment, and the physical environment, play a critical role in shaping living conditions and health outcomes at both individual and population levels. Compared with Dammam, both Al-Khobar and Dhahran generally exhibit higher socioeconomic profiles within the Eastern Province, with correspondingly higher living standards. Dhahran serves as a major center of the region’s oil economy and hosts the headquarters of Saudi Aramco, one of the world’s largest energy companies, contributing to the city’s advanced infrastructure and planned residential environments. Al-Khobar, on the other hand, functions as the province’s primary commercial and retail hub and is widely characterized by higher-end residential, leisure, and urban amenities. On the contrary, Dammam, the administrative capital and major industrial hub of the Eastern Province, has a higher population density (2000 persons/km 2 ) 90 than Al-Khobar and Dhahran and includes residential areas located near major roadways, port operations, and industrial facilities that may affect IAQ. These contextual differences may partly explain the observed variation in SBS prevalence across cities.

Biological contaminants were an emerging concern among the reported household conditions associated with SBS. In particular, visible mold showed a significant association with eye and skin symptoms. Previous studies consistently demonstrated that damp and mold-contaminated indoor environments were strongly linked to eye and skin symptoms among household residents.91-93 Widespread indoor fungal contaminants have been reported by many studies in SA, especially within AC systems and on construction materials.35,94,95 Elevated humidity levels in coastal areas, such as the DMA, further increase the likelihood of moisture accumulation and mold growth in buildings. 96

Our analysis also indicated that pets and cockroaches are another source of biological contaminants in the DMA households. Pet ownership was significantly associated with a higher risk of experiencing eye-related symptoms, which is likely due to exposure to airborne allergens derived from animal dander and their body fluids.97,98 In support of this suggested association, a recent large nationwide study in China found that pet ownership among urban residents was significantly associated with dry eye symptoms. 99 Similarly, cockroaches were also associated with a higher rate of respiratory and general symptoms. Cockroach allergens are a major contributor to asthma, wheezing, and other respiratory problems in addition to general SBS symptoms.100-103 Indoor pest infestation can also lead to psychological effects that may be overlooked by affected residents, such as stress, anxiety, and discomfort, which are potential causes of headaches, fatigue, and malaise.104,105

Passive or secondhand smoking was among the most substantial sources of indoor pollutants in this study and was associated with nearly all SBS symptom categories. Community-based studies from Iran, China, and Japan have also reported significant associations between passive smoking and the development of SBS symptoms.70,106,107 In SA, tobacco smoke remains a major contributor to household air pollution, with a high prevalence among both adults and adolescents.108-110 Other studies in SA have also indicated that indoor passive smoking is an important risk factor for asthma and various respiratory complaints among Saudi adults.111,112

Indoor dust was frequently reported by the study participants, specifically visible dust accumulation within their homes. The results of our analysis demonstrated significant associations between reported dust presence and skin, eye, and respiratory symptoms. The role of household cleanliness and dust loads in the development of SBS has been investigated around the world. In Ethiopia, a community-based study found that occupants living in unclean residential buildings had a 1.26 times higher odds of developing SBS compared with those in cleaner environments. 56 Similarly, research from Somalia reported that SBS prevalence was five times higher among residents in visibly dusty buildings than in clean homes. 66 With 10–60 events each year, the Eastern Province of Saudi Arabia is a well-known hotspot for sand and dust storms,34,41 which frequently deposits substantial PM onto residential areas, contributing to the considerable indoor dust accumulation reported by the participants.

Furthermore, recent home maintenance or renovation activities were also found to be associated with SBS, particularly in relation to skin, respiratory, and general symptoms. Renovation activities, such as painting, installing new materials, and replacing furniture, can be potential emission sources for VOCs, including formaldehyde, as well as fine PM, which can persist in poorly ventilated spaces, contributing to the development of SBS.86,107,113-117

Interestingly, this study found that tile flooring increases the risk of skin-related SBS symptoms, which might be linked to several factors. Hard flooring surfaces, such as tile, were found to promote greater resuspension of settled PM more readily than carpeted floors,118,119 increasing dermal exposure to airborne allergens. 120 In addition, tile floors typically require frequent cleaning with detergents and disinfectants, and exposure to these chemicals has been linked to skin irritation and dermatitis.121-124 Together, these factors may provide a plausible explanation for the observed association between tile flooring and skin symptoms.

Finally, several household conditions examined in this study (e.g., AC type, kitchen exhaust fan presence, and incense burning) were not significantly associated with SBS symptoms, possibly reflecting limited exposure variability, the high prevalence of certain features, or effect attenuation after controlling for personal factors.

5. Limitations

The findings of this study should be interpreted considering several limitations. The use of an online survey with convenience sampling via social media may introduce selection bias and limit generalizability. The reliance on self-reported exposures and symptoms is another limitation, which raises the possibility of misclassification and recall bias. The cross-sectional design adopted in the study is an additional limitation that may have affected the proper establishment of causal relationships; also, the data collection period (May–September) may not capture seasonal variability. Residual confounding also remains a significant limitation, as unmeasured factors such as household income, occupational exposures, and time spent at home may have influenced the observed associations. This is in spite of the efforts made to address these limitations, which included increasing the sample size, distributing the survey across various social media platforms, and ensuring proportional recruitment of participants across the DMA.

Regardless of these limitations, the study findings provide insight into this important topic and highlight the need for more thorough research that incorporates objective IAQ monitoring (e.g., PM2.5, VOCs, and mold) and well-designed field-based investigations, alongside longitudinal approaches, to reduce exposure misclassification and strengthen causal inference of indoor environmental health in residential settings in SA. Addressing these methodological gaps will enhance the robustness and generalizability of future research in the Saudi context.

6. Conclusion

This study identified various sources of indoor pollutants linked to SBS symptoms in nearly half of the surveyed adult residents in the DMA, highlighting a critical yet overlooked public health issue in SA. Passive smoking, dust accumulation, visible mold, pest infestation, tile flooring, and recent home renovation were among the sources found to be associated with respiratory, eye, skin, and general SBS symptoms. A particularly notable finding was the link between skin symptoms, the most common complaint, and tile flooring, a standard feature in Saudi homes, suggesting a need for further research into cleaning methods and tile flooring material properties.

These findings highlight the need for nationwide IAQ monitoring programs and evidence-based regulations. Practical household ventilation measures are also required to reduce dust, secondhand smoke, mold growth, and prevent exposure to these and other potential indoor air pollutants. At the same time, initiatives are needed to raise public awareness about all possible measures that contribute to the reduction of SBS risk, such as maintaining smoke-free homes, adopting integrated pest management, and following safe renovation practices. Mitigating these household hazards is crucial for reducing SBS and enhancing the overall health of residents throughout SA.
