Adaptive queue management in healthcare using supervised Q-learning with time-varying reward and cost structures
Authors: Kartick Bag, Raj Panda, Sweety Suman Jha, Arista Lahiri, Debjani Chakraborty
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Efficient patient management in hospitals requires adaptive decision-making under time-varying demand and dynamic service environments. This study proposes a heterogeneous medical patient queueing model that integrates reinforcement learning with stochastic queue dynamics to minimize overall patient waiting time. The model distinguishes between two categories of service providers (SPs): those attending first-time patients and those serving returning patients. Each category may differ in service rate but not in medical specialty. Patient arrivals follow a non-homogeneous Poisson process (NHPP) to capture realistic time-dependent flow variations. A Q-learning framework with a supervised ε-greedy policy is developed to determine optimal operational actions, such as adding or reallocating service providers, based on system state and event type. Separate Q-tables are maintained for arrival and departure events to account for differing cost and reward dynamics. Simulation results demonstrate that the proposed model significantly reduces total waiting time and system cost compared with conventional homogeneous queue models. This approach provides a data-driven mechanism for dynamic hospital queue management and can be extended to broader healthcare resource optimization scenarios.

Introduction

Effective patient management is one of the most critical challenges in modern healthcare systems, where fluctuating patient demand, limited resources, and service heterogeneity often lead to congestion and prolonged waiting times. There in outpatient departments (OPDs), the arrival rate of patients varies significantly throughout the day due to various external factors. In traditional queuing models, particularly those assuming homogeneous types service structures and constant Poisson arrivals, often fail to capture this dynamic and heterogeneous nature of the real-world hospital operations. Consequently, these types of models may not offer sufficient decision support for adaptive workforce management or resource allocation in the face of evolving circumstances.

To address these types of challenges, queuing-based learning models gained increasing attention in healthcare operations research. Reinforcement learning (RL), specifically Q-learning, offers a data-driven mechanism to optimize sequential decisions under uncertainty without requiring complete knowledge of the system dynamics. Several studies have been applied in RL to healthcare service management, including appointment scheduling, dynamic staffing, and emergency response optimization. However, several existing approaches rely on simplified service and arrival assumptions, which may limit their adaptability in dynamic medical environments. Moreover, few models explicitly account for time-varying arrival rates or distinct service patterns associated with different patient types, both of which are fundamental to real-life OPD operations scenarios.

The problem of controlling queueing systems with heterogeneous servers has been extensively studied in operations research literature. Early foundational work by Larsen1 conjectured that the optimal service policies in heterogeneous server systems could be characterized by threshold-type structures. This conjecture was subsequently verified for systems with two heterogeneous servers using policy iteration techniques2, value iteration methods3, and sample-path arguments4. Attempts to extend these results to systems having multiple heterogeneous servers were made by Rykov5, but the proposed proof was later shown to be incomplete by De Vericourt and Zhou6. Similar concerns were expressed with alternative formulations7. Recent studies emphasize that the characterization of optimal control policies for multi-server heterogeneous queueing systems remains an open and challenging problem8. Due to the analytical intractability of such systems, particularly under stochastic arrival processes and service times that follow a general distribution, learning-based and approximate dynamic programming approaches have emerged as practical alternatives. Learning-based approaches have been successfully applied to a range of queueing and service system problems, including network scheduling9, inventory control10, and emergency service deployment11. Comprehensive surveys further illustrate the increasing significance of RL in queueing and service systems12,13. Such methods are particularly attractive in settings where system dynamics are complex or partially unknown, as they enable adaptive policy learning without relying on explicit analytical characterizations14. In healthcare operations, queueing-based models have been widely employed to analyze patient flow, waiting times, and resource utilization in outpatient departments and emergency units. Several studies have emphasized the importance of capturing time-varying patient arrival patterns using non-homogeneous Poisson processes (NHPPs)15,16 to reflect peak-hour congestion and off-peak variability17,18. However, most existing RL-based healthcare queueing models continue to rely on homogeneous service assumptions or stationary arrival processes. Relatively limited attention has been given to integrating time-dependent arrival dynamics within a unified reinforcement learning framework, particularly in OPD environments where patient types and service requirements differ substantially.

Here, in this work, we propose a heterogeneous medical patient queuing model that integrates Q-learning with a non-homogeneous Poisson process (NHPP)-based arrival framework guided by a heuristic supervised ε-greedy policy to optimize long-term operational costs and system efficiency. This proposed model captures the dynamic interplay between patient inflow and service provider (SP) allotment through a reinforcement learning (RL)-based adaptive policy. Service providers are categorized in two different functional groups: those attending the first-time patients (FP-SPs) and those serving the returning or follow-up patients (RP-SPs). Each server from each server category operates with distinct service rates, reflecting the practical differences in consultation or service duration, thereby introducing heterogeneity into the system.

The arrival process is modeled as a time-dependent NHPP, described by a rate function λ (t), which varies with time to replicate realistic patient inflow patterns in OPDs. This allows the model to represent the peak-hour congestion and off-peak idle periods within a single stochastic framework. By coupling NHPP arrivals with a Q-learning–based decision mechanism, the proposed model dynamically adjusts operational actions to minimize the long-term total system cost, which encompasses both waiting and operational components.

The reinforcement learning framework is designed to autonomously learn optimal policies through trial-and-error interactions with the environment. Specifically, the agent selects actions such as adding new service providers, swapping between FP-SP and RP-SP queues, or maintaining the current configuration, depending on the event type and current system state. Unlike conventional approaches, the proposed learning strategy employs a supervised ε-greedy policy, where exploration decisions are guided by event-specific cost evaluations rather than purely random selection. This structure enhances learning stability and accelerates convergence toward optimal queue management policies.

Comprehensive simulation experiments were conducted to evaluate the proposed framework. Results demonstrate that the Q-learning–based heterogeneous queue significantly reduces total operational cost compared with a homogeneous baseline model under equivalent conditions. The adaptive nature of the learned policy enables the system to respond effectively to fluctuating patient arrival rates, balancing service efficiency and resource utilization. The findings highlight the potential of reinforcement learning–based adaptive queue management for improving healthcare service quality and operational resilience in OPD environments.

System-level performance and baseline comparison

While the convergence of the Q-learning algorithm minimizes expected system costs, evaluating practical hospital performance metrics is essential. To this end, a 100-day Monte Carlo simulation was conducted to compare the trained dynamic Reinforcement Learning (RL) policy against a conventional static allocation baseline.

The simulation environment was subjected to time-varying demand using a non-homogeneous Poisson process (NHPP). To rigorously test adaptability, the traffic was asymmetrically weighted, simulating a surge of first-time patients in the early hours and a shift toward returning patients later in the shift. The Static Baseline was restricted to a fixed provider allocation (reserving a strict number of providers for each patient class), whereas the RL model dynamically adjusted provider allocations from a shared pool based on real-time queue lengths. The action-switching penalty, utilized during training to prevent hyperactive reallocation, was excluded from the simulation to isolate core operational costs.

System-level performance was evaluated across four key operational metrics: Total Cost, Average Waiting Time, System Throughput, and Jain’s Fairness Index. The empirical data demonstrates that the dynamic RL policy significantly outperforms the static baseline across all measured metrics. Most notably, the RL model reduced the total average waiting time from 18.48 minutes to 11.76 minutes, achieving an approximate 36% improvement. Additionally, the system’s empirical throughput increased from 0.1830 to 0.1956 patients per minute, while the total operational cost was reduced by approximately 13.3%. Collectively, these results demonstrate that the proposed heterogeneous Q-learning model learns stable, feasible, and resource-efficient scheduling policies that adapt to heterogeneous patient queues and time-varying demand.

Discussion

The experimental results provide several important insights into the behavior and robustness of the proposed heterogeneous Q-learning–based scheduling framework. Unlike classical homogeneous queueing formulations, which typically assume constant arrival rates and uniform waiting-cost structures20, the present model incorporates both time-varying arrival intensity and class-specific service characteristics. The NHPP-driven arrival process enables the model to capture empirically observed multi-peak outpatient inflow patterns, while the heterogeneous cost structure allows first-time and returning patients to influence the optimal scheduling policy in distinct ways. A meaningful comparison emerges when examining the behavior of the learned policy across states containing a total of three patients. In the homogeneous Q-learning model reported by Ni et al.21, the optimal policy assigns two service providers for all such states and yields an approximate Q-value of -5.61. Methods

Proposed queueing model in the light of traditional approaches

Traditional queuing theory provides a well-established framework for analyzing service systems, in which performance measures such as average waiting time, queue length, and system utilization are derived under specific assumptions, typically involving stationary arrival processes and fixed service mechanisms. Classical models such as M/M/1 or M/G/K allow analytical derivation of these metrics under simplified conditions. However, real-world healthcare systems often exhibit time-varying arrivals. In the present study, patient arrivals are modeled using a non-homogeneous Poisson process (NHPP)15,16, and service times follow a general distribution. Furthermore, the system incorporates dynamic decision-making through adaptive service provider allocation. These features result in a non-stationary and state-dependent queueing system, making closed-form analytical expressions for performance measures, such as average waiting time, intractable. However, an approximate expression for the queue length can be obtained using standard multi-server queueing approximations, such as the Allen-Cunneen formula24. The corresponding waiting time is then estimated using Little’s law:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L_q(t) \approx \frac{\rho (t)^{\sqrt{2(K(t)+1)}}}{1 - \rho (t)} \cdot \frac{C_s^2 + 1}{2}, \quad W_q(t) \approx \frac{L_q(t)}{\lambda (t)},$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rho (t) = \frac{\lambda (t)}{K(t)\mu }$$\end{document} denotes the time-dependent traffic intensity, λ (t) is the arrival rate, μ is the service rate, and K(t) is the number of active servers.

To address these challenges, a reinforcement learning (RL)-based framework is adopted. The RL agent operates on the queueing system by observing the system state, which includes queue lengths and service provider configurations, and dynamically adjusts resource allocation to minimize total system cost.

Problem description

This study considers a heterogeneous healthcare queueing system representing an outpatient department (OPD), where patients arrive over time and require consultation services. The system distinguishes between two categories of patients: first-visit patients (F-patients) and returning or follow-up patients (R-patients), each forming separate queues upon arrival.

Patient arrivals are modeled as a non-homogeneous Poisson process (NHPP), allowing the capture of time-varying arrival intensities commonly observed in real healthcare settings. Upon arrival, patients are directed to their respective queues based on their classification. Each queue is served by a designated group of service providers (SPs), and patients wait if no provider is immediately available.

To efficiently manage system congestion and balance service allocation, a supervised Q-learning framework is integrated into the decision-making process. The learning agent observes the system state and selects actions that dynamically adjust the number and allocation of service providers, including adding new service providers, swapping providers between queues, or maintaining the existing configuration.

This is a heterogeneous queuing model for outpatient department (OPD) services, designed to minimize the long-term total system cost while concurrently reducing patient waiting times. Supervised Q-learning framework (SQL)

To enable adaptive and data-driven decision-making, the patient queueing environment is modeled as a Markov Decision Process (MDP). In this formulation, the system evolves over discrete decision epochs where actions correspond to scheduling or allocation decisions, and rewards reflect the balance between diminished waiting costs and operating expenditures. The Q-learning algorithm is employed as the reinforcement learning mechanism to identify optimum allocation strategies via environmental interaction. Supervised ε-greedy policy

In the proposed SQL model, action selection is governed by a supervised ε-greedy strategy.The fundamental concept is that the exploration phase is supervised by domain-specific cost rules, rather than being conducted entirely at random. This approach enhances the practical stability and learning efficiency of the heterogeneous patient queue management process. In the reinforcement learning literature, similar heuristic-guided exploration approaches have been investigated (e.g., heuristic-guided RL and preference-based ε-greedy methods 27,28), but in the present formulation adapts These ideas are specifically for the heterogeneous patient queue model by introducing event-wise cost evaluation functions for supervised exploration. With probability ε, the algorithm explores new actions; however, unlike the conventional ε-greedy policy where exploration is purely random, here, it is guided by heuristic cost evaluations corresponding to the current event type. For instance, during an arrival at a first-time patient queue (AF), if we have to add one SP in FP-SP, then the candidate exploratory actions \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(+1_{FP}, +1_{SFP})$$\end{document} are evaluated through the incremental cost functions \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta C(+1_{FP})$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta C(+1_{SFP})$$\end{document}, and the action that gives a lower incremental cost is selected. Similar cost-based comparisons are applied for other arrival or departure event categories (AR, DF, DR). With probability 1-ε, the algorithm performs exploitation by selecting the action with the maximum Q-value for the current state. This heuristic supervision ensures that exploratory decisions remain feasible and cost-effective, reducing impractical random choices while preserving the essential balance between exploration and exploitation.\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$a = {\left\{ \begin{array}{ll} \text {guided action from heuristic supervision}, & \text {with probability } \epsilon , \\ \arg \max \limits _{a'} Q(s,a'), & \text {with probability } 1-\epsilon \end{array}\right. }$$\end{document}
