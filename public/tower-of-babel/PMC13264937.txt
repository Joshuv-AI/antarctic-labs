Contemporary preoperative assessment for colorectal surgery: new insights and developments
Authors: Joyce Ong, Michael Wylie, Malcolm G Dunlop, Nicholas T Ventham
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPreoperative assessment is a critical step for every patient undergoing major colorectal surgery. Increasingly, major colorectal resection is being safely performed in older and multimorbid patients. Many established tenets of preoperative assessment—careful history and examination and selected laboratory investigations—have remained unchanged for decades. However, there is an increasing focus on the importance of this step in the pathway as a key opportunity to intervene and improve patient outcomes following surgery. Patients are increasingly complex, necessitating a multidisciplinary approach involving a range of specialists to help identify and optimise modifiable risk factors and manage comorbidities. Modern approaches use risk stratification scores to better communicate risk, nutritional and frailty assessments, intravenous iron therapy, antibiotic plus bowel cleansing regimens and the use of biomarkers. Preoperative patient education and prehabilitation approaches are being harnessed to better prepare patients for surgery. Comprehensive assessment and optimisation before surgery can improve surgical outcomes and contribute to safer perioperative care.

Introduction

Preoperative assessment is the comprehensive investigation and evaluation of patients before anaesthetic and surgical procedures.1 Many of the key tenets of preoperative assessment have remained unchanged for decades. The key aims are to form a comprehensive understanding of the patient’s current health status and comorbidities, optimise the patient’s health by managing their comorbidities accordingly, identify risk factors and reduce the risks associated with the procedure and obtain informed consent from the patient regarding anaesthesia and the surgical procedure.1 2 Several developments have been made to contemporary preoperative assessment. Novel risk scoring systems and the emerging use of biomarkers and other preoperative investigations have been used to facilitate communication of risk to patients, relatives and carers around the time of surgery. Moreover, there has been significant progress in operative strategies in colorectal surgery to minimise the impact of major surgery on patients. This includes the routine adoption of enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS), minimally invasive surgery (such as laparoscopic and robotic surgery) and techniques that avoid the need for intraperitoneal surgery (eg, transanal microsurgery and advanced endoscopic techniques). Improvements in 1-year CRC survival have been observed over the last 20 years,3 with much of this progress driven by safer preoperative care. This article aims to review contemporary preassessment prior to major colorectal surgery, with a particular focus on newer developments.

Components of preoperative assessment

Medical history and physical examination

Thorough medical history and physical examination remain the cornerstone of preoperative assessment.2 The Centre for Perioperative Care (CPOC) guideline states that a thorough history and examination are the most efficient way of identifying significant medical conditions. This evaluation includes a detailed assessment of a patient’s medical records, allergies and medication reconciliation. There may be a requirement to liaise with the patient’s General Practitioner and other specialty consultants to communicate vital information before the surgery.1 2

The preoperative assessment allows an opportunity to optimise some modifiable risk factors ahead of major surgery. The time to achieve such goals may be limited in the context of colorectal cancer (CRC) surgery. Patients undergoing neoadjuvant treatment for CRC, or those with Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD) or benign pathologies (eg, diverticular disease and hernia) may have additional time and scope for more careful optimisation.

Obesity

Obesity has been associated with adverse outcomes in colorectal surgery, including an increased risk of surgical site infections, wound complications, anastomotic leak, longer operative times and greater intraoperative blood loss.57 Weight loss strategies such as modified diets, exercise programmes and more recently, weight loss drugs have been proposed. Preoperative very low energy diets have been shown through the RetroPREPARE trial to have a significant reduction in 30-day postoperative morbidity in patients with obesity prior to colorectal surgery.8 Newer drugs available to treat predominantly diabetes and obesity have allowed an opportunity to modify this risk factor that has been traditionally difficult to address by lifestyle and dietetic advice only. Medications such as glucagon-like peptide 1 (GLP-1) agonists have recently been introduced for weight loss in the preoperative setting. GLP-1 agonists preoperatively may allow patients to reach a safe body mass index target, reducing the number of anaesthetic and surgical complications. GLP-1 agonist drugs can achieve a reported weight loss of up to 21 kg, with semaglutide and tirzepatide being the most effective.9 Novel research has shown that the continued use of GLP-1 agonists was not significantly associated with increased risk of perioperative complications or 30-day morbidity.10 11 The CPOC recommends the continuation of GLP-1 agonists in the perioperative period, supported by a full risk assessment, stratification and a shared decision-making (SDM) approach.12 Patients should also receive education on perioperative techniques which may help reduce the risk of pulmonary aspiration.12 More research is required to understand the potential for GLP-1 agonists and similar drugs to improve patient outcomes; however, it is likely to be restricted to the non-cancer/benign groups where more time is allowed for weight loss.

Optimising diabetic control

CPOC recommends that patients with suboptimal diabetes management (Haemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) greater than 69 mmol/mol (8.5%)) should be referred to a diabetes specialist team for optimising the patient’s diabetes control preoperatively.13 It may be appropriate to delay truly elective surgery until the HbA1c is below this level.13 Patients with diabetes should be referred to the preoperative assessment clinic as early as possible. HbA1c test should be offered to patients with diabetes who have not had their HbA1c tested in the last 3 months.13 The healthcare team should provide patients with advice on lifestyle modifications, including advice on exercise, weight management, nutrition, alcohol reduction and smoking cessation.13 The introduction of the new ‘Diabetes Passports’ may aid in the preoperative and perioperative management of diabetic patients, allowing diabetes medications to be managed and adjusted safely.

While routine HbA1c testing for patients without diabetes is not recommended by the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE), studies have shown that a significant proportion of non-diabetic surgical patients with the presence of comorbidities such as obesity and hypertension have undiagnosed diabetes. Selective targeted HbA1c may be appropriate for at-risk individuals where there is a high risk of undiagnosed diabetes.14

Optimising blood pressure control

The Association of Anaesthetists of Great Britain and Ireland (AAGBI) guideline states that patients referred for surgery by general practitioners should have documented blood pressure readings showing a systolic blood pressure of less than 160 mm Hg and a diastolic blood pressure of less than 110 mm Hg in the past 12 months.15 It is recommended for patients with hypertension to be deferred from elective surgery until their blood pressure is adequately controlled with readings of less than 160 mm Hg systolic and 110 mm Hg diastolic.15 Blood pressure readings exceeding 180 mm Hg systolic or 110 mm Hg diastolic are not suitable for elective surgery, and these patients should be referred back to their GP for optimisation of their blood pressure control, with their surgery postponed.15

Laboratory investigations

Laboratory investigations are commonly performed before surgical procedures to assess pre-existing health conditions, identify abnormalities and obtain a baseline for later reference.1 2 Despite being a standard part of routine preoperative assessment, the evidence to support the usefulness of routine preoperative blood tests is weak, particularly in the absence of specific clinical indications.16 Based on the AAGBI guideline, NICE recommends patients’ full blood count to be checked if patients are older than 60 years old and surgical severity ≥grade 2; for all adult patients if surgical severity ≥grade 3; or if they have severe renal disease.17 Urea, electrolytes and creatinine should be checked in patients who are older than 60 years old and surgical severity ≥grade 3; in all adult patients with surgical severity ≥grade 4; patients with renal disease and patients with severe cardiovascular disease.17 In the context of colorectal surgery, surgical severity encompasses grade 1 procedures such as diagnostic laparoscopy to grade 4 procedures that include colonic resection.17

Haemoglobin levels

Patients undergoing colorectal surgery, particularly CRC and IBD, frequently present with anaemia. Iron-deficiency anaemia (IDA) is by far the most common cause of anaemia in these patients, due to reduced intake, impaired absorption and chronic gastrointestinal (GI) blood loss.18 Anaemia of chronic disease is a form of anaemia related to and caused by chronic disease.19 Research has demonstrated that iron metabolism is affected by inflammation which leads to increased production of hepcidin in the liver, causing reduced iron absorption in the duodenum.19 Serum ferritin concentration <30 µg/L is the most sensitive and specific laboratory test used to identify iron deficiency.18 The AAGBI guideline recommends that anaemia should ideally be treated before surgery with oral or intravenous iron transfusion instead of blood transfusion. A systematic review and meta-analysis in 2016 showed that preoperative anaemia is associated with decreased overall survival and disease-free survival in patients with rectal cancer but not in patients with colon cancer.20 Studies have shown that anaemia is associated with negative postoperative outcomes and prolonged length of stay (LOS) after elective colorectal surgery.21 22 Receiving blood transfusions perioperatively has been associated with a poorer prognosis.20 Receiving preoperative iron therapy for the treatment of IDA significantly increases the patient’s haemoglobin (Hb) concentration before abdominal surgery.2325 Intravenous iron therapy is more effective than oral iron therapy in managing preoperative anaemia and replenishes iron stores faster in patients undergoing colorectal cancer surgery.26 However, the PREVENTT trial has shown that receiving intravenous iron 10–42 days before elective major abdominal surgery in patients with anaemia does not reduce the rate of blood transfusion or death in the perioperative period compared with patients who received placebo.27 It is important to note that this trial comprised patients undergoing elective major abdominal surgery and was not restricted solely to colorectal surgeries. Despite its widespread use in national guidelines, the effectiveness of intravenous iron therapy before colorectal surgery on patient-centred outcomes remains a topic of ongoing debate.

The Patient Blood Management (PBM) is an evidence-based approach to avoid blood transfusion safely.28 The three pillars of care used in PBM include detecting and managing anaemia, minimising blood loss and optimising the patient’s physiological tolerance of anaemia.28 These include oral or parenteral iron therapy, erythropoietin (EPO) therapy and correcting nutritional deficiencies.28

In some cases, preoperative blood transfusions remain appropriate.28 NICE guidance recommends transfusing patients with Hb levels below 70 g/L or below 80 g/L in patients with acute coronary syndrome and a single-unit blood transfusion in patients who do not have active bleeding.29

Jehovah’s witnesses and patients who refuse blood products

The Association of Anaesthetists guideline states that it is mandatory to hold a preoperative multidisciplinary meeting (MDT) for Jehovah’s Witnesses and patients who refuse blood products to allow the team to plan.30 Patient’s Hb levels should be optimised before surgery, and surgery should be delayed where possible until this has been achieved. Patients should also be screened for any bleeding or clotting disorders, particularly if there is a history of bleeding after procedures.30 If their Hb levels are less than 130 g/L in adult patients, treatment such as iron or EPO should be considered to optimise their Hb levels.30 The combination of recombinant EPO and parenteral iron therapy may be administered if patients’ Hb is less than 130 g/L and ferritin levels are more than 100 µg/L.30

Serum albumin

Serum albumin of <35 g/L is classified as hypoalbuminaemia. Hypoalbuminaemia is associated with an increased risk of postoperative morbidity, mortality, complications and lower 5-year overall survival rates and 5-year relapse-free survival rates in patients who underwent colon cancer surgery.3133 Studies have shown that even a slight decrease in albumin will impact the outcomes of colorectal surgery patients.32 Weight loss, congestive heart failure and ascites have been shown to have the strongest association with modest hypoalbuminaemia.32 There are conflicting results on studies done to assess the implication of hypoalbuminaemia as a risk factor for anastomotic leak in colorectal surgery. Several studies have found that hypoalbuminaemia is associated with an increased risk of anastomotic complications.31 34 35 However, in a study of 361 patients who underwent elective colorectal surgery as part of an ERAS programme, no association was found between preoperative serum albumin and protein levels and anastomotic leak.36 Another study of 200 patients who had undergone laparoscopic surgery for colorectal cancer also found no association between preoperative albumin levels and anastomotic leak.37 Based on the guidance of the Association of Coloproctology of Great Britain and Ireland, patients with hypoalbuminaemia (<32 g/dL) should avoid primary anastomosis where possible.38

Hypoalbuminaemia has also been shown to be an independent risk factor for surgical site infection (SSI) and is associated with deeper SSIs and increased LOS after GI surgery.33

Perioperative human albumin solution use in hypoalbuminaemic patients has been postulated, but without high-quality evidence of benefit, it is not presently recommended.39 40

Coagulation tests

NICE guidelines state that coagulation tests in patients with American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) grade 1 and 2 should not be routinely tested.41 Coagulation tests should be considered in patients with ASA grade 3 or 4 or those with chronic liver disease undergoing intermediate or major surgery.41 Patients taking anticoagulants who require modification of their treatment regimen should have an individualised plan made in line with local guidance.41 Point-of-care testing should be used in patients who require their coagulation to be checked.41

Troponin level

Preoperative troponin levels are not routinely tested before colorectal surgery. The European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guideline recommends measuring high-sensitivity cardiac troponin T or I (hs-cTn T/I) preoperatively in patients with existing cardiovascular disease, cardiovascular risk factors or symptoms indicating potential cardiovascular disease to improve the accuracy of preoperative risk assessment, allowing patients and clinicians to make well-informed decisions regarding the risk-benefit balance of the planned surgery.42 Recent studies suggest that elevated troponin levels are associated with adverse outcomes. Studies have shown that colorectal cancer patients undergoing colorectal tumour resection surgery with elevated troponin levels preoperatively had an increased risk for major adverse cardiovascular events and mortality.43 44 In patients with mildly elevated troponin levels, a delay in colorectal surgery may reduce the elevated risk of postoperative mortality; however, further prospective studies are needed to determine this.45

The relatively new concept of perioperative myocardial injury/infarct (PMI) is often silent or asymptomatic, causing it to be missed in clinical practice. The ESC states that PMI can be identified by measuring hs-cTn T/I before and after non-cardiac surgery at 24 and 48 hours.46 Early identification of PMI is important, due to its risk of higher mortality46 and may allow earlier interventions.42

NT-proBNP

The ventricles of the heart release the N-terminal pro B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) prohormone in response to pressure or volume changes.47 A cohort study involving 10 402 patients found that incorporating NT-proBNP into the Revised Cardiac Risk Index improved preoperative risk stratification in approximately 1 in 4 patients.48 NT-proBNP can serve as a screening tool for patients with symptoms suggestive of heart failure and help determine which patients may need a preoperative echocardiogram (ECHO).

In the context of small intestinal and colorectal neuroendocrine tumours that can fall into the practice of colorectal surgeons, NT-proBNP can be used as a valuable screening biomarker for carcinoid heart disease.49

NT pro-BNP is not currently routinely measured but may develop a wider utility among those undergoing major surgery, assisting in the stratification of those requiring more comprehensive evaluation of cardiovascular status.

Cardiopulmonary exercise testing

Cardiopulmonary exercise testing (CPET) is a test that observes changes in a patient’s physiology during a period of exercise-induced stress by measuring physiological parameters such as oxygen uptake (VO2), carbon dioxide production (VCO2) and heart rate.51 CPET is the gold standard method to screen for reduced functional capacity and physical fitness. CPET enables clinicians to assess a patient’s physiological response to stress, mimicking how the patient may respond to the systemic inflammatory response that happens in surgery. Poor CPET performance is correlated with increased LOS, postoperative complications, mortality and morbidity.5255 CPET is not routinely performed prior to colorectal surgery in the UK owing to limited evidence demonstrating an impact on clinical outcomes and restricted availability of the resource.56 Its use has been explored in selected populations, including in patients aged over 80 years old undergoing colorectal surgery57 and in individuals with impaired cardiovascular or pulmonary reserve, in accordance with CPOC guidelines.58

Pulmonary function tests

Preoperative pulmonary function tests are not routinely performed before colorectal surgery and have not been shown to be predictive of the risk of pulmonary complications postoperatively.59 60

Nutritional assessment

While colorectal cancer is not a traditional cachexia-inducing cancer, it is not uncommon for patients undergoing colorectal surgical procedures to be malnourished. A nutritional evaluation begins with a thorough history and physical examination. Common symptoms that patients may present with include weight loss, loss of appetite, abdominal pain, bloating or distension.2 Examination of the patient may reveal a thin, malnourished patient with significant muscle wasting. According to the 2018 ERAS guidelines, subjective global assessment (SGA), patient-generated SGA and malnutrition universal screening tool are considered the reference standard for nutritional screening (table 2).18

Nutrition in patients with IBD

Specific dietary interventions are used to treat inflammation (induce remission and maintain remission) in IBD, particularly in children. Such diets are restrictive and often difficult for patients to tolerate for more than 2 weeks. These include exclusive enteral nutrition (EEN) and partial enteral nutrition that replace all or part of the diet with oral nutritional supplements with polymeric (whole protein) or semi-elemental (oligopeptide) or elemental (amino acid) formulations. Specific complications of Crohn’s disease such as strictures may require specialised preoperative diets, particularly low fibre and/or residue diets and potentially EEN. Many preoperative dietetic strategies in patients with IBD with malnutrition are the same for other populations. EEN 2–4 weeks preoperatively has been postulated to reduce the inflammatory burden of CD and may reduce the extent of surgery required,61 62 and is the subject of the planned OCEAN trial (NIHR 133657). In certain IBD cases with severe malnutrition, there may benefit from a period parenteral nutrition (PN) preoperatively.63 Whenever possible, patients should be given enteral nutrition instead of PN because of its lower cost, more physiological nature and decreased risk of sepsis.64

Cognitive assessment

Patients who are noted to have cognitive concerns or with abnormalities on cognitive screening should undergo formal cognitive assessment.58 65 Delirium is an acute state of confusion that is fluctuating in nature. Predisposing factors of delirium, such as age and dementia and precipitating factors of delirium such as pain and infection should be used to assess patient’s risk of delirium.65 Interventions such as optimisation of vascular risk factors and modification of delirium risk should be considered.65

ERAS protocols in older patients reduce the frequency of delirium and functional decline and improve clinical outcomes. Measures such as orientation aids, avoidance of inappropriate medications for older patients, adequate analgesia, early mobilisation, short fasting times and a perioperative nutrition plan can reduce the frequency of delirium in older patients.66

Mental health assessment

CPOC recommends that patients who are considered for surgical intervention undergo screening for psychological distress using assessment tools such as Patient Health Questionnaire 9, Generalised Anxiety Disorder 7, Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale and Geriatric Depression Scale.58 65 Patients who are identified at risk should be offered a referral, and where such services are unavailable, patients should be referred to their general practitioner for assessment and signposting to relevant community services.58 65

Frailty assessment

The elderly population in the UK has increased to 19% in 2022 and is expected to grow to 27% of the population by 2027.67 There has been a rise in the number of older people undergoing surgery (POPS). The older population has a higher perioperative risk.66 Perioperative care for POPS is a service that provides multidisciplinary care for older patients led by a geriatrician. POPS has been shown to improve perioperative outcomes in older patients.68

Frailty is defined as a multisystem reduction in physiological reserve that leads to a state of increased vulnerability. It is associated with an increased risk of postoperative complications, morbidity and mortality.6971 It is now recognised that perioperative complications are common in patients with frailty, and their risk can be mitigated with multidisciplinary interventions if frailty is identified early.

Preoperatively, primary care teams should provide advice on lifestyle modifications with proven benefit and optimise patients’ comorbidities and medications.65 SDM is a collaborative process in which patients and physicians work together to reach a decision about their care based on the patient’s values, concerns and preferences, should be initiated at this point.58 65 It involves sharing information and discussing the risks, benefits, alternatives and possible consequences of different treatment options.58 65

Frailty status should be documented for all patients aged over 65 years or at risk of frailty at the time of referral, preoperative assessment or on admission.65 Patients with frailty should undergo comprehensive geriatric assessment, which is the gold standard for assessing and optimising frailty, and cognitive assessment before surgery, with a perioperative frailty team providing clinical care throughout the pathway.65 Multiple scoring systems are used to assess patients’ frailty (table 1).

Scoring systems for risk assessment

Risk scores are often calculated in the preoperative setting to help stratify patient risk and assist in shared decision making among healthcare providers, patients and family members. Many scoring systems provide an estimation of mortality but are increasingly used to provide an estimation of morbidity. This may include the risk of complications but may also help provide an estimation of prolonged hospital stay or requiring a higher level of care on discharge. There have been key developments in artificial intelligence (AI)-driven preoperative assessment tools. Recent studies have shown that machine learning models such as least absolute shrinkage and selection operator logistic regression and the super learner model can predict major complications and anastomotic leakage based solely on preoperative variables, achieving moderate to high accuracy.72 Such scores may help to stratify patients’ postoperative care to be directed to a level 1 (intensive care unit), level 2 (high dependency unit) or ward-based environment. It is important to keep in mind that risk scoring systems are based on different patient populations, and each has their limitations when it comes to applying the results beyond their original dataset. Methodologies in preoperative assessment

Multidisciplinary approaches

Patients with advanced cancer are discussed in MDT with input from other specialties such as pathology, oncology and cancer nurse specialists.

Prehabilitation strategies

Preoperative assessment identifies patients at high risk of postoperative morbidity and mortality. Modifiable factors can be addressed through prehabilitation, which will generally be a 4-week programme to allow changes to be seen.73 The PrepareABC trial is an ongoing trial to assess the effects of preoperative and postoperative exercise on short and long-term surgical outcomes postoperative recovery in patients with colorectal cancer undergoing surgical resection.74

Smoking cessation

Smoking has been shown to increase the risk of complications after colorectal surgery, such as anastomotic leaks.75 76 Smoking cessation as part of the prehabilitation programme can help to reduce these risks, improve surgical outcomes and is cost-effective.77 The preoperative period is a good opportunity to encourage smoking cessation in patients, as surgical patients have a higher rate of smoking cessation than the general population.76 A combination of interventions such as nicotine replacement therapy, pharmacotherapy and counselling is effective in achieving smoking cessation.76 Varenicline has been shown to increase rates of smoking cessation when used for 24 weeks.78 Studies have shown that patients using varenicline have the highest chance of quitting smoking successfully compared with other pharmacotherapies.79 Additional treatment with varenicline has been shown to maintain higher rates of continuous abstinence from smoking.80

Preoperative patient education

Patients who are undergoing surgery should be encouraged to attend surgery school before their surgery. Surgery school educates patients on their course of admission, preparation for surgery and equips patients with skills to support behaviour change. Content covered in surgery school includes prehabilitation strategies such as exercise, nutrition, mental health, reducing alcohol intake and smoking cessation and principles of ERAS, such as early mobilisation and intake of fluids and food. Surgery school can be delivered in person, online via a live conference or through recordings.

ERAS is a standardised multimodal programme used in colorectal surgery that has been shown to improve surgical outcomes and reduce LOS and postoperative complications.8183 Patients who have received preoperative education showed better adherence to ERAS protocols.81 As part of the ERAS protocol, patients are told to consume carbohydrate-rich meals 2 days before surgery and to consume a preoperative nutritional supplemental drink up to 2 hours before induction of anaesthesia, as consumption of preoperative carbohydrate drinks has been shown to reduce LOS in patients undergoing colorectal surgery.84 85 Early mobilisation is a key component of the ERAS protocol after colorectal surgery. Early ambulation within 24 hours postoperatively is associated with a reduced rate of postoperative complications and improved outcomes.86

Infection control: mechanical bowel preparation and oral antibiotics before colorectal surgery

The use of mechanical bowel preparation and oral antibiotics (MOAB) before colorectal surgery is a widely debated topic. The administration of mechanical bowel prep can be unpleasant for many patients and may have side effect profiles such as fluid and electrolyte shifts.87 In the past, the efficacy of mechanical bowel prep with and without oral antibiotics has been debated; however, there is now mounting evidence to suggest its efficacy in reducing SSIs and anastomotic leak.8789 There are concerns regarding the effect of oral antibiotics on gut microbiome and the impact on patients’ health; however, despite concerns, most studies suggest that MOAB does not increase the rates of Clostridium difficile-related infections.87

Conclusions

Comprehensive preoperative assessment requires communication between surgeons and physicians from other specialties and is crucial in optimising patient outcomes. An ageing surgical population and increasing comorbidities have increased the importance of perioperative assessment.90 This review encompasses the wide range of perioperative assessments now performed, including physiological, biochemical, nutritional and frailty. When combined, these assessments may synergise to provide a holistic view of the patient, allowing targeting of specific areas for optimisation or prehabilitation91 and to help communicate risk more effectively. Scoring systems enable clinicians to identify patients at high risk of developing adverse surgical outcomes and allow clinicians to intervene by optimising the patient’s fitness and comorbidities preoperatively or by monitoring patients closely postoperatively and intervening at the first sign of complications developing. However, it is essential to remember that scoring systems are indicative rather than patient-specific and should be used in conjunction with expert consultation to support SDM, as defined by CPOC as a collaborative process in which clinicians and patients work together to make evidence-based decisions informed by the patient’s values and preferences. At present, National Surgical Quality Improvement Program, Physiological and Operative Severity Score for the enUmeration of Mortality and morbidity and ASA are often the most frequently used and are well-validated scoring systems which help to adequately communicate risk to patients. Ultimately, the choice of which combination of assessment tools to use by individual MDTs is based on personal preference, applicability to local populations and resources available. With the growing complexity of surgical patients driven by increasing life expectancy and a higher prevalence of comorbidities in the ageing population, there is a need for more precise tools for risk stratification beyond conventional models. AI is bound to play a transformative role in this space. By integrating big data for machine learning, AI may be able to create algorithms that are more precise and individualised compared with conventional models.92 The superior performance of these models suggests that these are promising tools in aiding clinical decision-making.93 The development and increasing use of AI mark an exciting shift in clinical practice and represent an opportunity for the delivery of safer, more personalised and efficient patient care.
