Determinants of nurses’ knowledge on maggot debridement therapy: influence of demographic, professional, and educational factors in a Malaysian teaching hospital
Authors: Soon Lean Keng, Wong Mong Khim, Azlina Yusuf
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMaggot debridement therapy (MDT) is a biotherapeutic approach that uses sterile larvae to remove necrotic tissue and promote wound healing. Although its effectiveness in managing chronic wounds is well established, clinical adoption remains limited. Nurses are central to wound care, yet little is known about their MDT knowledge in Malaysia. This study assesses nurses’ knowledge of MDT and examined the influence of demographic, professional, and educational factors within a tertiary teaching hospital setting.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among 165 nurses selected through random sampling. Data were collected using a validated self-administered questionnaire assessing sociodemographic data, professional experience, and MDT knowledge. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize data, and Pearson chi square and Fisher’s Exact test were applied to determine associations between knowledge levels and key variables.ResultsThe mean age of participants was 32.72 years. Of these, 41.8% had 6–10 years of experience, 89.7% held a nursing diploma, and 87.9% had heard of MDT. Only 26.1% demonstrated high knowledge, while 52.7% showed low knowledge. A significant association was found between knowledge level and having heard of MDT (p = 0.040) and having performed MDT (p = 0.025).ConclusionNurses demonstrated limited knowledge and practical experience in Maggot Debridement Therapy despite general awareness of its clinical value. Knowledge was significantly associated with prior exposure and hands-on experience, underscoring the importance of experiential learning. Incorporating MDT into nursing curricula, continuous professional development, and institutional protocols is crucial to enhance evidence-based wound care and clinical adoption.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12906-026-05435-x.

Introduction

Maggot Debridement Therapy (MDT), the clinical application of sterile fly larvae to remove necrotic tissue, reduce wound bioburden, and promote granulation, is an established option for chronic, hard-to-heal wounds [12, 13, 15, 21]. In Malaysia, MDT has been implemented since the 2000s using Lucilia cuprina (rather than the temperate L. sericata used in many Western centres), with local work on larval sterilization and production supporting clinical use [9, 10, 17, 19]. MDT has demonstrated benefits for diabetic foot ulcers (DFU), including faster debridement and improved healing metrics in meta-analyses and cohort studies, and has been associated with reduced amputation rates and shorter hospital stays in several series [6, 13, 14, 18, 20, 21].

Despite clinical evidence and an expanding Malaysian service network, uptake is constrained by system-level and individual barriers — notably cost/availability, supply chain and regulatory issues, and importantly, healthcare staff knowledge, stigma and reluctance to use live larvae [1, 7, 13, 15]. Existing literature from Malaysia and the region has largely described attitudes, acceptance, and service development (Marimuthu & Wan Mohd Azizi, [9, 14, 19]). In contrast, rigorous assessments of nurses’ actual knowledge and the educational, professional, and demographic factors that influence it are scarce [1, 5]. Given that wound care in Malaysia is predominantly nurse-led and that MDT is provided across multiple hospitals, clarifying nurses’ knowledge and its determinants is essential to safe, effective implementation and scale-up. Therefore, this study assessed MDT knowledge among nurses in a tertiary teaching hospital in Malaysia and examined associated educational and professional factors to identify targets for training and policy interventions.

Methodology

Study design and sampling

A cross-sectional study was conducted among nurses in a tertiary teaching hospital in northeast Peninsular Malaysia from February to March 2023 using a validated survey questionnaire. The hospital was selected because it serves as a referral teaching institution. The required sample size was calculated using G*Power analysis with a medium effect size and a statistical power of 0.80, yielding a minimum sample of 150 participants. To account for an anticipated 10% non-response rate, the final target sample size was increased to 165 nurses.

A sampling frame was prepared using the hospital’s nursing staff registry, which contained a list of all nurses working in the medical and surgical wards who met the eligibility criteria. Eligible participants were registered nurses with at least one year of clinical experience in either medical or surgical wards. Each eligible nurse was assigned a unique identification number, and simple random sampling was performed using a computer-generated random number sequence. Nurses were selected without replacement until the required sample size was achieved. Because all eligible nurses were included in a single sampling frame and had an equal chance of being selected, equal selection probability was maintained throughout the sampling process. If a selected nurse declined participation or was unavailable during the data collection period, another nurse was randomly selected from the remaining eligible pool using the same procedure.

Development and validation of the questionnaire

We developed a survey questionnaire (supplementary material) based on the Ministry of Health Malaysia’s Maggot Debridement Therapy guidelines (Ministry of Health Malaysia, [11]) and relevant literature [14, 16]. The questionnaire was developed in English and has not been previously published elsewhere. The questionnaire consisted of two sections. The first section included items on the demographic characteristics of the participants (age, gender, nursing education level, years of experience, work area, and prior exposure to MDT, such as whether they had heard of MDT or performed it on a patient). The second section comprised 20 statements designed to assess MDT knowledge using a three-point Likert scale (Yes, No, Not Sure). Each correct answer received one point, while incorrect or unsure responses received 0 points. Knowledge levels were evaluated using Bloom’s Taxonomy cut-off points, categorizing them as high (80% and above), moderate (60–79%), and low (59% and below) [2, 22]. A pilot test was subsequently conducted on 17 nurses. The questionnaire’s internal consistency was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha, yielding a high reliability of 0.90, which indicated excellent internal consistency [8]. Content validity was established by inviting three independent subject experts, and a few modifications were made following their feedback.

Ethical considerations

Ethical clearance was sought from the Universiti Sains Malaysia Human Research Ethics Committee (Reference Number: USM/JEPeM/22120807). Participation was voluntary, and implied consent was obtained from all participants. To protect their privacy and maintain confidentiality, in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, participants’ names and identifiable information were not displayed.

Statistical analysis

All data were entered into IBM SPSS Statistics (version 27.0). Pearson Chi Square test or Fisher’s Exact test was used to investigate the association between these factors and MDT knowledge, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.

Demographic characteristics of nurses

Table 1 summarizes the characteristics of the 165 study participants. The mean age was 32.72 years (SD = 5.19). A significant portion of nurses had considerable experience, with 41.8% having 6–10 years and 31.5% having over 11 years. The vast majority held a diploma in nursing (89.7%), and 87.9% had heard of MDT. However, actual MDT experience was low, with only 9.7% having performed the therapy.

Distribution of item responses and overall knowledge level of maggot debridement therapy (MDT) among nurses

Table 2 highlights the distribution of nurses’ knowledge about MDT, with correct response rates ranging from 32.7% to 79.4%. While most nurses are aware of MDT’s benefits, a significant portion (64.3%) remains unsure about its physiology and the use of Lucilia cuprina for chronic wound debridement. Notably, the highest correct response rates were observed for questions related to MDT’s benefits (79.4%), suitable wounds (71.5%), physiology (61.2%), and side effects (58.8%). Conversely, low accuracy rates were found in question “Maggots are suitable for pressure ulcers” (34.0%) and “Maggots secrete digestive enzymes” (28.0%). Most nurses (52.7%) possess low knowledge of MDT, 21.2% have moderate knowledge, and 26.1% demonstrate high knowledge.

Discussion

This study revealed that only one-quarter of nurses demonstrated a high level of knowledge regarding Maggot Debridement Therapy (MDT), while more than half exhibited low knowledge levels. Although most participants had heard of MDT, substantial gaps remained in their understanding of the therapy’s indications, mechanisms of action, and clinical application. Importantly, knowledge level was significantly associated with prior awareness of MDT and previous experience performing MDT. These findings suggest that both educational exposure and practical clinical engagement contribute to the development of nurses’ competence in evidence-based wound care. Nurses who have been exposed to MDT-related information or clinical practice may be more likely to acquire, retain, and apply relevant knowledge, thereby enhancing their confidence and readiness to utilize MDT appropriately in wound management.

The significant association between prior awareness of MDT and higher knowledge levels highlights the important role of educational and professional exposure in knowledge acquisition. Awareness of MDT may arise through multiple pathways, including undergraduate nursing education, continuing professional development programmes, wound care workshops, hospital in-service training, professional conferences, multidisciplinary team discussions, and direct observation of MDT cases during clinical practice. Exposure through these channels may increase familiarity with the indications, mechanisms, benefits, and safety considerations of MDT, thereby facilitating knowledge development. Furthermore, repeated encounters with MDT-related information across educational and clinical settings may strengthen knowledge retention and improve acceptance of this evidence-based wound care intervention. Similar findings have been reported in studies demonstrating that exposure to wound care education and professional training is associated with improved knowledge and readiness to adopt MDT and other advanced wound management practices [1, 7, 15].

The predominance of low to moderate knowledge levels in this study mirrors findings from El-Tawdy et al., [3], who reported that nurses’ MDT competence depends largely on consistent access to updated clinical information and structured education. Similarly, Fairey and Holloway , [4] found widespread misconceptions among nurses regarding larval species and adverse effects of MDT, underscoring persistent informational and perceptual gaps across international settings. These parallels indicate that the limited MDT knowledge observed among Malaysian nurses is not an isolated issue but part of a broader global challenge in biotherapeutic wound management literacy.

Within the Malaysian context, these gaps may be amplified by limited institutional exposure, uneven access to continuing education, and reliance on conventional wound-care methods. The absence of structured MDT modules in pre-service curricula and the scarcity of hospital-based training further constrain opportunities for skill acquisition. Comparable barriers have been documented in other regions, where MDT implementation has been hindered by insufficient policy support, restricted access to sterile larvae, and inadequate integration of biotherapy within wound-care protocols [7, 13].

The significant relationship between previous MDT practice and higher knowledge levels further supports the value of experiential learning in clinical education. Nurses who have participated in MDT procedures gain firsthand exposure to wound assessment, patient selection, application techniques, monitoring processes, and management of potential complications. According to experiential learning theory, active participation in clinical practice enhances understanding through the integration of knowledge, reflection, and practical application. Consequently, nurses who have directly observed or performed MDT may develop a deeper understanding of the therapy than those who rely solely on theoretical instruction. Such practical experience may also improve confidence, clinical decision-making, and willingness to incorporate MDT into wound care management when clinically indicated. Similar observations have been reported in wound care education literature, where hands-on training and clinical exposure were associated with improved competency and confidence among healthcare professionals [5, 7]. Given nurses’ central role in caring for patients with chronic and complex wounds, experiential learning opportunities are essential for strengthening competency and promoting evidence-based wound care practices.

Addressing these deficiencies requires a comprehensive, multi-level approach. At the educational level, integrating MDT content into undergraduate nursing curricula and continuing professional development (CPD) programmes is essential. In addition to theoretical instruction, specialized wound care training programmes incorporating MDT-focused content should be developed to strengthen both knowledge and practical competency. Clinical workshops, simulation-based learning, case-based discussions, and supervised MDT practice sessions may provide valuable experiential learning opportunities that bridge the gap between theory and practice. Participation in multidisciplinary wound care teams may further enhance learning through collaboration with wound care specialists and other healthcare professionals. At the institutional level, establishing standardized MDT protocols, ensuring access to evidence-based guidelines, and supporting regular wound care training initiatives would facilitate knowledge translation into clinical practice. Such strategies may enhance nurses’ confidence and competency while promoting wider adoption of MDT as a safe and effective wound management modality [7, 13].

The slow adoption of MDT in Malaysia, similar to trends reported internationally, also reflects perceptual and cultural barriers. Feelings of disgust or stigma toward maggots persist among healthcare providers and patients alike, as noted in studies by Hopkins et al., [7] and Fairey and Holloway and [4]. Overcoming such barriers requires systematic educational strategies that normalize MDT as an evidence-based biotherapeutic intervention rather than an unconventional or “last-resort” method.

This study underscores the urgent need for structured, experiential learning opportunities to enhance nurses’ MDT competence. Hospital administrators should incorporate MDT-related competencies into performance appraisals and accreditation frameworks, while policymakers should integrate MDT into national wound management guidelines. Such efforts would facilitate consistency in practice, ensure equitable training, and enhance patient outcomes through evidence-based wound-care innovations.

Strengths and limitations

This study provides valuable insights into nurses’ knowledge of Maggot Debridement Therapy (MDT) within a Malaysian tertiary healthcare context—an area that remains underexplored regionally and globally. The use of a validated questionnaire and random sampling enhances the reliability, internal validity, and representativeness of the findings. Moreover, the identification of significant associations between knowledge level, prior awareness, and hands-on experience offers a strong empirical foundation for targeted educational and workforce development strategies.

Nevertheless, several limitations must be acknowledged. The cross-sectional design restricts causal interpretation, as observed associations cannot establish temporal relationships. The study’s single-centre setting may limit the generalizability of findings to other healthcare contexts, particularly in rural or non-teaching hospitals. Self-reported data also introduce potential response and recall bias, which may overestimate knowledge levels. Furthermore, the absence of qualitative inquiry constrains deeper understanding of the attitudinal and contextual factors that shape nurses’ perceptions and clinical decision-making regarding MDT. Future mixed-method and multi-centre studies are recommended to provide richer, more nuanced insights into the educational, organizational, and behavioural dimensions influencing MDT adoption. Another limitation is that multivariable regression analysis was not performed to assess the independent contribution of demographic, professional, and educational factors to MDT knowledge. Although bivariate analyses identified significant associations with prior awareness and practical experience of MDT, potential confounding between variables could not be fully evaluated. In addition, several subgroups contained relatively small numbers of participants, particularly nurses who had performed MDT and those with degree-level qualifications, which may have limited the stability and reliability of multivariable modelling. Future studies involving larger and more diverse samples should employ multivariable analytical approaches to determine the independent predictors of MDT knowledge among nurses.

Conclusion

This study highlights substantial knowledge gaps and limited clinical exposure to Maggot Debridement Therapy among Malaysian nurses. Despite widespread awareness of MDT, most participants demonstrated only low to moderate understanding, underscoring the need for structured educational interventions. The significant associations between knowledge, prior exposure, and direct experience indicate that practical engagement plays a critical role in building competence and confidence in this biotherapeutic wound-care technique.

Integrating MDT education into undergraduate nursing curricula and continuous professional development programmes is essential to strengthen both theoretical understanding and clinical application. Particular emphasis should be placed on specialized wound care and MDT-related training programmes that incorporate experiential learning opportunities, as practical exposure appears to be an important contributor to knowledge development and clinical readiness. Institutional and policy-level support—through the development of national guidelines, standardized protocols, and sustainable supply systems for sterile larvae—will further enhance practice consistency and safety. Future research should expand beyond quantitative assessment to include qualitative exploration of nurses’ attitudes, perceptions, and behavioural determinants influencing MDT adoption. Evaluating the effectiveness of tailored educational interventions could also inform the design of scalable, evidence-based training frameworks.

Collectively, these efforts can bridge existing knowledge gaps, normalize MDT within standard wound-care practice, and advance Malaysia’s alignment with global standards in evidence-based, cost-effective wound management.
