Some Classical Invariants, from Harmonic Quadruples to Triangle Groups
Giorgio Ottaviani

Abstract
These notes are an expanded version of the lectures held in Tromsø, in May 2025 at the Lie-Størmer Summer School : Invariant Theory from classics to modern developments , in the framework of TiME events. We emphasize the analogy between binary quartics and ternary cubics (and subsequently modular forms) based on their harmonic and equianharmonic invariants. Triangle groups are presented in both the elliptic and the hyperbolic setting with their associated tilings. The topics include the discussion of a short Hilbert paper on polynomials which are powers, that was proposed to the participants. The appendix contains some exercises, with sketches of solutions, and a section devoted to Pfaffians edited by Vincenzo Galgano.

Introduction: the harmonic quadruples and the cross-ratio

One of the first interesting configurations in invariant theory concerns the harmonic quadruples. Their use goes back to Pythagorean music scale, and the name
harmonic
was suggested by Archytas of Taranto
[
Sev
, §1, 15]
, three centuries BC. In the diatonic scale, three chords of length
1
,
\frac{4}{5}
.
\frac{2}{3}
make the fundamental chord C-E-G (do-mi-sol).

Figure 1:
chord C-E-G from an harmonic
4
-tuple.

The second length is the harmonic mean of the other two.

Figure 2:
AD is the harmonic mean between AB and AC. Recall that the sound frequencies are reciprocal to the lengths.

The algebraic relation is

\frac{2}{AD}=\frac{1}{AB}+\frac{1}{AC}.

It gives
AB\cdot AC-AD\cdot AC=AB\cdot AD-AB\cdot AC
,
which is easily recognizable as equivalent to

\frac{AC}{DC}=-\frac{AB}{DB}.

(1.1)

Equation (
1.1
) is the relation obtained by Apollonius in Prop. 37 of the third book of his famous treatise
Conics
.

In figure
3
consider

,
B
,
C
as given and
D
to be constructed.

Figure 3:
Apollonius construction: D is the fourth harmonic after A, B, C. The construction does not depend on the conic. The figure is from the introduction of
[

.

The lines AH and AK are tangent to the (smooth) conic, so that HK is the polar line of A. The fact that the intersection point D does not depend on the conic is quite surprising at first glance and shows the depth reached by the mathematical culture of the Hellenistic period. The conic can be chosen as a circle, so that a ruler and compass construction can be carried out by a craftsman.
Ruler and compass were indeed the main computational tools of the antiquity. See the exercises in Appendix
7.2
.
I recommend the historical account of Projective Geometry
“From Here to Infinity”

[
DCG

by Del Centina and Gimigliano for much more information.

Since the equation to find D is linear, it follows that the coordinates of
D
lie in the same field of
A
,
B
and
C
.
Square roots are not involved. Hence a construction without compass, with only the ruler, is possible.
This is the construction of the
4

Figure 4:
The harmonic property of the complete quadrilateral. Given A, B, C collinear, choose E not collinear, choose F on CE. Then G, I and D are determined successively by the picture. The point D does not depend on the choices of E, F and it is the fourth harmonic after A, B, C.

This second construction is algebraically important but, in practice, it is beated by Apollonius’ one, which requires fewer steps. One of the purposes of these TiME lectures is to keep the beauty of these constructions alive. I was encouraged by the participants of the Tromsø School to insert the two harmonic constructions in these notes.

It is worth remarking that the harmonic mean is a projective invariant, while the arithmetic mean is only an affine invariant. At the same time, it is wise to warn the reader that the fundamental chord C-E-G in music is a metric invariant. Applying an affine transformation, our ear still perceives a harmonic sound, corresponding to a musical key change, but a general projective transformation destroys the ratio between C and E. In other words, a musical piece is not projectively invariant. For example, the chord C-G-C (do-sol-do) still corresponds to a harmonic 4-tuple,
but it sounds completely different.

The harmonic quadruples of points in
{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
mark the birth of invariant theory.
Invariant theory provides a solid algebraic description of Geometry. The starting point is that geometric objects, like points, subspaces or subvarieties, are described in coordinates. Coordinate systems are not canonical but require some choices. Luckily, there is a group action on the set of these choices. The most relevant example is the group
GL(V)
, which describes exactly
the set of ordered basis of a vector space
V
. Hence this set comes equipped with a group structure, after a choice of an ordered basis has been made, to work as identity element. I learned this basic remark from Alan Huckleberry many years ago, at the beginning of my mathematical activity. We get the group action of
GL(V)
on
V
. At projective level, the group
PGL(V)
acts on
{\mathbb{P}}(V)
, but it is often preferable to replace
PGL(V)
with its universal cover
SL(V)
.
Given a group action, an invariant is a function on the geometric objects which does not change under the action.

Let’s describe the harmonic quadruples in this setting.
Given
4
ordered points on
{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
, with coordinates respectively

(a_{0},a_{1})
,
(b_{0},b_{1})
,
(c_{0},c_{1})

(d_{0},d_{1})
, denote

(ab):=\det\begin{pmatrix}a_{0}&a_{1}\\
b_{0}&b_{1}\end{pmatrix}
, and so on.
Consider the action of
SL(2)
on
{\mathbb{P}}^{1}\times{\mathbb{P}}^{1}\times{\mathbb{P}}^{1}\times{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
.
The three expressions

(ab)(cd)\qquad(ac)(bd)\qquad(ad)(bc)

are
SL(2)
-invariants, and they are linearly dependent, since their alternating sum is zero.
The invariant ring of four ordered points on
{\mathbb{P}}^{1}

is spanned by these expressions, see
[
O12
, Theorem 35 in §5.3]
for the precise statement.

Formula (
1.1
) is equivalent to

\frac{(ac)(bd)}{(ab)(cd)}=-1

or also to

\frac{(ac)(bd)}{(ad)(bc)}=\frac{1}{2}

where the left hand side is called the
cross-ratio
of the four points.
The cross-ratio of four distinct points may assume all the values
\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}
with the exception of
\lambda=0,1,\infty
, values corresponding to quadruples with a double point.

The introduction of complex numbers into the history of quadruples led to the discovery of a new special class of quadruples, in some ways even more special than the harmonic quadruples.
They are less well-known, because they never have four real points. The quadruples belonging to this new class are called equianharmonic. In terms of cross-ratio, they are determined by the following property, whose proof is a straightforward computation.

Proposition 1.1
.

Let
A=(a_{0},a_{1})
,
B=(b_{0},b_{1})
,
C=(c_{0},c_{1})

D=(d_{0},d_{1})
be four distinct points.
After permuting the
4
points, the cross-ratio
\frac{(ac)(bd)}{(ad)(bc)}=\lambda

assumes
6
distinct values

\lambda,\frac{1}{\lambda},1-\lambda,\frac{1}{1-\lambda},\frac{\lambda-1}{\lambda},\frac{\lambda}{\lambda-1}

unless

1.

\lambda\in\left\{-1,2,\frac{1}{2}\right\}
, which can be taken as definition of harmonic
4
-tuple,

2.

\lambda\in\left\{e^{i\pi/3},e^{-i\pi/3}\right\}
, which can be taken as definition of equianharmonic
4
-tuple

In closing this introduction, I feel it is important to remember that the tempered musical scale, based on
\sqrt[12]{2}
, used since the 19th century, has shifted the link between music and invariant theory from precision to approximation. The cross-ratio of the tempered C-E-G (do-mi-sol) is

\frac{(2^{-1/3}-2^{-7/12})}{2^{-1/3}(1-2^{-7/12})}=0,478\ldots

which approximates
\frac{1}{2}
at
4.3\%
, at the border of being perceptible to a trained ear.

Binary quartics

Binary quartics are homogeneous polynomials of degree
4
in two variables. They have
4
roots which can be seen as four points in
{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
, with multiplicity. Up to multiple scalars, they are determined by their roots,
if we work over
{\mathbb{C}}
, which we will do from now on.
The space of binary quartics is easily identified with the symmetrized tensor product
{\mathrm{Sym}}^{4}{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
. Up to multiple scalars, binary quartics can be thought as unordered quadruples of points on
{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
,
so as quadruples up to the action of the symmetric group
\Sigma_{4}
. A polynomial
{\mathrm{Sym}}^{4}{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
is called nonsingular if it has
4
distinct roots.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
Z=\left\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{4}\right\}
with
z_{i}\in{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
be the set of roots of a binary quartic
f=\sum_{i=0}^{4}a_{i}{4\choose i}x^{4-i}y^{i}
.

•

Z
is a harmonic
4
-tuple if and only if

J:=-a_{2}^{3}+2\,a_{1}a_{2}a_{3}-a_{0}a_{3}^{2}-a_{1}^{2}a_{4}+a_{0}a_{2}a_{4}=0
.

•

Z
is a equianharmonic
4
-tuple if and only if

I:=3\,a_{2}^{2}-4\,a_{1}a_{3}+a_{0}a_{4}=0
.

•

Z
is nonsingular if and only if
D:=I^{3}-27J^{2}\neq 0
.

.

Among the semistable quartics there is also the form

A systematic construction of invariants is possible through transvectants.

Theorem 2.6
.

Let
\dim U=2
. Let
\min(d,e)\geqslant n
. There is a unique bilinear
SL(2)
-invariant map

{\mathrm{Sym}}^{d}U\times{\mathrm{Sym}}^{e}U\to{\mathrm{Sym}}^{d+e-2n}U

such that

(u^{d},v^{e})\mapsto u^{d-n}v^{e-n}(u\wedge v)^{n}.

If
f\in S^{d}U
,
g\in S^{e}U
, its expression is

(f,g)_{n}=\frac{n!}{d!}\frac{n!}{e!}\sum_{i=0}^{n}(-1)^{i}{n\choose i}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x^{n-i}\partial y^{i}}\frac{\partial g}{\partial x^{i}\partial y^{n-i}}\ ,

which is called the
n
-th transvectant of
f
and
g
.

Proof.

The uniqueness is clear since any polynomial can be expressed as a sum of powers of linear forms (this statement
is equivalent to the fact that the linear span of the rational normal curve is the full ambient space).
To show the existence, we compute first

\displaystyle(u^{d},v^{e})_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{n!}{d!}\frac{n!}{e!}\sum_{i=0}^{n}(-1)^{i}{n\choose i}\frac{\partial u^{d}}{\partial x^{n-i}\partial y^{i}}\frac{\partial v^{e}}{\partial x^{i}\partial y^{n-i}}=

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{n}(-1)^{i}{n\choose i}u^{d-n}v^{e-n}(u_{0}v_{1})^{n-i}(u_{1}v_{0})^{i}=u^{d-n}v^{e-n}(u_{0}v_{1}-u_{1}v_{0})^{n}\ .

This computation makes easy to check the
SL(2)
-invariance, since by linearity it is sufficient to check it
when
f=u^{d}
,
g=v^{e}
. In this case, for any
h\in SL(2)
we have

(h\cdot u^{d},h\cdot v^{e})\mapsto(h\cdot u)^{d-n}(h\cdot v)^{e-n}(u\wedge v)^{n}=h\cdot(u^{d},v^{e})_{n}

The proof is complete.
∎

Remark 2.8
.

We have seen that, when projected on the sphere
S^{2}={\mathbb{P}}^{1}({\mathbb{C}})
, the four roots of a equianharmonic quartic are the four vertices of a regular tetrahedron, while the four roots of a harmonic quartic are coplanar and are the vertices of a square.
This beautiful description has to be understood with a grain of salt.
Up to
SL(2)
-action the four roots of a harmonic quartic may loose the metric configuration of the square,
but they remain coplanar. The same holds for the four roots of a equianharmonic quartic, that may loose the metric configuration of the tetrahedron.

3
Ternary cubics

Consider ternary cubics
f\in{\mathrm{Sym}}^{3}{\mathbb{C}}^{3}
. In this section we give for granted the well known correspondence between elliptic curves and quotients
{\mathbb{C}}/\Lambda

where
\Lambda
is a lattice, see for example
[
Hart
, IV §4]
. This is essentially Abel’s Theorem for genus one curves. Under the action of
SL(3)
, the behaviour of ternary cubics and binary quartics is quite similar.

The reason comes from the following construction.

Let
f\in{\mathrm{Sym}}^{3}{\mathbb{C}}^{3}
and pick
p\in V(f)\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{2}
.
The projection
\pi_{p}\colon V(f)\to{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
is
2:1
and by Riemann-Hurwitz has exactly four ramification points

z_{i}\in V(f)
and four branch points
\pi_{p}(z_{i})\in{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
.
We give for granted here the structure of complex torus on any elliptic curve,
which is Abel’s Theorem for elliptic curves. Hence, after a flex has been fixed as origin,
three points on an elliptic curve are collinear if and only if their sum is zero in the group structure.
In this setting the four ramification points of
\pi_{p}
are the four solutions
z
of the equation

2z+p=0
, clear from the lattice structure, see Figure
6
.

Figure 6:
The points
z_{1},\ldots z_{4}
are the solutions of the equation
2z+p=0
in the lattice

{\mathbb{C}}/\Lambda
, where the generators of
\Lambda
are the two independent sides of the parallelogram.

Theorem 3.1

(Salmon)
.

The
SL(2)
-class of the four (unordered) branch points does not depend on
p\in V(f)
.

Proof.

This is essentially
[
Hart
, Lemma IV 4.4]
. We provide an alternative proof.

The four branch points give a map on the invariant ring of binary quartics.
After Theorem
2.7
we get a map

V(f)\ \smash{\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits^{I^{3},J^{2}}}\ {\mathbb{P}}^{1}.

Since the four points are always distinct (see Figure
6
) , the image of the map sits in
{\mathbb{P}}^{1}\setminus(27,1)
, in other words it avoids the discriminant.
Since the image is contained in a affine variety, or in a simpler way it is a global regular function, it must be constant.
∎

The importance of Salmon’s Theorem in our story is that it gives a link from ternary cubics to
binary quartics.

Precisely, Salmon’s Theorem gives a map
from
SL(3)
-classes of ternary cubics to
SL(2)
-classes of binary cubics.

The two invariant rings are essentially the same.

Indeed the pullback of
I
is the invariant
\mathcal{A}
of degree
4
, called the Aronhold invariant.
The pullback of
J
is the invariant
\mathcal{T}
of degree
6
.

Let’s state the result precisely.

Theorem 3.2
.

The invariant ring of ternary cubics

{\mathbb{C}}[{\mathrm{Sym}}^{3}{\mathbb{C}}^{3}]^{SL(3)}
is the polynomial ring
{\mathbb{C}}[\mathcal{A},\mathcal{T}]
.

A ternary cubic
g
with
\mathcal{A}(g)=0
is called equianharmonic. In other words a cubic is equianharmonic
when the four branch points of the projection, centered at any of its points, make an equianharmonic binary quartic.
Salmon’s Theorem says that if projecting from one point we get an equianharmonic binary quartic,
then projecting from any point we get an equianharmonic binary quartic.
A basic example is
g=x^{4}+y^{4}+z^{4}
, the Fermat cubic. The Aronhold invariant can be written as a Pfaffian, see
[
O12
, §4.7]
and Appendix
7.1
.

A ternary cubic
g
with
\mathcal{T}(g)=0
is called harmonic. In other words a cubic is harmonic
when the four branch points of the projection centered at any of its points make a harmonic binary quartic.

A nice feature of these two special cases is that they correspond to the two special cases of lattices.

Proposition 3.3
.

Given a lattice
\Lambda\subset{\mathbb{C}}
,
\Lambda=\langle 1,\tau\rangle
with
\mathrm{Im}\tau>0
, there is a correspondence between
automorphisms of the elliptic curve
{\mathbb{C}}/\Lambda
which fix a point
P_{0}\in{\mathbb{C}}/\Lambda
and complex numbers
\alpha
such that
\alpha\Lambda=\Lambda
.

We have moreover

\left\{\alpha\in{\mathbb{C}}|\alpha\Lambda=\Lambda\right\}\simeq\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}{\mathbb{Z}}_{6}=\{e^{\pi ik/3}|k=0,\ldots 5\}&\textrm{\ if\ }\Lambda=\langle 1,e^{\pi i/3}\rangle\\
{\mathbb{Z}}_{4}=\{i^{k}|k=0,\ldots 3\}&\textrm{\ if\ }\Lambda=\langle 1,i\rangle\\
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}=\{\pm 1\}&\textrm{\ otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

The case
\Lambda=\langle 1,e^{\pi i/3}\rangle
corresponds to the equianharmonic curves.
The case
\Lambda=\langle 1,i\rangle
corresponds to the harmonic curves.

Proof.

The equianharmonic case is
[
Hart
, Example IV 4.20.2]
. In terms of the lattice
\Lambda
it corresponds to the vanishing of
\sum_{\omega\in\Lambda\setminus\{0\}}\frac{1}{\omega^{4}}

(see
[
Hart
, Theorem IV 4.12B]
).

The vanishing follows from the
{\mathbb{Z}}_{6}
-invariance of the lattice in the following way.
Due to absolute convergence, we may reorder the summation and sum over each
{\mathbb{Z}}_{6}
-orbit.
Let
\tau=e^{\pi i/3}
, which satisfies
\tau^{6}=1
. The six summands of the orbit of any fixed
\omega\in\Lambda\setminus\{0\}
give

\sum_{j=0}^{5}(\omega\tau^{j})^{-4}=\omega^{-4}(\tau^{0}+\tau^{-4}+\tau^{-2}+\tau^{0}+\tau^{-4}+\tau^{-2})=0
.
Hence the sum over any orbit is zero and it follows
\sum_{\omega\in\Lambda\setminus\{0\}}\frac{1}{\omega^{4}}=0
.

The harmonic case is
[
Hart
, Example IV 4.20.1]
. In terms of the lattice
\Lambda
it corresponds to the vanishing of
\sum_{\omega\in\Lambda\setminus\{0\}}\frac{1}{\omega^{6}}
.

The vanishing follows from the
{\mathbb{Z}}_{4}
-invariance of the lattice in the following way.
Due to absolute convergence, we may reorder the summation and sum over each
{\mathbb{Z}}_{4}
-orbit.
The four summands of the orbit of any fixed
\omega\in\Lambda\setminus\{0\}
give

\sum_{j=0}^{3}(\omega i^{j})^{-6}=\omega^{-6}(i^{0}+i^{-2}+i^{0}+i^{-2})=0
.
Hence the sum over any orbit is zero and it follows
\sum_{\omega\in\Lambda\setminus\{0\}}\frac{1}{\omega^{6}}=0
.

These sums are called Eisenstein series, see Theorem
6.2
.

∎

4
Finite polyhedral groups and the ADE classification

We recall that over the real numbers we have the
2:1
covering of real Lie groups
SU(2)\to SO(3,{\mathbb{R}})
.

The polyhedral groups can be found as finite subgroups of
SO(3,{\mathbb{R}})
.
Their pullback in
SU(2)
are called
binary polyhedral groups
.

The complex version is the
2\colon 1
covering
SL(2,{\mathbb{C}})\to SO(3,{\mathbb{C}})
.

A finite subgroup of
SO(3,{\mathbb{R}})
acts on the sphere.
The cyclic subgroup
{\mathbb{Z}}_{n}
is conjugated to the group generated by

\begin{pmatrix}\cos(\pi/n)&-\sin(\pi/n)&0\\
\sin(\pi/n)&\cos(\pi/n)&0\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix},

(4.1)

which acts as rotation of
2\pi/n
around the axis
South-North
.
The Earth covered by time zones gives a good representation.
Each time zone is a fundamental domain
T
for the action of the cyclic group, meaning that

\cup_{g}gT=S^{2}
,
\mathring{T}\cap g\mathring{T}=\emptyset\quad\forall g\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{n},g\neq 1
.
A time zone can be seen as a degenerate spherical triangle with angles
2\pi/n
,
2\pi/n
,
\pi
.

Klein classified the finite groups
G\subset SO(3,{\mathbb{R}})
and we review his classification.

There are two basic classes, namely the cyclic group just described
and the dihedral group
D_{n}
with
2n
elements.

In the cyclic case, the pullback to
SU(2)
is again a cyclic group of double order
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2n}
,
generated by one of the two pullbacks of a generator of
{\mathbb{Z}}_{n}
.
The fundamental domain for the action of the group
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2n}
is just half of the previous domain,
drawing the bisector of the angle
2\pi/n
.

The dihedral group
D_{n}
of size
2n
is the symmetry group of a regular
n
-gon
and it
can be embedded in
SO(3)

with the trick of swapping North and South Pole, like in

\begin{pmatrix}\cos(\pi/n)&\sin(\pi/n)&0\\
\sin(\pi/n)&-\cos(\pi/n)&0\\
0&0&-1\end{pmatrix}

(4.2)

The
k
-powers of (
4.2
) and (
4.2
) for
k=0,\ldots,n-1
are all the elements of
D_{n}
.
The sphere can be tasselated by
2n
geodesic triangles,
n
above the equator and
n

below the equator, each one with angles

\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{2\pi}{n}
.
The pullback to
SU(n)
is the binary dihedral group, of size
4n
.

Fundamental domain is again a triangle which is half of the previous ones,
namely with angles
\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{n}
.

The three most interesting subgroups correspond to the classes of Platonic solids under duality,
namely the tetrahedral
A_{4}
, the esahedral/octahedral group
S_{4}
and the dodecahedral/icosahedral group
A_{5}
. Each face is a regular polygon with
p
sides. Join the center of the face with all the vertices of the same face. We get
p
triangles, which are the fundamental domains for the groups in
SO(3)
.

The following table shows the group size and the angles of a geodesic triangle (here
p
means the angle
\pi/p
), which is a fundamental domain for the binary group. The size of any binary group is twice the size of the corresponding group. Note the first table covers exactly the binary polyhedral groups.

\begin{array}[]{c|c|c||c|c|c}&&&p&q&r\\
\hline\cr\textrm{cyclic}&{\mathbb{Z}}_{n}&n&1&n&n\\
\hline\cr\textrm{dihedral}&D_{r}&2r&2&2&r\\
\hline\cr\textrm{tetrahedral}&A_{4}&12&2&3&3\\
\hline\cr\textrm{octahedral}&S_{4}&24&2&3&4\\
\hline\cr\textrm{icosahedral}&A_{5}&60&2&3&5\end{array}

(4.3)

With this interpretation the polyhedral group are seen as
elliptic triangle groups
.

Each oriented graph
Q
is defined by a set

5
Covariants for the variety of polynomials which are powers. A short paper by Hilbert.

During the school, participants were divided into groups, discussing and trying to understand the main points
of the paper
[
Hil93
]
, published by Hilbert in Math. Ann. in 1886. This section is a interlude in the lectures and reflects the chronological order followed at the School.

Hilbert was born in 1862 and received his doctorate in Koenigsberg, now Kaliningrad, in 1885. So it is a youthful paper, only four pages long.
The question raised in the paper is the following

Question

Given a homogeneous polynomial
f
of degree
n=\mu\nu
, what are the conditions for the existence of a homogeneous polynomial
g
of degree
\nu
such that

f=g^{\mu}.

Hilbert gives a complete answer in the case of binary forms, by constructing a covariant vanishing exactly
on the above variety of
\mu
-powers.
It is rather strange that this paper by Hilbert was forgotten for more than one century, likely because it was not included in the valuable book
[
Hilinv
]
, collecting the English translation
of most of Hilbert’s Invariant Theory papers. Rota in 1999 pointed out
the importance of Hilbert result in the colloquium lecture
[
Ro
]
.
Let’s report Rota’s own words

For a long time, I thought the answer to this question to
be beyond reach, until one day, while leafing through the
second volume of Hilbert’s collected papers, I accidentally
discovered that Hilbert had completely solved it. The solution can be elegantly
expressed in umbral notation. This
is only one of several striking results of Hilbert’s on invariant theory that have been forgotten.

In 2014 Abdesselam and Chipalkatti published the beautiful paper
[
AC
]
, where they constructed the same
Hilbert covariant, in a completely different way. They even extend their construction in arbitrary dimension.

Let
d=\mu\nu
. Define

X_{d,\mu,n}:=\{g^{\mu}|g\in{\mathrm{Sym}}^{\nu}{\mathbb{C}}^{n+1}\},\qquad\textrm{the affine variety of\ }\mu\textrm{-powers}.

Proposition 5.1
.

X_{d,\mu,n}
is smooth (as a projective variety, meaning the affine variety is singular only at the origin), obtained as the cone of a projection of
v_{\mu}({\mathbb{P}}{\mathrm{Sym}}^{\nu}{\mathbb{C}}^{n+1})
.

T_{g^{\mu}}X_{d,\mu,n}=\{g^{\mu-1}h|h\in{\mathrm{Sym}}^{\nu}{\mathbb{C}}^{n+1}\}
.

Proof.

The tangent space is computed by expanding
(g+\epsilon h)^{\mu}=g^{\mu}+\epsilon\mu g^{\mu-1}h+\ldots
. Since all tangent spaces have the same dimension,
the variety is smooth.
∎

We want to sketch the first interesting cases of Hilbert results in
[
Hil93
]
. We construct equations
for the varieties of quartics which are square and sextics which are cubes.
Let’s recall the following characterization of powers of linear forms, giving equations for the rational normal curve.

Proposition 5.2
.

Given
f\in{\mathrm{Sym}}^{d}{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
,

\exists\textrm{\ a linear form\ }\ell\textrm{\ such that\ }f=\ell^{d}\Longleftrightarrow Hess(f)=0

Proof.

\Longrightarrow
Trivial.

\Longleftarrow
The hypothesis amounts to the vanishing of the Jacobian of the map
{\mathbb{P}}^{1}\smash{\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits^{(f_{x},f_{y})}}{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
.

The following Proposition shows that the Hessian of a power has a simple expression.
We prefer to state it at the end of this section to avoid interruptions around Hilbert paper, and also because it has an independent interest.
A particular case, when
m=2
, was proved in
[
COCDR
, Prop. 2.4]
, with an argument from Invariant Theory.

Proposition 5.12
.

Let
g=g(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n})
be a homogeneous polynomial of degree
m\geqslant 2
.
Then

Hess(g^{k})=\frac{k^{n+1}(km-1)}{m-1}g^{(n+1)(k-1)}Hess(g)

If
m=1
then
Hess(g^{k})=0
.

Proof.

Consider the following partial derivatives of
g
.

(g^{k})_{i}=kg^{k-1}g_{i}\quad i=0,\ldots,n

(g^{k})_{ij}=kg^{k-1}g_{ij}+k(k-1)g^{k-2}g_{i}g_{j}\quad i,j=0,\ldots,n

Then

Hess(g^{k})=\det\left(kg^{k-2}(gg_{ij}+(k-1)g_{i}g_{j})_{i,j}\right)=k^{n+1}g^{(n+1)(k-2)}\det\left((gg_{ij}+(k-1)g_{i}g_{j})_{i,j}\right)

The matrix
(gg_{ij}+(k-1)g_{i}g_{j})_{i,j}
is the sum of two matrices, where the second one has rank
1
.
Expanding its determinant, only two terms survive and we get the following expression

g^{n+1}Hess(g)-(k-1)g^{n}\det\begin{pmatrix}0&\nabla g\\
(\nabla g)^{t}&(g_{ij})\end{pmatrix}.

(5.3)

This trick is sometimes called (in a slightly different form) the ”matrix determinant lemma” in the literature.

By Euler identity

(m-1)g_{i}=\sum_{j=0}^{n}x_{j}g_{ij}

We perform column operations, summing to the zero column
the
j
-th column multiplied by
(-x_{j})
, getting for the last matrix in (
5.3
)

\begin{pmatrix}-\frac{1}{m-1}\sum_{j=0}^{n}x_{j}g_{j}&g_{0}&\ldots&g_{n}\\
\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\
g_{i}-\frac{1}{m-1}\sum_{j=0}^{n}x_{j}g_{ij}&g_{i0}&\ldots&g_{in}\\
\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\end{pmatrix}

which, using again Euler identity, simplifies to

\begin{pmatrix}-\frac{m}{m-1}g&\nabla g\\
0&(g_{ij})\end{pmatrix}

The column operations leave the determinant unchanged, so putting together we get

Hess(g^{k})=k^{n+1}g^{(n+1)(k-2)}\left(g^{n+1}Hess(g)+\frac{(k-1)m}{m-1}g^{n+1}Hess(g)\right)=

=\frac{k^{n+1}(km-1)}{m-1}g^{(n+1)(k-1)}Hess(g)

∎

Hyperbolic Triangle Groups and Modular Forms

It is well known that the upper plane
{\mathbb{H}}=\left\{w\in{\mathbb{C}}|(\mathrm{Im}\ w)>0\right\}
is biholomorphic to the disk
\Delta=\left\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}||z|<1\right\}
through the map

\begin{array}[]{ccl}{\mathbb{H}}&\to&\Delta\\
w&\mapsto&\frac{w-i}{w+i}=z\end{array}

(6.1)

which is called the Cayley transform.

Automorphisms of
{\mathbb{H}}
are defined by
PSL(2,{\mathbb{R}})=\left\{w\mapsto\frac{aw+b}{cw+d}|a,b,c,d\in{\mathbb{R}},ad-bc=1\right\}
.

Through the Cayley transform, automorphisms of
\Delta
correspond to automorphisms of
{\mathbb{H}}
and
we will identify both of them with
PSL(2,{\mathbb{R}})
. In the context of automorphisms of
\Delta
,
PSL(2,{\mathbb{R}})
appears in the form

\left\{z\mapsto\frac{az+b}{\bar{b}z+\bar{a}}|a,b\in{\mathbb{C}},|a|^{2}-|b|^{2}=1\right\}
.

The Cayley transform is moreover an isometry if
{\mathbb{H}}
is equipped with the Poincaré metric
\frac{dwd\bar{w}}{(\mathrm{Im}\ w)^{2}}

and the disk
\Delta
with the metric
\frac{4dzd\bar{z}}{(1-|z|^{2})^{2}}
. These metrics have both negative constant curvature, equal to
-1
.
All the automorphisms described above are also isometries for these metrics.

Let
\tau
,
\tau^{\prime}
two numbers in the half-plane
{\mathbb{H}}
.
Consider the two lattices
\Lambda=\langle 1,\tau\rangle_{{\mathbb{Z}}}
,
\Lambda^{\prime}=\langle 1,\tau^{\prime}\rangle_{{\mathbb{Z}}}
,
It is well known that the two elliptic curves
{\mathbb{C}}/\Lambda
and
{\mathbb{C}}/\Lambda^{\prime}
are isomorphic if and only if

\tau^{\prime}=\frac{a\tau+b}{c\tau+d}
for some
\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\in SL(2,{\mathbb{Z}})
. Hence the quotient
{\mathbb{H}}/SL(2,{\mathbb{Z}})

can be interpreted as the moduli space of elliptic curves. This quotient is a subtle object, for the presence of automorphisms
at the special points corresponding to harmonic and equianharmonic curves, which prevents the existence of a universal family of elliptic curves.
Just at level of GIT quotient, this quotient is isomorphic to
{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
. We saw this fact with the moduli space of ternary cubics (Theorem
3.2
)
but it is useful to recall the alternative analytic approach on
{\mathbb{H}}
.

The group
PSL(2,{\mathbb{Z}})
is generated by
T=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}
, corresponding to
z\mapsto z+1
and

S=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}
corresponding to
z\mapsto-\frac{1}{z}
.

Recall that the function
q(z)=e^{2\pi iz}
is a
{\mathbb{Z}}
-covering
{\mathbb{H}}\to\Delta^{*}
.
For every meromorphic function on
{\mathbb{H}}
, which is invariant by
T
, namely satisfying
f(z+1)=f(z)
, there exists a meromorphic function

\tilde{f}
defined on
\Delta^{*}
such that
f(z)=\tilde{f}(e^{2\pi iz})
. In other words, the invariance of

allows us to write
f
as a function of
q=e^{2\pi iz}
.
We say that such
f
is holomorphic at
\infty
if
\tilde{f}
can be extended as a homorphic function to the origin.

Consider
g=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\in SL(2,{\mathbb{Z}})
and compute
gz=\frac{az+b}{cz+d}

\frac{d(gz)}{dz}=(cz+d)^{-2}

(6.2)

This computation motivates the following

Definition 6.1
.

A modular form of weight
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}
is a holomorphic function on
{\mathbb{H}}

which satisfies

f\left(\frac{az+b}{cz+d}\right)=(cz+d)^{2k}f(z)\quad\forall\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\in SL(2,{\mathbb{Z}})

(6.3)

and it is moreover holomorphic at infinity in the above sense. This is meaningful since
(
6.3
) is equivalent to
f(z+1)=f(z)
and
f(\frac{1}{z})=z^{2k}f(z)
.

They are called ”forms” since, as well explained by Serre in
[
Ser
, VII 2.1]
the equation (
6.2
) may be written as

\frac{f(gz)}{f(z)}=\left(\frac{d(gz)}{dz}\right)^{-k}

or

f(gz)\left(d(gz)\right)^{k}=f(z)\left(dz\right)^{k}.

The basic examples of modular form are the Eisenstein series

G_{k}(z)=\sum_{(m.n)\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}\setminus(0,0)}\frac{1}{(mz+n)^{2k}}\qquad k\in{\mathbb{Z}},k\geqslant 1

We already met the Eisenstein series in the proof of Prop.
3.3
.
G_{k}
is a modular form of weight
k
, since the action of
SL(2,{\mathbb{Z}})
corresponds just to a reordering of the series,
which converges absolutely.

Denote by

the vector space of modular forms of weight
k
.
There are obvious maps

, defining a graded ring structure on

The main result, which shows that this graded ring is analogous to the invariant ring for binary quartics (Theorem
2.7
) or
for ternary cubics (Theorem

.

[

The graded ring

is isomorphic to the polynomial ring

Notice that the condition

corresponds once again to the equianharmonic case while the condition

Notice moreover that Theorem

Appendix: Pfaffians and Exercises, by V. Galgano

7.1
The Pfaffian

We turn our focus on an invariant of skewsymmetric forms: the Pfaffian. Given a complex vector space
V
of dimension
N
, we consider its skewsymmetric square
\bigwedge^{2}V
whose elements are
2
-(skew)forms of the form
\tilde{\omega}=\sum_{i}v_{i}\wedge w_{i}
as the vectors
v_{i},w_{i}\in V
vary. Fixing a basis
(e_{1},\ldots,e_{N})
of
V
allows us to define a linear isomorphism between
\bigwedge^{2}V
and the vector space of
N\times N
skewsymmetric matrices, which to a
2
-form
\tilde{\omega}=\sum_{i<j}\omega_{ij}e_{i}\wedge e_{j}
associates the skewsymmetric matrix
\omega=(\omega_{ij})
. Due to this, we will often use the exterior square to denote skewsymmetric matrices as well.

The natural action of
SL(V)
on
V
induces the action on
\bigwedge^{2}V
defined by
g\cdot(v\wedge w)=(g\cdot v)\wedge(g\cdot w)
, which corresponds to the action by congruence
g\cdot\omega=g\omega g^{t}
. The Pfaffian is a
SL(V)
-invariant of
\bigwedge^{2}V
, defined only for even
N=\dim V
. Therefore, we treat separately the cases of
V
having even or odd dimension.

Even-dimensional case.

Fix
N=2n
. The
n
-th exterior power
\tilde{\omega}^{\wedge n}
of a
2
-form
\tilde{\omega}\in\bigwedge^{2}V
is a
2n
-form in
\bigwedge^{2n}V\simeq\mathbb{C}^{\vee}
. Fixing a basis
(e_{1},\ldots,e_{2n})
of
V
implies fixing a generator
e_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge e_{2n}
of
\bigwedge^{2n}V
. The
Pfaffian

\mathrm{Pf}(\omega)\in\mathbb{C}
of the skewsymmetric matrix
\omega
is the complex scalar such that

\tilde{\omega}^{\wedge n}:=(n!)\mathrm{Pf}(\omega)e_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge e_{2n}\ .

Observe that the coefficient of
e_{i_{1}}\wedge\ldots\wedge e_{i_{2k}}
in
\tilde{\omega}^{\wedge k}
is the same as in
(\tilde{\omega}|_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{2k}})^{\wedge k}
since it is obtained by all possible partitions of
\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{2k}\}
into
k
subsets of two indices each. In particular, the coefficient of
e_{i_{1}}\wedge\ldots\wedge e_{i_{2k}}
in
\tilde{\omega}^{\wedge k}
is
k!\mathrm{Pf}(\omega|_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{2k}})
. This leads to the expression

\tilde{\omega}^{\wedge k}=(k!)\sum_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{2k}}\mathrm{Pf}(\omega|_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{2k}})e_{i_{1}}\wedge\ldots\wedge e_{i_{2k}}\ .

(7.1)

Example 7.1
.

\mathrm{Pf}\begin{pmatrix}0&a\\
-a&0\end{pmatrix}=a\ \ \ \ ,\ \ \ \ \mathrm{Pf}\begin{pmatrix}0&a&b&c\\
-a&0&d&e\\
-b&-d&0&f\\
-c&-e&-f&0\end{pmatrix}=af-be+cd\ .

Exercise 7.2
.

For any
g\in GL(2n)
it holds
\mathrm{Pf}(g\omega g^{t})=\det(g)\mathrm{Pf}(\omega)
. In particular,

\mathrm{Pf}\in\big[{\mathrm{Sym}}^{n}\big(\textstyle{\bigwedge^{2}V^{\vee}}\big)\big]^{SL(V)}\ .

Proof.

\displaystyle(n!)\mathrm{Pf}(g\omega g^{t})e_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge e_{2n}=(\widetilde{g\omega g^{t}})^{\wedge n}=(g\cdot\tilde{\omega})^{\wedge n}=g\cdot(\tilde{\omega})^{\wedge n}=

\displaystyle=(n!)\mathrm{Pf}(\omega)(g\cdot e_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge e_{2n})=(n!)\mathrm{Pf}(\omega)\det(g)e_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge e_{2n}\ .\qed

Remark 7.3
.

The Pfaffian defines a degree-
n
hypersurface in
\mathbb{P}(\bigwedge^{2}V)
: in here, the notion of Pfaffian is preferrable to the notion of determinant as the latter one defines non-reduced schemes.

Remark 7.4
.

From a representation-theoretical perspective, the Pfaffian of a generic

skewsymmetric matrix is the unique
SL(2n)
-invariant in the (reducible)
SL(2n)
-module
{\mathrm{Sym}}^{n}(\bigwedge^{2}\mathbb{C}^{2n})^{\vee}
.

Let
J_{1}:={\scriptsize\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{pmatrix}}
and let

block-diagonal matrix having the first

.

Exercise 7.8
.

For every skewsymmetric matrix
\omega

\det(\omega)=\mathrm{Pf}(\omega)^{2}\ .

Proof.

If

Remark 7.9
.

Historically, Cayley
[

]
showed that the determinant of any skewsymmetric matrice (no assumption on the parity of the size) is a square, and defined the Pfaffian precisely as the square root of the determinant. In particular, this relation motivates the fact that the Pfaffian of skewsymmetric matrices of odd size is defined to be zero.

Odd dimensional case.

Fix
N=2n+1
. The Pfaffian of a
N\times N
skewsymmetric matrix is defined to be zero. Given
\omega\in\bigwedge^{2}\mathbb{C}^{N}
, its

(or adjugate) is the

such that

\omega\cdot\mathfrak{ad}\omega=\det(\omega)I\ .

The matrix
\mathfrak{ad}\omega
is the transpose of the cofactor matrix of
\omega
, that is

\mathfrak{ad}\omega=\left((-1)^{i+j}\det(\Omega_{ji})\right)_{i,j}

where
\Omega_{ij}
is the submatrix of
\omega
obtained by erasing the
i
-th row and the
j
-th column.

Exercise 7.10
.

For
\omega\in\bigwedge^{2}\mathbb{C}^{2n+1}

Proof.

Since
\omega
is skewsymmetric, it holds
\Omega_{ji}=-\Omega_{ij}^{t}
, hence

(\mathfrak{ad}\omega)_{ji}=(-1)^{i+j}\det(\Omega_{ij})=(-1)^{i+j}\det(\Omega_{ji})=(\mathfrak{ad}\omega)_{ij}\ .\qed

Exercise 7.11
.

For every

. Then
A_{f}
is described by the
9\times 9
matrix

\begin{pmatrix}0&hess(f_{z})&-hess(f_{y})\\
-hess(f_{z})&0&hess(f_{x})\\
hess(f_{y})&-hess(f_{x})&0\end{pmatrix}

where
hess(f_{x})
is the hessian matrix of the partial derivative
f_{x}
(similarly for
f_{y},f_{z}
).

Exercise 7.23

(cf.
[
Ott09
]
, Theorem 2.1)
.

Let
B_{i}
be the
8\times 8
principal submatrix obtained by removing the
i
th row and column of
A_{f}
. Then
\mathrm{Pf}(B_{i})=\mathrm{Pf}(B_{j})
for all
i,j
. Moreover, the Aronhold invariant
\mathcal{A}
coincides with such Pfaffians.
