Mucinous tubular and spindle cell carcinoma of the kidney: A case report and brief review of literature
Authors: Rebaz M. Ali, Zhino Noori Hussein, Rawa M. Ali, Soran H. Tahir, Bnar S. Saida, Farman Mohammed Faraj, Shano M. Ali, Hawkar A. Nasralla, Hiwa O. Abdullah, Fahmi H. Kakamad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mucinous tubular and spindle cell carcinoma (MTSCC) is a rare subtype of renal cell carcinoma (RCC), accounting for less than 1% of primary renal tumors. Despite its generally favorable prognosis, accurate diagnosis remains challenging because it overlaps with other RCC variants. The present report describes a case of incidentally detected MTSCC in a patient presenting with elevated low-density lipoprotein. A 39-year-old man presented for evaluation of elevated lipid levels and was incidentally found to have 2 left renal masses on ultrasonography. Computed tomography scan revealed a 26 × 24 mm exophytic lesion in the mid-portion of the left kidney with faint, homogeneous enhancement, and a 7 mm lesion in the upper pole. Magnetic resonance imaging confirmed both lesions with diffusion restriction, suggestive of RCC. Laparoscopic partial nephrectomy was performed. Histopathology examination confirmed MTSCC in the mid-pole lesion and a benign renal tubulopapillary adenoma in the upper pole. The patient’s postoperative course was uneventful, and follow-up ultrasonography showed no recurrence. A brief review of the literature identified 5 recent reports encompassing 10 documented cases of renal MTSCC. The distribution by sex was equal, with 5 male and 5 female patients. Tumor occurrence was also evenly split between kidneys, with 5 cases on the right and 5 on the left. The patients’ ages varied from 41 to 70 years. Mucinous tubular and spindle cell carcinoma is a rare tumor that may be detected incidentally. Laparoscopic partial nephrectomy may provide an effective, kidney-sparing approach with favorable outcomes.

Introduction

Renal cell carcinoma (RCC) encompasses a spectrum of malignant epithelial tumors arising from the renal tubular epithelium, displaying a wide range of morphological features [1]. Mucinous tubular and spindle cell carcinoma (MTSCC) of the kidney is a rare RCC subtype, accounting for about 0.53% of RCC cases and less than 1% of all primary renal tumors [2,3]. Historically, it was described using several designations, including RCC with unusual differentiation, low-grade collecting duct carcinoma, and RCC with prominent spindle cell components possibly associated with the loop of Henle, until it was formally recognized as a distinct pathological entity in the 2004 WHO classification of renal tumors [4,5].

Histologically, the tumor is defined by tubular and spindle-shaped cells embedded within a mucinous stroma. The nuclei are generally round and uniform, with low nuclear density, although occasional areas of higher nuclear grade may be observed [6].

This RCC subtype primarily occurs in adults and is generally considered an indolent variant with a more favorable prognosis compared to other forms of RCC [7]. It demonstrates a marked female predominance, with an approximate male-to-female ratio of 1:4 [3]. Clinically and radiologically, its presentation closely resembles that of other renal malignancies, underscoring the importance of histopathological examination for definitive diagnosis and appropriate management [8]. The present report describes a case of incidentally detected MTSCC in a patient presenting with elevated low-density lipoprotein. The report was prepared in accordance with the CaReL guidelines, and all referenced sources were carefully assessed for eligibility [9,10]. What distinguishes the present case is the combination of incidental detection in a young male during a metabolic workup, synchronous ipsilateral dual renal lesions with differing histologies and detailed multimodality imaging characterization.

Case presentation

Patient information

A 39-year-old male presented to Smart Health Tower in Sulaymaniyah, Iraq, with a documented elevation in lipid profile. He reported no associated pain or systemic symptoms. His past medical and surgical history was unremarkable.

Clinical findings

On physical examination, no significant abnormalities or notable clinical signs were detected.

Diagnostic approaches

Abdominal ultrasonography revealed mild hepatic fatty infiltration and a well-defined, solid-appearing space-occupying lesion in the left kidney, measuring approximately 25 × 20 mm, exophytic from the anterior aspect. Computed tomography scan of the abdomen demonstrated a 26 × 24 mm exophytic solid lesion in the anterior mid-portion of the left kidney, with soft-tissue density and progressive, faint, homogeneous enhancement (>30 HU), findings suggestive of RCC. Additionally, a smaller exophytic lesion measuring approximately 7 × 6 mm was identified in the anterior upper pole of the left kidney, located about 3 cm above the larger mass.

Dynamic abdominal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) confirmed 2 lesions within the left kidney. The mid-portion lesion demonstrated exophytic enhancement and diffusion-weighted imaging restriction, consistent with RCC, without evidence of perinephric or renal sinus fat invasion. The smaller 7 mm upper-pole lesion also exhibited diffusion-weighted imaging restriction; however, enhancement could not be assessed due to its small size (Fig. 1, Fig. 2). Therapeutic intervention

Under general anesthesia and antibiotic prophylaxis, the patient was placed in the right lateral decubitus position. Through 4 laparoscopic ports, the left kidney was approached, revealing 2 masses, 1 located at the mid-pole and a smaller lesion at the upper pole. Laparoscopic partial nephrectomy was performed with an ischemia time of 11 minutes. Hemostasis was secured, a tube drain was placed, and the port sites were closed.

The resected specimens were sent for histopathological examination. The posterior mid-pole mass was diagnosed as MTSCC, while the anterior upper-pole lesion was identified as a renal tubulopapillary adenoma (benign). Follow-up and outcomes

During follow-up, ultrasonographic evaluation demonstrated no evidence of disease. A subsequent ultrasonography performed 8 months postoperatively revealed no imaging features suggestive of local tumor recurrence, renal calculi, or hydronephrosis.

Discussion

MTSCC is recognized as an indolent variant of RCC, typically exhibiting low-grade histological characteristics, minimal metastatic potential, and generally favorable clinical outcomes. Nonetheless, like other RCC subtypes, cases exhibiting high-grade histological features and sarcomatoid dedifferentiation have been documented, most frequently in association with metastatic disease [4]. The entity was likely first described in 1996 by Ordóñez et al. [11] as a renal carcinoma with distinctive morphological differentiation. Based on ultrastructural analysis, the authors proposed that MTSCC originates from cells of the loop of Henle, in contrast to papillary RCC, which is believed to derive from the proximal tubule [11].

The histogenesis of MTSCC remains a subject of ongoing debate, with most investigators suggesting an origin from either the distal nephron or the collecting duct. An alternative hypothesis proposes derivation from the loop of Henle, supported by the combined immunophenotypic expression of cytokeratin 7 and racemase, which aligns more closely with a proximal nephron origin [1]. This concept was further supported by Gong et al. [12], who noted that histological and imaging features in 5 MTSCC cases consistently showed intermediate differentiation between the collecting duct and the loop of Henle, highlighting its polymorphic nature [12].

In a brief review of the literature, a search was conducted using Google Scholar with the “allintitle” feature and the terms “mucinous tubular and spindle cell carcinoma and “MTSCC kidney,” limited to English-language articles published between 2020 and the date of search (October 18, 2025), identifying 6 studies reporting a total of 10 cases of MTSCC of the kidney. The cases showed an equal gender distribution, with 5 males (50%) and 5 females (50%). Tumor laterality was also balanced, with the right kidney involved in 5 cases (50%) and the left in 5 (50%). Like other renal tumors, MTSCC is frequently asymptomatic, though it may sometimes manifest with symptoms such as flank pain or hematuria [14]. Its distinctive mixture of tubular, spindle, and mucinous or myxoid components can pose diagnostic challenges, as the predominance of a single element may lead to misclassification. Tumors with a predominant tubular pattern may be mistaken for type 1 papillary RCC with a solid growth pattern or for metanephric adenoma. Conversely, spindle cell-dominant MTSCC can be misinterpreted as sarcomatoid RCC or as a smooth muscle neoplasm, such as a leiomyomatous-predominant angiomyolipoma. Notably, sarcomatoid RCC typically exhibits marked cytologic anaplasia, in contrast to the uniform, monomorphic spindle cells characteristic of MTSCC. Nonetheless, rare cases of sarcomatoid transformation and high-grade nuclear atypia have been reported in MTSCC [1]. The present case was asymptomatic and presented incidentally during evaluation for hyperlipidemia, illustrating the silent clinical course typical of low-grade MTSCC, unlike the symptomatic high-grade variant reported by Yamashita et al. [2] which manifested with dyspnea and systemic symptoms due to metastatic spread [2].

Imaging primarily serves to detect a solid renal mass, with definitive histological classification dependent on pathological examination [13]. In certain cases, MTSCC may be suspected based on specific imaging characteristics observed on CT or MRI. Features such as slow contrast enhancement with a plateau on dynamic studies, combined with intermediate-to-high T2 signal intensity and low apparent diffusion coefficient values on MRI, can be suggestive of this diagnosis [7]. In the present case, abdominal ultrasound and CT demonstrated 2 well-defined exophytic lesions in the left kidney, the larger measuring 26 × 24 mm and showing faint, progressive homogeneous enhancement, while MRI revealed diffusion restriction in both lesions without perinephric invasion. These findings are consistent with the subtle enhancement patterns reported by Ali et al. [7] and Chaker et al. [6] further confirming that imaging may suggest but cannot definitively diagnose MTSCC [6,7].

The majority of MTSCC cases are diagnosed based on morphological assessment alone; however, in challenging cases, immunohistochemistry and fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) to detect chromosomal losses may be required [13]. Although the role of percutaneous biopsy in managing localized renal masses is debated, its diagnostic accuracy has improved over time. Due to the rarity of MTSCC, data on biopsy performance in this context are limited [15]. In a study by Kenney et al. [15] 7 patients underwent biopsy of a renal mass, 4 of whom subsequently underwent nephrectomy. In 3 cases, the biopsy misclassified MTSCC as either conventional or papillary RCC, with the correct diagnosis established only after histopathological evaluation of the nephrectomy specimens [15]. Similarly, the diagnosis of the current case was established only after histopathological examination of the laparoscopically resected specimen, which showed classic tubular and spindle components in mucinous stroma, confirming MTSCC in the mid-pole lesion. The coexisting smaller lesion in the upper pole was a benign tubulopapillary adenoma, emphasizing the need for complete histopathological evaluation to exclude multifocal disease.

Immunohistochemically, MTSCC demonstrates markers of both distal (CK7) and proximal tubular differentiation (RCC Ma, AMACR, and CD15). Consequently, CK7, EMA, and AMACR are unreliable for distinguishing MTSCC from papillary RCC. In contrast, CD10 expression is observed in 93%-100% of papillary RCCs but in only approximately 15% of MTSCC cases, making it a useful marker for differentiating the 2 entities [1]. In agreement, Tamir et al. [13] reported that MTSCC is typically positive for AMACR, CK7, CK19, EMA, and PAX8 but negative for CD10 and CD15, a profile that parallels the present case’s findings and confirms the mixed distal-proximal immunophenotype typical of this tumor [13].

Surgical excision remains the cornerstone of RCC management, with the choice between partial or radical nephrectomy guided by tumor stage and size. Following resection, regular surveillance using cross-sectional imaging of the chest and abdominopelvic region is recommended, with additional targeted imaging based on clinical symptoms. Despite MTSCC being a low-grade tumor with an indolent course, follow-up is typically continued for 3-5 years because the risk of recurrence is low; further monitoring is individualized based on patient age, preferences, and life expectancy. Nevertheless, a subset of patients may present with locally advanced disease, highlighting the need for vigilant postoperative assessment. The precise recurrence rate of MTSCC remains unclear due to its rarity [13]. In the present case, a laparoscopic partial nephrectomy was performed successfully with an ischemia time of 11 minutes, and the patient recovered uneventfully. Follow-up ultrasonography demonstrated no residual or recurrent disease, paralleling the favorable outcomes reported by Ali et al. [7] Chaker et al. [6], and Ghabbani et al. [8] all of whom observed no recurrence within 3-12 months after nephrectomy [[6], [7], [8]]. Conversely, the fatal course reported by Yamashita et al. underscores the heterogeneity of prognosis depending on tumor grade and stage [2].

For patients with advanced diseases, including those with sarcomatoid differentiation or metastases, the efficacy of chemotherapy and immunotherapy continues to be explored [9]. While the present case required no adjuvant therapy due to localized disease, the literature highlights variable outcomes for advanced cases. Ghabbani et al. [8] reported a disease-free interval after laparoscopic nephrectomy in a 41-year-old female, whereas Gong et al. [12] described successful chemotherapeutic control with gemcitabine and cisplatin in a metastatic case [8,12].

This study has several limitations. First, it represents a single case report, limiting the generalizability of the findings. Second, the follow-up duration was relatively short, preventing assessment of long-term recurrence and survival outcomes. Third, the literature review was limited to English-language studies identified through Google Scholar, which may have resulted in the omission of relevant reports from other databases or languages. Finally, although detailed imaging findings were presented, definitive diagnosis still depended on histopathological examination because the radiological features of MTSCC overlap with those of other renal tumors.

Conclusion

Mucinous tubular and spindle cell carcinoma is a rare tumor that may be detected incidentally. Laparoscopic partial nephrectomy may provide an effective, kidney-sparing approach with favorable outcomes.
