On weak solutions for the stationary Cahn-Hilliard-Navier-Stokes equations with singular potential
Zhilei Liang, Sen Liu, Jiangyu Shuai, Dehua Wang

Abstract
. The stationary Navier–Stokes–Cahn–Hilliard equations are considered, governing the motion of a compressible, two-phase fluid mixture with a diffuse interface. The free energy density in this paper has a singular logarithmic (Flory–Huggins) form, ensuring that the mass fraction remains in the physical range and allowing for vacuum states. We prove the existence of weak solutions in a three-dimensional bounded domain under structural assumptions on the adiabatic exponent. The stationary setting poses two main mathematical challenges: the absence of an energy inequality driven by the evolution process to control the singular potential, and the degeneracy of the density near the vacuum. To address these issues, we introduce a specialized regularization of the logarithmic term that eliminates the quadratic growth induced by anti-diffusion, thereby restoring compactness. Uniform estimates are obtained through a special choice of artificial pressure and an interpolation argument that controls the desired norm of the density. A two-level limiting process then yields a weak solution that satisfies the physical bounds almost everywhere on the support of the density. To our knowledge, this is the first existence result for the steady compressible Navier–Stokes–Cahn–Hilliard system that incorporates both a singular free energy and vacuum regions.

1.
Introduction

We are concerned with the stationary system of partial differential equations that describes the motion of a three-dimensional compressible two-phase fluid mixture with a diffuse interface:

(1.1)

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} &{\rm div}(\rho{\bf u})=0,\\
&{\rm div}(\rho{\bf u}\otimes{\bf u})={\rm div}(\mathbb{S}_{ns}+\mathbb{S}_{c}-P\mathbb{I})+\rho{\mathbf{g}}_{1}+\mathbf{g}_{2},\\
&{\rm div}(\rho{\bf u}c)=\Delta\mu,\\
&\rho\mu=\rho\frac{\partial f(\rho,c)}{\partial c}-\Delta c,\end{aligned}\right.

where
\rho\geq 0
denotes the total density of the binary fluid mixture,
c
is the mass fraction of one component (with the physical constraint
|c|\leq 1
),
\mathbf{u}=(u_{1},u_{2},u_{3})
is the mass-averaged velocity field, and
\mu
is the chemical potential. The functions
{\textbf{g}}_{1}
and
\mathbf{g}_{2}
are the external body forces: the term
\rho\mathbf{g}_{1}
acts on the bulk mass, while
\mathbf{g}_{2}
acts on the fluid boundary. The viscous stress tensor
\mathbb{S}_{ns}
and the capillary (Korteweg-type) stress tensor
\mathbb{S}_{c}
are given by

(1.2)

\displaystyle\mathbb{S}_{ns}=\lambda_{1}(\nabla{\bf u}+(\nabla{\bf u})^{T})+\lambda_{2}{\rm div}{\bf u}\mathbb{I},\quad\mathbb{S}_{c}=-\nabla c\otimes\nabla c+\frac{1}{2}|\nabla c|^{2}\mathbb{I},

where
\mathbb{I}
is the identity matrix. The viscosity coefficients
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
satisfy the physical constraints
\lambda_{1}>0
and
2\lambda_{1}+3\lambda_{2}\geq 0
. The Helmholtz free energy density
f(\rho,c)
determines the total pressure
P
via the thermodynamic relation

P(\rho,c)=\rho^{2}\frac{\partial f(\rho,c)}{\partial\rho}.

Typically, the free energy decomposes as

\displaystyle f(\rho,c)=\int_{1}^{\rho}\frac{p(s)}{s^{2}}{\rm d}s+f_{mix}(\rho,c),

where
p
is the inner pressure and
f_{mix}(\rho,c)
is the mixing contribution. In this work, we consider a physically relevant singular free energy of the form

(1.3)

\displaystyle f(\rho,c)=\rho^{\gamma-1}+H(c)\ln\rho+F(c),

where
\gamma>1
is the adiabatic exponent,
H(c)
is a smooth function and
F(c)
is a logarithmic (also known as Flory-Huggins type) potential of the form

(1.4)

F(c)=\frac{\theta_{0}}{2}\left((1+c)\ln(1+c)+(1-c)\ln(1-c)\right)-\frac{\theta_{c}}{2}c^{2},\quad c\in(-1,1).

We note that the logarithmic term (
1.4
) represents the mixture’s entropy, and the temperature restriction
0<\theta_{0}<\theta_{c}
ensures that a double-well form actually occurs. If
F
is convex, the components are miscible and diffusion occurs; if
F
is non-convex, there is a spinodal region in which anti-diffusion prevents the fluids from mixing completely.

We are interested in the boundary value problem of (
1.1
) in a three-dimensional bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
The system (
1.1
) is closed by the total mass and relative mass constraints

(1.5)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\rho(x){\rm d}x=m_{1}>0,\quad\int_{\Omega}\rho(x)c(x){\rm d}x=m_{2},

where
m_{1},m_{2}
are prescribed constants, and by the boundary conditions

(1.6)

\displaystyle{\bf u}=0,\quad\frac{\partial c}{\partial\vec{n}}=0,\quad\frac{\partial\mu}{\partial\vec{n}}=0\quad{\rm on}\,\,\partial\Omega,

where
\vec{n}=\vec{n}(x)
is the outward unit normal at boundary point
x\in\partial\Omega
.

The Cahn–Hilliard equation, when combined with the Navier–Stokes equations, serves as a fundamental diffuse-interface model for binary fluid flows, applicable from turbulent multi-phase dynamics to microfluidics. The dynamic version of this coupled system is known as “Model H” and was introduced by Hohenberg and Halperin
[
22
]
for density-matched incompressible fluids. A comprehensive mathematical theory has been developed for this evolutionary system. Abels
[
1
,
2
]
proved the global existence of weak solutions. Giorgini, Miranville, and Temam
[
20
]
addressed uniqueness, regularity, and strong solutions. Miranville and Temam
[
16
]
showed the existence for a Cahn–Hilliard–Navier–Stokes system based on the Oono model with singular potential. Further results incorporating Flory–Huggins potentials can be found in
[
17
,
19
,
32
]
.

A preliminary compressible variant was introduced by Lowengrub and Truskinovsky
[
33
]
, wherein the total density is a function of the concentration,
\rho=\rho(c)
, and the chemical potential assumes a more complex form that incorporates the pressure and the divergence of the velocity:

\displaystyle\mu=f_{0}^{\prime}(c)-\frac{\rho^{\prime}(c)}{\rho^{2}}\left(p-\big(\lambda_{1}+\frac{2\lambda_{2}}{3}\big){\rm div}{\bf u}\right)-\frac{{\rm div}(\rho\nabla c)}{\rho},

where
f_{0}(c)
is the Helmholtz free energy.
To handle the lack of compactness for
\nabla c
in vacuum regions, Abels and Feireisl
[
3
]
introduced a modified compressible model:

(1.7)

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}\rho+{\rm div}(\rho{\bf u})=0,\\
&\partial_{t}(\rho{\bf u})+{\rm div}(\rho{\bf u}\otimes{\bf u})={\rm div}(\mathbb{S}_{ns}+\mathbb{S}_{c}-P\mathbb{I}),\\
&\partial_{t}(\rho c)+{\rm div}(\rho{\bf u}c)=\Delta\mu,\\
&\rho\mu=\rho\frac{\partial f(\rho,c)}{\partial c}-\Delta c.\end{aligned}\right.

In
[
3
]
, Abels and Feireisl proved the existence of weak solutions to (
1.7
) under the assumption of a sufficiently regular potential. More recently, Basarić and Giorgini
[
9
]
extended this analysis to include singular free energies.

The system (
1.1
) studied here is the stationary counterpart of (
1.7
). When the external forces vanish (
\mathbf{g}_{1}=\mathbf{g}_{2}=0
), its solutions align with the critical points of the total energy functional

(1.8)

\mathcal{E}(t)=\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{1}{2}\rho|{\bf u}|^{2}+\rho f(\rho,c)+\frac{1}{2}|\nabla c|^{2}\right)(x,t){\rm d}x,\quad t\geq 0.

The stationary problem differs fundamentally from the evolutionary one; it is not just a parallel case or a broader generalization. For the time-dependent equations (
1.7
), the
a priori
energy inequality
\mathcal{E}(t)\leq\mathcal{E}(0)
provides a crucial tool for the mathematical analysis. In the stationary setting, no such time-evolution bound is available, and the energy balance by itself yields far less information. When the mixing free energy
f_{\text{mix}}(\rho,c)
is regular, namely, the functions in (
1.3
) satisfy

(1.9)

|H(c)|+|H^{\prime}(c)|+|F(c)|+|F^{\prime}(c)|\leq C,\quad c\in\mathbb{R},

Liang-Wang
[
26
,
27
]
proved the existence of weak solutions for equations (
1.1
) if the adiabatic exponent
\gamma
satisfies
\gamma>4/3.
For stationary Cahn–Hilliard–Navier–Stokes equations of incompressible flows, we refer to
[
5
,
7
,
8
,
24
,
25
]
.
For the steady compressible Navier–Stokes equations (without phase separation), Lions
[
28
]
established the existence of a nontrivial equilibrium state for
\gamma\geq 5/3
. Subsequently, the admissible range of
\gamma
was extended to
\gamma>1
in various settings; see
[
14
,
15
,
34
]
for bounded domains with Dirichlet boundary conditions and
[
6
,
23
]
for periodic domains. Comprehensive treatments of steady compressible fluid flows can be found in the monographs
[
30
,
31
]
.

However, the regular mixture entropy violates the physical constraint
c\in[-1,1]
. A physically consistent Helmholtz free energy—particularly one with a singular logarithmic potential—is therefore both more relevant for applications and more challenging mathematically. In this paper, we assume a singular free energy and establish the existence of weak solutions to the stationary problem (
1.1
)-(
1.6
).

Definition 1.1

(Weak solution)
.

Let exponents
p>\frac{6}{5}
and

\theta>0
. A quadruple
(\rho,{\bf u},\mu,c)
is a weak solution to problem (
1.1
)-(
1.6
), if

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} 0\leq\rho\in L^{\gamma+\theta}(\Omega),\quad{\bf u}\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3}),\quad(\mu,c)\in H^{1}(\Omega)\times W^{2,p}(\Omega),\end{aligned}

and the following hold:

(1)

Equation
\eqref{eq1.1}_{1}
is satisfied in distributional sense.
Extended by zero outside
\Omega
, the pair
(\rho,{\bf u})
satisfies the renormalized continuity equation

\displaystyle{\rm div}(b(\rho){\bf u})+(b^{\prime}(\rho)\rho-b(\rho)){\rm div}{\bf u}=0\quad{\rm in}\,\,\,\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\Omega),

for any
b\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
with
b^{\prime}(z)=0
for large
z
.

(2)

The following weak formulation of momentum and concentration equations holds:

\int_{\Omega}\Bigl(\rho\mathbf{u}\otimes\mathbf{u}+\rho^{2}\frac{\partial f(\rho,c)}{\partial\rho}\mathbb{I}-\mathbb{S}_{ns}-\mathbb{S}_{c}\Bigr):\nabla\Phi\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}(\rho\mathbf{g}_{1}+\mathbf{g}_{2})\cdot\Phi\,\mathrm{d}x,

for any
\Phi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3})
; and

\int_{\Omega}(\rho c\mathbf{u}-\nabla\mu)\cdot\nabla\phi\,\mathrm{d}x=0,\quad\int_{\Omega}\Bigl(\rho\mu-\rho\frac{\partial f(\rho,c)}{\partial c}\Bigr)\phi\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}\nabla c\cdot\nabla\phi\,\mathrm{d}x,

for any
\phi\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
.

(3)

The mass constraints (
1.5
) are satisfied, and the following energy inequality holds:

(1.10)

\int_{\Omega}(\lambda_{1}|\nabla{\bf u}|^{2}+(\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2})({\rm div}{\bf u})^{2}+|\nabla\mu|^{2}){\rm d}x\leq\int_{\Omega}(\rho\mathbf{g}_{1}+\mathbf{g}_{2})\cdot{\bf u}{\rm d}x.

Our main result is the following existence theorem.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a bounded, simply connected domain with
C^{2}
boundary. Assume the external forces satisfy

(1.11)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{g}_{1}},\,\,{\mathbf{g}_{2}}\in L^{\infty}\left(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3}\right)

and that
H(c)
in (
1.3
) is constant, i.e.,

(1.12)

\displaystyle H(c)=H=\mathrm{const}>0.

Suppose the adiabatic exponent
\gamma
satisfies

(1.13)

\displaystyle\gamma>\frac{3}{2}\,\,\,\,\,{\rm if}\,\,\,\nabla\times{\mathbf{g}_{1}}=0,\quad\quad\gamma>\frac{5}{3}\,\,\,\,\,{\rm if}\,\,\nabla\times{\mathbf{g}_{1}}\neq 0,

and the total and relative masses satisfy

(1.14)

\displaystyle m_{1}>0,\quad m_{2}\in(-m_{1},\,m_{1}).

Then, the problem (
1.1
)-(
1.6
) admits a weak solution
(\rho,{\bf u},\mu,c)
in the sense of Definition 1.1. Moreover, the concentration satisfies the physical bounds

\displaystyle-1<c(x)<1\quad a.e.\,\,\,{\rm in}\,\,\,\,\{x\in\Omega:\,\rho(x)>0\}

and the singular term is well-defined:

(1.15)

\rho\frac{\partial f(\rho,c)}{\partial c}=\begin{cases}\rho F^{\prime}(c),&\text{if }\rho>0,\\
0,&\text{if }\rho=0.\end{cases}

Remark 1.1
.

The mixing free energy density is motivated by the logarithmic Helmholtz energy typical for Cahn–Hilliard models (cf.
[
3
,
9
]
):

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} f_{mix}(\rho,c)&=\frac{\theta_{0}}{2}\left((1+c)\ln(\rho(1+c))+(1-c)\ln(\rho(1-c))\right)-\frac{\theta_{c}}{2}c^{2}\\
&=H(c)\,\ln\rho+F(c)\\
&=\underbrace{\theta_{0}}_{H(c)}\,\ln\rho+\underbrace{\frac{\theta_{0}}{2}\left((1+c)\ln(1+c)+(1-c)\ln(1-c)\right)-\frac{\theta_{c}}{2}c^{2}}_{F(c)},\quad c\in(-1,1).\end{aligned}

More generally, we may allow
F
to satisfy, for some
\alpha_{0}
,

\displaystyle\lim_{c\rightarrow\pm 1}F^{\prime}(c)=\pm\infty,\quad\lim_{c\rightarrow\pm 1}F^{\prime\prime}(c)=+\infty,\quad F^{\prime\prime}(c)\geq\alpha_{0},

and assume
H
is bounded for some positive constants
\underline{H}
and
\overline{H}
,

(1.16)

\displaystyle 0\leq\underline{H}<H(c),\,H^{\prime}(c)\leq\overline{H}<\infty.

Remark 1.2
.

Based on results for the single-fluid steady compressible Navier–Stokes equations
[
15
,
30
,
34
]
, one expects that the range of adiabatic exponent
\gamma
in (
1.13
) can be further extended. However, it seems difficult to deal with the weighted estimate on
c
near the boundary, because the singularity of the free energy only yields control on
\|\sqrt{\rho}\frac{\partial f(\rho,c)}{\partial c}\|_{L^{2}}
.

Remark 1.3
.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first existence result for weak solutions of the stationary compressible Navier–Stokes–Cahn–Hilliard system that simultaneously allows for a singular free energy potential and the presence of vacuum states.

Outline of the proof and key ideas.

We establish Theorem
1.1
via a two-fold weak convergence procedure applied to a carefully constructed approximate system. The main difficulties arise from the singular nature of the free energy and the possible degeneracy of the density. We outline our strategy below.

For the evolutionary problem, the
a priori
energy inequality
\mathcal{E}(t)\leq\mathcal{E}(0)
provides a uniform
L^{2}
bound on
c
, making Taylor expansion regularization of the singular potential feasible (see, e.g.,
[
21
,
9
,
10
]
). This approach fails for the stationary problem because (
1.1
) alone yields no such control, and the
L^{1}
bounds from (
1.5
) are insufficient to control the nonlinearity in the potential. Our idea is to introduce a special approximate function
f_{2}^{\delta}(c)
for the singular logarithmic term, constructed so that the difference
f_{2}^{\delta}(c)-\frac{\theta_{c}}{2}c^{2}
increases only linearly as
|c|
becomes large. This cancellation effectively offsets the quadratic growth of the anti-diffusion term. See Remark
2.2
for further details.

We first introduce and analyze the regularized approximate system (
2.7
)-(
2.8
), and prove the existence of solutions via a fixed point argument, following
[
26
, Theorem 4.1]
. The proof, however, is not a straightforward extension: one must carefully pair the function
f_{2}^{\delta}
with the quadratic term
\frac{\theta_{c}}{2}c^{2}
to ensure they cancel each other.

Then we need to establish the estimates uniform in
\delta
for the approximate solutions (Proposition 4.1). A major challenge is controlling the
L^{\frac{3}{2}}
integrability of the density. Our strategy is to choose a specific artificial pressure, and use the observation

\frac{\partial f_{2}^{\delta}}{\partial c}-\theta_{c}c=O\Bigl(\ln\frac{1}{\delta}\Bigr)\quad\text{for }|c|>1+\delta,

and interpolation to obtain an estimate of the form

\|\rho\|_{L^{3/2}(\{|c|\geq 1+\delta\})}\leq\frac{1}{2}\|\rho(f_{2}^{\delta^{\prime}}-\theta_{c}c)c\|_{L^{1}}+\frac{1}{2}\Bigl(\ln\frac{1}{\delta}\Bigr)^{-1}\|\rho\|_{L^{11}}^{11}+C,

which allows us to close the estimates for all
\gamma>3/2
.
Another important observation is that condition (
1.14
) is also essential for obtaining
L^{p}
bounds on
\mu
.

Finally, when passing to the limit as the regularization parameters vanish, we must ensure that the limiting concentration
c
remains strictly within the physical interval
(-1,1)
on non-vacuum regions. The coupled Navier–Stokes–Cahn–Hilliard structure makes this delicate, especially since the pressure depends jointly on
\rho
and
c
and lacks monotonicity in
\rho
for all
c
. Adapting techniques from
[
28
,
14
,
30
,
31
]
and
[
26
,
27
]
, we prove strong convergence of the density. Then, following ideas from
[
9
]
, we establish that
c\in(-1,1)
almost everywhere where
\rho>0
, thereby verifying the physical constraint and ensuring the convergence of
F^{\delta}(c^{\delta})
and the well-definedness of
\partial_{c}f(\rho,c)
in the non-vacuum domains.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows.
In Section 2, we construct a carefully designed regularization of the singular logarithmic potential. This approximation is essential for the stationary problem, as it offsets the quadratic growth of the anti-diffusion term and restores the necessary compactness.
Section 3 focuses on the first-level approximate system with parameters
\epsilon
and
\delta
. We prove the existence of strong solutions using a fixed-point argument and establish uniform-in-
\epsilon
estimates. Taking the limit
\epsilon\to 0
, we obtain a weak solution to an intermediate system with the regularized free energy.
In Section 4, we derive uniform-in-
\delta
estimates for the solutions of this intermediate system. A crucial step is to control the
L^{3/2}
-norm of the density via a delicate interpolation argument that exploits the specific structure of the regularized potential. We then take the
\delta\to 0
limit to recover the original singular free energy.
Section 5 contains the proof of our main result, Theorem 1.1. We establish the strong convergence of the density and show that the limiting concentration
c
satisfies the physical bounds
-1<c<1
almost everywhere on the support of
\rho
.
Finally, technical lemmas and auxiliary estimates are collected in the Appendix.

2.
Regularization and Approximate System

As mentioned earlier, the usual Taylor expansion fails for the stationary problem (
1.1
) because there is no
a priori

L^{2}
bound on
c
to control the quadratic nonlinearity
\frac{\theta_{c}}{2}c^{2}
. So we need a new approximation for the singular potential.
From (
1.3
), we write

\displaystyle F(c)=f_{2}(c)-\frac{\theta_{c}}{2}c^{2},

where
f_{2}(c)
is a singular function of the form

(2.1)

f_{2}(c)=\frac{\theta_{0}}{2}\left((1+c)\ln(1+c)+(1-c)\ln(1-c)\right)

that is approximated by the following regularized extension:

\displaystyle f_{2}^{\delta}(c)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &f_{2}(c),&&0\leq c\leq 1-\delta,\\[4.0pt]
&\frac{\theta_{0}}{2\delta(2-\delta)}(c-1+\delta)^{2}+\frac{\theta_{0}}{2}\ln\left(\frac{2}{\delta}-1\right)(c-1+\delta)+f_{2}(1-\delta),&&1-\delta<c\leq 1,\\[4.0pt]
&\frac{\theta_{c}\delta(2-\delta)-\theta_{0}}{6\delta^{2}(2-\delta)}(c-1)^{3}+\frac{\theta_{0}}{2\delta(2-\delta)}(c-1)^{2}\\
&\quad+\left(\frac{\theta_{0}}{2-\delta}+\frac{\theta_{0}}{2}\ln\left(\frac{2-\delta}{\delta}\right)\right)(c-1)+\frac{\delta\theta_{0}}{2(2-\delta)}+\theta_{0}\ln(2-\delta),&&1<c\leq 1+\delta,\\[4.0pt]
&\frac{\theta_{c}}{2}(c-1-\delta)^{2}+\left(\frac{\theta_{c}\delta}{2}+\frac{3\theta_{0}}{2(2-\delta)}+\frac{\theta_{0}}{2}\ln\left(\frac{2-\delta}{\delta}\right)\right)(c-1-\delta)&&\\
&\quad+\frac{\theta_{c}\delta^{2}}{6}+\frac{11\theta_{0}\delta}{6(2-\delta)}+\frac{\theta_{0}\delta}{2}\ln\left(\frac{2-\delta}{\delta}\right)+\theta_{0}\ln(2-\delta),&&1+\delta<c.\end{aligned}\right.

Additionally, we assume

f_{2}^{\delta}(c)=f_{2}^{\delta}(-c)\quad\forall\,c\in(-\infty,\infty),

so that
f_{2}^{\delta}(c)
is well defined when
c\leq 0
.

Remark 2.1
.

The function
f_{2}^{\delta}(c)
is
C^{2}
over
(-\infty,\infty).
We can easily compute its first-order derivative

\displaystyle f_{2}^{\delta^{\prime}}(c)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{\theta_{0}}{2}\ln\left(\frac{1+c}{1-c}\right),&&0<c\leq 1-\delta,\\
&\frac{\theta_{0}}{\delta(2-\delta)}(c-1+\delta)+\frac{\theta_{0}}{2}\ln\left(\frac{2-\delta}{\delta}\right),&&1-\delta<c\leq 1,\\
&\frac{\theta_{c}\delta(2-\delta)-\theta_{0}}{2\delta^{2}(2-\delta)}(c-1)^{2}+\frac{\theta_{0}}{\delta(2-\delta)}(c-1)+\frac{\theta_{0}}{2-\delta}+\frac{\theta_{0}}{2}\ln\left(\frac{2-\delta}{\delta}\right),&&1<c\leq 1+\delta,\\
&\theta_{c}(c-1-\delta)+\frac{\theta_{c}\delta}{2}+\frac{3\theta_{0}}{2(2-\delta)}+\frac{\theta_{0}}{2}\ln\left(\frac{2-\delta}{\delta}\right),&&1+\delta<c,\end{aligned}\right.

and second-order derivative

\displaystyle f_{2}^{\delta^{\prime\prime}}(c)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{\theta_{0}}{1-c^{2}},&&0<c\leq 1-\delta,\\
&\frac{\theta_{0}}{\delta(2-\delta)},&&1-\delta<c\leq 1,\\
&\frac{\theta_{c}\delta(2-\delta)-\theta_{0}}{\delta^{2}(2-\delta)}(c-1)+\frac{\theta_{0}}{\delta(2-\delta)},&&1<c\leq 1+\delta,\\
&\theta_{c},&&1+\delta<c.\end{aligned}\right.

Furthermore, we have the following claims:

•

f_{2}^{\delta}(c)
converges uniformly to
f_{2}(c)
in any compact set of
(-1,1)
.

•

f_{2}^{\delta^{\prime}}(c)
converges uniformly to
f_{2}^{\prime}(c)
in any compact set of
(-1,1)
.

Indeed, for any
-1<a<b<1
, we may choose
\delta>0
so small to satisfy

\delta<\min\{1+a,1-b\},

then,

f_{2}^{\delta}(c)=f_{2}(c),\quad\forall c\in[a,b].

Similar argument runs for
f_{2}^{\delta^{\prime}}(c)
.

Remark 2.2
.

The construction of
f_{2}^{\delta}(c)
is to offset the nonlinear term
\frac{\theta_{c}}{2}c^{2}
appeared in (
1.3
). In particular,

•

The function
f_{2}^{\delta}(c)-\frac{\theta_{c}}{2}c^{2}
is linear with respect to
c
, if
|c|
is properly large.

•

Let

(2.2)

\displaystyle c^{*}=1-\frac{2}{1+\exp\{2\theta_{c}/\theta_{0}\}}.

Then there is a constant
M=M(\theta_{0},\theta_{c})
, independent of
\delta
, such that

(2.3)

\left|f_{2}^{\delta^{\prime}}-\theta_{c}c\right|<M\,\,\,\,{\rm if}\,\,|c|\leq c^{*},\quad\,\,\,\,(f_{2}^{\delta^{\prime}}-\theta_{c}c)c>0\,\,\,\,{\rm if}\,\,|c|>c^{*}.

•

The function

On the other hand, if we take

\displaystyle-\int\left(\left(\rho\mu\partial_{i}c+\overline{\rho\frac{\partial f^{\delta}}{\partial c}}\partial_{i}c\right)\phi\partial_{i}\Delta^{-1}(\rho)-(\rho\mathbf{g}_{1}+\mathbf{g}_{2})\phi\partial_{i}\Delta^{-1}(\rho)\right)

(6.6)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{5}\mathcal{J}_{i}.

Making use of (

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} |\mathcal{J}_{6}^{\epsilon}+\mathcal{J}_{7}^{\epsilon}|&\leq\epsilon^{4}\|\nabla\rho^{\epsilon}\|_{L^{2}}\|\rho^{\epsilon}\|_{L^{3}}\|{\bf u}^{\epsilon}\|_{L^{6}}+\epsilon^{2}\|\rho^{\epsilon}\|_{L^{2}}\|{\bf u}^{\epsilon}\|_{L^{2}}\|\partial_{i}\Delta^{-1}(2\rho^{\epsilon}-\rho_{0})\|_{L^{\infty}}\\

Proof of Lemma
5.1
.

The proof is as in
[
14
]
and the main idea is to use the cutoff technique to guarantee that
(\rho,{\bf u})
is still a renormalized solution, so that the defection of the density can be controlled.

Define

(6.7)

C^{1}[0,\infty)\ni T_{k}(z)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &z,&&z\leq k,\\
&k+0.5,&&z\geq k+1.\end{aligned}\right.

We claim that

(6.8)

\displaystyle\lim_{\delta\to 0}\int T_{k}(\rho^{\delta})\left((\rho^{\delta})^{2}\frac{\partial f^{\delta}}{\partial\rho^{\delta}}-(2\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}){\rm div}{\bf u}^{\delta}\right)=\int\overline{T_{k}(\rho)}\left(\overline{\rho^{2}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\rho}}-(2\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}){\rm div}{\bf u}\right).

In fact, multiply
\eqref{eq3.1}_{2}
by
\Phi=\phi\nabla\Delta^{-1}(T_{k}(\rho^{\delta}))
and
\eqref{eq4.57}_{2}
by
\phi\nabla\Delta^{-1}(\overline{T_{k}(\rho)})
respectively, to deduce

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\int\phi T_{k}(\rho^{\delta})\left((\rho^{\delta})^{2}\frac{\partial f^{\delta}}{\partial\rho^{\delta}}-(2\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}){\rm div}{\bf u}^{\delta}\right)\\
&=-\int\left(\ln\frac{1}{\delta}\right)^{-1}\phi T_{k}(\rho^{\delta})(\rho^{\delta})^{11}\\
&\quad-\int\partial_{i}\Delta^{-1}(T_{k}(\rho^{\delta}))\partial_{i}\phi\left(\left(\ln\frac{1}{\delta}\right)^{-1}(\rho^{\delta})^{11}+(\rho^{\delta})^{2}\frac{\partial f^{\delta}}{\partial\rho^{\delta}}-(\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}){\rm div}{\bf u}^{\delta}\right)\\
&\quad+\lambda_{1}\int\partial_{j}u^{\delta}_{i}\partial_{i}\Delta^{-1}(T_{k}(\rho^{\delta}))\partial_{j}\phi-u^{\delta}_{i}\partial_{j}\partial_{i}\Delta^{-1}(T_{k}(\rho^{\delta}))\partial_{j}\phi+T_{k}(\rho^{\delta}){\bf u}^{\delta}\cdot\nabla\phi\\
&\quad-\int(\rho^{\delta}\mathbf{g}_{1}+\mathbf{g}_{2})^{i}\phi\partial_{i}\Delta^{-1}(T_{k}(\rho^{\delta}))+\frac{1}{2}\int|\nabla c^{\delta}|^{2}(\phi T_{k}(\rho^{\delta})+\partial_{i}\phi\partial_{i}\Delta^{-1}(T_{k}(\rho^{\delta})))\\
&\quad-\int\partial_{i}c^{\delta}\partial_{j}c^{\delta}(\phi\partial_{j}\partial_{i}\Delta^{-1}(T_{k}(\rho^{\delta}))+\partial_{j}\phi\partial_{i}\Delta^{-1}(T_{k}(\rho^{\delta})))-\int\rho^{\delta}u^{\delta}_{j}u^{\delta}_{i}\partial_{j}\phi\partial_{i}\Delta^{-1}(T_{k}(\rho^{\delta}))\\
&\quad-\int u^{\delta}_{i}\phi[\rho^{\delta}u^{\delta}_{j}\phi\partial_{j}\partial_{i}\Delta^{-1}(T_{k}(\rho^{\delta}))-T_{k}(\rho^{\delta})\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\Delta^{-1}(\rho^{\delta}u^{\delta}_{j})]\\
&=\sum_{i=1}^{7}R_{i}^{\delta}\end{aligned}

and

\displaystyle\int\phi\overline{T_{k}(\rho)}\left(\overline{\rho^{2}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\rho}}-(2\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}){\rm div}{\bf u}\right)

\displaystyle=-\int\partial_{i}\Delta^{-1}(\overline{T_{k}(\rho)})\partial_{i}\phi\left(\overline{\rho^{2}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\rho}}-(\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}){\rm div}{\bf u}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\lambda_{1}\int\partial_{j}u^{i}\partial_{i}\Delta^{-1}(\overline{T_{k}(\rho)})\partial_{j}\phi-u^{i}\partial_{j}\partial_{i}\Delta^{-1}(\overline{T_{k}(\rho)})\partial_{j}\phi+\overline{T_{k}(\rho)}{\bf u}\cdot\nabla\phi

\displaystyle\quad-\int(\rho\mathbf{g}_{1}+\mathbf{g}_{2})\phi\partial_{i}\Delta^{-1}(\overline{T_{k}(\rho)})+\frac{1}{2}\int|\nabla c|^{2}(\phi\overline{T_{k}(\rho)}+\partial_{i}\phi\partial_{i}\Delta^{-1}(\overline{T_{k}(\rho)}))

\displaystyle\quad-\int\partial_{i}c\partial_{j}c(\phi\partial_{j}\partial_{i}\Delta^{-1}(\overline{T_{k}(\rho)})+\partial_{j}\phi\partial_{i}\Delta^{-1}(\overline{T_{k}(\rho)})-\int\rho u^{j}u^{i}\partial_{j}\phi\partial_{i}\Delta^{-1}(\overline{T_{k}(\rho)})

\displaystyle\quad-\int u^{i}\phi[\rho u^{j}\partial_{j}\partial_{i}\Delta^{-1}(\overline{T_{k}(\rho)})-\overline{T_{k}(\rho)}\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\Delta^{-1}(\rho u^{j})]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{7}R_{i}.

In this connection, we use the same argument as in Lemma
3.2
and conclude

\displaystyle\lim_{\delta\to 0}R_{i}^{\delta}=R_{i}\quad(i=1\sim 7),

and therefore, the desired (
6.8
) is obtained.

Having (
6.8
) obtained, we compute

(6.9)

\displaystyle(2\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2})\lim_{\delta\to 0}\int\left(T_{k}(\rho^{\delta}){\rm div}{\bf u}^{\delta}-\overline{T_{k}(\rho)}{\rm div}{\bf u}\right)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\delta\to 0}\int\left(T_{k}(\rho^{\delta})(\rho^{\delta})^{2}\frac{\partial f^{\delta}}{\partial\rho^{\delta}}-\overline{T_{k}(\rho)}\overline{\rho^{2}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\rho}}\right)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\delta\to 0}\int\left((\rho^{\delta})^{2}\frac{\partial f^{\delta}}{\partial\rho^{\delta}}-\rho^{2}\frac{\partial f(\rho,c)}{\partial\rho}\right)\left(T_{k}(\rho^{\delta})-T_{k}(\rho)\right)

\displaystyle+\int\left(\overline{\rho^{2}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\rho}}-\rho^{2}\frac{\partial f(\rho,c)}{\partial\rho}\right)\left(T_{k}(\rho^{\delta})-T_{k}(\rho)\right)

\displaystyle\geq\lim_{\delta\to 0}\int\left((\rho^{\delta})^{2}\frac{\partial f^{\delta}}{\partial\rho^{\delta}}-\rho^{2}\frac{\partial f(\rho,c)}{\partial\rho}\right)\left(T_{k}(\rho^{\delta})-T_{k}(\rho)\right),

where the last inequality sign is due to the concavity of
T_{k}
and

\overline{\rho^{2}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\rho}}=(\gamma-1)\overline{\rho^{\gamma}}+\overline{\rho\ln\rho}H\geq(\gamma-1)\rho^{\gamma}+\rho\ln\rho H=\rho^{2}\frac{\partial f(\rho,c)}{\partial\rho}.

By the fact

((\rho^{\delta})^{\gamma}-\rho^{\gamma})(T_{k}(\rho^{\delta})-T_{k}(\rho))\geq(T_{k}(\rho^{\delta})-T_{k}(\rho))^{\gamma+1},

the last integral in (
6.9
) satisfies

\displaystyle\int\left((\rho^{\delta})^{2}\frac{\partial f^{\delta}}{\partial\rho^{\delta}}-\rho^{2}\frac{\partial f(\rho,c)}{\partial\rho}\right)\left(T_{k}(\rho^{\delta})-T_{k}(\rho)\right)

\displaystyle=\int(\gamma-1)((\rho^{\delta})^{\gamma}-\rho^{\gamma})(T_{k}(\rho^{\delta})-T_{k}(\rho))+(\rho^{\delta}-\rho)H\left(T_{k}(\rho^{\delta})-T_{k}(\rho)\right)

\displaystyle\geq\int(\gamma-1)(T_{k}(\rho^{\delta})-T_{k}(\rho))^{\gamma+1}+(\rho^{\delta}-\rho)H\left(T_{k}(\rho^{\delta})-T_{k}(\rho)\right).

This and (
6.9
) provide us

(6.10)

\displaystyle(2\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2})\lim_{\delta\to 0}\int(T_{k}(\rho^{\delta}){\rm div}{\bf u}^{\delta}-\overline{T_{k}(\rho)}{\rm div}{\bf u})\geq(\gamma-1)\lim_{\delta\to 0}\int(T_{k}(\rho^{\delta})-T_{k}(\rho))^{\gamma+1}.

Introduce

b_{k}(z):=L_{k}(z)-\left(\ln k+\int_{k}^{k+1}\frac{T_{k}(s)}{s^{2}}ds+1\right)z,

where

\displaystyle L_{k}(z):=\left\{\begin{aligned} &z\ln z,&&z\leq k,\\
&z\ln k+z\int_{k}^{z}\frac{T_{k}(s)}{s^{2}}ds,&&z\geq k.\end{aligned}\right.

We see that
b_{k}(z)\in C[0,\infty)\cap C^{1}(0,\infty)
,
b_{k}^{\prime}(z)=0
if
z\geq k+1
, and
b_{k}^{\prime}(z)z-b_{k}(z)=T_{k}(z)
. Then, from the mass equation we obtain (approximating
b_{k}(z)
near
z=0
)

{\rm div}(b_{k}(\rho^{\delta}){\bf u}^{\delta})+T_{k}(\rho^{\delta}){\rm div}{\bf u}^{\delta}=0
.
Consequently,

\displaystyle\int T_{k}(\rho^{\delta}){\rm div}{\bf u}^{\delta}=0.

Recalling the definition of
T_{k}
, we see that, for any fixed
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle T_{k}(\rho^{\delta})-\!\!\!\rightharpoonup\overline{T_{k}(\rho)}\quad{\rm in}\,\,L^{p}(\Omega),\quad\forall p\in[1,\infty].

Hence,

(6.11)

\displaystyle\int\overline{T_{k}(\rho)}\,{\rm div}{\bf u}=\lim_{\delta\to 0}\int T_{k}(\rho^{\delta}){\rm div}{\bf u}^{\delta}=0.

By the Hölder inequality, (
6.10
) and (
6.11
), we have

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} C\|T_{k}(\rho)-\overline{T_{k}(\rho)}\|_{L^{2}}&\geq(2\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2})\int\left(T_{k}(\rho)-\overline{T_{k}(\rho)}\right){\rm div}{\bf u}\\
&=(2\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2})\lim_{\delta\to 0}\int\left(T_{k}(\rho^{\delta}){\rm div}{\bf u}^{\delta}-\overline{T_{k}(\rho)}{\rm div}{\bf u}\right)\\
&\geq(\gamma-1)\lim_{\delta\to 0}\int(T_{k}(\rho^{\delta})-T_{k}(\rho))^{\gamma+1},\end{aligned}

and therefore,

(6.12)

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}\lim_{\delta\to 0}\|T_{k}(\rho)-T_{k}(\rho^{\delta})\|_{L^{\gamma+1}}^{\gamma+1}

\displaystyle\leq C\lim_{k\to\infty}\|T_{k}(\rho)-\overline{T_{k}(\rho)}\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq C\lim_{k\to\infty}\lim_{\delta\to 0}(\|T_{k}(\rho)-\rho\|_{L^{2}}+\|T_{k}(\rho^{\delta})-\rho^{\delta}\|_{L^{2}}).

However, by the uniform bound

\|\rho^{\delta}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq\|\rho^{\delta}\frac{\partial f^{\delta}}{\partial\rho^{\delta}}\|_{L^{s}}^{s}\leq C
,
one has

\displaystyle\|T_{k}(\rho^{\delta})-\rho^{\delta}\|_{L^{2}}=\|T_{k}(\rho^{\delta})-\rho^{\delta}\|_{L^{2}({\rho^{\delta}\geq k})}\leq 2\|\rho^{\delta}\|_{L^{2}(\rho^{\delta}\geq k)}\to 0,\quad{\rm as}\,\,k\to\infty.

Thus,

(6.13)

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}\lim_{\delta\to 0}(\|T_{k}(\rho)-\rho\|_{L^{2}}+\|T_{k}(\rho^{\delta})-\rho^{\delta}\|_{L^{2}})=0.

The combination of (
6.12
) with (
6.13
) guarantees

\displaystyle\lim_{\delta\to 0}\|\rho^{\delta}-\rho\|_{L^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{k\to\infty}\lim_{\delta\to 0}\left(\|\rho^{\delta}-T_{k}(\rho^{\delta})\|_{L^{1}}+\|T_{k}(\rho^{\delta})-T_{k}(\rho)\|_{L^{1}}+\|T_{k}(\rho)-\rho\|_{L^{1}}\right)

\displaystyle=0.
