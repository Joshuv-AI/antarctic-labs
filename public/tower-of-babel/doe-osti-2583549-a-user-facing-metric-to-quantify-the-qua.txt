Executive Summary of CRADA Work:

The leading urban mobility data analytics firm StreetLight Data, Inc. partnered with the National
Renewable Energy Laboratory to explore a commercial version of the Mobility Energy
Productivity (MEP) metric. The commercialization effort was aimed to expand the adoption of
the metric to key stakeholders in the urban planning space. Research comprised industry analysis,
stakeholder feedback and conducting transportation practitioner focus groups. StreetLight
concluded that commercialization of MEP is not feasible in the current market because users
need a dynamic MEP tool that enables scenario planning. It should be able to calculate a MEP
score dynamically (near instantaneous) when different inputs are changed.

 1.7 Preliminary results and prototype demonstrations to two cities

In the first phase of the research, StreetLight and NREL partnered to establish a steering
committee, come to agreements for sharing data, and begin technical integration of StreetLight
provided data to power a MEP score in two cities for demonstration. Initial exploration showed
some discrepancies in the StreetLight provided OpenStreetMap (OSM) network that needs to be
resolved for calculating MEP scores (OpenStreetMap contributors, 2024). An OSM-specific
routing extension (called OSMnx) was used to resolve this issue (Boeing, 2024). Calculated
MEP scores for Tulsa, OK, and Orlando, FL were then demonstrated using OSM networks and
existing MEP data sources. Next, the MEP team needed to demonstrate the replacement of
proprietary CoStar land use data with OSM point of interest (POI) data. Once OSM POI and
network data were integrated to replace third party data sources, travel speed data form
StreetLight could be integrated to demonstrate StreetLight powered MEP scores with all other
data sources being open source. To ensure this approach was valid, the team compared new MEP
scores in Tulsa and Orlando to previous calculations that utilized proprietary datasets.
Correlations between baseline and StreetLight with OSM scores were strong: Tulsa correlation
0.95 for Drive mode, Orlando correlation for Drive mode 0.80). This successfully demonstrated
MEP calculations could be done with open-source data (from OSM) with StreetLight powered
insights (driving speed data).

In the second phase of research, NREL provided StreetLight with access to an online version of
the visualization dashboard. The team collected feedback and refined the dashboard based on

 4

feedback from StreetLight as well feedback from steering committee members. Next, the project
team discussed with StreetLight to identify participants for the focus groups. Two focus groups
were set up and conducted. The first group comprised of two industry practitioners. The second
focus group was comprised of three public sector practitioners. The biggest feedback from these
focus groups was the need for customizability in the dashboard (and the metric itself) is highly
desirable. There was also feedback that interpretation of the MEP metric could be a difficult
hurdle for industry decision makers. Other feedback included interests in the role of land use on
MEP and the role of emissions. After discussing feedback with StreetLight, it was determined
the biggest need for a commercial MEP tool (and dashboard) would be a scenario analysis tool.
As a result, the dashboard development focused heavily on creating a prototype scenario analysis
tool.

 3.5 CRADA Final Report

In the final phase of the project, final MEP scores for Tulsa and Orlando were integrated into a
prototype dashboard and shared with stakeholders from those regions. A new feature was
developed to integrate computing MEP on-the-fly for non-time based changes (i.e., changes to
energy use or cost). To demonstrate this feature, the team chose a vehicle fleet energy efficiency
scenario that could be manipulated by users in the dashboard. The prototype dashboard tool
enabled this by changing the projected vehicle fleet energy efficiency composition and
recomputed the MEP scores based on a different energy intensity of the vehicle fleet. Figure 1
shows a snapshot of the MEP prototype dashboard with MEP scores for Tulsa loaded.
After StreetLight demonstrated the prototype dashboard to additional stakeholders they
identified, StreetLight decided that it pursuing commercialization further did not make sense.
The reasons StreetLight listed were due to technically complex hurdles to scaling on-fly-
computations, the difficulty of creating flexible and scalable scenario analyses tools (e.g., editing
networks), and challenges with effectively communicating what MEP scores represent. As a
result, and to wrap up the project, the NREL team demonstrated additional MEP scores with
OSM data (using posted speed limits in place of StreetLight data) in additional cities. This
demonstrated the ability of the MEP metric to be powered by open source data and be ready for
future integration with partners such that data and licensing issues would not be a future issue.
