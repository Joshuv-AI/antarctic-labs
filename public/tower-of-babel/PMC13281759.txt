Recent Advances in the Study of Traditional Chinese Medicine in Reversing Hepatic Fibrosis Through the Regulation of Hepatic Stellate Cells Apoptosis Pathways
Authors: Ruihan Cheng, Baolong Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHepatic fibrosis is a pathological manifestation in which hepatic stellate cells are activated by inflammatory stimuli and produce excessive extracellular matrix, leading to excessive intrahepatic collagen deposition. As the core effector cells of hepatic fibrosis, hepatic stellate cells have multiple cellular states, including quiescent, activated, and quiescence-like dysfunctional states. In hepatic fibrosis, they can exert functions such as pro-inflammatory effects, strong proliferation, maintenance of permanent activation state, and resistance to cell death by activating multiple relevant signaling pathways under their different states. Therefore, inducing multiple death pathways in hepatic stellate cells and restoring their dysfunctional, quiescent, and senescent states are crucial for inhibiting hepatic fibrosis. The active ingredients and compound formulations of traditional Chinese medicine have advantages such as multi-pathway effects and low metabolic burden in the treatment of hepatic fibrosis, and significant progress has been made in studies on regulating hepatic stellate cells death and reversing hepatic fibrosis, though challenges remain such as identifying specific active components, clarifying mechanisms, and improving experimental models to better simulate the liver’s multicellular environment.

Introduction

Hepatic fibrosis (HF) is a pathological change resulting from excessive deposition of extracellular matrix (ECM) following the activation of quiescent hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) in response to an inflammatory environment within the liver. As a critical pathological process, HF commonly progresses in nearly all liver diseases and serves as a major precursor to complications such as cirrhosis and liver failure. According to an incomplete statistical report from 2024, the prevalence of advanced HF in the general global population is approximately 3.3% (ranging from 2.4% to 4.2%), which places a substantial burden on public health worldwide.1 Importantly, HF is now recognized as a potentially reversible process, which provides a crucial therapeutic window and underscores the significance of targeting its core mechanisms.

Currently, no well-defined targeted therapies are available for hepatic fibrosis (HF), and some existing treatments may increase the metabolic burden on the liver. In contrast, traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) has a long history of clinical application in liver diseases, including fibrotic conditions, and offers advantages such as multi-target regulation. For example, Yinchenhao (Artemisia capillaris) has been widely used to improve liver function.

Hepatic stellate cells (HSCs), as the key effector cells in HF, are characterized by rapid proliferation and resistance to apoptosis. Their activation involves multiple signaling pathways, including TGF-β/Smad and TLR4/NF-κB, which promote intercellular crosstalk and accelerate fibrotic progression. Therefore, inducing HSC apoptosis, inhibiting their activation, and restoring their quiescent phenotype have emerged as central therapeutic strategies for reversing HF and limiting disease progression.

This review systematically summarizes the regulatory effects of TCM on HSCs proliferation, activation, and apoptosis through relevant signaling pathways and molecular targets. It focuses on how active compounds and compound formulations of TCM modulate key pathways, including TGF-β/Smad and TLR4/NF-κB, as well as cell death processes such as apoptosis, necroptosis, autophagy, and ferroptosis in HSCs. Furthermore, this review evaluates the therapeutic potential of TCM interventions by integrating mechanistic evidence with their historical clinical applications, thereby providing a more targeted theoretical basis and novel insights for future research on the use of TCM in the treatment of HF.

Different States of Hepatic Stellate Cells in Hepatic Fibrosis

Quiescent State - Hepatic Stellate Cells

HSCs are non-parenchymal cells located between the liver sinusoidal endothelial cells and hepatocytes. Under normal conditions, they constitute approximately 10–15% of the total liver cell population and exhibit a quiescent, non-proliferative phenotype (quiescent hepatic stellate cells, qHSCs). Their main function is to store retinoids, predominantly in the form of retinyl esters, within specialized lipid droplets (LDs) in the cytoplasm.2 This role is considered to contribute to the maintenance of ECM homeostasis and hepatocyte proliferation, thereby supporting liver physiological functions.

Activated State - Hepatic Stellate Cells

When stimuli reach the liver, hepatocytes are initially damaged, producing damage-associated molecular patterns.3 These factors, along with macrophages, activate inflammatory signaling pathways, such as TLR4/NF-κB, leading to the release of inflammatory mediators, including tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), transforming growth factor-β1 (TGF-β1), and activate Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4), thereby establishing an initial inflammatory environment. Subsequently, these factors act together with chemokines released by macrophages,4 inducing the migration of qHSCs toward the site of injury. Once there, qHSCs consume LDs for energy, initiating their activation and transforming them into activated hepatic stellate cells (aHSCs). aHSCs undergo a phenotypic transition toward myofibroblast-like cells and begin to secrete ECM components, such as α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA). aHSCs continuously produce inflammatory factors, amplifying the inflammatory response, while also exhibiting strong proliferation and anti-apoptotic characteristics. This leads to a positive feedback loop of “HSCs activation - proliferation - fibrosis,” marking the official onset of HF.

Activation and Proliferation: TGF-β/Smad and PDGF

The TGF-β/Smad signaling pathway is one of the central pathways in the study of fibrosis in various organs. During liver injury, large amounts of TGF-β1 are secreted, and TGF-β1 serves as a key activator of HSCs. It acts as an activator in in vitro experiments, binding to receptors on the surface of HSCs and promoting the phosphorylation of Smad2/3 proteins intracellularly, thereby driving the transformation of qHSCs into aHSCs and initiating HF.5 Simultaneously, the liver undergoes vascular repair to restore blood flow. Repair factors, such as platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), interact with receptors on the surface of HSCs,6 including PDGF receptor β,5 which not only activates HSCs but also stimulates their proliferation and enhances their profibrotic capacity.

Anti-Apoptosis and Inflammation Amplification: TLR4/NF-κB/BCL-2/Caspase Signaling Pathway

Inflammatory factors, such as TNF-α and members of the interleukin family (ILs), bind to receptors on the surface of qHSCs, leading to the sequential activation of intracellular nuclear factor kappa-B (NF-κB). Once activated, NF-κB translocates to the nucleus to regulate transcription, upregulating anti-apoptotic genes such as B-cell lymphoma-2 (Bcl-2), and modulating cysteine-aspartate proteases (Caspases), which control the execution of apoptosis. This results in aHSCs acquiring an anti-apoptotic phenotype and undergoing positive feedback to secrete inflammatory factors, thereby amplifying the inflammatory response and accelerating HF progression. Studies have shown that increased NF-κB expression is positively correlated with Bcl-2 expression, leading to a significant decrease in aHSCs apoptosis.7 Conversely, when NF-κB expression is inhibited, aHSCs exhibit features such as nuclear condensation and fragmentation, with a marked increase in the apoptosis rate, which can reverse HF. Apoptosis of Hepatic Stellate Cells

Apoptosis and Necroptosis

Apoptosis

Apoptosis is a spontaneous, programmed form of cell death that accounts for a small proportion of HSCs death under physiological conditions. However, when the liver is damaged, both intrinsic and extrinsic apoptosis pathways in HSCs are activated and enhanced. Common extrinsic apoptosis activators include TNF-α and apoptotic ligands. Upon binding to their respective receptors, these factors form complex I, which then undergoes membrane dissociation and interacts with cytoplasmic death receptor domains, caspase 8, and receptor-interacting serine/threonine-protein kinase 1 (RIPK1) to form complex II.8 This complex subsequently recognizes the final apoptotic effector signal, caspase-3/7, initiating the caspase cascade and activating the apoptotic pathway.9 These caspases bind to various intracellular substrates, leading to cell shrinkage, chromatin fragmentation, cell membrane blebbing, and ultimately the formation of apoptotic bodies. In contrast, intrinsic apoptosis primarily occurs via the mitochondrial pathway. When cells experience damage, such as DNA injury or reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation-induced oxidative stress, the outer mitochondrial membrane is affected, resulting in structural damage. This allows the release of factors such as cytochrome c, which activates the mitochondrial apoptosis pathway via caspase-9. The release of additional cytochrome c further amplifies apoptosis, continuously inducing apoptosis in aHSCs.

Necroptosis

Necroptosis is a novel form of cell death that combines the “cell membrane rupture” characteristics of necrosis with programmed regulatory features of apoptosis. It primarily occurs in organs with high levels of inflammation. As liver injury is characterized by persistent inflammation, the concentration of inflammatory mediators constantly increases, leading to continuous necroptosis in HSCs. Similar to the extrinsic apoptosis pathway, necroptosis involves the formation of complex I, which subsequently dissociates from the membrane to form complex II. However, during necroptosis, RIPK1 interacts with RIPK3, leading to its phosphorylation and activation. Activated RIPK3 then specifically binds to mixed lineage kinase domain-like protein (MLKL), forming a new RIPK1/RIPK3/MLKL complex, which initiates the necroptotic process.10,11 Multiple studies have shown that RIPK3 and MLKL are core factors in necroptosis, and they play a key role in regulating mitochondrial metabolic pathways. Research has found12 that mitochondrial ROS guide pore-forming proteins to bind to the mitochondrial membrane, forming mitochondrial pores that release ROS, thereby promoting the transition of HSCs to RIPK3/MLKL-dependent necroptosis and inducing the expression of a necroptotic state in HSCs.

Autophagy: The AMPK/mTOR Lipophagy Axis

HSCs contain a large number of LDs, and during activation and transdifferentiation into myofibroblasts, they utilize the autophagy pathway to degrade LDs for energy production, a process termed lipophagy in HSCs. Autophagy is a form of cellular self-digestion in which lysosomes degrade intracellular metabolites, thereby maintaining the homeostasis of the liver microenvironment. The regulation of autophagy in HSCs is stage-specific and bidirectional. In the early stages of HF, lipophagy promotes the degradation of LDs in qHSCs, consuming vitamin A-like substances. The disappearance of LDs serves as a key marker for distinguishing HSCs activation states. However, as HF progresses, the metabolic products generated by lipophagy may exceed physiological thresholds, leading to the accumulation of ROS, ferrous ions (Fe2⁺), and other markers in mitochondria, which trigger various forms of HSCs death and inhibit HF progression.13

Lipophagy is initiated under the activation of the major energy regulation pathway involving AMPK/mTOR. AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) is a key molecule that regulates cellular energy metabolism and controls processes such as glucose and lipid metabolism. It is activated in response to changes in ATP levels and promotes autophagy. In contrast, the mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) inhibits autophagy. Upon pathway activation, the activation of the UNC-51-like kinase 1 (ULK1) complex is upregulated, recruiting autophagy-related protein 9 (ATG9) to the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) region. This activates the class III phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3KIII) complex, facilitating the nucleation of new autophagosomes or autophagic vesicles. These vesicles then detach from the ER surface, increasing the production of phosphatidylinositol-3-phosphate (PI3P) and initiating the recruitment of proteins to the autophagic vesicles. Subsequently, microtubule-associated protein 1 light chain 3 (LC3) interacts with autophagic vesicles and is converted to membrane-bound LC3-II, leading to the fusion of autophagosomes with lysosomes to form autolysosomes. This enables the degradation and utilization of LDs, generating ATP to supply energy for HSCs activation while also accumulating metabolic waste in the mitochondria and cytosol.14 Studies have shown that when the expression of AMPK/mTOR in aHSCs is inhibited, the release of growth factors, such as PDGF, decreases, and the activation and proliferation of aHSCs are restricted, thereby alleviating HF.15 In conclusion, the suppression of lipophagy in aHSCs can limit the energy supply for HSCs activation, reduce the number of activated cells, and help relieve HF.

Ferroptosis: Iron Metabolism-Mitochondrial Dysfunction

The liver is a key organ in iron metabolism and has one of the highest mitochondrial contents. Ferroptosis is a distinct form of regulated cell death, often triggered by pathways such as iron metabolic disorders, lipid peroxidation imbalances, and mitochondrial dysfunction in aHSCs. Nuclear receptor coactivator 4 is a key protein in ferroptosis that is capable of binding to ferritin. The LC3-interacting region of NCOA4 interacts with LC3 on the autophagosome membrane, leading to the degradation of ferritin and the release of large amounts of Fe2⁺, causing “iron pool overload.” This results in the accumulation of ROS and the formation of lipid hydroperoxide (LOOH). Normally, intracellular glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) clears LOOH by utilizing glutathione to reduce LOOH to non-toxic lipid alcohols. However, the continuous generation of Fe2⁺ leads to an accelerated rate of LOOH formation, far exceeding GPX4’s clearance capacity. Additionally, Fe2⁺ inhibits the activity of GPX4, weakening its antioxidant function and promoting uncontrolled lipid peroxidation, ultimately inducing ferroptosis in aHSCs and alleviating HF.16

Dysfunction, Quiescence-Like, and Senescent States

aHSC dysfunction refers to the failure of aHSCs to differentiate or to revert to their quiescent or senescent states, which is another way to limit the function of aHSCs, apart from inducing their death. However, the specific protein targets and regulatory pathways involved remain unclear, and most studies have focused on transcription factors and genes. Studies have shown that pre-B-cell leukemia transcription factor 1 (PBX1) and interleukin-7 receptor (IL-7R) are key transcription factors in HSCs activation. Reducing their expression can inhibit the transdifferentiation of HSCs into myofibroblasts and alleviate the severity of HF.17 Meanwhile, Brahma-associated gene 1 (BAG1) showed significantly increased expression during HSCs differentiation. When its expression is suppressed, there is a marked improvement in HF in mice and the differentiation status of aHSCs.18 These experimental findings suggest that the dysfunction of aHSCs differentiation and the restoration of quiescent or senescent-like states may represent a promising therapeutic approach for reversing HF.

In summary, although the modes of HSCs death differ, they often interact with each other. Autophagy and apoptosis are the main forms of HSCs death. Autophagy occurs throughout this process, promoting HSCs activation in the early stages. However, as activation progresses, autophagy can trigger ferroptosis. The ROS produced during this process continue to induce mitochondrial damage. Mitochondria are the primary sites for apoptosis initiation, and the activation of the apoptotic program leads to the leakage of cellular contents, which in turn promotes autophagy. Traditional Chinese Medicine Prevents and Treats Hepatic Fibrosis by Targeting Hepatic Stellate Cells

Active Ingredients from Traditional Chinese Medicine Regulate Hepatic Fibrosis via HSCs

Flavonoids

Ginkgetin is a biflavonoid compound extracted from Yinxing (Ginkgo biloba leaves) that exhibits anti-inflammatory and lipid metabolism-regulating properties. Wang19 conducted studies on apoptosis in human HSCs (LX-2) and primary mouse HSCs, and found that ginkgetin significantly reduced HSCs viability, inhibited the expression of α-SMA and Bcl-2, and induced apoptosis in a dose-dependent manner, suggesting that it may alleviate HF by promoting HSCs apoptosis and decreasing ECM deposition. Luteolin, a key bioactive component in Jinyinhua (Lonicera japonica) and Huangqin (Scutellaria baicalensis), was applied in studies of HF in mice and HSCs autophagy. The results showed a dramatic reduction in autophagosome numbers in mouse livers and HSCs, along with suppressed HSCs activation and decreased expression of ECM components, leading to reduced HF severity, indicating that luteolin may reverse HF by promoting autophagy in HSCs.20 Zhou21 performed in vitro and in vivo combined studies on the iron metabolism model of HSCs and observed that after treatment with total flavonoids of Sophora flavescens, the proliferation and survival rates of HSCs were significantly reduced, along with lower levels of ROS and transferrin receptors in the liver and aHSCs. Collagen deposition was also markedly decreased, suggesting that total flavonoids of Sophora flavescens may reverse HF by modulating HSCs ferroptosis and oxidative stress.

Terpenoids

Curcumol is a sesquiterpene active ingredient derived from Ezhu (Curcuma aromatica), with known anti-inflammatory and hepatoprotective effects. Curcumol suppressed HSCs-T6 proliferation through inducing autophagy and ferroptosis,22 downregulated Bcl-2 and α-SMA levels, and increased caspase 3 expression. Simultaneously, the mitochondria of HSCs-T6 showed significant swelling, disruption of cristae, decreased mitochondrial membrane potential, increased autophagosome formation, and enhanced apoptosis rates, indicating that curcumol may reverse HF by inducing mitochondrial autophagy and promoting aHSCs death. Perillaldehyde, an active constituent of Perilla frutescens var. crassipes, exhibits anti-inflammatory and choleretic activities. When applied in studies of HF in mice and activated LX-2 cells, it significantly reduced collagen accumulation in mouse liver tissue, suppressed the migratory and proliferative capacities of LX-2, and inhibited Smad2/3 expression, suggesting that perillaldehyde may reverse HF by modulating the TGF-β/Smad signaling pathway and inhibiting HSCs activation and proliferation.23 Astragaloside IV is an important terpenoid active ingredient in Huangqi (Astragalus membranaceus) and possesses various pharmacological activities, including anti-inflammatory and anti-fibrotic effects. Tian24 demonstrated in an HSCs-T6 oxidative stress model that Astragaloside IV could decrease the activation and survival rates of HSCs-T6, inhibit ECM secretion and the expression of ROS-related proteins, indicating that Astragaloside IV may alleviate HF by regulating oxidative stress, suppressing HSCs activation, and promoting their death.

Other Components

Huangjing (Polygonatum sibirica) lowers serum lipid levels and treats chronic hepatitis. Polysaccharides are considered key bioactive components.25 Shen26 evaluated the PDGF-induced apoptosis model of LX-2 cells and observed a decreased growth area of LX-2 cells, reduced expression of ECM components and anti-apoptotic proteins, and increased levels of caspase3/8/9, suggesting that polysaccharides from Polygonatum sibirica may promote aHSCs apoptosis and alleviate HF. Holarrhena, a coumarin compound extracted from Baizhi (Angelica dahurica), exhibits anti-inflammatory and antifibrotic properties. When used in studies of TGF-β1-induced LX-2 activation, it significantly reduced the survival and proliferation of LX-2 cells, induced their transition to a quiescent-like state, and suppressed the expression of α-SMA and PDGF receptors, indicating that inhibition of HSCs activation and proliferation may alleviate HF.26 These studies suggest that TCM components primarily alleviate HF by reducing inflammation, inhibiting the activation and proliferation of aHSCs, and inducing various forms of cell death. Traditional Chinese Medicine Compound Formulas Regulate Hepatic Fibrosis via Hepatic Stellate Cells

Qi Shen Tang

The composition of Qi Shen Tang includes Huangqi (Radix Astragali), Dangshen (Radix Codonopsis) or Renshen (Radix Ginseng), Baizhu (Rhizoma Atractylodis Macrocephalae), Fuling (Poria), Danggui (Radix Angelicae Sinensis), Chuanxiong (Rhizoma Chuanxiong), Danshen (Radix Salviae Miltiorrhizae), Chishao (Radix Paeoniae Rubra), and Gancao (Radix Glycyrrhizae). Qi Shen Tang is an empirical formula used in clinical treatment for HF, and it has demonstrated unique advantages in the field of HF therapy. Yang34 found in a study involving the co-culture of dendritic cells, HSCs, and CD4+ T helper lymphocytes that Qi Shen Tang suppressed NF-κB expression, decreased the percentage of positive cells with dendritic cell phenotypes, and significantly increased the expression of CD4+ T helper lymphocytes. This led to an increase in the apoptosis rate of aHSCs and a reduction in ECM deposition, suggesting that Qi Shen Tang may reverse HF by inhibiting dendritic cell maturation and differentiation, regulating immune balance, suppressing inflammatory responses, and inhibiting aHSCs proliferation and activation.

Qing Gan Yi Pi Fang

The composition of Qing Gan Yi Pi Fang includes Chaihu (Radix Bupleuri), Baishao (Radix Paeoniae Alba), Danggui (Radix Angelicae Sinensis), Baizhu (Rhizoma Atractylodis Macrocephalae), Fuling (Poria), Gancao (Radix Glycyrrhizae), Bohe (Herba Menthae, added later), Zhizi (Fructus Gardeniae). Qing Gan Yi Pi Fang is known for its effects of clearing heat and toxins, enhancing immunity, and has been found to effectively improve HF in clinical applications. Liu,35 through network pharmacology screening, discovered in in vitro and in vivo studies on HSCs-T6 cells that this formula could inhibit the activation of the TLR4/NF-κB pathway in rat livers and aHSCs, alleviate liver inflammatory infiltration, and reduce the proliferation and activation rates of aHSCs, indicating that Qing Gan Yi Pi Fang may reverse HF by suppressing the TLR4/NF-κB signaling pathway in the liver, reducing inflammatory infiltration and HSCs activation, and decreasing ECM secretion.

Jia Wei Tao He Cheng Qi Tang

The composition of Jia Wei Tao He Cheng Qi Tang includes Taoren (Semen Persicae), Dahuang (Radix et Rhizoma Rhei), Guizhi (Ramulus Cinnamomi), Mangxiao (Natrii Sulfas), and Gancao (Radix Glycyrrhizae), with additional herbs such as Danshen (Radix Salviae Miltiorrhizae), Chishao (Radix Paeoniae Rubra), Niuxi (Radix Achyranthis Bidentatae), and Chuanxiong (Rhizoma Chuanxiong). Jia Wei Tao he Cheng Qi Tang is a classical TCM formula with functions such as breaking up stasis, regulating qi, and eliminating accumulation. It is commonly used for the clinical treatment of HF and cirrhosis. When applied in combined in vitro and in vivo studies of HF models,36 it was observed that ECM deposition in the liver was alleviated, whereas the expression of autophagy-related proteins such as peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor (PPAR) and LC3 was suppressed. Additionally, the degradation of LDs in HSCs decreased, and the activation rate of HSCs was reduced, suggesting that this formula may reverse HF by affecting HSCs autophagy, lowering lipid accumulation, and inhibiting HSCs activation and proliferation.

However, a critical appraisal of this body of evidence reveals several key challenges. Firstly, the target specificity of many TCM compounds remains a major concern. Pathways like NF-κB and TGF-β/Smad, frequently reported as targets, are ubiquitous regulators of inflammation and fibrosis across multiple cell types and organs. The observed effects on HSCs may therefore be part of broader, less specific actions, raising questions about potential off-target effects. Secondly, the poor oral bioavailability and unfavorable pharmacokinetic profiles of many natural products, such as flavonoids and terpenoids, present significant hurdles for their translation into viable drugs. Most studies cited rely on in vitro models or direct intraperitoneal injection in animals, which do not adequately address absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion (ADME) challenges. Finally, the methodological rigor of many cited studies varies. While promising, findings from single-cell line experiments (eg., LX-2, HSC-T6) in monoculture may not fully recapitulate the complex multicellular microenvironment of the fibrotic liver. Therefore, moving forward, it is imperative to shift the research paradigm from a descriptive listing of observed effects to a more discerning evaluation framework. This framework should assess compounds not only for their efficacy in simplistic models but also for their mechanistic plausibility within the in vivo context, drug-like properties, and evidence from more physiologically relevant systems. It is crucial to acknowledge that the multi-component, multi-target nature of TCM means that different formulas and compounds are suited for different disease stages and patient constitutions, aligning with the TCM principle of syndrome differentiation and treatment. The goal is not to crown a single “best” compound, but to systematically identify the most appropriate therapeutic strategies—specific compounds or formulas—for defined pathological contexts, thereby bridging traditional wisdom with modern drug development criteria.

Discussion and Prospects

HSCs are the core effector cells in HF development. Intervention in their activation, secretion, and death pathways is currently the main research direction for reversing HF. TCM has been widely applied in the treatment of HF. It can regulate complex signaling pathways to prevent HSC-mediated multicellular crosstalk and fibrotic functions, while also alleviating liver metabolic stress to reverse HF. However, there are still limitations in TCM research on HSCs. First, due to the characteristic of TCM— “clinical use first, research later”—most studies lack clear identification of active components, mechanisms, and targets, as well as safety evaluation of the drugs. Second, HSCs play a complex role, but previous studies often had insufficient experimental design, failing to simulate the multicellular environment within the liver. Most research has focused on single in vitro experiments related to HSCs death, with superficial choices of targets and cell death pathways, and primarily aimed at drug efficacy validation rather than exploratory studies. However, recent trends in HSCs research have shown a shift toward multicellular co-culture systems and combined in vivo and in vitro validation.

To bridge the gap between promising preclinical findings and clinical translation, future research must adopt a more rigorous and integrated approach. Firstly, there is an urgent need to move beyond simplistic monoculture models. The adoption of more physiologically relevant systems, such as precision-cut liver slices (PCLS) and advanced multicellular co-culture systems that incorporate hepatocytes, Kupffer cells, and sinusoidal endothelial cells alongside HSCs, is critical to better mimic the liver’s microenvironment and assess compound efficacy and specificity. Secondly, for the most promising lead compounds identified (eg., those with multi-pathway efficacy like curcumol or luteolin), rigorous pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic (PK/PD) studies are essential. This includes detailed investigations into oral bioavailability, tissue distribution, metabolic stability, and potential toxicity. Thirdly, the integration of network pharmacology should evolve from a screening tool to a hypothesis-generating framework that is systematically validated. Predictions of multi-target interactions must be followed by experimental confirmation using techniques like CRISPR-Cas9 knockouts, specific pathway inhibitors, and spatial transcriptomics to deconvolute precise mechanisms of action. The ultimate goal is to leverage a deep mechanistic understanding of how TCM compounds modulate HSCs to rationally design evidence-based therapies. This involves identifying compounds with not only high efficacy but also favorable drug-like properties, thereby transforming traditional herbal knowledge into targeted, safe, and effective anti-fibrotic drugs.

In summary, HSCs are key target cells for reversing HF. Further in-depth exploration of their functions, combined with the unique advantages of TCM in treating HF, will provide a theoretical basis for developing new drugs and effective therapeutic strategies for HF.
