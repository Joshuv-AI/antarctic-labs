Comparison of obstetric, maternal, and neonatal outcomes between preterm prelabor rupture of membranes at 34 + 0–36 + 6 weeks and term prelabor rupture of membranes at ≥ 37 weeks
Authors: Orhan Ay, Pelin Bahçeci, Deniz Taşkıran, Kazım Gezginç
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTo compare preterm prelabor rupture of membranes (PPROM) at 34 + 0–36 + 6 weeks with term prelabor rupture of membranes at ≥ 37 weeks in terms of obstetric outcomes, maternal morbidity, and short-term neonatal outcomes.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included 646 singleton pregnancies diagnosed with PROM at 34 + 0 weeks or later in a tertiary care center between January 1, 2018 and July 30, 2025. Cases were grouped as PPROM at 34 + 0–36 + 6 weeks (n = 334) and term PROM at ≥ 37 weeks (n = 312). Maternal, obstetric, and neonatal outcomes were compared. Logistic regression was used to identify factors associated with NICU admission and the respiratory neonatal morbidity composite.ResultsCompared with term PROM, the 34 + 0–36 + 6 weeks PPROM group had longer antibiotic use, higher rates of tocolysis and antenatal corticosteroid administration and longer latency. Infectious maternal morbidity did not differ significantly between groups. However, PPROM at 34 + 0–36 + 6 weeks was associated with lower birth weight, a higher rate of NICU admission (25.7% vs. 6.7%, p < 0.001), and a higher rate of the respiratory neonatal morbidity composite (14.5% vs. 3.5%, p < 0.001). In multivariable analysis, PPROM at 34 + 0–36 + 6 weeks remained independently associated with NICU admission (aOR 5.05, 95% CI 2.90–8.76) and respiratory neonatal morbidity composite (aOR 5.91, 95% CI 2.82–12.37).ConclusionsPPROM at 34 + 0–36 + 6 weeks appears to be a distinct clinical entity rather than simply an earlier form of term PROM. Despite similar maternal infectious morbidity, its substantially higher neonatal morbidity burden supports individualized management and consideration of expectant management in carefully selected cases.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12884-026-09511-5.

Background

Prelabor rupture of membranes is defined as rupture of the fetal membranes before the onset of labor [1]. Occurrence before 37 weeks of gestation is termed preterm prelabor rupture of membranes (PPROM), whereas occurrence at or beyond 37 weeks of gestation is referred to as term PROM [2]. This distinction is not merely terminological, as the risk of maternal infection, prematurity-related neonatal morbidity, timing of delivery, and obstetric management strategies vary substantially according to gestational age [1, 2].

PPROM accounts for approximately 2–4% of all pregnancies and is responsible for a substantial proportion of preterm births. In contrast, term PROM is more common and affects approximately 8% of pregnant women. In PPROM cases, a considerable proportion of adverse neonatal outcomes is related to prematurity, whereas in term PROM the main clinical concern is generally the risk of maternal and neonatal infection associated with a prolonged latent period [1, 2].

The clinical significance of PPROM is not limited to the risk of preterm delivery alone. This condition is associated with numerous serious maternal and neonatal outcomes, including chorioamnionitis, placental abruption, cord prolapse, neonatal sepsis, respiratory distress, need for intensive care, and perinatal mortality. In particular, as gestational age decreases, prematurity-related neonatal morbidity increases, whereas any decision to prolong pregnancy must be balanced against maternal risks such as infection and abruption.

In term PROM, however, the evidence base is relatively clearer. The landmark TERMPROM trial demonstrated that induction of labor and expectant management were associated with similar rates of neonatal infection and cesarean delivery, whereas induction particularly with oxytocin was associated with a lower risk of maternal infection [3]. Subsequent Cochrane reviews and more recent meta-analyses have reported that, in term or near-term PROM, early initiation of delivery may confer advantages with respect to outcomes such as chorioamnionitis, endometritis, neonatal sepsis, and NICU admission, and that these benefits can be achieved without a substantial increase in the cesarean delivery rate [4, 5].

In contrast, the late preterm period (34 + 0–36 + 6 weeks) represents one of the most controversial thresholds in clinical management. At these gestational ages, prolongation of intrauterine residence is expected to enhance fetal maturation and reduce respiratory morbidity; however, the risks of infection and sudden obstetric complications persist. Therefore, in PPROM cases at 34 + 0–36 + 6 weeks, the balance between immediate delivery and expectant management constitutes a more complex clinical decision-making area than in term PROM at ≥ 37 weeks [1, 2, 6].

Current guidelines also indicate that this issue remains incompletely resolved. While ACOG states that expectant management may be considered in cases of PPROM at 34 + 0–36 + 6 weeks in the absence of maternal or fetal contraindications, the RCOG recommendation offers a more nuanced approach with respect to antenatal corticosteroid use, timing of delivery, and details of surveillance [2, 7]. Similarly, the Canadian JOGC guideline emphasizes that decisions regarding diagnosis, antibiotic use, corticosteroid administration, timing of delivery, and mode of delivery should be individualized according to gestational age and the clinical context [1]. In addition, a systematic review of clinical guidelines published in 2025 clearly demonstrated that there is no international consensus on several key aspects of PPROM management, particularly antibiotic selection, the use of corticosteroids in the late preterm period, and the timing of delivery [8].

One important reason for this heterogeneity in management is that much of the existing literature has examined either term PROM in isolation or PPROM across broad gestational age ranges. However, the boundary between 34 + 0–36 + 6 weeks and ≥ 37 weeks represents a true clinical threshold that alters the direction of management decisions. Below this threshold, prematurity-related neonatal risks remain highly relevant, whereas above it, expediting delivery to reduce the risk of infection becomes a more prominent consideration [1, 2, 6]. Therefore, a direct comparison between PPROM at 34 + 0–36 + 6 weeks and term PROM within the same clinical center and using similar data fields may provide a clearer understanding of the differences in obstetric course, maternal morbidity, and neonatal outcomes.

In light of these data, evaluating PPROM at 34 + 0–36 + 6 weeks and term PROM within the same framework is important not only for understanding the difference in gestational age itself, but also for clarifying how this difference is reflected in obstetric management, maternal infectious morbidity, and neonatal adaptation. However, studies directly comparing these two clinical groups are limited, and there is a need for analyses based on tertiary care center data that simultaneously address maternal, obstetric, and neonatal outcomes. Therefore, this study aimed to directly compare cases of PPROM at 34 + 0–36 + 6 weeks and term PROM at ≥ 37 weeks with respect to obstetric outcomes, maternal morbidity, and short-term neonatal outcomes.

Methods

Study design and study setting

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at the Department of Obstetrics and Gynecology of a single tertiary-care university hospital. Electronic medical records of women who were followed with a diagnosis of prelabor rupture of membranes after 34 + 0 weeks of gestation and delivered at the same center were reviewed retrospectively. The study was planned based on the comparison of two clinical groups: women who developed preterm prelabor rupture of membranes between 34 + 0 and 36 + 6 weeks of gestation and women who developed term prelabor rupture of membranes at 37 + 0 weeks of gestation or beyond.

Study population

The study population consisted of women diagnosed with PROM at 34 + 0 weeks of gestation or later at our center between January 1, 2018 and July 30, 2025. During this period, a total of 14,248 deliveries occurred at our institution. Among these, 815 pregnancies met the diagnosis of PROM at ≥ 34 + 0 weeks of gestation and were retrospectively screened for eligibility. Of these, 169 cases were excluded because of multiple pregnancy (n = 61), major fetal or chromosomal anomaly (n = 28), or missing primary outcome data (n = 80). The final analysis was performed on 646 singleton pregnancies, including 334 cases in the PPROM group at 34 + 0–36 + 6 weeks and 312 cases in the term PROM group at ≥ 37 weeks (Fig. 1).

Definitions

Gestational age was based primarily on first-trimester crown-rump length measurement, when available. Otherwise, a reliable last menstrual period supported by the earliest obstetric ultrasound was used. In cases of discrepancy, the gestational age documented in the obstetric record was accepted for analysis.

Because of the retrospective design of the study, the diagnosis of PROM/PPROM was evaluated based on documentation in medical records, obstetric examination notes, and delivery records. PROM/PPROM was considered present when the records documented a history of sudden or continuous vaginal fluid leakage before the onset of labor and when this history was confirmed by supportive clinical findings. These supportive findings included visualization of amniotic fluid pooling in the posterior vaginal fornix or leakage of fluid from the cervical os on sterile speculum examination.

In clinically equivocal cases, additional supportive findings were reviewed when available, including ultrasonographic assessment of amniotic fluid volume and available placental alpha microglobulin-1 (PAMG-1) immunoassay results, such as AmniSure. Amniotic fluid volume was considered only as a supportive finding, and oligohydramnios alone was not accepted as diagnostic of PROM/PPROM. Cases in which fluid leakage was more consistent with urinary incontinence, physiologic or infectious vaginal discharge, or another non-amniotic source were not classified as PROM/PPROM unless the diagnosis was supported by clinical examination and/or placental alpha microglobulin-1 (PAMG-1) testing.

For the purposes of this study, only cases with clinically confirmed membrane rupture were included in the analysis. Cases documented only as suspected PROM, transient fluid leakage without objective confirmation, or possible false PROM were not considered confirmed PROM/PPROM. Because layer-specific differentiation between isolated outer membrane rupture and complete chorioamniotic membrane rupture was not routinely documented in the retrospective records, cases with temporary leakage that subsequently resolved and lacked supportive examination or test findings were not included in the analytic cohort.

Oligohydramnios was treated as a binary variable according to the amniotic fluid assessment recorded in the study database (oligohydramnios / normal AFI). Nulliparity was defined as the absence of a history of previous live birth or stillbirth. Latency was calculated in days as the interval between membrane rupture and delivery.

Maternal infectious morbidity composite was defined as the presence of at least one of the following: chorioamnionitis, postpartum endometritis, or maternal sepsis. The respiratory neonatal morbidity composite was defined as the presence of at least one of the following: respiratory distress syndrome, transient tachypnea, pneumonia, pneumothorax, oxygen requirement, continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) requirement, or mechanical ventilation.

Clinical management at the study center

PROM/PPROM management was guided by gestational age, maternal clinical status, fetal condition, and evidence of infection. After membrane rupture was confirmed, maternal vital signs, uterine tenderness, vaginal discharge characteristics, fetal well-being, and laboratory findings were assessed. Antibiotic therapy was initiated in all admitted women and continued according to gestational age, latency, clinical findings, and the maternal–fetal course during follow-up.

In women with PPROM at 34 + 0–36 + 6 weeks, management after admission was guided by maternal-fetal stability, signs of infection, labor status, and obstetric indications. Maternal and fetal surveillance was performed, and delivery planning was individualized according to the clinical course. Antenatal corticosteroids were administered in selected late-preterm cases in whom delivery within 7 days was anticipated. In term PROM cases at ≥ 37 weeks, induction or augmentation of labor was considered if spontaneous labor did not occur within an appropriate clinical timeframe and there was no contraindication to vaginal delivery.

PROM or PPROM alone was not considered an indication for cesarean delivery. Cesarean delivery was performed for standard obstetric indications, including previous cesarean section, fetal distress, cephalopelvic disproportion, breech presentation, arrest of labor, fetal macrosomia, chorioamnionitis, maternal disease, severe preeclampsia, and placenta previa or placenta accreta spectrum.

Data collection

Data were obtained from the hospital information management system, obstetric patient records, delivery records, and neonatal intensive care unit records. Maternal demographic and obstetric variables included age, gravidity, nulliparity, history of preterm birth, history of PPROM, urinary tract infection during the index pregnancy, history of threatened abortion in the index pregnancy, presence of cerclage, gestational age at membrane rupture, C-reactive protein (CRP) level at admission, white blood cell count (WBC), and oligohydramnios status.

Management and obstetric variables included antibiotic use, duration of antibiotic therapy, tocolytic use, magnesium sulfate administration, antenatal corticosteroid administration, outpatient follow-up status, duration of outpatient follow-up, PROM-to-delivery interval, delivery within 24 and 48 h, latency duration of ≥ 7 days, gestational age at delivery, and mode of delivery.

Maternal outcomes included chorioamnionitis, placental abruption, cord prolapse, postpartum endometritis, maternal sepsis, maternal intensive care unit admission, total maternal hospital stay related to PPROM, and postpartum maternal length of hospital stay.

Neonatal outcomes included birth weight, sex, Apgar scores at 1 and 5 min, admission to the neonatal intensive care unit, length of NICU stay, neonatal death, neonatal jaundice, early- and late-onset neonatal sepsis, respiratory distress syndrome (RDS), transient tachypnea, pneumonia, pneumothorax, oxygen requirement, CPAP requirement, mechanical ventilation, retinopathy, bronchopulmonary dysplasia, intraventricular hemorrhage, necrotizing enterocolitis, and composite neonatal outcomes.

Study outcomes

The primary aim of this study was to compare cases of PPROM occurring between 34 + 0 and 36 + 6 weeks of gestation with cases of term PROM at ≥ 37 weeks in terms of obstetric outcomes, maternal morbidity, and short-term neonatal outcomes. In this context, management and obstetric variables such as antibiotic and tocolytic use, antenatal corticosteroid administration, latency duration, gestational age at delivery, and mode of delivery were evaluated between the groups, together with outcomes including maternal infectious morbidity, length of hospital stay, birth weight, NICU admission, and neonatal infectious and respiratory morbidity. The secondary aim was to identify independent clinical factors associated with NICU admission and the respiratory neonatal morbidity composite using logistic regression analyses. In addition, the associations of antenatal corticosteroid administration and duration of antibiotic use with these outcomes were explored in expanded models.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 22.0 (Armonk, NY, USA). Distributional characteristics of continuous variables were assessed using graphical methods and appropriate normality tests. Variables with a normal distribution were presented as mean ± standard deviation, whereas non-normally distributed variables were presented as median (interquartile range). Categorical variables were expressed as numbers and percentages.

For comparisons between the two groups, the independent samples t-test was used for continuous variables with a normal distribution, Welch’s t-test was used when homogeneity of variance was not met, and the Mann-Whitney U test was used for continuous variables that were not normally distributed. Categorical variables were compared using Pearson’s chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. A two-sided p value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant in all analyses.

Logistic regression analyses were performed to evaluate factors associated with NICU admission and the respiratory neonatal morbidity composite. First, univariable logistic regression analysis was performed for each candidate variable; subsequently, variables considered clinically relevant were included in the multivariable model. In the main multivariable models constructed for NICU admission and the respiratory neonatal morbidity composite, the following covariates were included: group (PPROM at 34 + 0–36 + 6 weeks vs. term PROM at ≥ 37 weeks), maternal age, nulliparity, history of preterm birth, urinary tract infection during the index pregnancy, presence of cerclage in the index pregnancy, CRP level at admission, WBC count at admission, presence of oligohydramnios, PROM-to-delivery interval, and mode of delivery. Results were presented as odds ratios or adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% confidence intervals. Variables that were likely to be strongly associated with gestational age were not included in the same model; therefore, the group variable was used as the main exposure variable in the primary models. Expanded models additionally including antenatal corticosteroid administration and duration of antibiotic use were also constructed; however, because these variables may also reflect clinical management preferences, findings from these models were interpreted as exploratory.

Ethics approval

The study protocol was approved by the Necmettin Erbakan University Non-Drug and Non-Medical Device Research Ethics Committee (decision no: 2025/6146; meeting date: November 28, 2025). The requirement for informed consent was waived by the ethics committee because of the retrospective design of the study and the use of anonymized data. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

A total of 646 cases were included in the study; of these, 334 were in the group diagnosed with PPROM between 34 + 0 and 36 + 6 weeks, and 312 were in the term PROM group at ≥ 37 weeks. The maternal and obstetric characteristics of these two groups are compared in Table 1. Cases in the PPROM group at 34 + 0–36 + 6 weeks were significantly older than those in the term PROM group (33.56 ± 6.99 vs. 29.70 ± 6.23 years, p < 0.001). Gravidity did not differ significantly between the groups (p = 0.132). The nulliparity rate was similar between the two groups (45.8% vs. 48.4%, p = 0.562). As expected, gestational age at membrane rupture was lower in the PPROM group at 34 + 0–36 + 6 weeks [35 (35–36) weeks vs. 38 (37–39) weeks, p < 0.001]. CRP levels at admission were similar between the groups (p = 0.681). Mean WBC values were 11.43 ± 3.30 × 10³/µL in the PPROM group and 10.38 ± 2.89 × 10³/µL in the term PROM group, respectively. These WBC values were interpreted as clinically comparable and were not considered diagnostic of clinical infection.

With regard to obstetric history, no significant differences were observed between the groups in the frequency of prior preterm birth or prior PPROM (p = 0.192 and p = 1.000, respectively). Urinary tract infection during the index pregnancy was observed more frequently in the PPROM group than in the term PROM group (5.7% vs. 4.2%). Given the clinical relevance of urinary tract infection during pregnancy and its potential association with membrane rupture, this finding was taken into account when interpreting the maternal risk profile. In contrast, a history of threatened abortion in the index pregnancy (9.0% vs. 2.9%, p = 0.001) and a history of cervical cerclage (4.8% vs. 0.6%, p = 0.001) were significantly more common in the PPROM group at 34 + 0–36 + 6 weeks. The frequency of oligohydramnios was similar between the PPROM group at 34 + 0–36 + 6 weeks and the term PROM group (35.3% vs. 38.8%, p = 0.364).

When management characteristics and obstetric outcomes were compared (Table 2), antibiotic use due to PROM was present in all cases in both groups, with no difference between the groups (p = 1.000). In contrast, the duration of antibiotic use was significantly longer in the PPROM group at 34 + 0–36 + 6 weeks than in the term PROM group [2 (2–3) days vs. 1 (1–2) days, p < 0.001]. Magnesium sulfate use was very low in both groups and did not differ significantly (0.9% vs. 0.6%, p = 1.000). Tocolytic use (9.9% vs. 1.9%, p < 0.001) and antenatal corticosteroid administration (41.3% vs. 8.0%, p < 0.001) were significantly higher in the PPROM group at 34 + 0–36 + 6 weeks. Although the proportion of cases managed as outpatients appeared to be higher in the PPROM group at 34 + 0–36 + 6 weeks, this difference did not reach statistical significance (4.2% vs. 1.6%, p = 0.114); the duration of outpatient follow-up was also similar between the groups [6 (2–11) days vs. 6 (4–12) days, p = 0.576]. The interval between membrane rupture and delivery was significantly longer in the PPROM group at 34 + 0–36 + 6 weeks [2 (2–3) days vs. 1 (1–2) days, p < 0.001]. Consistent with this, delivery within 24 h (18.3% vs. 59.0%, p < 0.001) and within 48 h (50.9% vs. 77.9%, p < 0.001) was more frequent in the term PROM group, whereas a latency duration of ≥ 7 days was more common in the PPROM group at 34 + 0–36 + 6 weeks (7.8% vs. 1.3%, p < 0.001). As expected, gestational age at delivery was lower in the PPROM group at 34 + 0–36 + 6 weeks [35 (35–36) weeks vs. 38 (37–39) weeks, p < 0.001]. With regard to mode of delivery, the distribution of delivery mode differed between the groups. PROM/PPROM alone was not considered an indication for cesarean delivery. The high cesarean rate likely reflects the case mix of a referral center and local obstetric practice patterns.

When maternal morbidity was evaluated (Table 3), no significant differences were observed between the PPROM group at 34 + 0–36 + 6 weeks and the term PROM group in terms of chorioamnionitis, placental abruption, postpartum endometritis, maternal sepsis, maternal intensive care unit admission, or the maternal infectious morbidity composite (all comparisons p > 0.05). Cord prolapse was not observed in any case. In contrast, the total maternal hospital stay related to PPROM/PROM was significantly longer in the PPROM group at 34 + 0–36 + 6 weeks [2 (2–3) days vs. 1 (1–2) days, p < 0.001]. Postpartum maternal hospital stay was also statistically longer in the PPROM group at 34 + 0–36 + 6 weeks; however, the median value was 2 days in both groups [2 (1–2) days vs. 2 (1–2) days, p = 0.002].

Discussion

In this study, cases of PPROM at 34 + 0–36 + 6 weeks were shown to exhibit a clearly distinct clinical profile compared with cases of term PROM at ≥ 37 weeks. The most notable finding was that, despite the absence of a significant increase in maternal infectious outcomes, the burden of neonatal morbidity was markedly higher in the PPROM group at 34 + 0–36 + 6 weeks. In this group, rates of NICU admission, early neonatal sepsis, neonatal jaundice, transient tachypnea, oxygen requirement, CPAP requirement, and the respiratory neonatal morbidity composite were all higher, whereas the maternal infectious morbidity composite was similar between the two groups. This pattern suggests that, in the late preterm period, the principal determinant of clinical decision-making is no longer solely the fear of infection, but also the continuing and clinically meaningful neonatal vulnerability related to prematurity. In this respect, our findings are consistent with current guidelines and review articles emphasizing that PPROM at 34 + 0–36 + 6 weeks should be considered as a distinct obstetric window with biological and clinical characteristics different from those of term PROM at ≥ 37 weeks [1, 2, 9].

In our study, the finding that the 34 + 0–36 + 6 weeks PPROM group had lower birth weight and an approximately fourfold higher rate of NICU admission indicates that late preterm birth should not be regarded clinically as merely “borderline prematurity,” but rather as a period that still carries a substantial burden in terms of resource utilization and short-term morbidity. This result is also consistent with population-based studies showing that the difference between late preterm and early term birth has meaningful implications for respiratory support, the need for surveillance, and neonatal care resources. In particular, Boyle et al. demonstrated in a prospective study that late preterm neonates required more resuscitation, respiratory support, and medical surveillance than term infants; in our data, a similar risk pattern re-emerged in the context of PROM [10]. Therefore, in cases of PPROM at 34 + 0–36 + 6 weeks, it does not seem appropriate to underestimate neonatal risk on the basis of the perception of a “relatively mature fetus.” This gestational window remains clinically challenging because management strategies vary considerably across centers, particularly with respect to expectant management, timing of delivery, antenatal corticosteroid administration, antibiotic use, and neonatal preparedness. Accordingly, the higher neonatal morbidity burden observed in our cohort supports careful risk stratification and individualized management rather than a uniform approach based solely on proximity to term gestation.

An important aspect of our findings is that, despite a longer latency period and lower rates of delivery within 24 and 48 h in the PPROM group at 34 + 0–36 + 6 weeks, maternal infectious morbidity did not increase. Admission WBC values were clinically comparable between the groups and were not considered diagnostic of maternal infection. This interpretation is further supported by the similar CRP levels and comparable rates of clinically defined maternal infectious morbidity between the groups. Urinary tract infection during the index pregnancy was observed more frequently in the PPROM group, and this finding was considered clinically relevant when interpreting the maternal risk profile. At first glance, the absence of increased infectious morbidity despite a longer membrane rupture-to-delivery interval may appear to contradict the classical expectation that prolonged latency would inevitably increase the risk of infection. However, this finding may be related to close clinical surveillance, individualized timing of delivery, and antibiotic use during the PROM/PPROM-related admission according to local physician practice. Therefore, the universal initiation of antibiotics in our cohort, particularly in term PROM cases, may reflect local practice patterns rather than a standardized evidence-based protocol. In addition to the classic Mercer and ORACLE data showing that antibiotic use in PPROM prolongs latency and reduces maternal and neonatal infectious morbidity, a recent network meta-analysis has also suggested that penicillin-based regimens in particular may provide benefit with respect to chorioamnionitis [11–13]. Therefore, in our cohort, the finding that maternal infectious outcomes remained similar despite a longer latency period suggests that a strategy of “controlled expectant management plus antibiotics plus close surveillance” may be safe in selected patient subgroups.

However, this finding should not be interpreted to mean that expectant management is universally and unconditionally superior in all cases of PPROM at 34 + 0–36 + 6 weeks. Randomized data point to a more nuanced balance. Studies such as PPROMT and PPROMEXIL-2, together with the individual participant data meta-analysis combining these trials, have shown that immediate delivery does not substantially reduce neonatal sepsis; in contrast, it may have unfavorable effects on respiratory morbidity, NICU admission, and cesarean delivery rates, whereas expectant management may also be associated with an increased risk of chorioamnionitis [14, 15]. A broader Cochrane review also reported that, in the overall PPROM population, planned early delivery may be associated with higher rates of RDS, ventilatory support, NICU admission, and cesarean delivery [16]. In our cohort, the absence of a significant increase in infectious maternal morbidity, together with the apparent neonatal benefit in favor of greater gestational maturation, may be interpreted not as conflicting with these randomized data, but rather as a real-world reflection of the clinical balance they suggest.

The markedly higher rates of delivery within 24 and 48 h in the term PROM group are consistent with current guidelines and the term PROM literature. The TERMPROM trial, the subsequent Cochrane review, and more recent meta-analyses have shown that initiating delivery within a reasonable time frame in term PROM reduces maternal infectious morbidity without conferring a neonatal disadvantage compared with expectant management [3–5, 17, 18]. In our cohort, the shorter latency period, higher rate of vaginal delivery, and lower neonatal morbidity observed in the term PROM group support the view that an approach based on expediting delivery in term PROM continues to rest on a strong evidence base. Accordingly, our findings indicate that PPROM at 34 + 0–36 + 6 weeks and term PROM at ≥ 37 weeks should not be viewed merely as two consecutive phases within the same clinical spectrum; rather, they should be considered two distinct obstetric conditions characterized by different risk profiles and different management priorities.

The high cesarean rate observed in our cohort should be interpreted with caution and should not be considered as a recommended management strategy for PROM/PPROM. PROM/PPROM alone was not considered an indication for cesarean delivery. The high cesarean rate likely reflects the case mix of a referral center and local obstetric practice patterns. Therefore, findings related to mode of delivery should be interpreted within the context of the retrospective design and local practice patterns.

The finding that antenatal corticosteroid administration was associated with lower odds of both NICU admission and the respiratory neonatal morbidity composite in the expanded models is biologically plausible, as the ALPS trial demonstrated that betamethasone reduces short-term respiratory morbidity in women at risk of delivery at 34 + 0–36 + 6 weeks of gestation [19]. Meta-analyses pooling randomized controlled trials have likewise reported reductions particularly in the need for respiratory support and surfactant administration [20, 21]. However, this finding should be interpreted with caution in our study, because corticosteroid administration was not evenly distributed between the two gestational age groups and likely reflects the clinical decision-making process. In other words, its apparent significance in the model does not imply that routine corticosteroid administration is warranted in term PROM. Moreover, recent studies on late preterm corticosteroid use have emphasized that the benefit is mainly observed in short-term respiratory outcomes and that careful patient selection remains essential because of concerns such as hypoglycemia and exposure of an inappropriate target population. Although the ALPS follow-up study did not demonstrate clear long-term neurodevelopmental harm, broader population-based meta-analyses also remind us that corticosteroid exposure should be used cautiously when applied outside appropriate indications [19]. Therefore, our finding regarding corticosteroid use should be interpreted not as causal, but as hypothesis-generating and within its clinical context.

Among our neonatal outcomes, the increases in early neonatal sepsis, jaundice and respiratory morbidity are particularly noteworthy. This pattern suggests that gestational maturation is not limited to surfactant biology alone; rather, immune adaptation, feeding competence, bilirubin metabolism, and cardiopulmonary transition also remain vulnerable during the late preterm period. It has long been recognized that late preterm neonates carry higher respiratory and metabolic risks than term infants; our data indicate that this physiological disadvantage becomes even more pronounced in the context of PROM/PPROM. In particular, the finding that respiratory distress was the predominant indication for NICU admission in the PPROM group at 34 + 0–36 + 6 weeks indicates that respiratory morbidity in this group is not only statistically significant but also a clinically central determinant.

The logistic regression analyses further support this clinical pattern. The fact that belonging to the PPROM group at 34 + 0–36 + 6 weeks remained an independent risk factor in the main models for both NICU admission and the respiratory neonatal morbidity composite indicates that the observed difference cannot be explained solely by accompanying maternal characteristics, laboratory values, or mode of delivery. In other words, the late preterm gestational age range itself represents one of the principal determinants of neonatal outcomes. This finding is also consistent with recent cohort data showing that advancing gestational age, particularly in the setting of PPROM, is associated with a reduction in adverse neonatal outcomes [22]. However, our study is additionally important in that this association was demonstrated in the context of a direct comparison with term PROM.

The strengths of this study include the use of a single-center cohort managed at a tertiary care level with a relatively homogeneous clinical approach, the simultaneous evaluation of maternal, obstetric, and neonatal data within the same database, and the construction of regression models for two clinically meaningful neonatal outcomes rather than relying solely on group comparisons. In addition, the presentation of indications for NICU admission in a supplementary table strengthens the clinical interpretation of the neonatal findings. Nevertheless, the retrospective design of the study, treatment selection based on indication, potential selection bias in management variables, and the low frequency of some rare maternal and neonatal events constitute important limitations. In addition, exclusion of 80 cases with missing primary outcome data may have introduced potential selection bias, although this exclusion was necessary to ensure reliable assessment of the study outcomes. In particular, the expanded models involving antenatal corticosteroid use and duration of antibiotic therapy should be considered exploratory, as they may also reflect clinical management preferences. Moreover, the low number of maternal infectious events may have limited the ability to detect small but clinically relevant differences statistically. Another important limitation is that this study was not designed to estimate the institutional incidence of PROM or PPROM. Although term PROM is expected to be more frequent than PPROM in the general obstetric population, the distribution of cases in this study should be interpreted within the context of a selected tertiary-center analytic cohort. PPROM cases at 34 + 0–36 + 6 weeks may be overrepresented in tertiary referral settings, whereas uncomplicated term PROM cases at ≥ 37 weeks may be managed in lower-level obstetric units or may be underrepresented in hospital-based referral cohorts. Therefore, the numerical distribution of the two groups should not be considered an estimate of PROM or PPROM incidence. Overall, our findings support that management in cases of PPROM at 34 + 0–36 + 6 weeks should not follow a uniform approach but rather should be individualized according to maternal-fetal stability, risk of infection, fetal well-being, and center-specific resources [1, 8, 9].

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that PPROM at 34 + 0–36 + 6 weeks is associated with a greater burden of neonatal morbidity than term PROM, whereas maternal infectious outcomes were comparable between the groups in this tertiary-care cohort. Therefore, PROM management in the late preterm period should not be regarded as a simple extension of term PROM algorithms, but rather as a distinct clinical decision-making domain that requires balancing the competing risks of prematurity and infection. Future prospective, well-defined, and preferably gestational age-stratified studies are needed to clarify more precisely which patients are likely to benefit more from expectant management and which from earlier delivery.
