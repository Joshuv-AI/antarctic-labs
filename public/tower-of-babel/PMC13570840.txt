Cost-Effectiveness of Bivalent Respiratory Syncytial Virus Prefusion F Vaccine for Older Adults in the Netherlands
Authors: Annefleur C. Langedijk, Floris van den Dungen, Frits M. E. Franssen, Marco Goeijenbier, Ahuva Averin, Erin Quinn, Mark Atwood, Diana Mendes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionLower respiratory tract disease caused by respiratory syncytial virus (RSV-LRTD) is associated with substantial clinical and economic burden among older adults and adults with chronic medical conditions. Bivalent RSV prefusion F protein-based vaccine (RSVpreF) has been authorized for use among all adults aged ≥ 60 years in the Netherlands, and The Health Council of the Netherlands has recommended RSV vaccination for at-risk persons aged 60–74 years and all persons aged ≥ 75 years. We estimated the cost-effectiveness of implementing this strategy.MethodsWe have developed a static cohort model to demonstrate lifetime clinical and economic outcomes associated with RSV-LRTD among adults in the Netherlands, with (vs. without) RSVpreF use (price: €180; age/risk-specific uptake: 44.6–80.4%). Model inputs include population characteristics, disease rates [hospitalization (RSV-H), general practitioner visit (RSV-GP), non-medically attended (RSV-NMA)], fatality rates, vaccine effectiveness (based on real-world and clinical trial data), utilities/disutilities, medical care costs, indirect costs associated with morbidity- and mortality-related productivity loss, and vaccination costs. Discounting was applied to costs (3%) and benefits (1.5%).ResultsAmong at-risk adults aged 60–74 years and all adults aged ≥ 75 years (n = 4.8 million) in the Netherlands, single-dose RSVpreF would prevent 84,228 RSV-LRTD cases (RSV-H: 16,642, RSV-GP: 21,788, RSV-NMA: 45,798) and 2304 RSV-LRTD deaths over a lifetime horizon. With 11,006 quality-adjusted life-years (QALY) gained and a €195 million increase in total costs, incremental cost-effectiveness ratio for RSVpreF would be €17,740/QALY gained. RSVpreF would thus prevent 16% of hospitalizations, 15% of deaths, 16% of medical costs, and 20% of indirect costs related to RSV among this population over their remaining lifetime. In probabilistic sensitivity analyses applying variability to key inputs, all 1000 replications yielded cost-effectiveness ratios within the Health Council’s willingness-to-pay threshold (< €50,000/QALY gained).ConclusionsFindings suggest that RSVpreF would greatly reduce the clinical and economic burden of RSV among older adults in the Netherlands.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s40121-026-01400-w.

Introduction

Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) is responsible for a substantial burden of acute illness experienced by older adults and may cause severe respiratory disease [1]. There is an increased risk of RSV-related complications (e.g., pneumonia) among persons aged ≥ 65 years and older adults with chronic medical conditions such as heart or lung disease [2]. In the Netherlands and globally, RSV outbreaks are common and burdensome throughout nursing homes and other residential care facilities where elderly persons live in close contact with one another [2]. A recent burden of disease study found the burden of severe RSV to be considerable among adults in the Netherlands, with in-hospital mortality rates as high as 13.8% [1]. Evidence on the burden of RSV among these populations is sparse due to limited awareness among health care practitioners, lack of routine testing, unreliability of diagnostic tests in older adults, and differences in the use of diagnostic codes employed within burden of disease studies [3, 4].

Until recently, there were no preventive measures available to protect adults against RSV. Beginning in 2021, the efficacy and safety of bivalent stabilized prefusion F subunit vaccine (RSVpreF) use among older adults has been evaluated in the “RSV vaccine Efficacy study iN Older adults Immunized against RSV disease” Phase III placebo-controlled clinical trial (“RENOIR”, NCT05035212) [5]. In August of 2023, the European Commission gave marketing authorization to RSVpreF for use in adults aged 60 years and older to prevent RSV-related lower respiratory tract disease (LRTD) [6]. Furthermore, in their 2025 review, the Health Council of the Netherlands recommended that at-risk persons aged 60–74 years and all persons aged ≥ 75 years be vaccinated against RSV; however, introduction of the program has been delayed pending evidence on vaccine duration of protection and cost-effectiveness [7]. While several recent studies have evaluated the clinical and economic outcomes associated with vaccine use (i.e., not specifically RSVpreF vaccine) to prevent RSV in the Netherlands, only one evaluates outcomes among the age group currently recommended for vaccination [8–10]. To address this gap as well as concerns from the Health Council of the Netherlands regarding vaccine duration of protection and cost-effectiveness, the current study employs a cost-effectiveness model to evaluate the public health and economic outcomes associated with RSV-LRTD and vaccination with RSVpreF (i.e., compared to no vaccination) among at-risk adults aged 60–74 years and all adults aged ≥ 75 years in the Netherlands.

Methods

Model Description

To estimate the burden of RSV-LRTD and impact of RSVpreF vaccination among older adults in the Netherlands over a lifetime, we employed a population-based Markov-type model (Fig. 1). The cost-effectiveness model has been recently described in detail in an analysis of RSVpreF use among older adults in Germany [11]. As described in the paper, the model population is characterized based on age and comorbidity profile (with vs. without chronic or immunocompromising medical conditions [CMC+ vs. CMC–)] [11]. Adults in the model population were assumed to be either protected against RSV-LRTD by way of RSVpreF or to remain vulnerable against RSV-LRTD. Clinical outcomes in the model include cases of RSV-LRTD, stratified by care setting [i.e., hospital (H), general practitioner’s office (GP), non-medically attended (NMA)], and RSV-related deaths. As detailed in the study by Averin et al., economic costs include direct medical care costs, costs of vaccination (i.e., including the vaccine and its administration), and indirect costs related to productivity loss [11]. Because persons may be retained in the model population over a lifetime, they are subject to experiencing health outcomes and economic costs on a monthly basis until death or age 99 years (i.e., whichever occurs first) [11]. Consistent with a standard cost-effectiveness model approach, the model follows a single cohort of adults and does not consider any adults entering the population after the first model year or receiving vaccinations after the first model year (i.e., even among the initial cohort). Model Population

The model population (i.e., CMC+ adults aged 60–74 years and all adults aged ≥ 75 years in the Netherlands) comprised 2.6 million persons, estimated from Dutch Central Bureau of Statistics 2023 data [12]. Adults were stratified by age group (i.e., 60–64, 65–74, 75–84, and 85–99 years) and comorbidity profile (CMC+ vs. CMC–). The age-specific distribution of persons aged ≥ 60 years by comorbidity profile was estimated based on data from a recent RSV vaccine cost-effectiveness analysis (Table 1) [13]. The CMC+ subgroup is reflective of the target subgroup eligible for free influenza vaccination in the Netherlands (i.e., included persons with chronic respiratory, cardiac, and kidney diseases, diabetes, immunosuppressive diseases, obesity, and neurological disorders) [13, 14].

Disease Rates

Age-specific incidence of RSV-LRTD-attributable hospitalizations was estimated based on a recent burden of disease modeling study conducted by Johannesen et al. [4]. Age-specific RSV-H rates were then allocated across comorbidity profiles based on relative risk data from a study conducted by Zeevat et al., which also evaluated the cost-effectiveness of RSV vaccination among older adults in the Netherlands [10]. Age-specific incidence of RSV-GP was estimated based on a recent study evaluating the burden of RSV treated in primary care settings among adults aged ≥ 50 years in the Netherlands [15]. RSV-GP incidence was assumed to be invariant by comorbidity profile. Incidence of RSV-NMA was estimated based on the study from Zeevat et al. which reported that 70.44% of RSV episodes would not require medical attention [10]. All rates of RSV (i.e., invariant by setting of care) were allocated across calendar months based on recent data from the National Institute for Public Health (Table 2) [16].

Case-Fatality and Mortality Rates

Case-fatality rate (CFR) was estimated based on Netherlands-specific data on mortality among patients hospitalized with RSV between 2022 and 2024 [17]. CFR was then allocated by comorbidity profile based on data from a retrospective cohort study of mortality in United States (US) adults hospitalized for pneumonia [18]. There was assumed to be no mortality among RSV episodes not requiring hospitalization (i.e., RSV-GP, RSV-NMA).

Age-specific general population mortality (i.e., unrelated to RSV-LRTD) was estimated based on Central Bureau of Statistics data [19]. Age-specific mortality data was then allocated by comorbidity profile based on the assumption that CMC+ persons are at a 75% increased risk of mortality (i.e., compared to CMC– persons). This aligns with assumptions employed in the recent cost-effectiveness analysis of RSVpreF use in Germany conducted by Averin et al. [11].

Vaccine Effectiveness

Vaccine effectiveness (VE) against RSV-H was estimated based on 2023–2025 real-world effectiveness data from the Kaiser Permanente Southern California (KPSC) study evaluating VE against RSV-related LRTD hospitalizations and emergency department (ED) visits (Fig. 2) [20]. Endpoints corresponding to season 1 follow-up (i.e., 1.9 months; 87% VE) and season 2 follow-up (i.e., 12.2 months; 77% VE) were employed, with linear interpolation applied between the two timepoints. Linear extrapolation (i.e., based on the estimated slope between 1.9 and 12.2 months) was applied for month 1 and months 13–90 since administration, after which point the VE curve naturally hits 0%.

VE against RSV-GP was estimated based on RENOIR clinical trial data as employed in Averin et al. [11]. As detailed by Averin and colleagues, efficacy against medically attended acute respiratory illness due to RSV (RSV-ARI) was used as a proxy for VE against RSV-GP [11]. Endpoints corresponding to season 1 follow-up (i.e., 7 months) and season 2 follow-up (i.e., 16.4 months) were employed, with linear interpolation applied between the two timepoints and linear extrapolation applied after 16.4 months until VE hits 0% at 41 months after administration [11].

VE against RSV-NMA was estimated similarly but was based on efficacy data against non-medically attended RSV-ARI from post hoc analyses [21]. Season 1 VE was estimated to be 60.0% and season 2 VE was estimated to be 31.4%. Waning of VE against RSV-NMA was estimated the same way as the RSV-GP waning curve. Due to the steep decline in efficacy estimates between season 1 and season 2, VE was estimated to reach 0% in month 27 after administration.

Costs

Hospitalization costs associated with RSV-LRTD were estimated based on a Netherlands-specific retrospective claims analysis and are reflective of episodes treated in both the general ward and intensive care unit (i.e., weighted by severity based on data included in the study) [22]. Because costs from Rozenbaum et al. are based on 2008–2011 data, they were then inflated to 2024 Euros [22, 23]. Cost per GP visit (€34.88) was estimated based on the Dutch manual for costing studies; cost per RSV-NMA episode (€4.32) was assumed to comprise only over-the-counter medication and estimated based on a recent RSV burden of disease study among older adults in the Netherlands [24, 25]. Medical costs associated with both GP and NMA episodes were inflated to 2024 Euros and were assumed to be invariant by patient age and comorbidity profile [23]. List price of RSVpreF was €180 [26]; administration cost associated with RSVpreF use was estimated to be €14.01 based on influenza vaccination reimbursement [27].

Indirect costs included morbidity and mortality related productivity loss among patients with RSV-LRTD and morbidity related productivity loss among caregivers (e.g., spouse/child) of patients. Productivity loss among patients and caregivers was estimated based on labor force participation data (i.e., which was assumed to be 0% among retired persons) [28], daily wage data [23, 29], and estimated number of days of work-loss. Work-loss for patients was assumed to vary based on severity of RSV-LRTD episode [9, 10]. For caregivers of persons hospitalized with RSV-LRTD, work loss was assumed to be the same as for patients [9]; there was assumed to be no work loss among caregivers of patients treated in GP or NMA settings. Lastly, work-loss associated with premature RSV-related mortality was estimated based on life expectancy data, with expected lifetime earnings discounted monthly [19].

Utilities and Disutilities

Age- and risk-specific general population utility values were estimated based on EQ-5D data from the United Kingdom (UK) [30]. Utility values among persons with a history of health conditions were assigned to CMC+ persons in this study, while utility values among persons with no history of health conditions were assigned to CMC– persons [30]. Care setting-specific disutility associated with RSV (i.e., RSV-H: 0.017, RSV-GP: 0.005, RSV-NMA: 0.002) was estimated using an area under the curve approach and data from two recent studies reporting EQ-5D VAS scores among persons with RSV treated in the hospital setting versus GP/NMA settings, respectively [24, 31]. Quality-adjusted life-year (QALY) loss was assumed to be invariant by patient age and comorbidity profile.

Analyses

Base case

Base case analyses evaluated the cost-effectiveness of the Hypothetical Strategy (i.e., CMC+ adults aged 60–74 years and all adults aged ≥ 75 years in the Netherlands are eligible for RSVpreF) versus the Current Strategy (no intervention). Age- and comorbidity-specific vaccine coverage was estimated based on observed influenza vaccination rates [32]. RSVpreF administration was assumed to occur leading up to and during the months of highest respiratory illness rates. Lacking Netherlands-specific data on respiratory disease vaccination by time of year, the distribution of vaccine administration by calendar month was based on 2018–2019 uptake of influenza vaccine as observed in the UK [33]. As such, vaccine administration occurs from September through February of the first year of the modeling horizon, with some protection from each vaccination lasting for the following 91 months after administration. Consistent with the Dutch guidelines for economic evaluations in healthcare, future costs were discounted at an annual rate of 3% while future QALYs were discounted at an annual rate of 1.5% [34]. Cost-effectiveness was reported as an incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) defined as cost per QALY gained (i.e., difference in costs divided by difference in QALYs).

Subgroup, Sensitivity, and Scenario

A subgroup analysis considering vaccination among all adults aged ≥ 60 years (i.e., regardless of comorbidity profile) was conducted to evaluate the cost-effectiveness of RSVpreF use among all adults authorized for vaccination in the Netherlands. One-way deterministic sensitivity analyses (DSA) considering changes (± 20% of base case values) to selected input values (i.e., RSV-H incidence rates, CFR, vaccine uptake, VE, and medical care costs), each in turn, were also performed to evaluate robustness of results to changes in key model parameters. In addition, to address concerns from the Health Council of the Netherlands, scenario analyses were conducted in which alternative vaccine duration of protection was considered for VE against RSV-related hospitalizations. Specifically, VE was estimated using the same methods as base case but employing data from the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) (Figure S1 in electronic supplementary material) [35]. Lastly, probabilistic sensitivity analyses (PSA) accounting for uncertainty surrounding key model parameters were conducted with 1000 simulations (Table S1). Results of subgroup, sensitivity, and scenario analyses can be found in Tables S2–S13 of the electronic supplementary materials.

Results

Base Case

With no vaccination to protect CMC+ adults aged 60–74 years and all adults aged ≥ 75 years (n = 2.6 M) in the Netherlands against RSV-LRTD, there would be 103,449 cases of RSV-H, 270,828 cases of RSV-GP, 889,002 cases of RSV-NMA, and 15,468 RSV-attributable deaths over the lifetime modeling horizon (Table 3, Tables 4, 5). Medical care costs would total €489 million with an additional €290 million in indirect costs. Use of RSVpreF among CMC+ adults aged 60–74 years and all adults aged ≥ 75 years would yield reductions in RSV-LRTD cases of 16,642 (16%), 21,788 (8%), and 45,798 (5%) for RSV-H, RSV-GP, and RSV-NMA episodes, respectively. By preventing cases of RSV-LRTD, RSVpreF would also prevent 2,304 (15%) RSV-related deaths, €97 million in medical care costs, and €66 million in indirect costs. With €358 million in vaccination costs, total costs would increase by €195 million. Discussion

Our results support existing evidence that RSV is responsible for a substantial clinical and economic burden among persons aged ≥ 65 years and those with chronic medical conditions such as heart or lung disease [2]. Even though RSVpreF has been authorized since 2023 by the European Commission for use among adults aged ≥ 60 years to prevent RSV-LRTD [6], and the Health Council of the Netherlands recommended in 2025 that at-risk persons aged 60–74 years and all persons aged ≥ 75 years be vaccinated against RSV, implementation of the program has been delayed, mainly due to concerns surrounding vaccine duration of protection [7]. We thus employed a Markov-type model to provide further evidence on the burden of RSV-LRTD and the cost-effectiveness of RSVpreF use (i.e., compared to no vaccination) among at-risk adults aged 60–74 years and all adults aged ≥ 75 years in the Netherlands.

Results suggest that employing the program recommended by the Health Council (i.e., RSVpreF use among at-risk adults aged 60–74 years and all adults aged ≥ 75 years) would substantially reduce the clinical and economic burden of RSV-LRTD to the Netherlands’ health care system and society. Assuming uptake comparable to levels for influenza vaccination, RSVpreF would prevent 16% of RSV-H, 15% of RSV-LRTD deaths, 16% of medical care costs, and 20% of indirect costs over a lifetime horizon. In the first year alone, RSVpreF use among the target population would prevent 33,573 cases of RSV-LRTD, 434 deaths, and €39.5 million in associated costs, reducing such outcomes by 34%, 47%, and 41%, respectively. This impact would be enhanced if the Health Council were to extend their recommendation to target all adults aged ≥ 60 years with RSVpreF.

To address concerns from the Health Council regarding duration of vaccine protection and cost-effectiveness of RSVpreF immunization, a scenario analysis was conducted employing real-world data on effectiveness against RSV-related hospitalizations from the US CDC [35]. As demonstrated in Supplementary Fig. S1, data from the CDC yielded VE that was lower overall and diminished to 0% at month 58 after administration (vs. month 91, in the base case). Assuming lower VE against RSV-H, RSVpreF still prevented 9% of hospitalizations, 11% of total medical care costs, and 15% of indirect costs; the ICER was €42,523/QALY. This suggests that, even if effectiveness inputs employed here are optimistic, RSVpreF use among the target subgroup would be a cost-effective solution to reducing the burden of RSV-LRTD. Lastly, subgroup analyses demonstrated that expanding the vaccination strategy to include all adults aged ≥ 60 years would prevent 48% more hospitalizations, 88% more GP encounters, and 76% more NMA episodes, compared to the base-case vaccination strategy. This expanded vaccination strategy would protect a larger portion of the population, prevent more RSV-related outcomes, and remain a cost-effective use of resources. Overall, across all base-case, subgroup, deterministic sensitivity, probabilistic sensitivity, and scenario analyses, the ICER ranged from €11,141 to €42,523 per QALY gained, consistently falling within the Health Council’s willingness-to-pay threshold.

To the extent of our knowledge, this is the first study to employ multi-season real-world effectiveness data to evaluate the cost-effectiveness of RSVpreF use among at-risk adults aged 60–74 years and all adults aged ≥ 75 years in the Netherlands. One recent study, by Zeevat et al. [9], considered vaccination among a target population consistent with the base case strategy employed herein; however, Zeevat and colleagues considered the use of a generic RSV vaccine (i.e., not RSVpreF-specific) for which VE data were based on a meta-analysis that mainly considered effectiveness over only the first season following vaccination (i.e., opposed to multi-season data employed herein). Zeevat and colleagues also considered costs and QALY loss associated with adverse events of vaccination, which we are not comfortable employing at this point due to lack of data [9]. Given differences in study design and estimation, outcomes averted (versus no vaccination) and ICERs were comparable to those reported herein [9]. Zeevat and colleagues reported vaccination to yield 8196 QALYs (vs. 11,006 herein) and €247 million in total costs (vs. €195 million herein); ICER would therefore be €30,162 (vs. €17,740 here).

Our analyses are subject to several notable limitations related to input parameters and modeling assumptions. Since the full duration of protection via RSVpreF will not be known until several more seasons of real-world data become available, we employed methods of linear extrapolation to estimate the VE waning curves. For all settings, it was assumed that the decline of effectiveness between the first two endpoints would persist until the curve naturally hits 0%. Specifically, VE data against RSV-H, RSV-GP, and RSV-NMA were extrapolated to obtain inputs corresponding to months 13–91, 17–41, and 17–27 after administration, respectively (Fig. 2). To evaluate the impact of these assumptions on model results, we conducted a scenario analysis evaluating the impact of employing alternative VE against RSV-H [35]. VE endpoints from the US CDC were lower than KPSC data and the curve waned to 0% VE nearly three years earlier than in the base case (i.e., 58 months opposed to 91 months after administration; Figure S1). The scenario analysis yielded a 46% lower reduction in hospitalizations than in the base case but remained cost-effective (ICER: €42,523/QALY gained; Table S13). We also note that there are many studies currently evaluating the effectiveness of RSV vaccines which were not chosen to inform VE inputs herein [35–38]. The KPSC study is most applicable to our analyses (i.e., considering use of RSVpreF specifically among adults aged ≥ 60 years, with the majority aged ≥ 75 years, which aligns with our modeled population) given its two seasons of data available and thorough methodology (e.g., includes testing of salvaged nasal swabs collected for influenza/SARS-CoV-2 testing) [20]. We look forward to the release of additional real-world data to inform the rate of VE waning over several seasons; however, we believe that, for the time being, linear extrapolation is a reasonable approach. It is also notable that the VE endpoints employed in the base case may not be perfectly aligned with model outcomes. For example, VE against RSV-H was estimated based on an endpoint associated with RSV-related hospitalizations or ED encounters [20]. Because VE is typically more effective against severe disease [39], it is likely that employing such values underestimates the impact of RSVpreF against hospitalizations alone.

Hospitalization rates employed were based on data on RSV-attributable infections from the modeling study by Johannesen et al., which were also employed by Zeevat et al. [4, 9]. We note that Johanessen and colleagues report both “RSV-coded” and “RSV-attributable” (i.e., accommodating for underreporting and underdiagnosis) incidence rates; as recommended by the authors, we employed the RSV-attributable incidence rates in order to evaluate the full burden of RSV [4]. These data on RSV-attributable infections accommodate for underreporting of severe RSV which has been acknowledged and adjusted for in many countries due to low diagnostic testing rates and limited accuracy in case definitions [4, 40–46]. The approach herein therefore follows recent standards employed globally to estimate the true burden of RSV-LRTD; however, to the extent that RSV-H incidence is under- or overestimated, the burden of disease may be over- or underestimated.

We also note that, in the absence of the actual net price, the list price of RSVpreF was employed; therefore, the price employed may be overestimated. Furthermore, due to limited availability of data, some model inputs were estimated based on data from comparable country settings. Specifically, estimates of baseline utility and the distribution of vaccine administration by calendar month were based on UK data due to the close proximity and socio-economic status of the two regions [30, 33]. Estimates of QALY loss were based on global/European data [24, 31]. To the extent that model inputs are not reflective of the setting of interest, the cost-effectiveness of RSVpreF may be mis-estimated.

There is also growing evidence that RSV infection causes hospitalizations related to exacerbations of existing cardiovascular and pulmonary conditions, and that other serious events often occur concurrently with RSV infection [47–50]. Because data related to RSV-attributable cardiovascular or pulmonary hospitalizations and the impact of RSVpreF in preventing such events is limited at this time, and because the cost-effectiveness model is not currently capable of assessing these outcomes, such potential benefits from vaccination were not included in the model. The model also did not consider other potential downstream effects of RSV illness. Especially among older adults and those with chronic medical conditions, evidence suggests that patients may experience an increased rate of other illnesses, all-cause hospitalizations, functional status decline, and/or need for nursing care following an episode of RSV-LRTD [51, 52]. Because the model did not evaluate these additional outcomes, the clinical and economic benefits of RSVpreF may be further underestimated. Future research should expand on this analysis by quantifying the impact of potential RSV-related exacerbations of chronic conditions, subsequent all-cause hospitalizations, and long-term functional decline, as well as the potential role of RSVpreF vaccination in mitigating these consequences.

Conclusions

Results suggest that single-dose RSVpreF vaccination among at-risk adults aged 60–74 years and all adults aged ≥ 75 years in the Netherlands would considerably reduce the clinical burden of RSV-LRTD and would be a cost-effective use of resources. Findings were consistent across all analyses conducted, including scenario analysis considering alternative vaccine effectiveness inputs.
