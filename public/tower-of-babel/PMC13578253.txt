Influence of pollen from different species on the fruit quality of ‘Hongyang’ kiwifruit
Authors: Weimin Zhong, Qing Liu, Jia Zhou, Sheng Zhang, Binbin Shi, Shiming Han, Te Xu, Yu Liu, Qiangfu Liu, Zhiwei Lei, Dongmei Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to investigate pollen types on the fruit quality of ‘Hongyang’ kiwifruit, providing a scientific basis for rational pollinizer tree allocation. Using ‘Hongyang’ as the test material and commercial pollen as the control, pollen from 12 male plants of 2 species ( Actinidia chinesis var. Deliciosa and A. chinensis ) was used to pollinate in orchards. PCA was applied for comprehensive evaluation of the pollinated fruit. The results were as follows: (1) Male A. chinesis var. deliciosa plants had a later flowering period but more pollen than male A. chinensis plants did, and there were notable intraspecific differences in floral traits. Among the six tested male plants of A. chinensis (‘a-1’, ‘a-2’, ‘a-3’, ‘a-5’, ‘a-6’, ‘a-7’), their flowering periods basically overlap with those of ‘Hongyang’, while the other materials were 5–23 days later than ‘Hongyang’.(2) ‘Hongyang’ had a significant pollen xenia on the fruit set, quality, and seed characteristics, particularly showing large differences in single fruit weight, sugar-acid ratio, vitamin C content, flesh color, and seed number. Different pollen pollination treatments had different impacts on ‘Hongyang’ fruit quality. (3) According to principal component scores, floral organ characteristics, and flowering period evaluations, male A. chinensis plants ‘a-6’, ‘a-2’, and ‘a-7’ are suitable and compatible pollinizers for ‘Hongyang’, whereas the male A. chinensis var. deliciosa male plants ‘a-10’ and ‘a-12’ are recommended for poststorage artificial pollination. In conclusion, there are significant differences in floral traits among inter- and intraspecific male kiwifruit plants. Introduction

Producers of over 75% of the world’s major food crops depend on cross-pollination to attain optimal yields and improve grain quality. This process is indispensable for fulfilling the food requirements of the global population and ensuring human health and nutrition1–3. Denney4 defined xenia as the phenomenon where cross-pollination influences the characteristics of the fruits or seeds (including the embryo and endosperm) produced by the maternal plant. This phenomenon directly affects multiple fruit attributes, such as shape, maturity period, size, color, flavor, and internal component content, within the same growing season5. Consequently, research on cross-pollination holds substantial importance for the breeding and production of horticultural plants, economic forest trees, and fruit and vegetable crops. For example, significant pollen direct effect phenomena have been observed in crops such as kiwifruit5, pear6, citrus7, chestnut8, corn9, and Chinese cabbage10. These phenomena provide a theoretical basis for improving varieties, configuring pollinator trees, increasing yield and quality, and enhancing economic benefits.

Kiwifruit species, which belong to the family Actinidiaceae and the genus Actinidia Lindl., are dioecious, large, deciduous, woody vines indigenous to China. At present, China stands at the forefront globally in terms of both the cultivation area and yield of kiwifruit, which has led to marked social, economic, and ecological benefits. Kiwifruit cultivation has emerged as a key industry propelling China’s targeted poverty alleviation, rural revitalization, and ecological system construction11.

In kiwifruit production, male pollinizer plants play a pivotal role. In New Zealand, the varieties of male kiwifruit plants are relatively stable. In contrast, in China, there is a lack of sufficient focus on the breeding of male kiwifruit plants. In practical production, the allocation of male plants and the male-to-female ratio are not scientifically regulated. Some kiwifruit orchards even entirely discard the use of male plants, relying solely on purchasing pollen from unregulated sources and conducting artificial pollination with commercial pollen. This practice has resulted in the spread of diseases such as bacterial canker through pollen as a vector, inconsistent fruit quality, and unstable annual yields.

Previous studies have demonstrated that different pollens exert both metaxenic (direct effect of pollen genotype on seed or fruit traits4, and xenic (effect of pollen on maternal tissues of the fruit4, effects on kiwifruit, affecting crucial commercial traits12–14, including fruit size, shape, flavor, nutritional composition, storability, and seed characteristics, in varieties such as Jinkui15, Hongyang16–18, Yate19, and Guichang20. However, these effects are not uniformly beneficial.

Therefore, the selection of appropriate pollinizer varieties for kiwifruit production is highly important, serving as a vital determinant of both the external appearance and internal quality of kiwifruit and helping safeguard the sustainable development of the kiwifruit industry in China, ensuring stable yields, high-quality products, and reduced disease risk. This research on kiwifruit pollination examines whether pollen exhibits variations in performance, not only tackling pressing production challenges but also securing the long-term prosperity of the kiwifruit sector, a prosperity deeply intertwined with China’s food security, economic growth, and ecological balance.

Materials and methods

Basic information on the experimental sites

The trial sites are located at the Miliu Red Heart Kiwifruit Demonstration Base (geographical coordinates: 26.38°N and 104.96°E; altitude: 1480 m, in Luomo Village, Miliu Town) and the Shaomi Hongyang Kiwifruit Demonstration Base (coordinates: 26.43°N and 104.90°E; altitude: 1300 m, in Guolipu Village, Shaomi Town), both in Shuicheng District, Liupanshui City, Guizhou Province, China. The region experiences an annual average temperature of 12.3 °C and annual precipitation ranging from 1100 mm to 1200 mm. This area experiences an average sunshine duration of 1900 h per year, a frost-free period of 300 days per year, and significant diurnal temperature variation, which is characteristic of a typical warm and humid plateau subtropical monsoon climate. The soil in the experimental orchard is classified as Haplic Acrisol, with moderate to high fertility and weak acidity.

A total of 13 pollen donors were included in this study, comprising 9 male plants of A. chinensis (labeled ‘a-1’ to ‘a-9’), 3 male plants of A. deliciosa (labeled ‘a-10’ to ‘a-12’), and a commercial pollen control (labeled CK). Among these, ‘a-7’ and ‘a-12’ represent the ‘Mushan No. 2’ and ‘Mushan No. 3’ varieties, respectively, both sourced from the Wuhan Botanical Garden of the Chinese Academy of Sciences; ‘a-2’ is a local elite plant provided by the Agriculture and Rural Affairs Bureau of Heping County(Guangdong Province, China), and the remaining plants are elite male plants collected within Guizhou Province. Details are provided in Table S1. Prior to pollination, the viability of all pollen exceeded 60%.

Observation of flowering phenology and floral organ traits

The flowering period and floral organ traits were recorded according to the methods described in the “Descriptors and Data Standards for Kiwifruit Germplasm Resources”21.

Pollination

From April 2 to April 4, 2024, artificial pollination was carried out in the ‘Hongyang’ kiwifruit orchards located in Miluo town and Shaomi town, Shuicheng District. Mature ‘Hongyang’ kiwifruit trees with uniform growth characteristics were carefully selected for the experiment. Specifically, fruit-bearing branches, measuring 30–40 cm in length, were chosen from various parts of the tree canopy, ensuring that they had similar growth conditions. Five healthy flower buds were retained from the middle part of each selected fruit-bearing branch. During the large-bud stage, the selected fruit-bearing branches were enclosed in white grape-specific paper bags with dimensions of 42 cm (length) × 30 cm (width). Once the flowers reached full bloom, the bags were removed, and the ‘Hongyang’ female flowers were manually pollinated with the 13 different pollen donors. Immediately after pollination, each flower was individually re bagged using white strawberry-specific paper bags measuring 10 cm (length) × 7.5 cm (width). Each pollen donor was applied to four fruiting branches on two separate trees at each experimental site, and the entire process was replicated three times. Ten days after pollination, the bags were removed, and the fruit set was recorded 30 days later.

Botanical characteristic indicators

On August 20, 2024, kiwifruit with a soluble solid content of 7.0% were harvested and transported to the laboratory for further analysis. Subsequently, 20 fruit were carefully selected on the basis of uniform size, absence of pests and diseases, and relatively consistent maturity.

A series of physical parameters were measured, including single-fruit weight, longitudinal diameter (L), transverse diameter (the maximum diameter of the cross-section, denoted W1), lateral diameter (the minimum diameter of the cross-section, denoted W2), and dry matter content. The single-fruit weight was accurately measured using an electronic balance, whereas the longitudinal, transverse, and lateral diameters were precisely measured with a Vernier caliper. The dry matter content was determined using the drying method.

After the initial measurements, the fruit were stored at room temperature until they softened and reached full ripeness. Then, a comprehensive evaluation of the fruit quality indices was conducted. The methods for assessing the color, flavor, and aroma of the flesh were based on the approach described by Zhang Weimin et al.22.

The inner and outer flesh colors were quantified on a scale from 1 to 11, corresponding to light green, medium green, dark green, yellow-green, light yellow, medium yellow, dark yellow, yellow-orange, orange, red, and red-purple, respectively. The core color was represented on a scale of 1 to 6, indicating white, green-white, yellow-white, orange, red, and red-purple, respectively.

The fruit flavor was evaluated on a scale of 1 to 7, representing astringent, bitter, sour, slightly sour, sweet - sour, sour - sweet, and sweet, respectively. The flesh aroma was scored from 1 to 5, indicating off-flavor, no aroma, light aroma, moderate aroma, and strong aroma, respectively. The flesh texture was rated from 1 to 5, corresponding to coarse, slightly coarse, medium, slightly fine, and fine, respectively. The hardness of the core was classified as either hard (represented by 1) or soft (represented by 2). The seed color was represented on a scale of 1 to 6, indicating purple-red, yellow, light brown, coffee, black, and red–brown, respectively.

Following fruit maturation, five fruits were selected per repetition for internal quality determination, with three repetitions per treatment. Fruit firmness was measured using a GY-1 fruit firmness tester. The total soluble solid (TSS) content was determined with a handheld refractometer. The total acid content was measured via the acid‒base titration method, and the vitamin C content was measured using the 2,6-dichlorophenolindophenol titration method. Detailed methods refer to the literature by Zhong Weimin et al.22. The flesh lightness (L* value), red–green value (a* value), yellow–blue value (b* value), color difference (△E), and hue ratio (H) of the kiwifruit flesh were determined using a colorimeter (Konica Minolta portable colorimeter, CR − 400, Japan). These parameters were calculated according to the method described by Zhang Min et al.20. Each physiological index was measured in triplicate to ensure data reliability.

Data analysis

The experimental data were statistically processed and organized using Microsoft Excel, with each physiological index results expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Correlation analysis and principal component analysis were performed using SPSS statistical software, and principal component was extracted based on the criterion of feature values greater than one. The 21 quality characteristics data were standardized as X1, ……, X21, which multiplied the feature vectors with the standardized data, to obtain the score expressions of the principal components. To explore relationships between variables and reduce data dimensionality, we established a comprehensive quality characteristics evaluation equation, employing the principal component variance contribution rate as its weighting mechanism.

Results and analysis

Floral characteristics of male kiwifruit plants and their synchronization with ‘Hongyang’ in flowering period

Table 1 reveals distinct variations in the flowering period and floral traits among male kiwi plants of different species. In the tested material, A. chinensis ‘a-2’ and ‘a-6’ exhibited the earliest flowering, with a relatively long flowering period, whereas A. deliciosa ‘a-10’ and ‘a-12’ exhibited the latest and longest flowering phases.

With respect to floral organ traits, the floral node position, number of sepals, and number of petals per inflorescence branch varied minimally. However, significant differences were observed in the number of flowers per flowering branch, corolla diameter, number of filaments, and anther shape.

Among the tested materials, the three A. deliciosa samples featured pleiochasium inflorescences with a profusion of flowers per branch. Each flowering branch usually had 1 to 10 flowering nodes, bearing approximately 28 to 62 flowers. The corolla diameter exceeded 5.0 cm, and the number of sepals ranged from 6 to 7. Each flower contained more than 170 stamens and anthers, with oblong or sword-shaped anthers that were yellow or orange‒yellow in color. The style was greenish-white or white. The flowering period of these plants occurred in late April, more than 23 days after that of ‘Hongyang’ (which flowers in late March).

In contrast, the nine A. chinensis male plant samples predominantly presented single flowers and dichasium inflorescences. Only ‘a-5’ and ‘a-6’ presented pleiochasium inflorescences with 26 to 27 flowers. The corolla diameter was between 3.0 and 4.0 cm, and the number of sepals ranged from 6 to 7. Each flower had 32 to 57 stamens and anthers, with oblong, kidney-shaped, or sword-shaped anthers that were yellow or orange‒yellow. The style was also greenish-white or white.

The main flowering period of A. chinensis is from late March to mid-April. The ‘a-2’ and ‘a-6’ plants bloomed 2 to 4 days earlier than ‘Hongyang’ (late March); ‘a-1’ and ‘a-5’ were in sync with ‘Hongyang’; ‘a-3’ and ‘a-7’ flowered 2 to 3 days later than ‘Hongyang’; ‘a-4’ and ‘a-8’ bloomed 5 to 10 days later than ‘Hongyang’; and ‘a-9’ bloomed more than 10 days after ‘Hongyang’.

Effects of different pollination treatments on the fruit set of ‘Hongyang’ kiwifruit

Figure 1 illustrates the varying impacts of different pollination treatments on the fruit set of ‘Hongyang’ kiwifruit. Among them, A. deliciosa ‘a-12’ and ‘a-10’, along with A. chinensis ‘a-8’, presented the highest average fruit set, reaching 96.39%, 95.91%, and 93.82%, respectively. A. chinensis ‘a-1’ and ‘a-6’ followed, with average fruit set exceeding 93%. In contrast, A. chinensis ‘a-9’ had the lowest average fruit set, which was significantly (P < 0.05) lower than those of the other 12 treatments. There were no significant differences in the fruit set among the remaining 12 treatments.

Moreover, the fruit set at the Miluo base in Mishui city was higher than that at the Shaomi base in Mishui city. Additionally, the fruit set resulting from pollination with A. deliciosa pollen relatively surpassed that resulting from pollination with A. chinensis pollen.

At the Miluo base, A. deliciosa ‘a-10’ and ‘a-12’ and A. chinensis ‘a-8’ and ‘a-6’ exhibited the highest fruit set, all exceeding 97%. Conversely, A. chinensis ‘a-2’, ‘a-3’, and ‘a-7’ exhibited lower fruit set, all below 90%. The fruit set of the other six treatments were all above 90%.

At the Shaomi base, A. deliciosa ‘a-12’ and A. chinensis ‘a-3’, ‘a-7’, and ‘a-2’ achieved the highest fruit set, all above 93%. On the other hand, A. chinensis ‘a-5’, ‘a-4’ and ‘a-6’, and A. deliciosa ‘a-9’ and ‘a-11’ exhibited lower fruit set, all below 90%. The fruit set of the other four treatments were all above 90%.

Effects of different pollination treatments on the external quality indicators of ‘Hongyang’ kiwifruit

Tables 2 and 3 showed that different pollination treatments at different bases significantly (P < 0.05) influenced the single-fruit weight, longitudinal diameter, transverse diameter, lateral diameter, and fruit shape index of ‘Hongyang’ kiwifruit. Notably, the fruit size and single-fruit weight from the pollination treatments at the Miluo base were smaller than those at the Shaomi base.

At the Shuicheng Miluo base, among the pollination treatments, ‘a-10’ had the greatest single-fruit weight and longitudinal diameter. The control (CK) presented the largest transverse diameter and transverse–lateral ratio but the smallest fruit shape index. In contrast, ‘a-2’ had the largest fruit shape index but the smallest transverse–lateral ratio, and ‘a-11’ had the smallest single-fruit weight and transverse diameter. All these differences were significant (P < 0.05) compared with the values for other treatments.

At the Shuomi base in Shuicheng, ‘a-6’ had the highest single-fruit weight, and ‘a-12’ had the largest fruit transverse diameter. CK again had the largest fruit transverse and lateral diameters but the smallest fruit shape index. Moreover, ‘a-8’ had the largest fruit shape index, ‘a-1’ had the largest transverse-to-lateral ratio, and ‘a-9’ had the smallest single-fruit weight as well as the smallest longitudinal, transverse, and lateral diameters. These differences were also significant (P < 0.05) compared with the values for other treatments.

Effects of different pollination treatments on the internal quality indicators of ‘Hongyang’ kiwifruit

As shown in Tables 4 and 5, no significant differences were detected in the hardness and color angle indicators of ‘Hongyang’ fruit across the different pollination treatments at various bases. However, significant effects (P < 0.05) of varying degrees were noted for other measured internal quality indicators, including the dry matter, soluble solid, total sugar, total acid, and vitamin C contents, solid‒acid ratio, sugar‒acid ratio, and flesh color. The overall fruit quality in the pollination treatments at the Miluo base was superior to that at the Shaomi base. Among the different pollination treatments at the Miluo base in Mishui city, ‘a-5’ presented the highest total sugar content, ‘a-6’ presented the highest total acid content but the lowest solid-acid ratio, pulp brightness, and color difference, ‘a-7’ presented the highest vitamin C content, ‘a-8’ presented the highest solid-acid and sugar-acid ratios but the lowest total acid and vitamin C content, ‘a-10’ presented the highest red‒green value but the lowest sugar-acid ratio, ‘a-11’ presented the highest pulp brightness and color difference, and ‘a-12’ presented the lowest total sugar content, with all these results being significantly (P < 0.05) higher or lower than those for the other treatments. Additionally, ‘a-5’ had the highest dry matter content, ‘a-6’ presented the highest soluble solid content, ‘a-10’ presented the highest fruit color ratio, and ‘a-12’ presented the highest yellow‒blue value in the fruit, although no significant differences were found compared with the other treatments. Among the various pollination treatments conducted at the Shuomi base in Water city, CK presented the highest total sugar content, pulp lightness, and color difference but presented the lowest dry matter content, red–green value, and color ratio. Moreover, ‘a-4’ presented the highest solid‒acid ratio and sugar‒acid ratio but presented the lowest total acid content; ‘a-5’ presented the highest total sugar content; ‘a-7’ presented the highest dry matter content; ‘a-9’ exhibited the highest total acid content but the lowest soluble solid content, solid‒acid ratio, and sugar‒acid ratio; and ‘a-10’ presented the highest soluble solid content, red‒green value, and color ratio but had the lowest vitamin C content. Conversely, ‘a-11’ presented the highest vitamin C content; ‘a-12’ presented the highest yellow-blue value but the lowest total sugar content. Moreover, ‘a-6’ presented the lowest pulp lightness, yellow‒blue value, and color difference. All these differences were significantly greater or lower (P < 0.05) than the values for the other treatments.

Effects of different pollination treatments on the sensory quality indicators of ‘Hongyang’ kiwifruit

As presented in Table S2, a comprehensive analysis of the sensory quality of ‘Hongyang’ kiwifruit subjected to different pollination treatments at distinct experimental bases was conducted. Under the experimental conditions, there were no significant differences (P > 0.05) in the sensory indicators related to fruit aroma among all the treatments across the bases. However, notable variations were observed in other key sensory evaluation indicators, including the coloration of the exocarp, endocarp, and core; the flavor profile; the fineness of the flesh; and the hardness of the central column.

Overall, a comparative assessment of the sensory evaluation indicators showed that the fruit from the Miluo base generally exhibited superior quality compared with those from the Shaomi base. Across all the pollination treatments, a common characteristic was the lack of a distinct aroma in the fruit. The cores presented a uniform white or yellowish-white color, and the flesh texture was predominantly fine or relatively fine.

Results from the Miluo base (Shui Cheng Mi Luo)

For the different pollination treatments implemented at the Miluo base, the results were as follows:

Mesocarp color: The mesocarp color substantially varied among the pollination treatments. The ‘a-8’ treatment exhibited a deep yellow mesocarp, which is considered a desirable trait in terms of visual appeal. Conversely, ‘a-1’, ‘a-9’, and ‘a-11’ had relatively few appealing mesocarp colors, exhibiting a yellowish-green hue. The remaining treatments presented mesocarps with light yellow or medium yellow coloration, which was also within the expected range for high-quality ‘Hongyang’ kiwifruit. Statistical analysis confirmed that these color differences were significant (P < 0.05) among the treatments.

Endocarp color: The endocarp color also varied among the treatments. The ‘a-3’, ‘a-6’, and ‘a-9’ treatments presented red endocarps, whereas the other treatments presented purplish-red endocarps. These differences in endocarp color may be attributed to genetic factors associated with the pollen donors or differential biochemical processes induced by the pollination treatments.

Central column hardness: The hardness of the central column was another differentiating factor. Under the experimental conditions, the central columns of fruit from ‘a-4’ and ‘a-12’ were hard, whereas those of fruit from the other treatments were soft. This difference in central column hardness can potentially impact the overall eating experience and postharvest handling of the fruit.

Results from the Shaomi base (Shuomi in Water city)

For the different pollination treatments implemented at the Shaomi base, the results were as follows:

Mesocarp color: As observed at the Miluo base, the color of the mesocarp at the Shaomi base varied. Only the mesocarps of CK, ‘a-5’, ‘a-7’, ‘a-8’, ‘a-10’, and ‘a-12’ had an inferior appearance, presenting a yellow-green color. The other treatments produced light yellow or medium yellow mesocarps. These color differences were statistically significant (P < 0.05), indicating that the pollination treatments had a distinct influence on mesocarp color development.

Endocarp color: The ‘a-1’, ‘a-3’, ‘a-5’, ‘a-6’, ‘a-10’, and ‘a-12’ treatments presented red endocarps, whereas the remaining treatments presented purplish-red endocarps. This pattern of variation in endocarp color is consistent with the findings at the Miluo base, suggesting the existence of a common underlying mechanism related to pollination-induced changes in fruit pigmentation.

Central column hardness: The central column of the ‘a-4’ fruit was hard, whereas that of fruit from the other treatments was soft. This consistency in the variation in central column hardness across the two bases further emphasizes the role of the pollen donor in influencing this particular fruit trait.

In conclusion, the different pollination treatments had a significant effect on the sensory quality of ‘Hongyang’ kiwifruit, particularly in terms of fruit color and central column hardness. These findings provide valuable insights for the selection of appropriate pollen donors in kiwifruit production to improve the overall sensory quality of the fruit.

Effects of different pollination treatments on seed traits of ‘Hongyang’ kiwifruit

As shown in Table S3, there were significant differences (P < 0.05) in the number of seeds, thousand-seed weight, and seed color of ‘Hongyang’ fruit under different pollination treatments across various bases. In the Mituo base of Shuicheng, among the different pollination treatments, the seed color of ‘a-9’ and ‘a-1’ was coffee brown, whereas that of the other treatments was light brown. Among the different pollination treatments at the Shuomi base in Water city, the ‘a-6’ fruit had the highest number of seeds, reaching 609, whereas the CK fruit had the lowest number, with 172 seeds, with a difference of 437 seeds between the two. The ‘a-3’ seeds had the highest thousand-seed weight at 1.49 g, whereas the CK seeds had the lowest thousand-seed weight, which was 69.80% of that of ‘a-3’. The seeds of ‘a-10’, ‘a-11’, and ‘a-12’ were coffee colored, whereas the seeds of the other treatments were light brown.

Principal component analysis of fruit quality indicators of ‘Hongyang’ kiwifruit under different pollination treatments

Principal component analysis was conducted on 21 indicators, including fruit set, fruit quality, and seed traits, from the 13 different pollination treatments. As shown in Table 6, the first six principal components with eigenvalues greater than 1 were extracted from both experimental bases, with cumulative contribution rates of 89.882% and 90.187%, respectively. The first principal component from the Miluo base consisted mainly of soluble solids, total acidity, the solid‒acid ratio, the sugar‒acid ratio, flesh color (flesh brightness, yellow‒blue value b*, color difference △E, and hue △H), and the transverse‒lateral ratio, reflecting the quality and color of the fruit; the second principal component included mainly single-fruit weight, fruit size (longitudinal diameter, lateral diameter, and fruit shape index), dry matter content, total sugar content, and seed thousand‒grain weight, reflecting the morphology and flavor of the fruit; the third principal component consisted of the fruit setting rate and vitamin C content, reflecting the fruit setting condition and the main characteristic nutritional components; and the fourth, fifth, and sixth principal components were the seed number, fruit transverse diameter, and flesh red‒green value, reflecting the fruit shape, seed quantity, and flesh coloration, respectively. Moreover, the first principal component at the ShaoMi base was composed primarily of the soluble solid content, total acid content, solid-to-acid ratio, sugar-to-acid ratio, fruit set, single-fruit weight, fruit longitudinal diameter, vitamin C content, and thousand-grain weight, reflecting the nutritional quality of the fruit and the fruit setting condition. The second principal component included primarily fruit size (transverse diameter, lateral diameter, and the fruit shape index) and flesh color (flesh brightness, yellow–blue value, color difference ΔE, and hue ΔH), thereby reflecting the morphology and coloration of the fruit. The third principal component represented the total sugar content, which is the primary nutritional characteristic. The fourth, fifth, and sixth principal components included the dry matter content and the transverse‒lateral ratio, seed count, and flesh red‒green value, respectively, which reflect fruit shape, seed quantity, and flesh coloration.

On the basis of principal component analysis, six principal component scores (Table S4) could be calculated using the eigenvectors and standardized values of the fruit quality indicators for each treatment, thereby providing a comprehensive evaluation of the impact of different pollen pollination treatments on ‘Hongyang’ fruit quality. A higher score indicates better overall performance of the fruit quality under that particular pollination treatment. As presented in Table S4, ‘a-10’, ‘a-12’, ‘a-2’, ‘a-6’, and ‘a-7’ occupied the top five positions in terms of average scores and overall rankings, with ‘a-10’ achieving the highest average score, suggesting that these pollens contributed to better overall fruit quality performance. Conversely, ‘a-8’, ‘a-1’, CK, and ‘a-3’ exhibited moderate average scores and overall rankings, reflecting medium overall fruit quality performance. In contrast, ‘a-4’, ‘a-11’, ‘a-9’, and ‘a-5’ had lower average scores and were ranked lower, indicating poorer overall fruit quality performance. Notably, differences in the principal component scores and rankings of different pollen treatments across various bases were detected. At the Miluo base, ‘a-10’, ‘a-9’, ‘a-1’, ‘a-7’, ‘a-8’, and ‘a-2’ had positive scores and ranked within the top six, whereas ‘a-6’, ‘a-12’, CK, and ‘a-5’ presented moderate scores and rankings; ‘a-3’, ‘a-4’, and ‘a-11’ ranked in the bottom three. At the Shaomi base, ‘a-12’, ‘a-2’, ‘a-6’, ‘a-10’, and ‘a-3’ scored above 0.2 and ranked in the top five, whereas CK, ‘a-7’, ‘a-4’, ‘a-9’, and ‘a-10’ had moderate scores and rankings. On the other hand, ‘a-1’, ‘a-5’, and ‘a-9’ scored negatively and ranked in the bottom three.

Discussion

Understanding the floral organ traits related to pollination in male kiwifruit plants and their xenic effects on female cultivars is important for determining the arrangement of pollinizer varieties, improving fruit quality, and increasing yield23. Observation of 12 male plants from the two main cultivated species (A. chinensis and A. deliciosa) showed that the number of anthers in A. deliciosa was significantly greater than that in A. chinensis, and the flowering period of A. deliciosa occurred later than that of A. chinensis. Moreover, there were considerable differences in floral traits among male plants within the same species, which is consistent with previous studies24–27 on the floral traits of male kiwifruit plants.In this study, only the ‘a-6’ flowering period covered the entire flowering period of ‘Hongyang’ kiwifruit. When a mismatch in flowering periods occurs during production, it is advisable to select 2–3 superior male plants whose flowering periods align with those of the female varieties as much as possible. For example, male plants are arranged as early-flowering types (flowering 2–3 days earlier than the female varieties) + late -flowering types (flowering 2–3 days later than the female varieties) or early-flowering types (flowering 2–3 days earlier than the female varieties) + synchronous types (flowering at the same time as the female varieties) + late-flowering types (flowering 2–3 days later than the female varieties). This arrangement relatively ensures that the male flowering period covers the entire flowering period of the female varieties. However, against the backdrop of global warming, cold demand plays a critical role in flower induction and blooming period regulation for deciduous fruit trees like kiwifruit. The ‘Hongyang’ kiwifruit cultivar, classified as a low cold-demand variety (minimum cold requirement: 576 CU)28, maintains robust flower bud differentiation and relatively uniform blooming patterns despite frequent warm winters in experimental regions. Nevertheless, this study primarily focused on observing flowering synchrony between male and female plants without delving into the cold demand thresholds of tested male cultivars. Future research should systematically measure cold demand parameters across male cultivars and develop a cold demand matching model for male-female plants to enhance pollination synergy efficiency and fruit set stability.

The effects of different pollination treatments on the fruit set of ‘Hongyang’ varied across different experimental bases. The fruit set of A. deliciosa was greater than that of A. chinensis among the pollination treatments, possibly because of the close genetic relationship between A. chinensis and A. deliciosa19. However, the fruit set of A. deliciosa ‘a-11’ and A. chinensis ‘a-9’ were lower than those of other superior strains within the same species, indicating that inappropriate selection of pollinizer trees can reduce the fruit set5. In this study, the external indicators, internal quality, and seed characteristics of the ‘Hongyang’ fruit were all affected by different types of pollen. There were varying degrees of stability in the effects of the same pollen treatment on the external indicators, internal quality, and seed characteristics of the ‘Hongyang’ fruit across different bases, indicating a significant xenic effect. Significant differences in xenia were detected in ‘Hongyang’ fruit in terms of single-fruit weight, longitudinal diameter, transverse diameter, total sugar content, total acid content, vitamin C content, flesh color, thousand-seed weight, and seed number under this experimental conditions. Particularly notable differences were found in the single-fruit weight, sugar‒acid ratio, vitamin C content, flesh color, and seed number. These findings indicate that the xenic effect of the use of pollen from different superior male plants has considerable potential for improving the fruit size and nutritional composition of ‘Hongyang’ kiwifruit. These findings are consistent with many previous studies on xenic effects in major kiwifruit cultivars, such as ‘Guichang’, ‘Hayward’, and ‘Xuxiang’5,17,20,29–31. Although the impact of the same pollination treatment on the seed trait indices of ‘Hongyang’ fruit exhibited varying degrees of stability across different bases, pollination of the diploid Chinese kiwifruit ‘Hongyang’ with hexaploid A. deliciosa and tetraploid A. chinensis resulted in fewer seeds at both bases. This outcome is probably attributed to the pollination between female and male plants with different ploidies, which is accompanied by the degeneration of ovules and nucelli, leading to a reduction in the quantity and quality of well-formed seeds32.

This study investigated the effects of pollination treatments with different pollen donors on the fruit quality of ‘Hongyang’. However, owing to the large number of indicators and significant differences among multiple indicators across treatments, it was challenging to objectively perform comprehensive evaluation of the xenic effect. Principal component analysis has been increasingly used in recent years for comprehensive evaluation of fruit quality in kiwifruit16, pear33, and litchi34 following pollen pollination. The cumulative contribution rates of the first six principal components extracted from 21 indicators were 89.882% and 90.187%. Among these, the cumulative contribution rates of the first three principal componentscollectively reflectied fruit morphology, flavor, flesh color, and fruit set. The cumulative contribution rates of the latter principal components reflectied mainly fruit shape, seed number, and flesh coloration. The comprehensive scores of each pollen were calculated on the basis of the principal component analysis results. Among them, ‘a-10’ and ‘a-2’ had high comprehensive scores at both bases, indicating that the fruit pollinated by these two male plants presented excellent stability in terms of overall quality. The fruit of other superior plants were less stable in terms of overall quality.

There are genetic differences in pollen characteristics among different male kiwifruit plants25,26,35,36, and this species has a significant xenic effect12–15,17,19,20. Methods for the selection of suitable male plants for female cultivars should not only ensure that they have a flowering period that is largely synchronized with that of the female cultivars, produce a large amount of pollen, have a high pollen germination rate, and show strong compatibility with the female cultivars but also pay special attention to the xenic effect for comprehensive evaluation11. On the basis of principal component scores, floral organ characteristics, and flowering periods, this study ranked and evaluated the tested pollen across species and concluded that the flowering periods of the male kiwifruit plants ‘a-6’, ‘a-2’, and ‘a-7’ of A. chinensis synchronized well that of ‘Hongyang’; these plants produced abundant pollen and exhibited excellent xenic effects, making them suitable as compatible male plants for ‘Hongyang’. The male ‘a-10’ and ‘a-12’ kiwifruit plants produced a large amount of pollen, and the overall fruit traits of ‘Hongyang’ were superior after pollination. However, owing to the mismatch in flowering periods, their pollen can be collected in the current year, stored at low temperatures, and used for artificial pollination of ‘Hongyang’ in the following year. This study was conducted only in mountain kiwifruit orchards in Guizhou Province, China, with limited geographical representativeness. It did not involve orchards in basins or hilly terrain to verify the compatibility of the selected male plants. Further regional trials over multiple years and sites could be carried out to validate their stability and promote the widespread adoption of compatible male plants. Meanwhile, molecular biology techniques were combined to deeply explore the direct perception mechanism of pollen and reveal its genetic basis.

Conclusion

The floral traits of male kiwifruit plants exhibited significant variation both among different species and within the same species. Following pollination, a noticeable xenic effect was observed on the fruit set rate, as well as on the external and internal quality indicators of the fruit and the seed characteristics of ‘Hongyang’. Through principal component analysis and a comprehensive evaluation of floral traits, it was concluded that the male plants ‘a-6’, ‘a-2’, and ‘a-7’ of A. chinensis are suitable compatible male plants for ‘Hongyang’, whereas the male plants ‘a-10’ and ‘a-12’ of A. deliciosa are deemed appropriate for artificial pollination of ‘Hongyang’.
