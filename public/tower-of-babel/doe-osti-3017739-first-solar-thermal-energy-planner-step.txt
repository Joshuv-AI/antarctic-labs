1 Project Overview
1.1 Background & Motivation
Industrial facilities consume about 26 Quads annually in the US, representing 28% of primary
energy consumption in the US 1, trailing only the energy sector of transportation. Industry uses this
energy to manufacture chemicals (e.g., nitrogenous fertilizers, petroleum, plastics), paper products,
food and beverages, steel, concrete, among many other feedstocks and products. On-site
combustion of fuels (e.g., natural gas and petroleum) generates the vast majority electrical and
thermal energy necessary to run these facilities. Thermal energy or process heat is used to drive
chemical reactions (e.g., petroleum refining, steam-methane reforming), dry or cook products and
represents between half and two-thirds of the energy consumed by industry. Industrial process
heat, therefore, represents around a fifth of annual energy consumption and a huge market for
alternative, low-cost technologies.

Solar thermal is one category of promising technologies aiming to tap this market, reduce energy
costs, and increase reliability. Solar thermal spans a wide variety of technologies – see Figure 1
for some examples. Solar thermal technologies can be concentrating and non-concentrating. Flat
plate collectors and evacuated tubes are often found on rooftops of residential and commercial
buildings to provide hot water for space heating. Parabolic trough and linear Fresnel are classified
as “linear” systems and concentrate direct solar radiation onto a central tube that contains a moving
fluid. Tower solar thermal technologies often contain thousands of two-axis tracking mirrors called
heliostats that focus on sunlight onto a central tower receiver that heats a fluid or media inside.

Concentrating solar thermal (CST) systems are often used for electricity generation, often also
referred to as concentrating solar power (CSP) systems with around 7 GWs deployed around the
world as of 2023 2. CSP systems convert the thermal energy generated and stored in the heat
transfer media into electricity using a power cycle. For industrial process heat applications, the
thermal energy generated can instead be used as thermal energy, removing the need for a power
cycle (which is often only 20-30% efficient and a significant capital expense).

Solar industrial process heat (SIPH) systems have already been deployed such as supplying
spacing heating loads at the Indianapolis International Airport 3 as well as steam demand at
California Dairy in California 4, Heineken in Spain 5, and coming soon to a Mars Pet Care facility
in Australia 6.

CST systems have been under development and deployed for decades, but the market in the US
and globally is just starting to ramp up. A main barrier to future deployment and growth is
awareness of these technologies and their cost-competitiveness for industrial facilities. Existing
CST tools often require coding experience, detailed understanding of the technologies themselves,
getting quotes for specific suppliers, and/or expensive consultation. Comparatively, there are a
multitude easy-to-use, free tools for homeowners and businesses to evaluate the potential of solar
PV such as Google Project Sunroof or NLR’s own tool REopt. The dearth of tools means solar
thermal technologies are often overlooked by energy consumers, technical assistance programs,
and federal agencies compared to other sources (e.g., PV, hydrogen, electrification, geothermal).
Additionally, a critical information gap exists in the cost-competitiveness of SIPH to business-as-
usual more generally, limiting motivation for additional R&D as well as consideration by key
decision makers.

1.2 Task Summary
The Solar Thermal Energy Planner (STEP 1) project aimed to address this two-fold problem with
three primary tasks; these tasks are summarized here.

 2

1.2.1 Task 1: Tool Development
Task 1 required a brand-new decision support tool called Solar Thermal Energy Planner or STEP
1. STEP 1 evaluates the techno-economics of SIPH systems for a site-specific industrial facility
accounting for land availability, process temperature, load profiles, and existing fuel costs among
others. STEP 1 is designed to be open-source, free and easy to use (i.e., no downloading, no
coding) to reach a broad audience – STEP 1 is hosted at NLR’s website (https://step1.nrel.gov) for
ubiquitous access. For the launch version of STEP 1, SIPH systems include various solar thermal
technologies, PV, and TES along with fuel and electricity (when PV is selected to enable resistive
heating of TES). To accomplish this task, a user interface (UI) needed to be built from the ground
up to be approachable, minimize necessary inputs, and meet the user where they are in their level
of understanding of their process heat needs.

1.2.2 Task 2: Nationwide Analysis
Task 2 required the project to analyze SIPH systems across the US to answer key questions
regarding the cost-competitiveness to offset fuel costs (in part or in fuel). Taking a nationwide
view enables examination of the sensitivity of cost-competitiveness to a multitude of factors such
as geospatial variability of solar resource profiles, natural gas prices, and industrial facility types
(different industries required different solar thermal technologies). Task 2 leveraged the
underlying techno-economic approach of STEP 1 but scaled the methodology to over 1,000
facilities using high-performance computing (HPC) resources at NLR. The analysis work
conducted in Task 2 was to form a draft of a peer-reviewed journal article for dissemination.

1.2.3 Task 3: Rapid Analysis
As the project progressed, an additional third task was created that leveraged the work of Task 2
but changed the set of industrial facilities and technologies examined. Task 3, called “Rapid
Analysis”, required the team focus on the food and beverage sector more specifically and only on
parabolic trough systems. The results of this analysis would be delivered to SETO under a
compressed timeline in FY24Q4.

2 Task 1: Tool Development
The STEP 1 tool development was a Task that took the entirety of the project period of
performance. Tool development, generally, is challenging due to the complexity of not only
creating the code to run the calculations behind the tool, but to wrap the code in an easy-to-use
user interface (UI) and control for the multitude of input combinations.

The STEP 1 project broke Task 1: Tool Development down into six milestones (see Table 1). First,
the STEP 1 team wanted to gather input from key stakeholders to inform decisions about the tool’s
development (Milestone 1.1.1: Stakeholder Engagement). With that input, the tool’s backend (i.e.,
computational code) and frontend (i.e., web interface) development began. The STEP 1 team broke
the backend workflow into multiple (10+) individual pieces called the Core Code Modules,

 4

distributed them to appropriate team members, built each individual module, tested the module’s
capabilities as a standalone unit, and then planned how to piece them back together to create a
coherent, functional workflow (Milestone 1.1.2: Core Code Modules & Architecture). In parallel,
the STEP 1 team continued to engage with relevant stakeholders to form a Technical Advisory
Committee; the stakeholders would be leveraged to support the successful completion of the STEP

Figure 5 shows the workflow and/or architecture of the STEP 1 tool. First, the necessary details
about the user’s facility is gathered as “User Inputs”; these inputs include their facility’s location
(used to obtain solar resource), process fluid and temperature (used to calculate energy demand
and assign appropriate solar technologies), land availability (used to calculate the upper limit on
space for solar panels and/or collectors to go), and other inputs. Next, STEP 1 calculates custom
default parameters that inform parameters of established solar technology performance models in
NLR’s System Advisory Model (SAM), a well-established and physics-based energy generating
and storage system simulation tool. The relevant solar technology performance models generate
annual hourly thermal production profiles which are then normalized. The thermal production
profile is an input to the mixed-integer linear program (MILP) that optimizes the size and dispatch
to find the least-cost combination of resources (i.e., PV+CST+TES+BESS with backup). The
MILP is a modified version of another NLR tool, REopt. REopt is a tool and optimization platform
written in Julia for optimizing hybridized, behind-the-meter energy generation and storage
systems. Once the least-cost combination of resources has been found, the financial metrics are
calculated and present to the user. The whole process is designed to take less than 20 minutes from
start to finish with the majority of time spent on the optimization step, but the optimization allows
for detailed assessment of multiple tradeoffs and sensitivities that could influence the economics
of solar+storage systems at a given facility. Still, in less than 20 minutes and for free, the user has
key financial metrics to help decide whether solar+storage systems are right for their industrial
facility.

2.2.1 Module #1: Optimization
The REopt® platform and accompanying analysis services optimize planning of generation,
storage, and controllable loads to maximize the value of integrated distributed energy systems for
buildings, campuses, and microgrids. Formulated as a mixed integer linear program, REopt (i)
recommends an optimally sized mix of renewable and distributed energy, conventional generation,
and energy storage technologies; (ii) provides a dispatch strategy for operating the technology mix
at maximum economic efficiency; and (iii) estimates the net present value of implementing those
technologies. REopt’s technology mix covers both electrical and (both hot and cold) thermal loads
and provides separate dispatch for multiple heating loads (e.g., domestic hot water, space heating,
and process heat). This section summaries the developments as part of the STEP 1 project made
to accommodate CST technologies to provide process heat and, optionally, electricity in REopt.
These improvements are in a feature branch of the REopt.jl GitHub repository available at
https://github.com/NREL/REopt.jl.

CST module
In the most recent phase, a CST module has been added to the source code to add concentrating
solar to the collection of heating technologies in REopt. The CST module accepts the following
parameters as input:
 • Cost factors, including capital cost, O&M, and MACRS depreciation schedule;
 • System sizing limits and land use rates;
 • Hourly production factors and electrical consumption factors;
 • Emissions rates; and,
 • Logic parameters indicating compatible destinations for the heat provided by the
 technology (i.e., eligible heating loads, storage technologies, and/or steam turbines).
While the logic parameters and emissions rates are user-specified with defaults in place, the
remaining parameters are recovered from the separate modules that interfaces with the SAM
simulation core to obtain outputs from the appropriate CST module in SAM for the technology
chosen.

While CSP is not explicitly added, there is an existing steam turbine technology in REopt, and
we have included specific logic parameters making it optional for the CST technology to only
provide heat to the steam turbine for an integrated CSP system.

 3. Objective function and emissions tracking
The objective function in REopt minimizes the sum of (i) capital costs, (ii) fixed and variable
O&M costs, (iii) fuel costs, (iv) electrical utility energy and demand charges, and (v) emissions
costs (if provided), net of any production incentives and exports of electricity to the grid. Grid
emissions for CO2, NOx, SO2, and PM2.5 are taken from the Cambium database
(https://www.nlr.gov/analysis/cambium) for domestic locations and defaults are in place for
multiple fuel types in REopt. In addition to accounting for emissions by including a nonzero cost,
there is an option to include a lower bound on the minimum CO2 emissions reduction from all
energy sources used to meet site loads. While none of these developments are specific to STEP 1,
they were useful in the nationwide analysis that was part of Task 2 and 3.

2.2.3 Module #3: Parabolic trough and molten salt tower, plus more
In additional to solar PV, the STEP 1 tool includes concentrating and non-concentrating solar
thermal technologies for thermal energy production. As mentioned in Section 2.1 on Stakeholder
Engagement, the original plan of including just parabolic trough and molten salt towers was not
sufficient. Therefore, the team expanded the list of solar thermal technologies considered to also
include flat plate collectors, evacuated tubes, and linear Fresnel technologies. These three
additional technologies are focused on lower temperature applications, especially flat plate
collectors and evacuated tubes which focus on hot water production. The stakeholder engagement
survey emphasized the large range of temperatures industrial users are interested in and, therefore,
the team found expanding the list of technologies considered to be advantageous to the usefulness
of the STEP 1 tool.

Five technologies now fall under the Module #3 umbrella: flat plate collector, evacuated tubes,
linear Fresnel, parabolic troughs, and molten salt tower. NLR’s System Advisory Model (SAM)

Tower: Defaults & Linearity
Tower technologies are not modular in the same way troughs, flat plates, and PV are. However,
there is interesting work on multi-tower systems and when those are advantageous over a single
larger tower. In STEP 1, multi-tower (or modular tower) systems are not considered and could be
a viable area of future development. Therefore, the team had to develop a unique workflow for
automating the design of a notional tower and the associated parameters. This standardized
workflow has a not been done before as towers are often designed for specific sites, with a
significant amount of time and resources. To develop this unique workflow, the team performed
several sensitivity analyses a priori for the user. The results of which informed a quick, empirical,
and analytical relationships where possible.

There are a multitude of nuances and parameters to the design of tower technology system. The
optimal value of these parameters is also highly complex and interdependent. Based on the
team’s experience, the top design parameters that should be revised for tower technologies are:

 1. Storage duration
 2. Receiver type
 3. Tower location
 4. Tower height
 5. Receiver dimensions
 6. Tube outside diameter (OD)
 7. Heliostat layout

These eight parameters can greatly influence the results in terms of performance and costs. The
workflow for how each of these key parameters are defined is presented here.

 21

 1. Storage duration

Storage duration is a fixed, STEP 1 default parameter (i.e., does not change with user inputs). Like
flat plate and troughs, the parameter’s value is specified to be whatever duration is required to
decouple thermal energy collection from storage duration and was determined through parametric
sweeps to be around 50 hours for a high solar resource location (i.e., Tucson, AZ).

 2. Receiver type

If the land boundary and tower location are not pre-defined by the user, then STEP 1 will determine
the receiver type based on the receiver capacity. Receiver capacity is equal to the user’s discharge
rated power multiplied by SM. Again, the default is to assume a SM of 3.0. If the receiver capacity
is below a threshold (e.g., 300 MWth), then the receiver type is cavity, otherwise the receiver type
is surround.

If the user specifies a land boundary and tower location in the UI, then STEP 1 will do an azimuth
calculation (i.e., field of view). Figure 15 visualizes this calculation. If the azimuth angle is less
than a threshold (i.e., 170 degrees), then the receiver type is cavity. If the azimuth angle is greater
than the threshold, then the receiver capacity determines the type; identical thresholds to receiver
capacity as if they user did not specific a land boundary and tower location.

  Figure 15. Azimuth calculator visualization. Red arc/circle indicates azimuth angle calculated.
 3. Tower location

In the UI, the user is able to market the exact location where they require the heat input to their
system. If the user specifies their heat input, then STEP 1 will assume this is the location for the
tower – see Figure 16. If the user does not specific the location of their input nor their land
boundaries, then STEP 1 will assume the tower is located generally at the site’s latitude and
longitude.

analytical function for tower height of these variables could be created. Again, this work is very
valuable for the development of STEP 1 and beyond. The quantity of sensitivity analysis has not
been done before and could be valuable for other CST tower work where needed.

For heliostat layout, the team has already documented and presented a module that can optimize
the heliostat field layout for irregular and discontinuous shapes. Therefore, that module will be

The linearity of tower cost, performance, and area are more uncertain than the previous two
technologies described because, again, towers are not as modular as troughs and flat plate

Under the same parametric sweep of tower, heliostat, and receiver cost assumptions, the

an analytical function to calculate a localized normalized installed cost value to accurately reflect

2.2.6 Module #6: Heat exchanger and heat transfer fluid selection
Module #6 was broken into two modules. The first covers the heat transfer fluid selection for the
user and then the other calculates the cost of the heat exchanger based on a fixed approach
temperature (50 C). The heat exchanger module captures the fact that thermal energy stored in the
heat transfer and storage media (i.e., the media that absorbs the solar radiation and is used in the
TES) is not perfectly nor freely converted into thermal energy used in the process. The thermal
energy will need to be extracted using a heat exchanger.

2.2.6.1 Heat transfer fluid selection
The heat transfer media (HTF) in a solar thermal system can greatly affect the performance and
cost of the technology and infrastructure. Many users of the STEP1 tool will not have the technical
background to select the most suitable HTF for their process. To address this issue, we developed
a module that automates the analysis and ranking of various HTFs according to the inputs that the

2.6.5 Input: Financial Page
The final input page is the Financial page. There is only one primary input on this page “Analysis
Years”. The input defines the duration of analysis which influences the cost of capital. STEP 1
assumes industrial facilities are okay with a 30-year payback period by default as industry is more
patient with capital than residential customers, which typically want a payback period of 10 years
or less. Additionally, the STEP 1 tool uses an internal rate of return (IRR) or discount rate of 11%
by default. The user can edit the IRR or discount rate assumption by expanding the “Advanced
Inputs” tab – see Figure 46. The user can also expand the Tax Credits section (also shown in Figure
46) to revise STEP 1’s tax credit assumptions. By default, STEP 1 assumes a 30% investment tax
credit (ITC) is applied to all PV, Li-ion, and TES capital expenditures, congruent with the current
48/48E tax credit. The current tax credit language is vague regarding the applicability of the 48/48E
to solar thermal technologies that only deliver heat; solar thermal technologies that produce
electricity (i.e., CSP) do seem to be eligible though. So, STEP 1 assumes the solar thermal

 63

technologies are not eligible, but the user can check the “Solar Thermal Tax Credit” box to apply
the 48/48E 30% ITC to solar thermal technologies as well. Additional tax credit bonuses are also
options the user can check to increase the ITC amount. The Help buttons link to the appropriate
tax credit language to help the user assess whether they qualify. Federal tax credits are subject to
change, and the STEP 1 team can revise these options in the future if needed and if time and
funding is made available to do so. Regardless, the user is cautioned to consult the latest Federal
tax credit availability and eligibility to futureproof the STEP 1 tool.

In the Advanced Inputs tab, the user can modify installed/capital cost assumption if they have
specific quotes from a supplier (or a supplier can input their own data here if running STEP 1 for

While Task 1 focused on the development of STEP 1 the web tool, Task 2 of the project focused
on applying the methodology underlying the tool to a broader analysis. While many studies have
previously looked at the technical potential of SIPH systems at a nationwide-level, little has been
done to understand the economic opportunity 31. Additionally, nationwide analysis efforts support
the by STEP 1 project support the inclusion of SIPH systems in future efforts to understand
emerging technologies for industrial process heat.

In this work, economic opportunity is defined as the opportunity to reduce “fuel” costs for
industrial facilities. Put another way, economic opportunity is defined as the cost-competitiveness
of SIPH systems compared to business-as-usual or alternatives. Business-as-usual is assumed to
be continuing to combust natural gas. If SIPH systems are to grow in market share, then there must
be an economic argument for industrial facilities. Therefore, Task 2 focused on a nationwide

First, the industrial energy dataset on which the analysis will be performed was created. The

Previous work by NLR has generated datasets such as the 2018 Industrial Energy Databook 32 and

To create this dataset, EPA’s Greenhouse Gas Reporting Program 35 (GHGRP) was used. The

made publicly available and can be downloaded from the EPA using the Envirofacts Query Builder
website 36. Using EPA’s standard emission factors and the fuel types specified by the reporting
facility, the emission rates can be converted to annual energy consumption.

3.1.2.2 Industrial Energy Dataset – Reduced Set
Next, a reduced set of facilities was created using established data clustering techniques. The
motivation for reducing the number of facilities explicitly examined in the analysis is reduce the
computational cost of considering similar facilities such that more resources can be dedicated to
sensitivity analysis.

The data clustering algorithm used to create the reduced set of facilities is called affinity
propagation. Affinity propagation is a useful data clustering technique because the algorithm picks
data points from within the full set instead of creating some notional averaged data point. The
critical parameter to define is the number of clusters the algorithm should create. For each cluster,
there is a single representative point for that given cluster. Clusters do not have to be equal in size.
The algorithm seeks to minimize the within-cluster-sum-of-squares (WCSS) which is measure of
error between the representative set and the full set. As the number of clusters specified decreases,
the minimum WCSS increases. In other words, the lower the number of clusters the greater the
error or difference between the representative set and the full set. Figure 48 demonstrates this
relationship. The appropriate number of clusters (i.e., representative data points) is in the “elbow
region” where the decreasing the number of clusters starts to have an increasing impact on WCSS
(i.e., relative error).

Figure 48. Relationship between number of clusters and relative error for the NAICS Code 311313.

For this analysis, the industrial dataset was clustered for each subset of NAICS codes to ensure
clusters represented the same type of facility. Each facility was clustered based on its annual
average direct normal irradiance (DNI) and global horizontal irradiance (GHI) in a typical
meteorological year. For all NAICS codes, the number of clusters was chosen once relative error
fell below 0.1. Figure 49 shows how this threshold was implemented for all NAICS codes.

Figure 50 shows the results of this data clustering in the two-dimensional space (normalized GHI

3.1.2.4 Metrics and Cost Assumptions
For the analysis, the primary metric is the total system’s levelized cost of heat (LCOH) (i.e., the
solar+storage system inclusive of any electrical and/or fuel purchases). For example, if the
solar+storage system supplies 50% of the annual thermal energy demand, then LCOH includes the
cost of fuel to supply the remaining 50%. The cost of land is excluded. The capital recover factor
(CRF) is calculated assuming a 30-year lifetime and 11% discount rate (i.e. CRF = 0.115). For all
facilities, the load profile is assumed be flat all year long (i.e., 24/7/365) hence the annual energy
produced (or delivered to the process) is divided by the thermal power demanded (P) multiplied
by 8760. Figure 53 visualizes this definition of LCOH.

To add context and measure cost-effectiveness, a metric corresponding to the business-as-usual
case is also calculated and defined as LCOHNG (see Figure 53 also). The business-as-usual case
represents the user continuing to combust natural gas for the entirety of their thermal energy
demand with state- and month-specific natural gas prices. In both metrics, the facility is assumed
to have an existing natural gas boiler and therefore the capital cost of the natural gas boiler is
excluded. Therefore, LCOHNG really represents a marginal cost of heat metric. The units of LCOH
and LCOHNG are both $/MWhth. LCOHNG includes O&M costs of the boiler at $1.50/MMBtu
which is equivalent to ~$5/MWhth. An 80% boiler efficiency is also assumed. In California, there
is a cap-and-trade program for greenhouse gas emissions from entities that emit more than 25,000
MTCO2e annually. The same threshold for being required to report annual emissions to the EPA’s
Greenhouse Gas Reporting Program from which facilities in our dataset were created. Therefore,
every Californian facility in our dataset is also subject to the state’s cap-and-trade program.
Currently, the cap-and-trade program has carbon credits trading for around $30/MTCO2 which is
equivalent to an additional $6.79/MWh to the LCOHNG for California.

LCOHNG is defined at the state-level, meaning all facilities within the state have the same LCOHNG
value from which to compare the LCOH of the solar+storage system. Figure 52 shows the variation
of LCOHNG across the US. The minimum and maximum LCOHNG corresponds to Texas at
$16.75/MWh and California at $66.05/MWh, respectively (excluding Hawaii at $128.05/MWh
where there are no facilities in our dataset), representing approximately 4x cost differential. The
median state is Ohio at $37.09/MWh. This calculation demonstrates the importance of using
localized fuel prices as the economics of solar+storage systems can vary significantly depending
on the baseline.

3.1.4 Nationwide Analysis: Discussion
The results of this analysis indicate SIPH systems can be cost-effective at substituting some of
industrial process heat demand around the US. The results indicate the significance of process
temperature leading to different CST technology and, therefore, different LCOH for similar solar
resources (i.e., location). While the results do not indicate a unanimous outcome, the results show
there is still a favorable and large market for CST technologies, particularly parabolic troughs, for
making inroads into industrial process heat. The analysis results match the trends seen in the
market today: initial deployments of systems in regions with high solar resource (i.e., desert SW
US, Spain) and high natural gas prices (e.g., California, Europe) and in lower temperature
applications (e.g., Breweries). As deployments grow, CST costs are expected to decline further
leading to additional market opportunities. As with any non-dispatchable energy source, the cost
and size of storage required to achieve higher and higher capacity factors diminishes the
economics. However, CST systems do integrate with low-cost TES well which limits the cost
increases compared to other common storage technologies (e.g., Li-ion).

3.1.5 Nationwide Analysis: Conclusion
The analysis presented provides a high-level view of the cost-competitiveness of SIPH systems in
a variety of industrial applications around the US. The analysis shows that it is important to include
the industrial process temperature and the geospatial distribution of industries to best understand
how and where SIPH systems could be deployed. For SIPH systems, the ideal would be all industry
is located in the sunny SW US, but industries have developed where they are located over decades
for a multitude of factors that cannot necessarily be easily changed to fit SIPH systems. Still, the
analysis shows promises and builds upon prior work to better include SIPH systems in the solution
space for industry energy demands. In higher temperature industrial applications located in
Northeastern region of the US (e.g., “Rust Belt”), SIPH systems might have limited deployment
opportunities today, but there is still a lot of room for the SIPH systems to grow.

suggestions given as the STEP 1 team socialized the project and tool with many stakeholders. Over
the course of the first year of the project’s work, the team has engaged continuously with industry
(e.g., thermal energy consumers and providers) and other important stakeholders (e.g., researchers,
SETO TMs, and other energy offices) to learn more about their needs from a tool like STEP 1 and
generally regarding challenges faced in implementing new solar thermal solutions. Through this
outreach, the team catalogued improvements and/or additions to the STEP 1 tool which would be
of value to key stakeholders with some suggestions implemented in the first version of the tool

advanced inputs). Expanding the capabilities of STEP 1 as proposed below will increase the
usefulness of the tool to answer more key questions raised about the prospects of solar thermal
energy for industrial heat applications, expanding its impact, and reducing costs.

6.1.1 Proposed Task #1: Expanded Technology Solution Space

The opportunity and interest from industry to hybridized solar thermal systems and demonstrate
economic value is high – the economics of hybridization is often the first and main question.
Industrial heat users are looking to electrification using electrified boilers, heat pumps, and
electrified-thermal energy storage. Direct electrification is promising due low capital costs and
the ability to procure low-cost electricity sources (e.g., PV). However, direct electrification of all
heat loads is not necessarily the most economical and presents significant concerns regarding
grid infrastructure (i.e., thermal energy demand is often significantly larger than a facility’s
electricity demand). Therefore, STEP 1 would be greatly improved to be able to examine the
complementary role of solar thermal assets to electrification – to avoid grid interconnection
limitations, grid upgrade costs, and/or manage variable electricity rates. Additionally, there are
many other promising, cost-saving technologies for meeting industrial heat demands that can
complement solar thermal to arrive at the least cost solution for the user. Various technologies
are proposed below such that a more holistic approach can be provided to the user as well as
continue to show where solar thermal makes techno-economic sense to users. Lastly, outside of
just the technology solution space, industry is exploring different ownership agreements such as
heat-as-a-serve and offtake agreements; there are many different reasons and motivations for
industrial facility owners and technology suppliers favoring different arrangements. STEP 1
would be improved greatly through this expansion.

1.1: The following technologies would be considered for additional to STEP 1:
 • Air-sourced heat pumps, geothermal heat pumps, and improve resistive heater models that
 capture varying capital costs and performance based on application temperature and
 capacity based on existing physics-based models in the teams and additional industry
 research.
 • Solar fuels, biofuels, hydrogen, and other synthetic fuels using established work, cost and
 performance estimates will be implemented (e.g., changes in boiler costs/performance if
 using biofuels).. Solar fuels are already in-scope as a static cost estimate for comparison,
 in the new version they would be implemented into the solution space more directly. On-
 site generation of hydrogen/fuels would also be considered as opposed to just merchant.
 These could show cost-effectiveness for high-capacity factor and/or resilient systems.
 • Additional TES configurations and customization: TES supplies have various designs and
 configurations such as charging/storage/discharging capacity ratio limits and cost
 relationships that are currently all seen as independent.
 • Wind: The code base to do this is already established, but would require more
 considerations of zoning and land availability.
 • Nuclear: We would have to review literature and network with colleagues at INL to discuss
 the costs, performance, and integration challenges to hybridizing with solar thermal and
 TES solutions.
1.2: Increased fidelity of grid interconnection considerations included limitations and upgrade
costs.

6.1.2 Increased Industrial Process Characterization

STEP 1 aims to meet as many users as possible where they are in their understanding of their
process. If the tool had an increased level of understanding about key facilities this would further
reduce the barrier of entry to more users. For example, a similar tool, REopt, has default load
profiles for various commercial entities (e.g., hospitals). Using the team’s networks, other national
labs (e.g., INL), and others, preliminary defaults (e.g., rated power, temperature, load profiles) for
key industries could be added to STEP 1. Having key industries represented would also more
directly signal to specific industries that STEP 1 is for them, can capture their nuances, and increase
the likelihood of an industrial facility owner using this tool and consider solar thermal.
Additionally, production usually involves several discrete processes at different temperatures and
energy levels. It may make more sense to address a single process or piece of equipment than to
simply offset the energy balance as a whole. This level of process information would be feasible
with more detailed characterization of processes.

2.1: Use industry and lab connections to create default parameters, load profiles, and other
integration challenge costs for specific industrial facility types.
 • Allow for more nuanced consideration of integration costs, challenges, and opportunities
 (see 2.2)
 • The industrial facility types explored would focus on those of pressing concern/interest to
 the CST industry (e.g., AI/data centers, food & beverage sectors, pulp/paper, refinery). For

 example, allow refinery owner to specify barrels/day as plant capacity for us to convert

6.1.3 Proposed Task #3: User Experience Improvements

The user interface provides several key interfaces that allow users who are inexperienced with the
concepts of solar energy production to obtain results easily by knowing key information about
their location, energy consumption, average process temperatures, and capacity. The algorithm can
then call REopt and SAM and run a technoeconomic analysis that takes about 10-20 minutes.
Currently, each session is an independent event with no means to create a user profile and securely
save data, details, or build upon earlier analysis. A key piece of STEP 1’s success will be measured
by how approach and easy-to-use the tool is to users – it is what motivated us to develop a web
application interface that does not require users to know how to code, install anything, etc.
However, a web application limits the ability for local storage of information. Initial discussions
with users have indicated some interest in saving inputs and results, but also express caution over
security of their industrial process information. Additionally, we believe that some users may
expect more rapid, reduced fidelity results before initiating the more robust TEA in the primary
version of the tool. The user experience can hypothetically be improved by redesigning the process
to “teach as you go.” A better process flow would be to provide instant results and gradually lead
users toward more customized accurate information.

3.1: Explore ways of reducing computational time to improve user experience.
 • The underlying optimization and techno-economic analysis is based on NLR’s well-
 established REopt tool which historically has had a commercial optimization solver that
 was really quick. Unfortunately, NLR/REopt and the solver company have not been able
 to renew the license at this time, so STEP 1 currently uses a free, open-source solver which
 increased the solution time significantly. Feasibility, logistics, and cost of moving to
 commercial solver would be explored.
 • Another alternative is increasing the Azure host server computational power. This will
 increase the monthly subscription cost.
3.2: Creation of user accounts. This subtask would enable the ability for users to secure store
their inputs and results using a username and password.
 • Storing results for users would require more direct engagement and time for NLR web
 development and cybersecurity teams but is feasible.
 • The cost of increased storage for our Azure hosting subscription is negligible.

6.2 Additional STEP 1 analysis (Proposed Task #5)

While the analysis work in the original STEP 1 project demonstrated there are instances where
solar thermal is cost-competitive, particularly in CA’s food and beverage sector (which was the
emphasis of the original STEP 1’s Task 3: Rapid Analysis), much more analysis could be done.
The goal of the proposed analysis below is to further investigate where and to what degree solar
thermal can reduce total costs for facility owners by reducing fuel consumption.

5.1: Facility-level analysis
 • Establish 3-5 “typical” industrial facility types, most notably defined by their sector,
 relative size, and process temperature. Examples include: data center, food & beverage,
 nitrogenous fertilizer, aluminum, and others. These should map to different solar thermal
 technologies (i.e., parabolic trough, molten salt, and particle).
 • For each facility, use STEP 1’s back end to explore the sensitivity of various parameters
 on the build out of solar thermal and thermal energy storage. Parameters of interest would
 be:
 o location (based on where those facility types are typically located) – based on ITO’s
 Onsite Energy program dataset or just using the EPA GHGRP
 o available land (or land cost) – ITO’s Onsite Energy program has land availability
 for 100,000+ facilities
 o load profile – Ideally, this could be available using the ITO’s Onsite Energy
 program, but can use some form of a “standard” load profile for each facility if this
 data is inaccessible. The goal here is to not get a specific load profile, but to explore
 the influence of load profile changes on whether solar thermal is built.
 o grid interconnection limitations and upgrade costs – this would be a big unknown
 and therefore a wide range of values would be explored
 o grid pricing profile (current or future) – can use current and projected future prices
 using Cambium
 o natural gas price – based on historical and location variations
 • The goal of the analysis is to generalize the factors that favor solar thermal deployment to
 better identify where it makes to deploy solar thermal and target funding/investment.
 • The end output: A journal paper draft

5.2: Revisit assumptions in Task 2 and Task 3 analysis
 • Load profiles
 o Currently, all load profiles are assumed to be flat, constant 24/7 loads. While this
 is a common load profile for industrial facilities, not all have this load profile.
 Incorporating different load profiles for different facilities could impact how much
 solar thermal and storage is required to meet a percentage of their load. Load
 profiles could be varied based on daily schedule or seasonality. Another option

 88

 would be to examine ITO’s Onsite Energy program database and evaluate if
 thermal load profiles could be extracted and used as an input to future STEP 1-like
 analyses.
 • Solar resource variability
 o Currently, only the TMY data is used for the analyses, which is statistically
 representative of solar resource over multiple years which is how to get average
 performance of an asset over its lifetime. However, there is still inter-year
 variability in solar resource. Future analyses could examine multiple specific years
 to understand what the difference would be in optimal system size and cost under
 better or worse solar resource years.
 • Cost curves
 o Limited cost sensitivity was conducted due to the addition of Task 3. Additional
 work could include a broader sensitivity analysis that analyzes the sensitivity of
 individual technology costs.
 • Other technologies
 o Similar Proposed Task #1, addition analyses could more comprehensively
 examine the solution space of technologies available to industrial facilities to
 better understand where solar thermal could complement or compete with
 alternatives.

7 Conclusion
Over the course of 18 months, the STEP 1 project brought together two national laboratory teams
and built the first version of a completely brand-new web tool that could significantly reduce the
barriers to considering solar thermal with TES systems for industrial process heat applications
and conducted high-impact analyses to support the commercialization of solar industrial process
heat systems to reduce costs for domestic manufacturers.
