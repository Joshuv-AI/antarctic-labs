Abstract
. Spielberg’s construction of C*-algebras from left cancellative small categories is a common generalization for most C*-algebras one would consider to come from “combinatorial data,” including graph and k k -graph C*-algebras, Li’s semigroup C*-algebras, Nekrashevych’s self-similar action algebras, and more. We use known groupoid models of these algebras and Exel’s theory of tight representations of inverse semigroups to prove uniqueness theorems for these C*-algebras. As applications, we improve on our previous uniqueness theorem for the boundary quotient C*-algebras of right LCM monoids, and we also generalize the uniqueness theorem of Brown, Nagy, and Reznikoff for row-finite higher-rank graphs to the finitely aligned case.



1.
Introduction

Broadly speaking, this paper concerns C*-algebra constructed from combinatorial data. Such constructions appear throughout the history of the subject, starting perhaps from the seminal work of Cuntz
[
Cun77
]
who gave the first examples (the Cuntz algebras
\mathcal{O}_{n}
) of simple C*-algebras generated by isometries. Discovery of these C*-algebras led to that of Cuntz-Krieger algebras associated to a
\{0,1\}
-matrix
[
CK80
]
, graph C*-algebras
[
KPR98
]
, Exel-Laca algebras
[
EL99
]
, and
k
-graph C*-algebras
[
KP00
]
.

Another generalization of the Cuntz algebras comes from semigroup theory. In his study of Wiener-Hopf operators, Nica
[
Nic92
]
defined a C*-algebra from certain group-embeddable monoids and showed that when he applies his construction to the free semigroup on two elements, he recovers
\mathcal{O}_{2}
. Li generalized this definition
[
Li12
]
to general left-cancellative monoids.

A common generalization to all these constructions was given by Spielberg
[
Spi20
]
who defined a C*-algebra from a general
left-cancellative small category
(LCSC). Each of the above combinatorial objects are special cases of such: for example, a monoid is a small category with one object, while the set of finite paths through a graph or a
k
-graph is a small category without inverses. More examples of LCSC C*-algebras appear in the literature: Nekrashevych’s self-similar group C*-algebras
[
Nek09
]
, Exel and Pardo’s generalization to self-similar actions on graphs
[
EP17
]
and further generalizations to self-similar actions on
k
-graphs
[
LY19
,
LY21
]
.

In
[
Exe08
]
, Exel had already proposed inverse semigroups as the appropriate framework for combinatorial C*-algebras. These perspectives are of course closely related, as
[
OP20
]

[
Li22
]
show how to obtain an inverse semigroup from an LCSC and use its
tight representations
to characterize Speilberg’s algebra. On the other hand,
[
DGK+20
]
shows that any inverse semigroup (up to Morita equivalence) arises from an LCSC.

This paper is about uniqueness theorems for LCSC C*-algebras. The importance of such theorems for C*-algebras coming from generators and relations cannot be overstated, as injectivity of
*
-homomorphisms from such can be difficult to determine: we could not write a better explanation of this problem than that in the introduction to
[
LS22
]
, so we direct the reader there.

We first recall the original Cuntz-Krieger uniqueness theorem for graph C*-algebras to motivate our results. If
E
is a row-finite directed graph, then its graph C*-algebra
C^{*}(E)
is the universal C*-algebra generated by a set of partial isometries
\{s_{e}\}_{e\in E^{1}}
indexed by the edges of
E
and a set of mutually orthogonal projections
\{p_{e}\}_{e\in E^{0}}
indexed by the vertices of
E
subject to the relations

(CK1)

s_{e}^{*}s_{e}=p_{s(e)}
, and

(CK2)

\sum_{e\in r^{-1}(v)}s_{e}s_{e}^{*}=p_{v}
for all vertices
v\in E^{0}

The original Cuntz-Krieger uniqueness theorem
[
KPR98
]
states that as long as every cycle in
E
has an entry, then any
*
-homomorphism
\varphi:C^{*}(E)\to A
into a C*-algebra
A
is injective if and only if
\varphi(p_{v})\neq 0
for all vertices
v
. Letting
D
be the C*-algebra generated by the set
\{p_{v}\}_{v\in E^{0}}
, we could equivalently say

(1)

A
*
-homomorphism
\varphi:C^{*}(E)\to A
is injective if and only if
\varphi|_{D}
is injective, or

(2)

Every nonzero ideal of
C^{*}(E)
has nonzero intersection with
D
.

Given the second item, we say that
D\subseteq C^{*}(E)
is an
ideal-detecting subalgebra
of
C^{*}(E)
. This was generalized by Reznikoff and Nagy
[
NR12
]
where they defined a subalgebra
\mathcal{M}(E)
that they called the
abelian core
of
E
that they showed detects ideals in
C^{*}(E)
without the assumption that every cycle in
E
has an entry. These ideas were further developed in
[
NR14
]
(defining abelian cores in other contexts), in
[
BNR14
]
(generalized to row-finite
k
-graphs), and finally in
[
BNR+16
]
.

The paper
[
BNR+16
]
is a crucial point in the story, as their uniqueness theorem is subalgebras arising from the underlying étale groupoid. In a way that will be explained below, to an étale groupoid
\mathcal{G}
one can construct a reduced C*-algebra
C^{*}_{r}(\mathcal{G})
. Then
[
BNR+16
, Theorem 3.1]
says that if
\mathcal{G}
is a locally compact Hausdorff étale groupoid, then
C^{*}_{r}(\textup{Iso}(\mathcal{G})^{\circ})
is an ideal-detecting subalgebra of
C^{*}_{r}(\mathcal{G})
(
\textup{Iso}(\mathcal{G})^{\circ}
is the interior of the isotropy subgroupoid of
\mathcal{G}
).

If a C*-algebra
A
is isomorphic to
C^{*}_{r}(\mathcal{G})
for some locally compact étale groupoid
\mathcal{G}
, we say
\mathcal{G}
is a
groupoid model
for
A
. As many of the combinatorial C*-algebras mentioned above are known to have groupoid models, the results of
[
BNR+16
]
can be applied to them. This is what we more-or-less did in
[
Sta22
]
. Specifically we:

(1)

Generalized
[
BNR+16
, Theorem 3.1]
to certain open subgroupoids
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\textup{Iso}(\mathcal{G})
when
\mathcal{G}
is Hausdorff
[
Sta22
, Theorem 2.1]
,

(2)

applied this result to inverse semigroups
S
whose C*-algebras have Hausdorff groupoid models
[
Sta22
, Theorem 3.3]
,

(3)

then applied the inverse semigroup result to C*-algebras of right LCM monoids that have Hausdorff groupoid models
[
Sta22
, Theorem 4.1]

[
BS24
, Theorem 2.1]
.

In the above list, the Hausdorff assumption is conspicuous. One gets the nicest results in the Hausdorff case, but there are many interesting examples of combinatorial C*-algebras whose underlying groupoids are non-Hausdorff. A major complication in proving uniqueness theorems in the non-Hausdorff case is the
singular ideal
. This ideal was defined in
[
CEP+19
]
(see also
[
KM21
]
,
[
EP22
]
) and can be explained as follows:
C^{*}_{r}(\mathcal{G})
is the completion of a certain
*
-algebra of functions
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{G})
; in the Hausdorff case these are continuous but not in general. Roughly speaking, if elements of
C^{*}_{r}(\mathcal{G})
can be viewed as functions, the singular ideal
J_{\text{sing}}(\mathcal{G})
contains the functions that only take nonzero values on meagre sets (see below for the proper definition).

We do not address the problem of determining when the singular ideal vanishes here (though recent work of Hume
[
Hum25
]
provides a potential path for future work on this). Instead, we sidestep this issue entirely and say a subalgebra
B\subseteq C^{*}_{r}(\mathcal{G})
is
essentially ideal-detecting
if any
*
-homomorphism
\pi:C^{*}_{r}(\mathcal{G})\to A
into a C*-algebra
A
that is injective on
B
satisfies
\ker\pi\subseteq J_{\text{sing}}(\mathcal{G})
.

We have two main applications of our results.

(1)

Right LCM monoids:
A monoid
P
is called

if it is left cancellative and, for any

,
pP\cap qP

P_{0}=\{p\in P:pP\cap qP\neq\emptyset\text{ for all }q\in P\}

(2)

k

A
k
-graph is a LCSC
\Lambda

such that
d(\alpha\beta)=d(\alpha)+d(\beta)
and has unique factorization in the sense that if
d(\alpha)=m+n

2.
Groupoids and their C*-algebras

We recall the definitions around étale groupoids and the construction of its C*-algebras. For solid references on étale groupoids and their C*-algebra, the reader is directed to
[
Ren80
]
and
[
Sim20
]
.

A
groupoid
is a set
\mathcal{G}
with a partially-defined multiplication which is associative where defined and for which each element has an inverse. Specifically, there is an inverse map
\gamma\mapsto\gamma^{-1}
from
\mathcal{G}
to
\mathcal{G}
and a set
\mathcal{G}^{(2)}\subseteq\mathcal{G}\times\mathcal{G}
called the set of
composable pairs
and a product map
(\alpha,\beta)\mapsto\alpha\beta
from
\mathcal{G}^{(2)}
to
\mathcal{G}
such that

(1)

(\alpha,\beta),(\beta,\gamma)\in\mathcal{G}^{(2)}
implies
(\alpha\beta)\gamma=\alpha(\beta\gamma)
,

(2)

(\gamma,\gamma^{-1}),(\gamma^{-1},\gamma)\in\mathcal{G}^{(2)}
for all
\gamma
and
\gamma^{-1}\gamma\alpha=\alpha
,
\beta\gamma\gamma^{-1}=\beta
whenever
(\gamma,\alpha),(\beta,\gamma)\in\mathcal{G}^{(2)}
, and

(3)

(\gamma^{-1})^{-1}=\gamma
for all
\gamma\in\mathcal{G}
.

The set
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}:=\{\gamma\gamma^{-1}:\gamma\in\mathcal{G}\}
is called the
unit space
of
\mathcal{G}
. The maps
r,d:\mathcal{G}\to\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
given by
r(\gamma)=\gamma\gamma^{-1}
and
d(\gamma)=\gamma^{-1}\gamma
are called the
range
and
source
maps respectively. A pair
(\alpha,\beta)
is in
\mathcal{G}^{(2)}
if and only if
r(\beta)=d(\alpha)
. A subset
U\subseteq\mathcal{G}
is called a
bisection
if
r|_{U}
and
d|_{U}
are injective.

A groupoid is called
étale
if it is a topological groupoid (meaning it has a topology in which the inverse and product maps are continuous) such that
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
is Hausdorff in the relative topology and the range map is a local homeomorphism. An étale groupoid admits a basis of open bisections. If
U,V
are (open) bisections, then so are
UV=\{\alpha\beta:\alpha\in U,\beta\in V,(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathcal{G}^{(2)}\}
and
U^{-1}:=\{\mathcal{G}^{-1}:\mathcal{G}\in U\}
. If
\mathcal{G}
is étale and admits a basis of
compact
open bisections, it is called
ample
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a locally compact étale groupoid. Any bisection
U\subseteq\mathcal{G}
is homeomorphic to an open subset of
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
, and hence is locally compact Hausdorff. For
f\in C_{c}(U)
,
f
is viewed as a function on
\mathcal{G}
by extending it to be 0 outside of
U
; note that when
\mathcal{G}
is non-Hausdorff this function might not be continuous. Let

\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{G})=\text{span}\{f\in C_{c}(U):U\subseteq\mathcal{G}\text{ is an open bisection}\}.

The set of functions
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{G})
was defined by Connes
[
Con82
]

[
Con94
]
and is a
*
-algebra in a natural way: addition and scalar multiplication are defined pointwise, while if
U
and
V
are open bisections and
f\in C_{c}(U)
,
g\in C_{c}(V)
, then we let
fg
be zero outside of the open bisection
UV
, and for
\gamma\in UV
we let

fg(\gamma)=f(\alpha)g(\beta)

where
(\alpha,\beta)
is the unique element of
U\times V
such that
\alpha\beta=\gamma
(uniqueness follows from the fact that
U
and
V
are bisections). This product is extended to
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{G})
linearly. For
f\in C_{c}(U)
define
f^{*}\in C_{c}(U^{-1})
by

f^{*}(\gamma)=\overline{f(\gamma^{-1})}.

We then extend this to
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{G})
conjugate linearly. These operations make
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{G})
a
*
-algebra.

For each
x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
, let
\rho_{x}:\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{G})\to\mathcal{B}(\ell^{2}(\mathcal{G}_{x}))
denote the
*
-representation of
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{G})
given by

\rho_{x}(f)\delta_{\gamma}=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{G}_{r(\gamma)}}f(\alpha)\delta_{\alpha\gamma}.

Then the
reduced C*-algebra of

\mathcal{G}
, denoted
C_{r}^{*}(\mathcal{G})
, is the completion of the image of
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{G})
under the representation
\bigoplus_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}\rho_{x}
. There is another C*-algebra associated to
\mathcal{G}
, called the
full C*-algebra of
\mathcal{G}
, denoted
C^{*}(\mathcal{G})
, which is universal for representations of
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{G})
. One defines a norm on
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{G})
by
\|f\|:=\sup\{\|\pi(f)\|:\pi\text{ is a representation of }\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{G})\}
, then
C^{*}(\mathcal{G})
is the completion of
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{G})
in this norm. It is universal in the sense that if
\pi:\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{G})\to A
is a
*
-homomorphism of
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{G})
into a C*-algebra
A
, there is a
*
-homomorphism
\varphi:C^{*}(\mathcal{G})\to A
that agrees with
\pi
on
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{G})
.

Renault’s
j
map
is the injective linear contraction
j:C^{*}_{r}(\mathcal{G})\to\ell^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})
given by

j(a)(\gamma)=\langle\rho_{d(\gamma)}(a)\delta_{d(\gamma)},\delta_{\gamma}\rangle\hskip 28.45274pt\gamma\in\mathcal{G},a\in C^{*}_{r}(\mathcal{G})

The following description of the singular ideal is given in
[
KKL+21
]
. For a locally compact Hausdorff space
X
, recall the following notation:

•

\mathcal{B}^{\infty}(X)
= the C*-algebra of bounded Borel functions (sup norm),

•

\mathcal{M}^{\infty}(X)
= the ideal of
\mathcal{B}^{\infty}(X)
of functions with meagre support,

•

Dix
(X)=\mathcal{B}^{\infty}(X)/\mathcal{M}^{\infty}(X)
, the Dixmier algebra of
X
.

The linear map
E_{\text{red}}:\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{G})\to\text{Dix}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
determined by
E_{\text{red}}(f)=\left.f\right|_{U}
, where
U
is any open dense subset of
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
on which
f
is continuous, defines a local conditional expectation on
C^{*}_{r}(\mathcal{G})
. The ideal of singular elements can then be defined by

J_{\text{sing}}(\mathcal{G}):=\{a\in C^{*}_{r}(\mathcal{G}):E_{\text{red}}(a^{*}a)=0\}

and the
essential C*-algebra of
\mathcal{G}
is

C^{*}_{\text{ess}}(\mathcal{G}):=C^{*}_{r}(\mathcal{G})/J_{\text{sing}}(\mathcal{G}).

By
[
KM21
, Proposition 7.18]
, when
\mathcal{G}
is covered by countably many bisections (as is the case for all the groupoids in this paper)
a\in J_{\text{sing}}(\mathcal{G})
if and only if
d(\text{supp}(j(a)))
is a meagre subset of
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
.

We now define the concept we will be investigating in this paper.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a locally compact étale groupoid. We say that a subalgebra
B\subseteq C^{*}_{r}(\mathcal{G})
is
essentially ideal-detecting
if any
*
-homomorphism
\pi:C^{*}_{r}(\mathcal{G})\to A
into a C*-algebra
A
that is injective on
B
satisfies
\ker\pi\subseteq J_{\text{sing}}(\mathcal{G})
. Equivalently, if an ideal
I
of
C^{*}_{r}(\mathcal{G})
has zero intersection with
B
, then
I\subseteq J_{\text{sing}}(\mathcal{G})
.

In what follows we will be applying the following result (which is a generalization of
[
CN24
, Theorem 4.7]
) to specific classes of groupoids.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a locally compact étale groupoid and suppose that
\mathcal{G}
is either Hausdorff or can be covered by countably many open bisections. Suppose
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\mathcal{G}
is an open subgroupoid of
\mathcal{G}
with the property that the set
Y=\{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}:\mathcal{G}^{x}_{x}=\mathcal{H}_{x}^{x}\}
is dense. Suppose that
I\subseteq C_{r}^{*}(\mathcal{G})
is an ideal and that
C_{r}^{*}(\mathcal{H})\cap I\subseteq J_{\text{sing}}(\mathcal{G})
. Then
I\subseteq J_{\text{sing}}(\mathcal{G})
.

Note: Proposition
2.2
has the same assumptions as
[
CN24
, Theorem 4.7]
, and there Christensen and Neshveyev conclude that
C^{*}_{r}(\mathcal{H})
is essentially ideal-detecting. So this is a slight improvement, as we relax the assumption that
C_{r}^{*}(\mathcal{H})\cap I=\{0\}
to
C_{r}^{*}(\mathcal{H})\cap I\subseteq J_{\text{sing}}(\mathcal{G})
. This generalization will be needed to prove part of Theorem
4.10
.

Proof.

By
[
CN24
, Section 4]
there exists an open dense subset
X\subseteq\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
such that

(2.1)

J_{\text{sing}}(\mathcal{G})=\bigcap_{x\in X}\ker(\rho_{x})

where
\rho
denotes the left regular representation of
C_{r}^{*}(\mathcal{G})
at the unit
x
. Furthermore, this equality remains true if
X
is replaced with any subset of
X
that is also dense in
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
. Because
Y=\{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}:\mathcal{G}^{x}_{x}=\mathcal{H}_{x}^{x}\}
is dense in
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
and
X
is open and dense, we have that
X\cap Y
is dense in
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
, and so

(2.2)

J_{\text{sing}}(\mathcal{G})=\bigcap_{x\in X\cap Y}\ker(\rho_{x}).

Suppose
a\in J:=C_{r}^{*}(\mathcal{H})\cap I
and
x\in X\cap Y
. Then
J\subseteq J_{\text{sing}}(\mathcal{G})
and (
2.2
) imply that
j(a)(\gamma)=0
for all
\gamma\in d^{-1}(x)\cup r^{-1}(x)
; in particular
j(a)(\gamma)=0
for all
\gamma\in\mathcal{G}_{x}^{x}=\mathcal{H}^{x}_{x}
. Hence
\left.j(a)\right|_{\mathcal{H}^{x}_{x}}=0
. Thus (using the notation of
[
CN24
, Proposition 4.6]
) we have that
J_{x}=0
. By that same proposition we then have that
I_{x}\cap C^{*}_{r}(\mathcal{H}_{x}^{x})=0
. But since
C^{*}_{r}(\mathcal{H}_{x}^{x})=C^{*}_{r}(\mathcal{G}_{x}^{x})
and
I_{x}\subseteq C^{*}_{r}(\mathcal{G}_{x}^{x})
we get that
I_{x}=0
for all
x\in X\cap Y
. Since
X\cap Y\subseteq\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
is dense,
[
CN24
, Lemma 4.1]
implies that
I\subseteq J_{\text{sing}}(\mathcal{G})
.
∎

3.
The tight C*-algebra of an inverse semigroup

3.1.
Inverse semigroups

A semigroup
S
is an
inverse semigroup
if for all
s\in S
there exists unique
s^{*}\in S
such that
ss^{*}s=s
and
s^{*}ss^{*}=s^{*}
. A
zero
in an
S
is an element
0\in S
such that
0s=s0=0
for all
s\in S
. If
S
has a zero it is necessarily unique. An element
e\in S
is called an
idempotent
if
e^{2}=e
. It is well-known that idempotents in an inverse semigroup commute.

Let
S
be an inverse semigroup with idempotent set
E\subseteq S
containing a zero element 0. Then
S
carries a canonical order
s\leqslant t\iff ts^{*}s=s
. On
E
this order becomes
e\leqslant f\iff ef=e
. With this order,
E
is a semilattice. The order is preserved by multiplication, i.e.

s\leqslant t\implies sr\leqslant tr\text{ and }rs\leqslant rt

for
r,s,t\in S
.

Two elements
s,t\in S
are called
compatible
if
s^{*}t
and
st^{*}
are idempotents—note that idempotents are always compatible. For any subset
A\subseteq S
, we write
\bigvee_{a\in A}a
for the least upper bound of
A
in
S
in the above canonical order, if it exists. By
[
Law98
, Lemma 1.1.6]
,
\bigvee_{a\in A}a\in S
implies that elements of
A
are pairwise compatible. We say
S
is
(resp. finitely) complete
if whenever
A
is a (resp. finite) subset of
S
whose elements are pairwise compatible, then
\bigvee_{a\in A}a\in S
. In addition, we say that
S
is
(resp. finitely) distributive
if for any
s\in S
and and (resp. finite) subset
A\subseteq S
, then

\bigvee_{a\in A}a\in S\implies s\left(\bigvee_{a\in A}a\right)=\bigvee_{a\in A}sa\in S

We record some facts about the order in an inverse semigroup for use later.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
S
be a (resp. finite) distributive inverse semigroup, let
A\subseteq S
be a (resp. finite) subset of
S
and suppose
s=\bigvee_{a\in A}a\in S
.

(1)

If
e\leqslant a^{*}a
for some
a\in S
, then
ses^{*}=aea^{*}
.

(2)

If
t\in S
and
tt^{*}\leqslant aa^{*}
for some
a\in A
, then
t^{*}st=t^{*}at
.

Proof.

(1) For any
b,c\in A
we calculate

bec^{*}=ba^{*}aea^{*}ac^{*}\leqslant aea^{*}

as
ba^{*},ac^{*}
are idempotents. Hence
ses^{*}=\bigvee_{b,c\in A}bec^{*}\leqslant aea^{*}
(while
\geqslant
is automatic).

(2) For
b\in A
, we have
aa^{*}b\leqslant a

For any set

, let

. Two functions

are compatible if and only if

By

For the rest of this paper, every inverse semigroup is assumed to have a zero element

A

Definition 3.2

Let

4.
Left cancellative small categories and associated inverse semigroups

A
small category
is a quadruple
(\Lambda,\Lambda^{0},r,s)
where
\Lambda^{0}\subseteq\Lambda
are sets and
r,s:\Lambda\to\Lambda^{0}
are maps called the
range
and
source
maps respectively (onto the set
\Lambda^{0}
of
objects
) satisfying the following:

(1)

r(x)=s(x)=x
for all
x\in\Lambda^{0}
,

(2)

there is a composition map from
\Lambda^{2}=\{(\alpha,\beta)\in\Lambda\times\Lambda:s(\alpha)=r(\beta)\}
to
\Lambda
with
(\alpha,\beta)\to\alpha\beta
such that
r(\alpha\beta)=r(\alpha)
and
s(\alpha\beta)=s(\beta)
,

(3)

r(\alpha)\alpha=\alpha=\alpha s(\alpha)
for all
\alpha\in\Lambda
, and

(4)

\alpha(\beta\gamma)=(\alpha\beta)\gamma
whenever the compositions on both sides are defined.

We say
\alpha\in\Lambda
is
invertible
if there exists
\beta\in\Lambda
such that
\alpha\beta=r(\alpha)
. The set of all invertible elements of
\Lambda
is denoted
\Lambda^{-1}
.

We say
\Lambda
is
left (resp. right) cancellative
if
\alpha\beta=\alpha\gamma\implies\beta=\gamma
(resp.
\alpha\beta=\gamma\beta\implies\alpha=\gamma
) for all
\alpha,\beta,\gamma\in\Lambda
, and say
\Lambda
is cancellative if it is both left and right cancellative.

If
\Lambda
is left cancellative, then if
\alpha\in\Lambda^{-1}
, the element
\beta
such that
\alpha\beta=r(\alpha)
is unique, is denoted
\alpha^{-1}
, and also satisfies
\alpha^{-1}\alpha=s(\alpha)
.

In what follows, we will be primarily dealing with left cancellative small categories, and for these we will use the abbreviation LCSC.

For
\alpha\in\Lambda
let

\alpha\Lambda=\{\alpha\beta:(\alpha,\beta)\in\Lambda^{2}\},\hskip 28.45274pt\Lambda\alpha=\{\beta\alpha:(\beta,\alpha)\in\Lambda^{2}\}.

The relation
\alpha\leq\beta\iff\alpha\in\beta\Lambda
is a preorder on
\Lambda
and so we can pass to equivalence classes for the relation
\alpha\approx\beta\iff\alpha\leq\beta
and
\beta\leq\alpha
and obtain a partial order. Then (see
[
OP23
]
) we have

\alpha\approx\beta\iff\alpha\Lambda=\beta\Lambda\iff\alpha\in\beta\Lambda^{-1}\iff\beta\in\alpha\Lambda^{-1}.

If
\Lambda
is a LCSC, we say it is
finitely aligned
if for all
\alpha,\beta\in\Lambda
there exists a finite (possibly empty) set
F\subseteq\Lambda
such that

(4.1)

\alpha\Lambda\cap\beta\Lambda=\bigcup_{f\in F}f\Lambda

and we say it is
singly aligned
or
right LCM
if the finite set in (
4.1
) can always be taken to have at most one element.

Now for a LCSC
\Lambda
and
\alpha\in\Lambda
, we also denote by
\alpha
the partial bijection
\alpha:s(\alpha)\Lambda\to\alpha\Lambda
that sends
\beta
to
\alpha\beta
. Then let

S_{\Lambda}=\text{ inverse semigroup generated by }\{\alpha\}_{\alpha\in\Lambda}\text{ inside }\mathcal{I}(\Lambda)

Note that its inverse in
\mathcal{I}(\Lambda)
is given by
\alpha^{*}:\alpha\Lambda\to s(\alpha)\Lambda
is given by
\alpha^{*}(\alpha\beta)=\beta
. We call
S_{\Lambda}
the

\Lambda
. This has also been called the collection of

(see
[
Spi20
]
and
[

By [Lemma 2.6 EP20] (and [Lemma 3.3 Sp14] in the inverse-free case), if

(4.2)

Remark 4.1
.

A couple words of caution are necessary concerning elements of

(1)

Even if

(2)

Furthermore, even if

For the reasons above, we will write a general element of

\{W_{\alpha}:\alpha\in\Lambda\}
subject to the relations

(S1)

W_{\alpha}^{*}W_{\alpha}=W_{s(\alpha)}
.

(S2)

W_{\alpha}W_{\beta}=W_{\alpha\beta}
if
s(\alpha)=r(\beta)
.

(S3)

W_{\alpha}W_{\alpha}^{*}W_{\beta}W_{\beta}^{*}=\bigvee_{i=1}^{n}W_{\gamma_{i}}W_{\gamma_{i}}^{*}
if
\alpha\Lambda\cap\beta\Lambda=\cup_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{i}\Lambda
.

(S4)

W_{x}=\bigvee_{\alpha\in F}W_{\alpha}W_{\alpha}^{*}
if
x\in\Lambda^{0}
and
F
is exhaustive at
x
.

By
[
Li23
]
,
\mathcal{O}(\Lambda)
is the universal C*-algebra for the tight groupoid of
S_{\Lambda}
. Upon making some assumptions on this groupoid we can formulate a uniqueness theorem akin to the original Cuntz-Krieger uniqueness theorem (where injectivity is equivalent to injectivity on a subalgebra generated by some subset of the generators).

Corollary 4.17
.

Let
\Lambda
be a singly aligned left cancellative small category and let
\Lambda_{c}
be the core of
\Lambda
. Suppose the tight groupoid of
S_{\Lambda}
is amenable and has zero singular ideal. Then if
\varphi:\mathcal{O}(\Lambda)\to B
is a
*
-homomorphism into a C*-algebra
B
,
\varphi
is injective if and only if it is injective on the subalgebra generated by
\{W_{\alpha}:\alpha\in\Lambda_{c}\}
.

We close this section by applying our results to right LCM monoids. The C*-algebras of such monoids have been studied by many authors, see
[
ABLS19
,
BOS18
,

,
BS16
,
LL20
,
LL21
,
Li19
,
NS19
,
Sta15a
,
Sta17
,
Sta15b
]
. In the language of this paper so far, a right LCM monoid is a singly aligned LCSC with only one object. With Theorem
4.15
in hand, we can now prove the following generalization of
[
Sta22
, Theorem 4.1]
(later, correctly proven in
[
BS24
]
) to the possibly non-Hausdorff case.

Corollary 4.18
.

(Cf.
[
Sta22
, Theorem 4.1]
,
[
BS24
, Theorem 2.1]
)
Let
P
be a right LCM monoid, let
\mathcal{Q}_{r}(P)=C^{*}_{r}(\mathcal{G}_{\text{t}}(S_{P}))
be its reduced boundary quotient, and let
\mathcal{Q}_{r,c}(P)
be the subalgebra generated by the core submonoid. Then
\mathcal{Q}_{r,c}(P)
is essentially ideal-detecting.

5.
k
-graphs

A
k
-graph
is a pair
(\Lambda,d)
where
\Lambda
is a small category and
d:\Lambda\to\mathbb{N}^{k}
(the
degree map
) satisfies

•

d(\alpha\beta)=d(\alpha)+d(\beta)
for all
\alpha,\beta\in\Lambda
with
r(\beta)=s(\alpha)
, and

•

if
d(\alpha)=m+n
for some
m,n\in\mathbb{N}^{k}
, there exist unique
\beta,\gamma\in\Lambda
with
\alpha=\beta\gamma
,
d(\beta)=m
, and
d(\gamma)=n
(this is called the
unique factorization property
).

One can see that the unique factorization property implies that
\Lambda
is left and right cancellative, and is also has no invertible elements.

In
[
BNR14
]
, they define what it means for
(\alpha,\beta)\in\Lambda\times\Lambda
to be a
cycline pair
. In our notation, this is equivalent to saying
s(\alpha)=s(\beta)
and
D_{\beta\gamma\Lambda}=D_{\alpha\gamma\Lambda}
for all
\gamma\in s(\alpha)\Lambda
, which one can easily see is equivalent to saying
\alpha\beta^{*}\in S^{\textup{Iso}}_{\Lambda}
.

If one assumes that
\Lambda
is finitely aligned, then this combined with right cancellation implies that
\mathcal{G}_{\text{t}}(S_{\Lambda})
is Hausdorff
[
Spi20
, Lemma 7.1]
. The map
\alpha\beta^{*}\mapsto d(\alpha)-d(\beta)
induces a cocycle
\phi:\mathcal{G}_{\text{t}}(S_{\Lambda})\to\mathbb{Z}^{k}
such that
\phi^{-1}(0)
is an AF-groupoid (see
[
RSY04
, Section 3]
), and so
\mathcal{G}_{\text{t}}(S_{\Lambda})
is amenable by
[
RW17
, Corollary 4.5]
. Thus we can apply our results to obtain the following generalization of the main theorem in
[
BNR14
, Theorem 7.1]
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
\Lambda
be a finitely aligned
k
-graph, let
\mathcal{O}(\Lambda)
be the Cuntz-Krieger algebra of
\Lambda
, and suppose
\varphi:\mathcal{O}(\Lambda)\to B
is a
*
-homomorphism into a C*-algebra
B
. Then
\varphi
is injective if and only if it is injective on the subalgebra
C^{*}(\{W_{\alpha}W_{\beta}^{*}:(\alpha,\beta)\text{ is cycline}\})
.

Proof.

By the discussion before the theorem,
\mathcal{O}(\Lambda)\cong C_{r}^{*}(\mathcal{G}_{\text{t}}(S_{\Lambda}))
with the isomorphism sending
W_{\alpha}
to
T_{\alpha}
. To apply Theorem
4.10
, we will show that the given subalgebra coincides with
\mathcal{Q}_{r}^{\textup{Iso}}(\Lambda)
. Let
s\in S_{\Lambda}^{\textup{Iso}}
, and write
s=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\beta_{i}^{*}
for
\alpha_{i},\beta_{i}\in\Lambda
. By work in
[
DM14
, Section 3]
following
[
Spi14
, Theorem 6.3]
, we have that
T_{s}=\bigvee_{i=1}^{n}T_{\alpha_{i}}T_{\beta_{i}^{*}}
, where this wedge in a C*-algebra is as in (
3.3
). By that formula and Lemma
4.3
we see
T_{s}
is in the C*-algebra generated by the set
\{T_{\alpha}T_{\beta}^{*}:\alpha\beta^{*}\in S_{\Lambda}^{\textup{Iso}}\}
.
∎
