Construction of a sensitive indicator system for the quality of neonatal jaundice care
Authors: Yong-Xiang Xie, Ya-Xin Jin, Hui Li, Wen-Fang Xian, Cheng-Xia Zeng, Wen He, He Wang, Shan Deng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNeonatal jaundice is a prevalent neonatal condition, with improper management potentially leading to severe neurological damage. Significant disparities in care quality exist globally, highlighting the urgent need for a standardized, disease-specific nursing quality evaluation system.Aims and objectivesThis study aimed to construct a scientifically rigorous and reliable sensitive indicator system for evaluating the quality of neonatal jaundice care, based on Donabedian's structure-process-outcome model.MethodsA methodological study design was employed. An initial draft was developed through a systematic literature review and semi-structured interviews. Subsequently, 18 experts participated in two rounds of Delphi consultations to reach a consensus on the indicators.ResultsThe effective response rates for the two consultation rounds were 90.0% and 100.0%, respectively. The expert authority coefficients were 0.851 and 0.879. The final system comprises 3 first-level, 10 second-level, and 24 third-level indicators.ConclusionThe successfully constructed indicator system demonstrates strong scientific validity and reliability. It provides a consensus-based, standardized framework for assessing neonatal jaundice care quality, requiring further empirical testing before large-scale implementation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-026-07141-6.

Patient or public contribution

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, analysis, or interpretation of this study. The research methodology was based on a systematic literature review, semi-structured interviews with clinical and managerial experts, and the Delphi technique involving a panel of nursing specialists. Consequently, the involvement of patients or the public was not applicable to this phase of indicator system development, which focused on obtaining professional consensus from healthcare providers.

Statement of significance

Problem or issue

Significant global disparities exist in neonatal jaundice care quality, with improper management risking severe neurological damage. A standardized, disease-specific tool for evaluating and improving nursing care is urgently needed.

What is already known

Nursing-sensitive indicators based on Donabedian’s structure-process-outcome model are established for care quality evaluation. However, existing tools lack specificity for neonatal jaundice, failing to comprehensively guide quality improvement in this area.

What this paper adds

This study constructs a scientifically rigorous, reliable sensitive indicator system tailored for neonatal jaundice care. The finalized system provides a standardized tool for objective assessment, monitoring, and continuous improvement of care quality in clinical practice.

Introduction

Neonatal jaundice is the most prevalent clinical issue during the neonatal period, affecting approximately 60% of term infants and 80% of preterm infants globally [1]. Although it is primarily a physiological phenomenon, persistently elevated levels of unconjugated bilirubin can lead to bilirubin encephalopathy, a condition that may cause irreversible neurological damage or even death [2]. Notably, there are significant geographic disparities in this risk; neonatal jaundice-related mortality rates are markedly higher in low- and middle-income countries compared to high-income countries, attributable to insufficient healthcare resources and inadequate surveillance systems [3–5]. This disparity underscores the critical importance of high-quality care in managing this condition.

Bilirubin encephalopathy is largely preventable, with its incidence closely linked to the quality of clinical care. However, standardizing nursing practices and ensuring adherence to them remain challenging.Studies from Sweden indicate that the occurrence of severe hyperbilirubinemia is primarily associated with insufficient compliance with best practices among healthcare professionals [6]. There is an urgent need for systematic monitoring and enhancement of care quality. Nursing-sensitive quality indicators serve as effective tools for this purpose.Grounded in Donabedian’s “structure-process-outcome” model [7, 8], these indicators systematically capture the entirety of nursing quality by quantitatively assessing the underlying conditions, implementation processes, and patient outcomes of nursing services [9].This model facilitates accurate measurement and real-time feedback, thereby fostering the continuous improvement of nursing care quality.

Although general quality-sensitive indicators for pediatric care exist, there is a lack of standardized and precise evaluation criteria for specific diseases or subspecialties, such as neonatal jaundice.Current assessment tools often fail to comprehensively identify nursing deficiencies, thereby impeding quality improvement efforts.Consequently, developing a systematic and reliable set of sensitive indicators for neonatal jaundice management is crucial for advancing equity in neonatal healthcare worldwide [10].

This study aimed to establish a set of sensitive quality indicators for neonatal jaundice care based on the Donabedian theoretical framework.These indicators encompass three dimensions: structure, process, and outcome. The goal of this study was to provide a scientific basis for the objective assessment of care quality and safety for neonates with jaundice.

Methods

Formation of a research team

The research team comprised pediatric nurse specialists, pediatric nurse managers, directors of nursing, and other relevant researchers.Their primary responsibilities included identifying the research topic, designing the indicator system, selecting expert consultants, summarizing and analyzing data, and conducting quality control throughout the research process.

Literature search strategy

The specific search strategy combined subject terms and free words. For PubMed, the search string was: ((“Infant, Newborn“[Mesh]) AND (“Jaundice, Neonatal“[Mesh] OR “Hyperbilirubinemia, Neonatal“[Mesh])) AND (“Quality of Health Care“[Mesh] OR “Nursing Care“[Mesh] OR “Sensitivity and Specificity“[Mesh] OR “Quality Indicators, Health Care“[Mesh]) AND (“Donabedian Model” OR “Structure-Process-Outcome”). Similar strategies were adapted for Chinese databases: CNKI, CBM, Wanfang, and VIP, using equivalent Chinese subject headings and keywords. The search was restricted to studies published in English or Chinese, with no lower time limit, up to May 28, 2024.

Inclusion criteria for the literature: ① The research topic includes sensitive evaluation index systems related to the nursing quality of neonatal jaundice; ② The literature types include original research, clinical practice guidelines, evidence summaries, and systematic reviews, etc.; ③ The language used in the literature is Chinese or English.Exclusion criteria: ① Direct translations or duplicate publications; ② Literature types such as plans, outlines, reports, or abstracts; ③ Literature with missing information or unable to obtain the full text.

Initial search yielded 536 records; 101 duplicates were removed. After screening titles and abstracts, 367 irrelevant records were excluded. Full-text assessment excluded 55 articles, leaving 13 studies included for initial indicator drafting [3, 11–22].

Formation of a first draft of sensitive indicators of quality of care for neonatal jaundice

Purposive sampling was used to select experts from the pediatrics departments of three tertiary hospitals in Hubei Province for semi-structured interviews.The sample size was determined by data saturation, resulting in the inclusion of six experts. All participants provided informed consent.Each interview lasted 25 to 40 min.The inclusion criteria for experts were: (a) a minimum of five years of experience in pediatric nursing management or clinical practice, and (b) a bachelor’s degree or higher. The interview questions included: ① Do you believe that traditional nursing quality indicators accurately reflect the quality of care for neonatal jaundice? Please explain your reasoning. ② What structural factors do you identify as influencing the quality of care for neonatal jaundice? ③ Based on your clinical and/or management experience, which aspects do you think should be emphasized in the process factors of the sensitive indicator system for neonatal jaundice care quality? ④ What outcome factors do you consider essential for evaluating the quality of neonatal jaundice care?

Audio recordings were transcribed within 24 h after the interviews. The textual data were analyzed using Colaizzi’s phenomenological analysis method [23]. After organizing, screening, and discussing the literature alongside the interview findings, the research team developed an initial draft of a sensitive indicator system for assessing the quality of neonatal jaundice care, grounded in the three-dimensional quality structure theory.

Delphi method

Develop an expert consultation questionnaire

The expert consultation questionnaire was formulated following deliberations within the research team. It comprises three sections: ① a preamble elucidating the study’s context, objectives, completion guidelines, return timeline, and confidentiality assurance;② detailed information on sensitive quality-of-care indicators for neonatal jaundice; and ③ essential details about the experts, encompassing their title, age, education, years of experience, familiarity with the study’s content, rationale for their assessments, and level of influence.

Identification of experts

Experts for the consultation were selected based on the principles of the Delphi method and the research content. Inclusion criteria comprised Nursing management experts from tertiary grade A hospitals and nursing departments of universities nationwide with a Bachelor’s degree or higher, an Intermediate or higher professional title, and a minimum of 10 years of nursing management experience, capable of offering valuable opinions, and committed to participating continuously until the study conclusion. Additionally, Clinical nursing experts included Pediatric specialist nurses from tertiary grade A hospitals nationwide with a Bachelor’s degree or higher, an Intermediate or higher professional title, and a minimum of 10 years of experience in neonatal jaundice care, capable of providing valuable insights and committed to continuous participation. Exclusion criteria encompassed discontinuation of participation and incorrect questionnaire completion.Typically, involving 15 to 50 experts yields satisfactory consultation outcomes [24]. In this study, 20 experts were invited, and 18 of them completed both consultation rounds. The general characteristics of the experts are presented in Table 1.

Implementation of expert advice

The research team conducted two rounds of expert consultation between April and June 2025, with each round lasting three weeks.Questionnaires were distributed either on-site or via email.The screening criteria for indicators were a mean importance score > 3.50 and a coefficient of variation < 0.25 [25]. Indicators were rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from “strongly disagree” (1) to “strongly agree” (5). Experts had the opportunity to suggest modifications, additions, or deletions in a designated column. Following two rounds of communication, experts’ opinions aligned, leading to the conclusion of the consultation.

Data analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS 25.0 software. Measurement data that conformed to a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (x ± s), while count data were represented as frequency and percentage (%). The effective recovery rate of the questionnaire served as an indicator of expert motivation. The coefficient of authority of the experts (Cr) was calculated using the formula “Cr=(coefficient of familiarity Cs + basis of judgment Ca)/2.” Additionally, the degree of coordination among the experts’ opinions was assessed through the coefficient of coordination (W).

Pairwise comparison matrices for the Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP) were constructed based on the mean importance scores of indicators from the second-round Delphi consultation. A 1–9 scaling system was adopted to quantify the relative importance of indicators hierarchically. Consistency was considered acceptable when the calculated Consistency Ratio (CR) was < 0.1. The weights were computed using yaahp 10.0 software, and the specific allocation of weights is detailed in Table 3.

Results

Degree of expert motivation and authority

In the first round, 20 questionnaires were distributed, and 18 were returned, resulting in a response rate of 90.0%. In the second round, all 18 distributed questionnaires were returned, yielding a response rate of 100.0%.The overall response rate across both rounds exceeded 70% [26], indicating a high level of engagement among the participating experts.In the first round, the judgment coefficient (Ca) was 0.889, the familiarity coefficient (Cs) was 0.813, and the authority coefficient (Cr) was 0.851. In the second round, Ca increased to 0.896, Cs rose to 0.862, and Cr reached 0.879.

Degree of harmonization of expert advice

Kendall’s coefficient of coordination (W) varies from 0 to 1, with larger values indicating greater synergy among expert opinions. In the first round of expert evaluations, the coordination coefficient ranged from 0.285 to 0.376. In the second round, the coordination coefficient increased, ranging from 0.332 to 0.419. Expert consultation results

Following the initial round of expert consultation and subsequent discussions within the research group, several revisions were made to the indicators. First, four indicators were modified: the “accuracy rate of family members’ skill operation” was changed to “mastery rate of family members’ daily care skills for newborns”; the “completeness rate of phototherapy equipment configuration” was revised to “integrity rate/standby rate of phototherapy equipment”; the “standardized rate of dynamic risk assessment” was updated to “dynamic assessment rate of neonatal jaundice risk”; and the “incidence of phototherapy skin injury in children patients” was altered to “incidence of skin injury in newborns.” Second, one new indicator was added: “incidence of nursing-related administration errors.” Lastly, five indicators were deleted, specifically “multidisciplinary collaboration,” “implementation rate of consultation for difficult cases,” “average length of hospital stay,” “compliance rate of bilirubin monitoring frequency,” and “implementation rate of skin protection.”

At the conclusion of the second round of expert consultation, all indicators satisfied the screening criteria, and one indicator was modified in accordance with the experts’ recommendations. The “implementation rate of feeding interventions” was revised to “implementation rate of early skin-to-skin contact/early sucking between mother and child.”

Ultimately, the finalized system comprised 3 first-level indicators, 10 s-level indicators, and 24 third-level indicators. The weights of all indicators were calculated using the Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP) based on the mean importance scores from the second round. The consistency ratios (CR) of all judgment matrices were < 0.1, indicating satisfactory logical consistency. Detailed weight allocations are presented in Table 3.

To address the requirement for transparency in quantitative findings: The combined weights of the 24 third-level indicators ranged from 0.017 to 0.085. The process indicator “timeliness of phototherapy interventions in high-risk children"carried the highest weight (0.085), reflecting expert consensus on the critical role of prompt treatment in preventing severe adverse outcomes. Conversely, “incidence of nursing-related administration errors"held the lowest weight (0.017).

It should be noted that the weight assignment is derived from expert consensus in tertiary Grade A hospitals in Hubei Province. Given the specific resource environment and clinical context of the panel, direct extrapolation of these weights to primary care settings with constrained staffing, equipment, or informatization capacity is not recommended without local validation.

Discussion

Scientific rigor and reliability of the indicator system

Nursing-sensitive quality indicators are scientific tools for the objective evaluation of clinical care quality and the effectiveness of nursing interventions [26]. Grounded in Donabedian’s structure–process–outcome framework, this study developed a disease‑specific nursing‑sensitive indicator system for neonatal jaundice through a combination of systematic literature review, semi‑structured interviews, and two‑round Delphi consultations.The research adhered to standardized Delphi method procedures to ensure the scientific rigor and reliability of the indicator system. The effective response rates (90.0% and 100.0%) indicated sustained expert engagement. Expert authority coefficients (Cr = 0.851 and 0.879) exceeded the accepted threshold of 0.7 [27], supporting the professional credibility of the panel. Kendall’s coordination coefficients increased from 0.285 to 0.376 in Round 1 to 0.332–0.419 in Round 2, with all indicators meeting the pre‑defined retention criteria (mean importance score > 3.50; coefficient of variation < 0.25). These findings demonstrate progressive convergence of expert opinion and adequate inter‑rater reliability.It should be noted that the current indicator set is derived from expert consensus rather than prospective clinical validation. The sensitivity of individual indicators in detecting care quality gaps—and their association with hard clinical endpoints such as bilirubin encephalopathy—requires confirmation through future pilot implementation studies.

Interpretation of the indicator framework

Structural indicators

Structural indicators define the resource foundation necessary for standardized care delivery. Within this dimension, nursing human resource allocationcarried the highest weight (0.132). Notably, pass rate of neonatal jaundice skills assessment(0.036) and pass rate of theoretical knowledge training(0.036) ranked highest among tertiary indicators, underscoring that professional competence is a prerequisite for quality assurance. These findings align with previous studies on specialty nursing indicator development [28, 29].

For tertiary care institutions with comparable resource levels, optimizing staffing ratios and institutionalizing competency-based training may contribute to quality improvement. However, the implementation pathways for primary care settings require further exploration. The specific thresholds for these indicators should be adapted according to local workforce availability and educational backgrounds.

Process indicators

Process indicators directly reflect adherence to evidence‑based protocols and mediate the relationship between structural inputs and clinical outcomes [30]. The three highest‑weighted process indicators—timeliness of phototherapy for high‑risk neonates(0.085), implementation rate of early skin‑to‑skin contact and early suckling(0.062), and screening rate of high‑risk newborns(0.068)—form a continuous quality‑control chain spanning prevention, risk identification, and targeted intervention. Gaps in any component may substantially increase the risk of severe hyperbilirubinemia and neurologic injury.

While current international guidelines recommend perinatal breastfeeding promotion and strict adherence to phototherapy eligibility criteria [31, 32], the prioritization of these specific indicators could be considered for implementation after local adaptation. Institutions should evaluate their specific patient case-mix and resource constraints before enforcing uniform compliance standards.

Outcome indicators

Outcome indicators represent the ultimate measure of nursing effectiveness. Short‑term treatment responsiveness is captured by rate of bilirubin reduction reaching the standard within 24 h post‑phototherapy(0.035), whereas incidence of bilirubin encephalopathy (0.040) serves as a sentinel indicator for irreversible harm. Evidence suggests that poorly managed severe hyperbilirubinemia adversely affects long‑term neurobehavioral development [33]. Institutions should establish routine monitoring and feedback loops for these outcomes, using audit findings to drive targeted staff training, guideline dissemination, and iterative process refinement, thereby closing the quality improvement cycle.

Institutions may consider establishing routine monitoring and feedback loops for these outcomes to drive targeted staff training. Nevertheless, the baseline incidence of these outcomes varies significantly across regions; therefore, the indicator targets should be adjusted based on the local epidemiological profile rather than adopting a one-size-fits-all benchmark.

Conclusion

This study developed a sensitive indicator system for assessing the quality of neonatal jaundice care, comprising three first-level, ten second-level, and twenty-four third-level indicators. Founded on rigorous scientific methodologies, the system demonstrates strong methodological rigor and expert consensus validity; its practical utility and measurement properties await confirmation in future clinical application.It preliminarily provides a standardized framework for assessing neonatal jaundice care quality in tertiary care settings, with its practical utility requiring further empirical verification, providing a reference tool for neonatal jaundice care quality assessment in similar tertiary care settings, pending further validation across diverse healthcare contexts.Prior to application in primary care institutions, local adaptation and threshold adjustment are recommended based on actual resource availability.

Limitations

This study has several limitations.First, all consulted experts were affiliated with tertiary Grade A hospitals in Hubei Province, China. While this ensured high professional authority, it introduces geographic and institutional-level bias: the indicator system may better fit resource-rich tertiary centers, and its adaptability to primary care facilities with limited staffing, equipment, or informatization capacity remains uncertain. Differences in regional healthcare policies, neonatal care protocols, and population characteristics may also affect the generalizability of the indicators across different settings.Second, this study has not yet undergone clinical empirical testing; thus, the sensitivity and specificity of the index system require validation in clinical settings. Future investigations could focus on the following aspects: First, establishing threshold standards that correspond to different levels of nursing quality to provide specific targets for quality improvement. Second, conducting multi-center applied research to assess the applicability of the indicator system across diverse medical environments.
