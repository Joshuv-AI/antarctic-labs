Test-then-Punish: A Statistical Approach to Repeated Games
Aymeric Capitaine, Antoine Scheid, Etienne Boursier, Alain Durmus, Michael I. Jordan

Abstract
We study discounted infinitely repeated games in which players agree on a cooperative mixed action profile but, at each step, observe only the realized pure actions. This form of imperfect monitoring breaks classical trigger strategies, since deviations cannot be identified with certainty. To address this problem, we study how hypothesis testing can be used to sustain cooperation. First, we develop a framework that embeds statistical inference directly into strategic behavior. We introduce relaxed equilibrium notions that allow players to ignore vanishing-probability histories arising from rare but extreme realizations of the monitoring process. Within this framework, we formalize a generic test-then-punish strategy: players commit ex ante to a cooperative mixed action profile, continuously test whether observed play is consistent with this prescription, and permanently switch to punishment once sufficient statistical evidence of deviation accumulates. Under mild conditions on the testing procedure, this construction sustains any feasible and individually rational payoff for sufficiently patient players, yielding a Folk-theorem–type result under imperfect monitoring. We then propose two explicit implementations of this strategy. The first relies on anytime-valid sequential tests, providing uniform control of Type I error over an infinite horizon and a finite expected detection time for payoff-relevant deviations. However, this strategy only accounts for stationary deviations and yields a Nash equilibrium. The second uses testing over batches with a fixed size, accommodating arbitrary deviations and achieving subgame-perfect Nash equilibrium, at the cost of losing global anytime guarantees on false punishments. Together, these results clarify a fundamental trade-off between statistical soundness and game-theoretic robustness, offering a principled foundation for data-driven cooperation in repeated strategic environments.

Introduction

Sustaining cooperation among strategic agents is central in many economic sectors, ranging from industrial organization and financial markets to environmental regulation and international trade. A common solution is given by punishment-based strategies: players agree on a cooperative course of action and continue to follow it as long as they observe that their opponents do the same. When a deviation is detected, they switch to a punishment phase. Provided that the threat of punishment is credible and sufficiently severe relative to the short-run gains from deviation, no player has an incentive to depart from the cooperative strategy.

Central to the literature on repeated games
(
30
;
43
)
, the
Folk Theorem

(
27
)
formalizes the effectiveness of such punishment-based strategies. This theorem asserts that
any
feasible and individually rational payoff profile of a given stage game can be sustained as a subgame-perfect Nash equilibrium
(
45
)
when the game is repeated infinitely and players are sufficiently patient. This foundational result has been established both under perfect monitoring, where players are assumed to observe their opponents’ strategies exactly, and under some imperfect monitoring setups, in which players observe only signals that are imperfectly correlated with those strategies.

The latter setting is of considerable practical interest, as economic actors are often obliged to operate with imperfect information structures. However, this strategy is notoriously difficult to analyze and
this has led to the development of a variety of approaches that primarily deliver existence results rather than providing implementable strategies. As a consequence, these approaches generally do not yield procedures that can directly exploit modern learning techniques and the abundance of data available in many real-world environments. This is all the more unfortunate given that real-world economic agents often make use of statistical analysis to implement punishment-based cooperative strategies, as illustrated by the following two examples.

A first concrete example is offered by financial auditing. Firms and auditors interact repeatedly over several periods, with firms expected to report financial statements truthfully and where deviations take the form of misreporting or manipulation. Because such behavior cannot typically be detected with certainty in a single period, compliance is assessed using statistical procedures. As formalized by the Public Company Accounting Oversight Board
(
51
)
in the United States, auditors form quantitative expectations, based on trends or regression-based models, and investigate outcomes that are statistically inconsistent with these benchmarks. When sufficient evidence accumulates, deviations trigger sanctions or legal liability. The prospect of delayed but severe punishment deters short-run deviations and sustains truthful reporting despite noisy monitoring.

Anti-doping enforcement in professional sports provides another example of cooperation sustained through explicit statistical testing in a repeated interaction. Athletes repeatedly choose whether to compete cleanly or not, while individual actions are unobservable and biological measurements are noisy. Under the Athlete Biological Passport, administered by the World Anti-Doping Agency, compliance is assessed using longitudinal statistical models that test whether an athlete’s biomarker profile is statistically consistent with natural variation. Sanctions are triggered when accumulated evidence renders clean behavior sufficiently unlikely
(
2
)
. Here again, the threat of punishment enforces cooperation.

The gap between the literature on repeated games with imperfect monitoring–which largely abstracts away from statistically grounded cooperative strategies–and real-world economic interactions, where data and learning techniques are pervasive, raises the following question:
how can we formalize the use of hypothesis testing to sustain cooperation in repeated games
?

Additionally, statistical monitoring would be even more attractive if it could exploit prediction and inference methods. This leads us to a second, more practice-focused question:
Is there a straightforward testing procedure that could realistically be employed in real-world instances of this problem
?

We answer the first question by developing a statistical framework for repeated games with imperfect public monitoring, which reposes on two key concepts. First, due to the probabilistic nature of statistical inference, we introduce equilibrium notions that relax Nash and subgame–perfect Nash equilibria by allowing players to disregard rare-probability histories and tail events arising from extreme realizations of the monitoring process. Second, we formalize a generic
test-then-punish
strategy: before play begins, players commit ex ante to a cooperative mixed action profile that implements a target feasible payoff. Then, observing only the realized pure actions, they continuously test the null hypothesis that each opponent is adhering to the prescribed mixed strategy. Cooperation persists as long as sequential tests fail to reject the null hypothesis. Once a test detects sufficient evidence of deviation, players switch permanently to a retaliatory punishment profile. Under mild conditions on the testing procedure—concretely met by the tests we design later—this template sustains any feasible and individually rational payoff for sufficiently patient players, yielding a Folk-Theorem–type result under imperfect public monitoring. We emphasize that our statistical approach allows us to bypass the standard decomposability and self-generation techniques typically used to analyze equilibria under imperfect monitoring
(
1
)
. Instead, we build on familiar proof techniques from the perfect monitoring literature and address the stochasticity generated by random signals using probabilistic tools. The interest of such approach is to derive a simple and strategy, relying on a punishing rule with a timing that is explicitely controlled.

We address the second question by introducing two explicit testing strategies. The first one leverages anytime valid testing and e-processes
(
53
)
. This framework enables the testing of a hypothesis across all game rounds simultaneously, maintaining validity regardless of the choice of stopping time. Assuming that players’ strategies are stationary, we show that it is possible to construct a strategy that almost attains any feasible, rational payoff profile as a Nash equilibrium. Two statistical errors are critical in this context. On the one hand, a Type I error occurs when the null hypothesis is rejected despite being true, which corresponds to entering the punishment phase while players are still cooperating. Anytime-valid testing ensures that the probability of this event remains below a pre-specified threshold. We emphasize that our approach is novel in providing such a guarantee for games, contrary to the belief-based
(
58
)
or belief-free
(
21
)
methods typically employed in the literature which overlook this risk. On the other hand, a Type II error occurs when players fail to detect a deviation and do not trigger punishment. We establish a finite bound on the expected stopping time of cooperation when a player deviates, as a function of the magnitude of their deviation. Our result on an upper bound for the expected stopping time new in the testing literature and may be of independent interest.

The second strategy is designed to overcome the two limitations of the previous approach that only supports stationary deviations and solely provides Nash equilibrium (rather than subgame-perfect equilibrium). Instead of continuously testing a single null across all periods, we introduce a batch test-then-punish strategy that partitions the game into consecutive blocks of rounds. At the end of each batch, players run a statistical test on the empirical distribution of realized actions in the block to assess consistency with the prescribed cooperative mixed strategy. If the null is rejected in some batch, players switch permanently to the punishment profile. We show that this construction can approximate any feasible and individually rational payoff and yields a subgame-perfect Nash equilibrium. However, the batch procedure does not provide uniform, explicit control of Type I and Type II errors over the entire horizon.

Overall, there is a trade-off between the two testing procedures we propose. On the one hand, the anytime approach enjoys explicit Type I and Type II errors guarantees, making it an attractive option when such requirements are desired, typically for reasons of risk aversion or fairness. However, the anytime test-then-punish is weaker from a game-theoretic standpoint since it only prevents stationary deviations and does not enjoy subgame perfection. On the contrary, the batch test-then-punish handles arbitrary deviations, and is a subgame perfect Nash equilibrium, but does not enjoy statistical guarantees regarding false positives. In summary, our main contributions include:

1.

Statistical monitoring framework.
We formalize repeated strategic interaction when players prescribe mixed actions but only observe realized pure actions. To account for rare events in statistical monitoring, we extend Nash/subgame-perfect equilibria to account for histories that occur with small probability.

2.

Generic test-then-punish strategy.
We propose a generic strategy where players commit ex ante to a cooperative mixed profile targeting a feasible payoff, test opponents’ action against this prescription, and switch to punishment upon detecting a deviation. Under mild conditions on the sequential test, this recovers a Folk-Theorem result under imperfect monitoring.

3.

Explicit testing strategies.
Finally, we instantiate the template with two sequential tests: the first one controls false punishment over an infinite horizon while still detecting deviations in finite time. The second block-based variant handles any form of deviation and yields stronger high-probability sequential guarantees, at the cost of weaker global anytime Type I error control.

{\bf s}_{\boldsymbol{v}}
is an SPNE.

The proof of
Theorem

1
, which is well known
(
29
)
, is given for completeness in
Appendix

B
. This theorem states that provided players are sufficiently patient (as captured by the lower bound on the discount factor
\beta
), the grim-trigger strategy (
3
) allows to reach the agreed utility profile
\boldsymbol{v}
as an SPNE.

Note that in
Theorem

1
, punishment corresponds to a Nash equilibrium, as is standard in the literature
(
29
)
. One could strengthen the result by considering punishments that deliver minmax payoffs, but this requires a more involved analysis—in particular, punishers must themselves be incentivized to continue punishing
(
29
)
. For clarity and ease of exposition, we therefore restrict attention to Nash-equilibrium punishments in this paper.

3
Equilibrium Under Imperfect Monitoring via Anytime Testing

The perfect monitoring setting presented in the previous section is unfortunately rarely encountered in real-life situations.
We thus instead ask whether similar results can be obtained in a setting where players do not observe actual mixed strategies, but only
pure actions drawn from them
. In this imperfect information setting, applying the grim-trigger (
3
) is impossible, since players do not directly observe the mixed strategies played by their opponents. In particular, it is no longer possible to assess with certainty whether a player
j
deviated from
w^{j}_{\boldsymbol{v}}
(except when a pure action from outside the support of
w^{j}_{\boldsymbol{v}}
is drawn). However, we will show that it is possible to use
statistical tests
to check whether such a deviation occurred with high probability. If a player gathers sufficient statistical evidence to support this hypothesis, they could trigger a punishment similarly to (
3
). This is the idea behind the
test-then-punish
strategy that we present formally below.

Assume that players agree on feasible payoffs
\boldsymbol{v}=(v^{1},\ldots,v^{N})\in\mathcal{U}^{1}\times\ldots\times\mathcal{U}^{N}
and profile of mixed strategies
{\bf w}_{\boldsymbol{v}}=(w^{1}_{\boldsymbol{v}},\ldots,w^{N}_{\boldsymbol{v}})\in\Delta(\mathcal{A})
such that
v^{i}=u^{i}({\bf w}_{\boldsymbol{v}})
for any
i\in[N]
. We then define
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{v}}=(\sigma_{\boldsymbol{v}}^{1},\ldots,\sigma_{\boldsymbol{v}}^{N})\in\mathcal{S}
where for any
j\in[N]
,
\sigma^{j}_{\boldsymbol{v}}(\text{h}_{t})=w^{j}_{\boldsymbol{v}}
for all
t\geqslant 0\text{ and }\text{h}_{t}\in\mathcal{H}_{t}
.
Then, each player
j
can test as follows for
i\neq j

\mathrm{H}^{i}_{0}:\;\;s^{i}=\sigma^{i}_{\boldsymbol{v}}\qquad\text{versus}\qquad\mathrm{H}^{i}_{1}\colon\;\,s^{i}\neq\sigma^{i}_{\boldsymbol{v}}\;.

(4)

\mathrm{H}^{i}_{0}
being true means that player
i
sticks to the cooperative strategy
w^{i}_{\boldsymbol{v}}
. On the contrary, rejecting
\mathrm{H}^{i}_{0}
amounts to concluding that the player
i
does not play the agreed-upon strategy.

To decide whether
\mathrm{H}^{i}_{0}
is true, player
j\neq i
may rely on a sequence of tests
\boldsymbol{\psi}^{i}=\{\psi^{i}_{t}\}_{t\geqslant 0}
where
\psi^{i}_{0}=0
and for any
t\geqslant 1,\psi^{i}_{t}\colon\mathcal{H}_{t}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
. If
\psi^{i}_{t}=1
, then
\mathrm{H}^{i}_{0}
has been rejected at time
t
. Here, we assume that all players
j\neq i
use the same test for
\mathrm{H}^{i}_{0}
(hence the absence of
j
indexing in
\psi^{i}_{t}
) given that they all have access to a common public history
\text{h}_{t}\in\mathcal{H}_{t}
at time
t
. We can then define the
test-then-punish
strategy
{\bf s}_{\boldsymbol{v}}\in\mathcal{S}
where for
j\in[N]
,
s^{j}_{\boldsymbol{v}}
is given by

s^{j}_{\boldsymbol{v}}(\text{h}_{t})=\begin{cases}w^{j}_{\boldsymbol{v}}&\text{if }\prod\limits_{s=0}^{t}\prod\limits_{i=1}^{N}\,(1-\psi^{i}_{s}(\text{h}_{s}))=1\;,\\
b^{\,j}&\text{otherwise}\;.\end{cases}

(5)

Informally, at time
t\geqslant 0
, if players have not detected any deviation, i.e.,
\psi_{t}^{i}=0
for all
i\in[N]
, they continue playing the cooperative mixed profile
w_{\boldsymbol{v}}
. Once a deviation is detected, that is,
\psi_{t}^{i}=1
for some
i
, all players permanently switch to the punishing strategy in the following rounds.

To simplify notation, for any
i\in[N]
we define the (random) rejection time associated with the sequence of tests
\boldsymbol{\psi}^{i}
as

\tau_{\boldsymbol{\psi}^{i}}=\inf\bigl\{t\geqslant 0\colon\psi^{i}_{t}=1\bigr\}\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}\;,

(6)

with the convention
\inf\varnothing=\infty
. In words,
\tau_{\boldsymbol{\psi}^{i}}
is the first time
\mathrm{H}^{i}_{0}
is rejected; that is, the first time player
i
has been detected as deviating. Similarly, we define
\tau_{\boldsymbol{\psi}}
as

\tau_{\boldsymbol{\psi}}=\min_{i\in[N]}\tau_{\boldsymbol{\psi}^{i}}\;,

which is the first time
any
null hypothesis
\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0},\ldots,\mathrm{H}^{N}_{0}
has been rejected, that is any player has been detected as deviating. It should be clear from (
5
) that players play
{\bf w}_{\boldsymbol{v}}
for any
t\leqslant\tau_{\boldsymbol{\psi}}
, and turn to the punishment
\mathbf{b}
for any
t>\tau_{\boldsymbol{\psi}}
.

Observe that the test-then-punish strategy (
5
) mimics the grim trigger strategy under perfect monitoring (
3
) with one notable difference: while the latter triggers a punishment as soon as a deviation is observed (as allowed by perfect monitoring), the former triggers a punishment as soon as one null hypothesis
\mathrm{H}^{i}_{0}
is rejected (because of imperfect monitoring). If tests were perfect—that is, if
\mathrm{H}^{i}_{0}
would be rejected if and only if player
i
actually deviated, the two approaches would be equivalent. However, statistical tests are never perfect. They may suffer from Type I error (rejecting
\mathrm{H}_{0}
while it is true) and Type II error (failing to reject
\mathrm{H}_{0}
while it is false), because they rely on stochastic observations. In our case, this means that the test-then-punish strategy (
5
) may either wrongfully trigger punishment while players are actually sticking to
{\bf w}_{\boldsymbol{v}}
, which corresponds to Type I error, or fail to punish a player who is deviating, which corresponds to Type II error. Both kinds of errors may prevent the test-then-punish procedure from achieving its goal of ensuring a target payoff profile
\boldsymbol{v}
under a (subgame perfect) Nash equilibrium.

It is therefore natural to ask that the family of sequential tests
\boldsymbol{\psi}=(\boldsymbol{\psi}^{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\psi}^{N})
satisfies some conditions ensuring that Type I and Type II errors remain low, so that
{\bf s}_{\boldsymbol{v}}
is effective. In particular, regarding the Type I error, we introduce the following requirement.

Condition 1

(Low Type I error probability)
.

There exists
\gamma\in(0,1)
such that for any
i\in[N]

\mathbb{E}^{{\bf s}_{\boldsymbol{v}}}[\text{1}\{\tau_{\boldsymbol{\psi}^{i}}<\infty\}]=\mathbb{P}^{{\bf s}_{\boldsymbol{v}}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tau_{\boldsymbol{\psi}^{i}}<\infty}}\right)\leqslant\gamma/N\;.

(7)

If
1
is true, then for any
i\in[N]
the test sequence
\boldsymbol{\psi}^{i}
is such that the probability to wrongfully reject
\mathrm{H}^{i}_{0}
under the cooperative strategy
{\bf w}_{\boldsymbol{v}}
remains low. Equivalently, the probability to wrongfully detect a deviation from player
i
while they cooperate is low. Note that
1
immediately controls the probability of wrongfully entering the punishment phase, since a union bound gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{{\bf s}_{\boldsymbol{v}}}(\tau_{\boldsymbol{\psi}}<\infty)=\mathbb{P}^{{\bf s}_{\boldsymbol{v}}}(\cup_{i\in[N]}\,\{\tau_{\boldsymbol{\psi}^{i}}<\infty\})\leqslant\sum_{i\in[N]}\mathbb{P}^{{\bf s}_{\boldsymbol{v}}}(\tau_{\boldsymbol{\psi}^{i}}<\infty)\leqslant\gamma\;.

This ensures that cooperation can be sustained in the long run with high probability. Note that
1
becomes more stringent with the number of players. Indeed, more players imply more hypothesis tests, which increases the probability of false alarms and therefore calls for tighter control of each individual test. Controlling the false discovery rate
(
9
)
, particularly via anytime extensions
(
66
)
, may provide a less conservative way to handle the growing number of hypotheses when a player faces multiple opponents, while still limiting false alarms.

Regarding Type II error, the family of sequential tests
\boldsymbol{\psi}=(\boldsymbol{\psi}^{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\psi}^{N})
should be such that if a player deviates, they ought to be detected in finite time with high probability. Note that it is extremely hard, if not hopeless, to detect deviations that are arbitrarily close to the collaborative strategy
{\bf w}_{\boldsymbol{v}}
. We thus focus on deviations that are “far enough” from
{\bf w}_{\boldsymbol{v}}
. To formalize this idea, we define for any
\varepsilon>0
and
i\in[N]
the total variation-ball of radius
\varepsilon
centered in
w^{i}_{\boldsymbol{v}}

\displaystyle\mathsf{B}^{i}({\bf w}_{\boldsymbol{v}}\,,\,\varepsilon)=\{s\in\mathcal{S}^{i}\colon\>\lVert s(\text{h}_{t})\,-\,w^{i}_{\boldsymbol{v}}\rVert_{1}\leqslant 2\varepsilon\text{ for any }t\geqslant 0\text{ and }\text{h}_{t}\in\mathcal{H}_{t}\}\;.

Notice that for any
\mathfrak{S}^{i}\subseteq\mathcal{S}^{i},\,\mathfrak{S}^{i}\setminus\mathsf{B}^{i}({\bf w}_{\boldsymbol{v}},\varepsilon)
contains all deviations in
\mathfrak{S}^{i}
for player
i
that are
\varepsilon
-significant, in the sense that they are at least
\varepsilon
far from the cooperative strategy
w^{i}_{\boldsymbol{v}}
in total variation. This situation is depicted on
Figure

1
. Then, for any
\mathfrak{S}=\mathfrak{S}^{1}\times\ldots\times\mathfrak{S}^{N}\subseteq\mathcal{S}
, we define the set of strategy profiles containing at least one
\varepsilon
-significant deviation

\mathfrak{S}({\bf w}_{\boldsymbol{v}},\varepsilon)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{{\bf s}=(s^{1},\ldots,s^{N})\in\mathfrak{S},\>\text{there exists }i\in[N]\colon\,s^{i}\in\mathfrak{S}^{i}\setminus\mathsf{B}^{i}({\bf w}_{\boldsymbol{v}}\,,\,\varepsilon)}}\right\}\;.

(8)

If one player

picks a strategy

.

We are now ready to state our second requirement. Intuitively, the following condition states that any strategy profile
{\bf s}\in\mathfrak{S}
containing a
\varepsilon
-significant deviation triggers a punishment in finite time.

Condition 2
.

For
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a sequence
(\zeta^{(\varepsilon)}_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
such that

\text{for any }t\geqslant 0\,,\quad\sup_{{\bf s}\in\mathfrak{S}({\bf w}_{\boldsymbol{v}}\,,\,\varepsilon)}\mathbb{P}^{{\bf s}}(\tau_{\boldsymbol{\psi}}\geqslant t)\leqslant\zeta^{(\varepsilon)}_{t}\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\,\zeta^{(\varepsilon)}_{t}<\infty\;.

(9)

2
ensures that for any strategy profile
{\bf s}\in\mathfrak{S}({\bf w}_{\boldsymbol{v}},\varepsilon)
(such that at least one player deviates from
w^{i}_{\boldsymbol{v}}
by at least
\varepsilon
), the punishment time
\tau_{\boldsymbol{\psi}}
does not exceed
t
with high probability. In particular,
2
implies that players enter the punishment phase in finite time, in expectation, when one of them
\varepsilon
-significantly deviates since

\sup_{{\bf s}\in\mathfrak{S}({\bf w}_{\boldsymbol{v}},\varepsilon)}\mathbb{E}^{{\bf s}}[\tau_{\boldsymbol{\psi}}]=\sup_{{\bf s}\in\mathfrak{S}({\bf w}_{\boldsymbol{v}},\varepsilon)}\sum_{t\geqslant 0}\mathbb{P}^{{\bf s}}(\tau_{\boldsymbol{\psi}}\geqslant t)\leqslant\sum_{t\geqslant 0}\,\sup_{{\bf s}\in\mathfrak{S}({\bf w}_{\boldsymbol{v}},\varepsilon)}\mathbb{P}^{{\bf s}}(\tau_{\boldsymbol{\psi}}\geqslant t)\leqslant\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\zeta^{(\varepsilon)}_{t}<\infty\;.

We remark that condition (
2
) leaves “small” deviations in
\mathsf{B}^{i}({\bf w}_{\boldsymbol{v}},\varepsilon)
aside: such deviations are not required to trigger a punishment in finite time. In fact, this is not a matter of concern since such deviations cannot bring a significant utility gain, and are therefore covered by our approximate equilibrium notion. This is established formally in the following proposition.

Proposition 1
.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
. For any
i\in[N]
, if
s^{i}\in\mathsf{B}^{i}({\bf w}_{\boldsymbol{v}}\,,\,\varepsilon)
, then

\;U^{i}(s^{i},s^{-i}_{\boldsymbol{v}})\leqslant v^{i}+\varepsilon\;.

To summarize,
2
together with
Proposition

1
ensures that a player
i
deviating to some
s^{i}\neq s^{i}_{\boldsymbol{v}}
either triggers a punishment in finite time (if
s^{i}\in\mathfrak{S}^{i}\setminus\mathsf{B}^{i}({\bf w}_{\boldsymbol{v}},\varepsilon)
, by
2
) or cannot enjoy a large utility gain (if
s^{i}\in\mathfrak{S}^{i}\cap\mathsf{B}^{i}({\bf w}_{\boldsymbol{v}},\varepsilon)
, by
Proposition

1
). This observation allows us to establish a Folk Theorem based on the test-then-punish strategy (
5
), provided
1
and
2
hold.

Theorem 2
.

Let
\mathfrak{S}=\mathfrak{S}^{1}\times\ldots\times\mathfrak{S}^{N}\subseteq\mathcal{S}
and consider a family of sequential tests
\boldsymbol{\psi}
satisfying
1
and
2
. Let
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{U}^{1}\times\ldots\times\mathcal{U}^{N}
be a feasible payoff such that for any
i\in[N]

\displaystyle v^{i}\geqslant\underline{u}^{i}=u^{i}({\bf b})\;,

where
{\bf b}=(b^{1},\ldots b^{N})\in\Delta(\mathcal{A})
is some reference mixed strategy Nash equilibrium, which exists by the Nash theorem. Denoting
\tau^{(\varepsilon)}=\sup\limits_{{\bf s}\in\mathfrak{S}({\bf w}_{\boldsymbol{v}}\,,\,\varepsilon)}\mathbb{E}^{{\bf s}}[\tau_{\boldsymbol{\psi}}]<\infty
, and
\bar{u}^{i}=\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}u^{i}(a)
, if for any
i\in[N]

\bar{\beta}^{i}=\frac{\bar{u}^{i}-(1-\gamma)v^{i}}{\bar{u}^{i}-\underline{u}^{i}}\in[0,1]\quad\text{and}\quad\beta^{\tau^{(\varepsilon)}}\geqslant\bar{\beta}^{i}\;,

(10)

then
{\bf s}_{\boldsymbol{v}}
defined in (
5
) satisfies

(i)

for any
i\in[N]
,
(1-\gamma)v^{i}\leqslant U^{i}({\bf s}_{\boldsymbol{v}})\leqslant v^{i}\;,

(ii)

{\bf s}_{\boldsymbol{v}}
is a
(\varepsilon+\gamma,\mathfrak{S})
-NE.

It is informative to compare
Theorem

2
with its perfect monitoring counterpart,
Theorem

1
. In the latter,
U^{i}({\bf s}_{\boldsymbol{v}})
is
exactly
equal to the target payoff
v^{i}
. In the former, it lies in an interval of length
\gamma
around
v^{i}
. This difference is related to Type I error: even though all players stick to the collaborative strategy
w_{\boldsymbol{v}}
, they bear the risk of wrongfully entering the punishment phase with probability at most
\gamma
. Note that a better test results in a lower error
\gamma
, and thereby decreases the utility gap.

Also, observe that in
Theorem

1
,
{\bf s}_{\boldsymbol{v}}
is an exact SPNE. On the contrary, in
Theorem

2
it is a
(\varepsilon+\gamma,\mathfrak{S})
-NE. The
\varepsilon+\gamma
approximation term stems from type I error, and the possibility for players to pick small deviations that remain undetected as discussed in
Proposition

1
. Moreover,
Theorem

2
only shows that
{\bf s}_{\boldsymbol{v}}
is a Nash equilibrium and not that it is subgame perfect.
As we shall see in
Section

4
, it is possible to obtain subgame perfection by adopting a different testing strategy, at the expense of Type I and II error control. Finally, observe that under perfect monitoring (
Theorem

1
) the patience condition depends directly on the discount factor
\beta
, since deviations are detected immediately. In contrast, under the test-then-punish strategy of
Theorem

2
, a deviation may go undetected for up to
\tau^{(\varepsilon)}
periods, so the relevant condition involves
\beta^{\tau^{(\varepsilon)}}
. Consequently, the longer it takes to detect deviations, the more patience is required. More powerful tests, which reduce
\tau^{(\varepsilon)}
, relax this requirement.

3.1
Anytime testing via plug-in e-processes

To make
Theorem

2
fully actionable, we still need to specify a way for players to design a family of sequential tests
\boldsymbol{\psi}
satisfying
1
and
2
.
This can be achieved by making use of
anytime testing
and e-processes
(
53
)
. We provide a brief review of this framework in
Appendix

A
, while we only provide here the main ideas and concepts of this framework.

Given a null hypothesis
\mathrm{H}_{0}
, anytime valid testing is built on the notion of an
e-process
that are typically a supermartingale
(M_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
for any probability distribution belonging to
\mathrm{H}_{0}
. Under this setting, Ville’s inequality
(
64
, see
Appendix

A
)
,
(M_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
satisfies for any
\mathbb{P}_{0}\in\mathrm{H}_{0}
,

\mathbb{P}_{0}\,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sup_{t\geqslant 0}M_{t}\geqslant 1/\gamma}}\right)\;\leqslant\;\gamma\qquad\mbox{for any }\ \gamma\in(0,1)\;.

(11)

In other words, under the null hypothesis, the event
\cup_{t\geqslant 0}\{M_{t}\geqslant 1/\gamma\}
cannot happen with probability greater than
\gamma
. Thus, a natural test statistic for testing
\mathrm{H}_{0}
at level
\gamma
is given by
\text{1}\{M_{t}\geqslant 1/\gamma\}
.

Considering
(\mathscr{H}_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
, the natural filtration associated to
(\text{h}_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
,
\mathscr{H}_{t}=\sigma(\text{h}_{s}\,:\,s\leqslant t)
, we now specify how a player
j\in[N]
can design a
(\text{h}_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
-supermartingale
\{E^{i}_{t}\}_{t\geqslant 0}
to test
\mathrm{H}^{i}_{0}
for an opponent
i\in[N]
. Since at any round
t
, the pure actions
A^{1}_{t},\ldots,A^{N}_{t}
are observed, players can count how many times each player
i\in[N]
played action
a\in\mathcal{A}^{i}
up to step
t-1
for which we denote by
N_{t}^{i}(a)=\sum_{s=0}^{t-1}\text{1}\{A_{s}^{i}=a\}
. It is then possible to define the (smoothed
2
2

2

1
and
K
play the role of smoothing as in a Laplace estimator.
) empirical frequencies of actions played by player
i
as

\hat{w}^{i}_{t}(a)=\frac{N_{t}^{i}(a)+1}{t+K}\qquad\text{for any }a\in\mathcal{A}\;,

(12)

and
\widehat{w}^{i}_{-1}(a)=1/K
. Then, we can form a plug-in e-process
53
to test
\mathrm{H}^{i}_{0}
for player
i
as follows. Set
E_{-1}^{i}=1
and for any
t\geqslant 0

E_{t}^{i}=\prod_{s=0}^{t}\frac{\widehat{w}^{i}_{s}\,(A_{s}^{i})}{w_{\boldsymbol{v}}^{i}\,(A_{s}^{i})}\;.

(13)

We formally establish that
(E^{i}_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
is a
(\text{h}_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
-supermartingale and therefore an e-process under
\mathrm{H}^{i}_{0}
in
Appendix

A
,
Theorem

6
. Intuitively, if player
i
draws a pure action from
w^{i}_{\boldsymbol{v}}
, then
\hat{w}^{i}_{s}(a)/w^{i}_{\boldsymbol{v}}(a)\approx 1
for large

and any
a\in\mathcal{A}^{i}
, so

does not grow too much on average over time. On the contrary, if player
i
deviates, then

ought to increase and triggers the test the first time it exceeds
1/\gamma
.
If we combine all the above elements, we obtain a practical testing procedure for players to test
\mathrm{H}^{i}_{0}
: they can keep track of
i
’s action empirical frequencies (
12
) to build the e-process (
13
) and check whether it exceeds a given threshold. For
\gamma\in(0,1)
, this leads to the sequential test
\boldsymbol{\psi}^{i}=\{\psi^{i}_{t}\}_{t\geqslant 0}
for
\mathrm{H}^{i}_{0}
defined for any

as

\tilde{\psi}^{i}_{t}=\text{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{E_{t}^{i}\geqslant N/\gamma}}\right\}\;.

(14)

By Ville’s inequality (
11
), we can explicitly control the significance level of this test by tuning the threshold. This immediately implies that such a test satisfies
1
, as formally recorded in the following proposition.

Proposition 2
.

Let
\gamma\in(0,1)
and consider for any
i\in[N]
, the sequence of tests
(\tilde{\psi}_{t}^{i})_{t\geqslant 1}
defined in (
14
)
and the resulting strategy
{\bf s}_{\boldsymbol{v}}
defined in (

). Then,
1
is satisfied.

We now show that the test sequence defined above also satisfies
2
, under a simplifying assumption. Before proceeding, we highlight a fundamental difficulty. For
\mathfrak{S}=\mathfrak{S}^{1}\times\ldots\times\mathfrak{S}^{N}\subseteq\mathcal{S}
, the Type II error control required in

must hold uniformly over the set
\mathfrak{S}({\bf w}_{\boldsymbol{v}},\varepsilon)
defined in (
8
). That is, any strategy
s^{i}\in\mathfrak{S}^{i}
such that

deviates by at least
\varepsilon
from

at some time

should, with high probability, eventually trigger a punishment. From a statistical perspective, this amounts to testing a simple null hypothesis
\mathrm{H}_{0}^{i}
against an arbitrarily large composite alternative
\mathrm{H}_{1}^{i}
. It is well known that, without additional structural restrictions on the alternative, uniformly powerful tests generally do not exist, and nontrivial control of the Type II error is in many cases information-theoretically impossible
(

)
. We therefore simplify the problem by restricting attention to a set of simple deviations as follows

\tilde{\mathfrak{S}}^{i}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\,s\in\mathcal{S}^{i}\;:\;\text{there exists }\tilde{w}^{i}\in\Delta(\mathcal{A}^{i})\text{ such that for any }t\geqslant 0,\ \text{h}_{t}\in\mathcal{H}_{t},\,s(\text{h}_{t})=\tilde{w}^{i}\,}}\right\}\;.

In plain words,
\tilde{\mathfrak{S}}=\tilde{\mathfrak{S}}^{1}\times\ldots\times\tilde{\mathfrak{S}}^{N}
contains

strategies, which stick to a single mixed strategy

. This simplifies the statistical analysis by ensuring that under

, any player

\tilde{w}^{i}
has been chosen by player

. In other words,
\mathrm{H}^{i}_{1}
is still a composite alternative. We show in the following proposition that

is satisfied when strategies lie in
\tilde{\mathfrak{S}}({\bf w}_{\boldsymbol{v}},\varepsilon)
, as defined in
Equation

8
.

We now have statistical leverage to obtain a bound on the expected stopping time

defined as

\displaystyle\tilde{\tau}^{i}_{\psi}=\inf\{t\geqslant 1\colon\tilde{\psi}^{i}_{t}=1\}\;,

(16)

where

.

Theorem 3
.

Consider

, where

\displaystyle\zeta^{(\varepsilon)}_{t}=C_{1}\Biggl\{t\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\frac{2t\varepsilon^{2}}{C_{2}\log(t)^{2}}}}\right)+\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-t\frac{4\varepsilon^{4}}{C_{2}\log(t)}}}\right)+\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\frac{t\varepsilon^{4}}{2|\log\underline{{\bf w}_{\boldsymbol{v}}}|}}}\right)\Biggr\}+\text{1}\{t\leqslant 10\log(1/\gamma)/\varepsilon^{2}\}\;,

Corollary 1
.

Under the same setting than

2
and

3
, sequential tests as defined in (

. It is therefore possible to instantiate

2

Corollary 2
.

Consider the same setting than

3

4
Equilibrium Under Imperfect Monitoring via Batch Testing

In the previous section, we saw that anytime testing can be used to implement the test-then-punish strategy, and that it can certify an arbitrarily low Type I error. However, the approach has from two limitations. First, our analysis is limited to stationary deviations,
i.e.
, belonging to
\tilde{\mathfrak{S}}
defined in (
15

We therefore ask whether it is possible to implement the test-then-punish strategy in full generality, by considering the set of

, and to ensure that it is a subgame perfect equilibrium. As we shall show, this more ambitious objective can indeed be achieved by switching to a different testing strategy. The price to pay, however, is the loss of explicit control over Type I error.

Broadly speaking, we propose to use a

Just as in the anytime case, one can ask if there are conditions that support the design sequences of batch tests
\boldsymbol{\phi}=(\boldsymbol{\phi}^{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\phi}^{N})

1

First, the probability to wrongfully detect a deviation
on a given batch
should be small for most continuation games. Recall that for any
t\geqslant 0
, we define
k_{t}=\lfloor t/L\rfloor
to be the batch to which

Condition 3
.

There exist
p_{L}\in(0,1)
and
q_{L}\in(0,1)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{\bar{{\bf s}}_{\boldsymbol{v}}}(\kappa_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\geqslant k+1\,|\,\kappa_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\geqslant k\,,\,\text{h}_{t})\geqslant 1-p_{L}\qquad\text{for any}\quad k\geqslant k_{t}\;.

Informally,

(with probability at least

), when players are still playing according to the cooperative strategy

(given that

), the event

). The probabilities

and

should be typically be thought of as small. Note that this assumption is weaker than
1
, since we only require the Type I error to be low
within each batch

and

depend on the batch size

, since we expect this probability to decrease as batch size increases.

Second, for any player
i\in[N]
, a deviation from

that remains undetected on a batch should not bring too much utility gain.

Condition 4
.

For
\beta\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0,1}}\right)
,
there exists
\Delta_{L}
such that for any
k\geqslant 0
, player
i\in[N]
sequence of actions
(a^{i}_{t})_{t\in\mathcal{B}_{k}}\in(\mathcal{A}^{i})^{L}
,

\text{1}\{\phi^{i}_{k}=0\}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{B}_{k}}\beta^{t}\,u^{i}(a^{i}_{t},w^{-i}_{\boldsymbol{v}})\leqslant\sum_{t\in\mathcal{B}_{k}}\beta^{t}(v^{i}+\Delta_{L})\;,

where
u^{i}(a^{i},w^{-i}_{\boldsymbol{v}})=\sum_{a^{-i}}u^{i}(a^{i},a^{-i})\,w^{-i}_{\boldsymbol{v}}[a^{-i}].

Broadly speaking,

ensures that if no deviation is detected on a batch (as captured by
\text{1}\{\phi^{i}_{k}=0\}
), then no sequence of actions
(a^{i}_{t})_{t\in\mathcal{B}_{k}}
for player

can result in a discounted payoff greater than

on this batch when opponents stick to

.

Recall that
2
required the probability of not triggering a punishment under deviation to be bounded. This requirement was precisely why, in the anytime framework, we restricted attention to stationary deviations
\tilde{\mathfrak{S}}
: establishing such a guarantee for arbitrary deviations was not feasible. In contrast,

merely requires that the utility gain from deviating be bounded. As we shall see later, this condition holds for arbitrary deviations under the class of tests considered in
Section

. This is the key advantage of the batch approach, and it explains why it can accommodate a substantially richer class of strategies than anytime testing. As a side remark, note that if players were to observe realized payoffs rather than actions, they could test condition

directly. This observation suggests a natural extension of our framework to settings with payoff-based monitoring rather than action-based monitoring, which we leave for future work.

3
and

Theorem 4
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
,
\beta\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0,1}}\right)
and consider
\bar{{\bf s}}_{\boldsymbol{v}}
defined in (

\boldsymbol{\phi}
satisfying
3
and

\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{U}^{1}\times\ldots\times\mathcal{U}^{N}
be a feasible payoff such that

for
i\in[N]
. Suppose in addition that for any
i\in[N]
,

\beta^{L}\geqslant\frac{\bar{u}^{i}-v^{i}-\Delta_{L}}{\bar{u}^{i}-\underline{u}^{i}}\;.

Then, it holds

1.

For any
i\in[N],\,

\beta^{L}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{p_{L}}{1-\beta^{L}}}}\right)\,v^{i}\leqslant U^{i}(\bar{{\bf s}}_{\boldsymbol{v}})\leqslant v^{i}\;,

2.

\bar{{\bf s}}_{\boldsymbol{v}}
is a
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\beta^{-L}(1-\beta^{2L}+\Delta_{L})+\frac{p_{L}}{1-\beta^{L}}\,,\,q_{L}}}\right)
-HP-SPNE.

4
shows that batch testing, provided
3
and
4
hold, yields an approximate Folk Theorem. This result exhibits both similarities to and differences from its anytime counterpart,

2
.

Regarding similarities, both results ensure that

remains close to

. In the anytime case, the approximation becomes sharper as the Type I error bound

becomes smaller; similarly, in the batch case, it improves as

decreases. Moreover, in both cases the strategy profile constitutes an approximate equilibrium. In the anytime setting, the approximation term shrunk with
\gamma
and

, the magnitude of the deviation; similarly, in the batch setting, it vanishes as

and
\Delta_{L}
, the maximum utility gain on each batch, decrease. Finally, the patience condition (
21
) involves
\beta^{L}
, just as (
10

\beta^{\tilde{\tau}_{\varepsilon}}
. The intuition is identical: because tests are conducted only at the end of batches, players may enjoy up to

However,

4
and

differ in two important respects. First, the batch construction of

is an equilibrium with respect to
all
possible deviations, whereas the anytime result provides guarantees only against stationary deviations. This is because

is more easily met for arbitrary deviations than
1
. Second, the batch equilibrium is subgame perfect (except for a small fraction
q_{L}
of continuation games), while in the anytime setting
{\bf s}_{\boldsymbol{v}}
is a Nash equilibrium only. The reason behind this difference is that for any step
t\geqslant 0
, the anytime procedure keeps track of the empirical frequencies
\hat{w}^{i}_{t}(a)
using
all
observed actions up to time
t

could be constructed at any round

so as to strongly support

for a given player

in the repeated games literature
(
43
)
, is precisely why the construction yields subgame-perfect equilibria. For any continuation game starting at
t\geqslant 0
, the history

influences only the test statistic for the
current

from a game-theoretic perspective.

4.1
Batch testing via concentration

In order to implement the batch test-then-punish strategy, it remains to determine sequences of tests
\boldsymbol{\phi}=(\boldsymbol{\phi}^{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\phi}^{N})
which satisfy
3
and
4
. We propose a very simple procedure: on each batch, players collect the pure actions played by player
i
, compute their empirical frequencies, and check whether they match with
w^{i}_{\boldsymbol{v}}
. Formally, writing
\mathcal{A}^{i}=\{a^{i}(1),\ldots,a^{i}(K)\}
, for any
k\geqslant 0
and
i\in[N]
, we let

\hat{w}^{i}_{k}=L^{-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{t\in\mathcal{B}_{k}}\text{1}\{A^{i}_{t}=a^{i}(1)\},\ldots,\sum_{t\in\mathcal{B}_{k}}\text{1}\{A^{i}_{t}=a^{i}(K)\}}}\right)^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\;,

(22)

be the vector of empirical frequencies of pure actions played by
i
on batch number
k
. Then, for a given threshold
\delta\in(0,1)
, we test
\mathrm{H}^{i}_{0,k}
with the test statistic

\tilde{\phi}^{i}_{k}=\text{1}\{\lVert\hat{w}^{i}_{k}-w^{i}_{\boldsymbol{v}}\rVert_{1}\geqslant\delta\}\;.

(23)

The intuition behind (
23
) is simple. If
\mathrm{H}^{i}_{0,k}
is true, then the empirical distribution
\hat{w}^{i}_{k}
concentrates around
w_{\boldsymbol{v}}^{i}
with high probability. The contrapositive immediately tells us that if
\lVert\hat{w}^{i}_{k}-w_{\boldsymbol{v}}^{i}\rVert_{1}
is high, we can suspect that the null
\mathrm{H}^{i}_{0,k}
is false. We show in the next proposition that our test (
23

3
and
4
.

Proposition 3
.

Let
\beta,\delta\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0,1}}\right)
. Consider for any
i\in[N]
, the test defined in (
23
)
and the resulting strategy
\bar{{\bf s}}_{\boldsymbol{v}}
defined in (
19
). Then,

1.

3
holds with
p_{L}=q_{L}=2KN\exp(-2L\delta^{2}/K^{2})\;,

2.

4
holds with
\Delta_{L}=\delta+3(1-\beta^{L})\;.

The first point of
Proposition

3
follows directly from a concentration argument. In the second point,
\Delta_{L}
features two terms. The first one stems from the possibility for player
i
to slightly deviate from
w^{i}_{\boldsymbol{v}}
within a
\delta
range, without triggering the test of player
i
. The second one follows from the ability of player
i
to re-order pure actions within a batch without changing
\hat{w}^{i}_{k}
, but in a way that most valuable actions are played at the beginning of the batch to enjoy a better discounting.

It is worth noting two trade-offs in
L
and
\delta
appear in
Proposition

3
. On the one hand, a larger

ensures that
\hat{w}^{i}_{k}
concentrates more tightly around

under

decreases with

gives the opponent more room to deviate within a batch and potentially obtain a sizeable payoff gain, since detection can only occur at the end of that batch (

increases with

decreases as

increases with

That said, it is possible to strike a balance by carefully tuning

While

Let
\beta,\delta\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0,1}}\right)
. Consider for any
i\in[N]
, the test defined in (

)
and the resulting strategy
\bar{{\bf s}}_{\boldsymbol{v}}
defined in (

\boldsymbol{v}
such that

for

. Suppose that

\text{ $\min\limits_{j\in[N]}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\lVert w^{j}_{\boldsymbol{v}}\rVert_{0}=\sum\limits_{\ell=1}^{K}\text{1}\{w^{j}_{\boldsymbol{v},\ell}\neq 0\}}}\right\}>1$ and $\delta<1-1/\mathop{\min}\limits_{j\in[N]}\lVert w^{j}_{\boldsymbol{v}}\rVert_{0}$ }\;.

We emphasize that

In such settings, implementing the anytime test–then–punish mechanism may be preferable. This comparison highlights that the two approaches involve distinct trade-offs, and the choice between them should ultimately depend on the institutional context and on players’ attitudes toward risk and fairness.

5
Conclusion

The folk theorem has long stood as the canonical way to study cooperation in repeated games, relying on punishments triggered by deviations. This paper proposes a new framework for analyzing how cooperation can be sustained
in multiplayer repeated games through statistical testing: players use mixed strategies but observe only realized pure actions, and enforcement is statistical, explicitly managing false alarms and missed detections. We introduce equilibrium notions that safely disregard tail probability histories generated by the monitoring process, and we provide a general test-then-punish strategy that yields two Folk-theorem-type resultsn showing that any feasible and individually rational payoff profile can be supported as an approximate equilibrium for sufficiently patient players. This study is, to our knowledge, the first to leverage the flexibility offered by the hypothesis testing framework to analyze repeated games with imperfect public monitoring.

We also provide two implementations with the corresponding tests. The anytime construction uses sequential inference to provide implementable strategies with uniform Type I error control over the whole horizon and finite-sample detection guarantees, while the batch construction loses anytime Type I error control to handle richer deviation patterns and to obtain a high-probability analogue of subgame perfection.

More broadly, the analysis highlights how repeated-game reasoning changes when interaction is mediated by data and inference. Promising directions for future work include richer information structures–e.g., private signals–heterogeneous agents, adaptive or nonstationary environments among others.

Statistical Background

Definition 3

(Filtration)
.

A
filtration
is a sequence of
\sigma
-fields
\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
on a common probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
such that

\mathcal{F}_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{1}\subseteq\cdots\subseteq\mathcal{F}\;.

The interpretation is that
\mathcal{F}_{t}
represents all information available up to and including time
t
.

Definition 4

(Supermartingale)
.

A real-valued stochastic process
(M_{t})_{t\geq 0}
adapted to a filtration
\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{t\geqslant 0}
is called a
supermartingale
if it satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|M_{t}|]<\infty\quad\text{for any }t\geqslant 0\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{E}[M_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\leqslant M_{t-1}\ \text{a.s.}

If equality holds,
(M_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
is a
martingale
. Supermartingales describe processes whose conditional expectation cannot increase over time.

Definition 5

(E-process)
.

An e-process (or
test martingale
) is a nonnegative,
\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{t\geqslant 0}
-adapted process
(\tilde{E}_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
satisfying

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\tilde{E}_{t}]\leqslant 1\quad\text{for any }t\geqslant 0\;.

Under the null hypothesis,
(\tilde{E}_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
is typically a nonnegative supermartingale with unit or subunit mean. For any
\gamma\in(0,1)
, the stopping rule “reject at the first

w^{j}_{\boldsymbol{v},r}\leqslant 1/\lVert w^{j}_{\boldsymbol{v}}\rVert_{0}\;,

(66)

since otherwise
\sum_{\ell\in[K]}w^{j}_{\boldsymbol{v},\ell}\geqslant\lVert w^{j}_{\boldsymbol{v}}\rVert_{0}\frac{1}{\lVert w^{j}_{\boldsymbol{v}}\rVert_{0}}>1
. Moreover,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{\bar{{\bf s}}_{\boldsymbol{v}}}(\kappa_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}<\infty)=\mathbb{P}^{\bar{{\bf s}}_{\boldsymbol{v}}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\bigcup_{i\in[N]}\bigcup_{k\geqslant 0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\|\hat{w}^{i}_{k}-w^{i}_{\boldsymbol{v}}\|_{1}>\delta}}\right\}}}\right)\geqslant\mathbb{P}^{\bar{{\bf s}}_{\boldsymbol{v}}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\bigcup_{k\geqslant 0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\|\hat{w}^{j}_{k}-w^{j}_{\boldsymbol{v}}\|_{1}>\delta}}\right\}}}\right)\;.

(67)

Finally, note that for any
k\geqslant 0
,
\cap_{t\in\mathcal{B}_{k}}\{A^{j}_{t}=a_{r}\}\subset\{\|\hat{w}^{j}_{k}-w^{j}_{\boldsymbol{v}}\|_{1}>\delta\}
since under this event

\displaystyle\|\hat{w}^{j}_{k}-w^{j}_{\boldsymbol{v}}\|_{1}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{K}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\hat{w}^{j}_{k,\ell}-w^{j}_{\boldsymbol{v},\ell}}}\right|=1-w^{j}_{\boldsymbol{v},r}\geqslant 1-1/\lVert w^{j}_{\boldsymbol{v}}\rVert_{0}>\delta\;,

where the two last inequalities follows from (
66
) and our assumption respectively. Connecting this observation with (
67
) then gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{\bar{{\bf s}}_{\boldsymbol{v}}}(\kappa<\infty)\geqslant\mathbb{P}^{\bar{{\bf s}}_{\boldsymbol{v}}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\bigcup_{k\geqslant 0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\|\hat{w}^{j}_{k}-w^{j}_{\boldsymbol{v}}\|_{1}>\delta}}\right\}}}\right)

\displaystyle\geqslant\mathbb{P}^{\bar{{\bf s}}_{\boldsymbol{v}}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\bigcup_{k\geqslant 0}\bigcap_{t\in\mathcal{B}_{k}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{A^{j}_{t}=a_{r}}}\right\}}}\right)

\displaystyle=1-\mathbb{P}^{\bar{{\bf s}}_{\boldsymbol{v}}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\bigcap_{k\geqslant 0}\bigcup_{t\in\mathcal{B}_{k}}\{A^{j}_{t}\neq a_{r}\}}}\right)

\displaystyle=1-\prod_{k=0}^{\infty}(1-(w^{j}_{\boldsymbol{v},r})^{L})=1\;,

where we used the independence of draws under
\bar{{\bf s}}_{\boldsymbol{v}}
in the last step.
∎
