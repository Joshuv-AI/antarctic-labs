Deep learning-based RUL and SOH prediction of lithium-ion batteries using LOCO
Authors: Bisma Ali, Irum Matloob, Nuha Alkhalifa, Amal K Alkhalifa, Nayab Asim, Shamsa Kanwal, Saba Inam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDegradation in lithium-ion batteries negatively affects the reliability, safety, and cost-effectiveness of modern energy storage systems, making accurate Remaining Useful Life (RUL) prediction and State of Health (SOH) assessment essential for battery prognostics. In this work, a comprehensive comparative benchmarking framework for battery prognostics is presented using three representative deep learning architectures, namely, Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM), Temporal Convolutional Network (TCN), and Temporal Fusion Transformer (TFT). To provide a fair and reproducible comparison between models and datasets, identical preprocessing, feature engineering, normalization, and training procedures are applied to two publicly available benchmark datasets, namely, the NASA Ames Battery Aging Dataset and the Oxford Battery Degradation Dataset. Using a sliding-window approach, degradation-related features and temporal statistical descriptors are organized as time-series sequences capable of capturing nonlinear degradation behavior. To make a realistic assessment of cross-cell generalization, a strict Leave-One-Cell-Out (LOCO) validation protocol is used, where the models are validated on completely new battery cells. The prediction task is defined as a regression problem for RUL prediction, and the SOH based on the normalized discharge capacity is used to analyze the degradation behavior. Model performance is evaluated by Mean Absolute Error (MAE), Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) and coefficient of determination (R2) and is expressed as mean ± SD standard deviations across repeated random seeds. We differentiate stochastic training effects from variations caused by different LOCO folds by additional statistical analysis with confidence interval estimation, seed-level variability, and evaluation on cell level. The results show that the degradation complexity and cross-cell heterogeneity affect the model performance, which highlights the importance of carefully designed evaluation protocols, statistical reliability analysis, and rigorous cross-cell validation to ensure reliable model benchmarking in lithium-ion batteries’ prognostics.Keywords: Lithium-ion batteries; Remaining useful life (RUL); State of health (SOH); Temporal Convolutional Network (TCN); Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM); Temporal Fusion Transformer (TFT); Leave-One-Cell-Out (LOCO) cross-validation; Battery degradation modeling; Regression metrics (MAE, RMSE, )Subject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Lithium-ion batteries are widely used in electric vehicles, renewable energy storage, smart grids, and consumer electronics due to their high energy density and long lifespan. However, they gradually lose their performance over time due to electrochemical and mechanical aging processes, which lead to capacity degradation, internal resistance, efficiency loss and safety hazards. Reliable prognostics of health indicators of particular importance for safe operation, reliability and predictive maintenance such as Remaining Useful Life (RUL) and State of Health (SOH) are required.

Traditionally, battery prognostics approaches are implemented using physics-based models or empirical degradation models. These methods are easy to understand, but they are based on some assumptions and degradation trends that may not reflect the nonlinear and complex physiology of a real battery environment. Rule-based estimation methods are still widely used in real-world Battery Management Systems (BMS), but are usually not very generalizable to different operating conditions and battery cells, which may limit the prediction accuracy and applicability.

In recent years, using data-driven modelling directly from time-series measurements, in line with recent advances in machine learning and deep learning, has become possible. LSTM, Temporal Convolutional Networks (TCN), and attention-based models like Temporal Fusion Transformer (TFT) have shown great potential for capturing temporal degradation patterns and forecasting battery health metrics. There are some key omissions in the existing literature, however. Many studies use random train-test splits to their detriment which can result in optimistic estimates of performance since the same sample of data from the same battery cell can be used for both training and testing. Evaluations like these do not accurately capture the deployment scenarios where models need to be general and work for all completely unseen battery cells. Second, the use of a single dataset or different preprocessing and training schemes makes it difficult to compare models fairly and to reproduce them.

To overcome these limitations, a unified comparative benchmarking framework for Remaining Useful Life (RUL) prediction and SOH-based degradation analysis is presented in this work following a strict Leave-One-Cell-Out (LOCO) cross validation protocol. The primary contribution of this work is an evaluation pipeline that allows for systematic comparison of the three models (LSTM, TCN, TFT) in the same preprocessing, feature engineering, normalization, and training settings with the same data across the two datasets, the NASA Ames Battery Aging and the Oxford Battery Degradation datasets.

The remaining useful life (RUL) of a battery is the time remaining until it reaches its end-of-life status, and the state of health (SOH) is the capacity of the battery when discharged divided by its initial capacity. Further, degradation behaviour is analysed by using SOH trajectories and RUL prediction analysis is done. All models are tested with several random seeds, and the mean of the mean absolute error (MAE), root mean squared error (RMSE), and coefficient of determination () are reported to provide more experimental reliability. A thorough comparative study is performed to assess the performance of models, ability to generalize them across cells, robustness and stability across data sets of various degradation characteristics.

Literature review

LSTM network propose an (AST-LSTM) consisting of input/forget gates, input/state interactions the element-wise, and peephole connections for predicting SOH and RUL on the NASA dataset. They achieve an RMSE that is very low, although their approach uses features that derive their capacity, and fixed thresholds to determine the end-of-life (EOL)1. The authors propose a framework consisting of two distinct models, DCNN and double-layer LSTM. The first model DCNN extracts features related to the batteries’ aging process from constant current charge data. The outputs from this first model (the aging features) are passed into a double-layer LSTM for RUL prediction. Hyperparameter optimization through the Bayesian approach produces a low RMSE, however the authors only examined single-condition charge profiles2.

Ma et al.’s DTV feature analysis combined with a long-term sequence model utilizes Bayesian optimization and dropout to predict the state-of-health and remaining useful life for a battery at an early stage based on Oxford data, while requiring the use of laboratory conditions for DTV indicator engineering in order to create interpretability3. A physics-based “equivalent circuit simulation” approach is taken by Nguyen and Bae to convert voltage-based features to ohmic resistance (ECS layers) through long-term sequence modeling with LSTM and transfer learning to predict resistance over time with little data, but only predicts resistance-like SoH versus capacity like remaining useful life across battery chemistries4.

Zhou et al, propose the use of dilatated causal convolutions with residual connections to develop a pure TCN architecture for both SOH monitoring and RUL prediction. They also demonstrate that by combining EMD-based de-noising methods, they can effectively separate local regenerative processes from global deteriorative ones, thereby improving the accuracy and robustness of their method over RNNs (including hybrid architectures) using two datasets, however, limited to modelling univariate capacity trajectories to date5. Li et al, have extended this work further by developing a TCN-LSTM hybrid with the use of Bayesian method to optimize its performance, which show improved accuracy in SOH as compared to CNN-LSTM, TCN, and LSTM on datasets from NASA and Oxford, while the performance of RUL depend on dataset, indicating architecture sensitivity to data characteristics6.

Zraibi et al, have proposed to create a CNN-LSTM-DNN hybrid), by using a CNN to extract capacity patterns locally, an LSTM to model the temporal evolution and a DNN to refine the RUL estimates. Evaluation on NASA and CALCE show lower MAE/RMSE values and longer runtimes compared to any single model, however direct RUL prediction only is possible7. Li et. al, have created a dual attention, sequence to sequence (encoder-decoder) TCN - GRU - DNN for RUL prediction using dual feature and temporal attention. Evaluation on NASA and CALCE data show RMSE values of approx. 2.4% and 0.9%, respectively. However the model is limited in that only RULs are predicted, and depends on clean capacity history8.

Chen et al, created a Transformer-based RUL estimator that uses a denoising autoencoder (DAE) before the Transformer to assist in reducing noise in the raw capacity values. The Transformer adds to RUL by considering and modeling long-term dependencies between degradation events. The Transformer model outperform RNN arch frameworks, but only on RUL and capacity based data9. Pan et al, added a transfer-learning based LSTM model and particle filtering into the model. The TL model transfers information from the LSTM and uses historical data at different levels of stress to predict capacity over long periods of time. The PF model adds an estimation of uncertainty to the TL model. The results of the models predicted <5% error at 50% of the data, although the authors only modeled RUL and used no mechanistic priors to develop the model10.

Li et al. reviewed 380 ML based studies for RUL dates; they identified the RNN model family (LSTM and GRU) as being the most common RUL algorithms and summarized the major themes of their findings as signal processing, algorithm fusing, transfer learning and physical-fusion of information. The authors noted many open areas for future research such as early prediction of the failure date of components, regeneration modeling and creating generalized transfer learning11.

Zhu et al. and Zhao et al. developed a hybrid CNN-BiLSTM-attention system for estimating the capacity of lithium-ion batteries and subsequently predicting State of Health (SoH) and Remaining Useful Life (RUL). The CNN portion of the network captures nonlinear interactions between features such as voltage, current, and temperature, or CALCE health indicators. The BiLSTM portion of the network learns temporal dependencies in both directions. The attention portion of the network highlights the cyclical nature of the data to improve the accuracy and robustness of predictions; however, they both still rely on using offline cyclical data and indirect indicators to show degradation12,13. Chen et al. created a multi-input-channel CNN-BiLSTM-GRU network with attention, which was preceded by removing outliers from the datasets and performing feature correlation analysis. Ablation experiments confirmed the multi-channel network would provide improved accuracy in predicting SoH and RUL as well as being more generalizable. The multi-input multi-channel network uses a large amount of computational resources due to its large number of parameters14.

Qu et al. introduce an LSTM network with particle swarm optimization and effect by using Attention mechanism, and combined with CEEMDAN (Complete Ensemble Empirical Mode Decomposition with Adaptive Noise) denoising techniques, produced more accurate RUL and SoH predictions than traditional methods. Their implementation uses NASA datasets, but they are generally restricted to using the same data source multiple times15. Wang et al. created a CNN-LSTM (multi-head attention) framework using random discharge voltage current fragments, which are found in both NASA’s and experimental databases, to jointly predict SOH and RUL without requiring sophisticated feature engineering. The authors achieved RMSE results; however, this relies on fragment representativeness and degradation pattern stationarity; that is unstated16.

Zhong et al. created a dual representation transferable knowledge sharing network, otherwise known as a multi-task transfer learning network, to jointly predict SOH and RUL. Using 2-dimensional feature representation and having a shared parameter community improves robustness and cross-domain generalizability via complex degradation of technology, but the abstract does not detail whether the model was built using recurrent vs. convolutional vs. transformer architecture17.

Yang et al. incorporated GBLS with a CNN-Transformer (Booster) to apply TPE-based hyperparameter optimization, engineered health indicator computation, RF-based feature selection and CEEMDAN denoising; this combined technology on both NASA and CALCE yields very low MAPE and RE for joint SOH/RUL computation while increasing the difficulty of using these algorithms by introducing enormous pre-processing complexity.

Recent works (2024–2025) have explored Transformer-based and hybrid architectures for battery prognostics; however, many rely on complex preprocessing pipelines or do not evaluate under strict LOCO settings, limiting their applicability to real-world scenarios.

A hybrid deep framework has been developed by Liu et al. which combines LSTM, bidirectional TCN, and bidirectional GRU in order to jointly predict SOH and RUL for vehicle batteries across a range of temperatures and non-standard charge-discharge cycles. Their study used Pearson-selected health indicators along with multi-dimensional temporal-spatial fusion structures which resulted in a MAPE reduction of more than 39% and kept the RUL error to within one cycle. This indicates strong robustness to real-world conditions, but the overall architecture is significantly more complex than other methods18.

Pei et al. have created IVCLNet, which combines ICEEMDAN and VMD decompositions with an attention-based CNN-LSTM (CBAM + LSTM) for creating SOH trajectories as well as RUL predictions (using SOH = 80% threshold) on two publicly available datasets. The proposed solution provides an accurate and robust forecasting method with minimal computational overhead; however, it does depend upon using a decomposed feature pipeline to process the data19.

In recent years, more attention has been paid to improve robustness, uncertainty-awareness and reliability in battery prognostics systems. Zhang et al. proposed a knowledge-guided deep learning framework for battery health estimation with incomplete charging curves showing improved robustness under partial charging information and practical operating constraints20.

Also, recent reliability-oriented studies have investigated Bayesian calibrated physics-informed neural networks for SOH estimation, where uncertainty-aware modeling is incorporated to improve prediction reliability and interpretability under degradation variability21.

Furthermore, Pozzi et al. proposed a neural network-based imitation learning scheme to approximate stochastic BMSs, emphasizing the importance of uncertainty modeling, robustness and deployment-aware control policies in real-world battery prognostics applications22. These recent works point out that accurate RUL and SOH prediction may not be enough for real-world deployment, unless prediction uncertainty, cell-to-cell variability, aging-related parameter drift, and computational requirements are also taken into account.

Many recent works rely on very complex hybrid architectures, probabilistic calibration or specific pre-processing pipelines. In contrast, the present work aims at a controlled and reproducible benchmarking framework under the strict Leave-One-Cell-Out (LOCO) protocol. Thus, the main contribution of this work is not the proposal of a new architecture but the fair comparative evaluation of LSTM, TCN and TFT models on the NASA and Oxford datasets, with the same preprocessing, feature engineering, normalization and training conditions, to evaluate a realistic cross-cell generalization capability.

Research Gap

Recent studies have demonstrated the excellent performance of deep learning techniques for lithium-ion battery prognostics, especially for Remaining Useful Life (RUL) prediction. However, many studies evaluate RUL and State of Health (SOH) separately, random train-test splitting strategies, or complex preprocessing and architecture-specific optimization, which may hinder realistic assessment of cross-cell generalization capability1,2,6,9. Moreover, recent works have also put more emphasis on robustness, uncertainty-awareness and deployment aspects for practical Battery Management Systems (BMS) applications21,22.

On the other hand, the present work adopts a unified and controlled evaluation framework, where the SOH is directly obtained from the capacity degradation and analyzed together with the RUL prediction, thus providing a more consistent interpretation of the battery degradation behavior. Instead of proposing a novel hybrid or highly specialized architecture, in this work we systematically benchmark LSTM, TCN and Temporal Fusion Transformer (TFT) models under a strict Leave-One-Cell-Out (LOCO) validation strategy on the NASA and Oxford datasets. The proposed framework allows for a fair and reproducible comparison of temporal modeling capability and realistic cross-cell generalization performance on previously unseen battery cells by testing all models under the same preprocessing, feature engineering, normalization, and training conditions.

Dataset description

The University of Oxford’s Oxford Battery Degradation Dataset 1 (OBDD1) was published in 2017, and is a popular benchmark data set, with long-term degradation data of eight Kokam SLPB533459H4 lithium-ion pouch cells. The cells were cycled under laboratory conditions until end-of-life, which is defined as a capacity loss of 20% relative to the nominal capacity. The dataset offers fine-scale, cycle-level voltage, current and capacity data, allowing the extraction of degradation-sensitive features such as incremental capacity (dQ/dV) and other indicators of cell health. The dataset is stored in MATLAB (.mat) format and is organized on a cell-by-cell and cycle-by-cycle basis, enabling the direct extraction of time-series degradation patterns for feature extraction and model training. The variability in manufacturing and operational conditions results in different degradation patterns for each cell, making this dataset useful for benchmarking the generalization ability across cells (e.g., Leave-One-Cell-Out (LOCO) cross-validation).

Furthermore, the NASA Prognostics Center of Excellence (PCoE) battery dataset offers run-to-failure data of lithium-ion batteries under different operating conditions, such as discharge currents, ambient temperatures, and load profiles. NASA data set exhibits more variability and non-stationarity in its operation than the OBDD1 data set. The system enables cycle-based measurements of voltage, current, temperature and capacity to conduct detailed investigations into state-of-health (SOH) degradation and remaining useful life (RUL) prediction in realistic conditions.

Both OBDD1 and NASA datasets are used to provide a full assessment and benchmarking of deep learning models under both controlled and variable operating conditions. This approach improves the assessment of the model robustness for SOH and RUL prediction, and allows generalization to new degradation trends and operational scenarios.

Methodology

This paper proposes a data-driven model framework for Remaining Useful Life (RUL) prediction and State of Health (SOH) analysis in the cross-cell generalization under lithium-ion battery. This work is not introducing a new neural network architecture. Still, rather it is a comparison using a common set of preprocessing and evaluation techniques for three models: LSTM, TCN and TFT. The degradation of batteries are modeled as multivariate time-series data and evaluated using a validation strategy called Leave-One-Cell-Out (LOCO) strategy, where several cells are used for training the battery models, while the remaining cells are left out for evaluation. Methodological framework overview

This framework assesses the generalization capabilities of various deep learning frameworks on new battery cells with similar preprocessing and training conditions. The Oxford data set includes measurements taken in detail for each cycle (MATLAB format files), while the NASA data set includes structured degradation trajectories from long-term charge-discharge experiments. The two data sets are converted into homogeneous multivariate time-series format through feature extraction, normalization and temporal sliding window sequence generation.

LSTM, TCN, and TFT are three deep learning architectures independently trained to learn the temporal degradation patterns. Normalized discharge capacity is used to calculate SOH and analyze the behavior of degradation, and RUL is used as the main prediction object. A LOCO validation strategy is used for the assessment of the robustness of the cross-cells, while the rest of the cells are used for training.

Battery cell structure and cycle representation

The Oxford Battery Degradation Dataset consists of eight Kokam Li-ion cells (Cell1–Cell8) cycled under CC-CV charging and dynamic discharge at around 40C. Different cells have different numbers of degradation cycles (approx. 49–83 cycles) giving rise to variable degradation.

NASA PCoE Battery Dataset contains four batteries labeled B0005, B0006, B0007 and B0018, which were charged using CC-CV and discharged using CC at approximately 24C. Each battery is run until about 70% capacity retention, causing a run to failure degradation path for each battery.

The relatively structured NASA degradation trajectories together with more variability in the Oxford dataset allows for a complete evaluation of model robustness and generalisation across cells in different battery ageing scenarios.

Data preprocessing

Pre-processing steps for both datasets consist of normalization of cycle indexes, smoothing of the capacity signals using a rolling filter and extraction of features related to degradation from capacity trajectories. Normalized cycle index, capacity difference between consecutive cycles, rolling mean capacity, rolling standard deviation and degradation-rate features based on a 5-cycle rolling window are extracted.

Normalization happens separately for each of the LOCO folds and will not allow data leakage, since only the training cells will be used for normalization. Both datasets are then subjected to Min-Max normalization, which ensures they share the same feature scaling and allows for fair comparisons.

For supervised temporal degradation modelling and RUL prediction, the multivariate degradation trajectories are transformed into fixed-length sliding window sequences having 30 cycles window size. The percentage of SOH is the percentage of the current discharge capacity to the initial discharge capacity, and the RUL is the remaining number of cycles until the end-of-life. This yields sequences, which are then used as input to the deep learning battery prognostics modeling. The Weighted Smooth L1 (Huber) Loss function is employed when it comes to model training to guarantee optimization is consistent across all architectures, because of its robustness to large prediction differences and noisy degradation behavior.

Feature extraction

Cycle-level features are taken out to analyze the battery degradation dynamics. The most important is discharge capacity (q), which directly is related to degradation at each cycle. Cycle index is normalized by the number of cycles to represent relative progress in battery life and differences in lengths of the cycle sequences across cells.

Additional features are obtained from capacity evolution to capture the temporal degradation behavior. These can be first-order capacity differences (Δ q) for short-term variations, a 5 cycle rolling mean to smooth fluctuations and give a sense of long-term degradation trends, and a rolling standard deviation to quantify local variability. A smoothed degradation rate feature is also calculated, to represent how fast the capacity decreases with time.

Two feature configurations are defined throughout both data sets: a light configuration of discharge capacity and cycle index and a full configuration of all the features derived from the degradation. To avoid data leakage and provide a fair comparison of the efficiency of the LSTM, TCN and TFT models, all features are normalized using Min-Max normalization, which is fitted only over the training cells in each Leave-One-Cell-Out (LOCO) validation fold.

Sliding window generation

A sliding window approach is used to transform cycle-wise degradation data into supervised learning samples for both the Oxford Battery Degradation dataset and the NASA PCoE dataset. Overlapping multivariate sequences with temporal degradation dependencies are generated by fixed-length windows of 30 cycles (lookback = 30) and stride 1.

The features of each window include multivariate aspects such as discharge capacity, normalized cycle index, capacity difference, rolling mean, rolling standard deviation, and degradation-rate features. Target variable is remaining useful life (RUL) expressed in hours, and state of health (SOH) is calculated without relying on normalized discharge capacity for analysis and interpretation of the degradation.

The window length of 30 cycles is chosen as a balance between the number of cycles to be used for capturing a representative long-term degradation behavior and the number of cycles to be used in the leave one cell out (LOCO) setting for validation. For stable degradation learning, smaller window sizes (e.g., 20 cycles) yield less temporal context, while larger window sizes (e.g., 40 cycles) result in fewer training samples and potentially less sensitivity to short-term variations in degradation. The configuration is the same in all models and is used to enable fair and repeatable benchmarking.

Temporal Convolutional Network (TCN)

A Temporal Convolutional Network (TCN) is used for modeling lithium-ion battery degradation trajectories for Leave-One-Cell-Out (LOCO) validation on the Oxford Battery Degradation dataset and the NASA PCoE dataset. All input features are normalized using Min-Max scaling, where the normalization parameters are only calculated based on the training cells in each LOCO fold, to prevent data leakage.

A fixed-length input sequence of 30 cycles is generated by a sliding window method; a model is trained to predict the Remaining Useful Life (RUL in hours) of the input sequence. State of Health (SOH) is the normalized discharge capacity measurement, and it is used to describe the degradation behavior and to analyze the battery health evolution through cycles.

To find multi-scale temporal dependency in degradation trajectories, the TCN is used, which is built using dilated causal convolutions. To enhance gradient flow and to stabilize training, residual connections are added. TCNs are superior in terms of parallelization and efficient long-range temporal pattern modeling of non-stationary battery degradation signals compared to recurrent architectures.

The network is trained with a weighted Smooth L1 (Huber) loss function with the Adam optimizer, with the same preprocessing and training conditions applied to all models studied to provide a fair and fair comparison according to the LOCO framework.

Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM)

A stacked Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network is used to model sequential degradation behavior in lithium-ion batteries under the Leave-One-Cell-Out (LOCO) validation framework. Input features are normalized using the min-max method. The normalization parameters are calculated using the training cells data in each fold avoiding data leakage. Preserving temporal degradation dependencies, 30-cycle windows are created using a sliding window approach.

The LSTM model contains two layers with 128 hidden units each to learn the degradation behavior of the battery over time from multi-modal features. The last hidden representation is passed through a fully connected layer to estimate the Remaining Useful Life (RUL, in hours).

The State of Health (SOH) is calculated from normalized discharge capacity measurements to assess degradation characteristics and to help analyze the battery degradation evolution over the cycle.

The model is trained with a weighted Smooth L1 (Huber) loss and Adam optimizer. All the models are trained with the same pre-processing pipeline, sequence length, and training strategy, which guarantees a fair comparison and reproducible evaluations under LOCO.

Temporal Fusion Transformer (TFT)

Temporal fusion transformer (TFT) is used for RUL prediction and SOH analysis in the Oxford Battery Degradation (OBD) and NASA PCoE datasets with the Leave-One-Cell-Out (LOCO) validation framework. Input features are scaled using Min-Max normalization, with the scaling parameters calculated just from the training cells of each fold for the avoidance of data leakage. All models that are evaluated have the same length of sequences: 30 cycles.

The core concept behind the TFT is to capture the short-term and long-term temporal dependencies by employing variable selection networks, gated residual connections, recurrent encoding layers, and multi-head attention mechanisms to learn from the battery degradation trajectory. This setup provides adaptive weighting of input features in time and enables modelling of the degradation heterogeneity of the various battery cells.

The model is trained in reverse, hour-wise prediction of Remaining Useful Life (RUL in hours). This is a process of normalizing the discharge capacity data and is expressed as the State of Health (SOH) to record the degradation trend and analyze the evolution of battery health over cycles.

A weighted Smooth L1 (Huber) loss function is used for training with the Adam optimizer. The processing and normalization of data, the way sequences are generated, and the evaluation settings are maintained the same so that all models can be benchmarked fairly, in a controlled and reproducible manner in the LOCO framework.

Model architecture

Three architectures are used to develop the deep learning models for remaining useful life (RUL) and SOH estimation: Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM), Temporal Convolutional Networks (TCN), and Temporal Fusion Transformers (TFT). The NASA and Oxford battery datasets are used to train all models in a common Leave-One-Cell-Out (LOCO) cross validation framework, with the batteries having different degradation behaviors.

All models use the same fixed length window (30 cycles) when creating their input sequences for consistent and fair comparison across architectures. Each model is then fed a multivariate sequence with a shape of 30 × F (with F being a feature dimension). The prediction task is formulated as a cycle-wise regression task, and the output of the prediction task is the remaining useful life (RUL in hours) of the next cycle. The state of health (SOH) is extracted from the measurement of the discharge capacity. It is used to analyze degradation trends and to help in the interpretation of the aging behavior of batteries.

All models were trained up to 120 epochs based on convergence in each LOCO fold, using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of . The Stable L1 (Huber) loss is extended to be less sensitive to noise and outliers by a cycle-based weighting scheme, which assigns more weight to the degradation stages in the latter part of the life.

Loss function and training strategy

All models (TCN, LSTM, and TFT) are trained under the Leave-One-Cell-Out (LOCO) validation framework using both the NASA PCoE Battery dataset and the Oxford Battery Degradation dataset. A battery cell is set aside for test in each LOCO fold, and the rest of the cells in the fold are used for training and validation. The remaining training cells are then further partitioned into a validation subset to assess training convergence and model selection for each LOCO fold. The input features are always normed using Min-Max norming which normalizes the features using only the features of the training cells of each fold to avoid data leakage.

A loss function with weights, Smooth L1 (Huber) loss is used to enhance the robustness of the model to the presence of outliers and abrupt degradation variations, especially in the late stage of battery ageing. The models are designed to predict the Remaining Useful Life (RUL in hours), and State of Health (SOH) is obtained from normalized discharge capacity measurements to capture the degradation behaviour and aid the analysis of battery health evolution over cycles.

The input features consist of degradation descriptors based on capacity, e.g. , , and , that are captured for both datasets on a cycle-by-cycle basis.

The Adam optimizer is used to train for 120 epochs with a learning rate of , and a batch size of 32. The length of the sliding window is always 30 cycles, so that temporal degradation dependencies are the same for all models under the same experimental conditions. Fixed random seeds are used in all experiments, to ensure reproducibility and fair benchmarking.

Evaluation metrics

Mean Absolute Error (MAE), Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) and Coefficient of Determination () are used to assess the performance of the model. Leave One Cell Out (LOCO) validation is the method used where each battery cell is tested separately, and the rest of the cells are used for training and validation. Final performance metrics are presented as average standard deviation, based on all LOCO folds and multiple trials with random seed.

The accuracy of the Remaining Useful Life (RUL) prediction and capability of the State of Health (SOH) degradation trajectories to capture the battery degradation behaviour for different cells are assessed.

Residual plots are also used to visually examine errors in predictions throughout the battery cycle, in addition to quantitative measures. These analyses are useful for determining model behavior at late stage degradation, where high uncertainty and high degradation variability are common.

Implementation details

All models are trained and tested on GPU hardware if available or CPU otherwise using PyTorch. The normalized parameters for feature scaling are determined only on cells in the training fold of the LOCO region of the dataset to prevent data leakage and to guarantee the process evaluation can be reproduced.

The training samples are prepared by sliding windows with a fixed look-back size of 30 cycles with the next time step used as the target of prediction. In the case of Temporal Fusion Transformer (TFT), the components of architecture such as Gated Residual Networks (GRNs), recurrent encoding layers and multi-head attention mechanisms are effectively used to model temporal degradation dependency.

For fair and controlled benchmarking, the same models (TCN, LSTM and TFT) are benchmarked through the same preprocessing, normalization, sequence generation, optimization and evaluation procedures.

LOCO validation strategy and its motivation

The Leave-One-Cell-Out (LOCO) validation scheme is used to assess generalization performance of the deep learning models to unseen lithium-ion battery cells. One battery cell is kept aside for testing in each LOCO fold and the remaining cells are used for training and validation. This is an approach that avoids information leakage between training and testing cells, and allows to evaluate in realistic cross-cell generalization conditions.

The LOCO framework is crucial for battery prognostics, as degradation behavior can differ significantly among cells as a result of varying conditions under which they are operated and the differences in their manufacturing, aging properties, and degradation mechanisms. A model evaluation on unseen cells can thus offer a more realistic estimate of the robustness of a model and the ability to put it into practice, rather than random sample-level splitting schemes.

Both NASA PCoE Battery dataset and Oxford Battery Degradation dataset are used with the LOCO approach to assess prediction of Remaining Useful Life (RUL) and State of Health (SOH) analysis for heterogeneous degradation conditions and limited-cell experimental environment.

Discussion

This paper benchmarks the LSTM, TCN, and TFT architectures to estimate the Remaining Useful Life (RUL) and State of Health (SOH) of Lithium-Ion Batteries. Uses a consistent Leave-One-Cell-Out (LOCO) evaluation framework for both NASA PCoE and Oxford Battery Degradation datasets. This work is directed towards achieving a fair and repeatable benchmarking framework, in contrast to most recent works that have focused on the creation of more sophisticated hybrid or decomposition based models. The same preprocessing, normalization and feature extraction and training conditions were used for all models. The LOCO protocol is evaluated for generalization to fully unseen cells, to further strengthen the evaluation. This approach is more in line with real usage scenarios than a random train/test split.

The experimental results indicate that the performance of the model is greatly influenced by the characteristics of the data set and its degradation behavior over time. The best performance in the RUL prediction was obtained for the NASA dataset (MAE = 16.30 ± 0.57 h, RMSE = 18.55 ± 0.63 h, = 0.60 ± 0.03), showing that the TCN model is able to capture relatively smooth degradation patterns with limited cells. A comparison shows that the TFT model obtains superior performance on the Oxford dataset (MAE = 3.36 ± 0.15 h, RMSE = 4.27 ± 0.15 h, = 0.85 ± 0.01), suggesting that attention-based temporal representations are better suited to handle the heterogeneity in degradation dynamics and complex operational variations. The LSTM model produced less consistent and lower results on both datasets, especially on the more challenging LOCO validation setting.

All the models reflected the overall monotonic degradation trend for SOH estimation. But, the trajectories of SOH obtained from the NASA dataset were smoother and stable for TCN. TFT had more accurate tracking results on the Oxford data set, meanwhile. The ablation analysis also reveals that the degradation-related features make a substantial contribution to the predictability of the model and stability of the model, particularly for the TCN and TFT architecture.

The findings are similar to recent studies which found that advanced temporal learning algorithms can improve battery prognostics in complex degradation environments. There has been a recent rise of attention mechanisms, uncertainty-aware frameworks and learning strategies that can be used to make the model more robust to boost the reliability and generalization capability20,21. Moreover, in addition to prediction-accuracy metrics, practical BMS should take into account the stochastic operating behavior, uncertainty propagation and deployment constraints, as pointed out by Pozzi et al.22. Unlike these studies the current work focuses on controlled benchmarking and realistic cross-cell evaluation for the same experimental conditions, resulting in a better understanding of the behaviour of the models and their generalisation capabilities.

While the results are promising, some limitations should be recognized. Firstly, both the NASA and Oxford datasets were collected in controlled lab conditions and may not fully reflect real world conditions. In such conditions, batteries are exposed to various temperature levels, irregular charging patterns, sensor noise and varying load conditions. Second, the number of battery cells is limited and thus the available range of degradation patterns for model training is limited. This may impact the model performance with unexpected aging behaviors. Third, mechanisms for uncertainty quantification and online adaptation are not included in the current framework. Both are essential for the reliable deployment of battery management systems. Finally, this work did not consider computational efficiency or deployment-related aspects such as inference latency and memory requirements. These factors should be investigated in future research.

Overall, the proposed LOCO-based benchmark framework provides a realistic and reproducible evaluation of the cross-cell generalization in lithium-ion battery prognostics. It also provides valuable insights on the strengths and weaknesses of LSTM, TCN and TFT architectures.

Limitations

Leave-One-Cell-Out (LOCO) validation is a realistic protocol for assessing cross-cell generalization but has a downside of limited battery cell data in the datasets used. The NASA dataset has only 4 cells, and the Oxford has 8 which limits the number of independent test folds and makes the dataset more sensitive to the behavior of individual cells. These small numbers can decrease the statistical validity of the evaluation and cause greater variation in the performance estimate, especially if the degradation rate varies significantly from cell to cell. Therefore the atypical aging characteristic of cells can have a significant impact on overall conclusions.

Moreover, the data set size is limited, which might result in less variety of degradation patterns, operational and aging conditions, and make data-driven models less generalizable under LOCO settings where there is no information available in the target cell during the data training. Consequently, while LOCO offers a more robust test of the cross-cell transferability than traditional random splits, it is important to keep in mind that the NASA and Oxford datasets are small scale and controlled when assessing model performance, reproducibility, and applicability to the broader real world.

Comparison with existing techniques

Most of the studies conducted on the NASA and Oxford battery datasets focus on model performance when trained on a limited number of partitions per cell or splits between the train and test sets. These methods typically achieve high prediction accuracy, but they can overestimate the prediction performance for generalization. The reason for this is that degradation trends of a specific group of battery cells can intrude into the training and testing sets. This study adopts a more strict validation procedure called Leave-One-Cell-Out (LOCO) in comparison. In this case, each battery cell is totally left out from training and used for testing only. It makes it easier to assess the generalisation capabilities of the model for new degradation conditions applied to the cells.

Although many recent studies highlight complex hybrid models, feature breakdown processes, or data-specific approaches, this study concentrates on the creation of an unambiguous and repeatable benchmarking system for three important temporal deep learning models: Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM), Temporal Convolutional Network (TCN), and Temporal Fusion Transformer (TFT). The same preprocessing, normalization, feature engineering and training parameters are applied to all models. This helps to compare the ability of the architectures to model time.

Furthermore, the Remaining Useful Life (RUL) prediction and State of Health (SOH) estimation are evaluated from the same datasets, which can be used to offer a more comprehensive evaluation of battery prognostics performance. The experimental results show that TCN performs better on the NASA dataset (MAE = 16.30 ± 0.57 hours, RMSE = 18.55 ± 0.63 hours, = 0.60 ± 0.03), which indicates its capability to learn relatively structured degradation trajectories under limited-data conditions. On the other hand, TFT achieves better performances on the Oxford dataset (MAE = 3.36 ± 0.15 hours, RMSE = 4.27 ± 0.15 hours, = 0.85 ± 0.01) which suggests that the attention-based temporal modeling is more appropriate for capturing the heterogeneous and complex degradation dynamics. The LSTM model is comparatively poorer and less stable on both datasets in the more stringent LOCO setting. All models are able to describe the desired monotonic SOH degradation process and generate relatively similar SOH trajectory values across the data sets for the capacity-based SOH estimation, which is generally more stable than direct RUL prediction. But TCN is more successful in tracking SOH on the NASA dataset, and TFT is more successful in fitting the trajectory on the Oxford dataset.

Uncertainty-aware and deployment-oriented battery prognostics systems have been the focus of recent studies. Works related to reliability have explored uncertainty calibration, robustness to variations in degradation and practical limits for deployment of Battery Management Systems (BMS)21. In the same way, Pozzi et al. discussed the crucial role of stochastic system modeling and deployment-aware learning strategies in real-life BMS applications22. The present work is compared to these studies as more emphasis is placed on the realistic cross-cell benchmarking and reproducible comparative evaluation than to probabilistic calibration or architectural complexity. However, this too enforces some restrictions on the validation of the models (LOCO) and statistical analysis of them (multi-seed) to improve the robustness assessment and to analyze the model consistency under unseen battery conditions.

The performance is positive, but there are some problems need to be addressed. NASA and Oxford data were taken in a controlled laboratory environment and may not accurately reflect actual operating conditions, such as fluctuating temperatures, charging cycles, sensor noise, and variability of usage. Furthermore, the number of battery cells is small, so there may not be many different paths of degradation to train the model. For future work, larger realistic datasets, uncertainty quantification, computational efficiency, and the constraints of deploying such systems online should be taken into account in practical battery prognostics systems.

Conclusion

This study presents a clear and reproducible framework for predicting Remaining Useful Life (RUL) and analyzing State of Health (SOH) based on battery degradation. It is based on the NASA and Oxford battery degradation datasets and adopts the strict Leave-One-Cell-Out (LOCO) evaluation protocol. The method is more realistic in terms of cross-cell generalization than the traditional random train-test split methods. Rather than proposing a new deep learning model, this work highlights the comparison of three models: Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM), Temporal Convolutional Network (TCN) and Temporal Fusion Transformer (TFT). They are tested in the same preprocessing, feature engineering, normalization and training settings to allow for a fair and reproducible comparison between datasets which degrade differently. As seen from the results, The degradation variability and complexity of the datasets significantly affect the performance of the model. The TCN model works best on the NASA dataset, as it is more successful in modelling structured degradation patterns. The TFT model, however, performs better on the more complex Oxford data set as it is able to model long-range time-dependencies and complex degradation processes. All models yield consistent and continuous degradation trends in terms of capacities for SOH analysis. The estimation of the SOH is more stable than the prediction of the RUL. In summary, the results highlight the importance of using controlled evaluation procedures, experimental conditions that are reproducible to different cells, and realistic cross-cell validation in battery prognostics. They also highlight constraints like small number of cells, limited diversity of datasets, and further studies to address uncertainty-aware modeling, statistical robustness, and real-world implementation of battery management systems.

Future work

In future, this study can be enhanced by utilizing a larger and more diverse set of batteries, incorporating information on the operational and environmental conditions, including temperature and load conditions, to improve prediction accuracy in real-world applications. A model that leverages a physics-informed or a hybrid approach, combining electrochemical knowledge with data-driven learning, can contribute to the clarification and reliability of the model. Furthermore, new architectures based on Transformer networks and 1D convolutional autoencoders (1D-CAE) may be investigated to make more stable prediction and detect problems earlier. The use of RBS with real-time update of RUL and SOH and quantitative failure analysis could be useful for the large-scale application, such as EV fleets.
