Persistence, patience and costly information acquisition
Benjamin Davies

Abstract
A forward-looking agent observes signals of a state that follows a Gaussian AR(1) process. He chooses the signals’ precisions sequentially, balancing their marginal cost and informativeness. I characterize his optimal learning strategy, and analyze his steady-state posterior beliefs and welfare. Higher persistence can tighten or loosen these beliefs, but always lowers welfare due to endogenously higher information costs. In contrast, higher patience raises welfare because the agent receives more information from his past selves. Keywords : AR(1) process, Bayesian learning, information acquisition, patience, persistence JEL classification : C61, D83

Introduction

This paper studies optimal learning about a time-varying state.
A forward-looking Bayesian agent observes signals of a payoff-relevant state that follows a Gaussian AR(1) process.
He chooses the signals’ precisions sequentially, balancing their marginal costs and informativeness.
I characterize the optimal learning strategy and analyze its steady state.

My headline result (Proposition
1
) says that persistence has a non-monotonic effect on steady-state beliefs.
Higher persistence has two opposing effects:

1.

it makes shocks propagate further into the future, loosening the agent’s beliefs;

2.

it raises the future value of information, so the agent acquires more and tightens his beliefs.

I show that the first effect dominates the second if and only if persistence is high enough.
However, higher persistence always lowers the agent’s steady-state welfare (Proposition
4
).
This is because the additional information cost outweighs any reduction in posterior variance.
The opposite is true when his discount factor (i.e., patience) rises—although he acquires more information, so do his past selves, and this tightens his beliefs enough to raise his welfare overall.

I obtain these results in a tractable model with Gaussian signals, quadratic payoffs, and linear signal precision costs.
My “Gaussian-quadratic” setup is standard in the information acquisition literature
(
Colombo et al., 2014
;
Hellwig and Veldkamp, 2009
;
Myatt and Wallace, 2012
)
.
Linear precision costs distinguish my model from the rational inattention literature, which typically assumes entropy-based costs
(
Maćkowiak and Wiederholt, 2009
;
Sims, 2003
)
.
Such costs complicate dynamic analyses because each signal changes future priors and, thus, future costs.
Linear costs are prior-independent and allow me to characterize the optimal learning strategy in closed-form.

Part (i) of Proposition

5
Conclusion

This paper studies optimal learning about a time-varying state.
I assume the state follows a Gaussian AR(1) process, and that a Bayesian agent observes Gaussian signals and takes actions with quadratic state-dependent payoffs.
These assumptions make my model tractable and yield simple expressions for the optimal sequential learning strategy.
I characterize this strategy and analyze its steady state.
Higher persistence can tighten or loosen the agent’s steady-state beliefs, but always lowers his steady-state welfare.
In contrast, higher patience raises his welfare because he receives more information from his past selves.

My model speaks to a range of empirical settings.
Portfolio managers and central banks who track time-varying fundamentals (e.g., inflation and exchange rates) face exactly the information value-cost trade-off that I study.
My non-monotonicity result (Proposition
1
) implies information should be acquired most intensively for fundamentals with intermediate persistence.
Consumers choosing how much news to read, firms conducting market research, and laboratories testing vaccines for mutating viruses must all answer the question I ask in this paper: how should one learn about a changing world?

Natural extensions of my model are to non-Gaussian processes, non-quadratic payoffs, and endogenous signal timing.
One could also consider agents with mis-specified beliefs about how the state evolves.
I defer these extensions to future research.

(A.1)

, and

To express the right-hand side of (

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(\sigma^{2}/\rho^{2})^{2}}-\frac{\delta\rho^{2}}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rho^{2}(\sigma^{2}/\rho^{2})+\sigma^{2}}}\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(4\rho^{2}-\delta)\rho^{2}}{4\sigma^{4}}.

Rearranging this equation yields a quadratic

in

The negative root is infeasible; rejecting it yields the result.
∎

A.7

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}C^{*}}{\mathrm{d}\rho}
