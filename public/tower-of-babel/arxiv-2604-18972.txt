Beyond Bellman: High-Order Generator Regression for Continuous-Time Policy Evaluation
Yaowei Zheng, Richong Zhang, Shenxi Wu, Shirui Bian, Haosong Zhang, Li Zeng, Xingjian Ma, Yichi Zhang

Abstract
We study finite-horizon continuous-time policy evaluation from discrete closed-loop trajectories under time-inhomogeneous dynamic. The target value surface solves a backward parabolic equation, but the Bellman baseline obtained from one-step recursion is only first-order in the grid width. We estimate the time-dependent generator from multi-step transitions using moment-matching coefficients that cancel lower-order truncation terms, and combine the resulting surrogate with backward regression. The main theory gives an end-to-end decomposition into generator misspecification, projection error, pooling bias, finite-sample error, and start-up error, together with a decision-frequency regime map explaining when higher-order gains should be visible. Across calibration studies, four-scale benchmarks, feature and start-up ablations, and gain-mismatch stress tests, the second-order estimator consistently improves on the Bellman baseline and remains stable in the regime where the theory predicts visible gains. These results position high-order generator regression as an interpretable continuous-time policy-evaluation method with a clear operating region.



Key words:
closed-loop diffusion; continuous-time policy evaluation; generator regression; temporal pooling; time-varying dynamic.

1. Introduction

Continuous-time policy evaluation from logged trajectories is a basic problem in reinforcement learning, stochastic control, and simulation-based dynamic programming. In the finite-horizon closed-loop setting, the target value surface is characterized by a backward parabolic equation, while the data are observed only on a discrete time grid. The natural statistical comparator is therefore the Bellman baseline: the backward value recursion obtained by combining the continuous-time value equation with a single forward time step. It uses the same logged-trajectory information class as the estimator studied here, and it isolates the exact question this paper asks—whether a higher-order construction buys anything beyond a standard backward update
(
2
;
26
;
28
;
36
;
5
;
29
)
. Its limitation is equally direct. Because it hard-wires a one-step difference into the backward solver, it remains first-order in the grid width.

This paper studies that first-order bottleneck for finite-horizon, time-inhomogeneous, continuous-time policy evaluation from discrete closed-loop trajectories. The logged data are generated by a fixed target controller with exploration noise, so the controller is absorbed into the drift and diffusion and the task is evaluation for one time-varying closed-loop system. Within that setting, we replace the Bellman baseline’s one-step discretization by high-order generator regression built from multi-step moment matching. The resulting estimator uses the same trajectory information class as the Bellman baseline, but its multistep coefficients cancel lower-order truncation terms in the space-time generator expansion.

The strongest statement of the paper is not merely that a high-order mechanism exists. It is that the mechanism has a describable operating region. A high-order generator surrogate reduces the leading discretization error from order
O(\Delta t)
to order
O(\Delta t^{i})
, but the realized gain is filtered through projection quality, temporal pooling bias, finite-sample fluctuation, and start-up control. The main theoretical task is therefore to connect the local generator expansion, the pooled regression analysis, and the backward recursion into one end-to-end theorem with explicit constants. The main empirical task is then to determine where the predicted gain is visible and where it disappears.

The paper makes three contributions.

for the corresponding state-space norm and
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}

2. Setup and Main Results

2.1. Setup

Let
(s_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
be a time-inhomogeneous diffusion on state space
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\mathrm{d}s_{t}=\mu(s_{t},t)\,\mathrm{d}t+\sigma(s_{t},t)\,\mathrm{d}W_{t},\qquad\Sigma(s,t):=\sigma(s,t)\sigma(s,t)^{\top},

(2.1)

where
W_{t}
is an
m
-dimensional Brownian motion. The controller is fixed and absorbed into
(\mu,\sigma)
, so the task is evaluation for one closed-loop system rather than policy search over actions. Given running reward
r:\mathcal{S}\times[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}
, terminal payoff
h:\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}
, and discount rate
\beta>0
, define the finite-horizon value function

V(s,t):=\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\int_{t}^{T}e^{-\beta(\tau-t)}r(s_{\tau},\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau+e^{-\beta(T-t)}h(s_{T})\,\Big|\,s_{t}=s\Bigg].

(2.2)

Under standard regularity conditions,
V
solves the backward parabolic equation

\partial_{t}V(s,t)+\mathcal{L}_{\mu,\Sigma}V(s,t)=\beta V(s,t)-r(s,t),\qquad V(s,T)=h(s),

(2.3)

with generator

(\mathcal{L}_{\mu,\Sigma}f)(s,t):=\mu(s,t)^{\top}\nabla_{s}f(s,t)+\tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{tr}\big(\Sigma(s,t)\nabla_{s}^{2}f(s,t)\big).

(2.4)

Fix a time step
\Delta t>0
and grid points
t_{n}=n\Delta t
. The direct discrete analogue of (
2.2
) is the Bellman baseline operator

(\mathcal{B}_{\Delta t}U)(s,t_{n}):=\mathbb{E}\Big[r(s_{t_{n}},t_{n})\Delta t+e^{-\beta\Delta t}U(s_{t_{n+1}},t_{n+1})\,\big|\,s_{t_{n}}=s\Big],

(2.5)

which induces the one-step backward recursion
\tilde{V}=\mathcal{B}_{\Delta t}\tilde{V}
on the grid. It is the natural baseline because it uses the same logged trajectories as the proposed estimator and differs from the continuous-time value equation only through the one-step time discretization.

The local reason for its first-order behavior is immediate. For any smooth test function
U
, Itô’s formula and the space-time semigroup expansion imply

\mathbb{E}\big[U(s_{t+j\Delta t},t+j\Delta t)\mid s_{t}=s\big]=\sum_{k=0}^{2}\frac{(j\Delta t)^{k}}{k!}(\partial_{t}+\mathcal{L}_{\mu,\Sigma})^{k}U(s,t)+O(\Delta t^{3}),\qquad j\in\{0,1\}.

(2.6)

Substituting
U=V
and using (
2.3
) gives
(\mathcal{B}_{\Delta t}V)(s,t)=V(s,t)+O(\Delta t^{2})
. The local defect is thus second-order, but the backward recursion is iterated over
O(T/\Delta t)
steps, so the accumulated global error is only first-order. The next proposition states this explicitly and records the exact leading constant.

Proposition 2.1

(The Bellman baseline is first-order)
.

Assume
r
is bounded and sufficiently smooth so that
\left\lVert r\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}
,
\left\lVert\mathcal{L}_{\mu,\Sigma}r\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}
, and
\left\lVert\partial_{t}r\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}
are finite. Then, as
\Delta t\to 0
,

\left\lVert V(\cdot,t)-\tilde{V}(\cdot,t)\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\leq\frac{T-t}{2}\Big(\left\lVert\mathcal{L}_{\mu,\Sigma}r\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}+\left\lVert\partial_{t}r\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}+\beta\left\lVert r\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\Big)\Delta t+o(\Delta t).

(2.7)

The derivation is short enough to matter. Write
f(s^{\prime},\tau):=e^{-\beta(\tau-t)}r(s^{\prime},\tau)
and
g(\tau):=\mathbb{E}[f(s_{\tau},\tau)\mid s_{t}=s]
. Then (
2.2
) is
\int_{t}^{T}g(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau
, while the Bellman baseline replaces that integral with the left-Riemann sum
\sum_{j}g(t+j\Delta t)\Delta t
. The left-Riemann error is
\tfrac{1}{2}(T-t)\Delta t\sup_{\tau}|g^{\prime}(\tau)|+o(\Delta t)
. Differentiating under the integral and using the forward equation for the transition density gives

g^{\prime}(\tau)=\mathbb{E}\big[e^{-\beta(\tau-t)}(\partial_{t}r-\beta r+\mathcal{L}_{\mu,\Sigma}r)(s_{\tau},\tau)\mid s_{t}=s\big],

which yields (
2.7
). The Bellman baseline is therefore the right statistical baseline, but it carries an unavoidable first-order discretization layer.

The high-order construction replaces that one-step layer by a multistep approximation to the space-time generator

\mathcal{G}:=\partial_{t}+\mathcal{L}_{\mu,\Sigma}.

For a smooth test function
U
, repeated Dynkin expansion gives, for each integer
j\geq 1
,

\mathbb{E}\big[U(s_{t+j\Delta t},t+j\Delta t)\mid s_{t}=s\big]=\sum_{k=0}^{i}\frac{(j\Delta t)^{k}}{k!}(\mathcal{G}^{k}U)(s,t)+O(\Delta t^{i+1}).

(2.8)

Choose coefficients
a^{(i)}=(a_{0}^{(i)},\dots,a_{i}^{(i)})
so that

\sum_{j=0}^{i}a_{j}^{(i)}j^{k}=\begin{cases}1,&k=1,\\
0,&k\in\{0,2,3,\dots,i\}.\end{cases}

(2.9)

The weighted combination of (
2.8
) then yields

\frac{1}{\Delta t}\sum_{j=0}^{i}a_{j}^{(i)}\mathbb{E}\big[U(s_{t+j\Delta t},t+j\Delta t)\mid s_{t}=s\big]=(\mathcal{G}U)(s,t)+\frac{\Delta t^{i}}{(i+1)!}\Big(\sum_{j=0}^{i}a_{j}^{(i)}j^{i+1}\Big)(\mathcal{G}^{i+1}U)(s,t)+O(\Delta t^{i+1}).

(2.10)

The first practical instances are the familiar one-sided stencils

a^{(1)}=(-1,1),\qquad a^{(2)}=\Big(-\tfrac{3}{2},2,-\tfrac{1}{2}\Big),\qquad a^{(3)}=\Big(-\tfrac{11}{6},3,-\tfrac{3}{2},\tfrac{1}{3}\Big).

The Bellman baseline is exactly the
i=1
member of this family. Gen2 and Gen3 are the
i=2
and
i=3
members applied to the generator instead of directly to the value recursion.

Using the same coefficients, define the order-
i
drift and diffusion surrogates

\displaystyle\hat{\mu}_{i}(s,t)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{\Delta t}\sum_{j=1}^{i}\mathbb{E}\!\left[a_{j}^{(i)}(s_{t+j\Delta t}-s_{t})\,\middle|\,s_{t}=s\right],

(2.11)

\displaystyle\hat{\Sigma}_{i}(s,t)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{\Delta t}\sum_{j=1}^{i}\mathbb{E}\!\left[a_{j}^{(i)}(s_{t+j\Delta t}-s_{t})(s_{t+j\Delta t}-s_{t})^{\top}\,\middle|\,s_{t}=s\right].

(2.12)

The corresponding surrogate value function
\hat{V}_{i}
solves

\partial_{t}\hat{V}_{i}(s,t)+\mathcal{L}_{\hat{\mu}_{i},\hat{\Sigma}_{i}}\hat{V}_{i}(s,t)=\beta\hat{V}_{i}(s,t)-r(s,t),\qquad\hat{V}_{i}(s,T)=h(s).

(2.13)

The rest of the paper studies how much of the
O(\Delta t^{i})
population improvement survives after feature approximation, temporal pooling, and finite-sample regression are introduced.

2.2. Main Results

Assumption 2.1

(Ellipticity and smoothness)
.

There exist constants
0<\lambda_{\min}\leq\lambda_{\max}<\infty
such that

\lambda_{\min}I_{d}\preceq\Sigma(s,t)\preceq\lambda_{\max}I_{d}
for all
(s,t)\in\mathcal{S}\times[0,T]
. Moreover,
\mu
,
\Sigma
, and
r
are
C^{i+1}
in time and
C^{2i+2}
in space with bounded derivatives up to those orders, and
h
is
C^{2i+2}
with bounded derivatives.

Theorem 2.1

(Population approximation of the high-order surrogate)
.

Under Assumption
2.1
, let
\hat{V}_{i}
solve (
2.13
). Then, as
\Delta t\to 0
,

\left\lVert V(\cdot,t)-\hat{V}_{i}(\cdot,t)\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\leq C_{\mathrm{gen}}\Big(\left\lVert\hat{\mu}_{i}-\mu\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}+\left\lVert\hat{\Sigma}_{i}-\Sigma\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\Big)\leq C_{\mathrm{gen}}^{\prime}\Delta t^{\,i},

(2.14)

where

C_{\mathrm{gen}}:=\frac{1-e^{-\beta(T-t)}}{\beta}\left(\left\lVert\nabla_{s}\hat{V}_{i}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}+\tfrac{1}{2}\left\lVert\nabla_{s}^{2}\hat{V}_{i}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\right)

(2.15)

and
C_{\mathrm{gen}}^{\prime}
depends only on
C_{\mathrm{gen}}
, the derivative bounds from Assumption
2.1
, and the stencil moment
\sum_{j=0}^{i}a_{j}^{(i)}j^{i+1}
.

To turn the population surrogate into an estimator, approximate
V(\cdot,t_{n})
by a linear feature class
V_{n}^{\phi}(\cdot)=\phi(\cdot)^{\top}w_{n}
on the grid
t_{n}=n\Delta t
. Let

G_{n}:=\mathbb{E}[\phi(s_{t_{n}})\phi(s_{t_{n}})^{\top}],\qquad A_{n}^{(i)}:=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\phi(s_{t_{n}})\Big(\frac{1}{\Delta t}\Delta^{(i)}\phi(s_{t_{n}})\Big)^{\top}\right],\qquad b_{n}:=\mathbb{E}[\phi(s_{t_{n}})r(s_{t_{n}},t_{n})],

where
\Delta^{(i)}\phi(s_{t_{n}})=\sum_{j=0}^{i}a_{j}^{(i)}\phi(s_{t_{n+j}})
. The pooled population recursion and its empirical analogue are driven by

M_{n}:=\Big(\beta-\frac{a_{0}^{(i)}}{\Delta t}\Big)G_{n}-A_{n}^{(i)},\qquad\widehat{M}_{n}:=\Big(\beta-\frac{a_{0}^{(i)}}{\Delta t}\Big)\widehat{G}_{n}-\widehat{A}_{n}^{(i)}.

(2.16)

The deterministic discrepancies are grouped into four local terms: generator misspecification
\eta_{n}^{\mathrm{gen}}
, projection error
\eta_{n}^{\mathrm{proj}}
, pooling bias
\eta_{n}^{\mathrm{pool}}
, and empirical moment deviation
\eta_{n}^{\mathrm{stat}}
. Start-up control is kept separate because it enters only through the terminal block of the multistep recursion.

Theorem 2.2

(One-step perturbation bound with explicit
C_{0}
)
.

Let
\bar{V}_{n}
denote the pooled population recursion in the fitted class, let
\widehat{V}_{n}
denote the empirical recursion, and define

e_{n}:=\left\lVert\widehat{V}_{n}-\bar{V}_{n}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\nu_{n})}.

Assume

\Lambda_{G}:=\max_{n}\lambda_{\max}(G_{n})<\infty,\qquad\lambda_{G}:=\min_{n}\lambda_{\min}(G_{n})>0,\qquad\underline{m}:=\min_{n}\sigma_{\min}(\widehat{M}_{n})>0.

For
1\leq j\leq i
, set

\gamma_{n,j}:=\sqrt{\frac{\Lambda_{G}}{\lambda_{G}}}\frac{\left|a_{j}^{(i)}\right|}{\Delta t}\left\lVert\widehat{M}_{n}^{-1}\widehat{G}_{n}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}},\qquad C_{0}:=\sqrt{\Lambda_{G}}\,\underline{m}^{-1}.

(2.17)

Then, for every interior grid point
0\leq n\leq N_{T}-i
,

e_{n}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{i}\gamma_{n,j}e_{n+j}+C_{0}\big(\eta_{n}^{\mathrm{gen}}+\eta_{n}^{\mathrm{proj}}+\eta_{n}^{\mathrm{pool}}+\eta_{n}^{\mathrm{stat}}\big).

(2.18)

The explicit form of
C_{0}
in (
2.17
) comes directly from one inversion of the empirical system matrix
\widehat{M}_{n}
and one translation from coefficient norm to
L^{2}(\nu_{n})
norm. Nothing else is hidden in it. This is why the constant is local: it depends on the conditioning of the recursion at one time slice, not on propagation through the full horizon.

Theorem 2.3

(Main end-to-end theorem with explicit
C_{\mathrm{ms}}
)
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
2.2
, define the block error vector

\mathcal{E}_{n}:=(e_{n},e_{n+1},\dots,e_{n+i-1})^{\top}

and the companion matrix

\mathcal{C}_{n}:=\begin{bmatrix}\gamma_{n,1}&\gamma_{n,2}&\cdots&\gamma_{n,i}\\
1&0&\cdots&0\\
0&1&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&0\end{bmatrix},\qquad\Phi(n,k):=\mathcal{C}_{n}\mathcal{C}_{n+1}\cdots\mathcal{C}_{n+k-1},\qquad\Phi(n,0):=I_{i}.

(2.19)

Let

C_{\mathrm{ms}}:=\max\Bigg\{\max_{0\leq n\leq N_{T}-i}\,\sum_{k=0}^{N_{T}-i-n}\left\lVert\Phi(n,k)\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}},\max_{0\leq n\leq N_{T}-i}\left\lVert\Phi\big(n,N_{T}-i+1-n\big)\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\Bigg\}.

(2.20)

If
\eta^{\mathrm{start}}:=\max_{0\leq\ell\leq i-1}e_{N_{T}-\ell}
denotes the terminal-block start-up error, then

\max_{0\leq n\leq N_{T}-i}e_{n}\leq C_{\mathrm{ms}}\left(C_{0}\max_{0\leq n\leq N_{T}-i}\big(\eta_{n}^{\mathrm{gen}}+\eta_{n}^{\mathrm{proj}}+\eta_{n}^{\mathrm{pool}}+\eta_{n}^{\mathrm{stat}}\big)+\eta^{\mathrm{start}}\right).

(2.21)

Moreover, if
\sup_{n}\left\lVert\mathcal{C}_{n}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\rho<1
, then

C_{\mathrm{ms}}\leq\frac{1}{1-\rho}.

(2.22)

In the linear-feature estimator studied here,

\eta_{n}^{\mathrm{gen}}=O(\Delta t^{\,i}),\qquad\eta_{n}^{\mathrm{pool}}=O\big(h(L_{\mu,t}+L_{\Sigma,t})\big),\qquad\eta_{n}^{\mathrm{stat}}=O\!\left(\frac{B^{2}S_{i}}{\lambda}\sqrt{\frac{d\log(N_{T}/\delta)}{M_{\mathrm{eff}}}}\right),

(2.23)

with
M_{\mathrm{eff}}\asymp Mh/\Delta t
under temporal pooling.

The theorem is the paper’s main structural result. It folds the approximation theorem, the pooling analysis, and the finite-sample recursion into one explicit block inequality. The meaning of the constants is now transparent:
C_{0}
is the local inversion-and-norm-transfer constant, while
C_{\mathrm{ms}}
is the horizon-wide amplification constant produced by the companion recursion.

Optimizing the bandwidth in (
2.23
) balances the pooling bias against the variance term and yields the nonstationarity floor

F_{\mathrm{ns}}\asymp\left(\frac{d(L_{\mu,t}+L_{\Sigma,t})\Delta t}{M}\right)^{1/3}.

(2.24)

Suppressing fixed projection and start-up constants, the leading envelopes are

E_{\mathrm{BE}}\asymp\Delta t+F_{\mathrm{ns}},\qquad E_{\mathrm{Gen2}}\asymp\Delta t^{2}+F_{\mathrm{ns}},\qquad E_{\mathrm{Gen3}}\asymp\Delta t^{3}+F_{\mathrm{ns}}+V_{3},

(2.25)

where
V_{3}
denotes the higher-order multistep variance and conditioning cost.

Corollary 2.1

(Decision-frequency regime map)
.

Under the optimized bandwidth from (
2.24
), three practically distinct regimes appear.

(i)

If
F_{\mathrm{ns}}\gtrsim\Delta t
, the Bellman baseline and the higher-order estimators are all floor-dominated, so visible order gains should be weak.

(ii)

If
\Delta t^{2}\lesssim F_{\mathrm{ns}}\lesssim\Delta t
, the Bellman baseline remains first-order while Gen2 has already suppressed its leading discretization term. In this middle regime, Gen2 should reliably improve on the Bellman baseline, but Gen3 need not stabilize beyond Gen2.

(iii)

If
F_{\mathrm{ns}}\lesssim\Delta t^{2}
and
V_{3}
does not dominate, third-order gains can become visible; otherwise Gen2 remains the safer default.

3. Results

The empirical study is written around the three theory questions introduced in the Introduction: calibration of order, benchmark gains over the Bellman baseline, and the location of the operating region and failure boundary. All results use fixed train/validation/test splits, repeated random seeds, validation-based bandwidth selection, and held-out Monte Carlo ground truth. The benchmark families are a nonlinear pendulum (
d=2
), a coupled regulator (
d=4
), and two networked linear-quadratic systems (
d=12,24
). The Bellman baseline and Gen2 are run on all four scales. Simple fitted-dynamic anchors are reported on the first three scales using the same logged-trajectory protocol.

The protocol is deliberately matched across the empirical sections. The calibration examples isolate the generator term in the theory. The main benchmark evaluates the full envelope from Theorem
2.3
. The mismatch and feature diagnostics then probe whether a new local perturbation enters through bias, through variance, or through bandwidth instability. Writing the experiments this way makes the empirical section read as a response to the theory rather than as a catalogue of unrelated checks.

3.3. Operating Region and Failure Boundary

The failure boundary is visible in two complementary ways. First, Figure
4
shows that high-order gains require a representation class rich enough to realize them. On the medium and large tasks only the linear and quadratic families were run; on the small task the richer and reduced families provide additional controls. Once the feature class is badly restricted, Gen2 loses its separation from the Bellman baseline. Second, Figure
5
shows how that separation changes under gain-mismatch stress. The improvement persists over a noticeably wider mismatch range on the medium and large tasks than on the small task, which is exactly the behavior one would expect from a method whose operating region is defined by both discretization error and nonstationary sampling error.

The online supplement collects the remaining details needed to read these results correctly: seed-wise distributions, selected bandwidths, start-up ablations, runtime scaling, conditioning diagnostics, and the heavy-suite tables with initial-time RMSE and runtime. Those displays support the same story as the main text rather than opening a second narrative. In particular, the start-up and bandwidth diagnostics help explain why Gen2 is usually the safest practical choice even though higher-order approximations are mathematically available.

4. Discussion

The main conclusion is now direct. In this closed-loop, time-inhomogeneous setting, the Bellman baseline is the correct matched comparator because it is the direct one-step discretization of the value PDE, and its limitation is exactly that it remains first-order in the grid width. High-order generator regression changes that local approximation order without changing the information class of the logged trajectories. The calibration study, the merged benchmark table, and the over-time profiles all point to the same conclusion: second-order generator regression reliably improves on the Bellman baseline across the main benchmark scales.

The same evidence also clarifies the paper’s boundary. The estimator is designed for target-policy or near-on-policy logged trajectories from one time-inhomogeneous closed-loop diffusion. Within that scope, the explicit end-to-end recursion and the diagnostics point to the same failure modes: feature restriction, temporal nonstationarity, unstable start-up, and sufficiently large behavior mismatch. The fitted-dynamic anchors sharpen that interpretation rather than changing it. They can be extremely accurate on the easiest tasks, but they are not the stable default once the benchmark becomes harder.

The next extensions are also specific. On the theory side, the natural step is an action-conditioned version of the same end-to-end recursion, with the local perturbation terms retained and only the fitted-class and behavior-mismatch controls replaced. On the empirical side, the natural step is a broader comparison against modern continuous-time value-learning baselines under the same logged-trajectory protocol. Both directions sharpen the current paper’s applicability without changing its central story.
