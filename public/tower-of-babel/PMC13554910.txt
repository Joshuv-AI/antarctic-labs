Recrossing a Covered Stent With Kissing Balloon Inflation for Side-Branch Rescue
Authors: Satoshi Watanabe, Atsunori Okamura, Tomohiro Yamasaki, Mutsumi Iwamoto, Kota Tanaka, Yoshitomo Tsutsui, Kiwamu Sudo, Yuta Nishimura, Heitaro Watanabe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCoronary artery perforation is a rare but serious complication of percutaneous coronary intervention. Side-branch compromise after covered stent implantation remains challenging, and the role of the kissing balloon technique (KBT) remains poorly described.Case SummaryAn 87-year-old woman underwent percutaneous coronary intervention for severe proximal left anterior descending artery stenosis and developed delayed coronary perforation with cardiac tamponade. Emergency pericardial drainage and covered stent implantation were performed, resulting in the occlusion of the left circumflex artery. Recrossing through the covered stent using a high-penetration guidewire enabled KBT, restoring side-branch flow without residual contrast leakage.DiscussionRecrossing through a covered stent followed by KBT may represent a feasible bailout strategy for preserving side-branch perfusion in complex bifurcation lesions after coronary perforation.Take-Home MessageRecrossing through covered stents followed by KBT may restore side-branch flow and preserve myocardial perfusion.Key Words: complication, intravascular ultrasound, percutaneous coronary intervention, stents

Bailout Strategy for Coronary Perforation With Side-Branch Occlusion

Schematic overview of the clinical course and bailout strategy in a patient with delayed coronary perforation after directional coronary atherectomy (DCA). Emergency covered stent implantation successfully sealed the perforation but resulted in left circumflex artery (LCx) occlusion. History of presentation

An 87-year-old woman presented with worsening exertional chest pain over several days. On admission, her blood pressure was 128/64 mm Hg, heart rate was 72 beats/min, and oxygen saturation was 98% on room air. Physical examination showed no signs of heart failure. Electrocardiography revealed no significant changes, and laboratory tests were unremarkable. Based on clinical findings, unstable angina was suspected, and coronary angiography was performed.

Past Medical History

The patient had hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes mellitus, prior 3-vessel percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) for multivessel coronary artery disease, and peripheral artery disease treated with peripheral intervention.

Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnosis included unstable angina, non–ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction, coronary vasospasm, and progression of previously treated coronary artery disease.

After considering these possibilities, unstable angina due to progression of coronary stenosis was regarded as the most likely diagnosis.

Investigations

Coronary angiography showed severe proximal left anterior descending artery (LAD) stenosis near the left circumflex artery (LCx) bifurcation (Figure 1). Intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) revealed a concentric plaque with mild calcification at the proximal edge of a prior stent (Figure 2A to 2C).

After directional coronary atherectomy, repeat angiography showed subtle aneurysmal change at the site (Figure 3). At that time, although IVUS demonstrated disruption of the external elastic lamina at the distal bifurcation involving the LCx (Figure 2D to 2F), there was no angiographic evidence of contrast extravasation and the patient remained hemodynamically stable. Considering the potential risk that further balloon dilatation or stent implantation could exacerbate vessel wall injury, we elected conservative management with strict blood pressure control and close observation.

Two days after the procedure, the patient developed chest discomfort and hemodynamic collapse. Transthoracic echocardiography revealed a massive pericardial effusion. Emergency coronary angiography confirmed coronary perforation with pericardial contrast leakage (Figure 4).

Management

Emergency pericardial drainagewas performed by inserting a pericardial catheter to relieve cardiac tamponade. The cardiovascular surgical team was consulted at an early stage and remained on standby. However, given the emergency situation and the time required for surgical preparation, catheter-based treatment was prioritized to achieve rapid hemostasis. Next, 2 workhorse guidewires were rapidly advanced into both the LAD and the LCx without microcatheter support. IVUS demonstrated rupture of the external elastic lamina with a large echolucent space adjacent to the vessel wall (Figure 4).

A covered stent (PK Papyrus 2.5 × 20 mm, Biotronik) was carefully positioned and deployed across the perforation site to seal the vessel rupture (Figure 5A). After this, angiography revealed the occlusion of the LCx (Figure 5B).

Covered Stent Positioning and Side-Branch Occlusion

(A) Covered stent positioning across the perforation site in the LAD bifurcation. (B) Postdeployment angiography demonstrating the occlusion of the LCx. Abbreviations as in Figure 1.

The initial attempt to recross into the LCx using a Conquest Pro 8-20 guidewire (Asahi Intecc) supported by a Corsair Pro microcatheter (Asahi Intecc) was unsuccessful. The operator then exchanged this for a Conquest Pro 12 ST guidewire (Asahi Intecc), which successfully penetrated the covered stent strut and re-entered the LCx by tracking the path of the previously placed guidewire under 2 orthogonal fluoroscopic projections (Figure 6A).

Recrossing and Kissing Balloon Technique After Covered Stent Implantation

(A) A high-penetration guidewire successfully penetrated the covered stent and enabled recrossing into the LCx along the path of the previously placed guidewire. (B) Final kissing balloon inflation using a 3.5-mm balloon in the LAD and a 2.75-mm balloon in the LCx. Abbreviations as in Figure 1.

For final kissing balloon inflation, a 3.5-mm noncompliant balloon was positioned in the LAD and a 2.75-mm balloon was advanced into the LCx. The 2 balloons were simultaneously inflated at the bifurcation to expand the stent (Figure 6B). Angiography performed immediately afterward demonstrated excellent vessel expansion without any residual contrast leakage (Figure 7A). After successful hemostasis, the patient was managed in the intensive care unit with continuous readiness for emergency surgical intervention if needed.

Final Angiographic Result After Kissing Balloon Inflation

(A) Final coronary angiography immediately after kissing balloon inflation demonstrating restoration of flow in the LAD and LCx, with faint residual contrast filling within the encapsulated cavity (orange arrow). (B) Follow-up coronary angiography performed 1 week later demonstrated complete resolution of the encapsulated cavity and sustained patency of both the LAD and the LCx. Abbreviations as in Figure 1.

Outcome and Follow-Up

Follow-up angiography 1 week later showed resolution of the cavity and patency of both the LAD and the LCx (Figure 7B).

At 6-month follow-up, coronary computed tomography angiography confirmed sustained stent patency. There was no recurrent aneurysmal change or restenosis.

Discussion

Coronary artery perforation is a life-threatening complication of PCI, particularly when associated with cardiac tamponade.1 Immediate recognition and management are crucial. In this case, delayed tamponade after angiographic changes highlighted the need for careful monitoring after an apparently successful procedure. Vessel injury in this case may have been associated with directional coronary atherectomy, which involves plaque excision using a cutting mechanism and may increase the risk of deep vessel wall injury and delayed coronary perforation. However, coronary perforation may occur with conventional PCI techniques,2 and the mechanism in this case was likely multifactorial.

Covered stent implantation is an effective strategy for sealing coronary perforations.3 However, it may compromise side-branch flow in bifurcation lesions. In this case, the covered stent achieved hemostasis but resulted in the occlusion of the LCx, illustrating the trade-off between sealing perforation and preserving side-branch patency.

Recrossing through a covered stent is technically challenging because the membrane may hinder guidewire passage into the side branch. In this case, the presence of a trapped guidewire in the LCx played a crucial role as a landmark for successful recrossing. Without a trapped wire, reaccess to the side branch may be considerably more challenging. In such situations, alternative bailout strategies, including double-barrel deployment of covered stents, may be considered, although long-term patency remains uncertain. In bifurcation lesions, jailed wire, jailed balloon, and jailed microcatheter strategies are commonly used to preserve side-branch access. However, these strategies were not feasible in the present case because of the urgent need for immediate covered stent deployment during coronary perforation. In addition, the membrane structure of covered stents increases the risk of device entrapment after expansion, further limiting the use of jailed techniques.

The PK Papyrus covered stent features a polyurethane membrane and a low crossing profile, enabling rapid delivery in emergency settings. Nevertheless, recrossing through covered stents remains technically challenging. In this case, an initial attempt using a Conquest Pro 8-20 guidewire failed whereas a Conquest Pro 12 ST guidewire enabled successful recrossing and re-entry into the LCx. The higher penetration force of the Conquest Pro 12 ST may have facilitated passage through the covered stent membrane. This observation underscores the importance of guidewire selection and the potential utility of high-penetration guidewires when attempting recrossing through covered stents. In addition, the relatively low crossing profile and single-layer membrane of the PK Papyrus stent may facilitate recrossing compared with earlier-generation covered stents.

After successful recrossing, kissing balloon inflation was performed to optimize bifurcation geometry and restore side-branch flow. The use of the kissing balloon technique after covered stent implantation has rarely been described. In this case, kissing balloon inflation restored side-branch flow without residual leakage, suggesting that bifurcation optimization may be safely achieved even after emergency covered stent placement. This strategy may be particularly useful in bifurcation lesions where preservation of side-branch flow is clinically important, especially in proximal bifurcation lesions. However, kissing balloon inflation may carry a risk of disrupting the covered stent membrane and compromising perforation sealing. Careful assessment using angiography and intravascular imaging is essential. In cases of significant membrane disruption, additional interventions such as culotte covered stenting may be required.

Follow-up angiography at 1 week and 6 months demonstrated resolution of the cavity and patency of both the LAD and the LCx, with no restenosis or recurrent aneurysmal change. These findings suggest that this bailout strategy may provide durable results.

This report is limited by its single-case design, and further studies are needed to confirm the generalizability of this strategy. Reports describing recrossing through covered stents followed by kissing balloon inflation remain limited.

This case demonstrates a feasible and reproducible bailout strategy for preserving side-branch perfusion in complex bifurcation lesions after emergency covered stent implantation and provides practical procedural insights for similar high-risk situations.

Conclusions

Recrossing a jailed side branch through a covered stent followed by kissing balloon inflation may be a feasible bailout strategy for preserving side-branch perfusion in bifurcation lesions complicated by coronary perforation.
