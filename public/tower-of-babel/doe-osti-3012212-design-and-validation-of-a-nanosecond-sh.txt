DESIGN AND VALIDATION OF A NANOSECOND SHORT-WAVE INFRARED STREAK CAMERA
 USING GASEOUS DETONATION EXPERIMENTS

 by

 NICOLAS BERUBE, PhD
 KYLE THURMOND, PhD
 JOHN BOWLAN, PhD

 Detonations were done at the University of Central Florida

 ABSTRACT

A custom short-wave infrared (SWIR) galvanometric streak camera was developed to provide nanosecond-

scale, time-resolved optical diagnostics. The system was designed as a compact, mechanically driven

alternative to traditional tube-based streak cameras, using a galvanometer mirror and multiple-reflection

optical path to achieve high temporal resolution while maintaining electronic synchronization for trigger

sequencing. Temporal resolution achieved 139.5 ns/px, allowing direct conversion of image-slope gradients

to physical wave velocities. The instrument was validated using hydrogen–oxygen–argon detonations

within a modular detonation tube, capturing transient emission structures and post-detonation decay

behavior with microsecond precision. Although signal levels in filtered configurations were limited by lens

transmission and detector sensitivity, the system successfully resolved intensity rise and decay

characteristics across 10–60 % argon mixtures. The observed trends demonstrate the feasibility of compact

galvanometer-based streak systems for high-speed SWIR imaging in reactive environments.

 Streak Camera Review

 Commercial tube-based streak cameras have been employed for decades in high-speed optical

diagnostics due to their ability to achieve exceptionally high temporal resolution, on the order of

nanoseconds and even femtoseconds [1], at the expense of one spatial dimension. In a conventional

configuration, photons entering through a narrow-slit strike a photocathode, releasing electrons that are

accelerated and deflected by a time-varying electric field oriented perpendicular to the slit. The deflected

electron stream is then projected onto a phosphor screen or sensor array, converting temporal variations in

light intensity into spatial displacements. Lai [2] developed a mechanical variety of this system driven by

a galvanometric mirror, replicating the nanosecond temporal resolution of tube-based streak cameras. By

introducing multiple optical reflections within the scanning path, this design increased the effective

sensitivity to mirror motion and enabled a compact, electronically controlled alternative to traditional

electron-deflection streak systems. This is reflected in the equations, where the simplification Lai provides

to set the minimum resolvable spacing of the airy disk diameter to the sensor pixel size at ½ is already done,

and then rearranged to solve for pixel dwell time:

 The advantage of such a design lies in its simplicity; it relies on mechanical beam steering and

optical reflections. Where the increase in reflections, mirror speed, or lens effective aperture can decrease

pixel dwell time, in other words, increase the sampling rate of the system. Subsequent studies have extended

the tubeless streak camera concept to diverse applications, including high-resolution color photography and

focusing schlieren imaging at rates up to 2.4 Msamples/s [3]. Other implementations have used the design

to tile their sensors, increasing the overall system frame rate by capturing multiple step segments before

each readout [4]. More recent analyses have provided theoretical frameworks describing optimal rotating-

mirror geometries, as well as detailed evaluations of mirror deformation, stress, and dynamic stability [5,

6]. Returning to tube-based streak cameras, they are capable of extending beyond the visible spectrum,

such as the Hamamatsu C2830, which covers wavelengths from 0.2 µm to 1 µm, with additional

investigations pushing into these ranges [7-11]. However, to date, no galvanometer-driven/rotating-mirror

streak camera has been demonstrated in the short-wave infrared regions.

 Short Wave Infrared Temperature Measurements

Thermal brightness refers to the radiative intensity emitted by a medium as a function of temperature,

species composition, and wavelength, and it provides a fundamental basis for optical diagnostics in high-

temperature reacting flows. In hydrogen-oxygen detonations, the reacting field comprises contributions

from both broadband continuum emission and discrete molecular bands associated with electronically or

vibrationally excited species, such as H2O and OH*. The relative strength of these emissions varies strongly

with temperature and pressure, making wavelength selection critical for isolating useful signals. To support

this selection, spectral modeling tools, such as SpectraPlot [12], are routinely employed in combustion

research. SpectraPlot uses established spectroscopic databases (e.g., HITRAN [13], HITEMP [14]) to

compute line-by-line emission intensities for specified thermodynamic conditions, incorporating

parameters such as wavelength range, path length, pressure, and species concentration. These modeled

spectra provide quantitative predictions of emissivity for individual species, allowing researchers to identify

spectral regions where target products dominate the radiative output and where interference from other

emitters is minimized. Such information is essential for selecting optical filters, interpreting detector

signals, and relating measured intensity to underlying thermochemical processes in high-enthalpy

environments such as detonation waves.

 Background on Infrared Cameras

Current commercial optical infrared thermal imaging systems are constrained by detector response time,

readout architecture, and cooling requirements, which limit their effective sampling rates to the kHz range

[15]. Such temporal resolution is insufficient to capture the nanosecond-scale events within detonations,

including the evolution of triple-point structures and thermal gradients within detonation cells. This gap

underscores the need for a custom-designed diagnostic system capable of nanosecond temporal resolution

in the infrared to resolve transient temperature and emission dynamics associated with detonation-wave

propagation.

A retroreflector provides an effective means of generating a large number of internal reflections, as rays

entering the cavity undergo repeated bounces before exiting in a direction nearly identical to the incoming

path. To illustrate the underlying behavior, consider first the case of 𝛽 = 0, corresponding to two parallel

reflective plates. In this configuration, the path traced by a ray forms a series of isosceles triangles, allowing

the horizontal displacement per bounce, dx, to be expressed as a function of the incident angle 𝜃 and the

height of the plates. From this geometry, one can derive a relation for the total number of reflections, Nr ,

which depends on (1) the length of the limiting or shorter mirror segment 𝑙, (2) the spacing between the

plates ℎ, and (3) the angle of incidence θ. As θ approaches 0° (equivalently 𝛼 → 90°, where 𝛼 is measured

from the surface), the ray becomes nearly axial and dx decreases, resulting in a larger number of reflections.

Likewise, increasing the mirror length 𝑙 or decreasing the plate spacing ℎ increases Nr .

In the laser design, after reflecting off the first gold concave mirror, the beam is reflected into the wedge-

Object Imaging Design

For the object-focused streak camera setup, the camera's internals had to be modified to collect more light

throughout the system. This resulted in the camera setup seen in Figure 4. Rather than incorporating varying

heights into the design, all optics are level, and no intentional tilt is included in the system. The 20 µm wide,

10 mm long slit was replaced with a 5 µm wide, 3 mm long slit. The concave mirror was replaced with a

100 mm (𝑓1 ), Ø50 mm plano-convex lens, once again collimating the slit beam, and the 200 mm Ø25 mm

has been replaced with a 200 mm (𝑓2 ), Ø50 mm plano-convex lens to account for the larger beam. In

addition, the retroreflector portion was removed to simplify the system.

Optical Paths

A Shimadzu HPV-X2 camera was used to directly image the detonation wave through a 532-nm bandpass

filter, which transmitted the Prizmatix light source while blocking broadband emission from the reacting

flow. The transmitted portion of the Prizmatix spectrum formed the shadowgraph image recorded by the

Shimadzu system. The bandpass filter also functioned as a dichroic filter, reflecting wavelengths outside

the passband, including both shorter and longer wavelengths emitted by the detonation, toward the streak-

camera optical path. This configuration enabled simultaneous acquisition of visible-light shadowgraph

images and radiative emission for streak imaging without interfering with either diagnostic.

Optical Components and Wavelengths

The hydrogen-oxygen-argon mixture was once again chosen for its chemical simplicity, ease of wave speed

control, and ease of temperature control with argon concentration. Figure 9 presents the SpectraPlot

predictions for the dominant emitting species under the conditions relevant to this study. The spectrum

displays dense H2O bands beginning near ~1.3 µm with prominent structure through ~1.5 µm, and a large

grouping of OH* transitions extending roughly from ~1.4 to ~1.7 µm. The axes in Figure 9, wavelength

Figure 9: SpectraPlot HITRAN emission for H2O and OH*.

SpectraPlot was used to (1) predict H2O and OH* emission for the mixtures employed in this study, (2)

identify SWIR bands with maximum sensitivity to the target species, and (3) avoid regions with significant

interspecies interference. The modeled emission peaks guided the selection of a long-pass and a band-pass

filter, chosen to maximize the total signal strength while enhancing the relative difference between H2O-

and OH*-dominated regions within the detonation. These filters were selected to ensure sufficient intensity

for detection, as single narrowband windows were expected to yield insufficient signal, given the low

exposure per column.

For the InGaAs sensor, the measured quantum efficiency rises to a broad plateau of >80 % between ≈1.0-

1.4 µm before slowly rolling off towards 0 % at 1.7 µm. The 60 mm UV-VIS-IR APO macro lens exhibits

near 1.5 µm. Mirrors contribute multiplicatively; protected gold mirrors typically maintain high reflectance

are important. In practice, maximizing overall optical efficiency requires multiple steps. The first is

operating within the 1.0–1.45 µm range, where camera QE remains high and lens transmission is still

acceptable. The second is minimizing the number of reflective surfaces and selecting high-reflectance

coatings. The third is employing long-pass and band-pass filters that avoid the steep transmission roll-off

of the macro lens while isolating the desired H2O or OH* emission features.

Experimental Conditions

As discussed previously, increasing the argon concentration in the mixture raises the post-detonation

temperature, thereby increasing the number of photons incident on the sensor array. Argon dilution also

alters the equilibrium composition of the detonation products, directly affecting the relative formation of

H2O and OH* species. These combined effects allow for quantitative comparison between test cases by

correlating the measured emission intensity with both detonation temperature and product-species

concentration. Accordingly, experiments were conducted over argon concentrations ranging from 10 % to

60 % in 10 % increments for stoichiometric hydrogen–oxygen mixtures.

 SWIR Streak Camera Investigation Results

Figure 13 presents the results of a 40 % argon detonation recorded without a filter. In this streak image,

time progresses from left to right along the horizontal axis, while spatial position increases upward along

the vertical axis. The upper portion of the image corresponds to the downstream end of the test section, and

the lower edge corresponds to the upstream region. Linear motion captured by the streak camera appears

as an angled trace between these two axes. For rapidly moving features, this angle may appear nearly

 14

vertical. However, for angled motions such as those shown previously, after applying the previously

described calibration corrections, the slope becomes more pronounced, allowing the wave velocity and

direction of motion to be accurately resolved.

Figure 13: Results with no filter but a brightness and contrast increase.

After applying the previously described realignment, background subtraction, and dark-current subtraction,

one can extract the object velocity by fitting a line to the angle of the front over time. For the 0 % argon

case, the fit found 14 points obtained by thresholding the front at 90 % of the first encountered pixel

intensity (1850 K in Figure 14), yielding a slope of m=5.705 px/px. As the argon concentration increases

(up to 60 % argon), the number of selected points increases to 30, which correlates with a decrease in wave

velocity, thereby improving the robustness of the fit. The resulting wave velocities inferred from the camera

agree closely with those from the PCB measurements, with residual differences primarily reflecting the

quality of the best-fit procedure.

Additionally, intensity profiles were extracted from the no filter experiment to examine variations in the

rise of the optical emission associated with the passage of the detonation front. Using a moving-average

window of 50 points to reduce noise, the measured rise times, from 10 % to 90 % intensity, for argon

concentrations of 0, 20, 30, 40, 50, and 60 % were 10.36, 10.65, 12.62, 10.28, 9.98, and 8.75 µs,

respectively, as shown in Figure 37. A general decrease in rise time is observed with increasing argon

concentration, indicating that the radiative response becomes sharper and more transient at higher diluent

levels. This suggests an increase in argon concentration, enhanced post-wave thermal gradients, and

accelerated energy transfer, leading to more abrupt photon emission from the reaction zone.

The datasets with filters exhibited signal levels only marginally above the background (Figure 33 and Figure

34), rendering the corresponding peak-temperature estimates effectively inconclusive. In these spectral

bands, the signal-to-noise ratio was insufficient for quantitative temperature retrieval (Figure 35 and Figure

36). Therefore, only qualitative timing and decay behavior are reported. These results provide intensity-

versus-time profiles for each condition, allowing relative comparison between mixture cases. However, low

transmission at higher wavelengths, likely a consequence of the VIS–UV–IR lens selected at the start of

testing, resulted in diminished signal strength for mixtures containing more than 20 % argon, with recorded

intensities only 1–2 a.u. above background. Because each experiment concluded before complete radiative

equilibrium was achieved, the emission fall time was defined as the time at which 25 % of the peak intensity

was reached. Notably, the post-incident decay of OH* emission was consistently slower than that of H₂O

(Figure 18 and Figure 19), suggesting longer-lived excited states or delayed recombination processes within

the reaction zone.

Improvements

The primary limitation of these experiments was the lack of fine detail observed at the detonation front.

This loss of definition originated from the narrow slit width selected for the streak camera, which produced

diffraction artifacts on the sensor when the image was precisely in focus. While this effect was minimal

with the 545 nm LED source, it became pronounced when the system was focused with an infrared source.

The longer wavelengths increased the diffraction term in the Airy disk function, resulting in a noticeable

smearing effect at the best focus. To address this limitation, future designs should employ a wider slit

geometry optimized for the operating wavelength range and incorporate an internal lens assembly to

achieve the desired magnification without sacrificing resolution. Additionally, maintaining a tightly

 19

collimated input beam will reduce optical aberrations and enable multiple controlled reflections, as in the

initial laser-based alignment tests.

Figure 20: Beam width on sensor.

The development of the short-wave infrared galvanometric streak camera represents a significant

advancement in detonation diagnostics, providing a compact, mechanically driven alternative to

traditional tube-based streak systems. The instrument successfully demonstrated nanosecond-scale

temporal resolution and directional imaging sensitivity across hydrogen-oxygen-argon detonations,

capturing the transient emissive structure of the detonation front in real time. Calibration of the spatial

and temporal axes enabled quantitative measurement of streak velocity, allowing direct conversion of

trace slopes into physical wave speeds. The implementation of interchangeable optical filters and

calibrated optics enabled spectral isolation of emission features associated with key species, such as H2O

and OH*, providing a foundation for future quantitative temperature and species diagnostics.

Although signal levels in filtered configurations were limited by detector sensitivity and lens

transmission, the system nonetheless revealed consistent qualitative behavior across mixtures, including a

faster decay of H2O emission relative to OH*. These results validate the feasibility of using compact

galvanometer-driven streak systems for high-speed infrared diagnostics in detonation environments.

Future optimization should focus on enhancing detector quantum efficiency, improving optical throughput

with optimized lenses and coatings, and integrating synchronized pressure and spectral measurements.

 20

 Conclusion

This research advanced the experimental understanding of detonation physics and multiphase flow

interactions by designing, developing, and applying a new modular detonation facility, novel optical

diagnostic techniques, and high-speed imaging platforms. A stainless-steel, high-pressure, modular

detonation tube was engineered to control initiation, confinement, and expansion conditions, enabling a

methodical investigation of detonation propagation across a range of reactive mixtures. Corresponding

diagnostic development, including the short-wave infrared galvanometric streak camera, soot-foil ghost

overlay visualization, and automated image-processing pipelines, provided new capabilities for resolving

transient detonation phenomena at sub-microsecond timescales.

These combined innovations allowed for detailed characterization of detonation-cell dynamics, wave

stability, and droplet–wave interactions under controlled conditions. High-speed optical and pressure

diagnostics revealed the spatial and temporal evolution of triple-point trajectories, cell-size variations, and

localized droplet deformation induced by impinging triple points. Collectively, this work establishes a

comprehensive experimental framework that integrates modular hardware, advanced optical diagnostics,

and data-driven analysis to study detonation structure and multiphase coupling with unprecedented

resolution and repeatability.
