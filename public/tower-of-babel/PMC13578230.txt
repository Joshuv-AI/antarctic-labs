Early prediction and risk assessment of adverse drug combinations using ensemble learning
Authors: S Prejesh, Tamilarasi Kathirvel Murugan, Logeswari Govindaraj
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractModern day healthcare has seen an increase in polypharmacy, which is the prescription of multiple drugs as medication to treat illnesses simultaneously. Therefore there is an increased risk of adverse drug reactions resulting from drug-drug interactions. Existing techniques in the field of pharmacovigilance suffer from many drawbacks. Many machine learning approaches using single models face difficulties in identifying complex interaction patterns. Some methods also overlook the fact that a single adverse event may contain a large number of drugs. In terms of interpretability in a clinical context, mere predicting a combination to be risky or not may not provide a clear enough picture. In an effort to address these challenges, in this study, we propose an ensemble learning approach to effectively predict adverse drug combinations using data obtained from the FDA Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS). Our proposed framework also makes use of DrugBank for mapping drugs and incorporates binary feature vector representations to handle the complexities of the pharmacovigilance data. The ensemble model developed in this study composed of logistic regression, random forest, and CatBoost algorithms proved to be effective compared to several existing techniques in detecting drug interactions with an accuracy of 93.6%, recall of 97.9%, ROC-AUC of 97.5% and PR-AUC of 96%. In addition to achieving strong predictive performance, the model also calculates a confidence score representing the risk associated with specific drug combinations. These results show how ensemble learning can help to enhance the detection of adverse drug reactions and serve as a clinical decision support tool.Keywords: Adverse drug reaction, Drug–drug interaction, Ensemble learning, FAERS, Pharmacovigilance, Risk assessmentSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Diseases, Drug discovery, Health care, Mathematics and computing, Medical research

Introduction

Modern healthcare increasingly relies on polypharmacy, where patients are prescribed multiple medications to manage coexisting conditions such as diabetes, cardiovascular diseases, hypertension, and chronic kidney disorders. This trend is driven by the rising prevalence of multimorbidity, particularly among aging populations. While polypharmacy improves therapeutic outcomes and quality of life, it simultaneously increases the risk of drug–drug interactions (DDIs) and adverse drug reactions (ADRs), which remain a significant global health concern1. Older adults are especially vulnerable, often consuming five or more medications daily, thereby increasing the likelihood of harmful interactions. Beyond individual patient safety, DDIs and ADRs impose a substantial burden on healthcare systems. These events are associated with increased hospital admissions, prolonged treatment durations, higher healthcare costs, and, in severe cases, mortality. As a result, early identification and prediction of harmful drug combinations have become critical in modern pharmacotherapy and healthcare analytics.

Background

A drug–drug interaction occurs when one drug alters the pharmacological effect of another. These interactions are broadly categorized into pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic mechanisms. Pharmacokinetic interactions affect the absorption, distribution, metabolism, or excretion of drugs, potentially leading to altered plasma concentrations. For instance, inhibition of hepatic enzymes may result in elevated drug levels beyond therapeutic limits. Pharmacodynamic interactions occur when drugs act on the same biological pathway or receptor, leading to additive, synergistic, or antagonistic effects. The clinical consequences can range from mild discomfort to severe toxicity or therapeutic failure2. ADRs are a major contributor to global healthcare challenges. They are among the leading causes of hospitalization and are associated with significant morbidity, mortality, and economic burden3. Ensuring drug safety throughout the lifecycle of pharmaceutical products—from development to post-marketing surveillance—remains a key priority for regulatory authorities and healthcare providers. Despite extensive preclinical testing and clinical trials, it is not feasible to identify all potential drug interactions prior to market approval4. Clinical trials are conducted under controlled conditions with limited populations, often failing to capture rare or complex interactions, particularly those involving multiple drugs. This limitation has led to the development of pharmacovigilance systems that monitor drug safety in real-world settings after market entry5.

Large-scale repositories such as the FDA Adverse Event Reporting System provide extensive real-world data on drug exposure, outcomes, and adverse events. These databases aggregate reports from healthcare professionals, pharmaceutical manufacturers, and patients, offering valuable insights beyond controlled clinical environments. However, the volume, heterogeneity, and inherent noise in such data make manual analysis impractical. Traditional statistical signal detection techniques, including disproportionality analysis, have been widely used to identify potential safety signals6. While effective for detecting simple associations, these methods struggle to capture complex multi-drug interactions and subtle patterns in high-dimensional data. To address these limitations, machine learning and artificial intelligence have emerged as promising solutions. These approaches enable automated analysis of large datasets and facilitate the discovery of hidden interaction patterns7. Existing studies have explored various data modalities, including molecular structures, pharmacological properties, clinical co-prescription patterns, and graph-based drug representations8. However, modeling real-world pharmacovigilance data remains challenging due to its sparsity, high dimensionality, and noise.

Research gaps

Despite advancements in computational methods for DDI prediction, several challenges limit their effectiveness and clinical applicability. First, pharmacovigilance datasets are inherently high-dimensional and sparse. Each report typically contains only a small subset of drugs selected from a large global vocabulary, resulting in sparse feature representations9. This characteristic reduces the effectiveness of many traditional machine learning models and complicates pattern learning. Second, most existing approaches assume pairwise drug interactions. While computationally convenient, this assumption does not reflect real-world clinical scenarios. Patients often consume multiple medications simultaneously, leading to higher-order interactions that cannot be captured by pairwise models alone. Third, individual modeling techniques exhibit inherent limitations. Linear models, such as logistic regression, are effective for capturing simple relationships but fail to model complex non-linear interactions. Conversely, non-linear models, including tree-based and boosting methods, can capture intricate patterns but are more prone to overfitting, particularly in noisy pharmacovigilance data10. This trade-off highlights the need for hybrid modeling strategies. Additionally, many existing systems provide only binary predictions, indicating whether a drug combination is interacting or non-interacting. Such outputs lack interpretability and do not convey the confidence or risk associated with predictions. In clinical practice, understanding prediction certainty is essential for decision-making and risk prioritization.

Although ensemble learning is a well-established approach for improving predictive performance, its direct application to pharmacovigilance data is non-trivial. The challenges of sparse multi-drug representations, label noise, and heterogeneous data sources require careful architectural design. Therefore, a tailored ensemble framework is necessary to effectively address these domain-specific challenges while ensuring robustness and generalization.

Literature review

A review of existing methodologies utilized to research DDI shows how varied many of these methods are with researchers utilizing data from a variety of resources, including pharmacovigilance data, molecular graphs, genomic information and numerous combinations of these. The DDI field is somewhat like a mosaic of concepts that are in different stages of convergence toward the same objective which is to find dangerous drug combinations prior to having a negative impact on patients.

An example of this would be the work in11 who utilized pharmacovigilance data collected through the FDA’s Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS). One who has ever looked at the data that exists in FAERS understands that the data from FAERS can be scattered in nature but each of the reports contains relevant information. However, rather than just relying on traditional pharmacological descriptors, the authors generated semantic embeddings for FAERS reports using BioWordVec. They attempted to describe how drugs, indications, and active ingredients are interconnected, both in clinical settings and language. Then they used different predictive variables, including the drug, indication, and active ingredient pairings described above, to create classifiers to predict potential adverse drug events (ADEs) occurring within the first 120 days of an individual taking a medication. The best-performing classifier was CatBoost with an F1 score of 73.3% and an AUC of 84%, though there was some concern regarding whether this model would generalize well due to the variability in the quality of reporting in FAERS. Nevertheless, this research clearly supports the effort to create early drug safety population-based risk prediction models.

A slightly different approach is discussed in12, who introduced pharmacogenomics into the picture. The approach is as follows: the authors used gene-drug-ADR associations derived from curated biological databases and fed them into a convolutional neural network. The idea is quite intuitive, and the reasoning is as follows: genetic factors often influence the response of a patient to a certain drug, and thus, if we do not take those factors into account, we might miss some essential information. The results of their experiment on benchmark datasets were as follows: AUROC = 92.76%, AUPRC = 92.49%, which is quite higher than those of many other classical machine learning approaches. However, one should note that, in some clinical datasets, such information is not available, and this might be a limitation of the approach.

Another type of method that has attracted significant attention is graph-based approaches. For instance,13 proposed a method for DDI prediction using a link prediction method in a drug interaction network, where the data source is DrugBank v5.1.9. The dataset contains more than four thousand drugs and over a million drug pairs. The method directly learned the embeddings of the drugs using the structure of the interaction network, where a graph convolutional network and collaborative filtering were applied together. On the DrugBank dataset and the other external dataset, called TWOSIDES, the method achieved precision@10 of 0.847 and precision@200 of 0.688, respectively. One interesting point of this method is the absence of negative sampling, which is usually a difficult step in graph learning approaches. Overall, the method demonstrates the power of network-based learning, as it is possible to discover new interaction patterns, which might not be easily observed when learning individual drugs.

Continuing along the same lines,14 have developed GAINET, which stands for graph neural network with attention. In this approach, the focus is not on the interaction network between the two compounds, but rather on each compound being represented as its own molecular graph. The attention layers focus on the parts of the molecules that have the greatest impact on the prediction. Their results on the DrugBank dataset have an accuracy of 90.5% and an AUC of 95%. What makes this approach particularly interesting is that, at least, one gets an idea of the reasoning behind the prediction. While interpretability in deep learning models is always slightly questionable, at least an approximation can be useful for the decision of whether to trust the prediction or not.

Deep learning architectures, particularly developed and tailored for the prediction of DDI, have also been proposed. Chen et al.15 presented a model called ACaps DDI, which is based on the integration of self-attention and capsule neural networks. In this model, the features of the drug, such as the molecular substructure, target and enzyme, are integrated. Capsule networks are based on the idea of retaining hierarchical relationships between the features, which could potentially help in the prediction of the interaction between the two drugs. In the experiment, the model was evaluated on the DrugBank dataset with 572 and 1,258 drugs, and the accuracy was reported to be 0.9269 and 0.9839, respectively. However, the drawback of using a deep model is the complexity associated with it. The other research direction is moving away from structured data and focusing on narrative clinical reports. Cao et al.16 used long short-term memory networks for adverse drug reaction signal detection from unstructured data in the ADRED dataset. The results of the study showed that the LSTM network achieved a sensitivity of 95.2% and an F1-score of 0.97, performing better than logistic regression and other traditional signal detection techniques. The advantage of using unstructured data is obvious, as clinical reports may contain subtle clues for adverse reactions, unlike structured data, which may fail to contain such clues. On the other hand, unstructured data is also noisy and context-dependent, and the results may vary based on how well the language model is able to understand clinical terminology.

Some studies also try to jointly represent several drug representations at once. Zhu et al.17 introduced a method called SSF DDI, where both sequence and structural drug representations are jointly considered. They applied a directed message passing network for representing molecular substructures and a convolutional network for sequence-based representations. The results of their study showed an accuracy of 96.45% in the transductive setting and 87.3% in the inductive setting. The distinction between the two settings is important. One thing is to predict the interaction for a drug included in the training set, and quite another is to predict the interaction for a completely novel drug.

Likewise, the study conducted in Abbas et al.18 also examined the graph neural network approach through several benchmarking datasets, including Biosnap DDI and DrugBank DDI. Their model had an accuracy of 99.2% for the Biosnap dataset, which sounds impressive at first. However, the authors also had difficulties in ranking the true positive interactions for the datasets. In fact, ranking does matter. Clinicians usually have to prioritize the most dangerous combinations first.

In addition to the individual prediction model, some researchers have adopted a more general approach. Bringhurst et al.19 undertook a scoping review of the use of artificial intelligence in the management of polypharmacy in elderly people. The study included the use of machine learning models, rule-based systems, and natural language processing techniques in the identification of potentially harmful drug combinations. Overall, the use of AI-based systems was more effective compared to traditional techniques. However, the gap between the development of the algorithms and their use in the field was recognized as an issue.

The effect of polypharmacy in the clinic is also well described in Olender et al.20, in which the prediction of emergency hospital admissions in the elderly was the main target. Even though this is not a problem of predicting DDIs, there are features related to medication, e.g., drug burden index and number of medications, used in the model. In fact, the Random Forest model was able to obtain an AUC of up to 0.78, indicating the risk of polypharmacy. In other words, the number of medications is a warning sign of potential risk, even before the actual DDIs are taken into account.

Hybrid pharmacovigilance systems have been investigated as well. In Khemani et al.21, a system that uses structured data from clinical trials and unstructured data from textual information, employing natural language processing techniques, was created. The system assessed various models, including Random Forest, Gradient Boosting, CNN, BERT, and GPT models. The study showed that the highest accuracy, 94.1%, was recorded by the model employing the GPT architecture.

It was demonstrated that using multiple data sources could be helpful for the ADR identification process; however, it takes a significant amount of computational capacity, which is not easy to achieve.

In a more specialized case, Wang et al.22 offered the neural network algorithm called DrIVeNN, developed to estimate the risks associated with the adverse events related to the use of multiple medications by cardiovascular patients.The model is based on the combination of molecular structure features, drug-protein interaction information, and side effects. The model resulted in an AUROC of 0.934 during the cross-dataset evaluation, demonstrating its potential in the field, although the focus is on cardiovascular drugs. A similar multi-feature approach is also discussed in Ou et al.23. The fusion deep learning approach incorporates drug sequence, structural, and protein interaction features using a modular approach. Though the ROC-AUC value of 0.7002 and F1-score of 0.6330 are slightly lower compared to other optimized models, the ablation study results show an interesting finding. The study found that the predictive results improved with the addition of all the features, and this indicates the multi-factorial nature of drug interaction.

The characteristics of the patient also play a role in this context. Farnoush et al.24 included demographic factors such as age and gender, as well as molecular and biological factors of the drug. They used more than 1.3 million reports of the FAERS database, with 946 drugs, and implemented both the random forest and deep learning approaches. The results of the deep learning approach were an AUC of 0.700. Although the difference may seem small, we should note that drug safety is not only related to the molecular level of the drug.

More techniques are also being investigated. In Li et al.25, a generative adversarial network framework that incorporates both molecular structure information and knowledge graph embeddings was presented. The experiment showed improvement in accuracy and F1-score. Although the GAN-based method is still new in this field, its long-term effectiveness is not clear; however, the concept of combining the two types of drug knowledge is interesting.

Evaluation frameworks themselves have also become a topic of interest. Shen et al.26 introduced DDI-Ben, a benchmarking framework designed to evaluate DDI prediction models under distribution shifts. Their experiments demonstrated that many existing models experience substantial drops in accuracy and F1-score under such conditions. Interestingly, text-augmented models and large language model–based approaches appeared somewhat more robust.

The second multi-feature graph-based model is the one proposed, based on the MDG-DDI model27. This model is based on the integration of various drug features and then the application of a graph convolutional network to learn the interactions between drug pairs. The model was able to achieve better results than the baselines in terms of AUC, AP, and F1 metrics when tested with the DrugBank, ZhangDDI, and DS datasets. The model is applicable in the early risk assessment of polypharmacy due to its ability to generalize to unseen drugs.

Clinical data analysis is another aspect that will add to this discussion. In this regard, regression-based models were used28 to analyze the ADRED emergency department dataset, which comprises more than seven thousand cases among the elderly population. In their models, demographic, metabolic, and drug exposure features are used to predict adverse drug reactions such as bleeding or falls. In their study, high-risk medications such as anticoagulants are highlighted. Although the models used are less complex than deep neural networks, their level of interpretability is high, which is desirable in the medical field. More complex representation learning techniques are being developed. In this regard, a capsule-based representation learning technique, dubbed Rot4Cap, was proposed29, which maps drugs and their interactions into a four-dimensional space. The technique integrates a GNN model with a text-based representation learning model, either BERT or CNN-based. The technique was shown to perform better than existing techniques, with accuracies ranging from 0.84 upwards on OGB-Biokg, DrugBank, and KEGG datasets. Although the technique is technically sound, it might be a bit demanding in terms of implementation.

A Transformer-based model called KITE-DDI was developed30, which combines the SMILES sequence of the molecule with the information in the knowledge graph. In this model, the inputs are directly used, unlike in other models, where feature engineering is a necessity. In the inductive testing scenario, the model was able to attain an AUC of 0.9646 on the DrugBank dataset. The simplicity of the model is a positive attribute. However, the model needs large amounts of data and is limited to small molecules.

Collectively all these studies illustrate the speed at which the field of DDI prediction is advancing. Graph neural networks, attention, knowledge graphs, and genomic information are all being explored, sometimes individually but also sometimes in combination with one another. The results are promising but the limitation is the same: getting these promising approaches into the hands of the people who need them, that is the clinicians. Accuracy is important, but so is interpretability, availability, and robustness to new drugs.

A graph contrastive learning framework, GraphCL-DTA, is proposed for predicting drug–target binding affinity. Unlike traditional methods, it performs data augmentation in the embedding space, preserving molecular semantics. A novel uniformity-based loss function is introduced to improve representation quality. The model exhibits improved performance on KIBA and Davis datasets, showing better generalization when compared to GraphDTA33.

A multi-task self-supervised learning paradigm is proposed to deal with label scarcity in drug repurposing. It incorporates both a principal task and an auxiliary task based on contrastive learning to facilitate the representation of drugs without resorting to labels. Such learning can improve generalization capability and predictive accuracy by jointly training and multi-input decoding34.

A new approach called NMFDR is proposed, where the classical inner product function is replaced by a generalized Euclidean distance metric function to model the drug-disease associations. It utilizes similarity information within latent variables and improves the ability of interpretation. Experiments show that this model outperforms other matrix factorization models35.

Omics Tweezer is an artificial intelligence algorithm that combines deep learning to perform cell deconvolution on transcriptomics, proteomics, and spatially resolved multi-omic datasets. Optimal transport was used to match simulated and observed data, overcoming batch effects and distributional differences. This resulted in higher accuracy and robustness in several datasets, and also identified clinically important cell types in cancer research36.

PUON is a framework that applies positive and unlabeled data (positive and unlabeled learning) to overcome the problem of inaccurate negative sampling. It also employs an outer neighborhood classifier to account for the interaction of different features in this process. This significantly increased the effectiveness of predictions on various datasets and criteria37.

Recommendation systems have been applied in the field of drug repurposing, focusing on critical issues like insufficient learning capacity, sparsity in data, and overfitting because of negative cases. The researchers stress the importance of having sophisticated models that will help in understanding the connection between drugs and diseases38.

Proposed method

Our study is designed for early detection and risk assessment of drug combinations using ensemble learning. This section presents the complete methodology developed in the study, including the description of the datasets used, preprocessing of data, drug combination representation, problem formulation, stacking ensemble architecture, strategy used for model training and the computations involved in obtaining a risk score used for adverse drug interaction prediction. Dataset description

For this study, we use The Food and Drug Administration Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS) database32 which serves as the primary source of information related to adverse drug reactions (ADR) and drug-drug interactions (DDI). The main reason for choosing this as our source of truth is due to the rigorous processes followed by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) which ensures all reports that are published are reliable and trustworthy. The database is essentially a collection of verified reports submitted by professionals working in healthcare, manufacturers of drugs and the consumers of these drugs. A FAERS report consists of patient demographic information, the list of drugs involved in the case, description of adverse reaction observed in the particular case, clinical indications, patient outcomes, details regarding patient therapy as well as the source of the report. The pharmacovigilance data published by the FDA FAERS was thus the most obvious choice for carrying out our experiments on drug interactions and their effects on patient well-being.

The FDA publishes quarterly files for each year on its website. The files from the last 10 years were downloaded and used for this study.

In addition to the FAERS dataset which provides comprehensive information regarding drug interactions, the study also makes use of the DrugBank benchmark dataset. DrugBank31 contains detailed information about drugs such as physical properties, chemical structures, side-effects as well as extensive data regarding the biological targets of each drug. In the context of our study, DrugBank serves as a reference dictionary for mapping drug identifiers extracted from the FAERS reports. In this way, DrugBank ensures pharmacological validity of the experimental dataset and provides a standardized way to capture features of each drug prior to training our model.

Data preprocessing

The FAERS and DrugBank datasets were subject to several preprocessing steps before their utilization for supervised learning.

Filtering and parsing of reports

Filtering and parsing of reports

The quarterly FAERS release files from the last ten years were first collected and ingested. Each file contains thousands of adverse drug event reports, which have been submitted by healthcare professionals, manufacturers, and occasionally even patients. These reports were not neatly organized, so the first step was to parse these reports into structured data. From each report, three data points were extracted: the unique case identifier, drugs involved in the event, and reactions that had been documented. Collecting all drugs that had been reported, however, was not especially useful. FAERS uses role annotations to describe drug involvement, which indicates whether it is a primary suspect, secondary suspect, or simply concomitant. In other words, it is important to determine whether it is the primary suspect, which is the drug that is thought to have caused the event, or whether it is simply part of the medication regimen. In this instance, since we are interested in co-administration, we filtered our data accordingly.

Only reports were retained if they included meaningful combinations of drugs. For example, if a report included two or more concomitant drugs, it was considered potentially valuable, since it was possible that these drugs might be working together in complex ways. In addition, reports were retained if they included a primary suspect drug and at least one concomitant drug. In contrast, reports with incomplete drug information, among other conditions, were discarded. This is, of course, potentially wasteful, since it means some valid reports will be discarded. However, without this step, there would be too much noise in the data.

It is important to note that the absence of recorded adverse reactions in FAERS reports does not necessarily imply the absence of true adverse events, due to the inherent under-reporting and incompleteness of spontaneous reporting systems. In this study, reports without documented reactions are treated as “non-adverse” as a pragmatic approximation, which is commonly adopted in pharmacovigilance-based machine learning research where explicitly validated negative samples are not available. To minimize potential label noise, strict preprocessing steps such as removal of incomplete reports, enforcement of consistent drug mapping using DrugBank, and elimination of duplicate entries were applied. Furthermore, the large-scale nature of the dataset helps reduce the influence of individual noisy labels.

(2)Drug name normalization and mapping identifiers

Drug name normalization and mapping identifiers

The other challenge with FAERS is related to how drug names are entered. Given that reporting is spontaneous, it is possible to find variations in spelling, formulations, and brand names. In this regard, it is possible to find amoxicillin, amoxicillin trihydrate, and brand names such as Amoxil. To a human, it is easy to see that they relate to each other. However, to a machine, they will appear to be unrelated.

In an effort to deal with this inconsistency, drug names were normalized before any mapping took place. This involved converting all drug names to uppercase, removing formulations and salts, and simplifying compound words. This was not intended to be an effort to standardize drug names, which is impractical, but to get it into a state where it could be matched against a reference database.

Once normalized, the drug names were matched to those in DrugBank. If a good match exists, the DrugBank identifier, or accession number, denoted as DBID, is used. Some of the drug names, of course, will not map neatly. A drug may be listed under some rare brand name, or the name may be truncated or misspelled. Rather than guessing, such reports are discarded. The resulting list of reports consists of those for which all co-administered drugs map nicely to DrugBank.

(3)Processing of side-effects

Processing of side-effects

Each of the FAERS reports could have one or more clinical reactions, which define the observed adverse event. These reactions were standardized using the terminology of the MedDRA system. This was necessary because the same event could be included under slightly different wordings in the report text.

After the parsing, the reports were classified as “adverse” if they had at least one of the standardized reaction terms. Reports with no reactions were classified as "non-adverse."

In this study, negative samples were not synthetically generated but derived directly from the FAERS database. Reports that did not contain any standardized adverse reaction terms after MedDRA normalization were considered as non-adverse instances (negative samples), while reports with at least one recorded reaction were labeled as positive. It is acknowledged that the absence of reported reactions in FAERS does not guarantee the absence of true adverse events due to under-reporting. However, this assumption is commonly adopted in pharmacovigilance-based machine learning studies where explicitly validated negative samples are not available. The rationale for this classification is fairly simple: if the report had reactions, then the event was adverse. Otherwise, the event was non-adverse. Of course, in the context of a voluntary reporting system, the absence of reactions in the report does not necessarily mean that the event did not take place. However, in the context of the large dataset, this classification is one possible compromise. Duplicate case reports were also removed during this stage to prevent repeated entries from biasing the model.

(4) Construction of final dataset

Construction of final dataset

Once the filtering, normalization, and outcome labeling were complete, the final dataset was assembled. Each sample is based on a single report that contains a set of co-administered drugs, normalized to DrugBank, and a binary outcome based on the presence or absence of a reported adverse reaction.

To improve the reliability of both positive and negative samples, strict preprocessing criteria were applied, including removal of incomplete reports, elimination of duplicate entries, and enforcement of consistent drug mapping using DrugBank identifiers. Furthermore, only reports containing valid multi-drug combinations (two or more drugs) were retained to ensure meaningful interaction modeling. During this stage, adverse reaction terms were standardized using MedDRA terminology, enabling consistent identification of reports with and without documented reactions for subsequent label assignment.

Drug combination dataset construction and multi-hot encoding.

The positive set is comprised of drug combinations that were associated with a reported clinical reaction, while the negative set is comprised of combinations that did not have a reported reaction.

The positive set is comprised of drug combinations that were associated with at least one standardized clinical reaction, while the negative set is comprised of combinations derived from reports that did not contain any standardized adverse reaction terms after MedDRA-based normalization. Since pharmacovigilance datasets do not explicitly provide confirmed non-adverse cases, such reports were treated as proxy negative samples. In order to obtain a balanced dataset, a random downsampling procedure was adopted by reducing the sample size of adverse events to that of non-adverse events such that a class ratio of 1:1 was obtained. This procedure ensures that the model is not biased towards the dominant class when evaluating the performance of the classifier. Additionally, the use of a stacking ensemble framework improves robustness to potential label noise by aggregating predictions from multiple heterogeneous models, thereby reducing the impact of misclassified negative samples.

Drug combination representation

Ensemble learning architecture

The study utilizes an ensemble learning approach for the prediction of adverse drug interactions. However, the high-dimensional and sparse nature of the drug combination representation also brings about certain challenges. Each drug combination is encoded as a multi-hot vector in a space of size , the majority of the elements being zero due to the low number of co-administered drugs in each report. Lone classifiers have difficulties generalizing under such circumstances. Therefore to overcome the limitations of the lone classifiers, the stacked ensemble learning approach is utilized. Multiple classifiers which are heterogeneous are employed to learn the various patterns of drug co-occurrence. Thereby this strategy leads to improved predictive performance and robustness. Model training

Model training follows the stacked ensemble framework described in the preceding section. The supervised dataset is first partitioned into mutually exclusive training and test subsets using an 80:20 split as shown in Eq. (13):

The idea behind this is simple. Most of the data is used to train the model, and a small part is used to see how the model behaves with data it has never seen before. However, this is not done in a simplistic manner. Positive and negative data are first separated and then split separately before they are combined again. Otherwise, the test set would end up biased towards one of the two. The test set is handled with care throughout the entire process. It is kept separate throughout the entire model creation process. Even small leaks of information from the test data into the training pipeline can inflate performance metrics.

The second precaution is the representation of the features. The multi-label binarization mapping, which transforms the set of drug identifiers into a vector, is only trained on . All samples in are transformed using this fixed mapping. If the test report contains a drug identifier that was never seen during the training set, the test sample is discarded.

Moreover, in order to prevent any leakage caused by overlap, duplicated records from FAERS were discarded in the preprocessing step to guarantee no identical drug pairs are contained in both training and testing datasets. Although the occurrence of duplications has been prevented, it should be noted that drug pair similarities might arise in both divisions, mimicking realistic settings for pharmacovigilance and facilitating generalization.

Furthermore, temporal validation was also implemented to improve the evaluation robustness. In contrast to random splitting, temporal validation retains the time sequence of the report records, thus eliminating any potential leakage of information from future reports to training. The model under this scheme showed stable performance patterns, suggesting generalizability to novel interaction patterns.

(2)Hyperparameter configuration

Hyperparameter configuration

The base learners also need several parameters to be tuned. Rather than using arbitrary values, grid search with cross-validation is used to select the best parameters, with the search space restricted to the training data only. The formulation for logistic regression is -regularized and the value of the regularization strength C selected from a logarithmic search space. C is varied over a grid of logarithmically spaced values. For Random Forest, the number of trees, the depth of the trees, and the minimum number of samples in a leaf node are tuned. For CatBoost, which is known to behave slightly differently from other boosting algorithms, parameters need to be tuned as well. Here, the number of boosting iterations, learning rates, and tree depths are tuned. Early stopping is also used, with loss on the validation set as the stopping criteria, so the model does not overfit the training data.

The meta-learner is again implemented as a logistic regression model, using regularization and the regularization parameter is again cross-validated on the aggregated meta-feature space. Here we note that all the hyperparameter tuning is performed using only the training folds, and the test data is untouched.

(3)Final model fitting and evaluation

Final model fitting and evaluation

Once cross-validation is finished and appropriate hyperparameters are found, the base learners are retrained on the full training set using the selected hyperparameters. The probabilities are combined and fed into the trained metalearner, producing predictions for the held-out test set . Metrics for how well the model is performing are standard binary classification metrics. If the results for the training and test sets are vastly different, it is likely overfitting, whereas similar results for both indicate good generalization of the model.

All trained models along with the fitted multi-label binarization mapping and selected hyperparameters, are kept to ensure reproducibility and support inference.

Results and discussion

This section provides the experiment settings and outcomes of the suggested ensemble framework for predicting and assessing risks of drug-drug interactions. The performance of the model is examined using the previously described drug interaction datasets, sparse drug representations, and standard classification metrics. Comparative analyses are conducted against the individual base learners to assess how ensemble learning leads to improved performance. In addition to model performance, probability-based outputs are further analyzed to characterize predictive confidence and risk stratification behaviour.

Experimental setup

All of our experiments were conducted using the Python programming language and existing machine learning and data manipulation packages. Data loading, manipulation, and preprocessing were done using pandas. For numerical data processing we made use of NumPy. Machine learning algorithm implementations were done using the scikit-learn library. CatBoost library was used to conduct gradient-boosted learning of decision trees. Data samples of drug–drug interactions were split into sets using a fixed seed for reproducibility of results. Drug interactions were represented using a sparse multi-hot encoded feature vector generated using a MultiLabelBinarizer to encode drug interactions in a high-dimensional space. Training of machine learning models was done using a structured approach consisting of data splitting, feature transformation, ensemble learning using stacking, and performance evaluation. Performance of machine learning models was evaluated using various performance metrics such as accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, confusion matrices, and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve. In addition, probabilistic outcomes of trained machine learning models were also evaluated to perform confidence-based predictions, which are useful in risk assessment. All these experiments were conducted using a machine with 4 CPU cores. To further prevent data leakage, the multi-label binarization mapping (MLB) was learned exclusively from the training data and subsequently applied to the test set. Those drug identifiers that appeared in the test set but were not seen in the training set were dropped to ensure consistency of features across both sets.

In addition to the conventional stratified train/test split approach, a temporal cross-validation scheme was adopted as an attempt to mimic real-world scenarios. The FAERS dataset, which is inherently time-stamped, was partitioned chronologically, where reports from earlier years were used for training, and more recent reports were reserved for testing. This approach ensures that the model is evaluated on future unseen data, thereby providing a more realistic estimate of its generalization capability in practical pharmacovigilance settings.

The proposed ensemble model demonstrates competitive performance in comparison to existing methods. As the reported results are derived from different benchmark datasets (e.g., FAERS, DrugBank, TWOSIDES) with variations in data distribution, feature representation, and evaluation protocols, the comparisons reflect contextual performance differences rather than direct one-to-one benchmarking under identical experimental settings.

Ablation study

An ablation study was carried out in order to determine the contribution of individual base learners to the overall performance of the proposed stacking ensemble method. In this case, the performance of the predictive models, namely Logistic Regression, Random Forest, and CatBoost, was evaluated individually before their overall performance in the ensemble method. As shown in the results, Logistic Regression emerged as having the best performance among the individual predictive models thereby indicating that linear relationships in the drug combination feature set are useful in predicting adverse drug interactions.

Random Forest also emerged with a very high recall rate, indicating a high number of false positives thus compromising overall accuracy. CatBoost, although it was capable of handling non-linear relationships, emerged with relatively lower performance in handling the high-dimensional feature space of the pharmacovigilance dataset.

XGBoost demonstrated strong performance among the additional baselines, effectively capturing non-linear feature interactions and achieving high overall accuracy and ROC-AUC. SVM also produced competitive results, particularly with high recall and stable generalization in the high-dimensional sparse feature space. However, when these individual base learners were combined in the proposed ensemble method, their overall performance was improved by the proposed stacking ensemble method. Accuracy, F1-score, ROC-AUC, and PR-AUC values were improved in the ensemble model compared to the individual classifiers. In addition, the false positive rate was reduced in the ensemble model. This proves that the proposed drug–drug interaction detection system can be improved significantly using heterogeneous learning algorithms with the stacking ensemble model.

Limitations

The limitation in this study is related to the labeling of negative samples. Given that FAERS doesn’t give explicit labels for those drug combinations which have no adverse effects, samples where there were no adverse effects reported have been treated as negative samples. This may lead to noisy labeling because of underreporting or lack of reporting. While preprocessing and modeling help in minimizing this problem, the problem cannot be totally ruled out. The future plan is to improve upon labeling using non-interacting drugs, clinical data at individual level, and other validation techniques.

Furthermore, although downsampling helps in creating a balance within the dataset that facilitates training the model, it may not represent the real distribution of the classes in the real world. In future, imbalance learning methods and semi-supervised models will be used to ensure real-world relevance.

Limitations of confidence score interpretation

The drawback of the suggested model is that there are no calibrated confidence scores. Even though the obtained probabilities show good discriminatory power, they might not actually correspond to the real risk of adverse interactions between drugs. Therefore, one must exercise extreme caution when using these probability scores as an indicator of actual clinical risk. Nevertheless, the obtained confidence scores will still be valuable for prioritizing different pairs of drugs.

Limitation in cross-dataset comparison of methods

One of the limitations of this research is that the comparison against previous models is made by considering results that have been achieved on different benchmark databases. The differences in the properties of those databases and the settings used for experiments can affect the performance scores, making it difficult to establish a fair comparison. Future efforts will concentrate on the assessment of the suggested model on a standard benchmark dataset or the recreation of existing models in the context of the same database.

Furthermore, external validation using independent datasets, like TWOSIDES or clinical databases, was not carried out in this research due to difficulties related to the harmonization of multiple heterogeneous data sources and their features. Nevertheless, external validation remains an important aspect that needs to be considered when evaluating any medical prediction system. Future efforts will center on the alignment of multiple data sources and subsequent testing of the proposed model on external datasets.

Conclusion

The study proposed an ensemble learning approach to identify potential adverse drug interactions from pharmacovigilance reports. From the findings of our study, we are able to conclude that aggregating multiple learners can increase overall reliability and performance and also help to capture complex drug combination patterns. The confidence based risk estimates are another strength of the proposed method, as it helps in prioritizing potentially threatening interactions which require closer attention. The approach offers a data driven way to assist pharmacovigilance analysis.

In the future, work can be done to have additional contextual information about drug usage such as dosage and patient demographics and real clinical validation. Future work will focus on incorporating probability calibration techniques such as Platt scaling and isotonic regression, along with external validation using independent datasets, to improve the reliability and clinical interpretability of predicted risk scores. Future research will aim to conduct comparative evaluations under unified experimental settings to enable more consistent benchmarking against existing methods.

Additionally, future work will emphasize more rigorous validation strategies, including large-scale external validation on independent pharmacovigilance datasets and prospective evaluation using temporally evolving data. Incorporating standardized benchmarking frameworks and reproducing existing baseline models under identical experimental settings will further improve the reliability and comparability of results.
