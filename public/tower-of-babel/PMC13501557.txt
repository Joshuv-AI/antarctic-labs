A 7-year retrospective evaluation of judicial cases of patients over 75 years of age, except traffic accidents
Authors: Abdulkadir Kantar, İsmail Altintop
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to evaluate the clinical, demographic, and forensic characteristics of non–traffic-related forensic cases among individuals aged ≥ 75 years, and to identify injury patterns and admission behaviors that may indicate elder abuse.MethodsThis retrospective, descriptive study included 160 patients aged ≥ 75 years who presented to the Emergency Department (ED) of Kayseri State Hospital between 1 June 2018 and 15 August 2025 and were recorded as non–traffic-related forensic cases. Demographics, comorbidities, type of incident, injury characteristics, time and manner of presentation, and clinical outcomes were obtained from electronic medical records and forensic documentation.ResultsAmong 3,040,767 ED visits and 43,451 forensic case registrations during the seven-year period, 160 patients met the inclusion criteria. The mean age was 79.98 ± 4.69 years, and 55% were female. Comorbidities were present in 74.3% of patients. Assault was the predominant cause of forensic presentation (80.6%), with injuries most commonly involving the head–neck and upper extremities. Abrasions (42.5%) and contusions (24.4%) were the most frequent physical findings. A striking 78.8% of patients attempted to conceal the incident, and ED presentations predominantly occurred between 16:00 and 00:00. Recurrent ED visits were identified in 13.8% of cases, suggesting ongoing or repetitive abuse. Most patients were discharged (84.4%), and the overall mortality rate was 2.5%.ConclusionNon–traffic-related forensic presentations in individuals aged ≥ 75 years are predominantly assault-related, with injury patterns strongly suggestive of elder abuse. Head–neck trauma, inconsistent history, concealed reporting, and recurrent presentations represent key red flags for clinicians. These findings highlight the critical need for heightened forensic awareness, systematic screening, and a multidisciplinary approach to the evaluation and management of suspected elder abuse in the very elderly population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07827-7.Keywords: Very elderly, Emergency department, Concealed reporting, Assault

Introductıon

The elderly population is rapidly increasing globally due to prolonged life expectancy and declining fertility rates. According to United Nations data, the number of individuals over 65 is projected to constitute one-fifth of the world population by 2050. This demographic shift creates an increasing burden on healthcare systems, enhancing the importance of not only chronic disease management but also forensic evaluation, abuse detection, and protective services [1].

While there is no universal definition for the concept of “elderly,” the threshold of ≥ 75 years is often preferred in clinical research because mortality, morbidity, and frailty levels increase significantly from this age onwards. This group, defined as “very elderly,” may present with atypical findings in the clinical presentation of forensic events due to high comorbidity burden, functional dependence, and infirmity, necessitating specialized clinical evaluation [2].

Neglect of elderly individuals is common and can lead to serious mortal and non-mortal consequences. However, physiological changes associated with aging and clinical findings of existing diseases may overlap with indicators of neglect, making it difficult to recognize and causing cases to be overlooked [3]. Elder abuse is defined as physical, emotional, sexual, economic, or neglect-based ill-treatment. The World Health Organization (WHO) reports that approximately 15% of elderly individuals experience some form of abuse during their lives [4]. Elder abuse is recognized as a universal phenomenon affecting all cultural and socioeconomic groups, despite receiving relatively limited attention in public and scientific circles. From a public health perspective, it is associated with serious consequences, such as increased physical and mental health problems and higher healthcare utilization, similar to other forms of domestic and interpersonal violence [5]. Lacerations, bruises, burns, or unexplained pain detected in elderly patients presenting to the emergency department are important indicators of abuse; however, these findings often blend with natural changes associated with aging. This situation complicates the recognition and reporting of forensic cases [6].

While numerous studies on elder abuse exist in current literature, the majority cover the general population aged 65 and over. Conversely, data on the clinical profile, trauma mechanisms, and forms of abuse in forensic cases specifically among “very elderly” individuals over 75 are quite limited. The number of studies examining forensic trauma or abuse cases specific to this age group is very low, and existing research is generally limited to small sample sizes or specific types of trauma, such as falls or physical assault [7].

According to data from the National Institute of Statistics and Economic Studies (INSEE) in France, individuals aged 60 and over are considered part of the elderly population. In the Virage study investigating gender-based violence, the threshold for old age was defined as 70 years and older, while the High Authority for Health (HAS) set this threshold at 75 years [8]. Pre-existing comorbidities and advanced age, particularly 75 years and over, lead to more pronounced patient-specific vulnerabilities such as frailty and decreased physiological reserve. This situation can make the clinical evaluation and management processes of trauma patients more complex and requires a multidisciplinary and careful approach to care planning [9]. In line with these varying definitions in the literature, our study included patients aged 75 and over to create a more homogeneous sample representing the advanced age group.

This study aims to provide a seven-year retrospective evaluation of non-traffic forensic cases in individuals aged 75 and over. Specifically, the distribution of physical, sexual, and neglect-based abuse types, trauma mechanisms, clinical findings, and their relationships with presentation time and prognosis were examined. It is anticipated that the results will contribute to increasing awareness for the early diagnosis and prevention of elder abuse and to improving the management and reporting processes of forensic cases in emergency departments. In this regard, the study seeks to fill the gap in the literature regarding the characteristics of forensic cases in individuals aged 75 and over.

Materıals and methods

Study design and setting

This research is a retrospective, descriptive, and observational study conducted at the Kayseri State Hospital Emergency Department. The study was carried out in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Kayseri City Hospital Ethics Committee.

Study population

All patients aged 75 and over who presented to the emergency department between June 1, 2018, and August 15, 2025, were screened retrospectively. Among these patients, those who presented for reasons coded as official forensic cases other than traffic accidents (all cases requiring a Forensic Report or involving mandatory reporting to judicial authorities) were included in the study. In accordance with the Turkish Penal Code, if a physician develops any suspicion that an injury observed in a patient resulted from an external influence (e.g., assault, traffic accident, poisoning, neglect, or similar external factors), the situation is legally classified as a “forensic case.” Within this framework, it is a legal obligation for the physician to report the case to the authorized judicial bodies (law enforcement or the Public Prosecutor’s Office) without delay. Failure to fulfill this reporting obligation may result in legal and criminal liability under Article 280 of the Turkish Penal Code. The official document issued by the physician for every patient evaluated as a forensic case is referred to as the “Forensic Report.” The Forensic Report is a formal document that systematically contains the clinical findings of the case, the nature of the trauma, the clinical evaluation, and elements of legal significance. This report is standardized by the Ministry of Health of the Republic of Turkey, prepared in accordance with designated medical and legal parameters, and serves as legal evidence before judicial authorities. Data collection and variables

All data were obtained from patient files, forensic reports, and the hospital information management system. For each case, information regarding age, gender, comorbidities, type of event, clinical findings, time and manner of presentation, forensic recurrence status, and outcome (discharge, hospitalization, referral, or death) was recorded.

Mortality records were verified through the e-Nabız System, while hospitalization and referral information were cross-checked with the hospital automation system. The e-Nabız System, which ensures the digital integration of health data in Turkey, is a centralized electronic health record platform developed by the Ministry of Health of the Republic of Turkey and utilized on a national scale. This system allows for the collection of all health data generated in public and private health institutions under a single digital infrastructure, where it is stored securely and can be accessed by authorized users.

Patients were classified into six main groups based on the type of forensic event: assault, fall, poisoning, sharp object injury, suspicious cardiac arrest, and burn. This classification encompassed all non-traffic cases carrying a potential suspicion of abuse or neglect (e.g., poisoning, unexplained cardiac arrest) during the forensic process, aiming to reflect a comprehensive forensic case profile. Suspicious cardiac arrest refers to situations where the underlying cause leading to the arrest is uncertain or unexplained, rather than the state of cardiac arrest itself. Within the framework of current legislation in Turkey, if an individual is found dead suddenly and unexpectedly in an out-of-hospital setting or is brought to the emergency department in cardiac arrest, and this clinical condition cannot be directly and reasonably explained by the patient’s known medical history (e.g., a terminal illness), the case is evaluated as a “suspicious death” or “suspicious arrest”. The determining criterion in this context is whether the arrest can be clearly established as having developed due to natural causes based on clinical and medical data; otherwise, the forensic dimension of the event arises, creating an obligation to report to the relevant authorities. Similarly, any fall that cannot be clearly established as spontaneous or that harbors suspicion regarding its mechanism can be evaluated by the physician as a forensic case. Particularly in cases where the mechanism of trauma is inconsistent with the patient’s clinical history, where the possibility of third-party intervention exists, or where the possibility of neglect/abuse cannot be ruled out, the event is accepted as having forensic significance, and the necessary reporting obligation arises. In trauma-related cases, injury regions were grouped as head-neck, chest, abdomen, upper extremity, lower extremity, and back. Physical examination findings were classified as abrasion, ecchymosis, laceration, hematoma, burn, fracture, intracranial hemorrhage, suspicious cardiac arrest, and intoxication.

Sample size

Since this study included all forensic cases aged 75 and over who met the inclusion criteria within the specified time interval, a separate sample size calculation was not performed. However, a sample size of 160 cases was evaluated as noteworthy and possessing sufficient representative power for a single-center retrospective study examining non-traffic forensic events in “very elderly” individuals, given the limited data in the field.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were expressed as medians (interquartile range, IQR), and categorical variables were expressed as numbers and percentages [n (%)]. The normality of the data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test.

The Chi-square (χ²) or Fisher’s exact test was used to compare categorical variables, while the Mann–Whitney U test was used for the comparison of continuous and non-normally distributed variables. A p < 0.05 value was considered statistically significant.

Dıscussıon

This study provides a significant contribution to the literature as one of the few investigations specifically examining non-traffic-related forensic cases in the 75 years and older age group. The findings demonstrate that the forensic case profile of this “very elderly” population carries distinct differences from the general elderly population. Contrary to the general elder abuse literature, our study fills gaps in the field by systematically analyzing the high prevalence of assault origin (80.6%), the high rate of head-neck trauma (49.4%), the strong tendency to avoid reporting to official authorities (78.8%), and the presence of forensic recurrence (13.8%). These unique characteristics clearly reveal that forensic violence against individuals aged 75 and over is an intricate process that is often hidden, repetitive, and requires a multidisciplinary approach.

Elder abuse is a major public health problem that is frequently undiagnosed, untreated, or inadequately managed. It has been shown to be associated with various negative health and social outcomes in both developed and developing countries. Possessing a multifactorial etiology, this phenomenon involves complex dynamics interacting at individual, familial, and societal levels [10]. According to the WHO report, one in every six elderly individuals is exposed to at least one type of abuse during their lifetime [11]. The concept of elder abuse was first defined in the literature in the 1970s with the term “granny battering.” Epidemiological studies conducted in the more than thirty years since then have revealed that elder abuse is remarkably prevalent, especially in European societies. In a study conducted in the United States, one in ten participants reported being exposed to at least one type of elder abuse [12]. Center-based studies conducted in emergency departments in Turkey report higher rates of elder abuse. Altintop et al. reported the rate of physical abuse in elderly patients evaluated within the scope of forensic cases as 63% [4]. In our study, the assault rate was found to be higher than this value. It is thought that this may be due to advanced age constituting an additional risk factor for abuse, as our sample consisted of the ≥ 75 age group. In this age group, functional dependence, the need for care, cognitive decline, and social isolation significantly increase the risk of abuse [13].

The literature emphasizes that social isolation and increased interpersonal stress factors brought about by the necessity of living together at home lead to a significant increase in elder abuse [14]. The data obtained from our study support this situation, revealing that individuals sharing the same home environment or being in close social relationships are the fundamental elements increasing the risk of elder abuse.

In their study on abuse cases presenting to the emergency department, Rosen et al. reported that head-neck and upper extremity injuries constitute critical findings that may indicate abuse [15]. In our study, the fact that head-neck and upper extremity region traumas accounted for the majority of our cases is consistent with this research. Exposure of these regions to trauma is associated with the individual’s vulnerability or the unexpected nature of the attack. In this context, head-neck and upper extremity traumas detected during emergency department presentation in individuals aged 75 and over should absolutely be evaluated as an abuse alarm (red flag).

When taking a history from an elderly individual, especially in cases where abuse or neglect is suspected, it is important to conduct the interview both in the presence and absence of the caregiver separately. The presence of the caregiver in the room during the history-taking process may cause the patient to be hesitant about sharing information regarding possible maltreatment and to avoid expressing the situation [16]. In our study, “avoidance of reporting” behavior was detected with a high prevalence. According to the studies of Lachs and Pillemer [6], it is reported that the vast majority of elderly individuals tend to hide the abuse event because the perpetrator is often a family member. The World Health Organization (WHO) report [17] estimates that actual abuse cases are 24 times more frequent than those reported. These findings demonstrate that health professionals should approach anamnesis (history) data critically and place more weight on injury patterns, behavioral observations, and clinical evaluation/intuition during diagnosis and intervention processes.

Individuals with comorbidities are not only sensitive to traumatic events but are also in a more vulnerable position against abuse; because the existing need for care establishes a dependency relationship between them and the perpetrator [13]. This dependency relationship can function as a fundamental factor leading both to the chronicity/persistence of the violence and to the prevention of abuse reporting. The presence of comorbidities in most of the patients included in our study supports this observation.

Abused elderly individuals frequently present to healthcare institutions with traumatic lesions that are explained by an inadequate history or are inconsistent with the current clinical presentation. These findings include, in particular, unexplained or suspiciously localized abrasions, ecchymoses, lacerations, and burns [18]. Rosen et al. [16] also reported that a significant portion of traumas in elderly abuse victims develop in the form of superficial skin lesions (abrasion/ecchymosis) and that this injury pattern may show inconsistency with the accident history. Within the scope of our study, when the findings detected in individuals aged 75 and over were analyzed, the most frequently detected injury types were abrasion and ecchymosis, respectively. These findings support that trauma in elder abuse cases occurs largely through blunt trauma mechanisms. The higher frequency of ecchymosis in women suggests that abuse against women occurs more through blunt trauma mechanisms compared to men. Conversely, the statistically significant higher detection of lacerations in men indicates that injuries in the male gender may be more related to penetrant trauma mechanisms than in women. These findings reveal that injury patterns and trauma mechanisms may differ by gender.

Among the detected traumatic lesions, serious traumatic findings such as fractures and intracranial hemorrhages were also observed. According to the report by Atinga et al. [19], head, costal and upper extremity fractures in elderly individuals can be important indicators for non-accidental trauma (abuse). In our current study, the finding of a higher prevalence of fractures in the female population (9.7%) can be associated with etiological factors such as osteoporosis and increased physical fragility.

The fact that older adults use prescription and non-prescription drugs at a higher prevalence compared to younger adults increases the risk of harmful drug interactions, misuse, and abuse/maltreatment [20]; corresponding to this clinical picture, a lower frequency of intoxication and burn findings were also detected in our study.

The fact that the injury size was between 2 and 4 cm in a significant portion of the patients is consistent with the small but multiple and different-stage lesion patterns frequently defined in elderly physical abuse in the literature. Wiglesworth et al. reported that bruises in elderly people who were physically abused were mostly multiple, in different stages of healing, and tended to be larger in size than accidental bruises [21]. Therefore, the fact that the vast majority of injuries in our study were in the 2–4 cm range can be evaluated as a warning finding in terms of abuse, especially in cases that are unexplained or inconsistent with the history.

Furthermore, it was observed in our study that presentations to the emergency department occurred most frequently between 16:00 and 00:00. This situation suggests that the abuse occurs during the day and the victim finds the opportunity to apply to a health institution in the time interval when he/she moves away from family members or the environment. These data show that when there is a discrepancy between the history and clinical findings, the time of presentation is also an important parameter in forensic evaluation. Our findings reveal that, in addition to the type or localization of the injury, the time of presentation and lesion size can also be critical indicators in forensic awareness. Therefore, in the evaluation of the trauma pattern of elderly individuals, not only the location of the lesion but also its size, number, healing stage, and presentation timing should be considered together.

These data reveal that the forensic trauma approach in very elderly individuals must include forensic-social-psychological components, distinguishing it from standard trauma management. In light of these findings, clinical evaluation in the emergency department should not be limited to questioning the presence of a lesion ; on the contrary, critical parameters such as the injury pattern, anatomical localization, stage of healing, and consistency with the provided anamnesis (history) should be analyzed in a detailed and comprehensive manner.

In conclusion, the vast majority of non-traffic forensic traumas observed in individuals aged 75 and over are of assault origin, and the pattern of these traumas is of a nature to raise a high level of suspicion of abuse. Head-neck traumas and the anamnesis inconsistency that arises especially in cases where reporting is concealed constitute an absolute alarm finding for the clinician. Therefore, early recognition of elder abuse in emergency departments, its multidisciplinary management, and the effective strengthening of mandatory reporting mechanisms are fundamental necessities.

Lımıtatıons

The fact that the study was conducted in a single center limits the generalizability of the findings to centers with different cultural and sociodemographic characteristics.

One of the significant limitations of our study is the relatively small sample size. The fact that Turkey has a younger population structure compared to European countries and the United States may be one of the factors limiting the general presentation rates of the patient population aged 75 and over. In addition, the fact that our hospital is a second-tier health institution and is preferred mostly by young and clinically stable patients may have contributed to the relatively low number of patients in the ≥ 75 age group within the scope of the study. Furthermore, the high number of patients under the age of 75 and traffic accident cases excluded from the study is noteworthy. The central location of our hospital in the city center and its easy accessibility for transportation facilitate patient admissions by outpatient or ambulance; this situation may have affected the patient distribution by increasing the number of admissions, especially of young and trauma patients.

Due to the retrospective design of the study, standardized and systematic data regarding the presence of family members during the inquiry for each case could not be obtained from emergency department records. However, considering the cognitive status and physical dependence of very elderly patients in emergency department practice, it is known that family members usually accompany the patient during the initial evaluation. It has been anticipated that the presence of family members at the time of evaluation may prevent the patient from making free statements in cases of potential abuse. In future studies, to obtain healthier data regarding abuse, it is recommended to implement a standard evaluation protocol that includes interviewing the patient alone and independently of family members whenever possible.

The inclusion of only presentations recorded as “forensic cases” in the hospital automation system may have caused potential abuse cases that were not reflected in the forensic process or were hidden by the patient to be excluded from the study. Therefore, although the actual prevalence of abuse may be higher, it could not be measured. In the study, the perpetrator of the abuse, the victim-perpetrator relationship, and the socio-environmental conditions in which the incident occurred could not be evaluated; as this information is not always documented in the forensic system, it could not be included in the analysis. Furthermore, the lack of a standardized elder abuse screening scale in the emergency department led to clinical suspicion-based evaluations being subjective.

Although outcomes such as injury severity, hospitalizations, and mortality could be evaluated, long-term functional outcomes (loss of mobility, quality of life, need for institutional care) could not be followed. Therefore, the long-term effects of serious injuries such as fractures and intracranial hemorrhages could not be determined.

Despite these limitations, the study provides a significant contribution to the literature in terms of revealing the clinical and forensic characteristics of non-traffic forensic cases specific to the ≥ 75 very elderly group and providing a detailed analysis of presentation behaviors. Future prospective, multi-center studies using standardized abuse screening methods are necessary.
