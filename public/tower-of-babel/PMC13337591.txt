Giving Antibiotics a Second Chance: Evolutionary Trade-Offs and Phage-Driven Restoration of Antibiotic Susceptibility
Authors: Michał Wójcicki, Martyna Cieślik, Andrzej Górski, Ewa Jończyk-Matysiak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Antimicrobial resistance poses a critical and escalating threat to global public health, driven by the widespread and often unjustified use of antibiotics and the rapid dissemination of resistance determinants. With the antibiotic discovery pipeline largely depleted, alternative and complementary strategies are urgently needed to preserve the effectiveness of existing antimicrobials. Bacteriophages—viruses that specifically infect bacteria—have re-emerged as promising tools not only for direct bacterial eradication but also for reshaping bacterial evolutionary trajectories. This review examines the concept of phage-driven restoration of antibiotic susceptibility, focusing on evolutionary trade-offs that arise when bacteria adapt to phage pressure. Resistance to bacteriophages frequently involves modifications of surface structures, capsules, or efflux systems, changes that often incur fitness costs manifested as reduced virulence, impaired biofilm formation, or increased antibiotic sensitivity. Experimental studies and clinical case reports demonstrate that phage–antibiotic synergy can suppress bacterial growth more effectively than monotherapy, limit resistance emergence, and resensitize multidrug-resistant pathogens to previously ineffective antibiotics. Particular attention is given to mechanisms involving efflux pump targeting, capsule loss, biofilm disruption, and temperate phage–antibiotic interactions. In addition, emerging strategies that combine bacteriophages with CRISPR-Cas systems enable precise targeting and removal of resistance genes, offering a highly selective means to restore antibiotic efficacy and curb horizontal gene transfer. Together, these findings highlight bacteriophages as powerful evolutionary and therapeutic tools capable of giving antibiotics a “second chance”. Integrating phage-based approaches into antibiotic stewardship frameworks may represent a sustainable path forward in combating multidrug-resistant bacterial infections.

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) has emerged as one of the most serious threats to global public health, compromising the effectiveness of antibiotics that have formed the foundation of modern medicine for decades [1]. In Europe, AMR is estimated to be directly responsible for approximately 133,000 deaths annually and may contribute indirectly to an additional 41,000 deaths. The resulting burden on healthcare systems, together with losses in workforce productivity, generates annual costs of approximately €11.7 billion. The growing ineffectiveness of antibiotics, combined with the limited development of new antimicrobial agents, has intensified the need for alternative and complementary therapeutic strategies [2–4].

The current antibiotic resistance crisis is caused by multiple interrelated factors, including the excessive and inappropriate use of antibiotics in human medicine, agriculture, and animal husbandry. These activities promote the spread of antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) in the environment [5, 6]. Horizontal gene transfer (HGT) further accelerates the spread of resistance genes between bacterial species [7, 8]. Mechanisms such as conjugation, transformation, and transduction enable rapid dissemination of ARGs across diverse bacterial populations. Mobile genetic elements, including plasmids, integrons, transposons, and bacteriophages, play a crucial role in this process [9, 10]. As a result, resistance determinants can rapidly spread across bacterial communities, leading to the emergence of highly resistant pathogens in both clinical and environmental settings.

These interconnected drivers of antimicrobial resistance are best understood within the One Health framework, which recognizes the close relationships between human health, animal health, and environmental ecosystems [11–13]. The One Health approach emphasizes that antibiotic resistance does not arise solely in clinical settings but develops and spreads across multiple interconnected environments. Antibiotic use in agriculture, environmental contamination with pharmaceutical residues, and global movement of humans, animals, and food products collectively contribute to the evolution and dissemination of resistant bacteria. Consequently, effective strategies to combat antimicrobial resistance must integrate interventions across medical, veterinary, agricultural, and environmental sectors [5, 14–16].

In response to this global challenge, increasing attention has been directed toward alternative therapeutic strategies capable of restoring the effectiveness of existing antibiotics. Among these, bacteriophages—viruses that specifically infect bacteria—have re-emerged as promising tools [2–4]. Beyond their direct antibacterial activity, bacteriophages can influence bacterial evolution by imposing selective pressure that leads to fitness trade-offs. These evolutionary trade-offs may result in reduced bacterial virulence, impaired biofilm formation, altered membrane permeability, and, importantly, restoration of antibiotic susceptibility [17, 18].

This review focuses on the concept of phage-driven restoration of antibiotic susceptibility, emphasizing evolutionary trade-offs that arise during phage–bacteria interactions. Particular attention is given to mechanisms involving efflux pumps, capsule modification, biofilm disruption, and CRISPR-based strategies. Understanding these interactions may help develop innovative therapeutic approaches aligned with the One Health framework and contribute to sustainable solutions for combating antimicrobial resistance.

Bacteriophages and Antibiotics—A Brief History

Bacteriophages (or phages) were discovered relatively recently, at the turn of the twentieth century. Their history can be traced back to 1896, when British bacteriologist Ernest Hankin observed the antibacterial properties of water from the Yamuna and Ganges rivers in India [19–23]. He noted that this water inhibited the growth of Vibrio cholerae, suggesting the presence of an unknown antibacterial agent. Two years later, in 1898, Russian bacteriologist Nikolay Gamaleya, along with several other researchers, observed a similar phenomenon in Bacillus subtilis [21, 24]. The nature of this phenomenon was further investigated in the early twentieth century. In 1915, Frederick Twort, an English bacteriologist, described a “glassy transformation” of bacterial colonies, which he attributed to an infectious agent capable of passing through filters [25, 26]. Two years later, in 1917, Félix d’Herelle, a French-Canadian bacteriologist, independently reported the existence of similar bacterial lytic agents and proposed that surface waters contained entities capable of infecting and destroying bacteria. He referred to this phenomenon as an “epizootic of bacteria” and introduced the term “bacteriophage”, defining it as an invisible, filterable, self-replicating obligate parasite of bacteria [27, 28]. Initially, the true nature of bacteriophages was the subject of intense debate. Their resistance to heat and their presence in bacterial cultures led some scientists to propose alternative explanations, including the idea that phages were catalytic agents activating bacterial pro-ferments or that bacterial lysis resulted from autolysis caused by metabolic imbalance. Others suggested that bacteriophages were enzymes or gene-mediated lytic factors rather than independent biological entities. Despite these controversies, d’Herelle strongly advocated the viral nature of bacteriophages and pioneered their use in medicine. In the early twentieth century, he conducted the first successful therapeutic applications of phages, notably in the treatment of bacillary dysentery and typhoid fever [21, 27]. Later, d’Herelle undertook several journeys during which he applied phage therapy to treat hemorrhagic fever in cattle caused by Pasteurella multocida in Indochina, bubonic plague in four patients aboard a ship passing through the Suez Canal, and for the prevention and treatment of cholera in India between 1927 and 1931 [27].

However, interest in phage therapy declined in much of the Western world following the discovery of penicillin in 1928 by Alexander Fleming (a Scottish physician), which marked the beginning of the antibiotic era and overshadowed the further development of phage-based treatments [21, 23, 29, 30]. Nevertheless, for the purposes of the present discussion, it is important to note that the first cases of penicillinase-dependent resistance to penicillin in Escherichia coli strains were documented as early as 1940 [31]. The period between 1950 and 1970 is widely regarded as the ‘golden age’ of antibiotic discovery, during which approximately half of the antibiotics in use today were identified. Since that time, however, no fundamentally new classes of antibiotics have been discovered [32, 33].

Antibiotics: Mechanisms of Action, Specificity, and Bacterial Resistance

Antibacterial drugs comprise a diverse group of compounds. Initially, the term ‘antibiotics’ was limited to naturally occurring metabolites produced by microorganisms, whereas fully synthetic agents lacking natural counterparts were referred to as chemotherapeutics [34]. In contemporary usage, however, the term ‘antibiotics’ encompasses both natural and synthetic antibacterial agents. These drugs can be classified according to their chemical structure, mode and mechanism of action, spectrum of activity, and route of administration, such as oral, intravenous, or topical delivery [34, 35]. Compounds with similar molecular structures frequently exhibit comparable efficacy, toxicity, and side-effect profiles [34, 36]. The introduction of new antibiotics to treat bacterial infections has inadvertently contributed to the emergence of multidrug-resistant bacterial strains. Acquired resistance arises from genetic changes in bacteria, which may result from spontaneous mutations caused by DNA polymerase errors or from the activity of transposable elements. Bacteria can also acquire resistance genes through HGT, a process involving the exchange of genetic material via conjugation, transformation, or transduction. Several factors facilitate the acquisition of antibiotic resistance, including (a) mobile genetic elements such as plasmids, bacteriophages, and integron resistance cassettes; (b) genetic mechanisms like homologous recombination, site-specific recombination, and transposition; and (c) selective pressure from antibiotic use, which favours the survival and proliferation of resistant strains.

Expert groups from the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control and the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention classify acquired antibiotic resistance as follows: (a) Multidrug-resistant (MDR), defined as resistance to at least one antibiotic in three or more classes of antimicrobial agents; (b) Extensively drug-resistant (XDR), defined as resistance to at least one agent in all but two or fewer antibiotic classes used to treat infections; and (c) Pandrug-resistant, defined as resistance to all approved antibiotics across all categories for the treatment of infections caused by the microorganism [60–62].

Bacteria have evolved multiple mechanisms to evade antibiotics, including (a) modifying the drug’s target site to reduce binding efficiency; (b) decreasing membrane permeability to limit drug entry; (c) producing enzymes that chemically inactivate the antibiotic; (d) activating efflux pumps to expel the drug; (e) bypassing the drug’s target via alternative metabolic pathways or enzymes; and (f) synthesizing protective proteins that shield essential cellular components [63, 64]. It is worth noting that bacteria are constantly developing increasingly sophisticated mechanisms to escape the action of antibiotics and the host's immune system. In 2024, the World Health Organization released an updated list of drug-resistant bacteria posing the greatest threat to human health, aiming to guide the development of new treatments and strategies to prevent and contain AMR [65].

Irrational Antibiotic Stewardship Policies Have Led to the Acquisition of Resistance by Bacteria to These Chemotherapeutic Agents

Inappropriate antibiotic stewardship (AMS) has significantly contributed to the emergence of the post-antibiotic era [66–68]. The efficacy of antibiotics in treating bacterial infections is declining, with the increasing prevalence of antibiotic resistance closely linked to the environmental presence of ARGs [69]. These genes can be mobilized into pathogenic bacteria via HGT [8, 70]. Conjugation, a principal mechanism for ARG dissemination, can circumvent conventional genetic barriers to gene transfer, and certain conjugative elements even facilitate the exchange of resistance plasmids across distantly related bacterial species [8].

The rapid dissemination of ARGs is primarily driven by the excessive use of antibiotics in agriculture and HGT [69]. For instance, the application of streptomycin in the 1950s to control Erwinia amylovora on fruit trees led to the development of resistance; streptomycin remains approved in some non-European Union countries, including Israel, Canada, Mexico, and New Zealand. Agricultural antibiotic use far exceeds medical usage—approximately 80% of antibiotic sales in the United States are intended for agricultural purposes [71]. Resistant bacteria originating from treated animals are released into the environment via faeces, contaminating manure-fertilized fields and aquatic ecosystems [72]. Since then, ARGs have been detected in meat and minimally processed plant-based foods. The dairy industry also contributes to the environmental dissemination of ARGs [8].

Although antibiotics have revolutionized the treatment of bacterial infections, their inappropriate and unjustified use has contributed to the rapid emergence of resistant strains. Antibiotic policies that are not grounded in current scientific evidence play a significant role in the development and dissemination of these strains. Unwarranted prescribing for viral infections, the use of broad-spectrum antibiotics when narrower-spectrum agents would suffice, inadequate monitoring of treatment duration, and the absence of proper diagnostic procedures all contribute to the selection of resistant bacteria and a reduction in therapeutic efficacy [73–77].

The consequences of uncontrolled antibiotic use are severe, including an increase in hard-to-treat infections, prolonged hospital stays, higher healthcare costs, and elevated mortality among patients with resistant infections [78–80]. Notable examples include MRSA, which emerged from the excessive use of β-lactams in hospitals; fluoroquinolone-resistant E. coli, resulting from widespread use of these drugs in urinary tract infections; and carbapenemase-producing Klebsiella pneumoniae, which arose due to the unjustified administration of carbapenems [81–83].

In animal production, antibiotics, in addition to treating diseases and preventing infections, were commonly used as growth promoters. Their excessive and inappropriate use has significantly contributed to the increase in MDR strains in the environment. For this reason, many countries have introduced regulations restricting the use of antibiotics as growth promoters in animal husbandry. In response, the EU banned the use of antibiotics as growth promoters in 2006 [84, 85], and antibiotic resistance now occupies a leading position in EU public health programs [86]. Member states are required to develop national action plans. The complete elimination of all resistance genes is not feasible, as some also perform functions unrelated to AMR. Therefore, efforts should focus on controlling the emergence, selection, and dissemination of these genes, particularly in bacteria interacting with humans, animals, and plants. In the face of rising resistance, responsible AMS based on scientific evidence, monitoring of drug usage, and education of both physicians and patients are essential to limit the development of resistant strains and preserve the effectiveness of antibiotics for future generations.

Researchers are actively investigating strategies to prevent HGT. One promising approach integrates principles of developmental biology, evolution, and ecology to curb the spread of AMR. Increasing attention is also being given to the use of bacteriophages as a potential solution against MDR strains [87–89].

Phage–Antibiotic Synergy

The combined application of bacteriophages and antibiotics may induce advantageous evolutionary trade-offs, arising from adaptive costs, in which bacterial resistance to one selective pressure occurs at the expense of susceptibility to another, such as antibiotic [90]. The combined strategy may not only enhance therapeutic efficacy and durability through phage–antibiotic synergy (PAS), but also help limit the dissemination of antibiotic resistance by restoring bacterial susceptibility to antibacterial agents. Consequently, such approaches may broaden available treatment options while enabling reductions in the effective doses of antibiotics required [91]. Optimizing phage–antibiotic combinations may thus represent a critical strategy in the management of bacterial infections.

At the same time, the emergence of phage resistance in bacterial strains has been reported relatively frequently (usually 10−5–10−7), similarly to resistance to antibiotics [2]. The development of this phenomenon in vitro depends on the type of bacterial cells used, whereas in animal models it is influenced both by the model system and the type of infection [92]. Importantly, resistance to bacteriophages is often associated with evolutionary trade-offs, including attenuated virulence, restored susceptibility to antibiotics, enhanced recognition by immune system cells, and/or reduced competitive fitness relative to native microbiome species. Recent compassionate-use applications of personalized phage therapy have monitored the occurrence of phage resistance in clinical settings [3].

The development of phage cocktails containing phages that differ in their lytic spectrum and that target distinct bacterial receptors for infection may help to avoid the emergence of phage resistance [93]. Moreover, careful evaluation of phage activity within a cocktail is also required, as different phage–phage interactions may be observed depending on whether the application occurs in vitro or in vivo. Genetic trade-offs is a phenomenon that occurs by natural selection when an advantageous trait evolves to solve an environmental challenge, such as phage infection, at the cost of reduced performance in another, non-selected trait [94].

Bacteria can develop resistance to bacteriophages through the modification or loss of surface receptors, including lipopolysaccharide (LPS), outer membrane proteins (OMPs), or flagella. These alterations may reduce the ability to interact with the host, often resulting in decreased virulence or impaired colonization capacity [90, 95, 96]. Such adaptive mutations frequently impose fitness costs and exert pleiotropic effects, as changes conferring phage resistance can simultaneously affect multiple bacterial traits.

Mechanisms of Phage-Driven Restoration of Antibiotic Susceptibility

Bacteriophages can restore bacterial susceptibility to antibiotics through multiple evolutionary and physiological mechanisms. These include targeting bacterial surface structures involved in resistance, modifying efflux pump activity, altering capsule synthesis, disrupting biofilms, and influencing gene expression (Fig. 1). Such phage-driven evolutionary trade-offs frequently impose fitness costs on bacteria, resulting in reduced virulence and increased antibiotic sensitivity. Phage-Driven Evolutionary Trade-Offs and Restoration of Antibiotic Susceptibility

Phage-driven adaptive trade-offs frequently result in reduced bacterial fitness and increased antibiotic susceptibility. Zhang and Ahn (2025), building on earlier findings by Laure and Ahn (2022), reported adaptive trade-offs in Salmonella enterica subsp. enterica serovar Typhimurium (S. Typhimurium) in vitro between resistance to bacteriophages and resistance to antibiotics. Bacteriophage-insensitive strains exhibited reduced motility, diminished biofilm-forming capacity, and increased antibiotic susceptibility [90, 97]. Furthermore, combined treatment with bacteriophages and antibiotics suppressed the growth of both bacteriophage-sensitive and bacteriophage-insensitive variants more efficiently than antibiotic monotherapy alone. These phenotypic alterations are likely to affect bacterial pathogenic potential by impairing traits associated with host colonization and persistence. Moreover, bacteriophage-insensitive strains displayed enhanced sensitivity to several antibiotics, including meropenem, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, cefotaxime, ceftriaxone, kanamycin, ciprofloxacin, chloramphenicol, and levofloxacin. These findings suggest that bacteriophage-driven selection may be exploited to resensitize bacteria to antibiotics, offering a promising strategy for the control of MDR bacterial pathogens.

Similarly, coevolution of multidrug-resistant Salmonella anatum (according to the current nomenclature: Salmonella enterica subsp. enterica serovar Anatum, S. Anatum) with phage JNwz02 over 60 days (30 passages) led to an antagonistic trade-off whereby phage resistance was associated with reduced competitiveness and a partial loss in resistance to amoxicillin, ampicillin, and gentamicin [98]. These findings suggest that phage selection pressure can shape the evolutionary dynamics between phage resistance and antibiotic resistance, with trade-offs being more pronounced during early arms-race stages of coevolution.

Capsule Modification and Loss of Virulence Factors

In another study with phage-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii mutants, genetic analysis revealed mutations within the K locus including the disruption of gtr29 in A. baumannii strain AB900 and gpi in A. baumannii strain A9844 [99]. Both genes are involved in capsule biosynthesis, a major virulence determinant in A. baumannii [100]. Loss of the capsule impaired bacteriophage adsorption, thereby conferring phage resistance. Capsule deficiency was accompanied by antibiotic resensitization, as observed in bacteria along with multiple phenotypic changes, including reduced biofilm formation, increased susceptibility to human complement, β-lactam antibiotics, and heterologous phages, as well as attenuated virulence in vivo.

Notably, the ΦF G02-R AB900 mutant (resistant to phage ΦFG02) displayed a 16-fold reduction in the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of ceftazidime compared with the wild-type strain, restoring clinical susceptibility, along with a two-fold decrease in MIC values for other β-lactams and the fluoroquinolone (ciprofloxacin). Similarly, the ΦCO01-R A9844 mutant (resistant to phage ΦCO01) exhibited two-fold reductions in MICs for minocycline, meropenem, cefepime, and ampicillin-sulbactam. Importantly, the decreased MICs for cefepime and ampicillin-sulbactam resulted in a shift in clinical classification, rendering A. baumannii strain A9844 susceptible to these antibiotics [99].

A similar mechanism was observed in a 68-year-old diabetic patient with necrotizing pancreatitis complicated by MDR A. baumannii infection [101]. In this case, bacteriophages were administered intravenously and percutaneously into the abscess cavities, which was associated with increased bacterial sensitivity to minocycline. A personalized combination phage cocktail active against A. baumannii patients’ isolate was subsequently evaluated in combination with varying concentrations of minocycline to assess its impact on antibiotic activity at subinhibitory levels. Although the bacteriophage cocktail itself lost lytic activity against the bacterium, it prevented the emergence of bacterial populations with increased resistance to minocycline during prolonged in vitro culture. The authors described this effect as an additive interaction between the bacteriophages and sub-MIC concentrations of minocycline when applied against phage-resistant A. baumannii isolate. Interestingly, the antimicrobial activity of minocycline was sustained for several weeks when the antibiotic was introduced 5 days after initiation of bacteriophage therapy. By day 115 of treatment, the A. baumannii isolate exhibited restored susceptibility to minocycline, allowing its inclusion in the therapeutic regimen. Subsequent mechanistic studies demonstrated that the phages within the cocktail targeted bacterial capsules composed of polysaccharide cell envelope structures [102]. Comparative analysis of isolates obtained before and after phage therapy revealed that phage resistance evolved at the expense of capsule biosynthesis, highlighting a fitness trade-off associated with phage adaptation. After one month of antibiotic therapy, the graft remained sterile and antibiotic treatment was discontinued.

Efflux Pump Targeting and Membrane Transport Modification

An important mechanism underlying phage-mediated antibiotic resensitization involves bacterial transmembrane efflux pumps, which may serve as phage receptors while simultaneously mediating antibiotic resistance [103]. Mutations leading to phage-resistant mutants may impair transport of antibiotics from bacterial cells, resulting in prolonged intracellular drug concentrations and increased antibiotic effectiveness.

For example, Pseudomonas aeruginosa-specific OMKO1 phage adsorbs the outer membrane porin M (OprM), a component of the multi-drug efflux systems MexAB and MexXY [104]. The acquisition of phage resistance may modify the functions of the porin, resulting in impairing antibiotic export from the cell. Notably, P. aeruginosa clinical isolates resistant to OMKO1 phage exhibited up to 50-fold increase in sensitivity to ciprofloxacin, tetracycline, ceftazidime, and erythromycin both in vitro and in vivo (animal model with the use of Galleria mellonella) experiments [104, 105].

A study demonstrated that prolonged suppressive antibiotic therapy following implantation of a Dacron aortic arch prosthesis, failed to eradicate the infection. The patient developed a chronic aortocutaneous fistula with an open sinus tract leading to the graft site, from which P. aeruginosa was continuously isolated despite extended parenteral treatment with ceftazidime [106]. In this patient, a lytic bacteriophage OMKO1 was applied, targeting the outer membrane protein M of the MexAB- and MexXY-multidrug efflux systems in P. aeruginosa [104]. The phage was locally administered via injection into the abscess cavity along the chronic aortocutaneous fistula. Phage-mediated selection against these efflux pumps resulted in increased antibiotic susceptibility, with a two-fold reduction in resistance to ceftazidime and a ten-fold increase in susceptibility to ciprofloxacin. Additionally, phage OMKO1 exhibited strong biofilm-disrupting activity against bacterial communities formed on the implanted prosthetic material.

Modification of Surface Receptors (LPS, OmpA, Pili, Flagella)

Phage resistance commonly arises through mutations in bacterial receptors such as the efflux pump protein TolC or structural barrier molecule LPS [107]. As these structures also contribute to antibiotic resistance (e.g., via drug efflux or membrane integrity), their alteration may increase antibiotic susceptibility. However, due to pleiotropic effects, some mutations instead enhance resistance, resulting in ‘trade-ups’ rather than trade-offs. Bacteriophage resistance acquisition in Shigella flexneri leads to reduced virulence [108]. Mutants resistant to A1-1 phage, which uses OmpA as a receptor, exhibited, among others, attenuated intercellular spread, altered cell membrane permeability, as well as increased susceptibility to antibiotics and other bacteriophages.

Phages that exploit virulence factors as receptors—such as those binding to flagella [109–111] or pili [110–112]—can drive the evolution of resistance that impairs the function of these structures, thereby reducing bacterial virulence [113]. Impairment of bacterial virulence may manifest to a greater extent as reduced adherence and colonization, diminished host invasion, and a decreased ability to evade the host immune system [94].

Reduction of Virulence and Fitness Costs Associated with Phage Resistance

Selection for phage resistance can impose fitness costs that attenuate bacterial virulence, highlighting a potential advantage of phage therapy beyond direct bacterial killing [114]. For example, phage-resistant P. aeruginosa isolates have evolved during both laboratory experiments and nasal phage treatment, exhibiting reduced virulence in vivo, a phenotype associated with slower growth rates. Interestingly, selection for phage resistance in the plant-pathogenic bacterium Ralstonia solanacearum leads to trade-offs between phage resistance and virulence, regardless of growth temperature (at 25 °C and 35 °C) [115]. It involved multiple pre- and post-infection defense mechanisms, including mutations in phcS and pilD. Phage-resistant bacteria exhibited markedly reduced infectivity and pathogenicity in plant experiments.

The phage steering concept refers to the deliberate use of bacteriophages to guide bacterial evolution in a therapeutically beneficial direction, rather than solely killing bacteria. By imposing selective pressure, phages can drive bacteria to acquire fitness costs, such as reduced virulence, slower growth, or increased antibiotic susceptibility, rendering them less harmful and more treatable. García-Cruz and co-workers (2024) demonstrated that phage-resistant P. aeruginosa populations became both less virulent and less antibiotic-resistant. Consistent with this, phage resistance can directly impact virulence factor production; resistant strains exhibited decreased biofilm formation, swarming motility, and type III secretion (T3SS), resulting in dramatically attenuated virulence in G. mellonella. This example highlights the evolutionary benefits of phage therapy that could be leveraged to develop more effective treatment strategies [116].

Biofilm Disruption and Increased Antibiotic Penetration

Phage therapy may also restore antibiotic susceptibility through biofilm disruption. For example, bacteriophage OMKO1 demonstrated strong biofilm-disrupting activity against P. aeruginosa communities formed on implanted prosthetic material [106].

Similarly, bacteriophage-insensitive S. enterica strains exhibited diminished biofilm-forming capacity, which may contribute to increased antibiotic susceptibility [90, 97].

Additional information on the effect of phages in loosening biofilm structure and facilitating antibiotic activity is presented in Section 9.

Changes in Gene Expression and Bacterial Physiology

Proteomic analysis of an extended spectrum beta-lactamase-positive E. coli strain revealed significant changes in protein expression, with 65 proteins upregulated and 246 proteins downregulated. The proteins with reduced expression were primarily involved in nucleic acid, protein, and lipid metabolism, and iron acquisition. Phage treatment also increased bacterial cell membrane permeability. Consequently, phage-infected bacteria exhibited enhanced susceptibility to multiple classes of antibiotics, including beta-lactams, fluoroquinolones, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, and aminoglycosides [117].

Genetically Engineered Bacteriophages

An important milestone in phage therapy was the first reported case of the use of genetically engineered bacteriophages against mycobacterial pathogens. This approach was applied in a young woman with cystic fibrosis after bilateral orthotopic lung transplantation who had a chronic Mycobacterium abscessus pulmonary infection. A personalized phage cocktail, targeting the M. abscessus strain isolated from the patient, was administered and contributed to clinical improvement [118].

Because isolation of strictly lytic mycobacteriophages is challenging, genetic engineering was employed to convert temperate phages to lytic ones by deleting repressor gene using bacteriophage recombineering of electroporated DNA, as previously reported by Marinelli and co-workers [119].

Using Bacteriophages and CRISPR Systems to Restore Bacterial Sensitivity to Antibiotics

In the face of the growing problem of antibiotic resistance, which, as previously mentioned, arises from mutations in bacterial genes, efflux pump activity, production of drug-neutralizing enzymes, and HGT, the development of novel therapeutic strategies capable of restoring the efficacy of existing antibiotics has become increasingly crucial [120, 121]. Traditional antibiotics act non-selectively, exerting strong selective pressure and promoting the emergence of resistant strains, thereby limiting their long-term clinical effectiveness. In this context, biological tools such as bacteriophages and programmable CRISPR-Cas (clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats—CRISPR-associated protein) systems offer promising approaches for the targeted removal of resistance determinants or modulation of resistance gene expression, thereby restoring bacterial susceptibility to previously ineffective antibiotics [122–124].

Bacteriophages, viruses that specifically infect bacteria, have long been considered an alternative or complement to antibiotic therapy, particularly against MDR strains. These phages can destroy bacteria through the lytic cycle, resulting in cell lysis. Additionally, they can be genetically engineered as delivery vectors for specific DNA sequences into bacterial cells. Such engineered phage-CRISPR systems enable precise targeting of resistance genes—both chromosomal and plasmid-borne—resulting in their cleavage by CRISPR nucleases and elimination, ultimately resensitizing bacteria to antibiotics that were previously ineffective [122, 125].

For example, studies on E. coli have shown that temperate bacteriophages programmed to deliver a CRISPR-Cas9 system targeting antibiotic resistance genes effectively eliminated plasmids carrying resistance determinants, leading to a significant reduction in the number of bacteria exhibiting resistant phenotypes. In vitro, engineered phages reduced populations of resistant strains by >5–6 orders of magnitude compared with controls and blocked plasmid transfer between cells, significantly limiting further dissemination of resistance [126].

In animal models, such phage-CRISPR systems demonstrated promising therapeutic activity, reducing bacterial load in the host and lowering levels of resistance genes. Importantly, strategies based on temperate phages equipped with CRISPR-Cas not only eliminated resistance mechanisms but also restricted the emergence of new mutational forms of resistance, which is often observed with conventional lytic phages or antibiotics [126].

The mechanism of action involves the delivery of CRISPR-Cas by the phage into the bacterial cell, where crRNA sequences guide the Cas9 enzyme (or other CRISPR systems) to recognize and cleave specific resistance genes. The resulting double-strand DNA breaks lead to degradation of the targeted genetic elements, which may result in the elimination of plasmids carrying resistance genes or bacterial cell death if the target gene is essential for survival [127].

In addition to gene cleavage, CRISPR can also be applied to regulate gene expression, allowing suppression of resistance mechanisms without removing the DNA sequences. Such approaches, referred to as CRISPR interference, can inhibit the expression of genes encoding resistance mechanisms, such as efflux pumps, increasing intracellular antibiotic concentrations and restoring drug efficacy [123].

Phage-CRISPR strategies also have the potential to be integrated with other forms of therapy, such as conventional antibiotic treatment, producing synergistic effects—removal of genetic resistance enhances bacterial susceptibility to standard drugs while simultaneously reducing the risk of developing new resistant strains [123].

Despite promising results, technical and biological challenges must be addressed before the widespread clinical application of such therapies. These include the limited host range of many phages, potential development of phage resistance, risks of HGT or bacterial resistance to CRISPR systems, and the need for precise design and control of CRISPR-phage delivery systems [22, 93, 128].

In summary, the combination of bacteriophages and CRISPR-Cas systems represents an advanced biological strategy enabling targeted elimination of antibiotic resistance determinants, reduction of HGT of resistance genes, and restoration of bacterial drug susceptibility. These methods have the potential to complement or replace traditional antibiotics, particularly against MDR bacterial pathogens, and may constitute a significant component of future therapies against bacterial infections.

Non-CRISPR-Cas-Dependent Mechanisms with a Focus on Temperate Phages

Prophages integrated into bacterial genomes often have a beneficial effect in bacterial pathogenicity, for example, by encoding toxin genes, antibiotic resistance determinants, or factors conferring resistance to harsh environmental conditions [129]. On the other hand, the use of prophages to restore antibiotic susceptibility in bacteria often relies on mechanisms in which the resistance gene remains present in the genome, while the resistance phenotype is functionally suppressed, resulting in renewed sensitivity to the antibiotic, as mentioned before [114]. A significant discovery regarding the potential use of temperate phages encoded in bacterial genomes was the description of the ability of certain classes of antibiotics to induce the switch from lysogeny to lysis. This phenomenon resulted in a pronounced phage–antibiotic synergistic effect against E. coli and significantly enhanced bacterial load reduction. It has been suggested that the effective killing of the bacterial host may result not only from antibiotic-mediated induction of dormant phages, but also from the inhibition of their transition into the dormant (lysogenic) state [130]. Similar effects were observed for temperate phages isolated from P. aeruginosa strains, which were associated with reduction in the MIC of antibiotics by up to several orders of magnitude, including in antibiotic-resistant strains [131]. In another approach, temperate bacteriophages may be employed to deliver, via lysogenic integration, the rpsL and gyrA sensitive genes, which dominantly restore susceptibility of E. coli strains to streptomycin and nalidixic acid, respectively [132].

It has also been described that in the presence of lytic phage, the expression of efflux pumps on the surface of Burkholderia thailandensis is reduced, resulting in the bacteria gaining better sensitivity to three major antibiotic classes (β-lactams, tetracyclines, and quinolones) used in infections caused by these bacterium. Phage-induced downregulation of genes associated with membrane transport of antibiotics was of particular importance in demonstrating the synergistic antibacterial effect of the combination of ciprofloxacin with phage [133]. The role of phages in this process is also explained by their ability to target components of efflux pumps when attaching to bacterial cells during infection [134]. This observation is noteworthy because bacteriophages often have been implicated in the horizontal transfer of genes encoding efflux pumps, a process that can contribute to the development and dissemination of antibiotic resistance [135]. In another report using a hypervirulent, K. pneumoniae-specific phage, single-cell Raman spectroscopy revealed that the combined application of phage and ceftazidime led to a reduction in bacterial metabolic activity, in contrast to the metabolic upregulation observed following phage treatment alone [136].

Notably, the synergistic interaction between antibiotics and bacteriophages has also been demonstrated from the opposite perspective—exposure of bacterial cells to sub-lethal concentrations of antibiotics induces morphological alterations (cell bloating and filamentation) that, in turn, enhance bacteriophage predation [137].

Increasing the Accessibility of Biofilms to Antibiotics by Phages

Bacteria forming biofilms may exhibit antibiotic resistance levels that are 10- to 1000-fold higher than those observed in their planktonic counterparts [138, 139]. This phenomenon is of particular concern in the hospital setting, where persistent bacterial biofilms on medical devices pose a significant clinical risk [140]. Although the specific molecular mechanisms underlying the restoration of antibiotic sensitivity in biofilm-forming, antibiotic-resistant bacteria remain unclear, multiple mechanisms of phage–biofilm interactions have been repeatedly demonstrated, which may contribute to biofilm destabilization and thereby facilitate antibiotic penetration and efficacy. Bacteriophages have developed various strategies to disrupt biofilms, including selective lysis of matrix-producing cells, enzymatic degradation of the extracellular polymeric substance (EPS), or induction of host-encoded depolymerases [141]. The importance of lytic enzymes encoded by phages is particularly emphasized [142]. Enzymatic components derived from bacteriophages, such as endolysins and depolymerases, have been shown to retain antibacterial and antibiofilm activity independently of bacterial resistance to phage infection or to conventional antimicrobials [143]. Importantly, they are capable of destroying, to some extent, the biofilm formed by bacteria resistant to the phage itself in standard tests [141]. As a consequence, dissolving the biofilms (e.g., by degrading the EPS structure and initially disrupting the bacterial cell membrane) facilitates access of antibiotics to the bacteria [144].

One interesting example of PAS is the use of gentamicin as an adjuvant for phage therapy of a dual-species (P. aeruginosa and Staphylococcus aureus) wound-infecting biofilm. An in vitro study described multiple and combined administration of both therapeutic components as a way to prevent the development of bacterial resistance [145]. The synergistic action of phage and ampicillin has also been described as the most effective in both prevention and degradation of biofilm formed by the Proteus mirabilis strain isolated from urinary tract infection [146]. Various combinations of bacteriophages with different antibiotics may also yield promising results in degrading biofilms formed by XDR A. baumannii strains [147]. However, it seems important to individually investigate the synergistic effects of specific phages with a broad panel of antibiotics, as well as to further elucidate the detailed molecular mechanisms that enable the combined anti-biofilm effects of both therapeutic approaches.

Limitations and Future Perspectives of Phage-Mediated Antibiotic Resensitization

Despite the tremendous potential and hope associated with the use of phages as an alternative to antibiotics, as well as the possibility of restoring bacterial sensitivity to conventional chemotherapeutics, it is important to remember the need for their responsible use. Improper and irrational use of bacteriophages, similar to the mismanagement of antibiotic stewardship, can lead to the development of phage resistance in bacteria (which we are already observing, e.g., adsorption inhibition through modification, masking or lack of receptors, blocking phage DNA injection through the superinfection exclusion system, abortive infection, restriction-modification or CRISPR-Cas system systems) [148], ecological disturbances, and, consequently, the risk of a “post-phage era” [149].

Conclusions

The accelerating global crisis of antimicrobial resistance demands innovative strategies that extend beyond the traditional paradigm of continuously discovering new antibiotics. As outlined in this review, bacteriophages offer a compelling and multifaceted approach to addressing this challenge—not only as direct antibacterial agents, but also as powerful evolutionary tools capable of reshaping bacterial susceptibility landscapes. By exploiting evolutionary trade-offs, phage-driven selection can impose fitness costs on bacteria, manifesting as reduced virulence, impaired colonization, altered membrane properties, and, critically, restored susceptibility to antibiotics.

Accumulating experimental and clinical evidence demonstrates that resistance to bacteriophages frequently arises through modifications of bacterial surface structures, efflux pumps, capsules, pili, or flagella—adaptations that often compromise mechanisms underlying antibiotic resistance. This phenomenon underpins phage–antibiotic synergy, whereby the combined or sequential application of phages and antibiotics enhances bacterial eradication, suppresses the emergence of resistance, and enables the reuse of previously ineffective antimicrobial agents at lower doses. Importantly, these effects extend beyond planktonic bacteria to biofilm-associated infections, in which phage-mediated biofilm disruption markedly improves antibiotic penetration and activity.

Beyond naturally occurring phage-bacteria interactions, advanced approaches integrating bacteriophages with CRISPR-Cas systems provide unprecedented precision in targeting resistance determinants. Such strategies enable selective elimination or silencing of resistance genes, limit HGT, and promote the long-term restoration of antibiotic efficacy, while minimizing collateral damage to commensal microbiota. Complementary non-CRISPR mechanisms involving temperate phages further expand the therapeutic landscape by modulating bacterial physiology and enhancing antibiotic susceptibility through prophage induction or interference with resistance-associated pathways.

Despite these promising advances, several challenges remain before phage-based resensitization strategies can be widely implemented in clinical practice. These include the narrow host range of many phages, the potential for phage resistance, regulatory and manufacturing hurdles, and the need for standardized protocols for phage–antibiotic combination therapies. Addressing these obstacles will require coordinated efforts across microbiology, evolutionary biology, clinical medicine, and regulatory science.

In conclusion, bacteriophages represent a powerful means of giving antibiotics a “second chance” by harnessing evolutionary trade-offs that favour therapeutic success. Rather than replacing antibiotics, phage-based strategies are poised to complement and revitalize existing antimicrobial arsenals. As our understanding of phage–bacteria–antibiotic interactions deepens, these approaches may become integral components of future precision antimicrobial therapies, offering a sustainable path forward in the fight against MDR bacterial infections.
