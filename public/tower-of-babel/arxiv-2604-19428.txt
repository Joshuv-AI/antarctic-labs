Directional Scattering-Induced Optical Forces on a Mie Particle near a Metal Interface
Semyon Borodulin, Natalia Kostina, Mihail Petrov

Abstract
Optical manipulation of Mie-resonant dielectric nanoparticles is strongly influenced by their enhanced scattering and multipolar response, which fundamentally modify the balance of optical forces. In this work, we study the optical forces acting on a resonant dielectric nanoparticle placed near a metal interface, where scattering occurs into both free-space and surface plasmon-polariton (SPP) channels. We show that the interference of electric and magnetic dipole moments leads to highly directional scattering in these channels, and the direction and magnitude of the scattering-induced force are directly linked to the angular directivity of the corresponding radiation channels. We show that in a cross-beam configuration, where the radiation-pressure contribution is suppressed, the optical force can be changed over almost 2 π \pi in a wide range of particle sizes, providing a route toward optical sorting of resonant nanoparticles.

INTRODUCTION

Figure 1
:
Directional scattering of a Mie-resonant nanoparticle near a metal interface into free-space and SPP channels generates a recoil optical force governed by momentum redistribution.

High-index dielectric nanoparticles supporting Mie resonances have become a central platform of modern nanophotonics. The coexistence of electric and magnetic multipolar modes in subwavelength particles enables low-loss control of scattering, near-field localization, and radiation directivity, underpinning applications in nonlinear optics, nanolasing, and biosensing
11
;
8
;
18
;
28
. Yet, compared with the rapid progress in these areas, the optical manipulation of Mie-resonant particles remains considerably less well developed.

This limitation originates from the very nature of resonant scattering. Near Mie resonances, dielectric nanoparticles exhibit large scattering cross-sections, so that radiation pressure and recoil compete with the restoring gradient force. As a result, stable trapping in conventional single-beam optical tweezers becomes more demanding and often requires beam engineering or interference-based configurations
1
;
12
. Recent studies further revealed that resonant particles can exhibit complex dynamics, including hopping between metastable positions and orbiting motion, even in simple trapping geometries
26
. In addition, the optical force can be actively tuned and even reversed in resonant systems, as demonstrated for phase-change nanoparticles with switchable attractive and repulsive regimes
13
. These results highlight the fundamentally multipolar and non-conservative nature of optical forces in the Mie regime.

From a practical perspective, an important unresolved problem is the sorting of resonant nanoparticles by size or spectral response. Conventional post-synthesis approaches, such as density-gradient separation of silicon colloids
7
, can reduce size dispersion but do not enable in-situ, light-driven sorting. Optical forces offer an alternative route, where strong scattering can be exploited as a resource for force engineering. This concept underlies directional optical sorting and force control based on electric–magnetic interference
19
;
25
;
27
.

Moreover, Mie-resonant nanoparticles exhibit highly directional scattering not only in free space
4
;
15
, but also in their coupling to surface plasmon-polariton (SPP) modes near metal interfaces
21
;
22
. Surface-wave-assisted optical manipulation has been extensively studied for effectively dipolar particles, where SPP excitation can generate pulling, trapping, or anti-trapping forces
17
;
6
. Here, we extend this approach to the regime of multipolar interference in resonant dielectric particles, where the interplay of electric and magnetic modes enables additional control over scattering channels and optical forces (see
1
), opening new opportunities for optical sorting.

2.1
Directional scattering by a resonant Mie particle

We consider a resonant dielectric nanoparticle placed at a distance
z_{0}
above a metal substrate.
Throughout this work, the particle response is restricted to the dipole regime, where the electromagnetic interaction is fully described by the induced electric and magnetic dipole moments

\mathsfbf{p}=\alpha_{E}\mathsfbf{E}_{\text{loc}},\qquad\mathsfbf{m}=\alpha_{H}\mathsfbf{H}_{\text{loc}},

(1)

with
\alpha_{E}
and
\alpha_{H}
determined by the first Mie scattering coefficients
2
. The local electromagnetic field acting on a particle is decomposed into the incident (
\mathsfbf{E}_{0},\mathsfbf{H}_{0}
) and scattered (
\mathsfbf{E}_{\text{sc}},\mathsfbf{H}_{\text{sc}}
) parts,

\displaystyle\mathsfbf{E}_{\text{tot}}

\displaystyle=\mathsfbf{E}_{0}+\mathsfbf{E}_{\text{sc}},

(2)

\displaystyle\mathsfbf{H}_{\text{tot}}

\displaystyle=\mathsfbf{H}_{0}+\mathsfbf{H}_{\text{sc}}.

(3)

In the following, we assume that the particle is excited by a plane wave, and the incident field includes the incident plane wave and the wave reflected from the interface. For dipoles located near a planar interface, the scattered fields are expressed through the dyadic Green’s functions:

\displaystyle\mathsfbf{E}_{\text{sc}}

\displaystyle=\frac{k^{2}}{\varepsilon_{0}}\mathsfbf{\hat{G}}_{\rm E}(\mathsfbf{r},\mathsfbf{r}_{0})\cdot\mathsfbf{p}+i\omega\mu_{0}\left[\nabla\times\mathsfbf{\hat{G}}_{\rm H}(\mathsfbf{r},\mathsfbf{r}_{0})\right]\cdot\mathsfbf{m},

(4)

\displaystyle\mathsfbf{H}_{\text{sc}}

\displaystyle=k^{2}\mathsfbf{\hat{G}}_{\rm H}(\mathsfbf{r},\mathsfbf{r}_{0})\cdot\mathsfbf{m}-i\omega\left[\nabla\times\mathsfbf{\hat{G}}_{\rm E}(\mathsfbf{r},\mathsfbf{r}_{0})\right]\cdot\mathsfbf{p}.

(5)

Here
\omega
is the angular frequency of the incident electromagnetic field. The wavenumber of the incident wave is denoted by
k=\omega/c
, where
c
is the speed of light in a vacuum. The constants
\varepsilon_{0}
and
\mu_{0}
represent the vacuum permittivity and permeability. The tensors
\mathsfbf{\hat{G}}_{\rm E}(\mathsfbf{r},\mathsfbf{r}_{0})
and
\mathsfbf{\hat{G}}_{\rm H}(\mathsfbf{r},\mathsfbf{r}_{0})
are the electric and magnetic dyadic Green’s functions of the system, accounting for the presence of the planar interface and describing the field response at
\mathsfbf{r}
due to a source dipole located at
\mathsfbf{r}_{0}
. The spectral representation of the Green’s tensor for a layered structure involves integration over the in-plane wavevector
k_{\rho}

16
. The integral naturally separates into two physically distinct domains:
the propagating sector
k_{\rho}<k
, which contributes to radiation into the upper half-space, and the evanescent sector
k_{\rho}>k
, which corresponds to the excitation of SPPs. Thus, the Green’s function can be decomposed into
\mathsfbf{\hat{G}}^{\rm E(H)}(\mathsfbf{r},\mathsfbf{r}_{0})=\mathsfbf{\hat{G}}^{\rm E(H)}_{\text{free-space}}(\mathsfbf{r},\mathsfbf{r}_{0})+\mathsfbf{\hat{G}}^{\rm E(H)}_{\text{evan}}(\mathsfbf{r},\mathsfbf{r}_{0})

17
;
9
;
10
. This separation allows us to decompose the scattered field into two channels, i.e. free-space and evanescent (SPP) channels,

\displaystyle\mathsfbf{E}_{\text{sc}}

\displaystyle=\mathsfbf{E}_{\text{free}}+\mathsfbf{E}_{\text{evan}},\quad\mathsfbf{H}_{\text{sc}}=\mathsfbf{H}_{\text{free}}+\mathsfbf{H}_{\text{evan}},

(6)

and correspondingly split the time-averaged Poynting vector as

\mathsfbf{S}_{\text{sc}}=\mathsfbf{S}_{\text{free}}+\mathsfbf{S}_{\text{evan}}+\mathsfbf{S}_{\text{mix}},

(7)

where

\displaystyle\mathsfbf{S}_{\text{free}}=\frac{1}{2}\real\!\left[\mathsfbf{E}_{\text{free}}\times\mathsfbf{H}^{*}_{\text{free}}\right],\ \mathsfbf{S}_{\text{evan}}=\frac{1}{2}\real\!\left[\mathsfbf{E}_{\text{evan}}\times\mathsfbf{H}^{*}_{\text{evan}}\right],

(8)

\displaystyle\mathsfbf{S}_{\text{mix}}=\frac{1}{2}\real\!\left[\mathsfbf{E}_{\text{free}}\times\mathsfbf{H}^{*}_{\text{evan}}+\mathsfbf{E}_{\text{evan}}\times\mathsfbf{H}^{*}_{\text{free}}\right].

(9)

The geometry of the excitation is shown in Figure
2
(a) where circularly (elliptically) polarized plane wave of wavelength
\lambda
incidents on a nanoparticle placed on top of the air-gold interface at the angle
\theta_{\text{inc}}
. The polarization state is described by two angles
\psi
and
\chi
that parametrize the polarization ellipse as follows: angle
0\leq\psi\leq\pi
defines the rotation of the major axis of the polarization ellipse, whereas angle
-\pi/4\leq\chi\leq\pi/4
specifies the ratio between the semi-axes of the polarization ellipse. For instance, right circular polarization (RCP) is defined at
\psi=0
,
\chi=\pi/4
, while left circular polarization (LCP) is defined by
\psi=0
,
\chi=-\pi/4
.

Figure
2
(b) shows the scattering cross sections into free-space,
\sigma_{\rm sc}^{\rm free}
, into the evanescent (SPP) channel,
\sigma_{\rm sc}^{\rm evan}
, and their interference term,
\sigma^{\rm mix}_{\rm sc}
, normalized to the geometrical cross section
\sigma_{0}=\pi R_{0}^{2}
. In the considered size interval (
R=100-165
nm), the particle supports electric- and magnetic-dipole Mie resonances, whose spectral/size evolution governs the redistribution of the scattered power between the free-space (air) and evanescent (SPP) channels. The resulting two-peak structure in the total response reflects the interplay of dipolar resonances in the presence of a metallic substrate; substrate-induced modifications of dipolar lineshapes and the corresponding resonance physics were analyzed in detail in Ref.
23
.

The oblique incidence and circular polarization of the field result in breaking the symmetry of the problem with respect to the
xOz
and
yOz
planes and stimulate strongly directional scattering of the EM energy in both free-space and SPP channels. The origin of the directional scattering in free space is related to the interference of electric and magnetic dipoles (Kerker effect), while the presence of the substrate adds additional complexity due to metal-induced dipole-images. At the same time, the presence of the circularly polarized electric and magnetic dipoles gives rise to directional excitation of SPPs
24
. The angular signatures of this redistribution are shown in Figure
2
(c,d) for a representative particle size
R=130
nm. The angular scattering density is defined as

p_{i}(\varphi,\theta)=\frac{1}{I_{0}}\mathsfbf{S}_{i}(\varphi,\theta,r\rightarrow\infty)\cdot\mathsfbf{n}(\varphi,\theta),

(10)

where
i\in\{\text{free},\text{evan}\}
,
\mathsfbf{n}
is the normal vector. Angular scattering density in Figure
2
(c) is normalized by the maxima of each corresponding line
p_{max}
.
Since SPPs propagate along the interface, their directivity is evaluated in the plane
\theta=\pi/2
,

D_{\rm evan}(\varphi)=\frac{2\pi\,p_{\rm evan}(\varphi,\pi/2)}{\int_{0}^{2\pi}p_{\rm evan}(\tilde{\varphi},\pi/2)\,d\tilde{\varphi}},

(11)

whereas radiation into the free-space is characterized by two angles. However, from the perspective of the generated optical force, we will be interested in the directivity projected on the interface plane that requires integration over the polar angle

D_{\text{free}}(\varphi)=\frac{2\pi\int_{0}^{\pi/2}p_{\text{free}}(\varphi,\theta)\sin\theta\,d\theta}{\int_{0}^{2\pi}\!\int_{0}^{\pi/2}p_{\text{free}}(\tilde{\varphi},\theta)\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\tilde{\varphi}}.

(12)

Figure
2
(c) presents the normalized angular scattering density in the
xOz
and
yOz
planes, comparing the radiation into the free-space (blue), the SPP-associated contribution (orange), and the total scattered field (black dashed). The key observation is that the angular patterns of the air and SPP channels are generally not co-directed: the direction maximizing radiation into the upper half-space differs from the direction maximizing SPP launching along the interface. This mismatch is a direct consequence of electric–magnetic dipole interference in the presence of the interface, where the same induced dipoles couple differently to propagating and evanescent parts of the Green’s function spectrum.

The size dependence of the directional response is summarized in Fig.
2
(e,f). Figure
2
(e) shows a map of the SPP directivity
D_{\rm evan}(\varphi)
versus azimuthal angle
\varphi
and particle radius
R
; the dashed curve traces the azimuth
\varphi(D_{\rm evan}^{\rm max})
corresponding to the direction of maximum SPP launching. In the dipolar regime, changing
R
effectively tunes the relative weight and phase of the electric and magnetic dipoles, which result in a continuous rotation of the preferential SPP launching direction across a broad angular range. This “full-angle” steering of SPP emission from a single dielectric nanoantenna, achieved through dipole-interference control, has been analyzed and demonstrated in Ref.
24
(ACS Photonics 2020).

In contrast, Fig.
2
(f) shows the corresponding map for the free-space channel,
D_{\rm free}(\varphi)
, together with the direction of its maximum
\varphi(D_{\rm free}^{\rm max})
. The qualitative difference between panels (e) and (f)—both in the location and in the evolution of the maxima—highlights that SPP launching and far-field radiation are governed by distinct interference conditions, even though they originate from the same induced dipole moments.

Recoil optical force due to free-space and SPP scattering

Within this section, we analyze the emergence of the recoil optical force due to directional rescattering of the incident field momentum. As mentioned earlier, there are two main scattering channels associated with free-space scattering and SPP generation. One of the main claims is that the direction of the optical force fully corresponds to the direction of free-space and SPP scattering. To demonstrate this, we will decompose the optical force into terms corresponding to SPP generation and free-space scattering.

We start with the well-known expression for the optical force acting on a particle with electric
\mathsfbf{p}
and magnetic
\mathsfbf{m}
dipole response form
3
:

\mathsfbf{F}=\underbrace{\frac{1}{2}\Re{\vb{p}^* \cdot(\nabla\vb{E}_{\rm loc})}}_{\mathsfbf{F}^{\text{e}}}+\underbrace{\frac{1}{2}\mu_{0}\Re{\vb{m}^* \cdot(\nabla\vb{H}_{\rm loc})}}_{\mathsfbf{F}^{\text{m}}}\underbrace{-\frac{k^{4}}{12\pi c\varepsilon_{0}}\Re{\vb{p} \times\vb{m}^*}}_{\mathsfbf{F}^{recoil}}\;.

(13)

Here, the optical force contains three terms: the force acting on an electric dipole in the electric field
\mathsfbf{F}^{e}
, the force acting on a magnetic dipole in the magnetic field
\mathsfbf{F}^{m}
, and the interference (recoil) term
\mathsfbf{F}^{\text{recoil}}
which originates from the directional scattering of the field momentum. In the non-homogeneous environment, the induced dipole moments may have additional contributions due to interaction with the surrounding structure. In our case, the substrate modifies the dipole response due to multiple scattering of the waves, which can be accounted via the effective electric and magnetic polarizability tensors
14
:

\displaystyle\mathsfbf{p}=\hat{\alpha}_{\rm ee}\varepsilon_{0}\mathsfbf{E}_{0}+\hat{\alpha}_{\rm em}\mathsfbf{H}_{0}

(14)

\displaystyle\mathsfbf{m}=\hat{\alpha}_{\rm me}\mathsfbf{E}_{0}+\hat{\alpha}_{\rm mm}\mathsfbf{H}_{0}

(15)

where the effective polarizabilities
\hat{\alpha}_{\rm ee}
,
\hat{\alpha}_{\rm em}
,
\hat{\alpha}_{\rm me}
,
\hat{\alpha}_{\rm mm}
are introduced and characterize the response of particles to the incident (external) fields
\mathsfbf{E}_{0},\mathsfbf{H}_{0}
as well as their coupling rather than the local fields
\mathsfbf{E}_{\rm loc},\mathsfbf{H}_{\rm loc}
. The latter ones contains the incident field
\mathsfbf{E}_{0}
as well as the scattered field
\mathsfbf{E}_{\text{sc}}=({k^{2}}/{\varepsilon_{0}})\mathsfbf{\hat{G}}^{\rm E}(\mathsfbf{r}_{0})\cdot\mathsfbf{p}+i\omega\mu_{0}[\curl\mathsfbf{\hat{G}}^{\rm H}(\mathsfbf{r}_{0})]\cdot\mathsfbf{m}
. Substituting the local fields acting on the dipole particle:

\displaystyle\mathsfbf{F}^{e}=\underbrace{\frac{1}{2}\Re{ \vb{p}^* \cdot\nabla\vb{E}_0}}_{\mathsfbf{F}^{\rm e}_{0}}+\underbrace{\frac{1}{2}\Re{\vb{p}^* \cdot\nabla\left( \frac{k^2}{\varepsilon_0} \GE(\vb{r}_0,\vb{r}_0) \cdot\vb{p} + i \omega\mu_0 [\rGH(\vb{r}_0,\vb{r}_0) ] \cdot\vb{m} \right)}}_{\mathsfbf{F}_{\rm sc}^{\rm e}},

(16)

\displaystyle\mathsfbf{F}^{m}=\underbrace{\frac{\mu_{0}}{2}\Re{ \vb{m}^* \cdot\nabla\vb{H}_0}}_{\mathsfbf{F}^{\rm m}_{0}}+\underbrace{\frac{\mu_{0}}{2}\Re{\vb{m}^* \cdot\nabla\left( k^2 \GH(\vb{r}_0,\vb{r}_0) \cdot\vb{m} - i \omega[\rGE(\vb{r}_0,\vb{r}_0) ] \cdot\vb{p} \right)}}_{\mathsfbf{F}_{\rm sc}^{\rm m}},

(17)

we formally split the force into two parts:
\mathsfbf{F}_{0}^{\text{e(m)}}
related to the incident field and
\mathsfbf{F}_{\text{sc}}^{\text{e(m)}}
related to the scattered field. The scattered force, in turn, has two terms related to the free space modes and evanescent modes in accordance with the Green’s function tensor, since
\mathsfbf{\hat{G}}^{\rm E}(\mathsfbf{\hat{G}}^{\rm H})
can be split into the integration over the free space and over the evanescent waves similarly to Eq. (
6
):

\mathsfbf{F}_{\text{sc}}^{\text{e(m)}}=\mathsfbf{F}_{\text{sc, free}}^{\text{e(m)}}+\mathsfbf{F}_{\text{sc, evan}}^{e(m)}.

Finally, the clear physical picture can be obtained once the corresponding terms will be collected:

\displaystyle\mathsfbf{F}=\mathsfbf{F}_{0}+\mathsfbf{F}_{\text{sc, free}}+\mathsfbf{F}_{\text{sc, evan}},

(18)

\displaystyle\mathsfbf{F}_{0}=\mathsfbf{F}_{0}^{e}+\mathsfbf{F}_{0}^{m},

(19)

\displaystyle\mathsfbf{F}_{\text{sc, free}}=\mathsfbf{F}_{\text{sc, free}}^{e}+\mathsfbf{F}_{\text{sc, free}}^{m}+\mathsfbf{F}_{\text{recoil}},

(20)

\displaystyle\mathsfbf{F}_{\text{sc, evan}}=\mathsfbf{F}_{\text{sc, evan}}^{e}+\mathsfbf{F}_{\text{sc, evan}}^{m}.

(21)

Their physical meaning can be interpreted quite clearly. The component
\mathsfbf{F}_{0}
corresponds to the optical force that appears due to the presence of the incident field. In the considered geometry, that is the standing wave in the
z
-direction; thus, this force contains a gradient-type force along the
z
-axis and
a pressure force
acting mainly along the
x
-axis (direction of incidence); thus, it has only
x
- and
z
-components
\mathsfbf{F}_{0}=F_{0,x}\mathsfbf{\hat{e}}_{x}+F_{0,z}\mathsfbf{\hat{e}}_{z}
. The component
\mathsfbf{F}_{\text{sc, free}}
corresponds to the field rescattered into free-space directly (“recoil” force term) and after reflection over the interface (“sc,free” term). Finally, the evanescent term
\mathsfbf{F}_{\text{sc, evan}}
contains the force due to the excitation of evanescent fields with the dominant contribution of the SPP wave.

To compare the scattering-induced optomechanical response with the radiation patterns discussed above, we now focus on the in-plane (lateral) recoil force and its decomposition into the free-space and SPP contributions. In the discussion below, we deliberately separate the incident-field part
\mathsfbf{F}_{0}
from the scattering-related terms in order to (i) directly connect the force to the momentum carried away by the scattered fields and (ii) enable a quantitative comparison with the directivity maxima in Fig.
2
. In particular, the force plotted in Fig.
3
corresponds to

\mathsfbf{F}_{\mathrm{sc}}\equiv\mathsfbf{F}-\mathsfbf{F}_{0}=\mathsfbf{F}_{\text{sc, free}}+\mathsfbf{F}_{\text{sc, evan}},

(22)

i.e., only the scattering-induced force, while the incident-field contribution
\mathsfbf{F}_{0}
(which contains the radiation pressure and the standing-wave gradient along
z
) is excluded.

Our analysis shows that directional scattering of the energy and momentum into the free-space and evanescent (SPP) fields defines the direction of the optical force. Figure
3
(a) schematically summarizes these physical mechanisms. The induced dipoles reradiate into two channels: propagating modes in the upper half-space (“air” scattering) and surface-bound SPP modes. Since both channels are strongly directional for oblique circular/elliptical excitation (Fig.
2
), the particle experiences a lateral recoil force opposing the dominant momentum flux in each channel. The radius dependence of the transverse scattering-induced force
F_{y}
is shown in Fig.
3
(b). Both analytical (solid curves) and numerical (dashed curves) results are presented, demonstrating good agreement across the full size interval. The total transverse component (black) is formed by the competition between the SPP-related contribution (orange) and the free-space contribution (blue). The SPP term remains of one sign over most of the considered range, reflecting the persistent preferential SPP launching direction, whereas the free-space term exhibits a pronounced dispersive behavior near the dipolar resonances. As a result, the total
F_{y}
shows distinct extrema and may change sign when the dominant momentum channel switches from one scattering pathway to the other.

Figure
3
(c) shows the longitudinal scattering-induced component
F_{x}
of (
22
). In contrast to the transverse force
F_{y}
, the longitudinal response is more sensitive to the redistribution of power between forward/backward propagating modes in the upper half-space and to the SPP momentum component projected onto the
x
axis. Near the dipolar resonances, where the relative phase between
\mathsfbf{p}
and
\mathsfbf{m}
varies rapidly, the longitudinal recoil exhibits large variations in magnitude and may reverse its sign. Importantly, one should note, the
x
-projection of
\mathsfbf{F}_{\mathrm{sc}}
does not include the radiation pressure exerted by the incident field and represents the recoil associated with scattering only. The incident-field radiation pressure (contained in
\mathsfbf{F}_{0}
) would add a typically dominant contribution along the direction of the incident wavevector and is intentionally omitted here to preserve a direct connection to the scattering directivities.

The directional correspondence between recoil forces and scattering maxima is demonstrated in Fig.

(d). The dashed curves reproduce the azimuthal angles of the directivity maxima for the SPP and free-space channels,
\varphi(D^{\max}_{\text{evan}})
and

\varphi(\mathsfbf{F}_{\text{evan (SPP)}})
and
\varphi(\mathsfbf{F}_{\mathrm{free}})

The close overlap between the dashed and solid curves confirms that the direction of each force component is set by the momentum carried away in the respective scattering channel. Therefore, the same dipole-interference mechanism that enables continuous steering of SPP directivity with particle size (Fig.

Finally, we note that in practical configurations the total force includes
\mathsfbf{F}_{0}
in addition to the scattering-induced contributions discussed here. In the present single-beam geometry
\mathsfbf{F}_{0}
contains a substantial radiation-pressure component in the plane of incidence and a gradient component along

F_{0,y}
remains zero by symmetry. This is precisely why the transverse recoil force shown in Fig.
3
(b) provides a particularly direct manifestation of the momentum asymmetry induced by oblique circular/elliptical excitation.

3
Optical sorting with directional scattering

The interplay between these two scattering channels allows one to control the direction and amplitude of the in-plane force. While the results shown in Fig.
2
and Fig.
3
were obtained for a purely RCP wave, one can change the force sign by altering the polarization state of the incident wave. In Fig.
4
(a), a map of the transverse force component is shown as a function of the polarization angles. One can see that by gradually varying the polarization states from right to left polarization with parallel rotation of the ellipse, it appears possible to change the sign and amplitude of
F_{y,\text{sc}}
component crossing zero at
\psi=\pi/2
and
\chi=0
what corresponds to a TE-polarized wave.

Finally, the proposed mechanism of the scattered part of the optical force control and variation can be applied to develop novel methods for the optical sorting of Mie particles by their size. In order to get a higher sensitivity of the optical force on the particle sizes, one has to suppress the dominant lateral component of the pressure force
\mathsfbf{F}_{0}
. That can be achieved in the cross beam geometry, where two beams of orthogonal polarization are incident at opposite angles as shown in Fig.
4
(c). Here the
x
-component of the optical pressure force will be compensated by the cross-polarized (TE and TM) beams counter propagating along the interface. The orthogonal polarization prevents the interference of beams along the
x
-axis and compensates the pressure part, so that the scattering part of the force starts to play the dominant role. Optimizing the relative parameters of the beams (relative field amplitudes and phases) allows finding the regime with almost
2\pi
variation of the lateral components of the optical force for the particle radius in the range from 120 nm to 160 nm making optical sorting of particles by their size possible within the considered geometry. The lateral component of the optical force is shown in Fig.
4
(d) for particles of different radius. One can see, that comparing to other approaches
20
, here one gets variation of the optical force in almost
2\pi
range for the considered particle parameters at the fixed parameters of the beams. Further optimization of the beam parameters can provide increased sensitivity by tuning the beam parameters for different particle radii.

4
CONCLUSION

In this work, we have demonstrated that optical forces acting on Mie-resonant dielectric nanoparticles near a metal interface are governed by directional scattering into free-space and SPP channels. The interference of electric and magnetic dipole moments leads to strongly asymmetric momentum redistribution, giving rise to a recoil force whose direction follows the scattering directivity in each channel.

By decomposing the force into incident, free-space, and evanescent contributions, we established a direct correspondence between the optical force and the underlying scattering processes. We showed that the competition between these channels enables continuous steering of the in-plane force and its sign reversal in the vicinity of dipolar resonances.

Finally, we proposed a cross-beam configuration that suppresses the radiation-pressure contribution and enhances the role of scattering-induced forces, providing a practical route for optical sorting of resonant nanoparticles by size. Our results highlight the potential of multipolar interference near interfaces as a versatile tool for optical manipulation and force engineering at the nanoscale.

5.1
Incident field expressions

Initial field acting on a particle is a superposition of initial incident plane wave and it’s reflected part by boundary layer.

\displaystyle\mathsfbf{E}_{0}=\mathsfbf{E}_{0}^{\text{inc}}+\mathsfbf{E}_{0}^{\text{ref}},

\displaystyle\mathsfbf{H}_{0}=\mathsfbf{H}_{0}^{\text{inc}}+\mathsfbf{H}_{0}^{\text{ref}},

where
\mathsfbf{E}_{0}^{\text{inc}},\mathsfbf{H}_{0}^{\text{inc}}
- incident electric or magnetic field and
\mathsfbf{E}_{0}^{\text{ref}},\mathsfbf{H}_{0}^{\text{ref}}
- components of the incident field reflected by the interface.

Incident and reflected plane waves defied as follows:

\mathsfbf{E}_{0}^{\text{inc}}=E_{0}\begin{bmatrix}\cos\theta_{\rm inc}\cos\beta e^{i\delta}\\
\sin\beta\\
\sin\theta_{\rm inc}\cos\beta e^{i\delta}\end{bmatrix}e^{i(k_{x}x-k_{z1}z)},\quad\mathsfbf{E}_{0}^{\rm ref}=E_{0}\begin{bmatrix}-r_{p}^{E}\cos\theta_{\rm inc}\cos\beta e^{i\delta}\\
r_{s}^{E}\sin\beta\\
r_{p}^{E}\sin\theta_{\rm inc}\cos\beta e^{i\delta}\end{bmatrix}e^{i(k_{x}x+k_{z1}z)},

(23)

\mathsfbf{H}_{0}^{\rm inc}=\frac{kE_{0}}{\omega\mu_{0}}\begin{bmatrix}\cos\theta_{\rm inc}\sin\beta\\
-\cos\beta e^{i\delta}\\
\sin\theta_{\rm inc}\sin\beta\end{bmatrix}e^{i(k_{x}x-k_{z1}z)},\quad\mathsfbf{H}_{0}^{\rm ref}=\frac{kE_{0}}{\omega\mu_{0}}\begin{bmatrix}-r_{p}^{H}\cos\theta_{\rm inc}\sin\beta\\
-r_{s}^{H}\cos\beta e^{i\delta}\\
r_{p}^{H}\sin\theta_{\rm inc}\sin\beta\end{bmatrix}e^{i(k_{x}x+k_{z1}z)},

(24)

where
k_{x}=k_{0}\sin\theta_{\rm inc}
,
k_{z1}=k_{0}\cos\theta_{\rm inc}
- absolute values of the corresponding projections of the k-vector in upper-space,
\mu_{0}
is a magnetic permeability of a vacuum,
\omega=
- angular frequency of radiation,
\beta
defines ratio between
s
and
p
polarized waves,
\delta
defines relative phase shift between them as well,
E_{0}
- amplitude of the incident radiation and
r^{s,p}_{E,H}
are Fresnel coefficients for
s
and
p
polarized light:

r_{E}^{s}(k_{\rho})=r^{p}_{H}(k_{\rho})=\frac{k_{z,1}-k_{z,2}}{k_{z,1}+k_{z,2}}\;\;,r_{E}^{p}(k_{\rho})=r_{H}^{s}(k_{\rho})=\frac{\varepsilon_{2}k_{z,1}-\varepsilon_{1}k_{z,2}}{\varepsilon_{2}k_{z,1}+\varepsilon_{1}k_{z,2}},

(25)

where
k_{z2}=\sqrt{k_{0}\varepsilon_{2}-k_{x}^{2}}
,
\varepsilon_{1}
- permittivity of upper half-space and
\varepsilon_{2}
- permittivity of lower half-sapce.

Polarization parameters can take values in the following ranges:
0\leq\delta\leq 2\pi
,
0\leq\beta\leq\pi/2
.
Following relations connect polarization ellipse parameters (
\psi
- orientation angle,
\chi
- ellipticity angle) with incident field parameters (
\delta
- phase shift,
\beta
- amplitude ratio):

\displaystyle\tan 2\psi=\tan 2\beta\cos\delta,

\displaystyle\sin 2\chi=\sin 2\beta\sin\delta.

5.2
Local field acting on a particle

Local field acting on a particle:

\begin{split}&\mathsfbf{E}_{\text{loc}}=\mathsfbf{E}_{0}+\mathsfbf{E}_{\text{ref}}^{\mathsfbf{p}}+\mathsfbf{E}_{\text{ref}}^{\mathsfbf{m}}\\
&\mathsfbf{H}_{\text{loc}}=\mathsfbf{H}_{0}+\mathsfbf{H}_{\text{ref}}^{\mathsfbf{p}}+\mathsfbf{H}_{\text{ref}}^{\mathsfbf{m}},\end{split}

(26)

where
\mathsfbf{E}_{0},\;\mathsfbf{H}_{0}
- incident and reflected electric and magnetic fields,
\mathsfbf{E}^{\mathsfbf{p},\mathsfbf{m}}_{ref},\;\mathsfbf{H}^{\mathsfbf{p},\mathsfbf{m}}_{ref}
- reflected electric and magnetic fields induced by electric and magnetic dipole moments. Using Green’s function approach reflected fields from dipole moments can be written as follows:

\displaystyle\mathsfbf{E}_{ref}^{\mathsfbf{p}}

\displaystyle=\frac{k^{2}}{\varepsilon_{0}}\mathsfbf{\hat{G}}^{\rm E}(\mathsfbf{r}_{0},\mathsfbf{r}_{0})\cdot\mathsfbf{p},

(27)

\displaystyle\mathsfbf{E}_{ref}^{\mathsfbf{m}}

\displaystyle=i\omega\mu_{0}[\curl\mathsfbf{\hat{G}}^{\rm H}(\mathsfbf{r}_{0},\mathsfbf{r}_{0})]\cdot\mathsfbf{m},

\displaystyle\mathsfbf{H}_{ref}^{\mathsfbf{m}}

\displaystyle=k^{2}\mathsfbf{\hat{G}}^{\rm H}(\mathsfbf{r}_{0},\mathsfbf{r}_{0})\cdot\mathsfbf{m},

\displaystyle\mathsfbf{H}_{ref}^{\mathsfbf{p}}

\displaystyle=-i\omega[\curl\mathsfbf{\hat{G}}^{\rm E}(\mathsfbf{r}_{0},\mathsfbf{r}_{0})]\cdot\mathsfbf{p},

with
\varepsilon_{0}
- dielectric permittivity of vacuum, upper-half space is air so the permittivity is equal to 1.

5.3
Effective dipole moments

Induced dipole moments inside of the resonant nano-particle can be estimated using effective polarizabilities:

\displaystyle\mathsfbf{p}=\alpha_{E}\mathsfbf{E}_{\text{loc}},

\displaystyle\mathsfbf{m}=\alpha_{H}\mathsfbf{H}_{\text{loc}},

where
\mathsfbf{E},\mathsfbf{H}_{\text{loc}}
- local electric and magnetic fields at the point of dipole particle,
\alpha_{E,H}
- polarizability coefficients for exact solution which can be found using first Mie scattering coefficients
a_{1}
and
b_{1}

boren
.

\displaystyle\alpha_{E}=\frac{6\pi i\varepsilon_{0}}{k^{3}}a_{1},

\displaystyle\alpha_{H}=\frac{6\pi i}{k^{3}}b_{1},

.

These Mie scattering coefficients have following form:

\displaystyle a_{1}=\frac{\varepsilon_{p}[xj_{1}(x_{0})]^{\prime}j_{1}(x_{1})-[x_{1}j_{1}(x_{1})]^{\prime}j_{1}(x_{0})}{\varepsilon_{p}[xh_{1}(x_{0})]^{\prime}j_{1}(x_{1})-[x_{1}j_{1}(x_{1})]^{\prime}h_{1}(x_{0})},

\displaystyle b_{1}=\frac{[x_{0}j_{1}(x_{0})]^{\prime}j_{1}(x_{1})-[x_{1}j_{1}(x_{1})]^{\prime}j_{1}(x_{0})}{[xh_{1}(x_{0})]^{\prime}j_{1}(x_{1})-[x_{1}j_{1}(x_{1})]^{\prime}h_{1}(x_{0})},

where
x_{0}=Rk
,
x_{1}=Rk\varepsilon_{p}^{2}
,
j_{1}
- first kind Bessel function,
h_{1}
- first kind Hankel function.

5.4
Dyadic Green’s function

Dyadic Green’s function in spectral representation:

\mathsfbf{\hat{G}}^{\rm E,H}(\mathsfbf{r},\mathsfbf{r}_{0})=\frac{i}{8\pi^{2}}\iint^{\infty}_{-\infty}[\mathsfbf{\hat{M}}^{s}+\mathsfbf{\hat{M}}^{p}]e^{i(k_{x}(x-x_{0})+k_{y}(y-y_{0})+k_{z}(z+z_{0}))}dk_{x}dk_{y},

(28)

where
\mathsfbf{r}_{0}=[x_{0},y_{0},z_{0}]
- point of the dipole particle,
\mathsfbf{r}=[x,y,z]
- observation point,
k
is the wavenumber,
k_{x,y,z}
- corresponding projection of
k
-vector with the following relation:
k=\sqrt{k_{x}^{2}+k_{y}^{2}+k_{z}^{2}}=2\pi/\lambda
.

Matrix for s-polarised radiation:

\mathsfbf{\hat{M}}^{\rm s}=\frac{r^{s}_{E,H}}{k_{z}(k_{x}^{2}+k_{y}^{2})}\begin{bmatrix}k_{y}^{2}&-k_{x}k_{y}&0\\
-k_{x}k_{y}&k_{x}^{2}&0\\
0&0&0\end{bmatrix},

(29)

Matrix for p-polarised radiation:

\mathsfbf{\hat{M}}^{\rm p}=-\frac{r^{p}_{E,H}}{k^{2}(k_{x}^{2}+k_{y}^{2})}\begin{bmatrix}k_{x}^{2}k_{z}&k_{x}k_{y}k_{z}&k_{x}(k_{x}^{2}+k_{y}^{2})\\
k_{x}k_{y}k_{z}&k_{y}^{2}k_{z}&k_{y}(k_{x}^{2}+k_{y}^{2})\\
-k_{x}(k_{x}^{2}+k_{y}^{2})&-k_{y}(k_{x}^{2}+k_{y}^{2})&-(k_{x}^{2}+k_{y}^{2})^{2}/k_{z}.\end{bmatrix},

(30)

In the place of origin
\mathsfbf{r}_{0}=[0,0,z_{0}]
, where
z_{0}
is a distance between dipole and boundary layer, Green’s functions have following form:

\mathsfbf{\hat{G}}^{\rm E,H}(\mathsfbf{r}_{0},\mathsfbf{r}_{0})=\frac{i}{8\pi k^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{k_{\rho}}{k_{z}}\begin{bmatrix}k^{2}r^{s}_{E,H}-k_{z}^{2}r^{p}_{E,H}&0&0\\
0&k^{2}r^{s}_{E,H}-k_{z}^{2}r^{p}_{E,H}&0\\
0&0&2k_{\rho}^{2}r^{p}_{E,H}\\
\end{bmatrix}e^{2ik_{z}z_{0}}dk_{\rho}.

(31)

Curl of Green’s function

\nabla\times\mathsfbf{\hat{G}}^{\rm E,H}(\mathsfbf{r},\mathsfbf{r}_{0})=-\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}}\iint^{\infty}_{-\infty}[\nabla_{k}\times][\mathsfbf{\hat{M}}^{s}+\mathsfbf{\hat{M}}^{p}]e^{i(k_{x}(x-x_{0})+k_{y}(y-y_{0})+k_{z}(z+z_{0}))}dk_{x}dk_{y},

(32)

\curl\mathsfbf{\hat{G}}^{\rm E}(\mathsfbf{r}_{0},\mathsfbf{r}_{0})=-\curl\mathsfbf{\hat{G}}^{\rm H}(\mathsfbf{r}_{0},\mathsfbf{r}_{0})=\frac{1}{8\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}k_{\rho}(r^{s}-r^{p})\begin{bmatrix}0&1&0\\
-1&0&0\\
0&0&0\\
\end{bmatrix}e^{2ik_{z}z_{0}}dk_{\rho}.

(33)

5.5
Derivatives of Dyadic Green’s function

Using spectral representation of Dyadic Green’s function it is not hard to get analytic formulas for derivative of such function.

\begin{split}\partial_{x}\mathsfbf{\hat{G}}^{\rm E,H}(\mathsfbf{r}_{0},\mathsfbf{r}_{0})&=-\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}}\iint_{-\infty}^{\infty}k_{x}[\hat{\mathsfbf{M}}^{s}+\hat{\mathsfbf{M}}^{p}]e^{2ik_{z}z_{0}}dk_{x}dk_{y}=\\
&=\frac{1}{8\pi k^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{p}_{E,H}k_{\rho}^{3}\begin{bmatrix}0&0&1\\
0&0&0\\
-1&0&0\\
\end{bmatrix}e^{2ik_{z}z_{0}}dk_{\rho},\end{split}

(34)

\begin{split}\partial_{x}[\curl\mathsfbf{\hat{G}}^{\rm E,H}(\mathsfbf{r}_{0},\mathsfbf{r}_{0})]&=-\frac{i}{8\pi^{2}}\iint_{-\infty}^{\infty}k_{x}[\nabla_{k}\times][\hat{\mathsfbf{M}}^{s}+\hat{\mathsfbf{M}}^{p}]e^{2ik_{z}z_{0}}dk_{x}dk_{y}=\\
&=-\frac{i}{8\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{k_{\rho}^{3}}{k_{z}}\begin{bmatrix}0&0&0\\
0&0&-r^{s}_{E,H}\\
0&r^{p}_{E,H}&0\\
\end{bmatrix}e^{2ik_{z}z_{0}}dk_{\rho},\end{split}

(35)

\begin{split}\partial_{y}\mathsfbf{\hat{G}}^{\rm E,H}(\mathsfbf{r}_{0},\mathsfbf{r}_{0})&=-\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}}\iint_{-\infty}^{\infty}k_{y}[\hat{\mathsfbf{M}}_{ref}^{s}+\hat{\mathsfbf{M}}_{ref}^{p}]e^{2ik_{z}z_{0}}dk_{x}dk_{y}=\\
&=\frac{1}{8\pi k^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{p}_{E,H}k_{\rho}^{3}\begin{bmatrix}0&0&0\\
0&0&1\\
0&-1&0\\
\end{bmatrix}e^{2ik_{z}z_{0}}dk_{\rho},\end{split}

(36)

\begin{split}\partial_{y}[\curl\mathsfbf{\hat{G}}^{\rm E,H}(\mathsfbf{r}_{0},\mathsfbf{r}_{0})]&=-\frac{i}{8\pi^{2}}\iint_{-\infty}^{\infty}k_{y}[\nabla_{k}\times][\hat{\mathsfbf{M}}^{s}+\hat{\mathsfbf{M}}^{p}]e^{2ik_{z}z_{0}}dk_{x}dk_{y}=\\
&=-\frac{i}{8\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{k_{\rho}^{3}}{k_{z}}\begin{bmatrix}0&0&r^{p}_{E,H}\\
0&0&0\\
-r^{s}_{E,H}&0&0\\
\end{bmatrix}e^{2ik_{z}z_{0}}dk_{\rho},\end{split}

(37)

\begin{split}&\partial_{z}\mathsfbf{\hat{G}}^{\rm E,H}(\mathsfbf{r}_{0},\mathsfbf{r}_{0})=-\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}}\iint_{-\infty}^{\infty}k_{z}[\mathsfbf{\hat{M}}^{s}+\mathsfbf{\hat{M}}^{p}]e^{2ik_{z}z_{0}}dk_{x}dk_{z}=\\
&=-\frac{1}{8\pi k^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\begin{bmatrix}r^{s}_{E,H}k^{2}-r^{p}_{E,H}k_{z}^{2}&0&0\\
0&r^{s}_{E,H}k^{2}-r^{p}_{E,H}k_{z}^{2}&0\\
0&0&2k_{\rho}^{2}r^{p}_{E,H}\end{bmatrix}k_{\rho}e^{2ik_{z}z_{0}}dk_{\rho},\end{split}

(38)

\begin{split}&\partial_{z}[\curl\mathsfbf{\hat{G}}^{\rm E,H}(\mathsfbf{r}_{0},\mathsfbf{r}_{0})]=-\frac{i}{8\pi^{2}}\iint_{-\infty}^{\infty}k_{z}[\nabla_{k}\times][\hat{\mathsfbf{M}}^{s}+\hat{\mathsfbf{M}}^{p}]e^{2ik_{z}z_{0}}dk_{x}dk_{y}=\\
&=\frac{i}{8\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}k_{z}k_{\rho}(r^{p}_{E,H}-r^{s}_{E,H})\begin{bmatrix}0&1&0\\
-1&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{bmatrix}e^{2ik_{z}z_{0}}dk_{\rho}.\end{split}

(39)

Analytical expression of optical force components

Let’s consider each term separately. If we substitute the local electric field

(

Later on we omit the arguments of the Green’s functions, assuming their values at the location of the dipoles. In case of plane waves (
23

F_{0,x}^{\rm e}=-\frac{k_{x}}{2}\Im{ \vb{p}^* \cdot\vb{E}_0},\quad F_{0,y}^{\rm e}=0,\quad F_{0,z}^{\rm e}=\frac{k_{z}}{2}\Im{ \vb{p}^*\cdot(\vb{E}_0^{\rm ref} - \vb{E}_0^{\rm inc})}.

Other contribution to the optical force acting on an induced electric dipole arises from the scattered field generated by both the electric
\mathsfbf{p}

\mathsfbf{m}

\mathsfbf{F}_{\rm sc}^{\rm e}=\frac{1}{2}\Re{\vb{p}^* \cdot\nabla\left( \frac{k^2}{\varepsilon_0} \GE\vb{p} + i \omega\mu_0 [\rGH] \vb{m} \right)}=\mathsfbf{F}^{\rm pp}+\mathsfbf{F}^{\rm pm}.

If we take into an account the structure of resulting derivative of Dyadic Green’s function (
34

F^{\rm pp}_{x}=\frac{k^{2}}{2\varepsilon_{0}}\Re{\vb{p}^* \cdot(\partial_x \GE\vb{p} )}=-\frac{k^{2}}{\varepsilon_{0}}\Im{p_x^* p_z}\Im{ \partial_x \GE_{xz}}.

(40)

Taking into account the non-zero components of the Green’s function (
35

pm
term have following form that we will simplify later:

\begin{split}F^{\rm pm}_{x}&=-\frac{\omega\mu_{0}}{2}\Im{\vb{p}^* \cdot\left( \partial_x [\rGH] \vb{m} \right) }=\\
&=-\frac{\omega\mu_{0}}{2}\Im{ p_y^* m_z (\partial_x [\rGH_{yz}]) + p_z^* m_y (\partial_x [\rGH_{zy}]) }\end{split}

(41)

Similar situation happens with the optical force acting on magnetic dipole
\mathsfbf{m}
in local magnetic field
\mathsfbf{H}_{\rm loc}
(
26
).

\mathsfbf{F}^{m}=\underbrace{\frac{\mu_{0}}{2}\Re{ \vb{m}^* \cdot\nabla\vb{H}_0}}_{\mathsfbf{F}^{\rm m}_{0}}+\underbrace{\frac{\mu_{0}}{2}\Re{\vb{m}^* \cdot\nabla\left( k^2 \GH\vb{m} - i \omega[\rGE] \vb{p} \right)}}_{\mathsfbf{F}_{\rm sc}^{\rm m}},

(42)

Pressure force from incident magnetic field
\mathsfbf{H}_{0}
(
24

\mathsfbf{m}
in a scalar form:

F_{0,x}^{\rm m}=-\frac{\mu_{0}k_{x}}{2}\Im{\vb{m}^* \cdot\vb{H}_0},\quad F_{0,y}^{\rm m}=0,\quad F_{0,z}^{\rm m}=\frac{\mu_{0}k_{z}}{2}\Im{\vb{m}^* \cdot\left( \vb{H}_0^{\rm ref} - \vb{H}_0^{\rm inc} \right)}.

(43)

Likewise, the force acting on a magnetic dipole
\mathsfbf{m}
resulting from the reflected field induced by dipole moments can be broken down into a magnetic-magnetic (
mm

me

\mathsfbf{F}^{\rm m}_{\rm sc}=\frac{\mu_{0}}{2}\Re{\vb{m}^* \cdot\nabla\left( k^2 \GH\vb{m} - i \omega[\rGE] \vb{p} \right)}=\mathsfbf{F}^{\rm mm}+\mathsfbf{F}^{\rm me}.

(44)

Substituting the expressions for the Green’s function (

F_{x}^{\rm mm}=\frac{\mu_{0}k^{2}}{2}\Re{\vb{m}^* \cdot(\partial_x \GH\vb{m})}=-k^{2}\mu_{0}\Im{m_x^* m_z}\Im{ \partial_x \GH_{xz}}.

(45)

Also taking into account (
34

\begin{split}F_{x}^{\rm me}&=\frac{\mu_{0}\omega}{2}\Im{ \vb{m}^* \cdot(\partial_x [\rGE] \vb{p})}=\\
&=\frac{\omega\mu_{0}}{2}\Im{m_y^* p_z (\partial_x [\rGE_{xy}]) + m_z^* p_y (\partial_x [\rGE_{zy}])}\end{split}

(46)

Note that
\partial_{x}[\curl\mathsfbf{\hat{G}}^{\rm E}_{zy}]=-\partial_{x}[\curl\mathsfbf{\hat{G}}^{\rm H}_{yz}]

\partial_{x}[\curl\mathsfbf{\hat{G}}^{\rm E}_{yz}]=-\partial_{x}[\curl\mathsfbf{\hat{G}}^{\rm H}_{zy}]

F_{x}^{\rm pm}+F_{x}^{\rm mp}=-\omega\mu_{0}\left(\Re{p_y^* m_z}\Im{\partial_x [\rGH_{yz}]}+\Re{p_z^* m_y}\Im{\partial_x [\rGH_{zy}]}\right)

(47)

Writing down the full expression for the total optical force acting on electric and magnetic dipoles located above the boundary layer

\begin{split}&F_{x}=-\frac{k_{x}}{2}\Im{ \vb{p}^* \cdot\vb{E}_0 + \mu_0 \vb{m}^* \cdot\vb{H}_0}-\frac{k^{2}}{\varepsilon_{0}}\Im{p_x^* p_z}\Im{ \partial_x \GE_{xz}}-k^{2}\mu_{0}\Im{m_x^* m_z}\Im{ \partial_x \GH_{xz}}-\\
&-\omega\mu_{0}\left(\Re{p_y^* m_z}\left(\Im{\partial_x [\rGH_{yz}]}+\frac{k^{3}}{12\pi}\right)+\Re{p_z^* m_y}\left(\Im{\partial_x [\rGH_{zy}]}-\frac{{k^{3}}}{12\pi}\right)\right)\end{split}

(48)

We see that to determine the value of
F_{x}
, we need to evaluate the integrals in the expression for
\Im{\partial_x \tens{G}^{\rm E,H}}
and
\Im{\partial_x [\rGH]}
. In case of spectral representation of Dyadic Green’s functions we can split integration into two parts. For example, first integration range

in

corresponds to the propagation of evanescent waves, where the dominant contribution arises from SPP excitation:

\partial_{x}\mathsfbf{\hat{G}}^{\rm E}_{xz}=\underbrace{\frac{1}{8\pi k^{2}}\int_{0}^{k}r^{p}_{E,H}k_{\rho}^{3}e^{2ik_{z}z_{0}}dk_{\rho}}_{\partial_{x}\mathsfbf{\hat{G}}^{\rm E}_{xz,\text{ prop.}}\text{- propagating waves}}+\underbrace{\frac{1}{8\pi k^{2}}\int_{k}^{\infty}r^{p}_{E,H}k_{\rho}^{3}e^{2ik_{z}z_{0}}dk_{\rho}}_{\partial_{x}\mathsfbf{\hat{G}}^{\rm E}_{xz,\text{ evas.}}\text{- evanescent waves}},

(49)

and substituting this expansion into (

F_{x}^{\rm pp}=\underbrace{-\frac{k^{2}}{\varepsilon_{0}}\Im{p_x^* p_z}\partial_{x}\mathsfbf{\hat{G}}^{\rm E}_{xz,\text{ prop.}}}_{F_{x\text{, prop.}}^{\rm pp}}\underbrace{-\frac{k^{2}}{\varepsilon_{0}}\Im{p_x^* p_z}\partial_{x}\mathsfbf{\hat{G}}^{\rm E}_{xz,\text{ evan.}}}_{F_{x\text{, evan.}}^{\rm pp}}

(50)

The same logic applies to the other parts of the x-component of the optical force. Thus, we can distinguish a part of the force associated primarily with the directional excitation of a surface plasmon-polariton:

\begin{split}&F_{\text{evan. (SPP), x}}=F_{x,\text{ evan.}}^{\rm pp}+F_{x,\text{ evan.}}^{\rm mm}+F_{x,\text{ evan.}}^{\rm pm}+F_{x,\text{ evan.}}^{\rm mp}=\\
&=-\frac{k^{2}}{\varepsilon_{0}}\Im{p_x^* p_z}\Im{ \partial_x \GE_{xz,\text{ evan.}}}-k^{2}\mu_{0}\Im{m_x^* m_z}\Im{ \partial_x \GH_{xz,\text{ evan.}}}-\\
&-\omega\mu_{0}\left(\Re{p_y^* m_z}\Im{\partial_x [\rGH_{yz,\text{ evan.}}]}+\Re{p_z^* m_y}\Im{\partial_x [\rGH_{zy,\text{ evan.}}]}\right)\end{split}

The force applied to dipoles due to incident radiation:

F_{0,x}=-\frac{k_{x}}{2}\Im{ \vb{p}^* \cdot\vb{E}_0 + \mu_0 \vb{m}^* \cdot\vb{H}_0}

(51)

Another component of the optical force associated with the reflection of radiation from the substrate is related to the propagating waves in the free-space region:

F_{\text{free-space sc., x}}=F_{x}-F_{0,x}-F_{\rm SPP,x}

(52)

Thus, the total optical power includes three main components associated with the incident radiation (
\mathsfbf{F}_{0})
, a component responsible for evanescent waves propagation with mainly SPP contribution (
\mathsfbf{F}_{\text{evan. (SPP)}})
, and a component associated with the free-space scattering of propagating waves in the upper half-space (
\mathsfbf{F}_{\text{free-space sc.}})

\mathsfbf{F}=\mathsfbf{F}_{0}+\mathsfbf{F}_{\text{evan. (SPP)}}+\mathsfbf{F}_{\text{free-space sc.}}.

(53)

For the purpose of completeness, we will provide the same detailed expressions for the y component of the optical force:

F_{y}^{\rm pp}=\frac{k^{2}}{2\varepsilon_{0}}\Re{ \vb{p}^* \cdot(\partial_y \GE\vb{p} )}=-\frac{k^{2}}{\varepsilon_{0}}\Im{\partial_y \hat{\vb{G}}_{ref, yz}^E}\Im{p_y^* p_z},

F_{y}^{\rm pm}=-\frac{\omega\mu_{0}}{2}\Im{\vb{p} (\partial_y [\rGH] \vb{m})}=-\frac{\omega\mu_{0}}{2}\Im{ p_x^* m_z (\partial_y [\rGH_{xz}]) + p_z^* m_x (\partial_y [\rGH_{zx}])},

\displaystyle F_{y}^{\rm mm}=\frac{k^{2}\mu_{0}}{2}\Re{\vb{m}^* \cdot(\partial_y \GH\vb{m})}=-k^{2}\mu_{0}\Im{m_y^* m_z}\Im{ \partial_y \GH_{yz}},

F_{y}^{\rm mp}=\frac{\omega\mu_{0}}{2}\Im{\vb{m}^* (\partial_y [\rGE] \vb{p})}=\frac{\omega\mu_{0}}{2}\Im{ m_x^* p_z (\partial_y [\rGE_{xz} ]) + m_z^* p_x (\partial_y [\rGE_{zx} ])},

Additionally, the z-component of the force:

5.8
Polarizability tensors

It is possible to introduce effective polarizability tensors for resonant nano-particle. In described system we have refracted field from induced dipole moments acting back on that dipole moments. In this case, a magneto-electric interaction arises between the dipoles due to the interface. Consequently, the effective polarizabilities of the particles are tensor quantities, and the dipole moments depend on both the electric and magnetic fields, by analogy with bi-anisotropic particle:

\begin{split}\mathsfbf{p}=\varepsilon_{0}\mathsfbf{\hat{\alpha}}_{\rm ee}\mathsfbf{E}_{0}+\mathsfbf{\hat{\alpha}}_{\rm em}\mathsfbf{H}_{0},\\
\mathsfbf{m}=\mathsfbf{\hat{\alpha}}_{\rm me}\mathsfbf{E}_{0}+\mathsfbf{\hat{\alpha}}_{\rm mm}\mathsfbf{H}_{0},\end{split}

Polarizability tensors have following form
14
:

\mathsfbf{\hat{\alpha}}_{ee}=\begin{bmatrix}\alpha_{\rm ee}^{x}&0&0\\
0&\alpha_{\rm ee}^{x}&0\\
0&0&\alpha_{\rm ee}^{z}\end{bmatrix},\quad\mathsfbf{\hat{\alpha}}_{\rm em}=\begin{bmatrix}0&\alpha_{em}&0\\
-\alpha_{em}&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{bmatrix},

\mathsfbf{\hat{\alpha}}_{\rm mm}=\begin{bmatrix}\alpha_{\rm mm}^{x}&0&0\\
0&\alpha_{\rm mm}^{x}&0\\
0&0&\alpha_{\rm mm}^{z}\end{bmatrix},\quad\mathsfbf{\hat{\alpha}}_{\rm me}=\begin{bmatrix}0&\alpha_{\rm me}&0\\
-\alpha_{\rm me}&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{bmatrix}.

\alpha_{\rm ee}^{x}=\left(1-\alpha_{\rm E}\mathsfbf{\hat{G}}_{\rm ee}^{xx}+\frac{\alpha_{H}\alpha_{E}\mu_{0}(\mathsfbf{\hat{G}}^{xy}_{\rm me})^{2}}{1-\alpha_{H}\mathsfbf{\hat{G}}_{\rm mm}^{xx}}\right)^{-1},\quad\alpha_{\rm ee}^{z}=\left(1-\alpha_{E}\mathsfbf{\hat{G}}_{\rm ee}^{zz}\right)^{-1},\quad\alpha_{\rm em}=\frac{\mu_{0}\alpha_{\rm ee}\alpha_{H}\mathsfbf{\hat{G}}_{me}^{xy}}{1-\alpha_{H}\mathsfbf{\hat{G}}_{\rm mm}^{xx}},

\alpha_{\rm mm}^{x}=\left(1-\alpha_{\rm H}\mathsfbf{\hat{G}}_{\rm mm}^{xx}+\frac{\alpha_{H}\alpha_{E}\mu_{0}(\mathsfbf{\hat{G}}^{xy}_{\rm me})^{2}}{1-\alpha_{E}\mathsfbf{\hat{G}}_{\rm ee}^{xx}}\right)^{-1},\quad\alpha_{\rm ee}^{z}=\left(1-\alpha_{H}\mathsfbf{\hat{G}}_{\rm mm}^{zz}\right)^{-1},\quad\alpha_{\rm me}=\frac{\mu_{0}\alpha_{mm}\alpha_{E}\mathsfbf{\hat{G}}_{me}^{xy}}{1-\alpha_{E}\mathsfbf{\hat{G}}_{\rm ee}^{xx}},

with such notation for Dyadic Green’s functions:

\begin{split}&\mathsfbf{\hat{G}}_{\rm ee}=\frac{k^{2}}{\varepsilon_{0}}\mathsfbf{\hat{G}}^{\rm E}(\mathsfbf{r}_{0},\mathsfbf{r}_{0}),\quad\mathsfbf{\hat{G}}_{\rm em}=i\omega\mu_{0}\curl\mathsfbf{\hat{G}}^{\rm H}(\mathsfbf{r}_{0},\mathsfbf{r}_{0}),\\
&\mathsfbf{\hat{G}}_{\rm mm}=k^{2}\mathsfbf{\hat{G}}^{\rm H}(\mathsfbf{r}_{0},\mathsfbf{r}_{0}),\quad\mathsfbf{\hat{G}}_{\rm me}=i\omega\curl\mathsfbf{\hat{G}}^{\rm H}(\mathsfbf{r}_{0},\mathsfbf{r}_{0}).\end{split}
