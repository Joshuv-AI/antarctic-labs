Broad-coverage targeted lipidomics reveals stage-specific lipid metabolic profiles and diagnostic and prognostic marker panels in ischemic stroke
Authors: Haoran Huang, Liu Liu, Can Zhao, Shuying Cheng, Wenxuan Li, Anbao Xu, Xiaoqin Yin, Xin Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIschemic stroke (IS) is a major cause of mortality and disability globally, with challenges in early diagnosis and prognosis prediction. Dysregulated lipid metabolism is key to IS pathophysiology, but comprehensive profiling of lipid changes during disease progression remains limited.MethodsThis study enrolled 223 IS patients and 57 healthy controls. Plasma lipid profiles were analyzed using broad-coverage targeted lipidomics by ultra-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC-MS/MS). Differential lipids were identified through orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) with univariate analysis, and their changes across acute, subacute, convalescent, and chronic phases were examined by clustering analysis. Machine learning algorithms, including least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression and support vector machine (SVM), were used to screen diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers, followed by logistic regression models and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve evaluation.ResultsFrom 607 identified lipids, 54 showed differential abundance between IS patients and healthy controls, grouped into four clusters. For diagnosis, six lipids—LPG(18:0), PE(O-16:0/18:2), TG(52:2/FA16:0), PE(O-16:0/22:6), PE(O-16:0/20:3), and PE(O-18:0/18:2)— achieved an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.984. For prognosis, six lipids—PE(O-16:0/22:5), PE(O-18:0/22:5), SM(d18:1/14:0), PG(18:0/18:1), PE(O-16:0/20:3), and LPI(16:0)—achieved an AUC of 0.925.ConclusionThis study characterizes lipid metabolism changes across IS stages reconstructed from cross-sectional data of different patient groups and establishes two six-lipid panels for diagnosis and prognosis. These findings provide insights into lipid metabolism evolution following stroke and offer candidate biomarker panels for IS management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s11306-026-02485-7.

Introduction

Stroke is a critical acute medical condition characterized by an interruption in cerebral blood flow, ranking as a leading cause of mortality and disability globally, thereby posing a significant public health challenge (Feigin et al., 2024). In China, the incidence of stroke remains elevated, with over two million new cases annually, and the disease burden is anticipated to increase further due to an aging population (Ji et al., 2025). Presently, stroke diagnosis predominantly relies on neuroimaging modalities such as computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) (Sarraj et al., 2024). Despite the advancements in diagnostic methodologies, challenges remain in accurately diagnosing stroke during the hyperacute phase and predicting long-term functional outcomes for patients. Consequently, the identification of biomarkers that can objectively and sensitively reflect the pathophysiological processes of the disease is essential for early and accurate diagnosis, prognostic evaluation, and personalized intervention for stroke.

Lipids are fundamental structural components of cell membranes and the primary energy storage form in the human body. Dysregulated lipid metabolism is closely associated with various cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases (Holmes et al., 2018). Specifically, in the initiation and progression of ischemic stroke (IS), lipid molecules significantly influence the disease trajectory and outcome by modulating oxidative stress, membrane damage, and inflammatory responses (Kloska et al., 2020; Malý et al., 2021). Although conventional lipid parameters (e.g., total cholesterol, triglycerides, low-density lipoprotein, and high-density lipoprotein) are widely employed in cardiovascular risk stratification, they provide limited metabolic information and fail to comprehensively capture the complex reprogramming of lipid metabolism under pathological conditions.

The rapid advancement of lipidomics technologies, particularly the widespread application of high-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS) and ultra-performance liquid chromatography (UPLC), has enabled researchers to systematically dissect alterations in lipid metabolic networks at the molecular level (Perez et al., 2021; Züllig & Köfeler, 2020). By combining high throughput with the stability and accuracy of conventional multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) approaches, broad-coverage targeted lipidomics enables large-scale semi-quantitative analysis of lipid molecules in biological samples and has become a pivotal strategy for identifying disease-associated lipid biomarkers (Zheng et al., 2020). Studies have demonstrated significant alterations in the plasma lipidome following IS, involving multiple classes, such as glycerophospholipids, sphingolipids, and triglycerides, with these changes closely associated with neurological damage, inflammatory responses, and repair processes (Becktel et al., 2024).

However, most current studies are limited to cross-sectional designs at single time points, failing to elucidate the dynamic evolution of lipid metabolism across different stages of the disease (Huang et al., 2024; Yu et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2023). The pathological processes following IS exhibit significant temporal dynamics, transitioning from acute hypoxia‑ischemia and membrane disruption, through subacute inflammatory responses, to neuroplasticity during convalescence. Lipid molecules assume distinctly different roles throughout these stages (Endres et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2025). Therefore, systematically characterizing the dynamic changes in lipids throughout the disease course is essential for a deeper understanding of their pathological mechanisms and for identifying stage-specific biomarkers.

This study employs broad-coverage targeted lipidomic analyses to profile plasma lipid metabolism in patients with IS, comparing lipid signatures between healthy controls and patients at different disease stages (from acute to chronic phase) to identify stage-specific lipid signatures. Furthermore, bioinformatics and machine learning approaches will be integrated to screen for diagnostic and prognostic markers, providing a foundation for precision management in stroke.

Methods

Reagents and materials

2, 6-Di-tert-butyl-4-methylphenol (BHT), acetic acid and ammonium acetate were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Methyl tert-butyl ether was purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Ultrapure H2O was supplied by the Milli-Q Pure Water System (Millipore, Bedford, MA, USA). SPLASH™ LIPIDOMIX™ Mass Spectrometry Standards [PC(15:0/18:1(d7)), PE(15:0/18:1(d7)), PS(15:0/18:1(d7)), PG(15:0/18:1(d7)), PI(15:0/18:1(d7)), PA(15:0/18:1(d7)), LPC(18:1(d7)), LPE(18:1(d7)), Chol Ester(18:1(d7)), MG(18:1(d7)), DG(15:0/18:1(d7)), TG(15:0/18:1(d7)−15:0), SM(18:1(d9)), Cholesterol(d7)], Deuterated Ceramide LIPIDOMIX™ Mass Spectrometry Standards [C16 Ceramide-d7 (d18:1-d7/16:0), C18 Ceramide-d7 (d18:1-d7/18:0), C24 Ceramide-d7 (d18:1-d7/24:0), C24:1 Ceramide-d7 (d18:1-d7/24:1(15Z))] and liquid chromatography (LC)-grade acetonitrile, isopropanol and methanol were purchased from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany).

Participant enrollment

This study prospectively enrolled 223 patients with IS who presented at the Affiliated Hospital of Nantong University between March 2025 and December 2025. This was a cross-sectional study; each patient contributed only one blood sample at the time of enrollment, without follow-up sampling. Simultaneously, 57 healthy volunteers from the hospital’s physical examination center were recruited as controls, resulting in a total of 280 participants. To ensure comparability between groups, the age and sex of the stroke patients and healthy volunteers were strictly matched. This study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Nantong University, and all participants provided written informed consent before enrollment.

Sample pretreatment

Frozen samples stored at −80 °C were thawed on ice and mixed by vortexing. A lipid extraction solution (methyl tert-butyl ether: methanol = 5:1, v/v) containing internal standards, BHT, and 1% acetic acid was prepared to prevent lipid oxidation. 700 µL of pre-cooled extraction solution was added to each sample and shaken at 4 °C for 60 min. After adding 140 µL ultrapure water and shaking for 15 min, the mixture was centrifuged. 500 µL of the supernatant was concentrated by nitrogen blow-drying, redissolved in 100 µL reconstitution solution (isopropanol: acetonitrile: water = 30:65:5, v/v/v), centrifuged, and used for analysis.

Data acquisition

Lipidomic data were acquired using ultra-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC-MS/MS). Our group refined mass spectrometry parameters using the LIPID MAPS database and created a comprehensive lipid ion pair database containing over 1,000 lipid molecules across five categories in both positive and negative ion modes, including fatty acids, glycerolipids, glycerophospholipids, sphingolipids, and sterol lipids. Using scheduled MRM technology, we set detection time windows for high-throughput lipidomic analysis. Following the Metabolomics Standards Initiative (MSI) and Lipidomics Standards Initiative (LSI), we assessed lipid annotation confidence in our targeted MRM method. Stable isotope-labeled internal standards were Level 1 (identified lipids). Other target lipids were Level 2 (putatively annotated lipids), based on MRM ion pairs from LIPID MAPS, retention time trends by lipid class and carbon chain, and fragmentation patterns. No features were Level 3 or Level 4.

The liquid chromatography system (ExionLC AD) used a Phenomenex Kinetex C18 column (2.6 μm, 3 × 100 mm). Mobile phase A was methanol-acetonitrile-water (1:1:1, v/v/v) with 5 mM ammonium acetate; phase B was isopropanol with 5 mM ammonium acetate. The elution program started at 20% B for 0.5 min, increased to 40% B (1.5 min), 60% B (3 min), 98% B (13 min), held for 2 min, returned to 20% B (0.1 min), and equilibrated for 3.9 min. Column temperature was 45 °C, flow rate 0.3 mL/min, injection volume 2 µL.

Mass spectrometry analysis used a Triple Quad 5500+ system with electrospray ionization (ESI) source, operating in positive and negative ion modes with scheduled MRM scanning. ESI temperature was 350 °C, with voltage at 5500 V (positive mode) and − 4500 V (negative mode). Ion source gases were maintained at 55 psi, curtain gas at 35 psi, and collision gas at 9 psi. Quality control (QC) samples, pooled equal aliquots from all study samples, were inserted every 10 test samples.

Data analysis and statistical processing

Raw peak areas were normalized to internal standard peak areas for relative quantification. To filter features with poor reproducibility, the relative standard deviation (RSD) of the internal-standard-normalized peak area was calculated across QC samples. Only lipid features with QC RSD ≤ 30% and detectable in ≥ 80% of samples in at least one group (IS or healthy control) were retained.

For multivariate and univariate analyses, the filtered data were log₁₀‑transformed to stabilize variance and then mean-centered and scaled to unit variance (autoscaling). Orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) assessed lipid profile differences between IS and healthy groups, validated through 7‑fold cross‑validation and 100 permutation tests (evaluated by Q², R²Y and P values). Variable importance in projection (VIP) values were extracted from the OPLS-DA model, combined with univariate analysis [|log2 fold change| ≥ 0.585 (i.e., FC ≥ 1.5 or ≤ 0.67), and false discovery rate (FDR) from t-test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test ≤ 0.05] to identify significantly different lipids. In addition, hierarchical clustering and heatmap visualization were performed on the differentially expressed lipids using Euclidean distance for similarity and Ward’s minimum variance method (ward.D) for linkage. Lipids clusters were obtained by cutting the dendrogram at a specific height, applied to lipid‑wise clustering.

Two machine learning algorithms—least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression and support vector machine (SVM)—were employed to identify biomarkers. The dataset was randomly divided into a training set and a test set in a 7:3 ratio, utilizing stratified sampling to ensure that the distributions of disease stages and the healthy control group were consistent between the two sets. The training set was used for feature selection, while the test set was reserved for the final performance evaluation. Data were normalized by log transformation and autoscaling. LASSO used ten-fold cross-validation to determine optimal λ, selecting lipid features with non-zero coefficients at the λ corresponding to the minimum cross-validation error (λ_min). SVM employed RBF kernel with hyperparameters optimized through grid search and cross-validation. SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) values evaluated feature importance. The combined features derived from the two algorithms underwent Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) analysis to assess the Area Under the Curve (AUC) for each feature. The six features exhibiting the highest AUC values were selected to develop a logistic regression model, which was subsequently evaluated using the test set. Statistical analysis was performed with R version 4.5.1 (https://www.R-project.org) and EasyR (https://www.easyr.cc Solutions, Inc., Shanghai).

Results

Identification of differential metabolites

Quality control assessment revealed that the median RSD across all detected features was 25.3%, with 75.1% of features exhibiting an RSD below 30%, indicating stable instrument performance. All plasma samples were analyzed in a single continuous batch, with the injection order randomized by disease status. No instrument reboots, major maintenance, or significant time-dependent signal drift were observed in the QC data; thus, no batch correction was applied.

After filtering, targeted lipidomics analysis identified a total of 607 lipids in both positive and negative ion modes, encompassing four major lipid classes: glycerolipids, glycerophospholipids, sphingolipids, and sterols. We compared the overall plasma lipid compositions between the IS and healthy groups. The results of the OPLS-DA analysis revealed significant differences between the two groups, although they were not completely separated. Following 7‑fold cross‑validation, the model demonstrated a certain degree of stability and predictive capability, with R²Y = 0.671 and Q² = 0.507 (P < 0.01 based on 100 response permutation tests). Subsequently, lipids with high VIP scores (≥ 1.0) were subjected to statistical evaluation to identify dysregulated lipids associated with the entire stroke progression process. Lipid features exhibiting a |log2 fold change| ≥ 0.585 in the t-test and a false discovery rate ≤ 0.05 in the Wilcoxon rank-sum test were selected as significantly differentially abundant. In total, 54 significantly different lipid features were identified (Fig. 1).

Dynamic changes of differential lipids

Patients in the IS group were categorized into four distinct subgroups based on sampling time points, reflecting the progression from acute injury to chronic sequelae in ischemic stroke. This categorization aligns with the International Stroke Recovery and Rehabilitation Roundtable (SRRR) framework: acute phase (0–7 days), subacute phase (8 days to 1 month), convalescent phase (> 1 month to 6 months), and chronic phase (> 6 months). By performing cross-sectional comparisons of lipid levels across subgroups at different stages, we reconstructed the dynamic trends of lipids throughout the disease course. The abundance levels of these differentially expressed lipids demonstrated four distinct clusters of change among the patient groups. The first cluster, exemplified by ether-linked phosphatidylethanolamines (PE(O)), exhibited a marked reduction during the acute phase, followed by a gradual recovery; however, most of the levels remained subnormal during the chronic phase. The second cluster, represented by specific triglycerides (TGs), demonstrated a pronounced increase in the acute phase, followed by a gradual decline. The third cluster, encompassing lysophosphatidylcholines (LPC) and lysophosphatidylethanolamines (LPE), showed continuously rising levels as the disease advanced. The fourth cluster, predominantly involving various lysophospholipids, displayed a consistent increase from the acute to the convalescent phase, followed by a slight decrease during the chronic phase, yet it did not return to normal levels (Fig. 2).

Identification and validation of diagnostic markers

Utilizing data from 57 healthy controls and 64 patients with IS (acute phase), a classification model was developed to differentiate stroke patients from healthy individuals. LASSO analysis, based on the minimum error model, identified 29 substances. SVM analysis selected 29 substances with SHAP values ≥ 0.02. To ensure the reliability of the selected substances, the intersection of the two sets was taken, yielding 13 consensus markers. These 13 markers were then evaluated by ROC analysis. Results indicated that LPG(18:0), PE(O-16:0/18:2), TG(52:2/FA16:0), PE(O-16:0/22:6), PE(O-16:0/20:3), and PE(O-18:0/18:2) demonstrated excellent discriminatory ability, with AUC values of 0.924, 0.890, 0.882, 0.879, 0.851, and 0.834, respectively. Subsequently, a logistic regression model combining these six markers was constructed and evaluated on the independent test set. The combined predictor achieved an AUC of 0.984, demonstrating robust discrimination. These findings suggest that the six lipid markers may have significant clinical implications for stroke diagnosis (Fig. 3).

Identification and validation of prognostic markers

Patients with IS constitute a highly heterogeneous group. Among the 223 patients with IS, 104 who were in the convalescent and chronic phases were assessed using the modified Rankin Scale (mRS), which evaluates the degree of neurological functional disability. Based on their mRS scores, patients were divided into a good-prognosis subgroup (scores 0–2, n = 63) and a poor-prognosis subgroup (scores 3–6, n = 41). LASSO analysis, utilizing the minimum error model, identified 25 substances. SVM analysis selected 16 substances with SHAP values ≥ 0.02. Then, nine substances identified by both LASSO and SVM were subjected to ROC analysis to evaluate the predictive performance of individual markers. Results revealed that PE(O-16:0/22:5), PE(O-18:0/22:5), SM(d18:1/14:0), PG(18:0/18:1), PE(O-16:0/20:3), and LPI(16:0) exhibited significant discriminatory ability, with AUC values of 0.788, 0.780, 0.744, 0.744, 0.739, and 0.732, respectively. Subsequently, a logistic regression model combining these six markers was constructed and evaluated on the independent test set. The combined predictor achieved an AUC of 0.925, demonstrating robust discrimination. These findings suggest that the six identified lipid markers could have significant clinical implications for stroke prognosis (Fig. 4).

Discussion

In this study, we performed broad-coverage targeted lipidomic analyses using UPLC-MS/MS, identifying 607 compounds. Based on VIP, FC values, and P-values, 54 differential metabolites were identified between the IS and healthy groups, including glycerophospholipids (GP) and TG. GP are fundamental structural components of cell membranes; their disturbance is associated with extensive structural damage, energy depletion, and phospholipase activation during IS onset. For instance, alterations in PE and PC may relate to membrane phospholipid degradation due to ischemia and hypoxia, releasing precursors such as arachidonic acid, which may exacerbate inflammation. TG are linked to insulin signaling and metabolic pathways (such as AMPK and mTOR). In the ischemic environment, TG imbalance is associated with insufficient energy supply and oxidative damage, and may be correlated with neuronal death, blood-brain barrier disruption, and neuroinflammation.

By categorizing IS patients into acute, subacute, convalescent, and chronic phases, we observed four distinct dynamic change clusters in the abundance levels of 54 differential lipids across disease stages.

The initial cluster shows a decline during acute and subacute phases, followed by incomplete recovery, as demonstrated by PE(O). PE(O) is a phosphatidylethanolamine subclass characterized by an alkyl chain connected through an ether bond at the sn-1 position, a configuration that endows it with substantial antioxidant properties (Jové et al., 2023). Its structure supports membrane integrity and signal transduction (Dean & Lodhi, 2017). Moreover, its sn-2 position contains polyunsaturated fatty acids like DHA, influencing pathways like AKT and TLR, affecting neuronal and glial functions (Alessandri et al. 2004; Lee et al. 2003a, b). The consistent pattern of PE(O) alterations suggests that its levels may serve as indicators of injury severity and potential for repair.

The second cluster shows increased TG levels during the acute phase, which gradually decrease to normal levels in the chronic phase. Hypertriglyceridemia is a risk factor for cardiovascular disease (Li et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025). TGs have been implicated in atherosclerosis progression, induce vascular endothelial dysfunction, and foster a systemic pro-inflammatory and pro-thrombotic state (Ting et al., 2007). Numerous epidemiological studies have confirmed a positive association between elevated TG levels and the risk of IS, making these lipids potential biomarkers for stress severity and recovery (Freiberg et al., 2008; Shi et al., 2020).

The third and fourth clusters show persistent elevation of lysophospholipids like LPC and LPE, which are products of phospholipase A2 hydrolysis and act as inflammatory mediators and signaling molecules (Tan et al., 2020). LPC can induces lymphocyte and macrophage migration, pro-inflammatory factor release, blood-brain barrier disruption, and neurotoxicity (Liu et al., 2020). High levels of these lipids may be evidence of chronic neuroinflammation following IS, with neuroinflammation acknowledged in IS pathophysiology (Wang et al., 2023). Understanding inflammatory mechanisms may inform new therapeutic strategies targeting neuroinflammatory pathways.

These clusters indicate that lipid metabolism disorders after IS are multifactorial, time-dependent, and complex, representing a superposition of different pathological events at the molecular level, including acute injury, stress response, and chronic inflammation. Given the observational cross-sectional reconstruction of dynamic trajectories, these interpretations should be viewed as hypothesis-generating rather than conclusive evidence of causality.

To advance the auxiliary diagnosis of IS, this research used LASSO regression and SVM to identify six lipid biomarkers: LPG(18:0), PE(O-16:0/18:2), TG(52:2/FA16:0), PE(O-16:0/22:6), PE(O-16:0/20:3), and PE(O-18:0/18:2). LPG(18:0), identified as lysophosphatidylglycerol, has been reported to impede the differentiation and immunosuppressive function of tregs, thereby fostering immune imbalance (Aili et al., 2024). The detection of four PE(O) species suggests ether phospholipid remodeling disruption, potentially related to peroxisomal dysfunction in IS. These lipids, which are crucial to cell membranes, contain alkyl chains that act as antioxidants; their disruption may intensify neuronal oxidative stress injury (Cui et al., 2021). Additionally, TG(52:2/FA16:0) may reflect energy metabolism disturbances (Poursharifi et al., 2025). These six metabolites highlight uncontrolled inflammation, membrane damage, and energy crisis, as potential features of IS. The logistic regression model using these biomarkers achieved an AUC of 0.984, indicating excellent discriminative capability. Combining LASSO for dimensionality reduction and SVM for feature importance mitigated single-method limitations, enhancing robustness. This model demonstrated high accuracy in differentiating IS patients from healthy individuals; however, prospective multicenter external validation is essential to evaluate generalizability and clinical applicability, and its absence represents a significant limitation.

In prognostic prediction, this study analyzed the modified Rankin Scale (mRS) scores of 104 patients during follow-up and identified six lipid prognostic markers using LASSO regression and SVM: PE(O-16:0/22:5), PE(O-18:0/22:5), SM(d18:1/14:0), PG(18:0/18:1), PE(O-16:0/20:3), and LPI(16:0). While this panel shares some components with the diagnostic biomarker panel (e.g., PE(O), it also presents differences, indicating interconnected yet distinct molecular processes potentially underlying disease onset versus clinical outcomes. SM(d18:1/14:0), a sphingomyelin and crucial structural element of the myelin sheath, is associated with myelin repair, apoptosis, and neuroplasticity (Bienias et al., 2016). Alterations in this lipid may indicate white matter damage or neural repair potential, which could correlate with long-term recovery (Mohamud Yusuf et al., 2024). PG(18:0/18:1), a mitochondrial phospholipid, may signal compromised neuronal energy metabolism. LPI(16:0), linked to neuroinflammation and synaptic plasticity via the GPR55 receptor, could affect post-stroke neurological remodeling (Alhouayek et al., 2018; Shen et al., 2022). Together, this panel provides insights into potential long-term recovery mechanisms following IS, including myelin integrity, mitochondrial function, neuroinflammation, and ether phospholipid metabolism. It offers a novel approach for individualized prognostic assessment and potential integration with clinical parameters. Further validation of its predictive capabilities in larger prospective cohorts is necessary.

The temporal lipid trajectories reconstructed from cross-sectional data in this study warrant cautious interpretation. Patients at different disease stages from independent samples may reflect inter-individual heterogeneity rather than genuine intra-individual changes. Additionally, multiple confounding variables may influence stage-specific lipid alterations. For example, lipid-lowering therapy (particularly statins) can reduce TG and certain glycerophospholipid levels, potentially obscuring true changes from acute to chronic phases (Yadav et al., 2021). Diabetes may alter the metabolism of lysophosphatidylcholines and TGs (Srivastava et al., 2012). Obesity often involves hypertriglyceridemia and a low-grade inflammatory state, with a lipid profile overlapping metabolic changes post-ischemic stroke (Zheng et al., 2024). Dietary fatty acid intake can rapidly modulate circulating lipid levels. Furthermore, different subtypes of IS (e.g., large-artery atherosclerosis and cardioembolism) exhibit distinct lipid metabolic features, and if the distribution of subtypes is unbalanced across disease stages, trajectory clusters may reflect subtype differences rather than disease progression (Hindy et al., 2018). Consequently, these findings should be considered exploratory and warrant validation in prospective longitudinal cohort studies. Nonetheless, they establish a foundation for lipidomics-based disease staging and prognostic models, which require further validation in independent cohorts.

Conclusion

This study maps plasma lipid metabolic changes across acute, subacute, convalescent, and chronic phases of IS, reconstructed from cross-sectional data using broad-coverage targeted lipidomics. We identified 54 differentially abundant lipids showing four temporal trajectories reflecting pathological processes, including membrane injury, oxidative stress, energy crisis, and neuroinflammation. Using LASSO regression and SVM algorithms, we developed two six-lipid panels: one for early diagnosis (LPG(18:0), PE(O-16:0/18:2), TG(52:2/FA16:0), PE(O-16:0/22:6), PE(O-16:0/20:3), PE(O-18:0/18:2)) with 0.984 AUC, and another for prognosis (PE(O-16:0/22:5), PE(O-18:0/22:5), SM(d18:1/14:0), PG(18:0/18:1), PE(O-16:0/20:3), LPI(16:0)) with 0.925 AUC. These findings advance understanding of lipid reprogramming in IS and provide candidate biomarker panels for diagnosis and prognosis. Future longitudinal studies with repeated measurements and external multicenter validation are needed.
