Renewed mandate for Schools of Public Health: a call to action
Authors: Afifah Rahman-Shepherd, Indira Dewi Kantiana, David M Bishai, Liam Smeeth, Karla Soares-Weiser, Yik Ying Teo, Michelle A Williams, Margaret Chan
Published: 2026

The world’s first School of Public Health was established in 1916. Today, in 2026, there are more than 150 Schools of Public Health worldwide. In those 100 years, even in the last year alone, the world of public health has changed immeasurably, and these changes demand a renewed mandate to ready Schools of Public Health for the future.

Over the past year, there has been no shortage of proposals to reform our health ecosystems at national, regional and global levels.1,3 But proposals must be accompanied by legitimate stewards, credible authorities and robust accountability. Who will lead and be responsible for implementing reforms is as important, if not more important, as what the reforms are. We challenge Schools of Public Health to reflect on these proposals with one question in mind: What is our role in reform?

At this year’s Prince Mahidol Awards Conference in Bangkok, a group of 40 deans and senior leadership of Schools of Public Health were challenged to answer this question, initiating this call to action. We propose four pillars on which Schools of Public Health renew their mandates, adapting and aligning to the contexts they shape and serve, especially in supporting and strengthening key national functions for health.

The first pillar is to forge partnerships with policy-making institutions. Health is, unironically, a casualty of populist, short-term policy-making.4 Health reforms that guarantee political wins within a single term are favoured above reforms that tackle the complex and compounding health challenges we face today. Partnerships between Schools of Public Health and policy-making institutions are not optional, they are a necessity. At subnational and national levels, partnerships can provide the evidence to guide priority setting to address long-term and future health issues with foresight. At regional and global levels, partnerships can convene and lead dialogues to identify diplomatic solutions, coordinate collective action to impact global public health and undertake policy-directed research to course correct global public health policies. We urge Schools of Public Health to build and nurture relationships with policy-making institutions, and to advocate to funders the need to expand their funding base to support our policy roles and functions.

The second pillar is to ground public health pedagogy in local contexts while growing public health pedagogy across contexts. Public health curriculum needs to reflect the evolving landscape of public health at subnational and national levels. Schools of Public Health have a duty to design and deliver quality public health curriculum that is shaped by the priorities and needs of the communities they serve, and to capacitate the next generation to practise public health in ways that are responsive, respectful and sustainable in their contexts. But public health is increasingly interdependent with regional and global health ecosystems, and Schools of Public Health should not exist in silos. It is also our duty to make public health education and training accessible across contexts. We invite Schools of Public Health to critically review their public health pedagogy to reflect their own contexts and to engage in mutual learning, adaptation and co-delivery of public health pedagogy with Schools of Public Health across contexts.

The third pillar is to defend scientific integrity. From climate change to the COVID-19 pandemic to the commercial determinants of health, science is increasingly buried by stakeholder interests.5 When those stakeholders are the most powerful politicians and industries, the risk is not only that science is buried but that we tolerate a society in which truth-seeking is censored. Schools of Public Health have a responsibility to defend and communicate scientific integrity against the tides of misinformation and disinformation, especially when state-sponsored or industry-sponsored. We behove Schools of Public Health, in partnership with Cochrane, to form regional and global research networks dedicated to tackling misinformation and disinformation, and restore trust in science.

The fourth pillar is to shift internal incentive structures. Public health needs to move beyond its traditional duties in teaching, research and service to the academic community towards a practice-oriented discipline. Academics worldwide are guided by metrics for promotion and tenure, and the first three pillars in this call to action will be ineffective unless Schools of Public Health explicitly recognise and reward practice contributions. We challenge Schools of Public Health to reassess their criteria for academic excellence to include and incentivise contributions to policy and practice beyond the traditional indicators of publications and grants.

Our call to action offers a renewed mandate for Schools of Public Health through partnership, pedagogy, scientific integrity and internal incentives. We call on the Deans of Schools of Public Health to seize reform processes as an opportunity for renewal.
