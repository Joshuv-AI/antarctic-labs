Global landscape of maternal hemorrhage mortality: a 40-year analysis based on GBD 2021 study
Authors: Lanfang Guo, Jun Hu, Shaokun Wang, Haiyan Wang, Lili Liu, You Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMaternal hemorrhage (MH) is the leading direct cause of global maternal mortality, accounting for over a quarter of all maternal deaths. While significant progress has been made, the decline in MH-related mortality varies widely across regions, reflecting disparities in healthcare access and quality. This study leverages the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021 database to analyze MH mortality trends and inequalities.MethodsWe analyzed MH mortality data from 1982 to 2021 for women aged 15–49. We assessed trends using crude data and age-standardized mortality rates, calculating the estimated annual percentage change (EAPC). We also employed an age–period–cohort (APC) model to examine the effects of age, period, and birth cohort on mortality. Countries were grouped into five categories based on their sociodemographic index (SDI).ResultsGlobally, the absolute number of MH deaths decreased by 63.13% between 1982 and 2021. High-middle SDI regions showed the greatest improvement with a 91.76% decrease, while low-SDI regions had the most modest progress at 21.57%. By 2021, Nigeria, India, and Pakistan had the highest absolute number of deaths, while Sierra Leone, Chad, and Niger had the highest age-standardized mortality rates. The APC analysis revealed a persistent disparity: successive generations in low-SDI regions still face substantially higher risks compared to their counterparts in high-SDI settings.ConclusionOur findings reveal significant global progress in reducing MH mortality, but also a growing health disparity, with the burden increasingly concentrated in low-SDI countries. This highlights the need for targeted policies that strengthen healthcare systems in vulnerable regions to achieve global equity in maternal health.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00404-026-08506-z.

Introduction

Maternal health serves as a core indicator reflecting social equity and health system performance, and maternal hemorrhage (MH) has long been the leading direct cause of maternal mortality worldwide [1, 2]. According to the World Health Organization, maternal hemorrhage remains the leading direct cause of maternal mortality worldwide, accounting for approximately 27% of maternal deaths globally, with postpartum hemorrhage constituting the vast majority of these deaths [3, 4]. MH is characterized by rapid onset and progression, posing a particularly severe challenge in resource-limited settings [5, 6]. Although significant progress has been made over the past few decades in reducing the overall maternal mortality ratio worldwide, the decline in MH-related mortality has varied considerably across regions, reflecting inequalities in the accessibility and quality of healthcare services [7–9]. Therefore, accurate assessment of the disease burden and spatiotemporal trends of MH is essential for consolidating current achievements, identifying underserved regions, and formulating targeted intervention strategies.

The Global Burden of Disease study (GBD) provides an unprecedented and systematic data source for such assessments [10, 11]. As a comprehensive epidemiological research initiative, GBD employs standardized modeling methods to systematically integrate data on mortality, morbidity, and risk factors at the global, regional, and national levels. Its aim is to produce comparable quantitative estimates of health loss, and its findings have become a cornerstone for global public health policy-making. Although maternal mortality has been widely examined using GBD data, fewer studies have focused specifically on maternal hemorrhage mortality trends, SDI-related inequalities, and age–period–cohort effects across countries and regions [12, 13]. Existing studies have yet to fully leverage the spatiotemporal resolution of GBD data to elucidate the differential patterns of MH mortality across regions with varying sociodemographic index (SDI) and its temporal trends, as well as the specific challenges posed by MH in achieving equity in maternal health outcomes [10, 14, 15]. Elucidating these aspects is essential for deepening the understanding of the epidemiological nature of MH, evaluating the effectiveness of interventions, and accurately identifying regions with scarce resources.

This study aims to utilize the GBD 2021 database to systematically describe the epidemiological characteristics of MH-related mortality at the global, regional, and national levels from 1982 to 2021. The findings are expected to provide a clear and quantified overview of the global burden and inequalities in MH-related mortality for the international community and national health authorities. This may thereby offer critical data support and evidence for policy-making aimed at prioritizing high-risk populations, strengthening perinatal healthcare systems, and ultimately reducing preventable maternal mortality worldwide.

Materials and methods

Study participants

The study population was women of childbearing age (WCBA), defined by the World Health Organization (WHO) as females aged from 15 to 49 years [16]. Sociodemographic index (SDI), developed by the GBD study, is a composite indicator of development status calculated as the geometric mean of lag-distributed income per capita, average educational attainment among individuals aged 15 years and older, and total fertility rate among those younger than 25 years, with values ranging from 0 to 1, where higher values indicate higher levels of socioeconomic development [17, 18]. Maternal hemorrhage (MH) was defined according to the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021 cause hierarchy as a Level-4 cause within maternal disorders, encompassing the following ICD-10 categories: postpartum hemorrhage (O72), intrapartum and other obstetric hemorrhage (O67, O62.2), placenta previa and placental abruption (O44–O46), placental disorders (O43.2), and hemorrhage in early pregnancy (O20).

Data source

This study is based on data from the Global Burden of Diseases, Injuries, and Risk Factors Study 2021 (GBD 2021), which provides comprehensive epidemiological data on 371 diseases and injuries across 204 countries and territories and 21 GBD regions over several decades [19, 20]. The GBD 2021 standard estimation period begins in 1990; mortality estimates for 1982–1989 rely on back-casted estimates using GBD modeling approaches, which may carry greater uncertainty than estimates from 1990 onward. Our research specifically focuses on mortality and death counts due to maternal hemorrhage. To ensure broad data coverage, GBD 2021 integrated data from hospital records, population-based registries, and community surveys, addressing data inconsistencies through robust statistical models. The GBD methodology has been detailed in several prior publications [10, 21, 22]. All metrics in this study (including mortality and death counts) are accompanied by 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs), which are determined by the 25th and 975th percentiles of 1000 ordered estimations from the GBD algorithm. Furthermore, we utilized the Sociodemographic Index (SDI) introduced in GBD 2021 for each country and territory, categorizing them into five levels: low, low-middle, middle, high-middle, and high.

Overall trend analysis

We assessed the overall trends in MH mortality between 1982 and 2021 by analyzing both crude data and age-standardized mortality rates. Age-standardized mortality rates were estimated using the direct standardization method, which treats the mortality rate as a weighted sum of independent Poisson random variables [23, 24]. Additionally, we used the estimated annual percentage change (EAPC) as a metric to quantify temporal trends. The EAPC was calculated by fitting a log-linear regression model: ln(rate) = α + β × (calendar year) + ε, where EAPC = 100 × (exp(β) − 1). We employed Poisson regression with robust standard errors to account for potential overdispersion in count data. Autocorrelation in the time-series residuals was assessed using the Durbin–Watson test, and heteroscedasticity was evaluated using the Breusch–Pagan test. Model goodness-of-fit was quantified by the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and deviance statistics. The 95% confidence interval (CI) for each EAPC was derived from the standard error of β. We present both absolute death counts and age-standardized mortality rates (per 100,000 WCBA) throughout. We elected to use the WCBA denominator rather than live births (i.e., the standard maternal mortality ratio, MMR) because GBD provides consistent, annually updated population estimates by age and sex across all 204 countries and territories, ensuring comparability across settings and over time.

Age–period–cohort model analysis

This study employed an age–period–cohort (APC) model to analyze MH mortality data, exploring the complex interplay of age, period, and cohort effects on the observed trends [25, 26]. The inherent identifiability problem in the APC (age–period–cohort) model was addressed through a Bayesian hierarchical framework based on structured smoothness priors, implemented in the BAPC package within the R software environment [27, 28]. Specifically, first- or second-order random walk priors (RW1/RW2) were applied to the age, period, and cohort effects, respectively, thereby regularizing the parameter space and resolving the identifiability issue without the need for artificial constraints. Model parameters were estimated using the integrated nested Laplace approximation (INLA) method [29], which provides computationally efficient approximate Bayesian inference for latent Gaussian. This model assumed a Poisson distribution for the outcome (death counts) with a log-link function and the log-transformed population as an offset term. The reference period was set as the 1982–1986 interval and the reference cohort as the 1962–1971 birth cohort. Model parameters were estimated using integrated nested Laplace approximation (INLA) for computational efficiency. Net drift and local drift values, which quantify the overall and age-specific annual percentage change in mortality rates after adjusting for period and cohort influences, were derived from the model output. To validate the robustness of our results, model fit was assessed using Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), and visual inspection of deviance residuals.

For the APC model input data, we used the estimated MH mortality and population data for each country and territory from GBD 2021. To ensure consistency, age intervals were matched with period intervals, with age grouped into 5 year segments and paired with 5 year calendar periods. The entire study period (1982–2021) was divided into eight 5 year intervals: 1982–1986, 1987–1991, 1992–1996, 1997–2001, 2002–2006, 2007–2011, 2012–2016, and 2017–2021. Correspondingly, this analysis included 13 partially overlapping 10 year birth cohorts, ranging from 1932–1941 to 1997–2006. This overlapping cohort division was designed to examine cohort effects in more detail, reduce information gaps, enhance the precision of APC analysis, and aid in identifying long-term trends across generations. Partially overlapping 10-year birth cohorts were specified to increase the resolution of cohort effect estimation and reduce information loss at cohort boundaries, consistent with established practice in BAPC/INLA-based APC analysis. Within the Bayesian hierarchical framework employed here, structured priors on cohort effects account for correlation between adjacent overlapping intervals, mitigating concerns about precision inflation from cohort non-independence.

The age–period–cohort (APC) model was employed to analyze both overall and age-specific temporal trends in mortality. Net drift, derived from the model, quantifies the overall annual percentage change in mortality rates after adjusting for period and cohort influences. In contrast, local drift delineates temporal variations within particular age groups. The statistical significance of all estimated trends was assessed using Wald chi-square test.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using R version 4.3.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing), with data visualization facilitated by the ggplot2 package [30]. Specific packages utilized for the analysis included BAPC, and INLA [31]. A two-tailed p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Global trends in MH mortality, 1982–2021

From 1982 to 2021, MH mortality demonstrated substantial global decline across all SDI quintiles (Table 1). Globally, the absolute number of MH deaths decreased dramatically from 125.69 thousand (95% UI 105.91 to 146.77) in 1982 to 46.34 thousand (95% UI 38.18 to 56.56) in 2021, representing an estimated 63.13% reduction (95% UI −73.89% to −46.59%) over the 40 year period. The age-standardized mortality rate (ASMR) declined even more sharply, from 11.21 per 100,000 WCBA (95% UI 9.46 to 13.06) in 1982 to 2.39 per 100,000 WCBA (95% UI 1.97 to 2.92) in 2021—a 78.7% reduction. This decline corresponded to an EAPC of −3.8% (95% UI −3.95% to −3.65%), with the net drift analysis confirming a consistent downward trend of −3.87% per year (95% CI −3.97% to −3.76%). SDI-related disparities in MH mortality

The magnitude of mortality reduction varied significantly across SDI quintiles. High-middle SDI regions achieved the greatest improvement with a 91.76% decrease (95% UI −94.78% to −87%) and annual decline of −6.88%, followed by high-SDI regions (−86.39% reduction, −5.15% annually). Conversely, low-SDI regions demonstrated the most modest progress with only a 21.57% decrease and the slowest annual decline of −3.17%. This disparity persisted in 2021, with low-SDI regions maintaining an age-standardized mortality rate of 9.94 per 100,000 WCBA compared to just 0.03 per 100,000 in high-SDI regions.

Country-level burden and EAPC patterns

At the country level, the burden of MH mortality showed distinct geographical patterns (Fig. 1). By 2021, Nigeria, India, and Pakistan emerged as the countries with the highest absolute number of MH deaths. However, age-standardized mortality rates were the highest in Sierra Leone, Chad, and Niger, indicating persistent challenges in maternal healthcare delivery in these regions. Notably, Chad was the only country with a positive EAPC point estimate (age-standardized: 0.11%, 95% CI − 0.10% to 0.32%; all ages: 0.08%, 95% CI − 0.13% to 0.29%); however, confidence intervals for both estimates spanned zero, and no statistically significant difference was observed relative to other low-SDI countries after correction for multiple comparisons. Age-specific mortality trends

The analysis of age-specific trends revealed distinct patterns across reproductive age groups and SDI regions from 1982 to 2021. All age groups demonstrated consistent downward trends, with annual percentage changes ranging from −2% to −8% across SDI quintiles (Fig. 2A). At the individual country level, Chad was the sole exception, showing a positive point estimate for EAPC, though this did not reach statistical significance. Globally, older reproductive age groups (45–49 years) showed the steepest mortality decline with annual changes of approximately −4.31%, while younger age groups (15–19 years) demonstrated more modest of −3.49%. High and high-middle SDI regions achieved the most pronounced reductions across all age groups, with annual changes reaching approximately −6% in younger groups, while low-SDI regions showed the smallest reductions of −3.47% to −2.93% annually. The proportional distribution of MH deaths across age groups evolved substantially over the study period (Fig. 2B). In 1982, deaths were more evenly distributed across the reproductive age span. By 2021, there was a clear shift toward concentration in peak reproductive years (25–34 years), which accounted for approximately 0.4–0.6 of total MH deaths in high-SDI regions. The relative contribution of teenage pregnancies (15–19 years) and advanced maternal age (40–49 years) decreased over time. Age–period–cohort effects on MH mortality

Age–period–cohort (APC) analysis revealed distinct patterns of maternal hemorrhage mortality across SDI regions and representative countries. The age effects on maternal mortality differed markedly across SDI strata (Fig. 3A). In lower SDI regions, mortality rates exhibited a pronounced non-linear pattern, with an initial increase followed by a decline across the reproductive age span, with rate doubling or tripling between the youngest and peak age groups. In contrast, high and high-middle SDI regions demonstrated comparatively flat age curves, with limited variation across maternal age. Low SDI regions showed the steepest age-related gradients overall. The age-specific mortality rates estimated from the APC model for each SDI quintile, including 95% confidence intervals, are provided in Supplementary Table S1. All SDI region aggregates showed downward trends though individual countries (notably Chad) deviated from this pattern (Fig. 3B). High SDI regions achieved greater reductions compared to low-SDI regions, indicating disproportionate gains in higher-resource settings. Successive birth cohorts born after the 1950s exhibited progressively lower MH mortality risks compared with earlier cohorts, and the most pronounced reductions were observed in women born after 1970 (Fig. 3C). Discussion

Based on the GBD 2021 database, this study systematically delineates the epidemiological characteristics of global mortality related to maternal hemorrhage (MH) from 1982 to 2021. Our analysis not only reveals a significant decline in global MH mortality but also highlights persistent and widening disparities across different socioeconomic strata and geographic regions. These findings extend prior GBD-based work that examined maternal mortality broadly by providing a dedicated hemorrhage-specific analysis over an extended 40-year time horizon with formal age–period–cohort decomposition [16, 32]. Our MH-specific results are broadly consistent with WHO global estimates of cause-specific maternal mortality and with projections that indicate substantial but uneven progress toward SDG 3.1. These findings provide a robust quantitative foundation for understanding the global burden of MH and for formulating targeted public health policies.

A marked decrease in global MH mortality was observed, consistent with broader trends in maternal health. The overall reduction in the global MH mortality can be associated with multiple factors, including the promotion of skilled birth attendance, improved access to emergency obstetric care, and advancements in the medical management of MH [33, 34]. Among these advancements, endovascular procedures—such as uterine artery embolization and prophylactic balloon occlusion—have emerged as critically important, minimally invasive hemostatic methods increasingly utilized in obstetric patients with severe hemorrhage, offering effective bleeding control while preserving fertility [35]. These interventions have long been prioritized by the World Health Organization (WHO) and other international agencies, and our results indicate that these efforts have yielded tangible outcomes. Global initiatives, such as Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 3.1 aimed at reducing the global maternal mortality (including MH), have also provided a framework for countries to invest in maternal healthcare [4, 36]. For instance, widespread use of uterotonics (e.g., oxytocin) for prevention and increased accessibility to blood banks have played pivotal roles in reducing mortality [37, 38]. The substantial decline in MH mortality in high and middle-high sociodemographic Index (SDI) regions, with annual percentage changes of −6.88% and −5.15%, respectively, reflects the successful integration of advanced healthcare systems, effective clinical guidelines, and well-trained medical professionals in these areas.

However, our study also untangles striking disparities in the rate of decline. Progress has been the slowest in low-SDI regions, where the MH mortality rate decreased by only 21.57% over the 40 year period, and the age-standardized mortality rate remained as high as 9.94 per 100,000 WCBA in 2021. In contrast, high-SDI regions have nearly eliminated this issue. This gap is a critical reflection of health inequities and the challenges faced in resource-limited settings [39, 40]. The slower progress in low-SDI regions may be associated with several factors, including limited healthcare infrastructure capacity, shortages of skilled health personnel, inadequate access to essential medications and safe blood transfusion services, and delays in recognizing and managing hemorrhage [41, 42]. Chad was the only country with a positive EAPC point estimate, though confidence intervals overlapped zero in both analyses, indicating no statistically significant upward trend. Nonetheless, Chad’s negligible progress—in contrast to rapid improvements seen in countries, such as Jordan and Maldives—underscores the persistent barriers to maternal survival in settings marked by political instability, health workforce shortages, and limited emergency obstetric capacity.

APC analysis further deepens our understanding of the temporal dynamics of MH mortality. The consistently declining period effects across all SDI groups highlight advancements in global medical technology and public health interventions. However, the steeper age gradients in low-SDI settings with mortality risk climbing from adolescence to a distinct peak at ages 35–39 before declining, indicating that the burden is concentrated at the peak reproductive years in countries, such as Nigeria and Ethiopia, rather than being broadly elevated across all ages. This age-specific elevation may be associated with factors including high parity, chronic malnutrition, limited access to specialized obstetric care, and the interaction between hemorrhage risk and comorbid conditions, all of which warrant further investigation in future studies [43]. Candidate mechanisms that merit hypothesis-driven testing include improvements in female educational attainment, rising socioeconomic status, better nutritional status during childhood and adolescence, increased contraceptive prevalence enabling birth spacing, and expanded healthcare coverage over successive generations. Cohort effects identified in our APC model suggest a gradual reduction in mortality risk among women born after 1970, which may hypothetically reflect generational improvements in educational attainment, socioeconomic status, nutritional status, contraceptive prevalence, and healthcare coverage over successive generations. However, as cohort effects represent statistical residuals after age and period adjustment rather than direct measures of social change, these interpretations remain speculative and should be regarded as hypothesis-generating; formal testing of specific mechanisms would require co-analysis with time-varying covariates, which falls beyond the scope of the current study. This is particularly evident in high-SDI regions, where cohort effects are minimal, indicating a consistently low-risk environment. In contrast, the persistently elevated risk among recent cohorts in low-SDI regions underscores the intergenerational transmission of health disadvantages and emphasizes the urgent need for sustained, equitable development initiatives.

The concentration of MH deaths within the peak reproductive ages (25–34 years) in high-SDI regions, compared to a more dispersed age distribution in low-SDI settings, offers unique insights into the challenges faced by different healthcare systems. This shift in high-SDI regions may reflect successful interventions targeting traditionally vulnerable groups—such as adolescents and women of advanced parity—which have made maternal care safer across the entire reproductive age distribution. In contrast, the persistent and widespread risk across all age groups in low-SDI regions reflects systemic failures in providing safe maternal care throughout the reproductive lifespan. This finding should prompt policymakers to reassess national strategies, ensuring that they not only focus on specific high-risk groups but also strengthen the overall health system to benefit all women of reproductive age.

Although this study provides a comprehensive analysis of MH-related mortality, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the GBD data rely on country-level reports, and vital registration is often incomplete in low-income settings, meaning observed regional disparities may partly reflect differential data quality rather than true mortality differences. MH deaths may be misclassified as other direct maternal causes in settings lacking standardized diagnostic protocols; although the GBD applies systematic adjustment procedures, residual attribution bias cannot be fully excluded. No formal sensitivity analysis restricting the model to countries with high vital registration completeness was conducted, and the robustness of SDI-stratified findings to data quality variation therefore remains uncertain. Second, individual-level risk factors are unavailable in GBD data, limiting exploration of underlying determinants. Estimates for 1982–1989 derive from back-casted models and carry greater uncertainty. Cohort effects cannot be causally attributed to specific determinants without covariate adjustment, and alternative cohort specifications were not examined in sensitivity analysis; findings should be interpreted with these constraints in mind.

Third, WCBA rather than live births was used as the denominator, precluding direct comparison with SDG 3.1 targets. No sensitivity analysis under a live birth-based denominator was performed, and the influence of this choice on reported trends has not been formally assessed. Future studies incorporating live-birth estimates would enable direct SDG benchmarking. Finally, the study period does not fully capture the impact of recent public health emergencies such as the COVID-19 pandemic on maternal mortality outcomes [44].

Conclusion

In summary, despite substantial global progress, maternal hemorrhage mortality remains unequally distributed and continues to disproportionately affect low-SDI countries. Our study provides compelling evidence that the burden of MH-related mortality has become increasingly concentrated in a few low-SDI countries, thereby exacerbating global health disparities. The global health community and national governments must acknowledge this reality and move away from a “one-size-fits-all” approach. Policies must be tailored to address region- and country-specific challenges, with a focus on strengthening primary healthcare, improving access to essential resources, and building resilient health systems in the most vulnerable regions. By leveraging data-driven insights such as those provided in this study, we can more effectively target interventions, allocate resources, and ultimately strive toward equity in global maternal health outcomes.
