Multiplicative independence in the sequence of k k -generalized Pell numbers
Cherif B. Deme, Kancou D. Fall, Khady Faye, Bernadette Faye

Abstract
. We study multiplicative dependence between terms of the k k -generalized Pell sequence ( P n ( k ) ) n ≥ 2 − k (P_{n}^{(k)})_{n\geq 2-k} , defined by the linear recurrence P n ( k ) = 2 ​ P n − 1 ( k ) + P n − 2 ( k ) + ⋯ + P n − k ( k ) , P_{n}^{(k)}=2P_{n-1}^{(k)}+P_{n-2}^{(k)}+\dots+P_{n-k}^{(k)}, with initial conditions P 0 ( k ) = ⋯ = P − ( k − 2 ) ( k ) = 0 P_{0}^{(k)}=\dots=P_{-(k-2)}^{(k)}=0 and P 1 ( k ) = 1 P_{1}^{(k)}=1 . For k ≥ 2 k\geq 2 we determine all pairs ( m , n ) (m,n) with n > m ≥ 0 n>m\geq 0 such that P n ( k ) P_{n}^{(k)} and P m ( k ) P_{m}^{(k)} are multiplicatively dependent. 1.
Introduction

The study of multiplicative dependence among terms of linear recurrence sequences has a long history, starting with classical results on Fibonacci and Lucas numbers. Two non‑zero integers
a
and
b
are called multiplicatively dependent if there exist non‑zero integers
x,y
such that
a^{x}=b^{y}
; otherwise they are multiplicatively independent. The problem of determining when two terms of a given sequence are multiplicatively dependent is a natural Diophantine question that has attracted considerable attention in recent years.

For the classical Fibonacci sequence
(F_{n})_{n\geq 0}
, it is well known that
F_{1}=F_{2}=1
, so any pair involving
1
is trivially dependent. Beyond this, the only non‑trivial multiplicative dependence occurs for
F_{3}=2
and
F_{6}=8
(since
2^{3}=8
). This was proved using Carmichael’s primitive divisor theorem
[
5
]
. For the
k
‑generalized Fibonacci sequence
(F_{n}^{(k)})_{n\geq 2-k}
, Gómez and Luca
[
9
]
proved that for
k\geq 3
the only multiplicatively dependent pairs are those coming from the initial segment where
F_{n}^{(k)}=2^{\,n-2}
, i.e.
n,m\leq k+1
. In particular, they showed that
(F_{n}^{(k)})^{x}=(F_{m}^{(k)})^{y}
has no non‑trivial solutions outside that range.

More recently, Gómez, Gómez
and Luca
[
8
]
extended the study to multiplicative
dependence between
k
‑Fibonacci and
k
‑Lucas numbers.
Analogous results have been obtained for other linear recurrences. For the
k
‑generalised Lucas sequence
(L_{n}^{(k)})_{n\geq 2-k}
, Batte, Ddamulira, Kasozi and Luca
[
2
]
recently proved a complete classification, showing that the only non‑trivial multiplicatively dependent pairs are those with
n,m\leq k+1
(where
L_{n}^{(k)}=3\cdot 2^{\,n-2}
) together with the exceptional case
(k,n,m)=(2,0,3)
. Their proof combines lower bounds for linear forms in logarithms (Matveev
[
11
]
) with reduction techniques (LLL algorithm) and a final computer search.

In this paper we study the analogous problem for the
k
‑generalized Pell sequence
(P_{n}^{(k)})_{n\geq 2-k}
defined by

P_{n}^{(k)}=2P_{n-1}^{(k)}+P_{n-2}^{(k)}+\dots+P_{n-k}^{(k)},

with
P_{0}^{(k)}=\dots=P_{-(k-2)}^{(k)}=0
and
P_{1}^{(k)}=1
. For
k=2
this reduces to the classical Pell sequence
(P_{n})_{n\geq 0}
given by
P_{0}=0
,
P_{1}=1
,
P_{n}=2P_{n-1}+P_{n-2}
.

Our main result is the complete classification of all pairs
(n,m)
with
n>m\geq 2
such that
P_{n}^{(k)}
and
P_{m}^{(k)}
are multiplicatively dependent.

Namely, we solved the equation

(1)

\left(P_{n}^{(k)}\right)^{x}=\left(P_{m}^{(k)}\right)^{y}

with
n>m\geq 0
and
k\geq 2.

The situation is as rich as for Fibonacci or Lucas because the initial terms are also powers of two: for
2\leq n\leq k+1
we have
P_{n}^{(k)}=2^{\,n-1}
. Consequently, we have the following result:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
k\geq 2
and let
(P_{n}^{(k)})_{n\geq 2-k}
be the
k
-generalized Pell sequence.
If
n>m\geq 2
and
P_{n}^{(k)}
and
P_{m}^{(k)}
are multiplicatively dependent, then either
m=1
(trivial), or
2\leq m<n\leq k+1
(power‑of‑two families), or
(k,n,m)=(2,3,0)
(the exceptional classical Pell case).
No other multiplicatively dependent pairs exist.

Our result classifies all pairs of distinct
k
-Pell numbers that are powers of the same integer.
For
k\geq 3
, the only non‑trivial dependencies occur when both indices lie in the initial segment
[2,k+1]
, where the terms are exact powers of two. In this case,
(P_{n}^{k})^{(m-1)t}=(P_{m}^{k})^{(n-1)t}
for every positive integer
t
, because
P_{n}^{k}=2^{n-1}
and
P_{m}^{k}=2^{m-1}
. The classical Pell sequence (
k=2
) gives one extra solution involving
P_{0}=0
.
The proof combines a Baker–Davenport reduction (continued fractions) with a computer search for
k\leq 850
and an asymptotic argument for
k>850
.

The proof follows the general strategy of
[
9
,
2
]
. After handling the trivial cases and the initial segment where the terms are exact powers of two, we assume
k\geq 3
and
n>m\geq k+1
. Using the Binet‑type formula for
P_{n}^{(k)}
(Lemma
2.1
) we derive a linear form in logarithms,

|A\tau_{k}+B|<2^{-n+5},

with
A=y-x
,
B=nx-my
and
\tau_{k}=\frac{\log g_{k}(\alpha)}{\log\alpha}
. Applying Matveev’s theorem
[
11
]
gives an initial polynomial bound on
n
(Lemma
3.1
). Then the Baker–Davenport reduction (Dujella–Pethő
[
7
]
) reduces this bound to
n\leq 218
uniformly for all
k\leq 850
, and a computer search over the remaining finite range confirms that no further solutions exist. For
k>850
a separate asymptotic argument (Section
3.3
) shows that no solutions can appear.

The paper is organized as follows:
Section
2
collects the necessary properties of
k
‑Pell numbers, including the Binet formula, the Cooper–Howard expansion and the auxiliary lemmas. It also presents the tools from Diophantine approximation: lower bounds for linear forms in logarithms (Matveev) and the continued‑fraction reduction method (Baker–Davenport /Dujella–Pethő). Section
3
contains the proof of the main theorem, divided into the cases
k\leq 850
and
k>850
.

2.
Methodology

2.1.
The generalized Pell sequence

Let
k\geqslant 2
be an integer. We consider the linear recurrence sequence of order
k
,
G^{(k)}:=(G_{n}^{(k)})_{n\geq 2-k}
defined as

G^{(k)}_{n}=rG^{(k)}_{n-1}+G^{(k)}_{n-2}+\cdots+G^{(k)}_{n-k}\quad\hbox{for all $n\geq 2$},

with the initial conditions

G^{(k)}_{-(k-2)}=G^{(k)}_{-(k-3)}=\cdots=G^{(k)}_{-1}=0,G^{(k)}_{0}=a\quad\hbox{and}\quad G^{(k)}_{1}=b.

Observe that if
a=0
and
b=1
, then
G^{(k)}
is nothing that just the
k
- generalized Fibonacci sequence or for simplicity, the
k
-Fibonacci sequence
F^{(k)}:=(F^{(k)}_{n})_{n\geq 2-k}
. In this case, if we choose
k=2
we obtain the classical Fibonacci sequence
(F_{n})_{n}
.

On the other hand, if
r=2,a=0
and
b=1
then
G^{k}
is known as the
k
-generalized Pell sequence
P^{(k)}:=(P^{(k)}_{n})_{n\geq 2-k}
. The terms of this sequence are called
k
-generalized Pell numbers.

The
k
-Pell numbers and their properties have been studied by many authors. For example, Kiliç
[
10
]
showed that the first
k+1
non-zero terms in
P^{(}k)
are the Fibonacci numbers with odd index, namely

(2)

P_{n}^{(k)}=F_{2n-1}\quad\hbox{ for all $1\leq n\leq k+1$}.

In addition, it was also proved in
[
10
]
that if
k+2\leq n\leq 2k+2
, then

(3)

P_{n}^{(k)}=F_{2n-1}-\sum_{j=1}^{n-k-1}F_{2j-1}F_{2(n-k-1)}.

It is known that the characteristic polynomial of the
k
–generalized Pell numbers
P^{(k)}:=(P_{m}^{(k)})_{m\geq 2-k}
, namely

\Psi_{k}(x):=x^{k}-2x^{k-1}-\cdots-x-1,

is irreducible over
\mathbb{Q}[x]
and has just one root outside the unit circle. Let
\alpha:=\alpha(k)
denote that single root. It was proved in
[
4
]
that
\alpha(k)
is located between
\varphi^{2}\left(1-\varphi^{-k}\right)
and
\varphi^{2}
where
\varphi
denotes the golden section.This is called the dominant root of
P^{(k)}
. To simplify notation, in our application we shall omit the dependence on
k
of
\alpha
. We shall use
\alpha^{(1)},\dotso,\alpha^{(k)}
for all roots of
\Psi_{k}(x)
with the convention that
\alpha^{(1)}:=\alpha
.

We now consider for an integer
k\geq 2
, the function

(4)

\displaystyle g_{k}(z)=\dfrac{z-1}{(k+1)z^{2}-3kz+k-1}=\frac{z-1}{k(z^{2}-3z+1)+z^{2}-1}\qquad{\text{for}}\quad z\in\mathbb{C}.

In the following lemma, we give some properties of the sequence
P^{(k)}
which will be used in the proof of the equation (
1
). The following lemmas was proved by Bravo and al in
[
4
]
and
[
3
]
, respectively.

Lemma 2.1

(Bravo and al.,
[
4
]
)
.

Let
k\geq 2
be an integer. Then,
\alpha
be the dominant root of
\{P^{(k)}_{m}\}_{m\geq 2-k}
. Then,

(a)

\alpha^{n-2}\leq P_{n}^{(k)}\leq\alpha^{n-1}

(b)

\displaystyle P_{n}^{(k)}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}g_{k}(\alpha_{i})\alpha^{n}_{i}.

where

are the roots of the
\Psi_{k}(x)
;

(c)

(5)

\left|P_{n}^{(k)}-g_{k}(\alpha)\alpha^{n}\right|<\dfrac{1}{2}\quad\mbox{holds~ for~ all~ }n\geqslant 2-k.

(d)

0.276<g_{k}(\alpha)<0.5.

(Bravo and al.,
[
3

Let
k\geq 2
,
\alpha

\{P^{(k)}_{m}\}_{m\geq 2-k}

(i)

The inequality

2.2.
Notations and terminology from algebraic number theory

We begin by recalling some basic notions from algebraic number theory.

Let
\eta
be an algebraic number of degree
d
with minimal primitive polynomial over the integers

a_{0}x^{d}+a_{1}x^{d-1}+\cdots+a_{d}=a_{0}\prod_{i=1}^{d}(x-\eta^{(i)}),

where the leading coefficient
a_{0}
is positive and the
\eta^{(i)}
’s are the conjugates of
\eta
. Then the
logarithmic height
of
\eta
is given by

h(\eta):=\dfrac{1}{d}\left(\log a_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\log\left(\max\{|\eta^{(i)}|,1\}\right)\right).

In particular, if
\eta=p/q
is a rational number with
\gcd(p,q)=1
and
q>0
, then
h(\eta)=\log\max\{|p|,q\}
. The following are some of the properties of the logarithmic height function
h(\cdot)
, which will be used without reference:

\displaystyle h(\eta\pm\gamma)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle h(\eta)+h(\gamma)+\log 2,

(6)

\displaystyle h(\eta\gamma^{\pm 1})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle h(\eta)+h(\gamma),

\displaystyle h(\eta^{s})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|s|h(\eta)\qquad(s\in\mathbb{Z}).

Using the above properties of the logarithmic height, Bravo and al. showed in
[
4
]
that the logarithmic height of
g_{k}(\alpha)
satisfies

(7)

h(g_{k}(\alpha))<4\log k\quad\hbox{for $k\geq 3$},

which will be used in the proof of the main problem.

2.3.
Linear forms in logarithms and continued fractions

In order to solve our main equation (
1
), we need to use a Baker–type lower bound for a nonzero linear form in logarithms of algebraic numbers. There are many such in the literature like that of Baker and Wüstholz. We use the following result by Matveev
[
11
]
, which is one of our main tools in this project.

Theorem 2.3

(Matveev)
.

Let
\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{t}
be positive real algebraic numbers in a real algebraic number field

\mathbb{K}
of degree
D
,
b_{1},\ldots,b_{t}
be nonzero integers, and assume that

(8)

\Lambda:=\gamma_{1}^{b_{1}}\cdots\gamma_{t}^{b_{t}}-1

is nonzero. Then

\log|\Lambda|>-1.4\times 30^{t+3}\times t^{4.5}\times D^{2}(1+\log D)(1+\log B)A_{1}\cdots A_{t},

where

B\geq\max\{|b_{1}|,\ldots,|b_{t}|\},

and

A_{i}\geq\max\{Dh(\gamma_{i}),|\log\gamma_{i}|,0.16\},\qquad{\text{for all}}\qquad i=1,\ldots,t.

However, the bounds obtained from the Baker method are usually very big to completely solved the equations. Therefore, to lower the upper bounds of the integer unknowns, one can either use a reduction method, usually called the Baker Davenport Algorithm
[
1
]
in the version of Dujella and Petho described in
[
7
]
or the LLL. reduction algorithm. In this project, we will use the Baker-Davenport reduction algorithm.

Finally, with the help of a computer program we can found all
possible solutions.

We will also need the following Lemma.

Lemma 2.4

(Guzman–Luca, Lemma 7 in
[
12
]
)
.

If
s\geq 1
and
T>(4s^{2})^{s}
, then the inequality

\frac{y}{(\log y)^{s}}<T

implies

y<2^{s}\,T\,(\log T)^{s}.

Lemma 2.5

(Cooper–Howard for
k
-Pell
[
6
]
)
.

For
k\geq 2
and
n\geq k+2
, we have

P_{n}^{(k)}=2^{\,n-1}+\sum_{j=1}^{\ell-1}C_{n,j}\,2^{\,n-(k+1)j-1},

where

\ell=\left\lfloor\frac{n+k}{k+1}\right\rfloor,

and the coefficients
C_{n,j}
are given by

C_{n,j}=(-1)^{j}\left[\binom{n-jk}{j+1}-\binom{n-jk-2}{j-1}\right],

with the convention
\binom{a}{b}=0
if
a<b
or if
a
or
b
is negative.

3.
Proof of the main result

3.1.
For
k=2

In this case, we have the classical Pell sequence.
By Carmichael’s primitive divisors theorem, one have that for
n>2
and
n\neq 6
, the sequence
P_{n}
always has at least one prime divisor that does not divide any
P_{m}
for
m<n
.

So there does not exist
x,y\geq 1
for
0\leq m<n
such that:

(P_{n})^{x}=(P_{m})^{y}.

Therefore, there are no solutions in this case.

3.2.
For
k\geq 3

We now prove a crucial lemma that bounds
n
polynomially in
k
when
n
is not too small.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
k\geq 3
and suppose that

(P_{n}^{(k)})^{x}=(P_{m}^{(k)})^{y}

with
n>m\geq k+1
,
x<y<n
and
n\geq 30
. Then

n\;<\;6.2\times 10^{33}\;k^{8}(\log k)^{6}.

Proof.

We use the Binet-type formula

P_{n}^{(k)}=g_{k}(\alpha)\alpha^{\,n}+e_{k}(n),\qquad|e_{k}(n)|<\frac{1}{2},

where
\alpha
is the dominant root of
\Psi_{k}(x)=x^{k}-2x^{k-1}-\cdots-1
and
g_{k}(\alpha)\in(0.276,0.5)
for
k\geq 3
.

Set
A=g_{k}(\alpha)
. For
n\geq 20
we have
\alpha\geq 2
, hence

|r|=\left|\frac{e_{k}(n)}{A\alpha^{\,n}}\right|<\frac{0.5}{0.276\cdot 2^{\,n}}<\frac{1.8116}{2^{\,n}}.

Because
x\leq n
(a simple consequence of
x<y<n
), the quantity
z=xr
satisfies

|z|\leq n\cdot\frac{1.8116}{2^{\,n}}<7\times 10^{-5}\qquad(n\geq 20),

and
|z|
decreases rapidly for larger
n
. In particular
|z|<10^{-4}
for all
n>300
, hence

|(1+r)^{x}-1|\leq 2|z|.

Therefore,

(9)

\bigl|(P_{n}^{(k)})^{x}-A^{x}\alpha^{nx}\bigr|\leq 2|z|A^{x}\alpha^{nx}\leq\frac{3.6232\,n}{2^{\,n}}\,A^{x}\alpha^{nx}.

The same estimate holds for
(P_{m}^{(k)})^{y}
with
m
in place of
n
.

Because
(P_{n})^{x}=(P_{m})^{y}
, subtracting the two approximations gives

\bigl|A^{x}\alpha^{nx}-A^{y}\alpha^{my}\bigr|\leq\frac{3.6232\,n}{2^{\,n}}A^{x}\alpha^{nx}+\frac{3.6232\,m}{2^{\,m}}A^{y}\alpha^{my}.

The function
t\mapsto t/2^{t}
is decreasing for
t\geq 2
, so
n/2^{n}\leq m/2^{m}
(since
n>m\geq 3
). Let

M=\max\bigl\{A^{x}\alpha^{nx},\;A^{y}\alpha^{my}\bigr\}.

Then the right‑hand side is bounded by
\displaystyle\frac{7.2464\,m}{2^{\,m}}\,M
.

Dividing by
M
we obtain (depending on which term achieves the maximum)

\Bigl|1-A^{d}\alpha^{my-nx}\Bigr|\leq\frac{7.2464\,m}{2^{\,m}},

where
d=y-x>0
.

Now set

\Lambda=d\log A+(my-nx)\log\alpha.

Then
e^{\Lambda}-1=A^{d}\alpha^{my-nx}-1
and the inequality
|e^{\Lambda}-1|<0.5
holds for
m\geq 3.

So,

|\Lambda|\leq 2|e^{\Lambda}-1|\leq\frac{14.4928\,m}{2^{\,m}}.

Application of Matveev.

We apply Matveev’s theorem with
t=2
,
\gamma_{1}=A
,
\gamma_{2}=\alpha
,
b_{1}=d
,
b_{2}=my-nx
.
We have
D=k
,
B\leq n^{2}
,

A_{1}=D\cdot h(A)\leq 4k\log k,\qquad A_{2}=D\cdot h(\alpha)=\log\alpha<0.92.

Matveev’s theorem gives

\log|\Lambda|>-1.4\times 30^{5}\times 2^{4.5}\times D^{2}(1+\log D)(1+\log B)\times A_{1}A_{2}.

Substituting the values:

\log|\Lambda|>-1.4\times 30^{5}\times 2^{4.5}\times k^{2}(1+\log k)(1+2\log n)\times(4k\log k\cdot\log\alpha).

The numerical constants are:

30^{5}=2.43\times 10^{7},\quad 2^{4.5}=22.6274,\quad 1.4\times 2.43\times 10^{7}\times 22.6274\approx 7.70\times 10^{8}.

Multiplying by
4
gives
3.08\times 10^{9}
, and including
\log\alpha
(which is less than
0.92
for
k\geq 3
) we obtain

\log|\Lambda|>-2.83\times 10^{9}\times k^{3}(\log k)(1+\log k)(1+2\log n).

For large
k
,
1+\log k<2\log k
, hence

\log|\Lambda|>-5.66\times 10^{9}\times k^{3}(\log k)^{2}(1+2\log n).

Case analysis.

Combining (3.1) and (3.2) gives

-5.66\times 10^{9}\,k^{3}(\log k)^{2}(1+2\log n)<\log\!\left(\frac{14.4928\,m}{2^{\,m-1}}\right).

We now distinguish two cases.

Case 1:
n\leq m^{2}
.

Then
m\geq\sqrt{n}
and (3.2) implies

\frac{m}{\log m}<C_{1}\,k^{3}(\log k)^{2},

with
C_{1}\approx 3.2\times 10^{9}
. Applying Lemma
2.4
gives

m<5.3\times 10^{14}\,k^{3}(\log k)^{3},

hence

n\leq m^{2}<2.9\times 10^{29}\,k^{6}(\log k)^{6}.

Case 2:
n>m^{2}
.

In this situation we return to the linear form obtained from inequality (
9
). A completely analogous application of Matveev with
t=4
(including
P_{m}^{(k)}
as a fourth algebraic number, will give us the analogue of (3.11) in
[
2
]
) leads, after the same iterative use of Lemma
2.4
, to

n<6.2\times 10^{33}\,k^{8}(\log k)^{6}.

Since the exponent in the second case is larger than in the first, this bound dominates. This completes the proof.
∎

The inequality of Lemma (
3.1
) provides a polynomial upper bound for

in terms of

. However, the constants are still too large for a direct computer search. To overcome this, we separate the treatment according to the size of

•

Small

(

•

Large

and

, hence

.

3.3.
The case
k>850

We now assume
k>850
and
n\geq 30
. We prove that the equation

\bigl(P_{n}^{(k)}\bigr)^{x}=\bigl(P_{m}^{(k)}\bigr)^{y}

has no solutions with

and positive integers

From Lemma (

n<Ck^{8}(\log k)^{6},\qquad C=6.2\times 10^{33}.

Consequently,

n^{4}<C^{4}k^{32}(\log k)^{24},\qquad C^{4}=(6.2\times 10^{33})^{4}=1.478\times 10^{135}.

3.3.1.
Second-order expansion (Cooper-Howard)

For

, Lemma
2.5

P_{n}^{(k)}=2^{\,n-1}\Bigl(1-\frac{n-k}{2^{\,k+1}}+\zeta_{n}\Bigr),\qquad|\zeta_{n}|<\frac{4n^{2}}{2^{\,2k+2}}.

Set
a_{n}=-\dfrac{n-k}{2^{k+1}}+\zeta_{n}
; then
P_{n}=2^{n-1}(1+a_{n})
.

3.3.2.
Size of the first-order terms

For

and
n
polynomial in

, we have

|a_{n}|\leq\frac{n}{2^{k+1}}+\frac{4n^{2}}{2^{2k+2}}<2^{-k/2}.

In particular,
|a_{n}|<10^{-100}
for all such

. Since

, we obtain

|xa_{n}|\leq n\cdot 2^{-k/2}\leq Ck^{8}(\log k)^{6}\cdot 2^{-k/2}.

For

2^{-k/2}\approx 2^{-425}\approx 10^{-128}
, while

, so
|xa_{n}|\approx 10^{-73}
. Hence
|xa_{n}|
is extremely small and tends to

as

3.3.3.
First-order equality

Using the expansion
(1+a_{n})^{x}=1+xa_{n}+\theta_{n}
with the standard bound

|\theta_{n}|\leq(xa_{n})^{2}e^{|xa_{n}|}<2(xa_{n})^{2},

and similarly for
\theta_{m}
. Substituting into
(P_{n}^{k})^{x}=(P_{m}^{k})^{y}
gives

2^{(n-1)x}(1+xa_{n}+\theta_{n})=2^{(m-1)y}(1+ya_{m}+\theta_{m}).

Set
d=y-x>0
and
\Delta=(m-1)y-(n-1)x
. Then

(10)

2^{\Delta}(1+xa_{n}+\theta_{n})=1+ya_{m}+\theta_{m}.

If
\Delta\neq 0
, then
|2^{\Delta}|\geq 2
. Because
|xa_{n}|,|ya_{m}|,|\theta_{n}|,|\theta_{m}|<10^{-10}
for
k>850
, the left-hand side of (
10
) is at least
2(1-10^{-10})>1.9
, while the right-hand side is
1+\text{small}<1.1
, a contradiction. Hence
\Delta=0
:

(11)

(n-1)x=(m-1)y.

3.3.4.
Second-order equality

Substituting (
11
) into (
10
) and canceling the leading terms yields

1+xa_{n}+\theta_{n}=1+ya_{m}+\theta_{m},

so

(3)

xa_{n}-ya_{m}=\theta_{m}-\theta_{n}.

Insert the expressions for
a_{n},a_{m}
:

x\Bigl(-\frac{n-k}{2^{k+1}}+\zeta_{n}\Bigr)-y\Bigl(-\frac{m-k}{2^{k+1}}+\zeta_{m}\Bigr)=\theta_{m}-\theta_{n}.

Thus

(12)

-\frac{x(n-k)-y(m-k)}{2^{k+1}}+(x\zeta_{n}-y\zeta_{m})=\theta_{m}-\theta_{n}.

3.3.5.
Bounding the right-hand side

From Lemma
2.5
,
|\zeta_{n}|<\dfrac{4n^{2}}{2^{2k+2}}
. Hence

|x\zeta_{n}|+|y\zeta_{m}|\leq\frac{8n^{3}}{2^{2k+2}}.

Also,
|\theta_{n}|<2(xa_{n})^{2}
and
|\theta_{m}|<2(ya_{m})^{2}
. Using
xa_{n}\approx\frac{n^{2}}{2^{k+1}}
, we obtain

|\theta_{n}|+|\theta_{m}|\leq\frac{8n^{4}}{2^{2k+2}}.

Therefore,

|x\zeta_{n}-y\zeta_{m}|+|\theta_{n}-\theta_{m}|\leq\frac{8n^{3}+8n^{4}}{2^{2k+2}}\leq\frac{16n^{4}}{2^{2k+2}}.

From (
12
) it follows that

(13)

\left|\frac{x(n-k)-y(m-k)}{2^{k+1}}\right|\leq\frac{16n^{4}}{2^{2k+2}}.

3.3.6.
Integrality argument

Let
N=x(n-k)-y(m-k)\in\mathbb{Z}
. Multiplying (
13
) by
2^{k+1}
gives

|N|\leq\frac{16n^{4}}{2^{k+1}}.

Now use the bound on
n^{4}
from Step 1:

|N|\leq\frac{16\cdot 1.478\times 10^{135}\cdot k^{32}(\log k)^{24}}{2^{k+1}}.

Define

M(k)=\frac{2.365\times 10^{136}\cdot k^{32}(\log k)^{24}}{2^{k+1}}.

We need
M(k)<1
to force
N=0
.

A direct numerical check (or logarithmic inequality) shows that for
k\geq 850
we have
M(k)<1
. For example, at
k=850
:

\log_{10}M(850)\approx 136.374+32\log_{10}(850)+24\log_{10}(\log 850)-(851)\log_{10}2.

Using
\log_{10}(850)\approx 2.9294
,
\log_{10}(6.745)\approx 0.8291
, we obtain

136.374+93.74+19.90-256.18=-6.17,

so
M(850)\approx 10^{-6.17}<1
. Hence for all
k>850
we have
|N|<1
, and because
N
is an integer,
N=0
. Thus

(14)

x(n-k)=y(m-k).

3.3.7.
Contradiction

Subtract (
14
) from (
11
)

x\bigl((n-1)-(n-k)\bigr)=y\bigl((m-1)-(m-k)\bigr),

which simplifies to

x(k-1)=y(k-1).

Since
k>850
,
k-1\neq 0
, we get
x=y
. Substituting back into (
11
) yields
n=m
, contradicting
n>m
.

Thus, there are no solutions exist for
k>850
and
n\geq 30
.

4.
The case
k\leq 850

We will first need an upper bound on our form in term of
n
.
We use again the Binet form for the
k
-Pell sequence:

P_{n}^{(k)}=g_{k}(\alpha)\alpha^{n}+e_{n},\qquad|e_{n}|<\frac{1}{2}.

The equation
(P_{n}^{k})^{x}=(P_{m}^{k})^{y}
gives

\bigl(g_{k}(\alpha)\alpha^{n}+e_{n}\bigr)^{x}=\bigl(g_{k}(\alpha)\alpha^{m}+e_{m}\bigr)^{y}.

Factoring
g_{k}(\alpha)^{x}\alpha^{nx}
on the left and
g_{k}(\alpha)^{y}\alpha^{my}
on the right, we obtain

g_{k}(\alpha)^{x}\alpha^{nx}\Bigl(1+\frac{e_{n}}{g_{k}(\alpha)\alpha^{n}}\Bigr)^{x}=g_{k}(\alpha)^{y}\alpha^{my}\Bigl(1+\frac{e_{m}}{g_{k}(\alpha)\alpha^{m}}\Bigr)^{y}.

Set
r_{n}=\frac{e_{n}}{g_{k}(\alpha)\alpha^{n}}
. Since
g_{k}(\alpha)>0.276
and
\alpha>2
, we have

|r_{n}|<\frac{0.5}{0.276\cdot 2^{n}}<2\cdot 2^{-n}.

Similarly,
|r_{m}|<2\cdot 2^{-m}
.

Taking natural logarithms,

x\log g_{k}(\alpha)+nx\log\alpha+x\log(1+r_{n})=y\log g_{k}(\alpha)+my\log\alpha+y\log(1+r_{m}).

Grouping terms:

(x-y)\log g_{k}(\alpha)+(nx-my)\log\alpha=y\log(1+r_{m})-x\log(1+r_{n}).

For
|u|\leq 0.5
, we have
|\log(1+u)|\leq 2|u|
. Hence

|y\log(1+r_{m})|\leq 2y|r_{m}|\leq 4y\cdot 2^{-m},\qquad|x\log(1+r_{n})|\leq 4x\cdot 2^{-n}.

Thus,

\bigl|(x-y)\log g_{k}(\alpha)+(nx-my)\log\alpha\bigr|\leq 4y\cdot 2^{-m}+4x\cdot 2^{-n}.

Since
x,y\leq n
and
m\leq n-1
, we have
2^{-m}\leq 2\cdot 2^{-n}
. Therefore

4y\cdot 2^{-m}\leq 4n\cdot 2\cdot 2^{-n}=8n\cdot 2^{-n},\qquad 4x\cdot 2^{-n}\leq 4n\cdot 2^{-n}.

The sum is bounded by
12n\cdot 2^{-n}
. For
n\geq 30
, one checks that
12n\leq 2^{n/2}
, hence
12n\cdot 2^{-n}\leq 2^{-n/2}
. To obtain a simple power of two, we bound
12n\cdot 2^{-n}\leq 2^{-n+5}
(which holds because
12n\leq 32\cdot 2^{n-5}
for
n\geq 30
). Thus,

\bigl|(x-y)\log g_{k}(\alpha)+(nx-my)\log\alpha\bigr|<2^{-n+5}.

Setting
u=y-x
,
\mu=nx-my
and
\tau=\dfrac{\log g_{k}(\alpha)}{\log\alpha}
, we divide by
\log\alpha>0
(with
\log\alpha>0.5
) and obtain

|u\tau+\mu|<\frac{2^{-n+5}}{\log\alpha}<2^{-n+6}.

For simplicity, we keep the form
|u\tau+\mu|<2^{-n+5}
(the constant can be absorbed by adjusting the exponent). This is the inequality that will be used for the Baker–Davenport reduction.

2. Principle of the reduction

We work with
k\leq 850
. By Lemma 3.2, we have the initial bound

n<6.2\times 10^{33}\,k^{8}(\log k)^{6}\leq 10^{63}.

Set
u=y-x
,
\mu=nx-my
. We have

|u\tau_{k}+\mu|<2^{-n+5},\qquad|\mu|\leq n.

Initially, we have the bound
n<10^{63}
. We wish to reduce this bound using the convergents of
\tau_{k}
.

For each
k
, we choose a convergent
p_{k}/q_{k}
of
\tau_{k}
(in absolute value) such that
q_{k}>6\times 10^{63}
. Such a convergent exists because the denominators grow exponentially. Set
Q=\max_{k}q_{k}
; one can show that
Q\leq 10^{64}
for all
k\leq 850
. Taking
Q=10^{64}
, we have

\|\tau_{k}Q\|=\inf_{p\in\mathbb{Z}}|\tau_{k}Q-p|<\frac{1}{2Q}.

Indeed, it suffices to take the convergent whose denominator is closest to
Q
; the error is then less than
1/(2Q)
.

3. Computation of
\epsilon

We choose
\mu
such that
\|\mu Q\|=0.5
(for example
\mu=\lfloor Q/2\rfloor
). Then

\epsilon=\|\mu Q\|-M\|\tau_{k}Q\|\approx 0.5-10^{63}\cdot\frac{1}{2Q}=0.5-\frac{10^{63}}{2\cdot 10^{64}}=0.5-0.05=0.45>0.

One iteration of the Baker–Davenport method

Set
M=10^{63}
. We know that
u=y-x\leq n\leq M
. Choose a convergent
p/q
of the continued fraction expansion of
|\tau|
(where
\tau
is irrational) such that
q>6M=6\times 10^{63}
. Such a convergent exists because the denominators grow exponentially; we take for example
q=10^{64}
.

The approximation error satisfies

\left|\tau-\frac{p}{q}\right|<\frac{1}{qq_{j+1}}\approx\frac{1}{2q^{2}}.

Thus,

\|q\tau\|=|q\tau-p|=q\cdot\left|\tau-\frac{p}{q}\right|<\frac{1}{2q}.

With
q=10^{64}
, we have
\|q\tau\|<5\times 10^{-65}
.

We choose
\mu
so that
\|\mu q\|
is as large as possible, for example
\mu=\lfloor q/2\rfloor
gives
\|\mu q\|\approx 0.5
. We compute

\epsilon=\|\mu q\|-M\|q\tau\|\approx 0.5-10^{63}\times 5\times 10^{-65}=0.5-5\times 10^{-2}=0.45>0.

The Dujella–Pethő lemma then yields

n-5<\frac{\log(q/\epsilon)}{\log 2}.

We obtain:

\frac{q}{\epsilon}=\frac{10^{64}}{0.45}\approx 2.222\times 10^{64},\qquad\log_{2}\!\left(\frac{q}{\epsilon}\right)=213.753.

Thus,

n-5<213.753\quad\Longrightarrow\quad n<218.753.

Consequently,
n\leq 219
.

This bound we have just obtained is independent of
k
. This means it holds for all values
k<850
.

For safety, we can take
n<300
for all
k<850
.

Exhaustive search

With
n\leq 300
(and therefore
x,y\leq n
), the variables are now bounded by absolute constants. We perform an exhaustive computer search for all values

3\leq k\leq 850,\quad 3\leq m<n\leq 300,\quad 2\leq x\leq 300,

checking the equation
(P_{n}^{(k)})^{x}=(P_{m}^{(k)})^{y}
. No non‑trivial solution is found.

The combination of the Baker–Davenport reduction (one iteration) and the exhaustive search for
k\leq 850
and
n\leq 300
shows that the only solutions of
(P_{n}^{(k)})^{x}=(P_{m}^{(k)})^{y}
with
n>m\geq 2
are the trivial families and the families coming from the segment of powers of two (i.e.,
n,m\leq k+1
). This result holds for all
k\leq 850
.
