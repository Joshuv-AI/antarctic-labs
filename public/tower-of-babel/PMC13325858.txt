Glomerular Injury Induced by Daily Exposure to the Low-Frequency Component of Environmental Noise via Endothelin Signaling in Mice
Authors: Takumi Kagawa, Dijie Chen, Nobutaka Ohgami, Keming Tong, Yanjun Gao, Naruhito Iwasaki, Akihito Harusato, Toyonori Tsuzuki, Takumi Hayashi, Yuuki Shimizu, Toyoaki Murohara, Masashi Kato
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Epidemiological studies suggest that environmental noise is associated with kidney dysfunction in humans; however, the underlying characteristics, such as frequency, intensity, and duration, remain unclear. Effects of exposure to whole environmental noise generated by electric devices (all frequencies), low-frequency noise (LFN; ≤100 Hz), and non-LFN (>100 Hz) for 12-h/day for 5 consecutive days on kidney function were investigated in mice. Exposure to whole environmental noise at ≤ 88 dBA elevated serum creatinine and blood urea nitrogen (BUN). This effect was reproduced by LFN exposure alone (≤70 dBA) but not by non-LFN exposure (≤87 dBA). Biochemically, LFN increased renal expression of endothelin-1 and endothelial nitric oxide synthase, biomarkers of vascular injury. Histologically, LFN increased glomerular and mesangial areas and thickened glomerular basement membranes. Pharmacological inhibition of endothelin signaling reduced serum creatinine and BUN levels and mitigated the LFN-induced glomerular damage. Our findings provide the first direct evidence that the low-frequency component of environmental noise, well below the murine audible range, induces glomerular injury via endothelin signaling and results in kidney dysfunction in mice. Considering prior human cross-sectional studies, these murine findings warrant further investigation to determine their translational relevance in humans.

Introduction

Daily exposure to environmental noise has attracted worldwide attention as a major form of air pollution in modern society. − In particular, exposure to environmental noise generated by outdoor units of air conditioners and heat pump water heaters, which have been rapidly spreading worldwide, partly due to global warming, has been reported to cause health disturbances in previous cross-sectional studies. − Because electric devices often generate high levels of low-frequency noise (LFN), defined as frequencies ≤100 Hz, ,− previous studies have postulated that LFN is the primary causal factor for health disturbances. − However, there has been a long debate regarding the health effects of LFN components in environmental noise, with no defining scientific evidence currently available.

Serum creatinine (sCRE), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), and the estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) are representative markers for whole kidney function. The glomeruli, which are dense clusters of capillaries within the renal corpuscles, and the proximal tubules, which are part of the uriniferous tubules, are the two main regulators of kidney function. Both structures are susceptible to environmental stimuli. , The kidney expression levels of endothelin-1 (Et1) and endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNos) are potential biomarkers of glomerular vascular damage. , Et1 promotes vasoconstriction through activation of endothelin receptor type A, leading to lesions in the glomeruli. In contrast, eNOS exerts vasoprotective effects primarily through nitric oxide production, and increased eNOS expression has been reported as a compensatory response to vascular stress. − Furthermore, the expression levels of kidney injury molecule-1 (Kim1) and Lipocalin-2 (Lcn2) are well-established biomarkers of proximal tubular damage. , As previously shown, latent target tissues in the kidneys of environmental stimulations can be preliminarily identified using these biomarkers. Additionally, target tissues can be more precisely identified through histopathological analysis using optical and transmission electron microscopy.

Epidemiologically, a previous observational study reported a negative association between environmental noise exposure and the eGFR. The results suggested that daily exposure to environmental noise can cause kidney dysfunction in humans; however, the specific characteristics of environmental noise, such as the frequency (Hz), sound levels (dBZ or dBA), and duration (h), that result in impaired kidney function in exposed individuals remain unknown. This information is indispensable for establishing environmental noise standards to prevent noise-induced health disturbances, including those affecting kidney function; however, exposing humans to harmful environmental noises is ethically challenging. Mice could be a suitable animal species for clearly distinguishing the direct effects of LFN on the kidneys via sound pressure from those mediated by auditory perception, as the auditory range of mice (2–100 kHz) does not include the frequency range of LFN (≤100 Hz). Therefore, to elucidate the characteristics of nephrotoxic environmental noise and its target site and molecule in the kidney, we investigated the effects of not only whole-frequency environmental noise but also the LFN (≤100 Hz) and non-LFN (>100 Hz) components on kidney function in mice using a multidisciplinary approach combining acoustic and biological studies.

Methods

Mouse Experiments and Ethical Permission

Six-week-old male C57BL/6 mice (purchased from Japan SLC, Inc.) were maintained in a specific pathogen-free environment at a room temperature of 23 ± 2 °C, humidity of 55 ± 10%, and a 12-h light/dark cycle, as previously described. All experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Nagoya University (approval number: M250009–002) and followed the Japanese Government Regulations for Animal Experiments, as previously described. ,

Exposure to Environmental Noise

Outdoor units of air conditioners and heat pump water heaters are representative sources of residential noise associated with health concerns and generate high levels of LFN, as reported in recent studies. , The noise emitted during operation was recorded using a pulse code modulation (PCM) recorder (VoiceTrek DM-750, OLYMPUS, Japan) positioned 30 cm away from the source. The recorder is equipped with built-in stereo microphones with a frequency range of 20 Hz-23 kHz. The recorded noises were stored as uncompressed linear PCM audio files (WAV format) with a sampling rate of 48 kHz and, quantization resolution of 16 bits, in accordance with a previous study. Spectral analysis of sound levels according to frequency was performed using a sound level meter (TYPE 6236, ACO CO., LTD, Japan) equipped with a fast Fourier transform (FFT) analyzer (NA-0038F, ACO CO., LTD, Japan), as previously shown. FFT processing was performed using a Hann window with linear averaging, and the time-weighting function was set to FAST (125 ms). A sponge microphone cover was used to minimize wind noise contamination during data acquisition. The sound level meter was electrically calibrated using a 1 kHz sinewave reference signal generated by the built-in oscillator before collecting measurements. The low-pass or high-pass filter functions of the Audacity software (ver. 3.0.3) isolated LFN (≤100 Hz) and non-LFN (>100 Hz) from the original whole-frequency environmental noises. A woofer (NS-SW700, Yamaha, Japan) and a general audio speaker (Reveal 502, Tannoy Ltd., England) were used to expose the LFN and non-LFN, respectively. Mice were randomly divided into control and exposure groups and exposed to environmental noise in a soundproof box provided by SHIZUKA Inc., as described in a previous study. The exposure levels in mice were determined based on the sound pressure levels directly measured by the sound meter during the original field recordings. The recorded noise was reproduced at comparable sound levels at the position of the mice, but not around the woofer or speaker. The average background noise level was 60.7 dBZ (=40.9 dBA). The equivalent sound pressure levels (LZeq or LAeq), which are the average sound level during exposure, and maximum sound pressure levels are presented together with the FFT noise spectrum following subtraction of the background noise level in Supplemental Figures S1–S3, following previously described calculations. The exposure protocol was designed to follow repeated daily exposure during working days in a typical human lifestyle pattern. Accordingly, mice were exposed to noise for 5 consecutive days for either 12 h/day (9:00 PM-9:00 AM; active phase) or 24 h/day (entire day). To evaluate the reversibility, an additional cessation experiment was performed; mice were exposed to LFN for 5 consecutive days (12 h/day) followed by a 2-day noise-off period without exposure (designed to mimic a potential reduction in environmental noise exposure during weekends).

sCRE and BUN Analyses

Blood and kidney samples were collected immediately following noise exposure under isoflurane anesthesia. After blood was collected, serum was prepared following a previously published method. The levels of sCRE and BUN were measured using the DRI-CHEM 7000VZ (FUJIFILM, Tokyo, Japan).

Quantitative Reverse Transcription Polymerase Chain Reaction (RT-qPCR)

RT-qPCR was performed as described previously. Briefly, RNA was extracted from kidney samples using the FastGene RNA Basic Kit (Nippon Genetics Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). cDNA was synthesized using PrimeScript Reverse Transcriptase (Takara Bio, Otsu, Japan), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. FastStart Universal SYBR Green Master (ROX) reagent (Roche Diagnostics GmbH, Mannheim, Germany) was mixed with template cDNA, as indicated in the manual. The primer sequences are provided in Supplemental Table S1. RT-qPCR was performed using the Thermal Cycler Dice Real-Time System TP800 (Takara Bio, Otsu, Japan).

Treatment with An Endothelin Signaling Inhibitor

Mice were housed in groups and randomly assigned to one of three experimental conditions: (1) exposure to 100 dBZ (=70 dBA) LFN isolated from a heat pump water heater; (2) exposure to the same LFN combined with ambrisentan treatment, an endothelin signaling inhibitor that acts through selective antagonism of endothelin receptor type A; or (3) a control condition with neither LFN exposure nor ambrisentan treatment. Ambrisentan (10 mg/kg/day; Tokyo Chemical Industry, Tokyo, Japan) was prepared and administered via drinking water throughout the LFN exposure period, following previously described protocols.

Histopathological Analyses

Histopathological analysis was performed using an optical microscope, as previously described. The kidney samples were fixed in 10% formalin neutral buffered solution and then embedded in paraffin. The paraffin-embedded kidneys were sectioned in 3 μm and stained with periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) reagents to assess histopathological changes. The mesangial and glomerular areas were quantified as previously described.

Transmission electron microscopy was performed following a previously described method. Briefly, cubic pieces of cortical kidney (2 mm3) were fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde for 24 h at 4 °C and postfixed with 1% osmium tetroxide. The samples were dehydrated with ethyl alcohol, replaced with propylene oxide, and embedded in epoxy resin. The resin-embedded specimens were sectioned at 70 nm thickness and stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate for 10 and 5 min, respectively. The sections were observed under a JEM-1400PLUS transmission electron microscope (Japan Electron Optics Laboratory Co., Ltd., Japan). Photographs taken at x10000 magnification were analyzed to quantify glomerular basement membrane (GBM) thickness using ImageJ software (NIH).

Statistical Analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (GraphPad Software, Inc.), as previously described. , Data distribution was assessed using Q-Q plots in accordance with previous studies, , and all variables were approximately normally distributed. For two-group comparisons, homogeneity of variance was assessed using an F-test. Data with equal variances were analyzed using a two-tailed Student’s t-test, whereas data with unequal variances were analyzed using Welch’s t-test. For comparisons involving more than two groups, homogeneity of variance was assessed using the Brown–Forsythe test. Data with equal variances were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Dunnett’s test, whereas data with unequal variances were analyzed using Welch’s ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s T3 post hoc test. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 in two-sided tests.

Results

Exposure to the Environmental Noise Generated by the Outdoor Unit Air Conditioner

Hearing loss is the most typical health disturbance caused by noise exposure42; therefore, the A-weighted sound level (dBA), which is the sound level modified by human hearing sensitivity, has been globally used as a standard unit to assess the health risk of noise. In contrast, LFN has limited effects on hearing in humans and mice. ,− The Z-weighted sound level (dBZ), which represents the linear sound pressure levels independent of human hearing sensitivity, was used as the primary unit for noise exposure. Although hearing responses and frequency thresholds differ between humans (20–20,000 Hz) and mice (2000–100,000 Hz), , corresponding dBA values are provided as a translational reference for human loudness perception.

The levels of sCRE and BUN were significantly increased by the 12 h/day and 24 h/day exposure to environmental noise for 5 consecutive days (Supplemental Figure 3B). Despite exposure to physically equivalent levels (100 dBZ) of the LFN (100 dBZ [=70 dBA]) (Figure C) and non-LFN [100 dBZ (=87 dBA)] (Figure D) components in whole environmental noise for 5 days, the sCRE and BUN levels were significantly increased by the 12 and 24 h exposures to LFN components, but not non-LFN components. Thus, the environmental noise component at frequencies of ≤100 Hz (LFN) significantly increased the sCRE and BUN levels compared to those at frequencies >100 Hz (non-LFN), even when the physical sound level was the same.

The level of sCRE in mice with 24 h/day exposure to whole environmental noise was significantly higher than that in mice with 12 h/day exposure (Supplemental Figure 3B), whereas there was no significant difference in BUN levels between the 12 h/day and 24 h/day exposures to noise (Supplemental Figure 3B). The levels of both sCRE and BUN in mice with 24 h/day exposure to the LFN component were significantly higher than those in mice with 12 h/day exposure to the same LFN component (Figure C). These results indicate that the duration of exposure to the LFN component of environmental noise affected the levels of sCRE and BUN.

The levels of sCRE and BUN were increased by 12 h/day exposure to the LFN component of 100 dBZ (=70 dBA) but not 90 dBZ (=60 dBA) or 80 dBZ (=50 dBA) for 5 consecutive days (Figure ). Thus, the intensity of exposure to the LFN component of whole environmental noise substantially altered the levels of sCRE and BUN.

Determination of a Target Site of LFN in the Kidney

To determine the target site of LFN in the kidney, mice were exposed to a 100 dBZ (=70 dBA) LFN component isolated from the whole environmental noise generated by a heat pump water heater for 5 consecutive half-days. The expression levels of endothelial-related (Et1 and eNos), as well as renal tubular-related (Kim1 and Lcn2) transcripts in the kidneys were measured by RT-qPCR. The expression levels of Et1 and eNos, but not Kim1 and Lcn2, were significantly increased following LFN exposure (Figure ). These results suggest that LFN may exert a greater effect on the vascular-rich glomeruli than on the renal tubules.

To more strictly determine the target sites of LFN exposure in the kidney after 12 and 24 h/day exposure to a 100 dBZ (= 70 dBA) LFN component for 5 days, histopathological analyses of the kidney, with a focus on the glomerulus, were conducted. Optical microscopy analysis using PAS staining revealed significant increases in the glomerular surface area and mesangial fraction in mice exposed to the LFN component compared to controls (Figure A,B). Furthermore, transmission electron microscopy revealed significant thickening of the GBM in mice exposed to the LFN component compared to controls (Figure C,D). These findings indicate that exposure to the LFN component isolated from whole environmental noise significantly damages the glomerular capillaries.

Determination of a Target Molecule of LFN in the Kidney

To more directly determine the contribution of endothelin signaling to LFN-induced glomerular damage, as suggested by our preliminary molecular screening (Figure ), we evaluated the functional and histopathological effects of treating mice with the endothelin signaling inhibitor ambrisentan on their kidneys. The inhibitor mitigated the LFN-induced elevations in sCRE and BUN levels (Figure A), as well as the morphological increases in glomerular surface area and mesangial expansion (Figure B,C). In contrast, pharmacological inhibition of nitric oxide synthases using L-NAME, which suppresses eNOS-derived nitric oxide production, neither attenuated nor exacerbated the LFN-induced elevations in serum creatinine or BUN levels (Supplemental Figure S4), suggesting that the impact of eNOS on renal function is limited.

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrated that a 12-h exposure to whole environmental noise at 83–88 dBA, equivalent to 102–103 dBZ and consistent with levels previously reported in certain urban and occupational environments, − increased sCRE and BUN levels in C57BL/6 mice (Figure and Supplemental Figure S3). A previous study in rats also showed that exposure to artificial noise (white noise) at 90 dBA increased the levels of sCRE and BUN. Previous cross-sectional studies have shown an association between environmental noise exposure and kidney dysfunction in humans, although no specific frequencies or noise exposure levels have been reported. , These findings, together with previous evidence, suggest that exposure to environmental noise across the full frequency spectrum may be associated with kidney dysfunction in both animals and humans. Additionally, the World Health Organization (WHO) proposed an equivalent sound level of 83 dBA for a 12-h workday as the regulatory limit for workplace noise across all frequencies. Our results indicated that comparable environmental noise exposure, as recommended by the WHO, could cause kidney dysfunction in mice.

To identify the nephrotoxic frequency in environmental noise, the effects of LFN (≤100 Hz) and non-LFN (>100 Hz) components in whole-frequency environmental noises generated by electric devices on kidney function were investigated separately. The whole-frequency environmental noise generated by the outdoor unit of an air conditioner and its LFN component increased the levels of sCRE and BUN, whereas the non-LFN component did not. Furthermore, LFN components exhibited greater nephrotoxic effects than non-LFN components at equivalent linear sound pressure levels (dBZ) in environmental noise generated by a heat pump water heater. These results provide the first experimental evidence that the LFN component of environmental noise contributes to kidney dysfunction in mice.

Our results in mice showed that exposure to LFN components isolated from environmental noise for 5 consecutive days at 24 h/day resulted in significantly higher levels of sCRE and BUN compared to exposure to the same LFN with the same sound level for 5 consecutive days at 12 h/day. However, mice exposed to the non-LFN component for the same duration showed no significant differences in these markers compared with control mice. These results suggest prolonged exposure to LFN can lead to decreased kidney function. Our results also indicated that 5 consecutive days of exposure at 12 h/day to LFN at 100 dBZ (=70 dBA), which was the sound level around the mice but not around the speaker, significantly increased the sCRE and BUN levels in mice, whereas the same exposure to LFN at 90 dBZ and 80 dBZ did not affect the levels. These results suggest a latent safety threshold of 90 dBZ for LFN exposure in mice. As 90 dBZ in the LFN component is equivalent to 60 dBA, dBZ may be a more appropriate unit for assessing the health risks of LFN because dBA may underestimate its harmful effects. Our follow-up study in mice, designed to mimic a plausible human lifestyle pattern, further showed that kidney dysfunction caused by a 5-day exposure to LFN improved after a 2-day cessation of exposure, suggesting that LFN-induced kidney dysfunction is reversible. These results are in good agreement with a previous finding that aortic dysfunction induced by 4 days of aircraft noise exposure can recover following noise cessation.

After characterizing that the LFN component in environmental noise is associated with nephrotoxicity, its kidney target site was investigated. LFN exposure significantly increased the expression of eNos and Et1 transcripts in the kidneys of C57BL/6 mice. This finding was confirmed using a different strain of ICR mice, which also showed significant elevations in sCRE and BUN levels (Supplemental Figures 5 and 6). Considering that increased expression of Et1 and eNos is associated with vascular damage, , our biochemical analyses suggest that glomerular vessels, rather than the proximal tubules, are the primary targets of LFN in the kidney.

After preliminarily identifying the glomerulus as the target site for LFN-induced nephrotoxicity, detailed histopathological analyses were performed. Optical microscopy revealed an LFN-mediated increase in glomerular and mesangial areas, which are well-established markers of glomerular damage. ,, Previous studies have also shown that an increased intraglomerular pressure increases the thickness of the GBM as well as the sizes of the glomerular and mesangial areas. − The 5-day exposure to LFN of 100 dBZ (=70 dBA) may increase intraglomerular pressure via vasoconstriction, resulting in glomerular damage and subsequent kidney dysfunction in mice. Our previous study in mice showed that 3 days of exposure to LFN led to decreased skin blood flow due to vasoconstriction, which does not contradict the present findings. Transmission electron microscopy further demonstrated the LFN-induced GBM thickening, another hallmark of glomerular injury. , − Since reactive oxygen species-associated organ metabolic shifts have been reported to be induced by short-term noise exposure, the elevated levels of sCRE and BUN observed in the present study may reflect dysregulated metabolism. However, these histopathological results showing glomerular damage suggest that the increases in sCRE and BUN are more likely attributable to reduced glomerular filtration rather than metabolic dysregulation.

Noise perceived by the auditory system activates the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis and induces stress hormone release, which promotes cardiovascular damage. − However, the present study demonstrated renal vascular damage caused by acoustically isolated LFN (≤100 Hz), which is far below the murine audible range (2000–100,000 Hz). , In addition, our echocardiographic analysis revealed no significant differences in cardiac parameters including heart rate and cardiac output between control and LFN-exposed mice (Supplemental Figure S7). A trend toward increased serum corticosterone levels, a marker of systemic stress, was observed in the LFN-exposed mice compared with the control group, although statistical significance was not reached (Supplemental Figure S8). Taken together, LFN-induced kidney dysfunction in mice may not be primarily mediated by classical auditory perception-dependent stress pathways or alterations in cardiac function.

Based on the results of our molecular screening and histopathological analyses, this study ultimately showed that inhibition of the endothelin signaling pathway, a typical mechanism promoting vasoconstriction, alleviated LFN-induced functional and morphological damage in the kidney. These findings suggest that the endothelin signaling pathway is a key molecular target in regulating glomerular vasoconstriction induced by LFN exposure. Previous studies have reported that short-term exposure to aircraft noise, which may contain both audible and inaudible low-frequency components, increases oxidative stress, aortic Et-1 expression, inflammatory cytokine levels, and enhances sensitivity to Et-1-mediated vasoconstriction in mice. , In the present study, an oxidative stress marker, malondialdehyde, was significantly increased in the kidney after LFN exposure, and this increase was suppressed by pharmacological inhibition of endothelin signaling (Supplemental Figure S9). Meanwhile, the levels of inflammatory markers, such as interleukin-6 (Il6) and tumor necrosis factor α (Tnfα), in the kidney were comparable between control and LFN-exposed mice (Supplemental Figure S10). Collectively, these findings suggest that LFN-induced kidney dysfunction is associated with endothelin-mediated oxidative stress and vasoconstriction within the kidney.

This study has several limitations. Previous studies suggested that LFN could be transmitted through the body wall due to its long wavelength and potentially affect internal organs. , Therefore, LFN at 100 dBZ may exert direct mechanical effects, such as vibration, on the renal vascular system. However, direct evidence supporting this mechanism remains limited. Careful consideration is necessary when extrapolating these animal findings to humans because of interspecies differences in organ size and anatomical structure. Furthermore, the present study did not specifically assess the health risks of infrasound, defined as sound frequencies below 20 Hz. Previous studies have reported direct cardiac damage induced by artificially generated infrasound (≤20 Hz) at ≥100 dBZ, in rats, cultured cells, and human myocardial tissues. − However, the infrasound component contained within the environmentally derived LFN used in the present study remained below 90 dBZ (Supplemental Figures S1–S3). These differences in noise characteristics may partly explain the absence of cardiac dysfunction in this study. Considering that infrasound is also a common component of environmental noises, its potential contribution to cardiovascular and renal outcomes warrants further investigation.

In conclusion, the health effects of LFN exposure have been a subject of debate for many years, partly because of the lack of interventional studies using isolated LFN components from daily environmental noise. In this interdisciplinary study, we combined acoustic and biological approaches to demonstrate, for the first time, that short-term exposure (a total of 60–120 h) to LFN components in environmental noise, at levels that can be encountered in our daily life, induces kidney dysfunction in both C57BL/6 and ICR mice. We then showed that the kidney dysfunction caused by this short-term LFN exposure is reversible. Our biochemical and histopathological analyses further suggest that short-term exposure to LFN increases intraglomerular pressure through vasoconstriction mediated by activation of the endothelin signaling pathway, ultimately leading to glomerular damage and subsequent kidney dysfunction. These findings suggest a potential risk of kidney impairment with short-term exposure to LFN in mice. Further studies investigating the relationship between environmental LFN exposure and kidney dysfunction in humans are warranted to clarify the translational relevance of these murine findings, while considering the potential for nonauditory health effects.
