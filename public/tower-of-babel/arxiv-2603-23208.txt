A One-Inclusion Graph Approach to Multi-Group Learning
Noah Bergam, Samuel Deng, Daniel Hsu

Abstract
We prove the tightest-known upper bounds on the sample complexity of multi-group learning. Our algorithm extends the one-inclusion graph prediction strategy using a generalization of bipartite b b -matching. In the group-realizable setting, we provide a lower bound confirming that our algorithm’s log ⁡ n / n \log n/n convergence rate is optimal in general. If one relaxes the learning objective such that the group on which we are evaluated is chosen obliviously of the sample, then our algorithm achieves the optimal 1 / n 1/n convergence rate under group-realizability.



1
Introduction

Multi-group learning
is a natural extension of statistical learning formalized by
Rothblum and Yona 2021
that seeks to address learnability on subpopulations. As in standard statistical learning (for binary classification), for an input space
\mathcal{X}
, label space
\mathcal{Y}=\{0,1\}
, and a hypothesis class
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{X}}
, the goal is to use an i.i.d. sample of
n
labeled training examples
S\in(\mathcal{X}\times\{0,1\})^{n}
from a probability distribution
P
to construct a classifier
A(S)(\cdot)
that can make accurate predictions on new points drawn from
P
. Multi-group learning introduces an additional consideration: a (potentially infinite) family of (possibly overlapping) subsets of the input space
\mathcal{G}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{X}}
, or
groups
.
Instead of seeking a classifier that is accurate on the entire domain
\mathcal{X}
, multi-group learning asks for accuracy guarantees on the groups in
\mathcal{G}
(either simultaneously for all
g\in\mathcal{G}
, or downstream on an
a priori
unknown target
g\in\mathcal{G}
).
A natural error measure on group
g\in\mathcal{G}
is the
group-conditional error rate

\Pr_{(x,y)\sim P}[A(S)(x)\not=y\mid x\in g].

Our benchmark on a group
g\in\mathcal{G}
is the minimum group-conditional error rate achievable by some (
g
-specific) hypothesis
h^{*}_{g}\in\mathcal{H}
.
The difficulty, of course, is that for different
g,g^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}
, the best benchmark predictors
h^{*}_{g}
and
h^{*}_{g^{\prime}}
may be quite different.

We focus on the
group-realizable
setting of multi-group learning, introduced in
Tosh and Hsu 2022
and extended by
Ardeshir et al. 2026
. Recall that classical realizable PAC learning assumes there exists some
h^{*}\in\mathcal{H}
that perfectly labels all points, i.e.,
\Pr_{(x,y)\sim P}[h^{*}(x)\not=y]=0.

Group-realizability
is the extension of this assumption to multi-group learning where, for each
g\in\mathcal{G}
, there are (potentially different) hypotheses
h^{*}_{g}\in\mathcal{H}
, such that
\Pr_{(x,y)\sim P}[h^{*}_{g}(x)\not=y\mid x\in g]=0.
Classical realizability implies group-realizability, but the converse need not hold. When
\mathcal{X}\in\mathcal{G}
, group-realizability reduces back to realizability, so one may view group-realizability as a natural generalization of the realizability assumption.

From this perspective, a natural question is whether comparable rates for learning obtained in classical (single-group) statistical learning models can be obtained in their multi-group extensions. In the
prediction model of learning
,
Haussler et al. 1994
showed that the
one-inclusion graph (OIG)
prediction strategy achieves the optimal
expected
error rate guarantee of

\E_{S\sim P^{n}}\left[\Pr_{(x,y)\sim P}\left[A_{\mathrm{OIG}}(S)(x)\not=y\right]\right]\leq\frac{d_{\mathcal{H}}}{n},

(1)

where
d_{\mathcal{H}}
denotes the VC dimension of
\mathcal{H}
.

Empirical risk minimization (ERM)
, on the other hand, introduces a suboptimal multiplicative
\log(n/d_{\mathcal{H}})
factor to (
1
). Similarly, in the well-known
PAC model of learning
,
Aden-Ali et al. 2023b
show that a simple online-to-batch aggregation atop the one-inclusion graph achieves optimal rates, while ERM picks up a suboptimal
\log(n/d_{\mathcal{H}})
factor.

The best-known results in the multi-group setting exhibit a comparable state of affairs. To analyze group-realizable multi-group learning,
Ardeshir et al. 2026
introduced the class of
group-realizable concepts
, the set of binary-valued functions on
\mathcal{X}
compatible with the group-realizability assumption. They show that ERM over this class is indeed a multi-group learner, but their rates suffer a similar
\log n
dependency. Though their results were originally stated for the multi-group extension of the PAC model, the same suboptimality can be found in the multi-group prediction model through simply integrating the tails of their high probability results.

In view of the applicability of the one-inclusion graph algorithm in shaving the
\log n
to achieve optimal rates in (
1
), a natural question is whether one can generalize the one-inclusion graph algorithm to our setting. Of course, a naïve application of the one-inclusion graph algorithm using
\mathcal{H}
cannot hope to provide group-wise guarantees. The challenge is how to extend this algorithm to address the (possibly infinite) collection of groups
\mathcal{G}.

Main results.

Our main result is a novel generalization of the one-inclusion graph algorithm to the multi-group setting that achieves a suite of optimal and near-optimal guarantees. Our learner obtains optimal guarantees when evaluation involves a single downstream
g\in\mathcal{G}
. In the prediction model, we guarantee

\E_{S\sim P^{n}}\left[\Pr_{(x,y)\sim P}[A_{\mathrm{MgOIG}}(S)(x)\neq y\land x\in g]\right]\leq\frac{d_{\mathcal{H}|g}}{n}\quad\forall g\in\mathcal{G},

where
d_{\mathcal{H}|g}
is the VC dimension of
\mathcal{H}
restricted to group
g.

(To get a conditional error rate bound, divide both sides by the probability mass of
g
under
P
.)
When evaluation requires a simultaneous guarantee over all
g\in\mathcal{G}
(as previous work considers), we achieve the near-optimal guarantee

\E_{S\sim P^{n}}\left[\sup_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\Pr_{(x,y)\sim P}[A_{\mathrm{MgOIG}}(S)(x)\neq y\land x\in g]\right]\leq O\left(\frac{d_{\mathcal{H}|\mathcal{G}}}{n}+\frac{d_{\mathcal{G}}\log(n/d_{\mathcal{H}|\mathcal{G}})}{n}\right),

where
d_{\mathcal{H}|\mathcal{G}}=\sup_{g\in\mathcal{G}}d_{\mathcal{H}|g}
, and
d_{\mathcal{G}}
is the VC dimension of
\mathcal{G}
.
Note that the two types of guarantees above (single downstream group vs. simultaneous) can be distinguished by whether the learner’s classifier is evaluated on a group chosen obliviously of the sample, or on a group chosen adversarially based on the sample.
In comparison to the previously best-known rates of
Ardeshir et al. 2026
, our result removes the
\log n
factor from the
d_{\mathcal{H}|\mathcal{G}}
term. For the second term, our lower bound in Theorem
2
demonstrates that the dependence on
\log n/n
cannot in general be improved.

Applying the recently developed machinery of
Aden-Ali et al. 2023b
,
Asilis et al. 2024
, and
Dughmi et al. 2025
, we extend our algorithm’s guarantees to the multi-group PAC model in both the group-realizable and agnostic settings. Together, these results give the tightest known upper bounds for multi-group learning. For clarity, we only state the results for the prediction model in Theorem
1
, our main theorem. We defer the guarantees for the PAC model to Appendix
B
(for the group-realizable case) and Appendix
C
(for the agnostic case). Our paper is organized as follows:

•

Section
2.1
defines
multi-group transductive error
and
multi-group prediction error
, which generalize the relevant notions of
Haussler et al. 1994
in the analysis of the one-inclusion graph, reviewed in Section
2.2
. Of particular importance is the multi-group transductive error, the main quantity we control to obtain the in-expectation and high probability results.

•

Section
3
introduces our generalization of the one-inclusion graph algorithm applied to the group-realizable concepts. The algorithm requires introducing a multi-group variant of the maximum bipartite matching problem in Section
3.1
that may be of independent interest. Section
3.2
demonstrates that an optimal solution to this “multi-group matching problem” results in a well-defined learner with guarantees on the multi-group transductive error. Section
3.3

•

Section

provides a novel lower bound for group-realizable multi-group learning.
In particular, it shows that the simultaneous guarantee may require the slower
\log n/n
rate that is avoided when one only seeks a guarantee for a single downstream group.

Related work.

Rothblum and Yona 2021
initiated the study of multi-group learning in the agnostic (batch) setting, in which the best benchmark hypothesis on each group does not necessarily have zero error.
Tosh and Hsu 2022
introduced the group-realizability assumption outlined in Section
1
alongside an algorithm to achieve an error rate scaling roughly as
\log n/n
for the case where
\mathcal{G}
is finite.
Ardeshir et al. 2026
introduced the class of
group-realizable concepts
we work with and demonstrate that ERM over this class manages to achieve
\log n/n
error rate guarantees even when
\mathcal{G}
is infinite (with finite VC dimension). Although these works suggest the statistical feasibility of “fast”
1/n
rates in the group-realizable setting (as opposed to the
1/\sqrt{n}
rates necessary in the general agnostic setting), the dependence suffers the
\log n
factor known to be suboptimal.

Multi-group extensions have also been studied for other learning paradigms such as online learning
(
Blum and Lykouris 2020
;
Acharya et al. 2024
;
Deng et al. 2024
)
and active learning
(
Rittler and Chaudhuri 2023
)
. The batch multi-group learning setting can be seen as a “special case” of other learning settings such as multiaccuracy
(
Kim et al. 2019
)
, multicalibration
(
Hébert-Johnson et al. 2018
)
, outcome indistinguishability
Dwork et al. 2021
, and multi-distribution learning
(
Haghtalab et al. 2022
;
Zhang et al. 2024
;
Peng 2024
)
. In these cases, specialing known algorithms to group-realizable learning only achieves
\Omega(1/\sqrt{n})
(or worse) error rates.

The interplay between the group system and the hypothesis class in terms of the group-conditional VC dimension
d_{\mathcal{H}|\mathcal{G}}=\max_{g\in\mathcal{G}}d_{\mathcal{H}|g}
is reminiscent of the mutual VC dimension
(
Hu and Peale 2023
)
and the VC dimension of partial concept classes
(
Alon et al. 2022
)
. Note that
d_{\mathcal{H}|\mathcal{G}}
is always larger, and can be arbitrarily larger, than the mutual VC dimension of
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{H}
. Meanwhile,
d_{\mathcal{H}|g}
is the VC dimension of a partial hypothesis class where each
h
is undefined outside of
g
.
In multi-group learning, however, we do not know on which
g\in\mathcal{G}
the learner will be evaluated.

Our main algorithmic technique is an extension of the one-inclusion graph introduced by
Haussler et al. 1994
, who originally
showed sharp guarantees for the one-inclusion graph in the
prediction model of learning
(displayed in (
1
)) and the closely related
transductive model of learning
.
(The multi-group extensions are defined in Section
2.1
.)
Due to the optimal
1/n
in-expectation error rate of the one-inclusion graph,
Warmuth 2004
conjectured that one could show that it also obtains the optimal PAC error, beating the suboptimal
\log n/n
rate of ERM. Recently, the line of work
Aden-Ali et al. 2023a
;
Aden-Ali et al. 2023b
showed that although the one-inclusion graph algorithm
itself
cannot obtain the optimal PAC error, a simple aggregation procedure atop one-inclusion graphs indeed achieves the optimal PAC error.
Optimal PAC error rates were previously achieved by
Hanneke 2016
and
Larsen 2023

through clever analyses of subsampling and majority vote.

The perspective we take on the one-inclusion graph algorithm to adapt it to the multi-group setting in Section
3.1
can be seen as a generalization of the classical bipartite
b
-matching problem where vertices have multiple simultaneous capacities for intersecting subgraphs. For more on this connection, see the Appendix of
Asilis et al. 2024
and the exposition of
Dughmi 2025
.

Because analysis of the one-inclusion graph algorithm avoids appealing to uniform convergence, it has also been extended to other learning settings in which uniform convergence does not imply learnability, such as multiclass learning
(
Rubinstein et al. 2009
;
Daniely and Shalev-Shwartz 2014
;
Asilis et al. 2024
)
and partial concept learning
(
Haussler et al. 1994
;
Bartlett and Long 1998
;
Alon et al. 2022
)
. Our focus is on the multi-group extension of the binary classification setting, and we leave the multi-group extension to these learning settings as future work.

Setting

We focus on the binary classification setting where
\mathcal{X}
denotes the input space and the label space is
\{0,1\}
.
Throughout, we let
\mathcal{G}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{X}}
denote a collection of (potentially overlapping) subsets of the input space, or
groups
. We let
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{X}}
denote our
hypothesis class
, a collection of benchmark classifiers, or
hypotheses

h\colon\mathcal{X}\to\{0,1\}
.
Let
d_{\mathcal{G}}
(resp.
d_{\mathcal{H}}
) denote the VC dimension of
\mathcal{G}
(resp.
\mathcal{H}
). For any
g\in\mathcal{G}
, let
d_{\mathcal{H}|g}
denote the VC dimension of
\mathcal{H}
restricted to
g
(i.e., the shattered set must come from
g
), and also let let
d_{\mathcal{H}|\mathcal{G}}=\sup_{g\in\mathcal{G}}d_{\mathcal{H}|g}
.
The following overloaded notation will be convenient to us: a group
g\in\mathcal{G}
can be regarded as a subset
g\subseteq\mathcal{X}
or an indicator function
g\colon\mathcal{X}\to\{0,1\}
where
g(x)=\mathds{1}\{x\in g\}
.
A
training sample
is a tuple
S=\lparen(x_{1},y_{1}),\dots,(x_{n},y_{n})\rparen\subseteq(\mathcal{X}\times\{0,1\})^{n}
of
n

training points
. We use
S^{-i}
to denote the training sample with the
i
th training point removed. We denote an unlabeled sample by
U=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathcal{X}^{n}
.
A
learner

A
takes a training sample
S
and outputs a classifier
A(S)(\cdot)
.

Throughout, we let
P
be a distribution over
\mathcal{X}\times\{0,1\}
with
P_{\mathcal{X}}
denoting the marginal distribution over
\mathcal{X}
. As shorthand, we denote the probability mass of a group
g\subseteq\mathcal{X}
as
P(g):=\Pr_{x\sim P_{\mathcal{X}}}[x\in g]
. The
error rate
of a classifier
f\colon\mathcal{X}\to\{0,1\}
is its average error over a labeled point from drawn from
P
:
\err(f):=\Pr_{(x,y)\sim P}[f(x)\not=y]
.

2.1
Multi-Group Learning: Prediction Model and Transductive Model

The high-level goal of multi-group learning is to produce a learner whose output classifier performs well over all groups in a pre-defined family of possibly intersecting groups
\mathcal{G}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{X}}
. Though previous work in multi-group learning focused only on
multi-group PAC error
to evaluate a learner (re-introduced in Appendix
A
), our main result hinges on defining two other notions of error:
multi-group prediction error
and
multi-group transductive error
.

The
group-only error rate
of a classifier
f\colon\mathcal{X}\to\{0,1\}
on group
g\subseteq\mathcal{X}
is defined as:

\err_{g}(f):=\Pr_{(x,y)\sim P}[f(x)\not=y\land x\in g].

Although it is common in the multi-group learning literature to analyze the
group-conditional error rates
, defined as
\err\lparen f\nonscript\>|\nonscript\>\mathopen{}g\rparen:=\Pr_{(x,y)\sim P}[f(x)\not=y\mid x\in g]
, it will be convenient for us to focus on the group-only rates instead. Of course (for groups with nonzero mass), the group-conditional error rate and the group-only error rate are simply related by normalization:
\err(f\mid g)=\err_{g}(f)/P(g).

Group-realizability.

The notion of group-realizability generalizes the classical assumption of
realizability
, which requires that that there exists
h^{*}\in\mathcal{H}
with zero error rate.

Group-realizability
requires that for every
g\in\mathcal{G}
, there exists
h^{*}_{g}\in\mathcal{H}
with zero error rate on
g
.

The classical realizability assumption implies the group-realizability assumption, as the single
h^{*}\in\mathcal{H}
with
\err(h^{*})=0
serves as a zero-error hypothesis on any group. However, the group-realizability assumption does not imply the realizability assumption in general, as there may be no
h^{*}\in\mathcal{H}
that functionally agrees with the behavior of all the various
h^{*}_{g}
on
\mathcal{X}
. One scenario in which group-realizability collapses to realizability is when
\mathcal{X}\in\mathcal{G}
, but we do not require this in general.

In the group-realizable setting,
Ardeshir et al. 2026
introduced a notion that will be central to our development: the collection of
group-realizable concepts

\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H}}^{P_{\mathcal{X}}}
, defined as follows.

Definition 1
.

Let
\mathcal{G}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{X}}
be a collection of groups and let
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{X}}
be a hypothesis class for the same domain
\mathcal{X}
.
We say the multi-group learning task
(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H},P)
satisfies

group-realizability

if
\min_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\err_{g}(h)=0
for all
g\in\mathcal{G}
.

Equivalently, with
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H}}^{P_{\mathcal{X}}}
denoting the set of
c\colon\mathcal{X}\to\{0,1\}
such that
\min_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\Pr_{x\sim P_{\mathcal{X}}}[h(x)\neq c^{*}(x)\wedge x\in g]=0
for all
g\in\mathcal{G}
, we say

(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H},P)
satisfies

group-realizability

if
\Pr_{(x,y)\sim P}[y=c^{*}(x)]=1
for some
c^{*}\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H}}^{P_{\mathcal{X}}}
. We call
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H}}^{P_{\mathcal{X}}}
the collection of
group-realizable concepts with respect to
(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H},P)
.

When analyzing the
multi-group transductive model
(defined below), there will be no underlying distribution
P
, only a sample of inputs
U=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathcal{X}^{n}
. In this case, we let
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H}}^{U}
denote the group-realizable concepts with respect
(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H},U)
: the collection of all concepts
c\colon\mathcal{X}\to\{0,1\}
such that for all
g\in\mathcal{G}
, there exists
h_{g}\in\mathcal{H}
such that
h_{g}(x_{i})=c(x_{i})
for all
x_{i}\in g
.

In view of Definition
1
, a multi-group learning task
(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H},P)
satisfying group-realizability can be specified through the marginal distribution
P_{\mathcal{X}}
over
\mathcal{X}
and a group-realizable concept
c^{*}\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H}}^{P_{\mathcal{X}}}
. The group-only error rate of learner
A
on a group
g
can thus be equivalently written as:

\err_{g}(A(S)):=\Pr_{x\sim P_{\mathcal{X}}}[A(S)(x)\not=c^{*}(x)\land x\in g].

Multi-group prediction error.

An natural criterion for learning is to measure a learner’s
expected
performance, as proposed by
Haussler et al. 1994
as the
prediction model of learning.
We define the straightforward extension of this notion of error to the multi-group case, focusing on the group-realizable setting. For
(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H},P)
satisfying group-realizability and a target error
\epsilon\in(0,1)
, a learner
A
achieves
\epsilon

multi-group prediction error on group
g
if

\E_{S\sim P^{n}}\left[\err_{g}(A(S))\right]\leq\epsilon.

(2)

Multi-group transductive error.

We also introduce the
transductive model of learning
in the multi-group setting. The transductive model, originally proposed by
Haussler et al. 1994
, is instrumental to analyzing the error of the one-inclusion graph (reviewed in Section
2.2
). It is intimately connected to the prediction model of learning through a simple exchangeability argument.

There is no underlying distribution
P
in the multi-group transductive model, but group realizability is encoded through the unknown group-realizable concept
c^{*}\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H}}^{U}
for a sample of inputs
U=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathcal{X}^{n}
. In this way, the notion of error in (
3
) is set up exactly so we can take advantage of recent techniques that “upgrade” transductive error guarantees to prediction error and PAC error guarantees.
Multi-group transductive learning
can be thought of as the following game:

•

An adversary chooses input points
U=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathcal{X}^{n}
and an unknown concept
c^{*}\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H}}^{U}
to construct a labeled sample
S=\lparen(x_{1},c^{*}(x_{1})),\dots,(x_{n},c^{*}(x_{n}))\rparen
.

•

Let
\Sigma_{n}
be the set of permutations on
[n]
. The sample is permuted according to a uniformly randomly drawn
\sigma\sim\Sigma_{n}
, yielding
S_{\sigma}=\lparen(x_{\sigma(1)},c^{*}(x_{\sigma(1)})),\dots,(x_{\sigma(n)},c^{*}(x_{\sigma(n)}))\rparen
.

•

The learner is presented the first
n-1
labeled points

S^{-n}_{\sigma}=\left((x_{\sigma(1)},c^{*}(x_{\sigma(1)})),\dots,(x_{\sigma(n-1)},c^{*}(x_{\sigma(n-1)}))\right)

as well as
x_{\sigma(n)}
as a test point. The label
c^{*}(x_{\sigma(n)})
is unknown to the learner.

•

The learner must predict the unknown label
c^{*}(x_{\sigma(n)})
of the test point
x_{\sigma(n)}
.

Departing from standard transductive error, we define
multi-group transductive error on group
g
as:

\err_{g}^{\mathrm{trans}}(A(S)):=\E_{\sigma\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\Sigma_{n})}\left[\mathds{1}\{A(S_{\sigma}^{-n})(x_{\sigma(n)})\not=c^{*}(x_{\sigma(n)})\land x_{\sigma(n)}\in g\}\right].

(3)

The difference between (
3
) and the standard notion of transductive error is that a learner errs only when the test point
x_{\sigma(n)}
is in group
g.
We will, of course, additionally demand that (
3
) is small for all
g\in\mathcal{G}.
When
\mathcal{G}=\{\mathcal{X}\}
, we recover the classical notion of transductive error.

Theorem
1
, our main theorem, hinges on constructing a learner
A
with

\err_{g}^{\mathrm{trans}}(A(S))\leq\epsilon_{g}(n)\quad\forall g\in\mathcal{G},

(4)

where
\epsilon_{g}(n)
is a decreasing function of
n
. Once (
4
) is established, we obtain prediction error and PAC error guarantees by applying techniques from
Haussler et al. 1994
;
Aden-Ali et al. 2023b
. These arguments are captured in Theorem
3
in Appendix
B
.

2.2
The One-Inclusion Graph

The
one-inclusion graph (OIG) algorithm
was originally developed by
Haussler et al. 1994
as a learner especially tailored to minimizing transductive error in the classical (single-group) realizable setting where
\mathcal{G}=\{\mathcal{X}\}.
Our main contribution in Section
3
is to develop a generalization of the OIG algorithm to minimize the multi-group transductive error.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph. The
density

\mathrm{dens}(G):=|E|/|V|
is simply defined as the number of edges divided by the number of vertices. For a subset of vertices
W\subseteq V
, we define the
induced subgraph

G^{W}=(W,E^{\prime})
to be the graph with vertex set
W
and edges
E^{\prime}=\{e\cap W\nonscript\>|\nonscript\>\mathopen{}e\in E,|e\cap W|\not=0\}
. The
maximum subgraph density
of a graph
G
is the quantity
\max_{W\subseteq V}\mathrm{dens}(G^{W})
, the largest density of any induced subgraph of
G
.

A one-inclusion graph depends on an unlabeled sample
U=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathcal{X}^{n}
and a binary-valued class
C\subseteq\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{X}}
. For now, we let the class
C
be generic, but, in the sequel,
C
will be the class
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H}}^{P_{\mathcal{X}}}
of group-realizable concepts. The
one-inclusion graph (OIG)

G_{C}(U)=(V,E)
for class
C
with respect to sample
U
is an undirected graph where:

•

the vertex set
V=\{(c(x_{1}),\dots,c(x_{n}))\nonscript\>|\nonscript\>\mathopen{}c\in C\}
is the set of behaviors of
C
on
U
.

•

the edge set
E=\{\{u,v\}\in\binom{V}{2}\nonscript\>|\nonscript\>\mathopen{}\|u-v\|_{0}=1\}
comprised of pairs of behaviors that differ on exactly on point in
U
(here,
\|u-v\|_{0}
denotes the Hamming distance).

Haussler et al. 1994
showed a beautiful combinatorial result relating the VC dimension of a concept class to the maximum subgraph density of its one-inclusion graph.

Lemma 1

(
Haussler et al. 1994
)
.

Let
C\subseteq\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{X}}
be a concept class with VC dimension
d.
For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\max_{U\in\mathcal{X}^{n}}\max_{W\subseteq V}\mathrm{dens}\left(G^{W}_{C}(U)\right)\leq d.

3
The Multi-group One Inclusion Graph Algorithm

We now propose our main algorithmic contribution: a generalization of the one-inclusion graph algorithm of
Haussler et al. 1994
to the multi-group setting that achieves guarantees on the multi-group transductive error, displayed in (
4
).
At a high-level, the algorithm solves a variant of the
maximum bipartite
b
-matching problem
on a bipartite network associated with the one-inclusion graph for the group-realizable concepts
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H}}^{P_{\mathcal{X}}}
(which is only ever accessed implicitly).
Unlike the classical bipartite
b
-matching problem, we demand that a matching must satisfy simultaneous constraints on subgraphs for each
g\in\mathcal{G}
; note that these subgraphs need not be disjoint.
The constraints themselves are intimately related to the subgraph densities of one-inclusion graphs restricted to
g\in\mathcal{G}
.
Once an optimal matching satisfying these constraints is found, we can recover a well-defined learner.

We prove our main result, Theorem
1
, as follows. Section
3.1
describes the
bipartite network
associated with a one-inclusion graph and the corresponding
multi-group matching
problem, which we encode into linear program (
5
). Section
3.2
shows that if a solution to this matching problem meets a certain value, we recover a well-defined learner
A_{f}^{\mathrm{MgOIG}}
that obtains guarantees on the multi-group transductive error. A guarantee on multi-group transductive error implies Theorem
1
, our main result, which shows that our learner obtains a suite of optimal and near-optimal rates for multi-group prediction error and PAC error. Finally, Section
3.3
shows that a solution to the linear program indeed meets the requisite value to properly define a learner through a duality argument.

Henceforth, we will exclusively be working with the projection of
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{H}
on a finite sample of inputs
U=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathcal{X}^{n}
, so
\mathcal{G}|_{U}
and
\mathcal{H}|_{U}
are finite sets with cardinalities

\left|\mathcal{G}|_{U}\right|\leq\binom{2n}{\leq d_{\mathcal{G}}}\quad\text{and}\quad\left|\mathcal{H}|_{U}\right|\leq\binom{2n}{\leq d_{\mathcal{H}}}

by Sauer’s Lemma. The definitions of this section apply to any concept class
C\subseteq\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{X}}
and any collection of groups
\mathcal{G}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{X}}
, but our main results focus on the case where
C=\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H}}^{P_{\mathcal{X}}}.

3.1
Multi-group Matching Problem

We now describe the
multi-group matching problem
for an OIG
G_{C}(U)=(V,E)
and a collection of groups
\mathcal{G}
. This is reminscent of the classical maximum bipartite
b
-matching problem, but capacities in our network will be enforced
simultaneously
for subnetworks corresponding to groups. The
b
-matching variant of bipartite matching is the generalization of the classical bipartite matching problem where nodes are prescribed a maximum number of required matches. We take inspiration from the works of
Asilis et al. 2024
;
Dughmi 2025
, which cast the classical (single-group) one-inclusion graph aglorithm as an instance of bipartite
b
-matching.

Recalling that vertices in
V
correspond to behaviors and edges in
E
correspond to behaviors that differ at exactly one point, edges can naturally be associated with groups.

Definition 2
.

Let
g\subseteq\mathcal{X}
be a group and let
G_{C}(U)=(V,E)
be an OIG. An edge
e=\{u,v\}
is
g
-relevant
if
x_{i}\in g
at the point
x_{i}
for which the behaviors
u
and
v
differ.

Since the groups can intersect arbitrarily, an edge can be
g
-relevant to multiple groups. For a particular group
g
, we will denote
E_{g}\subseteq E
as the set of
g
-relevant edges
.

Definition 3
.

Let
G_{C}(U)=(V,E)
be a one-inclusion graph and let
\mathcal{G}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{X}}
be a collection of groups. The corresponding
multi-group bipartite network

\mathcal{B}_{C}^{\mathcal{G}}(U)=(E\cup V,\mathcal{E})
is a bipartite graph with disjoint sets of (network) nodes
E
and
V
and a set of (network) arcs
\mathcal{E},
where:

•

the nodes in
E
correspond to the edge set
E
of
G_{C}(U)
;

•

the nodes in
V
correspond to the vertices
V
of
G_{C}(U)
;

•

the arcs are defined as
\mathcal{E}:=\{(\{u,v\},u),(\{u,v\},v)\nonscript\>|\nonscript\>\mathopen{}\{u,v\}\in E\}

•

each node in
V
has
g
-specific capacities

(d_{g})_{g\in\mathcal{G}|_{U}}
where

d_{g}:=\max_{W\subseteq V}|E_{g}^{W}|/|W|
and
E_{g}^{W}
is the set of
g
-relevant edges in the induced subgraph
G^{W}_{C}(U)
.

(To avoid confusion, we refer to the vertices in
E
and
V
in
\mathcal{B}_{C}(U)
as
(network) nodes
and
\mathcal{E}
as the
(network) arcs
to distinguish from the vertices and edges
(V,E)
of the associated OIG
G_{C}(U)
.)

Note that every network node
e=\{u,v\}\in E
has exactly two arcs in
\mathcal{E}
connecting it to two network nodes
u,v\in V.
The network nodes in
E
naturally associate with groups in the same way: for a group
g\in\mathcal{G}
, the
g
-relevant edges (Definition
2
)
E_{g}
in the OIG naturally picks out the same subset of
g
-relevant network nodes
in the network nodes
E.

We must now define what we mean by the
g
-specific capacities
in Definition
3
. In standard bipartite
b
-matching, the goal is to find the maximum (possibly fractional) matching of nodes
E
and
V
such that for all
v\in V
, the (possibly fractional) number of nodes in
E
matched to
v
does not exceed a capacity
b(v)
(the classical maximum bipartite matching problem is recovered when
b(v)=1
for all
v\in V
). In the
multi-group matching problem
, every
v\in V
has a capacity
d_{g}
for each
g\in\mathcal{G}
that must be satisfied
simultaneously
over the subgraphs corresponding to
E_{g}
. Thus, the aim in multi-group matching is to find a (possibly fractional) matching of nodes
E
and
V
such that for all
v\in V
, the (possibly fractional) number of nodes
e\in E_{g}
matched to
v
do not exceed
d_{g}
for
every

g\in\mathcal{G}
. Observe that if
\mathcal{G}=\{\mathcal{X}\}
and
d_{\mathcal{X}}=1
, then this is exactly the classical maximum bipartite matching problem.

Let
f\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\mathcal{E}}
be a vector encoding the matching, where entries
f_{\{u,v\},u}
and
f_{\{u,v\},v}
correspond to how much node
\{u,v\}\in E
is assigned to
u
and
v
, respectively. In an integral matching,
f_{\{u,v\},u}=1
corresponds to node
e=\{u,v\}
getting assigned to node
u\in V
; accordingly,
f_{\{u,v\},v}=0
because, in an integral matching, each
\{u,v\}\in E
can be assigned to one node in
V
. In a fractional assignment,
f_{\{u,v\},u}+f_{\{u,v\},v}=1
for all
\{u,v\}\in E
.

We can encode this entire multi-group matching problem into the linear program (
5
).

\displaystyle\max_{f\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\mathcal{E}}}\sum_{(e,v)\in\mathcal{E}}f_{e,v}\quad\textup{such that }\quad f_{\{u,v\},v}+f_{\{u,v\},v}\leq 1

\displaystyle\qquad\forall\{u,v\}\in E

(5)

\displaystyle\sum_{e\in E_{g}:v\in e}f_{e,v}\leq d_{g}

\displaystyle\qquad\forall v\in V,\ \ \forall g\in\mathcal{G}

Note that the linear program is feasible (the matching of all-zeros is a solution), and any feasible solution has objective value at most
|E|
(from the first set of constraints for bipartite nodes
E
).

In the standard analysis of the (single-group) one-inclusion graph in
Haussler et al. 1994
, the prediction strategy is found through reducing to maximium flow. However, this does not work on our multi-group matching problem because each bipartite node
v\in V
has
|\mathcal{G}|_{U}|
different capacities that each correspond to different subsets of
E
. Constructing a flow network with a well-defined terminal is not straightforward with these constraints.

3.2
From a Multi-group Matching to a Learner

Given the OIG
G_{C}(U)
, a collection of groups
\mathcal{G}
, and the corresponding bipartite network
\mathcal{B}_{C}^{\mathcal{G}}(U)
, we now describe how one constructs a learner
A
from a matching
f\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\mathcal{E}}
in the graph.

First, we define a crucial property of the matching that allows us to properly specify a prediction strategy for the transductive model of learning, and, hence, a learner.

Definition 4
.

Let
\mathcal{B}_{C}^{\mathcal{G}}(U)=(E\cup V,\mathcal{E})
be the multi-group bipartite network associated with an OIG
G_{C}(U)=(V,E)
. A matching
f\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\mathcal{E}}
is a
prediction sufficient matching
if it is feasible for (
5
) and for each
\{u,v\}\in E
,

f_{\{u,v\},u}+f_{\{u,v\},v}=1.

For any prediction sufficient matching
f\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{E}}_{+}
(Definition
4
), we can now define a learner
A
. Let
S=\lparen(x_{1},y_{1}),\dots,(x_{n},y_{n})\rparen\in(\mathcal{X}\times\{0,1\})^{n}
be any labeled training sample presented to
A
, and let
x\in\mathcal{X}
be an unlabeled test point. The learner
A
does the following:

•

Construct the OIG
G_{C}(U)
with
U=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n},x)
, composed of the
n
input points from
S
and the test point
x
.

•

Given the labeing
(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})
in
S
, there is either a unique
v\in V\subseteq\{0,1\}^{n+1}
consistent with the labeling or there are two
u,v\in V
consistent with the labeling. If there is a unique
v\in V
, then
A(S)(x)
is equal to entry
n+1
of
v.

•

Otherwise let
e=\{u,v\}
be the edge in
G_{C}(U)
where both
u
and
v
are consistent with the labeling. Then,
A(S)(x)
is entry
n+1
in
u
with probability
1-f_{\{u,v\},u}=f_{\{u,v\},v}
and
A(S)(x)
is entry
n+1
in
v
with probability
f_{\{u,v\},u}=1-f_{\{u,v\},v}.

We note that
A(S)(\cdot)
is well-defined because
f
is prediction sufficient, and, thus, the final step defines a valid Bernoulli distribution. We will henceforth denote this learner
A_{f}^{\mathrm{MgOIG}}
.

Our algorithm is to simply obtain a prediction sufficient matching by solving the linear program (
5
) and use it to construct
A_{f}^{\mathrm{MgOIG}}
as above. Lemma
2
ensures that an optimal solution to (
5
) is indeed prediction sufficient. Its proof is the content of Section
3.3
.

Lemma 2
.

The optimal solution
f\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\mathcal{E}}
to (
5
) is a prediction sufficient matching.

The resulting classifier
A_{f}^{\mathrm{MgOIG}}(S)(\cdot)
is randomized, predicting by flipping a coin with bias
f_{\{u,v\},v}.
One can also show that, with an algorithm for finding a matching in (
5
) reminiscent of the Ford-Fulkerson method applied to matching problems, there exists an
integral
optimal solution where
f\in\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{E}}
. This immediately implies that the learner
A_{f}^{\mathrm{MgOIG}}
constructed with this matching outputs deterministic classifiers. The proof is in Appendix
D
, and may be of independent interest.

Lemma 3
.

There exists an integral optimal solution
f\in\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{E}}
to the linear program (
5
).

In either case, the pieces are now in place to prove our main lemma, Lemma
4
. This will imply our main result, Theorem
1
, which gives guarantees on multi-group prediction error and PAC error.

Lemma 4
.

Fix groups and hypotheses
\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{X}}
. Consider any
U=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathcal{X}^{n}
and let
S=\lparen(x_{1},c^{*}(x_{1})),\dots,(x_{n},c^{*}(x_{n})\rparen\in(\mathcal{X}\times\{0,1\})^{n}
for any
c^{*}\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H}}^{U}
.

If
f
is a prediction sufficient matching (Definition
4
) for the one inclusion graph
G_{\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H}}^{U}}(U)
and multi-group flow matching problem
\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H}}^{U}}^{\mathcal{G}}(U)
, then the learner
A_{f}^{\mathrm{MgOIG}}
satisfies:

\err_{g}^{\mathrm{trans}}(A_{f}^{\mathrm{MgOIG}}(S))\leq\frac{d_{\mathcal{H}|g}}{n}\quad\forall g\in\mathcal{G}.

Proof.

Throughout, let
C=\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H}}^{U}
for ease of notation. Fix any
g\in\mathcal{G}.
By definition (see (
3
)),

\displaystyle\err_{g}^{\mathrm{trans}}(A_{f}^{\mathrm{MgOIG}}(S))

\displaystyle=\E_{\sigma\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\Sigma_{n})}\left[\mathds{1}\{A_{f}^{\mathrm{MgOIG}}(S_{\sigma}^{-n})(x_{\sigma(n)})\not=c^{*}(x_{\sigma(n)})\land x_{\sigma(n)}\in g\}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\sigma\in\Sigma_{n}}\mathds{1}\{A_{f}^{\mathrm{MgOIG}}(S_{\sigma}^{-n})(x_{\sigma(n)})\not=c^{*}(x_{\sigma(n)})\}\cdot\mathds{1}\{x_{\sigma(n)}\in g\}

Note that we have one less point than the description of
A_{f}^{\mathrm{MgOIG}}
above: the learner is given a labeled sample
S_{\sigma}^{-n}
with
n-1
points and the test point is
x_{\sigma(n)}.

Consider the OIG
G_{C}(U)=(V,E)
and let
v\in V
be the behavior in the OIG corresponding to the true concept
c^{*}\in C.
Let
N(v):=\{u\in V\nonscript\>|\nonscript\>\mathopen{}\{u,v\}\in E\}
denote the neighborhood of
v
in
G_{C}(U)
. For a
g\in\mathcal{G}
, let
N_{g}(v):=\{u\in V\nonscript\>|\nonscript\>\mathopen{}\{u,v\}\in E_{g}\}
be the neighborhood of
v
only reachable by
g
-relevant edges. With this notation, we have:

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\sigma\in\Sigma_{n}}\left(\sum_{u\in N(v)}\mathds{1}\{u_{\sigma(n)}\not=v_{\sigma(n)}\}\right)\mathds{1}\{A_{f}^{\mathrm{MgOIG}}(S_{\sigma}^{-n})(x_{\sigma(n)})\not=c^{*}(x_{\sigma(n)})\}\cdot\mathds{1}\{x_{\sigma(n)}\in g\}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\sigma\in\Sigma_{n}}\left(\sum_{u\in N(v)}\mathds{1}\{u_{\sigma(n)}\not=v_{\sigma(n)}\}\right)f_{\{u,v\},v}\cdot\mathds{1}\{x_{\sigma(n)}\in g\}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{u\in N(v)}\left(\sum_{\sigma\in\Sigma_{n}}\mathds{1}\{u_{\sigma(n)}\not=v_{\sigma(n)}\}\right)f_{\{u,v\},v}\cdot\mathds{1}\{\{u,v\}\in E_{g}\}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{u\in N(v)}(n-1)!\cdot f_{\{u,v\},v}\cdot\mathds{1}\{\{u,v\}\in E_{g}\}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{u\in N_{g}(v)}f_{\{u,v\},v}\leq\frac{d_{g}}{n}

The first inequality above is due to the definition of
A_{f}^{\mathrm{MgOIG}}
above: when deciding between predicting with the behavior of
u
or
v
on the
n
th point, we predict the incorrect label given by
u
with probability
f_{\{u,v\},v}.
The second inequality is because
f
is prediction sufficient and the second constraint of (
5
).
Finally, by Definition
3
of the network and Lemma
1
,

d_{g}=\max_{W\subseteq V}\frac{|E_{g}^{W}|}{|W|}\leq d_{\mathcal{H}|g},

where we observe that the maximum subgraph density of
G_{C}(U)
restricted to
g
is simply the
g
-restricted VC dimension of
\mathcal{H}
by definition of the group-realizable concepts
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H}}^{U}
.
∎

Combining Lemma
4
with Theorem
3
(in Appendix
B
) implies that
A_{f}^{\mathrm{MgOIG}}
gives our main result after verifying that
A_{f}^{\mathrm{MgOIG}}
is indeed permutation invariant (Definition
5
).

Theorem 1
.

Let
A_{f}^{\mathrm{MgOIG}}
be the learner described in Lemma
4
. Consider any
P
such that
(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H},P)
is group-realizable with labels generated by
c^{*}\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H}}^{P_{\mathcal{X}}}
, and let
S=\lparen(x_{1},c^{*}(x_{1})),\dots,(x_{n},c^{*}(x_{n}))\rparen
be an i.i.d. sample drawn from
P
. Then, for any
g\in\mathcal{G}
,

\E_{S\sim P_{\mathcal{X}}^{n}}\left[\err_{g}(A_{f}^{\mathrm{MgOIG}}(S))\right]\leq\frac{d_{\mathcal{H}|g}}{n+1}.

(6)

There also exists a learner
A_{f}^{\mathrm{MgOIG-A}}
with the guarantee

\E_{S\sim P_{\mathcal{X}}^{n}}\left[\sup_{g\in\mathcal{G}}~\err_{g}(A_{f}^{\mathrm{MgOIG-A}}(S))\right]\leq O\left(\frac{d_{\mathcal{H}|\mathcal{G}}}{n+1}+\frac{d_{\mathcal{G}}\log(n/d_{\mathcal{G}})}{n}\right).

(7)

Extensions of Theorem
1
to the PAC and agnostic settings are in Appendix
B
and Appendix

.

3.3
Proof of Lemma
2
through Duality

We dedicate this section to proving Lemma
2
, which shows that a prediction sufficient matching is indeed a solution to the linear program in (
5
) and, thus, the learner
A_{f}^{\mathrm{MgOIG}}
can be obtained. To do so, we analyze the dual problem of the linear program (
5
), stated here for OIG
G_{C}(U)=(V,E)
:

\displaystyle\max_{y\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{E},z\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\mathcal{G}\times V}}\sum_{e\in E}\quad

\displaystyle y_{e}+\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}}d_{g}\sum_{v\in V}z_{g,v}

such that

\displaystyle y_{e}+\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}:e\in E_{g}}z_{g,v}\geq 1\quad\text{for all $e\in E$ and $v\in e$}.

(8)

A
prediction sufficient matching
(Definition
4
) can alternatively be characterized by a matching
f\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\mathcal{E}}
with value
\sum_{(e,v)\in\mathcal{E}}f_{e,v}=|E|
that is feasible for (
5
). Therefore, if we can show that the dual linear program has an optimal value of
|E|
, strong duality implies that a feasible solution to (
5
) also has value
|E|
, proving Lemma
2
. Lemma
5
shows that this is indeed the case.

Lemma 5
.

The optimal dual value in (
3.3
) has value
|E|.

Proof.

To see that the dual has value at most
|E|
, witness the feasible solution
y_{e}=1
for all
e\in E
and
z_{g,v}=0
for all
(g,v)\in\mathcal{G}\times V
. This pair
(y,z)
achieves value
|E|.

We now show that the dual has value at least
|E|.
Consider any feasible dual solution
(y,z).
For any
\{u,v\}\in E
, the constraints imply

y_{\{u,v\}}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\mathds{1}\{\{u,v\}\in E_{g}\}(z_{g,u}+z_{g,v})\geq 1.

Summing the constraints over all the edges
\{u,v\}\in E
, we obtain:

\sum_{e\in E}y_{e}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\{u,v\}\in E}\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\mathds{1}\{\{u,v\}\in E_{g}\}(z_{g,u}+z_{g,v})\geq|E|.

To show that the optimal dual value is at least
|E|
, it suffices to show that

\sum_{e\in E}y_{e}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\sum_{\{u,v\}\in E}\mathds{1}\{\{u,v\}\in E_{g}\}(z_{g,u}+z_{g,v})\leq\sum_{e\in E}y_{e}+\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}}d_{g}\sum_{v\in V}z_{g,v}

In fact, we will show that for each
g\in\mathcal{G}
,

\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\{u,v\}\in E_{g}}(z_{g,u}+z_{g,v})\leq d_{g}\sum_{v\in V}z_{g,v}.

Positive homogeneity of the above inequality with respect to
z
allows us to assume
z\in[0,1]^{\mathcal{G}\times V}
without loss of generality. Let
(X_{g,v})_{v\in V}
be
\{0,1\}
-valued random variables with
\E[X_{g,v}]=z_{g,v}
and set
W_{g}:=\{v\in V\nonscript\>|\nonscript\>\mathopen{}X_{g,v}=1\}
. Then, by the handshaking lemma

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\{u,v\}\in E_{g}}(z_{g,u}+z_{g,v})=\frac{1}{2}\E\left[\sum_{\{u,v\}\in E_{g}}(X_{g,u}+X_{g,v})\right]=\frac{1}{2}\E\left[\sum_{v\in V}\deg^{W_{g}}(v)\right]=\mathbb{E}[|E_{g}^{W_{g}}|]

where
\deg^{W_{g}}(v)
is the number of
g
-relevant edges incident with
v
in the graph
G_{C}^{W_{g}}(U)
and
E_{g}^{W_{g}}
are the
g
-relevant edges of
G_{C}^{W_{g}}(U)
. By definition of
d_{g}
, we have

|E_{g}^{W_{g}}|\leq d_{g}|W_{g}|.

Therefore, chaining together the three previous displays, we have

\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\{u,v\}\in E_{g}}(z_{g,u}+z_{g,v})\leq\E\left[|E_{g}^{W_{g}}|\right]\leq\E\left[d_{g}|W_{g}|\right]=\E\left[d_{g}\sum_{v\in V}X_{g,v}\right]=d_{g}\sum_{v\in V}z_{g,v}

as claimed.
∎

4
Lower Bound

Our lower bound shows that the simultaneous guarantee can be more difficult to achieve than the downstream evaluation on a single (but unknown) group.
In fact, for sufficiently rich
\mathcal{G}
, the optimal rates may increase from
1/n
to
\log n/n
.
Let
\mathcal{G}(x):=\{g\in\mathcal{G}\nonscript\>|\nonscript\>\mathopen{}x\in g\}
denote the subset of groups that contain
x\in\mathcal{X}
, and define

\displaystyle I_{\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H},\epsilon}=\max\{I\leq 1/\epsilon\ |\ \exists(x_{1},\dots,x_{I})\in\mathcal{X}^{I}

\displaystyle\ \text{s.t.}\ \mathcal{G}(x_{i})\cap\mathcal{G}(x_{j})=\emptyset,\ \forall i,j\in[I],i\neq j

\displaystyle\text{and}\ \forall i\in[I],\ \exists h_{0},h_{1}\in\mathcal{H}\ \text{s.t.}\ h_{0}(x_{i})\neq h_{1}(x_{i}))\}.

For example, if
\mathcal{G}
contains at least
1/\epsilon
disjoint (nonempty) sets and
\mathcal{H}
separates points in
\mathcal{X}
(as, say, the hypothesis class of halfspaces do in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
), then
I_{\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H},\epsilon}=1/\epsilon
.

Theorem 2
.

Fix
\epsilon\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
,
\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{X}}
and learning algorithm
A
.
There is a distribution
P
on
\mathcal{X}\times\{0,1\}
such that
(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H},P)
is group-realizable and

n<\frac{d_{\mathcal{H}|\mathcal{G}}-1}{8\epsilon}+\frac{\log(I_{\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H},2\epsilon}/2)}{4\epsilon}\implies\Pr_{S\sim P^{n}}\left[\max_{g\in\mathcal{G}}{\err}_{g}(A(S))\geq\epsilon\right]\geq\frac{1}{2}.

The necessity of the
\log(I_{\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H},\epsilon})
term comes from the fact that ensuring low error on
I_{\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H},\epsilon}
disjoint groups requires seeing all such groups in the training sample, which is as hard as a coupon collecting problem.
The proof is given in Appendix
E
.

Appendix A
Multi-group PAC Model

In this section, we introduce the PAC model of multi-group learning, to be compared with the notions we defined in Section
2.1
.

Introduced by
Rothblum and Yona 2021
and extended in
Tosh and Hsu 2022
,
multi-group PAC learning
is a generalization of the classical notion of agnostic PAC learning.
For a given family of groups
\mathcal{G}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{X}}
, a collection of benchmark hypotheses
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{X}}
, a target error
\epsilon\in(0,1)
and failure probability
\delta\in(0,1)
, the learner’s goal is to produce a classifier using an i.i.d. sample
S=\lparen(x_{1},y_{1}),\dots,(x_{n},y_{n})\rparen
drawn from an unknown distribution
P
over
\mathcal{X}\times\{0,1\}
satisfying

\err_{g}(A(S))\leq\inf_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\err_{g}(h)+\epsilon\quad\text{for all }g\in\mathcal{G}

(9)

with probability
1-\delta
over
S
and any internal randomness of the classifier. Importantly, each group
g\in\mathcal{G}
may have a different optimal classifier
h^{*}_{g}\in\mathcal{H}
and the groups may intersect arbitrarily. Because of this, the classifiers
A(S)(\cdot)
satisfying multi-group PAC learning will not in general be contained in
\mathcal{H}.

The guarantee in (
9
) can be thought of as ensuring good accuracy for all groups in

\mathcal{N}(\epsilon,\mathcal{G},L_{1}(P))\leq e(d+1)\left(\frac{2e}{\epsilon}\right)^{d}.

Definition 6

Let

\displaystyle\E\left[\sup_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\err_{g}(A^{\prime}(S))\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\Pr\left[\sup_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\err_{g}(A^{\prime}(S))\geq t\right]dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{u}\Pr\left[\sup_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\err_{g}(A^{\prime}(S))\geq t\right]dt+\int_{u}^{\infty}\Pr\left[\sup_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\err_{g}(A^{\prime}(S))\geq t\right]dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{u}\Pr\left[\sup_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\err_{g}(A^{\prime}(S))\geq t\right]dt+\frac{1}{n}\int_{0}^{\infty}\Pr\left[\sup_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\err_{g}(A^{\prime}(S))\geq u+\frac{\delta}{n}\right]d\delta

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{u}dt+\frac{1}{n}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\delta}d\delta

\displaystyle=u+\frac{1}{n},

proving (
18
).
∎

We can immediately verify that our multi-group one-inclusion graph algorithm described in Section

is permutation invariant, as the ordering of

only changes the naming of the nodes and not the structure of the one-inclusion graph or its related bipartite network. Therefore, Theorem

Appendix C
Multi-group Agnostic PAC Guarantees

In this section, we extend our main result to also obtain a guarantee on the
multi-group agnostic PAC learning
. In particular, we show that, for binary classification with zero-one loss, our one-inclusion graph algorithm can be transformed into a learner in the agnostic setting, following the “agnostic one-inclusion graph” introduced in
Asilis et al. 2024
and further developed in
Dughmi et al. 2025
. The techniques in this section are straightforward adaptations of the arguments presented in
Asilis et al. 2024
and
Dughmi et al. 2025
once it is clear that our multi-group one-inclusion graph strategy can obtain group-wise guarantees.

As described in Section
A
,
multi-group agnostic PAC learning
removes the restriction of
group-realizability
assumed in Section
2.1
and demands that, for an arbitrary unknown distribution
P
over
\mathcal{X}\times\{0,1\}
,

\err_{g}(A(S))\leq\inf_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\err_{g}(h)+\epsilon

for all
g\in\mathcal{G}

S
. As in the group-realizability assumption, the best group-specific hypotheses achieving
\inf_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\err_{g}(h)
for each
g\in\mathcal{G}
could be different. However, in the agnostic setting, we do not assume that
\inf_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\err_{g}(h)=0
.

We obtain a learner for this setting by using our multi-group one-inclusion graph algorithm in the same way as Section
3.1
. We will first obtain a group-wise transductive error guarantee on a variant of the one-inclusion graph adapted to the agnostic setting, which is Lemma
12
. Applying a similar aggregation strategy to this agnostic one-inclusion graph gives us the main theorem of this section:

Theorem 5
.

Let
A_{f}^{\mathrm{MgOIG}}
be the learner described in Lemma

1.

Then, for any

(24)

2.

For any

Our result in (

The main statement we need to prove for Theorem

In order to prove (

in Section

to this transductive error guarantee, which show that an appropriate aggregation of agnostic one-inclusion graphs with transductive error guarantees obtains a PAC guarantee. Further, the transductive error guarantee can be shown to directly relate to the VC dimension, which completes the proof. These results are quoted for our purposes in Section

C.1
Agnostic One-Inclusion Graph Extension

We begin by defining the
multi-group agnostic transductive error
of a learner
A
. Let
\mathcal{G}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{X}}
be a collection of groups and let
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{X}}
be a hypothesis class. Throughout, we assume that
\mathcal{G}
has VC dimension
d_{\mathcal{G}}
and
\mathcal{H}
has VC dimension
d_{\mathcal{H}}
.

Extending Section
2.1
to the agnostic case, we consider
S\subseteq(\mathcal{X}\times\{0,1\})^{n}
, where we no longer require that the outputs are labeled by some unknown group-realizable concept
c^{*}\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H}}^{U}
. Instead, the learner receives an arbitrary sample
S=((x_{1},y_{1}),\dots,(x_{n},y_{n}))\in(\mathcal{X}\times\{0,1\})^{n}
and aims to minimize the
multi-group agnostic transductive error on group
g

\err_{g}^{\mathrm{ag-trans}}(A(S)):=\E_{\sigma\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\Sigma_{n})}\left[\mathds{1}\{A(S_{\sigma}^{-n})(x_{\sigma(n)})\neq y_{\sigma(n)}\land x_{\sigma(n)}\in g\}\right]-\frac{1}{n}\|S-\mathcal{H}\|_{g}

(28)

for each
g\in\mathcal{G}
, where

\|S-\mathcal{H}\|_{g}:=\min_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g(x_{i})\mathds{1}\{h(x_{i})\neq y_{i}\}.

In comparison to the multi-group transductive error of (
3
), the agnostic version in (
28
) considers the learner’s performance relative to the performance of the best hypothesis in each group.

We now define the
multi-group agnostic one-inclusion graph
, directly extending the definition by
Asilis et al. 2024
. For an unlabeled sample
U\in\mathcal{X}^{n}
, hypothesis class
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{X}}
and collection of groups
\mathcal{G}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{X}}
, the
multi-group agnostic one-inclusion graph

G_{\mathcal{H},\mathcal{G}}^{\mathrm{Ag}}(U)=(V^{\mathrm{Ag}},E^{\mathrm{Ag}})
, where

•

V^{\mathrm{Ag}}=\{0,1\}^{n}
, where each vertex corresponds to a labeling of
U
.

•

E^{\mathrm{Ag}}=\{(u,v)\nonscript\>|\nonscript\>\mathopen{}\|u-v\|_{0}=1\}
, comprised of pairs of behaviors that differ on exactly one point in
U
(here,
\|u-v\|_{0}
denotes Hamming distance).

Note that, though
G_{\mathcal{H},\mathcal{G}}^{\mathrm{Ag}}
does not explicitly depend on
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{H}
, it will be convenient to think of each vertex in the graph to have an associated vector of “credits” for each
g\in\mathcal{G}
. We associate to each
u\in V^{\mathrm{Ag}}
a
credit
for each
g\in\mathcal{G}|_{U}
, equal to

\|u-\mathcal{H}|_{U}\|_{g}:=\min_{h\in\mathcal{H}|_{U}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g(x_{i})\mathds{1}\{h(x_{i})\neq u_{i}\}.

Defined in this way, the agnostic variant of the multi-group one-inclusion graph is structurally the same as the
n
-dimensional Boolean hypercube, where each vertex is associated to a vector of
|\mathcal{G}|_{U}|
credits. Intuitively, because each vertex
u\in V^{\mathrm{Ag}}
is a labeling of
U
, the credits for
u
measure how “far” the hypothesis class
\mathcal{H}
is from the labeling
u
, where distance is the misclassification rates for the different groups. Of course, when
\mathcal{G}=\{\mathcal{X}\}
this reduces exactly to the agnostic one-inclusion graph of
Asilis et al. 2024
.

The relevant combinatorial quantity for the agnostic one-inclusion graph
G_{\mathcal{H},\mathcal{G}}^{\mathrm{Ag}}(U)=(V^{\mathrm{Ag}},E^{\mathrm{Ag}})
is the following
discounted density
of a subset
W\subseteq V^{\mathrm{Ag}}
for group
g
:

\mathrm{dens}_{\mathrm{disc}}^{g}(W):=\frac{|E_{g}^{W}|-\sum_{w\in W}\|w-\mathcal{H}|_{U}\|_{g}}{|W|}.

Note that the discounted density’s first term is simply the subgraph density (for
g
-relevant edges). Like the subgraph density, these quantities play a key role in the multi-group matching problem in the agnostic case, as well as the final error guarantee.

With a one-inclusion graph defined, we can define the associated multi-group bipartite network in the same way as Definition
3
. However, we modify the group-specific capacities of the vertices to incorporate the discounted densities and credits. Given agnostic one-inclusion graph
G_{\mathcal{H},\mathcal{G}}^{\mathrm{Ag}}(U)=(V^{\mathrm{Ag}},E^{\mathrm{Ag}})
, the
agnostic multi-group bipartite network

\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{H},\mathcal{G}}^{\mathrm{Ag}}(U)=(E^{\mathrm{Ag}}\cup V^{\mathrm{Ag}},\mathcal{E})
is the bipartite network defined exactly as in Definition
3
but the
g
-specific capacities
(d_{g}^{\mathrm{Ag}}(v))_{g\in\mathcal{G}|_{U}}
of each bipartite node in
v\in V^{\mathrm{Ag}}
is instead

d_{g}^{\mathrm{Ag}}(v):=\max_{W\subseteq V^{\mathrm{Ag}}}\mathrm{dens}_{\mathrm{disc}}^{g}(W)+\|v-\mathcal{H}\|_{g}.

Note that to adopt this notion of complexity in our setting, it is crucial that our algorithm could assign different vertices different sets of capacities.

With this set up, we can properly use the machinery of Section
3.2
to show that our multi-group matching problem induces a learner that has guarantees on multi-group agnostic transductive error. Lemma
8
shows this in a way similar to Lemma
4
, making sure to adjust for the agnostic term.

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\{u,v\}\in E_{g}}(z_{g,u}+z_{g,v})-\sum_{v\in V}z_{g,v}\|v-\mathcal{H}\|_{g}

\displaystyle=\E\left[|E_{g}^{W_{g}}|-\sum_{w\in W_{g}}\|w-\mathcal{H}\|_{g}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\E\left[\Phi_{g}|W_{g}|\right]

\displaystyle=\Phi_{g}\sum_{v\in V}z_{g,v},

which shows (

∎

C.2
Aggregation Argument

By Lemma

and Lemma

\err_{g}^{\mathrm{ag-trans}}(A_{f}^{\mathrm{MgOIG}}(S))\leq\frac{1}{n}\max_{W\subseteq V^{\mathrm{Ag}}}\mathrm{dens}_{\mathrm{disc}}^{g}(W)\quad\forall g\in\mathcal{G}.

(33)

To finish our proof of Theorem

by the appropriate error rate scaling in the VC dimension. We note that, the lemmas in

are stated and proven without restricting to

Lemma 10

(Lemma 7 of
Dughmi et al. 2025
)
.

Let

be the agnostic OIG corresponding to unlabeled sample

, hypothesis class
\mathcal{H}
, and group collection
\mathcal{G}.
For each

, the following inequality holds for all

:

\mathrm{dens}_{\mathrm{disc}}^{g}(W)\leq\mathrm{dens}_{\mathrm{disc}}^{g}(V^{\mathrm{Ag}}).

Dughmi et al. 2025
proceed to upper bound

with the empirical Rademacher complexity via a symmetrization argument. For a fixed

allows us to bound the empirical Rademacher complexity restricted to

\mathrm{dens}_{\mathrm{disc}}^{g}(V^{\mathrm{Ag}})=O(n)\cdot\left(\frac{1}{n}\E_{\sigma\sim\{-1,1\}^{n}}\left[\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g(x_{i})h(x_{i})\sigma_{i}\right]\right).

Using the same standard upper bound of the Rademacher complexity in terms of the VC dimension, but restricting to

dependence in the version of the lemma restricted to

Lemma 11

(Lemma 8 of
Dughmi et al. 2025
)
.

Let
G_{\mathcal{H},\mathcal{G}}^{\mathrm{Ag}}(U)=(V^{\mathrm{Ag}},E^{\mathrm{Ag}})
be the agnostic OIG corresponding to unlabeled sample

, hypothesis class
\mathcal{H}
, and group collection
\mathcal{G}.
For each

Combining Lemmas
10
and
11

Lemma 12
.

Consider any

. If

is a prediction sufficient matching (Definition

and agnostic multi-group flow matching problem

Proof.

Follows directly from applying Lemma
10
and

with Lemma

.
∎

It remains to convert Lemma

into the guarantee in (
25
) by using the aggregation scheme in

k\in\mathrm{poly}(1/\epsilon,\log(1/\delta))
distinct samples and predicting using the randomized predictor that simply chooses one of these

Precisely, let
n=m+k
where
S=((x_{1},y_{1}),\dots,(x_{n},y_{n}))
and denote the subset of the sample

for

Let

be the result of running the agnostic one-inclusion graph algorithm on sample

as the learner that, given sample

With this in mind, we quote the necessary aggregation result in

Lemma 13

(Lemma 2 from
Dughmi et al. 2025
)
.

Suppose learner
A
has agnostic transductive error rate

for any sample
S\in(\mathcal{X}\times\{0,1\})^{n}
. Then, for any

and a sample

, with probability

\Pr\left[\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\err(A(S_{i-1}))-\min_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\err(h)\geq\epsilon(m)+\sqrt{\frac{32\log(2/\delta)}{k}}\right]\leq\delta.

From Lemma

and any

\err_{g}(A_{f}^{\mathrm{OIG,Ag}}(S))\leq\min_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\err_{g}(h)+16\cdot\sqrt{\frac{4d_{\mathcal{H}|g}+\log(2/\delta)}{n}}.

Fix any
g\in\mathcal{G}
and

Applying Lemma

The total sample size is

Rearranging, this implies that the error bound is

Appendix D
Proof of Integrality

In this section, we provide an alternative method to obtain a prediction sufficient multi-group matching, as described in Section
3.1
and
3.2
. Rather than going through a duality argument as in Section
3.3
, we present an algorithm for directly solving the linear program in (
5
). This can be seen as a direct, constructive solution for the multi-group matching problem and thus proves Lemma
3
.

Throughout this appendix, we note that, because our algorithm works solely on projections of
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{H}
onto a set of finite examples
U=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathcal{X}^{n}
and
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{H}
have finite VC dimensions
d_{\mathcal{G}}
and
d_{\mathcal{H}}
, these projections are finite on a finite dataset:

\left|\mathcal{G}|_{U}\right|\leq\binom{2n}{\leq d_{\mathcal{G}}}\quad\text{and}\quad\left|\mathcal{H}|_{U}\right|\leq\binom{2n}{\leq d_{\mathcal{H}}}.

Thus, for ease of notation, our exposition in this section will be for the case where
\mathcal{G}
is finite, but note that these arguments can all be extended to the VC dimension case by working on the projection
\mathcal{G}|_{U}
.

D.1
Group-Specific Flow Networks

Although the multi-group matching problem in Section

does not correspond neatly to a maximum flow problem, restricting our focus to the collection of bipartite network nodes and corresponding edges in each

does yield
|\mathcal{G}|
different network flow subproblems.

For any

, recall that

is the subset of
E

g

. The
g

with parameter

•

•

The following lemma of

allows us to associate the maximum flow of

to the density of the corresponding OIG

Lemma 14

(Lemma 2.5 of

)
.

Let

denote the value of the maximum

\displaystyle\mathsf{OPT}\leq|E|,

\displaystyle\mathsf{OPT}\geq|E|-\left[\max_{W\subseteq V}|W|\left(\mathrm{dens}(G_{C}^{W}(U))-d\right)\right]_{+}

where

is the induced subgraph of

on vertices

.

The following lemma will be important. In what follows, for any subset of vertices

be the edges in the

Lemma 15
.

For each
g\in\mathcal{G}
, let

Then, for every
g\in\mathcal{G}
, there exists a solution

defined for all

with

and

\displaystyle f^{g}_{\{u,v\},u}+f^{g}_{\{u,v\},v}=1\quad\textrm{for all }\{u,v\}\in E_{g}

The

-specific flow network

corresponds to the

-specific one-inclusion graph

. By Lemma

, if

D.3
Correctness of the Algorithm

We now aim to show that when Algorithm
1

that is feasible for the optimization problem in (

We will also show that this is an integral matching if all the

At a high level, the proof of correctness is similar to the proof of correctness of the Ford-Fulkerson algorithm (see, e.g.

In our problem, however, showing the progress lemma on augmenting matchings requires reasoning about the

First, we show that the

is feasible. We aim to show that, at any step of the algorithm, updating feasible flow

If we are updating

Lemma 24
.

Fix any
\delta\in\lparen 0,1\rparen
and positive integer
k
, and let
G_{1},\dotsc,G_{k}
be independent geometric random variables, where
G_{i}
has success probability
\delta(1-(i-1)/k)
for each
i
.
Then for any
t>0
,

\Pr\left[\sum_{i=1}^{k}G_{i}\leq\frac{k\ln(k+1)-kt}{\delta}\right]\leq e^{-t}.

Proof.

This is a standard application of the Chernoff bounding method.
The moment generating function for
-\sum_{i=1}^{k}G_{i}
at
\lambda:=\delta/k
is bounded as follows:

\E\left[\exp\left\lparen-\lambda\sum_{i=1}^{k}G_{i}\right\rparen\right]=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{\delta i}{\delta i+(e^{\lambda}-1)k}\leq\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{\delta i}{\delta i+\lambda k}=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{i}{i+1}=\frac{1}{k+1}.

Therefore,

\Pr\left[\sum_{i=1}^{k}G_{i}\leq\frac{k\ln(k+1)-kt}{\delta}\right]=\Pr\left[\ln(k+1)-\frac{\delta}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}G_{i}\geq t\right]\leq e^{-t}.\qed
