Robust Transfer Learning with Side Information
Akram S. Awad Akram.Awad@ucf.edu, Shihab Ahmed Shihab.Ahmed@ucf.edu, Yue Wang Yue.Wang@ucf.edu, George K. Atia George.Atia@ucf.edu

Abstract
Robust Markov Decision Processes (MDPs) address environmental shift through distributionally robust optimization (DRO) by finding an optimal worst-case policy within an uncertainty set of transition kernels. However, standard DRO approaches require enlarging the uncertainty set under large shifts, which leads to overly conservative and pessimistic policies. In this paper, we propose a framework for transfer under environment shift that derives a robust target-domain policy via estimate-centered uncertainty sets, constructed through constrained estimation that integrates limited target samples with side information about the source-target dynamics. The side information includes bounds on feature moments, distributional distances, and density ratios, yielding improved kernel estimates and tighter uncertainty sets. The side information includes bounds on feature moments, distributional distances, and density ratios, yielding improved kernel estimates and tighter uncertainty sets. Error bounds and convergence results are established for both robust and non-robust value functions. Moreover, we provide a finite-sample guarantee on the learned robust policy and analyze the robust sub-optimality gap. Under mild low-dimensional structure on the transition model, the side information reduces this gap and improves sample efficiency. We assess the performance of our approach across OpenAI Gym environments and classic control problems, consistently demonstrating superior target-domain performance over state-of-the-art robust and non-robust baselines.



1
Introduction

Transfer reinforcement learning (RL) seeks to leverage knowledge from a source environment to accelerate and stabilize learning in a related target environment. Such a scheme is essential in real-world applications where collecting sufficient data in the target environment is costly, dangerous, or otherwise infeasible, and where policies must instead be transferred from simulation or a related operational setting.

However, the difference between the two environments, often referred to as the
sim-to-real gap
or
environmental mismatch
, arise from modeling errors in simulation, unmodeled disturbances, adversarial perturbations, or non-stationary conditions, and can result in severe performance degradation when directly deploying trained policies. This underscores the need for principled transfer mechanisms that reduce adaptation time and minimize unduly data collection in the target domain.

A popular approach for transfer under model mismatch is the framework of
robust MDPs
and
robust RL

[
3
,
26
,
13
]
, which constructs an uncertainty set of transition kernels centered on the source environment. By optimizing the worst-case performance, robust RL yields policies that guarantee a lower bound on return when the target kernel lies within the set, enhancing robustness when deployed to out-of-distribution environments. This enhancement makes robust RL an attractive approach to transfer. However, when the two environments are substantially different, the uncertainty set must be expanded to cover the target one, often leading to overly conservative solutions. Such pessimism can cause robust policies to underperform in the target domain.

To mitigate over-conservatism, alternative transfer methods such as multi-task learning, domain randomization, and model-free domain adaptation have been explored (see Sec.
5
). Multi-task RL seeks to learn representations that generalize across related tasks, while domain randomization exposes agents to diverse simulated conditions to promote robustness. Model-free adaptation methods reweight or adjust source samples to approximate the target distribution. Yet, these methods often fail when the target domain diverges sharply from the training conditions, as they do not explicitly account for the structure of uncertainty in the transition dynamics.

In this work, we develop a framework for robust transfer that explicitly leverages
side information
, i.e., prior knowledge about the relationship between the source and target environments. Such information may take the form of distance constraints, density ratios, or moment conditions that capture statistical or structural similarities between domains. By integrating side information with limited target samples, we construct improved estimates of the target transition kernel, yielding policies that are closer to optimal for the target and less conservative than standard robust RL. In effect, side information reduces the sim-to-real gap by anchoring the uncertainty set around an estimated target model rather than the source, thereby decreasing the adaptation time or the data required to achieve reliable performance.

We consider both the
non-robust setting
, which learns the optimal policy under the estimated target model, and the
robust setting
, which guards against residual model uncertainty around this estimate. Our main contributions are:

(1)

We develop a side-information–based framework for estimating target transition kernels and learning robust policies, integrating structural constraints into the estimation process.

(2)

We derive error bounds and establish convergence results for robust and non-robust value functions, providing asymptotic guarantees in terms of total variation distance and quality of side information.

(3)

We provide finite-sample guarantees on the robust optimality gap under mild assumptions on the transition kernel subspace, demonstrating that side information reduces suboptimality and improves sample efficiency.

(4)

Our approach is validated through experiments on OpenAI Gym and classic control tasks, showing consistent improvements over state-of-the-art baselines in both robust and non-robust settings.

A stationary policy

\displaystyle V^{\pi}_{\mathsf{P}}(s)=\mathbb{E}_{\pi,\mathsf{P}}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}r(s_{t},a_{t})\big|S_{0}=s\right].

Problem setup.

We consider an environment shift scenario, where an agent is trained in a source environment and subsequently deployed in a related, but distinct, target environment. This setting is formalized using two MDPs: the source domain MDP
\mathcal{M}_{\text{s}}=(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\mathsf{P}_{\text{s}},r,\gamma)
and the target domain MDP
\mathcal{M}_{\text{t}}=(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\mathsf{P}_{\text{t}},r,\gamma)
, which only differ in their transition dynamics, i.e.,
\mathsf{P}_{\text{s}}\neq\mathsf{P}_{\text{t}}
. Both
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{A}
are assumed to be finite sets of sizes
S
and
A
, respectively. The agent is provided with a fixed offline dataset
\mathcal{D}=\{(s_{i},a_{i},s^{\prime}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
, consisting of
N
transitions. Each tuple is generated according to some distribution
\mu
over state-action pairs, i.e.,
(s_{i},a_{i})\sim\mu
, and the next state
s^{\prime}_{i}
is sampled from the nominal target transition kernel,
s^{\prime}_{i}\sim\mathsf{P}_{\text{t}}^{s_{i},a_{i}}
. In addition, we assume the availability of side information
\Phi(\mathsf{P}_{\text{s}},\mathsf{P}_{\text{t}})
, which captures structural or statistical relationships between the source and target transition kernels, defined in Section
3
.

Our objective is to learn policies for the target domain using limited offline samples from the target MDP together with side information, under (i) a non-robust setting and (ii) a robust setting that accounts for transition uncertainty.

3
Main Approach

A key challenge is that the target environment is observable only through a
limited offline sample
. A natural baseline is offline RL
[
37
,
43
]
, but reliable performance typically requires abundant, high-quality data; with scarce samples, offline RL often yields suboptimal policies due to coverage gaps and extrapolation error
[
19
]
. Robust MDPs address distribution shift by optimizing over an uncertainty set
centered at the source
transition kernel
[
42
,
46
,
33
,
20
,
47
]
. However, when the source–target shift is large, the uncertainty set must be enlarged to include the target dynamics, inducing excessive pessimism and degraded performance in the target domain.

Our key idea is to transfer knowledge from source to target by estimating the target transition kernel using limited offline target data together with side information that encodes relationships between the domains (e.g., bounds on moments, distributional distances, or density ratios). We then optimize policies
around this estimated target kernel
rather than around the source. Intuitively, if the estimate is closer to the true target dynamics than the source is, uncertainty sets centered at the estimate require smaller radii to cover the target, reducing conservatism while retaining robustness.
We study both (i) a
non-robust
regime that learns the optimal policy for the estimated target model, and (ii) a
robust
regime that guards against residual model uncertainty around that estimate.

Uncertainty set construction
. For radius
R\!\geq\!0
, we define the
(s,a)
-rectangular set centered at
\mathsf{P}
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{P},R)\;\triangleq\;\bigotimes_{s,a}\,\mathbb{B}_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mathsf{P}^{s,a},R)\>,

(4)

where
\mathbb{B}_{\mathrm{TV}}(p,R)=\{q\in\Delta(\mathcal{S}):\|q-p\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq R\}
denotes the TV ball of radius
R
centered at
p
. Hence,
\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{P},R)
is the Cartesian product of per-
(s,a)
TV-balls centered at
\mathsf{P}^{s,a}
. The non-robust regime is the special case
R=0
.

Model-based pipeline.

Our approach is model-based and proceeds in three steps:

(1)

Estimate target dynamics.
Using limited target samples and side information, compute a constrained estimator
\widehat{\mathsf{P}}=\{\widehat{\mathsf{P}}^{s,a}\in\Delta(\mathcal{S}),a\in\mathcal{A},s\in\mathcal{S}\}
of the target kernel
\mathsf{P}_{\mathrm{t}}
(Sec.
3.1
).

(2)

Optimize a policy.

(Non-robust)

\displaystyle\pi^{\star}\in\arg\max_{\pi}\;V_{\widehat{\mathsf{P}}}^{\pi},

(Robust)

\displaystyle\pi^{\star}\in\arg\max_{\pi}\;\min_{Q\,\in\,\mathcal{P}(\widehat{\mathsf{P}},R^{\prime})}\;V_{Q}^{\pi}.

where
\mathcal{P}(\widehat{\mathsf{P}},R^{\prime})
is defined w.r.t
\widehat{\mathsf{P}}
.

(3)

Evaluate on target.
Assess
V^{\pi^{\star}}
in the target domain (non-robust) or its worst-case value over a target-domain uncertainty set (robust).

When
\widehat{\mathsf{P}}
is closer (in TV) to
\mathsf{P}_{\mathrm{t}}
than the source
\mathsf{P}_{\mathrm{s}}
is, the radius needed to cover the target is smaller, yielding tighter worst-case evaluations and less pessimistic policies while preserving robustness guarantees. Our theory (Sec.
4
) quantifies this via bounds that scale with
\max_{(s,a)}\|\widehat{\mathsf{P}}^{s,a}-\mathsf{P}_{\mathrm{t}}^{s,a}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}
.

Throughout this paper, we denote the target- and source-centered uncertainty sets by
\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{P}_{\mathrm{t}},R)
and
\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{P}_{\mathrm{s}},R)

\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{t}}(R)=\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{P}_{\mathrm{t}},R)

\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{s}}(R)=\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{P}_{\mathrm{s}},R)
, and
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}(R)=\mathcal{P}(\widehat{\mathsf{P}},R)
.

3.1
Information-Based Estimation

We propose an estimator for the target transition kernel that integrates limited offline target data with
side information
about the relationship between source and target dynamics. Given the dataset
\mathcal{D}=\{(s_{i},a_{i},s^{\prime}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}

from the target MDP, let
N(s,a)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}\{(s_{i},a_{i})=(s,a)\}
and
N_{s,a}(s^{\prime})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}\{(s_{i},a_{i},s^{\prime}_{i})=(s,a,s^{\prime})\},
where
\mathbf{1}\{.\}
denotes the indicator function. For each
(s,a)
, we estimate the next-state distribution
\widehat{\mathsf{P}}^{s,a}\in\Delta(\mathcal{S})
as

\widehat{\mathsf{P}}^{s,a}\triangleq\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} &\arg\max_{q\in\Delta(\mathcal{S})}&&\hskip-11.38109pt\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}N_{s,a}(s^{\prime})\log q(s^{\prime})\\
&\text{subject to}&&\hskip-5.69054pt\Phi\bigl(q,\mathsf{P}^{s,a}_{\mathrm{s}}\bigr),\end{aligned}&\hskip-2.84526ptN(s,a)>0,\\
q_{0}^{s,a},&\hskip-5.69054ptN(s,a)=0.\end{cases}

(5)

where
\Phi(\cdot,\mathsf{P}^{s,a}_{\mathrm{s}})
encodes side information tying the target to the source, and
q_{0}^{s,a}
is a prior-informed default when
(s,a)
is unseen. In our experiments we set
q_{0}^{s,a}=\mathsf{P}^{s,a}_{\mathrm{s}}
or uniform
q_{0}^{s,a}=\mathbf{1}/S
.
We refer to (
5
) as the
Information-Based Estimator (IBE)
.

Forms of side information.

We assume that, for each
(s,a)
, the source–target relationship satisfies
\Phi\!\big(\mathsf{P}^{s,a}_{\mathrm{t}},\mathsf{P}^{s,a}_{\mathrm{s}}\big)
. In addition to a no-side-information baseline (
Vanilla IBE
: unconstrained MLE on offline target transition counts), we instantiate
\Phi
in four ways, yielding the variants summarized in Table
1
. The constants (
d_{s,a},\beta_{s,a},B_{s,a}
) below may be per-
(s,a)
or global.

(1)

Distance IBE.

Bound the discrepancy to the source
\mathrm{dist}\!\big(q,\mathsf{P}^{s,a}_{\mathrm{s}}\big)\leq d_{s,a}
,
where
\mathrm{dist}
is either total variation
\|q-\mathsf{P}^{s,a}_{\mathrm{s}}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}
or Wasserstein-1
W_{1}(q,\mathsf{P}^{s,a}_{\mathrm{s}})
with a specified ground cost (Appendix
A
), reflecting the assumption
\mathrm{dist}\!\big(\mathsf{P}^{s,a}_{\mathrm{t}},\mathsf{P}^{s,a}_{\mathrm{s}}\big)\leq d_{s,a}
. In robotics and control, such bounds arise naturally from known limits on physical parameter variations (e.g., friction coefficients, actuator gains, or payload mass estimated via calibration) and can be derived formally from Lipschitz continuity of the dynamics in the parameters (Appendix
D
). In finite state spaces, the radius

d_{s,a}
can also be estimated from source and target samples via minimax-optimal divergence estimators
[
14
,
25
]
.

(2)

Moment IBE.

Constrain feature moments. Let
\phi:\mathcal{S}\to\mathcal{H}
, mapping the state space to a Hilbert space, and
\mu(\mathsf{P})=\mathbb{E}_{s^{\prime}\sim\mathsf{P}}[\phi(s^{\prime})]
. Impose
|\mu(q)-\mu(\mathsf{P}^{s,a}_{\mathrm{s}})|\leq\beta_{s,a}
,
matching the assumption
|\mu(\mathsf{P}^{s,a}_{\mathrm{t}})-\mu(\mathsf{P}^{s,a}_{\mathrm{s}})|\leq\beta_{s,a}
. This captures coarse aggregate information when full transition distributions are unavailable— for instance, known bounds on average velocity or energy dissipation in control systems—and is standard in empirical likelihood and conditional moment restriction frameworks
[
16
]
.

(3)

Density IBE.

Assume absolute continuity and a bounded density ratio on the relevant support:
0\leq\frac{\mathsf{P}^{s,a}_{\mathrm{t}}(s^{\prime})}{\mathsf{P}^{s,a}_{\mathrm{s}}(s^{\prime})}\leq B_{s,a}\quad(\text{whenever }\mathsf{P}^{s,a}_{\mathrm{s}}(s^{\prime})>0)
,
and enforce the element-wise constraint
0\leq q(s^{\prime})\leq B_{s,a}\,\mathsf{P}^{s,a}_{\mathrm{s}}(s^{\prime}),\penalty\ \forall s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}
. The cap
B_{s,a}
encodes practical limits on importance reweighting under distribution shift, preventing excessive variance from extreme weights, and can be estimated from paired source–target samples via ratio estimation methods
[
15
,
25
]
. If support mismatch is possible, we pre-smooth
\mathsf{P}^{s,a}_{\mathrm{s}}
with small pseudocounts.

(4)

Low-Dimensional Structure (LDS) IBE.

For each
(s,a)
, let
\{\mathsf{P}^{s,a}_{\theta}\}_{\theta\in\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{\mathsf{d}}}
be a parametric family (e.g., softmax). Write

\mathsf{P}^{s,a}_{\mathrm{s}}=\mathsf{P}^{s,a}_{\theta_{\mathrm{s}}}
and
\mathsf{P}^{s,a}_{\mathrm{t}}=\mathsf{P}^{s,a}_{\theta_{\mathrm{t}}}
.
Let
\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{shared}}\subseteq\{1,\dots,\mathsf{d}\}
be the coordinates shared by source and target, and define the affine subspace
\Theta_{0}\;\triangleq\;\bigl\{\theta\in\Theta:\ \theta(\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{shared}})=\theta_{\mathrm{s}}(\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{shared}})\bigr\},

whose intrinsic dimension is
\mathsf{d}_{0}=\mathsf{d}-|\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{shared}}|\ll\mathsf{d}
. Such shared coordinates arise naturally when source and target differ in only a subset of physical parameters (for instance, when kinematics are preserved across domains but actuator gains or payload vary) so that the intrinsic degrees of freedom governing the shift are low-dimensional. Estimate the remaining
\mathsf{d}_{0}
free coordinates by constrained MLE (CMLE) on offline target counts:

\widehat{\theta}_{\mathrm{t}}\in\arg\max_{\theta\in\Theta_{0}}\ \sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}N_{s,a}(s^{\prime})\log\mathsf{P}^{s,a}_{\theta}(s^{\prime}),\penalty\ \penalty\ \widehat{\mathsf{P}}^{s,a}=\mathsf{P}^{s,a}_{\widehat{\theta}_{\mathrm{t}}}\!.

If
N(s,a)=0
, use the uniform-initialized default
1/S
.

LDS-IBE

4
Theoretical Analysis

We provide finite-sample and asymptotic guarantees for the side-information–guided estimator and the induced (robust and non-robust) policies. We consider a discounted MDP with
\gamma\in(0,1)
and rewards in
[0,1]
. For exposition, we assume balanced coverage: for each
(s,a)
, we observe
n
i.i.d. target transitions

x^{s,a}_{1},\ldots,x^{s,a}_{n}\sim\mathsf{P}^{s,a}_{\mathrm{t}}
and denote by
\widehat{\mathsf{P}}_{n}^{s,a}
its IBE estimate. Collecting these estimates over all (s,a) pairs yields
\widehat{\mathsf{P}}_{n}=\{\widehat{\mathsf{P}}_{n}^{s,a}\in\Delta(\mathcal{S}):s\in\mathcal{S},a\in\mathcal{A}\}
. Define the estimate-centered uncertainty set
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{n}(R)\triangleq\bigotimes_{(s,a)}\mathbb{B}_{\mathrm{TV}}(\widehat{\mathsf{P}}_{n}^{s,a},R),

making the dependence on
n
explicit. The implementation does not require balanced coverage and instead uses empirical counts
N(s,a)
from the offline target data.

For any policy
\pi
and uncertainty set
\mathcal{P}(R)
, let

V_{\mathcal{P}}^{\pi}=\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{P}(R)}V_{Q}^{\pi}

denote the robust value. Define the target-centered and estimate-centered robust-optimal policies by
\pi^{\star}\in\arg\max_{\pi}V_{\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{t}}\vphantom{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{n}}}^{\pi},\pi_{n}\in\arg\max_{\pi}V_{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{n}}^{\pi}
, respectively.
We also write
V_{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{n}}^{\pi_{n}}
and
V_{\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{t}}\vphantom{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{n}}}^{\pi_{n}}
for the worst-case values of
\pi_{n}
evaluated on
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{n}(R)
and
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{t}}\vphantom{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{n}}(R)
, respectively. The non-robust setting corresponds to
R=0
, where
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{t}}\vphantom{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{n}}(0)
and
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{n}(0)
reduce to singletons. Proofs of all results are deferred to Appendix
F
.

4.1
IBE for Transfer

We show that IBE-based policies are consistent for the target domain.
Let
\delta_{n}\triangleq\max_{(s,a)}\lVert\widehat{\mathsf{P}}_{n}^{s,a}-\mathsf{P}^{s,a}_{\mathrm{t}}\rVert_{\mathrm{TV}}
.

Training and evaluation errors.
We distinguish (i) the
training error
, which compares the estimated-policy value on the estimate-centered set to the target-optimal value; and (ii) the
evaluation error
, which evaluates the estimated policy on the
target-centered
set.

Theorem 1

(Training error)
.

For rewards in
[0,1]
and any
\gamma\in(0,1)
,

\displaystyle\bigl\|V^{\pi_{n}}_{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{n}}-V^{\pi^{\star}}_{\mathcal{P}_{t}\vphantom{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{n}}}\bigr\|_{\infty}\leq\;\frac{2\,\delta_{n}}{(1-\gamma)^{2}}

(6)

The training error scales linearly with the uniform TV error
\delta_{n}
. Hence, the closer
\widehat{\mathsf{P}}_{n}^{s,a}
is to
\mathsf{P}^{s,a}_{\mathrm{t}}
uniformly over
(s,a)
, the closer the training value of
\pi_{n}
is to the target-optimal robust value. As the estimator improves (e.g., via side information),
\delta_{n}\!\downarrow
and
V^{\pi_{n}}_{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{n}}\to V^{\pi^{\star}}_{\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{t}}\vphantom{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{n}}}
.

Theorem 2

(Evaluation error)

Under the same conditions,

\displaystyle\bigl\|V^{\pi_{n}}_{\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{t}}\vphantom{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{n}}}-V^{\pi^{\star}}_{\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{t}}\vphantom{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{n}}}\bigr\|_{\infty}\;\leq\;\frac{4\,\delta_{n}}{(1-\gamma)^{2}}\,.

(7)

Corollary 3

(Consistency)

If

We now leverage IBE’s consistency to obtain

Assumption 5

Conclusion

We proposed a model-based transfer RL framework leveraging limited offline target data and side information to estimate target dynamics and learn effective policies. Centering uncertainty sets at an information-based target estimate (IBE) rather than the source avoids the over-conservatism of source-centered DRO, yielding tighter radii and less pessimistic policies. Incorporating side-information constraints
brings the estimate closer to the true target kernel, reducing the data needed for adaptation.
Theoretically, we established value-function error bounds, scaling with the estimator’s uniform TV error, and finite-sample guarantees. Under LDS, the robust suboptimality gap scales as
\tilde{O}(\sqrt{\mathsf{d}_{0}/n})
, explicitly quantifying the sample-efficiency gains from side information. Empirically, IBE—particularly its density-ratio and moment variants—consistently outperforms RL baselines in both robust and non-robust scenarios.

Appendix B
Cramér–Rao Bounds with Side Information

In this section, we examine the Cramér–Rao Bound (CRB) for the estimator
\widehat{\mathsf{P}}_{n}^{s,a}
under the constrained estimation setting. The CRB provides a lower bound on the variance of a single parameter estimator or the covariance matrix of a vector with multiple parameters. In general, given an unbiased estimator
\hat{\zeta}
of a
k
-element vector
\zeta
, the Fisher Information Matrix (FIM) is defined as
J(\zeta)=\mathbb{E}[\nabla\log(L(\zeta))\nabla\log(L(\zeta))^{\top}]
, where
\nabla\log(L(\zeta))
is the gradient of the log-likelihood function w.r.t.
\zeta
. The CRB then states that the covariance of the unbiased estimator is lower bounded by the inverse
J(\zeta)^{-1}
of the FIM. Before presenting the main result of this subsection, we derive an expression for the FIM
J(\mathsf{P}^{s,a}_{\mathrm{t}})
as stated in the next lemma.

Lemma 11
.

Given
n
i.i.d. samples
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}\sim\mathsf{P}^{s,a}_{\mathrm{t}}
, where
x\in\{s_{1},\dots,s_{k}\}
, with
\mathsf{P}^{s,a}_{\textup{t},j}:=\Pr(x=s_{j}),\mathsf{P}^{s,a}_{\textup{t},j}\neq 0,\forall j\leq k
, the FIM is given by

J(\mathsf{P}^{s,a}_{\mathrm{t}})=n\diag\left(\frac{1}{\mathsf{P}^{s,a}_{\textup{t},1}},\dots,\frac{1}{\mathsf{P}^{s,a}_{\textup{t},k}}\right)\>.

(10)

We can readily state the main result of this subsection.

Theorem 12
.

Let
\widehat{\mathsf{P}}_{n}^{s,a}
be an unbiased estimator of
\mathsf{P}^{s,a}_{\mathrm{t}}
based on
n
i.i.d. samples. For each of the following constraints, the diagonal elements of the CRB matrix,
C_{ii}(\mathsf{P}^{s,a}_{\mathrm{t}})
, are given by:

•

Regular Probability Constraint
(
\sum_{i}^{k}\mathsf{P}^{s,a}_{\textup{t},i}=1
):
C_{ii}(\mathsf{P}^{s,a}_{\mathrm{t}})=\frac{1}{n}\mathsf{P}^{s,a}_{\textup{t},i}(1-\mathsf{P}^{s,a}_{\textup{t},i})

•

Moment constraints
(
\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{P}^{s,a}_{\mathrm{t}}}[\phi(x)]=\mu
, where
x\in\mathcal{S}
and
\phi:\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
, and
\sum_{i}^{k}\mathsf{P}^{s,a}_{\textup{t},i}=1
) :

\displaystyle C_{ii}(\mathsf{P}^{s,a}_{\mathrm{t}})=C^{R}_{ii}(\mathsf{P}^{s,a}_{\mathrm{t}})-\Delta_{i}

(11)

where

–

C^{R}_{ii}
is the
i^{th}
diagonal element of the CRB matrix with regular probability constraints.

–

\Delta_{i}=\frac{(\mathsf{P}^{s,a}_{\textup{t},i})^{2}}{n^{2}}(a_{i}-\overline{a})\mathbb{S}^{-1}(a_{i}-\overline{a})
,
where
a_{i}=\phi(x_{i})-\phi(x_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{m},i=1,\dots,k

\mathbb{S}=\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{Cov}(a),
where
\operatorname{Cov(a)}
is the covariance matrix of
a
.

Theorem
12
provides valuable insights into the quality of the estimator as additional constraints are imposed. Specifically, the diagonal elements of the CRB give lower bounds on the variances of
\widetilde{\mathsf{P}}_{n,i}^{s,a},1\leq i\leq k
, where
\widehat{\mathsf{P}}_{i,n}^{s,a}
is the
i^{th}
element of
\widehat{\mathsf{P}}_{n}^{s,a}
. A smaller lower bound indicates the potential for a better minimum variance estimator. To quantify the CRB matrix, we can compute the trace of the CRB
C
, which provides a lower bound on the sum of the estimator variances. Formally, we have
\sum_{i=1}^{k}\operatorname{var}[\widehat{\mathsf{P}}_{n}^{s,a}]\geq\operatorname{trace}(C(\mathsf{P}^{s,a}_{\mathrm{t}}))=\sum_{i=1}^{k}C_{ii}(\mathsf{P}^{s,a}_{\mathrm{t}})

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathsf{P}^{s,a}_{\text{t},i}(1-\mathsf{P}^{s,a}_{\text{t},i})\geq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{\mathsf{P}^{s,a}_{\text{t},i}(1-\mathsf{P}^{s,a}_{\text{t},i})}{n}-\frac{(\mathsf{P}^{s,a}_{\text{t},i})^{2}(a_{i}-\overline{a})^{\top}\mathbb{S}(a_{i}-\overline{a})}{n^{2}}.

(12)

Since
\mathbb{S}
is positive definite (assuming
\operatorname{Cov}(a)
is full rank),
\Delta_{i}\geq 0

Equation (

be the Value-Aware 1-Wasserstein distance defined w.r.t the source metric

\widehat{\mathsf{P}}_{n}^{s,a}
be the IBE, given by (

Appendix D
Deriving Side Information from System Knowledge

In many robotics/control settings, the next state is generated as

s^{\prime}=f(s,a,\theta)+\xi
, where
\theta
collects physical parameters (e.g., masses, friction, damping, actuator gains) and
\xi
is additive noise with fixed law. Calibration, manufacturer tolerances, and system identification routinely yield
bounded parameter sets

\|\theta_{t}-\theta_{s}\|\leq\varepsilon
. For any fixed
(s,a)
, consider the two next-state laws
\mathsf{P}_{\theta_{1}}^{s,a},\mathsf{P}_{\theta_{2}}^{s,a}
induced by
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}
. We obtain

W_{1}\!\big(\mathsf{P}_{\theta_{1}}^{s,a},\mathsf{P}_{\theta_{2}}^{s,a}\big)\;\leq\;\mathbb{E}\,\big\|f(s,a,\theta_{1})-f(s,a,\theta_{2})\big\|.

If
f
is
L_{\theta}
-Lipschitz in
\theta
(uniformly in
(s,a)
), then

W_{1}\!\big(\mathsf{P}_{\theta_{1}}^{s,a},\mathsf{P}_{\theta_{2}}^{s,a}\big)\;\leq\;L_{\theta}\,\|\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}\|\quad\Rightarrow\quad W_{1}\!\big(\mathsf{P}_{\theta_{t}}^{s,a},\mathsf{P}_{\theta_{s}}^{s,a}\big)\;\leq\;L_{\theta}\,\varepsilon.

On discrete spaces with minimum separation
m>0
under the ground cost, Kantorovich–Rubinstein yields

\|\mathsf{P}_{\theta_{t}}^{s,a}-\mathsf{P}_{\theta_{s}}^{s,a}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\;\leq\;\tfrac{1}{m}\,W_{1}\!\big(\mathsf{P}_{\theta_{t}}^{s,a},\mathsf{P}_{\theta_{s}}^{s,a}\big)\;\leq\;\tfrac{L_{\theta}}{m}\,\varepsilon,

providing a non-vacuous TV and Wasserstein radius for our Distance–IBE constraints. When physical parameter bounds are unavailable, the Wasserstein radius
d_{s,a}
can alternatively be obtained from data via KL estimation. Specifically, on a state space of bounded diameter
D
, Pinsker-type inequalities give

W_{1}\!\big(\mathsf{P}_{\theta_{t}}^{s,a},\mathsf{P}_{\theta_{s}}^{s,a}\big)\;\leq\;D\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{KL}\!\big(\mathsf{P}_{\theta_{t}}^{s,a}\|\mathsf{P}_{\theta_{s}}^{s,a}\big)},

so that a minimax-optimal KL estimate
[
50
]
directly yields a valid Wasserstein radius, connecting sample-based divergence estimation to our Distance–IBE framework.

Appendix E
Explicit Dependence of
\delta_{n}
on different forms of the side information
\Phi

Overview.

Our value guarantees are indexed by the deviation

\delta_{n}\triangleq\max_{s,a}\|\widehat{\mathsf{P}}_{n}^{s,a}-\mathsf{P}_{\mathrm{t}}^{s,a}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}
.
This appendix explains how different forms of side information
\Phi
enter the bounds by restricting the feasible set of the constrained estimator and thereby reducing the deviation term
\delta_{n}
.

Distance-IBE (TV / Wasserstein constraints).

Suppose the side information specifies a source-anchored radius
d
, e.g.,

W_{1}(\mathsf{P}_{\mathrm{t}}^{s,a},\mathsf{P}_{\mathrm{s}}^{s,a})\leq d
or

\|\mathsf{P}_{\mathrm{t}}^{s,a}-\mathsf{P}_{\mathrm{s}}^{s,a}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq d
,
and that the estimator
\widehat{\mathsf{P}}_{n}^{s,a}
is constrained to the same ball around the source kernel
\mathsf{P}_{\mathrm{s}}^{s,a}
.
Then, by the triangle inequality,

\delta_{n}=\max_{s,a}\|\widehat{\mathsf{P}}_{n}^{s,a}-\mathsf{P}_{\mathrm{t}}^{s,a}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq\max_{s,a}\|\mathsf{P}_{\mathrm{t}}^{s,a}-\mathsf{P}_{\mathrm{s}}^{s,a}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}+\max_{s,a}\|\widehat{\mathsf{P}}_{n}^{s,a}-\mathsf{P}_{\mathrm{s}}^{s,a}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq 2d.

This yields an explicit, non-vacuous pre-asymptotic bound on
\delta_{n}
in terms of the side-information radius
d
.
When side information is expressed in
W_{1}
or KL, analogous bounds follow via standard inequalities such as Kantorovich–Rubinstein and Pinsker’s inequality (e.g., on discrete
\mathcal{S}
,
\mathrm{TV}\leq\tfrac{1}{m}W_{1}
and
\mathrm{TV}\leq\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{KL}}
).

Moment-IBE.

A constraint of the form
\|\mu(q)-\mu(\mathsf{P}^{s,a}_{\mathrm{s}})\|\leq\beta_{s,a}
for feature map
\phi
induces an integral probability metric–type control:

\sup_{\|g\|\leq 1}\big|\mathbb{E}_{q}[g(\phi)]-\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{P}^{s,a}_{\mathrm{t}}}[g(\phi)]\big|\leq\beta_{s,a}+\|\mu(\mathsf{P}^{s,a}_{\mathrm{t}})-\mu(\mathsf{P}^{s,a}_{\mathrm{s}})\|.

When the value function
V
is Lipschitz in
\phi
, this control translates into a TV or
W_{1}
bound, thereby reducing
\delta_{n}
relative to unconstrained maximum-likelihood estimation at the same sample size
n
.

Density-IBE.

Density-ratio constraints of the form
0\leq q\leq B\,\mathsf{P}_{\mathrm{s}}
enforce support overlap and prevent off-support mass.
For bounded
B
, such constraints yield uniform bounds on deviations of the form

|q^{\top}v-(\mathsf{P}_{\mathrm{t}})^{\top}v|
for bounded value functions
v
,
which again translate into TV control via dual norms, tightening
\delta_{n}
.

LDS-IBE.

Under a
d_{0}
-dimensional parameterization and a TV-Lipschitz mapping

\theta\mapsto\mathsf{P}_{\theta}^{s,a}
,
the constrained maximum-likelihood estimator admits a finite-sample deviation

R_{n}=\tilde{O}(d_{0}/\sqrt{n})
,
implying
\delta_{n}=\tilde{O}(d_{0}/\sqrt{n})
.
This rate underlies the improved robust suboptimality gap established in Theorem 7.

Collectively, these instantiations show how different choices of side information
\Phi
enter the guarantees explicitly through
\delta_{n}
, and hence directly affect both the training and evaluation errors as well as the robust suboptimality gap.

F.7
Proof of Theorem
13

Proof.

Denote the optimal policy w.r.t the uncertainty set
\mathcal{P}_{n}
by
\pi_{n}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\pi_{n}=\arg\max_{\pi}V^{\pi}_{\mathcal{P}_{n}}.

(53)

Then, we have that

\displaystyle\|V^{\pi_{n}}_{\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{t}}\vphantom{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{n}}}-V^{\pi^{*}}_{\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{t}}\vphantom{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{n}}}\|_{\infty}\leq\|V^{\pi_{n}}_{\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{t}}\vphantom{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{n}}}-V^{\pi_{n}}_{\mathcal{P}_{n}}\|_{\infty}+\|V^{\pi_{n}}_{\mathcal{P}_{n}}-V^{\pi^{*}}_{\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{t}}\vphantom{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{n}}}\|_{\infty}.

(54)

For the first term on the RHS of (
54
), we follow the proof of Theorem
1
. Therefore, we have for any
s\in\mathcal{S}
:

\displaystyle|V^{\pi_{n}}_{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{n}}(s)-V^{\pi^{*}}_{\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{t}}\vphantom{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{n}}}(s)|\leq\max_{\pi}|V^{\pi}_{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{n}}(s)-V^{\pi}_{\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{t}}\vphantom{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{n}}}(s)|.

(55)

For any policy
\pi
, we have

\displaystyle|V^{\pi}_{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{n}}(s)-V^{\pi}_{\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{t}}\vphantom{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{n}}}(s)|\leq\gamma\|V^{\pi}_{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{n}}-V^{\pi}_{\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{t}}\vphantom{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{n}}}\|+\gamma|(\widehat{\mathsf{P}}_{n}^{s,\pi(s)}-\mathsf{P}_{\text{t}}^{s,\pi(s)})^{\top}V^{\pi}_{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{n}}|\>,

(56)

By Kantorovich duality, given two distributions
\mathsf{P},\mathsf{Q}\in\Delta(\mathcal{S})
, the Wasserstein-1 distance induced by
d_{V}
is defined as

W_{d_{V}}(\mathsf{P},\mathsf{Q}):=\sup_{\|f\|_{\text{Lip}(d_{V})}\leq 1}\left|\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{P}}[f]-\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{Q}}[f]\right|,

where

\|f\|_{\text{Lip}(d_{V})}:=\sup_{s\neq s^{\prime}}\frac{|f(s)-f(s^{\prime})|}{d_{V}(s,s^{\prime})}.

Since
V^{\pi}_{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{n}}
is 1-Lipschitz with respect to
d_{V}
, we have

\displaystyle|(\widehat{\mathsf{P}}_{n}^{s,\pi(s)}-\mathsf{P}_{\text{t}}^{s,\pi(s)})^{\top}V^{\pi}_{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{n}}|\leq W_{d_{V}}(\widehat{\mathsf{P}}_{n}^{s,\pi(s)},\mathsf{P}_{\text{t}}^{s,\pi(s)}).

(57)

By the triangle inequality, we obtain,

W_{d_{V}}(\widehat{\mathsf{P}}_{n}^{s,\pi(s)},\mathsf{P}_{\text{t}}^{s,\pi(s)})\leq W_{d_{V}}(\widehat{\mathsf{P}}_{n}^{s,\pi(s)},\mathsf{P}_{\text{s}}^{s,\pi(s)})+W_{d_{V}}(\mathsf{P}_{\text{s}}^{s,\pi(s)},\mathsf{P}_{\text{t}}^{s,\pi(s)})\leq 2\beta_{1}.

Therefore, we have,

|V^{\pi}_{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{n}}(s)-V^{\pi}_{\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{t}}\vphantom{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{n}}}(s)|\leq\gamma\|V^{\pi}_{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{n}}-V^{\pi}_{\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{t}}\vphantom{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{n}}}\|_{\infty}+2\gamma\beta_{1}.

Applying the above recursively, we get

\|V^{\pi_{n}}_{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{n}}-V^{\pi^{*}}_{\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{t}}\vphantom{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{n}}}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{2\beta_{1}\gamma}{(1-\gamma)}.

Also, we have

\|V^{\pi_{n}}_{\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{t}}\vphantom{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{n}}}-V^{\pi_{n}}_{\mathcal{P}_{n}}\|_{\infty}\leq\max_{\pi}\|V^{\pi}_{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{n}}-V^{\pi}_{\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{t}}\vphantom{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{n}}}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{2\beta_{1}\gamma}{(1-\gamma)}.

Combining the RHS of both terms of (
54
) completes the proof.

F.9
Proof of Lemma
11

Proof.

To simplify notation, we denote
\mathsf{P}^{s,a}_{\mathrm{t}}
by
\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}}
.
Let
S
be a random vector that takes one of the values from
\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{k}\}
, where
e_{j},j=1,\ldots,k
, is a vector with a 1 in the
j
-th position and 0 elsewhere (one-hot encoding). The entries are denoted
s_{j}
, where
\Pr(s_{j}=1)=\Pr(S=e_{j})=\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}}_{j}
, so the vector
\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}}=[\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}}_{1},\ldots,\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}}_{k}]^{T}
, and
\sum_{j=1}^{k}\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}}_{j}=1
. To compute the Fisher Information Matrix (FIM), we define the likelihood

\Pr(S|\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}})=\prod_{j=1}^{k}{\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}}_{j}}^{s_{j}}.

Hence,

\log\Pr(s|\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}})=\sum_{j=1}^{k}s_{j}\log\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}}_{j}.

Therefore, the FIM,
I_{S}(\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}})=\mathbb{E}[\nabla\log\Pr(s|\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}})\nabla\log\Pr(s|\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}})^{T}]
, is given by:

I_{S}(\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}})=\mathbb{E}\left(\left[\begin{array}[]{c}s_{1}/\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}}_{1}\\
\vdots\\
s_{k}/\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}}_{k}\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}s_{1}/\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}}_{1}&\cdots&s_{k}/\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}}_{k}\end{array}\right]\right).

This simplifies to

I_{S}(\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}})=\mathbb{E}\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}(s_{1}/\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}}_{1})^{2}&s_{1}s_{2}/(\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}}_{1}\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}}_{2})&\cdots&s_{1}s_{k}/(\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}}_{1}\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}}_{k})\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
s_{k}s_{1}/(\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}}_{k}\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}}_{1})&\cdots&\cdots&(s_{k}/\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}}_{k})^{2}\end{array}\right].

Now,
\mathbb{E}[(s_{i}/\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}}_{i})^{2}]=\frac{1}{\mathsf{P}_{i}^{{}^{\prime}2}}\mathbb{E}[s_{i}^{2}]=1/\mathsf{P}_{i}^{{}^{\prime}2}(1\cdot\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}}_{i}+0)=1/\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}}_{i},i=1,\ldots,k
and
\mathbb{E}[(s_{i}s_{j}/\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}}_{i}\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}}_{j})]=(1/\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}}_{i}\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}}_{j})\mathbb{E}[s_{i}s_{j}]=0
, for
i\neq j
. Thus,

I_{S}(\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}})=\text{diag}\left(\frac{1}{\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}}_{1}},\ldots,\frac{1}{\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}}_{k}}\right).

If we observe
n
i.i.d. vectors
S
, we get that
J(\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}})=nI_{S}(\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}})
.

∎

F.10
Proof of Theorem
12

We use the same notation simplifications as in the proof of Lemma
11
.
We start by bringing in the following lemma.

Lemma 16

(Theroem 2,
[
23
]
)
.

Let the
k
-parameter vector
\widehat{\theta}
be a constrained unbiased estimator of the parameter
\theta
, with
M
constrains
f(\theta)=0
for some real valued function
f
. Then, the CRB is given by:

C(\theta)=J^{-1}-J^{-1}F(F^{T}J^{-1}F)^{-1}F^{T}J^{-1}.

(58)

where J is the FIM and F is
k\times M
gradient matrix w.r.t
\theta
.

Proof.

Regular probability constraint
(
\sum_{i}^{k}\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}}_{i}=1
):

Due to the normalization constraint
\sum_{i}^{k}\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}}_{i}=1
, the probabilities
\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}}_{i}
are dependent. To handle this, we express
\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}}_{k}=1-\sum_{i}^{k-1}\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}}_{i}
, and use a reduced parameter vector
\theta=[\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}}_{1},\dots,\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}}_{k-1}]
. Computing the FIM, we get:

\displaystyle J=n\left(\diag\Big(\Big[\frac{1}{\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}}_{1}},\dots,\frac{1}{\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}}_{k-1}}\Big]\Big)+\frac{1}{\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}}_{k}}\mathbf{1}_{k-1}\mathbf{1}_{k-1}^{\top}\right)

(59)

where
\mathbf{1}_{k-1}
is a vector of all ones of size
k-1
. Using Sherman-Morrison-Woodbury formula
[
28
]
, we compute the the CRB matrix, i.e.,
J^{-1}
:

\displaystyle C(\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}})=J^{-1}=\frac{1}{n}\left(\diag(\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}})-\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}}(\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}})^{\top}\right)\>.

(60)

Moment constraint
(
\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}}}[\phi(x)]=\mu
, where
x\in\mathcal{S}
and
\phi:\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
, and
\sum_{i}^{k}\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}}_{i}=1
):
By Lemma
16
, the CRB matrix
C
is given by:

\displaystyle C(\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}})={C_{R}}^{-1}-{C_{R}}^{-1}F(F^{T}{C_{R}}^{-1}F)^{-1}F^{\top}{C_{R}}^{-1}\>.

(61)

Here, we use
C_{R}
to denote the CRB in Equation (
60
), incorporating only the regular probability constraint.
The constraints,
f(\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}})=\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}}}[\phi(x)]-\mu=0\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, can be rewritten as:

\displaystyle f(\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\phi(x_{i})\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}}_{i}+\phi(x_{k})\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}}_{k}-\mu=0

\displaystyle\overset{(*)}{=}\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}(\phi(x_{i})-\phi(x_{k}))\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}}_{i}+\phi(x_{k})-\mu=0

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}a_{i}\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}}_{i}+b=0\quad(\text{$m$ constraints})

(62)

where (*) follows from the constraint
\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}}_{k}=1-\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}}_{i}
,
a_{i}=\phi(x_{i})-\phi(x_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, and
b=\phi(x_{k})-\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

Let the constraint set
f(\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}})=[f_{1},\dots,f_{m}]
, then
the gradient matrix F of size
m\times(k-1)
is given by:

\displaystyle\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}\frac{df_{1}}{d\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}}_{1}}&\dots&\frac{df_{1}}{d\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}}_{k-1}}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\frac{df_{m}}{d\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}}_{1}}&\dots&\frac{df_{m}}{d\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}}_{k-1}}\\
\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}a_{1,1}&\dots&a_{1,k-1}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
a_{m,1}&\dots&a_{m,k-1}\\
\end{array}\right]

where
a_{i}=[a_{1,i},\dots,a_{m,i}]
.
Then,

\displaystyle\mathbb{S}\overset{\Delta}{=}F^{T}{C_{R}}^{-1}F

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}a_{i}a_{i}^{\top}\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}}_{i}-\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}a_{i}\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}}_{i}\right)\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}a_{j}\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}}_{j}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{Cov}(a)

(69)

where
a
is a vector that takes the value
a_{i}
with probability
\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}}_{i}
, and
\operatorname{Cov}(a)
is its covariance matrix.
Also,
C_{R}^{-1}F=\frac{1}{n}(\diag(\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}}_{i})-\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}}(\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}})^{\top})F=\frac{1}{n}(\mathbb{P}-\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}}\overline{a}^{\top})
,
where
\mathbb{P}
is the
(k-1)\times m
matrix with rows
\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}}_{i}a_{i}
and
\overline{a}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}\mathsf{P}^{s,a}_{\textup{t},i}
.
Thus,

C(\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}})={C_{R}}^{-1}-({C_{R}}^{-1}F)\mathbb{S}^{-1}({C_{R}}^{-1}F)^{\top}

and

\operatorname{Var}[\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}}_{i}]\geq({C_{R}}^{-1})_{ii}-({C_{R}}^{-1}F)_{i}^{\top}\mathbb{S}^{-1}(C_{R}^{-1}F)_{i}

where
({C_{R}}^{-1})_{ii}=\frac{\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}}_{i}(1-\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}}_{i})}{n}
and
({C_{R}}^{-1}F)_{i}^{\top}=\frac{\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}}_{i}}{n}(a_{i}-\overline{a})
is the
i^{th}
row of
({C_{R}}^{-1}F)
.
Then,

\displaystyle\Delta_{i}

\displaystyle:=({C_{R}}^{-1}F)_{i}^{\top}\mathbb{S}^{-1}(C_{R}^{-1}F)_{i}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}}_{i}}{n}(a_{i}-\overline{a})\right)^{\top}\mathbb{S}^{-1}\left(\frac{\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}}_{i}}{n}(a_{i}-\overline{a})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{(\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}}_{i})^{2}}{n^{2}}(a_{i}-\overline{a})^{\top}\mathbb{S}^{-1}(a_{i}-\overline{a})\>.

(70)

Since
\mathbb{S}
is positive definite (assuming
\operatorname{Cov}(a)
is full rank),
\Delta_{i}\geq 0
, indicating that the lower bound on the variance of the estimator is reduced with the incorporation of the moment constraint.

We remark that, if
\operatorname{Cov}(a)
is not a full rank matrix, then
\mathbb{S}^{-1}
can be replaced with the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse
\mathbb{S}^{\dagger}
, i.e.,
C(\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}})={C_{R}}^{-1}-({C_{R}}^{-1}F)\mathbb{S}^{\dagger}({C^{R}}^{-1}F)^{\top}
. The matrix
\Delta:=({C_{R}}^{-1}F)\mathbb{S}^{\dagger}({C_{R}}^{-1}F)^{\top}
is positive semi-definite (PSD), so

C(\mathsf{P}^{{}^{\prime}})={C_{R}}^{-1}-\Delta\preceq{C_{R}}^{-1}\>,

so the constrained matrix is less than or equal to the unconstrained one in the PSD ordering, indicating that the reduction in the lower bound on the variance still holds.

c_{j}
first
M
