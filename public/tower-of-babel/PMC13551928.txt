Dual channel drug-drug interactions extraction based on cross attention
Authors: Yaxun Jia, Zhu Yuan, Yunchao Gong, Haixiang Yang, Zuo-lin Xiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Drug-drug interactions (DDIs) play a critical role in several biomedical applications, particularly in pharmacovigilance. While neural networks have shown promise in DDI extraction, existing methods often overlook salient information. To address this challenge, we propose a two-channel DDI relationship extraction model based on cross attention - DCCA-IE. This model consists of a sequence channel using BioBERT-CNN and a graph channel integrating BioBERT-LSTM-GAT. The dual channel architecture is crucial to capture both sequential and relational information present in biomedical texts. Furthermore, by employing a cross attention mechanism, we effectively integrate information from the dual channels, resulting in a comprehensive representation for DDI relationship extraction. Experimental evaluation on a benchmark dataset shows that our method outperforms state-of-the-art methods in DDI extraction.

Introduction

As the number of drugs used in clinical practice continues to grow, the potential for adverse reactions due to drug-drug interactions (DDIs) poses an escalating health risk to patients. A wealth of information on DDIs has accumulated in the biomedical literature, reflecting continuous scientific progress. However, this information is often scattered across an ever-growing corpus of text, making manual extraction not only challenging but also time consuming [1–4]. The development of automated systems for extracting DDIs from biomedical literature is therefore of paramount importance. Such systems can speed up the research process and facilitate the discovery of new DDIs, providing critical insights for clinical practitioners and healthcare professionals.

Recent advances have demonstrated the use of deep neural networks to extract DDIs from the DDI Extraction 2013 corpus, marking a significant shift towards more sophisticated computational techniques in pharmacological research [5–11]. While neural network-based methods have been found to generally outperform traditional feature-based approaches in identifying DDIs, they also have notable limitations. In particular, neural networks often struggle to process the long and complex sentence structures typical of medical texts. To address this issue, some researchers have integrated the shortest dependency path (SDP) technique into their neural network frameworks [12–14]. This method improves the extraction process by filtering out irrelevant data and focusing on the critical information needed to understand drug interactions, thereby enhancing the network’s ability to manage noise and retain essential details for accurate DDI extraction. However, despite the advantages of SDP, recent studies [15] suggest that this approach may not capture all relevant details, as the reliance on predetermined paths may result in missing critical information embedded in sentence structures. This points to the need for more sophisticated models that can effectively exploit the rich linguistic and semantic cues present in biomedical literature, without sacrificing the depth of information extracted. Improvements in these models could significantly boost the accuracy and reliability of automated DDI extraction systems, leading to safer pharmaceutical practices and improved patient outcomes. To address the above challenges, we propose a novel dual channel DDI relationship extraction model based on a cross attention mechanism. This model integrates sequence channels and graph channels using a dual channel approach to enrich the semantics of the text and facilitate the extraction of textual information. Specifically, the sequence channels are designed to capture the order and contextual relationships of drug names within the text, providing a linear perspective of interactions. Simultaneously, the graph channels exploit the connectivity between drug entities, adding a layer of semantic depth through a more explicit mapping of interrelationships. Building on this dual channel framework, we introduce a cross-attention mechanism that merges self-attention with gated attention. The self attention mechanism is instrumental in capturing global dependencies across long texts, facilitating a broader understanding of context. Gated attention complements this by refining the allocation of feature weights, thereby sharpening the model’s focus on the most relevant information for DDI relationships. This innovative application of cross attention ensures effective integration of the dual channel features, significantly strengthening the model’s ability to detect and prioritise drug interaction features. Additionally, this mechanism improves the overall weight configuration within the model, leading to improved accuracy and performance in text-based DDI extraction. Related work

Extracting drug drug interactions (DDIs) from literature is receiving increasing attention from the research community. According to the differences in methods, current research can be categorized into two main types: machine learning methods and deep learning methods.

Machine learning methods

Bjorne et al. [16] discuss the Turku Event Extraction System (TEES), which uses Support Vector Machine (SVM) and deep syntactic parsing, augmented by data from DrugBank and MetaMap, to identify drug names and DDIs from text. Kim et al. [17] present a linear kernel-based system for extracting DDIs from biomedical literature, demonstrating competitive performance against non-linear kernel methods. Segura et al. [18] investigate the automatic extraction of DDIs from biomedical texts using two approaches: a hybrid method combining shallow parsing with pattern matching, and a supervised learning method applying support vector machines (SVMs). The results suggest that their methods could significantly assist healthcare professionals in the early detection of DDIs. Chowdhury et al. [19] introduce a multi-stage relation extraction method that exploits semantic roles and negation ranges to filter training data and improve classifier accuracy. The approach combines dynamic and static knowledge with advanced kernel techniques to effectively reduce negative instance noise. Thomas et al. [20] propose a two-step ensemble learning approach, combining machine learning classifiers for initial drug pair extraction with subtype relabelling using TEES. F1 scores ranging from 73% to nearly 76% were achieved for interaction detection and 60.9% for DDI subtypes. Zheng et al. [21] present a graph kernel method that uses context vectors to extract DDIs from biomedical text, constructing graphs from sentences to analyse word relationships at different distances. Context vectors are generated for each vertex using an iterative process involving adjacent and non-adjacent nodes, allowing detailed sentence structure to be captured.

Deep learning methods

Quan et al. [22] present a multi-channel convolutional neural network (MCCNN) for biological relation extraction. By integrating multiple word embeddings and automating feature extraction with CNNs, the MCCNN model advances semantic representation and eliminates the need for manual feature engineering. Liu et al. [23] propose a novel method for DDI extraction from biomedical texts using convolutional neural networks (CNNs). By utilising word embeddings and positional embeddings, the approach captures semantic information and word relationships, eliminating the need for traditional NLP toolkits. Sahu et al. [24] describe an LSTM-based approach for extracting DDIs from biomedical text using three models: B-LSTM, AB-LSTM and Joint AB-LSTM, all of which use word and positional embeddings to avoid explicit feature engineering. The AB-LSTM and Joint AB-LSTM further enhance feature representation through attentive pooling in a Bi-LSTM network. Yi et al. [25] introduced the 2ATT-RNN model with two levels of attention: word-level and sentence-level. Word-level attention evaluates the importance of individual words for classification, while sentence-level attention leverages information from other relevant sentences containing the same drug entities to enrich feature extraction. Kavuluru et al. [26] propose a method for DDI extraction from unstructured text using recurrent neural network (RNN) architectures, specifically exploring character-level RNNs (Char-RNNs). Zhang et al. [15] introduce a hierarchical recurrent neural network (RNN) model for DDI extraction that integrates both sentence sequence and shortest dependency path (SDP) between drug entities. By applying an embedding attention mechanism, it enhances keywords relevant to DDI extraction. Asada et al. [27] introduce an approach for DDI extraction. By using convolutional neural networks (CNNs) for textual drug pairs and graph convolutional networks (GCNs) for their molecular counterparts, the method concatenates the outputs to improve extraction accuracy. Zhou et al. [28] propose a position-aware deep multi-task learning method utilizing an attention-based BiLSTM network to extract DDIs from biomedical text. This approach incorporates the relative positions of words with respect to target drugs into the learning process. By simultaneously predicting the presence of interactions and classifying interaction types in a multi-task framework, the model achieves better accuracy. Sun et al. [29] introduce DeepCNN2, which enhances network depth and incorporates multiple convolution sizes. DeepCNN2 aims to optimize network performance with a depth of 16 layers. Experimental results demonstrate that increasing the depth of CNNs improves the performance of DDI extraction. Xiong et al. [30] introduced a new graph convolutional neural network (GCNN) model that leverages a dependency graph combined with a bidirectional long short-term memory (BiLSTM) to extract DDIs from biomedical literature. BioBERT [31] boosts performance in named entity recognition, relationship extraction, and question answering tasks through pre-training on large biomedical datasets. It outperforms traditional BERT models and displays superior performance on various biomedical NLP tasks due to its specialization in handling biomedical terminologies and structures. The pre-training of BlueBERT [32] exploits rich biomedical text data, enabling the model to more accurately understand medical terminologies, entities, and relationships, thus supporting various text processing tasks in the biomedical domain. PubMedBERT [33] undergoes domain-specific pre-training from scratch, as opposed to pre-training on general domain corpora, leading to significant performance improvements. Su et al. [34] present a pioneering contrastive pre-training method that enriches the textual representation of BERT models for biomedical relation extraction tasks. This is achieved by integrating linguistic knowledge with data augmentation techniques. It also harnesses external knowledge sources to construct large datasets, thereby enhancing the generalization capability of BERT models through contrastive pre-training. Su et al. [35] incorporate sub-domain adaptation during pre-training to align domain and task-specific knowledge. Furthermore, they introduce a novel SSL fine-tuning mechanism that employs final-layer BERT knowledge to augment model performance. The goal is to enhance relation extraction tasks by fine-tuning the model to the requirements of the biomedical domain.

It can provide high quality embedding representations for text data, thus laying a solid foundation for various natural language processing tasks. BioBERT is a pre-trained model derived from BERT, specifically optimised for the biomedical domain to better handle semantic information related to drugs and other medical concepts. Dual channel module

After the embedding module completes the vectorized representation of the text, the dual channel uses different feature extraction modules from the sequence channel and graph channel to extract text features. The structure enriches the means of feature extraction, analyzing text features from multiple perspectives of sequence features and graph structure features, and mining text association information.

Through this approach, the CNN in the sequence channel can extract rich feature representations from the text and apply them to DDI classification tasks. This combination fully leverages the capabilities of BioBERT in biomedical text understanding, while exploiting the advantages of CNN in local feature extraction. Although recent research has explored deeper Transformer-based architectures for sequential modeling, we choose CNN for its computational efficiency and strong performance in local feature extraction. CNNs are highly effective at capturing n-gram-like patterns (e.g., short phrases involving drug names and interaction triggers), which are crucial in biomedical texts. Moreover, when combined with BioBERT embeddings, CNNs provide a good balance between local context capture and global semantic understanding, while avoiding the higher computational cost of recurrent or Transformer-based models.

By combining LSTM and GAT, graph channels can simultaneously account for the temporal dependencies in text sequences and the complex relationships between texts, providing a more comprehensive and accurate feature representation for DDI classification tasks. While newer models such as Graph Transformers have emerged, we adopt the BiLSTM + GAT architecture because it effectively captures both sequential dependencies (via BiLSTM) and structural relationships (via GAT) with lower computational overhead. GAT remains a competitive baseline for biomedical relation extraction, especially when the graph structures are of moderate size, as in the DDI dataset. This hybrid design ensures rich feature extraction while maintaining interpretability and efficiency.

The dual channel combines the feature representations of the sequence and graph channels through information fusion, strengthening the model’s ability to understand complex relationships between texts. This dual channel design provides stronger expressiveness and interpretability for addressing DDI relation extraction tasks.

Cross attention module

The cross attention module can effectively integrate feature representations from the sequence channel and the graph channel. The use of cross attention mechanism can promote the information interaction between different channels, allowing the model to better capture and leverage these features for DDI relationship classification. Compared with self attention, it can selectively calculate attention to feature information, achieve integration and optimization of feature information output from the fused dual-channel layer, improve the model’s ability to select output information from both channels, and enhance the model’s ability to extract text features.

In our model, the cross attention module combines two key mechanisms: self-attention and gated attention. The self-attention mechanism captures global dependencies by computing the relevance between all token pairs, which is crucial in biomedical texts where drug entities and interaction cues may be far apart. The gated attention mechanism introduces a dynamic weighting function that helps the model focus on the most relevant parts of the input while suppressing irrelevant information. This dual mechanism ensures that both global context and fine-grained features are fully utilized for better DDI extraction. The attention score can be calculated through a dot product operation and then normalised with the softmax function:14\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} Attention(H_s, H_g) = softmax(\frac{H_s H^T_g}{\sqrt{d}}) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where d is the dimension of the feature, used to scale the result of the dot product for gradient stability.

Use the obtained attention score to weight and combine features to generate an updated feature representation:15\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} H'_s = Attention(H_s,H_g)H_g \end{aligned}$$\end{document}16\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} H'_g = Attention(H_g,H_s)H_s \end{aligned}$$\end{document}\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$H'_s$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$H'_g$$\end{document} are weighted new feature matrices, and this mutual attention approach allows the features of the two channels to complement each other, enhancing the expression ability of the features.

To further refine the integrated features, we apply a gated attention mechanism after cross-attention. The gated attention is implemented as:17\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} {H'' = \sigma (W_g \cdot H') \odot H'} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where H' is the output from the cross-attention layer, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$W_g$$\end{document} is a learnable weight matrix, σ is the sigmoid activation function, and ⊙ denotes element-wise multiplication. This operation adaptively controls the flow of feature information, allowing the model to emphasize key patterns (e.g., drug names and interaction verbs) while reducing noise.

The cross attention module can significantly strengthen the model’s ability to integrate different types of features. This is particularly critical for DDI relationship extraction, as different channels may capture various aspects of semantic and structural information. Cross attention enables the model to make judgments based on more comprehensive information. By optimizing the interaction between features, the model can more accurately identify and classify complex DDI types, increasing overall classification performance.

Output module

In general, we consider DDI tasks as classification tasks. In our model, we assume that the feature vector received from the cross attention module is h, and the fully connected layer of the output layer can be described as:18\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} z = W_h X + b_h \end{aligned}$$\end{document}The weight matrix of the connection layer is denoted by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$W_h$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$b_h$$\end{document} indicates the bias vector. z is the output of this fully connected layer, commonly referred to as the “logic” or “score” vector, where each element corresponds to a specific DDI category.

To convert the output of the fully connected layer into a probability distribution, we apply the softmax function:19\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \hat{y} = softmax(z) = \frac{exp(z)}{\Sigma ^K_{k=1} exp(z_k)} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{y}$$\end{document} is the probability distribution predicted by the model, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{y_i}$$\end{document} is the probability that the predicted input belongs to the class i of the DDI relationship. K\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ is the number of categories in the DDI relations. exp(z_k)$$\end{document} is the exponential function of the kth element in z, ensuring that all output probabilities are positive and sum to 1.

During the training process, the cross-entropy loss function can be used to optimize the model parameters, which is a standard method for addressing multi-class classification problems.20\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} L=-\sum \limits _{i=1}^K y_i log(\hat{y_i}) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$y_i$$\end{document} is the unique hot encoding form of the true label, where if the sample belongs to class i, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$y_i=1$$\end{document}, otherwise \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$y_i=0$$\end{document}. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$log(\hat{y_i})$$\end{document} is the logarithm of the predicted probability, used to compute the negative logarithm of the predicted probability for the true class label, which is an important part of the loss function.

The output layer transforms the complex features from the cross attention layer into predicted probabilities for each DDI category through the fully connected layer and the softmax activation function mentioned above. The use of a cross entropy loss function not only helps the model optimize its performance during training but also guarantees the interpretability of the probabilities and the reliability of the final output. This design makes the model more accurate and effective in handling DDI relationship extraction tasks.

Discussion

In this study, our proposed DCCA-IE model demonstrated significant performance improvements on the DDI 2013 dataset, particularly in terms of accuracy, recall, and F1 score, surpassing many state-of-the-art methods. Furthermore, the 5-fold cross-validation results confirm the robustness of DCCA-IE, showing consistent performance across different data splits and reducing concerns of overfitting.

Firstly, compared to traditional machine learning approaches such as the TEES system developed by Björne et al. [16], which uses SVM and deep syntactic parsing, our DCCA-IE model exhibits superior performance. This is primarily due to the dual-channel mechanism and cross-attention mechanism in our model, which allows for a more comprehensive understanding and extraction of complex information from the text, rather than relying solely on shallow features.

Among deep learning methods, the linear kernel approach by Kim et al. [17] shows strong performance but still faces limitations in handling complex semantic relationships. Quan et al. [22] proposed a multi-channel convolutional neural network (MCCNN) that enhances semantic representation by integrating multiple word embeddings, but it falls short in capturing the global dependencies necessary for accurate DDI extraction. In contrast, our model leverages the combination of BioBERT, CNN, LSTM, and GAT to effectively extract both local sequential features and more complex relational features between drug entities.

Moreover, the hierarchical recurrent neural network (HRNN) model by Zhang et al. [15], which integrates sentence sequences and shortest dependency paths (SDP), improves DDI extraction accuracy but still struggles with long-distance dependencies. Our DCCA-IE model, through the use of the cross-attention mechanism, effectively integrates information from both sequence and graph channels, showing superior performance in handling complex dependency relationships.

Notably, the graph convolutional neural network (GCNN) model by Xiong et al. [30] also attempts to enhance DDI extraction by leveraging graph structures. However, it lacks the comprehensive information integration that our DCCA-IE model achieves. By combining the advantages of graph structures with sequence information through a dual-channel design, our model achieves a better balance in performance.

It is also worth noting that CA-SQBG (Cross-attention Guided Siamese Quantum BiGRU) by Zhang et al. [41] is the only other model that applies cross-attention mechanisms for DDI extraction. While CA-SQBG shows promising results, it mainly focuses on a single channel approach that combines a BiGRU network with cross-attention. However, our DCCA-IE model goes beyond this by integrating dual-channel mechanisms, using BioBERT-CNN for sequential features and BioBERT-LSTM-GAT for relational graph features. This dual-channel design allows us to capture both sequential dependencies and the more intricate relationships between drug entities, which significantly improves DDI extraction performance over CA-SQBG.

In summary, our study demonstrates that the DCCA-IE model outperforms existing models in multiple aspects, particularly in the task of identifying and classifying complex DDIs. This finding further underscores the potential of dual-channel mechanisms and cross-attention mechanisms in improving model performance.

Conclusion

In this study, we propose DCCA-IE, a cutting-edge dual-channel model for drug-drug interaction (DDI) extraction from biomedical texts. By combining BioBERT-CNN for sequential feature extraction and BioBERT-LSTM-GAT for graph-based feature extraction, along with a novel cross-attention mechanism, DCCA-IE delivers a powerful and highly effective solution for DDI extraction. The experimental results show that DCCA-IE outperforms several state-of-the-art models, achieving the highest F1-score of 0.835. This significant improvement underscores the model’s ability to effectively capture complex relationships in biomedical texts and highlights the advantages of combining multiple feature extraction strategies, including both sequential and structural (graph-based) information.

In practical terms, DCCA-IE holds great potential for use in pharmacovigilance, drug repurposing, and clinical decision support systems, where it can play a crucial role in improving drug safety by detecting drug-drug interactions. As seen in recent literature (e.g., [42–45]), advancements in deep learning for biomedical data are paving the way for improved methodologies, and models like DCCA-IE are part of this ongoing development in biomedical research and applications. In future research, we will focus on expanding the model’s generalizability by incorporating diverse biomedical datasets beyond DDI 2013.
