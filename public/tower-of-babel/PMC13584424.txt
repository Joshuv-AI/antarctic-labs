Prelimbic cortex layer 5 GABAergic neurons mediate chronic pain and memory impairment by regulating excitatory/inhibitory imbalance
Authors: Xiaoling Wang, Zhongyuan Lu, Tian Mao, Yitian Yang, Ruilou Zhu, Weiwei Li, Shuang Zeng, Yangyang Wang, Luyao Zhang, Yaowei Xu, Wei Zhang, Longjun Wu, Ji Hu, Mingyang Sun, Jiaqiang Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChronic pain can cause both hyperalgesia and cognitive impairment symptoms and involves long-term alterations in the neural circuits, leading to abnormal cortical activity. GABAergic signaling are particularly emerging as relevant components of pain processing within the prefrontal cortex. The mechanism through which cortical circuits change and cause chronic pain and memory impairment remains unclear to date.MethodsWe modeled chronic neuropathic pain in mice using chronic constrictive injury (CCI), an open field test, a Y maze spontaneous alternation experiment, a novel object recognition test and barnes maze were used to assess neurobehavioral changes in the mice. Whole-cell patch-clamp technique was used to assess the intrinsic activity of neurons, and fiber photometry recording was used to measure the calcium activity of GABAergic neurons. In vivo multi-channel technique was employed to assess alterations in cortical excitability. Western blot and immunofluorescence were used to measure the expression of activating transcription factor 4 (ATF4) and Neuronal excitability.ResultsIn this study, persistent elevation of GABAergic neuronal activity in prelimbic cortex layer 5 (PrL-L5) was demonstrated to regulate the chronic pain and memory impairment. This elevated GABAergic neuronal activity affects the excitatory/inhibitory (E/I) imbalance by influencing the excitability of pyramidal neurons. The inhibition of PrL-L5 GABAergic neuronal activity reversed the hypersensitivity and memory deficits. In contrast, the optogenetic activation of PrL-L5 GABAergic neurons induced hypersensitivity and memory impairment in naive mice. Furthermore, ATF4 regulates hyperpathia and memory impairment through impact GABAergic neuronal activity.ConclusionWe revealed a cortical GABAergic neural microcircuit that involved chronic pain and memory impairment through modulating E/I imbalance by influencing the excitability of pyramidal neurons. These findings provide novel insights for targeted interventions in patients with comorbid chronic pain and memory impairment.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s10194-026-02439-3.Keywords: Chronic pain and memory impairment, GABAergic neurons, Pyramidal neurons, Activating transcription factor 4(ATF4), Prelimbic cortex layer 5(PrL-L5), Excitatory/inhibitory (E/I) imbalance

Introduction

Chronic pain is often accompanied by cognitive impairment, particularly memory impairment [1–3]. However, the neural mechanism underlying this complication remains poorly understood. The brain regions that are the most commonly activated by noxious stimuli, as observed in human brain imaging, are the primary somatosensory cortex (S1), secondary somatosensory cortex (S2), anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), insula, prefrontal cortex (PFC), thalamus, and cerebellum [4–7]. Clinical evidence indicates that chronic pain is associated with structural alterations in medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) region subserving cognitive and emotional regulation [8]. The prelimbic cortex layer 5 (PrL-L5) of the mPFC is involved in pain and cognitive processing and plays a key role in the cognitive comorbidity of chronic pain [9]. GABAergic neurons are crucial in modulating excitatory and inhibitory (E/I) synaptic transmission and pain regulation, thereby contributing to the homeostatic regulation of neuronal circuits [10–12]. However, the role of PrL-L5 GABAergic neurons in the comorbidity of chronic pain and memory impairment remains unclear.

At the cellular level, chronic pain alters the structure and function of cortical pyramidal neurons [6, 13]. In addition to these changes in glutamatergic (CaMKIIα-expressing) pyramidal neurons, alterations in the highly interconnected network of GABAergic (mDLX-expressing) interneurons may lead to persistent pain [14]. Disruptions in cortical E/I balance may underlie the different abnormalities observed in psychotic disorders [15, 16]. E/I imbalance in the cortex is involved in the cognitive comorbidity of chronic pain [17]. Whether these alterations in neural structural networks are synergistic with the chronic pain and memory impairment remains to be elucidated. Unlike other cortical regions, PrL-L5 exhibits reduced excitability in the affective components of the pain sensation [18, 19].The mechanism through which the neuronal circuitry in PrL-L5 is modified and becomes hypoactive during the memory deficits induced by neuropathic pain remains unclear. This study aimed to explore the possible role of GABAergic neurons at PrL-L5 in the chronic pain and memory impairment.

Activating transcription factor 4(ATF4) is a ubiquitously expressed ATF/CREB family transcription factor with a basic leucine zipper domain and a key regulator of hyperalgesia in chronic pain [20, 21]. In addition to its well-characterized role in stress responses, ATF4 regulates synaptic plasticity and modulates learning and memory processes by controlling neuronal excitability [22]. Nevertheless, the involvement of ATF4 in the chronic pain and memory impairment has not been fully elucidated. This study further investigated the impact of ATF4 on chronic pain and memory impairment by modulating the excitability of PrL-L5 GABAergic neurons.

Materials and methods

Animals

In this study, male C57BL/6J mice (6–8 weeks old, each weighing 20–30 g; procured from Beijing SiPeiFu Biotechnology Co. Ltd.) were exclusively used to minimize phenotypic variability. All animals were housed in the Laboratory Animal Center of Zhengzhou University under standard conditions (12:12 h light-dark cycle) with ad libitum access to food and water. GAD2-Cre mice were provided by Song-xue Su (School of Basic Medicine, Zhengzhou University). Conditional knockout of ATF4 in GABAergic neurons was achieved through the stereotaxic delivery of AAV2/9-mDLX-Cre into the target brain region of ATF4Flox+/+ mice (procured from Cyagen Biosciences, Inc.).

Neuropathic pain model

The CCI-induced chronic neuropathic pain mouse model was generated as described in a previous report [23]. Following anesthesia with 1.5–2% isoflurane/oxygen, the left sciatic nerve was bluntly dissected and loosely ligated with three 5–0 catgut wires (Busan Ailee Co. Ltd., South Korea) at 1 mm intervals. In the sham group, an identical procedure was performed without nerve ligation. All the mice were then allowed access to food and water ad libitum.

Stereotaxic surgery, virus injection, and cannula implantation

Following anesthesia with 1.5–2% isoflurane/oxygen, the mice were positioned in a stereotactic frame. A viral vector was microinjected (150 nL per side, 15 nL/min) into the right prelimbic cortex layer 5 (PrL-L5) at the following coordinates relative to bregma: +1.85 mm AP, ± 0.35 mm ML, and − 2.4 mm DV. The microneedle was left in place for 10 min post-injection to facilitate diffusion and then slowly withdrawn before surgical wound closure. In the ATF4 knockout experiment, AAV2/9-mDLX-Cre-EGFP was injected into the PrL-L5 of ATF4Flox+/+ mice. All viruses were purchased from BrainVTA (Wuhan, China) unless otherwise stated. The mDLX viral strategy relies on a conserved mDLX enhancer derived from the DLX1/2 locus, which enables robust and specific transgene expression predominantly in telencephalic GABAergic inhibitory neurons [24]. This AAV-based targeting approach avoids expression in glutamatergic neurons and allows specific labeling, optical manipulation, and functional interrogation of GABAergic neuronal subpopulations in wild-type rodents. To allow microinjection, a guide cannula (Inper Ltd., Hangzhou, China) was targeted to PrL-L5. Following surgery, the mice were kept on a homeothermic heating pad until they regained consciousness and were subsequently monitored daily.

Chemogenetic and optogenetic manipulation

For the chemogenetics experiments, AAV2/9-mDLX-hM4D(Gi)-mCherry was injected into the PrL-L5 of C57BL/6J mice. The effectiveness of this viral vector in targeting has been verified [25]. After three weeks, the virus was fully expressed, and vehicle or CNO (1.5 mg/kg in 1% DMSO; MCE, China; HY-17366), an hM4D(Gi) agonist, was intraperitoneally injected 30 min before the behavioral test. All the mice were allowed to rest for 24 h between the tests. For the optogenetics experiments, AAV2/9-DIO-ChR2/NHRP3.0-mCherry was injected into the PrL-L5 of GAD2-Cre mice (Sham/CCI). The optical fibers (Inper, 788S4) were connected with a stimulator (Inper) used to generate blue (473 nm, 4 mW, 10 ms pulse, 20 Hz) or yellow (539 nm, 4 mW, constant) lights.

Fiber photometry recording and analysis

AAV2/9-DIO-GCaMP6s was injected into the PrL-L5 of GAD2-Cre mice, and fiber photometry experiments were conducted, in which GABAergic neuron calcium dynamics in PrL-L5 during the PWT and Y maze were recorded after cannula implantation. Fiber photometry system (Inper Ltd., Hangzhou, China) was used to acquire signals at 410 nm (background, calcium-independent) and 470 nm (GCaMP6s, calcium-dependent). To confirm successful viral expression and correct fiber targeting, all the mice were perfused for histology after the recording session. During the calcium signal recording, the mice received von Frey filament stimulation, and each mouse was subjected to five repeated trials. The recorded data were segmented according to the behavioral events observed in each trial. The percentage fluorescence change (ΔF/F) was calculated as (F - F0)/F0, where F0 was defined as the mean fluorescence during the 1-s interval preceding stimulus presentation or exploration of a novel arm. All photometry data were processed using InperPlot software (Inper Ltd., China).

In vivo multi-channel recording and analysis

For in vivo multi-channel recording, an 8-microwire electrode array (4 × 2 configuration; 25 μm diameter; Kedou, Suzhou, China) was implanted to target PrL-L5 (coordinates from bregma: +1.85 mm AP, − 0.35 mm ML, − 2.4 mm DV). Two skull screws connected to stainless steel wires served as references and ground. Post-surgery, mice were singly housed for a 7-day recovery, followed by a 3-day habituation to the recording headstage (10 min/day) in the test environment. Recordings were conducted either in a copper-mesh-lined square box (for freely moving tests) or on an insulated plastic net wrapped with copper mesh (for stimulation tests). Neural signals were acquired in vivo with a 32-channel digital signal processor (Plexon, USA), followed by amplification and band-pass filtering (300–5000 Hz). Upon experimental completion, the mice were perfused, and the brains were collected for histological verification of the electrode tracks. Spike sorting was conducted offline via an offline sorting system (Plexon, USA) and NeuroExplorer (Nex Technologies, USA). Single-unit spike sorting was performed with Offline Spike Sorter software (Plexon Inc.). Spikes were identified when a minimum waveform reached an amplitude threshold of 3 standard deviations higher than the noise amplitude. Principal component analysis (PCA) was employed to automatically separate waveforms into individual clusters. Manual checking was then performed to ensure that the spike waveforms were consistent and that the cluster boundaries were clearly separated. All isolated single units exhibited recognizable refractory periods (> 2 ms) in the inter-spike interval (ISI) histograms. Only well-isolated units (L ratio < 0.2, isolation distance > 15) were included in the data analysis. Well-isolated single units were classified via an unsupervised κ-means clustering algorithm [25]. Three principal components: the half-spike width, the half-valley width, and the mean firing rate. Neuronal responses were quantified by comparing the average Z score from the 1-second pre-stimulus baseline to the 2-second window following von Frey stimulation or during rest.

Electrophysiology

For the whole-cell patch clamp experiments, AAV2/9-DIO-mCherry was injected into the PrL of GAD2-Cre mice, and brain slices were prepared as described in previous report [26]. After deep anesthesia (isoflurane) was applied and the mice were sacrificed, the brain was quickly retrieved into ice-cold artificial cerebrospinal fluid (ACSF) bubbled with 5% CO2/95% O2 continuously. The ACSF consisted of the following (in mM): 125 NaCl, 2.5 KCl, 1.25 NaH2PO4.2H20, 25 NaHCO3, 2 CaCl22H2O, 1MgCl2.6H2O, 12.5 D-glucose. Coronal slices with a thickness of 300 μm were obtained using a vibrating microtome (Leica, Germany, VT1200s). The slices were incubated in a recovery chamber containing ACSF (in mM): 125 NaCl, 2.5 KCl, 1.25 NaH2PO4, 26 NaHCO3, 1 CaCl2, 1 MgCl2, 20 D-glucose, and 1 sodium pyruvate (bubbled with 95% O2, 5% CO2) at 32 °C for 30 min. The slices were then moved to room temperature. Whole-cell patch-clamp recordings were performed in PrL-L5 neurons. These neurons were visualized using a 40x water-immersion lens (Nikon, Japan, Ni-U) and an infrared camera connected to the video monitor. The pipettes (input resistance: 4–7 MΩ) were pulled using a horizontal pipette (Sutter Instrument, USA, P-97) and filled with a solution containing (in mM) the following: 125 potassium D-gluconate, 8 NaCl, 0.2 EGTA, 10 HEPES, 2 Mg-ATP, and 0.3 Na-GTP. The data were acquired using an Axon patch 700B amplifier (Molecular Devices, Sunnyvale, CA, USA), low-pass filtered at 2 kHz, and digitally sampled at 10 kHz. Neurons with series resistances of 15–30 MΩ and input resistances of over 100 MΩ were collected, and the data were analyzed using Clampfit software (Molecular Devices, Sunnyvale, CA, USA). The resting membrane potential (RMP) was recorded under a current clamp (I = 0 pA). Recording the resting membrane potential necessitates a stable baseline measurement. The action potential (AP) rheobase was measured by applying a series of current pulses (400 ms duration, − 10 to 140 pA intensity with an increment of 10 pA). The AP rheobase was defined as the current application that elicited the first action potential. To ensure the reliability of AP-related indicators, it is essential to exclude cells exhibiting unstable recordings, elevated leakage currents, or substandard waveform quality. The spike width was defined as half the height between the threshold and peak action potentials. To measure miniature excitatory postsynaptic currents (mEPSC), bicuculline (BIC, MCE, China, HY-N0219, 10 µM, to block GABA receptors) and tetrodotoxin (TTX, MCE, China, HY-12526, 1 µM, to block sodium current) were added to the bath solution. The holding potential was clamped at -70 mV. The miniature inhibitory postsynaptic current (mIPSC) was recorded in high Cl– solution (in mM): 110 CsCl, 10 NaCl, 5 MgCl2, 0.6 EGTA, 40 HEPES, 2 Mg2ATP, 5 QX314, and 0.2 Na3GTP, and the pH was adjusted to 7.2 using CsOH. TTX (1 µM) was added to block the sodium current to block voltage-gated sodium channels and eliminate action potentials in presynaptic neurons. NBQX (Abmole, N10046, USA, 10 µM) and AP5 (MCE, China, HY-100714 A, 50 µM) were then added to block glutamatergic excitatory synaptic transmission. By administering BIC (10 µM), we aimed to determine its efficacy in completely inhibiting all spontaneous synaptic currents, thereby confirming that the recorded events were mIPSC. The holding potential was clamped at -70 mV. We record mEPSC and mIPSC separately in two different sets of neurons under identical experimental conditions, and compare the normalized frequency and amplitude of mEPSC and mIPSC obtained from the two parallel neuron populations. The relative ratio of excitatory to inhibitory synaptic transmission was calculated to reflect the overall E/I balance in the neuronal network. These data were analyzed using the Clampfit Analysis Program (USA, Version 10.7.0.3).

Paw withdrawal threshold (PWT)

The paw withdrawal threshold was detected by assessing 50% withdrawal thresholds to evaluate mechanical sensitivity using a set of von Frey filaments (0.02–4 g, Stoelting, Wood Dale, IL, United States), following an up-down method, as previously described [27]. PWT was assessed one day prior to CCI and then at 7, 14, and 21 days after CCI. The mice were placed in cages with wire mesh floors to permit full access to the plantar surface. A series of von Frey filaments were applied to the hind paws in ascending order of force, with sufficient inter-stimulus intervals to allow the behavioral response to resolve. A positive pain response was defined as a sharp paw withdrawal, licking, or flinching.

Paw withdrawal latency (PWL)

Heat hypersensitivity was assessed based on PWL using a plantar analgesia tester (7370, Ugo Basile, Comerio, Italy) [28]. Briefly, the plantar surface of the hind paw was exposed to a radiant heat source positioned beneath a glass floor. PWL was measured three times per session for each hind paw, with tests alternating between sides at > 5 min intervals. The mean of the three measurements for each paw was calculated as the final result, with all assessments conducted by evaluators blinded to the experimental groups.

Open field test (OFT)

The OFT was employed to assess general locomotor activity. Each mouse was placed in the center of a square arena (40 × 40 × 40 cm) and allowed to explore freely for 5 min. Their movement was tracked to quantify the total distance traveled within the whole zone.

Y maze test

Spontaneous alternation behavior in the Y maze, indicative of the innate propensity to explore novel environments, was evaluated. The test was conducted 21 days post-surgery. The maze featured three enclosed arms (28 × 6 × 8 cm) angled at 120°. Each mouse was placed in the central area and allowed 8 min of free exploration. An experimenter blinded to the group assignments recorded the sequence of arm entries. Spontaneous alternation was defined as consecutive entries into all three arms without repetition (e.g., ABC, BCA, CBA). The alternation percentage was calculated as follows: (times of alternations/ total times of arm entries-2) ×100%.

Novel object recognition (NOR) test

The NOR test was conducted in the same open-field apparatus. The test was conducted 22–23 days post-surgery to assess working memory. The procedure consisted of three phases. First, the mice were habituated to the empty arena for 10 min. After 24 h, they were exposed to two identical objects (Object A and Object B) for a 10 min familiarization session. Following another 24 h interval, memory retention was tested by allowing the mice to explore for 5–10 min in the presence of one familiar Object A and one novel Object C. Object exploration, defined as direct sniffing or touching with the nose, was automatically recorded and quantified using Smart 3.0 video tracking software (Panlab, Harvard Apparatus, US). The discrimination index was calculated as follows: (time spent with the novel object - time spent with the familiar object)/ (total exploration time) ×100%.

Barnes maze test

The Barnes maze, a standard paradigm for assessing spatial memory [29], consists of a raised, white circular platform (75 cm diameter) with 18 equidistant holes (5 cm diameter). One hole is connected to a hidden escape box, while the others remain open. This experiment began 24 days post-surgery to assess spatial memory. During the 4-day acquisition phase, the mice underwent two trials daily, each beginning with a 15-second confinement in a central start chamber. Upon release, the animals were encouraged to locate the target hole under bright illumination within a 2.5 min cutoff. The mice that failed were gently guided to the escape box and left for 30 s to facilitate memory consolidation. A 15 min intertrial interval was enforced, and all surfaces were cleaned with 75% ethanol between trials. Spatial memory retention was evaluated in 2.5 min probe tests (with the escape box blocked) conducted 1 d and 7 d post-training. All the sessions were video tracked for analysis of latency and path efficiency to the target.

Western blotting

PrL-L5 tissues were immediately collected on ice and homogenized in ice-cold RIPA buffer (Solarbio Technologies Inc., Beijing, China) supplemented with protease inhibitors. The lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, and the resulting supernatants were subjected to protein concentration determination using a BCA assay. Protein samples were then separated by 8% SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes at 200 mA for 1.5 h. Subsequently, the membranes were blocked with 3% non-fat milk for 1 h at room temperature and incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: ATF4 (1:1000, Proteintech, 60035-1-lg), and GAPDH (1:2000, Abcam, ab9485). Following three washes, the membranes were incubated with an HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (1:5000, Abcam, ab6721) for 2 h at room temperature. Signal detection was performed using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) kit (Fluorchem, USA), and signals were visualized with a Bio-Rad imaging system. Band intensities were quantified densitometrically using ImageJ software (NIH, USA) and used the individual GAPDH intensity to normalize the target protein level for each corresponding lane. The control group was set to 100%, with experimental values expressed relative to this baseline. All experiments were independently repeated at least three times.

Quantitative reverse transcriptase polymerase chain reaction

Total RNA was extracted using the RNA simple Total RNA Kit (TIANGEN, DP419) and reverse-transcribed into cDNA with the Revert Aid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Quantitative real-time PCR was performed on a CFX96 system (Eppendorf, Germany) using Maxima SYBR Green/ROX qPCR Master Mix (Thermo Scientific, USA). Gene-specific primers for ATF4 (F: 5’-CTTAAGCCATGGCGCTTCTC-3’, R: 5’-GAAGGCATCCTCCTTGCTGTT-3’) were designed with Primer Blast software (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Relative mRNA expression levels were calculated using the ΔΔCt method and normalized to a reference gene.

Immunofluorescence

The mice were perfused with 4% paraformaldehyde, and their brains were subsequently dehydrated overnight in 30% sucrose. The brain tissues were then sectioned into 20 μm slices, and after another PBS wash, the slices were incubated overnight (or for 48 h at 4 °C) with antibodies against NeuN (Proteintech, 26975-1-AP, 1:200), GFAP (Proteintech,16825-1-AP, 1:400), IBA1(Proteintech, 10904-1-AP, 1:400), ATF4(Proteintech, 10835-1-AP, 1:200), or c-Fos (Abcam, ab302667, 1:400). After further washes with PBS, the slices were incubated with fluorescent-labeled secondary antibodies (Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-mouse IgG or Alexa Fluor 546 goat anti-rabbit IgG) for 2 h at room temperature. After another PBS wash, DAPI was added for 10 min at room temperature, and the slices were washed again with PBS. Finally, the slices were mounted on slides and covered with cover slips. The slides with samples were finally examined under a fluorescence microscope (Olympus BX53, Tokyo, Japan) or a high-resolution laser confocal fluorescence microscope (Nikon A1R MP+, Japan), and images were captured using a CCD spot camera.

Golgi staining

The Golgi-Cox staining procedure was meticulously executed using the FD Rapid Golgi Stain Kit (FD Neurotechnologies Inc., Columbia, Maryland). Initially, the brain tissues extracted from the mice were thoroughly washed with Milli-Q water to remove any residual contaminants. These brain tissue samples were subsequently immersed in a mixture of solutions A and B, maintained at room temperature, and shielded from light for a period of 14 days to ensure optimal impregnation. To maintain the integrity of the staining process, the mixture was replaced after the first 24 h of impregnation. Afterward, the brains were carefully transferred to solution C for an additional 3 days, and the solution was also replaced after the initial 24 h. Once the impregnation process was complete, the tissues were sectioned into 120 μm thick sections via a high-precision vibrating microtome (VT1000; Leica Microsystems). The sections were then stained in strict accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol to achieve consistent and reliable results. The pyramidal cells located in the PrL-L5 region were imaged using a state-of-the-art confocal microscope (Olympus Fluoview FV1000) at both 20× and 100× magnification under an oil-immersion objective. This high-resolution imaging allowed for detailed visualization of the cellular structures. To further examine neuronal morphology, the tracing of neurons and the quantification of total dendritic length and the number of dendritic spines were carried out using Neuron J software with the Neuron J plug-in (NIH, Bethesda, MD). Additionally, dendritic complexity was assessed through Sholl analysis, which involved counting the number of dendritic intersections within each 30-mm concentric radial interval emanating from the cell body.

In situ hybridization

In situ hybridization was performed as previously described via a commercial GAD1 detection kit (Boster, MK3963-M) [30]. Briefly, mouse brain tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight, cryoprotected in 20% and 30% sucrose, embedded in OCT, and sectioned at a thickness of 20 µm. The sections were subsequently fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde/PBS for 10 min at room temperature. After 3 h of prehybridization at 38–42°C, the sections were hybridized with the probe solution overnight at the same temperature. The following day, the sections were sequentially incubated with a sealing solution, a biotinylated anti-digoxin antibody, and a SABC-FITC complex. Finally, the slides were mounted with an anti-fade agent and visualized under a Nikon A1R MP+ laser confocal microscope. The GAD1-specific probe sequences used were as follows: 5’-CTTCG CCTGC AACCT CCTCG AACGC GGGAG CGGAT CCTAA-3’, 5’-CTACC AACCT GCGCC CTACA ACGTA TGATA CTTGG TGTGG-3’, and 5’-CCCAT GGATG CACCA GAAAA CTGGG CCTGA AGATC TGTGG-3’.

Statistical analysis

The normally distributed data were expressed as means ± SDs and analyzed with GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad, San Diego, CA, United States). The behavior data were analyzed statistically using two-tailed, unpaired Student’s t tests (two groups), one-way ANOVA (> 2 groups), or two-way ANOVA. Biochemical and electrophysiological data were analyzed by two-tailed unpaired Student’s t tests. Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess the normal distribution of all experimental data, for data that did not pass the normality test, non-parametric statistical analyses were applied accordingly. For the comparison of data before and after light exposure in the optogenetic experiment, we used a paired Student’s t test. Holm-Sidak’s post hoc method was used for pairwise comparisons of the means when the analysis of variance revealed significant differences. For all cumulative probability distribution graphs of mEPSC and mIPSC events, the Kolmogorov–Smirnov (K–S) test was used for statistical comparison between groups. The legend accompanying each result figure describes the detailed analytical process employed in the corresponding experiment. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Chronic constrict injury (CCI) induces chronic pain and memory impairment

First, a chronic neuropathic pain model was developed using the CCI to investigate the chronic pain and memory impairment (Fig. 1A and B). Compared with the sham group, the CCI group presented significantly lower PWT and PWL at post-surgery days 7, 14, and 21 (Fig. 1C and D). Chronic pain was defined based on established criteria, with behavioral assessments conducted on day 21 after CCI surgery [31]. The OFT results revealed no significant differences in total movement distance, confirming that surgery did not impair motor function (Fig. 1E). The Y maze results demonstrated that the CCI group presented significantly lower spontaneous alternation rates than the sham group (Fig. 1F and G), but there was no significant change in the times of arm entries, indicating preserved exploratory motivation (Fig. 1H). In the NOR test, the exploration time of the CCI mice toward the novel object did not significantly differ from that of the old object (Fig. 1I and J), and the discrimination index of the CCI mice was reduced compared with that of the sham group (Fig. 1K). Furthermore, the Barnes maze revealed significantly prolonged escape latencies in the CCI group during both types of acquisition training, and the time to enter the target hole was prolonged during memory retention tests on days 1 and 7 after training (Fig. 1L and O). These behavioral impairments indicate that chronic pain concurrently induces sustained hypersensitivity and spatial memory deficits.

Chronic pain and memory impairment caused PrL-L5 pyramidal neuron hypoactivity and reduced dendritic spine

Since c-Fos expression is a well-established marker of neuronal activation in the central nervous system under neuropathic pain conditions [32], c-Fos immunofluorescence staining was conducted to assess the activity of L5 neurons in CCI mice. The number of cells expressing c-Fos in PrL-L5 ipsilateral to or contralateral to the surgical site was significantly lower in the CCI mice than in the sham mice (Fig. S1). The co-expression of c-Fos with neuronal (NeuN) markers but not with astrocytic (GFAP) or microglial (IBA1) markers was subsequently observed (Fig. S2A-S2C). Whole-cell patch-clamp recordings of pyramidal neurons revealed that the AP rheobase was significantly increased in the PrL-L5 of CCI mice compared to that of Sham mice (Fig. 2A and B), but there was no change in the AP half-width (Fig. 2C) or the resting membrane potential (Fig. 2D). Consistent with our hypothesis, depolarizing current injections (+ 100 and + 140 pA) evoked a robustly decreased firing frequency in PrL-L5 pyramidal neurons from CCI mice compared to sham controls (Fig. 2E and G). The frequency of mIPSC was significantly greater in the contralateral PrL-L5 pyramidal neurons of the CCI mice than in those of the Sham mice (Fig. 2H and K), but there was no change in amplitude (Fig. 2L and M), indicating an increase in inhibitory synaptic transmission received by PrL-L5 pyramidal neurons. These data suggest that CCI mice exhibit reduced excitability in PrL-L5 pyramidal neurons, accompanied by an increased frequency mIPSC.

Furthermore, to evaluate structural plasticity in the context of chronic pain and impaired memory, Golgi staining and quantitative morphological analysis of PrL-L5 neurons were performed. Sholl analysis revealed comparable dendritic complexity in the groups (Fig. 2N and P). Compared with those in the sham group, both the dendritic shafts and branches in the CCI group presented decreased spine density (Fig. 2Q and R). These findings suggest that the chronic pain and memory impairment is accompanied by structural and functional damage to pyramidal neurons in the PrL-L5 region of mice.

PrL-L5 GABAergic neurons mediate the chronic pain and memory impairment

To further understand the mechanisms underlying the hypoactivity of PrL-L5 pyramidal neurons, the activity of local inhibitory interneurons during the chronic pain and memory impairment was assessed. Integrated immunostaining and in situ hybridization analyses revealed significantly increased c-Fos immunoreactivity, which preferentially colocalized with GAD1 mRNA-expressing neurons in the CCI mice compared with the sham group (Fig. 3A and C). The whole-cell patch clamp results revealed that the AP rheobase was significantly lower in PrL-L5 GABAergic neurons from CCI mice than in those from the Sham group (Fig. 3D and F), but there was no significant change in the AP half-width or resting membrane potential (Fig. 3G and H). Depolarizing current injections (+ 100 and + 140 pA) evoked a robustly increased firing frequency in PrL-L5 GABAergic neurons from CCI mice compared to sham controls (Fig. 3I and J). These findings indicate increased excitability of PrL-L5 GABAergic neurons.

To further elucidate the role of PrL-L5 GABAergic neurons in the chronic pain and memory impairment, fiber photometry combined with behavioral tests was used to measure spontaneous calcium activity quantitatively. A representative image of an optic fiber inserted into PrL-L5 of GAD2-cre mice is shown in Fig. 3K. The population calcium responses in these neurons before (baseline), during, and after 0.6 g von Frey stimulation were quantified, and the calcium signals in the CCI mice were significantly greater than those in the Sham mice (Fig. 3L). Both groups showed significant calcium responses to 2 g Von Frey stimulation, with the CCI mice demonstrating more pronounced activation (Fig. 3M). Furthermore, PrL-L5 GABAergic neurons from CCI mice presented significantly greater calcium transients than those from the sham group did in the Y maze test (Fig. 3N). These findings suggest that PrL-L5 GABAergic neurons have a heightened calcium influx to painful stimuli after CCI.

Optogenetic manipulations of PrL-L5 GABAergic neurons modulate chronic pain and memory impairment

To investigate the role of PrL-L5 GABAergic neurons in the chronic pain and memory impairment, AAV2/9-DIO-NpHR3.0-EGFP was administered to the PrL-L5 of GAD2-Cre mice after CCI surgery (Fig. 4A and B). L5 GABAergic neurons were labeled with EGFP (Fig. 4B). The PWT and PWL were significantly greater under yellow light (589 nm) than under pre-light (Fig. 4C). The OFT results indicated that there was no significant difference in the total movement distance during the pre-light or light-on phase (Fig. 4D), suggesting that light stimulation does not impair the motor function of the mice. Compared with the pre-light phase, the Y maze test revealed a significant increase in spontaneous alternation behavior but no significant difference in the total times of arm entries (Fig. 4E). In the NOR test, the discrimination index was significantly greater in the light-on phase than in the pre-light phase (Fig. 4F). Our data indicate that inhibition of PrL-L5 GABAergic neurons improves chronic pain and cognitive deficits under chronic pain conditions.

Next, the influence of L5 GABAergic neurons on hyperalgesia and memory in naive mice were investigated by injecting AAV2/9-DIO-ChR2-mCherry into the PrL-L5 of naive mice (Fig. 4G and H). Numerous mCherry+ neurons were observed in PrL-L5 (Fig. 4H). The activation of GABAergic neurons decreased the PWT and PWL (Fig. 4I). There was no significant difference in the movement distance of the mice in the OFT (Fig. 4J). Compared with that in the pre-light phase, spontaneous alternation (%) in the Y maze test decreased significantly during the light-on phase but had no influence on the times of arm entries (Fig. 4K). The discrimination index decreased significantly in the NOR test during the light-on phase compared with the pre-light phase (Fig. 4L). These findings suggest that activating the neuronal activity of L5 GABAergic neurons can induce hyperalgesia and memory impairment in naive mice.

PrL-L5 GABAergic neurons regulate the E/I balance of pyramidal neurons through enhanced inhibitory synapses

To observe the E/I ratio changes in PrL-L5 pyramidal neurons, we recorded mEPSC from pyramidal neurons and calculated the E/I ratio of the pyramidal neurons using the recorded ratio of mEPSC to mIPSC. The frequency of mEPSC was significantly lower in the contralateral PrL-L5 pyramidal neurons of the CCI mice than in those of the Sham mice (Fig. 5A and D), but there was no change in amplitude (Fig. 5E and F). We further analyzed the relative E/I amplitude ratio across groups. Although the high-chloride internal solution used for IPSC recording may artificially enhance apparent IPSC amplitude and limit the interpretation of absolute physiological E/I balance, we still compared the relative changes in E/I amplitude ratio among experimental groups. The results showed that the relative E/I amplitude ratio was significantly altered in the CCI group compared with the control group (Fig. 5G and H), reflecting an overall shift in the relative magnitude of excitatory and inhibitory synaptic inputs under this recording condition. To evaluate the synaptic actions of GABAergic neurons on pyramidal neurons within cortical circuits, we recorded the mEPSC from PrL-L5 GABAergic neurons. The analysis revealed an increased frequency of mEPSC in PrL-L5 GABAergic neurons in the CCI group compared with the sham group (Fig. 5I and L), but no change in amplitude (Fig. 5M and N). These findings suggest that increased excitability in PrL-L5 GABAergic neurons in the CCI mice may lead to an E/I imbalance through enhanced inhibitory synapses onto pyramidal neurons.

We then explored the important function of GABAergic neurons in PrL-L5 E/I imbalance using a chemogenetic approach involving designer receptors exclusively activated by designer drugs (DREADDs), in conjunction with multi-channel recordings (Fig. S3A). As expected, immunofluorescence analysis revealed extensive mCherry expression in PrL-L5 neurons 21 days post-microinjection (Fig. S3B). Importantly, chemogenetic inhibition with CNO significantly dampened the activity of GABAergic neurons relative to that of the vehicle-treated controls (Fig. S3C). Consistent with these findings, CNO administration significantly reversed hyperalgesia (Fig. S3D). Furthermore, CNO treatment significantly reduced spontaneous alternation rates in the Y maze test but did not affect total times of arm entries (Fig. S3E), suggesting specific effects on spatial memory rather than general locomotor activity. These results suggest that PrL-L5 GABAergic neurons may mediate the chronic pain and memory impairment.

Furthermore, we used chemogenetic techniques and in vivo multi-channel recording to investigate the effects of GABAergic neuronal inhibition of PrL-L5 on pyramidal neuron excitability (Fig. 5O). The chemogenetic virus exhibited robust expression within PrL-L5 (Fig. 5P). Scatter plots revealed distinct separation between clusters, with high-density clustering within each group (Fig. 5Q). Following administration of either Veh or CNO, recordings of PrL-L5 pyramidal neuron discharges demonstrated a significant increase in discharge frequency following CNO administration compared to Veh administration (Fig. 5R and U). These results demonstrated that PrL-L5 GABAergic neurons mediated both hypersensitivity and memory impairment by impacting the E/I imbalance.

ATF4 regulates the chronic pain and memory impairment by affecting the excitability of GABAergic neurons at PrL-L5

To gain deeper insight into the molecular mechanisms through which chronic pain activates PrL-L5 GABAergic neurons to induce memory impairment, the literature was reviewed, and it was revealed that ATF4 has either inhibitory or stimulatory effects on synaptic plasticity [22, 33]. The preliminary data generated by our research revealed no corresponding change in the ATF4 mRNA in PrL-L5 following CCI, whereas there was a significant upregulation of ATF4 protein expression (Fig. 6A and B). Subsequently, immunofluorescence staining was performed to characterize ATF4 expression in PrL-L5 neurons. ATF4 exhibited predominant neuronal localization, with dual cytoplasmic and nuclear distributions (Fig. S4A), whereas lower expression was detected in astrocytes (Fig. S4B) and microglia (Fig. S4C). Furthermore, immunofluorescence staining revealed no significant difference in the levels of ATF4 protein expression in CaMKIIα-positive pyramidal neurons in the two groups of mice. (Fig. S5). However, immunofluorescence combined with in situ hybridization demonstrated the specific upregulation of ATF4 in GABAergic neurons (Fig. 6C). These findings indicate that ATF4 possesses a physiological basis for regulating GABAergic neuronal activity.

To investigate the functional role of ATF4 in the chronic pain and memory impairment, AAV2/9-mDLX-Cre-EGFP (or AAV2/9-EGFP as a control) was delivered into the PrL-L5 of ATF4Flox+/+ mice during CCI surgery. Viral expression was confirmed through fluorescence microscopy images (Fig. 6D), and immunoblotting demonstrated efficient ATF4 knockdown in PrL-L5 neurons (Fig. 6E). Interestingly, knocking out ATF4 in PrL-L5 GABA neurons increased the PWT and PWL in CCI mice (Fig. 6F). The OFT results revealed no statistically significant differences in total movement distance (Fig. 6G). Compared with those in the AAV2/9-EGFP group, the spontaneous alternation rates of the PrL-L5 GABA neurons in the ATF4 knockout group were significantly greater (Fig. 6H), but there was no significant change in the times of arm entries, indicating preserved exploratory motivation (Fig. 6I). In the NOR test, the exploration time toward the novel object and the discrimination index were improved by ATF4 knockout (Fig. 6J and K). Furthermore, the Barnes maze test revealed significantly shorter escape latencies during acquisition training as well as in the memory retention test in the context of ATF4 knockout (Fig. 6L and M). These findings demonstrated that ATF4 in PrL-L5 GABAergic neurons specifically mediated chronic pain-related memory deficits but did not affect nociceptive sensitization. In addition, fiber photometry recordings revealed significantly lower calcium activity in GABAergic neurons from CCI+mDLX-Cre mice than in those from CCI+EGFP mice during the Y maze (Fig. S6A and S6B). These data indicate that ATF4 modulates the comorbidity of neuropathic pain and memory impairment by regulating the excitability of PrL-L5 GABAergic neurons.

Discussion

Clinical studies have consistently reported that patients with chronic pain frequently exhibit memory impairment [34, 35]. Growing evidence suggests that chronic pain is associated with cognitive deficits, including impaired concentration, memory recall, executive function, and information processing [36, 37]. Previous research by our group also corroborates this association, indicating a potential link between chronic pain and memory dysfunction [38, 39]. Importantly, emerging research indicates that memory impairment represents a ‘circuit-level’ pathology involving the dysregulation of specific neural networks [15]. Combining electrophysiological and fiber photometry recordings with behavioral tests revealed that PrL-L5 GABAergic neurons influence the chronic pain and memory impairment by regulating the activity of pyramidal neurons and thereby affecting the E/I imbalance. In addition, conditional knockout of ATF4 in genetically modified mice was used to determine its significance in regulating the comorbidity of neuropathic pain and memory impairment in GABAergic neurons. These findings suggest that hyperactivity of the GABAergic microcircuit in PrL-L5 plays a role in the comorbidity of neuropathic pain and memory impairment through reducing the activity of pyramidal neurons, indicating that manipulating the activity of GABAergic interneurons may be an effective strategy for treating chronic pain-related memory impairment.

The PrL is one of the cytoarchitectonic parts of the medial prefrontal cortex and is critical for complex behaviors, such as attention, memory, and behavioral flexibility [40, 41]. Memory impairment related to sleep deprivation is associated with a reduction in the PrL, the total number of neurons, the dendrite length, and the number of spines in each dendrite [42]. Multimodal circuit interrogation has revealed pathological hyperactivity in GABAergic neurons during chronic pain.

Central to cortical function is the dynamic equilibrium between glutamatergic excitation and GABAergic inhibition [43]. Despite their relative scarcity, GABAergic interneurons exert disproportionate control over neural circuit operations by governing pyramidal neuron activity [43, 44]. Fiber photometry revealed that calcium signaling in GABAergic neurons significantly increased in the context of chronic pain and memory impairment and was significantly associated with pain behavior and spatial memory processes. Inhibition of the excitability of GABAergic neurons can increase the excitability of pyramidal neurons and significantly improve hyperalgesia and memory impairment. These results suggest that increased calcium activity in GABAergic neurons plays a key role in the chronic pain and memory impairment by enhancing inhibitory synaptic input to pyramidal neurons.

Emerging evidence implicates disturbances in the E/I balance as a core pathophysiological feature shared across psychotic disorders [45, 46]. Our data revealed an elevated rheobase and an increased frequency of mIPSC in PrL-L5 pyramidal neurons. This preliminary finding suggests that enhanced regulation of the PrL-L5 inhibitory circuit results in excessive constraint of neuronal network excitability in a chronic pain and memory impairment comorbidity model. Furthermore, analysis of whole-cell patch-clamp and multi-channel recordings revealed increased excitability in GABAergic neurons, accompanied by a greater frequency of mEPSC. These findings suggest that GABA is released more frequently from GABAergic neurons, which potently regulate pyramidal neurons and consequently lead to a PrL-L5 E/I imbalance. Notably, mIPSC recordings in the present study were performed with a high-chloride internal solution, which shifts the chloride equilibrium potential and artificially increases mIPSC amplitude [47]. For this reason, the calculated E/I amplitude ratio cannot be simply regarded as the authentic in vivo physiological excitatory/inhibitory balance. Thus, we interpret these data only as relative intergroup changes rather than absolute physiological values. Despite this methodological limitation, the relative alteration of E/I amplitude ratio still provides supportive evidence for synaptic input remodeling in mPFC GABAergic neurons following peripheral nerve injury and chronic stress. Moreover, excessive inhibition of pyramidal neurons weakens the descending inhibitory control exerted by the prefrontal cortex over the dorsal horn of the spinal cord. This impairs cognitive function and leads to enhanced transmission of nociceptive signals at the spinal level. Ultimately, it promotes chronic pain and hyperalgesia [48–50]. These results position PrL-L5 as a potential therapeutic target for both hyperalgesia and memory deficits induced by chronic pain.

Research has demonstrated that ATF4 modulates the transcriptional expression of voltage-gated potassium channels and proteins related to calcium homeostasis [51], thereby directly impacting the AP threshold, resting membrane potential, and spontaneous discharge properties of pyramidal neurons. Moreover, the enhanced translation expression of ATF4 can substantially inhibit the activity of the CREB-dependent signaling pathway in CA1 neurons. This inhibition subsequently results in impairments in late-phase long-term potentiation (LTP) and the memory consolidation functions of the hippocampus [52]. In our study, elevated ATF4 levels in PrL-L5 GABAergic neurons were revealed as a molecular driver of chronic pain and memory deficits. A genetic reduction in ATF4 specifically in these neurons rescued memory impairment by normalizing synaptic inhibition and neuronal excitability, thereby establishing ATF4 as a critical regulator of the chronic pain and memory impairment. Diverging from the established peripheral nervous system patterns in which ATF4 is restricted to the cytoplasm of DRG neurons [53], this study revealed robust nuclear ATF4 expression in central neurons, demonstrating the fundamental difference in ATF4 biology between the peripheral and central nervous systems. This cell type-specific and region-specific expression pattern likely reflects the presence of distinct transcriptional regulatory mechanisms, and elucidating these molecular determinants is essential for fully understanding the pleiotropic roles of ATF4 in neural function and dysfunction. In contrast to the well-characterized role of ATF4 in supporting hippocampus-dependent memory processes [53], a pathological mechanism in which ATF4 drives memory deficits in chronic pain states was revealed in this study. The divergent outcomes likely reflect cell type-specific ATF4 functions, while acute hippocampal ATF4 knockdown impairs glutamatergic plasticity, and chronic reduction in GABAergic neurons may trigger distinct compensatory circuits that paradoxically preserve some functions while disrupting others. This study revealed preferential ATF4 upregulation in GABAergic neurons, with mechanistic studies establishing GABAergic ATF4 as a key driver of the chronic pain and memory impairment. The characteristic fast-spiking physiology (non-adapting firing, < 0.5 ms APs) of these interneurons may explain their heightened susceptibility to stress-induced dysfunction [54]. Following the conditional knockout of ATF4 in PrL-L5 GABAergic neurons, our immunoblotting results suggest a knockout rate of around 30%. This is consistent with the proportion of GABAergic neurons found in the cortex. Moreover, research has demonstrated that brain injury can result in elevated ATF4 expression [55, 56]. This experimental model also exhibits pronounced brain injury and neurological dysfunction, aligning with findings from previous studies. Furthermore, the lack of notable alterations in ATF4 mRNA levels, coupled with a significant increase in protein expression, may be attributed to epigenetic mechanisms [57].

Notably, the individual data demonstrated a clear bimodal distribution, with certain animals exhibiting enhanced behavioral performance while others showed a decline, despite the absence of statistically significant differences at the group level. This divergent pattern may be attributed to intrinsic individual variability or subtle experimenter-related procedural differences during behavioral testing [58]. Further controlled experiments with larger sample sizes will help clarify the underlying sources of this bimodal tendency.

Naturally, our research has certain limitations. First, in the optogenetic suppression experiment, the NpHR test was exclusively performed on CCI mice, without the inclusion of Sham controls or verification of baseline effects. Secondly, this study exclusively examines the alterations in expression and the functional roles of ATF4 within the context of chronic pain pathology. However, due to constraints inherent in the current experimental design, this investigation has not yet undertaken experiments to knock out ATF4 in GABAergic neurons, nor has it observed the resultant behavioral phenotypes in both normal control mice and those subjected to Sham operations. Thirdly, we did not set up a mCherry-only control group with CNO injection, making it difficult to exclude off-target effects induced by CNO metabolites. CNO can be reverse-metabolized into clozapine, which itself produces analgesic effects [59]. Thus, we are unable to separate the contributions of chemogenetic suppression of PrL-L5 GABAergic neurons and clozapine-mediated pain relief to the improved mechanical hyperalgesia, creating ambiguity for our behavioral conclusions. Furthermore, as distinct interneuron subtypes perform different roles within cortical microcircuits, subsequent studies will investigate the contributions of various inhibitory interneuron types to the co-occurrence of chronic pain and memory impairment.

Conclusions

Taken together, the above findings provide compelling evidence that PrL-L5 GABAergic-pyramidal neuron microcircuits can influence the chronic pain and memory impairment by affecting the E/I imbalance. Given the high conservation of the PrL in the neural mechanisms of rodents and humans, the novel results obtained in this study may improve the current understanding of the mechanisms of chronic pain and memory impairment.
