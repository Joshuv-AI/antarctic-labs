Early neonatal mortality and its associated factors at Doctor Bogalech Gebre Memorial General Hospital, Central Ethiopia: A cross-sectional study
Authors: Desalegn Berhanu Katiso, Tariku Laelago Ersado, Mesfin Menza Jaldo, Belayneh Hamdela Jena
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Early neonatal mortality remains one of the major public health challenges in Ethiopia. Therefore, this study was aimed at determining the magnitude of early neonatal mortality and its associated factors at Doctor Bogalech Gebre Memorial General Hospital, Central Ethiopia. A hospital-based cross-sectional study was conducted from September 11, 2021, to September 11, 2024, among 400 neonates aged up to 7 days. Data were extracted from medical records and the neonatal registration book using a checklist adapted from the World Health Organization and other literature. Multivariable logistic regression analyses were done to identify factors associated with early neonatal mortality. The magnitude of early neonatal mortality was 16.5%, 95% CI: 10.6, 22.3%. Early onset neonatal sepsis [AOR = 5.21, 95%CI: 2.29, 11.82], respiratory distress syndrome [AOR = 4.40, 95%CI: 2.04, 9.50], perinatal asphyxia [AOR = 10.06, 95%CI: 3.95, 25.60], preterm birth [AOR = 3.42, 95%CI: 1.34, 8.73], low Appearance, Pulse, Grimace, Activity, and Respiration (APGAR) score at 5 min [AOR = 3.76, 95%CI: 1.65, 8.60], continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) [AOR = 0.31, 95%CI: 0.14, 0.68], and phototherapy [AOR = 0.10, 95%CI: 0.02, 0.41] were factors associated with early neonatal mortality. The high magnitude of early neonatal mortality emphasizes the need to strengthen neonatal intensive care, skilled birth attendance, infection prevention, and early management of complications.

Introduction

Early neonatal mortality remains a significant public health problem, occurring within the first seven days after birth, with most deaths during this period largely resulting from prematurity, intrapartum complications, neonatal infections, and congenital anomalies1–3. The risk of death is highest on the first day of life, making the early neonatal period the most critical phase in a newborn’s survival4. This period represents a time of extra-uterine adaptation, during which neonates are particularly vulnerable to life-threatening conditions5. Early neonatal mortality accounts for approximately three-quarters of all neonatal mortality, and its prevention requires focused attention on causes specific to the first week of life, particularly birth asphyxia and prematurity6.

Globally, more than five million neonatal deaths occur annually, and preventing these avoidable deaths remains a major priority in global public health6. In 2022, the global neonatal mortality rate was estimated at 17 deaths per 1,000 live births, with approximately 6,500 early neonatal deaths occurring each day, making the neonatal period the time of highest risk for child survival6,7. Over 95% of early neonatal deaths occur in low- and middle-income countries, with the greatest burden observed in South Asia and Sub-Saharan Africa6. Regionally, neonatal mortality was highest in Sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia, with the neonatal mortality rate estimated at 26 and 22 deaths per 1,000 live births, respectively, in 20237. In Ethiopia, despite the implementation of various interventions, neonatal mortality has remained high and has shown a stable trend over time, reaching 33 deaths per 1,000 live births8. Nevertheless, evidence from a comprehensive study and meta-analysis indicates that early neonatal mortality in Ethiopia has been declining, although the proportion of deaths occurring in the early neonatal period remains substantial at 29.5 per 1000 live births9.

The causes of early neonatal mortality differ markedly across regions of the world, largely reflecting levels of socioeconomic development and health system capacity. In rural areas of Africa, infections and birth asphyxia are the leading causes of early neonatal mortality, whereas in high-income countries, prematurity, congenital anomalies, and sudden unexpected neonatal deaths predominate10. Furthermore, previous studies identified key determinants of early neonatal mortality, including younger neonatal age, prematurity, low birth weight, perinatal asphyxia, mode of delivery, hypothermia, delayed initiation of breastfeeding, and inadequate antenatal care attendance10–12. However, most existing studies have focused broadly on neonatal mortality or specifically on perinatal mortality, while large-scale studies that specifically assess the magnitude of early neonatal mortality remain limited.

Reducing neonatal mortality is a key focus of the third Sustainable Development Goal (SDG-3), which aims to reduce neonatal mortality to 12 deaths per 1,000 live births by 203013. Ethiopia must reduce the current rate (33 per 1000 live births) by half to meet the target. Ethiopia shared the Every Newborn Action Plan (ENAP) that seeks to reduce early neonatal mortality to fewer than 10 deaths per 1,000 live births, with an ultimate target of 5 per 1,000 live births14. Hence, clear and context-specific evidence on the magnitude of early neonatal mortality is critically important for prioritizing local initiatives, strengthening evidence-based advocacy, and implementing effective interventions aimed at reducing early neonatal mortality through informed local decision-making. Therefore, this study aimed at determining the magnitude of early neonatal mortality and its associated factors at Doctor Bogalech Gebre Memorial General Hospital in the Kambata Zone, Central Ethiopia.

Methods

Study design and setting

A hospital-based cross-sectional study was conducted at Doctor Bogalech Gebre Memorial General Hospital, which is found in Durame town, Kembata Zone, Central Ethiopia region. Durame town is located 334 km from the capital city of Ethiopia, Addis Ababa. The hospital is serving a total population of over 1.5 million people from the Kembata Zone and Mierab Badewacho of the Hadiya Zone.

The hospital is currently running the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) that provides services for the neonates. The unit is divided into a septic room, kangaroo mother care (KMC) room, and critical and sub critical rooms, which have 12 neonatal beds and 2 KMC beds. The unit also has four incubators and two radiant warmers. Additionally, there are four infusers, six oxygen cylinders, pulse oximetry, a glucometer, and neonatal resuscitation equipment. The unit is staffed with three pediatricians, three neonatal nurses, and 6 clinical nurses. The unit receives high-risk newborns from the labor and delivery ward and receives neonates referred from other health facilities and homes. Almost all high-risk neonates are admitted to the neonatal intensive care unit and evaluated by the NICU team.

Study duration

In this study, data of NICU admissions from September 11, 2021, to September 11, 2024, were considered. The data extraction period was from August 1 to September 30, 2024. For this study, data of NICU admissions from September 11, 2021, to September 11, 2024, were considered.

Population

All neonates aged less than seven days who were NICU admissions at Doctor Bogalech Gebre Memorial General Hospital were the target population. The study population was those randomly selected neonates aged less than seven days and included in the study. All neonates whose medical record contained incomplete data for relevant variables were excluded.

Sample size

The sample size was calculated by using a double population proportion formula by considering multiple births as a significantly associated factor in which the proportion of early neonatal mortality among neonates delivered as multiple births was 33% and those delivered as a singleton birth was 13.2% from the study conducted in Arbaminch hospital15, and a 95% confidence level, 80% power, and 10% possible incomplete records/non-response rate were considered. The sample size becomes 420.

Sampling procedure

A simple random sampling technique was used to select study participants. The total number of early neonates from September 11, 2021, to September 11, 2024, was 2400. By using the registration book of NICU admissions, the sampling frame was prepared for all early neonates using their serial numbers. Due to logistical and resource constraints, including the personnel costs and time required for retrospective chart retrieval, it was not feasible to review all 2,400 admission records. Therefore, out of 2400 total admissions, a representative sample of 420 early neonates were selected using a computer-generated random sampling technique. Medical records with full information were used, and if there was a missing chart, the next in the sequence was applied (Fig. 1).

Study variables

In this study, the dependent variable is early neonatal mortality, which is a binary categorical variable measured as ‘yes’ if it has occurred and ‘no’ if it did not occur. The independent variables considered are socio-demographic factors (such as age of mother, residence, age, and sex of neonate); reproductive characteristics (such as antenatal care follow-up, place of birth, parity, mode of delivery, type of pregnancy, presentation at delivery, and complications during pregnancy, labor, and delivery); newborn health characteristics (such as birth weight, birth asphyxia, APGAR score at 5 minutes, neonatal age at admission, gestational age at birth, the weight of the neonate, date of NICU admission and discharge, APGAR score at 1 and 5 minutes, respiratory distress syndrome (RDS), perinatal asphyxia, jaundice, hypothermia, and neonatal sepsis); and health care (such as partograph use, kangaroo mother care, and radiant warmer).

Operational and term definitions

Early neonatal mortality

is the death of a newborn occurring during the first seven days of life.

Neonatal mortality

the death of a newborn occurring within the first 28 days of life.

APGAR score

is a method used to assess a newborn’s condition at 1 and 5 min after birth. A score of less than 7 is considered abnormal, while a score of 7 or higher is considered normal.

Gestational age

is a measure of a pregnancy in weeks from the beginning of the woman’s last menstrual period (LMP). Infants born before 37 weeks are considered preterm, and those born at or after 37 weeks are considered term.

Respiratory distress

a breathing disorder in newborns like fast breathing, grunting, blue color flaring (widening) of nostrils with each breath and chest retractions. Normally, the newborn’s respiratory rate is 30–60 breaths per minute.

Data collection tools and procedures

Retrospective data were extracted from medical records, the NICU admissions registration logbook, and maternal records of newborns, using a structured data collection checklist. Reports from the medical records/charts were used to ascertain the survival status of the neonates (died or alive) at the time of discharge from the hospital. This checklist was adapted from the WHO document on the review and audit of neonatal death, as well as the national registration book of admitted neonates16. The checklist/data extraction tool was developed in the English language. Two BSc nurses collected the data and were supervised by the investigators. The supervisors had checked the collected data for completeness, accuracy, and consistency throughout the data collection period. The overall supervision was done by the principal investigator. His respiratory rate is 30–60 breaths per minute.

Data quality control measures

To assure the quality of data, a properly designed data collection tool was used. In addition, day-to-day activities during data collection were supervised and evaluated. Errors were corrected by the investigators before the following day’s activity. Moreover, to improve the quality of data, health professionals (BSc nurses) were involved in the data collection. Prior to the actual data collection, one day of training for data collectors was given, and the checklist was pretested on 5% of the sample size at Halaba General Hospital among the NICU admissions. After the pre-testing, difficult and vague questions were revised. Moreover, multivariable logistic regression analysis was done to control for confounding effects.

Data processing and analysis

The collected data was checked for completeness, coded, and entered by using EpiData version 4.6 and then exported to the SPSS version 25 statistical package for analysis. Data exploration was done before actual analysis, and cleaning was performed to check for accuracy and for any missing values. Descriptive statistics such as frequency and percentages were done to estimate the magnitude of early neonatal mortality and other variables.

A simple binary logistic regression analysis was performed to select candidate variables using a p < 0.25. Accordingly, candidate variables were included in the multivariable logistic regression analysis to control for potential confounding. In the final model, variables with a 95% CI that did not include 1 and a p < 0.05 were reported as statistically significant factors associated with early neonatal mortality. The strength of the association was reported using an adjusted odds ratio along with its 95% CI. The model’s goodness of fit was tested by the Hosmer and Lemeshow test, where a p-value greater than 0.05 indicates a good fit. The variance inflation factor (VIF) was used to check for the possible multicollinearity among the independent variables, using a cutoff of 10, where VIF less than 10 indicates no evidence of multicollinearity. Finally, the results were described and presented using texts, tables, and charts.

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics

A total of 420 early neonates were included from the NICU admissions of the hospital. A total early neonatal record was initially reviewed with a data completion rate of 95.2% (400 records). Excluding 20 incomplete records, this analysis focused on the remaining 400 records. The median age of mothers was 26 years with an interquartile range of 8 years. Most of the mothers, 310 (77.5%), were in the age range of 21–34 years. The rest, 55 (13.8%), were aged less than 20 years, and 35 (8.5%) belong to the age group of greater than or equal to 35 years. Two hundred eighteen (54.5%) were rural and 182 (45.5%) were urban in their residence.

Maternal reproductive/obstetric characteristics

About half of the mothers, 210 (52.5%) were multiparous. Among the mothers, 366 (91.5%) had ANC visit. Of them, 146 (39.9%) having ≥ 4 antenatal visits. The majority, 357 (89.2%) of the mothers had a singleton pregnancies. Out of the 400 delivered early neonates 267 (66.8%) of them were delivered through spontaneous vaginal delivery, 116 (29%) delivered via cesarean section, and 17 (4.2%) delivered by instrumental/assisted delivery (Table 1).

Neonatal characteristics

The mean weight of neonates was 2579.3 g (SD: ±756.4 g). More than half, 216 (54%), of the neonates weighed ≥ 2500 gm at admission. Most, 305 (76.3%), of the neonates had APGAR scores ≥ 7 at 5 min. Around 168 (42%) of the neonates had hypothermia at birth, 299 (74.8%) of the neonates cried immediately at birth, and a total of 289 (72.3%) of the neonates were admitted within 24 h of delivery. Around 91 (22.8%) of the neonates had jaundice at admission, and the widely held 165 (45.8%) were diagnosed with sepsis. Most of the neonates, 293 (73.3%) stayed at the hospital for more than 24 h (Table 2).

Magnitude of early neonatal mortality

The overall magnitude of early neonatal mortality was 66 (16.5%, 95% CI: 10.6, 22.3%). Among these cases, 29 (43.9%) of early neonates died within 24 h of birth, whereas 37 (56.1%) died after 24 h of birth. Regarding admission problems, early neonatal sepsis, respiratory distress syndrome, and perinatal asphyxia were identified as the top three leading causes of early neonatal mortality.

Factors associated with early neonatal mortality

In the bivariate logistic regression analysis, several factors showed significance at p < 0.25, including type of pregnancy (number of gestations), respiratory distress syndrome, APGAR score at 1 min, APGAR score at 5 min, pregnancy-related complication, perinatal asphyxia, early onset of sepsis, preterm birth, jaundice at admission, pre-eclampsia, eclampsia, place of birth, continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP), newborn weight at admission, newborn heated with a radiant warmer, and neonate receiving phototherapy. Predictor variables that were significant in bivariate analysis at p < 0.25 were considered for the multivariable analysis. In the multivariable analysis, early neonatal mortality was significantly associated with early-onset neonatal sepsis, respiratory distress syndrome, perinatal asphyxia, preterm birth, APGAR score at 5 min, CPAP, and neonatal phototherapy at p < 0.05. The Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test indicated adequate model fit (p = 0.68). Also, the variance inflation factor (VIF) for the fitted model (maximum VIF = 1.06) indicated that there is no evidence of multicollinearity.

Multivariable analysis indicated that newborns with early-onset neonatal sepsis had fivefold [AOR = 5.21, 95% CI: 2.29, 11.82] higher odds of early neonatal mortality as compared to those who had no early-onset neonatal sepsis. The odds of early neonatal mortality was 4 times [AOR = 4.40, 95% CI: 2.04, 9.50] higher among neonates diagnosed with respiratory distress syndrome compared to those without RDS. Early neonatal mortality was 10 times [AOR = 10.06, 95% CI: 3.95, 25.60] higher among neonates who had perinatal asphyxia as compared to those without perinatal asphyxia. Neonates who were born before 37 weeks of gestation (preterm births) had three times [AOR = 3.42, 95% CI: 1.34, 8.73] higher odds of early neonatal mortality as compared to those who were born at or after 37 weeks of gestation. Neonates with an APGAR score less than seven at 5 min had nearly 4 times [AOR = 3.76, 95% CI: 1.65, 8.60] higher odds of early neonatal mortality as compared to those who had a greater than or equal to APGAR score of seven at 5 min. Neonates who received CPAP had 69% [AOR = 0.31, 95% CI: 0.14, 0.68] lower odds of early neonatal mortality as compared to neonates who had not received CPAP. Neonates who received phototherapy had 90% [AOR = 0.10, 95% Cl: 0.02, 0.41] lower odds of early neonatal mortality as compared to neonates who had not received phototherapy (Table 3).

Discussion

This study aimed at determining the magnitude of early neonatal mortality and its associated factors among neonates admitted to the NICU at Doctor Bogalech Gebre Memorial General Hospital. Accordingly, the magnitude of early neonatal mortality was 16.5%, 95% CI: 10.6, 22.3%. Factors significantly associated with early neonatal mortality were early-onset neonatal sepsis, respiratory distress syndrome, perinatal asphyxia, preterm birth, and APGAR score less than seven at 5 min. In contrast, receiving CPAP and neonatal phototherapy were protective factors associated with lower odds of early neonatal mortality.

The magnitude of early neonatal mortality identified in this study (16.5%) reveals that mortality during the first week of life remains substantially high in the hospital, underscoring the need for neonatal survival interventions that focus on the intrapartum period as well as the immediate and early neonatal phases. This finding aligns with reports of the pooled estimate of Sub-Saharan Africa (11%)17, the pooled estimate of Africa (14%)18, public hospitals in Bahirdar city, Amhara region, Ethiopia (15.3%)3, and Hiwot Fana Specialized Hospital, eastern Ethiopia (10.6%)19. However, the result is higher than the facility-based study in the Tigray Region, northern Ethiopia (4.3%)20, the retrospective follow-up study in Nekemte Referral Hospital, Oromia Region, Ethiopia (8%)21, and the Indian study (3.6%)22. The variations in the estimates might be attributed to disparities in relation to access to and utilization of health services; differences in health facility delivery rates; care-seeking behavior for sick neonates; study settings; and geographic factors might also contribute to these discrepancies5. Furthermore, this study included only neonates admitted to the neonatal intensive care unit, where critically ill newborns are more likely to be managed, which might have increased the observed mortality rate. Evidence shows that the risk of neonatal death is highest immediately after birth and declines over subsequent days and weeks21. Approximately 36% of neonatal deaths occur within the first 24 h, and nearly 73% occur within the first week of life21.

This study revealed that neonates diagnosed with early-onset neonatal sepsis had nearly five times higher odds of experiencing early neonatal mortality compared with those without early-onset sepsis. This finding is consistent with evidence from studies conducted in Hiwot Fana Specialized Hospital, eastern Ethiopia19, and India22. The elevated risk of mortality associated with neonatal sepsis might be explained by its serious complications, such as abscess formation, venous thrombosis, and neurological impairment. In addition, the high magnitude of neonatal infections in developing countries has been linked to factors including low socioeconomic status, low birth weight, prolonged labor, and prolonged rupture of membranes23,24.

Early neonates born preterm had approximately three times higher odds of death compared with those born at term. This finding is consistent with existing evidence demonstrating an independent association between preterm birth and early neonatal mortality17,18. Preterm birth contributes to neonatal mortality mainly because vital organs—including the lungs, brain, heart, and kidneys—are not fully developed and are therefore unable to function adequately. This immaturity predisposes preterm neonates to serious complications such as respiratory distress syndrome, sepsis, and intracranial hemorrhage25.

Early neonates diagnosed with respiratory distress syndrome (RDS) had approximately four times higher odds of early death compared with those without respiratory distress. This finding is in agreement with previous studies conducted in different places3,19,22. The result suggests that delayed recognition of clinical signs and failure to promptly address the underlying cause of respiratory distress can result in severe short- and long-term complications, including chronic lung disease and respiratory failure, ultimately leading to death26.

The odds of death among early neonates who developed perinatal asphyxia were ten times higher than the odds of early neonatal mortality among those who did not develop perinatal asphyxia. Different studies have shown that perinatal asphyxia is related to intrapartum- and postnatal-related factors, which increase the odds of early neonatal mortality3,17,18,22. Perinatal asphyxia, resulting from inadequate oxygenation or impaired blood flow around the time of birth, contributes significantly to early neonatal mortality by triggering multi-organ dysfunction27.

The odds of death among early neonates diagnosed with an APGAR score less than seven at 5 min had nearly four times higher odds of early neonatal mortality as compared to those who had greater than or equal to an APGAR score of seven at 5 min. This might be due to the fact that neonates who had an APGAR score less than seven at 5 min were severely asphyxiated and also may be associated with complications of labor and pregnancy-induced complications. The finding replicates the result of other study conducted in Ethiopia20.

In this study, the odds of early neonatal mortality among neonates who had received CPAP was 69% lower as compared to those who had not received CPAP. This finding is in agreement with a study conducted in Malawi28. However, further studies are needed before inferring effects. CPAP is applied to maintain or increase the functional residual capacity of the lungs, prevent alveolar collapse, reduce the work of breathing, and enhance gas exchange in newborns. Subsequently, this reduces the risk of mortality among newborns29.

In this study, the odds of early neonatal mortality among neonates who had received phototherapy was 90% lower as compared to those who had not received phototherapy. This protective effect aligns with findings from previous studies indicating that timely phototherapy markedly improves survival among neonates with hyperbilirubinemia30. However, this study suffers from a relatively small number of cells or sparse data. As a result, the observed association seems overestimated. Hence, the interpretation needs taking this issue into consideration.

The finding of this study highlights the critical need for early detection, continuous monitoring, and prompt treatment of jaundice, particularly in low-resource settings where delays in intervention may result in preventable deaths. Furthermore, these findings stress the importance of reinforcing hospital protocols to ensure the timely initiation of phototherapy and providing healthcare workers with adequate training to identify and manage neonatal jaundice. Incorporating such measures into standard neonatal care can significantly enhance survival rates during the vulnerable first week of life.

This study is not free from limitations. First, due to the nature of the record review, some medical records were missed. Replacing missing charts with the next available record may have compromised the random sampling process and introduced selection bias. Consequently, the magnitude of early neonatal mortality may not be generalizable to the entire population. Second, the unavailability of information on potential factors, such as maternal characteristics, made it difficult to adjust for potential confounders, which might have affected the estimates. Third, because of the cross-sectional study design, a temporal relationship between the factors and the outcome could not be ascertained. Finally, further studies are recommended to verify the associations of certain variables, such as the effects of CPAP and phototherapy in reducing early neonatal mortality. Despite these limitations, the findings of this study provide additional input for decision-making in hospital settings.

Conclusions

Early neonatal mortality remains high, highlighting a need to strengthen neonatal intensive care, infection prevention, skilled birth attendance, and early management of complications such as sepsis, respiratory distress syndrome, perinatal asphyxia, preterm birth, and low APGAR scores. Provision of continuous positive airway pressure and neonatal phototherapy were found to be associated with lower odds of early neonatal mortality in the hospital setting. Strengthening the quality of intrapartum and immediate newborn care, ensuring prompt and effective neonatal resuscitation, and improving the capacity of neonatal intensive care units are critical. Furthermore, priority should be given to increasing access to and proper utilization of evidence-based interventions such as CPAP and neonatal phototherapy, along with ongoing training of healthcare providers and strict adherence to standardized neonatal care guidelines to reduce early neonatal mortality.
