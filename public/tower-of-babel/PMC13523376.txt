Effectiveness of a home-based exercise program on functional outcomes in women with asthma: A randomized controlled trial
Authors: Ayse Sena Manzak Dursun, Semiramis Ozyilmaz, Pinar Atagun Guney, Aysegul Yabaci Tak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundExercise training is a crucial non-pharmacological approach in asthma management, yet asthmatic patients are not routinely referred to these programs. Recognizing that significant benefits can be achieved even with highly accessible, standardized exercise programs, the aim of our study was to investigate the effectiveness of a standardized, low-cost, and easily implementable home-based exercise program, supported by weekly supervision, in women with asthma.MethodsThirty female patients with asthma were randomized into training and control groups. Patients in the training group participated in supervised exercise sessions with a physiotherapist once a week for 8 weeks and performed the prescribed exercise program at home 3 days per week. The control group received general physical activity recommendations via an educational booklet. Primary outcomes were respiratory function assessed using spirometry and functional capacity assessed using the 6-minute walk test (6MWT). Secondary outcomes included asthma control assessed using the Asthma Control Test, dyspnea assessed using the modified Medical Research Council dyspnea scale (mMRC), health-related quality of life assessed using the St. George’s Respiratory Questionnaire (SGRQ), and lower extremity muscle strength assessed using the 30-second sit-to-stand test (30-s STST).ResultsThe training group showed statistically significant improvements in peak expiratory flow parameter of spirometry (PEF%), 6MWT, activity subscore of SGRQ, and 30-s STST values compared with the control group (p < 0.05).ConclusionsThis study demonstrates that a standardized, accessible, and sustainable home-based exercise program, supplemented by minimal supervision, was associated with significantly greater improvements compared to general physical activity recommendations in enhancing key outcomes in asthma management. This approach offers a valuable, low-cost alternative for reducing sedentary behavior and improving clinical outcomes, particularly in settings where access to complex or technologically advanced rehabilitation centers is limited. These findings may be particularly relevant for women with asthma, who often face greater barriers to physical activity and experience a higher symptom burden.Clinical TrialThis trial registration was prospectively registered at ClinicalTrials.gov, ID number: NCT04088669.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12890-026-04437-2.

Introduction

Asthma is a chronic airway disease in which expiratory airflow is limited, characterized by respiratory symptoms, inflammation, and hyperresponsiveness of the airway [1]. In addition to symptoms such as wheezing, chest tightness, and cough, asthmatic patients may also experience deterioration in secondary parameters such as dyspnea, quality of life, asthma control, and functional capacity [2, 3]. This is primarily due to dysfunctional breathing patterns and dynamic hyperventilation mechanisms, beyond the bronchoconstriction seen in the airways [4, 5]. In addition to these secondary parameters, other physiological parameters, such as peripheral muscle function, may also be affected, although the available evidence remains limited [3, 6].

The primary management of asthma relies on pharmacological interventions. Despite optimal medication use, the potential deterioration of these secondary parameters underscores the importance of non-pharmacological approaches in asthma management [7]. Current guidelines highlight that non-pharmacological interventions—such as physical activity recommendations, aerobic exercise training, breathing exercises, and yoga—can contribute to asthma management [8]. With accumulating evidence, adopting a multidimensional approach is increasingly recognized as a critical component of effective asthma control.

Even with significant advances in asthma management, patients are not routinely referred to exercise training programs by healthcare professionals, and their participation in physical activity remains limited [9, 10]. The primary barrier to referral is the exercise-induced nature of asthma. Nevertheless, evidence indicates that asthmatics can safely engage in exercise programs when appropriate precautions—such as correct inhaler use, warm-up routines, and environmental modifications—are implemented [8]. The lack of routine referral often promotes a sedentary lifestyle, which worsens symptoms and increases the burden of treatment, further complicating asthma management [11, 12]. Additionally, women with asthma are reported to have lower levels of physical activity, greater barriers to exercise participation, and a higher symptom burden compared to men, which further supports the need for targeted interventions in this population [13, 14].

Exercise programs differ in structure, allowing for adaptation to various settings. Home-based exercise training programs have gained increasing attention, largely due to their capacity to facilitate behavioral change, cost-effectiveness, and greater accessibility [15, 16].

In the literature, there is evidence that exercise programs individually tailored using advanced technological equipment provide benefits to patients with asthma both in clinical settings and at home [2, 17, 18]. Building on this evidence, our study was designed based on the premise that a standardized, simple, and easily implementable exercise program could also produce meaningful clinical benefits. We hypothesized that a standardized home-based exercise program supported by weekly supervision would lead to greater improvements across key clinical outcomes, including asthma control, dyspnea, pulmonary function, functional capacity, health-related quality of life, and lower extremity muscle strength compared to standard physical activity recommendations in women with asthma.

Methods

Design

The design of this study was a prospective, randomized controlled, and single-blind. The physiotherapist supervising the exercise sessions was not blinded to group allocation due to the nature of the intervention. However, outcome assessments were conducted by an independent assessor blinded to group assignment. Ethics committee approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Bezmialem Vakıf University (12/246–2019). This study was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov with ID number NCT04088669. The study was conducted at Bahcelievler Public Hospital in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Participants were recruited from the pulmonology outpatient clinic.

All patients were informed about the purpose and procedures of the study prior to participation. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Participants were selected based on the following inclusion criteria: being female, having a diagnosis of asthma, being between the ages of 18–65. The exclusion criteria were as follows; having an asthma exacerbation in the last 1 month, hospital admission or hospitalization due to respiratory problems in the last 1 month, having an orthopedic, neurological, or mental disease that prevents participation in exercise; engaging in regular exercises at least 3 days a week with an exercise program. Thirty patients who met the inclusion criteria were included in the study after evaluation by a pulmonologist. Participants were randomly allocated into two groups using a computer-generated randomization sequence (www.random.org) by a researcher not involved in participant recruitment or outcome assessment. Group allocation was implemented after completion of baseline assessments.

Control of asthma, dyspnea, respiratory function, functional capacity, health-related quality of life, and lower extremity muscle strength were evaluated at the beginning and end of the study. All randomized participants completed the study and were included in the final analysis; therefore, no missing data were observed.

Measurement

Control of asthma was evaluated by Asthma Control Test. Based on the questionnaire results, a total score of 25 is considered full control, 20–24 is partial control, and < 19 is uncontrolled [19].

The modified Medical Research Council dyspnea scale (mMRC) can be used to measure patient’s dyspnea related to physical activity. Progression of the grades indicates the presence of dyspnea at a lower level of physical activity [20].

“COSMED Pony-FX” (COSMED-Italy) spirometry device was used to evaluate pulmonary functions, and measurements were performed in accordance with ATS/ERS criteria [21]. Forced vital capacity (FVC), forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1), Tiffeneau ratio (FEV1/FVC), and peak expiratory flow (PEF%), forced mid-expiratory flow (FEF 25-75) values were measured and recorded as percentages of expected values with the pulmonary function test [22].

Functional capacity was determined by 6MWT according to the criteria of the ATS [23]. The distance walked by the patients was recorded in meters.

Quality of life was assessed with the Turkish version of St. George Respiratory Questionnaire (SGRQ). The SGRQ is a self-answered questionnaire consisting of three categories, evaluating symptoms, activity, and the effects of disease on daily life [24].

30-s STST, which is generally used for the evaluation of physical performance and lower extremity muscle strength in the elderly, has also been tried for younger age groups and it has been reported that it can be used for the evaluation of functional capacity [25]. The number of completed sit-to-stand repetitions during the 30-s STST was recorded [26].

Interventions

Before the 8-week exercise training process, a one-time education session was undertaken for both groups. During this educational session, patients were informed about asthma-related topics such as what asthma is, comorbidities, exacerbations, proper inhaler use techniques, triggers, and environmental factors. Additionally, counseling was provided on the importance of physical activity, the relationship between physical activity and asthma, safety precautions, types of physical activity, and recommendations for physical activity for daily living. Educational brochures were also provided.

In addition, the training group participated in 8 weeks of home-based exercise training and continued individual, supervised exercise sessions with a physiotherapist once a week. They continued to do the prescribed exercise program at home for 3 days per week. The home-based, standard exercise training program consisted of 10-minute warm-up period with stretching exercises for large muscle groups, also strengthening exercises for the upper and lower extremities, and breathing exercises. Resistance training was performed at home with equipment that was easily accessible in the home setting, such as sit to stand with chair and dumbbells for upper extremity weights. All exercises are shown in Supplement I with their details and number of repetitions.

All stretching and strengthening exercises were performed as 2 sets of 10 repetitions, and breathing exercises were performed as 1 set of 10 repetitions once a day. Each strengthening exercise was performed during 5 s of contraction. The exercises given at the beginning became more difficult as the lists progressed. In order to progress the exercises, the contraction time was increased (10 s, 15 s) and the weight of the dumbbells used in the exercises were increased (0,25 kg, 0.5 kg, 1 kg). Exercise intensity was also monitored during supervised sessions using the Modified Borg scales for fatigue and dyspnea, targeting a moderate intensity level (scores 4–6) [27].

Additionally, patients were instructed to maintain a daily step count exceeding 5,000 steps, considered a minimal activity threshold [28]. They recorded their daily step counts using a pedometer and documented the days on which they performed exercises in their diaries. Diaries were used to monitor adherence in both groups for daily step counts, while exercise adherence was specifically tracked in the training group.

Statistical analysis and sample size

Statistical analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS v.26 (SPSS Inc.). The normality of the distribution of data was analyzed using Shapiro–Wilk Test. Categorical variables were compared between groups using χ2 test. Independent Samples T-test and Mann-Whitney U test were used for comparisons of baseline values depending on the distribution properties of the data. A repeated measure analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for the analysis of intragroup changes. A mixed ANOVA (repeated-measures analysis of variance with between-subjects factor) was used to analyze whether the effects of interventions differed between the groups. The results were considered significant with p values < 0.05. Complementarily, the effect size was calculated by partial eta-squared (ηp2) with small, medium, and large effect sizes classified as 0.01, 0.06, and 0.14, respectively [29].

The sample size was calculated using G*Power 3.1 (Universitaet Düsseldorf, Germany) [30]. The calculation was based on an effect size approach informed by the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) in 6-minute walk distance (6MWD) in patients with asthma, as well as the variability reported in previous exercise-based studies. Based on these data, the effect size was estimated to be 1.38. With 95% power, a two-tailed alpha level of 0.05, and an allocation ratio of 1:1, the required sample size was calculated as 15 participants per group. Accordingly, 15 participants were included in each group [31, 32].

Results

Forty patients with asthma were assessed for eligibility. Ten patients did not meet the study’s inclusion criteria. Five of these patients had additional diseases, and five were outside the age range. Thirty patients who met the inclusion criteria were randomized into two groups. All participants completed the study without dropout (Fig. 1).

Discussion

In this prospective, randomized, controlled, single-blinded study evaluating the effects of a home-based exercise training program on multiple clinical outcomes in patients with asthma, we found that a weekly supervised home-based exercise program was associated with greater improvements in PEF%, 6MWD, SGRQ-activity score, and 30-s STST repetitions compared to physical activity recommendations. Notably, all of these improvements were achieved using a standardized, simple exercise protocol, suggesting that low-cost and easily accessible exercise programs can provide meaningful benefits in this population. Given that our study specifically focused on women, it is important to interpret these findings within the context of sex-specific differences in asthma.

Women with asthma represent a clinically distinct and potentially more vulnerable subgroup due to a combination of biological and behavioral factors. Genetic predisposition, sex hormones, and anatomical differences, along with gender-specific lifestyle factors such as occupational exposures, smoking patterns, physical activity habits, and dietary behaviors, may contribute to a more unfavorable disease trajectory in women compared to men [33–35]. As a result of these multifactorial influences, women with asthma tend to experience greater symptom severity, poorer asthma control, reduced quality of life, and increased risk of exacerbations, along with involvement of both large and small airways [36, 37]. This disparity is further exacerbated by lower levels of regular physical activity among women and the presence of multiple barriers to exercise participation. Women are more likely to report limitations in physical functioning, social participation, sleep quality, and daily activities related to asthma, whereas men generally demonstrate higher engagement in physical activity and lower rates of obesity [13, 38].

Considering these factors, the development of simple, accessible, and home-based exercise interventions is particularly important for women with asthma. Such approaches may help overcome existing barriers to participation and provide clinically meaningful improvements in outcomes where women are disproportionately affected. In this context, the findings of the present study support the potential role of low-cost, scalable, home-based exercise strategies as a targeted approach to improve asthma-related outcomes in this population.

One of the primary goals of asthma treatment approaches is to achieve good asthma control by reducing associated symptoms. The Global Initiative for Asthma (GINA) 2024 report the relative beneficial effects of exercise training and regular physical activity on asthma symptoms [8]. The literature reveals that studies using different types of exercise, such as aerobic exercise, breathing exercises, and respiratory muscle training, applied at varying intensities, report positive improvements in asthma control [39–41]. Similar to the results of these studies, our study also found statistically significant improvements in asthma control in both training and control groups. This suggests that exercise training and promotion of regular physical activity have a positive effect on symptom perception and asthma control in patients with asthma. When the groups were examined in more detail, given that a score below 19 on the Asthma Control Test reflects uncontrolled asthma, the patients in our study had low baseline values. Only 14.28% of the patients in the treatment group entered the partially controlled asthma range before treatment (with an Asthma Control Test score between 20 and 24), while this rate increased to 50% after treatment. In the control group, this rate, which was 6.25% at the beginning, increased to 25% after the treatment. Despite all these improvements, home-based exercise training supported by supervised sessions did not appear to provide any additional benefit to physical activity recommendations in asthma control.

The statistically significant improvement in the mMRC score, indicating the severity of patient-reported dyspnea across various activity levels, was observed only in the training group. Despite this significant intragroup improvement, the magnitude of the change was insufficient to establish a statistically significant difference when compared between the groups. The exercise training program, implemented using minimal equipment, suggests potential benefits for asthmatic patients that extend beyond the recommendation of physical activity. Considering the relatively short duration of the present intervention, future studies are warranted that incorporate larger sample sizes and extended intervention periods to enable these observed differences to attain statistical significance.

Respiratory parameters, routinely employed in the clinical follow-up of asthma, may either be consistent with the disease’s obstructive pattern or, conversely, may fall within normal reference limits. This variability is often attributed to the inherent expiratory flow variability observed across the natural course of the disease [41]. In the present investigation, the pulmonary function test results for the enrolled patient cohort indicated values within normal limits for all measured parameters, with the exception of the PEF%.

The results of studies investigating the effects of different exercise training programs on pulmonary function test parameters in asthma are contradictory. The GINA 2024 report states that the effects of physical activity, pulmonary rehabilitation and breathing exercises on respiratory function in adults with asthma are quite limited. The meta-analyses examining the effect of exercise training in asthma reported that the effects of exercise training on spirometric parameters, especially FEV1 and FVC values, are questionable [42, 43]. Another recent meta-analysis reported that significant improvements were obtained in PEF values of asthmatic patients after exercise-based pulmonary rehabilitation [2]. Also, studies examining the effects of aerobic exercises in asthmatics have not shown a consistent improvement in spirometric values, except for the PEF value [44]. This change in PEF has been reported to be attributable to an increase in the strength of related muscles rather than to actual improvements in respiratory mechanics. Furthermore, while some studies show that breathing exercises can have positive effects on spirometric parameters by optimizing the respiratory pattern and regulating dysfunctional breathing, there are also studies reporting that these exercises have no significant effect on respiratory parameters [41, 45–47].

In our study, no significant changes were observed in the spirometric parameters (FEV₁, FVC, FEV₁/FVC) in either group, except for the PEF% value. A statistically significant increase in PEF was detected only in the treatment group, and this increase was also significant in favor of the treatment group when the groups were compared. The PEF% value represents the effort-dependent maximal airway flow and therefore reflects not only airway diameter but also muscle strength and voluntary effort. The significant increase observed in the group receiving home-based exercise training may be attributed to the inclusion of breathing exercises and strengthening exercises in addition to physical activity. These targeted interventions may have conferred an additional benefit in enhancing expiratory control and muscle strength within this cohort.

Recent systematic reviews and articles on physical activity levels in adults with asthma report that asthmatics have lower physical activity levels compared to their healthy peers [48, 49]. Previous studies indicate that women exhibit lower levels of physical activity than men, and that exercise avoidance behavior emerges at a younger age among women with asthma compared to their male counterparts [14, 50]. Exercise-induced asthma-related symptoms, misconceptions regarding physical activity, poor baseline asthma control, and limited access to exercise opportunities restrict participation in physical activity among these individuals, thereby contributing to a sedentary lifestyle [51]. In addition to these patient-related factors, insufficient awareness of asthma and exercise among healthcare professionals further contributes to this issue [10, 52]. Despite these barriers, perspectives on physical activity and exercise appear to be evolving in the current literature. Sedentary behavior has been shown to result in declines in key outcomes, including lung function, asthma control, and functional capacity in individuals with asthma [48]. Furthermore, reports indicate that exercise programs can be safely maintained with appropriate precautions [44]. In light of these findings, the importance of participation in exercise and physical activity programs is increasingly recognized.

Meta-analyses investigating the effects of exercise in patients with asthma have reported that exercise training significantly enhances functional capacity [2, 42, 43]. Similarly, randomized controlled trials assessing the impact of various exercise protocols (including core stabilization exercises, home-based exercise training, and pulmonary rehabilitation programs) on functional capacity in patients with asthma have demonstrated significant improvements following intervention [16, 18, 32, 53]. Following the treatment program implemented in our study, 6MWD scores, which assess patients’ functional capacity, increased in both groups; however, this improvement reached statistical significance only in the training group. (16.44 m ± 9.09 m; 49.69 m ± 9.76 m for the control and training groups, respectively).

The minimal clinically significant difference in 6MWD in asthmatics has been reported as 26 m in recent studies [31]. Considering this threshold, it is evident that the increase observed in our treatment group exceeds the minimal clinically important difference. This finding indicates that the improvement is not only statistically significant but also clinically meaningful. A home-based exercise program complemented by supervised sessions resulted in greater improvements in submaximal exercise tolerance in women with asthma compared to physical activity recommendations. Exercise training may be considered a valuable option for this patient population, who typically exhibit low levels of physical activity, given its sustainability and potential to provide meaningful clinical benefits, even in settings where individualized programming and technological support are limited. The observation that these gains were achieved through a simple and accessible program is particularly noteworthy, especially considering the high prevalence of sedentary behavior among individuals with asthma. Furthermore, incorporating supervised sessions to augment such programs may provide additional motivation for patients, thereby potentially enhancing the observed clinical outcomes.

GINA 2024 reported that physical activity can improve quality of life in adults with asthma [8]. Given the documented evidence that women with asthma experience a lower quality of life compared to men, exercise training represents a particularly crucial therapeutic approach for this subgroup [54]. When the literature is examined, there are many studies reporting the positive effects of various exercise programs on health-related quality of life in asthmatic patients [41, 45, 53, 55]. Similarly, in our study, significant improvements were observed in both groups for the total SGRQ score and most of its subdomains. The activity sub-score was the only parameter that demonstrated a statistically significant increase in the treatment group compared to the control group. These findings highlight the positive impact of both interventions—including general physical activity recommendations and structured exercise training—on quality of life in women with asthma. Notably, the improvement in the activity sub-score corresponded with the enhancement in functional capacity, suggesting that benefits derived from exercise training may extend beyond increased exercise tolerance and also positively influence physical activity–related quality of life in this population. Importantly, these improvements were achieved through a simple, accessible, and sustainable exercise training program.

A review of the literature indicates that adults with asthma are more susceptible to skeletal muscle weakness, and accumulating evidence suggests that patients with uncontrolled asthma exhibit increased fat mass and reduced muscle mass [6]. However, some studies report no significant differences in muscle strength. For instance, in the study by Ramos et al., asthmatic patients were compared with healthy peers, and muscle strength was found to be within normal limits [3]. Although the literature on this topic remains inconclusive, preventing skeletal muscle dysfunction and loss of muscle mass is crucial due to their strong association with functional capacity and overall quality of life [56]. Therefore, resistance training targeting all major muscle groups is recommended at least twice weekly for individuals with asthma to maintain muscle strength [7].

In our study, the number of 30-s STST repetitions increased significantly after treatment in both the training and control groups (1.19 and 2.77 repetitions for the control and training groups, respectively). Although an asthma-specific minimal clinically important difference has not been established for the 30-s STST, the improvement observed in the training group exceeded the threshold reported in COPD populations (≥ 2 repetitions), suggesting that this change may also be clinically meaningful. When the groups were compared, the difference was found to be statistically significant in favor of the training group. Unlike the result in our study, Majewski et al. did not report a significant increase in 30-s STST repeat scores after an 8-week training program in their study in which they applied home-based exercise training program to elderly women with asthma (mean age 70.8) [16]. This difference in results may be due to differences in the mean ages of the target populations included in the studies. The 30-s STST shows a significant correlation with 6MWT and peripheral muscle strength in asthmatic patients [3]. The increase observed in 30-s STST, which reflects lower extremity muscle strength in our study, may represent one of the potential underlying mechanisms contributing to the parallel improvement in functional capacity. Enhancing lower extremity muscle strength in individuals with asthma is important, regardless of the presence of skeletal muscle weakness, due to the strong correlation between this parameter and other functional outcomes. In asthmatics, these improvements can be achieved through both physical activity recommendations and participation in exercise training. However, in circumstances where participation in exercise training programs is feasible, even simply structured training programs may provide additional benefits compared to physical activity recommendations. Accordingly, referral of patients with asthma to such programs may be considered to support clinical outcomes.

Conclusion

This randomized controlled trial suggests that a standardized, low-cost, and easily implemented 8-week home-based exercise program supported by weekly supervision was associated with greater improvements in PEF%, functional capacity, activity-related quality of life, and lower extremity muscle strength in women with asthma compared to regular physical activity recommendations. Given that women with asthma often experience a higher symptom burden, lower physical activity levels, and greater barriers to exercise participation, these findings highlight the particular relevance of accessible and home-based exercise strategies for this population. Our study provides an accessible and sustainable non-pharmacological strategy that may be preferred in settings where access to advanced technological equipment or complex clinical settings is limited. This approach may have potential for integration into community-based asthma management and public health programs. Therefore, promoting home-based exercise training may contribute to reducing sedentary behaviors, improving disease control, and enhancing overall health and well-being in individuals with asthma.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, asthma severity and phenotypes were not specifically classified, and detailed baseline medication regimens were not analyzed, which may limit the interpretation of disease severity and treatment response. Additionally, the relatively small and specific study population, consisting exclusively of women with asthma, many of whom had higher BMI and suboptimal asthma control at baseline, may limit the generalizability of the findings.

Adherence to the home-based exercise program was monitored using patient diaries and pedometers; however, it was not quantified using a specific statistical measure or percentage, which may affect the precision of adherence reporting. Moreover, the difference in supervision and interaction between groups, inherent to the pragmatic study design, may have partially influenced the observed outcomes.

The inability to adjust for potential confounding variables such as asthma severity, medication use, and BMI due to the relatively small sample size represents another limitation. In addition, the lack of long-term follow-up data restricts the ability to determine the sustainability of the observed effects. As participation was voluntary, the possibility of selection bias cannot be entirely excluded.
