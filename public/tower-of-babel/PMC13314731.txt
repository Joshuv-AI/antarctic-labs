Miller Fisher Syndrome Should Only be Diagnosed if the Diagnostic Criteria are Met and All Other Differential Diagnoses Have Been Ruled Out
Authors: Josef Finsterer
Published: 2026

To the Editor:

We read with interest the article by Inokuchi et al1 about a 67-year-old man who developed acute diplopia due to ophthalmoparesis and was diagnosed with Miller Fisher syndrome (MFS) based on absent tendon reflexes and gait instability. The patient benefited from intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG).1 The study is promising, but some points require discussion.

First, the diagnosis of MFS is questionable.1 Muscle strength was described as "initially normal," which suggests that muscle weakness developed later in the course of the illness. Since MFS is defined by the triad of ataxia, ophthalmoparesis, and absent tendon reflexes, the patient most likely suffered from Guillain-Barré syndrome (GBS) involving cranial nerves II, IV, and VI prior to the onset of limb weakness. Another argument against MFS is the lack of information regarding GQ1b levels. Elevated levels of GQ1b antibodies are highly sensitive for MFS, as their level is elevated in up to 100% of cases.2

Second, no nerve conduction study (NCS) results have been reported.1 Knowledge of NCS results is crucial to assess whether GBS was of the axonal or demyelinating type and whether it could be classified as acute inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy (AIDP), acute motor axonal neuropathy (AMAN), or acute motor and sensory axonal neuropathy (AMSAN). For the classification of GBS, it is also important to know whether sensory or autonomic nerve fibers were clinically affected.

The third point concerns the lack of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis.1 Since all subtypes of GBS, including MFS, are characterized by a dissociation between normal cell count and elevated CSF protein level,3 confirmation of an elevated CSF protein level is crucial for confirming the diagnosis and ruling out differential diagnoses.

The fourth point concerns the patient's description of ophthalmoplegia at the initial examination; however, the case report later mentions an increase in extraocular muscle weakness.1 Since ophthalmoplegia cannot progress but can only remain stable or resolve, this discrepancy should be resolved.

The fifth point concerns the inadequate exclusion of various differential diagnoses. These include chronic progressive external ophthalmoplegia (CPEO), Kearns-Sayre syndrome (KSS), mitochondrial neurogastrointestinal encephalopathy or encephalomyopathy (MNGIE), sensory ataxic neuropathy, dysarthria, and ophthalmoparesis (SANDO), Pearson syndrome, mitochondrial encephalomyopathy, lactic acidosis, and stroke-like episodes (MELAS), myoclonic epilepsy with ragged red fibers (MERFF), internuclear ophthalmoplegia, myasthenia gravis, thyroid disorders, ocular myositis, multiple sclerosis, and Wernicke encephalopathy.4 How were these differential diagnoses ruled out? Ocular myasthenia gravis, for example, can manifest with ophthalmoparesis and respond to IVIG.

The sixth point concerns comorbidities and concomitant medications. To assess whether the clinical presentation is attributable to concomitant medications or comorbidities and whether these have influenced the course of the disease, it is essential to be aware of all these factors.

The seventh point concerns the trigger of GBS.1 The most common triggers are infections with bacteria, viruses, or protozoa; vaccinations; trauma; immunological disorders; surgery; or sepsis.5 Since infections can be subclinical, it is important to actively screen patients with GBS for them.

The eighth point concerns gait instability, which does not necessarily indicate an ataxic gait. It should be determined whether the finger-to-nose test or the heel-to-knee test was ataxic and whether an ataxic posture was present.
