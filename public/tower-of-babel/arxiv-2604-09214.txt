Abstract
Despite their advantages in scalability and energy efficiency for realizing extremely large reconfigurable intelligent surfaces (RISs) , liquid crystal (LC) elements exhibit frequency-dependent phase-shift characteristics. Ignoring this inherent property can severely impact the overall system performance, particularly in wideband scenarios. This challenge is particularly problematic for physical layer security, as even slight frequency-induced phase deviations can cause significant unintended signal leakage. This paper addresses these frequency-induced variations by developing a physics-based model for an LC unit cell across varying frequencies and proposing a novel phase-shift design framework that maximizes secure communication across all subcarriers. Furthermore, given the large number of elements in millimeter wave (mmWave) LC - RISs , acquiring full channel state information (CSI) is often impractical. Therefore, we optimize the phase shifts based solely on the locations of the legitimate mobile users (MUs) and potential eavesdroppers. Additionally, we optimize the RIS to illuminate a specific spatial zone instead of focusing on a precise target point. This area illumination strategy improves robustness against inaccurate positioning data for both the MUs and eavesdroppers, and reduces the overhead associated with frequent re-acquisition of positioning data. To solve the formulated problem, we introduce both a semi-definite programming (SDP) -based solution and a low-complexity heuristic method. While the SDP -based approach yields superior performance, it incurs higher computational complexity. Conversely, the scalable method exhibits a much slower scaling of complexity with respect to the number of RIS elements, which makes it highly suitable for extremely large RISs . Our simulation results demonstrate that both proposed algorithms improve the secrecy rate compared to baseline methods that neglect frequency-dependent effects. In our considered setup operating at 60 GHz, the proposed SDP -based and low-complexity frameworks achieve a secrecy rate of approximately 2 and 1 bits/symbol, respectively, while benchmark schemes cannot guarantee a non-zero secrecy rate across the entire 8 GHz bandwidth. Finally, the proposed wideband phase-shift design is validated through experimental evaluations on an LC - RIS setup, which has confirmed its practical effectiveness.

II-C

Frequency Response of an LC Cell

This subsection details the physical mechanism by which
LC
molecules impart a phase shift onto incident electromagnetic waves, as well as the frequency-dependent nature of this response.
LC
-based

[
9
]

Due to the elongated, rod-like structure of

radio frequency (RF)

\vec{E}_{\rm RF}

\vec{E}_{\rm RF}
) aligns with the major axis of the molecules, the effective permittivity increases, resulting in a larger induced phase shift, as depicted in Fig.
2
. Consequently, continuously tuning the applied voltage allows for precise control over the molecular orientation. This capability empowers the
RIS
to dynamically shape signal reflections, thereby facilitating a fully programmable wireless propagation environment.

II-D

Secrecy Rate

The secrecy rate serves as a fundamental metric for evaluating the performance of physical-layer security in communication systems. It is formally defined as the difference between the achievable data rate of the legitimate user and that of the eavesdropper

\displaystyle\mathrm{SR}(f_{k})

\displaystyle=[\widetilde{\mathrm{SR}}(f_{k})]^{+},

(8a)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathrm{SR}}(f_{k})

\displaystyle=\log(1\!+\!\mathrm{SNR}_{u}(f_{k}))\!-\!\log(1\!+\!\mathrm{SNR}_{e}(f_{k})),

(8b)

\displaystyle\mathrm{SNR}_{u}(f_{k})

\displaystyle=|(\mathbf{h}_{u}^{\mathrm{eff}}(f_{k}))^{\mathsf{H}}\mathbf{q}|^{2}/\sigma^{2}_{n},

(8c)

\displaystyle\mathrm{SNR}_{e}(f_{k})

\displaystyle=|(\mathbf{h}_{e}^{\mathrm{eff}}(f_{k}))^{\mathsf{H}}\mathbf{q}|^{2}/\sigma^{2}_{n},

(8d)

where
(\mathbf{h}_{g}^{\mathrm{eff}})^{\mathsf{H}}=\mathbf{h}_{d,g}^{\mathsf{H}}+\mathbf{h}_{r,g}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{\Gamma}\mathbf{H}_{t}

Low-complexity method

The

is computationally complex, see Section

for a complexity analysis. Therefore, in this section, we design a low-complexity scheme. To tackle the non-convexity of Problem P3 established by the definitions in (
14
), (
15
), and (
16
), we formulate the following problem:

P8:

\displaystyle~\underset{\gamma,\mathbf{s}_{c}}{\max}~\gamma

(26a)

\displaystyle~\text{s.t.}~~\text{C1: }\frac{1+\mathrm{SNR}_{e}(f_{k})}{1+\mathrm{SNR}_{u}(f_{k})}\geq\gamma,\forall(\mathbf{p}_{u},\mathbf{p}_{e})\!\in\!\mathcal{P}_{u}\!\times\!\mathcal{P}_{e},\,\forall k,

(26b)

\displaystyle\quad\hphantom{\text{s.t.}}\text{C2: }\mathbf{s}_{k}=\mathbf{s}_{c}^{\circ\beta_{k}},\,\forall k,

(26c)

\displaystyle\quad\hphantom{\text{s.t.}}\text{C3: }|[\mathbf{s}_{c}]_{n}|=1,\,\forall n.

(26d)

We optimize
\gamma
and
\mathbf{s}_{c}
iteratively. Even for a fixed
\gamma
, this problem remains non-convex due to the unit-modulus constraint C3. To render the problem tractable, we introduce and maximize a surrogate lower bound
[
22
]
. Let us define

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}_{k,u,e}(\gamma)

\displaystyle\triangleq\mathbf{A}^{u}_{k}(\mathbf{p}_{u})-\gamma\mathbf{A}^{e}_{k}(\mathbf{p}_{e}),

(27)

\displaystyle\bm{\Phi}_{k,u,e}(\gamma)

\displaystyle\triangleq\mathbf{A}_{k,u,e}(\gamma)-\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{A}_{k,u,e}(\gamma))\mathbf{I}_{N},

(28)

where
\lambda_{\min}(\cdot)
denotes the minimum eigenvalue of a matrix. This eigenvalue is inherently negative, stemming from the
-\gamma\mathbf{A}^{e}_{k}(\mathbf{p}_{e})
term in (
27
). Based on these definitions, we can reformulate constraint C1 given by
\mathbf{s}_{k}^{\mathsf{H}}\mathbf{A}_{k,u,e}(\gamma)\mathbf{s}_{k}-\gamma+1\geq 0
into a lower bound using the following lemma.

Lemma 3
.

A valid lower bound for
\mathbf{s}_{k}^{\mathsf{H}}\mathbf{A}_{k,u,e}(\gamma)\mathbf{s}_{k}
is

\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{A}_{k,u,e}(\gamma))N+2\mathrm{Re}\{\mathbf{s}_{k}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{\beta}_{k,u,e}(\gamma)\}-\tilde{\mathbf{s}}_{k}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{\Phi}_{k,u,e}(\gamma)\tilde{\mathbf{s}}_{k},

(29)

where

\bm{\beta}_{k,u,e}(\gamma)\triangleq\bm{\Phi}_{k,u,e}(\gamma)\tilde{\mathbf{s}}_{k}.

(30)

Here,
\gamma
and the constant vector
\tilde{\mathbf{s}}_{k}
are fixed, and equality is achievable by
\mathbf{s}_{k}=\tilde{\mathbf{s}}_{k},\,\forall k
.

Proof.

Because
\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{A}_{k,u,e}(\gamma))
represents the minimum eigenvalue, subtracting it multiplied by the identity matrix in (
28
) ensures that all eigenvalues of
\bm{\Phi}_{k,u,e}(\gamma)
are non-negative. Consequently,
\bm{\Phi}_{k,u,e}(\gamma)
is a positive semi-definite matrix, which implies that
(\mathbf{s}_{k}-\tilde{\mathbf{s}}_{k})^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{\Phi}_{k,u,e}(\gamma)(\mathbf{s}_{k}-\tilde{\mathbf{s}}_{k})\geq 0
. By expanding this inequality and substituting the definition of
\bm{\Phi}_{k,u,e}(\gamma)
, we obtain
\mathbf{s}_{k}^{\mathsf{H}}\mathbf{A}_{k,u,e}(\gamma)\mathbf{s}_{k}-\underbrace{\mathbf{s}_{k}^{\mathsf{H}}\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{A}_{k,u,e}(\gamma))\mathbf{I}_{N}\mathbf{s}_{k}}_{\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{A}_{k,u,e}(\gamma))\|\mathbf{s}_{k}\|^{2}}-2\mathrm{Re}\{\mathbf{s}_{k}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{\beta}_{k,u,e}(\gamma)\}+\tilde{\mathbf{s}}_{k}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{\Phi}_{k,u,e}(\gamma)\tilde{\mathbf{s}}_{k}\geq 0
. Rearranging these terms yields the proposed lower bound, concluding the proof.
∎

With the help of Lemma
3
, we can reformulate Problem P8 by maximizing its surrogate function as follows:

P9:

\displaystyle~\underset{\mathbf{s}_{c}}{\max}~\underset{k,\mathbf{p}_{u},\mathbf{p}_{e}}{\min}~\mathrm{Re}\{\mathbf{s}_{k}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{\beta}_{k,u,e}(\gamma)\}

(31a)

\displaystyle\quad\hphantom{\text{s.t.}}\text{C1: }\mathbf{s}_{k}=\mathbf{s}_{c}^{\circ\beta_{k}},\,\forall k,

(31b)

\displaystyle\quad\hphantom{\text{s.t.}}\text{C2: }|[\mathbf{s}_{c}]_{n}|=1,\,\forall n.

(31c)

Problem P9 can be solved iteratively. A naive approach would be to fix the solution in previous step
\tilde{\mathbf{s}}_{k}
at each iteration and optimize
\mathbf{s}_{k}
exclusively for the specific subcarrier and location that yields the minimum secrecy rate. However, this hard-minimum approach is highly susceptible to the ping-pong effect, where the algorithm continuously oscillates between a finite set of active constraints and fails to converge. To address this issue, rather than directly maximizing the non-smooth worst-case
secure rate (SR)
in Problem P9, we maximize its
log-sum-exp (LSE)
approximation. This approach provides a smooth and differentiable surrogate objective.

Lemma 4
.

A valid lower bound for
\underset{n}{\min}~x_{n}
is given by

\underset{n}{\min}~x_{n}\geq-\mu\log\left(\sum_{n}\exp\left(-\frac{x_{n}}{\mu}\right)\right),

(32)

where
\mu>0
is a smoothing parameter. This approximation becomes exact (i.e., equality is achieved) as
\mu\to 0
.

Proof.

Since the exponential function is strictly positive, it follows that
\sum_{n}\exp(-\frac{x_{n}}{\mu})\geq\exp(-\frac{\underset{n}{\min}~x_{n}}{\mu}),\,\forall\mu>0
. Because the natural logarithm is a monotonically increasing function, applying
\log(\cdot)
to both sides yields
\log\left(\sum_{n}\exp\left(-\frac{x_{n}}{\mu}\right)\right)\geq-\frac{\underset{n}{\min}~x_{n}}{\mu}
. Multiplying both sides by
-\mu
reverses the direction of the inequality, which directly results in (
32
).
∎

Applying Lemma
4
, we obtain:

\displaystyle\underset{k,\mathbf{p}_{u},\mathbf{p}_{e}}{\min}\widetilde{\mathrm{SR}}(f_{k},\mathbf{p}_{u},\mathbf{p}_{e})\geq-\mu\log(w_{\text{total}}),

(33a)

\displaystyle w_{\text{total}}\triangleq\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{\mathbf{p}_{u}\in\mathcal{P}_{U}}\sum_{\mathbf{p}_{e}\in\mathcal{P}_{e}}w(k,\mathbf{p}_{u},\mathbf{p}_{e}),

(33b)

\displaystyle w(k,\mathbf{p}_{u},\mathbf{p}_{e})\triangleq\exp(-\frac{\widetilde{\mathrm{SR}}(f_{k},\mathbf{p}_{u},\mathbf{p}_{e})}{\mu}),

(33c)

where
\widetilde{\mathrm{SR}}(f_{k},\mathbf{p}_{u},\mathbf{p}_{e})
is evaluated using the phase-shift vector
\tilde{\mathbf{s}}_{k}
from the previous iteration. By assigning exponentially heavier weights to the scenarios with the lowest
SR
, the optimal phase-shift vector is derived as:

\mathbf{s}_{c}=\exp\!\!\left(\mathsf{j}\arg\!\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{\mathbf{p}_{u}\in\mathcal{P}_{U}}\sum_{\mathbf{p}_{e}\in\mathcal{P}_{e}}\!\!\!\frac{w(k,\mathbf{p}_{u},\mathbf{p}_{e})}{w_{\text{total}}}\bm{\beta}_{k,u,e}(\gamma)\!\right)\!\!\right).

(34)

We execute this procedure iteratively. At each iteration, the previous solution vectors
\tilde{\mathbf{s}}_{k}
are updated with the newly computed
\mathbf{s}_{k}
for all
k
. Once the sequence of phase-shift vectors
\mathbf{s}_{k},\forall k
, converges, we update the auxiliary variable
\gamma
using (
25
). The proposed algorithm is summarized in Algorithm

For every iteration of Algorithm

In contrast, Algorithm

has a rank of at most two effectively for any specific user and eavesdropper location pair. Let us expand

and

\gamma

\mathbf{s}_{c}

\lambda^{2}-\mathrm{tr}\{\mathbf{C}\}\lambda+\det(\mathbf{C})=0
. Thus, the complexity of the Algorithm

\mathcal{O}(T_{\max}I_{\max}|\mathcal{P}_{u}||\mathcal{P}_{e}|NK)

VI
Conclusion and Future Directions

In this paper, we studied the effect of frequency variations on the phase-shift response of
LC
-
RIS
elements. To address the inherent frequency dependency in wideband systems, we proposed two novel algorithms that explicitly account for this behavior in the phase-shift design, with the objective of maximizing the secrecy rate within a targeted spatial area. The first algorithm leverages
SDP
, while the second employs a surrogate function approach, which successfully reduces the computational complexity compared to the
SDP
-based method. Extensive simulation results confirmed the critical importance of incorporating the frequency-dependent characteristics of
LC
-
RIS
elements, demonstrating significant performance gains over baseline approaches that neglect this factor. Specifically, the proposed
SDP
-based and scalable methods achieved secrecy rates of approximately 2 and 1 bits/symbol, respectively, over an 8 GHz bandwidth at a center frequency of 60 GHz. Furthermore, our experimental validations clearly demonstrated that the proposed framework effectively enhances the received power in practical scenarios compared to existing benchmark schemes.
