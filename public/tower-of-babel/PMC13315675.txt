Recurrent splanchnic venous thrombosis with mesenteric venous ischemia and secondary omental infarction after discontinuation of direct oral anticoagulation: A case report
Authors: Farnaz Delavari, Fabio Porões, Paul Salomon, Guillaume Fahrni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Splanchnic venous thrombosis (SVT) consists of thrombosis involving all or part of the portal, mesenteric, splenic, and related visceral veins. Noncirrhotic, nonmalignant SVT is uncommon, and decisions regarding duration of anticoagulation remain clinically challenging. We report a 66-year-old man with a history of a first episode of spleno-mesenteric venous thrombosis treated for 4 years with rivaroxaban. Eight years after the initial episode and 4 years following treatment discontinuation, he presented with severe diffuse abdominal pain. Contrast-enhanced abdominal computed tomography demonstrated recurrent partial thrombosis involving the porto-mesenteric venous axis with associated mesenteric venous ischemia and concomitant thrombosis of the left gastroepiploic vein complicated by infarction of the greater omentum. Therapeutic anticoagulation was resumed, and follow-up CT showed regression of portal thrombosis and improvement of ischemic findings, with imaging features consistent with collateral revascularization; however, a segmental infarction of the greater omentum persisted. An extensive thrombophilia and malignancy workup remained negative. This case highlights the imaging spectrum of recurrent SVT, including the uncommon association with secondary omental infarction, and supports consideration of long-term anticoagulation in apparently unprovoked SVT.

Introduction

Splanchnic venous thrombosis (SVT) represents an uncommon form of venous thromboembolism affecting the portal venous system and its tributaries [1]. Clinical presentation is variable, ranging from incidental imaging findings to acute abdominal pain with intestinal ischemia. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) is the cornerstone of evaluation because it simultaneously depicts thrombus extent, bowel and extraintestinal consequences, and alternative diagnoses [2]. Although anticoagulation is recommended for acute SVT, the optimal duration in noncirrhotic, nonmalignant and apparently unprovoked cases remains debated, and recurrent events after anticoagulant withdrawal are clinically relevant [3].

Moreover, associated findings consistent with mesenteric venous ischemia and a small volume of free perihepatic fluid were present (Fig. 2A). This presentation represented a recurrent episode occurring 8 years after the initial SVT diagnosis and 4 years after discontinuation of rivaroxaban.

Management and outcome

During the recurrent episode, the patient was managed conservatively for the segmental omental infarction, with analgesic therapy and clinical monitoring. No surgical or percutaneous intervention was required. Therapeutic anticoagulation was resumed by the hematology consultation given recurrent unprovoked SVT.

Long-term follow-up

Follow-up CT performed 47 days after the recurrent episode (Fig. 3) demonstrated regression of portal venous thrombosis and improvement of the ischemic changes; however, a segmental omental infarction persisted without clinical deterioration. Discussion

This case illustrates 3 clinically relevant points. First, it emphasizes the value of contrast-enhanced CT for comprehensive characterization of SVT extent and for detection of bowel and extraintestinal complications. Second, it demonstrates that recurrent SVT may present after anticoagulant withdrawal even when an extensive etiologic evaluation is unrevealing. Third, it highlights an uncommon CT association of recurrent porto-mesenteric venous thrombosis with left gastroepiploic vein thrombosis complicated by infarction of the greater omentum.

In noncirrhotic, nonmalignant SVT, anticoagulation is generally recommended [4] because it reduces thrombus propagation and promotes recanalization, and long-term management requires balancing recurrence risk against bleeding risk. The 2025 EASL Clinical Practice Guidelines on vascular diseases of the liver recommend early anticoagulation in acute portal and mesenteric venous thrombosis and emphasize etiologic evaluation [5], including testing for myeloproliferative neoplasms and antiphospholipid syndrome. Similarly, AASLD guidance supports anticoagulation for acute portal vein thrombosis and recommends systematic evaluation for underlying risk factors and close imaging follow-up [6].

Recent data highlight that recurrence can occur after anticoagulation withdrawal [7]. A multicenter study in noncirrhotic portal vein thrombosis associated with local factors reported higher rates of recurrent thrombotic events in patients not receiving anticoagulation, supporting the concept that prolonged anticoagulation may be beneficial in higher-risk settings [8]. In addition, a randomized controlled trial in chronic noncirrhotic portal vein thrombosis without major risk factors demonstrated that rivaroxaban prophylaxis reduced thrombotic events compared with no anticoagulation without an excess of major bleeding [9]. Together, these data support individualized consideration of extended or indefinite anticoagulation in patients with recurrent and apparently unprovoked SVT.

From a radiologic perspective, mesenteric venous ischemia is an important subset of acute mesenteric ischemia. Compared with arterial occlusive disease, venous ischemia may have a less abrupt onset and can demonstrate bowel wall thickening and submucosal edema, mesenteric edema, ascites, and venous filling defects on CT. A recent radiographics review advocates a pathophysiology-based approach and underlines that venous outflow obstruction generally has a different prognostic profile than arterial occlusive disease or nonobstructive mesenteric ischemia [10].

Omental infarction is an uncommon cause of acute abdominal pain and can be primary, caused by idiopathic torsion or spontaneous thrombosis, or secondary to adjacent inflammatory processes, hypercoagulable states, or vascular compromise. CT typically demonstrates a focal, heterogeneous fat-density lesion with surrounding inflammatory stranding in the greater omentum, and conservative management is often appropriate in stable patients [11,12]. In the present case, the presence of a thrombosed left gastroepiploic vein suggests a thrombotic mechanism linked to the SVT recurrence.

This case reinforces that recurrent SVT can be associated with uncommon extraintestinal ischemic manifestations such as omental infarction.

Differential diagnosis

In patients presenting with severe abdominal pain and CT evidence of mesenteric venous thrombosis, key differential considerations include: (1) arterial occlusive acute mesenteric ischemia, (2) strangulating small bowel obstruction, (3) inflammatory enteritis (including infectious or inflammatory bowel disease), and (4) other fat necrosis entities such as epiploic appendagitis or primary omental infarction. The presence of venous filling defects in the porto-mesenteric system and concordant ischemic findings supported the diagnosis of mesenteric venous ischemia in this patient.

Conclusion

Recurrent noncirrhotic, nonmalignant splanchnic venous thrombosis may occur after cessation of anticoagulation despite a negative thrombophilia and malignancy workup. Contrast-enhanced CT is essential to define thrombus extent and to identify complications, including mesenteric venous ischemia. Concomitant gastroepiploic vein thrombosis with secondary omental infarction represents an uncommon associated manifestation that radiologists should recognize. This case supports individualized consideration of long-term anticoagulation after apparently unprovoked or recurrent SVT.
