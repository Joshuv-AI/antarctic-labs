Holistic Assessment
of Actinide-Borohydride Covalency
Using NMR and X‐ray Absorption Spectroscopies

Hannah M Hansen, Nathan R Loutsch, Thien H Nguyen, Aaron Christianson, Jacob J Schuely, Mai Yer Yang, Peter A Zacher, Lucia Zuin, Trevor W Hayton, Bess Vlaisavljevich, Scott R Daly

Abstract

The influence of metal-ligand covalency on actinide electronic
properties and reactivity continues to be a topic of intense interest
given its implications for nuclear and radiochemical applications.
Extensive work has focused on developing spectroscopic methods that
can be used to quantify metal-ligand covalency within actinide complexes
for benchmarking and comparison with theoretical efforts. Ligand K-edge
X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS) and NMR spectroscopy are two physical
methods that have dominated these efforts, but surprisingly, they
have not been used together on the same systems to see how the methods
compare. Herein, we report the first comparative assessment of actinide-ligand
covalency using B K-edge XAS and multinuclear NMR spectroscopy. The
B K-edge XAS spectra of An­(κ3-BH4)­(NR2)3 (R = SiMe3) with An = Th (1) and U (2) reveal a dominant feature assigned to B
1s → An–H–B π* MOs due to covalent three-center,
two-electron bonding with the actinide 6d orbitals, as revealed by
comparison with time-dependent density functional theory (TDDFT) calculations.
By contrast, the data indicate that 5f-orbital contributions to the
bonds are significantly smaller. These findings were corroborated
by 1H and 11B NMR studies of diamagnetic 1 and DFT calculations used to quantify the impact of orbital
mixing via spin-orbit coupling on the chemical shifts. These results
represent the first instance of using B K-edge XAS and 11B NMR spectroscopy to assess covalency in actinide complexes. Moreover,
they demonstrate how ligand K-edge XAS, which provides rich orbital-specific
information relevant to chemical bonding, and NMR spectroscopy, which
is routinely available in most chemical research settings, can be
used as complementary techniques to provide a holistic assessment
of covalency in actinide-ligand bonds.

Introduction

Quantifying variations in metal-ligand
(M-L) covalency remains
an important objective in actinide science given its critical influence
on the reactivity and properties of f-elements.
−

 For example, successful separation of trivalent lanthanides and
actinides is often attributed to their differing M-L covalency with
extractant molecules, as first hypothesized over 70 years ago. These early qualitative observations subsequently
spurred the development of experimental methods that could quantify
the extent of covalency in actinide-ligand bonds.

One physical
method that has proven exceptionally successful for
measuring M-L covalency in actinide complexes (as well as those with
lanthanides)
−

 is ligand K-edge X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS).
−

 This technique uses synchrotron generated X-rays to probe dipole-allowed
transitions between core 1s orbitals localized on the ligand and vacant
molecular orbitals (MOs) derived from M-L bonds. The presence and intensity of the associated transitions
are governed by the amount of ligand np character
mixing in the corresponding MOs, thereby providing an experimental
measure of orbital mixing. Though this
technique can afford orbital-level resolution of electronic structure
and bonding, XAS is not readily accessible to most chemists due to
limited access to facilities and technical barriers associated with
sample preparation and data collection, especially on air-sensitive
systems ().

An alternative method to ligand K-edge XAS, that
has also been
used to quantify M-L covalency in actinide-ligand bonds, is NMR spectroscopy.
−

 NMR spectroscopy is widely available for researchers, in contrast
with synchrotron-based methods like XAS. Though it is much more accessible,
it relies more heavily on comparison to theory to abstract orbital-specific
covalency information, which typically limits this approach to diamagnetic
complexes due to the challenge of adequately modeling NMR shifts for
paramagnetic complexes (though this is starting to be addressed). This NMR-based approach relies on measuring
the chemical shifts (δ) and nuclear shielding constants (σ)
of actinide-bound nuclei, the latter of which is comprised of three
components, as shown in ,

where σdia, σpara, and σSO refer to the diamagnetic, paramagnetic,
and spin-orbit contributions, respectively. The magnitude of σSO is the crucial component for assessing M-L covalency, since
this component correlates with the amount of ligand s-character and
M-L orbital overlap present in the bonds. The σSO value is obtained indirectly by using DFT
to quantify the difference in σ values with scalar relativistic
(SR) and spin-orbit (SO) treatment.
−

 The chemical shift attained with
SO treatment (δSO) is directly comparable to experiment.
This approach has been demonstrated for f-block coordination complexes
with ligands containing NMR-active nuclei such as 13C,
−

15N,
−

29Si,
31P,
77Se, and 125Te. It has also been used to analyze the 1H NMR shifts of
Pb hydride and Tl borohydride complexes,
,
 as well as hydride complexes with Zr, Hf, Th, and U.

Both ligand K-edge XAS and NMR spectroscopy
(as well as EPR spectroscopy)
,
 have been used to investigate
M-L covalency in actinide complexes
containing ligands with many of the p-block elements, but a notable
exception is boron. In this context, there have been longstanding
questions about covalency in actinide borohydride complexes and its
impact on molecular properties. Homoleptic
actinide borohydrides, like U­(BH4)4, are remarkably
volatile and were investigated for isotopic separations during the
Manhattan Project.
−

 Trivalent actinide borohydride complexes are also known to exhibit
structural and reactivity differences consistent with increased covalency
when compared to isomorphous complexes with lanthanides.
−

Here we describe a combined B K-edge XAS and NMR study of
An­(κ3-BH4)­(NR2)3, where An = Th (1) and
U (2) and
R = SiMe3, that reveals corroborating evidence of covalent
actinide-borohydride bonding (). These complexes were selected for B K-edge XAS studies
because they have relatively high (axial) symmetry at the metal and
boron. The (BH4)1– ligand also offers
numerous advantages for these studies. It is electronically simple,
lacking substituents and competing π systems. Accordingly, the
NMR shifts are dominated by metal interactions, which should make
spin-orbit shifts easier to interpret as a covalency metric. Perhaps
most importantly, the same B 2p orbitals that result in B K-edge XAS
features are also involved in the spin-orbit deshielding observed
in the NMR spectra, which allows the two techniques to be directly
compared.

Though boron is not directly bound to the metals,
some of us recently
showed how the delocalized nature of M–H–B bonding (i.e., three-centered, two-electron bonds) allows boron to serve as a reporter of covalent mixing
in Zr- and Hf-borohydride bonds using B K-edge XAS. We demonstrate here that the same approach can be used
to interrogate actinide-borohydride bonds. Borohydrides also have
two common NMR-active nuclei, 1H and 11B, which
permitted us to evaluate how the covalency assessments from B K-edge
XAS compared to those obtained from NMR spectroscopy experiments with
diamagnetic 1. To our knowledge, this is the first instance
where both methods have been used to assess covalency in the same
complex.

Results and Discussion

Complexes 1 (An
= Th) and 2 (An = U)
were prepared as described previously by treating AnCl­(NR2)3 (R = SiMe3) with LiBH4 in Et2O. The complexes were crystallized
from pentane, and their structures confirmed by single-crystal XRD.
The structure of 1 had been described previously, but was recollected here, while the structure
of 2 had yet to be reported (a). Both complexes feature C3 point
group symmetry with An-B distances of 2.64(1) and 2.55(1) Å,
respectively, consistent with a κ3-BH4 binding mode and expected differences in Th4+ and U4+ radii. The 1H NMR
spectra collected in C6D6 matched those reported
previously. A 1:1:1:1 quartet assigned
to BH4 was observed at δH = 4.25 (1; 1
J
HB = 83 Hz) and
δH = −99.95 ppm (2; 1
J
HB = 78 Hz) (b). These assignments were confirmed by synthesizing
the BD4 analogues, 1-d
4
 and 2-d
4
 (see ESI for details). The 1H NMR data
indicates that the terminal and bridging hydrides are equivalent on
the NMR time scale due to rapid exchange, as reported for other tetrahydroborate
complexes. The 11B NMR spectra
revealed the complementary BH4 resonances at δB = −8.0 ppm (1) and δB = −10.0 ppm (2). Cooling a CD2Cl2 sample of either 1 or 2 to −40
°C results in no decoalescence of the hydride resonances, which
is not surprising since the barrier for exchange is known to be extremely
small.

B K-edge XAS data were collected at room temperature
on solid samples
of 1 and 2 at the Canadian Light Source
(CLS) using the Variable Line Spacing Plate Grating Monochromator
(VLS-PGM) beamline. Samples were finely
ground in a mortar and pestle and pressed into In foil. Total fluorescence
yield (FLY) and total electron yield (TEY) data were collected simultaneously.
The pressure in the experimental chamber was in the range of 10–8 to 10–9 Torr (UHV) during the measurements.
We note that TEY measurements are surface sensitive, primarily due
to the low energy and escape depth of electrons,
,
 whereas FLY measurements are bulk sensitive as they probe deeper
into the material due to photons’ decreased interaction with
matter.

It is important to start
by noting that these are remarkably challenging
measurements by XAS standards given the very high air- and water-sensitivity
of 1 and 2, in combination with the relatively
low B K-edge energy (ca. 188 eV). Any materials that are normally
used to protect samples from air during sample loading (e.g., polypropylene,
Kapton) block the beam due to the very short attenuation length of
light at this energy. Despite the challenges, we have developed robust
procedures for delivering and loading samples in the chamber with
minimum hydrolysis (see Experimental Section in the Supporting Information for full details).

Another challenge
is photodecomposition. We have previously shown
that molecular borohydride and dicarbollide complexes can readily
undergo photodecomposition during B K-edge XAS data collection.
,
 To help distinguish between spectral features for 1 (An = Th) and 2 (An = U) from those associated with
photodecomposition, B K-edge XAS data were collected using two different
scanning techniques: step scans and continuous scans (). To minimize light on the
sample and attenuate potential photodecomposition to the best of our
ability, step scans with 0.5 s dwell times were collected using a
piezoelectric (PZ) shutter that closed the beam between energy moves.
We also moved to a new sample position after every step scan, unless
noted otherwise. The second scanning technique, continuous scans,
used longer 1.0 s intervals and no PZ shutter. These scans were used
to intentionally induce and monitor for photodecomposition, as described
in the aforementioned B K-edge XAS studies of Ni­(C2B9H11)2 and M­(κ3-RBH3)4 complexes with M = Zr, Hf and R = aryl and benzyl.
,

The step scan data (with PZ shutter) collected for
1 and 2 revealed four main features in
the spectra of
both complexes, as evident in the second derivative traces (; ). Each spectrum possessed a
low-resolution pre-edge shoulder at 189.9 eV (1) and
189.8 eV (2) (labeled A in ), and a more prominent second feature labeled
B at 190.7 eV (1) and 190.8 eV (2). For
comparison, these features were not observed in the B K-edge XAS spectrum
of NaBH4 when collected using step scans; only a broad
rising edge feature was observed at 191.4 eV (; gray trace), consistent with values reported
in the literature. The step scan spectra
also revealed two additional features at higher energy above the edge
(C and D) with slight shifts in energy depending on the metal. These
features appeared at 192.5 and 193.8 eV when An = Th (1), and 192.2 and 194.8 eV when An = U (2).

Interestingly, the same features identified in the
step scan data
were observed at nearly identical energies when spectra were collected
using continuous scans (no PZ shutter). However, the continuous scan
and step scan spectra showed dramatic differences in intensity, especially
for features A and B (; ).
More remarkably, the samples did not initially show significant scan-to-scan
changes (as would be expected for photodecomposition) when repeat
continuous scans were collected on the same spot without moving sample
positions (Figures S14–S15). These
continuous scan spectra were also reproducible on numerous independently
prepared samples collected on separate experimental runs. Only slight
differences in peak intensity and resolution were observed (Figure S21).

To ensure that the differences
in the spectra collected with and
without the PZ shutter were not due to sample-to-sample differences,
we also collected repeat continuous scan data on the same samples
that yielded the step scan data shown in . This was performed by moving to a fresh
spot on the sample plate after the step scan data collection was complete.
The continuous scan data collected on these new sample positions once
again showed the characteristic increase in intensity for features
A and B (),
and they culminated in spectra like those shown in (see Figures S21 and S22 for overlays). Another notable observation is that
we were able to capture scan-to-scan spectral changes in these latter
experiments by further decreasing the energy range and time required
for each continuous scan (). This scan-to-scan transformation was also captured
in a repeat scan collected on the same spot of 1 using
the PZ shutter and step scans (Figure S16). Collectively, these data show that the differences in the step
scan and continuous scan spectra shown in can be attributed to photodecomposition
associated with different levels of exposure to the beam.

To round out our analysis, TEY spectra were collected
concurrently
with the FLY data to evaluate spectral differences between the sample
surface and the bulk (, S19 and S20). Generally, the
TEY data reproduced the spectral features in the FLY data for 1 and 2, though the spectra show more hydrolysis
on the sample surface, as expected given the high air sensitivity
of the samples. For example, the TEY spectra reveal a more intense
feature close to 194 eV, which is consistent with transitions associated
with B–O bonds. It is likely that
subtle sample-to-sample energy and intensity differences for features
C and D in the bulk FLY data are affected by small variations in hydrolysis
on the surface. However, we note that features C and D are predicted
by TDDFT calculations for 1 and 2 (see below),
so they do not appear to be ascribed to surface hydrolysis alone.

Repeat continuous scans collected on the same sample position again
revealed a stepwise increase in intensity, as illustrated with the
TEY scans of 2 shown in . The TEY signal corresponds to collected
Auger electrons ejected from the sample (as measured using drain current),
so the increasing intensity may suggest an increase in sample conductivity
as a function of beam exposure. Consistently, we observed that sample
positions exposed to the beam revealed spots that appeared to expose
more of the underlying In foil used for conduction (Figure S31).

With these observations, we can elaborate
on the origin of the
scan-to-scan intensity differences in the B K-edge XAS spectra for 1 and 2. One hypothesis is that the increasing
spectral intensity, especially for the first two features below 191
eV, are assignable to the ingrowth of photodecomposition products
as the sample is continuously exposed to the beam. The most compelling
evidence in support of this hypothesis is similar data collected for
NaBH4 subjected to repeat continuous FLY and TEY scans
on the same sample spot (). These NaBH4 data revealed the ingrowth
of new broad features below 191 eV. It is clear to see how the ingrowth
of similar features in repeat scans on the same positions for 1 and 2 would account for the growing intensity
of features A and B, as shown in . One unusual observation is that we do not
see features associated with the real sample disappear, as observed
by us previously, and as one would expect as sample is lost to photodecomposition
processes. The features for 1 and 2 only
appear to increase in intensity. This observation could suggest that
a different process occurs during repeated scanning of 1 and 2.

Another hypothesis is that samples of 1 and 2 sublime, or photodecompose to form boron-free
products that
then sublime, when exposed to the beam under UHV conditions. Evidence
in support of this hypothesis is the relatively uniform increase in
intensity in the FLY data. This behavior is consistent with decreasing
self-absorption (also called over-absorption)
,
 as the sample concentration in the path of the beam decreases with
loss of material. This hypothesis is also consistent with increasing
TEY signal (and sample conductivity), and the visible spots that appear
in the sample when scanning repeatedly on the position. Moreover,
any B–O containing products that form due to hydrolysis would
likely be less volatile (these are often oligomeric with B–O–B
linkages), and boron oxides are more robust with respect to photodecomposition
in the beam. This may account for why peaks occurring in the region
where hydrolysis features are expected (features C and D; and ), do not appear to change significantly
in intensity during repeated scanning on the same position ().

Most importantly,
this hypothesis is consistent with the known
chemistry of UIV tetrahydroborates, which are known to
undergo both photo and thermal reduction to UIII complexes
with net loss of H2 and B2H6.
,
 Similar reactivity occurs with 2. In particular, this complex thermally decomposes at 70
°C in C6D6 to form the UIV metallocycle
UIV[N­(SiMe2CH2)­R]­(NR2)2 (3; R = SiMe3), a process which is
presumed to proceed through the hydride intermediate UH­(NR2)3 (a). The Th analogue Th­[N­(SiMe2CH2)­R]­(NR2)2 (4; R = SiMe3) has similarly
been isolated and shown to undergo reactions with H2 to
form ThH­(NR2)3, which is unstable with respect
to 4 (b). Both 3 and 4 have appreciable
volatility, as is typically observed for f-element complexes with
[N­(SiMe3)2]1– ligands. Andersen
and coworkers reported that 3 and 4 sublime
at 100–110 °C under vacuum (pressure not reported), suggesting that samples could sublime when heated
by the beam under UHV conditions (which is much higher vacuum than
is typically used in synthetic preparations and lab-scale sublimations).
Any non-boron containing species like 3 and 4 generated during analysis of 1 and 2 would
not be observable by B K-edge XAS, so the spectra obtained would be
attributed almost exclusively to these BH4-containing samples.
This could explain why the energies of the first two features do not
change appreciably over repeat scanning despite the changes in intensity.

To summarize, the B K-edge XAS data strongly
suggest that 1 and 2 undergo beam-induced
photodecomposition,
but the associated spectral changes deviate from those typically observed
for photodecomposition in other B K-edge studies. Two hypotheses were
presented to address the underlying origin of these differences, including
one that accounts for the potential identity of the photodecomposition
products. Unfortunately, it is difficult to rule out either hypothesis
completely with the data at hand, and it is possible that both contribute
at some level to the spectra collected after repeated beam exposure.
However, it seems the first hypothesis is the most likely. The increasing
intensity of features A and B as 1 and 2 are subjected to repeated scanning mirror the appearance of new
pre-edge features when NaBH4 was analyzed under identical
conditions. The identity of the boron-containing material that is
formed is not known, but generation of similar photodecomposition
products in scans of 1 and 2 would account
for the intensity increase in features A and B after extended beam
exposure.

Despite the appearance of beam-induced photodecomposition
at longer
scanning times, it appears we have sufficiently minimized photodecomposition
with the step scans and PZ shutter so that reliable comparisons can
be made between the two samples. The step scan data reveal spectra
for 1 and 2 that are similar in profile
and peak positions, which would not be expected if there were substantial
5f contributions to the metal-borohydride bonding. It is well known
that 5f orbitals drop in energy as the actinide series is traversed
from Th to U, whereas the 6d orbitals energies do not. This has been
illustrated in previous ligand K-edge XAS studies of isomorphous complexes
containing different actinides in the +4 oxidation state.
,
 The similarities in the B K-edge XAS spectra for 1 and 2 therefore suggest that any covalent metal-borohydride bonding
is dominated by An 6d contributions. This hypothesis is indeed corroborated
by supporting calculations described in the following section.

DFT and TDDFT Calculations

To evaluate the underlying
electronic structure of 1 and 2 and assign
the B K-edge XAS features, density functional theory (DFT) and time-dependent
density functional theory (TDDFT) computations were performed. The
ground state geometries of 1 and 2 were
first optimized using DFT, and 14 different functionals were tested
(see SI for Computational Details). In general, the calculated DFT geometries are in
good agreement with the experimental structures with most exhibiting
only minor functional dependence (Figure S32).

Before comparing the TDDFT results, it is instructive to
first discuss the orbitals available for B K-edge XAS transitions
in the ground states of 1 and 2 (). The calculations
reveal that the lowest unoccupied molecular orbitals (MOs) are relatively
nonbonding 5f with B 2p contributions of 1.4% or less (Tables S12–S13). Among the 5f orbitals
for 1 is the one assigned with the 1a Mulliken symbol
shown in .
Though group theory predicts the possibility of “through-space”
An-B σ and σ* MOs that could arise from mixing between
the actinide 5fz3 and the B 2p
z
, the actual calculations reveal that the 1a MO does not have significant
B 2p character (; right). However, we note its presence because it is sometimes invoked
in discussions of metal-borohydride bonding. The small amount of orbital mixing in the remaining 5f-derived MOs
for 1 and 2 appear to occur through the
An–H–B linkages (Tables S12–S13). Among those for 1 is MO 5a (). Though it contains negligible B 2p character
(0.2%), it is worth noting because it exhibits a small amount of M-H
mixing with Th (47.8% 5f; 11.2% 6d; 2.7% H 1s). A similar MO is observed
for 2 with U (4.4% H 1s; assigned as 3a in ), though this MO contains
significantly less 5f character and more 6d mixing (3.0% 5f; 29.1%
6d) due to the decrease in U 5f orbital energies compared to those
with Th.

The most important MOs with respect to the B K-edge
XAS studies
are two degenerate 6d-containing MOs of e symmetry (3e) that appear
at higher energy relative to the MOs of 5f parentage. These can be
described either as An ← H–B σ* or An–H–B
π* based on the relative arrangement of the M 6d and B 2p orbitals
(; right).
We use the latter An–H–B π* description based
on previous precedent
,
 and to better distinguish the
relative arrangement of the B 2p orbitals. These 6d-derived 3e MOs
have significantly more B 2p mixing compared to the MOs of 5f parentage:
B2p = 7.4%/7.3% for 1 (14.7% total) and B2p = 6.5%/6.6% to 2 (13.1% total). The 3e MOs
for 1 exhibit some 5f/6d hybridization
(Th6d/5f = 50.8%/8.6% each; ), but this decreases in 2 as the 5f orbitals drop in energy (U6d/5f = 56.4%/0.9% and 56.0%/1.0%). As is typically observed
when traversing the actinide series, the 5f orbitals drop significantly
in energy from Th to U, whereas the 6d orbitals are less perturbed.
We note that the calculations indicate that the An–H–B
π* MOs in 1 shift to higher energy by 0.05–0.07
eV for 2 using the PBE functional (the same trend is
also observed with B3LYP-D3). The difference in energy is consistent
with the 0.1 eV shift of feature B in the experimental spectra (190.7
eV in 1 to 190.8 eV in 2).

were performed to simulate the B K-edge XAS

were calculated using the TPSSh-D4 and B3LYP-D4 functionals.

Based on orbital decomposition analysis of the

shown in .
The relative similarities in peak positioning are consistent with

Rounding out the analysis, the higher energy features labeled C and

The TDDFT calculations
suggest that the B K-edge XAS spectra of 1 and 2 are dominated by B 1s → An–H–B
π* transitions involving 3e MOs of primarily 6d parentage, though
we note the 5f hybridization in 1 () also contributes to the Th–H–B
π* bonding. This is relevant when considering covalency-induced
measurement of NMR shifts.

Calculated NMR Shifts

The presence of NMR active nuclei
in (BH4)1– provided a rare opportunity
to compare covalency assessments made using B K-edge XAS to those
obtained using 1H and 11B NMR spectroscopy with
diamagnetic 1. To account for deshielding in the hydride
and boron resonance of 1, NMR shifts and chemical bonding
were calculated with DFT using the scalar relativistic (SR) and spin-orbit
(SO) all electron ZORA Hamiltonian.
−

 Based on literature precedent,
the PBE0 functional was employed (PBE results were similar; Table S14). The 1H and 11B chemical shifts were computed both with (δSO)
and without (δSR) the use of spin orbit (SO) effects,
using SiMe4 (1H) and BF3·Et2O (11B) as references ().

Calculations performed on the DFT optimized structure
of 1 yield distinct δSO and δSR values for the terminal (1Hterminal) and bridging
(1Hbridging) hydride positions. Averaging these
values yields a calculated hydride shift (1Havg) of δH = 4.09 ppm, which is in excellent agreement
with the experimental shift in C6D6 at δH = 4.25 ppm. The calculated average J
BH (90 Hz) also closely matches the experimental value (83
Hz). The deshielding induced by SO effects (ΔSO)
contributes 2.33 ppm to the downfield shift. While small, this value
points to a modest Th–H covalency, in line with natural localized
molecular orbital (NLMO) analysis (see below). Curiously, ΔSO for 1Hterminal (δ 3.76 ppm)
is larger than those observed for 1Hbridging (δ 1.86 ppm), despite its substantially longer distance from
the Th center, and negligible orbital overlap (see below). We hypothesize
that the aforementioned “through-space” An-B σ*
interaction is responsible for this effect; however, understanding
its exact origin will require further work.

The calculated 11B shift (δB = −13.68
ppm) is in acceptable agreement with experiment (δB = −8.0 ppm), considering the large errors often found for
calculated 11B chemical shifts. Moreover, the 11B shift showed a ΔSO of 7.72 ppm, suggesting some degree of orbital mixing between Th
and B, consistent with the results of the B K-edge XAS study. The 1H and 11B chemical shifts of the Zr­(PhBH3)4 and Hf­(PhBH3)4 complexes previously
reported by Hansen et al. were additionally
computed (Table S17). The ΔSO values were noted to increase from Zr to Hf in 1H (0.26,
0.83 ppm) and 11B (1.25, 3.03 ppm) calculations, as expected
given the increase in SO effects on going down the group. For further
comparison, a recently reported thallium κ2-borohydride
complex exhibited a negligible ΔSO of 0.2 ppm for
its 11B chemical shift, due to the highly ionic Tl-borohydride
interaction.

While the B K-edge
XAS measurements emphasize orbital mixing in
the antibonding orbitals, the orbitals of interest in the comparison
of DFT with NMR data focuses on the occupied orbitals (in this case
the NLMOs). Moreover, the NMR shielding tensors can be decomposed
in terms of the NLMOs.
,
 Specifically, the NLMOs calculated
for 1 highlight the orbital interactions between the
borohydride and Th responsible for the spin-orbit shifts (Figures S37–S38; SI). The three bridging hydrides (Hbridging) each show a σ-type interaction along the B–Hbridging bond, which weakly donates via the Hbridging into empty orbitals on Th to yield the three-center, two-electron
Th–Hbridging-B bonds. On average, these three NLMOs
have atomic contributions with 6.7% from thorium, 42.0% from boron,
and 50.7% from hydrogen (Tables S23–S24; SI). Of the thorium contribution, 55%
is from 6d orbitals, while 39% is from 5f orbitals. While the thorium
contribution to the three NLMOs is somewhat smaller than that observed
for a typical Th–C bond, in line
with the small ΔSO value, the 6d/5f ratio is comparable. As expected,
the NLMO corresponding to the terminal hydride (Hterminal) shows a very small Th weight of only 0.5% (Tables S23–S24; SI).

QTAIM Calculations

As a final assessment of metal–ligand
(M-L) bonding in 1 and 2, quantum theory
of atoms in molecules (QTAIM)
−

 was used to obtain both the average
electron density (ρ) at bond critical points and the delocalization
indexes (δDI) between metal and hydrogen atoms (). Covalency in M-L
bonds can be influenced by changes in either orbital overlap or frontier

Concluding Remarks

In summary, we have described the
first investigation of actinide-borohydride
bonding using ligand K-edge XAS and NMR spectroscopy. The B K-edge
XAS spectra of An­(κ3-BH4)­(NR2)3 with An = Th (1) and U (2) and R = SiMe3 suggest appreciable An–H–B
π covalency involving the actinide 6d-orbitals with substantially
smaller contributions from the 5f-orbitals. The NMR results for 1 corroborate these results by revealing chemical shifts
attributed to small but measurable M-L covalency involving the 6d
and, to a lesser extent, 5f orbitals. These results also represent
the first instance of using 11B NMR spectroscopy and B
K-edge XAS to measure covalency in the actinides. Because B K-edge
XAS has not been used previously for evaluating metal-ligand covalency
in f-element borohydride complexes, the NMR studies provide independent
corroboration that demonstrates the viability of using boron as an
XAS reporter of three-center, two-electron An–H–B bonding.

The experimental results are tightly connected by two computationally
driven analyses: NLMOs that provide NMR shielding constants in 1 and NTOs and TDDFT calculations that corroborate the B K-edge
XAS results for 1 and 2. The relatively
smaller downfield chemical shift in 1 is a direct result
of a small δSO and ultimately the small participation
of Th 5f orbitals. This is verified by XAS measurements and the subsequent
DFT and TDDFT results that indicate predominate 6d character in the
bonding. The results demonstrate how these complementary spectroscopies
can be combined with connecting theoretical methods to establish a
holistic assessment of 5f and 6d orbital mixing in actinide-ligand
bonds. These findings open the door to similar comparative covalency
studies on the many complexes containing actinide-ligand bonds (e.g.,
An-C, An-F, An-Si, An-P) that are accessible using both ligand K-edge
XAS and NMR spectroscopy.
