A Helmholtz Equation for Surface Plasmon Polaritons on Curved Interfaces: Controlling Cooperativity with Geometric Potentials
Florian Bönsel, Flore K. Kunst

Abstract
Surface plasmon polaritons propagating along curved metal-dielectric interfaces experience geometry-induced modifications absent on flat surfaces. In this work, we derive a covariant, effective two-dimensional wave equation for the transverse magnetic surface plasmon mode on weakly curved smooth interfaces. By perturbatively expanding Maxwell’s equations with curvature-adapted boundary conditions, we find a Helmholtz equation with two geometric potential terms that enter at first order in the extrinsic curvature: an isotropic contribution proportional to the extrinsic curvature, and an anisotropic operator arising from the traceless part of the second fundamental form. These linear-in-curvature potentials distinguish convex from concave interfaces, in contrast to the quadratic potentials known from symmetrically confined systems such as dielectric waveguides. We show that our equation reproduces established results for spherical and cylindrical interfaces. We furthermore predict that the anisotropic contribution vanishes when the ratio of the material permittivities equals the square of the golden ratio. As an application, we demonstrate sign-dependent cooperative frequency shifts as well as a curvature-driven redistribution of superradiant and subradiant decay rates for a ring of quantum emitters on a curved metallic spheroid interacting through the surface plasmons.

II
Theory of surface plasmon polaritons and gauge conditions

The derivation of the curved-surface wave equation in Section
III
relies on the flat-surface SPP solution expressed in a gauge that generalizes to curved interfaces. In this section, we establish this solution and the associated gauge conditions.

Surface plasmon polaritons are electromagnetic surface modes arising from the coupling between the electromagnetic field and the collective oscillations of free electrons (plasmons) at a metal-dielectric interface
60
;
68
. They are solutions to Maxwell’s equations and require the real part of the permittivity to change sign across the interface boundary (
\mathrm{Re}\left[\epsilon_{m}\right]<0<\epsilon_{d}
), in particular, these modes exist when
\mathrm{Re}\left[\epsilon_{m}\right]<-\epsilon_{d}
. Denoting by
\eta
the coordinate normal to the interface, the permittivity profile reads

\epsilon(\eta)=\begin{cases}\epsilon_{d}&\text{for }\eta>0\quad(\text{dielectric})\\
\epsilon_{m}&\text{for }\eta<0\quad(\text{metal})\end{cases}.

(1)

We take non-magnetic materials, and for now, assume real values of
\epsilon_{m}
with later remarks on the case of a lossy metal. We also assume the dielectric permittivity
\epsilon_{d}
to be real throughout this work, which is a very good approximation in the visible regime
27
;
38
. For a flat interface, the dispersion relation of the transverse magnetic SPP mode reads

k_{\rm spp}=k_{0}n_{e}=k_{0}\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon_{m}\epsilon_{d}}{\epsilon_{m}+\epsilon_{d}}},

(2)

with
k_{0}
the vacuum wave number, and
n_{e}=\sqrt{\epsilon_{m}\epsilon_{d}/(\epsilon_{m}+\epsilon_{d})}
the effective index of the mode. The SPP field decays exponentially away from the interface with spatial decay rates

\kappa_{d,m}=\sqrt{k_{\rm spp}^{2}-\epsilon_{d,m}k_{0}^{2}}=\frac{k_{0}\,|\epsilon_{d,m}|}{\sqrt{-(\epsilon_{m}+\epsilon_{d})}},

(3)

satisfying the identity
\kappa_{d}\kappa_{m}=k_{\rm spp}^{2}
. Since
\kappa_{d,m}
and
k_{\rm spp}
are related through this identity, the spatial structure of the SPP mode, both along and perpendicular to the interface, is governed by the single scale
k_{\rm spp}
. This observation will underpin the perturbative expansion in Section
III
, where the ratio
H/k_{\rm spp}
of the surface extrinsic curvature
H
to this flat SPP length scale serves as a physical, small parameter.

In this work, we use a vector potential formalism, whereas most literature provides the physical fields instead. Our choice to study the vector potentials is motivated by the fact that it allows residual gauge freedom in the mathematical treatment. For the flat interface, the fields for the transverse magnetic (TM) SPP mode are given as Eqs. (2.10) and (2.11)

For what follows, two more properties of this flat-interface vector potential are important. First, the normal component is separable as

\left[\Delta+k^{2}_{\rm spp}\right]\psi=0

III
A surface Helmholtz equation for SPPs on curved interfaces

Having established the flat-surface SPP solution, we now derive a generalized wave equation for the TM SPP mode on a weakly curved metal-dielectric interface. The strategy is as follows. We start from Maxwell’s equations in the Lorenz gauge, written in a covariant formulation adapted to the curved interface, and split them into governing equations for the normal and tangential vector field components. Unlike the thin-layer limit employed in studies of light in curved dielectrics or quantum particles in confined geometries
19
;
7
, we retain a non-zero normal coordinate
\eta
, which is essential for capturing the asymmetric penetration of the SPP field into the metal and dielectric. We then perform a perturbative expansion in weak curvature and apply Maxwell’s boundary conditions in a curvature-compatible form to arrive at an effective wave equation for the SPP envelope
\psi
on the interface.

III.1
Covariant wave equations under weak curvature

Starting from Maxwell’s equations in the Lorenz gauge, we derive in Appendix
A
the coupled wave equations for the rescaled vector field components
\mathcal{A}^{i}=\sqrt{\Omega}\,A^{i}
on a curved interface with
\Omega=1-2H\eta

H

\gamma_{ab}
is the intrinsic 2D metric of the interface and
h_{ab}
the second fundamental form.

We adopt the convention that
\eta=0

(8)

III.2
Boundary conditions under curvature

Due to the curvature of the interface, the boundary conditions for the rescaled field
\mathcal{A}
differ from those of a flat interface
60

\phi=\nabla\cdot\mathbf{\mathcal{A}}/\left(i\omega\epsilon\mu\right)

\eta=0

\frac{1}{\epsilon_{d}}\nabla_{j}A^{j}_{d}=\frac{1}{\epsilon_{m}}\nabla_{j}A^{j}_{m}.

(12)

Equivalently, using the covariant divergence
\nabla_{j}A^{j}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{g}}\partial_{i}(\sqrt{g}A^{i})

\frac{\partial_{\eta}\mathcal{A}_{m}^{\eta}}{\epsilon_{m}}-\frac{\partial_{\eta}\mathcal{A}_{d}^{\eta}}{\epsilon_{d}}\Bigg|_{{\eta}=0}=\left(H\mathcal{A}^{\eta}-\nabla_{a}\mathcal{A}^{a}\right)\Bigg|_{{\eta}=0}\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon_{m}}-\frac{1}{\epsilon_{d}}\right),

(13)

with the derivation given in Appendix
B

\nabla_{a}\mathcal{A}^{a}

III.3
Perturbative expansion and main result

We introduce the dimensionless parameter
\alpha
that tracks the order of curvature corrections. The physical expansion parameter is the dimensionless ratio
H/k_{\rm spp}=\bar{\lambda}_{\rm spp}/R
of the reduced SPP wavelength to the local radius of curvature, which we assign the order
\alpha
, i.e.,
H\sim\alpha\,k_{\rm spp}
. This ratio emerges naturally from the 3D metric
g_{ab}=\gamma_{ab}-2\eta\,h_{ab}
. Since
h_{ab}\sim H
, cf. Eq. (
11
), the change in the metric when leaving the surface is
\sim\eta\,H
and the SPP field samples the normal direction as
|\eta|\sim 1/k_{\rm spp}
, cf. Eq. (
3
), before decaying exponentially. So the effective perturbation experienced by the mode is of order
H/k_{\rm spp}
.

For the normal vector field component in the metal (
i=m
) and dielectric (
i=d
) domain, we use the ansatz

\mathcal{A}_{i}^{\eta}=e^{-\kappa_{i}|\eta|}\bigg[1+\alpha\left(A_{i}\eta^{2}+B_{i}\eta+C_{i}\right)\bigg]\left(\psi^{(0)}+\alpha\psi^{(1)}\right)

(14)

which we truncate after first order in
\alpha
, and which is in line with the TM mode expansion in Ref.
21
.

To also expand the operators in Eq. (
8
) in powers of
\alpha
, we note that we may choose coordinates, e.g., a Monge description, in which we can locally write
\gamma_{ab}=\delta_{ab}+\mathcal{O}(\alpha^{2})
and
\Gamma^{a}_{bc}=\mathcal{O}(\alpha)
, see Appendix
A
. As
h_{ab}=\mathcal{O}\left(\alpha\right)
, the corrections distinguishing
\Delta_{\gamma}
from
\Delta_{\rm flat}=\delta^{ab}\partial_{a}\partial_{b}
and
h^{ab}\nabla_{a}\nabla_{b}
from
h^{ab}\partial_{a}\partial_{b}
are beyond our
\mathcal{O}(\alpha)
working accuracy. The operator in the square brackets of Eq. (
8
) therefore expands as

\hat{L}\equiv\underbrace{\Delta_{\rm flat}+\partial_{\eta}^{2}+k_{0}^{2}\epsilon(\eta)}_{\hat{L}^{(0)}}+\alpha\underbrace{2\eta\mathcal{D}^{(1)}_{h}}_{\hat{L}^{(1)}},

(15)

with

\mathcal{D}^{(1)}_{h}\equiv H^{(1)}\Delta_{\rm flat}+\sigma^{ab\,(1)}\partial_{a}\partial_{b}.

(16)

Here,
H^{(1)}
and
\sigma^{ab\,(1)}
denote the extrinsic curvature and traceless second fundamental form, respectively, with the
\alpha
factored out, i.e.,
H=\alpha H^{(1)}
and
\sigma^{ab}=\alpha\sigma^{ab\,(1)}
.

The coupling term
2h^{a}_{b}\nabla_{a}\mathcal{A}^{b}
in Eq. (
8
) and the source term
2h^{ab}\partial_{b}\mathcal{A}^{\eta}
on the right-hand side of Eq. (
9
) describe the curvature-induced mixing between the normal and tangential field components. The term
C^{a}
in Eq. (
9
) describes mixing among the tangential components and involves only the intrinsic surface geometry through the Christoffel symbols, and scales at least as
\sim\mathcal{O}(\alpha)
. Since the flat-surface SPP has vanishing tangential vector field components
\mathcal{A}^{a\,(0)}=0
, cf. Section
II

We apply all these expansions to Eq. (
8
) and use Eq. (
9
), which we also expand in orders of
\alpha
, to derive the tangential divergence
\nabla_{a}\mathcal{A}^{a}
entering the boundary condition (
13
). The full derivation, including the order-by-order matching of the field ansatz and boundary conditions, is given in Appendices
D
and
E
.

The derivation yields the wave equation in terms of the flat-space operators
\Delta_{\rm flat}
and
\partial_{a}\partial_{b}
. To obtain an equation that can be applied in any surface coordinate system, including closed surfaces such as spheres and tori, we restore the covariant operators
\Delta_{\gamma}
and
\nabla_{a}\nabla_{b}
in place of their flat-space counterparts, consistent at
\mathcal{O}(\alpha)

The resulting wave equation for the curvature-perturbed TM SPP envelope
\psi
on the two-dimensional interface is then

\left[\Delta_{\gamma}+k_{\text{spp}}^{2}+V_{\rm H}+V_{\sigma}\right]\psi=0,

(17)

with two geometry-induced potential terms accounting for the isotropic and anisotropic curvature contributions,

V_{\rm H}=\frac{k_{0}(\epsilon_{d}^{2}+\epsilon_{d}\epsilon_{m}+\epsilon_{m}^{2})}{(\epsilon_{d}+\epsilon_{m})\sqrt{-(\epsilon_{d}+\epsilon_{m})}}H\equiv C_{H}H,

(18)

and

V_{\sigma}=-\frac{\epsilon_{d}^{2}+3\epsilon_{d}\epsilon_{m}+\epsilon_{m}^{2}}{k_{0}\epsilon_{d}\epsilon_{m}\sqrt{-(\epsilon_{d}+\epsilon_{m})}}\sigma^{ab}\nabla_{a}\nabla_{b}\equiv C_{\sigma}\sigma^{ab}\nabla_{a}\nabla_{b},

(19)

respectively. Eq. (
17
) and the two geometric potentials are the main result of this work.

III.4
Remarks on the derived equation

Eq. (
17
) provides an effective two-dimensional Helmholtz equation for SPPs on a weakly curved metal-dielectric interface. The two geometric potentials
V_{H}
and
V_{\sigma}
arise from the finite and asymmetric penetration of the SPP field into the metal and dielectric, and therefore depend on the extrinsic curvature of the interface. This is in contrast to the geometric potentials found for systems confined to thin layers
19
;
7
;
77
;
12
. As a result, SPP modes become sensitive to whether the surface is bent convexly or concavely.

The prefactor
C_{H}
of the mean-curvature potential
V_{H}
is negative for any material combination allowing SPPs. A convex metallic domain (
H<0
) therefore produces a blue-shift of the SPP dispersion, while a concave interface (
H>0
) yields a red-shift, consistent with reports for the sphere and cylinder
56
;
2
;
83
.

The anisotropic potential
V_{\sigma}
vanishes on any isotropic surface, such as a sphere or a flat interface, and is nonzero whenever the two principal curvatures differ. In principal curvature coordinates, a plane-wave ansatz
\psi\sim e^{ik_{a}q^{a}}
yields
\sigma^{ab}\nabla_{a}\nabla_{b}\psi\propto-(\kappa_{1}-\kappa_{2})(k_{1}^{2}-k_{2}^{2})\psi
, showing that the SPP acquires direction-dependent momentum, cf. Fig.
1
(b). This is an effective curvature-induced birefringence.

The prefactor
C_{\sigma}
changes sign when
\epsilon_{d}^{2}+3\epsilon_{d}\epsilon_{m}+\epsilon_{m}^{2}=0
, which occurs at the permittivity ratio

\epsilon_{m}=-\frac{3+\sqrt{5}}{2}\,\epsilon_{d}=-\Phi^{2}\,\epsilon_{d},

(20)

where
\Phi
is the golden ratio and the negative root is retained to satisfy the SPP existence condition. At this special ratio,
V_{\sigma}
vanishes and Eq. (
17
) reduces to
[\Delta_{\gamma}+k_{\rm spp}^{2}+V_{H}]\psi=0
. In that case, the surface appears isotropic to the SPP regardless of its actual geometry. Support for this prediction comes from Ref.
83
, where the authors report, for a metal cylinder, that the axial and azimuthal SPP wavenumbers coincide when
(\epsilon_{m}+\epsilon_{d})^{2}=-\epsilon_{m}\epsilon_{d}
, which is equivalent to Eq. (
20
). Our framework identifies this as a general phenomenon, not restricted to cylindrical geometries. A surface of particular interest in this context is the catenoid. As a minimal surface, it has
H=0
at every point, so when the permittivity ratio additionally satisfies Eq. (
20
), both
V_{H}
and
V_{\sigma}
vanish and Eq. (
17
) reduces to
[\Delta_{\gamma}+k_{\rm spp}^{2}]\psi=0
. The SPP dispersion is then identical to that on a flat interface, with curvature entering only through the Laplace-Beltrami operator.

Two limiting regimes are worth noting. In the SPP resonance limit
\epsilon_{m}\to-\epsilon_{d}
, both
C_{H}
and
C_{\sigma}
diverge, consistent with the literature
2
;
21
;
70
, signaling the breakdown of the perturbative expansion near resonance. In the perfect metal limit
\epsilon_{m}\to-\infty
, both prefactors grow as
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{|\epsilon_{m}|})
, again indicating the failure of the weak-curvature framework.

Finally, we estimate the regime where the derived potential terms dominate over a quadratic correction. For this, we write the toy equation
\left[\Delta_{\gamma}+k_{\rm spp}^{2}+C_{H}H+H^{2}\right]\psi\penalty\ =\penalty\ 0
. The fraction of the total curvature correction attributable to the linear term is
1/\left(1+|H|/|C_{H}|\right)
. For an interface between silver (
\mathrm{Re}\left[\epsilon_{m}\right]=-16.12
) and air (
\epsilon_{d}=1
) at
\lambda_{0}=600\,\mathrm{nm}

43
, we have
C_{H}\penalty\ \approx\penalty\ -0.044\,\mathrm{nm}^{-1}
. The linear term accounts for
90\%
of the curvature correction in the toy equation when
R\gtrsim 2.2\bar{\lambda}_{\rm spp}
. Therefore, we assume that for any macroscopic radius of curvature, the linear potential derived in this work is the dominant geometric effect. Since this toy equation may underestimate the true second-order effects of curvature, we use much larger curvature radii when we apply Eq. (
17
) in Section
V
, where we analyze SPP-mediated interaction.

So far, we assumed real permittivity values
\epsilon_{m}
, yielding a Hermitian operator in Eq. (
17
). Real metals, however, exhibit Ohmic losses described by a complex permittivity
\epsilon_{m}=\epsilon_{m}^{\prime}+i\epsilon_{m}^{\prime\prime}
, where the real part is negative and dominant and the imaginary part is positive and small (
0<\epsilon_{m}^{\prime\prime}\ll|\epsilon_{m}^{\prime}|
) at optical frequencies. We treat the imaginary part as a perturbation. Expanding the flat-surface SPP wavenumber (
2
) to first order in
\epsilon_{m}^{\prime\prime}
gives

k_{\text{spp}}^{2}\approx k_{0}^{2}\frac{\epsilon_{d}\epsilon_{m}^{\prime}}{\epsilon_{d}+\epsilon_{m}^{\prime}}+ik_{0}^{2}\frac{\epsilon_{d}^{2}\epsilon_{m}^{\prime\prime}}{(\epsilon_{d}+\epsilon_{m}^{\prime})^{2}}\equiv(k_{\text{spp}}^{\prime})^{2}+iK_{\text{loss}},

(21)

where
K_{\text{loss}}
represents the spatially uniform Ohmic damping of an SPP on a flat interface. The curvature coefficients
C_{H}
and
C_{\sigma}
, being functions of
\epsilon_{m}
, likewise acquire imaginary parts when evaluated at the complex permittivity. Expanding around
\epsilon_{m}^{\prime}
yields
C_{H}\approx C_{H}^{\prime}+iC_{H}^{\prime\prime}
and
C_{\sigma}\approx C_{\sigma}^{\prime}+iC_{\sigma}^{\prime\prime}
, with the imaginary corrections proportional to
\epsilon_{m}^{\prime\prime}
. Substituting into Eq. (
17
), we obtain the non-Hermitian wave equation

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\left[\Delta_{\gamma}+(k_{\text{spp}}^{\prime})^{2}+C_{H}^{\prime}H+C_{\sigma}^{\prime}\,\sigma^{ab}\nabla_{a}\nabla_{b}\right]\psi

(22)

\displaystyle+\,i\left[K_{\text{loss}}+C_{H}^{\prime\prime}H+C_{\sigma}^{\prime\prime}\,\sigma^{ab}\nabla_{a}\nabla_{b}\right]\psi=0.

The first bracket containing real numbers describes the coherent SPP dynamics on the lossless surface, whereas the second bracket governs the dissipation. The dominant dissipative contribution is the flat-interface Ohmic loss
K_{\rm loss}
. The curvature-dependent corrections
C_{H}^{\prime\prime}H
and
C_{\sigma}^{\prime\prime}\sigma^{ab}\nabla_{a}\nabla_{b}
are additionally suppressed by a factor of
H/k_{\rm spp}\sim\alpha
relative to
K_{\rm loss}
and therefore represent only weak, geometry-dependent modifications. The term
C_{H}^{\prime\prime}H
introduces a spatially inhomogeneous damping: regions of stronger extrinsic curvature experience enhanced or reduced dissipation depending on the sign of
H
. The term
C_{\sigma}^{\prime\prime}\,\sigma^{ab}\nabla_{a}\nabla_{b}
represents anisotropic damping, where the decay rate of the SPP depends on its propagation direction relative to the principal curvature axes. As a consequence, the eigenvalue spectrum of the curved lossy system is complex, requiring a non-Hermitian treatment
4
;
10
in general.

IV
Consistency with prior work

We now demonstrate the generality of Eq. (
17
) by applying it to the geometries of a sphere and a cylinder. We show how well-established results in the literature emerge from our 2D effective wave equation (
17
) through inserting the corresponding geometric ingredients, such as extrinsic curvature
H
and the anisotropy tensor
\sigma^{ab}
.

IV.1
Spherical interfaces

We consider a convex metal sphere of radius
R
embedded in a dielectric. A spherical surface has isotropic curvature with
\sigma^{ab}=0
and extrinsic curvature
H=-1/R
. The anisotropic potential
V_{\sigma}
therefore vanishes, and Eq. (
17
) reduces to
[\Delta_{\gamma}+k_{\rm eff}^{2}]\psi=0
with

k_{\rm eff}\approx k_{\rm spp}\left(1-\frac{\epsilon_{d}^{2}+\epsilon_{d}\epsilon_{m}+\epsilon_{m}^{2}}{2k_{0}R\,\epsilon_{m}\epsilon_{d}\sqrt{-(\epsilon_{d}+\epsilon_{m})}}\right),

(23)

where we used
\sqrt{1+x}\approx 1+x/2
. Since
\epsilon_{d}\epsilon_{m}<0
and
\epsilon_{d}^{2}+\epsilon_{d}\epsilon_{m}+\epsilon_{m}^{2}>0
, the correction increases
k_{\rm eff}
relative to
k_{\rm spp}
, corresponding to a curvature-induced blue-shift on the convex interface. Our Eq. (
23
) agrees exactly with what Ref.
70
would predict for the sphere. It also captures the result in Ref.
2
, where the same blue-shift is predicted but with a simpler material prefactor as
k_{\rm eff}\approx k_{\rm spp}(1+1/[2k_{0}R\sqrt{-(\epsilon_{d}+\epsilon_{m})}])
.

IV.2
Cylindrical interfaces

IV.2.1
Effective Schrödinger equation in the paraxial limit

In this example, we consider a cylindrical metal-dielectric interface with SPPs propagating predominantly along the cylinder axis, as studied in Ref.
21
. The cylinder has radius
R
and the surface is parametrized by the coordinate pair
(z,s)
with
z
the axis coordinate and
s
the arc length around the circumference.
The intrinsic metric is flat (
\gamma_{ab}=\delta_{ab}
,
\Gamma^{a}_{bc}=0
), the extrinsic curvature is
H=-1/(2R)
, and the traceless part of the second fundamental form has components
\sigma^{zz}=1/(2R)
and
\sigma^{ss}=-1/(2R)
. The operators in Eq. (
17
) therefore evaluate to

\Delta_{\gamma}=\partial_{z}^{2}+\partial_{s}^{2},\quad V_{\sigma}=C_{\sigma}\,\sigma^{ab}\partial_{a}\partial_{b}=\frac{C_{\sigma}}{2R}\left(\partial_{z}^{2}-\partial_{s}^{2}\right),

(24)

and the full wave equation on the cylinder reads

\left(1+\frac{C_{\sigma}}{2R}\right)\partial_{z}^{2}\psi+\left(1-\frac{C_{\sigma}}{2R}\right)\partial_{s}^{2}\psi+\left(k_{\rm spp}^{2}-\frac{C_{H}}{2R}\right)\psi=0.

(25)

We apply the ansatz
\psi(z,s)=F(z,s)\,e^{-ik_{\rm spp}z}
for a mode propagating predominantly along the cylinder axis, with
F
a slowly varying envelope. We substitute this into Eq. (
25
), cancel the common exponential, divide by the prefactor of the first term, and apply the paraxial approximation, i.e., we drop
\partial^{2}_{z}F
, so that we find

-2ik_{\rm spp}\partial_{z}F+\left(1-\frac{C_{\sigma}}{R}\right)\partial_{s}^{2}F-\frac{C_{H}+C_{\sigma}\,k_{\rm spp}^{2}}{2R}F=0.

(26)

Assuming that the change of the envelope along the parameter
s
is already weak, in that
\partial^{2}_{s}F\sim\alpha
, we drop the additional
C_{\sigma}/R\sim\alpha
prefactor.
The result then reads

-i\lambdabar_{0}\,\partial_{z}F=-\frac{\lambdabar_{0}^{2}}{2n_{e}}\,\partial_{s}^{2}F-\frac{\lambdabar_{0}\,n_{e}}{2R\sqrt{-(\epsilon_{m}+\epsilon_{d})}}\,F,

(27)

with
\lambdabar_{0}=1/k_{0}
, and is a Schrödinger-like equation with a curvature-induced potential. This equation is, up to sign conventions of the curvature, identical to the one provided in Ref.
21
, Eq. (9).

IV.2.2
Curvature-induced geometric momenta

In this part, we show that our Eq. (
17
) also captures the findings in Ref.
83
, where the SPP wavenumber dependence on the propagation direction of the mode on a metal-dielectric cylinder interface was studied.
We use the ansatz
\psi(z,\theta)=e^{ik_{z}z}e^{im\theta}
and that the azimuthal wave number is quantized as
k_{\theta}=m/R
. The operators read

\Delta_{\gamma}=\partial_{z}^{2}+\frac{1}{R^{2}}\partial_{\theta}^{2},\quad\sigma^{ab}\partial_{a}\partial_{b}=\pm\frac{1}{2R}\partial_{z}^{2}\mp\frac{1}{2R^{3}}\partial_{\theta}^{2},

(28)

with the upper and lower sign meaning the convex metal cylinder and concave dielectric pin, respectively. Eq. (
17
) can be solved for
k_{z}^{2}
to get the momentum ellipse

k_{z}^{2}\left(1\pm\frac{C_{\sigma}}{2R}\right)+k_{\theta}^{2}\left(1\mp\frac{C_{\sigma}}{2R}\right)=k_{\text{spp}}^{2}\mp\frac{C_{H}}{2R},

(29)

which, as mentioned already in Section
III.4
, defines a circle for the magic permittivity ratio Eq. (
20
). After a Taylor approximation, we can write this as

k^{2}_{z}(m)=k^{2}_{\rm spp}\left(1\pm\frac{1}{V}\right)-\frac{m^{2}}{R^{2}},

(30)

with the dimensionless parameter
V=k_{0}R\sqrt{-(\epsilon_{d}+\epsilon_{m})}
. This matches Eq. (5) in Ref.
83
when also there a Taylor expansion is applied and terms
\sim 1/R^{3}
are dropped. If the SPP propagates solely along the azimuthal direction, i.e.,
k_{z}=0
, we predict

k_{\theta}=k_{\text{spp}}\sqrt{1\mp\frac{(\epsilon_{d}+\epsilon_{m})^{2}}{\epsilon_{d}\epsilon_{m}}\frac{1}{V}}.

(31)

The case of the convex metal cylinder matches again the predictions in Ref.
83
, Eq. (32). For the concave case, we predict the same sign under the square-root as in Ref.
83
, while there, the material prefactor reads
(\epsilon_{m}^{2}+\epsilon_{d}^{2})(\epsilon_{m}+\epsilon_{d})^{2}/\left(\epsilon_{d}\epsilon_{m}(\epsilon_{m}^{2}-\epsilon_{d}^{2})\right)>0
. This difference may be explained as follows. Our framework considers one interface from which the SPP decays exponentially into the metal and dielectric. The case of a dielectric pin hole can locally be viewed as a metal-dielectric-metal layer, meaning that the SPP mode cannot simply decay to zero infinitely far away from the metal. This represents a setting which requires more intricate boundary conditions and mode matching in the middle of the dielectric part, which our approach does not cover. We note that this is not in contradiction to a general concave metal-dielectric interface with only a dielectric domain for all
\eta>0
, such as the previously discussed spherical case. The additional factor is close to unity for typical noble metals and becomes important only close to the SPP resonance, which is indeed outside of our working regime, see Section
III.4
.

VI
Conclusions

We derived an effective covariant wave equation describing the transverse magnetic surface plasmon polariton mode on weakly curved metal-dielectric interfaces. Starting from Maxwell’s equations with curvature-modified boundary conditions, the full electromagnetic problem reduces to a scalar Helmholtz equation defined on the surface. The resulting operator contains two geometry-induced contributions: a scalar potential
V_{H}\propto H
proportional to the extrinsic curvature and an anisotropic operator
V_{\sigma}\propto\sigma^{ab}\nabla_{a}\nabla_{b}
originating from the traceless part of the extrinsic curvature tensor. The latter acts as a curvature-induced birefringence for the SPP mode. Both terms are, to their lowest order, first order in curvature, which is in sharp contrast with the quadratic curvature potentials known from light confined to thin dielectric waveguides
7
;
12
or quantum particles in thin layers
19
, and reflects the asymmetric confinement of the SPP field across the interface. We verified that the derived equation reproduces known results for highly symmetric geometries such as the sphere
2
;
70
and the cylinder
83
;
70
;
21
. Our theory further predicts that the anisotropic contribution vanishes if the permittivity ratio satisfies
\epsilon_{m}=-\Phi^{2}\epsilon_{d}
, with
\Phi
the golden ratio. This condition was confirmed for the particular case of the cylindrical geometry
83
. More broadly, the sensitivity of the geometric potentials to
\epsilon_{d}
suggests that any continuous tuning of the dielectric environment, for instance through liquid crystals
80
;
63
, could modulate the SPP dynamics without altering the surface shape, opening a route to reconfigurable curvilinear plasmonics.

Compared with previous treatments based on ray optics or asymptotic propagation along geodesics
70
;
21
, the present formulation provides a surface wave equation that captures the full two-dimensional SPP dynamics. It therefore allows one to study eigenmodes, scattering, and interference on arbitrary smooth interfaces, while naturally including forward propagation as well. In contrast to transformation optics, which treats the SPP as being on a two-dimensional sheet with zero transverse extent
94
;
95
, the first-order curvature effects derived here originate from the finite and asymmetric penetration of the SPP field into the metal and dielectric, sampling the curved geometry on both sides of the interface differently.

As an application, we investigated how curvature modifies SPP-mediated collective emission. For a ring of emitters near the pole of a metallic spheroid in close proximity to the metal surface, we found that macroscopic curvature redistributes superradiant and subradiant decay channels and produces curvature-dependent cooperative frequency shifts. Convex and concave geometries influence the collective dynamics differently, providing an additional degree of control for plasmon-mediated light-matter interactions, complementing recent descriptions of photon-mediated collective behavior insensitive to the sign of the extrinsic curvature
12
.

Looking forward, the framework established here provides a theoretical tool for applications in curvilinear plasmonics. The geometry-induced potentials appearing in our surface wave equation can be harnessed to analytically engineer reflection and localization landscapes for SPPs propagating across topographically varying metal-dielectric surfaces. Combining this continuous macroscopic curvature with microstructured plasmonic elements
33
or metasurfaces
48
;
93
opens theoretical avenues for understanding advanced surface-wave scattering. A possible extension of our formulation could also be multilayer plasmonic systems
18
with more elaborate boundary condition matching. We think this work further leverages spatial curvature as a deterministic design parameter for plasmonic architectures.

\mathcal{S}
is curved, the 3D ambient space is flat, so
R^{i}_{m}=0
with the spatial indices
i\in\{1,2,3\}
. However, following the standard approach laid in Ref.
19
and introducing a coordinate system describing
\mathcal{S}
with two tangential coordinates and a normal coordinate, will reveal the impact of the curved constraint geometry on the SPP physics.

The Christoffel symbols are defined as

\Gamma^{\alpha}_{\mu\nu}=\frac{1}{2}g^{\alpha\beta}\left(\partial_{\mu}g_{\beta\nu}+\partial_{\nu}g_{\mu\beta}-\partial_{\beta}g_{\mu\nu}\right)

(42)

within a coordinate-basis framework. We assume a static metric, i.e.,

g_{0i}=0,\qquad\partial_{0}g_{ik}=0,\qquad\partial_{i}g_{00}=0.

(43)

This means that all Christoffel symbols, except the purely spatial ones, vanish. Due to the metric-compatibility (
\nabla_{i}g^{jk}=0
)
22
, we can write

-\nabla_{\beta}\nabla^{\beta}A^{\alpha}=-g^{\beta\mu}\nabla_{\beta}\nabla_{\mu}A^{\alpha}.

(44)

Expanding this yields

\displaystyle-g^{\beta\mu}\nabla_{\beta}\nabla_{\mu}A^{\alpha}=

\displaystyle-g^{\beta\mu}\partial_{\beta}\partial_{\mu}A^{\alpha}-g^{\beta\mu}(\partial_{\beta}{\Gamma^{\alpha}}_{\sigma\mu})A^{\sigma}-g^{\beta\mu}{\Gamma^{\alpha}}_{\sigma\mu}\partial_{\beta}A^{\sigma}-g^{\beta\mu}{\Gamma^{\alpha}}_{\rho\beta}\partial_{\mu}A^{\rho}

(45)

\displaystyle-g^{\beta\mu}{\Gamma^{\alpha}}_{\rho\beta}{\Gamma^{\rho}}_{\sigma\mu}A^{\sigma}+g^{\beta\mu}{\Gamma^{\lambda}}_{\mu\beta}\partial_{\lambda}A^{\alpha}+g^{\beta\mu}{\Gamma^{\lambda}}_{\mu\beta}{\Gamma^{\alpha}}_{\sigma\lambda}A^{\sigma}.

Splitting into temporal and spatial parts, we get

\displaystyle-g^{\beta\mu}\nabla_{\beta}\nabla_{\mu}A^{\alpha}=

\displaystyle-g^{00}\partial_{0}^{2}A^{\alpha}-g^{jk}\partial_{j}\partial_{k}A^{\alpha}-g^{jk}(\partial_{j}\Gamma^{\alpha}_{km})A^{m}-g^{jk}\Gamma^{\alpha}_{km}\partial_{j}A^{m}-g^{jk}\Gamma^{\alpha}_{jm}\partial_{k}A^{m}

(46)

\displaystyle-g^{jk}\Gamma^{\alpha}_{jl}\Gamma^{l}_{km}A^{m}+g^{jk}\Gamma^{m}_{jk}\partial_{m}A^{\alpha}+g^{jk}\Gamma^{m}_{jk}\Gamma^{\alpha}_{ml}A^{l}

as every Christoffel symbol with an index
0
vanishes. In the following, we look at
\alpha=i
. For a monochromatic ansatz
A^{\alpha}(t,\mathbf{r})=\tilde{A}^{\alpha}(\mathbf{r})e^{-i\omega t}
and the refractive index of the medium
n_{0}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{-g_{00}}}
, the first term becomes

-g^{00}\partial_{0}^{2}A^{i}=g^{00}\omega^{2}A^{i}=-n^{2}_{0}\omega^{2}A^{i}=-k^{2}_{0}n^{2}_{0}A^{i}.

(47)

The second and seventh term are the negative of the scalar Laplacian

-g^{jk}\partial_{j}\partial_{k}A^{i}+g^{jk}\Gamma^{m}_{jk}\partial_{m}A^{i}=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{g}}\partial_{j}\!\left(\sqrt{g}\,g^{jk}\partial_{k}A^{i}\right)=-\Delta A^{i},

(48)

and the third, fourth, and eigth term can be summed as

-g^{jk}(\partial_{j}\Gamma^{i}_{km})A^{m}-g^{jk}\Gamma^{i}_{km}\partial_{j}A^{m}+g^{jk}\Gamma^{m}_{jk}\Gamma^{i}_{ml}A^{l}=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{g}}\partial_{j}\left(\sqrt{g}g^{jk}\Gamma^{i}_{kl}A^{l}\right)

(49)

Assembling all terms, multiplying with
-1
, we can write the wave equation as

\mathcal{L}^{i}=-\mu_{0}J^{i},

(50)

with

\mathcal{L}^{i}\equiv\underbrace{\left(\Delta+k^{2}_{0}\epsilon\right)A^{i}}_{\rm Term\,I}+\underbrace{\frac{1}{\sqrt{g}}\partial_{j}\left(\sqrt{g}g^{jk}\Gamma^{i}_{kl}A^{l}\right)+g^{jk}\Gamma^{i}_{jm}\partial_{k}A^{m}+g^{jk}\Gamma^{i}_{jl}\Gamma^{l}_{km}A^{m}}_{\rm Term\,II},

(51)

and
n_{0}^{2}=\epsilon
.
Applying now the parametrization of the surface
\mathcal{S}
, we can follow Ref.
19
and introduce
\mathbf{R}(q^{1},q^{2},\eta)=\mathbf{r}(q^{1},q^{2})+\eta\,\mathbf{n}(q^{1},q^{2})
. Then, the metric can be written as

g_{ab}=\gamma_{ab}-2\eta h_{ab},\qquad g_{\eta\eta}=1,\qquad g_{a\eta}=0,

(52)

since we truncate all terms at linear order in curvature, with
a\in\{1,2\}
the tangential indices. The inverse metric reads
g^{ab}\approx\gamma^{ab}+2\eta h^{ab}
and the volume element is
\sqrt{g}=\sqrt{\gamma}\Omega
with
\Omega\approx 1-2\eta H
.
Throughout this work, we keep
\eta
, while dropping any terms quadratic in the curvature (such as
H^{2}
,
Hh_{ab}
, or
h_{ac}h^{c}_{b}
) as well as the spatial derivatives of the curvature (
\partial_{b}H\to 0
,
\partial_{b}h_{cd}\to 0
), cf. Ref.
7
. Operators or symbols with only indices
a,b,c,d,...
are defined intrinsically on the surface. We now calculate the Christoffel symbols required for the wave equation using
\partial_{\eta}g_{ab}=-2h_{ab}
. They are calculated to

\displaystyle\Gamma^{\eta}_{ab}=h_{ab},

(53)

\displaystyle\Gamma^{a}_{\eta b}=\Gamma^{a}_{b\eta}=-h^{a}_{b},

\displaystyle\Gamma^{a}_{bc}=\tilde{\Gamma}^{a}_{bc}+2\eta h^{ad}\gamma_{de}\Gamma^{e}_{bc}\approx\tilde{\Gamma}^{a}_{bc},

with the 2D surface Christoffel symbols
\tilde{\Gamma}^{a}_{bc}\equiv\frac{1}{2}\gamma^{ad}(\partial_{b}\gamma_{dc}+\partial_{c}\gamma_{db}-\partial_{d}\gamma_{bc})
. In the following, however, we just write
\Gamma^{a}_{bc}
. All other symbols (
\Gamma^{\eta}_{\eta\eta}
,
\Gamma^{a}_{\eta\eta}
,
\Gamma^{\eta}_{\eta\eta}
) are zero. In the subsequent derivations, we make several times use of the Mainardi-Codazzi equations
\nabla_{b}h_{ac}=\nabla_{c}h_{ab}

22
to drop higher-order terms, such as
\nabla_{a}h^{ab}
, as

\displaystyle\nabla_{b}h_{ac}

\displaystyle=\nabla_{c}h_{ab}

(54)

\displaystyle\gamma^{ac}\nabla_{b}h_{ac}

\displaystyle=\gamma^{ac}\nabla_{c}h_{ab}

\displaystyle\nabla_{b}(\gamma^{ac}h_{ac})

\displaystyle=\nabla_{c}(\gamma^{ac}h_{ab})

\displaystyle\nabla_{b}(h^{a}_{a})

\displaystyle=\nabla_{c}(h^{c}_{b})

\displaystyle\nabla_{b}(2H)

\displaystyle=\nabla_{c}(h^{c}_{b})

\displaystyle 0\approx 2\partial_{b}H

\displaystyle=\nabla_{c}(h^{c}_{b}).

In line with the standard approaches for the physics on curved surfaces
19
, we use the rescaling of the field
A^{i}=\mathcal{A}^{i}/\sqrt{\Omega}
. In the next sections, we will need the following approximations

\partial_{k}A^{m}=\partial_{k}\left(\frac{\mathcal{A}^{m}}{\sqrt{\Omega}}\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\Omega}}\partial_{k}\mathcal{A}^{m}-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial_{k}\Omega}{\Omega^{3/2}}\mathcal{A}^{m}.

(55)

Using
\partial_{\eta}\Omega=-2H
and
\partial_{c}\Omega\approx 0
, we get

\partial_{c}A^{m}\approx\frac{1}{\sqrt{\Omega}}\partial_{c}\mathcal{A}^{m},\qquad\partial_{\eta}A^{m}\approx\frac{1}{\sqrt{\Omega}}\partial_{\eta}\mathcal{A}^{m}+H\mathcal{A}^{m}.

(56)

A.1
Term I

We start with the Laplacian in term I. We can split it into tangential and normal contributions as

\Delta A^{i}=\underbrace{\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma}}\partial_{a}\!\left(\sqrt{\gamma}\,g^{ab}\partial_{b}A^{i}\right)}_{\displaystyle\Delta_{\parallel}A^{i}}+\underbrace{\frac{1}{\Omega}\partial_{\eta}\!\left(\Omega\,\partial_{\eta}A^{i}\right)}_{\displaystyle\Delta_{\perp}A^{i}},

(57)

for which we already used that
\partial_{a}\Omega\approx 0
and that
\gamma
does not depend on
\eta
. We now expand the inverse metric
g^{ab}=\gamma^{ab}+2h^{ab}\eta+\mathcal{O}(\eta^{2})
and apply the rescaling of the vector field
A^{i}=\mathcal{A}^{i}/\sqrt{\Omega}
. The tangential part is

\Delta_{\parallel}A^{i}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma}}\partial_{a}\!\left(\sqrt{\gamma}\left(\gamma^{ab}+2\eta h^{ab}\right)\partial_{b}A^{i}\right)\approx\Delta_{\gamma}A^{i}+2\eta\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma}}\partial_{a}\!\left(\sqrt{\gamma}\,h^{ab}\partial_{b}A^{i}\right)

(58)

with
\Delta_{\gamma}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma}}\partial_{a}(\sqrt{\gamma}\,\gamma^{ab}\partial_{b})
as the Laplace-Beltrami operator based on the intrinsic surface metric. With the definition of the intrinsic surface divergence

\bar{\nabla}_{a}V^{a}\equiv\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma}}\partial_{a}\left(\sqrt{\gamma}\,V^{a}\right),

(59)

and identifying
V^{a}=h^{ab}\partial_{b}A^{i}
, since
A^{i}
is just a scalar on the 2D surface, we get

\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma}}\partial_{a}\!\left(\sqrt{\gamma}\,h^{ab}\partial_{b}A^{i}\right)=\nabla_{a}\left(h^{ab}\nabla_{b}\mathcal{A}^{i}\right)=\left(\nabla_{a}h^{ab}\right)\nabla_{b}A^{i}+h^{ab}\nabla_{a}\nabla_{b}\mathcal{A}^{i}\approx h^{ab}\nabla_{a}\nabla_{b}A^{i},

(60)

where we dropped
\nabla_{a}h^{ab}
using the Codazzi-Mainardi equations. The tangential Laplacian therefore reads

\sqrt{\Omega}\,\Delta_{\parallel}A^{i}=\left[\Delta_{\gamma}+2\eta\,h^{ab}\nabla_{a}\nabla_{b}\right]\mathcal{A}^{i}+\mathcal{O}(H^{2}).

(61)

For the normal part, we get

\sqrt{\Omega}\,\Delta_{\perp}A^{i}=\partial_{\eta}^{2}\mathcal{A}^{i}+\mathcal{O}(H^{2}).

(62)

Adding Eq. (
62
) with the tangential result of Eq. (
61
), yields

\sqrt{\Omega}\!\left(\Delta+k_{0}^{2}\epsilon(\eta)\right)\!A^{i}\approx\left[\Delta_{\gamma}+\partial_{\eta}^{2}+k_{0}^{2}\epsilon\right]\!\mathcal{A}^{i}+2\eta\,h^{ab}\nabla_{a}\nabla_{b}\mathcal{A}^{i}.

(63)

A.2
Term II

The first term of term II in Eq. (
50

\displaystyle T_{1}^{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Omega}\partial_{\eta}\left(\sqrt{\Omega}\Gamma^{i}_{\eta l}\mathcal{A}^{l}\right)+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma}\sqrt{\Omega}}\partial_{b}\left(\sqrt{\gamma}g^{bc}\Gamma^{i}_{cl}\mathcal{A}^{l}\right)

(64)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Omega}\partial_{\eta}\left[\sqrt{\Omega}\left(\Gamma^{i}_{\eta d}\mathcal{A}^{d}+\Gamma^{i}_{\eta\eta}\mathcal{A}^{\eta}\right)\right]+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma}\sqrt{\Omega}}\partial_{b}\left[\sqrt{\gamma}g^{bc}\left(\Gamma^{i}_{cd}\mathcal{A}^{d}+\Gamma^{i}_{c\eta}\mathcal{A}^{\eta}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Omega}\partial_{\eta}\left(\sqrt{\Omega}\Gamma^{i}_{\eta d}\mathcal{A}^{d}\right)+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma}\sqrt{\Omega}}\partial_{b}\left[\sqrt{\gamma}g^{bc}\left(\Gamma^{i}_{cd}\mathcal{A}^{d}+\Gamma^{i}_{\eta c}\mathcal{A}^{\eta}\right)\right]

Therefore,

\sqrt{\Omega}\,T_{1}^{i}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\Omega}}\partial_{\eta}\left(\sqrt{\Omega}\Gamma^{i}_{\eta d}\mathcal{A}^{d}\right)+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma}}\partial_{b}\left[\sqrt{\gamma}g^{bc}\left(\Gamma^{i}_{cd}\mathcal{A}^{d}+\Gamma^{i}_{\eta c}\mathcal{A}^{\eta}\right)\right].

(65)

Now for
i=a
, we get

\displaystyle\sqrt{\Omega}\,T_{1}^{a}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\Omega}}\partial_{\eta}\left(\sqrt{\Omega}(-h^{a}_{d})\mathcal{A}^{d}\right)+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma}}\partial_{b}\left[\sqrt{\gamma}g^{bc}\left(\Gamma^{a}_{cd}\mathcal{A}^{d}-h^{a}_{c}\mathcal{A}^{\eta}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=-\frac{h^{a}_{d}}{\sqrt{\Omega}}\partial_{\eta}\left(\sqrt{\Omega}\mathcal{A}^{d}\right)+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma}}\partial_{b}\left[\sqrt{\gamma}g^{bc}\left(\Gamma^{a}_{cd}\mathcal{A}^{d}-h^{a}_{c}\mathcal{A}^{\eta}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{\sqrt{\Omega}}\left(-h^{a}_{d}\partial_{\eta}\mathcal{A}^{d}\right)+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma}}\partial_{b}\left[\sqrt{\gamma}g^{bc}\left(\Gamma^{a}_{cd}\mathcal{A}^{d}-h^{a}_{c}\mathcal{A}^{\eta}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\approx-h^{a}_{d}\partial_{\eta}\mathcal{A}^{d}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma}}\partial_{b}\left[\sqrt{\gamma}g^{bc}\left(\Gamma^{a}_{cd}\mathcal{A}^{d}-h^{a}_{c}\mathcal{A}^{\eta}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=-h^{a}_{d}\partial_{\eta}\mathcal{A}^{d}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma}}\partial_{b}\left[\sqrt{\gamma}\left(\gamma^{bc}+2\eta h^{bc}\right)\left(\Gamma^{a}_{cd}\mathcal{A}^{d}-h^{a}_{c}\mathcal{A}^{\eta}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=-h^{a}_{d}\partial_{\eta}\mathcal{A}^{d}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma}}\partial_{b}\left[\sqrt{\gamma}\left(\gamma^{bc}\Gamma^{a}_{cd}\mathcal{A}^{d}-h^{ab}\mathcal{A}^{\eta}+2\eta h^{bc}\Gamma^{a}_{cd}\mathcal{A}^{d}\right)\right].

For the normal
i=\eta
component, we have

\displaystyle\sqrt{\Omega}\,T_{1}^{\eta}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\Omega}}\partial_{\eta}\left(\sqrt{\Omega}(0)\mathcal{A}^{d}\right)+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma}}\partial_{b}\left[\sqrt{\gamma}g^{bc}\left(h_{cd}\mathcal{A}^{d}+(0)\mathcal{A}^{\eta}\right)\right]

(67)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma}}\partial_{b}\left(\sqrt{\gamma}g^{bc}h_{cd}\mathcal{A}^{d}\right)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma}}\partial_{b}\left(\sqrt{\gamma}\gamma^{bc}h_{cd}\mathcal{A}^{d}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma}}\partial_{b}\left(\sqrt{\gamma}h^{b}_{d}\mathcal{A}^{d}\right)

\displaystyle=(\nabla_{b}h^{b}_{d})\mathcal{A}^{d}+h^{b}_{d}(\nabla_{b}\mathcal{A}^{d})

\displaystyle\approx h^{b}_{d}(\nabla_{b}\mathcal{A}^{d}).

We now continue with the second term of term II in Eq. (

\displaystyle T_{2}^{i}

\displaystyle=g^{jk}\Gamma^{i}_{jm}\partial_{k}A^{m}

\displaystyle=\left(\Gamma^{i}_{\eta d}\partial_{\eta}A^{d}+\Gamma^{i}_{\eta\eta}\partial_{\eta}A^{\eta}\right)+g^{bc}\left(\Gamma^{i}_{bd}\partial_{c}A^{d}+\Gamma^{i}_{b\eta}\partial_{c}A^{\eta}\right)

We set
i=\eta

\displaystyle\sqrt{\Omega}\,T_{2}^{\eta}

\displaystyle\approx-h^{a}_{d}\partial_{\eta}\mathcal{A}^{d}+\gamma^{bc}\Gamma^{a}_{bd}\partial_{c}\mathcal{A}^{d}+2\eta h^{bc}\Gamma^{a}_{bd}\partial_{c}\mathcal{A}^{d}-h^{ac}\partial_{c}\mathcal{A}^{\eta}.

The third term of term II in Eq. (

\displaystyle\sqrt{\Omega}\,T_{3}^{i}

\displaystyle=g^{jk}\Gamma^{i}_{jl}\Gamma^{l}_{km}\mathcal{A}^{m}

Final wave equation for
i=\eta

\displaystyle S_{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma}}\partial_{b}\left[\sqrt{\gamma}\left(\gamma^{bc}\Gamma^{a}_{cd}\mathcal{A}^{d}+2\eta h^{bc}\Gamma^{a}_{cd}\mathcal{A}^{d}\right)\right]

\displaystyle+\gamma^{bc}\Gamma^{a}_{bd}\partial_{c}\mathcal{A}^{d}+2\eta h^{bc}\Gamma^{a}_{bd}\partial_{c}\mathcal{A}^{d}

\displaystyle+\gamma^{bc}\Gamma^{a}_{bd}\Gamma^{d}_{ce}\mathcal{A}^{e}+2\eta h^{bc}\Gamma^{a}_{bd}\Gamma^{d}_{ce}\mathcal{A}^{e}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma}}\partial_{b}\left[\sqrt{\gamma}\left(\gamma^{bc}+2\eta h^{bc}\right)\Gamma^{a}_{cd}\mathcal{A}^{d}\right]+\left(\gamma^{bc}+2\eta h^{bc}\right)\Gamma^{a}_{bd}\partial_{c}\mathcal{A}^{d}+\left(\gamma^{bc}+2\eta h^{bc}\right)\Gamma^{a}_{bd}\Gamma^{d}_{ce}\mathcal{A}^{e}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma}}\partial_{b}\left(\sqrt{\gamma}g^{bc}\Gamma^{a}_{cd}\mathcal{A}^{d}\right)+g^{bc}\Gamma^{a}_{bd}\left(\partial_{c}\mathcal{A}^{d}+\Gamma^{d}_{ce}\mathcal{A}^{e}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma}}\partial_{b}\left(\sqrt{\gamma}g^{bc}\Gamma^{a}_{cd}\mathcal{A}^{d}\right)+g^{bc}\Gamma^{a}_{cd}\nabla_{b}\mathcal{A}^{d}

Therefore, we get

\sqrt{\Omega}\left(T_{1}^{a}+T_{2}^{a}+T_{3}^{a}\right)=-2h^{a}_{d}\partial_{\eta}\mathcal{A}^{d}-2h^{ab}\partial_{b}\mathcal{A}^{\eta}+C^{a},

(80)

with the short-hand notation

C^{a}\equiv\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma}}\partial_{b}\left(\sqrt{\gamma}g^{bc}\Gamma^{a}_{cd}\mathcal{A}^{d}\right)+g^{bc}\Gamma^{a}_{cd}\nabla_{b}\mathcal{A}^{d}

(81)

since
\bar{R}^{a}_{\ cdb}=\mathcal{O}(K)
.
After again dividing through by
\sqrt{\Omega}
, the wave equation for the tangential vector field component can then be written as

\left[\Delta_{\gamma}+\partial_{\eta}^{2}+k_{0}^{2}\epsilon(\eta)\right]\!\mathcal{A}^{a}+2\eta\,h^{bc}\nabla_{b}\nabla_{c}\mathcal{A}^{a}-2h^{a}_{d}\partial_{\eta}\mathcal{A}^{d}+C^{a}=2h^{ab}\partial_{b}\mathcal{A}^{\eta},

(82)

which is the same expanded form as written in Ref.
49
. Eq. (
82
) shows that the tangential components experience the normal vector field
\mathcal{A}^{\eta}
as a source term in the presence of curvature.
Since we use an expansion in orders of curvature in Appendices
D
and
D
, we must take care about the scaling of
C^{a}
. As explained in these sections, we can drop it if it scales at least as
h^{ab}\sim H
. With
g^{ab}=\gamma^{ab}+2\eta h^{ab}
, we split
C^{a}
into two parts as

C^{a}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma}}\partial_{b}\left(\sqrt{\gamma}\gamma^{bc}\Gamma^{a}_{cd}\mathcal{A}^{d}\right)+\gamma^{bc}\Gamma^{a}_{cd}\nabla_{b}\mathcal{A}^{d}+\frac{2\eta}{\sqrt{\gamma}}\partial_{b}\left(\sqrt{\gamma}h^{bc}\Gamma^{a}_{cd}\mathcal{A}^{d}\right)+2\eta h^{bc}\Gamma^{a}_{cd}\nabla_{b}\mathcal{A}^{d}.

(83)

The last two terms will be dropped as these are at least
\mathcal{O}(H)
. Using a Monge patch parametrization
\mathbf{r}(x^{1},x^{2})=\left(x^{1},x^{2},f(x^{1},x^{2})\right)
with a height function
f(x^{1},x^{2})
, one can show that the Christoffel symbols read
22

\Gamma^{a}_{bc}=\frac{\left(\partial_{a}f\right)}{\sqrt{\gamma}}h_{bc}\sim H\qquad\implies\qquad\partial_{c}\gamma^{ab}=-\left(\gamma^{bd}\Gamma^{a}_{dc}+\gamma^{ad}\Gamma^{b}_{dc}\right)\sim H.

(84)

Therefore, also the first two terms in
C^{a}
scale at least linearly in
H
.

Appendix B
Maxwell boundary conditions on a 2D curved interface

In the Lorenz gauge, the scalar potential is given by
\phi=(i\omega\epsilon\mu)^{-1}\nabla_{j}A^{j}
. Assuming no surface charges or dipole layers, both the tangential electric field and the scalar potential
\phi
must be continuous across the interface. For a non-magnetic system (
\mu_{d}=\mu_{m}
), this requires the continuity of
\epsilon^{-1}\nabla_{j}A^{j}
, which means

\frac{1}{\epsilon}\nabla_{j}A^{j}\Bigg|_{\eta=0^{+}}=\frac{1}{\epsilon}\nabla_{j}A^{j}\Bigg|_{\eta=0^{-}}.

(85)

Expanding the covariant divergence
\nabla_{j}A^{j}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{g}}\partial_{i}(\sqrt{g}A^{i})
into normal (
\eta
) and tangential (
a=1,2
) components at the interface (
\eta=0
) yields

\nabla_{j}A^{j}=\partial_{\eta}A^{\eta}-2HA^{\eta}+\nabla_{a}A^{a},

(86)

for which we used the expansion of
\sqrt{g}=\Omega\sqrt{\gamma}
with the factor
\Omega\approx 1-2H\eta
near the interface, resulting in
\partial_{\eta}\Omega|_{\eta=0}=-2H
. To express this in terms of the rescaled field
\mathcal{A}^{i}=\sqrt{\Omega}\,A^{i}
required for the correct volume measure
19
;
7
;
12
, we evaluate the normal derivative of the physical field at
\eta=0
. Since
\sqrt{\Omega}\big|_{\eta=0}=1
and
\partial_{\eta}\sqrt{\Omega}\big|_{\eta=0}=-H
, we get

\partial_{\eta}A^{\eta}\bigg|_{\eta=0}=\partial_{\eta}\left(\frac{\mathcal{A}^{\eta}}{\sqrt{\Omega}}\right)\Bigg|_{\eta=0}=\partial_{\eta}\mathcal{A}^{\eta}+H\mathcal{A}^{\eta}.

(87)

Substituting this into Eq. (
86
) gives

\nabla_{j}A^{j}\bigg|_{\eta=0}=\partial_{\eta}\mathcal{A}^{\eta}-H\mathcal{A}^{\eta}+\nabla_{a}\mathcal{A}^{a}.

(88)

As the physical field
\mathbf{A}
is continuous across the interface, the rescaled fields
\mathcal{A}^{\eta}
and
\mathcal{A}^{a}
, as well as the tangential divergence
\nabla_{a}\mathcal{A}^{a}
, are also continuous. The continuity condition then reads

\frac{1}{\epsilon_{d}}\left(\partial_{\eta}\mathcal{A}_{d}^{\eta}-H\mathcal{A}_{d}^{\eta}+\nabla_{a}\mathcal{A}_{d}^{a}\right)=\frac{1}{\epsilon_{m}}\left(\partial_{\eta}\mathcal{A}_{m}^{\eta}-H\mathcal{A}_{m}^{\eta}+\nabla_{a}\mathcal{A}_{m}^{a}\right),

(89)

where the lower index means dielectric
d
for
\eta=0^{+}
and metal
m
for
\eta=0^{-}
. Rearranging the terms yields the curvature-modified boundary condition used in the main text as Eq. (
13
).

Appendix C
Commuting covariant derivative and Laplace-Beltrami operator

We want to calculate the difference

\left[\nabla_{a},\Delta_{\gamma}\right]\mathcal{A}^{a}\equiv\nabla_{a}\left(\Delta_{\gamma}\mathcal{A}^{a}\right)-\Delta_{\gamma}\left(\nabla_{a}\mathcal{A}^{a}\right),

(90)

with
\Delta_{\gamma}=\nabla_{b}\nabla^{b}
. We expand the first term

\nabla_{a}\left(\nabla_{b}\nabla^{b}\mathcal{A}^{a}\right)\equiv\nabla_{a}\left(\nabla_{b}T^{ba}\right)

(91)

In the following, using the definition of the Riemann curvature tensor as in Ref.
65
, we need the Ricci identities

[\nabla_{a},\nabla_{b}]\,T^{ba}=R^{b}_{cab}T^{ca}+R^{a}_{cab}T^{bc},

(92)

and

[\nabla_{a},\nabla_{b}]\,\mathcal{A}^{c}=R^{c}_{dab}\,\mathcal{A}^{d},

(93)

and that for a 2D surface, dictated by the Bianchi identities, the Riemann tensor simplifies to

R^{a}_{bcd}=K(\delta^{a}_{c}\gamma_{bd}-\delta^{a}_{d}\gamma_{bc}).

(94)

So we get

[\nabla_{a},\nabla_{b}]\,T^{ba}=-K\gamma_{ca}T^{ca}+K\gamma_{cb}T^{bc}=K\left(T_{c}^{c}-T_{a}^{a}\right)=0.

(95)

Therefore,

\nabla_{a}\nabla_{b}\,T^{ba}=\nabla_{b}\nabla_{a}\,T^{ba},

(96)

and hence,

\nabla_{a}\nabla_{b}\nabla^{b}\mathcal{A}^{a}=\nabla_{b}\nabla_{a}\nabla^{b}\mathcal{A}^{a}.

(97)

Now we need

[\nabla_{a},\nabla^{b}]\mathcal{A}^{a}=\gamma^{bc}[\nabla_{a},\nabla_{c}]\mathcal{A}^{a}=\gamma^{bc}R^{a}_{dac}\mathcal{A}^{d},

(98)

which after a short calculation using Eq. (
94
) gives us

[\nabla_{a},\nabla^{b}]\,\mathcal{A}^{a}=K\,\mathcal{A}^{b}.

(99)

Therefore, the first term in Eq. (
90
) yields

\displaystyle\nabla_{a}\left(\Delta_{\gamma}\mathcal{A}^{a}\right)

\displaystyle=\nabla_{b}\nabla_{a}\nabla^{b}\mathcal{A}^{a}

(100)

\displaystyle=\nabla_{b}\left(\nabla^{b}\nabla_{a}\mathcal{A}^{a}+K\mathcal{A}^{b}\right)

\displaystyle=\Delta_{\gamma}(\nabla_{a}\mathcal{A}^{a})+(\nabla_{b}K)\mathcal{A}^{b}+K(\nabla_{b}\mathcal{A}^{b}).

Eq. (
90
) therefore becomes

\left[\nabla_{a},\Delta_{\gamma}\right]\mathcal{A}^{a}=(\nabla_{b}K)\mathcal{A}^{b}+K(\nabla_{b}\mathcal{A}^{b}).

(101)

Appendix D
Derivation of the effective surface wave equation

Starting from the separable flat-interface SPP mode, see Eq. (
7
), we write

\mathcal{A}_{i}^{\eta}=f_{i}(\eta)\psi(q_{\parallel}),

(102)

and expand both parts in orders of
\alpha
as

\displaystyle f_{i}(\eta)=f_{i}^{(0)}(\eta)+\alpha f_{i}^{(1)}(\eta)+\mathcal{O}(\alpha^{2}),

(103)

\displaystyle\psi(q_{\parallel})=\psi^{(0)}(q_{\parallel})+\alpha\psi^{(1)}(q_{\parallel})+\mathcal{O}(\alpha^{2}),

with
f_{i}^{(0)}(\eta)=e^{-\kappa_{i}|\eta|}
and the ansatz
f_{i}^{(1)}(\eta)=e^{-\kappa_{i}|\eta|}\left(A_{i}\eta^{2}+B_{i}\eta+C_{i}\right)
. This yields

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{i}^{\eta}

\displaystyle=\left(f_{i}^{(0)}(\eta)+\alpha f_{i}^{(1)}(\eta)\right)\left(\psi^{(0)}+\alpha\psi^{(1)}\right)

(104)

\displaystyle=f_{i}^{(0)}(\eta)\psi^{(0)}+\alpha\left[f_{i}^{(0)}(\eta)\psi^{(1)}+f_{i}^{(1)}(\eta)\psi^{(0)}\right]+\mathcal{O}(\alpha^{2}).

Writing out the exponential term, this gives the ansatz equation (
14
) presented in the main text.

D.1
Dielectric domain

For the dielectric region, this reads

\mathcal{A}_{d}^{\eta}=\underbrace{e^{-\kappa_{d}\eta}\psi^{(0)}}_{\mathcal{A}_{d}^{\eta\,(0)}}+\alpha\underbrace{e^{-\kappa_{d}\eta}\left[\psi^{(1)}+(A_{d}\eta^{2}+B_{d}\eta+C_{d})\psi^{(0)}\right]}_{\mathcal{A}_{d}^{\eta\,(1)}}.

(105)

The equation
\hat{L}\mathcal{A}_{d}^{\eta}=0
, with
\hat{L}
as in Eq. (
15
), must now be satisfied at each order in
\alpha
.

Order
\alpha^{0}
:

At zero order, we get

\hat{L}^{(0)}\mathcal{A}_{d}^{\eta\,(0)}=\left[\Delta_{\rm flat}+\partial_{\eta}^{2}+k_{0}^{2}\epsilon(\eta)\right]e^{-\kappa_{d}\eta}\psi^{(0)}=0,

(106)

which can be written as

\psi^{(0)}\left[\partial_{\eta}^{2}+k_{0}^{2}\epsilon(\eta)\right]e^{-\kappa_{d}\eta}+e^{-\kappa_{d}\eta}\left(\Delta_{\rm flat}\psi^{(0)}\right)=0.

(107)

Applying the second-order derivative on the exponential and using that
\kappa^{2}_{d}=k^{2}_{\rm spp}-k^{2}_{0}\epsilon_{d}
, see Eq. (
3
), yields, after factoring out the remaining exponential,

\Delta_{\rm flat}\psi^{(0)}=-k^{2}_{\rm spp}\psi^{(0)}.

(108)

This is simply the SPP Helmholtz equation for a flat interface, cf. Section
II
.

Order
\alpha^{1}
:

[\Delta_{\gamma}+2\eta\,\mathcal{D}_{h}+\partial_{\eta}^{2}+k_{0}^{2}\epsilon]\mathcal{A}^{\eta}+2h^{a}_{b}\nabla_{a}\mathcal{A}^{b}=0,

(109)

At first order, we obtain the inhomogeneous differential equation

\hat{L}^{(0)}\mathcal{A}_{d}^{\eta\,(1)}+\hat{L}^{(1)}\mathcal{A}_{d}^{\eta\,(0)}+\mathcal{M}_{a}^{(1)}\mathcal{A}_{d}^{a\,(0)}=0,

(110)

with

\mathcal{M}_{a}^{(1)}\equiv 2h^{b}_{a}\nabla_{b}.

(111)

First, we note again that the tangential fields for the flat interface vanish, i.e.,
\mathcal{A}_{d}^{d\,(0)}=0
, leaving

\hat{L}^{(0)}\mathcal{A}_{d}^{\eta\,(1)}+\hat{L}^{(1)}\mathcal{A}_{d}^{\eta\,(0)}=0,

(112)

which is written out as

\left[\Delta_{\rm flat}+\partial_{\eta}^{2}+k_{0}^{2}\epsilon_{d}\right]\left(e^{-\kappa_{d}\eta}\Big[\psi^{(1)}+(A_{d}\eta^{2}+B_{d}\eta+C_{d})\psi^{(0)}\Big]\right)+2\eta\mathcal{D}^{(1)}_{h}\left(e^{-\kappa_{d}\eta}\psi^{(0)}\right)=0.

(113)

First, we evaluate the geometric source term. Because the operator
\mathcal{D}^{(1)}_{h}=H^{(1)}\Delta_{\rm flat}+\sigma^{ab\,(1)}\partial_{a}\partial_{b}
consists only of surface derivatives (
\partial_{a}
), it commutes with the normal coordinate
\eta
and the exponential
e^{-\kappa_{d}\eta}
, and acts only on the surface envelope
\psi^{(0)}
as

2\eta\mathcal{D}^{(1)}_{h}\left(e^{-\kappa_{d}\eta}\psi^{(0)}\right)=2\eta e^{-\kappa_{d}\eta}\mathcal{D}^{(1)}_{h}\psi^{(0)}.

(114)

Second, we evaluate the action of the flat surface Laplacian
\Delta_{\rm flat}
on our
\mathcal{O}(\alpha)
field ansatz. Like
\mathcal{D}^{(1)}_{h}
, this operator only acts on the surface functions
\psi^{(0)}
and
\psi^{(1)}
as

\displaystyle\Delta_{\rm flat}\left(e^{-\kappa_{d}\eta}\Big[\psi^{(1)}+(A_{d}\eta^{2}+B_{d}\eta+C_{d})\psi^{(0)}\Big]\right)

\displaystyle=e^{-\kappa_{d}\eta}\Delta_{\rm flat}\psi^{(1)}+e^{-\kappa_{d}\eta}(A_{d}\eta^{2}+B_{d}\eta+C_{d})\Delta_{\rm flat}\psi^{(0)}

(115)

\displaystyle=e^{-\kappa_{d}\eta}\left[\Delta_{\rm flat}\psi^{(1)}-k_{\text{spp}}^{2}(A_{d}\eta^{2}+B_{d}\eta+C_{d})\psi^{(0)}\right].

for which we have used the zero-order result
\Delta_{\rm flat}\psi^{(0)}=-k_{\text{spp}}^{2}\psi^{(0)}
. Let us temporarily write the polynomial as
P(\eta)=A_{d}\eta^{2}+B_{d}\eta+C_{d}
. The second-order field then reads

\mathcal{A}_{d}^{\eta\,(1)}=e^{-\kappa_{d}\eta}\Big[\psi^{(1)}+P(\eta)\psi^{(0)}\Big].

(116)

We take the first normal derivative
\partial_{\eta}
using the product rule and get

\partial_{\eta}\mathcal{A}_{d}^{\eta\,(1)}=-\kappa_{d}e^{-\kappa_{d}\eta}\Big[\psi^{(1)}+P(\eta)\psi^{(0)}\Big]+e^{-\kappa_{d}\eta}\Big[P^{\prime}(\eta)\psi^{(0)}\Big].

(117)

Now we apply the product rule again to find the second derivative as

\displaystyle\partial_{\eta}^{2}\mathcal{A}_{d}^{\eta\,(1)}

\displaystyle=\kappa_{d}^{2}e^{-\kappa_{d}\eta}\Big[\psi^{(1)}+P(\eta)\psi^{(0)}\Big]-\kappa_{d}e^{-\kappa_{d}\eta}\Big[P^{\prime}(\eta)\psi^{(0)}\Big]-\kappa_{d}e^{-\kappa_{d}\eta}\Big[P^{\prime}(\eta)\psi^{(0)}\Big]+e^{-\kappa_{d}\eta}\Big[P^{\prime\prime}(\eta)\psi^{(0)}\Big]

(118)

\displaystyle=e^{-\kappa_{d}\eta}\left[\kappa_{d}^{2}\Big(\psi^{(1)}+P(\eta)\psi^{(0)}\Big)-2\kappa_{d}P^{\prime}(\eta)\psi^{(0)}+P^{\prime\prime}(\eta)\psi^{(0)}\right].

The derivatives of the polynomial are

P^{\prime}(\eta)=2A_{d}\eta+B_{d},\qquad P^{\prime\prime}(\eta)=2A_{d}.

(119)

Substituting these back into our second derivative and adding the
k_{0}^{2}\epsilon_{d}
term from the operator, we get

(\partial_{\eta}^{2}+k_{0}^{2}\epsilon_{d})\mathcal{A}_{d}^{\eta\,(1)}=e^{-\kappa_{d}\eta}\left[(\kappa_{d}^{2}+k_{0}^{2}\epsilon_{d})\Big(\psi^{(1)}+(A_{d}\eta^{2}+B_{d}\eta+C_{d})\psi^{(0)}\Big)-2\kappa_{d}(2A_{d}\eta+B_{d})\psi^{(0)}+2A_{d}\psi^{(0)}\right].

(120)

The full action of the zero-order operator
\hat{L}^{(0)}
on the second-order field
\mathcal{A}_{d}^{3\,(1)}
is then

\displaystyle\hat{L}^{(0)}\mathcal{A}_{d}^{\eta\,(1)}=e^{-\kappa_{d}\eta}\bigg[

\displaystyle\Delta_{\rm flat}\psi^{(1)}-k_{\text{spp}}^{2}\Big(A_{d}\eta^{2}+B_{d}\eta+C_{d}\Big)\psi^{(0)}

(121)

\displaystyle+(\kappa_{d}^{2}+k_{0}^{2}\epsilon_{d})\Big(\psi^{(1)}+(A_{d}\eta^{2}+B_{d}\eta+C_{d})\psi^{(0)}\Big)-2\kappa_{d}(2A_{d}\eta+B_{d})\psi^{(0)}+2A_{d}\psi^{(0)}\bigg].

The two appearing full polynomials cancel, since from Eq. (
3
) we have
\kappa_{d}^{2}+k_{0}^{2}\epsilon_{d}=k_{\text{spp}}^{2}
, leaving

\hat{L}^{(0)}\mathcal{A}_{d}^{\eta\,(1)}=e^{-\kappa_{d}\eta}\bigg[(\Delta_{\rm flat}+k_{\text{spp}}^{2})\psi^{(1)}+2A_{d}\psi^{(0)}-2\kappa_{d}B_{d}\psi^{(0)}-4\kappa_{d}A_{d}\eta\psi^{(0)}\bigg].

(122)

We can now also add
2\eta e^{-\kappa_{d}\eta}\mathcal{D}^{(1)}_{h}\psi^{(0)}
and divide through by the exponential
e^{-\kappa_{d}\eta}
, which is non-zero in the dielectric domain, to get

(\Delta_{\rm flat}+k_{\text{spp}}^{2})\psi^{(1)}+(2A_{d}-2\kappa_{d}B_{d})\psi^{(0)}+\eta\Big[2\mathcal{D}^{(1)}_{h}-4\kappa_{d}A_{d}\Big]\psi^{(0)}=0.

(123)

Since this differential equation must hold true for every arbitrary value of
\eta>0
within the dielectric domain, the coefficients of each linearly independent power of
\eta
must vanish. This yields

2\mathcal{D}^{(1)}_{h}\psi^{(0)}-4\kappa_{d}A_{d}\psi^{(0)}=0,

(124)

which is solved as

A_{d}\psi^{(0)}=\frac{1}{2\kappa_{d}}\mathcal{D}^{(1)}_{h}\psi^{(0)},

(125)

and

(\Delta_{\rm flat}+k_{\text{spp}}^{2})\psi^{(1)}+2A_{d}\psi^{(0)}-2\kappa_{d}B_{d}\psi^{(0)}=0,

(126)

which is solved as

B_{d}\psi^{(0)}=\frac{1}{2\kappa_{d}}(\Delta_{\rm flat}+k_{\text{spp}}^{2})\psi^{(1)}+\frac{1}{2\kappa_{d}^{2}}\mathcal{D}^{(1)}_{h}\psi^{(0)}.

(127)

D.2
Metal domain

In the metal, the field decays as
e^{\kappa_{m}\eta}
, such that the ansatz there is

\mathcal{A}_{m}^{\eta}=\underbrace{e^{\kappa_{m}\eta}\psi^{(0)}}_{\mathcal{A}_{m}^{\eta\,(0)}}+\alpha\underbrace{e^{\kappa_{m}\eta}\left[\psi^{(1)}+(A_{m}\eta^{2}+B_{m}\eta+C_{m})\psi^{(0)}\right]}_{\mathcal{A}_{m}^{\eta\,(1)}}.

(128)

We perform exactly the same steps as in the dielectric. This yields similarly

A_{m}\psi^{(0)}=-\frac{1}{2\kappa_{m}}\mathcal{D}^{(1)}_{h}\psi^{(0)},

(129)

and

B_{m}\psi^{(0)}=-\frac{1}{2\kappa_{m}}(\Delta_{\rm flat}+k_{\text{spp}}^{2})\psi^{(1)}+\frac{1}{2\kappa_{m}^{2}}\mathcal{D}^{(1)}_{h}\psi^{(0)}.

(130)

D.3
Evaluating the fields at the boundary

The fields at
\eta=0
are evaluated as

\mathcal{A}_{d}^{\eta\,(1)}\Big|_{\eta=0}=\psi^{(1)}+C_{d}\psi^{(0)},\qquad\mathcal{A}_{m}^{\eta\,(1)}\Big|_{\eta=0}=\psi^{(1)}+C_{m}\psi^{(0)}.

(131)

Because from our gauge choice, the normal field component must be continuous across the interface, and we get

\psi^{(1)}+C_{d}\psi^{(0)}=\psi^{(1)}+C_{m}\psi^{(0)},

(132)

which means
C_{d}=C_{m}\equiv C
. Let us recall

\partial_{\eta}\mathcal{A}_{d}^{\eta\,(1)}=-\kappa_{d}e^{-\kappa_{d}\eta}\Big[\psi^{(1)}+(A_{d}\eta^{2}+B_{d}\eta+C_{d})\psi^{(0)}\Big]+e^{-\kappa_{d}\eta}\Big[(2A_{d}\eta+B_{d})\psi^{(0)}\Big],

(133)

and

\partial_{\eta}\mathcal{A}_{m}^{\eta\,(1)}=\kappa_{m}e^{\kappa_{m}\eta}\Big[\psi^{(1)}+(A_{m}\eta^{2}+B_{m}\eta+C_{m})\psi^{(0)}\Big]+e^{\kappa_{m}\eta}\Big[(2A_{m}\eta+B_{m})\psi^{(0)}\Big],

(134)

which, at the boundary, take the values

\partial_{\eta}\mathcal{A}_{d}^{\eta\,(1)}\Big|_{\eta=0}=-\kappa_{d}\psi^{(1)}-\kappa_{d}C\psi^{(0)}+\frac{1}{2\kappa_{d}}(\Delta_{\rm flat}+k_{\text{spp}}^{2})\psi^{(1)}+\frac{1}{2\kappa_{d}^{2}}\mathcal{D}^{(1)}_{h}\psi^{(0)},

(135)

and

\partial_{\eta}\mathcal{A}_{m}^{\eta\,(1)}\Big|_{\eta=0}=\kappa_{m}\psi^{(1)}+\kappa_{m}C\psi^{(0)}-\frac{1}{2\kappa_{m}}(\Delta_{\rm flat}+k_{\text{spp}}^{2})\psi^{(1)}+\frac{1}{2\kappa_{m}^{2}}\mathcal{D}^{(1)}_{h}\psi^{(0)},

(136)

where we already inserted the above derived expressions for
A_{m,d}
and
B_{m,d}
. The tangential divergence, see Appendix
E
, evaluated at the boundary takes the value

\left(\nabla_{a}\mathcal{A}_{d}^{a}\right)^{(1)}\Big|_{\eta=0}=\left(\nabla_{a}\mathcal{A}_{m}^{a}\right)^{(1)}\Big|_{\eta=0}=-\frac{1}{k^{2}_{\rm spp}}\mathcal{D}^{(1)}_{h}\psi^{(0)}=-\frac{1}{k^{2}_{\rm spp}}\left(-H^{(1)}k^{2}_{\rm spp}+\sigma^{ab\,(1)}\partial_{a}\partial_{b}\right)\psi^{(0)}.

(137)

D.4
Boundary condition

The boundary condition taking into account the curvature is derived in Appendix
B
and reads at first order in
\alpha

\frac{1}{\epsilon_{d}}\bigg[\left(\nabla_{a}\mathcal{A}_{d}^{a}\right)^{(1)}+\partial_{\eta}\mathcal{A}_{d}^{\eta\,(1)}-H^{(1)}\mathcal{A}_{d}^{\eta\,(0)}\bigg]=\frac{1}{\epsilon_{m}}\bigg[\left(\nabla_{a}\mathcal{A}_{m}^{a}\right)^{(1)}+\partial_{\eta}\mathcal{A}_{m}^{\eta\,(1)}-H^{(1)}\mathcal{A}_{m}^{\eta\,(0)}\bigg],

(138)

The left-hand side of this equation writes

\text{LHS}=\frac{1}{\epsilon_{d}}\bigg[-\frac{1}{k_{\text{spp}}^{2}}\mathcal{D}^{(1)}_{h}\psi^{(0)}\underbrace{-\kappa_{d}\psi^{(1)}-\kappa_{d}C\psi^{(0)}+\frac{1}{2\kappa_{d}}(\Delta_{\rm flat}+k_{\text{spp}}^{2})\psi^{(1)}+\frac{1}{2\kappa_{d}^{2}}\mathcal{D}^{(1)}_{h}\psi^{(0)}}_{\partial_{\eta}\mathcal{A}_{d}^{\eta\,(1)}}-H^{(1)}\psi^{(0)}\bigg],

(139)

while the right-hand side writes

\text{RHS}=\frac{1}{\epsilon_{m}}\bigg[-\frac{1}{k_{\text{spp}}^{2}}\mathcal{D}^{(1)}_{h}\psi^{(0)}\underbrace{+\kappa_{m}\psi^{(1)}+\kappa_{m}C\psi^{(0)}-\frac{1}{2\kappa_{m}}(\Delta_{\rm flat}+k_{\text{spp}}^{2})\psi^{(1)}+\frac{1}{2\kappa_{m}^{2}}\mathcal{D}^{(1)}_{h}\psi^{(0)}}_{\partial_{\eta}\mathcal{A}_{m}^{\eta\,(1)}}-H^{(1)}\psi^{(0)}\bigg].

(140)

We now move the terms with
C
and the bare
\psi^{(1)}
terms to the left side. These are

-\frac{\kappa_{d}}{\epsilon_{d}}C\psi^{(0)}-\frac{\kappa_{m}}{\epsilon_{m}}C\psi^{(0)}=-\left(\frac{\kappa_{d}}{\epsilon_{d}}+\frac{\kappa_{m}}{\epsilon_{m}}\right)C\psi^{(0)},

(141)

and

-\frac{\kappa_{d}}{\epsilon_{d}}\psi^{(1)}-\frac{\kappa_{m}}{\epsilon_{m}}\psi^{(1)}=-\left(\frac{\kappa_{d}}{\epsilon_{d}}+\frac{\kappa_{m}}{\epsilon_{m}}\right)\psi^{(1)}.

(142)

Since
\kappa_{d}/\epsilon_{d}+\kappa_{m}/\epsilon_{m}=0
, they both vanish. Moving the remaining
\psi^{(1)}
terms to the left and grouping the
\psi^{0}
terms on the right, we arrive at

\left(\frac{1}{2\epsilon_{d}\kappa_{d}}+\frac{1}{2\epsilon_{m}\kappa_{m}}\right)(\Delta_{\text{flat}}+k_{\text{spp}}^{2})\psi^{(1)}=\left[\frac{\mathcal{D}^{(1)}_{h}}{\epsilon_{m}}\left(\frac{1}{2\kappa_{m}^{2}}-\frac{1}{k_{\text{spp}}^{2}}\right)-\frac{\mathcal{D}^{(1)}_{h}}{\epsilon_{d}}\left(\frac{1}{2\kappa_{d}^{2}}-\frac{1}{k_{\text{spp}}^{2}}\right)+H^{(1)}\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon_{d}}-\frac{1}{\epsilon_{m}}\right)\right]\psi^{(0)}.

(143)

To isolate the 2D wave operator
(\Delta_{\text{flat}}+k_{\text{spp}}^{2})\psi^{(1)}
, we divide the entire right-hand side by the corresponding prefactor and define the resulting right-hand side as the geometric potential operator
V_{\rm geom}
, such that

(\Delta_{\rm flat}+k_{\text{spp}}^{2})\psi^{(1)}=V_{\rm geom}\psi^{(0)}.

(144)

We now have two differential equations:

•

\mathcal{O}(\alpha^{0})
:
(\Delta_{\rm flat}+k_{\text{spp}}^{2})\psi^{(0)}=0
, and

•

\mathcal{O}(\alpha^{1})
:
(\Delta_{\rm flat}+k_{\text{spp}}^{2})\psi^{(1)}=V_{\rm geom}\psi^{(0)}

Now, we reconstruct the equation for the full envelope
\psi=\psi^{(0)}+\alpha\psi^{(1)}
. We therefore add the two equations and use
\psi^{(0)}=\psi-\alpha\psi^{(1)}
to get

(\Delta_{\rm flat}+k_{\text{spp}}^{2})\big(\psi^{(0)}+\alpha\psi^{(1)}\big)=\alpha V_{\rm geom}\psi-\alpha^{2}V_{\rm geom}\psi^{(1)},

(145)

and discard the
\alpha^{2}
term, such that

\left[\Delta_{\rm flat}+k_{\text{spp}}^{2}-\alpha V_{\rm geom}\right]\psi=0.

(146)

We now have to calculate the specific expression of the geometric potential, which is

V_{\rm geom}=\left(\frac{1}{2\epsilon_{d}\kappa_{d}}+\frac{1}{2\epsilon_{m}\kappa_{m}}\right)^{-1}\left[\frac{\mathcal{D}^{(1)}_{h}}{\epsilon_{m}}\left(\frac{1}{2\kappa_{m}^{2}}-\frac{1}{k_{\text{spp}}^{2}}\right)-\frac{\mathcal{D}^{(1)}_{h}}{\epsilon_{d}}\left(\frac{1}{2\kappa_{d}^{2}}-\frac{1}{k_{\text{spp}}^{2}}\right)+H^{(1)}\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon_{d}}-\frac{1}{\epsilon_{m}}\right)\right].

(147)

Simplifying the prefactors yields after an algebraic calculation

V_{\rm geom}=-\frac{k_{0}(\epsilon_{d}^{2}+\epsilon_{d}\epsilon_{m}+\epsilon_{m}^{2})}{(\epsilon_{d}+\epsilon_{m})\sqrt{-(\epsilon_{d}+\epsilon_{m})}}H^{(1)}+\frac{\epsilon_{d}^{2}+3\epsilon_{d}\epsilon_{m}+\epsilon_{m}^{2}}{k_{0}\epsilon_{d}\epsilon_{m}\sqrt{-(\epsilon_{d}+\epsilon_{m})}}\sigma^{ab\,(1)}\partial_{a}\partial_{b}.

(148)

Finally, we can write down the effective wave equation for the curvature-modified TM SPP envelope
\psi
on the metal-dielectric interface as

\left[\Delta_{\gamma}+k_{\text{spp}}^{2}+\frac{k_{0}(\epsilon_{d}^{2}+\epsilon_{d}\epsilon_{m}+\epsilon_{m}^{2})}{(\epsilon_{d}+\epsilon_{m})\sqrt{-(\epsilon_{d}+\epsilon_{m})}}H-\frac{\epsilon_{d}^{2}+3\epsilon_{d}\epsilon_{m}+\epsilon_{m}^{2}}{k_{0}\epsilon_{d}\epsilon_{m}\sqrt{-(\epsilon_{d}+\epsilon_{m})}}\sigma^{ab}\nabla_{a}\nabla_{b}\right]\psi=0.

(149)

While the derivation isolates the
\mathcal{O}(\alpha)
extrinsic curvature potentials in Eq. (
146
) using a locally flat Laplacian
\Delta_{\rm flat}
, we have replaced
\Delta_{\rm flat}\to\Delta_{\gamma}
and
\sigma^{ab}\partial_{a}\partial_{b}\to\sigma^{ab}\nabla_{a}\nabla_{b}
in the final equation to restore a global solution. Since intrinsic curvature corrections scale as the Gaussian curvature
K\sim\mathcal{O}(\alpha^{2})
, this is consistent within our second-order framework. As a demonstration, we show in Fig.
3
, Appendix
F
, the collective eigenvalues of a ring of emitters placed above a metal sphere. In contrast to Fig.
2
(a) in the main text, we have artificially set
V_{H}=0
, to observe the isolated impact of the intrinsic curvature contribution of the Laplace-Beltrami operator.

Appendix E
Derivation of the tangential divergence

To get the tangential divergence
\nabla_{a}\mathcal{A}^{a}
at the interface, we act with
\nabla_{a}
from left on Eq. (
9
). Since
\epsilon(\eta)
and
\partial_{\eta}
depend only on the normal coordinate, they commute with the tangential derivative
\nabla_{a}
, but not necessarily with
\Delta_{\gamma}
. The commutator is, however, see Appendix
C
,

\left[\nabla_{a},\Delta_{\gamma}\right]\,\mathcal{A}^{a}=(\nabla_{a}K)\mathcal{A}^{a}+K(\nabla_{a}\mathcal{A}^{a}),

(150)

which we can drop as it is
\mathcal{O}(K)=\mathcal{O}(\alpha^{2})
. We also drop
\nabla_{a}C^{a}
, see Appendix
A
. We then get

\displaystyle\nabla_{a}\left[\Delta_{\gamma}+2\eta\mathcal{D}_{h}+\partial_{\eta}^{2}+k_{0}^{2}\epsilon\right]\mathcal{A}^{a}-2\nabla_{a}\left(h^{a}_{d}\partial_{\eta}\mathcal{A}^{d}\right)

\displaystyle=2\nabla_{a}\left(h^{ab}\partial_{b}\mathcal{A}^{\eta}\right)

(151)

\displaystyle\left[\Delta_{\gamma}+\partial_{\eta}^{2}+k_{0}^{2}\epsilon\right]\nabla_{a}\mathcal{A}^{a}+2\eta\nabla_{a}\mathcal{D}_{h}\mathcal{A}^{a}-2h^{a}_{d}\nabla_{a}\left(\partial_{\eta}\mathcal{A}^{d}\right)

\displaystyle\approx 2h^{ab}\nabla_{a}\nabla_{b}\mathcal{A}^{\eta}=2\mathcal{D}_{h}\mathcal{A}^{\eta}.

The second and third term are at least
\mathcal{O}(\alpha)
as

2\eta\nabla_{a}\mathcal{D}_{h}\mathcal{A}^{a}-2h^{a}_{d}\nabla_{a}\left(\partial_{\eta}\mathcal{A}^{d}\right)=2\alpha\left[\eta\nabla_{a}\mathcal{D}_{h}^{(1)}\mathcal{A}^{a\,(0)}-h^{a\,(1)}_{d}\nabla_{a}\left(\partial_{\eta}\mathcal{A}^{d\,(0)}\right)\right]+\mathcal{O}(\alpha^{2}).

(152)

As the flat SPP mode has no tangential components, i.e.
\mathcal{A}^{a\,(0)}=\mathcal{A}^{d\,(0)}=0
, see Section
II
, this sum does not further contribute in our derivation. We now define
\Xi\equiv\nabla_{a}\mathcal{A}^{a}
and Eq. (
151
) defines an ordinary differential equation for
\Xi
up to
\mathcal{O}(\alpha)
as

\hat{O}^{(0)}\,\Xi=2\alpha\mathcal{D}_{h}^{(1)}\,\mathcal{A}^{\eta\,(0)},

(153)

with

\hat{O}^{(0)}\equiv\partial_{\eta}^{2}+k_{0}^{2}\epsilon(\eta)+\Delta_{\rm flat}.

(154)

Expanding also the yet unknown
\Xi
in orders of
\alpha
, we write

\hat{O}^{(0)}\left(\Xi^{(0)}+\alpha\Xi^{(1)}\right)=2\alpha f(\eta)\mathcal{D}_{h}^{(1)}\psi^{(0)}.

(155)

for which we used
\mathcal{A}^{\eta}
only up to zero order in
\alpha
on the right-hand side and the shorthand notation
\mathcal{A}^{\eta\,(0)}=f(\eta)\psi^{(0)}(q)
, see Eq. (
7
). At zero order, we get

\hat{O}^{(0)}\Xi^{(0)}=0,\quad\implies\Xi^{(0)}=0,

(156)

in line with the flat SPP physics, see Section
II
. At first order in
\alpha
, we can safely write

\hat{O}^{(0)}\Xi^{(1)}=0\quad\implies\quad\Xi^{(1)}=0.

(157)

The ODE then simplifies to

\alpha\hat{O}^{(0)}\Xi^{(1)}=2\alpha f(\eta)\mathcal{D}_{h}^{(1)}\psi^{(0)}.

(158)

The first order
\alpha
part of the equation is therefore

\left[\partial_{\eta}^{2}+k_{0}^{2}\epsilon(\eta)+\Delta_{\rm flat}\right]\Xi^{(1)}=2f(\eta)\mathcal{D}_{h}^{(1)}\psi^{(0)}.

(159)

Let us define the tangential source function as

S^{(1)}(q_{\parallel})\equiv 2\mathcal{D}_{h}^{(1)}\psi^{(0)}=2\left(H^{(1)}\Delta_{\rm flat}+\sigma^{ab\,(1)}\partial_{a}\partial_{b}\right)\psi^{(0)},

(160)

to keep the notation compact. Because the unperturbed SPP envelope satisfies the flat wave equation
\Delta_{\rm flat}\psi^{(0)}=-k_{\rm spp}^{2}\psi^{(0)}
, and because
\Delta_{\rm flat}
commutes with the constant curvature coefficients and the partial derivatives
\partial_{a}\partial_{b}
, the source function
S^{(1)}(q_{\parallel})
is itself an eigenfunction of the flat Laplacian with the same eigenvalue, i.e.,

\Delta_{\rm flat}S^{(1)}(q_{\parallel})=-k_{\rm spp}^{2}S^{(1)}(q_{\parallel}).

(161)

By making the separable ansatz
\Xi^{(1)}(q_{\parallel},\eta)=\chi(\eta)S^{(1)}(q_{\parallel})
, we therefore arrive at

\left[\partial_{\eta}^{2}+k_{0}^{2}\epsilon(\eta)-k_{\rm spp}^{2}\right]\chi(\eta)S^{(1)}(q_{\parallel})=f(\eta)S^{(1)}(q_{\parallel}),

(162)

or, using
\kappa_{i}^{2}=k_{\rm spp}^{2}-k_{0}^{2}\epsilon_{i}
from Eq. (
3
), at the differential equation for the vertical component
\eta
as

\left[\partial_{\eta}^{2}-\kappa_{i}^{2}\right]\chi_{i}(\eta)=f_{i}(\eta),

(163)

within each domain. Solving this by standard techniques and requiring continuity of
\chi
at the interface yields

\displaystyle\Xi_{d}^{(1)}(q_{\parallel},\eta)=\left[C-\frac{\eta}{2\kappa_{d}}\right]e^{-\kappa_{d}\eta}S^{(1)}(q_{\parallel})\quad\text{for }\eta>0

(164)

\displaystyle\Xi_{m}^{(1)}(q_{\parallel},\eta)=\left[C+\frac{\eta}{2\kappa_{m}}\right]e^{\kappa_{m}\eta}S^{(1)}(q_{\parallel})\quad\text{for }\eta<0

Continuity of
\partial_{\eta}\chi_{i}
at the interface yields

C=-\frac{1}{\kappa_{d}\kappa_{m}}=-\frac{1}{2k^{2}_{\rm spp}}.

(165)

Hence,

\Xi^{(1)}(q_{\parallel},\eta)=\nabla_{a}\mathcal{A}^{a}(q_{\parallel},\eta)=\left(-H^{(1)}k^{2}_{\rm spp}+\sigma^{ab\,(1)}\partial_{a}\partial_{b}\right)\psi^{(0)}\begin{cases}\left(-\frac{1}{k^{2}_{\rm spp}}-\frac{\eta}{\kappa_{d}}\right)e^{-\kappa_{d}\eta}&\text{for }\eta>0\\
\left(-\frac{1}{k^{2}_{\rm spp}}+\frac{\eta}{\kappa_{m}}\right)e^{\kappa_{m}\eta}&\text{for }\eta<0\end{cases},

(166)

and at the interface
\eta=0
, we get

\Xi^{(1)}(q_{\parallel},0)=-\frac{1}{k^{2}_{\rm spp}}\left(-H^{(1)}k^{2}_{\rm spp}+\sigma^{ab\,(1)}\partial_{a}\partial_{b}\right)\psi^{(0)}=H^{(1)}\psi^{(0)}-\frac{1}{k^{2}_{\rm spp}}\sigma^{ab\,(1)}\partial_{a}\partial_{b}\psi^{(0)}.

(167)

Appendix F
SPP-mediated emitter-emitter interaction on a metal spheroid

F.1
Wave function on the spheroid surface

We parametrize the spheroid using angular coordinates
(\theta,\phi)
, with
\theta\in[0,\pi]
the polar angle measured from the north pole and
\phi\in[0,2\pi)
the azimuthal angle. The equatorial and polar semi-axes are
a
and
c
, respectively, with
a=c=R
recovering the sphere. The embedding is

\mathbf{r}(\theta,\phi)=\begin{pmatrix}a\sin\theta\cos\phi\\
a\sin\theta\sin\phi\\
c\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}.

(168)

The tangent vectors
\mathbf{e}_{a}=\partial_{a}\mathbf{r}
give the first fundamental form
\gamma_{ab}=\mathbf{e}_{a}\cdot\mathbf{e}_{b}
,

\gamma_{ab}=\begin{pmatrix}\gamma_{\theta\theta}&0\\
0&\gamma_{\phi\phi}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\rho&0\\
0&a^{2}\sin^{2}\theta\end{pmatrix},\qquad\rho\equiv a^{2}\cos^{2}\theta+c^{2}\sin^{2}\theta,

(169)

with
\sqrt{\gamma}=a\sin\theta\sqrt{\rho}
and inverse metric
\gamma^{\theta\theta}=1/\rho
,
\gamma^{\phi\phi}=1/(a^{2}\sin^{2}\theta)
. The first fundamental form and all quantities derived from it alone are intrinsic and independent of the choice of normal orientation. To define the second fundamental form we must choose the unit normal
\mathbf{n}
. We introduce the sign parameter

s=\begin{cases}+1&\mathbf{n}\text{\, points outwards = convex metal surface}\\
-1&\mathbf{n}\text{\, points inwards = concave metal surface}\end{cases}\,\,,

(170)

and write

\mathbf{n}=s\,\frac{\mathbf{e}_{\theta}\times\mathbf{e}_{\phi}}{|\mathbf{e}_{\theta}\times\mathbf{e}_{\phi}|}=s\,\frac{\mathbf{e}_{\theta}\times\mathbf{e}_{\phi}}{\sqrt{\gamma}}.

(171)

The outward cross product evaluates to

\mathbf{e}_{\theta}\times\mathbf{e}_{\phi}=\begin{pmatrix}ac\sin^{2}\theta\cos\phi\\
ac\sin^{2}\theta\sin\phi\\
a^{2}\sin\theta\cos\theta\end{pmatrix},

(172)

so that
|\mathbf{e}_{\theta}\times\mathbf{e}_{\phi}|=a\sin\theta\sqrt{\rho}=\sqrt{\gamma}
, which yields

\mathbf{n}=\frac{s}{\sqrt{\rho}}\begin{pmatrix}c\sin\theta\cos\phi\\
c\sin\theta\sin\phi\\
a\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}.

(173)

The second fundamental form
h_{ab}=\mathbf{n}\cdot\partial_{a}\partial_{b}\mathbf{r}
is calculated to

h_{ab}=\begin{pmatrix}h_{\theta\theta}&0\\
0&h_{\phi\phi}\end{pmatrix}=-\frac{s}{\sqrt{\rho}}\begin{pmatrix}ac&0\\
0&ac\sin^{2}\theta\end{pmatrix}\implies h^{ab}=-s\begin{pmatrix}\frac{ac}{\rho^{5/2}}&0\\
0&\frac{c}{a^{3}\sin^{2}\theta\sqrt{\rho}}\end{pmatrix}.

(174)

The extrinsic curvature is then

H=\frac{1}{2}\gamma^{ab}h_{ab}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\gamma^{\theta\theta}h_{\theta\theta}+\gamma^{\phi\phi}h_{\phi\phi}\right)=-\frac{s}{2}\frac{c(a^{2}+\rho)}{a\,\rho^{3/2}},

(175)

which is negative for a convex surface (
s=+1
) and positive for a concave surface (
s=-1
), and becomes
H=-s/R
for the sphere at
a=c=R
. The traceless part of the shape operator,
\sigma^{ab}=h^{ab}-H\gamma^{ab}
, evaluates to

\sigma^{\theta\theta}=\frac{sc(c^{2}-a^{2})\sin^{2}\theta}{2a\,\rho^{5/2}},\qquad\sigma^{\phi\phi}=\frac{sc(a^{2}-c^{2})}{2a^{3}\rho^{3/2}},\qquad\sigma^{\theta\phi}=\sigma^{\phi\theta}=0.

(176)

We further need the Christoffel symbols for the covariant operator
\sigma^{ab}\nabla_{a}\nabla_{b}
. Expanding
\nabla_{a}\nabla_{b}\psi=\partial_{a}\partial_{b}\psi-\Gamma^{c}_{ab}\partial_{c}\psi
for the spheroid yields

\displaystyle\sigma^{ab}\nabla_{a}\nabla_{b}\psi

\displaystyle=\sigma^{\theta\theta}\nabla_{\theta}\nabla_{\theta}\psi+\sigma^{\phi\phi}\nabla_{\phi}\nabla_{\phi}\psi

(177)

\displaystyle=\sigma^{\theta\theta}\left(\partial_{\theta}^{2}\psi-\Gamma^{\theta}_{\theta\theta}\partial_{\theta}\psi-\Gamma^{\phi}_{\theta\theta}\partial_{\phi}\psi\right)+\sigma^{\phi\phi}\left(\partial_{\phi}^{2}\psi-\Gamma^{\theta}_{\phi\phi}\partial_{\theta}\psi-\Gamma^{\phi}_{\phi\phi}\partial_{\phi}\psi\right)

Using

\Gamma^{a}_{bc}=\frac{1}{2}\gamma^{ad}(\partial_{b}\gamma_{dc}+\partial_{c}\gamma_{bd}-\partial_{d}\gamma_{bc}),

(178)

and

\displaystyle\partial_{\theta}\gamma_{\theta\theta}=\partial_{\theta}(a^{2}\cos^{2}\theta+c^{2}\sin^{2}\theta)=2(c^{2}-a^{2})\sin\theta\cos\theta,

(179)

\displaystyle\partial_{\theta}\gamma_{\phi\phi}=\partial_{\theta}(a^{2}\sin^{2}\theta)=2a^{2}\sin\theta\cos\theta,

the required Christoffel symbols are calculated as

\Gamma^{\theta}_{\theta\theta}=\frac{(c^{2}-a^{2})\sin\theta\cos\theta}{\rho},\qquad\Gamma^{\phi}_{\theta\theta}=0,\qquad\Gamma^{\theta}_{\phi\phi}=-\frac{a^{2}\sin\theta\cos\theta}{\rho},\qquad\Gamma^{\phi}_{\phi\phi}=0,

(180)

so that

\sigma^{ab}\nabla_{a}\nabla_{b}\psi=\sigma^{\theta\theta}\left(\partial_{\theta}^{2}\psi-\Gamma^{\theta}_{\theta\theta}\partial_{\theta}\psi\right)+\sigma^{\phi\phi}\left(\partial_{\phi}^{2}\psi-\Gamma^{\theta}_{\phi\phi}\partial_{\theta}\psi\right)=\sigma^{\theta\theta}\partial_{\theta}^{2}\psi+\sigma^{\phi\phi}\partial_{\phi}^{2}\psi-\left(\sigma^{\theta\theta}\Gamma^{\theta}_{\theta\theta}+\sigma^{\phi\phi}\Gamma^{\theta}_{\phi\phi}\right)\partial_{\theta}\psi,

(181)

with

\sigma^{\theta\theta}\Gamma^{\theta}_{\theta\theta}=\frac{sc(c^{2}-a^{2})^{2}\sin^{3}\theta\cos\theta}{2a\,\rho^{7/2}},\qquad\sigma^{\phi\phi}\Gamma^{\theta}_{\phi\phi}=\frac{sc(c^{2}-a^{2})\sin\theta\cos\theta}{2a\rho^{5/2}}.

(182)

The full anisotropic operator (
19
) is then

C_{\sigma}\sigma^{ab}\nabla_{a}\nabla_{b}=sC_{\sigma}\left[\frac{c(c^{2}-a^{2})\sin^{2}\theta}{2a\,\rho^{5/2}}\partial_{\theta}^{2}+\frac{c(a^{2}-c^{2})}{2a^{3}\rho^{3/2}}\partial_{\phi}^{2}-\frac{c(c^{2}-a^{2})\sin\theta\cos\theta}{2a\rho^{5/2}}\left(1+\frac{(c^{2}-a^{2})\sin^{2}\theta}{\rho}\right)\partial_{\theta}\right].

(183)

The Laplace-Beltrami operator, being intrinsic, is independent of
s
, and reads

\Delta_{\gamma}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma}}\partial_{a}\left(\sqrt{\gamma}\,\gamma^{ab}\partial_{b}\right)=\frac{1}{\rho}\partial_{\theta}^{2}+\frac{a^{2}\cot\theta}{\rho^{2}}\partial_{\theta}+\frac{1}{a^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}\partial_{\phi}^{2}.

(184)

The curvature potential (
18
) becomes

V_{\rm H}=C_{H}H=-\frac{sC_{H}c(a^{2}+\rho)}{2a\,\rho^{3/2}}.

(185)

Collecting all terms, the wave equation on the spheroid reads

\displaystyle\Bigg\{

\displaystyle\left[\frac{1}{\rho}+sC_{\sigma}\frac{c(c^{2}-a^{2})\sin^{2}\theta}{2a\,\rho^{5/2}}\right]\partial_{\theta}^{2}+\left[\frac{1}{a^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}+sC_{\sigma}\frac{c(a^{2}-c^{2})}{2a^{3}\rho^{3/2}}\right]\partial_{\phi}^{2}

(186)

\displaystyle+\left[\frac{a^{2}\cot\theta}{\rho^{2}}-sC_{\sigma}\frac{c(c^{2}-a^{2})\sin\theta\cos\theta}{2a\rho^{5/2}}\left(1+\frac{(c^{2}-a^{2})\sin^{2}\theta}{\rho}\right)\right]\partial_{\theta}+k_{\rm spp}^{2}-sC_{H}\frac{c(a^{2}+\rho)}{2a\,\rho^{3/2}}\Bigg\}\psi=0.

Note that
C_{H}
and
C_{\sigma}
, defined in Eqs. (
18
) and (
19
), are complex numbers for a lossy metal with
\mathrm{Im}(\epsilon_{m})\neq 0
. In general, the Green’s function
G(\theta,\phi;\theta_{0},\phi_{0})
satisfies

\hat{L}\,G(\theta,\phi;\theta_{0},\phi_{0})=-\frac{\delta(\theta-\theta_{0})\delta(\phi-\phi_{0})}{a\sin\theta\sqrt{\rho}}

(187)

with the right-hand side representing a single point source located at
(\theta_{0},\phi_{0})
. Since all coefficients of
\hat{L}
are independent of
\phi
, as a result of the rotational symmetry, the azimuthal eigenfunctions are plane waves
e^{im\phi}
. Therefore, we can expand

G(\theta,\phi;\theta_{0},\phi_{0})=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}g_{m}(\theta,\theta_{0})\,e^{im(\phi-\phi_{0})},

(188)

using the completeness relation
\delta(\phi-\phi_{0})=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{m}e^{im(\phi-\phi_{0})}
for the right-hand side in Eq. (
187
). We therefore get

\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}\hat{L}\Big(g_{m}(\theta,\theta_{0})e^{im(\phi-\phi_{0})}\Big)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}\left[-\frac{\delta(\theta-\theta_{0})}{a\sin\theta\sqrt{\rho}}\right]e^{im(\phi-\phi_{0})}.

(189)

\hat{L}
is the differential operator in Eq. (
186
). Defining
\hat{L}_{m}=A(\theta)\partial_{\theta}^{2}+C(\theta)\partial_{\theta}+C_{m}(\theta)
, we get

\sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}\left\{\hat{L}_{m}g_{m}(\theta,\theta_{0})+\frac{\delta(\theta-\theta_{0})}{a\sin\theta\sqrt{\rho}}\right\}e^{im(\phi-\phi_{0})}=0.

(190)

Since the functions
e^{im(\phi-\phi_{0})}
form a complete orthogonal basis, we require

\hat{L}_{m}g_{m}(\theta,\theta_{0})=-\frac{\delta(\theta-\theta_{0})}{a\sin\theta\sqrt{\rho}},

(191)

for each m individually. Writing everything out, yields the one-dimensional ODE

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\Bigg\{\left[\frac{1}{\rho}+s\,C_{\sigma}\frac{c(c^{2}-a^{2})\sin^{2}\theta}{2a\,\rho^{5/2}}\right]\partial_{\theta}^{2}+\left[\frac{a^{2}\cot\theta}{\rho^{2}}-sC_{\sigma}\frac{c(c^{2}-a^{2})\sin\theta\cos\theta}{2a\rho^{5/2}}\left(1+\frac{(c^{2}-a^{2})\sin^{2}\theta}{\rho}\right)\right]\partial_{\theta}

(192)

\displaystyle-m^{2}\left[\frac{1}{a^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}+s\,C_{\sigma}\frac{c(a^{2}-c^{2})}{2a^{3}\rho^{3/2}}\right]+k_{\rm spp}^{2}-s\,C_{H}\frac{c(a^{2}+\rho)}{2a\,\rho^{3/2}}\Bigg\}g_{m}(\theta,\theta_{0})=-\frac{\delta(\theta-\theta_{0})}{a\sin\theta\sqrt{\rho}}.

Note that the
m
-dependence of the operator in Eq. (
192
) enters only as
m^{2}
. The ODEs for modes
+m
and
-m
are therefore identical, which means
g_{m}=g_{-m}
for all
m\in\mathbb{Z}
.

F.2
Sommerfeld radiation condition and perfectly matched layer

This section provides the preparation for the numerical computation. To enforce the Sommerfeld radiation condition
82
, meaning that waves propagate outward from the source without reflection, we introduce a perfectly matched layer (PML)
9
;
96
;
16
in the region
\theta\in(\theta_{\rm PML},\theta_{\rm max}]
with
\theta_{\rm PML}>\theta_{0}
through a complex coordinate stretching. For the PML, we define the stretched coordinate as

d\tilde{\theta}=\zeta(\theta)\,d\theta,\qquad\zeta(\theta)=1+i\sigma(\theta),\qquad\zeta^{\prime}(\theta)=i\sigma^{\prime}(\theta),\qquad\sigma(\theta)=\begin{cases}0&\theta\leq\theta_{\rm PML},\\
\sigma_{\rm max}\!\left(\dfrac{\theta-\theta_{\rm PML}}{\theta_{\rm max}-\theta_{\rm PML}}\right)^{3}&\theta>\theta_{\rm PML},\end{cases}

(193)

with
\sigma(\theta)
a smooth ramp-up of absorption and
\sigma_{\rm max}>0
controls the absorption strength. The derivative operators transform as

\partial_{\tilde{\theta}}=\frac{1}{\zeta(\theta)}\partial_{\theta},\qquad\partial_{\tilde{\theta}}^{2}=\frac{1}{\zeta^{2}(\theta)}\partial_{\theta}^{2}-\frac{\zeta^{\prime}(\theta)}{\zeta^{3}(\theta)}\partial_{\theta}.

(194)

We define the PML-modified coefficients

\displaystyle A(\tilde{\theta})=\frac{1}{\tilde{\rho}}+s\,C_{\sigma}\frac{c(c^{2}-a^{2})\sin^{2}\tilde{\theta}}{2a\,\tilde{\rho}^{5/2}},

(195)

\displaystyle B(\tilde{\theta})=\frac{a^{2}\cot\tilde{\theta}}{\tilde{\rho}^{2}}-sC_{\sigma}\frac{c(c^{2}-a^{2})\sin\tilde{\theta}\cos\tilde{\theta}}{2a\tilde{\rho}^{5/2}}\left(1+\frac{(c^{2}-a^{2})\sin^{2}\tilde{\theta}}{\tilde{\rho}}\right),

\displaystyle C_{m}(\tilde{\theta})=-m^{2}\!\left[\frac{1}{a^{2}\sin^{2}\tilde{\theta}}+s\,C_{\sigma}\frac{c(a^{2}-c^{2})}{2a^{3}\tilde{\rho}^{3/2}}\right]+k_{\rm spp}^{2}-s\,C_{H}\frac{c(a^{2}+\tilde{\rho})}{2a\,\tilde{\rho}^{3/2}},

with
\tilde{\rho}=a^{2}\cos^{2}\tilde{\theta}+c^{2}\sin^{2}\tilde{\theta}
. Eq. (
192
) is then rewritten as

\frac{A(\tilde{\theta})}{\zeta^{2}(\theta)}\,\partial_{\theta}^{2}\,g_{m}+\left[\frac{B(\tilde{\theta})}{\zeta(\theta)}-\frac{A(\tilde{\theta})\,\zeta^{\prime}(\theta)}{\zeta^{3}(\theta)}\right]\partial_{\theta}\,g_{m}+C_{m}(\tilde{\theta})\,g_{m}=-\frac{\delta(\theta-\theta_{0})}{a\sin\theta\sqrt{\rho}},

(196)

with

\tilde{\theta}=\tilde{\theta}(\theta)=\theta+i\int_{0}^{\theta}\sigma(\vartheta)d\vartheta,

(197)

and with the source term of the emitter evaluated at the real coordinate
\theta
, as the dipole sits outside the PML at
\theta_{0}<\theta_{\rm PML}
. Two additional boundary conditions must be introduced. For this, we examine the behavior of the ODE coefficients as
\theta\to 0
. Using
\sin\theta\approx\theta
,
\cos\theta\approx 1
and
\rho(0)=a^{2}
, the two singular terms are

B(\theta)\approx\frac{1}{a^{2}\theta},\qquad-\frac{m^{2}}{a^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}\approx-\frac{m^{2}}{a^{2}\theta^{2}},

(198)

so
\theta=0
is a regular singular point of the ODE for all
m
. To handle this, we impose a Dirichlet boundary condition

g_{m}(0,\theta_{0})=0,\qquad m\neq 0.

(199)

For
m=0
, we impose a Neumann boundary condition as

\partial_{\theta}g_{0}(\theta,\theta_{0})\bigg|_{\theta=0}=0.

(200)

While
g_{m}(\theta)
is continuous at
\theta=\theta_{0}
, there is a jump for the derivative, which we obtain by integrating Eq. (
196
) over
[\theta_{0}-\varepsilon,\theta_{0}+\varepsilon]
and then let
\varepsilon\to 0
. The terms involving
g_{m}
and
\partial_{\theta}g_{m}
vanish in this limit and only the
\partial_{\theta}^{2}
term survives, yielding

A(\theta_{0})\Bigl[\partial_{\theta}g_{m}\Bigr]_{\theta_{0}^{-}}^{\theta_{0}^{+}}=-\frac{1}{a\sin\theta_{0}\sqrt{\rho_{0}}}.

(201)

We discretize the continuous domain
\theta\in[0,\theta_{\rm max}]
using second-order central finite differences, which maps the differential operator to a tridiagonal matrix. The Neumann boundary condition for
m=0
is enforced up to second-order accuracy using a ghost-node method to prevent artificial back scattering at the pole. Finally, the source term, which is responsible for the jump in
\partial_{\theta}g_{m}
, is projected onto the discrete grid using a linear interpolation weighting. The full discrete Green’s function
g_{m}(\theta,\theta_{0})
is then obtained by a numerical inversion of the resulting linear system.

F.3
Collective eigenvalues

We place the
N
emitters in a ring configuration at a fixed polar angle
\theta_{0}
. As they are equally spaced along the azimuthal direction, the surface coordinates of the
j
-th emitter are
\mathbf{q}_{0}=(\theta_{0},\phi_{0,j})
, with the azimuthal angles given by

\phi_{0,j}=\frac{2\pi(j-1)}{N},\qquad j=1,2,\ldots,N.

(202)

The coupling between any two emitters in the ring is therefore written as, see Eq. (
33
) in the main text,

\displaystyle\Omega^{jl}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\mu_{0}\omega_{0}^{2}\wp^{2}}{\hbar}G_{\eta\eta}(\mathbf{q}_{j},\eta_{j}=d,\mathbf{q}_{l},\eta_{l}=d)

(203)

(204)

Because we know the eigenfunctions, we do not need to diagonalize the matrix

\sum_{l=1}^{N}\Omega^{jl}v_{l}^{(k)}=\lambda_{k}v_{j}^{(k)}\qquad\implies\qquad\lambda_{k}=\sum_{l=1}^{N}\Omega^{1l}e^{ik\frac{2\pi(l-1)}{N}}.

(205)

Substituting the expression for
\Omega^{1l}

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=1}^{N}\left[-\frac{\mu_{0}\omega_{0}^{2}\wp^{2}}{\hbar}C_{0}e^{-2\kappa_{d}d}\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}g_{m}(\theta_{0},\theta_{0})e^{-im\frac{2\pi(l-1)}{N}}\right]e^{ik\frac{2\pi(l-1)}{N}}

The sum over the index

\sum_{l=1}^{N}e^{i(k-m)\frac{2\pi(l-1)}{N}}=\sum_{l=1}^{N}\left(e^{i(k-m)\frac{2\pi}{N}}\right)^{l-1}=N\sum_{q=-\infty}^{\infty}\delta_{m,\,k-qN}.

(207)

Therefore, we get

\lambda_{k}=-\frac{\mu_{0}\omega_{0}^{2}\wp^{2}}{\hbar}C_{0}e^{-2\kappa_{d}d}\frac{N}{2\pi}\sum_{q=-\infty}^{\infty}g_{k+qN}(\theta_{0},\theta_{0}).

(208)

At this point, we define

S\equiv\sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}g_{m}(\theta_{0},\theta_{0}),\qquad S_{k}\equiv\sum_{q=-\infty}^{\infty}g_{k+qN}(\theta_{0},\theta_{0}),

(209)

for easier notation. Both
\mathrm{Re}[S]
and
\mathrm{Re}[S_{k}]
diverge logarithmically, whereas
\mathrm{Im}[S]
and
\mathrm{Im}[S_{k}]
are finite. Let us write
\lambda_{k}=\Delta_{k}-i\frac{\gamma_{k}}{2}

\gamma_{k}=-2\,\mathrm{Im}[\lambda_{k}]=\frac{\mu_{0}\omega_{0}^{2}\wp^{2}}{\pi\hbar}N\,\mathrm{Im}\left[C_{0}e^{-2\kappa_{d}d}S_{k}\right],

(210)

in which we must keep

(211)

As argued in the main text, we may use
e^{-2\kappa_{d}d}\approx 1
and discard the divergent point-dipole artifact contributions
\mathrm{Im}\left[C_{0}\right]\,\mathrm{Re}\left[S\right]
and
\mathrm{Im}\left[C_{0}\right]\,\mathrm{Re}\left[S_{k}\right]

(212)

respectively, yielding the normalized collective decay rate for mode

(213)

For the cooperative frequency shift, we start from

(214)

\frac{\Delta_{k}^{\rm coop}}{\gamma_{0}^{\rm curved}}=-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{Re}\!\left[NS_{k}-S\right]}{\mathrm{Im}\!\left[S\right]}.

(217)

Appendix G
Emitter decay into SPP modes on flat interfaces

G.1
General equations

We consider a planar interface at
z=0
separating a dielectric (
\epsilon_{d}>0
,
z>0
) and a metal (
\epsilon_{m}<0
,
z<0
) domain. Both media are non-magnetic (
\mu_{d}=\mu_{m}=1
). A quantum emitter modeled as a normally oriented electric dipole
\mathbf{p}=p_{z}\hat{\mathbf{z}}
is located at
\mathbf{r}_{0}=(0,0,z_{0})
with
z_{0}=d
. The electric field emitted by the dipole is governed by the free-space dyadic Green’s tensor. For the vertically oriented dipole, the p-polarized contribution reads
68

G_{0,zz}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}_{0})=\frac{i}{8\pi^{2}k_{d}^{2}}\iint_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{k_{\parallel}^{2}}{k_{dz}}e^{i[k_{x}(x-x_{0})+k_{y}(y-y_{0})+k_{dz}|z-z_{0}|]}dk_{x}dk_{y},

(218)

with
k_{d}=k_{0}\sqrt{\epsilon_{d}}
is the wavenumber in the dielectric,
k_{\parallel}^{2}=k_{x}^{2}+k_{y}^{2}
is the squared in-plane momentum, and
k_{dz}\penalty\ =\penalty\ \sqrt{k_{d}^{2}-k_{\parallel}^{2}}
is the out-of-plane wavenumber, with, importantly,
\mathrm{Im}\left[k_{dz}\right]\geq 0
. The scattered field
G_{zz}^{\rm ref}
, generated by the presence of the metal interface, is found by Setting
x_{0}=y_{0}=0
, so the reflected tensor component reads

G_{zz}^{\rm ref}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}_{0})=\frac{i}{8\pi^{2}k_{d}^{2}}\iint_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{k_{\parallel}^{2}}{k_{dz}}r_{p}(k_{\parallel})e^{i[k_{x}x+k_{y}y+k_{dz}(z+z_{0})]}dk_{x}dk_{y},

(219)

with the reflection coefficient

r_{p}(k_{\parallel})=\frac{\epsilon_{m}k_{dz}-\epsilon_{d}k_{mz}}{\epsilon_{m}k_{dz}+\epsilon_{d}k_{mz}},

(220)

and
k_{mz}=\sqrt{k_{m}^{2}-k_{\parallel}^{2}}
.
So far, we just followed the equations from Ref.
68
. We exploit the rotational symmetry by substituting
k_{x}=k_{\parallel}\cos\phi_{k}
and
k_{y}=k_{\parallel}\sin\phi_{k}
. The spatial coordinates are then defined as
x=\rho\cos\phi_{r}
and
y=\rho\sin\phi_{r}
. The integration measure transforms as
dk_{x}dk_{y}=k_{\parallel}dk_{\parallel}d\phi_{k}
, and the phase becomes
k_{x}x+k_{y}y=k_{\parallel}\rho\cos(\phi_k - \phi_r)
. Using

\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{ik_{\parallel}\rho\cos(\phi_k - \phi_r)}d\phi_{k}=2\pi J_{0}(k_{\parallel}\rho),

(221)

we can reduce the 2D surface integral to a 1D integral over the radial momentum as

G_{zz}^{\rm ref}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}_{0})=\frac{i}{4\pi k_{d}^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}dk_{\parallel}\,\frac{k_{\parallel}^{3}}{k_{dz}}r_{p}(k_{\parallel})J_{0}(k_{\parallel}\rho)e^{ik_{dz}(z+z_{0})}.

(222)

We now extend the integration path to the entire real axis
(-\infty,\infty)
and use the identity
J_{0}(x)=\frac{1}{2}[H_{0}^{(1)}(x)+H_{0}^{(2)}(x)]
. Let us define the integrand as

f(k_{\parallel})=\frac{k_{\parallel}^{3}}{\kappa_{d}}r_{p}(k_{\parallel})e^{-\kappa_{d}(z+z_{0})}.

(223)

This function has odd parity, since
f(-k_{\parallel})=-f(k_{\parallel})
. Using the identity
1

H^{(2)}_{0}\left(xe^{-i\pi}\right)=-H^{(1)}_{0}\left(x\right),

(224)

we can rewrite the integral as

G_{zz}^{\rm ref}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}_{0})=\frac{i}{8\pi k_{d}^{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{k_{\parallel}^{3}}{k_{dz}}\,r_{p}(k_{\parallel})\,H_{0}^{(1)}(k_{\parallel}\rho)\,e^{ik_{dz}(z+z_{0})}\,dk_{\parallel}.

(225)

Now, we assume that
\mathrm{Im}\left[k_{\parallel}\right]>0
, and use that for large arguments
|k_{\parallel}\rho|
, we can write
1

H^{(1)}_{0}\left(k_{\parallel}\rho\right)\sim\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi k_{\parallel}\rho}}e^{i\left(k_{\parallel}\rho-\pi/4\right)}\to 0.

(226)

For large
|k_{\parallel}|
, we can furthermore approximate
k_{dz}=\sqrt{k_{d}^{2}-k_{\parallel}^{2}}\approx\pm ik_{\parallel}
, such that

e^{ik_{dz}(z+z_{0})}\approx e^{-k_{\parallel}(z+z_{0})}\to 0

(227)

for
z+z_{0}>0
, since
\mathrm{Im}\left[k_{dz}\right]\geq 0
. Therefore, the integrand vanishes for
\mathrm{Im}[k_{\parallel}]>0
as
|k_{\parallel}|\to\infty
, which allows us to close the integration contour
\mathcal{C}
in the upper half of the complex plane by adding a semicircular arc of radius
R\to\infty
. Since the integrand vanishes on this arc, we have

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}=\oint_{\mathcal{C}}-\int_{\rm arc}=\oint_{\mathcal{C}},

(228)

and using the residue theorem, we get

G_{zz}^{\rm ref}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}_{0})=\frac{i}{8\pi k_{d}^{2}}\cdot 2\pi i\sum_{j}\,\mathrm{Res}\left[\frac{k_{\parallel}^{3}}{k_{dz}}\,r_{p}(k_{\parallel})\,H_{0}^{(1)}(k_{\parallel}\rho)\,e^{ik_{dz}(z+z_{0})},\,k_{\parallel,j}\right],

(229)

with the sum running over all poles
k_{\parallel,j}
enclosed by
\mathcal{C}
. The only pole of the integrand in the upper half-plane originates from the zeros of the denominator of
r_{p}(k_{\parallel})
, i.e., at

\epsilon_{m}\,k_{dz}+\epsilon_{d}\,k_{mz}=0,

(230)

which is the SPP dispersion relation satisfied at
k_{\parallel}=k_{\rm spp}

60
. For a metal with
\mathrm{Im}[\epsilon_{m}]>0

G_{zz}^{\rm spp}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}_{0})=\frac{1}{k_{0}^{2}\epsilon_{d}}\frac{k_{\rm spp}^{3}}{\kappa_{d}}\,\frac{i}{4}H_{0}^{(1)}(k_{\rm spp}\rho)\,e^{-\kappa_{d}(z+z_{0})}\cdot\mathrm{Res}\left[r_{p},k_{\rm spp}\right],

(231)

with
k_{dz}^{\rm spp}=k_{dz}(k_{\rm spp})=i\kappa_{d}

r_{p}
has a simple pole at

\mathrm{Res}\left[r_{p},k_{\rm spp}\right]=\frac{\epsilon_{m}k_{dz}-\epsilon_{d}k_{mz}}{\frac{d}{dk_{\parallel}}\left(\epsilon_{m}k_{dz}+\epsilon_{d}k_{mz}\right)}\Bigg|_{k_{\parallel}=k_{\rm spp}}=-\frac{2k_{0}}{(\epsilon_{m}-\epsilon_{d})}\left(\frac{\epsilon_{d}\epsilon_{m}}{\epsilon_{m}+\epsilon_{d}}\right)^{3/2}.

(232)

So we can finally write

G_{zz}^{\rm spp}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}_{0})=C_{0}\,e^{-2\kappa_{d}d}\,\,G^{\rm flat}_{\rm 2D}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}^{\prime}),

(233)

written as Eq. (
35

C_{0}=\frac{-2k_{0}\epsilon_{m}^{3}\epsilon_{d}\sqrt{-(\epsilon_{m}+\epsilon_{d})}}{(\epsilon_{m}-\epsilon_{d})(\epsilon_{m}+\epsilon_{d})^{3}}.

(234)

Here, we have set
z=d
, since for the interaction between emitters, we need the evaluation at the positions of the dipoles
68
.

G.2
Lossy metals

Considering material loss, we write
\epsilon_{m}=\epsilon_{m}^{\prime}+i\epsilon_{m}^{\prime\prime}
with
\epsilon_{m}^{\prime}<-\epsilon_{d}<0
and
0<\epsilon_{m}^{\prime\prime}\ll|\epsilon_{m}^{\prime}|
, and expand each factor in
C_{0}
to first order in
\epsilon_{m}^{\prime\prime}
. For the cubic factor in the numerator:

\epsilon_{m}^{3}\approx(\epsilon_{m}^{\prime})^{3}\!\left(1+\frac{3i\epsilon_{m}^{\prime\prime}}{\epsilon_{m}^{\prime}}\right).

(235)

For the square root, since
\epsilon_{m}^{\prime}+\epsilon_{d}<0
we have
-(\epsilon_{m}+\epsilon_{d})=|\epsilon_{m}^{\prime}+\epsilon_{d}|-i\epsilon_{m}^{\prime\prime}
, giving:

\sqrt{-(\epsilon_{m}+\epsilon_{d})}\approx\sqrt{|\epsilon_{m}^{\prime}+\epsilon_{d}|}\left(1+\frac{i\epsilon_{m}^{\prime\prime}}{2(\epsilon_{m}^{\prime}+\epsilon_{d})}\right),

(236)

where we used
|\epsilon_{m}^{\prime}+\epsilon_{d}|=-(\epsilon_{m}^{\prime}+\epsilon_{d})
to absorb the sign. For the two denominator factors:

(\epsilon_{m}-\epsilon_{d})\approx(\epsilon_{m}^{\prime}-\epsilon_{d})\!\left(1+\frac{i\epsilon_{m}^{\prime\prime}}{\epsilon_{m}^{\prime}-\epsilon_{d}}\right),\qquad(\epsilon_{m}+\epsilon_{d})^{3}\approx(\epsilon_{m}^{\prime}+\epsilon_{d})^{3}\!\left(1+\frac{3i\epsilon_{m}^{\prime\prime}}{\epsilon_{m}^{\prime}+\epsilon_{d}}\right).

(237)

Writing

C_{0}=C_{0}^{(0)}(1+i\epsilon_{m}^{\prime\prime}\beta),\qquad C_{0}^{(0)}=-\frac{2k_{0}(\epsilon_{m}^{\prime})^{3}\epsilon_{d}\sqrt{|\epsilon_{m}^{\prime}+\epsilon_{d}|}}{(\epsilon_{m}^{\prime}-\epsilon_{d})(\epsilon_{m}^{\prime}+\epsilon_{d})^{3}}\,\in\,\mathbb{R},

(238)

and combining the four first-order contributions from numerator and denominator via
(1+ia)(1+ib)^{-1}\approx 1+i(a-b)
, we obtain:

\frac{\mathrm{Im}(C_{0})}{\mathrm{Re}(C_{0})}=\epsilon_{m}^{\prime\prime}\left(\frac{3}{\epsilon_{m}^{\prime}}-\frac{5}{2(\epsilon_{m}^{\prime}+\epsilon_{d})}-\frac{1}{\epsilon_{m}^{\prime}-\epsilon_{d}}\right).

(239)

Using Ref.
43
, a short calculation shows that for gold at
\lambda_{0}=800\,\mathrm{nm}
with
\epsilon_{m}\approx-24.15+1.51i
, we get
\mathrm{Im}(C_{0})/\mathrm{Re}(C_{0})\approx 0.035
, and for silver at
\lambda_{0}=600\,\mathrm{nm}
with
\epsilon_{m}\approx-16.12+0.44i
, we find
\mathrm{Im}(C_{0})/\mathrm{Re}(C_{0})\approx 0.016
.
