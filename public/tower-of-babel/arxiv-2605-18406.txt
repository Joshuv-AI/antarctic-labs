Abstract
. The Volterra signature extends the classical path signature by incorporating general matrix-valued kernel into its iterated integral structure, yielding a flexible notion of memory for time series. Its components can be viewed as successive Picard iterates of linear controlled Volterra equations, making their exact computation of additional mathematical interest. However, the kernel introduces substantial algorithmic challenges. We provide a resolution by first decomposing the Chen-type convolution relation established in [ 13 ] into analytic and arithmetic parts, and then introducing several efficient algorithms: a general approximative scheme with quadratic complexity O ⁡ ( J 2 ) O(J^{2}) in the number of time steps J J , an FFT-based acceleration with complexity O ⁡ ( J ​ log ⁡ J ) O(J\log J) for convolution kernels on uniform grids, and an exact recursion with complexity O ⁡ ( J ​ R 2 ) O(JR^{2}) for kernels admitting a state-space representation of dimension R R ; retaining standard signature complexity in the path dimension and truncation level N N . We further show that the number of factors in matrix-valued kernels of the form K ⁡ ( t , s ) = ∑ p k p ​ ( t − s ) ​ A p K(t,s)=\sum_{p}k_{p}(t-s)A_{p} do not increase the asymptotic complexity in J J and N N . Finally, we derive a finite-difference predictor–corrector scheme for the associated Volterra signature kernel. All algorithms are implemented in the publicly available JAX-based package tensordev .

1.
Introduction

Iterated path integrals, first rigorously studied by Chen
[
7
]
and later developed by Lyons
[
25
]
into a cornerstone of rough path analysis before being branded as the path
signature
, have been established as a highly efficient feature map for analysis of sequential data.
As such, signatures yield a universal graded representation of path features with widespread use in machine learning across many data structures, from geometric trajectory data (e.g. pen strokes) through irregular event records (e.g. health care records) to high-frequency financial streams such as prices and volatility; see e.g.
[
3
,
26
,
8
]
and references therein.

The signature is defined on an analytical level for a path
x:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
with continuous derivative
\dot{x}
by the fundamental linear equation

(1.1)

\mathrm{Sig}(x)_{s,t}\;=\;1+\int_{s}^{t}\mathrm{Sig}(x)_{s,u}\otimes\dot{x}_{u}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{u},\qquad 0\leq s\leq t\leq T,

where
\otimes
denotes the tensor product in
T((\mathbb{R}^{d})):=\prod_{n=0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\otimes k}
, hence understanding
1=(1,0,\dots)\in T((\mathbb{R}^{d}))
.
This equation is only formally implicit; solving it tensor level-wise the signature directly expands into iterated integrals of
x
with itself (to be presented further below).
The property that makes the computation of signatures particularly convenient is Chen’s relation for joining signatures on adjacent path segments:

(1.2)

\mathrm{Sig}(x)_{s,t}=\mathrm{Sig}(x)_{s,u}\otimes\mathrm{Sig}(x)_{u,t},\qquad 0\leq s\leq u\leq t\leq T.

While the signature keeps track of the entire history of the path, i.e. it separates paths up to so-called tree-like equivalence and time reparameterization
[
15
]
, it does so without discriminating between recent and distant changes in
x
.
In applications one is often interested in introducing explicit control over memory effects.
A very elementary way of introducing such structure, at least going back to Volterra
[
39
,
40
]
, is via a kernel

K:\Delta^{2}=\{(t,s)\;|\;0\leq s\leq t\leq T\}\to\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{m}),

where memory effects act on different scales: the influence of distant history is governed by the decay of
K
away from the diagonal (e.g. exponential versus power-law decay), whereas the influence of recent history is governed by the behavior of
K
near the diagonal (e.g. fractional-type), and different effects such as periodic/oscillatory weighting can be reproduced as well.
The pressing question is therefore
not why
but rather
how
to incorporate a kernel into the signature (
1.1
).

Recently, in
[
13
]
a general approach was introduced based on earlier work on Volterra rough paths
[
16
]
via the following fundamental Volterra equation

(1.3)

\mathrm{VSig}(x;K)^{\tau}_{s,t}=1+\int_{s}^{t}\mathrm{VSig}(x;K)^{u}_{s,u}\otimes K(\tau,u)\dot{x}_{u}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u,\qquad 0\leq s\leq t\leq\tau\leq T.

While this may not be the only way to incorporate a kernel (cf.
[
20
,
4
]
for the exponential case), it is a natural approach for at least four reasons presented in
[
13
]
.
First, it allows for generic kernels under minimal integrability assumptions, including fractional kernels up to a certain singularity.
Secondly, the path separation property is retained under suitable assumptions on the kernel, entailing a universal approximation theorem on path space.
Thirdly, it satisfies a Chen-type relation

(1.4)

\displaystyle\mathrm{VSig}(x;K)^{\tau}_{s,t}=[{\mathrm{VSig}(x;K)_{s,u}}\oast{\mathrm{VSig}(x;K)}]^{\tau}_{u,t}\qquad 0\leq s\leq u\leq t\leq\tau\leq T,

where
\oast
denotes a combined tensor product and integration operation, to be made explicit below.
Lastly, the corresponding
signature kernel
,

\kappa^{K}(x,y)_{s,t}=\left\langle\mathrm{VSig}(x;K)^{s}_{0,s},\mathrm{VSig}(y;K)^{t}_{0,t}\right\rangle,

satisfies a closed two-parameter integral equation in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, thereby allowing for the application of the kernel trick in typical learning tasks on path space.

In this paper, we treat the task of computing these
Volterra signatures
and signature kernels on time series data.
For analytical purposes, the Chen relation (
1.4
) serves a role similar to its classical counterpart (
1.2
) (see
[
16
]
for its use in lifting Volterra rough paths). However, its computational consequences are far from evident.
Indeed, since the convolution
\oast
operation is not purely arithmetic, even when
x
is discretized a tractable computation without resorting to quadratures on the kernel requires additional structure and understanding.
We provide such structure for a large palette of kernels, including families of fractional and exponential type kernels.
Furthermore, even when the analytic component of
\oast
is resolved, the defining equation (
1.3
) suggests that computation is tied to quadratic complexity in the number of time steps.
We will show that this hurdle can be overcome for convolution kernels on uniform time grids using the fast Fourier transform (FFT) and for a large class of matrix exponential kernels by lifting the state.

To further illustrate the challenges that arise and the mathematical tools needed for the computation of Volterra signatures, we first compare the situation to the classical signature:

Computation of classical signatures.

Since the signature is defined for paths of a continuous time-parameter, when provided with a data stream
(x_{0},\dots,x_{J})\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{J+1}
we first need to interpolate to a continuous piecewise smooth path
x:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
x_{t_{j}}=x_{j}

(j=0,\dots,J)
.
Chen’s identity then allows to use

(1.5)

\mathrm{Sig}(x)_{t_{0},t_{j}}=\mathrm{Sig}(x)_{t_{0},t_{j-1}}\otimes\mathrm{Sig}(x)_{t_{j-1},t_{j}},

as a dynamic computation step.
For piecewise linear interpolation, the signature on the interval
[t_{j-1},t_{j}]
then simply reduces to a tensor exponential

\mathrm{Sig}(x)_{t_{j-1},t_{j}}~=~\exp_{\otimes}(x_{j}-x_{{j-1}})~=~1+\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{1}{k!}(x_{j}-x_{{j-1}})^{\otimes k},

allowing an algorithmic evaluation of (
1.5
) by iteratively applying tensor multiplications.
More precisely, the most efficient way of evaluating (
1.5
) is via a Horner-type scheme, which evaluates the tensor product with the previous signature and the exponential in a single run (introduced in this context in
[
21
]
).
The total costs of computing the signature are then of the order
Jd^{N}
asymptotically for
J
and
N
large, noting that
d^{N}
matches the output dimension asymptotically.

While linear interpolation may be seen as a modeling choice with apparent computational advantages, piecewise smooth interpolation itself is crucial.
If we were to directly lift the data
(x_{1},...,x_{J})
to a feature vector
\mathbf{S}
in the tensor algebra by using an Euler discretization of the signature fundamental equation (
1.1
)

\mathbf{S}_{j}=\mathbf{S}_{{j-1}}+\mathbf{S}_{{j-1}}\otimes(x_{j}-x_{{j-1}}),\qquad j=1,\dots,J,

then this feature does not share the same algebraic properties as the signature (the shuffle identity) and hence does not have the same expressiveness; that is, it does not enjoy the same universal approximation property.
Note that a minimal extension of
\mathbf{S}
to incorporate such algebraic structure is put forward in
[
10
]
as the
iterated sums signature
.

The need for numerical precision to preserve expressivity also appears in the computation of signature kernels
[
23
,
31
]
. In practice, accuracy is often improved by replacing the observed paths with their linear interpolations on refined grids. Since this leads to significant computational bottlenecks, the accuracy of the numerical scheme plays an important role and remain an active topic in the literature
[
24
,
6
,
36
]
.

Computation of Volterra signatures.

Having advocated for exact computations and approximations for signatures, let us highlight the challenges we are facing in the Volterra case and the remedies proposed in this paper. Let us start by mentioning that for a given suitably integrable kernel
K:\Delta^{2}\to\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{m})
, the Chen identity can be stated as

(1.6)

\displaystyle\mathrm{VSig}(x;K)^{\tau}_{t_{0},t_{j}}=\mathrm{VSig}(x;K)_{t_{0},t_{j-1}}^{\tau}-\int_{t_{j-1}}^{t_{j}}\mathrm{VSig}(x;K)_{t_{0},t_{j-1}}^{s}\otimes\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{s}}\mathrm{VSig}(x;K)_{s,t_{j}}^{\tau}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{s}.

In contrast to (
1.5
) the evaluation of (
1.6
) still requires the resolution of analytic operations.
Turning this identity into a computationally tractable scheme is the topic of this paper.
The main results and observations of this paper are summarized as follows:

(1)

An exact resolution of the single Chen step for Volterra signatures (
1.6
) in terms of iterated tensor products is generally not possible.
However, for piecewise linear interpolation we can
separate the computation of
\mathrm{VSig}(x;K)_{s,t}^{\tau}
with
(s,t)\in\Delta^{2}_{t_{j-1},t_{j}}
into an analytic (pre-computation) part depending only on the kernel
K
, and an arithmetic part depending only on the increment
(x_{t_{j}}-x_{t_{j-1}})
(cf.
Section

2.3
).
For a variety of common kernels, such as exponential and fractional-type, the analytic part of the computation results in closed-form and efficiently computable weight factors (see
Sections

3.4
and
4.2
).

(2)

Breaking down further to the increment level with Chen’s identity, a representation of the Volterra signature in terms of weighted sums of monomials of increments is possible (cf.
Section

2.3
), and once again the weight terms can be efficiently computed for a wide range of kernels.
A direct implementation of this fully combinatorial expansion of the Volterra signature is generally not feasible, but it readily connects to two approaches for which an efficient dynamic scheme is possible.

(3)

The first method alluded to in point (
2
) is based on the approximation

\mathrm{VSig}(x;K)_{t_{0},t_{j-1}}^{s}\approx\mathrm{VSig}(x;K)_{t_{0},t_{j-1}}^{t_{j-1}}+\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{B}}{\bf C}^{\alpha}_{t_{j-1},t_{j}}(s-t_{j-1})^{\alpha},

inserted into (
1.6
).
Provided that the Volterra signature on single intervals and its integrals against
(s-\cdot)^{\alpha}
can be computed exactly, see point (
1
) above, this yields an algorithmic procedure with computational cost proportional to
J^{2}
, where
J
denotes the length of the data stream.
The choice of exponents
\mathcal{B}
depends on the regularity of the kernel and on the desired order of convergence. For smooth kernels, integer powers suffice, for instance
\mathcal{B}=\{1,2\}
. For kernels with a fractional singularity of order
\beta-1
, one may instead choose
\mathcal{B}=\{\beta,1\}
.
This algorithm and its numerical accuracy are substantiated for general kernels in
3.5
, and specialized to fractional kernels in

3.6
.

(4)

Specializing to convolution kernels
K(t,s)=K(t-s)
, the inner iteration in the
general Volterra scheme takes the form of a discrete
convolution. This allows us to identify an algorithm that performs FFTs recursively over tensor levels
1
1

1

We note the similarity to the type of FFT acceleration used for higher-order schemes for Volterra convolution equations; see, e.g.,
[
14
]
.
, thereby reducing the computational cost in the sequence length from quadratic to
O(J\log J)
(cf. Section
3.3
).

(5)

For kernels of exponential-polynomial and periodic type (and linear combinations thereof) we can make use of a
state space lift
to provide an
exact
scheme that likewise overcomes the quadratic costs.
This algorithm is substantiated in
4.12
, and its costs are proportional to
J\times R^{2}
, where
R
is the dimension of the state space.
In this case, to compute the weight terms associated with the kernel, we make use of their relation to higher-order Fréchet derivatives of the matrix exponential map, which allows us to resort to efficient algorithms from numerical linear algebra (cf.
Section

4.2
).

(6)

Additional complexity in all of the above mentioned results stems from the fact that we treat matrix valued kernels.
We show that this becomes particularly tractable for general convolution kernels and piecewise constant kernels, where the number of matrix kernel components in
K(t,s)=k_{1}(t,s)A_{1}+\dots+k_{q}(t,s)A_{q}
with scalar kernels
k_{p}:\Delta^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
and matrices
A_{p}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{m})
does not influence the asymptotic order of computational costs in
J
and
N
.

(7)

Finally, for finite-state-space kernels we also treat the associated Volterra signature kernel. Using the kernel trick developed in
[
13
]
, its computation reduces to a closed system of two-parameter augmented Goursat-type equations with values in
\mathbb{R}^{R\times R}
. We propose an explicit predictor–corrector finite-difference scheme for this system, which can be evaluated cell-wise along anti-diagonals and also allows for static kernel lifts of the input paths (cf.
Section

5
).

All algorithms proposed in this paper have been thoroughly implemented and are publicly available at

https://github.com/hagerpa/tensordev
.
The implementation is based on the JAX backend
[
5
]
, which provides competitive computational efficiency and enables automatic differentiation with respect to all parameters.
A numerical validation of the implementation is presented in
Appendix

A
.

2.
Volterra signatures of piecewise linear paths

In this section, we provide representations of the Volterra signature for piecewise linear paths that decompose to the increment level of the underlying path, while keeping the matrix kernels in a general analytic form.
This expansion reveals the computational intricacies of the Volterra signature and will be used to derive algorithms in
Sections

3
and
4
.
We begin with the necessary algebraic preliminaries, and then briefly recall the definition and some elementary properties of the Volterra signature; we refer to
[
13
, Section 2]
for a full exposition.

2.1.
Preliminaries

We introduce some standard notation used in the study of signatures. Throughout this article, we focus on Euclidean spaces
V=\mathbb{R}^{d}
for some
d\in\mathbb{N}
. We begin with the notion of words over a given alphabet.

Definition 2.1
.

For any
d\in\mathbb{N}
, we call
\mathcal{A}_{d}=\{1,\dots,d\}
the alphabet of
d
letters. Moreover, we denote by
\mathcal{W}_{d}^{n}
the set of words of length
n\in\mathbb{N}
over the alphabet
\mathcal{A}_{d}
, that is

(2.1)

\mathcal{W}_{d}^{n}:=\left\{w=i_{1}\cdots i_{n}:i_{1},\dots,i_{n}\in\mathcal{A}_{d}\right\}.

For
n=0
we write
\mathcal{W}_{d}^{0}=\{\varnothing\}
, where
\varnothing
denotes the empty word. Finally, we denote by
\mathcal{W}_{d}
the set of all words of any length, and whenever
d
is clear from the context we write
\mathcal{W}=\mathcal{W}_{d}
.

Next, we introduce the extended tensor algebra –the ambient space for signatures –equipped with addition and a product, which turns it into an algebra.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
\{e_{1},\dots,e_{d}\}\subset V
be a basis.
Set
V^{\otimes 0}=\mathbb{R}
and for
n\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1}
denote by
V^{\otimes n}
the
n
-fold tensor power of the vector space
V
.
For any word
w=i_{1}\cdots i_{n}\in\mathcal{W}_{d}
we define
e_{w}=e_{i_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes e_{i_{n}}\in V^{\otimes n}
and note that
\{e_{w}\,|\,w\in\mathcal{W}^{n}_{d}\}
forms a basis of
V^{\otimes n}
.
The
extended tensor algebra
over
V
is given by the direct product

T((V)):=\prod_{n\geq 0}V^{\otimes n},

which forms a vector space by componentwise summation and scalar multiplication.
For an element
\mathbf{x}
in
T((V))
we will use the following equivalent notation as a sequence and as a formal series for the decomposition into its tensor
levels

\mathbf{x}=(\mathbf{x}^{(0)},\mathbf{x}^{(1)},\dots)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathbf{x}^{(n)}\in T((V)),

so that
\mathbf{x}^{(n)}\in V^{\otimes n}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
We define the
tensor product

\otimes
on
T((V))
in terms of the concatenation product of basis elements, i.e.,

e_{w}\otimes e_{v}=e_{wv}
for all
w,v\in\mathcal{W},

which extends to all of
T((V))
by bilinearity.
In particular, for
{\bf a},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in T((V))
we may write

\mathbf{z}={\bf a}+\mathbf{x}\otimes\mathbf{y},\qquad\mathbf{z}^{(n)}={\bf a}^{(n)}+\sum_{k=0}^{n}\mathbf{x}^{(k)}\otimes\mathbf{y}^{(n-k)}\in V^{\otimes n},\qquad n\in\mathbb{N}.

In order to work with finite truncations of signatures, we next introduce the canonical projection on
T((\mathbb{R}^{d}))
.

Definition 2.3
.

We write
\pi_{n}:T((V))\to V^{\otimes n}
for the canonical projection, so that
\pi_{n}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{x}^{(n)}
for all
\mathbf{x}\in T((V))
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Furthermore, for
N\in\mathbb{N}
we define the
tensor truncation

\pi_{\leq N}:T((V))\to T((V)),\qquad\mathbf{x}\mapsto(\mathbf{x}^{(0)},\mathbf{x}^{(1)},\dots,\mathbf{x}^{(N)},0,\dots).

The image of the truncation map
T^{N}(V):=\pi_{\leq N}T((V))
is called the
truncated tensor algebra
, which indeed forms an algebra under the truncated tensor product

\mathbf{x}\otimes_{N}\mathbf{y}:=\pi_{\leq N}(\mathbf{x}\otimes\mathbf{y}),\qquad\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in T^{N}(V).

We also define the tensor algebra by
T(V)=\cup_{N\geq 0}T^{N}(V)
.

Finally, we introduce the
shuffle product
on
T(V)
, a commutative product that will be of importance to us for the formulation of certain algorithms in Section
3
.

Definition 2.4
.

We define the shuffle product
\shuffle
for words by setting

w\shuffle\varnothing=\varnothing\shuffle w=w,\qquad w\in\mathcal{W}_{d},

and then recursively

(iw)\shuffle(jv):=i\big(w\shuffle(jv)\big)+j\big((iw)\shuffle v\big),\qquad i,j\in\mathcal{A}_{d},\;w,v\in\mathcal{W}_{d},

where
iw
denotes concatenation of the letter
i
with the word
w
.
Note that the shuffle of a word
i_{1}\cdots i_{n}\in\mathcal{W}
and a single letter
j\in\mathcal{A}_{d}
simply reduces to

(2.2)

(i_{1}\cdots i_{n})\shuffle j=\sum_{k=0}^{n}i_{1}\cdots i_{k}\,j\,i_{k+1}\cdots i_{n}.

We extend
\shuffle
to a bilinear and here symmetric map on
T(V)
by the identification
e_{i}\leftrightarrow i\in\mathcal{A}_{d}
.

2.2.
The Volterra Signature

As motivated in the introduction, we focus here on Lipschitz continuous paths
x:[0,T]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
, writing
x\in\mathcal{C}^{0,1}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and on the following class of matrix-valued kernels.

Definition 2.5
.

Consider a matrix-valued kernel
K:\Delta^{2}\to\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{m})
. In the sequel we assume
K\in L^{\infty,1}(\Delta^{2};\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{m}))
, where the latter space stands for the set of measurable functions on
\Delta^{2}
which satisfy

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\int_{0}^{t}|K(t,s)|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{s}<\infty,

with
|\cdot|
denoting here any matrix norm on
\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{m})
.

In the following definition, we introduce the Volterra signature as the collection of iterated integrals of
x
weighted by the kernel
K
. As discussed in
[
13
, Section 2.3]
, the resulting object is indeed the unique solution to the fundamental equation (
1.3
) motivated in the introduction.

Definition 2.6
.

Consider a signal
x\in\mathcal{C}^{0,1}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})
, as well as a kernel
K
with values in
\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{m})
that satisfies
Section

2.2
.
We define the
Volterra signature

\mathrm{VSig}
component-wise for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
i_{1}\cdots i_{n}\in\mathcal{W}^{n}_{m}
and
(s,t,\tau)\in\Delta^{3}
by

(2.3)

\mathrm{VSig}(x;K)^{i_{1}\cdots i_{n},\tau}_{s,t}=\int_{\Delta^{n}_{s,t}}\prod_{l=1}^{n}K^{i_{l}}(r_{l+1},r_{l})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x_{r_{l}}},

with the convention
r_{n+1}=\tau
, and where we denote by
K^{i_{l}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x
the component
i_{l}
in the
\mathbb{R}^{m}
valued vector
K(\tau,\cdot)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x_{\cdot}}
. The full Volterra signature
\mathrm{VSig}(x;K)
is then defined by the formal tensor series

\displaystyle\mathrm{VSig}(x;K)^{\tau}_{s,t}:=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathrm{VSig}(x;K)^{(n),\tau}_{s,t}:=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\in\mathcal{W}\\
|w|=n\end{subarray}}\mathrm{VSig}(x;K)^{w,\tau}_{s,t}e_{w}\in T((\mathbb{R}^{m})),

recalling that
T((\mathbb{R}^{m}))
is introduced in
Section

2.1
.

Next, we introduce the aforementioned convolutional tensor product
\oast
, which yields the Chen relation central to this work. We emphasize that the definition used here differs from the original one introduced in
[
16
]
, but it is shown to be equivalent in
[
13
, Lemma 2.26]
.

Definition 2.7
.

Consider a pair
(x,K)
as in
Section

2.2
with Volterra signature denoted by
\mathbf{z}=\mathrm{VSig}(x;K)
. For any bounded and measurable
\mathbf{y}:[0,T]\to T((\mathbb{R}^{m}))
, we define

(2.4)

[\mathbf{y}\oast\mathbf{z}]_{s,t}^{\tau}=\mathbf{y}_{\tau}-\int_{s}^{t}\mathbf{y}_{r}\otimes\Big(\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r}\mathbf{z}_{r,t}^{\tau}\Big)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r\in T((\mathbb{R}^{m})),\qquad(s,t,\tau)\in\Delta^{3},

recalling that
\otimes
is introduced in
Section

2.1
.

We conclude this section with the fundamental Chen relation for Volterra signatures, we refer to
[
13
, Lemma 2.26 and Corollary 2.36]
for the proof.

Proposition 2.8
.

For any pair
(x,K)
as in
Section

2.2
, we have

(2.5)

\mathrm{VSig}(x;K)^{\tau}_{s,t}=[{\mathrm{VSig}(x;K)^{\cdot}_{s,u}}\oast{\mathrm{VSig}(x;K)}]^{\tau}_{u,t},\qquad(s,u,t,\tau)\in\Delta^{4}.

Let us conclude with
a remark on the analogy to the case of classical signatures.

Remark 2.9
.

Suppose
m=d
and let
K(t,s)=\mathrm{I}_{d}
. Then it is easily observed from (
2.3
) that
\mathrm{VSig}(x;K)_{s,t}^{\tau}=\mathrm{Sig}(x)_{s,t}
for all
(s,t,\tau)\in\Delta^{3}.
Moreover, by definition (
2.4
), in this case the Chen relation (
2.5
) reads

\displaystyle\mathrm{Sig}(x)_{s,t}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Sig}(x)_{s,u}-\mathrm{Sig}(x)_{s,u}\otimes\int_{u}^{t}\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r}\mathrm{Sig}(x)_{r,t}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Sig}(x)_{s,u}-\mathrm{Sig}(x)_{s,u}\otimes(1-\mathrm{Sig}(x)_{u,t})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Sig}(x)_{s,u}\otimes\mathrm{Sig}(x)_{u,t},

which corresponds to the familiar Chen relation stated in (
1.2
).

2.3.
General discretization procedure

In this section we derive explicit expressions for the Volterra signature of a piecewise linear path.
The key observation is that, under the kernel decomposition (
2.7
), the Volterra signature on a single interval separates into analytic weight factors depending only on the kernel and algebraic terms depending only on the path increments (see
Section

2.3
).
Iterating this via the Chen relation (
2.5
) then yields a full breakdown of the Volterra signature over the entire partition in terms of weighted monomials of increments (see
Section

2.3
).
We first set the stage for our computations concerning piecewise linear paths with a couple of notation.

Notation 2.10
.

We are given a kernel

Remark 2.12

3.4
, a first natural numerical algorithm is obtained by applying a left-point approximation to the upper-parameter

s\mapsto\mathrm{VSig}(x;K)_{0,t_{i-1}}^{s}
. This yields the explicit weighted recursion

(3.3)

\mathbf{v}_{j}\approx 1+\sum_{i=1}^{j}\mathbf{v}_{i-1}\otimes\mathcal{E}_{t_{i-1},t_{i}}^{t_{j}}.

However, as we shall see below, for singular kernels
K
this naive scheme may converge slowly as the mesh size tends to zero. For example, when
k_{p}(t,s)=(t-s)^{\beta-1}
with
\beta\in(0,1)
in (
2.7
), the computations in
Section

3.4
show that the relevant upper-parameter maps are only
\beta
-Hölder continuous near the diagonal. Consequently, a left-point approximation of this dependence cannot be expected to yield high-order convergence. In the following, we introduce a higher-order generalization of (
3.3
) which is tailored to this fractional-type singular behavior.

3.1.
Higher-order fractional expansion scheme

In order to introduce a higher-order version of (
3.3
), we require more precise information on the upper-parameter regularity of the Volterra signature. More precisely, for each interval
[t_{i},t_{i+1}]
we consider the map

F_{i}(u):=\mathrm{VSig}(x;K)_{0,t_{i}}^{t_{i}+u},\qquad u\in[0,t_{i+1}-t_{i}].

The naive scheme (
3.3
) amounts to replacing this map by its left endpoint value
F_{i}(0)
. The higher-order scheme below instead assumes that
F_{i}
can be approximated locally by a finite expansion in fractional powers. This is the precise additional regularity property needed to improve the left-point approximation.

Fix a truncation level
N\in\mathbb{N}
and consider a finite set of exponents

\mathcal{B}=\{\rho_{0},\ldots,\rho_{|\mathcal{B}|-1}\}\subset[0,\infty),\qquad 0=\rho_{0}<\rho_{1}<\cdots<\rho_{|\mathcal{B}|-1}.

The higher-order scheme described below relies on the expansion

(3.4)

\pi_{\leq N}(F_{i}(u))=\sum_{\rho\in\mathcal{B}}\mathbf{a}_{i,\rho}u^{\rho}+R_{i}(u),\qquad u\in[0,h_{i}],

where
h_{i}:=t_{i+1}-t_{i}
and the coefficients
\mathbf{a}_{i,\rho}\in T^{(N)}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
are the interpolation
coefficients determined by

\sum_{\rho\in\mathcal{B}}\mathbf{a}_{i,\rho}(\theta_{a}h_{i})^{\rho}=F_{i}(\theta_{a}h_{i}),\qquad a=0,\ldots,|\mathcal{B}|-1,

for some interpolation nodes
\theta_{0},\dots,\theta_{|\mathcal{B}|-1}
with invertible Vandermonde matrix
(\theta_{a}^{\rho})
.

Given the expansion (
3.4
), we propose the following natural higher-order version of (
3.3
)

(3.5)

\pi_{\leq N}(\mathbf{v}_{j})=1+\sum_{i=1}^{j}\sum_{\rho\in\mathcal{B}}\mathbf{C}_{i-1,\rho}\otimes_{N}\mathcal{E}^{t_{j};\rho}_{t_{i-1},t_{i}},\qquad\mathcal{E}^{\tau;\rho}_{u,v}:=-\int_{u}^{v}(s-u)^{\rho}\dot{\mathcal{E}}_{s,v}^{\tau}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s,

where the coefficients
\mathbf{C}_{i,\rho}
are determined recursively by the interpolation conditions

(3.6)

\sum_{\rho\in\mathcal{B}}(\theta_{a}h_{i})^{\rho}\mathbf{C}_{i,\rho}=1+\sum_{b=0}^{i-1}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{B}}\mathbf{C}_{b,\sigma}\otimes_{N}\mathcal{E}_{t_{b},t_{b+1}}^{\,t_{i}+\theta_{a}h_{i};\sigma},\qquad a=0,\ldots,{|\mathcal{B}|-1}.

Remark 3.1
.

It is important to note that the generalized weights
\mathcal{E}^{\tau;\rho}_{s,t}
in
(
3.5
) preserve the structure of
\mathcal{E}^{\tau}_{s,t}
,
up to the introduction of one additional kernel
k_{\rho}(t,s):=(t-s)^{\rho}.

More precisely, using the representation of
\dot{\mathcal{K}}
in
(
2.16
), we have

\int_{u}^{v}(s-u)^{\rho}\dot{\mathcal{K}}_{s,v}^{p_{1}\cdots p_{n},\tau}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s=\dot{\mathcal{K}}_{u,v}^{p_{1}\cdots p_{n}0,\tau},

where the appended symbol
0
denotes the additional kernel
k_{\rho}
.
Consequently, for all kernel classes considered in this paper, whenever
closed-form expressions for the weights
\dot{\mathcal{K}}
in
(
2.16
) are available, the generalized weights

\mathcal{E}^{\tau;\rho}
admit closed-form expressions as well. Concrete examples
will be derived in Section
3.4
.

Example 3.2

(Naive scheme)
.

Choosing
\mathcal{B}=\{0\}
, we have
\mathcal{E}^{\tau;0}=\mathcal{E}^{\tau}
, and the coefficient equation (
3.6
) reads

\mathbf{C}_{i,0}=1+\sum_{b=1}^{i}\mathbf{C}_{b-1,0}\otimes_{N}\mathcal{E}_{t_{b-1},t_{b}}^{t_{i}},

and we immediately recover the scheme (
3.3
).

Example 3.3

(Higher-order fractional scheme)
.

We will see below that for fractional kernels
k_{p}(t,s)=(t-s)^{\beta-1}
, we may choose
\mathcal{B}=\{0,\beta,1\}
and interpolation nodes
\theta_{0}=0,\theta_{1}=\frac{1}{2}
and
\theta_{2}=1
. On a cell
[t_{i},t_{i+1}]
, define the local update

\widehat{\mathbf{F}}_{i}^{\theta}:=1+\sum_{b=0}^{i-1}\sum_{\rho\in\{0,\beta,1\}}\mathbf{C}_{b,\rho}\otimes_{N}\mathcal{E}_{t_{b},t_{b+1}}^{t_{i}+\theta h_{i};\rho}.

The system of for the coefficients (
3.6
) is then solved by

\mathbf{C}_{i,0}=\widehat{\mathbf{F}}_{i}^{0},\quad\mathbf{C}_{i,\beta}=\frac{\widehat{\mathbf{F}}_{i}^{1/2}-\widehat{\mathbf{F}}_{i}^{0}-\frac{1}{2}\left(\widehat{\mathbf{F}}_{i}^{1}-\widehat{\mathbf{F}}_{i}^{0}\right)}{h_{i}^{\beta}(2^{-\beta}-1/2)},\quad\mathbf{C}_{i,1}=\frac{2^{-\beta}\left(\widehat{\mathbf{F}}_{i}^{1}-\widehat{\mathbf{F}}_{i}^{0}\right)-\left(\widehat{\mathbf{F}}_{i}^{1/2}-\widehat{\mathbf{F}}_{i}^{0}\right)}{h_{i}(2^{-\beta}-1/2)}.

In order to quantify the convergence rate of the proposed scheme (
3.5
)-(
3.6
), we require precise assumptions on the local errors
R_{i}
in (
3.4
), as well as convolution stability estimates for the weights
\mathcal{E}
. These conditions can be verified for the kernels considered in this article, and we do so for fractional kernels (see Example
2.3
) in Theoerem
3.6
below.

Hypothesis 3.4
.

Given a set of exponents
\mathcal{B}
and interpolation nodes
(\theta_{a})
as above, assume that there exist an
\alpha>0
and non-negative kernels

\kappa_{m}:(0,T]\to[0,\infty)
such that

\left|\dot{\mathcal{E}}_{s,t}^{(m),\tau}\right|\leq C_{\xi}\kappa_{m}(\tau-s),\qquad s<t\leq\tau,\quad 1\leq m\leq N.

Moreover, we assume that the following two conditions hold.

(i)

The local error
R_{i}
in (
3.4
) satisfies,

\sum_{i=1}^{J}\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_{i}}\left|R_{i-1}^{(l)}(s-t_{i-1})\right|\kappa_{n-l}(T-s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\leq C_{H}\delta^{\alpha},\qquad 1\leq n\leq N.

(ii)

The kernels
(\kappa_{m})_{m=1}^{N}
satisfy for every
\theta_{a}
and a.e.
s\in[t_{p-1},t_{p}]

\sum_{i=p+1}^{J}\kappa_{q}(t_{i}+\theta_{a}h_{i}-s)\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_{i}}\kappa_{r}(T-u)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u\leq C_{\kappa}\kappa_{q+r}(T-s),

for every
1\leq p\leq J-1
, every
q,r\geq 1
with
q+r\leq N
.

Here

\delta:=\max_{[u,v]\in\mathcal{P}}|v-u|
, and the constants
C_{H},C_{\xi},C_{\kappa}

are independent of
\delta
.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
x
be a piecewise linear path on some grid
\mathcal{P}=\{0=t_{0}<t_{1}<\dots<t_{J}=T\}
, and
K
be a kernel as in Notation
2.3
. Suppose Hypothesis
3.1
holds with respect to some
\alpha>0
. Then, the explicit scheme determined by (
3.5
)-(
3.6
) is of order
\alpha
, that is

(3.7)

\max_{n=1,\dots,N}\left|\mathrm{VSig}(x;K)^{(n)}_{0,T}-\mathbf{v}^{(n)}_{J}\right|\leq C\delta^{\alpha},\qquad\delta:=\max_{[u,v]\in\mathcal{P}}|v-u|,

where the constant
C
depends on the constants
C_{\kappa},C_{H}
from Hypothesis
3.1
.

Proof.

First let us note that in our notation for piecewise linear paths
x_{t}=x_{t_{j}}+v_{j}(t-t_{j})
in (
2.6
), we may equivalently write
v_{j}=\frac{x_{t_{j+1}}-x_{t_{j}}}{t_{j+1}-t_{j}}
. In particular,
A_{p}(x_{t_{i}}-x_{t_{i-1}})=(t_{i}-t_{i-1})A_{p}v_{i}
, so the factor
(t_{i}-t_{i-1})^{n}
arising from the
n
-fold tensor product cancels the
(t-s)^{-n}
in (
3.2
). By definition of
\mathcal{E}
in (
3.2
), we have

\mathcal{E}^{t_{j}}_{t_{i-1},t_{i}}\big(A_{1}(x_{t_{i}}-x_{t_{i-1}}),\dots,A_{q}(x_{t_{i}}-x_{t_{i-1}})\big)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{p_{1}\cdots p_{n}\in\mathcal{W}_{q}^{n}}\mathcal{K}_{t_{i-1},t_{i}}^{p_{1}\cdots p_{n},t_{j}}(A_{p_{1}}v_{i})\otimes\cdots\otimes(A_{p_{n}}v_{i}).

From the identity (
2.8
) in Lemma
2.3
, it follows therefore that

\mathcal{E}^{t_{j}}_{t_{i-1},t_{i}}\big(A_{1}(x_{t_{i}}-x_{t_{i-1}}),\dots,A_{q}(x_{t_{i}}-x_{t_{i-1}})\big)=\pi_{N}\left(\mathrm{VSig}(x;K)_{t_{i-1},t_{i}}^{t_{j}}-1\right).

Writing
\mathbf{z}=\mathrm{VSig}(x;K)
, and projecting the exact identity (
3.1
) to levels
1\leq n\leq N
reads

(3.8)

{\mathbf{z}}_{0,t_{j}}^{(n),t_{j}}=-\sum_{i=1}^{j}\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_{i}}\mathbf{z}_{0,t_{i-1}}^{(l),s}\otimes\dot{\mathcal{E}}_{s,t_{i}}^{(n-l),t_{j}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s.

Let us now prove the claimed convergence rate in (
3.7
).
For
n=0
, we have
\mathbf{z}^{(0)}_{0,T}=\mathbf{v}^{(0)}_{J}
by definition, and for
1\leq n\leq N
, we use (
3.8
) to find

(3.9)

\big|\mathbf{z}^{(n),T}_{0,T}-\mathbf{v}_{J}\big|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{J}\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}\left|\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_{i}}\left(\mathbf{z}_{0,t_{i-1}}^{(l),s}-\sum_{\rho\in\mathcal{B}}\mathbf{C}_{i-1,\rho}^{(l)}(s-{t_{i-1}})^{\rho}\right)\otimes\dot{\mathcal{E}}_{s,t_{i}}^{(n-l),T}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\right|

Next, adding and subtracting the expansion in (
3.4
), we have

\displaystyle|\mathbf{z}^{(n),T}_{0,T}-\mathbf{v}_{J}\big|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{J}\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_{i}}\Big|\mathbf{z}_{0,t_{i-1}}^{(l),s}-\sum_{\rho\in\mathcal{B}}\mathbf{a}_{i-1,\rho}^{(l)}(s-{t_{i-1}})^{\rho}\Big|\cdot|\dot{\mathcal{E}}_{s,t_{i}}^{(n-l),T}|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{i=1}^{J}\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}\sum_{\rho\in\mathcal{B}}h_{i-1}^{\rho}\big|\mathbf{a}_{i-1,\rho}^{(l)}-\mathbf{C}_{i-1,\rho}^{(l)}\big|\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_{i}}|\dot{\mathcal{E}}_{s,t_{i}}^{(n-l),T}|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

(3.10)

\displaystyle=:\Sigma_{1}+\Sigma_{2}.

Using Hypothesis
3.1
(i), we have

\Sigma_{1}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{J}\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_{i}}|R^{(l)}_{i-1}(s-t_{i-1})||\dot{\mathcal{E}}_{s,t_{i}}^{(n-l),T}|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\leq C_{H}C_{\xi}\delta^{\alpha}.

On the other hand, we know
\mathbf{C}
solves the interpolation system (
3.6
) and that the matrix
(\theta^{\rho}_{a})
is invertible, so that we have

\sum_{\rho\in\mathcal{B}}h_{i}^{\rho}|\mathbf{a}_{i,\rho}^{(l)}-\mathbf{C}_{i,\rho}^{(l)}|\lesssim\max_{a=0,\dots,{|\mathcal{B}|-1}}|\mathbf{z}_{0,t_{i}}^{(l),t_{i}+\theta_{a}h_{i}}-\hat{\mathbf{F}}^{(l),\theta_{a}}_{i}|=:e_{i}^{(l)},

where
\hat{\mathbf{F}}
is defined by the right-hand side of (
3.6
).
Let us introduce the notation
\Omega_{l,i}^{\tau}:=\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_{i}}\kappa_{l}(\tau-s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s
. Using Hypothesis
3.1
, we have

\Sigma_{2}\lesssim\sum_{i=1}^{J}\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}e_{i-1}^{(l)}\Omega_{n-l,i}^{T}=:\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}B_{n}^{(l)}.

Note we can do the same error decomposition (
3.1
) again for
e_{i}^{(l)}
, but with upper-variable
t_{i}+\theta_{a}h_{i}
, which reads

\displaystyle e_{i-1}^{(l)}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{a=0}^{|\mathcal{B}|-1}\sum_{p=1}^{i-1}\sum_{r=0}^{l-1}\int_{t_{p-1}}^{t_{p}}(|R^{(r)}_{p-1}(s-t_{p-1})|+e_{p-1}^{(r)})\kappa_{l-r}(t_{i}+\theta_{a}h_{i}-s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

(3.11)

\displaystyle:=d_{i-1}^{(l)}+f_{i-1}^{(l)}

Next, multiplying
f_{i-1}^{(l)}
by
\omega_{n-l,i}^{T}
and summing over
i
gives

\displaystyle\sum_{a=0}^{|\mathcal{B}|-1}\sum_{i=1}^{J}\sum_{p=1}^{i-1}\sum_{r=0}^{l-1}e_{p-1}^{(r)}\Omega_{l-r,p-1}^{t_{i}+\theta_{a}h_{i}}\Omega_{n-l,i}^{T}

\displaystyle=\sum_{a=0}^{|\mathcal{B}|-1}\sum_{r=0}^{l-1}\sum_{p=1}^{J}e_{p-1}^{(r)}\sum_{i=p+1}^{J}\omega_{l-r,p-1}^{t_{i}+\theta_{a}h_{i}}\Omega_{n-l,i}^{T}

\displaystyle\leq C_{\kappa}\sum_{r=0}^{l-1}\sum_{p=1}^{J}e_{p-1}^{(r)}\Omega_{n-r,p-1}^{T}=C_{\kappa}\sum_{r=0}^{l-1}B_{n}^{(r)},

where we used Fubini for the first equality, and Hypothesis
3.1
(ii) for the inequality.
Doing the same Fubini-trick with
d_{i-1}^{(l)}
in (
3.1
), leads to

\displaystyle\sum_{a=0}^{|\mathcal{B}|-1}\sum_{r=0}^{l-1}\sum_{p=1}^{J}\int_{t_{p-1}}^{t_{p}}|R_{p-1}(s-t_{p-1})\sum_{i=p+1}^{J}\kappa_{l-r}({t_{i}+\theta_{a}h_{i}}-s)\ \Omega_{n-l,i-1}^{T}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\qquad\leq C_{\kappa}\sum_{r=0}^{l-1}\sum_{p=1}^{J}\int_{t_{p-1}}^{t_{p}}|R_{p-1}(s-t_{p-1})|\dot{\kappa}_{l-r,p-1}^{T}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\leq C_{\kappa}C_{H}\delta^{\alpha},

where we first used (ii) and then (i) from Hypothesis
3.1
.
We can therefore see that
1\leq l\leq n
, it holds that

B_{n}^{(l)}\lesssim\delta^{\alpha}+\sum_{r=0}^{l-1}B_{n}^{(r)}.

An induction over
1\leq l\leq n
then shows

\Sigma_{2}\lesssim\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}B_{n}^{(l)}\lesssim\delta^{\alpha}.

Combined with the estimate for
\Sigma_{1}
, we can conclude the proof.
∎

Let us now apply our main result to quantify convergence rates for fractional kernels.

Theorem 3.6
.

Suppose we are in the setting of Example
2.3
, that is
q=1
with fractional kernel
k(t,s)=\Gamma(\beta)^{-1}(t-s)^{\beta-1}
for some
\beta\in(0,1)
. Then the naive scheme in Example
3.1
is of order
\alpha=\beta
, while the higher-order scheme in Example
3.1
is of order
\alpha=1+\beta
.

Proof.

We verify Hypothesis
3.1
. From our computations in Example
2.3
, we see that the choice

\kappa_{m}(t):=\frac{t^{m\beta-1}}{\Gamma(m\beta)},\qquad 1\leq m\leq N,

leads to the required control in Hypothesis
3.1

\left|\dot{\mathcal{E}}_{s,t}^{(m),\tau}\right|\lesssim\kappa_{m}(\tau-s),\qquad s<t\leq\tau.

Moreover, since
\kappa_{q}*\kappa_{r}=\kappa_{q+r}
, a standard discrete
Beta-convolution estimate gives, uniformly in the interpolation node

\theta_{a}

\sum_{i=p+1}^{J}\kappa_{q}(t_{i}+\theta_{a}h_{i}-s)\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_{i}}\kappa_{r}(T-u)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u\lesssim\kappa_{q+r}(T-s),

for
s\in[t_{p-1},t_{p}]
and
q+r\leq N
. Thus Hypothesis
3.1
(ii)
holds.

It remains to check that (i) holds true. For the naive scheme we chose

\mathcal{B}=\{0\}
and the local interpolant is constant, so that

R_{i}(u)=F_{i}(u)-F_{i}(0)
. The fractional upper-variable regularity gives

|R_{i}(u)|\lesssim u^{\beta}
, uniformly up to level
N
. Therefore, for every

1\leq n\leq N
,

\sum_{i=1}^{J}\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_{i}}|R_{i-1}^{(l)}(s-t_{i-1})|\kappa_{n-l}(T-s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\lesssim\delta^{\beta}\int_{0}^{T}(T-s)^{\beta-1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\lesssim\delta^{\beta}.

Thus Hypothesis
3.1
(i) holds with
\alpha=\beta
,
and Theorem
3.5
yields the claimed order of the naive
scheme.

We now consider the higher-order scheme with
\mathcal{B}=\{0,\beta,1\}
. For

l\geq 1
, write the local level-
l
map as

F_{i}^{(l)}(u)=\mathbf{z}_{0,t_{i}}^{(l),t_{i}+u}=\int_{0}^{t_{i}}\frac{(t_{i}+u-r)^{\beta-1}}{\Gamma(\beta)}G_{l}(r)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r,\qquad G_{l}(r):=\mathbf{z}_{0,r}^{(l-1),r}\otimes\dot{x}_{r}.

We now split
G_{l}(r)=G_{l}(t_{i})+(G_{l}(r)-G_{l}(t_{i}))
. The constant part gives

\frac{G_{l}(t_{i})}{\Gamma(\beta+1)}\bigl((t_{i}+u)^{\beta}-u^{\beta}\bigr).

Expanding only the regular term
(t_{i}+u)^{\beta}
around
u=0
yields the
fractional term
-G_{l}(t_{i})u^{\beta}/\Gamma(\beta+1)
. Thus

F_{i}^{(l)}(u)=A_{i}^{(l)}+B_{i}^{(l)}u^{\beta}+C_{i}^{(l)}u+\overline{R}_{i}^{(l)}(u),

where

A_{i}^{(l)}=F_{i}^{(l)}(0),\qquad B_{i}^{(l)}=-\frac{G_{l}(t_{i})}{\Gamma(\beta+1)},

and

C_{i}^{(l)}=\frac{G_{l}(t_{i})t_{i}^{\beta-1}}{\Gamma(\beta)}+\frac{\beta-1}{\Gamma(\beta)}\int_{0}^{t_{i}}(t_{i}-r)^{\beta-2}\bigl(G_{l}(r)-G_{l}(t_{i})\bigr)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r.

The last integral is finite because we use the following endpoint estimate:

|G_{l}(r)-G_{l}(t_{i})|\leq L_{l,i}(t_{i}-r),\qquad 0\leq r\leq t_{i},

where

L_{1,i}\lesssim 1,\qquad L_{l,i}\lesssim t_{i}^{(l-1)\beta-1},\quad l\geq 2.

Indeed,
G_{l}(r)=\mathbf{z}_{0,r}^{(l-1),r}\otimes\dot{x}_{r}
and since
x
is
smooth

|\mathbf{z}_{0,t}^{(l-1),t}|\lesssim t^{(l-1)\beta},\qquad|\mathbf{z}_{0,t}^{(l-1),t}-\mathbf{z}_{0,r}^{(l-1),r}|\lesssim t^{(l-1)\beta-1}(t-r),

we obtain the bound for
G_{l}
. Hence

|\overline{R}_{i}^{(l)}(u)|\lesssim u^{2}t_{i}^{l\beta-2}+u^{1+\beta}\Lambda_{l}(t_{i}),\qquad\Lambda_{1}(t_{i})=1,\quad\Lambda_{l}(t_{i})=t_{i}^{(l-1)\beta-1}\text{ for }l\geq 2.

By stability of the interpolation operator in the basis
\{1,u^{\beta},u\}
, the
actual interpolation error satisfies the same bound as the analytic remainder.
Thus, still denoting the interpolation error by
R_{i}^{(l)}
, we have, for

0\leq u\leq h_{i}
,

|R_{i}^{(l)}(u)|\lesssim h_{i}^{2}t_{i}^{l\beta-2}+h_{i}^{1+\beta}\Lambda_{l}(t_{i}),\qquad\Lambda_{1}(t_{i})=1,\quad\Lambda_{l}(t_{i})=t_{i}^{(l-1)\beta-1}\quad(l\geq 2).

Finally, we now insert this estimate into Hypothesis
3.1
. It remains to show that, for every
1\leq l\leq n-1
,

\sum_{i}\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{i+1}}|R_{i}^{(l)}(s-t_{i})|\kappa_{n-l}(T-s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\lesssim\delta^{1+\beta}.

We split the estimate according to the two terms in the bound for
R_{i}^{(l)}
.
For the first part we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{i+1}}h_{i}^{2}t_{i}^{l\beta-2}\kappa_{n-l}(T-s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\lesssim\delta^{n\beta}\sum_{i}i^{l\beta-2}(J-i+1)^{(n-l)\beta-1}\lesssim\delta^{1+\beta}.

In the singular case
l\beta\leq 1
, the sum
is controlled by the left edge and gives

J^{(n-l)\beta-1}
; hence the contribution is

\delta^{l\beta+1}\leq\delta^{1+\beta}
. In the regular case
l\beta>1
,
the convolution gives the even smaller order
\delta^{2}
. For the second part, if
l=1
, then
\Lambda_{1}=1
and

\sum_{i}\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{i+1}}h_{i}^{1+\beta}\kappa_{n-1}(T-s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\lesssim\delta^{1+\beta}.

If
l\geq 2
, then

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{i+1}}h_{i}^{1+\beta}t_{i}^{(l-1)\beta-1}\kappa_{n-l}(T-s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\lesssim\delta^{n\beta}\sum_{i}i^{(l-1)\beta-1}(J-i+1)^{(n-l)\beta-1}\lesssim\delta^{1+\beta}.

Thus

\sum_{i}\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{i+1}}|R_{i}^{(l)}(s-t_{i})|\kappa_{n-l}(T-s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\lesssim\delta^{1+\beta}.

Summing over the finitely many levels
l=0,\ldots,n-1
gives

\sum_{i}\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{i+1}}|R_{i}^{(l)}(s-t_{i})|\kappa_{n-l}(T-s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\lesssim\delta^{1+\beta}.

Hence Hypothesis
3.1
(i) holds with

\alpha=1+\beta
.
∎

3.2.
Algorithmic aspects

In this section we discuss the algorithmic aspects of
3.5
. The recursion (
3.5
)-(
3.6
) directly translates into the iterative scheme summarized in
Algorithm

1
. At step
j
this scheme requires the summation of
j
truncated tensors of the form
\sum_{\rho}{\mathbf{C}}_{i-1,\rho}\otimes_{N}\mathcal{E}^{t_{j};\rho}_{t_{i-1},t_{i}}(y_{i}^{1},\dots,y_{i}^{q})
, where
y_{i}^{p}=A_{p}(x_{t_{i}}-x_{t_{i-1}})
,
p=1,\dots,q
, given the coefficient system
\mathbf{C}
from the interpolation (
3.6
). Hence, the central computational task is the evaluation of
\mathcal{E}^{\rho}
, or rather, of the products
{\mathbf{C}}_{i,\rho}\otimes_{N}\mathcal{E}_{t_{i-1},t_{i}}^{\tau;\rho}
, for many triples
(t_{i-1},t_{i},\tau)
on the time grid.

In general, no further simplification is available and one is led to a direct implementation of the definition (
3.2
) (resp.
\mathcal{E}^{\rho}
in (
3.5
)), i.e. a brute-force summation over all words
p_{1}\cdots p_{n}\in\mathcal{W}_{q}^{n}
.
However, for kernels whose weights satisfy the symmetry property

Remark 3.10
.

The term in parentheses in
Equation

3.13
is a shuffle polynomial of degree
N-1
in the variables
y_{1},\dots,y_{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. A straightforward evaluation is implemented in
Algorithm

2
. This scheme already reduces the computational cost for evaluating
\mathcal{E}^{\tau}_{s,t}(y_{1},\dots,y_{q})
from the brute-force order
m^{N}q^{N}
to the order
Nm^{N}
, asymptotically in
N
for fixed
m
and
q
(see the discussion of computational costs in
Section

6.2
). More elaborate multivariate Horner-type factorizations are available in the literature (see, e.g.,
[
29
]
), however, we find that for fixed
q
and
m
the asymptotic order of costs in terms of
N
cannot be further improved in this way.

Moreover, for each
p\in\mathcal{A}_{q}
the term in parentheses in
Equation

3.13
is a linear combination of shuffle monomials

y_{1}^{\shuffle\ell_{1}}\shuffle\cdots\shuffle y_{q}^{\shuffle\ell_{q}}
and hence a symmetric tensor.
Consequently, one may evaluate and store these intermediate symmetric tensors in a compressed representation, which can reduce memory consumption and the cost of evaluating
\mathcal{E}^{\tau}_{s,t}(y_{1},\dots,y_{q})
.
However, when subsequently forming
\mathbf{v}\otimes\mathcal{E}^{\tau}_{s,t}(y_{1},\dots,y_{q})
, the components are in general no longer symmetric, so that a passage to the full tensor basis is unavoidable.
Thus, while symmetry can be exploited to save memory during the evaluation of
Equation

3.13
, it seems not to improve the overall asymptotic costs once the final product with
\mathbf{v}
is formed.

\displaystyle=\frac{(\tau-s)}{\Gamma(\beta_{p_{n+1}})\Gamma(\sum_{l=1}^{n}\beta_{p_{l}}+1)}\int_{0}^{\frac{t-s}{\tau-s}}(\tau-s-v(\tau-s))^{\beta_{p_{n+1}}-1}(v(\tau-s))^{\sum_{l=1}^{n}\beta_{p_{l}}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v

\dot{\mathcal{K}}_{s,t}^{p_{1}\cdots p_{n+1},\tau}=-\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s}\mathcal{K}_{s,t}^{p_{1}\cdots p_{n+1},\tau},\qquad-\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s}\mathcal{K}_{s,t}^{p_{1}\cdots p_{n},t}=-\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s}k_{\sum_{l=1}^{n}\beta_{p_{l}}+1}(t-s)=k_{\sum_{l=1}^{n}\beta_{p_{l}}}(t-s),

4.
Finite state space kernels

In this section, we specialize to kernels whose scalar components are of the general form

(4.1)

k(t,s)=\sum_{r=1}^{Q}\sum_{\ell=1}^{m_{r}}e^{-\lambda_{r}(t-s)}\frac{(t-s)^{\ell-1}}{(\ell-1)!}\,\alpha_{r,\ell}\\
+\sum_{r=1}^{P}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n_{r}}e^{-a_{r}(t-s)}\frac{(t-s)^{\ell-1}}{(\ell-1)!}\Big(\beta_{r,\ell}\cos\big(\omega_{r}(t-s)\big)+\delta_{r,\ell}\sin\big(\omega_{r}(t-s)\big)\Big),

for
(s,t)\in\Delta^{2}
, where
P,Q\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, the degrees satisfy
m_{r},n_{r}\in\mathbb{N}
, the real rates

\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{Q}\in\mathbb{R}
are pairwise distinct, and the oscillatory parameters

(a_{1},\omega_{1}),\dots,(a_{P},\omega_{P})\in\mathbb{R}\times(0,\infty)
are pairwise distinct.
All coefficients
\alpha_{r,\ell},\beta_{r,\ell},\delta_{r,\ell}
are real.

Such kernels are widely used in several contexts and, in particular, form a general approximation class for convolution (memory) kernels.
On the one hand, kernels of exponential–polynomial type are exactly the impulse responses of finite-dimensional continuous-time linear time-invariant state space models, making them canonical in systems and control—hence the terminology
finite state space kernel
. On the other hand, sums of exponentials arise naturally as Laplace approximations of general memory kernels and underpin many fast numerical methods for Volterra and fractional-type models, including convolution quadrature and sum-of-exponentials compression. Moreover, such parametrizations are routinely fitted to data in system identification and signal processing via Prony-type and related spectral estimation methods. In stochastic settings, the same approximation principle yields Markovian state-augmentation (multi-factor) representations of non-Markovian Volterra dynamics, enabling efficient simulation and calibration.

Here we show that, for piecewise linear paths, an exact computation of the Volterra signature for finite state space kernels of the form (
4.1
) is possible via a dynamic algorithm that requires only a single iteration over the number of time steps.
This complements the finding in
[
13
, Section 2.4]
that the Volterra signature associated with such kernels solves a linear controlled differential equation in the tensor algebra.
To transition from this dynamical representation to an exact computation for a piecewise linear path, one may solve the system on each interval and then join the solutions across adjacent intervals.
Since such an increment-level decomposition has already been provided in
Section

2.3
in great generality, we use it as the starting point of our construction.

To this end, we first rewrite the kernel in matrix form in
Section

4.1
, which significantly simplifies the subsequent computations.
In
Section

4.2
we prove that the weight coefficients
\mathcal{K}^{(w_{1},\dots,w_{k}),\tau}
(defined in (
2.17
) and recalled below) factorize into a matrix product.
Moreover, in
Section

4.2.2
we show that the individual matrix factors can be evaluated efficiently for arbitrary truncation levels using tools from numerical linear algebra.
The factorization of the weights, together with the semigroup property of the intermediate factors, then allows us to present in
Section

4.3
a linear recursion for Volterra signatures at the state-space level.
This recursion translates into an algorithm, which we describe in
Section

4.4
.
Similarly to
Section

3.2
, but now on the matrix level, we obtain efficient implementation schemes due to symmetry of the weight factors, as well as a Horner-type scheme for scalar kernels.

4.1.
Kernel in matrix form

As a first step, we give a matrix representation for kernels of the form (

), which will
drastically simplify the computations later on.

\alpha_{r,m_{r}+1}:=0
, and define
d_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{r}}
by

(d_{r})_{\ell}:=\alpha_{r,\ell}-\alpha_{r,\ell+1},\qquad\ell=1,\dots,m_{r}.

In the same way for
r=1,\dots,P
we set
\beta_{r,n_{r}+1}:=0
and
\delta_{r,n_{r}+1}:=0
, and define
c_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n_{r}}
by

(c_{r})_{j}:=\frac{1}{2}\Big(\beta_{r,\ell}-\beta_{r,\ell+1}+(-1)^{j}\big(\delta_{r,\ell}-\delta_{r,\ell+1}\big)\Big),\qquad\ell:=\lceil j/2\rceil,\quad j=1,\dots,2n_{r}.

Proof.

We first show that if
k(t,s)=a^{\top}e^{-\Lambda(t-s)}b
for some
\Lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{R\times R}
and
a,b\in\mathbb{R}^{R}
, then
k
is of the analytic form (
4.1
).
By
[
19
, Theorem 3.4.1.5]
there exists an invertible matrix
S\in\mathbb{R}^{R\times R}
such that

\Lambda^{\circ}:=S^{-1}(-\Lambda)S
is in real Jordan normal form. Setting
\tilde{b}:=S^{-1}b
and
\tilde{a}^{\top}:=a^{\top}S
and using similarity invariance of the exponential we obtain

k(t,s)=\tilde{a}^{\top}e^{(t-s)\Lambda^{\circ}}\tilde{b}.

Writing
\Lambda^{\circ}
blockwise as in the statement and decomposing

\tilde{b}=(d_{1}^{\top},\dots,d_{Q}^{\top},c_{1}^{\top},\dots,c_{P}^{\top})^{\top}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\tilde{a}^{\top}=(v_{1}^{\top},\dots,v_{Q}^{\top},u_{1}^{\top},\dots,u_{P}^{\top})

accordingly, we have

k(t,s)=\sum_{r=1}^{Q}v_{r}^{\top}e^{(t-s)J_{m_{r}}(-\lambda_{r})}\,d_{r}+\sum_{r=1}^{P}u_{r}^{\top}e^{(t-s)J_{n_{r}}(-a_{r},\omega_{r})}\,c_{r}.

For
r\in\{1,\dots,Q\}
and
\delta>0
it follows from the standard exponentiation of matrices in Jordan normal form
[
27
, Method 16]
that

v_{r}^{\top}e^{\delta J_{m_{r}}(-\lambda_{r})}d_{r}=e^{-\lambda_{r}\delta}\sum_{\ell=1}^{m_{r}}\frac{\delta^{\ell-1}}{(\ell-1)!}\big(v_{r}^{\top}N^{\ell-1}d_{r}\big),

where
N\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{r}\times m_{r}}
is the nilpotent shift matrix (ones on the superdiagonal and zeros elsewhere), so that
N^{m_{r}}=0
.

Similarly, for
r\in\{1,\dots,P\}
and
\delta>0
, exponentiation of the real block Jordan matrix

J_{n_{r}}(-a_{r},\omega_{r})
(see
[
37
]
) yields

(4.3)

u_{r}^{\top}e^{\delta J_{n_{r}}(-a_{r},\omega_{r})}c_{r}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n_{r}}\frac{\delta^{\ell-1}}{(\ell-1)!}\,u_{r}^{\top}\mathbf{N}^{\ell-1}\,\mathrm{diag}\big(e^{\delta C(-a_{r},\omega_{r})},\dots,e^{\delta C(-a_{r},\omega_{r})}\big)\,c_{r},

where
\mathbf{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n_{r}\times 2n_{r}}
is the nilpotent block shift matrix (with
I_{2}
on the first superdiagonal block and zeros elsewhere), so that
\mathbf{N}^{n_{r}}=0
.
Write the
2
-block decompositions

u_{r}^{\top}=(u_{r,1}^{\top},\dots,u_{r,n_{r}}^{\top}),\qquad c_{r}=(c_{r,1}^{\top},\dots,c_{r,n_{r}}^{\top})^{\top},\qquad u_{r,j},c_{r,j}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}.

Then the block shift structure of
\mathbf{N}
implies, for each
\ell=1,\dots,n_{r}
,

u_{r}^{\top}\mathbf{N}^{\ell-1}\,\mathrm{diag}\big(e^{\delta C(-a_{r},\omega_{r})},\dots,e^{\delta C(-a_{r},\omega_{r})}\big)\,c_{r}=\sum_{j=\ell}^{n_{r}}u_{r,j-\ell+1}^{\top}e^{\delta C(-a_{r},\omega_{r})}c_{r,j}.

Moreover,

e^{\delta C(-a_{r},\omega_{r})}=e^{-a_{r}\delta}\begin{pmatrix}\cos(\omega_{r}\delta)&-\sin(\omega_{r}\delta)\\
\sin(\omega_{r}\delta)&\cos(\omega_{r}\delta)\end{pmatrix}.

Writing
u_{r,k}=(u_{r,k,1},u_{r,k,2})^{\top}
and
c_{r,j}=(c_{r,j,1},c_{r,j,2})^{\top}
, we obtain for each
\ell=1,\dots,n_{r}
,

\sum_{j=\ell}^{n_{r}}u_{r,j-\ell+1}^{\top}e^{\delta C(-a_{r},\omega_{r})}c_{r,j}=e^{-a_{r}\delta}\Big(\beta_{r,\ell}\cos(\omega_{r}\delta)+\delta_{r,\ell}\sin(\omega_{r}\delta)\Big),

where

\beta_{r,\ell}:=\sum_{j=\ell}^{n_{r}}\big(u_{r,j-\ell+1,1}c_{r,j,1}+u_{r,j-\ell+1,2}c_{r,j,2}\big),\quad\delta_{r,\ell}:=\sum_{j=\ell}^{n_{r}}\big(u_{r,j-\ell+1,2}c_{r,j,1}-u_{r,j-\ell+1,1}c_{r,j,2}\big).

Hence, plugging things together we obtain

(4.4)

u_{r}^{\top}\mathbf{N}^{\ell-1}\,\mathrm{diag}\big(e^{\delta C(-a_{r},\omega_{r})},\dots,e^{\delta C(-a_{r},\omega_{r})}\big)\,c_{r}=e^{-a_{r}\delta}\Big(\beta_{r,\ell}\cos(\omega_{r}\delta)+\delta_{r,\ell}\sin(\omega_{r}\delta)\Big).

Substituting (
4.4
) back into the expansion (
4.3
) yields

u_{r}^{\top}e^{\delta J_{n_{r}}(-a_{r},\omega_{r})}c_{r}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n_{r}}e^{-a_{r}\delta}\frac{\delta^{\ell-1}}{(\ell-1)!}\Big(\beta_{r,\ell}\cos(\omega_{r}\delta)+\delta_{r,\ell}\sin(\omega_{r}\delta)\Big),

which is the required oscillatory contribution in (
4.1
). This concludes the proof of the first direction.

Conversely, assume that
k
is given by (
4.1
) and let
\Lambda
,
b
,
c
and
d
be as in the second part of the statement.
We first note that, by definition of
(d_{r})_{j}=\alpha_{r,j}-\alpha_{r,j+1}
and
\alpha_{r,m_{r}+1}=0
, we obtain the telescoping identity

{\bf 1}^{\top}N^{\ell-1}d_{r}=\sum_{j=\ell}^{m_{r}}(d_{r})_{j}=\alpha_{r,\ell},

for all
\ell=1,\dots,m_{r}
.
Similarly, writing
c_{r}=(c_{r,1}^{\top},\dots,c_{r,n_{r}}^{\top})^{\top}
with
c_{r,j}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
{\bf 1}_{2}^{\top}:=(1,1)
we have

{\bf 1}^{\top}\mathbf{N}^{\ell-1}\,\mathrm{diag}\big(e^{(t-s)C(-a_{r},\omega_{r})},\dots,e^{(t-s)C(-a_{r},\omega_{r})}\big)\,c_{r}={\bf 1}_{2}^{\top}e^{(t-s)C(-a_{r},\omega_{r})}\sum_{j=\ell}^{n_{r}}c_{r,j}.

By the definition of
c_{r}
(with
\beta_{r,n_{r}+1}=\delta_{r,n_{r}+1}=0
), the tail sums telescope to

\sum_{j=\ell}^{n_{r}}c_{r,j}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}\beta_{r,\ell}-\delta_{r,\ell}\\
\beta_{r,\ell}+\delta_{r,\ell}\end{pmatrix}.

Since
-\Lambda
is already in real Jordan normal form and block diagonal, applying the block expansions established above with
a={\bf 1}
and inserting the above expressions for the coefficients, we readily obtain that

k(t,s)={\bf 1}^{\top}e^{-\Lambda(t-s)}b.

∎

Motivated by
Section

4.1
, we now define the class of (matrix-valued) finite state space kernels in matrix form.

Definition 4.2
.

Let
q,R\in\mathbb{N}
and let
\Lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{R\times R}
. For parameters
\{A_{r},b_{r}:1\leq r\leq q\}
with

A_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}
and
b_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{R}
we define the kernel
K_{A,b}^{\Lambda}\in L^{\infty,1}(\Delta^{2};\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{m}))
by

(4.5)

K_{A,b}^{\Lambda}(t,s)=\sum_{r=1}^{q}\big({\bf 1}^{\top}e^{-\Lambda(t-s)}b_{r}\big)\,A_{r},\qquad(s,t)\in\Delta^{2},

where
{\bf 1}^{\top}=(1,\dots,1)\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times R}
.

Remark 4.3
.

As follows from
Section

4.1
,
\Lambda
can be chosen, without loss of generality, in real Jordan normal form with distinct eigenvalues across diagonal blocks.

4.2.
Computation of weight factors

We now treat, for the case of finite state space kernels as defined in
Section

4.1
, the computation of the weight factors appearing in the discretization of the Volterra signature for piecewise linear paths in
Section

2.3
.
In particular, we will prove that these weights factorize into a matrix product and give precise instructions for their efficient numerical evaluation.

To this end, given kernel data
\{A_{r},b_{r}:1\leq r\leq q\}
with

A_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}
,
b_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{R}
, and
\Lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{R\times R}
, fix
K_{A,b}^{\Lambda}
according to
Section

4.1
and also define the corresponding scalar kernels as in
Section

2.3
(ii) by

(4.6)

\displaystyle k_{r}(s,t)=\mathbf{1}^{\top}e^{-\Lambda(t-s)}b_{r},\qquad(s,t)\in\Delta^{2},\quad r=1,\dots,q.

We further fix a partition
0=t_{0}<t_{1}<\dots<t_{J}=T
and recall that the weight factors of interest from
Section

2.3
are then given by

(4.7)

\mathcal{K}^{(w_{1},\dots,w_{k})}_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k+1}}:=\int_{t_{i_{1}-1}}^{t_{i_{1}}}\cdots\int_{t_{i_{k}-1}}^{t_{i_{k}}}\prod_{l=1}^{k}\dot{\mathcal{K}}^{w_{l},r_{l+1}}_{r_{l},t_{i_{l}}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r_{l}\;\;\in\mathbb{R},\quad r_{k+1}=t_{i_{k+1}},

for
0<i_{1}<\dots<i_{k+1}\leq J
,
w_{1},\dots,w_{k}\in\mathcal{W}_{q}
, where

(4.8)

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{K}}_{s,t}^{p_{1}\cdots p_{n},\tau}=

\displaystyle\;\int_{\Delta^{n-1}_{s,t}}k_{p_{1}}(r_{1},r_{0})\prod_{l=1}^{n-1}k_{p_{l}}(r_{l+1},r_{l})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r_{l},\qquad r_{0}=s,\;r_{n}=\tau,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{K}}_{s,t}^{p_{1}\cdots p_{n},\tau}=

\displaystyle\;\int_{s}^{t}\dot{\mathcal{K}}_{u,t}^{p_{1}\cdots p_{n},\tau}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{u}.

for any
p_{1}\cdots p_{n}\in\mathcal{W}_{q}
and
(s,t,\tau)\in\Delta^{3}
.
For notational convenience, set

(4.9)

E(\delta)=e^{-\Lambda\delta}\quad(\delta>0),\qquad C_{r}=b_{r}{\bf 1}^{\top}=(b_{r},\dots,b_{r})\in\mathbb{R}^{R\times R}\quad(r=1,\dots,q).

We divide the computation into two parts. First, we prove alternative equivalent analytic characterizations of
\dot{\mathcal{K}}
and then prove the factorization property for
\mathcal{K}^{(w_{1},\dots,w_{k})}_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k+1}}
.
We then discuss a concrete numerical scheme for the evaluation of these weight factors in a second subsection.

4.2.1.
Weight factorization

The key observation of this subsection is that the kernel increment
\dot{\mathcal{K}}^{p_{1}\cdots p_{n},\tau}_{s,t}
admits a factorization in which the dependence on the look-ahead variable
\tau-t
and the dependence on the increment
t-s
decouple. Using the semigroup property
E(t+\delta)=E(t)E(\delta)
, this is made precise in
Section

4.2.1
. Indeed, this lemma re-expresses
\dot{\mathcal{K}}
in terms of a family of matrix-valued functions
\Phi^{w}(\delta)
satisfying two equivalent convolution recursions. Resorting to this structure,
Section

4.2.1
then establishes that each weight factor
\mathcal{K}^{(w_{1},\dots,w_{k})}_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k+1}}
can be written as an ordered product of matrices
\Phi^{w}
and
\Psi^{w}
evaluated on the mesh sub-intervals. This will be the starting point for the numerical scheme developed in
Section

4.2.2
. Throughout, we use the shorthand
E(\delta)=e^{-\Lambda\delta}
introduced in (
4.9
).

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
k_{1},\dots,k_{q}\in L^{\infty,1}(\Delta^{2};\mathbb{R})
be as in (
4.6
).
Then for all
(s,t,\tau)\in\Delta^{3}
,
p_{1}\cdots p_{n}\in\mathcal{W}_{q}
and
\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{n-1}
(where
\sigma(p_{1}\cdots p_{n}):=p_{\sigma(1)}\cdots p_{\sigma(n)}
denotes the permutation action on words, as in
Section

2.3
) we have

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{K}}^{p_{1}\cdots p_{n},\tau}_{s,t}={\bf 1}^{\top}\Phi^{\sigma(p_{1}\cdots p_{n-1})}(t-s)E(\tau-t)b_{p_{n}},

where
C_{r}=b_{r}{\bf 1}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{R\times R}
is as in (
4.9
), and
\Phi^{w}(\delta)\in\mathbb{R}^{R\times R}

(\delta>0,w\in\mathcal{W}_{q})
is given by
\Phi^{\varnothing}(\delta)=E(\delta)
and any of the following equivalent recursions

(a)

\displaystyle\Phi^{p_{1}\cdots p_{n}}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\Phi^{p_{1}\dots p_{n-1}}(u)C_{p_{n}}E(t-u)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{u}\quad(t\geq 0),

(b)

\displaystyle\Phi^{p_{1}\cdots p_{n}}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}E(t-u)C_{p_{1}}\Phi^{p_{2}\cdots p_{n}}(u)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{u}\quad(t\geq 0),

Furthermore, for all
(s,t,\tau)\in\Delta^{3}
,
p_{1}\cdots p_{n}\in\mathcal{W}_{q}
and
\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{n-1}
it holds

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{K}}^{p_{1}\cdots p_{n},\tau}_{s,t}={\bf 1}^{\top}\Psi^{\sigma(p_{1}\cdots p_{n-1})}(t-s)E(\tau-t)b_{p_{n}},

where

\Psi^{w}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\Phi^{w}(u)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{u}.

Proof.

Plugging the expression
k_{p}(s,t)=\mathbf{1}^{\top}e^{-\Lambda(t-s)}b_{p}
from (
4.6
) into the definition (
4.8
) of
\dot{\mathcal{K}}^{p_{1}\cdots p_{n},\tau}_{s,t}
, and applying the semigroup property
E(t+\delta)=E(t)E(\delta)
(which follows from (
4.9
)), we directly observe that

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{K}}^{p_{1}\cdots p_{n},\tau}_{s,t}={\bf 1}^{\top}\left(\int_{\Delta^{n-1}_{0,t-s}}E(r_{1}-r_{0})\prod_{l=1}^{n-1}C_{p_{l}}E(r_{l+1}-r_{l})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r_{l}\right)E(\tau-t)b_{p_{n}},

with
r_{0}=0
and
r_{n}=t-s
.
One then easily verifies that the term in the parenthesis above satisfies the recursion (a).
We next verify the equivalence between the recursions (a) and (b).
Using a change of variable one easily verifies that this is the case for
n=1
.
Now assume the claim holds for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
then using Fubini’s theorem we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\delta}E(u)C_{p_{1}}

\displaystyle\Phi^{p_{2}\cdots p_{n}}(\delta-u)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{u}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\delta}E(\delta-u)C_{p_{1}}\left(\int_{0}^{u}\Phi^{p_{2}\cdots p_{n-1}}(r)C_{p_{n}}E(u-r)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{r}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{u}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\delta}\left(\int_{r}^{\delta}E(\delta-u)C_{p_{1}}\Phi^{p_{2}\cdots p_{n-1}}(u-r)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{u}\right)C_{p_{n}}E(r)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{r}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\delta}\left(\int_{0}^{\delta-r}E(u)C_{p_{1}}\Phi^{p_{2}\cdots p_{n-1}}(\delta-r-u)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{u}\right)C_{p_{n}}E(r)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{r}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\delta}\Phi^{p_{1}\cdots p_{n-1}}(\delta-r)C_{p_{n}}E(r)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{r}.

Well-posedness of these linear matrix differential equations is straightforward.
The representation of
\mathcal{K}
follows directly by integrating the representation of
\dot{\mathcal{K}}
.
The invariance under permutation of all but the last letter is a direct consequence of
Section

2.3
, since
(k_{1},\dots,k_{q})
are of convolution form.
∎

As mentioned above, the main role of
Section

4.2.1
is to factor
\dot{\mathcal{K}}^{w,\tau}_{s,t}
as a matrix depending only on
(t-s)
times a matrix depending only on
(\tau-t)
. We exploit this to establish the following factorization of the full weight
\mathcal{K}^{(w_{1},\dots,w_{k})}_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k+1}}
.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
k_{1},\dots,k_{R}\in L^{\infty,1}(\Delta^{2};\mathbb{R})
be as in (
4.6
), and recall that
\mathcal{K}^{(w_{1},\dots,w_{k})}_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k+1}}
is given by (
4.7
). Then for any
w_{1},\dots,w_{k}\in\mathcal{W}_{q}
,
p_{1},\dots,p_{k}\in\mathcal{A}_{q}
and
\sigma_{i}\in\mathcal{S}_{|w_{i}|}

(i=1,\dots,k)
it holds

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}^{(w_{1}p_{1},\dots,w_{k}p_{k})}_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k+1}}={\bf 1}^{\top}\Psi^{\sigma_{1}(w_{1})}(\delta_{i_{1}})\left(\prod_{l=2}^{k}E(\Delta_{i_{l-1},i_{l}})\Phi^{p_{l-1}\sigma_{l}(w_{l})}(\delta_{i_{l}})\right)E(t_{i_{k+1}}-t_{i_{k}})b_{p_{k}},

where
\Delta_{i,j}=t_{j-1}-t_{i}

(i<j)
,
\delta_{i}=t_{i}-t_{i-1}
where
\Phi^{w}(\delta),\Psi^{w}(\delta)\subset\mathbb{R}^{R\times R}

(\delta>0,w\in\mathcal{W}_{q})
are defined as in
Section

4.2.1
.

Proof.

Using
Section

4.2.1
we observe that

\displaystyle\prod_{l=1}^{k}\dot{\mathcal{K}}^{w_{l}p_{l},r_{l+1}}_{r_{l},t_{i_{l}}}

\displaystyle=\prod_{l=1}^{k}{\bf 1}^{\top}\Phi^{w_{l}}(t_{i_{l}}-r_{l})E(r_{l+1}-t_{i_{l}})b_{l}

\displaystyle=\prod_{l=1}^{k}{\bf 1}^{\top}\Phi^{w_{l}}(t_{i_{l}}-r_{l})E(t_{i_{l+1}-1}-t_{i_{l}})E(r_{l+1}-t_{i_{l+1}-1})b_{l}

\displaystyle={\bf 1}^{\top}\Phi^{w_{1}}(t_{i_{1}}-r_{1})E(t_{i_{2}-1}-t_{i_{1}})

\displaystyle\qquad\cdot\prod_{l=2}^{k}E(r_{l}-t_{i_{l}-1})C_{p_{l-1}}\Phi^{w_{l}}(t_{i_{l}}-r_{l})E(t_{i_{l+1}-1}-t_{i_{l}})

\displaystyle\qquad\cdot E(r_{k+1}-t_{i_{k+1}-1})b_{k}.

Hence the integrals in (
4.7
) factorize and using the recursion (b) in
Section

4.2.1

The permutation invariance follows directly from the permutation invariance of
\dot{\mathcal{K}}
established in
Section

4.2.1
.
∎

When

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
k(t,s)={\bf 1}^{\top}e^{-\Lambda(t-s)}b

,

,

\mu^{n_{1},\dots,n_{k}}_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k+1}}:=\int_{t_{i_{1}-1}}^{t_{i_{1}}}\cdots\int_{t_{i_{k}-1}}^{t_{i_{k}}}\prod_{l=1}^{k}\dot{\kappa}^{n_{l},r_{l+1}}_{r_{l},t_{i_{l}}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r_{l},\quad r_{k+1}=t_{i_{k+1}},

\displaystyle\mu^{n_{1},\dots,n_{k}}_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k+1}}={\bf 1}^{\top}\psi_{n_{1}}(\delta_{i_{1}})\left(\prod_{l=2}^{k}E(\Delta_{i_{l-1},i_{l}})\phi_{n_{l}}(\delta_{i_{l}})\right)E(t_{i_{k+1}}-t_{i_{k}})b,

.

Proof.

This follows directly from the previous lemmas by setting

∎

Finally we draw a connection between
\Phi
and higher order Fréchet derivatives of the matrix exponential map, which opens up various possibilities for the numerical evaluation of
\Phi
and
\Psi
discussed in the next subsection.

Lemma 4.7
.

Recall from
Section

4.2.1
that
\Phi^{w}(\delta)\in\mathbb{R}^{R\times R}

(\delta>0,\,w\in\mathcal{W}_{q})
is the matrix-valued function defined by the convolution recursions (a)–(b) therein. For any word
w=p_{1}\cdots p_{n}\in\mathcal{W}^{n}_{R}
we have

\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{n}}\Phi^{\sigma(w)}(t)=\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial h_{1}\cdots\partial h_{n}}\exp\bigg(t\bigg(-\Lambda+\sum_{i=1}^{n}h_{i}C_{p_{i}}\bigg)\bigg)\bigg|_{h_{1},\dots,h_{n}=0},\quad C_{p}=b_{p}{\bf 1}^{\top},

where the summation is over all permutations
\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
of
\{1,\cdots,n\}
, and as in
Section

2.3
we set
\sigma(p_{1}\cdots p_{n}):=p_{\sigma(1)}\cdots p_{\sigma(n)}
for any
p_{1}\cdots p_{n}\in\mathcal{W}^{n}_{q}
.
In particular, when
q=1
with
b=b_{1}
we have

\phi_{n}(t)=\frac{1}{n!}\left.\frac{\partial^{n}}{(\partial h)^{n}}\exp\Big(t\Big(-\Lambda+hC\Big)\Big)\right|_{h=0},\quad C=b{\bf 1}^{\top}.

Proof.

Let
p_{1}\cdots p_{n}\in\mathcal{W}^{n}_{q}
and set

A(h)=A(h_{1},\dots,h_{n}):=-\Lambda+\sum_{i=1}^{n}h_{i}C_{p_{i}}
. Then it follows from the classical integral representation of first derivative of the exponential map (see e.g.
[
28
, identity (2)]
) that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial h_{i}}e^{tA(h)}=\int_{0}^{t}e^{rA(h)}C_{p_{i}}e^{(t-r)A(h)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{r},\qquad h\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\quad t\geq 0.

Iterating this expression we arrive at

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial h_{1}\dots\partial h_{n}}e^{tA(h)}=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{n}}\int_{\Delta^{n}_{0,t}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left(e^{(r_{i}-r_{i-1})A(h)}C_{p_{\sigma(i)}}\right)e^{(t-r_{n})A(h)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{r_{1}}\dots\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{r_{n}},\quad r_{0}=0.

Setting
h=(0,\cdots,0)
and comparing with
Section

4.2.1
we observe that the integral term on the right-hand side is precisely given by
\Phi^{\sigma(p_{1}\cdots p_{n})}(t)
, which concludes the proof of the first identity.
The second identity for the
q=1
case the follows directly by observing that in this case
\Phi^{\sigma(w)}=\Phi^{w}=\phi_{|w|}
for all words
w\in\mathcal{W}_{q}
and permutations
\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{|w|}
.
∎

4.2.2.
Numerical evaluation

This subsection addresses the numerical evaluation of
\Phi^{w}(\delta)
and
\Psi^{w}(\delta)
.
First,
Section

4.4.1
shows, using the permutation invariance of the weight factors established in
Section

4.2.1
, that it suffices to evaluate one representative
w(\ell)=1^{\ell_{1}}\cdots q^{\ell_{q}}
per multi-index
\ell\in\mathbb{N}^{q}
, rather than all words
w\in\mathcal{W}_{q}
with
|w|\leq N
.
This reduces the number of required evaluations significantly compared to the full set of Fréchet derivatives appearing in
Section

4.2.1
.

The connection to Fréchet derivatives of the matrix exponential established in
Section

4.2.1
makes it possible to draw on a range of methods from numerical linear algebra, including block embedding techniques
[
18
]
, complex step methods
[
2
]
, and recursions in terms of divided differences
[
30
]
.
Here we specialize to the algorithm of
[
35
]
, based on approximating contour integral representations by quadrature.
This is particularly suitable in our setting since it exploits the available structure (
\Lambda
in real Jordan normal form, rank-one directions
C_{p}=b_{p}\mathbf{1}^{\top}
) and admits a fully parallel evaluation across words.
The starting point is
Section

4.2.2
, which provides contour integral representations for
\Phi^{w}
and
\Psi^{w}
; our proof, unlike
[
35
, Section 2.1]
, does not average over permutations and instead uses the complex inversion formula for the Laplace transform.
To this end, we use the following notation from complex analysis.

Notation 4.8
.

For a matrix
A\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
we denote by
\mathrm{spec}(A)\subset\mathbb{C}
the set of eigenvalues.
For a contour
\mathfrak{C}\subset\mathbb{C}
we write
\mathrm{spec}(A)\subset\mathrm{int}(\mathfrak{C})
if
\mathfrak{C}
encloses
\mathrm{spec}(A)
.
Furthermore, for
\alpha>\max\{\Re(\lambda)\;:\;\lambda\in\mathrm{spec}(A)\}
we call
\mathfrak{C}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}\;:\;\Re(z)=\alpha\}
a Bromwich line to the right of
\mathrm{spec}(A)
.

Remark 4.9
.

We assure the reader that these concepts from complex analysis are exclusively needed to state and prove the integral
representations for
\Phi
and
\Psi
below, which are then used for a quadrature on the complex plane.
For details on contour integrals in the context of Laplace transforms we refer to
[
33
, Section 3]
.

Lemma 4.10
.

Let
w=p_{1}\cdots p_{n}\in\mathcal{W}_{q}^{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1}
and
\delta>0
.
For
\zeta\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathrm{spec}(-\delta\Lambda)
set

R_{\delta}(\zeta):=(\zeta I+\delta\Lambda)^{-1},\qquad M^{w}_{\delta}(\zeta):=R_{\delta}(\zeta)\,C_{p_{1}}\,R_{\delta}(\zeta)\cdots C_{p_{n}}\,R_{\delta}(\zeta),

where
C_{p}=b_{p}{\bf 1}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{R\times R}
is as in (
4.9
), and
I\in\mathbb{R}^{R\times R}
denotes the identity matrix.
Then, for any Bromwich line
\mathfrak{C}
to the right of
\mathrm{spec}(-\delta\Lambda)
, or any simple, closed and positively oriented contour
\mathfrak{C}
with

\mathrm{spec}(-\delta\Lambda)\subset\mathrm{int}(\mathfrak{C})
, the matrices
\Psi^{w}(\delta),\Phi^{w}(\delta)\in\mathbb{R}^{R\times R}
of
Section

4.2.1
respectively satisfy

(4.10)

\displaystyle\Phi^{w}(\delta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\delta^{n}}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathfrak{C}}e^{\zeta}\,M^{w}_{\delta}(\zeta)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\zeta

(4.11)

\displaystyle\Psi^{w}(\delta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\delta^{n+1}}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathfrak{C}}\varphi_{1}(\zeta)\,M^{w}_{\delta}(\zeta)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\zeta,

where
\varphi_{1}(\zeta)=\zeta^{-1}(e^{\zeta}-1)
is defined on
\mathbb{C}
via analytic continuation.

Proof.

Following
[
33
, Definition 1.10]
we call a function
f:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{C}
of exponential order
\alpha

if there are
c,t_{0}\geq 0
such that
|f(t)|\leq ce^{\alpha t}
for all
t\geq t_{0}
.
We can then see that the components of the matrix-valued function
t\mapsto E(t):=e^{-t\Lambda}
(
t\geq 0
) are of
exponential order
\alpha_{0}+\varepsilon
for any
\varepsilon>0
, where

\alpha_{0}:=-\min\{\Re(\lambda):\lambda\in\mathrm{spec}(\Lambda)\}
.
Here
\varepsilon
accounts for the polynomial prefactor stemming from Jordan blocks; as noted after
Section

4.1
,
\Lambda
can be assumed without loss of generality to be in real Jordan normal form, in which case this is immediate, and otherwise follows by passing to Jordan form.

It thus follows by
[
33
, Theorem 3.1]
that the componentwise Laplace transform

\widehat{E}(z):=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-zt}E(t)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

is well defined and analytic for all
z\in\mathbb{C}
with
\Re(z)>\alpha_{0}
.
Furthermore, for such
z
the map
t\mapsto e^{-zt}E(t)
is differentiable and satisfies

\frac{d}{dt}\big(e^{-zt}E(t)\big)=-e^{-zt}(zI+\Lambda)E(t),

Hence, integrating over
[0,\infty)
yields

(zI+\Lambda)\widehat{E}(z)=-\big[e^{-zt}E(t)\big]_{t=0}^{\infty}=I,

and we readily see

(4.12)

\widehat{E}(z)=(zI+\Lambda)^{-1},\qquad\Re(z)>\alpha_{0}.

Next, using recursion (a) in
Section

4.2.1
we verify inductively that for any word
w\in\mathcal{W}_{q}
the components
of
t\mapsto\Phi^{w}(t)
are again of exponential order
\alpha_{0}+\varepsilon
, and thus

\widehat{\Phi}^{w}(z):=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-zt}\Phi^{w}(t)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

exists and is analytic in
\Re(z)>\alpha_{0}
.
The convolution theorem for Laplace transforms
[
33
, Theorem 2.39]
applied componentwise then yields,
by the same recursion, that

\widehat{\Phi}^{p_{1}\cdots p_{n}}(z)=\widehat{\Phi}^{p_{1}\cdots p_{n-1}}(z)\,C_{p_{n}}\,\widehat{E}(z).

Since
\Phi^{\varnothing}(t)=E(t)
, iterating and using (
4.12
) gives

(4.13)

\widehat{\Phi}^{p_{1}\cdots p_{n}}(z)=(zI+\Lambda)^{-1}C_{p_{1}}(zI+\Lambda)^{-1}\cdots C_{p_{n}}(zI+\Lambda)^{-1}.

In particular, we also observe that the estimate
\|\widehat{\Phi}^{w}(z)\|\leq c|z|^{-(n+1)}
holds for all
z\in\mathbb{C}
with
\Re(z)>\alpha_{0}+1

and a suitable constant
c>0
.
Now let
\delta>0
and let
\mathfrak{C}
be as in the statement of our lemma.
Due to our assumption
\mathrm{spec}(-\delta\Lambda)\subset\mathrm{int}(\mathfrak{C})
and the fact that

\mathrm{spec}(-\Lambda)=\delta^{-1}\,\mathrm{spec}(-\delta\Lambda)
, the scaled contour
\mathfrak{C}^{\prime}:=\delta^{-1}\mathfrak{C}
is again a Bromwich line to the right of (or a simple closed positively oriented contour enclosing) the set
\mathrm{spec}(-\Lambda)
.
Applying componentwise the Bromwich inversion formula for the Laplace transform
[
33
, Theorem 4.3]
on the Bromwich line
\mathfrak{C}^{\prime}
, and deforming to a closed contour using the Cauchy residue theorem
[
33
, p. 143]
, we obtain

(4.14)

\Phi^{w}(\delta)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathfrak{C}^{\prime}}e^{z\delta}\,\widehat{\Phi}^{w}(z)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z,\qquad\delta>0.

The identity (
4.10
) then follows by the change of variables
\zeta=\delta z
using
(zI+\Lambda)^{-1}=\delta R_{\delta}(\zeta)
.

For
\Psi^{w}(\delta)=\int_{0}^{\delta}\Phi^{w}(u)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u
, we insert the representation of
\Phi^{w}(u)
and use Fubini,
justified by the exponential order of
\Phi
discussed above, to obtain

\Psi^{w}(\delta)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathfrak{C}^{\prime}}\left(\int_{0}^{\delta}e^{zu}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u\right)\widehat{\Phi}^{w}(z)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathfrak{C}^{\prime}}\frac{e^{z\delta}-1}{z}\,\widehat{\Phi}^{w}(z)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z.

Once again the change of variables
\zeta=\delta z
leads to (
4.11
), and the proof is complete.
∎

We now restrict to the case where
\mathrm{spec}(\Lambda)\subset[0,\infty)
,
which is the regime relevant for our applications.
Approximating the integrals in (
4.10
) and (
4.11
)
by the trapezoidal rule on a suitable parametrization of
\mathfrak{C}
yields nodes

\zeta_{1},\dots,\zeta_{m}\in\mathbb{C}
and weights
\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{m}\in\mathbb{C}
and
\widetilde{\omega}_{1},\dots,\widetilde{\omega}_{m}\in\mathbb{C}
such that

(4.15)

\Phi^{w}(\delta)\;\approx\;\delta^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\omega_{j}\,M^{w}_{\delta}(\zeta_{j}),\qquad\Psi^{w}(\delta)\;\approx\;\delta^{n+1}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\widetilde{\omega}_{j}\,M^{w}_{\delta}(\zeta_{j}).

For the exponential function and spectra close to the negative real axis, optimized parabolic or hyperbolic contours were studied in
[
41
,
42
,
38
]
.
For instance, an optimized parabolic contour gives explicit nodes and weights
[
38
, Equation (3.1)]

\zeta_{j}=m\cdot\bigl(0.1309-0.1194\,\theta_{j}^{2}+0.25\,i\,\theta_{j}\bigr),\qquad\omega_{j}=\exp(\zeta_{j})\cdot\bigl(0.2388\,i\,\theta_{j}+0.25\bigr),

where
\theta_{j}
are equispaced points in
[-\pi,\pi]
.
For the evaluation of the matrix exponential this quadrature yields an approximation error of the order
\mathcal{O}(2.85^{-m})
.
Moreover,
[
35
, Proposition 12]
shows that the corresponding error asymptotics translate to the approximation
of higher-order Fréchet derivatives, which transfers to the approximation of
\Phi^{w}(\delta)
and
\Psi^{w}(\delta)
in (
4.15
).
For the approximation of
\Psi
we use the same nodes. In the quadrature weights, however, we replace
\varphi_{1}(\zeta)=(e^{\zeta}-1)/\zeta
by
e^{\zeta}/\zeta
, obtained by removing the vanishing
1/\zeta
contribution; see, e.g.,
[
34
]
. Thus we set

\widetilde{\omega}_{j}=\frac{\exp(\zeta_{j})}{\zeta_{j}}\,\bigl(0.2388\,i\,\theta_{j}+0.25\bigr).

Finally, we discuss the simplifications in our setting for the evaluation of
M^{w}_{\delta}(\zeta)
.
First note that, since
\Lambda
is real, we have the conjugate identity

\omega\,M^{w}_{\delta}(\zeta)+\overline{\omega}\,M^{w}_{\delta}(\overline{\zeta})=2\,\Re\big(\omega\,M^{w}_{\delta}(\zeta)\big),

Thus it suffices to evaluate the right-hand side for one representative per conjugate pair of quadrature nodes.

Next we exploit the rank-one and Jordan structure (cf. the rank-one simplification in
[
35
, Section 4.3]
).
To this end, for nodes
\zeta
on the contour we define the vectors

u_{p}(\zeta):=R_{\delta}(\zeta)b_{p}\in\mathbb{C}^{R},\qquad r(\zeta)^{\top}:={\bf 1}^{\top}R_{\delta}(\zeta)\in\mathbb{C}^{1\times R},\qquad\beta_{p}(\zeta):=r(\zeta)^{\top}b_{p}\in\mathbb{C}.

Then we can express
M^{w}_{\delta}(\zeta)
for
w=p_{1}\cdots p_{n}
with
n>1
without dense matrix–matrix multiplications as follows:

(4.16)

M^{w}_{\delta}(\zeta)=u_{p_{1}}(\zeta)r(\zeta)^{\top}\prod_{k=2}^{n}\beta_{p_{k}}(\zeta).

Thus each quadrature node requires only shifted solves with
\zeta I+\delta\Lambda
to form
u_{p}(\zeta)
and
r(\zeta)^{\top}
.
Since
\Lambda
is assumed to be in (real) Jordan normal form, the costs of these shifted solves are only of order
R
.

As discussed in
Section

4.4.1
we only need to compute
\Psi^{w(\ell)}
and
\Phi^{pw(\ell)}
for multiindices
\ell\in\mathbb{N}^{q}
where
w(\ell)=1^{\ell_{1}}\cdots q^{\ell_{q}}
.
To this end we see that

(4.17)

M^{pw(\ell)}_{\delta}(\zeta)=u_{p}(\zeta)r(\zeta)^{\top}\beta_{1}(\zeta)^{\ell_{1}}\cdots\beta_{q}(\zeta)^{\ell_{q}}.

Moreover, as readily observed in
4.12
we only ever need to evaluate the row vector
{\bf 1}^{\top}\Psi^{w}(\delta)
and not the full matrix
\Psi^{w}(\delta)
, so we obtain more simply

{\bf 1}^{\top}M^{w(\ell)}_{\delta}(\zeta)=r(\zeta)^{\top}\beta_{1}(\zeta)^{\ell_{1}}\cdots\beta_{q}(\zeta)^{\ell_{q}}.

In particular, the computation can be efficiently parallelized over multi-indices and quadrature nodes.

4.3.
The recursion

We now demonstrate how the factorization of the weight coefficients in
Section

4.2.1
yields a recursion for the Volterra signature of the finite state space kernel
K^{\Lambda}_{A,b}
along piecewise linear paths, involving only a linear number of recursion steps in the number of partition intervals.
To this end, we introduce the following notation, which makes both the presentation and the
implementation
more convenient:

Notation 4.11
.

We consider
\mathfrak{R}_{N}:=(T^{N}(\mathbb{R}^{m}),\otimes_{N})
as a ring and, over it, the set of matrices
(\mathfrak{R}_{N})^{k\times n}
for
k,n\geq 1
.
In particular, for two matrices
\mathcal{M}=(\mathcal{M}^{ij})\in(\mathfrak{R}_{N})^{k\times n}
and
\mathcal{N}=(\mathcal{N}^{ij})\in(\mathfrak{R}_{N})^{n\times q}
, the matrix product is denoted by
\mathcal{M}.\mathcal{N}\in(\mathfrak{R}_{N})^{k\times q}
and is defined componentwise by

\big(\mathcal{M}.\mathcal{N}\big)^{ij}=\sum_{l=1}^{n}\mathcal{M}^{il}\otimes_{N}\mathcal{N}^{lj}.

Any linear operation defined on
T^{N}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
extends to matrices
(\mathfrak{R}_{N})^{k\times n}
componentwise. This holds, in particular, for multiplication with a tensor
\mathbf{y}\in T^{N}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
, with

\mathbf{y}\otimes\mathcal{M}=(\mathbf{y}\otimes\mathcal{M}^{ij})
and
\mathcal{M}\otimes\mathbf{y}=(\mathcal{M}^{ij}\otimes\mathbf{y})
,
and for the projection
\pi_{l}
, with
\pi_{l}\mathcal{M}=(\pi_{l}\mathcal{M}^{ij})
.

Theorem 4.12
.

Let
K_{A,b}^{\Lambda}\in L^{\infty,1}(\Delta^{2};\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{m}))
be a kernel satisfying
Section

4.1
, i.e. of the form (
4.5
) for given data
(\Lambda,A,b)
, and
{x:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{d}}
be continuous piecewise linear path on some grid
t_{0}<t_{1}<\dots<t_{J}\leq T
.
We set
E(\delta)=e^{-\Lambda\delta}\in\mathbb{R}^{R\times R}

(\delta>0)
,
\delta_{j}=t_{j}-t_{j-1}
and

y_{j}^{p}=A_{p}(x_{t_{j}}-x_{t_{j-1}}),\qquad j=1,\dots,J,\quad p=1,\dots,q.

Fixed a truncation level
N\in\mathbb{N}
.
Then for any
j=0,\dots,J
and
\tau\in[t_{j},T]
it holds

(4.18)

\pi_{\leq N}\mathrm{VSig}(x;K_{A,b}^{\Lambda})^{\tau}_{t_{0},t_{j}}=1+\sum_{p=1}^{q}\mathbf{Z}^{p}_{j}.E(\tau-t_{j})b_{p},

where each
\mathbf{Z}^{j}
is an element of
(\mathfrak{R}_{N})^{1\times R}
, where the products
.
in (
4.18
) are understood in the sense of
Section

4.3
, and where we define the following iteration for all
p\in\mathcal{A}_{q}
:

(4.19)

\left\{\begin{split}{\bf Z}^{p}_{0}=&~\bigg.(0,\dots,0)\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times R}\subset(\mathfrak{R}_{N})^{1\times R},\\
\bigg.{\bf Z}^{p}_{j}=&~{\bf Z}^{p}_{j-1}.E(\delta_{j})+\left({\bf 1}^{\top}.\mathcal{F}_{\delta_{j}}\!\Big(y_{j}^{1},\dots,y_{j}^{q}\Big)+\sum_{l=1}^{q}{\bf Z}^{l}_{j-1}.{\mathcal{G}}^{l}_{\delta_{j}}\!\Big(y_{j}^{1},\dots,y_{j}^{q}\Big)\right)\otimes y_{j}^{p}.\end{split}\right.

Note that in (
4.19
) we have used the following additional notation: for
\delta>0
,
p_{0}\in\mathcal{A}_{q}
and

we define

(4.20)

\begin{split}\mathcal{F}_{\delta}(y_{1},\dots,y_{q})=&\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\frac{1}{\delta^{n+1}}\sum_{p_{1}\cdots p_{n}\in\mathcal{W}^{n}_{q}}\Psi^{p_{1}\cdots p_{n}}(\delta)y_{p_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes y_{p_{n}}\quad\in(\mathfrak{R}_{N})^{R\times R},\\

where
\Psi^{w}(\delta),\Phi^{w}(\delta)\in\mathbb{R}^{R\times R}

(w\in\mathcal{W}_{q})

are defined in

in terms of

.

Proof.

We set
v^{p}_{j}:=(\delta_{j})^{-1}y^{p}_{j}
and verify inductively that

takes the following expanded form:

(4.21)

\times(v_{i_{1}})^{w_{1}p_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes(v_{i_{k-1}})^{w_{k-1}p_{k-1}}\otimes(v_{i_{k}})^{w_{k}p},

for all
p\in\mathcal{A}_{q}
,

and

,
where

and

(4.22)

\mathcal{M}^{(w_{1}p_{1},\dots,w_{k-1}p_{k-1},w_{k})}_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k+1}}={\bf 1}^{\top}\Psi^{w_{1}}(\delta_{i_{1}})\left(\prod_{l=2}^{k}E(\Delta_{i_{l-1},i_{l}})\Phi^{p_{l-1}w_{l}}(\delta_{i_{l}})\right)E(\Delta_{i_{k},i_{k+1}}),

for all

and

with

(i<j
).
Indeed, the claim clearly holds for

as the first sum is empty and
\pi_{n}\mathbf{Z}^{p}_{0}=0
for all

by definition.
Now for the induction step we consider the three terms in the iteration step

in
(

First, note that using the semigroup property of

we obtain

\mathcal{M}^{(w_{1}p_{1},\dots,w_{k-1}p_{k-1},w_{k})}_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k},j}.E(\delta_{j})=\mathcal{M}^{(w_{1}p_{1},\dots,w_{k-1}p_{k-1},w_{k})}_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k},j+1}.

Thus the term

constitutes all terms in (

) corresponding to indices
(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})
where

.
Next, we note that

\pi_{n+1}\left({\bf 1}^{\top}.\mathcal{F}_{\delta_{j}}\!\Big(y_{j}^{1},\dots,y_{j}^{q}\Big)\otimes y_{j}^{p}\right)=\sum_{w\in\mathcal{W}_{q}^{n}}\mathcal{M}^{(w)}_{j,j+1}(v_{j})^{wp},

which hence constitute the terms corresponding to the indices
(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})
with

. The generalization to our framework requires the auxiliary states (
5.3
)–(
5.4
), which enter linearly into the main equation (
5.2
). In addition, these states are characterized by linear ODEs and therefore admit the explicit representations

(5.7)

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Psi}(s,t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{s}e^{-\Lambda(s-u)}\eta(u,t)\gamma(u,t)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u,

(5.8)

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Phi}(s,t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\gamma(s,u)\eta(s,u)e^{-\Lambda^{\top}(t-u)}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u.

With these observations in hand, it should not come as a surprise that the numerical schemes introduced in the classical case
[
31
]
admit straightforward generalizations to our framework, which we illustrate in the next sections.

5.1.
Static kernel lift

A key advantage of the classical signature kernel trick

[
22
,
31
]
is that, without losing tractability, one may first
lift the underlying data from the ambient space into an RKHS,

x_{t}\mapsto\phi(x_{t})\in\mathcal{H}
, and then apply the signature kernel transform
in this possibly infinite-dimensional space. The same idea can be incorporated
in our Volterra setting, as we briefly outline now.

Given a kernel from the class (
4.5
) and a path

x\in\mathcal{C}^{0,1}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})
, recall that the corresponding Volterra path reads

\int_{s}^{t}K(t,u)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x_{u}=\sum_{r=1}^{q}\int_{s}^{t}\mathbf{1}^{\top}e^{-\Lambda(t-u)}b_{r}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}(A_{r}x_{u}),\qquad A_{r}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{m}).

Thus, the linear maps
A_{1},\dots,A_{q}
may be interpreted as preprocessing
channels which transform the input data before the Volterra kernel is applied. In the spirit of the static kernel lift, one may replace these finite-dimensional
preprocessing maps by feature maps taking values in a Hilbert space. More
precisely, let
A_{r}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathcal{H}
be sufficiently regular feature maps. The resulting Volterra signature then takes values in

T((\mathcal{H}))
, so that even truncated signatures are infinite-dimensional whenever

\mathcal{H}
is infinite-dimensional. The important observation is that the PDE
characterization of the Volterra signature kernel in
(
5.1
)–(
5.3
) remains valid.
Only the coefficient
\gamma
has to be replaced by the Hilbert-space inner
product of the lifted increments, namely

(5.9)

\gamma(s,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{R\times R},\qquad\gamma_{ij}(s,t)=\sum_{r,p=1}^{q}b_{r}^{i}b_{p}^{j}\big\langle\dot{X}_{s}^{r},\dot{W}_{t}^{p}\big\rangle_{\mathcal{H}},\qquad X_{s}^{r}=A_{r}(x_{s}),\quad W_{t}^{p}=A_{p}(w_{t}).

Hence, provided that the Hilbert-space inner products can be evaluated
efficiently, for instance through a reproducing kernel such as the RBF kernel,
the computation of the Volterra signature kernel remains
tractable.

5.2.
A predictor-corrector finite-difference scheme

We now present our main numerical scheme for solving the PDE system
(
5.2
)-(
5.3
). The scheme applies,
in particular, to the general coefficient matrix (
5.9
) arising
from a static RKHS lift.

We consider two grids on
[0,S]
and
[0,T]
respectively

0=s_{0}<\cdots<s_{J_{s}}=S,\qquad 0=t_{0}<\cdots<t_{J_{t}}=T,

and set

\Delta s_{i}:=s_{i+1}-s_{i},\qquad\Delta t_{j}:=t_{j+1}-t_{j}.

In the sequel, we write

\mathbf{K}_{i,j}\approx\mathbf{K}(s_{i},t_{j}),\qquad\mathbf{\Psi}_{i,j}\approx\mathbf{\Psi}(s_{i},t_{j}),\qquad\mathbf{\Phi}_{i,j}\approx\mathbf{\Phi}(s_{i},t_{j}),

and set
\eta_{i,j}:=1+\mathbf{1}^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{i,j}\mathbf{1}.
Moreover, we denote the static kernel induced by the feature maps
A_{1},\dots,A_{r}
described in the last section, by
k^{r,p}_{\mathrm{stat}}(x,w):=\langle A_{r}(x),A_{p}(w)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
. The coefficient matrix
\gamma
is approximated by

\gamma_{nm}(s_{i},t_{j})\approx\gamma^{nm}_{ij}:=b^{\top}G_{i,j}b,\quad G_{i,j}=(G_{i,j}^{a,b})_{1\leq a,b\leq q},

where

\displaystyle G_{i,j}^{a,b}=k^{a,b}_{\mathrm{stat}}(x_{s_{i+1}},w_{t_{j+1}})

\displaystyle-k^{a,b}_{\mathrm{stat}}(x_{s_{i}},w_{t_{j+1}})-k^{a,b}_{\mathrm{stat}}(x_{s_{i+1}},w_{t_{j}})+k^{a,b}_{\mathrm{stat}}(x_{s_{i}},w_{t_{j}}).

According to the boundary conditions (
5.5
), we initialize

\mathbf{K}_{i,0}=0,\qquad\mathbf{K}_{0,j}=0,\qquad\mathbf{\Psi}_{0,j}=0,\qquad\mathbf{\Phi}_{i,0}=0,\qquad i=0,\dots,J_{s},\quad j=0,\dots,J_{t},

and hence, in particular,
\eta_{i,0}=\eta_{0,j}=1
. Moreover, we introduce the exponential transport operators

E_{i}^{s}:=e^{-\Delta s_{i}\Lambda},\qquad P_{i}^{s}:=\Delta s_{i}\,\varphi_{1}(-\Delta s_{i}\Lambda),\qquad Q_{i}^{s}:=\Delta s_{i}\,\varphi_{2}(-\Delta s_{i}\Lambda),

and

E_{j}^{t}:=e^{-\Delta t_{j}\Lambda^{\top}},\qquad P_{j}^{t}:=\Delta t_{j}\,\varphi_{1}(-\Delta t_{j}\Lambda^{\top}),\qquad Q_{j}^{t}:=\Delta t_{j}\,\varphi_{2}(-\Delta t_{j}\Lambda^{\top}),

where
\varphi_{1}(z)=(e^{z}-1)/z
and
\varphi_{2}(z)=(e^{z}-z-1)/z^{2}.
On each cell, we first compute a predictor. To this end, we set

\mathbf{H}^{(0)}_{i,j}:=\gamma_{i,j}\,\frac{\eta_{i,j}+\eta_{i,j+1}}{2},\qquad\mathbf{J}^{(0)}_{i,j}:=\gamma_{i,j}\,\frac{\eta_{i,j}+\eta_{i+1,j}}{2}.

Using these quantities, we define the predictor values for the auxiliary variables by

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Psi}^{p}_{i+1,j+1}

\displaystyle=E_{i}^{s}\,\mathbf{\Psi}_{i,j+1}+P_{i}^{s}\,\mathbf{H}^{(0)}_{i,j},

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Phi}^{p}_{i+1,j+1}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{\Phi}_{i+1,j}E_{j}^{t}+\mathbf{J}^{(0)}_{i,j}P_{j}^{t}.

With the notation
\mathcal{L}(M):=\Lambda M\Lambda^{\top}
, the predictor for the main variable is then given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}^{p}_{i+1,j+1}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{K}_{i+1,j}+\mathbf{K}_{i,j+1}-\mathbf{K}_{i,j}+\frac{\Delta s_{i}\Delta t_{j}}{2}\Bigl(\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{K}_{i+1,j})+\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{K}_{i,j+1})\Bigr)

\displaystyle\qquad-\frac{1}{2}\,\gamma_{i,j}\bigl(\eta_{i+1,j}+\eta_{i,j+1}\bigr)+\bigl(\mathbf{\Psi}^{p}_{i+1,j+1}-\mathbf{\Psi}_{i,j+1}\bigr)+\bigl(\mathbf{\Phi}^{p}_{i+1,j+1}-\mathbf{\Phi}_{i+1,j}\bigr).

Finally, we set
\eta^{p}_{i+1,j+1}:=1+\mathbf{1}^{\top}\mathbf{K}^{p}_{i+1,j+1}\mathbf{1}.
Given the predictors, the corrector step we update the source terms by is

\mathbf{H}^{(1)}_{i,j}:=\gamma_{i,j}\,\frac{\eta_{i+1,j}+\eta^{p}_{i+1,j+1}}{2},\qquad\mathbf{J}^{(1)}_{i,j}:=\gamma_{i,j}\,\frac{\eta_{i,j+1}+\eta^{p}_{i+1,j+1}}{2}.

We then define

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Psi}_{i+1,j+1}

\displaystyle=E_{i}^{s}\mathbf{\Psi}_{i,j+1}+P_{i}^{s}\mathbf{H}^{(0)}_{i,j}+Q_{i}^{s}\bigl(\mathbf{H}^{(1)}_{i,j}-\mathbf{H}^{(0)}_{i,j}\bigr),

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Phi}_{i+1,j+1}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{\Phi}_{i+1,j}E_{j}^{t}+\mathbf{J}^{(0)}_{i,j}P_{j}^{t}+\bigl(\mathbf{J}^{(1)}_{i,j}-\mathbf{J}^{(0)}_{i,j}\bigr)Q_{j}^{t}.

Finally, the corrected update for
\mathbf{K}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}_{i+1,j+1}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{K}_{i+1,j}+\mathbf{K}_{i,j+1}-\mathbf{K}_{i,j}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\Delta s_{i}\Delta t_{j}}{4}\Bigl(\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{K}_{i,j})+\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{K}_{i+1,j})+\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{K}_{i,j+1})+\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{K}^{p}_{i+1,j+1})\Bigr)

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{1}{4}\,\gamma_{i,j}\Bigl(\eta_{i,j}+\eta_{i+1,j}+\eta_{i,j+1}+\eta^{p}_{i+1,j+1}\Bigr)

\displaystyle\quad+\bigl(\mathbf{\Psi}_{i+1,j+1}-\mathbf{\Psi}_{i,j+1}\bigr)+\bigl(\mathbf{\Phi}_{i+1,j+1}-\mathbf{\Phi}_{i+1,j}\bigr).

The scalar quantity at the new corner is then updated by
\eta_{i+1,j+1}=1+\mathbf{1}^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{i+1,j+1}\mathbf{1}.
This yields an explicit predictor–corrector scheme for the transformed system
(
5.2
)–(
5.4
). More precisely, on each cell

[s_{i},s_{i+1}]\times[t_{j},t_{j+1}]
, the values

\mathbf{K}_{i,j},\quad\mathbf{K}_{i+1,j},\quad\mathbf{K}_{i,j+1},\quad\mathbf{\Psi}_{i,j+1},\quad\mathbf{\Phi}_{i+1,j}

are already known, and the above formulas produce

\mathbf{K}_{i+1,j+1},\qquad\mathbf{\Psi}_{i+1,j+1},\qquad\mathbf{\Phi}_{i+1,j+1},\qquad\eta_{i+1,j+1}.

6.
Computational cost analysis

In this section we analyze the computational costs of the algorithms for Volterra signatures introduced in
Section

3
and the finite state space kernels in
Section

4.4
, and finally, the finite-difference scheme presented in Section
5
. By
computational cost
we mean the number of elementary arithmetic operations (additions and multiplications) required by a dense implementation of the stated recursions, up to constant factors that depend only on fixed model parameters.

To limit the scope, we focus on the asymptotic dependence of these costs on the number of time steps
J
, the truncation level
N
, and, where applicable, the state space dimension
R
. Concretely, we introduce the following definition.

Definition 6.1
.

We say that the cost of a specific (dense) implementation of an algorithm is
asymptotically in
(J,R,N)
of order
\Theta(f(J,R,N))
for some function
f:\mathbb{N}^{3}\to(0,\infty)
if there exist constants
c,C>0
and
J_{0},R_{0},N_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that the number of elementary arithmetic operations
\mathrm{Cost}(J,R,N)
satisfies

c\,f(J,R,N)\leq\mathrm{Cost}(J,R,N)\leq C\,f(J,R,N)

for all
J\geq J_{0}
,
R\geq R_{0}
,
N\geq N_{0}
.

In general, it is of course not clear a priori that the cost of a given algorithm can be described by this multivariate
\Theta
-convention.
However, in our setting the dependence on
J
and
R
is visible directly from explicit prefactors in the stated recursions, so the main discussion below concerns the asymptotic order in the truncation level
N
.

Remark 6.2
.

The natural benchmark for our algorithms is the cost of computing the standard (non-Volterra) truncated signature of a piecewise linear path with
J
steps and truncation level
N
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, which is of order
\Theta(J\,d^{N})
. As recalled in the introduction (cf. (
1.5
) and the surrounding discussion), this cost follows from iterating Chen’s identity together with a Horner-type evaluation of the tensor exponential on each linear segment. The algorithms developed in this paper for Volterra signatures necessarily carry an additional cost: the general scheme of
Section

3
incurs a quadratic factor
J^{2}
instead of
J
, while the finite state space scheme of
Section

4
recovers linearity in
J
, but involves a factor
R^{2}
(where
R
is the state space dimension).

We first recap basic cost estimates for tensor operations in
Section

6.1
, then discuss the computational cost of the general approximation scheme from
Section

3
in
Section

6.2
. Finally, in
Section

6.3
we carry out the analogous cost analysis for the finite state space recursion.

6.1.
Basics

Throughout this subsection we assume a dense representation of homogeneous tensors, i.e. an element
\mathbf{x}^{(k)}\in(\mathbb{R}^{m})^{\otimes k}
is stored by its
m^{k}
coefficients in the standard basis.
For simplicity, we will exclude the trivial case
m=1
throughout as this would sometimes lead to case distinctions, i.e., we assume
m\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}
fixed.
We recap the computational costs of forming (truncated) tensor products and shuffle-by-vector updates (as used in
Algorithms

2
and
8
). Note that the following two lemmas are elementary and we include their proofs for lack of a proper reference.
For notational convenience, for two functions
f,g:\mathbb{N}\to(0,\infty)
we write

f(N)\sim g(N),\quad\text{if and only if}\quad\lim_{N\to\infty}f(N)^{-1}g(N)\in(0,\infty).

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
\mathbf{x}^{(k)}\in(\mathbb{R}^{m})^{\otimes k}
and
\mathbf{y}^{(\ell)}\in(\mathbb{R}^{m})^{\otimes\ell}
with
N:=k+\ell
. The cost of forming the concatenation product
\mathbf{x}^{(k)}\otimes\mathbf{y}^{(\ell)}\in(\mathbb{R}^{m})^{\otimes N}
is of order
\Theta(m^{N})
.
Moreover, for
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in T^{N}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
the costs of forming the truncated tensor product
\mathbf{x}\otimes_{N}\mathbf{y}
is of the order

\Theta(Nm^{N}).

Proof.

The tensor
\mathbf{x}^{(k)}\otimes\mathbf{y}^{(\ell)}\in(\mathbb{R}^{m})^{\otimes(k+\ell)}=(\mathbb{R}^{m})^{\otimes N}
has
m^{N}
coefficients in the standard basis and can be formed coefficientwise, hence the cost is of order
\Theta(m^{N})
.
For
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in T^{N}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
, at level
n
the truncated product satisfies

\pi_{n}(\mathbf{x}\otimes_{N}\mathbf{y})=\sum_{r=0}^{n}\mathbf{x}^{(r)}\otimes\mathbf{y}^{(n-r)},\qquad n=0,\dots,N.

Each summand is a tensor in
(\mathbb{R}^{m})^{\otimes n}
and can be formed coefficientwise at cost of order
\Theta(m^{n})
, so computing
\pi_{n}(\mathbf{x}\otimes_{N}\mathbf{y})
costs of order
\Theta((n+1)m^{n})
. Summing over
n=0,\dots,N
yields

\sum_{n=0}^{N}(n+1)m^{n}=\frac{m\big(1-(N+2)m^{N+1}+(N+1)m^{N+2}\big)}{(1-m)^{2}}\sim Nm^{N},

which proves the claim.
∎

Lemma 6.4
.

Let
\mathbf{x}^{(k)}\in(\mathbb{R}^{m})^{\otimes k}
and
y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
with
N:=k+1
. The cost of forming the shuffle product
\mathbf{x}^{(k)}\shuffle y\in(\mathbb{R}^{m})^{\otimes N}
is of order
\Theta(Nm^{N})
.

Proof.

By (
2.2
) in
Section

2.1
, the shuffle product
\mathbf{x}^{(k)}\shuffle y\in(\mathbb{R}^{m})^{\otimes(k+1)}
is obtained by inserting
y
into
\mathbf{x}^{(k)}
at all possible positions. Writing
\mathbf{x}^{(k)}=\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}=1}^{m}\mathbf{x}^{i_{1}\cdots i_{k}}\,e_{i_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes e_{i_{k}}
and
y=\sum_{j=1}^{m}y_{j}e_{j}
, one has for
i_{1},\dots,i_{k+1}\in\{1,\dots,m\}
that

(\mathbf{x}^{(k)}\shuffle y)^{i_{1}\cdots i_{k+1}}=\sum_{r=1}^{k+1}\mathbf{x}^{i_{1}\cdots i_{r-1}\,i_{r+1}\cdots i_{k+1}}\;y_{i_{r}}.

Thus, computing each coefficient
(\mathbf{x}^{(k)}\shuffle y)^{i_{1}\cdots i_{k+1}}
requires
k+1=N
multiplications and
k
additions. Since there are
m^{k+1}=m^{N}
coefficients, the total cost is of order
(k+1)m^{k+1}=Nm^{N}
, i.e.
\Theta(Nm^{N})
.
∎

Lemma 6.7
.

Fix

with

Proof.

We first verify (by elementary entire series arguments) that for the inner sum we have the two-sided estimate

(N-1-n)m^{N-1-n}\leq\sum_{k=0}^{N-2-n}(k+1)m^{k+1}\;\leq\;\frac{m}{m-1}(N-1-n)m^{N-1-n},

\sum_{n=0}^{N-2}{n+q-1\choose q-1}\sum_{k=0}^{N-2-n}(k+1)m^{k+1}\;\sim\;\sum_{n=0}^{N-2}{n+q-1\choose q-1}(N-1-n)m^{N-1-n}.

Using

and the binomial series (

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}{n+q-1\choose q-1}m^{-n}=\left(1-\frac{1}{m}\right)^{-q}<\infty,

\widehat{\mathbf{v}}_{j},&i=j-1.\end{cases}

Here

\omega^{\mathrm{E}}_{i,j}=\omega^{\mathrm{L}}_{i,j}+\omega^{\mathrm{R}}_{i,j}

and

\omega^{\mathrm{L}}_{i,j}:=\frac{1}{h_{i}}\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{i+1}}\frac{t_{i+1}-s}{h_{i}}k(t_{j},s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s,\qquad\omega^{\mathrm{R}}_{i,j}:=\frac{1}{h_{i}}\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{i+1}}\frac{s-t_{i}}{h_{i}}k(t_{j},s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s.

For uniform grids and convolution kernels, the weights depend only on the lag

j-i
, so that the sums can be accelerated by FFT; see the implementation for
details.

For the convergence experiments we consider the scalar fractional kernel
k_{\beta}(t,s)=\frac{(t-s)^{\beta-1}}{\Gamma(\beta)}
,
\beta\in(0,1)
.
In view of
3.6
, and the higher-order scheme in

\mathbf{v}^{\mathrm{ref}}(x^{(i)})

A.2.
Validation of the finite-state-space algorithm

Our first sanity check of the implementation was to compare the Volterra signature of a finite-state-space kernel
K^{\Lambda}_{A,b}
, see
Section

\Lambda=0

Comparing
\pi_{\leq N}\mathrm{VSig}(K^{\Lambda}_{A,b};x)
with
\pi_{\leq N}\mathrm{Sig}(y)
resulted in equality up to machine precision in all tested examples, with
N=10
and various shapes of
A
and
b
.

Secondly, we verified our implementation of
Algorithms

6
and
7
against a dyadically refined explicit Euler scheme for the tensor-algebra-valued ODE satisfied by the state lift
\mathbf{Z}
of the Volterra signature, see
[
13

. Each interval
[t_{j},t_{j+1}]
is split into
2^{p}
equal subintervals, with both
\Delta t_{j}
and
\Delta x_{j}^{(i)}
divided by
2^{p}
. Initializing
\widehat{Z}_{0,0}^{\ell,i}=0\in\mathfrak{R}_{N}
, we iterate over the refined grid using

\widehat{Z}_{j,k+1}^{\ell,i}=\widehat{Z}_{j,k}^{\ell,i}-\frac{t_{j+1}-t_{j}}{2^{p}}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\Lambda_{\ell r}\widehat{Z}_{j,k}^{r,i}+\left(1+\sum_{r=1}^{R}\widehat{Z}_{j,k}^{r,i}\right)\otimes_{N}\left(\sum_{\alpha=1}^{q}b_{\alpha,\ell}A_{\alpha}\frac{\Delta x_{j}^{(i)}}{2^{p}}\right),

for
i=1,\dots,M
,
j=0,\ldots,J-1
,
k=0,\ldots,2^{p}-1
, and
\ell=1,\ldots,R
, with
\widehat{Z}^{\ell,i}_{j+1,0}=\widehat{Z}^{\ell,i}_{j,2^{p}}
. The terminal Volterra signature is read out as

\widehat{V}_{i,p}=1+\sum_{\ell=1}^{R}\widehat{Z}_{J,0}^{\ell,i}.

We compare this with the output of
Algorithms

6
and
7
, which is exact up to quadrature errors in the Laplace approximation of the coefficients, and record the level-wise factorially rescaled Euler error

\delta V_{n,p}=\max_{i=1,\dots,M}\left\|n!\,\pi_{n}\left(\widehat{V}_{i,p}-\mathrm{VSig}(K^{\Lambda}_{A,b};x^{(i)})_{0,1}^{1}\right)\right\|_{\infty},\qquad n=1,\ldots,N.

We tested this for several choices of
q
and
R
and observed the expected first-order convergence of the Euler scheme under dyadic refinement towards the exact benchmark, uniformly across tensor levels.
In
Figure

A.3.
Validation of the signature kernel algorithm

We finally validate the finite-difference scheme from
Algorithm

11
for the computation of the Volterra signature kernel associated with finite-state-space kernels.
We first compare its accuracy against a truncated inner-product reference obtained from the finite-state-space Volterra signature recursion of
Algorithms

6
and
7
. More precisely, for a truncation level
N
we set

\kappa^{\mathrm{ref},N}(x,w):=\left\langle\pi_{\leq N}\mathrm{VSig}(x;K^{\Lambda}_{A,b})_{0,1}^{1},\pi_{\leq N}\mathrm{VSig}(w;K^{\Lambda}_{A,b})_{0,1}^{1}\right\rangle.

In the experiment below we take
N=10
.

We compare the predictor–corrector scheme of
Algorithm

11
with two simpler finite-difference schemes. The first is a direct “
naive
” discretization of (
5.2
)–(
5.4
). With the notation of
Section

5
, it uses

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Psi}_{i+1,j+1}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{\Psi}_{i,j+1}-\Delta s_{i}\Lambda\mathbf{\Psi}_{i,j+1}+\gamma_{i,j}\eta_{i,j+1},

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Phi}_{i+1,j+1}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{\Phi}_{i+1,j}-\Delta t_{j}\mathbf{\Phi}_{i+1,j}\Lambda^{\top}+\gamma_{i,j}\eta_{i+1,j},

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}_{i+1,j+1}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{K}_{i+1,j}+\mathbf{K}_{i,j+1}-\mathbf{K}_{i,j}+\frac{\Delta s_{i}\Delta t_{j}}{2}\bigl(\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{K}_{i+1,j})+\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{K}_{i,j+1})\bigr)

\displaystyle\qquad-\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{i,j}\bigl(\eta_{i+1,j}+\eta_{i,j+1}\bigr)+\bigl(\mathbf{\Psi}_{i+1,j+1}-\mathbf{\Psi}_{i,j+1}\bigr)+\bigl(\mathbf{\Phi}_{i+1,j+1}-\mathbf{\Phi}_{i+1,j}\bigr),

where
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{M})=\Lambda\mathbf{M}\Lambda^{\top}
and
\eta_{i,j}=1+\mathbf{1}^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{i,j}\mathbf{1}
.
The second alternative scheme uses
exponential integration
for the auxiliary equations, but keeps the same first-order cell stencil for the main equation. Namely,

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Psi}_{i+1,j+1}

\displaystyle=E_{i}^{s}\mathbf{\Psi}_{i,j+1}+P_{i}^{s}\gamma_{i,j}\eta_{i,j+1},

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Phi}_{i+1,j+1}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{\Phi}_{i+1,j}E_{j}^{t}+\gamma_{i,j}\eta_{i+1,j}P_{j}^{t},

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}_{i+1,j+1}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{K}_{i+1,j}+\mathbf{K}_{i,j+1}-\mathbf{K}_{i,j}+\frac{\Delta s_{i}\Delta t_{j}}{2}\bigl(\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{K}_{i+1,j})+\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{K}_{i,j+1})\bigr)

\displaystyle\qquad-\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{i,j}\bigl(\eta_{i+1,j}+\eta_{i,j+1}\bigr)+\bigl(\mathbf{\Psi}_{i+1,j+1}-\mathbf{\Psi}_{i,j+1}\bigr)+\bigl(\mathbf{\Phi}_{i+1,j+1}-\mathbf{\Phi}_{i+1,j}\bigr),

where
(E_{i}^{s},E_{j}^{t},P_{i}^{s},P_{j}^{t})
are defined as in

Algorithm

11
.

For dyadic refinement level
\lambda
, we denote the resulting terminal kernel value by
\kappa^{\mathrm{scheme}}_{\lambda}(x,w)
and record the error

\delta\kappa_{\lambda}:=\max_{i=1,\dots,M}\left|\kappa^{\mathrm{scheme}}_{\lambda}(x^{(i)},w^{(i)})-\kappa^{\mathrm{ref},N}(x^{(i)},w^{(i)})\right|.
