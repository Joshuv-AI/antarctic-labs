Intrinsic capacity subtypes in healthy Chinese older adults: links to possible sarcopenia and fall risk
Authors: Boyang Yu, Zechen Zhou, Guanzhou Chen, Chuanyu Zhou, Peiying Lv, Jiangyu Guo, Fei He, Hexin Li, Beidong Chen, Lina Ma, Qiaomei Lin, Qi Zhou, Jing Pang, Ji Shen, Hong Shi, Ruiyue Yang, Yan Wang, Liang Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Hidden IC impairment was common even in healthy older adults, challenging assumptions of preserved reserve.•Clustering revealed distinct IC subtypes with different risks for falls and possible sarcopenia (AWGS 2025).•Multi-domain IC impairment identified a high-risk subgroup linked to both outcomes, enabling targeted escalation.•Sensory decline emerged as an early potential upstream correlate, offering a practical entry point for prevention pathways.

Introduction

Population ageing is accelerating worldwide, with particularly pronounced trends in China [1]. These demographic shifts present substantial challenges for health care financing, long-term care infrastructure, and social security systems. In this context, healthy ageing and the preservation of functional ability have emerged as central public health priorities. Community-dwelling healthy older adults represent a critical population for upstream prevention strategies [2]. This group, typically aged 60–90 years, lives independently with preserved basic activities of daily living and without severe organ failure. They constitute a key functional reserve for healthy ageing and an essential target for interventions to maintain functional capacity and prevent disability [3].

To address the need for function-centered care, the World Health Organization (WHO) developed the Integrated Care for Older People (ICOPE) screening tool and care pathways [4,5]. This framework enables standardized, low-cost screening across five intrinsic capacity (IC) domains—locomotion, vitality, cognition, psychological well-being, and sensory function—and provides evidence-based guidance for integrated, person-centered care [6,7]. IC represents multidimensional functional reserve in older adults, and its decline is closely linked to functional vulnerability, including possible sarcopenia and fall risk. Possible sarcopenia reflects early muscle health decline and is closely related to locomotion and vitality, while falls represent a major source of disability, injury-related mortality, and disease burden among older Chinese adults [[8], [9], [10], [11], [12]].

Previous studies have linked lower IC to ageing-related adverse outcomes. Higher overall IC scores and better locomotion capacity have been associated with lower fall risk among adults aged ≥80 years [13], while greater IC impairment has been related to higher sarcopenia risk in hospitalized older patients, particularly through the vitality domain [14]. A systematic review further reported associations between IC decline and sarcopenia, especially involving vitality, cognition, and locomotion [15]. However, existing evidence remains limited in two aspects. First, many studies were conducted in hospital-based or functionally vulnerable populations, leaving insufficient evidence among healthy community-dwelling older adults. Second, most studies examined IC as an overall score or as separate domains, despite longitudinal evidence suggesting dynamic transitions and clustering across IC domains [[16], [17], [18]]. Whether WHO ICOPE-based IC subtypes are differentially associated with possible sarcopenia and fall risk in healthy community-dwelling older adults remains unclear. Clarifying these associations may support more precise early risk stratification and community-based prevention [3,19]. In this study, we evaluated the prevalence of impairments across WHO-ICOPE domains among 1,607 community-dwelling healthy older adults, and examined their associations with possible sarcopenia (AWGS 2025) and fall risk (World guidelines). Our findings will clarify how early functional declines relate to possible sarcopenia and elevated fall risk, and will inform the implementation of screening tools in community prevention settings.

Materials and methods

Participants

This cross-sectional study consecutively enrolled adults aged ≥60 years from “the Demonstration Bases for Active Health of Older Adults”. The target population was generally consistent with the concept of “healthy Chinese older adults” described in the Standard for Healthy Chinese Older Adults (2022) [2], which emphasizes independence or basic independence in daily living, relatively preserved physical and mental function, and willingness to participate in health assessment and management. These bases are community-based health management sites established under a national research program to integrate early screening, comprehensive assessment, geriatric health management, and long-term follow-up for older residents. The screening program includes assessments of intrinsic capacity, cognition, possible sarcopenia, and fall risk, as well as emerging digital health approaches.

The present analysis used cross-sectional data collected from two demonstration bases in Beijing and Deyang between March 2024 and June 2025. Eligible participants were community-dwelling adults aged ≥60 years who had preserved or basically preserved independent living ability, sufficient cognitive and communication ability to complete the assessments, and willingness to participate in community-based health assessment and management. Participants were excluded if they had falls or ophthalmic surgery within the past 3 months, severe organic or mental disorders, sensory impairments limiting assessment, or reliance on walking aids. After eligibility screening and written informed consent, trained investigators conducted standardized questionnaires, intrinsic capacity screening, and physical function assessments on site according to unified procedures. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Hospital/National Center for Gerontology (2021BJYYEC-114-02, 2024BJYYEC-KY128-02).

IC Assessment: WHO ICOPE

IC was assessed using the WHO ICOPE screening tool, which identifies older adults with priority conditions associated with IC decline across five domains: cognition, locomotion, vitality, sensory, and psychological capacity [6,20]. Trained assessors administered a standardized 10-item questionnaire supplemented by brief performance-based tests. Each domain was dichotomously classified as either “impaired” (coded as 1) or “not impaired” (coded as 0). Two summary indicators were subsequently derived: (1) binary impairment status for each individual IC domain and (2) the total number of impaired IC domains (ranging from 0 to 5), which was treated as a continuous variable in statistical analyses. These IC measures were employed in descriptive analyses, multivariable logistic regression models, and K-means clustering to identify distinct IC impairment profiles.

Fall risk

Fall risk was assessed according to the World Guidelines for Falls Prevention and Management in Older Adults [21,22]. Following the guideline algorithm, nine assessment items were integrated to stratify participants into low, mid, or high fall risk categories. "Increased fall risk" was operationally defined as mid or high risk versus low risk [21,22]. The structured questionnaire captured seven self-reported items: history of falls in the past year, unsteadiness when standing or walking, fear of falling, recurrent falls (≥2 within 12 months), injurious falls necessitating medical or surgical intervention, prolonged inability to rise independently (≥1 h after falling), and suspected transient loss of consciousness. Two performance-based measures complemented the self-report data: slow gait speed and impaired mobility.

Possible sarcopenia: AWGS 2025

According to the AWGS 2025 consensus [8], possible sarcopenia was defined as low muscle strength assessed by handgrip strength, using age- and sex-specific cut-off values. Handgrip strength was measured with a handheld dynamometer, and the maximum value from either hand was recorded in kilograms. For adults aged 50–64 and ≥65 years, low handgrip strength was defined as <34 and <28 kg in men, and <20 and <18 kg in women, respectively.

Other covariates

Age was verified using ID card records. Clinical parameters were assessed on site by trained investigators according to standardized procedures. Systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, and heart rate were measured in a seated position after a brief rest using an automated electronic blood pressure monitor. Height and weight were measured with participants wearing light clothing and no shoes, and body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared. Comorbidity was assessed using the 11 chronic conditions included in the FRAIL scale, including hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and heart diseases, based on self-reported physician diagnosis [23]. Additionally, the 6-m usual-pace walk test and five-time chair stand test were performed to assess sarcopenia-related physical performance outcomes [8].

Statistical analysis

Data analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 25 and R 4.0.2. Cross-sectional indicator distributions were summarized as mean ± standard deviation (continuous variables) or n (%) (categorical variables), with group differences analyzed via t-tests (continuous variables) and chi-square tests (categorical variables).

To identify distinct IC impairment patterns, a two-step unsupervised K-means clustering was applied: consensus clustering first estimated the optimal cluster number, with five binary IC domains (cognition, locomotion, vitality, sensory function, psychological wellbeing) as input variables. The elbow method was used and further validated by silhouette analysis [24,25]. The final number of clusters was determined by jointly considering clustering validity indices, average silhouette coefficient, Calinski–Harabasz index, and Davies–Bouldin index, as well as cluster size distribution and clinical interpretability; based on this overall assessment, the four-cluster solution was selected. Radar charts visualized the four subtypes’ characteristics across ICOPE domains.

Multivariate logistic regression models examined two primary outcomes: increased fall risk (mid + high) and possible sarcopenia. Independent variables comprised individual IC domains, impairments, the total number of impaired capacities, and cluster-based subtypes. Both crude and adjusted models were constructed, with the latter controlling for age (continuous), gender, BMI, SBP, DBP, and comorbidity status. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) were calculated for all associations. Two supplementary analyses were performed: (1) comparison of impairment rates for individual Fall Risk Questionnaire items across ICOPE-IC domain impairment clusters, and (2) examination of the distribution of K-means-derived ICOPE-IC clusters across key subgroups, including gender, age categories (<75 vs. ≥75 years), demonstration site, fall risk level, and possible sarcopenia status (yes vs. no).

Supervised extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost) models were developed to predict (1) mid/high fall risk and (2) possible sarcopenia. For each outcome, two distinct models were constructed: one including four IC clusters (Clusters 1–4) plus age and sex, and one including five binary ICOPE domain indicators plus age and sex. All models utilized complete cases with binary predictors coded as 0/1. Models were implemented in R using the xgboost package with logistic loss (objective = "binary: logistic") and fixed hyperparameters (η = 0.05, max_depth = 3, subsample = 0.80, colsamplebytree = 0.80, min_child_weight = 1). Optimal tree numbers were determined through stratified 5-fold cross-validation with early stopping (maximum 300 rounds; stopping criterion of 20 consecutive rounds without AUC improvement). Final models were refit on the complete dataset using the optimal tree counts identified during cross-validation. Discriminative performance was assessed using area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI), while sensitivity and specificity were calculated at optimal threshold determined by Youden’s index. For comparative evaluation, multivariable logistic regression models with identical predictors sets were fitted and evaluated using the same performance metrics.

The Structural equation modelling (SEM) was specified a priori based on the WHO ICOPE framework and the functional cascade hypothesis in ageing. Age and gender were included as upstream demographic variables. IC domain impairments were modeled as interrelated functional reserve deficits. Specifically, we hypothesized pathways from sensory and cognitive impairment to psychological and locomotor impairment, from psychological impairment to vitality and locomotor impairment, and from vitality impairment to locomotor impairment. Possible sarcopenia was modeled as an early muscle health outcome, and fall risk was modeled as a downstream physical vulnerability outcome. The model was estimated using robust weighted least squares (WLSMV) via the lavaan package in R. Missing data were handled through pairwise deletion, which retains all available data for each parameter estimate. We reported standardized path coefficients (β) and corresponding p-values for all pathways. Global model fit was evaluated using multiple indices: χ2/df ratio, comparative fit index (CFI), Tucker–Lewis index (TLI), root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), and standardized root mean square residual (SRMR). Acceptable model fit was defined as CFI/TLI ≥ 0.95, RMSEA ≤ 0.06, and SRMR ≤ 0.08. Paths with p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

BMI and SBP were comparable between genders, whereas DBP and heart rate were significantly higher in males (p < 0.001). Over 92% of participants had ≥1 impaired IC domain, with a higher mean in females (1.89 vs. 1.72). Sensory impairment was most prevalent (80.9%), followed by cognition (47.3%), and locomotion the least (5.8%). Females showed higher vitality and psychology impairment. Each additional impaired ICOPE IC domain was associated with 37% and 41% higher risks of elevated fall risk and possible sarcopenia, respectively, independent of major confounders (Table S1). In fully adjusted models, locomotion impairment most strongly predicted elevated fall risk (OR = 4.78, 95%CI:2.21–7.62), followed by cognition (OR = 1.48) and psychology (OR = 1.42). For possible sarcopenia, cognition showed the strongest association (OR = 3.40), followed by psychology (OR = 1.20).

Four distinct IC clusters were identified based on impairment patterns and the elbow method (K = 4; Fig. 1A–B, S1) with the final solution selected by jointly considering clustering validity indices, cluster size distribution, and clinical interpretability (Table S2). Cluster 1: isolated cognitive impairment; Cluster 2: predominant sensory impairment; Cluster 3: combined sensory‑psychological‑vitality impairment; Cluster 4: multi‑domain impairment including locomotion, sensory, vitality, and cognition (Fig. 1C). All clusters displayed some cognitive impairment, accompanied by mild sensory and vitality deficits. Cluster 2 was most common (49.7%), followed by Cluster 3 (27.1%), Cluster 1 (17.4%), and Cluster 4 (5.8%). Cluster 4 participants were older and more often female (Table S3), with the highest number of impaired domains. Elevated fall risk rose sharply in Cluster 3 (12.8%) and Cluster 4 (46.2%). Muscle strength and physical performance were similar in Clusters 1–3 but markedly poorer in Cluster 4. IC cluster distributions differed significantly by gender, age, fall risk, and possible sarcopenia (Figure S2). Cluster 3 was less common in males, while Cluster 4 was greatly enriched in participants aged ≥75 years, those with elevated fall risk, and those with possible sarcopenia (Figure S3). XGBoost ROC analysis showed that adding IC indicators improved risk prediction, with AUC rising from ∼0.6 to ∼0.7 (Figure S4).

In SEM, older age was associated with cognitive impairment (β = 0.14) and locomotion impairment (β = 0.19), which were further associated with possible sarcopenia (β = 0.13 and 0.27) and falls (β = 0.05 and 0.14; all P < 0.001). In addition, male sex was associated with lower psychological impairment (β=-0.08, P < 0.001), while sensory impairment was associated with psychological impairment (β = 0.16), and psychological impairment with vitality impairment (β = 0.59; both P < 0.001). In contrast, the paths from cognition to psychological impairment and from sensory, cognitive, psychological, or vitality impairment to locomotion were not statistically significant. Vitality, sensory, and psychological impairment were not directly associated with fall risk or possible sarcopenia. Discussion

This study applied the WHO ICOPE framework to characterize multidimensional IC impairment in healthy community-dwelling older adults, integrating fall risk screening with the updated AWGS 2025 definition of possible sarcopenia. IC impairment was common even in this relatively healthy population, with sensory impairment being the most frequent domain. A greater IC impairment burden was associated with higher risks of falls and possible sarcopenia, and cognitive and psychological factors also appeared to contribute beyond physical function alone. K-means clustering identified four IC subtypes: isolated cognitive impairment (17.4%), predominant sensory impairment (49.7%), sensory-psychological-vitality impairment (27.1%), and multidomain impairment (5.8%). Clusters 1–3 may represent earlier warning profiles requiring proactive monitoring, whereas Cluster 4 identified a smaller but clinically important high-risk subgroup with the greatest burden of fall risk and possible sarcopenia. Consistent with evidence that conventional IC studies often rely on summed scores rather than impairment patterns [26,27], our findings suggest that IC subtype classification may better capture co-occurring impairments in real-world older adults. Together with the SEM results, these findings further indicate plausible pathways linking demographic factors, interrelated IC domains, possible sarcopenia, and falls, particularly along the sensory–cognitive–locomotor axis. Therefore, community screening should move beyond simply counting impaired domains and incorporate cluster-informed IC profiles to guide earlier, stratified prevention and integrated management.

The gender distribution of this study was imbalanced, with women accounting for 65.7% of the sample. This may reflect greater willingness among older women to participate in community-based health activities and may have influenced the observed IC impairment patterns. Sex-stratified analyses showed that the overall conclusion remained consistent, with Cluster 4 associated with higher risks of both outcomes. However, the strength of association differed by sex: Cluster 4 was more strongly associated with fall risk in men, whereas its association with possible sarcopenia was more evident in women. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted with consideration of the gender composition of the study population and validated in cohorts with more balanced sex distributions.

IC evaluations in China focus mostly on tertiary hospital in/outpatients, with few standardized assessments or cohort studies in community-dwelling healthy older adults [26,[28], [29], [30]], leading to insufficient understanding of their IC status and links to falls/sarcopenia [31]. Notably, most research focuses on the total number of IC impairments [26,32], neglecting the unique effects of individual domains and synergistic impacts of concurrent impairments—this limitation impedes the formulation of proactive geriatric health management and precise intervention strategies prior to the onset of typical clinical symptoms. Our study demonstrates that sensory function is potential upstream correlate and the core of the negative feedback loop in the decline of IC among healthy older adults. We found that 80.9% of the participants had sensory impairment, with visual/hearing loss emerging as the earliest marker of aging—preceding physical or cognitive frailty [33]. In terms of the driving mechanism, uncorrected sensory deficits accelerate cognitive deterioration through a negative feedback loop: reduced sensory input increased cognitive load limited social participation—a key pathway underlying the trajectory of IC decline [34,35]. Furthermore, the phenomenon of "sensory-cognitive coupling" (e.g., widespread sensory impairment accompanied by cognitive deficits in Cluster 2) suggests that the underutilization of compensatory measures, such as hearing aids or eyeglasses among the Chinese older adults population lays the pathological foundation for subsequent cognitive decline [36]. This finding underscores the value and significance of enhancing national health literacy to address such gaps. Another major strength of this study is the preliminary identification of four clustered stages of IC—specifically a progressive continuum that evolves from partial impairment to motoric cognitive risk syndrome (MCR) [37,38], which are not separate subtypes but rather staged paths of IC decline. Cluster 2 (sensory-cognitive impairment) corresponds to the early stage, while Cluster 3 (extending to psychological and vitality domains) exhibits a "withdrawal phenotype" wherein sensory-cognitive deficits lead to reduced interest in daily activities and social withdrawal, marking the shift from partial impairment to overall physiological exhaustion [39]. The core breakthrough of our study lies in identifying Cluster 4 (multidomain impairment) as the "critical threshold for exhaustion of physiological reserve": its combined motor and cognitive impairments closely align with MCR, and the strong link between sarcopenia and cognitive decline supports the "brain-muscle crosstalk hypothesis." Importantly, Clusters 1–3 can be classified as "pre-MCR stages," highlighting the sequential progression of IC decline and providing a clear framework for understanding the transition toward MCR [40].

Future research directions include: longitudinal studies to explore IC impairment progression trajectories and risk factors for multi-domain decline, to inform phased interventions; targeted programmes for distinct IC clusters (e.g., sensory training + psychological counseling for female Cluster 3, multidimensional interventions for Cluster 4); and investigating IC tool feasibility in community screening, integrating clustering patterns into health records for stratified geriatric syndrome management. Clinically, providers should emphasize IC assessment in community older adults, monitoring female vitality/psychological impairment and male locomotor/muscle strength deficits. Community health centers should implement cluster-based management, e.g., regular sensory screening/rehabilitation for the prevalent Cluster 2 to reduce geriatric syndrome incidence.

One strength of this study is its community-based, function-centered design, which enabled assessment of IC in healthy older adults and characterization of IC impairment patterns associated with possible sarcopenia and fall risk in a population underrepresented in geriatric syndrome research. Second, it aligned closely with guidelines and consensus: instead of retrospective post-hoc analysis, the study integrated the IC screening tool of the ICOPE framework, fall risk screening tools, and the AWGS 2025 Consensus into its research design, complemented by pre-, intra-, and post-study quality control. Third, it employed SEM and XGBoost methods to simultaneously investigate the associations between IC impairment and two core geriatric syndromes (fall risk and possible sarcopenia), adjusting for multiple confounding factors such as age, gender, and comorbidities. This enhanced the reliability of research conclusions and the rigor of the association analyses.

This study had several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design limits causal interpretation; SEM pathways were therefore interpreted as theory-informed associations, and their directionality requires longitudinal validation. Second, IC was assessed using the ICOPE STEP1 rapid screening tool. Further evaluations, including ICOPE STEP2 assessments, objective measurements such as gait analysis, electronic health technologies, and biomarkers, are warranted—these have been incorporated into subsequent study designs. Third, participants were recruited from community demonstration bases and required to complete on-site assessments, which may have selected relatively healthy and health-engaged older adults. Geographic and urban–rural heterogeneity was not fully captured, potentially limiting generalizability; larger multicenter studies are warranted.

Conclusion

IC impairment was common in healthy community-dwelling older adults, with sensory impairment as a potential early marker of IC decline. Four IC subtypes were identified, among which Cluster 4, characterized by multidomain impairment, represented a high-risk subtype most strongly associated with fall risk and possible sarcopenia. Community prevention should prioritize ICOPE-based sensory screening, timely vision and hearing management, and targeted muscle strength, gait, and fall-risk assessment for high-risk IC subtypes.
