Siblings and twins in finite p p -groups and a group identification for the groups of order 2 9 2^{9}
Bettina Eick, Henrik Schanze

Abstract
We investigate which invariants of groups are powerful in distinguishing non-isomorphic p p -groups. We introduce the notions of siblings and twins for p p -groups that are difficult to distinguish; we describe the siblings and twins among the groups of small prime-power order. We then use these ideas to device an effective group identification algorithm for the 10 494 213 10\,494\,213 groups of order 2 9 2^{9} .



1
Introduction

Which invariants distinguish groups? This is a central question in group
theory that has been investigated in many different directions. Often
effective
to determine are invariants such as the group order, the abelian invariants
or the number of conjugacy classes. Such invariants tend to be rather weak
in the sense that large clusters of non-isomorphic groups can have identical
invariants. Significantly more powerful are invariants based on the
subgroup lattice, the character table or group rings. Here clusters of
non-isomorphic groups with identical invariants are much harder to find,
but the invariants are often time-consuming to evaluate.

The SmallGroups library
[
2
]
is a database of groups of small
order initiated by Besche, Eick & O’Brien
[
3
]
. Among others,
it contains the groups of order at most
2000
except
1024=2^{10}
.
For all orders at most
2000
except those divisible by
2^{9}
it also
provides a highly effective function to determine the group-id of a group

G
: this group-id is a pair
(o,n)
such that
G\cong SmallGroup(o,n)
.
The algorithm facilitating the group-id calculation is mainly based on
evaluating invariants, see
[
1
]
, since this often more effective
than a full isomorphism test of groups.

A motivation for the study in this paper was to extend the group-id function
so that it also covers the groups of order
2^{9}
. As a first attempt, we
studied the group-id function for the groups of order
2^{8}
. There are

56\,092
groups of order
2^{8}
and
10\,494\,213
groups of order
2^{9}
.
As to be expected, the ideas used for the group-id for the groups of order

2^{8}
are useful, but do not suffice to identify all groups of order
2^{9}
.

As a next step, we explored proper subgroups and proper factor groups and
ideas related to character theory and power maps. We introduce the notions
of
siblings
and
twins
in Section
2
for groups which
remain indistinguishable with these ideas. These groups are interesting
in their own right, as they show how closely related non-isomorphic

p
-groups can be. We observe that there are
56
twin-pairs among the

10\,494\,213
groups of order
2^{9}
, see Section
3
.

As the new ideas related to siblings and twins identify all but the small
number of
56
pairs of groups, it is now possible to use available
isomorphism testing techniques to obtain an id-group function for the
groups of order
2^{9}
. An implementation of this function is available
in the IdPGroup package
[
19
]
.

This paper is structured as follows. Section
2
introduces the
notions of siblings and twins, exhibits a first investigation of these
and algorithms to evaluate them. Section
3
evaluates the
siblings and twins of order dividing
2^{9}
; the list of
56
twin-pairs
of order
2^{9}
is exhibited in the Appendix
6
. Section
4

introduces a group-id algorithm for groups of order
2^{9}
and Section

5
lists a few open problems and questions related to twins
and siblings.

Siblings and twins

In this section we introduce our notions of siblings and twins. We evaluate
the twins and siblings among the
p
-groups for small order. More details
on groups of order dividing
2^{9}
are given in Section
3
.

Siblings: subgroups and factor groups

For a group
G
, let
\mathcal{S}(G)=\{U\leq G\mid U\neq G\}
denote the
set of all proper subgroups of
G
and let
\mathcal{F}(G)=\{G/N\mid N\unlhd G,N\neq\{1\}\}
the set of all proper quotients of
G
.
Two groups
G
and
H
are
subgroup equivalent
if there exists a
bijection
\varphi:\mathcal{S}(G)\rightarrow\mathcal{S}(H)
such that
U\cong\varphi(U)

for each
U\in\mathcal{S}(G)
. They are
factor equivalent
, if there exists
a bijection
\rho:\mathcal{F}(G)\rightarrow\mathcal{F}(H)
so that
Q\cong\rho(Q)
for each

Q\in\mathcal{F}(G)
. The two groups
G
and
H
are called
siblings
if
they are subgroup- and factor equivalent and additionally the following
holds.

(a)

\varphi
preserves conjugacy of subgroups; that is

U,V\in\mathcal{S}(G)
are conjugate in
G
if and only if
\varphi(U),\varphi(V)\in\mathcal{S}(H)
are conjugate in
H
.

(b)

\varphi
maps the Frattini subgroup, the proper terms of the lower and the
upper central series to their correspondent in
H
.

For a
p
-group
G
we define its
Subgroup Fingerprint

SF(G)
as
follows. First, determine the conjugacy classes of proper subgroups of
G
,
see for example Neubüser
[
17
]
. For each conjugacy class
U^{G}

determine a canonical identifier for
U
and the length of the class. If

U
has small order, then a canonical identifier can be its group-id in
the SmallGroups Library. Otherwise, the standard presentation of
U
as
determined by O’Brien
[
18
]
is a canonical identifier.

Similarly, we define the
Factor Fingerprint

FF(G)
. We first
compute all non-trivial normal subgroups of
G
and then, for each normal
subgroup
N
, use a canonical identifier for the quotient
G/N
.
The
Sibling Fingerprint

SS(G)
is then the combination of
SF(G)
,

FF(G)
and a canonical identifier for
\Phi(G)
and the proper terms of
upper and lower central series. We summarize a description of these
calculations.

Algorithm Subgroup Fingerprint (SF):
Input a group
G

(1)

Compute the conjugacy classes of proper subgroups of
G
.

(2)

For each conjugacy class
U^{G}
, determine the pair
(|U^{G}|,\mathrm{ID}(U))
.

(3)

Sort the list of resulting pairs and return it.

Algorithm Factor Fingerprint (FF):
Input a group
G

(1)

Compute all non-trivial normal subgroups of
G
.

(2)

For each normal subgroup
N
, determine
\mathrm{ID}(G/N)
.

(3)

Sort the resulting list of group-ids and return it.

Algorithm Sibling Fingerprint (SS):
Input a group
G

(1)

Determine
SF(G)
and
FF(G)
.

(2)

Compute canonical identifiers for
\Phi(G)
, and for upper and lower
central series.

(3)

Return the combined results of (1)+(2).

Note that two groups
G
and
H
are siblings if and only if

SS(G)=SS(H)
holds.

Twins: character tables and Brauer pairs

Two finite groups
G
and
H
with conjugacy classes
C(G)
and
C(H)
,
respectively, have equivalent character tables, if there exists a
bijection
\tau:C(G)\rightarrow C(H)
which induces a bijection on the
complex characters
\sigma:Irr(H)\rightarrow Irr(G):\chi\mapsto\chi\circ\tau
.
Pairs of groups with equivalent character tables are easy to find; an
example is given by
D_{8}
and
Q_{8}
.

Two groups
G
and
H
form a
Brauer pair
if their character tables
including power maps are equivalent; that is, if they have equivalent
character tables and additionally
\tau\circ\pi_{n}^{G}=\pi_{n}^{H}\circ\tau

holds for each
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, where
\pi_{n}^{G}:C(G)\rightarrow C(G)
is the
n
th
power map induced by
g\mapsto g^{n}
. Brauer
[
4
]
initiated the
search for Brauer pairs. The first Brauer pairs were found by Dade

[
5
]
and have order
p^{7}
.

An algorithm to check if two groups
G
and
H
form a Brauer pair is
available in GAP
[
12
]
. This determines the character tables of

G
and
H
and
then searches for a permutation on the conjugacy classes of
G
that
induces the desired equivalence of complex characters and power maps.

Isoclinism

Isoclinism is an equivalence relation of finite groups, weaker than
isomorphism. It was introduced by Hall
[
14
]
as a method to assist
the classification of
p
-groups. Two groups
G
and
H
are isoclinic if
there is a pair of isomorphisms
\alpha:G/Z(G)\rightarrow H/Z(H)
and

\beta:[G,G]\rightarrow[H,H]
that is compatible with the commutator
map
\gamma_{G}:G/Z(G)\times G/Z(G)\rightarrow[G,G]:(gZ(G),hZ(G))\rightarrow[g,h]
;
more precisely,
\gamma_{H}(\alpha(x),\alpha(y))=\beta(\gamma_{G}(x,y))

holds for all
x,y\in G/Z(G)
.

Checking isoclinism of two groups
G
and
H
involves in the worst case
to consider
all
pairs of isomorphisms
\alpha:G/Z(G)\rightarrow H/Z(H)
and

\beta:[G,G]\rightarrow[H,H]
. It thus can be very slow even on examples of
moderate size. Implementations of an isoclinism test are available in GAP
packages, for example in the XMod package
[
22
]
.

Most of the twin-pairs of order
2^{9}
are isoclinic, but not all of them,
as the Appendix
6
shows.

Group rings and the modular isomorphism problem

The modular isomorphism problem (MIP) asks if there exist two non-isomorphic

p
-groups
G
and
R
with isomorphic group rings
\mathbb{F}G\cong\mathbb{F}H
, where

\mathbb{F}
is a field of characteristic
p
. First examples of such groups were
found recently by Garcia-Lucas, Margolis & del Rio
[
13
]
. Among
these there is one pair of groups of order
2^{9}
and this is the smallest
order example.

An algorithm to check if
\mathbb{F}G\cong\mathbb{F}H
for two
p
-groups
G
and
H

and
\mathbb{F}
the field with
p
elements was developed by Eick
[
6
]

and implemented in
[
7
]
. This algorithm determined that there
are no examples of order dividing
2^{8}
or
3^{6}
.

Not all of the groups of order
2^{9}
have been checked for MIP-examples,
see Moede & Margolis
[
16
]
. We investigates whether one
of the twin-pairs determined here are MIP-examples, but found none, see

(Outer) automorphism groups

Two groups
G
and
H
are
(outer) automorphism equivalent
if

Aut(G)\cong Aut(H)
or
Out(G)\cong Out(H)
, respectively.
Automorphism equivalent groups are not difficult to find: An example
are the groups
D_{8}
and
C_{4}\times C_{2}
. Nonetheless, (outer)
automorphism equivalence is of interest in distinguishing groups.

An algorithm to compute the automorphism group of a finite
p
-group
was developed by Eick, Leedham-Green & O’Brien
[
8
]
and
implemented in the AutPGroup Package
[
10
]
. Given a

p
-group
G
, the method returns a list of generators for
Aut(G)
and

|Aut(G)|
. If
Aut(G)
is solvable, then the returned list of generators
is a polycyclic generating set. This allows to read off pc-presentations
for
Aut(G)
and
Out(G)
which, in turn, facilitates effective isomorphism
tests for these groups.

We include a report on the outer automorphism equivalence of the twin-pairs
of order
2^{9}
in the Appendix
6
.

4.1
Group identification for the groups of order
2^{8}

The id-function for groups of order
2^{8}
is organised as a decision
tree. Each vertex
v
in the tree corresponds to a set
s(v)
of
groups of order
2^{8}
. If
v
is the root of the tree, then
s(v)

is the set of all groups of order
2^{8}
(up to isomorphism). If
v

is a leaf in the tree,
then
|s(v)|=1
. Hence the decision tree for the groups of order

2^{8}
has
56\,092
leaves. If a vertex
v
has
l
immediate descendants

v_{1},\ldots,v_{l}
, then
s(v)
is the disjoint union of the sets

s(v_{1}),\ldots,s(v_{l})
.

Each non-leaf vertex
v
in the tree has an associated function
f(v)
.
This function can be applied to the groups in
s(v)
and evaluates an
invariant. If
u
is an immediate descendant of
v
in the tree, then

u
has an associated outcome
r(u)
and it holds that

s(u)=\{G\in s(v)\mid f(v)(G)=r(u)\}.

If a group
G
of order
2^{8}
is given, then the tree is used to
decide
\mathrm{ID}(G)
by moving
G
downwards through the tree. The group
G

starts at the root
v
. Applying
f(v)(G)
moves
G
to one of the
immediate descendant
u
of
v
. This process is iterated until
G

arrives at a leaf. The leaf determines
\mathrm{ID}(G)
.

It remains to describe the function
f(v)
in more detail. If
v
has
distance at most
3
to the root, then
f(v)
depends on this distance
only. The function
f(v)
computes:

\bullet

Dist
0
:
The abelian invariants of the groups in the derived series of
G
.

\bullet

Dist
1
:
The sizes of the quotients of the
p
-central series of
G
.

\bullet

Dist
2
:
The orders
o(g)
of the elements
g\in G
.

\bullet

Dist
3
:
For each conjugacy class
g^{G}
of
G
, the pair
(|g^{G}|,o(g))
.

Based on these invariants,
8349
groups of the
56\,092
groups of order

2^{8}
are identified.

For the vertices of distance at least
4
to the root, individual
invariants for each vertex are used. As the tree has a rather large
number of vertices, no overall description of
all
used invariants
is feasible. Instead, we include two important examples.

Conjugacy class clusters and power-maps

Let
C
be a conjugacy class of
G
, let
l(C)=|C|
and let
o(C)

the order of a representative of
C
. The
(l,o)
-cluster of conjugacy
classes of
G
is the union of all conjugacy classes
C
of
G
with

l=l(C)
and
o=o(C)
. Note that a conjugacy class cluster is
invariant under isomorphisms.

The conjugacy class clusters
C_{1},\ldots,C_{l}
are now sorted and
refined. For the refinement choose
r\in\mathbb{N}
and evaluate
C_{i}^{r}=\{g^{r}\mid g\in C_{i}\}
for
1\leq i\leq l
. For each
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,l\}
let
A(i,j)=\{g\in C_{i}\mid g^{r}\in C_{j}\}

and
B(i,j)=C_{i}^{r}\cap C_{j}
. We then split the cluster
C_{i}
into
its subsets
A(i,1),\ldots,A(i,l)
and we split
C_{j}
into its
subsets
B(1,j),\ldots,B(l,j)
. This refinement is still an
invariant under isomorphisms.

To simplify notation we denote the refined conjugacy class clusters
with
C_{1},\ldots,C_{l}
. We define the map
\varphi:\{1,\ldots,l\}\rightarrow\{1,\ldots,l\}
with
\varphi(i)=j
if
C_{i}^{r}=C_{j}
. This
map
\varphi
is an isomorphism invariant.

This approach can be used with
r
th powers for any
r\in\mathbb{N}
. A
variation of the approach is obtained if commutators are evaluated
instead of powers, or other words in the free group are considered
and evaluated. Hence the general idea described here allows a wealth
of different functions
f(v)
. The construction of the decision tree
is such that each vertex has an associated function
f(v)
which splits
up the associated set
s(v)
in small subsets.

6

Remark:

The result of the conjugacy class clustering allows to identify all but

38
pairs of groups among the groups of order
2^{8}
.

Random isomorphism testing

A random isomorphism test for solvable groups of small order has been
described in
[
1
]
. This is applied in some cases when
s(v)
has
two groups remaining and these are very hard to distinguish by invariants.
Let
G
be the given group and let
s(v)=\{G_{1},G_{2}\}
.

The random isomorphism test determines iteratedly new special
pc-presentations for a given group at random, in this case for
G
,

G_{1}
and
G_{2}
. To facilitate an easy comparision, each obtained
pc-presentation is coded by an integer using CodePcGroup. Two groups
are isomomrphic if the same code appears for both of them.

A more detailed description of the random isomorphism test is given
in
[
1
]
. It works well in practise, despite the randomised
approach.

7

Remark:

The result of this test is used for all but one of the remaining
38

pairs. The last pair is splitted by checking if a specific group is
a subgroup of
G
.

4.2
Group identification for the groups of order
2^{9}

The new id-function for groups of order
2^{9}
is organised as a decision
tree similar to that for the groups of order
2^{8}
. We continue to use
the notation for the decision tree as introduced in Section
4.1
.

In difference to the decision tree for the groups of order
2^{8}
, we do
not use individual functions
f(v)
for each vertex of the tree; we use
the same function for all vertices of a certain depth in the tree. The
function is evaluated only if it splits the set
s(v)
into proper subsets.

The function
f(v)
computes:

\bullet

Step (1):
The rank of the group
G
.

\bullet

Step (2):
The abelian invariants of the groups in the derived series of
G
.

\bullet

Step (3):
The orders
o(g)
of the elements
g\in G
.

\bullet

Step (4):
For each conjugacy class
g^{G}
of
G
, the pair
(|g^{G}|,o(g))
.

\bullet

Step (5):

\mathrm{ID}(G/\lambda(G))
, where
\lambda(G)
is the last non-trivial term
of the
p
-central series of
G
.

These steps are far from distinguishing the groups of order
2^{9}
, but
they do provide a useful splitting of all groups in more managable
subsets. We now go into detail with our further invariants.

Conjugacy classes and power-maps

We first apply a variation of the conjugacy class clustering of
Section
4.1
. Note that we have determined the conjugacy
classes of
G
for Step (4) in the decision tree; denote these
by
C_{1},\ldots,C_{l}
.

\bullet

Step (6):
We determine
C_{i}^{3}=\{g^{3}\mid g\in C_{i}\}
and
j

with
C_{i}^{3}\subseteq C_{j}
. The map
\varphi:\{1,\ldots,l\}\rightarrow\{1,\ldots,l\}:i\mapsto j
is a permutation, since
\gcd(|G|,3)=1
.
The cycle type of
\varphi
is an isomorphism invariant.

\bullet

Step (7):
We proceed with the conjugacy class clusters and power-maps as described
in Section
4.1
.

8

Remark:

The steps described so far identify
99.9\%
of all groups of
order
2^{9}
; that is, roughly
10\,000
groups remain.

Siblings

The following invariants use a simplified version of the subgroup- and
factor-equivalence concept of Section
2
.

\bullet

Step (8):
Determine
\mathrm{ID}(Q)
for all proper quotients
Q=G/N
with
N\leq Z(G)
cyclic,

\bullet

Step (9):
Determine
\mathrm{ID}(M)
for all maximal subgroups
M\leq G
.

9

Remark:

The steps described so far identify all but
139
pairs and one tripel of
groups of order
2^{9}
.

Random isomorphism testing

The remaining cases of groups can now be identified effectively using
an isomorphism test, since the sets of possible options for each group
are very small at this point.

\bullet

Step (10’):
We use the random isomorphism technique described in Section
4.1
.

\bullet

Step (10’):
Alternatively, the standard presentation algorithm of the ANUPQ package

[
11
]
identifies the remaining groups.

10

Remark:

The results of this test identify all remaining groups of order
2^{9}
.

5
Questions and open problems

Some interesting problems remain unsolved. We discuss some of them.

Question I:

Let
p
be a prime.

(a)

Are there twin-tupels or sibling-tupels or arbitrary length among the
finite
p
-groups?

(b)

Are there estimates for the number of siblings of twins among the groups
of order
p^{n}
?

Sibling-tupels arise often as central extensions of a finite
p
-group.
This raises the following question. A solution to this question might
also pave the way towards an answer to Question I(a).

Question II:

Given a finite
p
-group
G
, under which conditions on
G
does

G
have sibling-tupels as extensions? Can one classify the extensions
of
G
that are siblings?

Finally, investigating the twin-tupels arising among
p
-groups remains
a difficult problem. In the id-group algorithm we resort to isomorphism
testing for these cases. However, the following would be of interest.

Question III:

Which invariants are useful in distinguishing twins or siblings?

We note that Lewis and Wilson
[
15
]
investigate a similar problem
for families of finite
p
-groups for odd
p
.
