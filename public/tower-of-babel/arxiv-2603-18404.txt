Multi-Domain Empirical Bayes for Linearly-Mixed Causal Representations
Bohan Wu, Julius von Kügelgen, David M. Blei

Abstract
Causal representation learning (CRL) aims to learn low-dimensional causal latent variables from high-dimensional observations. While identifiability has been extensively studied for CRL, estimation has been less explored. In this paper, we explore the use of empirical Bayes (EB) to estimate causal representations. In particular, we consider the problem of learning from data from multiple domains, where differences between domains are modeled by interventions in a shared underlying causal model. Multi-domain CRL naturally poses a simultaneous inference problem that EB is designed to tackle. Here, we propose an EB f f -modeling algorithm that improves the quality of learned causal variables by exploiting invariant structure within and across domains. Specifically, we consider a linear measurement model and interventional priors arising from a shared acyclic SCM. When the graph and intervention targets are known, we develop an EM-style algorithm based on causally structured score matching. We further discuss EB g -modeling in the context of existing CRL approaches. In experiments on synthetic data, our proposed method achieves more accurate estimation than other methods for CRL.

4.2
Tweedie’s formula

The focal point of traditional empirical Bayes theory is the normal means model with isotropic noise
[
38
,
13
,
14
,
15
,
42

\mathbf{x}_{i}=\bm{\theta}_{i}+\bm{\zeta}_{i},\qquad\bm{\zeta}_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{iid}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}\left(\bm{0},\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{d_{X}}\right).

(4.1)

A central result is Tweedie’s formula
[
21
, Thm. 6.2]
, which states that the optimal estimator
t^{\star}
minimizing the posterior risk
\mathbb{E}\left[\|\bm{\theta}-t(\bm{x}_{i})\|_{2}^{2}\right]
for any prior on
\bm{\theta}_{i}
is given by

t^{\star}(\bm{x}_{i}):=\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{\theta}_{i}\mid\bm{x}_{i}\right]=\bm{x}_{i}+\sigma^{2}\nabla\log{f}(\bm{x}_{i}),

where
{f}
denotes the marginal density of
\bm{x}
.

By applying Tweedie’s formula to model (
3.1

\bm{A}\bm{z}
given
\bm{x}
. However, this does not lead to the posterior mean of
\bm{z}
as
\bm{A}
need not be invertible. To solve this problem, we impose an additional assumption on the class of mixing matrices
\bm{A}
that allows us to reduce the problem to a normal means model.

4.3

To model the causal variable

Assumption 4.1

The true mixing matrix

(\bm{A}^{\star})^{\top}\bm{A}^{\star}=(\bm{D}^{\star})^{2}

4.1

are orthogonal with possibly different norms, similar to assumptions exploited in independent mechanism analysis
[

4.1

, where
\bm{O}\in\mathcal{O}^{d_{X}\times d_{Z}}

\bm{D}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{Z}\times d_{Z}}

Let

\bm{y}_{ei}\!:=\!\bm{O}^{\top}\bm{x}_{ei}
. Since
\bm{O}^{\top}\bm{O}\!=\!\mathbf{I}_{d_{Z}}

\bm{y}_{ei}=\bm{D}\bm{z}_{ei}+{\bm{\zeta}}_{ei},\qquad{\bm{\zeta}}_{ei}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{iid}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}\left(0,\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{d_{Z}}\right).

(4.2)

4.4
Updating the latent variables

According to Tweedie’s formula applied to
Eq.

4.2
, the optimal estimator for the means
\mathbf{D}\mathbf{z}_{ei}
is given by

\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{D}\bm{z}_{ei}\mid\bm{y}_{ei}\right]=\bm{y}_{ei}+\sigma^{2}\nabla\log{f}_{\bm{a}_{e}}(\bm{y}_{ei}),

(4.3)

where
{f}_{\bm{a}_{e}}
is the true marginal density of
\bm{y}
in domain

\mathrm{p}^{\star}(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{a}_{e})
via (
4.2
).

For fixed
\widehat{\mathbf{A}}=\widehat{\mathbf{O}}\widehat{\mathbf{D}}
and
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}
, we therefore only need estimates of the scores
\nabla\log{f}_{\bm{a}_{e}}(\bm{y}_{ei})
to estimate the posterior mean of
\mathbf{z}_{ei}
via (

). In
Section

5
, we discuss how we estimate these scores using a flexible function class to obtain an estimate

via score matching
[
18
]
.
We then update the empirical Bayes estimates of all local latent variables
\bm{z}_{ei}
using a damped version of Tweedie’s formula:

\widehat{\bm{z}_{ei}}=\widehat{\bm{D}}^{-1}\left(\bm{y}_{ei}+\eta\widehat{\sigma}^{2}\widehat{s}(\bm{y}_{ei},\bm{a}_{e})\right),

(4.4)

where
\eta\in[0,1]
is the damping factor;
\eta=0
corresponds to no shrinkage and
\eta=1
to the classical Tweedie’s formula.

We also require the posterior second moments, defined as the element-wise squares
\mathbb{E}[\bm{z}_{ei}\odot\bm{z}_{ei}\mid\bm{x}_{ei},\bm{a}_{e}]
. To approximate them, we use the following estimates

\widehat{z_{eij}^{2}}\!=\!\widehat{z_{eij}}^{2}\!+\!\widehat{\sigma}^{2}\widehat{\bm{D}}_{jj}^{-2}\!+\!\widehat{\sigma}^{4}\widehat{\bm{D}}_{jj}^{-2}\partial_{j}\widehat{s}_{j}(y_{j},\mathbf{y}_{\mathrm{pa}(j)},\bm{a}_{e}),

(4.5)

where the component-wise score estimates
\widehat{s}_{jj}
for
j\in[d_{Z}]
are described in further detail in
Section

5
.

4.5
Updating the measurement model

Given our estimates of the posterior first and second moments of each

, in the M-step we update the parameters

to maximize the data log-likelihood,
resulting in

\widehat{\bm{A}},\widehat{\sigma}^{2}\in\argmax_{\bm{A}=\bm{O}\bm{D},\sigma^{2}\geq 0}\sum_{e=1}^{M}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{e}}\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{\mathrm{q}}_{ei}\left(\bm{z}\right)}\left[\log\mathrm{p}_{\bm{A},\sigma^{2}}\left(\bm{x}_{ei}\mid\bm{z}\right)\right],

where

Since

Given the estimates

(4.7)

The solution

\displaystyle\bm{M}

\displaystyle\leftarrow\sum_{e=1}^{M}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{e}}\bm{x}_{ei}\widehat{\bm{z}_{ei}}^{\top},\qquad\left[\bm{U},\bm{\Sigma},\bm{V}\right]\leftarrow\textsc{SVD}(\bm{M}),

\widehat{\sigma}^{2}

Finally, we update

5
Causal score matching

Approximating the posterior of
\mathbf{z}
via Tweedie’s formula in
Eqs.

4.3
,
4.4
and
4.5
involves the score function
\nabla\log{f}_{\bm{a}}(\bm{y})
. In this section, we propose a scalable method to estimate this score that allows for incorporating
knowledge of the causal directed acyclic graph (DAG)
\mathcal{G}^{\star}
from
3.5
.

Score decomposition.

The true marginal
{f}_{\bm{a}_{e}}(\bm{y}_{ei})
is determined by the normal means model in (
4.2
) and
depends on

\bm{a}_{e}
only through

\mathbf{z}_{ei}\sim\mathrm{p}^{\star}(\bm{z}\mid\bm{a}_{e})
.
Under
3.5
,
the score of
\bm{z}
follows from the Markov factorization in
Eq.

3.3
w.r.t. the true
causal graph
\mathcal{G}^{\star}
. Its
j
th
component is given by

\displaystyle\big[\nabla\log\mathrm{p}^{\star}(\bm{z}\mid

\displaystyle\bm{a})\big]_{j}=\partial_{j}\log\mathrm{p}^{\star}\big(z_{j}\mid\bm{z}_{\mathrm{pa}(j)},a_{j}\big)

(5.1)

\displaystyle+\sum_{k\in\textrm{ch}(j)}\partial_{j}\log\mathrm{p}^{\star}\big(z_{k}\mid\bm{z}_{\mathrm{pa}(k)},a_{k}\big)

and only depends on the Markov blanket
[

of
z_{j}
and the intervention target labels for
z_{j}
and its children.
4

4

The Markov blanket of node

in a DAG comprises
j

j

Even though

is simply a noisy, element-wise rescaling of
\mathbf{z}

\nabla\log f_{\bm{a}}(\bm{y})
due to the additional noise term
{\bm{\zeta}}
in

of

is determined by d-separation
[
31
]
in the extended graph of
\mathcal{G}^{\star}
with additional nodes for
\mathbf{y}
and
\mathbf{a}
and additional edges
\{a_{j}\to z_{j}\to y_{j}\}_{j\in[d_{Z}]}
, see
Figure

2
for an example.

In general, each component of
\nabla\log f_{\bm{a}}(\bm{y})
may depend on most or all of
\mathbf{y}
and
\mathbf{a}
, i.e., it is a dense
vector field
[
4
, §13.1]
. This poses challenges for estimation, particularly when
d_{Z}
is large. We therefore propose a sparse approximation of the true score that incorporates prior causal knowledge.

Causal score function.

Specifically, we
consider the class of
causal score functions

s:\mathbb{R}^{d_{Z}}\times\{0,1\}^{d_{Z}}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{Z}}

w.r.t. a given DAG
\mathcal{G}
over
[d_{Z}]
, defined component-wise by

\left[s(\bm{y},\bm{a})\right]_{j}:=s_{j}\!\left(y_{j},\mathbf{y}_{\mathrm{pa}(j;\mathcal{G})},a_{j}\right),

(5.2)

where

\mathrm{pa}(j;\mathcal{G})
refers to the parents in
\mathcal{G}
.
Natural choices of
\mathcal{G}
include
\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{G}^{\star}
, the empty graph, and complete DAGs w.r.t. the partial causal ordering induced by
\mathcal{G}^{\star}
.
5
5

5

Using a sparser DAG can be beneficial from a bias–variance perspective: while the restricted class (
5.2
) may be biased for the full score, restricting inputs to
\mathrm{pa}(j)
can reduce the variance of score estimation relative to conditioning on a larger set. It also reduces computational cost and thus scales better to large DAGs.

We fit the causal score function

via score-matching
[
18
]
w.r.t. the true score, which on the population level is equivalent to minimizing the following

loss,

\hskip-5.0pt\mathbf{L}^{\mathcal{G}}\left(s\right)\!=\!\sum_{e=1}^{M}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{y}\sim f_{\bm{a}_{e}}}\left[\left\lVert s(\bm{y},\bm{a}_{e})\!-\!\nabla\log f_{\bm{a}_{e}}\!\left(\bm{y}\right)\right\rVert^{2}_{2}\right]\!.

(5.3)

The optimal causal score function

minimizing

5.3
coincides with
\nabla\log f_{\bm{a}}(\bm{y})
(i.e., the approximation is exact) when
\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{G}^{\star}

is the empty graph.
Similarly, when
\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{G}^{\star}
and
\sigma^{2}=0
(and thus
\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{D}\mathbf{z}
), the optimal causal score recovers the true score for leaf nodes
j

(for which
{\textrm{ch}(j)=\varnothing}
and the second term on the RHS of
Eq.

5.1
vanishes).
Otherwise, it
serves as a sparse, structured approximation.

Decoupled estimation.

The following result characterizes the
structure for the minimizer of the score-matching loss.

Theorem 5.1
.

For all solutions
s^{\star}\in\argmin_{s\in{\mathcal{S}}}\mathbf{L}^{\mathcal{G}}(s)

and all

j\in[d_{Z}]
, the
j
th
component
s^{\star}_{j}
of
s^{\star}
minimizes

\sum_{e=1}^{M}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{y}\sim{f}_{\bm{a}_{e}}}\!\left[\left|s_{j}\left(y_{j},\mathbf{y}_{\mathrm{pa}(j)},a_{ej}\right)\right|^{2}\!+2\partial_{j}s_{j}\left(y_{j},\mathbf{y}_{\mathrm{pa}(j)},a_{ej}\right)\right]\!.

(5.4)

Theorem

5.1
provides a key structural insight into the causal score-matching problem: The optimization over
s
decouples across the components
\{s_{j}\}_{j=1}^{d_{Z}}

6

While our main focus is on the

As discussed in
Section

4

g

\mathrm{p}^{\star}(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{a}_{e})

{\{\mathrm{p}_{\bm{\gamma}}(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{a}_{e}):{\bm{\gamma}}\in\Gamma\}}
and aims to jointly infer the parameters
{\bm{\theta}}:=(\mathbf{A},\sigma^{2},{\bm{\gamma}})
by maximizing the log marginal likelihood

\displaystyle\log\mathrm{p}_{{\bm{\theta}}}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{e=1}^{M}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{e}}\log\mathrm{p}_{{\bm{\theta}}}(\mathbf{x}_{ei}\mid\mathbf{a}_{e}),

(6.1)

\displaystyle\text{where}\quad\mathrm{p}_{{\bm{\theta}}}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{a})

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{p}_{\bm{A},\sigma^{2}}(\bm{x}\mid\bm{z})\mathrm{p}_{\bm{\gamma}}(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{a})d\mathbf{z}.

Equation

6.1
is difficult to optimize exactly because it involves intractable integrals.
However, we can use variational inference (VI) to approximate it
[
5
]
.
For any variational posterior
\mathrm{q}_{ei}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{Z}})
over
\mathbf{z}_{ei}
,
we can bound the marginal likelihood by the
evidence lower bound
(ELBO),

\log\mathrm{p}_{{\bm{\theta}}}\left(\mathbf{x}_{ei}\mid\mathbf{a}_{e}\right)\geq\mathrm{ELBO}({\bm{\theta}},\mathrm{q}_{ei};\mathbf{x}_{ei},\mathbf{a}_{e}):=\\
\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{q}_{ei}(\bm{z})}\left[\log\mathrm{p}_{\bm{A},\sigma^{2}}(\bm{x}_{ei}\mid\bm{z})\right]-D_{\textsc{KL}}\left(\mathrm{q}_{ei}(\bm{z})\parallel\mathrm{p}_{\bm{\gamma}}(\bm{z}\mid\bm{a}_{e})\right).

The variational posteriors often come from a parametric family
\{\mathrm{q}_{{\bm{\phi}}}(\bm{z}):{\bm{\phi}}\in\Phi\}
.
Provided
\mathrm{p}_{\bm{\gamma}}
is differentiable w.r.t.
{\bm{\gamma}}
,
black-box VI
[
35
]
or amortized VI with reparametrization trick
[
25
,
37
]
yield differentiable objectives amenable to gradient-based optimization w.r.t. both
{\bm{\theta}}
and
{\bm{\phi}}
.
The learned variational posterior is then used as approximation of (
2.3
).

For a single domain and a parameter-free standard Gaussian prior,
p_{\bm{\gamma}}(\mathbf{z})=\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\mathbf{I})

[
25

[

More expressive choices for

[

{\bm{\gamma}}

and variational

Alternatively, the prior can be expressed directly in terms of the variational posterior

If we observe multiple domains and do not impose additional structure on

\mathbf{a}

For single-cell data, perturbations

Discussion

We proposed an empirical Bayes
f
-modeling approach to multi-domain causal representation learning that addresses the simultaneous inference of latent causal variables under a noisy measurement model. The main EM algorithm performs iterative Tweedie updates with score matching. The empirical results show accurate performance.

When the true DAG is small or when the DAG is unknown but a causal order is known, it may be safer to run CRL
f
-modeling with the complete DAG induced by that order, since it is well specified for the true score function. When the DAG is large, CRL
f
-modeling with the true DAG is typically the more scalable and accurate choice.

For nonlinear measurement models,
f
-modeling is less directly applicable because of the difficulty to adapt Tweedie’s formula. On the other hand,
g
-modeling becomes more natural since it extends directly to nonlinear or learned decoders
\mathrm{p}_{{\bm{\theta}}}(\bm{x}\mid\bm{z})
, including deep networks.

While our algorithm is rather focused on a particular scenario, we believe the framework of causal empirical Bayes to be more broadly applicable to other algorithms for other model classes. Extensions include richer intervention structures
\mathrm{p}(\bm{z}\mid\bm{a})
with non-binary
\bm{a}
(e.g., additive shifts) and relaxing the assumption of known graphs or targets by using the optimal EB objectives for graph/target discovery.

Appendix A
Details of the
f
-modeling Algorithm

In this appendix, we fill in the detailed derivation of
Algorithm

1
. The full procedure is a variational EM algorithm
[
53
]
.

The marginal likelihood of
(\bm{A},\sigma^{2})
is

\sum_{e=1}^{M}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{e}}\log\mathrm{p}_{\bm{A},\sigma^{2}}^{\star}(\bm{x}_{ei}\mid\bm{a}_{e})=\sum_{e=1}^{M}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{e}}\log\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{Z}}}\mathrm{p}_{\bm{A},\sigma^{2}}(\bm{x}_{ei}\mid\bm{z})\,\mathrm{p}^{\star}(\bm{z}\mid\bm{a}_{e})\,\mathrm{d}\bm{z}.

The log-likelihood is difficult to optimize because it involves intractable integrals, so we approximate it using variational inference. In the E-step, we lower bound the log-likelihood by an ELBO. For any
\mathrm{q}_{ei}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{Z}})
,

\displaystyle\log\mathrm{p}_{\bm{A},\sigma^{2}}^{\star}(\bm{x}_{ei}\mid\bm{a}_{e})

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{q}_{ei}(\bm{z}_{ei})}\left[\log\mathrm{p}_{\bm{A},\sigma^{2}}(\bm{x}_{ei}\mid\bm{z}_{ei})\right]-D_{\textsc{KL}}\left(\mathrm{q}_{ei}(\bm{z}_{ei})\parallel\mathrm{p}^{\star}(\bm{z}\mid\bm{a}_{e})\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{q}_{ei}(\bm{z}_{ei})}\left[\|\bm{x}_{ei}-\bm{A}\bm{z}_{ei}\|^{2}\right]-\frac{d_{X}}{2}\log\sigma^{2}-D_{\textsc{KL}}\left(\mathrm{q}_{ei}(\bm{z}_{ei})\parallel\mathrm{p}^{\star}(\bm{z}\mid\bm{a}_{e})\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{q}_{ei}(\bm{z}_{ei})}\left[\|\bm{x}_{ei}\|^{2}+\bm{z}_{ei}^{\top}\bm{D}^{2}\bm{z}_{ei}-2\,\bm{x}_{ei}^{\top}\bm{A}\bm{z}_{ei}\right]-\frac{d_{X}}{2}\log\sigma^{2}-D_{\textsc{KL}}\left(\mathrm{q}_{ei}(\bm{z}_{ei})\parallel\mathrm{p}^{\star}(\bm{z}\mid\bm{a}_{e})\right)

\displaystyle=:\mathrm{ELBO}^{\star}(\mathrm{q}_{ei},\bm{A},\sigma^{2}).

Then we design an EM algorithm which aims to iteratively maximize the objective
\sum_{e=1}^{M}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{e}}\mathrm{ELBO}^{\star}(\mathrm{q}_{ei},\bm{A},\sigma^{2})
.

Update
\bm{z}
:
For given
\bm{A}
and
\sigma^{2}
, the distribution
\mathrm{q}_{ei}
that maximizes the ELBO is the posterior

\mathrm{q}_{ei}^{\star}(\bm{z}_{ei})\propto\mathrm{p}_{\bm{A},\sigma^{2}}(\bm{x}_{ei}\mid\bm{z}_{ei})\,\mathrm{p}^{\star}(\bm{z}_{ei}\mid\bm{a}_{e}).

A direct calculation shows that, for updating
\bm{A}
and
\sigma^{2}
in the subsequent M-step, it suffices to keep track of the first and second moments of
\mathrm{q}_{ei}^{\star}
. To approximate these moments, we use the first- and second-order Tweedie formulas.

The score-matching problem can be expressed in terms of the diagonal score components
\left(\widehat{s}_{j}\right)_{j\in[d_{Z}]}
:

\widehat{s}_{j}\in\argmin_{s_{j}}\sum_{e=1}^{M}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{e}}\Bigg[\partial_{j}s_{j}\left(y_{eij},y_{ei\mathrm{pa}(j)},\bm{a}_{e}\right)+\frac{1}{2}s_{j}\left(y_{eij},y_{ei\mathrm{pa}(j)},\bm{a}_{e}\right)^{2}\Bigg].

(A.1)

The resulting score estimate is given by
[\widehat{s}(\bm{y},\bm{a})]_{j}=\widehat{s}_{j}(\bm{y}_{j},\bm{y}_{\mathrm{pa}(j)},\bm{a})
, or, under the sparse-intervention approximation,
[\widehat{s}(\bm{y},\bm{a})]_{j}=\widehat{s}_{j}(\bm{y}_{j},\bm{y}_{\mathrm{pa}(j)},\bm{a}_{j})
, for
j\in[d_{Z}]
.

Given an estimate
\widehat{\bm{D}}
of the diagonal matrix
\bm{D}
, we update the empirical Bayes estimate of the latent variable
\bm{z}_{ei}
using a damped version of Tweedie’s formula:

\widehat{\bm{z}_{ei}}=\widehat{\bm{D}}^{-1}\left(\bm{y}_{ei}+\eta\sigma^{2}\widehat{s}(\bm{y}_{ei},\bm{a}_{e})\right),

where
\eta\in(0,1)
is a damping factor.

The second-order Tweedie’s formula yields

\widehat{z_{eij}^{2}}=\widehat{z_{eij}}^{2}+\frac{\widehat{\sigma}^{2}}{\widehat{\bm{D}}_{jj}^{2}}+\frac{\widehat{\sigma}^{4}}{\widehat{\bm{D}}_{jj}^{2}}\partial_{j}\widehat{s}_{j}(y_{j},\mathbf{y}_{\mathrm{pa}(j)},\bm{a}_{e}),\quad j\in[d_{Z}].

Update
\bm{A}
:

In the M-step, we maximize the ELBO over
\bm{A}
, equivalently

\widehat{\bm{A}}=\arg\min_{\bm{A}\text{ orthogonal}}\sum_{e=1}^{M}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{e}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{q}(\bm{z}_{ei})}\left[\bm{z}_{ei}^{\top}\bm{D}^{2}\bm{z}_{ei}-2\bm{x}_{ei}^{\top}\bm{A}\bm{z}_{ei}\right].

(A.2)

This optimization depends on the posterior mean and second moments under
\mathrm{q}
. A natural choice is to take
\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{q}}\left[\bm{z}_{ei}\right]
as the Tweedie estimate
\widehat{\bm{z}_{ei}}
, and
\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{q}}\left[\bm{z}_{ei}^{2}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{Z}}
as the second-order Tweedie estimate
\widehat{\bm{z}_{ei}^{2}}
.

Define
\bm{M}:=\sum_{e=1}^{M}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{e}}\bm{x}_{ei}\widehat{\bm{z}_{ei}}^{\top}
. The optimization problem reduces to

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{A}}=\arg\min_{\bm{A}}

\displaystyle\sum_{e=1}^{M}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{e}}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{d_{Z}}\bm{D}_{jj}^{2}\widehat{\bm{z}_{eij}^{2}}-2\left\langle\bm{x}_{ei},\bm{A}\widehat{\bm{z}_{ei}}\right\rangle\right]

(A.3)

\displaystyle=\sum_{e=1}^{M}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{e}}\sum_{j=1}^{d_{Z}}\bm{D}_{jj}^{2}\widehat{\bm{z}_{eij}^{2}}-2\text{tr}\bigl(\bm{A}\bm{M}^{\top}\bigr).

where the second equality is due to

\sum_{e=1}^{M}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{e}}\left\langle\bm{x}_{ei},\bm{A}\widehat{\bm{z}_{ei}}\right\rangle=\sum_{e=1}^{M}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{e}}\text{tr}\left(\bm{A}\widehat{\bm{z}_{ei}}\bm{x}_{ei}^{\top}\right)=\text{tr}\bigl(\bm{A}\bm{M}^{\top}\bigr).

Now we parametrize
\bm{A}=\bm{O}\bm{D}
thanks to
4.1
.

Let
\bm{M}=\bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^{\top}
be the singular value decomposition of
\bm{M}
. Since the trace is cyclically invariant, the objective (
A.3
) is equivalent to
\text{tr}\bigl(\bm{O}\bm{D}\bm{M}^{\top}\bigr)=\text{tr}\bigl(\bm{O}\bm{V}\bm{D}\bm{\Sigma}^{\top}\bm{U}^{\top}\bigr)
. By the orthogonal Procrustes solution
[
16
, §6.4.1]
, for a given
\bm{D}
, the solution
\widehat{\bm{O}}
that maximizes
Equation

A.3
is given by

\widehat{\bm{O}}=\bm{U}\bm{V}^{\top}\in\mathcal{O}^{d_{X}\times d_{Z}}.

After plugging
\widehat{\bm{O}}
into the reformulated objective, the optimal
\widehat{\bm{D}}
requires solving

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{D}}

\displaystyle=\arg\min_{\bm{D}}\sum_{e=1}^{M}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{e}}\sum_{j=1}^{d_{Z}}\bm{D}_{jj}^{2}\widehat{\bm{z}_{eij}^{2}}-2\text{tr}\bigl(\bm{U}\bm{D}\bm{\Sigma}^{\top}\bm{U}^{\top}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\sum_{e=1}^{M}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{e}}\sum_{j=1}^{d_{Z}}\bm{D}_{jj}^{2}\widehat{\bm{z}_{eij}^{2}}-2\text{tr}\bigl(\bm{D}\bm{\Sigma}^{\top}\bigr).

The problem reduces to solving a quadratic function for each entry of
\bm{D}
. The optimizer is explicitly given by
\widehat{\bm{D}}_{jj}=\bm{\Sigma}_{jj}/\sum_{e=1}^{M}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{e}}\widehat{\bm{z}_{eij}^{2}}
for
j\in[d_{Z}]
.

Update
\sigma^{2}
.
Finally, we freeze
\{\widehat{\bm{z}_{ei}}\}
,
\{\widehat{\bm{z}_{ei}^{2}}\}
, and
\widehat{\bm{A}}
, and maximize the ELBO with respect to
\sigma^{2}
. The ELBO is concave in
1/\sigma^{2}
, so setting the derivative to zero yields the unique maximizer

\widehat{\sigma}^{2}=\frac{1}{d_{X}\sum_{e=1}^{M}N_{e}}\sum_{e=1}^{M}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{e}}\left(\|\bm{x}_{ei}\|_{2}^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{d_{Z}}\widehat{\bm{D}}_{jj}^{2}\,\widehat{\bm{z}_{eij}^{2}}-2\,\bm{x}_{ei}^{\top}\widehat{\bm{A}}\widehat{\bm{z}_{ei}}\right).

A.1
Score modeling with cubic splines

To estimate the diagonal score components
\{s_{j}\}_{j\in[d_{Z}]}
, we use a simple spline-based nonparametric model
[
47
]
that admits closed-form updates. Fix
j\in[d_{Z}]
and let
x_{eij}:=(y_{eij},y_{ei\mathrm{pa}(j)})\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathrm{pa}(j)|+1}
denote the input. We model

s_{j}(y_{eij},y_{ei\mathrm{pa}(j)},a_{ej})=\phi_{j}(x_{eij})^{\top}\theta_{j,a_{ej}},

where
\phi_{j}:\mathbb{R}^{|\mathrm{pa}(j)|+1}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
is a tensor-product cubic B-spline feature map with a fixed knot sequence on each coordinate, and
\theta_{j,a}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
are intervention-specific coefficients for
a\in\{0,1\}
. In particular, if we use
K
basis functions per coordinate, then the tensor-product construction yields
m=K^{|\mathrm{pa}(j)|+1}
features, so both memory and computational complexity grow exponentially in the input dimension
|\mathrm{pa}(j)|+1
.

Write
\Psi_{j}(x):=\partial_{y_{j}}\phi_{j}(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
for the derivative of the spline features with respect to the first coordinate
y_{j}
. Then

\partial_{y_{j}}\,s_{j}(y_{eij},y_{ei\mathrm{pa}(j)},a_{ej})=\Psi_{j}(x_{eij})^{\top}\theta_{j,a_{ej}}.

Plugging this parameterization into the empirical score-matching loss for the
j
th component yields the quadratic objective

\widehat{\bm{L}}_{j}(\theta_{j0},\theta_{j1})=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{e=1}^{M}\frac{1}{N_{e}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{e}}\left\{\Psi_{j}(x_{eij})^{\top}\theta_{j,a_{ej}}+\frac{1}{2}\bigl(\phi_{j}(x_{eij})^{\top}\theta_{j,a_{ej}}\bigr)^{2}\right\}.

We fit
\theta_{j0},\theta_{j1}
with ridge regularization,

(\widehat{\theta}_{j0},\widehat{\theta}_{j1})\in\arg\min_{\theta_{j0},\theta_{j1}}\widehat{\bm{L}}_{j}(\theta_{j0},\theta_{j1})+\frac{\lambda}{2}\bigl(\|\theta_{j0}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\theta_{j1}\|_{2}^{2}\bigr),

which decouples over
a\in\{0,1\}
. For each
a
, define the index set
\mathcal{I}_{ja}:=\{(e,i):a_{ej}=a\}
, the feature and derivative matrices

\Phi_{ja}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{I}_{ja}|\times m},\quad\Phi_{ja}(r,:)=\phi_{j}(x_{eij})^{\top},\qquad\Psi_{ja}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{I}_{ja}|\times m},\quad\Psi_{ja}(r,:)=\Psi_{j}(x_{eij})^{\top},

and the normal equations

\widehat{\theta}_{ja}=-\left(\Phi_{ja}^{\top}\Phi_{ja}+\lambda I_{m}\right)^{-1}\Psi_{ja}^{\top}\mathbf{1}.

The resulting score estimator is
\widehat{s}_{j}(x,a)=\phi_{j}(x)^{\top}\widehat{\theta}_{ja}
, and
\partial_{y_{j}}\widehat{s}_{j}(x,a)=\Psi_{j}(x)^{\top}\widehat{\theta}_{ja}
, which are the quantities used in the first- and second-order Tweedie updates.

A.3
Justifying the sparse intervention approximation

The sparse intervention approximation becomes accurate in the large-sample regime. By Bayes’ rule, we have

\displaystyle f_{\bm{a}_{e}}(y_{j}\mid\mathbf{y}_{\mathrm{pa}(j)})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{|\mathrm{pa}(j)|+1}}\mathrm{p}(y_{j}\mid z_{j})\mathrm{p}_{a_{ej}}(z_{j}\mid z_{\mathrm{pa}(j)})\mathrm{p}_{\bm{a}_{e}}(z_{\mathrm{pa}(j)}\mid\mathbf{y}_{\mathrm{pa}(j)})\mathrm{d}z_{\mathrm{pa}(j)\cup\{j\}}.

(A.4)

As we observe samples

, when

is large, the posterior
\mathrm{p}_{\bm{a}_{e}}(z_{\mathrm{pa}(j)}\mid\mathbf{y}_{\mathrm{pa}(j)})

\widehat{\mathrm{p}}(z_{\mathrm{pa}(j)}\mid\mathbf{y}_{\mathrm{pa}(j)})\propto\mathrm{p}(\mathbf{y}_{\mathrm{pa}(j)}\mid\widehat{z}_{\mathrm{pa}(j)})\,\widehat{\textrm{g}}(z_{\mathrm{pa}(j)})

where
\widehat{\textrm{g}}
is the empirical distribution of the denoised estimates
\{\widehat{z}_{ei\mathrm{pa}(j)}\}_{i=1}^{N_{e}}
learned from the normal means model. This substitute yields the approximation

f_{\bm{a}_{e}}(y_{j}\mid\mathbf{y}_{\mathrm{pa}(j)})\approx\int_{\mathbb{R}^{|\mathrm{pa}(j)|+1}}\mathrm{p}(y_{j}\mid z_{j})\,\mathrm{p}_{a_{ej}}(z_{j}\mid z_{\mathrm{pa}(j)})\,\widehat{\mathrm{p}}(z_{\mathrm{pa}(j)}\mid\mathbf{y}_{\mathrm{pa}(j)})\,\mathrm{d}z_{\mathrm{pa}(j)\cup\{j\}}=:f_{a_{ej}}(y_{j}\mid\mathbf{y}_{\mathrm{pa}(j)}),

thus asymptotically the conditional density depends on the environment only through the local intervention indicator
a_{ej}
. Thus, the constraint to depend on
a_{j}
can be viewed as a causal invariance constraint that requires
\bm{y}
to act in a sparse way given the intervention
\bm{a}

[
57

Appendix C
Weighted
f
-modeling CRL

The current method assigns uniform importance to each environment in the objective. However it is often desirable to learn a causal model that prioritizes accuracy in certain environments over others. For example, the base environment
\bm{a}=0
may be of particular interest. To account for such preferences, we could consider the weighted score-matching objective:

\widehat{s}=\arg\min_{s\in{\mathcal{S}}}\sum_{e=1}^{M}w_{e}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{e}}\sum_{j=1}^{d_{Z}}\left[\partial_{j}[s(\bm{y}_{ei},\bm{a}_{e})]_{j}+\frac{1}{2}\left([s(\bm{y}_{ei},\bm{a}_{e})]_{j}\right)^{2}\right],

where
w_{e}\geq 0
denotes the weight assigned to environment
e
, subject to
\sum_{e=1}^{M}w_{e}=1
. The weights determine the relative influence of each environment on the score matching loss. Higher
w_{e}
assign more importance to environment
e
.

In the absence of prior information or labeled test data, one may choose uniform weights
w_{e}=1/M
, or alternatively set
w_{e}
proportional to the sample size
N_{e}
to account for imbalance across environments. If a particular environment (e.g., the base SCM with
\bm{a}_{e}=0
) is of interest, one may assign it a dominant weight (e.g.,
w_{e}\approx 1
) and downweight the others accordingly. Other methods for choosing the environment weights are discussed
[
40
]
.

29
]
. Identification results can be algorithm-agnostic and do not require a specific method to recover the parameters. The working model (
3.1
) corresponds to the setting of causal representation learning (CRL) with a linear measurement model, a general SCM, and known causal DAG and intervention targets. We now review some useful notions of identifiability to discuss our results.

Definition E.1

(Element-identifiability / Disentanglement)
.

A learned representation
\widehat{\bm{z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{Z}}
is said to be
element-identifiable
if there exists a permutation matrix
\bm{P}_{\pi}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{Z}\times d_{Z}}
and an element-wise diffeomorphism
h(\bm{z}):=(h_{1}(z_{1}),\ldots,h_{d_{Z}}(z_{d_{Z}}))\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{Z}}
such that

\widehat{\bm{z}}=\bm{P}_{\pi}h(\bm{z}^{\star}).

(E.1)

Element-identifiability is also known as
perfect latent recovery
in the language of
49
, where a representation
\widehat{\bm{z}}
satisfying this condition is said to be
perfect
. It is also referred to as
disentanglement
in the sense of
29
.

Definition E.2

(Scale-permutation-identifiability)
.

A learned representation
\widehat{\bm{z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{Z}}
is said to be
scale-permutation-identifiable
if there exists a permutation matrix
\bm{P}_{\pi}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{Z}\times d_{Z}}
and a diagonal sign matrix
\bm{D}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{Z}\times d_{Z}}
such that

\widehat{\bm{z}}=\bm{P}_{\pi}\bm{D}\bm{z}^{\star}.

(E.2)

Scale-permutation-identifiability is a stronger form of element-identifiability. It describes recovery of the ground-truth latent variables up to permutation and rescaling, which are common forms of ambiguities in linear latent variable models.

Definition E.3

(Mixing-identifiablity)
.

A learned representation
\widehat{\bm{z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{Z}}
is said to be
mixing-identifiable
if there exists a permutation matrix
\bm{P}_{\pi}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{Z}\times d_{Z}}
, a diagonal matrix
\bm{D}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{Z}\times d_{Z}}
and sparse matrix
\bm{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{Z}\times d_{Z}}
satisfying that
\bm{C}_{jj}=0
for all
j\in[d_{Z}]
and

\textrm{ch}(i)\nsubseteq\textrm{ch}(j)\implies\bm{C}_{ij}=0,\forall i\in[d_{Z}],

such that
\widehat{\bm{z}}=\bm{P}_{\pi}(\bm{D}+\bm{C})\bm{z}^{\star}
.

This condition ensures that
\widehat{\bm{z}}
is constrained by the causal graph structure, where mixing occurs only among variables with nested parent sets.

There has been extensive recent work on the question of identifiability in CRL. Under
3.1
, identifiability typically holds only up to an equivalence class determined by the available interventions; for example,
44
establish such results under a linear measurement model together with a
linear
latent SCM. Several works clarify the limits and possibilities of identifiability. Under hard interventions and a linear measurement model,
44
and
7
show that latent variables are identifiable only up to scaling and permutation, and that this is optimal without further assumptions on the causal variables
\bm{z}
. For stochastic interventions,
48
show that under soft interventions and
3.1
, one can identify latent variables up to mixing with their parents and recover the DAG up to transitive closure; moreover, if each node receives one hard intervention, then under a linear latent SCM both the DAG and the latent variables are identifiable (up to scaling)
[
48
, Thm. 16]
. For general latent SCMs under soft interventions,
48
show that intervening on each node at least once suffices to recover the latent variables up to parent mixing. Beyond linear SCMs,
23
establish recovery guarantees for general nonparametric SCMs via score-matching objectives, identifying latents up to componentwise monotone transformations under suitable conditions. In the fully nonparametric measurement setting,
50
provide identifiability guarantees (and impossibility results) showing that element-identifiability in the sense of
Definition

E.1
is the sharp guarantee in general, even when one perfect intervention per node is available.

We refer to
48
for a comprehensive study of identifiability results for multi-domain CRL across linear and nonparametric models and a range of interventional designs. We emphasize, however, that most of the aforementioned CRL identifiability theory does
not
assume the causal graph (or intervention targets) to be known, whereas our approach requires the true causal graph and intervention targets as inputs. This regime is studied in the setting of causal component analysis
[
54
]
, who establish identifiability results for several classes of interventions in the known-graph setting.
