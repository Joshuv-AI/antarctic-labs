Factors influencing postoperative pain catastrophizing in patients with lower limb trauma and development of a nomogram prediction model
Authors: Jia Xie, Yuan Chen, Jingjing Fan, Jing Xu, Zhongbao Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to examine the determinants of postoperative pain catastrophizing among surgical patients with lower limb trauma and to develop a nomogram for individualized risk prediction.MethodsData encompassing demographic characteristics, Injury Severity Score (ISS), preoperative Numerical Rating Scale (NRS) for pain, Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) scores, and Fear-Avoidance Beliefs Questionnaire (FABQ) scores were collected. The level of pain catastrophizing was measured postoperatively using the Pain Catastrophizing Scale (PCS). The total sample of 320 patients was randomly allocated into a training set (n = 224) and a validation set (n = 96) at a 7:3 ratio. Independent factors identified through logistic regression analysis were utilized to construct a nomogram. The model’s predictive performance was assessed via receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis, calibration curves, and decision curve analysis (DCA).ResultsEducational attainment at college level or above served as a protective factor. In contrast, higher scores on the NRS, ISS, HADS, and FABQ were significant risk factors. The nomogram exhibited robust discriminative ability, with area under the curve (AUC) values of 0.850 (95% CI: 0.797–0.904) in the training set and 0.815 (95% CI: 0.718–0.911) in the validation set. Calibration analysis indicated good fit, and DCA confirmed favorable clinical net benefit across a range of threshold probabilities.ConclusionThe incidence of postoperative pain catastrophizing is considerable among patients with lower limb trauma and is influenced by multiple factors including trauma severity, educational level, anxiety/depression, fear-avoidance beliefs, and pain intensity. The constructed nomogram prediction model, based on independent influencing factors, exhibits good discrimination, calibration, and clinical utility, effectively predicting the risk of postoperative pain catastrophizing.

Introduction

Lower extremity traumatic fracture is a mechanical injury characterized by the complete or partial disruption of the cortical continuity of the femur, tibia, fibula, patella, and the bones of the foot and ankle, resulting from high- or low-energy acute external forces and accompanied by localized pain, deformity, and functional impairment. Pathological fractures caused by tumors, metabolic bone diseases, or cumulative stress are excluded [1]. Such injuries often involve fractures of critical weight-bearing bones (e.g., femur, tibia) and concomitant soft tissue damage, with clinical manifestations including limb pain, bony crepitus, deformity or swelling at the fracture site, and in severe cases, motor impairment or joint dislocation. Surgical intervention constitutes the primary treatment approach to restore limb function. However, the postoperative period is frequently accompanied by intense pain. Furthermore, the difficulty some patients experience in coping with this sudden stressor can predispose them to distressing and negative emotional responses [2].

Postoperative pain management strategies focusing solely on alleviating somatic pain while neglecting psychological and cognitive dimensions may inadvertently lead to pain catastrophizing [3]. Sullivan et al. [4] introduced the term pain catastrophizing, referring to an exaggerated negative mental set that arises during actual or anticipated painful experiences and involves negative cognitive as well as affective reactions. Postoperative pain is a common acute pain response following surgical trauma, which not only induces psychological stress in patients but also delays the rehabilitation process to some extent. The Fear-Avoidance Model posits [5] that catastrophic thinking can trigger physiological changes (e.g., functional disability, reduced pain tolerance), emotional distress (e.g., depression, anxiety), and subsequent fear-avoidance behaviors, thereby increasing the risk of adverse pain-related outcomes. Given these detrimental effects, developing an effective predictive model is crucial for early identification of patients at high risk for postoperative pain catastrophizing, guiding clinical interventions, and optimizing trauma care pathways.

Prior research, both domestic and international, has predominantly explored the effects of pain catastrophizing on functional disability [6] and pain intensity [7] within orthopedic populations. Conversely, studies aiming to identify its contributing factors have largely been conducted in cohorts with chronic pain [8]. A significant gap remains in the literature concerning pain catastrophizing specifically within the distinct population of patients with lower limb trauma. This lack of targeted evidence consequently impedes the advancement of refined pain management protocols for this surgical cohort. This study aimed to investigate postoperative pain catastrophizing and its associated factors in this population. The core task involved developing and validating a visual nomogram prediction model. This tool is intended to serve as a practical instrument for the early identification of high-risk patients, laying the groundwork for implementing targeted preventive interventions.

Materials and methods

Study population

Patient data were sourced from the hospital’s electronic medical record system at Nanjing First Hospital, Nanjing Medical University. The retrieved information comprised the following components: We retrospectively collected data from patients who underwent surgical intervention for lower limb trauma at our center between November 2023 and August 2025. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) a radiologically confirmed diagnosis of lower limb trauma requiring surgical intervention; (3) clear consciousness postoperatively and ability to cooperate with all scale assessments. The exclusion criteria were: (1) incomplete clinical data in the hospital electronic medical record system; (2) preoperative use of antidepressants, sedatives, or anxiolytics; (3) pain attributable to cancer or chronic diseases; (4) history of functional or organic mental disorders; (5) postoperative development of severe complications such as thromboembolism or infection; (6) concurrent severe trauma to other body regions; (7) concomitant severe dysfunction of vital organs (e.g., heart, liver, kidney). A total of 320 eligible patients with lower limb trauma were finally included. They were randomly allocated into a training set (n = 224) and a validation set (n = 96) at a ratio of 7:3. This split-sample design was chosen to provide an independent assessment of model generalizability, which is a key requirement for clinical prediction models prior to external validation. The study sample comprised patients aged 23 to 59 years, with a mean age of 42.14 ± 6.81 years. The gender distribution included 199 males (62.19%) and 121 females (37.81%).

Sample size assessment

Sample size adequacy was evaluated using the events per variable (EPV) criterion, which is the most widely accepted standard for logistic regression-based prediction model development [9]. According to this criterion, a minimum of 10 outcome events per candidate predictor variable is required to minimize overfitting and ensure reliable model estimates. The multivariate analysis included five candidate predictors, thus necessitating a minimum of 50 events. In the training set (n = 224), 55 patients (24.55%) exhibited postoperative pain catastrophizing, yielding an EPV of 11 (55 events / 5 variables), which exceeds the recommended minimum threshold of 10 [9, 10]. Therefore, the training set alone has adequate statistical power for model development. The validation set (n = 96), containing 23 events, serves as an independent cohort to assess model performance without further model refitting.

Data collection

Clinical and demographic data

This section included details such as sex, chronological age, educational attainment, relationship status, average monthly earnings per household member, principal guardian, origin of injury, force of impact during trauma, fracture categorization, surgical technique implemented, blood volume lost during operation, length of the procedure, hospitalization period, pain levels experienced, and overall trauma seriousness. Pain intensity was measured using the Numerical Rating Scale (NRS), which employs a 0–10 scale where elevated scores denote more severe pain. Trauma severity was quantified with the Injury Severity Score (ISS), an anatomical scoring system assessing regions such as the head, neck, and upper and lower extremities, with a maximum score of 75 and higher scores reflecting increased severity.

Pain Catastrophizing Scale (PCS)

The Chinese adaptation of the Pain Catastrophizing Scale (PCS), culturally adapted by Yap et al. [11] from the original instrument developed by Sullivan et al. [12], was utilized in this investigation. This 13-item assessment tool evaluates three dimensions: Helplessness (6 items), Magnification (3 items), and Rumination (4 items). Participants rated each item on a 5-point Likert-type scale, with response options ranging from 0 (“not at all”) to 4 (“all the time”), resulting in a possible total score between 0 and 52. Elevated total scores reflect a greater tendency toward pain catastrophizing. The instrument exhibited strong internal consistency in our research (Cronbach’s α = 0.905), aligning with previously documented reliability metrics (Cronbach’s α = 0.927).

All questionnaires were administered by two trained research nurses who were blinded to the patients’ grouping status. Assessments were conducted one week postoperatively via face-to-face interviews in a quiet, private setting within the orthopedic ward. Each patient received standardized verbal instructions prior to completing the questionnaires. For patients with literacy difficulties, the research nurses read each item aloud and recorded the patients’ responses verbatim.

Fear-Avoidance Beliefs Questionnaire (FABQ)

The research employed the Chinese version of FABQ, which was adapted by Lee et al. [13]. This assessment tool comprises 16 questions divided into two distinct subscales: one measuring beliefs about physical activity (FABQ-PA) and another concerning work-related concerns (FABQ-W). Responses to each item are rated on a scale from 0 to 6, producing a total score ranging between 0 and 96, with elevated scores indicating more pronounced fear-avoidance patterns. In this particular investigation, the questionnaire demonstrated solid internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.752), consistent with its previously documented dependability (Cronbach’s α = 0.812).

Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS)

To assess anxiety and depression symptoms, researchers utilized HADS, a tool crafted by Zigmond and Snaith [14]. This assessment comprises two distinct subscales: HADS-A for anxiety and HADS-D for depression, each containing seven items. Participants rated their agreement with statements on a scale from 0 (“completely disagree”) to 3 (“completely agree”). A cutoff score of 8 or higher on either subscale indicates clinically relevant anxiety or depression symptoms. The HADS demonstrated strong internal consistency in this investigation, with Cronbach’s alpha coefficients of 0.917 for the anxiety subscale and 0.849 for the depression subscale. These findings align with previous research that reported reliability measures of 0.922 and 0.855, respectively.

Grouping criteria

The presence of pain catastrophizing was determined based on the PCS score assessed at one week postoperatively, utilizing a threshold of ≥ 38. According to this criterion, patients were subsequently stratified into two groups: the catastrophizing group (PCS score ≥ 38) and the non-catastrophizing group (PCS score < 38).

Statistical analysis

We ran our statistical analysis using SPSS (version 25.0) and R (version 4.4.2) to get the job done. For our continuous data, we showed the results as mean ± standard deviation, while comparing groups with the independent samples t-test. When it came to categorical variables, we presented them as counts (n) and used the chi-square test to see how they stacked up against each other. To get a handle on the relationships between scale scores, we turned to correlation pearson analysis. First, we conducted univariate analysis before diving into multivariate logistic regression to pinpoint factors linked to pain catastrophizing. All variables were examined for multicollinearity using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF). From there, we built a nomogram prediction model based on the independent factors we identified using the training set (n = 224). Model performance was assessed in both the training set (apparent performance) and the validation set (independent assessment of generalizability). To see how well our model performed, we put it through its paces with Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves, calibration curves, and Decision Curve Analysis (DCA) in both the training and validation sets. The Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test was used to evaluate model calibration. The area under the ROC curve (AUC) was calculated with 95% confidence intervals using the DeLong method.

Results

Baseline characteristics: training vs. validation sets

Among all 320 patients, there were 199 males and 121 females, with a mean age of 42.14 ± 6.81 years (range: 23–59). Educational level was categorized as ≤ junior high school (n = 151), high school/secondary school (n = 121), or ≥ college (n = 48). Injury mechanisms included fall (n = 71), crush (n = 30), road traffic accident (n = 174), and fall from height (n = 45). Fracture types comprised calcaneal (n = 20), ankle (n = 35), patellar (n = 39), tibiofibular (n = 50), tibial (n = 164), and pelvic (n = 12) fractures. Patients were randomly assigned to training (n = 224) and validation (n = 96) sets at a 7:3 ratio. Importantly, no statistically significant differences were observed in any baseline characteristic between the training set and the validation set (all P > 0.05; Table 1), confirming that the random assignment successfully resulted in two comparable sets.

Construction of a nomogram prediction model for postoperative pain catastrophizing in lower limb trauma patients

The multivariate logistic regression analysis identified educational level, NRS, ISS, HADS, and FABQ scores as significant predictors. A nomogram integrating these factors was subsequently built, producing a total score of 0–280 that maps onto a prediction probability range of 0.1–0.9 (Fig. 1).

Performance analysis of the nomogram prediction model for postoperative pain catastrophizing in lower limb trauma patients

The ROC results revealed (see Fig. 2) that the AUC (95% CI) of the nomogram prediction model was 0.850 (0.797–0.904) for the training set and 0.815 (0.718–0.911) for the validation set, with sensitivities of 0.909 and 0.826, and specificities of 0.669 and 0.589, respectively. The consistent performance between the training and validation sets indicates good model generalizability.When put to the test across various risk cutoff points, the model really proved its worth in DCA, depicted in Fig. 3, by consistently delivering a net benefit above zero—a clear win over both the blanket treatment and no-treatment approaches. The calibration curves, illustrated in Fig. 4, painted a pretty picture, showing solid alignment with the ideal reference line. Finally, the Hosmer-Lemeshow test results gave the thumbs-up to the model’s fit, with χ² values of 12.089 (P = 0.147) for the training set and 2.721 (P = 0.951) for the validation set, indicating no statistically significant lack of fit.

Interpretability analysis of the prediction model based on SHAP

Discussion

The Cognitive-Behavioral Model of Pain posits that the dynamic interplay among cognitive, behavioral, and emotional dimensions is pivotal in influencing or exacerbating a patient’s pain experience [15, 16]. The first week following lower limb trauma surgery represents a critical period for both pain management and rehabilitation initiation. During this phase, patients are often preparing for or have just undergone discharge. While baseline pain may be partially alleviated by targeted analgesic strategies, incidental acute pain during activity, persistent dull discomfort at the surgical site, and uncertainty regarding recovery prospects may collectively heighten the perception of pain catastrophizing. In addition, patients with low pain threshold are more sensitive to nociceptive stimulation and more likely to develop pain catastrophizing, while those with a high pain threshold have a relatively lower risk [17].

The findings of this study revealed that among the 320 lower limb trauma patients, the mean PCS score one week postoperatively was 30.18 ± 8.08. Pain catastrophizing was identified in 78 patients, yielding an incidence of 24.38%. This rate closely aligns with the 21% incidence reported by Archer et al. in a similar population [18], underscoring a substantial risk for postoperative pain catastrophizing in this patient group. Thus, to facilitate early prediction and targeted interventions for improved outcomes, this study analyzed data from 320 patients, identifying independent influencing factors for pain catastrophizing and constructing a corresponding nomogram prediction model. The use of a split-sample (7:3) validation strategy allowed for an independent assessment of model generalizability, which is a key strength of this study. The validation set, which was not involved in model development, provided a conservative estimate of the model’s performance in unseen data.

The univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses in this study identified a college degree or higher as a protective factor against postoperative pain catastrophizing in lower limb trauma patients, while NRS, ISS, HADS, and FABQ scores were identified as risk factors. The underlying reasons for these associations can be analyzed as follows:

Educational Level: Consistent with Wilk et al. [19], higher education was inversely associated with pain catastrophizing. Education shapes illness conceptualization and somatic symptom appraisal. Patients with lower education often exhibit weaker self-regulation and pain awareness, struggle with initial self-care after trauma, and adopt more negative attitudes, leading to higher postoperative catastrophizing. Conversely, highly educated patients leverage diverse resources to acquire disease knowledge, fostering adaptive attitudes and confidence, thereby reducing catastrophic thinking [20]. Clinicians should therefore exercise greater patience with less-educated patients and employ multimodal education (e.g., ward broadcasts, one-on-one sessions) to enhance pain understanding and mitigate postoperative catastrophizing.

Pain Intensity: Preoperative NRS scores were directly associated with PCS, confirming NRS as a predictor of postoperative pain catastrophizing. Higher preoperative pain likely promotes maladaptive coping (rumination, magnification, helplessness), consistent with pain psychology theory, and impairs sleep, leading to fatigue that adversely affects pain threshold, pain perception, and emotional regulation [21]. For lower limb trauma patients with severe preoperative pain, analgesic treatment (e.g., NSAIDs, biologics, glucocorticoids) under medical guidance is recommended, along with close psychological monitoring. Integrating pharmacotherapy with psychological interventions such as cognitive-behavioral therapy may help mitigate pain catastrophizing.

Trauma Severity: ISS is a classical scale for assessing trauma severity, with higher scores indicating more extensive bodily injury and greater trauma severity [22]. Severe trauma not only induces more persistent and intense pain but also triggers psychological stress responses in patients, fostering negative emotions such as fear of death or limb loss. Under the combined physiological and psychological stress, the body’s sensitivity to pain can become significantly heightened, predisposing patients to excessive focus on and catastrophic interpretation of painful stimuli.

Anxiety and Depressive Symptoms: Preoperative anxiety may induce stress hormone release (e.g., adrenaline), amplifying pain perception. Anxious patients tend to catastrophize anticipated surgical pain, interpreting it as a persistent, severe threat that heightens helplessness and fosters a pain-catastrophizing pattern [23, 24]. Concurrently, anxiety and depression are associated with cognitive distortions (e.g., extreme thinking, overgeneralization) that exacerbate pain catastrophizing [25]. Thus, interventions such as cognitive-behavioral therapy and patient support groups are recommended to mitigate preoperative distress and reduce postoperative pain catastrophizing.

Fear-Avoidance Beliefs: Research by Monticone et al. [26] reported a moderate positive correlation between FABQ scores and PCS scores, which is consistent with our findings. Fear-avoidance beliefs refer to excessive worry and negative cognitions that patients develop towards painful or other aversive stimuli. These beliefs can trap patients in a vicious cycle of “pain-fear-avoidance.” Fear stemming from pain leads to the avoidance of physical activities, including rehabilitation exercises, thereby increasing the risk of postoperative complications such as joint stiffness and muscle atrophy. This, in turn, can intensify both the pain experience and the level of pain catastrophizing. Additionally, fear-avoidance beliefs contribute to the persistence of anxiety, directing the patient’s attention towards potential pain-related cues in the environment and subsequently worsening the pain experience [27]. Hence, healthcare providers should proactively communicate with patients, provide examples of successful surgical treatments and postoperative rehabilitation, offer positive encouragement, and enhance patients’ self-confidence to mitigate fear-avoidance beliefs.

The interpretability analysis based on SHAP plots revealed that the overall impact of the included variables on the prediction model, in descending order, was: FABQ, HADS, ISS, Educational Level, and NRS. Among these, educational level exerted a negative predictive effect, whereas FABQ, HADS, ISS, and NRS scores served as positive predictors, which is consistent with the results of the Logistic regression model. Incorporating the five independent predictors, the nomogram exhibited favorable performance in training and validation. This included discriminative capacity (AUC: 0.850 and 0.815, respectively), was well-calibrated (Hosmer-Lemeshow test P > 0.05), and translated into considerable clinical net benefit over wide risk thresholds. Furthermore, Bootstrap validation confirmed the model’s stability.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several notable strengths. First, it addresses a significant gap in the literature by specifically investigating pain catastrophizing within the distinct population of patients with lower limb trauma, an area that has been relatively underexplored compared to chronic pain cohorts. Second, the development of a visual nomogram integrated with SHAP-based interpretability analysis provides clinicians with both a practical risk prediction tool and transparent insights into the model’s decision-making process. Third, the inclusion of multiple validated psychological assessment instruments (PCS, HADS, FABQ) enables a comprehensive evaluation of the biopsychosocial factors contributing to pain catastrophizing. Fourth, the split-sample validation strategy (7:3 training-validation split) provides an independent assessment of model generalizability. The training set EPV of 11 meets the recommended minimum, ensuring adequate statistical power for model development, while the validation set (n = 96, 23 events) serves as a conservative test of model performance on unseen data.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this investigation was confined to a single institution and relied on retrospective data with a modest participant pool, which may introduce selection bias. Therefore, these findings require validation in larger, prospective studies spanning multiple medical centers. Second, while split-sample validation is transparent and provides an independent performance estimate, it is less efficient than bootstrap validation in small samples. Future studies with larger cohorts could employ k-fold cross-validation or external validation to further confirm model robustness. Third, pain threshold was not formally evaluated, and its impact on pain catastrophizing warrants further exploration. Fourth, the lack of extended follow-up data precluded assessment of the model’s ability to predict long-term outcomes such as persistent pain, functional improvement, and overall quality of life. Future research should incorporate longer observation periods to address these knowledge gaps and refine the predictive model.

Conclusion

Postoperative pain catastrophizing represents a significant clinical issue among patients sustaining lower limb trauma. Its development is multifactorial, being associated with trauma severity, educational attainment, anxiety/depressive symptoms, fear-avoidance beliefs, and pain intensity. The nomogram prediction model, developed from these independent factors, exhibits favorable discrimination, calibration, and clinical usefulness. As such, it may provide a promising instrument for risk prediction, fostering early high-risk detection and personalized intervention design grounded in evidence, thereby enhancing coordinated trauma and rehabilitation protocols.
