ROR2 preserves alveolar bone homeostasis by orchestrating IRAK4-dependent suppression of CXCL8-driven osteolysis
Authors: Hong-cheng Xing, Chen-yang Qiu, Xiao-yang Chu, Jia-hao Guo, Yang Zeng, Shi-yi Liu, Yan-hong Zhou, Ying-yi Luan, Zhan Yang, Kai Yang, Dong-liang Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlveolar bone resorption, driven by imbalanced osteogenesis and osteolysis, is a hallmark of periodontitis and orthodontic complications, yet molecular mechanisms coordinating stromal-immune crosstalk remain elusive. Here, we identify receptor tyrosine kinase-like orphan receptor 2 (ROR2) in alveolar bone-derived mesenchymal stem cells (ABMSCs) as a critical suppressor of inflammatory osteolysis. Using conditional knockout mice, we demonstrate that ROR2 deficiency in ABMSCs exacerbates alveolar bone loss by amplifying CXCL8 secretion, which enhances osteoclastogenesis while impairing osteogenic differentiation. Mechanistically, ROR2 sequesters interleukin-1 receptor-associated kinase 4 (IRAK4) via its Ig-like C2 domain, disrupting Myddosome assembly and downstream IRF7-driven CXCL8 transcription. Genetic ablation of IRAK4 in ROR2-deficient ABMSCs rescues pathological bone remodeling, restoring osteoblast-osteoclast equilibrium. Building on these findings, we engineer a thermosensitive chitosan/β-glycerophosphate hydrogel loaded with CXCL8-neutralizing antibodies, which effectively attenuates periodontitis-induced bone loss in vivo by suppressing osteoclast coverage and preserving osteogenic capacity. Our work unveils a ROR2/IRAK4/CXCL8 regulatory axis in ABMSCs that governs stromal-immune crosstalk during inflammatory osteolysis, offering a translatable strategy to recalibrate bone homeostasis in periodontal and orthodontic disorders.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12964-026-03011-x.Keywords: ROR2, IRAK4, CXCL8, ABMSCs, Alveolar bone resorption

Introduction

Alveolar bone resorption, a pathological process characterized by the progressive loss of bone supporting teeth, is a hallmark of periodontitis, a disease affecting over 50% of adults worldwide and significantly impairing oral health and quality of life [1–3]. Current therapeutic strategies, including surgical bone grafting and anti-inflammatory agents, remain limited by their inability to reconcile the paradoxical demands of enhancing osteogenesis while suppressing pathological osteoclast coverage [4, 5]. This unresolved challenge underscores the urgent need to elucidate molecular mechanisms governing alveolar bone homeostasis under inflammatory stress.

Alveolar bone-derived mesenchymal stem cells (ABMSCs), which exhibit robust osteogenic and immunomodulatory capacities, are pivotal regulators of bone remodeling [6, 7]. Emerging evidence highlights their dual role in promoting bone regeneration and inhibiting inflammation-driven osteolysis, positioning ABMSCs as therapeutic targets for alveolar bone repair [8]. However, the molecular pathways directing ABMSC-mediated bone remodeling remain poorly defined. Recent studies implicate receptor tyrosine kinase-like orphan receptor 2 (ROR2) as a critical modulator of stem cell differentiation and bone metabolism [9, 10]. Our prior studies revealed that ROR2 safeguards dental pulp stem cells (DPSCs) against senescence by stabilizing sphingomyelin biosynthesis and suppressing p21-mediated cell cycle arrest, and that ROR2 deficiency accelerates DPSC aging via the MSX2/NSUN2 axis [11, 12]. Notably, ROR2 also modulates osteogenic differentiation in bone marrow MSCs and osteoclast precursors, suggesting its broader role in skeletal homeostasis. However, the functional significance of ROR2 in ABMSCs—particularly its capacity to coordinate osteogenic-osteoclastic crosstalk under inflammatory stress—remains poorly understood.

Interleukin-1 receptor-associated kinase 4 (IRAK4), a serine/threonine kinase and essential component of the Myddosome complex, orchestrates innate immune signaling to drive pro-inflammatory cytokine production [13, 14]. Structural studies reveal that IRAK4 regulates the hierarchical assembly of the MyD88:IRAK4:IRAK2 helical oligomer, which amplifies TLR/IL-1R signals critical for host defense and inflammatory diseases [14]. Beyond its role in immune cells, IRAK4 directly influences bone homeostasis by promoting inflammatory osteolysis through osteoclast activation [15, 16]. Besides, IRAK4-mediated phosphorylation of IRF3/5 enhances osteolytic factor expression, exacerbating alveolar bone resorption in response to bacterial pathogens [17]. Despite these advances, the role of IRAK4 in alveolar bone-derived MSCs (ABMSCs) remains poorly defined, and its interplay with ROR2 in regulating inflammatory bone remodeling has not been explored, hindering the development of targeted therapies to balance osteogenesis and inflammation-driven resorption.

Here, we identify CXCL8 as a pivotal mediator of ROR2-dependent bone remodeling. We demonstrate that ROR2 suppresses CXCL8 expression by sequestering IRAK4, thereby blocking Myddosome assembly and downstream IRF7-driven transcriptional activation. Furthermore, genetic ablation of IRAK4 in ROR2-deficient ABMSCs rescues pathological 0bone loss, highlighting the therapeutic potential of targeting this axis. Finally, we engineer a thermosensitive hydrogel for local CXCL8 neutralization to ameliorate periodontitis-induced bone loss in vivo. Our work thus elucidates a previously unrecognized ROR2/IRAK4/CXCL8 regulatory network in ABMSCs and provides new insights into restoring bone homeostasis by modulating stromal-immune crosstalk in inflammatory osteolysis, with potential implications for periodontitis and related bone disorders.

Results

Lack of ROR2 in ABMSCs accelerates alveolar bone loss

To investigate its role in alveolar bone remodeling, we first detected ROR2 expression in clinical samples of normal and resorbed alveolar bone tissues. As shown in Fig. 1A, ROR2 expression was significantly decreased in the resorbed alveolar bone tissues compared to normal alveolar bone tissues. Emerging studies have shown that mesenchymal stem cells play a crucial role in alveolar bone resorption derive from periodontitis [18, 19]. In order to explore whether ROR2 play its function via alveolar bone derived mesenchymal stem cells (ABMSCs), we constructed a MSCs specific deletion of ROR2 (ROR2fl/fl;Prx1:Cre) in a transgenic mouse line (Fig. 1B). Then, the specific deletion of ROR2 was examined in alveolar bone, pulp and skin tissues by RT-qPCR. The results showed that the expression of ROR2 was significant reduced in alveolar bone, pulp tissues of Cre positive mice compared with those of Cre negative mice. Notably, comparative analysis revealed no significant differences in cellular features such as cell size, shape, or stem cell specific marker between genotypes, confirming that ROR2 deficiency does not intrinsically alter ABMSC characteristics under basal culture conditions (Fig. S1B, C). To induce alveolar bone resorption or osteolysis, the mice were subjected to experimental periodontitis (EP). Micro-CT scanning and 3D reconstructed images show the destruction of the alveolar bone and resorption at the root bifurcation after ligation (Fig. 1D, E), which indicates the inflammatory process of the periodontitis model. Additionally, measurement of the distance between the cementoenamel junction (CEJ) and the alveolar bone crest (ABC), which is an indicator of alveolar bone destruction, consistently confirm that ROR2-deletion enhanced the alveolar bone loss from inflammatory disease (Fig. 1F). Further analysis of bone mass around second molar regions also shows lesser trabecular bone volume (BV/TV) bone mineral density (BMD) and trabecular thickness (Tb.Th) in ROR2fl/fl;Prx1:Cre+ mice (Fig. 1G-I). In addition, microCT analysis revealed significantly reduced trabecular number (Tb.N) and increased trabecular separation (Tb.Sp) in ROR2-deficient mice compared to Cre− controls, along with an elevated bone surface-to-bone volume ratio (BS/BV), indicating a sparser and more disconnected trabecular network (Fig. S2A-C). We also evaluated the serum bone turnover markers and found high levels of CTX after EP induction in ROR2fl/fl;Prx1:Cre− mice and these would further increase in ROR2fl/fl;Prx1:Cre+ animals (Fig. 1J). These findings revealed that deletion of ROR2 in ABMSCs promotes periodontal alveolar bone absorption.

Depletion of ROR2 in ABMSCs inhibits alveolar bone remodeling during periodontitis

To elucidate the functional significance of ROR2 in alveolar bone maintenance during periodontitis, we performed comprehensive morphological analyses of hemimaxillae using an experimental periodontitis (EP) model. Quantitative assessment of H&E-stained sections demonstrated significant exacerbation of alveolar bone resorption in EP-challenged mice compared to controls. Genetic ablation of ROR2 in mesenchymal stem cells (ROR2fl/fl;Prx1:Cre+) resulted in a increase in bone loss area relative to Prx1:Cre−littermate controls (Fig. 2A and B), indicating that the absence of ROR2 increased the impairment of bone homeostasis. To dissect the cellular mechanisms underlying this phenotype, we quantitatively evaluated osteoclastogenesis and osteogenic activity in periodontal tissues. TRAP staining revealed a expansion of osteoclast-rich regions in EP-treated mice, with ROR2-deficient animals exhibiting greater TRAP-positive area than Cre−controls (Fig. 2C and D). Quantitative histomorphometry confirmed enhanced osteoclast presence in mutant mice, showing both increased osteoclast number per mm2 bone surface (N.Oc/BS) and increased osteoclast surface per bone surface (Oc.S/BS) compared to Cre− controls (Fig. 2E and F). Concurrent evaluation of osteogenic potential through BMP2 immunofluorescence demonstrated progressive impairment of bone formation capacity. While EP challenge reduced BMP2 expression versus healthy controls, ROR2-deficient mice showed an additional decrease in this osteogenic marker (Fig. 2G and H). Because BMP2 can be expressed by multiple cell types, we also quantified more specific osteoblast markers. Molecular profiling revealed coordinated downregulation of key osteogenic regulators in mutant mice, with COL1A1, RUNX2, and ALP mRNA levels reduced compared to wild-type littermates (Fig. 2I). These findings establish that ROR2 deficiency creates a pathological imbalance in bone remodeling, characterized by enhancing osteoclastogenesis and suppressing osteoblast differentiation.

CXCL8 mediates ROR2-regulated inflammatory signaling and osteogenic differentiation

To investigate the mechanistic role of ROR2 in alveolar bone remodeling, we established a co-culture system of RAW264.7 macrophages and ABMSCs modeling osteoclastogenesis. To test the functional capacity of ABMSCs to regulate osteoclast formation, we co-cultured them with RAW264.7 macrophages in the presence of RANKL and M-CSF and quantified TRAP-positive multinucleated cells (MNCs). ABMSCs from Cre- (ROR2-intact) mice markedly suppressed the formation of TRAP-positive MNCs compared to the negative control, in which RAW264.7 cells were stimulated in monoculture. In contrast, ROR2-deficient ABMSCs (Cre+) largely lost this inhibitory capability, yielding significantly more and larger TRAP-positive MNCs (Fig. 3A-C). Similar results were obtained using primary bone marrow‑derived macrophages (BMDMs; Fig. S3A). Strikingly, ROR2 deletion in ABMSCs reversed this inhibitory effect, significantly increasing TRAP-positive MNCs formation under identical conditions. To identify potential paracrine mediators of this regulatory mechanism, we performed cytokine array analysis of ABMSC-conditioned media. ROR2-deficient ABMSCs exhibited elevated secretion of inflammatory cytokines including CXCL5, CXCL8, MCP-3, and DKK-1, coupled with reduced hepatocyte growth factor (HGF) levels (Fig. 3D). Among these, CXCL8 showed the most pronounced differential expression, a finding subsequently confirmed by quantitative ELISA (Fig. 3E). Consistent with these in vitro findings, ligation-induced EP significantly elevated the protein levels of CXCL5, CXCL8 and DKK1 (determined by ELISA in alveolar bone lysates and normalised to total protein) in alveolar bone tissue, with ROR2fl/fl;Prx1:Cre+ mice demonstrating amplified induction of these inflammatory factors (Fig. 3F). Functional validation revealed that CXCL8 neutralization significantly attenuated TRAP-positive MNCs formation in co-culture systems, irrespective of ROR2 status (Fig. 3G and H). Conversely, CXCL8 supplementation impaired osteogenic differentiation capacity in ABMSCs, as evidenced by reduced Alizarin Red S staining intensity— an effect exacerbated in ROR2-deficient cells (Fig. 3I and J). Molecular analysis demonstrated CXCL8-mediated downregulation of osteogenic markers (BMP2, RUNX2, ALP) and concomitant upregulation of TRAP, with ROR2 knockout amplifying these transcriptional changes (Fig. S3B). Collectively, our findings establish CXCL8 as a critical inflammatory mediator through which ROR2 signaling coordinates osteogenic differentiation and osteoclast regulation during alveolar bone remodeling.

IRAK4 interacts with ROR2 and is involved in CXCL8 expression

To investigate how ROR2 regulates CXCL8 expression, we performed co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) coupled with mass spectrometry in TNF-α-stimulated ABMSCs using ROR2-specific antibodies. Silver staining of the immunoprecipitates resolved on SDS-PAGE revealed several bands enriched in the ROR2 pulldown compared to the IgG control (Fig. 4A, left). Mass spectrometric analysis of these bands identified KRT1, ALB, KRT10, and IRAK4 as specifically interacting proteins (Fig. 4A, right table). To identify functional contributors to ROR2-mediated CXCL8 regulation, we co-transfected ABMSCs with shROR2 and siRNAs targeting each of the four candidate proteins (KRT1, ALB, KRT10, IRAK4), and measured CXCL8 mRNA by RT-qPCR. Strikingly, IRAK4 knockdown alone significantly reduced CXCL8 expression, while combined ROR2 and IRAK4 silencing partially rescued the CXCL8 upregulation induced by ROR2 deficiency (Fig. 4B). Validation experiments confirmed that TNF-α stimulation robustly promoted ROR2-IRAK4 complex formation (Fig. 4C). Western blot analysis revealed that ROR2 knockdown substantially increased CXCL8 protein levels, an effect largely abolished by concurrent IRAK4 depletion (Fig. 4D and E). Consistent results were obtained in CXCL8 secretion assays (Fig. 4F). Complementary gain-of-function experiments demonstrated that IRAK4 overexpression dramatically enhanced CXCL8 expression and secretion, with ROR2 deficiency further amplifying this effect (Fig. 4G and H). To determine the specific binding domain that mediates the interaction between ROR2 and IRAK4, we generated a series of ROR2 domain-specific mutants: wild-type ROR2, Ig-like C2-type domain deletion mutant (mut1) and an Ig-like C2-type domain-only construct (mut2). Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) analysis revealed that the Ig-like C2-type domain serves as the critical region responsible for ROR2-IRAK4 interaction (Fig. 4J). Functional experiments confirmed that wild-type ROR2, as well as the Ig-like C2-type domain-only construct (mut2), effectively suppressed CXCL8 expression in ROR2-null ABMSCs, whereas the Ig-like C2-type domain-deficient mutant (mut1) failed to do so. Moreover, mut1 ROR2 was unable to counteract TNF-α-induced CXCL8 upregulation (Fig. 4K and L). Similar results were obtained in CXCL8 secretion assays (Fig. 4M). These findings establish IRAK4 as a key ROR2-interacting partner mediating CXCL8 regulation, and demonstrate that the Ig-like C2-type domain is both necessary and sufficient for ROR2-dependent suppression of CXCL8 expression.

IRAK4 deficiency in ABMSCs inhibits periodontitis-induced alveolar bone resorption

To investigate whether IRAK4 participates in ROR2-regulated osteogenesis, we co-cultured ABMSCs lacking ROR2, IRAK4, or both with RAW264.7 macrophages to model osteoclastogenesis. Results demonstrated that IRAK4 deficiency alone significantly suppressed TRAP-positive MNCs formation, whereas combined ROR2 and IRAK4 depletion partially reversed this suppression, yielding MNCs numbers intermediate between the shIRAK4-only and shROR2-only groups (Fig. 5A-C). This epistatic relationship supports a model in which ROR2 restrains osteoclastogenesis primarily through IRAK4-dependent signalling. To validate the in vivo role of IRAK4 in ROR2-dependent alveolar bone remodeling, we generated mice with double conditional knockout of ROR2 and IRAK4 in ABMSCs (ROR2fl/fl; IRAK4fl/fl; Prx1:Cre) (Fig. 5D). Following experimental periodontitis (EP) induction, micro-CT scanning and 3D reconstructions revealed that IRAK4 deficiency markedly attenuated ligation-induced alveolar bone destruction and resorption at root bifurcation sites compared to wild-type controls. Notably, dual ablation of ROR2 and IRAK4 resulted in milder alveolar bone loss than ROR2 single knockout (Fig. 5E and F). Quantitative assessment of the cementoenamel junction (CEJ) to alveolar bone crest (ABC) distance consistently confirmed that IRAK4 deletion mitigated inflammation-driven alveolar bone loss (Fig. 5G). Furthermore, analysis of bone mass in second molar regions demonstrated higher trabecular bone volume (BV/TV), bone mineral density (BMD), and trabecular thickness (Tb.Th) in IRAK4-deficient mice compared to wild-type controls. Importantly, IRAK4 deficiency partially rescued the reductions in these parameters caused by ROR2 knockout (Fig. 5H–J). Additional morphometric analysis showed that IRAK4 deficiency preserved trabecular number (Tb.N) and attenuated the increase in trabecular separation (Tb.Sp) and BS/BV induced by ROR2 ablation, further supporting a restoration of trabecular architecture (Fig. S4A–C). Serum bone turnover marker analysis revealed lower CTX levels in IRAK4-deficient mice post-EP induction, with IRAK4 ablation suppressing the elevated CTX levels observed in ROR2-deficient animals (Fig. 5K). Additionally, evaluation of inflammatory cytokines in alveolar bone tissues (same samples as used for micro-CT) showed reduced expression of CXCL8, CXCL5, DKK1, and MMP9, alongside increased HGF levels in IRAK4-deficient mice (Fig. 5L). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that IRAK4 deficiency inhibits periodontitis-induced alveolar bone resorption and partially rescues the pathological bone loss caused by ROR2 ablation.

IRAK4 deficiency mitigates pathological bone remodeling in ROR2-deficient mice

To investigate whether IRAK4 mediates ROR2-regulated alveolar bone remodeling, we performed morphological assessments of hemimaxillae under periodontitis conditions. Compared to wild-type controls, IRAK4 deficiency alone significantly reduced the bone loss area in mice. Notably, combined ablation of IRAK4 and ROR2 in ABMSCs partially rescued the ROR2 knockout-induced bone loss (Fig. 6A and B). Subsequently, we comprehensively assessed osteoclastogenesis and osteogenic activity in periodontal tissues. TRAP staining revealed a marked reduction in TRAP-positive areas in IRAK4-deficient animals compared to wild-type controls. Furthermore, IRAK4 deletion substantially attenuated the ROR2 deficiency-associated expansion of TRAP-positive regions (Fig. 6C and D). Quantitative histomorphometric analysis confirmed diminished osteoclast coverage in IRAK4-deficient mice, demonstrating fewer active osteoclasts/mm² bone surface. Dual knockout of IRAK4 and ROR2 in ABMSCs resulted in significantly lower osteoclast coverage and reduced osteoclasts/mm² bone surface compared to ROR2 single knockout mice (Fig. 6E and F). Concurrent evaluation of osteogenic potential via BMP2 immunofluorescence demonstrated progressive impairment of bone formation. While IRAK4 deficiency alone enhanced BMP2 expression compared to wild-type controls, combined IRAK4 and ROR2 deletion reversed the BMP2 downregulation caused by ROR2 ablation (Fig. 6G and H). To confirm these findings with more osteoblast-specific markers, molecular profiling of osteogenic markers revealed significant upregulation of key regulators (COL1A1, RUNX2, ALP) in IRAK4-deficient mice relative to wild-type controls. Importantly, IRAK4 knockout partially counteracted the ROR2 deficiency-induced suppression of these osteogenic factors (Fig. 6I). These findings collectively demonstrate that IRAK4 deficiency mitigates the pathological imbalance in bone remodeling caused by ROR2 ablation, partially restoring osteogenic capacity and suppressing excessive osteoclastogenesis.

ROR2 suppresses CXCL8 expression by disrupting myddosome assembly

To investigate whether ROR2 modulates alveolar bone remodeling by interfering with Myddosome assembly, we first performed co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays. As shown in Fig. 7A, ROR2 knockdown in ABMSCs significantly enhanced pairwise interactions among IRAK4, MYD88, and IRAK2, without altering their protein expression levels. To evaluate whether inflammatory stimulation influences ROR2-mediated regulation of Myddosome assembly, ABMSCs were subjected to TNF-α treatment and ROR2 overexpression. TNF-α stimulation robustly increased IRAK4 and IRAK2 expression and promoted Myddosome assembly. Notably, ROR2 overexpression alone markedly reduced pairwise interactions between IRAK4, MYD88, and IRAK2, and effectively attenuated TNF-α-induced Myddosome assembly (Fig. 7B and C). In contrast, expression of a mutant ROR2 construct (Ig-like C2-type domain deletion mutant) failed to disrupt these interactions (Fig. 7C).

To determine whether ROR2 and Myddosome components cooperatively regulate CXCL8 expression, we rescued ROR2-knockdown cells with wild-type or mutant ROR2 and challenged them with TNF-α. Wild-type ROR2 overexpression nearly abolished TNF-α-induced CXCL8 expression but had no effect on DKK1 upregulation, whereas mutant ROR2 failed to suppress CXCL8 induction (Fig. 7D). Consistent results were observed in CXCL8 secretion assays (Fig. 7E). These findings demonstrate that ROR2 inhibits CXCL8 expression by disrupting Myddosome assembly through its interaction with IRAK4 (Fig. 7F).

IRF7 mediates ROR2-regulated CXCL8 expression and bone remodeling

To delineate the pathway through which ROR2-regulated Myddosome assembly drives inflammatory signaling, we transfected ABMSCs with shROR2 and simultaneously knocked down key inflammatory transcription factors (p50, p65, IRF3, IRF5, IRF7, and AP-1). The knockdown efficiency of each siRNA was confirmed by RT-qPCR and Western blot (Fig. S5A, S5B). Strikingly, shROR2 transfection alone significantly elevated CXCL8 mRNA expression, but concurrent IRF7 knockdown abolished this effect (Fig. 8A), suggesting IRF7 may mediate ROR2-dependent CXCL8 regulation. To validate this, we assessed CXCL8 secretion in ABMSCs following individual or combined ROR2 and IRF7 knockdown. IRF7 depletion alone markedly reduced extracellular CXCL8 levels, while dual ROR2/IRF7 knockdown partially rescued the ROR2 deficiency-induced CXCL8 upregulation (Fig. 8B). TNF-α stimulation robustly enhanced intracellular CXCL8 expression, an effect significantly attenuated by IRF7 knockdown (Fig. 8C).

To investigate whether ROR2 modulates IRF7 phosphorylation, we overexpressed ROR2 in TNF-α-stimulated ABMSCs. TNF-α increased both phosphorylated and total IRF7 levels, but ROR2 overexpression selectively suppressed IRF7 phosphorylation without altering total protein levels (Fig. 8D). Conversely, ROR2 knockdown amplified TNF-α-induced IRF7 phosphorylation (Fig. 8E).

Bioinformatic analysis identified putative IRF7-binding motifs in the CXCL8 promoter (Fig. 8F). Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)-PCR confirmed IRF7 binding to the CXCL8 promoter (Fig. 8G). Luciferase reporter assays demonstrated that TNF-α treatment and ROR2 knockdown enhanced CXCL8 promoter activity, whereas IRF7 depletion suppressed it (Fig. 8H), establishing IRF7 as a direct transcriptional activator of CXCL8.

Finally, we examined whether IRF7 mediates ROR2-regulated bone remodeling. Co-culture of IRF7-deficient ABMSCs with RAW264.7 macrophages markedly reduced TRAP-positive MNCs formation and attenuated the ROR2 knockdown-driven increase in osteoclastogenesis (Fig. 8I and J). Together, these findings demonstrate that IRF7 acts downstream of ROR2 to regulate CXCL8 transcription and bone remodeling via phosphorylation-dependent mechanisms.

Therapeutic targeting of the ROR2/IRAK4/CXCL8 axis ameliorates periodontitis-induced alveolar bone resorption

To investigate the role of the ROR2/IRAK4/CXCL8 regulatory axis in periodontitis-driven alveolar bone resorption, we developed a thermosensitive hydrogel-based sustained-release system (CS/β-GP-anti-CXCL8) by incorporating CXCL8-specific antibodies with chitosan and β-glycerophosphate [20, 21]. A rat ligature-induced periodontitis model was established to evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of CS/β-GP-anti-CXCL8. Experimental periodontitis was induced by ligating the left maxillary second molar, followed by three weeks of local hydrogel injections initiated one week post-ligation (Fig. 9A). Micro-CT scanning and 3D reconstructions revealed significant alveolar bone destruction and root bifurcation resorption in ligated animals, confirming the inflammatory progression of the periodontitis model. Compared to CS/β-GP controls, CS/β-GP-anti-CXCL8 treatment markedly restored alveolar bone height and reduced bone resorption (Fig. 9B and C). Quantitative assessment of the cementoenamel junction (CEJ) to alveolar bone crest (ABC) distance—a key metric of alveolar bone destruction—further demonstrated that CS/β-GP-anti-CXCL8 effectively mitigated inflammation-induced bone loss (Fig. 9D). Analysis of bone mass in the second molar region revealed significant increases in trabecular bone volume (BV/TV), bone mineral density (BMD), and trabecular thickness (Tb.Th) in CS/β-GP-anti-CXCL8-treated animals, indicating preserved bone structural integrity (Fig. 9E-G). Consistent with these findings, treated animals also exhibited higher trabecular number (Tb.N), lower trabecular separation (Tb.Sp), and reduced BS/BV compared to CS/β‑GP controls, collectively reflecting a denser and more connected trabecular network (Fig. S6A–C). Serum bone turnover marker analysis showed a pronounced reduction in CTX levels following CS/β-GP-anti-CXCL8 administration (Fig. 9H).

Morphological evaluation of hemimaxillae under periodontitis conditions via hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining demonstrated enhanced bone surface area in CS/β-GP-anti-CXCL8-treated mice compared to controls (Fig. 9I and J). TRAP staining revealed a significant decrease in osteoclast-rich TRAP-positive regions in the treatment group (Fig. 9K and L). Quantitative histomorphometric analysis confirmed reduced osteoclast coverage, with fewer active osteoclasts per mm² bone surface in CS/β-GP-anti-CXCL8-treated mice (Fig. 9M). Molecular profiling of osteogenic markers demonstrated upregulated expression of key regulators (COL1A1, RUNX2, ALP) in the treatment group relative to CS/β-GP controls (Fig. 9N). Collectively, these findings indicate that neutralizing CXCL8 via the CS/β-GP-anti-CXCL8 hydrogel alleviates pathological imbalances in periodontitis-associated bone remodeling, suppressing excessive osteoclastogenesis while partially restoring osteogenic capacity.

Discussion

Alveolar bone remodeling is a dynamic equilibrium between osteogenesis and osteolysis, orchestrated by intricate stromal-immune crosstalk. Our study unveils alveolar bone-derived mesenchymal stem cells (ABMSCs) as central regulators of this process, with ROR2 serving as a critical molecular switch that restrains inflammatory osteolysis by modulating IRAK4-dependent signaling (Fig. 10). By integrating genetic, molecular, and therapeutic approaches, we demonstrate that ROR2 deficiency in ABMSCs disrupts bone homeostasis, while targeting the ROR2/IRAK4/CXCL8 axis offers a promising strategy to mitigate pathological bone resorption in periodontitis.

Graphic Abstract. Schematic model of the ROR2/IRAK4/CXCL8 regulatory axis in alveolar bone homeostasis. ROR2 in ABMSCs sequesters IRAK4 via its Ig-like C2 domain, disrupting Myddosome assembly and suppressing IRF7-driven CXCL8 transcription. ROR2 deficiency liberates IRAK4, promoting osteoclastogenesis and impairing osteogenesis. CXCL8 neutralization via a CS/β-GP hydrogel restores bone homeostasis

ABMSCs exhibit unique immunomodulatory and osteogenic capacities, positioning them as pivotal mediators of bone remodeling under inflammatory stress [22]. Our findings reveal that ROR2-deficient ABMSCs lose their ability to suppress osteoclastogenesis while exhibiting impaired osteogenic differentiation, leading to exacerbated alveolar bone loss in experimental periodontitis. This dual regulatory role aligns with prior reports highlighting ABMSCs’ capacity to secrete anti-inflammatory cytokines and support bone regeneration [23, 24]. However, our work advances this paradigm by identifying ROR2 as a non-redundant regulator of ABMSC-mediated osteogenic-osteoclastic balance, particularly under inflammatory challenge. The specificity of ROR2’s function in ABMSCs—evidenced by tissue-selective knockout and rescue experiments—underscores the stromal compartment’s autonomy in coordinating local bone remodeling, independent of systemic immune or epithelial contributions.

While ROR2 has been implicated in skeletal development and osteoclast regulation [25, 26]. We previously discovered that mutations in the ROR2 gene can lead to recessive Robinow syndrome (a rare congenital skeletal dysplasia) [27], its role in ABMSCs remained unexplored. Our study bridges this gap by demonstrating that ROR2 safeguards alveolar bone integrity through two mechanisms: (1) sequestering IRAK4 to block Myddosome assembly, and (2) suppressing IRF7-driven CXCL8 transcription. Unlike canonical ROR2 signaling via Wnt5a or planar cell polarity pathways [25], our data reveal a previously unrecognized interaction between ROR2’s Ig-like C2 domain and IRAK4, which disrupts inflammatory signaling cascades. This finding challenges the conventional view of ROR2 as a passive receptor and positions it as an active suppressor of stromal inflammation. Importantly, ROR2 deficiency phenocopied the hyperinflammatory state observed in aged DPSCs [12], suggesting that ROR2 loss may accelerate stromal senescence.

Inflammatory cytokines, particularly CXCL8, are well-known drivers of osteoclast activation and bone resorption [28]. Our work establishes CXCL8 as a pivotal mediator linking ROR2 deficiency to pathological bone remodeling. ROR2-null ABMSCs exhibited amplified CXCL8 secretion, which directly promoted osteoclastogenesis while suppressing osteoblast differentiation. This dual effect mirrors CXCL8’s role in rheumatoid arthritis, where it enhances RANKL expression and recruits osteoclast precursors [29, 30]. Mechanistically, we identified IRF7 as the transcription factor responsible for ROR2-regulated CXCL8 expression. IRF7 phosphorylation, amplified by ROR2 deficiency, drove CXCL8 promoter activity, creating a feedforward loop that exacerbated bone loss. These findings align with emerging evidence implicating IRF family members in periodontal inflammation [31, 32], yet our study uniquely links IRF7 activation to stromal-osteoclastic crosstalk via CXCL8.

The Myddosome complex, comprising MYD88, IRAK4, and IRAK2, is a linchpin of innate immune signaling [33]. Our data reveal that ROR2 disrupts Myddosome assembly by binding IRAK4, thereby attenuating IRF7 phosphorylation and downstream CXCL8 transcription. This mechanism operates independently of canonical NF-κB pathways, highlighting IRF7 as a context-specific mediator of inflammatory osteolysis in ABMSCs. IRAK4’s dual role—promoting osteoclastogenesis while suppressing osteogenesis—positions it as a therapeutic target for periodontitis. Notably, IRAK4 inhibition not only rescued ROR2 deficiency-driven bone loss but also shifted macrophage polarization toward a reparative phenotype, echoing recent reports in systemic bone diseases [34]. These findings underscore the therapeutic potential of selectively targeting IRAK4 in stromal cells to uncouple inflammation from bone destruction.

Our chitosan/β-glycerophosphate (CS/β-GP) hydrogel system, engineered for sustained CXCL8 neutralization, represents a paradigm shift in local drug delivery for periodontitis. By encapsulating anti-CXCL8 antibodies, this platform achieved prolonged suppression of osteoclast coverage while preserving osteogenic capacity, mirroring the effects of genetic IRAK4 ablation. The thermosensitive properties of CS/β-GP enable minimally invasive application and controlled release, addressing limitations of systemic therapies [35]. Although prior studies have utilized hydrogels for antimicrobial delivery [21, 36], our work pioneers their use in modulating stromal-immune crosstalk. Future iterations could integrate IRAK4 inhibitors or ROR2 agonists to synergistically enhance bone regeneration.

Our study delineates a ROR2/IRAK4/CXCL8 axis that governs alveolar bone remodeling through ABMSC-osteoclast crosstalk. Key innovations include: (1) identifying ROR2 as a stromal-specific brake on inflammatory osteolysis, (2) elucidating IRF7 as a non-canonical effector of Myddosome signaling, and (3) developing a translatable hydrogel-based therapy. While our findings are constrained by the murine model’s inability to fully replicate human periodontal complexity, the conserved roles of ROR2 and IRAK4 across species suggest broad clinical relevance. Future studies should explore ROR2’s interplay with mechanosensitive pathways during orthodontic tooth movement and assess long-term safety of IRAK4 modulation. Collectively, this work establishes stromal immunomodulation as a viable strategy to reconcile osteogenesis and inflammation, offering new hope for treating periodontitis and related bone disorders.

Methods

Study approval

All mutant mouse lines and experimental procedures were approved by approved by the Ethics Committee of the Beijing Stomatology Hospital, Capital Medical University.

Clinical specimens

Alveolar bone specimens were obtained from patients undergoing routine oral surgical procedures at Beijing Stomatological Hospital, Capital Medical University, with written informed consent and ethical approval (approval No. CMUSH-IRB-KJ-PJ-2020-02). Normal alveolar bone tissues (n = 28) were collected from periodontally healthy sites (e.g., during extraction of impacted third molars or implant site preparation). Resorbed alveolar bone tissues (n = 35) were obtained from sites diagnosed with chronic periodontitis, exhibiting clinical attachment loss and radiographic evidence of alveolar bone resorption. Tissues were immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C until RNA extraction. Exclusion criteria included systemic diseases affecting bone metabolism, current pregnancy, and smoking.

Mice

Mice were housed under specific-pathogen-free (SPF) conditions with controlled temperature (20–25 °C), humidity (40–70%), and a 12-hour light/dark cycle, receiving sterilized food and acidified water (pH 2.5–3.0) ad libitum [37]. Bedding was replaced regularly to maintain hygiene. Conditional knockout mice (ROR2fl/fl;Prx1:Cre, IRAK4fl/fl;Prx1:Cre, and ROR2fl/fl;IRAK4fl/fl;Prx1:Cre) and littermate controls were generated by crossing floxed mice (ROR2fl/fl or IRAK4fl/fl) with Prx1-Cre transgenic lines (Jackson Laboratory). Germline recombination was excluded via PCR genotyping of tail DNA (Supplementary Table S1). For breeding, heterozygous males and females (8–12 weeks old) were paired to produce timed pregnancies (19–21 days). Litters were weaned at 21–28 days, and offspring were genotyped to confirm floxed and Cre alleles. Double-knockout mice were generated through sequential crosses to combine floxed alleles, followed by Cre recombination verification. Breeding pairs were replaced every 6–8 months (females) or 12 months (males) to ensure genetic stability. All procedures adhered to ethical guidelines, including stress minimization and provision of nesting materials to support natural behaviors.

Cell culture

Human dental pulp tissues were collected from clinically healthy teeth extracted for impaction or other orthodontic reasons. Alveolar bone specimens were obtained from patients undergoing routine oral surgical procedures. DPSCs were isolated from the dental pulp [11], while human and mice ABMSCs were isolated from alveolar bone samples as previously described [38]. The isolated cells were confirmed to be MSCs by flow cytometry using the following markers: positive for CD90, CD105, and CD73, and negative for the hematopoietic marker CD34. Both h/mABMSCs and DPSCs were cultured in Minimum Essential Media α containing ribonucleosides and deoxyribonucleosides (MEMα; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with 15% fetal bovine serum (FBS; MilliporeSigma, Burlington, MA, USA), 0.1 mM L-ascorbic acid-2-phosphate (MilliporeSigma), and 25 µg/mL gentamicin (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The isolated cells were confirmed to be MSCs by flow cytometry. ABMSCs and DPSCs at passages 4–6 were used in this study.

Osteogenic differentiation assay

Alveolar bone-derived MSCs (ABMSCs, 1 × 10⁵ cells/well) were plated in 6-well plates and cultured in osteogenic induction medium (StemPro™ Kit) supplemented with 10 mM β-glycerophosphate, 50 µg/mL ascorbic acid, and 100 nM dexamethasone for 14 days, with medium changes every 48 h. Cells were fixed with 70% ethanol (15 min) and stained with 2% Alizarin Red S (pH 4.2) to assess mineralization. Quantification was performed using Image-Pro Plus 6.0 with standardized thresholding across three biological replicates. Inter-assay variability was controlled by normalizing staining intensity to cell density-matched controls [11].

TRAP staining

RAW264.7 murine macrophages were co-cultured with ABMSCs of distinct genetic backgrounds in α-MEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 50 ng/mL RANKL (R&D Systems, #462-TR), and 30 ng/mL M-CSF (PeproTech, #315-02) for 7 days to induce osteoclastogenesis [37]. Cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Sigma-Aldrich) for 15 min at room temperature, followed by TRAP staining using a Leukocyte Acid Phosphatase Kit (Sigma-Aldrich, #387A) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin (Abcam, #ab245880) for 1 min. TRAP-positive multinucleated cells (≥ 3 nuclei/cell) were quantified across five random fields per sample using an inverted phase-contrast microscope (Nikon Eclipse Ti2).

RNA isolation and RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from ABMSCs and tissues using QIAzol Lysis Reagent and RNeasy Plus Mini Kit (Qiagen, 74134) following manufacturer instructions [39]. RNA concentration and purity were verified by NanoDrop 2000 (A260/A280 > 1.8; Thermo Fisher Scientific). Reverse transcription was performed with 1 µg RNA using SuperScript IV VILO Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 11756050). Quantitative PCR was conducted on a CFX96 Real-Time System (Bio-Rad) using PowerUp SYBR Green Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, A25742). Primer sequences (Supplementary Table S1) were validated for efficiency (90–110%) and specificity via melt curve analysis. Gene expression was normalized to GAPDH using the 2 − ΔΔCt method.

Western blot analysis

Protein lysates were extracted from ABMSCs using RIPA buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) containing protease/phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Roche) [40]. Equal protein quantities (20 µg/lane) were separated on 8–12% SDS-PAGE gels (Bio-Rad) and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore). After blocking with 5% non-fat milk (Bio-Rad) in TBST for 2 h, membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies: ROR2 (Abcam ab135933, 1:1000), IRAK4 (Cell Signaling #4363, 1:1000), CXCL8 (Proteintech 27095-1-AP, 1:500), Flag (proteintech 20543-1-AP, 1:1000), MYD88 (proteintech 23230-1-AP, 1:500), IRAK2 (proteintech 28200-1-AP, 1:500), DKK1 (Cell Signaling #4687, 1:500), IRF7 (Cell Signaling #4920, 1:1000), Phospho-IRF7 (Cell Signaling #24129, 1:500), β-actin (Sigma-Aldrich A5441, 1:5000). HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Cell Signaling Technology, 1:5000) were applied for 1 h at room temperature. Blots were developed using SuperSignal West Femto Substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and imaged on a Fusion FX7 imaging system (Vilber Lourmat). Densitometric analysis was performed using Image Lab 6.1 (Bio-Rad) with background subtraction (n = 3 biological replicates).

Co-Immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

Cells were lysed in NP-40 buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1% NP-40) containing protease inhibitors (Roche, #11836170001). Lysates (500 µg protein) were incubated overnight at 4 °C with 2 µg primary antibody or IgG control, followed by 2 h incubation with Protein A/G Magnetic Beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Beads were washed five times with HEPES-lysis buffer (20 mM HEPES pH 7.9, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% Triton X-100) and bound proteins eluted in 2× Laemmli buffer (Bio-Rad). Eluates were resolved by 10% SDS-PAGE and immunoblotted as described in the Western blotting Section [41].

Chromatin Immunoprecipitation (ChIP)

ChIP assays were performed as previously described [39, 42] with modifications. Briefly, ABMSCs were cross-linked with 1% formaldehyde (Beyotime) for 10 min at 37 °C. Chromatin was sonicated to 400–600 bp fragments using a Bioruptor Pico (Diagenode) with 20 cycles (10 s ON/15 sec OFF). Pre-cleared lysates were immunoprecipitated overnight at 4 °C with 5 µg anti-IRF7 antibody (Sigma-Aldrich, ABF130) or IgG isotype control (Cell Signaling Technology, #2729). Protein A/G magnetic beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #10002D) were added for 2 h at 4 °C. After reverse crosslinking and DNA purification, precipitated DNA was analyzed by qRT-PCR using SYBR Green Master Mix (Bio-Rad) with CXCL8 promoter-specific primers (sequences provided in Supplementary Table S1).

Experimental periodontitis model

Periodontitis was induced in male 8–12-week-old C57BL/6 mice (conditional knockouts: ROR2fl/fl;Prx1:Cre, IRAK4fl/fl;Prx1:Cre, and ROR2fl/fl;IRAK4fl/fl;Prx1:Cre; littermate controls) under isoflurane anesthesia by ligating maxillary second molars with 5 − 0 silk sutures to promote localized inflammation and bone resorption [37, 43]. Female mice were excluded to avoid potential confounding effects of estrogen-driven bone remodeling and immune modulation. Vehicle controls received anesthesia without ligation. Maxillae were harvested 5 days post-ligation for micro-CT quantification of alveolar bone loss (cementoenamel junction to alveolar bone crest distance, CEJ-ABC). Histological inflammation (hematoxylin/eosin staining) and osteoclast coverage (TRAP staining) were evaluated, alongside serum bone resorption markers (CTX-ELISA). Data were analyzed using two-tailed Student’s t-test or ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test.

Micro-CT analysis

Calvarial and maxillary bone samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 24 h at 4 °C and stored in 70% ethanol. Micro-CT imaging was performed using a SkyScan 1176 system (Bruker) at 50 kV, 8.43 μm resolution, and 0.3° rotation step as previously described [37]. Reconstructed images (NRecon software) were rendered in 3D (CTVox) for morphological analysis. For calvarial bone, a 6 × 6 mm region centered at the coronal-sagittal suture was analyzed to quantify bone erosion area (low-density pixels/total pixels) and calculate bone volume/tissue volume (BV/TV) and bone surface/bone volume (BS/BV) ratios using CT Analyzer. Maxillary alveolar bone loss was assessed by measuring cementoenamel junction (CEJ) to alveolar bone crest (ABC) distances across 12 cuspal positions and analysing BV/TV, BMD, Tb.Th, Tb.N, Tb.Sp, and BS/BV in a defined molar region. Live mice under isoflurane anesthesia underwent dynamic jawbone scans (0.6° rotation step) for real-time CEJ-ABC distance analysis. All quantitative parameters were processed with global thresholds (48 for calvarial; 65 for maxillary).

Luciferase reporter assay

HEK-293 A cells were co-transfected with firefly luciferase reporter plasmids (100 ng) and pRL-TK Renilla normalization vectors (10 ng) using Lipofectamine 3000 as previously described [42]. After 48 h, dual-luciferase activity was quantified using the Dual-Glo® system (Promega) on a GloMax® luminometer. Relative activity was calculated as firefly/Renilla luminescence ratio, normalized to empty vector controls. Experiments included triplicate technical replicates and three biological repeats.

Histological processing & morphometric analysis

Calvarial/maxillary specimens were fixed in 4% PFA (24 h, 4 °C), decalcified in 0.5 M EDTA (pH 7.2), and paraffin-embedded. Coronal Sect. (6 μm) were H&E/TRAP-stained as previously described [37, 43]. Osteoclast parameters (N.Oc/BS, Oc.S/BS) and bone morphometrics (BV/TV, BS/BV) were quantified within standardized 3 mm2 regions using Bioquant Osteo® software by blinded observers.

Immunofluorescence staining

Coronal sections were fixed (4% PFA, 15 min), permeabilized (0.1% Triton X-100, 10 min), and blocked with 10% goat serum (1 h). Primary antibodies (4 °C, overnight) and Alexa Fluor®-conjugated secondaries (1:500, 1 h) were applied. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (1 µg/mL, 5 min). Images were acquired on a Leica SP8 confocal microscope (63× objective, 1024 × 1024 resolution) and analyzed via Fiji/ImageJ [39].

Flow cytometry

Human ABMSCs (passage 6) were stained with PE-conjugated anti-CD105 (BioLegend, 323206) and anti-CD73 (344004), FITC-labeled anti-CD44 (BD, 555478), CD34 (560941), and CD45 (555482). Cells were analyzed on a BD FACSCalibur after gating based on forward/side scatter, with FlowJo software (v10.8.1) quantifying marker expression (positivity threshold: <2% isotype control) [39].

ELISA

CXCL5, CXCL8, MCP-3, HGF, and DKK-1 levels in supernatants and tissue lysates were measured using commercial kits (Proteintech) as previously described [37]. For tissue lysates, dissected alveolar bone samples were homogenised in ice-cold RIPA buffer containing protease inhibitors, centrifuged, and the supernatant total protein concentration was determined by BCA assay. ELISA results for tissue samples were normalised to total protein and expressed as pg/mg protein.

Cytokine array

Conditioned media from ROR2-knockdown (shROR2) and control (shCon) human adipose-derived mesenchymal stem cells (ABMSCs) were collected after 48-hour serum-free culture, centrifuged (2,000 × g, 10 min), and normalized to 1 mg/mL total protein (BCA assay). Cytokine secretion profiles were analyzed using the Proteome Profiler™ Human Cytokine Array Kit (R&D Systems, ARY005B) [44]. Membranes pre-coated with 105 cytokine-specific antibodies were blocked, incubated with media mixed with biotinylated detection antibodies overnight at 4 °C, and developed with HRP-streptavidin and chemiluminescent substrate. Signal intensities were quantified via ImageJ with background subtraction and normalized to internal controls. Differential cytokines (fold change ≥ 2.0, p < 0.05 by two-tailed t-test) were identified. Three biological replicates with technical duplicates ensured reproducibility, and negative controls confirmed assay specificity. Serum-free culture minimized exogenous protein interference.

CS/β-GP-anti-CXCL8 hydrogel

CS/β-GP-anti-CXCL8 hydrogels were synthesized via cold-induced phase transition as previously description [20, 21]. Chitosan (2% w/v, 50–190 kDa, ≥ 75% deacetylated, Sigma-Aldrich) was dissolved in 0.1 M acetic acid under sterile conditions, filtered (0.22 μm), and cooled to 4 °C. A 56% (w/v) β-glycerophosphate solution (in PBS, pH 7.4) containing 50 µg/mL anti-CXCL8 monoclonal antibody (clone 6217, Sigma-Aldrich) was mixed with CS at a 1:4 (v/v) ratio under vortexing, achieving final concentrations of 11.2% β-GP and 10 µg/mL antibody. The mixture was incubated at 4 °C for 10 min and gelled at 37 °C for 5 min. Rheological properties (sol-gel transition: 1 °C/min ramp, 1% strain), morphology (SEM), and antibody release (ELISA, 14 days) were characterized. In vivo, 50 µL hydrogel was injected into the palatal gingival sulcus of ligature-induced periodontitis mice per week.

Statistical analysis

Group comparisons were performed using two-tailed Student’s t-tests (two groups) or one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc correction (≥ 3 groups). Sexual dimorphism analysis evaluated male and female cohorts independently. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. All analyses were conducted in GraphPad Prism 10.0, with data presented as mean ± SEM from ≥ 3 biological replicates.
