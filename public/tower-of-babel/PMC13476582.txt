Correction of image-guided radiotherapy and intrafraction motion using ExacTrac dynamic for head and neck cancer patients
Authors: Simona Denisia Iftime Holst, Maria Andersen, Annette Ross Jakobsen, Martin Skovmos Nielsen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDaily cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) based image-guided radiotherapy (IGRT) with six degrees of freedom (6DOF) is standard for head and neck cancer. We retrospectively analyzed 669 sessions from 20 patients, using CBCT guided 6DOF couch corrections followed by ExacTrac Dynamic adjustments. Surface-guided radiotherapy (SGRT) continuously monitored intrafraction motion, triggering verification images and couch shifts when tolerances were exceeded. Pitch and roll deviations were linked to translational misalignments up to 3 mm. SGRT prompted repositioning in 100 sessions (15%), although nearly half of these movements were false positives. This demonstrates SGRT's value as a complementary, non-invasive tool for intrafraction monitoring.Keywords: Surface Guided Radiotherapy, Intrafraction correction, Head and neck Cancer, Image-Guided Radiotherapy

Introduction

Radiation therapy for head and neck (HN) cancer requires a high degree of precision due to the complex anatomy and the proximity of multiple radiosensitive structures. Accurate and reproducible patient positioning during treatment is essential to maintain concordance with the CT simulation setup. Even minor setup deviations can lead to significant dosimetry errors, potentially causing underdosage to the target and/or overexposure to surrounding critical structures. Robust immobilization techniques and daily image guidance are therefore routinely employed to minimize geometric uncertainties and ensure optimal dose delivery [1], [2], [3].

Radiotherapy for HN cancer can be delivered using CBCT within an IGRT workflow. Setup corrections may involve translational shifts alone or full 6DOF adjustments [4]. Daily CBCT imaging improves patient positioning accuracy [5]. Combined with volumetric modulated arc therapy (VMAT), CBCT guidance enhances treatment precision and may reduce radiation-induced toxicity to sensitive structures [1].

Image guidance can utilize either two-dimensional (2D) kilovoltage (kV) imaging or three-dimensional (3D) CBCT. While 2D kV imaging allows rapid verification with lower imaging dose, it provides limited anatomical information and may not fully capture complex HN setup errors. In contrast, 3D CBCT enables volumetric visualization of bony anatomy and soft tissues, allowing detection of both translational and rotational setup errors. When paired with 6DOF CBCT guidance can correct rotational errors in addition to translational shifts, offering superior setup accuracy compared with conventional three-degree-of-freedom (3DOF) corrections [4], [6], [7].

Concerns have been raised regarding the use of rotational corrections, as significant pitch or roll adjustments may cause patients to compensate or move during repositioning (13, 15). To address this, additional IGRT verification can be performed after couch rotation to confirm accurate positioning.

Despite the benefits of IGRT, intrafraction motion remains a challenge, potentially compromising target coverage and increasing dose to surrounding normal tissues [3]. SGRT has emerged as a promising tool to address these limitations and further enhance treatment precision [2], [3], [8], [9]. SGRT provides continuous, real-time, non-ionizing optical monitoring of patient positioning, offering a non-invasive, patient-centric approach to ensure reproducible treatment delivery throughout each session [10], [11], [12].

Although SGRT has been successfully applied to various disease sites, including left breast, brain, and lung cancers [2], [5], only a limited number of studies have reported its use for HN radiotherapy [2], [5].

This study aimed to quantify the IGRT corrections required for 6DOF CBCT based positioning in HN radiotherapy. A secondary objective was to evaluate the frequency and magnitude of intrafraction motion detected using combined SGRT and X-ray–based IGRT.

Material and methods

Patient cohort

This study included 20 patients diagnosed with HN cancer who were scheduled for curative-intent radiotherapy. All patients were treated using VMAT with prescribed doses of 66–68 Gy delivered in 33–34 sessions. All consecutive HN cancer patients treated during a 3-month period were included. The cohort consisted of patients treated with curative intent predominantly tonsil and pharyngeal using thermoplastic mask immobilization. Inclusion criteria required patients to be immobilized with thermoplastic masks and able to tolerate treatment in the supine position. No additional selection criteria were applied beyond the availability of complete CBCT, ETD, and SGRT datasets for the treatment sessions analyzed and no patients were excluded based on treatment complexity or intrafraction motion.

Immobilization and simulation

All patients were immobilized using a 2.4-mm Head, Neck & Shoulders Candor EasyFrame Mask (500 × 460 mm), which includes shoulder fixation (Fig. 1). An indexed set of Candor foam neck was used, and the appropriate support was individually selected for each patient to achieve optimal and reproducible positioning. CT simulation scans were acquired with a Siemens SOMATOM go Open Pro scanner (Siemens Healthineers, Forchheim, Germany), reconstructed with a slice thickness of 2 mm. As part of the clinical protocol, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was obtained using a 3 T Philips Ingenia Elition X scanner (Philips Healthcare, Netherlands) for target segmentation.

Treatment planning and image guidance

CT and MRI images were co-registered and eclipse treatment planning system (Varian Medical Systems, Palo Alto, CA, USA) was used to generate VMAT plans according to institutional and national DAHANCA guidelines [13]. Plans were optimized to deliver conformal doses to the planning target volume (PTV) while respecting organ-at-risk constraints

Patients were initially positioned using the room lasers and reference markers on the mask before CBCT was performed. SGRT was used for subsequently intrafraction monitoring during treatment. Treatments were delivered using three arcs (Field 1, 2, and 3) using Varian TrueBeam LINACS equipped with Brainlab ExacTrac Dynamic (ETD) (Brainlab AG, Munich, Germany). Each session included 6DOF IGRT using couch shifts in vertical (Vrt), longitudinal (Lng), lateral (Lat), rotation (Rnt), roll (Roll), and pitch (Pitch) directions, with rotational limits set at 3°. Initial IGRT was applied using CBCT, followed by corrections using the ETD system. The region of interest (ROI) was defined consistently on both CBCT and ETD images, covering the cervical spine (columna cervicalis) defined on the CT scan. Treatment log files were exported and retrospectively analyzed.

Intrafraction motion monitoring

For each session, 6DOF X-ray-based IGRT match results and surface-based monitoring from ETD were recorded. Surface deviations exceeding 2 mm triggered X-ray-based IGRT verification. Following the final image verification prior to treatment, residual setup errors within our institutional tolerance were accepted. Specifically, a residual error of up to 2 mm was accepted for SGRT monitoring, while an additional IGRT correction was performed if the residual error exceeded 0.7 mm. Any SGRT-detected deviation exceeding the 2 mm tolerance triggered a beam hold. Before treatment could resume, the patient position was verified using ETD (IGRT), and correction was performed if exceeding the 0.7 mm tolerance. Recorded data included the number of SGRT-triggered verifications and the corresponding applied couch shifts. Fig. 1 illustrates the surface area used for tracking intrafraction motion.

Data analysis and statistics

Couch shifts for ETD corrections were modeled in MATLAB using linear regression of the form ∼ p1X + p2 (in mm, with 95% confidence intervals), where X represents CBCT-derived Roll (XRoll) or Pitch (XPitch). During treatment, ETD surface guidance monitored patient movement with a beam hold triggered at a 2 mm surface deviation. Exceeding this threshold prompted verification imaging and potential corresponding couch shifts. The 3D translational couch shift was calculated for analysis. No data points were excluded, regressions resulting in shifts <1 mm were considered clinically irrelevant.

All translational couch shifts included in the analysis corresponded to the additional image-guided corrections performed prior treatment following CBCT-based 6DOF setup. During intrafraction monitoring, SGRT was used to continuously assess patient position. If deviations exceeded the predefined 2 mm tolerance, a beam hold was triggered, and verification imaging was performed using ETD. Couch corrections were subsequently applied during treatment according to institutional IGRT thresholds.

The relationship between CBCT-derived rotational deviations and translational couch corrections was analyzed using simple linear regression in MATLAB (MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA). The translational couch shift (Y, mm) was modeled as a function of CBCT-derived rotational error (X), defined as either Roll (XRoll) or Pitch (XPitch, degrees), according to:

Y = p₁X + p₂.

where p₁ represents the regression slope (mm/°), corresponding to the change in translational couch shift per degree of rotational deviation, and p₂ represents the intercept, corresponding to the predicted translational shift at zero rotational deviation. Regression coefficients are reported with 95% confidence intervals. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the frequency and magnitude of SGRT-triggered intrafraction events.

Results

IGRT correction following CBCT

A total of 669 treatment sessions from 20 patients were included in the analysis. The ETD-based IGRT corrections applied after initial CBCT setup were in magnitude, with mean values (95% CI) of −0.2 mm (−0.3 to −0.1) in the Vrt direction, 0.8 mm (0.7 to 0.8) in the Lng direction, and − 0.3 mm (−0.4 to −0.1) in the Lat direction. A linear dependence was observed between CBCT-derived rotational offsets and subsequent ETD-based translational corrections, specifically for roll and pitch. For rotational deviations up to 3°, translational corrections of ≥1 mm were required. Linear regression analysis demonstrated a strong association between CBCT roll and Lat correction, with slope p1 = −0.98 (95% CI: −1.06 to −0.90) and intercept p2 = 0.06 (−0.05 to 0.17). Similarly, CBCT pitch showed a moderate association with Lng correction, with slope p1 = 0.49 (0.43 to 0.55) and intercept p2 = 0.27 (0.18 to 0.37) (Fig. 2).

Relationship between CBCT-derived rotational offsets and subsequent ETD-based translational corrections. Scatter plots show ETD corrections in the Vrt, Lng, and Lat directions as a function of CBCT rotations (pitch and roll). Each data point represents a single treatment session. Linear regression fits are overlaid where significant associations were identified. A strong negative correlation is observed between CBCT roll and lateral correction, and a moderate positive correlation between CBCT pitch and longitudinal correction, while no relevant trends are seen for vertical corrections.

Intrafraction motion monitoring

SGRT-triggered IGRT events were recorded in 100 out of 669 sessions (15%). Event distribution across the three treatment arcs was comparable: 29 events (29%) during Field 1, 35 events (35%) during Field 2, and 36 events (36%) during Field 3. Verification imaging confirmed that 52% of these movements exceeded the 2 mm threshold (Fig. 3). The maximum observed intrafraction displacement was 8 mm in a single session.

Discussion

In this study, we quantified the impact of 6DOF CBCT-based image guidance on translational corrections and intrafraction motion in head-and-neck radiotherapy. Our findings provide novel insight into the relationship between rotational CBCT corrections and later translational shifts. ETD effectively applied full 6DOF corrections, with CBCT roll and pitch deviations directly influencing lateral and longitudinal adjustments, respectively. Clinically, the regression model predicts for a CBCT roll of −3° would require approximately a 3 mm lateral correction, while a CBCT pitch of 3° would necessitate roughly half that magnitude longitudinally correction. From a clinical perspective, these findings highlight the importance of applying translational corrections after rotational (pitch and roll) adjustments during 6DOF CBCT-guided patient setup, as rotational deviations are associated with longitudinal and lateral offsets.

No other significant relationships were found between initial CBCT-based adjustments and subsequent ETD-based IGRT corrections, highlighting the specific relevance of rotational offsets for treatment accuracy.

Kung et al. demonstrated significant improvements in registration accuracy with 6DOF corrections. Using a 5-mm expansion margin, improvements were observed at the C7 vertebra, while a 3-mm margin produced significant gains for C2, C7, and the clivus. These findings suggest that centers treating HN cancers with 3-mm PTV margins may benefit from incorporating 6DOF corrections into IGRT protocols [6]. Our treatments were executed in field order 1, 2, and 3, with a balanced SGRT triggered X-ray distribution across arcs. This even allocation is particularly relevant for intrafraction motion, as patient movement can occur at any point during treatment; distributing arcs evenly helps prevent disproportionate impact on dose delivery from any single field.

The ETD system combines SGRT and IGRT for intrafraction monitoring, triggering automatic beam-hold if live and reference surface deviations exceed preset tolerances, followed by X-ray verification [3]. Phantom-based studies have shown ETD can detect shifts with accuracy comparable to CBCT [14], but these do not account for patient movement or anatomical changes [[7], [14]]. In our clinical scenarios, SGRT detected intra-fraction movements in 15% of sessions, where approximately half of which exceeded the 2 mm tolerance.

Although minor differences between CBCT and ETD-based image registration may have contributed to the measured secondary corrections, this effect was minimized through routine MV to CBCT and ETD co-calibration (<0.5 mm). For both CBCT and ETD identical cervical spine ROI were used, defined on the planning CT, for registration methods.

Chow et al. reported that beam-holds can be triggered by sudden patient motion, deep breathing, or environmental factors such as ambient lighting, thermoplastic mask temperature changes, or blankets near the tracking area [3]. In our study, blankets were not present in the tracked region. Real-time monitoring near the mandible allowed effective detection of movements and facilitated additional IGRT corrections. However, nearly half of detected intra-fraction movements were false positives, meaning a surface-based deviation of at least 2 mm could not be confirmed by X-ray imaging and hence were not clinically relevant. In some cases, this may reflect that the tracked area does not fully represent the anatomy critical for treatment delivery. Using thermoplastic masks with additional neck openings could allow tracking of a second ROI, potentially compensating for discrepancies due to different anatomic levels [2].

Although SGRT is designed to track surface motion, its reliability as an indicator of deeper anatomy, such as the cervical bone in the head-and-neck region, is less certain compared to superficial targets like breast tissue [5]. The inherent flexibility of the head and neck region further complicates accurate surface-based tracking. In contrast, SGRT is highly effective for intracranial treatments, where the cranium is rigid and closely coupled to the facial surface [15].

Correlating surface changes with internal anatomy, such as bone movement, is an important consideration. In our study, SGRT detected intra-fraction surface deviations, but not all detected movements corresponded to internal bone shifts, as verified by X-ray imaging. Beam-hold events can be triggered by sudden patient motions, including coughing or swallowing, but also by mask movement. The ETD system monitors the surface of the mask and patient, so small motions within the mask that do not alter the tracked surface may not trigger a beam hold. This highlights both the strengths and limitations of SGRT: it provides real-time detection of surface motion, but some internal displacements may go undetected if the mask surface remains within tolerance. Complementary IGRT verification remains essential, particularly in the head and neck region where internal anatomy may move independently of the mask.

A limitation of this study is that its findings may not be directly applicable to patients receiving laryngeal radiotherapy, as laryngeal motion may be more accurately reflected by SGRT than by bony anatomy alone. Further studies focusing specifically on this patient subgroup are warranted.

Another limitation of this study is that the single-camera SGRT system monitored only the chin, mandible, and adjacent upper neck region. Consequently, motion occurring exclusively in the lower neck would not have been detected by the SGRT system and should be considered when interpreting the results. In addition, all treatment plans were delivered using coplanar VMAT arcs, and the gantry did not obstruct the camera's view of the monitored region during treatment. The use of thermoplastic masks for patient immobilization, as their fit may change over the course of treatment due to anatomical changes such as weight loss, potentially reducing immobilization stability. Although no systematic issues related to mask deformation or loss of fit were observed during the treatment course in this cohort, this remains an inherent limitation of thermoplastic immobilization and should be considered when interpreting the findings.

Future directions include refinement of SGRT algorithms and integration of AI-based motion detection to better distinguish clinically relevant movements from false positives. Such approaches could improve accuracy in non-rigid anatomy, like the head and neck region, and enhance real-time intrafraction monitoring while reducing unnecessary beam-holds.

Conclusion

Additional IGRT is required to compensate for translatory patient movement due to pitch and roll. The combination of 6DOF IGRT with continuous SGRT monitoring enhances treatment precision, reduces the risk of underdosing the target, and may help minimize dose to surrounding critical structures. Real-time tracking near the mandible enables detection of intrafraction motion and facilitates additional image-guided corrections, although nearly half of detected movements were false positives.
