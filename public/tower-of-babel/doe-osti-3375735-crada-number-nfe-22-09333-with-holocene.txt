 ABSTRACT

Holocene Climate Corporation has developed and scaled up a novel technology for Direct Air Capture (DAC)
that is based on cutting-edge research initiated at Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL).

According to the IPCC, carbon dioxide capture from the air and its sequestration are necessary to keep global
temperature rise below 1.5°C by 2050. DAC is a viable and practical way to reach this goal. DAC can happen
anywhere in the world, takes up a fraction of the land of forests, and along with the sequestration of CO2,
provides permanent and verifiable removal of carbon from the atmosphere.

The proposed project aimed to facilitate knowledge transfer from ORNL to Holocene focusing on (i) the
synthesis of the ORNL patented material which builds the cornerstone of the DAC technology, and (ii) required
analytical methods needed to conduct crystallization bench-scale experimentation and technology
development. Furthermore, ORNL conducted research centered around CO2 absorption using an air-liquid
contacting structure with an aqueous amino acid solution.

The achievements under this project comprise successful knowledge transfer from ORNL to the entire
Holocene team to jump-start their internal R&D and technological development activities

Costas Tsouris at Holocene Pilot Plant Launch Event, May 2024 (from left to right).

 1. STATEMENT OF OBJECTIVES

Holocene’s DAC technology is truly novel in the field and provides a step-change opportunity in both system
design and performance. The system leverages a continuous process designed around two chemical loops. The
first loop is focused on CO2 loading, where selectivity, durability, and mass transfer are paramount. The second
is focused on CO2 unloading, where energy delivery and energy efficiency are the focus. Within the first loop,

 1

air comes into contact with an aqueous solution that is loaded with the first working chemical, an amino acid.
The amino acid increases the solution’s ability to uptake CO2 from air, and by leveraging a high surface-area-
to-volume ratio contactor, the system moves CO2 from ambient air into solution.

In the second loop, the CO2-loaded amino acid solution comes into contact with the second working chemical,
a guanidine, where a transfer reaction occurs and the CO2 moves from the amino acid to the guanidine, forming
an organic carbonate salt. This new compound precipitates out of solution, allowing for the organic carbonate
salts to be separated into a solid form that is heated to 100°C to regenerate the CO2. Each of these two working
chemicals is recovered and re-used in the process. The novel combination and application of these two organic
working chemicals, and the unique characteristics of the organic carbonate salt regenerating at lower
temperatures than traditional inorganic carbonates (~1,000°C) are what differentiates Holocene’s technology
from current approaches.

This project aims to ensure knowledge transfer and joint development of two important unit operations: 1)
absorption and 2) crystallization & precipitation.

 1.1 TASK 1: ABSORPTION CHARACTERISTICS AND CONTACTOR DESIGN

This task addressed the characterization of the CO2 absorption behavior of the amino-acid solution, through
experimental campaigns in which relevant parameters are investigated. In a first step, bench-scale testing was
performed to generate CO2 breakthrough curves and derive amino-acid performance data (e.g., CO2 absorption
capacity, and rates of the amino acid solution). The experimental setup aimed to reduce mass-transfer
limitations as much as possible to assess the maximum amino-acid solution absorption potential. In a second
step, a novel contacting structure facilitating the optimal mass transfer of CO2 from the air to the amino acid
solution was developed while keeping the pressure drop for air throughput to a minimum. The contacting
structure could accommodate the presence of additives (solids). The structures were developed and tested on a
bench-scale level and experimentally validated and/or modeled.

 1.2 TASK 2: KNOWLEDGE-TRANSFER & CHARACTERIZATION OF CRYSTALLIZATION
 FOR GUANIDINE CARBONATES

This task comprised the knowledge transfer from ORNL to Holocene in regard to synthesis of the proprietary
material previously patented by ORNL. After successful completion, Holocene has built the infrastructure to
conduct synthesis work of organic compounds, have learnt from ORNL to conduct the synthesis of the
proprietary material and has developed analytical methods to conduct quality control of the synthesized
material.

Furthermore, crystallization experiments were executed to quantify the amount of carbon transferred between
the aqueous amino acid solution and the guanidine carbonate salts.

 2. BENEFITS TO THE FUNDING DOE OFFICE’S MISSION

Over the course of this project, ORNL discovered and developed the fundamental understanding of a novel
chemical system optimal for DAC applications. Holocene recognized this breakthrough as an opportunity to
develop a transformative and novel DAC technology building on this basic research. The rapid advancement
of this technology is critical for cost-effectively addressing the global increase in atmospheric CO2 and
temperatures. and is in line with the U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) Office of Fossil Energy and Carbon
management (FECM) to achieve the Carbon Negative Shot target of under $100 per captured CO2 on a net-
tonne basis.

 3. TECHNICAL DISCUSSION OF WORK PERFORMED BY ALL PARTIES

3.1 TASK 1 - CO2 CAPACITY AND UPTAKE KINETICS OF SODIUM AND POTASSIUM SALTS
OF SARCOSINE

The CO₂ uptake kinetics of the sodium and potassium salts of sarcosine are presented in Figure 2. In these
experiments, 100 mL of 3 M sodium sarcosinate (Na-SAR) and 3 M potassium sarcosinate (K-SAR) solutions
were exposed to a gas stream containing 13 vol% CO₂ at a flow rate of 1 L/min (Figure 2, left). Samples of 1
mL were withdrawn every 20 minutes and analyzed for their CO₂ content using a Metrohm 888 Titrando Acid-
Base titrator. To maintain a constant solution volume and concentration, an equivalent amount of the respective
pure amino acid salt was added back into the reactor after each sample was taken.

The results indicate that the potassium-based salt exhibits higher CO₂ loading and faster uptake kinetics
compared to its sodium counterpart (Figure 2, right). Specifically, after 3 hours of absorption, the equilibrium
CO₂ loading was 0.53 mol CO₂/mol amine for Na-SAR and 0.63 mol CO₂/mol amine for K-SAR. This
corresponds to an 18% increase in loading capacity for the potassium salt. Moreover, within just 20 minutes of
loading, K-SAR achieved a CO₂ uptake that exceeded Na-SAR by 0.21 mol CO₂/mol amine and reached the
equilibrium loading value of Na-SAR in only 60 minutes.

These findings suggest that potassium sarcosinate is a more effective sorbent for CO₂ capture under the tested
conditions, offering both higher capacity and faster kinetics. The enhanced performance of K-SAR could
enable more efficient carbon capture processes by reducing absorber size or residence time, which in turn can
lower energy consumption and operational costs. This makes potassium-based amino acid salts promising
candidates for scalable, low-energy solvent-based carbon capture systems. It should be noted, however, that
KOH is more expensive than NaOH, so overall process economics should be considered before choosing
between KOH and NaOH. Slight precipitation was also seen to form in the gas washing bottle for Na-SAR
towards the end of its CO2 loading, suggesting the sodium salt of sarcosine is less soluble than its potassium
counterpart. A figure depicting this precipitation phenomenon can be found in Figure 3.

Dynamic viscosity measurements of Na-SAR and K-SAR, obtained between 40 °C and –10 °C, are shown in
Figure 4. These physical properties are particularly important because the solvent viscosity strongly influences
mass transfer resistance in gas–liquid systems. In the context of direct air capture, where plants may be
deployed across a wide range of climates, temperature-dependent changes in solvent properties can
significantly affect CO₂ absorption performance. To explore this aspect, the viscosities of Na-SAR and K-SAR
were measured using Anton Paar’s SVM 3001 Cold Properties viscometer.

For 3 M Na-SAR, the dynamic viscosity ranges from 3.38 mPa·s at 40 °C to 25.46 mPa·s at -10 °C. In contrast,
3 M K-SAR exhibits lower viscosity values over the same temperature range, from 2.28 to 10.76 mPa·s. As
expected, in both solutions, the viscosity increases as temperature decreases. However, the significantly higher
viscosity of Na-SAR across all temperatures implies greater mass transfer resistance, which may partially
explain the slower CO₂ uptake kinetics observed in sodium-based salts compared to potassium-based ones.

These findings highlight the importance of selecting solvents with favorable physical properties for DAC
applications. The lower viscosity of K-SAR not only supports faster mass transfer but also offers advantages
in cold climates where higher viscosities can impede performance. As DAC technologies are deployed globally,
including regions with sub-zero temperatures, solvents like K-SAR, which maintain manageable viscosities at
low temperatures, are more viable. Optimizing solvent formulations for both chemical reactivity and physical
transport properties is therefore essential for designing efficient, climate-resilient carbon capture systems.

3.2.2 Crystallization Experiments - with and without active mixing

In the crystallization experiment, the overall objective was to (i) demonstrate feasibility of precipitating
MGBIG carbonate crystals from CO2 rich amino acid solution, (ii) get directional information on potential
crystal yields, and (iii) understand impact of stirring during the precipitation process.
To do so, two different precipitation processes were set up in parallel:
 ● Batch 1: Precipitation of MGBIG carbonate from 1M K-SAR solution with active overhead stirring
 ● Batch 2: Precipitation of MGBIG carbonate from 1M K-SAR solution without active stirring.

 7. CONCLUSIONS
This CRADA between Holocene and ORNL led to development and optimization of a mature DAC technology
based on a combination of amino acids and guanidine CO2 absorbents. At the end of the project, successful
transfer of knowledge from ORNL to the Holocene team was successful, enabling Holocene to jump-start their
internal R&D and technological development activities. As a result, the technology was successfully
