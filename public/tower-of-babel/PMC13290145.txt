Predictors of Matching into Plastic Surgery Residency Integrated and Independent Tracks: A Systematic Review
Authors: Jaina Eckert, Abdulaziz Elemosho, Kyle Mangum, Osaid Alser, Jeffrey E. Janis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Plastic surgery residency training in the United States consists of 2 main pathways: the integrated and independent tracks. As more programs continue to transition to only offering the integrated model, competition for both tracks remains intense, with national match rates steadily declining in recent years. This systematic review synthesizes current evidence on predictors of successful match outcomes in both integrated and independent plastic surgery residency programs.Methods:A comprehensive literature search was conducted to identify studies examining predictors of match success in plastic surgery residency programs. We analyzed academic metrics, including United States Medical Licensing Examination scores, Alpha Omega Alpha membership, and research productivity, as well as nonacademic factors such as letters of recommendation, leadership experience, extracurricular involvement, and interview performance.Results:Strong academic performance was consistently associated with match success for both tracks, including high United States Medical Licensing Examination scores, significant research output with a high number of publications and first-author studies, and Alpha Omega Alpha membership. Nonacademic qualities, such as impactful letters of recommendation, leadership roles, and excellent interview skills, also significantly contributed to favorable outcomes. Between the independent and integrated pathways, there was a difference in levels of importance among these predictors, as institutional pedigree of prerequisite surgical training and number of interviews were all more emphasized in the independent track.Conclusions:By identifying and evaluating predictive factors for matching into integrated and independent plastic surgery programs, this review offers updated, evidence-based insights to help applicants optimize their residency applications and assists programs in refining selection criteria for this highly competitive specialty.

Takeaways

Question: What factors predict successful matching into plastic surgery residency programs for both the integrated and independent pathways?

Findings: A systematic review was conducted to synthesize current evidence on both objective metrics and subjective factors. The independent pathway prioritized subjective factors (interview performance, prerequisite training background), whereas the integrated pathway emphasized academic measures (research productivity, Alpha Omega Alpha status, and United States Medical Licensing Examination Step 2 scores).

Meaning: Each pathway had distinct evaluation patterns, with the independent pathway emphasizing subjective factors and the integrated pathway prioritizing objective academic measures.

INTRODUCTION

Plastic reconstructive surgery (PRS) residency remains one of the most competitive in the United States, with the integrated pathway’s match rate being 55.8% in 2025.1 The residency follows 2 pathways: independent (prerequisite surgical residency plus three years of plastic surgery training) and integrated (6-y program that directly matriculates medical students without prerequisite training).2

Integrated-only applicants rose from 216 in 2016 to 506 in 2026, whereas the number of positions only rose from 152 (69 programs) to 221 (91 programs) with a projected match rate of less than 50%.1,2 This disproportionate growth of applicants and positions offered, combined with the shift of United States Medical Licensing Examination (USMLE) Step 1 to pass/fail, which removed a key objective screening metric, contributed to a 2025 match rate of 55.8%, the lowest in the specialty’s history.3–9 This competitiveness has been reflected by higher performance in other objective metrics: for instance, matched integrated PRS applicants reported an average of 11.9 publications in 2016 versus 34.7 in 2025.2 Conversely, in the independent track over the same period, positions offered decreased from 66 positions (42 programs) to 57 positions (36 programs) despite an increase in the number of applicants (↑42.3%).10 This led to a reduction in match rate from 82% to 56%, similar to what was observed in the integrated pathway.2,3,6,11,12

Given the rapidly changing landscape of both pathways, this study synthesizes current evidence on predictors of successful plastic surgery residency matching across both pathways, examining objective (USMLE scores, research productivity) and subjective metrics (leadership, interview performance) to guide future applicants and inform program selection processes.

METHODS

We conducted a systematic review of PubMed, Ovid MEDLINE, Embase, Scopus, Google Scholar, Education Resources Information Center, and Elton B. Stephens Co. Discovery, following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis guidelines. An additional literature search was done by citation tracking.

Search terms combined “plastic surgery,” “match success,” “predictors,” and “residency” with “independent” and “integrated.” Studies were imported into Covidence. Full search terms and MesH headings are provided in the supplemental digital content. (See appendix, Supplemental Digital Content 1, which displays the search strategy and utilization of databases, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/E967.) (See appendix, Supplemental Digital Content 2, which displays the key search terms and progression of literature review with rationale of inclusion/exclusion criteria, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/E968.)

Studies were included if they (1) examined factors that impacted plastic surgery residency match success, (2) included US medical school graduates (osteopathic and allopathic), and (3) were published in English. All cohort, cross-sectional, case-control, or survey-based observational designs were eligible. Studies were excluded if they were not published in English, did not explicitly identify factors that impacted match success, or were letters to the editor.

A librarian executed initial database searches. Two authors (J.E. and K.M.) independently screened titles and abstracts, with a third author (O.A.) resolving conflicts and the senior author (J.E.J.) available, if needed. All three authors (J.E., K.M., and O.A.) independently reviewed full-text articles to determine final eligibility. Data extraction used Covidence software following full-text review and eligibility determination. The level of evidence for each study was assessed using Oxford Centre for Evidence-Based Medicine Levels of Evidence tool to evaluate the quality and strength of evidence provided by each study design.

RESULTS

After duplicate removal, 707 studies were identified. Based on title and abstract screening, 171 studies were selected for full-text review, and 47 studies ultimately met inclusion criteria for data extraction. More details can be found in Figure 1.

Integrated Program

USMLE Scores

Objective measures of academic performance have consistently helped residency programs assess and differentiate applicants. Historically, USMLE scores have served as a key screening tool for resident selection. In 2007, Rinard and Mahabir12 reported that a USMLE Step 1 score of greater than 230 was significantly associated with a successful match. This persisted into 2013 when Clairborne et al found higher USMLE Step 1 scores predicted match success (75% of applicants with Step 1 scores <233 failed to match) (Table 1).14

USMLE Predictive Value

After the transition of Step 1 to pass/fail in 2022, emphasis shifted toward Step 2 scores as one of the few remaining standardized objective metrics for evaluating applicants.6,12–18 Despite this shift, the mean Step 2 scores among matched applicants has remained ~256 across recent cycles.1 Because most integrated PRS applicants achieve high Step scores, this has led to the concept of diminishing returns, as Elemosho et al reported that the probability of matching during 2016–2022 did not increase further once an applicant achieved a Step 2 score of 250.6 Their updated 2023–2025 analysis reaffirmed that although the probability of matching notably rises at scores of 240 or higher, the benefit plateaus beyond 250.9 To mitigate overreliance on Step 2, many programs now opt to use it as a preliminary screening tool or as one component of holistic application review.8

Alpha Omega Alpha Membership Status

Alpha Omega Alpha (AOA) recognizes academic excellence, leadership, and professionalism and is awarded to students in the top 15%–25% of their class. AOA membership has consistently been associated with higher match rates (87%–88% among members versus 65% for nonmembers) (Table 2).6,9,19,20 Rogers et al reported that AOA members receive an average of 16 interview invitations compared to four for nonmembers.13 King et al examined whether AOA membership increased the odds of matching at a top 10 residency program but found no such association (P = 0.2536).23 Their study revealed that applicants without a home program were more likely to be AOA members than those with one (P = 0.0017), suggesting that AOA membership may serve as a compensatory credential for those lacking this advantage.23 However, institutional variability limits its utility, as not all schools offer AOA, creating inherent disparities in evaluation. Although AOA remains a meaningful indicator of competitiveness, it should be interpreted within a holistic review process, rather than as an absolute metric.4,6,7,12,14,19–23

Scholarly Activity

Research experience has become critical for integrated PRS applicants, particularly following the Step 1 pass/fail transition. Scholarly productivity now serves as a key indicator of academic rigor and long-term potential, with multiple studies documenting upward trends of publications among PRS applicants.4,6,13–15,24–28 The average number of publication materials (publications, conference abstracts and presentations) increased from 11.9 in 2016 to 34.7 in 2025 (Table 3). Although various studies have identified different thresholds conferring advantageous match outcomes, between 2016 and 2022, more than15 publications and 5 research experiences imparted the greatest advantage, with diminishing returns beyond these benchmarks.6,7,12–15,24–29 However, an analysis of the 2023–2025 match cycles demonstrated that these thresholds have risen, with 25 publications being the new point of greatest advantage.9 This trend underscores the ongoing “arms race” within the integrated PRS match.9,30

Research Predictive Value

It has also been found that plastic surgery-specific publications had a greater impact on match success for applicants outside of the top 40 National Institutes of Health–funded medical schools, suggesting that specialty-specific research may help level the playing field for applicants from less research-intensive schools.25

Given this established need for research materials, many applicants attempt to strengthen their research portfolios through dedicated research fellowships. Wang et al analyzed outcomes from 2017 to 2021 and found that 20% of applicants pursued a research year and had significantly higher match rates compared to those who did not (83% versus 74%, P = 0.008).26 Other studies have reported similar findings (Table 3).19–21,23 These fellowships had been shown to nearly double the match success rate of both traditional applicants and reapplicants.26,31 Hannoudi et al likewise found research years to be beneficial, though the number of publications did not differ between those who completed a research year and those who did not (median = 5).31 A recent 2025 study found that the combination of completing a research fellowship in combination with higher research productivity increased positive match outcomes to top National Institutes of Health-funded institutions, particularly for applicants without home programs.32 These findings indicate that these opportunities allow applicants to network, gain meaningful mentorships, and obtain familiarity with programs. Nevertheless, there remains a paucity of current data evaluating postresearch year match outcomes, including whether completion of a research fellowship confers an increased likelihood of matching at that same institution.

Despite these benefits, research fellowships can exacerbate disparities for applicants lacking a home program or facing socioeconomic barriers, as many are unfunded and offered informally through program networks.26,31,33 Interestingly, applicants who completed a research year were less likely to be AOA members (25% versus 36%, P = 0.003) and had lower median Step 2 scores (252 versus 255, P = 0.003), both typically favorable predictors of match success; however, they still demonstrated above-average USMLE scores compared to the national average.26

Away Rotations

Subinternships provide critical opportunities for medical students to gain institutional exposure, establish professional connections and assess program culture; between 2018 and 2024, 85.9% of applicants completed at least 1 away rotation, with an average of 2.04.34 These often serve as “audition rotations,” allowing programs to directly evaluate applicants’ clinical knowledge, work ethic, and team compatability.14,30,33–36 For applicants without a home program, away rotations offer valuable networking, mentorship, and compensatory clinical experiences.34–36 For those with a home program, they serve as a measure of applicant adaptability to a different environment, one of the subjective criteria in resident selection.36

From 2017 to 2024, over 80% of matched applicants matched at programs where they completed an away rotation, highlighting the importance of strong performance during these rotations (Table 4).34–36 Furthermore, these rotations can yield strong letters of recommendation, a well-established determinant of match success, particularly from authors with strong reputations in the specialty.7,16,17,27,33–38 Each away rotation completed in a given region had an average increase of 1.3 interviews and increased likelihood of matching within that same region over the past 5 cycles.34,39

Number of Interviews and Contiguous Ranks

Several studies utilized interview volume as a surrogate measure of success, given its historically strong correlation with favorable match outcomes.13,15,20,21,28,40 Applicants are generally advised to attend as many interviews as feasible, balancing time constraints, scheduling demands, and financial burden.41 Data from the 2021–2023 cycles indicate that receiving more than 13 interviews is significantly associated with successful matching, but this alone does not guarantee a match (Table 5).15,28 However, more interview invitations can yield a longer contiguous rank, another key predictor of a successful match.6 During 2016–2022 cycles, more than 10 contiguous ranks were associated with increased match success, with no additional benefit beyond 15.6 However, in 2025, more than 5 contiguous ranks were found to increase match success, with the point of diminishing return remaining the same as previous years.9 Given that the introduction of preference signaling coincided with the study period, with applicants allowed only 5–10 signals, these newer data are a reflection of the impact of preference signaling, as the majority of applicants rank or match at their signaled programs.42

Home Program

Home programs can influence match success by providing adequate clinical exposure, research opportunities and mentorship access. Mentorship offers guidance to students throughout the application process by offering insight into specialty-specific advice, providing strong letter of recommendations and providing networking opportunities, all of which are associated with higher match success.43 In contrast, applicants without a home program tend to have fewer opportunities for the previously described advantages.24 In 2022, only 18.7% of matched integrated residents lacked a home program, and students without home programs were also less likely to utilize peer and mentor relationships.25,29 Data from 2021 to 2024 further demonstrate that faculty and resident mentorship improves match outcomes, with home faculty mentorship emerging as the most influential factor (Table 6).13,23,29,37,39,43–47

Home Program Predictive Value

Medical school prestige also affects match outcomes. In 2020, the presence of a home program increased the likelihood of matching at a top 10 residency program (5.67×), with an even greater effect when the home program was a top 10 ranked institution (7.49×) (P < 0.0001).23 Overall, these findings highlight that although a home program substantially improves an applicant’s likelihood of matching, matriculation into an integrated PRS residency is still achievable without one, especially when applicants maximize available resources such as mentorship, away rotations, and research opportunities.

Pipelining and Geographical Bias

Furthermore, pipelining, defined as the preferential acceptance of medical students from the same institution, remains a significant factor in the integrated plastic surgery match. A recent analysis by Hannoudi et al demonstrated that home institution applicants achieved notably higher match rates compared to external candidates, particularly in the Northeast, a finding reflected in other studies that showed similar geographic bias in match rates.38,48,49 This underscores the ongoing debate regarding fairness and transparency in the residency selection process, highlighting the need for continued examination of institutional biases that may impact applicant diversity and equitable access to competitive surgical training programs.

Additional Advanced degrees

Current evidence indicates that advanced degrees (PhD or master’s), confer no heightened likelihood of matching into integrated PRS programs (Table 7).4,6,21,50,51 Conversely, 44.2% of faculty held graduate degrees in 2021, suggesting that advanced degrees may have greater relevance to long-term academic career trajectory than to match success itself.50

National Collegiate Athletic Association Athletics

Collegiate athletic participation has been examined as a nontraditional applicant factor, as it may reflect desirable applicant attributes of discipline, leadership, time management, and resilience, all of which aligned closely with surgical residency.52,53 Limited single-institution data suggest former National Collegiate Athletic Association athletes receive preferential ranking consideration (odds ratio: 1.83 for top 5 placement) (Table 8).52 Although these findings require broader validation, they highlight the potential value of diverse backgrounds and achievements outside of traditional academic metrics.

Independent Program

The independent pathway of PRS residency involves prerequisite training in general surgery or another surgical specialty (eg, otolaryngology, orthopedic surgery, urology), followed by 3 years of plastic-specific training.3 This track has undergone significant changes in recent years; although some programs are phasing it out, it remains a viable option, particularly for those who decide to pursue plastic surgery later in their training.3,7

Over the past decade, the number of independent positions decreased from 70 to 52, and the number of programs from 41 to 35, with further reduction in match rate from 80% in 2015 to 61% in 2025.3,7,53 We thereby examined trends and the predictors of successfully matching into plastic surgery via this track (Table 9).

Prerequisite Training

Institutional affiliation of an applicant’s prerequisite training emerged as a key predictor across multiple studies.3,7,10 More specifically, applicants who trained at programs affiliated with academic universities had higher match rates than those from community-based programs (52.5% versus 47.5%, respectively) (P < 0.05).3 Moreover, training at an institution with a top-6 PRS program and a top 10 general surgery program significantly improved match outcomes for applicants (P = 0.026 and 0.03, respectively).10 These findings emphasize the importance of institutional pedigree and prerequisite training quality over traditional academic metrics (Table 10).

Interviews with a “Magic Number”

Interview volume remains a critical predictor of successful matching into the independent pathway, with clear threshold effects documented across several match cycles.3,7,10,51,54,55 Successful applicants received and attended an average of 13 interviews (Tables 11, 12).3,51,55 The “magic number” concept refers to a critical interview threshold beyond which match probability rises substantially.51 Although earlier studies found this number to be 5, more recent analyses suggest a higher threshold of 8.3,51 A 2024 study revealed that 64.7%, 61.2%, and 60.7% of postgraduate year (PGY)-1, PGY-2, and PGY-3 independent residents, respectively, met this threshold (P < 0.05).3

Scores

Although a USMLE Step 1 score greater than 230 has historically been a key predictor of match success for the independent pathway (P < 0.05), this soon may no longer be applicable due to Step 1 transitioning to pass/fail.3,10 This will most likely shift the emphasis to Step 2 scores, mirroring trends in the integrated pathway. However, a number of applicants still hold a numerical Step 1 score; thus, performance on this examination continues to influence an applicant’s evaluation by programs.3,10

Furthermore, the American Board of Surgery In-service Training Examination scores during PGY1–3 have also been shown to significantly correlate with chances of a successful match, but no difference shown during PGY-4 and PGY-5.3 The optimal American Board of Surgery In-service Training Examination scores for PGY1–3 were found to be 439, 535, and 584, respectively.3

Medical Degree Type

Similar to the integrated pathway, graduating from a US allopathic medical school significantly increases the likelihood of matching (versus doctor of osteopathic medicine or international medical graduate), with an increased odds of matching of 1.78× (P = 0.027) between 2010 and 2020.10 A 2024 study by Amro et al similarly identified that graduating from a US allopathic medical school was a key predictor of match success in the independent match process (P < 0.05).3

Professional Society Membership

Induction into the Gold Humanism Honor Society correlates with match success, as 64.7%, 61.2%, and 60.7% of PGY-1, PGY-2, and PGY-3 independent plastic fellows were members of this society in 2024, respectively (P < 0.05).3 This differs from the integrated pathway, which shows no such association, likely due to timing differences in Gold Humanism Honor induction. Many applicants’ medical schools had not completed the induction process before applications were submitted during the 2023–2024 cycle.56

Academic Metrics: Limited Predictive Value

Traditional academic metrics have shown minimal association with independent match success.3,10 In 2024, Amro et al found no significant predictive value of the number of peer-reviewed publications or AOA membership.3 Azoury et al similarly found no association of match success with AOA membership nor with medical school ranking in 2020 (P = 0.2 and 0.59, respectively).10 These findings contrast with the integrated pathway and other competitive specialties, where AOA and research productivity typically confer significant advantages.3,10

Program Director Perspectives

Sarac et al supplied an National Resident Matching Program program director survey in 2022, providing greater insight into how programs evaluate independent applicants.7 Letters of recommendation were rated the most influential metric, with an emphasis on author identity and content quality, consistent with prior findings.7,57 Among nonacademic criteria, interview performance emerged as the strongest predictor of match success, followed by applicant “fit” within the program.7 These results highlight the critical importance of interpersonal and communication skills during the interview process.7

Despite similar core predictors, the 2 pathways show distinct evaluation patterns. Pathway Differences—Key Predictors

Interview timing represents an underexplored factor.70 Our analysis revealed pathway-specific differences (Table 15): the independent pathway outcomes were evenly distributed across interview days, whereas integrated pathway applicants interviewing on the first day were more likely to be ranked in the bottom quartile compared with those who interviewed the second day (odds ratio = 2.34, P = 0.02).70 Interestingly, interviewing at the end of a subinternship did not significantly affect final ranking for either pathway, suggesting that away rotations benefit overall match success but do not influence rank once interviews begin.70 Programs should remain mindful of potential timing effects when scheduling interviews (Supplemental Digital Content 1, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/E967; Supplemental Digital Content 2, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/E968).

LIMITATIONS

The study has several important limitations. First, the included studies are predominantly retrospective (Oxford Center for Evidence Based Medicine level 3), which can cause selection bias. Additionally, survey-based studies carry additional risk of recall, social desirability, and nonresponse bias. No single study encompassed all programs, limiting generalizability, and variation in cycles and predictors examined across studies precluded formal meta-analysis for either pathway.

CONCLUSIONS

This systematic review provides the first comprehensive synthesis of match predictors across both plastic surgery pathways, formally analyzing 47 studies to identify evidence-based factors for success. Although traditional academic metrics remain influential, successful matching now depends on pathway-specific strategies and excellence across research, clinical performance, and professional relationships. Understanding these evidence-based predictors is essential for navigating this competitive match process. As examination formats and program structures continue to evolve, ongoing research will be critical to ensure fair, data-driven and effective resident selection in plastic surgery training.
