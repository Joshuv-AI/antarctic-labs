Inverse design of a magneto-elastica for shape-morphing Journal: Elsevier
JiaHao Li 1,† , Yingchao Zhang 2,† , Weicheng Huang 3,† , Shenghao Ye 4 , HengAn Wu 1, , Dominic Vella 4, , and Mingchao Liu 5,

Abstract
Slender magnetic elements provide a versatile platform for programmable shape-morphing under remote magnetic actuation. However, a general and physically interpretable framework for the inverse design of a ‘magneto-elastica’ under prescribed boundary conditions remains lacking. In this work, we develop an explicit analytical formulation for the inverse design of a magneto-elastica based on the integral form of the moment equilibrium equations. This approach yields direct constraints on the admissible curvature and rotation fields, enabling a systematic characterization of the feasible design space. We identify the key dimensionless parameters that govern the competition between magnetic torques and elastic restoring moments and show that the applied boundary conditions are an essential ingredient. We obtain closed-form solutions for the beam tapering profiles required to generate desired actuated shapes in the cases of clamped–free and clamped–clamped configurations; in the latter case, this includes analytical expressions for the boundary reactions. The formulation recovers the classical inverse elastica in the absence of magnetic fields and reveals a linear scaling between curvature deviation and magnetic mismatch. A tessellation strategy based on stiffness tailoring is further proposed for the design of discretized morphing surfaces. The theoretical predictions are validated against discrete elastic rod simulations and experiments across representative geometries. This work establishes a consistent analytical framework for the inverse design of a magneto-elastica and provides new insight into magnetically-induced shape programming in slender structures.



1
Introduction

Shape-morphing structures that reconfigure under external stimuli provide a route to adaptivity and multifunctionality in mechanical systems, enabling a single device to switch between configurations, mechanical responses, and modes of interaction with its environment
(
Sharon and Efrati 2010
;
Oliver et al. 2016
;
Sydney Gladman et al. 2016
;
Van Rees et al. 2017
;
Dudek et al. 2025
)
. Such capabilities are central to applications including compact-to-deployed transformations
(
Gattas and You 2015
)
, reconfigurable locomotion and manipulation
(
Sun et al. 2023
)
, and conformal interfacing with complex and time-varying surfaces
(
Qamar et al. 2018
)
. Common strategies to achieve programmable morphing include geometric programming through architected layouts, material programming through built-in eigenstrain or intrinsic curvature, and field-driven actuation using remotely applied stimuli
(
Yang et al. 2023
)
. In many cases, complex three-dimensional (3D) shapes are generated by designing low-dimensional building blocks, such as beams, ribbons, and plates, whose kinematics allow large deformations at relatively low energetic cost
(
Liu et al. 2020
;
Bo et al. 2023
;
Li et al. 2026
)
.

Among field-driven actuation mechanisms, magnetic actuation has emerged rapidly in recent years due to its remote and tunable nature
(
Bastola and Hossain 2021
)
. By embedding magnetic particles within elastic matrices, structures can undergo large and reversible deformations under externally applied magnetic fields
(
Kim and Zhao 2022
)
. Their mechanical response is governed by the competition between elastic bending and magnetically induced body torques, leading to rich nonlinear behaviors and a broad range of accessible configurations
(
Zhao et al. 2019
)
. Magnetically actuated slender structures have therefore attracted significant attention as a platform for programmable shape-morphing
(
Lum et al. 2016
;
Hu et al. 2018
;
Wang et al. 2021
)
. In particular, one-dimensional (1D) magnetic structures, such as beams and rods, are especially attractive thanks to their ability to undergo large, controllable deformations
(
Yan et al. 2022
;
Sano et al. 2022
;
Zhang et al. 2023
)
. Such characteristics make them fundamental building blocks for morphing systems and have enabled applications in areas such as continuum robotics and minimally invasive devices, where flexible magnetic elements are used for navigation and manipulation
(
Kim et al. 2019
;
Kim and Zhao 2022
;
Dreyfus et al. 2024
;
Tong et al. 2025
;
Wang et al. 2026
)
.

Inverse design plays a central role in exploiting these capabilities: the goal is to determine the actuation or structural parameters that will produce prescribed shapes. For slender structures, inverse design has been studied extensively in the context of the (non-magnetic) elastica, including strain-programmed bilayer ribbons
(
Chen et al. 2011
;
Armon et al. 2011
;
Li et al. 2025
;
Levin et al. 2021
)
, buckling-driven rods with tailored intrinsic curvature and boundary conditions
(
Derouet-Jourdan et al. 2010
;
Derouet-Jourdan et al. 2013
;
Bertails-Descoubes et al. 2018
;
Qin et al. 2022
;
Li et al. 2026
)
, and stiffness-programmed structures
(
Liu et al. 2020
;
Fan et al. 2020
;
Hafner and Bickel 2021
;
Zhang et al. 2022
;
Hafner and Bickel 2023
)
. For the stiffness-programmed structures, a particularly convenient way of varying the stiffness spatially is to taper the strip width. However, most existing formulations are based on the differential form of the elastica equations, with inverse design achieved through local relations between curvature, loading, and material properties. While these approaches provide valuable theoretical insights, they do not explicitly reveal the global constraints imposed by boundary conditions and actuation, and therefore do not enable a systematic characterization of which shapes are attainable.

The inverse design of the magneto-elastica introduces additional challenges beyond those encountered in classical elastica
(
Lum et al. 2016
;
Wang et al. 2021
;
Wang et al. 2023
;
Li et al. 2025
)
. Magnetic actuation enters the equilibrium equations as a distributed torque density, giving rise to intrinsically nonlocal contributions to the internal moment
(
Wang et al. 2020
)
. At the same time, the governing equations remain strongly nonlinear because of the geometric coupling between curvature and rotation
(
Sano et al. 2022
)
. As a result, existing inverse design approaches based on the differential form of the elastica equations
(
Liu et al. 2020
;
Fan et al. 2020
)
do not directly reveal global constraints on admissible curvature and rotation fields, making it difficult to characterize the feasible design space under prescribed boundary conditions.

In this work, we develop an explicit analytical framework for the inverse design of magneto-elastica based on the integral form of the moment equilibrium equations. For convenience of manufacture, we consider the strip tapering required to generate a desired 3D shape under magnetic actuation. This formulation directly yields constraints on admissible curvature and rotation fields, enabling a systematic characterization of the feasible design space. By introducing appropriate dimensionless groups, we identify the key parameters governing the competition between magnetic torques and elastic restoring moments. Closed-form solutions are derived for both clamped–free and clamped–clamped configurations, including analytical expressions for the boundary reactions in the latter case. The proposed framework naturally recovers the classical inverse elastica in the absence of magnetic actuation
(
Liu et al. 2020
)
, ensuring consistency with existing theories. To fabricate a three-dimensional surface with rotational symmetry, an additional constraint on the beam width distribution must be satisfied
(
Liu et al. 2020
;
Zhang et al. 2022
)
. Therefore, a tessellation strategy based on stiffness tailoring is finally introduced for the design of discretized morphing surfaces. The theoretical predictions are validated through comparisons with discrete elastic rod simulations and experiments for representative examples.

2
Analytical inverse design framework

In this section, we develop an analytical framework for the inverse design of a magneto-elastica subject to a uniform magnetic field. The formulation is based on the integral form of the moment equilibrium equations, which provides a global description of the internal force–moment balance. The governing relations are then cast in dimensionless form to identify the key parameters controlling the competition between magnetic and elastic effects.

The framework is subsequently applied to inverse design (i.e. determining the tapering that yields a desired 3D shape) under two canonical boundary conditions (BCs): clamped–free and clamped–clamped configurations. Finally, an alternative design strategy based on tessellation is introduced, enabling the extension of the framework to discretized morphing structures.

2.1
Integral equations of moment equilibrium

We consider a slender elastic beam of length
L
with a non-uniform cross-section (Fig.
1
(a)), clamped at one or both ends and subject to a uniform magnetic field of magnitude
B
and orientation
\varphi
. The beam is assumed to be uniformly magnetized along the tangential direction with magnetization
M
, so that magnetic actuation induces a distributed body torque along the centerline. The mechanical response is governed by the balance between elastic bending moments and magnetically induced torques. Owing to its slenderness, the beam’s deformation is dominated by bending, and the beam is taken to be inextensible.
The beam’s configuration is then described by the tangent angle
\theta(s)
, where
s\in[0,L]
is the arc-length coordinate measured from the clamped end (
s=0
). The curvature is given by
\kappa(s)=\theta^{\prime}(s)
, which characterizes the degree of bending. The equilibrium of the beam is formulated in terms of the internal moment and its balance with the distributed magnetic torque, leading to an integral representation of the governing equations.

The equilibrium of the beam follows from the balance of moments, consistent with the principle of virtual work
(
Zhao et al. 2019
)
. As shown in Fig.
1
(b), this moment balance can be expressed in integral form as

\int_{s}^{L}m(x)\,\mathrm{d}x+M_{\mathrm{L}}-(\bm{r}(L)-\bm{r}(s))\times\bm{F}=D(s)\kappa(s),

(1)

where each term has a clear physical interpretation:

1.

m(s)=ABM\sin(\theta(s)-\varphi)
is the distributed magnetic torque per unit length, arising from the interaction between the remnant magnetization and the applied magnetic field, where
A
is the cross-sectional area;

2.

M_{L}
is the moment applied at the boundary,
s=L
;

3.

\bm{F}=(F_{x},F_{y})
is the force applied at
s=L
, which contributes to the moment through the lever arm
(\bm{r}(L)-\bm{r}(s))
;

4.

\bm{r}(s)=\left(\int_{0}^{s}\cos\theta(u)\,\mathrm{d}u,\ \int_{0}^{s}\sin\theta(u)\,\mathrm{d}u\right)
is the centerline position;

5.

D(s)=EI(s)
is the bending stiffness of the beam, in which
E
is the Young’s modulus and
I(s)
is the second moment of area.

For a rectangular cross-section of width
w(s)
and thickness
h(s)
,
I(s)=w(s)h(s)^{3}/12
. In general, varying the strip thickness is an efficient, but very challenging, means of changing
I(s)
. In practice, we shall consider strips with tapered widths,
w(s)
, but uniform thickness,
h(s)=\mathrm{cst}
.

2.2
Dimensionless formulation

The governing equations are cast in dimensionless form by scaling all lengths by the beam length
L
, i.e. we let

\bar{s}=\frac{s}{L},\quad\bar{w}(\bar{s})=\frac{w(\bar{s})}{L},\quad\bar{h}=\frac{h}{L},\quad\bar{\kappa}(\bar{s})=\kappa(\bar{s})L.

(2)

Dimensionless variables are denoted by an overbar. Energies and moments are scaled by a characteristic elastic bending energy

P_{0}=\frac{EL^{3}}{12}.

(3)

The corresponding characteristic force scale is
P_{0}/L
, obtained from the elastic energy scale, and is used to nondimensionalize forces. This yields the dimensionless quantities

\bar{F}_{x}=\frac{F_{x}L}{P_{0}},\quad\bar{F}_{y}=\frac{F_{y}L}{P_{0}},\quad\bar{M}_{1}=\frac{M_{L}}{P_{0}}.

(4)

By using these dimensionless variables, Eq. (
1
) transforms to

\int_{\bar{s}}^{1}{\alpha\bar{w}(u)\sin\left(\theta(u)-\varphi\right)\mathrm{d}u}+\bar{F}_{x}\int_{\bar{s}}^{1}{\sin\theta(u)\mathrm{d}u}-\bar{F}_{y}\int_{\bar{s}}^{1}{\cos\theta(u)\mathrm{d}u}+\bar{M}_{1}=\beta\bar{w}(\bar{s})\bar{\kappa}(\bar{s}),

(5)

where

\alpha={BML^{3}\bar{h}}/{P_{0}}=12BM/E\bar{h},

(6)

is the fundamental magnetic coupling parameter and quantifies the ratio of typical magnetic torques to elastic restoring moments. We also introduce

\beta=\bar{h}^{3},

(7)

for convenience.

This dimensionless formulation shows that the competition between magnetic actuation and elastic bending stiffness is characterized by
\alpha
: when
\alpha\ll 1
, the response is dominated by elastic effects and approaches that of a classical elastica; when
\alpha\gg 1
, magnetic actuation becomes dominant and significantly alters the deformation of the beam.

2.3
Clamped–free boundary condition

We consider first the case where the beam is clamped at
\bar{s}=0
and free at
\bar{s}=1
, with no external load applied at the free end (i.e.,
\bar{F}_{x}=\bar{F}_{y}=0,\bar{M}_{1}=0
). This configuration represents a cantilever beam subject only to distributed magnetic loading, as arises in applications such as magnetic micro-actuators and sensors. Under these conditions, Eq. (
5
) simplifies considerably to

\beta\theta^{\prime}(\bar{s})\bar{w}(\bar{s})-\alpha\int_{\bar{s}}^{1}{\bar{w}(u)\sin\mathrm{(}\theta(u)}-\varphi)\mathrm{d}u=0,

(8)

where
\theta^{\prime}(\bar{s})=\bar{\kappa}(\bar{s})
.

We assume that a desired target shape,
\theta(\bar{s})
, is known and treat (
8
) as an equation for the unknown strip width
w(s)
required to obtain this desired shape under the action of the imposed magnetic field. To render this equation more tractable, we differentiate with respect to
\bar{s}
, yielding a first-order differential equation for the width function
\bar{w}(\bar{s})

\bar{w}^{\prime}(\bar{s})+\mathcal{A}(\bar{s})\bar{w}(\bar{s})=0,

(9)

where the coefficient function

\mathcal{A}(\bar{s})=\frac{\beta\theta^{\prime\prime}(\bar{s})+\alpha\sin(\varphi-\theta(\bar{s}))}{\beta\theta^{\prime}(\bar{s})},

(10)

encapsulates the combined influence of curvature variation and magnetic loading. This equation admits the general solution

\bar{w}(\bar{s})=w_{1}\exp\left(\int_{\bar{s}}^{1}\mathcal{A}(u)\mathrm{d}u\right),

(11)

with
w_{1}=\bar{w}(1)
denoting the beam’s width at the free end. The expression in (
11
) reveals that the width variation along the beam required to realize a desired shape is very (exponentially) sensitive to the integrated effect of the mechanical-magnetic coupling.

To determine the design space of a magneto-elastica subject to clamped–free BCs, we discuss the constraints on target
\theta(\bar{s})
. First, we can read from Eq. (
8
) that when
\bar{s}=1
,
\theta^{\prime}(\bar{s})=0
. Second, Eq. (
8
) also means that when
\theta(\bar{s})<\varphi
,
\theta^{\prime}(\bar{s})>0
. Finally, the free-end condition
\theta^{\prime}(1)=0
, leads to a singularity in Eq. (
10
) unless
\theta^{\prime\prime}(1)+\alpha\sin(\varphi-\theta(1))
. Additionally, we require the initial value of
\theta(\bar{s})
,
\theta(0)
, to be 0 because the left end is clamped.

We summarize the constraints for
\theta(\bar{s})
as follows

\begin{cases}\theta^{\prime\prime}(1)+\alpha\sin(\varphi-\theta(1))=0,\\
\theta^{\prime}(1)=0,\\
\theta(\bar{s})<\varphi,\\
\theta^{\prime}(\bar{s})>0,\\
\theta(0)=0.\end{cases}

(12)

To obtain an approximate analytical solution that captures the essential features while maintaining mathematical tractability, we assume
\theta(\bar{s})
has a cubic polynomial form

\theta(\bar{s})=a+b(\bar{s}-1)+c(\bar{s}-1)^{2}+d(\bar{s}-1)^{3},

(13)

where
a=\theta(1)
is the angle at the free end. This kinematic assumption allows us to satisfy the essential BCs while introducing flexibility to capture the deformation shape. Substitution into the the constraints of Eq. (
12
) gives the conditions

\begin{cases}a<\varphi,\\
b=0,\\
c=\dfrac{\alpha}{2}\sin(a-\varphi),\\
d=a+\dfrac{\alpha}{2}\sin(a-\varphi),\\
a+\dfrac{\alpha}{6}\sin(a-\varphi)>0.\end{cases}

(14)

These relations reveal that the deformation is primarily governed by the free-end angle
\theta(1)
, which itself depends on the magnetic coupling parameter
\alpha
and the field direction
\varphi
. The inequality constraints ensure physical admissibility of the solution.

) can be visualized in the

, while the lower boundary (blue) satisfies
\theta(1)+\frac{\alpha}{6}\sin(\theta(1)-\varphi)=0
.

(b) shows the projection of this admissible region onto the

plane for different values of
\alpha
. As
\alpha
increases, the lower boundary shifts upward, leading to a progressive reduction of the admissible design space. This trend indicates that stronger magnetic actuation imposes more restrictive constraints on feasible inverse designs, ultimately limiting the range of achievable target shapes.

2.4
Clamped–clamped boundary condition

We now analyze the case where both ends are clamped. This configuration introduces significant additional constraints that dramatically alter the mechanical behavior; as a result this case is more complex than the clamped–free case already considered. In contrast to the clamped–free case, where the free end can rotate freely, both ends are now geometrically constrained, generating reaction forces
F_{x}
and
F_{y}
at the supports to maintain the imposed BCs. These reaction forces significantly complicate the mechanics, as they introduce additional moments that vary along the beam’s length. Now we must differentiate the full form of the Elastica equation (
5
) to obtain an equation for the width profile
w(s)
required to generate the target
\theta(s)
. Following this procedure, the governing equation for the width profile
w(s)
now takes the form

\bar{w}^{\prime}(\bar{s})+\mathcal{A}(\bar{s})\bar{w}(\bar{s})+\mathcal{B}(\bar{s})=0,

(15)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(\bar{s})

\displaystyle=\frac{\bar{F}_{x}\sin\theta(\bar{s})-\bar{F}_{y}\cos\theta(\bar{s})}{\beta\theta^{\prime}(\bar{s})}.

(16)

The function
\mathcal{A}(\bar{s})
is as before, see (
10
), captures the same mechanical-magnetic coupling as in the clamped–free case in Eq. (
10
), while
\mathcal{B}(\bar{s})
incorporates the additional effect of the reaction forces.

The linear ordinary differential equation for
\bar{w}(\bar{s})
, (
16
), may readily be solved using an integrating factor
1/p(\bar{s})
where
p(\bar{s})=\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{\bar{s}}\mathcal{A}(u)\mathrm{d}u\right)
; we may write the general solution as

\bar{w}(\bar{s})=p(\bar{s})\left(\mathcal{C}-\int_{0}^{\bar{s}}\frac{\mathcal{B}(u)}{p(u)}\mathrm{d}u\right),

(17)

where
\mathcal{C}
is an integration constant. This expression illustrates how the width variation results from the competing influences of the magneto-elastic coupling (through
p(\bar{s})
) and the reaction forces (through the integral term).

To determine the unknown reactions
\bar{F}_{x}
and
\bar{F}_{y}
, we must impose appropriate BCs; moreover, since there are two additional unknowns, we must impose two further conditions. Unlike the clamped–free case, where the width is imposed through a single boundary condition (at the free end), we now require compatibility at both ends and an intermediate point. Specifically, we impose
\bar{w}(0)=w_{0}
,
\bar{w}(1)=w_{1}
, and an intermediate condition
\bar{w}(\gamma)=w_{\gamma}
for some
\gamma\in(0,1)
.
Defining the auxiliary functions

\displaystyle D_{1}(\bar{s})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\bar{s}}\frac{\sin\theta(u)}{\theta^{\prime}(u)p(u)}\mathrm{d}u,

(18)

\displaystyle D_{2}(\bar{s})

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{\bar{s}}\frac{\cos\theta(u)}{\theta^{\prime}(u)p(u)}\mathrm{d}u,

which physically represent weighted integrals of the trigonometric functions that modulate the force components, we obtain explicit expressions for the reaction forces

\begin{cases}\mathcal{C}=w_{0},\\
F_{y}=\dfrac{(w_{0}-w_{1}/p(1))D_{1}(\gamma)-D_{1}(1)(w_{0}-w_{\gamma}/p(\gamma))}{D_{2}(1)D_{1}(\gamma)-D_{1}(1)D_{2}(\gamma)},\\
F_{x}=\dfrac{(w_{0}-w_{1}/p(1))D_{2}(\gamma)-D_{2}(1)(w_{0}-w_{\gamma}/p(\gamma))}{D_{1}(1)D_{2}(\gamma)-D_{2}(1)D_{1}(\gamma)}.\end{cases}

(19)

These relations complete the mathematical description of the clamped–clamped case, revealing how the reaction forces depend on the width BCs, the deformation geometry through
\theta(\bar{s})
, and the magnetic-elastic coupling through
p(\bar{s})
.

2.5
Reduction to classical inverse design of elastica

In this section, we briefly demonstrate that the above framework reduces to the classical inverse design theory of an elastica in the absence of a magnetic field, i.e. when
\alpha=0
. Since there is no magnetic field, we consider the clamped–clamped case of a naturally straight beam. Under these conditions, Eq. (
5
) reduces to

\beta\,\bar{w}(\bar{s})\,\theta^{\prime}(\bar{s})-\bar{F}_{x}\int_{\bar{s}}^{1}{\sin\theta(u)\,\mathrm{d}u}+\bar{F}_{y}\int_{\bar{s}}^{1}{\cos\theta(u)\,\mathrm{d}u}-\bar{M}_{1}=0,

(20)

where
\bar{x}^{\prime}=\cos\theta
and
\bar{y}^{\prime}=\sin\theta
. Using these relations, Eq. (
20
) can be rewritten as

\bar{w}(\bar{s})=\frac{\bar{M}_{1}-\bar{F}_{y}\left(x_{1}-\bar{x}(\bar{s})\right)+\bar{F}_{x}\left(y_{1}-\bar{y}(\bar{s})\right)}{\beta\,\theta^{\prime}(\bar{s})},

(21)

with
x_{1}=\bar{x}(1)
and
y_{1}=\bar{y}(1)
denoting the coordinates of the beam’s right end.
In the special case
\bar{F}_{x}=0
,
w(\bar{s})
can be determined analytically by imposing the BCs
\bar{w}(0)=w_{0}
and
\bar{w}(1)=w_{1}
, which gives

\bar{w}(\bar{s})=\frac{\theta^{\prime}(1)\,w_{1}\,\bar{y}(\bar{s})+(y_{1}-\bar{y}(\bar{s}))\,w_{0}\,\theta^{\prime}(0)}{y_{1}\,\theta^{\prime}(\bar{s})}.

(22)

Here, we have chosen the left end of the beam as the origin, i.e.,
y(0)=0
. Eq. (
22
) is equivalent to Eq. (14) of
Liu et al. 2020
, confirming that our framework consistently reduces to the inverse design theory of a classical elastica studied previously
(
Liu et al. 2020
;
Fan et al. 2020
)
.

2.6
Tessellation

For an axially symmetric surface, an alternative design principle is to achieve a surface that tesselates upon actuation. As we shall see, this constrains the width profile of the sheet, and so it is not possible to construct a desired surface that also tesselates without another variable, such as the thickness, being varied
(
Liu et al. 2020
)
. To provide this additional flexibility, we adopt a method to tailor the effective bending stiffness of the magneto-elastica by introducing holes along the centerline
(
Zhang et al. 2022
)
. Under this framework, tessellation with a desired 3D shape can be realized by prescribing an appropriate effective width distribution while simultaneously ensuring conformal fitting of each individual petal.
To achieve tessellation, an additional restriction on the width
\bar{w}(\bar{s})
is introduced

\bar{w}(\bar{s})=\bar{x}(\bar{s})\tan\frac{\pi}{N}.

(23)

After cutting holes along the centerline of the beam, the remaining portion is defined as the effective width
\bar{w}^{e}(\bar{s})
, and
\bar{w}^{e}(\bar{s})
can be solved from Eq. (
11
). Assume the width distribution of the holes is
\bar{w}_{\circ}(\bar{s})
, the effective width is

\bar{w}^{e}(\bar{s})=\bar{w}(\bar{s})-\bar{w}_{\circ}(\bar{s}).

(24)

The distribution of porosity
\lambda
can be calculated as

\lambda=\frac{\bar{w}_{\circ}(\bar{s})}{\bar{w}(\bar{s})}=1-\bar{w}^{e}(\bar{s})/\bar{w}(\bar{s}).

(25)

Note that the width distribution of the holes can readily be determined as
\bar{w}_{\circ}(\bar{s})=\bar{w}(\bar{s})-\bar{w}^{e}(\bar{s})
but the requirement that
\bar{w}_{\circ}(\bar{s})>0
everywhere introduces some constraints on the choice of
w_{1}
.

Having established the general theory in a number of scenarios of interest, we now proceed to demonstrate it in particular cases.

3
Illustrative examples and discussion

In this section, we illustrate the general theoretical framework presented in §2 through representative examples of the inverse-design strategies derived in §2. The performance of the designed width (and porosity) profiles is evaluated by comparing the resulting 3D shapes under magnetic actuation with the prescribed target configurations. Validation is carried out using a combination of physical experiments (see
A
for more details) and numerical simulations based on the discrete elastic rod (DER) framework
(
Jawed et al. 2018
;
Huang et al. 2025
)
(see
B
).
Results are presented for different boundary conditions and design criteria. To examine the role of magnetic actuation, we compare configurations obtained with and without an applied magnetic field, and quantify the deviation from the target shape through curvature-based metrics. This analysis further leads to a scaling relation for the deviation between the desired and obtained curvatures that result from mismatch in the magnetic field.

Unless otherwise specified, the following physical parameters are used: beam length
L\sim 0.04\,\mathrm{m}
, magnetization
M\sim 10^{5}\,\mathrm{A/m}
, magnetic field
B\sim 0.08\,\mathrm{T}
, Young’s modulus
E\sim 10^{6}\,\mathrm{Pa}
, and thickness
h\sim 0.002\,\mathrm{m}
. These correspond to dimensionless parameters
\beta\approx 1.25\times 10^{-4}
.
For different cases,
\beta
and
\varphi=\pi/2
are kept fixed, while
\alpha
is varied to explore different regimes of magnetic-to-elastic coupling. In particular,
\alpha=3\times 10^{-4}

The effect of magnetic actuation is illustrated in Figs

The deformation of the magneto-elastica is governed by the balance between magnetic and elastic moments. A perturbation in the magnetic field modifies the effective magnetic torque, leading to a deviation in curvature from the target configuration. To quantify this deviation, we use the
L^{2}
norm of the curvature difference, i.e. we define

|\Delta\theta^{\prime}|=\sqrt{\int_{0}^{1}\big(\theta^{\prime}(\bar{s})-\theta_{0}(\bar{s})\big)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bar{s}},

(28)

where
\theta^{\prime}(\bar{s})
denotes the curvature under the applied magnetic field, and
\theta_{0}^{\prime}(\bar{s})
corresponds to the target curvature.
A linear scaling relationship is observed from the moment equilibrium between magnetic moment,
ABM\sin(\varphi-\theta)\,\Delta L
and elastic moment,
D\theta^{\prime}(s)

denotes a small segment of the micro-element length:

|\Delta\theta^{\prime}(\bar{s})|\sim|\Delta\alpha|,

(29)

where
\Delta\alpha
denotes the deviation of the dimensionless parameter
\alpha
from its reference value.

This scaling law is validated against numerical results in Fig.

4
Conclusion

In this work, we developed an analytical framework for the inverse design of magnetically actuated elastica. Unlike previous approaches that directly invert the differential form of the elastica equations
(
Lum et al. 2016
;
Liu et al. 2020
;
Fan et al. 2020
)
, the present formulation is based on the integral form of the moment equilibrium, leading to explicit expressions for the required width distribution and associated design constraints.

For clamped–free boundary conditions, the framework enables the systematic characterization of admissible target shapes. For clamped–clamped configurations, the additional degrees of freedom associated with boundary reactions allow for a broader range of realizable geometries. In the absence of magnetic actuation, the proposed formulation naturally reduces to the classical inverse elastica theory
(
Liu et al. 2020
;
Fan et al. 2020
)
, ensuring consistency with existing results.

The predictive capability of the framework is demonstrated through representative examples, including semi-circular and cardioid shapes, with excellent agreement between theoretical predictions, physical experiments, and DER simulations. A scaling analysis further reveals that the curvature error scales linearly with deviations in the magnetic actuation parameter, providing a quantitative measure of robustness.
Finally, we extend the framework to tessellated structures, showing that discretized morphing surfaces with prescribed symmetry can be designed through appropriate stiffness and porosity distributions.

Overall, this work establishes a physically interpretable and analytically tractable framework for the inverse design of shape-morphing magneto-elastica, offering new opportunities for programmable soft magnetic actuators, deployable structures, and architected functional materials.

Appendix B
Discrete Elastic Rod algorithm

The planar discrete elastica rod (DER) approach is employed to validate the inverse design theory of magneto elastica
(
Bergou et al. 2008
;
Jawed et al. 2018
;
Huang et al. 2025
)
. A slender rod is represented by
N
discrete nodes
\{\bm{x}_{0},\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{N-1}\}
, which define
N-1
edges
\bm{e}^{i}=\bm{x}_{i+1}-\bm{x}_{i}
for
i=0,\ldots,N-2
. Subscripts denote node-based quantities, whereas superscripts indicate edge-based quantities. Each edge
\bm{e}^{i}
is associated with a material frame
\{\bm{m}^{i},\bm{t}^{i}\}
,
\bm{m}^{i}
is the normal vector and
\bm{t}^{i}
is the tangent vector
\bm{t}^{i}=\bm{e}^{i}/\|\bm{e}^{i}\|
. The system configuration is described by the generalized coordinate vector

\bm{q}=[\bm{x}_{0},\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{N-2},\bm{x}_{N-1}]^{T}\in\mathcal{R}^{2N\times 1},

which contains
2N
degrees of freedom (DOF), where
(\cdot)^{T}
denotes the transpose operator.
The stretching strain for each edge is

\varepsilon^{i}=\frac{\|\bm{e}^{i}\|}{\|\bar{\bm{e}}^{i}\|}-1,

(B.31)

where
\|\cdot\|
denotes the Euclidean norm and
\bar{\bm{e}}^{i}
is the corresponding reference edge vector.
We next formulate the bending curvature.
The discrete curvature is based on the turning angle between the two consecutive edges,

\kappa_{i}=2\frac{\tan(\phi_{i}/2)}{\Delta l_{i}},

(B.32)

where
\phi_{i}
is the turning angle and
\Delta l_{i}
is its Voronoi length.
The total elastic energy is the sum of the stretching and bending,

E_{s}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=0}^{N-2}EA^{i}(\varepsilon^{i})^{2}\|\bar{\bm{e}}^{i}\|.

(B.33)

E_{b}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N-2}EI_{i}(\kappa_{i})^{2}\Delta l_{i},

(B.34)

where
EA^{i}
(respectively
EI_{i}
) is the stretching (respectively bending) stiffness of each element, which is obtained from the theoretical width distribution.
Next, we use the Helmholtz free energy for the ideal hard-magnetic soft materials. The magnetization of the
i
-th segment is assumed to be
\bm{\mathcal{M}}^{i}
, and the external magnetic field applied to this segment is
\mathbf{B}_{a}^{i}
.
We assume the magnetized moments will not affect the magnetic field during actuation.
The discrete form of the Helmholtz free energy can be written as:

E_{m}=\sum_{i=0}^{N-2}E_{m}^{i}=-\sum_{i=0}^{N-2}(\bm{\mathcal{M}}^{i}\cdot\mathbf{B}_{a}^{i})\;||\bar{\mathbf{e}}^{i}||,

(B.35)

where the magnetization vector of
i
-th edge is:

\bm{\mathcal{M}}^{i}=\frac{A^{i}}{\mu_{0}}\left[(\mathbf{m}^{i}\otimes\bar{\mathbf{m}}^{i})+(\mathbf{t}^{i}\otimes\bar{\mathbf{t}}^{i})\right]\cdot\mathbf{B}_{r}^{i},

(B.36)

where
\otimes
is the tensor product,
A^{i}
is the local cross-section area,

\left[(\mathbf{m}^{i}\otimes\bar{\mathbf{m}}^{i})+(\mathbf{t}^{i}\otimes\bar{\mathbf{t}}^{i})\right]
is the reduced deformation gradient of a 1D rod,
\mu_{0}
is the magnetic permeability of free space, and
\mathbf{B}^{i}_{r}
is the local remnant magnetic flux density.
Finally, we use the dynamic relaxation method to solve the nonlinear magnetic-elastic rod system.
The update from time
t_{k}
to
t_{k+1}=t_{k}+h
satisfies

\mathbb{M}(\bm{q}_{k+1}-\bm{q}_{k}-h\bm{v}_{k})-h^{2}(\bm{F}_{k+1}^{\mathrm{int}}+\bm{F}_{k+1}^{\mathrm{mag}}+\bm{F}_{k+1}^{\mathrm{ext}})=\bm{0},

(B.37)

where
\bm{v}=\dot{\bm{q}}
is the velocity of the DOF vector,
\bm{F}^{\mathrm{int}}
is the internal elastic force,
\bm{F}^{\mathrm{mag}}
is the magnetic actuated force,

\bm{F}^{\mathrm{ext}}
is the other external forces, and
\mathbb{M}
is the lumped mass matrix.
The Jacobian matrix associated with Eq. (
B.37
) is

\mathbb{J}=\mathbb{M}+h^{2}\left(\frac{\partial^{2}(E_{s}+E_{b}+E_{m})}{\partial\mathbf{q}^{2}}\right),

(B.38)

The Jacobian matrix is banded, and the resulting linear system can be solved with computational complexity
\mathcal{O}(N)
.
