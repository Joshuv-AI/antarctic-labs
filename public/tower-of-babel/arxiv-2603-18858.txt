State Complexity of Shifts of the Fibonacci Word
Delaram Moradi, Pierre Popoli, Jeffrey Shallit ∗, Ingrid Vukusic

Abstract
The Fibonacci infinite word 𝐟 = ( f i ) i ≥ 0 = 01001010 ⋯ {\bf f}=(f_{i})_{i\geq 0}=01001010\cdots is one of the most celebrated objects in combinatorics on words. There is a simple 5 5 -state automaton that, given i i in lsd-first Zeckendorf representation, computes its i i ’th term f i f_{i} , and a 2 2 -state automaton for msd-first. In this paper we consider the state complexity of the automaton generating the shifted sequence ( f i + c ) i ≥ 0 (f_{i+c})_{i\geq 0} , and show that it is O ⁡ ( log ⁡ c ) O(\log c) for both msd-first and lsd-first input. This is close to the information-theoretic minimum for an aperiodic sequence. The techniques involve a mixture of state complexity techniques and Diophantine approximation.

Introduction

State complexity
is one of the major themes of descriptional complexity. We are given a regular language
L
, and we want to know what the size of the smallest finite automaton for
L
is. This topic has been studied for decades, starting with the fundamental papers of Maslov in the 1970’s
[
12
,
13
]
and later popularized in the West by Yu, Zhuang, and Salomaa
[
20
]
.

Within state complexity, a major subtheme has explored bounds on the state complexity of various operations on regular languages, such as union, concatenation, and Kleene star. Here we are given languages, say
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
, accepted by automata of
m
and
n
states respectively, and want to know how large the minimal automata can be after applying some unary or binary operation to the languages
[
20
]
.

Cobham
[
7
,
8
]
introduced the notion of
automatic sequence
: an automatic sequence
(a(i))_{i\geq 0}
is a sequence over a finite alphabet
\Delta
produced by a deterministic finite automaton with output (DFAO). We represent
i
in some numeration system (e.g., base
k
for
k\geq 2
) and feed the automaton with the digits
x
of the representation. There is an output map
\tau
from each state to
\Delta
, and
a(i)
is then the value of
\tau
applied to the last state reached on input
x
. There are two different variants: one where we read the representation starting with the most significant digit (called
msd-first
) and one starting with the least significant digit (called
lsd-first
). Although a sequence is automatic in the lsd-first sense if and only if it is automatic in the msd-first sense, the number of states required can be wildly different. More formally, a DFAO is a
6
-tuple
M=(Q,\Sigma,\delta,q_{0},\Delta,\tau)
where

Q
is a finite nonempty set of states;

\Sigma
is the input alphabet;

\delta:Q\times\Sigma\to Q
is the transition function with domain extended to
Q\times\Sigma^{*}
in the usual way;

q_{0}
is the initial state;

\Delta
is the finite nonempty output alphabet; and

\tau:Q\to\Delta
is the output function.
In this paper we assume that all DFAO’s give the same correct output no matter how many leading zeros there are in the input (when reading the input in the msd-first sense), and the same thing must be true for
trailing zeros (in the lsd-first sense).

We can, of course, combine the two topics, and study the state complexity of operations on automatic sequences, such as taking a linearly-indexed subsequence
(a(ci))_{i\geq 0}
, or shifting
the sequence some number of terms
to get
(a(i+c))_{i\geq 0}
. These topics were studied in
[
2
]
, and then more recently in a number of papers, such as

[
21
,
6
,
22
,
14
]
.

In this paper we focus on the operation of shifting a sequence by some fixed number of terms
c
, to get a new sequence
(a(i+c))_{i\geq 0}
. If
(a(i))_{i\geq 0}
is generated by an
m
-state DFAO with lsd-first input in base
k
, we can construct a DFAO for its
c
-shift by using a deterministic transducer to compute the digit-by-digit sum
i+c
on the fly, giving an automaton of
O(m\log c)
states. However, this technique doesn’t work for numbers in Zeckendorf representation (see Section
2
), because there is no transducer that can do addition in one pass
[
1
]
.

In contrast with the lsd-first case in base
k
, the msd-first case can result in larger state complexity, because once again there is no deterministic transducer to compute the sum (consider adding
1
to input
9999\cdots 9
in base
10
).
For example, for the celebrated Thue-Morse sequence
{\bf t}=(t(i))_{i\geq 0}

[
3
]
, it was recently shown that the msd-first state complexity of the DFAO for

(t(i+c))_{i\geq 0}
is
O(c)
, but exceeds
c^{0.694}
infinitely often
[
14
]
.

However, in some cases, much lower complexity is seen for shifts of sequences in the msd-first case. For example, if we consider the characteristic sequence
{\bf p}_{2}=(p_{2}(i))_{i}
of the powers of
2
, defined by

p_{2}(i)=1
if
i
is a power of
2
and
0
otherwise,
then it is possible to prove that the msd-first state complexity of the sequence
(p_{2}(i+c))_{i\geq 0}
is
O(\log c)

[
14
]
. This sort of logarithmic growth seems to be rather rare. Indeed, logarithmic growth is close to the minimum possible blow-up for aperiodic shifted sequences, because a simple counting argument demonstrates that if an automatic sequence
(a(i))_{i\geq 0}
is not eventually periodic, then its shifted sequence
(a(i+c))_{i\geq 0}
must have state complexity exceeding
c^{\prime}(\log c)/(\log\log c)
infinitely often.
1
1

1

Specifically, the number of possible
n
-state DFAOs with input alphabet
\{0,1,\ldots k-1\}
and output alphabet
\{0,1\}
is at most

n^{kn-1}2^{n}
. Either two different shifts are the same (which happens if and only if the sequence is eventually periodic), or there would have to be some shift by
c\leq n^{kn-1}2^{n}
that could not be computed by a DFAO of
\leq n
states. Expressing
n
in terms of
c
now gives the bound.

Thus the state complexity increase for shifts of
{\bf p}_{2}
is quite close to the smallest conceivable blow-up.

In this paper we examine the state complexity of shifts of one particular famous aperiodic infinite word—the Fibonacci word
{\bf f}=f(0)f(1)f(2)\cdots=01001010\cdots
. There are many equivalent ways to define
\bf f
, of which the most prominent seem to be the following:

(a)

It is the infinite fixed point, starting with
0
, of the morphism that sends
0
to
01
and

1
to
0
.

(b)

It is the infinite word arising from the limit of

X_{i}
as
i\rightarrow\infty
, where
X_{0}=1
,
X_{1}=0
, and
X_{i}=X_{i-1}X_{i-2}
.

(c)

It is the Sturmian characteristic word of slope

\gamma:=2-\varphi
, where
\varphi
is the golden ratio
(1+\sqrt{5})/2\doteq 1.61803
. In other words, it is the word given by

(\lfloor(i+2)\gamma\rfloor-\lfloor(i+1)\gamma\rfloor)_{i\geq 0}
.

(d)

The
i
’th symbol of
\bf f
is the last digit of the Zeckendorf representation of
i
. The Zeckendorf representation is recalled in
Section

2
.

There is yet a fifth way to define
\bf f
, which is not so well known, but will prove crucial to us in this paper. It concerns fractional parts. Let
\{x\}
denote the fractional part of
x
; that is,
x\bmod 1
. Throughout the paper, we also use the circular representation of intervals. If
\{x\}<\{y\}
, then the open interval
(x,y)\bmod 1
is understood to mean
(\{x\},\{y\})
. And if
\{x\}>\{y\}
, then
(x,y)\bmod 1
is understood to mean
(\{y\},1)\cup[0,\{x\})
. Thus, for example,
(\pi,e)\bmod 1=(.14159\cdots,.71828\cdots)
and
(e,\pi)\bmod 1=(.71828\cdots,1)\cup[0,.14159\cdots)
. Then we have the following fundamental lemma.

Lemma 1

The
i
’th bit of
{\bf f}
, namely
f(i)
, is

1
if and only if

\{i\varphi\}\in(-\varphi,-2\varphi)\bmod 1
.

Proof

Let
\gamma=2-\varphi
. Then from Item (c) of the equivalent definitions of
{\bf f}
we have

\displaystyle f(i)=1\iff\lfloor(i+2)\gamma\rfloor-\lfloor(i+1)\gamma\rfloor=1

\displaystyle\iff\{(i+1)\gamma\}\in(1-\gamma,1),

\displaystyle\iff\{(i+1)(2-\varphi)\}\in(\varphi-1,1),

\displaystyle\iff\{-(i+1)\varphi\}\in(\varphi-1,1),

\displaystyle\iff\{(i+1)\varphi\}\in(0,1-\varphi),

\displaystyle\iff\{i\varphi\}\in(-\varphi,-2\varphi)\bmod 1.

The Fibonacci word
\bf f
is celebrated and well-studied
[
4
]
, so understanding the state complexity of its shifts is a natural and significant problem. Our main result is that if we shift
\bf f
by
c
, then the resulting word can be generated by a DFAO of only
O(\log c)
states. Furthermore, this bound holds if the input is read in either msd-first or lsd-first formats. We will need the following lemma, that follows directly from
Lemma

1
.

Lemma 2

We have
f(i+c)=1\iff\left\{i\varphi\right\}\in(-(c+1)\varphi,-(c+2)\varphi)\bmod 1
.

Proof

By
Lemma

1
we have

\displaystyle f(i+c)=1

\displaystyle\iff\left\{(i+c)\varphi\right\}\in(-\varphi,-2\varphi)\bmod 1,

\displaystyle\iff\left\{i\varphi\right\}\in(-(c+1)\varphi,-(c+2)\varphi)\bmod 1.

While our main result concerns the state complexity of certain automata, the proof techniques are also of interest. We use a mixture of arguments from Diophantine approximation, state complexity, and logic. In particular, some parts of the proof are completed using the automated theorem-prover
Walnut

[
15
,
18
]
, which can give rigorous proofs of results about automatic sequences merely by stating the desired results in first-order logic. In this conference submission version, many details are relegated to the Appendix.

Zeckendorf representations and Fibonacci-automatic sequences

To state the results and proofs, we need a classic numeration system sometimes called the Zeckendorf numeration system
[
11
,
23
]
, although Ostrowski
[
16
]
had already published a much more general numeration system in 1922. In this system, natural numbers are represented as sums of distinct Fibonacci numbers
F_{i}
for
i\geq 2
, where as usual we write
F_{0}=0
,
F_{1}=1
, and
F_{i}=F_{i-1}+F_{i-2}
for
i\geq 2
. To ensure unique representation, we demand that a Fibonacci number can be used at most once and no two consecutive Fibonacci numbers be used. Thus, for example,
10
is written as
8+2
and not
5+5
or
5+3+2
.

We write a representation for
n
as a binary word over the alphabet
\{\mathtt{0},\mathtt{1}\}
, denoted by
(n)_{F}
, of the form

a_{t}a_{t-1}\cdots a_{2}
where

n=\sum_{2\leq i\leq t}a_{i}F_{i}
.
Thus, for example, the
representation of
17
is
(17)_{F}=\mathtt{1}\mathtt{0}\mathtt{0}\mathtt{1}\mathtt{0}\mathtt{1}
. Notice that the notation
(\cdot)_{F}
relies on msd-first representations. In a similar fashion, for a binary word
x
of length
\ell
of the form
x=x_{\ell-1}\cdots x_{0}
, we let
[x]_{F}
denote the corresponding integer value; i.e.,
[x]_{F}=\sum_{i=0}^{\ell-1}x_{i}F_{i+2}
. Then, for every integer
n\geq 0
, we have
[(n)_{F}]_{F}=n
, and for every binary word
x
containing no
\mathtt{1}\mathtt{1}
, we have
([x]_{F})_{F}=x
.
The requirement that no two consecutive Fibonacci numbers are used then translates to the requirement that a valid Zeckendorf representation must not contain two consecutive
\mathtt{1}
’s.

A sequence
(a(i))_{i\geq 0}
is said to be
Fibonacci-automatic
if there exists a DFAO that takes as input a valid Zeckendorf representation of
i
and outputs
a(i)
. The Fibonacci word
f
is the canonical example of a Fibonacci-automatic sequence; it is generated by the automata in

3
State complexity in the lsd-first case

In this section we prove our first main result about the state complexity of the shifted sequence
(f(i+c))_{i}
with lsd-first input.

Theorem 3.1

Let
(f(i))_{i\geq 0}
be the Fibonacci word
f
. For
c\geq 0
, there exists an lsd-first DFAO generating
(f(i+c))_{i\geq 0}
with
O(\log c)
states.

The proof of
Theorem

3.1
is delayed to the end of this section. We first introduce a partition of the interval
[0,1)
that is induced by the lsd-first representation.

Definition 1

For each
m
with
0\leq m<F_{d+1}
let
I_{d}(m)
be the interval defined by
I_{d}(m)=[L_{d}(m)\varphi,R_{d}(m)\varphi)\bmod 1
where if
d
is even we have

R_{d}(m)=-F_{d+1}+m\quad\text{and}\quad L_{d}(m)=\begin{cases}-F_{d}+m,&\text{if }m<F_{d};\\
-F_{d+2}+m,&\text{if }m\geq F_{d},\end{cases}

and if
d
is odd, the values of
R_{d}(m)
and
L_{d}(m)
are switched.

Let
d\geq 2
. For every integer
n
, let
n^{[\leq d]}

denote the integer obtained from
n
by discarding all digits with index larger than
d
; i.e., if
n=\sum_{2\leq i\leq t}a_{i}F_{i}
, then
n^{[\leq d]}=\sum_{i=2}^{\min\{d,t\}}a_{i}F_{i}
. Similarly, we define
n^{[\geq d]}=\sum_{i=\max\{2,d\}}^{t}a_{i}F_{i}
. Then clearly we have
n=n^{[\leq d]}+n^{[\geq d+1]}
. The following lemma states that the intervals
I_{d}(m)
for
0\leq m<F_{d+1}
form a partition of
[0,1)
. Its proof and all necessary details are given in Appendix
0.A
.

Lemma 3

Let
d\geq 2
. Then the intervals
I_{d}(m)
for
0\leq m<F_{d+1}
form a partition of
[0,1)
.
Moreover, for every nonnegative integer
n
we have

\left\{n\varphi\right\}\in I_{d}(m)\iff n^{[\leq d]}=m.

(1)

Informally, the low-order Zeckendorf bits of an integer
n
determine an interval where
n\varphi
lies, and the interval has length of order
\varphi^{-d}
.

Remark 1

For every
d\geq 2
, each partition
\{I_{d+1}(m)\colon 0\leq m<F_{d+2}\}
in
Lemma

3
is a refinement of the partition
\{I_{d}(m)\colon 0\leq m<F_{d+1}\}
. This is clear from the construction of the intervals, as well as from (
1
).

Remark 2

Let
c
be a positive integer with
F_{k}<c\leq F_{k+1}
for some
k\geq 2
(or
c=1
and
k=2
).
Set
m=F_{k+1}-c
and note that
m<F_{k+1}-F_{k}=F_{k-1}<F_{k}
. Therefore,
we get that

\displaystyle[L_{k}(m)\varphi,R_{k}(m)\varphi)\bmod 1

\displaystyle=[(-F_{k}+m)\varphi,(-F_{k+1}+m)\varphi)\bmod 1

\displaystyle=[(-F_{k}+m)\varphi,-c\varphi)\bmod 1,

if
k
is even. If
k
is odd, we switch the endpoints of the interval.
In particular, the point
\left\{-c\varphi\right\}
is an endpoint of some interval in the partition
\{I_{d}(m)\colon 0\leq m<F_{d+1}\}
for all
d\geq k
.
Moreover, one can check that by
Definition

1
the point
\left\{-c\varphi\right\}
is not an endpoint in the partition for
d\leq k-1
.

We are now ready to prove
Theorem

3.1
.

Proof
(of
Theorem

3.1
)

We provide the construction of the automaton
M_{c}=(Q,\Sigma,\delta,q_{0},\Delta,\tau)
for generating
f(i+c)
with lsd-first input. Let
\Sigma=\{\mathtt{0},\mathtt{1}\}
and
\Delta=\{0,1\}
.
We create the set of states
Q
and the output function
\tau
as follows. For
k\geq 0
, let
J_{k}=[-(k+1)\varphi,-(k+2)\varphi)\bmod 1
and
\overline{J_{k}}=[-(k+2)\varphi,-(k+1)\varphi)\bmod 1
.

First, the initial state is denoted by
q_{\varepsilon}
. Since the output associated with this state must match
f(c)
,
Lemma

1
implies that
\tau(q_{\varepsilon})=1
if and only if
\left\{c\varphi\right\}\in J_{0}
.

Next, we add four states
A_{\mathtt{0}},A_{\mathtt{1}}
and
R_{\mathtt{0}},R_{\mathtt{1}}
. Here
A_{0}
and
A_{1}
are sink states with output 1, and
R_{0}
and
R_{1}
are sink states with output 0; i.e., we have

\displaystyle\delta(A_{\mathtt{0}},\mathtt{0})=A_{\mathtt{0}},\;\delta(A_{\mathtt{0}},\mathtt{1})=A_{\mathtt{1}},\;\delta(A_{\mathtt{1}},\mathtt{0})=A_{\mathtt{0}},

\displaystyle\delta(R_{\mathtt{0}},\mathtt{0})=R_{\mathtt{0}},\;\delta(R_{\mathtt{0}},\mathtt{1})=R_{\mathtt{1}},\;\delta(R_{\mathtt{1}},\mathtt{0})=R_{\mathtt{0}},

\displaystyle\tau(A_{\mathtt{0}})=\tau(A_{\mathtt{1}})=1,\;\tau(R_{\mathtt{0}})=\tau(R_{\mathtt{1}})=0.

Notice that sink states are split in two due to the digit restrictions of the Zeckendorf numeration system; we are not permitted to simply self-loop on both
0
and
1
inputs.

The remaining states
q_{x}
correspond to the state that the automaton reaches on input
x\in\{\mathtt{0},\mathtt{1}\}^{*}
, given in lsd-first.
Assume
F_{k}<c+2\leq F_{k+1}
.
Then from
Remark

2
we know that both
\left\{-(c+1)\varphi\right\}
and
\left\{-(c+2)\varphi\right\}
are endpoints in the partition
\{I_{k}(m)\colon 0\leq m<F_{k+1}\}
.
Moreover,
Lemma

3
says that if we read the digits of an integer
n
corresponding to
F_{2},F_{3},\ldots,F_{k}
, then we know which interval from the partition the point
\left\{n\varphi\right\}
lies in.
This means that we need to read the first
k-1
digits.
After that we definitely know which interval from the partition
\{I_{k}(m)\colon 0\leq m<F_{k+1}\}
the point
\left\{n\varphi\right\}
lies in, and, in particular, whether
\left\{n\varphi\right\}\in J_{c}=[-(c+2)\varphi,-(c+1)\varphi)\bmod 1
or not.
Since by
Lemma

2
we have
f(n+c)=1\iff\{n\varphi\}\in J_{c}
, this means that the automaton reaches a sink state after reading at most
k-1
digits. Namely, it reaches the state
A_{\mathtt{0}}
or
R_{\mathtt{0}}
(resp.,
A_{\mathtt{1}}
or
R_{\mathtt{1}}
) if the last input in lsd is
\mathtt{0}
(resp.,
\mathtt{1}
).
Therefore, we only need to consider states which the automaton reaches on input
x
with length
\leq k-1
.

To estimate the size of the resulting automaton, we count how many of these words
x
of a given length
1\leq\ell\leq k-1
cause the automaton to reach a state other than a sink state.
First, we deal with the case
\ell=1
, where only two inputs are possible. We distinguish the following two cases:

•

If
\left\{-(c+1)\varphi\right\}
and
\left\{-(c+2)\varphi\right\}
belong to
J_{0}
, then we add one state
q_{\mathtt{0}}
. This case corresponds to
c
such that
f(c-1)=f(c-2)=0
. The transitions from
q_{\varepsilon}
are defined by
\delta(q_{\varepsilon},\mathtt{0})=q_{\mathtt{0}}
and
\delta(q_{\varepsilon},\mathtt{1})=A_{\mathtt{1}}
if
f(c+1)=1
or
\delta(q_{\varepsilon},\mathtt{1})=R_{\mathtt{1}}
if
f(c+1)=0
.

•

Otherwise, we add two states
q_{\mathtt{0}}
and
q_{\mathtt{1}}
, and transitions from
q_{\varepsilon}
are defined by
\delta(q_{\varepsilon},a)=q_{a}
for
a\in\{\mathtt{0},\mathtt{1}\}
.

Now suppose that
2\leq\ell\leq k-1
.
For an input word
x\in\{\mathtt{0},\mathtt{1}\}^{\ell}
,
let
m=[x]_{F}
and
I_{\ell+1}(m)
be the interval defined in
Definition

1
.
We distinguish two cases, as follows:

•

If
I_{\ell+1}(m)\subseteq J_{c}
, then by
Lemma

2
the automaton
M_{c}
reaches the sink state
A_{i}
after reading
x
, where
i
is the last letter of
x
. Similarly, if
I_{\ell+1}(m)\subseteq\overline{J_{c}}
, then
M_{c}
reaches the sink state
R_{i}
after reading
x
, where
i
is the last letter of
x
.

•

Otherwise, exactly one of the two points
\{-(c+2)\varphi\}
or
\{-(c+1)\varphi\}
is included in
I_{\ell+1}(m)
. In this case, we add a new state
q_{x}
to
Q
, and the output of
q_{x}
is given by
\tau(q_{x})=1
if and only if
\{m\varphi\}\in J_{c}
. Since the set
\{I_{\ell+1}(m)\colon 0\leq m<F_{\ell+2}\}
is a partition by
Lemma

3
, this case can occur at most twice for each length
\ell
.

Therefore, for each given length
1\leq\ell\leq k-1
, at most two non-sink states are added. The transition function on these states is defined by
\delta(q_{x},a)=q_{xa}
. So the total number of states in
Q
is at most
1+4+2(k-1)=2k+3
, which is indeed
O(\log c)
, since
F_{k}<c+2\leq F_{k+1}
.

5
Final words

Examining the state complexity of transformations of Fibonacci-automatic sequences poses challenging questions at the border of formal language theory, combinatorics on words, and number theory. There are many other questions we can examine.

It has not escaped our attention that there are obvious generalizations of these results, in some cases to quadratic irrationals, and in other cases to irrational numbers more generally. Similarly, one can ask the same kinds of questions about the Tribonacci word (fixed point of the morphism
0\rightarrow 01
,
1\rightarrow 02
,
2\rightarrow 0
), where the analysis is more puzzling. We hope to take up these questions in a future paper.

Diophantine approximation and proof of
Lemma

3

We first recall some basic properties of the Fibonacci numbers and the golden ratio. From the Binet formula for the Fibonacci numbers, one can deduce (or see, e.g.,
[
10
]
for a reference)
that

\left\{F_{i}\varphi\right\}=\left\{(-1)^{i}\varphi^{-i}\right\}.

(4)

Let
d\geq 2
. For every integer
n=\sum_{2\leq i\leq t}a_{i}F_{i}
, recall that
n^{[\leq d]}=\sum_{i=2}^{\min\{d,t\}}a_{i}F_{i}
, and
n^{[\geq d]}=\sum_{i=\max\{2,d\}}^{t}a_{i}F_{i}
. Since
n=n^{[\leq d]}+n^{[\geq d+1]}
, and from Eq. (
4
), we obtain

\left\{n\varphi\right\}=\left(n^{[\leq d]}\varphi+\sum_{i=d+1}^{t}(-1)^{i}a_{i}\varphi^{-i}\right)\bmod 1.

(5)

We want to estimate the term
\sum_{i=d+1}^{t}(-1)^{i}a_{i}\varphi^{-i}
; it can be understood as an error term.
Note that we get negative terms for odd indices
i
, and positive terms for even indices
i
.
The following simple identity, and the bound that follows from it, together show that we can indeed estimate the error term in the obvious way.
We have

\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\varphi^{-(d+1+2i)}=\varphi^{-d}=\left\{(-1)^{d}F_{d}\varphi\right\},

(6)

and, in particular,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\varphi^{-(d+1+2i)}+\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\varphi^{-(d+2+2i)}\leq\varphi^{-2}+\varphi^{-3}=\varphi^{-1}<1.

(7)

Thus, we get from Eqs. (
5
), (
6
), and (
7
) that

\left\{n\varphi\right\}\in\begin{cases}\left({n^{[\leq d]}\varphi}-\varphi^{-d},{n^{[\leq d]}\varphi}+\varphi^{-(d+1)}\right)\bmod 1,&\text{if $d$ is even};\\
\left({n^{[\leq d]}\varphi}-\varphi^{-(d+1)},{n^{[\leq d]}\varphi}+\varphi^{-d}\right)\bmod 1,&\text{if $d$ is odd}.\end{cases}

(8)

Moreover, if
a_{d}=1
, then
a_{d+1}=0
, so actually
\varphi^{-(d+1)}
cannot show up in the error term. Note that by the greedy property of the Zeckendorf representation, we have
a_{d}=1
if and only if
n^{[\leq d]}\geq F_{d}
. Thus, if
n^{[\leq d]}\geq F_{d}
, we have in fact

\left\{n\varphi\right\}\in\begin{cases}\left((n^{[\leq d]}-F_{d+2})\varphi,\,(n^{[\leq d]}-F_{d+1})\varphi\right)\bmod 1,&\text{if $d$ is even};\\
\left((n^{[\leq d]}-F_{d+1})\varphi,\,(n^{[\leq d]}-F_{d+2})\varphi\right)\bmod 1,&\text{if $d$ is odd}.\end{cases}

(9)

We are now ready to prove
Lemma

3
.

Proof
(of
Lemma

3
)

From (
8
) and (
9
) and the fact that the points
\left\{n\varphi\right\}
are dense in
(0,1)
, it is clear that the half open intervals
I_{d}(m)

0\leq m\leq F_{d+1}-1
, cover
[0,1)
.

It remains to show that the intervals are disjoint.
We do this simply by computing their total length.
By construction, we have exactly
F_{d}
intervals of length

\left\{(-1)^{d}(-F_{d+1}+F_{d})\varphi\right\}=\left\{(-1)^{d-1}F_{d-1}\varphi\right\}=\varphi^{-(d-1)},

and
F_{d+1}-F_{d}=F_{d-1}

\left\{(-1)^{d}(-F_{d+1}+F_{d+2})\varphi\right\}=\left\{(-1)^{d}F_{d}\varphi\right\}=\varphi^{-d}.

Thus, the total length equals
F_{d}\varphi^{-(d-1)}+F_{d-1}\varphi^{-d}=1
, via the Binet formula (or see, e.g.,
[
10
]
). Therefore, the intervals must be disjoint, and form a partition. Also, (
1
) clearly holds as well by the arguments above.

is almost optimal. That is, we show that the constructed automaton
M_{c}=(Q,\Sigma,\delta,q_{0},\Delta,\tau)
is, possibly after one modification, minimal, and we count the number of states in the set
Q
.
Recall that
Q
contains

•

the initial state
q_{\varepsilon}
,

•

the four sink states
A_{\mathtt{0}},A_{\mathtt{1}},R_{\mathtt{0}},R_{\mathtt{1}}
,

•

a certain number of intermediate states
q_{x}
, where the strings
x
correspond to certain intervals.

To be more precise about the intermediate states, we have that the strings
x
correspond to pairs of integers
(d,m)
such that
\left\{-(c+1)\varphi\right\}\in I_{d}(m)
or
\left\{-(c+2)\varphi\right\}\in I_{d}(m)
, and
m=[x^{R}]_{F}
, where
z^{R}
is the reverse image of
z
, and
x
might be padded with
\mathtt{0}
’s so that
|x|=d-1
. We distinguish the two following cases.

Case 1:
There exists some
k\geq 1
such that
F_{k}<c+1<c+2\leq F_{k+1}
. Then by
Remark

2
we know that
\left\{-(c+1)\varphi\right\},\left\{-(c+2)\varphi\right\}
become endpoints of the partition
\{I_{d}(m)\colon 0\leq m<F_{d+1}\}
exactly at stage
d=k
.
This means that we only add states at the stages
d=2,3,\ldots k-1
.
Recall that in the construction of the automaton, at the first stage (
d=2)
, i.e., after reading one digit, we add either one or two states. In fact, we add only one state if and only if
f(c-1)=f(c-2)=0
. Since by definition
g(c)=1
in this case, and
g(c)=0
otherwise, we add exactly
2-g(c)
states at stage
d=2
.
At all other stages (
d=3,\ldots,k-1
), we add two states. Thus, we overall get
5+(2-g(c))+2(k-3)=2k+1-g(c)
states.
Since
F_{k}\leq c<F_{k+1}
, we have
|(c)_{F}|=k-1
, and the number of states is indeed
2(|(c)_{F}|+1)+1-g(c)
, as desired.

It is clear that all states are reachable. Therefore, to prove minimality, we only need to prove that no two states are equivalent.
That is, we need to show that for all states
q\neq q^{\prime}\in Q
there exist strings
u,u^{\prime}\in\Sigma^{*}
such that
\tau(\delta(q,u))\neq\tau(\delta(q^{\prime},u))
.

Let
m_{1},m_{2}
be the unique integers such that
\left\{-(c+1)\varphi\right\}\in I_{k-1}(m_{1})
and
\left\{-(c+2)\varphi\right\}\in I_{k-1}(m_{2})
. These intervals are “critical intervals” in the sense that at the next stage they each split into two subintervals which are each fully contained in
[-\varphi,-2\varphi)\bmod 1
or in
[-2\varphi,-\varphi)\bmod 1
. Let
x_{1},x_{2}
be the corresponding “critical strings” with
|x_{1}|=|x_{2}|=k-2
and
[x_{1}^{R}]_{F}=m_{1}
,
[x_{2}^{R}]_{F}=m_{2}
.
Then, by construction, the strings
x_{1}\mathtt{0}
and
x_{1}\mathtt{1}
must be valid inputs and must lead to distinct outputs, and the same holds for the strings
x_{2}\mathtt{0}
and
x_{2}\mathtt{1}
.

We now prove that no two states are equivalent. First, it is clear that none of the sink states
A_{\mathtt{0}},A_{\mathtt{1}},R_{\mathtt{0}},R_{\mathtt{1}}
can be equivalent to any other state.

Now let
q=q_{x}
and
q^{\prime}=q_{x^{\prime}}
be two intermediate states corresponding to the (possibly empty) strings
x\neq x^{\prime}
. By construction of
Q
, the strings
x,x^{\prime}
are each prefixes of
x_{1}
or
x_{2}
.
Let us write
xy=x_{i}
and
x^{\prime}y^{\prime}=x_{j}
for some (possibly empty) words
y,y^{\prime}
, and
i,j\in\{1,2\}
(where we might have
i=j
).
Assume without loss of generality that
|x|\leq|x^{\prime}|
, and so
|y|\geq|y^{\prime}|
as
|x_{1}|=|x_{2}|
. We now distinguish two cases, according to whether
y=y^{\prime}
or not.

Case a:
Assume that
y\neq y^{\prime}
. Then we know that after reading
x^{\prime}y
we reach a sink state. Let
b
be the output of that sink state, i.e.,
\tau(\delta(q_{\varepsilon},x^{\prime}y))=b
and for every digit
a\in\{\mathtt{0},\mathtt{1}\}
, we have
\tau(\delta(q_{\varepsilon},x^{\prime}ya))=b
.
Since
x_{i}=xy
is a critical string, we can choose a digit
c\in\{\mathtt{0},\mathtt{1}\}
such that
\tau(\delta(q_{\varepsilon},xyc))=1-b
. Thus,
\tau(\delta(q_{x^{\prime}},yc))=b
and
\tau(\delta(q_{x},yc))=1-b
, and so the two states
q=q_{x}
and
q^{\prime}=q_{x^{\prime}}
are not equivalent.

Case b:
Assume that
y=y^{\prime}
, i.e.,
x_{i}=xy
and
x_{j}=x^{\prime}y
. Since
x\neq x^{\prime}
, we have
i\neq j
, which means that we have states corresponding to prefixes of the two distinct critical strings
x_{1},x_{2}
.
By Eq. (
4
), the parity of
|x_{1}|=|x_{2}|
determines whether reading
\mathtt{0}
after having read
x_{1}
or
x_{2}
means going to the subinterval to the left of the corresponding critical point, or to the right.
However, for one of the two critical points, going to the left means that we output
0
, whereas for the other critical point, going to the left means that we output
1
. Therefore,
\tau(\delta(q_{x},y\mathtt{0})\neq\tau(\delta(q_{x^{\prime}},y\mathtt{0}))
, and so the two states
q=q_{x}
and
q^{\prime}=q_{x^{\prime}}
are not equivalent.

Case 2:
There exists
k\geq 1
such that
c+1=F_{k+1}
and
c+2=F_{k+1}+1
. In this case, by
Remark

2
, the point
\left\{-(c+1)\varphi\right\}
becomes an endpoint of the partition at stage
k
, whereas the point
\left\{-(c+2)\varphi\right\}
becomes an endpoint of the partition at stage
k+1
. As in Case 1, let
x_{1},x_{2}
be the two critical strings, where now
|x_{2}|=|x_{1}|+1
.

Now, since
|x_{1}|,|x_{2}|
have distinct parities, we see from Eq. (
4
) that appending
\mathtt{0}
to
x_{1}
means going to the corresponding left subinterval if and only if appending
\mathtt{0}
to
x_{2}
means going to the corresponding right subinterval. Thus, appending
\mathtt{0}
either means output
0
in both cases, or output
1
in both cases.
Therefore, we can merge the last two intermediate states
q_{x_{1}}
and
q_{x_{2}}
, since they are equivalent.
Thus the total number of states remains
2(|(c)_{F}|+1)+1-g(c)
, as desired.

The proof that no other two states are equivalent in this case is analogous to the proof in Case 1.
The only difference is that the argument in Case b of the minimality proof does not work. However, Case b never occurs here. Indeed, one can check that if
c=F_{k+1}-1
, then the critical strings must be of the shapes
\mathtt{0}^{*}
and
(\varepsilon|\mathtt{0})(\mathtt{1}\mathtt{0})^{*}
, and so, in particular, the second-to-last digits in
x_{1},x_{2}
are distinct.

Overall, we have proven that the number of states in the minimal lsd-first DFAO generating
(f(i+c))_{i\geq 0}
is indeed
2(|(c)_{F}|+1)+1-g(c)
. Since both functions
c\mapsto|(c)_{F}|
and
c\mapsto g(c)
are Fibonacci-regular, the function that maps
c
to the state complexity of
(f(i+c))_{i\geq 0}
is also Fibonacci-regular; see
[
18
]
, for instance, for more details on regular sequences.

; we have
f(10)=f(11)=0

(-13)_{F}=(-\mathtt{1})\mathtt{0}\mathtt{0}\mathtt{0}\mathtt{0}
,
m_{1}=0
and
(-14)_{F}=(-\mathtt{1})\mathtt{0}\mathtt{0}\mathtt{1}\mathtt{0}\mathtt{1}\mathtt{0}
,
m_{2}=7
. So in this case, we have two paths that are given by
\mathtt{0}\mathtt{0}\mathtt{0}\mathtt{0}
and
\mathtt{0}\mathtt{1}\mathtt{0}\mathtt{1}\mathtt{0}
, and the states
q_{\mathtt{0}\mathtt{0}\mathtt{0}\mathtt{0}}
and

r_{j}^{\prime}=(-1)^{\prime}=-1=r_{j}
.
Either way, Eq. (
13
) contradicts the fact that
I\in\mathcal{P}(r)
.

Case 1b:

r_{\ell}=-2
. In this case,
I=(r_{k}\varphi,-2\varphi)\bmod 1\subset(-\varphi,-2\varphi)\bmod 1
, so by
Lemma

1
we have
f(n)=1
for all
n
with
\left\{n\varphi\right\}\in I
. Thus, it suffices to consider
a=0
. Then the rest of the proof goes through exactly as in the previous subcase, since for
a=0
we still have
((r_{k})^{(a)})^{\prime}=r_{k}
and
((-2)^{(a)})^{\prime}=-2
.

Case 2:

r_{k}=-1
. Then
I=(-\varphi,r_{\ell}\varphi)\bmod 1\subseteq(-\varphi,-2\varphi)\bmod 1
, and so as in Case 1b, we can set
a=0
.
As before,
Lemma

7
implies that for
n
with
\left\{n\varphi\right\}\in I
we have

\left\{n^{(0)}\varphi\right\}\in((r_{\ell})^{(0)}\varphi,(-1)^{(0)}\varphi)\bmod 1=((r_{\ell})^{(0)}\varphi,-2\varphi)\bmod 1.

In view of
Lemma

1
, since the least significant digit of
n^{(0)}
is of course
0
, we must actually have

\left\{n^{(0)}\varphi\right\}\in\left((r_{\ell})^{(0)}\varphi,-\varphi\right)\bmod 1=:I_{0}.

Now as in Case 1, we check that no other endpoint lies in
I_{0}
.
Assuming the contrary, we have
\left\{r_{j}\varphi\right\}\in((r_{\ell})^{(0)}\varphi,-\varphi)\bmod 1,
for some
0\leq j\leq R-2
.
Note that
(r_{\ell})^{(0)}\neq-2
because
r_{\ell}\neq-1
.
Therefore, we can apply
Lemma

8
, and obtain

\left\{r_{j}^{\prime}\varphi\right\}\in(-\varphi,r_{\ell}\varphi)\bmod 1=I,

and we can finish the proof as in Case 1a.

Case 3:

r_{k}=-2
, and so
I=(-2\varphi,r_{\ell}\varphi)\bmod 1
. Note that
r_{\ell}=-1
is impossible in this case since
r\leq-3
and
\left\{-3\varphi\right\}\in(-2\varphi,-\varphi)\bmod 1
, so this last interval is not in
\mathcal{P}(r)
.
Thus, we may assume
r_{\ell}\leq-3
.
For
a=0
,
Lemma

7
again implies that for
n
with
\left\{n\varphi\right\}\in I
we have

((r_{\ell})^{(0)}\varphi,(-2)^{(0)}\varphi)\bmod 1=((r_{\ell})^{(0)}\varphi,-3\varphi)\bmod 1:=I_{0},

and the interval
I_{0}
does not contain
\left\{-\varphi\right\},\left\{-2\varphi\right\}
. We can show that
I_{0}\in\mathcal{P}(r)
as in Case 1a (since for
a=0
we still have
((-2)^{(a)})^{\prime}=-2
).

Finally, for
a=1
,
Lemma

7
implies that for
n
with
\left\{n\varphi\right\}\in I
we have

\left\{n^{(1)}\varphi\right\}((r_{\ell})^{(1)}\varphi,-2\varphi)\bmod 1:=I_{1},

and the interval
I_{1}
does not contain
\left\{-\varphi\right\},\left\{-2\varphi\right\}
.
We check that no other endpoint lies in
I_{1}
.
Assuming the contrary, we have
\left\{r_{j}\varphi\right\}\in I_{1}
for some
0\leq j\leq R-2
. Then from
Lemma

8
(note that
(r_{\ell})^{(1)}\neq-1
) and
Lemma

6
(note that
r_{\ell}\neq-2
) we get that
\left\{r_{j}^{\prime}\varphi\right\}\in(-\varphi,r_{\ell}\varphi)\bmod 1
(since
(-2)^{\prime}=-1
).
Since
\left\{r_{j}\varphi\right\}\in I_{1}\subset(-\varphi,-2\varphi)\bmod 1
, the least significant digit of
r_{j}
is
1
. But then the second least significant digit has to be
0
. In other words, the least significant digit of
r_{j}^{\prime}
is
0
, and so in fact

\left\{r_{j}^{\prime}\varphi\right\}\in(-2\varphi,r_{\ell}\varphi)\bmod 1=I,

and we can finish the proof as in Case 1a.
