Hydrogeochemical Characteristics and Water Quality Assessment of the Carboniferous Shanxi Formation Sandstone Water in the Huafeng Coal Mine Using an Integrated GTCWM-FCE-TOPSIS Framework
Authors: Mei Qiu, Jie Dong, Tongju Xing, Ping Sun, Liandong Wang, Jianhe Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Groundwater in coal mining areas is extensively extracted to prevent coal roof water inrush, generating substantial volumes of mine water that must be properly managed and treated. Accurate hydrochemical assessment is therefore crucial for developing effective strategies for water resource protection and utilization. This study proposes an integrated groundwater quality assessment framework that synergistically combines the game theory combinatorial weighting method (GTCWM) with fuzzy comprehensive evaluation (FCE) and Order Preference by Similarity to an Ideal Solution (TOPSIS). Within this framework, GTCWM reconciles subjective weights derived from the analytic hierarchy process (AHP) with objective weights from the entropy weight method (EWM) through game theory optimization, providing a balanced weighting strategy for subsequent evaluation. FCE is then applied to classify water quality into discrete categories, while TOPSIS enables refined ranking of samples within the same quality class, thereby complementing the classification with finer differentiation. The framework was applied to assess the Carboniferous Shanxi Formation sandstone aquifer in the Huafeng coal mine. Results reveal severe groundwater quality degradation, with all samples exceeding Class V standards for TDS and 77.3% exceeding Class V for total hardness (TH). Na+, Cl– are identified as the predominant contaminants. Hydrochemical analysis indicates that water–rock interactions govern the hydrochemical evolution within this confined synclinal structure. The shallow southern aquifer receives lateral recharge and is influenced by silicate weathering, and halite/gypsum dissolution, whereas the deep northern aquifer exhibits stagnant conditions characterized by halite/gypsum dissolution, reverse cation exchange, and silicate weathering. GTCWM weight analysis identifies Cl–, TH, and Na+ as the key water quality parameters and thus the primary targets for treatment and resource recovery. GTCWM-FCE classification demonstrates that 81.8% of samples fall into Class V, indicating widespread contamination that renders the groundwater unsuitable for direct use without treatment. GTCWM-TOPSIS ranking further reveals substantial spatial heterogeneity. WQRI values range from 0.011 to 0.489 (a 45-fold difference), and exhibit a 12-fold variability within Class V samples. These findings underscore the need for differentiated management strategies. Accordingly, a tiered management strategy is proposed: minimal treatment and direct reuse for the southern high-quality zone (Class III–IV); conventional treatment and industrial reuse for the treatment-oriented zone (Class V, WQRI <0.356); and salt resource recovery (NaCl and Na2SO4) via membrane concentration and crystallization for the resource-oriented zone (Class V, WQRI >0.356). This dual-method framework provides a robust tool for groundwater quality evaluation in mining-affected areas, offering critical insights for targeted water treatment and resource utilization strategies that contribute to sustainable mining practices.

Introduction

Groundwater is a vital resource for sustaining production, livelihoods, and ecological balance in coal mining areas. On one hand, coal mining activities inevitably disturb aquifer systems, altering groundwater flow paths and hydrochemical conditions, which can lead to water quality degradation. On the other hand, water hazards pose a significant threat to safe mining operations; large-scale dewatering is often required to prevent water inrush incidents, generating substantial volumes of mine water that require proper management. Accurate groundwater quality assessment is therefore critical for developing effective mine water treatment and resource utilization strategies, an essential prerequisite for sustainable water resource management in mining areas.

In recent years, characterizing the hydrochemistry and assessing the quality of groundwater in coal mining areas has become a major research focus. To elucidate hydrochemical features and evolution mechanisms, researchers typically combine classical graphical methods (e.g., Piper trilinear diagrams, Gibbs plots, and ion ratios) with multivariate statistical techniques such as principal component analysis and correlation analysis. − For groundwater quality assessment, a diverse range of methods is employed, including single-factor evaluation method, Nemerow pollution index method, fuzzy comprehensive evaluation (FCE), normal cloud model, agglomerative hierarchical cluster analysis, factor analysis and Man-Kendall trend analysis, discrete hidden markov model, single-index unascertained measurement function and VIKOR multiattribute decision-making method, variable fuzzy set theory, and Technique for Order Preference by Similarity to an Ideal Solution (TOPSIS).

The research methods mentioned above have yielded positive results in hydrochemical characterization and water quality assessment. Nevertheless, water environment constitutes a complex system involving multiple media and pollutants. Thus, its assessment process involves multicriteria decision-making under significant uncertainty. The weighting of indicators is therefore crucial for achieving accurate assessment results. However, traditional single-method weighting approaches suffer from inherent limitations that can lead to significant deviations in assessment outcomes. , The analytic hierarchy process (AHP) and the entropy weight method (EWM) are typical representatives of subjective and objective weighting, respectively. While AHP systematically integrates expert judgments through pairwise comparisons, it is fundamentally constrained by expert subjectivity. Conversely, EWM assigns weights based objectively on data dispersion but may neglect the practical significance of parameter sand the distinct role of critical pollutants. Sole reliance on either method may lose valuable data insights or expert knowledge, underscoring the necessity for an integrated weighting strategy.

To address these challenges, this study proposes a novel integrated framework for groundwater quality assessment that synergistically combines the game theory combinatorial weighting method (GTCWM) with FCE and TOPSIS. Designed to overcome the limitations of conventional single-method evaluations, this framework integrates multiple techniques into a structured analytical process that progresses from optimized weighting and fuzzy classification to precise ranking. First, subjective and objective weights for water quality parameters are calculated using AHP and EWM, respectively. These weights are then reconciled and optimized through GTCWM to derive a consolidated weight set. This method employs game theory principles to integrate and balance subjective and objective weights, thereby mitigating the shortcomings of single weighting methods. Subsequently, water quality classification is conducted using the integrated GTCWM-FCE approach. Finally, a refined water quality ranking is achieved based on GTCWM-TOPSIS. Applied to the sandstone groundwater of the No. 4 coal seam roof in the Carboniferous Shanxi Formation at Huafeng coal mine, this framework provides a scientific basis for developing targeted water treatment and resource utilization strategies in the study area.

Study Area

Located in Huafeng Town, Ningyang County, Tai’an City, Shandong Province (Figure ), the Huafeng coal mine covers an area of approximately 16.49 km2. Geographically, it lies between 35°51′49″–35°57′52″ N and 117°07′28″–117°11′11″ E, with a strike length of 7.7 km and a dip width of 2.14 km. The mining area is bounded by the floodplain and alluvial plain of the Chaiwen River to the north and is drained by the Gucheng River, a tributary of the Chaiwen River, in the east. Its southern part features low-lying mountains composed of Cambrian and Ordovician limestone, while the central region is characterized by gently rolling hills of Paleogene conglomerates.

The base map in (a) is from the Standard Map Service System, Ministry of Natural Resources (approval No. GS (2019)­1674), with no modifications to the base map boundaries.

Structurally, the Huafeng coal mine possesses an asymmetrical, dustpan-shaped syncline with a 10–15° northeastward plunge. The coalfield exhibits a relatively simple structural framework, comprising secondary folds, localized normal faults having displacements of no more than 30 m, and minor thrust faults. The stratigraphic sequence is dominated by Ordovician, Carboniferous-Permian, Paleogene, and Quaternary systems. The main aquifers are the Quaternary sand and gravel unit, the Paleogene conglomerate, sandstone of the Shanxi Formation, the thin-bedded limestones within the Taiyuan Formation, the Xujiazhuang limestone, and the Ordovician limestone.

Currently, the No. 4 and No. 6 coal seams are the primary targets being mined in the study area. Their direct water sources are the Shanxi Formation sandstone and the thin-bedded limestone layers of the Taiyuan Formation. During the mining of the No. 4 coal seam, multiple roof water inrushes have occurred, and the consequent predrainage for roof water hazard control generates large amounts of mine water. Hence, assessing the hydrochemistry and water quality of the overlying sandstone aquifer is essential for guiding its treatment and utilization.

Material and Methods

Sample Collection and Testing

In this study, a total of 22 water samples were collected from the sandstone aquifer overlying the No. 4 coal seam for hydrochemical analysis and water quality evaluation. Due to the difficulty of direct sampling from the No. 4 coal seam roof aquifer, the samples were obtained from borehole drainage and water inrushes from the roof sandstone at working faces. As shown in Figure , the sampling points are distributed across most of the areal extent of the aquifer.

A rigorous cleaning protocol was followed, wherein all sampling containers were rinsed three times with deionized water and subsequently three times with the native water to be collected. All samples were delivered to the laboratory within 24 h of collection and stored under refrigeration at 4 °C to preserve their integrity until analysis. The measured parameters included Na+, Ca2+, Mg2+, Fe2+, NH4 +, Cl–, SO4 2–, HCO3 –, NO3 –, NO2 –, total hardness (TH), total dissolved solids (TDS), and pH. Analytical Methods

Determination of Weights Based on Game Theory Combinatorial Weighting Method

Assigning appropriate weights to evaluation parameters is critical for reliable water quality assessment. Single-weighting methodswhether subjective (e.g., AHP, which captures expert judgment but risks bias) or objective (e.g., EWM, which is data-driven but may lack practical relevance)have inherent limitations. This study therefore employs a game theory combinatorial weighting method to integrate subjective and objective weights, minimizing intermethod discrepancies and yielding a robust combined weight vector. This vector subsequently serves as the foundation for both fuzzy comprehensive evaluation and TOPSIS ranking, as detailed in the following steps.

Determining Subjective Weights Based on AHP.

The Analytic hierarchy process is a subjective weighting method that constructs a judgment matrix based on the relative importance of different parameters to derive their respective weights. The main steps are as follows.

Judgment matrix construction.

Parameters are compared pairwise, and their relative importance is quantified to construct the judgment matrix. Instead of employing the conventional nine-scale expert scoring method commonly used in AHP, this study treats each water sample as an independent “expert” and derives the importance levels of parameters from the single-factor pollution index. Specifically, for the sth sample (s = 1, 2, ..., l), the scale value for the ith parameter is calculated as 1 di(s)=ci(s)c0i where d i (s) is the scale value of the ith parameter in the sth sample; c i (s) is its measured concentration; c oi is the mean value of the corresponding standard limits across all water quality classes (determined by the weighted average method). This scale value represents the pollution contribution of parameter i in a given sample, thereby serving as a measure of its relative importance.

To obtain an overall scale value representing the importance of each parameter across all samples, the average of the single-factor indices is used 2 d̅i=1l∑s=1ldi(s)

Based on these scale values, the relative importance between any two parameters F i and F j is obtained as a ij = d i /d j , the judgment matrix A is then constructed as 3 A=[a11a12a21a22···a1m···a2m······am1am2·········amm] where each element a ij is the quantified judgment of the relative importance of parameters F i over F j . This matrix is subsequently used for consistency check and weight calculation.

Consistency check.

To ensure the reliability of the judgment matrix, a consistency check is performed. The consistency index CI is defined as follows 4 CI=λmax−mm−1 where λmax is the maximum eigenvalue of A; m is the total number of quality evaluation parameters; A CI value of zero indicates perfect consistency; value close to zero suggest acceptable consistency, while larger values reflect greater inconsistency.

To assess whether the inconsistency is within an acceptable range, the consistency ratio (CR) is introduced 5 CR=CIRI where RI is the random consistency index, a standard value depending on the matrix order. If CR < 0.1, the inconsistency is considered acceptable, and the matrix passes the consistency test. Otherwise, the judgment matrix must be revised.

Weight calculation.

Once the consistency check is passed, the weight vector W AHP=(w 1, w 2, ..., w m)T is obtained as the normalized eigenvector corresponding to the maximum eigenvalue λmax of matrix A, satisfying 6 AWAHP=λmaxWAHP

This eigenvector represents the subjective weights of the evaluation parameters derived from the AHP.

Combined Weighting Based on Game Theory.

To derive optimal composite weights that integrate both subjective and objective information, a game theory-based weighting method is employed. Unlike single-weighting approaches, this model effectively balances the advantages of different weighting techniques. The core idea is to synthesize multiple weight vectors by minimizing the between the combined weight and each basic weight, thereby enhancing the robustness of the final weighting results. The procedure consists of the following steps.

Construction of basic weight vectors.

Suppose p different weighting methods are used to determine the weights of m paraments. A set of basic weight vectors is formed as 11 ωk=(ωk1,ωk2,···,ωkm),k=1,2,...,p where ω k denotes the weight vector obtained by the kth method.

A linear combination of these p vectors can be expressed as 12 ω=∑k=1pαkωkT where ω is the combined weight vector; α k > 0 are the linear combination coefficients; ω k T is the transpose of ω k (treated as a column vector).

Groundwater Quality Ranking Based on the GTCWM-TOPSIS Method

TOPSIS is a multicriteria decision-making method that ranks alternatives based on their relative closeness to the positive and negative ideal solutions derived from a normalized matrix. While the fuzzy comprehensive evaluation method classifies groundwater into discrete quality categories, it cannot differentiate pollution levels among samples within the same class. To overcome this limitation, this study integrates the game theory combinatorial weights with TOPSIS to establish a refined, comparable ranking of groundwater quality across all samples. Conventional Hydrochemical Mechanisms

The Gibbs diagram is a classical tool for identifying dominant mechanisms controlling water chemistry, delineating three primary fields: precipitation-dominated, water-rock interaction-dominated, and evaporation-dominated regions. , The diagram was initially developed for surface water, but a lot of recent work has also used it for deep groundwater. − In our study area­(Figure ), most samples plot in the evaporation-dominated field. One might initially take this as evidence that evaporation drives the water chemistry. Geologically, however, this view is problematic. For one thing, the aquifer is deep, confined, and located in a relatively closed synclinal, where evapotranspiration is physically impossible. For another, even if one argued for paleo-evaporation, the Permian Shanxi Formation in Shandong was deposited under a warm, humid climate, which directly contradicts that idea. , This contradiction reveals a genuine limitation of the Gibbs diagram when applied to deep groundwater. Marandi and Shandb made a similar point: the processes represented on the Gibbs diagram are not always directly applicable for interpreting groundwater chemistry, and relying solely on the position of samples within the plot can be misleading. Water-rock interactions can occur anywhere on the diagram, especially in groundwater systems where residence times are extremely long (often exceeding 12,000 years), allowing ample time for reactions to take place. Moreover, groundwater chemical is governed by a complex range of factors that differ fundamentally from those in surface water, including mineral availability and solubility, geochemical environment (e.g., pH and redox potential), ion exchange, and other.

The preceding analysis indicates that water–rock interactions, rather than evaporative processes, are the primary force controlling groundwater chemistry in this confined setting. These interactions encompass multiple hydrogeochemical processes, including the dissolution/weathering of carbonate rocks, silicate rocks, and evaporite minerals (e.g., halite and gypsum), as well as ion exchange. A series of ionic ratio diagrams was used to identify the specific processes involved.

The combined evidence points to cation exchange as a key process modulating the divalent cation budget. The (Ca2++Mg2+)/(HCO3 –+SO4 2–) milliequivalent ratio serves a robust tool for distinguishing the contributions of carbonate/sulfate dissolution from those of cation exchange. If carbonate and sulfate dissolution were the sole controls on groundwater chemistry, samples would plot along the 1:1 line. As illustrated in Figure f, southern samples generally fall near this line, indicating that dissolution processes (primarily sulfate dissolution) dominate their hydrochemical composition. In contrast, all northern samples plot substantially above the 1:1 line, with (Ca2++Mg2+) exceeding (HCO3 –+SO4 2–) by several to tens of times. This systematic enrichment of divalent cations provides compelling evidence for reverse cation exchangea conclusion strongly corroborated by the prevalence of γNa+/γCl– < 1 (Figure a) and positive CAI values (Figure c) in these samples.

In summary, the groundwater in the study area exhibits exceptionally high TDS, a distinct ionic sequence of Na+ > Ca2+ > Mg2+ and Cl– > SO4 2– > HCO3 –, and a clear south-to-north spatial gradient. High TDS arises from the dissolution of evaporite minerals (halite supplying Na+ and Cl–; gypsum supplying Ca2+ and SO4 2–) and silicate weathering under prolonged water–rock interaction in a confined synclinal structure. The cationic sequence reflects multiple processes: halite dissolution supplies the bulk of Na+; however, Na+ depletion (γNa+/γCl– < 1) in northern samples indicates reverse cation exchange, which elevates Ca2+ to the second position; meanwhile, Mg2+, though lowest in concentration, derives mainly from silicate weathering (biotite, chlorite), as evidenced by Mg2+/Na+ ratios consistently exceeding 0.1. The anionic sequence is characterized by conservative Cl– from halite, moderate SO4 2– from gypsum, and extreme HCO3 – depletion in the north (<5 mequiv/L). This depletion is caused by calcite precipitation induced by excess Ca2+ from cation exchange, which consumes bicarbonate from silicate weathering or carbonate dissolution. Spatially, southern samples (lower TDS) lie in the recharge zone, receiving infiltration from the overlying Paleogene aquifer, and are dominated by halite/gypsum dissolution (Na+/Cl– ≈ 1, Ca2+/SO4 2– ≈ 1) and silicate weathering. Northern samples (high TDS) are characterized by halite/gypsum dissolution, reverse cation exchange, and silicate weathering, as indicated by positive CAI, high Ca2+/SO4 2– ratios, and excess (Ca2++Mg2+) over (HCO3 –+SO4 2–). Thus, the high-TDS, Cl–Na waters with elevated Ca2+ and Mg2+ are attributed to the interplay of halite/gypsum dissolution, silicate weathering, and reverse cation exchange under long-term water–rock interaction.

The integrated assessment results provide a scientific foundation for developing targeted mine water management strategies. The weight hierarchy identifies Cl–, TH, and Na+ as the key targets for treatment and resource recovery. The FCE classification provides regulatory baselines, while the TOPSIS ranking enables fine-grained differentiation of contamination severity within the same regulatory class. Based on these findings, a unified framework for mine water treatment and resource utilization is proposed, integrating refined spatial zonation with WQRI-based tiered strategies, as illustrated in Figure and detailed below.

Southern Relatively High-Quality Water Zone (Class III–IV: S15, S19–S21)

Characterized by Class III–IV water and top-ranked WQRI values (0.011–0.026), this zone represents the only area with relatively better water quality in the study area. The remaining 18 samples (81.8%) are classified as Class V, with WQRI values ranging from 0.041 to 0.489. Depending on concentration levels and market demand, freezing or evaporation crystallization can extract Glauber’s salt (Na2SO4·10H2O) or anhydrous Na2SO4, both of which have established markets in detergent, glass, textile, and paper industries.

The sequence may begin with reverse osmosis to concentrate the brine, followed by selective crystallization to sequentially recover sodium sulfate and sodium chloride based on solubility differences. This approach maximizes resource recovery, minimizes waste, and ultimately achieves zero liquid dischargean increasingly important environmental objective for sustainable mining operations. Conclusions

This study successfully implemented an integrated groundwater quality assessment framework combining the GTCWM-FCE and GTCWM-TOPSIS methods for evaluating groundwater in the sandstone aquifer of the No. 4 coal seam roof of the Carboniferous Shanxi Formation. The key findings and implications are summarized as follows.(1)Groundwater in the study area exhibits severe quality degradation, with TDS exceeding Class V standards in all samples and TH exceeding limits in 77.3% of samples. The hydrochemical profile shows cation dominance in the order Na+ > Ca2+ > Mg2+ and anion sequence Cl– > SO4 2– > HCO3 –. Principal contaminants Na+, Cl–, and SO4 2– display widespread exceedances, particularly in northern deep aquifer zones where 86.4% of samples surpass Class V standards for SO4 2–. While Fe2+, NH4 +, and NO2 – generally comply with Class I–III standards in most areas, localized contamination hotspots exist.(2)Hydrogeological evolution within the confined synclinal structure is governed by water–rock interactions. The northern deep zone is predominantly influenced by halite/gypsum dissolution, reverse cation exchange, silicate weathering, as evidenced by systematic Na+ depletion (γNa+/γCl– < 1), positive chloro-alkaline indices (CAI >0), and excess (Ca2++Mg2+) relative to (HCO3 –+SO4 2–). While the southern zone, located at the exposed syncline limb receiving infiltration from the overlying Paleogene aquifer, is dominated by silicate weathering and halite/gypsum dissolution. These coupled reactions operating over long residence times collectively produce the characteristic high-TDS, Cl–Na waters with elevated Ca2+ and Mg2+.(3)GTCWM weight analysis identifies Cl–, TH, and Na+ as the key parameters controlling water quality and thus the key targets for treatment and resource recovery. GTCWM-FCE classification reveals that 81.8% of samples fall into Class V, indicating systematic and regional water quality degradation. GTCWM-TOPSIS ranking uncovers substantial spatial heterogeneity, with WQRI values ranging from 0.011 to 0.489, and a 12-fold variability within Class V samples. Based on these results, a tiered management strategy is proposed: (i) Southern relatively high-quality water zone (Class III–IV): priority for protection and direct reuse after minimal treatment; (ii) Treatment-oriented zone (Class V water zone, WQRI <0.356): conventional treatment (e.g., sedimentation, filtration, lime softening, or ion exchange) for industrial reuse; (iii) Resource-oriented zone (Class V water zone, WQRI >0.356): membrane concentration coupled with evaporation crystallization to recover industrial-grade NaCl and Na2SO4, achieving zero liquid discharge and economic value.(4)Several limitations should be acknowledged. Due to difficulties in sampling the coal seam roof aquifer, the 22 samples collected from mine water inrush and drainage holes may not fully capture the natural hydrochemical evolution, though mining impacts are considered minor compared to prolonged water–rock interactions. The lack of seasonal sampling precludes characterization of temporal dynamics related to recharge events or mining activities. The absence of isotopic tracers (e.g., δ18O, δD, δ34S) limits precise quantification of water sources and sulfate origins. Future research should expand the sampling network, incorporate multi-isotope techniques, and develop coupled hydrogeochemical models to predict water quality evolution under different mining scenarios, thereby strengthening the scientific basis for sustainable mine water management and resource utilization.
