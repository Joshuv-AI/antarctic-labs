Clinical profile of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease patients with and without type 2 inflammation: A comparative study
Authors: Mohammed Al Ghobain, Waleed Alsowayan, Afra Juma, Altayb Yousif, Renad Almogren, Razan S Al Kahtani, Munira Aldossari, Horia Zurnuq
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:The association between chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) with type 2 inflammation and comorbidities and functional outcomes remains incompletely defined. We aimed to compare clinical characteristics, comorbidities, and exacerbation risk between COPD patients with and without type 2 inflammation.METHODS:This was a retrospective observational study. Patients with spirometry-confirmed COPD between January 2020 and July 2025 were included. Patients were stratified by serum eosinophil count into type 2 inflammation COPD (≥300 cells/μL) and nontype 2 inflammation COPD (<300 cells/μL). Demographics, comorbidities, pulmonary function, and exacerbation history were assessed. Logistic regression identified independent predictors of exacerbation.RESULTS:The total number of patients were 270 (mean age: 70 years; 91.5% of males). Among them, 54.8% had type 2 inflammation COPD. Those with type 2 inflammation had more comorbidities (mean 3 vs. 2; P = 0.001), higher prevalence of ischemic heart disease (31.1%; P = 0.042), and increased exacerbation frequency (mean 2 vs. 1 per year; P = 0.006). Predictors of exacerbation included older age (odds ratio [OR] 1.02, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.00–1.04), high eosinophilic count (OR 1.002, 95% CI 1.001–1.003), number of exacerbations in the past 12 months (OR 1.04, 95% CI 1.01–1.06), elevated right ventricular pressure (OR 1.06, 95% CI 1.03–1.10), lower postbronchodilator FEV1 (OR 1.03, 95% CI 1.01–1.05) and multiple comorbidities (OR 1.18, 95% CI 1.05–1.34).CONCLUSION:COPD with type 2 inflammation is associated with a higher prevalence of ischemic heart disease and a higher prevalence and higher risk of exacerbation compared with nontype 2 inflammation. Serum eosinophil count is a valuable biomarker for phenotyping and risk stratification.

Background

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a common and complex progressive respiratory disorder characterized by persistent airflow limitation that is not fully reversible. It is primarily associated with an abnormal inflammatory response of the lungs to noxious particles or gases, especially cigarette smoke and air pollution.[1] COPD is a major contributor to global morbidity and mortality. The World Health Organization (WHO) estimates that over 3.2 million people die from COPD annually, ranking it as the third leading cause of death worldwide – a figure projected to rise due to aging populations and ongoing exposure to risk factors.[2] Moreover, COPD is associated with a huge economic and social burden and healthcare resources utilization encompassing direct medical costs and indirect costs such as reduced productivity and premature death. Beyond impaired lung function and diminished quality of life, COPD is frequently complicated by acute exacerbations and comorbidities, including cardiovascular disease, metabolic syndrome, and lung cancer, which further elevate its clinical and public health impact.[34]

From a pathophysiological standpoint, COPD involves a multifaceted interplay of airway remodeling, parenchymal destruction, and pulmonary vascular changes. These alterations are driven by chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, and a protease-antiprotease imbalance, ultimately resulting in airflow obstruction and impaired gas exchange.[5] On a cellular level, airway inflammation is marked by infiltration with neutrophils, macrophages, and CD8+ T lymphocytes. These immune cells release pro-inflammatory mediators such as interleukin-8 (IL-8), tumor necrosis factor-alpha, and leukotriene B4 (LTB4), thereby perpetuating tissue injury.[6]

Unlike asthma, which is typically eosinophilic, inflammation in COPD is predominantly neutrophilic. However, a distinct eosinophilic phenotype (type 2 inflammation) has been identified in a significant subset of patients. This phenotype is increasingly recognized for its role in predicting therapeutic response, particularly to inhaled corticosteroids (ICS). Eosinophils may contribute to airway remodeling and hyperresponsiveness via the release of granule proteins and cytokines, including interleukin-5 (IL-5) and eotaxin.[7] Biomarkers such as serum and sputum eosinophil counts have emerged as practical tools to phenotype COPD patients and guide therapy. High serum eosinophils level is associated with high risk of COPD exacerbations and better response to ICS.[8]

The eosinophilic phenotype (COPD with type 2 inflammation) is estimated to occur in approximately 20-40% of COPD patients, although considerable variability exists between individuals and over time.[9] Clinical studies reported conflicting results about the relation between COPD with type 2 inflammation and risk of exacerbations, worsening lung function, cardiovascular risk, and worsening outcomes; however, some of these studies indicate that such patients may experience more frequent exacerbations but also derive greater benefit from corticosteroid-based regimens.[10]

Given the heterogeneity of COPD and the growing evidence about COPD with type 2 inflammation (eosinophilic phenotype), this study aims to describe and compare the clinical characteristics, risk of comorbidities, impact on exacerbation, and lung function between COPD with type 2 inflammation (eosinophilic phenotype), and COPD without type 2 inflammation (noneosinophilic phenotype). By elucidating the role of eosinophilic inflammation in COPD, we hope to contribute to the refinement of patient stratification and the optimization of COPD management strategies.

Materials and Methods

This is a retrospective observational electronic medical records review study from two tertiary care centers in Riyadh city, Saudi Arabia, namely King Abdulaziz Medical City and Security Forces Hospital. The study participants included all patients with a diagnosis of COPD who met the inclusion criteria between January 2020 and July 2025. The research protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board at King Abdullah International Medical Research Center. Informed consent was not needed as this is a retrospective observational study.

The inclusion criteria included all patients aged ≥40 years with a confirmed diagnosis of COPD based on reliable and good quality spirometry testing, according to the GOLD 2024 guidelines, for example, postbronchodilator forced expiratory volume in 1 s over forced vital capacity (FVC) (post BD FEV1/FVC) ratio <0.7[1] or the presence of emphysema on CT scan of the chest. Patients were excluded if spirometry data were unavailable or unreliable due to poor quality, or if post-BD testing was not performed. Patients with a diagnosis of asthma, asthma-COPD overlap symptoms, presence of bronchiectasis, lung cancer, or restrictive lung diseases were excluded. The patients were divided into two groups based on the serum eosinophil count: Group 1: Type 2 inflammation COPD group (serum eosinophilic count ≥300 cells/μl) and Group 2: Nontype 2 inflammation COPD group (serum eosinophilic count <300 cells/μl) [Figure 1].

Demographic characteristics were collected, including age, gender, Body Mass Index (BMI), and smoking history, in addition to the presence of associated medical comorbidities such as diabetes, dyslipidemia, hypertension, obstructive sleep apnea, ischemic heart disease, and chronic kidney disease. Ischemic heart diseases (IHD) included both acute or chronic coronary syndrome (unstable angina or myocardial infarction) and were diagnosed by cardiologists according to the European and American cardiac guidelines.[1112]

Other data collected included the number of inhalers used by the patients, in addition to the use of home oxygen and home BiPAP. Exacerbation of COPD is defined based on the GOLD 2024 report as an event marked by worsening dyspnea and/or cough with sputum over a period of up to 14 days. The number of exacerbations in the previous 12 months was collected and were classified based on severity as mild exacerbations (ER visits only), moderate exacerbations (admission to regular ward), and severe exacerbations (admission to intensive care unit [ICU]). Pulmonary function testing data collected, including pre- and postbronchodilator FVC in 1 s (FEV1), FVC, FEV1/FVC%, vital capacity (VC), residual volume (RV), and diffusion capacity (DLCO).

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using Stata version 15 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). Continuous variables were summarized as mean and standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Comparisons between patients with Type 2 inflammation and those with nontype 2 inflammation were performed using the independent t-test for continuous variables and the Chi-square or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate. For variables where the distribution of differences was of interest, mean differences with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were reported. Multivariate binary logistic regression was used to identify predictors of COPD exacerbations; Variables were entered using the enter method based on the total number of ER visits (exacerbations), with results expressed as odds ratios (ORs) and 95% CIs. P ≤0.05 was considered statistically significant, 85%–95% power to detect at least one meaningful predictor.

Results

Overall, 1,500 patients labeled with a diagnosis of COPD in the electronic medical records systems in both centers were assessed for inclusion criteria; Patients were excluded if spirometry data were unavailable or unreliable due to poor quality, or if post-BD testing was not performed. History of asthma, asthma-overlap symptoms, bronchiectasis, lung cancer, or restrictive lung diseases was also excluded. A total of 270 patients met the inclusion criteria and were included in the data analysis. Of those patients, 148 (54.8%) were type 2 inflammation COPD (serum eosinophilic count ≥300 cells/μL), and 122 (45.2%) were nontype 2 inflammation COPD (serum eosinophilic count <300 cells/μL).

The demographic and clinical characteristics of patients are summarized in Table 1. The mean age was 70 years, and 91.5% were males. 143 (53%) were current smokers, 92 (34.1%) were ex-smokers. The mean post bronchodilators FEV1, FVC, and FEV1/FVC% were 55.8%, 75.2%, and 70.8%, respectively. The presence of emphysema in CT scans of the chest was 93%. Almost half of the patients were diabetic (48.9%) or hypertensive (57.4%) or had dyslipidemia (23.7%). The prevalence of IHD was 31.1%. Among all patients, 91 (33.7%) were using home oxygen, and 44 (16.3%) were using home BiPAP. The mean number of all exacerbations, including admission to the ward or ICU in the past 12 months, was 2.3 (±2.9) exacerbations per patient per year.

Multiple logistic regression was performed to identify independent predictors for COPD exacerbation. BMI, smoking, using home oxygen, or home BiPAP were not significantly correlated with a higher risk of COPD exacerbation. High serum eosinophilic count was found to be independent predictors for COPD exacerbation (OR 1.002, 95% CI 1.001–1.003), after adjusting for age, BMI, smoking status, post BD FEV1%, and number of comorbidities.

Other independent predictors of COPD exacerbation included older age (OR 1.02, 95% CI 1.00–1.04), number of exacerbations in the past 12 months (OR 1.04, 95% CI 1.01–1.06), elevated right ventricular pressure (OR 1.06, 95% CI 1.03–1.10), lower postbronchodilator FEV1 (OR 1.03, 95% CI 1.01–1.05) and multiple comorbidities (OR 1.18, 95% CI 1.05–1.34) [Table 4].

Discussion

In our study, we compared clinical characteristics, comorbidities, pulmonary function parameters, and the exacerbation rate between COPD patients with type 2 inflammation (eosinophilic phenotype), and COPD patients without type 2 inflammation (noneosinophilic phenotype). Our results showed that there were no significant differences between the two groups regarding age, gender, smoking status, BMI, using home oxygen or home BiPAP, number of inhalers used by the patients. Moreover, we found out that the total number of comorbidities was higher in patients with type 2 inflammation COPD than in nontype 2 inflammation COPD; however, diabetes, hypertension, and dyslipidemia are highly prevalent in both groups with no significant differences. Interestingly, patients with type 2 Inflammation COPD have a higher prevalence rate of IHD and have a higher prevalence rate of exacerbation than patients with nontype 2 inflammation COPD. Factors contributed to a higher rate of exacerbation are age, high serum eosinophilic count, number of exacerbations in the past 12 months, elevated right ventricular pressure (pulmonary hypertension), lower Post BD FEV1 and multiple comorbidities In contrast, BMI and smoking status were not significantly associated with exacerbation risk.

Nearly 30% of COPD exacerbations are associated with eosinophilic airway inflammation,[13] and this is evident in bronchial biopsy[14] and sputum eosinophil levels.[15] Our findings showed that COPD patients with type 2 inflammation have a higher prevalence rate of exacerbation than COPD patients with nontype 2 inflammation. There are few studies in our region addressing the relationship between serum eosinophilic count and the risk of COPD exacerbation, hospital stay, and outcomes. Albanna et al. demonstrated that COPD patients with type 2 inflammation (eosinophilic phenotype) had better clinical outcomes than COPD patients with nontype 2 inflammation (noneosinophilic phenotype).[16] In another single-center study, no significant differences were found between the two COPD groups regarding clinical characteristics and exacerbation history.[17] A retrospective study from Syria reported that COPD patients with nontype 2 inflammation (noneosinophilic phenotype) have worse hospital stays and more frequent ICU admissions in comparison to COPD patients with type 2 inflammation (eosinophilic phenotype).[18] A study from Oman showed that COPD patients with type 2 inflammation (eosinophilic phenotype) had a reduced length of hospital stay but an increased risk of readmission in 1 year.[19] Another study from Turkey showed that COPD patients with type 2 inflammation (eosinophilic phenotype) have shorter hospital stays and lower readmission rates.[20] A prospective 1-year follow-up study from Iran revealed that high eosinophil count increased the risk of COPD exacerbation but not the severity of exacerbation or the hospital stays or the outcomes.[21]

Several global studies demonstrated that COPD patients with type 2 inflammation (eosinophilic phenotype) had lower ICU length of stay and lower mortality rate[2223] and better FEV1[24] in comparison to COPD patients with nontype 2 inflammation (noneosinophilic phenotype). Wu et al. found a significant correlation between high eosinophil count and an increased risk of exacerbation among Chinese COPD patients.[25] A study from Denmark found that there was a higher rate of exacerbations for 5 years of follow-up in COPD patients with the eosinophil levels >2%.[26] COPD patients with nontype 2 inflammation (noneosinophilic phenotype) experienced worse clinical outcomes (higher ICU admission and higher mortality) than COPD patients with type 2 inflammation (eosinophilic phenotype).[27] All these studies are consistent with our findings that COPD patients with type 2 inflammation (eosinophilic phenotype) have a higher risk of exacerbation, but in our study, we addressed only predictors of exacerbation; we did not address the hospital stay and outcomes, as in the cited studies. On the contrary, post hoc analysis from 11 Phase III and IV randomized clinical trials observed no clinically significant correlation between the baseline blood eosinophil count and exacerbation risk among patients with COPD.[28] These findings show that COPD patients with type 2 inflammation have a higher exacerbation rate, but quick recovery and shorter hospital stay indicate that patients with higher eosinophil counts might response better to ICS therapy. Therefore, serum eosinophils count can be used as a biomarker to predict exacerbation risk and outcomes of patients with COPD. It is worth noticing that most of these studies are retrospective in nature and done in different regions with different populations and different sample sizes.

In our study, diabetes, hypertension, and dyslipidemia are highly prevalent in both COPD groups, and these findings are consistent with other Saudi studies, which showed a high prevalence rate of these diseases in patients with COPD.[14] Our findings are not surprising, as most of our study patients are elderly and at higher risk for metabolic diseases. In our study, COPD patients with type 2 inflammation (eosinophilic phenotype) have higher prevalence rates of IHD than patients with nontype 2 inflammation. Though the finding of such a relationship in our study in interesting, it should not be interpreted as a fact or causal relationship looking to the nature of our retrospective study; it is an area for future prospective research. In general, COPD is associated with a higher risk of developing IHD, but data about COPD phenotypes, eosinophilic count, and risk of IHD are limited and not consistent to make a solid conclusion and it is an area of future research, some reports claimed that high eosinophilic count is associated with risk of atherosclerosis, which is a risk factor for IHD.[29] In our study, predictors that contributed to a higher rate of exacerbation are age, high eosinophilic count, number of exacerbations in the past 12 months, elevated right ventricular pressure (pulmonary hypertension), and lower Post BD FEV1. Multiple comorbidities were predictors for the risk of exacerbations as well. This reflects the systematic nature of the disease, which is associated with systematic inflammation and increase risk of metabolic diseases and exacerbations. Alshehri et al. found that heart failure, GOLD 3 severity, use of triple therapy, and use of noninvasive ventilation were significantly correlated with a higher risk of COPD exacerbation.[17]

Severed limitations should be considered during interpretations of our findings; it is a retrospective data review in nature, evaluating serum eosinophilic count, which can be variable over time. Other limitations we did not collect data about the use of ICS, but we included data about the number of inhalers used by the patients. Though the higher number use of inhalers by the patients, the more likely patients are to use ICS, but there is no significant difference between the two groups in regard to the number of inhalers. We retrospectively collected data during the COVID-19 lockdown period, which may affects hospital admissions rate and the number of sample size. Other limitations of the study are that most of our patients are males, as COPD is more common in males, as smoking is more common in males than in females. We collected data from hospitalized patients with moderate-to-severe COPD, while mild COPD patients are usually unreachable because they are in the community or undiagnosed.

The strengths of the study are a multicenter study done in tertiary care centers with very strict inclusion and exclusion criteria. Certainly, future research in the field of COPD phenotypes should focus on a prospective follow-up study which should expand to the country level with a larger sample size to better understand the clinical profile, comorbidities, exacerbation risk, and outcomes in COPD patients with and without type 2 inflammation.

Conclusion

COPD with type 2 inflammation is associated with a higher prevalence of ischemic heart disease and a higher prevalence and risk of exacerbation compared with nontype 2 inflammation. Serum eosinophil count is a valuable biomarker for phenotyping and risk stratification.
