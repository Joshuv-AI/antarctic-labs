Abstract
. We consider an iterative eigensolver for Schrödinger equations that constructs low-rank approximations of eigenfunctions with accuracy-adapted ranks, with particular focus on fermionic Schrödinger equations in second-quantized form and on matrix product state approximations enforcing particle number conservation. We provide a complete analysis of a solver based on preconditioned inverse iteration combined with rank truncation and propose a generalization to subspace iteration for the joint approximation of several eigenspaces. The practical performance of the method is illustrated by numerical tests for several model problems. Keywords. second quantization, particle number conservation, matrix product states, preconditioned inverse iteration, subspace iteration Mathematics Subject Classification. 15A69, 65F15, 65Y20, 65Z05



1.
Introduction

In this paper, we consider eigenvalue solvers operating with convergence guarantees on low-rank tensor representations, with particular focus on Hamiltonians associated to fermionic quantum systems in second-quantized representation.
The fermionic nature of the particles entails antisymmetry of the sought eigenfunctions under exchange of particle coordinates. Such functions are commonly approximated by antisymmetrized tensor products of single-particle basis functions, which in this context are called
orbitals
.
Elementary antisymmetrized tensor products, called
Slater determinants
, are determined by selections of distinct orbitals.

In second quantization, linear combinations of Slater determinants are parameterized by
occupation numbers:
each selection of orbitals corresponds to a set of binary indices into a tensor holding the coefficients of the linear combination.
In many cases of interest, these high-order occupation number tensors are observed to have efficient low-rank approximations in terms of matrix product states (MPS, also known as tensor trains) or more general tree tensor networks.
Constrainting the problem to a fixed particle number leads to certain block-sparse structures in the components of matrix product states.

The eigensolvers in low-rank format that we consider here are geared towards the particular setting of second-quantized formulations and block sparsity with particular attention to structure of operator representations, but the basic elements of the analysis given here may be of independent interest.
We obtain a method that is guaranteed to converge in terms of the
H^{1}
-norm angle with respect to the true eigenspace. At the same time, the achieved error bounds are balanced with the ranks of MPS approximations, where we obtain a near-optimal relation to ranks of best approximations of comparable accuracy.
This is achieved by a combination of error reduction based on preconditioned inverse iteration with adaptive rank truncation.

1.1.
Eigenvalue problems for Hamiltonians in second quantization

Our considerations are motivated by applications in quantum chemistry, where the central object of study are eigenvalue problems of the electronic Schrödinger Hamiltonian, which for a system with
N
electrons takes the form

(1.1)

H=-\frac{1}{2}\Delta+\sum_{i=1}^{N}V(x_{i})+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\neq j}\frac{1}{\lVert x_{i}-x_{j}\rVert_{2}}

with electron coordinates
x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
,
where
V
is an external potential, such as a Coulomb potential corresponding to the electrostatic attraction of atomic nuclei.
We make the standard assumption (which holds true in the absence of magnetic fields) of real-valued
V
, which implies that the sought eigenvalues and eigenfunctions are also real-valued.

Due to the fermionic nature of electrons, the physically relevant eigenfunctions, called wave functions, additionally need to satisfy certain antisymmetry conditions. There we need to take into account the additional spin degree of freedom of each particle, which can take the values
\pm\frac{1}{2}
; the complete wave functions are determined by their spin components that are functions on
\mathbb{R}^{3N}
. Each such spin component
u
needs to satisfy the antisymmetry property

u(\ldots,x_{i},\ldots,x_{j},\ldots)=-u(\ldots,x_{j},\ldots,x_{i},\ldots)

whenever
i\neq j
correspond to particles of equal spin.
For simplicity, in what follows we will always assume spin configurations where all spins are equal, so that the sought eigenfunctions are antisymmetric under exchange of any pair of particle coordinates in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
With this restriction, the main aim is then to find
u\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3N})\cap\bigl(\bigwedge_{i=1}^{N}L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\bigr)\subset H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3N})
,
u\neq 0
, such that
Hu=\lambda u
with minimal
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
.

We assume a family of orbitals
\phi_{i}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
,
i\in\mathbb{N}
, that are an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
. The Slater determinants
\phi_{i_{1}}\wedge\phi_{i_{2}}\wedge\ldots\wedge\phi_{i_{N}}
for
i_{1}<i_{2}<\ldots<i_{N}
are then an orthonormal basis of
\bigwedge_{i=1}^{N}L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
.
In what follows, we consider approximations using the
K
orbitals
\{\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{K}\}
.
Any linear combination of Slater determinants formed from these
K
orbitals, that is,

\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\ldots<i_{N}\leq K}c_{i}\,\phi_{i_{1}}\wedge\ldots\varphi_{i_{N}}

with
c_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
, can be represented as a tensor of occupation numbers
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{F}^{K}
, where

\mathcal{F}^{K}=(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{\otimes K}\simeq\mathbb{R}^{(2^{K})}.

This is achieved by identifying
i\in\{1,\ldots,K\}^{N}
,
i_{1}<\ldots<i_{N}
, with precisely one
\alpha\in\{0,1\}^{K}
by
\alpha_{k}=1
if
k\in\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{N}\}
, and
\alpha_{k}=0
otherwise for
k=1,\ldots,K
, and defining the nonzero entries of
\bm{x}
by
\bm{x}_{\alpha}=c_{i}
for such
\alpha
.

We have the corresponding second-quantized representation of the Hamiltonian acting on occupation number tensors,

(1.2)

\bm{H}=\bm{T}+\bm{V}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{K}t_{ij}\bm{a}_{i}^{\ast}\bm{a}_{j}+\sum_{i,j,k,l=1}^{K}v_{ijkl}\bm{a}^{\ast}_{i}\bm{a}^{\ast}_{j}\bm{a}_{k}\bm{a}_{l},

in terms of the
annihilation operator

\bm{a}_{i}

(1.3)

\bm{a}_{i}=\biggl(\bigotimes_{k=1}^{i-1}S\biggr)\otimes A\otimes\biggl(\bigotimes_{k=i+1}^{K}I\biggr)

and the corresponding
creation operator

\bm{a}_{i}^{\ast}
. These are given in terms of the elementary components

(1.4)

S=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix},\quad A=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},\quad I=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}.

The operators
\bm{a}^{\ast}_{i}
and
\bm{a}_{i}
can be thought of as transferring particles from the unoccupied to the occupied state of orbital
i
or back, respectively. The antisymmetry of wavefunctions corresponds to the anticommutation relations

(1.5)

\bm{a}_{i}\bm{a}^{\ast}_{j}+\bm{a}^{\ast}_{j}\bm{a}_{i}=\delta_{ij},\quad\bm{a}^{\ast}_{i}\bm{a}^{\ast}_{j}+\bm{a}^{\ast}_{j}\bm{a}^{\ast}_{i}=\bm{a}_{i}\bm{a}_{j}+\bm{a}_{j}\bm{a}_{i}=0\,.

Neglecting electron spin for the moment, the coefficient tensors in (
1.2
) are given by

(1.6)

\displaystyle t_{ij}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\nabla\phi_{i}\cdot\nabla\phi_{j}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}V\phi_{i}\phi_{j}\,\mathrm{d}x,

\displaystyle v_{ijkl}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\phi_{i}(x_{1})\phi_{j}(x_{2})\frac{1}{\lVert x_{1}-x_{2}\rVert_{2}}\phi_{k}(x_{2})\phi_{l}(x_{1})\,\mathrm{d}x_{1}\,\mathrm{d}x_{2}\,.

Although in this paper, we test our methods on model problems with particles living in a one-dimensional space, they can be generalized immediately to the above general case.

A common strategy for handling large
K
is to approximate elements of
\mathcal{F}^{K}
in low-rank tensor formats. One instance are MPS
[
34
,
33
]
, which especially in the mathematical literature are also known as the tensor train (TT) format
[
26
]
, which in turn are a variant of hierarchical tensors
[
17
,
16
]
.
Using tensor formats in this manner has the advantage that the occupation number tensors do not need to satisfy antisymmetry requirements, which would be difficult to incorporate directly
[
15
]
.

However, the second-quantized representation (
1.2
) of the Hamiltonian is valid for any number of particles, and thus this number generally needs to be explicitly enforced.
Prescribing a number of
N
particles amounts to restricting the eigenvalue problem for
\bm{H}
to the subspace
\mathcal{F}_{N}^{K}
of those occupation numbers that are also eigenvectors of the

particle number operator

\bm{P}=\sum_{i=1}^{K}\bm{a}_{i}^{\ast}\bm{a}_{i}

with eigenvalue
N
. The particle number constraint does not need to be implemented explicitly: as considered in detail in
[
4
]
, every particle number eigenspace corresponds to a certain
block-sparse structure
in the cores of MPS. This fact has a long history in the physical literature (see, e.g.,
[
27
,
8
,
25
,
30
,
32
]
). The block sparsity is associated to gauge symmetries of the Hamiltonian, such as
U(1)
symmetry corresponding to particle number conservation.

An approximate solution for the lowest eigenvalue (or eigenvalues) with
N
particles of the Hamiltonian
\bm{H}
is then sought within the subspace
\mathcal{F}_{N}^{K}\subset\mathcal{F}^{K}
of
N
-particle states,

\mathcal{F}_{N}^{K}=\bigl\{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{F}^{K}\colon\bm{P}\bm{x}=N\bm{x}\bigr\},

which leads to the eigenvalue problem

(1.7)

\langle\bm{H}\bm{x},\bm{y}\rangle=\lambda\langle\bm{x},\bm{y}\rangle\quad\text{for all $\bm{y}\in\mathcal{F}_{N}^{K}$}

for
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{F}_{N}^{K}
,
\bm{x}\neq 0
. Here and in what follows, we use
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
to denote the standard Euclidean inner product and
\lVert\cdot\rVert
for the induced vector norm as well as the corresponding matrix norm.

1.2.
Novelty and relation to previous work

We build on
[
4
]
in using the block-sparsity pattern of MPS representations of element of
\mathcal{F}_{N}^{K}
. In our present setting, all involved operations preserve the particle number and thus again yield tensors that have a representation with the same block structure. Thus eigenvalue solvers can be implemented to operate only on the separate blocks in the tensor representation, which may reduce the computational costs.

The most commonly used numerical method for solving (
1.7
) with
\bm{x}
in MPS representation is the
density matrix renormalization group
(DMRG) algorithm
[
34
,
33
]
. Although this alternating minimization method is often observed to perform well in practice, it can fail to converge. This can be seen from simple counterexamples for low-order tensors as, for example, in
[
28
, Ex. 5.12]
, but is also observed in larger-scale problems due to effects that have to do with the global structure of eigenvectors, as noted in
[
9
]
. Beyond these basic difficulties, as outlined in
[
4
]
, the restriction to the
N
-particle subspace
\mathcal{F}_{N}^{K}
may impact the convergence of eigensolvers. In particular, the choice of rank parameters in the respective starting values can have a crucial influence.

While DMRG is an instance of a method operating on separate components in a tensor representation, one can also construct methods that approximately perform steps of a solver (such as a Krylov subspace method) on vectors represented in a low-rank tensor format. When the error tolerances for rank truncation are sufficiently small, the convergence properties of the underlying schemes can be preserved. As in the case of DMRG, however, one faces the problem of controlling the resulting ranks of approximations.
In methods that operate on manifolds of tensors of fixed ranks, such as Riemannian optimization methods, conversely one faces the problem that it is difficult to ensure convergence.

Our central aim in this work is the construction of an eigenvalue solver operating on matrix product states that interacts well with the block structures associated to fixed particle numbers, that is ensured to converge (at least for good enough initial guesses) and comes with bounds on the generated ranks of approximations.

The strategy that we follow here for balancing approximation errors and tensor ranks is based on the one developed for linear elliptic operator equations in
[
2
,
3
]
, as presented with some adjustments in the review article
[
1
]
.
While in these works, the target error measure is the total norm error with respect to the exact solution of a PDE, in the present work some major adaptations are required, since we aim for controlling angles between approximate and exact eigenspaces.
For this reason, we do not consider here another strategy for controlling ranks based on soft thresholding of tensors as in
[
6
]
, which is more strongly tied to norm errors.

We achieve control of approximation ranks by an interplay of error reduction, by an iterative solver providing a guaranteed improvement of approximation errors, with an approximation complexity reduction, by tensor rank truncation up to carefully chosen tolerances. Such combinations of iterative schemes with rank truncation have been considered previously for eigenvalue problems, for example, in
[
24
,
11
,
23
]
, but without bounds on the tensor ranks in terms of approximation errors.

As our basic iterative solver, we use
preconditioned inverse iteration
(PINVIT). This method is well suited for implementation in low-rank format and for the combination with preconditioning in our particular setting, but in principle it is not the only possible choice. The crucial point, however, is that for suitable starting values, it ensures a reduction of the eigenspace approximation error.
In this regard, we draw on the classical results on PINVIT for matrix eigenvalue problems by Knyazev and Neymeyr
[
21
,
22
]
and on their adaptation to problems on function spaces with approximate residual evaluation
[
29
]
.

2.1.
Matrix product states and operators

The
matrix product state
(or
tensor train
) representation of
\bm{x}\in{\mathcal{F}}^{K}
reads

(2.1)

\bm{x}_{\alpha}=\bm{x}_{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{K}}=\sum_{j_{1}=1}^{r_{1}}\cdots\sum_{j_{K-1}=1}^{r_{K-1}}X_{1}(j_{0},\alpha_{1},j_{1})X_{2}(j_{1},\alpha_{2},j_{2})\,\cdots\,X_{K}(j_{K-1},\alpha_{K},j_{K})

for
\alpha\in\{0,1\}^{K}
, where for notational reasons we set
j_{0}=j_{K}=1
,
r_{0}=r_{K}=1
.

For the third-order component tensors
X_{k}
in such a representation, called
cores
, we write

(2.2)

\mathsf{X}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{K}),\quad X_{k}=\bigl(X_{k}(j_{k-1},\alpha_{k},j_{k})\bigr)_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{k-1}=1,\ldots,r_{k-1},\\
\alpha_{k}=0,1,\\
j_{k}=1,\ldots,r_{k}\end{subarray}}\,.

For specifying the components of an MPS explicitly, we use the notation

X^{[j_{k-1},j_{k}]}_{k}=\bigl(X_{k}(j_{k-1},\alpha_{k},j_{k})\bigr)_{\alpha_{k}=0,1},

In terms of the vectors
X_{k}^{[j_{k-1},j_{k}]}\in\mathbb{R}^{\{0,1\}}
, a core
X_{k}
is then given by the rankwise block representation

(2.3)

X_{k}=\begin{bmatrix}X_{k}^{[1,1]}&\cdots&X_{k}^{[1,r_{k}]}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
X_{k}^{[r_{k-1},1]}&\cdots&X_{k}^{[r_{k-1},r_{k}]}\end{bmatrix}.

Complementing this, we also introduce the matrices

X^{\{\alpha_{k}\}}_{k}=\bigl(X_{k}(j_{k-1},\alpha_{k},j_{k})\bigr)_{j_{k-1}=1,\ldots,r_{k-1}}^{j_{k}=1,\ldots,r_{k}}\,,

here and in what follows, we write row indices as subscripts and column indices as superscripts.

For a compact way of writing (
2.1
) in terms of the cores (
2.2
), we use the
strong Kronecker product
,

(X_{1}\SKP X_{2})^{\{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\}}=X_{1}^{\{\alpha_{1}\}}X_{2}^{\{\alpha_{2}\}}.

For example, for two cores
X,Y
of ranks
2\times 2
, we obtain

\begin{multlined}\begin{bmatrix}X^{[1,1]}&X^{[1,2]}\\
X^{[2,1]}&X^{[2,2]}\\
\end{bmatrix}\SKP\begin{bmatrix}Y^{[1,1]}&Y^{[1,2]}\\
Y^{[2,1]}&Y^{[2,2]}\\
\end{bmatrix}\qquad\\
\qquad=\begin{bmatrix}X^{[1,1]}\otimes Y^{[1,1]}+X^{[1,2]}\otimes Y^{[2,1]}&X^{[1,1]}\otimes Y^{[1,2]}+X^{[1,2]}\otimes Y^{[2,2]}\\
X^{[2,1]}\otimes Y^{[1,1]}+X^{[2,2]}\otimes Y^{[2,1]}&X^{[2,1]}\otimes Y^{[1,2]}+X^{[2,2]}\otimes Y^{[2,2]}\\
\end{bmatrix}.\end{multlined}

With this notation, we have

[\bm{x}]=X_{1}\SKP X_{2}\SKP\cdots\SKP X_{K}\,,

where the block
[\bm{x}]\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times\{0,1\}^{K}\times 1}
has leading and trailing dimensions of mode size 1.
For simplicity, we ignore such singleton dimensions, that is,
we identify
[\bm{x}]
with the tensor
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{\{0,1\}^{K}}
of order
K
.

2.2.
Singular value decomposition

For
k=1,\ldots,K-1
, using
(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k})
as row index and
(\alpha_{k+1},\ldots,\alpha_{K})
as column index, one obtains the
k
-th
matricization
(or
unfolding
) of a tensor
\bm{x}\in{\mathcal{F}}^{K}
. Any representation
\mathsf{X}
of
\bm{x}
can be transformed by operations on its cores such that these matricizations are in variants of an SVD form, which also yield the singular values
\sigma_{k,j}
for
k=1,\ldots,K-1,j=1,\ldots,r_{k}
. The
Vidal decomposition

[
33
]
is an instance of such a representation. A very similar form results immediately from the
tensor train SVD (TT-SVD)
algorithm
[
26
]
, which in turn can be regarded as a special case of the
hierarchical SVD

[
14
]
of more general tree tensor networks.

We write
\rank_{i}(\bm{x})
for the rank of the
i
-th matricization of
\bm{x}
and

\ranks(\bm{x})=\bigl(\rank_{1}(\bm{x}),\ldots,\rank_{K-1}(\bm{x})\bigr)\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{K-1}

for the vector of these ranks. These correspond to the entrywise minimal tupel
(r_{1},\ldots,r_{K-1})
of any representation
\mathsf{X}
of
\bm{x}
and they are therefore called
ranks
of the tensor
\bm{x}
.
The rank truncation of each matricization yields quasi-optimal approximations of lower ranks
[
26
,
14
]
: let
\bm{x}
be given with ranks
r_{1},\ldots,r_{K-1}
, and denote by
\operatorname{trunc}_{s_{1},\ldots,s_{K-1}}(\bm{x})
its truncation to ranks at most
s_{k}\leq r_{k}
,
k=1,\ldots,K-1
, then

(2.4)

\begin{multlined}\lVert\bm{x}-\operatorname{trunc}_{s_{1},\ldots,s_{K-1}}(\bm{x})\rVert\leq\biggl(\sum_{k=1}^{K-1}\sum_{j=s_{k}+1}^{r_{k}}\sigma_{k,j}^{2}\biggr)^{\frac{1}{2}}\\
\qquad\qquad\qquad\leq\sqrt{K-1}\min\bigl\{\lVert\bm{x}-\bm{y}\rVert\colon\bm{y}\text{ of ranks at most $s_{1},\ldots,s_{K-1}$}\bigr\}.\end{multlined}

Using the above error bound in terms of the matricization singular values, one obtains an approximation
\operatorname{trunc}_{\varepsilon}(\bm{x})
with
\lVert\bm{x}-\operatorname{trunc}_{\varepsilon}(\bm{x})\rVert_{2}\leq\varepsilon
for any
\varepsilon>0
by truncating ranks according to the smallest singular values.

2.3.
Block structure of matrix product states

We represent the tensors with fixed particle number
N\leq K
,
\bm{x}\in{\mathcal{F}}^{K}_{N}
in block-sparse MPS format, see
[
4
]
for more details. The matrices
X^{\{0\}}_{k}
and
X^{\{1\}}_{k}
are either block-diagonal or they have blocks only just above or just below the diagonal. We denote the blocks representing an unoccupied
k
-th orbital by

\hbox{\pagecolor{gray!30}\pic@makebox@{\makebox}{}(8.0,8.0){$\mathsf{X}$}}_{k,n}\in\mathbb{R}^{\rho_{k-1,n}\times\rho_{k,n}}\qquad\text{for $n\in{\mathcal{K}}_{k-1}\cap{\mathcal{K}}_{k}$},

and those representing an occupied orbital by

\hbox{\pagecolor{black}{\color[rgb]{1,1,1}\pic@makebox@{\makebox}{}(8.0,8.0){$\mathsf{X}$}}}_{k,n}\in\mathbb{R}^{\rho_{k-1,n}\times\rho_{k,n+1}}\qquad\text{for $n\in{\mathcal{K}}_{k-1}\cap({\mathcal{K}}_{k}-1)$},

where
{\mathcal{K}}_{k}:=\bigl\{\max\{0,N-K+k\},\ldots,\min\{N,k\}\bigr\}
.
Note that for the block sizes, it holds
\sum_{n\in{\mathcal{K}}_{k}}\rho_{k,n}=r_{k}
and
\rho_{0,0}=\rho_{K,N}=1
.

For
k
such that
N<k<K-N+1
, which we refer to as the
generic case
, we have
{\mathcal{K}}_{k-1}={\mathcal{K}}_{k}=\{0,\ldots,N\}
; otherwise, the number of particles to the right and to the left of orbital
k
, and hence the elements of
{\mathcal{K}}_{k-1}
and
{\mathcal{K}}_{k}
, are restricted. The corresponding block structure has the form

(2.5)

X^{\{0\}}_{k}=\begin{pmatrix}\hbox{\pagecolor{gray!30}\pic@makebox@{\makebox}{}(8.0,8.0){$\mathsf{X}$}}_{k,0}&0&\cdots&0\\
0&\hbox{\pagecolor{gray!30}\pic@makebox@{\makebox}{}(8.0,8.0){$\mathsf{X}$}}_{k,1}&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&\hbox{\pagecolor{gray!30}\pic@makebox@{\makebox}{}(8.0,8.0){$\mathsf{X}$}}_{k,N}\end{pmatrix}\qquad\text{and}\qquad X^{\{1\}}_{k}=\begin{pmatrix}0&\hbox{\pagecolor{black}{\color[rgb]{1,1,1}\pic@makebox@{\makebox}{}(8.0,8.0){$\mathsf{X}$}}}_{k,0}&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&\hbox{\pagecolor{black}{\color[rgb]{1,1,1}\pic@makebox@{\makebox}{}(8.0,8.0){$\mathsf{X}$}}}_{k,N-1}\\
0&0&\cdots&0\end{pmatrix}.

Since nonzero blocks for the unoccupied orbital never occur in the same position as the ones for the occupied orbital, the two layers
\alpha=0
and
\alpha=1
can be summarized in the core representation

X_{k}=\begin{bmatrix}\hbox{\pagecolor{gray!30}\pic@makebox@{\makebox}{}(8.0,8.0){$\mathsf{X}$}}_{k,0}^{\uparrow}&\hbox{\pagecolor{black}{\color[rgb]{1,1,1}\pic@makebox@{\makebox}{}(8.0,8.0){$\mathsf{X}$}}}_{k,0}^{\uparrow}&\cdots&0\\[-3.0pt]
0&\hbox{\pagecolor{gray!30}\pic@makebox@{\makebox}{}(8.0,8.0){$\mathsf{X}$}}_{k,1}^{\uparrow}&\ddots&\vdots\\[-2.0pt]
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\hskip 4.0pt\hbox{\pagecolor{black}{\color[rgb]{1,1,1}\pic@makebox@{\makebox}{}(8.0,8.0){$\mathsf{X}$}}}_{k,N-1}^{\uparrow}\\[3.0pt]
0&0&\cdots&\hskip-7.0pt\hbox{\pagecolor{gray!30}\pic@makebox@{\makebox}{}(8.0,8.0){$\mathsf{X}$}}_{k,N}^{\uparrow}\end{bmatrix}=:\mathop{\rm bidiag}\left(\begin{bmatrix}\hbox{\pagecolor{gray!30}\pic@makebox@{\makebox}{}(8.0,8.0){$\mathsf{X}$}}_{k,n}^{\uparrow}&\hbox{\pagecolor{black}{\color[rgb]{1,1,1}\pic@makebox@{\makebox}{}(8.0,8.0){$\mathsf{X}$}}}_{k,n}^{\uparrow}\end{bmatrix}_{n\in{\mathcal{K}}_{k-1}}\right),

where each block is composed of vectors, and where we define the lift

\hbox{\pagecolor{gray!30}\pic@makebox@{\makebox}{}(8.0,8.0){$\mathsf{X}$}}_{k,n}^{\uparrow}=\hbox{\pagecolor{gray!30}\pic@makebox@{\makebox}{}(8.0,8.0){$\mathsf{X}$}}_{k,n}\uparrow e^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{\rho_{k-1,n}\times 2\times\rho_{k,n}}\qquad\text{for $n\in{\mathcal{K}}_{k-1}\cap{\mathcal{K}}_{k}$}

and

\hbox{\pagecolor{black}{\color[rgb]{1,1,1}\pic@makebox@{\makebox}{}(8.0,8.0){$\mathsf{X}$}}}_{k,n}^{\uparrow}=\hbox{\pagecolor{black}{\color[rgb]{1,1,1}\pic@makebox@{\makebox}{}(8.0,8.0){$\mathsf{X}$}}}_{k,n}\uparrow e^{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{\rho_{k-1,n}\times 2\times\rho_{k,n+1}}\qquad\text{for $n\in{\mathcal{K}}_{k-1}\cap({\mathcal{K}}_{k}-1)$}.

Here, the
lift product

\uparrow
is to be understood as a Kronecker product with reordered indices.
The cases where either
k\leq N
or
k\geq K-N+1
have the last rows or first columns (and zero rows and columns) removed, respectively, as illustrated in the following example.

Example 2.1
.

A tensor
\bm{x}\in{\mathcal{F}}^{5}_{2}
of order
K=5
and particle number
N=2
, representing
5
orbitals and

particles, has the form

2.4.
Operator representations

All operators used in this article are of the form (
1.2
): They are the scaled sum of
one-particle interactions

\bm{a}_{i}^{\ast}\bm{a}_{j}
, described by the one-particle operator
\bm{T}
, and
two-particle interactions

\bm{a}_{i}^{\ast}\bm{a}_{j}^{\ast}\bm{a}_{k}\bm{a}_{\ell}
, stored in the two-particle operator
\bm{V}
. As such, they are also particle-number preserving and thus map
{\mathcal{F}}^{K}_{N}
to itself.

These operators can be cast in a
matrix product operator
(MPO) representation that is compatible with MPS representations of vectors.
The corresponding MPO rank is an upper bound for the factor by which the action of the operator can increase MPS ranks.
The following rank bounds for
\bm{T}
and
\bm{V}
are shown in
[
4
, Theorems 5.4, 5.5]
(see also
[
10
,
18
]
and
[
7
,
20
]
).

Theorem 2.2
.

For
\bm{T}
,
\bm{V}
as in (
1.2
), the following rank bounds hold true:

(i)

\bm{T}
has an MPO representation of ranks bounded by
K+2
, and
\bm{V}
has an MPO representation of ranks bounded by
\frac{1}{2}{K^{2}}+\frac{3}{2}{K}+2
.

(ii)

If for some
d\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, we have
t_{ij}=0
whenever
\lvert i-j\rvert>d
, then the MPO ranks of
\bm{T}
are bounded by
2d+2
,
and if there exists
\tilde{d}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
such that
v_{ijkl}=0
whenever

\max\{\lvert i-j\rvert,\lvert i-k\rvert,\lvert i-l\rvert,\lvert j-k\rvert,\lvert j-l\rvert,\lvert k-l\rvert\}>\tilde{d},

then the MPO ranks of
\bm{V}
are bounded by
\tilde{d}^{2}+3\tilde{d}-1
if
\tilde{d}
is odd and by
\tilde{d}^{2}+3\tilde{d}-2
if
\tilde{d}
is even.

In
[
4
]
, it was shown that the action of such operators on an MPS in block-sparse representation can be performed by a
matrix-free
scheme: Applying a single term in the sum (
1.2
) consists only of reordering and possibly rescaling the blocks in the MPS. The resulting tensors then have to be summed up by concatenating the blocks. This preserves the block structure but increases the ranks. We follow this paradigm and think of all operators that appear in this article as matrix-free reorderings, scalings, and concatenations of blocks, so that in particular, no additional memory is required for MPO representations.

3.
Overview of the Method and Main Results

In order to approximate the lowest eigenvalue of the Hamiltonian
\bm{H}
, we consider an inexact preconditioned inverse iteration that is compatible with the block-sparse MPS format described in
section

2.3
. We prove convergence of the proposed algorithm and obtain bounds on the ranks of the iterates and thus on the overall computational complexity.
Although we consider the number
K
of orbitals (and thus the order of the tensors that we operate on) to be fixed, we are in particular interested in how the behaviour of the method depends on
K
.

3.1.
Construction of the iterative method

In what follows, we assume
\gamma>0
to be a shift parameter such that

\bm{H}_{\gamma}=\bm{H}+\gamma\bm{I}

is symmetric positive definite.
While the choice of
\gamma
is problem-dependent, in the case of (
1.1
) it can be chosen to be independent of
K
, as considered in more detail in
section

4.1
.
In what follows, we take the positive eigenvalues of
\bm{H}_{\gamma}
to be ordered as

(3.1)

0<\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\ldots\leq\lambda_{n},

where typically the lowest eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}
is of primary interest.

For a suitable self-adjoint, positive definite preconditioner
\bm{S}:\mathcal{F}_{N}^{K}\rightarrow\mathcal{F}_{N}^{K}
, we then replace the eigenvalue problem
eq.

1.7
by the
equivalent symmetrically preconditioned generalized eigenvalue problem

(3.2)

\langle\bm{S}\bm{H}_{\gamma}\bm{S}\bm{x},\bm{y}\rangle=\lambda\langle\bm{S}^{2}\bm{x},\bm{y}\rangle\quad\text{for all $\bm{y}\in\mathcal{F}_{N}^{K}$.}

With the self-adjoint matrices

\bm{A}=\bm{S}\bm{H}_{\gamma}\bm{S},\quad\bm{E}=\bm{S}^{2},

the sought eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}
is the minimum of the Rayleigh quotient

(3.3)

\lambda(\bm{x}):=\frac{\langle\bm{A}\bm{x},\bm{x}\rangle}{\langle\bm{E}\bm{x},\bm{x}\rangle}

for
eq.

3.2
, that is,

\lambda_{1}=\min_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{F}_{N}^{K}}\frac{\langle\bm{H}_{\gamma}\bm{x},\bm{x}\rangle}{\langle\bm{x},\bm{x}\rangle}=\min_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{F}_{N}^{K}}\lambda(\bm{x}).

The low-rank eigenvalue solvers that we consider in what follows are based on preconditioned inverse iteration, which with the above symmetric preconditioning and with an initial guess
\bm{x}^{0}
takes the basic form

(3.4)

\bm{x}^{n+1}=\bm{x}^{n}-\omega\bigl(\bm{A}\bm{x}^{n}-\lambda(\bm{x})\bm{E}\bm{x}^{n}\bigr)

with a step size parameter
\omega>0
such that

(3.5)

\lVert\bm{I}-\omega\bm{A}\rVert_{\bm{A}}=\lVert\bm{I}-\omega\bm{A}\rVert\leq\varepsilon_{\mathrm{iter}}<1

with some
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{iter}}\in(0,1)
; here and in what follows, we write
\lVert\bm{x}\rVert_{\bm{A}}=\sqrt{\langle\bm{A}\bm{x},\bm{x}\rangle}
for
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{F}^{K}
and use the same notation for the induced operator norm.

Our further analysis is based on assumptions that are appropriate for representations of iterates and approximate eigenvectors in block-sparse MPS format and for corresponding representations of matrices. In this setting, achieving (
3.5
) with
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{iter}}
independent of
K
leads to certain requirements on
\bm{S}
; in particular, the MPO ranks of
\bm{S}
generally need to be larger than one, so that its action increases the ranks of MPS representations. The preconditioners that we construct in
section

4
are of the form

\bm{S}=\sum_{k=M^{-}}^{M^{+}}\bm{S}_{k},

where each summand
\bm{S}_{k}:\mathcal{F}_{N}^{K}\rightarrow\mathcal{F}_{N}^{K}
for
k=M^{-},\ldots,M^{+}
is a positive definite, diagonal matrix of MPO rank one (that is, a
K
-fold Kronecker product of matrices in
\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
).

Due to the combined MPO ranks of
\bm{S}
and
\bm{H}_{\gamma}
, in our analysis we allow for inexact application of
\bm{E}
and
\bm{A}
with tensor rank truncations up to prescribed error tolerances in (
3.4
).
However, the Rayleigh quotient
\lambda
in
eq.

3.3
, which takes the form

(3.6)

\displaystyle\lambda(\bm{x})=\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{k,k^{\prime}=M^{-}}^{M^{+}}\langle\bm{H}_{\gamma}\bm{S}_{k}\bm{x},\bm{S}_{k^{\prime}}\bm{x}\rangle}{\displaystyle\sum_{k,k^{\prime}=M^{-}}^{M^{+}}\langle\bm{S}_{k}\bm{x},\bm{S}_{k^{\prime}}\bm{x}\rangle},

can be evaluated term by term without additional rank truncations. Due to this exact evaluation of
\lambda
we can also take advantage of the faster convergence of Rayleigh quotients.

3.2.
Main results on convergence, rank estimates and computational complexity

Our aim is to approximate the eigenpair
(\lambda_{1},\bm{u}_{1})
of the preconditioned problem (
3.2
) with
\lVert\bm{u}_{1}\rVert_{\bm{A}}=1
, under the assumption
\lambda_{1}<\lambda_{2}
. This amounts to finding
\bm{y}\in{\mathcal{F}}^{K}_{N}
in low-rank representation such that
\sin\angle_{\bm{A}}(\bm{u}_{1},\bm{y})\leq\lVert\bm{u}_{1}-\bm{y}\rVert_{\bm{A}}\leq\varepsilon
for each desired error tolerance
\varepsilon
. However, the ranks of such
\bm{y}
should ideally not be larger than necessary for achieving an approximation of this quality. A natural point of reference is thus the least value of the maximal MPS rank
\lVert\ranks(\bm{y})\rVert_{\infty}
that is required for
\lVert\bm{u}_{1}-\bm{y}\rVert_{\bm{A}}\leq\varepsilon
, in other words,

(3.7)

r_{\mathrm{best}}(\bm{u}_{1},\varepsilon)=\min\bigl\{r\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\colon\exists\bm{y}\in{\mathcal{F}}^{K}_{N},\lVert\ranks(\bm{y})\rVert_{\infty}\leq r\colon\lVert\bm{u}_{1}-\bm{y}\rVert_{\bm{A}}\leq\varepsilon\bigr\}\,.

Our method has the following basic structure, where all operations on approximate eigenvectors are performed in MPS low-rank format: with a starting value
\bm{y}^{0}
, fixed
c_{K}\in(0,1)
and
C_{K}>0
sufficiently large, an initial error bound
\eta_{\mathrm{outer}}^{0}>0
, and
\eta_{\mathrm{outer}}^{m}=2^{-m}\eta_{\mathrm{outer}}^{0}
,

for
m=0,1,2,\ldots
,

(I1)

with
\bm{x}^{0}=\bm{y}^{m}
, determine
\bm{x}^{N_{m}}
with sufficiently large
N_{m}
by applying
eq.

3.4

with inexact residual evaluation and rank truncation, that is,

\bm{\tilde{x}}^{n+1}=\operatorname{trunc}_{\eta_{\mathrm{inner}}^{n}}(\bm{x}^{n}-\omega_{n}\operatorname{res}_{\eta_{\mathrm{res}}^{n}}(\bm{x}^{n}))

with suitably chosen
\eta_{\mathrm{inner}}^{n},\eta_{\mathrm{res}}^{n}>0
and

\operatorname{res}_{\eta_{\mathrm{res}}^{n}}(\bm{x}^{n})
such that
\lVert\operatorname{res}_{\eta_{\mathrm{res}}^{n}}(\bm{x}^{n})-(\bm{A}\bm{x}^{n}-\lambda(\bm{x}^{n})\bm{E}\bm{x}^{n})\rVert_{\bm{A}}\leq\eta_{\mathrm{res}}^{n}
,

\bm{x}^{n+1}=\bm{\tilde{x}}^{n+1}/\lVert\bm{\tilde{x}}^{n+1}\rVert_{\bm{A}}
,

such that
\lVert\bm{u}_{1}-\bm{x}^{N_{m}}\rVert\leq c_{K}\eta_{\mathrm{outer}}^{m}
.

(I2)

determine
\bm{y}^{m+1}
by rank truncation of
\bm{x}^{N_{m}}
,

\bm{\tilde{y}}^{m+1}=\operatorname{trunc}_{C_{K}\eta_{\mathrm{outer}}^{m}}(\bm{x}^{N_{m}}),\qquad

\bm{y}^{m+1}=\bm{\tilde{y}}^{m+1}/\lVert\bm{\tilde{y}}^{m+1}\rVert_{\bm{A}}.

Whereas the inner iteration in step (I1) yields an error reduction, step (I2) performs a complexity reduction that, as shown below, ensures near-optimal ranks of
\bm{y}^{m}
,
m\in\mathbb{N}
.
The complete scheme with the concrete choices of algorithmic parameters is given in
Algorithm

3
in
section

5
.

Theorem 3.1
.

For the results of steps
(I1)
and
(I2)
above, with
\gamma
and
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{iter}}
in (
3.5
) independent of
K
and parameters chosen as in
Algorithm

3
,
we have

\lVert\bm{u}_{1}-\bm{y}^{m}\rVert_{\bm{A}}\leq\eta_{\mathrm{outer}}^{m},

while the number of inner iterations performed for each
m
is bounded, uniformly in
m
, by
C\log K
with
C
independent of
K
, and

\lVert\ranks(\bm{y}^{m})\rVert_{\infty}\leq r_{\mathrm{best}}\left(\bm{u}_{1},\frac{c_{1}\eta_{\mathrm{outer}}^{m}}{1+c_{2}\sqrt{K-1}}\right)

with
c_{1},c_{2}>0
independent of
K
and
m
.

The proof is given in
Section

5.6
.
We thus establish bounds for the ranks of iterates in the outer iterations indexed by
m
in terms of best approximation ranks.
However, ranks may in general grow beyond these values in inner iterations, and with our present approach we can only give bounds on the intermediate ranks that can arise in the inner iterations that also depend on the ranks of the involved operators.
While for this reason, we do not arrive at final assertions on the total computational complexity of methods, but we summarize some first results in this direction in
remark

5.12
.

4.
Preconditioning in Second Quantization

4.1.
Spectral shifts

As discussed in
Section

3.1
, the method that we study here requires a shift
\gamma>0
such that
\bm{H}_{\gamma}=\bm{H}+\gamma\bm{I}
is positive definite, as well as a suitable preconditioner for
\bm{H}_{\gamma}
. The choice of such a shift is problem-dependent and can thus be expected to depend in particular on the number of particles
N
. However, it is desirable to avoid a dependence of
\gamma
on the number of basis orbitals
K
, which plays the role of a discretization parameter.

For the Hamiltonian
H
as in (
1.1
), we obtain shifts with this latter property of
K
-independence by finding
\gamma
such that the shifted Hamiltonian
H+\gamma I
becomes elliptic on the appropriate energy space. In this case, under appropriate assumptions on the external potential
V
, there exists
\gamma>0
such that

(4.1)

\langle Hv,v\rangle+\gamma\lVert v\rVert_{L_{2}}^{2}\geq\textstyle\frac{1}{4}\displaystyle\lVert v\rVert_{H^{1}}^{2},\quad\forall v\in H^{1}\,.

If
V
in (
1.1
) is the molecular potential of the standard electronic Schrödinger equation,

V(x)=-\sum_{\nu}\frac{Z_{\nu}}{\lVert x-R_{\nu}\rVert_{2}},\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{3},

then the minimal
\gamma
such that (
4.1
) holds depends only (algebraically) on
N
and on
\sum_{\nu}Z_{\nu}
; see
[
35
, eq. (3.16)]
.

With
\gamma
as in (
4.1
), and with the second-quantized representation
\bm{L}
of the operator
-\Delta+I
, which reads

(4.2)

\bm{L}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{K}\ell_{ij}\,\bm{a}_{i}^{\ast}\bm{a}_{j},\quad\ell_{ij}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\nabla\phi_{i}\cdot\nabla\phi_{j}+\phi_{i}\phi_{j}\,\mathrm{d}x\,,

for each
N
and
K
we obtain

(4.3)

\textstyle\frac{1}{4}\displaystyle\langle\bm{L}\bm{x},\bm{x}\rangle\leq\langle\bm{H}_{\gamma}\bm{x},\bm{x}\rangle\leq\Gamma\langle\bm{L}\bm{x},\bm{x}\rangle,\quad\forall\bm{x}\in{\mathcal{F}}_{N}^{K},

where
\gamma,\Gamma>0
depend only
N
and
Z
, but neither on
K
nor on the orbitals. This implies in particular

(4.4)

\operatorname{cond}\bigl(\bm{L}^{-1/2}\bm{H}_{\gamma}\bm{L}^{-1/2}\bigr)\leq 4\Gamma.

Note that
\bm{x}
is a solution of the original eigenvalue problem
eq.

1.7
for
\bm{H}
if and only if
\bm{z}=\bm{L}^{1/2}\bm{x}
solves

\langle\bm{L}^{-1/2}\bm{H}_{\gamma}\bm{L}^{-1/2}\bm{z},\bm{y}\rangle=\lambda\langle\bm{L}^{-2}\bm{z},\bm{y}\rangle\quad\text{for all $\bm{y}\in\mathcal{F}_{N}^{K}$,}

where we use that
\bm{L}
and its powers preserve the particle number, that is, leave
\mathcal{F}_{N}^{K}
invariant.

While this two-sided preconditioning thus leads to a formulation that is suitable for preconditioned inverse iteration, the action of
\bm{L}^{-1/2}
is difficult to realize numerically.
We thus instead use a substitute for this mapping that is compatible with the low-rank representations used for elements of
\mathcal{F}_{N}^{K}
.

4.2.
Exponential sums

We make use of exponential sum approximations of
t^{-\lambda}
,
\lambda>0
, that are based on sinc quadrature with step size parameter
h>0
. By
[
31
, Lemma 5.3]
, we have

(4.5)

\left|\frac{1}{t^{\lambda}}-\frac{h}{\Gamma(\lambda)}\sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}e^{\lambda mh}\exp(-e^{mh}t)\right|\leq\frac{C(\lambda,h)}{t^{\lambda}}\quad\text{for all $t>0$}

with a
C(\lambda,h)>0
independent of
t
.
For
\lambda=\frac{1}{2}
, with an appropriate truncation of the summation, this yields an approximation of
t\mapsto t^{-1/2}
on any desired subinterval of
(0,\infty)
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\bm{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be symmetric with spectrum
\sigma(\bm{W})\subset[1,T]
. Let
c_{0}\in(0,1)
and

(4.6)

S(t)=\frac{h}{\sqrt{\pi}}\sum_{m=M^{-}}^{M^{+}}e^{mh/2}\exp(-e^{mh}t),

where

(4.7)

h\!=\!\frac{\pi^{2}}{\ln(\frac{8\sqrt{2}}{c_{0}}+1)},\;\;M^{+}=\left\lceil h^{-1}\ln\biggl\lvert\ln\frac{\sqrt{\pi}c_{0}}{4}\biggr\rvert\right\rceil,\;\;M^{-}=-\left\lceil h^{-1}\left(2\biggl\lvert\ln\frac{\sqrt{\pi}c_{0}}{4}\biggr\rvert+\ln T\right)\right\rceil.

Then

(4.8)

\displaystyle(1-c_{0})^{2}\langle\bm{x},\bm{x}\rangle\leq\langle S(\bm{W})\bm{W}S(\bm{W})\bm{x},\bm{x}\rangle\leq(1+c_{0})^{2}\langle\bm{x},\bm{x}\rangle,\quad\forall\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Proof.

We combine the truncation estimates in
[
1
, Corollary 4.3]
with the upper bound
C(\frac{1}{2},h)\leq\frac{2\sqrt{2}}{e^{\pi^{2}/h}-1}
shown in
[
31
]
.
For (
4.6
), the choices (
4.7
)
ensure in particular that
C(\frac{1}{2},h)\leq c_{0}/4
and thus the relative error bound

(4.9)

\left|\frac{1}{\sqrt{t}}-S(t)\right|\leq\frac{c_{0}}{\sqrt{t}},\quad\forall t\in[1,T].

This immediately implies (
4.8
).
∎

Note that (
4.8
) implies

\operatorname{cond}\bigl(S(\bm{W})\bm{W}S(\bm{W})\bigr)\leq\frac{(1+c_{0})^{2}}{(1-c_{0})^{2}}.

By appropriate rescaling, as shown in
4.2
below, the above applies to any matrix
\bm{W}
with
\mathrm{cond}(\bm{W})\leq T
.

4.3.
Expression for second quantization

For constructing a preconditioner of
\bm{H}_{\gamma}
, we use an auxiliary matrix
\bm{D}
of diagonal one-particle interactions,

\bm{D}=\sum_{i=1}^{K}d_{i}\,\bm{a}_{i}^{\ast}\bm{a}_{i},

where we assume
\gamma
and
\bm{D}
to be chosen such that
\bm{D}^{-1/2}\bm{H}_{\gamma}\bm{D}^{-1/2}
is well-conditioned, with a condition number that is in particular independent of
K
. Provided that we have an estimate of the form (
4.4
) for the given problem, this can be achieved by choosing
\bm{D}
to be spectrally equivalent to the representation of the Laplacian
\bm{L}
as in (
4.2
). That this can be achieved uniformly in
K
amounts to requiring that for the underlying
L^{2}
-orthonormal orbitals
\{\phi_{i}\}
, the rescaling
\{\phi_{i}/\lVert\phi_{i}\rVert_{H^{1}}\}
yields a Riesz basis of
H^{1}
with uniform constants.
We thus assume that with
C_{\mathrm{lower}},C_{\mathrm{upper}}>0
,

(4.10)

C_{\mathrm{lower}}\langle\bm{x},\bm{x}\rangle\leq\langle\bm{D}^{-1/2}\bm{H}_{\gamma}\bm{D}^{-1/2}\bm{x},\bm{x}\rangle\leq C_{\mathrm{upper}}\langle\bm{x},\bm{x}\rangle,\quad\forall\bm{x}\in{\mathcal{F}}^{K}_{N}.

We now construct low-rank approximations of
\bm{D}^{-1/2}
based on exponential sums.

Corollary 4.2
.

Let
t_{\min},t_{\max}
be chosen such that
[t_{\min},t_{\max}]\supset[\min(\sigma(\bm{D})),\max(\sigma(\bm{D}))]
, and with
c_{0}\in(0,1)
and
S
as in
Theorem

4.1
, let

(4.11)

\bm{S}=\alpha_{0}S\bigl(t_{\min}^{-1}\bm{D}\bigr),\quad\alpha_{0}=\sqrt{\frac{2}{t_{\min}(C_{\mathrm{lower}}(1-c_{0})^{2}+C_{\mathrm{upper}}(1+c_{0})^{2})}}\,.

Then for
\bm{A}=\bm{S}\bm{H}_{\gamma}\bm{S}
, we have

(4.12)

\displaystyle(1-c)\langle\bm{x},\bm{x}\rangle\leq\langle\bm{A}\bm{x},\bm{x}\rangle\leq(1+c)\langle\bm{x},\bm{x}\rangle,\quad\forall\bm{x}\in\mathcal{F}_{N}^{K},

where

(4.13)

c=\frac{C_{\mathrm{upper}}(1+c_{0})^{2}-C_{\mathrm{lower}}(1-c_{0})^{2}}{C_{\mathrm{upper}}(1+c_{0})^{2}+C_{\mathrm{lower}}(1-c_{0})^{2}}\in(0,1)\,,

and as a particular consequence,

(4.14)

\lVert\bm{I}-\bm{A}\rVert_{2}\leq c.

Proof.

In a first step, we adapt the approximation on the normalized interval
[1,T]
provided by
Theorem

4.1
by setting

\bm{S}_{0}=t_{\min}^{-1/2}S\bigl(t_{\min}^{-1}\bm{D}\bigr)\,.

By
Theorem

4.1
, with
c_{0}
as in (
4.10
), for all
\bm{x}
we have

(1-c_{0})^{2}\langle\bm{x},\bm{x}\rangle\leq\langle\bm{S}_{0}\bm{D}\bm{S}_{0}\bm{x},\bm{x}\rangle\leq(1+c_{0})^{2}\langle\bm{x},\bm{x}\rangle,

which combined with (
4.10
) yields

\displaystyle(1-c_{0})^{2}C_{\mathrm{lower}}\langle\bm{x},\bm{x}\rangle\leq\langle\bm{S}_{0}\bm{H}_{\gamma}\bm{S}_{0}\bm{x},\bm{x}\rangle\leq(1+c_{0})^{2}C_{\mathrm{upper}}\langle\bm{x},\bm{x}\rangle,\quad\forall\bm{x}\in{\mathcal{F}}^{K}_{N},

or in other words,

\sigma(\bm{S}_{0}\bm{H}_{\gamma}\bm{S}_{0})\subseteq[C_{\mathrm{lower}}(1-c_{0})^{2},C_{\mathrm{upper}}(1+c_{0})^{2}]\,.

We now use that the
\omega>0
such that
\lVert\bm{I}-\omega\bm{S}_{0}\bm{H}_{\gamma}\bm{S}_{0}\rVert_{2}
is minimal subject to these spectral bounds is given by

\omega=2/(C_{\mathrm{lower}}(1-c_{0})^{2}+C_{\mathrm{upper}}(1+c_{0})^{2})
to arrive at the scaling factor in (
4.11

6.
Simultaneous Approximation of Multiple Eigenvalues

In this section, we consider a generalization of
Algorithms

2
and
3
to the simultaneous approximation of multiple eigenvalues and eigenvectors. For this, let

(6.1)

\bm{X}:=(\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{D}),\;\bm{Y}:=(\bm{y}_{1},\ldots,\bm{y}_{D})\;\,\in\,\;{\mathcal{F}}^{K}_{N}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{D}

be collections of
D

D

(6.2)

\displaystyle\Lambda(\bm{X}):=\mathop{\rm diag}(\lambda(\bm{x}_{1}),\ldots,\lambda(\bm{x}_{D}))

as the matrix containing the Rayleigh quotients of
\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{D}
on the diagonal.
The residual is given by

(6.3)

\bm{R}(\bm{X})=\bm{A}\bm{X}-\bm{E}\bm{X}\Lambda(\bm{X})=\sum_{k,k^{\prime}}\bigl(\bm{S}_{k}\bm{H}_{\gamma}\bm{S}_{k^{\prime}}\bm{X}-\bm{S}_{k}\bm{S}_{k^{\prime}}\bm{X}\Lambda(\bm{X})\bigr).

The inner iteration for
D>1
(summarized in
algorithm

4
) is analogous to
algorithm

2
for
D=1
, in which case both algorithms coincide.
The joint representation of eigenspace bases described in
section

6.1
allows for an efficient implementation.
However, it also entails that we can apply only a single rank truncation tolerance to the entire tensor including all columns of the represented matrix iterate.
Such truncations are thus handled under the worst-case assumption that the entire error may occur in a single one of the
D
approximate eigenvectors, but without knowing which one. With this restriction, the outer iteration requires only minor adaptation compared to the single-vector case
D=1
, such that the first approximate eigenvector still provides the same adaption criterion. Additionally, the residual of this first column is heuristically compared with those remaining for the termination of the outer iteration. The development of a more involved scheme that uses all
D
Rayleigh quotients and in particular controls also the remaining
D-1

6.1.

We describe the choice of stepsize for multiple eigenvalues in detail, because it differs more substantially from the case

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{X}}^{\top}\bm{A}\bm{\mathcal{X}}V=\bm{\mathcal{X}}^{\top}\bm{E}\bm{\mathcal{X}}V\Lambda_{\ast},\quad\text{where $\bm{\mathcal{X}}=(\bm{X}^{n}\ \bm{R}^{n})\in\mathcal{F}_{N}^{K}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{2D}$,}\quad V\in\mathbb{R}^{2D\times D}.

6.2.
Joint tensor parameterization of several eigenvectors

In the simultaneous approximation of several eigenvectors
\bm{X}=(\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{D})
by low-rank tensor methods, instead of maintaining a separate tensor representation for each eigenvector, it is natural to instead consider a
joint tensor parameterization
where the index numbering the eigenvectors is simply treated as an additional mode in the tensor decomposition. This strategy is also called a
block tensor train
representation in the literature
[
11
,
23
]
.
For the joint approximation of eigenvectors
\bm{x}_{i}\in{\mathcal{F}}^{K}
,
i=1,\ldots,D
, for
D\in\mathbb{N}
, this amounts to considering the matrix product state representation of the tensor

\bm{\check{x}}=(\bm{x}_{i,\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{K}})_{i\in\{1,\ldots,D\},\alpha\in\{0,1\}^{K}}

in the form

\bm{\check{x}}=\check{X}\SKP X_{1}\SKP\cdots\SKP X_{K}.

In the same manner, the additional core can be added at any other position
1\leq p\leq K+1
(that is, before component
X_{p}
) to yield a representation

\bm{\check{x}}=X_{1}\SKP\cdots\SKP X_{p-1}\SKP\check{X}\SKP X_{p}\SKP\cdots\SKP X_{K},

where
X_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{r_{k-1}\times 2\times r_{k}}
for
k=1,\ldots,p-1
,
X_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{r_{k}\times 2\times r_{k+1}}
for
k=p,\ldots,K
, and the additional core
\check{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{r_{p}\times D\times r_{p+1}}
carries the eigenvector index. Note that now,
r_{K}>1
and the rank indices for the component on the right are shifted by one. The new ranks
(1,r_{1},\ldots,r_{K},1)
required for representing
\bm{\check{x}}
in this block representation can be at most the sum of the ranks of the collected eigenvectors.

This representation extends naturally to the block-sparse representation of
\bm{x}_{i}\in{\mathcal{F}}_{N}^{K}
: For
\bm{x}_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,D
, with
\mathsf{X}_{i}=(X_{i,1},\ldots,X_{i,K})
, we set the components
X_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{r_{k-1}\times 2\times r_{k}}
by concatenating the blocks

\hbox{\pagecolor{gray!30}\pic@makebox@{\makebox}{}(8.0,8.0){$\mathsf{X}$}}_{k,n}=\begin{pmatrix}\hbox{\pagecolor{gray!30}\pic@makebox@{\makebox}{}(8.0,8.0){$\mathsf{X_{1}}$}}_{k,n}&0&\cdots&0\\
0&\hbox{\pagecolor{gray!30}\pic@makebox@{\makebox}{}(8.0,8.0){$\mathsf{X_{2}}$}}_{k,n}&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&\hbox{\pagecolor{gray!30}\pic@makebox@{\makebox}{}(8.0,8.0){$\mathsf{X_{D}}$}}_{k,n}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{\rho_{k-1,n}\times\rho_{k,n}}

for
n\in{\mathcal{K}}_{k-1}\cap{\mathcal{K}}_{k}
and where
\rho_{k,n}=\sum_{i=1}^{D}\rho_{i,k,n}
is the sum of the block sizes. The occupied blocks
\hbox{\pagecolor{black}{\color[rgb]{1,1,1}\pic@makebox@{\makebox}{}(8.0,8.0){$\mathsf{X}$}}}_{k,n}

k=1
and
k=K

7.1.1.
Approximate eigenfunctions as orbital functions

To derive suitable (real-valued) orbital functions
\{\phi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{K}
on
\Omega=[-b,b]\subset\mathbb{R}
, we approximate the eigenfunctions corresponding to the lowest eigenvalues of the one-particle interaction on a larger basis,

\bm{T}^{\psi}:\mathcal{F}_{N}^{B}\rightarrow\mathcal{F}_{N}^{B},\quad B\gg K.

We therefore apply an
L_{2}
-orthonormal basis
\{\psi_{j}\}_{j=1}^{B}
of piecewise degree
6
polynomial,
C^{1}(\Omega)
-multiwavelets
[
12
]
under Dirichlet boundary conditions on
[-b,b]
, refined around
0
.
1
1

1

A wavelet
w
is included in this basis if and only if
\mathop{\rm supp}(w)\cap(-b_{w},b_{w})\neq\emptyset
,
b_{w}:=2^{m_{\min}-\mathrm{level}(w)}b
, as well as
\mathrm{level}(w)\leq m_{\max}
.

The corresponding
(B\times B)
-matrix
T^{\psi}=(t^{\psi}_{ij})_{ij}
with

\displaystyle t^{\psi}_{ij}:=\int_{\Omega}\psi_{i}(x)\big(-\textstyle\frac{1}{2}\displaystyle\Delta+V\big)\psi_{j}(x)\ \mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\psi_{i}^{\prime}(x)\psi_{j}^{\prime}(x)\ \mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}\psi_{i}(x)\ V(x)\ \psi_{j}(x)\ \mathrm{d}x

is computed via sufficiently accurate quadrature rules, each adapted to the intersection of supports of pairs
(\psi_{i},\psi_{j})
for
i,j=1,\ldots,B
. The first
K
(discrete, orthonormal) eigenvectors
\{q_{i}\}_{i=1}^{K}
with eigenvalues
\{\omega_{i}\}_{i=1}^{K}
of
T^{\psi}
are then used to construct the orbitals

\displaystyle\phi_{i}(x):=\sum_{j=1}^{B}q_{ij}\psi_{j}(x),\quad T^{\psi}\phi_{i}=\omega_{i}\phi_{i},\ i=1,\ldots,K.

Consequently, these yield the first
K
approximate,
L_{2}
-orthonormal eigenfunctions of
\bm{T}
.
For
\bm{T}
, we thus obtain the diagonal coefficient matrix

\displaystyle(t_{ij})_{i,j=1}^{K}=\mathrm{diag}(\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{d}).

The coefficient matrix for the shifted operator
\bm{T}+\gamma I
is accordingly

\displaystyle\mathrm{diag}(\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{K})+\frac{\gamma}{N}I_{K}.

Due to the exponentially decreasing support sizes of higher-level wavelets, the matrix
(t^{\psi}_{ij})_{ij}
is sparse, whereas the eigenvectors can be computed by a suitable solver based on inverse iteration. The above process may be improved by combining the single, consecutive steps into a basis-adaptive eigenfunctions solver, but the present form suffices for the subsequent experiments.
For the preconditioner, similarly to

]

\theta_{i}:=\frac{\gamma}{N}+t_{ii}+\sum_{j=k_{i}+1}^{k_{i}+N}(v_{ijji}-v_{ijij}),\quad k_{i}:=\max(0,i-N).

7.1.2.
Evaluation of the two-particle interaction operator

In principle, every entry

v_{ijk\ell}^{\psi}:=\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\psi_{i}(x)\psi_{j}(x^{\prime})\ V_{2}(x,x^{\prime})\ \psi_{k}(x^{\prime})\psi_{\ell}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}

could be computed via an adaptive quadrature rule as described for
t^{\psi}
in
section

7.1.1
, where
V_{2}
describes the two-particle interaction potential. However, as the number of non-zero entries of
v^{\psi}
equals the squared number of those of
t^{\psi}
, it becomes impractical to proceed the required basis reduction to
\{q_{i}\}_{i=1}^{K}
, even with sparse arithmetics. Instead, in order to evaluate the coefficient tensor
(v_{ijk\ell})_{i,j,k,\ell=1}^{K}
, the same summed Gaussian quadrature rule

\int_{\Omega}f(x)\mathrm{d}x\ \approx\ \sum_{u=1}^{U}w_{u}f(x_{u}),\quad w_{u}>0,\ x_{u}\in\Omega,\ u=1,\ldots,U

of degree
n\geq 7
is applied to all required integrals, where the quadrature is exact for piecewise polynomials up to degree
2n-1
. This includes
f(x)=\psi_{i}(x)\psi_{j}(x)
for
i,j=1,\ldots,B
and
f(x)=\phi_{i}(x)\phi_{j}(x)
for
i,j=1,\ldots,K
.
2
2

2

The quadrature intervals are chosen such that the length of any interval
I
equals
2b\,2^{-(m(I)+3)}
, where

m(I)=\max\{\mathrm{level}(\phi_{j})\colon\mathop{\rm supp}(\phi_{j})\cap I\setminus\delta I\neq\emptyset,\ j=1,\ldots,B\}
. The values
m(I)
and intervals lengths are well-defined due to properties of the wavelets. The additional factor
2b
is due to the rescaling of wavelets originally defined on
[0,1]
to
\Omega=[-b,b]
.

In this manner, we can efficiently compute

\displaystyle v_{ijk\ell}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\phi_{i}(x)\phi_{j}(x^{\prime})\ V_{2}(x,x^{\prime})\ \phi_{k}(x^{\prime})\phi_{\ell}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}

\displaystyle\approx\ \sum_{s=1}^{U}\sum_{t=1}^{U}w_{s}\phi_{i}(x_{s})\phi_{j}(x_{t})V_{2}(x_{s},x_{t})w_{t}\phi_{k}(x_{t})\phi_{\ell}(x_{s})

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=1}^{U}\sum_{t=1}^{U}\big(\phi_{i}(x_{s})\phi_{\ell}(x_{s})\big)\big(w_{s}V_{2}(x_{s},x_{t})w_{t}\big)\big(\phi_{j}(x_{t})\phi_{k}(x_{t})\big).

The wavelets are first evaluated on all quadrature points, the result of which is then transformed with the basis
\{q_{i}\}_{i=1}^{K}
. This evaluation only requires objects of (the usually much smaller) sizes
U\times U,U\times B
and
U\times K^{2}

7.3.
Observations on numerical experiments

In summary, we observe in particular a clear effect of the inner-outer iteration scheme, where the truncation in each step of the outer iteration produces approximations with substantially smaller ranks than obtained by a naïve use of the inner iteration for all steps. While the ranks of iterates are observed to depart from such near-best approximations during the inner iterations due to the action of operators, this rank increase is far less pronounced than the individual maximal ranks of iterates and operators would suggest. In particular, we observe favorable performance of the method and controlled convergence also for larger values of
K
, including in the case of simultaneous approximation of multiple eigenvalues in joint tensor representation.

8.
Conclusions

We have constructed an eigenvalue solver operating on low-rank tensor representations based on inexact preconditioned inverse iteration. While the central convergence estimates, which sharpen those given in
[
29
]
, are generally applicable and may be of independent interest, in our construction we pay particular attention to Schrödinger eigenvalue problems in second-quantized form as common in quantum chemistry.
By a rigorous construction of a low-rank preconditioner for such problems, for the single-vector version of the iterative scheme, under standard assumptions on starting values we achieve systematic convergence with guaranteed error reduction in each step. Moreover, we show this method to provide approximations of near-optimal ranks.
We have also proposed a version of the scheme operating on higher-dimensional subspaces that can be combined with joint low-rank representations of several eigenvectors. While this method performs equally well in practice, its analysis will be a subject of future work.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
\bm{x},\bm{y},\bm{z}
be vectors such that
\lambda(\bm{z})\leq(1-t)\lambda(\bm{y})+t\lambda(\bm{x})
as well as
\lambda(\bm{x}),\lambda(\bm{y})\in[\lambda_{k},\lambda_{k+1})
and
\lambda(\bm{x})\geq\lambda(\bm{y})

Then if for a

(A.1)

\frac{\lambda(\bm{y})-\lambda_{k}}{\lambda_{k+1}-\lambda(\bm{y})}\leq q^{2}\frac{\lambda(\bm{x})-\lambda_{k}}{\lambda_{k+1}-\lambda(\bm{x})},

we also have

\frac{\lambda(\bm{z})-\lambda_{k}}{\lambda_{k+1}-\lambda(\bm{z})}\leq((1-t)q^{2}+t)\frac{\lambda(\bm{x})-\lambda_{k}}{\lambda_{k+1}-\lambda(\bm{x})}.

Proof.

By monoticity,

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda(\bm{z})-\lambda_{k}}{\lambda_{k+1}-\lambda(\bm{z})}\leq\frac{(1-t)\lambda(\bm{y})+t\lambda(\bm{x})-\lambda_{k}}{\lambda_{k+1}-(1-t)\lambda(\bm{y})-t\lambda(\bm{x})}=\frac{(1-t)(\lambda(\bm{y})-\lambda_{k})+t(\lambda(\bm{x})-\lambda_{k})}{(1-t)(\lambda_{k+1}-\lambda(\bm{y}))+t(\lambda_{k+1}-\lambda(\bm{x}))}.

With
a\coloneqq\lambda(\bm{y})-\lambda_{k}
,
b\coloneqq\lambda(\bm{x})-\lambda_{k}
,
c\coloneqq\lambda_{k+1}-\lambda(\bm{y})
,
d\coloneqq\lambda_{k+1}-\lambda(\bm{x})
it thus suffices to show

(A.2)

\frac{(1-t)a+tb}{(1-t)c+td}\leq\bigl((1-t)q^{2}+t\bigr)\frac{b}{d}.

By (
A.1
), we have

, and thus
((1-t)a+tb)d\leq(q^{2}(1-t)c+td)b
. Moreover, a simple direct calculation shows that whenever

, which in turn is equivalent to our assumption

, it holds that

q^{2}(1-t)c+td\leq\bigl(q^{2}(1-t)+t\bigr)\bigl((1-t)c+td\bigr)\,.

In summary, this gives (
A.2
) and thus the assertion.
∎

A.2.
Rayleigh quotient under perturbation

To derive a lower bound for the Rayleigh quotient under perturbation, we here consider a general linear selfadjoint operator
\bm{B}:V\rightarrow V

4
4

4

With our application in mind, we keep the explicit dependency of the inner product on the operator

For every

\frac{\mu(\bm{p})-L}{\mu(\bm{v})-L}\geq\left(\max\left\{\cos(\alpha)-\frac{\varrho(\bm{v})}{\mu(\bm{v})-L}\sin(\alpha),0\right\}\right)^{2}.

Remark A.3
.

The bound is sharp when

itself is sharp (and no further information about
\bm{v}
,
\bm{p}
or
\bm{B}
is provided). Assume for simplicity that

When

In general, equality is obtained if and only if the space

For the non-trivial angles

\mu(\bm{v})-\mu(\bm{p})\leq g_{\varrho(\bm{v})}(\alpha),\quad g_{\varrho(\bm{v})}(\alpha):=\varrho(\bm{v})\sin(2\alpha)+\frac{(\mu(\bm{v})-L)^{2}-\varrho(\bm{v})^{2}}{\mu(\bm{v})-L}\sin(\alpha)^{2}.

It is the smooth transition between the usual quadratic bound for eigenvectors, with thus zero residual, and the general linear one that does not use the residual. That is,

The proof of

(A.4)

\mu(\bm{p})\geq\min_{\mathcal{X}\subseteq V:\,\bm{v}\in\mathcal{X},\,\dim(\mathcal{X})=2}\ \min_{\bm{\tilde{p}}\in\mathcal{X}:\,\angle_{\bm{A}}(\bm{v},\bm{\tilde{p}})\leq\alpha}\ \mu(\bm{\tilde{p}}).

Since it is much simpler to express properties of the vectors in those of a general two-dimensional subspace than it would be vice versa, relevant terms can be related to each other in form of comparitively simple equalities (cf.

A.4

Lemma A.4

Let

g(\mu(\bm{v})-b,\varrho|_{\mathcal{X}}(\bm{v}),\beta)\leq g(\mu(\bm{v})-L,\varrho(\bm{v}),\alpha).

Thus, for

For larger angles, it simply follows

∎
