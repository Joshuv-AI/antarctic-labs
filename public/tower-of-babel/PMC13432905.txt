Upward intergenerational support and vaccination decisions among community-dwelling older adults in China: A multicenter qualitative study
Authors: Mengyue Zhang, Siwen Huang, Chi Ruan, Dingwan Chen, Xiaosong Hu, Na Liu, Liang Wang, Lei Li, Mingli Fang, Xinyi Zhang, Huinan Hou, Dijun Liu, Yuhang Zhang, Yaqin Zhou, Jingtao Zhou, Qingyu Li, Linjie Wang, Xinyu Shi, Yudan Song, Wenzhou Yu, Sitong Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRoutine vaccination coverage among older adults in China remains suboptimal despite established public health recommendations. Family engagement, deeply embedded in Chinese culture, is shown to influence health decisions; however, the specific role of upward intergenerational support (UIS) from adult children in older adults’ vaccination decisions remains understudied in the post-pandemic era. To explore the forms and mechanisms through which UIS influences vaccination decision‑making among community‑dwelling older adults in post‑pandemic China, we conducted this multicenter qualitative study. From November 2024 to August 2025, a total of 261 semi‑structured interviews were conducted with older adults (≥ 60 y) and adult children (18‑59 y) across seven Chinese cities selected for maximum variation in socioeconomic status and vaccine policies. Thematic analysis revealed five key forms of UIS, including financial, practical, informational, emotional, and appraisal support, through which adult children influence parental vaccination decisions. Across families, these five forms were enacted in two qualitatively different patterns: a substitutive pattern, where adult children assumed decision-making authority, and a facilitative pattern, where support preserved and strengthened older adults’ autonomy. The salience of these supports and patterns was contextualized by regional socioeconomic disparities, local vaccine policies, and lingering post‑COVID vaccine concerns. Findings reveal that UIS is a central social mechanism influencing vaccination decisions among older adults. Public health strategies need to shift from individual-focused to family-systems interventions, equipping adult children as facilitative supporters who respect older adults’ autonomy, while concurrently addressing structural and relational barriers to improve routine vaccine uptake among community-dwelling older adults in China.

Introduction

Vaccination is one of the most effective public health interventions for preventing infectious diseases and mitigating severe outcomes, especially among the growing global population of older adults.1,2 China has the largest aging population in the world: by the end of 2024, approximately 310 million people aged 60 and above accounted for 22.0% of the total population,3 and this proportion is projected to reach 28% by 2040.4 While the COVID-19 pandemic has profoundly reshaped public perceptions of vaccines and disrupted routine immunization services worldwide, it has also highlighted the critical importance of protecting vulnerable populations through routine vaccination.5,6 Influenza, pneumococcal, and herpes zoster vaccines are routinely recommended vaccines for older adults.2,7 Despite this increased awareness, post-pandemic vaccination coverage among adults aged 60 and above in China remains alarmingly low.8,9 During 2022 and 2023 influenza season, the national influenza vaccination coverage among this group was only 4.16%, significantly lower than the target rate of 75% recommended by the World Health Organization (WHO).10 Similarly, by the end of 2022, coverage rates for pneumococcal and herpes zoster vaccines for this demographic were only 3.23% and 0.10%, respectively.8 As establishing life-course immunization becomes a priority in the post-pandemic era,11 addressing this significant gap in routine vaccination uptake among older adults is an urgent public health imperative in China.

Vaccination decision-making is a complex process influenced by multilevel factors.12–14 The World Health Organization’s Behavioral and Social Drivers (BeSD) of vaccination framework provides a comprehensive perspective for understanding vaccine uptake, categorizing determinants into three domains: thinking and feeling (e.g., perceived risk, confidence), social processes (e.g., social norms, recommendations from trusted others), and practical issues (e.g., affordability, accessibility).15 Existing research has primarily focused on individual-level determinants within the thinking and feeling domain and practical barriers,16–19 while the social processes domain, particularly family involvement as a relational determinant, has received comparatively less attention. Given robust evidence that family involvement plays a decisive role in healthcare decision-making among older adults across diverse cultures,19–22 to understand the vaccination decisions of Chinese older adults specifically, a focused exploration of family dynamics is essential.

Within the Chinese socio-cultural context, where family ties and filial piety are paramount, health decisions for older adults, particularly for those with limited health literacy, are frequently embedded within family dynamics and rely on the active engagement of adult children.20 This engagement can be conceptualized through the lens of upward intergenerational support (UIS), which is defined as informal social support encompassing financial assistance, caregiving, and emotional support provided by adult children to their aging parents.23–25 Grounded in social support theory, UIS is a key determinant influencing older adults’ health literacy, access to care, and ultimately, their healthcare decision-making processes.24,26–29 Therefore, to understand and promote routine vaccination acceptance among older adults in post-pandemic China, investigating the role of UIS is critical.

Hence, the study aimed to address this gap by exploring how UIS influences vaccination decisions among older adults in China. Employing a qualitative research design, a methodology which has been well-established for exploring attitudes and utilization of vaccination services across populations,30–33 the study was guided by two research questions: 1) What are the key forms of UIS that influence routinely recommended vaccination decisions (for influenza, pneumococcal, and herpes zoster vaccines) among community-dwelling older adults in China? 2) How do these forms of support contribute to distinct decision-making patterns in such choices? By answering these questions, this study moves beyond identifying UIS forms to explore the mechanisms through which they influence older adults’ vaccination decisions regarding influenza, pneumococcal, and herpes zoster vaccines.

Methods

Study design

This study was part of a multicenter qualitative project that employed a phenomenological approach34,35 and aimed to explore the lived experiences and subjective meanings attached to older adults’ vaccination decision-making and upward intergenerational support from the dual perspectives of older adults and adult children in post-pandemic China. Applied throughout the research process, this approach informed the design of open-ended semi-structured interview guides with separate but thematically aligned versions for older adults and adult children to elicit unconstrained first-person narratives from both generations, and guided an inductive, meaning-centered analysis in which all themes were derived directly from participants’ descriptions. This study followed the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR) reporting guideline.36

Settings and participants

We employed a maximum variation sampling strategy at both the regional and individual levels to capture a wide spectrum of sociodemographic contexts and personal experiences, thereby enhancing the transferability of our findings.37 Regionally, a purposive sampling strategy was employed to select seven cities as study sites, guided by three key dimensions: geographical location, socioeconomic development level, and regional vaccination policies. The selected cities were located in East, Central, West, and North China, and covered diverse socioeconomic contexts, ranging from high-income to lower-income regions. Local financial subsidies for recommended vaccines (influenza, pneumococcal, and herpes zoster vaccines) for older adults varied across these cities, encompassing regions offering full government subsidies for the specific recommended vaccine, regions with discounted price promotion for the specific vaccine, and regions where recommended vaccines were entirely self-pay. To further capture local diversity within each city, with assistance from local primary health care professionals, we selected two distinct communities (one located in the new urban district and one in the old urban district) and recruited participants from these communities. At the individual level, we recruited two independent populations: (1) community-dwelling older adults aged ≥ 60 y, and (2) adult children aged 18–59 y self-identifying as primary caregivers for aging parents. For this study, a primary caregiver was operationally defined as an individual who self-identified as being primarily responsible for their parents’ healthcare-related decisions and was recognized within the family as the key contact for their parents’ health affairs. Within each community, these two groups were recruited in parallel and the eligibility for each group was assessed independently (Table 1). Data analysis

All audio recordings were transcribed verbatim in Mandarin shortly after each interview. The resulting transcripts were managed and coded using NVivo 14 (QSR International) software for data analysis. A multi-stage thematic analysis approach guided by Braun and Clarke’s six-phase framework was applied,39,40 through which data analysis proceeded in three structured, iterative stages to balance depth with consistency. This process was designed to respect the richness of data from our maximum variation sampling strategy while rigorously achieving integration across sites. Data analysis was conducted iteratively and in parallel with data collection, aiming to allow for preliminary thematic development which contributed to data saturation assessment to inform the ongoing participant recruitment.

Prior to formal coding, all researchers involved in data analysis completed a standardized training program on qualitative research methods, including detailed familiarization with the semi-structured interview guides and thematic coding principles. To establish inter-coder consistency, 10% of randomly selected early interview transcripts (n = 26) were used for practice coding sessions.41 Discrepancies were resolved through consensus discussion until a unified understanding of coding concepts was achieved across the entire research team. These transcripts were subsequently re-coded and included in the final analysis.

Stage 1: within-site analysis

To preserve contextual depth, the interview data from the seven study sites were analyzed independently by three dedicated pairs of researchers (i.e., each pair was responsible for analyzing data from one or more study sites). Each pair followed the same procedure: familiarization with the transcripts of the site(s) assigned to them, independent open coding, followed by joint discussions to compare codes, resolve discrepancies, and develop a preliminary set of site-specific themes for each assigned site.

Stage 2: cross-site global analysis

To develop an integrative perspective unimpeded by local framing, a core coding team (three researchers with rich qualitative research experiences) was established. First, the core team collaboratively reviewed a random subset of transcripts selected from all seven sites to familiarize themselves with the full scope of data and resolve potential inconsistencies in coding understanding. On this basis, the team jointly developed a unified preliminary coding framework (codebook) through iterative discussions, following a combined deductive-inductive approach. This unified framework was then systematically applied to the entire dataset across all sites to identify cross-site global themes, which served as a critical counterpoint and synthesis framework for the subsequent integration.

Stage 3: integrative consensus and refinement

The entire research team convened for a series of structured consensus meetings. The site-specific themes from Stage 1 and the global framework from Stage 2 were compared, debated, and refined. Particular attention was paid to how the socioeconomic context and local vaccine policies of each site might contextualize the salience or expression of the themes. The conceptual dimensions of social support theory guided code and theme generation throughout the analysis process. All themes were validated against raw data excerpts until a consensus-based final structure was achieved.

The finalized themes, subthemes, and supporting quotations were documented in Mandarin. Then these materials were translated into English by two independent bilingual research assistants, with a back-translation check performed to ensure conceptual and semantic fidelity.

Trustworthiness

Credibility, dependability, confirmability, transferability and reflexivity were considered throughout the research process.42 To ensure credibility, triangulation was used, including method triangulation (semi-structured interviews supplemented by field notes), investigator triangulation (independent coding by researcher pairs), and data source triangulation (perspectives from both older adults and adult children).43,44 To ensure dependability, semi-structured interview guides were used consistently through data collection process, and interviews were conducted in private settings, while research notes were used to document the entire research process. To enhance confirmability and reflexivity, analytic memos and diaries were employed to record all analytical steps including coding decisions, theme development, and personal reflections on potential preconceptions and interpretive biases, and regular team debriefings challenged individual assumptions to ensure findings were grounded in the data.42 To ensure transferability, we provided thick descriptions of participants’ experiences alongside their contextual backgrounds, including regional conditions and vaccine policies.42

Forms of UIS on older adults’ vaccination decision-making

This study identified five key forms of UIS that influence older adults’ vaccination decisions: financial, practical, informational, emotional, and appraisal support, which emerged as central to older adults’ routine vaccination decisions, with their provision and salience linked to regional socioeconomic and health policy contexts (Figure 1). Theme 1: financial support: the material basis for decision-making

Financial support from adult children constituted a fundamental factor in older adults’ vaccination decision, serving as the critical foundation that underpinned their willingness and ability to get vaccinated. This support was not a single dimensional factor but had a multifaceted influence on the vaccination decision-making process of older adults. It influenced not only whether they could access vaccines initially but also expanding their choices when they decide to be vaccinated.

Enabling the vaccine accessibility

Financial support often determined whether older adults’ vaccination could proceed. Its role as a decisive barrier was most acute in regions with lower average incomes, regions lacking government-subsidized or free vaccination programs, and for families where older adults lacked stable pensions. This pattern was particularly observable for the high-cost, self-paid herpes zoster vaccine, where the substantial out-of-pocket expense presented a notable barrier for many older adults, making financial support from adult children a key determinant of uptake. Enabling for expanded vaccine choices

Adult children’s financial support also functioned to broaden older adults’ available options after their basic decision to vaccinate was made. The extent of financial support influenced the type, brand, or number of vaccines chosen. For some affluent families, adult children preferred to provide more money to enable their parents to receive more types of vaccines and imported vaccines, to ensure their parents could receive more comprehensive protection. Theme 2: practical support: reducing logistical barriers to action

Practical support facilitated both the formulation and, ultimately, the execution of the vaccination decision by addressing tangible challenges, including digital navigation, transportation, and on-site companionship. This support demonstrated that for older adults, a positive choice of vaccination decision would be made if logistical support was assured. For geographically separated and non-coresident parent-child households, practical support adapted to physical distance: it shifted from in-person companionship to a mix of remote digital coordination (e.g., booking online appointments and preparing printed documents for parents to carry) and concentrated transportation assistance scheduled around adult children’s periodic home visits.

Unfamiliarity with digital devices was a critical barrier that reduced older adults’ vaccination intention. The pervasiveness of this digital divide was impacted by both regional development and age. While a lack of digital autonomy was common among older adults overall, it was most pronounced among the higher-age elderly and in less socioeconomically developed regions. Even in more developed areas, significant reliance on children persisted, particularly for older adults in older age or with lower personal digital literacy. Theme 3: informational support: navigating the post-pandemic infodemic

Informational support referred to transmission of vaccine-related narratives, knowledge and guidance from adult children to aging parents, which contributed to influencing their parents’ cognitions to vaccinations.46,47 Adult children served as information gatekeepers on vaccination for their aging parents. By selecting, interpreting, and reframing health messages, their informational support shaped older adults’ perceptions of vaccine-related risks and benefits, thereby influencing vaccination decisions. However, this influence was closely tied to adult children’s own health and vaccine literacy, which determined whether the information they conveyed built vaccine confidence or fostered fear.

Adult children fostered their parents’ vaccine confidence by transferring and explicating information from sources they perceived as credible. This positive guidance was especially pronounced in regions with supportive vaccine policies or active public health outreach targeting older adult vaccination, where a steady stream of authoritative information was available for families to reference. In regions with free vaccination policies for a specific vaccine (e.g., influenza vaccine), informational support often centered on disseminating local free vaccination policies and enhancing older adults’ awareness of the relevant disease. Emotional support referred to the transmission of feelings, affective attitudes, and emotional responses related to vaccination from adult children to aging parents, which influenced their parents’ emotional state and affective risk perception in decision-making.46,47 In the post-pandemic context, where vaccine-related anxieties were salient,48,49 emotional support from adult children performed a pivotal yet dual role. It served either as a crucial resource to alleviate concerns and foster their parents’ acceptance of vaccinations, or conversely, as an unintentional source of amplified fear that suppressed their vaccination intention.

Through expressions of care and encouragement, adult children contributed to addressing their parents’ fears including those exacerbated or generalized from pandemic experiences, which provided essential emotional security. This reassurance helped reframe older adults’ perceived risk to vaccination, enhancing older adults’ resolve to overcome hesitancy. Patterns of UIS navigating vaccination decisions

These five forms of support were not isolated but rather interact as an interconnected system to influence older adults’ vaccination decisions. These forms of support shaped older adults’ decision-making process through two distinct patterns: the substitutive pattern and the facilitative pattern. In the substitutive pattern, by providing forms of support, adult children considered themselves entitled to directly make the decision for their parents. In the facilitative pattern, intergenerational support served as a resource base to enable a decision made by the older adults themselves.

Theme 1: the substitutive pattern: intergenerational support determining compliance to decision

In this pattern, adult children acted as agents who directly made vaccination decisions for their aging parents. By providing key forms of support, they assumed dominance and positioned themselves as the decision-makers eligible to determine their parents’ vaccination choices. In such circumstances, although older adults ultimately consented to their children’s decisions, they were seldom involved in the decision-making process. This substitutive decision-making did not constitute an autonomous choice, but rather represented compliance with the directives of those providing support. Financial support served as a core material foundation for this pattern. When older adults relied on their children for vaccination costs, adult children often assumed greater decision-making authority, giving rise to the substitutive dynamic where children determined whether their parents received vaccination. Compounding this dynamic, residential distance appeared to be associated with a greater tendency toward the substitutive pattern. Since financial support could be fully delivered remotely via digital channels, with limited opportunities for in-depth in-person discussion, adult children who handled these remote financial arrangements tended to assume direct decision-making responsibility, with less nuanced, face-to-face deliberation of the older adult’s preferences and consent. Discussion

This study elucidates the multifaceted role of upward intergenerational support in influencing the vaccination decision-making of older adults in post-pandemic China. Our findings move beyond a simplistic individual-level analysis of vaccine hesitancy, positioning the family as the central locus where resources, norms, and authority converge to enable or constrain health behavior. Our analysis revealed that upward intergenerational support manifests in five key forms, including financial, practical, informational, emotional, and appraisal support. These forms of support converged into two distinct patterns that families navigate vaccination decisions: a substitutive pattern, where adult children directly make decisions for their parents, and a facilitative pattern, where support is provided in ways that preserve and strengthen the older adult’s own decision-making capacity. The impact of these supports and patterns are not merely interpersonal but are profoundly contextualized by macro-level factors and vaccine-specific attributes, including regional socioeconomic disparities, the legacy of the COVID-19 infodemic, the differences in vaccines’ prices, and the presence or absence of governmental vaccine subsidies.

Consistent with the BeSD framework which identifies practical issues such as availability, affordability, and access as foundational determinants of vaccine uptake,15 our findings on financial and practical support illustrate how these determinants manifest within family dynamics. Adult children act as financial sponsors and logistical navigators, directly influencing the economic and physical accessibility of vaccination services for their aging parents. A systematic review focused on older adults globally also corroborated that cost and access are key factors in older adults’ vaccination.51 Furthermore, we found that in regions lacking government subsidies and among older adults with poor economic conditions, adult children’s financial support became a preliminary factor in vaccination accessibility, especially for high-cost self-paid vaccines such as the herpes zoster vaccine, for which intergenerational financial support often functions as a necessary condition for access. Similarly, the demand for “digital bridge” support from adult children was exacerbated in regions with less developed digital health infrastructure or among older adults with lower socioeconomic status.52 These findings illustrate that structural inequities can be reflected and negotiated within households, necessitating locally tailored interventions. In under-resourced regions, engaging and empowering families may be a more viable and immediate strategy than awaiting relevant policy adjustment.

However, we also identified a critical nuance that moves beyond a simple economic transaction: the impact of financial support is moderated by intergenerational differences in health consumption values. A thrift-oriented perspective common among older adults, which may view preventive health expenditure as unnecessary, can lead to the refusal of children’s financial assistance, even when it was economically feasible for the family. This finding suggested that the barrier is not solely financial capacity but also the perceptual alignment regarding the value of health investment. Therefore, future intervention should also focus on bridging this values gap and foster a shared understanding of vaccination as a worthwhile priority.

Informational, emotional, and appraisal support operate primarily within the cognitive and motivational domains of decision-making, with adult children serving as crucial “information gatekeepers” and “meaning-makers” for their aging parents. This role has been critically amplified by the COVID-19 infodemic, while adult children served as essential informational intermediaries and filters and for their parents.53–55 Our findings revealed the dual functional nature of the informational support, which thereby gave rise to two distinct forms of risk judgment: evidence-based reasonable risk assessment and emotionally driven excessive protectionism. When equipped with higher health literacy, adult children can select, translate, and convey effective information that facilitates parents’ acceptance of vaccination. Conversely, in some cases, in cases driven by a generalized avoidance of risk, adult children tended to focus primarily on potential adverse events. By conveying excessive concern over side effects, they may inadvertently amplify anxiety and reinforce vaccine hesitancy among their parents. These findings were consistent with results of previous quantitative research within the Chinese context. One study illustrated that positive informational support from adult children significantly enhances the health literacy of older adults themselves.56 Another study demonstrated that in very close parent–child relationships, adult children’s heightened concerns about their parents’ health can translate into significant hesitancy toward their parents’ vaccination.57 Hence, public health interventions need to strategically target adult children not merely as information conduits, but as key agents to be equipped. Improving their vaccine literacy and providing practical communication skills are essential to empower them as facilitators of vaccine confidence, rather than unintentional sources of hesitation.

The identification of the substitutive and facilitative patterns explains how UIS translates into intrafamily decisions on older adults’ vaccination. The substitutive pattern, rooted in a traditional filial duty to “act for” one’s parents,58 was mainly triggered by the self-identified authority after providing the significant financial support. Residential separation further amplified this dynamic. As financial support could be fully delivered via digital channels, geographic distance would not erode adult children’s financially grounded decision-making authority; instead, reduced in-person interaction might limit the opportunities for parent-child in-depth deliberation, thereby reinforcing the substitutive decision-making pattern. While it can promote vaccine uptake, it may also risk fostering passive dependency and undermining the older adult’s autonomy and long-term health engagement.20 In contrast, the facilitative pattern represents an older adult-centered collaborative model, where older adults’ capacity for health decision-making is enhanced, aligning with health empowerment theory in geriatric care.59 The efficacy of this pattern is supported by a cluster randomized controlled trial, which demonstrated that educating grandchildren significantly increased COVID-19 vaccination uptake among their grandparents: serving as influential role models, grandchildren’s knowledge and attitudes toward vaccination can encourage and support their grandparents’ decision-making.60 Therefore, future interventions seeking to leverage UIS for promoting vaccination should focus not on inserting adult children as new decision-makers, but on coaching them to take an enabling, facilitative role that supports older adults in making autonomous vaccination choices, thereby promoting sustainable vaccine acceptance and long-term health engagement. Hence, health programs can adopt dyadic (child-parent pair-based) engagement strategies, such as developing vaccine-specific family communication toolkits. These toolkits should equip adult children with shared decision-making training and deliberative communication techniques, rather than relying on simple persuasion.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, as a sub-study, we were limited in our ability to iteratively refine the sampling strategy specifically for the research question on intergenerational support. We recruited older adults and adult children as two independent populations rather than as pre-defined dyads. Parent-child dyads were identified post hoc during data management and constituted only 6 pairs (12 individuals) with the remaining participants coming from different families. Given the limited number of dyads, systematic within-dyad concordance/discordance analysis was not feasible. Our analytic approach prioritized thematic saturation across the full sample and triangulation of perspectives between the two generational groups. The six dyads provided illustrative insights into within-family dynamics that informed theme development, but our thematic framework was built on patterns observed across the entire dataset. Future research with a formal dyadic design is needed to examine within-family concordance and discordance. Second, this study was conducted among an urban population across selected regions of eastern, central, western, and northern China; therefore, findings may not be fully generalizable to rural contexts. For instance, limited regional socioeconomic development and individual income level in rural areas may heighten the financial burden of vaccination, altering the influence of financial support compared to urban contexts. Meanwhile, limited healthcare infrastructure and lower digital literacy may amplify the need for practical and navigational support. Moreover, higher rates of adult child out-migration in rural areas may limit in-person deliberation, potentially shifting decision-making patterns toward substitutive rather than facilitative pattern. To systematically explore the influence of these contextual conditions, future studies with rural-urban comparative design are needed. Third, self-reported interview data may introduce social desirability bias, particularly when participants were asked about filial roles and family decision-making. Furthermore, variations in interviewers’ communication styles and research settings across the seven study sites may introduce potential information bias. To mitigate these risks, we implemented several rigorous quality control measures: standardized semi-structured interview guides used consistently across all sites, standardized researcher training, cross-site data triangulation to identify generalizable patterns, and centralized core team oversight of all coding processes to ensure uniform interpretation. Fourth, considering that public attitude toward vaccines and public health policies continue to evolve in the post-pandemic context, future longitudinal research is needed to understand how these upward intergenerational support dynamics change over time. Additionally, future quantitative studies are needed to measure how these support mechanisms influence vaccination decisions, thereby providing the evidence base for developing family-centric interventions to increase vaccine acceptance among older adults.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that upward intergenerational support (UIS) played a pivotal role in influencing the vaccination decisions of older adults through a multidimensional system encompassing financial, practical, informational, emotional, and appraisal dimensions. These support mechanisms operated through two qualitatively distinct relational patterns: a substitutive pattern, which may enhance vaccine uptake but risks undermining individual autonomy, and a facilitative pattern, which strengthened older adults’ own agency and decision-making capacity. Notably, the influence of intergenerational support on vaccination decisions was further contextualized by post-pandemic concerns and regional socioeconomic disparities. Therefore, to improve the uptake of recommended vaccines among older adults, a strategic shift from an individual-centric to a family-systems paradigm is essential. This transition requires the development and implementation of targeted interventions that empower families to provide facilitative, autonomy-respecting support for sustainable vaccine acceptance. Specifically, public health campaigns should tailor messaging for both older adults and their adult children, emphasize the value of facilitative support, and address structural barriers including vaccine affordability and digital access that constrain family-based vaccination decision-making.
