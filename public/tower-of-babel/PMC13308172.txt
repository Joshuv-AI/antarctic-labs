Understanding communication challenges in nursing care of patients with limited health literacy: a qualitative study
Authors: Norah M Alyahya, Abdulaziz M Alodhailah, Waleed M Alshehri, Thurayya Eid, Bader M Almutairy, Bandar S Alharbi, Faihan F Alshaibany, Albandari Almutairi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLimited health literacy affects millions globally, impairing patient understanding, treatment adherence, and health outcomes. Nurses, as primary healthcare educators, navigate complex communication challenges with vulnerable patients. However, research examining nurses’ lived experiences of managing these challenges remains limited, particularly regarding how organizational and systemic factors enable or constrain communication effectiveness. This study aimed to explore registered nurses’ experiences, perceived barriers, adaptive communication strategies, and systemic factors influencing nurse-patient communication in acute care settings with diverse patient populations.MethodsDescriptive qualitative study employing semi-structured interviews with 18 registered nurses across emergency, intensive care, medical-surgical, and specialized acute care units. Data were analyzed using Braun & Clarke’s six-phase thematic analysis approach. Trustworthiness was enhanced through member checking, peer debriefing, comprehensive audit trail documentation, and prolonged engagement with data.FindingsFour interconnected themes emerged: (1) Multifaceted Communication Barriers encompassing language limitations, cultural-epistemological incongruence, and foundational knowledge gaps; (2) Strategic Communication Approaches including simplified language, visual demonstrations, teach-back methods, and family engagement; (3) Systemic Obstacles comprising inadequate resources, time constraints, unclear policies, and institutional ambivalence substantially constraining communication effectiveness; and (4) Emotional Labor and Professional Resilience capturing nurses’ psychological experiences, moral distress, and coping mechanisms.ConclusionsEffective nurse-patient communication with individuals with limited health literacy requires interventions at individual, interpersonal, and organizational levels. Findings suggest that communication effectiveness is substantially shaped by organisational factors staffing, resourcing, policy, and accountability and that framing communication gaps as primarily individual competence deficits misrepresents what is, in large part, a structural problem. Individual skill and professional effort remain important; however, they operate within organisational parameters that participants frequently experienced as inadequate. Healthcare organisations must allocate dedicated resources, establish explicit patient education policies, and create accountability mechanisms to enable nurses to communicate effectively with patients with limited health literacy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-026-04869-5.Keywords: Health literacy, Nurse-patient communication, Qualitative research, Patient education, Communication barriers, Healthcare systems, Nursing practice, Acute care

Introduction

Limited health literacy, difficulty understanding and applying health information necessary for health decisions, affects approximately 258 million adults globally, creating substantial risks for patient safety, treatment non-adherence, preventable hospitalizations, and mortality [1–3]. While health literacy is frequently conceptualized as an individual characteristic rooted in patient education level and cognitive capacity, contemporary understanding recognizes it as a dynamic interaction between individual capabilities and healthcare system design, provider communication quality, and accessibility of health information [1, 4].

Nurses, spending substantial clinical time educating patients and families, bear primary responsibility for health communication in healthcare settings. Effective nursing communication directly improves patient satisfaction, understanding, medication adherence, and clinical outcomes [5, 6]. Contemporary acute care practice demands sophisticated communication competencies; nurses must adapt explanations to diverse literacy levels, cultural backgrounds, and health belief systems. Paradoxically, nursing curricula historically provide limited content on health literacy assessment and responsive communication strategies, leaving nurses to develop approaches through informal experience and mentoring rather than systematic preparation [7, 8].

Communication barriers operate across multiple levels. Language differences create fundamental obstacles when patients’ primary languages diverge from the healthcare settings’ dominant language. Beyond linguistic challenges, deeply held cultural beliefs and health conceptualizations frequently conflict with biomedical frameworks that nurses present, patients may attribute illness to fate, supernatural forces, or hereditary factors rather than biomedical mechanisms. Educational disparities mean some patients lack foundational health knowledge nurses assume, necessitating teaching from basic anatomical and physiological concepts [9, 10]. Simultaneously, systemic constraints impose structural barriers: inadequate staffing creates time pressures limiting patient education time; insufficient translation services force reliance on family interpretation; inadequate educational materials in patients’ languages impede comprehension; and unclear institutional policies leave nurses uncertain about their education responsibility and accountability [11, 12].

While extensive literature documents health literacy’s relationship to patient outcomes and identifies communication barriers, relatively few qualitative studies examine the process perspective, how nurses actually navigate these challenges in real clinical practice, what strategies they perceive as effective, how systemic factors enable or constrain efforts, and how this work affects nurse wellbeing. Understanding nursing perspectives is essential to developing implementable interventions addressing real-world practice constraints while maintaining patient-centered care. This study addresses this gap by centering nurses’ lived experiences communicating with patients with limited health literacy, recognizing that nurses are expert observers of both interpersonal communication dynamics and systemic barriers constraining practice. Understanding these complex experiences can inform educational, policy, and organizational interventions.

Methods

Research design and epistemological positioning

This descriptive qualitative study, informed by an interpretive descriptive methodology and a critical realist epistemology [13], explored how nurses experience and navigate communication challenges when delivering care in acute settings. Critical realism was adopted as the epistemological foundation because it acknowledges that the social world, including nurse–patient communication is shaped by both objective structural conditions (e.g., staffing ratios, resource availability, organisational policies) and the subjective meanings that nurses ascribe to those conditions [14]. This framing allowed the study to move beyond purely descriptive accounts toward an analysis of how real-world constraints and professional meanings interact to produce the communication experiences nurses described.

Interpretive description [15] was chosen as the overarching qualitative methodology because it is designed for health-related inquiry that moves beyond surface description toward analytical coherence grounded in participants’ lived experience. It does not require adherence to a single philosophical tradition, which suited this study’s aim of examining nurses’ communication practices as they occur in real clinical contexts. A phenomenological sensibility, drawing on interpretive phenomenological principles [16], guided analytic attention to the meanings nurses attributed to their communication encounters, viewing these experiences as contextualised and shaped by environmental demands, interpersonal relationships, and professional values. Rather than applying strict phenomenological reduction, the study used this sensibility as an orienting lens within the broader interpretive descriptive framework. Where the discussion moves from data-grounded observation toward theoretical contextualisation using Bourdieu’s theory of cultural capital [17] and Benner’s expertise development model [18], this is explicitly signalled to readers.

Setting and participant selection

The study was conducted at a tertiary-level acute care facility serving a diverse patient population with varied socioeconomic, educational, and cultural backgrounds. The hospital provides specialized and general acute care for complex and critically ill patients, many of whom have limited health literacy and diverse language backgrounds.

Eligibility criteria included: (1) registered nurses with at least two years of clinical experience; (2) current employment in acute care settings such as the emergency department, intensive care unit, or medical–surgical units; (3) regular direct patient care responsibilities involving patient education and communication; and (4) fluency in the study language. Nurses in administrative roles without patient care, student nurses with less than two years of experience, and those unable to provide informed consent were excluded.

Purposive maximum variation sampling was used to recruit 18 registered nurses with diverse clinical specialties, years of experience (2–26 years), educational backgrounds (diploma to master’s level), and gender. This approach enhanced credibility by capturing perspectives across varied acute care contexts. Sample size was determined by purposive sampling principles rather than a predetermined numerical target: recruitment continued until thematic saturation was achieved, which occurred by interview 16, with interviews 17 and 18 confirming rather than expanding existing themes.

Data collection procedures

Individual in-depth semi-structured interviews (ranging from 35 to 50 min) were conducted and audio-recorded with participants’ consent. Interviews took place during participants’ non-clinical time in private settings to ensure confidentiality and comfort.

The interview guide, refined through pilot testing with two experienced nurses, covered five domains: (1) communication experiences with patients with limited health literacy, (2) effective communication strategies, (3) contextual facilitators and barriers, (4) patient and family interactions, and (5) professional development needs. This structure ensured consistency while allowing flexibility for participants’ narratives.

Probing questions such as “Tell me more about that” and “How did that affect your practice?” encouraged deeper reflection. Researchers avoided imposing predefined definitions, instead asking clarifying questions to capture participants’ own interpretations.

Demographic data were also collected, including age, gender, education level, years of nursing experience, experience with patients with limited health literacy, and clinical specialty.

Researcher reflexivity and positionality

The research team consisted of faculty members with extensive clinical experience in emergency, intensive care, and medical–surgical nursing, along with expertise in qualitative research, patient education, and health services research. This background positioned the researchers as clinical insiders, enabling a nuanced understanding of participants’ experiences while also posing a risk of assuming shared norms in nursing practice.

To minimize potential bias, several reflexive strategies were employed. Regular research team meetings during data collection and analysis were used to examine assumptions and reflect on how clinical experience might shape interpretation. Researchers maintained reflexive memos to document evolving perspectives and analytical decisions.

Interview techniques were designed to prioritize participants’ own meanings by avoiding predefined assumptions about health literacy or communication challenges. Additionally, an audit trail documented coding and analytical decisions throughout the process.

Finally, member checking with six participants from diverse clinical settings provided opportunities to validate interpretations and refine emerging themes, particularly regarding the emotional and relational dimensions of nursing care. Despite these strategies, the researchers acknowledge that their professional perspectives may still have influenced the interpretation of findings.

Thematic analysis procedures

Thematic analysis was conducted using Braun and Clarke’s [19] systematic six-phase approach, integrated with interpretive description principles, using NVivo 12 qualitative analysis software. NVivo served as an organisational and retrieval tool to manage codes, store reflexive memos, and facilitate systematic comparison of data segments across participants; all analytical and interpretive decisions were made by the research team.

Phase 1: Familiarization

All interviews were transcribed verbatim. Transcripts were checked for accuracy; researchers listened to audio recordings while reading transcripts to ensure fidelity. All transcripts were read repeatedly, with marginal notes capturing preliminary observations.

Phase 2: Initial coding

Two researchers independently coded two interviews line-by-line, generating initial codes capturing meaningful units remaining grounded in participant language and context. Team meetings compared coding approaches, refined code definitions, and established codebook consistency.

Phase 3: Iterative coding

All transcripts were systematically coded using the refined codebook. Codes were refined as patterns emerged; new codes were created as needed while maintaining grounding in participant language. Approximately 200 codes were generated across all interviews.

Phase 4: Theme development

Coded segments were organized into potential theme clusters reflecting overarching patterns. Relationships among codes were examined systematically. Conceptually coherent phenomena were identified and organized into candidate themes. Theme labels were refined to reflect both data content and theoretical significance. Specifically, the approximately 200 initial codes were sorted using NVivo 12’s node-grouping function into 14 candidate sub-theme clusters based on conceptual similarity and shared empirical content. Research team meetings then examined coherence and distinctiveness across clusters. Four overarching themes were identified because they captured functionally separable dimensions of nurses’ experience: communication barriers, adaptive strategies, systemic constraints, and emotional labour. Two early candidate themes (“professional identity” and “patient agency”) were dissolved and their content integrated into Themes 2 and 1 respectively, as team review determined they lacked sufficient distinctiveness to warrant independent theme status. These decisions are documented in the study’s audit trail.

Phase 5: Theme refinement

Themes were interrogated through a systematic review of supporting data, confirming internal consistency and distinctiveness from other themes. Weak themes (with insufficient supporting evidence) were reconsidered, consolidated with related themes, or eliminated. This phase involved reviewing each theme against the full dataset to ensure all supporting data had been captured and were appropriately coded.

Phase 6: Reporting

Themes were defined and exemplified through carefully selected participant quotations integrated with relevant literature to construct coherent narrative accounts demonstrating relationships among themes and theoretical significance.

Trustworthiness and rigor

Trustworthiness was ensured using Yvonna S. Lincoln and Egon G. Guba’s [20] framework, addressing credibility, dependability, confirmability, and transferability.

Credibility was strengthened through iterative data analysis, involvement of multiple analysts, and member checking. Six participants representing diverse clinical settings reviewed a summary of preliminary themes. Most confirmed the findings, while one emergency nurse suggested that emotional adaptation was underrepresented, leading to refinement of Theme 4 to distinguish acute emotional responses from chronic adaptation and burnout risk.

Dependability was supported through a comprehensive audit trail documenting coding development, code consolidation, and analytical decisions. Theme development was discussed across several research meetings, with iterative refinement to ensure conceptual clarity.

Confirmability was enhanced through extensive use of participant quotations and reflexive documentation addressing potential researcher assumptions. These strategies allowed readers to assess how interpretations were grounded in the data.

Transferability was supported through maximum variation sampling across specialties, experience levels, education, and gender. Detailed descriptions of the study context and participant experiences enable readers to judge relevance to other settings.

Thematic saturation was reached by interview 16, with the final interviews confirming rather than expanding the existing themes.

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by King Saud University’s Institutional Research Ethics Committee (Reference KSU-HE-25-1439). All procedures adhered to institutional guidelines, national regulations, and international ethical standards for human subjects research, including the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants after explaining the study’s purpose, procedures, potential risks and benefits, confidentiality measures, right to withdraw without consequence, and intended use of data for research purposes only. Participant identification numbers replaced personal identifiers in all transcripts and analyses, and demographic information was stored separately with restricted access. Audio recordings and transcripts were kept on encrypted, password-protected devices accessible only to the authorized research team. No identifying information appears in any publications or presentations, ensuring participants’ confidentiality and anonymity throughout the study.

Findings

Participants ranged in age from 27 to 58 years (mean 39.4 ± 10.2 years); 13 participants (72.2%) were female, reflecting nursing workforce demographics in many developed healthcare systems. Educational backgrounds included six (33.3%) with nursing diplomas, ten (55.6%) with bachelor’s degrees, and two (11.1%) with master’s degrees. Total nursing experience ranged from 2 to 26 years (mean 11.2 ± 7.8 years). Experience specifically with patients with limited health literacy ranged from 2 to 20 years (mean 7.8 ± 5.2 years).

Thematic framework: four interconnected themes

Detailed thematic analysis

Theme 1: multifaceted communication barriers, barriers as epistemological and cultural challenges

Nurses described communication barriers operating at multiple levels, encompassing language limitations, cultural-epistemological incongruence, and foundational knowledge gaps. Critically, analysis reveals these barriers operate not simply as communication obstacles amenable to technique-based solutions, but as deeper challenges rooted in fundamentally different meaning-making frameworks.

Language and linguistic barriers

Fourteen of 18 participants (77.8%) described language differences as primary obstacles. A nurse working in emergency care noted: “When patients don’t speak fluent [institutional language], family translators distort medical information. Visual demonstration helps, but without a clear language explanation, patients remain genuinely confused.” Language barriers extended beyond simple linguistic differences; they involved patients’ lack of medical terminology in their primary languages and the absence of culturally equivalent concepts. For example, explaining “hypertension” when patients’ languages lack precise equivalents required nurses to create circumlocutions, often resulting in imprecise understanding.

Cultural-epistemological incongruence

More profoundly, ten participants (55.6%) described encounters where patients’ illness attributions conflicted with biomedical explanations. Rather than representing ignorance, participants’ accounts revealed these beliefs were sophisticated, internally coherent explanatory systems embedded in religious faith, family experience, and community knowledge. One intensive care nurse described: “I tried explaining why antibiotics matter when they believe prayer is the real cure, that’s a different level of challenge. It’s not that they don’t understand the words. They understand, but they operate from a different knowledge system.”

Foundational knowledge gaps

Eight participants (44.4%) described patients as lacking the foundational health knowledge that nurses assumed. A medical-surgical nurse explained: “I start explaining diabetes management, and I realize they don’t understand what a pancreas is. I have to go back to basics, what organs exist, what they do, before explaining disease.” These gaps made explanations more time-intensive and required nurses to develop creative teaching strategies.

Theme 2: strategic communication approaches, nurses as adaptive communicators

Nurses employed sophisticated communication strategies grounded in clinical wisdom and a genuine commitment to patient understanding. Notably, most participants had not learned these approaches through formal education; they represented tacit knowledge developed through experiential learning, what Benner describes as progression from novice to expert practice.

Simplified language and plain communication

Fifteen participants (83.3%) described deliberately simplifying language. One nurse noted: “I avoid medical jargon. I use everyday words. Instead of ‘myocardial infarction,’ I say ‘a major blood clot blocked blood going to your heart.’ Instead of ‘hypotension,’ I say ‘your blood pressure dropped too low.‘” This strategy reflects explicit recognition that professional language creates barriers; simplification requires nurses to translate concepts into patients’ linguistic worlds.

Visual demonstrations and tangible teaching

Participants frequently described using visual aids, anatomical models, demonstrations, and concrete examples. One emergency nurse explained: “I show them on the anatomical model what happened in their body. Or if they have swelling, I point to it, show them the contrast with other areas. Concrete, visual things communicate across language barriers better than talking.” Thirteen participants (72.2%) described systematic use of visual approaches. Remarkably, these evidence-based strategies (supported by health literacy literature) were not uniformly taught in nursing education; nurses developed them through self-directed learning or clinical experience.

Teach-back methods and verification of understanding

Ten participants (55.6%) described explicitly asking patients to explain back what was taught. One intensive care nurse noted: “I don’t just explain and leave. I ask them to tell me back what they understood. If they can explain it, I know they understood. If not, I know I need to explain differently.” This strategy immediately reveals understanding gaps and allows iterative explanation, yet many nurses reported implementing teach-back only when they thought to do so rather than systematically.

Family engagement and cultural brokering

Twelve participants (66.7%) described involving family members in patient education. Beyond translation, families served important cultural brokering roles, helping nurses understand patients’ values and health beliefs. One nurse explained: “Family members understand the cultural context. They help me understand why patients might refuse treatment. Then I can address the actual concern rather than just explaining why the treatment is medically necessary.” This approach recognizes family as a partner in communication rather than viewing them as an interference.

Theme 3: systemic obstacles, communication barriers as organizational design problems

A striking finding was that all 18 participants described systemic constraints substantially limiting communication effectiveness. Rather than representing barriers individual nurses might overcome through skill or effort, systemic obstacles appeared to be structural issues remissible through organizational policy and resource allocation.

Time pressures and workload constraints

Every participant described insufficient time for patient education. One emergency nurse stated: “I’m responsible for six patients, two are on ventilators. I do my best to explain, but I’m often giving condensed versions because I’m running between rooms.” A medical-surgical nurse noted: “I know patients need thorough education, but with my assignment, I’m lucky to do basic explanations and documentation.” These accounts represent not individual failures but structural design issues, nursing care assignments, staffing ratios, and expectations for patient education were not aligned. When hospitals expect comprehensive patient education while staffing inadequately, they create impossible situations.

Inadequate resources and translation services

Participants frequently reported insufficient translation services, requiring reliance on family interpretation with its attendant risks of information distortion. One nurse explained: “Professional interpreters aren’t available during nights and weekends. I end up asking family members to translate medication information, though I know they may not be accurate.” Only three participants (16.7%) reported routine access to professional interpretation services; others described ad hoc arrangements, which were often inadequate for clinical complexity.

Educational materials were frequently unavailable in patients’ languages. When materials existed, they were often at reading levels too advanced for patients with limited literacy. Several nurses reported creating custom educational materials during personal time to address this gap, while demonstrating professional dedication, this represents reliance on individual nurses’ unpaid labor to provide the infrastructure that healthcare organizations should provide.

Ambivalence and policy gaps

Organizations professed commitment to patient education without providing resources or accountability mechanisms. One nurse stated: “Administration says patient education is important. But they don’t allocate time for it in our assignments. They don’t provide materials. They don’t track whether we’re doing it. It feels like they want us to do it without actually supporting it.” Fourteen participants (77.8%) described similar institutional ambivalence, stated values regarding patient education unaccompanied by resource allocation, explicit policies, or accountability measures.

Lack of institutional support and mentorship

Participants frequently reported limited systematic support for developing communication skills. Only four participants (22.2%) reported having received formal training in health literacy communication. Others described learning through trial and error, peer mentoring, or self-directed learning. Several noted they had created educational materials or pursued specialized learning on personal time. While individual initiative reflects professionalism, relying on nurses’ unpaid labor to compensate for institutional inadequacy is neither sustainable nor equitable. Participant accounts illustrated the extent of this gap. One nurse explained: “I created patient education materials during my personal time because they weren’t available. I learned about cultural health beliefs by reading on my own time.” (P7, Intensive Care). Another described: “Nobody taught me how to assess whether a patient actually understood me. I figured it out after years of noticing when things went wrong.” (P15, Medical-Surgical). A third reflected: “We have a lot of training on clinical procedures. Nothing on how to explain those procedures to someone who has never heard of them.” (P4, Emergency). These accounts reflect a pattern across units and experience levels: formal institutional support for communication skill development was largely absent, leaving nurses to develop competence through unrecognised individual effort.

Theme 4: emotional labor and professional resilience, complex emotional experiences

Theme 4 captured emotional dimensions of communication work with patients with limited health literacy, revealing paradoxical patterns where nurses simultaneously experienced frustration and moral distress alongside remarkable resilience and commitment.

Moral distress and professional frustration

Participants experienced genuine moral distress, and gaps between professional values and organizational constraints created a psychological burden. One nurse stated: “It troubles me deeply when I know patients should receive thorough education but time and resources prevent it.” Another explained: “I feel like I’m failing patients when I can’t help them understand. I know understanding is essential to safe care, but I don’t have time to explain adequately.” Sixteen participants (88.9%) described moral distress; healthcare organizations simultaneously expect comprehensive patient education while structuring work in ways making this impossible.

Professional resilience and adaptive coping

Paradoxically, many nurses demonstrated remarkable resilience, viewing communication challenges as opportunities for professional growth rather than threats. One nurse noted: “I’ve invested time learning about cultural health beliefs common in my community. That investment paid off, I communicate more effectively.” Another explained: “When patients don’t understand, I take it as signal I need to explain differently. It’s like a puzzle to solve.”

However, the finding that many nurses proactively developed resources and pursued self-directed learning to compensate for institutional inadequacy raises ethical concerns. While individual initiative is admirable, relying on nurses’ unpaid labor to create essential infrastructure is neither sustainable nor equitable. Institutional responsibility must shift toward systematically supporting all nurses, not only those with exceptional initiative or personal resources.

Chronic emotional adaptation and burnout risk

Eight participants (44.4%) described patterns of emotional adaptation over time. Some described becoming “numb” to communication limitations: “I’ve been doing this for 20 years. I used to get frustrated about the time issues. Now I’m just… numb to it. I do what I can.” Others expressed concern about sustainability: “This constant gap between what I know patients need and what I can actually provide, I worry about how long I can sustain this.” These accounts suggest that sustained moral distress may contribute to emotional exhaustion and professional disengagement over time.

Summary of thematic integration

The four themes represent interconnected dimensions of nurse-patient communication operating across individual-interpersonal and organizational-systemic levels. Discussion

Understanding communication as systemic rather than an individual problem

This qualitative study revealed a notable paradox: nurses demonstrated sophisticated communication competence and genuine commitment to patient understanding, yet consistently reported that organisational factors substantially constrained what they could achieve. Findings suggest that communication challenges in this context are not primarily attributable to individual skill deficits; rather, they are substantially shaped by organisational design, resource allocation, and healthcare system structures [21, 22]. This reframing has profound implications for how nursing education, practice, and policy address health literacy [12, 23].

Existing health literacy literature emphasizes individual factors, patient comprehension, numeracy, educational attainment, and positioning improvement as contingent on individual learning or, in clinical contexts, on individual provider skill [24, 25]. Our findings extend this model by documenting how organizational and systemic factors function as primary barriers. When an emergency nurse described being simultaneously responsible for six patients, including two on ventilators, and having to condense patient education into brief exchanges while moving between rooms (P3), this was not a communication skill failure but rather a resource allocation failure. Healthcare organizations had structured acute care work in ways making thorough patient education nearly impossible. This finding aligns with critical systems perspectives in nursing and health services research, suggesting that addressing health literacy gaps requires organizational-level interventions in addition to individual-level education. These systemic gaps carry patient safety implications. Research demonstrates that limited health literacy is associated with elevated mortality risk and higher rates of preventable hospitalisation and emergency department use [1, 11]. The specific communication failures documented here, time-constrained explanations, reliance on untrained family interpreters, and inconsistent application of teach-back—are plausible proximal mechanisms through which system-level resource deficits translate into adverse patient outcomes. Prospective, mixed-methods studies are needed to test these causal pathways directly and to quantify the patient outcome burden attributable to modifiable communication barriers.

Barriers as epistemological and cultural challenges

Theme 1’s findings illuminate limitations of simplistic communication models and underscore the need for deeper engagement with how patients’ health beliefs constitute legitimate knowledge systems. When participants described patients attributing illness to supernatural causes or preferring traditional remedies, they were not describing irrational patients requiring correction. Rather, they documented encounters between competing knowledge systems, both internally coherent and socially validated within their respective communities.

This conceptualization draws on Bourdieu’s theory of cultural capital, wherein knowledge and beliefs represent legitimate social capital within specific fields [17, 26]. When nurses explained why antibiotics matter while patients believed prayer was the real cure, they were not conveying “truth” to ignorant patients but rather advocating for an alternative (albeit professionally dominant) knowledge system. Effective communication in these contexts requires nurses to: (1) recognize cultural health beliefs as epistemologically legitimate even if clinically divergent; (2) negotiate rather than impose biomedical frameworks; (3) seek integration points between patients’ existing beliefs and medical recommendations.

Implications for nursing practice are significant. Current nursing education emphasizes patient education as information-transmission; if nurses explain clearly enough, patients will understand and comply [27, 28]. Findings suggest this model is insufficient when patients hold fundamentally different health epistemologies. A more effective approach recognizes cultural humility, acknowledging that patients’ health beliefs have legitimacy, seeking understanding of their frameworks, and negotiating integration between medical recommendations and existing beliefs. This requires professional development beyond “cultural competence” (which can imply superficial awareness of cultural differences) toward deeper engagement with how cultural and religious frameworks shape health meaning-making [29, 30].

Evidence-based communication strategies and expertise development

Theme 2’s documentation of nurses’ sophisticated adaptive strategies, visual demonstrations, teach-back methods, family engagement, and simplified language aligns with evidence-based practices increasingly recognized as foundational to health literacy-responsive communication. Notably, most participants had not learned these approaches through formal education; they represented tacit knowledge developed through experiential learning. This finding illuminates Benner’s expertise development model by demonstrating how experienced nurses develop sophisticated adaptive strategies [18]. However, it simultaneously suggests that current professional development leaves this learning to chance [31].

The concerning corollary is that patients cared for by nurses without opportunity or inclination for self-directed learning may receive suboptimal education [32]. Equity concerns emerge: systematic approaches should ensure all nurses master evidence-based communication strategies, not only those with exceptional initiative or resources [33]. Nursing programs should systematically incorporate communication strategies and health literacy responsive approaches into the core curriculum, with simulation-based learning and clinical practice enabling deliberate practice of teach-back methods, visual aids, and family engagement before nurses enter practice [34].

Systemic obstacles and organizational responsibility

Theme 3’s findings, time pressures, inadequate resources, institutional ambivalence, and unclear policies, align with the literature on organizational constraints in healthcare. However, what distinguishes this study’s contribution is the documentation of how these systemic barriers create moral distress for conscientious nurses. Epstein and Hamric [35] define moral distress as distress arising from gaps between professional values and organizational constraints. Participants’ accounts reveal the depth of this moral distress. As one medical-surgical nurse stated: “It troubles me deeply when I know patients should receive thorough education but time and resources prevent it.” (P12, Medical-Surgical).

Rather than representing individual failure, this distress reflects system-level misalignment. Healthcare organizations simultaneously expect nurses to provide comprehensive patient education while staffing units inadequately and allocating minimal time for education within care assignments [36]. This structural contradiction creates moral distress that even highly committed nurses cannot resolve through individual effort.

Critically, the current approach places responsibility on individual nurses to compensate for systemic failures. Several participants reported creating informal educational materials, seeking self-directed learning on personal time, and mentoring colleagues despite a lack of institutional recognition or compensation. While this initiative reflects professional dedication, it is unsustainable and raises equity concerns. Relying on individual nurses’ unpaid labor to provide infrastructure that healthcare organizations should provide is neither ethical nor feasible as a scalable solution.

Professional resilience and burnout risk

Theme 4 captured heterogeneous nurse responses to systemic constraints. While many experienced frustration and moral distress, others demonstrated remarkable resilience, viewing communication challenges as professional growth opportunities. This heterogeneity suggests that systemic constraints do not operate deterministically; rather, individual responses vary based on factors this study only partially illuminates. The variability documented in Table 2 warrants explicit acknowledgment: nurses embedded in the same organisational context responded very differently to shared systemic pressures, with some sustaining professional initiative and communication innovation across decades while others described progressive emotional numbing. This variation suggests that individual professional values, team culture, and unit-level subculture function as genuine moderating factors—neither trivial nor decisive, but meaningfully shaping outcomes within system-level constraints. Future research should examine how unit-level professional culture and team dynamics interact with institutional resourcing to influence communication quality.

Literature on burnout in healthcare documents that sustained gaps between professional values and organizational constraints contribute to emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and diminished personal accomplishment [37]. Over time, recurrent moral distress erodes professional satisfaction. The findings suggest healthcare system design systematically creates conditions for moral distress, and organizations expect communication excellence while constraining resources necessary to enable it. In the absence of systemic change, individual resilience and professional commitment, however admirable, may be insufficient to prevent burnout among conscientious nurses.

Theoretical synthesis and implications for nursing knowledge

This study highlights how communication challenges encountered by nurses in acute care settings extend beyond patient health literacy levels alone, implicating organisational design, resource allocation, and nurses’ own need for targeted communication training. Nurses demonstrated advanced communication strategies and professional commitment, yet systemic constraints, time pressures, limited resources, unclear policies, and institutional ambivalence frequently hindered effective patient education. For nursing education, findings highlight the need to integrate diverse communication strategies and health literacy-responsive care across curricula as a foundational framework [38]. Simulation-based learning and explicit instruction in evidence-based strategies such as teach-back, visual aids, and family engagement should be central [39], alongside cultural humility and recognition of diverse health beliefs. Programs should also prepare nurses to recognize systemic barriers and advocate for organizational change, supporting professional commitment and mitigating moral distress. In clinical practice, healthcare organizations should standardize communication protocols, ensure access to visual and multilingual materials, provide professional interpretation services, and allocate dedicated time for patient education within staffing and care assignments [40]. At the organizational and policy level, patient education should be institutionalized as a core operational responsibility [41]. This includes clear policies, dedicated resources (including specialist roles), accountability mechanisms integrated into quality dashboards, and interdisciplinary health literacy committees involving nursing, medicine, education, and patient advocates [42].

Study strengths and contextual limitations

The study’s strengths include maximum variation sampling across specialties, experience levels, and educational backgrounds, which enhanced transferability. Rigorous qualitative analysis, multiple analysts, systematic coding comparisons, member checking with one-third of participants, and detailed audit trails supported credibility and dependability. The single-site design should be understood in terms of its purposive intent rather than as a limitation in the traditional sense: the study was not designed to produce generalisable findings but to develop an in-depth understanding of communication dynamics within a specific acute care context. Transferability, the extent to which the findings may resonate in comparable settings, is supported by the detailed contextual descriptions provided and the maximum variation sampling approach. Readers are encouraged to judge applicability to their own settings rather than assume direct generalisability. The principal analytical limitation is the exclusive reliance on nurses’ self-reported perspectives; patient experiences and perspectives were not captured, meaning that the effectiveness of the communication strategies described cannot be verified from the patient side. Future research incorporating patient perspectives, direct observational methods, and multi-site designs across institutions with varying resource profiles would substantially strengthen the evidence base. Potential volunteer selection bias is acknowledged; nurses who participated may have had stronger views or greater engagement with communication issues than those who did not.

Conclusions and recommendations

Effective communication in acute care settings with patients who have varying levels of health literacy is shaped by individual, interpersonal, and organisational factors, as reported from the perspectives of nurses in this study. Nurses in this study demonstrated professional skill and commitment, and drew on sophisticated self-developed communication strategies; yet they consistently reported that systemic constraints substantially limited what they could achieve. These findings suggest that equipping nurses with diverse communication strategies through structured training-alongside organisational support in the form of adequate staffing, accessible resources, and clear patient education policies, may improve communication quality in such settings. At the same time, the heterogeneity documented among participants cautions against overly deterministic interpretations: individual professional values, unit-level culture, and team dynamics function as genuine moderating factors within shared systemic constraints.

Future research should evaluate organisational interventions that support health literacy-responsive communication, examine how differences in staffing, resources, and policy frameworks affect communication outcomes, explore long-term impacts of moral distress on nurse retention and workplace satisfaction, and incorporate patient perspectives to validate communication effectiveness. Comparative multi-site studies across healthcare systems with varying resources and policy contexts are recommended to identify generalisable strategies. Research on patient outcomes is also warranted: evidence links limited health literacy to elevated mortality risk and higher rates of preventable hospitalisation and emergency service use, and the communication failures documented here, time-constrained explanations, reliance on family interpretation, and inconsistent verification of understanding, represent plausible mechanisms through which system-level deficits translate into adverse outcomes. Prospective studies are needed to test these pathways directly. Finally, many of the adaptive communication practices described by participants are likely embedded in nurses’ free-text clinical notes, where they remain invisible to standard quality metrics. Natural language processing and semantic mapping approaches applied to unstructured nursing documentation could extract and operationalise these practices at scale, enabling reproducible investigation of their frequency, variation across contexts, and association with patient outcomes, a methodological direction we recommend for future inquiry [43].
