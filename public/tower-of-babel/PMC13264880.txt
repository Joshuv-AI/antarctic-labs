Development and validation of the Good Limb Position Management Scale for Stroke Patients with Hemiplegia by Nurses: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Minmin Leng, Yan Dong, Bei Liu, Xinyue Zhang, Xiaoxu Ji, Fangfang Sun, Min Li, Lijuan Yang, Xia Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractBackgroundA good limb position (GLP) plays an important role in the rehabilitation process of patients who have had a stroke with hemiplegia. However, there remains a lack of effective assessment tools for clinical nurses to evaluate their GLP management practices.ObjectiveThis study aimed to develop and test the psychometric properties of the Good Limb Position Management Scale for Stroke Patients with Hemiplegia by Nurses (GLPMSSPHN).DesignA quantitative and cross-sectional design.Setting89 hospitals in 16 cities of China.ParticipantsA total of 516 participants completed the questionnaire and were finally used for the analyses.Main outcome measuresAn initial scale was developed based on the Capability, Opportunity and Motivation-Behaviour model combined with a comprehensive literature review, semi-structured interviews, Delphi expert consultations and a pilot test. A field survey was then performed using the initial scale to test the reliability and validity of the scale. Reliability analysis was conducted by calculating Cronbach’s α coefficients and test–retest reliability. The results of exploratory factor analysis and confirmatory factor analysis were used as the validity index to further verify the model structure of the scale and develop a formal scale.ResultsThe GLPMSSPHN was formulated with 4 dimensions and 35 items. Exploratory factor analysis extracted four factors, with a cumulative variance contribution rate of 81.842%, and confirmatory factor analysis indicated that the scale had good construct validity. The Cronbach’s α coefficient of the scale was 0.978, and the test–retest reliability was 0.863.ConclusionsThe GLPMSSPHN has ideal reliability and validity and provides a valid and reliable tool for clinical nurses to identify and assess the management level of GLP in patients who have had a stroke with hemiplegia.Keywords: Stroke, Nurses, REHABILITATION MEDICINE

Introduction

An analysis of data from the Global Burden of Disease study1 revealed that there were 12.2 million incident cases of stroke, 101 million prevalent cases of stroke, 143 million disability-adjusted life-years (DALYs) due to stroke, and 6.55 million deaths from stroke in 2019. According to the World Stroke Organization: Global Stroke Fact Sheet 2022,2 stroke remains the second-leading cause of death and the third-leading cause of death and disability combined (as expressed by DALYs) worldwide. The bulk of the global stroke burden (86.0% of deaths and 89.0% of DALYs) resides in lower-income and lower-middle-income countries.1 According to the China Stroke Prevention and Treatment Report (2021), stroke seriously endangers the physical and mental health of Chinese people and is the leading cause of death and disability among adults in China, characterised by a high incidence rate, high recurrence rate, high disability rate, high mortality rate and high economic burden.3 The latest data showed that there were 4.09 million incident cases of stroke, 26.34 million prevalent cases of stroke in 2021.4 Data from the ‘China Health and Wellness Statistical Yearbook 2023’ revealed that the number of patients who have had a stroke discharged from hospitals and the average medical expenses per person in China have gradually increased from 2010 to 2022. In 2022, the number of discharged patients with ischaemic stroke and haemorrhagic stroke in China increased by 2.93 times and 1.31 times respectively compared with 2010.5

As a common neurological disease, most patients who have had a stroke experience unilateral limb dysfunction, commonly known as hemiplegia. Hemiplegia severely affects the daily life of patients who have had a stroke and causes significant distress to their families. Good limb position (GLP), also known as anti-spasticity position, is a temporary position adopted to prevent or counteract spasticity posture.6 It plays an important role in the rehabilitation process of patients who have had a stroke with hemiplegia and has become the key to early rehabilitation and the highest priority in health economics evaluation.7 Studies8,10 have shown that the placement of patients who have had a stroke with hemiplegia in GLP can effectively prevent the occurrence of spasm of the affected side limb; improve neurological function, limb function and daily living ability; and significantly reduce complications such as foot drop, shoulder and hand syndrome, joint stiffness, joint muscle contracture, abnormal posture of hemiplegic limbs and pressure ulcers. Many domestic and foreign guidelines or expert consensuses611,14 also recommend placing GLPs for patients who have had a stroke with hemiplegia to promote functional recovery. However, there remains a lack of effective assessment tools for clinical nurses to evaluate their management of GLP in patients who have had a stroke with hemiplegia. Effective assessment tools are a prerequisite in clinical practice and are key to identifying influencing factors and implementing interventions.15 16 Therefore, it is necessary to develop an assessment tool for nurses to evaluate their management of GLP in patients who have had a stroke with hemiplegia.

Theoretical frameworks play an important role in the development of assessment tools. At present, several theoretical frameworks, such as the Knowledge-Attitude-Practice model17 18 and the Information-Motivation-Behavioural model,19 20 are widely used in the development of assessment tools. However, they focus only on individual knowledge and individual motivation, ignoring the external environment. The role of an individual’s own ability in behaviour change has not been fully explored and used to maximise the intervention effect. The Capability, Opportunity and Motivation-Behaviour (COM-B) model21 can compensate for the inadequacies in the existing theoretical models. In the COM-B model, capability is defined as the individual’s psychological and physical capacity to engage in the activity of concern, such as knowledge and skills; opportunity is defined as all the factors that lie outside the individual who make the behaviour possible or prompt it, such as medical and family support; motivation is defined as all brain processes that energise and direct behaviour, including habitual processes, emotional responding and analytical decision-making; and behaviour is defined as the change or occurrence of health-related behaviours.21 The COM-B model emphasises the factors and mechanisms influencing individual behaviour; focuses on the capabilities, opportunities and motivations that affect individual behaviours; and has been widely applied in the field of health behaviour promotion.22,24

The COM-B model represents a comprehensive method for exploring the relevant factors of individual behaviour to provide a suitable theoretical framework. Therefore, this study aimed to develop and psychometrically validate a scale—the Good Limb Position Management Scale for Stroke Patients with Hemiplegia by Nurses (GLPMSSPHN)—based on the COM-B model. We hypothesised that the developed scale would be a reliable and valid tool for systematically assessing nurses’ GLP management capabilities for patients who have had a stroke with hemiplegia.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a cross-sectional design to examine the psychometric properties of the new tool GLPMSSPHN. We followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology statement in reporting this study. The GLPMSSPHN was developed according to DeVellis’s scale development guidelines.25 This process consists of two main steps: (1) instrument development and (2) psychometric tests. These steps are detailed below.

Instrument development

Item generation

Guided by COM-B theory, the initial items for the GLPMSSPHN were derived from two main sources: (1) a comprehensive review of the literature related to GLP management for patients who have had a stroke with hemiplegia and (2) the results of a semistructured interview with 24 clinical nurses.

For the literature review, we comprehensively searched the PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, CINAHL, CNKI, Wanfang and SinoMed databases to identify relevant studies that were published up to 31 March 2024. The search terms consisted of three parts: “stroke/cerebral hemorrhage/cerebral infarction/cerebrovascular accident’, ‘hemiplegia/paralysis” and “rehabilitation/functional position/position management/care/nursing”. The key search terms were a combination of medical subject heading terms (MeSH) and entry terms. The detailed information about the search strategies of each database is available in online supplemental table S1. Studies were included if they met the following criteria: (1) the topic of research was related to the content of stroke rehabilitation; (2) the type of research performed in the article was not restricted and (3) written in Chinese or English. The exclusion criteria included (1) meeting abstracts, letters from editors or case reports and (2) studies for which the full text was not available.

For the semistructured interviews, to make the sample more representative, we adopted maximum difference sampling and selected 24 clinical nurses covering different departments (the neurology department, neurosurgery department, rehabilitation department and geriatric department), different ages, different positions and different genders. Semistructured interviews were conducted by two researchers with experience in qualitative interviews, and participants were introduced to the purpose, methodology, content and time required for this study before the start of the interviews. The major interview questions included “Are you familiar with GLP (such as the concept, placement timing, auxiliary materials, precautions during placement, placement methods for different positions, etc.)?”, “Do you think it is necessary to arrange GLPs for stroke patients with hemiplegia?”, “Do you place them in GLPs when caring for stroke patients with hemiplegia?” and “What factors do you think promote or hinder the placement of GLPs for stroke patients with hemiplegia in your daily work?”.

Delphi consultation

In the present study, the Delphi consultation aimed to identify whether the domains and items can be used to evaluate GLP management for patients who have had a stroke with hemiplegia. The Delphi consultation was implemented through four steps: designing the Delphi consultation questionnaire, selecting the experts for consultation, conducting the Delphi consultation and completing the Delphi consultation. (1) Designing the Delphi consultation questionnaire: Based on the literature review and semistructured interviews, the researcher designed the Delphi consultation questionnaire, which included four parts: (a) an introduction to the purpose and content of the scale; (b) an evaluation of the importance of the domains and items and open suggestions; (c) experts’ sociodemographic information and (d) experts’ judgement basis and familiarity with the scale content. (2) Selecting the experts for consultation: The research team selected 21 experts from nine cities including Beijing, Shanghai, Hangzhou, Zhengzhou, Changsha, Jinan, Heze, Weihai and Liaocheng. Experts who (1) were primarily engaged in stroke rehabilitation, scale development or cerebrovascular medicine research and (b) had a doctoral degree or more than 10 years of working experience were included in the Delphi consultation. (3) Conducting the Delphi consultation: The questionnaires were distributed to the experts via email. The experts rated each item’s importance via a 5-point Likert scale (1—very unimportant, 5—very important). Items with a mean importance rating of 3.5 and a coefficient of variation (CV) <0.25 were retained for further analysis.26 Additionally, the experts provided feedback on modifying, combining, adding or deleting items. (4) Completing the Delphi consultation: The Delphi consultation ended when the expert opinions were generally consistent.

Content validity test

The content validity index (CVI) of the scales was assessed through expert ratings, which included both the item-level CVI (I-CVI) and scale-level CVI (S-CVI). Generally, the number of experts involved in these evaluations ranges from 3 to 10.27 Nine clinical care specialists in the field of stroke rehabilitation evaluated the content of the scale on the CVI and rated it on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from ‘1-not relevant’ to ‘4-very relevant’. The I-CVI was calculated by calculating the ratio of the number of experts who gave the item a rating of 3 or 4 to the total number of experts,28 and the S-CVI was the average of each I-CVI.29 Items with a rating higher than the I-CVI of 0.78 were selected, and items with a rating lower than 0.78 were deleted or revised according to the experts’ opinions.

Pilot test

A pilot test was conducted on 30 clinical nurses in stroke-related departments to identify inappropriate questions by checking the GLPMSSPHN’s language expression for ease of understanding (‘how difficult or easy was it to understand the questionnaire as a whole’), appropriate item length and readability. The time taken to complete the questionnaire was also assessed.

Psychometric tests

Participants

In August 2024, convenience sampling was used to select clinical nurses from the neurology, neurosurgery, rehabilitation and geriatric departments in 89 hospitals in 16 cities in China. The inclusion criteria for the participants were as follows: (1) formal clinical nurses with nursing practice qualifications; (2) daily work involving the care of patients who have had a stroke with hemiplegia; (3) age ≥18 years and (4) provided informed consent to voluntarily participate in this study. Researchers provided clinical nurses with information about the study, including the study purpose, procedure and participant’s right to refuse and withdraw from the study at any time. Following the sample estimation principle, the sample size was at least five to ten times the number of independent items.30 31 The preliminary questionnaire included a total of 38 items. The calculated sample size range was 190–380. Considering a 20% rate of invalid data, the total number of participants required was estimated to be 238–475. Ultimately, a total of 519 participants completed the questionnaire and 516 eligible participants were ultimately included in the analyses.

Measurements

The assessment tools used in this study included the participant’s general information questionnaire and the GLPMSSPHN. The general information questionnaire was designed on the basis of a literature review and included age, sex, years in nursing, education level, marital status, role, type of contract, monthly income (CNY), night shift frequency, type of hospital and unit of assignment. GLP management for patients who have had a stroke with hemiplegia by nurses was measured by initial GLPMSSPHN. To enhance the accuracy of data collection and reduce potential bias, we excluded participants whose collected questionnaires contained logically contradictory.

Data analysis

The data collected through this study were analysed using SPSS V.26.0 and AMOS V.26.0 (IBM). The sociodemographic characteristics of the participants are presented by percentages, means and SD. The details of the validity and reliability testing methods are listed below.

Item analyses

Three methods of item analyses were employed: (1) The critical ratio method: The GLPMSSPHN scale scores were arranged in ascending order, with the first 27% defined as the high subgroup and the last 27% defined as the low subgroup. Then, an independent sample t test was performed on the data of the two groups, and items with t<3 or p>0.05 were deleted.32 (2) The corrected item-total correlations method: The representativeness of items was tested by calculating the correlation coefficients between the GLPMSSPHN scores for each item and the total score, and items with correlation coefficients (r) 0.4 or lower were deleted.32 (3) The Cronbach’s α coefficient method. If the Cronbach’s α coefficient becomes higher after deleting an item, it indicates that this item is not homogeneous with the scale and is considered for deletion.

Reliability test method

Internal consistency reliability: The internal consistency reliability coefficient (Cronbach’s α) was calculated to verify how consistently all the items in the scale measured the construct. A Cronbach’s α value above 0.7 is generally considered indicative of good reliability.33

Test–retest reliability: Test–retest reliability was evaluated at 2 week intervals in 30 participants. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was applied to indicate test–retest reliability. The ICC is considered poor at less than 0.40, fair at 0.4 to 0.6, good at 0.6 to 0.75 and excellent at 0.75 to 1.00.33

Construct validity

Both exploratory factor analysis (EFA) and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) were conducted to assess the construct validity of the scale. First, the total data (n=516) were randomly classified into dataset A (n1=258) and dataset B (n2=258). The EFA was conducted with dataset A and the CFA was conducted with dataset B.

EFA: Before EFA, the suitability of the data for factor analyses was assessed using the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) index and Bartlett’s sphericity test. A KMO value greater than 0.8 and a significance level of p<0.05 in the Bartlett’s sphericity test are generally considered indicative of suitable data for factor analyses.32 EFA was conducted to extract the factors by applying principal components analysis (PCA) and the orthogonal rotation (Varimax) method. Items were removed if they had factor loadings in the EFA of less than 0.4 or if they exhibited multiple factor loadings (simultaneous loadings on 2 or more factors of more than 0.4 and a commonality of less than 0.2).30 34

CFA: The factors extracted from the EFA were tested using CFA. Model parameters and fit indices for CFA were estimated using the maximum likelihood method. A χ2 fit statistic/df (χ2/df) value less than 3 and a root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) value less than 0.08 were considered indicative of good model fit. Tucker-Lewis index (TLI), comparative fit index (CFI), goodness-of-fit index (GFI), normed fit index (NFI) and incremental fit index (IFI) values greater than 0.8 indicate acceptable fit, whereas values greater than 0.9 indicate good fit.35 36

Convergent and discriminant validity

Convergent validity was applied to assess similar constructs and tested by examining the average variance extracted (AVE) and composite reliability (CR) for the domains. A scale demonstrating good convergent validity has an AVE value exceeding 0.5 and a CR value exceeding 0.6 for each factor.35 Discriminant validity refers to the ability of a scale to distinguish between different concepts based on their measured values. The square root of the AVE coefficient was adopted to assess the discriminant validity of the scale. A scale demonstrates good discriminant validity when the square root of the AVE coefficient is greater than the bivariate correlations between the domains.35

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of our research.

Results

Instrument development

Item generation

Based on the data extracted from the comprehensive literature review and semistructured interviews, the scale development team initially formed a pool of 45 items in 4 dimensions for the GLPMSSPHN. The scale was scored on a 5-point Likert scale, with scores ranging from 1 to 5.

Delphi consultation

Of the 45 items of the original scale, 10 items were modified, 3 items were deleted and 3 items were added after the first round of expert consultation. In addition, based on the experts’ opinions, 11 items were combined, of which 5 items related to GLPs of different postures (including the supine position, affected side position, healthy side position, bed sitting position, wheelchair sitting position) were combined into 1 item, and the other 6 items were combined into 3 items. The name of the scale was also modified according to expert opinions, changing the word ‘paralysis’ to ‘hemiplegia’. A 4-domain, 38-item GLPMSSPHN was formed and entered the second round of Delphi consultation. When the experts’ suggestions were combined, 6 items were modified, and no items were added or deleted. As a result, 4 domains and 38 items remained in the scale.

Content validity test

A total of nine experts evaluated the content validity of the scale. The S-CVI was 0.968, and the I-CVIs ranged from 0.889 to 1.000, indicating excellent content validity.

Pilot test

A total of 30 participants were investigated, and 30 questionnaires were recovered, with an effective recovery rate of 100%. The results of the pilot test showed that all 30 participants reported that the content of the GLPMSSPHN items was clear and easy to understand, that it took approximately 5 min to complete the scale, and that the overall content of the scale was complete. In the pilot test, no changes were made to the content of GLPMSSPHN. All the items in this scale adopted were positively scored. Likert’s 5-level scoring method was used for the capability dimension (1—not know at all, 5—know very well); opportunity dimension (1—very inconsistent, 5—very consistent); motivation dimension (1—strongly disagree, 5—strongly agree); and behaviour dimension (1—never, 5—always). The higher the scores of the capability, opportunity, motivation and behaviour dimensions are, the higher the level of GLP management is for patients who have had a stroke with hemiplegia.

Psychometric tests

Sociodemographic characteristics of the participants

Among the initially collected 519 questionnaires, 3 (0.58%) were excluded because the age, educational level and years of work provided were logically contradictory (eg, reporting an age of 26 years with education level of university and 10–14 years of nursing experience, which is impossible in the Chinese context). Thus, the final analytic sample consisted of 516 participants. The age ranged from 20 to 53 years, and the mean age was 34.56±7.47 years. Most of the participants were female (93.02%), married (74.22%), had a university educational level (85.85%), were staff nurses (83.33%), had a monthly income of CNY5000–CNY7499 (60.27%), were working in tertiary hospitals (78.88%) and had a unit of assignment to the neurology department (69.38%). Item analysis

The results of the critical ratio method revealed that the t values for the items ranged from 12.365 to 20.634, all of which were >3 and p<0.05, suggesting that all the items may contribute importantly to the scale’s overall measurement. The correlation coefficients (r) between the GLPMSSPHN scores for each item and the total score ranged from 0.424 to 0.830, all of which were >0.4. Additionally, after item-by-item deletion, Cronbach’s α did not significantly increase, indicating that each item contributes to the internal consistency of the entire scale. Based on the findings from the item analyses, no items were excluded. Details about item analysis can be found in the online supplemental table S2.

Internal consistency reliability: The total scale of the GLPMSSPHN demonstrated a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.978. The four domains exhibited Cronbach’s α coefficients ranging from 0.928 to 0.981, all of which were >0.7, indicating that the scale had excellent internal consistency reliability (table 2).

Convergent and discriminant validity

The results of the convergent validity analysis showed that the AVE values of the domains ranged from 0.655 to 0.890, and the CR values ranged from 0.919 to 0.980. The results of discriminant validity analysis revealed that the square root of the corresponding AVE ranged from 0.809 to 0.944, and the square roots of the AVE for the four domains were greater than those for the construct correlations (table 5). The above results indicated that the scale had good convergent and discriminant validity.

Discussion

Summary and interpretation of findings

The present study developed a comprehensive instrument to measure the management of GLP in patients who have had a stroke with hemiplegia by clinical nurses. The final version of the GLPMSSPHN is composed of four domains and 35 items, including a capability dimension (13 items), opportunity dimension (9 items), motivation dimension (6 items) and behaviour dimension (10 items). The instrument demonstrates strong internal consistency, with an overall Cronbach’s α value of 0.978. It explains a total variance of 81.842%, indicating its effectiveness in measuring the complexity of clinical nurses’ management of GLP in patients who have had a stroke with hemiplegia in clinical work environments. In addition, the instrument shows acceptable construct validity, convergent validity and discriminant validity.

This study applied the COM-B model to guide the development of the GLPMSSPHN. The COM-B model provides clear guidance for behaviour analysis,37 making it an ideal framework for developing scale dimensions in this study. Based on the characteristics of patients who have had a stroke with hemiplegia and the COM-B model, we conducted a literature review and semistructured interviews to further compile the initial item pool of the GLPMSSPHN to ensure that the scale is comprehensive, scientific and practical. For the literature review, we conducted a systematic search of relevant literature in the field of stroke rehabilitation and referred to authoritative guidelines and expert consensus on the management of patients with stroke,38 39 making the item content of the scale reliable. Moreover, through the analysis of the semi-structured interview data, the scale item pool was further supplemented and enriched with content that could not be extracted in the literature review; thus, the content of the whole scale became more comprehensive. Thereafter, 21 experts with professional practical experience and extensive theoretical research in the field of stroke were selected for two rounds of expert consultation, and 30 clinical nurses in stroke-related departments were selected for a small sample pilot test. The scale was revised repeatedly according to expert suggestions and small sample pilot test results to ensure the rationality and completeness of the scale. After the questionnaire survey, item analysis and reliability test, EFA and CFA of the scale items were performed successfully, resulting in four domains and 35 items, which further ensured the scientificity and reliability of the scale development.

The purpose of item analyses of scales is to apply statistical methods for objective screening of items.40 Many methods are available for screening scale items, and a single screening method may not be comprehensive. Therefore, a combination of several item screening methods is usually used to conduct a comprehensive item analysis of a scale. In this study, the critical ratio method, the corrected item-total correlations method, and Cronbach’s α coefficient method were used for item analyses, and scale items were screened in terms of three factors, including discriminability, representativeness and internal consistency.32 All the items met the indicators of the three screening methods, indicating good discriminability, representativeness and internal consistency of the scale items. The structure and characteristics identified in the GLPMSSPHN scale align with the multifaceted definition of GLP management in patients who have had a stroke with hemiplegia. Moreover, the study revealed significant relationships among the four elements of GLP management, with association coefficients ranging from 0.454 to 0.732. This finding is consistent with previous research that explored the interconnectedness of these elements.41 42

During the development process, the internal consistency reliabilities and factor loadings of the assumed domains demonstrated satisfactory results. We carried out an internal consistency analysis and test–retest reliability analysis to evaluate the reliability of the scale. The Cronbach’s α coefficient of the total scale was 0.978, and the Cronbach’s α coefficients of the four domains ranged from 0.928 to 0.981, indicating that the scale had excellent internal consistency reliability. The test–retest reliability coefficient of the scale at an interval of 2 weeks was 0.863, indicating that it has good stability over time. The validity of a scale refers to whether it meets the intended design objectives and indicators.41 The content validity was identified by inviting authoritative experts in the field of stroke to review the item pool. The results show that the content validity index was 0.968, which is higher than the content validity reported by similar studies.43 44

In the EFA, Items O2, O3 and O4 were removed because they exhibited multiple factor loadings. The remaining items were then subjected to the second EFA. The results of the second EFA showed that the factor loadings of all the remaining 35 items on the scale were all≥0.4 without multiple factor loadings. The four common factors explained 81.842% of the total variation. In the CFA, a high correlation was noted between the residuals of Items C1 and C3, C11 and C12, and B5 and B6. Items C1 and C3 focus on the basic knowledge of GLP. Items C11 and C12 focus on posture state of GLP. Items B5 and B6 focus on precautions during the placement of GLP, interactions between them had a high correlation. By adding a covariance link between the residuals of these items, the TLI, CFI, GFI, NFI, and IFI were 0.903, 0.910, 0.726, 0.872, and 0.911, respectively, to further verify the construct validity of this study. To further validate the scale’s structure and model fits, future research should involve testing the GLPMSSPHN among culturally diverse clinical nurses.

The development and validation of the GLPMSSPHN fills the gap in assessment tools for GLP management for patients who have had a stroke with hemiplegia, offering an effective way to evaluate nurses’ GLP management capabilities. Unlike existing tools45 46 that often focus solely on theoretical knowledge or skills and, crucially, have not undergone rigorous psychometric testing, the GLPMSSPHN is not only theoretically grounded in the COM-B model but has also been subjected to strict and standardised validation of its reliability and validity. This framework enables a comprehensive, multidimensional assessment encompassing not only nurses’ capability (knowledge and skills) but also their opportunity (environmental and resource factors) and motivation (beliefs and intentions), which collectively drive clinical behaviour. This holistic approach represents a significant innovation over previous checklists or knowledge tests, as it captures the complex interplay of factors that ultimately determine practice quality. Consequently, the GLPMSSPHN provides a more practical tool for identifying specific barriers—whether educational, systemic or attitudinal—to optimal GLP management in clinical settings.

Strengths and limitations

Notably, this study possesses several strengths. First, a rigorous scale adaptation process was adopted in this study. In step 1, the COM-B model served as the framework for generating an initial pool of scale items through a comprehensive literature review and semistructured interviews. Two rounds of Delphi expert consultation were subsequently conducted to refine and modify the scale items. The content validity of the scale was subsequently evaluated, and its acceptability was assessed through a pilot test. In step 2, we conducted a large sample survey to perform psychometric tests on the scale. Second, our statistical analysis is relatively comprehensive, with not only EFA but also CFA to make the results more convincing.

This study also has several limitations. First, the survey participants were primarily from hospitals at various levels in Shandong Province and did not cover hospitals from different regions and economic levels across the country. This may affect the representativeness and generalisability of the sample. Further validation of the applicability of this assessment tool nationwide is needed in the future. Second, the data collected from clinical nurses relied on self-report measures, which can introduce social desirability bias or response bias. However, to mitigate this limitation, we included data from nurse managers and conducted tests using data from clinical nurses. Third, although the GLPMSSPHN has good reliability and validity, further validation in different cultural contexts is necessary. The current research focused on clinical nurses with professional qualifications in China. Future research should aim to conduct cross-cultural validation to ensure the scale’s robustness across diverse cultural contexts. Last, in the final model, the value of the GFI (0.726) on the scale did not meet the best fit criteria of the model. Therefore, further research on this scale is needed to reconfirm the construct validity of the overall scale. Given the above limitations of this study, caution should be exercised when interpreting the results and drawing conclusions.

Implications for nursing practice

The development and validation of the GLPMSSPHN hold important implications for nursing management and practice. The scale not only serves as an assessment tool but also provides actionable pathways for improving nursing quality. Below are specific suggestions for its clinical application. First, clinical nurses can apply the GLPMSSPHN to self-evaluate their management level of GLP in patients who have had a stroke with hemiplegia. Based on the scored results, they can identify specific gaps, such as the basic knowledge of GLP, the timing of GLP placement or the standard placement methods, and formulate personalised learning plans. Second, the GLPMSSPHN offers nurse educators and managers a valuable tool to analyse the obstacles and promoting factors of GLP placement, providing a reference for targeted training and personalised management measures in the future. Third, nurse educators and managers can incorporate the GLPMSSPHN into periodic competency assessments. Training effectiveness can then be re-evaluated using the same scale, enabling a feedback loop for continuous improvement. Last, the GLPMSSPHN can also assist nurse managers in identifying areas for improvement in their management capabilities and provide a supportive work environment for their clinical nurses that is conducive to nurse engagement and the rehabilitation of patients who have had a stroke with hemiplegia. In summary, the GLPMSSPHN offers a structured, evidence-based approach not only to assess but also to systematically enhance nursing practice in GLP management. By translating scale outcomes into concrete actions—personalised learning, targeted training, continuous quality improvement and managerial support—this tool can contribute directly to improving both nurse competency and patient rehabilitation outcomes.

Conclusions

The GLPMSSPHN has been developed and validated. This scale comprises 4 domains (capability dimension, opportunity dimension, motivation dimension and behaviour dimension) with a total of 35 items. The GLPMSSPHN is scored on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from a minimum of 35 to a maximum of 175, with higher scores indicating higher management levels of GLP in patients who have had a stroke with hemiplegia. The GLPMSSPHN demonstrates robust psychometric properties, including good internal consistency and construct validity; it can serve as an assessment tool for clinical nurses, providing a reliable measure of their ability to manage GLP in patients who have had a stroke with hemiplegia. Further research on the psychometric properties of the GLPMSSPHN among clinical nurses from diverse cultural backgrounds, and on its application in investigating the management level of GLPs in patients who have had a stroke with hemiplegia by nurses, will be of great value.
