Predictors of survival after penetrating cardiac trauma: injury patterns and presentation factors
Authors: Michelle Mendiola Pla, Jalia Anderson, Eliza Mundenmaney, Jeremy Miller, Holly Gui, Crystal Valdez, Grace Chang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPenetrating cardiac trauma is associated with high mortality. Factors influencing survival remain incompletely characterized. This study aimed to identify injury patterns and presentation characteristics associated with survival after penetrating cardiac trauma at an urban, level 1 trauma center.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study of patients with penetrating cardiac trauma identified from the hospital trauma registry between 2017 and 2025. Patients with documented penetrating cardiac injury were included. Demographic, physiological, and injury characteristics were compared between survivors and non-survivors using the Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables and Pearson’s χ2 test or Fisher’s test for categorical variables. Univariate Firth’s logistic regression was used to calculate ORs with 95% CIs.ResultsAmong 18 041 trauma activations, 57 patients with penetrating cardiac injury were identified. Overall survival was 25%. Gunshot wound (GSW) was the most common mechanism of injury (67%) and was associated with a higher risk of mortality compared with stab wound injuries (SWIs) (OR 3.70, 95% CI 1.10 to 12.1). Multichamber cardiac injury (OR 10.9, 95% CI 1.26 to 1439), thoracic vascular injury (OR 12.3, 95% CI 1.43 to 1615), and concurrent abdominal injury (OR 4.40, 95% CI 1.12 to 24.7) were associated with death. Conversely, isolated cardiac injury (OR 0.21, 95% CI 0.06 to 0.75), spontaneous respirations (OR 0.06, 95% CI 0.01 to 0.24), and signs of life on presentation (OR 0.01, 95% CI 0 to 0.10) were predicted survival.ConclusionPenetrating cardiac trauma carries a high mortality rate. Survival was associated with SWI, preserved physiologic status on presentation, and injuries isolated to the heart, whereas GSW and concurrent thoracic vascular or abdominal injuries were associated with death. Recognition of these factors may assist clinicians in early triage and resuscitative decision-making.Level of evidence: level III, prognostic study.Keywords: penetrating trauma, Cardiac, thoracic, critical care

Background

Penetrating cardiac trauma is a rare but highly lethal injury associated with a substantial mortality rate.1,4 Many patients die at the scene due to hemorrhage or cardiac tamponade. Survival often depends on rapid recognition and prompt intervention (figure 1).4 Patients who survive long enough to receive medical care often require immediate resuscitative measures during prehospital transport.4 5 On arrival to the emergency department (ED), patients frequently present with profound hemodynamic instability or cardiac arrest requiring rapid clinical decision-making. Emergency procedures, such as resuscitative thoracotomy, may be necessary to restore circulation and repair cardiac injuries.5,11

Timeline of critical timepoints after penetrating cardiac trauma. (A) At the time of injury, the majority of patients die at the scene. (B) Those who survive are able to be evaluated and transported by EMS to the hospital; during this time, patients in hemorrhagic shock are able to be stabilized with intravenous fluid intervention (ie, crystalloids, blood products) or undergo CPR if they progress to cardiac arrest. (C) Once patients arrive at the ER, more comprehensive assessments and interventions can be provided, including initiation of massive transfusion protocols and emergency left anterolateral thoracotomy. (D) Those who survive past the ER are then able to undergo more definitive stabilization and recovery, whether it is for further surgical interventions in the OR or further resuscitation in the ICU. ICU, intensive care unit. Abbreviations: CPR - cardiopulmonary resuscitation, IV - intravenous, MTP - massive transfusion protocol, OR - operating room, ER - emergency room, EMS - emergency medical services.

Despite advances in trauma systems and surgical management, the factors associated with survival after penetrating cardiac trauma remain incompletely characterized. Prior studies have identified several variables that may influence outcomes, including mechanism of injury, physiological status at presentation, and injury severity.1 4 10 12 However, findings have been inconsistent regarding the influence of specific injury patterns, such as cardiac chamber involvement, associated thoracic injuries, or abdominal injuries.

The purpose of this study was to characterize injury patterns and presentation factors associated with survival in patients with penetrating cardiac trauma treated at an urban Level 1 trauma center. We compared patients who survived with those who died, focusing on demographics, mechanism of injury, physiological status on presentation, and associated injury patterns. We hypothesized that specific injury characteristics and presentation variables would differ between survivors and non-survivors and could help identify factors associated with improved survival in penetrating cardiac trauma.

Methods

Study design

Approval for this project was provided by the Sinai Chicago Institutional Review Board under study number 24–31. Given the retrospective nature of this study and no need for identifiers, individual patient informed consent was waived. This study screened all trauma activation patients with a penetrating cardiac injury who presented to Mount Sinai Hospital, an urban, level 1 trauma center in Chicago, Illinois, for inclusion. The timeframe studied was from January 2017 to May 2025. We excluded trauma patients younger than 17 years old or with concurrent penetrating brain injury. The Sinai Chicago electronic medical record systems were used to screen patients for inclusion using International Classification of Diseases, 10th edition codes S26 and their subcategories and Current Procedural Terminology codes 33 300 and 33 305. The primary outcome was death. Secondary outcomes included demographics such as age, sex, and race; mechanism of injury; initial vital signs; cardiac arrest on presentation to the ED (absence of a palpable pulse); injury pattern; Injury Severity Score (ISS); Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score; hospital and intensive care unit (ICU) length of stay (LOS); and treatment interventions. Physiological variables were obtained from the time of patient presentation to the ED.

Clinical management and interventions

Fellowship-trained attending trauma surgeons actively participated in all of the management decisions and surgical interventions. Patients were resuscitated using treatment protocols outlined in the Advanced Trauma Life Support Manual of the American College of Surgeons. Patients in cardiac arrest underwent immediate endotracheal intubation and infusion of crystalloid solutions and blood products in the setting of hemodynamic instability and evidence of massive hemorrhage. Emergency left anterolateral thoracotomy was performed in the ED on all patients who presented in cardiac arrest. The decision to proceed to the operating room was at the discretion of the attending trauma surgeon.

Statistical analysis

Patient characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics and reported as median with IQR or as a percentage (n (%)). Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare continuous, non-parametric variables. Categorical variables were analyzed with either Pearson’s χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test for variables with expected cell counts <5. Because of the small sample size and the presence of rare events, Firth’s penalized logistic regression was used to reduce small sample bias in the estimation of ORs with 95% CIs. Results were considered significant when p value <0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using R V.4.3.1 (Vienna, Austria).

Results

Overall demographics of patients

A total of 18 041 trauma activations were screened, of which 57 qualifying penetrating cardiac injuries were identified. Demographics and presentation characteristics are shown in table 1. Median age was 26 (21 to 33) years. The majority of patients were male (51 (89%)) and African American (38 (69%)). The most common mechanism of injury was gunshot wound (GSW) (38 (67%)) over stab wound injury (SWI) (19 (33%)). Median transport time was 13 (9.0 to 15.5) min. The majority of patients presented with an ISS ≥30 (40 (73%)) and a GCS ≤8 (41 (72%)). In concordance with these clinical scores, the median value for the mean arterial pressure on presentation was 0 (0 to 65) mm Hg and the median heart rate was 0 (0 to 120) beats per minute. Most patients in this cohort presented to the hospital in cardiac arrest (43 (75%)). A minority of patients presented with signs of life (21 (38%)), spontaneous respirations (16 (30%)), or with pulseless electrical activity (PEA) (16 (29%)).

Comparison of demographics and presentation characteristics by survival

All results showing the comparison of demographics and presentation characteristics between those who survived versus those who died are shown in table 1. There were 14 (25%) survivors and 43 (75%) who died in this cohort. There were no statistically significant differences between the two groups in age, sex, or race. There was a significantly higher rate of mortality associated with sustaining a GSW (32 (74%)) than an SWI (11 (26%)) (p=0.011). Transport time was not significantly different between the two groups; however, these data were missing for approximately half of the cohort. ISS <30 (11 (92%) vs 1 (8.3%), p<0.001) and GCS >8 (10 (71%) vs 4 (29%), p<0.001) were significantly associated with survival. Additionally, having signs of life (12 (100%) vs 9 (21%), p<0.001) and spontaneous respirations on presentation (9 (82%) vs 7 (17%), p<0.001) were associated with survival. Conversely, presentation with PEA (16 (38%) vs 0, p=0.005) or in cardiac arrest (42 (98%) vs 1 (7.1%), p<0.001) was significantly associated with death.

Comparison of injury characteristics by survival

Injury characteristics were compared between those who survived versus those who died. The results are shown in table 2. The rate of concurrent abdominal injury was significantly higher in the group that died (21 (50%)) than in the group that survived (2 (14%)) (p=0.019). Injury to any specific heart chamber (right atrium, left atrium, right ventricle (RV), left ventricle (LV)) was also not significantly different between those who survived versus those who died. In the entire cohort, there was an equal incidence of LV and RV injury, and these were the most commonly injured areas of the heart. Concurrent injury to any of these specific thoracic blood vessels (aorta, superior vena cava, or inferior vena cava) was not significantly different between the two groups. However, the rate of injury to any of these vessels was significantly higher in the group that died than in those who survived (0 vs 12 (30%), p=0.024). Involvement of multiple heart chambers was not significantly different between the two groups. Injury presentation isolated to only the heart was significantly greater in those who survived than in those who died (8 (53%) vs 6 (15%), p=0.006). To analyze if variables were indicative of higher impact injury (isolated cardiac injury, multichamber injury, abdominal injury, thoracic vascular injury), we compared the distribution of these injuries by SWI versus GSW (online supplemental table 1). As expected, isolated cardiac injury was significantly more common among SWI patients (9 (47%) vs 5 (14%), p=0.009). However, only concurrent abdominal injury was significantly more common among GSW patients (3 (17%) vs 20 (53%), p=0.011). Sustaining multichamber cardiac injury and thoracic vascular injury was not significantly different between those who had SWI versus GSW.

Univariate Firth’s logistic regression analysis of survival after penetrating cardiac trauma

Results of the univariate analysis for survival performed on select variables are presented in table 3 and summarized in the Forest plot in figure 2. GSW significantly predicted death over SWI (OR 3.70, 95% CI 1.10 to 13.1, p=0.034). ISS ≥30 on presentation was also significantly associated with death (OR 67.3, 95% CI 12.0 to 741, p<0.001). Having PEA (OR 18.1, 95% CI 2.14 to 2364, p=0.004) or cardiac arrest (OR 255, 95% CI 33.7 to 4350, p<0.001) on initial assessment significantly predicted death. Conversely, presenting with a GCS >8 was significantly associated with survival (OR 0.07, 95% CI 0.02 to 0.28, p<0.001). In concordance, presenting with signs of life (OR 0.01, 95% CI 0 to 0.10, p<0.001) or with spontaneous respirations (OR 0.06, 95% CI 0.01 to 0.24, p<0.001) was significantly associated with survival.

Univariate analysis of injury characteristics demonstrated several significant patterns. Involvement of any one specific heart chamber or major thoracic blood vessel was not significantly associated with either survival or death. Yet multichamber involvement (OR 10.9, 95% CI 1.26 to 1439, p=0.026) or injury to any thoracic vessel (OR 12.3, 95% CI 1.43 to 1615, p=0.017) were significantly associated with death. Interestingly, concurrent injury to the abdomen was also found to be significantly associated with death (OR 4.40, 95% CI 1.12 to 24.7, p=0.033). The only injury characteristic that was significantly associated with survival was if the injury was isolated to only the heart (OR 0.21, 95% CI 0.06 to 0.75, p=0.017).

Management and outcomes of patients with penetrating cardiac trauma

Interventions and hospitalization outcomes are presented in table 4. On presentation to the ER, the majority of patients underwent an emergency thoracotomy (35 (61%)). Notably, none of the patients who survived their injury underwent an emergency thoracotomy; meanwhile, most who died had received the procedure (35 (81%)) (p<0.001). Also on presentation to the ER, massive transfusion protocol (MTP) was activated for 23 (41%) patients. There was no significant difference in rates of MTP activation between those who survived and died (5 (36%) vs 18 (43%), p=0.638). There were also similar rates of thoracotomy performed in the operating room between the two groups (p=0.240). However, there was a significant difference in the rate of sternotomy performed in the operating room, such that most people who went on to survive had undergone a sternotomy (10 (71%)) compared with those who died (2 (4.8%)) (p<0.001). This moreover reflects the stability of the patient to undergo sternotomy than a sternotomy conferring a survival advantage to individuals. There were similar rates of concurrent laparotomy performed between the two groups (p>0.999).

Although only 21 (37%) of patients were admitted to the ICU, all survivors were ultimately admitted to the ICU compared with only a small portion of patient who died surviving long enough to be admitted to the ICU (14 (100%) vs 7 (16%), p<0.001). Survivors had a median ICU LOS of 3.5 (2.0 to 11.0) days; in contrast, those who died did not even reach a median of 1 day ICU LOS (p<0.001). For overall hospital LOS, survivors had a median LOS of 8.5 (6.0 to 13.0) days whereas those who died had a median LOS of 1.0 (1.0 to 1.0) (p<0.001). This indicates that most of the individuals who died did so soon after presentation and not late after presentation. It is important to note that most patients in this cohort did not require long-term follow-up with trauma or cardiology past 1 month after their presentation.

Discussion

Penetrating cardiac trauma remains a highly lethal injury with mortality rates exceeding 70% in many studies.4 10 13 Here, we observed an overall mortality rate of 75%. Most patients presented with severe physiologic compromise, including high ISS, low GCS scores, or in cardiac arrest, reflecting the extreme acuity of this injury pattern. As observed in similar studies, these markers of physiologic instability were strongly associated with mortality.4 12

Mechanism of injury was an important determinant of outcome in our cohort. GSWs were the most common mechanism and were associated with significantly higher mortality rates than SWIs. This finding is consistent with prior studies and likely reflects the greater tissue destruction caused by ballistic injuries and likelihood of the injury to involve multiple chambers.1 4 10 12 Bullets can cause extensive damage to tissues along the bullet trajectory.6 9 14 In contrast, knife-inflicted injuries typically cause more localized injury which confers lower impact injuries along the wound trajectory.

We also evaluated whether specific cardiac injury patterns predicted survival. Involvement of individual cardiac chambers was not significantly associated with death in our cohort, consistent with findings reported by Asensio et al12; although, other studies have reported chamber-specific differences in survival.2 4 The RV and LV were the most frequently injured structures, reflecting their size and positioning in the chest. Other studies have identified the RV as the most frequently affected chamber due to its anterior positioning in the chest.1 6 9 10 Involvement of multiple cardiac chambers was significantly associated with death in concordance with prior studies.10 13 This was likely due to the increased difficulty of achieving rapid hemorrhage control and extensive compromise of myocardial tissue.

Associated injuries also influenced outcomes. Again, although concurrent injury to any specific thoracic vessel was not associated with mortality, having concurrent injury to any thoracic vessel was significantly associated with mortality. Kim et al observed that there was no statistically significant difference in cardiac injury for a GSW contained within the cardiac box (sternal notch, xiphoid process, nipples) versus outside of the box.14 However, GSW within the cardiac box had a significantly higher burden of mortality, which they attributed to a higher likelihood of causing damage to neighboring vessels in the chest. Concurrent involvement of any major thoracic vessel aggravates hemorrhagic blood loss and subsequent rapid hemodynamic deterioration.

We analyzed whether injury involving only the heart was associated with a higher survival rate and found that there was a significantly higher association with survival. This was similarly reported in Barbero et al.6 This is possibly because surgical repair can be performed more quickly when additional injuries are not present. We also looked at whether there was a significant difference in survival if a patient had concurrent penetrating abdominal injuries. There was a statistically significant higher incidence rate of concurrent abdominal injury in those who died. Literature on combined penetrating cardiac and abdominal trauma is limited. A small retrospective study involving 25 patients suggested that these combined injuries carry higher mortality due to massive hemorrhage and greater difficulty in managing multiple bleeding sources simultaneously.15

Physiological status on presentation was one of the strongest predictors of survival. Patients who presented with signs of life, spontaneous respirations, and higher GCS scores were significantly more likely to survive. In contrast, presentation with PEA or in cardiac arrest was strongly associated with death. These findings highlight the importance of early injury identification and rapid transport. Physiological condition on arrival to the hospital could potentially be used to analyze whether aggressive resuscitative interventions are likely to be beneficial versus futile. A notable finding in this cohort is that all 35 patients who underwent an emergency thoracotomy ultimately died. Although this 0% survival rate invites scrutiny of whether this procedure should be continued, this outcome should be interpreted in the context of our cohort demographics. Patients who underwent emergency thoracotomy were experiencing cardiac arrest on presentation, which in and of itself was a strong predictor of death in this cohort. This does not necessarily indicate that the procedure itself is futile, but rather is reflective of selection bias in that it was performed as a salvage maneuver for patients presenting in extreme physiological extremis. The findings of this study support that the chances of survival are exceedingly rare after an emergency thoracotomy for penetrating cardiac trauma when a patient progresses to pulselessness prior to presentation. These findings further validate the experience reported in Seamon et al in the Eastern Association for the Surgery of Trauma Emergency Practice Management Guidelines for Emergency Department Thoracotomy.16 Altogether, the findings identified from this cohort suggest that both injury pattern and physiological presentation play important roles in determining survival after penetrating cardiac trauma. Recognizing these factors may assist trauma surgeons and emergency medicine doctors in rapidly identifying patients most likely to benefit from aggressive resuscitative and surgical interventions.

Limitations

There are several limitations of this study to acknowledge. Despite screening over 18 000 trauma activations covering a span of 8 years, this study only identified 57 qualifying patients. As such, it is not sufficiently powered to detect all the differences that may exist between those who lived and those who died from penetrating cardiac trauma or to robustly assess for multivariable interactions. Another limitation is that this cohort did not have extensive data surrounding medical history given that a history was not able to be obtained from the majority of patients. There was also not extensive long-term follow-up data available as most patients did not present for follow-up after being discharged from the hospital. Apart from more studies assessing mortality risk from presentation variables, studies dedicated to the long-term follow-up of patients who survive penetrating cardiac trauma are needed to characterize the most common complications after these injuries and propose screening strategies. The retrospective, single-center nature of this study carries inherent biases that cannot be sufficiently controlled for; as such, the results presented here may not be generalizable to other trauma centers, particularly in the non-urban setting. Lastly, because many patients with penetrating cardiac trauma die before reaching the hospital, this study also carries an inherent survival bias of patients who survived long enough to present to an ED. A future direction for this study would be to perform a prospective, multicenter study to overcome the limitations described.

Conclusion

Penetrating cardiac trauma remains associated with very high mortality. In this cohort, survival was more likely among patients with SWI, preserved physiological status on presentation, and injury isolated to the heart. Conversely, GSW, multichamber cardiac involvement, and concurrent thoracic vascular or abdominal injury predicted a higher risk of death. These findings highlight the importance of both injury pattern and physiological status on presentation in determining outcomes and may help guide early triage and resuscitative decision-making in patients with penetrating cardiac trauma. A future direction for this study would be to expand the cohort to include multiple trauma centers and to collect data in a prospective manner. Following up this study with a prospective design will also allow for deliberate long-term monitoring of patients who survive after penetrating cardiac trauma to be able to characterize the types of complications associated with these injuries.
