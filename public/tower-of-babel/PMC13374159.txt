Small extracellular vesicle-mediated transfer of HPV16 transcripts from Ca Ski cells increases proliferation of HPV-negative recipient cancer cells
Authors: Sergen Arslan, Alexandre Barbaud, Jason Bernauer, Laure Avoscan, Marion Tissot, Jean-Luc Prétet, Jessica Gobbo, Carmen Garrido, Isabelle Lascombe, Sylvie Fauconnet
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTumor-produced extracellular vesicles (EVs) are key players in cancer progression by transferring their bioactive cargo (nucleic acids, lipids, proteins) to target cells leading to increase their proliferation, migration and invasive properties. EVs are involved in virus-associated carcinogenesis. Cervical cancer is due to a persistent infection with a high-risk human papillomavirus (HPV) whose integration into the cellular genome causes the continuous and deregulated expression of viral oncoproteins E6/E7, responsible for the carcinogenesis process. This study aimed to isolate EVs of HPV-infected cells, to explore their viral material content, to determine whether viral cargo can impact the proliferative status of HPV-lacking recipient cells.MethodsEVs were isolated from HPV16-harboring Ca Ski cells by ultracentrifugation or a kit. Their metrological, microscopic and biochemical characterization was performed by NTA for size, TEM for morphology and western blotting for EV markers (CD9, CD63, CD81, ALIX, TSG101, Flotillin-1, Syntenin). Viral material cargo (HPV16 DNA; e6/e7, e6*I, e6^e7 early transcripts; E6/E7 oncoproteins) was analyzed by PCR/RTqPCR and immunoblotting. Confocal microscopy was used to observe the internalization of fluorescent EVs deposited over HPV-negative bladder cancer T24 cells. The effect of Ca Ski EVs on the proliferation of T24 cells was assessed by IncuCyte and CCK-8 assay. The involvement of early transcripts in proliferation was validated by siRNA experiments.ResultsA sEV-enriched fraction was obtained from Ca Ski cell supernatants. The unspliced e6/e7 transcripts and the spliced variants e6*I /e6^e7 were detected inside EVs but HPV16 DNA or E6/E7 proteins were not detected. After 24 h-incubation, EVs were internalized by T24 cells and led to an increase of their proliferation. This biological effect was partially due to e6/e7 transcripts loaded in EVs.ConclusionsAfter a complete characterization of Ca Ski sEVs (NTA, microscopy, PCR, western-blotting), our data show for the first time, a functional transfer of HPV16 early transcripts via EVs driving the proliferation of HPV-negative target cells. EV-loaded HPV16 e6/e7 mRNA could be used as early biomarkers before the disease sets in and to predict the evolution of cervical precancerous lesions towards cancer and facilitate patient monitoring through a simple liquid biopsy.Keywords: Cervical cancer, Small extracellular vesicles, Human PapillomaVirus 16, HPV16 early transcripts, Cell proliferation

Background

Intercellular communication is ensured by direct contact between cells, secreted molecules, or the release of extracellular vesicles (EVs). All cells, healthy or pathological, produce EVs which are particles surrounded by a membrane organized in a lipid bilayer with the capacity to carry bioactive molecules such as nucleic acids (DNA, mRNA, miRNA, lncRNA), lipids, membrane and soluble proteins. EVs are found in biological fluids (blood, urine, saliva, sperm, breastmilk.) and are involved in many physiological and pathological processes since they deliver their contents to target cells for altering their phenotype and behavior. The classification of EVs depends on their size, density, biochemical composition, and biogenesis mechanisms [1]. Thus, three categories of EVs have been first defined: exosomes (30–150 nm), microvesicles (ectosomes, microparticles) (100–1000 nm), and apoptotic bodies (1 to 5 μm). Exosomes are of endosomal origin. They are produced after the fusion of intraluminal vesicle-containing multivesicular bodies with the plasma membrane. Microvesicles are released by the outward budding of the plasma membrane. Apoptotic bodies originate from cells undergoing programmed cell death. However, given the overlapping size between exosomes and small microvesicles (40–100 nm), their separation is defying [2]. That is why ISEV (International Society of Extracellular Vesicles) recommended distinguishing small EVs < 200 nm in diameter from other EV subtypes > 200 nm if the biogenesis origin is not demonstrated [1]. Transmembrane proteins, such as the members of the tetraspanin family, CD9, CD63 and CD81, are commonly used as EV biomarkers. Other membrane bound and cytosolic proteins are incorporated in sEVs: ESCRT (endosomal sorting complex required for transport), ALIX (ALG-2 interacting protein-X), TSG101 (Tumor susceptibility gene 101), integrins, HSP (heat shock protein), flotillins.

Tumor-produced EVs play a major role in cancer progression by transferring their bioactive cargoes to increase proliferation, migration, and invasive capacities of target tumor cells close to secretory tumor cells. They also modify the behavior of non-tumor cells in the tumor microenvironment in promoting angiogenesis, leading to immunosuppression of the antitumor response, and contributing to the formation of pre-metastatic niches [3]. Several studies indicated that EVs are involved in virus-associated carcinogenesis. Viral components such as virions, viral proteins and viral nucleic acids are incorporated into EVs [4]. Consequently, oncogenic viruses can install a microenvironment conducive to tumor development.

Mucosa-associated high-risk HPV (Human Papillomavirus) are directly linked to different cancers such as cervical cancer [5]. This cancer is due to a sexually transmitted persistent HPV infection and is the fourth most common cancer in women with 661,021 new cases and 348,189 deaths worldwide in 2022 [6]. Some cases of high stage cervical cancer are associated with invasion of the bladder. HPV genotypes 16 and 18 are the most prevalent but as specified by the International Agency for Research on Cancer, HPV16 is the most carcinogenic [7] and is responsible for 60.5% cervical cancer cases [8]. In 90% of cases, the viral infection is eliminated by the host’s immune system and benign lesions develop into pre-cancerous and cancerous lesions in persistent infection [9].

HPVs are small, non-enveloped, double-stranded, circular DNA viruses comprising 8 genes under the control of two promoters. HPV genome is structured into three different regions: the early region, encoding non-structural proteins such as E6 and E7, essential for viral replication and transcription, the late region encoding L1 and L2 capsid proteins and a long control region containing the origin of replication as well as regulatory sequences for cellular and viral transcription factors [9, 10]. HPV E6 and E7 oncoproteins are encoded by a bicistronic mRNA subjected to a complex alternative splicing producing splice variants such as e6*I, and e6^e7 in addition to the unspliced transcripts e6 and e7. The unspliced transcript encodes the E6 and E7 proteins, the e6*I transcript potentially encodes E6*I (truncated protein identical to the first 41 residues of E6) and E7, and the e6^e7 transcript encodes a fusion protein [11, 12].

The cervical carcinogenesis process requires the continuous, deregulated expression of E6 and E7 viral oncoproteins, following the integration of the viral genome into the infected cell genome. E6/E7 act on over 150 cellular proteins; in particular, on the tumor suppressors p53 and pRb, crucial for cell cycle regulation and genome stability [13, 14]. E6 leads to the proteasomal destruction of p53 via the formation of a complex with the ubiquitine ligase E6-AP (E6-associated protein). E7 binds to pRb and induces its proteolytic degradation, releasing E2F factors from the E2F/pRb repressor complexes to transactivate E2F-responsive genes and lead to the G0/G1 transition of the cell cycle and the S-phase entry. Overall, E6 and E7 affect proliferation, differentiation, migration, and invasion processes, induce resistance to apoptosis and promote immune escape.

There is limited data on the viral content of EVs produced by HPV-infected cells. HPV DNA is detected in EVs isolated from cervical cancer cell lines (HeLa, Ca Ski) and HPV-positive cervical samples [15, 16]. However, it is not always clearly stated whether the DNA detected is actually inside the EVs. The presence of HPV16/18 transcripts has been evaluated by RT-PCR in EVs from SiHa (HPV16) and HeLa (HPV18) cell lines and authors showed that the e6/e7 unspliced transcripts were undetectable and only the splice variant e6*I in Hela EVs was detected [17]. The search for the E6 and E7 oncoproteins yielded conflicting results. According to studies, E6, E7, both, or neither are found inside EVs [18]. The impact of HPV material packaged in vesicles on target cells and the mechanisms at play are not completely understood and poorly documented. E6/E7 oncoproteins from HPV16/HPV18-positive cells impact the exosomal content of miRNA involved in regulating cell proliferation. Thus, exosomes derived from HPV-infected cells could strengthen the oncogenic potential of the virus in healthy cells surrounding the tumor [19–21]. Although the specific function of E7 remains unclear, the exposure of HIV-1-infected macrophage cell line U1 to Ca Ski vesicles containing the E7 oncoprotein resulted in increased viral load and oxidative stress [22]. Cervical cancer EVs are reported to control different aspects of tumor progression including the peritumoral neovascularization [23], the development of metastases [24]. Studies reported that HPV16 DNA transmitted by exosomes has been found in the fibroblasts and epithelial cells of breast cancers, thereby increasing their proliferative and invasive capacities [15].

In order to explore the potential proliferative impact of sEVs derived from HPV16-positive cancer cells on HPV-negative bladder cancer cells, this study investigated whether sEVs secreted by Ca Ski cells harboring HPV16 could transfer viral material (DNA, RNA, viral oncoproteins E6 and E7) into HPV-free tumor target cells to modulate their proliferative status. After thoroughly characterizing Ca Ski vesicles, we demonstrated the transfer of viral e6/e7 transcripts and for the first time their involvement in increasing the proliferation of T24 bladder cancer target cells not infected with the virus.

Materials and methods

Cell line culture

The human cancer cell lines Ca Ski (cervical carcinoma, CRL-1550) and T24 (grade 3 bladder carcinoma, HTB-4) were obtained from ATCC (Rockville, MD, USA). Cells were maintained in DMEM (Ca Ski) or Mc COY’s 5 A medium (T24) containing L-glutamine supplemented with 10% fetal calf serum (FCS) (Invitrogen, Cergy Pontoise, France), 1% antibiotics (10 mg/ml streptomycin, 10 000 U/ml penicillin, Eurobio Scientific, Les Ulis, France) in a humidified atmosphere at 5% CO2/95% O2 and 37 °C. Tests for the absence of mycoplasma were carried out before the beginning of experiments. According to the experiments, cells were seeded in 100 mm Petri dishes, 96-, 24-, 6- or 4-well plates, and cultured with 5% FCS.

EV isolation from cell culture supernatants by differential ultracentrifugation

Ca Ski cells were seeded in six 100 mm dishes (300,000 cells/dish) with 5% FCS-supplemented medium. At confluence, they were washed twice with FCS-free medium and a fresh culture medium without FCS was added for 24 h. Supernatants (60 mL) were then collected and centrifuged at 300 x g for 10 min at 4 °C to pellet floating cells. The supernatants were centrifuged at 2000 x g for 20 min at 4 °C (= 2 K dead cell pellet). The new supernatants were centrifuged at 10,000 x g for 40 min at 4 °C (= 10 K cell debris pellet). Finally, the obtained supernatants were filtered through a 0.2 μm syringe filter and centrifuged at 100,000 x g (Beckman Coulter, TH-401) for 90 min at 4 °C (= 100 K isolated EV pellet). The 100 K pellet was washed in 0.1 μm filtered-PBS 1X and subjected to the same ultracentrifugation. The 2 K and 10 K pellets were resuspended in RIPA buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.4), 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1% NP-40, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate) for protein extraction. According to the analysis, the 100 K pellets were resuspended in 0.1 μm filtered-calcium and magnesium-free PBS 1X (NTA, TEM, DNA extraction), FCS-free culture medium (confocal microscopy), RIPA buffer (western blotting) or TRI REAGENT ® (RNA extraction for RTqPCR) and frozen at − 80 °C until further use.

EV isolation by a kit

Ca Ski EV isolation was performed from cell culture media with the Total Exosome Isolation Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Illkirch, France) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. This kit is used to validate the collection of EVs with a quality comparable to that obtained with ultracentrifugation. Indeed, it allows work to be carried out using a small number of cells. The starting volumes of cell culture media were 12 mL (siRNA experiments) or 60 mL (characterization experiments). Briefly, cell media were centrifuged (300 x g, 10 min, at 4 °C; 2000 x g, 20 min, at 4 °C, 10,000 x g, 40 min, at 4 °C). Following the last centrifugation, supernatants were concentrated using Pierce™ Protein concentrator PES, 3 kDa MWCO (Thermo Scientific) to 1 mL, collected in new tubes and 0.5 volume of the Total Exosome Isolation Reagent was added. The culture media/reagent mixture was incubated at 4 °C overnight and then centrifuged at 10,000 x g for 1 h at 4 °C. The supernatants were discarded and the EV-containing pellets were resuspended in an appropriate volume of 0.1 μm filtered-PBS 1X or lysis buffer (RIPA, TRI REAGENT ®) and frozen at − 80 °C until further use.

Nanoparticle tracking analysis

The analysis was performed using extracellular vesicles isolated by ultracentrifugation and a kit. EV pellets were resuspended in 200 µL PBS 1X (without calcium and magnesium) filtered through a 0.1 μm syringe filter. EV concentration and size were determined with an NanoSight NS300 instrument (Malvern Panalytical, Malvern, UK) according to the following parameters: sample dilution 1:20, total number of frames: 1498, camera level: 14, detection threshold: 10, 3x videos of 60 s. The data were analyzed using Nanosight NTA 3.2 Analytical Software (Malvern Instruments, Malvern, the UK). As a negative control, a blank 0.1 μm-filtered PBS 1X was also run.

Transmission electron microscopy

The morphology of EVs was observed by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) using extracellular vesicles isolated by ultracentrifugation and a kit. Isolated EVs were resuspended in 200 µL PBS 1X (without calcium and magnesium) filtered through a 0.1 μm syringe filter. An aliquot was fixed with 2% paraformaldehyde (15 min at room temperature) and deposited on a formwar/carbon-coated copper effluved grid and left for 15 min. For contrast staining, a drop of UranyLess solution (Delta Microscopies) was added for 2 min and excess liquid was absorbed on a filter paper. Images were acquired with a Hitachi HT7800 transmission electron microscope (Hitachi high technologies, Tokyo, Japan) operating at 100 kV and equipped with a AMT NanoSprint43 camera (AMT, Woburn, USA).

Cellular and sEV DNA isolation and PCR

Small EV pellets were resuspended in 0.1 μm filtered-PBS 1X. To investigate whether viral DNA was located outside or within the vesicles, the pellet of vesicles was treated with DNAse I to remove any external DNA (New England Biolabs, Evry, France), after which EVs were lysed and DNA extraction was performed. To certify the presence of DNA, extracted DNA was subjected to one or two further DNAse I treatments. DNA was extracted from Ca Ski cells and Ca Ski sEVs using QIAamp® DNA Mini kit (Qiagen, Courtaboeuf, France) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. DNA concentration and purity were evaluated using NanoDrop One spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, Courtaboeuf, France). PCR assays were performed using the DreamTaq™ Green PCR Master Mix (2X) (Thermo Scientific) with 50 ng DNA, and specific primers for e6, e7 viral oncogenes (Table 1). PCR mixtures were subjected to 40 amplification cycles on the TProfessional Trio Thermocycler BIOMETRA (Analytik Jena, Saint Aubin, France). The PCR starts with denaturation and Taq polymerase activation step (5 min, 95 °C). The following PCR cycle settings were used: denaturation at 95◦C for 30 s, primer hybridization at 68 °C for 20 s, and elongation at 72 °C for 30 s. Then, the reaction is completed by an elongation step (5 min, 72 °C). Each reaction was run in triplicates with three independent experiments. PCR products were electrophoresed in 2% agarose gels (Agarose BP 160–500, Fisher Scientific) stained with SYBR™ Safe DNA gel stain 1X (ThermoFisher Scientific). Resulting DNA band images were captured by ChemiDoc XRS + with image lab 5.1 software (BioRad Laboratories, CA, USA).

HPV16 genome copy number determination

Ca Ski cell supernatant obtained after the first centrifugation at 100,000 g during EV isolation and the PBS 1X washing solution kept after the second centrifugation at 100,000 g of the PBS 1X-resuspended EV pellet were sent to the French HPV National Reference Centre (CNR Papillomavirus, Besançon, France) for DNA extraction and HPV16 genome copy number evaluation. DNA was extracted using the QIAamp DNA Blood Mini Kit (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. PCR TaqMan assays were performed as previously described in detail [25] using the Light Cycler® 480 system (Roche Diagnostics, Meylan, France). Specific primers and probes were the following: HPV16 e6, forward 5’-GTTCATAAAGCTAAATTGTACGTGGAA-3’, reverse 5’-TGTGAATCTTGTCCGTCCACTT-3’, probe 5’-FAM-ACAATATCCTTTAGGGTAACATGTCTTCCATGCATG-BHQ1-3’ ; albumin, forward 5’-GCTGTCATCTCTTGTGGGCTGT-3’, reverse 5’-AAACTCATGGGAGCTGCTGGTT-3’, probe 5’-FAM-CCTGTCATGCCCACACAAATCTCTCC-BHQ1-3’. Primer final concentrations used were 500 nM for HPV16 e6 and 200 nM for albumin. Probe final concentration used was 100 nM for both HPV16 e6 and albumin. HPV16 genome copy number was expressed as HPV16 e6 copy number/mL as previously described [25].

Cellular and sEV total RNA isolation, reverse transcription and qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from Ca Ski cells and sEVs isolated from these cells using TRI REAGENT® (Euromedex, Mundelsheim, France). Small EV pellets were treated or not with RNAse (Promega, Charbonnières, France) to remove outer RNA then EVs were lysed and RNA was extracted. RNA concentration and purity were evaluated using NanoDrop One spectrophotometer. Total RNA (1 µg, 25 ng, 120 ng) was then reverse transcribed into cDNA using Maxima First Strand cDNA synthesis kit for RTqPCR with dsDNAse (Thermo Scientific) following the manufacturer’s guidelines. The qPCR assays were performed with the StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Thermo Scientific) using SYBR Green technology in a reaction mixture of 20 µL containing 15 µL of Power SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (2X) (Applied Biosystems), 5 µL of cDNA diluted 1:10 and primer pairs for e6, e7, splice variants e6*I, e6^e7, and β-2-microglobulin expression (Table 1). A denaturation and Taq polymerase activation step at 95 °C for 10 min was followed by 32 amplification cycles. The following PCR cycle settings were used: denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s, hybridization/elongation at 60 °C for 1 min. Each reaction was run in triplicates with three independent experiments. qPCR products were electrophoresed in 2% agarose gels stained with SYBR™ Safe DNA gel stain 1X. The resulting cDNA band images were captured by ChemiDoc XRS + (version 5.1) with image lab software. In siRNA transfection experiments, the fold differences in expression of the target genes between control siRNA transfected cells and HPV16 e6 siRNA or HPV16 e7 siRNA-transfected cells were calculated by the 2−ΔΔCt method [26] using β-2-microglobulin as an internal control gene.

RNA interference and cell transfection

Control siRNA (negative control for evaluating RNAi off-target effects, sc-37007), HPV16 e6 (sc-156008), and HPV16 e7 (sc-270423) specific siRNA were from Santa Cruz Biotechnology. Ca Ski cells were seeded in 24-well plates (70,000 cells/well) and cultured in DMEM with 5% FCS without antibiotics. After 24 h, cells were transfected with 30 nM siRNA using Lipofectamine™ 2000 reagent (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After 24 h transfection, cells were incubated in a serum-free medium for 24 h more. Total RNA was extracted from cells and kit-isolated sEVs for viral oncogene expression assessment by RTqPCR.

Western blotting

Cells were washed, scraped in cold PBS 1X and centrifuged at 2000 x g for 10 min at 4 °C. Pellets were resuspended in RIPA lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1% Nonidet P40, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate) supplemented with protease inhibitors (Roche Diagnostics, Meylan, France). Pellets of isolated sEVs were also resuspended in RIPA buffer. Then, whole cell or sEV lysates were sonicated and centrifuged at 10,000 x g for 10 min at 4 °C. EV lysates and the supernatants were stored (-80 °C) for analysis. Protein concentration was estimated using the Bio-Rad protein assay (Bio-Rad, Marnes-la- Coquette, France). Protein extracts were solved in 2X Laemmli buffer (Bio-Rad) and separated by a 7.5 or 12% SDS-PAGE. Proteins were transferred onto 0.2 μm PVDF membranes (GE Healthcare, Buckinghamshire, UK), and non-specific binding was blocked in TBS-Tween 20 buffer (0.5 mM Tris-HCl, 45 mM NaCl, 0.1% Tween 20, pH 7.4) containing 5% nonfat milk or bovine serum albumin. Membranes were incubated with the appropriate primary antibodies listed in Table 2. Bound primary antibodies were detected using HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Table 2). Proteins were visualized by using an enhanced chemiluminescence detection method (GE Healthcare) followed by film exposure (Hyperfilm ECL, GE Healthcare) or by using ChemiDoc XRS+ (version 5.1) with image lab software (Bio-Rad).

Uptake of labeled sEVs

Small EVs isolated from Ca Ski supernatants were labeled with PKH67 Green Fluorescent Cell Linker Midi Kit (Sigma-Aldrich) according to the manufacturer’s protocol and centrifuged at 100,000 x g for 90 min at 4 °C. The final pellet was resuspended in FCS-free culture medium. T24 recipient cells were seeded on coverslips in 6-well plates and incubated in 5% FCS-supplemented Mc COY’s 5a medium for 48 h at 37 °C. After two washes of the cells in FCS-free medium, the labeled exosomes were then deposited on cells for 24 h. To observe actin cytoskeleton and cell nuclei, phalloidin and Hoechst 33,342 staining were performed. Briefly, cells were washed twice with PBS 1X, fixed with 2% paraformaldehyde/0.2% Triton X100 for 15 min and washed again twice with PBS 1X. A solution of phalloidin (Sigma-Aldrich) diluted 1:20 was then added to the cells for 30 min at room temperature, in the dark followed by a Hoechst 33,342 labeling (5 µg/mL, ThermoFisher Scientific) for 2 min at room temperature. After three washes in PBS 1X, the coverslips were mounted on a glass slide using Fluoromount-G mounting medium (Southern Biotech). The slides were then observed using a Zeiss LSM800 confocal microscope (Zeiss, Germany) and photographs were taken.

IncuCyte proliferation analysis

T24 cells were seeded in 96-well plates (3000 cells/well) and grown in 5% FCS-containing medium. After 24 h, cells were washed twice with FCS-free medium and then incubated in FCS-free medium (control), 10% FCS-supplemented medium (positive control), or increasing amounts of Ca Ski sEVs (0.05.105, 0.1.105, 0.3.105, 105, 2.105, 5.105, 20.105, 50.105 particles resuspended in FCS-free medium) for 24 h. The percentage of cell confluence was analyzed with the real-time microscope IncuCyte S3™ (Sartorius, Göttingen, Germany) using the IncuCyte 2023 A Rev2 software. For each stimulation condition of T24 cells and at each experimental time, eight photos were taken every 6 h from nine separate zones per well with a 10 × objective. Values obtained from the nine zones of each well were pooled and the average was calculated.

Optical microscopy cell observation

T24 cells were seeded in 4-well plates (18,000 cells/well) and grown in 5% FCS-containing medium. After 24 h, cells were washed twice with FCS-free medium and then incubated for 24 h in FCS-free medium (control) or 2.105 Ca Ski sEVs treated or not with DNAse/RNAse. The photos were taken in phase-contrast imaging mode with the EVOS™ M3000 Imaging System (ThermoFisher Scientific) under 4x, 10x and 20x objectives.

CCK-8 cell growth detection

Cells were seeded in a 96-well plate with 5% FCS-medium (3000 cells/well). After 24 h, they were incubated in FCS-free medium for 24 h. They were then stimulated or not with 10% FCS-supplemented medium (positive control) or with Ca Ski sEVs (2.105 particles resuspended in FCS-free medium) for 24 h. The proliferation was evaluated using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (Sigma-Aldrich). A volume of 10 µL of the CCK-8 solution was added to each well of the plate and cells were incubated at 37 °C for 2 h in the dark. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm on a microplate reader (Multiskan™ FC, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Twelve determinations were performed by experimental condition.

Statistical analysis

Data were expressed as mean ± SD of three independent experiments or as specified for each figure. The significant differences between groups were evaluated using the two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Ca Ski cell EV characterization after ultracentrifugation isolation

To characterize and qualify EV-containing 100 K fraction isolated from Ca Ski cell culture conditioned media, metrological, microscopic, and biochemical analyses were carried out following the guidelines of the ISEV (International Society for Extracellular Vesicles). The size and distribution of the isolated EVs were evaluated by NTA analysis (Fig. 1A). This method presented a peak conforming with a majority of particles with a diameter less than 200 nm between 85 and 180 nm, consistent with the size of typical small EVs (sEVs). Specifically, the size of the majority of particles was 84.4 ± 0.5 nm. The total EV concentration was 7.3 × 109 ± 1.53 × 107 particles/mL. The morphology of the vesicles was then assessed by TEM (Fig. 1B) revealing a typical rounded shape. Western blotting analysis was carried out from each centrifugation pellet obtained during the EV isolation process (2 K, 10 K, 100 K) and from cell lysate used as a positive control of protein expression. As presented in Fig. 2A, from same amount of proteins (10 µg) in all samples, immunoblots showed the presence of the tetraspanins CD9, CD63 and CD81 (EV hallmarks), ALIX (ESCRT-associated protein), TSG101 (a subunit of the ESCRT-I complex), Flotillin-1 (a lipid raft-associated protein involved in plasma membrane invagination) and Syntenin (ALIX-associated protein) in the 2 K, 10 K fractions and as supposed in the 100 K fraction. These proteins commonly found in sEVs confirmed the presence of this EV subtype in the 100 K fraction. The presence of organelle-specific proteins (GM130, Calnexin, Calreticulin, Lamin A/C, COX IV, characteristic of Golgi, endoplasmic reticulum, nucleus, and mitochondria respectively) was detected in whole cell lysate and 2 K fraction but most importantly, they were not found out in the 100 K fraction excluding any organelle component contamination (Fig. 2B). However, the 100 K fraction was indeed deposited on the immunoblot (Fig. 2B), as the EV markers HSP70, TSG101, HSP90, CD9 and Flotillin-1 were detected on the same membrane, thus confirming the presence of proteins in the 100 K fraction. The overall data validated the identity of EVs as small EVs in the 100 K fraction of Ca Ski cells. A highly sEV-enriched fraction was obtained by ultracentrifugation attesting to the quality of our EV isolation procedure.

HPV viral material was highlighted inside Ca Ski sEVs

Before exploring the potential role of sEVs in transmitting HPV16 viral material in recipient cells, our objective was to investigate the presence of HPV DNA, HPV e6/e7 unspliced transcripts, e6*I/e7^e7 spliced variants, and E6/E7 proteins inside sEVs. First of all, to check whether our sEV-enriched 100 K fraction was not contaminated by free-viral DNA, the eventual presence, in cell supernatants, of circulating viral DNA originating from flotting alive and dead Ca Ski cells was investigated by PCR. The procedure description and the results obtained are shown in Fig. 3A. DNA was extracted from supernatant of the first ultracentrifugation and of the second ultracentrifugation after washing with PBS 1X. Thus, Ca Ski conditioned medium underwent successive centrifugations (300 g, 2000 g, 10,000 g) and was filtered through a 0.2 μm syringe filter. After a first centrifugation at 100,000 g, the supernatant obtained was used for DNA extraction and the 100 K pellet obtained was resuspended in PBS 1X washing solution. The supernatant obtained after 100,000 g centrifugation of the 100 K pellet resuspended in PBS 1X was also used for DNA extraction. A copy number of HPV16 genome of 3.4 × 106 copies HPV16/mL was detected in the Ca Ski cell supernatant. On the other hand, no viral DNA was detected in the PBS 1X washing solution indicating that a second centrifugation at 100,000 g removes any circulating viral DNA contamination. Moreover, our 100 K fractions are obtained following two centrifugations at 100,000 g.

Ca Ski sEVs increase the proliferation of HPV-negative T24 bladder cancer target cells

To evaluate the biological effect of Ca Ski sEVs on HPV-negative tumor cells, we assessed cell proliferation using the IncuCyte S3™ Live-cell analysis system. HPV-free T24 bladder cancer cells seeded in 96-well plates were co-incubated or not with increasing amounts of sEVs ((0.05, 0.1, 0.3, 1, 2, 5, 20, 50) x 105) for 24 h. As shown in Fig. 5A (IncuCyte assay), the capacity of T24 cells to proliferate increased significantly from 0.3.105 sEVs until 20.105 particles with a maximum at 2.105 particles compared to control cells without sEVs. The effect of 10% FCS (positive control) or 2.105 Ca Ski sEVs on the proliferation of T24 cells was then assessed using the CCK-8 assay. The results showed that after 24 h of incubation, Ca Ski sEVs acted to almost the same extent as FCS in relation to the control cells, confirming the proliferation increase of T24 cells incubated with 2.105 Ca Ski sEVs (Fig. 5B). The photos obtained with the real time microscope IncuCyte in Fig. 5C illustrate the results described for 2.105 particle experimental condition, namely that the cells proliferated. A zoom of the photos is shown between the original photos. A CCK-8 assay was also carried out by exposing T24 cells to Ca Ski sEVs that had been pre-treated with DNase and RNase. The results obtained (Fig. 5D ) show that the cells proliferated in the presence of both treated and untreated vesicles. However, there was no statistically significant difference in proliferation with the treated sEVs and the untreated sEVs. The images showed (Fig. 5E) clearly illustrate the proliferative effect upon vesicle exposure. Cell proliferation is not due to the DNA or RNA that could be associated with the outer membrane of the vesicles.

Characterization of Ca Ski EVs isolated by a kit

We hypothesized that the e6 and e7 early transcripts of HPV16 loaded inside Ca Ski sEVs were responsible for the proliferation of T24 cells incubated in the presence of EVs. To validate this theory, we performed siRNA-based transfection experiments on Ca Ski cells and then recovered the EVs from the transfected cells. These EVs were deposited on T24 cells for 24 h. For EV isolation by ultracentrifugation, we started with 60 ml of supernatants from cells seeded in 100 cm² plates. For e6 and e7 oncogene knocking-down experiments, T24 cells were seeded in 24-well plates, so we had fewer cells and consequently less supernatant volume. To optimize EV recovery, we used a commercial kit. Thus, for each experimental conditions, supernatant volumes of 12 mL were collected for sEV isolation. However, using a commercial kit required the vesicles to be completely recharacterized using NTA, transmission electron microscopy and western blotting. The results (Fig. 7) were similar to those obtained with the differential ultracentrifugation method and did not display significant differences in terms of physical and biochemical characteristics. The NTA analysis (Fig. 7A) presented a peak conforming with a majority of particles with a diameter between 52 and 180 nm and revealed that the most of particles were 106.3 ± 1.9 nm in size. The total EV concentration was 5 × 109 ± 4.40 × 106 particles/mL. The observation of the particles by TEM revealed a typical rounded shape of EVs (Fig. 7B). The western blotting analysis was carried out from the EV pellet and from the cell lysate used as a positive control of protein expression. As presented in Fig. 7C, immunoblots showed the presence of the tetraspanins CD9, CD63 and CD81, ALIX, TSG101, and Flotillin-1. These proteins commonly found in sEVs confirmed the presence of these vesicles in the isolated pellet. The presence of organelle-specific proteins (GM130, Calnexin, Lamin A/C, COX IV) was detected in whole cell lysate and these markers were not found out in the sEV pellet excluding any organelle component contamination. The overall data validated the identity of EV as small EVs isolated by the kit.

Discussion

Cervical cancer is the result of the malignant progression of an untreated precancerous lesion caused by a persistent high risk-HPV infection. HPV16 persistence is associated with the highest risk to develop an invasive cancer that can spread to nearby organs such as the bladder. The global public health challenge is therefore to early detect which infections will persist and eventually develop into cancer so that they can be treated. Liquid biopsy is a relevant tool to track down early stage of the disease before the cancer sets in and progresses to the development of metastases. EVs found in biological fluids carry a molecular signature that is unique to the tumor cell that produced them and thus constitute a supply of circulating biomarkers. A wealth of published scientific data account for the role of EVs on tumor development but the role of tumor-derived sEVs in the progression from precancerous cervical lesions harbouring high-risk HPV to cancer has not yet been clearly demonstrated. Nevertheless, some studies reported the oncogenic potential of exosomes produced by cervical cancer cells by providing nucleic acids such as circular RNA, miRNA, lncRNA to facilitate metastasis spreading [24], to control the angiogenic/lymphangionenic process [27, 23], or to modulate tumor progression [28].

In this study, we used the Ca Ski cell model because these cells harbour HPV16. Most importantly, they do not produce virions since the HPV genome is integrated with around 600 copies into the host cell genome. The procedure used to isolate sEVs has an influence on the shape and size distribution of the isolated vesicles. However, differential ultracentrifugation remains the standard reference method as it produces highly enriched sEV fractions. We isolated and well characterized a 100 K fraction obtained from Ca Ski cell supernatants by ultracentrifugation and also by a commercial kit following the recommendations established by ISEV [1]. The quality of EV isolation procedure was equivalent with both methods and EV qualification gave similar results. NTA analysis revealed that the size distribution of isolated particles was in the range of sEV diameter. The TEM showed the typical spherical shape of EVs. The western blotting analysis highlighted the presence of the EV surface markers, CD9, CD81 and CD63. The organelle-specific proteins were not found out in Ca Ski sEVs. The overall data demonstrates the high quality of our EV isolation. The high expression of CD63 [29] in comparison with CD9 and CD81, associated with the presence of ALIX, TSG101, Flotillin-1 and Syntenin suggest that our 100 K fraction was enriched in MVB-derived vesicles, i.e. exosomes. Indeed, ALIX and TSG101 are implicated in forming intraluminal vesicles in MVB via the ESCRT (Endosomal Sorting Complex Required for Transport) machinery-dependent pathway [30]. Flotillin-1 is a lipid raft-associated protein that enables the invagination of the plasma membrane to form early endosomes, which are the first step in the formation of exosomes [31]. Syntenin is an ALIX-associated protein [32].

It appears that cancer cell-derived exosomes transport nuclear materials. Our analysis of Ca Ski sEVs showed that there was no HPV16 DNA (e6, e7 gene sequences) inside the vesicles but DNA would be associated to the outer surface of sEVs since after DNAse I treatment of vesicles before DNA extraction, e6/e7 gene sequences were not found out. On the contrary, others have detected in the EVs of these cells, HPV16 e7 gene [15]. However, this study did not characterize the EV type in detail. And above all, the viral DNA detected by HPV16 specific Digital PCR assay was not inside the vesicles but rather associated with them. In fact, treating the vesicles with DNase I before EV lysis and DNA extraction reduced the number of viral copies. Furthermore, HPV18 e6 and e7 fragmented sequences have been identified by Next Generation Sequencing and PCR from HeLa cell exosomes isolated by ultracentrifugation [16]. Once again, the type of EVs isolated was not very well characterized, and these EVs were not pretreated with DNase I. This study does not demonstrate the presence of viral DNA sequences within HeLa cell extracellular vesicles. On the other hand, the e7 gene was detected by digital droplet PCR in EVs derived from the GUMC-395 cell line established from a neuroendocrine carcinoma of the cervix (a rare and aggressive form of cervical cancer) [33]. Genomic DNA (gDNA) has been detected inside a population of exosomes containing nuclear proteins such as lamin A/C [34]. This could be due to micronuclei being produced as a result of improper chromosome or other nuclear component segregation during mitosis [35]. Our investigation showed that the isolated EVs did not contain lamin A/C confirming the absence of DNA inside Ca Ski cell-produced sEVs. A study reported that the association of DNA with sEV-outer surface could be due to the presence of antibiotics in the culture medium [36].

Our analysis showed the presence of pro-tumorigenic transcripts in Ca Ski-derived sEVs. We detected the unspliced transcripts e6, e7, as well as the splice variants e6*I and e6^e7. Importantly, we also demonstrated that these transcripts were inside the vesicles. It is not surprising to find out e6/e7 transcripts in EVs. The e6, e7 mRNAs were found out in EVs from human foreskin keratinocytes after a transduction experiment with HPV16 e6/e7 [20] but were not detected in sEVs from HPV18-positive HeLa cells [17]. In contrast, the splice variant e6*I was detectable for the first time in HPV18-infected HeLa cell sEVs [17]. The HPV16 e7 mRNA was discovered inside the EVs derived from the GUMC-395 cervical neuroendocrine carcinoma cell line [33].

Cervical cancer is caused by the targeted action of the viral oncoproteins E6 and E7. However, we did not find these proteins in EVs using the methods employed. Indeed, E6/E7 oncoproteins were not detected in our analysis of Ca Ski sEV proteins by western blotting. This result is the same as that obtained in HPV18-positive HeLa cells, where no E6/E7 oncoprotein was detected [37]. Only one other study has detected the E6 protein, but not the E7 protein, in well-characterized Ca Ski sEVs. However, the amount of protein loaded onto the acrylamide gel is not specified, and the detected protein band is extremely faint [22]. On the other hand, HPV16 E6 and E7 proteins were found in vesicles described as exosomes from HPV-positive head and neck cancer cell lines (UM-SCC-2, UM-SCC-47, UPCI: SCC-90) [38]. In our study, the expression levels of p53 and pRb, the respective targets of the viral E6 and E7 proteins, did not vary when HPV-negative T24 cells were incubated with Ca Ski sEVs, and yet there are the HPV16 transcripts inside sEVs. This provides further evidence that E6/E7 proteins are not present in sEVs, and that they are not translated in T24 cells from e6/e7 transcripts transferred by sEVs.

The impact of viral transcript transfer via EVs has not been described to date. However, when sEVs carrying e6/e7 transcripts were deposited on HPV-lacking tumor target cells, these cells increased their proliferation. Our study demonstrated that Ca Ski sEVs were internalized by HPV-negative T24 cells, and HPV16 transcripts were found in these target cells. This capture of sEVs resulted in an increase in T24 cell proliferation. We hypothesized that the e6 and e7 transcripts were responsible for this biological effect. Our experimental approach using siRNA clearly demonstrated that the viral e6 and e7 transcripts contained in Ca Ski sEVs were involved in the increase of T24 cell proliferation. Indeed, the number of T24 cells in contact with sEVs produced by CaSki cells transfected with siRNAs targeting e6 and e7 decreased by 50% compared to that observed with T24 cells in contact with sEVs from CaSki cells transfected with a control siRNA. However, if the transcripts e6 and e7 are indeed involved, this does not appear to be in the form of their translation products, since the expression levels of their targets p53 and pRb in T24 cells are not reduced. This suggests that these transcripts act differently.

How do HPV16 transcripts increase cell proliferation? It is presumed that they are not translated after transferring into target cells. The viral transcripts may act as a molecular sponge trapping a RNA molecule present in T24 cells, a miRNA or a lncRNA with antiproliferative properties and targeting proliferative genes or act as a scaffold molecule for EV-protein transport involved in cellular proliferation. They may also direct certain microRNAs into EVs to enrich their content in « oncomiR ». For example, miR-21, identified as an « oncomiR » could be an interesting target. It increases T24 bladder cancer cell proliferation [39] and miR-21-enriched EVs are reported to stimulate tumor cell proliferation through the down-regulation of PTEN [40]. Interestingly, miR-21 was overexpressed in EVs isolated from HPV16-immortalized primary human keratinocytes [41]. Based on this rationale, we could speculate that, in our study model, an increase in T24 cell proliferation would be triggered by the capture of Ca Ski sEVs loaded with early viral transcripts carrying miR-21. Further experiments are needed to answer this question.

Other questions remain unanswered: how are viral transcripts selected for transport by vesicles? What molecular mechanism do they use to influence the proliferation of T24 cells? Different potential mechanisms have been suggested for RNA packaging into EVs [42]: (1) a passive incorporation mechanism depending on the amount of RNA present at the site of EV biogenesis, (2) the presence of a specific sequence facilitating RNA incorporation into EVs ; this sequence could act through interaction with the EV lipid membrane during the formation of the vesicle or via interaction with a RNA binding protein (RBP) thus modifying the amount of RNA present close to the endosomal compartment, (3) the modification of the RNA sequence such as 3’end uridylation could determine the incorporation of RNA, (4) the post-translational modifications of RBP could modify RBP/RNA interactions.

How HPV16 viral transcripts are packaged into vesicles is unknown. However, two mechanisms have been described for TBSV (tomato bushy shunt virus) and HCV (hepatitis C virus) RNA viruses. TBSV viral RNA interacts with ESCRT machinery components that have RNA-binding capabilities [43]. HCV genomic RNA is present in infected patient exosomes in the form of a stabilized ribonucleoprotein complex containing miR-122 with the Ago2 and HSP90 proteins [44]. RNA-binding proteins have been identified in EVs and are thought to facilitate the transport of RNAs into the vesicles and their retention inside [42, 45] An unknown protein to date could be required to sort HPV16 mRNAs into EVs in cells. Further investigations are needed to provide answers to these questions. How are RNAs carried by EVs protected from degradation when they are delivered to recipient cells? The mechanism by which they are transported to the endosomal compartment or into the cytoplasm remains unclear to this day. The RNAs may be protected by their association with proteins such as AGO2, or stabilised in the form of a secondary structure. To escape lysosomal degradation, they must exit the endosome. Acidification of the endosomal compartment may play an important role in this process by facilitating RNA transfer. Studies have shown that single-stranded RNAs can activate Toll-like receptors within endosomes or the cytosolic RNA sensor RIG-I. It is conceivable that in receptor-expressing tumour cells, endolysosomal function could be impaired or the RIG-1 pathway inactive. This would allow the RNA to remain in the receptor cell for a sufficient duration to exert a biological effect [42, 46].

Conclusions

Our study highlighted a functional horizontal transfer via EVs of HPV16 early transcripts leading to the increase of the proliferation of HPV-negative bladder cancer recipient cells. The decision to use bladder cancer cells (HPV-negative T24 cells) as target cells for Ca Ski sEVs is based on a study showing that cells from certain types of cervical cancer (HPV-infected) can metastasize to the bladder. Our study sheds new light on the mechanism that may underlie this process [47].

The proliferative effect is due to mRNA as such. Further investigations are required in depth to elucidate the mechanisms involved to understand how EVs derived from HPV-associated cancer can act on tumor cells to increase their proliferation. From a clinical perspective, the analysis of e6/e7 early transcripts as potential candidate biomarkers to detect women with a high-risk to develop high-grade lesions could be interesting. Few studies have been performed from cervical specimens. However, a higher prevalence of e6/e7 mRNA was reported in HPV-positive patients presenting a higher-grade cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN) lesion with a high risk of progression [48]. The level of e6 mRNA was higher in high-grade lesions and was associated with the viral load in cervical samples representing the different stage of the disease (CIN1, CIN2, CIN3, cervical cancer) [49]. Similarly, the percentage of e6*I transcripts, detected in HPV16-positive patients, increased from CIN2 to CIN3 to squamous cell carcinoma [50]. The HPV16 early transcript cargo contained in sEVs could be used to follow the evolution of cervical precancerous lesions to cancer and potentially metastasis spreading. These EV-incorporated mRNA are protected from ribonuclease degradation and their analysis from liquid biopsy isolated-EVs can be an applicable analytical method providing reproducible results. Furthermore, EVs isolated from a liquid biopsy (serum, plasma, cervicovaginal fluids) are a source of biomarkers since their molecular content is reflective of the parental productive cells. Their obtention is non-invasive, better accounts for tumor heterogeneity and allows repeated sampling for long-term patient follow-up.
