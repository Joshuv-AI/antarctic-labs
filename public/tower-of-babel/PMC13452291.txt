Incidentally detected crossed nonfused renal ectopia on contrast-enhanced trauma CT: A case report and review of the literature
Authors: Yigeng Li, Dilshad Dooreemeah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Crossed renal ectopia is a rare congenital anomaly in which a kidney is situated on the opposite side of the midline from its ureteral orifice in the bladder. The nonfused variant (type B) is an uncommon subtype, with a reported historical autopsy incidence of approximately 1 in 75,000, accounting for roughly 10% of crossed ectopias. Most cases are asymptomatic and diagnosed incidentally on imaging. A 35-year-old man with a previously documented right-sided duplex kidney underwent contrast-enhanced trauma computed tomography (CT) following an alleged assault. No acute traumatic injury was identified. CT of the abdomen and pelvis demonstrated a normally positioned right kidney in the right renal fossa alongside a second inferior reniform structure on the right side with normal enhancement and preserved corticomedullary differentiation, confirmed on portal venous phase axial and coronal multiplanar reconstructions. The left renal fossa was empty. The ureter of the ectopic kidney was traced crossing the midline, inserting orthotopically into the left side of the urinary bladder, with no parenchymal fusion between the 2 right-sided kidneys. These findings were diagnostic of crossed nonfused renal ectopia (McDonald and McClellan type B). Contrast-enhanced CT is central to the diagnosis and anatomical delineation of crossed nonfused renal ectopia, enabling characterization of renal position, ureteral course, and vascular anatomy. Recognition of this anomaly is critical to avoid misdiagnosis as renal agenesis and prevent inadvertent injury during surgical or interventional procedures. Clinicians and radiologists should maintain awareness of this rare entity and its associations with obstruction, nephrolithiasis, and malignancy.

Introduction

Crossed renal ectopia (CRE) is a rare congenital renal anomaly in which a kidney is situated on the opposite side of the midline from its ureteral orifice in the bladder [1]. Based on historical autopsy series, the overall incidence is estimated at approximately 1 in 7000, with the nonfused variant occurring in roughly 1 in 75,000 individuals and accounting for approximately 10% of all crossed ectopias [2,3]. Left-to-right ectopia is more common than right-to-left, and males are more frequently affected [4]. We present a case of crossed nonfused renal ectopia identified incidentally on contrast-enhanced trauma CT and provide a review of the relevant literature.

Case presentation

Written informed consent was obtained from the patient for publication of this case report and accompanying images. A 35-year-old man presented to the emergency room following an alleged assault with blunt trauma to the head and torso. He was intoxicated on arrival, with a fluctuating Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score between 11 and 13. His past medical history included anxiety and a previously documented right-sided duplex collecting system. Clinical examination and laboratory investigations were unremarkable, and there was no evidence of focal neurological deficit or intra-abdominal injury.

Given the mechanism of injury and fluctuating GCS, a contrast-enhanced trauma CT scan of the brain, cervical spine, chest, abdomen, and pelvis was performed. No acute intracranial hemorrhage, skull or facial fracture, cervical spine injury, pneumothorax, or intra-abdominal visceral injury was demonstrated.

Contrast-enhanced CT of the abdomen and pelvis (portal venous phase, 3-mm axial reconstructions with coronal and sagittal multiplanar reformats) revealed an unusual renal configuration. A normally positioned right kidney was identified within the right renal fossa, demonstrating normal dimensions and enhancement (Fig. 1). Inferior and medial to this, a second discrete reniform structure was visualized on the right side, with normal size, morphology, and corticomedullary differentiation on contrast-enhanced images (Fig. 2). Crucially, there was no evidence of parenchymal continuity or fusion between the 2 right-sided renal units. The left renal fossa was empty. The previously documented right-sided duplex collecting system was confirmed in the normally positioned right kidney; the inferior ectopic kidney demonstrated a single collecting system. Vascular assessment revealed an anomalous arterial supply to the crossed ectopic kidney, with a single artery arising from the right common iliac artery (Fig. 3). The artery measured approximately 4.1 mm at its origin and 4.5 mm more distally. Venous drainage of the crossed ectopic kidney originated from its posterosuperior aspect (Fig. 4), with the renal vein measuring 4.5 mm in diameter and joining the inferior vena cava (Fig. 5). The ureter of the inferior ectopic kidney was seen to course medially, crossing the midline to insert into the urinary bladder on the contralateral (left) side. There was no evidence of fusion between the 2 kidneys. These findings were consistent with an unfused crossed renal ectopia. No hydronephrosis, calculi, or focal renal lesions were identified.

The patient was admitted for tertiary trauma survey and post-traumatic amnesia assessment, which were unremarkable, and he was discharged the following day. The crossed renal ectopia represented an incidental finding on trauma imaging.

Discussion

Crossed renal ectopia is the second most common renal fusion anomaly after horseshoe kidney, with fused variants accounting for approximately 90% of cases [3,5]. McDonald and McClellan originally classified crossed renal ectopia into 4 types: type A (crossed ectopia with fusion), type B (crossed ectopia without fusion), type C (solitary crossed ectopia), and type D (bilaterally crossed ectopia) [6,7]. Our case represents type B crossed renal ectopia without fusion, a considerably rarer entity.

Embryologically, CRE results from abnormal renal ascent and rotation during early gestation. Disruption in migration of the ureteric bud and metanephric blastema between the fourth and eighth weeks of development is believed to contribute to ectopic positioning and abnormal rotation [8], although the exact mechanism remains incompletely understood.

A defining and clinically important feature of crossed renal ectopia is the frequent presence of aberrant renal vasculature, reflecting the persistence of the transient arterial supply acquired during arrested embryological ascent. Consequently, the origin, number, and course of both the renal arteries and veins are highly variable between patients. Previous reports have described ectopic kidneys supplied by multiple renal arteries arising from the abdominal aorta, common iliac arteries, or less commonly, the contralateral renal artery, with the orthotopic kidney also occasionally demonstrating anomalous arterial anatomy [9,10]. In contrast, not all cases demonstrate extensive vascular anomalies. Mudoni et al. [11] reported a patient with crossed fused renal ectopia demonstrating normally configured renal arteries and no venous abnormalities [11], illustrating that vascular patterns remain heterogeneous despite the underlying developmental anomaly. Contemporary reports likewise describe variable combinations of 2 renal arteries arising directly from the abdominal aorta with either a single renal vein or additional venous anomalies, while other complex variants have demonstrated separate arterial and venous supplies for each renal moiety [12,13]. Given this marked anatomical variability, preoperative vascular mapping with CT angiography or MR angiography is essential before abdominal, retroperitoneal, pelvic or urological surgery to minimize the risks of hemorrhage, renal ischemia, and inadvertent vascular injury.

Although many patients remain asymptomatic, CRE can present with nonspecific symptoms, including abdominal or flank pain, palpable mass, hematuria, or dysuria [1,14]. Observational series in children describe variable urinary tract anatomy and potential functional implications in cases of renal ectopia and fusion anomalies [15,16]. Associated genitourinary abnormalities have been reported, including supernumerary kidney [12] and genital malformations such as absent testis in association with cross-fused ectopia [17].

Complications most commonly include obstruction, infection, nephrolithiasis, and neoplasia [18]. Renal calculi have been reported in crossed-fused renal ectopia and may pose technical challenges due to abnormal rotation and vascular anatomy [19]. Infection may unmask previously silent anomalies, including acute obstructive pyelonephritis in nonfused CRE [20]. Malignant associations, while rare, have been documented, including renal cell carcinoma arising in crossed renal ectopia [13] and urothelial carcinoma involving both upper and lower urinary tracts in crossed fused ectopic kidneys [21].

Imaging plays a central role in the diagnosis and anatomical delineation of crossed renal ectopia. Multidetector CT urography remains particularly valuable in defining renal position, vascular anatomy, collecting systems, and the ureteral course—including midline crossing with orthotopic bladder insertion—and in identifying associated hydronephrosis, hydroureter, calculi, or mass lesions [5]. Renal ultrasound may serve as an initial modality, demonstrating an empty renal fossa and ectopic renal tissue [12]. Functional assessment can be performed using nuclear medicine studies such as MAG3 or DTPA renal scans, which assist in evaluating differential renal function and drainage patterns [12]. Radioisotope scanning has also been used to confirm non-fused crossed renal ectopia and associated ductal anomalies [22]. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) provides an alternative cross-sectional modality for detailed anatomical assessment, particularly when radiation avoidance is preferred [20].

In our case, contrast-enhanced CT demonstrated the absence of the kidney in the expected renal fossa and identified a contralateral ectopic kidney without parenchymal fusion. The ureter crossed the midline and inserted orthotopically into the bladder, confirming crossed nonfused renal ectopia. Recognition of this anatomy is essential to avoid misdiagnosis as renal agenesis and to reduce the risk of inadvertent injury during surgical or interventional procedures.

Conclusion

Crossed nonfused renal ectopia is a rare congenital renal migration anomaly that is frequently asymptomatic and detected incidentally. Trauma imaging, particularly contrast-enhanced CT, plays a crucial role in identifying the abnormal renal position and confirming the ureteric course with orthotopic bladder insertion. Awareness of this anomaly is essential to avoid misdiagnosis as renal agenesis and to prevent inadvertent injury during surgical or interventional procedures. Although often clinically silent, crossed renal ectopia may be associated with obstruction, infection, nephrolithiasis, and, rarely, malignancy, warranting appropriate clinical awareness and follow-up when indicated.
