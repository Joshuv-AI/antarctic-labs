Tensorial Constraints for Commuting Endomorphisms of the Generalized Tangent Bundle
Marco Aldi, Sergio Da Silva, and Daniele Grandini

Abstract
. In this paper we consider families of mutually commuting endomorphisms of the generalized tangent bundle. We identify natural tensorial constraints extending the notion of a generalized Kähler structure to endomorphisms that are not necessarily generalized almost complex structures. These tensors form ideals whose generators we explicitly construct and study using Gröbner basis techniques.

Primary: 53D18; Secondary: 13P10, 13A35, 14P99

1.
Introduction

Originally motivated by string theory as a framework to unify
B
-field transformations and diffeomorphisms, the generalized tangent bundle
[
Hitchin03
]
has emerged as an indispensable tool in differential geometry. This remarkable geometric object naturally accommodates generalized complex structures
[
Gualtieri11
]
, which are integrable skew-symmetric endomorphisms of the generalized tangent bundle that include both complex and symplectic structures as particular cases. Generalized CRF-structures
[
Vaisman08
]
provide a broader class of endomorphisms of the generalized tangent bundle and, in addition to generalized complex structures, include odd-dimensional geometries like normal almost contact and cosymplectic structures. Generalized CRF-structures were further extended in
[
AldiGrandini22
]
(see also
[
BlagaNannicini20
,
EtayoGomezSantamaria22
,
EtayoGomezSantamaria24
]
) to generalized polynomial structures: skew-symmetric endomorphisms satisfying a constant-coefficient polynomial equation. A central property of these structures is integrability which specifies their compatibility with the natural Courant-Dorfman bracket. While preliminary notions of integrability for generalized polynomial structures were explored in
[
AldiGrandini22
]
, in
[
AldiDaSilvaGrandini24
]
we proved that the vanishing of the shifted Courant-Nijenhuis torsion is the strongest tensorial integrability condition that can be imposed uniformly across all generalized polynomial structures, without requiring adaptation to the specific underlying polynomial.

In this paper, we continue the work of
[
AldiDaSilvaGrandini24
]
and address the case of several commuting endomorphisms of the generalized tangent bundle. We allow for the possibility that some of these endomorphisms may be skew-symmetric (e.g. generalized complex structures) or symmetric (e.g. generalized Riemannian metrics) with respect to the tautological inner product on the generalized tangent bundle. The central question that we address is: what possible tensorial conditions can one impose for the compatibility of the commuting endomorphisms with the Courant-Dorfman bracket? Just as in the case of a single endomorphism, we show that these compatibility conditions form an ideal in a suitable polynomial ring. Our main result is the explicit identification of a natural set of generators for this ideal. These generators come in two basic types: cubic and quadratic. The cubic generators can be thought of as direct generalizations of the shifted Courant-Nijenhuis torsion to the case of several commuting endomorphisms. The quadratic generators appear in the ideal only if the commuting family consists of at least two symmetric endomorphisms, and they have no apparent analogue for a single endomorphism. Both types can be constructed out of a natural bivariate version of the Courant-Nijenhuis torsion which we call the
semiconcomitant
.

The proof of our main theorem uses Gröbner basis techniques, a crucial tool in commutative algebra used to compute elimination of variables, kernels of ring homomorphisms, ideal membership, projective closures, syzygies, primary decomposition, and normalization, just to name a few
[
E
]
. In our case, greatly extending the techniques of
[
AldiDaSilvaGrandini24
]
, the use of Gröbner bases to compute the intersections of ideals will be especially important. While the Gröbner techniques we employ in this manuscript are well-known to algebraists, the direct application of these techniques to the study of the generalized tangent bundle is novel.

One surprising result is the connection of these compatibility conditions with Frobenius splitting. This positive characteristic property has implications for algebraic varieties defined over a field of characteristic 0, made precise with the notion of a Knutson ideal
[
Seccia
]
. In particular, a Frobenius split variety is reduced, has rational singularities, and has certain local cohomology vanishing properties
[
BK
]
. Many well-known determinantal varieties, such as Schubert varieties and Kazhdan-Lusztig varieties
[
BK
]
, certain
K
-orbit closures
[
BS
]
, and some Hessenberg varieties are Frobenius split
[
CDSHR
]
.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we collect some basic facts about the generalized tangent bundle and Gröbner bases to make the paper as self-contained as possible. In Section
3
, we translate the condition of tensoriality for expressions involving the endomorphisms and the Courant-Dorfman bracket into a statement about polynomial membership in a specific ideal. This ideal depends solely on the number of commuting endomorphisms and on their signature, which serves as a bookkeeping device to record which endomorphisms are symmetric and which are skew-symmetric. Section
4
contains our main result, namely the identification of the above mentioned cubic and quadratic generators of this ideal. In Section
5
, we specialize to the case in which all commuting endomorphisms are semisimple. With this assumption we are able to recast the vanishing of the tensorial ideal in terms of involutivity constraints on the common eigenbundles for the commuting endomorphisms. In the Appendix we give an alternate proof, also using Gröbner bases, of our main theorem in the special case where all commuting endomorphisms are symmetric.

2.1.
The Generalized Tangent Bundle

Throughout the paper,

i)

the

, defined as the projection map
\pi:\mathbb{T}M\rightarrow TM
;

ii)

the

\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
, which is the nondegenerate, symmetric
\Omega_{M}^{0}
-bilinear map defined by

\langle X+\alpha,Y+\beta\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\alpha(Y)+\beta(X)\right)

iii)

{[}\!{[}\cdot,\cdot{]}\!{]}:\Gamma(\mathbb{T}M)\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\Gamma(\mathbb{T}M)\rightarrow\Gamma(\mathbb{T}M)

{[}\!{[}X+\alpha,Y+\beta{]}\!{]}=[X,Y]+{\mathcal{L}}_{X}\beta-i_{Y}d\alpha,

for all

and

Here

is the Lie derivative along the vector field

]

(1)

\pi(\mathbf{a})\langle\mathbf{b},\mathbf{c}\rangle=\langle{[}\!{[}\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}{]}\!{]},\mathbf{c}\rangle+\langle{[}\!{[}\mathbf{a},\mathbf{c}{]}\!{]},\mathbf{b}\rangle=\langle{[}\!{[}\mathbf{b},\mathbf{c}{]}\!{]},\mathbf{a}\rangle+\langle{[}\!{[}\mathbf{c},\mathbf{b}{]}\!{]},\mathbf{a}\rangle\,,

for all
\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b},\mathbf{c}\in\Gamma(\mathbb{T}M)
, where
\pi(\mathbf{a})\langle\mathbf{b},\mathbf{c}\rangle
denotes the usual action of the vector field
\pi(\mathbf{a})
on the smooth function
\langle\mathbf{b},\mathbf{c}\rangle
.
Property (
1
) implies the Leibniz identities

(2)

{[}\!{[}\mathbf{a},f\mathbf{b}{]}\!{]}=f{[}\!{[}\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}{]}\!{]}+\pi(\mathbf{a})(f)\mathbf{b},\qquad{[}\!{[}f\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}{]}\!{]}=f{[}\!{[}\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}{]}\!{]}-\pi(\mathbf{b})(f)\mathbf{a}+2\langle\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\rangle df

for all
\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\in\Gamma(\mathbb{T}M)
and

.

Definition 1
.

Let
\Theta
be the
\Omega_{M}^{0}
-module of
\mathbb{R}
-trilinear forms

(3)

\tau:\Gamma(\mathbb{T}M)\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\Gamma(\mathbb{T}M)\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\Gamma(\mathbb{T}M)\rightarrow\Omega_{M}^{0}\,.

Distinguished among all elements of

is the

such that

(4)

\tau_{C}(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b},\mathbf{c})=\langle{[}\!{[}\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}{]}\!{]},\mathbf{c}\rangle

for all
\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b},\mathbf{c}\in\Gamma(\mathbb{T}M)
(see
[

]
).

2.2.
Commuting endomorphisms

Throughout the paper,
N
will denote a positive integer.

Definition 2
.

Let
\varepsilon=(\varepsilon_{1},\dots,\varepsilon_{N})
such that
\varepsilon_{i}\in\{\pm 1\}
. A
commuting family of signature

\varepsilon
is an ordered
N
-tuple
\phi=(\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{N})
such that, for all
i,j\in\{1,\dots,N\}

i)

\phi_{i}\in\Gamma({\rm End}(\mathbb{T}M))
;

ii)

\phi_{i}^{*}=\varepsilon_{i}\phi_{i}
;

iii)

\phi_{i}\phi_{j}=\phi_{j}\phi_{i}
.

Here
\phi^{*}
denotes the adjoint with respect to the tautological inner product. Therefore, if
\varepsilon_{i}=1
(resp.
\varepsilon_{i}=-1
) then
\phi_{i}
is symmetric (resp. skew-symmetric).

Example 3
.

Consider the case of two commuting generalized almost complex structures
\phi_{1}
,
\phi_{2}
on
M
. Since
\phi_{1}
and
\phi_{2}
are both skew-symmetric,
(\phi_{1},\phi_{2})
is a commuting family of signature
(-1,-1)
. Equivalently, the same information can be repackaged by defining the symmetric endomorphism
\mathcal{G}=\phi_{1}\phi_{2}
. Then the commuting pair
(\phi_{1},\mathcal{G})
has signature
(-1,1)
.

We will denote the set of all commuting families of signature
\varepsilon
by
\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}^{N}
(or
\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}
when
N
is understood). Note that
\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}^{N}
is an
\Omega_{M}^{0}
-module with respect to the usual operations on
N
-tuples.

2.3.

Throughout the paper, for every pair of

2.5.
Gröbner Bases

Many of the arguments in Section
4
and Appendix
A
will involve techniques from commutative algebra regarding
Gröbner bases
. In this section, we will briefly recall some of the fundamentals regarding Gröbner bases and refer the reader to
[
CLO
,
E
,
EH
]
for further details and background.

Let
R=\mathbb{K}[u_{1},\ldots,u_{r}]
be a polynomial ring with
\mathbb{K}
any field, and let
<
be a
monomial order
on
R
. A monomial order, which is a total order on the monic monomials of
R
respecting monomial multiplication, allows us to distinguish a leading monomial for any polynomial
f\in R
. More specifically, given a polynomial
f
, we will denote by
{\rm in}_{<}(f)
the term of
f
which is greater than all of the other terms of
f
with respect to
<
. As we do this for every polynomial in an ideal
I
of
R
, we obtain the
initial ideal
of
I
defined as the monomial ideal

{\rm in}_{<}(I)=\langle{\rm in}_{<}(f)|f\in I\rangle\,.

Many important algebro-geometric properties may be studied using
{\rm in}_{<}(I)
instead of
I
itself. For example, if
{\rm in}_{<}(I)
is a squarefree monomial ideal, then
I
is a radical ideal. In fact, many properties that are true for
{\rm in}_{<}(I)
(like being
Gorenstein, Cohen-Macaulay, smooth, factorial
, etc.) also hold for
I
. Since proving results for monomial ideals is often far easier than for general ideals, a common approach is to consider a problem from this perspective (often called a
Gröbner degeneration
). Furthermore, combinatorial representations of monomial ideals, like using simplicial complexes arising from the
Stanley-Reisner correspondence
, allow for combinatorial descriptions of ideal properties.

By Hilbert’s basis theorem
{\rm in}_{<}(I)
is finitely generated, leaving the question of how to describe a finite generating set for the ideal. This motivates the following definition.

Definition 6
.

Let
I\subset R
be an ideal and
<
a monomial order on
R
. Suppose that
\mathcal{G}=\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{k}\}
is a generating set of
I
. We say that
\mathcal{G}
is a Gröbner basis of
I
with respect to
<
if

{\rm in}_{<}(I)=\langle{\rm in}_{<}(f_{1}),\ldots,{\rm in}_{<}(f_{k})\rangle.

Example 7
.

Let
I=\langle x-y,x-z\rangle\subset\mathbb{K}[x,y,z]
, and let
<
be the lexicographic order
x>y>z
. Then
{\rm in}_{<}(x-y)={\rm in}_{<}(x-z)=x
, but it is not true that
{\rm in}_{<}(I)=\langle x\rangle
. Indeed,
y-z=(x-z)-(x-y)\in I
, but
{\rm in}_{<}(y-z)=y
.

This means that
\{x-y,x-z\}
is not a Gröbner basis of
I
with respect to
<
. If we however extend this list by including
y-z
, then we do get a Gröbner basis.

Given a generating set of
I
and a monomial order
<
, we can always compute a Gröbner basis for
I
, using
Buchberger’s algorithm
for instance (computationally faster techniques exist
[
BFS
]
, but Buchberger’s will be the most convenient for proving results). In the previous example, an issue arose where the cancellation of initial terms of
x
in
x-y
and
x-z
resulted in a polynomial
y-z
with a smaller initial term with respect to
<
. To systematically ensure that all such cancellation is considered, one arrives at the notion of an
S
-polynomial (also referred to as an
S
-pair). Given two polynomials
f
and
g
of
R
, we define

S(f,g):=\frac{{\rm in}_{<}(g)}{\mathrm{GCD}({\rm in}_{<}(f),{\rm in}_{<}(g))}f-\frac{{\rm in}_{<}(f)}{\mathrm{GCD}({\rm in}_{<}(f),{\rm in}_{<}(g))}g.

Buchberger’s algorithm can be summarized as follows:

i)

Given
I=\langle f_{1},\ldots,f_{k}\rangle
, compute
S(f_{i},f_{j})
for all pairs of generators with
i<j
.

ii)

Using multivariate polynomial division with respect to
<
and a fixed ordering of the list of generators
\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{k}\}
, write
S(f_{i},f_{j})=\sum a_{t}f_{t}+r_{i,j}
where no
{\rm in}_{<}(f_{i})
divides any term of the remainder
r_{i,j}
. This is called a
standard form
for
S(f_{i},f_{j})
.

iii)

The generating set
\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{k}\}
is a Gröbner basis with respect to
<
if and only if all of the
r_{i,j}=0
.

iv)

For all
i,j
for which
r_{i,j}\neq 0
, append
r_{i,j}
to the list of generators
\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{k}\}
, and repeat the process with the expanded list.

The algorithm always terminates after finitely many steps since the terms of
r_{i,j}
are strictly smaller than the initial terms of the current generating set.

3.
Tensoriality

Given a polynomial
P\in\mathcal{R}
, a commuting family
\phi
and an
\mathbb{R}
-trilinear form
\tau
on
\mathbb{T}M
, the polynomial action in Section
2.3
allows us to define a new
\mathbb{R}
-trilinear form
P\bullet_{\phi}\tau
. Note that, if
\tau
is
\Omega_{M}^{0}
-linear (or equivalently, a section of the vector bundle
(\mathbb{T}M\otimes\mathbb{T}M\otimes\mathbb{T}M)^{*}
), then so is
P\bullet_{\phi}\tau
. In this section we focus on the case when
\tau
is not
\Omega_{M}^{0}
-linear (specifically for
\tau=\tau_{C}
, the Courant element) and look for conditions on the pair
(P,\phi)
ensuring that
P\bullet_{\phi}\tau
is
\Omega_{M}^{0}
-linear.

Definition 8
.

Let
\phi\in\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}
, and
P\in\mathcal{R}
. The pair
(P,\phi)
is called
tensorial
if

P\bullet_{\phi}\tau_{C}\in\Gamma((\mathbb{T}M\otimes\mathbb{T}M\otimes\mathbb{T}M)^{*}).

Moreover, a polynomial
P\in\mathcal{R}
is said to be
universally tensorial of signature
\varepsilon
if
(P,\phi)
is a tensorial pair for all
\phi\in\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}
and all smooth manifolds
M
.

Example 9
.

Definition
8
generalizes the notion of a tensorial pair for a single endomorphism
\phi=(\phi_{1})
introduced in
[
AldiGrandini22
]
. In that case the Courant-Nijenhuis torsion
\mathcal{T}_{\phi_{1}}
satisfies

\langle\mathcal{T}_{\phi_{1}}(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}),\mathbf{c}\rangle=(P\bullet_{\phi}\tau_{C})(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b},\mathbf{c})

for all
\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b},\mathbf{c}\in\Gamma(\mathbb{T}M)

with
P=(x+z)(y+z)
. If
\phi_{1}
is a generalized almost complex structure, then
(P,\phi)
is tensorial even though
P
is not universally tensorial of signature
-1
. On the other hand, consider the
shifted Courant-Nijenhuis torsion

\mathcal{S}_{\phi_{1}}
defined by

\langle\mathcal{S}_{\phi_{1}}(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}),\mathbf{c}\rangle=\langle\mathcal{T}_{\phi_{1}}(\phi_{1}\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})+\mathcal{T}_{\phi_{1}}(\mathbf{a},\phi_{1}\mathbf{b}),\mathbf{c}\rangle=(S\bullet_{\phi}\tau_{C})(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b},\mathbf{c})

for all
\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b},\mathbf{c}\in\Gamma(\mathbb{T}M)
with
S=(x+z)(y+z)(x+y)
. Then
S
is universally tensorial of signature
-1
.

Example 10
.

Let
\phi=(\phi_{1},\phi_{2})
be a commuting pair of endomorphisms of the generalized tangent bundle. Then

(7)

\mathcal{K}_{\phi}(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})={[}\!{[}\phi_{1}\mathbf{a},\phi_{2}\mathbf{b}{]}\!{]}-\phi_{1}{[}\!{[}\mathbf{a},\phi_{2}\mathbf{b}{]}\!{]}-\phi_{2}{[}\!{[}\phi_{1}\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}{]}\!{]}+\phi_{1}\phi_{2}{[}\!{[}\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}{]}\!{]}

satisfies

\langle\mathcal{K}_{\phi}(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}),\mathbf{c}\rangle=(P\bullet_{\phi}\tau_{C})(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b},\mathbf{c})

with
P=(x_{1}-\varepsilon_{1}z_{1})(y_{2}-\varepsilon_{2}z_{2})
. While
P
is not universally tensorial of signature
\varepsilon
,
(P,\phi)
is a tensorial pair under the additional assumption that
\phi_{1}=\phi_{2}
is a generalized almost complex structure, in which case
\mathcal{K}_{\phi}
reduces to the Courant-Nijenhuis torsion of
\phi_{1}
. The symmetrization

\mathcal{K}_{(\phi_{1},\phi_{2})}+\mathcal{K}_{(\phi_{2},\phi_{1})}=\mathcal{T}_{\phi_{1}+\phi_{2}}-\mathcal{T}_{\phi_{1}}-\mathcal{T}_{\phi_{2}}

coincides with the
Nijenhuis concomitant
studied in
[
AntunesNunes13
]
and with the bivariate Courant-Nijenhuis torsion discussed in
[
AldiGrandini22
]
. For this reason we propose the terminology
semiconcomitant
for
\mathcal{K}_{\phi}
.

In light of Definition
8
, we can consider two natural ideals of
\mathcal{R}
:

i)

{\mathcal{I}}(\phi)
, consisting of all
P\in\mathcal{R}
such that
(P,\phi)
is a tensorial pair;

ii)

{\mathcal{I}}_{\varepsilon}
, consisting of the
universally tensorial polynomials of signature
\varepsilon
.

In the
N=1
case, the ideal
\mathcal{I}(\varphi)
was introduced in
[
AldiGrandini22
]
. We will now consider the general case.

Proposition 11
.

Let
P
be as in (
5
) and let
\phi\in\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}
. The pair
(P,\phi)
is tensorial
if and only if the following conditions are satisfied:

\displaystyle\sum_{I,J,T}\varepsilon^{J}a_{I,J,T-J}\pi\left(\phi^{I}(\mathbf{a})\right)(f)\phi^{T}=0

(8)

\displaystyle\sum_{I,J,T}\left(\varepsilon^{T-J}a_{T-J,I,J}\pi\left(\phi^{I}(\mathbf{b})\right)(f)\phi^{T}(\mathbf{c})\right)=\sum_{I,J,T}\left(\varepsilon^{J}a_{J,T-J,I}\pi\left(\phi^{I}(\mathbf{c})\right)(f)\phi^{T}(\mathbf{b})\right)\,.

Proof.

We adapt the proof of Proposition 8 in
[
AldiDaSilvaGrandini24
]
. Given
f\in\Omega_{M}^{0}
,

(P\bullet_{\phi}\tau_{C})(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b},f\mathbf{c})-f(P\bullet_{\phi}\tau_{C})(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b},\mathbf{c})

is equal to

\sum_{I,J,K}a_{I,J,K}\langle{[}\!{[}\phi^{I}\mathbf{a},\phi^{J}\mathbf{b}{]}\!{]},\phi^{K}(f\mathbf{c})\rangle-f\sum_{I,J,K}a_{I,J,K}\langle{[}\!{[}\phi^{I}\mathbf{a},\phi^{J}\mathbf{b}{]}\!{]},\phi^{K}\mathbf{c}\rangle

which vanishes by the
\Omega_{M}^{0}
-linearity of
\phi^{K}
and of the tautological inner product. Hence,
P\bullet_{\phi}\tau_{C}
is always
\Omega_{M}^{0}
-linear in the third variable. Moreover,

\displaystyle(P\bullet_{\phi}\tau_{C})(\mathbf{a},f\mathbf{b},\mathbf{c})-f(P\bullet_{\phi}\tau_{C})(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b},\mathbf{c})

\displaystyle=\sum_{I,J,K}a_{I,J,K}\pi(\phi^{I}\mathbf{a})(f)\langle\phi^{J}\mathbf{b},\phi^{K}\mathbf{c}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{I,J,K}\varepsilon^{J}a_{I,J,K}\pi(\phi^{I}\mathbf{a})(f)\langle\mathbf{b},\phi^{J+K}\mathbf{c}\rangle\,.

Using the substitution
T=J+K
, the above sum becomes

=\sum_{I,J,T}\varepsilon^{J}a_{I,J,T-J}\pi(\phi^{I}\mathbf{a})(f)\langle\mathbf{b},\phi^{T}\mathbf{c}\rangle

which is equal to
0
for all
\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b},\mathbf{c}\in\Gamma(\mathbb{T}M)
if and only if

\sum_{I,J,T}\varepsilon^{J}a_{I,J,T-J}\pi(\phi^{I}\mathbf{a})(f)\phi^{T}=0

for all
\mathbf{a}\in\Gamma(\mathbb{T}M)
. The calculation for the
\Omega_{M}^{0}
-linearity in the first variable is similar.

∎

Remark 12
.

The above construction can be extended to polynomials with nonconstant coefficients; that is, coefficients which are elements of the
\Omega_{M}^{0}
-module

\mathcal{R}(M):=\Omega_{M}^{0}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{R}.

In fact, the
\mathbb{R}
-linear action of
\mathcal{R}
on
\Theta
extends uniquely to an action of the module
\mathcal{R}(M)
on
\Theta
such that, for all
f\in\Omega_{M}^{0}
,
P\in\mathcal{R}
and
\tau\in\Theta
,

(f\otimes P)\bullet\tau=f\cdot(P\bullet\tau).

The notion of tensorial couple can be extended verbatim to polynomials in
\mathcal{R}(M)
. However, the notion of “universal tensoriality” cannot be transported to this extended context, but can be replaced by the following condition of
pointwise universal tensoriality
:

P_{p}\in\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}\mbox{ for all }p\in M\,.

It is straightforward to see that pointwise universally tensorial polynomials are simply the elements of

\Omega_{M}^{0}\otimes{\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}}.

Theorem 13
.

Let
P=\sum_{I,J,K}a_{IJK}x^{I}y^{J}z^{K}\in\mathcal{R}
. The following conditions are equivalent:

i)

P
is universally tensorial;

ii)

the following system of equations is satisfied:

(9)

\sum_{J}\varepsilon^{J}a_{I,J,T-J}=0,\quad\sum_{J}\varepsilon^{J}a_{J,T-J,I}=0,\quad\sum_{J}\varepsilon^{J}a_{T-J,I,J}=0

for all
I,T
;

iii)

the polynomial
P
vanishes on the following subvariety of
\mathbb{C}^{3N}
:

V_{\varepsilon}:=\left\{(x,y,z):y_{i}=\varepsilon_{i}z_{i}\ \forall i\right\}\cup\left\{(x,y,z):z_{i}=\varepsilon_{i}x_{i}\ \forall i\right\}\cup\left\{(x,y,z):x_{i}=\varepsilon_{i}y_{i}\ \forall i\right\}\,.

Proof.

We adapt the proof of Theorem 9 in
[
AldiDaSilvaGrandini24
]
. By substituting the condition of iii) into the expression for
P
we recover ii). Moreover, ii) coupled with Proposition
11
implies i). We are left to show that if
P
is universally tensorial, then it vanishes when restricted to
V_{\varepsilon}
. It suffices to look at a particular manifold
M
endowed with a commuting family
\phi
of endomorphisms of the generalized tangent bundle of signature
\varepsilon
such that each
\phi_{i}
maps vectors to vectors and with linearly independent vector fields
X,Y
such that
\phi_{i}(X)=\lambda_{i}X
and
\phi_{i}(Y)=\mu_{i}Y
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
. Then, by Proposition
11
,
P(\lambda,{\rm diag}(\varepsilon)\mu,\mu)X(f)Y=0
for all
f
and thus
P(\lambda,{\rm diag}(\varepsilon)\mu,\mu)=0
. The remaining cases are proved in a similar way.

∎

It follows from equations (
9
) that the ideal
{\mathcal{I}}_{\varepsilon}
is
S_{3}
-invariant. Also, it is preserved by the multi-grading, i.e.,

{\mathcal{I}}_{\varepsilon}=\bigoplus_{D}{\mathcal{I}}^{D}_{\varepsilon}

where
{\mathcal{I}}^{D}_{\varepsilon}={\mathcal{I}}_{\varepsilon}\cap\mathcal{R}^{D}
. It is also clear that the subspaces
{\mathcal{I}}^{D}_{\varepsilon}
are
S_{3}
-invariant as well.

Remark 14
.

From Theorem
13
follows that if
P\in\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}
and additionally
P
is invariant under the permutations of the letters
x,y,z
(that is,
\sigma P=P
for all

), then all tensors resulting from it are necessarily alternating. In fact, for all
\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b},\mathbf{c}\in\Gamma(\mathbb{T}M)
, define the
\mathbb{R}
-trilinear function
\theta(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b},\mathbf{c})=\pi(\mathbf{a})\langle\mathbf{b},\mathbf{c}\rangle
.
If
P
is as in (
5
) and
\phi\in\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}
, then we have

(10)

\left(P\bullet_{\phi}\theta\right)(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b},\mathbf{c})=\sum_{I,J,T}\varepsilon^{J}a_{I,J,T-J}\pi\left(\phi^{I}(\mathbf{a})\right)\langle\mathbf{b},\phi^{T}(\mathbf{c})\rangle\,.

On the other hand, since
P
is universally tensorial we can invoke Theorem
13
and obtain

\left(P\bullet_{\phi}\theta\right)(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b},\mathbf{c})=\sum_{I,T}\left(\sum_{J}\varepsilon^{J}a_{I,J,T-J}\right)\pi\left(\phi^{I}(\mathbf{a})\right)\langle\mathbf{b},\phi^{T}(\mathbf{c})\rangle=0.

Now, recall that

\left(P\bullet_{\phi}\tau_{C}\right)(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b},\mathbf{c})=\sum_{I,J,K}a_{I,J,K}\langle{[}\!{[}\phi^{I}\mathbf{a},\phi^{J}\mathbf{b}{]}\!{]},\phi^{K}\mathbf{c}\rangle\,,

so that from the identities (
1
) and the invariance of

follows that the quantity

\left(P\bullet_{\phi}\tau_{C}\right)(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b},\mathbf{c})+\left(P\bullet_{\phi}\tau_{C}\right)(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{c},\mathbf{b})

is equal to

\sum_{I,J,K}a_{I,J,K}\left(\langle{[}\!{[}\phi^{I}\mathbf{a},\phi^{J}\mathbf{b}{]}\!{]},\phi^{K}\mathbf{c}\rangle+\langle{[}\!{[}\phi^{I}\mathbf{a},\phi^{K}\mathbf{c}{]}\!{]},\phi^{J}\mathbf{b}\rangle\right)=\left(P\bullet_{\phi}\theta\right)(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b},\mathbf{c})\,.

Similarly,

\left(P\bullet_{\phi}\tau_{C}\right)(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b},\mathbf{c})+\left(P\bullet_{\phi}\tau_{C}\right)(\mathbf{b},\mathbf{a},\mathbf{c})=\left(P\bullet_{\phi}\theta\right)(\mathbf{c},\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})\,,

which is sufficient to conclude that the tensor
P\bullet_{\phi}\tau_{C}
is alternating. ∎

Example 15
.

Given a commuting family
\phi\in{\mathcal{F}}_{\varepsilon}
and

, consider the polynomial

(11)

T^{ijk}_{\varepsilon}=(x_{i}-\varepsilon_{i}y_{i})(y_{j}-\varepsilon_{j}z_{j})(z_{k}-\varepsilon_{k}x_{k})\,.

By Theorem

. Moreover,

\widetilde{{\mathcal{T}}}^{ijk}_{\phi}(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b},\mathbf{c}):=(T^{ijk}_{\varepsilon}\bullet_{\phi}\tau_{C})(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b},\mathbf{c})=\langle{\mathcal{T}}^{ijk}_{\phi}(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}),\mathbf{c}\rangle

for all sections

of the generalized tangent bundle, where

\displaystyle{\mathcal{T}}^{ijk}_{\phi}(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})

\displaystyle=\varepsilon_{k}\phi_{k}{[}\!{[}\phi_{i}\mathbf{a},\phi_{j}\mathbf{b}{]}\!{]}-\varepsilon_{k}\varepsilon_{i}\phi_{k}{[}\!{[}\mathbf{a},\phi_{i}\phi_{j}\mathbf{b}{]}\!{]}+\phi_{j}\phi_{k}{[}\!{[}\phi_{i}\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}{]}\!{]}+\varepsilon_{k}\varepsilon_{i}\phi_{j}\phi_{k}{[}\!{[}\mathbf{a},\phi_{i}\mathbf{b}{]}\!{]}+

Using the semiconcomitant introduced in Example

(12)

{\mathcal{T}}^{ijk}_{\phi}(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})=\varepsilon_{i}\varepsilon_{k}\mathcal{K}_{(\phi_{k},\phi_{j})}(\mathbf{a},\phi_{i}\mathbf{b})-\varepsilon_{k}\mathcal{K}_{(\phi_{k},\phi_{j})}(\phi_{i}\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})\,.

If

\mathcal{T}^{111}_{\phi}(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})=\mathcal{T}_{\phi_{1}}(\mathbf{a},\phi_{1}\mathbf{b})-\varepsilon_{1}\mathcal{T}_{\phi_{1}}(\phi_{1}\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})

If

\mathcal{T}_{\phi}^{lll}

\mathcal{T}_{\phi}^{mll}(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})=\varepsilon_{l}\varepsilon_{m}\mathcal{T}_{\phi_{l}}(\mathbf{a},\phi_{m}\mathbf{b})-\varepsilon_{l}\mathcal{T}_{\phi_{l}}(\phi_{m}\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})

4.
Tensorial Ideals

In the last section, we showed that a polynomial
P
is universally tensorial if and only if it vanishes on the set

V_{\varepsilon}
, by Theorem
13
. If we define the following ideals in
\mathcal{R}
,

\displaystyle I_{\varepsilon}^{x}

\displaystyle:=\langle y_{i}-\varepsilon_{i}z_{i}:\ i=1\dots N\rangle,

\displaystyle I_{\varepsilon}^{y}

\displaystyle:=\langle z_{i}-\varepsilon_{i}x_{i}:\ i=1\dots N\rangle,

\displaystyle I_{\varepsilon}^{z}

\displaystyle:=\langle x_{i}-\varepsilon_{i}y_{i}:\ i=1\dots N\rangle,

then by the Real Nullstellensatz
[
Powers22
, Theorem A.3]
we have

\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}=I(V_{\varepsilon})=\sqrt[\mathbb{R}]{I_{\varepsilon}^{x}\cap I_{\varepsilon}^{y}\cap I_{\varepsilon}^{z}}\,,

where
\sqrt[\mathbb{R}]{J}
denotes the real radical of an ideal
J
. It is easy to see that the
S_{3}
-action on the variables permutes these ideals and that each ideal is a prime ideal (indeed,
\mathcal{R}/I_{\varepsilon}^{x}
is isomorphic to a polynomial ring in fewer variables). First we will show that
I_{\varepsilon}^{x}\cap I_{\varepsilon}^{y}\cap I_{\varepsilon}^{z}
is a real radical ideal.

Lemma 17
.

Let
I_{\varepsilon}^{x},I_{\varepsilon}^{y}
and
I_{\varepsilon}^{z}
be defined as above for some fixed integer
N>0
. Then

\sqrt[\mathbb{R}]{I_{\varepsilon}^{x}\cap I_{\varepsilon}^{y}\cap I_{\varepsilon}^{z}}=I_{\varepsilon}^{x}\cap I_{\varepsilon}^{y}\cap I_{\varepsilon}^{z}
.

Proof.

By
[
Sch
, Definition 3.2.13]
, since

I_{\varepsilon}^{x}\cap I_{\varepsilon}^{y}\cap I_{\varepsilon}^{z}\subseteq\sqrt[\mathbb{R}]{I_{\varepsilon}^{x}\cap I_{\varepsilon}^{y}\cap I_{\varepsilon}^{z}}=\displaystyle\bigcap_{\begin{subarray}{c}{I_{\varepsilon}^{x}\cap I_{\varepsilon}^{y}\cap I_{\varepsilon}^{z}\subseteq J}\\
\text{$J$ is prime and real}\end{subarray}}\hskip-17.07164ptJ,

it suffices to show that
I_{\varepsilon}^{x}
,
I_{\varepsilon}^{y}
and
I_{\varepsilon}^{z}
are real ideals (we showed that they are prime above). By
[
Sch
, Definition 3.2.13]
, a prime ideal
J\subset R
is a real ideal if the field of fractions of
R/J
is a real field (i.e. the residue field of
J
can be ordered). The quotient
\mathcal{R}/I_{\varepsilon}^{x}
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{N},z_{1},\ldots,z_{N}]
whose field of fractions is the field of rational functions (which is known to be an ordered field). A similar statement holds for
I_{\varepsilon}^{y}
and
I_{\varepsilon}^{z}
. Therefore,
\sqrt[\mathbb{R}]{I_{\varepsilon}^{x}\cap I_{\varepsilon}^{y}\cap I_{\varepsilon}^{z}}
is contained in
I_{\varepsilon}^{x},I_{\varepsilon}^{y}
and
I_{\varepsilon}^{z}
, proving the result.

∎

We have reduced finding a generating set of
\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}
to finding a generating set for an intersection of ideals. In Gröbner theory, a common way to compute the intersection of two ideals
I,J\subset R=\mathbb{K}[u_{1},\ldots,u_{r}]
is to compute a Gröbner basis
\mathcal{G}
for the ideal
T=tI+(1-t)J\subset R[t]
with respect to an elimination order on
R[t]
. Since
I\cap J=T\cap R
, the elements in
\mathcal{G}
that don’t involve the variable
t
generate
I\cap J
. See
[
E
, Section 15.10.4, Exercise 15.43]
for further details.

Using this method to compute
I_{\varepsilon}^{x}\cap I_{\varepsilon}^{y}\cap I_{\varepsilon}^{z}
directly for general
N
is cumbersome. In particular, the process of finding a Gröbner basis for
J_{\varepsilon}=tI_{\varepsilon}^{x}+(1-t)I_{\varepsilon}^{y}\cap I_{\varepsilon}^{z}
involves also computing a Gröbner basis for
I_{\varepsilon}^{x}\cap I_{\varepsilon}^{y}\cap I_{\varepsilon}^{z}
, which is stronger than simply finding a generating set for it. Indeed, finding a conjectural Gröbner basis for
J_{\varepsilon}
or even
I_{\varepsilon}^{x}\cap I_{\varepsilon}^{y}\cap I_{\varepsilon}^{z}
was not immediately straightforward and the
S
-polynomial computations quickly became challenging. Instead, we will take advantage of the inductive definition of
I_{\varepsilon}^{x}
,
I_{\varepsilon}^{y}
and
I_{\varepsilon}^{z}
to reduce the computation of a generating set to the
N=6
case, and then check this case for all signatures by computer.

We will proceed by introducing and subsequently applying some commutative algebra techniques that will hopefully prove useful in other geometric contexts. In Subsection
4.1
, we start by describing the intersection of any two of the ideals
I_{\varepsilon}^{x},I_{\varepsilon}^{y}
and
I_{\varepsilon}^{z}
using a lesser known technique called Frobenius splitting
[
Knutson
]
. We will then use Gröbner theory and the reduction described above to find a generating set for
\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}
in Subsection
4.2
. We also provide a Gröbner basis for
I_{\varepsilon}^{x}\cap I_{\varepsilon}^{y}\cap I_{\varepsilon}^{z}
in the special case where
\varepsilon=(1,1,\ldots,1)
in Appendix
A
, introducing tools that may be useful for subsequent research on this topic.

4.1.
Knutson ideals

In this section we will prove that
I_{\varepsilon}^{x}I_{\varepsilon}^{z}=I_{\varepsilon}^{x}\cap I_{\varepsilon}^{z}
, which simplifies computations later. As highlighted in the last section, there are multiple general approaches to computing an intersection of two ideals, but because of the very specific linear form of the generators of each ideal, we will use the method called Frobenius splitting whose technical details, such as the issue of passing from positive characteristic to characteristic zero, can be conveniently described using Knutson ideals
[
Seccia
]
.

Definition 18
.

Let
f\in R=\mathbb{K}[u_{1},\ldots,u_{r}]
be a polynomial such that
{\rm in}_{<}(f)
is a squarefree monomial for some term order
<
on
R
. Recall that given ideals
I,J
in a commutative ring
A
,
(I:J)
denotes the ideal of all
a\in A
such that
aJ\subseteq I
. Define
\mathcal{C}_{f}
to be the smallest set of ideals satisfying the following conditions:

(1)

\langle f\rangle\in\mathcal{C}_{f}
,

(2)

if
I\in\mathcal{C}_{f}
then
(I:J)\in\mathcal{C}_{f}
for every
J\subseteq R
,

(3)

if
I
and
J
are in
\mathcal{C}_{f}
, then
I+J
and
I\cap J
are in
\mathcal{C}_{f}
.

If
I
is an ideal in
\mathcal{C}_{f}
, we say that
I
is a
Knutson ideal associated to
f
.

Theorem 19
.

[
Seccia
, Main Theorem 1]
Let
R=\mathbb{K}[u_{1},\ldots,u_{n}]
be a polynomial ring over any field
\mathbb{K}
and let
f
be a polynomial in
R
such that
{\rm in}_{<}(f)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}
with respect to some term order
<
. If
I\in\mathcal{C}_{f}
, then
{\rm in}_{<}(I)
is squarefree, and hence
I
is radical.

Define the polynomial

F_{\varepsilon}^{xz}=\prod_{i=1}^{N}(x_{i}-\varepsilon_{i}y_{i})(y_{i}-\varepsilon_{i}z_{i})z_{i}\in\mathcal{R}\,.

Under the monomial order
x_{N}>y_{N}>z_{N}>x_{N-1}>y_{N-1}>z_{N-1}>\cdots>x_{1}>y_{1}>z_{1}
, we see that

{\rm in}_{<}(F_{\varepsilon}^{xz})=\prod_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}y_{i}z_{i}.

Theorem 20
.

The ideals
I_{\varepsilon}^{x}
,
I_{\varepsilon}^{z}
and
I_{\varepsilon}^{x}I_{\varepsilon}^{z}
are Knutson ideals in
\mathcal{C}_{F_{\varepsilon}^{xz}}
.

Proof.

Since
{\rm in}_{<}(F_{\varepsilon}^{xz})
is squarefree, we may consider the set of Knutson ideals defined by
F_{\varepsilon}^{xz}
. Each factor of
F_{\varepsilon}^{xz}
defines a principal Knutson ideal in
\mathcal{C}_{F_{\varepsilon}^{xz}}
by property
(2)
of Definition
18
. In particular,
\langle x_{i}-\varepsilon_{i}y_{i}\rangle
and
\langle y_{i}-\varepsilon_{i}z_{i}\rangle
are Knutson ideals for
i=1,\ldots,N
. By property
(3)
of Definition
18
, intersections and sums of Knutson ideals are also Knutson ideals. Therefore,
\langle x_{i}-\varepsilon_{i}y_{i}\rangle\cap\langle y_{j}-\varepsilon_{j}z_{j}\rangle=\langle(x_{i}-\varepsilon_{i}y_{i})(y_{j}-\varepsilon_{j}z_{j})\rangle
is a Knutson ideal for every pair
i,j
. This follows from the fact that the intersection of finitely many principal ideals in a unique factorization domain is again a principal ideal (generated by the least common multiple of the irreducible factors
[
AldiDaSilvaGrandini24
, Lemma 13]
). Since
I_{\varepsilon}^{x}I_{\varepsilon}^{z}
can be written as a sum of Knutson ideals, namely

I_{\varepsilon}^{x}I_{\varepsilon}^{z}=\displaystyle\sum_{i,j}\langle(x_{i}-\varepsilon_{i}y_{i})(y_{j}-\varepsilon_{j}z_{j})\rangle,

then it is Knutson too. Similarly,
I_{\varepsilon}^{x}
and
I_{\varepsilon}^{z}
are Knutson since they are also a sum of Knutson ideals.
∎

Corollary 21
.

I_{\varepsilon}^{x}I_{\varepsilon}^{z}=I_{\varepsilon}^{x}\cap I_{\varepsilon}^{z}
,
I_{\varepsilon}^{x}I_{\varepsilon}^{y}=I_{\varepsilon}^{x}\cap I_{\varepsilon}^{y}
, and
I_{\varepsilon}^{y}I_{\varepsilon}^{z}=I_{\varepsilon}^{y}\cap I_{\varepsilon}^{z}
.

Proof.

From the cyclic shift in variables (including in the monomial order)
x_{i}\rightarrow y_{i}\rightarrow z_{i}\rightarrow x_{i}
, it’s sufficient to prove only the first identity.

By Theorem
19
, since
{\rm in}_{<}(F_{\varepsilon}^{xz})=\prod_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}y_{i}z_{i}
, all of the Knutson ideals in
C_{F_{\varepsilon}^{xz}}
are radical. Therefore,

I_{\varepsilon}^{x}I_{\varepsilon}^{z}=\sqrt{I_{\varepsilon}^{x}I_{\varepsilon}^{z}}=\sqrt{I_{\varepsilon}^{x}\cap I_{\varepsilon}^{z}}=\sqrt{I_{\varepsilon}^{x}}\cap\sqrt{I_{\varepsilon}^{z}}=I_{\varepsilon}^{x}\cap I_{\varepsilon}^{z}\,.

∎

By
[
Knutson

I_{\varepsilon}^{x}I_{\varepsilon}^{z}
are also a Gröbner bases for the ideal with respect to this monomial ordering, providing another proof of some of the statements in Proposition

in the Appendix. In fact, the entire poset of Knutson ideals in

4.2.
Generating
\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}

We are now prepared to describe a generating set for
\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}=I_{\varepsilon}^{x}\cap I_{\varepsilon}^{y}\cap I_{\varepsilon}^{z}
in terms of the polynomials
T^{ijk}_{\varepsilon}
,
P^{ij}_{\varepsilon}\in\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}
introduced in, respectively, (
11
) and (
16
). Computing a generating set for
I_{\varepsilon}^{x}\cap I_{\varepsilon}^{y}\cap I_{\varepsilon}^{z}
involves computing a Gröbner basis for

J_{\varepsilon}=tI_{\varepsilon}^{x}+(1-t)I_{\varepsilon}^{y}I_{\varepsilon}^{z}

using the fact that
I_{\varepsilon}^{y}I_{\varepsilon}^{z}=I_{\varepsilon}^{y}\cap I_{\varepsilon}^{z}
. When computing a Gröbner basis for
J_{\varepsilon}
, an elimination order is needed where the variable
t
has the highest weight. For this section, we will use the lexicographic order
<_{N}
defined by

t>x_{N}>y_{N}>z_{N}>x_{N-1}>y_{N-1}>z_{N-1}>\cdots>x_{1}>y_{1}>z_{1}.

In applying Buchberger’s algorithm to the ideal
J_{\varepsilon}
, we start with a generating set
\mathcal{G}
of
J_{\varepsilon}
, compute
S
-polynomials between elements of
\mathcal{G}
, reduce by
\mathcal{G}
using the division algorithm to get a remainder, and adjoin the remainder to
\mathcal{G}
if nonzero (see Section
2.5
for details). An important observation is that any element of
\mathcal{G}
at any iteration of Buchberger’s algorithm applied to
J_{\varepsilon}
with
<_{N}
will only involve three indices. We will say that a polynomial
f
is
defined by at most three indices
if there exists
i,j
and
k
such that
f\in\mathbb{R}[t,x_{i},y_{i},z_{i},x_{j},y_{j},z_{j},x_{k},y_{k},z_{k}]
.

The key to the argument that follows is to show that the division algorithm applied to any
S
-polynomial involving two generators defined by some fixed subset
\mathfrak{I}
of indices
i,j,k,r,s,t
will only involve that set of indices. Consider two polynomials
g^{ijk}
and
g^{rst}
, each defined by at most three indices, with
S
-polynomial

S(g^{ijk},g^{rst})=\frac{{\rm in}_{<_{N}}(g^{rst})}{\mathrm{GCD}({\rm in}_{<_{N}}(g^{ijk}),{\rm in}_{<_{N}}(g^{rst}))}g^{ijk}-\frac{{\rm in}_{<_{N}}(g^{ijk})}{\mathrm{GCD}({\rm in}_{<_{N}}(g^{ijk}),{\rm in}_{<_{N}}(g^{rst}))}g^{rst}.

We label the reduced polynomial on the right hand side by
f_{\mathfrak{I}}
. The division algorithm sequentially checks whether the lead term of
f_{\mathfrak{I}}
is divisible by the lead term of each element in the current list of polynomials
\mathcal{G}
from Buchberger’s algorithm. We claim that any element of
\mathcal{G}
whose lead term divides the lead term of
f_{\mathfrak{I}}
must use only indices in
\mathfrak{I}
. A priori, it is possible that the division algorithm uses elements
g\in\mathcal{G}
whose lead term has indices in
\mathfrak{I}
, but which involves other terms with indices not in
\mathfrak{I}
. We can show that there is an ordering of the generators in
\mathcal{G}
where this does not happen. We will do this be converting the problem into one where
\mathfrak{I}\subseteq\{1,2,3,4,5,6\}
, as demonstrated in the next example.

Example 23
.

Given two generators of the ordered list
\mathcal{G}
involving at most three indices, define an order preserving bijection between the indices
i,j,k,r,s,t
(where some indices may be equal) with a subset of the indices
\{1,2,3,4,5,6\}
.

For instance, if we are considering
S(T^{487}_{\varepsilon},T^{289}_{\varepsilon})
, then
\mathfrak{I}=\{2,4,7,8,9\}
, which is in bijection with
\{1,2,3,4,5\}
via any permutation
\sigma\in S_{9}
which sends
2
to
1
,
4
to
2
,
7
to
3
,
8
to
4
and
9
to
5
. We apply
\sigma
to a polynomial by applying it to the indices of the variables in its support. Let
\varepsilon_{\sigma}
be the signature of length five where entry
k
in
\varepsilon_{\sigma}
is equal to entry
\sigma^{-1}(k)
in
\varepsilon
. For the
S
-polynomial, we get

S(T^{487}_{\varepsilon},T^{289}_{\varepsilon})=\sigma^{-1}(S(T^{243}_{\varepsilon_{\sigma}},T^{145}_{\varepsilon_{\sigma}})).

If
\sum f_{i}g_{j}+r\in\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\ldots,z_{5}]
is the standard form for
S(T^{243}_{\varepsilon_{\sigma}},T^{145}_{\varepsilon_{\sigma}})
with respect to
\sigma(\mathcal{G})
, then the hope is that

\sigma^{-1}(\sum f_{i}g_{j}+r)=\sum\sigma^{-1}(f_{i})\sigma^{-1}(g_{j})+\sigma^{-1}(r)

is the standard form for
S(T^{487}_{\varepsilon},T^{289}_{\varepsilon})
with respect to
\mathcal{G}
. There are several sufficient conditions to guarantee that we do get a standard form:

•

Taking initial terms and applying
\sigma
should commute. That is,
{\rm in}_{<_{5}}(\sigma(f))=\sigma({\rm in}_{<_{9}}(f))
so that applying
\sigma^{-1}
results in a term which is actually a leading term.

•

The indices appearing in the initial term of an element of
g\in\mathcal{G}
should be the same as the indices appearing in
g
. This is to prevent issues with two elements having the same initial term but involve a different set of indices in their remaining terms.

•

Each element of
\mathcal{G}
should involve at most three indices. This ensures that we only need to reduce to the
N=6
case for each
S
-polynomial computation.

•

\sigma^{-1}(g_{i})
must appear before
\sigma^{-1}(g_{j})
in the ordered list of generators
\mathcal{G}
if
i<j
and
g_{i},g_{j}
are in the ordered list for the current stage of Buchberger’s algorithm for the
N=6
case. Polynomial division depends on the ordering of the polynomials being divided (except when that list of polynomials is already a Gröbner basis).

Assuming that these conditions are met (and we will prove that this is enough below), then the standard form in the indices
\{1,2,3,4,5\}
provides a standard form for the original
S
-polynomial in the indices
\{2,4,7,8,9\}
.

Therefore, if all
S
-polynomials reduce to
0
for the indices
\{1,2,3,4,5,6\}
, then all
S
-polynomials reduce to
0
for all arbitrary pairs of generators in general indices. Since we have verified the
N=6
case by computer for all signatures, the result follows.

Theorem 26
.

Fix
N\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
and a signature
\varepsilon
. Let
I_{\varepsilon}^{x},I_{\varepsilon}^{y},
and
I_{\varepsilon}^{z}
be ideals of
\mathcal{R}
as defined above, endowed with the monomial order
<_{N}
. Then

\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon}=\{T^{ijk}_{\varepsilon}|1\leq i,j,k\leq N\}\cup\{P^{ij}_{\varepsilon}|\varepsilon_{i}=\varepsilon_{j}=1\}

is a generating set of
\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}=I_{\varepsilon}^{x}\cap I_{\varepsilon}^{y}\cap I_{\varepsilon}^{z}
.

Proof.

The ideal
J_{\varepsilon}^{N}=tI_{\varepsilon}^{x}+(1-t)I_{\varepsilon}^{y}I_{\varepsilon}^{z}
is generated by the polynomials
\{t(y_{i}-\varepsilon_{i}z_{i}),(1-t)(z_{i}-\varepsilon_{i}x_{i})(x_{j}-\varepsilon_{j}y_{j})\}_{i,j}
where
1\leq i,j\leq N
. Denote this generating set by
\mathcal{G}_{N}^{1}(\varepsilon)=\{g_{1},\ldots,g_{n_{1}}\}
, ordered as in the proof of Lemma
25
. Consider Buchberger’s algorithm applied to
\mathcal{G}_{N}^{1}(\varepsilon)
. Any
S
-polynomial between
g_{i}
and
g_{j}
will involve no more than four indices (in fact, no more than six in the general inductive step). There is an order preserving bijection
\sigma
between these
r
indices and the set
\{1,\ldots,r\}
. As in Example
23
, let
\varepsilon_{\sigma}
be a signature defined from
\varepsilon
and
\sigma
, and let
\sigma(g_{i})=\bar{g_{i}}
be defined by applying
\sigma
to the subscript/index of each indeterminate. Then

S(g_{i},g_{j})=\sigma^{-1}(\bar{S}(\bar{g_{i}},\bar{g_{j}}))

where
\bar{S}(\bar{g_{i}},\bar{g_{j}})\in\mathcal{R}_{6}=\mathbb{R}[x_{1},y_{1},z_{1},\ldots,x_{6},y_{6},z_{6}]
. Performing polynomial division with respect to
\mathcal{G}^{1}_{6}(\varepsilon_{\sigma})
allows us to write

\bar{S}(\bar{g_{i}},\bar{g_{j}})=\sum_{\alpha}f_{\alpha}h_{\alpha}+r_{i,j}

where
h_{\alpha}\in\mathcal{G}^{1}_{6}(\varepsilon_{\sigma})
and
f_{\alpha},r_{i,j}\in\mathcal{R}_{6}
. By definition of
J_{\varepsilon}^{N}
,
\sigma^{-1}(h_{\alpha})\in\mathcal{G}_{N}^{1}(\varepsilon)
. Then

S(g_{i},g_{j})=\sum_{\alpha}\sigma^{-1}(f_{\alpha})\sigma^{-1}(h_{\alpha})+\sigma^{-1}(r_{i,j}).

We claim that this is the standard form for
S(g_{i},g_{j})
with respect to
\mathcal{G}_{N}^{1}(\varepsilon)
. By definition of
<_{N}
, we have
{\rm in}_{<_{N}}(\sigma^{-1}(f))=\sigma^{-1}({\rm in}_{<_{6}}(f))
for any
f\in\mathcal{R}_{6}
. Assume that for some
k
,
{\rm in}_{<_{N}}(g_{k})
divides some term of
\sigma^{-1}(r_{i,j})
. This would imply that
{\rm in}_{<_{6}}(\bar{g_{k}})
divides some term of
r_{i,j}
since
{\rm in}_{<_{N}}(g_{k})={\rm in}_{<_{N}}(\sigma^{-1}(\bar{g_{k}}))=\sigma^{-1}({\rm in}_{<_{6}}(\bar{g_{k}}))
, a contradiction. So
\sigma^{-1}(r_{i,j})
satisfies the conditions of a remainder term.

Next, assume that
g_{A}
is the first element in the ordered list
\mathcal{G}_{N}^{1}(\varepsilon)
such that
{\rm in}_{<_{N}}(g_{A})
divides the initial term of
S(g_{i},g_{j})
, but that
\sigma(g_{A})
is not the first element of
\mathcal{G}^{1}_{6}(\varepsilon_{\sigma})
whose initial term divides
\bar{S}(\bar{g_{i}},\bar{g_{j}})
. Then there is some element
g_{B}
appearing before
g_{A}
in
\mathcal{G}^{1}_{6}(\varepsilon_{\sigma})
where
{\rm in}_{<_{6}}(g_{B})
divides
{\rm in}_{<_{6}}(\bar{S}(\bar{g_{i}},\bar{g_{j}}))
. This contradicts the consistent ordering of
\mathcal{G}_{N}^{1}(\varepsilon)
described in Lemma
25
. In particular, if
g_{A}
uses one index, then
g_{B}
uses one index, and
\sigma
preserves the index ordering, so
g_{B}
appearing before
g_{A}
in
\mathcal{G}^{1}_{6}(\varepsilon_{\sigma})
implies that
\sigma^{-1}(g_{B})
appears before
\sigma^{-1}(g_{A})
in
\mathcal{G}_{N}^{1}(\varepsilon)
. A similar statement holds for more indices.

Finally, assume that in performing the polynomial division on
S(g_{i},g_{j})
, there is some element
g_{C}
such that
{\rm in}_{<_{N}}(g_{C})
divides the current polynomial, but
g_{C}
uses indices other than the indices appearing in the original
S(g_{i},g_{j})
. In this case, it may be possible that new indices appear in the division algorithm which don’t match the indices
\sigma^{-1}(\{1,2,3,4,5,6\})
appearing in
\sigma^{-1}(\bar{S}(\bar{g_{i}},\bar{g_{j}}))
. Since
g_{C}
uses at most 3 indices, there is some order preserving bijection
\pi
from these indices to a subset of
\{1,2,3\}
. By Lemma
25
,
\pi(g_{C})
has the property that the indices of
g_{C}
are the same as the indices of its initial term under
<_{3}
. Since
{\rm in}_{<_{N}}(g_{C})
divides a term with indices in
\sigma^{-1}(\{1,2,3,4,5,6\})
, all of
g_{C}
must be defined in these indices too, a contradiction.

This proves that the multivariate polynomial division algorithm applied to
\mathcal{G}_{N}^{1}(\varepsilon)
with respect to
<_{N}
produces a standard form which is in bijection through
\sigma
with the standard form coming from
\mathcal{G}_{6}^{1}(\varepsilon_{\sigma})
.

To complete the first step of Buchberger’s algorithm, perform this polynomial division for all pairs
i,j
with
i<j
. Create an extended list of polynomials
\mathcal{G}_{N}^{2}(\varepsilon)
by adjoining to the list
\mathcal{G}_{N}^{1}(\varepsilon)
any non-zero remainder term
\sigma^{-1}(r_{i,j})
(here
\sigma
actually varies based on the indices, but we will abuse notation and use
\sigma
to represent the general situation). This list should be ordered so that elements with fewer indices appear first, and
(i,j,k)
appears before
(i^{\prime},j^{\prime},k^{\prime})
if
i<i^{\prime}
, or
j<j^{\prime}
if
i=i^{\prime}
, or
k<k^{\prime}
if both
i=i^{\prime}
and
j=j^{\prime}
. In the case that there is a set of two or more elements that use the same set of indices
\{i,j,k\}
(but possibly in different sets of variables), then order this set so that
f
appears before
h
if
\sigma(f)
appears before
\sigma(h)
in
\mathcal{G}_{6}^{2}(\varepsilon_{\sigma})
. This ensures consistency with the list being produced in the
N=6
code computation.

By Lemma
25
,
r_{i,j}
is defined by at most three indices, and therefore
\sigma^{-1}(r_{i,j})
is too. Then
\mathcal{G}_{N}^{2}(\varepsilon)
consists of polynomials which are each defined by at most three indices. This is important since all
S
-pairs will involve at most six indices, allowing the computation to be reduced to the
N=6
case. Continuing the process, we can successively compute each
\mathcal{G}^{\ell}_{N}(\varepsilon)
, until all remainders
r_{i,j}
are
0
(which is guaranteed by Buchberger’s algorithm). The terminal list
\mathcal{G}_{N}^{L}(\varepsilon)
will be a Gröbner basis for
J_{\varepsilon}^{N}
with respect to
<_{N}
. The subset of elements
\mathcal{H}
in this list which do not involve
t
will be a Gröbner basis for
\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}
(that is
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{G}_{N}^{L}(\varepsilon)\cap\mathcal{R}
).

The result now follows by Lemma
25
since for any
h\in\mathcal{H}
,
\sigma(h)
can be written as a combination of
T^{ijk}_{\varepsilon_{\sigma}}
and
P^{ij}_{\varepsilon_{\sigma}}
for
N=6
, which means that
h
can be written as a combination of
\sigma^{-1}(T^{ijk}_{\varepsilon_{\sigma}})=T^{\sigma^{-1}(i)\sigma^{-1}(j)\sigma^{-1}(k)}_{\varepsilon}
and
\sigma^{-1}(P^{ij}_{\varepsilon_{\sigma}})=P^{\sigma^{-1}(i)\sigma^{-1}(j)}_{\varepsilon}
, proving that the set
\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon}
is a generating set of
\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}
.
∎

Remark 27
.

The proof of Theorem
26
shows that the Gröbner basis computation of
J_{\varepsilon}^{N}
can be reduced to a series of independent Gröbner basis computations for a fixed set of at most six indices
\mathcal{J}
and fixed signature
\varepsilon_{\sigma}
. The entire argument works because for a fixed
\mathcal{J}
, the
S
-pair computations occurring between the polynomials of
\mathcal{G}^{\ell}_{N}
with indices in
\mathcal{J}
are in bijection with the
S
-pair computations occurring in
\mathcal{G}_{6}^{\ell}(\varepsilon_{\sigma})
. This reduces the entire Gröbner basis computation to repeated independent copies of the Gröbner basis computation of
J_{\varepsilon}^{6}
with varying signatures
\varepsilon
. Therefore, Theorem
26
is a careful bookkeeping argument to show this reduction.

As a final note, for the same reasons as described in the previous remark, finding a Gröbner basis of
\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}
can be reduced to finding a Gröbner basis for the
N=6
case. We investigate this question in Appendix
A
. Although we did not need an explicit Gröbner basis to provide a generating set of
\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}
, we did briefly explore this question, and with respect to
<_{N}
there were additional polynomials besides
T^{ijk}_{\varepsilon}
and
P^{ij}_{\varepsilon}
that were needed in the Gröbner basis. For the signature with all
-1
’s, these elements could be viewed as the determinant of a certain
3\times 3
matrix. Unfortunately we could not give a general description for other signatures, although the extra elements appeared to be determinantal in nature. This could be the topic of future research, especially if Gröbner degeneration techniques prove useful in studying tensorial constraints in generalized geometry.

5.
Uniform Integrability

Given a commuting family
\phi
, we can now consider the set
\mathcal{Z}(\phi)
of all
P\in\mathcal{R}
such that
P\bullet_{\phi}\tau_{C}
vanishes identically.
It is straightforward to verify that
\mathcal{Z}(\phi)
is an ideal of
\mathcal{R}
contained in
\mathcal{I}(\phi)
. We will say that
\phi
is
uniformly integrable
if
\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}\subseteq\mathcal{Z}(\phi)
.

5.1.
Semisimple families

Following
[
AldiGrandini22
]
, we say that an endomorphism
\phi
of the generalized tangent bundle is
semisimple
if there is a finite set
\Sigma
of complex numbers and a decomposition
\mathbb{T}M\otimes\mathbb{C}\cong\bigoplus_{\lambda\in\Sigma}L_{\lambda}
into eigenbundles
L_{\lambda}=\ker(\phi-\lambda I)
of (the complexification of)

Theorem 29

A semisimple commuting family

\displaystyle\|\lambda-{\rm diag}(\varepsilon)(\mu)\|\cdot{[}\!{[}\Gamma(L_{\lambda}),\Gamma(L_{\mu}){]}\!{]}\subseteq\Gamma(L_{\lambda}+L_{\mu}),

A commuting family

For instance, let

Appendix A
An Alternate Approach Using Gröbner Basis Techniques

In this appendix, we will explore an alternate technique that could be used to compute
\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}
. We use this method to show that the set of generators
P^{ij}_{\varepsilon}
and
T^{ijk}_{\varepsilon}
form a Gröbner basis for the signature
\varepsilon=(1,1,\ldots,1)
, allowing us deduce other properties of the ideal coming from the Gröbner degeneration.

A.1.
Gröbner Squeeze Theorem

We will begin with a simplification that reduces the computation of a generating set for the intersection of
I_{\varepsilon}^{x},I_{\varepsilon}^{y}
and
I_{\varepsilon}^{z}
in terms of a Gröbner basis computation for the intersection of any two of the three ideals. We start by providing the required technical results to make this reduction precise, and then we proceed to compute specific cases in the next subsection.

Lemma 40
.

[
EH
, Problem 2.8]
Let
<
be a monomial order on a polynomial ring
S
. Suppose that
I\subseteq J
are ideals of
S
such that
{\rm in}_{<}(I)={\rm in}_{<}(J)
. Then
I=J
.

Suppose that we have an intersection of ideals
I_{1}\cap\cdots\cap I_{r}
for which we do not have a generating set, and a conjectured Gröbner basis for that intersection, say
\mathcal{G}=\{g_{1},\ldots,g_{n}\}
. If we can find another ideal
J
which contains
I_{1}\cap\cdots\cap I_{r}
and has an initial ideal equal to the monomial ideal generated by the initial terms of the
g_{i}
, then
\mathcal{G}
is a Gröbner basis for the intersection. The next proposition, in the special cases where it applies, allows us to bypass the need to compute
S
-polynomials for
I_{1}\cap\cdots\cap I_{r}
if we can find a clever containment of ideals for which computing the Gröbner basis is easier.

Proposition 41

(Gröbner Squeeze Theorem)
.

Let
<
be a monomial order on a polynomial ring
S
. Let
\mathcal{G}=\{g_{1},\ldots,g_{n}\}\subset I_{1}\cap\cdots\cap I_{r}\subset S
. Suppose that
J_{1},\ldots J_{k}
are ideals in
S
such that
I_{1}\cap\cdots\cap I_{r}\subseteq J_{1}\cap\ldots\cap J_{k}
and for which

\langle{\rm in}_{<}(g_{1}),\ldots,{\rm in}_{<}(g_{n})\rangle=\bigcap_{i=1}^{k}{\rm in}_{<}(J_{i}).

Then
\mathcal{G}
is a Gröbner basis of
I_{1}\cap\cdots\cap I_{r}
with respect to
<
.

Proof.

We have the following containment of ideals

\langle\mathcal{G}\rangle\subseteq I_{1}\cap\cdots\cap I_{r}\subseteq J_{1}\cap\ldots\cap J_{k}

and

\langle{\rm in}_{<}(g_{1}),\ldots,{\rm in}_{<}(g_{n})\rangle\subseteq{\rm in}_{<}(\langle\mathcal{G}\rangle)\subseteq{\rm in}_{<}(I_{1}\cap\cdots\cap I_{r})\subseteq{\rm in}_{<}(J_{1}\cap\ldots\cap J_{k})\subseteq\bigcap_{i=1}^{k}{\rm in}_{<}(J_{i}).

Since
\langle{\rm in}_{<}(g_{1}),\ldots,{\rm in}_{<}(g_{n})\rangle=\bigcap_{i=1}^{k}{\rm in}_{<}(J_{i})
, we must have equality between all initial ideals in the above containment. In particular,
{\rm in}_{<}(\langle\mathcal{G}\rangle)={\rm in}_{<}(I_{1}\cap\cdots\cap I_{r})
. By Lemma
40
,
\langle\mathcal{G}\rangle=I_{1}\cap\cdots\cap I_{r}
. Furthermore,
\mathcal{G}
is a Gröbner basis of
I_{1}\cap\cdots\cap I_{r}
since
{\rm in}_{<}(I_{1}\cap\cdots\cap I_{r})=\langle{\rm in}_{<}(g_{1}),\ldots,{\rm in}_{<}(g_{n})\rangle
.

∎

We now turn our attention to computing a Gröbner basis for

I_{\varepsilon}^{x}I_{\varepsilon}^{z}

For the purposes of this section, it will be easier to work with the monomial order

{\rm in}_{\prec_{N}}(f_{i}g_{j})=-x_{j}y_{i}\hskip 28.45274pt{\rm in}_{\prec_{N}}(f_{i}h_{j})=x_{j}y_{i}\hskip 28.45274pt{\rm in}_{\prec_{N}}(g_{i}h_{j})=-x_{i}x_{j}.

With respect to the monomial order

Corollary 43
.

The initial ideal, with respect to
\prec_{N}
, for each ideal in Proposition
42
is:

{\rm in}_{\prec_{N}}(I_{\varepsilon}^{x}I_{\varepsilon}^{y})=\langle x_{i}y_{j}|1\leq i,j\leq N\rangle

{\rm in}_{\prec_{N}}(I_{\varepsilon}^{x}I_{\varepsilon}^{z})=\langle x_{i}y_{j}|1\leq i,j\leq N\rangle

{\rm in}_{\prec_{N}}(I_{\varepsilon}^{y}I_{\varepsilon}^{z})=\langle x_{i}x_{j},x_{k}y_{\ell}|1\leq i,j,k,\ell\leq N,k<\ell\rangle

Furthermore, the intersection
{\rm in}_{\prec_{N}}(I_{\varepsilon}^{x}I_{\varepsilon}^{y})\cap{\rm in}_{\prec_{N}}(I_{\varepsilon}^{x}I_{\varepsilon}^{z})\cap{\rm in}_{\prec_{N}}(I_{\varepsilon}^{y}I_{\varepsilon}^{z})
is minimally generated by

\langle x_{i}x_{j}y_{k},x_{\ell}y_{m}|1\leq i,j,k,\ell,m\leq N,\ell<m,k\leq i,j\rangle.

The intersection
{\rm in}_{\prec_{N}}(I_{\varepsilon}^{x}I_{\varepsilon}^{y})\cap{\rm in}_{\prec_{N}}(I_{\varepsilon}^{x}I_{\varepsilon}^{z})\cap{\rm in}_{\prec_{N}}(I_{\varepsilon}^{y}I_{\varepsilon}^{z})
is straightforward to compute. In fact, we don’t need
{\rm in}_{\prec_{N}}(I_{\varepsilon}^{x}I_{\varepsilon}^{y})
in the intersection since it equals
{\rm in}_{\prec_{N}}(I_{\varepsilon}^{x}I_{\varepsilon}^{z})

Also note that if
k>i
or
k>j

or
x_{j}y_{k}

Theorem 44
.

Fix
\varepsilon=(1,1,\ldots,1)
and let

and

\mathcal{R}
as defined as in Section

\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}=I_{\varepsilon}^{x}\cap I_{\varepsilon}^{y}\cap I_{\varepsilon}^{z}
with respect to
\prec_{N}

Proof.

Since

, by Corollary
43

{\rm in}_{\prec_{N}}(\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon})={\rm in}_{\prec_{N}}(I_{\varepsilon}^{x}I_{\varepsilon}^{y})\cap{\rm in}_{\prec_{N}}(I_{\varepsilon}^{x}I_{\varepsilon}^{z})\cap{\rm in}_{\prec_{N}}(I_{\varepsilon}^{y}I_{\varepsilon}^{z})

∎
