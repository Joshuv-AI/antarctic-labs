The survival status of Porphyromonas gingivalis under low heme conditions
Authors: Chengying Du, Rui Sun, Chengzhi Xie, Siyu Sun, Qian Li, Qi Zhao, Hongyan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground Porphyromonas gingivalis (P. gingivalis) is a key pathogen in the initiation and progression of chronic periodontitis. As a heme auxotrophic bacterium, P. gingivalis must acquire heme from the host environment as a source of both iron and the porphyrin ring. The scarcity of heme in healthy periodontal tissues and during the early stages of periodontitis serves as a critical factor governing the viability and pathogenic potential of P. gingivalis. The unique heme uptake pathway supports targeted treatment development.ObjectiveThis review systematically summarizes the adaptive mechanisms of P. gingivalis in nutrient-restricted environments, its crosstalk with oral microbes, and related therapeutic strategies.DesignWe review the published literature and elucidate the survival mechanisms of P. gingivalis in nutrient-restricted environments from five dimensions: growth morphology, virulence phenotype, heme transport system, metabolic enzyme activity, and transcriptional regulatory network. We also explore the possible mechanisms by which P. gingivalis disrupts immune homeostasis, leading to microbial dysbiosis, and analyze its synergistic interactions with other oral microorganisms. Based on the unique heme acquisition mechanism of P. gingivalis, we summary novel therapeutic strategies reported in existing research.ResultsHeme limitation reshapes P. gingivalis morphology, virulence, metabolism and heme acquisition systems. Interactions with S. gordonii and C. albicans boost its pathogenicity and trigger microbial dysbiosis. Porphyrin-conjugated antibiotics, antimicrobial photodynamic therapy and vaccines are promising heme-targeted therapies.ConclusionsHeme scarcity is a decisive environmental factor regulating the survival and pathogenic transformation of P. gingivalis. Under heme-limited conditions, the bacterium activates a complete set of adaptive survival programs and interacts with other oral microbes to disrupt tissue immune balance and trigger microbial dysbiosis. Targeting its distinctive heme acquisition system is a feasible and promising direction for developing novel therapies against chronic periodontitis.

Introduction

Haem is a coordination complex composed of iron ions and a porphyrin ring, mainly derived from the decomposition of haemoglobin in red blood cells. Porphyromonas gingivalis (P. gingivalis) is a Gram-negative obligate anaerobe and is considered a major pathogenic microorganism in the occurrence and development of periodontitis. As a haem auxotrophic bacterium, P. gingivalis cannot synthesise protoporphyrin IX (PPIX) by itself and needs to capture haem from the environment as a source of iron ions and PPIX to maintain its own growth and pathogenicity [1,2]. Since haem easily combines with haem-binding proteins to form complexes, P. gingivalis must compete with host cells by hydrolysing and degrading the complexes through proteolysis or expressing high-affinity binding receptors, and then internalising haem. In the human body, the haem released from haemoglobin will quickly form a complex with haptoglobin in host cells [3]. Although haemoglobin is the main haem source, haemoglobin-haptoglobin complex, haem-albumin, and hemopexin complex are important haem sources. Studying the physiological changes of P. gingivalis under haem-limited conditions will help us understand its pathogenic mechanism from multiple perspectives.

In this review, we summarise the changes in P. gingivalis and its various components under low haem conditions, aiming to facilitate the understanding of its survival mechanisms and the interactions among various substances under hemin-limited conditions, thereby providing supplements to the pathogenic mechanisms of P. gingivalis under different states.

The significance of haem to P. gingivalis

Haem is a hydrophobic iron-porphyrin complex. When the iron ion is in the ferrous state (Fe2 +), the complex formed with the porphyrin ring is haem; when the iron ion is in the ferric state (Fe3 +), it forms hemin [4]. The absence of either of them will lead to a decrease in haem levels. Haem exists in various forms in nature, among which haem b is the most common. It is present in most hemoproteins, such as haemoglobin, myoglobin, cytochrome b, and cytochrome P450. Haem b is also a precursor of haem a and haem c, which are present in cytochromes a and c, respectively [5]. Haem is an essential molecule present in cells and functions as a prosthetic group for various hemoproteins. Hemoproteins have multiple biological functions, including oxygen binding, aerobic respiration, and signal transduction. In cytochromes, haem participates in electron transport and facilitates the transfer of electrons along the mitochondrial electron transport chain to generate ATP [4]. The hydrophobicity and lipophilicity of haem enable it to embed in membranes and bind non-specifically to proteins. The binding or dissociation of haem with target proteins can change their expression or activity. For example, the binding of haem to transcription factors can regulate various pathways, including mitochondrial respiration, circadian rhythm, and cell proliferation [6]. As a nutrient, haem is the main source of iron for many microorganisms and animals, and is more easily absorbed than non-haem iron [7]. However, due to its redox activity and hydrophobicity, free haem has potential cytotoxicity. If haem catalysers harmful redox reactions or binds to proteins incorrectly, it will damage the structure of biomolecules and cause tissue injury.

Most bacteria require haem for colonisation and the establishment of infection. Haem can be present in cytochromes and participate in electron transport. Meuric et al. divided the bacterial electron transport chain into five complexes, among which complex II is composed of fumarate reductase and succinate dehydrogenase [8]. The haem present in cytochrome b is an essential component for the synthesis of fumarate reductase. The reduction of fumarate to succinate is a key pathway for energy production in P. gingivalis metabolism; therefore, haem serves as a critical factor in the growth and pathogenicity of P. gingivalis. However, due to the lack of 5-aminolevulinic acid synthase and porphobilinogen deaminase [9], P. gingivalis cannot synthesise PPIX, a precursor of haem [10]. Although enzyme genes involved in the biosynthesis of PPIX have been identified in the P. gingivalis W83 genome [10], such as hemD (an uroporphyrinogen III synthase), hemN (a coproporphyrinogen oxidase), hemG (a protoporphyrinogen oxidase), and hemH (a porphyrin ferrochelatase) [11,12], they are still insufficient for haem biosynthesis. Therefore, when culturing P. gingivalis, it is necessary to add additional hemin to the medium to meet the bacterial growth conditions. When P. gingivalis is cultured, it exhibits a black colony appearance, which is due to the deposition of μ-oxo dimers forming pigmentation containing iron PPIX [2]. The μ-oxo dimers are proposed to have a nutritional role, acting as a barrier to protect the bacterial outer membrane from oxygen free radical-mediated damage and maintaining a local anaerobic microenvironment [13,14]. In addition to haem, P. gingivalis can also utilise inorganic iron, transferrin, and lactoferrin as nutrients, but haem is the preferred choice of P. gingivalis [15–17].

P. gingivalis is mainly present in periodontal pockets rich in gingival crevicular fluid. The composition of gingival crevicular fluid is similar to that of serum, containing haemoglobin, haptoglobin, albumin, etc. [18]. The main source of haem for P. gingivalis in the periodontal pocket is haemoglobin from lysed red blood cells associated with bleeding caused by periodontal disease. However, in periodontally healthy individuals or the early stage of periodontal disease, the high-affinity binding ability enables albumin and hemopexin to chelate free haem [3,19,20]. The formed haem-albumin and haem-hemopexin complex serve as haem sources for P. gingivalis in the absence of bleeding. Moreover, free haemoglobin is capable of binding to haptoglobin, making the haemoglobin-haptoglobin complex an alternative source of haem [3].

Changes of Porphyromonas gingivalis under low haem conditions

Changes in the growth, virulence, and morphology of P. gingivalis

P. gingivalis, as one of the important members of the red complex, is closely related to the pathogenicity of periodontitis. As discussed earlier, the growth of P. gingivalis is dependent on haem. Studies have shown that P. gingivalis cannot grow under hemin-deficient conditions; when the hemin concentration reaches 0.5 μg/mL, the growth of P. gingivalis is inhibited; and its growth accelerates under hemin-sufficient conditions [21–23]. Secondly, compared with the environment with sufficient hemin, under low hemin conditions, the bacteria grow into a coccobacillus-like or short rod shape, with a small number of fimbriae and a large number of extracellular vesicles [21]. Moreover, there are fewer exopolysaccharides in the single-colony biofilm of P. gingivalis, and the bacteria present an elongated rod shape [24]. Furthermore, haem also modulates bacterial virulence. In mouse models, bacterial virulence is markedly attenuated under low hemin availability compared to sufficient hemin availability [21,25].

Lipopolysaccharide is a key virulence factor of P. gingivalis. The lipid A component within it can interact with host cell receptors. Therefore, differences in the fatty acid composition of lipid A may contribute to changes in the virulence of P. gingivalis. Existing study has confirmed that the hemin concentration in the culture medium can regulate the structural content of lipid A [26]. Low haem availability elicits a predominant penta-acylated lipid A structure. Conversely, high haem availability promotes a spectrum of both tetra- and penta-acylated structures [26]. As these forms are known as Toll-like receptor 4 antagonists and agonists, respectively [27], the remodelling of lipid A may consequently alter the host cellular immune response to bacteria.

Bacteria in the oral cavity typically exist as biofilms, the formation of which involves three stages: acquired pellicle formation, adhesion and coaggregation, and biofilm maturation [28,29]. Following the formation of the acquired pellicle, early colonising bacteria like Streptococcus gordonii (S. gordonii), attach to it through adhesins. This attachment, in turn, provides a surface for the adherence of late colonisers, including P. gingivalis [30]. Apart from its role in coaggregation, S. gordonii glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (SgGAPDH) creates a haem reservoir through histidine-mediated haem binding, which can be sequestered by P. gingivalis HmuY and its homologues [31]. The haem reservoir function of SgGAPDH facilitates the colonisation of P. gingivalis, promoting its ability to thrive in complex microenvironments. Additionally, H₂O₂ produced by S. gordonii mediates α-haemolysis and oxidises oxyhemoglobin (oxyHb) to methemoglobin (metHb) [32]. Later, P. gingivalis can extract haem from metHb, leading to the formation of an HmuY-Fe(III)haem complex [32]. This finding suggests that S. gordonii supports haem acquisition by P. gingivalis during co-aggregation. Beyond bacteria, fungi like Candida albicans (C. albicans) are also oral colonisers capable of participating in biofilm formation and supporting bacterial growth [33]. Research has revealed the pivotal role of the P. gingivalis InlJ protein and the C. albicans Als3 protein in mediating coaggregation within their dual-species biofilm [34]. The hyphal network of C. albicans serves as a protective microaerophilic niche for P. gingivalis, supporting its survival in aerobic environments while shielding it from host immune detection [35,36]. This facilitates the long-term colonisation of P. gingivalis and the establishment of chronic infection [36]. Under low-haem conditions, competition between P. gingivalis and C. albicans for haem unexpectedly boosts the pathogenic potential of P. gingivalis [24]. This is manifested as increased viability of P. gingivalis, greater complexity of biofilm structure, substantial production of extracellular polysaccharides, and enhanced antibiotic resistance [24]. This competitive pressure drives P. gingivalis to upregulate haem acquisition-related genes (HmuY, HmuR, HusA, Tlr) and gingipain genes, thereby reinforcing its competitive advantage [24]. Collectively, these adaptations enhance the survival of P. gingivalis in the biofilm niche during early periodontitis when bleeding is minimal.

Accumulating evidence suggests that the pathogenic mechanism of P. gingivalis is not primarily dependent on bacterial load, but rather operates through the C5aR1-TLR2 crosstalk signalling axis [37–41]. In neutrophils, activation of this signalling pathway leads to MyD88 ubiquitination and subsequent proteasomal degradation, thereby blocking neutrophil bactericidal function [40,42,43]. As the MyD88 pathway is inhibited, the alternative TLR2-Mal-PI3K pathway becomes activated, which not only suppresses immune cell phagocytosis of bacteria but also strongly induces the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines [40,41]. A similar mechanism exists in macrophages [44,45]. After invading epithelial cells, P. gingivalis secretes serine phosphatase B, inducing local chemotactic paralysis [46]. This effect suppresses the secretion of IL-8 by P. gingivalis and other bacteria, thereby delaying neutrophil recruitment [46–48]. Additionally, P. gingivalis can downregulate E-selectin expression on endothelial cells, thus impairing neutrophil migration [49]. Suppression of host immunity allows P. gingivalis and other pathogenic bacteria to evade clearance, while persistent tissue destruction releases nutrients that support the proliferation of these pathogens, ultimately leading to microbial dysbiosis [41]. Evidence indicates that P. gingivalis can eliminate C. albicans during haem competition, thereby diminishing the viability of the fungal species [24]. Collectively, the synergistic interactions of P. gingivalis with other pathogenic species, combined with its inherent capacity to disrupt immune homoeostasis, likely constitute the critical basis by which P. gingivalis triggers microbial dysbiosis under haem-limited conditions.

Most studies use 5 μg/mL hemin as the standard concentration for P. gingivalis in vitro culture, and 0.1 μg/mL hemin as a hemin-limited condition [24,50,51]. When cultured under hemin-limited conditions (0.1 μg/mL), P. gingivalis exhibits reduced virulence and decreased biofilm formation [24]. In contrast, genes related to the hemin uptake system are upregulated, and bacterial invasive ability is enhanced [50,52]. Concurrently, surface μ-oxo bishemin of P. gingivalis decreases, while antioxidant enzymes are upregulated to resist reactive oxygen species released by neutrophils [50]. Comparative studies on the physiological effects of different hemin concentrations on P. gingivalis revealed that the activities of gingipains, hydrogen peroxide, and superoxide dismutase do not exhibit a linear relationship with hemin concentration [53,54]. Proteomic profiling further indicates that P. gingivalis adapts to fluctuations in hemin availability through proteomic remodelling, thereby augmenting its pathogenicity and persistence, thus demonstrating adaptability [55]. Shizukuishi et al. demonstrated that P. gingivalis possesses an extremely high affinity for host haemoglobin, with even very low concentrations of haemoglobin being sufficient to support its growth [56]. This may represent a key mechanism by which P. gingivalis establishes latent colonisation in healthy periodontal tissues. As periodontitis advances, hemin accumulates within the periodontal pocket, triggering a phenotypic switch in P. gingivalis from a latent coloniser to an invasive proliferator, and from a commensal to a pathogen. When hemin concentrations exceed the standard level, P. gingivalis continues to thrive, becoming the predominant species in the periodontal niche. Notably, its gingipain activity is slightly lower than that observed under hemin-limited conditions, suggesting that hemin concentration may exert a threshold effect on P. gingivalis [53,54]. Subtyping of P. gingivalis based on the FimA genotype, followed by cultivation under varying hemin concentrations, revealed that each subtype possesses a distinct hemin optimum for growth [53]. The distribution of these subtypes within the periodontal pocket correlates with the clinical state of periodontal health or disease. Although changes in P. gingivalis subtype distribution and gingipain activity within the periodontal pocket can be attributed to hemin availability, current research has yet to identify the precise hemin concentration thresholds that induce alterations in P. gingivalis physiological activity.

Changes in the transport system of P. gingivalis

After haem is decomposed from host proteins, it is internalised by bacteria via surface receptors and membrane transport systems. As a Gram-negative bacterium, P. gingivalis needs to cross two layers of membranes when transporting haem, a process that requires the joint action of outer membrane transporters, periplasmic binding proteins, and cytoplasmic permeases. Three polygenic clusters of proteins involved in haem transmembrane transport were detected in the genome of P. gingivalis W83, designated as hmu, iht, and tlr (Figure A1). Notably, the mutant strain with a deletion of the tlr gene failed to grow in low-hemin medium, suggesting that the gene facilitates the growth of P. gingivalis under hemin-limited conditions [57]. Lewis et al. showed that a 45% and 70% reduction in hemin binding and uptake, respectively, in the hmu mutant compared to the parent strain, indicating that the hmu gene plays a critical role in hemin acquisition [58]. Through the identification of the 23 kDa iron-regulated hemin-binding protein, Lewis found that the hmu locus consists of 6 genes, namely hmuYRSTUV, which encode a new iron-dependent hemin uptake system [58]. HmuY is a hemin-binding protein associated with the outer membrane. HmuR is a TonB-dependent outer membrane receptor. HmuSTUV encodes a putative fusion protein of cobalamin biosynthesis/magnesium chelatase and other putative multi-transmembrane domain proteins, primarily responsible for inner membrane transport and processing [58–60].

Distinct from other bacteria [61], the P. gingivalis HmuY protein employs a unique mechanism, utilising two histidine residues for hemin coordination, and retains high-affinity binding regardless of the haem's redox state (oxidised or reduced) [62,63]. Although HmuY typically exists as a dimer, the presence of hemin induces its assembly into a tetramer [60]. This structural rearrangement thereby protects the hemin from degradation by environmental agents [64,65]. Multiple studies have demonstrated that HmuY and HmuR are cotranscribed, and the transcription is markedly enhanced under low hemin conditions [59,60]. Furthermore, the deletion of hmuY or hmuR, either individually or in combination, reduces the amount of hemin bound and consequently retards bacterial growth [60]. When serum serves as the sole source of hemin, and P. gingivalis is co-cultured with THP-1-derived macrophages in vivo, inactivation of hmuY significantly impaired the ability of P. gingivalis to adhere to and invade macrophages [66]. Thus, hmuY is a pivotal virulence factor that enables P. gingivalis to infect macrophages under hemin limitation, thereby contributing to bacterial proliferation and orchestrating a potent immune response from the macrophages. Gmiterek et al. propose that in order to cope with P. gingivalis infection, macrophages mainly activate intracellular pathways through Toll-like receptor 7, leading to upregulation of genes encoding NF-κB, pro-inflammatory factors (IL-1, IL-6), anti-inflammatory factors (IL-10) and chemokines (CCL3L1, CCL4, CXCL10, PTX3) [67]. However, P. gingivalis with hmuY gene deletion stimulates macrophage immune response by reducing the gene expression of NF-κB, IL-1, IL-10, CCL3L1 and PTX3.

Apart from the system mentioned above, the outer membrane of P. gingivalis possesses other proteins that function in hemin acquisition. Slakeski et al. found that upstream of tlr lie four open reading frames htrABCD (hemin transporter ABCD), which encode a putative ATP-binding cassette transport system, namely the ABC transport system, and their sequences show similarity to hemin transport systems in other microorganisms [68]. ABC transporter genes also affect bacterial adhesion and invasion by regulating the activity of gingipains [69,70]. ABC transporter expression is modulated by haem availability, being enhanced in low haem and repressed in sufficient haem states [50]. Some research has implicated DNA methylation as a potential mechanism behind this gene-specific response to varying hemin concentrations [23].

The Hus system includes HusA (hemophore-like protein), HusB (TonB-dependent receptor), HusC (Mar family transcriptional regulator), and HusD (putative protein) [71]. HusA is a high-affinity hemophore-like protein that is either membrane-anchored or secreted to compete for haem-binding or to bind free haem from dying cells [71]. It has been demonstrated that the hmu, iht, and tlr cannot compensate for the growth defect of a husA mutant, confirming that HusA is obligatory for P. gingivalis. Previous studies have shown that under low hemin conditions, the transcription level of husA increases, and the protein is predominantly released extracellularly as an adaptive response to this stress [71]. Contrary to this, new research reveals that HusA is not regulated by haem concentration [72]. Compared to HmuY, HusA exhibits weaker haem-binding affinity and lower protein stability. Consequently, the Hus system is not central to haem uptake or homoeostasis, but rather plays a more significant role in host cell invasion and adhesion [51,72]. Moreover, HusA exhibits a higher binding affinity for deuteroporphyrin IX and non-iron metalloporphyrins than for haem [73]. Exploiting this trait, Gao et al. created a deuteroporphyrin-metronidazole conjugate, the prototype antibiotic P8 [73]. Upon HusA-mediated delivery into P. gingivalis, the reduction of the nitro group on imidazole ring of P8 activates its bactericidal function. Additionally, this study revealed that the coproporphyrin III can sustain the growth of P. gingivalis [73]. The bacterium retains the enzymatic machinery required to convert coproporphyrinogen III into PPIX, and ultimately into haem [10]. It is hypothesised that, in response to haem deficiency, the bacterium employs the Hus system for haem precursor uptake and subsequently synthesise haem via HemG and HemN. However, this proposed pathway awaits experimental proof.

The Iht system is composed of IhtA (TonB-dependent outer membrane receptor), IhtB (lipoprotein), IhtC (periplasmic protein), IhtD (permease), and IhtE (ATP-binding protein) [74]. IhtB exhibits homology with the cobalt chelatase CbiK and mediates iron extraction from haem, which is then transported into the cytoplasm through the Iht system [74–76]. Although transcriptomic studies show that the iht operon is not induced under haem limitation in P. gingivalis [50,77], strain-specific regulation is observed under nutrient stress: ihtB expression is upregulated in strain A7436 but stable in ATCC 33277 [78]. This disparity likely reflects divergent strategies for maintaining haem homoeostasis across different strains [78]. Beyond its role in haem acquisition, the Iht system also facilitates the entry of antibacterial non-iron metalloporphyrins into bacterial cells, such as In-PPIX, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target [75] (Figure A2).

In contrast to P. gingivalis, which acquires iron from haem, most bacteria are capable of synthesising and secreting siderophores. Examples include pyoverdine and pyochelin from Pseudomonas aeruginosa [79,80], enterobactin and yersiniabactin from Escherichia coli [81], and staphyloferrin from Staphylococcus aureus [82]. Based on their chelating groups, siderophores can be classified into catecholates, hydroxamates, and carboxylates [83]. Beyond the production of endogenous siderophores, bacteria are capable of acquiring iron through the utilisation of siderophores synthesised by other microbial species [83,84]. Such siderophores are termed xenosiderophores. The existence of xenosiderophore mechanisms allows bacteria to conserve energy by reducing their own siderophore production, while also enabling them to adapt to iron-limited environments and enhancing their survival and infectious capacity [85]. However, since P. gingivalis is incapable of synthesising or secreting siderophores [10], it has instead specialised in haem and evolved a unique haem uptake system, of which HmuY is a representative example. Structurally, P. gingivalis HmuY coordinates haem using two histidine residues (His134 and His166) [62]. In contrast, its homologues—such as Tfo from Tannerella forsythia, PinO and PinA from Prevotella intermedia, and Bvu from Bacteroides vulgatus—utilise two methionine residues [61], whereas the HmuY homologue from Porphyromonas endodontalis employs a histidine-methionine pair [63]. Regarding haem-binding characteristics, Bvu preferentially binds haem and captures haem bound to serum albumin only under reducing conditions [86]. By contrast, P. gingivalis HmuY is capable of binding haem under both oxidising and reducing conditions, and can even sequester haem that is already bound to Bvu [86]. Although the HmuY homologue from Porphyromonas endodontalis can acquire haem from serum albumin and from Tannerella forsythia HmuY, it is unable to extract haem from P. gingivalis HmuY [63]. Furthermore, unlike P. gingivalis HmuY, which directly acquires haem from metHb, the P. endodontalis HmuY can only obtain haem after haemoglobin has been digested by proteases [63]. Collectively, multiple studies indicate that the histidine residues provide P. gingivalis HmuY with a competitive edge in haem coordination [62,87].

Changes in various enzymes of P. gingivalis

The primary energy source for P. gingivalis is the catabolism of amino acids, notably glutamate and aspartate. Glutamate is fermented to butyrate, propionate, and ammonia, while aspartate yields butyrate, propionate, acetate, and ammonia. Hemin restriction elicits alterations in the abundance of enzymes within the glutamate and aspartate metabolic networks [50]. Within the glutamate pathway, the abundance of acyl-CoA dehydrogenase, electron transfer flavoprotein alpha subunit, and the enzyme catalysing the conversion of succinyl-CoA to crotonyl-CoA was significantly elevated. Under hemin-excess conditions, fumarate reductase in the aspartate pathway catalysers the reduction of fumarate to succinate, which is subsequently channelled into the glutamate pathway for butyrate production. Nevertheless, hemin restriction leads to decreased fumarate reductase abundance, resulting in less succinate entering the glutamate pathway. Consequently, aspartate is predominantly catabolized to acetate, a shift reflected in the final organic acid profile of the culture medium [50]. Furthermore, under hemin-restricted conditions, the deficiency or reduction of μ-oxo dimers leads to increased oxidative stress, which consequently triggers a significant upregulation of peroxidase abundance [50].

Gingipains are cysteine proteases present in the outer membrane, membrane vesicles, or extracellular space of P. gingivalis, including Arginine-gingipain (Rgp) and Lysine-gingipain (Kgp) [88]. Rgp is encoded by the rgpA and rgpB genes, while Kgp is encoded by the kgp gene. Gingipains exhibit proteolytic activity against a broad spectrum of host molecules, such as erythrocytes, hemoproteins, haptoglobin, and transferrin [89,90]. The proteolysis of haemoglobin by gingipains generates μ-oxo bisheme on the bacterial surface resulting in the distinctive black colonies. In this process, Rgp and Kgp play a synergistic role in pigment synthesis. Rgp is responsible for oxidising oxyHb to metHb, while Kgp degrades metHb to release haem, ultimately leading to the formation of μ-oxo bisheme [72,91]. Research has found that Kgp-deficient mutants produce pigmentless colonies due to their inability to degrade metHb, while RgpA/RgpB double-deficient mutants generate brown colonies containing the haemoglobin hemochrome on culture media [2,91]. In contrast, when RgpA or RgpB is individually deleted, the formation of μ-oxo bisheme still occurs [91]. The pigment deposited on the surface of P. gingivalis serves both as a haem reservoir and as a protective barrier, defending against reactive oxygen species from neutrophils [91,92]. Under hemin-restricted conditions, P. gingivalis can utilise the pigment deposited on its surface and non-pigmented mutant strains exhibit reduced survival [25]. Simultaneously, studies have found that gingipain activity increases under low hemin conditions, coupled with the isolation of lipopolysaccharide that demonstrates an attenuated ability to prime neutrophils [25,93]. Smiga et al. further elucidated a collaborative pathway where HmuY orchestrates hemin capture with support from Kgp/RgpA: RgpA generates metHb, Kgp degrades it, and HmuY sequesters the hemin [72]. Importantly, this coordinated process is context-dependent, being crucial in hemin-abundant settings but of reduced importance during hemin scarcity (Figure A3).

Changes in ferric uptake regulator

In Gram-negative bacteria, metal ion homoeostasis is typically modulated by the Ferric Uptake Regulator (Fur). The Fur dimer is characterised by three metal-binding sites: site S1 for zinc, site S2 for Fe2+, and an additional site, S3. Butler et al. proposed that a Fur family homologue exists in P. gingivalis and designated it the haem-associated regulator (Har) [94]. Har contains a double-C-X-X-C motif involved in zinc binding at the S1 site but lacks the conserved S2 site, which is replaced by a haem regulatory motif (HRM; -97C-98 P-99L-), and also lacks the S3 site.

Current understanding suggests that PgFur diverges from the typical role of Fur proteins; it does not control iron homoeostasis but functions as a regulator of virulence in P. gingivalis under low hemin availability, acting through an intricate network [95]. Microarray analysis of RNA from wild-type and mutant strains revealed that, under hemin-restricted conditions, 11 out of 35 downregulated genes are typically upregulated during the growth of P. gingivalis. Compared to the wild-type and complemented strains, the mutant showed markedly decreased biofilm volume and average thickness. Furthermore, hemin limitation itself also diminished biofilm volume and thickness in the mutant. This provides evidence for a connection between PgFur, hemin availability, and biofilm properties [94]. Although both PgFur and HmuY are upregulated during hemin limitation, PgFur deficiency results in suppressed HmuY expression, elevated expression of other hmu constituents [95,96], and downregulation of the two-component systems (PG1431-PG1432、PG0719-PG0720), thereby impairing biofilm formation. Thus, PgFur likely governs biofilm development in low hemin by coordinately regulating HmuY and the PG1431/PG1432 system. PgFur acts as a master regulator that modulates virulence, in part by governing extracytoplasmic sigma factors (ECF σ-factors) and HtrA. Its inactivation weakens P. gingivalis by impairing proteolytic/gingipain activity and host cell adhesion/invasion [95,97]. The regulatory effect is partly tied to hemin acquisition, thereby facilitates the establishment of infection and the onset of inflammation.

Two-component system

P. gingivalis facilitates tissue destruction, modulates host immune responses, and perturbs the dynamic balance between the host and microbiota through the secretion of various virulence factors [98]. Gingipains, a key virulence factor of P. gingivalis, are secreted to the cell surface via the Type IX Secretion System (T9SS) [99]. T9SS is a multi-subunit transmembrane complex comprising 18 identified conserved proteins, which function in genetic regulation, membrane core structure, and protein modification [100,101]. Within the regulatory group, there exists a two-component system (TCS) composed of PorX and PorY. TCS is one of the bacterial signal transduction mechanisms, typically consisting of a histidine kinase (HK) and a response regulator (RR) [102].

PorX/PorY functions as a virulence factor for P. gingivalis. A porX knockout manifests as a loss of pigment, decreased T9SS gene expression, and a weakened suppression of pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion by dendritic cells and T cells in a murine virulence model [103,104]. Yang et al. showed that PorX/PorY transcription is time-dependent during P. gingivalis growth, while also mediating gene expression in response to fluctuating hemin concentrations [103]. High hemin availability augments gene transcription driven by the PorX/PorY system, such as PorT, SigH, and PGN_0341. Among these, SigH has been demonstrated to help P. gingivalis counteract the oxidative stress induced by excess hemin [105]. In addition to SigH (PG1827), the P. gingivalis W83 genome encodes five other sigma factors, including SigP (PG0162), SigCH (PG0214), PG1660, PG0985, and PG1318 [106]. Among them, both SigH and SigCH are implicated in hemin uptake [107,108]. SigCH plays a role in hemin acquisition under restriction. Its expression, along with that of HmuY, HmuR, and PG1237, is inversely correlated with cell density due to quorum sensing. SigCH functions by directly targeting the HmuYR operon and PG1237, which in turn affects hemin utilisation [108]. Currently, no studies have clearly elucidated the transcriptional changes of PorX/PorY under low hemin conditions. However, given that Kgp and Rgp are directly regulated by the PorX-PorY-SigP pathway and exhibit increased activity under low hemin conditions, it is plausible that PorX/PorY is activated in nutrient-restricted environments, thereby upregulating the expression of T9SS (Figure A4).

Based on current research, certain functional TCS in P. gingivalis may be non-functional in typing, such as the TCS composed of PG0719 (HK) and PG0720 (RR) in P. gingivalis W83, HaeS-HaeR. However, in strain ATCC 33277, a 2.5 kb deletion in the homologous region of PG0719-PG0720 results in a non-functional PGN_0752 (HK), while PGN_0753 (RR), although structurally intact, shows no expression [109]. Scott et al. demonstrated that HaeR directly binds to promoter regions of key virulence operons, including hemin transport systems (iht, hmu, htr), gingipains, and hemagglutinins. The HaeS-HaeR exhibits hemin concentration-dependent regulation: under hemin-replete conditions, activated HaeR enhances its own transcription while concurrently repressing expression of rgpA, kgp, and hagABC, thereby optimising energy allocation and minimising host immune detection. Conversely, during hemin restriction, reduced HaeR activity derepresses these virulence factors, facilitating enhanced hemin acquisition [109]. This dual regulatory mechanism enables P. gingivalis to dynamically adapt to fluctuating hemin availability, ensuring survival within the host environment.

Preventive or therapeutic strategies based on haem uptake mechanisms

The treatment of periodontitis mainly includes mechanical removal of plaque biofilm, such as subgingival scaling and root planing, adjunctive use of antimicrobial agents, and periodontal surgery when necessary [110,111]. Nevertheless, the prolonged and extensive application of traditional antibiotics has given rise to antibiotic-resistant P. gingivalis, thereby compromising therapeutic efficacy [112,113]. Therefore, targeted antibacterial agents have gradually become a research hotspot. Antimicrobial studies targeting the haem transport system are currently underway. In addition to the HusA-mediated deuteroporphyrin-metronidazole conjugate and the Iht system-mediated In-PPIX described above [73,75], recent studies have shown that non-iron metalloporphyrins can bind to HmuY and exhibit antibacterial activity [114–117]. The porphyrin-nitroimidazole antibiotic targeting the HA2 receptor exhibits selectivity for P. gingivalis, more effectively inhibiting growth and eradicating intracellular bacteria [118–120]. Natural compounds can also serve as alternative inhibitors to antibiotics, exerting antibacterial effects. He et al. found that the quorum sensing inhibitor coumarin may inhibit biofilm formation by binding to the HmuY protein [121]. Meanwhile, the vitamin B3 derivative nicotinamide can inhibit gingipain activity, interfere with the haem uptake system, and suppress bacterial virulence [122]. Furthermore, considering the ability of P. gingivalis to adhere to and utilise haem from erythrocytes, Tang et al. constructed a gallium-porphyrin-loaded erythrocyte membrane nanovesicle to mimic red blood cells [123]. This nanovesicle can precisely target P. gingivalis and is subsequently lysed and utilised by the bacteria, leading to intracellular metabolic dysregulation. Consequently, it inhibits bacterial growth, reduces epithelial cell invasion, and attenuates the progression of periodontitis.

Antimicrobial photodynamic therapy (aPDT) can also serve as an alternative approach to inhibit P. gingivalis. Existing studies have shown that red or blue LED light effectively reduces bacteria associated with periodontal disease [124]. Yuan et al. found that antimicrobial blue light affects the expression of haem uptake-related genes (RgpA, RgpB, HmuR) and enhances oxidative damage to achieve antibacterial effects [125]. aPDT using various photosensitizers and LED light has been demonstrated to reduce the viability and survival rate of P. gingivalis [126–129]. However, aPDT is less effective under anaerobic conditions. To address this limitation, a nanosensitizer named oxyHb@IR820 was developed, which combines oxyHb with a photosensitizer to specifically combat P. gingivalis infection [130].

Various surface antigens present in P. gingivalis are capable of activating the host immune system, leading to the production of specific antibodies and cellular immune responses, which in turn provide immune protection [131,132]. These antigens may serve as potential vaccine candidates, facilitating the development of vaccines against P. gingivalis. The O-antigen polysaccharide region of LPS can be recognised by the immune system; however, its weak immunogenicity when used alone makes it difficult to induce long-lasting immune memory. Therefore, automated glycan assembly and E. coli recombineering technologies have been employed to produce structurally stable, high-purity LPS for immune protection [133,134]. Similarly, the capsule-conjugate vaccine constructed using the eCRM® carrier protein induced high levels of IgG antibodies in mouse immunisation experiments and inhibited P. gingivalis-induced alveolar bone resorption [135]. Furthermore, the gingipain rgpA, hemagglutinin adhesin, and its functional domain (cleaved adhesin) can all serve as antigens for DNA vaccine development, inducing a predominantly IgG1-mediated humoral immune response and suppressing the expression of inflammatory cytokines [136,137]. Although various vaccines exhibit strong targeting, low risk of inducing drug resistance, and favourable safety profiles, their development remains technically challenging. Currently, vaccine studies are still confined to animal experiments, and their actual efficacy in clinical trials remains unclear. Nevertheless, P. gingivalis vaccines may hold promise as novel approaches for early prevention and adjunctive therapy in the future (Table 1).

Conclusion

Haem exerts pleiotropic effects on P. gingivalis, directly modulating cellular morphology and indirectly influencing virulence through transcriptional and post-transcriptional regulatory mechanisms. Nevertheless, the comprehensive regulatory network governing these processes remains incompletely characterised. Under hemin-restricted conditions, transcriptional upregulation of specific hemin acquisition systems correlates with enhanced bacterial adherence and invasive capabilities. However, the functional roles and hierarchical regulation of other virulence determinants in periodontal pathogenesis constitute knowledge gaps requiring systematic investigation. While P. gingivalis maintains ecological dominance in polymicrobial biofilms, the molecular basis beyond HmuY's unique structural attributes remains largely unexplored. Elucidating the adaptive responses of P. gingivalis to hemin limitation may reveal phase-dependent virulence modulation during periodontal disease progression. Such mechanistic insights could inform targeted antimicrobial strategies that selectively pathogen inhibition while preserving commensal microbiota and mitigating antibiotic resistance development.
