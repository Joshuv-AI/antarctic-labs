On couplings for kinetic Langevin diffusions
Nawaf Bou-Rabee and Sonja Cox and Roy Schieven

Abstract
. For the kinetic Langevin diffusion and its splitting discretizations, the hypoelliptic noise structure makes the relationship between couplings and total variation (TV) bounds more subtle than in the elliptic case. We establish that, for the kinetic Langevin equation with quadratic potential, no Markovian coupling (continuous or discrete) captures the asymptotic decay rate of the TV distance between two solutions with different initial values; the canonical iterated one-shot (or sticky) coupling, for which we derive an exact contraction formula, saturates this lower bound. On the constructive side, we show that the recent sharp TV bounds of [ 15 ] admit a natural interpretation through an explicit non-Markovian coupling, built from an optimal coalescence trajectory characterized by a classical minimum-energy control problem. For the OBABO splitting scheme, this approach additionally eliminates the Hessian-Lipschitz, step-size, and final-time assumptions of [ 15 ] .

Introduction

The
kinetic Langevin diffusion
is the
2d

\begin{split}dX_{t}&=V_{t}\,dt,\\

Compared to its overdamped counterpart, the kinetic Langevin diffusion

The TV distance admits the coupling characterization

Preliminaries and notation

Throughout the paper,
\mathbb{N}=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}
. For a measurable space

(X,\mathcal{F})
and
x\in X
,
\delta_{x}
denotes the Dirac measure at
x
.

The total variation (TV) distance between two probability measures
\nu
and
\tilde{\nu}
on a countably separated measurable space
(X,\mathcal{F})
is

{d_{\textup{TV}}{\left(\nu,\tilde{\nu}\right)}}=\sup_{A\in\mathcal{F}}\{|\nu(A)-\tilde{\nu}(A)|\}.

Let
\Gamma(\nu,\tilde{\nu})
denote the set of couplings of
\nu
and
\tilde{\nu}
, i.e. the set of all probability measures on
(X\times X,\mathcal{F}\otimes\mathcal{F})
with marginals
\nu
and
\tilde{\nu}
. The TV distance admits the
coupling characterization
[
35
, Sections I.2 & I.5]

(2.1)

{d_{\textup{TV}}{\left(\nu,\tilde{\nu}\right)}}=\min_{\gamma\in\Gamma(\nu,\tilde{\nu})}\{\gamma(\{(x,y)\in X\times X\colon x\neq y\})\}.

A coupling
\gamma\in\Gamma(\nu,\tilde{\nu})
that attains this minimum is called
maximal
(since it maximizes
\gamma(\{(x,y)\in X\times X\colon x=y\})
); maximal couplings exist for any two probability measures on a countably separated measurable space
[
35
, Section I.5]
and can be constructed by
rejection sampling; see Section
5.1.1
for
the Gaussian case used in this paper.

For two Gaussians on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with common non-singular covariance

\Sigma
and (possibly distinct) means
\mu,\tilde{\mu}
,

(2.2)

{d_{\textup{TV}}{\left(\mathcal{N}(\mu,\Sigma),\mathcal{N}(\tilde{\mu},\Sigma)\right)}}=2\Phi{\left(\frac{\left|B^{-1}(\tilde{\mu}-\mu)\right|}{2}\right)}-1,

where
B\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
satisfies
BB^{T}=\Sigma
, and
\Phi\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is the standard normal CDF
[
3
, Theorem 1]
.

Throughout,
|\cdot|
denotes the Euclidean norm on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and also the
induced norm on
\mathbb{R}^{2d}
under the splitting
z=(x,v)
used below; for

A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
,
\|A\|
denotes the operator norm and
\|A\|_{F}
the
Frobenius norm. We write
\textup{Law}(X)
for the law of a random variable
X
.
For probability measures
\nu,\tilde{\nu}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the
L^{p}
-Wasserstein
distance of order
p\in[1,\infty)
is

W_{p}(\nu,\tilde{\nu})\;=\;\Bigl(\,\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma(\nu,\tilde{\nu})}\int|x-\tilde{x}|^{p}\,\gamma(dx,d\tilde{x})\Bigr)^{1/p}.

A recurring theme of this paper is the conversion of Wasserstein control
between two distributions into total variation control between their
evolutions under a Markov chain. We refer to estimates of this form as

Wasserstein-to-TV regularization
. Let
P
be a Markov kernel on

\mathbb{R}^{2d}
and let
\nu,\tilde{\nu}
be probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{2d}
;
write
\nu P^{n}
for the law at time
n
of the chain with kernel
P
and
initial distribution
\nu
. A Wasserstein-to-TV regularization bound for

P
is an inequality of the form

(2.3)

{d_{\textup{TV}}{\left(\nu P^{n},\,\tilde{\nu}P^{n}\right)}}\;\leq\;C_{n}\,W_{p}(\nu,\tilde{\nu}),\qquad n\geq 1,

for some
p\in[1,\infty)
and a constant
C_{n}
that depends on
P
but not
on
\nu,\tilde{\nu}
. The two distances on the right- and left-hand sides
are not comparable in general: at
n=0
, two distinct Dirac masses

\delta_{z},\delta_{\tilde{z}}
satisfy

{d_{\textup{TV}}{\left(\delta_{z},\delta_{\tilde{z}}\right)}}=1
while

W_{p}(\delta_{z},\delta_{\tilde{z}})=|z-\tilde{z}|
, so (
2.3
)
fails uniformly at
n=0
. The content of the bound is that the kernel
P

regularizes the laws sufficiently in
n
steps that a transport-based
control of the initial distance produces a statistical-distance control of
the resulting laws. Equivalently, for every
n\geq 1
, the map

\mu\mapsto\mu P^{n}
is
C_{n}
-Lipschitz from

(\mathcal{P}_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2d}),\,W_{p})
to

(\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{2d}),\,{d_{\textup{TV}}{\left(\cdot,\cdot\right)}})
, whereas the identity map
between these two metric spaces is not even continuous, as the Dirac-mass
example above shows.

3.
The non-Markovian finite-time coupling

The starting point for this section is the recent work of

[
15
]

[
15
]
, who establish
Wasserstein-to-TV regularization for a broad class of generalized
Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (gHMC) Markov chains. The OBABO discretization of
the kinetic Langevin equation, with step size
h>0
, generates a chain in
this class; we review the OBABO scheme in
Section
3.1
and specialize to it throughout the
remainder of this section. The regularization mechanism is necessarily
multi-step: the position component of a single step of the OBABO chain has
variance of order
h^{3}
, too degenerate to support a Gaussian coupling on
its own. To circumvent this,
[
15
]
introduce a

coalescence map
, a measurable transport of the Gaussian increments
driving the chain from
z
onto Gaussian increments driving the chain from

\tilde{z}
; controlling the deviation of this map from the identity over

n
steps yields the bound of
Theorem
1.1
.

This section refines that analysis in three respects.
First, we relax the hypotheses of Theorem
1.1
: the
step-size restrictions
hn\leq 1
and
h\leq h_{0}
are removed for the OBABO
chain (Theorem
3.2
), so that the bound
holds for an arbitrary number of integration steps
n\in\mathbb{N}
and arbitrary
step sizes
h>0
, and the Lipschitz-Hessian assumption on
U
in

[
15
]
is dropped, retaining only the Lipschitz-gradient
assumption. Second, we
identify the coalescence map that is optimal for the linearized dynamics,
as the unique minimizer of a discrete linear-quadratic control problem
(Section
3.3
); this map differs from the
choice in
[
15
]
. The proofs of the first two
refinements share a common structural ingredient: the Jacobian of the
coalescence map associated with the OBABO scheme is lower triangular
(Lemma
3.11
),
which makes both the inversion required for the optimal map and the bounds
needed for arbitrary
(h,n)
tractable. Several other splitting schemes
share this lower-triangular structure
(Remark
3.13
). Third, we show that the
resulting analysis admits a coupling interpretation: it defines an explicit
non-Markovian coupling of two OBABO chains started from different initial
values (Section
3.4
), whose meeting
probability coincides with the TV bound. When
U
is quadratic, this
coupling is maximal
(Remark
3.19
).

3.1.
A TV distance bound for the OBABO scheme

We refine Theorem
1.1
for the

Fix the step size

The kinetic Langevin equation,

\begin{split}dX_{t}&=V_{t}\,dt,\\

\displaystyle\theta_{h}^{(\textup{B})}(x,v)

\displaystyle=\bigl(x,\;v-h\,\nabla U(x)\bigr),

\displaystyle\theta_{h}^{(\textup{A})}(x,v)

The Ornstein–Uhlenbeck flow

The OBABO scheme is the Strang composition

\xi^{(1)},\xi^{(2)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{iid}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}(0,\id_{d})

\theta_{h}:=\theta_{h/2}^{(\textup{B})}\circ\theta_{h}^{(\textup{A})}\circ\theta_{h/2}^{(\textup{B})}
, the OBABO map is

O_{h/2}(\xi^{(2)})\circ\theta_{h}\circ O_{h/2}(\xi^{(1)})
.

The associated Markov chain

(Z^{h}_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}=(X^{h}_{k},V^{h}_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is defined by
Z^{h}_{0}=z
and

Z^{h}_{k+1}=O_{h/2}(\xi_{k+1}^{(2)})\circ\theta_{h}\circ O_{h/2}(\xi_{k+1}^{(1)})(Z^{h}_{k}),

with
\xi_{k}=(\xi^{(1)}_{k},\xi^{(2)}_{k})
iid copies of

(\xi^{(1)},\xi^{(2)})
. Equivalently,

(3.3)

Z^{h}_{k+1}=A_{h}\,Z^{h}_{k}+L_{h}\,\xi_{k+1}-\frac{h}{2}\begin{pmatrix}h\,\nabla U(X^{h}_{k})\\
e^{-\gamma h/2}\,\bigl[\nabla U(X^{h}_{k})+\nabla U(X^{h}_{k+1})\bigr]\end{pmatrix},

where

(3.4)

A_{h}=\begin{pmatrix}\id_{d}&h\,e^{-\gamma h/2}\id_{d}\\
0&e^{-\gamma h}\id_{d}\end{pmatrix},\qquad L_{h}=(1-e^{-\gamma h})^{1/2}\begin{pmatrix}h\,\id_{d}&0\\
e^{-\gamma h/2}\id_{d}&\id_{d}\end{pmatrix}.

A single OBABO step, viewed as a function of its driving noise, defines a
map
\Psi_{z}\colon\mathbb{R}^{2d}\to\mathbb{R}^{2d}
for each
z\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
:

(3.5)

\Psi_{z}(\xi)=O_{h/2}(\xi^{(2)})\circ\theta_{h}\circ O_{h/2}(\xi^{(1)})(z),\qquad\xi=(\xi^{(1)},\xi^{(2)})\in\mathbb{R}^{2d},

so that
Z^{h}_{k+1}=\Psi_{Z^{h}_{k}}(\xi_{k+1})
. Writing

\Psi_{z}=(\Psi^{X}_{z},\Psi^{V}_{z})
for the position and velocity components
and
z=(x,v)
,

(3.6)

\displaystyle\Psi^{X}_{z}(\xi^{(1)})

\displaystyle=x+h\,e^{-\gamma h/2}v+(1-e^{-\gamma h})^{1/2}h\,\xi^{(1)}-\tfrac{h^{2}}{2}\nabla U(x),

\displaystyle\Psi^{V}_{z}(\xi^{(1)},\xi^{(2)})

\displaystyle=e^{-\gamma h}v+(1-e^{-\gamma h})^{1/2}\bigl(e^{-\gamma h/2}\xi^{(1)}+\xi^{(2)}\bigr)-\tfrac{h}{2}\,e^{-\gamma h/2}\bigl(\nabla U(x)+\nabla U(\Psi^{X}_{z}(\xi^{(1)}))\bigr).

The map
\Psi_{z}
, like the
matrices
A_{h}
and
L_{h}
above, depends on
h
and
\gamma
through the
OBABO scheme; we suppress this dependence in the notation throughout,
retaining
h
as an explicit subscript only on
A_{h}
and
L_{h}
.

Proof.

be any coupling of
\nu

Remark 3.4

the small-time behavior of the continuous kinetic Langevin semigroup
under hypoelliptic regularization. The

the linearized dynamics; the nonlinear force

. Combined with a Wasserstein convergence estimate,

3.3
yields TV
convergence for the chain. Such estimates are available for the

[
15
, Theorem 2.1]
and for the UBU
scheme in
[
44
]
.

Remark 3.5

The TV bound provided in Theorem

[
15

). Moreover, similar results can presumably be obtained for other splitting schemes. For details, see Remark

3.2.
Coalescence maps

Fix initial values
z,\tilde{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
, and recall from
(
3.7
) that
\Psi^{n}_{z}\colon\mathbb{R}^{2dn}\to\mathbb{R}^{2d}

sends a noise vector
\xi=(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})
to the state of the OBABO
chain (
3.3
) after
n
steps, started
from
z
with driving noise
\xi

z

with noise
\xi
and the other from
\tilde{z}
with noise
\tilde{\xi}

coincide at time

if and only if

(3.9)

\Psi^{n}_{\tilde{z}}(\tilde{\xi})=\Psi^{n}_{z}(\xi).

Definition 3.6
.

Let
z,\tilde{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
. A map

\Psi^{n}_{z,\tilde{z}}\colon\mathbb{R}^{2dn}\to\mathbb{R}^{2dn}
is a

if (
3.9
) holds with
\tilde{\xi}=\Psi^{n}_{z,\tilde{z}}(\xi)
,
for every
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{2dn}
.

Every coalescence map yields an upper bound on

{d_{\textup{TV}}{\left(\pi_{n}(\delta_{z}),\pi_{n}(\delta_{\tilde{z}})\right)}}
, a consequence of the contractivity of TV under measurable maps (cf.
[
38
, Lemma 3]
).

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
z,\tilde{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
, and let
\Psi^{n}_{z,\tilde{z}}

be a coalescence map. Then, for
\xi\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\id_{2dn})
,

(3.10)

{d_{\textup{TV}}{\left(\pi_{n}(\delta_{z}),\,\pi_{n}(\delta_{\tilde{z}})\right)}}\;\leq\;{d_{\textup{TV}}{\left(\textup{Law}(\xi),\,\textup{Law}(\Psi^{n}_{z,\tilde{z}}(\xi))\right)}}.

Proof.

Let
(\tilde{\xi},\tilde{\zeta})
be a maximal coupling of
\textup{Law}(\xi)
and
\textup{Law}(\Psi^{n}_{z,\tilde{z}}(\xi))
.
Applying the measurable map

\Psi^{n}_{\tilde{z}}
componentwise yields the pair

(\Psi^{n}_{\tilde{z}}(\tilde{\xi}),\Psi^{n}_{\tilde{z}}(\tilde{\zeta}))
, whose
marginals are

\displaystyle\textup{Law}(\Psi^{n}_{\tilde{z}}(\tilde{\xi}))

\displaystyle=\pi_{n}(\delta_{\tilde{z}}),

\displaystyle\textup{Law}(\Psi^{n}_{\tilde{z}}(\tilde{\zeta}))

\displaystyle=\textup{Law}\bigl(\Psi^{n}_{\tilde{z}}(\Psi^{n}_{z,\tilde{z}}(\xi))\bigr)=\textup{Law}(\Psi^{n}_{z}(\xi))=\pi_{n}(\delta_{z}),

the second by the coalescence map property. Hence

(\Psi^{n}_{\tilde{z}}(\tilde{\xi}),\Psi^{n}_{\tilde{z}}(\tilde{\zeta}))
is a
coupling of
\pi_{n}(\delta_{\tilde{z}})
and
\pi_{n}(\delta_{z})
, and

{d_{\textup{TV}}{\left(\pi_{n}(\delta_{z}),\pi_{n}(\delta_{\tilde{z}})\right)}}\;\leq\;\mathbb{P}\bigl(\Psi^{n}_{\tilde{z}}(\tilde{\xi})\neq\Psi^{n}_{\tilde{z}}(\tilde{\zeta})\bigr)\;\leq\;\mathbb{P}(\tilde{\xi}\neq\tilde{\zeta})\;=\;{d_{\textup{TV}}{\left(\textup{Law}(\xi),\,\textup{Law}(\Psi^{n}_{z,\tilde{z}}(\xi))\right)}},

as required.
∎

Let

Lemma/Definition 3.9

Let

Let

Since

Remark 3.13
.

In order to construct coalescence maps
\Psi^{n}_{z,\tilde{z}}
for the OBABO splitting scheme and arrive at the bound (
3.17
), we argued that

(1)

\Psi^{n}_{z,\tilde{z}}
is well-defined through the relation (
3.12
);

(2)

\Psi^{n}_{z,\tilde{z}}
is a diffeomorphism; and

(3)

the Jacobian matrix
D\Psi^{n}_{z,\tilde{z}}
is a lower-triangular matrix, so that the last two terms in equation (
3.11
) vanish.

The same line of reasoning can be applied to other gHMC methods and splitting schemes. More specifically, if the single step update map
\Psi_{z}
is invertible for each
z\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
, then (
1
) is satisfied. It subsequently suffices to verify that
(z,\xi)\mapsto\Psi_{z}(\xi)
and
(z,y)\mapsto\Psi^{-1}_{z}(y)
are continuously differentiable to ensure (
2
).

These invertibility and differentiability properties of the single-step update map
\Psi_{z}
are satisfied for the gHMC method, as discussed in
[
15
]
, provided that
U
is twice continuously differentiable and the integration time of the corresponding velocity Verlet integrator is sufficiently small. Unfortunately, the Jacobian matrices
D\Psi^{n}_{z,\tilde{z}}
of the resulting coalescence maps are not necessarily lower triangular. As a consequence, the final two terms in (
3.11
) do not vanish and need to be treated separately in order to derive TV bounds, requiring the additional assumption that
U\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
has a Lipschitz continuous Hessian.

We note that the framework described above extends naturally beyond the gHMC setting of
[
15
]
to include several well-known splitting schemes associated to the kinetic Langevin equation. More specifically, the BOAOB splitting scheme has a single step update map
\Psi_{z}
that satisfies the aforementioned invertibility and differentiability properties, thus giving rise to diffeomorphic coalescence maps. Similar to the OBABO scheme, these coalescence maps have an explicit expression and a Jacobian matrix that is lower triangular with all diagonal entries equal to
1
, so that (
3.17
) again holds true.

Regarding the OABAO splitting scheme, the additional assumption that
\frac{Lh^{2}}{4}<1
is needed to ensure that the single step update maps
\Psi_{z}
,
z\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
, are invertible. The resulting coalescence maps are implicit and the final two terms in (
3.11
) do not vanish, so that the additional assumption that
U\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
has a Lipschitz continuous Hessian is again needed for the analysis. These are standard assumptions that are also required in
[
10
]
to ensure existence of the one-shot map for the OABAO scheme.

The splitting schemes BAOAB, ABOBA and AOBOA do not have diffeomorphic update mappings, and therefore do not fit the framework in its current state. We expect that it is feasible to extend the framework to these schemes by using the fact that
n\geq 2
steps of these schemes consist of
n-1
steps of the OABAO, OBABO and BOAOB schemes respectively accompanied by two remaining partial updates at the beginning and the end. Finally, the framework can also be transferred over to the UBU splitting scheme under similar assumptions as for the OABAO splitting scheme.

Remark 3.20
.

The trajectory used in
[
15
]
differs from ours.
Recall the position-velocity splitting
\Delta z=(\Delta x,\Delta v)

with
\Delta x=\tilde{x}-x
and
\Delta v=\tilde{v}-v
.
Instead of the recursive definition (
3.19
),
the CM trajectory is given explicitly, for
k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, by

\begin{gathered}w_{k}={\left(1-\frac{k}{n}-\frac{3k(n-k)}{n^{2}-n}\right)}\Delta v-\frac{6k(n-k)}{(n^{3}-n)he^{-\gamma h/2}}\Delta x,\\

(A direct calculation verifies

This choice is designed to cancel the leading divergent term in

[

The two trajectories differ structurally as follows
(see Figure
2
). The position
component of (

, which is the position update of the
OBABO chain in the absence of force and noise: the
trajectory (
3.26
) uses noise corrections
only on the velocity component, with the positions evolving freely along

(
3.19

corrections in both components and, by construction, minimizes

\sum_{k=1}^{n}|E_{k}|^{2}
over all admissible trajectories. The trajectory
of
[

is therefore suboptimal for

\sum_{k=1}^{n}|E_{k}|^{2}

(cf. (

Consequently, the additional position corrections
in (

higher-order terms, and the two trajectories yield TV bounds of the same
leading order in

.

Both trajectories are independent of
\nabla U

the linearized problem
\nabla U\equiv 0
, and the
\nabla U

contribution to the general case is absorbed via the Lipschitz argument
in Lemma

information about
\nabla U
into a coalescence trajectory directly is
not straightforward in either framework, since such a trajectory would
require knowing the chain trajectories in advance.

Lemma 3.22
.

Let

Proof.

Let

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(f(X)=Y)

\displaystyle=\int\mathbbm{1}_{A}(x,y)\,(\mu_{X}\otimes\mu_{Y})(dx\,dy)=\int\int\mathbbm{1}_{A}(x,y)\,\mu_{Y}(dy)\,\mu_{X}(dx)

\displaystyle=\int\int\mathbbm{1}_{f(x)}(y)\,\mu_{Y}(dy)\,\mu_{X}(dx)=\int\mu_{Y}(\{f(x)\})\,\mu_{X}(dx)=0,

where the last equality comes from the assumption that
\mu_{Y}
is non-atomic.

Proof of Proposition

Remark 3.23
.

Proposition

shows that the coupling
(\xi,\tilde{\xi})
between the two standard Gaussians
\xi

\tilde{\xi}
that we construct above is such that

{d_{\textup{TV}}{\left(\textup{Law}(\xi),\textup{Law}{\left(\Psi^{n}_{z,\tilde{z}}(\xi)\right)}\right)}}=\mathbb{P}{\left(Z^{h}_{n}\neq\tilde{Z}^{h}_{n}\right)},

where

and

are the final values of the two OBABO discretizations with initial values

and increments
\xi
and

respectively. The coupling

thus induces a coupling
(Z^{h}_{k},\tilde{Z}^{h}_{k})_{0\leq k\leq n}=(\Psi^{k}_{z}(\xi_{1},\dots\xi_{k}),\Psi^{k}_{\tilde{z}}(\tilde{\xi}_{1},\dots\tilde{\xi}_{k}))_{0\leq k\leq n}
of the Markov chains that has the property that

matches the TV bound of

. This coupling is
non-Markovian
in the sense that the joint chain
(Z^{h}_{k},\tilde{Z}^{h}_{k})_{0\leq k\leq n}
is not a Markov chain with respect to the filtration
\mathcal{F}_{k}\coloneqq\sigma(\{(Z^{h}_{i},\tilde{Z}^{h}_{i})\mid 0\leq i\leq k\})
. This is due to the fact that the vector of increments
\tilde{\xi}
depends on the entire vector of increments
\xi
at once.

Another aspect of the coupling constructed above is that if
z\neq\tilde{z}
, then typically

\mathbb{P}(Z_{k}^{h}=\tilde{Z}_{k}^{h})=0,\quad k\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}.

In other words, the processes
(Z_{k}^{h})_{k=0}^{n}
and
(Z_{k}^{h})_{k=0}^{n}
fail to meet prior to the final time
n
. This is due to the fact that on
A
it holds that

\Psi_{z}^{k}(\xi)=\Psi_{\tilde{z}}^{k}(\xi)+y_{k}

(see Lemma/Definition
3.9
), and typically

for

(see (
3.18
) and (
3.19
)). In particular, the coupling constructed above is designed specifically for
n
time steps, and cannot be extended to the whole time-line in an obvious way.

The non-Markovianity is unavoidable: Section
4

shows that no Markovian coupling achieves the optimal asymptotic TV
decay for the linear kinetic Langevin equation.

Remark 3.24
.

The construction of the above coupling relies solely on the fact that the coalescence mapping
\Psi^{n}_{z,\tilde{z}}
is a diffeomorphism. Recall from
Remark

3.13
that other gHMC methods as well as other splitting methods give rise to diffeomorphic coalescence maps. In particular, the same reasoning can be applied to obtain a coupling for these sampling methods. However, in order to explicitly implement these couplings, we need an explicit (closed form) expression of the corresponding coalescence map
\Psi^{n}_{z,\tilde{z}}
. Out of the splitting schemes mentioned in
Remark

3.13
, such an explicit expression of
\Psi^{n}_{z,\tilde{z}}
is only available for the OBABO and the BOAOB schemes.

4.
There are no asymptotically optimal Markovian couplings

In the search for explicit couplings achieving the correct asymptotic TV
decay rate, Markovian couplings are a natural first candidate: they have
been used successfully for TV mixing of the overdamped Langevin
dynamics
[
25
]
and for Wasserstein mixing of the kinetic
Langevin dynamics
[
26
]
. The main result of this
section is that this approach fails for the kinetic Langevin equation
with quadratic potential:
Theorems
4.3

and
4.5

below show that no Markovian coupling – continuous-time or discrete –
can achieve the optimal asymptotic TV decay rate. The argument
follows
[
2
]
, where an analogous suboptimality is
established for the Kolmogorov diffusion (the joint process of a
standard Brownian motion and its time integral).

Let us briefly introduce the setting. We consider the kinetic Langevin equation (
1.1
) with the (isotropic) quadratic potential
U(x)=\alpha|x|^{2}
, where
\alpha\geq 0
. This results in the following
2d
-dimensional SDE:

(4.1)

\displaystyle\begin{split}dX_{t}&=V_{t}\,dt,\\
dV_{t}&=-\alpha X_{t}\,dt-\gamma V_{t}\,dt+\sqrt{2\gamma}\,dW_{t},\end{split}

where
W
is a standard
d
-dimensional Brownian motion. Throughout this section we fix
x,\tilde{x},v,\tilde{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and let
(Z_{t})_{t\geq 0}=(X_{t},V_{t})_{t\geq 0}
and
(\tilde{Z}_{t})_{t\geq 0}=(\tilde{X}_{t},\tilde{V}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
denote solutions to (
4.1
) with initial values
z=(x,v)
and
\tilde{z}=(\tilde{x},\tilde{v})
, respectively. We set
\Delta x=\tilde{x}-x
and
\Delta v=\tilde{v}-v
. We also define
\lambda_{-},\lambda_{+}\in\mathbb{C}
by

(4.2)

\lambda_{\pm}=-\frac{\gamma}{2}\pm\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\gamma^{2}-4\alpha};

these are the eigenvalues of the drift matrix associated with (
4.1
) (see also (
4.8
) below).

Definition 4.1
.

A coupling
\mu
of the joint processes
(Z_{t},\tilde{Z}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is called
Markovian
if
(Z_{t},\tilde{Z}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a Markov process under
\mu
(with respect to the filtration
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
generated by
(Z_{t},\tilde{Z}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
). A coupling
\mu
satisfies the
now-equals-forever
property if for every
0\leq s\leq t
we have that
\mu(\{Z_{s}=\tilde{Z}_{s}\}\cap\{Z_{t}\neq\tilde{Z}_{t}\})=0
.

Remark 4.2
.

The
now-equals-forever
property seems to have been introduced in
[
43
]
, couplings with this property are also sometimes called
sticky
. Somewhat confusingly, the term
faithful
is also sometimes used for the
now-is-forever
property; this concept was also introduced in
[
43
]
but with a distinctly different meaning.

Theorem 4.3
.

Assume that
\gamma^{2}>4\alpha\geq 0
and assume
\lambda_{-}\Delta x-\Delta v=0
and
\Delta x\neq 0
.
Then there exists a constant
C>0
such that for all
t>0
one has

(4.3)

{d_{\textup{TV}}{\left(\textup{Law}(Z_{t}),\textup{Law}(\tilde{Z}_{t})\right)}}\leq Ce^{\lambda_{-}t}|\Delta z|.

On the other hand, for all choices of
\Delta x,\Delta v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and for every Markovian coupling
\mu
of
(Z_{t},\tilde{Z}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
there exists constants
t_{\mu},c_{\mu}>0
such that for all
t\geq t_{\mu}
one has

(4.4)

\mu(Z_{t}\neq\tilde{Z}_{t})\geq c_{\mu}\min\big(t^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}},e^{\lambda_{+}t}\big).

If moreover
\mu
satisfies the now-equals-forever property, then (
4.4
) holds true for all
t>0
.

Note that if
\lambda_{-}\Delta x-\Delta v=0
, then
\Delta z=(\Delta x,\Delta v)
is in the eigenspace of the drift operator corresponding to eigenvalue
\lambda_{-}
, i.e.
\Delta z
lies in the direction where the drift of
Z
has the steepest decline. This results in the decline in TV distance given by (
4.3
). However, as one sees in the proofs below, a Markovian coupling cannot result in a meet-up if it stays in this eigenspace, which results in the suboptimal probability of not meeting (
4.4
). Note moreover that
\lambda_{+}=0
if and only if
\alpha=0
; this is the situation that the probability of not meeting is bounded from below by
c_{\mu}t^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}
.

It is typically easier to construct couplings for discrete-time processes than for continuous-time processes. While discrete-time couplings indeed provide some more ‘wiggle room’, the following theorem demonstrates that as the step size tends to
0
, the meeting probability of discrete Markovian couplings exhibits the same issues as in the continuous time setting.

For
h>0
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
we set
Z^{h}_{k}=Z_{hk}
and
\tilde{Z}^{h}_{k}=\tilde{Z}_{hk}
; the Markov processes
(Z^{h}_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(\tilde{Z}^{h}_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
represent discrete approximations of
(Z_{t})_{t\geq 0}
and
(\tilde{Z}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
for which one samples exactly from the distribution of the increments.

Definition 4.4
.

A coupling
\mu_{h}
of the joint chain
(Z^{h}_{k},\tilde{Z}^{h}_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is called

if
(Z^{h}_{k},\tilde{Z}^{h}_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is a Markov chain under
\mu_{h}
(with respect to the filtration

generated by

\mu_{h}
satisfies the

property if for every

we have that
\mu_{h}(\{Z_{k}=\tilde{Z}_{k}\}\cap\{Z_{\ell}\neq\tilde{Z}_{\ell}\})=0
.

Theorem 4.5
.

Assume that
\gamma^{2}>4\alpha\geq 0

.

,

4.1.
Proof of upper bound (
4.3
)

The linear SDE (
4.1
) can be represented in terms of
(Z_{t})_{t\geq 0}=(X_{t},V_{t})_{t\geq 0}
as

(4.8)

dZ_{t}=AZ_{t}\,dt+B\,d\bar{W}_{t},

where
(\bar{W}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is now a
2d
-dimensional Brownian motion and
A,B\in\mathbb{R}^{2d\times 2d}
are given by

A=\begin{pmatrix}0&\id_{d}\\
-\alpha\id_{d}&-\gamma\id_{d}\end{pmatrix},\qquad B=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&\sqrt{2\gamma}\id_{d}\end{pmatrix}

The solution to this SDE at time
t>0
is given by

Z_{t}=e^{tA}Z_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}e^{(t-s)A}Bd\bar{W}_{s}.

In the case of a deterministic initial value
Z_{0}=z=(x,v)\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
this means that the solution is distributed as
Z_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(e^{tA}z,\Sigma_{t})
, where the covariance
\Sigma_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d\times 2d}
is given by

\Sigma_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}e^{sA}BB^{T}e^{sA^{T}}ds.

By (
2.2
), the TV distance of two solutions
(Z_{t})_{t\geq 0}
,
(\tilde{Z}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
of (
4.1
) with initial values
z,\tilde{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
at time
t>0
is therefore

(4.9)

{d_{\textup{TV}}{\left(\textup{Law}(Z_{t}),\textup{Law}(\tilde{Z}_{t})\right)}}=2\Phi{\left(\frac{|\Sigma_{t}^{-1/2}e^{tA}\Delta z|}{2}\right)}-1,

where
\Delta z=\tilde{z}-z
.

Note that the eigenvalues of the matrix
A
are given by (
4.2
). If
\lambda_{-}\Delta x-\Delta v=0
, then
\Delta z
is in the eigenspace of
\lambda_{-}
so that
e^{tA}\Delta z=e^{\lambda_{-}t}\Delta z
. As
2\Phi(x)-1\leq\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{\pi}}x
for all
x\geq 0
, all that remains to establish (
4.3
) is to prove that the operator norm of
\Sigma_{t}^{-1/2}
appearing in (
4.9
) can be bounded uniformly in time. For this, we employ the following lemma.

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
(M_{t})_{t>0}
be sequence of
d
-dimensional positive definite matrices such that
M_{t}\succeq M_{s}
for all
t\geq s
. Then for all
t\geq s
we have

\big\|M_{t}^{-1/2}\big\|\leq\big\|M_{s}^{-1/2}\big\|.

Proof.

The matrix
M_{t}
is positive definite for any
t>0
and is therefore symmetric and invertible. Hence,

\big\|M_{t}^{-1/2}\big\|^{2}=\lambda_{\max}(M_{t}^{-1})={\left(\lambda_{\min}(M_{t})\right)}^{-1},

where
\lambda_{\max}(M_{t}^{-1}),\lambda_{\min}(M_{t})>0
denote the largest and smallest eigenvalue of
M_{t}^{-1}
and
M_{t}
respectively. Suppose that
t\geq s
, then for all
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
we have

z^{T}M_{t}z=z^{T}M_{s}z+z^{T}(M_{t}-M_{s})z\geq z^{T}M_{s}z.

Taking the minimum over
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
|z|=1
on both sides and using that
M_{t}
and
M_{s}
are symmetric we obtain

\lambda_{\min}(M_{t})=\min_{|z|=1}z^{T}M_{t}z\geq\min_{|z|=1}z^{T}M_{s}z=\lambda_{\min}(M_{s}).

This means that for all
t\geq s
we have

\big\|M_{t}^{-1/2}\big\|={\left(\lambda_{\min}(M_{t})\right)}^{-1/2}\leq{\left(\lambda_{\min}(M_{s})\right)}^{-1/2}=\big\|M_{s}^{-1/2}\big\|,

which completes the proof.
∎

The sequence of matrices
(\Sigma_{t})_{t>0}
satisfies the assumptions of the lemma above, as for
{0<s<t}
we have that
\Sigma_{t}-\Sigma_{s}=\int_{s}^{t}e^{rA}BB^{T}e^{rA^{T}}dr
, which is positive definite. In view of the discussion preceding Lemma
4.7
, this completes the proof of the upper bound (
4.3
).

4.2.
The process
(Q_{t})_{t\geq 0}

For a solution
(Z_{t})_{t\geq 0}=(X_{t},V_{t})_{t\geq 0}
to (
4.1
), let

Q_{t}=X_{t}-\lambda_{-}^{-1}V_{t}.

This choice is exactly such that
Q_{t}=0
if and only if
Z_{t}
is contained in the eigenspace of the drift matrix
A
corresponding to
\lambda_{-}
(see (
4.2
) and (
4.8
)).

We can rewrite the SDE (
4.1
) in terms of
(X_{t},Q_{t})_{t\geq 0}
by noting that

\displaystyle dQ_{t}

\displaystyle=dX_{t}-\lambda_{-}^{-1}\,dV_{t}

\displaystyle=\alpha\lambda_{-}^{-1}X_{t}\,dt+(1+\gamma\lambda_{-}^{-1})V_{t}\,dt-\sqrt{2\gamma}\lambda_{-}^{-1}\,dW_{t}

\displaystyle=(\alpha\lambda_{-}^{-1}+\gamma+\lambda_{-})X_{t}\,dt-(\gamma+\lambda_{-})Q_{t}\,dt-\sqrt{2\gamma}\lambda_{-}^{-1}\,dW_{t}

Quick calculations show that
\lambda_{-}(\alpha\lambda_{-}^{-1}+\gamma+\lambda_{-})=\alpha+\gamma\lambda_{-}+\lambda_{-}^{2}=0
and
-(\gamma+\lambda_{-})=\lambda_{+}
, so that (
4.1
) is equivalent to

(4.10a)

\displaystyle dX_{t}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{-}X_{t}\,dt-\lambda_{-}Q_{t}\,dt,

(4.10b)

\displaystyle dQ_{t}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{+}Q_{t}\,dt-\frac{\sqrt{2\gamma}}{\lambda_{-}}\,dW_{t}.

We see that on its own,
(Q_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process, with exact solution

(4.11)

Q_{t}=e^{\lambda_{+}t}q-\frac{\sqrt{2\gamma}}{\lambda_{-}}\int_{0}^{t}e^{\lambda_{+}(t-s)}\,dW_{s},

where
q=x-\lambda_{-}^{-1}v
is its initial value. In particular, if
(Q_{t})_{t\geq 0}
,
(\tilde{Q}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
satisfy (
4.11
) (in the distributional sense) with initial values
q,\tilde{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then clearly
q=\tilde{q}
implies that
\textup{Law}(Q_{t})=\textup{Law}(\tilde{Q}_{t})
for all
t\geq 0
. Moreover,
Q_{t}
is normally distributed with mean
e^{\lambda_{+}t}q
and covariance that for
\alpha>0
equals

\textup{Cov}(Q_{t})=\frac{2\gamma}{\lambda_{-}^{2}}{\left(\int_{0}^{t}e^{2\lambda_{+}s}\,ds\right)}\id_{d}=-\frac{\gamma}{\lambda_{+}\lambda_{-}^{2}}(1-e^{2\lambda_{+}t})\id_{d}=-\frac{\gamma}{\alpha\lambda_{-}}(1-e^{2\lambda_{+}t})\id_{d},

where we use that
\lambda_{+}\lambda_{-}=\alpha
, while for
\alpha=0
we likewise have covariance
{\textup{Cov}(Q_{t})=2\gamma^{-1}t}
. Thus, by (
2.2
) the processes
(Q_{t})_{t\geq 0}
,
(\tilde{Q}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
satisfying (
4.11
) with initial values
q,\tilde{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
have a TV distance that for
\alpha>0
is given by

(4.12)

{d_{\textup{TV}}{\left(\textup{Law}(Q_{t}),\textup{Law}(\tilde{Q}_{t})\right)}}=2\Phi{\left(\frac{\kappa e^{\lambda_{+}t}|\Delta q|}{2\sqrt{1-e^{2\lambda_{+}t}}}\right)}-1=2\Phi{\left(\frac{\kappa|\Delta q|}{2\sqrt{e^{-2\lambda_{+}t}-1}}\right)}-1,

where
\kappa=\sqrt{-\alpha\lambda_{-}/\gamma}
and
\Delta q=\tilde{q}-q
, and for
\alpha=0
is given by

(4.13)

{d_{\textup{TV}}{\left(\textup{Law}(Q_{t}),\textup{Law}(\tilde{Q}_{t})\right)}}=2\Phi{\left(\frac{|\Delta q|}{2\sqrt{2\gamma^{-1}t}}\right)}-1.

4.3.
Proof of the lower bound (
4.4
) for
\alpha>0

The following lemma demonstrates that the probability of not meeting
\mu(Z_{t}\neq\tilde{Z}_{t})
of a Markovian coupling eventually contracts at best exponentially in
t
with rate
|\lambda_{+}|
. Note that the lower bound (
4.4
) in Theorem
4.3
is a direct consequence of this lemma, the proof of which is an adaptation of the proof of Lemma 1 in
[
2
]
to our setting.

Lemma 4.8
.

Assume that
\gamma^{2}>4\alpha>0
and let
\mu
be a Markovian coupling of
(Z_{t},\tilde{Z}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
of two solutions to (
4.1
) with initial values such that
\Delta z\neq 0
. Then there exist constants
t_{\mu}\geq 0
,
c_{\mu}>0
such that for all
t\geq t_{\mu}
we have

(4.14)

\mu(Z_{t}\neq\tilde{Z}_{t})\geq c_{\mu}e^{\lambda_{+}t}.

Furthermore, if
\mu
satisfies the now-equals forever property, then we can pick
t_{\mu}=0
.

Proof.

Let
(Q_{t})_{t\geq 0}
and
(\tilde{Q}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
satisfy
Q_{t}=X_{t}-\lambda_{-}^{-1}V_{t}
and
\tilde{Q}_{t}=\tilde{X}_{t}-\lambda_{-}^{-1}\tilde{V}_{t}
,
t\geq 0
.
Suppose first that
\mu(Q_{t}=\tilde{Q}_{t})=1
for all
t\geq 0
. Fubini’s theorem implies that

(4.15)

\mu(Q_{t}=\tilde{Q}_{t}\textup{ for almost every }t\geq 0)=1.

Since the paths of
Q
and
\tilde{Q}
are continuous this implies
Q
and
\tilde{Q}
are synchronously coupled
\mu
-almost surely. In particular, by (
4.10
) we have that

\mu(\tilde{X}_{t}-X_{t}=e^{\lambda_{-}t}\Delta x\textup{ for all }t>0)=1,

showing that
\mu(Z_{t}\neq\tilde{Z}_{t})=1
for all
t>0
.

Now assume that there exists some
t_{0}\geq 0
such that
\mu(Q_{t_{0}}\neq\tilde{Q}_{t_{0}})>0
. Since the coupling
\mu
is Markovian, the shifted process
(Q_{t_{0}+s},\tilde{Q}_{t_{0}+s})_{s\geq 0}
conditioned on
\mathcal{F}_{t_{0}}
constitutes a coupling between two OU processes that have different initial values with positive probability. Since
Z_{t}=\tilde{Z}_{t}
implies that
Q_{t}=\tilde{Q}_{t}
, we have for every
t>t_{0}
that

(4.16)

\mu(Z_{t}\neq\tilde{Z}_{t})\geq\mu(Q_{t}\neq\tilde{Q}_{t})=\mathbb{E}_{\mu}{\left[\mathbb{E}^{\mathcal{F}_{t_{0}}}_{\mu}{\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\{Q_{t}\neq\tilde{Q}_{t}\}}\right]}\right]}.

The TV distance of the two OU processes
Q
and
\tilde{Q}
is given by (
4.12
); for
t>t_{0}
we have

(4.17)

\mathbb{E}^{\mathcal{F}_{t_{0}}}_{\mu}{\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\{Q_{t}\neq\tilde{Q}_{t}\}}\right]}\geq 2\Phi{\left(\frac{\kappa|\tilde{Q}_{t_{0}}-Q_{t_{0}}|}{2\sqrt{e^{-2\lambda_{+}(t-t_{0})}-1}}\right)}-1.

If
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfies
|y|\leq\tfrac{2}{\kappa}\sqrt{e^{-2\lambda_{+}(t-t_{0})}-1}
, then we can bound

\displaystyle 2\Phi{\left(\frac{\kappa|y|}{2\sqrt{e^{-2\lambda_{+}(t-t_{0})}-1}}\right)}-1

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}{\left(\left|\mathcal{N}(0,1)\right|\leq\frac{\kappa|y|}{2\sqrt{e^{-2\lambda_{+}(t-t_{0})}-1}}\right)}

(4.18)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\kappa}{\sqrt{2\pi e}}\frac{|y|}{\sqrt{e^{-2\lambda_{+}(t-t_{0})}-1}},

using the fact that the standard normal density function can be bounded as
\varphi(z)\geq 1/\sqrt{2\pi e}
for
z\in[-1,1]
.

Since
\lambda_{+}<0
, we have that
\mathbb{E}_{\mu}{\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{|\tilde{Q}_{t_{0}}-Q_{t_{0}}|\leq\tfrac{2}{\kappa}\sqrt{e^{-2\lambda_{+}(t-t_{0})}-1}\right\}}|\tilde{Q}_{t_{0}}-Q_{t_{0}}|\right]}
monotonically increases to
\mathbb{E}_{\mu}{\left[|\tilde{Q}_{t_{0}}-Q_{t_{0}}|\right]}
as
t\to\infty
, and thus there exists a
t_{\mu}>t_{0}
such that for all
t\geq t_{\mu}
we have that

(4.19)

\mathbb{E}_{\mu}{\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{|\tilde{Q}_{t_{0}}-Q_{t_{0}}|\leq\tfrac{2}{\kappa}\sqrt{e^{-2\lambda_{+}(t-t_{0})}-1}\right\}}|\tilde{Q}_{t_{0}}-Q_{t_{0}}|\right]}\geq\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{\mu}{\left[|\tilde{Q}_{t_{0}}-Q_{t_{0}}|\right]}=\frac{\sqrt{2\pi e}}{\kappa}c_{\mu},

where
c_{\mu}\coloneqq\tfrac{\kappa}{2\sqrt{2\pi e}}\mathbb{E}_{\mu}{\left[|\tilde{Q}_{t_{0}}-Q_{t_{0}}|\right]}
. Note that
c_{\mu}>0
by definition of
t_{0}
.

Combining the above observations we see that the probability of not meeting at time
t\geq t_{\mu}
can be bounded from below as follows

\displaystyle\mu(Z_{t}\neq\tilde{Z}_{t})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq: coupling probability simple bound (Markovian proof)}}}{{\geq}}\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\mu}^{\mathcal{F}_{t_{0}}}[\mathbbm{1}_{\{Q_{t}\neq\tilde{Q}_{t}\}}]\right]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq: dTV Q_t0 (Markovian proof)}}}{{\geq}}\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left[2\Phi{\left(\frac{\kappa|\tilde{Q}_{t_{0}}-Q_{t_{0}}|}{2\sqrt{e^{-2\lambda_{+}(t-t_{0})}-1}}\right)}-1\right]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{|\tilde{Q}_{t_{0}}-Q_{t_{0}}|\leq\tfrac{2}{\kappa}\sqrt{e^{-2\lambda_{+}(t-t_{0})}-1}\right\}}\left\{2\Phi{\left(\frac{\kappa|\tilde{Q}_{t_{0}}-Q_{t_{0}}|}{2\sqrt{e^{-2\lambda_{+}(t-t_{0})}-1}}\right)}-1\right\}\right]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq: Q_t TV lower bound (Markovian proof)}}}{{\geq}}\mathbb{E}_{\mu}{\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{|\tilde{Q}_{t_{0}}-Q_{t_{0}}|\leq\tfrac{2}{\kappa}\sqrt{e^{-2\lambda_{+}(t-t_{0})}-1)}\right\}}\frac{\kappa}{\sqrt{2\pi e}}\frac{|\tilde{Q}_{t_{0}}-Q_{t_{0}}|}{\sqrt{e^{-2\lambda_{+}(t-t_{0})}-1}}\right]}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq: Q_t difference restricted lower bound (Markovian proof)}}}{{\geq}}\frac{c_{\mu}}{\sqrt{e^{-2\lambda_{+}(t-t_{0})}-1}}\geq c_{\mu}e^{\lambda_{+}(t-t_{0})}\geq c_{\mu}e^{\lambda_{+}t}.

This completes the proof of (
4.14
). If in addition
\mu
satisfies the now-equals forever property, then we have for all
0\leq t<t_{\mu}
that
\mu(Z_{t}\neq\tilde{Z}_{t})\geq\mu(Z_{t_{\mu}}\neq\tilde{Z}_{t_{\mu}})
. The bound (
4.14
) can therefore be extended to all
t\geq 0
by appropriately scaling
c_{\mu}
.
∎

4.4.
Proof of the lower bound (
4.6
) for
\alpha=0

Note that when
\alpha=0
, then the equation (
4.1
) admits an exact solution
(Z_{t})_{t\geq 0}=(X_{t},V_{t})_{t\geq 0}
for any initial value
z=(x,v)\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
,
which is given by

X_{t}=x+\gamma^{-1}(1-e^{-\gamma t})v+\sqrt{2\gamma^{-1}}\int_{0}^{t}(1-e^{-\gamma(t-s)})\,dW_{s},

V_{t}=e^{-\gamma t}v+\sqrt{2\gamma}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\gamma(t-s)}\,dW_{s}.

In particular, at time
t>0
the solution
(Z_{t})_{t\geq 0}
to the potential free kinetic Langevin equation with initial value
z\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
is distributed as
Z_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(e^{tA}z,\Sigma_{t})
, where
e^{tA},\Sigma_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d\times 2d}
are given by

(4.20)

\displaystyle\begin{split}e^{tA}&=\begin{pmatrix}\id_{d}&\gamma^{-1}{\left(1-e^{-\gamma t}\right)}\id_{d}\\
0&e^{-\gamma t}\id_{d}\end{pmatrix},\\
\Sigma_{t}&=\begin{pmatrix}{\left(2\gamma^{-1}t-4\gamma^{-2}{\left(1-e^{-\gamma t}\right)}+\gamma^{-2}(1-e^{-2\gamma t})\right)}\id_{d}&\gamma^{-1}{\left(1-e^{-\gamma t}\right)}^{2}\id_{d}\\
\gamma^{-1}{\left(1-e^{-\gamma t}\right)}^{2}\id_{d}&{\left(1-e^{-2\gamma t}\right)}\id_{d}\end{pmatrix}.\end{split}

Moreover, in this case the eigenvalues of the drift matrix
A
in (
4.2
) are simply given by

\lambda_{-}=-\gamma,\qquad\lambda_{+}=0.

Recall that given processes
(Z_{t})_{t\geq 0}=(X_{t},V_{t})_{t\geq 0}
and
(\tilde{Z}_{t})_{t\geq 0}=(\tilde{X}_{t},\tilde{V}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
we define
Q_{t}=X_{t}+\gamma^{-1}V_{t}
and
\tilde{Q}_{t}=\tilde{X}_{t}+\gamma^{-1}\tilde{V}_{t}
,
t\geq 0
. Recall that
Q_{t}=0
whenever
Z_{t}
is in the eigenspace of the drift matrix
A
corresponding to eigenvalue
\lambda_{-}=-\gamma
. We also define
Q^{h}_{k}=Q_{hk}
and
\tilde{Q}^{h}_{k}=\tilde{Q}_{hk}
,
h>0
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

The main difference compared to the argument providing a lower bound in the continuous time setting is that the assumption that
\mu_{h}(\cap_{k=0}^{\infty}\{Q^{h}_{k}=\tilde{Q}^{h}_{k}\})=1
and
\Delta x\neq 0
does
not
guarantee that
\mu_{h}(\cap_{k=0}^{\infty}\{Z^{h}_{k}\neq\tilde{Z}^{h}_{k}\})=1
. This is inherent to the discrete set-up, where we can not control the behavior of the processes on the time intervals
(hk,h(k+1))
, on which the positions could be driven together. The value of the step size, however, crucially limits the meeting probability, due to the fact that the variance of the positions over these time intervals scales as
h^{3}
for small
h
. This is demonstrated in Lemma
4.10
below. First, however, we use arguments analogous to the proof of Lemma
4.8
to deal the situation that
\mu_{h}(\cap_{k=0}^{\infty}\{Q^{h}_{k}=\tilde{Q}^{h}_{k}\})<1
. The Lemmas
4.9
and
4.10
together provide the lower bound (
4.6
) when
\alpha=0
.

Lemma 4.9
.

Assume that
\gamma^{2}>\alpha=0
, let
h>0
, and let
\mu_{h}
be a Markovian coupling of
(Z_{k}^{h},\tilde{Z}_{k}^{h})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}=(Z_{hk},\tilde{Z}_{hk})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
where
(Z_{t},\tilde{Z}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
are two solutions to (
4.1
) with initial values such that
\Delta z\neq 0
. Suppose that there is a
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\mu_{h}(Q^{h}_{k}\neq\tilde{Q}^{h}_{k})>0
. Then there exist constants
k_{\mu_{h}}\in\mathbb{N}
,
c_{\mu_{h}}>0
such that for all
k\geq k_{\mu_{h}}
we have

(4.21)

\mu_{h}(Z^{h}_{k}\neq\tilde{Z}^{h}_{k})\geq\frac{c_{\mu_{h}}}{\sqrt{hk+1}}.

Furthermore, if in addition
\mu
satisfies the now-equals forever property, then we can pick
k_{\mu_{h}}=0
.

Proof.

Let
k_{0}=\min\{k\in\mathbb{N}:\mu_{h}(Q^{h}_{k}\neq\tilde{Q}^{h}_{k})>0\}
. Since the coupling
\mu_{h}
is Markovian, the shifted chain
(Q^{h}_{k_{0}+k},\tilde{Q}^{h}_{k_{0}+k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
conditioned on
\mathcal{F}_{k_{0}}
constitutes a coupling between two processes following the (discretized) dynamics of (
4.10b
) and having different initial values with positive probability under
\mu_{h}
. Since
Z^{h}_{k}=\tilde{Z}^{h}_{k}
implies that
Q^{h}_{k}=\tilde{Q}^{h}_{k}
, we have for every

5.
Quantifying the lack of optimality for iterated one-shot couplings

The iterated one-shot coupling is a coupling for Markov chains; it is defined such that the probability of two chains meeting at time
n\in\mathbb{N}
is maximized given the state at time
n-1
. In particular, this is Markovian coupling satisfying the now-equals-forever property in the sense of Definition
4.4
, and it is the natural discretization of the well-known reflection coupling for continuous diffusion equations. The coupling can also be thought of as a greedy algorithm for maximizing the meeting probability iteratively. It has been used effectively in e.g.
[
23
]
to bound the TV distance of Euler discretizations of the overdamped Langevin equation, as well as in other places, such as
[
30
]
.

While
[
23
]
demonstrates that the iterated one-shot coupling provides asymptotically optimal meeting probabilities for the
overdamped
Langevin equation, Theorem
4.5
above implies that this cannot be the case for the
kinetic
Langevin equation. Indeed, Theorem
5.1
below provides an explicit (exact) expression for the meeting probability under the iterated one-shot coupling for linear Markov chains with Gaussian increments, i.e.  for the type of Markov chain arising when discretizing a linear Langevin equation. This explicit expression for the meeting probability reveals that at least one of terms obtained as a lower bound in Theorem
4.5
is sharp, see Example
5.12
below.
Theorem

5.1
and the subsequent remarks are also of independent interest: they provide guidelines on when the iterated one-shot coupling may provide asymptotically optimal bounds for the TV distance for more general diffusion equations. Both the notation and most of the arguments in this section are inspired by results in Section 6 of
[
23
]
.

5.1.
Iterated one-shot coupling for linear Markov chains

As mentioned above, Theorem
5.1
below concerns establishing an expression for the meeting probability for an iterated one-shot coupling for Markov chains with Gaussian increments. More specifically, the covariance of the increment is assumed to be independent of the state (though it may be time-dependent). The goal of this section is to provide the construction of an iterated one-shot coupling in this setting. In order to do so, one needs a maximal (i.e. optimal) coupling for Gaussian distributions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with the same non-singular covariance matrix
\Sigma
but with (possibly) different means
\mu
and
\tilde{\mu}
.
Multiple such maximal couplings exist. In this section we use the
reflection coupling
, which we outline below; see e.g.
[
7
,
12
,
23
]
for a more extensive treatment.

5.1.1.
Reflection coupling for Gaussians with shared covariance

Let
\mu,\tilde{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and let
\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
. As announced above, we provide the construction of a maximal coupling for
\mathcal{N}(\mu,\Sigma^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}})
and
\mathcal{N}(\tilde{\mu},\Sigma^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}})
known as the reflection coupling.

Let
B\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
by a matrix such that
BB^{T}=\Sigma
and define

\displaystyle E=B^{-1}(\tilde{\mu}-\mu),\qquad e=\begin{cases}\frac{E}{|E|}&\textup{if }E\neq 0,\\
0&\textup{if }E=0.\end{cases}

and

p(z)=\frac{\varphi(E-z)}{\varphi(z)}=\exp{\left(-|E|^{2}/2+\langle E,z\rangle\right)},\qquad z\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

where
\varphi\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is the density function of a one-dimensional standard Gaussian. Let
\xi\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\id_{d})
and
U\sim U(0,1)
be independent from each other and set
X=\mu+B\xi
. Furthermore, consider the random variable

(5.1)

\tilde{\xi}=\mathbbm{1}_{\{U\leq p(\xi)\}}(\xi-E)+\mathbbm{1}_{\{U>p(\xi)\}}(\id_{d}-2ee^{T})\xi

and set
\tilde{X}=\tilde{\mu}+B\tilde{\xi}
. Note that
(\id_{d}-2ee^{T})
describes a reflection in the linear subspace orthogonal to
E=B^{-1}(\tilde{\mu}-\mu)
, so that
X=\tilde{X}
on
\{U\leq p(\xi)\}
and
B^{-1}(\tilde{X}-\tilde{\mu})
is the reflection of
B^{-1}(X-\mu)
in this linear subspace on
\{U>p(\xi)\}
.

The random variable
\tilde{\xi}
is distributed as
\mathcal{N}(0,\id_{d})

[
8
, Section 2.3.2]
. Consequently, the random variable
(X,\tilde{X})
has marginals
\mathcal{N}(\mu,\Sigma)
and
\mathcal{N}(\tilde{\mu},\Sigma)
and is therefore a realization of a coupling between these distributions. As mentioned, this coupling is optimal in the sense that

(5.2)

\mathbb{P}(X\neq\tilde{X})={d_{\textup{TV}}{\left(\mathcal{N}(\mu,\Sigma),\mathcal{N}(\tilde{\mu},\Sigma)\right)}}=2\Phi{\left(\frac{\left|B^{-1}(\tilde{\mu}-\mu)\right|}{2}\right)}-1,

where
\Phi\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is the cumulative distribution function of the one-dimensional standard Gaussian distribution, see
[
8
, Section 2.3.2]
(and see Equations (
2.1
) and (
2.2
)). We note that if
\mu=\tilde{\mu}
, then
E=0
,
p\equiv 1
, and thus
\tilde{\xi}=\xi
and
\tilde{X}=X
.

We now construct an iterated one-shot coupling for discrete time Markov chains with Gaussian increments based on the reflection coupling introduced above. First we introduce the Markov chains: for
k\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
let

5.2.

Theorem

Theorem 5.1
.

Remark 5.2

Since both

5.5
) that

|B_{k}^{-1}\Delta Z_{k}|=\left|B_{k}^{-1}{\left(A_{k}\Delta Z_{k-1}+B_{k}(\tilde{\xi}_{k}-\xi_{k})\right)}\right|=\left|E_{k}-2\langle e_{k},\xi_{k}\rangle e_{k}\right|=\left||E_{k}|-2\langle e_{k},\xi_{k}\rangle\right|.

Furthermore, note that
U_{k}>p_{k}(\xi_{k})
ensures that
p_{k}(\xi_{k})<1
, which by definition of
p_{k}
implies that

(5.9)

|E_{k}|-2\langle e_{k},\xi_{k}\rangle>0.

Combination of these two observations shows that if both
E_{k}\neq 0
and
U_{k}>p_{k}(\xi_{k})
we also have that
\Delta Z_{k}\neq 0
, proving the first equivalence. The second equivalence follows immediately from the fact that
E_{k}=B_{k}^{-1}A_{k}\Delta Z_{k-1}
and that
A_{k}
and
B_{k}
are non-singular.
∎

If the two chains have not met before the
k^{\text{th}}
step, the vector
e_{k}
determines the linear subspace in which
\xi_{k}
is reflected to obtain
\tilde{\xi}_{k}
in the case that
U_{k}>p_{k}(\xi_{k})
. The following lemma provides a deterministic expression for
e_{k}
.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
(Z_{k},\tilde{Z}_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be given by the iterated one-shot coupling defined in Section
5.1.2
applied to the Markov chains (
5.7
). For
k\in\mathbb{N}
consider the event
\{Z_{k-1}\neq\tilde{Z}_{k-1}\}
, i.e. the event that the chains have not met before the
k^{\text{th}}
step. On this event we have that

e_{k}=\frac{B_{k}^{-1}\Pi_{k}\Delta z}{\left|B_{k}^{-1}\Pi_{k}\Delta z\right|},

where
\Pi_{k}\coloneqq A_{k}A_{k-1}\dots A_{1}
.

Proof.

We will prove this by induction. The statement for
k=0
follows directly from the fact that
E_{1}=B_{1}^{-1}A_{1}\Delta z
. So let
k\geq 1
and assume the statement is true for
k-1
. On the event
\{Z_{k}\neq\tilde{Z}_{k}\}
we have that
\tilde{\xi}_{k}=\xi_{k}-2\langle e_{k},\xi_{k}\rangle e_{k}
, so that

\displaystyle\Delta Z_{k}

\displaystyle=A_{k}\Delta Z_{k-1}-2\langle e_{k},\xi_{k}\rangle B_{k}e_{k}=B_{k}E_{k}-2\langle e_{k},\xi_{k}\rangle B_{k}e_{k}

\displaystyle={\left(|E_{k}|-2\langle e_{k},\xi_{k}\rangle\right)}B_{k}e_{k}.

This means that on
\{Z_{k}\neq\tilde{Z}_{k}\}
we have

(5.10)

E_{k+1}=B_{k+1}^{-1}A_{k+1}\Delta Z_{k}={\left(|E_{k}|-2\langle e_{k},\xi_{k}\rangle\right)}B_{k+1}^{-1}A_{k+1}B_{k}e_{k}.

On
\{Z_{k}\neq\tilde{Z}_{k}\}
we also have
|E_{k}|-2\langle e_{k},\xi_{k}\rangle>0
, as stated in (
5.9
). Combined with the induction hypothesis, which can be applied since
Z_{k}\neq\tilde{Z}_{k}
implies that
Z_{k-1}\neq\tilde{Z}_{k-1}
, we see that
E_{k+1}
is a positive multiple of
B_{k+1}^{-1}A_{k+1}\Pi_{k}\Delta z=B_{k+1}^{-1}\Pi_{k+1}\Delta z
on
\{Z_{k}\neq\tilde{Z}_{k}\}
. The statement now follows from the fact that
e_{k+1}=E_{k+1}/|E_{k+1}|
is normalized.
∎

We will also need the following standard integral; see
[
23
, Lemma 20]
for proof.

Lemma 5.5
.

For all
a>0
and
t\geq 0
we have

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\varphi(y)\left\{1-\left(1\wedge\frac{\varphi(t-y)}{\varphi(y)}\right)\right\}\left\{2\Phi{\left(\frac{|t-2y|}{2a}\right)}-1\right\}\,dy=2\Phi{\left(\frac{t}{2{\left(1+a^{2}\right)}^{1/2}}\right)}-1,

where
\varphi
denotes the density of the
1
-dimensional standard Gaussian
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
.

5.2.2.
Proof of Theorem
5.1

Fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
. We will show by backward induction that

(5.11)

\mathbb{P}(Z_{n}\neq\tilde{Z}_{n})=\mathbb{E}\left[2\Phi{\left(\frac{|E_{k}|}{2\Xi_{k,n}^{1/2}}\right)}-1\right],

for all
k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, where

\Xi_{k,n}=\sum_{j=k}^{n}\frac{|B_{k}^{-1}\Pi_{k}\Delta z|^{2}}{|B_{j}^{-1}\Pi_{j}\Delta z|^{2}}.

The proof is then completed by considering the case
k=1
and noting that
\Theta_{n}=\Xi_{1,n}/|E_{1}|^{2}
.

First, the base case
k=n
is covered by (
5.6
). So let
k\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
and suppose that (
5.11
) is true for
k+1
. Since
\Delta Z_{k}=0
implies that
E_{k+1}=0
, which in turn implies that
2\Phi(|E_{k+1}|/2)-1=0
, we can write

(5.12)

\mathbb{P}(Z_{n}\neq\tilde{Z}_{n})=\mathbb{E}\left[2\Phi{\left(\frac{|E_{k+1}|}{2\Xi_{k+1,n}^{1/2}}\right)}-1\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\{Z_{k}\neq\tilde{Z}_{k}\}}\left\{2\Phi{\left(\frac{|E_{k+1}|}{2\Xi_{{k+1},n}^{1/2}}\right)}-1\right\}\right].

Thanks to the expression for
E_{k+1}
on
\{Z_{k}\neq\tilde{Z}_{k}\}
given by (
5.10
) and the results of
Lemma

5.3
and
Lemma

5.4
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbbm{1}_{\{Z_{k}\neq\tilde{Z}_{k}\}}|E_{k+1}|

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\phantom{xx}\eqref{eq: E_k expression}\phantom{xx}}}{{=}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{Z_{k}\neq\tilde{Z}_{k}\}}\left||E_{k}|-2\langle e_{k},\xi_{k}\rangle\right|\left|B_{k+1}^{-1}A_{k+1}B_{k}e_{k}\right|

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{Lemma }\ref{lemma: rejection equivalences}}}{{=}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{Z_{k-1}\neq\tilde{Z}_{k-1}\}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{U_{k}>p_{k}(\xi_{k})\}}\left||E_{k}|-2\langle e_{k},\xi_{k}\rangle\right|\left|B_{k+1}^{-1}A_{k+1}B_{k}e_{k}\right|

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{Lemma }\ref{lemma: e_k expression}}}{{=}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{Z_{k-1}\neq\tilde{Z}_{k-1}\}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{U_{k}>p_{k}(\xi_{k})\}}\left||E_{k}|-2\langle e_{k},\xi_{k}\rangle\right|\frac{\left|B_{k+1}^{-1}\Pi_{k+1}\Delta z\right|}{\left|B_{k}^{-1}\Pi_{k}\Delta z\right|}.

The term inside of the expectation on the right hand side of (
5.12
) therefore has, conditioned on
\mathcal{F}_{k-1}
, the expectation

\mathbb{E}^{\mathcal{F}_{k-1}}\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\{Z_{k}\neq\tilde{Z}_{k}\}}\left\{2\Phi{\left(\frac{|E_{k+1}|}{2\Xi_{k+1,n}^{1/2}}\right)}-1\right\}\right]\\
=\mathbbm{1}_{\{Z_{k-1}\neq\tilde{Z}_{k-1}\}}\mathbb{E}^{\mathcal{F}_{k-1}}\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\{U_{k}>p_{k}(\xi_{k})\}}\left\{2\Phi{\left(\frac{\left||E_{k}|-2\langle e_{k},\xi_{k}\rangle\right|}{2\frac{\left|B_{k}^{-1}\Pi_{k}\Delta z\right|}{\left|B_{k+1}^{-1}\Pi_{k+1}\Delta z\right|}\Xi_{k+1,n}^{1/2}}\right)}-1\right\}\right].

The uniformly distributed random variable
U_{k}
is independent of
\mathcal{F}_{k-1}
and of
\xi_{k}
. Consequently, the expected value of
\mathbbm{1}_{\{U_{k}>p_{k}(\xi_{k})\}}
conditioned on both
\mathcal{F}_{k-1}
and
\xi_{k}
is equal to
1-(1\wedge p_{k}(\xi_{k}))=1-(1\wedge\exp{\left(-|E_{k}|^{2}/2+|E_{k}|\langle e_{k},\xi_{k}\rangle\right)})
and hence we have by the tower property that

\mathbb{E}^{\mathcal{F}_{k-1}}\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\{Z_{k}\neq\tilde{Z}_{k}\}}\left\{2\Phi{\left(\frac{|E_{k+1}|}{2\Xi_{k+1,n}^{1/2}}\right)}-1\right\}\right]\\
=\mathbbm{1}_{\{Z_{k-1}\neq\tilde{Z}_{k-1}\}}\mathbb{E}^{\mathcal{F}_{k-1}}\left[{\left(1-1\wedge\exp{\left(-\frac{|E_{k}|^{2}}{2}+|E_{k}|\langle e_{k},\xi_{k}\rangle\right)}\right)}\left\{2\Phi{\left(\frac{\left||E_{k}|-2\langle e_{k},\xi_{k}\rangle\right|}{2\frac{\left|B_{k}^{-1}\Pi_{k}\Delta z\right|}{\left|B_{k+1}^{-1}\Pi_{k+1}\Delta z\right|}\Xi_{k+1,n}^{1/2}}\right)}-1\right\}\right],

Furthermore, if
e_{k}\neq 0
, the random variable
\langle e_{k},\xi_{k}\rangle
is a projection of
\xi_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\id_{d})
onto the
1
-dimensional linear subspace spanned by
e_{k}
. This means it has the conditional distribution
\textup{Law}{\left(\langle e_{k},\xi_{k}\rangle|\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\right)}=\mathbbm{1}_{\{e_{k}\neq 0\}}\mathcal{N}(0,1)+\mathbbm{1}_{\{e_{k}=0\}}\delta_{0}
, where the event
\{e_{k}\neq 0\}
is equivalent to
\{Z_{k-1}\neq\tilde{Z}_{k-1}\}
by
Lemma

5.3
. We can therefore apply the integral of
Lemma

5.5
with
a={\left(\left|B_{k}^{-1}\Pi_{k}\Delta z\right|/\left|B_{k+1}^{-1}\Pi_{k+1}\Delta z\right|\right)}\Xi_{k+1,n}^{1/2}
and
t=|E_{k}|
to obtain

\mathbb{E}^{\mathcal{F}_{k-1}}\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\{Z_{k}\neq\tilde{Z}_{k}\}}\left\{2\Phi{\left(\frac{|E_{k+1}|}{2\Xi_{k+1,n}^{1/2}}\right)}-1\right\}\right]\\
=\mathbbm{1}_{\{Z_{k-1}\neq\tilde{Z}_{k-1}\}}\left\{2\Phi{\left(\frac{|E_{k}|}{2\Xi_{k,n}^{1/2}}\right)}-1\right\},

where we use that

1+\frac{\left|B_{k}^{-1}\Pi_{k}\Delta z\right|^{2}}{\left|B_{k+1}^{-1}\Pi_{k+1}\Delta z\right|^{2}}\Xi_{k+1,n}=\Xi_{k,n}

by the definition of
\Xi_{k,n}
. We thus obtain, from (
5.12
) and the calculations above, that

\mathbb{P}(Z_{n}\neq\tilde{Z}_{n})=\mathbb{E}{\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\{Z_{k-1}\neq\tilde{Z}_{k-1}\}}\left\{2\Phi{\left(\frac{|E_{k}|}{2\Xi_{k,n}^{1/2}}\right)}-1\right\}\right]}.

Since
Z_{k-1}=\tilde{Z}_{k-1}
implies that
2\Phi(|E_{k}|/(2\Xi_{k,n}^{1/2}))-1=0
, we can drop the indicator function and obtain

\mathbb{P}(Z_{n}\neq\tilde{Z}_{n})=\mathbb{E}{\left[2\Phi{\left(\frac{|E_{k}|}{2\Xi_{k,n}^{1/2}}\right)}-1\right]},

concluding the proof of
Theorem

5.1
.

5.3.
Examples

We will now use
Theorem

5.1
to assess the performance of the iterated one-shot coupling in several settings. First, we will give a sufficient condition for when the coupling is optimal in the sense that (
5.8
) is equal to
d_{\textup{TV}}(\textup{Law}(Z_{n}),\textup{Law}(\tilde{Z}_{n}))
and give an example where this condition is met. In addition, we will discuss the homogeneous setting with a contracting drift matrix and isotropic noise, where the iterated one-shot reproduces the TV distance up to a constant factor. Afterwards, we will turn to the exact discretization of the kinetic Langevin equation, which does not satisfy the aforementioned sufficient condition, and show that the coupling performance deteriorates when the step size
h
decreases to
0
, in line with the third term on the right-hand side of (
4.7
).

Example 5.6
.

Consider the homogeneous case of (
5.7
), where the Markov chains are given by
Z_{0}=z
,
\tilde{Z}_{0}=\tilde{z}
and

(5.13)

Z_{k+1}=AZ_{k}+B\xi_{k+1},\quad\tilde{Z}_{k+1}=A\tilde{Z}_{k}+B\tilde{\xi}_{k+1},

for some non-singular matrices
A,B\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
. Then the iterated one-shot coupling introduced in Section
5.1.2
applied to (
5.13
) is optimal, i.e.
\mathbb{P}(Z_{n}\neq\tilde{Z}_{n})=d_{\textup{TV}}(\textup{Law}(Z_{n}),\textup{Law}(\tilde{Z}_{n}))
if

ABB^{T}A^{T}=a^{2}BB^{T}

for some nonzero constant
a\in\mathbb{R}
. Indeed, in this case, the TV distance between
\textup{Law}(Z_{n})
and
\textup{Law}(\tilde{Z}_{n})
is given by

(5.14)

{d_{\textup{TV}}{\left(\textup{Law}(Z_{n}),\textup{Law}(\tilde{Z}_{n})\right)}}=2\Phi{\left(\frac{|B^{-1}\Delta z|}{2{\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}a^{-2k}\right)}^{1/2}}\right)}-1=\mathbb{P}(Z_{n}\neq\tilde{Z}_{n}),

see equations (
D.3
) and (
D.4
) in
Appendix

D
(the first identity is based on the explicit expression for the TV distance between Gaussians, see (
2.2
); the second identity is based on the explicit expression for the meeting probability provided by Theorem
5.1
).

Remark 5.7
.

The assumption that
ABB^{T}A^{T}=a^{2}BB^{T}
is equivalent to stating that
\frac{1}{a}B^{-1}AB
is orthogonal, and is for example satisfied in the case where
\frac{1}{a}A
is an orthogonal matrix that commutes with
BB^{T}
.

Remark 5.8
.

The result of
Example

5.6
can be generalized to the iterated one-shot coupling introduced in Section
5.1.2
applied to the inhomogeneous linear Markov chains (
5.7
) under the assumption that for each
k\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
there exists a nonzero constant
a_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

A_{k+1}B_{k}B_{k}^{T}A_{k+1}^{T}=a_{k}^{2}B_{k+1}B_{k+1}^{T},

which is equivalent to stating that
\frac{1}{a_{k}}B_{k+1}^{-1}A_{k+1}B_{k}
is orthogonal.

Example 5.9
.

Consider the case where the Markov chains are of the form (
5.7
) with
A_{k}=\bar{\omega}_{k}\id_{d}
and
B_{k}=\sigma_{k}\id_{d}
for nonzero constants
\bar{\omega}_{k},\sigma_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
. In this case, we find that
\Theta_{n}=|\Delta z|^{-2}\sum_{k=1}^{n}{\left(\sigma_{k}^{2}/\prod_{j=1}^{k}\bar{\omega}_{j}^{2}\right)}
, so that in light of
Remark

5.8
we have

(5.15)

{d_{\textup{TV}}{\left(\textup{Law}(Z_{n}),\textup{Law}(\tilde{Z}_{n})\right)}}=2\Phi{\left(\frac{|\Delta z|}{2\Xi_{n}^{1/2}}\right)}-1,

where
\Xi_{n}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}{\left(\sigma_{k}^{2}/\prod_{j=1}^{k}\bar{\omega}_{j}^{2}\right)}
. This shows that the upper bound on the TV distance between two functional autoregressive chains of the form
Z_{k+1}=h_{k+1}(Z_{k})+\sigma_{k+1}\xi_{k+1}
provided by the iterated one-shot coupling as stated in Theorem 19 of
[
23
]
is attained if all
h_{k}
are of the form
h_{k}(x)=\bar{\omega}_{k}x
.

One specific instance of the above example, where the upper bound on the TV distance derived in
[
23
]
is actually an equality, is given by the setting of independent OU-processes with a common mean reversion rate:

Example 5.10
.

Let
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
consist of
d
independent OU processes with common mean reversion rate
\gamma>0
, i.e. it is the solution to the
d
-dimensional stochastic differential equation

dX_{t}=-\gamma X_{t}\,dt+\sqrt{2\gamma}\,dW_{t},

where
(W_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a
d
-dimensional standard Brownian motion. The solution of this SDE for some initial value
X_{0}=x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is given by

(5.16)

X_{t}=e^{-\gamma t}x+\sqrt{2\gamma}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\gamma(t-s)}\,dW_{s}.

Consider for some fixed step size
h>0
the Markov chains
(X^{h}_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
,
(\tilde{X}^{h}_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
defined inductively by
X^{h}_{0}=x
,
\tilde{X}^{h}_{0}=\tilde{x}
and

X^{h}_{k+1}=e^{-\gamma h}X^{h}_{k}+(1-e^{-2\gamma h})^{1/2}\xi_{k+1},\quad\tilde{X}^{h}_{k+1}=e^{-\gamma h}\tilde{X}^{h}_{k}+(1-e^{-2\gamma h})^{1/2}\tilde{\xi}_{k+1},

where
(\xi_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}}
and
(\tilde{\xi}_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}}
are two sequences of i.i.d.
\mathcal{N}(0,\id_{d})
-distributed random variables. The distribution of each of these Markov chains is the same as that of the exact solution (
5.16
) (with initial value
x
and
\tilde{x}
respectively) at times
t_{k}=hk
. This setting is an instance of (
5.13
) with
A=e^{-\gamma h}\id_{d}
and
B=(1-e^{-2\gamma h})^{1/2}\id_{d}
. In particular, we have that
ABB^{T}A^{T}=a^{2}BB^{T}
with
a=e^{-\gamma h}
.
Example

5.6
thus guarantees that the iterated one-shot coupling is optimal, and if we apply it to
(X_{k}^{h},\tilde{X}^{h}_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
we have for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
that

{d_{\textup{TV}}{\left(\textup{Law}(X^{h}_{n}),\textup{Law}(\tilde{X}^{h}_{n})\right)}}=\mathbb{P}(X^{h}_{n}\neq\tilde{X}_{n}^{h})=2\Phi{\left(\frac{(1-e^{-2\gamma h})^{-1/2}|\Delta x|}{2{\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}e^{2\gamma hk}\right)}^{1/2}}\right)}-1

by (
5.14
). The geometric series in this expression can be written as

\sum_{k=1}^{n}e^{2\gamma hk}=\frac{e^{2\gamma h(n+1)}-e^{2\gamma h}}{e^{2\gamma h}-1}=\frac{e^{2\gamma hn}-1}{1-e^{-2\gamma h}}=\frac{e^{2\gamma T}-1}{1-e^{-2\gamma h}},

where
T=hn
. The TV distance is thus given by

{d_{\textup{TV}}{\left(\textup{Law}(X^{h}_{n}),\textup{Law}(\tilde{X}^{h}_{n})\right)}}=\mathbb{P}(X^{h}_{n}\neq\tilde{X}_{n}^{h})=2\Phi{\left(\frac{|\Delta x|}{2{\left(e^{2\gamma T}-1\right)}^{1/2}}\right)}-1.

As argued in
Example

5.9
, this expression equals the upper bound given in Theorem 19 of
[
23
]
.

The iterated one-shot coupling also performs well in more general homogeneous settings with a contracting drift matrix and isotropic noise, as the next example shows.

Example 5.11
.

Consider homogeneous linear Markov chains of the form (
5.13
) with covariance matrix
BB^{T}=\sigma^{2}\id_{d}
and a contracting drift matrix
A
satisfying
\|A\|\leq\alpha<1
. The covariance after
n
steps is
\Sigma_{n}=\sigma^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{n}A^{k}(A^{T})^{k}
. In particular,

\|\Sigma_{n}\|\leq\sigma^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\|A\|^{2k}\leq\sigma^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\alpha^{2k}=\sigma^{2}\frac{1-\alpha^{2n}}{\alpha^{-2}-1},

and since
\Sigma_{n}
is symmetric, this shows that

|\Sigma_{n}^{-1/2}A^{n}\Delta z|\geq\|\Sigma_{n}\|^{-1/2}|A^{n}\Delta z|\geq\frac{\sqrt{\alpha^{-2}-1}}{\sigma\sqrt{(1-\alpha^{2n})}}|A^{n}\Delta z|.

Let
(Z_{k},\tilde{Z}_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be given by the iterated one-shot coupling defined in Section
5.1.2
applied to the current setting. The factor
\Theta_{n}
can be lower bounded as

\Theta_{n}=\sigma^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{1}{|A^{k}\Delta z|^{2}}\geq\frac{\sigma^{2}}{|A^{n}\Delta z|^{2}},

so that

\frac{1}{\Theta_{n}^{1/2}}\leq\frac{|A^{n}\Delta z|}{\sigma}\leq\frac{\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2n}}}{\sqrt{\alpha^{-2}-1}}|\Sigma_{n}^{-1/2}A^{n}\Delta z|.

Hence,
Theorem

5.1
shows that

\mathbb{P}(Z_{n}\neq\tilde{Z}_{n})\leq 2\Phi{\left(\frac{\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2n}}|\Sigma_{n}^{-1/2}A^{n}\Delta z|}{2\sqrt{\alpha^{-2}-1}}\right)}-1.

If we compare this to the TV distance after
n
steps,

{d_{\textup{TV}}{\left(\textup{Law}(Z_{n}),\textup{Law}(\tilde{Z}_{n})\right)}}=2\Phi{\left(\frac{|\Sigma_{n}^{-1/2}A^{n}\Delta z|}{2}\right)}-1,

which follows from (
2.2
) (see also (
D.1
)), we see that for small values of
|\Sigma_{n}^{-1/2}A^{n}\Delta z|
, the probability of not meeting under the iterated one-shot coupling differs from the actual TV distance up to a factor of at most
1/\sqrt{\alpha^{-2}-1}
.

The next example shows that the iterated one-shot coupling applied to the exact discretization of the potential free kinetic Langevin equation gives a probability of not meeting that deteriorates as the terminal time
T\coloneqq hn
is fixed while decreasing the step size
h
. This is the same behavior as the third term in (
4.7
) of
Theorem

4.5
, showing that this part of the bound is sharp.

Example 5.12
.

Consider the exact discretization of the potential free kinetic Langevin equation for
h>0
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
given by
Z^{h}_{k}=Z_{hk}
and
\tilde{Z}^{h}_{k}=\tilde{Z}_{hk}
, where
(Z_{t})_{t\geq 0}
and
(\tilde{Z}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
denote the solutions to the linear system (
4.1
) with
\alpha=0
and with initial values
z
and
\tilde{z}
respectively. This means that we have two homogeneous linear chains satisfying the recursive relation

(5.17)

Z^{h}_{k+1}=A_{k+1}Z^{h}_{k}+B_{k+1}\xi_{k+1},\quad\tilde{Z}^{h}_{k+1}=A_{k+1}\tilde{Z}^{h}_{k}+B_{k+1}\tilde{\xi}_{k+1},

where
(\xi_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}}
and
(\tilde{\xi}_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}}
are two sequences of i.i.d.
\mathcal{N}(0,\id_{2d})
-distributed random variables and where
A_{k}=e^{hA}
and
B_{k}=B_{h}
for each
k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, with
B_{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d\times 2d}
being such that
B_{h}B_{h}^{T}=\Sigma_{h}
and
e^{hA}
and
\Sigma_{h}
given by (
4.20
).
Theorem

5.1
shows that if
(Z^{h}_{k},\tilde{Z}^{h}_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is given by the iterated one-shot coupling defined in Section
5.1.2
, then

(5.18)

\mathbb{P}(Z_{n}^{h}\neq\tilde{Z}_{n}^{h})=2\Phi{\left(\frac{1}{2\Theta_{n,h}^{1/2}}\right)}-1,

where

\Theta_{n,h}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\left|B_{h}^{-1}A_{h}^{k}\Delta z\right|^{2}}.

Suppose
\Delta z=(\Delta x,\Delta v)
is such that
\gamma\Delta x+\Delta v=0
, so that
\Delta z
is contained in the eigenspace of
A
corresponding to the eigenvalue
-\gamma
. Then
e^{hA}\Delta z=e^{-\gamma h}\Delta z
, so that

\Theta_{n,h}=\left|B_{h}^{-1}\Delta z\right|^{-2}\sum_{k=1}^{n}e^{2\gamma hk}=\left|B_{h}^{-1}\Delta z\right|^{-2}\frac{e^{2\gamma hn}-1}{1-e^{-2\gamma h}}.

In order evaluate
|B_{h}^{-1}\Delta z|^{2}=\Delta z^{T}\Sigma_{h}^{-1}\Delta z
, let us write

\Sigma_{h}=\begin{pmatrix}\sigma^{xx}_{h}\id_{d}&\sigma^{xv}_{h}\id_{d}\\
\sigma^{xv}_{h}\id_{d}&\sigma^{vv}_{h}\id_{d}\end{pmatrix}.

Recalling that
\Delta v=-\gamma\Delta x
and using that
\sigma^{xx}_{h},\sigma^{xv}_{h}>0
we find that

\left|B_{h}^{-1}\Delta z\right|^{2}=\frac{(\sigma^{vv}_{h}+2\gamma\sigma^{xv}_{h}+\gamma^{2}\sigma^{xx}_{h})}{\sigma^{xx}_{h}\sigma^{vv}_{h}-(\sigma^{xv}_{h})^{2}}|\Delta x|^{2}\geq\frac{\sigma^{vv}_{h}}{\sigma^{xx}_{h}\sigma^{vv}_{h}}|\Delta x|^{2}=\frac{1}{\sigma^{xx}_{h}}|\Delta x|^{2}.

We therefore have that

\mathbb{P}(Z^{h}_{n}\neq\tilde{Z}^{h}_{n})=2\Phi{\left(\frac{1}{2\Theta_{n,h}^{1/2}}\right)}-1\geq 2\Phi{\left(\frac{|\Delta x|}{2\sqrt{\sigma^{xx}_{h}}\sqrt{\frac{e^{2\gamma hn}-1}{1-e^{-2\gamma h}}}}\right)}-1.

The expression on the right hand side is the same as that obtained in the final stage of the proof of
Lemma

4.10
, where a lower bound is derived. With this lower bound we can conclude that there exists a constant
c>0
(depending on
\gamma
but not on
h
) such that

\mathbb{P}(Z^{h}_{n}\neq\tilde{Z}^{h}_{n})\geq c\min(1,h^{-1}e^{-\gamma hn}|\Delta z|).

This shows us that the probability of not meeting under the iterated one shot coupling behaves as the third term in (
4.7
). This result is no surprise, as
Lemma

5.4
shows that if
\Delta z
is in the eigenspace of
A
corresponding to the eigenvalue
-\gamma
, then so is
B_{h}e_{k+1}
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
. In particular,
\Delta Z^{h}_{k}
is contained in this eigenspace too for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
, and we thus have that
\Delta Q^{h}_{k}=0
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
, so that the iterated one-shot coupling satisfies the conditions of
Lemma

4.10
.

We also observe that for the iterated one-shot coupling, the probability
\mathbb{P}(Z^{h}_{n}\neq\tilde{Z}^{h}_{n})
deteriorates for small values of
h
regardless of the choice of initial values. Indeed, if we instead assume that
\Delta v=0
, so that
\Delta z
is contained in the eigenspace of
A
corresponding to the eigenvalue
0
, we find that

\Theta_{n,h}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left|B_{h}^{-1}e^{hkA}\Delta z\right|^{-2}=|B_{h}^{-1}\Delta z|^{-2}=n\sigma^{xx}_{h}|\Delta z|^{-2},

so that a similar argument as before shows that there exists a constant
c>0
such that

\mathbb{P}(Z^{h}_{n}\neq\tilde{Z}^{h}_{n})=2\Phi{\left(\frac{1}{2\Theta_{n,h}^{1/2}}\right)}-1\geq c\min(1,h^{-1}(hn)^{-1/2}|\Delta z|).

Appendix A
Auxiliary inequalities

In order to analyze the performance of the coalescence map based on the optimized trajectory as discussed in
Section

3.3
we will need a few auxiliary bounds on terms involving exponential functions.

Lemma A.1
.

For
x\geq 0
we have

(A.1)

\frac{x}{1+x}\leq 1-e^{-x}\leq\frac{2x}{2+x},

(A.2)

\frac{6-2x}{6+4x+x^{2}}\leq e^{-x}\leq\frac{6}{6+x^{3}},

(A.3)

0\leq\frac{1-e^{-2x}}{2x}-e^{-x}\leq\frac{2}{3}x^{2}.

In addition, for
a\geq b\geq 0
we have

(A.4)

2ax(1+e^{-x})-4b(1-e^{-x})\geq\frac{2ax^{3}}{6+4x+x^{2}},

(A.5)

2ax-4b(1-e^{-x})+b(1-e^{-2x})\leq 2(a-b)x+\frac{4}{3}bx^{3}.

(A.6)

2ax-2b(1-e^{-x})-b(1-e^{-x})^{2}\geq\frac{24ax^{3}+13ax^{4}+2ax^{5}}{(6+4x+x^{2})^{2}}.

Proof.

Rewriting
1+x\leq e^{x}
readily gives the first inequality of (
A.1
). The second inequality of (
A.1
) is equivalent to

f(x)\coloneqq{\left(1+\frac{x}{2}\right)}e^{-x}+\frac{x}{2}-1\geq 0.

Since
f(0)=0
and

f^{\prime}(x)=-\frac{1}{2}{\left(1+x\right)}e^{-x}+\frac{1}{2}\geq 0,

where the final inequality uses that
1+x\leq e^{x}
, we see that indeed
f(x)\geq 0
for all
x\geq 0
. The first inequality of (
A.2
) is equivalent to

\frac{(6+4x+x^{2})e^{-x}-6+2x}{6+4x+x^{2}}\geq 0,

which is true if the numerator is nonnegative. We thus let
g(x)=(6+4x+x^{2})e^{-x}-6+2x
and note that
g(0)=0
and

g^{\prime}(x)=-(2+2x+x^{2})e^{-x}+2=2{\left(1-{\left(1+x+\frac{1}{2}x^{2}\right)}e^{-x}\right)}\geq 0,

which is nonnegative due to the fact that
1+x+\frac{1}{2}x^{2}\leq e^{x}
. This shows that
g(x)\geq 0
for all
x\geq 0
, proving the first inequality of (
A.2
). The second inequality follows immediately from
e^{x}\geq 1+x+\frac{1}{2}x^{2}+\frac{1}{6}x^{3}\geq 1+\frac{1}{6}x^{3}
for
x\geq 0
. The first inequality of (
A.3
) follows from

\frac{1-e^{-2x}}{2x}-e^{-x}=e^{-x}{\left(\frac{e^{x}-e^{-x}}{2x}-1\right)}\geq 0

using that
e^{x}-e^{-x}\geq 2x
for
x\geq 0
. For the second inequality, we use the inequalities
e^{-x}\geq 1-x+\frac{1}{2}x^{2}-\frac{1}{6}x^{3}
and
e^{-x}\geq 1-x
, to obtain

\frac{1-e^{-2x}}{2x}-e^{-x}\leq\frac{2x-2x^{2}+\frac{4}{3}x^{3}}{2x}-1+x=\frac{2}{3}x^{2}.

We see that (
A.4
) is a consequence of the first inequality of (
A.2
), as

\displaystyle 2ax(1+e^{-x})-4b(1-e^{-x})

\displaystyle=2ax-4b+(2ax+4b)e^{-x}

\displaystyle\geq 2ax-4b+\frac{(2ax+4b)(6-2x)}{6+4x+x^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{24(a-b)x+4(a-b)x^{2}+2ax^{3}}{6+4x+x^{2}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{2ax^{3}}{6+4x+x^{2}}.

Moving on to (
A.5
), by the inequalities

1-x+\frac{1}{2}x^{2}-\frac{1}{6}x^{3}\leq e^{-x}\leq 1-x+\frac{1}{2}x^{2},

we have
1-e^{-x}\geq x-\frac{1}{2}x^{2}
and
1-e^{-2x}\leq 2x-2x^{2}+\frac{4}{3}x^{3}
. Applying these bounds and collecting terms of the same order gives

2ax-4b(1-e^{-x})+b(1-e^{-2x})\leq 2(a-b)x+\frac{4}{3}bx^{3},

showing (
A.5
). Finally, for (
A.6
) we have by the first inequality of (
A.2
) that

1-e^{-x}\leq 1-\frac{6-2x}{6+4x+x^{2}}\leq\frac{6x+x^{2}}{6+4x+x^{2}}.

Applying this twice we get

\displaystyle 2ax-2b(1-e^{-x})-b(1-e^{-x})^{2}

\displaystyle\geq 2ax-2b\frac{6x+x^{2}}{6+4x+x^{2}}-b\frac{(6x+x^{2})^{2}}{(6+4x+x^{2})^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{12(a-b)x+(8a-2b)x^{2}+2ax^{3}}{6+4x+x^{2}}-\frac{36bx^{2}+12bx^{3}+bx^{4}}{(6+4x+x^{2})^{2}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{6ax^{2}+2ax^{3}}{6+4x+x^{2}}-\frac{36bx^{2}+12bx^{3}+bx^{4}}{(6+4x+x^{2})^{2}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{36(a-b)x^{2}+(36a-12b)x^{3}+(14a-b)x^{4}+2ax^{5}}{(6+4x+x^{2})^{2}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{24ax^{3}+13ax^{4}+2ax^{5}}{(6+4x+x^{2})^{2}},

showing (
A.6
).
∎

Appendix B
The optimized trajectory of the OBABO scheme

In this section we derive an explicit expression of the position part of the optimized trajectory for the OBABO scheme as introduced in
Section

3.3
. This forms a crucial ingredient for the proof of
Lemma

3.16
.

Throughout this section we fix
h>0
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
. To improve readability, we will suppress the explicit notation of dependence on
h
unless needed. We will also use the notation
\eta=e^{-\gamma h}
.

B.1.
The OBABO mean and the covariance matrices

Recall that the matrices
A=A_{h}
,
L=L_{h}
that govern the potential free part of the OBABO scheme are given by (
3.3
). Denoting
\eta=e^{-\gamma h}
, these matrices are

A=\begin{pmatrix}\id_{d}&h\eta^{1/2}\id_{d}\\
0&\eta\id_{d}\end{pmatrix},\qquad L=(1-\eta)^{1/2}\begin{pmatrix}h\id_{d}&0\\
\eta^{1/2}\id_{d}&\id_{d}\end{pmatrix}.

We will now calculate the matrices
A^{n}
and
\Sigma_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}A^{k}LL^{T}(A^{T})^{k}
that govern respectively the mean and the covariance of the OBABO chain after
n
steps if
\nabla U\equiv 0
. First, using the upper triangular structure of
A
, we have

(B.1)

\ A^{n}=\begin{pmatrix}\id_{d}&b_{n}\id_{d}\\
0&c_{n}\id_{d}\end{pmatrix},

where

b_{n}=h\eta^{1/2}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\eta^{k}=\frac{h\eta^{1/2}}{1-\eta}(1-\eta^{n}),\qquad c_{n}=\eta^{n}.

Consequently,

A^{k}LL^{T}(A^{T})^{k}=(1-\eta)\begin{pmatrix}(h^{2}+2h\eta^{1/2}b_{k}+(1+\eta)b_{k}^{2})\id_{d}&(h\eta^{1/2}c_{k}+(1+\eta)b_{k}c_{k})\id_{d}\\
(h\eta^{1/2}c_{k}+(1+\eta)b_{k}c_{k})\id_{d}&(1+\eta)c_{k}^{2}\id_{d}\end{pmatrix},

so that the
n
step covariance matrix is

(B.2)

\Sigma_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}A^{k}LL^{T}(A^{T})^{k}=\begin{pmatrix}\sigma^{xx}_{n}\id_{d}&\sigma^{xv}_{n}\id_{d}\\
\sigma^{xv}_{n}\id_{d}&\sigma^{vv}_{n}\id_{d}\end{pmatrix},

with

\displaystyle\sigma^{xx}_{n}

\displaystyle=(1-\eta)\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}(h^{2}+2h\eta^{1/2}b_{k}+(1+\eta)b_{k}^{2}),

\displaystyle\sigma^{xv}_{n}

\displaystyle=(1-\eta)\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}(h\eta^{1/2}c_{k}+(1+\eta)b_{k}c_{k}),

\displaystyle\sigma^{vv}_{n}

\displaystyle=(1-\eta)(1+\eta)\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}c_{k}^{2}.

The various summations in these expressions are given by

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}b_{k}=\frac{h\eta^{1/2}}{1-\eta}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}(1-\eta^{k})=\frac{h\eta^{1/2}}{1-\eta}{\left(n-\frac{1-\eta^{n}}{1-\eta}\right)},

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}b_{k}^{2}=\frac{h^{2}\eta}{(1-\eta)^{2}}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}(1-2\eta^{k}+\eta^{2k})=\frac{h^{2}\eta}{(1-\eta)^{2}}{\left(n-2\frac{1-\eta^{n}}{1-\eta}+\frac{1-\eta^{2n}}{1-\eta^{2}}\right)},

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}c_{k}=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\eta^{k}=\frac{1-\eta^{n}}{1-\eta}

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}b_{k}c_{k}=\frac{h\eta^{1/2}}{1-\eta}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}(\eta^{k}-\eta^{2k})=\frac{h\eta^{1/2}}{1-\eta}{\left(\frac{1-\eta^{n}}{1-\eta}-\frac{1-\eta^{2n}}{1-\eta^{2}}\right)},

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}c_{k}^{2}=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\eta^{2k}=\frac{1-\eta^{2n}}{1-\eta^{2}}.

In particular, using the fact that
(1+\eta)(1-\eta)=(1-\eta^{2})
throughout, we have

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \sigma^{xx}_{n}=&\frac{h^{2}}{1-\eta}{\left[{\left((1-\eta)^{2}+2(1-\eta)\eta+(1+\eta)\eta\right)}n-2\eta{\left(1+\frac{1+\eta}{1-\eta}\right)}(1-\eta^{n})+\frac{\eta(1+\eta)}{1-\eta^{2}}(1-\eta^{2n})\right]}\\
=&\frac{h^{2}}{1-\eta}{\left[{\left(1+\eta\right)}n-\frac{4\eta}{1-\eta}(1-\eta^{n})+\frac{\eta}{1-\eta}(1-\eta^{2n})\right]}\\
=&\frac{h^{2}}{(1-\eta)^{2}}{\left[(1-\eta^{2})n-4\eta(1-\eta^{n})+\eta(1-\eta^{2n})\right]},\end{aligned}

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \sigma^{xv}_{n}&=h\eta^{1/2}{\left(1+\frac{1+\eta}{1-\eta}\right)}(1-\eta^{n})-\frac{h\eta^{1/2}(1+\eta)}{1-\eta^{2}}(1-\eta^{2n})=\frac{h\eta^{1/2}}{1-\eta}{\left(2(1-\eta^{n})-(1-\eta^{2n})\right)}\\
&=\frac{h\eta^{1/2}}{1-\eta}(1-\eta^{n})^{2},\end{aligned}

\displaystyle\sigma^{vv}_{n}=\frac{(1-\eta)(1+\eta)}{1-\eta^{2}}(1-\eta^{2n})=1-\eta^{2n}.

B.2.
The explicit optimized trajectory

The trajectory
(y_{0},\dots,y_{n})
defining the coalescence map
\Psi^{n}_{z,\tilde{z}}
via (
3.12
) as proposed in (
3.19
) is given inductively by
y_{0}=\Delta z
and

y_{k+1}=Ay_{k}-LE_{k+1},

where
E_{k}=L^{T}(A^{T})^{n-k}\Sigma_{n}^{-1}A^{n}\Delta z
. By induction, it follows that

y_{k}=A^{k}\Delta z-\sum_{j=1}^{k}A^{k-j}LE_{j}=A^{k}\Delta z-\sum_{j=1}^{k}A^{k-j}LL^{T}(A^{T})^{n-j}\Sigma_{n}^{-1}A^{n}\Delta z.

This can be rewritten into

\displaystyle y_{k}

\displaystyle=A^{k}\Delta z-\underbrace{{\left({\sum_{j=1}^{k}A^{k-j}LL^{T}(A^{T})^{k-j}}\right)}}_{=\Sigma_{k}}(A^{T})^{n-k}\Sigma_{n}^{-1}A^{n}\Delta z

\displaystyle=A^{k-n}{\left(\Sigma_{n}-A^{n-k}\Sigma_{k}(A^{T})^{n-k}\right)}\Sigma_{n}^{-1}A^{n}\Delta z.

The matrix inside the brackets reduces to

\displaystyle\Sigma_{n}-A^{n-k}\Sigma_{k}(A^{T})^{n-k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}A^{n-j}LL^{T}(A^{T})^{n-j}-\sum_{j=1}^{k}A^{n-j}LL^{T}(A^{T})^{n-j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=k+1}^{n}A^{n-j}LL^{T}(A^{T})^{n-j}=\sum_{j=1}^{n-k}A^{n-k-j}LL^{T}(A^{T})^{n-k-j}=\Sigma_{n-k}.

In conclusion, the trajectory at step
n-k
is

y_{n-k}=A^{-k}\Sigma_{k}\Sigma_{n}^{-1}A^{n}\Delta z.

We can evaluate this using the expressions (
B.1
) and (
B.2
). First, the inverses appearing in this expression are given by

A^{-k}=\begin{pmatrix}\id_{d}&-\frac{b_{k}}{c_{k}}\id_{d}\\
0&\frac{1}{c_{k}}\id_{d}\end{pmatrix},

and

\Sigma_{n}^{-1}=\frac{1}{\sigma^{xx}_{n}\sigma^{vv}_{n}-(\sigma^{xv}_{n})^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}\sigma^{vv}_{n}\id_{d}&-\sigma^{xv}_{n}\id_{d}\\
-\sigma^{xv}_{n}\id_{d}&\sigma^{xx}_{n}\id_{d}\end{pmatrix}.

The part of
y_{n-k}
that depends on
k
is

A^{-k}\Sigma_{k}=\begin{pmatrix}{\left(\sigma^{xx}_{k}-\frac{b_{k}}{c_{k}}\sigma^{xv}_{k}\right)}\id_{d}&{\left(\sigma^{xv}_{k}-\frac{b_{k}}{c_{k}}\sigma^{vv}_{k}\right)}\id_{d}\\
\frac{1}{c_{k}}\sigma^{xv}_{k}\id_{d}&\frac{1}{c_{k}}\sigma^{vv}_{k}\id_{d}\end{pmatrix},

where

\displaystyle\sigma^{xx}_{k}-\frac{b_{k}}{c_{k}}\sigma^{xv}_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{h^{2}}{(1-\eta)^{2}}{\left[(1-\eta^{2})k-4\eta(1-\eta^{k})+\eta(1-\eta^{2k})-\eta\frac{(1-\eta^{k})^{3}}{\eta^{k}}\right]}

\displaystyle=\frac{h^{2}}{(1-\eta)^{2}}{\left[(1-\eta^{2})k-\eta{\left(3-4\eta^{k}+\eta^{2k}+\frac{1}{\eta^{k}}-3+3\eta^{k}-\eta^{2k}\right)}\right]}

\displaystyle=\frac{h^{2}}{(1-\eta)^{2}}{\left[(1-\eta^{2})k-\frac{\eta}{\eta^{k}}{\left(1-\eta^{2k}\right)}\right]},

and

\displaystyle\sigma^{xv}_{k}-\frac{b_{k}}{c_{k}}\sigma^{vv}_{k}=\frac{h\eta^{1/2}}{1-\eta}{\left[(1-\eta^{k})^{2}-\frac{1}{\eta^{k}}(1-\eta^{k})(1-\eta^{2k})\right]}=-\frac{h\eta^{1/2}}{1-\eta}\frac{1}{\eta^{k}}(1-\eta^{k})^{2}.

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\Sigma_{n}^{-1}A_{n}\Delta z

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sigma^{xx}_{n}\sigma^{vv}_{n}-(\sigma^{xv}_{n})^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}\sigma^{vv}_{n}\id_{d}&-\sigma^{xv}_{n}\id_{d}\\
-\sigma^{xv}_{n}\id_{d}&\sigma^{xx}_{n}\id_{d}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\Delta x+b_{n}\Delta v\\
c_{n}\Delta v\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sigma^{xx}_{n}\sigma^{vv}_{n}-(\sigma^{xv}_{n})^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}\sigma^{vv}_{n}{\left(\Delta x+b_{n}\Delta v\right)}-\sigma^{xv}_{n}c_{n}\Delta v\\
-\sigma^{xv}_{n}{\left(\Delta x+b_{n}\Delta v\right)}+\sigma^{xx}_{n}c_{n}\Delta v\end{pmatrix},

The position part of the trajectory at the
(n-k)th
step is therefore given by

\displaystyle u_{n-k}=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\id_{d}&0\end{pmatrix}y_{n-k}=\begin{pmatrix}\id_{d}&0\end{pmatrix}A^{-k}\Sigma_{h,k}\Sigma_{h,n}^{-1}A^{n}\Delta z

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sigma^{xx}_{n}\sigma^{vv}_{n}-(\sigma^{xv}_{n})^{2}}{\left[{\left(\sigma^{xx}_{k}-\frac{b_{k}}{c_{k}}\sigma^{xv}_{k}\right)}\sigma^{vv}_{n}-{\left(\sigma^{xv}_{k}-\frac{b_{k}}{c_{k}}\sigma^{vv}_{k}\right)}\sigma^{xv}_{n}\right]}(\Delta x+b_{n}\Delta v)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\sigma^{xx}_{n}\sigma^{vv}_{n}-(\sigma^{xv}_{n})^{2}}{\left[{\left(\sigma^{xv}_{k}-\frac{b_{k}}{c_{k}}\sigma^{vv}_{k}\right)}\sigma^{xx}_{n}-{\left(\sigma^{xx}_{k}-\frac{b_{k}}{c_{k}}\sigma^{xv}_{k}\right)}\sigma^{xv}_{n}\right]}c_{n}\Delta v.

The terms in the squared brackets are

(B.3)

\displaystyle\bigg(\sigma^{xx}_{k}

\displaystyle-\frac{b_{k}}{c_{k}}\sigma^{xv}_{k}\bigg)\sigma^{vv}_{n}-{\left(\sigma^{xv}_{k}-\frac{b_{k}}{c_{k}}\sigma^{vv}_{k}\right)}\sigma^{xv}_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{h^{2}}{(1-\eta)^{2}}{\left[{\left((1-\eta^{2})k-\frac{\eta}{\eta^{k}}{\left(1-\eta^{2k}\right)}\right)}(1-\eta^{2n})+\frac{\eta}{\eta^{k}}(1-\eta^{k})^{2}(1-\eta^{n})^{2}\right]}

\displaystyle=\frac{h^{2}}{(1-\eta)^{2}}{\left[(1-\eta^{2})(1-\eta^{2n})k-\frac{\eta}{\eta^{k}}{\left((1-\eta^{2k})(1-\eta^{2n})-(1-\eta^{k})^{2}(1-\eta^{n})^{2}\right)}\right]}

\displaystyle=\frac{h^{2}}{(1-\eta)^{2}}{\left[(1-\eta^{2})(1-\eta^{2n})k-2\eta(1+\eta^{n-k})(1-\eta^{k})(1-\eta^{n})\right]},

and

\displaystyle\bigg(\sigma^{xv}_{k}

\displaystyle-\frac{b_{k}}{c_{k}}\sigma^{vv}_{k}\bigg)\sigma^{xx}_{n}-{\left(\sigma^{xx}_{k}-\frac{b_{k}}{c_{k}}\sigma^{xv}_{k}\right)}\sigma^{xv}_{n}

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} =-\frac{h^{3}\eta^{3/2}}{(1-\eta)^{3}}\bigg[&\frac{1}{\eta^{k}}(1-\eta^{k})^{2}{\left((1-\eta^{2})n-4\eta(1-\eta^{n})+\eta(1-\eta^{2n})\right)}\\
&+{\left((1-\eta^{2})k-\frac{\eta}{\eta^{k}}{\left(1-\eta^{2k}\right)}\right)}(1-\eta^{n})^{2}\bigg]\\
=-\frac{h^{3}\eta^{3/2}}{(1-\eta)^{3}}\bigg[&(1-\eta^{2}){\left(\frac{1}{\eta^{k}}(1-\eta^{k})^{2}n+(1-\eta^{n})^{2}k\right)}\\
&-\frac{\eta}{\eta^{k}}(1-\eta^{k})(1-\eta^{n}){\left(4(1-\eta^{k})-(1-\eta^{k})(1+\eta^{n})+(1+\eta^{k})(1-\eta^{n})\right)}\bigg]\\
=-\frac{h^{3}\eta^{3/2}}{(1-\eta)^{3}}\bigg[&(1-\eta^{2}){\left(\frac{1}{\eta^{k}}(1-\eta^{k})^{2}n+(1-\eta^{n})^{2}k\right)}-2\frac{\eta}{\eta^{k}}(1-\eta^{k})(1-\eta^{n})(2-\eta^{k}-\eta^{n})\bigg].\\
\end{aligned}

As a shorthand, we introduce

(B.4)

\alpha_{k}\coloneqq(1-\eta^{2})(1-\eta^{2n})k-2\eta(1+\eta^{n-k})(1-\eta^{k})(1-\eta^{n}),

(B.5)

\beta_{k}\coloneqq(1-\eta^{2}){\left(\frac{1}{\eta^{k}}(1-\eta^{k})^{2}n+(1-\eta^{n})^{2}k\right)}-2\frac{\eta}{\eta^{k}}(1-\eta^{k})(1-\eta^{n})(2-\eta^{k}-\eta^{n}).

The relation (
B.3
) reduces for
k=n
to

\sigma^{xx}_{n}\sigma^{vv}_{n}-(\sigma^{xv}_{n})^{2}=\frac{h^{2}}{(1-\eta)^{2}}\alpha_{n}.

We therefore find the final expression

(B.6)

\displaystyle u_{n-k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha_{n}}{\left(\alpha_{k}{\left[\Delta x+\frac{h\eta^{1/2}}{1-\eta}(1-\eta^{n})\Delta v\right]}-\beta_{k}\frac{h\eta^{1/2}}{1-\eta}\eta^{n}\Delta v\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha_{k}}{\alpha_{n}}\Delta x-\frac{h\eta^{1/2}}{1-\eta}\frac{\eta^{n}\beta_{k}-(1-\eta^{n})\alpha_{k}}{\alpha_{n}}\Delta v.

With this explicit expression, we can derive an uniform bound on
|u_{n-k}|
as in
Lemma

3.16
by using the following result.

Lemma B.1
.

Both
(\alpha_{k})_{0\leq k\leq n}
and
(\beta_{k})_{0\leq k\leq n}
defined by (
B.4
) and (
B.5
) are increasing.

Proof.

We first show that
(\alpha_{k})_{0\leq k\leq n}
is increasing. For
k\in\{0,\dots n-1\}
we have

(B.7)

\alpha_{k+1}-\alpha_{k}=(1-\eta)(1-\eta^{n}){\left[(1+\eta)(1+\eta^{n})-2\eta(\eta^{k}+\eta^{n-k-1})\right]},

where we use that

(1+\eta^{n-k-1})(1-\eta^{k+1})-(1+\eta^{n-k})(1-\eta^{k})=(1-\eta)(\eta^{k}+\eta^{n-k-1}).

The term in the square brackets in (
B.7
) can be expanded in rearranged as

\displaystyle(1+\eta)(1+\eta^{n})

\displaystyle-2\eta(\eta^{k}+\eta^{n-k-1})=1+\eta+\eta^{n}+\eta^{n+1}-2\eta^{k+1}-2\eta^{n-k}

\displaystyle=(1-\eta^{k+1})+\eta(1-\eta^{k})-\eta^{n-k}(1-\eta^{k})-\eta^{n-k}(1-\eta^{k+1})

\displaystyle=(1-\eta^{k+1})(1-\eta^{n-k})+\eta(1-\eta^{k})(1-\eta^{n-k-1})\geq 0,

since
\eta\in(0,1)
, showing that
(\alpha_{k})_{0\leq k\leq n}
is indeed increasing. For
(\beta_{k})_{0\leq k\leq n}
, we again fix some
k\in\{0,\dots n-1\}
and rewrite

\displaystyle\beta_{k}

\displaystyle=(1-\eta^{2}){\left(\frac{1}{\eta^{k}}(1-\eta^{k})^{2}n+(1-\eta^{n})^{2}k\right)}-2\frac{\eta}{\eta^{k}}(1-\eta^{k})^{2}(1-\eta^{n})-2\frac{\eta}{\eta^{k}}(1-\eta^{k})(1-\eta^{n})^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\eta^{k}}(1-\eta^{k})^{2}{\left((1-\eta^{2})n-2\eta(1-\eta^{n})\right)}+{\left((1-\eta^{2})k-2\frac{\eta}{\eta^{k}}(1-\eta^{k})\right)}(1-\eta^{n})^{2},

and note that

\frac{1}{\eta^{k+1}}(1-\eta^{k+1})^{2}-\frac{1}{\eta^{k}}(1-\eta^{k})^{2}=\frac{1}{\eta^{k+1}}(1-\eta)(1-\eta^{2k+1}),

\frac{1}{\eta^{k+1}}(1-\eta^{k+1})-\frac{1}{\eta^{k}}(1-\eta^{k})=\frac{1}{\eta^{k+1}}(1-\eta).

We therefore have that

\beta_{k+1}-\beta_{k}=\frac{1}{\eta^{k+1}}(1-\eta){\left[(1-\eta^{2k+1}){\left((1-\eta^{2})n-2\eta(1-\eta^{n})\right)}+{\left((1+\eta)\eta^{k+1}-2\eta\right)}(1-\eta^{n})\right]}.

This expression can be rearranged using the fact that

(1+\eta)\eta^{k+1}-2\eta=-\eta(2-\eta^{k}-\eta^{k+1})=-\eta{\left((1-\eta^{k})(1-\eta^{k+1})+(1-\eta^{2k+1})\right)},

to obtain

(B.8)

\displaystyle\beta_{k+1}-\beta_{k}=\frac{1}{\eta^{k+1}}(1-\eta)\bigg[

\displaystyle(1-\eta^{2k+1}){\left((1-\eta^{2})n-2\eta(1-\eta^{n})-\eta(1-\eta^{n})^{2}\right)}

\displaystyle-\eta(1-\eta^{k})(1-\eta^{k+1})(1-\eta^{n})^{2}\bigg].

In order to bound this result from below, we use several of the inequalities of
Lemma

A.1
. First, recall that
\eta=e^{-\gamma h}
, so that
\frac{1-\eta^{2}}{2\gamma h}\geq\eta
by (
A.3
), and so we can use (
A.6
) with
a=\frac{1-\eta^{2}}{2\gamma h}
,
b=\eta
and
x=\gamma hn
to find

(B.9)

(1-\eta^{2})n-2\eta(1-\eta^{n})-\eta(1-\eta^{n})^{2}\geq{\left(\frac{1-\eta^{2}}{2\gamma h}\right)}\frac{24(\gamma hn)^{3}+13(\gamma hn)^{4}+2(\gamma hn)^{5}}{{\left(6+4\gamma hn+(\gamma hn)^{2}\right)}^{2}}\geq 0.

In addition,

1-\eta^{2k+1}\geq 1-\eta^{2k+1}=(1-\eta^{k})(1+\eta^{k}),

and, by rewriting the first inequality of (
A.2
),

(1-\eta^{n})^{2}\leq\frac{(6\gamma hn+(\gamma hn)^{2})^{2}}{{\left(6+4\gamma hn+(\gamma hn)^{2}\right)}^{2}}=\frac{36(\gamma hn)^{2}+12(\gamma hn)^{3}+(\gamma hn)^{4}}{{\left(6+4\gamma hn+(\gamma hn)^{2}\right)}^{2}}.

Combining these observations, we see from (
B.8
) that
\beta_{k+1}-\beta_{k}\geq 0
if

{\left(\frac{1-\eta^{2}}{2\gamma h}\right)}(1+\eta^{k})\frac{24(\gamma hn)^{3}+13(\gamma hn)^{4}+2(\gamma hn)^{5}}{{\left(6+4\gamma hn+(\gamma hn)^{2}\right)}^{2}}-\eta(1-\eta^{k+1})\frac{36(\gamma hn)^{2}+12(\gamma hn)^{3}+(\gamma hn)^{4}}{{\left(6+4\gamma hn+(\gamma hn)^{2}\right)}^{2}}\geq 0.

In particular, since
\frac{1-\eta^{2}}{2\gamma h}\geq\eta
, and

24(\gamma hn)^{3}+13(\gamma hn)^{4}+2(\gamma hn)^{5}\geq\frac{2}{3}\gamma hn{\left(36(\gamma hn)^{2}+12(\gamma hn)^{3}+(\gamma hn)^{4}\right)},

it suffices to show that

\frac{2}{3}(1+\eta^{k})\gamma hn\geq 1-\eta^{k+1}.

In fact, we will show the stronger inequality

\frac{1}{2}(1+\eta^{k})\geq\frac{1-\eta^{k+1}}{\gamma h(k+1)},

which suffices since
n\geq k+1
. Using the first inequality of (
A.2
) twice get both

\frac{1}{2}(1+\eta^{k})\geq\frac{6+\gamma hk}{6+4\gamma hk+(\gamma hk)^{2}},

and

\frac{1-\eta^{k+1}}{\gamma h(k+1)}\leq\frac{6+\gamma h(k+1)}{6+4\gamma h(k+1)+(\gamma h(k+1))^{2}}.

By combining these two fractions we get

\frac{1}{2}(1+\eta^{k})-\frac{1-\eta^{k+1}}{\gamma h(k+1)}\geq\frac{18\gamma h+6(\gamma h)^{2}(2k+1)+(\gamma h)^{3}k(k+1)}{(6+4\gamma hk+(\gamma hk)^{2})(6+4\gamma h(k+1)+(\gamma h(k+1))^{2})}\geq 0,

showing the claimed result. We conclude that

is indeed increasing.
∎

\displaystyle\lambda_{\textup{max}}{\left((A^{n})^{T}\Sigma_{n}^{-1}A^{n}\right)}

using that the trace of a matrix gives an upper bound on its eigenvalues and the fact that

\displaystyle=\frac{h^{2}}{(1-\eta)^{2}}(1-\eta^{n}){\left[(1-\eta^{2})(1+\eta^{n})n-4\eta(1-\eta^{n})\right]}

Appendix D
Sufficient condition for optimality of the iterated one-shot coupling

This section contains proofs for the identities in equation (
5.14
) of
Example

5.6
. Indeed, let
A,B\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
be non-singular matrices and consider the homogeneous linear Markov chains given by
Z_{0}=z
,
\tilde{Z}_{0}=\tilde{z}
and

Z_{k+1}=AZ_{k}+B\xi_{k+1},\quad\tilde{Z}_{k+1}=A\tilde{Z}_{k}+B\tilde{\xi}_{k+1},

where
(\xi_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}}
and
(\tilde{\xi}_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}}
are two sequences of i.i.d.
\mathcal{N}(0,\id_{d})
-distributed random variables. By an induction argument it follows that

Z_{n}=A^{n}z+\sum_{k=1}^{n}A^{n-k}B\xi_{k}.

Consequently,
Z_{n}\sim\mathcal{N}(A^{n}z,\Sigma_{n})
, where

\Sigma_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}A^{k}BB^{T}(A^{T})^{k}.

The TV distance between the two chains after
n
steps thus follows from (
2.2
) and is given by

(D.1)

{d_{\textup{TV}}{\left(\textup{Law}(Z_{n}),\textup{Law}(\tilde{Z}_{n})\right)}}=2\Phi{\left(\frac{|\Sigma_{n}^{-1/2}A^{n}\Delta z|}{2}\right)}-1.

Let us now assume that
ABB^{T}A^{T}=a^{2}BB^{T}
for some nonzero
a\in\mathbb{R}
. Under this assumption, the covariance of the chain at each step reduces to a positive multiple of
BB^{T}
:

\Sigma_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}A^{k}BB^{T}(A^{T})^{k}={\left(\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}a^{2k}\right)}BB^{T}.

Moreover, by the non-singularity of
A
, the assumption that
ABB^{T}A^{T}=a^{2}BB^{T}
is equivalent to

(D.2)

A^{T}(BB^{T})^{-1}A=a^{2}(BB^{T})^{-1}.

Applying both these observations, we deduce that

\displaystyle(A^{T})^{n}\Sigma_{n}^{-1}A^{n}

\displaystyle={\left(\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}a^{2k}\right)}^{-1}(A^{T})^{n}(BB^{T})^{-1}A^{n}={\left(\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}a^{2k}\right)}^{-1}a^{2n}(BB^{T})^{-1}

\displaystyle={\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}a^{-2k}\right)}^{-1}(BB^{T})^{-1}.

The term
|\Sigma_{n}^{-1/2}A^{n}\Delta z|
, as it appears in (
D.1
), can therefore be simplified to

|\Sigma_{n}^{-1/2}A^{n}\Delta z|^{2}={\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}a^{-2k}\right)}^{-1}\Delta z^{T}(BB^{T})^{-1}\Delta z={\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}a^{-2k}\right)}^{-1}|B^{-1}\Delta z|^{2},

so that the TV distance is given by

(D.3)

{d_{\textup{TV}}{\left(\textup{Law}(Z_{n}),\textup{Law}(\tilde{Z}_{n})\right)}}=2\Phi{\left(\frac{|B^{-1}\Delta z|}{2{\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}a^{-2k}\right)}^{1/2}}\right)}-1.

On the other hand, the probability that the two chains have met under the iterated one-shot coupling is, due to
Theorem

5.1
, given by

\mathbb{P}(Z_{n}\neq\tilde{Z}_{n})=2\Phi{\left(\frac{1}{2\Theta_{n}^{1/2}}\right)}-1,

where

\Theta_{n}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\left|B^{-1}A^{k}\Delta z\right|^{2}}.

Under (
D.2
) this value can be simplified to

\Theta_{n}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\left|B^{-1}A^{k}\Delta z\right|^{2}}=\frac{1}{\Delta z^{T}(BB^{T})^{-1}\Delta z}\sum_{k=1}^{n}a^{-2k}=\frac{1}{|B^{-1}\Delta z|^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}a^{-2k}.

This shows that

(D.4)

\mathbb{P}(Z_{n}\neq\tilde{Z}_{n})=2\Phi{\left(\frac{|B^{-1}\Delta z|}{2{\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}a^{-2k}\right)}^{1/2}}\right)}-1,

which is equal to
{d_{\textup{TV}}{\left(\textup{Law}(Z_{n}),\textup{Law}(\tilde{Z}_{n})\right)}}
given in (
D.3
).
