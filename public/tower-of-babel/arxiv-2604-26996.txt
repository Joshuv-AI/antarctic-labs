LUCid: Redefining Relevance For Lifelong Personalization
Chimaobi Okite, Anika Misra, Joyce Chai Rada Mihalcea

Abstract
Current approaches to lifelong personalization operationalize relevance through semantic proximity, causing them to miss essential user information from topically unrelated interactions. To address this gap, we introduce LUCid, a benchmark designed to measure situational user-centric relevance in personalization. The benchmark consists of 1,936 realistic queries paired with interaction histories from up to 500 sessions. Across multiple architectures, our experiments show significant performance collapse when relevant context must be surfaced from semantically distant history: retrieval recall drops to near zero on the hardest instances, and response alignment remains near 50% even for state-of-the-art models such as Gemini-3-Flash, GPT-5.4, and Claude Haiku. These results expose a fundamental mismatch between the notion of relevance encoded by current systems and the situational relevance required for personalization, with direct implications for robustness and safety when critical user attributes remain undetected. LUCid enables the systematic evaluation of whether current models can surface situationally-relevant user information from previous interactions, and serves as a step toward realigning personalization with user-centered relevance.

Introduction

People vary widely in their goals, preferences, and contexts, even within the same task or prompt
(
33
)
. As they interact with LLM-powered conversational systems, they generate rich, evolving interaction histories that reflect these individual differences
(
46
;
38
)
. Effective lifelong personalization therefore requires models to reason over this history; without it, conversational agents default to generic knowledge, producing responses that are not only unhelpful
(
14
)
but potentially unsafe
(
39
;
11
)
, undermining user trust over time.

Given the cost and context-window limitations of full-history conditioning
(
38
;
46
)
, RAG and its advanced variants, including GraphRAG
(
28
)
and memory-augmented approaches
(
36
;
37
)
, have emerged as the dominant paradigm for scalable personalization. Alongside these methods, several benchmarks have been developed to evaluate whether systems can surface and utilize relevant user history
(
46
;
38
;
15
;
16
)
. However, both methods and benchmarks share a critical underlying assumption that relevance is synonymous with semantic proximity. Retrieval methods encode this assumption via semantic similarity scoring functions, while benchmarks reinforce it by constructing evaluation instances where relevant context and the current query share sufficient semantic overlap–—a bias we quantify through a new metric, Proximity Advantage (PA).

This assumption is critically flawed for true lifelong personalization. Meaningful user signals frequently appear in historical sessions that are topically distant from the present query (Figure
1
). Information retrieval research captures this through
situational relevance
, which defines relevance by usefulness to the user’s task rather than semantic overlap
(
3
)
. We formalize this as
latent user context
: user-specific information concealed within semantically distant sessions yet situationally relevant and necessary for a correct personalized response. Current systems lack the notion of relevance needed to surface it.

To bridge this gap, we introduce
L
atent
U
ser
C
ontext benchmark (LUCid), a benchmark designed to evaluate
situational relevance
in lifelong personalization. LUCid comprises
1,936 realistic user queries
whose correct responses hinge on user-specific attributes, including domain affiliation, cultural/geographic context, age group, religion, health constraints, and general preferences, shared within semantically distant sessions of a lifelong interaction history of up to
500 sessions (620K tokens)
. Instances require either single-session inference, where a latent attribute must be inferred from one session, or multi-session reasoning, where evidence must be aggregated across conversations. By design, LUCid breaks the semantic proximity bias of current benchmarks, forcing systems to surface situationally relevant context.

Using LUCid, we ask:

What notion of relevance do current lifelong personalization systems encode, and can they reliably identify and utilize latent user context?
Our experiments evaluate state-of-the-art approaches spanning long-context conditioning, RAG-based memory architectures, and reranking systems. Our results demonstrate that existing systems largely encode relevance as semantic proximity, suffering significant performance collapse when user context must be inferred from semantically distant history, with retrieval recall dropping to near zero on hard instances and response alignment failing to exceed 50% even for state-of-the-art models such as GPT, Qwen and Claude. We further observe that failures to surface latent user context can lead to inappropriate or unsafe responses, and that in the absence of such signals, models often default to dominant pretraining priors, exhibiting WEIRD-aligned assumptions
(
24
)
. These findings expose a fundamental gap between the relevance current systems capture and what true lifelong personalization demands, highlighting the need for a richer notion of relevance that goes beyond semantic proximity.

In summary, our contributions are as follows: (1) We formalize
latent user context
and identify
semantic proximity bias
as a fundamental limitation of current personalization systems; (2) we introduce the
Proximity Advantage (PA)
score, a simple reusable metric for quantifying proximity bias in personalization benchmarks, and show that existing benchmarks (PAs of 91–97%) are largely solvable via semantic-based retrieval; (3) we introduce
LUCid
, 1,936 queries paired with histories of up to 500 sessions (620K tokens), explicitly designed to break proximity bias (PA = 52.6% for LUCid, 22.1% for LUCid-HARD); and (4) demonstrate through extensive experiments that state-of-the-art systems largely encode relevance as semantic proximity, exposing a fundamental gap between what current systems capture and what lifelong personalization demands.

Related Work

Methods for Lifelong Personalization.

Current approaches fall into three paradigms. Long-context methods directly condition on full interaction histories
(
46
;
2

7

, but face computational bottlenecks and needle-in-a-haystack failures

4

Critically, all three paradigms ultimately rely on semantic similarity at the retrieval frontier—whether over raw sessions, graph entry points/traversals, or compressed memory units—preserving the same proximity bottleneck we identify in Section

Benchmarks for Lifelong Personalization.

Existing benchmarks have progressively scaled in scope. Early work such as LaMP
(

)
and LongLaMP
(
18

cover personalized NLP tasks, and LoCoMo
(

targets long-term dialogue memory. More recent benchmarks probe dynamic user modeling across longer histories, including LongMemEval
(

, PrefEval
(

, PersonaMem
(
16
;
15
)
, and PersonaBench
(

)
.

(

introduces semantically diverse multi-session signals requiring implicit reasoning, but frames queries from a third-person perspective, stopping short of first-person personalization.
Across all benchmarks, relevant context remains largely recoverable via semantic overlap with the query, leaving the harder regime of latent, situationally relevant but semantically distant user context entirely unaddressed: the gap LUCid is designed to fill (see Appendix

3
Preliminaries

3.1
Lifelong Personalization: Problem Setting

Let
\mathcal{H}=\{s_{1},s_{2},\dots,s_{n}\}
be a lifelong interaction history consisting of
n
sessions, and let
q_{n+1}
be a new user query. A personalization system aims to generate a response
y
by conditioning on a subset of the history:
P(y\mid q_{n+1},\phi(\mathcal{H}))
, where
\phi
is a selection or compression function.

3.2
The Contrastive Bottleneck

The most common implementation of
\phi
is the Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) framework
(
19
)
, which selects a subset of
k
sessions from
\mathcal{H}
via a scoring function. Dense retrievers operationalize this by encoding queries and sessions into a shared embedding space, where relevance is approximated by vector similarity. While such retrievers could in principle encode
true relevance
, their notion of relevance is fundamentally shaped by their training objective and data. Models like Contriever
(
13
)
are optimized using a contrastive loss (e.g., InfoNCE) to minimize the distance between a query
q
and a positive context
z^{+}
(the relevant document):

\mathcal{L}=-\log\frac{\exp(\text{sim}(q,z^{+})/\tau)}{\sum_{j\in\{z^{+},z_{1},\dots,z_{m}\}}\exp(\text{sim}(q,z_{j})/\tau)}

(1)

The bottleneck arises from the training data sampling strategy:
z^{+}
is constructed from another span of the same document via augmentations such as the Inverse Cloze Task or independent cropping
(
13
)
. Since spans are drawn from the same document, they are semantically related, causing their embeddings to cluster in vector space. Computing cosine similarity between these representations therefore operationalizes relevance as semantic proximity. In a lifelong personalization setting, this implicitly forces the retriever to treat
latent user contexts
as a negative sample
z_{j}
, preventing it from surfacing the signals necessary for safe and personalized generation.

While current benchmarks aim to evaluate lifelong personalization, they implicitly
equate relevance with semantic proximity: relevant historical context and the current
query share sufficient embedding-space similarity that a retriever can surface them

4
LUCid

LUCid is designed to break the “proximity bias” identified in Section
3.3
, enabling systems to reason about
situational relevance
that goes beyond semantic proximity. Following Section
3.1
, each evaluation instance in LUCid is defined as
(\mathcal{H},q_{n+1},A^{*})
, where
A^{*}
denotes the
latent user attribute
that must be inferred from the interaction history to produce the correct personalized response
y^{*}=f(q_{n+1},A^{*})
. This attribute is not explicitly stated in the query nor in any individual session and must therefore be inferred. Consequently, we define the
Latent User Context

\mathcal{C}_{L}
as the set of sessions in
\mathcal{H}
from which
A^{*}
can be inferred. As we will see, by design
\mathcal{C}_{L}
often have low semantic similarity to the user query. Thus, solving a LUCid instance requires identifying and reasoning over sessions that contain user contexts that are situationally necessary for the response despite their weak semantic similarity to the query.

To accommodate varying computational constraints and research objectives, we introduce four benchmark variants:
LUCid-C
(controlled reranking probe, 30 sessions,
\sim
47K tokens),
LUCid-S
(Small, 50 sessions,
\sim
64K tokens),
LUCid-B
(Base, 200 sessions,
\sim
270K tokens), and
LUCid-L
(Large, 500 sessions,
\sim
620K tokens), with the latter providing a high-difficulty setting to push the boundaries of long-context reasoning. Across all variants, the benchmark consists of
1,936
evaluation queries, each paired with histories manually designed to reflect the dynamics of real-world conversational logs. We further define a challenging subset,
LUCid-HARD
, consisting of
856
instances from LUCid-B where the relevant sessions lie in the extreme semantic tail of the interaction history. We describe the dataset construction in the next section.

4.1
Constructing LUCid

We construct LUCid through a multi-stage pipeline that mirrors real-world conversational dynamics. Our pipeline consists of four stages: (1) query synthesis, (2) relevant session construction, (3) irrelevant session curation, and (4) interaction history
aggregation.

Query Synthesis.

We first identify a set of user-specific dimensions along which a correct personalized response varies meaningfully (Table
2
). Guided by these dimensions, we synthesize queries using three complementary strategies: (1) extraction and adaptation of naturally occurring user queries from real-world conversational logs such as UltraChat and ShareGPT
(
5
;
26
)
(N=125); (2) adoption or light modification of queries from existing datasets aligned to specific dimensions, including SituatedQA
(
44
)
for geographic and location-aware queries (N=273), PersonaMem
(
16
)
for health-related queries (N=561), PrefEval
(
46
)
for movie recommendation queries (N=75), and SafeBench
(
42
)
and CASE-Bench
(
34
)
for safety-critical age-sensitive queries (N=43); and (3) manual writing of queries for dimensions not covered by existing sources (N=859). All queries are manually inspected to ensure (i) clarity, (ii) realism, and (iii) presence of personalization cues that signal a user’s expectation of a tailored response. Query samples are provided in (Table

Relevant Session Construction.

Relevant sessions are those comprising the latent user context
\mathcal{C}_{L}

for a given query (Section
4
). Drawing on conversational logs from
UltraChat
(
5
)
and ShareGPT
(
26
)

to inform structure and linguistic style, sessions are sourced via three methods:
(1) direct extraction from logs where a session naturally surfaces a target attribute;
(2) dimension-value translation, where an extracted session is adapted to reflect a
different value within the same dimension (e.g., adapting a session revealing US residency to indicate UK residency); and (3) LLM-based synthesis via a self-chat pipeline
(
41
)
for attribute-value pairs underrepresented in existing logs.

For each dimension-value pair (e.g., age-group: teen), we construct a group of sessions
spanning diverse topics, each revealing the same latent attribute through indirect signals:
a user’s age, for instance, may surface across sessions about a first concert experience or a generational event. Some instances require multi-session reasoning, where no single session suffices: a user who consistently resists formal responses signals a preference for casual communication; one whose sessions span compilers, data mining, and tokenization plausibly indicates a computer science affiliation. This mirrors real-world personalization, where user identity emerges gradually across a history rather than through
isolated disclosures.

Irrelevant Session Construction.

Irrelevant sessions serve as realistic distractors: they contain no personally attributable user information and neither contribute latent context nor interact with or contradict signals from relevant sessions. We manually inspect over 10,000
sessions from UltraChat
(
5
)
and ShareGPT
(
26
)
and retain approximately 700 that satisfy this criterion, predominantly general knowledge-seeking or curiosity-driven conversations spanning diverse topics (e.g., wildlife behavior, general science, social phenomena).

Interaction History Aggregation.

For each evaluation instance — defined by a query, its personalization dimension, and a target attribute value — we select the corresponding relevant session group and compute the similarity between each session and the query. We retain the most semantically dissimilar session(s), specifically the single most dissimilar for single-signal instances and the five most dissimilar for multi-session instances. From the irrelevant pool, we select the
K
sessions most topically similar to the query, where
K
is determined by the benchmark variant (C, S, B, or L). The two sets are combined to form the final haystack for that evaluation instance, as illustrated in Figure
2
for both single-session (left) and multi-session (right) instances.

This construction is central to LUCid’s diagnostic intent: relevant sessions are selected for low similarity while irrelevant sessions are selected for high similarity, explicitly decoupling semantic similarity from true relevance. Crucially, because the two pools are independent, the relevant session does not necessarily occupy the lowest-similarity position in the haystack, reflecting natural variability in real interaction histories and preventing LUCid from reducing to a trivial inverse-similarity retrieval task. Further construction details are provided in Appendix
C.1
.

Experiments and Results

latent user context

RQ1:
To what extent can current retrieval systems identify latent user contexts?

RQ2:
What notion of relevance do rerankers/LLMs encode?

Can long-context LLMs natively surface and condition on latent user attributes?

6
Lessons Learned

6.1
Situational Relevance Should Be the Target for Lifelong Personalization

Relevance has been a central and contested concept in IR for decades

(
32
;
3
)
.

3
distinguishes between algorithmic, topical,
pertinence-based, and situational relevance (SR), with SR widely regarded as the most realistic form: it evaluates information with respect to the user’s actual task and context rather than lexical or topical overlap alone. Since objects may be situationally relevant without being algorithmically relevant, and vice versa
(
3
)
, SR is the appropriate target for lifelong personalization. The purpose of personalization is to make models more helpful for particular users, under their goals, constraints, and contexts. Once framed this way, relevance should be defined not by whether a past interaction is topically adjacent to the current request, but by whether it is useful for generating the right response for that user.

However, current personalization pipelines encode a narrower notion: while generation is evaluated using user-centered metrics, retrieval remains governed by semantic similarity, creating a structural mismatch where systems are optimized for personalized outputs but rely on mechanisms that systematically exclude the user context required to produce them. We argue for placing situational relevance at the forefront of personalization—at retrieval time, by prioritizing context that is situationally necessary even when semantically distant; at generation time, by evaluating responses on their ability to utilize that context appropriately and safely. Under this framing, LUCid is a step toward realigning personalization with the user-centered notion of relevance long established in IR.

6.2
Under Ambiguity, LLMs Default to WEIRD Assumptions

In the absence of user-specific signals, models resolve ambiguity by defaulting to dominant pretraining priors — producing U.S.-centric responses to location-sensitive queries rather than acknowledging uncertainty or eliciting missing context (see Appendix
D.4
for the full breakdown). While such defaults may be corrected through interaction, they are problematic when users implicitly assume the model understands their context, leading to misaligned or exclusionary responses. We posit that in such cases of unresolved ambiguity, a preferable strategy is to adopt an Overton pluralistic approach
(
33
)
, where models present a spectrum of plausible interpretations or elicit missing context rather than committing to a single implicit assumption.

6.3
Benchmark-Driven Alignment Does Not Generalize to Situational Safety

Under Gold conditioning, models achieve near-perfect performance on age-sensitive queries related to alcohol and explicit content (e.g., 100% for Qwen, see Appendix
D.3
for the full breakdown), yet systematically fail on entertainment recommendation queries targeting the same age group (e.g., 22.67% for Qwen), often recommending R- and MA-rated content to minors (see Figure
6.3
). This disparity reflects a structural bias in current alignment pipelines: models are highly optimized for harm categories well-represented in safety benchmarks, but exhibit inconsistent behavior where appropriateness is less explicitly defined. Since our explicit-content queries are derived directly from SafeBench and CASE-Bench
(
34
;
42
)
, models appear to have internalized benchmark-specific surface patterns rather than a generalizable principle of age-appropriateness. Addressing this requires moving beyond benchmark-driven alignment toward frameworks that treat safety as situational and user-specific — not reducible to a fixed set of harm categories.

7
Conclusion

We introduced LUCid, a benchmark that evaluates situational relevance in lifelong personalization by explicitly decoupling relevant user context from semantic proximity. Our experiments reveal a two-stage bottleneck: at retrieval, all systems—spanning RAG, memory-augmented architectures, and rerankers—collapse to near-zero recall on hard instances; at generation, state-of-the-art models (Gemini, GPT, Claude, Qwen) still struggle to condition on latent attributes even when the relevant session is directly provided. Beyond these core failures, models default to WEIRD-aligned priors under ambiguity and exhibit benchmark-driven inconsistencies in safety-critical settings. These findings expose a fundamental mismatch between the relevance current systems encode and what lifelong personalization demands, motivating a shift toward situational relevance at both retrieval and generation. We offer LUCid as a step toward that realignment.

8
Limitations

LUCid currently covers six user dimensions and multi-session reasoning across up to five sessions; extending to additional attributes such as disability or socioeconomic context, inter-dimension interactions, and longer evidence chains are natural directions for future work. Our evaluation covers representative architectures across dominant paradigms—a broader empirical comparison was precluded by cost, though we provide a theoretical account of why the proximity bottleneck extends to broader retrieval classes in Appendix
B.4
.

Ethics Statement

LUCid is constructed from publicly available conversational logs (UltraChat, ShareGPT) and existing benchmarks. No personally identifiable information was collected or retained. All user attributes (age group, religion, health constraints, geographic location, domain affiliation, and communication style) are synthetic and assigned to fictional personas; they do not correspond to or derive from real individuals.

Our benchmark deliberately incorporates sensitive user dimensions, including age, religion, and health, as these are precisely the attributes that matter most for safe and personalized generation. We emphasize that these dimensions are included to expose failures in current systems, not to enable or encourage their exploitation. Researchers using LUCid should be mindful of these sensitivities when reporting results or building systems evaluated on this benchmark.

Our findings reveal that current models exhibit benchmark-driven alignment inconsistencies in safety-critical settings, most notably recommending age-inappropriate content to minors even when the user’s age is explicitly provided. We surface these failures to motivate more robust and generalizable safety alignment, and encourage the community to treat safety as situational and user-specific rather than reducible to a fixed set of harm categories.

LUCid is intended for research evaluation of lifelong personalization systems and is not designed for adversarial probing of deployed systems or for identifying exploitable safety gaps in production models. We release the benchmark and curation pipeline to facilitate reproducible research and encourage extensions to additional user dimensions and demographic groups currently underrepresented in personalization evaluation.

We hope LUCid encourages the development of personalization systems that are more equitable, context-aware, and genuinely user-centered.

A.1
Current Methods for Lifelong Personalization

Current methods for lifelong personalization can be broadly categorized into three paradigms: long context methods, Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG), and augmented memory methods. Long context methods directly input extensive user histories into models’ context windows
(
46
;
2
;
7
)
; however, scaling user history introduces significant computational bottlenecks and suffer the well-documented “needle-in-a-haystack” failures
(
12
)
. To mitigate context limitations, RAG
(
19
)
methods conditioning the model’s responses on only the top-k most semantically relevant user sessions. Extensions such as GraphRAG
(
10
;
6
;
9
;
40
)
organize interaction histories into structured entity–relation graphs to enable multi-hop reasoning across sessions. Memory-augmented systems
(
36
;
27
;
4
;
47
;
37
)
further consolidate histories into structured, queryable memory units via memory agents and hierarchical compression.
Augmented memory methods treat personalization and memory as an evolving state, using systems However, both RAG and GraphRAG approximate relevance as semantic proximity at the retrieval frontier: flat RAG scores sessions by embedding-space similarity to the query; GraphRAG encodes proximity bias into both entry-point selection and in some implementations graph traversal
(
6
;
28
)
, causing it to compound at each hop; and memory-augmented systems surface their compressed units using same backbone (flat RAG, GraphRAG, or a dual-stage retrieve-then-rerank pipeline) thus, preserving the bottleneck on a compressed index rather than eliminating it. We evaluate LUCid across a representative set of these approaches as well as provide a theoretical account of their structural limitations in Section
3
.

(

Appendix B
Extended Preliminaries

B.1
Lifelong Personalization: Problem Setting

Let
\mathcal{H}=\{s_{1},s_{2},\dots,s_{n}\}
be a lifelong interaction history consisting of
n
sessions, and let
q_{n+1}
be a new user query. A personalization system aims to generate a response
y
by conditioning on a subset of the history:
P(y\mid q_{n+1},\phi(\mathcal{H}))
, where
\phi
is a selection or compression function.

B.2
Retrieval-Augmented Generation

The most common implementation of
\phi
is the Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) framework
(
19
)
, which selects a subset of
k
sessions from
\mathcal{H}
via a scoring function:
\phi(\mathcal{H},q_{n+1})=\text{top-}k_{s_{i}\in\mathcal{H}}\left(\text{score}(q_{n+1},s_{i})\right)

Early retrievers such as DPR
(
17
)
learn these scores from supervised question-answer pairs, while more recent systems such as Contriever
(
13
)
use large-scale contrastive objectives for more general-purpose embeddings. In both cases, relevance reduces to vector similarity in the embedding space:

\text{score}(q_{n+1},s_{i})=E(q_{n+1})^{\top}E(s_{i})

(2)

As we show next, this shared reliance on embedding-space proximity has fundamental consequences for lifelong personalization.

B.3
The Contrastive Bottleneck

While dense retrievers could, in principle, encode
true relevance
, their utility is shaped by their training objective. Models like Contriever are optimized using a contrastive loss (e.g., InfoNCE) to minimize the distance between a query
q
and a positive context
z^{+}
:

\mathcal{L}=-\log\frac{\exp(\text{sim}(q,z^{+})/\tau)}{\sum_{j\in\{z^{+},z_{1},\dots,z_{m}\}}\exp(\text{sim}(q,z_{j})/\tau)}

(3)

The bottleneck arises from the training data sampling strategy:
z^{+}
is constructed from another span of the same document via augmentations such as the Inverse Cloze Task or independent cropping. Since spans are drawn from the same document, they inevitably share topical content, causing their embeddings to cluster in vector space. Computing cosine similarity between these representations therefore operationalizes relevance as topical proximity. In a lifelong personalization setting, this implicitly forces the retriever to treat
latent user contexts
as a negative sample
z_{j}
, preventing it from surfacing the signals necessary for safe and personalized generation.

B.4
Beyond Standard RAG

Several works augment standard RAG to address its granularity and structural limitations.
Memory-augmented systems
(
27
;
4
;
36
)
compress interaction histories into structured, queryable memory units; however, these units are still surfaced at query time via embedding-space similarity search, preserving the proximity bottleneck on a compressed index rather than eliminating it. Graph-augmented systems
(
10
;
6
;
9
;
40
)
organize history into entity-relation graphs to enable multi-hop reasoning across sessions. However, both entry-point node selection and, in some implementations, graph traversal are guided by similarity-based scoring function
(
6
;
28
)
, causing proximity bias to compound at each hop. These augmentations thus share the same structural failure: without a richer notion of relevance at the retrieval frontier, latent user context remains systematically inaccessible.

B.5
Retriever Recall@
k
as an Upper Bound on Reranker Performance

To reduce noise from the initial retrieval stage, recent works across all the aforementioned approaches
(
8
;
36
;
6
)
incorporate a multi-stage pipeline in which a reranker
\psi
re-evaluates and re-ranks the top-
k
candidate sessions surfaced by an initial retriever
\phi_{\text{ret}}
, selecting the final
m\leq k
sessions for generation:

\phi_{\text{rerank}}(\mathcal{H},q_{n+1})=\text{top-}m_{\,s_{i}\in\phi_{\text{ret}}(\mathcal{H},\,q_{n+1},\,k)}\bigl(\psi(q_{n+1},s_{i})\bigr)

(4)

While
\psi
can model complex query-history dependencies, it is constrained by the initial retrieval step. Let
\mathcal{Z}_{k}=\phi_{\text{ret}}(\mathcal{H},q_{n+1},k)
denote the candidate set and
\mathcal{R}(q_{n+1})\subseteq\mathcal{H}
the set of truly relevant sessions. Since
\mathcal{Z}_{m}\subseteq\mathcal{Z}_{k}
, it follows directly that

\left|\mathcal{R}(q_{n+1})\cap\mathcal{Z}_{m}\right|\leq\left|\mathcal{R}(q_{n+1})\cap\mathcal{Z}_{k}\right|

(5)

meaning reranker recall is upper bounded by retriever’s
\mathrm{Recall@}k
. Therefore, rerankers may improve precision within the candidate set but cannot recover information absent from the retriever’s top-
k
. If latent user context is not retrieved in the first stage, no downstream reranker can surface it.

B.6
Relevance as Semantic Proximity in Existing Benchmarks

While current benchmarks aim to evaluate lifelong personalization, they often implicitly equate
true relevance
with semantic proximity: the relevant historical context and the query are sufficiently similar in embedding space that a similarity-based retriever can surface them through shallow matching alone. To quantify this, we define the
Semantic Proximity Score (SPS)
between a query
q_{i}
and a session
s
as their cosine similarity in embedding space:
\text{SPS}(q_{i},s)=\cos(E(q_{i}),E(s))
. For a dataset with
N
queries, we compute the mean SPS between each query and its ground-truth relevant session(s)
\mathcal{R}(q_{i})
:

\overline{\text{SPS}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{|\mathcal{R}(q_{i})|}\sum_{s\in\mathcal{R}(q_{i})}\cos(E(q_{i}),E(s))

(6)

However, absolute SPS values are difficult to interpret in isolation. We therefore ground each benchmark’s SPS against a
random baseline
: for each query
q_{i}
, we sample a non-relevant session
s^{-}\notin\mathcal{R}(q_{i})
from the same history and compute
\text{SPS}(q_{i},s^{-})
. This null reflects the background similarity between arbitrary session-query pairs under the same embedding model. We then report the
proximity advantage
as the fraction of queries for which the gold session is more similar to the query than a randomly sampled non-relevant session:

\text{PA}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{1}\left[\frac{1}{|\mathcal{R}(q_{i})|}\sum_{s\in\mathcal{R}(q_{i})}\cos(E(q_{i}),s)>\cos(E(q_{i}),s^{-})\right]

(7)

A high PA indicates that relevant sessions are systematically more similar to the query than random sessions, meaning a similarity-based retriever has a strong exploitable signal. An PA near 50% indicates that relevant sessions are
indistinguishable from noise
under similarity search.

Applying this analysis to
PrefEval

(
46
)
,
LongMemEval

(
38
)
,
PersonaMem

(
15
)
, and
ImplexConv

(
21
)
reveals a consistent pattern: across 3/4 of the benchmarks, gold sessions are ranked above a random session in 91–97% of cases (Table
7
), confirming that existing benchmarks are largely solvable through similarity-based retrieval alone. ImplexConv, despite introducing semantically diverse multi-session signals, still yields a PA of 61.1%, indicating that implicit reasoning cues retain sufficient surface similarity to remain retrievable. In contrast, LUCid’s retrieval advantage collapses to 52.6% — statistically indistinguishable from a random baseline — and further to 22.1% on LUCid-H, where latent context is drawn from the extreme semantic tail of the interaction history, demonstrating that latent user context carries no exploitable similarity signal and cannot be surfaced through proximity-based retrieval.

Appendix C
Data Synthesis and Analysis

C.1
Further Construction details

Our dataset was constructed in a systematic four-stage process that mirrors real-world conversations between users and chatbots: (1) query synthesis, (2) relevant session synthesis, (3) irrelevant session synthesis, and (4) aggregation.

C.1.1
1. Query synthesis

We identify six user-specific dimensions (domain, age group, location, religion, medical condition, and communication style) and synthesize queries via three strategies: extraction from UltraChat and ShareGPT
(
5
;
26
)
(N=125), modification of existing dataset queries, and manual authoring (N=859).

Domain.

Domain queries are constructed around
polysemous terms
, words whose meaning or application varies across professional fields (e.g., “entropy” in physics vs. information theory). We use WordNet
(
29
)
to identify such terms via their sense distinctions across domains, then prompt an LLM to generate query variations around each term, followed by manual review for validity. The full pipeline is illustrated in Figure
3
.

Figure 3:
Pipeline for generating domain queries.

Age Group.
We identify three sub-dimensions where appropriate responses vary meaningfully by age: health, fitness, and entertainment. Fitness and health queries are generated via LLM prompting; entertainment queries are extracted from PrefEval
(
46
)
. Safety-critical age-sensitive queries are sourced from SafeBench
(
42
)
and CASE-Bench
(
34
)
(N=43). All queries are manually reviewed and rewritten where necessary to ensure that a generic response would be insufficient — that is, a correct response requires knowing the user’s age group.

Country/Location.
Queries are extracted from SituatedQA
(
44
)
and supplemented with LLM-generated queries verified through a human-in-the-loop process. All queries are rewritten to embed implicit location cues, ensuring that a correct personalized response requires inferring the user’s geographic context rather than defaulting to a generic answer.

Religion and Communication Style.
Queries are manually authored and augmented with LLM generation, covering topics where correct responses vary meaningfully based on the user’s religious norms or preferred communication style.

Medical/Health Condition.
Queries are extracted from PersonaMem
(
16
)
and supplemented with LLM-generated queries, covering health and lifestyle topics where advice varies based on the user’s medical condition.

C.1.2
Relevant Session Synthesis

Relevant sessions are constructed from UltraChat, and ShareGPT
(
5
;
26
)
. We first apply LLM-based screening to identify sessions from which at least one target dimension can be implicitly inferred, followed by manual verification to confirm that the latent attribute is recoverable without explicit disclosure.

To address representational imbalances across dimension values, we apply dimension-value translation: existing sessions are adapted to reflect underrepresented values by swapping entities or injecting implicit cues. For location, U.S.-centric sessions are localized to other regions (e.g., replacing U.S. store references with Nigerian equivalents). For age group, senior citizen sessions are augmented with temporal cues (e.g., retirement references, allusions to historical events) and teenager sessions with school-related markers and implicit birth year indicators. For religion, general sessions are adapted by inserting faith-based contextual cues (e.g., references to Ramadan). Medical/health condition sessions are sourced directly from PersonaMem
(
16
)
. Domain and communication style sessions are synthesized via the self-chat pipeline described in Figure
7
. For communication style, a user’s preference is distributed across multiple sessions, each reflecting the same stylistic tendency in a different conversational context, such that inferring the attribute requires multi-session reasoning rather than a single salient disclosure. Both domain and communication-style query instances in LUCid requires reasoning across multiple sessions to infer the user attribute
A^{*}
.

C.1.3
Irrelevant Session Curation

Irrelevant sessions serve as realistic distractors within the interaction history, containing no personally attributable user information and neither contributing latent context nor contradicting signals from relevant sessions. We apply LLM-based screening to rapidly filter sessions from UltraChat and ShareGPT
(
5
;
26
)
where no user-specific information could be inferred, followed by manual inspection of over 10,000 candidate sessions to verify that none reveal information across any of our six target dimensions. This process yields a pool of 700 irrelevant sessions, predominantly general knowledge-seeking and day-to-day conversations spanning diverse topics.

and illustrated in Figure

Qualitative analysis reveals that Gemini more actively leverages implicit cues from relevant sessions; for instance, it correctly conditioned on the phrase “in my grade” to infer the user was a teenager, the exact signal embedded in the session by design.

D.4
WEIRD Assumptions Under Ambiguity

In the absence of user-specific context (No Retrieval setting), we analyze how models respond to location-sensitive queries. As shown in Figure
11
, models consistently produce U.S.- or Western-centric responses rather than acknowledging geographic ambiguity or eliciting missing context. Across all models, the majority of responses assume a Western context, with non-Western locations accounting for a small fraction of outputs despite representing a significant portion of the world’s population.

This pattern is consistent with WEIRD-aligned biases documented in LLMs
(
24
)
, where models default to Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, and Democratic assumptions when contextual signals are absent. In a personalization setting, such defaults are particularly problematic: when models silently commit to implicit assumptions about a user, those who fall outside dominant pretraining priors receive misaligned responses with no indication that the model has filled in missing context on their behalf. Beyond degraded personalization, this has broader implications for safety, equity, and user trust—silent assumptions can lead to harmful recommendations, exclusionary responses, and an erosion of confidence in systems that users expect to understand them. These findings motivate the need for models to either acknowledge uncertainty explicitly or adopt a pluralistic approach
(
33
)
when user context is unavailable.
