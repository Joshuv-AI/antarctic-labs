Modular synthesis of trisubstituted olefins and 1,3-dienes from renewable alcohols via ligand-enabled nickel catalysis
Authors: Adrija Ghosh, Purushotam, Chao-Jun Li, Debasis Banerjee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOlefins are fundamental functional groups and of substantial commercial importance across a broad range of value-added chemicals. Their production from biomass-derived alcohols remains a longstanding goal, while the selective synthesis of trisubstituted olefins presents a formidable challenge that demands precise catalytic control to suppress undesired product formation. Herein, we demonstrate sequential cross-coupling/isomerization and cross-coupling/dehydrogenation of two different alcohols leading to stereoselective synthesis of trisubstituted olefins (27 examples; E/Z up to 98:2) and 1,3-dienes (23 examples; E/Z > 20:1) using a commercially available nickel catalyst enabled by ligand control. Late-stage functionalization using DL-galactose, α-tocopherol and post-product modification to a tamoxifen analogue and polyaromatic hydrocarbon highlight the significance of our developed method. Preliminary mechanistic studies indicate that alcohol dehydrogenation to the corresponding aldehyde is the rate-determining step (PH/PD = 4.3). Detailed mechanistic studies and deuterium labelling experiments support an underlying DCR (dehydrogenation-condensation-rehydrogenation) approach, followed by stereo-selective isomerization or dehydrogenation enabled by nickel.Subject terms: Synthetic chemistry methodology, Catalyst synthesis, Homogeneous catalysis

Introduction

Trisubstituted olefins and 1,3-dienes are ubiquitous in natural products and pharmaceuticals and serve as highly versatile intermediates in the synthesis of specialty chemicals in the petrochemical industry (Fig. 1a)1–3. More broadly, the stereochemical outcome of olefin functionalization is largely dictated by the configuration of the parent olefin; consequently, a wide array of stereoselective strategies for olefin synthesis has been developed over the years. However, to achieve precise stereocontrol in the synthesis of functionalized alkenes has long been a central challenge in organic chemistry. Conventional strategies for the efficient synthesis of olefins or dienes include the Wittig olefination, the Mizoroki-Heck coupling, and catalytic cross-metathesis to name a few4–8. Despite significant advances, key challenges remain, including reliance on pre-defined organometallic reagents or electrophiles, the generation of stoichiometric amounts of unwanted by-products, and limited stereoselectivity, particularly in the synthesis of trisubstituted olefins. In addition, substrate-controlled access to both cis- and trans-isomers from readily available renewable feedstocks continues to pose a longstanding challenge9–12.

In this regard, alkyne hydroalkylation13–16, and positional isomerization of 1,1-disubstituted alkenes as well as dienes17–21, have been extensively explored under nickel catalysis (Fig. 1b). Dual nickel/photoredox catalysis has emerged as a powerful ligand-controlled approach for achieving selective alkyne difunctionalization with a wide range of coupling partners under mild conditions14. The later strategy has offered improved atom economy to highly selective synthesis of trisubstituted olefins. Nickel in combination with specially designed and expensive phosphine or highly sensitive NHC ligands are key for successful transformations.

However, multi-step ligand synthesis as well as pre-functionalized 1,1-di-substituted olefins are often associated challenges and limit their applicability towards the synthesis of complex molecules22–28. Therefore, the development of a more general catalytic approach delivering improved selectivity in the synthesis of unsaturated systems from renewable resources is the need of the hour. In this context, readily accessible biomass-derived alcohols have been scarcely explored, yet are increasingly recognized as sustainable alkyl synthons in cross-coupling reactions29–35. Their influence in olefin synthesis has only been limited to dehydroformylation, dehydroxymethylation, deoxydehydration of diols or polyols, and dehydrogenative couplings36–41. However, in all these cases, the reaction scope is limited to di-substituted or self-coupled olefins. Furthermore, the requirement of sacrificial olefin acceptors and proton shuttles, and instability of the in-situ formed intermediates are the associated challenges and have led to the search for more general alcohol to alkene transformation (Fig. 1c).

Thus, a general strategy that directly transforms readily accessible alcohols into stereodefined trisubstituted olefins via sequential catalytic processes remains elusive. Moreover, sequential one-pot intermolecular coupling of two distinct alcohols—capable of rapidly increasing molecular complexity—has not yet been explored for the synthesis of olefins or dienes42–45. Driven by our interest in developing sustainable and cost-effective base-metal catalysis using alcohol as alkyl synthons, we have demonstrated a series of sustainable strategies, including the synthesis of (E)-vinylarenes42,46–51. Building on this foundation, we sought to extend the scope of our approach toward more structurally complex and synthetically versatile olefin frameworks such as β,β-di-substituted styrenes, which serve as key precursors for important organic transformations, such as epoxidation, aziridination, cyclopropanation, etc52–54 (Fig. 1d). Controlling product selectivity and regioselectivity in the cross-dehydrogenative coupling of alcohols remains a significant challenge, owing to the propensity for alcohol and ketone side products to form under well-established borrowing-hydrogen conditions. Furthermore, tuning catalyst reactivity to enable both cross-coupling and isomerization in a tandem sequence—while suppressing undesired pathways—is highly demanding and requires precise, synergistic control of the catalyst and ligand. Herein, we report the selective synthesis of β,β-di-substituted styrenes via the direct catalytic cross-coupling of two different primary alcohols (Fig. 1d). Employing a simple, inexpensive and commercially available nickel catalyst (Ni-Cat. A) in the presence of a bidentate nitrogen-donor ligand (1,10-phenanthroline), we have successfully obtained a series of (E)-selective α-methyl styrene derivatives via a tandem regioselective cross-coupling/dehydrative elimination/stereocontrolled isomerization sequence. Next, we explored the cross-coupling of secondary and primary alcohols in the presence of a more flexible bidentate nitrogen-donor ligand (2,2’-bipyridine) and observed the unprecedented formation of highly selective (E, E)−1,3-dienes, which are widespread in drug intermediates, biosynthetic metabolites and natural products55–58 and have previously been synthesized via rearrangement of allenes or allenols or the cross-coupling of vinylic carbons59,60. Notably, we demonstrated the synthetic application of both the olefin and 1,3-diene via the synthesis of tamoxifen analog and a polyaromatic hydrocarbon, respectively.

To begin our study, we hypothesized that in-situ synthesis of 1,1-disubstituted olefin can be achieved via a nickel-mediated cross-coupling of two different alcohols (Fig. 2). Subsequent nickel-mediated positional isomerization of the less stable 1,1-disubstituted olefin would afford the trisubstituted olefin product in which the nickel catalyst plays a dual role in dehydrogenation as well as isomerization. In addition, a tandem regioselective cross-coupling/dehydrative elimination/dehydrogenation sequence would lead to direct access to a variety of 1,3-dienes.

Results

Reaction optimization

To devise the highly selective sequential alcohol to olefin transformation, 2-(4-methoxyphenyl)ethan-1-ol 1a and benzyl alcohol 2a were chosen as model substrates for the targeted cross-coupling of alcohols. We began our initial investigation using various reaction parameters as presented in Table 1. A series of commercially available nickel salts were examined using 1,10-phenanthroline as the ligand, NaOH as the base in toluene at 150 °C for 24 hs (Table 1, entries 1–5, Supplementary Table S1). Ni(OTf)2 outperformed the rest and delivered the desired internal olefin 4 in 52% GC yield (entry 5). Thereafter, application of bidentate nitrogen-based ligands (L2-L6, Supplementary Table S2) did not further improve the yield of 4 (Table 1, entries 6–9). Variation of bases proved detrimental to the product yield and the reaction was highly selective to NaOH as base (Table 1, entries 10-11, Supplementary Table S3). The efficasy of different polar as well as non-polar solvents were studied and proved incompatible in comparison to toluene (Table 1, entry 12 Supplementary Table S4).

Reaction scope

Next, to establish the generality of the nickel-catalyzed olefination, we explored the scope of the reaction using a combination of 2-aryl ethanol derivatives with benzyl alcohol under the optimized reaction conditions as shown in Fig. 3. A variety of electron rich as well as electron deficient 2-aryl ethanols bearing sensitive functionalities were efficiently converted to the desired α-methyl styrenes in moderate to good yields with high trans-selectivity (E/Z up to 98:2). (Fig. 3). Initially, we studied the influence of the methoxy group at the ortho-, meta- and para- position of 2-phenylethanol (1b, 1c) respectively and observed 47–52% isolated yields of olefins 5-6. Further, we explored the effect of electron-donating (Me, Et) as well as electron-poor (CF3) functionalities on benzyl alcohol (2b-2d) and resulted in up to 56% yield to α-methyl styrene 7-9. Interestingly, the phenoxy group substituted at the meta-position of 2-phenyl ethanol (1d) afforded the corresponding olefin 10 in 59% yield.

For instance, 3-phenyl-1-propanol (1i), reacted with 2e and gave acceptable yield of trisubstituted olefin, 22. Thereafter, un-activated alkyl alcohols having varying chain length, such as 1-propanol (1j), n-hexanol (1k), n-octanol (1 l), and n-dodecanol (1 m) were efficiently coupled with 2i and 2e respectively, leading to trisubstituted olefins 23–26. Further, long-chain fatty acid (palmitic acid) derived alcohol (1n), and citronellol, resulted in trisubstituted olefins 27-28 in up to 66% yield. Notably, p-amino substituted 2-phenyl ethanol (1 g) coupled with cyclobutyl methanol (2k) and yielded to 29 with high selectivity.

Variation on the substitution (EDG/EWG) on the p-position of aryl alcohol (2), the E/Z ratios of the internal olefin did not change much, indicated no effect of electronics on the stereo-selectivity of the trisubstituted olefin (Fig. 4a).

On the contrary, steric effects considerably influenced the stereo-selectivity of the desired products as illustrated by using a methoxy group substituted at the p-, m-, or o-positions of the 2-aryl ethanol (Fig. 4b). Noteworthy to mention that, when aryl ethanol is replaced with an alkyl alcohol, a decrease in the E/Z ratio from 96:4 to 91:9 was observed. Further, increasing the chain length led to a reduction in the E/Z ratio to 73:27 (Fig. 4c).

Thereafter, we explored the reactivity of 1-aryl ethanol derivatives with secondary alcohols as indicated in Fig. 5. We anticipated that the sequential events associated with the dehydrogenative coupling of two distinct alcohols, secondary and primary alcohols, would lead to the formation of highly-substituted olefins under the optimized reaction conditions of Table 1. In this direction, our initial trials were not successful, and brief optimization studies were conducted. To our delight, a combination of NiBr2 and 2,2’-bipyridine in the presence of NaOH, led to serendipitous formation of 1,4-diaryl substituted buta-1,3-dienes 30–52, respectively in good to high yield (see supplementary section [1.3.2], Supplementary Tables S10–13). GC analysis of the representative reaction between 3a and 1 f indicated complete consumption of 3a, while the moderate yield of 30 primarily arises from competing β-alkylation pathways under the reaction conditions (SI, Supplementary Table S15). While both olefin and 1,3-diene synthesis may proceed through a common dehydrogenation manifold, the subsequent bond-forming events appear to require different catalyst/ligand environments to achieve optimal reactivity and catalyst stability.

To our delight, 1-phenyl ethanol (3a) coupled with 2-phenyl ethanol (1 f) under the optimized conditions and led to the formation of 1,3-diene (30) in 56% yield. Thereafter, 1-phenyl ethanol bearing electronic and sterically different substituent, such as, p-Me, p-Et, p-cy, 1-naphthyl, 2-naphthyl, p-OMe, m-OMe, including other functionalities, afforded 1,3-dienes in acceptable yield (31–38). 1-phenyl ethanol bearing p-F group (3j) gave the desired product 39 in 55% yield. 1-thiophen-2-yl-ethanol (3k) also smoothly participated and resulted in the desired thiophen-substituted olefin 40. Importantly, 2-arylethanol bearing p-Cl and p-CF3 substituent smoothly transformed to 1,3-dienes 41 and 42. Interestingly, secondary alcohol derived from tetralone (3 l) also smoothly participated and afforded the desired 1,3-diene 43. Following the success of activated cyclohexanol derivatives, we probed into the reactivity of cyclic aliphatic alcohols under otherwise similar reaction conditions. Notably, sequential coupling of cyclohexanol (3 m) with various 2-phenylethanols resulted in conjugate diene 44-47 in up to 70 % yield, respectively. Notably, 2-phenyl ethanol bearing p-bromo, and p-chloro functionalities were well tolerated in the transformations with moderate yield (48-49). To demonstrate the synthetic potential of this strategy, late-stage functionalization using 1-phenyl ethanol-derived citronellol (3n) (monoterpene alcohol), DL-Galactose (3o) (monosaccharide), and α-Tocopherol (3p) (a fat-soluble vitamin) with 2-phenylethanol (1 f) gave moderate to good yields of the desired dienes 50-52 in one pot operation (Fig. 5).

Synthetic applications

Thereafter, we conducted post-product modification of the selected trisubstituted olefin to a class of non-steroidal stilbenoids. For instance, sequential cross-coupling of internal olefin 12 and 2-(4-bromophenoxy)-N,N-dimethylethan-1-amine (53),61 afforded 60% yield to tetra-substituted olefin (54), an analog of tamoxifen, an anti-estrogen widely used for the treatment of breast cancer (Fig. 6a). Further, highly substituted poly-aromatic hydrocarbon (56) was obtained in 82% yield via a sequential cross-coupling to 1,3-diene 30 followed by Diels-alder cycloaddition using dimethyl but-2-ynedioate (55) (Fig. 6b)62.

Mechanistic studies

Following the exploration of the scope of internal olefins and 1,3-dienes along with synthetic applications, we further extended our studies toward the mechanistic aspect of our developed catalytic strategy (Fig. 7)47,51–54,63,64. We conducted various control experiments in the absence of catalyst and base to illustrate their role for successful transformations (see Supplementary section 1.4a, Supplementary Fig. S1). Both the catalyst and the base played a key role, and the reaction failed to give any desired product in their absence. Thereafter, we prepared defined Ni-Cat. A (see Supplementary section 1.4b, Supplementary Fig. S2) and subjected to cross-coupling of 1 f with 2a under otherwise similar reaction conditions of Table 1 (See Supplementary Fig. S3). GC analysis of the crude reaction mixture showed 64% product formation (12), thereby evident that Ni-Cat. A is the active nickel species formed under standard reaction conditions (Fig. 7a). Thereafter, we monitored the coupling reactions of 1 f with 2a under Ni-catalyzed conditions using GC-analysis for the formation of internal olefin. After 4 hours, GC analysis showed the presence of an α,β-unsaturated aldehyde intermediate (int A) along with intermediate (12’) bearing an exocyclic double bond (Fig. 7bi). Thereafter, 1H-NMR analysis identified the characteristic peak at δ = 5.11 ppm and evident the formation of external olefin (see Supplementary Fig. S4). Afterward, a time-dependent GC-study was conducted using 1 f with 2a under standard conditions A over 4 h interval and plotted against time to observe the formation of 12 and 12’ (Fig. 7bii, see Supplementary Figs. S5, S6). A gradual increase in the product concentration was observed over time, whereas the external olefin intermediate 12’ was present for a short interval, indicating its transient nature25,26. We envisioned the possibility for the involvement of the branched primary alcohol intermediate via metal-catalyzed hydrogenation of int A. To prove this hypothesis, we independently prepared the branched alcohol int B and subjected to nickel-catalyzed transformation using standard reaction conditions A. Notably, 68% yield of the desired olefin 12 was obtained and confirm the involvement of int B. (Fig. 7biii, see Supplementary Fig. S7)47,51–54,63,64. Control experiments using int B in the absence of base and catalyst also revealed their key role, and no product was detected in the absence of base. Further, standard reaction with the intermediate 12’ and control experiments in the absence of base and catalyst also revealed similar results, proving their crucial role in the isomerization step.

Next, a series of deuterium labeling experiments were conducted and observed the formation of trisubstituted olefins bearing variable amounts of deuterium incorporation at the olefin core (Fig. 7c, see Supplementary section 1.4 d, Supplementary Fig. S8–12)47,63. For instance, coupling of 1 f with d2-benzyl alcohol (2a-d2) resulted in a poor product (12 A) with < 5% deuterium incorporation. However, the reaction of 1f-d2 with benzyl alcohol (2a) resulted in 50% product (12B) in up to 29% deuterium incorporation and observed a PH/PD value of 4.353.

These experimental outcomes indicated the possibility of involvement of benzylic C-H bond breaking as the rate-determining step. Moreover, such experimental observation also supports the involvement of the borrowing hydrogen strategy and micro-reversible transformation of H/D exchange using an alcohol coupling partner. Thereafter, a series of the kinetic studies were conducted using two different concentrations of 2-phenyl ethanol (1 f) and benzyl alcohol (2a) (see Supplementary section 1.4ei, Supplementary Fig. S13) and establish that the catalytic reaction follows first-order kinetics (see Supplementary Fig. S14). Next, a Hammett study was conducted with variously substituted benzyl alcohols (R = H, Me, OMe, Cl, CF3) and a negative ρ-value indicated the generation of a positive charge during the progress of the reaction (Fig. 7d, see Supplementary Figs. S15–17).

Subsequently, we also conducted a series of mechanistic studies for the synthesis of 1,3-dienes (Fig. 8a–c).59,60 Herein, we have independently prepared the defined Ni-catalyst (Cat. C) and employed for the cross-coupling of 3a with 1 f (see Supplementary Figs. S18, S19). Successful product formation proved the involvement of such catalytic species under standard reaction conditions (Fig. 8a). Thereafter, int. C was independently synthesized and explored under standard conditions B, 44% yield to the desired 1,3-diene (44) confirmed the involvement of alcohol int. C. Control experiments in the absence of base or catalyst further implied their crucial role in this step (Fig. 8b and see Supplementary Fig. S20).

Further, a series of deuterium labeling studies were conducted for the cross-coupling of secondary and primary alcohols (Fig. 8c and see Supplementary Figs. S21–S23). For instance, coupling of deuterated cyclohexanol (2m-d4) with 2-phenylethanol (1 f) resulted in 40% deuterated 1,3-diene (44 A) in up to 48% deuterium incorporation at the desired positions. Similarly, when the coupling of cyclohexanol (2 m) with deuterated 2-phenyl ethanol (1f-d2 and 1 f’-d2) were conducted, proved the involvement of the borrowing hydrogen approach. We have realized that, initially, Ni-catalyzed coupling led to β-alkylation of secondary alcohol with primary alcohol, which subsequently undergoes dehydration in the presence of base. Thereafter, Ni-catalyzed sequential dehydrogenation resulted in the desired 1,3-dienes65.

Based on the mechanistic studies and deuterium labeling experiments,25,26,47,53 we proposed a plausible reaction mechanism as illustrated in Fig. 9. Initially, active species (Ni-Cat. A) is formed using Ni(OTf)2 and 1,10-phenanthroline and allowed the facile dehydrogenation of primary alcohols 1 and 2 via formation of the corresponding Ni-alkoxy species A and B, respectively. Notably, dehydrogenated species 1’ and 2’ were detected via GC-MS analysis using the crude reaction mixture. We also envisioned the in-situ generation of the transient nickel hydride species (Ni-Cat. B). Next, base mediated condensation of these aldehydes led to the formation of α,β-unsaturated aldehyde intermediate (int A) (detected in HRMS), which further underwent hydrogenation in the presence of Ni-Cat. B resulting the formation of the alcohol intermediate (int B) along with the regeneration of the active catalyst (Ni-Cat. A). Dehydration of int B at higher temperatures resulted the transient exocyclic olefin intermediate (12’, detected in GC-MS and NMR) and subsequently undergoes Ni-mediated isomerization to the thermodynamically more stable internal olefin product 12.

Similarly, in the case of secondary alcohols, we have detected the intermediate species aldehyde (1’) and ketone (3’). Subsequently, base mediated condensation of these species leads to the formation of int A’, which was detected in the HRMS analyses. Further, int A’ underwent in-situ re-hydrogenation to int C and detected using 1H-NMR analyses. Following this, base mediated dehydration and sequential Ni-mediated dehydrogenation resulted in 1,3-diene 3059,60,65.

Discussion

Herein, we have demonstrated the modular synthesis of trisubstituted olefins and 1,3-dienes enabled by a nickel catalyst. Two distinct renewable alcohols undergoes sequential cross-coupling, dehydrative elimination and isomerization/dehydrogenation strategy, selectively controlled by suitable ligands using nickel. A series of β,β-di-substituted styrene via cross-coupling of two different primary alcohols with high stereoselectivity were obtained in good to high yield (27 examples; E/Z up to 98:2). Notably, cross-coupling of a secondary and a primary alcohols resulted in highly selective 1,3-dienes (23 examples; E/Z > 20:1). For the synthetic application, anti-estrogen drug, tamoxifen, and poly-aromatic hydrocarbon were obtained via direct post product modification using our approach. Overall, we anticipated that this deoxygenative olefination strategy will serve as a benchmark for future developments from abundant alcohols following sustainable catalysis.

Methods

General Procedure for Nickel-catalyzed sequential cross-coupling/ isomerization and dehydrogenation strategy: Procedure A for the synthesis of internal olefin (4)

In a 15 mL oven-dried pressure tube, 2-(4-methoxyphenyl)ethan-1-ol (1a) (38 mg, 0.25 mmol, 1.0 equiv.), Ni(OTf)2 (8.9 mg, 0.025 mmol, 0.10 equiv.), 1,10-phenanthroline (5.4 mg, 0.03 mmol, 0.12 equiv.), NaOH (20 mg, 0.50 mmol, 2.0 equiv.), and benzyl alcohol 2a (81 mg, 0.75 mmol, 3.0 equiv.) were dissolved in toluene (1.0 mL, 0.25 M) under a nitrogen atmosphere and heated at 150 °C in a pre-heated oil bath for 36 h in a closed system. The reaction mixture was cooled to room temperature and quenched with 2.0 mL of ethyl acetate and concentrated in vacuo. The residue was purified by column chromatography using a gradient of hexane and ethyl acetate (eluent system) to afford the 4 in 65% isolated yield.

Procedure B for the synthesis of 1,3-diene (30)

In a 15 mL oven-dried pressure tube, 1-phenyl ethanol 3 (31 mg, 0.25 mmol, 1.0 equiv.), NiBr2 (5.4 mg, 0.025 mmol, 0.10 equiv.), 2,2’-bipyridine (4.7 mg, 0.030 mmol, 0.12 equiv.), NaOH (20 mg, 0.50 mmol, 2.0 equiv.), and 2-phenyl ethanol 1 (92 mg, 0.75 mmol, 3.0 equiv.) were dissolved in toluene (1.0 mL, 0.25 M) under a nitrogen atmosphere and heated at 150 °C in a pre-heated oil bath for 36 h in a closed system. The reaction mixture was cooled to room temperature and quenched with 2.0 mL of ethyl acetate and concentrated in vacuo. The residue was purified by column chromatography using a gradient of hexane and ethyl acetate (eluent system) to afford the 30 in 56% isolated yield.
