Executive Summary
GTI Energy, in partnership with University of Texas at Austin – Center for Electromechanics (UT-
CEM), Argonne National Laboratory (ANL), and Quantum Fuel Solutions (Quantum), successfully
designed, fabricated, and tested a linear free-piston hydrogen expander controlled by linear
motors which also generated electrical power during the process.

Pre-cooling of hydrogen to very low temperature is required for fast fueling of vehicles. This
pre-cooling is currently completed using conventional refrigeration technology at a high capital
and operating cost. The operating cost includes electricity use of 0.3kWh/kg dispensed. During
this project the team developed an expander to conduct this pre-cooling while generating
power. The expander uses the pressure differential between ground storage and the vehicle to
drive an electric generator, extracting energy from the high-pressure gas, causing it to cool as
low as -100°C. The specific expander technology selected for this task is a free-piston expander
driven by linear motors. The free-piston technology was chosen due to its high efficiency and
flexibility to operate over a wide range of expansion ratio.

In the first Budget Period of the project, the team validated the concept by operating a low-
pressure linear expander with 90psig nitrogen. As well as confirming the validity of the concept,
this testing introduced the team to the operating characteristics and thermodynamic losses of
linear expansion systems as well as experience programming the controls of the system. Budget
Period 1 also included preliminary design and economic analyses. Budget Period 2 included the
detailed design and fabrication of the expander system as well as testing of critical components,
most notably the expander inlet and outlet valves and the linear motor control system. The
valve testing showed valve durability and valve opening timing were acceptable, but the valve
closing time was about 3 times longer than required to achieve the fill time target. Two options
for addressing this issue were implemented. In Budget Period 3, the team commissioned and
tested the expander system. The first round of testing was conducted with nitrogen in order to
provide initial data at full pressure before switching to hydrogen. The hydrogen testing yielded
an efficiency of 68-73%, which is a reasonable efficiency for a first design iteration but short of
the 90%+ efficiency required to eliminate the need for a refrigeration unit.

The cost of the system was also determined to be comparable with refrigeration precoolers
currently used in hydrogen refueling stations. The key benefit to the cost of the expander is the
electricity generated by the expander providing a negative utility cost when compared to
refrigeration units that require electrical power to operate the refrigeration loop.

Future work is being pursued to continue the development of this technology. The team was
able to identify several design improvements to attain a much higher efficiency up near 90%
with relatively minor changes and investment. Additionally, more operating experience is
needed to get an accurate representation of the maintenance costs of the system, which will be
necessary prior to commercialization.

Background

Project Goal
The goal of this project was to simulate, design, fabricate, and test a free piston expander that
achieves the equivalent energy removal of the SAE J2601 T40 category over the course of a
light-duty hydrogen fueling event. This expander system is intended to replace the very costly
pre-cooling unit and flow control valve in a traditional refueling station, reducing the size and
pieces of equipment needed. The project targeted a reduction of the purchase and installation
costs by 60% compared to current pre-cooling unit costs, with the aim of reducing the total
station capital cost by 6%. A secondary goal of the expander was to eliminate the power
consumption of the pre-cooling unit and show a net power generation with the expander.
These effects will help achieve the US Department of Energy (DOE) goal of reducing the cost of
compression, storage, and delivery of hydrogen to cell vehicles to less than $2/kg.

Background
A main drawback to the use of hydrogen as a transportation fuel is the cost of building and
operating refueling stations. While gas compression is the main source of equipment and
energy cost, another major contributor is the pre-cooling of the hydrogen before dispensing to
a vehicle. This is currently done with a large block of aluminum (~1400kg) which is kept cold by
a large refrigeration system. It is estimated about 10% of the fueling station capital cost
originates from this cooling system (about $140,000 installed cost), with a levelized cost
between $0.50-$5.00/kg depending on station utilization (including electricity cost).

The hydrogen expander concept developed in this project can remove large amounts of energy
from the hydrogen gas as it undergoes the pressure reduction between ground storage and the
vehicle fuel tank, thus reducing or even eliminating the need for a pre-cooling unit. The project
targeted a 60% reduction in the installed capital cost compared to existing cooling systems, as
well as a significantly reduced electrical load of the system. This results in a potential reduction
in the levelized cost of hydrogen cooling from $0.50-$5.00/kg down to $0.20/kg, a significant
step toward $2/kg hydrogen.

Feasibility
Expanders are common process equipment, most notably used in large-scale cryogenic gas
separation plants. These expanders enable cryogenic distillation by cooling high-pressure gas
to extremely low temperatures by use of a near-isentropic pressure reduction (typically using
rotating turboexpanders).

A similar pressure reduction occurs during every hydrogen fueling event; however, the cooling
potential is not harnessed and the pressure reduction occurs without any change in enthalpy.
This isenthalpic pressure reduction results in the temperature increasing by up to 40°C, resulting
in even more demand on the pre-cooling unit to reduce the temperature to the required -33°C
to -40°C range for a T40 fill. The two pathways for pressure reduction are shown in the
pressure-enthalpy diagram in Figure 1 below. The isentropic pathway shows how using the

pressure differential to drive an expander (for this project driving an electric linear generator)
removes energy from the gas, resulting in a much lower temperature. Although the low
temperature of -155°C is not expected due to efficiency losses at the initial conditions, it is
technically possible (although these conditions will only exist for a short period of time). As the
pressure difference narrows during the fill, the lowest possible temperature will increase as well.

Although it is apparent that the initial temperature can be very low, the team needed to
demonstrate that enough energy would be removed from the gas to match a T40 fill as
designated by the SAE J2601 fueling protocol. Figure 2 below shows the outlet temperature for
a 94% fill at 20°C ambient conditions. This efficiency was selected because it matches the
energy removal from the gas that is seen for a T40 pre-cooling scenario over the course of the
entire fill. The plot shows that the initial outlet temperature is low, then increases throughout
the fill as the pressure difference between ground storage and the vehicle decreases.

Since we’ve shown the use of the expander as a hydrogen pre-cooler is feasible within the laws
of thermodynamics, the next step was to determine which available technology could meet the
required performance. The performance requirements can be summarized as follows:

 1. High efficiency over a range of pressure ratios (10:1 down to 1.1:1).
 2. Quick response to changes in operating conditions (highly dynamic pressure ratio).
 3. Non-lubricated system for operation with fuel-cell grade hydrogen.
 4. Low cost to be competitive with conventional chiller technology.

Most available expander technologies are not flexible enough to operate efficiently at small
scale and over the wide range of expansion ratio experienced during a fueling scenario.
Industrial cryogenic turbo-expanders can achieve >95% efficiency, but this performance
degrades as the expander size is reduced due to the leakage between the wall and rotor vanes
making up a larger fraction of the throughput. These industrial turbo-expanders are also
designed for optimum operation over a narrow range of expansion ratio. Operation over a wide
range of expansion ratio, as experienced in a hydrogen fueling station, would result in a high
variation in efficiency and would likely require a multi-stage turbine expander. Other rotating
expander technologies, such as screw and scroll expanders, suffer from similar limitations and
are therefore not considered for this application.

Unlike rotating expanders, reciprocating piston expanders can operate at much slower speeds
while maintaining high efficiencies. They can also scale down because leakage is prevented
using seals rather than solely relying on tight tolerances. However, traditional reciprocating
piston expanders have 3 drawbacks: 1) they are designed for operation at one expansion ratio,
2) they require oil lubrication, and 3) the additional moving parts, such as crankshaft, connecting
rods, etc. increase their cost and complexity. Free-piston expanders can harness the high
efficiency of piston expanders while mitigating the drawbacks. In a free-piston design, there is
only one major moving part in the expander, reducing the cost and complexity of the expander

 Page 4

and eliminating the need for oil lubricant. The variable expansion ratio can be addressed with
adjustable inlet valve timing, and although this is possible with traditional reciprocating
compressors, the advanced electronic controls of the linear motors for the free-piston design
offer key advantages in variable valve control. For these reasons, the free-piston expander was
selected for development of a hydrogen expander, with the most important performance metric
for this system being the high isentropic efficiency. Note that only the extraction of energy from
the gas, resulting in a low gas temperature, is required to eliminate the existing pre-cooling
equipment and electric load. Efficient utilization of that energy (e.g. in conversion to electricity)
is secondary as it can further reduce the station’s energy consumption but will not replace
existing equipment. Therefore, the project has focused on achieving expansion efficiency as
close to 100% as possible.

Free-piston linear expanders have been proven to operate within isentropic efficiency ranges of
55-93% [1,2], although these are sometimes reported as whole-system efficiencies (energy
produced divided by energy available). As mentioned above, the primary metric for success in
this application is the energy extracted from the high-pressure hydrogen, and not how
efficiently it is utilized. Preliminary calculations show the expander efficiency required to
eliminate the pre-cooling requirement completely is around 94% (at 21°C ambient temperature).
Figure 2 shows the hydrogen temperature after pressure reduction using the variable area
control device (isenthalpic pressure drop) vs. the proposed 94% efficient expander technology
and compares them to the J-2601 T40 maximum delivery temperature. Although with much
more variation in outlet temperature, the expander shows average energy removal equivalent to
the T40 cooling. This variation in outlet temperature will be levelized using a thermal buffer
(essentially a thermal mass from a metal or a phase change material).

Although the target efficiency of 94% is high, it is not out of reach, especially with hydrogen.
The three main causes of inefficiency in a free-piston expander are: 1) valve pressure drop, 2)
seal friction, and 3) heat migration. The initial gas flow through the valves, when open, is
isenthalpic in nature. This reduces the efficiency of the system but can be minimized by
maintaining a low pressure drop across the valves and minimizing the dead volume when the
expansion piston is at top dead center. The relative slow speed of the free-piston expander as
well as the control over the linear motors will both help to minimize the pressure drop losses.
Seal friction was also determined to be a major loss due to the high pressure ratios involved.
Materials were selected with the intent of minimizing the heat generated.

Lastly, heat migration to the expanding gas from the expander walls is technically another
potential inefficiency, but due to the need for a thermal buffer as discussed above, this is not a
large drawback during the fueling event. However, the heat gain from the surrounding
environment to the expander in between fueling events will result in small losses over time, but
these losses are expected to be significantly smaller than the thermal losses from existing cooler
systems due to the relatively smaller surface area and higher settled temperature.

Project Plan
This project was conducted with oversight from the U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) Hydrogen
and Fuel Cell Technology Office (HFTO). The scope of this project included the modeling,
design, fabrication, and testing of a hydrogen expander capable of replacing the traditional
hydrogen pre-cooling system and variable area control device which controls the vehicle
pressure ramp rate. Budget Period 1 included the preliminary design and economic analysis of
the expander application, as well as early-stage testing of a linear motor expander operating
with low pressure nitrogen. Budget Period 2 included preliminary valve testing, as well as
detailed design, procurement of parts, and fabrication of the expander test unit. Budget Period
3 included the final testing and economic modeling of the hydrogen expander.

GTI Energy, UT-CEM, ANL, and Quantum collaborated on this project. GTI was in charge of
project management, high-level design of the full system, detailed design of the mechanical
components and the balance of plant components (to represent a hydrogen fueling station).
UT-CEM led the electrical design, including the creation of a detailed simulation of the expander
electrical and thermodynamic details. UT-CEM also helped with mechanical design and some
component testing. ANL performed modeling of a hydrogen fueling station with an integrated
expander. Lastly, Quantum advised the design from a commercial perspective and provided
Type 4, 700bar hydrogen tanks for the project.

Budget Period 1: Preliminary Expander Design
The preliminary design included modeling, simulation, and down-selection of designs for the
overall expander system and several key components. These key components include the
expansion chamber, the inlet and outlet valves of the expander, and the motor used for power
generation and stroke control. The task also investigated other potential applications beyond
hydrogen as well as the economic and other benefits of the device. Lastly, the design and build
of a test apparatus to validate component performance was started. At the end of this task,
preliminary design solid models and simulations were completed, which allowed the detailed
design tasks to begin. Although not initially planned, the team had an opportunity to test a
linear motor-controlled free-piston expander with minor modifications from an existing unit.
This allowed validation of the ability of the commercial linear motors to act as generators.

Task 1: Preliminary Design and Analysis

Subtask 1.1 and 1.2: Expander Design Down-Selection
To analyze different aspects of the expander design and perform trade-off studies, a detailed
simulation was developed early in the project. The team created a thermodynamic simulation in
Matlab Simulink using real gas properties from National Institute of Standards and Technology
(NIST) database REFPROP. The simulation used a high-pressure gas supply to feed hydrogen
into two opposing expansion chambers through controlled inlet and outlet valves. A controlled
amount of gas first enters the expansion chamber at high pressure through the inlet valve, the
inlet valve then closes so the gas can expand as the piston increases the volume of the
expansion chamber (Figure 3). After the gas is fully expanded, the outlet valve is opened, and
the piston pushes the gas out of the expander. The gas then flows to a simulated vehicle,
entering an adiabatic target cylinder.

 Figure 3: Example of free-piston expander operation, starting at top dead center. The downward stroke allows gas into
the cylinder through V1, which closes at a position calculated from the pressure ratio. The gas then expands through the
 rest of the downward stroke, then V2 opens and the gas is pushed out on the upward stroke.

 Page 10

Once the simulation was operational, the team decided to force the efficiency as close to 100%
as possible to gain confidence in the simulation. There are three key losses in the expander
system: valve pressure drop, heat transfer, and seal friction. The team addressed the first by
initially making the valve flow path very large. The other two were calculations that could
effectively be made zero.

Initially, a fairly low value of 75% was observed. Valve timing was identified as a likely cause of
the low efficiency. The inlet valve must close at the right time to avoid allowing too much gas or
not enough gas into the chamber, resulting in under- or over-expansion, respectively. Under-
expansion results in not capturing all of the available energy, and therefore reduced efficiency.
Over-expansion results in underachieving on flow rate and a reduced efficiency from requiring
the re-compression of the gas. This inlet valve timing was described by a simple ratio of inlet to
outlet densities and corrected for valve actuation delays and the dead volume in the chamber.
The team also encountered an issue with the valve timing overlapping, so that both the inlet and
outlet valves were briefly open at the same time, allowing gas to simply flow through the
expander without any energy removal. This caused a severe drop in efficiency. Ultimately the
team was able to achieve a maximum simulated isentropic efficiency of about 99%, (“Friction
OFF” curve in Figure 4). The team believes this efficiency is sufficient to demonstrate that the
simulation and expander controls are working properly. The remaining 1 percent of losses are
likely a combination of valve pressure drop that is unavoidable in the simulation design and
some remaining valve timing issues that are nearly impossible to completely eliminate due to
the highly dynamic operation of the expander.

Figure 4: The lossless simulation of expander efficiency is compared to the simulation with friction added. The frictionless
 expander achieved an efficiency of about 99% while the addition of friction dropped the efficiency to about 94%.

Following the successful simulation of the nearly ideal expander, the team introduced realistic
friction, valve sizes, heat transfer and other real-world losses that are expected to impact the
operation of the expander when filling a vehicle. The team also integrated system dynamics such

 Page 11

as inertia and motor control forces. These variables were evaluated across a wide range of
dynamic operating conditions in order to optimize the design of the full-scale expander, and aid
in the selection of components such as linear motors, valves, and seals.

Using the results from the simulation, the team decided to move forward with a design that
used a stroke length of 500 mm and an expander piston diameter of 22 mm. The 500 mm stroke
length was selected based on results from an analysis that looked at the impact of valve timing
and speed on the performance of the expander. Based on the analysis performed, Figure 5, the
500 mm stroke length maintains relatively high levels of efficiency even with relatively slow
valves that require 50 milliseconds to open or close. At this point in the project, the team had
theorized that the valves would take some time between cracking open and achieving the fully-
open position. This is what is described as the ‘valve timing’ in Figure 5 below. Later in the
project, the team found that the valve actuation occurred very fast once it started, but had a
delay before actuation started, especially for the valve closing operation. Although the
mechanism is slightly different, the impact is the same: the expander system needed valve
actuation <50ms to be able to operate at well at high speeds with a 500mm stroke length.

 Figure 5: Simulation results are pictured showing the impact of valve timing and stroke length on the efficiency of the
expander. Faster valves perform better than slow valves. Increased stroke length can also improve the performance slow
 valve as they have more time to actuate.

Following the decision to operate using a 500 mm stroke length, the team focused on the
expander diameter. At a given stroke length the expander will operate at a fixed speed
regardless of the fluid end diameters, so the diameter directly impacts the flow through the
expander and the force exerted on the motors. The goal of the analysis was to maximize the
diameter to maximize the flow without exceeding the limits of the motors. The team ran a range
of cases using diameters from about 20 mm to 40 mm. These were compared to the motor
limits to determine the optimal fluid end size. Based on the analysis, the team found the 22 mm

The team further refined the simulation, looking closely at the controls and power management
during expansion to ensure the motors and drives can handle the electrical power requirements.
This analysis included the wall power supplying the motor controllers that rectify the AC supply
to DC. The DC power is supplied to the linear motors using insulated-gate bipolar transistors
(IGBT) and a proprietary control loop that controls the current and motion of the motors. The
detailed expander dynamics and motor controls are all simulated together to estimate the true
power draw or power generation of the system.

Results from the power analysis indicate that the expander will produce power. For the purposes
of this project, that power was rejected using two large power resistors attached to the motor
drives. Rejecting the power simplified the system design for testing; however, in the future the
power can be supplied to the compressor or returned to the grid. Figure 7 shows averaged
powers from the primary expander power systems. These include input power from the wall

to the power resistors. The wall power is zero for most of the fill, until the pressure differential
decreased below about 50bar. The net result of this power analysis is a predicted net power

The team also focused on the integration and simulation of a thermal buffer that will be
necessary to keep the expander’s discharge temperature above within the range of a T40 fill. At
the beginning of a fill, the large expansion ratio resulting from high supply pressures (~90MPa)
and a low vehicle pressure (~5MPa) can cause the gas exiting the expander to be extremely
cold. This cold gas could potentially damage components in the dispenser and/or vehicle. To
address this issue, the team integrated a thermal buffer that will transfer heat to the gas at the
beginning of the fill, keeping the gas above the -40°C limit for most components, and then
remove heat from the gas at the end of the fill when the gas expansion ratio has been reduced.
The thermal buffer essentially consists of a small block of steel or aluminum; an advanced
version of this design may include a calibrated phase change material that will add additional
thermal capacity to the thermal buffer while maintaining a smaller size.

The team developed a computational fluid dynamics (CFD) model of the thermal buffer. This
model was built in Solidworks and was used to better estimate the required thermal buffer mass
and heat transfer area. The CFD model was supplied with the mass flow and discharge
temperatures from the dynamic Matlab Simulink simulation. The heat transfer to the thermal
buffer was then calculated within Solidworks. Some results from the CFD analysis are shown in
Figure 8. Two designs were explored. The first was a short cylindrical design with multiple gas
passages drilled through the body to efficiently utilize the mass of the cylinder. The second
design was a single tube with a thick wall so only one pass is required. In Task 2, this design was
further refined, eventually resulting in a stainless-steel thermal buffer, measuring 5 feet long and
8” in diameter, weighing approximately 400kg.

Subtask 1.3: Preliminary expander design
During the preliminary development and setup of the simulation the team needed to make a
few assumptions about the design and operation of the expander. For starters, the team needed
to decide if the expander would operate independently or be mechanically linked to a
compressor to directly capture the expansion energy. During the proposal phase, the team was
leaning towards a mechanical integration of the two devices to improve efficiency; however,
after some additional analysis the team decided to keep the expander as a separate unit from
the station compressors so that they are only linked electrically.

This decision was made for the following reasons. 1) The complexity of a multi-stage
expander/compressor integrated into a single moving shaft introduces unnecessary design
complexity and risk into the project. 2) The team originally thought that the compressor force
would offset the expander forces, helping to balance the load on the linear motors being used
to control the system; however, preliminary simulation of the system indicates that the peak
forces likely will not overlap to offset each other in a combined system, resulting in higher motor
currents than if they were separated. 3) Mechanically linking the two devices forced them to
operate at the same frequency which resulted in imbalanced flow rates during a vehicle fill. This
is caused by the fact that an expander operating at a constant frequency will result in an
increasing flow as the outlet pressure increases. However, the compressor flow at a fixed
frequency remains nearly constant because the suction pressure is constant. These issues led the

 Page 15

team to make the decision to operate the expander independent of the station’s compression
capacity and use an electrical connection to utilize any power generated.

Following the decision to operate the expander independently, the team focused on the
preliminary design and layout of the expander fluid ends. The team considered a few expander
layouts that included single acting pistons, double acting pistons, and one-sided expanders with
a spring return, but settled on a single-acting piston design that includes one piston located at
each end of the motor frame.

The team advanced the design and layout of the linear motor frame and expander pistons. The
design included an oil-free, hermetically sealed motor housing located between the opposing
expansion chambers (Figure 9). The necessity to pressurize the large motor housing, along with
the requirement that the motor housing be a very flat surface for mounting the electrical motors
required structural and thermal analysis on the motor frame and bearings to ensure they will
operate properly across a range of operating temperatures and pressures from -40°C to 60°C
and up to 7barg. An example of the finite element analysis (FEA) results is shown in Figure 10.
The team conducted a system hazard analysis and then a detailed mechanical design review to
finalize the design of the assembly and ensure it could be operated safely.

The team was able to use an existing linear motor rig to test the control and performance of a

Subtask 1.5: Investigate other applications for linear expander
This task intended to create a wider base for the development of future linear expander systems
by investigating what other applications may exist and can be a realistic market. As with any
technology, increases in manufacturing volume will decrease cost. With the linear expander, in
order to understand the economic viability of the technology, it is also important to understand
where else the technology can be utilized. The following 3 areas have materialized as potential
separate avenues for application.

Compressed natural gas (CNG) dispensing

Similar to hydrogen vehicle fueling, natural gas vehicles (NGVs) suffer from issues related to
heat of compression in the tanks during fueling. Although the gas is not usually pre-cooled, the
result is very often that the vehicle tanks do not achieve a full fill. Vehicles regularly leave the
fueling station 15-25% underfilled, resulting in larger tanks required to provide the appropriate
vehicle range. The virtual pipeline industry also suffers from this underfilling. Virtual pipeline
trucks haul large tanks filled with CNG. Underfilling these tanks has a large impact on their
profitability. Some of these companies have installed CNG pre-cooling chillers, at a cost of $50-
$100 per scfm. These chillers, while only designed to pre-cool the gas to about 20°F, require a
large capital investment. GTI completed a project to create an expander for CNG pre-cooling.
That completed project showed the expander showed a positive power production and
operating at a 40% isentropic efficiency. The team is pursuing options for further improving the
efficiency to strengthen the value of this linear expander application.

 Page 22

Cryo-cooler applications

Expanders are commonly used to cool gas to very low temperatures (<100K). One large
industry application is for liquefaction of gases. Other applications include cooling of
electronics and magnets for everything from cellular towers to medical equipment such as MRI
machines. Figure 17 shows an overview of the different temperatures and duty requirements of
various cryocooler applications. Several different cryocoolers are already on the market, mostly
the Stirling, pulse-tube, and Gifford-McMahon types. These are mostly used at small scale due
to their ability to scale down.

The linear expander could be used in a Brayton cycle, where the gas is compressed and
expanded in separate devices with a heat exchanger in between. This is usually completed with
a turboexpander, which is inefficient and expensive at small scales. Therefore, as shown in
Figure 18, the ‘Turbo-Brayton’ cycle is only utilized at large scales. The linear expander could
change that with the ability to maintain high efficiency at low feed rates. Also, the system would
not have to be customized for different fluids or expansion ratios; the changes for these systems
could be made in the control electronics and the application chosen from a software menu. This
would lead to much less custom design than required for turbomachinery, and therefore a much
lower cost unit.

 Figure 17: Applications for cryogenic cooling [3]

 Page 23

 Figure 18: Overlay of preferred technologies for applications [3]

The market for cryocoolers was expected to increase at a 7% annualized rate through 2022 and
possibly further [4]. This growth is being fueled partially by increasing installation of
applications requiring cooling and a shortage in available helium gas typically used for cooling
in liquid form. While a Brayton-cycle cryocooler cannot supplant all applications, there are
potentially some mid-range applications where a linear expander Brayton cycle could provide
value over a Gifford-McMahon or pulse-tube cryocooler. More work needs to be done to select
a specific application and develop a market analysis, but this does show promise.

Power generation applications

The other main area for utilization of a linear expander is small-scale power generation
applications. Cycles for generation of power from low-grade heat, such as the Organic Rankine
Cycle (ORC), have garnered a lot of attention in the last few decades. One of the main costs
with these systems is the expander. Typically, turboexpanders are used in large-scale
applications, but at smaller scale the efficiency is too low. Also, turboexpanders require a lot of
design effort and are tailored for the specific fluid and pressure ratio desired. Lastly, small
turboexpanders require very high rotational speeds (>200krpm), resulting in difficulty selecting
bearings and alternators. Because of these factors, turboexpanders are not economically
favored for small-scale applications (< 20 kW) [5].

ORC systems in the 1-20 kW power range are of interest with applications particularly in vehicles
and small solar plants. Scroll and rotary vane expanders are the subjects of most attention.
However, there are drawbacks. Many of these expanders require lubrication and suffer from
efficiency losses due to complex fluid dynamics. A linear piston expander can avoid lubrication

 Page 24

and maintain a simple operation which boosts efficiency. The control electronics of the linear
expander allow it to be adjustable to optimize operation for many different fluids and pressure
ratio conditions. There are two areas where the linear expander needs further development to
determine its suitability for ORC applications:

 1) Power generation efficiency over range of conditions needs to be quantified.

 2) Cost needs to be better determined. For economic viability, the ORC system cost cannot
      exceed $3,500/kW [6].

While not available at the time this task was concluded, the team was later able to quantify the
power generation efficiency of the hydrogen expander. Although the instantaneous efficiency
was higher, the power generated from the entire fill process was about 40% of the available
energy. The team found several areas for improvement for the design and operation of the unit
that will significantly aid in an ORC application as well.

Additionally, the cost of the unit was defined better by the end of the project and found to be in
the range of $1,300-2,000/kW. This shows a promising path for ORC applications if the power
generation efficiency can be improved.

Subtasks 2.1 and 2.2: Component concept testing
As described above, the expander motor control system was fully tested in the first year of the
project. That testing demonstrated that the linear motor could not only be used to control the
expander pistons, but they would also produce some power that could be used to offset the
power required for compression. However, that system was built from existing equipment, so
the motors, valves, and sensors were controlled by different hardware manufacturers and
required two control units to operate the system.

To prepare for hydrogen expander testing, the team integrated all the expander control
hardware into one package. This used a real-time programmable logic controller (PLC). The PLC
not only directly interfaced with the motor drives but measured the hydrogen pressures and
temperatures and controlled the valve timing to efficiently expand the gas. In addition, the PLC
also handled all the safety and shutdown procedures for the test rig.

The team purchased one of the new motor drives that was used to integrate with the PLC and
control the motors being used to operate the expander. Testing started by working to integrate
the drive into the linear motor frame pictured in Figure 13. Motor files were programmed into

 Page 35

the PLC. This included the motors used on the nitrogen test stand (Figure 13) and the motors to
be used during hydrogen expander testing. These motor files included detailed motor
characteristics so that the drive accurately controls them during operation.

The team fully integrated the PLC system into the N2 expander that was used earlier in the
project. Figure 24 shows the motor drive and data acquisition system wired to the nitrogen
expander, pressure and temperature sensors, and the control valves. This demonstration was
critical because it showed that the new motor drives could control the selected motors during
expansion. This was also the first time all the hardware was integrated into a single PLC that
monitored and controlled the complete expander operation. After taking some time to learn the
PLC language and tune the motors, the team ran the N2 expander and reached similar
efficiencies that were achieved during preliminary testing. This rig was used to test and
demonstrate key safety and shutdown features that are critical for the hydrogen expander to
operate safely.

 Figure 14: The complete PLC control system is pictured for the low pressure N2 test expander.

The other component testing in this task was for inlet and outlet valves. The team acquired
commercial solenoid valves from Seitz, who manufactures commercial valves for hydrogen
dispensers. These valves were tested for their opening and closing time and durability, utilizing

 Page 36

the test setup described in Subtask 1.4 above. The valves were first leak tested using high
pressure gas and also tested for durability by actuating the valve at a high frequency for 10,000
cycles. More important to the performance of the expander, the valves were tested for their
opening and closing speed and consistency. Despite caution from the manufacturer, the
commercial valve showed potential to achieve the required performance. In opening tests, the
valve consistently opened rapidly in about 12-16ms as shown in Figure 25. This performance is
acceptable for the 500mm stroke length design based on the analysis pictured in Figure 5.
However, the valve closing time is a bit slower, requiring about 125ms to start closing and up to
150ms to fully close. This was about three times longer than the 50ms target.

Figure 25: Valve opening timing is plotted across a range of differential pressures. The time it takes for the valve to crack
 is consistently about 15ms.

 Figure 26: Valve closing time is plotted for a range of differential pressures. The time before the valve starts to close is
 consistent but the time to fully close varies depending on the pressure differential.

The slow closing time was found to be related more to the solenoid than the nature of the valve
design. The valves are pilot-actuated solenoids, so the solenoid only opens a very small internal
valve that allows gas behind the primary valve element to flow downstream, decreasing the
pressure differential across the primary element, causing it to open. When closing, the internal

 Page 37

pilot valve closes causing gas to build up pressure behind the primary element, causing it to
close. As it turns out, the process of moving the primary element only took a few milliseconds
once the pilot valve was opened, but there was a long delay between de-energizing the
solenoid and the pilot valve closing.

The team conducted additional testing on the valves to determine the cause of the delay. By
the use of a contact microphone, we could tell that the pilot element was closing right around
120ms after the signal current was shut off, signaling the valve to close. We were also able to
disassemble some of the solenoid circuitry and install a small current transmitter. The results of
this test are shown in Figure 27. The red line shows the applied voltage, which increases very
sharply when the power is applied. The current in green responds more slowly, but achieves a
current sufficient to open the pilot element at around 9ms after the voltage is applied (note that
the required current is less than half of the maximum current). Once voltage is removed, the
current trace drops sharply, but takes a long time to achieve a current low enough to allow the
pilot element to close (at 119ms).

  Figure 27: Solenoid timing test results with coil voltage (red), current (green) and contact microphone signal (black).

To improve the valve performance the team tested stronger springs that can overcome the
solenoid force more quickly than the stock valve spring (Figure 28). This initially worked to
reduce the closing time to 45ms (below the 50ms target), but the team would later find that this
prevented the valve from opening at all at the very high operating pressures (>10,000psig) that
the valve test rig wasn’t able to achieve. Luckily, in addition to the new spring, the team also
investigated modifications that can be made to the solenoid circuit to speed up the discharge
time. This included using DC-only solenoids (stock solenoids were AC/DC) which did not contain
a diode bridge and therefore allowed current to flow in either direction. This meant that, using
an advanced solenoid controller, the voltage across the solenoid could be reversed, forcing the
current to zero much more quickly.

 Page 38

  Figure 28: The valve pilot element is pictured with the spring that will be adjusted to hopefully improve the valve’s
 closing time.

We were able to do some bench testing with these advanced valve controllers in BP2. Using the
controller’s ability to control the valve current amplitude and direction the team was able to
significantly speed up the valve’s opening and closing time. The testing completed in Figure 27
was repeated with these controllers, and showed a closing time of 15ms was possible. This
testing did include the heavier springs, so the reduction was from 45ms to 15ms. Achieving
these fast-closing times were expected to improve the expander performance as the valves
more ideally opened and closed to optimize the thermodynamics of the expansion.

The project team also developed preliminary designs for custom valve options. This was
primarily pursued as a backup plan in case the commercial valves didn’t meet the required
performance criteria. Preliminary solid models of the valves were developed, and the team
integrated the characteristics of those solid models into a dynamic simulation of the valve
movement when opening and closing. Results from the simulation were then used to iteratively
improve the custom valve element designs.

The preliminary inlet and outlet valve designs are pictured in Figure 29. The inlet valve is on the
top of the image and the discharge valve is on the bottom. This design simplifies the valve
control and improves valve timing by directly controlling the valve with the piston. For example,
the inlet valve is designed to be pushed open using the piston, and then held open using a
direct actuating solenoid. This allows the valve to be directly opened even when there is a large
pressure differential acting on the valve element. The solenoid then simply needs to hold the
valve element until the proper amount of gas has been drawn into the expansion chamber. The
discharge valve opens using a spring that pushes the valve open at a target pressure differential
between the discharge and the expansion chamber. The discharge valve is closed using the
piston to push the valve directly when it reaches the end of the stroke. The discharge valve is

 Page 39

held closed as the inlet valve is opened. The high expansion chamber pressure then holds the
discharge valve closed until the gas has sufficiently expanded, allowing the discharge spring to
push the discharge valve open. Using this design, the discharge valve has a passive design and
does not require an active solenoid.

The team also evaluated the strength of the valve elements using various materials that include
virgin and embedded PEEK materials. Some preliminary results of the inlet valve are pictured in
Figure 30. These results show the valve stress at the start and end of the fill. Based on the FEA
results, the team believes the valves should last for over 1 million fills making the design
extremely durable.

The development of the custom valve design was put on hold once the team determined that
the commercial valves were capable of achieving the required performance. The main drawback
of the custom valve design was the resources that would have been required to fully develop
the design as well as the uncertainty of having an un-tested component in the system. This
design will ultimately be revisited after testing revealed that the commercial valves have some
unforeseen drawbacks.

Details on each of these parameters and how the expander meets these targets is given below.

Expander components designed for 30 g/s flowrates

The expander valves and fluid ends are sized for high flow rates, far above the 30 g/s required.
There is a limitation on the speed of the linear motors that may limit flow rate. The linear
motors have a maximum speed at a given power output. At the beginning of the fill when the
pressure drop is high, the speed is limited to 3.4 m/s. At a constant velocity, the maximum gas
mass flow rate possible changes during the fill. This maximum flow rate is lowest at the
beginning of a fill due to the lower gas density. This increases as the fill progresses. As this is a
moving target, the team found it more useful to consider the entire fill to determine if the fill
rate meets the targets of the J2601 average pressure ramp rate (APRR). The team simulated two
tank fill conditions, one starting at 21°C (APRR of 18.5 MPa/min) and 40°C (APRR of 11.5
MPa/min). The flow rates in each of these cases are shown in Figure 37 below. In both cases,
the expander is able to achieve the maximum required flow rate. Although the flow rates will be
higher at temperatures below 21°C, there are options to boost the flow rates when maximum
operational efficiency is not required. One option is to over-fill the expansion cylinder. This will
result in a partial expansion and lower efficiency, but higher flow rate. Options like this require
no additional equipment and will allow a wider range of operation.

 Figure 37: Mass flow rates for fill of a 129L tank at 21°C (left) and 40°C (right)

Expander components shown to meet performance required for > 70% efficiency

With the valve characteristics from testing included, the expander still operates at a high
isentropic efficiency on each stroke (> 90%). The efficiency losses are largely associated with the
thermal buffer unit. As the initial outlet temperature of the expander is less than -100°C, the gas
needs to be heated to avoid exposing dispenser components to very low temperatures. This
heating is done with a small thermal buffer mass. The result is a flattening of the temperature
profile across the fill, which causes the measured efficiency to be low at first and climb

 Page 46

throughout the fill as shown in Figure 38. The simulation does indicate the total fill efficiency is
85-90%, well above the 70% target.

 Figure 38: Isentropic efficiency of a fill at 40°C

The efficiency losses were quantified and split into the categories of frictional losses (heat
created in the system by seal friction from the piston rings) and pressure losses (loss due to
pressure drop through valves and dead volume in expansion cylinders). The losses are shown in
Figure 39 below. Thermal losses to the environment are expected in this operation but are
difficult to quantify as these losses depend on utilization. In conventional pre-cooling systems,
the unit is designed for a limited number of back-to-back fills, after which the aluminum block is
too warm to effectively cool the hydrogen and the fill must wait for the refrigeration unit to cool
the block. With the expander, back-to-back fills cause the smaller thermal buffer block to drop
lower and lower in temperature, which is beneficial. However, as the unit sits for a long time
without being used, the aluminum block will warm up to ambient. Simulating this thermal loss
is difficult to do accurately and it’s only a loss if the unit sits idle for a long time. These losses
will be quantified when the unit is operational.

Task 3: Commissioning, Testing, and Final Analysis

Subtask 3.1: Expander commissioning
The expander test unit underwent a detailed pre-startup safety review (PSSR) prior to operation
with nitrogen. This review would be repeated and updated prior to operation with hydrogen.
All safety systems were tested to verify operation. The unit was leak-checked and walked down
to confirm all components were in place and the enclosure was free of clutter and flammable
materials.

Once the PSSR was satisfied, the team began pressurizing the storage with nitrogen, verifying
pressure, flow, and temperature readings and checking any safety shutdowns that required
pressure to verify operation. Several issues with wiring and PLC coding were discovered, and all
were fixed prior to moving into the testing stage.

Subtask 3.2: Testing with nitrogen
Operation was successfully conducted with nitrogen at 1000psig, then ramped up to about
5000psig. The operation was slow, with the peak velocity of only 500mm/s (target with
hydrogen is 3000mm/s). This slow velocity was due to the higher molecular weight of nitrogen.
This heavier gas results in an expected ~3.5x increase in pressure drop for nitrogen over

 Page 51

hydrogen. In order to operate the unit with pressure drops similar to what would be expected
for hydrogen, the system had to be run much more slowly.

The team noticed right away that the piston seals were leaking much more than anticipated.
These seals are located at the end of the piston and are in direct contact with the highest-
pressure gas. The leakage was expected to be <1 scfm at 5000psig but was measured at well
over 10scfm. As mentioned previously, these seals are mostly used in compression applications,
where the heat from compression causes the seal to swell and fill most gaps. In expansion,
cooling occurs instead of heating, leaving more paths for leakage. Working with Dover, the
team got new seals fabricated and installed, leading to the plots in gray in Figure 46 below.
While this leakage is still larger than desired, it is a big improvement over the initial seals and
allowed the expander testing to proceed at a reasonable efficiency.

 Figure 46: Test data taken during nitrogen leak testing of the expander piston seals.

After the seal leaks were reduced, the team was able to operate with nitrogen at an increasing
supply pressure until we eventually achieved a 12,000psig (83MPa) supply of nitrogen. There
was initially an issue with the solenoid valves not opening at these pressures. We had replaced
the pilot springs with heavier versions to force the valve to close faster but didn’t realize that
would hurt the ability of the solenoid to open at higher pressures (12,000psig was not available
at the time of valve testing). This issue was resolved by switching back to the lighter springs.
The valve timing was slow, which was fine for nitrogen operation which was slow anyway. The
team decided the DC solenoids would eventually need to be installed with the advanced
solenoid controllers.

The isentropic efficiency for operation with nitrogen was low at around 30%. This was not
unexpected since the system was designed for the much lighter hydrogen gas. The specific
causes of the efficiency losses were the same as for hydrogen, but with a larger pressure drop

 Page 52

component. Rather than describing these losses in detail here, we will save the discussion for
the hydrogen testing section.

Following the first tests with nitrogen the team ran into more and more frequent encoder faults
which halted operation. These issues forced GTI to disassemble the expander motor which led to
the identification of a broken encoder wire. The encoder connections were redesigned and
routed outside of the motor housing to make future repairs easier. Following the repairs, the
team was able to reliably run the expander up to 12,000psig using nitrogen without further
issues. The expander speed was also increased to test that the system could be controlled,
although with very poor efficiency due to the heavy gas. GTI continued testing with nitrogen
until the expander reached full pressure and all the control and design attributes were tested
and verified. The expander was then switched to hydrogen.

The first step toward switching to hydrogen was to leak check the motor housing and ensure
that no hydrogen from the fluid ends could migrate into the motor housing, as even small
amounts of hydrogen could damage the magnets in the housing. This was done using helium.
The fluid ends were operated with high pressure helium with all nitrogen purges in place. The
team took gas samples from the motor housing and then tested those using a gas
chromatograph. This testing verified that no helium was leaking from the fluid ends into the
motor housing if the purges were active. With this test complete the team has officially
transitioned to hydrogen testing.

Subtask 3.3: Testing with Hydrogen
The expander was converted to hydrogen operation in early August 2023. Another pre-startup
safety review was completed to ensure all safety systems and procedures were updated for the
operation with hydrogen. This included the activation of continuous mechanical ventilation of
the enclosure. The first run with hydrogen was completed on August 15th with 2000psig of
supply pressure and 500psig in the target vessel. That supply pressure was increased in
subsequent runs up to 12,000psig (83MPa). Various improvements and modifications were
made over the course of the 10 weeks of testing to characterize and optimize the operation of
the unit.

Valve Modifications

Based on testing in 2020 and 2021, the team elected to use commercial solenoid valves to
control the inlet and outlet of the expander. The control of the expander, especially at higher
speed, requires fast actuation of both the inlet and outlet valves. The target valve actuation time
for this project was 50ms. The valve testing detailed under Subtasks 1.4 and 2.2 showed how
the commercial solenoid valves selected could achieve a closing time of 120-150ms, which
required improvement to operate at the full design speed of the unit. As described in Subtask
2.2, this issue could be addressed with DC-only solenoid coils and new control modules.

The team purchased more sophisticated valve control modules from Beckhoff (the PLC and
motor drive company used for this unit). These modules allow us to reverse the voltage on the
solenoid and drive the current to zero much faster than natural current decay. This also required

 Page 53

changing the valve solenoids. The stock solenoids were designed to operate with either AC or
DC power, which meant they included a diode bridge, which limited the voltage to being
applied in one direction across the solenoid. We were able to acquire additional DC-only
solenoids from the valve manufacturer that did not include the diode bridge and allowed the
voltage reversal. The end result is the valve timing plot in Figure 47 below. The initial spike in
current forces the valve to open (with the small bounce at 300mA indicating the pilot magnet
has moved). With the advanced controller, we are then able to reduce the current to a ‘holding
current’ value that’s enough to just barely keep the valve open. Then when it’s time to close the
valve, a negative voltage is applied, which drops the current to below zero within a few
milliseconds, much faster than the previous solenoids.

 Figure 47: Valve current data after solenoid and control changes, September 2023

The time between the pilot element closing and the main valve element closing is 20-30ms, so
the total valve closing time after modification was 30-40ms, under the time required to achieve
maximum operational rate.

Discharge Valve Leakage and Re-compression

Early in testing we discovered that the discharge valves would pop open briefly when the inlet
valve opened. This was due to rapid pressurization of the small volume at the end of the stroke
(top dead center) when the inlet valve opened. Unfortunately, this would allow hydrogen to
pass directly through the fluid end with no energy removal, reducing efficiency. To avoid this,
we started recompressing the gas in the cylinder by closing the discharge valve prior to the end
of the stroke. While this worked to eliminate the blow-through, we found that the valve had to
be closed much earlier than should have been necessary to compress the gas.

It turns out the valve doesn’t fully seal when it’s being recompressed. This is likely due to the
increasing pressure on the inlet side of the valve needing to bleed through the pilot orifice to
provide pressure to keep the valve element closed. If the inlet pressure is constantly increasing,

 Page 54

the orifice struggles to keep up and constantly bleeds out gas. This is shown in Figure 48 below
where the pressure starts to rise when the valve is closed, but the calculated mass remaining in
the cylinder continues to decline. Since this decline is still much greater than what we would
expect from piston seal leakage, we concluded the valve element is not completely sealing. This
causes efficiency losses that will be discussed later.

 Figure 48: In-cylinder mass and pressure during recompression at end of discharge stroke.

Thermodynamic Efficiency Calculations

We conducted several fills at various velocity and acceleration setpoints. The outlet temperature
of the two fluid ends for one of those tests is shown in Figure 50 below. The outlet temperature
dips to as low as -55°C on the west outlet. The difference between the west and the east
performance is mostly due to slight differences in leakage through the closed discharge valves
that was described above. The isentropic efficiency for this same fill test is shown in Figure 51.
The efficiency holds around 70% for most of the test, although the east side starts off a bit lower
and ends higher. This efficiency is important as it can be used to extrapolate these test results
to a simulation of a fill in a different scenario (such as a station with larger volumes of ground
storage). It should be noted that the fluid ends in this test were not pre-cooled and therefore
started at ambient temperature of 70°F. In tests where the fluid ends have been pre-cooled (to
simulate back-to-back fills) the efficiency increases to 77%.

 Figure 50: Inlet and discharge temperatures during fill test.

 Page 56

 Figure 51: Isentropic expansion efficiency.

Losses in thermodynamic efficiency fall into a few different categories:

 1) Friction (10-12%). The piston seals hold the pressure inside the fluid end against
 ambient pressure and are therefore exposed to huge pressure differentials. This pressure
 differential creates heat that is transferred directly into the gas or into the cylinder wall
 then inevitably into the gas. This may be improved, but only with a full redesign of the
 fluid end sealing system.

 2) Pressure drop losses (4-8%). There are losses when gas flows both in and out of the
 system. The inlet valve shows a 200psig pressure drop during intake stroke, which
 amounts to about a 2% loss. The leakage through the discharge valve is a larger
 contributor to overall losses, taking up around 2-6%, mostly due to the through-valve
 leakage described above.

 3) Re-compression losses (5-10%). The additional recompression at the end of the stroke
 creates heat which reduces system efficiency. This could be reduced with more effective
 discharge valves and/or reduction in dead volume at the end of the stroke.

 4) Heat transfer to the wall (3-10%). This is difficult to quantify due to large temperature
 gradients through the wall of the fluid end. We have a skin temperature measurement
 on the outside of the fluid ends, but that is a delayed indicator and the relatively low
 conductivity of 316SS widens the error band. We could improve the efficiency by
 shrinking the size of the fluid end, which is over-designed in this system due to excessive
 caution with the first design iteration.

Many of the inefficiencies improve with a larger diameter of the piston as the losses scale with
surface area not volume. Only the valve pressure drop losses would increase due to the higher
flow rate required through the same valve.

Motor Power Analysis

Analysis of the motor power showed that the motors were only being used to a maximum of
65% of their rated capacity. This means we could increase the diameter of the cylinder bore and

 Page 58

the piston which would increase flow capacity and efficiency of the system. Since we’re only
operating with a 12,000psig inlet and we should design for a maximum inlet of 13,500psig, the
current design would utilize 75% capacity at the beginning of the fill. Therefore, we could
increase the capacity by 33% to utilize the full capacity of the motors.

An analysis of the motor forces was also completed to ensure we understood all forces present
in the system. The result was a recalculation of friction coefficient to 0.04. Although static-
pressure testing was conducted that calculated a friction coefficient of 0.1 to 0.2, that doesn’t
appear to be the case during expansion cycling. The plot below shows the net expander force
matching up with the measured motor force, indicating that all the forces are balancing as they
should.

 Figure 54: Force balance showing motor force equal to calculated force from expander.

Motor and expander power data show the expander is net-producing power at a rate of
0.12kWh/kg. This compares with conventional pre-cooling duty power consumption of
0.3kWh/kg and the target for this project of producing power at a rate of 0.2kWh/kg. The
power generation would be improved by operating with an inlet pressure of 13,500psig as

The fueling procedure would follow this general process:

 1. Vehicle arrives, connects nozzle.
 2. Dispenser determines initial conditions and calculates initial average pressure ramp rate
 (APRR) based on the MC method in SAE J2601 fueling protocol. That APRR is transmitted
 to the expander control unit.
 3. The expander controller opens the first bank of hydrogen storage and begins operation.
 4. Pressure and temperature feedback from the dispenser is sent back to the expander to
 adjust flow rate. Adjustments to the APRR are continuously calculated in the dispenser
 controller and transmitted to the expander control unit.
 5. As banks are drawn down, the expander monitors the pressure differential available and
 switches supply banks when the pressure differential is below 1MPa.
 6. The dispenser terminates the fill when the hose pressure has achieved the target
 determined by J2601 or when another fill termination condition has occurred.
 7. The expander controls close the valve to the open hydrogen bank and moves to idle
 mode awaiting the next vehicle.

The initial project plan was to have a more detailed interface design completed with the
involvement of Quantum Fuel Solutions, who was a partner at the beginning of the project. At
the time of the start of the project in 2018, Quantum was developing a hydrogen dispenser. In
2021, Quantum had a change to business strategy and moved away from dispenser
manufacturing to focus on their core product, vehicle cylinders for hydrogen and compressed
natural gas. With this change, Quantum decided to terminate their involvement in the expander
project. Without participation from a dispenser manufacturer, the details of the expander-
station interface cannot be fully defined, and therefore are left undetermined here.

Another shift has occurred in the 5 years between the start and end of this expander project
(which also included the COVID pandemic). With the increased adoption of light-duty electric
vehicles, the focus for hydrogen vehicles shifted to heavy-duty and off-road vehicles. The
expander is even more ideally suited for this increased scale and slightly higher fueling
temperature requirement for heavy duty vehicles and is very well-suited for mobile refueling
applications. Because of these shifts in the market, the design detail for integration of the
expander with a light duty fueling station was determined to not be a productive use of project
resources.

Conclusions
GTI Energy, in partnership with UT-CEM, ANL, and Quantum Fuel Solutions, successfully
designed, fabricated, and tested a linear free-piston hydrogen expander controlled by linear
motors which also generated electrical power during the process.

In the first Budget Period of the project, the team validated the concept by operating a low-
pressure linear expander with 90psig nitrogen. As well as confirming the validity of the concept,
this testing introduced the team to the operating characteristics and thermodynamic losses of
linear expansion systems as well as experience programming the controls of the system. Budget

 Page 64

Period 1 also included preliminary design and economic analyses that provided assurance that
the concept was worthy of moving into Budget Period 2, which officially began on January 1,
2020.

Budget Period 2 included the detailed design and fabrication of the expander system as well as
testing of critical components, most notably the expander inlet and outlet valves and the linear
motor control system. The valve testing showed valve durability and valve opening timing were
acceptable, but the valve closing time was about 3 times longer than required to achieve the fill
time target. Two options for addressing this issue were identified, a stronger spring and
advanced solenoid controls with a DC-only solenoid. Both of these options would be found
useful during testing in Budget Period 3, which officially began July 1, 2021.

In Budget Period 3, the team commissioned and tested the expander system. The first round of
testing was conducted with nitrogen in order to provide initial data at a range of pressures
without the flammability hazard of hydrogen. The nitrogen testing was conducted at pressures
up to 12,000psig (83MPa), but with limited speed and poor isentropic efficiency (30%) due to
the high molecular weight of nitrogen. The hydrogen testing yielded an efficiency of 68-73%,
which is a reasonable efficiency for a first design iteration but short of the 90%+ efficiency
needed to completely eliminate the need for a refrigeration unit. The team was able to identify
several design improvements to attain a much higher efficiency up near 90%.

The cost of the system was also determined to be comparable with refrigeration precoolers
currently used in hydrogen refueling stations. The key benefit to the cost of the expander is the
electricity generated by the expander actually providing a negative utility cost when compared
to refrigeration units that require electrical power to operate the refrigeration loop. More
operating experience is needed to get an accurate representation of the maintenance costs of
the system.

Path Forward
The project team is planning on continuing the development of the expander, with an emphasis
on heavy duty applications as this has become the focus of the hydrogen refueling industry.
The team is also developing linear expanders for other applications that will advance the
technology and potentially reduce component costs. The detailed path forward as it pertains to
the design of the expander is documented below for future work.

Expander Modifications to Improve Performance

This project has allowed extensive development of the linear expander design and validation of
the ability to control the system with linear motors. While the test results have been promising,
some improvement is needed to maximize the value of the system to fueling stations. With the
efficiency analysis above, the team identified some potential improvements that can be made in
the next iteration of the design. This section details those improvements and the expected
performance benefit.

 Page 65

Step 1: Discharge valve modification

Expected Benefit: Thermodynamic efficiency is expected to increase by 8-10%. This is partly
due to eliminating the need to recompress the gas at the end of the discharge stroke. That
recompression is costing 5-15% more energy than if there was no recompression. The current
valves require recompression because they are designed with a pilot that causes the valve to
briefly blow open when hit with pressure from the inlet. However, even with recompression, the
discharge valve tends to leak or chatter during the recompression stroke. This recompression
and leakage impact the flow, friction, expander thermal efficiency, and the power production
efficiency. All of these issues can be addressed by simplifying the discharge valve design and
eliminating the pilot feature.

The re-designed discharge valve will fit within the end plug of the current fluid ends. The valve
will open using a simple spring when the expansion chamber drops low enough in pressure
relative to the discharge pressure. The gas will then be discharged until the piston pushes the
valve closed (similar to the custom valve design discussed in Subtask 2.2 above). This would
almost eliminate dead volume and remove the need to recompress the gas, creating huge
benefit with relatively little effort.

Figure 57: In step 1, the plug at the end of the cylinder can be replaced with a custom valve. The team believes this valve
 can be designed to open into the cylinder when the in-cylinder pressure is within a few hundred psi of the discharge
 pressure. The piston can then be used to tap this valve shut after the discharge stroke is complete and hold the
 discharge valve shut as the inlet opens to prevent blow through and eliminate the need for recompression.

Step 2: Seal system modification (includes the valve modification in step 1)

Expected Benefit: Increased thermodynamic efficiency by 6-8%, for a total efficiency
improvement of 14-16%. Currently, the expander piston seals hold the full 12,000 psi of
expanding hydrogen back from nearly atmospheric pressure. The rod seals, highlighted in blue
in Figure 59, hold a small amount of pressure, but were mostly designed to prevent hydrogen
from entering the motor housing. The high pressure drop across the piston seals pushes those
seals into the expander wall, resulting in significant friction heat going into the wall of the
expander, and then directly into the gas. The issue worsens toward the end of the fill as the
average pressure in the expander increases to the final fill pressure (blue curve in Figure 58).

The friction can be reduced by replacing the rod seals with a design that can seal the full system
pressure and allow the space between the rod and piston seals to float on the vehicle pressure.

 Page 66

With this design, the pressure drop across the piston seals will be reduced and will decrease
further over the course of the fill. The rod seals will see a higher-pressure drop and more
friction, but since those are in a separate section of the fluid end, they are thermally insulated
from the gas and will have a reduced heat transfer to the gas. The expected reduced friction
power is shown with the orange line in Figure 58.

 Figure 58: Friction power profiles for the current design and potential redesign. Integration under each plot gives total

  This design is common in high-pressure cryogenic pumps to minimize heat that enters the cryogenic fluid and should

Step 3: Increase piston diameter to improve flow rate and motor utilization

Expected Benefit: 30% increase in gas flow. Friction, leakage, and heat transfer reduced by
12%. Based on testing, we underutilized our motor capacity and could increase the piston
cross-sectional area by 33%. Because seal friction, leakage, and wall heat transfer scale by bore
circumference and not area, these are expected to all decrease by 12% relative to flow. Overall,
the impact on efficiency is small, totaling a 2-3% increase. The primary benefit of this
modification is the increased flow rate.

Mechanical design simplification and cost reduction

In addition to the effort to improving the thermodynamic performance of the expander, the
mechanical design is also a target for improvement in order to lower the cost and the footprint
of the system. The design shown in Figure 44 above will reduce the footprint of the system by
arranging it vertically, and well as reduce the cost by locating the motors in a large pipe. One of
the largest costs of the test system was the large rectangular custom-machined motor housing,

with a design pressure over 100psig. Locating everything inside a large pipe will enable the use
