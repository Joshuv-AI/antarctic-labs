On invariant subrings of Orlik–Solomon and Varchenko–Gel’fand algebras in type A
Trevor Karn

Abstract
. We provide simple presentations in terms of generators and relations for the invariant subring of both the Orlik–Solomon algebra and Varchenko–Gel’fand ring of the type A n A_{n} reflection arrangement acted upon by the type A n − 1 A_{n-1} reflection group. This may be interpreted as a presentation for the cohomology of the “mixed configuration space” of n n red points and one blue point. We provide increasingly refined descriptions of the invariant ring starting with the total dimension and ending with the simple presentation in terms of generators and relations.



1.
Introduction

The primary goal of this paper is to provide a simple presentation for a certain ring in terms of its generators and relations.
For any ring
R
carrying the action of a group
G
, the
G
-invariant subring of
R
is

\displaystyle R^{G}:=\{x\in R:g\cdot x=x\;\forall g\in G\}.

The two fundamental questions of invariant theory are (i) determine a generating set for
R^{G}
and (ii) determine relations among those generators.
Our main results,
Theorems

1
and
2
, answer these two fundamental questions for specific choices of
R
and
G
that arise in the study of hyperplane arrangements.

Classical invariant theory focuses on the case where
R
is a polynomial ring.
The most well studied groups which act on the polynomial ring are reductive algebraic groups and reflection groups.
Theorems of Chevalley and Shephard–Todd (see
[
11
, Chapter 3]
) give a particularly elegant answer to these questions when
G
is a reflection group, such as the symmetric group
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
.
Whereas classical invariant theory starts with a group acting on a polynomial ring, we will instead start with a group acting on the Orlik–Solomon algebra
\mathsf{OS}(\mathcal{A})
and the Varchenko–Gel’fand ring
\mathsf{VG}(\mathcal{A})
.
For the particular arrangement
\mathcal{A}_{n}
that is the focus of this paper,
\mathsf{OS}(\mathcal{A}_{n})
and
\mathsf{VG}(\mathcal{A}_{n})
are defined below.

When a hyperplane arrangement
\mathcal{A}
has the symmetry of a group
G
, both
\mathsf{VG}(\mathcal{A})
and
\mathsf{OS}(\mathcal{A})
“see” this symmetry and thus carry
G
representations.
The prototypical example of a hyperplane arrangement with the symmetry of a finite group is the rank-
n
braid arrangement

\mathcal{A}_{n}:=\{h_{ij}:1\leq i<j\leq n+1\},

where
h_{ij}\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
is the hyperplane defined by
z_{i}=z_{j}
.
This is the reflection arrangement associated to the symmetric group
\mathfrak{S}_{n+1}
, which is the type
A_{n}
Coxeter group.
The symmetric group
\mathfrak{S}_{n+1}
acts on
\mathcal{A}_{n}
by permuting the coordinates of
\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
.
This action restricts to
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
by fixing the last coordinate and only permuting the first
n
coordinates.

The two main results of this paper are presentations, in terms of generators and relations, for the rings
\mathsf{OS}(\mathcal{A}_{n})^{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}
and
\mathsf{VG}(\mathcal{A}_{n})^{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}
.
We write
\mathsf{VG}_{n}:=\mathsf{VG}(\mathcal{A}_{n})
and
\mathsf{OS}_{n}:=\mathsf{OS}(\mathcal{A}_{n})
.
We will also write the invariants
\overline{\mathsf{VG}}_{n}:=\mathsf{VG}_{n}^{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}
and
\overline{\mathsf{OS}}_{n}:=\mathsf{OS}_{n}^{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}.

Our first theorem answers the two fundamental questions of invariant theory for
\overline{\mathsf{VG}}_{n}
.

Theorem 1
.

For
n\geq 2
,
\overline{\mathsf{VG}}_{n}\cong\mathbb{Q}[z]/\langle z^{n+1}\rangle
.

\overline{\mathsf{OS}}_{n}
is slightly more subtle.
Consider the free associative algebra
\mathbb{Q}\langle A\rangle
on an alphabet
A
.
Endow the alphabet
A
with a degree function
|\cdot|:A\to\mathbb{Z}
.
The
free graded-commutative algebra

\mathbb{Q}\{A\}
is the quotient of
\mathbb{Q}\langle A\rangle
by the two-sided ideal
\langle ab-(-1)^{|a||b|}ba:a,b\in A\rangle
.

Example 1
.

Let
A=\{\alpha,\mu,\gamma\}
with degree function
|\alpha|=|\mu|=1
and
|\gamma|=2
.
Then in
\mathbb{Q}\{A\}
, the following identities hold:

•

\alpha^{2}=\mu^{2}=0
,

•

\gamma^{d}\neq 0
for all
d\geq 0
,

•

\alpha\mu=-\mu\alpha
,

•

\alpha\gamma=\gamma\alpha
and
\mu\gamma=\gamma\mu
.

\mathbb{Q}\{\alpha,\mu,\gamma\}
can be described as the tensor product of an exterior algebra with a univariate polynomial ring, that is
\mathbb{Q}\{\alpha,\mu,\gamma\}\cong\wedge\{\alpha,\mu\}\,\otimes\,\mathbb{Q}[\gamma]
.
The isomorphism is one of
graded
algebras when the polynomial generator has degree
2
.
The monomials of the form
\alpha^{\epsilon}\mu^{\delta}\gamma^{d}
are a
\mathbb{Q}
-basis for
\mathbb{Q}\{A\}
when
\epsilon,\delta\in\{0,1\}
and
d\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
.

Definition 2
.

Let
p=\lfloor\frac{n+1}{2}\rfloor
and define the following two-sided ideal of
\mathbb{Q}\{\alpha,\mu,\gamma\}
:

I_{n}=\begin{cases}\langle\gamma^{p}\rangle&\text{if $n$ is even}\\
\langle\gamma^{p}\;,\;\alpha\gamma^{p-1}-(p-1)\mu\gamma^{p-1}\rangle&\text{if $n$ is odd}\end{cases}

Our second main theorem, whose proof makes up the bulk of this paper, answers the two fundamental questions of invariant theory for
\overline{\mathsf{OS}}_{n}
.

Theorem 2
.

For
n\geq 2
,

\overline{\mathsf{OS}}_{n}\cong\mathbb{Q}\{\alpha,\mu,\gamma\}/I_{n}
.

Graded-commutative algebras often arise naturally in topology, especially as cohomology rings.
As we will explain below,
\overline{\mathsf{OS}}_{n}
and
\overline{\mathsf{VG}}_{n}
have interpretations as cohomology rings.
Thus, one should not be surprised that
\overline{\mathsf{OS}}_{n}
may be presented as a quotient of a free graded-commutative algebra.
Orlik and Solomon
[
15
]
showed that the topology of the complement of a hyperplane arrangement and the combinatorics of hyperplane intersections are deeply related.

In the case of
M_{2}(\mathcal{A}_{n}):=\mathbb{C}^{n+1}-\mathcal{A}_{n}
, the complement of the arrangement consists of points in
\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
where no two coordinates are the same.
Regarding each complex coordinate
z_{i}
as a point in the complex plane
\mathbb{C}\cong\mathbb{R}^{2}
, this is the same as saying that
M_{2}(\mathcal{A}_{n})
is the
ordered configuration space of points in the plane
.
The fundamental group of
M_{2}(\mathcal{A}_{n})
is well known to be the braid group, giving the braid arrangement its name.
A related topological space is the
unordered configuration space of indistinguishable points in the plane
.
Cohen
[
3
, Part 3]
described the homology (and the corresponding induced
\mathfrak{S}_{n+1}
action) of the unordered configuration space as a Gerstenhaber algebra.
The unordered configuration space is formed by identifying points in the in the same orbit under the natural
\mathfrak{S}_{n+1}
action on the ordered configuration space
M_{2}(\mathcal{A}_{n})
; in other words, the unordered configuration space is the orbit space
M_{2}(\mathcal{A}_{n})/\mathfrak{S}_{n+1}
.

There is also a
mixed configuration space
that lives between the ordered and unordered configuration spaces.
A further configuration space to consider is that of
n+1
points in the plane where the first
n
points are indistinguishable from each other and the last point is distinguishable from any of the first
n
points.
To simplify this we will call the first
n
points “red points” and the last point a “blue point.” Thus, there is a mixed configuration space consisting of configurations of
n
red points and one blue point.
As an orbit space, it is
M_{2}(\mathcal{A}_{n})/(\mathfrak{S}_{n}\times\mathfrak{S}_{1})
.
The study of orbit spaces naturally connects algebraic topology to invariant theory.
The connection comes through the isomorphism
H^{\bullet}(X/G;\mathbb{Q})\cong H^{\bullet}(X;\mathbb{Q})^{G}
when
G
acts freely on
X
(see for example
[
8
, Section 3.G]
for further details).
Thus, one may regard
Theorem

2
as a presentation for the cohomology of the configuration space of
n
red points and
1
blue point.

In the past decade, attention has turned to look at the relationship between subspace arrangement complements in Euclidean spaces and combinatorics.
This has led to study of the Varchenko–Gel’fand ring
\mathsf{VG}(\mathcal{A})
.
When a subspace arrangement in
\mathbb{R}^{dn}
comes as the thickening
\mathcal{A}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{d}
of a real hyperplane arrangement
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, Moseley showed that (up to a linear shift of grading) the cohomology of a subspace arrangement complement is either an Orlik–Solomon algebra or a Varchenko–Gel’fand ring.
Moseley’s work inspires the following philosophy: given a nice result about either the Orlik–Solomon algebra or the Varchenko–Gel’fand ring, one hopes there is a similar nice result for the other.
This philosophy was the motivation for investigating
Theorem

1
.

As mentioned above, the complement of the braid arrangement is the unordered configuration space of points in the plane.
But it is also just one example of a reflection group acting freely on the complement of reflecting hyperplanes.
More generally, reflection groups
W
provide a rich collection of examples where a group acts freely on the complement of a hyperplane arrangement.
Douglass, Pfeiffer, and Röhrle
[
4
,
5
]
have systematically studied the corresponding
W
-representations of
\mathsf{OS}(\mathcal{A}_{W})
.
With an eye towards invariant theory, one is first led to wonder about the structure of the invariant rings
\mathsf{OS}(\mathcal{A}_{W})^{W}
and
\mathsf{VG}(\mathcal{A}_{W})^{W}
.
One of the important classes of subgroups of reflection groups are the parabolic subgroups
P\leq W
.
In the context of our main results, one wonders if there is a general story to be told about the invariant rings
\mathsf{OS}(\mathcal{A}_{W})^{P}
and
\mathsf{VG}(\mathcal{A}_{W})^{P}
.
Further investigation of this story is left for future work.

2.
Background

There is a famous linear basis for
\mathsf{OS}(\mathcal{A})
of any arrangement known as the NBC basis.

Definition 3
.

Fix an order on the hyperplanes
h_{1}<h_{2}<\cdots<h_{k}
.
A
circuit
is a set of hyperplanes
C=\{h_{i}\}
such that
\codim\cap_{h\in C}h<|C|
and
\codim\cap_{h\in C-h^{\prime}}h=|C-h^{\prime}|
for any
h^{\prime}\in C
.
In other words, a circuit is a minimally dependent set of hyperplanes.
A set
S
is called a
broken circuit set
if there is a hyperplane
h\in\mathcal{A}
such that
\{h\}\cup S
is a circuit and
h<h_{i}
for all
h_{i}\in S
.
A
no broken circuit (NBC) set
is a set which does not contain any broken circuit as a subset.
Generators of
\mathsf{OS}(\mathcal{A})
(respectively
\mathsf{VG}(\mathcal{A})
) are indexed by hyperplanes, and so the image of a monomial in
\mathsf{OS}(\mathcal{A})
(respectively
\mathsf{VG}(\mathcal{A})
) corresponds to a set of hyperplanes.
If the corresponding index set of hyperplanes is an NBC set, we say that the monomial is an
NBC monomial
.

Remark 4
.

We will often abuse notation and refer to monomials in
\mathsf{OS}(\mathcal{A})
or
\mathsf{VG}(\mathcal{A})
.
This is taken to mean the images of monomials in
\mathsf{OS}(\mathcal{A})
and
\mathsf{VG}(\mathcal{A})
, which are defined as quotients.

For any fixed order on the hyperplanes of
\mathcal{A}
, the NBC monomials form a basis for
\mathsf{OS}(\mathcal{A})
and
\mathsf{VG}(\mathcal{A})
. Indeed as graded vector spaces we have
\mathsf{OS}(\mathcal{A})\cong\mathsf{VG}(\mathcal{A})
, but the isomorphism is emphatically not one of rings or of representations.
In the special case of the braid arrangement
\mathcal{A}_{n}

Experts will be well aware of how much more generally Frobenius reciprocity may be stated.

The irreducible representations of

This association is an isometry of inner product spaces between the representation ring, for which irreducible representations form an orthonormal basis, and the ring of symmetric functions, for which

Having found a nonzero degree-

Lemma 9
.

Let

Recall from

\mathcal{A}_{n}
by handfuls with no two fingers on the same hand.

Then

We are now ready to prove

.

Proof (of

Example

4.
The Hilbert-Poincaré polynomial of Orlik–Solomon invariants

The story for the Orlik–Solomon algebra is more subtle.
There is a presentation for
\mathsf{OS}_{n}
that is similar to
Equation

6
, originally due to Arnol’d
[
1
]
:

(7)

\mathsf{OS}_{n}\cong\wedge\{e_{ij}\}_{1\leq i\neq j\leq n+1}/\langle e_{ij}-e_{ji},\,e_{ik}e_{jk}-e_{ij}e_{jk}+e_{ij}e_{ik}\rangle.

The relation
e_{ij}^{2}=0
in the exterior algebra mirrors the
x_{ij}^{2}=0
relation in
\mathsf{VG}_{n}
.

In their landmark paper
[
15
]
, Orlik and Solomon were able to determine that the graded
G
representation structure of
\mathsf{OS}(\mathcal{A})
for any arrangement
\mathcal{A}
where
G
acts by permuting hyperplanes depends only on the lattice of flats
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{A})
.

Lemma 10

(
[
15
, Cor. 5.6]
)
.

Let
WH_{\bullet}(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{A}))
denote the Whitney homology of the lattice of flats
L(\mathcal{A})
of the arrangement
\mathcal{A}
.
Then

\mathsf{OS}(\mathcal{A})\cong WH_{\bullet}(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{A}))

as graded representations.

Using the combinatorics of
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{A})
to compute the representation structure allows for a better understanding of the structure of
\mathsf{OS}(\mathcal{A})
.
As one example of this, Sundaram
[
19
]
proved the following equivariant recursion for a finite poset
P
that is Cohen–Macaulay.
Recall this means that
P
is ranked and that the reduced homology of the order complex
\Delta(x,y)
of the open interval
(x,y)
vanishes below the top degree
\rk(y)-\rk(x)-2
.

Lemma 11
.

[
19
, Prop. 1.9]

Let
P
be a Cohen–Macaulay poset of rank
n
and let

P(r)=\{x\in P:\rk x\leq r\}.

Then, for
1\leq r\leq n-1
there is an isomorphism of representations

\tilde{H}(P(r))\oplus\tilde{H}(P(r-1))\cong WH_{r}(P),

when taking the convention that
\tilde{H}(P(0))=WH_{0}(P)
and
\tilde{H}P(r))=0
for
r<0
and
r>n-1
.

Without precisely defining what it means for a poset to be Cohen–Macaulay, we will say that the set partition lattice
\Pi_{n+1}
is a prototypical example of a Cohen–Macaulay poset.
This is a particularly important example because of the well known fact that
\Pi_{n+1}
is isomorphic to the lattice of flats
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{A}_{n})
of the braid arrangement.
Let
\mathsf{OS}_{n,d}
and
\overline{\mathsf{OS}}_{n,d}
denote the degree-
d
graded components of
\mathsf{OS}_{n}
and
\overline{\mathsf{OS}}_{n}
respectively.
Sundaram’s result implies the following corollary.

Corollary 12
.

Let
V_{n+1}(d)=\tilde{H}_{d}(\Pi_{n+1})
.
Then, as graded representations,

\mathsf{OS}_{n,d}\cong WH_{d}(\Pi_{n+1})\cong V_{n+1}(d)\oplus V_{n+1}(d-1).

Sundaram
[
19
]
was also able to determine the multiplicity of some irreducible
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
representations inside of the homology
\tilde{H}_{\bullet}(\Pi_{n}(d))
.

Lemma 13
.

[
19
, Cor. 2.3]

Let
n\geq 3
and
0\leq r\leq n-2
and let
V_{n}(r)=\tilde{H}(\Pi_{n}(r))
.
Then

(1)

\displaystyle\langle V_{n}(r),V^{n}\rangle=0

(2)

\displaystyle\langle V_{n}(r),V^{n-1,1}\rangle=1

where
V^{\lambda}
is the irreducible representation of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
such that
\Frob(V^{\lambda})=s_{\lambda}
.

In
Example

8
, we computed
\dim\overline{\mathsf{OS}}_{n}=2n.

We refine that computation into the Hilbert series of
\overline{\mathsf{OS}}_{n}
.

Proposition 14
.

For
n\geq 2
,

\Hilb(\overline{\mathsf{OS}}_{n};t)=(1+t)(1+t+t^{2}+\cdots+t^{n-1}).

Proof.

Recall that
\mathsf{OS}_{n,d}
is naturally thought of as an
\mathfrak{S}_{n+1}
representation.
By Frobenius reciprocity,

\displaystyle\dim{\overline{\mathsf{OS}}_{n,d}}

\displaystyle=\langle\mathsf{OS}_{n,d}\downarrow_{\mathfrak{S}_{n}},\mathds{1}_{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\rangle_{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}

\displaystyle=\langle\mathsf{OS}_{n,d},\mathds{1}_{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\uparrow^{\mathfrak{S}_{n+1}}\rangle_{\mathfrak{S}_{n+1}}

\displaystyle=\langle\mathsf{OS}_{n,d},V^{n+1}\oplus V^{n,1}\rangle_{\mathfrak{S}_{n+1}}.

By
Corollaries

12
and
13

\displaystyle\langle\mathsf{OS}_{n,d},V^{n+1}\oplus V^{n,1}\rangle_{\mathfrak{S}_{n+1}}

\displaystyle=\langle V_{n+1}(d)\oplus V_{n+1}(d-1),V^{n+1}\oplus V^{n,1}\rangle_{\mathfrak{S}_{n+1}}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}2&1\leq d\leq n-1\\
1&d=0,n\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}\qed

First we show linear independence in small degrees.

Lemma 20

(1)

For

(2)

For

When

When

\displaystyle 0=k_{1}am+k_{2}c

\displaystyle=k_{1}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\sum_{1\leq k\leq n}e_{ij}e_{k,n+1}

\displaystyle\quad+k_{2}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\left(e_{ij}e_{i,n+1}+e_{ij}e_{j,n+1}\right)

\displaystyle=(k_{1}+k_{2})\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\{i,j\}\end{subarray}}e_{ij}e_{k,n+1}

Since the NBC monomials form a basis

∎

The technical tool which we will use to proof

\partial(xy)=\partial(x)y+(-1)^{|x|}x\partial(y)

The following identities will allow us to use this fact to prove the linear independence of

Lemma 21
.

(1)

\partial(a)=\binom{n}{2}

(2)

\partial(m)=n

(3)

\partial(am)=-na+\binom{n}{2}m

(4)

\partial(c^{d})=-2dac^{d-1}+d(n-1)mc^{d-1}

(5)

\partial(amc^{d})=-nac^{d}+\binom{n}{2}mc^{d}

(6)

\partial(g)=\begin{cases}0&n~\text{even}\\
a-(p-1)m&n~\text{odd}\end{cases}

Proof.

Since
\partial(e_{ij})=1
, we see that
\partial(a)
(respectively
\partial(m)
) is an integer counting the number of monomials in

(respectively

) which is
\binom{n}{2}
(respectively

Then

\displaystyle\partial(am)

\displaystyle=\partial(a)m-a\partial(m)

\displaystyle=\binom{n}{2}m-na

The Leibniz rule implies the power rule that
\partial(c^{d})=dc^{d-1}\partial(c)
.
Thus, we compute

\displaystyle\partial(c)

\displaystyle=\partial\left(\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}e_{ij}e_{i,n+1}+e_{ij}e_{j,n+1}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}e_{i,n+1}-e_{ij}+e_{j,n+1}-e_{ij}

\displaystyle=(n-1)m-2a.

So

\partial(c^{d})=dc^{d-1}\left((n-1)m-2a\right).

Next,

\displaystyle\partial(amc^{d})

\displaystyle=\partial(am)c^{d}+am\partial(c^{d})

\displaystyle=\left(-na+\binom{n}{2}m\right)c^{d}+am\left(-2dac^{d-1}+d(n-1)mc^{d-1}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(-na+\binom{n}{2}m\right)c^{d}.

Lastly,

\displaystyle\partial(g)

\displaystyle=\partial(am-pc)

\displaystyle=(-na+\binom{n}{2}m)-p\partial(c^{1})

\displaystyle=(-na+\binom{n}{2}m)-p\left(-2a+(n-1)m\right)

\displaystyle=\left(-n+2p\right)a+\left(\binom{n}{2}-(n-1)p\right)m.

When
n
is even,

.
When
n
is odd,

.
Thus,

\displaystyle\partial(g)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\left(-n+2\frac{n}{2}\right)a+\left(\frac{n(n-1)}{2}-(n-1)\frac{n}{2}\right)m&n~\text{even}\\
\left(-n+2\frac{n+1}{2}\right)a+\left(\frac{n(n-1)}{2}-(n-1)\frac{n+1}{2}\right)m&n~\text{odd}\end{cases}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0&n~\text{even}\\
a-(p-1)m&n~\text{odd}\end{cases}\qed
