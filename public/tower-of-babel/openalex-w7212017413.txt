Abstract
Myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) play an important role in colorectal cancer progression by contributing to
the evasion of the antitumor immune response, treatment resistance, and metastasis formation. This integrative review
aimed to analyze the mechanisms by which MDSCs promote the suppression of the antitumor immune response in
colorectal cancer, as well as their contribution to disease progression. Studies published between 2020 and 2025 were
analyzed using the PubMed and LILACS databases. The selected studies demonstrated that MDSCs employ different
mechanisms to promote immunosuppression, including metabolic alterations that limit nutrient availability for
immune cells, the production of cytotoxic molecules capable of impairing lymphocyte function, and the secretion of
anti-inflammatory mediators that reduce immune system activity. Particularly, the gut microbiota and the presence of
Fusobacterium nucleatum stimulate MDSC expansion and activity, contributing to the maintenance of chronic
inflammation associated with colorectal cancer. Interactions between these cells and T lymphocytes, invariant natural
killer cells, and neutrophils further intensify the immunosuppressive environment, while extracellular vesicles
released by tumor and myeloid cells participate in feedback mechanisms that exacerbate immune dysfunction. These
findings reinforce the central role of MDSCs in colorectal cancer progression and highlight their potential as
therapeutic targets. However, the high heterogeneity of these cells and the lack of standardized methods for their
identification remain challenges for their clinical application. Future studies integrating multiparametric flow
cytometry, single-cell RNA sequencing, spatial analyses, and metabolomic approaches may provide a broader
understanding of MDSC biology and contribute to the development of more specific and effective therapeutic
strategies.
Keywords: MDSCs; Colorectal cancer; Immunosuppression; Tumor microenvironment; Antitumor immunity.

1. Introduction
Colorectal cancer (CCR), which originates in the colon or rectum, represents a major public health concern. In Brazil,
an estimated 45,630 new cases were expected between 2023 and 2025, corresponding to an approximate risk of 21.1 cases per
100,000 inhabitants, with the highest concentration in the Southeast region (Santos et al., 2023). The disease is biologically
heterogeneous and generally develops from adenomatous and serrated polyps that accumulate progressive mutations (Lee et
al., 2026; Santos et al., 2023). Globally, substantial geographic variation exists: among men, the highest incidence rates occur
in Central, Northern, and Southern Europe, whereas among women, the highest rates are observed in Oceania and Northern
Europe. In the United States, CRC is the second leading cause of cancer-related death, with approximately 150,000 cases
diagnosed annually, of which 25% are metastatic. An age-related shift in the epidemiological profile has also been observed,
with a decline in the median age at diagnosis and projections of increases of up to 124% among younger individuals by 2030
(Johnson, 2023).
Risk factors for CCR include behavioral, environmental, and genetic components, such as physical inactivity, obesity,
smoking, alcohol consumption, low-fiber diets, inflammatory bowel disease, family history, hereditary syndromes, and
occupational exposure to ionizing radiation (Santos et al., 2023). Because its natural history involves the gradual
transformation of benign polyps into invasive tumors, CRC is highly amenable to prevention and screening strategies, which
can substantially reduce incidence and mortality (Santos et al., 2023).
Myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) have been widely recognized as important modulators of the immune
response in various pathological contexts, particularly in neoplasms (Gabrilovich & Nagaraj, 2009). These cells are of myeloid
origin and exhibit an immature phenotype, with potent immunosuppressive capacity that enables tumors to evade recognition

therapeutic resistance.
During chronic inflammatory processes, such as those observed in the tumor microenvironment, myelopoiesis
undergoes pathological activation, resulting in the generation of heterogeneous populations of immature myeloid cells. Among
these, two major MDSC subsets are recognized: polymorphonuclear MDSCs (PMN-MDSCs) and monocytic MDSCs (MMDSCs), which differ in their phenotypic markers, morphological characteristics, and distinct suppressive mechanisms.
Despite the growing number of studies, the heterogeneity of these cells remains a significant challenge for their standardized
identification and characterization (Bronte et al., 2016).
In an effort to standardize the nomenclature and criteria for the phenotypic and functional definition of MDSCs,
Bronte et al. (2016) proposed international guidelines for their classification, emphasizing the need to consider molecular,
immunophenotypic, and functional aspects when studying these cell populations. According to these criteria, in mice, MMDSCs are defined as CD11b⁺ Ly6Chigh Ly6G⁻, whereas PMN-MDSCs are characterized as CD11b⁺ Ly6Clow Ly6G⁺ (Bronte
et al., 2016). In humans, MDSCs are classified into three main subtypes: monocytic MDSCs (mMDSCs), characterized by
HLA-DR⁻/low CD33⁺ CD11b⁺ CD14⁺; polymorphonuclear or granulocytic MDSCs (gMDSCs), identified as Lin⁻ HLA-DR⁻/low
CD33⁺ CD11b⁺ CD15⁺; and early-stage MDSCs (eMDSCs), which are Lin⁻ (including CD3, CD14, CD15, CD19, and CD56)
and HLA-DR⁻/low CD33⁺, reflecting their phenotypic and functional diversity in inflammatory environments (Bronte et al.,
2016). These recommendations have contributed to greater standardization across studies and to a better understanding of the
role of MDSCs in different neoplasms, including colorectal cancer.
Myeloid cells represent the largest population of nucleated cells derived from hematopoiesis and include
macrophages, dendritic cells, and granulocytes, which are essential for host defense and tissue homeostasis (Abbas, 2023).
Under physiological conditions, these cells participate in innate and adaptive immunity, eliminate pathogens and apoptotic
cells, and contribute to tissue remodeling, exhibiting marked heterogeneity and a high capacity to adapt to the
microenvironment (Johnson et al., 2023). In CRC, this plasticity plays a central role: the tumor microenvironment (TME) is
enriched in cytokines and growth factors, such as granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF), macrophage
colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF), IL-6, and IL-1β, which pathologically reprogram myeloid cells and induce
immunosuppressive phenotypes. Among the main effector populations are MDSCs and tumor-associated neutrophils, which
are extensively expanded within the TME (Yin et al., 2020; Johnson et al., 2023).
The immunosuppression mediated by these cells involves increased production of reactive oxygen and nitrogen
species, secretion of regulatory cytokines, and metabolic competition with T lymphocytes, thereby impairing their function.
They also promote the expansion of regulatory T cells (Tregs), further reinforcing the barrier to antitumor cytotoxic activity
(Johnson et al., 2023). This set of mechanisms is particularly relevant in metastatic microsatellite-stable (MSS) CRC, a subtype
characterized by a low mutational burden and reduced immunogenicity, which contributes to poor responses to immune
checkpoint blockade. In these tumors, the marked presence of immunosuppressive myeloid populations is a major determinant
of therapeutic failure, in addition to intrinsic genomic alterations, such as mutations in the RAS family, which activate
oncogenic proliferative pathways and reduce the efficacy of targeted therapies (Johnson et al., 2023). Furthermore, the
activation of MDSCs, Tregs, and tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) is also associated with resistance to chemotherapy
and the promotion of metastasis, making the myeloid axis a strategic target for novel therapeutic approaches (Johnson et al.,
2023).

Considering the abovementioned aspects, this integrative review aimed to analyze the mechanisms by which MDSCs

2. Methodology
This study is an integrative literature review (Page et al., 2021; Snyder, 2019), employing a quantitative approach to
describe the 19 articles selected for the research corpus and a qualitative approach to analyze and discuss the role of myeloidderived suppressor cells (MDSCs) in the mechanisms underlying antitumor immune response suppression in colorectal cancer
(CRC) (Risemberg et al., 2026; Pereira et al., 2018). The review was conducted in accordance with the recommendations of
the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020), aiming to ensure transparency and
reproducibility throughout the study selection and analysis processes.
The bibliographic search was conducted in the PubMed and LILACS (Latin American and Caribbean Health Sciences
Literature) databases, covering publications from 2020 to 2025. The search strategy employed English-language descriptors,
including “Myeloid-Derived Suppressor Cells,” “MDSCs,” and “Colorectal Carcinoma,” identified based on the controlled
vocabularies of the Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and Health Sciences Descriptors (DeCS). The descriptors were
combined using the Boolean operators “AND” and “OR,” and the combinations used in the search strategy are presented in
Table 1.

Studies were eligible if they were original research articles published in English between 2020 and 2025, with fulltext availability, and addressed mechanisms of antitumor immune response suppression mediated by MDSCs in colorectal
cancer using in vitro or in vivo models or human CRC samples. Studies were excluded if they were published outside the
predefined period, were not written in English, did not address MDSCs in the context of CRC, or consisted of literature
reviews or systematic reviews. Letters to the editor, editorials, duplicate publications identified across the databases, and
articles without access to the full text were also excluded. These criteria were established to prioritize recent original studies
specifically investigating the involvement of MDSCs in colorectal cancer and their mechanisms of immune suppression.

4. Discussion
The studies included in this review consistently demonstrate that myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) play a
central role in the immunosuppressive microenvironment of colorectal cancer (CRC), contributing to tumor immune evasion,
disease progression, and therapeutic resistance. Rather than representing a passive consequence of tumor development, MDSC
accumulation appears to be actively driven by interactions between tumor cells, inflammatory signals, immune populations,
and the intestinal microbiota. The evidence analyzed indicates that these cells contribute to CRC progression through
interconnected mechanisms involving their recruitment, differentiation, functional activation, and interaction with effector
immune cells.
One of the most relevant findings is the ability of the tumor and its surrounding microenvironment to promote MDSC
accumulation. Guo et al. (2024) demonstrated that deletion of CUL4B in the intestinal epithelium accelerated adenoma
formation in ApcMin/+ mice by increasing G-CSF expression and promoting MDSC recruitment. This finding illustrates how
tumor-associated alterations in epithelial signaling can influence the myeloid compartment and contribute to an
immunosuppressive environment. Similarly, Gu et al. (2024) demonstrated that CRC-derived LC3-dependent extracellular
vesicles promote M-MDSC accumulation through the Hsp60–TLR2–MyD88 pathway. These vesicles were associated with
increased M-MDSC levels and enhanced expression of immunosuppressive mediators, including IL-10 and arginase-1.
Together, these findings indicate that CRC cells actively participate in the generation and maintenance of MDSCs through
soluble and vesicular signals.
The heterogeneity of MDSCs represents another important aspect of their role in CRC. Saleh et al. (2020) and
Sasidharan Nair et al. (2020) demonstrated that different MDSC subsets exhibit distinct molecular profiles and signaling
pathways. PMN-MDSCs, M-MDSCs, and immature MDSCs showed different transcriptional programs, suggesting that their
contribution to tumor progression is not functionally uniform. Importantly, specific molecular signatures identified in these
populations were associated with unfavorable clinical outcomes, including poorer overall and disease-free survival (Saleh et
al., 2020). This heterogeneity has important implications for both biomarker development and therapeutic strategies, as
targeting MDSCs as a single homogeneous population may overlook biologically relevant differences among subsets.
The clinical relevance of MDSC accumulation is further supported by studies conducted in patients with CRC.
Siemińska et al. (2022) reported increased circulating M-MDSCs in patients with CRC, with higher levels associated with
increased Treg frequency, reduced CD8+ T-cell responses, and a greater risk of tumor recurrence following surgery. Similarly,
Gunarsa et al. (2025) observed increased PMN-MDSCs and M-MDSCs in CRC patients and found that an early reduction in
these populations following oxaliplatin-based chemotherapy was maintained during treatment. These findings suggest that
changes in circulating MDSC populations may have potential prognostic or predictive value, although larger prospective
studies are required to establish their clinical utility.
MDSCs also contribute to immune suppression through direct interactions with effector immune cells. Gneo et al.
(2022) demonstrated that TGF-β plays a central role in the polarization of monocytes toward an immunosuppressive M-MDSC

M-MDSCs, and antitumor T-cell responses.
The spatial organization of these cellular interactions may be as relevant as the absolute abundance of MDSCs. Zwing
et al. (2020) showed that the spatial distribution of suppressive myeloid cells and effector T cells within CRC tissue is
associated with the immune contexture of the tumor and may provide prognostic information. Therefore, the biological impact
of MDSCs may depend not only on their frequency but also on their anatomical localization and proximity to immune effector
populations. This observation reinforces the importance of spatial approaches for understanding the complexity of the CRC
immune microenvironment.
Similarly, Wang et al. (2023) used single-cell RNA sequencing and spatial transcriptomics to demonstrate that CRC
progression is accompanied by spatial reprogramming of immunosuppressive cells, including MDSCs. MDSCs and other
regulatory populations were associated with EMT-related invasive programs, with distinct inflammatory and hypoxic niches
identified across tumor stages. These findings further support the importance of spatial organization in shaping the
immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment.
The intestinal microbiota constitutes an additional component of this regulatory network. Ashkenazi-Preiser et al.
(2024) demonstrated that interactions between dysbiotic intestinal microbiota and MDSCs contribute to chronic inflammation
and immunosuppression during the development of colitis-associated CRC. In parallel, Lattanzi et al. (2023) demonstrated that
Fusobacterium nucleatum-associated activation of iNKT cells promotes the recruitment of neutrophils with an MDSC-like
immunosuppressive phenotype, thereby favoring CRC pathogenesis. These findings indicate that microbial alterations may
influence the myeloid compartment indirectly through inflammatory and cellular interactions, reinforcing the concept of CRC
as a disease shaped by reciprocal interactions between tumor cells, immune populations, and the intestinal ecosystem.
Several studies also identified specific molecular pathways that regulate MDSC activity and may represent therapeutic
targets. The CSF1R pathway appears particularly relevant. Tong et al. (2024) demonstrated that CSF1R contributes to the
maintenance of MDSC function through mechanisms involving JAK/STAT3 signaling and fatty acid metabolism. CSF1R
inhibition reduced MDSC abundance and immunosuppressive activity and enhanced the antitumor effect of anti-PD-1 therapy.
These findings support the rationale for combining myeloid-directed interventions with immune checkpoint blockade to
overcome immunosuppressive mechanisms that limit T-cell-mediated tumor control.
The PGE₂–EP4 axis represents another mechanism involved in MDSC-mediated immunosuppression. Lu et al. (2021)
demonstrated that PGE₂ signaling through EP4 promotes the immunosuppressive activity of myeloid cells in CRC.
Pharmacological inhibition of EP4 reduced this immunosuppressive phenotype and improved the response to immune
checkpoint blockade. These findings suggest that targeting inflammatory lipid signaling may represent an alternative strategy
for modulating the myeloid compartment and enhancing antitumor immunity.
Other studies have identified mechanisms involving inflammatory cytokines and immune checkpoint-related
pathways. Imazeki et al. (2021) demonstrated that CD11b +CTLA-4+ myeloid cells represent an important driver of tumor
immune evasion in CRC. These cells produced IL-1β and promoted functional exhaustion of CD8 + T cells, characterized by
increased expression of inhibitory receptors and reduced antitumor activity. Blocking the CTLA-4–IL-1β axis restored T-cell
function and reduced tumor progression and metastasis, including in models resistant to anti-PD-1 therapy. This evidence
suggests that myeloid-mediated immunosuppression may represent an important mechanism underlying resistance to immune

The IL-17/IL-17RA pathway provides another example of the interaction between inflammatory signaling and MDSC
accumulation. Jiang et al. (2024) found that reduced IL-17RA expression was associated with a more favorable prognosis in
CRC, whereas inhibition of IL-17RA reduced tumor growth and vascularity in murine models and was accompanied by
alterations in immunosuppressive immune populations, including MDSCs. These findings suggest that inflammatory cytokine
signaling may influence tumor progression partly through modulation of the myeloid compartment.
In contrast, IL-33 may exert antitumor effects by modulating the tumor immune microenvironment. Kienzl et al. (2020)
demonstrated that IL-33 treatment reduced tumor growth in murine models of CRC through eosinophil-dependent
mechanisms, including enhanced eosinophil recruitment, activation, and degranulation. Although the study did not directly
establish MDSCs as the primary mediator, these findings highlight the potential of modulating the tumor microenvironment to
promote antitumor immunity.
Additional mechanisms involving MDSC functional regulation have been identified at the molecular and epigenetic
levels. Feng et al. (2024) demonstrated that ALKBH5 overexpression reduces MDSC immunosuppressive activity by
decreasing the stability of arginase-1 mRNA through modulation of m6A methylation. This finding highlights the importance
of post-transcriptional regulation in maintaining the suppressive phenotype of MDSCs and suggests that epigenetic
mechanisms may provide additional therapeutic opportunities.
Conversely, the functional activity of MDSCs may also be reflected by circulating biomarkers. Shirasuna et al. (2020)
found that higher plasma iNOS levels and lower PDGF-BB levels were independently associated with a greater proportion of
circulating mMDSCs in patients with CRC. Although these findings do not establish a causal relationship, they suggest that
circulating molecular markers may potentially assist in estimating MDSC burden and could contribute to the development of
minimally invasive biomarkers.
The interaction between MDSCs and other immune populations may also be therapeutically modulated. Chen et al.
(2021) demonstrated that activated T cells can reprogram immunosuppressive myeloid cells through IFN-α/β and TNF-α
signaling, promoting MDSC apoptosis and reducing their suppressive activity. This process contributed to the efficacy of antiPD-1 therapy and provides further evidence that the functional state of MDSCs is dynamically regulated by interactions with
other immune cells. Therefore, therapeutic strategies aimed at modifying the TME may be more effective when they
simultaneously influence both tumor cells and immunosuppressive myeloid populations.
Although the evidence reviewed supports an important role for MDSCs in CRC, several limitations must be
considered. A major limitation is the predominance of preclinical studies, particularly murine models. Although these models
are essential for investigating mechanisms of tumor progression and immune regulation, they cannot fully reproduce the
complexity and heterogeneity of the human TME. Differences in MDSC phenotypes, marker expression, signaling pathways,
and interactions with other immune populations may limit the direct translation of experimental findings to clinical settings.
Methodological heterogeneity is another important challenge. The studies included in this review used different
approaches to identify and characterize MDSCs, including variations in immunophenotypic markers, experimental models,
tissue compartments, and functional assays. This lack of standardization makes direct comparison between studies difficult and
may contribute to discrepancies in the reported frequency and biological properties of MDSC subsets. The transcriptional
heterogeneity identified by Saleh et al. (2020) and Sasidharan Nair et al. (2020) further highlights the importance of
establishing more precise criteria for the characterization of these populations.
Furthermore, although several studies have associated MDSC accumulation with tumor progression, recurrence, or
treatment response, robust clinical validation remains limited. Most clinical studies involve relatively small cohorts, and few
have established standardized MDSC measurements that can be routinely incorporated into clinical decision-making.

Consequently, an important gap remains between the mechanistic evidence supporting MDSC involvement in CRC and the

Another limitation is that many studies have focused on individual pathways or cellular interactions, such as TGF-β,
CSF1R/JAK–STAT3, PGE₂–EP4, IL-17RA, or CTLA-4–IL-1β. However, MDSC activity is determined by the simultaneous
influence of multiple signals originating from tumor cells, immune cells, inflammatory mediators, metabolic conditions, and
the intestinal microbiota. Therefore, studying individual pathways in isolation may not fully capture the dynamic and
interconnected nature of MDSC-mediated immunosuppression.
Future studies should therefore integrate advanced immunophenotyping, single-cell transcriptomics, spatial analyses,
and metabolomic approaches with well-characterized clinical cohorts. Such strategies may improve the identification of
functionally distinct MDSC subsets, clarify their spatial and molecular interactions within the TME, and identify biomarkers
with greater clinical applicability. In addition, prospective studies should evaluate whether changes in MDSC populations can
reliably predict treatment response, recurrence, and survival.
Overall, the evidence analyzed in this review indicates that MDSCs occupy a strategic position in the immunological
landscape of CRC. Their effects are mediated through coordinated interactions with tumor cells, T cells, iNKT cells,
neutrophils, cytokines, intracellular signaling pathways, and the intestinal microbiota. This multifactorial role makes MDSCs
promising targets for therapeutic intervention and potential biomarkers of disease progression and treatment response.
However, further clinical validation and methodological standardization are necessary before MDSC-directed strategies can be
routinely incorporated into CRC management.

5. Final Considerations
The evidence analyzed in this review indicates that myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) play a central role in
the immunosuppressive microenvironment of colorectal cancer (CRC), contributing to immune evasion, tumor progression,
metastasis, and therapeutic resistance. Their activity results from complex interactions with tumor cells, effector immune
populations, inflammatory mediators, signaling pathways, and the intestinal microbiota. The findings also highlight the marked
heterogeneity of MDSC subsets, which exhibit distinct phenotypic and functional characteristics and may contribute differently
to disease progression and treatment response.
Despite advances in understanding the mechanisms underlying MDSC-mediated immunosuppression, important gaps
remain, particularly regarding phenotypic and functional standardization and the translation of experimental findings into
clinical practice. Future studies integrating advanced immunological, molecular, spatial, and clinical approaches are needed to
better characterize these populations and identify reliable biomarkers and therapeutic targets. In this context, modulation of
MDSCs represents a promising strategy that may complement existing treatments and enhance antitumor immune responses in
CRC.
