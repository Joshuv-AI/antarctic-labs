ADHD, Impulsivity, and Affective Symptoms in Metabolic and Bariatric Surgery Candidates
Authors: Anna Rajtar-Wysocka, Michał Wysocki, Anna Starowicz-Filip, Barbara Bętkowska-Korpała, Andrzej Budzyński
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAttention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) is increasingly recognized among metabolic and bariatric surgery candidates and has been associated with affective burden, impulsivity, and difficulties with treatment adherence. This study assessed the prevalence of ADHD diagnosed with a structured clinical interview and examined its associations with depressive symptoms, anxiety, and impulsivity in adults undergoing preoperative psychological evaluation for metabolic and bariatric surgery.MethodsSeventy adults with obesity undergoing routine bariatric qualification were assessed for ADHD using the Diagnostic Interview for ADHD in Adults, DSM-5 version (DIVA-5), and completed the Beck Depression Inventory–II (BDI-II), Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI), and Barratt Impulsiveness Scale–11 (BIS-11). Group comparisons were conducted between participants with and without ADHD. Two multivariable linear regression models examined predictors of depressive symptom severity and anxiety severity, including age, sex, BMI, ADHD diagnosis, and impulsivity.ResultsADHD was diagnosed in 18.6% of participants (13/70). Compared with participants without ADHD, those with ADHD reported significantly higher depressive symptoms, anxiety symptoms, and impulsivity, and more frequently met criteria for depressive and anxiety disorders. In multivariable analyses, female sex, ADHD diagnosis, and impulsivity independently predicted depressive symptom severity, whereas female sex and ADHD diagnosis predicted anxiety severity.ConclusionADHD was relatively common in this sample of metabolic and bariatric surgery candidates and was independently associated with greater depressive and anxiety symptom severity. Impulsivity provided additional explanatory value for depressive symptoms beyond ADHD diagnosis. Introduction

Obesity is associated with substantial medical and psychological burden worldwide [1, 2]. Psychiatric disorders are highly prevalent among individuals seeking metabolic and bariatric surgery, with depression, anxiety disorders, and binge-eating pathology among the most commonly reported conditions [3–6]. Preoperative psychopathology and psychosocial functioning may negatively affect treatment engagement, postoperative adherence, and long-term weight-related outcomes after metabolic and bariatric surgery [5, 7]. Consequently, comprehensive psychological assessment has become an important component of multidisciplinary bariatric care [7–9].

In recent years, attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) has gained increasing attention in bariatric populations [10, 11]. ADHD is characterized by persistent patterns of inattention, impulsivity, and executive dysfunction that may interfere with behavioral self-regulation and long-term treatment adherence. Meta-analytic evidence suggests that ADHD is more prevalent among metabolic and bariatric surgery candidates than in the general adult population [11]. ADHD symptoms have also been associated with binge eating, emotional eating, reduced follow-up attendance, and poorer postoperative outcomes after metabolic and bariatric surgery [10, 12, 13]. In addition, impulsivity and executive-function deficits have been linked to difficulties maintaining dietary recommendations and increased risk of postoperative weight regain [14, 15].

Depressive and anxiety symptoms are also common among metabolic and bariatric surgery candidates and may further compromise motivation, adherence, and postoperative adjustment [5, 6]. Emerging evidence suggests that ADHD frequently co-occurs with affective symptoms and psychological distress in individuals with obesity [16–18]. However, relatively few studies have examined the combined associations between clinically diagnosed ADHD, impulsivity, and affective symptom severity in metabolic and bariatric surgery candidates using structured diagnostic assessment methods. Improved understanding of these interrelationships may help identify psychologically vulnerable patients who could benefit from enhanced preoperative monitoring and individualized psychological support.

Therefore, the present study aimed to assess the prevalence of ADHD diagnosed using the Diagnostic Interview for ADHD in Adults (DIVA-5) and to examine associations between ADHD, impulsivity, depressive symptoms, and anxiety symptoms in adults undergoing metabolic and bariatric surgery evaluation. Additionally, ADHD diagnosis and impulsivity were examined as independent predictors of affective symptom severity in multivariable regression models.

Methods

Participants and Procedure

Seventy adults with obesity (59 women, 11 men; mean age = 39.4 ± 8.7 years; mean BMI = 43.2 ± 11.5 kg/m²) seeking metabolic and bariatric surgery at an academic hospital were enrolled between May 2024 and June 2025. All participants underwent comprehensive multidisciplinary preoperative assessment, including standardized psychological and clinical evaluation as part of routine metabolic and bariatric surgery qualification. Psychological assessments were conducted by trained clinical neuropsychologists and constituted an integral component of preoperative care. All participants provided written informed consent prior to study participation. The study was approved by the institutional bioethics committee and conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Psychological Assessment

Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) was diagnosed using the Diagnostic Interview for ADHD in Adults, DSM-5 version (DIVA-5) [19], a structured clinical interview assessing childhood and adult symptoms of inattention and hyperactivity/impulsivity, as well as associated functional impairment across multiple life domains. Depressive symptom severity was assessed using the Beck Depression Inventory–II (BDI-II) [20], a 21-item self-report questionnaire evaluating depressive symptoms experienced during the previous two weeks. Anxiety symptom severity was measured using the Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI) [21], a 21-item self-report instrument assessing cognitive and somatic anxiety symptoms experienced during the previous week. Impulsivity was evaluated using the Barratt Impulsiveness Scale–11 (BIS-11) [22], a widely used 30-item self-report measure assessing attentional, motor, and non-planning impulsivity.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using Statistica 13.3 (TIBCO Software Inc.). Continuous variables were summarized as means and standard deviations (M ± SD), whereas categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Between-group comparisons between participants with and without ADHD diagnosis were conducted using independent-samples t tests for continuous variables and chi-square (χ²) tests for categorical variables. Additional analyses compared participants with and without clinically significant depressive symptoms, defined as a BDI-II total score ≥ 20. To identify predictors of emotional symptom severity, two multiple linear regression models were computed. In the first model, depressive symptom severity (BDI-II total score) served as the dependent variable. Predictor variables included age, sex, BMI, ADHD diagnosis (0 = no, 1 = yes), and impulsivity (BIS-11 total score). The overall model was statistically significant (R = 0.646, R² = 0.418, p < 0.001), explaining 41.8% of the variance in depressive symptom severity. Female sex (β = 0.378, p < 0.001), ADHD diagnosis (β = 0.365, p < 0.001), and impulsivity (β = 0.421, p < 0.001) emerged as significant independent predictors, whereas age (p = 0.710) and BMI (p = 0.869) were not statistically significant.

The second regression model examined predictors of anxiety symptom severity (BAI total score) using the same independent variables. This model was also statistically significant (R = 0.457, R² = 0.209, p = 0.008), accounting for 20.9% of the variance in anxiety symptom severity. Female sex (β = 0.256, p = 0.032) and ADHD diagnosis (β = 0.342, p = 0.005) were significant predictors, whereas age (p = 0.224), BMI (p = 0.286), and impulsivity (p = 0.178) did not reach statistical significance. All analyses were two-tailed, and the threshold for statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Regression assumptions, including normality of residuals, homoscedasticity, and multicollinearity, were examined and considered acceptable. Variance inflation factors were below commonly accepted thresholds.

Results

Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) was diagnosed in 13 participants (18.6%). Compared with participants without ADHD, those with ADHD reported significantly higher depressive symptom severity (BDI-II: 18.16 ± 10.38 vs. 8.19 ± 7.11; p < 0.001), anxiety symptom severity (BAI: 26.92 ± 11.56 vs. 15.51 ± 12.41; p 0.004), and impulsivity scores (BIS-11: 42.69 ± 5.52 vs. 34.21 ± 12.14; p = 0.021). Participants with ADHD also more frequently met criteria for depressive disorders (69.2% vs. 26.3%; p = 0.002) and anxiety disorders (61.5% vs. 36.8%; p = 0.031). Although binge-eating disorder (BED) was more frequent among participants with ADHD (23.1% vs. 10.6%), this difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.226). No significant between-group differences were observed for age, sex distribution, or BMI. Detailed results are presented in Table 1.

Discussion

The present study found that ADHD was relatively common among metabolic and bariatric surgery candidates and was consistently associated with greater depressive and anxiety symptom severity. Participants with ADHD reported significantly higher levels of emotional distress and impulsivity and more frequently met criteria for depressive and anxiety disorders. Importantly, ADHD diagnosis independently predicted both depressive and anxiety symptom severity, whereas impulsivity independently predicted depressive symptoms beyond ADHD diagnosis and demographic variables.

The prevalence of ADHD observed in this sample (18.6%) was higher than estimates reported for the general adult population and remained consistent with previous studies demonstrating elevated ADHD prevalence among metabolic and bariatric surgery candidates [11, 16]. These findings support growing evidence that ADHD symptoms and executive dysfunction represent clinically relevant but frequently under-recognized features in bariatric populations [10, 11]. Previous studies have emphasized the importance of comprehensive psychosocial assessment as part of multidisciplinary bariatric care [7]. ADHD-related difficulties with self-regulation, planning, inhibitory control, and long-term behavioral consistency may interfere with adherence to dietary recommendations, follow-up attendance, and postoperative lifestyle modifications required after metabolic and bariatric surgery [10, 12, 13].

The findings also highlight the close relationship between ADHD and affective symptom burden in bariatric candidates. Participants with ADHD reported substantially higher depressive and anxiety symptom severity than participants without ADHD, consistent with previous studies linking ADHD with internalizing psychopathology, impaired psychological functioning, and neurodevelopmental vulnerability in individuals with obesity [11, 16, 17, 23]. Notably, impulsivity independently predicted depressive symptom severity even after controlling for ADHD diagnosis, suggesting that behavioral dysregulation itself may contribute to emotional vulnerability in this population.

In contrast, impulsivity was not independently associated with anxiety symptom severity in multivariable analyses. This finding may indicate that depressive symptoms in bariatric populations are more strongly linked to behavioral dysregulation and impaired inhibitory control, whereas anxiety symptoms may be influenced by additional psychological and neurobiological mechanisms not captured by impulsivity measures alone. Further longitudinal research is needed to clarify these relationships and their potential impact on postoperative functioning and weight-related outcomes.

These findings may have important implications for multidisciplinary bariatric care. Identification of ADHD symptoms and elevated impulsivity during preoperative assessment may help recognize patients at increased risk of emotional distress, difficulties with behavioral self-regulation, and reduced postoperative adherence. Although comprehensive diagnostic interviews may not be required for all bariatric candidates, patients presenting with attentional difficulties, impulsivity, emotional dysregulation, or longstanding functional impairment may benefit from further ADHD assessment and enhanced psychological support before surgery. Tailored interventions focusing on executive functioning, emotional regulation, and behavioral consistency may improve psychological adjustment and long-term treatment adherence in vulnerable patients.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Second, the relatively small sample size limits generalizability. Third, several psychological variables were assessed using self-report measures and may therefore be influenced by response bias. Finally, the predominance of women in the study sample limits conclusions regarding sex-specific effects. Future longitudinal studies should examine whether ADHD symptoms and impulsivity predict postoperative adherence, weight-loss outcomes, and long-term psychosocial functioning after metabolic and bariatric surgery.

Conclusion

ADHD was relatively common among metabolic and bariatric surgery candidates and was independently associated with greater depressive and anxiety symptom severity. Impulsivity independently predicted depressive symptoms beyond ADHD diagnosis. These findings support incorporating targeted screening for ADHD symptoms and impulsivity into routine preoperative psychological assessment to identify patients who may benefit from enhanced psychological support and individualized perioperative care strategies.
