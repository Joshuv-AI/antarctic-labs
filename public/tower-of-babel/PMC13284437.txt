Efficacy and effectiveness of long-acting monoclonal antibodies and vaccines against RSV: a systematic review and meta-analysis of studies from 2018 to 2025
Authors: Jiayi Du, Sophie Li, Ivan FN Hung, Joseph T Wu, Kathy Leung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundRespiratory syncytial virus (RSV) remains a leading cause of lower respiratory tract infection (LRTI) and mortality among infants and older adults globally. The recent approval of long-acting monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) and prefusion F protein-based vaccines marks a significant shift in the prevention landscape. This systematic review aims to synthesise current evidence regarding the efficacy and real-world effectiveness of RSV interventions.MethodsWe searched PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science for randomised controlled trials and observational studies published between January 1, 2018, and September 8, 2025, that evaluated palivizumab, nirsevimab, and RSV vaccines in infants, pregnant women, and older adults. The primary outcome was RSV-associated lower respiratory tract infection (RSV-LRTI) hospitalisation. Pooled efficacy and effectiveness were estimated using random-effects meta-analysis. This study is registered with PROSPERO, CRD420261285934.FindingsIn infants, palivizumab demonstrated effectiveness against RSV-LRTI hospitalisation up to 180 days post-discharge that varied substantially by analytical approach: OR-based studies yielded a pooled effectiveness of approximately 81% (95% CI: 57%–91%), while rate-ratio-based cohort studies produced a non-statistically significant estimate of 34% (95% CI: −17%–63%). Nirsevimab showed pooled efficacy/effectiveness of approximately 80–85% through 150–180 days post-dose, with consistent results across OR-based (84%, 95% CI: 78%–88%) and rate-ratio-based analyses (79%, 95% CI: 72%–85%). Both palivizumab and nirsevimab provide robust protection against RSV-LRTI hospitalisation in their respective target populations. Additionally, both maternal and older adult vaccinations are effective in reducing RSV-associated diseases.FundingGeneral Research Fund, AIR@InnoHK.Keywords: Respiratory syncytial virus, RSV monoclonal antibodies, RSV vaccines, Meta-analysis

Research in context

Evidence before this study

We searched PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science for randomised controlled trials and observational studies published between Jan 1, 2018, and Sep 8, 2025. The search was restricted to English-language publications. We used search terms related to respiratory syncytial virus combined with specific interventions, including monoclonal antibodies and vaccines. Prior to 2023, RSV prophylaxis was limited to palivizumab, with evidence focused largely on high-risk infants. While clinical trials led to the recent approval of long-acting monoclonal antibodies and prefusion F protein-based vaccines for infants, pregnant women, and older adults, there has been a lack of comprehensive synthesis integrating these trial results with emerging real-world effectiveness data, particularly regarding the comparative performance of prophylactic options and their impact on severe clinical outcomes.

Added value of this study

Recent systematic reviews have provided vital summaries of the real-world effectiveness of RSV. Our study builds upon and extends the search through September 2025 and synthesising both randomised controlled trials and real-world observational studies, providing a more up-to-date comprehensive evaluation of the global RSV immunisation landscape. We demonstrate that both palivizumab and nirsevimab provide robust protection against RSV-LRTI hospitalisations in their respective target populations. Furthermore, we clarify that maternal vaccination provides high protection against severe RSV-LRTI hospitalisation (72%) and RSV-LRTI hospitalisation (67%) in infants, though effectiveness against medically attended RSV-LRTI yields an estimate with a wide confidence interval (54%) due to the limited number of available studies. Similarly, vaccines for older adults showed robust effectiveness (77%) against RSV-LRTI hospitalisation, though protection varies by frailty and comorbidity status.

Implications of all the available evidence

Next-generation prophylactics represent a robust protection in preventing RSV-associated disease. Both palivizumab and nirsevimab provide robust protection against RSV-LRTI hospitalisation in their respective target populations. Maternal and older adult vaccines are effective against RSV-LRTI hospitalisations and severe outcomes. As real-world implementation expands globally, ongoing post-marketing surveillance remains important. Future research should prioritise monitoring long-term durability of protection and assessing feasibility in low- and middle-income countries.

Introduction

Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) remains a major cause of lower respiratory tract infection (LRTI) and a leading cause of mortality among infants and young children globally.1,2 The virus is responsible for an estimated 33 million episodes of LRTI, over 3 million hospitalisations, and more than 101,000 deaths annually in children under five years of age.1 Similarly, the virus poses a significant threat to older adults aged ≥65 years,3 often precipitating acute exacerbations of comorbidities like chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and heart failure, with mortality rates comparable to those of influenza during non-pandemic seasons.4 Beyond acute mortality, severe infection in both groups is linked to long-term sequelae, including recurrent wheezing in children and functional decline in the elderly, frequently overwhelming healthcare systems with surges in demand.5

Prior to 2023, there was no accessible and effective prophylaxis available for the broader population. For over two decades following its approval in 1998, pharmaceutical prevention was limited to palivizumab (Synagis®), a short-acting monoclonal antibody (mAb).6 Palivizumab possesses a standard half-life of approximately 20 days, necessitating monthly intramuscular injections to maintain protective serum neutralising antibody levels throughout the typical 5-month RSV season.7 The use of palivizumab was restricted to high-risk infants in high-income settings due to the high costs and logistical complexity of this multi-dose regimen. In the IMpact-RSV trial, palivizumab was reported to reduce RSV-related hospitalisations by 55% overall, and by 78% in preterm infants without BPD/CHD born ≤35 wGA, over a 150-day follow-up period with a safety profile characterised by mild injection site reactions, but it required monthly administration throughout the RSV season.6

However, the stabilisation of the RSV prefusion F protein transformed the prevention landscape, catalysing a rapid expansion of options beyond first-generation mAbs.8 While palivizumab remains in use, the introduction of next-generation long-acting mAbs, specifically nirsevimab (Beyfortus®) approved in 2023, and the recently approved clesrovimab (Enflonsia®), has offered extended protection with a single dose.9 Long-acting mAbs have modified Fc regions that reduce cellular degradation and extend their half-life to several months, enabling a single dose to provide durable protection for an entire RSV season.9 In the MELODY trial, nirsevimab was reported to reduce medically attended RSV-LRTI by 74.5% compared to placebo through 150 days after injection,10 while the real-world setting HARMONIE trial demonstrated an 83.2% reduction in hospitalisations over a 12-month follow-up period, with adverse events occurring at rates similar to those in the standard-care group.11

Concurrently, the introduction of protein-based subunit vaccines further shifted the immunisation landscape. For older adults, vaccines such as Arexvy™ (GSK) and Abrysvo™ (Pfizer) demonstrated high efficacy against RSV-LRTI.12,13 Furthermore, the approval of mRESVIA™ (Moderna) in 2024 established mRNA technology as a viable platform for adult RSV protection.14 In addition to protecting older adults, Abrysvo™ was also approved in 2023 for maternal immunisation to protect infants. The global MATISSE (Maternal Immunisation Study for Safety and Efficacy) trial demonstrated that a single maternal dose of the vaccine achieved an efficacy of 82.4% against severe RSV-associated medically attended LRTI in infants within 90 days of birth and 70.0% within 180 days.15,16

Driven by the rapid transformation of the RSV prevention landscape and the recent endorsements by the WHO Strategic Advisory Group of Experts on Immunization (SAGE) and Gavi,17 there is a critical need to synthesise the expanding body of evidence regarding the new prophylactic options against RSV. Recent systematic reviews, such as the living review by Lee et al. have provided vital summaries of the real-world effectiveness and safety of RSV immunisations up to March 2025. In this systematic review, we aimed to build upon and provide a more up-to-date comprehensive evaluation of market-approved RSV interventions, including both active immunisation and next-generation passive immunoprophylaxis. We included both clinical trials and emerging real-world evidence to assess the efficacy and effectiveness, and discussed challenges in implementing RSV interventions.

Methods

Search strategy and selection criteria

This systematic review was conducted following the PRISMA guidelines.18 We systematically searched PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science Core Collection to identify published studies from Jan 1, 2018, to Sep 8, 2025, restricted to English. The search strategy combined terms for RSV and respiratory infections with specific interventions, categorised into monoclonal antibodies (palivizumab, nirsevimab, clesrovimab) and vaccines (Arexvy™, Abrysvo™, mRESVIA™). Both strategies were further combined with terms relating to effectiveness, including “effectiveness” and “efficacy”. Full search strategies are in supplementary materials (Table S1).

We used Rayyan QCRI to manage, de-duplicate and screen the articles.19 Two reviewers (JD and SL) independently screened titles, abstracts, and full texts. Any discrepancies were resolved through consensus or by consulting a third reviewer (KL). We included clinical trials and original observational studies that evaluated the clinical efficacy or real-world effectiveness of RSV mAbs or vaccines in reducing RSV infection, symptomatic disease, severe LRTI, hospitalisations, intensive care unit (ICU) admissions, or deaths. Eligible populations included infants, young children, pregnant women, and older adults with no geographic restrictions applied during the literature search. We excluded animal studies, research focussing solely on immunogenicity or laboratory surrogates without clinical endpoints, and non-original research (editorials, reviews, conference abstracts). Clesrovimab is not included in this review due to the limited clinical data currently available. The flow diagram is shown in Fig. 1.

PRISMA 2020 flow diagram of the study selection and screening process.

Data extraction

Data extraction was performed using Covidence.20 For each eligible study, data were extracted on study characteristics (author, year, country, design, follow-up duration, and sample size), population details (age group and risk category), intervention characteristics (type, product name, and number of doses), and comparator (placebo, standard of care, or other preventive measures).

Efficacy/effectiveness data were extracted for all predefined primary outcomes and secondary outcomes, including RSV-LRTI, severe RSV-LRTI, RSV-associated hospitalisations, ICU admissions, and RSV-related mortality. The following quantitative outcome data were collected: event numbers in treatment and control groups, total sample sizes, effect estimates, 95% confidence interval (CI). Additional information on secondary outcomes, such as ED visits, follow-up period, and RSV-associated acute respiratory disease (RSV-ARI), was also recorded when available.

Data analysis

We summarised the baseline characteristics and methodological details of the included studies in descriptive tables. All reported clinical outcomes associated with RSV were extracted for analysis. To avoid data duplication, we prioritised the most recent comprehensive report when multiple publications were available for a single trial.

To ensure statistical validity and comparability across studies, effect measures were normalised prior to pooling. Data were stratified into two distinct analytical groups based on the originally reported metrics. In the first group, effect measures were standardised to odds ratios (OR). When a study reported an adjusted risk ratio (RR), it was mathematically converted to an OR using the baseline risk of the outcome in the control group (P0) according to the formula: OR=RR×(1−P0)1−(RR×P0).21 In the second group, hazard ratios (HR) and incidence rate ratios (IRR) were grouped and analysed together as rate ratios. Following standardisation, the natural logarithm of each normalised effect estimate and its corresponding standard error were calculated for pooling.

Meta-analyses were conducted using the ‘meta’ package within the R statistical software environment,22 accessed via RStudio (version 2025.09.2 + 418). Pooled results were calculated using a random-effects model (Hartung-Knapp method) with inverse variance weighting applied to the log-transformed estimates. We performed a subgroup meta-analysis for each outcome (RSV-LRTI hospitalisations, severe RSV-LRTI hospitalisations, RSV-LRTI, ED visits, ICU admissions, RSV-ARI). To facilitate clinical interpretation, the final pooled summary effect estimates were subsequently converted into efficacy/effectiveness percentages for both vaccines and mAbs using the standard formula: efficacy/effectiveness (%) = (1−pooledeffectestimate)×100. Furthermore, we also analysed outcomes using fixed-effect model and leave-one-out analysis as sensitivity analyses to ensure that smaller studies did not disproportionately influence the pooled estimates.

We undertook univariable and multivariable meta-regression analyses considering variables including age group, geographical region, study design, RSV season, follow-up duration, and population risk status. Heterogeneity was assessed by visually inspecting forest plots and using the I2 statistic, which represents the percentage of total variation across studies due to heterogeneity.23 Publication bias was evaluated using funnel plots for meta-analyses including at least 10 studies.24 Statistical significance was set at p<0.05 for all analyses.

Quality assessment

The risk of bias of the included studies was assessed using the appropriate Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklists tailored to each specific study design.25 Two reviewers (JD and SL) independently evaluated each study against the specific criteria outlined in the respective checklists. These criteria covered essential domains such as randomisation, comparability of groups, identification of confounding factors, reliability of outcome measurements, and appropriateness of statistical analysis. Each item was appraised as “Yes,” “No,” “Unclear,” or “Not Applicable.” Any discrepancies between the reviewers were resolved through discussion to reach a consensus.

Role of the funding source

The funders of the study had no role in study design, data collection, data analysis, data interpretation, or writing of the report.

Results

Study selection

A total of 1175 records were identified through the database search. After removal of 403 duplicates, 772 records remained for screening (Fig. 1). Following title and abstract screening, 664 records were excluded, leaving 108 full-text articles assessed for eligibility. A total of 56 studies were included in this review, comprising 15 vaccine studies and 41 mAbs studies. Among the 41 studies evaluating mAbs, there were five RCTs and 36 real-world observational studies. The observational studies consisted of 15 cohort studies, 20 case–control studies, of which 14 utilised a test-negative design (TND), and one prospective interventional program. Regarding the 15 vaccine studies, seven were RCTs and eight were observational studies, comprising seven TNDs and one cohort study using target trial emulation approach.

Study characteristics

The included studies represented a diverse range of designs conducted across multiple countries in North America, Europe, Asia, Oceania, and Latin America (Table S6). The research evaluated mAbs and vaccines in three distinct populations: infants, pregnant women, and older adults (aged ≥60 years) (Table S2). Paediatric studies focused on healthy term newborns, preterm infants, and children with high-risk comorbidities. Interventions included palivizumab (Synagis®) and nirsevimab (Beyfortus®). Maternal vaccine studies included RSVpreF (Abrysvo™) and RSVPreF3-Mat, which was terminated due to an identified preterm birth risk. Studies in older adults evaluated vaccines including RSVPreF3-OA (Arexvy™), RSVpreF (Abrysvo™), and mRNA-1345 (mRESVIA™). Detailed study-level characteristics, including design, sample size, population, dosing, and key outcomes, are summarised in supplementary material (Table S2).

Quality assessment

The majority of cohort (13/16), case–control (24/27), and randomised controlled trials (9/12) demonstrated a low risk of bias. Moderate risk of bias was identified in two cohort studies, one case–control and two RCTs. High risk of bias was observed in one cohort study, two case–control studies, one RCT, and the single quasi-experimental study included. The complete results of the critical appraisal are provided in the supplementary material (Table S3).

Effectiveness of palivizumab

Six studies evaluated the effectiveness of palivizumab against RSV-LRTI hospitalisations up to 180 days post-discharge (Fig. 2). In the OR-based analysis, the pooled effectiveness was approximately 81% (95% CI: 57%–91%) with low heterogeneity (I2=0.0%;τ2=0;p=0.817). The rate-ratio-based cohort analysis yielded a non-statistically significant pooled estimate of 34% (95% CI: −17%–63%), with low heterogeneity (I2=0.0%;τ2=0;p=0.481). In the OR-based analysis, the prediction interval ranges from −13% to 97%, and in the rate-ratio-based analysis, it ranges from −30% to 67%.

Efficacy/effectiveness of nirsevimab

Nirsevimab demonstrated high efficacy/effectiveness against RSV-associated LRTI hospitalisations (Fig. 3). In the OR-based analysis, the pooled efficacy/effectiveness was 84% (95% CI: 78%–88%) with moderate heterogeneity (I2=59.5%;τ2=0.230;p=0.0005). The case–control subgroup showed 85% effectiveness (95% CI: 79%–90%; I2=64.4%), while the RCT subgroup yielded 74% efficacy (95% CI: −744%–99%; I2=0%). In the rate-ratio-based analysis, the pooled efficacy/effectiveness was approximately 79% (95% CI: 72%–85%) with substantial heterogeneity (I2=83.6%;τ2=0.117;p<0.0001). Funnel plots indicate the presence of publication bias (Figure S4). Additionally, we evaluated the effectiveness of nirsevimab against all-cause respiratory hospitalisations, with overall pooled estimated effectiveness of 53% (95% CI: 40%–64%) (Figure S6).

We also conducted a series of meta-analyses to evaluate the efficacy/effectiveness of nirsevimab across four distinct clinical outcomes up to 180 days post-administration (Figure S1). The results demonstrated robust and high protection across evaluated endpoints. Specifically, pooled effectiveness was 76% (95% CI: 71%–81%) for general RSV-LRTI and 79% (95% CI: 68%–86%) for RSV-associated ED visits. Protection ranged from 70% (95% CI: 58%–78%) to 81% (95% CI: 64%–90%) against severe RSV-LRTI hospitalisations, and from 69% (95% CI: 19%–88%) to 84% (95% CI: 77%–88%) against ICU admissions. Furthermore, meta-regression indicated that geographical region was associated with RSV-LRTI hospitalisation estimates (p=0.013, R2=46.5%). Additionally, study design was associated with nirsevimab effectiveness against ICU admissions (p=0.039, R2=89.5%). No association was identified for nirsevimab against severe hospitalisations or ED visits.

Efficacy of maternal vaccination

Maternal RSV vaccination demonstrated a pooled efficacy/effectiveness of 67% (95% CI: 53%–77%; I2=0.0%;τ2=0;p=0.661) against RSV-LRTI hospitalisations in infants through 180 days after birth, with consistent results across RCT and case–control subgroups (Fig. 4). The pooled efficacy/effectiveness against severe RSV-LRTI hospitalisation in infants through 180 days after birth was 72% (95% CI: 61%–79%; I2=0.0%;τ2=0;p=0.871) (Figure S2). For RSV-LRTI, two RCTs yielded a non-statistically significant pooled estimate of 54% (95% CI: −245%–94%; I2=10.7%;τ2=0.008;p=0.290). The pooled vaccine effectiveness against all-cause respiratory hospitalisations through 6 months post-birth from the two RCTs is 31% (95% CI: 23%–39%) (Figure S6).

Efficacy/effectiveness of older adults vaccination

In older adults, first-season vaccine effectiveness against RSV-LRTI hospitalisations was 77% (95% CI: 69%–82%; I2=39.9%;τ2=0.008;p=0.156) in OR-based analysis (Fig. 5). This was heavily weighted by the Fry (2025) study (66.2% weight), which reported an effectiveness of 76% (95% CI: 73%–78%). Furthermore, the pooled second-season effectiveness against RSV-LRTI hospitalisation was 52% (95% CI: 39%–63%) (Figure S7).

Vaccination also provided consistent first-season protection across other clinical outcomes (Figure S3). The pooled vaccine effectiveness against RSV-LRTI was 78% (95% CI: 50%–90%; I2=0.0%;τ2=0.010;p=0.428); the OR-based RSV-LRTI analysis with two RCTs yielded a pooled efficacy of 74% with wide confidence interval (95% CI: −1374%–100%). Protection against RSV-associated ED visits was 76% (95% CI: 73%–79%; I2=0.0%;τ2=0;p=0.752). Effectiveness against RSV-ARI was 75% (95% CI: 72%–78%; I2=29.5%;τ2<0.0001;p=0.235).

Discussion

We evaluated the rapidly evolving landscape of RSV prevention by conducting a systematic review on 56 studies incorporating both clinical trials and post-market real-world evidence up to late 2025 across multiple countries. Our findings show that the introduction of next-generation mAbs and prefusion F protein-based vaccines could contribute to the reduction of RSV-related disease burden in specific populations.

The first-generation mAb palivizumab demonstrated variable effectiveness against RSV-LRTI hospitalisations up to 180 days post-discharge, with estimates diverging substantially by the type of effect measure used (OR: 81%; rate-ratio: 34%). Our 2018–2025 search window includes major nirsevimab trials but excludes the IMpact-RSV trial for palivizumab.6 Our pooled palivizumab estimates reflect real-world effectiveness (81% OR-based effectiveness) and are similar to the efficacy reported in the IMpact-RSV trial (55% overall; 78% in preterm infants without BPD/CHD born ≤35 wGA).6 Furthermore, trial data for nirsevimab in a comparable preterm population (29–35 wGA) demonstrated a similar efficacy of 78.4%.

The observed variance is likely attributable to methodological differences, population-level contexts, and implementation factors. Real-world studies demonstrate that effectiveness is highly dependent on adherence to multi-dose protocols, which can range from 1 to 8 doses depending on the length of the local RSV season and the infant's birth date and local healthcare access. Therefore, in settings with practical delivery obstacles or poor monthly dosing adherence, observed effectiveness is substantially attenuated, leading to the wide prediction intervals observed in our meta-analysis. Because these implementation challenges limit the real-world clinical impact of palivizumab, next-generation options like nirsevimab offer an implementation advantage due to its extended half-life and single-dose regimen.9

Nirsevimab demonstrated robust effectiveness of approximately 84% against RSV-LRTI hospitalisation through 150–180 days after administration, with consistent efficacy/effectiveness across severe RSV-LRTI hospitalisations, RSV-LRTI, and RSV-associated ICU admissions. Despite this overall protection, moderate to substantial heterogeneity was observed across nirsevimab studies, particularly for the RSV-LRTI hospitalisation outcomes. In meta-regression analyses, “Geographical Region” was associated with pooled effectiveness. For instance, slightly higher effectiveness in the US was likely influenced by 2023–2024 rollout dynamics, where supply shortages led to the prioritisation of high-risk infants, and delayed administration aligned peak antibody levels with peak RSV circulation.26 This suggests that the real-world effectiveness is influenced by regional differences in RSV epidemiology, circulating subtypes,27 local healthcare practices, including hospitalisation thresholds and rapid testing availability,28 and distinct national immunisation rollout strategies. Additionally, the heterogeneity could be driven by limited events. For example, wide confidence intervals for the RCT subgroup in the OR-based analysis were due to few observed cases resulting from pandemic-related disruptions.10 Similarly, estimates for ICU admissions and ED visits face methodological challenges. Rare ICU admissions are susceptible to confounding by indication, a scenario where the most vulnerable infants prioritised for immunisation inherently possess a higher baseline risk for severe disease, while ED visit estimates remain highly sensitive to local healthcare utilisation patterns and admission criteria. Despite this heterogeneity, prediction intervals indicated that nirsevimab is expected to remain protective across new or different clinical settings.

Although clesrovimab was not included in our meta-analysis, recent findings from the phase 2b/3 CLEVER trial demonstrate its substantial prophylactic potential.29 In a cohort of 3614 healthy preterm and full-term infants, a single dose of clesrovimab exhibited an efficacy of 60.4% (95% CI: 44.1%–71.9%) against RSV-associated medically attended lower respiratory infection and 84.2% (95% CI: 66.6%–92.6%) against RSV-associated hospitalisations over a 150-day period, with a safety profile comparable to placebo.

In addition to infant monoclonal antibodies, our meta-analysis provides evidence that maternal vaccination confers substantial protection against severe respiratory outcomes associated with RSV. The pooled efficacy/effectiveness through 180 days after birth against RSV-LRTI hospitalisations was 67%. Protection against severe RSV-LRTI hospitalisations was higher at 72%. This finding aligns with the biological plausibility of respiratory vaccines, which often ameliorate disease severity even when they do not fully prevent infection or mild symptoms.30 However, the wide confidence interval observed in our analysis of the general RSV-LRTI outcome suggested lower statistical power and variability in outcome definitions.

Although current data indicate a protective effect, monitoring the safety of maternal vaccination remains crucial. While the GSK trial was terminated due to a potential increased risk of preterm births (6.8% vs. 4.9%),31 a recent analysis of Pfizer's MATISSE trial reported no association with increased preterm birth risk for the RSVpreF vaccine.32 Global administration guidelines currently vary: the US CDC recommends a narrow vaccination window of 32–36 weeks' gestation,33 whereas the UK, WHO, and PAHO recommend starting earlier at 28 weeks34,35 to advocate for expanded vaccination windows to maximise maternal antibody transfer and population coverage; and in Brazil, although the vaccine was approved by its regulatory agency from 24 weeks of gestational age, the Ministry of Health recommends its use from 28 weeks.36 More real-world data and ongoing safety monitoring across diverse settings are essential to establish a safety profile for maternal RSV vaccination.

For older adults, the vaccines demonstrated high effectiveness against RSV-LRTI hospitalisations during the first RSV season following vaccination. Protection was similarly robust against RSV-LRTI, RSV-associated ED visits, and RSV-ARI. Modest heterogeneity was observed for RSV-LRTI hospitalisations and RSV-ARI. Several factors explain this discrepancy: The Surie study enrolled hospitalised patients with acute respiratory illness, a population with higher frailty, immuno-senescence or cardiovascular diseases, potentially blunting the immune response. Bajema et al. and DAN-RSV trial noted that while relative VE in CVD patients might be similar, their absolute risk reduction remains higher due to baseline morbidity.37,38 Furthermore, efficacy drops significantly in immunocompromised adults (∼29–44%).39,40

The variation in follow-up durations among the included studies reveals that a longer time after administration is associated with lower effectiveness. This time-dependent decline is evident in second-season follow-ups, where the overall vaccine effectiveness dropped to 52%. Finally, alongside durability concerns, safety surveillance by Fry et al. in a cohort of >4.7 million vaccinees detected a statistically significant, though rare, excess risk of Guillain-Barré Syndrome (GBS) associated with the RSVPreF (Pfizer) vaccine.40

While sensitivity analyses confirm the overall reliability of our estimates, the current evidence base is shaped by additional limitations. Most included studies were conducted in high-income countries, limiting the generalisability of these findings to low- and middle-income countries. Additionally, our 2018 search start date excluded older palivizumab efficacy trials while capturing recent nirsevimab RCTs and real-world data. Furthermore, follow-up for older adult vaccines is largely limited to a single RSV season, leaving the long-term durability of protection unclear.

Despite these limitations, current evidence establishes that the recent approval of the long-acting mAbs and prefusion F protein vaccines represents an improvement in RSV prevention. Our findings demonstrate that both palivizumab and nirsevimab, alongside maternal and older adult vaccines, provide robust protection against RSV-LRTI hospitalisations and severe disease. Future efforts should focus on long-term safety and effectiveness monitoring and ensuring equitable access in low- and middle-income countries to fully realise the global public health potential of these interventions and significantly reduce the burden of respiratory viral infections.
