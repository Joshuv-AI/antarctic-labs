Exploring the relationship between problem-solving skills and prosocial behaviors in adolescents in Turkey
Authors: Nurten Arslan, Aysel Topan, Meltem Kürtüncü
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aims to investigate the relationship between problem-solving skills, which are crucial for overcoming the challenges adolescents face in social interactions, and prosocial behaviors that reflect social adaptation and empathy. It is anticipated that the findings will pave the way for the development of effective educational programs to support adolescents’ social and emotional development.MethodThis descriptive, analytical and cross-sectional study was conducted with adolescents aged 10–14 years who were enrolled in secondary education institutions in the central district of a province in Turkey. No sampling procedure was employed; rather, the study included the entire accessible population. The sample consisted of 1,000 adolescents who voluntarily agreed to participate in the research. Data were collected through face-to-face interviews using the “Sociodemographic Questionnaire,” the “Problem Solving Inventory for Children,” and the “Adolescent Prosociality Scale.”ResultsThe mean age of the adolescents who participated in the study was 12.07 ± 1.274 years, and 27% of them were female. The total mean score for the Problem Solving Inventory was 74.20 ± 22.32, while the total mean score for the Prosociality Scale was 64.75 ± 12.85. A statistically significant positive correlation was observed between adolescents’ total problem-solving scores (r = 0.356) and confidence subscale scores (r = 0.484) with their prosociality scores (p < 0.001). Conversely, as the scores for the self-control (r = − 0.276) and avoidance (r = − 0.147) subscales increased, prosociality scores significantly decreased (p < 0.001).ConclusionAdolescents with higher problem-solving skills were found to exhibit a greater tendency toward prosocial behaviors, particularly when their sense of confidence was strong. In contrast, self-control and avoidance behaviors were found to have a negative influence on adolescents’ levels of prosociality.Keywords: Adolescent, Avoidance, Prosociality, Problem-solving skills, Self-control

Introductıon

Adolescence is a critical developmental stage characterized by significant cognitive, social, and emotional transformations [1]. Within this period, the development of problem-solving skills is foundational; it enables adolescents to navigate complex social environments and assume agency over their learning processes [1, 2]. Empirical evidence and meta-analytic findings consistently demonstrate that robust problem-solving skills are predictive of better academic performance, emotional balance, and successful social adaptation [3–5].

Problem solving and prosocial behavior are linked through a cognitive-behavioral process. Problem-solving skills function as a cognitive regulator; they allow the individual to process social cues, manage internal emotional responses, and evaluate potential consequences before acting [5–7]. Consequently, adolescents who exhibit higher problem-solving competence are more likely to translate these cognitive assessments into constructive social competence outcomes, specifically prosocial behaviors [7, 8].

Prosocial behavior is a broad concept encompassing actions that an individual voluntarily performs for the benefit of others without expecting a reward from them [9, 10]. In the literature, this concept is considered as an overarching structure that brings together a wide variety of social actions such as sharing, cooperating, comforting, and donating [5, 11]. The concept of helping behavior, which is often used synonymously with positive social behavior, actually represents a specific sub-dimension of prosocial behavior. While helping behavior focuses more on meeting a specific need of another or supporting them in solving a problem; positive social behavior expresses a much more inclusive attitude that considers social harmony and the well-being of others [6–9, 12]. In this study, the social interaction processes of adolescents were evaluated within the holistic and multidimensional nature of positive social behavior.

In addition to the primary variables of the study, demographic characteristics such as gender, chronic disease status, grade level, and maternal education level were included in the analyses. The literature emphasizes that problem-solving skills and prosocial behavioral tendencies are influenced by gender roles, academic experience gained through different grade levels, and familial factors—specifically maternal education—which serve as early-stage social support mechanisms [13, 14]. Furthermore, individual health conditions, such as chronic diseases, are known to impact psychological resilience and perceived self-efficacy [15]. Accordingly, these demographic variables were integrated into the analytical framework both to allow for comparisons with existing literature and to control for potential confounding effects in the primary analyses.

Conducting this study within the Turkish sample carries strategic importance. Turkey offers a unique sociocultural synthesis where Western individualistic values and collectivistic orientations coexist. The high demographic mobility of the province where the study was conducted also strengthened the study in terms of reflecting different cultural contexts.

Furthermore, prosocial behaviors and problem-solving approaches are not culture-independent; rather, they are significantly shaped by societal norms [5, 10]. Understanding how Turkish adolescents—within a culture that prioritizes group harmony and social solidarity—transform their problem-solving skills into prosocial outcomes fills a critical gap in testing universal theories within diverse cultural contexts.

A review of the literature reveals that different skills interact with each other during the developmental processes of adolescents. For example, a significant relationship has been found between adolescents’ problem-solving skills and their empathic tendencies [16], while the impact of problem-solving capacity on general social behaviors is also emphasized [17]. Furthermore, there are current studies examining the role of social problem-solving mechanisms on prosocial behaviors [18]. However, it is noteworthy that studies directly and comprehensively addressing adolescents’ problem-solving skills as a cognitive strategy and their prosocial behaviors—such as helping, sharing, and cooperating—are quite limited. The present study aims to address this gap by deeply examining the relationship between these two fundamental socio-cognitive structures within a Turkish sample. In doing so, the research builds upon a cognitive-behavioral framework, positing that problem-solving skills function as cognitive regulators that determine the quality of social interaction [8, 14, 18]. Since these skills modulate how adolescents process social cues and translate them into behavioral outcomes, it is theoretically expected that higher problem-solving competence would be associated with and predict greater prosocial action. To empirically validate this mechanism, the following research questions and hypotheses are proposed.

Research questions and hypotheses

RQ1: What is the relationship between adolescents’ problem-solving skills and prosocial behaviors?

H0: There is no significant relationship between adolescents’ problem-solving skills (confidence, self-control, avoidence) and prosocial behaviors.

H1: There is no significant relationship between adolescents’ problem-solving skills (confidence, self-control, avoidence) and prosocial behaviors.

RQ2: Do the sub-dimensions of problem-solving skills significantly predict prosocial behaviors?

H0: The sub-dimensions of problem-solving skills do not significantly predict prosocial behaviors.

H1: The sub-dimensions of problem-solving skills significantly predict prosocial behaviors.

Methods

Study design

This research was designed as a descriptive, analytical, and cross-sectional study. The use of a large sample in our study allowed for a cross-sectional design. The fact that the data collection instruments were proven reliable enabled the simultaneous examination of prosocial characteristics and problem-solving skills according to different sociodemographic characteristics. The research adhered to the guidelines set by the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE).

Setting

The study was conducted between February 1 and May 31, 2022, with secondary school students aged 10–14 in a province located in Turkey’s Western Black Sea region.

Sample and participants

The study population consisted of adolescents residing in the city center of a province in the Western Black Sea region of Turkey. The participation criteria were: being between 10 and 14 years old, not having any learning disabilities that would prevent participation in the study, speaking Turkish, and consenting to participate.

Sample size

According to data obtained from the Provincial Directorate of National Education, a total of 1,158 students aged 10–14 years were enrolled in secondary schools in the city center. The study did not involve sample size calculations; instead, the aim was to reach the population that was accessible and met the participation criteria. The research was conducted with 1,000 adolescents who met the inclusion criteria and voluntarily agreed to participate in the study. The sample’s representativeness of the population was 86%, indicating that more participants than the minimum calculated number were reached, and that the sample had a high representational capacity [19]. Since the study successfully captured nearly the entire accessible population rather than utilizing a complex, stratified, or clustered sampling design, a standard multiple linear regression model was directly applied to the unweighted dataset without requiring complex survey design adjustments.

Data collection tools

Data were collected using three instruments: the “Sociodemographic Form,” the “Problem Solving Inventory for Children (PSIC),” and the “Adolescent Prosociality Scale (APS).”

Sociodemographic form

This form consisted of 13 questions designed to assess the adolescents’ sociodemographic characteristics. It was developed by the researchers based on relevant literature (reference).

Problem Solving Inventory for Children (PSIC)

Developed by Serin, Serin, and Saygılı [20], the PSIC was designed to evaluate children’s self-perceptions of their problem-solving abilities. The scale consists of 24 items grouped under three factors: “Confidence in Problem Solving” (12 items), “Self-Control” (7 items), and “Avoidance” (5 items). It is a self-report instrument rated on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 to 5. The total score can range from 24 to 120, with higher scores indicating greater perceived competence in problem solving [20]. The Cronbach’s alpha reliability coefficient was 0.80 in the original study and 0.81 in the present sample. In our study, the alpha values of the sub-dimensions of the scale ranged from 0.75 to 0.83.

Adolescent Prosociality Scale (APS)

Developed by Ata and Arten [21], this scale consists of 20 items, nine of which are reverse-coded, rated on a five-point Likert scale ranging from “Does not describe me at all (1)” to “Describes me completely (5).” The APS comprises two subscales: ınternal prosociality and external prosociality. After reverse scoring, total scores can range from 20 to 100, with higher scores indicating higher levels of prosocial tendencies [21]. The Cronbach’s alpha reliability coefficient for the original study was 0.80, while it was calculated as 0.75 for this sample. The alpha value for the Internal Prosociality subdimension is 0.84, and the alpha value for the External Prosociality subdimension is 0.74.

Data collection

Before administering the questionnaire, informed consent forms were distributed to families through the students. These forms explained the purpose of the study, confidentiality of the data, and participants’ right to withdraw at any time. Only adolescents whose parents signed the informed consent forms were included in the study. In addition, both written and verbal assent were obtained from all adolescent participants. The questionnaire was administered at school during non-class hours to avoid disrupting lessons. The average completion time was approximately 15 min.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using the SPSS 22.0 statistical software package. Descriptive statistical methods, including frequency, percentage, mean, and standard deviation, were used to summarize the data. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was applied to assess normality.

For comparisons between two groups, the Independent Samples t-test was used, while comparisons among more than two groups were performed using one-way ANOVA, followed by the Tukey test to identify the source of differences. For non-normally distributed data, non-parametric tests the Kruskal–Wallis H test were employed. Correlation and multiple regression analyses were conducted to examine relationships between variables. Since the variables were found to exhibit a normal distribution, Pearson Correlation Analysis was applied to determine the relationships between the variables. Subsequently, Multiple Regression Analysis, a parametric test, was performed to determine the predictive power of problem-solving skills on positive social behaviors. In the structured multiple linear regression model, the total score of the Adolescent Prosociality Scale (APS) was defined as the dependent variable. The three sub-dimensions of the Problem Solving Inventory for Children (PSIC)—namely Confidence, Self-control, and Avoidance—were entered into the model simultaneously as the independent predictors. Before conducting the multiple regression analysis, its core assumptions were strictly verified. Linearity was confirmed via bivariate scatter plots. The normality of residuals was verified using normal P-P plots and histograms, and homoscedasticity was confirmed via residual scatterplots. The Durbin-Watson statistic (DW = 1.70) ensured the independence of observations. Multicollinearity was ruled out as all Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) values were well below 5 and tolerance values. A VIF value of 2.30 and a tolerance value greater than 0.10 were established as the thresholds for the absence of significant multicollinearity. Cook’s distance values remained below 1.0, confirming the absence of influential outliers. While the large sample size (N = 1,000) and acceptable skewness/kurtosis limits ( < ± 1.5) supported the initial parametric steps, the validation of these residual diagnostics mathematically ensured the robustness of the regression estimation. Missing data were handled using the listwise deletion method during the regression analyses, and cases with incomplete responses were excluded from the model [19]. Reliability of the scales in the sample was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha. Statistical significance was accepted at p < 0.05.

Preliminary and descriptive analyses

The demographic distribution of the students participating in the study is presented in Table 1. Of the adolescents who participated in the study, 51.8% were male, and their mean age was 12.07 ± 1.274 years. When the distribution of participants according to their grade level is examined, it is seen that 27.7% are in 7th grade and 26.9% are in 8th grade. When the education levels of the adolescents’ mothers are examined, it was determined that the highest rate is middle school with 31.5% and the lowest rate is primary school with 12.9%. In addition, it was determined that 12.2% of the students have a chronic disease (Table 1).

When the scores obtained from the Problem Solving Inventory for Children were examined, the adolescents’ mean score for the Confidence subscale was 40.17 ± 10.47; the Self-Control subscale mean score was 21.64 ± 6.82; the Avoidance subscale mean score was 12.39 ± 5.03; and the total mean score of the inventory was 74.20 ± 22.32.

Regarding the Adolescent Prosociality Scale, the mean score for the Internal Prosociality subscale was 39.41 ± 9.84; the External Prosociality subscale mean score was 25.34 ± 7.60; and the total mean score of the scale was 64.75 ± 12.85. Overall, adolescents’ problem-solving and prosociality scores were at a moderate level (Table 2).

When comparing the scales and their sub-dimensions with the descriptive variables in Table 4, the females’ APS total score (2,091), APS internal prosocial sub-dimension scores (2.962), PSIC total score (3.889) and PSIC self-control sub-dimension scores (3.320) were found to be significantly higher than those of males (p < 0.01).

The APS sub-dimension scores of external prosocial (2.715) and the PSIC sub-dimension scores of avoidance (3,337) of the adolescents with chronic disease were found to be significantly higher than those without a chronic disease (p < 0.05).

The 5th and 6th graders’ total scale score of APS (KW = 17.324), external prosocial sub-dimension of APS (KW = 17.360), total scale score of PSIC (KW = 15.935), confidence sub-dimension of PSIC (F = 3.555), self-control sub-dimension of PSIC (KW = 17.785) and avoidance sub-dimension of PSIC mean scores (KW = 3.853) were higher than those of 8th graders.

When the mother’s education level was compared with the scales and their sub-dimensions, the APS external prosocial sub-dimension scores (KW = 24.329) of the mothers who were graduates of primary, secondary and high school were significantly higher than those of university graduates. The APS internal prosocial sub-dimension scores (KW = 14.665) of the mothers who were graduates of high school were significantly higher than those of primary and secondary graduates (Table 3).

Hypothesis testing: relationships and predictors of prosocial behavior

When the relationships between the Problem Solving Inventory for Children, the Adolescent Prosociality Scale, and their subdimensions were analyzed, it was found that as adolescents’ problem-solving scores increased (r = 0.356) and particularly their confidence subscale scores increased (r = 0.484), their prosociality scores also increased significantly (p < 0.001).

Conversely, increases in the Self-Control subscale (r = − 0.276) and the Avoidance subscale (r = − 0.147) were associated with significant decreases in prosociality scores (p < 0.001) (Table 4).

The results of a multiple regression analysis conducted to identify factors predicting prosocial behavior in adolescents were examined. The analysis revealed that the established regression model was statistically significant. The sub-dimensions of problem-solving skills explain 31.2% of the total variance in prosocial behaviors (β = 1.713; R = 0.559; R2 = 0.312; F = 150.67; p < 0.001) Confidence in problem-solving skills is the strongest and most positive predictor of prosocial behavior. Self-control negatively predicts prosocial behavior, while avoidance predicts it to a lesser extent but positively (Table 5).

Discussion

Evaluation of preliminary and descriptive findings

This study examined the relationship between the perceived problem-solving styles of adolescents in Turkey and their prosocial behavior tendencies, revealing the determining role of cognitive processes on social outcomes. The total mean score for problem-solving obtained in the study (74.20 ± 22.32) indicates that adolescents generally possess a moderate-to-high level of problem-solving capacity, considering that high scores on this scale signify positive and effective problem-solving styles. This finding is consistent with literature stating that while adolescents’ problem-solving skills are developing, they have not yet fully matured to adult levels [5, 22–24]. This situation can be interpreted as the identity search, emotional fluctuations, and complex social relationships brought about by the adolescent period temporarily challenging an individual’s capacity to cope effectively with problems.

When the sub-dimension scores are examined, the high mean score obtained from the Confidence sub-dimension indicates that adolescents have a strong belief in themselves regarding problem-solving. This finding is an expected result, particularly in this period where adolescents’ beliefs regarding their own competence increase, and it supports studies reporting similarly high confidence scores [25–28]. Therefore, it is thought that confidence in problem-solving may form the basis for the individual to demonstrate the social courage and conviction required for prosocial behavior.

Considering that high scores on the scale represent structured and good Self-Control, the negative relationship found between self-control scores and prosociality in our study is remarkable. This relationship suggests that the highly controlled behaviors exhibited by adolescents during the problem-solving process may lead to a more distant, calculated attitude or an orientation toward their own internal processes, rather than spontaneous, empathy-oriented, and sincere helping behaviors in social situations. The results of a study conducted within the Turkish culture also support the positive relationship between empathy and problem-solving skills [27]. In other words, it can be interpreted that at moments when adolescents focus on controlling their immediate impulses, they may find it difficult to reflect the spontaneous empathy required to exhibit prosocial behavior [3, 6, 28]. Indeed, multiple regression analysis results also support that the control sub-dimension negatively predicts prosociality.

The fact that adolescents’ Internal Prosociality scores are significantly higher than their External Prosociality scores indicates that adolescents in Turkey act based on internalized moral values and empathy rather than social approval or rewards [27, 28]. This finding can be associated with the collectivist structure of Turkish culture and its norms that view helping as a virtue [25, 29]. When analyzed by gender, the significantly higher internal prosociality scores of female adolescents compared to males support the findings in the literature regarding women’s higher tendency toward empathy.

In the study, high scores in the Avoidance sub-dimension indicate that adolescents possess the skills to face problems directly; however, it should be noted that in some cases, this may result in an overly analytical approach or avoidance of social interactions. This finding shows similarity with the existing literature [30, 31]. Consequently, adolescents with high self-confidence in problem-solving may struggle with the planning and impulse control skills necessary to translate this confidence into action.

The research findings present a remarkable profile consistent with the literature regarding adolescents’ prosociality scores. The total prosociality score obtained indicates that adolescents generally have a moderate-to-high level of prosocial tendency. This finding aligns with the developmental fact that adolescence is a stage where individuals move away from egocentrism and peer relationships and social approval gain importance. Similarly, the literature states that adolescents may exhibit prosocial behaviors due to the need for social acceptance and belonging [32, 33].

Discussion on hypothesis testing: relationships and predictors of prosocial behavior

The correlation analysis results of the study statistically demonstrate the theoretical link between problem-solving styles and prosocial behaviors. A moderately significant positive correlation was found between adolescents’ perception of confidence in the problem-solving process and total prosociality (r = 0.489; p < 0.01). This proves that an individual’s belief in their own competence is a fundamental driving force in transforming into helping behavior, which requires social risk and responsibility. Indeed, the regression analysis results also revealed that the Confidence sub-dimension is the variable that most strongly and positively predicts prosocial behavior (β = 0.496; t = 180.494; p < 0.001).

This finding fully overlaps with the views in the literature that individuals with effective problem-solving skills can exhibit more harmonious and constructive behaviors in social interactions [24, 27]. In a study by Schoeps et al. [30], it is stated that behavioral problems, coping with issues, peer relationships, and empathy skills influence the level of prosociality in adolescents [30].

On the other hand, one of the most original findings of the study is the negative relationship between the Self-Control dimension, where a high score represents structured and good control, and prosociality (r = -0.276; p < 0.01). In the regression model, this sub-dimension also significantly predicts prosocial behavior negatively (β = -0.198; t = -60.194; p < 0.001). This statistical result supports the hypothesis that when adolescents focus their cognitive resources on controlling their impulses and avoiding mistakes, they have difficulty exhibiting a spontaneous and empathy-based orientation towards others. These findings are also reported in the literature in studies by González Moreno and Molero Jurado and Katona et al. [18, 34].

Our study shows that the established regression model explains 31.2% of the total variance (R2 = 0.312; F = 150.67; p < 0.001) and that the model is hypothetically robust (DW = 1.70; VIF = 2.30). This high explanatory power provides a scientific basis for the idea that positive social behaviors in adolescents are not merely an emotional preference but are directly related to higher-level cognitive processes such as problem-solving.

In conclusion, the findings of this study strongly support that prosocial behavior in adolescents is not merely an emotional tendency but also has a cognitive and strategic foundation. While confidence in problem-solving is a resource that fuels prosocial action, excessive control and avoidance emerge as cognitive barriers to this action. A study by Wenchao & Xinchun indicates that adolescents’ empathy skills influence problem-solving and prosociality, and as problem-solving skills increase, prosocial behaviors also increase [35]. Therefore, intervention programs aimed at increasing adolescents’ prosocial behaviors and general social adjustment should not be limited to empathy training alone; they must also include components that strengthen their problem-solving skills, especially their self-confidence, and help them manage their control mechanisms.

Furthermore, based on the motivational behavior classification system proposed by Ahmadi et al., designing intervention programs that transform controlled behaviors into prosocial outcomes will be a critical step in ensuring the permanence of prosocial behaviors [36]. To increase the generalizability of the study, the use of longitudinal or multi-informant designs in future research is recommended.

Limitation

This study has some limitations in terms of the interpretation and generalizability of the findings. One of these limitations is the sample size. The study is limited to adolescents aged 10–14 studying in a central region of one province in Türkiye. This restricts the generalizability of the findings to adolescent populations throughout the country or in different socio-cultural contexts. Therefore, it is recommended that this research be conducted with a larger population to represent all regions of the country. Another limitation of the study is that the data were collected through self-report. Since the data were collected through scales based on the adolescents’ own perceptions, the responses may contain biases stemming from social desirability tendencies or cognitive/emotional states. This is a limitation arising from the fact that behaviors were not measured through objective observation. In order to increase the reliability of the research and reduce potential biases, participants were guaranteed complete confidentiality within the framework of the research’s ethical principles, and they were informed in advance that the data collection tools would not cause stress or anxiety. Taking these limitations into account in future research will allow for the acquisition of more comprehensive and reliable information on the subject. Finally, this study did not include potential socio-demographic confounding variables (such as gender, exact age, or socio-economic status) directly into the multiple regression model as control variables. This approach limits our ability to draw fully adjusted predictive inferences, as these unmonitored confounders might partially interact with both problem-solving skills and prosocial tendencies in early adolescence. However, it is worth noting that the direct psychological model tested here still explained 31.2% of the total variance (R2 = 0.312), indicating a robust and meaningful direct relationship. Future research should utilize hierarchical regression models to control for these socio-demographic confounders alongside psychological constructs.

Conclusion

This study determined that adolescents’ problem-solving styles are significantly associated with their prosocial behaviors. Adolescents with higher levels of perceived problem-solving confidence were found to display a greater tendency to engage in prosocial behaviors, predominantly driven by internal motivations. Conversely, higher levels of self-control (indicating structured regulation) and avoidance behaviors were identified as factors associated with lower levels of prosociality in this context.

These findings highlight the importance of developing intervention programs based on structured classification systems, such as the one proposed by Ahmadi et al. [36], to enhance adolescents’ problem-solving competencies—particularly those that foster confidence and manage the relationship between self-regulation and prosocial outcomes. Given the collectivist undertones of Turkish culture, these programs should focus on transforming controlled behaviors into genuinely internalized, rather than transactional, prosocial actions. Supporting adolescents in acquiring these skills can contribute to their social adjustment, emotional well-being, and overall positive development.
