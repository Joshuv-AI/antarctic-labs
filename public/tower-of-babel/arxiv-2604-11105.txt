Abstract
We propose Nesterov acceleration with Operator Decomposition (NOD), which extends Nesterov’s accelerated gradient descent (NAG) from smooth strongly convex optimization to the broader setting of strongly monotone, Lipschitz operators. The key insight is to decompose the operator into cyclically monotone and monotone components, with the Asplund decomposition providing the tightest such representation, and to have the algorithm utilize the decomposed oracles. NOD and its analysis subsume the classical theory of Nesterov acceleration and yield an iteration complexity for finding an ϵ \epsilon -accurate solution of Θ ⁡ ( L ϕ μ + L 𝕊 2 μ 2 ​ log ⁡ 1 ϵ ) , \Theta\left(\sqrt{\frac{L_{\phi}}{\mu}+\frac{L_{{\mathbb{S}}}^{2}}{\mu^{2}}}\,\log\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right), where μ \mu is the strong monotonicity parameter, L ϕ L_{\phi} is the Lipschitz constant of the cyclically monotone component, and L 𝕊 L_{{\mathbb{S}}} is the Lipschitz constant of the (possibly acyclic) monotone remainder.



1
Introduction

Consider the monotone inclusion problem

\displaystyle\underset{{\bm{z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}{\mathrm{find}}\quad{\mathbb{T}}({\bm{z}})=\bm{0},

where
{\mathbb{T}}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a maximal, strongly monotone, and Lipschitz operator.
This general problem class subsumes the widely studied classes of minimization problems,

\displaystyle\min_{{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}f({\bm{x}}),

where
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is strongly convex and has Lipschitz gradients,
and minimax optimization problems,

\displaystyle\min_{{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}}\max_{{\bm{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}}\mathcal{L}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}),

where
\mathcal{L}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}\to\mathbb{R}
is strongly-convex-strongly-concave (SCSC) and has Lipschitz gradients.
Finding the optimal point of the minimization problem is equivalent to finding a zero of
{\mathbb{T}}({\bm{x}})=\nabla f({\bm{x}})
, and finding the (pure) Nash equilibrium of the minimax optimization problem is equivalent to finding a zero of the saddle operator

{\mathbb{T}}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})=\begin{bmatrix}\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\mathcal{L}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})\\
-\nabla_{{\bm{y}}}\mathcal{L}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})\end{bmatrix}.

In 1970, Asplund
[
1
]
showed that a single-valued maximal monotone operator
{\mathbb{T}}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
admits an
Asplund decomposition
of the form

\displaystyle{\mathbb{T}}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\partial\phi}_{\text{cyclically monotone}}+\underbrace{{\mathbb{S}}}_{\text{acyclic monotone}}

where
\partial\phi
is a cyclically monotone operator, i.e., the subdifferential operator of a convex function
\phi\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

[
11
]
, and
{\mathbb{S}}
is an
acyclic monotone
operator, which is a monotone operator with only affine cyclically monotone components.
The canonical example is the saddle gradient operator of a smooth convex-concave saddle function with bilinear coupling
\mathcal{L}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})=g({\bm{x}})+{\bm{x}}^{\top}{\bm{M}}{\bm{y}}-h({\bm{y}})
, for which we have
\phi({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})=g({\bm{x}})+h({\bm{y}})
and
{\mathbb{S}}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})=[{\bm{M}}{\bm{y}};\ {-{\bm{M}}^{\top}{\bm{x}}}]
(as discussed further in Example
2.5
).

However, the Asplund decomposition can also admit non-bilinear coupling terms.
Specifically, consider
\mathcal{L}\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(x,y)=x^{2}-y^{2}+\sin x\sin y,

which is SCSC and has Lipschitz gradients. In
Theorem

2.4
, we characterize its Asplund decomposition and show that the associated acyclic monotone coupling term is non-bilinear. To the best of our knowledge, this provides the first example of a saddle operator whose Asplund decomposition features a non-skew-linear coupling term.

In this work, we introduce
N
esterov acceleration with
O
perator
D
ecomposition (NOD), which extends Nesterov’s accelerated gradient descent (NAG) to monotone inclusion problems. In particular, NOD utilizes operator decomposition into cyclically monotone and monotone components, with the Asplund decomposition serving as the tightest such decomposition.

NOD achieves accelerated convergence rates for strongly monotone-smooth inclusion problems, including SCSC minimax optimization problems. The resulting rates are analogous to recent results under bilinear coupling (BC)
[
4
,
5
,
6
]
.

2
Preliminaries

We quickly introduce some standard definitions and notations related to convex functions, monotone operators, and operator decompositions.
Let
\|\cdot\|
be the standard Euclidean norm for vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and the operator norm for matrices in
\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
.
For functions and operators, we follow the standard notation of the field
[
13
]
.

2.1
Functions

We say that a function
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
is convex if

\displaystyle f(\lambda{\bm{z}}+(1-\lambda){\bm{z}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leq\lambda f({\bm{z}})+(1-\lambda)f({\bm{z}}^{\prime}),\qquad\forall\,{\bm{z}},{\bm{z}}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\,\lambda\in[0,1].

We say that
f
is
\mu
-strongly convex for
\mu>0
if
f({\bm{z}})-\frac{\mu}{2}\lVert{\bm{z}}\rVert^{2}
is convex.
We say that
f
is (
\mu
-strongly) concave if
-f
is (
\mu
-strongly) convex.

We say that
{\bm{g}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a subgradient of a convex function
f
at
{\bm{z}}
if

\displaystyle f({\bm{z}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\geq f({\bm{z}})+\left\langle{\bm{g}},{\bm{z}}^{\prime}-{\bm{z}}\right\rangle,\qquad\forall\,{\bm{z}}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

and the subdifferential
\partial f
at each point
{\bm{z}}
is defined as the collection of subgradients.

A function is closed if the epigraph

\operatorname{epi}f=\left\{(x,s)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}:f(x)\leq s\right\}

is a closed set.
A function is proper if its value is never
-\infty
and is finite somewhere.
We will often focus on convex functions that are closed and proper and refer to those as CCP functions.
It is well known that a proper function is closed if and only if it is lower semicontinuous, and that the subdifferential of a CCP function is always maximal monotone
[
8
,
9
]
.
It is also known that if
f
is CCP, then for all
{\bm{z}}\in\operatorname{int}\operatorname{dom}f
,
\partial f({\bm{z}})
is a singleton iff
f
is differentiable at
z
(Theorem 25.1,
[
12
]
).
Our main results will focus on
L
-smooth CCP functions, which we define below, for which
\operatorname{dom}f=\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
f
is differentiable, and thus
\partial f({\bm{z}})=\{\nabla f({\bm{z}})\}
is a singleton for all
{\bm{z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

We say that
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is
L
-smooth for
L>0
if it is differentiable and
\nabla f
is
L
-Lipschitz, i.e.,

\displaystyle\left\lVert\nabla f({\bm{z}})-\nabla f({\bm{z}}^{\prime})\right\rVert\leq L\left\lVert{\bm{z}}-{\bm{z}}^{\prime}\right\rVert,\qquad\forall\,{\bm{z}},{\bm{z}}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Furthermore, if
f
is differentiable, the definition of
\mu
-strong convexity becomes equivalent to

\displaystyle f({\bm{z}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\geq f({\bm{z}})+\left\langle\nabla f({\bm{z}}),{\bm{z}}^{\prime}-{\bm{z}}\right\rangle+\frac{\mu}{2}\lVert{\bm{z}}^{\prime}-{\bm{z}}\rVert^{2},\qquad\forall\,{\bm{z}},{\bm{z}}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

and a convex function
f
is
L
-smooth if and only if

\displaystyle f({\bm{z}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leq f({\bm{z}})+\left\langle\nabla f({\bm{z}}),{\bm{z}}^{\prime}-{\bm{z}}\right\rangle+\frac{L}{2}\lVert{\bm{z}}^{\prime}-{\bm{z}}\rVert^{2},\qquad\forall\,{\bm{z}},{\bm{z}}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

For (unconstrained) minimax optimization problems, we write
{\bm{z}}=({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}
for
{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}
and
{\bm{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}
.
We say that a function
\mathcal{L}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is
(\mu_{x},\mu_{y})
-strongly-convex-strongly-concave (SCSC) for
\mu_{x},\mu_{y}>0
if
\mathcal{L}(\cdot,{\bm{y}})
is
\mu_{x}
-strongly convex for all
{\bm{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}
and
\mathcal{L}({\bm{x}},\cdot)
is
\mu_{y}
-strongly concave for all
{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}
.
Also, we say that
\mathcal{L}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})
is
(L_{x},L_{y},L_{xy})
-smooth if
\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\mathcal{L}(\cdot,{\bm{y}})
is
L_{x}
-Lipschitz for all
{\bm{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}
,
\nabla_{{\bm{y}}}\mathcal{L}({\bm{x}},\cdot)
is
L_{y}
-Lipschitz for all
{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}
, and

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \|\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\mathcal{L}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}^{\prime})-\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\mathcal{L}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})\|&\leq L_{xy}\|{\bm{y}}^{\prime}-{\bm{y}}\|\\
\|\nabla_{{\bm{y}}}\mathcal{L}({\bm{x}}^{\prime},{\bm{y}})-\nabla_{{\bm{y}}}\mathcal{L}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})\|&\leq L_{xy}\|{\bm{x}}^{\prime}-{\bm{x}}\|\end{aligned}\qquad\quad\forall\,{\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}},\,{\bm{y}},{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}.

We say
\mathcal{L}
is
\mu
-SCSC if it is
(\mu,\mu)
-SCSC and
L
-smooth if the saddle operator is
L
-Lipschitz in
\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}
.
An
(L_{x},L_{y},L_{xy})
-smooth function is
L
-smooth for

\displaystyle L=\left\|\begin{bmatrix}L_{x}&L_{xy}\\
L_{xy}&L_{y}\end{bmatrix}\right\|.

2.2
Operators

A set-valued operator
{\mathbb{T}}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{d}
maps each point
{\bm{z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
to a set
{\mathbb{T}}({\bm{z}})\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
The domain of an operator
{\mathbb{T}}
is defined as
\mathrm{dom}\,{\mathbb{T}}=\{{\bm{z}}:{\mathbb{T}}({\bm{z}})\neq\emptyset\}
, and
{\mathbb{T}}
is of full domain if
\mathrm{dom}\,{\mathbb{T}}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
We say
{\mathbb{T}}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{d}
is monotone if

\displaystyle\langle{\bm{w}}-{\bm{w}}^{\prime},{\bm{z}}-{\bm{z}}^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle\geq 0,\qquad\forall\,{\bm{z}},{\bm{z}}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\,{\bm{w}}\in{\mathbb{T}}({\bm{z}}),\,{\bm{w}}^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{T}}({\bm{z}}^{\prime}).

We say
{\mathbb{T}}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{d}
is maximal monotone if its graph has no proper monotone extension.
We say
{\mathbb{T}}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{d}
is
\mu
-strongly monotone if
\mu>0
and

\displaystyle\langle{\bm{w}}-{\bm{w}}^{\prime},{\bm{z}}-{\bm{z}}^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle\geq\mu\lVert{\bm{z}}-{\bm{z}}^{\prime}\rVert^{2},\qquad\forall\,{\bm{z}},{\bm{z}}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\,{\bm{w}}\in{\mathbb{T}}({\bm{z}}),\,{\bm{w}}^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{T}}({\bm{z}}^{\prime}).

We say
{\mathbb{T}}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{d}
is
L
-Lipschitz if
L>0
and

\displaystyle\lVert{\bm{w}}-{\bm{w}}^{\prime}\rVert

\displaystyle\leq L\lVert{\bm{z}}-{\bm{z}}^{\prime}\rVert,\qquad\forall\,{\bm{z}},{\bm{z}}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\,{\bm{w}}\in{\mathbb{T}}({\bm{z}}),\,{\bm{w}}^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{T}}({\bm{z}}^{\prime}).

In order for a possibly set-valued operator of full domain to be
L
-Lipschitz, it must be single-valued, i.e.,
{\mathbb{T}}({\bm{z}})
must be a singleton for all
{\bm{z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
We write
{\mathbb{T}}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
{\mathbb{T}}({\bm{z}})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
for single-valued operators with a slight abuse of notation.
If an operator
{\mathbb{S}}
is Lipschitz, we will sometimes write
L_{{\mathbb{S}}}
to denote its Lipschitz constant.

If
{\mathbb{T}}
is maximal strongly monotone, then it must have a unique zero
{\bm{z}}_{\star}
by the Minty surjectivity theorem
[
3
,
7
]
.
This includes the case when
{\mathbb{T}}
is the saddle gradient of a SCSC function defined on
\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}
, which is single-valued, continuous, and of full domain, which implies that
{\mathbb{T}}
is maximal
[
3
]
.
Our main results will focus on maximal strongly monotone, Lipschitz operators
{\mathbb{T}}
of full domain.

2.3
Operator decompositions

Now we formally define
acyclic monotone
operators and the Asplund decomposition of monotone operators.

Definition 2.1
.

We say that a monotone operator
{\mathbb{S}}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{d}
is
acyclic monotone
if whenever
{\mathbb{S}}=\partial k+{\mathbb{A}}
for some CCP
k\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
and monotone
{\mathbb{A}}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then
k
is affine on
\operatorname{dom}{\mathbb{S}}
.

The
+
operation between operators is defined as Minkowski sums, i.e., the operator
{\mathbb{A}}+{\mathbb{B}}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
({\mathbb{A}}+{\mathbb{B}})({\bm{z}})=\{{\bm{a}}+{\bm{b}}:{\bm{a}}\in{\mathbb{A}}({\bm{z}}),\,{\bm{b}}\in{\mathbb{B}}({\bm{z}})\}
.

Definition 2.2
.

For a maximal monotone
{\mathbb{T}}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{d}
, an
Asplund decomposition
is a decomposition of the form
{\mathbb{T}}=\partial\phi+{\mathbb{S}}
such that
\phi\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
is CCP and
{\mathbb{S}}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{d}
is acyclic monotone.

Theorem 2.3

(Theorem 1,
[
1
]
)
.

For any single-valued, maximal monotone operator
{\mathbb{T}}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\operatorname{int}\operatorname{dom}{\mathbb{T}}\neq\emptyset
, an
Asplund decomposition
exists.

If
{\mathbb{T}}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is maximal monotone and
L
-Lipschitz for some
L<\infty
and we have the decomposition (not necessarily Asplund)

{\mathbb{T}}=\partial\phi+{\mathbb{S}}

with CCP
\phi\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
and maximal monotone
{\mathbb{S}}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we can show that
\phi
is, in fact,
L
-smooth and thus differentiable (as we show in
Lemma

A.1
in the appendix), and
{\mathbb{S}}
is
2L
-Lipschitz as it is the difference of two
L
-Lipschitz operators.
If we further assume that
{\mathbb{T}}
is
\mu
-strongly monotone, then there is an Asplund decomposition
{\mathbb{T}}=\partial\phi+{\mathbb{S}}
such that
\phi
is
\mu
-strongly convex, obtained by finding an Asplund decomposition of
{\mathbb{T}}-\mu\cdot\mathrm{id}
, where
\mathrm{id}
is the identity operator.

We note that the proof of
Theorem

2.3
is based on the application of Zorn’s lemma, and thus is non-constructive.
To the best of our knowledge, there is no known nontrivial class of monotone or saddle operators for which the Asplund decomposition can be written explicitly, beyond saddle operators induced by convex–concave functions
with bilinear coupling
.

Interestingly, we identify a saddle operator outside this class whose Asplund decomposition admits a closed-form expression.

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
\mathcal{L}\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(x,y)

\displaystyle=x^{2}-y^{2}+\sin x\sin y.

Then, the saddle gradient of
{\mathcal{L}}
,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{T}}(x,y)=\begin{bmatrix}\partial_{x}\mathcal{L}(x,y)\\
-\partial_{y}\mathcal{L}(x,y)\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}2x+\cos x\sin y\\
2y-\sin x\cos y\end{bmatrix},

has an Asplund decomposition of the form
{\mathbb{T}}=\nabla\phi+{\mathbb{S}}
, where

\displaystyle\phi(x,y)

\displaystyle:=h(x)+h(y),\qquad\qquad h(w):=\int_{0}^{w}\int_{0}^{t}(2-\lvert\sin s\rvert)\,\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}t,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{S}}(x,y)

\displaystyle:={\mathbb{T}}(x,y)-\nabla\phi(x,y).

We defer the proof of
Theorem

2.4
to
Appendix

A
.
The proof does not utilize any high-level operator-theoretic abstractions, but is technically involved. We believe there is room to further develop operator structure theory that would simplify such questions, but we leave this direction for future work.

Example 2.5

(Saddle gradients)
.

Suppose that
{\bm{z}}=({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}
for
{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}
and
{\bm{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}
, and
\mathcal{L}
is an SCSC, smooth function with
bilinear coupling
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})=g({\bm{x}})-h({\bm{y}})+{\bm{x}}^{\top}{\bm{M}}{\bm{y}}.

Then we can decompose the saddle gradient of
\mathcal{L}
, defined by

\displaystyle{\mathbb{T}}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})=\begin{bmatrix}\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\mathcal{L}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})\\
-\nabla_{{\bm{y}}}\mathcal{L}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})\end{bmatrix},

into
{\mathbb{T}}=\nabla\phi+{\mathbb{S}}
with the following components,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \phi({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})&=g({\bm{x}})+h({\bm{y}}),\\
{\mathbb{S}}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})&=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{0}&{\bm{M}}\\
-{\bm{M}}^{\top}&\bm{0}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}{\bm{x}}\\
{\bm{y}}\end{bmatrix}.\end{aligned}

We can observe that
\phi
is strongly convex and
{\mathbb{S}}
is monotone.
In particular,
{\mathbb{S}}
is a
skew linear operator, which is known to be acyclic monotone
[
2
]
.
Furthermore, if
g
is
\mu_{x}
-strongly convex and
L_{x}
-smooth,
h
is
\mu_{y}
-strongly convex and
L_{y}
-smooth, and
{\bm{M}}
has bounded spectral norm
\|{\bm{M}}\|\leq L_{xy}
, then
\mathcal{L}
is
(\mu_{x},\mu_{y})
-SCSC and
(L_{x},L_{y},L_{xy})
-smooth.

3
ODE formulation

Before we propose and analyze the (discrete-time) algorithm NOD, we present a motivating continuous-time formulation:

\displaystyle\ddot{{\mathbf{z}}}_{t}+2\sqrt{\mu}\dot{{\mathbf{z}}}_{t}+\nabla\phi({\mathbf{z}}_{t})+{\mathbb{S}}\left({\mathbf{z}}_{t}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu}}\dot{{\mathbf{z}}}_{t}\right)=\bm{0}

(1)

for
t>0
.
(We use the notation
{\mathbf{z}}_{t}
for continuous-time flows and
{\bm{z}}_{k}
for discrete-time algorithms.)
If
{\mathbb{S}}=0
, then (
1
) recovers the continuous-time ODE of NAG
[
14
]
for a strongly convex function
\phi
.
We show that the ODE in (
1
) enjoys exponential stability.

We assume that there exists a unique
{\bm{z}}_{\star}
such that

\nabla\phi({\bm{z}}_{\star})+{\mathbb{S}}({\bm{z}}_{\star})=\bm{0},

which holds when
{\mathbb{T}}=\nabla\phi+{\mathbb{S}}
is a maximal strongly monotone operator.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\phi\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a differentiable and
\mu
-strongly convex function and
{\mathbb{S}}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a (single-valued and) monotone operator.
Let
\{{\mathbf{z}}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
be a solution of the ODE (
1
) and
{\bm{z}}_{\star}
be the (unique) point satisfying
\nabla\phi({\bm{z}}_{\star})+{\mathbb{S}}({\bm{z}}_{\star})=\bm{0}
.
Then, the Lyapunov function

\displaystyle\Psi_{t}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\dot{{\mathbf{z}}}_{t}+\sqrt{\mu}({\mathbf{z}}_{t}-{\bm{z}}_{\star})\right\rVert^{2}+2\big(\phi({\mathbf{z}}_{t})-\phi({\bm{z}}_{\star})-\langle\nabla\phi({\bm{z}}_{\star}),{\mathbf{z}}_{t}-{\bm{z}}_{\star}\rangle\big)

satisfies
\dot{\Psi}_{t}\leq-\sqrt{\mu}\Psi_{t}
.

Proof.

Observe that

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left\lVert\dot{{\mathbf{z}}}_{t}+\sqrt{\mu}({\mathbf{z}}_{t}-{\bm{z}}_{\star})\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=2\left\langle\ddot{{\mathbf{z}}}_{t}+\sqrt{\mu}\dot{{\mathbf{z}}}_{t},\dot{{\mathbf{z}}}_{t}+\sqrt{\mu}({\mathbf{z}}_{t}-{\bm{z}}_{\star})\right\rangle

\displaystyle{}={}-2\left\langle\sqrt{\mu}\dot{{\mathbf{z}}}_{t}+\nabla\phi({\mathbf{z}}_{t}),\dot{{\mathbf{z}}}_{t}+\sqrt{\mu}({\mathbf{z}}_{t}-{\bm{z}}_{\star})\right\rangle

\displaystyle\phantom{{}={}}-2\left\langle{\mathbb{S}}\left({\mathbf{z}}_{t}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu}}\dot{{\mathbf{z}}}_{t}\right),\dot{{\mathbf{z}}}_{t}+\sqrt{\mu}({\mathbf{z}}_{t}-{\bm{z}}_{\star})\right\rangle.

First, we have

\displaystyle-2\left\langle\sqrt{\mu}\dot{{\mathbf{z}}}_{t},\dot{{\mathbf{z}}}_{t}+\sqrt{\mu}({\mathbf{z}}_{t}-{\bm{z}}_{\star})\right\rangle=-\sqrt{\mu}\left(\left\lVert\dot{{\mathbf{z}}}_{t}+\sqrt{\mu}({\mathbf{z}}_{t}-{\bm{z}}_{\star})\right\rVert^{2}+\left\lVert\dot{{\mathbf{z}}}_{t}\right\rVert^{2}-\mu\left\lVert{\mathbf{z}}_{t}-{\bm{z}}_{\star}\right\rVert^{2}\right)

and by monotonicity of
{\mathbb{S}}
, we have

\displaystyle\left\langle{\mathbb{S}}\left({\mathbf{z}}_{t}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu}}\dot{{\mathbf{z}}}_{t}\right),\dot{{\mathbf{z}}}_{t}+\sqrt{\mu}({\mathbf{z}}_{t}-{\bm{z}}_{\star})\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\mu}\left\langle{\mathbb{S}}\left({\mathbf{z}}_{t}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu}}\dot{{\mathbf{z}}}_{t}\right),\left({\mathbf{z}}_{t}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu}}\dot{{\mathbf{z}}}_{t}\right)-{\bm{z}}_{\star}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\geq\sqrt{\mu}\left\langle{\mathbb{S}}({\bm{z}}_{\star}),\left({\mathbf{z}}_{t}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu}}\dot{{\mathbf{z}}}_{t}\right)-{\bm{z}}_{\star}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\mu}\left\langle{\mathbb{S}}({\bm{z}}_{\star}),{\mathbf{z}}_{t}-{\bm{z}}_{\star}\right\rangle+\left\langle{\mathbb{S}}({\bm{z}}_{\star}),\dot{{\mathbf{z}}}_{t}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=-\sqrt{\mu}\left\langle\nabla\phi({\bm{z}}_{\star}),{\mathbf{z}}_{t}-{\bm{z}}_{\star}\right\rangle-\left\langle\nabla\phi({\bm{z}}_{\star}),\dot{{\mathbf{z}}}_{t}\right\rangle

and therefore

\displaystyle-2\left\langle{\mathbb{S}}\left({\mathbf{z}}_{t}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu}}\dot{{\mathbf{z}}}_{t}\right),\dot{{\mathbf{z}}}_{t}+\sqrt{\mu}({\mathbf{z}}_{t}-{\bm{z}}_{\star})\right\rangle

\displaystyle\leq 2\sqrt{\mu}\left\langle\nabla\phi({\bm{z}}_{\star}),{\mathbf{z}}_{t}-{\bm{z}}_{\star}\right\rangle+2\left\langle\nabla\phi({\bm{z}}_{\star}),\dot{{\mathbf{z}}}_{t}\right\rangle.

Also, by the chain rule we have

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}2(\phi({\mathbf{z}}_{t})-\phi({\bm{z}}_{\star})-\langle\nabla\phi({\bm{z}}_{\star}),{\mathbf{z}}_{t}-{\bm{z}}_{\star}\rangle)

\displaystyle=2\left\langle\nabla\phi({\mathbf{z}}_{t})-\nabla\phi({\bm{z}}_{\star}),\dot{{\mathbf{z}}}_{t}\right\rangle,

and by strong convexity of
\phi
we have

\displaystyle-2\left\langle\nabla\phi({\mathbf{z}}_{t}),\sqrt{\mu}({\mathbf{z}}_{t}-{\bm{z}}_{\star})\right\rangle

4.2
Oracle models and matching lower bound

While NOD considers a decomposition
{\mathbb{T}}=\nabla\phi+{\mathbb{S}}
and accesses the two components via
decomposed oracles

\nabla\phi
and
{\mathbb{S}}
, most prior work instead treats the operator
{\mathbb{T}}
as a whole, which we refer to as the
single-evaluation oracle
model.

For
bilinearly coupled
minimax optimization problems, these two oracle models are, in fact, equivalent. Indeed, for
\mathcal{L}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})=g({\bm{x}})-h({\bm{y}})+{\bm{x}}^{\top}{\bm{M}}{\bm{y}}
, we have

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \nabla g({\bm{x}})&=\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\mathcal{L}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})-{\bm{M}}{\bm{y}}=2\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\mathcal{L}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})-\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\mathcal{L}({\bm{x}},2{\bm{y}})\\
{\bm{M}}{\bm{y}}&=(\nabla g({\bm{x}})+2{\bm{M}}{\bm{y}})-(\nabla g({\bm{x}})+{\bm{M}}{\bm{y}})=\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\mathcal{L}({\bm{x}},2{\bm{y}})-\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\mathcal{L}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}),\end{aligned}

and similarly for the
{\bm{y}}
-component.
Thus, the decomposed oracle
(\nabla g({\bm{x}}),\nabla h({\bm{y}}))
can be obtained from three calls/queries to the single-evaluation oracle
{\mathbb{T}}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})
,
{\mathbb{T}}({\bm{x}},2{\bm{y}})
, and
{\mathbb{T}}(2{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})
, and the same holds for
({\bm{M}}{\bm{y}},-{\bm{M}}^{\top}{\bm{x}})
.
Therefore, both NOD and other algorithms from previous work formulated in terms of decomposed oracles
[
4
,
5
,
6
]
are implementable in the single-evaluation-oracle model with the same asymptotic iteration-complexity upper bounds in the bilinear coupling setup.
For general SCSC minimax optimization problems with
non-bilinear
coupling, the above equivalence no longer holds.

Our rate for NOD matches the lower bound of
[
16
]
, which is constructed using a bilinearly coupled minimax instance.
This establishes that NOD is optimal within the class of algorithms that access decomposed oracles.
However, whether the same rate can be achieved for general non-bilinear coupling problems to match the lower bound of
[
16
]
under the single-evaluation oracle model is open.
It is possible that future work will either (i) develop improved algorithms achieving the same rate with a single-evaluation oracle, or (ii) establish stronger lower bounds based on constructing hard instances with non-bilinear coupling.

5
Conclusion

We introduce
N
esterov acceleration with
O
perator
D
ecomposition (NOD), a method that leverages the classical notion of Asplund-type operator decomposition to extend Nesterov’s accelerated gradient descent (NAG) to the broader class of monotone inclusion problems, including minimax optimization. Under decomposed oracle access, NOD achieves an accelerated rate that matches the complexity lower bound for the bilinearly coupled minimax problems established in prior work, as discussed with further nuance in
Section

4.2
.

Our approach represents a slight departure from the prevailing framework in the minimax optimization literature, which typically assumes access to a single-evaluation oracle as discussed in
Section

4.2
. In this sense, NOD is not a “pure” first-order method. Nonetheless, by drawing on classical notions of operator decomposition, our work opens the door to new avenues for designing accelerated algorithms and suggests new research directions in minimax optimization and operator structure theory.
