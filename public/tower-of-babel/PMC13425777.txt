Examining the influences of media on food practices among adolescents in Salem district of Tamil Nadu: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Priyanka Krishnamoorthy, Sendilkumar Balasundaram, Indra Sambasivam, Ravichandran Rajendhran, Gayathri Lakshmanan, Priyamathi T, Tamilchudar Raju, Deepiga Gnanadass, Dhasarathi Kumar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSocial media-based food advertising has notably emerged as a significant and modern form of exposure for adolescents, frequently exceeding traditional media in terms of reach and influence. Food labels also play a role in their nutritional choices, albeit with varied understanding.ObjectiveTo determine the association of advertisements and food labelling practices on dietary choices among adolescents and to examine associations with junk food consumption.MethodsA descriptive cross-sectional study was conducted among 385 adolescents aged 10–19 years in Salem, Tamil Nadu, using stratified random sampling. Data on demographics, media exposure, food habits, and label reading practices were collected through a semi-structured questionnaire. Association was examined using chi-square test.ResultsNearly, 69.4% of adolescents reported being tempted by advertisements to consume junk food (p = 0.002). Advertisements were significantly associated with the frequency of junk food intake, belief in food marketing, and intent to purchase. Social media platforms like Instagram and YouTube were identified as significantly associated channels for food marketing.ConclusionCurrent study highlighting that media advertisements were significantly associated with unhealthy eating patterns. This highlights the need for educational interventions and regulatory policies to enhance media literacy and improve understanding of food labelling to promote healthier dietary behaviors.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40795-026-01394-1.Keywords: Adolescents, Media influence, Food advertisement, Food labelling, Junk food, Dietary behaviour

Introduction

The modern media landscape has drastically altered how adolescents interact with food content [1, 2]. Numerous literature states that more than 90% of adolescents have at least one active social media account, demonstrating that they are growing up in a media-rich environment. Adolescents' interactions with information, particularly commercial ads, have been significantly transformed by their continuous exposure to television, laptops, the internet, and mobile phones [2–4]. Social media-based food advertising has become a significant and modern form of exposure, with platforms like Instagram and YouTube playing a crucial role in influencing adolescents' perceptions related to food. The food industry has adeptly leveraged these channels, employing colourful ads and popular characters to influence adolescents' eating preferences [5]. In contrast, food labelling represents an individual-level interpretive skill, requiring adolescents to cognitively process nutritional information, which is often poorly understood. These factors, coupled with poor awareness of food labelling, contribute to rising rates of unhealthy food consumption and obesity among adolescents.

The “Food Safety and Standards Regulations, 2018, define Advertisement broadly to encompass any form of audio or visual publicity across various media” [6]. In India, a significant majority of households have access to television, with a large proportion also having satellite TV. In an environment where there is still no governmental monitoring of online advertising, the rapid proliferation of smartphones and the pervasive use of social media platforms by adolescents increase exposure to digital food marketing. This widespread media penetration ensures constant exposure to food marketing [7]. Snacks, defined as light meals consumed between main meals, including pre-cooked and ready-to-eat items, are a primary target for intense marketing efforts by manufacturing companies [8, 9]. Adolescents are specifically targeted as they represent future adult consumers [10]. Food marketing reaches adolescents through numerous avenues, including television, movies, videos, product placements, cross-promotions, product packaging, athlete and celebrity endorsements, billboards, radio, print media, internet, mobile phones, and social media [11, 12]. These advertisements manifest in various forms, such as images, videos, and games promoting specific brands [13, 14]. Marketing strategies often include the portrayal of popular figures, brand promotion, and false health, nutritional, or ethical claims on food/drink labels [15].

The increasing exposure to food-related advertisements carries serious public health implications, especially among adolescents [16]. The tempting nature of these advertisements, coupled with appealing food packaging, stimulates desire and fosters a preference for unhealthy foods [17]. Evidence from various studies indicates a significant relationship between food advertising and the intake of fast foods characterized by elevated levels of salts, oils, fats, chocolates, refined grains, and red meats [18]. This is associated with the rising prevalence of overweight and obesity among adolescents [19]. Unhealthy food advertising, in this context, refers to television commercials that encourage the consumption of products high in fat, sugar, and/or sodium [20, 21]. Adolescents are highly susceptible to media-driven food marketing, which is associated with their food preferences and consumption patterns. Coupled with poor understanding of food labelling, this increases the risk of unhealthy dietary behaviors and subsequent health issues. However, limited evidence exists on how social media advertising and food labelling together influence dietary practices among adolescents in Indian settings, particularly at the district level, especially concerning food advertising on social media platforms. Addressing the gap, this study seeks to investigate the relationships between advertising and food labelling practices and their association on the nutritional choices of adolescents in Salem, Tamil Nadu.

Methodology

Study design

The study used a community based cross-sectional design aimed to examine the association between media exposure, food labelling practices and dietary choices among adolescents. Because a cross-sectional study design makes the ability to gather information at a specific time, it provides an overview of how media influences adolescent dietary choices in the Salem, Tamil Nadu.

Study approach

A descriptive quantitative methodology was applied.

Study settings and duration

Salem district has 14 Taluks in the state of Tamil Nadu. This community-based cross-sectional study was carried out in Salem, Edapadi and Salem South Taluks namely Seeragapadi, Kondalampatti, Veerapandi, and Edappadi were selected for data collection. The study was conducted at selected areas in and around Salem, Tamil Nadu. The duration of the study was 3 months, during which sufficient data were collected to ensure robust findings.

Sampling techniques

The present cross-sectional study was done in Salem, Tamil Nadu. Salem district comprises 14 Taluks, which were listed and finally Seeragapadi, Kondalampatti, Veerapandi, and Edappadi wards were chosen for data collection based on the simple random sampling technique using the lottery method to ensure the equal representation of Urban and Semi-Urban areas. After selecting the study areas, the participants were selected at the community level using a stratified random sampling technique. A stratified random sampling was employed to ensure the representativeness and minimise the selection bias of both genders. Participants were stratified into female and male, and then proportionate allocation was used to identify the required sample size in each stratum of male and female. Following the stratified random sampling method, the simple random sampling method was employed to select the participants in each stratum and the participants were approached through the household visits in the community. The use of random sampling enhances the generalizability of findings to the broader population of Salem.

Data collection

Survey instrument

The questionnaire consists of structured, closed-ended, dichotomous questions (Yes/No) to assess the wide aspects of social media usage and dietary choices among adolescents. It was designed to gather information on demographics, media usage, food habits, nutrition information on food labelling, food choices, food marketing, and media exposure. Internal consistency measures like Cronbach’s alpha were not considered to be suitable, since the items were not planned to form a composite scale.

Content validity was obtained from five experts in the field of Public health and Nutrition to assess the clarity, relevance, and comprehensiveness of the items with the study objectives. The Questionnaire was also pilot tested among 50 participants (n = 50) to assess the clarity and feasibility of the items in the questionnaire. Based on the observations from the pilot study, minor modifications were made before the data collection, and the expert opinion was again sought for validation. The questionnaire has three sections, which consist of Demographic details, Media usage, and Food habits of the participants. The demographic details consist of age, gender, educational qualification, income, and occupation. The media usage domains consist of items that include frequent use of media platforms, hours spent on watching media or television, source of media usage, purpose of using media, etc.. The food habits domains consisted of closed-ended items, and the variables were categorised into perception towards food advertisements, Exposure to food advertisement types, behavioural influence of advertisements, food label reading behaviour, food preference, frequency of healthy food consumption, junk food consumption, and perceived health consequences (Example: Have you ever tried a new food or beverage because you saw it in an advertisement/How do food advertisements make you feel/Do you read food labels every time you buy a product).

Variables

In the study, the exposure variables were adolescents' social media usage (e.g., time spent on social media, time spent on seeing food-related advertisements), and the outcome variables were food practices and food preferences. Key variables measured include age, gender, educational level, income, height, weight, awareness, and understanding of food labels. Other factors considered were adolescents' exposure to advertisements, individual taste and preference for certain products, healthy food options, and socioeconomic factors.

Data analysis

Before the main data collection, a pilot study was conducted to ensure the representation of the samples and to test the clarity, relevance, and appropriateness of the questionnaire. The analysis was aimed to examine the association between media influence on dietary choices among adolescents. Data was entered in MS-Excel and analyzed using statistical software SPSS trial version 30. Descriptive statistics were used to analyse participant characteristics and key variables. The categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Inferential analysis was used to examine the association between social media influence on dietary choices, bivariate analysis was conducted using the Chi-square test. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Due to the limited sample size and the number of categorical variables with response categories, performing multivariable models will not be feasible. Hence, these findings were presented as preliminary exploratory associations without statistical adjustments.

Discussion

The findings of the present study suggest a significant association between exposure to food advertisements and junk food consumption among adolescents. Perceived credibility and frequent exposure to such advertisements were linked with higher reported intake of unhealthy foods. Additionally, factors such as socioeconomic status and patterns of mass media use appeared to shape these observed associations. These findings are consistent with existing literature indicating that media environments are associated with adolescents’ dietary behaviours and highlight the importance of strengthening media literacy and food labelling awareness among young populations.

Association of advertisements on nutritional choices and junk food consumption

The study revealed a significant association between exposure to advertisements and unhealthy nutritional choices among adolescents, as evidenced by the chi-square test results (p < 0.05). The data indicated that advertisements, particularly on social media platforms like Instagram (40%) and YouTube (37%), were highly influential in shaping food preferences among adolescents. The significant correlation between advertisement believability and increased consumption of junk food (p < 0.05) suggests that the more credible the advertisement is perceived, the greater its association on food choices. These findings align with the research of Harris et al. (2009) [23], who found that food marketing tactics heavily influence adolescents' food preferences by exploiting emotional appeals, social influence, and peer conformity. The persuasive nature of food advertisements, often depicting junk foods as desirable and socially rewarding, has been consistently shown to result in increased consumption of unhealthy foods among young viewers.

Additionally, the study's results indicate that adolescents exposed to frequent advertisements are more likely to consume junk food multiple times a week. This pattern corresponds with existing literature, which emphasizes the role of repetitive exposure in reinforcing unhealthy eating behaviors. Kelly et al. (2015) [24] highlight that repeated exposure to food advertising on social media platforms can significantly alter adolescents' perceptions of what constitutes normal eating habits, often normalizing the consumption of high-calorie, low-nutrient foods. The chi-square findings from this study (p < 0.05) support this, showing that students who frequently see food advertisements are significantly more likely to choose junk foods over healthier options. These results are particularly concerning given the broader public health implications, including increased risks of obesity, diabetes, and other diet-related health issues among adolescents.

Media usage patterns and the influence of social media

The study identified Instagram and YouTube as the most frequently used platforms among participants, with 40% and 37% usage rates, respectively. These platforms are known for hosting a high volume of food advertisements, particularly those targeting adolescents through influencer marketing and visually appealing content. According to Buijzen and Valkenburg (2003), social media significantly amplifies the reach and effectiveness of food advertising by integrating promotional content seamlessly into users' daily media consumption, often without clear isolation between advertising and entertainment [25]. The significant chi-square associations in the study (p < 0.05) between media exposure and junk food consumption further highlight the profound association of these platforms on adolescents' food choices.

Research by Kelly et al. (2015) [24] supports these findings, noting that adolescents who frequently engage with social media are more likely to encounter advertisements that promote unhealthy food choices, contributing to a normalization of poor dietary habits. The study's results also align with findings from Livingstone and Helsper (2006), who emphasize the need for stronger advertising literacy among young people to help them recognize and critically assess the intent behind food advertisements [26]. The perceived credibility of advertisements, as seen in the study's data, plays a significant role in influencing consumption patterns, underscoring the importance of equipping adolescents with the skills to question and interpret advertising messages.

Believability of food advertisements and behavioral implications

The study found that many students considered food advertisements believable, and this perception was significantly associated with increased weekly junk food consumption frequency (p < 0.05). This aligns with research showing that trust in advertisements is a key factor influencing consumer behavior, particularly among young audiences who may not have developed the critical faculties to question marketing claims. The significant chi-square results in the study suggest that adolescents' belief in the credibility of advertisements is associated with their food choices, contributing to a cycle of unhealthy eating habits that are reinforced by constant exposure to persuasive marketing.

The study's findings are consistent with the broader literature, which indicates that the portrayal of junk foods in advertisements often emphasizes convenience, taste, and social status, resonating strongly with adolescent audiences. Harris et al. (2009) found that such advertisements not only encourage immediate food choices but also shape long-term eating behaviors, as repeated exposure to advertising content creates lasting impressions about food preferences. This highlights the critical need for interventions that address the influence of advertising on young people, particularly through media literacy programs that teach adolescents how to critically evaluate and resist persuasive marketing tactics.

Conclusion

The research highlights the critical need for media literacy programs and policy involvements to mitigate the negative association of food marketing on adolescent nutrition and public health. This examination of the multifaceted influence of media on food practices among adolescents reveals a complex interplay of factors that significantly shape dietary behaviors. Media, encompassing television, social media, and digital platforms, has a profound association on the food choices and eating habits of young individuals. This study synthesizes the primary findings, discusses their implications, and proposes directions for future research and interventions.

Strengths and limitations

This research contributes significantly to the field by establishing a statistically significant association between various facets of media advertisements (temptation, believability, and purchase intent) and the frequency of junk food consumption among adolescents in Salem, Tamil Nadu. The use of stratified random sampling enhances the generalizability of these findings to the broader local adolescent population. However, the study's cross-sectional design confines the ability to draw a relationship between social media usage and food labelling practices. The chronological order of exposure and outcome is unknown and there is a possible of reverse causality in which adolescents who consume more junk food might be exposed to media content. Hence, these findings should be inferred as exploratory connections rather than causal relationships. This study excluded adolescents with low social media access and exposure, which underrepresents lower socio-economic status and rural groups, which limits generalizability. Since multivariable analysis was not performed, confounding factors like age, gender, and socioeconomic status were not adjusted for, which may influence the observed associations. Since the study was held only in the Salem district over a short period of time, the results may not be generalised to other places, specifically with the view of a sudden shift in social media exposure and dietary choices. The elaborative psychometric properties of the questionnaire were not thoroughly evaluated, which may also limit the accuracy of the measured constructs. Furthermore, the reliance on self-reported data gathered via a questionnaire introduces the potential for recall bias or social desirability bias in reporting consumption frequency and media engagement. Participants may have under- or over-reported their dietary behaviors or media engagement.

Recommendation

Future research on the influence of media on food practices among adolescents (10–19 years old) should prioritize longitudinal and experimental study designs to establish causality and effectiveness of interventions. Investigating diverse samples and emerging media platforms, such as social media, podcasts, and virtual reality, will provide a comprehensive understanding of media's association. Additionally, analyzing food-related media content and social media conversations will reveal trends and influencers. Examining the interplay between media influences and social factors, like family and peers, will also be crucial. Furthermore, developing and evaluating media-based nutrition education programs and technology-based interventions will help promote healthy food choices. By exploring these directions, researchers can inform policies regulating food marketing to adolescents and develop targeted strategies to mitigate the negative effects of media on food practices, ultimately promoting healthier habits among adolescents.
