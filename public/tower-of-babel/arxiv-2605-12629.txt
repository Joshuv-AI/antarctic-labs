Relative accessibility for graphs
Joseph Paul MacManus

Abstract
. We relativise the Thomassen–Woess definition of accessibility in graphs, defining what it means for a graph to be accessible relative to a peripheral system . In the case of locally finite, quasi-transitive graphs, we characterise relative accessibility in terms of a certain subring of the Boolean ring of the graph, and apply this to show that our definition agrees with the usual algebraic notion of relative accessibility in finitely generated groups. This implies, in particular, that relative accessibility is a quasi-isometry invariant amongst finitely generated groups, when the quasi-isometry coarsely preserves the left cosets of the peripheral subgroups. We also deduce a relative variant of Hamann’s accessibility theorem on graphs with finitely generated cycle spaces.



1.
Introduction

The definition of an
accessible group
is due to Wall
[
22
]
. In the language of Bass–Serre theory, a finitely generated group
G
is
accessible
if it splits as a finite graph of groups with finite edge groups and vertex groups with at most one end. Wall conjectured that every finitely generated group is accessible, suggesting a natural extension of the Grushko–Neumann theorem.
Much progress was made on this conjecture, most notably by Linnell
[
14
]
and Dunwoody
[
4
]
, who verified Wall’s conjecture for groups with bounded-order finite subgroups and (almost) finitely presented groups, respectively. Eventually, a counterexample was discovered by Dunwoody in the 1993
[
5
]
.
That same year, the idea of accessibility was re-cast in an new, geometric light when Thomassen and Woess presented a purely graph-theoretical definition of accessibility
[
21
]
. Their definition runs as follows.

Definition 1.1

(Accessibility for graphs)
.

Let
\Gamma
be a connected, locally finite graph. We say that
\Gamma
is (
edge-
)
accessible
if there exists
k\in\mathbf{N}
such that for every pair of distinct ends
\omega_{1}
,
\omega_{2}
of
\Gamma
, there exists an edge-cut consisting of at most
k
edges which separates
\omega_{1}
and
\omega_{2}
.

Thomassen and Woess go on to prove that a finitely generated group is accessible if and only if some (and hence any) of its Cayley graphs are accessible graphs. This geometric characterisation has the immediate consequence that accessibility is a quasi-isometry invariant. Their work opened the door to questions about how the classical study of infinitely-ended groups can be applied more generally to quasi-transitive graphs through purely combinatorial means; see e.g.
[
11
,
17
,
7
,
15
]
.

Within geometric group theory, there is an established algebraic notion of ‘relative’ accessibility, described in e.g.
[
10
]
. Briefly, we are given a system of
peripheral subgroups
along with our group, and we ask for a ‘maximal’ finite splitting of the group where each peripheral subgroup fixes a vertex of the Bass–Serre tree; see Section
4
for a precise definition.
Studying properties of groups ‘relative to a given peripheral system’ is a very successful idea, leading to many deep insights. The purpose of this paper is to advertise the following relativisation of Definition
1.1
.

Definition 1.2

(Relative accessibility for graphs)
.

Let
\Gamma
be a connected, locally finite graph. A
peripheral system
is a collection
\mathcal{H}
of subsets of
V(\Gamma)
.
We say that a cut
b\in{\mathscr{B}}(\Gamma)
is
\mathcal{H}
-elliptic
if for every
H\in\mathcal{H}
, either
b\cap H
or
b^{\ast}\cap H
is finite.

We then say that
\Gamma
is
accessible relative to
\mathcal{H}
if there exists
k\geq 1
such that for every pair of distinct ends
\omega_{1}
,
\omega_{2}
of
\Gamma
, if there exists an
\mathcal{H}
-elliptic cut
b
separating
\omega_{1}
and
\omega_{2}
then there exists a (possibly different)
\mathcal{H}
-elliptic cut
b^{\prime}
separating the
\omega_{i}
such that
|\delta b^{\prime}|\leq k
.

It can be verified (see Proposition
3.3
) that the set of
\mathcal{H}
-elliptic cuts forms a subring of the Boolean ring
{\mathscr{B}}(\Gamma)
, which we denote by
{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathcal{H}}(\Gamma)
. As is the case with absolute accessibility, we show that relative accessibility is characterised by the generation properties of this ring.

Theorem A
.

Let
\Gamma
be a connected, locally finite, quasi-transitive
G
-graph and
\mathcal{H}
a thin,
G
-invariant peripheral system. The following are equivalent:

(1)

\Gamma
is accessible relative to
\mathcal{H}
.

(2)

{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathcal{H}}(\Gamma)
is finitely generated as a
G
-module.

In fact, the finite generating set we obtain is nested. Here,
thin
means that every vertex is contained in finitely many peripherals; see Definition
3.4
.
The proof of Theorem
A
passes through the ‘cone-off’ of
\Gamma
over
\mathcal{H}
, denoted
\widehat{\Gamma}_{\mathcal{H}}
; see Section
3.5
. The key observation is that—after possibly replacing
\mathcal{H}
with something slightly more well-behaved—there is a canonical isomorphism

{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathcal{H}}(\Gamma)\cong{\mathscr{B}}(\widehat{\Gamma}_{\mathcal{H}}).

This allows access to the full strength of the Dicks–Dunwoody theory of the Boolean ring of cuts. Even so, care has to be taken in order to take advantage of this map, as naïvely there is no control on how the cardinalities of coboundaries change when passing through the isomorphism. Regaining this control is therefore the crux of this problem, which is achieved by substituting
\mathcal{H}
with yet another, even better-behaved peripheral system. Exactly what we mean by ‘better-behaved’ is not immediately obvious here, and pinning this down constitutes much of the hard work done towards proving Theorem
A
.

Using Theorem
A
, we are able to prove that our definition of relative accessibility coincides with the standard algebraic definition for finitely generated groups. This motivates that our definition is, in some sense, the ‘correct’ one.

Theorem B
.

Let
G
be a finitely generated group and
\mathcal{P}
a finite collection of subgroups. Let
\Gamma
be some locally finite Cayley graph of
G
. Let

\mathcal{H}=\{gH:H\in\mathcal{P},\ g\in G\}.

Then
G
is accessible relative to
\mathcal{P}
if and only if
\Gamma
is accessible relative to
\mathcal{H}
.

Our proof of Theorem
B
is algebraic and combinatorial in nature, relating actions on trees with finite edge stabilisers to homomorphisms between Boolean rings.
We remark that the above theorem has the following immediate geometric consequence.

Corollary C
.

Let
G_{1}
,
G_{2}
be finitely generated groups, equipped with fixed word metrics. Let
\mathcal{P}_{i}
a finite collection of subgroups of
G_{i}
. Suppose that there exists a quasi-isometry
f:G_{1}\to G_{2}

such that the following holds:

(1)

For all
g\in G_{1}
,
H\in\mathcal{P}_{1}
, there exists
h\in G_{2}
,
K\in\mathcal{P}_{2}
such that

\dHaus(f(gH),hK)<\infty.

(2)

For all
h\in G_{2}
,
K\in\mathcal{P}_{2}
, there exists
g\in G_{1}
,
H\in\mathcal{P}_{1}
such that

\dHaus(f(gH),hK)<\infty.

Then,
G_{1}
is accessible relative to
\mathcal{P}_{1}
if and only if
G_{2}
is accessible relative to
\mathcal{P}_{2}
.

Note that we do not require the Hausdorff distances in the above statement to be uniformly bounded.

As mentioned above, a cornerstone result in the theory of accessible groups is Dunwoody’s accessibility theorem for (almost) finitely presented groups
[
4
]
. The relative variant of this appears in
[
10
, Prop. 2.24]
. Hamann gave a combinatorial generalisation of Dunwoody’s theorem, in terms of the cycle space of a quasi-transitive graph
[
13
]
. Using our machinery, we are able to deduce the following relative variant of Hamann’s theorem, by considering the cycle space of the cone-off of our graph over its peripheral system.

Theorem D
.

Let
G
be a connected, locally finite, quasi-transitive
G
-graph. Let
\mathcal{H}
be a thin,
G
-invariant peripheral system. Suppose that
{\mathscr{C}(\widehat{\Gamma}_{\mathcal{H}})}
is topologically
G
-finitely generated. Then
\Gamma
is accessible relative to
\mathcal{H}
.

This recovers and generalises the fact that a finitely generated group
G
which is finitely presented relative to some collection of peripheral subgroups
\mathcal{P}
is accessible relative to
\mathcal{P}
. We remark that
\mathcal{P}
is necessarily finite under this hypothesis
[
18
, Thm. 1.1]
.

Note the condition of
topological

G
-finite generation in the above theorem. This is strictly weaker than asking
{\mathscr{C}(\widehat{\Gamma}_{\mathcal{H}})}
simply admit a
G
-finite generating set. The exact definition of topological generation and its distinction from the usual ‘algebraic’ generation is discussed in Section
5.2
below. We remark that Hamann’s theorem also holds verbatim when only assuming the weaker hypothesis of topological generation, with only a minor modification to the proof. This modification is explained in Appendix
A
.

We are interested in this weakening as it is often more directly relevant to certain applications, most notably when dealing with planar graphs. Indeed, this has been observed several times in the literature
[
6
,
9
]
. Perhaps most relevant for us, Theorem
D
is applied by the present author with this weaker hypothesis in
[
15
]
, where it is used to prove that a locally finite, quasi-transitive graph which is quasi-isometric to a planar graph is accessible. Indeed, some of the techniques of the current paper originally appeared in a rudimentary form in an earlier draft of
[
15
]
. The purpose of this paper is to further explore and greatly elaborate on these techniques, as they appear to be independently deserving of study.

2.2.
Ends and limit sets

We now recall the basic theory of ends and limit sets in graphs. We will only recall this theory so far as we need it. To avoid any unnecessary pathologies, we only work with locally finite graphs when discussing ends.

Let
\Gamma

is a topological embedding, and

is a closed subset of

, and thus is naturally compact. See
[
23

for details.

Given a subset

\Lambda(H)
its

in
\Omega(\Gamma)
. That is, we define

\Lambda(H):=\overline{H}\cap\Omega(\Gamma)
,
where

denotes the closure of

|\Gamma|
. If

is a group acting on

is some choice of vertex. It is easy to see that this does not depend on the choice of

. In particular, we may even take finitely many vertices, and then it is a short exercise in point-set topology to verify that the equality

We record the following classical facts about limit sets, which can be found in most standard texts on the subject; we will appeal to Woess’ monograph

]

2.4.
Some more useful results

Before we dive in, it will be helpful to record some basic observations for later reference.
First, we have the following fact which was first observed by Möller

Proposition 2.8
.

Let
\Gamma
be a connected, locally finite graph. Let
S\subset{\mathscr{B}}(\Gamma)
. Suppose
\omega_{1},\omega_{2}\in\Omega(\Gamma)
are separated by some

in the additive span of

. Then there exists
b^{\prime}\in S
which separates
\omega_{1}
and
\omega_{2}
.

Proof.

Let
a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\in S
be such that
b=\sum_{i}a_{i}
. Suppose for the sake of a contradiction that no
a_{i}
separates
\omega_{1}
and
\omega_{2}
. Let
P_{i}
be (the vertex set of) an infinite simple ray approaching

, such that for all
j
we have that
P_{i}
is contained entirely in either
a_{j}
or
a_{j}^{\ast}
. Suppose that for each
j
, we have that either
P_{1}\cup P_{2}\subset a_{j}
or
P_{1}\cup P_{2}\subset a_{j}^{\ast}
. Then
P_{1}
and

lie in the same piece of the Venn diagram formed by the

. In particular, we must either have that
P_{1}\cup P_{2}\subset b
or

. This contradicts the fact that

separates the
\omega_{i}
.
∎

The next result provides a criterion for constructing ring generating sets of a given subring of
{\mathscr{B}}(\Gamma)
.

Proposition 2.9
.

Let
\Gamma
be a connected, locally finite graph. Let
M\subset{\mathscr{B}}(\Gamma)
be a subring. Let
S\subset M
be such that

\text{$\exists b\in M$ separating $\omega_{1}$, $\omega_{2}$ $\iff$ $\exists b^{\prime}\in S$ separating $\omega_{1}$, $\omega_{2}$ }.

Then for every
b\in M
there exists
b^{\prime}\in\langle S\rangle
, the subring generated by
S
, such that
b+b^{\prime}
is finite.

Proof.

Let
b\in M
be arbitrary. We will find an element of
\langle S\rangle
such that
b+b^{\prime}
is finite. Recall that
b
induces a bipartition of
\Omega(\Gamma)
into two clopen sets
W_{1}\sqcup W_{2}
, say
W_{1}=\Lambda(b)
and
W_{2}=\Lambda(b^{\ast})
. Let us assume without loss of generality that both
W_{1}
and
W_{2}
are non-empty, lest we are done already.

Fix
p\in W_{1}
. For every
q\in W_{2}
, by assumption we have that there exists
b_{q}\in S
such that
p\in\Lambda(b_{q})
,
q\in\Lambda(b_{q}^{\ast})
. Note that

W_{1}\cup\bigcup_{q\in W_{2}}\Lambda(b_{q})

is an open cover of
\Omega(\Gamma)
. Since the space of ends is compact, there is a finite subcover. In particular, there exists a finite collection
q_{1},\ldots,q_{k}
such that
\bigcup_{i}\Lambda(b_{i}^{\ast})\subset W_{2}
, where
b_{i}:=b_{q_{i}}
. Thus, we have that
a_{p}:=\bigcap_{i}b_{i}\in\langle S\rangle
separates

from all of
W_{2}
, i.e.
p\in\Lambda(a_{p})
and
W_{2}\subset\Lambda(a_{p}^{\ast})
.
Now, we have an open cover

W_{2}\cup\bigcup_{p\in W_{1}}\Lambda(a_{p}),

where each
\Lambda(a_{p})
is disjoint from
W_{2}
. Passing to a finite subcover, we find a finite collection
p_{1},\ldots,p_{\ell}
such that
\bigcup_{i}\Lambda(a_{i})=W_{1}
, where
a_{i}:=a_{p_{i}}
. It follows that
c:=\bigcap_{i}(a_{i})^{\ast}
induces the same clopen bipartition on
\Omega(\Gamma)
, up to orientation. More precisely, we have that
b+c^{\ast}
is finite.
∎

As discussed earlier, we may sometimes wish to ‘forget’ the multiplicative operation on a given subring of
{\mathscr{B}}(\Gamma)
. The generating sets we construct can often be salvaged during this erasure process, using the following neat observation; see the proof of
[
21
, Prop. 6.2]
.

Proposition 2.10
.

Let
\Gamma
be a connected graph. Let
M\subset{\mathscr{B}}(\Gamma)
be a subring and
S\subset M
be closed under taking complements, such that
S
generates
M
as a ring. Suppose that
S
is nested. Then
S
generates
M
as an abelian group.

Proof.

It is sufficient to observe that we may express intersection in terms of the symmetric difference operation, given nested elements. Suppose that
a,b\in S
, so
a
and
b
are nested. Then, we have that

a\cap b=\begin{cases}\emptyset&\text{if $a\subset b^{\ast}$,}\\
a&\text{if $a\subset b$,}\\
b&\text{if $b\subset a$,}\\
a+b+V(\Gamma)&\text{if $b^{\ast}\subset a$.}\end{cases}

It is easy to see that these cases are exhaustive. Note that
V(\Gamma)=a+a^{\ast}
for any
a\in S
, and so, since
S
is closed under complementation, we also get this element for free in the additive span. The proposition follows.
∎

3.
Elliptic cuts

In this section we introduce the notions of a
peripheral system
and an

, and define what it means for a graph to be
accessible relative to a peripheral system

3.1.
Peripheral systems and elliptic cuts

We begin by introducing the following core definitions.

Definition 3.1

(Peripheral system)
.

Let
\Gamma

\mathcal{H}\subset\mathscr{P}(V(\Gamma))

.
If
\Gamma

G
-graph for some group

Note that if
\mathcal{H}
is

-invariant then this induces an action of

\mathcal{H}
by permutations.

Definition 3.2

(Elliptic cuts)
.

Let
\Gamma
be a connected graph and
\mathcal{H}
a peripheral system. A cut
b\in{\mathscr{B}}(\Gamma)
is said to be
\mathcal{H}
-elliptic
if for all
H\in\mathcal{H}
, either
b\cap H
is finite or
b^{\ast}\cap H
is finite.
Let

{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathcal{H}}(\Gamma)
denote the set of
\mathcal{H}
-elliptic cuts.

We may often abuse terminology and refer to an
\mathcal{H}
-elliptic cut as simply an
elliptic cut
, if
\mathcal{H}
is clear from context.
The following is a straightforward exercise in the algebra of sets.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
\Gamma
be a connected graph and
\mathcal{H}
a peripheral system. Then

{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathcal{H}}(\Gamma)
is a subring of
{\mathscr{B}}(\Gamma)
.

Proof.

Let
b_{1},b_{2}\in{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathcal{H}}(\Gamma)
. We need to check that
b_{1}\cap b_{2}
and
b_{1}+b_{2}
are in
{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathcal{H}}(\Gamma)
. Fix
H\in\mathcal{H}
. We now split into three cases.
Suppose first that
b_{1}\cap H
and
b_{2}\cap H
are finite. Then
(b_{1}\cap b_{2})\cap H
is certainly finite and so
b_{1}\cap b_{2}\in{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathcal{H}}(\Gamma)
. Also,

(b_{1}+b_{2})\cap H=b_{1}\cap H+b_{2}\cap H,

which is finite.
Secondly, assume now that
b_{1}\cap H
and
b_{2}^{\ast}\cap H
are finite. Then we see that
(b_{1}\cap b_{2})\cap H\subset b_{1}\cap H
is finite. Note that

(b_{1}+b_{2})^{\ast}\cap H=(H\cap b_{1}^{\ast}\cap b_{2}^{\ast})\cup(H\cap b_{1}\cap b_{2}),

and so
(b_{1}+b_{2})^{\ast}\cap H
is also finite.
Finally, in the third case we suppose that
b_{1}^{\ast}\cap H
and
b_{2}^{\ast}\cap H
are finite. Then

H\cap(b_{1}\cap b_{2})^{\ast}=H\cap(b_{1}^{\ast}\cup b_{2}^{\ast})=(b_{1}^{\ast}\cap H)\cup(b_{2}^{\ast}\cap H),

which is finite. Furthermore,
b_{1}^{\ast}+b_{2}^{\ast}=b_{1}+b_{2}
, and so

(b_{1}+b_{2})\cap H=(b_{1}^{\ast}+b_{2}^{\ast})\cap H=b_{1}^{\ast}\cap H+b_{2}^{\ast}\cap H,

which is finite.
In each case, we have shown that both
b_{1}\cap b_{2}
and
b_{1}+b_{2}
lie in
{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathcal{H}}(\Gamma)
. It follows that
{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathcal{H}}(\Gamma)
is a subring of
{\mathscr{B}}(\Gamma)
.
∎

We will almost exclusively be interested in peripheral systems which satisfy the following natural hypothesis.

Definition 3.4

(Thin peripheral system)
.

Let
\Gamma
be a connected graph, then a peripheral system
\mathcal{H}
is called
thin
if for all
v\in V(\Gamma)
, there exists at most finitely many
H\in\mathcal{H}
such that
v\in H
.

Note that if
\Gamma
is a connected, locally finite, quasi-transitive
G
-graph and
\mathcal{H}
is thin and
G
-invariant, then
\mathcal{H}/G
is finite.

Our standing assumption on any peripheral system will usually be that it is thin. However, thinness alone is often not enough to immediately say anything meaningful, as thin peripheral systems can still be wild.
Much of the hard work done in this section is to establish how we can take a thin peripheral system
\mathcal{H}
and substitute in its place a ‘nicer’ system
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
. What ‘nicer’ here means is not obvious, and we will pass through several iterations of ‘niceness’ before arriving at something well-behaved enough that we gain some useful geometric control. The pay-off of all this is that, in the end, our substitutions ultimately have no effect on the material content of our results, and our main theorems will still only ask that the hypothesised peripheral system be thin.

The first iteration of ‘niceness’ we will consider is the following strengthening of the thin property, which we call
tameness
.

Definition 3.5

(Tame peripheral system)
.

Let
\Gamma
be a connected graph, then a peripheral system
\mathcal{H}
is called
tame
if for any elliptic cut
b\in{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathcal{H}}(\Gamma)
we have that only finitely many
H\in\mathcal{H}
intersect both
b
and
b^{\ast}
.

It is easy to see that every tame peripheral system of a locally finite graph is thin, in the above sense. The converse is false, even for quasi-transitive
G
-graphs with
G
-invariant peripheral systems, as can be seen in Example
3.6
below.

Example 3.6
.

Consider the 3-regular tree
\Gamma=T_{3}
with a distinguished end
\omega_{0}\in\Omega(\Gamma)
, thus viewing
\Gamma
as an infinite binary tree.
Let
G\subset\Aut(\Gamma)
be the subgroup which stabilises
\omega_{0}
, and thus preserves the level sets. It is easy to see that
G
acts on
\Gamma
vertex- and edge-transitively.
Let
\mathcal{H}
denote the set of level sets, which is
G
-invariant and thin. Moreover, it is not too hard to see that every
b\in{\mathscr{B}}(\Gamma)
is an elliptic cut, and so
{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathcal{H}}(\Gamma)={\mathscr{B}}(\Gamma)
. However,
\mathcal{H}
is not tame, as removing any edge from
\Gamma
separates vertices in infinitely many level sets. See Figure
1
for a cartoon.

3.2.
Coarse connectivity of peripherals

We now take a quick detour to introduce some terminology to help us understand the geometric structure of peripherals. In particular, we will look at their ‘coarse components’.

Definition 3.7

(Coarse components)
.

Let
\Gamma
be a connected graph,
H\subset V(\Gamma)
, and
r>0
. We say that two vertices
x,y\in H

lie in the same
r
-coarse component of
H
if they are contained in the same connected components of the induced graph
\Gamma[B(H,r)]
.

Note that the
r
-coarse components of
H
naturally partition
H
, much in the same way that connected components do.
For brevity, we introduce the following terminology.

Definition 3.8
.

We say that
H
has
unbounded coarse components
if there exists
r>0
such that every
r
-coarse component of
H
is infinite in diameter.

If there exists
r>0
such that every
r
-coarse component
K
of
H
satisfies
|\Lambda(K)|=\infty
, then we say that
H
has
big coarse components
.

Finally, if there exists
r>0
such that
H
has exactly one
r
-coarse component, then we say that
H
is
(
r
-)coarsely connected
.

Example 3.9
.

The peripheral system in Example
3.6
does not have unbounded coarse components, despite every peripheral containing infinitely many vertices.

Remark 3.10
.

It is easy to see that, given
r>0
, if we replace a peripheral system
\mathcal{H}
with

\mathcal{H}_{+r}:=\{B(H,r):H\in\mathcal{H}\},

then
{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathcal{H}}(\Gamma)={\mathscr{B}}_{\mathcal{H}_{+r}}(\Gamma)
. Moreover, if
\mathcal{H}
is thin/tame, then so is
\mathcal{H}_{+r}
.

3.3.
Minimised peripheral systems

There are many ways a peripheral system can contain ‘redundancies’. The first, most obvious one is that some peripherals may be finite.
Note that we do not, in general, lose anything by assuming that every
H\in\mathcal{H}
is infinite. Given a peripheral system
\mathcal{H}
, we can define

\mathcal{H}_{\inf}:=\{H\in\mathcal{H}:|H|=\infty\}.

Then, it is easy to see that any
b\in{\mathscr{B}}(\Gamma)
is
\mathcal{H}
-elliptic if and only if it is
\mathcal{H}_{\inf}
-elliptic.
We can do slightly better than this by inspecting the limit sets of peripherals.

Definition 3.11

(Minimised peripheral system)
.

Let
\Gamma
be a connected, locally finite graph and
\mathcal{H}
a peripheral system. The
minimisation
of
\mathcal{H}
, denoted
\mathcal{H}^{\bullet}
, is defined as

\mathcal{H}^{\bullet}:=\Big\{H\in\mathcal{H}:|\Lambda(H)|>1\Big\}.

We say that
\mathcal{H}
is
minimised
if
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}^{\bullet}
.

Proposition 3.12
.

Let
\Gamma
be a connected, locally finite graph and
\mathcal{H}
a peripheral system. Then
{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathcal{H}}(\Gamma)={\mathscr{B}}_{\mathcal{H}^{\bullet}}(\Gamma)
.

Proof.

Fix
b\in{\mathscr{B}}(\Gamma)
. Firstly, it is easy to see that if b is
\mathcal{H}
-elliptic then
b
is
\mathcal{H}^{\bullet}
-elliptic, since
\mathcal{H}^{\bullet}\subset\mathcal{H}
.
Conversely, suppose that
b
is
\mathcal{H}^{\bullet}
-elliptic. Given
H\in\mathcal{H}\setminus\mathcal{H}^{\bullet}
, we have that
|\Lambda(H)|\leq 1
, which implies that either
b\cap H
is finite or
b^{\ast}\cap H
is finite. In particular,
b
is
\mathcal{H}
-elliptic. The proposition follows.
∎

The following justifies our decision to minimise our peripheral sets.

Lemma 3.13
.

Let
\Gamma
be a connected, locally finite, quasi-transitive
G
-graph. Let
\mathcal{H}
be a minimised,
G
-invariant, thin peripheral system. Then the following hold:

(1)

Every
H\in\mathcal{H}
has unbounded coarse components.

(2)

If
H\in\mathcal{H}
satisfies
|\Lambda(H)|=\infty
, then
H
has big coarse components.

Proof.

Fix
H\in\mathcal{H}
. By Proposition
2.3
, there exists a loxodromic element
t\in G_{H}
, where
G_{H}\leq G
denotes the setwise stabiliser of
H
. Thus, for every
x\in H
, there exists
r>0
such that
\Gamma[B(\langle t\rangle\cdot x,r]
is connected. Since
G_{H}
acts on
H
with finitely many orbits, we may take this value of
r
uniformly. In particular,
H
has unbounded coarse components.

Suppose the limit set of
H
is infinite. Then by Proposition
2.3
there exists a finitely generated subgroup
K\subset G_{H}
such that
\Lambda(K)
is infinite. In particular,
K
-orbits of vertices are coarsely connected. Again, since
G_{H}
acts with finitely many orbits on
H
, we deduce that
H
has big coarse components.
∎

Lemma 3.14
.

Let
\Gamma
be a connected, locally finite, quasi-transitive
G
-graph. Let
\mathcal{H}
be a
G
-invariant, thin peripheral system. Suppose every infinite
H\in\mathcal{H}
has unbounded coarse components. Then
\mathcal{H}
is tame.

Proof.

Suppose
\mathcal{H}
is not tame, so there exists
b\in{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathcal{H}}(\Gamma)
and an infinite sequence
H_{1},H_{2},\ldots\in\mathcal{H}
of distinct elements such that both
H_{i}\cap b
and
H_{i}\cap b^{\ast}
are non-empty for all
i\geq 1
. Without loss of generality, let us assume that each
H_{i}
is infinite (indeed, only finitely many of the
H_{i}
can be finite since
\mathcal{H}
is thin), and
b^{\ast}\cap H_{i}
is finite for every
i\geq 1
. We have
H_{i}
has unbounded
r
-coarse components, for some uniform
r>0
since
\mathcal{H}/G
is finite. It follows every
H_{i}
intersects a bounded neighbourhood around
\delta b
, and so
\mathcal{H}
cannot be thin.
∎

The above lemmas have the following nice consequence.

Theorem 3.15
.

Let
\Gamma
be a connected, locally finite, quasi-transitive
G
-graph. Let
\mathcal{H}
be a minimised, thin,
G
-invariant peripheral system. Then
\mathcal{H}
is tame.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.13
(
1
), since
\mathcal{H}
is minimised we have that each
H\in\mathcal{H}
is infinite and has unbounded coarse components, and so we conclude that
\mathcal{H}
is tame by Lemma
3.14
.
∎

3.4.
Separable peripheral systems

Minimisation is not the only way we can simplify the structure of our peripheral systems. It is quite common that two peripherals might be completely indistinguishable by elliptic cuts, in that they always fall on the same side of any such cut. When this happens, we might as well combine these peripherals into one. This is formalised by the following definition.

Definition 3.16

(Separable peripheral system)
.

Let
\Gamma
be a connected graph and
\mathcal{H}
a peripheral system. Given
H_{1},H_{2}\in\mathcal{H}
, we say that
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
are
distinguishable
if there exists
b\in{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathcal{H}}(\Gamma)
such that
b\cap H_{1}
and
b^{\ast}\cap H_{2}
are both infinite. If
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
are not distinguishable, we say they are
indistinguishable
.

We say that
\mathcal{H}
is
separable
if all pairs of distinct, infinite
H_{1},H_{2}\in\mathcal{H}
are distinguishable.

The following proposition is easy, but useful to note.

Proposition 3.17
.

Let
\Gamma
be a connected, locally finite graph,
\mathcal{H}
be a peripheral system, and
H_{1},H_{2}\in\mathcal{H}
.
Suppose
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
are distinguishable. Then
\Lambda(H_{1})\cap\Lambda(H_{2})=\emptyset
.

Proof.

Suppose there exists
\omega\in\Lambda(H_{1})\cap\Lambda(H_{2})
, and also that there exists
b\in{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathcal{H}}(\Gamma)
such that
b\cap H_{1}
and
b^{\ast}\cap H_{2}
are both infinite. Any sequence of points in
\Gamma
which approaches
\omega
must eventually be contained in one of
b
or
b^{\ast}
, say
b
without loss of generality. But then
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
both have infinite intersection with
b
, and so in particular
b
cannot be elliptic.
∎

Given a non-separable peripheral system
\mathcal{H}
, we can define a relation
\sim
on
\mathcal{H}
, where

H_{1}\sim H_{2}\ \ \Longleftrightarrow\ \ \begin{cases}\text{$H_{1}=H_{2}$, or}\\
\text{$H_{1}$ and $H_{2}$ are both infinite and indistinguishable.}\end{cases}

We now verify this is an equivalence relation.

Proposition 3.18
.

Let
\Gamma
be a connected graph and
\mathcal{H}
a peripheral system. Then
\sim
is an equivalence relation on
\mathcal{H}
.

Proof.

Firstly,
\sim
is obviously reflexive and symmetric. We need only verify transitivity. Suppose
H_{1}\sim H_{2}
and
H_{2}\sim H_{3}
, but there exists
b\in{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathcal{H}}(\Gamma)
which distinguishes
H_{1}
and
H_{3}
. Then
H_{2}
must have infinite intersection with either
b
or
b^{\ast}
. But then
b
must distinguish
H_{2}
from either
H_{1}
or
H_{3}
, a contradiction.
∎

This allows us to define the
consolidation
of a peripheral system
\mathcal{H}
, which we denote as
\mathcal{H}^{\circ}
, via

\mathcal{H}^{\circ}:=\Bigg\{\bigcup_{H\in C}H:C\in\mathcal{H}/\sim\Bigg\}.

Note that naïve consolidation may sometimes change the set of elliptic cuts, as is illustrated by the following examples.

Example 3.19
.

Consider the following examples:

(1)

Let
\Gamma
be a bi-infinite path with
V(\Gamma)=\mathbf{Z}
identified in the natural way. Let

\mathcal{H}=\Big\{\{m:m>n\}:n\in\mathbf{Z}\Big\}.

Then
{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathcal{H}}(\Gamma)={\mathscr{B}}(\Gamma)
. However,
\mathcal{H}^{\circ}=\{V(\Gamma)\}
, and so it follows that
{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathcal{H}^{\circ}}(\Gamma)=\mathscr{F}(V(\Gamma))
.

(2)

For a thin example, we may return our gaze to Example
3.6
. We see in this example that
\mathcal{H}^{\circ}=\{V(\Gamma)\}
, and thus once again we conclude that

{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathcal{H}}(\Gamma)={\mathscr{B}}(\Gamma)\neq\mathscr{F}(V(\Gamma))={\mathscr{B}}_{\mathcal{H}^{\circ}}(\Gamma).

For balance, we present the following interesting example of consolidation of peripheral systems in planar graphs, which demonstrates how consolidation can return a much more ‘natural’ peripheral system.

Example 3.20
.

Let
\Gamma
be an infinite-ended, 2-connected, locally finite, planar graph, with some fixed embedding
\vartheta:|\Gamma|\to\mathbb{S}^{2}
of its Freudenthal compactification in the 2-sphere. This embedding exists by
[
19
, Lem. 12]
. Each complementary components of the image is a topological disk, and its boundary is a simple closed curve
[
19
, Prop. 3]
. These simple closed curves maybe correspond to either finite facial cycles in
\Gamma
, or possibly infinite topological circuits, called the
infinite facial circuits
. In general, an infinite facial circuit is formed of a disjoint union of bi-infinite simple rays, called
facial rays
, together with a collection of ends.

Consider the peripheral system
\mathcal{F}=\{V(R):\text{$R$ is a facial ray}\}
. It is not too hard to see that two facial rays
R_{1},R_{2}\in\mathcal{F}
are distinguishable if and only if they do not lie on a common facial circuit. Indeed, this follows from cut-cycle duality in planar graphs. In particular, we have that

\mathcal{F}^{\circ}=\{V(C):\text{$C\subset|\Gamma|$ is a facial circuit}\}.

Thus, consolidation has recovered a very natural partition of our peripherals. See Figure
2
for a cartoon.

The above examples show that we must certainly take some care while consolidating our peripheral systems, but doing so can really help illuminate the relationship between our peripherals.
To clarify the situation seen in the bad examples of Example
3.19
, we prove the following.

Proposition 3.21
.

Let
\Gamma
be a connected graph, and
\mathcal{H}
a peripheral system. Then
{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathcal{H}^{\circ}}(\Gamma)\subseteq{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathcal{H}}(\Gamma)
. If
\mathcal{H}
is tame, then we have equality.

Proof.

Suppose that
b
is
\mathcal{H}^{\circ}
-elliptic. Then it is easy to see that
b
is
\mathcal{H}
-elliptic, since elements of
\mathcal{H}^{\circ}
are just unions of elements of
\mathcal{H}
. This proves the first part of the proposition.

Assume further that
\mathcal{H}
is tame, and suppose that
b
is
\mathcal{H}
-elliptic, but there exists
H\in\mathcal{H}^{\circ}
such that both
b\cap H
and
b^{\ast}\cap H
are infinite. Write

H=\bigcup_{H^{\prime}\in C}H^{\prime}
,
where
C
is some
\sim
-equivalence class in
\mathcal{H}
. Since
b
is
\mathcal{H}
-elliptic and
\mathcal{H}
is tame, there must exist
H_{1},H_{2}\in C
such that
H_{1}\cap b
and
H_{2}\cap b^{\ast}
are both infinite. But then
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
are infinite and distinguishable, which contradicts the assumption that
H_{1}\sim H_{2}
. This proves the proposition.
∎

We now verify that consolidation preserves tameness.

Proposition 3.22
.

Let
\Gamma
be a connected graph and
\mathcal{H}
a peripheral system. Suppose
\mathcal{H}
is tame. Then
\mathcal{H}^{\circ}
is tame.

Proof.

By Proposition
3.21
, since
\mathcal{H}
is tame we know that

{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathcal{H}^{\circ}}(\Gamma)={\mathscr{B}}_{\mathcal{H}}(\Gamma)
.
Let
b\in{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathcal{H}}(\Gamma)
.
Suppose then that there exists infinitely many
H\in\mathcal{H}^{\circ}
which intersect both
b
and
b^{\ast}
, say
H_{1},H_{2},\ldots\in\mathcal{H}^{\circ}
. Without loss of generality, we may assume that each
H_{i}
is a union

H_{i}=\bigcup_{j}H_{i}^{j}

of at least two distinct, infinite, pairwise indistinguishable elements of
\mathcal{H}
. Since
{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathcal{H}^{\circ}}(\Gamma)={\mathscr{B}}_{\mathcal{H}}(\Gamma)
, we deduce that for each
i
there is some
j
such that
H_{i}^{j}
intersects both
b
and
b^{\ast}
. Let
H_{i}^{\prime}
denote such a choice. We necessarily have that
H_{i}^{\prime}\neq H_{j}^{\prime}
for all
i\neq j
, but then
\mathcal{H}
itself is not tame, a contradiction.
∎

Of course, we must verify that consolidation actually produces separable peripheral systems. This is the content of the next proposition.

Proposition 3.23
.

Let
\Gamma
be a connected graph and
\mathcal{H}
a tame peripheral system. Then
\mathcal{H}^{\circ}
is separable.

Proof.

We may assume without loss of generality that
\mathcal{H}^{\circ}
contains at least two infinite elements, lest there is nothing to prove.
Let
H_{1},H_{2}\in\mathcal{H}^{\circ}
be distinct, infinite elements, say

H_{i}=\bigcup_{K\in C_{i}}K

for some some distinct equivalence classes
C_{1},C_{2}\in\mathcal{H}/\sim
. Let
K_{i}\in C_{i}
be arbitrary. Note that the
K_{i}
are necessarily infinite. Since
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
are not in the same equivalence class, they are distinguishable. Thus, there exists some
b\in{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathcal{H}}(\Gamma)
such that
K_{1}\cap b
is infinite and
K_{2}\cap b^{\ast}
is infinite. By Proposition
3.21
, we have that
b
is also
\mathcal{H}^{\circ}
-elliptic. In particular, we must necessarily have that
b
separates
H_{1}
from
H_{2}
.
∎

One major benefit of working with separable peripheral systems that we gain access to the following dichotomy for pairs of ends.

Proposition 3.24
.

Let
\Gamma
be a connected, locally finite graph and
\mathcal{H}
a separable, tame peripheral system. Then for all distinct
\omega_{1},\omega_{2}\in\Omega(\Gamma)
, exactly one of the following holds:

(1)

There exists
b\in{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathcal{H}}(\Gamma)
separating
\omega_{1}
and
\omega_{2}
, or

(2)

There exists
H\in\mathcal{H}
such that
\omega_{1},\omega_{2}\in\Lambda(H)
.

Proof.

Let
H\in\mathcal{H}
, and
\omega\in\Omega(\Gamma)\setminus\Lambda(H)
. Of course, if no such
\omega
exists then there is nothing to prove. If there exists
H^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}
such that
\omega\in\Lambda(H^{\prime})
, then since
\mathcal{H}
is separable we are done. Thus, we may assume without loss of generality that no such
H^{\prime}
exists.

Since
\Lambda(H)
is closed in
\Omega(\Gamma)
, there exists a clopen set
W\ni\omega
such that
\Lambda(H)\cap W=\emptyset
. Write
W^{\prime}=\Omega(\Gamma)\setminus W
. Choose
b\in{\mathscr{B}}(\Gamma)
such that
\Lambda(b)=W^{\prime}
and
\Lambda(b^{\ast})=W
. If
b
is
\mathcal{H}
-elliptic then we are done, else there exist peripherals in
\mathcal{H}
which have infinite intersection with both
b
and
b^{\ast}
. Since
\mathcal{H}
is tame, this collection is finite, say
H_{1},\ldots,H_{k}\in\mathcal{H}
. For each
H_{j}
, by separability we have that there exists
c_{j}\in{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathcal{H}}(\Gamma)
such that
c_{j}\cap H_{j}
is finite and
c_{j}^{\ast}\cap H
is finite, i.e.
c_{j}
separates
H_{j}
from
H
.
Consider now the cut

a:=b\cap\bigcap_{j=1}^{k}c_{j}.

We first observe that
a
is
\mathcal{H}
-elliptic. Indeed, suppose some
H^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}
had infinite intersection with both
a
and
a^{\ast}
. Then
H^{\prime}
has infinite intersection with
b
and with all of the
c_{j}
. Since the
c_{j}
are
\mathcal{H}
-elliptic, we have that
H^{\prime}\cap c_{j}^{\ast}
is finite. Thus,
H^{\prime}\cap b^{\ast}
is infinite. But then
H^{\prime}=H_{j}
for some
j
. This contradicts the fact that
c_{j}\cap H_{j}
is finite. Thus
a
is
\mathcal{H}
-elliptic. Also, observe that
a
separates
\omega
from
\Lambda(H)
. Indeed, we have that
\Lambda(H)\subset\Lambda(a)
since
\Lambda(H)\subset\Lambda(b)\cap\bigcap_{j}\Lambda(c_{j})
, and
\omega\in\Lambda(b^{\ast})\subset\Lambda(a^{\ast})
. This proves the proposition in the case where at least one of the
\omega_{i}
is contained in the limit set of some peripheral.

Now, fix distinct ends
\omega_{1}
,
\omega_{2}
such that neither
\omega_{i}
is contained in the limit set of any peripheral. We will proceed very similarly, applying the previous case to aid us.
Fix
b\in{\mathscr{B}}(\Gamma)
which separates
\omega_{1}
and
\omega_{2}
, say
\omega_{1}\in\Lambda(b)
,
\omega_{2}\in\Lambda(b^{\ast})
. If
b
is
\mathcal{H}
-elliptic then we are done. Otherwise there are finitely many
H_{1},\ldots,H_{k}\in\mathcal{H}
which have infinite intersection with both
b
and
b^{\ast}
. By assumption, neither
\omega_{i}
is contained in the limit set of any of the
H_{j}
. Applying the previous case, for each
j
there exists
c_{j}\in{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathcal{H}}(\Gamma)
such that
\omega_{1}\in\Lambda(c_{j})
and
\Lambda(H_{j})\subset\Lambda(c_{j}^{\ast})
. As before, consider the cut
a:=b\cap\bigcap_{j}c_{j}
. We can verify in exactly the same way that
a
is
\mathcal{H}
-elliptic, and separates
\omega_{1}
from
\omega_{2}
. The proposition now follows.
∎

Everything above allows us to prove the following helpful result.

Theorem 3.25
.

Let
\Gamma
be a connected, locally finite, quasi-transitive
G
-graph. Let
\mathcal{H}
be a thin,
G
-invariant peripheral system. Then there exists a tame, minimised, separable,
G
-invariant peripheral system
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
such that
{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathcal{H}^{\prime}}(\Gamma)={\mathscr{B}}_{\mathcal{H}}(\Gamma)
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}=\mathcal{H}^{\bullet\circ}
. That is,
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
is the consolidation of the minimisation of
\mathcal{H}
. Since
\mathcal{H}
is tame, we have by Theorem
3.15
that
\mathcal{H}^{\bullet}
is tame, and thus by Proposition
3.22
we have that
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
is also tame. We trivially have that
\mathcal{H}^{\bullet}
is minimised, from which it immediately follows that
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
is minimised. By Proposition
3.23
, since
\mathcal{H}^{\bullet}
is tame we have that
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
is separable.
Finally, we have by Propositions
3.12
and
3.21
, that

{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathcal{H}}(\Gamma)={\mathscr{B}}_{\mathcal{H}^{\bullet}}(\Gamma)={\mathscr{B}}_{\mathcal{H}^{\prime}}(\Gamma).

The second equality uses the fact that
\mathcal{H}^{\bullet}
is tame. The theorem follows.
∎

3.5.
Coning-off a peripheral system

We now give an alternative interpretation of relative Boolean ring. Given a connected graph
\Gamma
and a peripheral system
\mathcal{H}
, form a new graph
\widehat{\Gamma}_{\mathcal{H}}
as follows:

(1)

For every
H\in\mathcal{H}
add a new vertex
v_{H}
to
\Gamma
, called the
cone vertex at
H
.

(2)

Connect every
u\in H
to
v_{H}
by an edge.

The graph
\widehat{\Gamma}_{\mathcal{H}}
is called the
cone of
\Gamma
over
\mathcal{H}
. For the present paper, the utility of this construction lies in the following.

Proposition 3.26
.

Let
\Gamma
be a connected graph and
\mathcal{H}
a peripheral system. Then the inclusion
\Gamma\hookrightarrow\widehat{\Gamma}_{\mathcal{H}}
induces a ring homomorphism

F:{\mathscr{B}}(\widehat{\Gamma}_{\mathcal{H}})\to{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathcal{H}}(\Gamma).

We have that
F
is surjective if and only if
\mathcal{H}
is tame, and
F
is injective if and only if every
H\in\mathcal{H}
is infinite. If
\Gamma
is a
G
-graph and
\mathcal{H}
is
G
-invariant then
F
is
G
-equivariant.

Proof.

Let
F:{\mathscr{B}}(\widehat{\Gamma}_{\mathcal{H}})\to{\mathscr{B}}(\Gamma)
be given by restriction. That is,

F(b)=b\cap V(\Gamma)
.
It is routine to check that this is a (
G
-equivariant) homomorphism of Boolean rings.

We claim that the image of
F
is contained in
{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathcal{H}}(\Gamma)
. Firstly, suppose for the sake of a contradiction that there exists some
b\in{\mathscr{B}}(\widehat{\Gamma}_{\mathcal{H}})
and some
H\in\mathcal{H}
such that both
H\cap F(b)
and
H\cap F(b^{\ast})
are infinite. Now, either
v_{H}\in b
or
v_{H}\not\in b
. In either case we see that
\delta b
is infinite, a contradiction. The claim follows.

Suppose now that
\mathcal{H}
is tame. We claim that
F
is surjective. Fix
b\in{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathcal{H}}\Gamma
. We extend
b
to an element
b^{\prime}\in{\mathscr{B}}(\widehat{\Gamma}_{\mathcal{H}})
by including a cone vertex
v_{H}
if and only if
b\cap H
is infinite. It follows from the tameness of
\mathcal{H}
that
\delta b^{\prime}
is finite. Clearly
F(b^{\prime})=b
, and so
F
is surjective. Conversely, suppose
\mathcal{H}
is not tame, so there exists
b\in{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathcal{H}}(\Gamma)
such that both
b\cap H
and
b^{\ast}\cap H
are non-empty for infinitely many
H\in\mathcal{H}
. Let
S\subset\mathcal{H}
denote the set of elements of
\mathcal{H}
which intersect both
b
and
b^{\ast}
. Suppose there exists
b^{\prime}\in F^{-1}(b)
. Then, for all
H\in S
, we have that each
v_{H}
is abutted by an edge lying in
\delta b^{\prime}
. Since no two of the
v_{H}
are adjacent, these edges contributed to
\delta b^{\prime}
are pairwise distinct. Thus, since
S
is infinite we conclude that
\delta b^{\prime}
must be infinite and so
b^{\prime}\not\in{\mathscr{B}}(\widehat{\Gamma}_{\mathcal{H}})
. It follows that such a
b^{\prime}
does not exist, and so
F
is not surjective.

Finally, suppose every
H\in\mathcal{H}
is infinite. We need only show that
F
has trivial kernel. Here, the 0-element is the empty set. Clearly any
b\in{\mathscr{B}}(\widehat{\Gamma}_{\mathcal{H}})
which satisfies
F(b)=\emptyset
cannot contain any cone vertices lest
b
must have infinite coboundary. Thus, any element
b
such that
F(b)
is empty must itself be empty. Conversely, suppose some
H\in\mathcal{H}
is finite. Then the singleton
b=\{v_{H}\}
has finite coboundary and so is an element of
{\mathscr{B}}(\widehat{\Gamma}_{\mathcal{H}})
. However,
F(b)=\emptyset
, and so
F
has non-trivial kernel and is not injective.
∎

We remark that Proposition
3.26
is incredibly useful in this setting, since it allows us to reduce most questions about
{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathcal{H}}
to questions about
{\mathscr{B}}
on a different graph. It is this proposition which motivated the definition of a tame peripheral. As a first application, we learn the following strong fact for free.

Theorem 3.27
.

Let
\Gamma
be a connected
G
-graph and
\mathcal{H}
a tame,
G
-invariant peripheral system. Then
{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathcal{H}}(\Gamma)
contains a
G
-invariant, nested set which additively generates
{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathcal{H}}(\Gamma)
.

Proof.

We know by Theorem
2.4
and Proposition
2.10
that
{\mathscr{B}}(\widehat{\Gamma}_{\mathcal{H}})
admits a
G
-invariant, nested, additive generating set
E
. Let
F:{\mathscr{B}}(\widehat{\Gamma}_{\mathcal{H}})\to{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathcal{H}}(\Gamma)
be the
G
-equivariant homomorphism introduced in Proposition
3.26
. Since
\mathcal{H}
is tame,
F
is surjective. Clearly
F
preserves nesting. We may thus take
F(E)
as our desired generating set.
∎

Note that we cannot generally obtain a generating set consisting of tight elements, contrary to the situation for
{\mathscr{B}}(\Gamma)
. Indeed, it could be the case that
{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathcal{H}}(\Gamma)
contains no tight elements at all.

.

Let

denote the 4-regular tree, viewed as the standard Cayley graph of the free group

. Consider the peripheral system

consisting of left cosets of the cyclic subgroups
\langle a\rangle
and

. This is depicted in Figure

.

Corollary 3.29
.

Let
\Gamma
be a connected
G
-graph and
\mathcal{H}
a tame,
G
-invariant peripheral system. Suppose

Let
S=\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{k}\}\subset{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathcal{H}}(\Gamma)
be a finite set such that

generates

as a ring. Let

be a nested,

-invariant generating set, given by Theorem

as a sum of elements in

. But then

-finite,

which generates

as a ring. We may assume without harm that

is closed under taking complements, as adding these complements doesn’t affect the fact that

G

3.6.
Relative accessibility

We now define what it means for a graph to be accessible relative to a peripheral system.

Definition 3.30

(Relative accessibility)
.

Let
\Gamma
be a connected, locally finite graph, and
\mathcal{H}
a peripheral system.
We then say that
\Gamma
is
accessible relative to
\mathcal{H}
if there exists
k\in\mathbf{N}
such that for every pair of distinct ends
\omega_{1}
,
\omega_{2}
of
\Gamma
, if there exists an elliptic cut
b
separating
\omega_{1}
and
\omega_{2}
, then there exists one satisfying
|\delta b|\leq k
.

The goal of this section is to characterise relative accessibility in quasi-transitive graphs in terms of the generation properties of the subring
{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathcal{H}}
.
To this end, we will prove the following theorem, which is one of the main results of this paper. This should be compared with
[
21
, Prop. 6.2]
.

We will prove this in a few stages, as its surprisingly subtle. Throughout the remainder of this section, fix
\Gamma
be a connected, locally finite, quasi-transitive
G
-graph, and
\mathcal{H}
a thin,
G
-invariant peripheral system.
By Theorem
3.25
, we may assume without loss of generality that
\mathcal{H}
is tame, minimised, and separable. The rough plan is then as follows. First, we may bipartition
\mathcal{H}
into two parts

, where

\mathcal{I}=\{H\in\mathcal{H}:|\Lambda(H)|=\infty\},\ \ \mathcal{T}=\{H\in\mathcal{H}:|\Lambda(H)|=2\}.

If
\mathcal{T}=\emptyset
, then the proof becomes substantially shorter (see Lemmas
3.32
,
3.33
). The task is to somehow reduce to this case, and show that we may safely delete
\mathcal{T}
without affecting relative accessibility. Note that
{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathcal{I}}(\Gamma)\neq{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathcal{H}}(\Gamma)
in general, so this reduction is a bit subtle.

By Lemma

and Remark

, we may assume without loss of generality that for every

, we have that every connected component

satisfies

.

We first observe the easy direction.

Lemma 3.31
.

If
{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathcal{H}}(\Gamma)
is a finitely generated
G
-module then
\Gamma
is accessible relative to
\mathcal{H}
.

Proof.

Let
S
be a
G
-invariant,
G
-finite generating set of
{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathcal{H}}(\Gamma)
as an abelian group. Let
\omega_{1}
,
\omega_{2}
be two ends and
b\in{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathcal{H}}(\Gamma)
be an
\mathcal{H}
-elliptic cut which separates them. Then, by Proposition
2.8
, there exists
b^{\prime}\in S
which separates
\omega_{1}
from
\omega_{2}
. Since
S
is
G
-finite, there is a uniform bound on
|\delta b^{\prime}|
. The claim follows.
∎

We now proceed with the more difficult direction. We will begin by first only considering
\mathcal{I}
, ignoring the two-ended peripherals contributed by
\mathcal{T}
. Later, we will see why this is sufficient.
Let

F:{\mathscr{B}}(\widehat{\Gamma}_{\mathcal{I}})\to{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathcal{I}}(\Gamma)

denote the isomorphism given by Proposition
3.26
. To ease notation, we will use a caret to denote the inverse
F^{-1}
; i.e.
F^{-1}(b)=:\widehat{b}
. Our first claim establishes some quantitative control on how this map effects coboundaries.

Lemma 3.32
.

There exists an increasing function
\rho:\mathbf{N}\to\mathbf{N}
such that for all
b\in{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathcal{I}}(\Gamma)
, we have

|\delta\widehat{b}|\leq\rho(|\delta b|).

Proof.

We will prove this using an isoperimetric argument.
If the claim were false, there would exist a sequence
(b_{n})
in
{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathcal{I}}(\Gamma)
, with
|\delta b_{n}|
uniformly bounded, such that
|\delta\widehat{b}_{n}|\to\infty
. Without loss of generality, we may assume that
|\delta b_{n}|=|\delta b_{m}|
for all
m,n>0
.

Note that, given
b\in{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathcal{I}}(\Gamma)
, we may compute
|\delta\widehat{b}|
via the formula

|\delta\widehat{b}|=|\delta b|+\sum_{H}|b\cap H|+\sum_{H^{\prime}}|b^{\ast}\cap H^{\prime}|,

where the first sum is taken over those
H\in\mathcal{I}
such that
b\cap H
is finite, and the second is taken over those
H^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}
such that
b^{\ast}\cap H^{\prime}
is finite.

Back to our sequence
(b_{n})
, we may assume without loss of generality that, say, the first sum
\sum_{H}|b_{n}\cap H|\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
. Since every
H\in\mathcal{I}
has big components, we have that for each
n>0
there exists a
H_{n}\in\mathcal{I}
, and a component
K_{n}\subset H_{n}
, such that
b_{n}\cap K_{n}|
is finite for all
n
, and
|b_{n}\cap K_{n}|\to\infty
. Passing to another subsequence, we may assume without loss of generality that all
K_{n}
are
G
-orbit equivalent. In particular, by translating we may assume that
K_{n}=K_{m}
for all
m,n>0
. Let us write

to simplify notation. Note that
\Delta:=\Gamma[K]
itself is a connected, locally finite, quasi-transitive graph. However,

contains a sequence of arbitrarily large finite subsets

such that
\delta A_{n}
is uniformly bounded. This implies that
\Delta
is two-ended. In particular,
|\Lambda(K)|\leq 2
, but this contradicts the fact that
|\Lambda(K)|=\infty
. The claim follows.
∎

We will use Lemma
3.32
to prove the following.

Lemma 3.33
.

Suppose
\Gamma
is accessible relative to
\mathcal{I}
. Then
{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathcal{I}}(\Gamma)
is a finitely generated
G
-module.

Proof.

By relative accessibility, quasi-transitivity, and Proposition
2.9
, let
S\subset{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathcal{I}}(\Gamma)
be a subset with the following properties:

(1)

|\delta b|
is uniformly bounded across all

.

(2)

S
generates
{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathcal{I}}(\Gamma)
as a ring.

Consider the lift
\widehat{S}:=F^{-1}(S)

\widehat{S}

as a ring. By Lemma

,

, and so we deduce that

Since
\mathcal{I}
is thin and the action of

This essentially completes the proof in the case where

S=S_{\mathrm{ell}}\sqcup S_{\mathrm{hyp}}

S_{\mathrm{ell}}:=\{b\in S:\text{$b$ is $\mathcal{H}$-elliptic}\},\ \ \ S_{\mathrm{hyp}}:=\{b\in S:\text{$b$ is not $\mathcal{H}$-elliptic}\}.

Note that if
S_{\mathrm{hyp}}=\emptyset
, then there is nothing to prove, so let’s assume this set is non-empty. For each
b\in S_{\mathrm{hyp}}
, let
H_{1}^{b}\ldots,H_{k_{b}}^{b}\in\mathcal{T}
denote the (necessarily finite and non-empty) collection of
\mathcal{T}
-peripherals which have infinite intersection with both
b
and
b^{\ast}
.

Every
b\in S_{\mathrm{hyp}}
induces a non-trivial bipartition of
\Omega(\Gamma)
into clopen sets
W_{1}^{b}\sqcup W_{2}^{b}
. Let
\Lambda(H_{j}^{b})=\{\lambda_{j,1}^{b},\lambda_{j,2}^{b}\}
, indexed so that
\lambda^{b}_{j,i}\in W_{i}^{b}
.
We now apply Claim
3.36
, and for each
b\in S_{\mathrm{hyp}}
we obtain pairwise disjoint neighbourhoods
U_{j,i}^{b}
of
\lambda_{j,i}^{b}
, such that
U_{j,i}^{b}\subset W_{i}^{b}
, and such that for all
j\in\{1,\ldots,k_{b}\}
, and all
p\in U_{i,j}^{b}
, there exists
g\in G_{H_{j}^{b}}
such that:

(1)

gp\in W_{1}^{b}\setminus\bigcup_{\ell}U_{\ell,1}^{b}
, and

(2)

gU_{j,2}^{b}\cap W_{1}^{b}=\emptyset
.

Now, apply Claim
3.35
, and for each
j\in\{1,\ldots,k_{b}\}
find
c^{b}_{j}\in{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathcal{H}}(\Gamma)
such that for all
\omega\in\Omega(\Gamma)\setminus(U_{j,1}^{b}\cup U_{j,2}^{b})
, we have that
c_{j}^{b}
separates
\omega
from
\Lambda(H_{j}^{b})
. These are oriented in such a way that
H_{j}^{b}\cap c_{j}^{b}
is finite.

Now, consider the set

R=S_{\mathrm{ell}}\cup\Big\{c_{j}^{b}:b\in S_{\mathrm{hyp}},\ j=1,\ldots,k_{b}\Big\}\cup\Bigg\{b\cap\bigcap_{j=1}^{k_{b}}c_{j}^{b}:b\in S_{\mathrm{hyp}}\Bigg\}.

Note that
R
is finite. We have the following two claims.

Claim 3.38
.

We have that
R\subset{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathcal{H}}(\Gamma)
.

Proof.

Indeed, it is sufficient to show that
a=b\cap\bigcap_{j=1}^{k_{b}}c_{j}^{b}
is
\mathcal{T}
-elliptic for every
b\in S_{\mathrm{hyp}}
. Suppose some
H\in\mathcal{T}
has infinite intersection with both
a
and
a^{\ast}
. Then
H
has infinite intersection with
b
and with every
c_{j}^{b}
. Each
c_{j}^{b}
is
\mathcal{H}
-elliptic, so since
H\cap a^{\ast}
is infinite, we must have that
H\cap b^{\ast}
is infinite. But then
H=H_{j}^{b}
for some fixed
j
. But this contradicts the fact that
H\cap c_{j}^{b}
is infinite, which is false by construction. It follows that
R\subset{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathcal{H}}(\Gamma)
.
∎

Claim 3.39
.

For all distinct pairs
\omega_{1},\omega_{2}\in\Omega(\Gamma)
, either
\omega_{1},\omega_{2}\in\Lambda(H)
for some
H\in\mathcal{H}
, or there exists
a\in G\cdot R
, such that
a
separates
\omega_{1}
and
\omega_{2}
.

Proof.

Fix
\omega_{1}
and
\omega_{2}
, and assume without loss of generality that they do not lie in the limit set of some common peripheral. After translating, we may assume that
\omega_{1}
and
\omega_{2}
are separated by some fixed
b\in S
. Assume without loss of generality that
b\in S_{\mathrm{hyp}}
, and that
\omega_{i}\in W_{i}^{b}
. We have a few cases to consider.

Case 1.
It could be the case that some
c_{j}^{b}
separates
\omega_{1}
and
\omega_{2}
, in which case we are done.

Case 2.
Suppose that no
c_{j}^{b}
separates
\omega_{1}
and
\omega_{2}
, and that both
\omega_{1}
and
\omega_{2}
are contained in (the limit set of) the intersection

\bigcap_{j=1}^{k_{b}}c_{j}^{b}
.
Then it is easy to see that

b\cap\bigcap_{j=1}^{k_{b}}c_{j}^{b}

separates
\omega_{1}
from
\omega_{2}
. In this case, we are done.

Case 3.
Suppose that no
c_{j}^{b}
separates
\omega_{1}
and
\omega_{2}
, but the hypotheses of Case 2 do not hold. Note that, by construction, the limit sets of the
(c_{j}^{b})^{\ast}
are pairwise disjoint, since

\Lambda(c^{b}_{j})\cap(U_{j,1}^{b}\cup U_{j,2}^{b})=\emptyset.

In particular, in this case we must have that
\omega_{i}\in U_{j,i}^{b}
for some fixed
j\in\{1,\ldots,k_{b}\}
. By assumption, we do not have that both
\omega_{i}
lie in
\Lambda(H_{j}^{b})
, so let’s say without loss of generality that
\omega_{1}
does not lie in
\Lambda(H_{j}^{b})
. Now, applying Claim
3.36
, we have by construction that there exists
h\in G_{H_{j}^{b}}
such that
h\omega_{1}\in W_{1}^{b}\setminus\bigcup_{\ell}U_{\ell,1}^{b}
, and
h\omega_{2}\in W_{2}
. Note that
h\omega_{1}
,
h\omega_{2}
are still separated by
b
. By replacing
\omega_{1}
and
\omega_{2}
with their
h
-translates, we now have that we must be in either Case 1 or Case 2. In particular,
\omega_{1}
and
\omega_{2}
are separated by some
G
-translate of some element in
R
.

These three cases are exhaustive, and so the claim follows.
∎

It now follows from Proposition
2.9
and Corollary
3.29
that
{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathcal{H}}(\Gamma)
is a finitely generated
G
-module. This concludes the proof of the lemma.
∎

A
now follows by chasing the following cycle of implications:

4.
Finitely generated groups

We now discuss how our definition of relative accessibility relates to the usual group-theoretic notion. Throughout this section, we let
G
be a finitely generated group, and fix a choice of locally finite Cayley graph
\Gamma
. Let
\mathcal{P}
be a finite collection of subgroups of
G
, and let

\mathcal{H}=\{gH:g\in G,\ H\in\mathcal{P}\}

denote the set of left cosets of elements of
\mathcal{P}
. Note that since
\mathcal{P}
is finite, this is a thin,
G
-invariant peripheral system of
\Gamma
.

4.1.
Algebraic relative accessibility

We will need to briefly establish some terminology relating to actions of
G
upon trees. Our conventions will mostly follow those set out in
[
10
]
.

Let
T
be a simplicial tree. We say that
T
is a
G
-tree
if
G
acts on
T
by automorphisms. We say that this action is

without inversions
if, given any
g\in G
, if
g
fixes an edge then it fixes the two endpoints of said edge. Note that this condition is fairly inconsequential, as it can always be assured by simply passing to the barycentric subdivision of
T
.
Call
T

non-trivial
if there the action has no global fixed point, and
cocompact
if
T/G
is compact.
A
G
-tree is
minimal
if there does not exist a proper
G
-invariant subtree. It is a standard fact that, since
G
is finitely generated, every non-trivial
G
-tree contains a unique minimal
G
-invariant subtree, and the action on this minimal subtree is cocompact.
If
T
is a minimal
G
-tree without inversions, then we call
T
a
finite splitting
of
G
.
Given two
G
-trees
T_{1}
,
T_{2}
, we say that
T_{1}

dominates

T_{2}
if there exists a
G
-equivariant map
f:V(T_{1})\to V(T_{2})
. We will call such an
f
a
G
-map
, and often abuse notation by writing
f:T_{1}\to T_{2}
.
We say that a subgroup
H\leq G
acts
elliptically
on
T
if it fixes a vertex.
It is easy to see that
T_{1}
dominates
T_{2}
if and only if every vertex stabiliser of
T_{1}
acts elliptically on
T_{2}
. Also, domination is not affected by passing to subdivisions, or replacing the
T_{i}
with
G
-invariant subtrees.

A
G
-tree
T
is said to be
\mathcal{P}
-elliptic
if for all
H\in\mathcal{P}
, we have that
H
acts elliptically on
T
.
The group is said to be
one-ended relative to
\mathcal{P}
if every finite splitting of
G
is not
\mathcal{P}
-elliptic.
The splitting
T
is said to be
terminal
if
T
dominates any other
\mathcal{P}
-elliptic finite splitting
T^{\prime}
. We now have the following definition.

Definition 4.1

(Algebraic relative accessibility)
.

If there exists a terminal
\mathcal{P}
-elliptic finite splitting of
G
, then we say that
G
is
accessible relative to
\mathcal{P}
.

4.2.
Structure trees of nested sets

We now recount the construction of the
structure tree
of a nested subset of
{\mathscr{B}}(\Gamma)
. This construction is due to Dunwoody
[
3
]
, though we will follow the more modern conventions set out by Roller
[
20
]
.

The following notation will be convenient. Given a tree
T
, write

\mathscr{E}(T)=\{b\in{\mathscr{B}}(T):|\delta b|=1\}.

That is,
\mathscr{E}(T)
consists of those single-edge cuts. Note that
\mathscr{E}(T)
additively generates
{\mathscr{B}}(T)
.

Let
\Gamma
be a connected
G
-graph.
Let
E\subset{\mathscr{B}}(\Gamma)
be a symmetric,
G
-invariant,
G
-finite subset. An
ultrafilter
on
E
is a subset
U\subset E
such that:

(1)

For all
b\in E
, exactly one of
b
or
b^{\ast}
lies in
U
.

(2)

If
b\subset b^{\prime}
and
b\in U
, then
b^{\prime}\in U
.

We say that
U
is
well-founded
if for all
b\in U
, the set
\{a\subset b:a\in U\}
is finite.
Form a tree
T=T(E)
, called the
structure tree
of
E
, as follows:

(1)

V(T)=\{\text{well-founded ultrafilters on $E$}\}
,

(2)

Connect
U_{1}
,
U_{2}
by an edge if and only if
|U_{1}+U_{2}|=2
.

This is a indeed a simplicial tree, upon which
G
acts with finitely many orbits. There is a canonical equivariant bijection between
E
and
\mathscr{E}(T)
, which extends to an isomorphism of
G
-modules between
{\mathscr{B}}(T)
and the submodule of
{\mathscr{B}}(\Gamma)
generated by
E
, and in particular a canonical embedding
{\mathscr{B}}(T)\hookrightarrow{\mathscr{B}}(\Gamma)
.

The following observation is straightforward.

Proposition 4.2
.

If
E\subset{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathcal{H}}(\Gamma)
is nested,
G
-invariant, and
G
-finite, then the structure tree
T=T(E)
is
\mathcal{P}
-elliptic.

Proof.

Suppose
T
is not
\mathcal{P}
-elliptic, so there exists some
H\in\mathcal{P}
which acts on
T
with unbounded orbits.
If
H
contains an element acting loxodromically on
T
, then it is easy to see that some
b\in E
must satisfy that both
b\cap H
and
b^{\ast}\cap H
are infinite. Thus,
H
acts parabolically on
T
, fixing a unique end
\omega\in\Omega(T)
. However, this will contradict the fact that
T
has finite edge stabilisers. Indeed, let
P
be a geodesic in
T
, then for all
g\in H
, there must exist a tail
P^{\prime}\subset P
such that
gP^{\prime}=P^{\prime}
, lest
g
be acting loxodromically. From here, it follows quickly that
T
contains infinite edge stabilisers.
∎

4.3.
Finite splittings and cuts

We now discuss how finite splittings of
G
relate to
{\mathscr{B}}(\Gamma)
.
First, we have the following lemma.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\Delta
be some connected, cocompact
G
-graph with finite edge stabilisers, and
T
a
G
-tree with finite edge stabilisers. Let
f:V(\Delta)\to V(T)
be a
G
-equivariant map. Then the map

F:{\mathscr{B}}(T)\to{\mathscr{B}}(\Delta),\ \ F(b)=f^{-1}(b)

is a well-defined, monotone, complement-respecting,
G
-equivariant ring homomorphism.
Moreover, if
T
is minimal then we have that

F^{-1}(\mathscr{F}(\Delta))\subset\mathscr{F}(T).

Proof.

Since preimages distribute over symmetric difference and intersection, it is easy to see that
F
is
G
-equivariant homomorphism of rings
F:{\mathscr{B}}(T)\to\mathscr{P}(\Delta)
. It is also clear that
F
is monotone and complement-respecting. We must verify that
F(b)
has finite coboundary for every
b\in{\mathscr{B}}(T)
.
It is sufficient to consider the case where
b\in\mathscr{E}(T)
, say
\delta b=\{e\}
.
Write
c=F(b)
, and assume that
\delta c
is infinite.
Let
e_{1},e_{2},\ldots
denote the edges in
\delta c
. Let
u_{i}
,
v_{i}
denote the endpoints of
e_{i}
, say
u_{i}\in c
and
v_{i}\in c^{\ast}
.
This means that
f(u_{i})\in b
, and
f(v_{i})\in b^{\ast}
.
Pick orbit representatives
e^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,e_{k},^{\prime}\in E(\Delta)
.
For each
i>0
, fix
g_{i}\in G
such that
e_{i}=g_{i}e_{\ell}^{\prime}
for some (unique)
e_{\ell}^{\prime}
. Consider the geodesic
P_{i}=[f(u_{i}),f(v_{i})]
, which must contain the edge
e
. Note that
\bigcup_{i}g_{i}^{-1}P_{i}
is finite, since the endpoints of the
g_{i}^{-1}P_{i}
are exactly the images of the endpoints of the
e^{\prime}_{\ell}
, of which there are finitely many.
This induces a finite partition on
\{g_{i}\}
, where
g_{i}
,
g_{j}
lie in the same part if and only if
g_{i}^{-1}e=g_{j}^{-1}e
. Let
\pi
be a part of this partition. Fix
g\in\pi
, and let
h\in\pi
be arbitrary. Then

g^{-1}e=h^{-1}e\implies hg^{-1}\in G_{e}\implies h\in G_{e}g,

Since
G_{e}
is finite, we deduce that
\pi\subset G_{e}g
is finite. In particular, this implies that
\{g_{i}\}
is finite and so
\delta c
is finite. This concludes the first part of this lemma.

Suppose now that
T
is minimal.
Let
b\in{\mathscr{B}}(T)
, and suppose
F(b)\in\mathscr{F}(\Delta)
. Without loss of generality, let’s assume that
F(b)
is finite (as opposed to cofinite), since
F
respects complements.
This means that
f^{-1}(b)
is finite, and so
b\cap\operatorname{Im}(f)
is finite. Since
T
is cocompact, the image of
f
coarsely covers
V(T)
; that is, there exists
r>0
such that
B(\operatorname{Im}(f),r)
covers
T
. Since
\delta b
is finite,
T[b]
has finitely many connected components, and each of these components has finite coboundary in
T
. If one of these components
U
were infinite, since
T
is minimal and thus leafless, we must have that
U
contains vertices arbitrarily far from
\delta U
. But then
U
must intersect the image of
f
infinitely often, which is a contradiction. In particular, we conclude that
F^{-1}(\mathscr{F}(\Delta))\subset\mathscr{F}(T)
.
∎

Let
T
be a cocompact
G
-tree with finite edge stabilisers. By fixing a basepoint
x_{0}\in V(T)
, and consider the orbit map

\varphi_{x_{0}}:V(\Gamma)\mapsto V(T),\ \ \ g\mapsto gx_{0}.

This map is
G
-equivariant, and well-defined, since the action of
G
upon itself is free. Pulling back, this induces a map

\Phi_{x_{0}}:{\mathscr{B}}(T)\to{\mathscr{B}}(\Gamma),\ \ \ \Phi_{x_{0}}(b)=\varphi_{x_{0}}^{-1}(b).

By Lemma
4.3
, this is a well-defined homomorphism of
G
-modules.

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
T
be a cocompact
G
-tree with finite edge stabilisers. Then the following hold:

(1)

If
T
is
\mathcal{P}
-elliptic then
\operatorname{Im}(\Phi)\subset{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathcal{H}}(\Gamma)
.

(2)

For all
x_{0},y_{0}\in V(T)
,
b\in{\mathscr{B}}(T)
, we have
\Phi_{x_{0}}(b)+\Phi_{y_{0}}(b)
is finite.

(3)

Suppose that
T=T(E)
is the structure tree of a nested,
G
-invariant,
G
-finite subset
E\subset{\mathscr{B}}(\Gamma)
, then there exists
x_{0}\in V(T)
such that
\Phi_{x_{0}}
is precisely the canonical embedding
{\mathscr{B}}(T)\hookrightarrow{\mathscr{B}}(\Gamma)
.

Proof.

We first prove (
1
). Fix
x_{0}\in V(T)
and write
\Phi=\Phi_{x_{0}}
.
Suppose now that
T
is
\mathcal{P}
-elliptic, and let
b\in\operatorname{Im}(\Phi)
. Suppose some
H\in\mathcal{H}
has infinite intersection with both
b
and
b^{\ast}
. Up to translating, we may assume that
H
is one of the subgroups in
\mathcal{P}
. We necessarily have that
|\Lambda(H)|>1
, as it intersects both
\Lambda(b)
and
\Lambda(b^{\ast})
. Then,
H
must contain a loxodromic element
g
with one limit point in
\Lambda(b)
, and the other in
\Lambda(b^{\ast})
. Indeed,
\Omega(\Gamma)
is bipartitioned into clopen sets
\Lambda(b)\sqcup\Lambda(b^{\ast})
, with
\Lambda(H)
intersecting both sides of this bipartition. By
[
12
, Thm. 2.6(i)]
, there exists loxodromics
h_{1},h_{2}\in H
such that

\Lambda(h_{1})\cap\Lambda(b)\neq\emptyset,\ \ \ \Lambda(h_{2})\cap\Lambda(b^{\ast})\neq\emptyset.

If neither
h_{i}
satisfy our requirements, then
\Lambda(h_{1})\cap\Lambda(h_{2})=\emptyset
, and so then we may apply
[
12
, Thm. 2.8]
to find our desired loxodromic
g\in H
.
But then, given this, it is clear that
g
cannot fix a point on
T
, which contradicts the fact that
T
is
\mathcal{P}
-elliptic.

We now prove (
2
). Let
x_{0},y_{0}\in V(T)
, and write
\Phi=\Phi_{x_{0}}
,
\Psi=\Phi_{y_{0}}
. Let
P
denote the geodesic between
x_{0}
and
y_{0}
, which contains finitely many edges. Let
b\in{\mathscr{B}}(T)
. It is sufficient to consider the case where
b\in\mathscr{E}(T)
, say
\delta b=\{e\}
. Consider

\Phi(b)+\Psi(b)=\varphi^{-1}(b)+\psi^{-1}(b).

Suppose this were infinite, and enumerate the elements
g_{1},g_{2},\ldots
, and so on. Without loss of generality, let’s say
g_{i}\in\varphi^{-1}(b)\setminus\psi^{-1}(b)
for all
i>0
, so
g_{i}x_{0}\in b
and
g_{i}y_{0}\in b^{\ast}
. This means that
g_{i}P
contains the single edge
e
in
\delta b
, and so
g^{-1}_{i}e
lies in
P
for all
i>0
. Passing to a subsequence, we may assume that
g^{-1}_{i}e=g^{-1}_{j}e
for all
i,j>0
. But then
g_{i}g^{-1}_{j}e=e
for all
i,j>0
. Since the
g_{i}
are pairwise distinct, this implies the stabiliser of
e
is infinite, which is a contradiction.

Finally, we observe that (
3
) holds. Suppose that
T=T(E)
is a structure tree of a nested,
G
-invariant,
G
-finite, nested subset
E\subset{\mathscr{B}}(\Gamma)
. We must choose
x_{0}
so that
\Phi_{x_{0}}
recovers precisely the canonical embedding
{\mathscr{B}}(T)\hookrightarrow{\mathscr{B}}(\Gamma)
. There is a natural choice for such an
x_{0}
. Let

U=\{b\in E:1\in b\}.

It is easy to verify that
U
is a well-founded ultrafilter on
E
, and in particular is a vertex of
T
. We then set
x_{0}=U
. It is straightforward to verify that
\Phi_{x_{0}}
recovers the canonical embedding
{\mathscr{B}}(T)\hookrightarrow{\mathscr{B}}(\Gamma)
.
∎

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
T_{1}
,
T_{2}
be cocompact
G
-trees with finite edge-stabilisers. Let
f:T_{1}\to T_{2}
be a
G
-map. Then this induces a
G
-equivariant ring homomorphism

F:{\mathscr{B}}(T_{2})\to{\mathscr{B}}(T_{1}).

Moreover, given
x_{0}\in V(T_{1})
, we have that

\Phi_{f(x_{0})}=\Phi_{x_{0}}\circ F.

Proof.

The existence of
F
is automatically ensured by Lemma
4.3
, given by the formula

F(b)=f^{-1}(b)
.
Let
x_{0}\in V(T_{1})
and write
y_{0}=f(x_{0})
.
Let
b\in{\mathscr{B}}(T_{2})
. We have that
\Phi_{y_{0}}(b)=\varphi_{y_{0}}^{-1}(b)=:A
, where
\varphi_{y_{0}}(g)=gy_{0}
.
We also have that

\Phi_{x_{0}}\circ F(b)=\varphi^{-1}_{x_{0}}(F^{-1}(b))=:B.

Given
g\in V(\Gamma)
, we have that

g\in B\Longleftrightarrow f\circ\varphi_{x_{0}}(g)\in b\Longleftrightarrow f(gx_{0})\in b\Longleftrightarrow gy_{0}\in b\Longleftrightarrow g\in A.

It follows that
\Phi_{f(x_{0})}=\Phi_{x_{0}}\circ F
.
∎

Conversely, we have the following.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
T_{1}
,
T_{2}
be cocompact
G
-trees with finite edge-stabilisers. Suppose there is a monotone, complement-respecting,
G
-equivariant homomorphism of rings

\Phi:{\mathscr{B}}(T_{2})\to{\mathscr{B}}(T_{1})

such that

\ker(\Phi)\cap\mathscr{E}(T_{2})=\emptyset
.
Then
T_{1}
dominates
T_{2}
.

Proof.

Fix
v\in V(T_{1})
, and let
G_{v}\leq G
denote its stabiliser. It is sufficient to show that
G_{v}
acts elliptically on
T_{2}
. Consider the inclusion
\iota:\mathscr{E}(T_{2})\hookrightarrow{\mathscr{B}}(T_{2})
, and let
\Psi=\Phi\circ\iota
. Consider

U_{1}=\{b\in\operatorname{Im}(\Psi):v\in b\}
.
This is an ultrafilter on
\operatorname{Im}(\Psi)
. Let

U_{2}=\Psi^{-1}(U_{1}).

It is easy to verify that
U_{2}
is an ultrafilter on
\mathscr{E}(T_{2})
, since
\Phi
is monotone and complement preserving. We claim that
U_{2}
is well-founded. To see this, we will need the following.

Claim 4.7
.

Suppose

5.1.
The cycle space of a graph

First, we recall some basic definitions and terminology related to the cycle space of a graph.
Given a graph
\Gamma
, write

\mathbf{Z}_{2}^{E(\Gamma)}=\prod_{E(\Gamma)}\mathbf{Z}_{2},

equipped with the product topology. By Tychonoff’s theorem, this is a compact topological abelian group. The
cycle space

is the subgroup of
\mathbf{Z}_{2}^{E(\Gamma)}
consisting of all finite Eulerian subgraphs, or equivalently the subgroup generated by all simple cycles. Note that
\mathscr{C}(\Gamma)
can be identified with the first simplicial homology group of
\Gamma
with
\mathbf{Z}_{2}
-coefficients.

5.2.
Topological generation

We wish to work with a slightly weaker notion of generating set for the cycle space, taking advantage of the fact that
\mathbf{Z}_{2}^{E(\Gamma)}
is a compact topological abelian group. This will be useful for applications.
We introduce the following terminology.

Definition 5.1

(Topological generation)
.

Let
\Gamma
be a graph. We say that a subset

\mathscr{C}(\Gamma)\subset\overline{\langle S\rangle}.

If
\Gamma
is a
G
-graph, then we say that
\mathscr{C}(\Gamma)
is
topologically
G
-finitely generated
if there exists a
G
-invariant,
G
-finite topological generating set

.

To distinguish topological generation from the case where
\langle S\rangle=A
, we may refer to the latter as

.
We will be interested in the case where

is the cycle space of

.

Remark 5.2

When

In the context of finitely generated groups, topological generation of the cycle space has already appeared in the literature under a different guise. Let

with each

,

By Claim

∎
