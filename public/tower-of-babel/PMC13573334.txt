Hemodynamic improvement but worsening hypoxemia after balloon pulmonary angioplasty: severe eosinophilic asthma unmasked in CTEPH
Authors: Hiroyuki Fujii, Sarasa Isobe, Yuichi Tamura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We report a patient with peripheral-type chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH) in whom exertional hypoxemia and dyspnea worsened despite substantial hemodynamic improvement after repeated balloon pulmonary angioplasty (BPA), prompting reassessment for a competing ventilatory limitation. Optimization of concomitant severe eosinophilic asthma with benralizumab, an anti–interleukin-5 receptor α monoclonal antibody, and sinus surgery improved airflow obstruction and oxygenation and was accompanied over time by a further reduction in pulmonary arterial pressure. These changes suggest that a superimposed hypoxia-related pulmonary vascular component, compatible with group 3 physiology, may have been attenuated.

Background

Chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH) results from nonresolving organized thromboemboli with associated microvascular remodeling, leading to elevated pulmonary vascular resistance and right ventricular pressure overload [1]. Outcomes have substantially improved with multimodal treatment strategies—including pulmonary endarterectomy, balloon pulmonary angioplasty (BPA), and targeted pharmacotherapy—either alone or in combination as hybrid approaches, and BPA has now become an established therapeutic option for patients with peripheral-type disease [2, 3].

Despite successful BPA with marked hemodynamic improvement, some patients continue to report dyspnea and exertional hypoxemia. In such cases, the clinical challenge is to determine whether symptoms reflect residual thromboembolic or microvascular disease, or whether a competing ventilatory limitation has become dominant. We describe a patient with peripheral-type CTEPH in whom persistent post-BPA symptoms were ultimately explained by uncontrolled severe eosinophilic asthma with sinonasal disease; recognizing this phenotype prompted a shift in management that aligned functional recovery with improved hemodynamics.

Case presentation

A 40-year-old woman was referred for evaluation of progressive dyspnea that persisted for six months after an episode of acute pulmonary embolism. She had been treated with apixaban and long-term oxygen therapy without meaningful symptomatic improvement. Her medical history was notable for bronchial asthma diagnosed in her 30s, treated with high-dose inhaled corticosteroids, long-acting β₂-agonists, and long-acting muscarinic antagonist therapy. She had no history of asthma exacerbation requiring systemic corticosteroids or hospitalization within the previous year.

On presentation, blood pressure was 124/84 mmHg, heart rate 89 beats/min, and oxygen saturation 92% while receiving oxygen at 3 L/min. Physical examination showed jugular venous distension, fixed splitting of the second heart sound, and bilateral lower-extremity edema, without audible wheezes or crackles on lung auscultation. Perfusion scintigraphy demonstrated segmental, wedge-shaped defects, whereas ventilation scintigraphy showed mosaic defects with a non-segmental distribution pattern, suggesting concomitant peripheral airway disease (Fig. 1). On the 6-minute walk test (6MWT), she walked 350 m with a nadir SpO₂ of 83% while receiving oxygen at 4 L/min.

Right heart catheterization revealed a mean pulmonary arterial pressure (mPAP) of 41 mmHg, pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) of 7.54 Wood units, and cardiac output (CO) of 3.98 L/min. BNP was 65.4 pg/mL, and echocardiography showed a reduced TAPSE/sPAP ratio of 0.19, indicating impaired right ventricular–pulmonary arterial coupling and suggesting right ventricular dysfunction in the setting of pulmonary hypertension. Pulmonary angiography demonstrated distal lesions typical of peripheral-type CTEPH, including webs and abrupt narrowing (Fig. 2). After multidisciplinary discussion, riociguat was initiated and BPA was planned.

Three BPA sessions were performed over three months, targeting multiple segmental branches bilaterally. Follow-up catheterization performed three months after completion of the three BPA sessions demonstrated hemodynamic improvement (mPAP 31 mmHg; PVR 2.47 Wood units; CO 6.49 L/min). BNP decreased to 5.8 pg/mL, and TAPSE/sPAP improved to 0.41 (Fig. 3). Angiography confirmed recanalization with improved distal perfusion (Fig. 2). However, functional status did not improve: the 6MWT distance decreased to 262 m with a nadir SpO₂ of 74% on 4 L/min oxygen, and the patient reported minimal relief of dyspnea.

Given the discrepancy between improved pulmonary hemodynamics and worsening oxygenation and exercise performance, comorbid conditions were reassessed. Baseline spirometry showed severe obstructive ventilatory impairment (%VC 88.6%; %FEV₁ 34.2%; FEV₁/FVC 35.5%), which further deteriorated after BPA (%VC 85.7%; %FEV₁ 29.8%; FEV₁/FVC 32.0%). Arterial blood gas analysis, performed in the context of worsening obstructive ventilatory impairment, showed hypercapnia (PaCO₂ 48.1 mmHg while receiving oxygen at 4 L/min); body mass index was 24.4 kg/m², and total lung capacity was 4.51 L. Peripheral eosinophil count was elevated at 1,269/µL (23.5%), concomitant with an increased serum IgE level of 1,220 IU/mL. Computed tomography of the paranasal sinuses demonstrated chronic rhinosinusitis with bilateral nasal polyps. The patient also noted that breathing in felt easier after BPA, whereas difficulty breathing out persisted. These findings supported uncontrolled severe eosinophilic asthma with sinonasal involvement as a major contributor to persistent hypoxemia and symptoms. Given the marked eosinophilia and severe obstructive ventilatory impairment, treatment with benralizumab was planned to target eosinophilic airway inflammation.

Additional BPA was paused, and benralizumab was initiated one week after the follow-up evaluation performed three months after completion of the three BPA sessions. Peripheral eosinophils decreased to 0%, and dyspnea improved; the patient subsequently underwent bilateral endoscopic sinus surgery. Six months after benralizumab initiation, spirometry improved substantially (%VC 119.7%; %FEV₁ 63.4%; FEV₁/FVC 50.8%). Repeat catheterization showed further improvement (mPAP 25 mmHg; PVR 2.08 Wood units; CO 6.26 L/min). BNP remained within the normal range, and TAPSE/sPAP increased further to 0.52, indicating marked improvement in right ventricular–pulmonary arterial coupling and clear recovery of right ventricular function in parallel with hemodynamic improvement. Functional measures also improved: the 6MWT distance increased to 359 m, with a nadir SpO₂ of 90% while receiving oxygen at 2 L/min (Fig. 3).

Discussion and conclusion

Balloon pulmonary angioplasty can normalize pulmonary hemodynamics in peripheral-type CTEPH, yet functional recovery may lag when the dominant physiologic limitation is not circulatory. In this patient, staged BPA plus riociguat reduced mPAP from 41 to 31 mmHg and PVR from 7.54 to 2.47 Wood units, with a marked rise in cardiac output; paradoxically, exercise capacity and oxygenation worsened (6-minute walk distance 350 to 262 m; nadir SpO₂ 83% to 74% on 4 L/min oxygen), and airflow obstruction progressed (%FEV₁ 34.2% to 29.8%). These discordant trajectories shifted the diagnostic question from residual pulmonary vascular disease to identifying the dominant physiologic limitation.

Several features supported uncontrolled type 2 airway disease as the primary driver of persistent symptoms: severe obstructive ventilatory impairment with hypercapnia, marked eosinophilia with elevated IgE, and chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyps. Although hypereosinophilic syndrome-associated pulmonary hypertension was considered in the differential diagnosis, the absence of systemic organ involvement made eosinophilic asthma, rather than hypereosinophilic syndrome, the most plausible underlying condition in this patient with pulmonary hypertension and eosinophilia. Importantly, the ventilation–perfusion study already showed non-segmental mosaic ventilation abnormalities, an early clue to small-airway involvement that may be overlooked when attention is focused on perfusion defects. In the presence of airway-related ventilation defects, the characteristic ventilation–perfusion mismatch of CTEPH may become less apparent, potentially complicating interpretation of perfusion defects and distinction from matched ventilation–perfusion defects. Asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease are not uncommon comorbidities in CTEPH cohorts, reinforcing the need to interpret post-BPA symptoms through both vascular and ventilatory perspectives [4, 5].

At the time of initial treatment planning, the patient was considered to have symptomatic peripheral-type CTEPH with significant pulmonary hypertension and characteristic angiographic findings, and BPA was therefore prioritized. Although a history of asthma was recognized, the severity of concomitant airway disease and its contribution to hypoxemia and exercise limitation were likely underestimated before BPA. In retrospect, earlier optimization of severe eosinophilic asthma might have influenced the interpretation of residual symptoms and the timing of additional pulmonary vascular intervention. This case highlights the importance of reassessing the dominant physiologic limitation throughout the treatment course in patients with mixed vascular and ventilatory abnormalities.

Benralizumab, an interleukin-5 receptor α monoclonal antibody, induces near-complete eosinophil depletion through antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity [6]. Although IL-5 pathway biologics improve outcomes in severe eosinophilic asthma [7, 8], they are not established therapies for pulmonary hypertension. After benralizumab initiation and sinus surgery, eosinophils fell to 0%, lung function improved substantially, oxygen requirement decreased, and exercise performance recovered. During the same period, mPAP decreased further to 25 mmHg. Although pulmonary hypertension caused primarily by asthma is uncommon, severe airflow obstruction, hypercapnia, and chronic hypoxemia in the present case may have contributed to a superimposed hypoxia-related pulmonary vascular component (consistent with group 3 physiology). Improved ventilation may have reduced ventilation–perfusion mismatch and attenuated hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction, potentially associated with the additional reduction in pulmonary arterial pressure observed during the same period. Nonetheless, causality cannot be inferred from a single case, and delayed BPA effects and ongoing medical therapy remain potential confounders.

From a practical standpoint, persistent dyspnea or exertional desaturation after hemodynamically successful BPA should prompt structured reassessment rather than escalation of pulmonary vascular intervention. When hemodynamics improve but symptoms persist, clinicians should evaluate both residual vascular disease and competing ventilatory limitation using spirometry, blood gas analysis, imaging, and, when appropriate, cardiopulmonary exercise testing. Previous studies have shown that exertional desaturation may persist despite substantial hemodynamic improvement after BPA, suggesting mechanisms beyond resting pulmonary hemodynamics [9]. In particular, cardiopulmonary exercise testing may help distinguish residual circulatory limitation from ventilatory inefficiency or gas exchange abnormalities. Assessment of cardiopulmonary exercise responses or intrapulmonary shunting might have further clarified the relative contributions of residual pulmonary vascular dysfunction and ventilatory limitation in our patient, although these evaluations were not performed. In this case, recognition of severe eosinophilic asthma as the dominant limitation prompted a pause in additional BPA and optimization of airway and sinonasal inflammation; subsequent improvements in functional status and oxygenation were observed in parallel with favorable hemodynamic findings.
