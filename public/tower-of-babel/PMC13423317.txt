Real-World Data on First Relapse in Multiple Myeloma: Treatment Patterns and Outcomes in Finland Using Automated Data Collection
Authors: Johanna Vikkula, Kari Remes, Outi Laatikainen, Merja Utriainen, Saara Tikka, Juha Lievonen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThis study aimed to characterize multiple myeloma (MM) patients at the first relapse and examine treatment outcomes.MethodsThis registry-based retrospective study examined relapsed MM patients in two Finnish Wellbeing Services Counties (Helsinki and Uusimaa, and Southwest Finland) from 2013 to 2022. Relapse was identified per current guidelines using serum paraprotein, plasma immunoglobulins, and free light chain data. Data were collected from hospital data lakes, Social Insurance Institution and Digital and Population Services Agency. Patients were stratified by age (≤70 vs. >70), index year, and time to first relapse (≤2 vs. >2 years post-diagnosis).ResultsOf 1,221 patients treated for MM between 2012 and 2022, 286 met the study inclusion criteria. Median overall survival from the relapse was higher in the younger patients (32.4 months vs. 20.0 months). In the first half of follow-up, lenalidomide was the most common pharmacotherapy in both age groups. After 2018, CD38-based treatments and pomalidomide increased significantly for younger patients, remaining limited in the elderly.ConclusionWhile new therapies have been adopted for younger MM patients, the treatment of older patients at first relapse has had little change. Given their poorer outcomes, there is a need to improve care for older MM patients.

Introduction

Multiple myeloma (MM) is a progressive disease that burdens the life of patients with recurring relapses. Globally, MM incidence ranges from age-standardized global estimates of 0.54–5.3 per 100,000, up to incidences of 9–14 per 100,000 people [1–3]. The onset of the disease is typically between 65 and 70 years [1]. In Finland, the incidence of MM has doubled after the year 2000, from 4.11 to 8.33 per 100,000 people [4]. Similarly to other developed countries, most of the increase is thought to arise from the change in the age structure of the general population.

The past 20 years have marked a change in treatment options for MM due to the introduction of new therapies, such as proteasome inhibitors (PIs, e.g., bortezomib, carfilzomib, ixazomib), immunomodulatory drugs (IMiDs, e.g., lenalidomide, pomalidomide, thalidomide), and anti-CD38 monoclonal antibodies (CD38Ab, e.g., daratumumab, isatuximab) [5]. In Finland, patients’ access to PIs and IMiDs was initially delayed as, for example, lenalidomide became reimbursed in first line for stem cell transplantation (SCT) ineligible patients in 2016, and for maintenance treatment after SCT in 2019 [6]. Together with SCT these therapies have shifted MM from an untreatable condition toward a more manageable disease [7]. Still, novel treatments, such as antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs, e.g., belantamab mafodotin), bispecific antibodies (e.g., teclistamab), and Car-T cell therapies continue to emerge, offering new therapeutic options for the treatment of MM.

The changes in treatment landscape have also been reflected in the overall survival (OS) of MM patients [4, 8–12]. In Finland, the median OS (mOS) of MM patients, measured from diagnosis, has increased from 2.8 years in the early 2000s to 4.4 years between 2017 and 2021 [4]. The improvements have not, however, changed the overall course of the disease. Relapses are still frequent among MM patients, and, with each recurrence, the remission periods shorten as the disease grows in aggressiveness. Therefore, relapses appear as an important phase in MM, with a potential to provide additional information of factors affecting the disease course and treatment, for example, duration of response. [13]. Understanding these factors is essential for advancing future treatments for MM.

Although previous studies have described the treatment patterns and outcomes of MM patients, the first relapse has not yet been the focus of real-world studies in Finland. Due to universal healthcare and its finance base, the development of treatment landscape in Finland has distinct differences compared to many other Western countries. Thus, Finnish data will offer insights into treatment patterns with more restricted treatment options. The main objective of this study was to characterize MM patients at the onset of the first relapse and to describe the treatment landscape and outcomes following it using automated data collection.

Methods

Data Collection

This retrospective register study utilized secondary medical data generated as part of the treatment and follow-up of MM patients at Wellbeing Services Counties of Helsinki and Uusimaa and Southwest Finland, which cover approximately 40% of the Finnish population. The study data were collected from the data lakes of the two Wellbeing Services Counties (specialized care data of diagnoses, procedures, laboratory and pathology tests, healthcare contacts, and inpatient medications), from Social Insurance Institution (data of reimbursed drug purchases), and from Digital and Population Services Agency (demographics data) (Fig. 1). The data were collected from all adult patients diagnosed with MM (ICD-10 C90.0) and treated at either of the Wellbeing Services Counties between January 1, 2013, and December 31, 2022. Patients who had relocated outside the two hospital districts at the end of follow-up were excluded.

Construction of Treatment Lines

Treatment lines were not readily available in the data lakes and were thus constructed using data on drug administrations and prescriptions from the data lakes and data on reimbursed drug purchases from Social Insurance Institution. Treatment lines were defined according to the international guidelines of defining MM treatment lines [14]. In general, the treatment line was considered to change when the therapy regimen changed and when a new drug was added to the regimen unplanned (i.e., addition after 60 days from the start of the regimen). Additionally, the treatment line was considered to switch, if the same regimen was restarted after a pause of more than 6 months. All unusual treatment combinations (e.g., combinations with ≥4 drugs) were examined manually and adjusted, if needed, to ensure they were appropriately split into separate treatment lines if the algorithm had falsefully merged them into one single treatment line. For patients who underwent SCT, the treatment line consisted of the induction, SCT, and possible consolidation and maintenance therapies. More details of the definitions for the treatment lines are in online supplementary methods (for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000552684).

Formation of the Relapsed MM Cohort

This study focused on MM patients with their first relapse. Relapsed patients were identified from the MM cohort using laboratory data on monoclonal components, i.e., paraprotein, and free light chains (FLCs). Relapsed disease was defined as disease progression after initial response: an increase of ≥25% and ≥5 g/L in paraprotein levels after at least minimal response (MR; ≥25% reduction), when monitoring serum paraprotein or plasma immunoglobulins, and an increase of ≥25% and ≥100 mg/L in difference of the free light chains (dFLC) after at least partial response (PR; ≥50% reduction), when monitoring serum FLC (online suppl. Table 1). Using the selected definitions, the relapsed cohort will consist only of patients with disease progression defined conventionally, i.e., rise in paraprotein or dFLC levels per guideline definitions, and excludes patients with clinical progression (new lytic lesions or other CRAB findings [hyperCalcemia, Renal problems, Anemia, Bone disease/lesions]) without rise of paraprotein above progression criteria. Many of these patients are hyposecretory at relapse and thus would have been excluded from clinical trials since they do not have a measurable disease at progression. Although this may exclude a few relapsed patients, it was estimated the best way to define a cohort with little to no false positive relapses. Index date was set as the start of the next treatment line following the first relapse. All identified patients were followed from index until death or end of study (EOS; December 31, 2022).

Statistical Analysis

Patients were stratified by the age at index to younger (≤70 years old) and older (>70 years old) subgroups, by index year (2013–2017 vs. 2018–2022), and by timing of the relapse (≤2 years vs. >2 years since diagnosis). Demographics and prior treatments (see online suppl. methods for definitions) were summarized at index with median and interquartile range (IQR) for continuous variables, and with number and proportion of patients for categorical variables. Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) [15] was calculated using diagnosis data from specialized care from 5 years prior to the first relapse (excluding ICD-10 C90). The difference between strata was tested using Kruskall-Wallis test for continuous variables and chi-squared/Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. OS was analyzed with Kaplan-Meier fit as time from index until death (event) or EOS (censoring). Association of baseline characteristics on survival was assessed using a Cox proportional hazards model. Event-free survival (EFS) was analyzed using a competing risk model as time from index until start of the next treatment line (event), death (competing risk), or EOS (censoring). Overall response rate (ORR) was defined as proportion of patients having PR or better for the index treatment. All-cause specialized healthcare resource use (HCRU; inpatient days and outpatient contacts) was analyzed using a mean cumulative function separately for the baseline and follow-up. For the baseline, patients were reversely followed from index until MM diagnosis. From the mean cumulative function fit, the resource use per year pre and post index was extracted. Outpatient contacts were analyzed in more detail as outpatient clinic visits, sequential therapy, emergency room visits, remote care (such as calls and letters), and other outpatient contacts. All analyses were performed with R version 4.3.1.

Results

Relapsed Cohort Patient Characteristics

There were 1,221 patients who were diagnosed with MM and started the 1L treatment between 2013 and 2022 (Fig. 1). After the 1L treatment, 510 patients did not meet the disease progression criteria used in this study for the following reasons: 90 patients did not respond to treatment during the follow-up (primary refractory), 97 patients whose increase in paraprotein levels did not meet with the criteria of disease relapse (either increase of ≥25% OR ≥5 g/L in paraprotein levels OR ≥100 mg/L in dFLC), and 323 (26.5%) patients did not experience disease progression after the 1L during the study follow-up period, i.e., were in remission at the end of follow-up. Furthermore, 330 patients were excluded due to missing or incomplete data. Altogether 381 (31.2%) patients met the criteria for the first relapse, out of whom 42 (11.0%; with median age of 80.2 years at death) died without further treatment and 53 (13.9%) did not start any further treatment after the relapse during the study follow-up. Notably, this cohort excludes patients that experience disease progression that differs from the conventional progressive disease course, i.e., patients who experience clinical progression (CRAB findings) without paraprotein rise above the guideline threshold for progression.

Finally, 286 (23.4%) patients fulfilled the study criteria for the first relapse and initiated treatment for relapsed disease and were thus included in the study cohort (Fig. 1). The relapsed patient cohort contained equal number of men and women, with a median age of 72.0 years (IQR 65.6–78.1) at index (Table 1). Most patients were determined with R-ISS stage II (70.6%) or stage III (19.4%). Based on ECOG and CCI distribution, patients in both age groups had good general health condition (online suppl. Table 2). The number of patients requiring dialysis decreased from 25 to 8 patients between diagnosis and first relapse.

Treatment before the First Relapse

Although most patients (64.3%) had received only one previous therapy line at first relapse, more than one third had needed multiple therapy lines before their first relapse (Table 2). The underlying cause for changes in therapy lines were not investigated in this study, but probable causes could be adverse events or poor therapeutic response. PIs were the most used therapy agents in both age groups (90.2%), but the use was more common in younger patients, of whom all had received PIs (p < 0.001). In both age-groups, PI was typically combined with other therapy agents (such as cyclophosphamide). Similarly, IMiD therapies were more often received by patients ≤70 years (59.7% vs. 42.8%, p = 0.006). Overall, bortezomib (89.5%), lenalidomide (47.2%), and cyclophosphamide (39.5%), were the most used MM therapies before the first relapse. The treatments preceding the first relapse resulted in PR (42.3%) or at least very good PR (VGPR, 46.5%) in most cases with no statistically significant differences between the age-groups (p = 0.076).

Treatment of the First Relapse

Re-treatment, i.e., the use of same therapy agent for the treatment of MM both before and at the first relapse, was high in patients previously treated with IMiDs in both age-groups (Fig. 2a, b), although the total use of pomalidomide was low. As index treatment, lenalidomide combination therapies (doublet therapy with corticosteroids; Rd, or triplet therapy with bortezomib-corticosteroid; VRd) were the most common treatments in both age groups in the first half (2013–2017) of the study follow-up (Fig. 2c, d). The dominant role of lenalidomide was especially pronounced in the younger patients, in contrast to the more heterogenous use in the older age group. During the years 2018–2022, the use of Rd further increased in the older patients, from 32% to 37%, whereas novel therapies, such as CD38ab, carfilzomib, and pomalidomide, were used marginally (Fig. 2f). In contrast, the second half of the follow-up marked a change in therapy practices in the younger patients, as the use of CD38ab, carfilzomib, and pomalidomide-based treatments increased compared to the early years of the study (Fig. 2e). Also, the use of triplet/quadruplet therapies increased from 41.5% in 2013–2017 to 77.4% in 2018–2022 among the younger patients (p < 0.001), while no change in use of triplet therapies was observed among the older patients between the two time periods (42.5% in 2013–2017 vs. 31.2% 2018–2022, p = 0.274).

Response data for index treatment were available for 263 (92%) patients. The ORR for the treatment of the first relapse was 61% among all patients, where 26% reached at least VGPR (online suppl. Fig. 1C). As expected, ORR was better in the younger patient group (p = 0.002), primarily due to a greater PR rate compared to the older patients (43% vs. 28%). In contrast, the frequency of VGPR or higher was relatively similar between the two groups. Median (alive and next treatment line not yet started) was similar in both age-groups: 9.0 months (95% CI: 6.7–11.9) in the younger group and 9.9 months (95% CI: 7.2–12.5) in the older group (online suppl. Fig. 1A, B). However, younger patients reached the next treatment line more often than the older patients. At 60 months, 56.5% of the older patients (95% CI: 48.2–66.2) had started the next treatment line, while 40.3% of the older patients (95% CI: 32.5–50.0) had died.

Survival after First Relapse

The mOS after the first relapse was 22.7 months (95% CI: 18.6–29.6) for all patients (Fig. 3a), and 32.4 month (95% CI: 17.5–39.4) for the younger patients and 20.0 months (95% CI: 15.4–23.1) for the older patients (Fig. 3b). When stratified with the onset of the first relapse (within 2 years vs. after 2 years), the younger patients demonstrated a clear difference where those with the onset of first relapse after 2 years had significantly better OS (44.3 months, 95% CI: 34.8–79.3) compared to those relapsing within 2 years of the initial diagnosis (13.2 months, 95% CI: 8.1–28.6, log rank p < 0.001) (Fig. 4c) with hazard ratio of 0.4 (95% CI: 0.3–0.6, p < 0.001) (Fig. 3e). In contrast, in the older patient group, higher age was associated with inferior survival (hazard ratio for each additional year 1.1, 95% CI: 1.0–1.1, p = 0.012), whereas the timing of the relapse did not affect the survival (Fig. 3d, f).

Healthcare Resource Utilization

The all-cause HCRU, as measured in the number of events per patient, showed a growing trend already 2 years before the first relapse, reaching the highest level during the first year after the relapse, especially in the younger patients (Fig. 4a). Notably, the overall HCRU was lower for older patients both before and after the first relapse (Fig. 4b), compared to the younger patients, which mainly resulted from lower number of inpatient days and sequential therapy contacts. When stratified by the main diagnosis of healthcare contact, MM accounted for most of the outpatient contacts throughout the years, while hospitalizations were caused by various diseases (online suppl. Fig. 2), especially in patients >70 years of age.

Discussion

This study captured the treatment of 286 MM patients at the time of their first relapse, which has not been described in detail by earlier studies in Finland. All study patients were treated in publicly funded specialized healthcare. Notably, the study captured only 23% of the 1,221 patients initiating 1L treatment in the overall MM population, suggesting a significant exclusion of relapsed patients from the initial MM patient population, mainly due to data coverage issues, but also based on the first relapse criteria. However, our study provides accurate data on defined relapse cases, and the treatment patterns reflect the national landscape for first-relapse MM in Finland.

In this study, the median time to first relapse was 27.3 months, consistent with previous findings from the USA and Singapore, where the average time from diagnosis to first relapse ranged from 22 to 25 months. [9, 12]. Although earlier studies have shown better outcomes in younger MM patients [4, 9, 16], our study found only a small difference in the time to first relapse between the age groups ≤70 and >70 years (29.8 months vs. 26.0 months, respectively). This most probably is due to selection bias in the study design, where 323 (26.5%) of the 1,221 patients remained progression-free after 1L and were not captured as relapsed. As long remissions are more common in patients <70 years [9, 16], this may bias survival downward in the younger subgroup and contribute to the modest age difference in time to first relapse observed here. Conversely, some patients with very poor outcomes were also excluded (e.g., primary refractory patients and those who died after progression without receiving subsequent therapy). Finally, in routine practice treatment changes may occur without patients meeting formal relapse criteria, which can lead to multiple lines without documented progression and therefore an underestimation of progression rates in retrospective analyses.

Considering the selection criteria and study design, the outcomes were in line with prior studies. The mOS from the first relapse in the overall cohort was 22.7 months. Combined with the median time to first relapse (27.3 months), this places outcomes at the lower end of previous findings from Finland and other Western countries, which report mOS of 4–6 years [4, 8, 11, 17–19]. Survival differed by age: younger patients had a mOS 12.4 months longer than older patients (32.4 vs. 20.0 months), though still below the 45 months reported in similarly aged relapsed MM cohorts [9, 12]. In patients ≤70 years, early relapse (≤2 years) identified a high-risk group with markedly shorter survival (mOS 13.2 months) compared with later relapse (mOS 44.3 months), consistent with earlier studies [9, 16, 20]. In patients >70 years, relapse timing did not affect survival, which may result from generally lower ORR and hence lower proportion of patients reaching next treatment line after the first relapse.

The follow-up of this study captures the timespan in which several new MM treatment options entered clinical practice, offering insight into changes driven by novel pharmacotherapies [6]. Compared to other Nordic countries, the treatment of MM in Finland has remained more conventional longer, and the result reflects countries with slower adaptation of novel agents into clinical practice [18, 21]. From 2013 to 2017, first relapse treatment was largely guided by lenalidomide in both age-groups, immediately after its’ release to clinical practice. Between 2018 and 2022, there was a clear decrease in use of lenalidomide doublet therapy within the younger patients, while in the older patients, the use of lenalidomide doublet therapy increased (32%–37%) and the use of triplet therapy decreased (15%–9%). Overall, treatment patterns changed far less in older patients. In contrast, in younger patients there was a shift from lenalidomide to CD38 antibodies, pomalidomide-based therapies, and other new agents like carfilzomib, consistent with previous MM reports [22].

The reason for the difference in the treatment of the elderly is affected by factors like general condition and comorbid background at MM diagnosis. As shown in a study and by Hemminki et al. [23], older patients across the Nordic countries face clear survival disadvantages. Our study also demonstrated that older patients were more likely to be dropped out of active treatment regimens after the first relapse. Hemminki et al. [23] called for inclusive actions of older patients regarding clinical decisions with long-term positive survival trajectories. Moore et al. [22] found that novel therapies are both effective and well-tolerated in patients aged ≥75, which aligns with this study’s finding of high (33%) re-treatment rates with carfilzomib, pomalidomide, and lenalidomide in older patients. Considering the developing resistance and related limitations in the use of therapies in multiple consecutive treatment lines, the re-treatment of newer therapy options emphasizes the need for new and improved treatment options in the MM treatment landscape. This is especially relevant for the older MM patients based on the observed slow intake of new treatment options in patients over the age of 70 years.

Comprehensive data obtained from the two data lakes and complemented with national data provided a sound foundation for this RWE study. Nonetheless, the use of real-world data inherently carries a risk of missing or erroneous entries. Limitations include missing or incomplete response data for a subset of patients, limited ability to determine reasons for treatment-line changes or regimen choice (e.g., refractoriness, intolerance, or frailty), and mandatory small-number censoring under Finnish legislation, which reduces detail for uncommon regimens. Furthermore, multiple patients with potential relapse were excluded as they did not meet the study definition for first relapse. As a result, the cohort in this study is excellent in describing patients with conventional paraprotein-based criteria of progressive disease and first relapse, but clearly excludes a large number of patients, i.e., patients who experience clinical progression (CRAB findings) without paraprotein rise above the guidelines threshold for progression. Thus, the results here only represent a proportion of MM patients with first relapse. Second, real-world data does not provide a full description of causes behind treatment-line switches, which, in addition to, e.g., heterogenous documentation practices, may have caused some inaccuracy in the timing of the first relapse, however, expected to be minimal.

Final Conclusions

To our knowledge, this is one of the first retrospective real-world studies, at least in Finland, aiming to capture the first relapse of MM based on laboratory results, instead of approximating the relapse based on the start of the second treatment line. Early onset of the first relapse is associated with poorer outcome in patients under 70 years while similar association was not observed in patients over 70 years. The treatment landscape for older cohort at the first relapse has not changed comparably with younger MM patients, which might be reflected in the poorer treatment outcomes in the elderly patients. More research is needed to assess the unmet medical need for new agents suitable for elderly MM patients.

Statement of Ethics

Ethical approval is not required for this study in accordance with local or national guidelines. According to Finnish legislation (Finnish Act on Secondary use of health and social data [552/2019]), secondary use of health and social data can be approved by the Social and Health Data Permit Authority Findata. The secondary health data used in this study was granted by Findata (data permit no: THL/3455/14.02.00/2022), collected by register holders, and combined by Findata. This study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Adult participant consent was not required in this study as, under the Finnish act of secondary use of social and health data, participant consent is not required for the use of pseudonymized, retrospective, individual-level data. Furthermore, study participants are not known or cannot be contacted.
