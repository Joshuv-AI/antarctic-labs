Nephrologist Workforce Capacity and Kidney Disease Burden in Mexico
Authors: Alfonso M. Cueto-Manzano, Olynka Vega-Vega, Jesús M. Ramos-Gordillo, Ricardo Mendiola-Fernández, Hugo E. Chávez-Chávez
Published: 2026

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) has become one of the fastest growing causes of mortality worldwide, rising from the 27th leading cause of death in 1990 to the 10th in recent estimates.1 Projections suggest that CKD may become one of the top 5 causes of years of life lost globally by 2040 if current trends continue.1,S1

Using data from dialysis and transplant registries in the states of Jalisco and Aguascalientes (because there is no national registry), Mexico is among the top 10 countries in the world with the highest incidence and prevalence of end-stage kidney disease.S2 Furthermore, our country presents a particularly concerning epidemiologic profile because of the high prevalence of metabolic diseases. National surveysS3 indicate that approximately 11% of adults have diabetes (self-reported), 76% are overweight or obese, and nearly 30% have hypertension, the major drivers of CKD progression.

CKD generates substantial clinical and economic burden through cardiovascular complications, disability, premature mortality, and the high costs associated with renal replacement therapy.1,S4 Early referral to nephrology care has been shown to improve clinical outcomes through better complication control, delayed dialysis initiation, and improved survival.2,S5 Moreover, adequate screening, early diagnosis, and opportune treatment by primary care professionals are necessary to prevent and properly control CKD during the early stages,3,S6 achieving the best outcomes, mainly through multidisciplinary models of primary health care teams led by nephrologists.4

Despite all the above, limited attention has been given to nephrologist workforce adequacy as a determinant of CKD outcomes in Mexico. This review evaluates whether Mexico currently has sufficient nephrologist workforce capacity relative to disease burden and future demand.

Nephrologist Workforce Capacity

Limited nephrologist workforce capacity is not only a structural limitation but a direct determinant of adverse clinical outcomes. A characteristic of the global nephrologist workforce is the substantial variability in the numbers of nephrologists between countries, with many countries without sufficient specialists, particularly in low- and lower-middle-income countries, to guarantee optimal provision and delivery of kidney health services.5 In Latin America, the average number of nephrologists, although highly heterogeneous across the region, is 19 per million population (pmp),6 with only 7 countries exceeding the minimum target of ≥20 nephrologists pmp proposed by the Latin American Society of Nephrology and Hypertension more than a decade ago. The Latin American Society of Nephrology and Hypertension recognized several years ago, that encouraging the training of more nephrologists to reach this minimum target was 1 way to overcome the great challenges of CKD in the region.7

The Mexican Board of Nephrology8 includes data on specialists certified by examination (almost always residents completing their specialization) or recertified by points (those nephrologists who, after their initial certification, achieve sufficient scores for recertification; otherwise, they must take the examination). According to this council, there were 1668 certified and active nephrologists in Mexico as of December 2025, of which 1427 were adult nephrologist and 241 were pediatric nephrologists. Considering the total population of the country estimated during the same period (133 million),S7 the rate was 12.5 nephrologists pmp in Mexico until last year. Therefore, there is currently a shortage of 992 specialists in the country to meet the target of 20 nephrologists pmp.

To date, there are no published estimates on how many patients with CKD at all stages should be treated by each nephrologist to achieve adequate control, nor are there national data on the prevalence of CKD at all stages in the Mexican population. However, considering a possible prevalence of 14.7% found in a study conducted in the state of Jalisco,S8 there could be 20 million Mexicans with CKD, which means that each nephrologist would have to take care of 11990 people with some degree of kidney disease in the country, in the hypothetical case that all these patients should be treated exclusively by specialists.

Factors Related With the Nephrologist Shortage

The shortage of nephrologists worldwide is due to multiple factors, which can vary according to health systems and countries. Potential contributors include the aging of the workforce, declining interest in nephrology among trainees, lack of exposure to nephrology among undergraduate students and residents, rising cost of medical education and specialist training, increasing reliance on physicians trained abroad, inflexible work hours, erosion of nephrology practice by other specialists, inadequate training, reduced focus on scholarship and research funds, greater demand to meet quality of care standards, and the development of new care delivery models.S9

In Mexico, workforce distribution seems to represent a major structural limitation of kidney care. More than half of the total nephrologists are located in Mexico City (n = 521, 31%) and in the states of Jalisco (n = 194, 12%) and Nuevo León (n = 86, 5%), whereas states such as Baja California Sur, Colima, Nayarit, Campeche, and Durango have ≤7 nephrologists (Figure 1). However, even within the same state, the distribution of nephrologists is not uniform; for example, in Jalisco, of the 194 nephrologists in the state, 184 are located in the Guadalajara metropolitan area. It could be thought that nephrologists would be concentrated in large communities where patients with CKD are concentrated; however, even considering these factors, the uneven distribution persists. In Mexico City (not considering the whole metropolitan area), for instance, there are 57 nephrologists pmp and in Guadalajara 22 pmp, whereas in Baja California Sur there are 3 nephrologists pmp and in Durango 4 pmp. Strategic Framework for Potential Solutions

Workforce Expansion

A possible solution to the shortage of nephrologists in Mexico would be to increase the number of nephrology training centers and nephrology residency positions in order to increase the number of annual graduates. Thanks to the great effort of the Mexican nephrology community, the number of training programs and graduates has increased in recent years (Supplementary Table S1); however, the current rate of the total workforce expansion (Supplementary Figure S1) remains insufficient to close the existing gap within a reasonable timeframe.

There are 33 training centers for adult nephrologists (Supplementary Figure S2A) and 7 for pediatric nephrologists (Supplementary Figure S2B) in Mexico, primarily located in Mexico City and Guadalajara. These 40 centers graduated 181 nephrologists in 2025; assuming this rate is maintained, that the Mexican population does not increase, and that there is no decrease in the number of nephrologists due to retirement or other reasons, it would take 5 to 8 years to resolve the current shortage of these specialists and reach the goal of 20 nephrologists pmp; however, considering projected CKD growth, the workforce gap may widen further.

In addition, several structural barriers limit workforce expansion, including lengthy training pathways, burnout concerns, and compensation disparities. Training in nephrology typically requires 6 to 7 years of medical school, 2 to 4 years of internal medicine, and 3 years of nephrology. Therefore, total training time may be > 11 years. Therefore, in the search for solutions to this problem, the first step is inevitably to increase the number of residency positions in nephrology and the number of training centers, as well as the number of jobs for newly graduated nephrologists. In addition, it is important to increase the interest of medical students by providing them with more information about nephrology from the undergraduate level.

Transformation and Integration of Kidney Health Care

The problem is not simply a shortage of nephrologists; it must be understood as multifactorial, where the number of specialists is only 1 component within a complex system that includes prevention, access, and the organization of the health care system (Table 1). Countries with the best outcomes in treating kidney disease do not necessarily have more nephrologists, but rather integrated systems of prevention and early intervention. One of the main challenges facing nephrology worldwide, and especially in countries such as Mexico, is that nephrologists must evolve from being administrators of renal replacement therapies to leaders of multidisciplinary teams and strategic consultants. Until the shortage of nephrologists in the country is resolved (an objective that does not seem achievable in the short-term), some strategies can be implemented to try to control the CKD epidemic. These strategies include the implementation of national kidney health programs for the prevention, early diagnosis, and timely treatment of CKD focused on primary health careS13,S14; the formation and implementation of comprehensive care models based on multidisciplinary teams of professionals (which have been shown to improve efficiency and outcomes)4; the use of tele-nephrology (which has shown potential to improve access to kidney care in the regions)S15; and the redistribution of tasks with the training of physicians and multidisciplinary professionals in primary health care to manage early CKD and reduce the burden on specialists.4,S6

The recently developed National Health Care Protocols9 are guideline documents that provide evidence-based recommendations for the prevention, diagnosis, and treatment of diseases identified by the Mexican Health Secretariat as major threats to the health of the population. Their purpose is to promote equitable, comprehensive, and standardized care across the entire health sector, with a strong emphasis on primary health care; the development of a National Health Care Protocol on CKD represents an important opportunity to strengthen kidney health care in the country. Finally, the presence of a well-organized nephrology community, represented by the Mexican Institute of Nephrological Research and the College of Mexican Nephrologists, is essential for achieving the changes needed in the care of patients with kidney disease. These organizations play a crucial role in supporting the training of new nephrologists, promoting continuous professional development, and fostering the coordination and education of multidisciplinary health care teams.

Finally, the considerations and proposals presented in this analysis could be useful for other countries that share similar realities to those of Mexico, especially in Latin America, but also in other regions of the world.

In conclusion, Mexico faces a significant mismatch between CKD burden and nephrologist workforce capacity. This gap reflects both insufficient specialist numbers and geographic inequities. Addressing these challenges requires coordinated national planning integrating workforce development, prevention strategies, and health system reform. Without urgent and coordinated action, this gap will continue to widen, threatening equitable access to kidney care and patient outcomes nationwide within the coming decades.
