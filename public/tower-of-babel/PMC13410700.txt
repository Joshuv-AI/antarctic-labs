Newborn screening for external congenital anomalies at three public hospitals in Karachi, Pakistan
Authors: Lubna Samad, Shazia Junejo, Ammar Ali Muhammad, Maryam Sherwani, Mahwish Fatima, Mahnoor Nawab, Naila Baig-Ansari, Felicity V Mehendale, Nobhojit Roy, Thorkild Tylleskär, Hakon Angell Bolkan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionReliable prevalence data on congenital anomalies are lacking in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), including Pakistan. This study aimed to determine the prevalence of external congenital anomalies through systematic newborn screening (NBS) in public sector hospitals in Karachi, Pakistan.MethodsA cross-sectional NBS programme was implemented from July 2023 to June 2024. Health workers (HWs) conducted head-to-toe newborn examinations, documented anomalies and provided counselling and referral guidance. Rates of intrauterine deaths and pre- and post-discharge neonatal mortalities were documented.ResultsOf the 25 414 births in the study period, 18 728 (73.7%) were screened. Major external anomalies were identified in 334 births (1.8%) and minor anomalies in 3384 (18.1%). The most common major anomalies included clubfoot, hypospadias, cleft lip/palate and spina bifida. Of all neonates screened, follow-up at the end of the neonatal period was completed for 70.6%, with 23.0% deaths recorded for those with major anomalies as compared to 1.8% with minor anomalies and 1.3% with no identified anomalies. Preterm birth, male sex and study site were significantly associated with the presence of anomalies (p<0.001).ConclusionThis is the largest systematically conducted NBS for external congenital anomalies in Pakistan, highlighting a substantial burden of congenital anomalies and associated neonatal mortality. Screening by trained non-physician HWs is feasible and scalable. However, screening must be paired with effective referral and care pathways to reduce mortality and morbidity.Keywords: Low and Middle Income Countries, Developing Countries, Mortality, Health services research

Introduction

Over 90% of congenital anomalies occur in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), yet reliable prevalence data from these regions remain largely unavailable.1–3 Estimates indicate that the Eastern Mediterranean Region (EMRO) bears the highest burden of birth defects, with Pakistan reporting a rate of 7.4% among live births.1 4 Accurate data are crucial for driving advocacy efforts, informing policy decisions and optimising clinical services.5

In the absence of systematic newborn screening (NBS) in our region, prevalence estimates are usually derived from data on individuals with congenital anomalies who seek care, which may not reflect the true burden.6,7 Screening of all births is essential for accurate estimates.

The terms ‘congenital anomalies’ and ‘birth defects’ are often used interchangeably and encompass a broad spectrum of structural and functional abnormalities.8 From a surveillance point of view, birth defects can be broadly categorised as (1) structural abnormalities visible on external examination; (2) internal anomalies that require diagnostic testing and (3) metabolic or chromosomal defects. Surveillance of external anomalies represents a pragmatic starting point for NBS in low-resource settings, such as ours, since these conditions are detectable through physical examination at birth, without reliance on diagnostic equipment or laboratory testing that may not be readily available. This approach can support the gradual maturation of surveillance capacity with the subsequent introduction of more resource-intensive screening. External congenital anomalies constitute a substantial proportion of all birth defects; however, accurately determining their contribution to the overall burden is challenging due to variations in classification systems, surveillance tools and methodological approaches used across studies and settings.9

This study aims to determine the prevalence of external congenital anomalies in a birth cohort screened by trained health workers (HWs) at free-of-cost public sector facilities catering to low-income populations in Karachi, Pakistan. We also aimed to determine stillbirth and neonatal mortality rates and the correlation of these outcomes with the presence of anomalies.

Methods

Objectives

The primary objective of this study was to determine the prevalence of external congenital anomalies in all births screened, while the secondary objective was to document mortality at the time of birth (stillbirth versus live births) in all screened, at the time of mother’s discharge from the birthing facility (early neonatal deaths) for all newborns alive at the time of screening, and at the end of the neonatal period (post-discharge neonatal deaths) amongst those that were alive at the time of mother’s discharge and contactable on the day 29 follow-up call.

Study design and duration

A cross-sectional NBS programme was conducted from 10 July 2023 to 30 June 2024, to screen newborns for external congenital anomalies at three selected facilities in Karachi, Pakistan.

Sample size

Sample size considerations were based on precision of prevalence estimation rather than statistical power, assuming a prevalence of major external anomalies ranging between 2.6% and 2.9%, a screened sample of approximately 18 719 births allows estimation with a 95% confidence level with a precision of ±0.2% to 0.3%.10 11 The achieved screened sample n=18 728 therefore provided adequate precision for facility-based prevalence estimates. G*power software (version 3.1.9.7, Heinrich-Heine-Universität Düsseldorf, Düsseldorf, Germany) was used to calculate the sample size.

Development of neonatal screening curriculum and toolkit

A learner-centred curriculum was designed to enable HWs to effectively screen neonates for external congenital anomalies, counsel affected families and provide timely referral. A standardised protocol for consent and photo-documentation of anomalies was developed.

A contextualised curriculum and tools were developed (online supplemental figure S1). Iterative changes were documented, and tools were revised during project implementation; thus, all material was co-created by HWs, programme team and clinical experts.

HW training

Based on pre-defined criteria, 15 HWs were recruited. At the end of the training period, 13 were selected based on their performance. HWs underwent comprehensive training by expert trainers using the developed curriculum and tools and employing interactive teaching strategies that have been described in detail in a companion publication.12

Study sites

Three facilities in Karachi were selected based on their birthing volumes: Sindh Government Qatar Hospital (SGQH), Civil Hospital Karachi (CHK) and Jinnah Postgraduate Medical Centre (JPMC). SGQH is a secondary-level hospital, whereas the latter two are tertiary-level facilities. All three are public sector hospitals offering free services, the catering to low-income populations of Karachi and surrounding areas. The average daily births are 15, 35 and 40 at SGQH, CHK and JPMC, respectively. Surveillance at these sites was dependent on post-delivery in-hospital stay for mothers. At SGQH, mothers are required to stay 10–24 hours after normal deliveries; therefore, surveillance at this site was conducted 7 days a week from 9 am to 5 pm, which allowed a screening coverage of more than 90% (table 1). CHK and JPMC each have three Gynaecology and Obstetrics wards where mothers are usually discharged within 4–6 hours after normal deliveries. Surveillance was initiated sequentially at the facilities and wards; initially, screening was conducted during daytime hours and once the HWs were able to screen reliably and independently, surveillance was extended to evening and night shifts.

Newborn screening

All consecutive births at the selected facilities were part of the surveillance sample (figure 1). The inclusion criteria were being born at the hospital after 28 weeks of gestation (as determined by the date of the last menstrual period recorded in clinical notes) and either a live birth or stillbirth (intrauterine death, IUD); a small number of deliveries that occurred en route to the hospital were included. Miscarriages (pregnancy losses before 28 weeks’ gestation as per institutional policy) were excluded. For the purpose of this study, ‘Early Neonatal Deaths’ (Early NNDs) were defined as all newborns that died prior to the mother’s discharge from hospital, and newborns alive at the time of mother’s discharge were labelled ‘Neonates Live at Discharge’ (NLDs).

Data captured in the study period (10 July 2023 to 30 June 2024) by CARe HWs during screening and follow-up. IUD, intrauterine death; NND, neonatal death; NLD, neonates live at discharge.

The HWs were stationed in proximity to the labour and operating rooms at each facility. After obtaining verbal informed consent from a parent or caregiver, a head-to-toe examination of the newborn was conducted by the HW in the presence of a family member, data were recorded, and photographs were taken of identified anomalies. Anomalies were classified as major or minor; if undetermined, they were labelled ‘category not established’. Newborns with both major and minor anomalies were categorised under major anomalies. Counselling and referral advice were provided to the families of the affected newborns based on the developed guidelines (online supplemental tables S1-S4).

HWs examined IUDs and early NNDs or derived information from the frontline staff if examination was not possible. A death form was filled, with the HW recording any visible anomalies and cause of death as ascertained by the hospital medical team. For those newborns referred urgently to the neonatology department, the HWs documented any obvious anomalies if feasible.

Follow-up call

At the end of the neonatal period (defined as 28 days after birth), the family of each enrolled newborn was contacted over the telephone by a HW on the first working day following day 28 to ascertain outcomes. Using a pre-scripted questionnaire, late manifestations of congenital anomalies were noted and neonatal deaths, as reported by the families on the follow-up call, were documented as ‘Post-discharge Neonatal Deaths’ (P/d NNDs). In case of no response, two additional attempts were made at different times of the same day, after which the case was documented as lost to follow-up.

Data collection and analysis

Initially, data were collected on paper forms and photographs saved on encrypted WhatsApp group chats. Study data collection was transitioned and managed using REDCap electronic data capture tools hosted at IRD, with linked photographs.13 14

Statistical analyses were performed using Stata version 17 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA). Missing data were imputed based on most common responses, ie, mode (observations with the highest count or frequency among all observed values).

Descriptive analyses were conducted using contingency tables (cross tabulations), with frequencies and proportions reported for the primary outcomes (overall prevalence of major and minor congenital anomalies) and the secondary outcome (neonatal mortality). Overall prevalence estimates were reported as proportions with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Additionally, characteristics were compared between contactable and lost to follow-up neonates.

Associations between outcomes and pre-specified covariates—including study site, gestational age at delivery, sex, birth weight, place of delivery, ethnicity, number of births in a single pregnancy and maternal and paternal age—were examined using the chi-square test, and in cases where the cell counts were less than five, Fisher’s exact p value was reported.

Bivariate and multivariable logistic regression models were used to identify predictors of major and minor congenital anomalies in all screened births compared to those without anomalies (primary objective) and predictors of mortality in screened neonates who did not survive versus those who were alive at the end of the neonatal period (secondary objective). Robust standard errors were applied to account for clustering at the study-site level. Results were presented as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CIs. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Programme and data monitoring

A manual register was maintained by HWs to record births, newborns screened and referred and site summaries shared for each shift.

Data were reviewed daily by the data team (SJ, MF, AAM, MN); any missing data were completed and discrepancies corrected after discussion with the relevant HW. Analysed data were presented in the form of monthly reports. Regular site visits were conducted by the programme team (SJ, MF) for monitoring and evaluation.

Data validation

A helpline physician provided support and confirmation for all anomalies identified by HWs during the initial implementation period. Concordance between the HWs and helpline physician’s identification of anomalies was assessed and reported.12 Additionally, expert reviews were conducted at 3, 9 and 12 months. In the first review, a panel of experts provided confirmation/suggestions after review of de-identified photographs of selected anomalies. The panel included a paediatric, plastic, orthopaedic, ENT and neuro-surgeons; a paediatrician and a dermatologist.

Screening criteria were reviewed and revised to address identified challenges. For grossly visible anomalies like cleft lip, HW screening was equated with confirmed diagnosis, but for anomalies like clubfoot which may require clinical expertise to confirm, a screened anomaly was not considered a confirmed diagnosis. Misdiagnosis and minor anatomical variants were addressed, and where required, were re-classified as normal: for example, the presence of minimal, clinically insignificant hydrocoele was reviewed and re-designated as a physiological variation, in contrast to moderate or severe hydrocoeles that were classified as anomalies. Some anomalies that were not included in the initial curriculum but commonly seen during surveillance, for example, lip-tie and bowed legs, were included in the revised curriculum. The second review was sought from the orthopaedic surgeon in response to increased clubfoot numbers seen during data review. Screening guidelines were revised based on the expert’s recommendations and HWs were re-trained. A final review was conducted after the conclusion of the project. Some doubtful cases of ambiguous genitalia and anorectal malformation were reviewed by experts for confirmation. Data were revised based on specialist diagnosis; thus, we have reported the prevalence based on confirmed anomalies.

Ethical considerations

Informed consent for enrolment in the study and for photographing the anomalies was obtained from a family member. Privacy and confidentiality were ensured at the time of screening and any concerns about the newborn’s health were immediately brought to the attention of the hospital staff. A unique study identification number was assigned to each participant. All data stored on the cloud-based server, laptops and drives were password-protected and accessible only to the programme team. Any dissemination of data, especially photographs, was done while safeguarding the identity of participants. All members of the study team were certified for Human Subjects Research by the Collaborative Institutional Training Initiative (CITI Program).

Administrative and relevant clinical heads of the study sites were engaged and approvals received for implementation at the selected sites. Ethical approval for the study was obtained prospectively from IRD’s Institutional Review Board (IRD_IRB_2023_03_001), and additional facility-level IRB approvals were obtained for CHK and JPMC. Modifications were resubmitted to the IRB for approval.

WhatsApp offers end-to-end encryption, and only group members can access shared information. During the first 3 months of data collection, WhatsApp was employed as a tool for guided clinical supervision; it was subsequently discontinued once HWs became more confident in their screening skills and transitioned to REDCap. All photos were deidentified, coded and transferred to a password-protected drive by the programme team, after which they were deleted from mobile devices to maintain confidentiality and data security. While we recognised the potential security risks of using WhatsApp for data sharing, this application is widely used, providing a convenient, readily available tool. Customized alternatives could be considered for future use at larger scale.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting or dissemination plans of our research.

Results

During the 12-month screening period, 25 414 births occurred at the three study sites (Figure 1 and table 1), which included live births and IUDs. CARe HWs screened 73.7% of these births (n=18 728), with varying coverage at the three hospitals (SGQH 92.2%, CHK 68.0%, JPMC 69.5%). Of all screened births, 17 957 (95.9%) were live births, 771 (4.1%) were IUDs and 65 were early NNDs (0.3% of all screened births, or 0.4% of all live births). Thus, 17 892 neonates were alive at the time of discharge (NLDs) from the facility.

Of the births screened by the HWs, 19.9% (n=3730) were identified with anomalies distributed across 334 (1.8%) major and 3384 (18.1%) minor categories (table 1). The prevalence of major and/or minor external anomalies in NLDs, early NNDs and IUDs is presented in table 1. The site-wise data indicates a higher prevalence of major anomalies in NLDs in the tertiary facility births (2.4% at JPMC and 1.3% at CHK) as compared to the secondary facility (1.0% at SGQH).

A solitary anomaly was identified in 3100 births (83.1%), while 630 (16.9%) were identified to have two or more anomalies (table 2). Thus, a total of 4405 anomalies were documented, including 394 major (8.9%) and 3988 (90.5%) minor anomalies; for the remaining 23 anomalies, major/minor category could not be established. The proportion of major anomalies, as compared to minor anomalies, was higher in the early NND and IUD groups (65.9% and 93.3%, respectively).

The major and minor anomalies identified among NLDs were categorised (online supplemental table S5). The commonest major anomalies identified were clubfoot (n=78), hypospadias (n=47), CL/P (n=43), spina bifida aperta (n=40), limb deficiencies (n=23) and anorectal malformations (ARM) (n=18). Tongue tie (n=1,102) was the most common minor anomaly identified, followed by spina bifida occulta (n=650), abnormally shaped ear (n=459) and lip tie (n=299).

Contact at the follow-up call was successfully established in 70.6% of NLDs, with 220 (1.7%) reported neonatal deaths (table 3). The characteristics of the contactable versus loss to follow-up (LTF) groups were compared (online supplemental table S6). The proportion of LTF neonates had a significantly higher overall prevalence of anomalies, but there was no difference between major and minor anomalies between the two groups. The two groups differed significantly by screening site, gestational age, maternal and paternal age, multiple pregnancy and ethnicity; the reasons for these findings are not possible to assess with the available information. The overall mortality during the neonatal period thus includes early NNDs (0.3%) from 18 728 total births screened and P/d NNDs (1.7%) from 12 640 contactable families. Neonatal mortality by site is provided in online supplemental table S7 which shows that the mortality rate of neonates delivered at JPMC is the highest at 2.0% (95% CI 1.6 to 2.4), followed by CHK.

It was observed that the mortality rate in neonates with anomalies was more than twice the mortality rate in neonates without anomalies and neonates with major anomalies had a higher mortality rate as compared to neonates with minor anomalies (table 3); both these findings were statistically significant (p-value <0.001).

On analysing the predictors of neonatal mortality (table 4), a significantly higher proportion of neonates with anomalies was seen in the ‘Deceased Neonates’ group. Among all deceased neonates, 21.8% had at least one major anomaly, as compared to 1.4% major anomalies among live neonates. The proportion of neonatal deaths was higher in CHK and JPMC births as compared to SGQH. Among deceased neonates, there was a significantly higher proportion of preterm, male, multiple births and low birth weight newborns. On multivariable logistic regression analysis, these findings were confirmed: newborns with major anomalies had 16.8 times higher odds (OR 95% CI 14.0 to 20.3, p<0.001) of dying in the neonatal period as compared to those without anomalies, adjusting for study site, gestational age at delivery and paternal age; newborns delivered at CHK and JPMC had 2.1 (OR 95% CI 2.0 to 2.2, p<0.001) and 1.9 times higher odds (OR 95% CI 1.8 to 1.9, p<0.001) of dying in the neonatal period, respectively, as compared to SGQH adjusting for type of anomaly, gestational age at delivery and paternal age and preterm newborns were 8.5 times more likely to die in the neonatal period as compared to full-term newborns, adjusting for study site, type of anomaly and paternal age (OR 95% CI 7.4 to 9.8, p<0.001) (table 5).

During the follow-up call, ten newborns were reported to have additional anomalies by caregivers, which were not detected at the time of initial examination. These anomalies were minor and included umbilical and inguinal hernias (n=3 each), haemangioma (n=2), sacral pit and neck swelling (n=1 each); this subset is not included in the main database.

The combined anomaly-specific mortality rates in IUDs and in early NNDs and p/d NNDs during the neonatal period were calculated (Supplementary Table S8). High mortality rates were seen for index major congenital anomalies within the neonatal period: anencephaly (100.0%), gastroschisis (88.9%), congenital hydrocephalus (69.0%), omphalocele (50.0%), encephalocele (50.0%), ARM (36.8%), spina bifida aperta (23.8%) and CL/P (20.0%).

The predictors of major anomalies in all screened births were assessed using cross tabulations (table 6). More than 80% of the births with major anomalies were screened at the tertiary care hospitals. Additional predictors were preterm births, male gender, low birth weight, maternal age more than 30 years, multiple births and Punjabi ethnicity. These associations were confirmed on multivariable logistic regression analysis (table 7); the strongest associations were with preterm births that had three times more likelihood of having a major anomaly as compared to the full-term births (OR 95% CI 2.0 to 4.4, p<0.001) adjusting for study site, gender, maternal age, single/multiple births and ethnicity; male births had 1.5 times higher odds of a major anomaly (OR 95% CI 1.4 to 1.7, p<0.001) as compared to females adjusting for the study site, gestational age at delivery, maternal age, single/multiple births and ethnicity; and multiple births were 1.5 (OR 95% CI 1.4 to 1.6, p<0.001) to 4.4 times (OR 95% CI 2.2 - 9.1, p<0.001) more likely to develop major anomalies as compared to single births adjusting for study site, gestational age at delivery, gender, maternal age and ethnicity. A similar analysis for predictors of minor anomalies is presented in table 8 and table 9; site of birth, gestational age and male gender were found to be associated with the presence of minor anomalies.

Discussion

We report a prevalence of 1.8% for major external congenital anomalies from the largest NBS programme reported from Pakistan. A wide prevalence range of 0.3–3.7% has been reported from LMICs, which we believe is more indicative of the lack of standardised screening methodology and definitions, rather than marked differences in prevalence.15–18 This wide range of prevalence is exemplified by a cross-sectional study from Tanzania reporting a prevalence of 0.3% of selected major anomalies in a large sample, which did not include major defects like anorectal malformation, omphalocele or gastroschisis;18 in comparison, a study from Nigeria documents 3.7% prevalence of major anomalies classified according to the site of the defects in a small cohort of 624 neonates.17 A retrospective review of hospital records from North India spanning two decades reported a major anomaly prevalence of 1.8% in live births.16

Many LMICs have implemented systematic NBS programmes, although the scope and focus vary widely. In the Asia-Pacific region, approximately 5 million newborns are screened annually, with programmes ranging from targeted metabolic screening in the Philippines to broader initiatives in India, including the Rashtriya Bal Swasthya Karyakram (RBSK).19–22 The RBSK provides a notable example of a national programme with early detection of externally visible birth defects as a key component, leveraging public healthcare resources and emphasising physical examination and observation to screen for a wide range of congenital conditions, although prevalence data from RBSK are not publicly available.21 22 Conversely, in Africa, screening efforts have mainly focused on single high-burden conditions such as sickle cell disease in Uganda and clubfoot in Ethiopia.23 24 In these resource-limited settings, professional bodies and specialist champions have been central to driving these initiatives in the face of severe public sector funding constraints.20 25 Overall, direct comparisons across LMIC programmes are challenging due to differences in methodology and target conditions.

We examine CL/P as a point metric, with a prevalence of 2.3 per 1000 births determined in our study. In LMICs, the CL/P prevalence ranges from 0.5 to 3.3 per 1000 births, which includes passive ascertainment through registries or birth records and active ascertainment through clinical examinations.26 A registry-derived prevalence rate previously reported from Pakistan was 1.9 per 1000 live births; the higher prevalence determined in our study may reflect the active screening methodology adopted.27 Additionally, neural tube defects were among the most common major congenital anomalies in our study population, reflecting the persistent burden of these conditions in Pakistan, a country with one of the highest rates of these defects in Asia.3 This burden is driven by the fact that periconceptional folate supplementation is not routinely practised and mandatory food fortification policies are inconsistently implemented.28

In our series, 18.1% newborns were identified to have minor external anomalies. This includes a diverse group of findings that do not necessarily translate into significant morbidity, disability or mortality, or may require further investigation to confirm diagnosis (eg, spina bifida occulta). The HWs were intentionally trained to refer newborns with suspected diagnoses for specialist review, minimising the chances of missing anomalies. This ‘safety net’ approach inevitably results in a higher number of false positives.

The high-mortality rate in our study is a cause for concern: one in four newborns with a major anomaly in our study population did not survive beyond the neonatal period. We note that this screening was conducted in an urban facility setting, in a city with Pakistan’s most well-developed public and private healthcare system, with specialist neonatal medical and surgical care available. Moreover, these newborns were screened within a few hours of birth by trained HWs, counselled and provided referral guidance. The absence of reliable pre-intervention data for our study setting makes it difficult to assess the impact of our screening and referral on outcomes. However, a condition-specific comparison indicates unacceptably high mortality rates: neonates with gastroschisis, ARM and CL/P had mortality rates of 88.9%, 36.8% and 20.0%, respectively, in our study as compared to <5%, <10% and <5% in developed settings.29–31 While prenatal screening and pregnancy termination have reduced neonatal mortalities from birth defects in high-income countries, other factors are likely to contribute to these disparities. The spectrum and severity of anomalies encountered in low-resource settings are potentially influenced by limited prenatal detection and differences in termination policies as well as postnatal factors, including delays in accessing care following birth and restricted neonatal intensive care and surgical capacity. Further efforts are needed to quantify delays in care and identify failures within referral and treatment pathways to better understand factors contributing to the high neonatal mortality identified in this study.

This screening programme was implemented at urban facilities. Currently, over 30% of births in Pakistan occur at home, most commonly among women with lower education levels and those from low-income households or rural areas.32 Since our study design does not include home births, our prevalence estimates may not be nationally representative. That said, it is important to note that complicated pregnancies are more likely to be referred to urban, tertiary facilities, leading to possible overestimation of both anomaly prevalence and mortality rates in our data.33 We recognise these biases in our facility-based screening and report our findings in that context.

Births occurring out of hours were not screened, which may introduce potential selection bias in the reported prevalence estimates. In the initial phase of the programme with daytime screening, the overall screening coverage in the two tertiary care hospitals (CHK and JPMC) was 62.7% and 70.0%, respectively. Site-wise analysis showed that in the latter months when round-the-clock screening was being conducted at both sites, screening coverage increased to 82.6% and 72.8%, respectively. The missed births mostly included neonates that were either preterm or critically sick at birth and needed immediate specialist care and referral. As per protocol, HWs did not examine these newborns to avoid delays in transfer and care; however, any observed gross anomalies or those recorded in hospital notes were noted. Since a higher prevalence of congenital anomalies in preterm and sick babies is well-documented in literature, these data may not represent the true prevalence of congenital anomalies in our cohort.34 Other studies have reported that newborns with major congenital malformations constitute a significant burden of NICU admissions.35 36 A Canadian study of 17 NICUs documented anomalies in 13.7% of admissions that were postnatal transfers from birthing facilities; these newborns had more severe illness and higher mortality.37 Further NICU-based studies may clarify the prevalence of congenital anomalies and their impact on outcomes in Pakistan.

Birth defect surveillance has received low priority in LMICs due to the high resource demands for surgical and medical management, limited health system capacity and the lack of primary preventive strategies.22 The greatest burden of congenital anomalies lies in the EMRO region, where over half of the member countries lack population-based surveillance programmes.4 38 As the common causes of neonatal mortality are successfully addressed, the proportionate contribution of birth defects has increased, drawing greater attention to birth defects within maternal and child health agendas in LMICs.39 With the decline in communicable diseases, this epidemiological shift demands robust data to shape evidence-based policy. However, population-based registries require extensive resources that are often difficult for LMICs to allocate.

The global advancement of cancer registries can serve as a valuable model for developing congenital anomaly registries. In resource-limited settings, hospital-based cancer registries (HBCRs) provide critical outcome data where population-based systems are absent. Though not comprehensive, HBCRs inform policy and resource planning, laying the foundation for broader surveillance efforts.40

In the absence of routine NBS in our setting, examination and documentation were carried out by HWs, ensuring the methodological rigour required for a robust prevalence study. Unlike rotating clinical staff, dedicated HWs helped provide uniformity in study processes, thereby improving the quality of surveillance. Co-creation and iterative refinement of the curriculum and training model also required intensive feedback that would not have been possible for overburdened facility staff. However, when translating this to a long-term scale-up model, sustainable approaches that integrate screening into existing health system roles need to be explored. While physician-led clinical examination is the standard in many LMICs, the use of trained non-physician providers has been widely adopted as a feasible alternative for surveillance in resource-constrained contexts.41 42 The ability of HWs, as the least formally trained cadre, to conduct screening effectively suggests that other non-physician personnel with more advanced clinical training could perform this role proficiently, provided they receive appropriate training, resources and capacity-building support.

Our model was able to successfully screen and identify a broad range of external anomalies; however, we believe that it is essential for surveillance to translate into improved outcomes. The high mortality rates for index conditions indicate that screening and referral guidance in themselves do not result in improved outcomes in LMIC settings. Future efforts must adopt a patient-centred approach that links screened newborns to effective care pathways, ensuring that recommended care translates into received care. Therefore, subsequent cycles of implementation research need to focus on developing care linkages and documenting impact on outcomes. In a resource-constrained setting like ours, a staged approach may be more pragmatic, with an initial focus on high-burden anomalies that are feasible to treat and where early intervention has the highest potential to affect reductions in mortality, morbidity and disability.

Strengths

A major strength of this study is the large sample size, which allowed for a statistically reliable estimation of the burden of congenital anomalies. The study design incorporated multiple layers of quality assurance, including continuous monitoring, second-line clinical assessments and regular supervision, all of which enhanced the screening process. Iterative refinement of data collection tools and processes, based on ongoing feedback, allowed for timely adaptations that improved both efficiency and accuracy. A key output of the programme was a context-specific training model for NBS, with potential for broader adaptation within similar settings.

Rigorous quality control mechanisms, standardised case definitions and the use of trained HWs for screening and data collection ensured data consistency across sites. Additionally, the inclusion of post-discharge follow-up strengthened outcome documentation, serving as an additional quality control measure. In our setting, where systematic medical records are often unavailable, our 28-day follow-up period stands as a significant achievement.43

Limitations

Selection bias

Selection bias due to missed screening of births has been discussed above in detail.

Diagnostic uncertainty and outcome misclassification

Screening was conducted by HWs, who cannot be expected to have the expertise and diagnostic reliability of specialists; this limitation is especially relevant for the detection of minor anomalies. Nevertheless, the screening approach was designed to have a safety net, favouring overdiagnosis over missed diagnoses.

Classification of cases as IUDs or early NNDs relied on the judgment of frontline hospital staff, with uncertain accuracy due to challenges in differentiation compounded with the absence of neonatal monitoring equipment. This may also explain the higher proportion of major anomalies observed in IUDs; as anomaly data were secondarily recorded from frontline staff or registers, minor anomalies were likely underreported due to limited direct examination by HWs.

Follow-up attrition

Since families of approximately 30% of screened neonates were uncontactable at the follow-up call, mortality outcomes were ascertained only among ‘contactable newborns’, which constitutes a potential source of selection bias given the observed differences in anomaly prevalence between contactable and non-contactable groups.

Facility-based referral bias

Facility-based referral patterns may have resulted in selection bias, as tertiary centres are more likely to receive referrals for complex obstetric cases, potentially inflating observed anomaly detection rates and neonatal mortality.

Conclusion

This HW-led, facility-based study represents the largest NBS initiative for external congenital anomalies in Pakistan, establishing robust prevalence data in a region lacking routine surveillance. While the surveillance in our study was conducted by HWs, all levels of frontline staff can be trained using an adapted approach. The high mortality rates observed in affected neonates emphasise the urgent need for not only surveillance to be established but also the importance of linking identified children to effective care pathways. Moving forward, efforts should be concentrated on integrating screening into the existing health systems, prioritising high-burden anomalies where early intervention has the highest potential for averting mortality, morbidity and disability, and contextualising referral mechanisms to address local healthcare constraints.
