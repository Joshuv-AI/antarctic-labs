Digesting detention: how to document eating patterns in jail
Authors: Elizabeth Schmitter, Nicolette I Teufel-Shone, Sara Shuman, Javier Lopez, Beya Thayer, Richard Martin, Ricky Camplain
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPeople incarcerated experience significant health inequities compared to the non-incarcerated population, including higher rates of many diet-related diseases. Despite widespread acceptance of the association between diet and health, research documenting the dietary intake and nutritional status of individuals incarcerated in jail is limited. Food intake is notoriously difficult to measure, and current measurement tools are not designed for the jail setting. This study aimed to assess the acceptability and feasibility of a comprehensive tool to assess food intake during incarceration.MethodsThe cycle menu and commissary list from a rural county jail in the Southwest United States were used to design a dietary assessment questionnaire tailored to the carceral setting. The instrument included structured tables listing all menu items by meal (breakfast, lunch, and dinner), allowing participants to indicate the foods and proportions consumed (none, less than half, about half, more than half but not all, all, or more than one serving). Additional items captured the type and quantity of foods obtained and consumed from the commissary.Participants completed the food intake questionnaire over a 4-day period. At the end of the four days, participants provided feedback on the questionnaire’s appropriateness and feasibility during group listening sessions. Verbal and written feedback was compiled and coded to identify common themes. Daily total and mean caloric, macronutrient, and micronutrient intakes were estimated using NutritionCalc Plus® and compared to appropriate Dietary Reference Intakes.ResultsOf the 65 people recruited, 64 agreed to participate in the study and 61 participants completed all four days of the questionnaire. Participants reported that they enjoyed reflecting on their diets and recommended several modifications to the questionnaire design to improve accuracy when documenting intake. Women reported consuming excess total calories and men reported consuming deficient total calories. Macronutrient consumption was within the recommended ranges for men and women, while sodium exceeded healthy recommendations. Participants also reported consuming inadequate amounts of fruits, vegetables, and minerals.ConclusionsWith specific modifications to improve documentation accuracy, the food intake questionnaire can be used in future research assessing nutritional status in jails, prisons, and other controlled food environments.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40795-026-01393-2.

Background

More than 12 million people cycle through jails every year in the United States and face numerous health challenges [1]. People incarcerated are more likely to have one or more chronic medical conditions, including hypertension, diabetes mellitus, heart disease, and cancer, compared to the non-incarcerated general population, all of which can be affected by dietary intake [2, 3]. Despite widely accepted associations between diet and health outcomes, studies documenting nutrition among incarcerated populations are rare. Research is even more limited on nutrient in jails, facilities that house people who are pre-trial, awaiting sentencing, or serving a short sentence (usually less than one year) [1, 4].

Carceral facilities design menus to meet budgetary constraints while also providing individuals incarcerated with nutritionally adequate meals [3]. Prisons typically spend $2 to $4 per day to feed an individual incarcerated all three meals [5]. County and city jails generally have fewer guidelines and oversight compared to prisons when designing, budgeting, and implementing their menus. Generally, jails are only required to have their menus approved annually by either a registered dietitian or food service director, who may not have any formal nutrition education or RD credentials [3, 5]. When a registered dietitian is involved, they often evaluate menus for nutritional adequacy based on Dietary Reference Intakes (DRIs), ensure appropriate macronutrient and micronutrient balance, and assess whether meals meet the needs of the people incarcerated, including those with chronic conditions or special dietary requirements. They also consider food safety, menu variety, and operational feasibility. However, due to the lack of national regulation and oversight of jails, only 38 states have nutritional standards for jails and detention facilities, and not all require mandatory implementation [5].

Jails typically provide meals with high sodium and saturated fat content; minimal availability of fruits, vegetables, and whole grains; and products low in fiber and micronutrients [3, 5–7]. Previous research assessed nutrition and diet adequacy in jails by analyzing the nutritional quality of the food described in cycle menus rather than actual food intake. [3, 6–7] Traditional food intake and dietary assessment tools including food records, food frequency questionnaires, 24-hour recalls, and screening tools may not be appropriate for use in carceral settings, including jails and prisons. Food records and food frequency questionnaires generally require motivated participants with the skills to accurately complete written assessments, which may not be reflective of the jail population, where approximately 46.5% have not completed high school, compared to 18.4% of the non-incarcerated population [8, 9]. Further, 24-hour recalls generally require expensive interviewer training and software to collect the data (e.g., Automated-Self-Administered or ASA-24) that are not available to individuals while incarcerated in jail, where access to computers and other technology is limited [8]. Other self-report measures of dietary intake are also subject to a high level of random and systematic measurement error, regardless of the study sample [8].

In jail settings, meal components and portion sizes are predetermined, and additional food options are limited to items available in the jail’s commissary. Given the advantages of having this a priori information, we identified an opportunity to develop a measurement tool tailored to more accurately capture dietary intake among individuals while they are incarcerated, while also functioning effectively within the operational constraints of the jail environment. Because primary data on diet within carceral facilities remain sparse, the objectives of this cross-sectional study were to (1) determine the acceptability and feasibility of a comprehensive dietary assessment tool for use during incarceration and (2) conduct a preliminary analysis of dietary intake patterns of the male and female study participants.

Methods

Study aim, design, and setting

Our study was conducted as part of a larger research project that aims to understand opportunities for physical activity in jails [10]. The food intake questionnaire was piloted along with a time use diary used to quantify physical activity and sedentary behavior among study participants. The full study procedure is described elsewhere [10].

Food intake questionnaire development

We obtained the 28-day cycle menu from the study site, which is a series of menus that is repeated over a specific period. In this case, unique menus are provided each day for four weeks (28 days), at which point the entire 28-day menu is repeated. All men and women incarcerated in the study site facility are served the same menus in the same portion sizes, unless they have dietary restrictions. For those that do have restrictions due to heath concerns or religious beliefs, special diets are available which were not evaluated specifically for the purposes of this paper. The cycle menu specified the food items served at each meal, the quantity provided per serving, and other criteria such as “all casseroles and combination dishes are made with soy unless otherwise indicated” and “imitation cheese with calcium is used”. Because food available in jail is limited to the cycle menu and foods purchased through commissary, this allowed a unique opportunity to capture food intake based on very finite options.

Using the cycle menu, we created tables with all menu items listed and determined measurement categories that could be adequately estimated by study participants without the use of a scale or other measurement tools. Categories including “none”, “less than half”, “about half”, “more than half but not all”, “all”, or “more than one serving” were added to the questionnaire. We developed additional questions for participants to document foods consumed each day not provided by the jail during mealtimes. We asked if participants had eaten any food from commissary that day, and if so, what and how much, as well as if they had consumed other foods that day that were not listed on the pre-published menus, such as lunches provided during work assignments in other locations in the jail or meals from special dietary menus. We provided participants with an additional free-response section asking them to describe anything else they wanted to tell us about their answers. Once the food intake questionnaire was finalized, specific days of the questionnaire that aligned with the cycle menu during the study period were added to the end of the four-day time use diary [10].

Study sample and compensation

All participants were recruited on the same day. The study sample was recruited from one male and one female general population housing unit in the study site. To participate in the study, participants had to be 18 years or older and have had an initial court appearance prior to the time of recruitment. Each participant received a pre-packaged commissary kit worth $45 when the study was completed that included popular self-care, food, and drink items. If a participant was released before the end of the study, they received an e-gift card worth $45. If a participant was transferred to the Department of Corrections (prison) before the end of the study, $45 was added to their prison commissary account. All participants provided informed consent, and all study activities were approved by Northern Arizona University’s Institutional Review Board, which included a “Prisoner Advocate”. A Prisoner Advocate is a voting member of an IRB who represents the interest of people incarcerated involved in research studies and can either be a former person incarcerated or a representative with an appropriate background and experience to understand the carceral setting from the incarcerated person’s perspective.

Demographic questionnaire

At the time of recruitment, participants completed a brief demographic questionnaire that included the date they were admitted into the jail, age, ethnicity, race, and if they had a job assignment at the time of the study.

Food intake questionnaire

Over a four-day period, participants filled out a pencil-and-paper record of their food intake while completing the time use diary for the parent research project [10]. Participants were asked to check the appropriate box in the questionnaire based on what and how much they ate and to answer the free response questions previously described. They were instructed to complete the questionnaire after each meal or snack to limit recall bias.

Listening sessions

At the end of the study period, the study researchers facilitated two listening sessions where participants were asked a series of questions regarding their experience with the time use diary and food intake questionnaire, including concerns, confusion, and recommendations for the questionnaire. The researchers took detailed notes of the listening sessions for analysis.

Food intake questionnaire data entry

All four days of data from each of the food intake questionnaires were entered into NutritionCalc Plus® (version 6.0.10), a dietary assessment tool used to quantify macronutrient, micronutrient, and food group intake [11]. Individual entries were created for each menu item based on product specifications included in the jail-provided menus, and meals were entered as breakfast, lunch, or dinner. Each menu item was entered using the term “Aramark” (the study site’s food service vendor) or “USDA” whenever possible. If NutritionCalc Plus® did not include a specific menu or food item, similar entries were used to match the nutritional value as close as possible (Appendix 1). The jail-provided menus indicated that all casseroles and combination dishes were made with soy unless otherwise stated; starches, vegetables, and cooked cereal were prepared with margarine unless indicated as low fat; no pork was used; and food was served with imitation cheese with calcium. All specifications were taken into consideration when translating the menu items into NutritionCalc Plus®.

When entering participant-reported quantities of food, questionnaire responses indicating “none” were entered as zero; “less than half” were entered as 25% of the portion size indicated on the menu; “about half” were entered as 50% of the indicated portion size; “more than half but not all” were entered as 75% of indicated portion size; “all” were entered as 100% of the indicated portion size; and “more than 1 serving” responses were entered as 200% of the indicated portion size. When entering the additional free response data, the written items were matched to the list of available commissary items at the study site. If a quantity was listed, the same translations for menu items (above) were used based on the package size of the available commissary items. If no quantities were listed, then 100% of the package size was entered. This process was repeated for each completed food intake questionnaire for all participants, and the “All Daily Reports” function was run using NutritionCalc Plus®. Reports were saved for each participant and nutrient data points were extracted and entered into Microsoft Excel for analysis.

Qualitative analysis

Participants were encouraged to write down their thoughts, feedback, and any other reflections from the study on the back page of their time use diary, along with answering the free response questions included in the food intake questionnaire. All feedback provided in both sections was recorded, and any reflections related to food, beverages, meals, or the food intake questionnaire were extracted and compiled with the notes from the listening sessions. All quotes were then tagged with keywords and themes were synthesized based on the common keywords in the collected feedback.

Statistical analysis

NutritionCalc Plus® calculated mean daily intake values for each participant over the four-day study period for the following variables: calories (kcal), carbohydrates (g), protein (g), fat (g), calories from fat (kcal), calories from saturated fat (kcal), total sugar (g), added sugar (g), fiber (g), sodium (mg), calcium (mg), magnesium (mg), potassium (mg), fruit intake (cup equivalent), and vegetable intake (cup equivalent). NutritionCalc Plus® also calculated the percentage of calories from protein, carbohydrates, and fat during the same period. The percentage of calories from saturated fat and added sugar were calculated using the total calorie intake, saturated fat (9 kcal/g), and added sugar (4 kcal/g) from NutritionCalc Plus®. Microsoft Excel was used to calculate descriptive statistics, including sample means for individual variables with corresponding 95% confidence intervals. The mean age of the men and women in the sample was calculated and used when comparing the results to age-specific recommendations from the Dietary Guidelines for Americans. Calorie, macronutrient, and specific micronutrient intakes were compared to the respective Dietary Reference Intakes (DRI), including the Acceptable Macronutrient Distribution Range (AMDR), Recommended Daily Amount (RDA), Adequate Intake (AI), and Dietary Guidelines for Americans (DGA), although the comparisons were based on generalized recommendations that do not account for individual age, height, weight, or physical activity level among study participants [12, 13].

Most participants (78%) identified as White, while 23% identified as Hispanic/Latino(a), 20% as Native American, and 5% as Black (categories were non-mutually exclusive). The mean age for male participants was 40 years, and for females it was 37 years. All participants returned their questionnaires, including those released or transferred during the study period. The time use diaries were determined to be acceptable in the parent study based on completion rates and feedback [10]. More than 95% of participants (n = 61) completed all four days of the food intake questionnaire. Of the participants who did not complete all four days, two did not complete any days, while one only recorded one day of intake, so all three were excluded in the preliminary statistical analyses. One participant was excluded for discrepancies in their responses (checked “none” and “all” for every food item in the questionnaire). The remaining 60 food intake questionnaires were included in statistical analyses. Feedback from all the questionnaires was included in the qualitative analysis.

Participant feedback (listening sessions and open-ended questionnaire responses)

During the listening sessions, participants reported that they enjoyed reflecting on their diets through the questionnaire. Most feedback highlighted that the menu items listed in the food intake questionnaire were inaccurate or frequently did not match what was served, making it difficult to determine which foods to choose from the prespecified list. Participants recommended that future versions of the questionnaire allow them the ability to write in the food items they consume rather than selecting from a list. These themes were echoed in the open-ended responses and reflections sections of the food intake questionnaire. Many participants wrote terms like “misleading” and “inflated” when describing the menu items as written in the questionnaire. Questionnaire responses related to the discrepancy included: “The items marked ‘none’ were not served to us at all”; “The menu looks far better than it actually is. We get about ½ of these items – substituted for others”; “The food they actually serve here is different than what this list says it is”; and “The menu items they provided you are inflated and not accurate.” Participants frequently documented which food items they did not receive and often specified a lack of fruit and limited variety in their protein and bread options. The participants’ written and verbal reflections expressed frustration and suggested a lack of accountability and transparency around the institutional food service. Although other themes were identified during analysis, the menu discrepancy was the primary feedback that was considered when understanding the participants’ perceived accuracy of the food intake questionnaire and was incorporated into the improvements made in future versions of the questionnaire.

Food intake

Protein, carbohydrate, fat, and added sugar intakes were within the acceptable range of total caloric intake (Fig. 1). Specifically, protein accounted for 14.2% of total calories (95% CI: 13.5, 14.8), within the AMDR of 10–35%. Carbohydrate accounted for 56.4% of total calories (95% CI: 55.5, 57.3), within the AMDR of 45–65%. Fat accounted for 31.1% of total calories (95% CI: 30.4, 31.8), within the AMDR of 20–35%. Added sugar was 3.3% of total calories (95% CI: 2.9, 3.7), meeting the recommendation of less than 10%. Saturated fat intake slightly exceeded the recommended limit of 10% of total calories (11.2%, 95% CI: 10.9, 11.5).

Women reported consuming more calories per day (2,258, 95% CI: 1,975, 2,540) compared to men (1,984, 95% CI 1,733, 2,235) (Fig. 2). Women’s average caloric intake exceeded the recommendation of 1,800 calories per day for a sedentary 37-year-old female (the mean age for female study participants). These trends in excess and inadequate caloric intake among women and men, respectively, were consistent when the data was stratified based on whether the participants consumed commissary items. This difference in intake between men and women may be explained by women in fact consuming more food overall or women being more thorough in their reporting of intake.

Discussion

This study piloted and evaluated the acceptability of a novel food intake questionnaire to assess dietary intake among individuals incarcerated in jail. Participants were instructed to complete the questionnaire in real-time after each meal to limit recall bias, although the research team could not guarantee that some recall bias was not introduced, as participants may have chosen to fill out the questionnaire at the end of the day or at the end of the study period. Nearly all (98%) recruited individuals agreed to participate in the study. All individuals who consented to participate returned their diaries and food records at the end of the study period. Only two participants did not complete the food questionnaire. All other responses were complete and indicated a general acceptability of this type of food intake questionnaire among individuals incarcerated, although participants recommended improvements.

Based on feedback from the study participants, both verbally during the listening sessions and written in the reflections and free response questions, the biggest obstacle to completing the food intake questionnaire accurately was the discrepancy between what was listed on the menu and what was served. The tables in the questionnaire were designed based on the cycle menu that the jail administration provided to the research team in advance of the study period. The differences that participants reported between the written menu and what was served may have been the result of jail staff altering the menu based on limited product availability. The discrepancy may also be explained, at least in part, by the participants’ general dislike of the food items provided by the jail and subsequent difficulty reconciling food items written on the menu with actual food items served. For example, the “Irish blend vegetables” menu item may have appeared as peas and/or carrots on the participants’ plates, making it difficult to match the written menu item with the served food item.

Although we could not definitively determine which items were served by the jail, participants’ feedback about discrepancies in the menu and foods served informed modifications to the questionnaire to improve accuracy for future research (Appendix 2). Specifically, a column was added to the table where participants could indicate if a food item was not served on that day. Free response questions were also updated to more specifically prompt participants to write down any food items that were served but were not included in the table (e.g., “Did you eat anything else today not included in your previous responses? This could include food served that isn’t listed in the above table, food provided at work, etc.”). In future uses of the questionnaire, we recommend that researchers verify menu items with jail administration for the days in the study period.

Although participants did not explicitly express concerns about portion-size categories, an inherent limitation to the questionnaire is that some measurement categories required approximation. For example, when participants selected “less than half” of a serving, we quantified this as 0.25 servings, although actual intake could have been as little as a single bite. Similarly, “more than one serving” was operationalized as two servings, despite the possibility that a participant may have only consumed slightly more than one serving (e.g., 1.1 or 1.2 servings) or more than two servings. Of the 4,758 recorded responses, “less than half” was selected only 85 times (1.8%) and “more than one serving” 153 times (3.2%), indicating that these potentially imprecise categories were used infrequently. As a result, any over‑ or underestimation introduced by these approximations is unlikely to meaningfully influence overall findings.

Importantly, the purpose of this study was not to estimate clinically precise individual‑level dietary intake estimates but rather characterize group-level dietary patterns within a jail setting. Given the constraints of the correctional environment and the need for a simple, low‑burden assessment tool, categorical portion estimates were both pragmatic and appropriate. Increasing the granularity of portion‑size reporting (e.g., distinguishing between 1.1 and 2.0 servings) would not have been feasible for participants and could have increased respondent burden, ultimately reducing data quality and participation. Thus, although the portion categories necessarily involved estimation, they represent a reasonable balance between feasibility and accuracy for assessing aggregate dietary intake patterns among incarcerated individuals.

Due to discrepancies in the menu and what was served, as well as the convenient nature of our sample, our general findings about dietary intake and overall dietary quality may not be accurate or representative of people incarcerated in the study site facility. Nevertheless, our findings suggest that people incarcerated in jail consumed an appropriate distribution of proteins, carbohydrates, and fats; excess sodium; and inadequate amounts of fruits, vegetables, and minerals, including potassium, calcium, and magnesium. These consumption patterns may contribute to a higher risk of chronic conditions, such as hypertension, among people incarcerated [15–17].

Women reported consuming more total calories daily than men, despite having lower caloric needs on average. High caloric intake may have implications for weight and body composition changes among women incarcerated in jail, particularly for those with extended incarceration timelines. Jails often design their menus for men, as they make up 80–90% of people incarcerated in jail [18]. Provisions of excess total calories and sodium, especially when the jail menu is consumed in conjunction with food items purchased through commissary, contributes to poor health outcomes for women, who generally have lower resting metabolic rates and lower physical activity levels compared to men [19]. Thus, women face unique health inequities when incarcerated [20, 21]. Previous research has demonstrated incarceration is associated with weight gain and an increased prevalence of overweight and obesity, particularly among women [16]. Women often gain weight during and following incarceration, often significantly more than their male counterparts [19, 21, 22].

While still preliminary and limited in their application, our findings are consistent with previous research that analyzed jail menus as a proxy for dietary intake among individuals incarcerated in jails. One menu analysis for a Georgia county jail found that macronutrients were available within the AMDR-recommended ranges, while vegetable offerings fell below recommended levels [3]. Sodium content in previous studies exceeded the dietary guidelines, even when jail-provided menu items were evaluated independently of more heavily processed commissary items [3, 5–7]. Similar trends were reflected in our results, highlighting the importance of collecting observational food intake data from men and women incarcerated in jail to address various nutritional risk factors. If our future research yields results that are consistent with these trends, these findings will inform policy recommendations for jail and detention facilities’ foodservice programs to support the health of people incarcerated. Examples of policy support may include improved budgets for food, training and storage to incorporate additional fresh fruits and vegetables into the menu, provision of lower sodium items through commissary, and the opportunity for nutrition education for foodservice providers.

Future iterations of the questionnaire will allow for more flexibility and customization when completing the food log to account for changes in the menu (when the items served do not match the items listed in the questionnaire) and special dietary trays. The updated version that will be used in future research also includes questions designed to assess palatability, in addition to intake, to understand how preferences impact the food environment in jails.

Conclusions

Dietary intake is notoriously difficult to measure across most populations. Specific dietary intake measurement tools, such as the food frequency questionnaire, are not designed for use in carceral settings. However, the unique conditions in these settings also present an opportunity to assess intake based on a finite number of food options. With the inclusion of the modifications and improvements outlined in this article, the custom food intake questionnaire designed for this study is an acceptable and feasible way to assess food intake among populations incarcerated in jails and prisons. A similar design may also be useful for research in other controlled food environments such as schools and residential care facilities. Our future research will take a longitudinal approach to data collection, utilizing this improved food intake questionnaire to assess dietary patterns in those incarcerated long-term, shedding light on the accumulative, documented disease risk associated with incarceration. In future data analysis, the research team will also incorporate individual-level factors, including height, weight, age, pregnancy status, and physical activity level, when comparing dietary intake with recommendations. Future research will also evaluate changes in dietary patterns after release from incarceration and evaluate the long-term impact of incarceration on diet.
