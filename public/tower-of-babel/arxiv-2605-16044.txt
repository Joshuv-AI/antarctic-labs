Quantum Feature Amplification Network (QFAN) as An Autoregressive Quantum Generative Model
Jamal Slim, Saverio Monaco, Florian Rehm, Dirk Krücker, Kerstin Borras

Abstract
Existing direct-register, direct pixel-output quantum generative models for calorimeter shower simulation tie the effective quantum output dimension to the image dimension, so the required register size grows with the full image size. More recent quantum-assisted approaches alleviate this only by shifting part of the generative burden into hybrid latent-variable constructions. As a result, current quantum demonstrations remain far below the detector geometries used in high-energy physics experiments. We introduce the Quantum Feature Amplification Network (QFAN) algorithm, which removes this bottleneck by decomposing an image into sequential blocks, each produced by the same small parameterized quantum circuit whose input is a compressed summary of all previously generated pixels. Because the circuit is reused rather than enlarged, the qubit requirement is set by block size rather than by the full image dimension, while the per-step quantum processing cost (the number of quantum circuits executed in one gradient step) is independent of image size for the family of Pauli observables used here. We derive a conservative worst-case bound on shot-noise propagation through the generation chain and identify an empirical decoder-capacity heuristic that bounds the sequential depth reachable within the present architecture. A three-qubit circuit with twelve shared quantum variational parameters, together with closed-form classical ridge decoders and a post-hoc residual sampler, reproduces per-pixel intensity distributions, inter-pixel correlations, and the total energy distribution of calorimeter showers on both a simulator and IBM quantum hardware. At this scale, the observed hardware–simulator gap is consistent with optimization-budget limitations dominating over device noise, although the present experiments do not causally separate these effects. These results establish a hardware-compatible proof of principle and motivate, but do not validate, larger-scale extrapolations within the same circuit family.

Most particles entering a calorimeter initiate a stochastic cascade of secondary interactions, depositing energy across hundreds to thousands of detector cells. Simulating these showers from first principles with

Quantum generative models offer a qualitatively different design point in the form of compact circuits whose correlation structure is mediated by entanglement rather than by deep layered architectures. In principle, a few-qubit parameterized circuit can encode nontrivial dependencies with a parameter count far smaller than that of modern classical generative networks.
In practice, however, existing gate-model quantum calorimeter proof-of-concepts and quantum-assisted calorimeter generators

This is an architectural limitation of the direct-register, direct pixel-output formulation: the quantum register must scale with the full image rather than with a compressed or blockwise representation.

The insight behind the Quantum Feature Amplification Network (QFAN) is that a quantum circuit does not need to hold the whole image at once.
If image generation is decomposed into autoregressive blocks, and each block is produced by the
same

II
Problem Formulation

A calorimeter shower image
\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
records the energy deposited in
d
detector cells.
The target distribution
p_{\mathrm{data}}
combines two difficulties that make it a stringent test for any generative model.
Each cell marginal is highly non-Gaussian, typically unimodal with a skewed tail whose width and peak position vary across cells, and the cells are strongly correlated across detector layers through conservation laws and shower-shape constraints.
A faithful surrogate must reproduce both the marginals and the correlation structure; matching one without the other is insufficient for downstream physics analysis.

The architectural constraint that drives every design decision in this work is
n_{q}\ll d
.
At the demonstration scale this ratio is modest (
n_{q}{=}3
,
d{=}12
, so
d/n_{q}{=}4
), whereas realistic calorimeter image dimensions are far larger
9
.

In a direct-register, direct pixel-output formulation, the mapping from quantum register to image pixels imposes one of two costs. Born machines and amplitude-encoded models use only
\lceil\log_{2}d\rceil
qubits, but they produce probability distributions rather than real-valued pixel intensities, and generic state preparation requires circuit depth that scales linearly in
d
. Angle-encoding models that emit real-valued
outputs require
n_{q}=\Omega(d)
qubits. In either case, scaling to the full image dimension
d
imposes a resource cost, in register size, circuit depth, or output representation, that grows with
d
.
The central question is therefore how to bridge this gap without simply enlarging the quantum register.

Our approach is to exploit an autoregressive factorization of the joint distribution.
Partition the
d
-pixel image into
B
contiguous blocks
\bm{y}_{1},\ldots,\bm{y}_{B}
, each containing up to
b
pixels, and denote by
\bm{y}_{<\beta}\equiv(\bm{y}_{1},\ldots,\bm{y}_{\beta-1})
the pixels generated before block
\beta
.
The joint distribution then factors exactly as

p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{y})=\prod_{\beta=1}^{B}p_{\bm{\theta}}\!\left(\bm{y}_{\beta}\,\big|\,\bm{y}_{<\beta}\right).

(1)

By assigning each block
\bm{y}_{\beta}
to a separate invocation of the same circuit and compressing the conditioning history
\bm{y}_{<\beta}
into a streaming sketch, the register need not grow with
d
.
Figure
2
summarizes this compression–expansion mechanism:
n_{q}
qubits yield
p_{f}{=}n_{q}^{2}{+}n_{q}
Pauli features, these support ridge decoding of block outputs up to size
b_{\max}
under the empirical heuristic introduced below, and repeating the same circuit over
B
autoregressive steps tiles the full image.

III.2
Sketch conditioning

The autoregressive factorization in Eq. (
1
) requires each block to be conditioned on all previously generated pixels.
A straightforward approach, namely feeding the raw prefix
\bm{y}_{<\beta}
directly into the circuit, would couple the circuit input dimension to
d
, defeating the purpose of a small register.
Instead, we compress the history into a count-sketch
6

\bm{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
(
m{=}32
), updated incrementally after each block according to

s_{j}\mathrel{+}=\sum_{k\in\mathrm{block}\,\beta}\sigma(k)\,\tilde{y}_{k}\,\mathbf{1}[h(k){=}j],

(2)

where
h{:}[d]\to[m]
and
\sigma{:}[d]\to\{{\pm}1\}
are fixed random hash and sign functions.
A near-identity mixing layer
\tilde{\bm{s}}{=}\tanh(\bm{s}\bm{M}^{\top})
decorrelates hash collisions before the sketch enters the circuit.

We use a count-sketch rather than, say, a learned embedding for three properties are essential.
First, the update cost is
\mathcal{O}(b)
per block and the memory cost is
\mathcal{O}(m)
, both independent of prefix length.
The sketch does not become more expensive as the image grows.
Second, the sketch introduces no trainable parameters coupled to
\bm{\theta}
, so the optimization remains clean and no bilevel training problem is introduced at this stage.
Third, the sketch is composable in a streaming setting.
This means a precomputed cache
\mathcal{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times N\times m}
can be stored once and reused across training steps, amortizing the compression cost.
Throughout this work, the hash/sign functions and the linear maps
\bm{M}
,
\bm{A}
, and
\bm{b}
are fixed after initialization and are not optimized by SPSA; the only trainable quantum parameters are
\bm{\theta}
, while the ridge and residual components are fit in closed form or post hoc.

Proposition 1

(Sketch distortion)
.

Let
S\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}
denote the count-sketch matrix induced by the hash and sign functions in Eq. (
2
), and define the sketch inner-product estimator

\widehat{\langle\bm{y},\bm{y}^{\prime}\rangle}\equiv\langle S\bm{y},S\bm{y}^{\prime}\rangle.

For standard count-sketch randomness, the estimator is unbiased and satisfies

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\bigl(\widehat{\langle\bm{y},\bm{y}^{\prime}\rangle}-\langle\bm{y},\bm{y}^{\prime}\rangle\bigr)^{2}\right]\leq\frac{C}{m}\,\|\bm{y}\|_{2}^{2}\|\bm{y}^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}

(3)

for a universal constant
C
.

Proof.

The count-sketch matrix
S
has exactly one nonzero entry per column, equal to a random sign in a uniformly chosen row.
Hence
\widehat{\langle\bm{y},\bm{y}^{\prime}\rangle}=\langle S\bm{y},S\bm{y}^{\prime}\rangle
is an unbiased estimator of
\langle\bm{y},\bm{y}^{\prime}\rangle
.
Expanding the second moment and averaging over the sketch randomness leaves only collision terms, whose total contribution is bounded by
C\|\bm{y}\|_{2}^{2}\|\bm{y}^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}/m
.
This is the standard count-sketch second-moment guarantee.
∎

Design heuristic.

Calorimeter showers are sparse and spatially clustered, with substantially fewer than
d
effectively independent entries.
This motivates the practical heuristic
m{\gtrsim}c\sqrt{d}
used in the scaling discussion below, but this rule is not derived from Proposition
1
and is not claimed as a theorem.
We use
m{=}32
at
d{=}12
, where the sketch is over-provisioned and distortion is negligible.

III.4
Pauli features

The quantum state produced by the circuit must now be converted into classical features that the ridge decoder can use.
We measure the observable family

\mathcal{O}=\{Z_{i},X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n_{q}}\cup\{Z_{i}Z_{j},X_{i}X_{j}\}_{1\leq i<j\leq n_{q}},

yielding
p_{f}{=}n_{q}^{2}{+}n_{q}
expectation values per shot batch from exactly two tensor-product measurement settings.

Lemma 1

(Feature count and measurement groups)
.

Let
G
denote the number of distinct tensor-product measurement
settings required to estimate all observables in
\mathcal{O}
. For the observable family

\mathcal{O}=\{Z_{i},X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n_{q}}\cup\{Z_{i}Z_{j},X_{i}X_{j}\}_{1\leq i<j\leq n_{q}},

the number of observables is

p_{f}=2n_{q}+2\binom{n_{q}}{2}=n_{q}^{2}+n_{q},

(4)

and all observables can be estimated from exactly two tensor-product measurement settings: the all-
Z
basis and the all-
X
basis.
Hence
G{=}2
, independent of
n_{q}
.

Proof.

The all-
Z
basis yields the expectations of every single-qubit operator
Z_{i}
and every pairwise operator
Z_{i}Z_{j}
from the same bitstring sample.
Likewise, the all-
X
basis yields all
X_{i}
and
X_{i}X_{j}
expectations.
Therefore the full observable family is covered by two measurement settings.
Counting terms gives

n_{q}+\binom{n_{q}}{2}+n_{q}+\binom{n_{q}}{2}=n_{q}^{2}+n_{q}.

∎

The inclusion of weight-2 operators (
ZZ

Single-qubit expectations carry no information about pairwise qubit correlations, whereas weight-2 terms map directly to inter-pixel correlations within a block.

III.5
Ridge decoder and the capacity heuristic

The feature matrix
\bm{F}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times p_{f}}
must now be decoded into pixel values.
A neural decoder would introduce additional trainable parameters, creating a bilevel optimization problem under SPSA, because the decoder would need to be re-optimized at every outer step for the gradient estimate to remain meaningful. Ridge regression avoids this entirely

\bm{W}_{\beta}=\left(\bm{F}^{\top}\bm{F}+\alpha\bm{I}\right)^{-1}\bm{F}^{\top}\bm{Y}_{\beta}.

(5)

Because
\bm{W}_{\beta}
is a closed-form smooth function of
\bm{F}
for
\alpha>0
, the decoder updates immediately when the optimizer perturbs
\bm{\theta}
, and SPSA always evaluates a well-defined scalar loss.

This closed-form solution is reliable only when there are enough features to constrain the fit.
If the block size
b
approaches or exceeds
p_{f}
, the inverse problem becomes poorly conditioned and shot noise is amplified through the decoder.
This motivates the central empirical heuristic used in the scaling discussion.

Remark 1

(Empirical decoder-capacity heuristic)
.

For stable ridge decoding at the demonstration scale, the feature-to-target ratio

\rho\equiv\frac{p_{f}}{b}=\frac{n_{q}^{2}+n_{q}}{b}

(6)

should remain above a practical threshold inferred from the present block-size sweep,
\rho_{\min}\approx 1.5
.
Treating this threshold as a working heuristic at the present scale yields the estimates

b_{\max}(n_{q})=\left\lfloor\frac{n_{q}^{2}+n_{q}}{\rho_{\min}}\right\rfloor,\qquad B_{\min}(d,n_{q})=\left\lceil\frac{d\,\rho_{\min}}{n_{q}^{2}+n_{q}}\right\rceil.

(7)

The threshold
\rho_{\min}\approx 1.5
is inferred from a
single block-size sweep at
d{=}12
(Table
1
)
and should be treated as a working heuristic at the present
scale, not as a universal constant.

Equation (
7

Its most important consequence is that each additional qubit reduces the minimum block count roughly quadratically, making modest increases in qubit count disproportionately valuable.

III.6
Gated residual sampler

The ridge decoder captures the linear relationship between Pauli features and pixel values, but calorimeter distributions have a pronounced nonlinear structure that a linear map cannot fully represent.
The residuals
\bm{R}_{\beta}
between the ridge prediction and the true pixel values absorb this structure and are modeled with a small
K
-means cluster bank (
M{=}8
clusters).
At generation time, a logistic gate
g{:}\,\,\mathbb{R}^{m}{\to}\Delta^{M-1}
predicts cluster membership from the sketch, and a residual is drawn from the corresponding cluster.
The choice
M{=}2^{r}
is deliberate. The cluster sampler is a natural drop-in replacement for a Born sampler on
r
qubits, providing an upgrade path when additional qubits become available.

In the present implementation, the residual bank is fitted after the quantum encoder has been trained, using residuals from the frozen ridge predictor on the training set.
It therefore refines sampling at generation time without entering the SPSA objective.

Training-time conditioning versus generation-time rollout.

During training, the sketch cache for block
\beta
is constructed from ground-truth prefixes, so the blockwise objective is evaluated under teacher-forced conditioning.
During generation, by contrast, the sketch is updated with model outputs, yielding a free-running autoregressive rollout.
At the present demonstration scale (
B{=}2
) this distinction is mild, but for long chains it can induce exposure bias.
Accordingly, the scaling analysis developed below concerns architectural resource requirements and noise propagation under the stated conditioning mechanism; it does not by itself guarantee unchanged sample fidelity at arbitrarily large block count.

IV
Training and Per-Step Cost

With the pipeline defined, the question is whether the 12 shared quantum variational parameters
\bm{\theta}
can be trained efficiently, specifically whether the training cost per step depends on the image dimension.

Parameters are optimized via SPSA
17
with common-random-number variance reduction.
At each step a random block
\beta
and minibatch
\mathcal{I}
are drawn.
The pre-computed teacher-forced sketch cache
\mathcal{C}[\beta,\mathcal{I}]
is looked up, and two forward passes at
\bm{\theta}{\pm}c_{t}\bm{\Delta}
yield a stochastic gradient estimate.
Each forward pass executes
Gn_{b}
circuits on the QPU.
All remaining computation (ridge inversion
\mathcal{O}(p_{f}^{2}n_{b})
and MMD evaluation
\mathcal{O}(n_{b}^{2}b)
) is classical.
During this stage the loss is computed from the ridge predictor alone; the residual sampler is fitted only after the encoder parameters are frozen.

Theorem 1

(Per-step circuit-count independence)
.

For the observable family of Lemma
1

circuit executions, independent of

,

,

, and the total number of optimization steps.

With

4n_{b}N_{s}
for the present choice and
2Gn_{b}N_{s}
in the general case.

Proof.

Each SPSA step evaluates the loss at two parameter settings

\bm{\theta}\pm c_{t}\bm{\Delta}
, each requiring
Gn_{b}
circuit
executions (
G

n_{b}
samples).
The total is
2Gn_{b}=4n_{b}
for

V
Shot-Noise Propagation

Theorem
1
shows that the number of circuits per step is constant in
d
, but each circuit’s
shots
are a separate resource.
Because blocks are generated sequentially, shot noise in block
\beta
propagates forward; pixel errors corrupt the sketch, which biases the circuit angles for block
\beta{+}1
, and so on down the chain. Bounding this cascade is essential for determining shot-count requirements at scale.

Theorem 2

(Expected sketch-noise accumulation)
.

Let
\delta\mathbf{f}_{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{f}}
denote the shot-noise error in the estimated Pauli features for block
\beta
, and assume each feature estimator is unbiased with

\mathbb{E}\,[\delta f_{\beta,\ell}]=0,\qquad\mathrm{Var}(\delta f_{\beta,\ell})\leq\frac{1}{N_{s}},\qquad\ell=1,\dots,p_{f}.

(11)

Define the conservative decoder gain

\bar{w}\equiv\max_{\beta}\max_{j\in[b]}\sum_{\ell=1}^{p_{f}}\left|(\bm{W}_{\beta})_{\ell j}\right|.

(12)

Then the induced pixel error satisfies

\mathbb{E}\,\|\delta\tilde{\bm{y}}_{\beta}\|_{\infty}\leq\bar{w}\sqrt{\frac{p_{f}}{N_{s}}},

(13)

and after
B
blocks with
Bb{=}d
, the expected perturbation of any sketch entry obeys

\mathbb{E}\,|\delta s_{j}|\leq d\,\bar{w}\sqrt{\frac{p_{f}}{N_{s}}}.

(14)

Consequently, if the sketch scale and decoder gain remain controlled, a sufficient condition for expected sketch SNR at least
\tau_{\min}
is

N_{s}\geq\frac{d^{2}\bar{w}^{2}p_{f}}{\tau_{\min}^{2}\|\bm{s}\|_{\infty}^{2}}.

(15)

Proof.

Because the decoder for block
\beta
is linear, the generated-pixel perturbation is

\delta\tilde{\bm{y}}_{\beta}=\delta\mathbf{f}_{\beta}\,\bm{W}_{\beta}.

For any output coordinate
j
,

|(\delta\tilde{\bm{y}}_{\beta})_{j}|\leq\sum_{\ell=1}^{p_{f}}|\delta f_{\beta,\ell}|\,|(\bm{W}_{\beta})_{\ell j}|\leq\|\delta\mathbf{f}_{\beta}\|_{\infty}\sum_{\ell=1}^{p_{f}}|(\bm{W}_{\beta})_{\ell j}|.

Taking the maximum over
j
gives

\|\delta\tilde{\bm{y}}_{\beta}\|_{\infty}\leq\bar{w}\,\|\delta\mathbf{f}_{\beta}\|_{\infty}.

Next,

\mathbb{E}\,\|\delta\mathbf{f}_{\beta}\|_{\infty}^{2}\leq\mathbb{E}\,\|\delta\mathbf{f}_{\beta}\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{p_{f}}\mathrm{Var}(\delta f_{\beta,\ell})\leq\frac{p_{f}}{N_{s}}.

By Jensen’s inequality,

\mathbb{E}\,\|\delta\tilde{\bm{y}}_{\beta}\|_{\infty}\leq\bar{w}\,\bigl(\mathbb{E}\,\|\delta\mathbf{f}_{\beta}\|_{\infty}^{2}\bigr)^{1/2}\leq\bar{w}\sqrt{\frac{p_{f}}{N_{s}}}.

Each generated pixel contributes to exactly one sketch bucket in Eq. (
2
), with unit-magnitude sign.
Therefore the contribution of one block to any sketch coordinate is at most
b\,\|\delta\tilde{\bm{y}}_{\beta}\|_{\infty}
, so summing over
B
blocks yields

\mathbb{E}\,|\delta s_{j}|\leq\sum_{\beta=1}^{B}b\,\mathbb{E}\,\|\delta\tilde{\bm{y}}_{\beta}\|_{\infty}\leq d\,\bar{w}\sqrt{\frac{p_{f}}{N_{s}}}.

Requiring expected sketch SNR,

\frac{\|\bm{s}\|_{\infty}}{\mathbb{E}|\delta s_{j}|}\geq\tau_{\min},

then gives the stated sufficient condition on
N_{s}
.
∎

Corollary 1

(Worst-case sufficient shot scaling)
.

Under bounded decoder gain and controlled sketch scale, maintaining a fixed expected sketch SNR under the worst-case accumulation model of Theorem
2
requires

N_{s}=\mathcal{O}(d^{2}p_{f}).

(16)

For the present observable family,
p_{f}{=}n_{q}^{2}+n_{q}
.
Along deployment curves where
n_{q}
is held fixed or increased only modestly relative to
d
, the dominant dependence is therefore
d^{2}
.
This is a conservative sufficient condition, not a claim of tightness or of observed practical scaling.

Proof.

The result follows directly from the sufficient condition in Theorem
2
.
∎

This corollary completes the resource picture. QFAN converts a qubit-count scaling problem into a conservative worst-case shot requirement whose dominant dependence is quadratic in image dimension, with an additional factor set by the measured feature family.
The per-step circuit count stays constant, and the price of larger images is paid in measurement overhead rather than in register size.

The most stringent test is the total deposited energy
E{=}\sum_{j}y_{j}
, a global observable that aggregates over both blocks and is therefore maximally sensitive to inter-block conditioning errors. If the sketch failed to propagate the energy budget, the two blocks would be sampled nearly independently. The total-energy variance would then be the sum of the individual block variances, producing a distribution systematically broader than MC truth.

The MC total-energy distribution (Fig.
6

E{\approx}2.45
(in normalized intensity units) with a standard deviation
\sigma{\approx}0.3
and a moderate positive skew reflecting the stochastic nature of shower development. Both QFAN paths reproduce the width and tail behavior and remain within
{\sim}6\%
of the MC peak position, with both simulator and hardware paths peaking near
E{\approx}2.30
. Quantitatively, the simulator-path Wasserstein-1 distance on this observable is
W_{1}^{(E)}{=}0.01751

W_{1}^{(E)}{=}0.02996

E{=}\sum_{j}y_{j}
aggregates a small coherent per-pixel bias across both blocks into a visible mean shift; they therefore probe a stricter global consistency condition rather than a directly comparable numerical scale. The residual per-pixel bias is within the single-pixel
W_{1}
resolution (Table
3
) but accumulates constructively in the total-energy sum.

This agreement confirms that the sketch conditioning mechanism is functional on both backends: the energy budget consumed by block 1 is successfully encoded in the sketch and read by the circuit when generating block 2. At the same time, the larger hardware value indicates that the global cross-block consistency of the hardware path is somewhat less precise than that of the simulator, even though its single-pixel marginals remain close to MC. A severely degraded sketch would produce a systematically broader total-energy distribution, which is not observed.

XII
Discussion

QFAN demonstrates that the quantum register can be decoupled from image dimension without abandoning the circuit’s role in modeling inter-pixel correlations.
But demonstrating architectural viability at
d{=}12
is not the same as demonstrating benchmark-scale quantum calorimeter generation, and the manuscript should be explicit about that distinction rather than hoping the reader will infer it charitably.

The architecture produces faithful marginals, correlations, and total-energy distributions at
d{=}12
with 3 qubits, 12 shared quantum variational parameters, closed-form blockwise ridge decoding, and a post-hoc residual sampler.
For the chosen observable family, the per-step QPU cost is independent of
d
, shot-noise propagation is bounded in expectation under a conservative worst-case accumulation model, and the decoder-capacity heuristic produces a concrete empirical extrapolation rule consistent with the optimal block size at the demonstration scale.
The hardware execution confirms that the circuit logic transfers to a real device, with the correlation-difference analysis indicating that hardware and simulator residuals have similar structure and magnitude.
An initial extension to
d{=}25
(Appendix
A
) shows no systematic degradation over a longer autoregressive chain.

At present, the most accurate calorimeter surrogate models at benchmark scale remain classical, while quantum and quantum-assisted approaches are still in an architectural proof-of-principle phase.

XIII
Limitations and next steps

Several limitations remain. First, training uses teacher forcing whereas generation runs free, so the present results at
B{=}2
and the initial extension to
B{=}5
do not resolve whether exposure bias will dominate at the much longer autoregressive depths implied by the current heuristic for larger geometries. The shot-noise analysis of Theorem
2
controls measurement noise only and does not bound model-bias accumulation under long rollouts. Second, the observed feature-efficiency advantage over classical random Fourier features is established only at
d{=}12
and only against a simple random-projection baseline, not against an optimized classical encoder. Third, the threshold
\rho_{\min}\approx 1.5
should be read as a practical interpolation heuristic inferred from the present block-size sweep, not as a universal constant. Finally, although the hardware and simulator paths show similar residual structure, matched controls would be needed to isolate hardware-specific effects more decisively. These limitations define the main directions for future work: intermediate-scale free-running validation, stronger classical benchmarks, and systematic characterization of exposure bias as a function of autoregressive depth
B
.

Three observations organize the present resource picture. For the observable family used here, the per-step circuit count is independent of the image size
d
. Under bounded decoder gain and controlled sketch scale, maintaining fixed expected sketch SNR admits a conservative sufficient shot requirement of order
\mathcal{O}(d^{2}p_{f})
. Finally, the empirical decoder-capacity heuristic suggests that, at the present scale, the required sequential depth grows approximately as
d/n_{q}^{2}
.

At
d{=}12
, the architecture reproduces shower distributions on both simulator and IBM hardware, providing a hardware-compatible proof of principle. An initial extension to
d{=}25
with
B{=}5
autoregressive blocks (Appendix
A
) indicates that the method remains stable over a longer chain, although this regime is still modest relative to the rollout depths that would arise at larger image dimensions.

The main limitations are equally clear. The present training setup uses teacher forcing, so the shot-noise analysis controls measurement noise but does not bound model-bias accumulation under long free-running rollouts. The decoder-capacity threshold is inferred from a single-scale sweep and should be interpreted as a practical heuristic rather than a universal constant. The observed feature-efficiency advantage over classical random Fourier features is established only at
d{=}12
and only against a simple random-projection baseline. The next decisive tests are therefore intermediate-scale free-running validation, stronger classical baselines, and direct measurement of exposure-bias growth with autoregressive depth
B
.
