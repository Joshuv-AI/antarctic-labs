Associations of the CHG index combined with obesity indicators with cardiovascular disease in early CKM syndrome using CHARLS data
Authors: Luo Lv, Yuli Guo, Xiaofang Li, Qinghua Han, Haixiong Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The cholesterol–high-density lipoprotein–glucose (CHG) index reflects integrated lipid and glucose metabolism, yet its predictive value for cardiovascular disease (CVD) within the cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) framework remains unclear. This study developed CHG-BMI, CHG-WC, and CHG-WHtR by incorporating obesity metrics and evaluated their associations with CVD risk. Data were derived from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS). Cox models estimated hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Kaplan–Meier and restricted cubic spline (RCS) analyses assessed cumulative incidence and dose–response patterns. Predictive utility was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, Harrell’s C-index, net reclassification improvement (NRI), and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI). During a median follow-up of 7 years, 6,705 participants were included, contributing 42,098 person-years of observation, and 1,262 incident CVD events occurred. Cumulative incidence and incidence rates increased progressively across quartiles of CHG-related indices. In the fully adjusted model, each 1-SD increase in CHG, CHG-BMI, CHG-WC, and CHG-WHtR was associated with 8% (HR = 1.08, 95% CI: 1.02–1.14), 15% (HR = 1.15, 95% CI: 1.09–1.21), 16% (HR = 1.16, 95% CI: 1.09–1.23), and 13% (HR = 1.13, 95% CI: 1.07–1.21) higher risk of CVD, respectively. Compared with the lowest quartile, participants in the highest quartile had higher CVD risks for CHG, CHG-BMI, CHG-WC, and CHG-WHtR, with HRs of 1.23, 1.48, 1.44, and 1.50, respectively. RCS analyses showed a generally linear association for CHG-BMI and nonlinear associations for CHG-WC and CHG-WHtR. CHG-derived indices showed modestly higher discriminatory and reclassification performance than CHG alone, with CHG-WHtR generally showing the most favorable but still limited performance. CHG-derived indices were associated with incident CVD among individuals with early-stage CKM syndrome. These indices may provide supplementary information for cardiovascular risk stratification, but their clinical utility requires further validation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-57748-z.

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) remains the leading cause of mortality and disease burden globally. In China, the burden of CVD is particularly substantial1, with more than 330 million individuals affected and CVD accounting for a large proportion of total deaths2,3. Despite advances in controlling traditional cardiovascular risk factors, the persistent high incidence of events in certain populations underscores the limitations of current risk assessment tools and the urgent need for simple, accessible, and clinically applicable indicators to improve early risk stratification and prevention.

CVD frequently coexists with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), chronic kidney disease (CKD), and obesity, which share overlapping metabolic, renal, and vascular mechanisms4,5. In 2023, the American Heart Association proposed the cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) syndrome framework to characterize the systemic interplay among metabolic risk factors, kidney dysfunction, and CVD6. CKM stages 0–3 represent a critical prevention window before the development of clinically manifest CVD or end-organ failure7,8. Therefore, readily obtainable biomarkers that reflect early metabolic abnormalities may help refine risk stratification among individuals within the early CKM spectrum.

Insulin resistance (IR) is widely recognized as a central pathophysiological mechanism in the development and progression of CVD9. IR contributes to atherosclerosis and cardiovascular events through multiple mechanisms, including endothelial dysfunction, chronic low-grade inflammation, lipid metabolism abnormalities, and oxidative stress9,10. The cholesterol–high-density lipoprotein–glucose (CHG) index, which integrates total cholesterol (TC), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, and fasting glucose, has been proposed as a simple marker of glucose–lipid metabolic dysregulation and IR11. However, CHG primarily reflects metabolic abnormalities and may not fully capture the additional cardiovascular burden associated with excess adiposity and body fat distribution.

Obesity represents a key pathophysiological driver of cardiometabolic disease and contributes to CVD development through IR, adipose tissue dysfunction, lipotoxicity, chronic low-grade inflammation, oxidative stress, and endothelial injury12,13. Body mass index (BMI), waist circumference (WC), and waist-to-height ratio (WHtR) represent different dimensions of adiposity, including general obesity, abdominal obesity, and height-adjusted central fat distribution, respectively. These obesity-related indicators may provide information complementary to CHG by capturing adiposity burden and fat distribution. Accordingly, incorporating BMI, WC, or WHtR into the CHG index may better characterize the combined burden of glucose–lipid dysregulation and adiposity than CHG alone. Such a composite metabolic–adiposity profile may be particularly useful for risk stratification in CKM stages 0–3, where overt CVD has not yet developed but heterogeneous metabolic and subclinical vascular abnormalities may already exist.

Based on this rationale, the present study constructed three CHG-derived composite indices—CHG-BMI, CHG-WC, and CHG-WHtR—by combining the CHG index with commonly used obesity indicators. Leveraging data from the nationally representative China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), we evaluated the prospective associations of these indices with incident CVD among individuals at CKM stages 0–3. This study aimed to provide epidemiological evidence for the potential value of CHG-derived composite indices as practical tools for identifying individuals at elevated cardiovascular risk within the early CKM syndrome.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This study was based on data from the CHARLS, a nationally representative prospective cohort initiated by Peking University. CHARLS was designed to systematically collect information on socioeconomic status, lifestyle, health conditions, and biological measures among Chinese adults aged 45 years and older. The detailed design and data collection procedures of CHARLS have been described elsewhere14. Baseline recruitment was conducted in 2011–2012 using a multistage, stratified, probability-proportional-to-size sampling strategy. Approximately 17,000 individuals (about 10,000 households) from 450 villages/communities across 150 counties/districts in 28 provinces were enrolled, with follow-up surveys carried out every 2–3 years. For the present analysis, we included data from four waves (2011, 2013, 2015, and 2018). Ethical approval was granted by the Biomedical Ethics Review Committee of Peking University (IRB00001052-11015), and all participants provided written informed consent. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) participants younger than 45 years; (2) those with a history of CVD at baseline (Wave 1) or prior to baseline; (3) individuals with insufficient follow-up duration (< 2 years); (4) individuals with missing or abnormal values for exposure variables; and (5) those lacking key variables required for CKM syndrome classification. Both lipid and glucose measurements were obtained from fasting venous blood samples collected during the 2011 baseline survey. Definition of CKM syndrome stages 0–3

CKM stages 0–3 were classified according to the 2023 American Heart Association scientific statement and adapted based on variables available in CHARLS. Because stage 4 represents clinically manifest CVD and incident CVD was the primary outcome of this study, participants with clinical CVD at baseline were excluded, and only CKM stages 0–3 were included. Briefly, stage 0 was defined as the absence of cardiovascular, kidney, or metabolic risk factors, including normal BMI, waist circumference, glycemic status, blood pressure, lipid profile, and kidney function, with no evidence of subclinical or clinical CVD. Stage 1 was defined as the presence of early adiposity- or glucose-related metabolic risk, including overweight/obesity, abdominal obesity, or prediabetes, while maintaining normal blood pressure, triglyceride levels, kidney function, and no evidence of subclinical or clinical CVD. Stage 2 was defined as the presence of established metabolic or kidney abnormalities, including hypertriglyceridemia, hypertension, diabetes, metabolic syndrome, or eGFR of 30–60 mL/min/1.73 m2 and/or self-reported CKD, in the absence of subclinical or clinical CVD. Stage 3 was defined as the presence of eGFR < 30 mL/min/1.73 m2 or subclinical CVD, together with at least one cardiometabolic or kidney risk condition and no clinical CVD. Subclinical CVD was defined as very-high-risk CKD with eGFR < 30 mL/min/1.73 m2 or elevated 10-year cardiovascular risk estimated using the Framingham risk score. The sex-specific Framingham risk score algorithms are provided in Supplementary Tables S1 and S2. Detailed CKM stage classification criteria and definitions of related metabolic, kidney, and cardiovascular conditions are provided in Supplementary Tables S3 and S4.

Assessment of outcome

The primary outcome of this study was the occurrence of CVD events. CVD was ascertained based on self-reported information from the CHARLS follow-up questionnaires, in which participants were asked: “Have you ever been diagnosed by a doctor with a heart disease (including myocardial infarction, coronary heart disease, angina, congestive heart failure, or other heart diseases) or stroke?” Participants who responded “yes” were classified as having experienced a CVD event. In this study, CVD was defined as self-reported physician-diagnosed heart disease or stroke, consistent with previous studies using CHARLS data15. Event time was determined according to the first follow-up wave at which CVD was reported. Specifically, CVD status was assessed at the 2013, 2015, and 2018 waves; participants who first reported physician-diagnosed heart disease or stroke at these waves were assigned follow-up times of 2, 4, and 7 years from the 2011 baseline, respectively. Participants who did not report CVD during follow-up were censored at the last wave in which they were confirmed to be free of CVD. Given the self-reported nature of CVD ascertainment, potential outcome misclassification cannot be completely excluded.

Covariates

Covariates were selected based on prior literature and potential confounders of CVD risk, including demographic characteristics, lifestyle behaviors, clinical measurements, laboratory biomarkers, medical history, and medication use. Demographic and socioeconomic variables included age (continuous), sex (male/female), education (Below high school/High school or above), marital status (married/other), and residence (urban/rural). Lifestyle behaviors included smoking status (never, former, current) and drinking status (never, former, current). Anthropometric measurements, including height, weight, and waist circumference (WC), were obtained by trained healthcare professionals following standardized protocols. In addition, laboratory biomarkers were measured from fasting venous blood samples collected from all participants after an overnight fast of at least 8 hours16. These indicators included blood lipids [triglycerides, TC, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C)], fasting blood glucose (FBG), glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), inflammatory marker C-reactive protein (CRP), and renal function parameters [blood urea nitrogen (BUN), serum creatinine (SCr), and uric acid (UA)]. Medical history variables included hypertension, diabetes and cancer. Hypertension was defined as SBP ≥ 130 mmHg, DBP ≥ 80 mmHg, a prior diagnosis of hypertension, or the use of antihypertensive medications17. Diabetes was defined as FBG ≥ 126 mg/dL, HbA1c ≥ 6.5%, self-reported physician diagnosis, or use of antidiabetic medications18. Cancer was defined as a physician-diagnosed malignancy or a history of cancer treatment. Medication use included antihypertensive, antidiabetic, and lipid-lowering medications. All measurements were obtained by trained interviewers or central laboratories following standardized protocols, and missing data were minimal (≤ 1.3% for all variables) (Supplementary Table S5).

Statistical analysis

We performed statistical analyses to evaluate the associations between the CHG index and its derived composite indices with incident CVD in early-stage CKM. Continuous variables were presented as means ± standard deviations (SD) or medians with interquartile ranges (IQR), and compared between groups using independent sample t tests or Mann–Whitney U tests, as appropriate. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies (percentages) and compared using chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests. To explore the associations and trends between exposures and CVD risk, all CHG-derived indices—CHG, CHG-BMI, CHG-WC, and CHG-WHtR—were categorized into quartiles (Q1–Q4). Cumulative incidence of CVD was visualized with Kaplan–Meier survival curves. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression models were used to assess potential non-linear relationships between each index and incident CVD. Cox proportional hazards models were employed to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the association between CHG indices and CVD risk. Three hierarchical models were constructed: Model 1 (unadjusted): included only the exposure variable. Model 2 (minimally adjusted): additionally adjusted for demographic and lifestyle factors, including age, sex, education level, location, marital status, smoking, and alcohol consumption. Model 3 (fully adjusted): further adjusted for clinical and biochemical covariates, including SBP, DBP, CRP, BUN, SCr, UA, cancer, antihypertensive medication, antidiabetic medication, and lipid-lowering medication. The primary analyses used unweighted Cox models because the objective was to estimate exposure–outcome associations within the eligible analytic cohort rather than nationally representative absolute risks. Sampling weights, clustering, and stratification were not incorporated because the cohort was restricted by study-specific eligibility criteria and had incomplete weight-related information.

RCS models with four knots, fitted using the rcssci R package, were used to assess the dose-response associations between CHG-related indices and incident CVD in the overall early-stage CKM population and within CKM stages 2 and 3. Three-knot RCS models were additionally performed as supplementary analyses. To evaluate the predictive performance of CHG and its derived indices for incident cardiovascular disease, we employed time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and Harrell’s C-index at 2, 4, and 7 years to assess their discriminative ability. Incremental predictive performance was further assessed at 7 years using category-free continuous net reclassification improvement (NRI) and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI). The basic/reference model was the fully adjusted Model 3 without CHG-related indices, and the extended models were constructed by separately adding CHG, CHG-BMI, CHG-WC, or CHG-WHtR to the basic model. Censoring was accounted for using follow-up time and event status in the survival-based prediction analyses. Subgroup analyses were performed to investigate potential effect modifications across baseline characteristics, including age group, sex, smoking status, alcohol consumption, and CKM stage. Sensitivity analyses were performed using multiple imputation with five replications via the Markov chain Monte Carlo method to handle missing data. Cox models were fitted in each imputed dataset, and the results were pooled using Rubin’s rules to obtain final estimates for the associations between CHG-derived indices and CVD risk. Additionally, logistic regression models were applied in the imputed datasets to assess the associations with incident CVD as a binary outcome. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.0; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Two-sided P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Table 1 summarizes the baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of participants stratified by CKM syndrome stages (0 to 3). A total of 6,705 eligible participants were included in this study, with females accounting for 52.7% and a mean age of 58.6 ± 9.2 years. Compared with those in CKM stage 0, individuals in stage 3 were significantly older, had a higher proportion of males, and reported greater prevalence of smoking and alcohol consumption. Regarding metabolic comorbidities, the prevalence of hypertension, and diabetes increased markedly with advancing CKM stages. Clinically, participants in higher CKM stages exhibited progressively elevated levels of SBP, DBP, BMI, WC, WHtR, FBG, HbA1c, TC, TG, UA, SCr, BUN, and CRP, whereas levels of HDL-C declined. Additionally, the CHG index and its composite derivatives incorporating obesity measures (CHG-BMI, CHG-WC, and CHG-WHtR) showed a consistent upward trend across CKM stages. Relationship between CHG and CHG-derived indexes with CVD incidence among people with CKM stage 0–3

Supplementary Table S7 shows that both cumulative incidence and incidence rates of incident CVD increased progressively across quartiles of CHG-related indices and across CKM stages. During a median follow-up of 7 years, 1,262 incident CVD events occurred among 6,705 participants, contributing 42,098 person-years of observation. The overall incidence rate was 29.98 per 1,000 person-years. Both cumulative incidence and incidence rates of CVD increased progressively across quartiles of CHG, CHG-BMI, CHG-WC, and CHG-WHtR, as well as across CKM stages, with significant increasing trends observed for all comparisons (all P for trend < 0.001).

In the Kaplan–Meier survival analysis (Supplementary Figure S1), the cumulative incidence of CVD increased progressively across quartiles of CHG and its three derived composite indices (CHG-BMI, CHG-WC, and CHG-WHtR), with statistically significant differences observed among the groups (Log-rank P < 0.0001 for all). Multivariable Cox proportional hazards models (Table 2) further confirmed the positive associations between CHG-related indices and CVD risk. In the fully adjusted model (Model 3), each one standard deviation increment in CHG, CHG-BMI, CHG-WC, and CHG-WHtR was associated with an 8% (hazard ratio [HR] = 1.08, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.02–1.14), 15% (HR = 1.15, 95% CI: 1.09–1.21), 16% (HR = 1.16, 95% CI: 1.09–1.23), and 13% (HR = 1.13, 95% CI: 1.07–1.21) higher risk of CVD, respectively. When categorized into quartiles, a clear dose–response relationship was also observed. Compared with the lowest quartile (Q1), participants in Q4 had significantly higher risks of CVD: HR = 1.23 (95% CI: 1.04–1.46) for CHG, HR = 1.48 (95% CI: 1.24–1.78) for CHG-BMI, HR = 1.44 (95% CI: 1.21–1.71) for CHG-WC, and HR = 1.50 (95% CI: 1.25–1.80) for CHG-WHtR. Among these, CHG-WHtR showed the greatest risk elevation in the quartile analysis. The P-values for trend were statistically significant for all indices (P for trend = 0.004 for CHG and < 0.001 for CHG-BMI, CHG-WC, and CHG-WHtR), further supporting a graded dose-response association between CHG-related indices and CVD risk. Predictive performance comparison of CHG and CHG-derived indices for CVD

Time-dependent ROC and Harrell’s C-index were used to evaluate model discrimination (Supplementary Figure S4). Across the 2-, 4-, and 7-year follow-up periods, CHG-derived indices showed modestly higher AUC and C-index values than the original CHG, although the absolute discriminatory performance remained limited. Among them, CHG-WHtR generally showed the most favorable, but still modest, predictive performance. Supplementary Table S9 details the time-dependent predictive ability of CHG and its derivatives, suggesting incremental rather than substantial improvement in discrimination by the derived indices. Supplementary Table S10 shows that CHG- and triglyceride-glucose (TyG)-related indices yielded broadly comparable predictive performance, as reflected by similar values of receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) and Harrell’s C-index. Additionally, Table 3 evaluates the incremental predictive value of these indices over the basic model. While CHG alone showed only modest, non-significant improvements in NRI (0.021, P = 0.458) and IDI (0.001, P = 0.286), the CHG-derived indices showed statistically significant but small improvements in risk reclassification and discrimination. Specifically, CHG-BMI, CHG-WC, and CHG-WHtR achieved NRI values of 0.057 (P = 0.007), 0.077 (P < 0.001), and 0.080 (P < 0.001), and IDI values of 0.003 (P = 0.018), 0.005 (P = 0.006), and 0.006 (P = 0.004), respectively. Discussion

Utilizing data from the nationally representative CHARLS cohort, this study evaluated the prospective associations between three CHG-derived composite indices—CHG-BMI, CHG-WC, and CHG-WHtR—and the risk of incident CVD among individuals at CKM stages 0–3. Higher levels of these composite indices were associated with increased CVD risk after adjustment for demographic, clinical, and metabolic covariates. Compared with the original CHG index, all three derived indices showed relatively stronger associations and modest improvements in risk discrimination and reclassification, with CHG-WHtR generally demonstrating the most favorable performance among the evaluated CHG-related indices.

The CHG index has been proposed as a simple metabolic indicator integrating TC, HDL-C, and fasting glucose, thereby reflecting glucose–lipid metabolic dysregulation and insulin resistance-related abnormalities11. Previous evidence suggests that CHG is associated with metabolic disorders and cardiovascular outcomes, supporting its potential relevance as an accessible cardiometabolic risk marker11,19,20. In the present study, CHG was independently associated with incident CVD, indicating that the glucose–cholesterol metabolic burden captured by CHG may be relevant to cardiovascular risk assessment. More broadly, previous studies have shown that routinely available metabolic and adiposity-related indices, including TyG, TyG-BMI, AIP, and TG/HDL-C, may contribute to cardiovascular risk prediction and prognostic stratification21–23. However, although CHG captures metabolic abnormalities related to glucose and cholesterol, it does not directly reflect adiposity burden or body fat distribution. By incorporating BMI, WC, or WHtR, the CHG-derived indices further integrate anthropometric information related to general obesity, abdominal obesity, and height-adjusted central adiposity. Therefore, the stronger associations and more favorable predictive performance of CHG-derived indices compared with CHG alone may partly reflect the contribution of the obesity-related components, because BMI, WC, and WHtR are themselves established cardiometabolic risk markers. However, additional comparisons with the corresponding obesity indicators suggested that the composite indices provided small incremental information beyond adiposity measures alone, particularly for CHG-WC and CHG-WHtR, whereas the added value of CHG-BMI over BMI was less evident. Among them, CHG-WHtR showed the most favorable performance, possibly because it integrates glucose–cholesterol dysregulation with height-adjusted central adiposity, which may better reflect obesity-related cardiometabolic risk than BMI or WC alone. This observation is consistent with previous evidence supporting WHtR as a useful anthropometric marker for cardiovascular and metabolic risk assessment24. Nevertheless, the observed improvement in discrimination was incremental rather than large, indicating that these indices may be more appropriately interpreted as supplementary markers for risk stratification rather than independent prediction models. Although NRI and IDI analyses indicated that CHG-derived indices provided additional reclassification information compared with CHG alone and, for CHG-WC and CHG-WHtR, modest incremental information beyond the corresponding obesity indicators, further studies are needed to determine whether this incremental improvement has practical value when applied to independent populations and established cardiovascular risk prediction models.

The underlying mechanisms through which CHG and its derived indices are associated with CVD risk remain incompletely understood. Several interrelated metabolic pathways may plausibly contribute to these associations. First, IR may link elevated CHG levels with vascular dysfunction. Impaired insulin signaling can suppress the PI3K-Akt pathway while relatively activating the MAPK pathway, thereby reducing endothelial nitric oxide synthesis and promoting vasoconstrictive and proliferative signaling25. These alterations may contribute to endothelial dysfunction, arterial remodeling, and atherosclerotic initiation26,27. Elevated CHG levels, characterized by increased TC and reduced HDL-C, indicate dyslipidemia and excessive accumulation of free fatty acids (FFAs), which exacerbate oxidative stress and inflammation within vascular tissues28,29. In parallel, central adiposity, as reflected by WC and WHtR, may amplify these adverse metabolic effects through increased lipotoxicity and impaired insulin sensitivity12,30,31. The combination of CHG with WC or WHtR may therefore capture the joint burden of metabolic dysregulation and central adiposity, which may partly explain the nonlinear associations observed for CHG-WC and CHG-WHtR.

These findings may have particular relevance within the early CKM spectrum. CKM stages 0–3 include individuals who have not yet developed clinically manifest CVD but may already differ substantially in metabolic dysfunction, adiposity burden, and subclinical vascular risk. In this context, CHG-derived composite indices provide a continuous measure of metabolic–adiposity burden that may complement categorical CKM staging. The observed associations between higher CHG-derived indices and incident CVD suggest that combined metabolic–anthropometric measures may help identify individuals with higher cardiovascular risk during this preclinical prevention window.

Several strengths merit consideration. This study used a large, nationally representative longitudinal cohort with standardized data collection and long-term follow-up. It evaluated multiple CHG-derived indices, applied Cox regression, RCS analyses, subgroup analyses, sensitivity analyses, and incremental predictive metrics, thereby providing a comprehensive epidemiological assessment of the associations between CHG-related indices and incident CVD. In addition, these indices are derived from routine laboratory and anthropometric measures, supporting their feasibility for future population-based risk assessment research.

Nonetheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, incident CVD was defined as a broad composite outcome of self-reported physician-diagnosed heart disease or stroke, which may have introduced clinical heterogeneity, recall bias, and outcome misclassification. Because medical record linkage or formal event adjudication was unavailable in CHARLS, the degree of misclassification could not be directly quantified; such misclassification may have biased the observed HRs toward or away from the null and reduced the precision of effect estimates. Second, exposure variables were measured only at baseline, limiting our ability to assess longitudinal changes in metabolic status or adiposity. Third, although multiple covariates were adjusted for, residual confounding cannot be fully excluded because of the observational design. Fourth, the predictive performance of CHG-derived indices was assessed within a single cohort without external validation; therefore, their generalizability and clinical applicability require confirmation in independent populations. Finally, as the CHARLS population consists primarily of middle-aged and older Chinese adults, extrapolation of these results to other age groups or ethnic populations should be undertaken with caution.

Conclusion

In this nationally representative cohort study, CHG-BMI, CHG-WC, and CHG-WHtR were independently associated with incident CVD among individuals at CKM stages 0–3. Compared with CHG alone, these composite indices showed modestly improved risk-stratification performance, with CHG-WHtR generally performing best. These findings suggest that integrating glucose–lipid metabolic information with obesity-related indicators may provide additional epidemiological information for early CVD risk assessment.
