Central Message

Time-resolved 4DCT identified silent cerebral hypoperfusion from supra-aortic true-lumen compression and supported brachiocephalic/right carotid reconstruction, with postoperative improvement.

Chronic aortic dissection involving supra-aortic branches may cause clinically silent but hemodynamically significant cerebral hypoperfusion.1,2 We report a case in which time-resolved 4-dimensional computed tomography (4DCT) identified occult hypoperfusion, supported surgical planning, and confirmed postoperative improvement.

Case Description

This report was approved by the Juntendo University Ethics Committee (JHS25-035; January 6, 2026); written informed consent for publication was obtained from the patient. A 72-year-old woman with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and interstitial lung disease was incidentally found to have a 53-mm chronic dissecting aortic aneurysm (aortic size index, 3.53 cm/m2), for which surgical repair was indicated. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) demonstrated an aortic dissection with an entry at the level of the left subclavian artery, primarily involving the brachiocephalic artery and extending into the right common carotid artery (Figure 1). The false lumen extended continuously from the brachiocephalic artery into the right common carotid artery, resulting in severe true-lumen compression throughout both vessels. Surgical Technique

The operation was performed via median sternotomy. Cardiopulmonary bypass was established via ascending aortic cannulation, followed by systemic cooling (tympanic 24 °C, bladder 28 °C) and circulatory arrest. Intraoperative findings confirmed chronic dissection with an organized false-lumen thrombus and a rigid intimal flap. The brachiocephalic artery was transected approximately 5 mm proximal to its bifurcation to secure an adequate distal anastomotic margin. The intimal flap was then resected along the right common carotid artery as distally as possible. Reconstruction of the brachiocephalic bifurcation was subsequently performed using a double-barrel distal anastomosis incorporating the right common carotid and right subclavian arteries. A 14-mm vascular graft was then anastomosed to the brachiocephalic bifurcation. Selective antegrade cerebral perfusion was then established to all supra-aortic branches. Distal anastomosis was performed at the left subclavian artery using a 26-mm 4-branched graft. Lower-body perfusion was resumed via a graft side branch after 35 minutes of lower-body circulatory arrest. Complete cerebral circulatory arrest, retrograde cerebral perfusion, and antegrade cerebral perfusion times were 2, 6, and 84 minutes, respectively. Crossclamp and cardiopulmonary bypass times were 78 and 142 minutes, respectively. The postoperative course was uneventful, and the patient was discharged on postoperative day 11. Early postoperative noncontrast CT of the chest showed no major complications, including graft kinking, perigraft fluid collection, or pericardial effusion. Because postoperative acute kidney injury developed, contrast-enhanced CT of the chest was not performed. Discussion

Clinically silent cerebral hypoperfusion may occur in chronic aortic arch dissection involving the supra-aortic branches, and symptom-based assessment may underestimate its hemodynamic significance.1,2 Chronic hypoperfusion has been implicated in vascular cognitive impairment, underscoring the importance of detecting occult ischemia.2

In this case, time-resolved 4DCT demonstrated the hemodynamic significance of the supra-aortic branch lesions by revealing impaired right cerebral perfusion. This physiologic insight suggested that isolated brachiocephalic reconstruction might be insufficient and strengthened the rationale for additional intervention to the dissected right common carotid artery, including extended flap resection. Postoperative imaging demonstrated symmetric supra-aortic opacification, improved right hemispheric perfusion, and resolution of retrograde right vertebral flow.

Limitations include the single-case design, the absence of formal neurocognitive testing despite no obvious focal neurologic deficits on routine clinical examination, and practical limitations of time-resolved 4DCT, including additional radiation exposure, longer acquisition and postprocessing requirements, protocol-dependent contrast burden, limited availability, and potentially greater costs.3
