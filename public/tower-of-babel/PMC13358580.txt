Impact of non-pharmacological adjuvant interventions on adult asthma patients: a systematic review and network meta-analysis
Authors: Rui Mao, Yujia Liu, Yuying Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAdult asthma is a prevalent chronic respiratory disease. Non-pharmacological adjuvant interventions show promise in improving outcomes. This network meta-analysis (NMA) comprehensively evaluated the comparative efficacy of such interventions.MethodsDatabases were searched from inception until August 2025 for randomized controlled trials (RCTs) on non-pharmacological interventions for adult asthma. Two reviewers independently performed study screening, data extraction, and risk-of-bias assessment using the revised tool for risk of bias in randomized trials (RoB 2). NMA was conducted using R 4.4.1 and Stata 17.0, with publication bias assessed via funnel plots. The quality of evidence was evaluated utilizing the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) tool.ResultsTotally 37 RCTs involving 4,693 participants were included, encompassing 5 distinct intervention categories. Emphases were placed on six key asthma outcomes, namely asthma quality of life questionnaire (AQLQ), asthma control test (ACT), forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1), forced vital capacity (FVC), peak expiratory flow (PEF), and FEV1/FVC. Based on surface under the cumulative ranking curve (SUCRA) results and pairwise comparisons, breathing training (BT), mind-body therapy (MBT), and biopsychosocial approach (BA) showed the highest probability of improving ACT, FEV1/FVC and AQLQ, and FVC, FEV1 and PEF scores, respectively. Meta-regression analysis demonstrated that follow-up duration had no significant effect on each outcome. The funnel plot indicated no risk of potential publication bias. Based on the GRADE system for quality of evidence assessment, the overall evidence level of the included studies was moderate.ConclusionsThe five non-pharmacological adjuvant interventions for adult asthma exhibited consistent effectiveness in mitigating asthma symptoms, improving living quality, and enhancing lung function. NMA showed that MBT, BA, and BT had only complementary relative efficacy in improving prognosis in asthma patients, with no clear differences in clinical superiority. Given the low GRADE certainty, these results should be interpreted with caution. Due to sparse network connections for some interventions and the fact that most comparisons relied on indirect evidence, the SUCRA results should be considered exploratory findings and are insufficient to offer definitive clinical recommendations. More high-quality studies are still needed to further validate the clinical application value and long-term efficacy of various interventions.

Highlight box

Key findings

• This study is the first to comprehensively evaluate the divergent effects of non‑pharmacological adjuvant interventions for adult asthma using network meta-analysis (NMA). Breathing training (BT) showed the highest probability of improving asthma control test scores; mind-body therapy (MBT) of improving forced expiratory volume in 1 second/forced vital capacity (FEV1/FVC) ratio and asthma quality of life questionnaire scores; and the biopsychosocial approach (BA) of improving FVC, FEV1, and peak expiratory flow scores. No publication bias was detected. These findings highlight the exploratory complementary roles of MBT, BA, and BT, providing exploratory evidence to support future research rather than direct clinical decision‑making. Given the heterogeneity and limited certainty of the current evidence, no immediate changes to clinical practice are indicated. Future high‑quality studies are needed to verify these exploratory results and support the translation of evidence‑based interventions into sustainable strategies to improve asthma control, quality of life, and lung function.

What is known and what is new?

• Non-pharmacological interventions benefit asthma management, but head-to-head efficacy comparisons remain limited.

• This network meta-analysis is the first to systematically compare five non‑pharmacological interventions across six core asthma outcomes, clarifying their distinct and complementary advantages for symptom control, quality of life, and lung function.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Findings provide exploratory comparative evidence to support hypothesis generation and future research on personalized non-pharmacological strategies for asthma.

• No immediate changes to clinical guidelines or practice are warranted. Further high-quality, head-to-head randomized controlled trials with long-term follow-up are required to confirm efficacy, safety, and clinical relevance.

Introduction

Asthma represents a chronic respiratory disease featuring airway inflammation, altered lung function, and heightened airway responsiveness (1-3). Epidemiological data indicate that asthma represents a serious global health issue, affecting all age groups, with a worldwide adult prevalence ranging from 1% to 21% (4). Currently, approximately 300 million people worldwide are suffering from asthma. This disease not only impairs patients' lung function and quality of life but also imposes a heavy financial burden on families and healthcare systems (5,6). As a populous country, China also has a large number of people affected by asthma, facing enormous challenges in disease prevention, control, and management (7). More concerning, the mortality rate of asthma has remained persistently high, and there are approximately 436,000 deaths attributable to asthma-related conditions globally each year. This further highlights that asthma is a major public health issue requiring focused attention worldwide (5). The pathogenesis of asthma is complex. Although recent studies have elucidated key regulatory pathways, certain aspects remain incompletely understood. It is currently widely recognized that the synergistic imbalance between innate and adaptive immunity, dysregulated cytokine networks, and the pivotal mediating role of airway epithelial cells constitute the dominant mechanisms driving the occurrence and progress of the disease (8).

In the management of adult asthma, pharmacotherapy serves as the foundational approach. Single-inhaler maintenance and reliever therapy (SMART) with inhaled corticosteroids combined with long-acting beta2-agonists (LABA) enables both control and rapid relief (9). Although pharmacotherapy can effectively manage asthma in most cases, it has limitations. Concerns regarding long-term safety, suboptimal patient adherence, and steroid resistance in some patients all contribute to the complexity of disease management (10-12). While recently introduced biologics have expanded treatment options, their long-term effectiveness and safety profiles are not yet fully established (13,14). Consequently, exploring scalable and cost-effective complementary therapies is of significant importance for improving clinical outcomes and patient experience. Asthma is often accompanied by declines in lung function and exercise (EX) capacity. Traditional management focuses primarily on controlling symptoms and reducing acute exacerbations. However, issues encompassing inadequate symptom control, recurrent attacks, and reduced living quality persist (15). Such issues have driven increased interest in examining safe and non-pharmacological adjuvant interventions. This exploration represents a promising research direction. Based on high-quality clinical research evidence and synthesized findings from review studies in recent years, we selected five representative and widely applied non-pharmacological adjunctive interventions for asthma, including EX, breathing training (BT), biopsychosocial approach (BA), mind-body therapy (MBT), and mobile health (mHealth). Multiple existing evidence-based studies have confirmed that the above five interventions can effectively improve prognosis in asthma patients (16-19). Non-pharmacological adjuvant interventions offer distinct clinical advantages. Specifically, they can reduce patient reliance on medication, enhance living quality, and ameliorate the disease by mitigating systemic and airway inflammation, thereby providing crucial support for the comprehensive management of asthma (20).

While non-pharmacological adjuvant interventions for adult asthma have been extensively investigated, comparative evaluations and meta-analyses integrating various categories of interventions like MBT, BT, BA, EX, and mHealth remain scarce. This scarcity hinders the evidence-based prioritization of treatment strategies. A systematic review and network meta-analysis (NMA) was implemented to fill these gaps (21). This approach facilitates the ranking of treatment options by effectiveness and helps identify optimal therapeutic options by pooling all accessible comparison information. The NMA was employed to comprehensively examine the relative efficacy of distinct non-pharmacological adjuvant interventions for adult asthma. This research intended to examine the influence of distinct non-pharmacological adjuvant interventions on patients’ living quality, symptom control, and lung function, thus offering evidence for clinical practice. We present this article in accordance with PRISMA-NMA reporting checklist (22) (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-0757/rc).

Methods

The proposal was enrolled in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) (CRD420251138295).

Retrieval strategy

PubMed, Embase, the Cochrane Library, Web of Science, China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), Chongqing VIP Database (VIP), Chinese Biomedical Literature Service System (SinoMed), and Wanfang Database were retrieved from creation until August 2025 to identify randomized controlled trials (RCTs) investigating the impact of distinct non-pharmacological adjuvant interventions on the living quality and symptoms of individuals with asthma. The medical subject headings and free-text terms were combined for search and the strategies were adapted to the specific features of individuals databases. The retrieval strategies are detailed in Table S1.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria for the current research were developed in strict adherence to the population, intervention, comparator, outcomes, and study design (PICOS) framework.

Studies satisfying conditions below were included: (I) population: individuals with asthma, aged >18 years. (II) Intervention: any single non-pharmacological adjuvant intervention, encompassing EX (e.g., moderate-intensity exercise, aerobic exercise, physical training), BT (e.g., inspiratory/expiratory muscle training, abdominal breathing, diaphragmatic breathing, pursed-lip breathing), BA (e.g., cognitive-behavioral intervention, mindfulness-based stress reduction, group acceptance and commitment therapy), MBT (e.g., yoga, progressive relaxation therapy), and mHealth (e.g., management via smartphone applications and WeChat mini-programs). (III) Comparator: usual care (UC), usual treatment (UT), or placebo (AP). (IV) Outcomes: asthma quality of life questionnaire (AQLQ), asthma control test (ACT), forced vital capacity (FVC), forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1), peak expiratory flow (PEF), and FEV1/FVC. (V) Study design: RCTs.

Exclusion criteria were outlined below: (I) to ensure that each intervention category was methodologically independent and clear in attribution of effects, and met the assumptions of network meta-analysis, we excluded combined interventions (e.g., EX combined with BT, or BT combined with BA); (II) research proposals, conference abstracts, case reports, reviews, meta-analyses, or duplicate publications; (III) articles without relevant outcomes; (IV) publications with relevant outcome data unextractable.

Study selection and data collection

EndNote 21 was utilized to identify and eliminate duplicate records. Subsequently, titles and abstracts were initially filtered independently by two assessors in adherence to pre-determined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Full contents of potentially eligible articles were checked in detail. Any divergences between the two assessors were addressed by means of consultation with a third author or by consensus among multiple authors. Two authors collected key data from every individual included RCT, encompassing the first author, publication year, intervention type, sample size, patient characteristics, study design, intervention details, control group, follow-up duration, and post-intervention outcome data (mean and standard deviation). When the mean and standard deviation were not directly presented in the full-text articles, they were estimated from available data encompassing the median and range, interquartile range (IQR), and/or the 95% confidence interval (CI) (23).

Risk of bias assessment of enrolled studies

Two investigators examined the risk of bias of enrolled studies utilizing the revised tool for risk of bias in randomized trials (RoB 2) for RCTs, in adherence to the Cochrane Handbook (24). The evaluation results were cross-validated, and any divergences were addressed by means of discussion with a third investigator. The assessment covered five fields: randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of the outcome, and selection of the reported result. The risk of bias for every individual field was identified as “low risk”, “some concerns”, or “high risk”.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses for the NMA were implemented within a Bayesian framework utilizing R 4.4.1 and Stata 17.0. The analysis in R utilized the meta (version 8.2-1), metafor (version 4.8-0), and gemtc (version 1.1-0) packages. A random-effects model was applied for the NMA to account for heterogeneity between studies. As all outcomes were continuous variables, the standardized mean difference (SMD) along with its 95% CI was utilized as the effect measure. The ranking of interventions was implemented with the surface under the cumulative ranking curve (SUCRA). A greater SUCRA indicated a higher probability of the intervention being the most effective (25). Heterogeneity across articles was evaluated utilizing I2 statistic. Established cutoffs were adopted: low (I2=0–49%), moderate (I2=50–74%), and high (I2=75–100%) heterogeneity (26,27). The evidence network was presented with nodes representing the interventions (node size proportional to the sample size) and edges standing for direct comparisons between interventions (line thickness indicating the quantity of head-to-head RCTs). When closed cycles were detected in the network, consistency was ascertained utilizing the node-splitting method. An inconsistency model was applied when inconsistency was detected (28). Model convergence was examined leveraging the potential scale reduction factor (PSRF). Publication bias for primary outcomes was analyzed utilizing Egger’s linear regression test, and funnel plots were produced (29). The Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach for meta-analysis was used to assess the certainty of evidence for the outcomes.

Results

Process and results of literature retrieval

Totally 93,722 potentially relevant records were initially identified. After 33,619 duplicate records were eliminated, 152 articles were identified for reviewing full contents according to their titles and abstracts. Of these, 94 articles were potentially eligible for data extraction, but 57 were subsequently excluded after a detailed assessment against the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Ultimately, 37 RCTs were incorporated. The 37 trials covered BT (30-34), mHealth (35-46), BA (47-54), EX (55-61) and MBT (62-66). were incorporated. The process and results of article screening are detailed in Figure 1.

Basic characteristics of enrolled studies

Totally 37 eligible RCTs involving 4,693 participants were incorporated. The mean age across individual participants varied between 23.59 and 73.17 years, representing a span of 49.58 years. The individuals in control groups received UC, UT, or AP. In contrast, participants in intervention groups received non-pharmacological adjuvant therapy in addition to treatments received by control groups. The basic characteristics of enrolled studies are detailed in Table S2.

The enrolled studies investigated five categories of non-pharmacological adjuvant interventions: EX (n=7), BT (n=5), MBT (n=5), BA (n=8), and mHealth (n=12). The treatment duration differed among studies, varying between 1 and 12 months. Six clinically related endpoints were analyzed: AQLQ, ACT, FVC, FEV1, PEF, and FEV1/FVC.

Results of risk of bias assessment

The methodological quality of the 37 included studies was evaluated utilizing the RoB 2. Among the 37 RCTs, three were rated as having a low risk of bias. The main fields raising some concerns about risk of bias were the randomization process (28 studies), the measurement of the outcome (25 studies), and the selection of the reported result (20 studies). However, overall, no studies were found to have a high risk of bias. The results of the risk of bias assessment are detailed in Figure 2.

NMA

Consistency test

In the current research, the evidence network of RCTs was established. The node size and the thickness of connecting lines were in direct proportion to the total number of enrolled individuals assigned to every individual intervention and the number of studies investigating each direct comparison, respectively. When closed cycles were detected in the network for the measurement of the outcome, inconsistency tests were required. The aggregate inconsistency tests for the five categories of interventions all revealed P>0.05 (Figure S1). Thus, a consistency model was adopted for all statistical analyses. The complete forest plot for the NMA is presented in Figure S2.

Differences in effects among distinct interventions

AQLQ

For this outcome, 15 studies involving 1,311 individuals reported results of AQLQ for five non-pharmacological adjuvant interventions. The evidence network is shown in Figure 3A. The model showed good convergence with a PSRF of 1 (Figure S3A). On the basis of SUCRA values, MBT maintained the highest odds of becoming the most effective intervention for enhancing living quality in individuals with asthma (97.51%), followed by BT (69.92%), EX (56.62%), BA (41.79%), and mHealth (28.10%) (Figure S3B). Furthermore, in comparison with UT and UC in control groups (SMD =2.57, 95% CI: 1.19, 3.94), mHealth (SMD = 2.24, 95% CI: 0.69, 3.80), and BA (SMD =2.00, 95% CI: 0.48, 3.56), MBT substantially enhanced asthma-related living quality. The detailed league table is presented in Figure S3C. A comparison-adjusted funnel plot was utilized to evaluate publication bias for AQLQ. All points on the funnel plot were relatively symmetrically distributed, and Egger’s test yielded a P>0.05. These results suggested no potential publication bias (Figure S3D).

ACT

Regarding this outcome, 12 studies involving 2,575 individuals presented results of ACT for five non-pharmacological adjuvant interventions. The evidence network is illustrated in Figure 3B. The model demonstrated good convergence with a PSRF of 1 (Figure S4A). On the basis of SUCRA values, BT maintained the highest odds of becoming the most effective intervention for symptom control in asthma patients (85.7%), followed by MBT (76.9%), BA (54.81%), EX (41.38%), mHealth (40.19%), and AP (29.26%) (Figure S4B). Additionally, in comparison to UT and UC in control groups, BT (SMD =8.16, 95% CI: 1.68, 14.61) markedly controlled asthma symptoms. The detailed league table is shown in Figure S4C. A comparison-adjusted funnel plot was leveraged to evaluate publication bias for ACT. All points on the funnel plot were relatively symmetrically distributed, and Egger’s test yielded a P>0.05. These results revealed no potential publication bias (Figure S4D).

FVC

For this outcome, 8 studies involving 1,355 individuals reported results of FVC for five non-pharmacological adjuvant interventions. The evidence network is shown in Figure 3C. The model demonstrated good convergence with a PSRF of 1 (Figure S5A). On the basis of SUCRA values, BA maintained the highest odds of becoming the most effective intervention for improving the pulmonary ventilation index FVC in asthma patients (81.94%), followed by MBT (57.18%), BT (56.61%), EX (42.77%), and mHealth (41.03%) (Figure S5B). Compared to UT and UC in control groups, MBT (SMD =0.53, 95% CI: 0.07, 0.99) markedly enhanced FVC, while no statistically substantial variations were identified in all other pairwise comparisons. The detailed league table is presented in Figure S5C. A comparison-adjusted funnel plot was employed to evaluate publication bias for FVC. All points on the funnel plot were relatively symmetrically distributed, and Egger’s test yielded a P>0.05. These results uncovered no potential publication bias (Figure S5D).

FEV1

Regarding this outcome, 13 studies involving 1,805 individuals presented results of FEV1 for five non-pharmacological adjuvant interventions. The evidence network is illustrated in Figure 3D. The model exhibited good convergence with a PSRF of 1 (Figure S6A). On the basis of SUCRA values, BA maintained the highest odds of becoming the most effective intervention for improving the pulmonary ventilation index FEV1 in asthma patients (87.53%), followed by MBT (67.25%), EX (56.46%), BT (37.64%), and mHealth (41.03%) (Figure S6B). Compared to UT and UC in control groups, BA (SMD =0.56, 95% CI: 0.21, 0.92) considerably improved FEV1, while no statistically substantial variations were identified in all other pairwise comparisons. The detailed league table is presented in Figure S6C. A comparison-adjusted funnel plot was applied to evaluate publication bias for FEV1. All points on the funnel plot were relatively symmetrically distributed, and Egger’s test yielded a P>0.05. These results revealed no potential publication bias (Figure S6D).

PEF

For this outcome, eight studies involving 1,348 individuals reported results of PEF for five non-pharmacological adjuvant interventions. The evidence network is shown in Figure 3E. The model demonstrated good convergence with a PSRF of 1 (Figure S7A). Based on SUCRA values, BA maintained the highest odds of becoming the most effective intervention for improving the pulmonary ventilation index PEF in asthma patients (99.64%), followed by MBT (58.62%), BT (57.63%), EX (44.54%), and mHealth (19.15%) (Figure S7B). Furthermore, BA considerably enhanced PEF in comparison to other interventions. The detailed league table is presented in Figure S7C. A comparison-adjusted funnel plot was leveraged to evaluate publication bias for PEF. All points on the funnel plot were relatively symmetrically distributed, and Egger’s test yielded a P>0.05. These results suggested no potential publication bias (Figure S7D).

FEV1/FVC

Regarding this outcome, 13 studies involving 1,042 individuals presented results of FEV1/FVC for five non-pharmacological adjuvant interventions. The evidence network is illustrated in Figure 3F. The model exhibited good convergence with a PSRF of 1 (Figure S8A). On the basis of SUCRA values, MBT maintained the highest odds of becoming the most effective intervention for improving the pulmonary ventilation index FEV1/FVC in asthma patients (91.17%), followed by EX (69.03%), BT (53.61%), BA (50.82%), AP (47.39%), and mHealth (25.13%) (Figure S8B). Furthermore, in comparison to UT and UC in control groups, MBT (SMD =17.04, 95% CI: 13.85, 20.16) markedly improved FEV1/FVC. Additionally, MBT significantly improved FEV1/FVC compared to BT, mHealth, or BA. The detailed league table is presented in Figure S8C. A comparison-adjusted funnel plot was adopted to evaluate publication bias for FEV1/FVC. All points on the funnel plot were relatively symmetrically distributed, and Egger’s test yielded a P>0.05. These data suggested no potential publication bias (Figure S8D).

Meta-regression

Due to substantial heterogeneity in follow-up durations across the included studies, a meta-regression analysis was conducted to explore the potential impact of follow-up duration on each outcome. The results showed no substantial association between follow-up duration and any outcome, and this finding was not statistically significant (Table S3).

GRADE

The GRADE tool was used to assess the quality of evidence for all outcomes. The results showed that the overall quality of evidence for the outcomes was moderate (Table S4).

Discussion

This is the first NMA of non-pharmacological adjuvant interventions aimed at improving the health status of adult asthma patients, encompassing the optimization of living quality, effective symptom control, and enhancement of pulmonary function. An NMA evaluating MBT for asthma patients has indicated that MBT helps improve asthma symptoms and health-associated living quality, and also increases pulmonary function parameters encompassing FEV1, FVC, and FEV1/FVC (67). An analysis by Wang et al. has showed that BA can enhance the living quality and alleviate symptoms of asthma patients (68). A systematic review by Kuder et al. has demonstrated that EX can enhance pulmonary function, disease control, and living quality of asthma patients without worsening asthma-related outcomes (69). Nevertheless, current evidence was still limited to assessments based on individual non-pharmacological adjuvant interventions. A comprehensive evaluation of multiple non-pharmacological methods is lacking. Based on 37 studies (n=4,693), this research comprehensively contrasted the influences of five non-pharmacological adjuvant interventions on asthma patients. This investigation addresses the limitations of traditional meta-analyses focusing on individual interventions, providing important evidence for clinical decision-making. The data uncovered that MBT-based interventions were associated with significant improvements in AQLQ, ACT, FVC, and FEV1/FVC. Similarly, BA-based interventions led to substantial improvements in key outcomes encompassing FEV1, PEF, and FEV1/FVC, while BT-based interventions were linked to marked improvements in key outcomes like AQLQ, ACT, and FEV1/FVC.

Based on the SUCRA results, MBT showed a higher probability of improving both AQLQ and FEV1/FVC in asthma patients compared with other interventions. MBT focuses on facilitating positive mind-body interactions (19) and falls into the category of complementary and alternative medicine. This therapy emphasizes relaxation and stretching of skeletal muscles, breath regulation, and active contemplation. Patients can leverage respiratory muscle strength to lower breathing frequency, thus enhancing oxygen uptake and alleviating hypoxia (70). Simultaneously, it may alleviate airway inflammation by downregulating the expression of inflammation-related genes, like those in the nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB) signaling pathway (71). This molecular mechanism provides a theoretical basis for the potential value of this therapy in asthma symptom control and pulmonary function improvement. In addition, this mechanism, together with evidence that MBT can reduce levels of peripheral blood inflammatory factors (72), further confirms multi-dimensional pathways of action of MBT in asthma management. However, in line with the conclusions of existing systematic reviews and clinical guidelines, the improvement in objective lung function measures with non-pharmacological interventions was overall limited, and there was significant heterogeneity across different study results. Therefore, the improvements in lung function observed in this study should only be regarded as trend changes rather than definitive evidence of efficacy, and should be interpreted with caution. At this stage, it is not yet recommended to prioritize the promotion of MBT in clinical practice.

This study indicates that BA is more likely to improve lung function measures, including FEV1, FVC, and PEF. Negative psychological factors encompassing stress, anxiety, and depression are among the triggers for asthma onset and can contribute to the pathogenesis of asthma through interactions across behavioral, neural, endocrine, and immune systems. Specifically, these negative emotions not only directly affect the stability of airway smooth muscle, inducing bronchospasm, but also promote the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines via neuroimmune regulatory pathways, exacerbating persistent airway inflammation. Furthermore, chronic stress can lead to hyporeactivity of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis in individuals with asthma, resulting in abnormal cortisol secretion. Such endocrine disturbances further aggravate airway inflammatory injury, ultimately contributing to a decline in lung function (73-75). BA can improve lung function in asthma patients through a dual pathway of psychophysiological regulation and optimized disease self-management. On one hand, interventions encompassing cognitive-behavioral therapy and mindfulness training can reduce negative emotions and reduce individual stress perception, thereby suppressing hyperactivity of the HPA axis and sympathetic nervous system, decreasing abnormal cortisol secretion and catecholamine release, and ultimately reducing airway inflammatory infiltration and smooth muscle spasm, leading to improved pulmonary ventilation. On the other hand, these interventions can directly modulate physiological systems encompassing the autonomic nerves system, endocrine system, and immune system, thus reducing abnormal contraction of airway smooth muscle and airway inflammation mediated by psychological factors. In addition, this approach improves patients’ self-management ability and medication adherence (76,77). Based on a lot of existing research literature, the improvement in objective lung function measures is limited and research on this topic is still at an exploratory stage. Nevertheless, the benefits of this intervention in symptom control and emotional regulation have been supported by more robust and consistent evidence.

Moreover, BT showed a higher probability of benefit in improving ACT. Asthma patients often experience anxiety and fear due to recurrent symptoms, thereby exacerbating hyperventilation and airway constriction. BT exerts its effects through multidimensional mechanisms. On one hand, it activates the vagus nerve and reduces sympathetic stress levels, thus significantly alleviating anxiety and depressive emotions and lowering patients' subjective perception of dyspnea. On the other hand, it regulates heart rate variability through controlled breathing rhythms, thereby suppressing airway constriction reflexes mediated by the vagus nerve and enhancing the relaxation function of airway smooth muscle (17,78).

This research has certain methodological strengths. First, the application of strict inclusion and exclusion criteria improved homogeneity of enrolled studies, promoting effective comparative analyses. Second, calculations of effect sizes for all outcomes both pre- and post-treatment were incorporated, thereby improving the precision of estimates in the NMA.

The research provides comprehensive evidence through NMA. Nevertheless, it has several limitations. First, this meta-analysis was implemented based on published aggregate data rather than data of individual patients. This approach restricts the capacity to comprehensively consider the impact of other potential variables on outcomes. Therefore, relevant measures should be taken in the future, including increasing sample sizes to lower small-study effects and improve the robustness of the findings. Second, there was significant clinical heterogeneity across the included studies, involving variations in intervention duration, intervention intensity, implementation form, asthma severity and phenotype of patients, as well as a wide range of follow-up durations (ranging from one month to one year). This heterogeneity may reduce the comparability of interventions, affect the robustness of indirect comparison results, pose challenges to the transitivity and consistency assumptions of network meta-analysis, and limit the comparative analysis of long-term efficacy. Future researchers should develop more rigorous long-term follow-up protocols to better evaluate the sustained benefits of interventions.

Furthermore, the acute exacerbation of asthma was not included as an outcome measure in this study. Due to scarce data and inconsistent reporting across the included studies, a reliable pooled analysis could not be performed. To meet the methodological assumptions of network meta-analysis, we only included individual non-pharmacological interventions. Although combined/multimodal intervention regimens are common in clinical practice, they were excluded from this study to ensure the methodological rigor required for a valid network meta-analysis. This may limit the generalizability of the findings to clinical practice. As a result of insufficient data, multimodal interventions could not be analyzed as independent nodes. Future research is still needed to evaluate the application effects of integrated multimodal intervention strategies in asthma management. Lastly, this review integrated evidence from direct comparisons between non‑pharmacological adjuvant interventions and other treatment modalities and it also leveraged indirect evidence to guide the evaluation. However, these findings should be further corroborated through more head‑to‑head trials.

Conclusions

The present research comprehensively evaluated divergent effects of non‑pharmacological adjuvant interventions on asthma through the NMA for the first time. The results highlight the roles of MBT, BA, and BT. The findings of this study indicate that various interventions only offer complementary relative benefits, and no single intervention demonstrates absolute superiority. The SUCRA results should be regarded as exploratory findings and are insufficient to provide definitive clinical recommendations. Future high-quality, head-to-head RCTs with long-term follow-up are needed to further validate the effect sizes, clinical application value, and long-term efficacy of various interventions, thereby providing a more robust evidence base for the comprehensive non-pharmacological management of adult asthma.
