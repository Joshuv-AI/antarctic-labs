Moments of Pólya urns balanced in expectation
Colin Desmarais

Abstract
In this work, recent results on the moments of balanced Pólya urns are generalized to unbalanced urns, with the condition that the expected change in total activity at each step is constant. We also provide applications of our results to the degree distributions of random trees grown by uniform attachment with freezing and to the degree distribution of hooking networks; in both cases we prove a normal limit law with convergence of all moments.



1
Introduction

A generalized Pólya urn contains several balls of different colours. At each step, a ball is selected from the urn at random proportional to its
activity
, a real value assigned to each ball of the same colour. The ball is then replaced with a (potentially random) set of other balls which depends on the colour of the drawn ball. The expected replacement scheme at each step is encoded in an
intensity matrix

A
, defined below. As is well-studied, the eigenvalues of this matrix influence the asymptotic behaviour of the urn. Under some irreducibility assumptions, the intensity matrix
A
admits a simple real eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}
such that
\lambda_{1}\geq\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda
for all other eigenvalues
\lambda
. If
\lambda_{1}\geq 2\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda
, the urn is called
small
and in several cases is asymptotically normal, see
[
14
, Theorems 3.22 & 3.23]
for the general result. It was further shown in several recent works that convergence of moments also holds in these cases if the urn is
balanced
; that is, if the change in the sum of the activities of the balls at each step is constant
[
16
,
17
,
18
]
; we note also the work of Pouyanne
[
23
]
on the moments of balanced
large urns
(where
\lambda_{1}<2\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda
for some eigenvalue
\lambda\neq\lambda_{1}
of
A
).

Despite several works on the moments of balanced urns, similar results for unbalanced urns have remained elusive (see
[
16
, Problem 1.1]
and
[
17
, Problem 1.1]
). In this work we generalize several results to a class of unbalanced urns. Our results are stated in slightly more generality (see Section
3
), but the main result can be summarized as showing convergence of moments for strictly small urns (where
\lambda_{1}>2\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda
for all other eigenvalues of
A
) if we assume the change in the sum of the activities of the balls at each step is constant in expectation, what we call
balanced in expectation
(see (
2.4
) below).

To prove our results, we perform a similar decomposition of the Pólya urn appearing in previous works, notably in
[
3
,
4
,
16
,
17
]
. The idea is to rewrite the contribution of the
k
-th draw on the composition of the urn at a later time as a linear transformation of a martingale difference sequence. In the balanced case, the linear transformation is deterministic (not random), and so amenable to analysis; this property does not hold in greater generality. However, when the urn is balanced in expectation, the contribution at the
k
-th draw can be further decomposed into a part that maintains the martingale difference sequence property, and a noisy part which can be controlled, all while maintaining the deterministic linear transformation applied to the contributions (see (
4.17
)).

Pólya urns have long been applied to classes of random increasing trees and their generalizations, and used to prove normal limit laws for their degrees; see for example
[
13
,
15
,
20
,
21
,
22
]
. In all of the examples cited, the urns used are balanced and satisfy the conditions of
[
16
,
17
,
18
]
to guarantee the convergence of the moments as well. However, some recently introduced models of increasing trees and generalizations thereof do not grow in a ‘balanced-like’ manner, but do grow in a way that is ‘balanced in expectation’; these include for example trees constructed via uniform attachment with freezing, and hooking networks. In Section
6
, we prove central limit theorems and convergences of moments for the degree distributions of these models.

We gather relevant terminology and assumptions in Section
2
, mostly copying the summaries provided in
[
16
,
17
]
(though with the assumption of balanced urns replaced with urns balanced in expectation). Our main results are presented in Section
3
; these include a general bound for moments of urns balanced in expectation, convergence of moments for strictly small urns admitting a central limit theorem, and asymptotics for the expectations of urns balanced in expectation. The main technical work of the proofs is contained in Section
4
, and the completions of the proofs are contained in Section
5
. Applications of our results are provided in Section
6
.

2
Definitions, notation and assumptions

Some general notation.
Throughout this work, all vectors are column vectors, with
v^{T}
used to denote the (row vector) transpose of
v
. We will use
\cdot
to denote the Euclidean inner product of two vectors.

The standard Euclidean norm for vectors is denoted
|\cdot|
, while we use
||M||
to denote the operator norm of the matrix
M
. For a random variable or random vector
X
, the
L^{p}
-norm of
X
is denoted
||X||_{p}:=\left(\mathbb{E}|X|^{p}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}
.

We reserve the letter
q
for the dimension of our vectors and matrices, i.e., all vectors belong to
\mathbb{F}^{q}
for some field
q
, and all matrices
A
belong to
\mathbb{F}^{q\times q}
. The letter
p
is reserved for norms; i.e., when denoting the
L^{p}
-norm
||\cdot||_{p}
.

Generalized Pólya urns.
We suppose there are
q\geq 2
types (or colours) of balls, each with a non-negative activity
a_{i}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
and assigned a random vector
\xi_{i}=(\xi_{i,1},\ldots,\xi_{i,q})^{T}
. The urn at time
n\geq 0
is denoted by the non-negative vector

X_{n}=(X_{n,1},\ldots,X_{n,q})^{T}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{q}.

Set the
activity vector
of the urn as
a:=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{q})^{T}
, and define the
total activity of the urn
at time
n
to be the value

S_{n}:=a\cdot X_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{q}a_{i}X_{n,i}.

(2.1)

The composition of the urn
(X_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
evolves as a discrete-time Markov process as follows. We initiate with a non-zero and non-random vector
X_{0}
such that

S_{0}=a\cdot X_{0}>0.

At each step
n\geq 0
, if
S_{n}>0
then a ball is selected from the urn where the probability of selecting a ball of type
i
for
i=1,\ldots q
is given by

\frac{a_{i}X_{n,i}}{\sum_{j=1}^{q}a_{j}X_{n,j}}=\frac{a_{i}X_{n,i}}{S_{n}},

(2.2)

while if
S_{n}=0
, then no ball is selected. We then sample the
replacement vector

\Delta X_{n}=(\Delta X_{n,1},\ldots,\Delta X_{n,q})
where
\Delta X_{n}\sim\xi_{i}
if a ball of type
i
is selected, and
\Delta X_{n}=0
if no ball is selected. Importantly, the distribution of
\Delta X_{n}
depends only on the type of the ball selected at time
n
(if a ball is selected), independently of anything that has occurred so far. We then update the urn by setting

X_{n+1}=X_{n}+\Delta X_{n}.

(2.3)

Note that if the type
i
has activity
a_{i}=0
, then it is never selected (and so
\xi_{i}
has no effect on the urn process).

Balanced in expectation, tenability, and extinction.
Throughout this article, we will say that an urn is
balanced in expectation
if

\text{for all }i=1,\ldots,q,\qquad\text{if }a_{i}>0\text{ then }\sum_{j=1}^{q}a_{j}\mathbb{E}\xi_{i,j}=b>0

(2.4)

for some constant
b
. Contrast with
balanced
urns, which is defined similarly but with the expectation removed in (
2.4
). In this article, we are concerned with urns that are balanced in expectation, and show that many results for balanced urns hold for urns balanced in expectation.

We will say that
X_{n,i}
is the ‘number of balls’ of type
i
at time
n
, though
X_{n,i}
need not be an integer. We allow for
\xi_{i,j}
to be negative (so balls may be subtracted from the urn), though we require the urn to be
tenable
, in the sense that
X_{0}
and the distributions of the random vectors
\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{q}
must be defined such that the
X_{n}
are non-negative at each step (and we cannot ‘remove’ balls from the urn that do not exist). This can be accomplished for example by assuming that
\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{q}
satisfy a.s.

\text{for }i\neq j,\qquad\xi_{i,j}\geq 0\qquad\text{and}\qquad\xi_{j,i}\text{ is a multiple of }\xi_{i,i}.

(see for example
[
14
, Remark 4.2]
), though we do not make this assumption explicitly here.

Define the event of
non-extinction up to time
n
as

\mathcal{E}^{c}_{n}:=\{S_{k}>0\text{ for all }0\leq k\leq n\}.

(2.5)

We say that the urn is non-extinct if it is non-extinct at time
n
for all
n\geq 0
, that is, if
S_{n}>0
for all
n\geq 0
. From the definition of
S_{n}
, we see that the urn is non-extinct if there exist balls with nonzero activity; the urn may be extinct but still contain balls with activity zero. In the case of non-trivial balanced urns, the total activity
S_{n}
increases by a constant non-zero amount at each step; that is, the urn is non-extinct at all times. Since normal limit laws were proved for urns with possible extinction
[
14
]
, we will also consider such urns here; in fact one of our applications produces an urn with a positive probability of extinction (see Section
6.1
).

Intensity matrix and spectral decomposition.
A fundamental object for understanding limit results for Pólya urns is the
intensity matrix

A:=(a_{j}\mathbb{E}\xi_{j,i})_{i,j=1}^{q}.

(2.6)

Note the indices of
A
; the
j
-th
column
is given by
a_{j}\mathbb{E}\xi_{j}
, the use of the transpose
A^{T}
is used in some of the literature.

Letting
\sigma(A)
be the set of eigenvalues of
A
, there exists a set of projections
\{P_{\lambda}:\>\lambda\in\sigma(A)\}
such that

\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma(A)}P_{\lambda}=I,

(2.7)

AP_{\lambda}=P_{\lambda}A=\lambda P_{\lambda}+N_{\lambda},

(2.8)

where
N_{\lambda}=P_{\lambda}N_{\lambda}=N_{\lambda}P_{\lambda}
is nilpotent. Also,
P_{\lambda}P_{\mu}=0
whenever
\lambda\neq\mu
, while
N_{\lambda}^{\nu_{\lambda}}\neq 0
and
N_{\lambda}^{\nu_{\lambda}+1}=0
, where
\nu_{\lambda}+1
is the size of the largest Jordan block in the Jordan decomposition of
A
with
\lambda
on the diagonals. Thus we see that
\nu_{\lambda}=0
if
\lambda
is simple.

In the case of urns balanced in expectation, we have that
b
is an eigenvalue of
A
and that
a^{T}
is a left eigenvector of
A
associated with
b
, which follows by (
2.4
) and (
2.6
) since

a^{T}A=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{q}a_{i}a_{j}\mathbb{E}\xi_{j,i}\right)_{j=1}^{q}=\left(a_{j}\mathbb{E}(a\cdot\xi_{j})\right)_{j=1}^{q}=ba^{T}.

We will also assume throughout that
b\geq\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda
for all other eigenvalues
\lambda
of
A
. Much like in the case of balanced urns, this assumption is rather weak. In particular, this assumption is satisfied whenever the urn satisfies the assumptions (A1)–(A6) of
[
14
]
and is balanced in expectation (see
[
14
, Lemma 5.4]
).

The eigenvalues of
A
are labelled
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{q}
in decreasing order of their real parts and Jordan block size, that is,
\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda_{1}\geq\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda_{q}
and if
\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda_{i}=\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda_{j}
and
\nu_{\lambda_{i}}>\nu_{\lambda_{j}}
, then
i>j
. By assumption we have
b=\lambda_{1}
is real. We say that the urn is
large
if
\lambda_{1}<2\lambda_{2}
,
small
if
\lambda_{1}\geq 2\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda_{2}
, and
strictly small
if
\lambda_{1}>2\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda_{2}
. We further make use of the following Lemma from
[
17
]
that also holds for urns balanced in expectation:

Lemma 2.1
.

[
17
, Lemma 2.3]

Suppose
\lambda_{1}=b
is simple. Then there exists a unique right eigenvector
v_{1}
of
A
such that

a\cdot v_{1}=1.

Furthermore, the projection
P_{\lambda_{1}}
is given by
P_{\lambda_{1}}=v_{1}a^{T}
and as a consequence for any
v\in\mathbb{C}^{q}
,

P_{\lambda_{1}}v=(a\cdot v)v_{1}.

3
Main Results

For simplicity of notation, we will use
C
to denote an unspecified constant which is possibly different at each occurrence, and which depends only on the urn; that is, it depends only on
q
, the activity vector
a
, the initial composition
X_{0}
, and the distribution of
\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{q}
. If the constant also depends on the
p
-norm, it is denoted by
C_{p}
. In particular, throughout, the constants
C
and
C_{p}

do not
depend on
n
.

For
p\geq 1
, denote the following statement

\mathrm{(U}_{p}\mathrm{)}
:

the urn is tenable and balanced in expectation,
\lambda_{1}=b>0
, and
\left\lVert\xi_{i}\right\rVert_{p}<\infty
for all
i=1,\ldots q
,

and recall the event
\mathcal{E}^{c}_{n}
of non-extinction up to time
n
(
2.5
). We first state the following general bound for the moments.

Theorem 3.1
.

Assume
\mathrm{(U}_{p}\mathrm{)}
for some
p\geq 2
. Then for all
n\geq 2
,

\left\|\left(X_{n}-\mathbb{E}[X_{n}|\mathcal{E}^{c}_{n}]\right)\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}^{c}_{n}}\right\rVert_{p}\leq\begin{cases}C_{p}n^{1/2}&\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda_{2}<\lambda_{1}/2\\
C_{p}n^{1/2}(\log n)^{\nu_{2}+1}&\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda_{2}=\lambda_{1}/2\\
C_{p}n^{\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda_{2}/\lambda_{1}}(\log{n})^{\nu_{2}}&\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda_{2}>\lambda_{1}/2.\end{cases}

(3.1)

We note that in the case
\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda_{2}=\lambda_{1}/2
of (
3.1
), we were not able to achieve a bound that matches the normalizing constant for well-known normal limit laws (for example from
[
14
, Theorem 3.23]
). However, in the case
\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda_{2}<\lambda_{1}/2
of (
3.1
), the bound
C\sqrt{n}
does match the normalizing constant for well-known normal limit laws, in particular in the case where conditioned on non-extinction,

\frac{X_{n}-\mathbb{E}\left[X_{n}|\mathcal{E}^{c}_{n}\right]}{\sqrt{n}}\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)

(3.2)

for some covariance matrix
\Sigma
.

Theorem 3.2
.

Assume
\mathrm{(U}_{p}\mathrm{)}
for all
p\geq 2
. Assume further that the urn is strictly small and that conditioned on non-extinction, the normal limit law (
3.2
) holds. Then (
3.2
) holds with convergence of all conditional moments. In particular, the following convergence of the covariance matrix holds:

\mathrm{Cov}\left[X_{n}|\mathcal{E}^{c}_{n}\right]/n\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}\Sigma.

For the expectation, we are able to prove the following asymptotics.

Theorem 3.3
.

Assume
\mathrm{(U}_{2}\mathrm{)}
. If
\lambda_{1}=b>\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda_{2}
, then

\mathbb{E}\left[X_{n}|\mathcal{E}^{c}_{n}\right]=n\lambda_{1}v_{1}+o(n).

If additionally, the urn is strictly small (
\lambda_{1}=b>2\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda_{2}
), then

\mathbb{E}\left[X_{n}|\mathcal{E}^{c}_{n}\right]=n\lambda_{1}v_{1}+O(\sqrt{n}).

Supposing some technical assumptions and that the urn is strictly small, it is proved in
[
14
, Theorem 3.22]
that conditioned on non-extinction,

\frac{X_{n}-n\lambda_{1}v_{1}}{\sqrt{n}}\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)

(3.3)

for the vector
v_{1}
from Lemma
2.1
and for a matrix
\Sigma
given by
[
14
, (3.19)]
. To apply Theorem
3.2
, we need to replace
n\lambda_{1}v_{1}
with
\mathbb{E}\left[X_{n}|\mathcal{E}^{c}_{n}\right]
in (
3.3
), which is guaranteed by the following theorem.

Theorem 3.4
.

Assume
\mathrm{(U}_{2}\mathrm{)}
. If the urn is strictly small and conditioned on non-extinction admits the convergence (
3.3
), then

\mathbb{E}\left[X_{n}|\mathcal{E}^{c}_{n}\right]=n\lambda v_{1}+o(\sqrt{n}),

and so conditioned on non-extinction, the distributional convergence (
3.2
) also holds.

Remark 3.5
.

In applications, if
\mathrm{(U}_{p}\mathrm{)}
holds for all
p\geq 2
and one can prove the convergence (
3.3
) holds (for example by applying
[
14
, Theorem 3.22]
), Theorem
3.4
along with Theorem
3.2
implies that (
3.3
) and (
3.2
) converge to the same limit, with convergence of all moments holding as well. This is the approach we use in Section
6
.

Remark 3.6
.

We note some minor mistakes in
[
14
]
relevant to our results.

The impossibility of non-extinction stated in
[
14
, Lemma 2.1]
only holds if
\sum_{j}\xi_{i,j}\geq 0
for all
i
and
\sum_{j}\mathbb{E}\xi_{i,j}>0
for some
i
. (i.e., by removing the expectations in the first inequality). Only then does the number of balls in the urn never decrease (guaranteeing non-extinction). The remainder of the proof appears to be correct; that is, assuming that
A
is irreducible, that (A1) and (A2) hold, and that
\sum_{j}\mathbb{E}\xi_{i,j}\geq 0
for all
i
and
\sum_{j}\mathbb{E}\xi_{i,j}>0
for some
i
, then assumptions (A1)–(A6) of
[
14
]
hold.

We also note a minor mistake in the statement of
[
14
, Lemma 5.4]
. It is stated that if the urn is balanced in expectation, the matrix
\Sigma
from (
3.3
) can be replaced with a matrix
b\Sigma_{1}
admitting a simpler calculation. However, in the proof of the lemma, it is only assumed the urn is balanced (and not balanced in expectation), and in fact counter examples exist that show that this replacement is not true if we only assume balanced in expectation. The remaining statement of
[
14
, Lemma 5.4]
appears to be correct, and the replacement of
\Sigma
with
b\Sigma_{I}
does hold if the urn is balanced.

4
Bounding spectral projections of moments

The goal in this section is to prove the following theorem, the proof of which is at the end of this section.

Theorem 4.1
.

Assume
\mathrm{(U}_{p}\mathrm{)}
for some
p\geq 2
. The following hold:

(i)

For all eigenvalues
\lambda\in\sigma(A)
and
n\geq 2
,

\left\lVert P_{\lambda}\left(X_{n}-\mathbb{E}\left[X_{n}|\mathcal{E}^{c}_{n}\right]\right)\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}^{c}_{n}}\right\rVert_{p}\leq\begin{cases}C_{p}n^{1/2}&\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda<\lambda_{1}/2\\
C_{p}n^{1/2}(\log n)^{\nu_{\lambda}+1}&\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda=\lambda_{1}/2\\
C_{p}n^{\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda_{2}/\lambda_{1}}(\log{n})^{\nu_{\lambda}}&\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda>\lambda_{1}/2.\end{cases}

(4.1)

(ii)

If
\lambda_{1}=b
is a simple eigenvalue, then
P_{\lambda_{1}}\left(X_{n}-\mathbb{E}\left[X_{n}|\mathcal{E}^{c}_{n}\right]\right)=\left(S_{n}-\mathbb{E}\left[S_{n}|\mathcal{E}^{c}_{n}\right]\right)v_{1}
and

\left\lVert P_{\lambda_{1}}\left(X_{n}-\mathbb{E}\left[X_{n}|\mathcal{E}^{c}_{n}\right]\right)\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}^{c}_{n}}\right\rVert_{p}\leq C_{p}\sqrt{n}.

(4.2)

A note on extinction.
A method used to study Pólya urns is to embed the process into a continuous time Markov branching process, notably in
[
1
]
,
[
2
, Sec. V.9]
, and
[
14
]
. The process
\mathcal{X}(t)=(\mathcal{X}_{1}(t),\ldots,\mathcal{X}_{q}(t))
is defined according to the same parameters
a_{1},\ldots,a_{q}
and
\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{q}
. A particle of type
i
lives for an exponentially distributed time with mean
a_{i}^{-1}
, independently of all other particles. After it dies, the particle is replaced with a number of particles distributed as
(\xi_{i}+\delta_{i,j})_{j=1}^{q}
independently from everything that has occurred so far. Each new particle of type
j
is assigned a new lifespan exponentially distributed with mean
a_{j}^{-1}
. The process is defined to be right-continuous. Define
\tau_{n}
to be the time of the
n
-th birth event; then
(\mathcal{X}(\tau_{n}))_{n=0}^{\infty}
is equal in distribution to
(X_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
. As is well-known, the condition
\lambda_{1}=b>0
guarantees that
\mathcal{X}(t)
has positive probability of non-extinction, meaning that
\mathcal{X}(t)
contains particles with positive activity for all
t\in\mathbb{R}

[
2
, Sec. V.7]
.

From the defined embedding, we see that the probability of non-extinction is positive for
(X_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
given our assumption that
\lambda_{1}=b>0
, see also the discussion contained in
[
14
, Sec. 2]
. We have also defined
(\mathcal{E}_{n}^{c})_{n=0}^{\infty}
to be a nested sequence of sets (with
\mathcal{E}_{n+1}^{c}\subseteq\mathcal{E}_{n}^{c}
), and so

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{n}^{c}}\right]=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{E}_{n}^{c}\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcap_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{E}_{n}^{c}\right)>0.

(4.3)

From our definition of non-extinction (
2.5
) and from the definition of
S_{n}
(
2.1
), an extinct urn may still contain balls with activity zero. Since the columns of
A
corresponding to balls with activity zero are also zero, we see that

S_{n}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{n}^{c}}=a\cdot X_{n}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{n}^{c}}=a\cdot X_{n}=S_{n},\qquad\text{and}\qquad AX_{n}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{n}^{c}}=AX_{n}.

(4.4)

Decomposition of
X_{n}
.
We will be evaluating martingales and martingale difference sequence, and so will be encountering conditional expectations with respect to
\sigma
-fields. To avoid confusion for the remainder of this section up to Section
5
, we will denote the expectation of a random variable
X
conditioned on the
event
of non-extinction up to time
n
by

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}_{n}^{c}}\left[X\right]:=\mathbb{E}\left[X|\mathcal{E}_{n}^{c}\right]=\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[X\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{n}^{c}}\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{n}^{c}}\right]},

(4.5)

and reserve the notation
\mathbb{E}\left[X|\mathcal{F}\right]
for conditional expectation with respect to the
\sigma
-field

\mathcal{F}
. From (
4.3
), we see that (
4.5
) is well-defined since the denominator is never zero.

Throughout the following discussion before the statement of lemmas, assume
\mathrm{(U}_{p}\mathrm{)}
holds for some
p\geq 2
. Let
\mathcal{F}_{n}
be the
\sigma
-field generated by
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
. Let
I_{n}
be the colour of the
n
-th drawn ball if
S_{n-1}>0
. Recall that no ball is selected if
S_{n-1}=0
(i.e., if the urn is extinct), and so for all
n
,

\sum_{j=1}^{q}\mathbb{P}(I_{n+1}=j|\mathcal{F}_{n})=\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}^{c}_{n}}.

(4.6)

Then by (
2.2
)

\mathbb{P}(I_{n+1}=j|\mathcal{F}_{n})=\frac{a_{j}X_{nj}}{S_{n}}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}^{c}_{n}},

and recalling
\Delta X_{n}\sim\xi_{j}
if the
j
’th ball is selected,

\mathbb{E}\left[\Delta X_{n}|\mathcal{F}_{n}\right]=\sum_{j=1}^{q}\mathbb{P}(I_{n+1}=j|\mathcal{F}_{n})\mathbb{E}\xi_{j}=\frac{\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}^{c}_{n}}}{S_{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{q}a_{j}X_{n,j}\mathbb{E}\xi_{j}=\frac{1}{S_{n}}AX_{n}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}^{c}_{n}},

(4.7)

while by assumption
\mathrm{(U}_{p}\mathrm{)}
,

\mathbb{E}\left[|\Delta X_{n}|^{p}|\mathcal{F}_{n}\right]=\sum_{j=1}^{q}\mathbb{P}(I_{n+1}=j|\mathcal{F}_{n})\mathbb{E}|\xi_{j}|^{p}\leq C_{p}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}^{c}_{n}},

(4.8)

and so

\left\lVert\Delta X_{n}\right\rVert_{p}\leq C_{p}.

(4.9)

Recalling the vector of activities
a
, (
2.4
), (
4.6
) and (
4.7
) yield

\mathbb{E}\left[a\cdot\Delta X_{n}|\mathcal{F}_{n}\right]=\sum_{j=1}^{q}\mathbb{P}\left(I_{n+1}=j|\mathcal{F}_{n}\right)a\cdot\mathbb{E}\xi_{j}=b\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{n}^{c}},

(4.10)

and so by (

),
\mathbb{E}\left[a\cdot\Delta X_{n}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[a\cdot\Delta X_{n}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{n}^{c}}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[b\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{n}^{c}}\right]
.
Conditioning on non-extinction,

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}_{n}^{c}}\left[a\cdot\Delta X_{n}\right]=\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[a\cdot\Delta X_{n}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{n}^{c}}\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{n}^{c}}\right]}=b.

(4.11)

From (

) and (

),

S_{n}=a\cdot\left(X_{0}+\sum_{\ell=0}^{n-1}\Delta X_{\ell}\right)=a\cdot X_{0}+\sum_{\ell=0}^{n-1}a\cdot\Delta X_{\ell},

which we use to find the expected total activity of the urn conditioned on non-extinction at time

\omega_{n}:=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}_{n}^{c}}\left[S_{n}\right]=a\cdot X_{0}+\sum_{\ell=0}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}_{n}^{c}}\left[a\cdot\Delta X_{\ell}\right]=a\cdot X_{0}+nb.

(4.13)

Define

\displaystyle Y_{n}

\displaystyle:=\Delta X_{n-1}-\mathbb{E}\left[\Delta X_{n-1}|\mathcal{F}_{n-1}\right],

(4.14)

\displaystyle Z_{n}

\displaystyle:=\left(\frac{\omega_{n-1}-S_{n-1}}{\omega_{n-1}}\right)\mathbb{E}\left[\Delta X_{n-1}|\mathcal{F}_{n-1}\right].

(4.15)

Noting from (

By (

By defining

F_{i,j}:=\prod_{i\leq k<j}(I+\omega_{k}^{-1}A),

(4.16)

we are left with the decomposition of

Since

P_{\lambda}\left(X_{n}-\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}_{n}^{c}}\left[X_{n}\right]\right)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}P_{\lambda}F_{\ell,n}Y_{\ell}-\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}P_{\lambda}F_{\ell,n}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}_{n}^{c}}\left[Y_{\ell}\right]+\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}P_{\lambda}F_{\ell,n}Z_{\ell}-\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}P_{\lambda}F_{\ell,n}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}_{n}^{c}}\left[Z_{\ell}\right].

Lemma 4.4

Proof.

Define the sequence of random variables

From Minkowski’s inequality,

(4.22)

From (

Since

for all

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{\ell=0}^{n-1}\left(a\cdot\Delta X_{\ell}-b\right)\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{\ell}^{c}}\right)\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{n}^{c}}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}W_{\ell}\right)\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{n}^{c}}.

The result now follows from (

Lemma 4.5
.

Assume
\mathrm{(U}_{p}\mathrm{)}
for some

. For all

\left\|\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}P_{\lambda}F_{\ell,n}Z_{\ell}\right\|_{p}\leq\begin{cases}C_{p}n^{1/2}&\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda<\lambda_{1}/2\\
C_{p}n^{1/2}(\log n)^{1+\nu_{\lambda}}&\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda=\lambda_{1}/2\\
C_{p}n^{\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda/\lambda_{1}}(\log{n})^{\nu_{\lambda}}&\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda>\lambda_{1}/2.\end{cases}

Proof.

First we show that

\left\lVert Z_{n}\right\rVert_{p}\leq\frac{C_{p}}{\sqrt{n}}.

(4.24)

Since
\omega_{n}^{-1}(\omega_{n}-S_{n})
is
\mathcal{F}_{n}
-measurable, we apply (

) and get

\mathbb{E}\left[|Z_{n}|^{p}|\mathcal{F}_{n}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\left.\left(\frac{\omega_{n}-S_{n}}{\omega_{n}}\mathbb{E}\left[\Delta X_{n}|\mathcal{F}_{n}\right]\right)^{p}\right\rvert\mathcal{F}_{n}\right]\leq\left(\frac{\omega_{n}-S_{n}}{\omega_{n}}\right)^{p}\mathbb{E}\left[|\Delta X_{n}|^{p}|\mathcal{F}_{n}\right]\leq C_{p}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}^{c}_{n}}\left(\frac{\omega_{n}-S_{n}}{\omega_{n}}\right)^{p}.

(4.25)

Then (
4.24
) follows by Lemma
4.4
, (
4.13
), and (
4.25
).

Let
\gamma:=\text{Re}\lambda/b
. First suppose
\gamma<1/2
. From (
4.24
), Lemma
4.2
, and Minkowski’s inequality,

\left\|\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}P_{\lambda}F_{\ell,n}Z_{\ell}\right\|_{p}\leq\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}||P_{\lambda}F_{\ell,n}||\,||Z_{\ell}||_{p}\leq\frac{C_{p}}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\left(\frac{\ell}{n}\right)^{-\gamma-1/2}\left(1+\log\frac{n}{\ell}\right)^{\nu_{\lambda}}\\
\leq C_{p}\sqrt{n}\int_{0}^{1}x^{-\gamma-1/2}\left(1+\log\frac{1}{x}\right)^{\nu_{\lambda}}dx=C_{p}\sqrt{n}.

Similarly if
\gamma=1/2
, then

\left\|\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}P_{\lambda}F_{\ell,n}Z_{\ell}\right\|_{p}\leq C_{p}n^{1/2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\ell^{-1}\left(1+\log\frac{n}{\ell}\right)^{\nu_{\lambda}}\leq C_{p}n^{1/2}(\log{n})^{1+\nu_{\lambda}}.

Finally, if
\gamma>1/2
,

\left\|\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}P_{\lambda}F_{\ell,n}Z_{\ell}\right\|_{p}\leq C_{p}n^{\gamma}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\ell^{-\gamma-1/2}(\log n)^{\nu_{\lambda}}.

∎

Proof of Theorem

.

(4.26)

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

First suppose
\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda_{2}=\lambda_{1}=b
. In this case the result follows from Minkowski’s inequality,

\left\lVert X_{n}\right\rVert_{p}\leq\left\lVert X_{0}\right\rVert_{p}+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\left\lVert\Delta X_{k}\right\rVert_{p}\leq C_{p}+C_{p}n\leq C_{p}n.

By Jensen’s inequality and (
4.3
),
\left\lVert\mathbb{E}\left[X_{n}|\mathcal{E}_{n}^{c}\right]\right\rVert_{p}\leq C\left\lVert\mathbb{E}X_{n}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{n}^{c}}\right\rVert\leq\left\lVert X_{n}\right\rVert_{p}
and so Minkowski’s inequality once more yields
\left\lVert(X_{n}-\mathbb{E}\left[X_{n}|\mathcal{E}_{n}^{c}\right])\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{n}^{c}}\right\rVert_{p}\leq C_{p}n
.

Now assume that
\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda_{2}<\lambda_{1}
, and so
\lambda_{1}
is simple. From Theorem
4.1
(ii), we have that

\left\lVert P_{\lambda_{1}}\left(X_{n}-\mathbb{E}\left[X_{n}|\mathcal{E}_{n}^{c}\right]\right)\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{n}^{c}}\right\rVert_{p}\leq C_{p}\sqrt{n}.

Thus from the decomposition (
2.7
) and Minkowski’s inequality,

\left\lVert\left(X_{n}-\mathbb{E}\left[X_{n}|\mathcal{E}_{n}^{c}\right]\right)\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{n}^{c}}\right\rVert_{p}\leq\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma(A)}\left\lVert P_{\lambda}\left(X_{n}-\mathbb{E}\left[X_{n}|\mathcal{E}_{n}^{c}\right]\right)\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{n}^{c}}\right\rVert_{p}\leq C_{p}\sqrt{n}+\sum_{\lambda\neq\lambda_{1}}\left\lVert P_{\lambda}\left(X_{n}-\mathbb{E}\left[X_{n}|\mathcal{E}_{n}^{c}\right]\right))\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{n}^{c}}\right\rVert_{p}

is dominated by the bound for
\lambda=\lambda_{2}
from Theorem
4.1
(i), completing the proof. ∎

Proof of Theorem
3.2
.

As is well-known, for
1\leq p_{1}<p_{2}
, if non-negative random variables
M_{1},M_{2},\ldots
satisfy

, then

is uniformly integrable (see for example
[

, Theorem 5.4.2]
). Thus by assuming
\mathrm{(U}_{p}\mathrm{)}
for all

, Theorem
3.1
therefore implies that

\left\{\left\lvert\frac{X_{n}-\mathbb{E}\left[X_{n}|\mathcal{E}_{n}^{c}\right]}{\sqrt{n}}\right\rvert^{p}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{n}^{c}}\right\}_{n=2}^{\infty}

is uniformly integrable for all
p\geq 1
. The convergence in distribution (
3.2
) then implies the convergence of moments (see for example
[
12
, Theorem 5.5.9 (i)]
). ∎

Proof of Theorem
3.3
.
Recalling the definition of
S_{n}
from (

\mathbb{E}\left[P_{\lambda_{1}}X_{n}|\mathcal{E}_{n}^{c}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[(a\cdot X_{n})v_{1}|\mathcal{E}_{n}^{c}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[S_{n}|\mathcal{E}_{n}^{c}\right]v_{1}=(\omega_{0}+nb)v_{1}=(\omega_{0}+n\lambda_{1})v_{1}.

(5.1)

Let

Proof of Theorem

.
By Theorem

, Theorem

, and Minkowski’s inequality,

\left\lVert\left(X_{n}-n\lambda_{1}v_{1}\right)\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{n}^{c}}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\left\lVert\left(X_{n}-\mathbb{E}\left[X_{n}|\mathcal{E}_{n}^{c}\right]\right)\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{n}^{c}}\right\rVert_{2}+\left\lvert\left(\mathbb{E}\left[X_{n}|\mathcal{E}_{n}^{c}\right]-n\lambda_{1}v_{1}\right)\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{n}^{c}}\right\rvert\leq C\sqrt{n}

uniformly for all

and so conditioned on non-extinction,

6
Applications

As mentioned in the introduction, generalized Pólya urns have long been used to prove normal limit laws for the degree distributions of increasing trees, and in most cases, the associated urns are balanced and so also admit convergence of all moments thanks to previous results on balanced urns. Here we highlight two examples where we may apply our results to prove new results for the convergence of moments for degree distributions of certain recent random graph models similar to increasing trees.

6.1
Uniform attachment with freezing

Uniform attachment trees with freezing are a class of random trees of recent interest (see for example
[
5
,
6
,
7
,
19
]
). In the general case, we are given an infinite sequence
\bm{x}=(x_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}
where
x_{i}\in\{-1,1\}
. The initial tree
\mathcal{T}_{0}(\bm{x})
consists of a single vertex with the label
a
(for
active
). At each step
n\geq 1
, the tree
\mathcal{T}_{n}(\bm{x})
is constructed from
\mathcal{T}_{n-1}(\bm{x})
as follows: if there are no active vertices (labelled
a
) in
\mathcal{T}_{n-1}(\bm{x})
, then
\mathcal{T}_{n}(\bm{x})=\mathcal{T}_{n-1}(\bm{x})
. Otherwise, a vertex
v_{n}
is selected uniformly at random among the vertices with label
a
. If
x_{n}=-1
, then
v_{n}
becomes
frozen
and is given the label
n
, and if
x_{n}=+1
, then a edge is drawn from
v_{n}
to a new active vertex with label
a
.

When
x_{i}=+1
for all
i\geq 1
, the trees
\mathcal{T}_{n}(\bm{x})
are distributed as random recursive trees. Here we consider the case where the
x_{i}
are i.i.d. with
\mathbb{P}(x_{i}=+1)=p
and
\mathbb{P}(x_{i}=-1)=1-p
. As previously shown
[
5
, Theorem 2]
, in this case the limiting tree
\mathcal{T}_{\infty}(\bm{x})
is distributed as a Bienaymé tree with Geometric
\text{Ge}(1-p)
offspring distribution.

A Pólya urn for the vertex degrees.
For any fixed
K\geq 1
, we construct an urn where balls represent vertices with outdegree
0,\ldots,K-1
; the outdegree of a vertex is the number of children. Our urn has
2K+2
types of balls; for
m=0,\ldots,K-1
, balls of type
2m+1
represent active vertices with outdegree
m
and assigned the activity
a_{2m+1}=1
, and balls of type
2m+2
represent frozen vertices with outdegree
m
and assigned the activity
a_{2m+2}=0
. The balls of type
2K+1
represent active vertices with outdegree
m\geq k
, and given the activity
a_{2K+1}=1
, while frozen vertices with outdegree
m\geq K
are represented by balls of type
2K+2
and have activity
a_{2K+2}=0
.

For
m=0,\ldots,K-1
, if an active vertex
v_{n}
with degree
m
is selected in the tree at step
n-1
, then it either becomes frozen with probability
1-p
(if
x_{n}=-1
), or with probability
p
a new active vertex with outdegree 0 is attached and the outdegree of the vertex is increased by one (if
x_{n}=1
). As a consequence, we see that

\text{for }m=0,\ldots,K-1,\qquad\mathbb{E}\xi_{2m+1,j}=\begin{cases}p&m\neq 0,j=0,\\
-1&m\neq 0,j=2m+1,\\
p-1&m=0,j=0,\\
1-p&j=2m+2,\\
p&j=2m+3.\end{cases}

(6.1)

As for active vertices with outdegree
m\geq K
, with probability
1-p
the vertex becomes frozen (if
x_{n}=-1
), and with probability
p
the vertex remains active and a new active vertex with outdegree 0 is added to the tree (if
x_{n}=+1
). Therefore,

\mathbb{E}\xi_{2K+1}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}p\\
0\\
\vdots\\
0\\
-(1-p)\\
1-p\end{array}\right).

(6.2)

Normal limit law and convergence of moments.
With the Pólya urn setup described above, the vector
X_{n}=(X_{n,1},\ldots,X_{n,2K+1})^{T}
is defined so that
X_{n,2m+1}
is the number of active vertices in
\mathcal{T}_{n}(\bm{x})
with outdegree
m=0,\ldots,K-1
,
X_{2m+2}
is the number of frozen vertices with outdegree
m=0,\ldots,K-1
, and
X_{2K+1}
is the number of active vertices with outdegree
m\geq K
. Note that as defined, we have that the total activity of the urn is given by

S_{n}:=\sum_{j=1}^{2K+2}a_{j}\cdot X_{n,j}=\sum_{j=0}^{K}X_{n,2j+1}=\max\left(0,1+\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\right).

We therefore see that the event of non-extinction up to time
n
is given by

\mathcal{E}^{c}_{n}:=\left\{1+\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{i}>0\text{ for all }0\leq k\leq n\right\}.

We can now prove the following result:

Proposition 6.1
.

Fix
K\geq 1
. Let
\mathcal{T}_{0}(\bm{x}),\mathcal{T}_{1}(\bm{x}),\ldots,\mathcal{T}_{n}(\bm{x}),\ldots,
be a sequence of uniform attachment trees with freezing, where
\bm{x}=(x_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}
is given by i.i.d. distributed
x_{i}
with
\mathbb{P}(x_{i}=+1)=p
and
\mathbb{P}(x_{i}=-1)=1-p
, and let
X_{n}
be defined as above. For all
p\in(1/2,1]
, conditioned on non-extinction,

\frac{X_{n}-\mathbb{E}[X_{n}|\mathcal{E}_{n}^{c}]}{\sqrt{n}}\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)

(6.3)

for some covariance matrix
\Sigma
, with convergence of all conditional moments holding as well. Furthermore, conditioned on non-extinction,

\frac{X_{n}}{n}\xrightarrow{a.s.}(2p-1)v_{1},

where
v_{1}
is given by

v_{1}=\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1-p}{2(2p-1)},\frac{1}{4},\frac{1-p}{4(2p-1)},\ldots,\frac{1}{2^{K}},\frac{1-p}{2^{K}(2p-1)},\frac{1}{2^{K}},\frac{1-p}{2^{K}(2p-1)}\right)^{T}.

Remark 6.2
.

Since multivariate normal limit laws hold if and only if normal limit laws hold for all linear combinations of the components, we see immediately that if we let

Y_{n}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}X_{n,0}+X_{n,1}\\
X_{n,2}+X_{n,3}\\
\vdots\\
X_{n,2K-1}+X_{n,2K}\end{array}\right),

so that
Y_{n,m}
is the number of vertices (both active and frozen) of outdegree
m+1
, then the convergence

\frac{Y_{n}-\mathbb{E}[Y_{n}|\mathcal{E}_{n}^{c}]}{\sqrt{n}}\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma^{\prime})

(6.4)

also holds conditioned on non-extinction, along with the convergence of the conditional moments, for the appropriate covariance matrix
\Sigma^{\prime}
. The joint convergence of infinitely many random variables is by definition the same as joint convergence of any finite subset. Since (
6.3
) and (
6.4
) hold for any arbitrary
K
, we therefore also have a joint normal limit law for the infinite vector of all degrees in
\mathcal{T}_{n}(\bm{x})
.

Proof.

From (
6.1
) and (
6.2
) along with the activities
a_{2m+1}=1
and
a_{2m+2}=0
, the intensity matrix of the urn is given by

A=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccccccc}p-1&0&p&0&\cdots&p&0&p&0\\
1-p&0&0&0&\cdots&0&0&0&0\\
p&0&-1&0&\cdots&0&0&0&0\\
0&0&1-p&0&\cdots&0&0&0&0\\
0&0&p&0&\cdots&0&0&0&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&0&\cdots&-1&0&0&0\\
0&0&0&0&\cdots&1-p&0&0&0\\
0&0&0&0&\cdots&p&0&-(1-p)&0\\
0&0&0&0&\cdots&0&0&1-p&0\end{array}\right)

To find the eigenvalues of
A
, we use the same procedure as
[
10
, Lemma 3.2]
; we perform row and column operations to
A-\lambda I
. For all
i=1,\ldots,2K
, add
a_{i}
times row
i
to row
2K+1
, and afterwards, subtract column
2K+1
from columns
j=1,\ldots,2K
. The resulting matrix is given by

A^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccccccc}-1-\lambda&0&0&0&\cdots&0&0&p&0\\
1-p&-\lambda&0&0&\cdots&0&0&0&0\\
p&0&-1-\lambda&0&\cdots&0&0&0&0\\
0&0&1-p&-\lambda&\cdots&0&0&0&0\\
0&0&p&0&\cdots&0&0&0&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&0&\cdots&-1-\lambda&0&0&0\\
0&0&0&0&\cdots&1-p&-\lambda&0&0\\
0&0&0&0&\cdots&0&0&-(1-2p)-\lambda&0\\
0&0&0&0&\cdots&0&0&1-p&-\lambda\end{array}\right).

Determinants are unchanged after adding/subtracting a row/column to another. Thus we can read off the characteristic polynomial of
A
by finding the determinant of
A^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)
, which by expanding along the last columns and then row
2K-1
, gives

-\lambda^{K+1}(\lambda+1)^{K}(\lambda+1-2p).

The eigenvalues of
A
are then given by
\lambda_{1}=2p-1>0
along with
0
and
-1
.

For the eigenvector
v_{1}
, we solve the system

Av_{1}=(2p-1)v_{1},\qquad\text{and}\qquad a\cdot v_{1}=1.

To start we see that

-v_{1,1}+p\sum_{m=0}^{K}v_{1,2K+1}=(2p-1)v_{1,1}\Longrightarrow 2pv_{1,1}=p(a\cdot v_{1})\Longrightarrow v_{1,1}=\frac{1}{2}.

(6.5)

Then for
m=1,\ldots,K-1
,

pv_{1,2m-1}-v_{1,2m+1}=(1-2p)v_{1,2m+1}\Longrightarrow v_{1,2m+1}=\frac{v_{1,2m-1}}{2},

(6.6)

and so from (
6.5
) and (
6.6
),

\text{for }m=0,\ldots,K-1,\qquad v_{1,2m+1}=\frac{1}{2^{m+1}}.

We also see that

p\,v_{1,2K-1}-(1-p)v_{1,2K+1}=(2p-1)v_{1,2K+1}\Longrightarrow v_{1,2K+1}=v_{1,2K-1}=\frac{1}{2^{K}}.

Finally, for
m=0,\ldots,K
,

(1-p)v_{1,2m+1}=(1-2p)v_{1,2m+2}\Longrightarrow v_{1,2m+2}=\frac{(1-p)v_{1,2m+1}}{2p-1}=\frac{1-p}{(2p-1)2^{m+1}}.

It is easy to verify that our urn satisfies the assumptions (A1) – (A6) of
[
14
]
, and so by
[
14
, Theorem 3.21 & Theorem 3.22]
, conditioned on non-extinction,

\frac{X_{n}}{n}\xrightarrow{a.s.}\lambda_{1}v_{1},\qquad\text{and}\qquad\frac{X_{n}-n\lambda_{1}v_{1}}{\sqrt{n}}\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma).

Next we see that
\mathrm{(U}_{p}\mathrm{)}
holds for all
p\geq 2
. Thus by Theorem
3.3
and Theorem
3.4
, conditioned on non-extinction,

\frac{X_{n}-\mathbb{E}[X_{n}|\mathcal{E}_{n}^{c}]}{\sqrt{n}}\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma),

with the convergence of all conditional moments holding by Theorem
3.2
.
∎

6.2
Hooking networks

Let
\chi\geq 0
and
\rho\in\mathbb{R}
be two fixed parameters so that
\chi+\rho>0
, and let
\mathcal{C}=\{G_{1},G_{2},\ldots,G_{m}\}
be a collection of graphs, called
blocks
, each with a vertex labelled as the
hook

h_{i}
of
G_{i}
, and each assigned a value
p_{i}
so that
p_{1}+p_{2}+\ldots+p_{m}=1
.
Hooking networks
are defined as a sequence of random graphs
\mathcal{G}_{0},\mathcal{G}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{G}_{n},\ldots
constructed recursively as follows. Starting with
\mathcal{G}_{0}
consisting of a single vertex labelled
H
called the
master hook
,
\mathcal{G}_{n}
is constructed from
\mathcal{G}_{n-1}
by first sampling a vertex
v_{n}
called a
latch
from
\mathcal{G}_{n-1}
, where the probability of selecting
v_{n}
is given by

\frac{\chi\deg(v_{n})+\rho}{\sum_{u\in V(\mathcal{G}_{n-1})}\chi\deg(u)+\rho}.

Then a block
B_{n}
is sampled as a copy of one of the graphs in
\mathcal{C}
, where the probability that
B_{n}\simeq G_{i}
is
p_{i}
. The hook of
B_{n}
is then fused with the latch
v_{n}
. Recently, results on the degree distributions of hooking networks have been proved,
[
10
,
20
]
, as have results on the insertion depth; the distance from the block
B_{n}
to the master hook
H

[
9
,
11
]
.

An integer
k
is called an
essential
degree if there is a positive probability that at least two vertices have degree
k
in some
\mathcal{G}_{n}
; it is shown that only the master hook
H
can have a non-essential degree
[
10
, Proposition 3]
. For a positive integer
r
, let
k_{1},\ldots,k_{r}
be the
r
smallest essential degrees that can appear in the sequence of hooking networks, and let
X_{n}=(X_{n,1},\ldots,X_{n,r})
, where
X_{n,i}
is the number of vertices with degree
k_{i}
in
\mathcal{G}_{n}
.

The degree distribution of hooking networks constructed from a single graph in the collection
\mathcal{C}=\{G\}
, called
self-similar hooking networks
, were studied in
[
20
]
. The general case was later studied in
[
10
, Theorem 4]
, where it was shown that

\frac{X_{n}-n\lambda_{1}\nu}{\sqrt{n}}\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)

(6.7)

for
\lambda_{1}
and
\nu
given explicitly in
[
10
, Section 1.3.1]
. The ‘balanced’ case was also examined explicitly, where conditions were given to guarantee convergence of moments
[
10
, Corollary 6]
. In light of our results in Section
3
, convergence of moments can now be guaranteed for any finite collection of graphs
\mathcal{C}
to construct hooking networks.

Proposition 6.3
.

Let
\mathcal{G}_{0},\mathcal{G}_{1},\ldots,
be a sequence of hooking networks constructed as above, and let
X_{n}=(X_{n,1},\ldots,X_{n,r})
be the vector of
r
smallest essential degrees. The convergence (
6.7
) holds with convergence of all moments. Also,
\mathbb{E}X_{n}=n\lambda_{1}\nu+o(\sqrt{n})
, and so
n\lambda_{1}\nu
can be relpaced by
\mathbb{E}X_{n}
in (
6.7
).

Proof.

It is shown in
[
10
, Section 3.1.1]
that
X_{n}
can be described as an urn process, and as is shown in
[
10
, Remark 20]
, the urn is also balanced in expectation. Since the collection
\mathcal{C}
is finite, it is also evident that
\mathrm{(U}_{p}\mathrm{)}
holds for all
p\geq 2
. The proposition then follows from (
6.7
), Theorem
3.3
, Theorem
3.4
, and Theorem
3.2
.
∎
