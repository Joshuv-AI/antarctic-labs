Nonlinear Decay of Fast Magnetosonic Waves through Weak Turbulence: Force-Free Electrodynamics Simulations
Siddhant Solanki, Jens F. Mahlmann, Alexander Philippov

Abstract
We investigate the propagation of low-frequency fast-magnetosonic (FMS) waves in highly magnetized environments. Such conditions are relevant to the escape of GHz fast radio bursts potentially produced in the inner magnetospheres of magnetars. It remains an open question whether such waves can escape without substantial reprocessing. Using relativistic force-free electrodynamics simulations, we confirm the key theoretical predictions of Golbraikh & Lyubarsky 2023 and demonstrate that FMS waves undergo efficient nonlinear conversion into secondary FMS and Alfvén waves via the parametric decay instability. This process continues to drain energy from the primary FMS waves even after approximate energy equipartition between the FMS and Alfvén components is established. The resulting spectrum of excited waves is broad, extending across much of the inertial range in k k -space within the simulation domain. Our results indicate that FMS waves likely do not escape magnetar magnetospheres without substantial dissipation and spectral broadening.



I
Introduction

In the magnetospheres of isolated neutron stars, the electromagnetic (EM) energy density exceeds the plasma’s thermal and rest-mass energy density by many orders of magnitude
(
Goldreich & Julian 1969
)
. Such environments can be modeled using force-free electrodynamics
(
Gruzinov 1999
;
Blandford 2002
, FFE,)
. In this regime, modes with frequencies
\omega<\omega_{p}
, where
\omega_{p}
is the local plasma frequency, behave like magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) waves. FFE supports two linear modes: fast magnetosonic (FMS) and Alfvén waves. When the wave amplitudes are small compared to the background field, AWs propagate along magnetic field lines and remain confined, whereas FMS waves can travel across the field lines and potentially escape the system.

The different behavior of FMS and Alfvén waves constrains theoretical models of Fast Radio Bursts (FRBs). While the emission mechanism of FRBs is still unknown, some bursts are likely powered by magnetars. So-called ‘near-field’ models associate the FRB signal generation with the inner magnetar magnetosphere, at distances of
10
–
10^{2}R_{\rm NS}
from the star, where
R_{\rm NS}\sim 10^{6}
cm is the magnetar radius
(
Kumar & Bošnjak 2020
;
Lu et al. 2020
;
Zhang 2022
;
Zhang 2023
;
Qu & Zhang 2024
)
. At these distances, for plasma densities expected in magnetar magnetospheres
(
Beloborodov 2013
)
, the plasma frequency greatly exceeds
1\,\mathrm{GHz}
, and the wave amplitude remains below the background magnetic field. Thus, propagating FRBs correspond to FMS waves of FFE
(
Golbraikh & Lyubarsky 2023
)
.

As these waves travel in an FFE background, the local electric currents adjust to preserve the orthogonality of electric,
\mathbf{E}
, and magnetic,
\mathbf{B}
, fields,
\mathbf{E}\cdot\mathbf{B}=0
, introducing nonlinearity to the system
(
McKinney 2006
;
Komissarov 2011
)
. The nonlinearity allows for resonant interactions: linear waves can transform into lower-frequency modes (parametric decay) or interact with other linear waves to generate higher-frequency modes
(
Zakharov et al. 1992
)
. This process is commonly described as weak turbulence (WT).

Understanding WT in the relativistic force-free regime is critical for evaluating FRB escape from the inner magnetar magnetosphere. As emphasized by
Lyubarsky 2021
, WT may quench FRB escape: the FMS waves can resonantly transfer their energy to AWs that become trapped along closed field lines. To quantitatively evaluate this proposal,
Golbraikh & Lyubarsky 2023
solved WT spectral equations constructed in the random phase approximation
(
Chandran 2005
, see the non-relativistic framework by)
. Their treatment includes the bidirectional processes
F\leftrightarrow F+A
and
F\leftrightarrow A+A
, where
F
and
A
correspond to FMS and Alfvén waves, respectively. These processes, and their respective inverses, represent the conversion of a high-frequency FMS wave into a lower-frequency FMS wave and an AW, or two AWs. However, the calculations by
Golbraikh & Lyubarsky 2023
do not yet account for potential interactions between two counter-propagating AWs that could lead to weak Alfvénic turbulence
(
Schekochihin et al. 2012
;
Howes & Nielson 2013
;
TenBarge et al. 2021
;
Ripperda et al. 2021a
)
. Once a significant fraction of energy is deposited into AWs from the
F\leftrightarrow F+A
and
F\leftrightarrow A+A
processes, weak Alfvénic turbulence can become important. In this process, counter-propagating AWs interact with a 2D condensate composed of nonlinear modes
(
Schekochihin et al. 2012
)
, making the WT treatment of three-wave interactions challenging. Fully nonlinear force-free simulations complement spectral methods and offer additional insight. Moreover, such simulations conserve Poynting flux, which, as we demonstrate explicitly, is carried in nonlinear fields and cannot be fully captured by a WT approach.

In this work, we simulate WT in force-free magnetospheres using three-dimensional high-order, high-resolution FFE simulations. Our method integrates all nonlinear interactions without pre-selecting dominant resonant channels.

II
Wave Interactions in Magnetar Magnetospheres

As mentioned above, within the inner magnetospheres of magnetars, FRBs correspond to FMS waves of FFE. In the following sections, we discuss how these waves interact and evolve via nonlinear processes.

II.1
Resonant three-wave interactions

Three-wave interactions constitute the lowest-order nonlinear wave-coupling processes and form the foundational elements of WT theory
(
Zakharov et al. 1992
)
. A resonant interaction among three waves, characterized by wavevectors
\mathbf{k}_{1}
,
\mathbf{k}_{2}
, and
\mathbf{k}_{3}
and angular frequencies
\omega_{1}
,
\omega_{2}
, and
\omega_{3}
, is permitted when the following wave-matching (resonance) conditions are satisfied:

\displaystyle\mathbf{k_{1}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{k_{2}}+\mathbf{k_{3}}\,,

(1)

\displaystyle\omega_{1}(\mathbf{k_{1}})

\displaystyle=\omega_{2}(\mathbf{k_{2}})+\omega_{3}(\mathbf{k_{3}}),

(2)

where
\omega_{1}
,
\omega_{2}
, and
\omega_{3}
are determined by the wave dispersion relations of the medium. If the three waves satisfy Equations (
1
) and (
2
), they form a resonant triad. Such resonant interactions require nonvanishing interaction coefficients, which determine the strength of the coupling and depend on the nonlinear properties of the medium supporting the waves.

The FFE dispersion relations for FMS and Alfvén waves in the ultra-magnetized limit are
\omega^{F}=|\mathbf{k}|c
and
\omega^{A}=|\mathbf{k}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{B}}|c=|k_{\parallel}|c
, respectively, where
\hat{\mathbf{B}}
is the unit vector along the background magnetic field. In the most general case, FFE permits the interactions
F\leftrightarrow F+A
,
F\leftrightarrow A+A
, and
A\leftrightarrow A+A
, while the
F\leftrightarrow F+F
and
A\leftrightarrow A+F
interactions are forbidden
(
Thompson & Blaes 1998
;
Li et al. 2019
)
. In Appendix
A
, we compute the interaction rates
V
for the
F\leftrightarrow F+A
and
F\leftrightarrow A+A
processes, denoted by
V_{FFA}
and
V_{FAA}
, respectively, which scale as
V_{FFA}\sim V_{FAA}\sim V\sim\mathcal{O}(\theta)\,\omega^{3/2}B_{0}^{-1}
, where
\mathcal{O}(\theta)
is an order-unity factor depending on the angle between
\mathbf{k}_{1}
and
\mathbf{k}_{2}
, and
B_{0}
is the background magnetic field (see also the appendices of
Lyubarsky 2019
;
Li et al. 2019
). For an FMS wave with wavevector
\mathbf{k}_{1}
decaying into product modes with wavevectors
\mathbf{k}_{2}
and
\mathbf{k}_{3}
, the corresponding matrix coefficients
V_{FFA}
and
V_{FAA}
are given by:

\displaystyle V_{FFA}(\mathbf{k_{1}},\mathbf{k_{2}},\mathbf{k_{3}})=\frac{k_{3,\perp}c^{3/2}}{B_{0}}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}\frac{|k_{3,\parallel}|k_{2}}{k_{1}}}

\displaystyle\times\left(1-\mathrm{sign}(k_{3,\parallel})\frac{k_{2,\parallel}}{k_{2}}\right)\mathbf{\hat{z}}\cdot\left[\mathbf{\hat{k}}_{1,\perp}\times\mathbf{\hat{k}}_{3,\perp}\right]

(3)

\displaystyle V_{FAA}(\mathbf{k_{1}},\mathbf{k_{2}},\mathbf{k_{3}})=\frac{k_{3,\perp}c^{3/2}}{B_{0}}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}\frac{|k_{2,\parallel}||k_{3,\parallel}|}{k_{1}}}

\displaystyle\times\left(1-\mathrm{sign}(k_{2,\parallel}k_{3,\parallel})\right)\mathbf{\hat{k}}_{1,\perp}\cdot\left[\mathbf{\hat{k}}_{2,\perp}+\frac{k_{2,\perp}}{k_{3,\perp}}\mathbf{\hat{k}}_{3,\perp}\right],

(4)

Here,
\mathbf{k}_{3}
denotes the wavevector of the product Alfvén wave in the
F\leftrightarrow F+A
process, and the wavevectors
\mathbf{k}_{i}
satisfy Equation (
1
). Subscripts
{\parallel}
and
\perp
denote the components of the wavevector parallel and perpendicular to the background magnetic field, respectively.

The
F\leftrightarrow A+A
process does not constrain the perpendicular components
k_{(2,3),\perp}
of the excited waves via the resonance conditions (
1
) and (
2
), allowing excitation of modes with
k_{(2,3),\perp}\gg k_{1,\perp}
. This occurs because the frequencies of Alfvén waves are independent of
k_{\perp}
, and highly anisotropic, counter-propagating Alfvén waves with
k_{\perp}\gg k_{\parallel}
and
\mathbf{k_{2}}\approx-\mathbf{k_{3}}
satisfy the three-wave resonance as long as the condition
\mathbf{k_{2,\perp}}+\mathbf{k_{3,\perp}}=\mathbf{k_{1,\perp}}
is fulfilled. Thus, the decay of FMS waves into Alfvén waves with arbitrarily high
k_{\perp}
is possible
(
Golbraikh & Lyubarsky 2023
)
. Moreover, the rate of the
F\to A+A
process in this limit does not depend on
\mathbf{k_{2}}
and
\mathbf{k_{3}}
. Using the wave dispersion relations and the resonance conditions in Equations (
1
) and (
2
), and assuming counter-propagating Alfvén waves, one finds

k_{2,\parallel}=(k_{1}+k_{1,\parallel})/{2}
,
k_{3,\parallel}=({k_{1}-k_{1,\parallel}})/{2}
.
Substituting these expressions into Equation (
4
) and taking
k_{1,\perp}/k_{2,\perp},\,k_{1,\perp}/k_{3,\perp}\ll 1
, the interaction coefficient reduces to:

\displaystyle V_{FAA}(k_{2,\perp},k_{3,\perp}\gg k_{1,\perp})\approx\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\frac{(k_{1}c)^{3/2}\sin^{2}\theta}{B_{0}}\times

\displaystyle(1-2\cos^{2}\Phi),

(5)

Here,
\theta
is the angle between
\mathbf{k}_{1}
and
\hat{\mathbf{B}}
, and
\Phi
is the angle between
\mathbf{k}_{1}
and
\mathbf{k}_{2}
. For a given
\mathbf{k}_{1}
, the maximum value of the interaction coefficient is
\sqrt{{\pi}/{2}}(k_{1}c)^{3/2}{\sin^{2}\theta}/{B_{0}},
which is independent of the ratio
k_{(2,3),\perp}/k_{1,\perp}
.

II.2
Three-wave system

For a set of three resonantly interacting waves (see also Appendix
A
), the evolution equation for the rescaled wave amplitudes,
a_{\mathbf{k}_{i}}
, can be written in the form:

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}t}+i\omega_{1}\right)a_{\mathbf{k}_{1}}

\displaystyle=iVa_{\mathbf{k}_{2}}a_{\mathbf{k}_{3}}

(6)

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}t}+i\omega_{2}\right)a_{\mathbf{k}_{2}}

\displaystyle=iVa_{\mathbf{k}_{1}}a_{\mathbf{k}_{3}}^{*}

(7)

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}t}+i\omega_{3}\right)a_{\mathbf{k}_{3}}

\displaystyle=iVa_{\mathbf{k}_{1}}a_{\mathbf{k}_{2}}^{*},

(8)

where the asterisk denotes complex conjugation. In FFE, the wave amplitude is defined as

a_{\mathbf{k}_{i}}=\frac{\delta E(\mathbf{k}_{i})}{\sqrt{2\pi\omega_{i}}},

where
|\delta E(\mathbf{k}_{i})|
is the amplitude of the electric (or magnetic) field with wavevector
\mathbf{k}_{i}
and angular frequency
\omega_{i}
. The phase of
\delta E(\mathbf{k}_{i})
evolves on a fast timescale, set by the wave angular frequency, and on a slow timescale due to the resonant interactions.

It can be shown from Equations (
6
)–(
8
) that the quantities
\mathcal{F}\equiv|a_{\mathbf{k}_{1}}|^{2}+|a_{\mathbf{k}_{2}}|^{2}
,
\mathcal{G}\equiv|a_{\mathbf{k}_{2}}|^{2}-|a_{\mathbf{k}_{3}}|^{2},
and
\mathcal{H}\equiv iV(a_{\mathbf{k}_{1}}a_{\mathbf{k}_{2}}^{*}a_{\mathbf{k}_{3}}^{*}+a_{\mathbf{k}_{1}}^{*}a_{\mathbf{k}_{2}}a_{\mathbf{k}_{3}})
are conserved. The conservation equations for
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{G}
are called the Manley-Rowe relations, while
\mathcal{H}
is the Hamiltonian of the system with canonical coordinates
q_{i}=a_{\mathbf{k}_{i}}
and
p_{i}=a_{\mathbf{k}_{i}}^{*}
. The terms
|a_{\mathbf{k}_{i}}|^{2}
can be interpreted as the number of mode quanta (or wave occupation number),
n_{\mathbf{k}_{i}}
, with the total energy contained in mode
i
given by
U_{\mathbf{k}_{i}}=n_{\mathbf{k}_{i}}\omega_{\mathbf{k}_{i}}=\omega_{\mathbf{k}_{i}}|a_{\mathbf{k}_{i}}|^{2}.
The independent variables in the three-wave equations (
6
)–(
8
) are
|a_{\mathbf{k}_{i}}|
and
\Delta\phi\equiv\phi_{\mathbf{k}_{1}}-\phi_{\mathbf{k}_{2}}-\phi_{\mathbf{k}_{3}},
where
\phi_{\mathbf{k}_{i}}
are the wave phases. That is, the evolution equations for the individual phases,
\dot{\phi}_{\mathbf{k}_{i}}
, can be determined from the governing equations for
|a_{\mathbf{k}_{i}}|
and
\Delta\phi
. Given the three constants of motion
\mathcal{F},\mathcal{G},\text{ and }\mathcal{H},
the evolution equations are completely integrable.

In the limit where
|a_{\mathbf{k}_{1}}|\gg|a_{\mathbf{k}_{2}}|
and
|a_{\mathbf{k}_{3}}|
, the system is susceptible to the parametric decay instability, in which mode
1
exponentially decays and transfers its energy to modes
2
and
3
. We define the approximate interaction timescale for this process as the ‘e-folding’ time for the growth of modes
2
and
3
in units of the periods of wave
1
:

\frac{t_{\rm int}^{(1)}}{T_{\rm w,1}}\sim\frac{\omega_{1}}{2\pi}(V|{a}_{\mathbf{k_{1}}}|)^{-1}\sim\frac{1}{\pi}\cdot\left(\frac{\sin^{2}(\theta)\delta B_{\mathbf{k_{1}}}}{B_{0}}\right)^{-1}.

(9)

In the last step, we assumed that the interaction coefficient scales according to Equation (
5
).

In this limit,
t_{\rm int}^{(1)}
is
independent
of
|a_{\mathbf{k}_{2}}|
,
|a_{\mathbf{k}_{3}}|
, and
\Delta\phi
. Assuming a narrow spectrum of propagating waves, the parametric decay of FMS modes is the dominant process during their escape from the inner magnetosphere of a magnetar. This process becomes increasingly important as the FMS waves propagate away from the magnetar and
\delta B_{\mathbf{k}_{1}}/B_{0}
grows.

II.3
Evolution of a Spectrum of Resonant Modes

The discussion in the previous section applies to a system composed of only three resonantly interacting waves. For the more general case of a broad spectrum of interacting modes, each mode can participate in multiple resonantly interacting triads. In this case, the three-wave equations must incorporate all possible interactions by expressing Equations (
6
)–(
8
) as integrals, which we do in this section. Following
Golbraikh & Lyubarsky 2023
, we do not consider the

process in the following equations. We first define
\tilde{V}^{F}_{123}
and
\tilde{V}^{A}_{123}

\displaystyle\tilde{V}^{F}_{123}\equiv\quad

\displaystyle V_{FFA}(\mathbf{k_{1}},\mathbf{k_{2}},\mathbf{k_{3}})\delta(\mathbf{k_{1}-k_{2}-k_{3}})

\displaystyle\tilde{V}^{A}_{123}\equiv\quad

(11)

Here,

and

for Alfvén waves.

II.4
Momentum Conservation

In the preceding sections, we discussed how FMS waves can decay into other FMS waves and Alfvén waves via resonant interactions. An important question is whether these product linear modes conserve the Poynting flux (momentum) of the original FMS wave. To address this, we consider the
F\leftrightarrow A+A
process: an FMS wave with wavevector
\mathbf{k}_{1}
transforms into two Alfvén waves with wavevectors
\mathbf{k}_{2}
and
\mathbf{k}_{3}
. Let
\mathbf{S}_{1}
,
\mathbf{S}_{2}
, and
\mathbf{S}_{3}
denote the corresponding Poynting fluxes of the three waves,

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}_{1}

\displaystyle=(c/4\pi)\left\langle\delta E(k_{1})\right\rangle^{2}\mathbf{\hat{k}_{1}}\,\,\,\,=n_{\mathbf{k_{1}}}\omega_{1}\,c\,\mathbf{\hat{k}_{1}}

(17)

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}_{2}

\displaystyle=(c/4\pi)\left\langle\delta E(k_{2})\right\rangle^{2}\mathbf{\hat{k}_{2,z}}=n_{\mathbf{k_{2}}}\omega_{2}\,c\,\mathrm{sign}(k_{2,z})\,\mathbf{\hat{z}}

(18)

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}_{3}

\displaystyle=(c/4\pi)\left\langle\delta E(k_{3})\right\rangle^{2}\mathbf{\hat{k}_{3,z}}=n_{\mathbf{k_{3}}}\omega_{3}\,c\,\mathrm{sign}(k_{3,z})\,\mathbf{\hat{z}},

(19)

where
\delta E(k_{i})
is the electric field amplitude of mode
i
and
\left\langle\cdots\right\rangle
denotes the RMS value over a wave period. Using the conservation of
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{G}
, it follows that
n_{\mathbf{k_{2}}}=\mathcal{F}-n_{\mathbf{k_{1}}}
and
n_{\mathbf{k_{3}}}=\mathcal{F}-\mathcal{G}-n_{\mathbf{k_{1}}}
. Then, the total Poynting flux in the
z
-direction is:

\displaystyle n_{\mathbf{k_{1}}}k_{1,z}+n_{\mathbf{k_{2}}}k_{2,z}+n_{\mathbf{k_{3}}}k_{3,z}=

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(k_{2,z}+k_{3,z})-\mathcal{G}k_{3,z}+n_{\mathbf{k_{1}}}

\displaystyle(k_{1,z}-k_{2,z}-k_{3,z})

(20)

Equation (
1
) implies that the RHS of the above equation is constant, and the three-wave process conserves Poynting flux in
z
:

\displaystyle\frac{\rm d}{\rm dt}(n_{\mathbf{k_{1}}}k_{1,z}+n_{\mathbf{k_{2}}}k_{2,z}+n_{\mathbf{k_{3}}}k_{3,z})=0,

\displaystyle\implies c^{2}\frac{\rm d}{\rm dt}(n_{\mathbf{k_{1}}}k_{1,z}+n_{\mathbf{k_{2}}}k_{2,z}+n_{\mathbf{k_{3}}}k_{3,z})\mathbf{\hat{z}}=\mathbf{0},

\displaystyle\implies c\frac{\rm d}{\rm dt}(n_{\mathbf{k_{1}}}\omega_{1}(\mathbf{\hat{k}_{1}}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{z}})+n_{\mathbf{k_{2}}}\omega_{2}\,\mathrm{sign}(k_{2,z})+

\displaystyle n_{\mathbf{k_{3}}}\omega_{3}\,\mathrm{sign}(k_{3,z}))\mathbf{\hat{z}}=\mathbf{0},

\displaystyle\implies\mathbf{\dot{S}_{1}}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{z}}=-(\mathbf{\dot{S}_{2}}+\mathbf{\dot{S}_{3}}).

Thus, the two Alfvén waves carry the
z
-component of the Poynting flux of the decaying FMS wave. However, if
\mathbf{k}_{1}
has a component perpendicular to the background field, the two Alfvén waves cannot carry
\mathbf{S}_{1,\perp}
since
\mathbf{S}_{2,\perp}=\mathbf{S}_{3,\perp}=\mathbf{0}
.

Momentum conservation still holds in FFE, and the ‘missing’ Poynting flux resides in higher-order fields that are not linear modes of the system. Specifically,
\mathbf{S}_{1,\perp}
is accounted for by the Poynting flux produced by a spatially and temporally uniform electric field
\mathbf{\bar{E}^{(2)}}
with
\omega=0
and
\mathbf{k}=\mathbf{0}
. The corresponding Poynting flux from this nonlinear electric field is given by
\mathbf{S}_{\rm NL}=(c/4\pi)\,\mathbf{\bar{E}^{(2)}}\times B_{0}\,\mathbf{\hat{z}}
. In Appendix
B
, we show that

\mathbf{S}_{1,\perp}(t)+\mathbf{S}_{\rm NL}(t)=\mathbf{S}_{1,\perp}(t=0)=\mathrm{const.}

(21)

In summary, when FMS waves decay into two Alfvén waves, they also produce nonlinear electromagnetic fields carrying Poynting flux along
\mathbf{\hat{k}}_{1,\perp}
, ensuring momentum conservation. A similar process occurs for initially broad spectra of FMS waves. Fully nonlinear FFE simulations show that when the FMS waves deposit most of their energy into Alfvén waves, the perpendicular momentum of the original FMS waves is carried solely by the nonlinear fields,
\mathbf{S}_{\rm NL}
.

III.1
Initial conditions

Our simulations initialize a background magnetic field
\mathbf{B}_{0}\equiv B_{0}\mathbf{\hat{z}}
, on which we superimpose different wave modes with amplitudes
0\leq\xi_{\mathbf{k}}<1
and random phases
\phi_{\mathbf{k}}\in[0,2\pi]
. The total EM fields in the simulation are
\mathbf{E}=\delta\mathbf{E}=\sum_{i=A,F}\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\xi_{i}(\mathbf{k})\cos(\mathbf{k\cdot r}+\phi_{i})\mathbf{\hat{e}}_{i}
and
\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{B}_{0}+\delta\mathbf{B}=B_{0}\mathbf{\hat{z}}+\sum_{i=A,F}\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\xi_{i}(\mathbf{k})\cos(\mathbf{k\cdot r}+\phi_{i})\mathbf{\hat{b}}_{i}
, where
\mathbf{\hat{e}}_{i}
and
\mathbf{\hat{b}}_{i}
are the polarization vectors for the electric and magnetic fields of FMS and Alfvén waves, respectively (see Appendix
A
).
3
3

3

Since the sum of linear modes is not necessarily force-free, i.e., does not exactly satisfy
\mathbf{E\cdot B}=0
at higher order in wave amplitudes, we enforce condition (
23
) before the first timestep. This correction introduces low-amplitude, transient structures that have no dynamical importance at later times.

In the first set of simulations (Section
III.3
), we initialize the FMS and AWs with two different distributions of wavevectors: FMS waves start with a Gaussian spectrum and AWs with a flat spectrum, where each wavevector
\mathbf{k}
carries the same energy. Wave amplitudes for the Gaussian spectra are computed as
\xi(\mathbf{k})=A_{0}\exp(-|\mathbf{k-\bar{\mathbf{k}}}|^{2}/(2\sigma_{k}^{2}))
, where
A_{0}
is a normalization constant,
\bar{\mathbf{k}}
is the mean wavevector, and
\sigma_{k}
is the width of the spectrum.
A_{0}
is set such that the total FMS wave energy matches a target value at the start of the simulation. We ensure that
U_{\rm w}/U_{\rm bg}\lesssim 1
, where
U_{\rm w}
is the total wave energy summing all modes,
U_{\rm w}=\sum_{i=A,F}\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\xi_{i}^{2}(\mathbf{k})/2
, and
U_{\rm bg}=B_{0}^{2}/2
is the energy of the background magnetic field.

We perform an additional simulation (Section
III.4
) in which the FMS and AW energies are initialized in equipartition. The initial FMS spectrum is Gaussian, centered at
\bar{\mathbf{k}}_{1}=(\bar{\mathbf{k}}_{1,\perp},\bar{k}_{1,z})
, with a peak frequency
\omega_{1}=|\bar{\mathbf{k}}_{1}|
. The AW spectrum is initialized with two Gaussians centered at
\bar{\mathbf{k}}_{2}=(\bar{\mathbf{k}}_{1,\perp},\frac{1}{2}(|\bar{\mathbf{k}}_{1}|+|\bar{k}_{1,z}|))
and
\bar{\mathbf{k}}_{3}=(\bar{\mathbf{k}}_{1,\perp},\frac{1}{2}(|\bar{\mathbf{k}}_{1}|-|\bar{k}_{1,z}|))
, chosen such that their frequencies satisfy the resonance condition
\omega_{1}=\omega_{2}+\omega_{3}
.

III.2
Scale separation requirements

On a numerical grid with finite resolution, the minimally expected frequency mismatch in the wave triads is of the order
\Delta\omega\sim 2\pi c/L
. The waves thus lose resonance on a timescale
t_{\rm dec}\sim 2\pi/\Delta\omega\lesssim L/c
. We identify the requirements for mildly nonlinear FMS waves with a wavelength
\lambda_{i}
to efficiently participate in resonant three-wave interactions. First, the nonlinear interaction timescales,
t^{(1)}_{\rm int}
and
t^{(2)}_{\rm int}
, must be shorter than
t_{\rm dec}
. For narrow spectra, Equation (
9
) demands
\delta B_{\mathbf{k_{i}}}/B_{0}\gtrsim\lambda_{i}/L
; for broad spectra, Equation (
16
) requires
\delta B_{\mathbf{k_{i}}}/B_{0}\gtrsim(\lambda_{i}/L)^{1/2}
. Studying mildly nonlinear waves therefore requires a significant separation between the box size and the FMS wavelength. Second, the FMS wavelength and grid scale must be well separated to allow for a large range of
k
of excited waves, such that
\lambda_{i}\gg\Delta_{xyz}
, where
\Delta_{xyz}=L/N_{xyz}
is the grid cell size. The initial simulation conditions satisfy the scale requirement
d_{xyz}<\lambda_{i}<L
, with
\lambda_{i}/L\sim 2\times 10^{-2}
and
\lambda_{i}/\Delta_{xyz}\sim 20
.

IV
Discussion and Conclusions

In this paper, we performed direct numerical simulations of the parametric decay of a mildly non-linear spectrum of FMS waves via the
F\longleftrightarrow F+A
and
F\longleftrightarrow A+A
interactions. These interactions involve non-local energy transfer in
k
-space: AWs with large
k_{\perp}
can be excited directly from FMS waves with smaller
k_{\perp}
. Our results are in agreement with theoretical predictions from
Lyubarsky 2021
and spectral calculations presented in
Golbraikh & Lyubarsky 2023
, which suggest that in the inner magnetar magnetosphere, FMS waves are efficiently converted into an Alfvénic continuum. We find that even for comparable energies of FMS and Alfvén waves, the parametric decay of the FMS wave remains the dominant interaction process. FRB-like signals produced deep in magnetar magnetospheres are therefore likely to suffer substantial energy loss before escaping.

Low-frequency electromagnetic waves can lose energy through several mechanisms as they propagate through magnetized plasma. In the magnetar context, these include:
(i) an enhanced scattering cross-section for waves whose amplitudes exceed the background magnetic field
(
Beloborodov 2021
;
Lyutikov 2021
;
Qu et al. 2022
;
Beloborodov 2024
;
Huang & Dai 2024
;
Nishiura et al. 2025
)
,
(ii) the formation of shocks when the electric field approaches the strength of the magnetic field
(
Beloborodov 2023
;
Mahlmann et al. 2024
;
Bernardi et al. 2025
;
Vanthieghem & Levinson 2025
)
, and
(iii) parametric decay via weak turbulence
(
Lyubarsky 2021
;
Golbraikh & Lyubarsky 2023
)
, which is the focus of this paper. Among these processes, weak turbulence is particularly notable because it can operate even when the FMS wave is only mildly non-linear, transferring energy into Alfvén waves via the
F\longleftrightarrow A+A
process.
4
4

4

It is additionally insensitive to the plasma velocity along the magnetic field, as EM field dynamics in FFE are unaffected by parallel plasma motion, provided sufficient current is supplied.
This poses a significant challenge for near-field FRB models, which rely on the propagation of FMS waves through the inner magnetosphere.

Our simulations were performed in a periodic box to allow direct comparison with existing analytical work. In realistic magnetospheres, significant amplification of low-amplitude background AWs can occur only if they spatially overlap with the outgoing FMS pulse. Since the group velocity of Alfvén waves is aligned with the background magnetic field and approaches the speed of light, the interaction time is limited. In a companion paper (Solanki et al., in preparation), we confirm that the conclusions drawn in this work remain valid for the propagation of more realistic FMS pulses with finite envelope lengths.

F\longleftrightarrow F+A
and
F\longleftrightarrow A+A
mode coupling in FFE (similar derivations have been done in the Appendices of
Lyubarsky 2019
;
Li et al. 2019
). As the simplest case, we consider a system with an FMS wave with wavevector
\mathbf{k}_{1}
, an Alfvén wave with a wavevector
\mathbf{k}_{2}
, and a third mode, which can either be an FMS or an Alfvén wave, with a wavevector
\mathbf{k}_{3}
. We will further assume the three waves satisfy the resonance conditions
\mathbf{k}_{1}=\mathbf{k}_{2}+\mathbf{k}_{3}
and
\omega_{\mathbf{k}_{1}}=\omega_{\mathbf{k}_{2}}+\omega_{\mathbf{k}_{3}}
.

Combining Ampere’s and Faraday’s Laws, we can write a wave equation for the total electric field:

\displaystyle c^{2}\mathbf{\nabla}\times\mathbf{(\mathbf{\nabla}\times\mathbf{\mathbf{E}})}+\frac{\partial^{2}\mathbf{E}}{\partial t^{2}}=-4\pi\frac{\partial\mathbf{J}}{\partial t},

(A1)

which is supplemented by the force-free current:

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}=\frac{c}{4\pi B^{2}}\left[\left(\mathbf{\nabla}\cdot\mathbf{E}\right)\hskip 5.0pt\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{B}+\mathbf{B}\left(\mathbf{B}\cdot(\mathbf{\nabla}\times\mathbf{B})-\mathbf{E}\cdot(\mathbf{\nabla}\times\mathbf{E})\right)\right].

(A2)

We perform the calculations in a quasi-linear fashion, where we expand the electric and magnetic fields up to second order. The zeroth-order background field is taken along the
z
-axis and has a magnitude
B_{0}
. The total electric and magnetic fields in the system are given by
\mathbf{E}=\mathbf{E}^{(1)}+\mathbf{E}^{(2)}
and
\mathbf{B}=B_{0}\mathbf{\hat{z}}+\mathbf{B}^{(1)}+\mathbf{B}^{(2)}
, respectively.
The fields of the three plane waves sum up to
\mathbf{E}^{(1)}
and
\mathbf{B}^{(1)}
, where each wave has a slowly amplitude and phase given by phasors
\chi_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}(t)
and polarizations
\mathbf{\delta\hat{E}}(\mathbf{k}^{\prime})
and
\mathbf{\delta\hat{B}}(\mathbf{k}^{\prime})
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}={\mathbf{k}_{1},\mathbf{k}_{2},\mathbf{k}_{3}}}\chi_{\mathbf{k^{\prime}}}(t)e^{i(\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\cdot\mathbf{r}-\omega_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}t)}\mathbf{\delta\hat{E}}(\mathbf{k}^{\prime})+\mathrm{c.c.}

(A3)

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}={\mathbf{k}_{1},\mathbf{k}_{2},\mathbf{k}_{3}}}\chi_{\mathbf{k^{\prime}}}(t)e^{i(\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\cdot\mathbf{r}-\omega_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}t)}\mathbf{\delta\hat{B}}(\mathbf{k}^{\prime})+\mathrm{c.c.}.

(A4)

The polarizations for FMS and Alfvén waves with a wavevector
\mathbf{k}^{\prime}
are given by:

\displaystyle\delta\mathbf{\hat{E}}^{(1)}_{\rm F}(\mathbf{k^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{\hat{k}^{\prime}}_{\perp}\times\mathbf{\hat{z}}

(A5)

\displaystyle\delta\mathbf{\hat{B}}^{(1)}_{\rm F}(\mathbf{k^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=\frac{k^{\prime}_{z}}{k^{\prime}}\mathbf{\hat{k}^{\prime}}_{\perp}-\frac{k^{\prime}_{\perp}}{k^{\prime}}\mathbf{\hat{z}}

(A6)

\displaystyle\delta\mathbf{\hat{E}}^{(1)}_{\rm A}(\mathbf{k^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{\hat{k}^{\prime}}_{\perp}\qquad

(A7)

\displaystyle\delta\mathbf{\hat{B}}^{(1)}_{\rm A}(\mathbf{k^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{sign}(k^{\prime}_{z})\mathbf{\hat{z}}\times\mathbf{\hat{k}^{\prime}}_{\perp}\qquad.

(A8)

Additionally, the
\mathbf{\hat{z}}
component of
\mathbf{E}^{(2)}
is constrained from the
\mathbf{E}\cdot\mathbf{B}=0
condition. That is,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}^{(2)}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{z}}=-\frac{\mathbf{E}^{(1)}\cdot\mathbf{B}^{(1)}}{B_{0}}

(A9)

Expanding the non-linear current using the electric and magnetic fields, and keeping only the second order terms, the evolution equation for the electric field in Eq. (
A1
) now becomes:

\displaystyle c^{2}(\nabla\times(\nabla\times{\mathbf{E}^{(2)}}))+\frac{\partial^{2}\mathbf{E}^{(2)}}{\partial t^{2}}-\sum_{\mathbf{k^{\prime}}}\left(2i\omega_{\mathbf{k^{\prime}}}\frac{\partial\chi_{\mathbf{k^{\prime}}}}{\partial t}e^{i(\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\cdot\mathbf{r}-\omega_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}t)}\mathbf{\delta\hat{E}(\mathbf{k^{\prime}})}+\mathrm{c.c.}\right)=-4\pi\frac{\partial\mathbf{J}^{(2)}}{\partial t},

(A10)

where the sum runs over
\mathbf{k}_{1},\mathbf{k}_{2}
and
\mathbf{k}_{3}
, and the second-order non-linear current is given by:

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}^{(2)}=\frac{c}{4\pi B_{0}}\Big[

\displaystyle(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E}^{(1)})(\mathbf{E}^{(1)}\times\mathbf{\hat{z}})+(\mathbf{\hat{z}}\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf{B}^{(1)}))\mathbf{B}^{(1)}

(A11)

\displaystyle+\mathbf{\hat{z}}\Big(\mathbf{B}^{(1)}\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf{B}^{(1)})-\mathbf{E}^{(1)}\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf{E}^{(1)})

\displaystyle-2(\mathbf{B}^{(1)}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{z}})(\nabla\times\mathbf{B}^{(1)})_{z}+B_{0}(\nabla\times\mathbf{B}^{(2)})_{z}\Big)\Big].

We want to solve for
\frac{\partial\chi_{\mathbf{k^{\prime}}}}{\partial t}
in Eq. (
A10

\mathbf{\delta\hat{E}}(\mathbf{k}^{\prime})

\mathbf{k}^{\prime}=\mathbf{k}_{1},\mathbf{k}_{2}

\mathbf{k}_{3}

\mathbf{k}_{3}

(A14)

(A15)

\displaystyle=iV_{\rm FFA}a_{\mathbf{k}_{1}}a_{\mathbf{k}_{3}}^{*}

(A16)

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\rm d}{\rm dt}+i\omega_{\mathbf{k}_{3}}\right)a_{\mathbf{k}_{3}}

which are of the same form as Eqs. (

Appendix B
Momentum Conservation

The total momentum in the product FMS and AWs in three-wave interactions is insufficient to account for the total momentum of the initial FMS wave. However, FFE maintains momentum conservation by creating a second-order, non-linear electric field
\mathbf{\bar{E}^{(2)}}
, so that
\mathbf{S}_{\rm NL}\equiv(c/4\pi)\,\mathbf{\bar{E}^{(2)}}\times\mathbf{B_{0}}
accounts for the change in perpendicular momentum of the decaying FMS wave. This electric field has a spatially uniform component
\mathbf{k}=0
, and is produced via non-linear currents at the third order
5
5

5

Third-order currents are necessary to get a zero-wavevector term via the wavevector matching condition. That is, the terms of the form
A_{1}A_{2}A_{3}e^{i(\mathbf{(k_{1}-k_{2}-k_{3})\cdot r})}
in the non-linear current, where
A_{i}\equiv|\chi_{\mathbf{k}_{i}}|
are the wave amplitudes, will produce
\mathbf{k}=0
electric fields.
.

). The top panel in Fig.

and grows the two AWs at the expense of its energy.

There are a few steps involved in the generation of
\mathbf{\bar{E}^{(2)}}
: initially, first-order linear waves interact and produce non-linear second-order electric and magnetic fields
\mathbf{E^{(2)}}
and
\mathbf{B^{(2)}}
with
\omega\neq 0

\mathbf{J^{(3)}}
, which has a
\omega=0
and
\mathbf{k}=(0,0,0)

\mathbf{\bar{J}^{(3)}}

\omega=0
and
\mathbf{k}=(0,0,0)

and becomes second-order in wave-amplitudes:
\mathbf{\bar{E}^{(2)}}=-(4\pi/c)\int_{0}^{t^{(1)}_{\rm int}}\mathbf{\bar{J}^{(3)}}dt
. It is the Poynting flux from
\mathbf{\bar{E}^{(2)}}
that balances out the missing Poynting flux from the initial FMS wave.

(B1)

Appendix D
Non-resonant Three-wave Interactions

The parametric decay and the merger of two lower frequency modes into a higher frequency one in the
F\longleftrightarrow F+A
and
F\longleftrightarrow A+A
interactions require the resonance conditions from Equations (
1
) and (
2
) to be satisfied. In this section, we explore how a frequency mismatch
\Delta\omega\equiv\omega-\omega_{1}-\omega_{2}
affects the amplitudes of two modes merging into a high-frequency product mode. Physically, the decorrelation timescale
t_{\rm dec}\sim\frac{2\pi}{\Delta\omega}
must be longer than the growth timescale
t^{(1)}_{\rm int}
of the waves to resonantly transfer energy between the modes (e.g., see Eq. (56) of
Li et al. 2019
). We verify this by numerically integrating Equations (
6
)-(
8
) by including a
e^{i\Delta\omega t}
term in the matrix element for three different triads of waves with different values of
\Delta\omega
. The wavevectors and initial amplitudes for each of the three triads are given in the Table
3
.

The absolute values of the square wave amplitudes as a function of time are plotted in Figure

. Each color represents
|a_{i}|^{2}
, where
a_{i}
is the phasor for mode
i
. The degree of mismatch, i.e.,
|\Delta\omega|
, increases from left to right. The solid lines are the numerically integrated solutions, where we use RK-4 integration to advance the time. We also perform force-free simulations for the same set of initial conditions, and the dots represent values taken from the simulation. The simulations are in good agreement with the numerically integrated solutions of the non-resonant three-wave equations.

Since the waves are simulated on a discrete grid, the number of possible wave frequencies is also discretized. This limits the amount of resonant triads of fast and Alfvén waves. Furthermore, the distinct dispersion relations of the fast and Alfvén waves imply that the frequency-matching condition is usually not met. More concretely, the possible wavevectors on the grid are integer multiples of
\frac{2\pi}{L}
, where
L
is the length of the grid. From the dispersion relation of Alfvén waves, their frequencies on the grid are of the form
\omega=|k_{z}c|=\frac{2\pi c|m|}{L}
, where
m\in\mathbb{Z}
and
c
is the speed of light. The possible fast wave frequencies instead take the form
\omega=|kc|=\frac{2\pi c}{L}\sqrt{m_{1}^{2}+m_{2}^{2}+m_{3}^{2}}
, with
\{m_{1},m_{2},m_{3}\}\in\mathbb{Z}
. The term in the square roots is, in general, not an integer. Therefore, the typical frequency mismatch between a fast wave and a pair of Alfvén waves is
\Delta\omega\sim\frac{2\pi c}{L}
, so that
\frac{\Delta\omega}{\omega}\sim\frac{\lambda}{L}
, where
\lambda
is the wavelength of the fast wave. Such a triad of waves de-correlates on a timescale of
t_{\rm dec}\equiv\frac{2\pi}{\Delta\omega}\sim\frac{2\pi L}{\omega\lambda}
. For resonant growth to occur, the decorrelation timescale must be longer than the nonlinear interaction timescale. Using the estimates from
II.1
, this implies that when
\delta B/B_{0}\lesssim\frac{\lambda}{2\pi L}
, modes stop growing resonantly. Tackling the above issue requires a scale separation between the box size, wavelength of interacting waves, and dissipative scale. The former allows resonant interactions to occur, and the latter ensures that there is sufficient phase space for the waves to cascade to smaller scales.
