 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

 This project develops and pilots a Continuous Improvement (CI) Process Guide and
supporting data‑capture system for Los Alamos National Laboratory (LANL) as it transitions
from a research and development environment to a production environment. The core finding is
that LANL’s existing approach to production improvements is informal and fragmented:
improvement ideas move through different paths depending on which group is involved,
ownership and approval requirements are often unclear, and critical knowledge is lost with
employees changing roles because LANL is experiencing great growth. The project shows that
by formalizing the CI process, defining clear roles, and using a simple, quantitative scoring
system tied to key manufacturing indicators, LANL can prioritize higher‑impact improvements
and combine improvement incorporation to reduce non‑conformances, process cycle time,
paperwork, increase process success rate, and achieve a positive financial return.

 The literature indicates that organizations frequently struggle with CI because they chase
easy wins, lack a structured method, and fail to build an organization‑wide CI mindset, which
leads to short‑lived efforts and loss of momentum. Successful CI programs instead rely on broad
participation, transparent data capture, clear responsibilities, and disciplined follow‑through on
known improvements. These lessons guided the design of a mixed‑methods confirmatory study
at LANL that combined qualitative interviews with quantitative performance data to create a
practical, sustainable CI framework.

 The project’s methods focused on a single production inspection process as a pilot area.
Qualitative data was collected through interviews with Design Agency (DA), Product
Engineering (PE), Process Engineering (PrE), and Quality Engineering (QE) teams to map how
improvements are currently proposed, reviewed, and implemented, and to gather a set of known
improvement opportunities. This data was used to construct a detailed CI flowchart that traces
more than 50 steps and handoffs across groups, and to develop a scoring system that rates each
improvement on its expected impact on four key indicators. The scoring system was iteratively
reviewed with representatives from each group to reduce bias and ensure that no single team’s
priorities dominate the ranking.

 The results show that the new CI framework can convert scattered ideas into an ordered,
defensible list of improvements. Twelve interviews produced 38 distinct improvement
suggestions, focused on a specific inspection process, confirming both high need and broad
support for change. Applying the scoring matrix identified one improvement as the highest
priority. Analysis of sample and historical data indicates that implementing this improvement is
projected to prevent 5 non‑conformances per year, reduce inspection cycle time by 37.3 percent,
eliminate 166 hours of paperwork annually, and increase the success rate by 61.9 percent.

 The financial analysis translates these operational gains into a return on investment for
LANL. The project required 172 hours of effort, resulting in a total fully burdened cost estimated

 4

between 45,150 and 51,600 dollars, which is lower than the original 54,600 to 62,400 dollar cost
estimate. Valuing only the 166 saved hours per year from the top‑ranked improvement, and using
LANL’s burdened labor rate, yields annual savings of approximately 43,575 dollars and a
three‑year cumulative savings of about 130,725 dollars. Comparing this benefit to the project
cost results in a three‑year ROI of roughly 190 percent and a payback period of about 1.04 years,
even before accounting for additional benefits from reduced non‑conformances and improved
throughput.

 In conclusion, the project found that LANL would greatly benefit from adopting the
Continuous Improvement Process Guide and the associated scoring and data‑capture tools as
standard elements of its production environment. This framework provides a clear, shared
method for recording and prioritizing improvements, reduces knowledge loss, and strengthens
cross‑functional collaboration. The demonstrated ROI from a single high‑impact improvement,
combined with the potential to apply the same approach to additional processes, indicates that
continued use and expansion of this CI system will yield ongoing operational and financial
benefits for LANL as it matures as a production process.

 1. INTRODUCTION

1.1 Project Proposal

A. Introduction

 Los Alamos National Laboratory (LANL) is adapting from a research and design (R&D)
facility to a production facility; with this transition, there has been an issue in implementing
production improvements. The purpose of this project is to develop a continuous improvement
guide that captures the necessary improvements and provides a framework for implementing
them.

B. Problem

 LANL is changing from an R&D facility to a production facility. This means that
LANL’s mindset also needs to change. In an R&D mindset, innovation is prioritized over
refinement and efficiency. Now that LANL is focusing on production, LANL needs a formal
way to record improvements. Making changes to production requires multiple levels of testing
and approval, depending on the process and its owner. The improvement needed for a given
process may involve one or more stakeholders, each of whom may require a different approval
process to implement the change. Identifying who owns the issues and who is responsible for
implementing and approving changes to the production process is cumbersome and challenging,
especially for new employees. Through normal attrition and adding numerous additional
positions, resulting in numerous new hires. Without a formal system to capture improvement
information, it is lost when a person leaves LANL or relocates to a new position within LANL,
which means the new employee must rework the same issue again, rather than building on where
the last person left off.

C. Project Overview

 This project will create a space to record and track the progress of necessary
improvements and develop a guide to identify the correct approval process for implementing
these improvements. The first step will be creating a template in Excel to track known issues that
need to be addressed. The continuous improvement (CI) coordinator will record suggestions and
improvements, and employees within production will be able to make new suggestions. Each

 7

major stakeholder group in production will need to be interviewed to gather initial improvements
and identify the approval process for implementing them within each group. Once this
information is collected, it will be sorted into a process flowchart. After the flowchart and data
capture method are created, the CI coordinator will manage the list of improvements and ensure
that the owning party actively addresses the recorded improvements and follows the correct
approval process.

D. Metrics

 The success of the project will be measured by tracking the current production Key
Performance Indicators (KPIs) and comparing them to the KPIs after each improvement is made.
The first phase of this project will involve determining manufacturing metric indicators that best
serve the current lab mission and tracking and recording the current state of each, thereby
creating a benchmark to review as new improvements are implemented.

E. Summary

 This project will capture critical data to improve production by stopping unnecessary
rework and creating a formal process to track and implement production improvements and to
see their effects on KPIs.

1.2 Literature Review

A. Background

 To build and implement a Continuous Improvement (CI) program at LANL, the
motivation needs to be defined. Why is continuous improvement significant? Along with
motivation, the various methodologies for continuous improvement need to be explored to
determine which is best suited to be implemented at LANL to be an effective, sustainable, and
successful.

B. Why Companies Struggle to Implement Continuous Improvement?

 Implementing Continuous Improvement seems like an obvious thing for every company
to do. Who wouldn’t want to get more for less, but in reality, implementing continuous
improvement is difficult and requires a culture shift [1]. This means that there isn’t just a team
working on CI, but the organization needs to have a CI mindset. Without a systematic approach,
CI can lose support quickly [2]. Often, when CI is attempted, the easiest items are attacked first,
which seems appealing; however, without a structured approach, the momentum from the early
success will fade, leading to only short-term success. The lack of sustained results leads to a
decrease in support for CI within the organization [2]. Without sustained support for CI, there is
no time to build an organizational culture that understands the value and importance of CI.

 8

C. Successful Continuous Improvement Methodologies

 The implementation of CI may vary between companies, but the methodology is similar
across many organizations. CI is most successful when the entire company can participate in CI
[3]. Successful CI programs take a structured approach [2]. A significant component of enabling
LANL to move towards CI is creating a flowchart that guides each employee in participating in
CI [3]. This flowchart will create a path to transfer knowledge between groups and act as an aid
so that each team knows what the other team is responsible for and what is required for each
team to implement an identified improvement. Defining roles and responsibilities and giving
everyone access to the CI process is crucial for success [4]. Once the roles are well defined, a
data capture will be created to record potential improvements. This allows a systematic approach
to be taken on the implementation of the improvements. Rather than each group working blindly,
there will be a single point where all information converges, and the entire organization can have
access [1]. The final element that needs to be considered is the follow-through. The final step in
CI is to follow through and implement the identified issues [5]. For CI to be successful long-
term, there needs to be a plan for addressing the potential improvement that has been recorded.

D. How to Implement Continuous Improvement Successfully.

 To successfully implement CI, everyone needs to be involved, and sufficient
Benchmarking needs to be created. CI needs to be integral to the design of each process [6]. This
project will act as a modified Kaizen event. A Kaizen event is a short-term, focused team with an
accelerated timeframe to improve a targeted area of a company [7]. In this modified version, this
project will act as a pilot focused on one area of production. Data will need to be collected
throughout the project to measure success [8]. Creating the right benchmarking will be a pivotal
element in the project’s success, so all available historical, current, and future data will need to
be collected [9]. Group participation will be a key benchmark indicator for the project’s
sustainability [10]. CI is only as good as the input it receives from the production floor. If certain
groups aren’t participating, CI can hurt overall performance by handicapping a less active group,
which will cause resistance to CI and ultimately lead to a failure to maintain a CI program.

E. Expected Results

 The implementation of CI is associated with two primary benefits: increased production
and decreased non-conformances [11, 12]. In one documented case, a manufacturing process
experienced a 13.3% increase in cycle time, while specific process improvements resulted in
efficiency gains of up to 50% [11]. Furthermore, establishing a sustainable CI program facilitates
the identification of the root causes of non-conformances, allowing for the necessary adjustments
to be made to reduce their occurrence [12].

 9

F. Implications

 The key implications that need to be highlighted are the importance of participation in the
CI program. This project needs to be cross-functional between all the teams that support
production. Each team needs the ability to make input and see how the CI will affect their
process. This means that the data in SharePoint will need to be available to each team. This will
create an environment where a CI culture can grow. Participation in the CI program will also
serve as an indicator of the program's sustainability. The flowchart will act as a bridge between
each team, enabling them to understand the process of implementing an improvement and the
effort required to enact it. The final element that will significantly improve the project’s
performance is leaning on a structured approach to CI priority. Just because something might be
easy to implement does not justify it to be high on the priority list. When implementing each
recorded CI, there needs to be a benefit analysis to determine which improvement will make the
most of the available resources. Though initial results might be delayed, taking the time to
formalize and structure a process for CI will enable the project to build a good reputation and
build confidence within the company that the data being captured will result in beneficial action.

1.3 Business Case

A. Problem

 LANL is transitioning from a research and development environment to a production
environment, and this shift has exposed significant gaps in how production improvements are
identified, documented, and implemented. In the current state, improvement ideas move through
a convoluted and largely informal process, where ownership is often unclear, and the path to
approval depends on which group happens to be involved. Multiple stakeholders, such as the
Design Agency (DA), Product Engineering (PE), Process Engineering (PrE), and Quality
Engineering (QE), may each require different levels of authorization, which makes it difficult for
new and existing employees to determine who is responsible for approving a change. As LANL
experiences immense growth and changes in staff members, critical knowledge is lost, forcing
new employees to rediscover and rework problems that had already been identified instead of
building on prior work. Without a formal, visible system for capturing and executing
improvements, production struggles to refine its processes, protect knowledge, and consistently
meet LANL’s evolving manufacturing mission.

B. Deliverables

 This business case proposes two primary deliverables that together establish a structured
CI system for production. The first deliverable is a Continuous Improvement Process Guide that
clearly defines how improvement opportunities are recorded, evaluated, approved, and
implemented, including the roles and responsibilities of each stakeholder group. The second
deliverable is a data‑capture tool that serves as a central repository for improvement ideas,
associated process data, and status tracking. The CI coordinator will use this tool to record

 10

known issues, log new suggestions from the production floor, and ensure that each improvement
is routed to the correct owner and follows the appropriate approval path.

C. Proposed Execution

 The proposed execution follows a phased approach using a mixed‑methods methodology.
In Phase 1, the team will work with leadership to identify key manufacturing indicators that best
represent LANL’s current production priorities, such as paperwork and inspection cycle times,
failed runs, and non‑conformance counts. Phase 2 will establish a baseline by gathering data on
these indicators and creating the SharePoint environment, including the initial templates for
recording process information and suggested improvements. In Phase 3, interviews with Design
Agency, Product Engineering, Process Engineering, and Quality Engineering will be used to
document the existing improvement‑incorporation process and to collect a first set of
improvement opportunities, which will be organized into a process flowchart. Phase 4 focuses on
developing and refining an improvement‑scoring system that aligns each suggestion with the key
manufacturing indicators, using iterative feedback from the stakeholder groups to reduce bias
and ensure that the scoring remains balanced. Finally, Phase 5 will use the guide and scoring
system to implement selected improvements, monitor participation across groups, and compare
post‑implementation performance against the established baseline.

D. Improvement Scoring

 The improvement‑scoring approach is intended to move LANL away from an ad hoc
method of choosing which ideas to pursue and toward a data‑driven prioritization framework.
When an improvement suggestion is submitted to the data capture tool, it will be evaluated
against the key manufacturing indicators, including its projected impact on cycle time, reduction
of failed runs and non‑conformances, and potential effect on cross‑group coordination. Each
improvement will receive a quantitative score that reflects both its expected benefit and its
resource requirements, allowing the CI coordinator and leadership to rank improvements. This
structure is designed to avoid the common pitfall of implementing the “easiest” changes first,
regardless of their strategic value, and instead directs attention to the improvements most likely
to advance LANL’s production mission.

E. Return on Investment (ROI)

 The anticipated return on investment for the Continuous Improvement Process Guide is
realized through increased production efficiency, reduced rework, and improved reliability in
meeting program demands. By capturing and reusing improvement knowledge rather than
allowing it to leave with individual employees, LANL can avoid repeated troubleshooting of the

 11

same issues and shorten the time from problem identification to resolution. The standardized
process and scoring system also support the “speed of trust” within the organization: as
stakeholders see that recorded improvements are handled consistently and fairly, confidence in
the CI program grows, which in turn encourages more participation and faster decision‑making.
Over time, improvements in key manufacturing indicators, such as lower non‑conformance
counts and shorter cycle times, translate directly into lower operating costs and greater
throughput, yielding a positive financial impact that justifies the initial investment in developing
and maintaining the CI infrastructure.

F. Recommendation

 Based on the identified problem, the proposed deliverables, and the expected operational
and financial benefits, the recommended course of action is to implement the Continuous
Improvement Process Guide and associated data‑capture tool as a standard element of LANL’s
production environment. Formalizing how improvements are recorded, scored, and approved
will clarify issue ownership, strengthen cross‑functional communication, and significantly
reduce the knowledge loss that currently limits sustained progress. This structured CI system
aligns with the broader transition from an R&D mindset to a production mindset by emphasizing
refinement, efficiency, and data‑driven decision‑making. Implementing the guide now will
position LANL to improve production performance in a measurable way while building a
durable culture of continuous improvement that supports long‑term mission success.

 2. METHODS

 The purpose of this project is to implement a continuous improvement (CI) program that
tracks production metrics to verify that production is improving with the incorporation of new
improvements and to establish a data-driven system to prioritize their incorporation. To best
support this, a mixed-methods confirmatory research design will be used to gather qualitative
and quantitative data to build a process flow guide and a system to record and score known
improvements, enabling better resource allocation to the most impactful issues.

2.1 Methodology

A. Research Design

 The program director has emphasized a push to make data-driven decisions rather than
the shotgun approach, which often results from working in an R&D environment. As LANL
refines its production program, it will need to formalize its CI process. To initialize the project,
both qualitative and quantitative data will need to be gathered. This will be done in a concurrent
parallel design. The qualitative data will be the information gathered for the process flow,
showing what each group goes through to incorporate a new improvement. This data will be

 12

collected through interviews, and CI recommendations will also be collected for use later in the
project. The initial quantitative data will be collected to create a benchmark for production to
compare over the next year and used to create a scoring system for the new improvements. This
means that this project will be a mixed-methods confirmatory study using both qualitative and
quantitative data to verify the effectiveness of a formalized CI program. At this stage, a scoring
system will need to be created that ranks each improvement previously collected against the key
manufacturing data. This will be an iterative process that will require feedback from each group.
This will be a key to avoiding bias from a specific group, especially the process engineer. This
project focuses on one particular process within production, and to avoid Process Engineer bias,
with the engineer as the process owner, feedback from the other groups will be crucial. After the
scoring system is approved and all improvements are ranked, a summary of all potential
enhancements will need to be prepared to calculate a projected ROI for the forthcoming year.

B. Participants

 To gain process flow knowledge, the following groups will need to be interviewed,
starting with the least available and proceeding to the most available. This means that the Design
Agency would be interviewed first, then the Product Engineers, Process Engineers, and finally
the Quality Engineers. Each of these groups will again be called on during the scoring system
development to ensure no one team is favored over another. The project sponsor and the FLM
for the Quality Engineering Group will have the final say on the scoring system in the event of a
disagreement that requires a tiebreaker when weighing improvements. The Quality Engineering
groups will have access to non-conformance data and will be relied on heavily to gather that
data. The CI coordinator will be responsible for reviewing and collecting previous process data.

 In parallel with the interview process, key manufacturing data will need to be collected

 The data analysis will be completed in three parts. The first two parts will involve
gathering interview data and creating a process flow sheet that outlines the process for
implementing and approving new improvements. The format of this process flow chart will use
the four standard symbols. The four symbols are shown in Fig. 1. The flowchart will be created
using Visio.

 The second part of the data analysis will involve creating a benchmark using the
previously gathered key indicators. The collected data will need to be formatted into the
following metrics: average non-conformances per part over the last year, average cycle time per
part over the previous year, average failed inspections due to process failures over the last year,
and paperwork cycle time per part. A potential pitfall to be carefully avoided is that non-
conformances resulting from a part defect must not count against the benchmark. So, organizing
and filtering the data to include only results that pertain to the process will be crucial to ensuring
the project has an accurate benchmark that serves as a foundation for the rest of the project.

 The next analytical step will be the creation of a scoring system. Each CI suggestion will
need to be scored and rated on its impact on these key indicators. Once a rough draft of the
scoring system is in place, it will be reviewed by a member of each group to identify potential
bias and ensure no one type of issue is inappropriately over- or underweighted.

 Each CI suggestion will have an impact score to set its priority. Additionally, a
summation of each improvement will be calculated so the potential of the process can be
projected for a given improvement. This will be key for identifying paths forward and making
process decisions to determine whether a current process has the potential to meet its
performance targets, or whether a certain inspection needs to be reworked entirely.

E. Summary

 This project will encompass both qualitative and quantitative data. It will confirm the
hypothesis that production will improve by introducing a formal CI system that scores potential
improvements and strategically incorporates them. This will require one-on-one interviews and
looking at historical data over the last year to create a process benchmark and scoring system.
This will allow production to strategically focus resources on the highest-impact issues within
process improvement, improve overall production efficiency, and build greater trust within the
National Laboratory Complex.

F. Risk Management Plan

 A significant risk factor is the inability to interview the required groups within the
scheduled timeframe to obtain the information needed to create the flow diagram. To mitigate
this risk, the groups with the least availability will be interviewed first, allowing the most
flexibility to complete the necessary interviews. This will be the DA; the next least accessible
group is PEs, then QEs, and finally the PrEs, which have the most access and will be prioritized
last. Preliminary discussions to schedule interviews have already begun to mitigate this risk.

 The potential risk is that unforeseen bias may appear in the scoring system. When
creating the scoring system, each production team will review the preliminary scores of each

G. Research Plan Methodology

 The program director has emphasized a push to make data-driven decisions rather than
the shotgun approach, which often results from working in an R&D environment. As LANL
refines its production program, it will need to formalize its CI process. To initialize the project,
both qualitative and quantitative data will need to be gathered. This will be done in a concurrent
parallel design. The qualitative data will be the information gathered for the process flow,
showing what each group goes through to incorporate a new improvement. This data will be
collected through interviews, and CI recommendations will also be collected for use later in the
project. The initial quantitative data will be collected to create a benchmark for production to
compare over the next year and used to create a scoring system for the new improvements. This
means that this project will be a mixed-methods confirmatory study using both qualitative and
quantitative data to verify the effectiveness of a formalized CI program. At this stage, a scoring
system will need to be created that ranks each improvement previously collected against the key
manufacturing data. This will be an iterative process that will require feedback from each group.
This will be a key to avoiding bias from a specific group, especially the process engineer. This
project focuses on one particular process within production, and to avoid Process Engineer bias,
with the engineer as the process owner, feedback from the other groups will be crucial. After the
scoring system is approved and all improvements are ranked, a summary of all potential
enhancements will need to be prepared to calculate a projected ROI for the forthcoming year.

 Each available member of each group will ask a standard set of questions. Table II will

3.2 Decision Making Report

A. Introduction:

 To further analyze the project data and how it will be applied to future actions,
descriptive and prescriptive analyses will be used. Descriptive and prescriptive analyses provide
a coherent decision-making framework that directly supports the goals of this continuous
improvement project at LANL. First, characterizing the current inspection and improvement
incorporation process and then using that information to prescribe specific actions, this strategy
ensures that recommended changes are both data-driven and practical within the organization’s
operational context and operational goals.

B. Decision-making methodology

 This project uses descriptive and prescriptive analysis together to first map how
inspection improvements are currently identified and implemented at LANL and then to select
and justify which proposed changes should be implemented first. Descriptive analysis
summarizes how the improvement process actually works today by mapping each step, role, and
handoff in the CI flowchart and using interview notes to highlight where work holds up or
creates unnecessary work. Using descriptive analysis, one can draw insights from the flowchart
to better understand the current state and make decisions to best capitalize on it [1]. Prescriptive

 26

analysis then builds on that foundation by applying a structured, criteria‑based decision method
(such as scoring improvements against key performance measures and constraints) to answer
“given what we know, what should we do next?,” enabling the project to prioritize improvement
options, articulate expected benefits, and recommend concrete changes that are both data‑driven
and operationally realistic[13]. The goal of this project is to outline how to improve the current
process now and do so with a data-driven approach, which is why this decision-making method
works so well for this project. Naturally, the improvement process should improve over time as
new improvements are suggested and implemented. Still, the goal is to outline the current
process and enable employees by giving them the process knowledge to incorporate
improvements, and to do so with a sound rationale, by giving employees a method to rank each
improvement idea they have. Descriptive and predictive analyses are essential to the project's
success.

 Descriptive analytics is used to understand what has happened [14]. The project’s

E. Summary

 The project shows that combining descriptive and prescriptive analysis offers a practical
path for advancing continuous improvement within LANL’s inspection processes by grounding
recommendations in both observed reality and quantified impact. Descriptive analysis first
clarified the current state by mapping the CI flowchart into five distinct organizational legs,
revealing how DOPs, SPIs, SQAs, and RENs interact within the flowchart and where redundant
review cycles and handoffs slow progress. Interviews with DA, PE, QE, and PrE personnel
produced 38 improvement suggestions, many focused on inspection Process A, which, along
with observations of siloed expertise and unclear responsibilities, underscored both the desire
and need for change and the work culture barriers that currently limit efficiency. Prescriptive
analysis then transformed these insights into prioritized action by applying a structured scoring
matrix to all 38 ideas against key manufacturing indicators (NCRs, cycle time, paperwork hours,
and failed runs), elevating a top‑ranked improvement projected to cut cycle time by 37.3%,

reduce paperwork by 166 hours, and increase success rate by 61.9%. This evidence supports
moving forward with that improvement, bundling all related Process A changes into a single
REN to maximize the gain from the review burden required of a REN with the off‑site DA, and
decoupling DOP, SPI, and SQA revisions where feasible to prevent unnecessary cascading
updates. This descriptive‑to‑prescriptive decision-making supported a Lab-wide initiative to push
for more data-driven decisions, and LANL can shift from being reactive to proactive through
new improvements that will resolve issues now and make future changes easier. The impact of
this will support a metrics‑based CI culture that systematically captures ideas, evaluates them
against mission‑critical goals, and implements the highest‑value improvements first and the
improvements associated with the same specific process associated with the highest value to
improve efficiency.

compound, which will strengthen the inspection performance within the production line, build
confidence within the group and across the National Laboratory complex, and improve LANL’s
competitive position when pursuing future DOE work.

 4. DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION

4.1 Discussion

 The literature on continuous improvement emphasizes that many organizations fail to
sustain CI because they rely on ad hoc problem solving, chase easy wins, and do not create a
common, structured method that withstand excessive company growth. Currently, improvements

 34

in the inspection process have historically been handled through informal networks, with each
group following its own path for proposing and implementing changes, which leads to unclear
ownership, inconsistent follow‑through, and repeated work when people move roles. In contrast,
successful CI programs stress broad participation, clear roles and responsibilities, and transparent
data capture tied to measurable performance indicators. The CI flowchart developed in this
project directly responds to those findings by creating a single, shared flowchart for
implementing improvements and a data‑driven scoring system that links each suggestion to key
manufacturing metrics, such as non‑conformances, cycle time, paperwork effort, and failed runs.

 The results confirm that this structured approach can convert scattered, qualitative
feedback into a prioritized portfolio of improvement opportunities that is defensible to
management and understandable to operators. Twelve interviews across DA, PE, PrE, and QE
generated 38 improvement suggestions, many concentrated on a single inspection process,
indicating a willingness to engage if a clear path is provided. The iterative development of the
flowchart with each group, and later with subject‑matter expert feedback, not only produced a
more accurate representation of the real process but also helped build early buy‑in for using the
flowchart as the standard reference for future improvements. Likewise, the scoring system
started as a simple matrix but evolved through cross‑team review into a tool that balances
different perspectives and reduces the risk that a single group can dominate priorities based on
local concerns rather than overall mission impact.

 For the financials and operations, the most important implication is that a relatively
modest investment in formalizing the CI process can unlock substantial and recurring benefits.
The top‑ranked improvement, focused on inspection, is projected to reduce inspection cycle time
by 37.3 percent, prevent 5 NCRs per year, eliminate 166 hours of paperwork annually, and
increase success rate by 61.9 percent. When even just the time savings are monetized using a
fully burdened labor rate, the three‑year ROI is approximately 190 percent with a payback period
of about 1.04 years, which provides a strong quantitative justification for moving this
improvement, and the CI framework itself, into regular operations. In addition, the flowchart
have already improved cross‑group understanding of who owns each step and what approvals are
required, which supports the broader cultural shift from an R&D mindset to a production
mindset.

4.2 Conclusion and Future Work

 This project effectively tested whether a formal CI framework, consisting of a shared
process flowchart, a centralized data‑capture tool, and a quantitative scoring system, could be
used at LANL to prioritize and justify production improvements, starting with inspection. The
findings indicate that the framework is both technically sound and operationally acceptable: it
produces clear, measurable projections of improvement impact, aligns with existing key
performance indicators, and has been validated with stakeholders from DA, PE, PrE, and QE. As
such, the project provides a strong basis for transitioning the CI Process Guide from a capstone
deliverable into a standard operational tool, first within inspection and then, if successful, across
additional production processes.

 35

 Several new project opportunities emerged from this work. First, the current flowchart
and scoring system are focused on one inspection process; future projects could expand the same
methodology to other operations. Second, once more improvements are implemented, there is an
opportunity to conduct a longitudinal study comparing projected gains to actual gains, which
would refine the scoring weights and further increase confidence in the tool for decision‑making.
Third, the data‑capture and tracking system could be enhanced with simple dashboards that
automatically display CI pipeline status, realized benefits, and group participation rates, turning
CI into a visible management system rather than a background activity.

 At a broader level, this project reinforces lessons that are applicable beyond LANL.
Organizations transitioning from R&D to production, or simply trying to scale operations with
limited resources, benefit from treating CI as a structured, cross‑functional process rather than a
side activity handled informally. It is possible to use metrics to build and test a scoring system,
making the method adaptable to other highly regulated environments. Going forward, the plan is
to continue to work with management to implement the top‑ranked improvements using the
flowchart, measure actual performance changes against the projected baseline, and then use those
results to advocate for expanding this CI framework across additional processes and, ultimately,
across the broader LANL complex.
