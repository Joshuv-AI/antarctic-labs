Dietary practices, nutritional knowledge, and socio-cultural influences among pregnant adolescents in rural Ghana: a qualitative study from North Dayi District
Authors: Leticia Emefa Obobi, Phyllis Addo, Fedelix Agyemang Owusu, Celestine Yeboah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdolescent pregnancy is associated with heightened nutritional vulnerabilities due to the concurrent demands of adolescent growth and fetal development. Despite the critical importance of adequate dietary intake during this period, dietary practices among pregnant adolescents in rural Ghanaian settings are poorly understood. This study assessed dietary practices, nutritional knowledge, and socio-economic and cultural influencing factors among pregnant adolescents in North Dayi District, Volta Region, Ghana.MethodsA qualitative study design was employed, using in-depth interviews with eleven (11) pregnant adolescents aged 16–19 years. Participants were recruited via a combination of purposive and snowball sampling from rural communities in North Dayi District. Data were collected using a semi-structured interview guide and analysed using Braun and Clarke’s six-phase thematic analysis framework. The Health Belief Model (HBM) provided the theoretical framework for interpreting how adolescents’ perceptions of susceptibility, barriers, benefits, and self-efficacy shaped their dietary behaviours.ResultsThree overarching themes emerged: (1) dietary habits centred on starchy staples (banku, kenkey, rice, fufu) with reportedly limited dietary diversity and irregular meal patterns; (2) severely limited nutritional knowledge, with most participants unable to define a balanced diet or identify pregnancy-appropriate foods despite attending antenatal care (ANC); and (3) multiple intersecting barriers to adequate nutrition, including financial constraints, cultural food taboos, limited household decision-making autonomy, and reportedly inadequate dietary counselling at ANC. Food cravings frequently went unmet due to financial limitations, and ANC services reportedly focused predominantly on hygiene and medication rather than dietary guidance.ConclusionsPregnant adolescents in North Dayi District face significant dietary challenges stemming from poverty, knowledge gaps, cultural food restrictions, and perceived inadequate health system support. Current ANC nutrition education approaches appear insufficiently tailored to the needs of this population. Comprehensive, multi-level interventions may be needed, including food assistance programmes, adolescent-friendly nutrition education, enhanced health worker training, family engagement strategies, and policy reforms addressing adolescent-specific nutritional needs.Keywords: Adolescent pregnancy, Dietary practices, Nutritional knowledge, Food taboos, Maternal nutrition, Qualitative research, Health Belief Model, Ghana, Rural health

Background

Nutrition during pregnancy is a cornerstone of maternal and child health, directly influencing pregnancy outcomes and long-term health trajectories [1]. For adolescents, pregnancy presents compounded nutritional challenges because the adolescent body is simultaneously supporting its own ongoing growth and the development of the fetus, substantially elevating nutrient requirements [2]. Pregnant adolescents are therefore at heightened risk of malnutrition, anaemia, low birth weight (LBW), preterm birth, and obstetric complications [3].

Globally, approximately 21 million girls aged 15–19 years become pregnant annually in developing countries, with approximately 12 million giving birth [4]. Sub-Saharan Africa carries one of the highest adolescent birth rates worldwide. In Ghana, nearly 14% of girls aged 15–19 years have begun childbearing [5], reflecting the persistence of adolescent pregnancy as a public health challenge.

Existing research on dietary practices among pregnant adolescents in Ghana and similar contexts provides important quantitative evidence. Gyimah et al. (2021) documented low dietary diversity scores and heavy reliance on cereal-based staples among pregnant adolescents in the Ashanti Region, with limited consumption of animal-source foods, fruits, and vegetables [6]. Nsiah-Asamoah et al. (2019) reported that pregnant women in rural Ghana consumed diets deficient in key micronutrients and that nutrition counselling at antenatal care (ANC) was often superficial [7]. A systematic review by Keats et al. (2018) confirmed that adolescent girls across low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) consistently consume energy-dense but nutrient-poor diets [3]. Rossés et al. (2020) further demonstrated that dietary patterns during adolescent pregnancy are shaped by a complex interplay of poverty, cultural food beliefs, and restricted autonomy within households [8].

However, most existing studies in Ghana have employed quantitative dietary assessment methods (such as food frequency questionnaires and dietary diversity scores) and have focused on pregnant women of reproductive age broadly, without specifically disaggregating adolescent experiences [7]. Qualitative evidence that captures the lived experiences, decision-making processes, cultural contexts, and subjective perceptions of pregnant adolescents in rural Ghanaian settings remains scarce. This gap is significant because understanding the ‘how’ and ‘why’ behind dietary behaviours is essential for designing effective, contextually appropriate interventions [9].

Cultural food taboos represent an additional and underexplored dimension of dietary practice in Ghana. Appiah et al. (2021) documented a range of food taboos and misconceptions among pregnant women across Ghanaian communities, including restrictions on the consumption of specific animal proteins and fruits based on beliefs about potential harm to the fetus [10]. Aborigo et al. (2012) similarly identified deeply entrenched food taboos affecting infant and maternal nutrition in northern Ghana, emphasising the role of elder women and traditional authorities in enforcing dietary restrictions [11]. These cultural influences may disproportionately affect adolescents, who typically occupy subordinate positions within household hierarchies.

The North Dayi District in the Volta Region of Ghana represents a particularly relevant context for investigation. The district is predominantly rural, with scattered communities along the Volta Lake and highland areas. Economic activities centre on subsistence farming, fishing, and petty trading, with many households facing significant poverty and limited access to health services [12]. These structural conditions may create unique nutritional challenges for pregnant adolescents.

A qualitative approach is particularly appropriate for addressing this research question because it enables exploration of the subjective meanings, cultural beliefs, and social dynamics that quantitative dietary assessment methods cannot capture [9]. In-depth interviews allow pregnant adolescents to describe their food choices, knowledge, and constraints in their own words, providing insights into the contextual factors that shape dietary behaviour in ways that numerical data alone cannot reveal.

This study therefore aimed to assess dietary practices among pregnant adolescents in North Dayi District, Ghana, examining their nutritional knowledge, dietary habits, and the socio-economic and cultural factors influencing their food choices. The specific objectives were to: (1) examine the dietary habits of pregnant adolescents; (2) assess the level of nutritional knowledge; and (3) identify socio-economic and cultural factors affecting dietary practices.

Theoretical framework

The Health Belief Model (HBM) was selected as the theoretical framework for this study because it provides a comprehensive structure for understanding health-related behaviours through six core constructs: perceived susceptibility, perceived severity, perceived benefits, perceived barriers, cues to action, and self-efficacy [13]. These constructs are particularly relevant to understanding adolescent dietary decision-making in resource-limited settings, where perceptions of nutritional risk, awareness of dietary benefits, structural barriers to food access, and confidence in the ability to make dietary changes collectively shape food-related behaviours.

In this study, the HBM was not applied as a deductive coding framework during data analysis. Rather, it served as an interpretive lens during the discussion phase, guiding the examination of how participants’ perceptions, knowledge, barriers, and sources of health information related to their reported dietary practices. This approach is consistent with the use of the HBM in qualitative nutrition research, where the model provides a structured means of interpreting behavioural findings without constraining the inductive emergence of themes from the data [13].

Methods

Study design

This study employed a qualitative research design using in-depth interviews as the primary data collection method. A qualitative approach was appropriate for exploring the lived experiences, perceptions, beliefs, and contextual factors shaping dietary practices among pregnant adolescents, providing rich, detailed insights into the ‘how’ and ‘why’ of dietary behaviour [9]. In-depth interviews were preferred over focus group discussions to ensure privacy and encourage open disclosure on sensitive topics related to pregnancy, food access, and family dynamics.

Study setting

The study was conducted in North Dayi District, one of 18 districts in the Volta Region of Ghana, established by Legislative Instrument (L.I.) 2076 of 2012. The district covers approximately 461 km² with Anfoega as its capital. According to the 2021 Population and Housing Census, the district has a total population of 39,268 [12]. The population is predominantly rural and Ewe-speaking, with economic activities centred on subsistence farming, fishing, petty trading, and artisanal work. Health facilities include the Anfoega Catholic Hospital, health centres, and Community-based Health Planning and Services (CHPS) compounds.

Participants were recruited from ten communities within the district: Tafi Madzi, Toveme Danyigba, Danyigba Dzigbe, Tokorme Kpodzi, Bume Wuve, Vakpo Gborxome, Wusuta, Kpebe, New Adomi, and Aveme Beme.

Study population and eligibility criteria

The study population comprised pregnant adolescents aged 16–19 years residing in North Dayi District. Inclusion criteria were: (a) pregnant adolescents aged 16–19 years; (b) residence in North Dayi District; and (c) ability to communicate in Ewe or English. Adolescents with severe illness affecting capacity to participate were excluded.

Sample size and sampling strategy

A total of eleven (11) pregnant adolescents participated. A combination of purposive and snowball sampling was employed. Initial participants were identified through health facilities and community health volunteers, who then referred other eligible pregnant adolescents within their networks. Purposive sampling ensured variation in age, educational background, and community of residence to capture diverse perspectives.

Data saturation was assessed iteratively during data collection by reviewing transcripts after each interview. By the ninth and tenth interviews, no new themes, codes, or substantive variations in participant accounts were emerging. An eleventh interview was conducted to confirm saturation. This approach is consistent with Guest et al. (2006), who demonstrated that thematic saturation in relatively homogeneous samples typically occurs within 6–12 interviews [14].

Data collection

Data were collected using a semi-structured interview guide developed by the principal investigator (LEO), drawing on the study objectives, a review of relevant literature on adolescent dietary practices in LMICs [3, 6–16], and the constructs of the Health Belief Model. The draft guide was reviewed by the research supervisor (PA), an experienced qualitative researcher and public health nutritionist, who provided feedback on question phrasing, sequencing, and alignment with study objectives. The guide was subsequently pretested with two pregnant adolescents in a neighbouring district to ensure clarity, cultural sensitivity, and linguistic appropriateness, with minor revisions made based on pretesting feedback. The final guide was organised into six thematic sections: demographic background, dietary habits, nutritional knowledge, socio-economic and cultural influences, health system support, and personal reflections.

All interviews were conducted by the principal investigator (LEO), who is fluent in Ewe. Each interview lasted 30–45 min, was audio-recorded with prior participant consent, and conducted at locations convenient and private for participants, including health facilities and community spaces. Detailed field notes captured non-verbal cues and contextual observations. Interviews were conducted in Ewe and transcribed verbatim, then translated into English by the researcher.

Data analysis

Data were analysed using Braun and Clarke’s six-phase thematic analysis framework [17]: (1) familiarisation with data through repeated reading of transcripts; (2) generation of initial codes; (3) searching for themes; (4) reviewing themes; (5) defining and naming themes; and (6) producing the final report using illustrative participant quotations. An inductive approach allowed themes to emerge organically from the data. The HBM was applied as an interpretive lens during the discussion phase.

Initial coding was conducted manually by the principal investigator (LEO) with multiple transcript reviews to ensure thoroughness. Codes and emerging themes were reviewed and discussed with the research supervisor (PA) at multiple stages of the analysis process. Where discrepancies in interpretation arose, these were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached. Additionally, the data analysts (FAO and CY) independently reviewed the coding framework and thematic structure to verify internal consistency and coherence of the analysis.

Trustworthiness

Credibility was strengthened through the use of a pretested, supervisor-reviewed semi-structured interview guide and iterative probing during interviews. Dependability was supported by maintaining an audit trail of interview notes, coding decisions, and theme development. Confirmability was enhanced by comparing emerging interpretations against raw transcripts and using illustrative quotations to demonstrate grounding in participant accounts. Translation accuracy was supported through back-translation verification prior to analysis.

To address potential researcher bias arising from the principal investigator conducting interviews, transcription, and initial translation, several strategies were employed. First, the researcher maintained a reflexive journal documenting personal assumptions, observations, and analytical decisions throughout the research process. Second, a subset of translated transcripts was independently reviewed by the research supervisor (PA), who is bilingual in Ewe and English, to verify translation accuracy and identify potential interpretive bias. Minor discrepancies in translation were discussed and resolved through consensus. Third, emerging themes and interpretations were discussed with the supervisory team to ensure analytical rigour and reduce the influence of individual researcher perspectives. We acknowledge that the absence of fully independent dual coding represents a methodological limitation, which is noted in the Strengths and Limitations subsection.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the University of Health and Allied Sciences Research Ethics Committee (UHAS-REC) prior to data collection. Institutional permission was granted by the North Dayi District Health Directorate. Full details of consent procedures are provided in the Declarations section.

Results

Characteristics of study participants

Eleven pregnant adolescents aged 16–19 years participated (mean age 17.5 ± 1.0 years). All resided in rural communities within North Dayi District. Most participants were currently enrolled in or had completed Junior High School (JHS 2 or JHS 3). Seven were single, two were married, and two were cohabiting. Nine were primigravidae (G1P0) and two were in their second pregnancy (G2P1). Gestational ages ranged from 4 to 8 months. Occupations included petty trading, farming, and schooling, with some combining multiple activities. Demographic details are presented in Table 1.

Theme 1: dietary habits of pregnant adolescents

Predominance of starchy staple foods

Participants’ self-reported diets were centred on starchy staples, including banku, kenkey, rice, fufu, and yam, typically accompanied by soups or stews. Proteins were reportedly consumed mainly as fish, with chicken mentioned occasionally. Based on participants’ own descriptions, vegetables appeared to feature minimally in their reported dietary patterns, though it should be noted that soups and stews consumed alongside staple foods may contain vegetables, legumes, and other ingredients not individually enumerated during interviews. Most participants reported similar daily dietary patterns:

“Waakye and spaghetti with stew, banku with groundnut soup and fish, boiled maize, banku with okro soup with salmon. (17-year-old, single participant)”

“Banku with okro soup with fish, morning fried potato, boiled fried plantain. (16-year-old, single participant)”

“Boiled rice and sauce with egg, stew. (17-year-old, married participant)”.

Banku was the most frequently cited food item, consumed with okro soup, groundnut soup, or pepper sauce. No participant described regular consumption of fruits, dairy products, or a broad range of vegetables in their reported diets.

Irregular meal frequency and patterns

Meal frequency varied from 2 to 4 times daily, with most participants reporting 3–4 meals when appetite permitted. However, eating patterns were frequently disrupted by pregnancy-related symptoms, including nausea, fatigue, and discomfort, resulting in meal skipping:

“It varies, but I eat mostly thrice daily unless not feeling well. (17-year-old, married participant)”

“Three to four times a day. (16-year-old, single participant)”

Inconsistency in meal timing was reported across participants, reflecting the challenge of maintaining adequate dietary intake alongside pregnancy symptoms.

Food cravings and aversions

Participants reported cravings for fruits (particularly banana), groundnuts, carbonated drinks, and specific traditional dishes. However, many cravings went unsatisfied due to cost or unavailability, causing considerable distress:

“Banana, milk, Fruitful [drink] and Flakes. (17-year-old, married participant)”

“Boiled or roasted groundnut, tea bread, carbonated drinks. (17-year-old, single participant)”

“Kenkey with pepper, fufu and palmnut soup — unable to get due to unavailability. (16-year-old, single participant)”

Pregnancy also altered food preferences. Some participants reported loss of appetite for previously enjoyed foods while others developed new preferences, with these fluctuations potentially affecting overall nutritional intake.

Food sources

Food was obtained from multiple sources including home cooking using farm produce and local market purchases. Home-grown produce provided some degree of household food security:

“From farm produce or from the market. (17-year-old, married participant)”

“From the farm; we grow cassava and sweet potato, tomato. And the market. (16-year-old, single participant)”

Theme 2: nutritional knowledge among pregnant adolescents

Limited understanding of balanced diet

The majority of participants demonstrated very limited understanding of what constitutes a balanced diet. Most were unable to define the concept or identify the major food groups:

“I have no idea about balanced diet. (17-year-old, married participant)”

“I do not understand. (16-year-old, single participant)”

This knowledge gap was pervasive across age groups and educational backgrounds, suggesting systemic inadequacies in nutrition education rather than individual-level factors.

Limited knowledge of pregnancy-appropriate foods

Participants struggled to identify foods specifically beneficial during pregnancy beyond their regular dietary staples. Responses reflected familiarity with habitual foods rather than understanding of pregnancy-specific nutritional requirements:

“I have no idea about food that is good for a pregnant woman. (17-year-old, married participant)”

“Banku. Relevant information: there is weight in it. (16-year-old, single participant)”

“Banku with okro or green leafy vegetables and yam with stew and fish. (17-year-old, single participant)”

Sources of nutrition information and poor retention

Participants received nutrition information primarily from health workers during ANC visits and from family members. However, despite this exposure, retention and practical application were consistently poor:

“From home and the community. Yes, the health worker and families. (17-year-old, married participant)”

“It was clear, but I cannot remember any. (17-year-old, single participant)”

“I have tried, but due to the condition, I could not get it right. (17-year-old, married participant)”

Theme 3: socio-economic and cultural factors influencing dietary practices

Financial constraints

Economic hardship emerged as the dominant and most pervasive barrier to adequate nutrition across all participants. Financial dependency on male partners, parents, or other family members severely limited participants’ ability to purchase desired or nutritionally beneficial foods:

“Money or finance. (17-year-old, married participant, when asked about the main barrier to eating well)”

“Unable to get it due to unavailability. (16-year-old, single participant)”

Cultural food taboos and restrictions

Cultural beliefs and traditional food taboos restricted dietary options for several participants. Prohibitions against certain foods during pregnancy were reported, often communicated by mothers, mothers-in-law, or community elders:

“My mother says I should not eat unripe fruits; otherwise my stomach will hurt. (16-year-old, single participant)”

“She does not eat snail because the people do not eat it. (17-year-old, married participant)”

Limited decision-making autonomy

Most participants reported limited control over food choices, with decisions predominantly made by mothers, mothers-in-law, or male partners:

“The mother-in-law does the cooking; at times she also cooks when I crave to eat some particular food. (17-year-old, married participant)”

“My boyfriend is an electrician and also the one who takes the meat pie for me to sell. (17-year-old, single participant)”

Inadequate health system support

Despite regular ANC attendance, participants reported receiving minimal nutritional counselling. Health workers were described as focusing primarily on personal hygiene and medication:

“Yes, but only on personal hygiene, not on diet. (17-year-old, married participant)”

“There was no advice given to me about diet; it would have been better if I was given advice. (17-year-old, married participant)”

Discussion

This study explored dietary practices, nutritional knowledge, and influencing factors among pregnant adolescents in rural North Dayi District, Ghana. Three overarching themes emerged: dietary habits characterised by reportedly limited diversity, severely limited nutritional knowledge, and multiple intersecting socio-economic and cultural barriers. These findings are discussed below in relation to existing literature and the Health Belief Model.

Dietary habits: starchy staples and reported limited diversity

The predominance of starchy staples in participants’ reported diets — centred on banku, kenkey, fufu, and rice with accompanying soups — reflects the broader dietary culture of the Ewe-speaking Volta Region, where starchy staples prepared with fermented maize or cassava dough constitute the foundation of household meals [18]. These patterns are not unique to pregnant adolescents but are characteristic of rural Ghanaian households more broadly. However, the critical concern emerging from participants’ accounts is the apparently limited accompaniment of protein-rich foods, fruits, and diverse vegetables alongside these staples, which may be particularly consequential during pregnancy.

This pattern aligns with evidence from Ghana and other LMICs [6, 8]. Gyimah et al. (2021) similarly reported heavy reliance on maize, cassava, and yam among pregnant adolescents in Ghana [6], while Keats et al. (2018) confirmed that adolescent girls across LMICs consistently consume energy-dense but nutrient-poor diets [3]. It should be noted, however, that dietary intake in this study was explored qualitatively through participants’ self-reported descriptions rather than through standardised dietary assessment methods such as 24-hour recall or food frequency questionnaires. Consequently, definitive conclusions about dietary adequacy or diversity cannot be drawn. The soups and stews consumed alongside staple foods may contain vegetables, legumes, and other ingredients that were not individually enumerated by participants during interviews.

From an HBM perspective, perceived barriers — particularly food cost and limited market access — appeared to prevent consumption of diverse foods even where participants demonstrated some awareness of their potential benefits. This finding suggests the potential inadequacy of knowledge-based interventions alone when structural barriers remain unaddressed. The dietary patterns reported may place these adolescents at potential risk for nutrient insufficiencies relevant to maternal and fetal health [1, 3]; however, biochemical or anthropometric assessment would be required to confirm specific nutritional deficiencies.

Irregular meal patterns, driven by pregnancy-related nausea, fatigue, and appetite loss, are consistent with findings by Tesfaye et al. (2023) in Ethiopia [15]. The HBM construct of perceived susceptibility may partly explain this: participants appeared to prioritise immediate symptom management over nutritional adequacy, suggesting that health education specifically addressing how to manage pregnancy symptoms while maintaining adequate intake could be beneficial.

Nutritional knowledge: critical gaps and poor retention

The finding that most participants could not define a balanced diet or identify pregnancy-appropriate foods despite ANC attendance represents a significant programmatic concern. This aligns with Akunor-Sackey (2022), who found that while some adolescents in Ghana received general healthy eating messages, comprehensive understanding of pregnancy-specific nutritional requirements was absent [16]. Tesfaye et al. (2023) similarly documented widespread nutritional knowledge gaps among pregnant adolescents in Ethiopia, attributing them to limited structured nutrition education and ineffective health provider communication [15].

Poor information retention suggests that current ANC educational approaches may be insufficiently tailored to the comprehension levels, learning styles, or practical contexts of adolescents with limited formal education. The HBM’s self-efficacy construct is relevant here: without clear, practical, and repeated guidance in accessible language, adolescents may lack the foundation to act on health messaging. This finding highlights the potential need for adolescent-specific educational materials using visual aids, local language, and contextually relevant examples.

Socio-economic and cultural barriers

Financial constraints emerged as the dominant reported barrier to adequate nutrition, consistent with extensive literature documenting poverty as a root cause of malnutrition in LMICs [1]. Economic dependency on partners or family members severely limited participants’ purchasing power for nutritious foods. In HBM terms, this may represent an overwhelming perceived barrier that overrides perceived benefits and cues to action, illustrating the limitations of purely educational interventions in the absence of structural support.

Cultural food taboos — avoiding snails, unripe fruits, and other foods based on traditional beliefs — further restricted reported dietary diversity. Tesfaye et al. (2023) documented similar practices in Ethiopia [15]. In the Ghanaian context, Appiah et al. (2021) identified a range of food taboos and misconceptions among pregnant women, including restrictions on specific animal proteins and fruits [10]. Aborigo et al. (2012) similarly described deeply entrenched food taboos affecting maternal nutrition in northern Ghana, mediated by elder women and traditional authorities [11]. Addressing food taboos requires culturally sensitive approaches that engage respectfully with community knowledge systems while providing evidence-based guidance on nutritional needs during pregnancy.

Limited decision-making autonomy, with food choices controlled by mothers-in-law, partners, or parents, reflects patriarchal and hierarchical household structures documented broadly in sub-Saharan Africa [8]. This finding reinforces the importance of engaging family decision-makers — not only adolescents — in nutrition interventions. The HBM recognises that environmental and social factors mediate the relationship between knowledge and behaviour, underscoring the necessity of family-level engagement.

Participants consistently reported receiving minimal nutritional counselling at ANC, with health workers described as focusing primarily on hygiene and medication rather than dietary guidance. This characterisation of ANC nutrition counselling as inadequate is based on two converging sources of evidence: participants’ own accounts, and supporting literature from comparable LMIC settings. Tesfaye et al. (2023) documented similar findings in Ethiopia, where ANC providers gave minimal dietary guidance to pregnant adolescents [15]. Nsiah-Asamoah et al. (2019) reported that nutrition counselling at ANC in rural Ghana was often superficial and did not address specific dietary needs [7]. While this study captured participants’ perceptions of ANC counselling rather than directly observing or auditing ANC sessions, the convergence of participant accounts with findings from comparable settings suggests a pattern worthy of programmatic attention. The HBM’s cues to action construct suggests that regular, authoritative messaging from trusted health providers could motivate behaviour change — yet this mechanism appears currently underutilised in this setting.

Implications for the health belief model

The findings demonstrate the complex interplay of HBM constructs in shaping dietary practices. Knowledge gaps appeared to reduce perceived benefits of healthy eating. Limited health education meant few cues to action. Poverty and cultural restrictions created overwhelming perceived barriers. Low self-efficacy appeared to stem from limited autonomy and resources. The model suggests that effective interventions may need to address all components simultaneously rather than focusing solely on knowledge or motivation.

Strengths and limitations

This study provides contextually grounded qualitative insights into the lived dietary experiences of pregnant adolescents in a rural Ghanaian district, addressing a meaningful gap in the literature. The use of in-depth interviews conducted in Ewe by a fluent principal investigator facilitated trust and open disclosure, enhancing data quality. The application of the HBM as an interpretive framework provided structured theoretical grounding for the findings.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The sample of 11 participants from one district limits transferability of findings to other settings. Dietary data were self-reported through qualitative interviews and were not assessed using standardised dietary assessment tools; consequently, definitive conclusions about dietary adequacy or diversity cannot be drawn. The cross-sectional nature precludes assessment of dietary changes across pregnancy or causal inference. The principal investigator served as interviewer, transcriber, translator, and primary coder, which may introduce researcher bias despite the mitigation strategies employed (reflexive journalling, supervisor review of transcripts, and collaborative theme development). The absence of fully independent dual coding is a further limitation. Translation from Ewe to English may have resulted in some loss of nuance despite back-translation verification. Future research should employ larger samples across multiple districts, incorporate standardised dietary assessment methods and biochemical measures of nutritional status, and use longitudinal designs to capture dietary changes across the course of pregnancy.

Conclusions

This study provides qualitative evidence that pregnant adolescents in North Dayi District, Ghana, face significant dietary challenges arising from multiple intersecting factors. With regard to the first study objective, participants’ reported diets were centred on starchy staples with apparently limited diversity in protein-rich foods, fruits, and vegetables, though this interpretation is based on self-reported qualitative accounts rather than standardised dietary assessment. Second, nutritional knowledge was severely limited; most participants were unable to define a balanced diet or identify pregnancy-appropriate foods, and information received at ANC was poorly retained. Third, dietary practices were shaped by financial constraints, cultural food taboos, limited household decision-making autonomy, and reportedly inadequate dietary counselling in ANC services. These findings suggest that current health system approaches may inadequately address the specific nutritional needs of this population.

Recommendations

Addressing these challenges may require comprehensive, multi-level interventions. At the policy level, targeted food assistance programmes for pregnant adolescents (such as food vouchers, subsidised nutritious foods, or conditional cash transfers) and adolescent-specific nutrition policies mandating specialised counselling at all health facilities warrant consideration. At the health service level, ANC services could incorporate dedicated time for dietary assessment and adolescent-friendly nutrition education using visual aids and local language, and healthcare workers may benefit from training in adolescent nutrition counselling with communication approaches appropriate for this age group.

At the community level, family decision-makers — mothers, mothers-in-law, and partners — should be considered for inclusion in nutrition education to address cultural taboos and improve household support for adolescent dietary needs. Future research should conduct larger-scale studies incorporating standardised dietary assessment methods and biochemical measures to quantify the nutritional status of pregnant adolescents in rural Ghana, evaluate the effectiveness of specific interventions, and explore male partner involvement in supporting adolescent maternal nutrition.
