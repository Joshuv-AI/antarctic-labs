Composition-processing-property optimization of multi-waste ceramic wall products based on Ekibastuz TPP ash
Authors: Zhanar Kaliyeva, Danara Mazhit, Lyazat Aruova, Altynay Shinguzhiyeva, Zurani Kayupova
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The recycling of thermal power plant ash and other industrial wastes in ceramic wall products is a promising strategy for reducing natural clay consumption; however, the interaction between ash dosage, multi-waste composition, pressing time, firing temperature, and ceramic performance remains insufficiently clarified. This study evaluates ceramic wall products based on Ekibastuz thermal power plant ash, red brick waste, metallurgical slag, and glass waste, with emphasis on composition-processing-property relationships and microstructural mechanisms. Ceramic mixtures containing 0–30% thermal power plant ash and selected multi-waste combinations were pressure-molded at 15 MPa, pressed for 60–90 s, dried, and fired at 900–1100 °C. Compressive strength, water absorption, density, and microstructure were assessed to identify the optimal balance between waste incorporation and material performance. The highest performance was achieved by the mixture containing 20% thermal power plant ash, which reached 43.5 MPa compressive strength, 6.1% water absorption, and 2.30 g/cm³ density after firing at 1100 °C and pressing for 90 s. Increasing ash content to 30% reduced strength because of increased residual porosity and microstructural heterogeneity. Multi-waste mixtures containing red brick waste, metallurgical slag, and glass waste produced technically acceptable ceramics but did not exceed the optimized ash-only composition, showing that maximum waste replacement does not automatically provide maximum performance. The main innovation of the study is the identification of a controlled composition-processing-property window in which moderate ash incorporation improves densification and pore refinement, whereas excessive ash or multi-waste loading promotes heterogeneity. The findings demonstrate the engineering significance of optimized waste-derived ceramic systems for sustainable wall-product manufacturing.

Introduction

In the modern world, the problems associated with industrial waste processing and disposal are increasingly relevant1,2. One of the most common waste products generated by thermal power plants (TPPs) is fly ash, which accumulates in enormous quantities in ash dumps. These wastes have a serious negative impact on the environment, including soil pollution, contamination of surface water and groundwater, and increased greenhouse gas emissions during storage. Therefore, there is an urgent need to find solutions for the rational use of TPP ash3–5.

TPP fly ash has a unique chemical and mineral composition, making it a promising material for use in the construction industry6–8. The main components of fly ash (silicon, aluminum, and iron oxides) play a key role in forming the structure of building materials such as concrete, cement, and ceramics. In recent years, researchers have extensively studied the possibility of integrating TPP ash into the production of ceramic products. This material reduces the environmental impact but also improves the properties of the final product9–11.

The environmental relevance of waste-derived construction materials is also linked to the high energy demand and CO₂ emissions associated with ordinary Portland cement production. Recent studies on low-carbon supersulfated cement and solid waste-based binders show that alternative binder systems can reduce dependence on Portland cement while maintaining engineering performance. Although the present study focuses on fired ceramic wall products, these works support the broader transition toward construction materials based on waste valorization and reduced primary raw-material consumption12,13.

Previous studies have confirmed that TPP ash can be used as a functional component in clay-based ceramics, bricks, blocks, cementitious composites, and lightweight construction materials. Its beneficial effects are mainly associated with the high contents of SiO₂, Al₂O₃, Fe₂O₃, and CaO, which contribute to densification, phase formation, partial vitrification, and reduced consumption of natural clay during firing14–19. Reported results also indicate that controlled fly ash incorporation may improve compressive strength, decrease water absorption, reduce firing-related energy demand, and support the development of lightweight or thermally efficient construction products in studies where thermal properties were directly measured20–27. However, most previous studies have focused either on single-waste systems or on cement-based materials, while fewer works have examined ceramic wall products produced from a combined waste system involving TPP ash, red brick waste, metallurgical slag, and glass waste under the same pressing and firing conditions.

Literature data also provide quantitative evidence of these effects. Fly ash/lime lightweight bricks have been reported to reach a thermal conductivity of 0.225 W·m⁻¹·K⁻¹ and a compressive strength of 76.5 kgf/cm², demonstrating their potential as thermally efficient masonry units. Fly ash-based lightweight and foam construction materials have also shown thermal conductivity values of approximately 0.15–0.48 W·m⁻¹·K⁻¹, with dry densities ranging from about 585 to 1370 kg/m³ depending on porosity and mixture design. In fired clay or ceramic systems, these improvements are generally associated with particle packing, fluxing behavior, phase formation, and controlled pore development; however, excessive ash content may increase porosity and reduce mechanical performance28,29.

The recycling of ash is an extremely acute problem in Kazakhstan, where high coal consumption for energy generation leads to massive waste accumulation30,31. The Ekibastuz coal basin, the largest in the country, is the primary source of coal for coal-fired TPPs. Consequently, TPP ash in Ekibastuz is generated in large quantities, creating additional challenges for its disposal. In this context, the integration of ash in ceramic wall products could be an effective solution for Kazakhstan32,33.

The application of TPP ash from Ekibastuz for ceramic wall products represents a rational approach to addressing this issue. Firstly, it enables the recycling of substantial waste volumes, minimizing environmental impact. Secondly, the use of local raw materials promotes the development of the regional construction industry and enhances its economic efficiency. Finally, adapting international experience to local conditions opens up new opportunities for scientific research and industrial implementation.

In addition to TPP ash, materials such as red brick waste, metallurgical slags, and glass waste are of significant interest for their potential integration into the production of ceramic wall products34–36. Red brick waste, generated from the demolition of old buildings or brick manufacturing, contains substantial amounts of silicon and aluminum oxides, making it a promising component for ceramic mixtures. Its use facilitates the recycling of construction waste and may affect the mechanical behavior and high-temperature stability of ceramic mixtures37. Metallurgical slags, such as those from steel and iron production, have a unique chemical composition, including iron, calcium, and magnesium oxides. These components can act as fluxing agents, lowering the sintering temperature of ceramic products while improving their density and strength. The utilization of slags also contributes to reducing waste volumes in the metallurgical industry, holding profound environmental importance38. Glass waste, generated in large quantities from household and industrial consumption, contains a high percentage of silicon oxide. When added to ceramic mixtures, glass can serve as a flux, reducing firing temperatures and promoting the formation of a vitreous phase, thereby improving the density and water absorption of the products. Furthermore, the use of glass waste helps address disposal challenges and lightens landfill loads39. Recent studies have further emphasized the sustainability potential of waste glass in cement-free and alkali-activated construction materials. Recycled glass has been used together with slag-based binders and waste steel fibers to produce sustainable composites with improved mechanical performance, while waste glass powder and waste glass sand have also been investigated as substitutes for conventional mineral components in alkali-activated slag systems. These findings confirm that glass waste can act not only as a recycled filler but also as a functional silica-rich component contributing to resource conservation and reduced dependence on primary raw materials40–42. Thus, the integration of red brick waste, metallurgical slags, and glass waste into the production of ceramic wall products presents a novel avenue for the development of environmentally sustainable and cost-effective building materials. This approach not only alleviates environmental impact but also enhances the physical and mechanical properties of the final product, rendering these materials promising for further research and industrial implementation.

However, the scientific challenge of multi-waste ceramic production is not limited to replacing natural clay with secondary raw materials34–42. In such systems, each waste-derived component may perform a different technological function during forming and firing. TPP ash acts mainly as an aluminosilicate source and may contribute to matrix densification when its content is controlled. Red brick waste provides ceramic-compatible silicate and aluminosilicate particles that can participate in particle packing and high-temperature consolidation. Metallurgical slag introduces CaO- and MgO-rich phases that may act as fluxing components and influence liquid-phase sintering. Glass waste supplies silica and alkali oxides, which can promote partial vitrification and reduce open porosity. Therefore, the combined use of these wastes may improve ceramic performance only when their chemical and granulometric effects are balanced. If the total waste loading is excessive, the same components may increase heterogeneity, residual porosity, and local microstructural discontinuity.

Despite the growing number of studies on fly ash, brick waste, slag, and glass waste in construction materials, most available works examine these wastes separately or focus on cement-based systems rather than fired ceramic wall products14–19. Limited attention has been given to the comparative behavior of ash-only and multi-waste ceramic systems produced under identical pressing and firing conditions. As a result, it remains unclear whether combining several waste-derived components provides a synergistic improvement in ceramic performance or whether the increased complexity of the mixture produces a trade-off between sustainability, densification, and mechanical strength. This gap is especially important for local Kazakhstan raw materials, including Ekibastuz TPP ash, because their chemical and mineralogical variability may strongly affect sintering behavior and final product stability.

Accordingly, the novelty of this study lies in the controlled comparison of ash-only and multi-waste ceramic wall products under unified processing conditions. Unlike studies that evaluate waste replacement mainly as an environmental measure, this work identifies how waste composition, pressing time, and firing temperature interact to control densification, pore development, water absorption, compressive strength, density, and microstructural compactness. This approach makes it possible to determine not only the optimal replacement level but also the material mechanism explaining why moderate TPP ash incorporation improves performance, while excessive ash or excessive multi-waste loading may reduce the structural quality of fired ceramics.

The goal of this study was to develop and experimentally evaluate ceramic wall products incorporating Ekibastuz TPP ash, red brick waste, metallurgical slag, and glass waste, with emphasis on the relationship between mixture composition, processing conditions, and final material performance. Key research objectives are:

1) Chemical and mineralogical analysis of TPP ash, red brick waste, metallurgical slags, glass waste, and clay to determine their composition and suitability for ceramic production;

2) Formulation and preparation of ceramic mixtures with varying contents of TPP ash and other additives (red brick waste, metallurgical slags, glass waste) to study their influence on the final product’s properties;

3) Molding, drying, and firing of samples at different temperatures to determine optimal production conditions;

4) Evaluation of compressive strength, water absorption, density, and microstructure to determine how waste composition, pressing time, and firing temperature affect ceramic product performance;

5) Identification of optimal proportions of TPP ash and other additives in ceramic mixtures to achieve the best physical and mechanical properties.

Sample preparation

To investigate the effect of TPP ash, red brick waste, metallurgical slag, and glass waste on ceramic wall products, mixtures with varying contents of these materials were prepared.

The mixture design was structured to separate the effect of TPP ash dosage from the combined effect of multi-waste modification. Mixtures M, M1, M2, and M3 were used to evaluate the influence of increasing TPP ash content from 0 to 30% under identical processing conditions. Mixtures M4, M5, and M6 were designed to assess whether the addition of red brick waste, metallurgical slag, and glass waste can provide comparable or improved performance through combined aluminosilicate contribution, fluxing action, and densification during firing.

Molding, drying, and firing conditions.

For all mixtures, a constant pressure of 15 MPa was applied, ensuring sufficient density and structural uniformity of the samples. The pressing time varied: 60, 75, and 90 s for each mixture. This made it possible to examine the effect of pressing time on sample density and mechanical properties. All samples had identical dimensions of 50 × 50 × 50 mm, ensuring equal conditions for subsequent testing. The sample drying process consisted of two stages: initial drying at 50 °C for 24 h and main drying at 105 °C until a constant mass was reached. This two-stage drying enabled complete moisture removal from the samples before firing. The samples were fired at three temperatures: 900 °C, 1000 °C, and 1100 °C. The heating time to reach the firing temperature was 4, 5, or 6 h, depending on the mixture. After reaching the target temperature, the samples were held in the furnace for 2 h to complete the sintering process.

After the holding stage, the samples were cooled inside the furnace to minimize thermal shock and reduce the risk of crack formation. The average cooling rate was maintained at approximately 2.5–3.0 °C/min until the temperature decreased below 200 °C, after which the samples were allowed to cool naturally to room temperature. Figure 3 presents the temperature-time profile used for the heat-treatment process, including heating, holding, controlled furnace cooling, and final natural cooling stages.

Controlled cooling was used to reduce thermal gradients inside the samples and limit cracking caused by rapid temperature changes. This regime ensured comparable thermal histories for all mixtures and supported reliable comparison of the resulting physical and mechanical properties.

Results

This section presents the findings of the study on the effects of TPP ash on the properties of ceramic wall products, focusing on their physical, mechanical, and microstructural characteristics. The results were interpreted by considering the combined influence of waste composition, pressing time, and firing temperature on densification, pore development, and strength formation. Therefore, the comparison focuses not only on the numerical differences between mixtures but also on the material mechanisms responsible for changes in compressive strength, water absorption, and density.

In most mixtures, increasing the firing temperature from 900 to 1100 °C increased compressive strength and reduced water absorption, indicating improved sintering and lower open porosity. The best result was obtained for M2 at 1100 °C, where compressive strength reached 43.5 MPa and water absorption decreased to 6.1%. The lower performance of M3 confirms that increasing TPP ash content to 30% creates excessive porosity and weakens the ceramic matrix.

The effect of pressing time was also evident from the comparative results. For all mixtures, increasing the pressing time from 60 to 90 s generally increased compressive strength and density and reduced water absorption. This trend can be explained by improved particle packing and lower initial pore volume before firing. The 90 s pressing time provided the most favorable compaction conditions, which is why it was selected as the reference condition for graphical presentation and statistical comparison. However, the improvement was more pronounced for mixtures with balanced waste contents, especially M2, whereas the 30% ash mixture remained limited by residual porosity even after longer pressing.

The low CV values indicate acceptable repeatability of the experimental measurements. Based on Figs. 5 and 6 and the statistical data in Table 8, the control mixture (M), consisting solely of clay, demonstrated the lowest performance characteristics under the reference condition: compressive strength did not exceed 33.5 MPa at 1100 °C, water absorption reached 8.8%, and density was 2.08 g/cm³, indicating relatively high porosity and limited mechanical stability. The introduction of 10% TPP ash (M1) improved the properties: strength increased to 36.2 MPa, water absorption decreased to 8.1%, and density rose to 2.15 g/cm³, suggesting densification of the material structure. However, the best results were observed in mixture M2 (20% TPP ash): strength reached its maximum value of 43.5 MPa, water absorption decreased to 6.1%, and density increased to 2.30 g/cm³. This result can be attributed to the optimal pore distribution at this ash content, reduced internal porosity, and partial vitrification of the material structure, which enhances mechanical properties. Further increase of ash content to 30% (M3) resulted in the deterioration of properties: strength decreased to 39.2 MPa, density reduced to 2.22 g/cm³, and water absorption increased to 6.9%. An ash content of more than 20% creates excessive porosity, affecting density and mechanical integrity, and the material becomes less resistant to loads and moisture.

M4-M6, containing additional components, showed results similar to M2 but were slightly less effective in key parameters. M4 (10% ash, 10% brick waste, 5% slag, 5% glass) achieved a strength of 42.0 MPa, a water absorption of 6.2%, and a density of 2.28 g/cm³. The resulting material exhibits a high level of density yet remains slightly inferior to the strength of M2. M5 (15% ash, 15% brick waste, 5% slag, 5% glass) demonstrated a strength of 39.9 MPa, a water absorption of 6.8%, and a density of 2.25 g/cm³, indicating a minor increase in porosity when compared to M2. Although M6 contained the same TPP ash content as M2, its compressive strength was lower because the additional red brick waste, metallurgical slag, and glass waste increased the total secondary raw-material fraction and may have introduced local heterogeneity into the ceramic matrix. Therefore, the difference between M2 and M6 should not be interpreted as a detrimental effect of TPP ash itself. Instead, it indicates that the beneficial effect of fly ash depends on the balance between ash content, clay fraction, fluxing components, and pore development during firing.

A critical finding of the study is that the multi-waste approach provided a clear sustainability and resource-efficiency advantage but did not automatically produce the highest mechanical performance. Mixture M6 contained 50% waste-derived components and therefore achieved the largest clay replacement, the highest estimated material-related CO₂ reduction, and the highest raw-material cost saving. However, its compressive strength remained lower than that of M2, although both mixtures contained 20% TPP ash. This result shows that the performance of waste-derived ceramic wall products is controlled not only by the amount of secondary raw material but also by the balance between the clay matrix, aluminosilicate contribution, fluxing oxides, particle packing, and residual pore formation. Therefore, the main benefit of the multi-waste mixtures is not the achievement of maximum strength, but the ability to maintain technically acceptable ceramic properties while substantially increasing waste utilization and reducing natural clay consumption.

This finding is important because it separates two optimization targets: maximum technical performance and maximum resource substitution. M2 represents the technical optimum, with the highest compressive strength, lowest water absorption, and highest density under the tested conditions. In contrast, M4-M6 demonstrate the feasibility of multi-waste ceramic production and show that combined red brick waste, metallurgical slag, and glass waste can partially compensate for clay reduction through ceramic-compatible particles and fluxing action. However, when the total secondary raw-material fraction becomes too high, local heterogeneity and residual porosity can limit further strength improvement. Thus, the results provide a more specific contribution than the general observation that firing temperature affects ceramic quality: they identify the trade-off between mechanical optimization and multi-waste sustainability in fired ceramic wall products.

Thus, M2 should be considered the technical optimum, whereas M4-M6 should be interpreted as resource-efficiency compositions. The 20% TPP ash content in M2 provided the most favorable combination of matrix densification, pore control, compressive strength, water resistance, and density. The reduced performance of M3 indicates the upper limit of ash-only replacement under the tested firing regime, while the results for M4-M6 show that multi-waste incorporation is feasible but must be optimized to avoid excessive heterogeneity. This distinction strengthens the practical interpretation of the results because it shows that ceramic mixture design should be selected according to the required balance between mechanical performance, waste utilization, and raw-material saving.

Discussion

The main scientific contribution of this study is the identification of a composition-processing-property relationship for ceramic wall products incorporating local TPP ash and additional waste-derived components. The results show that the beneficial effect of TPP ash is not linear: 20% ash content improved densification and strength, whereas 30% ash increased porosity and reduced performance. Therefore, the study demonstrates that waste incorporation must be optimized together with pressing and firing conditions rather than treated as a simple raw-material substitution.

TPP ash has been widely investigated as an alternative aluminosilicate raw material for construction composites and ceramic-based products43–47. Previous studies reported that controlled ash incorporation can improve strength, reduce water absorption, and support denser matrix formation when the replacement level remains within an optimal range14,16. The present results are consistent with these findings: mixture M2 with 20% TPP ash reached the highest compressive strength of 43.5 MPa and the lowest water absorption of 6.1%, confirming that moderate ash addition can improve ceramic performance under suitable firing conditions.

The improvement observed for M2 can be attributed to a balance between aluminosilicate phase formation, pore refinement, and partial vitrification during firing. At 20% TPP ash, the ceramic matrix became denser, which is reflected in the simultaneous increase in compressive strength and density and the decrease in water absorption. In contrast, the 30% ash mixture showed lower performance, indicating that excessive ash addition can increase residual porosity and reduce the continuity of the fired ceramic skeleton.

At the microstructural level, the improvement in M2 can be explained by the simultaneous development of particle rearrangement, liquid-phase-assisted bonding, and pore closure during firing. The fine fractions of TPP ash can occupy voids between clay particles and increase packing density before firing. During heating, aluminosilicate components participate in the formation of a more continuous ceramic skeleton, while CaO- and alkali-containing minor phases may support the development of a limited liquid phase. This liquid phase improves interparticle bonding and promotes partial vitrification, which reduces open porosity and limits water penetration. As a result, M2 achieved the most favorable combination of high density, low water absorption, and high compressive strength.

The role of the SiO₂-Al₂O₃-CaO system is especially important for explaining the difference between ash-only and multi-waste mixtures. SiO₂ and Al₂O₃ form the main ceramic framework, whereas CaO, MgO, and alkali oxides introduced by slag and glass waste act as fluxing components. In moderate amounts, these oxides can lower the effective sintering temperature and promote the formation of a glassy phase that fills pores and strengthens interparticle contacts. However, when the amount of secondary raw materials becomes excessive, the distribution of fluxing phases may become less uniform, producing local differences in shrinkage, pore closure, and matrix continuity. This explains why the multi-waste mixtures remained mechanically acceptable but did not exceed the optimized ash-only composition.

The decrease in performance at 30% TPP ash agrees with studies showing that excessive ash addition may increase porosity and reduce the mechanical continuity of the fired matrix23,26. In the present study, M3 reached 39.2 MPa at 1100 °C and 90 s, which was lower than the 43.5 MPa obtained for M2. This confirms that the maximum waste replacement level is not necessarily the most effective option and that the mixture must be optimized according to both strength and water-resistance criteria. The reduction in M3 can therefore be interpreted as a threshold effect rather than a simple dilution effect. At 30% TPP ash, the amount of ash-derived particles becomes too high for the clay matrix to bind uniformly during firing. This may lead to ash-rich zones, incomplete sintering of local regions, and the preservation of interconnected pores. Such pores act as preferential paths for water absorption and as stress concentrators during compression, which explains the simultaneous decrease in strength and increase in water absorption.

The influence of firing temperature also supports the importance of processing optimization. In most mixtures, higher firing temperature improved strength and density and reduced water absorption, indicating more effective sintering. However, the comparison between M2 and M3 shows that temperature alone cannot compensate for an excessive ash content. This observation is consistent with literature emphasizing that the benefits of ash-based ceramic modification depend on both composition and firing regime25,43–47.

The behavior of the multi-waste mixtures M4-M6 shows that combining several waste streams produces both beneficial and limiting effects. Red brick waste can act as a ceramic-compatible silicate filler and improve particle packing because its composition is close to fired clay material. Metallurgical slag supplies CaO- and MgO-rich phases that may enhance fluxing and support liquid-phase sintering. Glass waste contributes silica and alkali oxides, promoting partial vitrification and reducing open porosity. These mechanisms explain why M4-M6 maintained acceptable strength, water absorption, and density despite higher levels of clay replacement. However, the simultaneous presence of several waste-derived components also increases the risk of local chemical and granulometric heterogeneity. When the total secondary raw-material fraction becomes high, as in M6, the ceramic matrix may contain regions with different sintering behavior, uneven vitrified phase distribution, and residual pores. This explains why M6 achieved strong resource-efficiency benefits but did not surpass M2 in compressive strength.

Compared with previous studies on ash-modified ceramic and construction materials, the present work adds two important findings. First, it confirms that moderate TPP ash incorporation can improve fired ceramic performance through densification and pore refinement, which agrees with earlier observations for controlled ash replacement systems. Second, and more importantly, it shows that multi-waste ceramic design does not follow a simple rule in which more waste produces better performance. The comparison between M2 and M6 demonstrates that mechanical optimization and waste-utilization optimization may lead to different mixture choices. This distinction strengthens the contribution of the study because it moves the discussion from simple waste replacement toward a composition-processing-property mechanism for selecting ceramic wall-product formulations43–47.

The economic potential of using TPP ash and other waste-derived components is associated mainly with partial replacement of natural clay and reduced dependence on primary raw materials. Previous studies have reported that the use of ash as a secondary raw material can reduce production-related material costs19. In the present study, the 20% TPP ash mixture is especially relevant because it improved performance while reducing clay content, which supports both resource efficiency and practical feasibility.

From an environmental perspective, the use of TPP ash and other industrial wastes can reduce landfill disposal and decrease the demand for natural clay extraction. Previous studies have emphasized that fly ash utilization can contribute to lower environmental burdens when it replaces primary raw materials in construction products23,30. In this study, the environmental benefit is mainly associated with the partial substitution of clay by waste-derived components.

A key limitation of ash-based ceramic production is the variability of TPP ash composition, which may affect sintering behavior and final product stability8. Therefore, future optimization should include ash pre-treatment, compositional normalization, and durability testing. Previous studies have shown that mechanochemical treatment and combined waste systems can improve the stability and performance of ash-containing materials24,26. The present results support this direction, but they also show that multi-waste incorporation must be controlled to avoid excessive porosity and heterogeneous microstructure.

Overall, the study confirms that 20% TPP ash is the most effective replacement level under the tested conditions. The main practical implication is that ceramic wall products can incorporate locally available industrial and construction wastes while maintaining suitable strength, water absorption, and density. However, before large-scale implementation, further studies should evaluate long-term durability, leaching behavior, thermal conductivity, abrasion resistance, dimensional stability, freeze-thaw resistance, and chemical or UV exposure resistance.

Although the results confirm the technical feasibility of incorporating TPP ash, metallurgical slag, red brick waste, and glass waste into ceramic wall products, the potential leaching risk of trace elements should be considered as an important environmental limitation. In this study, leaching behavior was not experimentally evaluated; therefore, no conclusion can be made regarding the release of heavy metals or other potentially hazardous elements from the fired products. Although high-temperature firing can promote the immobilization of some elements within aluminosilicate or glassy phases, this assumption must be verified experimentally for the specific waste compositions used in this study. Future work should include standardized leaching tests to assess the environmental safety of the ceramic materials before large-scale practical application.

The use of the produced ceramic wall products also has certain limitations that should be considered before practical implementation. In this study, the material performance was evaluated mainly through compressive strength, water absorption, density, and SEM analysis; therefore, dimensional stability, cracking susceptibility, abrasion resistance, chemical resistance, UV exposure resistance, and freeze-thaw durability were not directly tested. Based on the obtained results, mixtures with lower water absorption and denser microstructure, especially M2, are expected to show better resistance to moisture-related deterioration than more porous mixtures. However, products containing higher waste contents, such as M3 and M6, may be more sensitive to local heterogeneity, pore concentration, and potential cracking during service. Therefore, the proposed material is most suitable for non-load-bearing or moderately loaded wall applications under controlled exposure conditions until additional durability and environmental-resistance tests are performed.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that Ekibastuz TPP ash can be effectively incorporated into ceramic wall products when the replacement level is optimized together with pressing and firing conditions. The best performance was obtained for the mixture containing 20% TPP ash, which achieved 43.5 MPa compressive strength, 6.1% water absorption, and 2.30 g/cm³ density after firing at 1100 °C and pressing for 90 s.

The results show that the effect of TPP ash is not linear. Moderate ash incorporation improved densification and reduced water absorption, whereas 30% ash increased porosity and lowered mechanical performance. Multi-waste mixtures containing red brick waste, metallurgical slag, and glass waste also produced acceptable ceramic properties, but they did not exceed the performance of the 20% ash-only mixture.

The main significance of the study is the identification of a composition-processing-property relationship for ceramic wall products produced from local waste-derived materials. The findings support the use of TPP ash and related industrial wastes as secondary raw materials for reducing natural clay consumption and improving resource efficiency in ceramic production. Further work should evaluate long-term durability, leaching behavior, thermal conductivity, abrasion resistance, dimensional stability, and resistance to environmental exposure before industrial-scale implementation.
