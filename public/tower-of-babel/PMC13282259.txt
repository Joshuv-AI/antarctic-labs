Acupuncture Versus Clomiphene for Ovulation Induction in Women With Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS): A Randomized Clinical Trial
Authors: Leili Hafizi, Hamideh Azizi, Malihe Mahmoudinia, Mona Jafari, Hamidreza Bahrami Taghanaki, Habibollah Esmaili, Shiva Golmohammadzadeh, Hoda Azizi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsThere is evidence of the beneficial effects of acupuncture in the treatment of polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS). This study aimed to compare acupuncture with clomiphene in ovulation induction among infertile women with PCOS.MethodsThis randomized clinical trial was conducted on infertile women with PCOS who were referred to the gynecology clinic of Imam Reza Hospital and Milad Infertility Center, Mashhad, Iran, from December 2015 to July 2016. Patients were randomly assigned to two groups; the intervention group (38 patients) received true acupuncture plus placebo clomiphene, whereas the control group (39 patients) received clomiphene plus sham acupuncture. Patients were compared in terms of folliculare growth, evidence of ovulation, and side effects of treatment.ResultsThere was no statistically significant difference between the two groups in terms of demographic characteristics and PCOS criteria. The ovulation rates were 31.5% and 41.6% in the intervention and control groups respectively, indicating no statistically significant difference(p = 0.36). Also, in terms of side effects, the statistical difference between the two groups was not significant, except for headache which was significantly more common in the intervention group (p = 0.001). The rate of folliculargrowth was significantly higher in the control group during the first and third cycles (p = 0.01), whereas no significant difference was observed in the second cycle (p = 0.93).ConclusionAcupuncture can be as effective as clomiphene treatment in inducing ovulation in infertile women with PCOS. Moreover, considering the few side effects of acupuncture, its application can be considered a safe option. Trial Registration: IRCT201512057265N3.

Introduction

Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is the most common disorder associated with chronic anovulation, with a global prevalence of 6%–21%, and can lead to infertility [1]. This condition is characterized by a combination of hyperandrogenism (clinical or biochemical), chronic anovulation, and polycystic ovaries [2]. It is also a leading cause of hyperandrogenism, hirsutism, and infertility due to anovulation in developed countries [3, 4]. Ovulation, the release of a mature oocyte from the ovary, is a prerequisite for natural conception, whereas anovulation is a major cause of infertility in women with PCOS. Infertile women with anovulation who wish to conceive are eligible candidates for ovulation induction [5].

Currently, the first‐line treatment for infertility caused by anovulation in women with PCOS is selective estrogen receptor modulators, such as clomiphene [6]. It is typically prescribed with an empirical incremental method, 50–150 mg per day for 5 days starting from the third to the fifth day of menstruation, until the lowest effective dose is identified [5]. The main problem with clomiphene treatment is resistance, which occurs in 15%–40% of women with PCOS [7]. In cases where clomiphene treatment fails, combined treatment with metformin, followed by gonadotropins or laparoscopic ovarian cautery considered an alternative option [8]. Gonadotropin therapy has an ovulation rate of 70%–72% and a pregnancy rate of 15%–30%, but it is expensive and requires intensive ovarian monitoring [9, 10].

Laparoscopic ovarian surgery, such as ovarian drilling with electrocautery or laser, results in an ovulation rate of 30%–90% and pregnancy rates of 13%–88%. However, this method is invasive, costly, requires hospitalization and anesthesia, and is associated with certain side effects [11]. These challenges highlight the need to identify effective of adjuvant treatments to improve the management of infertility and ovulatory disorders in women with PCOS.

Acupuncture has received considerable attention in recent years as a treatment for reproductive endocrine disorders and infertility [12, 13, 14, 15, 16]. It appears that acupuncture may induce ovulation in women with PCOS by reducing plasma beta‐endorphin and sympathetic nerve activity, as well as by affecting nerve growth factor (NGF) and adrenergic receptors [17, 18]. Findings from previous clinical and experimental studies suggest that acupuncture may stimulate ovulation and improve fertility in women with PCOS. However, the quality of these studies varies in terms of methodology, and some are considered to be of low quality [19, 20, 21].

Although the majority of these studies indicate the effectiveness of acupuncture, a recent meta‐analysis comprising 51 studies concluded that acupuncture and its combined therapies may improve clinical outcomes in women with PCOS. However, the observed risk of bias and substantial heterogeneity indicate that current evidence is insufficient to draw definitive conclusions, and further well‐designed randomized controlled trials are strongly recommended [22]. Therefore, the present study was designed to evaluate the effectiveness of acupuncture in inducing ovulation in infertile women with PCOS, compared with clomiphene.

Methods

Ethics Statement

This study was a single‐blind randomized clinical trial conducted among infertile women with PCOS who were referred to the gynecology clinic of Imam Reza Hospital, Milad Infertility Treatment Center, and the Acupuncture Clinic of Imam Reza Hospital in Mashhad, Iran. It was conducted from December 2015 to July 2016. All participants were informed about the study and provided written informed consent. The Ethics Committee of Mashhad University of Medical Sciences approved the study (approval code:IR.MUMS.fm.REC.1394.346). The study was registered in the Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials with the number IRCT201512057265N3. This study was reported in accordance with the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines [23], as illustrated in the CONSORT flow diagram (Figure 1).

CONSORT flow diagram illustrating participant recruitment, eligibility assessment, randomization, allocation to intervention and control groups, follow‐up, and inclusion in the final analysis for a randomized controlled trial of infertile women with polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS).

Sample Size

Sample size calculation was performed based on the results of Li et al. [24], considering P1 = 0.8, P2 = 0.5, Z1 − α/2 = 1.96, Z1 − β = 0.84, with 95% confidence and 80% power, a sample size of 40 patients per group was determined. Participants were recruited using a convenience sampling method.

Eligibility Criteria and Recruitment

Eligible participants were infertile women aged 20–40 years with a diagnosis of PCOS based on the Rotterdam criteria, which require at least two of the following: chronic oligomenorrhea (cycle length > 35 days or < 8 periods/year) or amenorrhea (> 90 days between periods); clinical and/or biochemical hyperandrogenism (modified Ferriman–Gallwey score ≥ 5 or serum testosterone > 80 ng/dL); and polycystic ovarian morphology on ultrasound (≥ 12 antral follicles measuring 2–9 mm or ovarian volume > 10 cm3). and willingness to participate in the study.

Exclusion criteria included other endocrine disorders (hyperprolactinemia, follicle‐Stimulating Hormone(FSH) > 15 IU/L, thyroid‐Stimulating Hormone(TSH) < 0.2 or > 5.5 mIU/L, diabetes, suspected Cushing's syndrome), use of hormonal or hormone‐containing herbal medications during the past 3 months, pregnancy within the past 6 months, recent abortion/childbirth or breastfeeding within the past 6 months, liver or kidney disorders, anemia (Hb < 10 g/dL), history of deep vein thrombosis (DVT), pulmonary embolism, or cerebrovascular accident, substance abuse, use of medications affecting reproductive or metabolic function (e.g., oral contraceptive ills (OCPs), gonadotropin‐Releasing Hormone (GnRH) agonists/antagonists; 2‐month washout required), uncontrolled hypertension, and unwillingness to continue participation.

Randomization, Blinding, and Treatment Allocation

Patients were randomly assigned to either the intervention or control group using a random number table, with allocation concealment ensured through sealed, opaque, and sequentially numbered envelopes. In this trial, participants, the outcome assessor, the data collector, and statistician were blinded to the treatment allocation to minimize potential sources of bias. The acupuncturist could not be blinded to the treatment assignments due to the nature of the interventions.

Treatment in both groups commenced on cycle Day 3 following the onset of a spontaneous menstrual period or withdrawal bleeding induced by progesterone.

Intervention

The intervention group received true acupuncture and a clomiphene placebo.The true acupuncture protocol followed the STRICTA guidelines [25] and was performed twice weekly for 30 min by a licensed acupuncturist with more than 10 years of experience. Sterile, single‐use stainless steel filiform needles (0.25 mm in diameter and 30–40 mm in length) were inserted at the following acupoints: CV3 (Zhongji), CV4 (Guanyuan), CV6 (Qihai), ST28 (Shuidao), ST29 (Guilai), EX‑CA1 (Zigong), SP10 (Xuehai), ST36 (Zusanli), SP6 (Sanyinjiao), LR3 (Taichong), and LI4 (Hegu). These points were selected because they share somatic innervation with the ovaries and uterus, have been shown in previous studies to increase uterine or ovarian blood flow, or may act through central nervous system mechanisms. The combination of SP4 (Gongsun) and PC6 (Neiguan), also LU7 (Lieque) and KI6 (Zhaohai), was selected base on traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) indication for regulating the Chong and Ren meridians. Limb points were inserted perpendicularly to a depth of 15–25 mm and abdominal points to 25–35 mm, with bidirectional manual manipulation until the patient reported deqi. Abdominal points were connected to a low‐frequency electroacupuncture device (continuous wave, 2 Hz) with intensity adjusted to elicit a gentle, comfortable muscle twitch. Acupuncture treatment was discontinued upon confirmation of ovulation, and the time interval from the start of the intervention to ovulation was recorded. If ovulation did not occur, treatment continued for a maximum of 32 sessions. Sterile disposable needles of 0.25 × 40 mm (Suzhou, China) were used. Clomiphene placebo tablets were prepared by the Faculty of Pharmacy of Mashhad University of Medical Sciences. Participants were instructed to take the tablets from the third day of the cycle for 5 nights, one tablet daily in the first cycle, two tablets per day in the second cycle, and 3 tablets per day in the third cycle.

Control

The control group received sham acupuncture and clomiphene. Clomiphene citrate was started at a dose of 50 mg daily for 5 days in the first cycle. If the treatment continued in the second cycle, the patient was given a dose of 100 mg daily (two tablets), and in the third cycle, 150 mg daily (three tablets). Patients in the control group underwent sham acupuncture (30‐min sessions) twice a week during the entire treatment period. Sham points were located on true point locations, but needles were inserted through adhesive sterile pads, touched the skin without penetrating it, and were then withdrawn so that the needle tips remained inside the pads. Abdominal needles were connected to the electroacupuncture device. The device was off, but its timer was turned on to create a sound resembling real electroacupuncture.Needles identical in size and brand to those used in the true acupuncture group were employed. Sham acupuncture sessions were matched to the intervention group in timing and frequency.

Outcome

The primary outcome measure in this study was the comparison of ovulation rates between the two groups. Ovulation was confirmed by serial transvaginal ultrasound examinations performed every 3 days starting from cycle day 3, demonstrating a dominant follicle ≥ 18 mm followed by follicular collapse and free fluid in the cul‑de‑sac.

Secondary outcomes included the comparison of the number of growing follicles (≥ 14 mm) and mature follicles (≥ 18 mm) during the follow‐up cycles and the comparison of side effects between the two groups.

The treatment was stopped when ovulation was confirmed, and time interval from the start of the intervention to ovulationwas recorded. If ovulation did not occurn, the treatment was continued for up to 32 sessions.

Data Analysis (Statistics) Section

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 19 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test with Lilliefors' correction. For normally distributed variables, independent samples t‐tests were applied; for non‐normally distributed variables, Mann–Whitney U tests were used. Associations between categorical variables were evaluated using Pearson's χ 2 test or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate. All tests were two‐sided unless stated otherwise, with a priori significance set at α = 0.05. Effect sizes and 95% confidence intervals are reported where applicable.

Results

Among the 82 patients initially included in the study, five participants were excluded prior to analysis: two due to withdrawal of consent, one due to elevated TSH (> 5.5 mIU/L), and two due to newly diagnosed diabetes.the final analysis included 77 patients : 38 patients in the intervention group and 39 patients in the control group. Ovulation assessment could not be performed in two women in the intervention group and one in the control group (Figure 1).

Maximum follicle diameter

In the first cycle, the mean maximum follicle diameter was 20.9 ± 4.2 mm in the intervention group (n = 38) and 18.2 ± 3.7 mm in the control group (n = 39), with a mean difference of 2.7 mm (95% CI: −0.1 to 5.5, p = 0.06; independent samples t‐test). In the second cycle, follicle diameter was 20.0 ± 5.2 mm vs. 17.0 ± 2.9 mm, mean difference 3.0 mm (95% CI: −0.5 to 6.5, p = 0.09).

In the third cycle, the control group had a larger mean maximum follicle diameter (21.6 ± 5.5 mm) compared with the intervention group (14.2 ± 3.8 mm), mean difference −7.4 mm (95% CI: −10.6 to − 4.2, p= 0.01). Overall, across all three cycles, no statistically significant difference was detected in maximum follicle size between groups (Table 2).

Ovulation

Ovulation occurred in 27 of 74 patients (36.5%) across all cycles, with a mean ovulation day of 17.7 ± 2.6 of the cycle. In the intervention group, ovulation occurred in 15 of 36 patients (41.7%, 95% CI: 25.5–58.9), most frequently during the second cycle. In the control group, ovulation occurred in 12 of 38 patients (31.6%, 95% CI: 17.5–48.7), more often during the third cycle.

The difference in ovulation frequency between groups was not statistically significant (risk difference 10.1%, 95% CI: −11.9 to 31.1; p = 0.36; χ 2 test) (Table 3).

Side Effects

The most common side effect observed in this study was headache with an incidence rate of 19.5%, followed by mood disorders and nausea 16.9%, breast tenderness 11.7%, flushing 1.9%, pelvic pain and visual disturbance 3.9%, severe pain at acupuncture points 2.6%, hematoma 1.2% %, and cyst formation 6.5%. Local infection and syncope were not observed in any of the patients.

Using the χ 2 test, the two groups demonstrated no statistically significant difference in terms of complications following the intervention. The prevalence of headache was significantly higher in the intervention group (p = 0.001); it was reported by 13 patients (34.2%) in the intervention group and 2 patients (5.2%) from the control group (Table 5).

Discussion

The results of this study showed that acupuncture treatment with placebo clomiphene may be as effective as clomiphene treatment and sham acupuncture in inducing ovulation. Furthermore, based on the findings of this study, ovulation in the intervention group treated with true acupuncture was approximately 10% higher than that in the control group.

In the present study, the ovulation rate in the intervention group was relatively higher than in the control group (41.6% vs. 31.57%), although the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.36). Nevertheless, this finding may suggest a potential clinical benefit of acupuncture in improving one of the infertility‐related parameters in women with PCOS. While Wu's study indicated that clomiphene significantly improved ovulation and live birth rates and that acupuncture had no significant effect in women with PCOS, other studies have reported findings more consistent with our results.[15] In a study by Pastore et al., similar to ours, there was no significant difference between the two treatment groups acupuncture and clomiphene in terms of the ovulation rate. Moreover, the ovulation rate in their study was also reported to be over 40%, indicating that acupuncture was as effective as clomiphene for ovulation induction [26].

[14]. Additionally, Johansson et al. reported that the frequency of ovulation during acupuncture treatment was significantly higher than in the control group, which received only routine physician visits [27]. Compared with that study, our findings may provide stronger evidence for the effectiveness of acupuncture, as true acupuncture was associated with a higher ovulation rate than sham acupuncture. Ding et al. found that adding acupuncture to letrozole therapy in women with PCOS improved ovulation and pregnancy rates, reduced androgen levels, and enhanced endometrial receptivity compared with placebo acupuncture plus letrozole, supporting its potential as an adjunctive treatment in individualized fertility management [28].

Furthermore, in an RCT by Pan et al., acupuncture combined with herbal medicine in infertile women with PCOS resulted in a significantly higher rate of pregnancy and ovulation [2].

A systematic review and meta‐analysis conducted by Wu et al., which included findings from 22 RCTs involving with 2315 patients, investigated the effects of acupuncture in women with PCOS. The results showed that acupuncture improved menstrual regularity and led to reduction in LH and testosterone levels. However, there was no remarkable change in live birth, pregnancy, and ovulation rates [1].

Similarly, another systematic review conducted by Huang et al., which analyzed 28 RCTs involving 2821 participants found that acupuncture alone, compared to western medical treatment, increased the pregnancy and ovulation rates while decreasing LH levels and LH/FSH ratio. Acupuncture combined with Western medicine also led to similar outcomes [12].

Several factors may explain the small differences between our finding and other similar studies; one such factor is the variation in acupuncture protocols and the differing duration of therapeutic interventions.

As mentioned above, differences in the type of sham intervention, as well as the placebo or medication used, may also contribute to the discrepancies between our findings and other studies. Among the studies mentioned above, Johansson et al. [27] and Pastore et al. [26] used acupuncture alone as the main intervention and did not evaluate acupuncture in combination with drug therapy. In the study by Johansson et al. [27], relaxation techniques were employed, whereas Pastore et al. [26] used placebo acupuncture in their control groups. Yu et al. attempted to compare acupuncture plus conventional drug treatment with clomiphene, making their study the most comparable to ours [29]. They showed that the combination of electroacupuncture and clomiphene was effective in promoting ovulation and enhancing pregnancy rates in women with PCOS. However, the key difference was that the Yu et al. study did not include sham acupuncture in the clomiphene group. In our study, to minimize bias and reduce the potential psychological effects of acupuncture or drug therapy on the final outcomes, the placebo intervention for each group was also administered to the other group. The Budihastuti et al. study focused exclusively on follicle growth enhancement with electroacupuncture plus clomiphene, whereas our study primarily assessed ovulation induction rates in a randomized design comparing true acupuncture plus placebo clomiphene with clomiphene plus sham acupuncture [30].

In this study, the therapeutic effect of acupuncture appeared to be comparable to that of clomiphene, which highlights the effectiveness of acupuncture as an alternative treatment for ovulation induction in women with PCOS. It is important to note that before acupuncture can be proposed as an alternative or complementary treatment for ovulation, its effectiveness must first be demonstrated to be at least equivalent to that of the standard treatment,clomiphene. However, superior statistical results should not necessarily be interpreted as greater efficacy.

Follicular growth was assessed as another outcome across three follow‐up cycles. In most follow‐up periods, the frequency of follicles measuring > 14 mm and > 18 mm was higher in the clomiphene group. The observed reduction in follicle size in the acupuncture group may reflect normalization of follicular development patterns, decreased cystic morphology, and improved hormonal profiles resulting from acupuncture's modulatory effects on the hypothalamic–pituitary–ovarian axis. Nevertheless, given the higher ovulation rate observed in the acupuncture group, it can be inferred that acupuncture may require a longer treatment duration to achieve its full therapeutic effect. Over time, this approach may lead to more favorable ovulatory outcomes than clomiphene.

Women with PCOS generally have elevated levels of circulating beta‐endorphins, which are implicated in the inadequate inhibition of GnRH by beta‐endorphins in the central nervous system [26]. Acupuncture has been shown to reduce plasma beta‐endorphin levels, decrease sympathetic nerve activity, and increase skin temperature.

In PCOS, ovarian nerve growth factor (NGF) production is increased in the follicular fluid of women with PCOS and in experimental mouse models, leading to excessive ovarian stimulation, impaired follicular development, and heightened sensitivity to gonadotropins. These effects are associated with increased ovarian adrenergic receptor expression, highlighting sympathetic nervous system involvement [31]. Experimental studies show that acupuncture can reduce ovarian NGF and adrenergic receptor expression, as well as decrease NGF mRNA and sympathetic activity in PCOS animal models [31].

Moreover, several studies suggest that acupuncture enhances uterine blood flow, reduces uterine contractions, regulates immune function, and alleviates anxiety, depression, and stress. By integrating these mechanisms, acupuncture is expected to contribute to improved placental function and implantation [32, 33]. Additionally, acupuncture may support ovulation by reducing circulating sex steroid hormones, their precursors, and glucuronide androgen metabolites, as well as by lowering inhibin B levels [27].

According to the available literature, acupuncture can definitely reduce the risk of ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome (OHSS), an adverse condition in patients undergoing fertility treatments. This effect is potentially mediated through various mechanisms, such as the reduction of vascular endothelial growth factor production, lower serum anti‐Müllerian hormone levels, and decreased ovarian volume in PCOS patients [34]. In addition, emerging evidence suggests that acupuncture may be beneficial for patients with diminished ovarian reserve, one of the most severe forms of reproductive dysfunction [35].

One of the main strengths of our study, compared to others, is the inclusion of a sham intervention in patients receiving clomiphene. In this regard, our findings may provide more robust evidence than previous studies, which often employed only one therapeutic intervention or a combination of treatments without a sham control group. Additionally, only a limited number of studies, including ours, have investigated ovulation rates following acupuncture treatment. Most prior studies have instead focused on biochemical variables and clinical symptoms as measures of improvement.

Limitation

However, our study also had some limitations. First, there was a dropout rate during the follow‐up period, although it was minimized to below 10% through consistent follow‐up calls. The relatively long treatment duration occasionally resulted in irregular attendance; therefore, participants were regularly reminded and encouraged to attend sessions.

Moreover, in our study, the dose of clomiphene was gradually increased. If the acupuncture treatment (i.e., the number of sessions) had also been increased in cases of non‐ovulation, different results might have been observed. Future studies should consider this variable. Additionally, multicenter trials conducted across diverse medical institutions are recommended to enhance the generalizability of the findings.

Conclusion

The results of this study suggest that, while clomiphene was associated with greater folliculare growth in some cycles, acupuncture appears to be equally effective in inducing ovulation in infertile women with PCOS. Given its minimal side effects, acupuncture can be considered a safe treatment option for these patients. Therefore, acupuncture may be proposed as an alternative or complementary therapy for PCOS patients who are resistant to clomiphene.
