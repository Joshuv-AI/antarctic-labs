ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionEpilepsy affects 1% of the US population, imposing a significant burden through seizures, distress and medication side effects. Many adverse outcomes can be reduced with patient education and an acute seizure action plan. Electronic health record clinical templates can support this care but are often seen as burdensome. This study will develop a clinical template embedding an acute seizure action plan into routine neurology visits and examine its effect on care quality and healthcare use, as well as barriers and facilitators to adoption.Methods and analysisThis study is a single-site, prospective, quality improvement, hybrid type 1 effectiveness-implementation study, sequential mixed-method design. Providers are randomised to either the acute seizure action plan plus general health education, or general health education alone control group, with 10 patients per provider enrolled over 12–15 months and followed for 1 year. Phase 1 will collect baseline interviews and pre-enrolment seizure outcomes. Phase 2 will implement the intervention with quantitative data along with provider interviews at 6 months. Phase 3 will include 12-month interviews to assess real-world use of the acute seizure action plan. The primary outcome is change in epilepsy quality measure scores before and after implementation between groups. Secondary outcomes include changes in individual quality measures, seizure metrics and healthcare utilisation. Implementation processes will be assessed by the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology and Theoretical Domains Framework.Ethics and disseminationThe study was approved by the Yale Institutional Review Board (Protocol ID: 2000032913). Findings will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publications and conference presentations.Keywords: EPILEPSY, EVIDENCE-BASED NEUROLOGY, HEALTH POLICY & PRACTICE, QUALITY OF LIFE, ANTICONVULSANTS

Introduction

Epilepsy is a chronic disease that affects 1% of the US population1 and millions of individuals worldwide.2 People with epilepsy (PWE) face numerous challenges, continually underscoring the importance of finding ways to improve standards of epilepsy care and meaningfully improve quality of life (QoL).3 Approximately 30% of PWE have uncontrolled or poorly controlled seizures, despite treatment with antiseizure medication,4 placing them at risk for premature mortality5; injury from falls, drowning, and automobile accidents6; underemployment7; social stigma8; psychological distress9 and adverse drug effects.10 Furthermore, PWE may suffer from seizure clusters, many seizures in a short period of time, and are associated with early mortality and frequent emergency department visits.11

Professional societies, such as the American Academy of Neurology (AAN) and the National Association of Epilepsy Centers (NAEC), have established epilepsy quality measures to standardise and improve care.12,16 The AAN, NAEC outpatient team and the Quality Indicator for Epilepsy Treatment (QUIET) team recommend systematic screening for behavioural health conditions, counselling on safety (eg, driving, swimming), review of medication side effects and interactions, discussions of pregnancy-related risks and development of an acute seizure action plan that delineates steps to take during a seizure, strategies to prevent clusters and indications for urgent evaluation.14 16 Despite clear recommendations, implementation into routine practice is limited. Unfortunately, <20% of clinical encounters document counselling and behavioural screening by neurologists,17 and few PWE have a documented acute seizure action plan.18,20

Tracking the use of these measures is impeded by variability in documentation habits of healthcare professionals, which makes longitudinal tracking of the quality metrics challenging. Healthcare providers often record care in free-form electronic health record (EHR) notes, which obscures key data needed for longitudinal assessment. Several measures have been retired due to the difficulty of extracting reliable information from unstructured documentation.14 17 18

EHR-based tools, including templates and registries, can embed prompts, standardise data capture and support clinical decision-making.21,23 Previous implementation of EHR-based templates by the NorthShore University Health System, the Epilepsy Learning Healthcare System and the Veterans Health Administration (VHA) Epilepsy Centers of Excellence demonstrated feasibility and potential benefit.22 24 25 NorthShore University Health System created a structured clinical documentation support tool that captures extensive data, ranging from imaging and labs to behavioural health screening and integrates it into patient visits. Although this model supports comprehensive data collection, barriers such as added visit time, need for additional staff and workflow disruption have limited adoption and implementation.22 26

Similarly, the Epilepsy Learning Healthcare System is a patient-centred network of academic hospitals, patient and family partners, and nonprofit organisations that seeks to improve care through data collection and iterative interventions.24 27 Its approach captured key seizure data (frequency, type, date of last seizure) via case report forms, but many quality AAN (eg, medication adherence, behavioural health screening) relied on patient-reported outcomes or manual entry by additional clinical staff, creating workflow and efficiency challenges.21 23 In contrast, the VHA integrated its epilepsy registry directly into the EHR to enable real-time data collection and decision support.25 Nevertheless, clinician behaviour and workflow disruptions have limited adoption, and acute seizure action plans have not been integrated into these tools.28

This quality improvement, hybrid type 1 effectiveness-implementation study will develop and implement a point-of-care EHR template that operationalises epilepsy quality measures and generates an acute seizure action plan during the clinical encounter. We will evaluate clinical outcomes, QoL and healthcare resource utilisation. We hypothesise that this approach will improve quality of care, decrease injuries, expedite recognition and management of seizure clusters, enhance access to and use of rescue medications, and ultimately decrease the burden and cost of care for PWE.

Preparatory stakeholder interviews and tool development

We consulted leaders from the Northshore University Health System, the Epilepsy Learning Healthcare System and the VHA Epilepsy Centers of Excellence to inform the design of a clinical template that incorporated an acute seizure action plan. Across systems, clinicians cited scepticism, resistance to workflow changes, and added workload as persistent barriers. In response, we prioritised a clinical template with an acute seizure action plan that fits into healthcare providers’ routine visits, requires minimal training and aligns with existing documentation practices.

The design emphasises usability, fewer clicks, streamlined data collection using toggles, dropdown menus and clearly organised sections to minimise cognitive load while capturing structured data. The template captures seizure details (frequency, type, date of last seizure), epilepsy history (risk factors, duration, triggers, severity), social history, diagnostic imaging and patient education and counselling within a clinician-focused design. Pilot testing indicates completion in approximately 6 min for new patients and less for follow-ups, addressing concerns about visit length, staffing and workflow integration.

The EQUIP score includes seizure-specific assessments (eg, frequency, type, aetiology, date of last seizure), occurrence of severe events (eg, seizure clusters, status epilepticus), treatment adherence and counselling on medication side effects. It also includes quality indicators related to reproductive health counselling for women of childbearing age, behavioural health screening and referrals and patient education on safety and lifestyle-related seizure triggers. A detailed summary of the EQUIP set and their alignment with existing quality measurement sets is provided in table 1. Each applicable primary measure is scored as completed (1 point) or not completed (0 points) and the percentage score is calculated as (completed measures/total applicable measures) × 100, with 10 primary measures for men and women not of reproductive age and an additional reproductive health counselling measure for women of reproductive age.

Secondary measures include documentation of neurodiagnostic evaluations (electroencephalogram and MRI), referrals for surgery in drug-resistant epilepsy, acute seizure action plans and referrals to epilepsy self-management and wellness programmes. This evaluation framework is designed to be feasible, scalable and clinically actionable for improving epilepsy care delivery.

Theoretical frameworks

Implementing new practices in clinical care requires changes in healthcare providers’ behaviour, which is facilitated by understanding the determinants of current and goal behaviour. To better understand these determinants and thus support implementation strategy development, this study will be guided by the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology Model and the Theoretical Domains Framework. The Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology Model is one of the most frequently used models of information technology adoption. It proposes that behavioural intention to use a technology is influenced by several factors, specifically, performance expectancy, effort expectancy, social influence and facilitating conditions. Sex, age, experience and voluntariness of use are identified moderators.30 31 The Theoretical Domains Framework is an integrated framework consisting of 14 domains and was initially developed for implementation research to identify influences on healthcare professional behaviour related to implementation of evidence-based practices.32 These frameworks will guide the development of the interview and focus group guides, and the results will be mapped back to them (online supplemental material).

This study will be conducted in patients with diagnosed epilepsy receiving care within the Yale New Haven Health System. Ten healthcare providers will be randomised to either general health education alone (control group) or general health education plus the clinical template with an acute seizure action plan (intervention group), and all patients enrolled in the study will receive the routine clinical care allocated to them. Data sources

Data for this study will be derived from multiple sources, including patient medical records, patient-reported outcomes, healthcare provider interviews and patient and caregiver focus groups. Both primary and secondary data will be collected to examine baseline characteristics and outcomes. Data linkage is possible due to a single site and a single system that has inter-operability between the data sets.

Study size

This cluster-randomised trial assigns healthcare providers to either an acute seizure action plan plus general health education or general health education alone control arm (1:1 ratio). We aim to recruit ≥10 providers (5 per arm) and 10 patients per healthcare provider. This sample size provides 80% power to detect a two-point improvement on the EQUIP scale (SD=4.572). We defined this two-point change—a 20% increase on the 10-point scale—as our threshold for clinical significance.33 This sample size calculation assumes an estimated intracluster correlation is 0.01 with a two-sided test at the 0.05 significance level using the two means completely randomised statistic.

Data analysis

Quantitative analysis (survey)

Data will be collected electronically (through REDCap). Quantitative data will be analysed using the principle of intention-to-treat. This means patients will be analysed according to the initial healthcare provider’s randomisation (acute seizure action plan plus general health education or general health education alone control arm), with secondary analyses of per-protocol results.

Patient characteristics will be described in the overall population and the treatment and control arms over the baseline period. Categorical variables will be detailed by counts and proportions. Continuous variables will be described by mean, SD, median, and interquartile range (first and third quartiles). For the QoL measures, a multilevel mixed model will be used that includes the healthcare provider as a random cluster effect and the treatment group as a fixed effect. The cluster will be included as a random effect to account for the dependency between patients treated by the same healthcare provider.

Analysis of the primary outcome measure

To account for the dependency between patients treated by the same healthcare provider, a multilevel mixed model will be used. The primary analysis will compare the change from baseline in EQUIP measures between patients in the clinical template with an acute seizure action plan plus general health education arm and those in the general health education alone control arm. This means a random effects least-squares mean regression model will be applied with the healthcare provider as the cluster level random effect and the treatment group as the fixed effect. Additional variables to potentially adjust for include age at enrolment, sex, number of years with epilepsy, seizure severity, race and ethnicity. Precision will be assessed using two-sided 95% CIs.

Analysis of the secondary outcome measures

For secondary outcomes, this study will evaluate the number of seizure-related emergency department visits and the number of inpatient neurology admissions over a 12-month time period, treating these as separate outcomes. Change in QoL will be evaluated from baseline between patients in the clinical template with an acute seizure action plan plus general health education arm and those in the general health education alone control arm. To analyse these outcomes, a random effects least squares mean regression model will be applied with the healthcare provider as the random cluster effect and the treatment group as fixed effect. In addition, we will examine the number of monthly seizure clusters over a 6-month and 12-month time period using a zero-inflated Poisson mixed model with healthcare provider as the cluster level random effect and treatment group as the fixed effect. This method will be applied to account for the anticipated high level of patients with zero hospital visits. If overdispersion is detected by dividing the residual deviance by the degrees of freedom, then a zero-inflated negative binomial mixed model is recommended. Precision for the rate ratios will be assessed using two-sided 95% CIs.

Similar modelling approaches will be applied to the clinical (average monthly seizures) and healthcare resource utilisation (neurology visit, inpatient visit, emergency department visit) outcomes for the objective of describing and comparing rescue medication use (yes/no) differences on clinical and healthcare resource utilisation outcomes. Depending on the type of measurement, the hierarchical model will always include the healthcare provider as a random cluster effect, along with treatment and rescue medication use as a fixed effect.

Descriptive analyses of baseline demographics and clinical characteristics will be displayed and stratified by refractory epilepsy (yes/no), seizure clusters (yes/no) (online supplemental table S1), and treatment arm.

Outcome definition and measurement for quantitative variables are illustrated in online supplemental table S1.

Sensitivity analysis

A sensitivity analysis on treated patients will be performed. This will help to identify the proportion of patients that may have been misallocated and analyse them according to the treatment (clinical template with an acute seizure action plan plus general health education and those with general health education alone) that they received, instead of the intent to treat, which analyses patients according to the initial healthcare provider randomisation (clinical template with an acute seizure action plan plus general health education or general health education alone) that they are allocated to.

Analysis of safety variables

A separate safety analysis will not be conducted. Safety measures such as status epilepticus, adverse medication events, seizure clusters and healthcare resource utilisation will be included during the primary analysis. Any safety results found will be described.

Qualitative analysis (interviews and focus groups)

All interview and focus group audio recordings will be uploaded within 24 hours to a secure study-specific file-sharing site and deleted from audio recorders. The recordings will be transcribed verbatim, and the transcribed data will be reviewed by qualitative scientific staff to ensure that all personally identifiable information and protected health information are removed and to correct obvious transcription errors. The narrative data from the interview and focus group transcripts will be entered into ATLAS.ti qualitative data analysis software (V.22.0) by a study team member.34 ATLAS.ti is a specialised software designed for the qualitative analysis of textual, graphical, audio and video data. The coding structure used for thematic analysis will be created from a combination of inductive and deductive coding and will take the three participant groups (healthcare providers, patients and caregivers) into account. Data collection and analyses will be conducted iteratively.

Limitations of the research methods

As a single-site study, conducted at a tertiary epilepsy centre with relatively few healthcare providers, generalisability may be constrained. Lack of masking and potential provider contamination given they are all in the same practice setting are additional limitations, along with the risk of differential loss to follow-up. However, patients in the control arm continue to receive standard clinical care, mitigating ethical concerns. Additionally, no minimum seizure frequency threshold was specified as an inclusion criterion, as the Acute Seizure Action Plan is intended to benefit patients across the full spectrum of epilepsy presentations (including newly diagnosed, refractory and treatment-responsive cases), regardless of seizure burden.35 Baseline seizure frequency and epilepsy subtype will be included as covariates in all analyses to account for this heterogeneity. Second, seizure types are classified using the 2017 International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE) operational classification,36 which remains the standard embedded in current quality frameworks (AAN, NAEC, QUIET); retaining the 2017 framework ensures comparability with the existing literature. The 2025 ILAE classification37 was published after the clinical template had been developed, deployed in the EHR. We intend to add the equivalent new terms to the old ones in the database and in future reporting. In light of these limitations, this study evaluates whether a clinical template with an acute seizure action plan may improve epilepsy quality performance and recommends the use of consolidated epilepsy quality performance measures to evaluate clinical and QoL outcomes.

Ethics and dissemination

Patient consent was waived because this study is evaluating an implementation strategy to improve care quality. However, patients were informed about the study, and their participation was entirely voluntary. Patients were also informed by the study team that their participation choices would not affect their clinical care or relationship with providers. We intend to disseminate and publish the results of this study whether they are positive, negative or inconclusive. The results of the study will be published in peer-reviewed journals and presented at national conferences.

Patient and public involvement

Patients were not involved in the initial protocol development, formulation of the research question, selection of outcome measures or the design of study procedures and recruitment strategies. Patients and caregivers will not be directly involved in recruitment or conduct of study. However, patient and caregiver perspectives will be sought during the prospective phase of the trial through focus groups as part of the qualitative component of the study to support implementation and guide future research.
