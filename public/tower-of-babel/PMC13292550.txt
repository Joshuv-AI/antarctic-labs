STAR Protocols question-and-answer series: Organoids
Authors: Haymanti Bhanot
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Organoids can recapitulate essential physiological features of the tissue of origin, making them a promising in vitro model for studying human organ development and disease. Organoid technology can complement animal models by enabling research questions that were not addressable due to lack of tissues. However, reproducibility remains challenging, likely due to variability introduced during organoid generation and/or handling. In this backstory, STAR Protocols authors and advisory board members share their advice on common questions about organoids.

Main text

I have seen variation from organoid to organoid and from batch to batch. It is hard to have consistent differentiation from batch to batch. Therefore, for the endpoint assay, the batch-to-batch variation is huge, and more samples are needed. Any suggestions for improving reproducibility?

H. Erdinç Beşikcioğlu and Ekin Demir: Timing, handling of the organoids, and variability of products (such as ECM and recombinant proteins) cause variation between organoids and batches. We recommend always performing the medium change and passaging in the same frequencies. The mechanical disassociation of organoids during passaging causes cellular stress and affects organoid viability. The optimum disassociation level should be determined and approved on all batches. Performing the experiments in approximately the same time durations can also reduce batch-to-batch variations. The concentrations of ingredients such as ECM gels, conditioned media, and recombinant proteins can be very different from lot to lot. It is very important to use the same lot for these kinds of culture components, but it is not always feasible to obtain the same lot from a company. We recommend checking the products’ certificates of analysis and using products that have similar protein concentrations.

Zhichao Li, Wei Chen, and Weiren Huang: We think the uneven distribution of organoids was one of the main reasons for inconsistent results. Since organoids always sink to the bottom of the tube, we recommend inverting the tubes gently to obtain a homogeneous suspension of organoids before distributing organoids into replicate wells, especially when a single-channel pipette is used. In addition, organoids distributed in 96 wells or 384 wells may aggregate into large cell clusters. Since the sizes of organoids affect the results of several assays, such as drug screening, we recommend increasing the concentration of Matrigel or BME in the organoid medium if aggregation of organoids occurs.

Christopher Mayhew: This is a major challenge in the field, and unfortunately, there isn’t a one-size-fits-all answer, especially for improving organoid-to-organoid consistency.

To minimize batch-to-batch variability in organoid yield, we recommend the following:•Ensure that hPSCs used for directed differentiation are properly quality controlled and banked to ensure a consistent supply of high-quality cells for experimentation. We never culture an hPSC line for more than 20 passages before discarding the culture and thawing replacement cells from a working bank. At a minimum, banked hPSCs should be mycoplasma free and karyotypically normal and have identity authenticated by STR (or similar analysis).•Use of high-quality reagents is imperative. Batches of reagents such as growth factors should be appropriately prepared, and strict storage requirements should be adhered to. Compared to formulating media for directed differentiation from separate components, commercially available differentiation media kits may be a more consistent and reliable (but usually more expensive) source of media for hPSC differentiation. Steps to enhance organoid-to-organoid consistency typically require optimization of differentiation protocols. Many approaches have been described, including initiating self-organization with patterned progenitors rather than undifferentiated hPSCs (Nickels et al., 2020), improving size consistency by using microwell aggregation (Takebe et al., 2017), or eliciting enhanced differentiation through using miniature (Qian et al., 2016) or spinner flask bioreactors (Velasco et al., 2019). If available for your organoid of interest, implementing these improved protocols is possible for most well-equipped research labs.

We recommend performing robust quality control metrics for each batch of organoids by analysis of unequivocal markers of cell identity. Ideally, this should be done by microscopic analysis that allows visual inspection of organoid architecture and cellular organization.

Ana Ivonne Vazquez-Armendariz and Elie El Agha: In our experience, there is not much variation when using freshly sorted mouse stem cells. Maybe you could try to adjust the stem cell concentration or change media more often to have enough nutrients to keep MCs healthy and stimulate cell differentiation.

Yangfei Xiang and In-Hyun Park: Sample variation is one of the key challenges in current organoid technology. Although there hasn’t been a perfect solution to this yet, several approaches could be considered to minimize the batch-to-batch variation:•As a starting point, ensure good cell quality when preparing organoid differentiation (e.g., no contamination, no spontaneous differentiation, similar passage number).•Keep the size of the starting embryoid body (EB) appropriate and consistent. It is better to precisely control the cell number used to form EBs. The purpose of this is to obtain a consistent starting size of EBs because the following differentiation steps could be affected by this parameter. That said, it is suggested that for different cell lines, a cell-number titration experiment may be needed since different lines may show distinct survival rates after single-cell dissociation, and consequently, the size of EBs could be different even if the same number of single cells are plated.•For protocols that included Matrigel embedding or dissolved Matrigel, it is preferred to use the same batch of Matrigel products because the components of different batches of Matrigel may vary as well. It is also essential to keep all other reagents consistent during organoid culture.•For the unguided differentiation approach (i.e., cerebral organoids), several studies have applied biomaterials to improve the differentiation, such as the enCORs system, which should be a good alternative to improve reproducibility. Overall, brain organoids generated from a small-molecule-guided neural induction approach show good reproducibility once the differentiation condition is confirmed effective for the cell line used. Some cell lines may show difficulties in forming EBs and consequently display bad organoid production. For cell lines that efficiently generate EBs, they normally show good reproducibility of organoid formation using the guided differentiation approach.

Immunocytochemistry on organoids in high-throughput biology is extremely tricky because it is difficult to retain them during the staining procedure. I have had trouble finding good protocols for staining whole organoids. Any advice/recommendations?

H. Erdinç Beşikcioğlu and Ekin Demir: There are two options for immunocytochemistry on organoids: (1) removing ECM gel from the organoids is useful for better antibody penetration, but washing steps cause the deformation of organoid morphology and sample loss; and (2) coating the plastic laboratory wares (such as pipette tips, tubes, etc.) with BSA reduces sample loss by preventing adhesion of organoids on the surface. We perform all washing steps in well plates standing on a dark surface so we can easily see the organoids and perform pipetting without damaging them. In the need for the protection of organoid morphology, immunocytochemistry should be performed without removing the ECM gel. However, this method comes up with low antibody penetration and high background. Diluting ECM gel with culture media in a 1:10 ratio and longer antibody incubation up to 2 days in 4°C increase the antibody penetration. We perform the final washing steps as 4 × 15 min with PBS-T and 4 × 15 min with PBS at room temperature (∼21°C–22°C) for less background.

Holly Chen and Anand Swaroop: Immunostaining of the whole organoids largely depends on the penetration of the antibodies, particularly if the targeted cell types are located in the center of the organoids. Percentage of detergents and incubation time of the primary and/or secondary antibodies could be optimized to facilitate the entry of antibodies into the organoids. Below is the protocol we use to perform whole-mount staining of retinal organoids in the lab. It has been working well even for staining of synapses inside the organoids.1.Block (5% donkey serum in PBS-T [0.3%]) overnight.2.Incubate in primary antibody for 3 nights at 4°C with gentle shaking.3.Wash four times with PBS-T for about 1.5 h each at room temperature with gentle shaking.4.In the dark, incubate in secondary antibody and DAPI for 3 nights at 4°C with gentle shaking.5.Wash four times with PBS-T for about 1.5 h each at room temperature with gentle shaking. The final wash can go overnight with gentle shaking.

Zhichao Li, Wei Chen, and Weiren Huang: Since organoids are small and cannot be treated in conventional tissue-processing methods, we embed collected organoids in gel matrices such as melted 2% agarose solution before performing the following dehydration and clearing processes.

Santiago Ramirez, Abhisek Mukherjee, and Claudio Soto: We routinely perform immunostaining on formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded organoid sections for immunohistochemistry (IHC) and immunofluorescence (IF) analysis. We think it is important to dry the sections overnight at 20°C and 25°C before the immunostaining procedures. This prevents section detachment from the slides. Based on the choice of primary antibody, we also use cryosections. For this procedure, we recommend coating the slides with 0.3% gelatin and 0.05% chromium potassium sulfate to increase the attachment of the sections to the glass slides.

Ana Ivonne Vazquez-Armendariz and Elie El Agha: We normally do the staining procedure directly on the well and cut out the filter together with the cultures at the end just before mounting. In general, we use room-temperature solutions to avoid Matrigel dilution. You could also try to extend the incubation periods. For example, we do overnight staining in the fridge for both primary and secondary antibodies.

For other organoids, percentage of detergents and incubation time of the primary and/or secondary antibodies could be optimized to facilitate the entry of antibodies into the organoids. We also adapted expansion microscopy to organoid sections (Wassie et al., 2018), and it is beneficial for high-resolution imaging of delicate proteins and structures, but we have never tried the protocol using whole organoids.

Live imaging of whole organoids depends on the dye. For small lipophilic dyes such as TMRE for mitochondria, we follow the protocol from the manufacturer, and it works very well. However, some dyes (e.g., Lysotracker for lysosome staining) do not readily enter the organoid, likely due to the presence of the outer limiting membrane, which is present on the surface of retinal organoids. Dissection of the organoids or performing vibratome sectioning would facilitate the entry of these dyes. In the case of retinal organoids or brain organoids, the vibratome section could survive for at least 3 days.

When capturing images of whole organoids, using a two-photon microscope or high-speed light sheet scanner would largely reduce photobleaching. We also use high-resolution Airy scanning in a Zeiss confocal system to capture images with weak staining.

I am working with human retinal organoids. For retinal organoids, or for organoids in general, how can I make the cells survive to enable long-term organoid maintenance (>200 days)?

H. Erdinç Beşikcioğlu and Ekin Demir: According to our experience, murine pancreas organoids can survive long-term if the media are changed every third day and if they are passaged when the confluency reaches 70%. We recommend always using fresh media. It is also very important not to damage them with too much mechanical disruption while passaging them.

Holly Chen and Anand Swaroop: Premature degeneration of human retinal organoids is currently a technical challenge of the field. Retinal organoids develop to a certain stage and then gradually degenerate. Although some protocols culture retinal organoids up to 300 days (Wahlin et al., 2017), the photoreceptor morphology in those organoids is comparable to others at day 200 (Regent et al., 2020), and single-cell RNA-seq of week-30 and week-38 organoids indicated similar maturity (Cowan et al., 2020). Therefore, unless the goal is to investigate photoreceptor maintenance, it is not necessary to maintain organoids for more than 200 days. In addition, the cells in retinal organoids do not die immediately, even after 200 days. Instead, the quality of photoreceptors gradually decreases with opsin mislocalization, and the interneurons start to degenerate. It is a long process, and typically, the cells still survive at day 300 in organoids. Adding cell-culture-grade antioxidants such as 55 mM beta-mercaptoethanol should help with cell survival in organoid cultures.

Hiroaki Kasashima, Maria T Diaz-Meco, and Jorge Moscat: In our experience, the timing of passage is important. Once the organoids are starved and start to die, it becomes very difficult to recover and keep them in culture for a long time. When you see the medium is consumed quickly (you can recognize this by checking the colors of culture medium) and you must change it frequently (every 2 days to every day), pass the organoids immediately. When you split organoids, try not to seed them too sparsely. Splitting one well into 3 or 4 wells is good. Too much dissociation should be avoided if you want to keep the organoids long term. Using mechanical dissociation (such as vigorous pipetting) is less harsh. In order to get rid of the debris well, you can do enzymatic dissociation using TrypLE, but keep a piece of cell aggregates consisting of around 10 cells. Dissociating organoids into a single cell is not good for a long-term culture.

Zhichao Li, Wei Chen, and Weiren Huang: We find that keeping organoids in good condition is essential for long-term culture maintenance. We added Rho kinase inhibitor Y-27632 during the dissociation processes to avoid anoikis. In addition, the presence of dead cells affects the maintenance of organoid culture. Dead cells can be removed using dead-cell removal kits when organoids are digested into single cells. We gently remove the supernatant containing dead cells or debris after harvesting organoids and incubate organoids in adDMEM/F12 for 10–15 min.

Philipp Wörsdörfer and Süleyman Ergün: In general, neural organoids can be grown for a very long time (>200 days). This is even required as some cell populations such as astrocytes arise late during development (after more than 100 days), and cortical layering also needs extended culture times.

To keep organoids healthy over a long period of time, I would suggest the following:•Make sure to change the medium regularly. Monitor the pH indicator in the cell culture medium daily. If necessary, increase the frequency of medium changes.•Organoids increase in size during culture time. If several organoids are cultured in one petri dish, reduce the number of organoids per dish to reduce media consumption.•Culture organoids on a 2D cell culture rocker or orbital shaker, or in spinner culture, to improve oxygen and nutrient diffusion.•Culturing thick cortical organoid slices has been shown to maintain organoid growth and laminar expansion over long-term culture and to reduce necrotic core formation within the organoids (Qian et al., 2020).•It is important to avoid bacterial contamination during long-term culture. For this reason, we sterile-filter the cell culture medium before use. Moreover, antibiotics (e.g., penicillin/streptomycin) may be added to the culture medium to reduce the risk of contamination.

I am working with lung organoids. I have trouble recovering the cells from the organoid culture. I used dispase to dissolve Matrigel. However, the recovery rate is low, and most of the cells are dead. Any suggestions on increasing the recovery rate?

Zhichao Li, Wei Chen, and Weiren Huang: We use TrypLE Express in the passage of organoids. Rho-kinase inhibitor Y-27632 was added to prevent detach-induced anoikis. We think over-digestion might be the reason for the death of cells. Decreasing the digestion time and dissociating organoids into small clusters instead of single cells might help increase cell viability. Besides, we recommend transferring cryopreserved organoids to liquid nitrogen the next day after freezing because we noticed decreased cell viability when organoids were stored at −80°C for a long time.

Ana Ivonne Vazquez-Armendariz and Elie El Agha: You could try to first use BD Cell Recovery Solution to get rid of the Matrigel. Then, proceed with the organoid digestion using dispase on ice. In addition, you could try shorter incubation times or lower dispase concentrations to further improve cell viability.

I am working with human and mouse neural and cerebral organoids. I am having trouble controlling morphological maturity. Any suggestions?

Holly Chen and Anand Swaroop: Morphological maturity of organoids could be cell line dependent. It is normal to have a lot of intra- and inter-batch variation, particularly for brain organoids with a pool of different cell types. Generally speaking, mouse organoids would be more variable compared to the human ones due to their shorter differentiation time. If you have never reached morphological maturity but are confident there are no mistakes in the differentiation, trying a different cell line or using an earlier passage (usually P15–P25) might help. If you experience huge variations between organoids/batches, try to be consistent with experimental procedures and reagents. The number of organoids per plate and the volume of media per plate should be consistent between batches. Use reagents (particularly Matrigel and FBS) from the same lot for all replicates of one experiment.

Hyosung Kim and Peng Jiang: In our studies (Kim et al., 2019; Xu et al., 2021), we find that culturing organoids with BrainPhys Medium can promote neuronal maturation. This may also improve morphological arborization of neurons in the organoids as they mature.

Richa Singhania: The main challenge of working with cerebral organoids comes from the self-patterning, non-directed differentiation process leading to morphological and cellular variability both within and between batches. To overcome such variability and generate brain organoids with a higher reproducibility, you can try more directed differentiation protocols such as cortical or forebrain organoids (Pasca et al., 2015; Velasco et al., 2019).

I am working with human cortical organoids and need advice on methods to transfect them, apart from CRISPR on the iPSCs or ex vivo electroporation. For example, I am transfecting with siRNAs that are given in the medium, but unfortunately, it requires specific media that could interfere with organoid development. Any suggestions on potential alternative approaches?

Holly Chen and Anand Swaroop: Morpholino has been used quite well for siRNA in various cell lines and in vivo experiments. The low toxicity of morpholino in retinal organoids has been demonstrated by Parfitt et al. (2016). The quantity of morpholino and siRNA will need to be optimized for cortical organoids depending on the size and stage of differentiation.

Santiago Ramirez, Abhisek Mukherjee, and Claudio Soto: Viral transfection methods have been successfully used to deliver genetic cargo into brain organoids. These methods are recently summarized by Wieland Huttner’s group (https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fncel.2019.00558/full). In our lab, we are trying to optimize a lipofectamine-based genetic cargo delivery method. However, it is important to consider that non-viral delivery methods such as lipofectamine can only deliver the genetic cargo. An integration machinery has to be coupled with the genetic cargo if stable integration is required.

Richa Singhania: For siRNA transfections in cortical organoids, you can try different transfection reagents such as Lipofectamine RNAiMax (Invitrogen), siLentFect (Bio-Rad), and TransIT-2020 (Mirus Bio) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. siRNA complex medium can be added to individual organoids seeded per well in an ultralow attachment 24-well plate overnight and replaced the following day with normal organoid medium for further culture.

Yangfei Xiang and In-Hyun Park: Instead of performing standard transfection, which is not optimized for organoid samples, one can consider using viral vectors to deliver siRNAs into the organoids if neither CRISPR editing on PSCs nor electroporation of organoids is preferred. Typically, brain organoids can be incubated within normal organoid culture medium containing viral vectors (e.g., AAV, lentivirus, etc.) overnight (statically), then transferred back to bioreactors or shakers for normal culturing. By using this approach, not only does the medium remain the same, but the targeted cell types (e.g., neurons, glial cells, or both) also can be controlled if needed.

We have been using organoids derived from human and mouse lung and connective tissue (epithelia and endothelia). We predominantly work on tumor organoids. Sometimes, we find that metastatic primary cells and cell lines form thin films instead of forming 3D spherical structures. Sometimes, the formed organoids are not mechanically stable; rather, they are stable if they are prepared with cell lines instead of isolated primary cells. Any suggestions on troubleshooting these issues?

Ana Ivonne Vazquez-Armendariz and Elie El Agha: We have little experience with tumor organoids, but we could suggest increasing the Matrigel concentration and monitoring whether stable organoids can be formed by having a stiffer matrix.

I am working with healthy tissue- and tumor-derived organoids from CRC patients, and I need help with the following issue: We are seeing that our tumor organoids grow relatively slowly, and the normal/healthy organoids tend to grow much faster. Optimizing culture conditions for tumor organoids to make them grow faster would help us build a large-scale assay. Any advice on this issue or any suggestions for clear quantification of a reference would also help.

Hiroaki Kasashima, Maria T Diaz-Meco, and Jorge Moscat: The addition of EGF, bFGF, and NECA (adenosine agonist) to the culture medium helps CRC organoids grow faster. If the growth rate is still slow, consider adding a p38 inhibitor such as SB202190, though it sometimes has the opposite effect (slows growth speed). The addition of a small amount of FBS (5%) would also be helpful.
