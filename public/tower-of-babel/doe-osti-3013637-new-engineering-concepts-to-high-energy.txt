Project Introduction
Li-S batteries (LSBs) with energy densities > 500 Wh/kg @ C/5 and 1000 cycles, cycle life operating over
wide temperatures has been identified to be critical for meeting the global energy demands. Progress is
however, very much limited due to several major hurdles related to the following: (a) poor Li+ reaction
kinetics (conductivity/diffusivity) enabling full reversible S conversion to Li2S, (b) inferior electron (e-)
conductivity, specific capacity and cycle life due to electrolyte soluble species of polysulfides (PSs) formed
with the reaction of Li ions with sulfur migrating to anode, (c) unstable solid electrolyte interphase (SEI)
formation and finally, (d) dendrites that are formed on the Li metal anode severely limiting the cycle life
due to polarization at the solid/liquid interfaces causing safety, inferior capacity, energy density, rate, and
cycle life issues. Creation of a fully functional economical $80/kWh, high energy density LSBs is vital
for enabling its widespread commercial deployment and use in current and next generation electric vehicles
(EVs).

 1

Objectives
The major objectives/goals of this project are to develop a lithium sulfur pouch cell with a cell capacity
>300mAh with energy density ≥500Wh/kg, ≥750Wh/L with cycling over 1000 cycles @C/3 rate, cycle life
of 1000 at C/3 and ≤ 20% fade in energy @ C/10-C/3, and ≤$80/kWh. To achieve the proposed objectives,
the following major goals will be attained during the three-year funded project:
(a) identification of effective electrocatalysts (ECs) and Li-ion conductors (LICs) lowering the activation
barrier; (b) in situ synthesis of electrocatalysts and LIC dispersed complex carbon-based framework
material (CFM) and sulfur nanocomposite with sulfur content ≥ 90 wt%; (c) Achieve initial areal capacity
of ~6 mAh/cm-2 of sulfur electrode with initial S loading of > 6 mg/cm2 sulfur and a final ~15 mAh/cm2
areal capacity of S electrode with final S loading ≥ 10mg/cm-2; (d) Li-containing structurally isomorphous
alloy (Li-SIA) of  1000 cycles with 15 mAh/cm-2 areal capacity @C/3 rate; and (e) identification of
optimal electrolyte compositions giving Li+ conductivities ~10-2S/cm and polysulfide dissolution < 1 ppm.

Impact
The work from this project will have a strong impact on development of high-energy-density Li-S
batteries. The results of this work will lead to new knowledge on identification and generation of
high-capacity sulfur cathodes and dendrite-free Li-metal anodes using scalable synthesis and fabrication
approaches. Both fundamental and applied knowledge will be gathered from the study. Systematic
characterization will provide new scientific insights into the mechanisms contributing to the
corresponding response in coin-cell and pouch-cell formats. Results of this work also will likely have an
impact on other fields such as electrocatalysts for water splitting and fuel cells and other fields engaged
in electrochemistry. Plans are also in place to engage underrepresented minority undergraduate students
to gain research experience in various aspects of the project. With new materials identified as the project
is executed, opportunities will emerge for filing invention disclosures and provisional/nonprovisional
patent applications. The training and opportunities presented by this project to undergraduate and graduate
students as well as postdoctoral research associates will foster next-generation work force training,
equipping workers with the necessary skillsets to transform the quality of life all over the globe by
generating high-energy-density systems that can be deployed in electric vehicles as well as in laptops and
cell phones including innumerable portable and wireless devices, opening the doors to a new green and
sustainable energy environment improving overall quality of life.

Approach
Work involves executing a theory-driven study directed at identification of electrocatalysts (ECs) for
efficient conversion of polysulfides to Li2S during the forward discharge process and backward charge
process to elemental sulfur and lithium. Also, first-principles computational approaches have been applied
to identify solid-state LICs with conductivity >10-4 S/cm. Employing expertise in the principal
investigator’s laboratory, suitable low-temperature (<240°C) scalable synthesis techniques have been used
to generate theoretically identified electrocatalysts and LICs. Low polysulfide solubility and stable solid-
electrolyte interphase (SEI) forming electrolytes were then prepared from commercially available
solvents, additives, and salts. The effectiveness of the electrocatalysts and LICs to improve the polysulfide
to Li2S transformation kinetics and the ability to achieve the targeted specific capacity by trapping
polysulfides, including desired structural transformation during charging/discharging, have been assessed;
accordingly, suitable modification of electrocatalysts and LICs have been performed to generate the ideal
microstructures. Suitable modification, if required, of lithium alloy as anode has also been performed by
alloying with other metals to improve the Li-ion diffusivity kinetics and the Gibbs-Thomson parameter.
The scalable approaches developed have been further refined to optimize sulfur utilization in the cathode,
engineer the surface and bulk structure of the novel Li-SIA and lithium multicomponent alloy (Li-MCA)
anodes to yield the desired areal capacity with optimal Coulombic efficiency (CE), and finally optimize
the electrolyte structure to minimize and eliminate polysulfide dissolution with desired Li + conductivities.
 2

Finally, the developed systems have been scaled up, fabricated, and tested in pouch-cell configurations to
achieve the required target performance specifications.

Another computational task that was conducted during this year was an identification of alloying
elements facilitating the likely enhancement of Li-ion mobility in the identified multicomponent
alloys, MCAs and thus, to improve the overall cyclability and performance of the MCA anode.
Using the first principles climbing nudged elastic band approach the activation barriers for Li-ion
diffusivity were calculated and collected in Figure 9. The higher the barrier, the lower the Li-ion
mobility in the alloy. One can see that the alloying elements El1, El2, and El3 exhibit a higher
barrier and thereby decrease the Li-ion mobility compared to pure Li-bcc metal, while El4, El5
and El6 are more favorable and thus will noticeably likely enhance the Li-ion mobility.
The team also studied the system of MCA as a dendrite free anode material that was synthesized by high
energy mechanical milling process to validate the theoretical findings presented in Figure 6(b). The
structurally isomorphous system, MCA4 having similar lattice registry to metallic Li, prevents Li dendrite
growth by forming a solid solution with the reacting Li-ions electrochemically presented to it from the
metallic Li serving as the counter electrode. The ensuing electrochemical alloying reaction allows a
significant amount of Li to solubilize into the parent MCA4 alloy causing minimal change in volume due
to the solid solution formation. The crystallographic arrangements of atoms in the MCA4 alloy being similar
to that of Li thus serves to preserve the overall crystallographic symmetry and allows the formation of a
solid solution with optimal interfacial energy. The MCA4 alloy also improves the lithium diffusion
preventing surface segregation while continuing to maintain dendrite - free cycling, hence, serving as
effective anodes as well as current collectors in Li-S batteries corroborating the theoretical findings. The
MCA4 alloy was generated by a simple high energy alloying approach and the anode electrode for
electrochemical testing was prepared by standard slurry casting method followed by electrochemical testing
in a coin cell configuration, the results of which are shown in Figure 10. The MCA4 cells were cycled for
1 mAh/cm2 at 1 C-rate with an average electrode loading of 3.2 mg cm−2. The MCA4 cells show excellent
plating/stripping behavior affording a stable areal capacity of ~1 mAh/cm2 for continuous 30 cycles. The
propensity of the MCA4 alloy electrode to demonstrate reversible Li cycling (plating/stripping) is seen in
Figure 10 as the overpotential of the cell decreases with cycle number. Further electrochemical testing of

Conclusions for Year 1
Research work was conducted in three areas of the project. These include experimental identification and
synthesis of new confinement cathodes for embedding sulfur and preventing polysulfide migration. The
second aspect of the project is related to conducting theoretical studies to identify functional electrocatalysts
for promoting the complete conversion of polysulfides to Li2S and back to elemental sulfur. The work also
related to calculation of the activation barriers and identification of electrocatalysts that will lower the
activation barriers. A third aspect of the work was directed at theoretical identification of alloying elements
and composition of alloys that will exhibit solid solution formation with lithium during electrochemical
cycling yielding high areal capacities of 10 m Ah cm-2 and above without inducing phase separation. This
portion of the work also included experimental synthesis of these alloys determined and identified by
theory. Experimental cell fabrication and testing were also conducted. All aspects of these three components
were successfully conducted and completed in the first year of the project. This project was therefore
successfully completed in FY2022, meeting the goals of year 1. All anticipated aims planned to be
completed in year 1 have been achieved. One entire book and one book chapter were also published as well
as two invited presentations were delivered in FY2022. Extensive collaboration between the team members
helped achieve very fruitful results.

Year 2 (2022-2023)
Results obtained during Year 2 (October 2022 - September 2023)
Throughout the course of Year 2 of this funded project, a combination of experimental and computational
methods was employed to work on different aspects of the Li-S batteries (LSBs), to meet the milestones of
the project. Experimental investigations were conducted to evaluate the electrochemical performance of the
various Complex Framework Materials (CFMs) developed and utilized as sulfur host materials to improve
the cathodic performance of the carbon-sulfur (C/S) composites. Various electrolyte additives were
identified and used in the baseline electrolyte to improve the interface between the electrode and electrolyte
to facilitate stable and robust SEI layers resulting in improved overall electrochemical performance of the
LSBs. Additionally, the potential of Multicomponent Alloy (MCA) identified by computational studies and
reported in the Year 1 section of the present report as dendrite-free anode materials was shown that can
reversibly cycle Li+ and exhibit a stable areal capacity of more than 15 m Ah cm-2. The promise of MCA to
be used as anode material by standard slurry coating was shown and their ability to be used as current
collectors was also demonstrated. In addition to the above experimental studies, research efforts were also
directed at conducting computational calculations dedicated to identifying strategies aimed at reducing the
solubility of polysulfides (PS) species in the solvents used in the electrolytes for the Li-S batteries. The
progress made in the project emphasizing the above aspects is presented in this annual report in the
following sections.

Electrochemical performance of MCA:

Initially, MCA systems were studied electrochemically and reported in the annual report of the previous
year. The MCA systems were further evaluated for their electrochemistry response and reported herein with
extended cycling showing their robustness and cyclic stability. The MCA serves as dendrite-free anode
material and the current collector was synthesized by a high-energy mechanical milling and alloying
(HEMM/HEMA) process. The structurally isomorphous system, MCA3 having a similar lattice registry
(bcc phase) to metallic Li, prevents Li dendrite growth by forming a solid solution with the reacting Li-ions
electrochemically presented to it from the metallic Li serving as the counter electrode. The ensuing
electrochemical alloying reaction occurring thereby, allows for a significant amount of Li to be solubilized
into the parent MCA3 alloy causing a minimal change in volume due to the solid solution formation. The
crystallographic arrangements of atoms in the MCA3 alloy being identical to that of Li metal enables it to
preserve the overall crystallographic symmetry and allows the formation of a solid solution with optimal
interfacial energy. The MCA3 alloy also improves the lithium diffusion preventing surface segregation
while continuing to maintain dendrite-free cycling, hence, serving as effective anodes as well as current
collectors for anode free cells in Li-S batteries.

The MCA3 alloy was prepared by high-energy mechanical alloying (HEMA) and the anode electrode for
electrochemical testing was prepared by standard slurry casting method (95 wt.% MCA3 and 5 wt.% PVDF)
in NMP solvent followed by electrochemical testing in a coin cell as shown in Figure 13. The electrode
with an area of 1.26 cm2 was used to assemble cells and flooded with 60 L of standard electrolyte. The
MCA3 cells with an average electrode loading of 6.2 mg cm−2 were initially cycled for rate capability at
different current rates viz. 1mA for 1h alloying and dealloying (A/D), 1mA 2h A/D, 1mA 4h A/D, 1.5mA
4h A/D for 31 cycles each and 2mA 4h A/D for 100 cycles followed by exploring cycling stability at 2.5mA
4h A/D for another 100 cycles. The cycling stability of MCA3 after 150 cycles of rate capability is depicted
in Figure 13 revealing excellent Li plating/stripping behavior affording a stable areal capacity of ~8 m Ah
cm-2 for continuous 100 cycles. The propensity of the MCA3 alloy electrode to demonstrate reversible Li
cycling (plating/stripping) is evinced in Figure 13 as the overpotential of the cell remains constant even
after cycling for more than 250 cycles. Further electrochemical testing of cells with MCA electrodes is
presented in upcoming sections. Additionally, efforts are directed at fabricating free-standing dense pellets
of the MCAs to serve as dendrite-resistant anode current collectors for reversibly cycling Li ions. Results
of these studies conducted on the MCAs electrodes and the electrochemical testing of the dense pellet of
MCAs serving as current collectors are presented in the subsequent sections of this report.

Pushing the boundaries of MCA alloy electrochemistry:
The MCAs are structurally isomorphous systems as explained above, and MCA3, a specific MCA system
as described earlier has a similar lattice registry (bcc phase) to metallic Li with an optimal interfacial
energy that prevents Li dendrite growth by electrochemically forming a solid solution with the reacting
Li-ions rather than plating. The crystallographic arrangements of atoms in the MCA3 alloy, being identical
to that of Li metal confer the ability to preserve the overall crystallographic symmetry preventing huge
volumetric changes and allowing the formation of a solid solution with Li preserving the bcc crystal
structure. The MCA3 alloy also improves the lithium diffusion preventing surface segregation while
continuing to maintain dendrite-free cycling, hence, serving as effective anodes as well as current
collectors in LSBs.
The HEMA-derived MCA3 alloy tested as an anode electrode was prepared by standard slurry casting
method (95 wt.% MCA3 and 5 wt.% PVDF) in N-methyl pyrrolidone (NMP) solvent followed by
electrochemical testing in a coin cell as shown in Figure 16. The cells were flooded with 60 L of standard
electrolyte. The MCA3 cells were prepared with an average electrode loading of 6.2 mg cm−2 and were
initially cycled for rate capability at different current rates as indicated in the previous section. To evaluate
the MCA3 alloy electrochemical capability to cycle Li+, it was tested and further studied at 3mA current
for 4 hours of A/D for another 200 cycles ending at 525 cycles which is depicted in Figure 16. The figure
reveals excellent Li plating/stripping behavior affording a stable areal capacity of ~9.5 m Ah cm-2 for

dendrite-free current collectors offering its use in a pre-lithiated alloyed form as well as anode-free
configuration of lithium-containing systems serving as cathodes.

Electrochemical response of CFM-S cathode:

The first part of this section is focused on evaluating the electrochemical performance of our CFM-S
cathodes at higher C-rates (C/10, C/5) in the standard electrolyte (1.0 M LiTFSI, 2 wt.% LiNO 3 in
DOL:DME, 1:1 v/v). The CFMs consist of submicron size (<2 m) carbon fibers intertwined, forming a
thick 3D conducting carbon mat utilized as a binder-less, free-standing sulfur confinement system. The
CFM-S cathodes avoid the use of binder and solvent by passing the standard procedure of slurry coating
method. Moreover, the CFMs are electronically conductive and exhibit inherent N-doping in the carbon
skeleton of the mats due to the nitrogen moieties present in the carbon precursor that can additionally trap
the LPSs and reduce their shuttling thereby, enhancing the overall electrochemical performance of the Li-
S cells. The CFM-S cathode mats were prepared by simple carbonization of electro-spun polymeric
precursor and by infusing sulfur into the mats by incipient wetting with sulfur dissolved in carbon
disulfide, S-CS2 solution. Firstly, a polymeric mat was prepared by electrospinning a 10% PAN solution
in DMF solvent. Electrospinning (e-spinning) is used to create the fine fiber mats of a submicron diameter
range from a polymer solution and the characteristics of the mats can be tailored by modulating the
respective e-spinning parameters. The e-spun PAN mat was stabilized in an air atmosphere for inducing
the cyclization of the polymeric chains followed by carbonization at 750 ℃ in an inert atmosphere. The
resultant carbon fiber mats (CFMs) thus obtained were cut into electrode size (1.26 cm2) and were soaked
in 10% S-CS2 solution for sulfur infusion to obtain the sulfur infiltrated CFM-S cathodes. Further
following this, these CFM-S cathodes were evaluated for their electrochemical performance in the coin
cell configuration.
The CFM-S cathodes with a range of areal sulfur loading of 3.6 – 4.5 mg cm-2 were assembled in coin cell
configuration and examined for their electrochemistry with an even leaner electrolyte conditions of

corresponding Li-S cell using CFM-S as cathode with 3.6 mg cm-2 sulfur loading cycled at various current

showed an initial specific capacity of 880 m Ah g-1. After 10 cycles of cycling, the current rate was altered,
and the cells were cycled at C/10 rate for the next 90 cycles. The specific capacity as shown in Figure 19
steadily increases after initial variation, possibly due to the electrolyte wetting issues attaining stabilization

showed a specific capacity of 1051 m Ah g-1 and exhibited moderately stable cyclic performance. After
100 cycles, the cells displayed moderately stable reversible capacity of 939 m Ah g-1 with a coulombic

structure due to optimal interfacial energy and results in negligible volume change during cycling. These
results highlight the potential of the MCA3 alloy to serve as dendrite-free current collectors, making the
alloy system suitable for use in both pre-lithiated alloyed forms for non-lithium containing cathode systems
such as the Li-S batteries, and as part of an anode-free configuration in lithium-containing systems
functioning as cathodes. Moreover, the MCA systems demonstrate gravimetric densities over 50% lower
than those of the currently employed copper current collectors, rendering them appealing as lightweight
current collectors. These results therefore highlight the potential of the MCA3 alloy to serve as dendrite-
free current collectors, making the alloy suitable for use in both pre-lithiated alloyed forms for Li metal
batteries (LMBs) and even in anode-free configuration for lithium-containing systems functioning as
cathodes such as LiCoO2, LiNiO2, and high Ni containing LiNixCoyMn1-x-yO2, (NMC), etc.

identification of different electrolyte additives with the capability to suppress or at least, to hinder the rapid

LiS6- and 2) further continuing the dissociation of LiS6- into Li+ and S62- species.

2. Experimental Studies
2.1. Enhancing Sulfur Cathode Performance: Electrocatalysts, Binders, and Framework Design
In the previous annual reports, the team reported to have identified plausible functional electrocatalyst
(FEC) from theory-driven computational studies that would reduce the energy barrier of the rate-limiting
step enhancing the reaction kinetics of sulfur cathode for its complete conversion and facilitate its proper
utilization. This year, among theory-identified FECs, the team experimentally synthesized the FEC (Cat38)
using a simple and scalable precipitation method followed by thermal treatment and incorporated in the
carbon framework material (CFM) by adopting three different approaches to obtain carbon/sulfur cathode
composites. Additionally, different binders were also explored as they play a critical role in maintaining the
structural integrity of the cathode ensuring optimal electrochemical performance. Moreover, the sulfur
confinement system (CFM) architecture was modified and redesigned to address critical challenges related
to the physical and chemical properties of sulfur thereby enhancing the battery performance.
2.1.1. Exploring various approaches for the integration of FEC in the CFMs and their electrochemical
 performance
It was reported in the previous annual reports that FEC (Cat38) reduces the kinetic energy barrier of the
rate-limiting step of Li2S2 to Li2S conversion by more than 70% from 1.0 eV to 0.26 eV. The improvement
in the reaction kinetics of this rate-determining step facilitates the rapid conversion of Li2S2 to Li2S and S
and the proper utilization of the S in the cathode. The experimentally synthesized FEC also shows the
ability to adsorb and trap Li-polysulfides (LPS) on the surface of the FEC. The incorporation of FEC in

 26

the CFM plays a crucial role and affects the overall electrochemical performance of the Li-S cells.
Achieving a uniform dispersion and ensuring intimate contact between the FEC and the CFM particles is
crucial for realizing the full potential of the functional electrocatalyst in enhancing the sulfur cathode
performance. Thus, the FEC was incorporated into the CFM and the sulfur cathodes following three
different strategies. Furthermore, the final amount of FEC in the cathodes is influenced by the method
used to incorporate the FEC in the CFM. Initially, the FEC was incorporated by the in-situ precipitation
method on the CFM (FC1) which resulted in a non-uniform distribution of 12.5 wt. % of the electrocatalyst
on the CFM surface. Conversely, employing the in-situ incorporation method via the coating approach on
CFM (FC2) a more uniform distribution with 4.5 wt% content was achieved, creating an improved
interface conducive to promoting the conversion reactions. In the third approach, a 5 wt.% FEC was
directly incorporated as an additive into the cathode slurry (FC3) to effectively alleviate the LPS shuttling
and enhance the stability of the cathode.
The FC1 and FC2 were infused with sulfur by the melt diffusion process and electrodes were prepared by
the standard slurry casting method using PVDF as a binder with 64% S content. The CFM was also infused
with sulfur and tested as a baseline cathode (C/S) using lean electrolyte conditions of E/S ratio of 8 ml/mgS
to examine the influence of FEC on the overall electrochemical performance of the cathode composites in
Li-S coin cells. Additionally, FC3/S cathodes were prepared by adding FEC as an additive to the baseline
C/S during slurry preparation. A standard electrolyte (1.0 M LiTFSI, 2 wt.% LiNO3 in DOL:DME, 1:1
v/v) was used for the electrochemical testing. Multiple coin cells were tested for each sample with low
and high areal sulfur loadings with selected results presented in this report. Fig. 23a shows the
electrochemical cycling performance of baseline C/S cathode cells at various current rates. The cells show
an initial specific discharge capacity of ~825 mAh g-1 at a C/20 rate that drastically fades in the subsequent
cycles and then shows stable cycling performance at a C/10 rate for 60 cycles. However, the specific
capacity of the C/S electrode declines and shows signs of LPS shuttling as the coulombic efficiency (CE)
decreases thereafter. At the C/5 rate, the C/S cells show an initial capacity of ~475 mAh g-1 which reduces
steadily over cycling. Fig. 23b also shows the cycling performances of FEC-incorporated sulfur cathode
materials. FC1/S cathode cells show significantly higher initial discharge capacities of ~1025 mAh g-1
than the baseline (C/S) due to the presence of FEC. However, the specific capacities rapidly fade in
subsequent cycles at C/20 and C/10 rates possibly due to the non-homogeneous distribution of FEC
agglomerates in the CFM. However, the FC1/S cells (Fig. 23b) show stable cycling performance with
capacities of ~396 mAh g-1 even after 150 cycles at various C-rates. Although FC1/S shows high initial
specific capacities and long-term stability it is crucial to address the rapid capacity decay after the first

2.3. Optimization of electrolyte composition for stable Li-S batteries
It is well known that the dissolution of polysulfides in the electrolyte is one of the major concerns of Li-S
batteries. To mitigate the same, the team attempted to identify potential electrolyte additives and
additionally, conduct electrolyte optimization by theoretical calculations. The theoretical studies so far
discussed in this report indicate both DOL and DEE exhibit lower polysulfide solubility in comparison to
DME. Therefore, to prove the same experimentally, the team conducted UV-vis absorption spectrometry
analysis on 50 mM Li2S6 solution dissolved individually in all three DOL, DME, and DEE solvents.

Initially, 0.1 M Li2S6 solution was prepared in the three individual solvents and stirred individually for 24

Furthermore, the intensity around 250 nm and 320 nm corresponds to the S62-, S62-/S42- phase conversions,

different ratios of these solvents and further employ the best composition exhibiting optimal polysulfide

Conclusions for Year 3
Research work was conducted both experimentally and computationally on enhancing the individual
performance of the three different components (anode, cathode, and electrolyte) of the LSBs. These include
experimental synthesis and optimization of the new confinement cathodes for embedding sulfur and
preventing polysulfide migration combined with theoretical studies. The work resulted in the following
outcomes that were achieved following the studies conducted in Year 3. Research in the cathode area led
to successful generation of density functional theory (DFT) identified functional electrocatalysts (FECs)
and incorporating these FECs directly onto the carbon framework materials (CFM) following infusion of
sulfur via melt diffusion process demonstrate the ability of the FEC containing systems to exhibit higher
specific capacity of ~1025 m Ah g-1 which is significantly higher than the specific capacity of ~825 m Ah
g-1 exhibited by fabricated baseline C/S cells for S loadings of 4.5 mg cm -2 and 4.7 mg cm-2, respectively
in coin cells when cycled at C/20 rates under electrolyte/sulfur ratios of 8 ml g -1S. Further work conducted
in synthesizing and incorporating theory identified functional electrocatalyst (FEC) into the carbon
framework materials (CFM) involved utilizing various strategies to successfully incorporate FEC in CFM
and evaluating their electrochemical performance. Results show that incorporation of FEC helps to achieve
areal capacities of ~4 mAh cm2 in the initial cycles with long term stability albeit with some decrease in
areal capacity. Continuing the work in the cathode systems, three strategies for FEC integration were
explored and it was determined that integration of FEC into the slurry provided the best results using
polymeric binder B1 with areal sulfur loading of 4.9 mg/cm2 showing an initial specific capacity of ~1032
mAh/g corresponding to an areal capacity of ~4 mAh/cm2 displaying stable capacities at various C-rates
exhibiting 650 mAh/g after 60 cycles at C/5 rate with more than 99% coulombic efficiency. Finally, the
synthesis and processesing of the carbon framework material (CFM) as well as subequent melt infusion
process was modified to achieve homogeneous infiltration of sulfur into the CFM network. The results
show that specific capacity of 1087 mAh g-1 (~4.6 mAh cm-2) was achieved at C/10 rate with reversible
capacity of 876 mAh g-1 at C/5 after 90 cycles. In the anode area, the potential of MCA alloy, namely
MCA3 made into a pellet without the use of any additives and binders to exhibit stable cycling for 250
cycles with an areal capacity of 5 m Ah cm-2 and coulombic efficiency of 100% after initial variations in
the energy efficiency during initial cycles possibly due to SEI formation was demonstrated. Additionally,
the potential of MCA to be pre-lithiated and use it as a Li reservoir for cycling was explored. The Li-ions
from the pre-lithiated MCA was used for reversible cycling and the promise of MCA as an anode-free
current collector was demonstrated showing reversible areal capacities of ~6 mAh cm2 with 100%
coulombic efficiency from the first cycle which was maintained for over 300 cycles attesting to the promise
of these alloys. Successful extended cycling was also demonstrated giving an areal capacity of 6 mAh/cm2
showing promise for use as current collectors with 100% energy efficiency. Finally, efforts were directed
at generating dendrite free Li-containing structurally isomorphous alloy (SIA) as alternative Li-metal anode
for high energy density batteries. The SIA system showed excellent cycling for over 140 cycles while also
exhibiting a reduction in the overpotential from ~48 mV to ~18 mV. Modification by adding theory
identified alloying element improved the Li-ion diffusion in SIA and demonstrated their ability to form
solid-solution by reducing the nucleation overpotential and diffusion barrier. These cells also show
excellent cycling for more than 350 cycles with a reduction in overpotential from ~47 mV to ~14 mV.
Theoretical studies involved utilizing computational first principles approach to study effect of various
 36

electrolyte additives on stability of SEI at the Li-based anode. Adsorption energy were calculated for two
chemical agents, CA-1 and CA-2 to adsorb to (100) surfaces of different metallic elements constituting the
MCA and structurally isomorphous alloy (SIA) alloyed anodes and the results show that these elements
bind to CA-1 more strongly than to pure Li demonstrating the beneficial effect of formation of SEI at MCA
and SIA anodes compared to metallic Li alone. In addition, efforts were directed to study the effects of
electrolyte additives on the adsorption energies of various elements in the MCA to form a stable solid
electrolyte interphase (SEI) as well as enabling reversible Li cycling. Results show the influence of
chemical additives binding more strongly with the MCA elements thus stabilizing the SEI. Computational
studies were also conducted to explore electrolyte additives using 1,2-diethoxyethane (DEE). The results
showed that DEE and DOL exhibit lower degree of dissolution of polysulfides and can improve Li-S battery

2. The potential of Li-SIA alloys as dendrite-free anodes with robust stability and long cyclability

As reported in the previous quarter, the team has initiated the study of Li-containing structurally
isomorphous alloys (Li-SIA) as anode for Li-S batteries. It is well known that Li metal is the most suitable
anode for Li-S batteries owing to its high specific capacity of 3860 mAh/g and low reduction potential of
-3.04 V vs. SHE. However, the well-known instability of the electrode-electrolyte interface during cycling
results in instabilities and perturbations which is one of the limitations impeding practical application. The
low reduction potential of Li metal also makes it more reactive towards electrolytes and results in the
formation of a passive layer immediately on the surface of the metal. This passive layer is too fragile to
withstand the mechanical stresses during cycling accompanied by inhomogeneous nucleation and growth
of the lithium that leads to continuous failure and reconstruction of the surface also contributing to the
formation of recalcitrant dendrites. Secondly, the interface instabilities, lack of uniform wettability, and
crystallographic mismatch combined with inhomogeneous deposition of Li together results in a large
change in volume which directly relates to the dendrite formation. To address the issue, solid solution-
based alloy anodes have come into the limelight. These SIAs have a similar bcc crystallographic crystal
structure matching that of Li and exhibit optimal interfacial energy minimizing the interface instability
thus, forming a solid solution with Li. As a result, Li-ions dissolve into the SIA alloy rather than plating
over it preventing formation of dendritic structures and then consequently, overcome the large-volume

3. Validating the design of mCFM/S and the efficacy of the theory identified functional
 electrocatalyst, EC integrated into the cathode composite in coin cell configuration.

To overcome key challenges associated with sulfur cathodes, the carbon framework material (CFM)
architecture was systematically redesigned to enhance sulfur confinement and overall battery
performance. A modified CFM (mCFM) was developed by blending highly porous commercial Ketjen
Black (KB) with synthesized CFM in a 1:1 ratio. The composite was subjected to wet milling, vacuum
drying, and controlled heat treatment to achieve a micro-mesoporous structure with high electronic
conductivity, optimal porosity and specific surface area attributes well-suited for accommodating sulfur
and facilitating redox kinetics. Sulfur was incorporated into the mCFM through a melt infusion process
conducted at 155 °C for 15 hours in a quartz ampoule, ensuring uniform sulfur distribution and effective
infiltration within the porous carbon matrix. The resulting composite, referred to as mCFM/S, served as
the baseline cathode material. For electrochemical testing, the mCFM/S cathode composite was
formulated into a slurry using an aqueous binder system (LA133), with a composition ratio of 80:10:4:6
for active material (mCFM-S), Super P, highly crystalline CNTs, and LA133, respectively. The areal sulfur
loading was maintained in the range of 4.0 - 4.2 mg/cm². Coin cells were assembled using a standard
ether-based electrolyte (1 M LiTFSI with 2 wt.% LiNO3 in DOL:DME, 1:1 v/v) under lean electrolyte
conditions (E/S ratio = 8 µl/mgS). The electrochemical performance of the baseline cathodes was
evaluated through formation cycles at C/20 rate, followed by cycling for 10 more cycles at a C/10 rate
and extended cycling at a C/5 rate using 1C = 1675 mAh. Representative electrochemical data from one
of the multiple tested cells are presented in Fig. 36a–c. The cycling performance of the baseline cathodes
at various C-rates, including the formation cycles at C/20, C/10, and extended cycling at C/5 rates, is
shown in Fig. 36a.

During the initial formation cycles, the baseline mCFM/S cell delivered a reversible capacity of
1042 mAh/g at a C/20 rate. The cell maintained a stable capacity of 780 mAh/g up to 50 cycles,
underscoring the effectiveness of the redesigned sulfur confinement architecture. Continued cycling
beyond 100 cycles showed a retained capacity of approximately 700 mAh/g, demonstrating reasonable
stability. However, the modified carbon framework, while effective in sulfur confinement, did not
sufficiently enhance the redox reaction kinetics or suppress the shuttle of lithium polysulfides (LPS). This
resulted in declining energy efficiency after 50 cycles, primarily due to LPS migration and subsequent
side reactions at the Li-metal anode. These reactions led to the formation of a passivating Li2S layer,

increased impedance, and gradual loss of active material. By the 105th cycle, the cells failed due to these
side effects and elevated cell resistance. To mitigate these issues, functional electrocatalyst Cat38 (EC)
was incorporated directly into the mCFM/S composite during slurry preparation, enabling uniform
integration with the active material to promote faster redox kinetics and suppress LPS shuttling.

Previous reports demonstrated that the theory-identified electrocatalyst (Cat38) lowers the Li2S2 to Li2S/S
conversion barrier to 0.26 eV, accelerating polysulfide conversion. Direct integration of Cat38 into the

theory-identified, experimentally synthesized EC. Compared to baseline mCFM/S cathodes, mEC3/S
cathodes with EC exhibited higher specific capacities, improved reaction kinetics, better sulfur utilization,
and enhanced cycling stability, demonstrating superior electrochemical performance for Li-S batteries.

4. Evaluating the electrochemical performance of mCFM/S and mEC3/S in pouch cell configuration

To assess the practical viability of the developed cathode materials, prototype pouch cells were fabricated
using the baseline cathode (mCFM/S) and electrocatalyst incorporated cathode (mEC3/S) cathodes. The
baseline mCFM/S pouch cell was assembled with an area of 20 cm2 giving an areal sulfur loading of
2 mg/cm2. The pouch cells were fabricated with a lean (E/S) ratio of 2 mL/g and the electrochemical cycling

C/20 rate using 1C = 1675 mAh, the cell delivered a promising discharge capacity of ~950 mAh/g (Fig.

framework. When cycled at a C/10 rate again using 1C = 1675 mAh, the pouch cell retained a stable and

low throughout the cycling process, suggesting the occurrence of LPS shuttling and associated parasitic
side reactions at the lithium metal anode. These challenges indicate the limitations of the mCFM/S system
in fully mitigating polysulfide shuttling effects, underscoring the need for further modifications to enhance
the redox kinetics and interfacial stability. These limitations were addressed through the incorporation of
functional electrocatalysts, EC in the sulfur cathode design (mEC3/S).

The mEC3/S cathodes were, accordingly, evaluated in pouch cell configurations to assess their
electrochemical performance under practical loading and electrolyte conditions. The results of the
electrochemical cycling of the mEC3/S pouch cell are shown in Fig. 37 d, e. The cathodes were fabricated
with an areal sulfur loading of 3.5 mg/cm² and assembled with an electrolyte-to-sulfur (E/S) ratio of 5 mL/g.

and shown in Fig 37 a-e also show the feasibility of fabrication and testing of the single-layer pouch cells

7. Evaluating of the electrochemical performance of mCFM/S and mEC3/S full cells with Lithium-
 containing SIA (Li-SIA) alloys as anode in a pouch cell configuration
As detailed in previous reports, the carbon framework material (CFM) architecture was optimised
and modified by blending highly porous Ketjen Black (KB) carbon with a synthesised CFM to
 50

create a modified, conductive, and porous mCFM structure. Sulfur was then subsequently infused
into the mCFM through a melt diffusion process at 155 °C for 15 hours, resulting in the baseline
mCFM/S composite. Furthermore, the DFT identified functional electrocatalyst (Cat38) was
then incorporated into the mCFM/S cathodes, as previously reported, to enhance the reaction
kinetics by lowering the conversion barrier of the rate-limiting reversible Li2S2 → Li2S/S step to
0.26 eV. In earlier studies that were reported, electrochemical evaluations demonstrated that Li-
containing structurally isomorphous alloy (Li-SIA) anodes significantly outperformed Li-metal
anodes, exhibiting superior cycling stability, higher capacity retention, and improved resistance
to polysulfide reduction. Building upon these findings, this quarter, the team focused all their
efforts towards the evaluation of the electrochemical performance of the mCFM/S and the
functional electrocatalyst (EC) incorporated mEC3/S cathodes in pouch cell configurations to
assess their practical applicability. The corresponding results are presented and discussed in Figs.
41-42. The mCFM/S and mEC3/S cathodes were tested using 1 M LiTFSI, 2 wt.% LiNO 3 in
DOL:DME (1:1) with 1% chemical electrolyte additives and cathode modifying electrolyte
additive used as electrolyte additives EA1 and EA2, respectively, under practical and lean
conditions electrolyte to Sulfur ratio (E/S = 4.2 µL/mg) with a 100 µm Li-SIA1 alloy anode. It
should be noted that both EA1 and EA2 are demonstrated to form stable SEI layers on the Li-
SIA anodes and the C-S cathodes respectively, in previous reports. Cathode slurries were thus
prepared with 80:14:6 (mCFM/S) and 80:5:9:6 (mEC3/S) ratios of the active material, DFT
identified functional electrocatalyst, Cat 38 (EC), conducting carbon, and LA133 binder,
maintaining 64% S content in the cathodes. The mCFM/S and mEC3/S cathodes were calendared
to reduce electrode porosity, ensuring uniform thickness, enhanced compactness, and improved
electrical conductivity. The pouch cells, with an electrode area of 20 cm2 are paired with Li-SIA1
alloy anode (100 µm thick) developed by the team as a safer and more stable dendrite-free
alternative to conventional Li-metal anodes prone to corrosion and dendrite formation.
Correspondingly, Li-SIA1 alloy anodes store Li-ions within their crystal lattice through a solid
solution mechanism preserving the crystal lattice registry throughout the charge and discharge
processes. This structure inhibits the dendrite formation and moreover, also is prone to prevent
Li-polysulfide reduction on its surface, thereby improving the safety and overall cycling stability
of the Li–S system. Notably, the fabricated cathodes exhibited minor surface particle
agglomeration, indicating that further optimization of the slurry preparation parameters such as

cathodes, subsequent efforts were directed toward optimizing the slurry preparation parameters to achieve
uniform particle dispersion and improved electrode homogeneity. Particle agglomeration can lead to
uneven current distribution, hindered ionic transport, and reduced electrochemical activity ultimately
contributing to poor sulfur utilization and increased polarization during cycling. To overcome these issues,
the active materials were initially pre-mixed in powder form using a dual asymmetric centrifugal mixer,
which promotes high-shear, homogeneous intermixing of the components. This approach ensures uniform
dispersion of the electrocatalyst and conductive carbon within the sulfur–carbon composite matrix. The
resulting powder blend was then incorporated into a binder solution and mixed for 24 hours to fully
eliminate agglomerates and achieve optimal slurry rheology before casting onto the current collector. This
optimization led to the successful fabrication of mEC3/S cathodes with minimal surface agglomeration

rate-capability tests at C/5 and C/3 to evaluate their long-term stability and performance under practical
cycling conditions. These cells following successful completion will be assembled in a stacked form to
yield 1Ah rated capacity and will be delivered to Argonne National Laboratory (ANL) for further

Conclusions for Year 4 and the entire Project.
The team experimentally validated the design architecture of the modified carbon framework material and
sulfur (mCFM/S) cathode composite using density functional theory (DFT) identified functional
electrocatalyst (EC) incorporated in the cathode composite (mEC/S) during slurry preparation. Results
show that with the incorporation of ECs promoting efficient reversible conversion of polysulfides to Li2S,
and an improved shear mixing utilizing dual asymmetric centrifugal force and electrode formation
process, the cells show high initial specific capacity of 1225 mAh g-1 for an areal capacity of 5.34 mAh
cm-2. Dendrite-free, Li-containing structurally isomorphous alloys (SIA) are proposed as alternatives to
Li-metal anodes for high-energy-density batteries. The addition of a theory-identified alloying element,
M1, served as a diffusion promoter and enhanced Li-ion diffusion, reduced nucleation overpotential, and
lowered the diffusion barrier, enabling solid-solution formation. Thus, these new M1 containing SIA alloys
exhibit stable areal capacities of 0.25mAh cm-2 with 100% coulombic efficiency for 1000 cycles with

remarkable decrease in overpotential from ~49 mV to ~47 mV for the first cycle and from ~16 mV to ~

than 100 cycles.

Overall Conclusions.
The 45 month project spanning 4 years witnessed extensive progress in the following four areas that have
been compiled by each year above and detailed in the conclusions for every year. Provided below are the
overall highlights and summaries of the progress made in each of the four categories of anodes,
electrolytes and additives, cathode and the full cell tests involving the anode, modified electrolytes, and
cathodes.
Anode: Progress was made in identifying and developing several novel non-Li containing
multicomponent alloys (MCA) determined by density functional theory (DFT) and experimentally
generated. These MCA systems serve as dendrite free current collectors and similarly, several Li-
containing structurally isomorpous alloys (Li-SIA) containing DFT determined Li-ion diffusion enhancers
also alloyed into the parent structure as a single solid solution serving as dendrite free anodes. The novel
specific MCA3 alloy exhibits areal capacity as high as ~12 mAh/cm2 when used as a slurry casted
electrode for over 750 cycles. On the other hand, when pressed into a pellet without any binding agents,
the electrode was able to cycle for more than 700 cycles exhibiting an areal capacity of 6 mAh/cm2. The
Li-SIA alloys exhibited stable cycling for 1000 cycles at areal capacities of 0.25 mAh/cm2 when cycled
against Li while exhibiting more than 500 cycles at areal capacities of 5 mAh/cm2 in symmetric cell
configurations. Addition of 1 wt. % of Li-ion diffusion enhancers determined by DFT analysis
significantly lowered the over-potential from 47.2 mV to 15.18 mV, after 1000 cycles contrasted with the
SIA without the additive (49.04 mV and 16.36 mV).
Electrolyte and Electrolyte Additives: Progress was made in identifying electrolyte additives that stabilize
the solid electrolyte interfaces (SEI) on the cathode and anode electrodes. DFT calculations also validated
the influence of the additives on improving the binding energy to the SIA surface and carbon and S.
Furthermore, DFT analysis confirmed the ability of electrolyte to demonstrate resistance to polysulfide
dissolution indicating that polysulfides (PSs) solubility decreases in the following order: DME > DOL >
DEE > F4DEE > F6DEE. This finding, therefore, will be useful to design new liquid electrolytes
demonstrating lower PS solubility and thus, contribute to improving the Li-S battery characteristics.
Cathode: In the area of cathodes, significant progress was made to identify carbon framework materials
(CFM) with optimal electronic conductivity and porosity to incorporate high loadings of sulfur.
Experimental approaches were designed to also incorporate the DFT identified electrocatalyst. The
optimized electrodes exhibited ~3.8 mAh/cm² areal capacity at a C/10 rate. Even at a C/5rate, the cathodes
demonstrated a reversible areal capacity of ~3.3 mAh/cm2, maintaining stability for over 100 cycles.
Full Cell (coin cell and pouch cell) Studies: Finally, the optimized SIA anodes and cathodes along with
the electrolyte additives were assembled in full cells in coin cell and pouch cell formats. The results to
date showed that with SIA1 anode, mCFM/S cell with S loading of 4.36 mg/cm2 in E/S = 8 mL/gS, shows
an initial capacity of ~1076 mAh/g at C/20 rate, stabilizing at ~785 mAh/g after 12 cycles at C/10 rate,
retaining capacity of ~633 mAh/g even after 176 cycles at C/5 rate, validating long-term stability.
Moreover, with SIA1 electrodes, the mEC3/S electrodes with 3.63 mg/cm2 areal S loading showed a first
cycle discharge capacity of 979 mAh/g at C/20 rate and ~782 mAh/g after 15 cycles at C/10 rate, and 628
mAh/g after 215 cycles at C/5 rate showing an impressive capacity retention of 89% for 200 cycles at C/5
rate and discharge capacity of 528 mAh/g at the 290th cycle at C/3 rate demonstrating the long term
cyclability of SIA anodes for high energy density Li-S batteries. In pouch cell tests, single layer pouch
cells of mEC3/S containing electrolyte additives EA1 and EA2 with SIA1 anodes revealed improved
electrochemical reversibility giving a stable capacity of ~740 mAh/g at C/10 after 77 cycles (3.37
mAh/cm2 at areal sulfur loading of 4.55 mg/cm2) due to DFT identified EC, favoring faster redox
 55

conversion of polysulfides obviating kinetic polarization. Notably, negligible capacity loss is seen when
the current rate was increased from C/20 to C/10. With improved slurry preparation, the mEC3/S cathodes
