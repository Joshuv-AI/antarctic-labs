Association between caesarean birth and childhood acute respiratory infection in low- and middle-income countries: a meta-analysis of population-based data
Authors: Kusse Urmale Mare, Engida Yisma, Zohra S Lassi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCaesarean section (CS) rates are rising across low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), frequently exceeding levels considered medically necessary. While CS can be lifesaving, emerging evidence from high-income settings suggests that delivery by CS may increase children’s susceptibility to respiratory infections. However, population-based evidence from LMICs remains limited. We investigated the association between mode of delivery and acute respiratory infection (ARI) among children younger than five years across diverse LMIC settings.MethodsWe analysed nationally representative Demographic and Health Surveys data from 47 LMICs collected between 2015 and 2024, including 534,423 singleton live births. Mode of delivery (caesarean section vs. vaginal birth) was the exposure. ARI was defined as maternal report of cough with rapid or difficult breathing in the preceding two weeks before each survey. Country-specific adjusted prevalence ratios (aPRs) were estimated using modified Poisson regression models accounting for sociodemographic, maternal, perinatal, and environmental factors. Country-specific estimates were then pooled using random-effects meta-analysis, including subgroup analyses by national income level and caesarean section rate.ResultsThe prevalence of caesarean section ranged from 1.3% in Chad to 44.9% in Bangladesh. The prevalence of ARI ranged from < 1% in Mozambique, Cote d’Ivoire, the Philippines, and the Maldives to 14% in Pakistan. In pooled analyses, children born by caesarean section had a higher prevalence of ARI than those born vaginally (pooled aPR 1.11; 95% CI 1.02–1.20), with low between-country heterogeneity (I² = 9.62%). The association was stronger in low- and lower-middle-income countries and in countries with very low caesarean section rates (< 10%), where caesarean birth was associated with a 17% higher prevalence of ARI.ConclusionsAcross 47 LMICs, caesarean birth was associated with a modestly higher prevalence of acute respiratory infection in children younger than five years. While this finding is consistent with existing evidence, it should be interpreted with caution given the potential for residual confounding, including confounding by indication. These findings highlight the importance of reducing non-medically indicated caesarean sections while ensuring access to life-saving obstetric care. Background

Caesarean section (CS) rates have increased globally, particularly in Low- and Middle-Income Countries (LMICs), where the highest percentage increase has occurred since 2010 [1–3]. Evidence shows that over one-fifth (21%) of births worldwide occur by CS, with rates ranging from 5% in Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) to nearly 43% in Latin America and the Caribbean. The number of CS births in LMICs is projected to reach 33.5 million by 2030 [1, 2]. Moreover, a 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis involving 600,431 participants in East African countries showed that about 24% of women gave birth by CS [4]. Although caesarean section can be a lifesaving intervention when medically indicated, its rapid increase is a growing global public health concern, particularly in resource-limited settings where health system constraints may exacerbate potential risk [1–3].

Emerging evidence suggests that caesarean birth may be associated with early-life respiratory complications, including acute respiratory infection (ARI) [5–7]. Several biological mechanisms may explain this relationship. One key underlying mechanism for a higher risk of ARI among caesarean births is that mode of delivery alters a newborn’s respiratory adaptation by delaying lung fluid clearance and hormonal shifts that prepare the lungs for extra-uterine life, exposing infants to higher risks of respiratory problems [6, 8, 9]. Moreover, CS-born infants also miss exposure to beneficial maternal vaginal microbiota, which aids mucosal immune maturation [10]. It has been demonstrated that CS delays the initiation of breastfeeding, and this in turn limits the transfer of protective antibodies, impairing the immune system’s resistance against lung infections, thereby increasing the risk of ARI and mortality in young children [11–13].

ARI remains the main cause of illness and mortality among children under five years worldwide [14], with pneumonia accounting for 14% of all deaths in this age group [15]. A secondary analysis of data from 25 countries across SSA reported that the prevalence of ARI in children under five ranged from 1.0 % to 9.8% [16], while a scoping review of 43 studies in Africa reported a wider range of 1.9% to 60.2% [17], respectively. Moreover, a 2019 study by Seidu et al., which included 28 African countries, reported an ARI rate of 25% in young children, with the highest burden in Congo (40%) and the lowest in Togo (7%) [18]. In resource-constrained regions such as LMICs, limited neonatal care, weak infection prevention, and poor postnatal follow-up intensify the risk of illness and death from ARI [19]. A higher rate of ARI across these settings is also attributed to poor living conditions, illiteracy, malnutrition, indoor air pollution, and lack of healthcare access [17, 20].

Existing evidence from high-income countries suggests that caesarean birth is linked with a higher risk of respiratory symptoms and disorders during childhood, particularly when the birth occurs before 37 weeks of gestation [7–9, 11, 21, 22]. Likewise, a 2020 systematic review and meta-analysis of 14 observational studies in high-income countries showed that neonates born by CS are at a higher risk of respiratory morbidities compared with vaginal births [6]. In contrast, two observational studies conducted in Switzerland [23] and Japan [24] found no association between CS and incidence of respiratory illness in children during infancy and school age. However, the Swiss study was limited by a relatively small sample size and restricted to term neonates born to healthy mothers, while the Japanese study was based on the data from children under 12 months of age.

A CS rate of 10 to 15% at the population level is considered to be optimal by the World Health Organization (WHO) because the rate within this range is linked to a positive outcome for mother and newborn [25]. Evidence revealed that rates exceeding this threshold are not associated with better outcomes, instead raising concerns about the potential unnecessary use of surgical intervention and the associated health risks [26]. This established range serves as a benchmark for assessing and guiding obstetric care quality and resource allocations within the healthcare system. Despite this guidance, current evidence shows a rising trend in CS rates across all regions and the global rate is projected to reach 28.5% by 2030 [2].

The implementation of non-clinical interventions (e.g., financial incentive system for healthcare providers and free vaginal delivery policy) [27], and clinical strategies (e.g., labour companionship and midwife-led-care models) [28, 29] has shown promise in optimising childbirth practices and reducing unnecessary caesarean sections, particularly in high-income countries. However, the proportion of births delivered by CS continues to rise across both high- and low-income settings [1–3]. Although increasing evidence indicates that neonates born by CS are at a higher risk of acquiring ARI and other long-term complications in childhood, most of the studies examining the association between CS and ARI have been conducted in high-income settings [6, 7, 21–24]. On the other hand, previous population-based studies across LMICs mainly focused on the influence of socio-economic, maternal, child, and environmental factors on ARI in early life, with comparatively limited attention to the role of mode of delivery in respiratory morbidities in children under five years [14, 16–18, 20]. Moreover, population-based evidence from LMICs examining the association between CS and ARI in children younger than five years of age remains limited. The purpose of this study was to investigate the association between mode of delivery and ARI among children under five years across 47 LMICs.

Methods

Data sources

We obtained the most recent datasets from the standard Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS) conducted across 47 LMICs between 2015 and 2024. The selection of countries was based on the availability of the most recent DHS datasets (DHS VII and DHS VIII). The 2015–2024 period was chosen to ensure adequate coverage of recent trends and contextual changes within countries, including changes in caesarean section rates.

Before inclusion, each country’s DHS dataset was assessed for the availability of data on the main exposure (mode of delivery) and the outcome of interest (ARI). Countries lacking data on these key variables were excluded from the analysis.

The study population was further restricted to singleton live births occurring within the survey reference period for assessing caesarean birth (i.e., five years preceding the survey for DHS VII and two years preceding the survey for DHS VIII). Accordingly, DHS datasets from 47 countries comprising 534,423 singleton live births were included in the final analysis. Countries highlighted in blue from which the data for this study were drawn

The DHS are nationally representative surveys conducted periodically, typically every five years, by host country’s Ministry of Health or relevant government agency, with technical support from ICF International. To ensure consistency and comparability across countries, the DHS uses standardised methodologies and instruments throughout the design and data collection processes. The survey gathers data on a wide range of demographic and health indicators, including maternal and child health, mortality, nutrition, and other socioeconomic and health-related factors.

Exposure

In DHS, information on the mode of delivery is collected for the most recent birth occurring within the two years (DHS VIII) or five years (DHS VII) preceding each survey. In resource-limited settings, self-reported DHS data have been shown to provide a more reliable estimate of national CS rate than often incomplete facility-based records [30]. In this study, the exposed group comprised singleton live births delivered by CS, while the unexposed group included children born vaginally within the two- or five-year reference period preceding each DHS survey.

Outcome

The outcome variable in this study was ARI, assessed based on the maternal recall of respiratory symptoms in the index child. Consistent with previous studies [14, 16, 17], ARI was defined as the presence of cough accompanied by rapid or difficult breathing in a child under five years of age within the last two weeks preceding each survey.

Confounders

Confounder selection in this study was guided by established epidemiological principles, whereby a confounder is defined as a common cause of both the exposure and the outcome. The confounders were identified based on a priori subject-matter knowledge and evidence from previous literature [4, 14, 16, 18, 24, 31, 32]. They included place of residence (urban or rural), perceived distance to health facility (big problem or not a big problem), household wealth index (poorest, poorer, middle, richer or richest), maternal age at birth (< 20 years, 20–34 years or 35–49 years), maternal education (no education, primary, secondary or higher), maternal occupation (not working, professional/technical/managerial, sales and services, skilled and unskilled manual occupations, agriculture or others), sex of child (female or male), pregnancy planning (wanted then, wanted later or wanted no more), number of antenatal care visits (none, one, 2–3 or ≥ 4), place of delivery (home, public sectors, private sectors or others), birth order (one, 2–3, 4–5 or ≥ 6), indoor air pollution (yes or no), size of child at birth (large, average or small), and maternal smoking status (yes or no).

Maternal age at birth of last child was computed as a difference (in years) between the mother’s date of birth and the child’s date of birth [33]. Birth interval was defined as the interval, in months, between two consecutive births. Household cooking fuel type was used as a proxy measure of indoor air pollution. Households using clean fuels (electricity, liquefied petroleum gas, natural gas, or biogas) were classified as having no indoor air pollution, whereas households using polluting fuels (kerosene, coal, lignite, charcoal, wood, animal dung, straw/bush/grass, cardboard, or paper) were classified as having indoor air pollution [34].

Statistical analysis

We employed a two-stage analytic approach to examine the association between CS and ARI in children under five across 47 LMICs. In the first stage, country-specific datasets were weighted to ensure national representativeness and account for the complex survey design, including non-response, clustering, and stratification. Weighted analyses were then used to estimate unadjusted and adjusted prevalence ratios (PRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the association between CS and ARI in each country using modified Poisson regression models (generalised linear models (GLM) with a log link, Poisson distribution, and robust standard errors [35].

Adjusted models included a consistent set of potential confounders across countries. However, maternal smoking was unavailable in Chad and Bangladesh, indoor air pollution data were unavailable in Bangladesh and Zambia, and child size at birth was unavailable in the Maldives; therefore, these variables were excluded from the corresponding country-specific adjusted analyses. As missing data for variables included in the analyses were minimal (< 5%), complete-case analysis was performed.

In the second stage, a meta-analysis was performed to estimate the pooled prevalence ratio of ARI associated with caesarean birth across 47 LMICs. Country-specific adjusted prevalence ratios (aPRs) were pooled using a random-effects model to account for within-country sampling error and between-country heterogeneity in effect estimates. An inverse-variance weighting method was used to calculate the pooled aPR and corresponding 95% confidence interval (CI). The I² statistic was used to quantify heterogeneity across countries, and τ² was reported as an estimate of between-country variance [36]. Subgroup analyses were also performed to examine potential variations in the association between CS and ARI according to national income categories. All analyses were conducted using Stata version 17 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA).

Subgroup analyses

Subgroup analyses were conducted to assess whether the association between CS and ARI varied according to country income level and national CS rate. The pooled aPRs showed that children born by CS had a 10% higher prevalence of ARI in low-income countries (pooled aPR 1.11, 95% CI 0.96–1.29), a 13% higher prevalence in lower-middle-income countries (pooled aPR 1.13, 95% CI 1.00–1.28), and a 5% higher prevalence in upper-middle-income countries (pooled aPR 1.05, 95% CI 0.73–1.50) (Fig. 4).

Subgroup analyses of the association between caesarean section and acute respiratory infection in LMICs stratified by income level (2015–2024)

In countries with CS rates below 10%, CS was associated with a 17% higher prevalence of ARI (aPR 1.17, 95% CI 0.99–1.38). Corresponding estimates for countries with CS rates of 10–15% (aPR 1.22, 95% CI 0.92–1.62) and greater than 15% (aPR 1.07, 95% CI 0.97–1.19) were comparatively weaker or less precise (Fig. 5).

Discussion

Our multicountry analysis examined the association between mode of delivery and ARI among singleton live births across LMICs. We observed substantial variation in caesarean section (CS) rates, ranging from 1.3% in Chad to 44.9% in Bangladesh. Notably, 44 of the 47 countries had CS rates outside the range considered optimal (10–15% of births) by organisations such as the WHO, highlighting the dual challenges of underuse and overuse of CS and the broader inequities in access to and utilisation of obstetric care [37].

CS rates below 10% likely reflect substantial unmet need for emergency obstetric care due to shortages of skilled providers, inadequate infrastructure, and persistent financial and geographic barriers. Conversely, rates exceeding 15% may indicate over-medicalisation, often driven by sociocultural preferences, provider incentives, and the rapid expansion of private-sector maternity care [38]. These contrasting patterns illustrate the “caesarean paradox”, whereby some women lack access to medically necessary CS while others undergo unnecessary procedures.

Addressing this imbalance requires context-specific and evidence-informed strategies that ensure equitable access to medically necessary CS while avoiding unnecessary interventions. Strengthening health system capacity, ensuring the implementation and audit of evidence-based clinical guidelines, and addressing provider- and patient-level drivers of non-medically indicated CS are critical to reducing these disparities [28, 39].

For example, the very low CS rate observed in Chad likely reflects a fragile health system characterised by low-skilled birth attendance and limited access to emergency obstetric interventions [40]. On the other hand, the high CS rate in Bangladesh is consistent with broader trends across South Asia, where increasing institutional deliveries have coincided with increases in CS, particularly in private facilities [32]. Beyond systemic and economic factors, variation in CS use across LMICs is shaped by a complex interplay of maternal preferences, including fear of labour pain and social influences, provider incentives, prevailing clinical norms, and health system capacity, rather than direct regulatory control of clinical decision-making [31, 41].

We also observed wide variation in ARI prevalence, from less than 1% in Mozambique, Côte d’Ivoire, the Philippines, and the Maldives to 14% in Pakistan. Similar variability in ARI rates has been reported in a 2024 study [16] that pooled data from 25 countries in SSA, with rates between 1.0% and 9.8%. However, higher rates of respiratory infection in children under five were documented in a scoping review of 43 studies in Africa (1.9% to 60.2%) [17] and another study that utilised a population-based data of 28 countries in SSA (7% to 40%) [18]. These differences likely reflect variations in socio-economic conditions, environmental exposures, geographic disadvantages, and access to preventive and curative child health services [16, 20, 42, 43].

Our meta-analytic findings revealed that compared with vaginal birth, caesarean delivery was associated with an 11% higher prevalence of ARI among children under five years of age. This finding corroborates the existing evidence linking CS, particularly elective pre-labour procedures, to increased neonatal respiratory morbidity [5–7, 21, 22]. Possible mechanisms include altered neonatal lung transition [9] and reduced exposure to maternal vaginal microbiota important for immune development [10]. These mechanisms provide a plausible biological pathway linking mode of delivery to respiratory vulnerability in early life.

From a public health perspective, these findings highlight the importance of avoiding non-medically indicated caesarean sections while ensuring access to life-saving obstetric care. Although ARI prevalence associated with CS varied across income categories, subgroup analyses did not demonstrate clear differences in effect estimates between low-, lower-middle-, and upper-middle-income countries. The pooled estimates (10%, 13%, and 5% higher prevalence, respectively) showed substantial overlap in confidence intervals, suggesting limited precision and little evidence of effect modification by country income level. These findings should therefore be interpreted cautiously.

Although differences in the quality, coverage, and timeliness of maternal and child health services may plausibly influence this association, as suggested in previous studies [44–46], our findings do not provide definitive evidence that systemic inequities modulate the association between CS and ARI.

Strengths and limitations

This study utilised nationally representative DHS data from 47 LMICs collected using standardised methodologies, enhancing statistical power, generalisability, and comparability across countries. In addition, the analyses adjusted for a broad range of socioeconomic, demographic, obstetric, and environmental confounders, strengthening the robustness of the observed associations between CS and ARI.

Nevertheless, several limitations should be considered. First, residual confounding, including confounding by indication, remains possible. DHS datasets do not capture clinical indications for CS, such as fetal distress or maternal infection, which may themselves be associated with an increased risk of respiratory morbidity in children. Although we adjusted for several important confounders, unmeasured or incompletely measured factors may still have influenced the findings. Given the modest overall effect size (approximately 11%), part of the observed association may reflect residual confounding rather than a direct effect of CS. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted cautiously and should not be considered causal.

Second, ARI was assessed based on maternal recall of respiratory symptoms, including cough and short or difficult breathing, which may be subject to recall and reporting bias. Consequently, the prevalence of ARI may have been under- or overestimated, potentially influencing the effect estimates.

Future research implications

Future research should extend beyond early childhood respiratory outcomes to examine longer-term health trajectories associated with caesarean birth, including obesity, metabolic disorders, and immune-related conditions, particularly in LMIC settings where evidence remains limited. Longitudinal cohort studies and linked administrative data are needed to better characterise life-course effects and to disentangle the role of residual confounding, including confounding by indication. In addition, replication of similar analyses in other middle-income contexts and diverse health system settings would help assess the consistency and generalisability of these findings. Further research should also explore potential biological mechanisms, including alterations in neonatal microbiome development and immune function, as well as contextual factors such as access to postnatal care and infection prevention practices, to better understand pathways linking mode of delivery to child health outcomes.

Conclusions

Caesarean delivery was associated with a modestly higher prevalence of ARI among children under five years of age across diverse low- and middle-income settings. Although this finding is consistent with previous evidence, it should be interpreted cautiously given the potential for residual confounding, including confounding by indication. These findings highlight the importance of avoiding non-medically indicated caesarean sections and strengthening maternal counselling regarding potential long-term child health implications. Promoting physiological birth, where clinically appropriate, may contribute to reducing preventable childhood morbidity in resource-limited settings.

Data availability

The datasets analysed in this study are publicly available through the Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS) Program repository (https://dhsprogram.com/data/available-datasets.cfm). Access to these data is subject to controlled access due to ethical and confidentiality considerations, as they contain de-identified information from human participants. Researchers can obtain access by registering with the DHS Program and submitting a brief data request outlining the intended use. Access is granted upon approval of the request, typically within a few days. All users are required to adhere to the DHS data use agreement, which prohibits any attempt to identify survey participants and restricts redistribution of the data to third parties. The datasets were not generated or owned by the authors, and no special access privileges were granted. The authors accessed the data through the standard application process available to all researchers. No additional restrictions on data use apply beyond those specified by the DHS Program.
