Circulating circRNAs at the frontier of cancer detection, prognosis, and therapy
Authors: Yue Zhang, Guanxuan Chen, Zhaoyun Liu, Xianrang Song, Xingguo Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCircular RNAs (circRNAs) are distinguished from other noncoding RNAs by their unique loop structure, without 5′ polar ends and 3′ polyadenylated tails. They are stable and abundant in a wide range of eukaryotic taxa and exert functions via interactions with microRNAs (miRNAs), proteins, and DNA, including tumor-suppressive or oncogenic functions. Emerging evidence suggests that circRNAs show specific expression patterns in tumor tissues or circulation and changes in expression with respect to tumor stage; therefore, they possess strong potential as diagnostic, prognostic, and predictive biomarkers. Nevertheless, translating theoretical achievements in circRNA research to clinical practice is challenging. Herein, this review focuses on the important functions of circRNAs, including their roles in cancer, highlighting their utility for early cancer detection, prognosis, and treatment, in addition to a summary of vital detection methods for circulating circRNAs.

Introduction

Circular RNAs (circRNAs), a class of endogenous noncoding RNAs generated by backsplicing to form a covalently closed loop, are predominantly located in the cytoplasm and exosomes of many eukaryotes.[1–3] First identified approximately four decades ago,[4] circRNAs have garnered increasing attention in recent years due to their diverse regulatory roles in gene expression.[5] Mechanistically, circRNAs regulate gene expression directly or indirectly through interactions with macromolecules, including RNA, RNA-binding proteins (RBPs), or DNA, to exert various functions. It is believed that circRNAs are closely related to tumorigenesis and progression; abnormal expression levels have been observed in multiple cancers.

CircRNAs are generated from linear precursor messenger RNAs (pre-mRNAs) in a nonclassical splicing way; they show no 5′ end cap structure or 3′ poly(A) tail structure,[6] providing a longer half-life and greater resistance to ribonuclease R (RNase R) than those of linear RNAs.[7] Thereby, circRNAs are stable and maintain high sequence conservation in mammals, making them candidate diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets in cancer. In this review, we focus on the important functions of circRNAs, including their roles in cancer, demonstrating that circulating circRNAs are at the frontier of early detection, prognosis, and cancer treatment. We expect this review to contribute to circRNA-targeted translational research and clinical applications.

Mechanism: From Sponges to Systems

Since the development of high-throughput sequencing technology, several circRNA subtypes have been identified, including exonic circRNAs (ecircRNAs),[8,9] circular intronic RNAs (ciRNAs),[10,11] exonic-intronic circRNAs (EIciRNAs),[12] and transfer RNA (tRNA) intronic circRNAs (tricRNAs).[13] EcircRNAs are generated by ligation of downstream 5′ donor sites to upstream 3′ acceptor sites after intron removal, while EIciRNAs retain introns in their lariat-like structures. CiRNAs, containing only introns, rely on 7-nt GU-rich and 11-nt C-rich motifs for biogenesis,[14] and tricRNAs are derived from pre-tRNA splicing via 3′-5′ phosphodiester bonds[13] [Figure 1]. The circularization process is facilitated by canonical splice signals,[15] inverted intronic repeats such as Alu elements,[9] nonrepetitive complementary sequences,[16] and dimerization of RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) such as Quaking (QKI),[17] heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoprotein L (HNRNPL),[18] and splicing factor proline-rich and glutamine-rich (SFPQ).[19] Subcellular localization varies by subtype: ecircRNAs are cytoplasmic, ciRNAs and EIciRNAs are nuclear,[12] and cytoplasmic circRNAs can be packaged in extracellular vesicles (EVs) or degraded by endoribonucleases.[20] This structural and subcellular diversity lays the foundation for their multifaceted mechanisms in cancer, which have evolved from simple molecular interactions to complex regulatory systems. The mechanisms by which circRNAs influence cancer development and progression mainly depend on their interactions with other functional biomolecules, including RNA, protein, and DNA [Figure 2].

Core interactions: miRNA sponging and protein binding

The most well-established mechanism of action of circRNAs is the ability to act as competing endogenous RNAs (ceRNAs), especially miRNA sponges.[21] Cytoplasmic circRNAs contain miRNA response elements and competitively bind to miRNAs to suppress interactions with downstream target genes. A typical example is circular RNA sponge for miR-7 (ciRS-7), which is abundant in a variety of tissues, especially brain tissue; it contains >60 binding sites for miR-7 and thereby increases the levels of miR-7 target mRNAs, which are associated with tumor progression.[22] However, the function of ciRS-7 as a ceRNA has been challenged, spatial expression analyses revealed that the ceRNA network involving ciRS-7 and miR-7 depends on whether they are co-expressed or mutually exclusive, rather than on miR-7 binding sites within the circRNAs, highlighting the context dependency of this core interaction.[23]

Beyond miRNA sponging, circRNAs engage with various RBPs, including those involved in their own biogenesis, such as argonaute 2 (AGO2).[24] Through these interactions, circRNAs can act as protein decoys, scaffolds, or recruitment platforms, thereby expanding their core regulatory roles. For instance, circCcnb1 interacts with cyclin B1 (Ccnb1) and cyclin dependent kinase 1 (CDK1) to form large complexes, which impair the intrinsic activity of Ccnb1 in promoting cell migration, invasion, proliferation, and survival.[25] Another interesting example is circIPP2A2, which serves as a molecular scaffold in modulating the hornerin/phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K)/protein kinase B (AKT)/glycogen synthase kinase 3 beta (GSK3β) axis and promotes malignant behaviors in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC).[26] Notably, circRNAs exhibit a lower RBP binding density compared to linear RNAs,[27] and individual RBPs can interact with circRNAs to form circRNA-protein complex (circRNP) families.[28] Therefore, more detailed investigations into these core circRNA–protein interactions are essential to fully unravel their regulatory potential.

Functional expansion: from translation to nuclear regulation

Most circRNAs do not encode a protein owing to the lack of a 5′ cap and 3′ tail structure.[28,29] However, a subset of circRNAs contain internal ribosome entry sites (IRES) and open reading frames (ORFs), indicating their potential to translate peptides or proteins in a cap-independent way.[30–32] A recent study uncovered a novel mechanism related to immune evasion in melanoma, where a circPIAS1-encoded oncogenic protein inhibitor of activated STAT 1 (PIAS1) variant significantly hinders immunogenic ferroptosis triggered by immune checkpoint blockade therapy.[33] In addition to IRES-driven mechanisms, certain circRNAs can undergo m6A modification to facilitate protein synthesis.[34,35] Many translatable circRNAs possess m6A sites, with the ability to interact with eukaryotic translation initiation factor 4 gamma 2 (eIF4G2) and m6A reader YTH N6-methyladenosine RNA binding protein F3 (YTHDF3) to drive translation initiation,[36] implicating circRNAs in epigenetic regulation. For example, one such circRNA recruits lysine demethylase 4B (KDM4B) to demethylate histone H3K9me3, which accelerates the progression of triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC).[37]

Parallel to the expansion of cytoplasmic functions, nuclear ciRNAs and EIciRNAs exert direct regulatory effects at the transcriptional level, moving beyond cytoplasmic interactions to modulate gene expression at its source. EIciRNAs interact with RNA polymerase II and U1 small nuclear ribonucleoproteins (snRNP), thereby promoting eukaryotic translation initiation factor 3 subunit J (EIF3J) and polyadenylate-binding protein-interacting protein 2 (PAIP2) transcription in a cis-acting manner.[12] Furthermore, circRNAs assembled in nuclei can bind to DNA to form circRNA: DNA hybrids (circR–loops) to regulate transcription and alternative splicing.[38] For example, circMLL, a partner molecule of the mixed lineage leukemia (MLL) recombinome, can bind to DNA and form circR loops. These structures promote transcriptional pausing and inhibit the proteasome, ultimately leading to mutations and chromosomal rearrangements that generate common oncogenic MLL gene fusions.[39]

Systems integration: as pivotal nodes in oncogenic networks

The core interactions and novel functional dimensions of circRNAs do not operate in isolation; instead, they integrate into interconnected regulatory systems that span subcellular compartments and drive key cancer hallmarks, solidifying circRNAs as central nodes in cancer progression. Nuclear circRNAs (ciRNAs, EIciRNAs) regulate transcription and epigenetic modifications, while cytoplasmic circRNAs (ecircRNAs) act as miRNA sponges, interact with proteins, or undergo translation, and EV-packaged circRNAs bridge intracellular and extracellular signaling to remodel the tumor microenvironment (TME),[20] creating a cross-compartmental regulatory network. For example, EV-derived circMYBL1 induces CD44 expression in adenoid cystic carcinoma cells and pulmonary endothelial cells, promoting lung metastasis by mediating intercellular communication,[40] illustrating how circRNA-mediated regulation extends beyond individual cells to shape the TME.

This systems integration is further reflected in the involvement of circRNAs in all stages of cancer progression, from initiation to progression. In cancer initiation, endogenous circRNAs inhibit double-stranded RNA-activated protein kinase (PKR) related to innate immunity to evade immune destruction,[41] and circMYO1C enhances programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) mRNA stability by cooperating with insulin like growth factor 2 mRNA binding protein 2 (IGF2BP2), accelerating immune escape in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC).[42] During proliferation and progression, circRNAs drive tumor growth through metabolic reprogramming, such as circular RNA enolase 1 (circRNA-ENO1) promoting glycolysis in lung adenocarcinoma,[43] and sustain cancer stemness, as seen with hsa_circ_002178 enhancing cancer stem-like cell properties in breast cancer via regulating the miR-1258/lysine-specific demethylase 7A (KDM7A) axis.[44]

Pathogenesis: Bridging Cancer Driver and Biomarker

There is accumulating evidence for the abnormal expression of circRNAs in a wide range of cancer types, including lung cancer (LC),[45,46] breast cancer (BC),[47] HCC,[48] gastric cancer (GC),[49] colorectal cancer (CRC),[50] and ovarian cancer (OC).[51] Dysregulated circRNAs play tumor-suppressive or oncogenic roles during the full lifespan from tumor initiation to proliferation and progression and contribute to therapy resistance and deeply participate in key molecular and cellular processes underlying cancer, including tumor immunology, metabolic reprogramming, cancer stemness, and apoptosis.

CircRNAs in the cancer lifespan: from initiation to progression

Cancer comprises tumor cells and a multitude of noncancerous cells; therefore, it is not simply a genetic disease but rather a complex ecosystem.[52] Genetic alterations as well as reactions involving the TME, such as immune surveillance, contribute significantly to cancer initiation. In this context, circRNAs play diverse roles in the earliest stages of carcinogenesis. For instance, circRNA encompassing the E7 oncogene (circE7), encoded by high-risk human papillomavirus (HPV), promotes immune evasion in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma by binding to acetyl-CoA carboxylase 1 (ACC1), leading to epigenetic downregulation of galectin-9 and suppression of T cell cytotoxicity.[53] This HPV-encoded circRNA thereby facilitates oncogenesis by enabling immune evasion, a hallmark of virus-induced cancers. Additionally, circRNA derived from the maternally expressed gene 3 (circMEG3), downregulated in liver cancer stem cells, inhibits telomerase activity by reducing dyskerin pseudouridine synthase 1 (DKC1) expression, thereby shortening telomere lifespan and suppressing malignant differentiation.[54] Thus, loss of circMEG3 relieves this suppression of centromere-binding factor 5 (Cbf5), restoring telomerase activity and enabling the sustained proliferative capacity that drives tumorigenesis.

In cancer progression, circRNAs drive the hallmark of sustained proliferation and metastatic dissemination by regulating cancer stem cell (CSC) stemness, metabolic reprogramming, and intercellular communication. Metabolically, many circRNAs rewire cellular metabolism to support continuous proliferation, with the Warburg effect (aerobic glycolysis) being a key target.[55,56] For example, circRNA derived from TatD DNase domain containing 3 (circTATDN3) promotes the Warburg effect and proliferation in CRC.[57] CircRNAs also play a crucial role in the regulation of cancer stem cells, contributing to tumor proliferation and progression. CircRNA derived from cofilin 1 (CircCFL1), which acts as a scaffold to enhance the interaction between histone deacetylase 1 (HDAC1) and c-Myc, promotes the stemness of TNBC cells and, in turn, facilitates proliferation and metastasis.[58]

Beyond intracellular regulation, EV-derived circRNAs mediate intercellular communication to activate the hallmark of invasion and metastasis. For example, circMYBL1 enriched in small EVs enhances prometastatic phenotypes of adenoid cystic carcinoma cells and promotes the arrest of circulating adenoid cystic carcinoma cells and subsequently increase lung metastatic burden.[40] Additionally, circRNAs enable cancer cells to resist cell death by regulating cell death pathways. For example, circBIRC6[59] and circ_0025202[60] are involved in the regulation of cancer cell apoptosis, circCDYL[61] and circATG7[62] participate in autophagy, and circRNA_101093,[63] circSAV1,[64] and circRAPGEF5[65] regulate ferroptosis.

Clinical translation: from pathogenesis to biomarker potential

The intrinsic pathogenic mechanisms of circRNAs lay a solid foundation for their clinical translation, particularly as cancer biomarkers. CircRNAs possess inherent properties that make them ideal translational candidates: their circular structure confers resistance to RNase-mediated degradation,[66,67] enabling stable detection in various body fluids (peripheral blood, urine, cell-free saliva, feces) and facilitating non-invasive sampling. Moreover, circRNAs exhibit high cell-type and tissue specificity,[68–71] with differential expression between cancer tissues and adjacent non-cancerous/healthy tissues, and close associations with key clinical features such as tumor size, histological grade, and TNM (tumor, lymph node, metastasis) stage.[5] These characteristics allow them to reflect the pathological state of tumors, supporting their application in cancer diagnosis.

Notably, the pathogenic functions of circRNAs further enhance their translational value as prognostic and predictive biomarkers. Although functional involvement is not a strict prerequisite for effective biomarkers, circRNAs with disrupted expression in neoplastic cells, resulting from their participation in cancer hallmark regulation, often serve as reliable prognostic tools. Their dysregulated expression profiles can mirror the biological aggressiveness and proliferative capacity of tumors, even in the absence of direct functional contributions to malignant progression.[72] Integrating their intrinsic pathogenic roles with inherent stability and circulatory detectability, circRNAs bridge the gap between basic research on cancer mechanisms and clinical management, holding great promise as non-invasive, precise biomarkers for cancer diagnosis, prognosis, and treatment response prediction.

Circulating circRNAs: From Biomarker to Theranostics

The analysis of circRNAs in circulation holds significant promise for the early identification of malignancies, especially for cancer types that are frequently diagnosed at advanced stages due to the absence of early symptomatic manifestations. This approach leverages the stability and abundance of circRNAs in bodily fluids, offering a non-invasive yet highly sensitive method for liquid biopsy, and demonstrating considerable potential for both disease diagnosis and prognosis assessment.[73] Numerous studies have systematically validated the diagnostic, prognostic, and predictive utility of circRNAs across a spectrum of malignancies, but we discussed the most rigorously validated candidates and summarized them in Figure 3 and Tables 1–4.

Diagnostic and prognostic value across biofluids

The analysis of circRNAs in blood-based liquid biopsies holds significant promise for the non-invasive early identification and prognosis of cancers. Their stability and abundance in circulation enable highly sensitive detection. A landmark pan-cancer study characterized circRNA expression across 22 cancer subtypes, creating the valuable MiOncoCirc database.[74] For specific cancers, diagnostic panels show high accuracy, such as an 8-circRNA signature for GC (AUC: 0.87 in the training set, 0.83 in the validation set), effective even in early-stage disease.[75] Similarly, circ_0013958 demonstrates diagnostic potential (AUC: 0.815) in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) and correlates with TNM stage and lymph node metastasis.[76] CircRNA derived from epidermal growth factor receptor (circEGFR) is upregulated in plasma exosomes from BC patients, serving as a diagnostic biomarker with higher expression in TNBC, indicating subtyping value.[77,78]

Beyond diagnosis, circulating circRNAs offer robust prognostic insights. Individual circRNAs like ciRS-7 are abnormally expressed and correlate with clinical outcomes across multiple cancer.[22] High ciRS-7 expression is associated with poor prognosis in multiple myeloma (MM),[79] ovarian cancer,[80] renal cell carcinoma,[81] colon cancer,[82] and lung cancer.[83] Others, such as circFGFR1 and circRNA_100876 in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), are associated with shorter survival,[84,85] while circ_0003195 and circ_0000419 in GC correlate with better overall survival.[86,87] CircRNA derived from chromodomain Y-like gene (circCDYL) promotes BC progression, and its elevated levels in serum are linked to higher tumor burden, shorter survival, and poorer therapy response.[61] Multi-circRNA panels enhance prognostic robustness by capturing tumor complexity. For instance, a global circRNA dysregulation index (CRI) in HCC is associated with worse prognosis and impaired CD8+ T cell infiltration.[88] Furthermore, specific circRNAs can predict metastasis; elevated plasma circ_0115744 effectively differentiates CRC with liver metastasis (AUC: 0.863).[89]

Notably, circRNA biomarkers are not confined to plasma or serum; they can be detected in various blood components and other biofluids, expanding the scope of liquid biopsy. Platelet-derived circNRIP1 (circRNA derived from nuclear receptor interacting protein 1) was downregulated in NSCLC patients.[90] Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), in particular, is valuable for central nervous system tumors due to its direct contact with brain tissues, with CSF circRNAs serving as prognostic and monitoring tools for brain metastases and medulloblastoma.[91,92] Tissue-specific biofluids like gastric juice, saliva, and urine further complement blood-based detection, providing accessible, cancer-specific biomarkers to improve clinical evaluation comprehensiveness.

Challenges for circRNA biomarkers: from bench to bedside

Despite the promise of circulating circRNAs as biomarkers, their clinical translation faces key challenges. An ideal cancer biomarker must meet two essential criteria: specificity and feasibility. Specificity entails the precise distinction of the target cancer from healthy tissues, benign conditions, and other malignancies to minimize false positives. Feasibility requires that biomarkers can be reliably measured in readily accessible biofluids using standardized, cost-effective, and scalable assays suitable for routine clinical use.

Although circRNAs exhibit cell-type-specific expression, the expression of ideal candidate circRNA biomarkers should be restricted to the tumor itself.[38] First, the mechanisms underlying the roles of circRNAs in diverse cancers have been elucidated. For example, circRNA-mediated miRNA sponges, driven by complementary base pairing,[93] have been detected across various human tumors. Second, not all cancer types can generate sufficiently high levels of circulating circRNAs[94]; their detection and quantification can be technically challenging. Standardized methods for circRNA analysis are still evolving, which further complicates their validation and clinical application. Third, no circRNA-based biomarkers have been successfully integrated into clinical practice. This is largely because most research in this field remains focused on discovery, with limited efforts dedicated to validation. Particularly, both internal and external validation cohorts are essential, and they must be adequately powered to confirm the generalizability of a prognostic or predictive model. These cohorts should be prospectively collected, sufficiently large, uniform, and well-characterized, with comprehensive and reliable clinical data to ensure the robustness and applicability of the findings. As observed in complex diseases like idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), integrating multiple biomarker types is crucial for improving diagnostic accuracy and prognostic power, highlighting the need for multi-omics panels that combine circRNAs with other circulating analytes.[95]

In summary, while circRNAs hold promises as biomarkers, their transition from discovery to clinical application requires more rigorous validation, prospective studies, and clinical trials.

Beyond diagnosis: circRNA-based therapeutics

Circulating circRNAs have emerged as a central class of molecules bridging cancer diagnosis, dynamic treatment monitoring, and novel therapeutic strategies. Their ability to reflect molecular pathways and tumor dynamics allows for the identification of patients likely to benefit from specific therapies. For instance, serum hsa_circ_0007507 increases upon postoperative recurrence in GC,[96] while rising levels of serum/exosomal circ_0041150 correlate with acquired chemotherapy resistance and disease progression in small cell lung cancer (SCLC).[97] In targeted therapy settings, circRNA derived from mesenchymal epithelial transition factor receptor (circMET) tracks mesenchymal epithelial transition factor receptor (MET)-driven tumor activity and clonal evolution during treatment,[98] and fusion circular RNA from echinoderm microtubule associated protein like 4-anaplastic lymphoma kinase fusion gene (EML4-ALK) (F-circEA) serves as a specific liquid biopsy marker for the EML4–ALK fusion in NSCLC.[99] In immunotherapy, exosomal circRNA derived from cell division cycle and apoptosis regulator 1 (circCCAR1) from HCC enters CD8+ T cells, stabilizes programmed cell death protein 1 (PD‑1) to drive anti‑PD1 resistance, and thus may serve as a biomarker for monitoring treatment efficacy.[100] Moreover, circulating circRNAs may predict treatment‑related toxicities, as demonstrated by circRNA derived from insulin receptor (circINSR) and circRNA derived from forkhead box O3 (circFOXO3), which are involved in doxorubicin‑ and radiation‑induced cardiotoxicity, respectively.[101,102]

Certain circulating circRNAs are not only biomarkers but also functional drivers of therapy resistance, positioning them as direct therapeutic targets. Neutralizing these pathogenic circRNAs using antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) or small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) has demonstrated substantial potential in restoring treatment sensitivity in preclinical models, as exemplified by the reversal of endocrine therapy resistance in estrogen receptor-positive breast cancer via ASO-mediated targeting of circRNA derived from forkhead box K2 (circFOXK2).[103] Beyond endogenous roles, engineered synthetic circRNAs represent an emerging therapeutic platform with high stability and low immunogenicity. These constructs can be optimized and delivered via lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) or viral vectors to function as miRNA sponges, protein decoys, or therapeutic peptide expression templates.[104] Thus, the translational path of circRNAs is shifting from monitoring endogenous species to deploying synthetic versions as programmable therapeutics. Recent breakthroughs, such as circRNA-based vaccines, highlight this potential,[104] while key challenges remain in tissue-specific delivery and off-target effects.[105]

Analytical pipeline: from purification to clinical-grade detection

The analytical pipeline for circulating circRNAs involves purification, sequencing, and targeted detection. After total RNA extraction from peripheral blood, the isolation of highly purified circRNAs is a key challenge. Three main strategies are employed: depletion of ribosomal RNA (rRNA), digestion with RNase R, and poly(A) tailing.[106] RNase R digestion is a standard approach to enrich circRNAs by degrading linear RNA, but RNAs with highly structured 3′ ends or G-quadruplexes exhibit natural resistance to RNase R.[9,108,109] Improved protocols include poly(A) tailing before or after RNase R digestion and buffer modification to overcome these limitations (for example, Li+ instead of K+).[110–112]

CircRNA-sequencing (CircRNA-seq) is the preferred method to discover and sequence novel circRNAs. The development of RNA sequencing technology accelerated research in this area, allowing the simultaneous detection of multiple samples and circRNAs in a high-throughput manner,[74,113] whose greatest advantage is the ability to explore novel types of circRNAs to improve the circRNA library, thus providing a basis for subsequent research.[114] However, the sequencing depth is generally insufficient to capture some circRNA fragments with low abundance in vivo, which results in missed detection and reduces sequencing sensitivity. For this reason, it is necessary to remove rRNAs from the sample and digest linear RNAs to improve circRNA enrichment during the construction of the sequencing library. Library preparation is the key step affecting the sequencing results. Unlike linear RNAs, circRNAs lack poly(A) tails and are thus excluded during standard poly(A) selection, leading to their underrepresentation in conventional RNA-seq libraries. Specialized methods, including rRNA depletion and RNase R treatment, are therefore required to enrich for circular transcripts.[115] After the library is built and the reads are sequenced, circRNA identification, distribution statistics, annotation, and expression analyses can be carried out to explore functions and mechanisms. Besides, improved methods such as Nanopore sequencing, which discriminates nucleic acids via nanopore-derived electrical signals[10,116,117] and induro-RT mediated circRNA-sequencing (IMCR-seq), which employs group II intron reverse transcriptase for rolling circle amplification, have also been developed.[118]

Targeted circRNA detection methods include polymerase chain reaction (PCR)-based and probe-based approaches. The only distinction between circRNAs and their cognate linear RNAs lies in the back-splicing junction (BSJ) region, which thus serves as the target for designing specific primers.[119] Reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) is the most widely used method owing to its low cost and simplicity, whereas droplet digital PCR (ddPCR) achieves higher accuracy and sensitivity by mitigating interference from rolling circle reverse transcription products, an advantage derived from the Poisson distribution principle underlying its algorithm.[120,121] Probe-based methods include microarrays for high-throughput profiling of multiple circRNAs and electrochemical biosensors, which provide high sensitivity, simplicity, and cost-effectiveness for clinical-grade detection.[122] The thiolated DNA probe immobilized onto the electrode can capture the target circRNA, which can bind to another biotinylated probe; then, a streptavidin-coupled nanoparticle is attached and approaches the electrode surface to achieve voltammetric quantification due to the circular structure of circRNA,[123] paving the way for convenient, automated circRNA assays.

Translational bottlenecks: standardization and validation challenges

Despite advancements in detection technologies, several translational bottlenecks persist. Standardization of sample collection, processing, and storage is lacking, leading to variability between studies. For example, different methods for RNA extraction and circRNA purification can affect yield and purity, and pre-analytical factors such as blood collection tubes, processing time, and storage conditions may impact circRNA stability. Additionally, there is no consensus on standard operating procedures (SOPs) for circRNA detection, including RNase R digestion conditions, library construction protocols, and data analysis pipelines, hindering interlaboratory comparisons.

Validation challenges include the need for large, well-characterized cohorts to confirm the diagnostic, prognostic, and predictive value of circRNAs. Many studies rely on small sample sizes or retrospective cohorts, lacking prospective validation to demonstrate clinical utility. Furthermore, the specificity of circRNA biomarkers needs to be rigorously tested against other diseases and cancer types to avoid false positives. Technical limitations such as cross-reactivity with linear RNAs, low-abundance circRNA detection, and high background noise in clinical samples also pose challenges for translation.

Looking forward, the field must focus on integrated strategies that enhance sensitivity, standardization, and clinical utility. The development of highly sensitive detection platforms, such as clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR)/CRISPR-associated protein 13a (Cas13a) systems and advanced sequencing methods like crosslinking poly(A) pulldown RNase R sequencing, will improve both detection limits and functional insights.[124,125] Combining complementary technologies, for example, integrating circRNA-seq with ddPCR or electrochemical biosensors, can streamline the discovery-to-validation pipeline. Concurrently, establishing comprehensive SOPs for the entire analytical process and creating reference materials are paramount for ensuring reproducibility. Embedding circRNAs into multi-omics liquid biopsy panels that also include miRNAs, circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA), and proteins promises to enhance diagnostic accuracy beyond single-marker approaches. Parallel advancements in microfluidics and point-of-care devices, such as droplet-digital CRISPR platforms, pave the way for rapid, decentralized testing. Finally, leveraging artificial intelligence and machine learning for data analysis and predictive modeling will be crucial in translating circRNA research into clinically actionable tools.

Perspectives: Towards a circRNA‑Centric Oncology

Circulating circRNAs represent a transformative class of molecules in oncology, bridging tumor biology and clinical practice through their unique structural stability, specific expression patterns, and diverse functional mechanisms. From canonical miRNA sponges to integrative regulatory hubs involving protein interactions, translation, and nuclear governance, circRNAs act as pivotal nodes in oncogenic networks, orchestrating tumor initiation, progression, and therapy resistance. Their presence in various biofluids enables non-invasive liquid biopsy, offering significant potential as diagnostic, prognostic, and predictive biomarkers across a wide range of cancers.

The strategic value of circulating circRNAs lies in their dual role as both biomarkers and therapeutic targets. As biomarkers, they provide real-time insights into tumor biology and clinical outcomes, overcoming the limitations of traditional tissue biopsies. As therapeutic targets, they offer opportunities to disrupt oncogenic pathways and reverse therapy resistance, paving the way for personalized medicine. Despite current challenges, the integration of circRNA research into clinical oncology holds great promise for improving cancer detection, treatment efficacy, and patient survival.

The future vision of circRNA research in oncology is to close the theranostic loop, integrating circRNA-based monitoring with targeted therapy to optimize patient care. This requires addressing current translational challenges through rigorous clinical trials, standardization of detection methods, and validation of circRNA biomarkers in large, diverse cohorts. Additionally, further research is needed to elucidate the functional mechanisms of circRNAs beyond miRNA sponging, focusing on their interactions with proteins, DNA, and other RNAs, as well as their roles in epigenetic regulation and translation.

In conclusion, circulating circRNAs are at the frontier of early cancer detection, prognosis, and treatment. With continued research and technological advancements, we are moving toward a circRNA-centric era in oncology, where circRNAs will play a central role in transforming cancer care from reactive treatment to proactive monitoring and personalized therapy.
