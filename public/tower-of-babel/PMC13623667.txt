Cardiac timing modulates the contribution of compliance to effective arterial elastance

Thomas Murchie

Abstract

Effective arterial elastance (E
a) is a lumped descriptor of arterial load. The contributions of arterial characteristics to E
a, and their interactions with cardiac timing parameters, have not been fully elaborated. Previous studies have shown that E
a can be approximated by equations of the form E
a ≈ R/T + k/C, where R is either total or peripheral resistance, T is cardiac cycle time, and C is arterial compliance, but reported values for the coefficient k vary substantially, and some analyses suggest that compliance contributes little to E
a. Here, an analytical approximation is derived from Sunagawa's Windkessel expression for E
a by replacing the exponential term with a truncated Taylor expansion. The resulting approximation predicts that k is not constant but varies with cardiac timing, such that k ≈ 0.5 (t
d/T)2, where t
d is diastolic time. Approximation accuracy was assessed across 693 combinations of resistance, compliance, and heart rate and in a virtual population of 4374 adults derived from a published one‐dimensional vascular model. The linear approximation for E
a closely tracked the Windkessel expression across the full parameter space (R
2 > 0.9999, mean absolute percentage error 0.51%). In the virtual population, the approximation showed good agreement with the E surrogate P
ES/SV (end‐systolic pressure/stroke volume), surpassing previous approximations. These findings show that the contribution of compliance to E
a is modulated by cardiac timing and provide a mechanistic explanation for previously reported values of k.

INTRODUCTION

Effective arterial elastance (E
a) is a lumped descriptor of arterial load in the pressure‐volume domain. The respective contributions of arterial resistance and compliance characteristics to E
a, and their interactions with cardiac timing parameters, have not been fully elaborated. Previous analyses of the contribution of compliance to E
a have produced divergent results.

In a three‐element Windkessel model of the arterial system, E
a is determined by total systemic resistance (R
T), which is the sum of peripheral resistance (R
p) and characteristic impedance (Z
c), along with arterial compliance (C) through the time constant τ = R
p
C, and the durations of systole (t
s) and diastole (t
d), as described by Sunagawa et al. ():
(1)
Ea=RTts+τ1−e−td/τ
A widely recognized surrogate for E
a is given by the ratio of end‐systolic pressure to stroke volume (P
ES/SV). This ratio and its Windkessel counterpart show good agreement in vivo (Kelly et al., ), but both obscure the respective influences of the underlying arterial resistance and compliance characteristics on E
a.

Previous modeling and experimental studies, however, have identified linear approximations of the form:
(2)
Ea=RT+kC
where the contributions of the resistance term R (previously represented by R
p or R
T), and the compliance term C, are described more explicitly. While cardiac cycle time (T) has a clear physiological meaning, k has functioned as a fitted coefficient and empirically derived values for k of 0.314 (Segers et al., ) and 0.42 (Chemla et al., ) have been found. Meanwhile, it has been claimed that E
a is minimally affected by C, that is, that k ≈ 0 (Chirinos et al., ; Li & Atlas, ). The physiological determinants of k have not been described analytically.

Here, we show that k is not constant but scales with the duration of diastole as a proportion of the cardiac cycle (t
d/T). The resulting linear approximation of E
a, following the form of Equation (), is found to closely track the parent Windkessel equation (Equation ) across a wide range of inputs and to correlate well with the surrogate P
ES/SV in a 1‐dimensional vascular model. This analysis clarifies how arterial compliance and cardiac timing contribute to E
a, and offers an explanation for the divergent empirical estimates of k.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

A linear approximation for E
a (Equation ) was derived analytically from the three‐element Windkessel expression for E
a reported by Sunagawa (Equation ) by replacing the exponential term with a polynomial approximation, which gives rise to an approximation for k (Equation ).

The linear approximation for E
a was assessed against the parent Windkessel equation and against the E
a surrogate P
ES/SV. These comparisons were used to assess whether the assumptions and truncations introduced during the derivation were reasonable across a relevant range of inputs. Statistical analysis was performed using R statistical software version 4.5.2 (R Project for Statistical Computing).

Comparison with the parent equation

To map the agreement between the linear approximation of E
a and the parent Windkessel equation, a set of 693 evenly distributed combinations of R
T, C, and HR values representing the adult left ventricle and systemic circulation was generated, allowing paired values of E
a to be compared across this broad parameter space and revealing the sensitivity of error in the approximation to each input variable.

HR values (60, 80 and 100 bpm) were chosen to reflect the adult range in which E
a might be assessed. The definition of t
s as ventricular ejection time and t
d as T − t
s were retained from the Windkessel derivation of E
a (Sunagawa et al., ) and used for both the Windkessel parent equation and the linear approximation in all analyses. While the assumption τ ≈ R
T
C was used to derive the linear approximation, the convention τ = R
p
C was used for the parent equation. Given that ejection time varies with HR, input values for t
s were adjusted according to the relationship observed during exercise (t
s (ms) = HR (bpm) × −1.15 + 379) (Mertens et al., ).

R
T values of 0.5–1.75 mmHg s/mL at intervals of 0.125 mmHg s/mL were chosen to encompass the ranges observed in adults in health (Cattermole et al., ) and during treatment for septic shock (Monge García et al., ) and cardiogenic shock (Mathew et al., ). R
p was modeled as a fixed fraction of total resistance, using R
p = 0.94 R
T, based on the observation that Z
c is approximately 5%–7% of peripheral resistance (Westerhof et al., ). C values of 0.5–2.5 mL/mmHg at intervals of 0.1 mL/mmHg were chosen to encompass the range found by invasive measurement in normotensive and hypertensive adults (Randall et al., ). All reference cohorts included male and female subjects.

Comparison with P
ES/SV

To evaluate agreement between the linear approximation of E
a and the E
a surrogate P
ES/SV, both quantities were calculated for 4374 simulated adults. This virtual population was generated by Charlton et al., using a one‐dimensional vascular model, described in detail by its authors (Charlton et al., ). The model inputs were specified to reproduce age‐appropriate distributions of cardiovascular parameters, simulating a population age range of 25–75 years. Model inputs, arterial pressure and flow data and derived indices are presented in a publicly accessible database (Charlton et al., ).

The linear approximation of E
a was calculated using database‐supplied values for HR, where T (s) = 60/HR (bpm), LVET, R
T (reported in the source dataset as SVR), and C (reported there as C).

P
ES/SV was calculated using database‐supplied SV, with P
ES obtained from the aortic root pressure waveform. Since the supplied SV values represent net aortic root flow per cardiac cycle, allowing for a small volume of end‐systolic flow reversal (~0.73 mL), P
ES was sampled at the end of the reverse flow period, so that P
ES corresponded to the time at which SV was established. This time point was identified using the publicly available AorticFlowWave script used by Charlton et al. to generate the model inflow waveforms, which locates the end of reverse flow at 0.309/0.282 LVET.

Two previous approximations for E
a were similarly compared with P
ES/SV. These were E
a ≈ R
T/T and Segers et al.'s linear regression model (Segers et al., ) E
a ≈ 1.023 R
p/T + 0.314/C – 0.127. R
p was calculated for this analysis as R
T – Z
c, with characteristic impedance (Z
c) derived from database‐supplied values for aortic root diameter (d), aortic pulse wave velocity (PWV) and blood density (ρ) as Z
c = ρ PWV/[π(d/2)2].

DISCUSSION

The present analysis shows that the contribution of C to E
a is modulated by cardiac timing and that the coefficient k ≈ 0.5 (t
d/T)2 closely approximates this effect across the range of physiologically relevant parameters examined. Thus, the influence of C on E
a increases as the fraction of the cardiac cycle occupied by diastole increases.

Previous analyses (Chirinos et al., ; Li & Atlas, ) have suggested that compliance contributes little to effective arterial elastance because the Windkessel expression can be approximated as E
a ≈ R
T/T. This reduction follows from truncating the Taylor series expansion of the exponential expression after the linear term: τ(1 – e−td/τ
) ≈ τ(t
d/τ), which cancels τ, and therefore C, from the resulting expression. The present analysis, using a higher‐order approximation, gives a linear expression for E
a that takes the form previously identified empirically by Segers and Chemla. This approximation, which retains C and a leading‐order approximation for its coefficient k, showed substantially closer agreement with the parent Windkessel expression and with the E
a surrogate P
ES/SV across the range of inputs examined. Thus, analytical approximations that reduce E
a to R
T/T and empirical models of the form E
a ≈ R/T + k/C correspond with different orders of approximation.

Conceptually, the factor k/C can be understood as accounting for the portion of pressure‐volume load not already accounted for by R
T/T. Since E
a ≈ P
ES/SV and R
T/T ≈ MAP/SV, k/C ≈ (P
ES − MAP)/SV. Therefore, k approximates two separate timing‐dependent factors that relate C to this excess pressure per unit of SV: the fraction of SV stored in the arterial compartment at end‐systole, rather than lost to systolic peripheral runoff (dV/SV), which approaches zero as t
d/T approaches zero; and the fraction of the associated arterial compartment pressure change (dV/C) that lies above MAP. The latter depends on the temporal weighting of the arterial pressure decay, which diminishes as t
d/T approaches zero, causing MAP and P
ES to converge regardless of dV.

The effect of cardiac timing on the relationship between C and E
a is a factor that may be significant for empirical work. Since t
d/T is known to vary substantially with HR (Mertens et al., ), the relationship between C and E
a, observed across a population containing natural variation in HR, may not reflect the contribution of C to E
a for each individual unless the timing‐dependence of this contribution is taken into account. If t
d/T covaries systematically with C, the compliance‐dependent term k/C might vary minimally across a population, despite the physical dependence of E
a on C.

This analysis examines the interpretation of vascular Windkessel parameters in the range of the systemic circulation, but does not address uncertainty in their estimation, does not include parameters in the range of the pulmonary circulation, and does not examine the relationships between derived arterial load expressions and pressure‐flow relationships in vivo.
