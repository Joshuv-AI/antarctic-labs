Cannon–Thurston maps for Anosov foliations
Ellis Buckminster

Abstract
. Universal circles, introduced by Thurston and Calegari–Dunfield, are not well understood in general. Recently, the author together with Taylor showed that Anosov foliations with branching admit nonconjugate universal circles. We continue the study of these universal circles and show that for an Anosov foliation with branching on a hyperbolic manifold, the leftmost universal circle admits a Cannon–Thurston-type map to the ideal 2-sphere. This is a new type of construction of a Cannon–Thurston map. As a corollary, we show the fundamental group of the manifold acts on the leftmost universal circle with pseudo-Anosov dynamics.



1.
Introduction

Given a closed, fibered hyperbolic 3-manifold
M
, let
\Sigma
denote a fiber surface. The lift
\widetilde{\Sigma}
of
\Sigma
to the universal cover
\widetilde{M}
of
M
can be identified with the hyperbolic plane
\mathbb{H}^{2}
. Thus,
\widetilde{\Sigma}
can be compactified with its
ideal circle boundary

\partial_{\infty}\Sigma\cong\partial_{\infty}\mathbb{H}^{2}
. This circle admits an action of
\pi_{1}(M)
preserving a pair of laminations and is naturally identified with the ideal circle boundary of any other lifted fiber. The monodromy is a pseudo-Anosov homeomorphism on
\Sigma
, and the orbit space
\mathcal{O}
of the suspension flow of this map is naturally homeomorphic to
\widetilde{\Sigma}
. Furthermore, the ideal circle boundary
\partial\mathcal{O}
of
\mathcal{O}
is naturally homeomorphic to
\partial_{\infty}\widetilde{\Sigma}
. See
Figure

1
for an overview of how these structures fit together.

Figure 1.
How the different structures fit together in the fibered case.

Universal circles
, defined by Thurston
[
24
]
and studied further by Calegari–Dunfield
[
5
]
, are an attempt to generalize this picture to a broader class of actions of 3-manifold groups on circles coming from taut foliations. In general, there is much we do not understand about the various possible universal circles for a given foliation, or when two constructions of universal circles result in nonconjugate actions on circles.

Some rigidity results have been obtained by Calegari for
\mathbb{R}
-covered foliations
[
7
]
and for foliations with one-sided branching
[
8
]
, and more recently by Huang for depth-one foliations transverse to pseudo-Anosov flows without perfect fits
[
21
]
.

On the other hand, the author together with Taylor recently showed that for the weak stable or unstable foliations of non
\mathbb{R}
-covered Anosov flows, two constructions of universal circles, due to Calegari–Dunfield
[
5
]
and Fenley and Landry–Minsky–Taylor
[
15
,
22
]
, result in nonconjugate actions on circles
[
4
]
. Thus, this class of foliations seems like a good testing ground for various questions about universal circles.

Returning to the fibered case, Cannon–Thurston showed that the inclusion of
\widetilde{\Sigma}
into
\widetilde{M}
extends continuously to a
\pi_{1}(M)
-equivariant map
\partial_{\infty}\widetilde{\Sigma}\to S^{2}_{\infty}
, where
S^{2}_{\infty}
is the ideal 2-sphere boundary of
\widetilde{M}\cong\mathbb{H}^{3}

[
10
]
. As the action
\pi_{1}(M)\curvearrowright S^{2}_{\infty}
is minimal, the map
\partial_{\infty}\widetilde{\Sigma}\to S^{2}_{\infty}
is necessarily surjective. This map can be obtained by collapsing the
\pi_{1}(M)
-invariant prelaminations on
\partial_{\infty}\widetilde{\Sigma}
. More generally, Frankel showed that the ideal circle boundary of a
quasigeodesic flow
on a hyperbolic 3-manifold
M
admits a continuous,
\pi_{1}(M)
-equivariant map to the ideal 2-sphere boundary of
\widetilde{M}
, and the map produced by Cannon–Thurston is a special case of this result
[
18
]
.

Thus, it is natural to ask the following.

Question 1.1
.

When does a universal circle for a foliation on a closed, hyperbolic 3-manifold
M
admit a Cannon–Thurston-type map, i.e. a continuous,
\pi_{1}(M)
-equivariant map to
S^{2}_{\infty}
?

This is the question we address here, in the case of the
leftmost universal circle
for the weak unstable foliation of a non
\mathbb{R}
-covered Anosov flow.

Results

Throughout,
\mathcal{C}^{\ell}
denotes the leftmost universal circle of a non
\mathbb{R}
-covered Anosov foliation in a closed, hyperbolic 3-manifold
M
, and
S^{2}_{\infty}
is the 2-sphere boundary of
\widetilde{M}\cong\mathbb{H}^{3}
(see
Section

2
for definitions). We focus on non
\mathbb{R}
-covered Anosov flows because the case of
\mathbb{R}
-covered Anosov flows is essentially already known; see below. Our main result is the following, which says that the leftmost universal circle
\mathcal{C}^{\ell}
admits a Cannon–Thurston-type map.

Theorem A
.

There is a continuous, surjective,
\pi_{1}(M)
-equivariant map

\mathrm{CT}\colon\mathcal{C}^{\ell}\to S^{2}_{\infty}.

As the actions of
\pi_{1}(M)
on
\mathcal{C}^{\ell}
and
\partial\mathcal{O}
(the boundary of the orbit space for the Anosov flow) are nonconjugate by
[
4
, Theorem B]
, the map
\mathrm{CT}
is different from the Cannon–Thurston map
e\colon\partial\mathcal{O}\to S^{2}_{\infty}
due to Frankel
[
18
]
and Fenley
[
17
]
.

Forthcoming work of Fenley–Mann–Potrie
[
11
]
shows that any Cannon–Thurston map is uniformly finite-to-one. Combining this fact with the dynamics of
\pi_{1}(M)\curvearrowright S^{2}_{\infty}
, we obtain the following corollary.

Corollary B
.

Every element of
\pi_{1}(M)
has some power acting on
\mathcal{C}^{\ell}
with a positive, finite number of fixed points, which alternate between attractors and repellors.

In general, given a foliation with the continuous extension property, we could ask when the limit sets of the different leaves can be ‘stitched together’ into a single
\pi_{1}(M)
-equivariant sphere-filling curve. To make this precise, we make the following definition.

1.1
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a taut, coorientable foliation of a closed, hyperbolic 3-manifold
M
whose leaves have the continuous extension property. Let

i_{\lambda}\colon\partial_{\infty}\lambda\to S^{2}_{\infty}

denote the continuous extension of a leaf
\lambda\in\mathcal{L}
. A
Cannon–Thurston map
for
\mathcal{F}
is a minimal universal circle
(\mathcal{C},\{\pi_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in\mathcal{L}})
together with a continuous,
\pi_{1}(M)
-equivariant map

h\colon\mathcal{C}\to S^{2}_{\infty}

such that

h(s)=i_{\lambda}\circ\pi_{\lambda}(s)

whenever
s\in\mathrm{core}(\pi_{\lambda})
.

Note that Cannon–Thurston maps coming from fibered 3-manifolds satisfy this definition where
\mathcal{F}
is the foliation by fibers and
\mathcal{C}
is
\partial\mathcal{O}
, the boundary of the flow space for the suspension flow. More generally, Landry–Taylor show in forthcoming work
[
23
]
that for any foliation
\mathcal{F}
(almost) transverse to a quasigeodesic pseudo-Anosov flow, the map
e\colon\partial\mathcal{O}\to S^{2}_{\infty}
gives a Cannon–Thurston map for
\mathcal{F}
in the sense of
Section

1
.

Intuitively, this definition says that the sphere-filling curve resulting from the Cannon–Thurston map stitches together the continuous extensions of each leaf in a way that is compatible with the universal circle structure. We show in
Theorem

6.1
that the map
\mathrm{CT}
satisfies this definition.

Given an appropriate foliation
\mathcal{F}
and a universal circle
\mathcal{C}
for
\mathcal{F}
,
Section

1
can be viewed as an attempt to
define
a Cannon–Thurston map

h\colon\mathcal{C}\to S^{2}_{\infty}

as follows. Given a point
s\in\mathcal{C}
, if
s\in\core(\pi_{l})
, then define
h(s)=i_{l}\circ\pi_{l}(s)
. The issues that then arise are the following. The map is not well-defined, as we may have
s\in\core(\pi_{\mu})
for some other leaf
\mu
. Furthermore, not every point of
\mathcal{C}
is guaranteed to be in some core, so we have not defined the map at every point. Finally, it is not clear that the map is continuous, on whatever domain on which it is defined.

\mathbb{R}
-covered case

For the weak unstable foliation of an
\mathbb{R}
-covered Anosov flow
\varphi
on a hyperbolic 3-manifold, the rightmost universal circle arises as the flow space boundary
\partial\mathcal{O}_{\psi}
of a
regulating pseudo-Anosov flow

\psi
, constructed by Calegari
[
7
, Corollary 5.3.16]
and Fenley
[
15
]
(see also
[
4
, Section 6]
). This flow has no perfect fits
[
15
, Theorem G]
and therefore is quasigeodesic
[
16
, Main Theorem]
. Thus, the map
e\colon\partial\mathcal{O}_{\psi}\to S^{2}_{\infty}
constructed by Frankel
[
19
]
defines a Cannon–Thurston map for the leftmost universal circle. This is a Cannon–Thurston map in the sense of
Section

1
by
[
23
]
.

Proof strategy

The idea behind the construction of the map
\mathrm{CT}
is this. The Anosov flow
\varphi
is quasigeodesic by work of Fenley
[
17
]
. Thus, the inclusion
\lambda\to\widetilde{M}
of each leaf of the lifted unstable foliation
\widetilde{W}^{u}
extends continuously to a map
i_{\lambda}\colon\partial_{\infty}\lambda\to S^{2}_{\infty}
on the boundary at infinity of the leaf
\lambda
. Morally, for each section
s
of
\mathcal{C}^{\ell}
we define the Cannon–Thurston map at
s
by picking a point
s(\lambda)\in\partial_{\infty}\lambda
for some leaf
\lambda
that ‘looks like’ a basepoint for that section. We then map that point to
S^{2}_{\infty}
using the map
i_{\lambda}
.

Two main issues arise. First, the choice of basepoint is not canonical. Second, we have to verify continuity of the Cannon–Thurston map. The maps
i_{\lambda}\colon\partial_{\infty}\lambda\to S^{2}_{\infty}
do not vary continuously as you move through the leaf space. Furthermore, for a sequence of sections
\{s_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
converging to a section
s
, the basepoints of the
s_{j}
’s might not converge to a basepoint of
s
, or might fail to converge at all.

The way we address both of these issues is by studying a type of extremal behavior that sections of
\mathcal{C}^{\ell}
can exhibit over paths in the leaf space. One place this behavior occurs is over the set of ‘basepoints’ of a section, which is used in proving well-definedness. It is also related to the limiting behavior of sections, which is important in the proof of continuity. Via the
stitching map

\Phi\colon\mathcal{O}\to E_{\infty}
defined in
[
4
]
, extremal portions of a section are the images of subsets of
master sets
in the orbit space, and therefore have constant images under the maps
i_{\lambda}
by work of Frankel in
[
20
]
.

Outline of paper

In
Section

2
we go over the necessary background on Anosov flows and universal circles. In
Section

3
we present a few different viewpoints on the maps
i_{\lambda}
and extend these maps to the end space of the leaf space
\mathcal{L}^{u}
. In
Section

4
we extend the notion of a basepoint of a special section to all sections of
\mathcal{C}^{\ell}
. This is done by studying the
leftmost up
and
rightmost down
regions in
\mathcal{L}^{u}
for a given section. We show that every section of
\mathcal{C}^{\ell}
is either special, or is ‘based at’ a unique end of
\mathcal{L}^{u}
. In
Section

5
we define the Cannon–Thurston map and introduce the notion of
quadrant-local extremality
for leftmost sections. This is the idea that relates the leftmost up and rightmost down behavior of sections under limits with the structure of master sets in the orbit space, allowing us to prove that the Cannon–Thurston map is well-defined and continuous in
Section

6
.

Future directions

We end this section by discussing some questions raised by
A
.

Landry–Minsky–Taylor conjectured that as actions on circles, all universal circles for non
\mathbb{R}
-covered foliations arise as flowspace boundaries of transverse almost pseudo-Anosov flows
[
22
, Conjecture 1.5]
. If this is true in our setting, it would mirror the picture of
regulating flows
for skew Anosov foliations developed by Calegari
[
7
]
and Fenley
[
14
]
. They show that for
W^{u}
the weak unstable foliation of a skew Anosov flow, the action on the leftmost universal circle is conjugate to the action on the flowspace boundary of a pseudo-Anosov flow transverse to
\psi
.

One could also ask the following, which takes into account the universal circle structure of
\mathcal{C}^{\ell}
.

Question 1.2
.

Is
\mathcal{C}^{\ell}
isomorphic
as a universal circle
to the flowspace boundary of some almost transverse pseudo-Anosov flow?

A positive answer to
1.2
would require showing that
\mathcal{C}^{\ell}
is equivariantly homeomorphic to
\partial\mathcal{O}_{\psi}
for some pseudo-Anosov flow
\psi
, and showing that this homeomorphism intertwines the monotone maps coming from the universal circle structure (see
Section

2.4
).
A
could be viewed as evidence that
[
22
, Conjecture 1.5]
and
1.2
should have positive answers.

2.
Background

We now review the background we’ll need for the paper. In particular, we go over limit sets and accumulation sets, leaf spaces of foliations, Anosov flows and foliations, universal circles, and quasigeodesic flows.

2.1.
Topological preliminaries and notation

Given a sequence
\{x_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
of points in a topological space
X
, we denote by

\acset_{j\to\infty}x_{j}

the set of
accumulation points
of the sequence, i.e. the set of points
y\in X
such that for all open neighborhoods
U
of
y
, infinitely many elements of the sequence lie in
U
. We denote by

\limset_{j\to\infty}x_{j}

the set of
limit points
of the sequence, i.e. the set of points
y\in X
such that for all open neighborhoods
U
of
y
, all but finitely many elements of the sequence lie in
U
. As we will be working in non-Hausdorff spaces, it may happen that

\limset_{j\to\infty}x_{j}

contains more than one point. In the case that

\limset_{j\to\infty}x_{j}

is a singleton, we may denote it by

\lim_{j\to\infty}x_{j}

and call it the
limit
of the sequence.

2.2.
Leaf spaces

Given a two-dimensional foliation
\mathcal{F}
of a 3-manifold
M
, we define the
leaf space
of
\mathcal{F}
by

\mathcal{L}=\widetilde{M}/\widetilde{\mathcal{F}},

where
\widetilde{M}
is the universal cover of
M
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}
is the lift of
\mathcal{F}
to
\widetilde{M}
. In general,
\mathcal{L}
is a simply connected, possibly non-Hausdorff 1-manifold. The foliation
\mathcal{F}
is said to be
\mathbb{R}
-covered
if
\mathcal{L}\cong\mathbb{R}
, and
non
\mathbb{R}
-covered
otherwise, i.e. if
\mathcal{L}
is non-Hausdorff. A
cataclysm
is a maximal set of pairwise nonseparated leaves. Two leaves within a cataclysm are said to be
branching
, and a leaf that is not part of a cataclysm is a
nonbranching leaf
. A pair of leaves
\lambda,\mu
are said to be
comparable
if there is an embedded interval connecting them in the leaf space, and
incomparable
otherwise (thus, nonseparated leaves are incomparable).

A coorientation on
\mathcal{F}
gives an orientation on
\mathcal{L}
. All our foliations will be coorientable, so we always assume
\mathcal{L}
is oriented. In figures, we draw the leaf space so that the orientation points up. Given a pair of comparable leaves
\lambda,\mu
, we say
\lambda
is
below

\mu
, written
\lambda<\mu
, if the orientation on
\mathcal{L}
restricted to the embedded interval connecting
\lambda
to
\mu
points from
\lambda
to
\mu
. Similarly, we say
\lambda
is
above

\mu
, written
\lambda>\mu
, if
\mu
is below
\lambda
. When
\lambda<\mu
, we denote by
[\lambda,\mu]
the interval in
\mathcal{L}
connecting
\lambda
to
\mu
. Given a pair of nonseparated leaves
\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}
, we say they are
branching from above
if they are limit points of a sequence of leaves
\{\lambda_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
above
\lambda
and
\lambda^{\prime}
. Similarly, we say they are
branching from below
if they are simultaneously limited onto by leaves below them.

Figure 2.
In purple, the zigzag path
\gamma
connecting
\lambda
and
\mu
. The arrows denote the orientation on
\gamma
. The ‘degenerate’ orientations at
\nu_{4}
,
\nu_{10}
, and
\nu_{12}
can be deduced from the fact that the orientations alternate between up and down.

Any pair of points
\lambda
and
\mu
in
\mathcal{L}
can be connected by a unique minimal broken oriented path
\gamma
from
\lambda
to
\mu
. We will call these
zigzag paths
. We use
zigzag ray
to refer to a zigzag path that is infinite in one direction.

In general,
\gamma
contains a sequence of (possibly nondistinct) leaves
\lambda=\nu_{0},\nu_{1},\ldots,\nu_{n}=\mu
as in
Figure

2
such that

(1)

for
j
odd,
\nu_{j}
and
\nu_{j+1}
are nonseparated, and

(2)

for
j
even,
\nu_{j}
and
\nu_{j+1}
are comparable, and
\gamma
is the union of the intervals connecting
\nu_{j}
and
\nu_{j+1}
.

Intuitively,
\gamma
travels along an embedded interval, jumps across some number of cataclysms, travels along another interval, and so on. For
j
even,
\gamma
determines an orientation on the interval from
\nu_{j}
to
\nu_{j+1}
(even when this is a degernate interval), and the orientations alternate between agreeing and disagreeing with the orientation on
\mathcal{L}
. The
\nu_{j}
’s are called
breakpoints
of the zigzag path
\gamma
. For
j\neq n
odd,
\nu_{j}
is called a
launching leaf
, and
\nu_{j+1}
is the corresponding
landing leaf
, since
\gamma
launches from
\nu_{j}
and lands on
\nu_{j+1}
as it jumps across the cataclysm.

Let
\mathcal{H}\colon\mathcal{L}\to\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{L})
denote the Hausdorffification of
\mathcal{L}
, i.e. the minimal quotient that identifies nonseparated points in
\mathcal{L}
. We denote by
\mathrm{Ends}(\mathcal{L})
the
Freudenthal space of ends
of
\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{L})
. In our case, we can think of
\mathrm{Ends}(\mathcal{L})
as equivalence classes of properly embedded rays. See
[
6
, Section 2.2]
for a discussion of the ends of a topological
\mathbb{R}
-tree that works in our setting.

2.3.
Anosov flows and foliations

A
topological Anosov flow
on a 3-manifold
M
is a flow
\varphi
preserving a pair of transverse two-dimensional foliations
W^{s}
and
W^{u}
, called the
weak stable and unstable foliations
, such that the following three conditions hold.

(1)

Leaves of
W^{s}
and
W^{u}
are foliated by flow lines and intersect along flow lines.

(2)

For all sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
, for
x\in\lambda
a leaf of
W^{s}
(respectively, a leaf of
W^{u}
), and for all
y\in\lambda_{\varepsilon}(x)
, where
\lambda_{\varepsilon}(x)
is the connected component of
B_{\varepsilon}(x)\cap\lambda
containing
x
, there is an increasing homeomorphism
\sigma\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
with
\sigma(0)=0
such that
d(\varphi_{t}(x),\varphi_{\sigma(t)}(y))\to 0
as
t\to\infty
(respectively, as
t\to-\infty
).

(3)

For all sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
\delta>0
such that for
x\in\lambda
a leaf of
W^{s}
(respectively
W^{u}
), if
y\in\lambda_{\varepsilon}(x)
and
y
isn’t in the same
\varepsilon
-local orbit of
x
, then there exists some time
t
such that for any
\sigma\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
as above,
d(\varphi_{t}(x),\varphi_{\sigma(t)}(y))\geq\delta
.

The foliations
W^{s}
and
W^{u}
are called
Anosov foliations
. The flow
\varphi
is said to be
\mathbb{R}
-covered
if
W^{s}
(equivalently,
W^{u}
) is, and
non
\mathbb{R}
-covered
otherwise. We denote the respective leaf spaces by
\mathcal{L}^{s}
and
\mathcal{L}^{u}
.

Given an Anosov flow
\varphi
, we construct the
flow space
(or
orbit space
)
\mathcal{O}
by lifting to the universal cover and collapsing flow lines, i.e.

\mathcal{O}=\widetilde{M}/\widetilde{\varphi}

where
\widetilde{M}
is the universal cover of
M
and
\widetilde{\varphi}
is a lift of
\varphi
to
\widetilde{M}
. We let

\Theta\colon\widetilde{M}\to\mathcal{O}

be the quotient map. By work of Barbot
[
1
]
and Fenley
[
12
]
, the flow space is topologically a plane. The lifted two-dimensional foliations
\widetilde{W}^{s/u}
are preserved by the lifted flow, and therefore descend to one-dimensional foliations
\mathcal{O}^{s/u}
of
\mathcal{O}
, still called the
stable and unstable foliations
. These foliations are typically quite complicated; see
Figure

3
for an idea of what they might look like.

Figure 3.
The orbit space of a non
\mathbb{R}
-covered Anosov flow (reproduced from
[
4
, Figure 3]
). Per
2.1
,
\mathcal{O}^{s}
is in blue and
\mathcal{O}^{u}
is in red.

When the foliations
W^{s/u}
are orientable and coorientable, we get orientations and coorientations on
\mathcal{O}^{s/u}
. The action
\pi_{1}(M)\curvearrowright\widetilde{M}
descends to an action
\pi_{1}(M)\curvearrowright\mathcal{O}
preserving the foliations
\mathcal{O}^{s/u}
. We set the following convention on colors and notation.

Convention 2.1
.

In the orbit space, we will always draw leaves of
\mathcal{O}^{s}
in blue and leaves of
\mathcal{O}^{u}
in red. Following
[
4
]
, we use Greek letters (such as
\lambda
,
\mu
, and
\nu
) for leaves in
\widetilde{W}^{s/u}
, and use the corresponding Latin letter (respectively
\ell
,
m
, and
n
) for the corresponding leaf in
\mathcal{O}^{s/u}
. We preserve subscripts and superscripts. For instance, if
\lambda^{u}_{j}
is a leaf of
\widetilde{W}^{u}
, then
\Theta(\lambda^{u}_{j})=\ell^{u}_{j}
, a leaf of
\mathcal{O}^{u}
.

By work of Fenley
[
15
]
, the flow space can be compactified with a circle
\partial\mathcal{O}
, called the
flow space boundary
, such that
\overline{\mathcal{O}}=\mathcal{O}\cup\partial\mathcal{O}
is homeomorphic to the closed disk. The action of
\pi_{1}(M)
on
\mathcal{O}
extends continuously to an action on
\overline{\mathcal{O}}
.

A leaf
\ell
of
\mathcal{O}^{s}
or
\mathcal{O}^{u}
will have two ‘endpoints’ in
\partial\mathcal{O}
. When
\ell
is oriented, we denote the forward endpoint by
\ell^{+}
and the backward endpoint by
\ell^{-}
. We denote the pair of endpoints by
\partial\ell
. We denote by
\partial\mathcal{O}^{s/u}
the set of endpoints of leaves of
\mathcal{O}^{s/u}
. Given an arbitrary subset
X
of
\mathcal{O}
, we denote by
\overline{X}
the closure in
\overline{\mathcal{O}}
, and we denote by
\partial X
the set
\overline{X}\cap\partial\mathcal{O}
.

2.3.1.
Structures in the orbit space

A
perfect fit rectangle
is a proper embedding

[0,1]\times[0,1]\smallsetminus\{(1,1)\}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{O}

such that the horizontal and vertical foliations are sent to portions of leaves of
\mathcal{O}^{s}
and
\mathcal{O}^{u}
, respectively. The leaves
\ell^{s}
and
\ell^{u}
contained in the image of
\{(1,t)\,\mid\,t\in[0,1)\}
and
\{(t,1)\,\mid\,t\in[0,1)\}
are said to
make a perfect fit
. See
Figure

4
.

Figure 4.
On the left, a perfect fit rectangle. The leaves
\ell^{u}
and
\ell^{s}
make a perfect fit. On the right, a branching chain, with adjacent perfect fit rectangles in yellow.

Nonseparated leaves of
\mathcal{O}^{u}
(or
\mathcal{O}^{s}
), also called branching leaves, form
branching chains
, which are ordered sets of nonseparated leaves such that each shares an endpoint in
\partial\mathcal{O}
with the leaves on either side of it, as in
Figure

. Between adjacent leaves
\ell^{u}_{1}
,
\ell^{u}_{2}
in a branching chain of
\mathcal{O}^{u}
, there is a stable leaf
\ell^{s}
making a perfect fit with each of
\ell^{u}_{1}
and
\ell^{u}_{2}
.

We refer the reader to the recent book by Barthelmé–Mann for more details on Anosov flows
[
3
]
.

2.4.
Universal circles

A
universal circle
for a foliation on a 3-manifold
M
is a way of producing an action of
\pi_{1}(M)
on a circle that is related to the geometry of the foliation at infinity. They were first defined by Thurston, and later made rigorous and studied further by Calegari–Dunfield in
[
5
]
. We review general universal circles only briefly, as the
stitching map
from
[
4
]
makes the study of universal circles for Anosov foliations much more concrete. We refer the reader to
[
5
]
for a thorough treatment of general universal circles.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a taut, coorientable foliation on a closed, atoroidal 3-manifold
M
, and let
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}
be the lift of
\mathcal{F}
to the universal cover
\widetilde{M}
. By Candel’s theorem on uniformizing foliations
[
9
]
, we can put a metric on
M
such that the restriction to any leaf of
\mathcal{F}
is hyperbolic. Thus, any leaf
\lambda
of
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}
can be identified with
\mathbb{H}^{2}
, and therefore compactified with an
ideal circle

\partial_{\infty}\lambda
. The set

\bigcup_{\lambda\text{ a leaf of }\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}}\partial_{\infty}\lambda

can be topologized in a natural way, resulting in the
circle bundle at infinity

E_{\infty}
, which is a circle bundle over the leaf space
\mathcal{L}
of
\mathcal{F}
. The circles of the form
\partial_{\infty}\lambda
are called
circle fibers
. The action of
\pi_{1}(M)
on
\widetilde{M}
by deck transformations defines an action of
\pi_{1}(M)
on
E_{\infty}
preserving the circle fibers.

2.1
.

A
universal circle
is a circle
S^{1}_{\mathrm{univ}}
, together with an action
\pi_{1}(M)\curvearrowright S^{1}_{\mathrm{univ}}
and a collection of
monotone maps

\{\pi_{\lambda}\colon S^{1}_{\mathrm{univ}}\to\partial_{\infty}\lambda\;\mid\;\lambda\in\mathcal{L}\}
(i.e. maps preserving a circular order) such that the following conditions are satisfied.

(1)

The map
\pi\colon S^{1}_{\mathrm{univ}}\times\mathcal{L}\to E_{\infty}
defined by
\pi(z,\lambda)=\pi_{\lambda}(z)
is continuous.

(2)

For all
\gamma\in\pi_{1}(M)
and
\lambda\in\mathcal{L}
, the following diagram commutes.

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces S^{1}_{\mathrm{univ}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces S^{1}_{\mathrm{univ}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\partial_{\infty}\lambda}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\partial_{\infty}\gamma\cdot\lambda}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cdot\gamma}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{\lambda}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{\gamma\cdot\lambda}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cdot\gamma}

(3)

For incomparable leaves
\lambda,\mu\in\mathcal{L}
, the
core
of the map
\pi_{\lambda}
, i.e. the set of points in
S^{1}_{\mathrm{univ}}
on which
\pi_{\lambda}
is injective, is contained in a single
gap
of
\pi_{\mu}
, i.e. the preimage in
S^{1}_{\mathrm{univ}}
of a single point in
\partial_{\infty}\mu
under
\pi_{\mu}
.

By
[
5
]
, any such foliation
\mathcal{F}
admits a universal circle called the
leftmost universal circle
, which we now describe.

A
marker
is an embedding
m\colon I\to E_{\infty}
that is transverse to the circle fibers, and such that the corresponding leaves of
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}
stay a uniformly bounded distance apart along the direction toward infinity that the marker defines. We also use the term ‘marker’ to refer to the image of a marker. A point in
E_{\infty}
with a marker through it is called a
marker point
, and is called a
nonmarker point
otherwise.

Figure 5.
Approximating
s^{\ell}_{p}
by adding in more and more markers and travelling leftmost up, rightmost down without crossing markers (reproduced from
[
4
, Figure 7]
). Markers are drawn in blue, the point
p
is the purple dot, and
s^{\ell}_{p}
and its approximants are drawn in purple.

A
section
is a map
s\colon\mathcal{L}\to E_{\infty}
that intersects each circle fiber exactly once, i.e. a section of
E_{\infty}
as a circle bundle over
\mathcal{L}
. We often call the image of a section a ‘section’. An
admissible section
is a section
s
whose image does not cross markers, though it may coalesce with markers. The
leftmost section
based at a point
p\in E_{\infty}
, denoted
s^{\ell}_{p}
, is the admissible section through
p
which, over leaves comparable to the leaf containing
p
, travels as left as possible while travelling up from
p
, and travels as right as possible while travelling down from
p
. The
turning corners rule
defines what
s^{\ell}_{p}
does over other leaves in the leaf space. Intuitively, there is a special point (which in our case will be the unique nonmarker point of a leaf) that
s^{\ell}_{p}
lands on as it jumps across a cataclysm. From there, it continues travelling leftmost up and rightmost down from where it landed. Such a section can be defined rigorously by taking limits; see
Figure

5
for the idea.

The set of special sections admits an action by
\pi_{1}(M)
and a circular order, coming from the fact that two special sections cannot cross. Thus, this circular order can be completed to produce an actual circle
\mathcal{C}^{\ell}
, which is the
leftmost universal circle
. This is a
minimal
universal circle, meaning there is a bijection between points in
\mathcal{C}^{\ell}
and the sections they define. Thus, we consider sections as points of
\mathcal{C}^{\ell}
. Points in
\mathcal{C}^{\ell}
are either special sections or
limit sections
, which are limits of special sections under pointwise convergence. The monotone maps are defined by evaluating sections on circle fibers.

Given a set
A\subset E_{\infty}
and a set
\gamma\subset\mathcal{L}
, we set the notation

A\mid_{\gamma}=A\cap E_{\infty}\mid_{\gamma}.

Typically,
A
will be a section or a marker, and
\gamma
will be a zizag path or ray.

2.4.1.
Universal circles for Anosov foliations

We now specialize to the case where
\mathcal{F}=W^{u}
, the unstable foliation of an Anosov flow on
M
. Let
\mathcal{L}^{u}
be the leaf space of
W^{u}
, let
E_{\infty}
be the circle bundle at infinity for
W^{u}
, and let
\mathcal{O}
be the orbit space of the Anosov flow. For each leaf
\lambda^{u}
of
\widetilde{W}^{u}
, the ideal circle
\partial_{\infty}\lambda^{u}
contains a unique nonmarker point, which is the common backwards endpoint of every orbit in
\lambda^{u}
. These points vary continuously in the leaf space, and trace out a section
s_{\mathbf{nm}}
which we call the
nonmarker section
. Following
[
4
]
, we define the
stitching map

\Phi\colon\mathcal{O}\to E_{\infty}\smallsetminus s_{\mathbf{nm}}
by

\Phi(\alpha)=\alpha^{+},

where
\alpha^{+}
is the forwards endpoint of the orbit
\alpha
in the ideal boundary
\partial_{\infty}\lambda^{u}
of the leaf
\lambda^{u}
of
\widetilde{W}^{u}
containing the orbit
\alpha
. Via the following theorem, the stitching map and the structure of the orbit space tell us everything we need to know about
E_{\infty}
.

Theorem 2.2

(Theorem C of
[
4
]
)
.

The map
\Phi
is a
\pi_{1}(M)
-equivariant homeomorphism sending leaves of
\mathcal{O}^{s}
to markers and sending leaves of
\mathcal{O}^{u}
to sets of the form
\partial_{\infty}\lambda\smallsetminus s_{\mathbf{nm}}(\lambda)
for
\lambda\in\mathcal{L}^{u}
.

In light of this, we set the following convention for drawing
E_{\infty}
.

Convention 2.3
.

In figures, we draw the nonmarker section in orange, the circle fibers in red, and the markers in blue, so that the coloring of
\mathcal{O}
according to
2.1
lines up with the coloring of
E_{\infty}
after applying
\Phi
.

By convention, whenever we write
\Phi^{-1}(X)
for
X\subseteq E_{\infty}
, we really mean
\Phi^{-1}(X\cap s_{\mathbf{nm}})
.

Given a leaf
\lambda
of
W^{u}
and an interval neighborhood
\tau
of
\lambda
in
\mathcal{L}^{u}
the circle fiber
\partial_{\infty}\lambda
and
s_{\mathbf{nm}}\mid_{\tau}
divide a neighborhood of
s_{\mathbf{nm}}(\lambda)
in
E_{\infty}\mid_{\tau}
into four
quadrants
, which we call the upper left, upper right, lower left, and lower right quadrants, using the orientation from
\mathcal{O}
. Mapping this picture to
\mathcal{O}
using
\Phi^{-1}
, we see that leaves of
\mathcal{O}^{s}
making perfect fits with
\ell
, and branching leaves of
\mathcal{O}^{u}
adjacent to
\ell
are in one of these four quadrants.

2.5.
Quasigeodesic flows

We now review the necessary background on quasigeodesic flows. We restrict to the case where the flow is Anosov for simplicity, but much of this still holds for general quasigeodesic flows.

Let
M
be a closed hyperbolic 3-manifold. Since
M
is hyperbolic, we have
\widetilde{M}\cong\mathbb{H}^{3}
, and thus
\widetilde{M}
can be compactified with an ideal 2-sphere
S^{2}_{\infty}\cong\partial_{\infty}\mathbb{H}^{3}
. By work of Fenley
[
17
]
, an Anosov flow on
M
is
quasigeodesic
, meaning that (parameterized) orbits of
\widetilde{\varphi}
stay uniformly bounded distance from geodesics in
\widetilde{M}
, if and only if the flow is non
\mathbb{R}
-covered. Let
\varphi
be a non
\mathbb{R}
-covered Anosov flow on
M
. Since
\varphi
is quasigeodesic, any flowline
\alpha
of
\widetilde{\varphi}
has well-defined forwards and backwards endpoints
\alpha^{\pm}\in S^{2}_{\infty}
, which are necessarily distinct. Using this, we define the
endpoint maps

e^{\pm}\colon\mathcal{O}\to S^{2}_{\infty}
by

e^{\pm}(\alpha)=\alpha^{\pm}.

Note that
e^{+}
is constant on leaves of
\mathcal{O}^{s}
, while
e^{-}
is constant on leaves of
\mathcal{O}^{u}
.

Frankel showed that
e^{+}
and
e^{-}
can be continuously extended to maps

\mathbf{e}^{\pm}\colon\overline{\mathcal{O}}\to S^{2}_{\infty}

and that these maps agree on
\partial\mathcal{O}
; see
[
18
]
. We let
e\colon\partial\mathcal{O}\to S^{2}_{\infty}
denote the common map on the boundary. Thus, the maps
\mathbf{e}^{\pm}
agree with
e^{\pm}
on
\mathcal{O}
and with
e
on
\partial\mathcal{O}
. The map
e\colon\partial\mathcal{O}\to S^{2}_{\infty}
defines a
\pi_{1}(M)
-equivariant sphere-filling curve, generalizing the Cannon–Thurston maps coming from pseudo-Anosov suspension flows.

It will be important for us the understand the preimages of points in
S^{2}_{\infty}
under
\mathbf{e}^{+}
and
\mathbf{e}^{-}
. Following Frankel in
[
20
]
, given a point
z\in S^{2}_{\infty}
, we call

\mathcal{X}=\left(\mathbf{e}^{+}\right)^{-1}(z)\cup\left(\mathbf{e}^{-}\right)^{-1}(z)

the
master set rooted at
z
. As
\mathbf{e}^{+}
(resp.
\mathbf{e}^{-}
) is constant on leaves of
\mathcal{O}^{s}
(resp.
\mathcal{O}^{u}
), we have that
\mathcal{X}\cap\mathcal{O}
is a union of leaves of
\mathcal{O}^{s/u}
. Since the flow is quasigeodesic, a flowline has distinct forward and backward limit points on
S^{2}_{\infty}
. Thus,
\mathcal{X}\cap\mathcal{O}
is a union of
disjoint
leaves of
\mathcal{O}^{s/u}
. Since
\mathbf{e}^{+}
and
\mathbf{e}^{-}
agree on
\partial\mathcal{O}
, we have that if two leaves of
\mathcal{O}^{s/u}
share an endpoint in
\partial\mathcal{O}
, then either both are in
\mathcal{X}
or neither are.

Putting all this together, we see that if
\mathcal{X}
contains a point
p\in\mathcal{O}
, then
\mathcal{X}
contains either
\ell^{s}
, the stable leaf through
p
, or
\ell^{u}
, the unstable leaf through
p
. Suppose
\mathcal{X}
contains
\ell^{s}
. Then
\mathcal{X}
also contains any leaves of
\mathcal{O}^{s/u}
connected to
\ell^{s}
by a chain of leaves sharing endpoints in
\partial\mathcal{O}
, as in
Figure

6
, as well as the endpoints of all these leaves. By
[
20
, Lemma 5.2]
, master sets are connected. Thus, this process builds the entire master set. If
\mathcal{X}
does not contain any points in
\mathcal{O}
, then it must be a single point in
\partial\mathcal{O}
.

By work of Fenley
[
16
,
13
]
, we have the following.

Lemma 2.4
.

For
\varphi
a non
\mathbb{R}
-covered Anosov flow on
M
a closed, hyperbolic 3-manifold, every master set contains finitely many leaves of
\mathcal{O}^{s/u}
.

Proof.

By
[
16
, Main Theorem]
, no closed orbit of
\varphi
is nontrivially homotopic to itself, and the number of closed orbits in a free homotopy class is bounded. Suppose
\mathcal{X}
were a master set with infinitely many leaves. Either
\mathcal{X}
contains infinitely many branching leaves, or it does not.

If
\mathcal{X}
contains infinitely many branching leaves, then some element
g\in\pi_{1}(M)
sends a branhing leaf
\ell
in
\mathcal{X}
to a different branching leaf
\ell^{\prime}
in
\mathcal{X}
, and therefore fixes
\mathcal{X}
. This is because the number of branching leaves of
\mathcal{O}^{s/u}
is finite up to the
\pi_{1}(M)
action by
[
13
, Theorem E]
. There is a second element
f\in\pi_{1}(M)
fixing
\mathcal{X}
because branching leaves are periodic by
[
13
, Theorem A]
. Thus,
f
and
g
generate a
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
subgroup of
\pi_{1}(M)
, contradicting the fact that
\pi_{1}(M)
is Gromov-hyperbolic.

If
\mathcal{X}
contains only finitely many branching leaves, then
\mathcal{X}
contains an infinite chain of perfect fits. By
[
16
, Theorem C]
, this produces infinitely many closed orbits in a free homotopy class, contradicting the fact that
\varphi
is quasigeodesic.
∎

making perfect fits with the leaves shown appear in the figure.

Given a master set
\mathcal{X}

\mathcal{X}

\mathbf{e}^{-}

\mathbf{e}^{+}
and
\mathbf{e}^{-}
are constant on stable and unstable leaves respectively, and
\mathbf{e}^{+}

\mathbf{e}^{-}
agree on

.

3.
Continuous extensions of leaves

Throughout the paper, fix a non
\mathbb{R}
-covered Anosov flow
\varphi
on a closed, oriented hyperbolic 3-manifold
M
. Let
\widetilde{\varphi}
be a lift of
\varphi
to the universal cover
\widetilde{M}\cong\mathbb{H}^{3}
. Let
S^{2}_{\infty}
denote the ideal 2-sphere boundary of
\widetilde{M}
. Let
W^{s/u}
denote the stable and unstable foliations of
\varphi
in
M
, and let
\widetilde{W}^{s/u}
be lifts to
\widetilde{M}
. Furthermore, assume
W^{u}
is oriented and cooriented. Let
\mathcal{O}
be the orbit space of
\varphi
.

As in
Section

2.5
, the flow
\varphi
is quasigeodesic by
[
17
]
. Given a leaf
\lambda
of
\widetilde{W}^{u}
, this allows us to define a continuous extension

i_{\lambda}\colon\partial_{\infty}\lambda\to S^{2}_{\infty}

of the inclusion
\lambda\hookrightarrow\widetilde{M}
as follows. For the nonmarker point
s_{\mathbf{nm}}(\lambda)
, we define

i_{\lambda}(s_{\mathbf{nm}}(\lambda))=\alpha^{-}

for
\alpha
any orbit in
\lambda
. For a marker point
z\in\partial_{\infty}\lambda
, let
\alpha_{z}
be orbit in
\lambda
limiting to
z
in forward time. Then we define

i_{\lambda}(z)=\alpha_{z}^{+},

which is a continuous extension of the inclusion
\lambda\hookrightarrow\widetilde{M}
.

We can see this as follows. Using our conventions for notation, we have
\ell=\Theta(\lambda)
, a leaf of
\mathcal{O}^{u}
. Given a point
z\in\partial_{\infty}\lambda\smallsetminus\{s_{\mathbf{nm}}(\lambda)\}
, again let
\alpha_{z}
be the orbit in
\lambda
limiting to
z
in the future. Then
\Theta(\alpha_{z})
is a single point in
\mathcal{O}
, and

i_{\lambda}(z)=e^{+}\circ\Theta(\alpha_{z}).

Moving along the line
\ell
and applying
e^{+}
traces out
i_{\lambda}(\partial_{\infty}\lambda\smallsetminus\{s_{\mathbf{nm}}(\lambda)\})
. As we approach
\ell^{\pm}\in\partial\mathcal{O}
, the points
e^{+}\circ\Theta(\alpha_{z})
converge to
e(\ell^{\pm})
, and we have

i_{\lambda}(s_{\mathbf{nm}}(\lambda))=e(\ell^{\pm}).

Equivalently, for any point
p\in\ell
, we could take

i_{\lambda}(s_{\mathbf{nm}}(\lambda))=e^{-}(p).

We could further rephrase things to use the stitching map

\Phi\colon\mathcal{O}\to E_{\infty}.

To do this, we define an extension of
\Phi^{-1}
,

\Psi\colon E_{\infty}\to\overline{\mathcal{O}}

by

\Psi\mid_{E_{\infty}\smallsetminus s_{\mathbf{nm}}}=\Phi^{-1}

and

\Psi(s_{\mathbf{nm}}(\lambda))=\ell^{+}.

Of course, the choice of
\ell^{+}
over
\ell^{-}
was arbitrary, and the map
\Psi
is not continuous. However, it allows us to succinctly rephrase
i_{\lambda}
as

i_{\lambda}=\mathbf{e}^{+}\circ\Psi\mid_{\partial_{\infty}\lambda}.

For convenience, we collate each of these maps
i_{\lambda}
into a single map
i
as defined below.

3.0
.

For
\lambda\in\mathcal{L}^{u}
, let

i_{\lambda}\colon\partial_{\infty}\lambda\to S^{2}_{\infty}

be the continuous extension of
\lambda\hookrightarrow\widetilde{M}
. Let

i\colon E_{\infty}\to S^{2}_{\infty}

be the (discontinuous) function defined by

i(z)=i_{\lambda}(z)

for
z\in\partial_{\infty}\lambda\subset E_{\infty}
.

Using the characterization of
i_{\lambda}
in terms of
\Psi
, we have

i=\mathbf{e}^{+}\circ\Psi.

The map
i
is not continuous, as illustrated by the following example. Let
\{p_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of points in
\mathcal{O}
limiting to a point
p\in\mathcal{O}
on a branching unstable leaf
\ell_{1}^{u}
nonseparated from a leaf
\ell_{2}^{u}
, as in
Figure

7
.

Figure 7.

\{p_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\mathcal{O}

in

, a branching leaf.

Then using the stitching map to transfer this setup to
E_{\infty}
, the sequence
\{\Phi(p_{j})\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
approaches both
\Phi(p)
and
s_{\mathbf{nm}}(\lambda_{2}^{u})
. Under
i
, we have

i\circ\Phi(p)=e^{+}(p)

and

i(s_{\mathbf{nm}}(\lambda_{2}^{u}))=e^{-}(\ell_{2}^{u})=e^{-}(p).

However,
e^{+}(p)\neq e^{-}(p)
since
\varphi
is quasigeodesic.

Although
i
is not continuous, the above example is the only thing that goes wrong, in the sense of the following two lemmas.

Lemma 3.1
.

The restricted maps

i\mid_{s_{\text{nm}}}\colon s_{\text{nm}}\to S^{2}_{\infty}

and

i\mid_{E_{\infty}\smallsetminus s_{\text{nm}}}\colon E_{\infty}\smallsetminus s_{\text{nm}}\to S^{2}_{\infty}

are continuous.

Proof.

On
s_{\mathbf{nm}}
, the map
i
is conjugate to the map
e^{-}\colon\mathcal{L}^{u}\to S^{2}_{\infty}
, which is continuous since the map
e^{-}\colon\mathcal{O}\to S^{2}_{\infty}
is. On
E_{\infty}\smallsetminus s_{\mathbf{nm}}
, the map
i
is conjugate to the map
e^{+}\colon\mathcal{O}\to S^{2}_{\infty}
via the stitching map, which is continuous.
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\{z_{j}\in\partial_{\infty}\lambda_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of points in
E_{\infty}
limiting to a set of points
\{y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}\}
in circle fibers
\{\partial_{\infty}\mu_{1},\ldots,\partial_{\infty}\mu_{n}\}
. If either

(1)

y_{k}\neq s_{\mathbf{nm}}(\mu_{k})
for all
k
, or

(2)

y_{k}=s_{\mathbf{nm}}(\mu_{k})
for all
k
,

then

\lim_{j\to\infty}i(z_{j})=i(y_{k})

for all
k
.

Proof.

First, suppose we are in case (1). Then we must have
n=1
, as
E_{\infty}\smallsetminus s_{\mathbf{nm}}
is homeomorphic to a plane via
[
4
, Theorem C]
, and in particular is Hausdorff. Since

y_{1}=\lim_{j\to\infty}z_{j},

we must have that past some index, each
z_{j}
is contained in
E_{\infty}\smallsetminus s_{\mathbf{nm}}
. Thus,

\lim_{j\to\infty}i(z_{j})=i\left(\lim_{j\to\infty}z_{j}\right)=i(y_{1})

by
Lemma

3.1
.

Now suppose instead that we are in case (2). Split the sequence
\{z_{j}\}
into two subsequences,
\{z_{j}^{\prime}\}
and
\{z_{j}^{\prime\prime}\}
, such that

z_{j}^{\prime}\in s_{\mathbf{nm}}

and

z_{j}^{\prime\prime}\in E_{\infty}\smallsetminus s_{\mathbf{nm}}

for all
j
. If either sequence is finite, we ignore it. For the sequence
\{z_{j}^{\prime}\}
, we have that

\lim_{j\to\infty}i(z_{j}^{\prime})=i(y_{k})

for all
k
by
Lemma

3.1
.

For the sequence
\{z_{j}^{\prime\prime}\}
, pick some
\mu_{r}\in\{\mu_{1},\cdots,\mu_{n}\}
, and let
\tau
be a transversal determining an open interval neighborhood
\tau\subset\mathcal{L}^{u}
of
\mu_{r}
. Since

\limset_{j\to\infty}\lambda_{j}=\{\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n}\},

we must have that past some index, all the
\lambda_{j}
are contained in
\tau\subset\mathcal{L}^{u}
. Let
\mathcal{S}^{u}(\tau)
denote the unstable saturation of
\tau
in
\mathcal{O}
, and let
\overline{\mathcal{S}^{u}(\tau)}
denote its closure in
\overline{\mathcal{O}}
. Since

\Phi^{-1}(z_{j}^{\prime\prime})\in\mathcal{S}^{u}(\tau),

we must have that

\acset_{j\to\infty}\Phi^{-1}(z_{j}^{\prime\prime})\subset\overline{\mathcal{S}^{u}(\tau)}.

Since

\limset_{j\to\infty}\lambda_{j}=\{\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n}\},

we in fact have that

\acset_{j\to\infty}\Phi^{-1}(z_{j}^{\prime\prime})\subset\overline{\{m_{1},\ldots,m_{n}\}},

where the closure is taken in
\overline{\mathcal{O}}
. Furthermore, the accumulation set must be contained entirely in
\partial\mathcal{O}
. This is because
y_{k}=s_{\mathbf{nm}}(\mu_{k})
for every
k
by assumption. Thus,

\acset_{j\to\infty}i(z_{j}^{\prime\prime})=\acset_{j\to\infty}e^{+}\circ\Phi^{-1}(z_{j}^{\prime\prime})=e(m_{r}^{+})=i(y_{r}),

as
e
is a continuous extension of
e^{+}
to
\partial\mathcal{O}
.
∎

3.1.
Extension to the end space

We can extend
i
to
\mathrm{Ends}(\mathcal{L}^{u})
as follows. Let
\varepsilon
be an end of
\mathcal{L}^{u}
, and let
\gamma
be a zigzag ray in
\mathcal{L}^{u}
limiting to
\varepsilon
, and suppose
\gamma
is oriented toward
\varepsilon
. For each leaf
\lambda
in
\gamma
, let
\mathrm{span}^{+}(\ell)
be the component of
\partial\mathcal{O}\smallsetminus\{\ell^{+},\ell^{-}\}
on the positive side of
\ell
, where the coorientation on
\ell
is induced by the orientation on
\gamma
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\varepsilon\in\mathrm{Ends}(\mathcal{L}^{u})
, and let
\gamma
be a zigzag ray in
\mathcal{L}^{u}
limiting to
\varepsilon
. Then the set

\bigcap_{\lambda\in\gamma}\mathrm{span}^{+}(\ell)

is a single point in
\partial\mathcal{O}
, and is independent of the chosen
\gamma
.

Proof.

Suppose we have
\varepsilon
and
\gamma
as in the lemma statement. Let
\lambda,\mu\in\gamma
such that
\mu
is closer to
\varepsilon
than
\lambda
. Then

\mathrm{span}^{+}(m)\subset\mathrm{span}^{+}(\ell),

which shows that

\bigcap_{\lambda\in\gamma}\mathrm{span}^{+}(\ell)

is a nonempty, possibly degenerate interval. We aim to show it is a single point, so suppose toward contradiction it is a nondegenerate interval
I
in
\partial\mathcal{O}
.

Since
\gamma
is a properly embedded zigzag ray in
\mathcal{L}^{u}
, we have that in
\mathcal{O}
,

\limset_{\lambda\in\gamma}\ell=\varnothing.

Thus in
\overline{\mathcal{O}}
, we must have that

\limset_{\lambda\in\gamma}\ell=I.

However, using the density of regular periodic points
[
2
, Lemma 2.30]
and the corresponding multi sink-source dynamics on
\partial\mathcal{O}

[
2
, Proposition 3.7]
, we can find an unstable leaf
\ell^{u}
with both endpoints in the interior of
I
, contradicting that
\{\ell\}_{\lambda\in\gamma}
has no limit points in
\mathcal{O}
. Thus,

\bigcap_{\lambda\in\gamma}\mathrm{span}^{+}(\ell)

is a single point in
\partial\mathcal{O}
.

To see that this point doesn’t depend on the choice of ray
\gamma
, note that since
\mathcal{L}^{u}
is simply connected, any two zigzag rays
\gamma
and
\gamma^{\prime}
to
\varepsilon
eventually coincide.
∎

Thus, we can make the following definition.

3.3
.

Define

f\colon\mathrm{Ends}(\mathcal{L}^{u})\to\partial\mathcal{O}

by

f(\varepsilon)=\bigcap_{\lambda\in\gamma}\mathrm{span}^{+}(\ell)

for
\gamma
a zigzag ray in
\mathcal{L}^{u}
limiting to
\varepsilon
. Then we define

\widehat{i}\colon\mathrm{Ends}(\mathcal{L}^{u})\to S^{2}_{\infty}

by

\widehat{i}(\varepsilon)=e\circ f(\varepsilon).

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\gamma
and
e
as above. Then for leaves
\{\lambda_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\gamma
limiting to
\varepsilon
, we have

\lim_{j\to\infty}i(z_{j})=\widehat{i}(\varepsilon)

for any points
z_{j}\in\partial_{\infty}\lambda_{j}
.

Proof.

By construction of the map
f
, we have that

\lim_{j\to\infty}\Psi(z_{j})=f(\varepsilon).

Thus,

\lim_{j\to\infty}i(z_{j})=\lim_{j\to\infty}\mathbf{e}^{+}\circ\Psi(z_{j})=e\circ f(\varepsilon)=\widehat{i}(\varepsilon).

∎

4.
Basepoints of sections

4.1.
Intuition

Let
s^{\ell}_{p}
be a special section based at a point
p\in\partial_{\infty}\lambda
for some
\lambda\in\mathcal{L}^{u}
. Then, it seems natural enough to attempt to partially define
\mathrm{CT}
by

\mathrm{CT}(s^{\ell}_{p})=i(p).

That is, we use the map
i\colon E_{\infty}\to S^{2}_{\infty}
to map the basepoint
p
of
s^{\ell}_{p}
to a point in
S^{2}_{\infty}
, and that is where we map the section
s^{\ell}_{p}
. This is ultimately how we will define
\mathrm{CT}
on special sections. Of course, basepoints of special sections are not uniquely defined, so we will have to show that this map is well-defined. Our principal concern in this section, however, will be extending this definition to sections that are not special. If we let
s=s^{\ell}_{p}
and let
\partial_{\infty}\lambda
be the circle fiber containing
p
, then the candidate definition for
\mathrm{CT}(s)
above becomes

\mathrm{CT}(s)=i\circ s(\lambda).

In other words, we pick out a leaf
\lambda\in\mathcal{L}^{u}
that contains a basepoint of
s
, we evaluate
s
at that leaf, and then we map it via
i
to get a point in
S^{2}_{\infty}
. To extend this to a non-special section
s
, we need a way of picking out something that ‘looks like’ it contains a basepoint of
s
. We do this by considering the structure of the
leftmost up
and
rightmost down regions
for
s
in the leaf space. This leads us to a definition of the
base
of a section, which is some subset of
\mathcal{L}^{u}\cup\mathrm{Ends}(\mathcal{L}^{u})
.

Before setting up everything we need to define the base of a section, we discuss an example of a special section and two limit sections, as well as what the base of each will be, in order to give some intuition for the rest of the section.

4.2.
Leftmost up and rightmost down regions

We now formalize the leftmost up and rightmost down regions of
\mathcal{L}^{u}
for sections of
\mathcal{C}^{\ell}
and prove some of the basic properties we will need. Throughout,
s
will be a section of
\mathcal{C}^{\ell}
.

4.4
.

We say
s
is
leftmost up
from a point

if for all leaves
\mu\geq\lambda
, we have

s(\mu)=s^{\ell}_{p}(\mu).

Similarly,
s
is
rightmost down
from
p
if for all
\mu\leq\lambda
,

s(\mu)=s^{\ell}_{p}(\mu).

4.4
.

The
leftmost up region of
s
is

\text{LU(s)}=\{\lambda\in\mathcal{L}^{u}\;\colon\;s\text{ is leftmost up from }\lambda\}.

Similarly, the
rightmost down region of
s
is

\text{RD(s)}=\{\lambda\in\mathcal{L}^{u}\;\colon\;s\text{ is rightmost down from }\lambda\}.

Convention 4.5
.

In figures, we will draw
\mathrm{LU}(s)
in yellow and
\mathrm{RD}(s)
in green. The drawings in the margins serve as a key for this convention.

The following lemma is immediate from the definition.

Lemma 4.6
.

If
\lambda\in\text{LU}(s)
, then the upwards saturation of
\lambda
, i.e.
\{\mu\in\mathcal{L}^{u}\;\mid\;\mu\geq\lambda\}
, is in
\text{LU}(s)
. Similarly, for
\mu\in\text{RD}(s)
, the downwards saturation of
\mu
is in
\text{RD}(s)
.

Lemma 4.7
.

Every leaf
\lambda\in\mathcal{L}
is in at least one of
\mathrm{LU}(s)
or
\mathrm{RD}(s)
.

Proof.

Suppose toward contradiction that there exists a leaf
\lambda
in neither
\mathrm{LU}(s)
nor
\mathrm{RD}(s)
. Let
\tau
be a line in
\mathcal{L}^{u}
containing
\lambda
, and let
s^{\prime}=s^{\ell}_{s(\lambda)}
. Since
\lambda\notin\mathrm{LU}(s)
, there is some leaf above
\lambda
where
s
and
s^{\prime}
disagree; let
\mu^{+}
be the infimum in
\tau
of such leaves. Similarly, let
\mu^{-}
be the supremum in
\tau
of leaves below
\lambda
where
s
and
s^{\prime}
disagree.

As
s^{\prime}
diverges from
s
at
\mu^{+}
travelling up, it heads further left than
s
. Similarly, travelling down, it heads further right than

at
\mu^{-}
. Thus,
s
and

cross over a neighborhood of the interval

, contradicting
[

.
∎

4.3.
The base of a section

Our next goal is to define the ‘base’ of a section. This will end up being either the intersection of
\mathrm{LU}(s)
and
\mathrm{RD}(s)
in the case that
s
is a special section, as in
Example

4.1
, or it will be a single point in
\mathrm{Ends}(\mathcal{L}^{u})
, as in
Example

4.2
and
Example

4.3
.

To do this, we will consider zigzag paths whose orientations agree or disagree with the coloring on
\mathcal{L}^{u}
, in the sense of the following definition.

4.9
.

Let
\gamma
be a zigzag path in
\mathcal{L}^{u}
, and let
s
be a section of
\mathcal{C}^{\ell}
. We say
\gamma
is
oriented with the
s
-current
if it is contained in
\mathrm{LU}(s)
as it travels up and
\mathrm{RD}(s)
as it travels down. To be more precise, let
I\subseteq\gamma
be a maximal embedded interval. Then we require
I\subset\mathrm{LU}(s)
if the orientation on
I
is upwards, and
I\subseteq\mathrm{RD}(s)
if the orientation is downward. (Even on degenerate intervals, there is an ‘orientation’ that makes sense). Similarly,
\gamma
is
oriented against the
s
-current
if it is contained in
\mathrm{RD}(s)
as it travels up and
\mathrm{LU}(s)
as it travels down.

As in the examples at the beginning of this section, we want to intuitively define the base of a section as the leaves (or ends) where every zigzag path starting at that leaf is colored correctly. Thus, we define the base as follows.

4.9
.

For
s
a section of
\mathcal{C}^{\ell}
, we define the
base
of
s
, denoted
B(s)
, as follows. If
\mathrm{LU}(s)\cap\mathrm{RD}(s)\neq\varnothing
, we let

B(s)=\mathrm{LU}(s)\cap\mathrm{RD}(s).

Otherwise, we let
B(s)
be the set of points
\lambda\in\mathcal{L}^{u}\cup\mathrm{Ends}(\mathcal{L}^{u})
such that for every zigzag path
\gamma
starting at
\lambda
,
\gamma\smallsetminus\{\lambda\}
is oriented with the
s
-current.

To show that
B(s)
is nonempty, we essentially have to show that there is a global source for the
s
-current on
\mathcal{L}^{u}
. The idea behind showing this will be to start at an arbitrary leaf, flow against the
s
-current until we get stuck, and then show that where we got stuck is a global source.
Lemma

4.10
shows that there is at most one way to flow against the
s
-current from a given leaf, while
Lemma

4.11
shows that a local source for the
s
-current is in fact a global source.

Lemma 4.10
.

Let
s
be a section of
\mathcal{C}^{\ell}
and
\lambda\in\mathcal{L}^{u}
. If
\mathrm{LU}(s)\cap\mathrm{RD}(s)=\varnothing
, then for any two nondegenerate zigzag paths
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}
starting from
\lambda
and oriented against the
s
-current, there exists a nondegenerate zigzag path that is an initial subpath of both
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
.

Proof.

First, suppose
\lambda
is a nonbranching leaf. Then any two zigzag paths travelling up from
\lambda
agree on an open interval, as do any two zigzag paths travelling down from
\lambda
. So, we just need to show a zigzag path
\gamma
going up from
\lambda
and a zigzag path
\gamma^{\prime}
going down from
\lambda
can’t both be oriented against the
s
-current. If they were, the first part of
\gamma
would be in
\mathrm{RD}(s)
, so
\lambda
would be in
\mathrm{RD}(s)
by
Lemma

4.6
. Similarly, the first part of
\gamma^{\prime}
would be in
\mathrm{LU}(s)
, so
\lambda\in\mathrm{LU}(s)\cap\mathrm{RD}(s)
.

\lambda
is a branching leaf, and let
\gamma_{1}

be two zigzag paths from

oriented against the

-current. Each
\gamma_{j}
jumps across some number of cataclysms (possibly zero), before going either up or down on some interval
I_{j}
. Suppose toward contradiction that
I_{1}
and
I_{2}
are disjoint. Consider the zigzag path
\alpha
from a point on
I_{1}
to a point on
I_{2}
. This zigzag path travels along
I_{1}

alternate between being in
\mathrm{LU}(s)
and being in
\mathrm{RD}(s)
. Thus, we find that

Lemma 4.11
.

Let
s
be a section of
\mathcal{C}^{\ell}
. Let
\gamma
be a zigzag path in
\mathcal{L}^{u}
with a nondegenerate initial subpath that is oriented with the
s
-current. Then all of
\gamma
is oriented with the
s
-current.

Suppose otherwise, so there is some zigzag path
\gamma
with a nondegenerate initial subpath that is oriented with the
s
-current, but such that not all of
\gamma
is oriented with the

\lambda

\gamma
stops being oriented with the

-current and starts being oriented against the
s
-current. This contradicts

, as travelling from
\lambda
along
\gamma
in either direction gives a zigzag path that is oriented against the
s
-current.

Putting

4.10
and

together, we can show the following.

Lemma 4.12
.

For
s
a section of
\mathcal{C}^{\ell}
, the set

Now that we know the base of a section exists, we turn to proving some properties of

4.15
.

(for ‘special’). If

We will shortly prove that in our setting, type (I) sections do not exist. First, we need to develop a bit more structure.

If

5.
Extremal behavior and master sets

In this section, we first define the Cannon–Thurston map for
\mathcal{C}^{\ell}
using the base of a section. We then define and discuss
quadrant-local extremality
, which is the idea we will need to prove that this map is well-defined and continuous.

5.1.
Defining the Cannon–Thurston map

We are now ready to define the Cannon–Thurston map for
\mathcal{C}^{\ell}
. See
3
and
3.1
for definitions of
i
and
\widehat{i}
.

5.0
.

Define

\mathrm{CT}\colon\mathcal{C}^{\ell}\to S^{2}_{\infty}

as follows.

For
s
a section of
\mathcal{C}^{\ell}
, let

\mathrm{CT}(s)=i_{\lambda}\circ s(\lambda)

for
\lambda
any leaf of
\mathcal{L}^{u}
in
B(s)
, or

\mathrm{CT}(s)=\widehat{i}(\varepsilon)

for
\varepsilon
a point of
\mathrm{Ends}(\mathcal{L}^{u})
in
B(s)
.

This function is not a priori well-defined, as special sections do not always have base a single leaf. And of course, it is not at all clear that this function is continuous. The tool we will need to prove both well-definedness and continuity is the idea of
quadrant-local extremality
, which is a type of extremal behavior that sections can exhibit over zigzag paths.

To give some intuition for this, first consider what is special about the behavior of a special section over its base – it is both leftmost up and rightmost down in this region. Quadrant-local extremality is a generalization of this property, where a section is only required to be leftmost/rightmost up/down within a quadrant.

The importance of quadrant-local extremality for our purposes is that it relates when portions of sections map to master sets under
\Psi
, as in
Lemma

5.1
, with the leftmost up and rightmost down behavior of sections under limits, as in
Proposition

5.3
.

5.2.
Extremal behavior over zigzag paths

We now formally define quadrant-local extremality and prove the properties that we will need for
Section

6
.

5.0
.

Let
\gamma\subset\mathcal{L}^{u}
be a zigzag path, let
m
be a marker, and let
m^{+}
and
m^{-}
be the upper and lower limit points of
m\mid_{\gamma}
in
s_{\mathbf{nm}}
, if they exist. We say
m
is
quadrant-locally extremal
over
\gamma
(abbreviated

) if
m
is left or rightmost up from
m^{-}
among markers with the same endpoint in the same quadrant, and left or rightmost down from
m^{+}
among markers with the same endpoint in the same quadrant. See
Figure

16
.

A section

is
quadrant-locally extremal
over
\gamma
if the following three conditions hold:

(1)

Every marker in
s\mid_{\gamma}
is ql-extremal over
\gamma
,

(2)

The set

is discrete, and

(3)

For all breakpoints
\lambda\in\gamma
,

s(\lambda)=s_{\mathbf{nm}}(\lambda).

is leftmost up and rightmost down over its entire base.

We now show how ql-extremality relates to master sets.

Lemma 5.1
.

Suppose a section
s
is ql-extremal over a zigzag path

\Psi(s\mid_{\gamma})
is contained in a master set.

Proof.

Let

be a maximal embedded interval. We will show
\Psi(s\mid_{I})
is contained in a master set, and then show that this master set is constant as

varies.

If
s\mid_{I}
is contained in a single marker, then
\Psi(s\mid_{I})
is contained in a single stable leaf of
\mathcal{O}^{s}
, and we’re done. So, suppose
s(\lambda)=s_{\mathbf{nm}}(\lambda)
for some leaf
\lambda\in I
. Then by property (2),
s(\lambda)
is the endpoint of a marker on any side of
\lambda
contained in
I
. Let
m
be such a marker, and suppose
s(\lambda)
is its lower endpoint. By property (1),
m
is ql-extremal over
I
. Then for any leaf
\nu\in I
just above
\lambda
, we see that
m
intersects
\nu
but not
\lambda
. Mapping this to
\overline{\mathcal{O}}
via
\Psi
shows that
\Psi(m)
makes a perfect fit with
\ell
or a leaf nonseparated from
\ell
. Thus,
\Psi(s\mid_{I})
is contained in a master set.

Now consider a pair of break points
\nu_{1},\nu_{2}\in\gamma
. By property (3), we have
s(\nu_{i})=s_{\mathbf{nm}}(\nu_{i})
for
i=1,2
. Since
\nu_{1}
and
\nu_{2}
are nonseparated, we have that
\Psi(s(\nu_{1}))
and
\Psi(s\nu_{2}))
are endpoints of nonseparated leaves. Thus, for the adjacent intervals
I_{1}
and
I_{2}
containing
\nu_{1}
and
\nu_{2}
respectively, we have that
\Psi(s\mid_{I_{1}})
and
\Psi(s\mid_{I_{2}})
are contained in the same master set.
∎

Since
\mathbf{e}^{+}
is constant on leaves of
\mathcal{O}^{s}
within a single master set, we have the following corollary.

Corollary 5.2
.

Suppose a section
s
is ql-extremal over a zigzag path
\gamma
. Then for any leaves
\lambda,\mu\in\gamma
, we have

i\circ s(\lambda)=i\circ s(\mu).

Our primary tool for proving ql-extremality is the following proposition.

Proposition 5.3
.

Suppose

\lim_{j\to\infty}s_{j}=s

for sections
\{s_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
and
s
of
\mathcal{C}^{\ell}
. Let
\gamma
be the zigzag path between leaves
\lambda,\mu\in\mathcal{L}^{u}
. Suppose
\gamma
is oriented with the

is ql-extremal over
\gamma
.

Proof.

We break the proof into three steps. We first show property (2), i.e. that

over

is discrete. To do this, we need to rule out the situation where
\lambda\in\gamma
is a leaf such that
s(\lambda)=s_{\mathbf{nm}}(\lambda)
, and
s(\lambda)
is a limit of nonmarker points in
s
over
\gamma
, say from above. Pick a small neighborhood

containing

such that there is some marker

intersecting every leaf in

. Then

is composed of two rectangles

, and

and

as a pair of opposite sides. Suppose without loss of generality that

and

for all

. The setup is depicted in

Let

.
Since

\lim_{j\to\infty}s_{j}=s,

we must have

Eventually, the

at

. This is because once

Finally, we address property (3), that

6.
Proving the main theorem

In this section, we prove the main theorem of the paper, as well as
B
.

Theorem 6.1
.

The map

\mathrm{CT}\colon\mathcal{C}^{\ell}\to S^{2}_{\infty}

defines a continuous, surjective,
\pi_{1}(M)
-equivariant map. Furthermore, it is a Cannon–Thurston map for
W^{u}
in the sense of
Section

1
.

The idea behind the proof is this. Well-definedness is essentially immediate from the fact that sections are ql-extremal over their base, so any ambiguity in the choice of basepoint disappears after mapping to
S^{2}_{\infty}
. Thus, the main thing to show is continuity. To do this, we suppose we have a sequence of sections
s_{j}
converging to a section
s
. If basepoints
\lambda_{j}
of the
s_{j}
’s converge to a basepoint
\lambda
of
s
, we are good to go. However, this is not guaranteed; in fact, plenty of sequences of basepoints of the
s_{j}
’s will have no convergent subsequence, as
\mathcal{L}^{u}
is not locally compact. In this case, we can still identify a leaf (or end)
\lambda_{\infty}
that ‘looks like’ a limit point of the basepoints of the
s_{j}
’s, using the compactness of
\overline{\mathcal{O}}
. In particular, we will have

\lim_{j\to\infty}i\circ s_{j}(\lambda_{j})=i\circ s(\lambda_{\infty}).

Then, we show that the section
s
is ql-extremal over the zigzag path
\gamma
connecting
\lambda_{\infty}
with some
\lambda\in B(s)
. By
Corollary

5.2
, this shows that

i\circ s(\lambda_{\infty})=i\circ s(\lambda),

which proves continuity. Finally,

6.3.

Finally, we apply

. In particular, we show the following.

Corollary 6.10
.

Every element of
\pi_{1}(M)
has some power acting on
\mathcal{C}^{\ell}
with a positive, finite number of fixed points, which alternate between attractors and repellors.

Note that the action of
\pi_{1}(M)
on

also has this property. Thus,

6.10
can be seen as further evidence that

on some orbit space boundary

for
\psi
a pseudo-Anosov flow transverse to
W^{u}
.

Proof of
Corollary

6.10
.

Let

. Since
M
is a closed hyperbolic 3-manifold, we know

acts on
\widetilde{M}\cong\mathbb{H}^{3}
as a hyperbolic isometry. In particular,
g
fixes exactly two points on

, which are a sink and a source. Denote these points by

and
g_{-}
respectively.

We first show that the set of points of
\mathcal{C}^{\ell}
that are periodic under

are exactly the points sent to

by
\mathrm{CT}
. First suppose

is periodic under

. Then by
\pi_{1}(M)
-equivariance,
\mathrm{CT}(s)
is also periodic under
g
, and thus
\mathrm{CT}(s)=g_{\pm}
. For the other direction, suppose
\mathrm{CT}(s)=g_{\pm}
. Then by
\pi_{1}(M)
-euivariance, the entire

-orbit of

is sent to
g_{\pm}
. Since every Cannon–Thurston map is uniformly finite-to-one by
[
11
]
, we have that the
g
-orbit of
s
is finite, and therefore
s
is periodic under
g
.

Since
\mathrm{CT}
is surjective and uniformly finite-to-one, there are a positve, finite number of points of
\mathcal{C}^{\ell}
that are periodic under
g
. Fix a positive power

of
g
such that
g^{k}
fixes each of these points. Let
s
be a point in
\mathcal{C}^{\ell}
that is sent to
g_{+}
under
\mathrm{CT}
. By continuity of
\mathrm{CT}
, some small neighborhood of
s
in
\mathcal{C}^{\ell}
is mapped into a small neighborhood of
g_{+}
in
S^{2}_{\infty}
, and so
s
is a local sink for the action of

on
\mathcal{C}^{\ell}
. Similarly, points of
\mathcal{C}^{\ell}
sent to
g_{-}
