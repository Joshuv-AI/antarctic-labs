Monolithically Integrated VO 2 Mott Oscillators for Energy-Efficient Spiking Neurons
Fabio Bersano, Cyrille Masserey, Vanessa Conti, Andrea Iaconeta, Niccolò Martinolli, Ehsan Ansari, Anna Varini, Igor Stolichnov, Adrian Mihai Ionescu

Abstract
Brain-inspired non-Boolean computing offers intrinsic error tolerance and parallelism, but its practical deployment is limited by the lack of compact, energy-efficient spiking hardware compatible with large-scale integration. Mott phase-transition materials provide a promising device-level route, as their abrupt insulator-to-metal transitions enable neuron-like thresholding and oscillatory dynamics. Among these, vanadium dioxide (VO 2 ) stands out for its near-room-temperature transition, fast switching, and scalability. However, existing VO 2 -based neuristor implementations rely on discrete components, constraining integration density and system-level applicability. Here, we report monolithic back-end-of-the-line (BEOL) integration of one-transistor-one-VO 2 -memristor (1T-1MR) spiking neurons directly on CMOS-compatible platforms. VO 2 nanosheet devices are fabricated by pulsed-laser deposition at temperatures below 430 ∘ C on dielectrically isolated silicon-on-insulator (SOI) p-type junctionless field-effect transistors (JLFETs) in a compact 1T-1MR configuration. The resulting architecture exhibits gate-tunable oscillations spanning 40-410 kHz in 60 nm-thin VO 2 devices with an active area of 6 µm 2 , and achieves energy consumption as low as 18 pJ per spike at room temperature with a memristor power dissipation of 8 µW, with potential for scaling toward sub-3 µW operation. We further uncover a non-monotonic dependence of oscillation frequency on current and temperature, together with bias-dependent stochastic firing dynamics, highlighting the rich physical behavior accessible in integrated VO 2 memristor systems. Finally, we demonstrate voltage-controlled oscillator functionality and on-chip actively tunable resistive coupling of two nano-oscillators mediated by a JLFET. These results establish a viable pathway toward dense, energy-efficient, and fully monolithically integrated Mott-based neuromorphic hardware, bridging emerging phase-transition devices with CMOS technology for future computing and spiking sensing systems.

Introduction

Traditional computing systems based on the von Neumann (VN) architecture have long dominated digital hardware design due to their instruction-level programmability, sequential control flow, and compatibility with deeply scaled CMOS implementations supported by hierarchical memory integration

Nevertheless, these systems fundamentally suffer from energy and latency overheads associated with extensive data movement between physically separated memory and processing units, a bottleneck that becomes particularly severe in data-centric and edge-processing machine learning (ML) and artificial intelligence (AI) workloads

;
22
;

This limitation has stimulated the development of low-power AI accelerators that enable parallel processing of data streams rather than sequential computation. Such approaches mainly rely on artificial neural network (ANN), deep neural network (DNN), and spiking neural network (SNN) algorithms implemented through neuro-inspired hardware architectures

;

;

This temporal and event-driven encoding enables sparse communication for fast inference, low power consumption, and efficient interaction with biomimetic spiking sensory signals

In these systems, information is encoded in the phase or frequency of oscillation, while computation emerges from synchronization phenomena within the network

Results

Individual units emulating spiking neurons or oscillatory nodes are typically implemented in a one-transistor-one-memristor (1T-1MR) or one-resistor-one-memristor (1R-1MR) architecture, where self-sustained oscillations are enabled by a DC voltage or current bias. We developed a nanofabrication process enabling systematic 3D integration of 1T-1MR oscillatory nodes with an unprecedented compactness, low parasitics, and reduced footprint. Our approach combines depletion-mode junctionless (JL) SOI FETs technology and VO
2
thin films. This choice is motivated by the ease of nanofabrication and scalability of JL devices, their suitability for current-biased analog applications
12
, and the low current required to induce an IMT compared to thick VO
2
films. In the following, a description of the 3D integration scheme is presented.

An analog spiking neuron was then characterized as a function of temperature and bias current. Representative spiking waveforms under matched bias conditions across different temperatures (Fig.
4
a) reveal a non-monotonic evolution of the oscillation frequency, motivating the development of an analytical model to capture its current and temperature dependence. Conventional models based on switching between two linear resistive states, governed by threshold and holding voltages, were found insufficient to reproduce the diversity of behaviors observed experimentally. In particular, the pronounced nonlinearity of the insulating-state resistance in patterned VO
2
(Fig.
2
f) requires repeated fitting of circuit elements (resistances and capacitance) for each bias point.
To address this limitation while preserving analytical tractability, the resistance in both memristive states is approximated using affine functions. From a circuit perspective, this is implemented as a linear resistor in series with a voltage source to introduce an offset. The objective is to accurately reproduce the nonlinear I-V characteristics of the memristor in both insulating and metallic states between the holding and threshold voltages (model illustration provided in the Supplementary Information).

Analytical model of a stochastic integrated neuron
-
The proposed model introduces six parameters: the threshold voltage (
V_{TH}
) and holding voltage (
V_{HL}
); the insulating-state resistance (
R_{i}
), which approximate the I-V slope between the threshold and holding voltage; and the insulating offset voltage (
V_{oi}
), which captures the nonlinearity of the experimental I-V curves. The metallic state is described analogously by the resistance (
R_{m}
) and offset voltage (
V_{om}
). All the model parameters were extracted from oscillation measurements performed over a range of bias currents and temperatures. Dynamic extraction was preferred over quasi-static I-V characterization to account for time-dependent effects. The extraction procedures for each parameter are detailed in Methods. As predicted by finite-element modeling (FEM) simulations, the threshold and holding voltages exhibit negligible dependence on current, but a strong dependence on temperature (see Figs.
4
b and
4
d, respectively).

Remarkably, the temperature dependence of all six model parameters can be accurately fitted using first-order polynomials. The extracted temperature dependence of
V_{TH}
and
V_{HL}
is shown in Fig.
4

4

A comparison between static and dynamic extraction reveals a slight deviation, particularly at low temperature, suggesting a dynamics-dependent contribution to these parameters arising from thermal dissipation. No discernible effect of parameter variations on the oscillation frequency was observed, suggesting that the associated changes in the time constants occur on timescales much longer than the measured oscillation periods. This behavior is consistent across all six parameters included in the model (Supplementary Information).

For the investigated device,
R_{i}
and

also exhibit negligible dependence on the bias current, remaining within variations of
15.8\%
and

(2)

This formulation directly highlights the two conditions for oscillation:

By construction, the analytical model in Eq.

The difference between
V_{af}
and
V_{HL}

We define the rising and falling escape times as the time between the signal reaching 50 mV from the threshold or holding voltage and the start of the next cycle. This definition enables the inclusion of noise effects in the analytical description of the charging and discharging time. Fig.
5
e shows the dependence of the falling escape time on the holding voltage margin (median values over all cycles are reported), where the dashed line corresponds to the analytical model prediction. A simple fit to the experimental data reproduces the trend predicted by the analytical model, with an offset of approximately 10 mV, consistent with the noise level of the measurement setup. The dependence of the rising escape time on the threshold voltage margin for a different device is shown in the inset of Fig.
5
e. Consistent dependencies are observed across other devices for both oscillation boundaries, supporting an intrinsic physical origin of this behavior (Supplementary Information).

Figs.
5

5

5

This behavior can be mostly attributed to fluctuations in the holding voltage, as observed in the quasi-static I-V curves. These fluctuations were independently characterized and incorporated into the simulations (Fig.
15
in the Supplementary Information), and an additional
1/f
noise contribution was included to reproduce the measured distribution.

As the holding voltage margin increases, the signal slope decreases, leading to a progressive skewing of the distribution. In Fig.
5
g, the combined effect of
1/f

This time shift can be described by a time-dependent variation of the holding voltage due to thermal dissipation in the substrate, which is modeled in the simulations as an equivalent thermal RC delay. At larger holding voltage margins, the escape process approaches a Poisson-like distribution (Fig.
5

Finally, Figs.
5

The period distributions follow exponential, Gaussian, and Gamma distributions, as also observed in sub-species of biological neurons

Importantly, this analysis of firing stochasticity opens up new possibilities for fully integrated randomness-based devices, such as random number generators

2.3.4
On-chip active resistive coupling of integrated VO
2
nano-oscillators

Physical computing with ONNs typically exploits the rich collective dynamics of coupled oscillators, which span regimes ranging from chaotic behavior to synchronization. The latter is particularly relevant, as it reflects the stability and intrinsic memory of a network of coupled dynamical systems, where electrical coupling is commonly achieved using resistors or capacitors
44
.

Following the characterization of the tunability of the oscillation frequency with current bias (or gate voltage) and its temperature dependence, we investigated the synchronization of two VCOs monolithically integrated on the same substrate. The coupling is mediated by the resistive channel of a p-JLFET connecting the oscillating nodes. When the junctionless transistors are biased in deep accumulation at low V
DS
, their channel can be modeled by an equivalent series resistance of 343 k
\Omega
. This configuration therefore mimics previous studies on the synchronization dynamics of discrete VO

oscillators coupled through an external resistor

2
devices are shown in Fig.

2
devices, reflecting the intrinsic variability of the technology. To compensate for the different voltage windows of the two devices, an asymmetric V
SS

SS1
= -11.5 V, V
SS2
= -10 V), resulting in different bias currents for the two VCOs.

Self-sustained oscillations measured by the oscilloscope at the two output nodes (V
out1
and V
out2
in the circuit schematic) are shown in Fig.
7

G1
, V
G2

{}_{G3}<0

\mu=43

The mean and median frequencies of the two waveforms differ by only 0.017% and 0.14%, respectively, while the different standard deviations reflect the distinct bias points at which the two devices traverse their respective I-V curves.

This proof-of-concept result establishes the feasibility of extending integrated VO

3
Discussion

This work demonstrates the first integrated 1T-1MR VO
2
-based oscillators achieved through stack engineering of an ultra-thin-body, highly doped SOI substrate.

Previous integrated architectures based on functional oxides have explored different material systems
5
. Among these, a subset of studies relies on TaO
x
-based memristors directly integrated on CMOS circuits to implement multi-state memory and oscillator functionalities, exploiting bistability arising from the temporary formation and dissolution of conductive filaments
34
;
35
;
17
. In contrast, the polycrystalline PLD VO
2
nanosheet devices engineered in our study exhibit a fully reversible, current-driven IMT phase transition. This switching mechanism is not solely governed by ionic motion (defects) and typically results in better reversibility of the conductance states than filamentary resistive switching approaches (for physical insights into the percolation dynamics in VO
2
films, see Tiwari et al.
43
).

Quasi-static and dynamic measurements were performed from 25 °C up to 45
∘
C, revealing voltage-controlled oscillatory dynamics and spike thresholding behavior that had previously been reported only in discrete-component VO
2
oscillators. The compact devices achieve room-temperature frequency tunability from 40 kHz to 410 kHz, with sub-10
\mu
W power consumption in the VO
2
device.

The dependence of the oscillation frequency on device temperature and bias current was experimentally characterized and accurately captured through analytical fitting and lumped-element circuit simulations. The results show an almost linear dependence of both threshold and holding voltages on device temperature, as well as a non-monotonic frequency evolution, which is governed by the time constants associated with the insulating and metallic resistances of the thin film. These experimental studies provide new insights into the design of fully integrated oscillating systems operating under varying temperature conditions.

The analysis of the stochastic spiking shows an evolution of the escape-time and period distributions from exponential to Gaussian and back to exponential when sweeping across the hysteresis window from the threshold to the holding voltage. This analysis was enabled by the relatively low resistive ratio of the fabricated devices, which allows to explore the oscillation regime beyond the holding-defined range at bias current levels compatible with transistor operation, thereby opening new possibilities for low-power randomness-based devices such as fully-integrated random number generators.

Device operation as a voltage-controlled oscillator was demonstrated using linear ramp, sinusoidal, and square pulse waveforms. Moreover, for the first time, resistively coupled oscillatory dynamics were experimentally realized using an integrated JLFET as an active coupling element.

Further investigations of device-to-device and die-to-die variability in the integrated oscillators will be required to optimize the process for very large-scale integration. Nevertheless, this proof-of-concept experimental study highlights the feasibility of 3D integration of VO
2
devices with CMOS platforms within a More-than-Moore (
i.e.
, CMOS+X) framework, and opens a pathway toward fully integrated systems for oxide electronics, neuromorphic sensing, and computing.

4.1
Fabrication of junctionless FETs and VO

UTB-SOI JLFETs -

VO

Devices (active area and residual layer) were patterned by direct laser writing (Heidelberg MLA 150) using a AZ 1512 HS photoresist, and subsequently etched with IBE. 4.2
Electrical measurements

4.4
Simulation of 1T-1MR spiking systems and randomness analysis

The analysis of the measured waveforms was performed by defining a trigger level and identifying signal edges, allowing segmentation of individual oscillation cycles. From each cycle, the period and frequency were extracted, along with the maximum voltage (
V_{TH}
) and minimum voltage (
V_{HL}
). The energy per cycle was obtained by integrating the product of the voltage signal and the bias current over one period.

In a second step, the rising and falling phases of each cycle were fitted using a standard capacitor charge/discharge model:

V_{C}\left(t\right)=\left(V_{0}-V_{a}\right)\exp\left(-\frac{t-t_{0}}{\tau}\right)+V_{a}

(3)

The fitting parameters - namely the initial voltage (
V_{0}
), the asymptotic voltage (
V_{a}
), the time constant (
\tau
), and the initial time (
t_{0}
) - were determined for both phases. Using the estimated capacitance (
C_{L}
), the corresponding resistances and offset voltages for the insulating (rising) and metallic (falling) states were then calculated as:

R_{i/m}=\tau_{r/f}/C_{L}\quad V_{oi/om}=V_{ar/af}-IR_{i/m},

(4)

where
I
is the bias current.
For the simulation of the falling escape time,
10000
discrete iterations were performed to reconstruct the histograms shown in Fig.
5
f-h. In each iteration, Eq.
3
was used to model the noise-free falling phase of the cycle. The median values of the falling time constant (
\tau_{f}
) and asymptotic voltage (
V_{af}
) extracted from the corresponding measurements were used as input parameters. The initial voltage (
V_{0f}
) was set to 50 mV higher than
V_{af}
and the initial time was fixed to
t_{0}=0
s.

A band-limited
1/f
noise signal was then generated and superimposed onto the noise-free waveform. For each simulation, the holding voltage was selected following a normal distribution mimicking the one observed in measurements. To account for the observed time dependence of the holding voltage, an equivalent thermal RC delay was introduced, with the asymptotic value given by the holding voltage randomly selected for the iteration. The falling escape time was defined as the time corresponding to the first crossing between the noisy signal and the instantaneous holding voltage.

We assume that the time dependence of the holding voltage arises from thermal relaxation processes associated with Joule heating during switching. Accordingly, a thermal RC model is adopted as a first-order approximation.
