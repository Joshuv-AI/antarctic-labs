Aerobic exercise enhances α-tubulin lactylation and neurological recovery after ischemic stroke
Authors: Yun Zhang, Xianli Tang, Zeheng Li, Han Wu, Zhen Wang, Yiming Zhu, Jiran Li, Xingrui Wu, Bo Ning, Guiyun Cui, Jinxia Hu, Jie Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIschemic stroke causes severe neurological disability, with some patients still presenting with residual neurological dysfunction. Aerobic exercise shows potential for post-stroke recovery. However, the mechanism behind its beneficial effects on the brain remains to be further explored. This study aimed to explore aerobic exercise’s role in the neurological recovery of ischemic stroke mice and to clarify the specific protective mechanisms involved.MethodsA mouse model of ischemic stroke was employed in this study. Mice were subjected to aerobic exercise intervention, with additional exogenous lactate administration and RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis conducted to explore the molecular mechanisms.ResultsResults demonstrated that aerobic exercise significantly elevated protein lactylation levels in ischemic stroke mice and facilitated neurological recovery. In addition, aerobic exercise attenuated neuroinflammation in the ischemic penumbra and promoted neural cell proliferation in the subventricular zone. Mechanistically, aerobic exercise-induced lactylation of α-tubulin may enhance the dynamic properties of microtubules, thereby facilitating neural repair. Exogenous lactate intervention was found to augment α-tubulin lactylation, promote hippocampal neurogenesis, and increase dendritic spine density as well as synaptic plasticity in both the ischemic penumbra and hippocampus. Consistent with these findings, exogenous lactate administration improved neurological function in ischemic stroke mice. RNA-seq analysis further revealed that elevated lactate levels exerted a promotive effect on neural repair.ConclusionAerobic exercise promotes post-ischemic stroke neurological recovery via elevating protein lactylation (α-tubulin lactylation), attenuating neuroinflammation, and enhancing neural repair. These findings highlight the potential of targeting lactate metabolism and protein lactylation as therapeutic strategies for facilitating neural repair and improving neurological outcomes following ischemic stroke.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13578-026-01608-w.

Introduction

Stroke is one of the leading causes of disability and death worldwide, imposing a heavy burden on patients, families, and society [1, 2]. Despite advances in acute reperfusion therapies, effective strategies for long-term neurological recovery after stroke remain limited [2–4]. A subset of patients still experiences residual neurological deficits, including motor and sensory impairments [5–7]. These impairments have a severe impact on their quality of life and result in long-term socio-economic burdens [8]. Therefore, delving into the pathological mechanisms underlying post-stroke neurological recovery and developing effective therapeutic and rehabilitative intervention strategies represent critical issues that require urgent attention.

The post-stroke rehabilitation has long relied on passive, compensatory training targeting specific functional impairments [9]. In recent years, active, whole-body interventions, such as aerobic exercise, have been gradually applied in clinical practice [10, 11]. The brain possesses an inherent capacity for structural and functional adaptive changes following injury, known as neuroplasticity [12], and aerobic exercise is considered a key driver of this process [13–15]. Both clinical and animal studies indicate that aerobic exercise benefits not only cardiovascular health [16], but also promotes neuroplasticity [13, 14] and stimulates angiogenesis [17, 18]. However, the precise mechanisms by which aerobic exercise promotes neurological recovery following ischemic stroke constitute a multidimensional and interconnected complex regulatory network that has not yet been systematically elucidated.

Lactate, as a key metabolic product of aerobic exercise, has been newly identified as an important substrate for lysine lactylation, a novel post-translational modification (PTM) [19]. Previous studies have revealed that lactate molecules act as intracellular signals to regulate the activity of the synaptic protein synaptosome-associated protein 91 (SNAP91), thereby influencing synaptic transmission and enhancing stress resilience in mice [20]. Moreover, lactate is extensively involved in various molecular regulatory processes. Therefore, elucidating the mechanisms of lactate and its modifications will lay a crucial foundation for advancing related research from animal studies to clinical translation [19–21]. Under high lactate concentrations, HDAC6 significantly enhances the lactylation levels of α-tubulin, and this lactylation modification competes with acetylation to potentiate microtubule dynamics [22]. The dynamic growing plus end of microtubules acts as “sensors” of the cellular microenvironment, which allows the rapid reorganization of the cytoskeleton for successful axon regeneration [23]. Another study has demonstrated that Fmn2 deficiency in the dorsal root ganglia of mice with sciatic nerve injury extends the highly dynamic phase of microtubules at the plus ends of microtubules in growing axons by enhancing microtubule polymerization rate and prolonging polymerization duration, thereby promoting neural repair [24]. Two key questions are addressed in the present study: whether exercise-induced lactate is involved in regulating α-tubulin lactylation, and whether neural functional recovery after ischemic stroke correlates with α-tubulin lactylation. Given that aerobic exercise can significantly increase peripheral and central lactate levels, we propose that exercise may induce α-tubulin lactylation to enhance microtubule dynamics, thereby promoting neural functional recovery. In addition, previous studies have demonstrated that modulating synaptic plasticity and its associated regulatory mechanisms contributes to promoting neural functional recovery. For instance, exercise therapy could activate the PKA/CREB signaling pathway to enhance synaptic and dendritic spine plasticity and facilitate myelin repair [25]. Environmental enrichment enhanced cognitive function by inducing changes in synaptic plasticity [26]. The bFGF-Chitosan gel enhanced sensorimotor function by promoting synapse formation and neural network reconstruction [27].

This study employed a model of moderate-intensity aerobic exercise administered over 14 days during the recovery phase in mice with ischemic stroke. Treadmill training was found to effectively promote the neurological function recovery in these mice. Experimental results demonstrated that aerobic exercise significantly elevated circulating and intracerebral lactate levels in mice, inducing lactylation of α-tubulin at Lys40 (K40) and thereby leading to alterations in microtubule dynamics. Immunological and behavioral assays further indicated that lactylation on α-tubulin played a pivotal role in neural repair following ischemic stroke. This study reveals a non-metabolic role for lactate in exercise-induced neurological recovery. It provides experimental evidence to elucidate the mechanism of protein lactylation in post-ischemic repair and offers foundational support for developing individualized, precise exercise rehabilitation strategies for stroke.

Materials and methods

The reagents used were Sodium L-Lactate (Cat# 71,718, Sigma-Aldrich), Sodium Dichloroacetate (Cat# 347,795, Sigma-Aldrich), BrdU (Cat# 19–160, Sigma-Aldrich), TRIzol reagent (Cat# 15596026CN, Thermo Fisher Scientific), Maxima Reverse Transcriptase (Cat# EP0742, Thermo Fisher Scientific), Rose Bengal solution (Cat# 330,000, Sigma). The antibody against α-tubulin K40 lactylation was a gift from Dr. Li Lei in ShanghaiTech University, China. The SYBR Green PCR Kit was purchased from Bio-Rad Laboratories, USA. Lactate colorimetric assay kit (KTB1100) was purchased from Abbkine Scientific, Inc (Wuhan, China).

Animals

All C57BL/6 J mice (6–8 weeks of age) were purchased from the Animal Centre at Xuzhou Medical University. The exact number of each experiment is indicated in the relevant results or figure legends. The mice were housed under controlled temperature, humidity, and light conditions (12 h light/dark cycle) with water and rodent chow diet freely available. Anesthesia in mice was induced with 3% isoflurane (R510-22–10, RWD) and maintained at 1.5% isoflurane via a face mask. All mouse experiments were conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) and under an approved IACUC protocol of Xuzhou Medical University (202501T020).

Photothrombotic ischemia

The cortical injury was induced by photothrombotic ischemia as previously described [28]. Briefly, mice were settled in a brain stereotaxic apparatus (RWD Life Sciences, China) with the exposed skull. Then, 10 min after injection of Rose Bengal solution (100 mg/kg, i.p.), the exposed skull was illuminated using a cold laser beam with a 2 mm-diameter and 12,000 lx-illumination intensity (Leica, Germany) for 12 min. The center of the light source was positioned at 1.5 mm lateral to the bregma. Body temperature was maintained at 37 ± 0.5 °C during the whole procedure until the mice recovered. Sham-operated mice were subjected to the same procedure without illumination.

Drug administration

Sodium L-Lactate and Sodium Dichloroacetate (DCA) were dissolved in sterile saline and administered intraperitoneally at concentrations of 1 g/kg for Sodium L-Lactate and 200 mg/kg for Sodium Dichloroacetate in mice [20]. The drug administration timing schedule is indicated in the figure.

Quantification of lactate (L-lactic acid)

The concentration of lactate was quantified by using a micro lactate assay kit following the manual instructions. In brief, the serum or protein lysate was mixed with the reagent buffer and working reagent using a 96-well plate, followed by 37 °C for 10 min. Measured the optical density value at 450 nm wavelength using a microplate reader.

Behavioral tests

An investigator blinded to the treatment assignments evaluated functional and neurological deficits of mice by the Cylinder test, the Corner test, and the Modified Neurological Severity Score (mNSS) as previously described [29–31]. Briefly, for the Cylinder test, the mice were allowed to freely rear in a transparent glass cylinder. The laterality index from a total of 20 rears was calculated as (right-left) / (right + left + both). A score of zero indicates equal use of forelimbs, while a greater positive number indicates a more severe left hemiparesis. For the Corner test, animals were allowed to advance into a 30° corner and exit by turning either to the left or right. Choice of turning was recorded for a total of 10 trials, and a score was calculated as the number of left turns/all trials × 100. The mNSS is a composite of motor, sensory, balance, and reflex tests. It includes motor testing, such as raising the mouse by its tail to assess forelimb flexion, hindlimb flexion, and head movement; the ability to walk straight when placed on the ground; a timed beam balance test; sensory (visual and tactile) tests; and the corneal reflex. Neurological function was graded on a scale of 0 to 14 (normal score = 0; maximal deficit score = 14), with one point awarded for the exhibition of specific abnormal behavior or for lack of a tested reflex.

The morris water maze

The Morris Water Maze (MWM) test, a widely recognized method for evaluating spatial learning and memory, was conducted in a controlled environment to assess the cognitive functions of mice [32]. A video analysis system (Anhui Zhenghua Biologic Apparatus Facilities., Ltd., China) was used to observe and record the swimming trajectories of each mouse. The pool was rendered opaque with edible white pigment to conceal a dark cylindrical platform. An escape platform (10 cm in diameter) was placed in the pool, and the top of the platform was 1.5 cm below the water surface. Mice were trained for 6 days with 4 trials/day (60 s/trial, 30 min inter-trial intervals), and mice were placed into the water from different quadrants on each trial. If the animal could not find the platform within 60 s, they were placed on the platform for 10 s. On the seventh day, a probe test was administered. During the probe test, the platform was removed from the pool, and the mouse was allowed to swim freely for 60 s.

Laser speckle contrast imaging

The cerebral surface blood flow changes were monitored with laser speckle contrast imaging (RFLSI Ⅲ, RWD Life Science, China) before and after exercise training. Briefly, after making an incision in the scalp, the skull of the mice was fully exposed and wetted with saline. Then, the ischemia territory of the right hemisphere was selected, and the mean flux measurements were recorded.

Immunoblotting

Immunoblotting was performed as described previously [31]. The tissue homogenate was prepared using common radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) (Beyotime Biotechnology) buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors at 4 °C and then centrifuged at 12,000 × g. The supernatant was collected for subsequent assays. The protein samples were separated by SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE), transferred to the nitrocellulose membrane (Amersham), and blocked with 5% skim milk for 60 min at room temperature. Primary antibodies were incubated at 4 °C overnight. Primary antibodies to detect the proteins of interest are listed in the part of the reagents and antibodies. After washing with Tris-Buffered Saline with Tween-20 (TBST), the HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Life Technology) were incubated at room temperature for 1 h. Immunoreactive bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (Abbkine), imaged using a Bio-Rad ChemiDoc XRS + imaging system (Bio-Rad Laboratories, USA), and analyzed with ImageJ (NIH).

Immunofluorescent staining

Immunostaining was performed as described previously [31]. For brain immunofluorescence staining analysis, mice were anesthetized and transcardially perfused with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). The brains were dissected, postfixed in 4% PFA at 4 °C for 4–6 h, and then sectioned into 40-μm-thick slices by a vibratome (VT1000S, Leica, Germany). The brain tissue sections were blocked and permeabilized in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) containing 10% normal goat serum and 0.5% Triton X-100 at room temperature for 1 h, and incubated with the indicated primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. After washing with PBS, the samples were incubated with appropriate secondary antibodies conjugated to Alexa Fluor 488 or Alexa Fluor 594 at room temperature for 1 h. The sections were counterstained with 4',6-Diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, Beyotime Biotechnology) for 10 min, followed by washing in PBS. Images were acquired by ZEISS LSM880 confocal microscopy.

Nissl staining

Nissl staining was performed to measure the neuronal loss after ischemic stroke [34]. The 40-μm-thick brain coronal slices of mice were soaked in Nissl staining solution for 5–15 min and then decolorized in 75, 80, 95, and 100% ethanol for 2 min each. Then the slices were sealed and observed under a microscope. Nissl-positive cells were counted with FIJI ImageJ software under 20 × magnification.

RNA-Seq

The ischemic penumbra was isolated from the brain tissues of Sham, exercise-treated, lactate-intervened, and DCA-inhibited mice. Total RNA was isolated from the sample using the RNeasy mini kit (Qiagen, Germany). Paired-end libraries were synthesized by using the TruSeq® RNA Sample Preparation Kit (Illumina, USA). Briefly, the poly-A-containing mRNA molecules were purified using poly-T oligo-attached magnetic beads. Cluster was generated by cBot with the library diluted to 10 pM and then was sequenced on the Illumina Novaseq 6000 (Illumina, USA). The library construction and sequencing were performed at Shanghai Biotechnology Corporation.

Quantitative PCR analysis (qPCR)

Total RNA was isolated from the cortex by TRIzol extraction and converted to cDNA using Maxima reverse transcriptase. cDNA products were amplified in 20 μl of reaction mixture containing the SYBR Green PCR Kit with the primers. The PCR program comprised an initial step at 95 °C (5 min) followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C (15 s) and annealing at 60 °C (1 min). Samples were run in triplicate, and all signals were normalized to the Actin intensity. The relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2− ∆ ∆ Ct method. Statistical analysis

Animal or replicate numbers for each experiment and the results of the statistical analyses are mentioned in the Figure legends. All statistical analysis was performed by GraphPad Prism 10.1.2 (La Jolla, CA, USA). For data following a normal distribution, the Student’s t-test was used for the comparison between two groups. Results

Aerobic exercise elevates protein lactylation levels in mice with ischemic stroke

To investigate whether aerobic exercise-induced lactate is involved in regulating lactylation, we prepared ischemic stroke model mice for studies and combined this with a moderate-intensity aerobic exercise intervention. Previous studies have demonstrated that initiating exercise immediately after model induction in mice not only fails to ameliorate injury but may instead trigger the subsequent cascade of secondary injury, leading to further deterioration of neurological function [34, 35]. Similarly, our findings revealed that the survival rate of mice subjected to exercise starting on day 3 post-ischemic stroke was lower than that of mice commencing exercise on day 6 post-ischemic stroke (Fig. S1A). From postoperative day 6 onwards [36], the mice underwent 14-day moderate-intensity aerobic exercise (30 min daily, 10 m/min), with the running speed determined using a previously established protocol [37], after which analyses were performed (Fig. S1B). To determine the exercise duration in mice, different time points were selected: 0 min, 15 min, 30 min, and 45 min. Blood samples were collected from the inner canthus after exercise cessation to measure serum lactate levels. Results indicated that in mice with ischemic stroke, lactate levels gradually increased following exercise, peaking at 30 min of activity (Fig. 1A). However, concentrations remained within a non-detrimental range for health, with statistically significant differences relative to baseline levels (Fig. 1B). Protein lactylation is an important post-translational modification. However, whether aerobic exercise can induce lactylation remains to be clarified. Accordingly, we assessed pan-lactylation (pan-Kla) levels in the brain tissue of mice following exercise, revealing a significant upregulation of protein lactylation (Fig. 1C and D). Aerobic exercise promotes neurological recovery in mice with ischemic stroke

To investigate the impact of exercise on neurological recovery after ischemic stroke in mice, a series of neurological deficits and functional assessments were conducted after the mice completed a 14-day aerobic exercise regimen. Laser speckle blood flow imaging system (RFLSI Ⅲ, RWD Life Science, China) revealed increased cerebral perfusion in the exercise group compared to controls (Fig. 2A and B). Nissl staining results of mouse brain sections revealed that the number of Nissl-positive neurons in the exercise group was significantly higher than that in the control group (Fig. 2C and D). To further assess neuromotor function and spatial learning/memory in the ischemic stroke mice, behavioral tests were conducted before and after the exercise intervention in both groups. Sensorimotor coordination was evaluated using the mNSS, the cylinder test, and the corner test. Prior to exercise, there was no significant difference in sensorimotor function between the two groups of mice after ischemic stroke. Following the exercise, however, the exercise group showed a marked improvement in motor function (Fig. 2E and G). During the spatial navigation phase of the MWM experiment, mice in the exercise group exhibited significantly shorter escape latency to locate the hidden platform than those in the control group (Fig. 2H and I). In the spatial exploration phase, mice in the exercise group spent more time in the target quadrant and crossed the former platform location more frequently than control mice (Fig. 2J-L). Co-staining for GFAP and NeuN showed a reduction in GFAP-positive cells and an increase in surviving neurons within the ischemic penumbra of the exercise group (Fig. 3A and B). In immunofluorescence staining for Iba1, we observed widespread microglia activation in the control group, with activated cells displaying a morphology characterized by enlarged cell bodies and reduced, shorter branches (Fig. 3C and D, S2A and B). Aerobic exercise induced lactylation of α-tubulin, and facilitates neural repair after stroke

Microtubules are highly dynamic structures that undergo continuous polymerization and depolymerization. Increased tubulin polymerization and microtubule dynamics hold promising potential for axon regeneration and functional recovery after injury [39]. Research indicates that lactylation K40 of α-tubulin promotes changes in microtubule dynamics and accelerates the growth of damaged dendrites [22]. This study found that aerobic exercise can enhance the level of lactylation at K40 on α-tubulin within tissues (Fig. 4A and B). To further investigate the role of aerobic exercise in neural repair, we subsequently examined the expression levels of the neurorestorative markers. We observed increased DCX expression in the hippocampal dentate gyrus (DG) of the exercise group, and the neurite length of neurons was significantly longer than that in the control group (Fig. 4C and F). Similarly, identical findings were observed in the SVZ (Fig. S3). Class III β-Tubulin (Tuj1) expression was increased in the ischemic penumbra of the exercise group (Fig. 4D and E). And Microtubule-associated protein 2 (MAP2) immunofluorescence staining revealed higher MAP2 expression in the exercise group compared to the control group, accompanied by more continuous microtubule structures (Fig. 4G and H). Protein level analysis showed that the neurorestorative markers showed a significant upregulation in the exercise group (Fig. 4I and J). Exogenous lactate intervention enhanced α-tubulin lactylation, promoted hippocampal neurogenesis, and increased dendritic spine density as well as synaptic plasticity in both the penumbra and hippocampus regions

Recent studies have demonstrated that exogenous lactate supplementation can also enhance the level of protein lactylation [40–42]. Based on the preceding experimental findings, we demonstrated that exercise-induced lactate enhanced α-tubulin lactylation and promoted neural function recovery in mice.

To verify whether exogenous lactate intervention achieves similar effects to aerobic exercise, we intraperitoneally injected sodium lactate and the lactate inhibitor DCA into cerebral ischemic mice [20]. DCA can modulate pyruvate dehydrogenase and Lactate Dehydrogenase (LDH) activity to inhibit lactate production [43]. Mice were allocated into the following groups: Control (Ctrl), Aerobic Exercise (AE), DCA, DCA + AE, and Lactate. Both the pharmacological intervention and the aerobic exercise regimen lasted for 14 days (Fig. 5A). Given that lactate promoted neurite outgrowth in immature neurons of the hippocampus, this study further investigated synaptic plasticity. Synaptic plasticity typically manifests as adaptive alterations in synaptic structure and function, including increased neurotransmitter release [44, 45], heightened dendritic spine density [46, 47], and an increase in the number of functional synapses [48]. To clarify the morphological structure of the observed neurons, we employed the Golgi staining technique to visualize these neurons. Results indicated that compared with the control group and the DCA inhibitor group, both the aerobic exercise group and the exogenous lactate intervention group exhibited significantly increased dendritic spine density in hippocampal and penumbral regions (Fig. 6K and M). These results confirm that both aerobic exercise and exogenous lactate promote neurofunctional recovery following ischemic stroke by increasing dendritic spine density and enhancing synaptic plasticity.

Exogenous lactate administration improved neurofunction in mice with ischemic stroke

To further verify the effect of exogenous lactate administration on neural recovery in mice with ischemic stroke, subsequent behavioral tests were performed to assess the neurological function of mice in each group (Fig. 7A). We observed that neurological deficit scores were higher in the DCA and DCA + AE group than those in the Lactate group, suggesting poorer neurological recovery (Fig. 7B). During the first 6 days of place navigation training in the Morris water maze test, the escape latency of the DCA and DCA + AE group was significantly longer than that of the AE group and lactate intervention group, with no statistically significant difference between these two groups and the Ctrl group. This result indicated that both lactate intervention and aerobic exercise shortened the escape latency in mice, reflecting a significant improvement in their spatial learning ability (Fig. 7C and D). In the spatial exploration experiment on day 7, compared with the Ctrl, DCA, and DCA + AE groups, mice in the Lactate and AE groups exhibited superior spatial exploration and memory abilities. These were evidenced by increased average swimming speed, higher frequency of crossings over the original platform location, prolonged dwell time in the target quadrant, and greater total swimming distance (Fig. 7E–H). Exogenous lactate intervention promoted neurological recovery in mice with ischemic stroke, exhibiting effects comparable to those induced by exercise. In contrast, administration of a lactate inhibitor attenuated the neuroprotective effects induced by aerobic exercise. Discussion

Protein lactylation, a newly identified protein PTM in recent years, plays a significant role in the pathogenesis of glycolysis-related diseases [19]. It is currently being actively investigated as a potential therapeutic target for various conditions, including tumors, neurodegenerative diseases, and cardiovascular diseases [51–53]. Here, Aerobic exercise increases elevated lactate levels and α-tubulin K40 lactylation, promotes neurogenesis and synaptic plasticity, attenuates neuroinflammation, and thereby enhances neurological functional recovery in mice with ischemic stroke. Our findings systematically clarify the novel mechanism by which aerobic exercise promotes neurological recovery following ischemic stroke (Fig. 8E). Furthermore, via exogenous lactate administration and intervention with a lactate inhibitor (DCA), we confirmed that lactate and the lactylation it mediates may be key mediators by which aerobic exercise exerts its neuroprotective effects.

Traditionally, lactate has been regarded as the end product of anaerobic metabolism and a marker of metabolic acidosis [54]. Lactylation was first identified in macrophages and directly regulates the activity and function of target proteins by covalently conjugating a lactate group to lysine residues [19]. In this study, aerobic exercise significantly increased lactate levels in the serum of mice after ischemic stroke (Fig. 1B). We performed a total protein blotting using pan-Kla antibody, which revealed a trending increase in lactylation levels in the brain under exercise training (Fig. 1C-F). Currently, the specific lactylation modification of non-histone proteins within brain tissue represents an emerging field of research. Unlike histone lactylation, which primarily modulates gene expression, non-histone lactylation directly regulates the function of key signaling pathway proteins, rendering it critical for elucidating the direct intracellular effects of lactate. Against this backdrop, the present study focused on ischemic stroke mouse models subjected to aerobic exercise intervention and preliminarily identified lactylation of specific non-histone proteins such as α-tubulin, providing novel experimental evidence and mechanistic insights into the direct molecular targets through which exercise promotes neural repair.

One of the core processes in neural injury repair is the remodeling of neuronal structure, which involves the regeneration and extension of axons, a process that is highly dependent on the dynamic assembly of cytoskeletons such as microtubule [55, 56]. Microtubule plus-end polymerization is required for regenerating axons through cycles of polymerization and depolymerization (dynamic instability), which allows microtubules at the growing ends to probe the intracellular environment and to facilitate the projection of regenerating axons back to their original target area [57]. A recent study suggested that HDAC6 may catalyze the lactylation modification of α-tubulin, thereby enhancing microtubule dynamics in neurons [22]. The present study confirmed that aerobic exercise also induces K40 lactylation of α-tubulin in the brain, and this modification is involved in the regulation of microtubule dynamics. (Figs. 4 and 5). Moreover, in both the aerobic exercise group and the exogenous lactate intervention group, the expression of DCX (a marker of immature neurons) in the hippocampal DG, Tuj1, and MAP2 in the ischemic penumbra was markedly upregulated, accompanied by more continuous and well-defined microtubule structures (Figs. 4 and 6). The DCA intervention group, however, reversed the promoting effect of lactate. Therefore, our findings indicate that exercise-derived lactate mediates α-tubulin K40 lactylation, enhances synaptic plasticity, and consequently promotes neural repair following ischemic stroke.

The inflammatory response after ischemic brain injury, particularly the aberrant activation of microglia and astrocytes, serves as a key barrier to neuronal survival, exacerbating neuronal damage and impeding neuronal repair [38]. Research demonstrates that lactate promotes a phenotypic switch of macrophages towards an M2-like phenotype, characterised by low glycolytic and high TCA cycle activity, via mitochondrial metabolism and ACLY-dependent histone acetylation [58]. Lactate-induced lactylation promotes the secretion of anti-inflammatory cytokines by macrophages, thereby suppressing inflammatory responses and driving glial cells towards M2-like polarization [59, 60]. Our study indicates that both the aerobic exercise and the exogenous lactate intervention markedly inhibit the M1 pro-inflammatory polarization of microglia in the ischemic penumbra, attenuate astrocytic scarring (manifested by reduced GFAP expression), and enhance the survival of NeuN-positive neurons in this region (Figs. 3 and 5).

Meanwhile, both the aerobic exercise and the exogenous lactate intervention have been shown to promote neurogenesis in the SVZ, as evidenced by increased numbers of BrdU⁺ and DCX⁺ cells (Figs. 3G and 6F). Microtubule dynamics serve as a key prerequisite for the mitosis of neural stem cells, migration of neuroblasts, and morphological maturation of newborn neurons during adult neurogenesis [61, 62]. Studies have indicated that alterations in microtubule dynamics exert a marked regulatory effect on stem cell proliferation; this effect is characterized by both cell-type specificity and regulation mode dependency (stabilization/depolymerization/enhanced dynamics) and is mediated via multiple molecular pathways [63, 64]. α-tubulin lactylation may exert its effects at multiple stages of neurogenesis by enhancing microtubule dynamics. This indicates that lactylation acts as a central regulatory hub, coordinately regulating two core reparative processes of anti-inflammation and neurogenesis. Previous studies have shown that exogenous lactate holds considerable potential for multi-system repair: it not only promotes the self-renewal of mesenchymal progenitor cells in idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) [65], axonal regeneration and motor function recovery following spinal cord injury [25], but also facilitates angiogenesis and neurogenesis in intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) models [66] and accelerates the tissue repair after pancreatic injury [67]. Our findings indicate that exogenous lactate intervention and exercise exert comparable effects on neurological recovery in mice with ischemic stroke. Both interventions increase lactate levels, attenuate neuroinflammation, promote neurogenesis and synaptic plasticity, and improve neurological outcomes (Figs. 5 and 8). We further demonstrate that when endogenous lactate production was inhibited with the lactate generation inhibitor DCA, the neuroprotective effects of exercise were significantly attenuated or even abolished (Fig. 8). Collectively, our results confirm that exercise-induced endogenous lactate is one of the key signaling molecules mediating the neuroprotective effects of exercise.

This study consistently demonstrates that both aerobic exercise and exogenous lactate supplementation markedly improved motor coordination, spatial learning, and memory functions in mice with ischemic stroke, as assessed by the mNSS score, cylinder test, corner test, and Morris water maze test (Figs. 2 and 7). To further clarify the mechanisms underlying neural function improvement, our Golgi staining results revealed that the neuronal dendritic spine density in the hippocampus and ischemic penumbra was significantly increased in both the aerobic exercise and exogenous lactate intervention groups, providing direct evidence to support enhanced synaptic plasticity. (Fig. 6). As a critical structure for receiving synaptic signals, the density and morphology of dendritic spines are closely associated with learning and memory abilities [46, 68]. The remodeling of dendritic spines also relies on the coordinated regulation of the actin and microtubule cytoskeletons [57]. Our study suggests that α-tubulin lactylation levels increase the dendritic spine density in the ischemic penumbra and hippocampus, thereby promoting synaptic plasticity. Subsequently, this study employed RNA-seq combined with bioinformatics analysis to identify DEGs in the ischemic penumbra (Fig. 8). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis demonstrated that neurorepair-related pathways were significantly enriched in mice with ischemic stroke following exogenous lactate intervention, while GO analysis indicated significant changes in the expression of neuron-, synapse-, and dendrite-associated genes. Furthermore, we validated the upregulated mRNA expression levels of 6 of these genes using q-PCR.

In conclusion, aerobic exercise increases circulating lactate levels, whereby lactate functions as a key signaling molecule to induce α-tubulin lactylation at K40. This modification in turn enhances neurogenesis and synaptic plasticity, ultimately improving neurological recovery in mice with ischemic stroke. Lactate has emerged as a promising and viable therapeutic candidate, and its therapeutic efficacy in a range of diseases has been increasingly corroborated by clinical studies [22, 69–71]. This study not only deepens our understanding of the non-metabolic functions of lactate but also provides a novel theoretical basis for the molecular mechanisms underlying clinical exercise rehabilitation in ischemic stroke.

Research limitations and future prospects

Given that sex differences in exercise-induced lactate have been reported in humans, the present study used only male mice, and thus our findings have not yet been validated in female mice. The current conclusions are based solely on animal studies and require further support from clinical data. Furthermore, beyond α-tubulin, aerobic exercise also induces lactylation of other key target proteins. How these proteins synergistically modulate neurorepair represents a promising direction for future research. For stroke patients unable to undergo exercise therapy, the development of targeted lactylation-mimetic therapeutic strategy compounds may serve as an exercise-mimetic strategy to improve their functional outcomes.
