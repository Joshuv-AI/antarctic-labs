Stem cell therapies for treatment of diabetes mellitus
Authors: Feng Guo, Yi Song, Guijun Qin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe increasing global prevalence of diabetes mellitus (DM) necessitates the development of innovative therapeutic strategies that effectively address its symptoms and alleviate its enduring pathological consequences. Stem cells (SCs), which have biological advantages such as multidirectional differentiation capabilities and immune regulatory functions, exhibit tremendous potential for diabetes treatment. Human embryonic SCs or induced pluripotent SC-derived insulin-producing cells offer a promising source for islet cell replenishment in the treatment of insulin-depleted diabetes; however, immune rejection and safety concerns remain significant challenges in further clinical applications. Transplantation of hematopoietic and mesenchymal SCs also shows remarkable potential for the treatment of diabetes. These cells possess the ability to modulate the immune system, stimulate endogenous β-cell regeneration, and preserve residual β-cell function, ultimately leading to enhanced pancreatic islet function and reduced insulin resistance. However, safety and efficacy considerations for SCs remain paramount. This review comprehensively summarizes the recent advancements and molecular mechanisms of SCs in the treatment of DM, underscoring the feasibility, benefits, and current limitations of SC therapy for DM, and offering a lucid perspective on their therapeutic possibilities.

Introduction

The rapidly increasing prevalence of diabetes mellitus (DM) worldwide has become a major health concern over the past few decades; however, its control rate remains unsatisfactory.[1] Type 1 DM (T1DM), accounting for approximately 10% of diabetes cases, is characterized by an autoimmune-mediated destruction of insulin-producing β cells in the pancreatic islets. Insulin was originally thought to be a potential cure for T1DM; however, exogenous insulin therapy has proven inadequate in precisely mimicking endogenous insulin production and regulating metabolic disturbances, thus exacerbating the development of chronic diabetic complications.[2] Type 2 DM (T2DM) constitutes the majority of DM cases. Scholars have conducted in-depth explorations into the pathophysiology of T2DM, discovering mechanisms, such as impaired insulin sensitivity in the liver and adipose tissue, dysfunction of brain neurotransmitters, microecological imbalances, inflammation, and immune dysregulation, involved in the occurrence and development of T2DM.[3] Based on these mechanisms, various new hypoglycemic drugs have been developed and achieved good clinical efficacy. Although these medications can partially improve insulin sensitivity and hyperglycemia, they cannot fully reverse insulin resistance and the decline in β-cell function; therefore, many patients still require exogenous insulin injections for treatment.[4] Islet transplantation effectively restores the number of insulin-secreting cells in patients with diabetes by implanting healthy islet cells, ultimately leading to reconstitution of islet function. In the early Edmonton protocol, the rate of insulin independence among patients with T1DM was only 14% at two years posttransplantation.[5] However, in recent years, the insulin independence rate has increased to 42% at two years posttransplantation through refined immunosuppression, anticoagulation, antiplatelet therapy, and various other therapeutic approaches.[6] A notable milestone was achieved in 2023 when the United States (US) Food and Drug Administration approved the listing of Lantidra, a revolutionary allogeneic islet therapy tailored specifically for the treatment of T1DM, marking a substantial leap forward in diabetic cell therapy.[7] Nevertheless, this methodology is not without challenges, including the limited availability of pancreatic donor resources, the intricacies of surgical procedures, and the inherent risks associated with immunosuppression and its potential complications.[8] Therefore, devising alternative strategies is imperative for the effective treatment of diabetes and the maintenance of its therapeutic effects, giving rise to the emergence of stem cell therapy for diabetes. Given that a single β cell can detect extracellular glucose variations and secrete insulin accordingly, theoretically, generating adequate insulin-producing cells (IPCs) from human embryonic stem cells (hESCs) or human pluripotent stem cells (hPSCs) and implanting them could potentially cure diabetes associated with pancreatic dysfunction.[9] Technological advancements have brought this vision closer to clinical reality. Despite the inability of mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) to differentiate into IPCs, they exhibit potential in the treatment of diabetes and its complications. MSCs can enhance insulin sensitivity, reverse β-cell dedifferentiation, and facilitate the repair of damaged pancreatic β cells [Table 1]. However, challenges persist, including inconsistent treatment outcomes and limited therapeutic effects.[10] Although the future for SCs therapy for diabetes remains long and arduous, its future is promising.

Stem Cell–Derived Insulin–Producing Cells and Reconstruction of Islet Function

Embryonic stem cells (ESCs) and induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) are the two most widely used types of pluripotent SCs that can develop IPCs [Figure 1]. ESCs, which are primitive cells originating from the blastocyst, have the potential to differentiate into cells of the endoderm, mesoderm, and ectoderm. Considered as the gold standard for pluripotent cells, ESCs offer distinct advantages such as standardized protocols for maintenance and differentiation, along with the capability to correct heritable gene defects.[11] However, their application is limited by immunogenic concerns, ethical issues, and the risk of teratoma formation. iPSCs are derived from reprogrammed somatic cells and possess differentiation abilities similar to those of ESCs. The use of iPSCs offers advantages such as reduced ethical concerns and the potential to avoid allogeneic immune responses by utilizing autologous cells.[12] However, challenges, such as low reprogramming efficiency, risk of teratoma formation, and potential genetic and epigenetic abnormalities, need to be addressed.

Embryonic stem cells

ESCs have the utmost potential for cell differentiation and are regarded as a proper candidate for islet β-cell regeneration therapy. Initial studies primarily focused on murine ESCs (mESCs). In vitro studies have validated the potential of mESCs to differentiate into pancreatic cells exhibiting endocrine traits; however, the efficiency of this process has been notably low, and the differentiation process is difficult to manage precisely.[13,14] Kahan et al[15] showed that MSCs did not achieve optimal differentiation in vitro until they were cocultured with other islet cells, which is consistent with the finding that the differentiation rate increased after MSCs were transplanted into mice and continued to develop in vivo. These results indicate the significance of the microenvironment during ESC differentiation, including local growth factors, cellular interactions, and signal transduction.

Advancements in the differentiation of mESCs into pancreatic islet-like cells have similarly spurred additional research into the differentiation of hESCs into stem cell (SC)-derived IPCs. Brolén et al[16] successfully differentiated hESCs into pancreatic and endocrine progenitors; however, these cells failed to produce insulin or respond to hyperglycemia. Cotransplantation of differentiated hESCs with dorsal pancreatic tissue from mouse embryos led to the formation of β-cell-like cell clusters with the functions of proinsulin synthesis, C-peptide processing, and nuclear localization of critical transcription factors, echoing the differentiation observed in previous mESC studies.[16] D’Amour et al[17] first differentiated hESCs into insulin-secreting cells in vitro; however, the cells showed a weak response to glucose, indicating the need for further maturation. The in vitro culture protocol for ESCs has been continuously improved; Rezania et al[18] successfully utilized a four-step protocol to differentiate hESCs into pancreatic progenitors, which further developed into mature pancreatic endocrine cells in vivo. Subsequently, the same research group developed a seven-step protocol, mimicking embryological development, to differentiate hESCs into IPCs.[19] In 2014, Melton developed a six-step approach to amplify functional SC-IPC, which was a milestone in the field of SC-IPC induction protocols.[20] Some growth factors and cytokines are considered crucial mediators in the differentiation of hESCs into IPCs, whereas some chemicals such as (-)-indolactam V have been shown to turn hESCs into β cells.[21] Transplanting differentiated cells into mice produces biological effects that mimic those observed in human islet cells. A study revealed a glucose-dependent insulin secretion response after 2 weeks, with blood glucose levels returning to normal after 8–12 weeks in diabetic mice.[22] Recently, the Melton research group further improved the purity of islet β-like cell isolation to 80% through a two-step enrichment method of cell reaggregation and anti-CD49a magnetic bead screening.[23]

Other studies have transplanted in vitro-differentiated ESC-derived pancreatic progenitor cells into recipients, whose subsequent differentiation and maturation are driven by the host in vivo microenvironment. Compared with ESC-derived β-like cells, pancreatic progenitor cells have a higher differentiation rate in vitro, lower oxygen consumption rate in vivo, and a higher survival rate; however, they need to undergo a long period of differentiation and development in vivo.[24] Previous studies have shown that pancreatic progenitor cells differentiate into islet-like tissues 2–3 months after transplantation into mice, which can effectively prevent and manage diabetes occurrence and progression.[18] However, 15–45% of transplanted mice develop teratoma-like lesions, and immunogenicity is another challenge in the transplantation of hESC-derived IPCs. Considering the immunogenicity and potential tumorigenicity of pancreatic progenitor cells, ViaCyte, an American company, developed a double-membrane encapsulation device that encapsulates human islet progenitor cells and implants them subcutaneously (Encapsulated Pancreatic EndocrineProgenitor Cell Therapy, PEC-EncapTM), thereby exerting an immune isolation effect.[25] Although some studies have successfully demonstrated the feasibility of engrafting stem cell-derived endoderm cells from ViaCyte in patients with T1DM and their differentiation into islet cells, none have reported insulin independence among these patients.[26,27] The subsequently developed direct vascularization pancreatic endocrine progenitor cell therapy, designated PEC-DirectTM, is engineered from the aforementioned device with an integrated vascular-permissive structure that enhances cell engraftment, thereby boosting graft vascular perfusion and long-term graft survival. Currently, both PEC-EncapTM and PEC-DirectTM have been approved by the US Food and Drug Administration for phase I/II clinical studies (NCT02239354, NCT03163511) for the treatment of T1DM. These studies will provide an important basis for clinical safety and efficacy.

Notably, differences in gene expression profiles, microstructure, and intracellular calcium ion concentration persist between ESC-derived β-like cells and primary human islet cells. Further exploration of the key regulatory molecules of islet β-like cell differentiation and development, and optimizing the combination scheme of different inducing factors or small-molecule substances to more precisely regulate ESC differentiation is necessary. In addition, optimization of the composition ratio of different endocrine cells and better simulation of the anatomy and physiology of human islets are urgent problems to be solved in future research. Recent studies have utilized isogenic β-like cells derived from hESCs to systematically investigate the functions of genes linked to T2DM using clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR) technology. Among the candidate genes, the hepatocyte nuclear factor 4α gene plays a critical role in the development and function of β cells. The ablation of hepatocyte nuclear factor 4α leads to widespread alterations in both transcriptome and chromatin accessibility, highlighting its significant regulatory role in β-cell-related gene expression and cellular function. Additionally, the study identifies four genes related to insulin production (CP, RNASE1, PCSK1N, and GSTA2) and two genes sensitive to β-cell lipotoxicity (TAGLN and DHRS2).[28] Moreover, inhibition of the wingless-related integration site (WNT) pathway, particularly by adenomatous polyposis coli in the endocrine domain, is pivotal for islet formation in vivo and increases the proportion of differentiated endocrine cells in vitro.[29] Other studies have confirmed the functions of some vital genes in hESC differentiation into IPCs.[30,31] The integration of functional traits and genomic alterations aids in the accurate identification of putative causal variants and genes that influence the differentiation of ESCs into IPCs, thereby offering invaluable insights into the genetic structure of DM.

Induced pluripotent stem cells

In 2006, Japanese scholars Takahashi et al[32] successfully transferred four transcription factors, Oct3/4, Sox2, c-Myc, and Klf4, into mouse fibroblasts using adenoviral vectors, transforming them into SCs with biological characteristics similar to those of ESCs. These cells were termed iPSCs. Since then, researchers have successfully transformed similar ESC-like iPSCs by transferring Oct4, Sox2, c-Myc, and Klf4 into human fibroblasts or by transferring other factors such as Oct4, Sox2, Nanog, and Lin28 into somatic cells.[33,34] Subsequently, researchers replaced Fbx15 with Nanog for screening and finally obtained a Nanog-positive iPSC cell line. This cell line closely resembles mESCs in terms of cell morphology, growth status, marker expression, and teratoma formation upon subcutaneous transplantation and shares analogous DNA methylation patterns, gene expression profiles, and capacity to generate chimeric animals.[35] Researchers have established iPSC cell lines from rats, rhesus monkeys, and humans, demonstrating that iPSCs can be derived from a wide range of mammalian species.[36,37] This also proves that species for which it is difficult to establish ESC lines can be established using iPSC technology. Research on iPSCs has expanded, and some breakthroughs have been achieved. For instance, disease-specific human induced pluripotent stem cells (hiPSCs) have been established,[38,39] virus-free iPSCs have been efficiently prepared using transposon-mediated transgene integration,[40] and previously introduced transcription factor genes have been successfully removed from iPSCs.[41] Chemical stimulation using small molecules is an alternative method for manipulating cell fate in a straightforward and highly controllable manner. Recently, researchers have succeeded in chemically reprogramming human somatic cells into human chemically iPSCs (CiPSCs), which exhibit essential characteristics similar to ESCs, while eliminating the initial reliance on exogenous gene transfer.[42]

At the cellular level, iPSCs have unparalleled advantages over other SCs types. First, iPSCs and ESCs have similarities in morphology, growth patterns, epigenetics, and genome-wide expression patterns and possess robust self-renewal and pluripotent differentiation potentials.[33] Second, iPSC acquisition obviates the need to destroy embryos, thereby avoiding legal and ethical obstacles. iPSCs are abundant, dependable, and easily obtainable. Recently, a research team reprogrammed human peripheral blood cells to generate iPSCs, marking a significant milestone in developing the most convenient and efficient approach for acquiring SCs for clinical applications.[43] In addition, iPSCs can be derived from patients’ somatic cells, thereby ensuring genotype compatibility and mitigating immune rejection upon transplantation. This innovation offers patients customized autologous SCs that are tailored to their unique needs.[44] Moreover, iPSCs offer an optimal cellular model for exploring the regulatory mechanisms underlying SCs pluripotency and elucidating the functions of individual transcription factors at the primitive SCs stage, thereby exhibiting potential in diverse biomedical domains.[45]

iPSCs hold significant value in studying pancreatic embryogenesis, the pathogenesis of diabetes, the screening of hypoglycemic drugs, and gene therapy, and serve as a powerful tool in diabetes therapy research. Alipio et al[46] successfully reprogrammed mouse fibroblasts into iPSCs and differentiated them into β-like cells using retroviral transcription factors. These cells secreted insulin in response to glucose and effectively corrected hyperglycemia in mouse models of both T1DM and T2DM, providing an initial proof of principle for further research and applications.[46] Subsequently, researchers isolated and transplanted iPSCs from nonobese diabetic (NOD) mice, a mouse model that shares similar pathogenic characteristics with human T1DM cells. After iPSC transplantation into the kidney capsule of diabetic mice, these cells differentiated into IPCs in a specific microenvironment. Additionally, iPSCs can promote endogenous β-cell function by enhancing blood supply and regulating the islet microenvironment. Under high glucose and potassium chloride stimulation, these cells secrete and release insulin. However, when the transplanted iPSCs are removed, blood glucose levels increase rapidly.[47] Researchers have observed the role of key regulatory factors in the differentiation of mouse iPSCs into IPCs, including pancreatic duodenal homeobox-1, neurogenic differentiation factor 1, and musculoaponeurotic fibrosarcoma oncogene homolog. These three genes jointly regulate pancreatic development and insulin gene transcription by binding to the upstream promoter regulatory regions A3, C1 and E1 of insulin. Both fasting glucose and glucose tolerance tests in diabetic mice have shown that transplanted iPSC exert good therapeutic effects in diabetic mice.[48] Zhu et al[49] confirmed that iPSCs isolated from rhesus monkeys can also differentiate into cells with insulin secretion functions, further proving that iPSCs from various mammals can be induced into IPCs. These studies provide a solid foundation for clinical application research.

The production of IPCs from hPSCs involves a multistage process that systematically utilizes small molecules and growth factors, with each phase targeting specific developmental pathways to mimic human pancreatic development.[50] Initially, hPSCs were induced into the definitive endoderm by utilizing activin A, an activator of the WNT pathway, frequently with CHIR99021. Subsequently, keratinocyte growth factor was used to guide the definitive endoderm cells into a primitive gut tube state. Notably, certain methods incorporate additional agents targeting the WNT and transforming growth factor-beta signaling pathways to enhance specification of the primitive gut tube.[51] To generate pancreatic progenitor cells, further activation of protein kinase C signaling with 12-Deoxyphorbol 13-phenylacetate and retinoic acid signaling is required along with the concurrent inhibition of bone morphogenetic protein signaling using LDN193189 and sonic hedgehog signaling with smoothened antagonist 1. Additionally, epidermal growth factor and nicotinamide effectively enhance the production of pancreatic duodenal homeobox-1+/ NK6 homeobox 1+ pancreatic progenitors.[52] In the later stages of stem cell-derived islet differentiation, a mixture of retinoic acid, smoothened antagonist 1, activin receptor-like kinase 5 inhibitor II, and LatA combined with thyroid hormone and γ-secretase inhibitor was used to induce endocrine cells.[53] Finally, mature stem cell-derived β cells were generated using aurora kinase inhibitor.[54] Several reported protocols exhibit diversification using different compounds in later stages of stem cell-derived islet (SC-islet) differentiation.[55] Other protocols focused on improving SC-islet function have also been reported. Velazco et al[56] introduced a differentiation method highlighting the regulation of transforming growth factor-beta signaling, management of cellular cluster size, and utilization of enriched serum-free media for culturing. This aimed to generate SC-islets capable of producing insulin in response to glucose stimulation, accompanied with distinct first- and second-phase insulin release dynamics. Nair et al[57] have upgraded cell culture conditions to induce enriched β-cell clusters akin to primary human β cells, which exhibit dynamic insulin secretion, enhanced mitochondrial energization, and heightened calcium signaling. Another group presented a simplified six-stage planar differentiation methodology to generate stem cell-derived β cells from different cell lines, which exhibits the insulin-producing capability in response to glucose stimulation.[58] Other researchers have confirmed that the feed/fast cycle and circadian rhythm are involved in the maturation of human iPSC (hiPSC)-derived islets.[59] New technologies such as single-cell sequencing have provided new insights into pluripotent stem cell-derived islet differentiation, maturation, and biology.[60]

Significant progress has also been made in the research on transforming hiPSCs into IPCs. In 2008, Park et al[61] used induction technology to derive iPSCs from skin fibroblasts of patients with juvenile-onset T1DM, thereby providing an opportunity to reveal normal formation and pathological changes in human islets in vitro. Subsequently, iPSCs derived from patients with T1DM were successfully differentiated by reprogramming their adult fibroblasts into cells capable of producing insulin.[62] Reports documented that iPSCs could be generated from epidermal keratinocytes isolated from patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), and these iPSCs were capable of differentiating into insulin-secreting islet-like clusters.[63] Tateishi et al[64] successfully generated islet-like cell clusters from skin fibroblast-derived iPSCs, following a procedure by which hESCs were successfully differentiated into insulin-secreting islet-like clusters. Other researchers have successfully developed a protocol using miR-186 and miR-375 to differentiate hiPSCs into islet-like cell clusters in vitro, which showed morphological changes, increased pancreatic-specific transcription factor expression, and insulin secretion in response to glucose.[65] Furthermore, the generation of IPCs from iPSCs derived from patients with T1DM and T2DM offers a pluripotent platform for specialized diagnostic and therapeutic strategies.[66,67] In addition, iPSCs have been isolated from patients with clearly diagnosed maturity-onset diabetes of the young subtypes, serving as a research tool for β cell function and pathological changes in special genotypes.[68]

Recently, researchers generated islets from human CiPSCs (hCiPSC-islets) and demonstrated their effectiveness in diabetic nonhuman primates. The results showed that all recipients exhibited decreased fasting blood glucose levels and increased postprandial C-peptide levels. The average amount of exogenous insulin used decreased by 49%, whereas hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) levels decreased by approximately 2% in four macaques during the long-term follow-up period.[69] This study confirms the potential of hPSC-islets in the treatment of diabetes, indicating significant progress in clinical application. In 2021, Vertex Pharmaceuticals registered its product VX-880 for phase I/II clinical trials, intending to recruit 17 patients with T1DM who had severe hypoglycemia, to test the safety, tolerability, and effectiveness of VX-880 when injected into the hepatic portal vein. The initial results from the first patient showed significant improvement. Specifically, on the 90th day after receiving half the therapeutic dose of VX-880, the patient’s HbA1c level dropped from 8.6% to 7.2%, accompanied by an increase in the fasting C-peptide level from undetectable levels to 0.78 ng/mL. A mixed meal tolerance test revealed a peak C-peptide of 1.68 ng/mL. Moreover, his insulin use decreased by 90%, and no serious adverse events were reported. The second patient was a woman. Her HbA1c level decreased from a baseline of 7.5% to 7.1% on the 150th day, her time in range (TIR) increased from a baseline of 35.9% to 51.9%, and her exogenous insulin requirement decreased by 30%.[70] These results provide the first evidence of the remarkable efficacy of induced terminal differentiation of β cells in patients with T1DM. In 2024, two research groups in China made clinical breakthroughs in the treatment of diabetes using iPSCs. A research group from Shanghai Changzheng Hospital reprogrammed the peripheral blood mononuclear cells of patients with T2DM into autologous iPSCs. These iPSCs were further induced to differentiate into endodermal SCs, which were ultimately induced to form endoderm stem cell-derived islet tissue and were transplanted back into the patient. Following the surgery, the patient discontinued insulin after 3 months, acarbose after 12 months, and metformin after 14 months, and has remained insulin-free for 33 months without experiencing any severe adverse reactions. Significant improvements were also observed in fasting and postprandial C-peptide levels, and glycated hemoglobin decreased to 4.6%, indicating the effective restoration of islet function.[71] Another thrilling research accomplishment emerged from the collaborative efforts between Peking University and Nankai University. Wang et al[72] successfully transplanted islets derived from CiPSCs beneath the abdominal anterior rectus sheath of a patient with T1DM, marking the first such procedure. The patient attained persistent insulin independence 75 days after transplantation and sustained stable glycemic control for a year, with a consistent TIR glycemic level of over 98% and a stable HbA1C level lower than 5.7%. No signs of transplant-related abnormalities were observed during follow up. The success of these two studies not only demonstrates the enormous clinical application potential of iPSCs in the treatment of diabetes but also provides new avenues for the application of SCs therapy in other diseases, marking a significant milestone in science.

Basic and clinical investigations on the differentiation and induction of islets from iPSCs have further enriched and complemented the SCs technology approach for diabetes treatment [Table 2]. Nevertheless, researchers still need to explore and tackle pertinent issues in this domain before it can be applied for clinical treatment. (1) Given the intricate nature of the SC–IPC differentiation process, a risk of cells accidentally differentiating into hazardous off-target cells, such as undifferentiated progenitor cells, exists. Numerous studies have indicated the possibility of pancreatic progenitor cells erroneously differentiating into teratomas and exocrine pancreatic cells.[18] The following measures can help mitigate the generation of potentially harmful off-target cells and prevent the transplantation of problematic cells into patients: First, improving differentiation protocols and procedures, such as precise control of differentiation conditions (temperature, pH, oxygen concentration, and type and concentration of growth factors), and optimization of the differentiation efficiency of β-like cells. Notably, small-molecule compounds drive SCs toward specific intended cell fates, purifying β-like cell populations and minimizing undifferentiated and off-target cell contaminants, which mitigates transplant-related risks including teratoma generation and abnormal tissue formation in vivo. Second, gene editing techniques such as CRISPR/Cas9 allow for the precise modification of gene sequences in SCs, thereby eliminating or inhibiting gene expression that may lead to the formation of off-target cells. Third, a combination of flow cytometry or magnetic bead separation can be used to identify and isolate β-like cells. Finally, after transplanting iPSC- or ESC-derived β-like cells into the body, regular monitoring is necessary to evaluate the survival, differentiation, and functional status of the cells. (2) Similar to allogeneic transplantation, transplanted SC-islets are likely to be recognized and attacked by the host immune system, resulting in graft rejection. Furthermore, despite sharing the same genetic material as the patient’s own somatic cells, IPCs derived from iPSCs induced by these cells still face the risk of being targeted by the autoimmune system because T1DM is an autoimmune disease. The application of encapsulation materials has emerged as a promising therapeutic approach.[73] Studies have shown that convective nutrient transport enhances the loading capacity and cell viability of macroencapsulation devices, demonstrating rapid glucose equilibration and physiological insulin secretion in hyperglycemic rats, thus paving the way for optimizing the design for long-term clinical efficacy.[74] In addition, genetic engineering techniques can be used to overcome immune rejection challenges.[75] (3) Despite the establishment and differentiation of individual-, disease-, and patient-specific hiPSCs into IPCs through the induction of specific microenvironments in the body, the precise regulatory signaling pathways and molecular mechanisms remain elusive.[76] This uncertainty constitutes one of the challenges and focal points in global scientific research. The aging of iPSCs after successful transplantation, induction, and growth remains a concern. Following a series of somatic gene reorganizations, certain cellular protein levels decrease, posing challenges in establishing a long-term stable cell system and achieving permanent cell growth. Although some researchers have suggested inhibiting the Rb and P53 pathways or downregulating telomerase activity to counteract fibroblast aging,[77] further investigation is warranted to ascertain whether differentiation-induced iPSCs inherently possess the ability to delay cell aging and whether direct anti-aging treatments are necessary for these cells.

Stem Cells Improve the Immune Microenvironment and Restore Pancreatic Islet Function

Hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs)

HSCs, which originate from blood and immune cells, have strong self-renewal capability and multilineage differentiation potential and are widely used in the clinical treatment and research of hematological and autoimmune diseases. The immunoregulatory potential of HSCs has been previously investigated in NOD mice. The infusion of HSCs with mixed chimerism promoted the deletion of autoreactive T cells in the thymus, thereby preventing the onset of diabetes in NOD mice.[78] Given that common major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class II polymorphisms in patients with T1DM and NOD mice elevate T1DM risk, deleting autoreactive T cells that are uninvolved in the response by genetically modifying MHC class II molecules via lentiviral delivery holds promise for new T1DM therapies.[79] Furthermore, HSCs can be genetically engineered to induce immune tolerance or replace disease-susceptibility alleles, and studies are exploring whether genetic engineering or pharmacological modulation can repair altered HSCs in diabetic NOD mice.[80] In light of this, studies have investigated whether genetic engineering or pharmacological modulation can repair potential alterations in HSCs of diabetic NOD mice, particularly by addressing elevated C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 12 (CXCL12) levels in bone marrow HSCs, which may disrupt HSC and Treg trafficking in the periphery and promote T1DM.[81] Studies have also revealed a deficiency in the immune checkpoint programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) in HSCs of NOD mice. Restoring PD-L1 expression in HSCs by genetic engineering or pharmacological modulation reverses diabetes in NOD mice by inhibiting autoimmune responses.[82] These approaches have paved new ways for the development of HSC-based diabetes therapy interventions.

To date, encouraging results have been achieved using autologous nonmyeloablative HSC transplantation (AHSCT). In 2014, three research centers summarized the follow-up data of 65 patients with T1DM with an average follow-up period of 48 months, further confirming that AHSCT can induce complete clinical remission in 59% of patients with an average of 12 months off insulin.[83] Other clinical trials have also demonstrated that AHSCT significantly contributes to insulin independence and maintains impressive glycemic control over both short- and mid-term follow-ups.[84] Interestingly, patients newly diagnosed with T1DM who underwent AHSCT demonstrated not just preserved β-cell function but also a reduction in microvascular complications compared with patients in the control group.[85] Long-term follow-up analyses of patients with T1DM who underwent autologous HSC transplantation are limited, and a few studies have shown that only a small percentage of patients resumed insulin treatment for diabetes after AHSCT.[86] To determine the differences in the effects of HSC transplantation, researchers have established distinct immune profiles between patients in short and prolonged remission. In particular, patients in prolonged remission tend to have lower frequencies of islet-specific autoreactive CD8 (+) T cells, accompanied by a noticeable increase in immunoregulatory T cells.[87] This advanced therapeutic approach has demonstrated significant potential for effectively treating a range of hematological conditions, offering new hope to patients seeking innovative treatment options.

HSCs are the first type of SCs to be used for the clinical treatment of T1DM. However, the following issues need to be addressed to further promote its clinical application: (1) Strategies to enhance the safety of HSC transplantation. A meta-analysis reported a 52% incidence of adverse reactions, mainly affecting the bone marrow, digestive tract, and immune system. Fatal cases of Pseudomonas aeruginosa sepsis have been reported abroad.[88] Recent studies have shown significantly reduced side effects by improving the nonmyeloablative pretreatment regimen, and all subjects completed treatment in the transplantation clinic.[89] Therefore, optimizing technical plans and improving safety are key to promoting clinical transformation. (2) The efficacy mechanism of AHSCT is mainly related to inducing immune tolerance and repairing damaged islets; however, research evidence is still lacking on whether HSC can differentiate into islet β-like cells after transplantation. (3) Previous studies have reported heterogeneity in the clinical efficacy of nonmyeloablative autologous peripheral HSC transplantations. Age, disease course, history of diabetic ketoacidosis, baseline islet function, and number of islet autoantibodies are potential factors that affect efficacy.[83] Further prospective studies are needed to provide a scientific basis for predicting efficacy and standardizing the indications for clinical application.

Mesenchymal stem/stromal cells

In 1966, Friedenstein et al[90] reported the pluripotent behavior of precursor cells in mouse bone marrow for the first time. In 1991, Caplan et al[91] introduced the characteristics, sources, and isolation of MSCs as well as a new therapeutic technique using MSCs for self-cell repair. MSCs are found in almost all organs and tissues, including the umbilical cord, periosteum and cartilage, liver, pancreas, spleen, thymus, muscle, fat, and amniotic fluid.[92] Furthermore, MSCs have been used in basic research and clinical applications for various diseases.[93] In 2006, the International Society for Cellular Therapy proposed the following minimum criteria for the standardization of MSCs: (1) MSCs must have plastic adherence capability when cultured in standard conditions; (2) MSCs must express the surface markers CD105, CD73, and CD90, and be lack of CD45, CD34, CD14, CD11b, CD79α, CD19, or human leukocyte antigen-DR (HLA-DR); (3) MSCs must have the potential to differentiate into adipocytes, chondrocytes, and osteoblasts in vitro.[94] In 2018, a nature commentary questioned the accuracy of MSC definition and differentiation potential, highlighting inconsistencies in molecular identification across studies and significant variations in gene profiles and differentiation capabilities among MSCs of different origins.[95] In the field of diabetes treatment, bone marrow-derived MSCs (BM-MSCs), adipose-derived MSCs (AD-MSCs), and umbilical cord-derived MSCs (UC-MSCs) are commonly used. Although numerous studies have focused on MSCs, AD-MSCs and UC-MSCs demonstrate several advantages: (1) BM-MSCs are obtained through invasive procedures like bone marrow aspiration, which can cause discomfort and pose a risk of infection; (2) AD-MSCs and UC-MSCs, on the other hand, can be easily sourced from autologous umbilical cord and adipose tissue, involving fewer ethical and legal complexities; (3) Owing to their distinct tissue origins, AD-MSCs and UC-MSCs exhibit superior in vitro proliferation capabilities over BM-MSCs, giving them an advantage in cell production; (4) In addition, studies have shown that AD-MSCs and UC-MSCs display reduced heterogeneity, ensuring more consistent therapeutic effects across different batches.[96,97]

Researchers believe that owing to the damaged chemotaxis and multi-differentiation potential of MSCs, they can migrate to damaged pancreatic islets and differentiate into IPCs. This was reinforced by the observation that MSC infusion increased islet cell clusters and improved C-peptide levels, potentially leading to an improvement in blood glucose disorders.[98] However, Ezquer et al[99] demonstrated that the therapeutic effect of intravenous administration of MSCs in T1DM mice was unrelated to transdifferentiation, but restored the balance of the immunological microenvironment of islets. Recent studies have increasingly emphasized the need to elucidate the mechanisms of action of MSCs in treating DM. Studies conducted both domestically and internationally have used MSCs derived from various sources to treat NOD mice. These studies consistently demonstrated that MSCs can prevent, delay, or even reverse the occurrence and progression of autoimmune diabetes. Although most studies agree that MSCs fail to differentiate into IPCs in vivo, their therapeutic mechanisms are primarily attributed to tissue homing, damage repair, and immune modulation.[100] BM-MSCs, when administered via the tail vein to NOD mice, do not only exhibit high distribution and prolonged residence time in the pancreas, indicating their homing ability to damaged islets, but also significantly increase the number of regulatory T cells in both the islets and peripheral circulation through further immunological mechanisms, thereby correcting the imbalance between proinflammatory and anti-inflammatory cytokines, ultimately leading to the alleviation of insulitis and improvement in islet function and blood glucose levels.[101] The interaction between AD-MSCs and immune cells results in anti-inflammatory effects, specifically manifesting as the downregulation of interleukin-1β (IL-1β), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), and interleukin-6 (IL-6), while also upregulating interleukin-10 (IL-10) and prostaglandin E2 (PGE2).[102] AD-MSC transplantation promotes β-cell recovery, reduces apoptosis and inflammation, and improves islet vascularization, primarily due to the upregulation of angiogenic factors and the decrease in proinflammatory and apoptotic cytokines in the pancreas.[103] The glucose level-lowering effect of AD-MSC infusion is also closely linked to hepatic glucose and lipid metabolism, particularly when MSCs undergo genetic modification.[104,105] Furthermore, preclinical studies and in vitro experiments strongly suggest that the secretome of AD-MSCs is effective in reducing diabetes manifestations, with intraperitoneal administration of AD-MSC-derived exosomes improving islet function and maintaining normoglycemia in a T1DM animal model.[106,107] Using the hyperglycemic clamp technique, researchers worldwide have proposed for the first time that BM-MSCs can improve insulin resistance in T2DM rats, thereby lowering blood glucose.[108] Further research has confirmed that UC-MSCs regulate macrophage polarization in adipose tissue by secreting IL-6 and promoting the transition of macrophages from the M1 to M2 phenotype, thereby reducing chronic inflammation and alleviating insulin resistance in adipose tissue. In addition, activation of the NOD-like receptor family pyrin domain containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome is inhibited in hepatocytes after UC-MSC treatment, thus reducing the release of inflammatory factors from hepatocytes and improving insulin resistance in hepatocytes.[109,110]

Basic research has further promoted their clinical application. A previous study showed that, compared with conventional insulin therapy, a single autologous BM-MSC transplantation can effectively preserve residual pancreatic function. However, no statistically significant differences in blood glucose control or insulin dosage were observed between the two treatment groups.[111] Hu et al[112] used single allogeneic UC-MSC transplantation to treat patients newly diagnosed with T1DM. The pancreatic function of the transplantation group increased by more than 50% compared with baseline, which was significantly higher than that of the insulin treatment group, and three patients (20%) discontinued insulin treatment. In 2016, Cai et al[113] conducted a clinical study on patients with T1DM lasting 2–16 years, and without relevant complications. The study found that UC-MSC transplantation increased insulin and C-peptide levels and decreased HbA1c, fasting blood glucose, and daily insulin requirements. After an 8-year follow-up, researchers reported that cotransplantation of UC-MSCs and autologous bone marrow mononuclear cells in patients with T1DM reduced the incidence of diabetic peripheral neuropathy, diabetic nephropathy, and retinopathy, with no signs of malignancy.[114] A recent study evaluated the safety and effectiveness of intravenous autologous BM-MSC transplantation for the treatment of patients newly diagnosed with T1DM. The results indicate that it significantly decreases hypoglycemic episodes, enhances glycated hemoglobin and C-peptide levels, alters cytokine patterns, boosts regulatory T cells, and improves quality of life, especially when early transplantation is combined with exercise.[115] A combined phase I/II trial found that allogeneic UC-MSCs (ProTrans) are a safe and potentially effective treatment for recent-onset T1DM, as they preserve β-cell function by reducing the decline in C-peptide levels and maintaining stable insulin levels over a 12-month follow-up, compared with the placebo group.[116] Another retrospective cohort study found that patients with T1DM who received therapy with AD-MSC infusion and vitamin D supplementation within 3 months of diagnosis had a higher frequency of clinical remission and lower total daily insulin requirement at all time points during the 36-month follow-up, than that of patients in the control group despite similar glycemic control between the two groups, suggesting that this intervention may prolong the preservation of residual insulin secretion and favor the development of a remission phase.[117]

Since 2009, numerous clinical trials have explored the potential of MSCs in treating T2DM, primarily focusing on patients with a long disease duration, poor glycemic control with oral medications, and high insulin requirements. These studies have consistently demonstrated that cell transplantation significantly reduces blood glucose and HbA1c levels while increasing C-peptide levels under glucose stimulation, resulting in varying degrees of insulin reduction.[118,119] Nguyen et al[120] studied the safety and efficacy of autologous BM-MSC therapy in patients with T2DM and found that it was well tolerated with short-term benefits, primarily in patients with a disease duration less than 10 years and a body mass index (BMI) lower than 23 kg/m2. Their study also revealed that T2DM duration directly affects BM-MSC proliferation, glycolysis, mitochondrial function, and mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) mutation accumulation. Another study demonstrated that Wharton’s jelly-derived MSC infusion significantly improved β-cell function and decreased the incidence of diabetic chronic complications, indicating its potential as an effective treatment option.[121] Recently, a phase II trial found that UC-MSC transplantation safely and effectively improved glycemic control and insulin sensitivity in Chinese adults with T2DM, suggesting it as a potential therapeutic approach.[122]

Although numerous clinical studies have demonstrated the safety and efficacy of MSCs derived from different cell sources for the treatment of diabetes and achieved certain clinical efficacy, some problems with MSCs still persist in the treatment of diabetes. First, numerous factors influence the extraction and culturing of MSCs, leading to variations in their immunogenicity, proliferation, and differentiation capabilities in vitro, depending on the culturing conditions. These variations can potentially affect the efficacy of MSCs in the treatment of diabetes. In addition, inconsistencies exist in terms of administration timing, infusion dosages, dosing frequencies, and treatment cycles. Therefore, establishing more comprehensive cell identification standards and conducting clinical studies to compare the efficacy of different infusion frequencies and dosages is imperative. Second, determining the appropriate source of MSCs for clinical applications is of utmost importance. Autologous MSC transplantation circumvents host immune responses; however, factors such as donor age, diabetes progression, and comorbidities influence the quantity and function of BM-MSCs and AD-MSCs.[123,124] These variables affect the proliferative potential, cellular senescence, and death of MSCs, ultimately diminishing their therapeutic efficacy. Furthermore, disparities exist in the gene expression and cytokine secretion patterns between UC-MSCs/Wharton’s jelly-derived MSC and BM-MSCs, necessitating further assessment of their long-term therapeutic outcomes.[125] Finally, the urgent challenges in the clinical application of MSCs center on prolonging their survival time in vivo, elevating their therapeutic efficacy, and preserving their stability. Numerous innovative strategies have been implemented to augment the healing effect of MSCs, including synergistic approaches that incorporate medications such as decitabine.[126] Genetic modifications of MSCs have also demonstrated promising outcomes. Specifically, in a study where MSCs overexpressing the glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) analog exendin-4 were used via a lentiviral vector, activation of the glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor led to an extended survival duration of MSCs. Moreover, this activation mitigated senescence and apoptosis of pancreatic beta cells in mice with T2DM.[127] Furthermore, MSC-derived exosomes emerged as a cutting-edge therapeutic strategy. These exosomes exhibit high efficiency and stability by directly fusing with target cells to elicit biological responses. Notably, they also carry lower risks of immunogenicity and tumor formation. Animal models have shown that injecting BM-MSC-derived exosomes into T1DM rats notably enhances insulin secretion and decreased islet inflammation and fibrosis.[128] Similarly, the intravenous administration of UC-MSC-derived exosomes to T2DM rats improved insulin sensitivity and hepatic glucose metabolism.[129] However, further clinical investigations are necessary to ascertain the therapeutic potential of genetically engineered MSCs and their exosomes in the treatment of human DM.

Challenges Concerning Stem Cell Therapy

Although SCs appear to be an ideal solution in medicine, many obstacles still need to be overcome.

Species differences are one of the major challenges in the field of stem cell-derived β-cell differentiation. Significant differences exist between rodents and humans in terms of genes, physiology, and metabolism, which may result in effective treatment methods in rodents but not in humans. Immunological rejection is another major barrier to successful SCs transplantation. Researchers have explored several possible solutions. Researchers have chosen nonhuman primates (such as rhesus monkeys) that are similar to humans in terms of physiology, genetics, and metabolism, as preclinical research models. Deng et al[72] transplanted CiPSC-generated islets into a T1DM patient via implantation beneath the anterior rectus sheath. The patient gained lasting insulin independence 75 days after surgery, with one year of well-controlled blood glucose (TIR >98%, HbA1C <5.7%) and no transplant-related complications.

An increase in the number of transplanted SCs can also lead to teratoma formation. For SCs transplantation to become a popular and widely accessible procedure, tumor risk must be assessed. Optimizing the source and preparation of SCs is crucial, including selecting high-quality and reliable sources of SCs, ensuring their high purity, and rigorous testing and screening to reduce contamination risks. Optimizing the preparation process to improve the differentiation efficiency of SCs and reduce the number of undifferentiated or abnormally differentiated cells is also crucial to minimize teratoma formation. Moreover, precise control over the number of transplanted cells is essential. Accurately calculating the required number of SCs based on the patient’s condition and transplantation needs using microtransplantation technology ensures a precise and controllable transplantation quantity and avoids excessive transplantation. Monitoring and management after transplantation should not be ignored. Regular imaging and laboratory examinations should be conducted to assess the survival, differentiation, and functional status of the transplanted cells. Furthermore, immunomodulatory drugs and gene editing technologies can be used to reduce immune rejection and the risk of teratoma formation. Finally, by conducting numerous clinical trials and accumulating safety and efficacy data, a scientific basis is provided for the precise control of transplantation strategies. At the same time, establishing a SCs transplantation database to collect and analyze patient data can help gain a deeper understanding of cell status and complications after transplantation.

Complications after SCs transplantation are a serious issue. Researchers should strictly screen SCs sources and follow sterile operating procedures during collection, storage, and transportation to prevent contamination. Moreover, the patient’s age and physical status should be comprehensively considered to develop a personalized treatment plan and reasonably determine the SCs transplantation dose to avoid complications. Sterile operations should be enhanced during transplantation to reduce the risk of infection, and immunosuppressive drugs should be used to alleviate rejection reactions. In addition, the pretreatment protocol should be optimized by selecting the appropriate doses for chemotherapy drugs and radiotherapy.

Conclusions

SCs therapy for diabetes has led to significant breakthroughs and encouraging achievements in recent years, with treatment goals shifting from adjunctive to curative therapy. However, SCs therapy for diabetes is still in the clinical research phase and faces various challenges such as high costs, immune rejection, heterogeneity of treatment efficacy, operational standardization, and safety concerns. Enhancing the efficiency of SCs therapy, prolonging effective treatment duration, and reducing adverse reactions are urgent issues that need to be addressed during the translation of basic research into clinical practice. Nevertheless, with the application of encapsulation technology, exosomes, gene modification, proteomics, secretomics, and other related techniques as well as the advent of commercialized SCs products for treatment, these problems are gradually being solved. Researchers anticipate that within the next 5–10 years, SCs therapy for diabetes will enter the clinical phase and revolutionize the treatment paradigm for diabetes.
