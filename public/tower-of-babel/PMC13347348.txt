Advances in Fabrication Technology of Fine Metal Masks for OLED Material Deposition: Research Status and Future Development Directions
Authors: Zouwei Liu, Xialun Yun, Xuesong Mei, Yiwen Li, Xianhong Zhang, Binbin Pei, Yuanqing Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Fine metal mask (FMM) is the essential consumable for high-precision pixel patterning in OLED vacuum evaporation, yet its fabrication remains a critical bottleneck for high-resolution displays. This review systematically summarizes three manufacturing routes for Invar-alloy FMMs: chemical etching method (CEM), electroforming method (EFM), and laser processing method (LPM). CEM is currently the only mature method for large-scale production because of its high throughput, but the coupled effects of chemical reaction, mass transfer, and double-sided etching make it susceptible to unetched and partially etched holes, leading to low yield, material waste, and economic loss. EFM offers potential for finer structures, but alloy composition control, internal stress, cost, and insufficient evaporation-performance verification still limit its practical application. LPM, especially ultrafast laser machining, can fabricate high-quality microholes in Invar foils and has demonstrated pixel densities up to 3000 PPI; however, processing millions of holes one by one remains too inefficient for full-mask mass production. Based on this comparison, we propose a hybrid “CEM + laser repair” route, in which CEM provides high-throughput primary fabrication and LPM is used for localized reprocessing of defective holes. Four key issues for enabling this route are identified: precise defect localization, laser-parameter optimization, layered modeling of defective-hole geometries, and scanning-trajectory design. This review clarifies the present status and limitations of FMM fabrication technologies and highlights laser repair of defective FMMs produced by CEM as a promising strategy for improving manufacturing yield, reducing waste, and supporting sustainable production of high-resolution OLED displays.

Introduction

With the development of semiconductor and communication technologies, the widespread adoption of smart devices has placed higher demands on display performance. − Compared with display technologies such as liquid crystal displays (LCDs) and quantum dot light emitting diode displays (QLEDs), organic light emitting diodes (OLEDs) have become one of the mainstream technologies in the mid to high end display market because of their self-emissive nature, high contrast, low power consumption, and compatibility with flexible substrates. −

In OLED panel fabrication, the high precision selective deposition of red, green, and blue emissive materials requires the use of a mask. , However, temperature fluctuations during the deposition process can cause thermal deformation of conventional masks, resulting in hole misalignment and reduced patterning accuracy. , Therefore, the mask material must possess a low coefficient of thermal expansion, high elastic modulus, and good flatness. Ultrathin Invar alloy foil satisfies these requirements and is therefore widely used to fabricate Fine Metal Masks (FMMs) for OLED material deposition.

The stringent accuracy and consistency requirements for the selective deposition of OLED materials place extremely high demands on the precision and uniformity of the holes in the FMM. Specifically, the precision tolerance of the aperture size of the via openings on the side near the substrate is typically required to be within ±1 μm of the design dimensions, in order to ensure uniform pixel sizes after evaporation. The cone angle is typically required to be controlled between 45° and 55° to avoid defects in OLED deposition, thereby improving the production yield of OLED panels. Although extensive research has been conducted in industry on FMM fabrication, publicly available academic studies remain limited. There is still a lack of a comprehensive review that systematically summarizes the current state of related technologies and provides guidance for future research directions. To this end, this paper provides the first review of the current research status of FMM fabrication technologies. First, the principles of Chemical Etching Method (CEM), its latest research progress, and the factors influencing its sensitivity to defect formation are introduced. Then, the principle of Electroforming Method (EFM) for FMM fabrication and its current exploratory stage of development are briefly described. Subsequently, the research status of the Laser Processing Method (LPM) process is reviewed, with particular emphasis on the suitability of low-fluence femtosecond lasers for fabricating micropores in FMMs. Finally, the advantages, limitations, processing performance, and scalability of these three processes are analyzed. On this basis, with consideration of both economic viability and environmental impact, a novel “CEM + laser repair” route for FMM production is proposed to improve the manufacturing yield of FMMs, and the key technical challenges that must be addressed to enable laser repair of defective openings in FMMs are highlighted.

CEM refers to the use of chemical solutions to selectively etch the Invar alloy substrate, thereby producing an FMM containing the desired microhole array. The EFM for processing FMM refers to the process of electroplating metal onto a photolithographically patterned mold, and then stripping off the FMM with microholes. LPM refers to the use of laser precision machining technology to ablate the required microhole array in an Invar alloy substrate, thereby completing the fabrication of the FMM. Among these methods, CEM is the primary method for large-scale commercial production of FMM. Its advantages lie in high processing efficiency and suitability for mass production. The maximum pixel density that can be processed using CEM is 600 PPI. , EFM and LPM are still in the laboratory research stage. At present, there have been no reported studies on the performance testing of FMMs fabricated using EFM, and the capability of EFM to manufacture FMMs with OLED material evaporation performance still requires further verification. , On the other hand, the precision of the microhole arrays fabricated by LPM and their effectiveness in depositing OLED materials have been experimentally verified, and this method can achieve a maximum microhole density of up to 3000 PPI. Although LPM has significant advantages in processing high pixel density FMMs, a single FMM contains millions of microholes, which makes the large-scale commercial production of FMMs by using LPM challenging.

With the advancement of artificial intelligence technologies, the accelerated electrification of electric vehicles, and the continuously increasing performance requirements for OLED displays, the market demand for displays is expected to grow significantly, which will lead to a substantial increase in the required production volume of high-pixel-density FMMs. Due to the technical principles of CEM processing FMM, four types of defective holes are likely to occur during the etching of Invar alloys: complete failure of substrate etching, incomplete etching of large openings, incomplete etching of small openings, and simultaneous failure to etch both openings. Defective holes in an FMM can result in the scrapping of the entire mask, which directly reduces the manufacturing yield of FMMs and also leads to additional resource waste and economic loss. However, it is worth noting that although LPM exhibits low efficiency in processing the micro-openings across an entire FMM, it offers exceptional machining precision. Combined with previous studies on laser-based repair of other types of masks, it is believed that employing laser processing techniques to repair defective openings in CEM-fabricated FMMs represents an important future research direction in this field.

The content reviewed in this paper has practical value for helping readers quickly understand the research progress of the related technologies. The proposed novel approach of using LPM to repair defective FMMs manufactured by CEM is of significant importance for promoting the sustainable development of the FMM manufacturing industry. In addition, the key technical challenges identified in the paper for repairing defective FMMs using LPM provide clear directions for further research by researchers.

The Function and Structure of FMM

The main production process of OLED screen is shown in Figure (a): the fabrication process of the OLED display begins with the precision cleaning of an ultrathin glass substrate, followed by the deposition of a transparent conductive layer of indium tin oxide (ITO). Subsequently, fine thin-film transistor (TFT) backplane circuits are fabricated on the substrate using semiconductor processes such as photolithography and etching. The device is then transferred to an ultrahigh vacuum environment, where red, green, and blue organic emissive material layers, along with the necessary hole and electron transport layers, are sequentially deposited using the FMM. A metal cathode is then deposited to complete the OLED diode structure and form the electrical circuit. Critical encapsulation is carried out in an environment strictly isolated from moisture and oxygen, typically via laser-sealing a glass cover plate or depositing thin-film encapsulation layers, to ensure the long-term stability of the organic materials. Finally, the touch panel and polarizer are laminated, driver ICs are bonded, and the module is assembled, followed by rigorous aging tests and color calibration to guarantee reliable performance and accurate color reproduction.

(a) The main steps of OLED panel manufacturing; (b) FMM enables the deposition of OLED organic materials onto precise locations of the substrate; (c) physical image of FMM; (d) schematic diagram of FMM structure; (e) shape types of vapor deposition holes in FMM.

FMM is the core consumable in the production process of OLED screens. The vapor deposition process of OLED materials is shown in Figure (b): the FMM is magnetically secured to the substrate, and OLED materials within the evaporation source is heated either by resistive filaments or electron beams, vaporized, and then precisely deposited through the holes in the FMM onto predetermined positions of the TFT, ensuring proper light emission from the OLED screen. The opening size and density of the vapor deposition holes in FMM directly determine the pixel height of OLED displays. The smaller the opening size and higher the density of the vapor deposition holes, the higher the pixel size of the produced screen. The schematic diagram and physical image of FMM is shown in Figure (c) and Figure (d), at present, a commercial FMM required for OLED screen production usually includes millions to tens of millions of vapor deposition holes. As shown in Figure (e), these vapor deposition holes can be classified into regular-shaped holes and irregular-shaped holes based on the outline differences between the upper and lower openings. For regular-shaped holes, the outlines of the upper and lower openings are identical, with one being a proportionally scaled-up or scaled-down version of the other. Irregular-shaped holes can be further divided into two types: one is based on regular-shaped holes but introduces an additional rotation angle between the upper and lower openings, and the others have completely different contour shapes for its upper and lower openings. For different OLED pixel deposition requirements, FMMs containing microholes of specific shapes and sizes are selected.

The functionality and structure of FMM make its manufacturing extremely difficult, which can be reflected in the following aspects: First, during the evaporation process of OLED materials, the operating temperature will experience severe fluctuations, which may lead to thermal deformation of FMM and result in deposition failure or color mixing of OLED materials at designated locations caused by hole misalignment. Second, the FMM typically has a thickness of 10–30 μm, making it highly susceptible to permanent deformation during process handling. Third, the precision tolerance of the processed glass-side opening size is generally required to be within ±1 μm of the design dimensions, and the accuracy error of the Taper angle of the vapor deposition hole is ±1.5° to ensure uniform pixel size after OLED materials deposition. Fourthly, a single FMM contains tens of millions of evaporation holes, each requiring strict dimensional accuracy, which imposes extraordinarily demanding standards on manufacturing consistency. To address this need, researchers have proposed three methods, including CEM, EFM, and LPM, for fabricating ultrathin Invar alloy foils containing large numbers of micro holes with specific shapes.

Chemical Etching Method for Processing Fine Metal Mask

The CEM is currently the main method used in the commercial production of FMM. The essence of CEM processing FMM lies in the reactive flow process which can be seen in Figure . The specific process is as follows:

Schematic diagram of the chemical etching method process flow.

Step 1: Inspect and clean the Invar alloy substrate to ensure the surface is free of contaminants.

Step 2: Apply photoresist to both sides of the substrate and perform double-sided exposure.

Step 3: Development and drying: Develop and dry the substrate to obtain the desired pattern on the photoresist.

Step 4: After applying an etching barrier layer to the large aperture side, etch the small opening to the required depth. Then remove the photoresist from the small aperture side and apply an etching barrier layer to that side.

Step 5: Remove the etching barrier layer from the large aperture side and etch the large opening to the required depth. Finally, remove the photoresist from the large aperture side and the etching barrier layer from the small aperture side to complete the process.

In the CEM processing FMM technology, the material removal rate is determined by the etching rate at the material surface, which is jointly governed by the etching reaction and the mass-transfer process. , Therefore, the factors influencing the etching rate include the chemical reaction rate, flow-field transport characteristics, and the geometry of the mask. At present, extensive studies have examined the effects of chemical reaction rates: it has been shown that the etchant concentration, composition, and etching temperature significantly affect the reaction rate. Moreover, variations in these reaction conditions also lead to changes in surface roughness, which is a key indicator for evaluating surface quality. Yun et al. investigated the etching behavior of Invar alloy in FeCl3 solutions under different reaction conditions, and their results indicated that the chemical reaction rate follows first-order kinetics; increasing the etchant concentration effectively improves the surface roughness of the etched surface.

The aforementioned studies analyzed the factors affecting the etching rate from the perspective of regulating the chemical reaction rate. However, in practical etching processes, the flow velocity inside the etching chamber is extremely low, and the etching behavior is jointly determined by both mass transfer and chemical reaction rates. This coupling becomes particularly significant in the etching of high-aspect-ratio or micro/nanoscale structures, where mass-transfer resistance increases substantially, easily leading to insufficient supply of reactants or accumulation of reaction products. Therefore, this may lead to four types of defects, namely complete absence of etching the substrate, incomplete etching of small opening, incomplete etching of large opening, and incomplete etching of both openings, as shown in Figure .

Four types of defective holes on FMM produced by CEM.

The advantages of CEM lie in its high production efficiency and suitability for large-scale manufacturing. Using this method, the maximum resolution attainable for FMMs is approximately 600 PPI, indicating inherent limitations in producing FMMs required for higher-resolution OLED panels. In addition, defects such as unetched or partially etched micropores in the Invar alloy substrate may render the entire FMM sheet unusable. At present, the average yield of FMMs fabricated by CEM is about 30%, while the yield of Gen 8.6 FMMs is even lower. Defective FMMs produced through CEM also result in additional economic loss, resource waste, and increased manufacturing costs.

Electroforming Method for Processing Fine Metal Mask

The schematic diagram of the FMM fabrication using EFM is shown in Figure (a). At first, an electroplating solution containing Invar alloy metal ions is used as the electrolyte. Then, a cathode substrate is used as the base, onto which photoresist is applied. After ultraviolet (UV) mask exposure and development, electroplating is carried out to deposit the Invar material onto the cathode substrate in the desired pattern. Finally, the photoresist and the cathode substrate are removed to obtain the FMM. Figure (b) shows the best quality FMM currently publicly manufactured using this EFM.

The advantage of EFM is that it can manufacture FMM with higher hole density (up to 1200 PPI) than CEM. However, it also presents technical challenges, such as the complexity of codepositing binary alloys and higher production costs. Moreover, no publicly reported studies have investigated whether the physical performance of FMMs produced by this method satisfies the demands of the OLED evaporation process.

Laser Processing Method for Processing Fine Metal Mask

The laser processing method is directly ablating the Invar alloy substrate by using laser micromachining technology to produce a microhole array that meets production requirements, thereby obtaining the FMM product, which is shown in Figure (a). Figure (b) and Figure (c) respectively demonstrate the OLED material evaporation effects by using FMM manufactured by CEM and LPM. Since the CEM involves double-sided etching of the Invar alloy substrate, which may create shadowing effects in the edge regions of small openings. In contrast, the LPM can directly fabricate holes in FMM, which significantly reduces shadowing effects during evaporation.

The use of LPM for FMM fabrication has emerged only within the past decade. Heo et al. publicly study using laser to fabricate microholes required for OLED evaporation on Invar-like materials at first. In their research, the authors compared the effects of two different types of nanosecond lasers for micromachining on Permalloy foils. As depicted in Figure (a), material processed with a high-pulse energy/low-repetition rate laser at 1064 nm (pulse width is 5 ns, repetition rate is 10 Hz, pulse energy is 200 mJ, beam size at focus is 30 μm) exhibited fracture under these parameters. For processing microholes on the materials by using a low-pulse energy/high-repetition rate laser at 355 nm (pulse width is 5 ns, repetition rate is 25 kHz, pulse energy is 90 μJ, beam size at focus is 30 μm), the result is shown in Figure (b), after etching melt/condensed particles around the generated hole with a mixture of HCl/HNO3 solution (HCl/HNO3 = in volume) for 30 s, clear micropores with a diameter of 20 μm can be obtained on the material. Subsequently, the authors employed this method to fabricate a 5 × 5 array of microholes on Permalloy foils shown in Figure (c), providing the first demonstration that lasers possess the potentiality to fabricate evaporation holes in FMM. However, the three-dimensional morphology required for evaporation holes should be further considered, and there are also differences in material properties between Permalloy foils and Invar alloys.

While ultraviolet femtosecond laser processing demonstrates superior performance, infrared femtosecond laser processing offers significant processing efficiency advantages. Researchers have conducted targeted investigations into both infrared and ultraviolet laser technologies, respectively.

For infrared femtosecond laser processing, Shin form APS Corporation publicly disclosed the structure of their experimental setup for the first time. As shown in Figure , the structure of the experimental setup is as follows: A coherent infrared femtosecond laser source was used, with an average pulse duration of 300 fs at 1035 nm, a maximum output power of 40 W, a pulse repetition rate tunable up to 50 MHz, and a pulse energy of 0.8 μJ. The processing system is composed of a scanning galvanometer and an f-theta field lens with a focal length of 100 mm. Through experimental and numerical simulation studies, the authors determined that when energy density and spot size are fixed, the number of pulses irradiated on a position continuously during the scanning process can be controlled by adjusting the scanning speed for different repetition rates, thereby preventing thermal damage to the material.

In summary, layer-by-layer scanning of the Invar alloy substrate using a low-fluence UV femtosecond laser can produce holes of the highest quality. At present, research on LPM for FMM fabrication is primarily focused on case studies and qualitative analyses, while quantitative analysis and fundamental theoretical research require further development. Moreover, although LPM currently achieves very high precision in machining holes on Invar alloys, the presence of millions of holes on a single FMM makes it difficult for this technology to be applied to large-scale commercial production.

This enables the maximum microhole density achievable in FMMs processed by LPM to be much higher than that obtained by CEM, reaching up to 3000 PPI. However, because a FMM contains a vast number of holes, using LPM to process each hole individually results in a low throughput which limits its application in commercial large-scale production at present.

Comparison of CEM, EFM and LPM

CEM is currently the most mature and widely used process for commercial FMM production. Its main advantage lies in its high processing efficiency, which makes it suitable for the fabrication of large area FMMs containing millions of micro holes. Therefore, CEM remains the only process that has been verified for industrial scale manufacturing. However, because the formation of micro holes in CEM depends on chemical reaction, mass transport, and double sided etching control, the process is intrinsically sensitive to local variations in photoresist patterning, etching rate, solution flow, and substrate quality. These factors can lead to defects such as unetched regions, incomplete etching, and over etching. As a result, although CEM has clear advantages in manufacturing throughput and production cost, its capability for fabricating ultra high pixel density FMMs is limited, with the maximum pixel density generally reaching about 600 PPI.

Compared with CEM, EFM provides a possible route for improving the dimensional accuracy of FMM microstructures because the mask is formed by electroplating on a patterned mold rather than by removing material from an Invar substrate. In principle, this process can reduce some of the geometric limitations associated with isotropic chemical etching and may enable finer microhole structures. However, EFM still faces several unresolved challenges. In particular, the electroforming of binary alloy materials with stable composition, uniform thickness, low internal stress, and sufficient mechanical reliability remains difficult. In addition, the process cost is relatively high, and the evaporation performance of EFM-fabricated FMMs has not yet been fully verified in practical OLED manufacturing. Therefore, although EFM has been reported to achieve a pixel density of up to about 1200 PPI, it is still at an exploratory stage and cannot yet be regarded as a mature alternative to CEM for commercial FMM production.

LPM shows the highest processing accuracy among the three methods. By using ultrafast laser pulses and optimized processing parameters, LPM can fabricate high-quality microholes in Invar alloy foils with a small heat-affected zone and good geometric controllability. This makes it particularly suitable for the fabrication of high-pixel-density FMMs and complex hole structures. The reported maximum pixel density of LPM can reach up to 3000 PPI, and its feasibility for OLED material evaporation has been experimentally verified. Nevertheless, the main limitation of LPM is its low processing efficiency. Since a single FMM usually contains millions or even tens of millions of microholes, fabricating each hole individually by laser ablation would result in excessive processing time. In addition, the high cost of ultrafast laser equipment further limits its direct application in large-scale commercial production.

Overall, CEM has the strongest industrial applicability but is limited by defect formation and pixel-density improvement; EFM has potential advantages in precision but still lacks sufficient material-process stability and application verification; and LPM provides the highest machining accuracy and pixel-density capability but is constrained by processing efficiency and equipment cost. Therefore, replacing CEM entirely with EFM or LPM is not realistic in the near term. A more feasible development route is to retain CEM as the main high-throughput manufacturing process while introducing LPM as a precision repair technology for defective holes generated during CEM fabrication. This hybrid route can take advantage of the production efficiency of CEM and the high local machining accuracy of LPM, and therefore provides a promising direction for improving FMM manufacturing yield, reducing material waste, and supporting the sustainable development of high-resolution OLED display manufacturing.

Future Development Direction

As summarized in Table , the three FMM fabrication methods, including CEM, EFM, and LPM, each offer distinct technical advantages, but none of them can simultaneously satisfy the requirements of high throughput, high precision, low cost, and large-scale manufacturability. Among them, CEM remains the only process that has been successfully adopted for industrial FMM production. However, its intrinsic dependence on chemical etching makes defect formation difficult to eliminate completely. In practical fabrication, defects such as unetched regions, incomplete etching, and overetching are still likely to occur because the etching behavior is highly sensitive to local variations in photoresist patterning, solution flow, etching rate, and substrate condition. With the continuous increase in display resolution, the demand for higher pixel density and tighter dimensional control of FMMs is becoming increasingly stringent. Under this trend, improving the yield of CEM-based fabrication is more realistic than attempting to replace CEM outright in the near term.

From a manufacturing and sustainability perspective, as shown in Figure a defective FMM not only represents the loss of the economic, but also wastes the energy, materials, and processing resources consumed throughout the entire fabrication chain. Therefore, reducing the number of scrapped masks is of great significance both economically and environmentally. The feasibility of laser-based repair can be supported by previous studies on photomask repair. Morishige developed a ps UV solid-state laser repair system and evaluated its repair performance on Cr binary masks and MoSi halftone phase-shift masks, showing that short-pulse UV laser ablation could remove absorber defects with high accuracy while limiting damage to the transparent substrate. Haight et al. developed the Mask Advanced Repair System (MARS), a femtosecond laser mask repair system for Cr defect removal in a manufacturing environment. They found that femtosecond laser ablation greatly reduced the thermal problems associated with nanosecond laser repair, such as metal splashing, haze, reduced transmission, and quartz pitting, and the repaired regions exhibited nearly complete optical transmission without observable glass damage. In addition to Cr defect removal, laser repair has also been extended to more complex defect types and larger mask formats. Dinsdale et al. used a DUV ultrashort-pulse laser repair tool for advanced photomasks and demonstrated selective removal of absorber defects on critical edges and two-dimensional patterns without damaging the optically transmissive substrate. Haight et al. further demonstrated high-resolution femtosecond-laser material ablation and deposition, indicating that laser repair can combine localized material removal and material addition for photomask defect correction. For large-area display-related masks, Treyger et al. developed a commercial femtosecond DUV laser repair tool for large-area FPD photomasks, demonstrating that laser repair can be scaled from semiconductor photomasks to large-area mask substrates. These studies collectively indicate that laser repair has already achieved high spatial resolution, low thermal damage, controllable repair geometry, and practical equipment integration in different mask systems. FMMs fabricated by LPM can achieve a pixel density of up to 3000 PPI, far exceeding that of FMMs fabricated by chemical etching. Therefore, extending laser processing to the repair of defect holes in FMMs is considered a feasible secondary processing route. Accordingly, a hybrid “CEM + laser repair” route is proposed for future FMM manufacturing. In this route, CEM is retained as the high-throughput main fabrication process, while laser repair is introduced to reprocess defective holes generated during CEM, thereby improving manufacturing yield and reducing material waste.

Four key technologies that need be studied for laser repairing FMM.

As shown in Figure , the realization of laser repair for FMMs mainly depends on four key technical issues, which are discussed as follows:1Accurate localization of defective holes in the FMM. Since a single FMM contains millions of microholes, the defective ones must first be precisely identified and localized on the target mask. This step is the basis of the subsequent laser repair process and directly determines the machining accuracy of the repaired structures.2Optimization of laser parameters during the repair process. The selected laser parameters must ensure that the defective region can be effectively removed or reshaped without causing thermal damage to the surrounding Invar substrate. In particular, the microstructure and mechanical properties of the material near the repaired hole should remain unaffected after processing.3Layered modeling of the structures to be processed on the substrate. After CEM etching, defective holes generally include two typical forms, namely unetched holes and partially etched holes. For each defect type, the three-dimensional geometry that needs to be reprocessed by laser repair should be determined first. Based on the selected laser parameters, a layered model of the target 3D structure can then be established to guide multilayer laser ablation.4Optimization of scanning parameters. After the layered model is determined, the scanning trajectory and scanning speed of each processing layer must be optimized to balance processing efficiency and repair quality. Only by coordinating scanning strategy with laser parameters can the repaired holes meet both geometric precision and throughput requirements.

Conclusion

In the paper, the current research landscape of FMM manufacturing technologies was comprehensively reviewed. At present, CEM and LPM are the only methods available for fabricating microholes in Invar alloy substrates to produce FMMs suitable for OLED material evaporation deposition. While CEM remains the only commercially viable method due to its high throughput and suitability for large-scale production, its inherent limitations lead to the frequent generation of defective holes, resulting in low manufacturing yield, substantial material waste and economic loss. LPM, on the other hand, offers superior machining precision and enables extremely high pixel densities, yet its limited processing efficiency presents major barriers to commercial deployment. To overcome the shortcomings of CEM-based manufacturing, the concept of laser repair technology for FMMs is introduced, in which aims to use laser processing to conduct secondary machining on defective holes formed during CEM. This hybrid “CEM + laser repair” technology routing is expected to significantly enhance the yield of FMM production, reduce resource waste, and support the sustainable growth of the display industry. The four key technical challenges for realizing laser repair of defective holes in FMMs are identified as accurate positioning of defective holes in FMM, layered modeling of the structures to be machined, optimization of laser processing parameters, and determination of processing trajectory during the repair process. The paper has important guiding significance and practical value for promoting the sustainable manufacturing of FMMs, and it indicates clear directions for subsequent research on laser-assisted precision machining technologies for FMMs.
