Subarachnoid hemorrhage presenting as cardiac arrest mimicking acute myocardial infarction due to multivessel coronary vasospasm: a case report
Authors: Yukako Otsuka, Fumiko Nakamura, Kenta Uemura, Hiroto Yamamoto, Riki Kitahashi, Yoichi Tsutano, Kazuhiro Kobuke, Takeshi Aoyama, Norio Kanamori
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A 58-year-old man presented with out-of-hospital cardiac arrest. The initial rhythm was pulseless electrical activity, which deteriorated into ventricular fibrillation during resuscitation. After return of spontaneous circulation, electrocardiography demonstrated diffuse ST-segment depression with ST-segment elevation in lead aVR, strongly suggesting acute coronary syndrome. Emergent coronary angiography revealed simultaneous angiographic occlusion with TIMI 0 flow in the ostial left anterior descending artery and distal right coronary artery. During preparation for percutaneous coronary intervention, coronary flow spontaneously recovered to TIMI 3 without balloon dilation, stenting, or intracoronary vasodilator administration. Intravascular ultrasound demonstrated no plaque rupture or thrombus. Persistent coma prompted neuroimaging, which revealed diffuse subarachnoid hemorrhage with hydrocephalus. Although cardiac arrest associated with subarachnoid hemorrhage usually presents with non-shockable rhythms, this case demonstrated recurrent ventricular fibrillation and transient multivessel coronary occlusion closely mimicking acute myocardial infarction.

Introduction

Subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) is a recognized cause of cardiac arrest and neurogenic myocardial injury.1, 2, 3 Electrocardiographic abnormalities, troponin elevation, and transient ventricular dysfunction are frequently observed in patients with SAH due to sympathetic overactivation and catecholamine surge.4 However, angiographically documented simultaneous multivessel coronary occlusion associated with SAH is exceedingly rare.5, 6, 7 We report a case of SAH presenting as out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) with ventricular fibrillation (VF) and transient simultaneous occlusion of two major coronary arteries, closely mimicking acute myocardial infarction (AMI).

Case presentation

A 58-year-old previously healthy man was last seen well at 21:00 on the day before admission. The following morning at 7:00, emergency medical services (EMS) were called due to sudden altered mental status and stertorous breathing. Upon EMS arrival at 7:13, the initial rhythm was pulseless electrical activity (PEA), and cardiopulmonary resuscitation was initiated immediately. After intravenous epinephrine was administered, the rhythm deteriorated into VF requiring multiple defibrillation attempts. Return of spontaneous circulation was achieved at 7:34, and the patient arrived at our hospital at 7:39.

He had no known cardiovascular or cerebrovascular disease. He was a non-smoker, took no regular medications, and had no significant family history.

On arrival, the patient’s Glasgow Coma Scale score was 3 (E1V1M1), with fixed, 5-mm dilated pupils. Blood pressure was initially 155/133 mmHg but rapidly became unmeasurable.

Electrocardiography revealed ST-segment elevation in lead aVR with diffuse ST-segment depression in leads I, II, III, aVF, and V3–V6, suggesting global subendocardial ischemia (Fig. 1). Transthoracic echocardiography showed diffuse left ventricular hypokinesis not confined to a single coronary territory. Initial cardiac troponin T was elevated at 171 ng/L. Given the high suspicion of ACS, emergency coronary angiography (CAG) was performed. Initial angiography demonstrated total occlusion (TIMI grade 0) of the ostial left anterior descending artery (LAD) and the distal right coronary artery (RCA) involving the posterodescending and posterolateral branches (Fig. 2A, B, Videos 1 and 2). Because of recurrent PEA and VF during the procedure, mechanical ventilation and intra-aortic balloon pump support were initiated. While preparing for percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), a guidewire was advanced across the LAD lesion. Coronary flow improved immediately to TIMI grade 3 without balloon inflation, stenting, or intracoronary vasodilator administration (Fig. 2C and Video 3). Similar restoration of flow was observed in the RCA without any interventions (Fig. 2D and Video 4). Intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) of the LAD showed only mild atherosclerotic change without plaque rupture, dissection, or thrombus (Fig. 3 and Video 5). Discussion

This case highlights a profound clinical dilemma in post-cardiac arrest care: SAH masquerading as AMI with malignant arrhythmias and angiographically documented multivessel coronary obstruction. While SAH is a recognized non-cardiac cause of OHCA, it typically presents with non-shockable rhythms such as PEA or asystole, likely secondary to abrupt intracranial pressure elevation and respiratory arrest.1, 2 Previous studies suggest that VF occurs in only approximately 5% of SAH-associated OHCA cases.3 In the present case, recurrent VF combined with diffuse ST-segment depression and ST-segment elevation in lead aVR strongly suggested a primary cardiac etiology, thereby obscuring the underlying neurological catastrophe.

The deterioration of the initial rhythm from PEA to VF during resuscitation is particularly noteworthy from a resuscitation science perspective. Acute SAH is known to trigger massive sympathetic activation and catecholamine release.8 In the present case, administration of exogenous epinephrine during advanced life support, superimposed on an already hyperadrenergic state, may have contributed to further augmentation of epicardial coronary vasoconstriction and microvascular spasm.4 This profound sympathetic surge may have precipitated simultaneous multivessel coronary obstruction and severe global myocardial ischemia, ultimately leading to recurrent VF and marked electrical instability.

Several angiographic and intravascular imaging findings support severe multivessel coronary vasospasm rather than atherothrombotic AMI as the presumed mechanism of coronary obstruction. First, simultaneous complete occlusion of two major independent epicardial coronary arteries is exceedingly uncommon in conventional AMI. Second, restoration of TIMI grade 3 flow following guidewire passage alone—without balloon angioplasty, stenting, thrombectomy, or intracoronary vasodilator administration— suggested a functional rather than structural obstruction. Third, IVUS demonstrated no plaque rupture, erosion, thrombus, or significant fixed stenosis, making atherothrombotic AMI highly unlikely. Thus, the angiographic finding was best described as transient coronary occlusion, whereas the presumed underlying mechanism was severe functional vasoconstriction consistent with coronary vasospasm. Although coronary vasospasm associated with SAH has previously been reported, angiographically documented simultaneous complete occlusion of multiple major epicardial coronary arteries with spontaneous reperfusion remains exceptionally rare.5, 6, 7

The distribution of SAH was clinically suggestive of aneurysmal rupture; however, computed tomography angiography, cerebral angiography, and autopsy were not performed because of the patient's poor neurological condition. Therefore, the precise cause of SAH could not be definitively confirmed. Nevertheless, the abrupt clinical deterioration, diffuse basal subarachnoid hemorrhage, hydrocephalus, and subsequent catastrophic neurological course were consistent with severe spontaneous SAH.

From a clinical decision-making perspective, this case illustrates the diagnostic complexity of post-resuscitation care rather than establishing a general diagnostic sequence. Current international resuscitation guidelines support early coronary angiography in patients with electrocardiographic findings suggestive of ongoing myocardial ischemia.9 The presence of recurrent VF and diffuse ischemic ST-segment changes in this patient provided a strong rationale for emergent coronary evaluation. At the same time, the subsequent diagnosis of SAH demonstrates that intracranial catastrophes may occasionally present with profound cardiac manifestations. The choice between coronary angiography and neuroimaging, or the decision to perform both in parallel, is highly context-dependent and should be guided by hemodynamic stability, electrocardiographic findings, neurological examination, suspected etiology, and institutional workflow.

Conclusions

SAH can present as cardiac arrest with transient multivessel coronary occlusion and electrocardiographic findings mimicking AMI. In this case, the angiographic appearance of TIMI 0 flow in two major coronary arteries was interpreted as transient coronary occlusion, with severe functional vasoconstriction presumed to be the underlying mechanism. Intracranial pathology should remain in the differential diagnosis in comatose survivors of cardiac arrest with apparent myocardial ischemia.
