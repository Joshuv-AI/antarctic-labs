C–H···Au– Interaction: To Be or Not to Be a Genuine Hydrogen Bond?
Authors: Qiaolin Wang, Xiaojian Li, Zefeng Hua, Xianfeng Zheng, Junyao Zhang, Zhongfa Sun, David H. Parker, Jun Chen, Gang Li, Zhengbo Qin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The ability of a gold ion to act as a proton acceptor in C–H hydrogen bonding remains an open question. Although increasing evidence has pointed to the existence of C–H···Au hydrogen bonding, benchmark measurements of its bond strength are scarce. Here, we present a gas-phase photoelectron velocity-map imaging spectroscopy study of the archetypical M–···CH3CN (M = Cu, Au) hydrogen bonded complexes. In combination with density functional theory (DFT) calculations, the interaction strengths are determined to be 0.43 eV (9.9 kcal mol–1) for Cu–···CH3CN and 0.51 eV (11.8 kcal mol–1) for Au–···CH3CN. Energy-decomposition analysis indicates that electrostatic interactions provide the dominant contribution, accounting for approximately 60% of the total interaction energy in both complexes. These results establish a benchmark for C–H···metal hydrogen bonding and clarify the physical nature of these interactions.

Introduction

Hydrogen bonding is one of the most important noncovalent interactions in chemistry. , In recent years, increasing attention has been directed toward interactions in which metals act as hydrogen-bond acceptors, although whether these contacts should be regarded as genuine hydrogen bonds remains controversial. − Such interest arises not only from the widespread use of metals in catalysis but also from the important roles that weak interactions play throughout catalytic cycles. , However, interactions involving metals as hydrogen-bond acceptors often display features distinct from those of conventional hydrogen bonds, particularly when the donor is a C–H group. A representative example is the agostic interaction in three-center-two-electron (3c–2e) C–H···M systems. − Meanwhile, related but distinct M···H–C interactions that fall outside the “agostic” category have also been recognized. − Terms such as anagostic and preagostic have been proposed for these contacts, but they are generally viewed as predominantly electrostatic in origin and closer in nature to hydrogen bonding. , Even so, whether a metal can truly act as a proton acceptor for a C–H donor remains an open question.

Gold is often considered a potentially favorable hydrogen-bond acceptor, because of its strong relativistic effects, high electronegativity, and low coordination number. − Indeed, numerous examples of short Au···H contacts have been reported in the crystal structures of Au–, Au­(I), and Au­(III) complexes. However, as emphasized by Schmidbaur and co-workers in a review, many of these contacts may be attributed primarily to ligand–counterion interactions and/or crystal-packing effects, rather than to genuine attractive interactions between gold and hydrogen atoms. More recently, Kraus and co-workers presented an even more cautious view, showing that short Au···H–C contacts alone do not provide convincing evidence for hydrogen bonding. Important progress was later made by Bakar et al., who spectroscopically identified C–H···Au interactions in coordinated gold clusters, and by Rigoulet et al., who provided crystallographic, NMR, IR, and computational evidence for genuine hydrogen bonding in cationic Au­(I) complexes. Although these studies have considerably strengthened the case for gold-centered hydrogen bonding, no spectroscopically characterized hydrogen-bond-like C–H···Au– interaction has yet been reported. This is in sharp contrast to other donors, such as O–H, F–H, and N–H, for which Au–···H hydrogen bonds are well documented. −

Two main challenges must be overcome to establish hydrogen bonding in Au– complexes. First, the C–H···Au– interaction must be favored over other competing binding motifs. Second, convincing evidence is required that the Au···H contact is not merely a geometrical proximity, but reflects a genuine attractive interaction with an electrostatic component. Previous studies have considered CH4 as a possible donor for C–H···Au– interaction, and its small binding energy has sometimes been taken to suggest that C–H···Au– hydrogen bonding is intrinsically weak. , However, because the CH4···Au– interaction is dominated by dispersion, it does not provide an ideal system for assessing a genuine C–H···Au hydrogen bond. Since carbon is only slightly more electronegative than hydrogen, an electron-withdrawing substituent is expected to enhance the donor character of the C–H bond. Halogen substituents, as exemplified by CH3I, are obvious candidates in this regard. However, Au– readily undergoes oxidative insertion into the C–I bond of CH3I to form the stable complex [I–Au–CH3]−. − A suitable system is therefore required that is stable enough to prevent reactions between the donor and acceptor. We reasoned that the pseudohalogen–CN group could meet this requirement, allowing CH3CN to serve as a suitable donor for forming a hydrogen-bonded complex. As reported herein, this strategy proved fruitful and enabled us to generate and characterize hydrogen-bonded Au– complexes.

In this work, we selected CH3CN as a donor and successfully generated the archetypal gas-phase complexes M–···CH3CN (M = Cu, Au), which were then interrogated by anion photoelectron velocity-map imaging spectroscopy. This system provides a well-defined model system for a high-precision determination of the C–H···Au interaction strength. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were further carried out to characterize the hydrogen-bonding geometries and analyze the energetic components of the interaction, thereby elucidating the physical nature of the interaction.

Results and Discussion

Detailed descriptions of the experimental and theoretical methods are provided in the Supporting Information. The photoelectron images and corresponding spectra of Cu– and Cu–(CH3CN) taken with photon energy of 2.33 eV, and Au– and Au–(CH3CN) taken with photon energy of 3.50 eV, are shown in Figure . The peak of Cu– at 1.23(2) eV and Au– at 2.31(2) eV is the anionic ground state to neutral ground state transition, which corresponds to detachment of the electron from the s orbital; these perfectly match reported electron affinities (EA) values. Compared with M–(M = Cu, Au), the photoelectron spectra (PES) of M–(CH3CN) show almost the same features as in metal atomic anion. These PES reveal that M–(CH3CN) are formed by physisorption, where the acetonitrile molecule is weakly bound by noncovalent interactions to the metal atomic anion. The M– moiety remains the chromophore for photodetachment in this case. Therefore, M–(CH3CN) are “solvated” anions, in which M– is stabilized by CH3CN molecule. Additionally, the spectra show a clear blue shift to higher electron binding energy (EBE) for M–(CH3CN) relative to bare M–. These shifts can be attributed to the attractive interaction between the departing electron and the solvent molecules in the complexes during photodetachment. , The experimental adiabatic detachment energies (ADEs) were determined from the EBE thresholds. − Because weak hot-band contributions can appear on the low-EBE side of the origin peak, the leading edge of each band on the low-EBE side was extrapolated to zero intensity. The experimental ADE values of Cu–(CH3CN) and Au–(CH3CN) were thus determined to be 1.60(1) eV and 2.73(1) eV, respectively. The vertical detachment energies (VDEs), corresponding to the transitions with the largest Franck–Condon overlap, were determined from the maxima of the photoelectron bands. The experimental VDEs of Cu–(CH3CN) and Au–(CH3CN) are 1.65(1) eV and 2.78(1) eV, respectively. Relative to the electron affinities of the corresponding bare metal atoms, the ADE shifts are 0.37 eV for Cu–(CH3CN) and 0.42 eV for Au–(CH3CN), respectively. For the Cu–(CH3CN) complex, the Cu–···H distance is 2.72 Å and the ∠CuHC bond angle is 138.3°. In comparison, the Au–(CH3CN) complex exhibits a much shorter Au–···H distance of 2.52 Å and a bond angle ∠AuHC closer to linearity (152.9°). The increased linearity in the Au– complex reflects the significant relativistic contraction of the gold 6s orbital, which enhances its ability to engage in directional electrostatic coupling. Note that a typical hydrogen bond H···Y distance ranges from 2.0 to 2.7 Å and the angle X–H···Y usually ranges from 150 to 180°. The theoretical analyses presented below indicate that Cu–(CH3CN) can also be classified as a hydrogen-bonded complex, albeit with a weaker interaction than Au–(CH3CN). However, based on the contact distance, a strong interaction between the two is not expected, especially for Cu–(CH3CN). A rigorous theoretical framework is subsequently employed to explain the physical origin of this nontrivial interaction.

The HOMO of Cu–(CH3CN), from which the photoelectron is detached, is mostly the Cu-centered 4s orbital. Likewise, the HOMO of Au–(CH3CN) is also dominated by the Au 6s atomic orbital. The photoelectron angular distribution (PAD) extracted from the velocity-map images provide the orbital character of the photodetached electron. As shown in Figure , the reconstructed photoelectron images of M– and M–(CH3CN) (M = Cu, Au) exhibit a one prominent ring with pronounced anisotropy along the laser polarization direction. For bare Cu– and Au–, the detachment of electron from an atomic s orbital leads to the maximum anisotropy parameter of β = 2. Upon complexation with CH3CN, mixing between the metal s orbital and the molecular orbitals introduces small contributions from higher angular momentum components, resulting in a reduction of the β value. The measured anisotropy parameters for Cu–(CH3CN) and Au–(CH3CN) are 1.96 ± 0.01 and 1.98 ± 0.02, respectively, showing similar deviations from the atomic anions at similar electron kinetic energies (eKEs). These results indicate that the excess electron remains largely localized on the metal center with dominant s character. These experimental observations are consistent with the calculated HOMOs.

Natural population analysis (NPA) provides additional insight into this solvation motif. The calculated NPA charges on the Cu– kernel is −0.96 e for Cu–(CH3CN) and the Au– kernel is −0.95 e for Au–(CH3CN), indicating that the charge is almost exclusively on the metal atomic anion. These results are consistent with the conclusions inferred from the experimental photoelectron imaging spectra.

To further elucidate the nature and characteristics of C–H hydrogen bonding, the electrostatic potential (ESP) surfaces of the Cu–(CH3CN) and Au–(CH3CN) are displayed in Figure a, with the potentials ranging from −25 to +25 kcal•mol–1. The induced negative (red) and positive (blue) ESP are mapped on the 0.03 e/bohr3 surfaces. For the anionic Cu–(CH3CN) and Au–(CH3CN) complexes, a blue region is clearly observed on the H atom sides of the C–H bond axis, where ESP is more positive than the surrounding area. The feature can be explained by the strong electron-withdrawing nature of the –CN group in CH3CN, which draws electron density toward itself, thereby leaving a less negative region on the opposite side. Therefore, based on the ESP distribution, the C–H···M– hydrogen bond is formed primarily through electrostatic interactions between the negative charged M–(red) and the H atom (blue) with the locally positive. Additionally, the independent gradient model based on Hirshfeld partition (IGMH) , was used to better visualize the weak interaction regions. As shown in Figure b, Cu– and Au– serve as hydrogen-bond acceptors, interacting with the hydrogen atom of a solitary C–H bond in the CH3CN molecule, respectively. For Cu–, the IGMH δginter isosurface is small and appears nearly completely green, indicating that this interaction is weak. Compared with Cu–, the C–H···Au– hydrogen-bond interaction is exhibited by a larger δginter isosurface with a blue-colored region in the center. This feature suggests that the C–H···Au– interaction is stronger than the corresponding C–H···Cu– interaction.

The calculated binding energy differences of 0.42 and 0.47 eV are in good agreement with the EA shifts of 0.37 eV for Cu–(CH3CN) and 0.42 eV for Au–(CH3CN), respectively (Figure ). It is worth noting that Wang and Bowen have investigated the interaction between Au– and CH4 by combining anion photoelectron spectroscopy with quantum-chemical calculations. In their experimental and theoretical studies, the weak Au–···CH4 interaction perturbs the detachment energies only marginally, giving blue shifts of 0.05 and 0.04 eV, respectively. However, such weak physisorption should not be regarded as a C–H···Au– hydrogen-bond interaction. Consistent with this interpretation, the optimized geometries of M–CH4 (M = Cu, Au) are provided in Table S2 in the Supporting Information. Although the C–H···M– arrangement is linear (∠MHC = 180.0°), the corresponding M···H separations are 3.59 Å for Cu···H and 3.19 Å for Au···H, which are well beyond the typical H···Y distance range for hydrogen bonding. Accordingly, as pointed out by Wang, the Au–(CH4) interaction is better described as a weak charge-induced intermolecular interaction. Energy-decomposition analysis further highlights the fundamental differences between such weakly bound Au–(CH4) complexes and the hydrogen-bonded M–CH3CN systems discussed here (see below).

To better understand the nature of the interaction between Cu–/Au– and CH3CN, we investigated the variation of SAPT interaction terms with respect to the intermolecular separation using the SAPT2+(3)­δMP2 method with the def2-TZVPD basis set. As shown in Figure a, the minimum point of Cu–CH3CN occurs around the position of R­(Cu···H) = 2.8 Å. The repulsive effect purely comes from the exchange term, while all other terms, including electrostatics, induction and dispersion, are attractive. A similar trend is observed for Au–CH3CN, with the minimum point located around R­(Au···H) = 2.6 Å. The figure also shows that the C–H···M– hydrogen bonds in both complexes are dominated by electrostatic interactions over the entire range of M···H separations. Further, we employed SAPT2+(3)­δMP2 method in conjunction with large def2-QZVPD basis to characterize the physical components of the binding energies at the minimum energy geometry. From Figure b, the relative contributions of electrostatics, dispersion, and induction to the total interaction energy are 62.9%, 22.9%, and 14.2% for Cu–CH3CN, and 58.3%, 26.0%, and 15.7% for Au–CH3CN, respectively. SAPT analysis reveals that the interaction is fundamentally electrostatic in nature (∼60%), distinguishing these complexes from typical dispersion-driven physisorbed species. Additionally, the Supporting Information provides an energy-decomposition analysis of the M–CH4 (M = Cu, Au) complexes at their minimum-energy geometries for comparison. As shown in Figure S1, the relative contributions of electrostatics, dispersion, and induction to the total interaction energy are 32.5%, 43.8%, and 23.7% for Cu–CH4, and 27.8%, 45.8%, and 26.4% for Au–CH4, respectively. These results clearly indicate that the M–CH4 interactions are not dominated by electrostatics; instead, dispersion and induction provides the largest contribution. According to the IUPAC definition of the hydrogen bond, interactions that are dominated by dispersion are generally not classified as hydrogen bonds. For example, Ar–CH4 and CH4–CH4 are not considered hydrogen-bonded systems. Thus, Au–CH4 should likewise not be regarded as a hydrogen bond. In contrast, the M–CH3CN (M = Cu, Au) systems studied here provide a well-defined benchmark for C–H···M– hydrogen bonding.

Conclusions

In summary, we provide the first quantitative experimental characterization of an isolated C–H···Au– hydrogen-bonded complex, with bond energies of 0.51 eV (11.8 kcal mol–1) for Au–···CH3CN and 0.43 eV (9.9 kcal mol–1) for Cu–···CH3CN. These results show that C–H···M– interactions can attain strengths comparable to those of many conventional O–H/N–H···anion hydrogen bonds, thereby challenging the common assumption that only strongly polarized X–H groups can act as effective hydrogen-bond donors toward metal anions. Energy decomposition analysis further reveals that electrostatics dominate the interaction, accounting for approximately 60% of the total binding, whereas dispersion and induction make smaller contributions. In contrast to dispersion-dominated M–···CH4 complexes, the present system highlights the decisive role of local electrostatic polarization, particularly the electron-withdrawing effect of the −CN group, in promoting genuine C–H hydrogen bonding to a metal anion. Beyond establishing a benchmark for the C–H···Au– interaction, this work broadens our understanding of hydrogen bonding involving metal anions and establishes a new benchmark for metal-centered hydrogen bonding, positioning the Au– kernel as a potent and directional acceptor for molecular recognition and catalysis.
