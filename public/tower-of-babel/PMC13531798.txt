Hemodynamic effects of dexmedetomidine versus propofol in cardiac surgery: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Mohammad Montaseri, Mehran Saberian, Alun C. Jackson, Fathiyeh Bahramnejad, Fatemeh Bahramnezhad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCardiac surgery, particularly open-heart procedures such as coronary artery bypass grafting and valve surgery, is one of the most common curative options for ischemic heart disease, congenital heart defects, and valvular disease but is also associated with severe hemodynamic hazards. Intravenous anesthetic agents such as propofol and dexmedetomidine are employed to sedate such patients in routine clinical practice. There is, however, conflicting and sporadic evidence in the literature regarding the relative effects of these two agents on hemodynamic parameters. This review systematically analyzed and contrasted the hemodynamic effects of dexmedetomidine and propofol in patients undergoing cardiac surgery.MethodsSystematic searches in the PubMed, Scopus, Embase, Web of Science, CINAHL, Cochrane Library, and Google Scholar databases were performed for randomized controlled trials published between December 2000 and 18 January 2025. Studies in which adult patients underwent cardiac surgery (e.g., coronary artery bypass grafting or valve surgery) and received dexmedetomidine or propofol for intraoperative or immediate postoperative intensive care unit (ICU) sedation were included.This was a PRISMA-guided review, and the extracted data were meta-analyzed and descriptively analyzed. The primary outcomes were hypotension and bradycardia. Secondary outcomes included vasopressor requirements, tachycardia, heart rate, atrial fibrillation (AF), ventricular tachycardia, and bleeding. Odds ratios (ORs) or mean differences (MDs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were pooled via random-effects models. Sensitivity analysis and assessment of publication bias were performed as needed.ResultsCompared with propofol, dexmedetomidine significantly increased the risk for hypotension (OR = 1.76; 95% CI: 1.25–2.48; p < 0.001) and bradycardia (OR = 2.89; 95% CI: 1.28–6.49; p = 0.01). Alternatively, dexmedetomidine significantly reduced vasopressor requirements (OR = 0.52; 95% CI: 0.32–0.84; p = 0.007) and lowered the heart rate (MD = − 4.78 beats/min; 95% CI: − 7.47 to − 2.26; p < 0.001). No considerable differences were observed in tachycardia, ventricular tachycardia, AF, or bleeding. Sensitivity analysis was employed to check for the stability of the findings, and no publication bias was detected.ConclusionThis meta-analysis revealed that, compared with propofol, dexmedetomidine is associated with a greater incidence of hypotension and bradycardia but lower vasopressor requirements. No considerable differences were observed in ventricular tachycardia, AF, bleeding or any other hemodynamic parameters. Evidence for several secondary outcomes remains limited; therefore, findings for less frequently reported endpoints should be interpreted cautiously.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12871-026-04027-z.

Background

More than two million cardiac surgeries are performed each year around the world, primarily for the treatment of ischemic heart disease, correction of congenital cardiac malformations, and valvular disease due to endocarditis, rheumatic heart disease, and atherosclerosis [1, 2]. Despite phenomenal developments in surgical technology and intraoperative care, operations are associated with risks of severe hemodynamic instability, cardiovascular derangement, and high mortality [3–7]. Therefore, the selection of effective and safe anesthetic medications to minimize these adverse outcomes is of paramount importance [8].

Anesthetic management for cardiac surgery is especially demanding from a clinical standpoint because of the need to maintain hemodynamic stability with precision [9]. Among the intravenous sedatives, dexmedetomidine and propofol are significant contributors [10]. Dexmedetomidine is a potent selective α2-adrenergic receptor agonist with clearly documented sedative, anxiolytic, and analgesic effects and little respiratory depression [11, 12].

These characteristics make it an acceptable option in high-risk patients, particularly when spontaneous ventilation should be preserved [13, 14]. Propofol, with its rapid onset and recovery and ability to provide more accurate control of sedation depth, is widely applied in cardiac anesthesia, albeit with the potential to cause side effects such as hypotension and myocardial depression [15, 16].

Several previous systematic reviews and meta-analyses have compared dexmedetomidine with propofol in peri-operative and ICU settings, as well as in adult cardiac surgery patients or broader ICU populations undergoing mechanical ventilation; however, these reviews have mainly emphasized clinical recovery and neurocognitive outcomes, including duration of mechanical ventilation, time to extubation, postoperative delirium, and ICU or hospital length of stay [17–20]. Although selected haemodynamic adverse events, particularly bradycardia and hypotension, were reported as safety outcomes in some of these reviews [18, 19], they were not designed to provide a comprehensive haemodynamic safety synthesis across endpoints such as hypotension, bradycardia, vasopressor requirement, tachycardia, heart rate, atrial fibrillation, ventricular tachycardia, and bleeding. More recently, Peng et al. specifically examined arrhythmic endpoints and found that dexmedetomidine reduced in-hospital ventricular arrhythmias but increased bradycardia compared with propofol in adult cardiac surgery patients [21]; however, that analysis was restricted to arrhythmic endpoints and did not comprehensively evaluate broader haemodynamic safety outcomes. In parallel, Queiroz et al. conducted a large Bayesian network meta-analysis of 79 randomised trials and 29 pharmacological interventions for the prevention of postoperative delirium after cardiac surgery, identifying dexmedetomidine—particularly when combined with melatonin—as one of the highest-ranked strategies for delirium reduction, albeit with low overall certainty of evidence [22]. In addition, some prior syntheses included multiple pharmacological interventions beyond direct dexmedetomidine-versus-propofol comparisons, which may limit their direct applicability to sedation practice in adult cardiac surgery patients [19, 22].

Furthermore, additional randomized controlled trials in adult cardiac surgery patients have continued to accumulate and have not been incorporated consistently across earlier reviews, supporting the need for an updated and focused synthesis of haemodynamic outcomes in this population [23–26].

Therefore, the present systematic review and meta-analysis was undertaken with the aim of presenting a focused comparative evaluation of the haemodynamic effects of dexmedetomidine and propofol in patients undergoing cardiac surgery, in order to provide better evidence to guide sedative choice in this setting.

Methods

Protocol search and registration plan

This protocol for systematic review and meta-analysis was registered on 18 January, 2025, on the PROSPERO database with reference number CRD42025629714. The study was conducted and reported according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement.

Two impartial reviewers of all the literature conducted a systematic search in PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Scopus, CINAHL, and the Cochrane Library from December 2000 to 18 January 2025. Google Scholar (the first ten pages of results) was also used to identify gray literature and potentially relevant studies. The search was restricted to English-language papers, and non-English-language studies were excluded.

In multi-arm or factorial trials, we only included arms in which dexmedetomidine and propofol were compared under the same background regimen (for example, dexmedetomidine + placebo vs. propofol + placebo), and excluded arms receiving additional active sedatives or analgesics that were not balanced between groups, so that the only systematic difference between groups in our meta-analysis was the choice of sedative.

The exclusion criteria were nonrandomized comparisons, review articles, case reports, animal models, pediatric patients, or studies with no direct comparison between dexmedetomidine and propofol.

Additional prognostic outcomes:

In addition to hemodynamic endpoints, we also extracted descriptive data on prognostic outcomes, including intensive care unit (ICU) and hospital length of stay, duration of mechanical ventilation, and postoperative delirium or major complications, when reported. Because these outcomes were not consistently defined or reported across trials, they were summarized qualitatively and were not entered into the quantitative meta-analysis.

Study screening and selection process

EndNote 20 software was used to organize the references and automatically delete duplicate records. Manual verification was subsequently performed to check for the removal of all duplicates.

Independent title and abstract screening by two reviewers identified studies that were potentially eligible. The full texts of the included articles were subsequently assessed for inclusion. Discrepancies were managed via discussion until a consensus was reached.

Data extraction

Key data, including (1) the first author's name; (2) publication year; (3) nation; (4) study type; (5) age (average); (6) surgery (type); (7) dosage, time, and duration of dexmedetomidine and propofol infusion; (8) outcomes; and (9) corresponding odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), were independently extracted from two authors and are listed in Table 1.

Quality assessment

All the studies included in the title and abstract screening were independently evaluated for methodological quality by two reviewers. The appraisal used the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) standardized critical appraisal checklist (2017) [37], which is a widely used, internationally recognized, and validated instrument for assessing study quality in systematic reviews. The checklist covers certain items, such as (1) definitions of the purpose of the study, (2) randomization procedures, (3) comparability of groups at baseline, (4) blinding, (5) use of validated measurement tools for outcomes, and (6) appropriateness of statistical analysis. Each study was given a quality rating of “high,” “moderate,” or “low” according to its sum score. The reviewer discrepancies were resolved by discussion and consensus. The results of the quality assessment of all included studies are reported in Supplementary File 1.

Risk of bias assessment

The Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 tool (RoB 2) was applied to assess the risk of bias in the included trials. The tool examines five key domains, namely, the process of randomization, deviations from interventions as intended, missing data on the outcomes, measurement of the outcome, and selection of the presented results. Each category was rated as “low,” “some concerns,” or “high.” Reviewers completed the assessments independently, and any differing opinions were resolved through discussion and consensus. In addition, a rigorous assessment of the risk of bias was performed to identify common methodological errors and the probability of how they may impact the study results.

Statistical analysis

This meta-analysis was conducted to compare the hemodynamic effects of dexmedetomidine (intervention group) and propofol (control group) in patients undergoing cardiac surgery. The primary outcomes of this meta-analysis were haemodynamic variables (hypotension and bradycardia), and secondary outcomes were vasopressor requirement, tachycardia, heart rate, atrial fibrillation, ventricular tachycardia and bleeding. In addition, when available, we extracted data on prognostic endpoints such as ICU and hospital length of stay, duration of mechanical ventilation and major postoperative complications. However, these prognostic outcomes were not prespecified as primary or secondary endpoints of the review and were reported only in a subset of trials, often without sufficient numerical detail for reliable pooling (e.g. medians without measures of dispersion or no separate data for each treatment arm). To avoid selective, post hoc meta-analyses based on incompletely reported data, these endpoints were summarised qualitatively rather than entered into the quantitative synthesis.

For dichotomous outcomes, odds ratios (ORs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated. When there was a zero event in a cell in the 2 × 2 contingency table, treatment‒arm continuity correction (TACC) was used, adding small values in proportion to the intervention and control group sizes to the concerned cells for effect estimation. In the case of continuous outcomes (heart rate), mean differences (MDs) were used. A random-effects framework was employed for all analyses, and the restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimator was employed to estimate between-study variability.

Statistical heterogeneity was assessed via the I² statistic and Cochran’s Q test. In the presence of significant heterogeneity (I² > 50%), sensitivity analyses were conducted by sequentially excluding studies and by restricting analyses to low-risk-of-bias trials to determine the consistency and robustness of the aggregated estimates.

All the statistical tests were carried out via Stata software version 17. A two-tailed p value of < 0.05 was used to determine statistical significance.

Where data permitted, we conducted subgroup analyses to explore potential effect modification by (i) type of cardiac surgery (CABG-only, valve-only, mixed) and (ii) overall risk of bias (RoB 2: low risk, some concerns, high risk). Subgroup analyses were only interpreted when a subgroup contained a sufficient number of trials to yield minimally informative estimates; subgroups with very few trials were reported but not interpreted. Dose- or regimen-based subgroup analyses were prespecified but were not feasible because dosing definitions and reporting were inconsistent across trials and dose ranges substantially overlapped between groups.

Results

Study selection

The study selection process is summarized in Fig. 1. A total of 25 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility. Several studies were excluded because of the absence of relevant outcome data, inappropriate interventions, nonrandomized designs, or duplicate data. Ultimately, 15 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) were included in the final analysis.

Study characteristics

Fifteen randomized controlled trials published between 2003 and 2024 were included. Two of them employed double-blinding [15, 36], and five employed single-blinding [23, 25, 30, 32, 35]. The trials were conducted in various countries, namely, the United States [23, 27], Canada [28, 30], Turkey [29], China [31, 32, 36], Egypt [24, 33–35], India [15, 26], and Serbia [25], as shown in Fig. 2.

Fig. 2Geographical distribution of the included studies

Geographical distribution of the included studies

All but one were single-center studies [27]. Six trials determined hemodynamic parameters as primary measures [7, 15, 24, 32, 33, 36]. Overall, 1,602 patients who underwent cardiac surgery were enrolled, with between 50 and 289 patients in each sample. Most trials used dexmedetomidine or propofol as the sole sedative, whereas a few studies (e.g. factorial or combination trials) combined these agents with a fixed background regimen such as ketamine or intravenous acetaminophen [23, 33]. In all such cases, the co-interventions were identical across the relevant arms (for example, dexmedetomidine + placebo vs. propofol + placebo), so that the primary difference between groups included in the meta-analysis remained the use of dexmedetomidine versus propofol. Nine studies [15, 23–26, 30, 32, 34, 35] recruited mixed samples of coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) and valvular surgery patients, and four trials [27–29, 33] recruited only CABG patients. One trial [31] recruited only valvular surgery patients, and one [36] compared open-heart surgery overall.

The most frequently reported maintenance infusion dose of dexmedetomidine is 0.2 to 0.7 µg/kg/h [25–27, 30, 33, 35], and that of the propofol most frequently used in maintenance infusion is 25 to 50 µg/kg/min [25, 30, 33].

Risk of bias in studies

Among the 15 studies included, one trial’s overall risk of bias was assessed as low [31], and nine trials’ overall risk of bias was assessed as some concern [15, 23, 25, 27–29, 32, 34, 35]. Five trials’ overall risk of bias was assessed as high [24, 26, 30, 33, 36], of which the risk of bias in 1 trial arose due to deviations from intended interventions [30] and that in 3 trials due to the process of randomization [24, 26, 36]. In addition, a single trial was rated high risk overall, as it had some issues in multiple categories [33]. The overall risk of bias for each study was determined from these scores, and the results are presented in Fig. 3.

Results of syntheses

The following results present the findings of outcome-specific meta-analyses comparing dexmedetomidine and propofol across various cardiac-related outcomes. Studies were included in each meta-analysis if they reported extractable quantitative data for the respective outcome; lack of reporting of the primary outcomes did not preclude inclusion in meta-analyses of secondary outcomes. Accordingly, the number of studies contributing to each meta-analysis varied by outcome, as summarized in Supplementary File2.

Primary outcomes

Hypotension

In relation to hypotension, 7 studies with a total of 807 patients revealed, on average, that patients receiving dexmedetomidine had a 76% increased risk of developing hypotension compared with those receiving propofol (OR = 1.76; 95% CI: 1.25–2.48; p < 0.001), with no heterogeneity present (Fig. 4). No publication bias was indicated by Begg’s test (p = 0.37), as verified by the symmetry of the funnel plot (Fig. 5).

For bradycardia, six studies involving 718 patients yielded data indicating that, on average, patients who were given dexmedetomidine had 2.89-fold greater odds of developing bradycardia than those given propofol did (OR = 2.89; 95% CI: 1.28–6.49; p = 0.01), and there was no heterogeneity among the studies (Fig. 6). Secondary outcomes

A larger number of studies contributed to secondary outcome analyses compared with primary outcomes, as several studies did not report hypotension or bradycardia but reported other hemodynamic outcomes.

Vasopressor requirement

For vasopressor requirements, five trials with 706 patients were analyzed. Patients in the dexmedetomidine group needed, on average, 48% fewer vasopressors than did those in the propofol group (OR = 0.52; 95% CI: 0.32–0.84; p = 0.007), without study heterogeneity (Supplementary File 3-A). Publication bias was not an issue according to Begg’s test (p = 0.81; funnel plot Supplementary File 4-A).

Tachycardia

The outcome of tachycardia was reported in two trials involving 359 participants. There was no between-group difference noted in the dexmedetomidine and propofol groups (OR = 0.77; 95% CI: 0.26–2.34; p = 0.65), with no observed heterogeneity (Supplementary File 3- C). Publication bias was not assessed for this outcome because of the low number of included studies.

Heart rate

Finally, for heart rate, three trials with 280 participants were included. Dexmedetomidine was associated with a greater heart rate reduction than was propofol (MD = − 4.78 bpm; 95% CI: − 7.47 to − 2.26; p < 0.001), but high heterogeneity was observed (I² = 64.28%; Supplementary File 3-D). No evidence of publication bias was detected (Begg’s test p = 0.29; funnel plot Supplementary File 4-C). Exclusion of the Sheikh et al. study [15] in the sensitivity analysis reduced heterogeneity to 9.66% without a loss of statistical significance (mean difference = − 3.75 bpm; 95% CI: − 6.00 to − 1.50; p < 0.001; Supplementary File 3-E).

Atrial fibrillation events

For AF events, the findings of nine trials with a total of 1,058 patients revealed no statistically significant heterogeneity between the propofol and dexmedetomidine groups (OR = 0.69; 95% CI: 0.38–1.25; p = 0.22), and there was moderate heterogeneity (I² = 60.01%; Supplementary File 3-F). There was no publication bias detected by Begg’s test (p = 0.92), and visual assessment of the funnel plot (Supplementary File 4-D) also supported this finding. Sensitivity analysis excluded the studies of Abdallah et al. [24], which greatly reduced heterogeneity (I² = 14.59%) but did not significantly differ (OR = 0.88; 95% CI: 0.60–1.30; p = 0.53; Supplementary File 3-G).

Ventricular tachycardia

For ventricular tachycardia, a total of 384 patients were included in two studies. There was no heterogeneity (Supplementary File 3-H) and no difference between the propofol group and the dexmedetomidine group (OR = 0.13; 95% CI: 0.02–1.13; p = 0.06), and publication bias was not assessed because of the limited number of studies.

Bleeding

Regarding bleeding, four studies with 658 patients were included. The meta-analysis revealed no significant difference between the dexmedetomidine and propofol groups (OR = 0.88; 95% CI: 0.58–1.33; p = 0.54), and heterogeneity was not detected (Supplementary File 3-I). There was no publication bias (p = 0.65 by Begg’s test; funnel plot Supplementary File 4-E).

Other clinically relevant outcomes

Although the primary focus of this meta-analysis was short-term hemodynamic outcomes, several included randomized trials also reported data on clinically important prognostic endpoints such as ICU and hospital length of stay, duration of mechanical ventilation, delirium, and major postoperative complications. Across these studies, dexmedetomidine-based sedation was frequently associated with a shorter duration of mechanical ventilation or earlier extubation compared with propofol [15, 25, 27, 33, 35], whereas its effects on ICU and hospital length of stay were more mixed: some studies reported shorter ICU and/or hospital stay with dexmedetomidine, while others found similar ICU and hospital length of stay in both groups despite improvements in ventilation time or delirium [15, 24, 25, 33, 35]. Overall, major complications such as myocardial infarction, stroke, renal failure, or high-grade atrioventricular block were infrequent and did not differ consistently between sedative regimens [15, 23–25, 28–30, 32, 35, 36]. Because only a subset of trials reported these prognostic outcomes and the available data were often insufficient for formal pooling (for example, medians without measures of dispersion or lack of per-group values), we did not perform a quantitative meta-analysis for these endpoints and present them descriptively instead.

Assessment of publication bias

Publication bias was evaluated via funnel plots and Begg’s rank correlation test. Since there were fewer than 10 studies for each outcome, Begg’s test was used because it is comparatively more powerful than Egger’s test in small-sized meta-analyses. Note, however, that the power to detect publication bias is poor for outcomes with fewer than five studies, and the results should therefore be interpreted with caution(Supplementary File 5) .

Subgroup analyses by risk of bias

For hypotension and bradycardia, the effect estimates in the “some concerns” subgroup was directionally consistent with the overall pooled results, with negligible heterogeneity, supporting robustness of the main findings. For vasopressor requirement, estimates were broadly consistent across RoB strata, although precision was limited in some subgroups. For AF, the low- and high-risk subgroups contained too few trials for reliable interpretation; the “some concerns” subgroup showed no significant difference, with low heterogeneity (Supplementary File 6-A, B,C, D).

Subgroup analyses by surgery type

For hypotension, both CABG-only and mixed-surgery subgroups showed significantly higher odds with dexmedetomidine, consistent with the overall result and with negligible heterogeneity. For vasopressor requirement, the CABG-only subgroup showed a significant reduction with dexmedetomidine, whereas the mixed-surgery subgroup showed a similar direction with wider uncertainty. For AF and bradycardia, some surgery-type strata contained too few trials for reliable interpretation; the mixed-surgery subgroup did not show a statistically significant difference for bradycardia (Supplementary File 6-E, F,G, H).

Subgroup findings should be interpreted with caution, as several strata contained few trials and were therefore not considered informative. Only subgroups with an adequate number of contributing studies were interpreted.

Discussion

Despite substantial advances in cardiac surgery procedures that have significantly reduced postoperative mortality and morbidity, the need for effective pharmacologic agents that can maintain hemodynamic stability intraoperatively and postoperatively remains a priority concern. The present systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted to compare the hemodynamic effects of dexmedetomidine and propofol when administered to cardiac surgery patients, highlighting not only similarities but also important differences between the two medications.

Hypotension

Our results indicated that patients receiving dexmedetomidine had an increased risk of hypotension compared with those receiving propofol. The reason behind this lies in the pharmacological effect of dexmedetomidine, as it is a very selective α₂-adrenergic receptor agonist and inhibits the activity of the sympathetic nervous system, thereby lowering blood pressure and heart rate. On the other hand, propofol primarily acts via agonism of GABA(A) receptors and can also cause hypotension, but it typically has a lower intensity and duration than does dexmedetomidine [38–40]. The findings of Abouali et al.‘s (2020) study also revealed that the incidence of hypotension was significantly greater in patients receiving dexmedetomidine sedation after valve surgery and CABG than in those receiving propofol [18]. Whereas such hypotensive episodes are typically remedied by prompt fluid administration, careful blood pressure monitoring is recommended, with the avoidance of unnecessary fluid boluses [41]. In contrast, Xi et al.‘s (2013) study detected no difference in the risk of hypotension for the two drugs in ICU patients [42], possibly because of variations in patient populations, clinical environments, and dosing regimens.

Vasopressor requirement

The findings demonstrated that patients receiving dexmedetomidine required vasopressors 48% less frequently than those receiving propofol did. Although this observation may at first appear conflicting with respect to the increased frequency of hypotension with dexmedetomidine, it is rationalized on the basis of its pharmacodynamic effects. Dexmedetomidine induces transient hypotension but simultaneously stabilizes sympathetic responses and enhances vascular sensitivity, thereby reducing the net need for vasopressor therapy [43]. On the other hand, propofol induces direct vasodilation and decreases systemic vascular resistance, which generally requires increased vasopressor administration [44, 45].

Bradycardia

The present meta-analysis revealed that, compared with propofol, dexmedetomidine was associated with a 2.89-fold higher risk of bradycardia. A tendency toward a greater reduction in heart rate was also observed in the dexmedetomidine group; however, this analysis was based on only three small trials (280 patients) and therefore has limited statistical power and should be interpreted as exploratory. These patterns are broadly consistent with the previous meta-analyses of Peng et al. (2024) [21], Abouali et al. (2020) [18], and Sattar et al. (2023) [17]. The mechanism involves the activation of α₂-adrenergic receptors, blockade of norepinephrine release, and enhancement of vagal tone, which cumulatively results in the inhibition of sympathetic activity [46]. This action can be particularly significant in cardiac surgical patients, especially those who are also receiving other cardiac medications simultaneously or who have preexisting sinoatrial node dysfunction [47]. In contrast, propofol has a less direct inhibitory effect on the sinoatrial node, and its shorter half-life is responsible for a lower incidence of postoperative bradycardia [48]. From a clinical standpoint, in high-risk populations such as elderly patients, those with sinoatrial node dysfunction, atrioventricular block, cardiomyopathy, or valvular heart disease, propofol may be advantageous over dexmedetomidine [49].

Tachycardia

In the present review, no significant difference in the incidence of tachycardia between propofol and dexmedetomidine was observed. While no prior meta-analysis has investigated cardiac surgery patients, a systematic review of dexmedetomidine compared with propofol in mechanically ventilated adults by Heybati et al. (2022) also revealed no difference in the incidence of tachycardia between the two drugs [19]. However, because this outcome was reported in only two trials in the present study, the findings need to be interpreted with caution.

Atrial fibrillation

The present meta-analysis did not reveal any statistically significant difference between dexmedetomidine and propofol in the occurrence of atrial fibrillation. This finding is consistent with that of Peng et al. (2024) [21] but inconsistent with earlier findings. For example, Peng et al. (2021) reported that, in comparison with propofol, morphine, placebo, and other medications, dexmedetomidine reduced the risk of postoperative atrial fibrillation [21]. Similarly, Wang et al. (2022) [50] and Liu et al. (2020) [51] reported that the administration of dexmedetomidine was associated with a lower occurrence of arrhythmia. There was no direct head‒to-head comparison with propofol in these studies, possibly because the coadministration of multiple agents may have influenced the findings. Moreover, the majority of studies did not differentiate between new-onset and recurrent atrial fibrillation, which could have led to overestimation of incidence rates. Therefore, a more valid evaluation of this outcome must be through well-designed randomized controlled trials with direct comparisons.

Ventricular tachycardia

In our meta-analysis, we did not detect a statistically significant difference in the incidence of ventricular tachycardia between the propofol and dexmedetomidine groups; however, this comparison was informed by only two relatively small trials (384 patients) and is likely underpowered, so the absence of a statistically significant difference should not be interpreted as evidence of no effect. By contrast, a recent review by Peng et al. (2024) [21] reported a lower incidence of ventricular arrhythmias with dexmedetomidine across a broader set of studies. These apparent discrepancies may reflect differences in sample size, patient populations, timing of administration, infusion rates, and dosing regimens. For example, Liu et al. (2020) [51] suggested that continuous infusion without a loading dose may be effective and safe and could mitigate adverse hemodynamic effects. Furthermore, the timing of dexmedetomidine administration (pre- or intraoperative vs. postoperative) may also influence its overall efficacy for ventricular arrhythmia suppression and warrants further evaluation in adequately powered trials.

Our results regarding arrhythmic outcomes partially align with and partially differ from those of Peng et al., who specifically meta-analysed ventricular arrhythmias, atrial fibrillation and bradycardia in seven randomised trials involving 1,004 cardiac surgery patients [21]. In their analysis, dexmedetomidine significantly reduced in-hospital ventricular arrhythmias but increased the risk of bradycardia compared with propofol, with no clear effect on atrial fibrillation. In our more broadly haemodynamic-focused synthesis, dexmedetomidine was likewise associated with a nearly three-fold higher risk of bradycardia, whereas the difference in ventricular tachycardia did not reach statistical significance, and no benefit was observed for atrial fibrillation. These discrepancies may reflect differences in outcome definitions, follow-up windows and the weighting of individual trials between the two meta-analyses, as well as the limited number of events. Overall, both analyses converge on the need for vigilant monitoring of bradycardia when dexmedetomidine is used in cardiac surgical patients.

Bleeding and other hemodynamic parameters

Pooled analysis did not reveal any difference between dexmedetomidine and propofol in terms of intraoperative bleeding. Cardiac output, central venous pressure (CVP), and the incidence of hypovolemia were also comparable between the two groups. These findings demonstrate that both agents have similar effects on fluid balance and overall hemodynamic stability during cardiac surgery. Notably, no relevant systematic reviews or meta-analyses are available in this context, which is a major limitation for broader interpretation. Therefore, future studies should strive to quantify and report these hemodynamic parameters to provide a better picture of the potential differences or true similarities between the two agents.

Our review also needs to be interpreted alongside the recent Bayesian network meta-analysis by Queiroz et al. [22], which evaluated 79 randomised trials and 29 pharmacological interventions for the prevention of postoperative delirium after cardiac surgery. In that analysis, dexmedetomidine—particularly in combination with melatonin—ranked among the most effective regimens for reducing delirium, and dexmedetomidine-based strategies also tended to shorten ICU and hospital stay, although the overall certainty of evidence was low because of between-study variability in dosing, timing and patient populations. In contrast, our meta-analysis restricts the comparison to direct head-to-head trials of dexmedetomidine versus propofol and focuses primarily on haemodynamic endpoints (hypotension, bradycardia, vasopressor requirement and tachyarrhythmias), which were not the central outcomes of the network meta-analysis. Together, the two bodies of evidence suggest that while dexmedetomidine may confer advantages in terms of delirium and early recovery in some settings, these potential benefits need to be weighed against an increased propensity for bradycardia and hypotension when it is used as a sedative alternative to propofol in adult cardiac surgery patients.

Prognostic outcomes

Although the primary focus of this meta-analysis was short-term haemodynamic outcomes, several included randomised trials also reported clinically important prognostic endpoints such as ICU and hospital length of stay, duration of mechanical ventilation, delirium and major postoperative complications. In several trials, dexmedetomidine-based sedation was associated with a shorter duration of mechanical ventilation or earlier extubation compared with propofol, whereas its effects on ICU and hospital length of stay were more mixed, with some studies reporting modest reductions and others showing no clear difference between the two regimens. Overall, major complications such as myocardial infarction, stroke, renal failure or high-grade atrioventricular block were infrequent and did not differ consistently between dexmedetomidine and propofol. Because these prognostic endpoints were not prespecified as primary or secondary outcomes of our review and were reported in sufficient detail only in a subset of trials, we did not undertake formal meta-analyses for these endpoints and instead present a qualitative summary of the available data.

Our findings complement several recent conventional meta-analyses that compared dexmedetomidine with propofol after cardiac surgery. Sattar et al. [17] and Chowdhury et al. [20] synthesised 14–15 randomised trials and reported that dexmedetomidine was associated with shorter duration of mechanical ventilation, reduced ICU and hospital length of stay, and a lower incidence of postoperative delirium compared with propofol. Abouali et al. [18] similarly observed a reduction in time to extubation and ICU stay with dexmedetomidine, although without a clear decrease in hospital length of stay, and reported differences in bradycardia and hypotension between sedatives. Heybati et al. [19] extended these findings to a broader ICU population, showing that dexmedetomidine reduced mechanical ventilation time and delirium risk in cardiac surgical patients but did not significantly change ICU length of stay, while increasing the risk of bradycardia across ICU subgroups. Taken together, these reviews suggest that dexmedetomidine-based sedation may favour early recovery and delirium prevention in selected patients, yet highlight a consistent signal of bradycardia and hypotension that requires careful haemodynamic monitoring.

The meta-analysis demonstrated that, compared with propofol, dexmedetomidine is associated with an increased incidence of hypotension and bradycardia but a reduced requirement for vasopressor support. We did not detect statistically significant differences between the two groups in the incidence of atrial fibrillation, ventricular tachycardia, bleeding, or other hemodynamic variables; however, several of these analyses—particularly those for ventricular tachycardia and some hemodynamic parameters—were based on few and relatively small trials and are likely underpowered, so they should be interpreted as exploratory rather than definitive. Taken together, these findings emphasize the importance of individualizing sedative choices according to each patient’s hemodynamic profile and comorbidities. Properly designed, adequately powered clinical trials with direct comparisons of these drugs are necessary to confirm and refine these results and to optimize sedation regimens in cardiac surgery.

Future trials should therefore be designed to jointly evaluate haemodynamic stability, arrhythmic events, delirium, duration of mechanical ventilation and length of stay within the same study framework, so that the trade-offs between delirium prevention and haemodynamic safety of dexmedetomidine versus propofol can be quantified more precisely.

Limitations

Several planned subgroup analyses were not feasible or not informative because many outcomes were reported by few trials, and further stratification would have produced severely underpowered and potentially unstable estimates. Accordingly, subgroup analyses were restricted to clinically plausible moderators (surgery type and RoB level) and were interpreted only when an adequate number of studies contributed to a subgroup. These analyses should be viewed as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than definitive tests of effect modification.

Despite extensive efforts to collect data and perform a comprehensive analysis, this systematic review and meta-analysis has several important limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, there was substantial clinical and methodological heterogeneity across the included trials, including differences in dosing regimens, sedation protocols, timing and duration of therapy, and patient populations. In particular, the dose sensitivity of dexmedetomidine in some trials is likely to have contributed to heterogeneity in haemodynamic effects; however, dose-stratified subgroup analyses were not feasible because of inconsistent reporting and substantial overlap in dosing ranges across trials. Therefore, residual confounding cannot be excluded.

Second, in several instances the assessment and reporting of risk of bias and methodological quality were limited, which may undermine the credibility and reliability of some pooled estimates despite our use of structured tools. Third, outcome reporting was inconsistent and heterogeneous: key variables such as blood pressure changes and vasopressor requirements were not uniformly defined or reported, and different indices and thresholds were used across studies. This heterogeneity highlights the need for more standardised outcome definitions and data-collection procedures in future trials.

Fourth, most of the available evidence focused on short-term haemodynamic parameters, whereas longer-term outcomes, drug combinations, and broader clinical endpoints were less frequently assessed, restricting the scope and generalisability of our conclusions. Fifth, many outcomes were reported by only a few trials, which reduced statistical power and the precision of pooled estimates. In particular, heart rate was analysed in only three randomised trials including 280 patients, and ventricular tachycardia in only two trials including 384 patients; the corresponding effect estimates therefore have wide confidence intervals and should be viewed as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than definitive. The small number of studies per outcome also limits our ability to reliably detect or characterise publication bias. Furthermore, the effect measures (primarily odds ratios) were extracted as crude, unadjusted effects without accounting for potential confounders, raising the possibility that some associations may partly reflect residual confounding rather than true causal effects. Also, the interpretation of the funnel plot should be approached with caution, as in some analyses fewer than ten studies were available, which limits the reliability of publication bias assessment.

Furthermore, several clinically important prognostic endpoints, including ICU and hospital length of stay, duration of mechanical ventilation and major postoperative complications, were not prespecified as primary outcomes and were reported only in a subset of trials, sometimes without sufficient numerical detail for meta-analysis. Consequently, our assessment of these endpoints is limited to a qualitative description, and the present review should be interpreted primarily as a synthesis of haemodynamic outcomes rather than a definitive evaluation of longer-term prognosis.

Moreover, while we used a uniform definition of hypotension for data extraction (SBP < 90 mmHg or MAP < 65 mmHg), not all trials specifically reported the same thresholds or periods for the definition of hypotension, bradycardia, and other hemodynamic outcomes. Whenever there were differences, we applied the original definition provided by the authors in their publication. This constitutes another source of heterogeneity that ought to be minimized by uniform reporting in future studies.

Finally, as with all systematic reviews, this study is subject to inherent limitations such as selective publication, database-searching constraints, and incomplete access to grey literature. In light of these limitations, future research should prioritise multicentre trials with larger sample sizes, standardised protocols, and harmonised reporting procedures to reduce heterogeneity and enhance the generalisability and robustness of the evidence.

Conclusion and clinical implications

In adult cardiac surgery patients, dexmedetomidine (vs. propofol) was associated with higher odds of hypotension and bradycardia, and lower odds of vasopressor requirement in the available randomized evidence. For other haemodynamic outcomes (e.g., atrial fibrillation, ventricular tachycardia, bleeding, and tachycardia), pooled estimates did not demonstrate statistically significant differences; however, several of these analyses were informed by few trials and limited events and should be interpreted cautiously. Overall, these findings support careful, individualized sedative selection and vigilant haemodynamic monitoring when dexmedetomidine is used, while highlighting the need for larger, well-reported trials to clarify effects on less frequently reported outcomes.

Clinical relevance and implications

The available randomized evidence suggests a trade-off between haemodynamic adverse events and vasoactive support when dexmedetomidine is used instead of propofol in adult cardiac surgery. Clinicians should weigh the increased odds of hypotension and bradycardia against the lower odds of vasopressor requirement, in the context of individual patient risk factors and institutional sedation protocols. Because evidence for several secondary outcomes is limited or underpowered, strong conclusions regarding arrhythmias and bleeding cannot be made from the current data.

Translating evidence into practice

In practice, when dexmedetomidine is selected for sedation after cardiac surgery, close monitoring for hypotension and bradycardia is warranted, with predefined management strategies consistent with local protocols. Given limited evidence for several secondary outcomes, future trials should adopt standardized haemodynamic definitions and report outcomes consistently to enable more definitive synthesis.
