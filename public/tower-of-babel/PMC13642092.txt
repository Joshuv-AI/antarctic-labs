Single‐cell marker gene clustering: A unified deep learning framework for marker gene‐based clustering of single‐cell RNA‐sequencing data
Authors: Shahriar Rahman Niloy, Toushif Muktashid Hasan, Md Saiduzzaman Apu, Fahim Hafiz, Salekul Islam, Riasat Azim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSingle‐cell RNA sequencing (scRNA‐seq) has transformed the study of cellular heterogeneity by making it possible to classify individual cells and their functional states. However, the analysis remains difficult because high dropout rates lead to sparse and noisy expression data. Existing marker gene selection methods are often fragmented, typically relying on a single strategy that overlooks complementary biological signals. Without a unifying framework for denoising and marker identification, clustering can suffer in accuracy, stability, and interpretability. This makes it more difficult to define cell subpopulations and extract meaningful biological insights clearly. We introduce single cell marker gene clustering (scMGC), a unified framework that integrates a denoising autoencoder with multi‐method marker selection for scRNA‐seq clustering. The autoencoder reduces dropout noise, whereas a unified marker gene scoring system balances the contributions of multiple methods to select reliable markers. These markers are then applied in graph‐based clustering to uncover accurate and interpretable cell subpopulations. scMGC was benchmarked against seven state‐of‐the‐art methods across seven scRNA‐seq datasets. On average, it outperformed competing approaches by 31.3% in adjusted rand index and 28.2% in normalized mutual information, showing consistent improvements in clustering accuracy. Enrichment and disease association analyses further validated that the discovered clusters are both biologically meaningful and clinically relevant. The codes and datasets used are available on the GitHub website (srniloy/scMGC).Keywords: clustering, deep learning, marker genes, optimization, scRNA‐seq

INTRODUCTION

In recent years, single‐cell analysis techniques, such as single‐cell RNA sequencing (scRNA‐seq), allow for the characterization of cell states and special functions that may profoundly impact subsequent research. This advancement has catalyzed the development of sophisticated cell clustering techniques. Meanwhile, traditional approaches are effective in classifying scRNA‐seq in varying degrees. However, although sequencing data obtained from such techniques provides stable estimates of cell‐type heterogeneity over a widening array of biological settings, thus maximizing the potential of single‐cell sequencing. Another big challenge remains when scRNA‐seq datasets are marred by a very high dropout rate. In these cases, a majority of expressed transcripts cannot be detected. Hence, the matrix that is produced becomes sparse and noisy. This obscures actual biological signals, as shown in Figure 1. Consequently, reducing the problem to a small but functionally meaningful set of genomic determinants driving this heterogeneity is an inherently difficult problem, often making computational approaches infeasible.

Illustration of the need for a unified marker gene selection strategy to improve downstream analyses in scRNA‐seq. Existing methods select marker genes from high‐dimensional feature spaces using diverse computational approaches. Yet often fail to capture the full distributional characteristics of marker genes. Our proposed method, single‐cell marker gene clustering, integrates multiple state‐of‐the‐art selection techniques into a unified pipeline. Recognizing distinct cell types is essential for interpreting the functional units of an organism within complex biological systems [1]. The advancement of scRNA‐seq analysis has transformed biological studies, enabling tasks such as building cellular trajectories, detecting differentially expressed genes (DEG), and characterizing the boundaries of individual cell subgroups [2]. However, a significant gap remains in the availability of a scalable and generalizable method for selecting marker genes. This deficiency hinders our ability to accurately delineate distinct subpopulations of cells, which is essential for the design of gene expression for transcriptomics. Consequently, there is a pressing need for a method that can effectively denoise the expression data to recover reliable signals before attempting to identify defining features. Given that a multitude of scRNA‐seq methodologies continue to hinge predominantly upon the stratification of cellular populations into discrete classifications as their foundational analytical paradigm [3]. Additionally, the methodology segments into two distinct methods of marker gene‐specific identification from scRNA‐seq data: the careful curation of gene lists based on expert knowledge and the use of differential expression analysis (DEA). Yet, when implemented in large and complex networks, such algorithms tend to encounter local optimum‐related issues that may adversely impact their capacity to yield optimal clustering results.

There have been many computational methods aimed at improving cell type identification and classification from scRNA‐seq data. One of them is Wang et al., who introduced CHLNMF [4]. This method uses a Cauchy loss function instead of the traditional Euclidean distance within the non‐negative matrix factorization (NMF) framework. This replacement helps eradicate the effects of noise. CHLNMF also employs hypergraph regularization to maintain high‐order structural relationships among cells. This provides better clustering accuracy and biologically more relevant cell‐type groupings. It is one of the strengths of this algorithm that it can quickly determine the optimal number of clusters and thus enable precise cell‐type identification. However, it may not always achieve a global optimum due to the employment of half‐quadratic optimization. This is true when applied to complex or noisy datasets.

Huang et al. proposed a high‐order element‐wise weighting strategy within a self‐representative ensemble learning framework, termed scEWE [5]. This approach assigns distinct base clustering weights to individual cells. These weights facilitate the careful construction and optimization of the consensus matrix. Furthermore, high‐order information between cells is extracted, enhancing the representation of similar relationships among them.

Wang et al. presented scHFC [6], a hybrid fuzzy clustering technique that combines fuzzy C‐means (FCM) with the Gath–Geva (GG) algorithm, further refined via natural computation. The method uses principal component analysis (PCA) to make scRNA‐seq data less complex. Then, the enhanced FCM makes a membership matrix that helps the GG algorithm make the final clustering results. They also suggested a multi‐index comprehensive estimation strategy that uses four clustering effectiveness indices to find the best number of clusters. Simultaneously, Zhu et al. proposed scMEB [7], a method for finding DEG across different cell types and unlabeled samples that does not depend on clustering. scMEB cuts down on false negatives in DEG detection and ranks genes by minimizing the enclosing ball of stably expressed genes. It also ranks genes based on their distance from the center of the sphere. The use of PCA makes the method even more accurate and efficient. In addition to these methods, Missarova et al. proposed geneBasis [8], a computational framework that finds useful gene subsets from scRNA‐seq data. GeneBasis allows clustering and visualizing data with small but biologically important gene sets while keeping the diversity and structure of cells.

Wang et al. [9], on the other hand, differ from traditional low‐rank representation (LRR) techniques by dynamically adjusting their dictionary matrix during optimization. This adaptability makes it well‐suited for scRNA‐seq data, allowing direct subspace clustering without relying on spectral clustering. Consequently, it reduces noise interference and enhances cell‐type annotation accuracy. Nonetheless, the high noise levels and significant missing values in scRNA‐seq data pose challenges. Using the raw data as the basis for constructing the low‐dimensional subspace representation may hinder the accuracy of the LRR matrix. Furthermore, the clustering method used may not align with the learned affinity matrix, limiting the ability to achieve optimal clustering results.

Shu et al. introduced scMMN [10], a deep learning framework that amalgamates contrastive learning and graph‐based methodologies to generate multi‐view representations of scRNA‐seq data. By using k‐nearest neighbors (kNN), diffusion mapping, and graph Laplacian filters together, scMMN improves clustering performance, especially in cross‐species datasets and complex tissue structures. It also shows big gains in scalability and flexibility compared to traditional graph‐based frameworks. Yin et al. also created CellTICS [11], an interpretable neural network for identifying cell types and sub‐cell types. It uses Reactome pathway hierarchies to make biological data easier to understand. CellTICS uses a multi‐predictive‐layer strategy to get better results and show important pathways that explain expression stochasticity. This is better than other methods and gives new information about cellular heterogeneity in situations such as aging and disease.

Zhang et al. introduced a feature selection method based on gene expression decomposition (FEED) [12], which utilizes a gene correlation calculation technique to assess the relationships between genes from a distributional perspective. Following this, a permutation‐based approach is employed to establish the threshold for gene importance, allowing for the identification of marker gene subsets. Consequently, genes with high scores are retained as marker genes.

Vlot et al. introduced SEMITONES [13], a method for identifying markers in single‐cell omics without clustering. It recognizes features that are enriched in neighborhoods of cells based on continuous developmental trajectories and distance‐based sampling, such as radial basis function kernel on uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP). The software circumvents predefined clusters by using linear regression to test feature enrichment significance on shuffled data.

Nelson et al. developed SMaSH [14], a scalable framework for the selection of marker genes in scRNA‐seq data utilizing supervised learning techniques. SMaSH uses ensemble models such as XGBoost or deep neural networks with Shapley value attribution to rank genes by their classification value. This makes it possible to find cell‐type and tissue‐specific markers accurately. It has been demonstrated to surpass conventional linear approaches in terms of both accuracy and specificity, even with highly granular datasets. Its compatibility with the ScanPy framework renders it widely applicable to subsequent tasks, including spatial transcriptomics. Dai et al. also came up with COSG [15], a method based on cosine similarity that is fast and accurate for finding marker genes in many single‐cell modalities. COSG quantifies gene expression specificity in relation to an idealized cell‐type‐specific marker, demonstrating resilience to variations in scale and noise. It is much more accurate and faster than traditional statistical methods, and it works well even with datasets with millions of cells. It can also be used for scATAC‐seq and spatial transcriptomics, which makes it useful for a wide range of applications in cell‐type annotation and biological discovery.

Our approach attempts to improve the existing clustering methods by integrating autoencoders and combining clustering techniques that can sufficiently optimize feature learning operations, as deep learning has systematically catalyzed finer accuracy over single‐cell studies. However, these technologies require the purification of cell populations before sorting. Moreover, large‐scale datasets require expensive computational resources and pose challenges in segregating adjacent cell cultures within tissues. Compared to the traditional methods, we considered three different frameworks for our benchmark analysis to evolve our pipeline: CELLTICS [11], COSG [15], and SMaSH [16]. The wider array of performance from other independent models that enhance learning capabilities yields a greater variation of performance across different data sets. Furthermore, the model leverages the knowledge acquired from each method to identify and list the specific marker genes that characterize cell type features. Every technique possesses unique strengths and weaknesses, demanding that the choice of a particular technique be tailored to the specific traits of the dataset and objectives of the analysis. By employing a graph‐based clustering strategy, our framework reduces the likelihood of becoming confined to local optima, thereby yielding more stable and consistent results compared to traditional clustering approaches. This substantially improves the ability of our method to cope with issues of scalability and convergence to local optima. Finally, it reduces the high computational demands often observed in widely adopted clustering techniques.

Despite the widespread use of full‐expression or highly variable gene representations, clustering performance in scRNA‐seq analysis is often hindered by high dimensionality, dropout noise, and the inclusion of genes that are weakly related to cell identity. Marker genes provide a biologically grounded alternative by capturing cell‐type‐specific transcriptional programs that directly define cellular phenotypes. By focusing on genes that are consistently expressed within cell types while remaining distinct across populations, marker gene‐based representations enhance the signal‐to‐noise ratio and reduce the influence of irrelevant or noisy features. This property is particularly beneficial for graph‐based clustering methods, which rely on local similarity structures and are sensitive to noise and sparsity. Consequently, clustering driven by marker gene expression is expected to yield more coherent cell neighborhoods, improved cluster separability, and greater biological interpretability, motivating the design of unified frameworks, as shown in Figure 1 that explicitly integrate marker gene identification with downstream clustering.

To directly leverage the advantages of marker gene‐based representations while addressing sparsity, noise, and inconsistent feature selection, we introduce single‐cell marker gene clustering (scMGC), a new framework that greatly improves scRNA‐seq analysis through the combination of denoising autoencoders and selection of multi‐method markers used in the gene detection pipeline. A specialized denoising autoencoder first addresses high dropout rates by learning to impute missing values and suppress noise and yields a clean expression matrix. From these denoised data, scMGC then unifies three different marker gene selection methods to take advantage of each of their complementary strengths and produce a complete candidate gene set. An important component of our framework is the unified marker gene (UMG) scoring system. This system conducts feature selection by giving each method a dynamic weighting. The weight is a function of the method’s specific clustering performance. This allows the framework to intelligently combine each method into a single set of high‐confidence marker genes. This selected set of genes is then applied to graph‐based clustering to obtain precise and biologically relevant cell subpopulation identification. Through the unification of each of the pieces of our framework, scMGC successfully addresses the shortcomings of prior methods and represents a scalable, accurate, and interpretable solution to distinguishing cellular heterogeneity.

Moreover, our model was able to outperform similar applications when dealing with a wide variety of scRNA datasets. Through appropriate treatment of the diverse forms of noise while training, the model performs better on various datasets, thereby rendering it well‐suited for analyzing large‐scale scRNA‐seq data. We compare benchmarking with seven other models, which illustrates the overall superiority of our model across a diverse set of evaluation metrics. The experimental outcomes provide additional evidence that our framework effectively tackles major challenges in single‐cell data annotation, performing strongly in areas such as precise cell type identification and clustering. These results establish our approach as both dependable and efficient for supporting progress in single‐cell biology. The main contributions of this study are summarized as follows:

RESULTS

Clustering performance

We compared scMGC to eight marker–gene‐based methods across seven scRNA‐seq data sets (Biase, Goolam, Kolod, Kumar, Pollen, Robert, and Chu2). DUBStepR identifies a stable marker gene set by leveraging gene–gene correlations together with a Density Index that quantifies inhomogeneity in feature space [17]. Expr selects genes based on their overall expression level, following the baseline approach used in systematic clustering benchmarks [18]. FEAST ranks genes by iteratively performing initial clustering and estimating feature significance via an F‐statistic, refining the feature set through a validation step [19]. GiniClust detects both common and rare cell populations by selecting genes with a high Gini index, capturing skewed, sparsely expressed markers [20]. HVG selects highly variable genes relative to their mean expression, as implemented in the Seurat pipeline [21]. SCMarker performs ab initio marker gene selection using discriminative expression and mutual information criteria, without requiring predefined cell clusters [22]. Clustering performance was assessed using the adjusted rand index (ARI) [23] and the normalized mutual information (NMI) [24], which are widely accepted measures for evaluating predicted cluster assignments relative to reference labels. As shown in Figure 3, scMGC always achieved maximum ARI values across all datasets, often having a distinct lead relative to other methods. For example, in the Biase dataset, scMGC correctly determined the ground truth cell types with ARI 1.00 while the closest baseline method, FEAST, only achieved ARI 0.84 and highly variable genes (HVG) further went down to 0.544. Once again, in the Pollen dataset, scMGC achieved ARI 0.956 while distinctly outperforming other methods such that none of them achieved higher than 0.82. The superiority of scMGC is even more notable on difficult datasets. Baseline methods returned very low ARI scores (less than 0.3) in Kolod and Goolam in most cases, whereas scMGC significantly improved clustering with ARI 0.58 on Kolod and ARI 0.54 on Goolam. Figure 4 confirms the same phenomenon on NMI: scMGC returned NMI 0.711 on Kolod and 0.721 on Goolam, which is a two‐fold better solution (e.g., feature selection method based on gene expression decomposition [FEED] only returned NMI 0.447 on Kolod). On difficult sets such as Robert, where several methods actually returned near‐random or negative‐worth ARI clustering outputs, scMGC again showed good performance (ARI 0.621, NMI 0.609). On sets where separations were clearer such as Kumar and Chu2, scMGC again led the field, achieving ARI 0.744 and NMI 0.842 on Kumar and a virtually perfect recovery on Chu2 (ARI 0.976, NMI 0.978).

Overall, these results as listed in Tables 1 and 2, depicted in Figures 3 and 4 are definitive proof that scMGC is superior to existing methods of clustering on a consistent basis. Overall, scMGC achieved substantial performance gains, yielding weighted average improvements of 31.3% in ARI and 28.2% in NMI relative to the second‐best method, consistently outperforming all competing approaches across datasets.

To further assess scalability beyond small benchmark datasets, we additionally evaluated scMGC on a larger Tabula Sapiens dataset containing 7276 cells. On this dataset, scMGC achieved an ARI of 0.4600 and an NMI of 0.5714, demonstrating stable clustering performance under increased data scale and heterogeneity. These results indicate that the proposed framework maintains robustness when applied to substantially larger scRNA‐seq datasets.

Computational complexity and efficiency

This section presents a comparison of scMGC across the seven datasets with respect to runtime efficiency, memory consumption, and the number of detected genes. This precise computational comparison highlights that the three marker gene detection approaches have significant differences in performance. CellTICS demonstrated remarkable computational efficiency by completing the datasets in time ranges from 0.06 to 0.1406 s while keeping relatively small memory profiles ranging from 12.00 to 152.44 MB. Thus, this makes it highly suitable for rapid initial analyses. COSG showed a medium performance level, with processing times between 0.0625 and 0.2969 s and memory demands between 19.84 and 124.34 MB; thus, it presents a good balance between speed and comprehensiveness.

SMaSH, on the other hand, although having the capability to identify the most marker genes 4261 genes for the Pollen dataset needed a lot more computing power, taking between 3.1875 and 26.3906 s and requiring between 35.04 and 318.10 MB of memory. Its demands were especially high for big datasets, such as Kolod, which took up 25.2188 s and 318.10 MB of memory. The usage patterns seen were directly linked to the dataset size and complexity; small datasets, such as Biase, containing 25,737 genes, permitted real‐time processing times of less than 0.08 s for all methods, whereas intricate datasets, such as Kolod, with 10,685 genes, and Pollen, with 14,805 genes, had a noticeable impact on SMaSH’s computational demands. The results suggest the following guidance for choosing the appropriate method based on particular research goals: CellTICS is preferred for time‐critical exploratory analysis, COSG for research that requires a middle ground of speed and comprehensiveness, and SMaSH for exhaustive investigations where the priority of maximum gene detection makes the added computational burden worthwhile, particularly when working with smaller datasets or when high‐performance computing capabilities are being utilized.

Furthermore, Table 3 compares the computational efficiency of three makers’ identification algorithms: CellTICS, COSG, and SMaSH. CellTICS is the most efficient algorithm with execution times between 0.06 and 0.14 s and the lowest memory usage requirement across all datasets. COSG also displays competitive execution times, often being the best‐performing algorithm on certain datasets such as Goolam and Robert, although it consumes almost twice the amount of random access memory (RAM) needed by CellTICS. On the contrary, SMaSH is the least efficient algorithm, with execution times that are slower by up to two orders of magnitude (e.g., Pollen with 26.39 s) and with the highest memory usage requirement of 318.10 MB. The UMG scoring system seamlessly integrates the three algorithms to optimize their performance and ensure efficient overall execution while performing maker identification with high accuracy. We further examined scalability on the larger Tabula Sapiens dataset (7276 cells). scMGC completed clustering in 4.20 s with a memory usage of 2347.56 MB. For comparison, SMaSH required 89.78 s with 254.66 MB of memory, while COSG required 8.77 s with 4683.91 MB of memory. These results demonstrate that scMGC achieves a favorable balance between runtime efficiency and memory consumption when scaling to larger datasets.

Differential expression and GSEA

In addition to validating the biologically meaningfulness of our marker gene‐based clustering for seven datasets, we conducted a differential gene expression analysis using DESeq2 [25], a widely used statistical framework for identifying genes that are significantly differentially expressed between groups by modeling count‐based RNA‐seq data. It identified genes with significant differential expression among cluster groups. The findings indicated highly significant differential expression patterns for many genes at p < 0.01, indicating robust transcriptional variation across clusters. As illustrated in Figure 5, there is a clear overlap between the DEG derived from the raw dataset and the marker genes identified by our clustering framework, further supporting the biological consistency and reliability of our method.

To further investigate the clinical relevance of the identified marker genes, functional enrichment of the identified marker genes was examined with the DAVID bioinformatics platform and the generalized anxiety disorder (GAD) disease enrichment module. The enrichment results are summarized in Table 4. The analysis identified strong associations between the marker genes from the Chu2 dataset and several diseases, such as ovarian cancer, spinal cord injury, breast cancer, type 2 diabetes, and prostate cancer, thus highlighting the clinical relevance of the clusters. A cluster‐level mapping of GAD disease associated genes onto Leiden clusters was performed to examine their expression distributions across cells. The analysis revealed structured cluster‐specific enrichment patterns rather than uniform expression across all clusters, indicating that disease signals arise from defined transcriptional subpopulations. Detailed visualizations and comprehensive interpretations of these patterns, including cluster‐ordered heatmaps, hierarchical clustering, and dot‐plot analyses, are presented in Supporting Information S1: Section S2 with Figures S2–S5.

Performance on rare and closely related cell types

In the rare cell type scenario, extremely low‐abundance populations such as oocytes (0.23%), spermatogonia (0.9%), and male germ cells (1.6%) were evaluated separately. scMGC achieved strong clustering performance on rare populations, with an ARI of 0.9447 and an NMI of 0.9250, indicating accurate identification despite severe class imbalance. The silhouette score of 0.2073 reflects the expected compactness limitation of rare populations while maintaining clear cluster separation.

To assess fine‐grained discrimination, we applied scMGC to closely related germ cell developmental stages (spermatogonium, spermatocyte, and spermatid). These cell types represent consecutive stages of spermatogenesis and are known to share overlapping transcriptional programs. scMGC achieved an ARI of 0.7163 and an NMI of 0.6716, demonstrating effective separation among highly similar cell states. Notably, the silhouette score reached 0.4570, indicating strong structural separation in the embedding space despite biological similarity.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we presented scMGC, a unified framework that represents a significant advance in marker gene‐based clustering of scRNA‐seq data. scMGC resolves the fundamental obstacles of heterogeneity, sparsity, and noise in scRNA‐seq data by incorporating a denoising autoencoder and graph‐based clustering with multiple marker gene selection strategies. A central innovation of our approach is the UMG scoring system, which adaptively combines the strengths of individual marker selection methods to yield a stable and biologically reliable feature set.

Across seven benchmark datasets, scMGC consistently achieved higher ARI and NMI scores than competing methods, with particularly strong advantages on more difficult cases such as Kolod, Goolam, and Robert. These datasets frequently produce near‐random or unstable outputs when using baseline methods, but scMGC maintained stable clustering performance. On cleaner datasets such as Pollen, Kumar, and Chu2, scMGC preserved its superior accuracy, confirming both robustness and generalizability. Beyond numerical performance, scMGC’s clusters were biologically validated. The discovered marker genes correlate with significant biological pathways and disease associations, such as breast cancer, type 2 diabetes, and ovarian cancer, according to differential expression and enrichment analyses. This demonstrates that scMGC not only improves clustering accuracy but also produces interpretable results with clinical relevance. From a robustness perspective, rare cell populations and closely related developmental states represent two major challenges for single‐cell clustering methods. The strong performance of scMGC on extremely rare cell types demonstrates its robustness to severe class imbalance. Moreover, its ability to separate closely related germ cell stages highlights its sensitivity to subtle transcriptional differences along developmental trajectories. Together, these results indicate that scMGC is well suited for both coarse‐grained cell type identification and fine‐grained resolution of continuous biological processes. Although the framework shows strong performance, some limitations remain. The current evaluation primarily focuses on benchmark datasets with available ground‐truth annotations. Although we additionally validated scMGC on a larger dataset containing 7276 cells, experiments on ultra‐large datasets (e.g., >100,000 cells) were not conducted due to computational resource constraints. Future work will extend scalability validation to such large‐scale single‐cell atlases to further demonstrate the efficiency of the framework in modern high‐throughput settings. Furthermore, our marker selection module (SMaSH, COSG, and CellTICS) requires pre‐defined cell type labels to identify marker genes. In fully unsupervised scenarios, an initial clustering step would be required to generate pseudo‐labels before applying the scMGC framework. In addition, scMGC currently relies solely on transcriptomic information; future extensions may incorporate multi‐omics or trajectory‐based analyses to further enhance biological insights.

Overall, scMGC offers a robust and interpretable solution for marker gene‐based clustering of scRNA‐seq data, contributing both methodological improvements and biological insight to single‐cell genomics research. The integration of denoising autoencoders with a unified multi‐method marker selection strategy addresses long‐standing challenges of sparsity, noise, and inconsistent feature selection that have hindered single‐cell analysis. The UMG scoring system, by adaptively weighting each marker selection method based on clustering performance, ensures that the most biologically informative genes drive downstream analysis, enhancing both accuracy and interpretability. As single‐cell technologies continue to generate data of increasing scale and complexity, frameworks such as scMGC that combine computational rigor with biological grounding will be essential for extracting meaningful insights from the next generation of transcriptomic studies.

CONCLUSION

This study presents scMGC, a deep learning framework designed to improve the clustering of scRNA‐seq data through the integration of denoising, marker gene selection, and graph‐based community detection methods. The UMG scoring system is fundamental to the framework, as it adaptively integrates various marker selection strategies to generate stable and biologically reliable feature sets. In evaluations across seven benchmark datasets, scMGC demonstrated superior clustering accuracy compared to established baseline methods. The performance advantage was particularly evident in sparse and heterogeneous datasets, where current methods encountered difficulties in recovering coherent cell populations. In addition to numerical metrics, downstream differential expression and enrichment analyses validated that the identified clusters align with significant pathways and disease associations, thereby reinforcing the computational and biological value of the method. The framework demonstrates scalability and computational efficiency, rendering it suitable for extensive single‐cell investigations. Future extensions may include multi‐omics integration, trajectory inference, or transfer learning to enhance its scope. Overall, scMGC offers a robust and interpretable solution for marker gene‐based clustering of scRNA‐seq data, contributing both methodological improvements and biological insight to single‐cell genomics research.

Preprocessing

Low‐quality cells and genes with negligible expression were removed, a process known to reduce technical noise and mitigate sparsity [34, 35]. Data loading and conversion to the Scanpy AnnData format enabled effective manipulation and visualization. All preprocessing was performed using the Scanpy [36] and Scikit‐learn [37] libraries. Data cleaning filters out low‐quality data, preventing noise from distorting results. Normalization and log transformation ensure consistent data and eliminate sequencing depth biases.

The raw gene expression values, X raw, represent gene activity in each cell. The total sum of counts per cell is the sum of gene expressions in a cell, and a scaling factor (usually 10,000) normalizes the data across cells.

After X normalized as shown in Equation (1), a logarithmic transformation is then applied to stabilize the data and reduce the impact of large variations, resulting in X log, the log‐transformed values as shown in Equation (2). In this study, each dataset was analyzed individually using a preprocessing workflow designed to minimize technical variation, including quality control filtering, log transformation, and PCA. To mitigate potential batch effects, our pipeline provides optional correction methods, including Harmony, which adjusts PCA embeddings, and batch balanced k‐nearest neighbors (BBKNN), which constructs a batch‐aware neighbor graph for clustering and visualization. Most datasets lack explicit batch metadata, but inspection of PCA and uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) embeddings indicated that variation was primarily driven by biological factors rather than batch, so batch correction was applied only when necessary.

De‐noising

scRNA sequencing data often contain many zero values due to technical issues, called dropouts. To improve data quality, we used an autoencoder model to remove noise and recover missing gene expression values. This method applied the log transformation and standard scaling and added 10% of non‐zero expression values were randomly masked to simulate technical dropout during training. This percentage was chosen as a conservative corruption level that introduces sufficient sparsity to improve model robustness without overwhelming true biological signal, consistent with prior deep learning‐based scRNA‐seq denoising studies reporting effective artificial dropout rates in the range of 5%–20% [38, 39]. The autoencoder compresses and reconstructs the data using layers with ReLU and sigmoid activations, batch normalization, and dropout. The model was trained using the Adam optimizer and a custom loss function that treats zero and non‐zero values differently. This helps reduce the overestimation of missing values.

SMaSH

SMaSH is a marker gene selection framework based on ensemble learning [42]. Genes are ranked using machine learning classification models such as random forest, balanced random forest, or XGBoost (with XGBoost [16] as the default). The ranking is performed by sorting genes according to their total Gini importance or mean Shapley value, with the latter applied in network mode. From this ranking, a set of relevant markers is produced for each class defined by the user, where the top five markers per class are selected by default.

To ensure robustness, a final heterogeneity check is performed when multiple samples are analyzed, verifying that the selected marker genes are uniformly distributed across at least 70% of the samples included in the dataset. This step increases the reliability of the identified markers by minimizing sample‐specific bias.

Clustering algorithm

The Louvain algorithm, originally proposed by Blondel et al. [44], is a widely used and computationally efficient approach for community detection in large‐scale networks. This method relies on optimizing a quality function called modularity. This modularity measures how well a network is partitioned into communities. It performs this measurement by comparing the observed density of edges within communities. This density is then weighed against the density expected in a randomized network. The procedure consists of two iterative steps. Initially, every node is assigned to its own community. Subsequently, each node is evaluated for reassignment to the community of one of its neighbors, and the move is accepted when it yields the maximum improvement in modularity. In the second phase, a new graph is constructed where each community identified in the first phase becomes a single node. Edges between these new nodes are weighted based on the total weight of edges between the corresponding communities. These two phases are repeated on the reduced graph until no further increase in modularity is possible. This hierarchical and greedy approach allows the Louvain algorithm to scale efficiently to large networks while producing high‐quality community structures.

GSEA

GSEA was applied to explore the biological significance of the DEG. GSEA evaluates whether a predefined set of genes is non‐randomly distributed across a ranked list based on expression changes. An enrichment score is calculated to indicate how strongly a gene set is concentrated near either the top or bottom of the ranked list. The score is then normalized, and its statistical significance is estimated via permutation testing. Gene sets with FDR‐adjusted p‐values below a chosen threshold were identified as significantly enriched, revealing key pathways and functional annotations associated with specific cell populations [47].

Robustness analysis on rare and closely related cell types

To evaluate the robustness of scMGC under challenging biological scenarios, we conducted two additional analyses: (i) rare cell type detection and (ii) discrimination of functionally closely related cell types. Rare cell types were defined as those comprising less than 5% of the total cell population. For closely related cell types, we focused on germ cell developmental stages (spermatogonium, spermatocyte, and spermatid), which originate from the same lineage and exhibit highly similar transcriptional profiles [25]. For each scenario, scMGC was applied independently, and clustering performance was evaluated using ARI, NMI, and silhouette score.
