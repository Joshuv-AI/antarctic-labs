One Index for Subsumption and Roll-up across Time, Geography, and Ontology
Madhulatha Mandarapu, Sandeep Kunkunuru

Abstract
Time-series, geospatial, and ontology systems each maintain a hierarchy — day ⊑ \sqsubseteq month ⊑ \sqsubseteq year , zip ⊑ \sqsubseteq city , is-a / part-of — and each indexes it in a separate silo. We observe these are all subsumption posets , with one recurring workload: order testing (is x x under y y ?) and hierarchical roll-up (aggregate a measure over everything under y y ). We present OEH , a single declarable index that, by a cheap structural probe, encodes a hierarchy as a nested-set order-embedding (trees) or a chain decomposition (low-width DAGs), and answers both subsumption and index-resident monoid roll-up from one structure. On five real hierarchies — Gene Ontology, NCBI Taxonomy (1.3M), GeoNames (330k), a 2.6M-node calendar, and git commit DAGs — OEH on trees matches a 2-hop index on query latency using ∼ \sim half the space and building 6–7 × \times faster, and adds roll-up that 2-hop cannot. Its roll-up matches TimescaleDB’s continuous aggregates exactly and in the same latency regime, while also answering subsumption. On high-width DAGs the chain index is declined and 2-hop dominates. Order-embedding is classical; our contribution is the unification and the structure-selected index over subsumption and index-resident roll-up.

3
The OEH index

A cheap structural probe (the “knob”) picks the encoding.

Trees
\to
nested-set.

A DFS assigns each node an interval
[\textsf{in},\textsf{out}]
;

x\sqsubseteq y\iff\textsf{in}(y)\leq\textsf{in}(x)\wedge\textsf{out}(x)\leq\textsf{out}(y)
(2-D
containment). The subtree of
y
is the contiguous
in

[\textsf{in}(y),\textsf{out}(y)]
,
so roll-up is a
Fenwick range-sum in
O(\log n)
; two integers per node.

Low-width DAGs
\to
chain decomposition.

A path partition gives each node a
(\textit{chain},\textit{pos})
; for each node
v
and chain
c

\textsf{reach}[v][c]

position on
c
(Jagadish’s chain index
[
15
]
); the chain count relates to the poset
width
[
8
]
. Subsumption is
O(\textit{width})
. Crucially,
the descendants of
v

c
are the contiguous
suffix
from
\textsf{reach}[v][c]
, so
set-semantics roll-up is
\sum_{c}\textsf{suffix-sum}_{c}

Width cap.

Chain space is
O(n\cdot\textit{width})
; it only beats a 2-hop index while width
is small. OEH declines chain mode above
\sim\!8\sqrt{n}
(so chain space stays
\sim\!O(n^{1.5})
) and
defers to 2-hop, which is the right substrate for high-width DAGs (§
4

Index-resident roll-up.

In both encodings the partial aggregate is answered
from
the
index in
O(\log n)
(trees) or
O(\textit{width})
(DAGs), not by an engine aggregation over the group.
This is the capability that distinguishes OEH from prior hierarchy indexes (§
5
).

6
Limitations and honest findings

•

Static only.
OEH supports no online insert/delete; on the dynamic axis
[
6
,
10
]
are ahead. We do not claim a dynamism advantage.

•

Chain mode rarely wins on real DAGs.
Real multi-parent DAGs (ontologies, merge histories)
are high-width, so chain declines and 2-hop wins; low-width multi-parent DAGs are uncommon.

•

Roll-up loses on tiny subtrees
(the
O(\log n)
constant exceeds
O(\textit{small})
).

•

The TimescaleDB comparison is in-process Python vs. SQL — same regime, not a system benchmark.

•

Order-embedding, nested-set, and dominance are prior art (§
5
).

•

GRAIL/PLL are re-implementations (validated exact vs. the oracle); GeoNames is a near-tree DAG
(0.9% multi-parent kept to one canonical parent); the chain decomposition is greedy and a near-minimum
cover would be smaller.

7
Conclusion

Relationship hierarchies unify the hierarchies that time-series, spatial, and ontology systems index
separately. OEH is one structure-selected index that answers subsumption and index-resident roll-up
across all three domains, validated on real data and against a production time-series engine. The honest
boundary — high-width DAGs defer to 2-hop — and a future dynamic variant are the natural next steps.
