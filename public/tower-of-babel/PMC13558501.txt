Achieving Minimal Disease Activity in Atopic Dermatitis Is Associated with Fewer Flares, Reduced Healthcare Resource Utilization, and Improved Disease Control: Analysis from the Real-World MEASURE-AD Study
Authors: Jonathan I. Silverberg, Vimal H. Prajapati, Susanne Abraham, Alan D. Irvine, Melinda J. Gooderham, Emma Guttman-Yassky, Hideaki Tanizaki, Marius Rademaker, Shirley H. Chen, Alvaro Moreira, Richard Ta, Wan-Ju Lee, Kilian Eyerich
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionAtopic dermatitis (AD), a chronic inflammatory condition, is the leading cause of global burden from skin disease. Optimal targets for clinician-reported outcomes (ClinROs) and patient-reported outcomes (PROs), including minimal disease activity (MDA; defined as simultaneous achievement of ≥ 1 optimal ClinRO and ≥ 1 optimal PRO), were established following the Aiming High in Eczema/Atopic Dermatitis (AHEAD) initiative. This study evaluated the impact of MDA achievement on flare activity, healthcare resource utilization, and patient-reported disease control in adults with moderate-to-severe AD.MethodsThis retrospective post hoc analysis used data from the real-world cross-sectional MEASURE-AD study and included adults with moderate-to-severe AD across 28 countries. Three definitions were used to identify achievement of MDA. Outcomes were measured using patient-reported questionnaires evaluating flare activity and healthcare resource utilization over the preceding 6 months, as well as patient-reported disease control assessed at the time of the questionnaire. Statistical analyses compared patients achieving MDA targets with non-MDA groups using Kruskal–Wallis tests.ResultsPatients achieving MDA experienced fewer flares (means ranging from 0.8 to 1.4) compared with non-MDA groups (3.8–9.8) over the preceding 6 months. Flares were shorter in duration, with 9.9–13.4% of patients who achieved MDA reporting flares longer than 7 days compared with 26.4–51.1% of patients across non-MDA groups. Patients who met neither optimal ClinRO or PRO reported a 2.2- to 2.7-fold increase in average number of healthcare visits compared with MDA groups. More patients who achieved MDA indicated complete agreement that their AD was effectively controlled (86.7–94.2%) compared with those who did not achieve MDA (19.2–74.1%).ConclusionsAchievement of MDA in patients with moderate-to-severe AD was associated with less frequency and duration of flares, reduced healthcare resource utilization, and increased patient-reported disease control compared with those who did not achieve MDA. These findings emphasize the importance of holistic assessments when evaluating treatment outcomes in AD.Trial Registration ClinicalTrials.gov Identifiers NCT03569293 (Measure Up 1), NCT03607422 (Measure Up 2), and NCT03568318 (AD Up).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s13555-026-01865-0.

Introduction

Atopic dermatitis (AD) is a leading cause of global burden from skin disease due to its high prevalence and patient burden [1]. Hallmark characteristics of AD include eczematous lesions and pruritus, with periods of acute exacerbations or flares that vary in frequency and duration [2]. Key burdensome signs and symptoms of moderate-to-severe AD include intense itching, erythema, and dryness or scaling [3]. These often interfere with daily activities and relationships, meaningfully reducing quality of life (QoL) across patients’ physical, psychosocial, and socioeconomic well-being [4–6]. While systemic therapy can improve QoL, patients with moderate-to-severe AD still face substantial multidimensional burdens and ongoing disease activity. In a recent study, approximately half of patients (40–60%) with AD who met moderate treatment targets (e.g., Eczema Area and Severity Index [EASI] 75) still experienced moderate-to-severe disease activity, such as uncontrolled itch [7]. The refractory nature of the disease also increases healthcare resource utilization, imposing a substantial economic burden on patients with AD, as well as time demands on both patients and physicians [8].

The multidimensional disease burdens of AD present ongoing challenges in achieving adequate disease control. Therapeutic options for managing moderate-to-severe AD include topical therapy, phototherapy, conventional systemic therapies (e.g., traditional immunosuppressants), and advanced systemic therapies (e.g., biologics and Janus kinase [JAK] inhibitors). Advanced systemic therapies and newer topical therapies focus on selectively targeting immune dysregulation present in AD [9–11]. Given the complexity of AD, a structured framework that establishes optimal outcome targets is needed to ensure treatment strategies address the heterogeneous nature of disease activity and QoL outcomes that are meaningful to patients.

A recently published international consensus by the AHEAD (Aiming High in Eczema/Atopic Dermatitis) workgroup outlined several recommendations for raising the standard of care and disease management in AD [12]. One key recommendation advised to aim for optimal treatment targets by using a treat-to-target and shared decision-making approach, as measured by clinician- and patient-reported outcomes (ClinROs and PROs). The AHEAD workgroup defined minimal disease activity (MDA) in AD as the simultaneous achievement of ≥ 1 optimal ClinRO and ≥ 1 optimal PRO, an optimal treatment goal for patients with AD. This novel approach of combining ClinROs and PROs provides a more holistic assessment from both clinical and psychosocial patient perspectives. A recent analysis pooling three phase 3 AD clinical trials demonstrated that achievement of MDA is associated with improved health-related QoL, sleep, and treatment satisfaction in patients with moderate-to-severe AD [13]. Additionally, a recent analysis in AD patients receiving biologics or JAK inhibitors found that the majority of patients that achieved MDA at week 16 maintained MDA at week 52, indicating that MDA is a sustainable target for AD treatment [14]. Drawing on real-world data from the MEASURE-AD study, this study aims to evaluate the benefits of achieving MDA with moderate-to-severe AD, including occurrence of flares, healthcare resource utilization, and patient-reported disease control.

Methods

Study Design

This retrospective post hoc analysis used data from MEASURE-AD, a real-world cross-sectional study that examined treatment patterns and multidimensional disease burdens in adolescents and adults with moderate-to-severe AD across 28 countries. The methodology for MEASURE-AD was previously reported [7]. Patients aged ≥ 12 years with a physician-confirmed diagnosis of moderate-to-severe AD who were receiving or eligible for AD systemic therapy were enrolled between December 2019 and December 2020 during routine visits to dermatology clinics. Data for assessments were collected at a single visit. All patients (or their parent/legal guardian for adolescents) provided informed consent or authorization to disclose personal health information. Only adult patients were included in this post hoc analysis evaluating flare activity and healthcare resource utilization outcomes over the last 6 months, as well as patient-reported disease control.

MEASURE-AD was a global disease state study conducted in multiple countries around the globe. Each country obtained their own ethics committee or institutional review board approvals. The independent ethics committee or institutional review board at each study site approved the study protocol, informed consent forms, and recruitment materials before patient enrollment. The studies were conducted in accordance with the International Conference for Harmonisation guidelines, applicable regulations, and the Declaration of Helsinki. All patients provided written informed consent to participate in the study and for publication before screening.

Assessments

Three definitions were used to identify achievement of MDA in this analysis, each of which comprised the simultaneous achievement of one optimal ClinRO target and one optimal PRO target recommended by AHEAD (Fig. 1, Table 1). The selection of the three MDA definitions was based on the outcomes recommended from the Harmonising Outcome Measures for Eczema (HOME)-endorsed core outcome set for AD clinical trials [15], which include scores for EASI, Worst Pruritus Numerical Rating Scale (WP-NRS), Dermatology Life Quality Index (DLQI), and Patient-Oriented Eczema Measure (POEM). In this post hoc analysis, MDA achievement was defined as EASI ≤ 3 + WP-NRS ≤ 1, EASI ≤ 3 + DLQI ≤ 1, or EASI ≤ 3 + POEM ≤ 2, respectively. Study outcomes were evaluated using the patient-reported AD flare questionnaire, the healthcare resource utilization questionnaire, and the patient-reported disease control questionnaire. The AD flare questionnaire assessed the number and duration of AD flares over the preceding 6 months, defining a flare as a sudden worsening of AD with a need for treatment escalation and/or a need to visit a healthcare provider due to worsening AD [16]. Average flare duration was categorized into four categories: ≤ 2 days, 3–7 days, 8–14 days, and ≥ 15 days. The healthcare resource utilization questionnaire evaluated the number of AD-related healthcare and acute care visits in the last 6 months. The patient-reported disease control questionnaire evaluated treatment control at the time of the study visit. Patients were asked to rate their agreement with the statement “I feel my current treatments are effective in controlling my atopic dermatitis” using a scale of 1 (“completely disagree”) to 5 (“completely agree”), with higher scores indicating better disease control with the current treatment.

Statistical Analyses

Descriptive analyses of clinical outcomes were conducted for those achieving MDA (EASI ≤ 3 + WP-NRS ≤ 1, EASI ≤ 3 + DLQI ≤ 1, or EASI ≤ 3 + POEM ≤ 2, respectively) and those who did not achieve MDA (i.e., achieving ≥ 1 non-optimal target). Non-optimal targets were defined as EASI 4–72, WP-NRS 2–10, DLQI 2–30, and POEM 3–28. Statistical analyses using Kruskal–Wallis tests were conducted to compare the number of AD flares and AD-related healthcare visits between patients achieving MDA and patients not achieving MDA.

Results

Patient Population

As previously described, a total of 1434 adult patients with moderate-to-severe AD were enrolled in the MEASURE-AD study and fulfilled all selection criteria [7]. For adult patients, the mean age (standard deviation [SD]) was 39.1 (16.3) years, and 52.2% were male (n = 748). The mean (SD) duration of AD was 23.7 (15.6) years. Almost all patients (n = 1411, 98.4%) were currently receiving AD treatment, and most patients (n = 813, 56.7%) were currently receiving systemic therapy either alone or in combination with other treatments. The most commonly used systemic therapies by these individuals included dupilumab (n = 468, 57.6%), corticosteroids (n = 146, 18.0%), and methotrexate (n = 124, 15.3%). In this post hoc analysis based on observed cases, 10.5% (n/N = 149/1420) of patients achieved MDA in EASI ≤ 3 + WP-NRS ≤ 1, 9.2% (129/1409) achieved MDA in EASI ≤ 3 + DLQI ≤ 1, and 6.2% (87/1406) achieved MDA in EASI ≤ 3 + POEM ≤ 2.

AD Flare Activity

Patients who achieved MDA experienced fewer flares over the last 6 months compared with those who did not achieve MDA (Fig. 2). Patients who achieved MDA had a mean number of flares ranging from 0.8 flares (EASI ≤ 3 + POEM ≤ 2) to 1.4 flares (EASI ≤ 3 + WP-NRS ≤ 1) over the preceding 6 months. Patients who did not achieve MDA experienced more flares (mean range 3.8–9.8), with the mean number of flares generally increasing with higher EASI, WP-NRS, DLQI, and POEM scores. Patients who met neither optimal ClinRO nor PRO targets experienced an average of 5.5–5.7 additional flares over 6 months (i.e., 11.0–11.5 additional flares in 1 year), which represents a 4.9- to 7.9-fold increase in the mean number of flares compared to the group that achieved MDA.Fig. 2Mean number of AD flares experienced by patients in the last 6 months. Patient-Reported Disease Control in AD

Patients who achieved MDA were more likely to completely agree that their AD was effectively controlled compared with those who did not achieve MDA (Fig. 5). Discussion

Achievement of MDA in patients with AD was associated with fewer and shorter duration of AD flares, a reduced number of AD-related healthcare visits, and a higher rate of patient-reported AD disease control compared with those who did not achieve MDA. Patient benefits in these examined outcomes were generally greatest in those who achieved MDA (i.e., simultaneous achievement of both ClinRO and PRO optimal targets), highlighting the value of achieving MDA in AD. A relatively low proportion of patients achieved MDA regardless of the definition used (6.2–10.5%), underscoring the need for improved treatment monitoring and strategies for disease management. These results support the use of AHEAD recommendations when setting treatment goals for AD.

The finding that achievement of MDA is associated with improved clinically relevant outcomes builds upon previous research investigating the impact of MDA achievement on patients’ health-related QoL outcomes. A post hoc analysis study combining data from the phase III Measure Up 1, Measure Up 2, and AD UP studies showed that patients who achieved MDA reported greater improvements in skin symptoms, daily activities, emotional state, work productivity, and treatment satisfaction compared to those who did not achieve MDA [13]. In this study, we further demonstrated the impact of MDA achievement on flare occurrence, flare duration, healthcare resource utilization, and patient-reported disease control, reflecting that the benefits of achieving MDA extend beyond patients to physicians and payers in the AD management sector. Achieving MDA provides benefits for all stakeholders by not only improving patients’ QoL but also by reducing physicians’ time spent managing disease flares and thereby lowering healthcare resource utilization and associated healthcare costs. This study showed that failing to achieve optimal targets in both ClinRO and PRO could result in an extra 11.0–11.5 flare episodes per year, as well as 4.3–5.1 more AD-related healthcare visits annually compared with patients who achieved MDA. These additional flares and visits may lead to significant healthcare expenses to payers, along with increased time and effort required for disease management from both patients and physicians.

MDA criteria holistically integrate optimal ClinRO and PRO endpoints to elevate the standard of care for AD. The association between MDA achievement and QoL outcomes that are important to both clinicians and patients makes MDA valuable for guiding patient-centered care and informing clinical decision-making. The heterogenous nature of AD necessitates the use of outcome targets that fully address the multidimensional disease burden to optimize effective treatment strategies. Shared patient–clinician decision-making by using holistic endpoints helps ensure that impacts to the physical, social, and emotional well-being of patients are recognized in clinical practice [17]. Through the inclusion of MDA and optimal outcomes in setting treatment goals, clinicians can better provide patient-tailored care that addresses the physical and psychosocial impacts of AD. Similarly, the continued integration and reporting of MDA in clinical trials, real-world research, as well as clinical practice and guidelines, will help ensure that treatment evaluations include aspects of AD that are meaningful to patients. This approach helps address unmet needs and improves the standard of care by evaluating the overall impact of AD on patients’ lives.

These study findings provide real-world insight into the associations between MDA and improvement in clinically relevant outcomes in an international patient cohort. However, the study limitations should be considered when interpreting the results. First, as a post hoc analysis of an observational study, this type of study design cannot establish causal relationships. Second, the small sample size for some categorizations also may have contributed to outlier scores. Third, owing to the high number of various combinations of ClinROs and PROs, we used HOME-endorsed outcomes, an international initiative to standardize outcome measures in AD, as MDA definitions in this study. The criteria for optimal targets may continue to evolve as MDA becomes more integrated into clinical practice. Outcomes can vary among the MDA definitions based on WP-NRS, POEM, or DLQI, with numerical differences observed in the frequency and duration of AD flares. Given the heterogeneity of the disease and the patient experience, these findings underscore the importance of shared decision-making between patients and physicians when setting treatment goals. Future studies should assess the variety of possible ClinRO and PRO combinations and the impact of achieving them. Additionally, the impacts of time to achieve MDA and duration of time maintaining MDA on patient outcomes also require future studies to delineate the holistic benefit of MDA achievement. Future longitudinal studies should also assess which treatment selections, optimization strategies, topical/proactive therapy approaches, clinical phenotypes, or biomarkers are associated with rapid and sustained MDA achievement to better inform individualized treat-to-target management in AD. Finally, the limitations of the original MEASURE-AD study also apply to this post hoc analysis, including eligibility criteria that selected only patients receiving or eligible for systemic therapy and a relatively long recall period of 6 months for flares, which might increase potential recall bias [7].

Conclusion

Achievement of MDA in patients with moderate-to-severe AD was associated with improved clinical outcomes, including a decrease in frequency and duration of flares, reduced healthcare resource utilization, and a greater proportion of patient-reported disease control than those who did not achieve MDA. These real-world findings underscore the importance of using both optimal ClinROs and PROs as treatment goals to holistically assess treatment outcomes across the multidimensional disease burden experienced by patients with AD.
