Shaping Maximally Localized Wannier Functions via Discrete Adiabatic Transport
Yuji Hamai, Katsunori Wakabayashi

Abstract
Maximally localized Wannier functions (MLWFs) are conventionally constructed by iteratively minimizing a spread functional over a high-dimensional gauge landscape. In this work, we present a non-variational constructive algorithm that unifies gauge smoothing and the eigenvalue problem of the projected position operator into a single deterministic framework. We demonstrate that discrete adiabatic transport across band degeneracies emerges naturally as an integral part of the solution procedure for the position eigenvectors. In this transport-aligned gauge, the Bloch overlaps exhibit an approximately linear phase dependence, allowing the Wannier centers to be extracted via deterministic fixed-point iterations and self-consistent updates rather than spread-functional minimization. Benchmark calculations for one- and two-dimensional systems yield spreads and orbital shapes in good agreement with standard minimization schemes. Furthermore, this analytical approach transparently isolates the physical origin of the 𝒪 ⁡ ( L ) \mathcal{O}(L) mesh-dependent spread scaling ( L L being the boundary seam resolution) observed in graphene, demonstrating that it is an intrinsic geometric manifestation of non-commuting projected position operators forcing finite gauge defects to accumulate along a one-dimensional boundary seam.

III
Band peeling for a composite band with internal degeneracies

We address an isolated composite-band subspace that may have internal
degeneracies, while being separated from the rest of the spectrum by finite gaps
throughout the Brillouin zone. We will also show that two ostensibly different
viewpoints—(i) eigenvalue equation for
e^{i\delta\bm{k}\cdot\hat{\bm{x}}}
and (ii) the multi-band adiabatic transport— coincide to
first order in
|\delta\bm{k}|
, and provide a first-order accurate
smoothing of gauge discontinuities in
\mathbb{K}_{I}
. We call this procedure band
peeling to distinguish it from the variational disentanglement of
Souza–Marzari–Vanderbilt
48
, which selects an
optimal subspace from energetically entangled bands.

Furthermore, it is shown that the adiabatic transport/projection in the current
study is equivalent to Kato’s parallel transport consistent with the gapless
adiabatic framework of Avron and
Elgart
30
;
7
;
8
, which eventually
provides a justification of the current procedures.

Throughout this section and the appendices,
|v^{n}_{\bm{k}}\rangle
denotes the local energy-ordered periodic Bloch frame at each
\bm{k}
, indexed by the band label
n
, whereas
|u^{s}_{\bm{k}}\rangle
denotes the peeled/transported frame indexed by the series label
s
.

III.1
Adiabatic change induced by
e^{i\delta\bm{k}\cdot\hat{\bm{x}}}

As shown in Fig.
1
,
it is reasonable to assume that
the
natural
connection of energy bands might be achieved
by extending the energy bands with the adiabatic approximation

14
.
And hence, this section describes how the periodic parts of BFs are

Let
\hat{H}(\bm{k})
be the

and
\epsilon^{n}(t)
be the instantaneous eigenvalue of the system,

\hat{H}(\bm{k})|v_{\bm{k}}^{n}\rangle=\epsilon^{n}(t)|v_{\bm{k}}^{n}\rangle,

(24)

where
\bm{k}
is a function of time
t

\bm{k}=\bm{k}(t).

(25)

By expanding the transient solution

\begin{array}[]{c}\begin{aligned} |\Psi(t)\rangle&=\sum_{n}c_{\bm{k}}^{n}e^{-i\theta^{n}(t)}|v_{\bm{k}}^{n}\rangle,\end{aligned}\end{array}

When the initial condition is:

(31)

after setting the dynamical factor to unity,
the state at
\bm{k}
develops to:

\displaystyle|u_{\bm{k}+\delta\bm{k}}^{s}\rangle

\displaystyle=|\Psi(\bm{k}+\delta\bm{k})\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\{\sum_{m}|v_{\bm{k}+\delta\bm{k}}^{m}\rangle\langle v_{\bm{k}+\delta\bm{k}}^{m}|\right\}|u_{\bm{k}}^{s}\rangle+\mathcal{O}(\delta\bm{k}^{2}).

Since the projection is summation over all bands in the composite-band system
and

is projected to the closest vector at

\bm{k}+\delta\bm{k}

By multiplying

\displaystyle|u_{\bm{k}}^{s}\rangle

\displaystyle=e^{-i\delta\bm{k}\cdot\bm{x}_{0}}\left\{\sum_{m}|v_{\bm{k}}^{m}\rangle\langle v_{\bm{k}}^{m}|\right\}|u_{\bm{k}-\delta\bm{k}}^{s}\rangle+\mathcal{O}(\delta\bm{k}^{2}),

The

e^{-i\delta\bm{k}\cdot\bm{x}_{0}}

has to be
determined separately, so that it reconciles the phase jumps across the border

\text{Eq.~(\ref{eq:composite-peeling-projection})}\ \xrightarrow[\bm{x}_{0}=\bm{0}]{\bm{k}\to\bm{k}+\delta\bm{k}}\ |u_{\bm{k}+\delta\bm{k}}^{s}\rangle=\left\{\sum_{m}|v_{\bm{k}+\delta\bm{k}}^{m}\rangle\langle v_{\bm{k}+\delta\bm{k}}^{m}|\right\}|u_{\bm{k}}^{s}\rangle+\mathcal{O}(|\delta\bm{k}|^{2}),

III.3
Underlying Symmetry
of the Adiabatic Expansion

The primary objective of this subsection is to explicitly demonstrate that the discrete adiabatic transport (Eq. (
32
)) and the eigenvalue equation of the projected translation operator (Eq. (
35
)) are formally equivalent and originate from the same first-order geometric transport.

Dropping the explicit time dependence of
Eq. (
29
) is equivalent to setting the
left-hand side of Eq. (
27
) to zero. Equivalently, for any
orthonormal frame
\{|v_{\bm{k}}^{m}\rangle\}
, at
\bm{k}
,

\left\{\sum_{m}|v_{\bm{k}}^{m}\rangle\langle v_{\bm{k}}^{m}|\right\}\frac{d}{dt}|\Psi(\bm{k}(t))\rangle=0,

(36)

where the summation is performed over all relevant band indices.
Since
|\Psi(\bm{k})\rangle
depends on
t
only through
\bm{k}(t)
, the
following equation then holds:

\delta t\left\{\sum_{m}|v_{\bm{k}}^{m}\rangle\langle v_{\bm{k}}^{m}|\right\}\frac{d\bm{k}}{dt}\nabla_{\bm{k}}|\Psi(\bm{k}(t))\rangle=0,

(37)

where
\delta t
is an infinitesimally short time duration.
If
\{|u^{s}_{\bm{k}}\rangle\}
is a specific
realization of
|\Psi(\bm{k})\rangle
,
by replacing
\delta t\,(d\bm{k}/dt)
with
\delta\bm{k}
,
the following equation
has to hold:

\begin{split}\left\{\sum_{m}|v_{\bm{k}}^{m}\rangle\langle v_{\bm{k}}^{m}|\right\}\delta\bm{k}\nabla_{\bm{k}}|u_{\bm{k}}^{s}\rangle&=\left\{\sum_{m}|v_{\bm{k}}^{m}\rangle\langle v_{\bm{k}}^{m}|\right\}\left\{|u_{\bm{k}}^{s}\rangle-|u_{\bm{k}-\delta\bm{k}}^{s}\rangle\right\}\\
&=0.\end{split}

(38)

Thus we arrive at Eq. (
35
) without the twist.

|u_{\bm{k}}^{s}\rangle=\left\{\sum_{m}|v_{\bm{k}}^{m}\rangle\langle v_{\bm{k}}^{m}|\right\}|u_{\bm{k}-\delta\bm{k}}^{s}\rangle.

(39)

Therefore, the role of the adiabatic expansion/transport in the present construction is to make the transported local subspace spanned by
\{|u_{\bm{k}}^{s}\rangle\}
as uniform as possible along the transport direction. In this sense, the transport virtually removes one degree of gauge discontinuity from the Bloch frame by enforcing first-order consistency between neighboring local subspaces.

Although the adiabatic expansion is introduced from the Schrödinger equation with the Bloch Hamiltonian
\hat{H}(\bm{k})
, the Hamiltonian plays no direct role in the construction from Eq. (36) onward. Its role in the peeling and in identifying the eigenvectors of the projected position/translation operator is only to specify the family of composite-band subspaces at each
\bm{k}
. The essential content of the adiabatic transport, as expressed in Eq. (36), is that the periodic part should remain unchanged to first order along the transport direction
d\bm{k}/dt
, and hence along
\delta\bm{k}
.

III.4
Kato’s transport and justification of the adiabatic transport in the presence of internal degeneracies

In standard treatments of the adiabatic theorem, one typically assumes that
the instantaneous eigenstates are nondegenerate. Here we justify the use of
Eq. (
32
) for isolated composite‑band systems
in the presence of internal degeneracy.

In the works of Kato, Avron and Elgart
7
;
8
;
30
, the
validity of the adiabatic theorem is investigated, and they have shown that the
approximation is valid even when gaps close, as long as,

\hat{P}(\bm{k})=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{N}_{C}}|v_{\bm{k}}^{m}\rangle\langle v_{\bm{k}}^{m}|\in C^{1}.

(40)

In their formula, the state at
\bm{k}
evolves in the following way,

|\Psi_{\bm{k}+\delta\bm{k}}\rangle=\hat{U}(\bm{k})|\Psi_{\bm{k}}\rangle,

(41)

where,

\displaystyle\dot{\hat{U}}(\bm{k})

\displaystyle=-i\hat{E}(\bm{k})\hat{U}(\bm{k})

(42)

\displaystyle\hat{E}(\bm{k})

\displaystyle=i\left[\dot{\hat{P}}(\bm{k}),\hat{P}(\bm{k})\right],

and
\bm{k}
is a function of
t
and the dot, e.g.,
\dot{P}(\bm{k})
, denotes the time derivative of the respective variable.
The first order solution is thus, given as follows:

\displaystyle\hat{U}(\bm{k})

\displaystyle=I-i\delta t\hat{E}(\bm{k})+\mathcal{O}(\delta k^{2}),

(43)

By limiting the initial states at
\bm{k}
and final states at
\bm{k}+\delta\bm{k}

in the composite energy bands
(see Appendix
C.1
for details),

\displaystyle\hat{P}(\bm{k}+\delta\bm{k})\hat{U}(\bm{k})\hat{P}(\bm{k})

\displaystyle=\hat{P}(\bm{k}+\delta\bm{k})\hat{P}(\bm{k})+\left(\delta\bm{k}\nabla\hat{P}(\bm{k})\right)^{2}\hat{P}(\bm{k})

(44)

\displaystyle=\hat{P}(\bm{k}+\delta\bm{k})\hat{P}(\bm{k})+\mathcal{O}(|\delta\bm{k}|^{2}).

And hence, the following propagation/translation equation is obtained,

\displaystyle|u_{\bm{k}+\delta\bm{k}}^{s}\rangle

\displaystyle=\hat{P}(\bm{k}+\delta\bm{k})\hat{U}(\bm{k})\hat{P}(\bm{k})|u_{\bm{k}}^{s}\rangle

(45)

\displaystyle=\hat{P}(\bm{k}+\delta\bm{k})\hat{P}(\bm{k})|u_{\bm{k}}^{s}\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\{\sum_{m_{1}}|v_{\bm{k}+\delta\bm{k}}^{m_{1}}\rangle\langle v_{\bm{k}+\delta\bm{k}}^{m_{1}}|\right\}\left\{\sum_{m_{2}}|v_{\bm{k}}^{m_{2}}\rangle\langle v_{\bm{k}}^{m_{2}}|\right\}|u_{\bm{k}}^{s}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{m}|v_{\bm{k}+\delta\bm{k}}^{m}\rangle\langle v_{\bm{k}+\delta\bm{k}}^{m}|u_{\bm{k}}^{s}\rangle,

which coincides with the geometrical parts of
Eq. (
32
). Therefore,
Eq. (
32
) is justified at first order for
isolated composite-band systems even in the presence of internal degeneracies,
without assuming a uniform spectral gap, provided
\hat{P}(\bm{k})
remains
C^{1}
along the path.

III.5
Correction to
Eqs. (
32
) and
(
35
) based on Kato’s formula

There are two
practical differences between Kato’s transport and the first-order projection
described by Eqs. (
32
) and
(
35
). First, the latter employ a one-sided
finite-difference step; this is not essential and can be improved by
higher-order schemes. Second, the overlap matrix between the frames at

\bm{k}
and
\bm{k}+\delta\bm{k}
is not exactly
unitary at finite mesh spacing. We enforce unitarity by replacing it with the
unitary factor of the polar decomposition via singular value
decomposition (SVD)
55
.

Writing the projected vectors as,

\displaystyle|u_{\bm{k}+\delta\bm{k}}^{s}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{N}_{C}}|v_{\bm{k}+\delta\bm{k}}^{m}\rangle\langle v_{\bm{k}+\delta\bm{k}}^{m}|u_{\bm{k}}^{s}\rangle=\sum_{m}M_{m,s}|v_{\bm{k}+\delta\bm{k}}^{m}\rangle,

(46)

where,

M_{m,s}=\langle v^{m}_{\bm{k}+\delta\bm{k}}|u^{s}_{\bm{k}}\rangle,

(47)

we compute the SVD
M=V\,\Sigma\,W^{\dagger}
with
\Sigma=\mathrm{diag}(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{J})
and define the unitary polar factor,

Q=VW^{\dagger}.

(48)

The orthonormalized transport step is then,

|u_{\bm{k}+\delta\bm{k}}^{s}\rangle=\sum_{m}|v_{\bm{k}+\delta\bm{k}}^{m}\rangle Q_{m,s}.

(49)

IV
Fixing Wannier Center and Initial Condition of
|u_{\bm{k}}^{s}\rangle

Since the parallel transport Eqs. (
32
) and
(
35
) alone do not provide the initial condition of
\{f_{\bm{k}}^{s,n}\}

nor fix the Wannier center
\bm{x}_{0}
, this section is devoted to solving these
problems. It should be noted, however, that when the following condition,

\langle u_{\bm{k}}^{s_{0}}|u_{\bm{k}}^{s_{1}}\rangle=\delta_{s_{0},s_{1}},

(50)

is satisfied, the periodic parts remain approximately orthogonal, as
detailed in Appendix
C.2
,

\langle u_{\bm{k}+\delta\bm{k}}^{s_{0}}|u_{\bm{k}+\delta\bm{k}}^{s_{1}}\rangle=\delta_{s_{0},s_{1}}+\mathcal{O}(\delta k^{2}).

(51)

And hence, by provisionally setting,

|u^{s}_{\bm{k}_{0}}\rangle=|v^{s}_{\bm{k}_{0}}\rangle,

(52)

somewhere in the k-space, where the energy bands are reasonably separated, the
obtained WFs are approximately orthogonal up to the first order of
\delta k

and resemble the right eigenvectors of the projected translation operator
except the Wannier center and the initial value of
\{f^{s,n}_{\bm{k}_{0}}\}
.

IV.1
Fixing the Initial Condition of
\{f^{s,n}_{\bm{k}}\}

By using the provisional WFs
\{|w^{s}_{\bm{0}}\rangle\}
, the
right MLWFs,
\{|W^{s}_{\bm{0}}\rangle\}
, the eigenvectors of a
position operator, have to be obtained by applying a unitary transformation,

|\bm{W}_{\bm{0}}\rangle=|\bm{w}_{\bm{0}}\rangle\bm{F},

(53)

if expressed element-by-element:

\begin{array}[]{c}\begin{aligned} \left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}|W^{0}{}_{\bm{0}}\rangle&|W^{1}{}_{\bm{0}}\rangle&\ldots&|W^{N_{C}-1}{}_{\bm{0}}\rangle\end{array}\right]&=\\
\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}|w^{0}_{\bm{0}}\rangle&|w^{1}_{\bm{0}}\rangle&\ldots&|w^{N_{C}-1}_{\bm{0}}\rangle\end{array}\right]&\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}F_{0}^{0}&F_{0}^{1}&\ldots&F_{0}^{N_{C}-1}\\
F_{1}^{0}&F_{1}^{1}&\ldots&F_{1}^{N_{C}-1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
F_{N_{C}-1}^{0}&F_{N_{C}-1}^{1}&\ldots&F_{N_{C}-1}^{N_{C}-1}\end{array}\right].\end{aligned}\end{array}

(54)

By requiring
|\bm{W}_{\bm{0}}\rangle
to be the eigenvectors of the position operator

\hat{x}
, we have the following eigenvalue equation.

\begin{split}\langle\bm{W}_{0}|\hat{x}|\bm{W}_{0}\rangle&=\bm{F}^{\dagger}\langle\bm{w}_{0}|\hat{x}|\bm{w}_{0}\rangle\bm{F}\\
&=\bm{F}^{\dagger}X\bm{F}\\
&=\bm{\Lambda},\end{split}

(55)

where,
\bm{X}
is the matrix representation of
\hat{\bm{x}}

calculated with the provisional WFs,
\{|w^{s}_{\bm{0}}\rangle\}
,
and
\bm{\Lambda}
is the diagonal eigenvalue matrix.
\bm{F}
,
the eigenvector matrix of the eigenvalue equation, is easily obtained with a
numerical library, such as the GNU Scientific Library (GSL)
25
. And
hence, the arbitrariness of the initial condition of
\{f^{s,n}_{\bm{k}}\}
is fixed and the Wannier centers are obtained from
\bm{\Lambda}
.

In the current study,
\{f^{s,n}_{\bm{k}}\}
is provisionally fixed at

\bm{k}
where all energy bands in the composite system are separated
with reasonable sizes of energy gaps, e.g.,
\bm{k}_{0}=(\pi,\pi)
in
the case of lower energy bands of graphene,

f^{s,n}_{\bm{k}_{0}}=\delta_{s,n}.

(56)

Accordingly, the initial condition is revised as follows:

f^{s,n}_{\bm{k}_{0}}=\sum_{p}\delta_{p,n}F_{s,p}.

(57)

IV.2
Fixing Wannier Centers

By solving Eq. (
55
), the Wannier centers are, theoretically,
obtained as the elements of the diagonal eigenvalue matrix
\bm{\Lambda}
. In a numerical context, the values of
\bm{X}=\langle\bm{w}_{0}|\hat{x}|\bm{w}_{0}\rangle
are affected by the
concentration of
\bm{w}(\bm{x})=\langle\bm{x}|\bm{w}_{0}\rangle
, which recursively depends on the Wannier centers
given in
\bm{\Lambda}
. Thus, an iterative method to obtain the Wannier
centers with theoretically guaranteed stability is introduced. In practice, the coupled determination of
\bm{X}
and
\bm{\Lambda}
implied by Eq. (
55
) is handled iteratively; this is a numerical solver for the explicit equations, not a search or minimization over a spread functional.

In this subsection, the WFs are treated series by series
through Eq. (
55
), and hence the superscript indicating its
series is dropped.

IV.2.1
Wannier Group and Transformation

From the periodic parts
\{|u_{\bm{k}}\rangle\}
,
obtained from Eqs. (
33
), (
55
)
and (
57
), we have a WF with a candidate Wannier center

\bm{r}
,

|W_{\bm{M}}(\bm{r})\rangle=L^{-d/2}\!\sum_{\bm{k}}e^{-i\bm{r}\cdot\bm{k}}e^{i\bm{k}\cdot(\hat{\bm{x}}-\bm{M})}|u_{\bm{k}}\rangle,\;\bm{r}\in[0,1)^{d}.

(58)

We further define sets labeled by
\bm{r}
,

\mathcal{G}_{\bm{r}}^{s}=\bigl\{\,\ket{W_{\bm{M}}(\bm{r})}:\ \bm{M}\in\mathbb{M}\,\bigr\}.

(59)

Since the space spanned by the elements of each group has to be identical,

\mathrm{Span}\{\mathcal{G}_{\bm{p}}\}=\mathrm{Span}\{\mathcal{G}_{\bm{r}}\}\quad\forall\bm{r},\bm{p}\in[0,1)^{d}.

(60)

A transformation between them must exist, and it is given by:

\begin{split}|W_{\bm{M}}(\bm{r})\rangle=\sum_{\bm{N}}\prod_{\mu=1}^{d}&\operatorname{sinc}\!\bigl(M_{\mu}-N_{\mu}+r_{\mu}-p_{\mu}\bigr)|W_{\bm{N}}(\bm{p})\rangle,\end{split}

(61)

where,

\operatorname{sinc}x=\frac{\sin\pi x}{\pi x},

(62)

and the following approximation is utilized ( see Appendix
D.1
for details and
simplified calculations),

\displaystyle\frac{1}{L}\lim_{\delta k\rightarrow 0}\sum_{k}e^{-ik(M-N+r-p)}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{sinc}(M-N+r-p).

(63)

Matrix elements calculated by basis belonging to different groups are
also transformed in the following way,

\displaystyle X_{\bm{M}_{1}\bm{M}_{2}}(r)

\displaystyle=\langle W_{\bm{M}_{1}}(\bm{r})|\hat{x}|W_{\bm{M}_{2}}(\bm{r})\rangle

(64)

\displaystyle=\!\!\!\!\!\!\sum_{\bm{N}_{1},\bm{N}_{2}}\prod_{\mu=1}^{d}\operatorname{sinc}\!\bigl(M_{1,\mu}\!-N_{1,\mu}+r_{\mu}\!-p_{\mu}\bigr)\cdot

\displaystyle\prod_{\mu=1}^{d}\operatorname{sinc}\!\bigl(M_{2,\mu}\!-N_{2,\mu}+r_{\mu}\!-p_{\mu}\bigr)X_{\bm{N}_{1}\bm{N}_{2}}(\bm{p}).

IV.2.2
Finding Wannier center of MLWFs

For the sake of conciseness and clarity, the derivation of the method to obtain
Wannier centers is done in a 1D model. The multidimensional formulation is given
in Appendix
D.4
.

A WF in 1D with a fractional shift
r
is given as follows:

|W_{M}(r)\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{L}}\sum_{k}e^{-ikr}e^{ik(\hat{x}-M)}|u_{k}\rangle.\\

(65)

Let us assume that the right value
r=x_{0}
is found, then the following holds:

\begin{split}\langle W_{M}(x_{0})|\hat{x}|W_{N}(x_{0})\rangle=\delta_{M,N}(M+x_{0}).\end{split}

(66)

Equation (
64
) relates the above to a known, calculable value,

\begin{split}X_{0,0}(r)=\langle W_{0}(r)|\hat{x}|W_{0}(r)\rangle,\end{split}

(67)

and using Eq. (
147
), we have:

\begin{split}X_{0,0}(r)\!\!\!&=\!\!\!\sum_{M,N}\!\!\!\mathrm{sinc}(M+x_{0}-r)\mathrm{sinc}(N+x_{0}-r)\delta_{M,N}(M+x_{0})\\
&=\frac{\sin 2\pi(x_{0}-r)}{2\pi}+r.\\
\end{split}

(68)

X_{0,0}^{r}
is implicitly related to
x_{0}

via the right-hand side of Eq. (
67
).
And hence, the following two iterative dynamics are identical,

r^{n+1}=\langle W_{0}({\bm{r}^{n}})|\hat{x}|W_{0}({\bm{r}^{n}})\rangle,

(69)

r^{n+1}=\frac{\sin\!\bigl(2\pi(x_{0}-r^{n})\bigr)}{2\pi}+r^{n},

(70)

where
n
appearing on the shoulder of
r
is the iteration number.
The former (Eq. (
69
)) is used for the actual numerical calculation, while the latter (Eq. (
70
)) reveals the underlying mathematical structure and is used to prove convergence.
Through the iteration described in Eqs. (
69
) and (
70
),
the following holds, as detailed in Appendix
D.2
.

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}r^{n}=x_{0}.

(71)

\displaystyle X_{\bm{0}\bm{0}}(\bm{r})=\frac{\sin 2\pi(x_{0}-r_{x})}{2\pi}+r_{x},

and hence the above argument also applies.

V
Single-Band System

Before moving to benchmarks, we specialize the general framework to an isolated
single band, where the gauge group reduces to
U(1)
. In this limit, it is easier
to see how the phase flattening done by the transport actually contributes to
minimizing the spread, since the spread functional is reduced to an analytic
Euler–Lagrange equation, a k-space Poisson problem.

In a single band system,
f_{k}^{s,n}
is reduced to:

f_{\bm{k}}^{s,n}\rightarrow e^{i\phi_{\bm{k}}},\phi_{\bm{k}}\in\mathbb{R},

(74)

and hence, the BFs and WFs are expressed as follows:

\begin{cases}&|\psi_{\bm{k}}\rangle=L^{-d/2}e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\hat{\bm{x}}}|v_{\bm{k}}\rangle\\
&|W_{\bm{M}}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{L}}\sum_{\bm{k}}e^{-i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{M}}e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\hat{\bm{x}}}|u_{\bm{k}}\rangle\\
&|u_{\bm{k}}\rangle=e^{i\phi_{\bm{k}}}|v_{\bm{k}}\rangle.\end{cases}

(75)

V.1
Berry Connection

By introducing
\theta_{\bm{k}}(\bm{x})
,
the phase of
v_{\bm{k}}(\bm{x})
, as follows:

v_{\bm{k}}(\bm{x})=|v_{\bm{k}}(\bm{x})|e^{i\theta_{\bm{k}}(\bm{x})}\ (\theta_{\bm{k}}(\bm{x})\in\mathbb{R}),

(76)

the Berry connection
\langle v_{\bm{k}}|v_{\bm{k}-\delta\bm{k}}\rangle

for
-\pi<k<\pi
is calculated as follows:

\displaystyle\langle v_{\bm{k}}|v_{\bm{k}-\delta\bm{k}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle v_{\bm{k}}|v_{\bm{k}}\rangle-\delta\bm{k}\langle v_{\bm{k}}|\bm{\nabla_{\bm{k}}}|v_{\bm{k}}\rangle+\mathcal{O}(\delta\bm{k}^{2})

(77)

\displaystyle=1-i\delta\bm{k}\cdot\bm{A}_{\bm{k}}+\mathcal{O}(\delta\bm{k}^{2}),

where,

\displaystyle\bm{A}_{\bm{k}}

\displaystyle=-i\langle v_{\bm{k}}|\bm{\nabla_{\bm{k}}}|v_{\bm{k}}\rangle.

(78)

By utilizing,

\bm{\nabla_{\bm{k}}}\int d^{d}\bm{x}|v_{\bm{k}}(\bm{x})|^{2}=0,

(79)

\bm{A}_{\bm{k}}
is also expressed as follows:

\displaystyle\bm{A}_{\bm{k}}

\displaystyle=\int d^{d}\bm{x}|v_{\bm{k}}(\bm{x})|^{2}\bm{\nabla_{\bm{k}}}\theta_{\bm{k}}(\bm{x}).

(80)

Note that
\bm{A}_{\bm{k}}
is decomposed into the transverse and longitudinal part:

\bm{A}_{\bm{k}}=\bm{A}^{\mathrm{T}}_{\bm{k}}+\nabla_{\bm{k}}\varphi_{\bm{k}},\qquad\nabla_{\bm{k}}\cdot\bm{A}^{\mathrm{T}}_{\bm{k}}=0.

(81)

V.2
Minimum Spread Condition

To find out a constraint on
\phi^{n}_{\bm{k}}
in
\mathbb{K}_{I}
, the functional equation
for the minimum spread is reviewed.
The spread of a WF with its center at
\bm{x}_{0}
is calculated as follows
37
:

\displaystyle\sigma(\bm{x}^{2})

\displaystyle=\langle W_{\bm{0}}|(\hat{\bm{x}}-\bm{x}_{0})^{2}|W_{\bm{0}}\rangle

(82)

\displaystyle=\langle W_{\bm{0}}|\hat{\bm{x}}^{2}|W_{\bm{0}}\rangle-\bm{x}_{0}^{2},

where
\bm{x}_{0}
is the Wannier center.
The minimum spread is therefore achieved, when the following is minimized:

\displaystyle\langle\hat{\bm{x}}^{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle W_{0}|\hat{\bm{x}}^{2}|W_{0}\rangle

(83)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{L^{d}}\sum_{\bm{k}}\langle\nabla_{\bm{k}}u_{\bm{k}}|\nabla_{\bm{k}}u_{\bm{k}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{L^{d}}\sum_{\bm{k}}\int d^{d}\bm{x}\left(\nabla_{\bm{k}}|v_{\bm{k}}(\bm{x})|\right)^{2}+|v_{\bm{k}}(\bm{x})|^{2}\left\{\nabla_{\bm{k}}\left(\theta_{\bm{k}}+\phi_{\bm{k}}\right)\right\}^{2}.

By utilizing Eq. (
80
) and approximating the summation with integration, the functional derivative of Eq. (
83
) is given as follows

22
;
26
;
41
;
24
,

\displaystyle\left\langle\frac{\delta\hat{\bm{x}}^{2}\left[\phi\right]}{\delta\phi},\lambda\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi^{d}}\frac{d}{d\epsilon}\int d^{d}\bm{k}\int d^{d}\bm{x}\,\left|v_{\bm{k}}(\bm{x})\right|^{2}\cdot

(84)

\displaystyle\left|\nabla_{\bm{k}}\left(\theta_{\bm{k}}(\bm{x})+\phi_{\bm{k}}+\epsilon\lambda_{\bm{k}}\right)\right|^{2}\Bigg|_{\epsilon=0}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\pi^{d}}\int d^{d}\bm{k}\left(\bm{A}_{\bm{k}}+\nabla_{\bm{k}}\phi_{\bm{k}}\right)\cdot\nabla_{\bm{k}}\lambda_{\bm{k}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{2}{\pi^{d}}\int d^{d}\bm{k}\,\lambda_{\bm{k}}\left(\nabla_{\bm{k}}\cdot\bm{A}_{\bm{k}}+\Delta_{\bm{k}}\phi_{\bm{k}}\right),

where
\Delta_{\bm{k}}
is the Laplacian operator in the k-space, and

\lambda_{\bm{k}}
is an arbitrary trial function satisfying the
following(see Eq. (
3
)):

\lambda_{\bm{k}}\big|_{\partial\mathbb{K}}=0.

(85)

Thus, the minimum spread condition is expressed in the following partial
differential equation (see Eq. (
4
)),

\Delta_{\bm{k}}\phi_{\bm{k}}+\nabla_{\bm{k}}\cdot\bm{A}_{\bm{k}}=0,\;\bm{k}\in\mathbb{K}_{I},

(86)

Denoting a particular solution by
\phi^{0}_{\bm{k}}
, we have a family of solutions,

\phi_{\bm{k}}=\phi^{0}_{\bm{k}}+\bm{k}\cdot\bm{x}_{0},

(87)

where
\bm{k}\cdot\bm{x}_{0}
represents a homogeneous solution.
Hence, the Berry connection made of
\{|u_{\bm{k}}\rangle\}
is,

\tilde{\bm{A}}_{\bm{k}}=\bm{A}_{\bm{k}}+\nabla_{\bm{k}}\phi_{\bm{k}}.

(88)

Thus,

\nabla_{\bm{k}}\cdot\tilde{\bm{A}}_{\bm{k}}=0.

(89)

If the band admits an ELWF and an MLWF, and if, in addition,

\bm{A}^{T}_{\bm{k}}=0,

(90)

then Eqs. (
86
) and (
89
) have a solution of the form,

\tilde{\bm{A}}_{\bm{k}}=-\bm{x}_{0}.

(91)

From the above,

\begin{split}\langle u_{\bm{k}}|u_{\bm{k}+\delta\bm{k}}\rangle&=\langle u_{\bm{k}}|\{|u_{\bm{k}}\rangle+\delta\bm{k}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{k}}|u_{\bm{k}}\rangle\}+\mathcal{O}(\delta\bm{k}^{2})\\
&=\langle u_{\bm{k}}|u_{\bm{k}}\rangle+i\delta\bm{k}\cdot\tilde{\bm{A}}_{\bm{k}}+\mathcal{O}(\delta\bm{k}^{2})\\
&=1-i\delta\bm{k}\cdot\bm{x}_{0}+\mathcal{O}(\delta\bm{k}^{2}).\end{split}

(92)

The last line is the infinitesimal form of a phase-plane relation.
Indeed, to first order in the mesh spacing,

1-i\delta\bm{k}\cdot\bm{x}_{0}=e^{-i\delta\bm{k}\cdot\bm{x}_{0}}+\mathcal{O}(\delta\bm{k}^{2}).

(93)

Equivalently, if
\bm{k}(t)
is a path in
\mathbb{K}_{I}
and

\theta(t)=\arg\langle u_{\bm{k}(0)}|u_{\bm{k}(t)}\rangle,

then Eq. (
92
) gives the first-order phase equation

\frac{d\theta}{dt}=-\dot{\bm{k}}(t)\cdot\bm{x}_{0}.

(94)

Integrating this relation along a link from
\bm{k}_{1}
to

\bm{k}_{2}
gives the finite-link phase factor. For a finite
link, the numerical overlap may also have a positive modulus. Since the
present discussion concerns the phase-plane relation, this positive
modulus is understood to be separated off, and only the unit-modulus
phase factor is written explicitly below. In vector form and in the
two-dimensional component form used below, this phase factor is

\begin{split}e^{i\arg\langle u_{\bm{k}_{1}}|u_{\bm{k}_{2}}\rangle}&=e^{-i(\bm{k}_{2}-\bm{k}_{1})\cdot\bm{x}_{0}}\\
&=e^{i(k_{1_{x}}-k_{2_{x}})x_{0}}e^{i(k_{1_{y}}-k_{2_{y}})y_{0}},\end{split}

(95)

where
\bm{k}_{1},\bm{k}_{2}\in\mathbb{K}_{I}
.
The first line follows from the constant-connection condition
Eq. (
91
), while the second line is the
two-dimensional component form of the same phase factor.

Equivalently, Eq. (
95
) gives the following phase-plane
equation:

\begin{split}\arg\langle u_{\bm{k}_{1}}|u_{\bm{k}_{2}}\rangle&=(\bm{k}_{1}-\bm{k}_{2})\cdot\bm{x}_{0}\\
&=(k_{1_{x}}-k_{2_{x}})x_{0}+(k_{1_{y}}-k_{2_{y}})y_{0}.\end{split}

(96)

Thus Eq. (
96
) is the equation of a tilted plane for
the overlap phase. The slopes of this phase plane are the components of
the Wannier center. This relation is the link between the
transport-aligned gauge and the later center-fixing iteration.
The first line of Eq. (
95
) with

\bm{x}_{0}=\bm{0}
is also obtained from
Eq. (
35
) and
Eq. (
51
). Hence,
\{|u^{s}_{\bm{k}}\rangle\}

generated by the adiabatic transport satisfy the minimum spread condition,
in this curvature-free case.

\mathbb{K}_{I}

\bm{x}_{0}

VI
Results and Discussion

The purpose of the section is to validate the formulations in the previous
sections. Therefore, not only the comparison of spreads with published
results but also the key equations are numerically verified with
graphical representation of the results.

The calculation of the matrix elements of the projected position operators is
the most important part of the procedure and there are two ways to
perform it. In Secs.
VI.1

These two approaches are theoretically equivalent. The former is more
straightforward, whereas the latter is more computationally efficient.
Thus, this section discusses not only the numerical results and the actual
implementation, but also the procedural differences.

&=\int\overline{W}_{0}^{s_{1}}(x)xW_{0}^{s_{2}}(x)dx.\end{split}

(97)

55

VI.2
2D Square Potential

This section presents the calculation procedures, implementation and results of
a numerical model. The system consists of a unit square with a
step-function-like potential well,

V(x,y)=\begin{cases}&1\;(x,y\in[1/2,3/4])\\
&0\;(\textrm{otherwise}).\end{cases}

(98)

The periodic boundary condition is employed on the four sides and the calculations are carried out
in the unit cell and k-space both having
32\times 32
resolution.

VI.2.1
Numerical procedure

The energy bands are first peeled from each other with the following equation derived from Eq. (
32
):

\displaystyle|u_{\bm{k}+\delta\bm{k}}^{s}\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\{\sum_{m}|v_{\bm{k}+\delta\bm{k}}^{m}\rangle\langle v_{\bm{k}+\delta\bm{k}}^{m}|\right\}|u_{\bm{k}}^{s}\rangle,

(99)

with the initial condition:

|u^{s}_{\bm{k}_{0}}\rangle=|v^{s}_{\bm{k}_{0}}\rangle,

(
52
)

where,
\bm{k}_{0}=(0,0)
and
s
indicates the peeled
energy band
(series) number.

The projection (transport) is applied outward from the center to all k-points, as schematically depicted in Fig.
10
. Specifically, the bases defined on the line
k_{x}=0
are transported along the
k_{y}
direction toward the boundaries at
k_{x}=\pm\pi
.

The directions of the transport and that of the position operator
are, theoretically, desired to be consistent. In this calculation,
the direction of the transport does not show any discernible effect
on the results.

Figure 10:
(Color online)
A schematic diagram of transporting the bases at the center all over the
k-space.

The square-potential calculation below follows the general workflow already summarized in Fig.
3
. After the peeling, the following two equations are repeated alternately
until a predetermined convergence criterion is met.
The matrix elements are first calculated with a set of provisional WFs with
\{|u^{s}_{\bm{k}}\rangle\}

calculated by Eq. (
99
),

\bm{X}^{n}=\langle\bm{W}_{0}(\bm{\Lambda}^{n-1})|\hat{x}|\bm{W}_{0}(\bm{\Lambda}^{n-1})\rangle.

(100)

With the estimated matrix,
\bm{X}^{n}
,
the following eigenvalue problem is solved,

\begin{split}\bm{\Lambda}^{n+1}=\bm{F}^{\dagger}\bm{X}^{n}\bm{F},\end{split}

(101)

and hence the unitary matrix
\bm{F}
and the Wannier centers are obtained.
As the iteration proceeds, the matrix
\bm{X}
approaches a diagonal form, namely,

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\|\bm{\Lambda}^{n}-\bm{X}^{n}\|\rightarrow\bm{0},

(102)

Because of the convergent nature of the sinc-loop described in
Sec. IV B 2, we use
\bm{\Lambda}=\bm{0}
as the initial
value in the current study.
For the same reason, Eq. (
100
) can be repeated
with a convergence criterion before proceeding to Eq. (
101
).

Since
y_{0}
cannot be obtained from Eq. (
100
) alone,

y^{s,m+1}_{0}=\langle W_{0}^{s,n}(x_{0}^{s,n},y_{0}^{s,m})|\hat{y}|W_{0}^{s,n}(x_{0}^{s,n},y_{0}^{s,m})\rangle,

(103)

is inserted to have convergence of
y_{0}
, prior to Eq. (
101
).

When the convergence is reached, the series with the smallest spread is removed
from the bases. From the symmetry of the system,
\hat{x}
is replaced with

\hat{y}
and the same iteration is repeated. For the final WF, Wannier center is
obtained by simply repeating the single-element version of Eq. (
100
).

(107)

(k_{\xi},k_{\eta})

First-principles calculations were carried out using
Quantum ESPRESSO

(QE)
28
;
27
;
52

12\times 12

36\times 36

the corresponding centers and spreads are summarized later in

4

;
43
;
53

(see Appendix
F
for notation).

As a setting of the QE calculation, we choose five Wannier functions consisting
of two
C-p_{z}
orbitals (
\pi
manifold) and three bond-centered orbitals (
\sigma
bonds),
providing a minimal chemically-motivated set for graphene within the five-band

\displaystyle-\left.\langle v_{(k_{x},k_{y})}^{n_{1}}|v_{(k_{x}-\delta k,k_{y})}^{n_{2}}\rangle f_{(k_{x}-\delta k,k_{y})}^{s_{2},n_{2}}\right\}

38

VII
SUMMARY

We proposed a non-variational constructive algorithm to build maximally localized Wannier
functions (MLWFs) that unifies gauge smoothing and the eigenvalue problem of the
projected position operator into a single deterministic framework. Rather than
treating gauge alignment and center determination as separate steps or minimizing the spread functional over the gauge manifold, we demonstrated that discrete Kato parallel
transport across band degeneracies emerges naturally as an integral part of the
solution procedure for the position eigenvectors.

By directly aligning the periodic parts of the Bloch functions, this transport
suppresses band-index swaps and approximately linearizes the overlap phase in the interior of the Brillouin zone.
Operating within this transport-aligned gauge, we introduced a sinc-kernel
transformation that maps the Wannier center search to deterministic fixed-point iterations and self-consistent updates for explicit center equations and projected-position matrices, rather than spread-functional minimization over the gauge manifold.

Benchmarks on 1D models, a 2D square potential, and graphene yield Wannier
centers and orbital shapes in good agreement with standard gradient-based
minimization (e.g., W90). Furthermore, because our sequential extraction works
to flatten the interior gauge instead of globally distributing the geometric
mismatch, it transparently isolates the physical origin of the
\mathcal{O}(L)

spread growth observed in graphene. The finite
\mathcal{O}(L^{0})
local defects
accumulated on the one-dimensional boundary seam strictly integrate to an

\mathcal{O}(L)
macroscopic spread growth. This confirms that the
resolution-dependent spread divergence is an intrinsic geometric manifestation
of non-commuting projected position operators. Remaining challenges include
addressing the non-uniqueness of projection directions for co-centered WFs and
managing directional incompatibilities during multi-step extractions.

And hence,

(142)

Appendix F
Reciprocal-space formulas and seam diagnostics for graphene

This appendix reformulates the standard reciprocal-space spread expressions
37
;
38
;
43
to provide a rigorous theoretical basis for the
\mathcal{O}(L)
scaling observed in Sec.
VI.3
. The purpose is to explicitly evaluate the geometric gauge defects using two complementary diagnostics, the single-state overlap
\sigma_{\alpha}^{s}
and the subspace-level overlap
\sigma_{\min}
.

F.1
Nearest-neighbor overlaps and discrete measure

For each nearest-neighbor link
\delta\bm{k}_{\alpha}
(
\alpha\in\mathcal{N}
),
we define the overlap matrix

M_{sp}^{\alpha}(\bm{k})=\langle u_{\bm{k}}^{s}|u_{\bm{k}+\delta\bm{k}_{\alpha}}^{p}\rangle.

(166)

We also introduce the discrete measure

\nu_{\alpha}=\frac{w_{\alpha}}{N_{\bm{k}}},\qquad N_{\bm{k}}=L^{2},

(167)

so that the weights do not appear explicitly in the local kernels discussed below.
On a uniform mesh,
\nu_{\alpha}=\mathcal{O}(L^{0})
.
Using Eq. (
167
), the Marzari–Vanderbilt decomposition becomes

\Omega=\Omega_{I}+\Omega_{OD}+\Omega_{D},

(168)

with

\displaystyle\Omega_{I}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{k},\alpha}\nu_{\alpha}\left(J-\sum_{s,p}\left|M_{sp}^{\alpha}(\bm{k})\right|^{2}\right),

(169)

\displaystyle\Omega_{OD}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{k},\alpha}\nu_{\alpha}\sum_{s\neq p}\left|M_{sp}^{\alpha}(\bm{k})\right|^{2},

(170)

\displaystyle\Omega_{D}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{k},\alpha}\nu_{\alpha}\sum_{s}\left[\Im\log M_{ss}^{\alpha}(\bm{k})+\delta\bm{k}_{\alpha}\cdot\bm{x}_{0}^{s}\right]^{2}.

(171)

The seam analysis below uses only the amplitude part of the links.
The phase contribution in
\Omega_{D}
belongs to the full spread functional,
but it is not needed for the curvature diagnostics introduced here.

F.2
Single-band(series) distortion carried by an extracted MLWF

For a fixed extracted series
s
, we consider the diagonal link

m_{\alpha}^{s}(\bm{k})=M_{ss}^{\alpha}(\bm{k})=\langle u_{\bm{k}}^{s}|u_{\bm{k}+\delta\bm{k}_{\alpha}}^{s}\rangle.

(172)

Because this is a
1\times 1
overlap matrix, its singular value is simply

\sigma_{\alpha}^{s}(\bm{k})=\left|m_{\alpha}^{s}(\bm{k})\right|.

(173)

We then define the corresponding single-state kernel by

\omega_{\alpha}^{s}(\bm{k})=1-\left[\sigma_{\alpha}^{s}(\bm{k})\right]^{2}=1-\left|m_{\alpha}^{s}(\bm{k})\right|^{2}.

(174)

Equation (
174
) is the explicit relation between

\sigma_{\alpha}^{s}
and
\omega_{\alpha}^{s}
.
Thus
\omega_{\alpha}^{s}
measures how far the one-dimensional subspace carried by the

s
-th Wannier series departs from perfect continuity across the link

\delta\bm{k}_{\alpha}
.
No logarithmic phase term is included here, because the present diagnostic is meant to isolate the
curvature of the carried one-dimensional subspace itself.

F.3
Subspace-level diagnostic for the residual transported space

The complementary diagnostic for the transported
J
-dimensional subspace is the minimum singular value of the full overlap matrix:

\sigma_{\min}(\bm{k};\delta\bm{k}_{\alpha})=\min_{i}\sigma_{i}\!\left(M^{\alpha}(\bm{k})\right)=\cos\theta_{\max}.

(175)

The associated defect is

d_{\alpha}(\bm{k})=1-\sigma_{\min}^{2}(\bm{k};\delta\bm{k}_{\alpha}).

(176)

Equation (
175
) measures the mismatch of the neighboring
J
-dimensional transported subspaces, whereas Eq. (
174
) measures the geometric curvature of the one-dimensional subspace carried by a particular extracted MLWF. Both quantities are evaluated strictly in reciprocal space using the unified discrete measure
\nu_{\alpha}
.

F.4
Order evaluation of the boundary seam defects

Appendix
D.3
shows that the interior links along each transported string are flattened,
so the nontrivial geometric mismatch is pushed entirely to the boundary seam.
Let
\beta
denote the link direction in which the seam appears after the first extraction.
The order evaluations of the single-state and subspace-level diagnostics on this boundary are strictly
\mathcal{O}(L^{0})
:

\begin{cases}\omega_{\beta}^{1}(\bm{k})=\mathcal{O}(L^{0})\\
d_{\beta}(\bm{k})=1-\sigma_{\min}^{2}(\bm{k};\delta\bm{k}_{\beta})=\mathcal{O}(L^{0})\end{cases}\quad(\bm{k}\in\mathrm{seam}).

(177)

Equivalently, the singular values themselves exhibit a constant
\mathcal{O}(L^{0})
floor:

\begin{cases}\sigma_{\beta}^{1}(\bm{k})=\mathcal{O}(L^{0})\\
\sigma_{\min}(\bm{k};\delta\bm{k}_{\beta})=\mathcal{O}(L^{0})\end{cases}\quad(\bm{k}\in\mathrm{seam}).

(178)

These finite
\mathcal{O}(L^{0})
evaluations confirm that neither the single-state nor the subspace-level local kernels develop any anomalous
L
-dependent divergence. The macroscopic accumulation of these local defects over the seam, which yields the
\mathcal{O}(L)
scaling of the total spread, is explicitly evaluated in Sec.
VI.3
.
