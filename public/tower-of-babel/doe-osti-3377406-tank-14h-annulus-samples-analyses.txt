 Executive Summary
Savannah River Mission Completion (SRMC) Waste Removal and Tank Closure has requested that Savannah River
National Laboratory (SRNL) perform characterization analyses of Tank 14H annulus samples in support of closure
activities specifically annulus cleaning. SRMC requested assistance due to encountering relatively insoluble solids while
washing the annulus with water.
This report provides important characterization including radiochemical and identification of the annulus compounds
including filtrate and air-dried solids in the November and December 2025 Tank 14H samples, HTF-14-25-74, HTF-14-25-
76, and HTF-14-25-80. HTF-14-25-74 was a mostly liquid sample with a minor quantity of solids while HTF-14-25-76 and
HTF-14-25-80 were mainly composed of wet solids. The HTF-14-25-80 sample was obtained due to an insufficient
volume of solids present in HTF-14-25-76. The HTF-14-25-80 solid sample was also analyzed for mineral identification by
X-ray diffraction and found to be primarily gibbsite. The results reported here were utilized by SRMC to halt annulus
washing and later declare preliminary cease waste removal for Tank 14H.

1.0 Introduction
Tank 14H, a Type II storage tank, completed construction in 1956 and entered service in 1957 as an H-Canyon
waste receipt tank. The double-shelled Type II tanks are composed of a carbon steel primary wall and a concrete
secondary wall with a carbon steel containment pan. From 1957 through 1959, Tank 14H received high heat
waste from the H-Canyon Plutonium-Uranium Reduction Extraction (PUREX) process and H-Modified (HM)
process high-heat waste until 1960 where waste leakage into the Tank 14H annulus was detected and further
high-heat waste receipts were diverted to another waste tank. In 1965, Tank 14H was filled to capacity at
1,061,000 gallons from HM low-heat waste and Thorium Recovery Extraction (THOREX) low-heat waste
transfers. In 1968, supernatant was removed from Tank 14H in preparation for a sludge removal campaign and,
in 1969, a sludge removal campaign (waste removal phase 1) was completed. In 1970, Tank 14H received
supernatant and refilled to capacity. Tank 14H remained idle (excluding minor transfers and receipts) until 1978
when the majority of the supernatant was removed and the tank became inactive.

Tank 14H has about 50 leak sites in the primary tank.1 The waste that leaked into the secondary containment
has been contained within the annular pan and has not reached the surrounding soils.2 The supernatant in the
annulus space dried and formed solids highly resistant to dissolution via water. Fifteen cleaning batch campaigns
were recently performed over the period of approximately 3 months on the solids in the Tank 14H annulus that
consisted of approximately 5000 gallons of water additions, soaking periods of 24 hours to 11 days, then pump
outs/partial transfers (to remove the potential boundary layer) to the primary tank. The initial and final solid salt
volumes were estimated to be 7 kgals and 3.7 kgals, respectively.3

Savannah River Mission Completion (SRMC) Waste Removal and Tank Closure requested via a Technical Task
Request (TTR)4 that the Savannah River National Laboratory (SRNL) characterize samples from tanks in F and H
Tank Farms as they transition into closure mode for requirements including inhalation dose potential for low-
rem/high-rem transfers and documented safety analysis closure storage mode determination.5 The TTR
stipulates that additional scope can be requested as necessary. As part of this additional scope SRMC requested
via emails (see Appendix A) that SRNL perform radionuclide and chemical analyses to assist with identifying
radiochemical concentrations and improving washing strategies of the solids. By revealing the concentrations of
the radionuclide analytes of interest (Cs-137, I-129, Sr-90, and Tc-99) in the solids samples were sufficiently low,
further washing of the annulus was ceased.6-8 Once annulus cleaning was ceased, the primary tank underwent
further waste removal to move towards preliminary cease waste removal (PCWR) determination.3

2.0 Experimental Procedure
Initially, two Tank 14H samples (HTF-14-25-74 and HTF-14-25-76) were pulled; HTF-14-25-74 was a mostly liquid
82mL sample, and HTF-14-25-76 was a solid sample in a 200mL dip bottle. Both samples were pulled from the
annulus bottom through the inspection port IP-169. The solids for HTF-14-25-76 were obtained through a
fabricated tool.
SRNL received HTF-14-25-74 on November 19, 2025 in a 82mL stainless-steel dip bottle. The contents of the
bottle were transferred to a plastic bottle and then filtered through a 0.45 µm nylon filter (See Figure 1). The
undiluted filtrate was analyzed for Cs-137, I-129, Sr-90, Tc-99, and density. The solids on the filter were air-dried
for 4 days and transferred into a plastic bottle for later analysis.

On November 21, 2025, SRNL received HTF-14-25-76 in a 200mL stainless-steel dip bottle. Upon opening the
bottle, it was observed that the bottle contained minimal solids (See Figure 2). The “as-received” wet solids
were approximately 5.7 grams, and the air-dried solids (air-dried for 9 days) were approximately 2.5 grams.
Upon air drying the solids, the sample was analyzed for bulk density using a container of known volume that was
previously calibrated with water. Additionally, the solids were utilized in digestions including alkali fusions9 and
I-129 preparations.

 2

 Figure 2. HTF-14-25-76 Sample Dip Bottle Containing “As-Received” Solids.

To measure I-129 in the solids (in triplicate), an aliquot of solids (approximately 0.4-0.6 grams for each replicate)
was transferred, in the shielded cells, into a bottle containing potassium iodide tracer. Nitric acid was added to
the bottle containing sample and KI and left overnight to digest the sample. On the following day (further delay
would lead to iodine loss from sublimation), sodium hydroxide solution was added to the bottle and left
overnight to precipitate the sludge solids. After a day, the caustic sample was added to a bottle containing
crystalline silicotitanate (CST) and monosodium titanate (MST) to remove Cs-137, Sr-90, and actinides from the
sample. Additional sodium hydroxide solution was added to the bottle and then filtered through a 0.2 µm filter.
The resulting filtrate was measured for I-129.
Cs-137 activity was measured in both the aqua regia digestions of HTF-14-25-74 and the peroxide fusions of HTF-
14-25-76. Due to the insufficient amount of solids present in HTF-14-25-76, the HTF-14-25-74 air-dried solids
from the filter were utilized for aqua regia digestions10 for Tc-99 and Cs-137. In order to have the amount
necessary for a washing characterization study and x-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis to determine the identity of
the mineral to assist with the washing strategy, another solids sample, HTF-14-25-80, was pulled from the
inspection port IP-169. This sample, after air-drying for 18 days, yielded approximately 79.6 grams of solids. A
small aliquot (~0.13 g) of HTF-14-25-80 unwashed air-dried solids was analyzed by XRD. Additionally, as per
SRMC email request (see Appendix A), HTF-14-25-80 air-dried solids were measured for bulk density using a
container of known volume and Tc-99 post aqua regia digestions. The washing characterization study was not
completed as radiochemical measurements demonstrated that further washing of the tank was unnecessary for
waste removal purposes (see Appendix A).

2.1 Radiochemical Analyses
2.1.1 Gamma
Digested sample and liquid sample aliquots were prepared and diluted in the laboratory to levels appropriate for
gamma pulse height analysis on high purity germanium (HPGe) detectors. Each sample was counted in a
calibrated geometry for a length of time to obtain the minimum method uncertainty of 5.00% for Cs-137.

2.1.2 I-129
Filtrates from the shielded cells were acidified prior to addition of Bio-Rad ammonium molybdophosphate
(AMP), which must be in acidic condition for successful filtration and removal of Cs-137. These additions and
filtrations were performed twice to reduce interference. After reduction with sodium sulfite, the iodine was
precipitated to AgI using silver nitrate and then separated by filtration. The filter was then counted in a
calibrated geometry using low energy photon/x-ray, thin-windowed, semi-planar, HPGe spectrometers. The
filters were then folded and placed into rabbits for yield determination of the elemental iodine by neutron
activation analysis (NAA). A known amount of dissolved tracer was irradiated, along with the samples, to
quantify yield on a per sample basis to correct for losses during separation. A blank sample was included to track
for contamination throughout the method.

2.1.3 Sr-90
Digested sample and liquid sample aliquots were first spiked with elemental Sr to trace separation efficiency on
a per sample basis. Two blank samples were spiked with a known Sr-90 standard to calibrate the samples’ final
results. A non-spiked blank sample was included to track for contamination throughout the method. A serial
dilution of one of the samples was also included as an additional quality control (QC) check for data quality. The
samples were run through crown-ether-based solid phase extractant to separate the Sr-90 from the matrix.
Equal aliquots were measured by Inductively Coupled Plasma- Emission Spectroscopy (ICP-ES) and liquid
scintillation counting (LSC). ICP-ES measured the elemental Sr for recovery purposes. The blank spikes allowed
for correction of all samples due to being spiked with a known amount of Sr-90. The LSC counts measured gross
beta at a calibrated energy range to convert from cpm to dpm Sr-90.

2.1.4 Tc-99
Digested sample and liquid sample aliquots with separated Tc-99m tracer that was produced in-house from Mo-
99 using the NAA facility. Two blank samples were spiked with a known Tc-99 standard to calibrate the samples’
final results. A non-spiked blank sample was included to track for contamination throughout the method. To
minimize volatility, the sample solutions were made basic before extracting the Tc-99 using Eichrom TEVA resin
columns. After this purification, the samples were then run through an Eichrom TEVA filter to collect the Tc-99
for counting. The filters were folded and placed into LSC vials, where the Tc-99m yields were measured by an
HPGe well detector to correct for losses during the radiochemical separations. After waiting several days for the
Tc-99m to decay, so as not to cause interference, the samples were counted by LSC to attain a gross beta value.
Calibrated efficiencies allowed the gross beta cpm value to be converted to dpm Tc-99 after recovery correction.

The radionuclide concentrations were higher in the air-dried solids compared to the filtrate which is expected as
the air-dried solids are composed of dried residual supernatant along with the insoluble solids. The Sr-90
concentration was much higher in the air-dried solids than the filtrate as Sr-90 tends to be insoluble in caustic
media. The higher activity of Cs-137 than Sr-90 is also expected as the annulus material did not contain much
sludge which would have remained in the primary tank. The concentrations of the radionuclides between the
solids and the filtrate cannot be directly compared as the concentrations are on different bases, e.g.,
concentration of radionuclide in a gram of solids or a milliliter of filtrate. In order to directly compare the two
bases, a conversion would be needed utilizing weight percent solids data of the sample. In the case of the HTF-
14-26-74 sample, the weight percent solids measurement was not performed as insufficient material was
present.

 Figure 3. HTF-14-25-80 Unwashed Air-Dried Solids XRD

In Figure 3, the mineral phases are reported for the HTF-14-25-80 unwashed air-dried solid sample. Gibbsite,
trona, sodium aluminum silicate hydrate, and zinc phosphide were found in the sample. Gibbsite is likely the
most prevalent mineral present in the sample as gibbsite is highly neutral pH water insoluble. In acidic and
alkaline environments, the solubility of gibbsite is higher. One process at the Savannah River Site (SRS) that
regularly dissolves gibbsite solids in primary waste tanks is low temperature aluminum dissolution (LTAD).16
However, LTAD cannot be performed in the annulus due to safety basis concerns.

In Table 4, the density analyses are reported for the HTF-14-25-74 filtrate, HTF-14-25-76 solid, and HTF-14-25-80
solid samples. The filtrate density was similar to water as the solids in the tank had been previously washed with
water during the cleaning process. The HTF-14-25-74 solid density was not measured due to insufficient sample
volume. The air-dried solid samples were prepared by mixing with a spatula; therefore, the particle sizes of the
solids and void volumes between the solids can vary. A smaller, less varied void volume would lead to a higher
density. Additionally, although the HTF-14-25-80 solids were air-dried longer, there was a much higher quantity
of solids that were dried. Potentially, the difference of density between the solids of HTF-14-25-76 and HTF-14-
25-80 is due to difference in particle size and water content.

4.0 Conclusions
SRNL performed characterization analyses of Tank 14H annulus samples in support of closure activities for
annulus cleaning. This report provided important characterization of the solids and filtrate in the Tank 14H
samples, HTF-14-25-74, HTF-14-25-76, and HTF-14-25-80, for Cs-137, I-129, Sr-90, Tc-99, XRD, and density. The
results reported here were utilized by SRMC to cease washing and waste removal activities in the Tank 14H
annulus and move towards finishing waste removal in the primary tank. Additionally, these results were
incorporated in the decision for PCWR and to enter the sampling and analysis phase.3
