Aligning Validation with Deployment: Target-Weighted Cross-Validation for Spatial Prediction
Alexander Brenning alexander.brenning@uni-jena.de, Thomas Suesse thomas.suesse@uni-jena.de

Abstract
Cross-validation (CV) is commonly used to estimate predictive risk when independent test data are unavailable. Its validity depends on the assumption that validation tasks are sampled from the same distribution as prediction tasks encountered during deployment. In spatial prediction and other settings with structured data, this assumption is frequently violated, leading to biased estimates of deployment risk. We propose Target-Weighted CV (TWCV), an estimator of deployment risk that accounts for discrepancies between validation and deployment task distributions, thus accounting for (1) covariate shift and (2) task-difficulty shift. We characterize prediction tasks by descriptors such as covariates and spatial configuration. TWCV assigns weights to validation losses such that the weighted empirical distribution of validation tasks matches the corresponding distribution over a target domain. The weights are obtained via calibration weighting, yielding an importance-weighted estimator that directly targets deployment risk. Since TWCV requires adequate coverage of the deployment task distribution’s support, we combine it with a spatially buffered validation task generator that diversifies the task difficulty distribution. In a simulation study with controlled sampling designs, conventional CV estimators as well as recently proposed spatial estimators exhibit substantial bias under clustered and preferential sampling, whereas TWCV with a buffered task generator remains approximately unbiased across scenarios. A case study in environmental pollution mapping further confirms that discrepancies between validation and deployment task distributions can materially affect performance assessment, and that buffered TWCV estimates better reflect the prediction task over the target domain. These results establish task distribution mismatch as a primary source of CV estimator bias in spatial prediction and show that calibration-based reweighting combined with a suitable validation task generator provides a viable approach to estimating predictive risk under dataset shift.

Introduction

Reliable estimation of predictive performance is a central component of machine learning workflows. In many applications including environmental mapping, model selection and assessment rely on cross-validation (CV) when independent test data are unavailable
[
28
,
30
,
32
,
27
]
. Standard CV implicitly assumes that validation observations are representative of the prediction tasks encountered during deployment. Under this assumption, CV provides an approximately unbiased estimate of predictive risk
[
14
]
.

In practice, this assumption is frequently violated because the distribution of prediction tasks differs from that induced by the sampling design. Such situations are commonly described as
dataset shift
or
covariate shift
in the machine learning literature
[
29
,
36
]
. When deployment and validation distributions differ, CV may estimate predictive performance under conditions that do not reflect actual deployment tasks. Spatial prediction provides a particularly transparent example: environmental monitoring networks are often preferentially located due to logistical constraints or targeted sampling, whereas predictions are typically required across an entire geographic domain. Consequently, CV evaluates tasks defined by the monitoring network rather than those corresponding to the full prediction domain.

Beyond covariate shift, spatial prediction also highlights
task-difficulty shift
. Prediction distance is a key driver of prediction skill under spatial dependence
[
5
]
, and differences in prediction-distance distributions between validation and deployment settings can strongly affect performance estimates, especially under clustered sampling
[
9
,
25
]
. In this context, it is important to understand that CV can be viewed as a two-step process: (i) a
task generator
that samples validation tasks, and (ii) a
risk estimator
that aggregates their losses. In the presence of dataset shift, bias may arise from either component.

Previous work has shown that spatial dependence between training and validation samples can lead to overly optimistic performance estimates
[
30
,
18
]
. Spatially structured resampling strategies therefore aim to reduce dependence by enforcing spatial separation
[
3
,
4
,
30
,
31
]
. Related approaches such as nearest-neighbour distance matching attempt to approximate deployment conditions through resampling
[
25
,
21
]
, while other work proposes diagnostics of extrapolation in feature space
[
24
,
27
]
or of distance-dependent model performance
[
5
]
. However, these approaches either modify the validation design or provide diagnostics, rather than explicitly targeting predictive risk under the deployment task distribution.

In this paper we propose
Target-Weighted Cross-Validation
(TWCV), a CV estimator that targets predictive performance under the distribution of prediction tasks across a specified deployment domain. TWCV can be interpreted as a calibration-based approximation to importance-weighted risk estimation, in which weights are constructed by matching marginal distributions of task descriptors rather than by estimating density ratios directly. It assigns weights to validation losses so that the weighted distribution of validation tasks matches the covariate and task-difficulty distributions of tasks across the target domain. The weighting scheme is obtained using calibration weighting (raking), a well-established approach in survey statistics
[
10
,
23
]
. By incorporating prediction distance as a task descriptor
[
5
]
, TWCV accounts for both covariate and task-difficulty shift through reweighting. In contrast to approaches that modify the resampling design, TWCV operates at the level of the risk estimator, correcting distributional mismatch through weighting rather than altering the task generator.

Contributions.
This paper makes three main contributions.

1.

We formulate CV as an estimation problem over a distribution of prediction tasks, explicitly separating the roles of the validation task generator and the risk estimator. This perspective clarifies that bias in CV arises primarily from mismatches between validation and deployment task distributions.

2.

We introduce Target-Weighted Cross-Validation (TWCV), a calibration-weighting estimator of deployment risk that reweights validation losses to match the distribution of task descriptors in the target domain. TWCV can be interpreted as a calibration-based approximation to importance-weighted risk estimation that trades flexibility for improved stability in structured prediction settings such as spatial prediction.

3.

Through simulation experiments and a real-world case study in environmental pollution mapping, we show that task-distribution mismatch is a dominant source of bias in commonly used cross-validation schemes, and that TWCV substantially reduces this bias when combined with validation task generators that ensure sufficient coverage of the deployment-task space.

2
Deployment-Oriented Risk Estimation

2.1
Prediction tasks

Consider a spatial prediction problem in which observations

\{(s_{i},x_{i},z_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}

are available at spatial locations
s_{i}
, where
x_{i}
denotes a vector of predictors and
z_{i}
the observed response variable. A predictive model
f
is used to generate predictions
\hat{z}=f(x)
at locations where observations are not available.

Predictions are typically required across a specified spatial domain
D
, for example a grid of locations covering a geographic region. Each prediction location induces a
prediction task
that depends not only on the predictors at that location but also on the spatial configuration of the available training observations, or more generally, descriptors of task difficulty.

We therefore represent prediction tasks by descriptors

T=(x,d),

where
x
denotes the covariate vector at the prediction location and
d
summarizes the task difficulty.

The inclusion of
d
reflects the fact that prediction difficulty in spatial models depends on the configuration of nearby observations relative to the prediction location. In geostatistical prediction, for example, prediction uncertainty is known to increase with distance to nearby observations and with sparse sampling configurations
[
45
]
. Yet even the prediction performance of conceptually non-spatial machine-learning models can depend systematically on spatial prediction distance
[
5
,
13
]
.
More generally,
T
may be any measurable function of the prediction location and training configuration (see Section
7.3
).

2.2
Deployment risk

Let
L(y,\hat{y})
denote a loss function measuring prediction error. The predictive performance of a model under deployment conditions can be expressed as

R_{\text{deploy}}(f)=\mathbb{E}_{T\sim P_{\text{target}}}[L(T,f)],

where
P_{\text{target}}
denotes the distribution of prediction tasks across the target prediction domain. We assume that the deployment-task distribution is known or can be approximated from a predefined target domain (for example, study area).

In spatial mapping applications, this distribution is induced by the set of prediction locations in
D
, the associated covariate values at these locations, and the configuration of the training data used to generate predictions. The quantity
R_{\text{deploy}}(f)
therefore represents the expected prediction loss for the model when applied across the entire target domain.

This formulation parallels the notion of deployment risk in the presence of dataset shift, where the distribution of prediction tasks encountered during deployment differs from the distribution observed during model training or validation
[
29
,
36
]
.

2.3
Cross-validation as a validation task generator

In cross-validation (CV), predictive performance is estimated using
n_{\text{val}}
validation observations obtained from held-out subsets of the data. Let
T_{i}
denote the prediction task associated with validation observation
i
, and let
L_{i}
denote the corresponding prediction loss. Standard CV estimates predictive risk as

\hat{R}_{\text{CV}}=\frac{1}{n_{\text{val}}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\text{val}}}L_{i}.

This estimator implicitly targets the quantity

R_{\text{CV}}=\mathbb{E}_{T\sim P_{\text{CV}}}[L(T,f)],

where
P_{\text{CV}}
denotes the distribution of validation tasks induced by the sampling design and the CV procedure. Conventional CV and other resampling procedures such as its spatial variants or the boostrap can therefore be conceptualized as
task generators
that draw tasks from an underlying task distribution.

When observations are sampled independently from the deployment distribution, the task distributions
P_{\text{CV}}
and
P_{\text{target}}
coincide, and standard random CV provides an approximately unbiased estimate of deployment risk. However, when the validation relies on a task distribution that differs from the distribution of prediction tasks across the target domain, CV may provide biased estimates of predictive performance
[
29
,
36
,
42
,
5
]
.

2.4
Deployment shift in spatial prediction

In spatial prediction problems, differences between
P_{\text{CV}}
and
P_{\text{target}}
commonly arise from two sources. First, monitoring networks may sample environmental conditions unevenly, leading to differences in the distribution of predictors between sampled locations and prediction locations
[
34
,
21
]
. Second, the spatial configuration of training observations relative to prediction locations may differ between validation tasks and deployment tasks, resulting in differences in prediction-distance distributions
[
5
,
25
]
.

Previous work has highlighted the importance of accounting for such structure in validation procedures. Spatial CV strategies attempt to reduce dependence between training and validation samples by enforcing spatial separation
[
30
,
4
,
31
,
25
]
. However, even when spatial dependence is addressed, the resulting validation tasks may still differ systematically from prediction tasks encountered across the target domain
[
5
]
.

2.5
Weighted estimators of deployment risk

A natural approach to addressing deployment shift is to reweight validation losses so that validation tasks reproduce the distribution of prediction tasks across the target domain. Importance-weighted risk estimation is a standard strategy for addressing dataset shift in machine learning
[
36
]
. In the spatial prediction setting considered here, weights can instead be constructed through calibration weighting, which adjusts the empirical distribution of validation tasks to match known margins of the target domain
[
10
,
23
]
.

This perspective is closely related to importance-weighted risk estimation under covariate shift
[
35
,
36
]
. If the density ratio
w(T)=p_{\text{target}}(T)/p_{\text{CV}}(T)
were known, deployment risk could be expressed as

R_{\text{deploy}}(f)=\mathbb{E}_{T\sim P_{\text{CV}}}\!\left[w(T)\,L(T,f)\right],

and estimated by a weighted empirical average over validation tasks. In practice, direct estimation of density ratios in high-dimensional task spaces is challenging and often unstable
[
37
]
. Calibration weighting provides an alternative by constructing weights that match selected moments or marginal distributions of task descriptors between validation and deployment tasks
[
10
]
, thereby yielding a stable, low-dimensional approximation to importance weighting that directly targets the deployment-task distribution. In this sense, TWCV can be interpreted as a calibration-based importance-weighted estimator of deployment risk.

In the following section we consider two instances of this idea. Distance-weighted CV corrects for differences in prediction-distance distributions, while the more general target-weighted CV estimator balances multiple descriptors of spatial prediction tasks, accounting for both covariate shift and task-difficulty shift.

3
Distance-Weighted and Target-Weighted Cross-Validation

When the distribution of prediction distances differs between validation tasks and prediction tasks across the target domain, CV estimates may be biased for deployment risk due to the often observed distance-dependence of prediction error
[
5
,
13
]
.
To address this issue, we consider a weighted estimator that adjusts validation losses according to prediction distance. Let
d_{i}
denote the prediction distance associated with validation task
T_{i}
, defined here as the distance between the prediction location and its nearest training observation within the corresponding CV fold.
Distance-Weighted CV
(DWCV) estimates deployment risk as

\hat{R}_{\text{DWCV}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\text{val}}}w_{i}^{(d)}L_{i},

where weights
w_{i}^{(d)}
are chosen such that the weighted distribution of prediction distances among validation tasks matches the distribution of prediction distances across the target domain.

In practice, prediction distances are discretized into a small number of classes (for example quantile-based intervals) to stabilize the weighting procedure. Let
g_{d}(T_{i})
denote an indicator vector identifying the distance class of task
T_{i}
. The weights are then constructed so that

\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\text{val}}}w_{i}^{(d)}g_{d}(T_{i})=\sum_{u\in D}g_{d}(T_{u}),

where the right-hand side represents the empirical distribution of distance classes among prediction tasks across the target domain.

Thus, distance-weighted CV adjusts validation losses so that the spatial configuration of validation tasks reflects that of prediction tasks across the deployment domain.

DWCV addresses deployment shift arising from differences in prediction distance but does not account for differences in environmental conditions between validation tasks and prediction tasks. These differences may be critical for judging the performance of machine learning models in the deployment situation since challenging prediction tasks with a poor model performance may be more or less prevalent there than on the validation set, as in non-stationary or heteroskedastic environments.

To address both sources of mismatch simultaneously, we introduce
Target-Weighted CV
(TWCV), which balances multiple descriptors of prediction tasks.
Let
g(T)
denote a vector of balancing variables derived from the task descriptor
T=(x,d)
. These variables may include discretized descriptors of prediction task difficulty (for example, prediction distance) as well as discretized environmental predictors that characterize the conditions under which predictions are made. The target distribution of prediction tasks across the deployment domain can be summarized through the empirical margins

m=\frac{1}{|D|}\sum_{u\in D}g(T_{u}),

where the sum runs over prediction tasks induced by locations in the target domain
D
.

TWCV assigns weights
w_{i}
to validation tasks such that the weighted distribution of task descriptors matches these target margins. Formally, weights are obtained by solving the calibration equations

\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\text{val}}}w_{i}g(T_{i})=m,

subject to normalization
\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\text{val}}}w_{i}=1
.

The resulting estimator of deployment risk is

\hat{R}_{\text{TWCV}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\text{val}}}w_{i}L_{i}.

The weights are obtained using
calibration weighting
(also known as raking), which adjusts the empirical distribution of validation tasks to match known margins of the target population
[
10
,
23
]
. In this way, TWCV can be interpreted as a calibration-based estimator of deployment risk.

TWCV requires a validation task generator whose support covers that of the deployment-task distribution. We propose buffered resampling with a range of buffer distances as a validation-task generator as it allows us to create relevant combinations of environmental predictors and prediction distances in a more controlled way than unbuffered random CV or leave-one-out (LOO) sampling. Buffered task generation can be implemented as leave-
m
-out resampling with randomly varying buffer radii while ensuring that training sample sizes do not deteriorate too strongly (for example,
\geq 80
% of original sample size).

In summary, by weighting validation losses so that validation tasks reproduce the distribution of prediction tasks across the target domain, TWCV provides an estimator of deployment risk that accounts for both spatial configuration and environmental representativeness of prediction tasks with regards to a known deployment situation. It requires a task generator such as buffered resampling that ensures adequate coverage of the deployment task distribution.

4
Simulation Study

A simulation study was conducted to evaluate the performance of the proposed estimators under controlled conditions. It assesses how well CV estimators approximate the (known, true) deployment risk when the distribution of validation tasks differs from the distribution of prediction tasks across the target domain.

We compare conventional non-spatial and spatial estimators as well as distance-weighted and target-weighted CV. For each estimator we evaluate the bias of the estimated deployment risk across repeated simulations.

4.1
Data-generating process

The simulated response surface was generated on the unit square domain

D=[0,1]^{2}
according to

Z(s)=\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}x_{1}(s)+\beta_{2}x_{2}(s)+\varepsilon(s),

where
x_{1}(s)=s_{x}
is a large-scale west–east environmental gradient,
x_{2}(s)
is a
spatially structured Gaussian predictor field, and
\varepsilon(s):=\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}(s)\cdot\varepsilon_{0}(s)
is a heteroskedastic residual random field derived from a
mean-zero Gaussian random field
\varepsilon_{0}(s)
with exponential covariance
C(h)=\exp(-h/\rho)

and a linearly scaled variance
\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}(s)=0.4+1.2s_{x}
exhibiting a fourfold variance increase from left to right.
We considered two trend-strength settings,

(\beta_{1},\beta_{2})\in\{(1,0.5),(3,1)\}
, and two residual correlation ranges,

\rho\in\{0.05,0.10\}
, with
\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}=1
.

To assess the effect of irrelevant information on TWCV, we additionally consider
x_{3}(s)=s_{y}
(north-south gradient) and a second spatially structured Gaussian field
x_{4}(s)
generated independently from the other fields.

Simulations were repeated 100 times.
For each simulated dataset a sample of
n=200
observations was drawn from the spatial domain according to one of three designs representing common monitoring-network configurations and sampling practices encountered in environmental applications:

1.

Random sampling
: observation locations were sampled uniformly in continuous space across the domain
D
.

2.

Clustered sampling
: observation locations were drawn from
10
spatial clusters.

3.

Environmentally biased sampling
: observation locations were generated
in continuous space with inclusion probabilities depending on the large-scale
predictor
x_{1}(s)
, yielding preferential sampling toward one end of the
environmental gradient.

Illustration of the three sampling designs and their effects on spatial prediction. Columns correspond to random, clustered, and preferentially biased sampling for one representative simulation setting (strong trend,
\rho=0.1
). The top row shows the simulated response field on
D=[0,1]^{2}
together with sampled locations. The second and third rows show the corresponding prediction maps obtained from random forest and heteroskedastic regression–kriging, respectively.

4.2
Spatial prediction models

Two contrasting prediction models were considered: Random forest (RF;
[
2
]
) and heteroskedastic regression–kriging (HRK), both with
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
as predictors. We used standard non-spatial RF in the ranger package
[
47
]
. Regression–kriging (or kriging with external drift) combines a linear trend model with spatially autocorrelated residuals
[
45
]
. We implement RK in gstat
[
26
]
with a heteroskedastic residual variance model with variance dependent on population density. RK is therefore the correctly specified probabilistic model for the simulated situation, but it requires the empirical estimation of linear model coefficients, variance model coefficient and semivariogram model parameters in every simulation run. Refer to Supplementary Section
S1.1.3
for implementation details.

4.3
Deployment task and validation design

Deployment performance was evaluated over a dense grid of
100\times 100
prediction locations covering the spatial domain. The true deployment risk was computed by comparing predictions to the simulated response values at these grid locations.

Prediction tasks for the grid locations were used to determine the target distribution of task descriptors required for DWCV and TWCV weighting.

Prediction models were evaluated using the following validation procedures.

Conventional non-spatial CV was implemented as leave-one-out CV (
LOOCV
) and as
random

k
-fold
CV
(
k=10
). Spatial CV uses spatially structured folds generated by partitioning observation coordinates into
k=10
clusters using
k
-means clustering
[
31
]
; it was implemented as leave-one-block-out CV (
LOBOCV
). Additionally, we include two recently proposed CV methods that match validation distributions with prediction-distance (
Spatial kNNDM
) and feature-space distributions (
Feature kNNDM
, meaning
k
-nearest-neighbor distance matching;
[
21
]
); the latter version is based on the first two principal components and is considered experimental.

For
DWCV
, validation losses were weighted so that the distribution of prediction distances among validation tasks matched the corresponding distribution across the prediction grid. 500 validation tasks were sampled from the training data with a buffered leave-one-out (LOO) procedure that approximates this distribution.

TWCV
extends this approach by balancing multiple descriptors of prediction tasks. Balancing variables were discretized before computing calibration weights. We split each quantitative covariate into five categories based on quintiles and ran a finer discretization based on deciles for comparison. We generated 500 tasks through buffered LOO, thus ensuring full coverage of the targeted prediction distance distribution.

In the simulation study, we considered two contrasting TWCV weighting schemes, each with coarse and fine binning. (1)
TWCV
and
TWCV–fine
used only the large-scale predictor
x_{1}(s)
, in addition to prediction distances as in DWCV. (2)
TWCV–extended
(
–fine
) and
Feature kNNDM–extended
additionally ingested
x_{2}
,
x_{3}
and
x_{4}
, challenging the calibration-weighting estimator to deal with a higher-dimensional setting that involves irrelevant information. For the extended and fine variants, raking weights were shrunk towards uniform weights using a fixed shrinkage parameter of 0.2 in order to stabilize estimation under the richer balancing specification
[
22
]
. This value was chosen a priori as a moderate regularization strength. To assess the stability of these estimators, we analyzed weight distributions and calculated effective sample sizes (ESS), collapsing the generated tasks onto the original training instances. Details on weight shrinkage and the definition of ESS are provided in Appendix
S1.1.1
.

As an additional baseline, we considered importance-weighted CV (
IWCV
), a standard covariate-shift approach in which validation losses are weighted by estimated density ratios between deployment and validation tasks
[
36
,
35
]
. In contrast to IWCV, which estimates density ratios via probabilistic classification, TWCV constructs weights by enforcing calibration constraints on selected task descriptors. We implemented two variants, denoted Random IWCV and Buffered IWCV, using random 10-fold CV and buffered LOO as validation task generators, respectively. Density ratios were estimated by probabilistic classification using logistic regression with forward variable selection. To ensure a fair comparison, IWCV uses the same set of task descriptors as the extended TWCV specification (for details see Supplementary Section
S1.1.2
). As with other importance-weighting approaches, IWCV may yield unstable weights when overlap between validation and deployment task distributions is limited.

For reference we considered model-based uncertainty estimates. For HRK, we used the kriging variance as a model-based estimator of mean squared prediction error, which is unbiased under correct model specification
[
45
]
. For RF, we adopted the out-of-bag (OOB) prediction error. It is estimated using observations not used in the construction of individual trees and can be interpreted as an internal resampling-based estimate of generalization error
[
2
]
.

4.4
Evaluation

For each simulation replicate we computed the true deployment risk

R_{\text{deploy}}(f)=\frac{1}{|D|}\sum_{u\in D}L(Z(u),\hat{Z}(u)),

where
D
denotes the set of prediction locations in the target domain and

L(\cdot,\cdot)
is the loss function used throughout the evaluation. In the
experiments presented below we consider squared error loss.

For each estimator we then computed the estimated deployment risk
\hat{R}
using the respective validation procedure.
Estimator performance was summarized across simulation replicates using the root mean square error (RMSE).

5
Case Study

5.1
Case study: Air quality mapping

To illustrate the practical implications of deployment-oriented validation, we consider a spatial prediction problem with strongly non-uniform sampling. The objective is to assess how different CV estimators characterize predictive performance across a deployment region, and how different task generators approximate the deployment task distribution under realistic sampling configurations.

We analyze annual mean nitrogen dioxide (NO
2
) concentrations across Germany for the year 2018
[
39
,
40
]
using spatial predictors representing emission sources and environmental conditions. This type of analysis is commonly referred to as land-use regression and has been widely applied for large-scale air pollution mapping
[
41
,
15
]
. Resulting prediction surfaces are routinely used by environmental agencies for regulatory reporting and public information services
[
19
,
46
]
.
Monitoring networks are typically denser in urban areas and therefore biased relative to the full prediction domain
[
34
]
.

The dataset comprises annual mean NO
2
concentrations at 503 monitoring stations (Figure
2
). Predictions were generated on a nation-wide 2 km grid. Spatial predictors include elevation, distance to the coastline, population density, topographic roughness index (TRI), and topographic position index (TPI; data sources: USGS EROS
[
11
]
, SEDAC
[
6
]
, Copernicus/EEA
[
12
]
). Spatially smoothed population density served as a proxy for emission sources.

The monitoring network exhibits a strong bias toward urban areas: 50% of the stations are located within the 2% most densely populated areas (Figure
2
). Prediction distances for deployment locations are also substantially larger than the nearest-neighbour distances among monitoring stations.

Predicted annual mean NO
2
concentrations across Germany obtained with random forest (left) and regression–kriging models (right), along with the locations of 503 monitoring stations. Both models use the same set of topographic and demographic covariates.

Exploratory analysis indicated weak but long-range spatial dependence (practical range
\sim 700
km) and substantial small-scale variability (nugget-to-sill ratio
\sim 0.7
). Linear model residuals are non-stationary and heteroskedastic, with contrasting spatial dependence structures in rural versus urban areas (Supplementary Figure

We used the same task generators and estimators as in the simulation study, but without ‘extended’ and ‘fine’ versions. All predictors were included in TWCV balancing and Feature kNNDM distribution matching. We ran buffered TWCV and DWCV with 10,000 validation tasks.
Similar to the simulation study we applied random forests (RF) and regression–kriging (RK), in this case the common homoskedastic version. Predictive performance was evaluated using the RMSE.

6
Results

6.1
Simulation study

Validation task distributions generated by conventional CV schemes differed substantially from the deployment situation, particularly with respect to prediction distance in clustered and preferential sampling (Figure
3
). LOOCV and random CV predominantly produced short prediction distances because validation points remained surrounded by nearby training observations. LOBOCV shifted prediction distances towards larger values, although unevenly across the environmental gradient
x_{1}
. Buffered LOO sampling, in contrast, yielded a broader coverage of the
(x_{1},d)
task space and thus provided the information required for target-oriented weighting to approximate the deployment-task distribution. Spatial kNNDM showed slightly greater deviation from the deployment distribution while especially simple Feature-based kNNDM induced task distributions that are closer to random CV.

Figure 3:

Joint distribution of prediction tasks in
(x_{1},d)
space in the simulation study.
Panels compare the deployment task distribution with validation tasks generated by LOOCV, random
10
-fold CV, LOBOCV, and buffered LOOCV, separately for the three sampling designs.
Here,
x_{1}
denotes the large-scale environmental gradient and

the nearest-neighbour prediction distance to the corresponding training set.

These differences in task distributions translated into systematic biases of RMSE estimators (Figure
4
). Buffered TWCV and buffered IWCV were the only estimators that remained nearly unbiased across all sampling designs and for both prediction models (RF and HRK). In contrast, non-spatial LOOCV and random CV schemes exhibited substantial optimistic bias under clustered as well as preferential sampling. DWCV and spatial kNNDM removed this bias only in the clustered sampling design, where discrepancies in prediction distance dominate the mismatch between validation and deployment tasks. LOBOCV tended to produce pessimistically biased RMSE estimates, particularly under random and preferential sampling, reflecting its over-representation of harder prediction tasks with large prediction distances. Feature kNNDM was biased in all non-random designs. The same general pattern was observed across simulation scenarios, with stronger differences when autocorrelation range was larger (
\rho=0.1
vs.
0.05
; Supplementary Figure
S7
.

Model-based uncertainty estimates show contrasting behavior for the two models. For RF, the OOB RMSE estimate is optimistically biased under clustered and preferential sampling, consistent with the similarity between out-of-bag and random or LOOCV task distributions. For HRK, the model-based RMSE derived from kriging variances is nearly unbiased across most scenarios. A small optimistic bias under clustered sampling may arise because uncertainty in semivariogram estimation is not propagated into the kriging variance in the classical geostatistical implementation.

Random variability of RMSE estimators was greatest under the clustered sampling design (Supplementary Figure
S8
). For any given sampling design, the spatial-CV estimators showed the greatest variability. TWCV and HRK’s model-based estimator also showed slightly increased estimation uncertainty, i.e. variability across simulations.

Calibration weight diagnostics among buffered DWCV/TWCV variants revealed that under non-random sampling the concentration of weights increased and the ESS decreased with increasing complexity of balancing scheme, i.e. from DWCV (ESS fraction 80% for clustered and 99% for biased sampling) and simple TWCV (58 and 40%) to extended TWCV with fine bins (13 and 19%). Under random sampling, where weighting should be redundant, ESS was
>90
% for DWCV/TWCV and remained at
\sim
80% for extended and fine TWCV, but dropped to 50% in the ‘extended, fine‘ variant. Although this was mirrored by increases in extreme weights, TWCV complexity did not affect mean RMSE estimator error (Supplementary Figure
S9
).

Overall, the results show that mismatches between validation and deployment task distributions can be an important source of bias in CV-based uncertainty estimation, and that explicitly targeting the deployment-task distribution via TWCV nearly removes this bias when combined with a task generator offering broad enough support.

To assess robustness under more realistic ecological conditions, we conducted an additional simulation experiment based on the virtual species framework of Mila et al.
[
25
]
, which generates species occurrence patterns as nonlinear functions of multiple environmental predictors
[
20
]
. In contrast to our controlled simulations, this setting involves higher-dimensional covariates and more complex response surfaces. Results (Supplementary Section
S1.3
) were consistent with our main findings: Across different sampling biases, buffered TWCV achieves the most consistent RMSE estimator bias reduction, while especially spatial CV and plain LOOCV can be biased.

6.2
Case study: Air quality mapping

Among all resampling strategies, buffered LOO sampling produced the most complete coverage of the
(\text{population density},\text{prediction distance})
task space (Figure
5
), providing the variation necessary for weighted estimators. Random CV and LOOCV, in contrast, reproduced the sampling bias toward short prediction distances and high population densities and therefore fail to adequately represent the range of prediction tasks encountered across the deployment domain. LOBOCV yielded substantially larger prediction distances than those occurring in deployment, while still exhibiting a bias toward high population densities.

Across models, buffered TWCV and IWCV yielded substantially smaller RMSE estimates than the other validation strategies, typically by about 15–35% (Figure

Model-based uncertainty estimates from the RK model were pessimistically biased due to the over-representation of highly variable urbanized regions in the sample, which affected the model’s representation of spatial covariation and prediction variance based on the residual semivariogram (see Supplementary Figure

7
Discussion

Rather than restating the formal framework, this section focuses on interpreting the empirical findings and situating TWCV within the broader literature.

7.1
Task distribution mismatch as a source of bias

Building on the formalization in Section
2
, we interpret cross-validation for spatial prediction as an estimation problem under dataset shift, where the target is deployment risk over a distribution of prediction tasks.
From this perspective, mismatches between validation and deployment conditions are not incidental but a primary source of bias. The results show that commonly used validation strategies implicitly target different task distributions, leading to systematic over- or underestimation of predictive performance depending on how prediction distance and environmental conditions are represented.

As discussed in Section
2.5
, this mismatch corresponds to a covariate-shift setting in which importance-weighted risk estimation provides a principled correction. TWCV implements a calibration-based approximation to this idea by matching marginal distributions of selected task descriptors rather than estimating density ratios directly.
While density-ratio estimation offers a flexible solution in high-dimensional settings, it is often unstable in finite samples, whereas calibration weighting provides a more stable alternative when task distributions can be summarized by a low-dimensional set of descriptors
[
37
,
10
]
.

Across our simulation and case studies, bias in CV estimates was driven primarily by differences between validation and deployment task distributions. Prior work has emphasized dependence between training and validation data as a source of optimistic bias
[
3
,
30
,
18
]
, but our results show that reducing dependence alone is insufficient. Even spatial resampling schemes can induce systematic distortions in prediction distance and environmental conditions. These effects are reflected in the systematic biases observed in Section
6
, where different validation schemes emphasize different regions of the task space. These findings are consistent with critiques of spatial CV that highlight the importance of aligning validation design with prediction conditions
[
42
]
.

7.2
Relation to existing validation strategies

The recently proposed NNDM and kNNDM estimators address task distribution mismatch by modifying the CV resampling design so that the resulting validation tasks empirically resemble deployment conditions
[
25
,
21
]
. In contrast, TWCV operates at the level of the risk estimator rather than the task generator: it modifies the validation design only to ensure sufficient coverage and instead reweights validation losses to match the deployment-task distribution. In this sense, kNNDM and TWCV represent two complementary strategies—distribution matching by resampling versus distribution matching by weighting. Our results suggest that resampling-based matching alone may be insufficient in settings with preferential sampling or multiple sources of shift, whereas weighting provides a more flexible adjustment when suitable task descriptors are available. TWCV weighting can be applied to tasks generated by kNNDM, but in our study the simpler buffered leave-one-out resampling achieved better coverage and bias reduction.

Recent work has also proposed dissimilarity-adaptive CV, which assigns weights to validation losses based on measures of dissimilarity between training and validation samples
[
44
]
. While this approach shares the goal of accounting for heterogeneous prediction difficulty, it does not explicitly target a deployment-task distribution but emphasizes difficult prediction tasks. TWCV, in contrast, also addresses task difficulty, but it ensures representativeness of validation tasks with respect to deployment conditions.

Consistent with Section
2.5
, TWCV can be viewed as a calibration-based approximation to importance-weighted risk estimation.
This contrasts with approaches in the covariate-shift literature that estimate density ratios directly, for example via kernel mean matching or probabilistic classification
[
16
,
1
]
. By operating on discretized task descriptors, TWCV trades flexibility for robustness, reducing the variance of the resulting weights and avoiding the instability that often arises in high-dimensional density-ratio estimation with limited amounts of training data often found in environmental mapping. This perspective clarifies that TWCV does not introduce a fundamentally different objective, but rather provides a practically stable way of approximating importance-weighted deployment risk in structured prediction settings.
The additional IWCV baseline confirms this interpretation empirically: both IWCV and TWCV target deployment risk by reweighting validation losses, but TWCV replaces direct density-ratio estimation by calibration weighting on selected task descriptors, which may improve stability in structured, finite-sample settings.

In contrast to previously proposed spatial validation strategies, TWCV conceptually and algorithmically separates task generation from risk estimation. Validation tasks are generated using a resampling scheme that provides sufficient coverage of the task space, such as buffered leave-one-out sampling, and are then reweighted to match the deployment distribution. This separation clarifies that neither spatial separation nor task matching alone ensures valid performance estimation; what matters is alignment with the deployment task distribution. We could in fact show that unlike buffered LOO, random CV may not provide sufficient coverage of the deployment distribution’s support in order to achieve unbiased estimation.

7.3
Choice of task descriptors and balancing variables

The effectiveness of both TWCV and IWCV depends on the choice of task descriptors introduced in Section
2.1
. In our implementation, we focused on prediction distance and environmental predictors, capturing both spatial configuration and covariate distributions.
Prediction distance captures the availability of nearby training information and serves as a proxy for task difficulty, as established in prior work
[
45
,
5
]
. Environmental predictors characterize the domain over which predictions are made and connect TWCV to covariate shift settings
[
36
]
.

In practice, relatively parsimonious sets of balancing variables were sufficient to remove most of the bias. Including many variables can lead to unstable weights due to sparsity in the joint task distribution. This trade-off between representativeness and stability is well known in calibration weighting
[
23
]
and suggests that careful selection of balancing variables is important in practice.

This trade-off mirrors analogous considerations in importance-weighted learning, where flexible density-ratio estimators can capture complex distributional differences but often suffer from high variance, particularly in high-dimensional settings
[
37
]
. By contrast, calibration-based approaches such as TWCV restrict attention to low-dimensional summaries of the task distribution, which improves stability at the cost of potentially incomplete adjustment. This highlights a general bias–variance trade-off in deployment-oriented performance estimation: richer representations of task distributions can reduce bias but require stronger regularization or larger samples to remain stable.

7.4
Relation to extrapolation and feature-space distance

The framework does not explicitly incorporate measures of extrapolation in feature space or geographic space. Such measures have been proposed to assess model applicability and prediction difficulty beyond geographic proximity
[
24
,
33
]
.

In TWCV, aspects of feature-space mismatch are partially captured through environmental predictors included in the calibration variables. However, this does not fully represent local extrapolation in high-dimensional predictor space. Extending the framework to incorporate explicit feature-space similarity measures is a natural direction for future work, particularly in settings where extrapolation dominates prediction error.

Local point density (LPD) has recently been proposed as a predictor-space support metric that quantifies the density of training samples in a weighted covariate space
[
33
]
. In our conceptual framework, LPD can be interpreted as a task difficulty descriptor, as it characterizes the local data support underlying individual prediction tasks. However, the LPD approach of Schumacher et al.
[
33
]
depends on variable weights derived from a specific predictive model, rendering it inherently model-dependent. Incorporating LPD into the calibration-weighting framework would therefore lead to model-specific target task distributions, which is at odds with the goal of model-agnostic performance estimation. As a one-dimensional proxy for task difficulty in multivariate feature space, it also does not identify specific relevant dimensions, such as
x_{1}
in our simulation study or population density in the air-quality case study. For this reason, LPD should primarily serve as a post hoc diagnostic tool, rather than as a balancing variable within TWCV. In our simulation and case studies, LPD would not enrich our existing task descriptors, which are accounted for by the TWCV procedures in a model-agnostic way.

7.5
Limitations and practical considerations

Reliable estimation depends on adequate overlap between validation and deployment task distributions. When validation tasks do not sufficiently cover the support of the deployment-task distribution, calibration weights may become highly variable or undefined. This limitation is analogous to the positivity assumption in causal inference and covariate shift correction
[
36
]
. Buffered resampling strategies can help improve coverage of the task space, but will not fully resolve this issue in cases of strong sampling bias. It also introduces an additional computational overhead relative to standard cross-validation due to the large number of train–test repetitions required, but this cost can be controlled by restricting the number of validation tasks to what is required for stable estimation.

A more fundamental requirement is that the deployment-task distribution can be specified or approximated for a given application, for example through a defined prediction domain or a representative set of prediction locations. This assumption may be difficult to satisfy in settings with evolving data-generating conditions, such as non-stationary, dynamically evolving environmental regimes or time-series forecasting
[
17
]
, or in applications involving rare extreme events with limited empirical support, such as early warning systems.

While developed in a spatial context, the framework applies more broadly to settings in which prediction tasks exhibit structured variation. In time-series forecasting, task descriptors may include forecasting horizon or temporal distance to the training data, reflecting the well-known dependence of prediction accuracy on lead time
[
38
]
. In graph-structured or network data, task difficulty may depend on graph distance or connectivity relative to observed nodes, which has been shown to influence predictive performance in graph-based learning
[
7
]
. More generally, TWCV can incorporate metadata describing the varying reliability or precision of covariate information, linking the approach to domain adaptation and dataset shift settings where input distributions or noise characteristics differ between training and deployment domains
[
29
,
43
]
. Extending the framework to these settings requires careful specification of task descriptors and appropriate strategies for ensuring coverage of the resulting task space.

8
Conclusion

We proposed Target-Weighted Cross-Validation (TWCV) as a method for estimating predictive performance under deployment conditions when validation tasks are not representative of the prediction domain. By reweighting validation losses using calibration weighting, TWCV aligns the distribution of validation tasks with the target distribution of prediction tasks and thereby directly targets deployment risk. We showed that spatially buffered resampling induces a validation task generator that is suitable for achieving adequate coverage of the target distribution.

Simulation results show that conventional non-spatial as well as spatial cross-validation estimators can exhibit substantial bias under clustered or preferential sampling, whereas TWCV remains approximately unbiased across a greater variety of scenarios. Such discrepancies often occur in real-world applications such as air-quality monitoring or species distribution modeling and can materially affect performance assessments.

These findings highlight that mismatches between validation and deployment task distributions are a key source of bias in cross-validation. The framework is conceptually applicable to other settings with structured deployment conditions, such as forecasting, although empirical evaluation in these settings remains future work.

S1.1
Methods

S1.1.1
Weight shrinkage and effective sample size

For the extended and fine TWCV variants, stabilization of raking weights was achieved by shrinkage towards uniform weights
[
22
]
. Let
w_{i}
denote normalized calibration weights with
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}=1
. Shrunk weights were computed as

w_{i}^{(\lambda)}=(1-\lambda)w_{i}+\lambda,

with shrinkage parameter
\lambda\in[0,1]
. This transformation preserves the mean of the weights while reducing their variance and limiting the influence of extreme values. In this study, we fixed
\lambda=0.2
for all extended and fine TWCV variants.

To quantify the stability of weighting schemes, we computed the effective sample size (ESS) of the weights,

\mathrm{ESS}=\frac{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}\right)^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}^{2}},

which provides a measure of the dispersion of the weights
[
22
]
. ESS attains its maximum value
n
under uniform weights and decreases as weights become more uneven. Low ESS values indicate that a small number of validation tasks dominate the weighted estimator, which may lead to increased estimation variance and reduced robustness. In the case of resampling schemes such as buffered LOO that may re-use the same observation in multiple validation tasks, we calculated case-collapsed weights as the sum of task weights using this observation.

ESS was evaluated for all DWCV/TWCV variants and used as a diagnostic to assess the impact of increasing balancing dimensionality and finer discretization on the stability of the resulting estimators.

S1.1.2
Importance-weighted cross-validation

We considered importance-weighted cross-validation (IWCV) as an additional deployment-oriented baseline
[
36
,
35
]
. Let
T_{i}
denote the descriptor vector of validation task
i
, and let
L_{i}
be the corresponding validation loss. IWCV estimates deployment risk as

\hat{R}_{\mathrm{IWCV}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\mathrm{val}}}w_{i}L_{i},

where
w_{i}
is proportional to the density ratio
p_{\mathrm{target}}(T_{i})/p_{\mathrm{val}}(T_{i})
, normalized to sum to one over validation tasks.

Density ratios were estimated by probabilistic classification. Specifically, deployment tasks and validation tasks were pooled into a binary classification dataset, with deployment tasks coded as 1 and validation tasks as 0. We then fitted a logistic regression model and derived density-ratio weights from the fitted odds. To improve parsimony, predictors were selected by forward stepwise selection using the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). Candidate predictors comprised the available environmental covariates and prediction distance, corresponding to the extended set of task descriptors also used in the ‘extended’ TWCV specifications. Class priors were accounted for when fitting the model.

We considered two IWCV variants that differed only in the validation task generator: Random IWCV used random 10-fold CV, whereas Buffered IWCV used the same buffered LOO generator as DWCV and TWCV. This isolates the distinction between task generation and risk estimation: the task generator determines the support of the observed validation-task distribution, whereas importance weighting adjusts the contribution of these tasks to better reflect deployment conditions.

Importance weight distribution was assessed using the same weight diagnostics as for DWCV and TWCV.

S1.1.3
Model implementation details

We employed random forest (RF;
[
2
]
), regression–kriging (RK; also referred to as kriging with external drift;
[
45
]
), and a heteroskedastic extension thereof (HRK) for spatial prediction of continuous responses in the simulation and air-quality case studies.

In the air-quality case study the deterministic trend component in RK-type models was specified via linear models with stepwise variable selection based on the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC).
HRK models were implemented by modeling the conditional residual variance as a function of covariates:
x_{1}
in the simulation study and local population density in the case study.
Semivariograms were specified as exponential models. Semivariograms were fitted to residuals from the trend model using Cressie’s robust semivariogram estimator and iteratively reweighted least squares
[
8
]
. Standardized residuals were used for this in the case of HRK. Kriging and semivariogram analysis were conducted using the
gstat
package
[
26
]
.

RF models were fitted with 500 trees. All other hyperparameters were kept at their default settings as implemented in
ranger

[
47
]
.

S1.3.3
Interpretation

These results confirm that the limitations of unweighted validation under distributional mismatch extend beyond controlled synthetic settings to ecological simulations mimicking real-world scenarios. In particular, they highlight that purely distance-based validation approaches implicitly assume that sampling locations are representative of the deployment domain. When this assumption is violated, for example under preferential sampling, weighting is required to obtain unbiased performance estimates. Overall, buffered TWCV and buffered IWCV showed the most consistent bias reduction across the dataset-shift situations considered here. Sophisticated matching algorithms such as kNNDM appear to be less reliable than the combination of buffered task generators with calibration weighting.
