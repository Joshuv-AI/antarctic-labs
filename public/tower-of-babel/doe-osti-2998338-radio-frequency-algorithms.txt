Abstract

This project supported the design, simulation, analysis, and optimization of robust
algorithms to conﬁgure, optimize, and control Radio Frequency (RF) and Low-Level RF (LLRF)
systems for several modern accelerator systems. In particular, this project improved the
eGiciency and performance of accelerator systems at the Brookhaven National Laboratory
(BNL) and the European Organization for Nuclear Research (CERN) in Geneva, Switzerland.

Objectives

 The PI and supported Cal Poly undergraduate students developed control algorithms
for the High Luminosity Large Hadron Collider (HL-LHC) at CERN, studied LHC injector
upgrades and in particular the Super-Proton Synchrotron (SPS) at CERN, developed
algorithms to reduce gap transients at the Electron-Ion Collider (EIC), and analyzed design
speciﬁcations for the Compact Linear Collider (CLIC) at CERN. All of these projects focus on
designing a new generation of LLRF systems that include more complex feedback algorithms
to both control individual RF stations and provide global systems for simultaneous control
of multiple systems. Such algorithms are a critical component of future accelerators and
light sources. We then studied crab cavities for both the HL-LHC and EIC.

 In addition, the project involved undergraduate students in accelerator physics,
helped them develop research skills, and exposed them to world-renowned laboratory
environments.

SPS

 The SPS project goal was to develop schemes for optimal conﬁguration and operation
of the upgraded SPS LLRF system considering maximum impedance reduction, hardware
limitations, and noise issues. Additionally, we wanted to estimate the reduction of RF station
impedance and transient beam loading eGects in the presence of the updated feedback
systems, thus providing an estimate of the expected beam quality.

 Models of the SPS LLRF system have been developed in MATLAB and Simulink. These
models were validated with measurements at the SPS. Optimal conﬁgurations for maximum
impedance reduction have been determined for the present system. Multiple cavity ﬁlters
were studied.

 In addition, future possible upgrades have been investigated including coupling the
feedback systems of multiple cavities, thus creating a Multiple Input-Multiple Output

(MIMO) control system. To optimize the coupling terms, a Linear-Quadratic Regulator (LQR)
in conjunction with a Kalman ﬁlter was developed and their performance was compared to
more traditional feedback system performance.

 An optimal design was proposed for the SPS LLRF. The performance of this system
with HL-LHC beam intensities was estimated.

 A CERN technical note was published in 2020 presenting the models and algorithms
used to determine the optimal LLRF design [9]. A peer-reviewed article presented an
algorithm to optimally conﬁgure the Feedforward system in the SPS [14]. It also described
models for the Feedback and Feedforward systems, as well as a simulation tool to optimize
the system design using those models. The simulation was used to estimate the
performance of the proposed design.

 The system was deployed, and its performance was presented in a conference paper
[31].

 This work has helped optimize the performance of the SPS, and as such, provide more
protons and higher quality particle beams to the LHC, thus expanding its discovery potential.

EIC

 The US EIC will be built at Brookhaven National Laboratory. The design includes an
electron and an ion ring. Because of the dissimilarity of the two rings, the RF transients
created by the clearing gaps will be very diGerent in the two rings and will impact the
luminosity, will limit the RF power, and will aGect the performance of the RF loops. This
makes the problem of managing the eGects of the gap transients crucial to the operation of
the EIC. One of the project’s objectives was to develop LLRF algorithms to match the gap
transients in the two dissimilar EIC rings, by applying a feed-forward algorithm to the RF
reference. These algorithms should automatically adjust to beam intensity and pattern.

 A collaboration was initially established with Thomas JeGerson National Accelerator
Facility (JLab). A LLRF design was proposed and modeled in Simulink. This model was then
used to study possible mitigations techniques for the asymmetric gap transients in the EIC.
Preliminary results were presented at the EIC collaboration meeting [5], at IPAC 2019 [3], and
NAPAC 2019 [4]. The completed studies were included in the JLEIC pre-CDR and were
published in Physical Review Accelerators and Beams [2].

 Since the DOE selected BNL for the EIC site, the simulations and models were
adapted appropriately to assist with design studies. A collaboration was established with
Brookhaven National Lab (BNL) in 2020. Simulations were performed to estimate transient

beam loading eGects and power limitations. Both the electron and hadron rings (all ﬁve RF
systems) were studied. A Reversed Phasing RF scheme for the electron ring was investigated
as well: some of the fundamental cavities are detuned in opposite ways, so that all cavities
provide acceleration but some of the cavities provide focusing voltage and the rest provide
defocusing voltage. This achieves the power per cavity as the normal scheme but reduces
the detuning of individual cavities and reduces transient beam loading. The results have
been included in the EIC Conceptual Design Report [13], as well as in the EIC Task Force for
RF beam physics and critical component R&D – FY’20 Final Report. They have also been
presented in two talks at the EIC Collaboration Meeting [6] and the APS 2020 meeting [7].
BNL technical notes are being prepared for publication summarizing these results.

 In addition, the collaboration has been expanded to include studies and simulations
of the Crab Cavity LLRF. Of particular importance are RF noise eGects on transverse
emittance, as well as the design of a LLRF system that would not only regulate the
crabbing/uncrabbing stations independently but would also keep the crabbing and
uncrabbing total voltage as close to zero as possible to avoid residual crabbing outside of
the interaction region. A BNL technical note and a conference paper (with Cal Poly
undergraduate students as co-authors) was published in February 2022 presenting
estimated of the transverse emittance growth due to RF noise in the EIC, as well as proposing
a mitigating feedback system and estimating its performance [15, 33]. The speciﬁcations for
the required pickup were presented in [28]. The results were also presented in BNL talks in
May [21] and July 2021 [20]. The crab cavity impedance eGect on transverse instabilities was
studied. A model was developed and estimated presented in a peer-reviewed publication
with a student co-author [24]. A simulation was developed to study the crab cavity beam
interaction. Cal Poly students participated in the model development and were co-authors
in the resulting BNL technical note [26]. The studies have been used to set requirements for
the crab cavity LLRF [32].

 Furthermore, a data-driven technique was developed to characterize the inter-bunch
and intra-bunch beam dynamics in particle accelerators, with an emphasis on the EIC. This
technique was presented at the 10th International Beam Instrumentation Conference [16].

CLIC

 The Compact Linear Collider (CLIC), is a proposed linear collider at CERN. CLIC will
explore the energy frontier. It is the only mature design of a linear accelerator in the multi-TeV
range. It will collide electrons and positrons and it aims to discover physics beyond the
Standard Model.

 The CLIC damping rings need to generate ultra-low emittance bunches to achieve
high luminosity. Therefore, big energy loss wigglers are required, and a high total voltage RF
system is needed to compensate these losses. The resulting strong beam- loading transients
aGect the bunch position and length. In addition, the RMS bunch position has to be regulated
within 0.2 degrees at 2 GHz (280 fs) and the RMS bunch length should be less than 2% along
the bunch train at extraction. These conﬂicting speciﬁcations lead to a challenging design
for the LLRF system.

 Models and simulations of the proposed LLRF architecture were developed and the
system performance was estimated in terms of the rms bunch position and the klystron
power requirements. The completed studies were published in Nuclear Instruments and
Methods in Physics Research Section A [8]. Two talks presented some of the results in
September 2020 [11] and February 2021 [12].

 A novel cavity design with ultra-low R/Q was investigated next and the promising
results were published as a CERN note in December 2021 [17] and presented at a CERN talk
in September 2021 [19].

 Finally, we further optimized the cavity design, studied alternative repetition rates,
and a Reversed Phasing RF system design, like the system proposed for the EIC electron ring.

HL-LHC

 There was also an opportunity to study the eGect of crab cavity RF noise on transverse
emittance in the HL-LHC. The ﬁrst HL-LHC crab cavities were installed in the SPS and the
ﬁrst Machine Development tests were performed. A dedicated feedback system to mitigate
these noise eGects was modeled. The performance improvement with such a system was
estimated and the results published in a CERN note [1]. A Cal Poly undergraduate student
was a co-author to this note. Possible damping eGects due to the bunch interaction with the
transverse impedance were investigated via simulations [10] and additional Machine
Development tests and simulations [18, 22]. We then developed an analytical model for the
optimization and performance estimation of the crab cavity noise feedback [23]. We aslo
studied and published estimates for the Crab Cavity LLRF noise threshold [25]. A simulation
was developed to study the crab cavity beam interaction [29].

Undergraduate Students

 Fourteen undergraduate students were involved in accelerator physics research
through this project. The students gained hands-on experience with simulations, tests, and

measurements of full-scale LLLRF prototypes and actual operational conditions. They
developed academic and scientiﬁc research skills, engaged deeply in scientiﬁc
collaboration and communication, and were active participants in accelerator science
research in exceptional laboratory environments.

 The project-supported students have presented their work at the Cal Poly College of
Science and Mathematics Student Research Conferences. Student have been co-authors
on two CERN technical notes [1, 29]. Students have been co-authors two in peer-reviewed
publications [8, 24]. Students were co-authors on two BNL technical notes [15, 26]. Finally,
students were co-authors in two conference papers [32, 33].

Project Impact

 This project has been instrumental in the development and optimization of the
necessary LLRF for multiple accelerators. As such, it provided signiﬁcant contributions to
the performance and reliability of these machines, and therefore improve accelerator
physics more broadly. This higher performance also translates to higher discovery potential
for these accelerators, and thus an indirect impact on theoretical and particle physics.

 In addition, this project involved undergraduate students in accelerator science
research. The students were guided through literature research, developed scientiﬁc
programming (Matlab, Simulink, Mathematica, Python, and more) and modeling skills,
learned how to create and validate simulations, conducted measurements using network
and spectrum analyzers, and worked on their scientiﬁc communication skills with
colleagues and fellow researchers. These experiences were a realistic introduction to
possible graduate work.
