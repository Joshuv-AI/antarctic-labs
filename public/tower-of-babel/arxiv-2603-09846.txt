Almost-Optimal Upper and Lower Bounds for Clustering in Low Dimensional Euclidean Spaces
Vincent Cohen-Addad, Karthik C. S, David Saulpic, Chris Schwiegelshohn

Abstract
The k k -median and k k -means clustering objectives are classic objectives for modeling clustering in a metric space. Given a set of points in a metric space, the goal of the k k -median (resp. k k -means) problem is to find k k representative points so as to minimize the sum of the distances (resp. sum of squared distances) from each point to its closest representative. Cohen-Addad, Feldmann, and Saulpic [JACM’21] showed how to obtain a ( 1 + ε ) (1+\varepsilon) -factor approximation in low-dimensional Euclidean metric for both the k k -median and k k -means problems in near-linear time 2 ( 1 / ε ) O ⁡ ( d 2 ) ​ n ⋅ polylog ⁡ ( n ) 2^{(1/\varepsilon)^{O(d^{2})}}n\cdot\mathrm{polylog}(n) (where d d is the dimension and n n is the number of input points). We improve this running time to 2 O ~ ​ ( 1 / ε ) d − 1 ⋅ n ⋅ polylog ⁡ ( n ) 2^{\widetilde{O}(1/\varepsilon)^{d-1}}\cdot n\cdot\mathrm{polylog}(n) , and show an almost matching lower bound: under the Gap Exponential Time Hypothesis for 3-SAT, there is no 2 o ⁡ ( 1 / ε d − 1 ) ​ n O ⁡ ( 1 ) 2^{{o}(1/\varepsilon^{d-1})}n^{O(1)} algorithm achieving a ( 1 + ε ) (1+\varepsilon) -approximation for k k -means.

Fine-Grained Complexity.

Despite those hardness results, the importance of Euclidean inputs in statistics and

On the technical side, this algorithm deepens our understanding of the

1.2
Our Techniques

For the ease of presentation, we detail below our upper bound for the
k
-means problem in the Euclidean plane (i.e., with
d=2
). Our input is a set of points
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
, a set of candidate centers
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and the goal is to compute a set of centers
S\in\mathcal{C}^{k}
(
\mathcal{C}^{k}
is the
k
-wise partition product of
\mathcal{C}
) that minimizes the sum of squared distances from points of
P
to their closest center in
S
.

Upper bound.

Our starting point is the quadtree dissection equipped with portals. We briefly recall in the next few paragraphs its construction and main property.

First, find an axis-aligned rectangle that contains all input points. Then, randomly split the rectangle into 4 axis-aligned rectangles of side length reduced by a factor of approximately 2. Continue this splitting recursively until each rectangle contains a single point: this defines a recursive decomposition of the input.
We say that two points are
cut at level
i
when the smallest rectangle containing both points is at the
i^{\text{th}}
level of the decomposition. Assuming that the leaves of the decomposition have diameter 1, the rectangles at the
i^{\text{th}}
level have diameter roughly
2^{i}
.

Now, a set of
portals
is placed regularly along the boundary of each rectangle.
Instead of connecting points to centers via straight lines, we consider only
portal-respecting
paths, defined as follows. To connect two points
p
and
q
cut at level
i
, a portal-respecting path consists of a sequence of segments connecting
p,p_{1},...,p_{i},q_{i},...,q_{1},q
, where
p_{j}
(resp.
q_{j}
) is a portal of the rectangle at level
j
containing
p
(resp.
q
).
Considering only those paths enforces some detours, but a simple dynamic program can compute the best portal-respecting solution, whose complexity naturally depends (exponentially) on the number of portals.
Furthermore, if
1/\rho
many portals are placed along each boundary, it can be computed that a portal-respecting path between two points
p,q
cut at level
i
has length (essentially)
\|p-q\|+\rho 2^{i}
. Indeed, since the boundaries of the rectangles are geometrically decreasing, the detour to go through a portal at level
i
dominates all others; and since the rectangles at this level have diameter
2^{i}
, by regularly placing
1/\rho
many portals, we can ensure that there is a portal at distance at most
\rho 2^{i}
from the point where the straight line path crosses the boundary.
Therefore, the goal of the analysis is to show that, for a small number of portals, a portal-respecting solution with almost optimal cost exists.

The standard argument goes as follows. The main property of the quadtree decomposition is that, for any pair of points
p,q\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, the probability that they are cut at level
i
is
\|p-q\|/2^{i}
. By using
1/\rho
portals, the detour to connect
p
and
q
via portals in case they are cut at level
i
is
\rho 2^{i}
.
Therefore, the expected detour at level
i
is
\|p-q\|/2^{i}\cdot\rho 2^{i}=\rho\|p-q\|
. Since there are
\log n
levels in the decomposition, the expected detour is
\rho\|p-q\|\cdot\log n
, and one has to take
\rho=\varepsilon/\log n
to get a tiny expected detour.

However, having
\log n/\varepsilon
many portals does not yield an efficient algorithm.
More importantly, this analysis does not extend to
k
-means problem: although the expected distance between
p
and
q
is small, the expected
squared
distance is not! Indeed, the expected squared detour at level
i
is
\|p-q\|/2^{i}\cdot(\rho 2^{i})^{2}=\rho^{2}\|p-q\|2^{i}
, and the term
2^{i}
cannot be easily controlled.
To cope with this,
[
13
]
introduced a careful preprocessing of the input, to ensure that no point is cut at a level higher than
\|p-q\|/\varepsilon
. In that case, taking
1/\varepsilon^{2}
portals is enough to ensure a small detour – and also small squared detour, as this is not an average-case argument (unlike the previously seen argument). This preprocessing is essentially the following: start from a constant-factor approximate solution
\mathcal{A}
(which is a set of
k
centers), and for each point
p
, let
\mathcal{A}_{p}
be its distance to the closest center of
\mathcal{A}
.
If the ball
B(p,\mathcal{A}_{p}/\varepsilon)
is cut by the decomposition at a level higher than
\log(\mathcal{A}_{p}/\varepsilon^{2})
, then replace
p
by a copy of its closest center in
\mathcal{A}
. They showed that each point is replaced with probability at most
\varepsilon
, therefore yielding an expected cost increase of
\varepsilon\mathrm{cost}(P,\mathcal{A})=O(\varepsilon)\text{OPT}
.
Furthermore,
[
13
]
showed that placing
1/\varepsilon^{3}
many portals ensured that, for any center of
\mathcal{A}
, either it can be connected to the optimal solution through portals with a small detour, or one can assume that the center is part of the optimal solution. Therefore, after preprocessing, each client can be connected through portals to the optimal solution, and dynamic programming allows to compute a near-optimal solution.

However, this analysis may be seen as “worst-case”, in the sense that it treats all the clients for which the ball is cut at a level below
\log(\mathcal{A}_{p}/\varepsilon^{2})
in the same way.
Our contribution is to mix the average-case analysis with the techniques of
[
13
]
in order to reduce the number of necessary portals.
We carefully define a budget for each point, according to the level where the point is cut from the approximate solution
\mathcal{A}
, as in
[
13
]
, but now also according to the level where it is cut from the optimal solution.
We first show that, with constant probability, this budget is very cheap, namely an
\varepsilon
-fraction of the optimal cost.
We then show that this budget is enough to pay for the preprocessing of the input, and for the detour incurred by making a solution portal-respecting. The algorithm is the same as the one of
[
13
]
: our contribution lies in a much tighter analysis.

This analysis differs from
[
13
]
as the detour they tolerated was independent of the optimal solution: using this additional information allows us to be more precise, and reduce the number of required portals to
(\log(1/\varepsilon)/\varepsilon)^{d-1}
, instead of
1/\varepsilon^{O(d)}
.
Although the global picture remains similar, finding the right balance between a budget (1) small enough to not blow up the cost, and (2) that is still enough to pay for making the optimal solution portal-respecting is a non-trivial extension of their proof.
Indeed, it requires new insights on the way to connect a point
p
that is
badly cut
, namely cut from its optimal center
s
at a level higher than
\log\left(\|p-s\|/\varepsilon\right)
.
For this, we show that our budget allows to connect the point in a portal-respecting way to the center
\widetilde{s}
closest to
\mathcal{A}(p)
, where
\mathcal{A}(p)
is the closest center to
p
in the constant-factor approximate solution
\mathcal{A}
.

Lower bound.

Our lower bound construction builds upon the techniques of de Berg et al.
[
25
]
. Given a 3-SAT formula, they show how to construct a graph embedded into
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that solving Vertex Cover on the embedded graph is equivalent to deciding whether the formula has a valid assignment or not. This graph has
|E|=n^{d/(d-1)}
many edges. We use the (by now standard, see e.g.
[
11
]
) relationship between
k
-means and Vertex Cover, and build a
k
-means instance so that computing its optimal cost allows to determine whether the formula is satisfiable or not.

To cope with approximation, we observe that the framework of
[
25
]
can be readily extended to handle constant gaps in the completeness and soundness cases by relying on Gap-ETH
[
26
,
43
]
instead of ETH (much like in the work of
[
35
]
). Thus, we show that any
(1+\varepsilon)
-factor approximation to
k
-means leads to a vertex cover that covers almost all the edges, leaving behind only
O(\varepsilon|E|)
many edges uncovered.

Thus, starting from a hardness of approximation instance of the Vertex Cover problem which can be embedded on the grid in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where every edge can be thought of as a line-segment, we think of the mid point of these line segments as the points to cluster and the points corresponding to the vertices as the set of candidate centers. A crucial property of this clustering instance embedding is that the only candidate centers close to the midpoints of an edge are the points corresponding to the two vertices that it is incident on. In the completeness case, by picking the points corresponding to the vertex cover we have that every point is close to one of the
k
-centers (in particular the vertex that covers that edge), and in the soundness case one can recover from any
k
centers with a low clustering cost, a vertex cover for the original graph that covers most edges.

Further Related Work

We covered previously the hardness results for
k
-means and
k
-medians; in terms of upper-bound, for general metrics a recent breakthrough from
[
15
]
achieves a
(2+\varepsilon)
-approximation for
k
-median, and a
5.6
-approximation for

-means
[

]
. Those two works improve a long line of works, based on primal-dual techniques and the so-called Lagrangian-multiplier preserving algorithms for Facility Location, see e.g.
[
34
,
33
,
39
]
.

To improve those approximation ratio, one can resort to algorithms parameterized by the number of clusters,
k
: one can get FPT algorithm with approximation matching the lower bound of
[
33
]
, namely
1+2/e
for
k
-median and
1+8/e
for
k
-means
[
16
]
.

Other techniques, specific to Euclidean Space, have been developed in the past decades. Dimension reduction
[
42
]
and coreset
[
22
]
allow to reduce the dimension of the input to
O(\log k/\varepsilon^{-2})
and the number of distinct points to
\widetilde{O}(k\varepsilon^{-O(1)})
, hence leading to simple FPT algorithms based on naive enumeration.
Recent papers improved this simple approach, reducing the dependency in
k
(in the exponent)
[
1
]
.

We covered in the introduction most of the literature related on low-dimensional clustering – which is the study of the problems parameterized by

. To compute a
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximation in time polynomial in

and

, any algorithm must have a running time at least doubly exponential in

[

, with a near-linear running time of

Finally, tractability of clustering has also been studied with different parameters, e.g., the cost by Fomin, Golovach and Simonov
[

, who showed a

-median, where

Preliminaries

2.1
Definitions

We consider the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with the
\ell_{2}
metric:
\dist(p,q):=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{d}(p_{i}-q_{i})^{2}}
.
For any point
p
and
r\geq 0
, the ball centered at
p
of radius
r
is
B(p,r):=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\dist(p,x)\leq r\}
.
The (discrete)
k
-median and
k
-means problems are defined as follows: we are given a set of
clients

P
and of
candidate centers

\mathcal{C}
. A
solution
is any subset of
\mathcal{C}
with size
k
.
The goal is to compute the solution
\mathcal{S}
that minimizes
\mathrm{cost}(P,\mathcal{S}):=\sum_{p\in P}\dist(p,\mathcal{S})^{z}
, with
z=1
for
k
-median and
z=2
for
k
-means.
We say a solution is an
\alpha
-approximation if its cost is at most
\alpha
times the minimal cost.

Remark 2.1
.

We consider in this paper the
discrete
Euclidean clustering. In the continuous version, centers can be placed arbitrarily in the space. A classical result shows how to reduce continuous to discrete, by computing a set of
1/\varepsilon^{d}\cdot|P|
candidate centers that contain a
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximation
[
44
]
.

For some set of points
\mathcal{S}
, and any point
p\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we define
\mathcal{S}(p)=\argmin_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\dist(p,s)
be the closest point in
\mathcal{S}
to
p
; and we let
\mathcal{S}_{p}:=\dist(p,\mathcal{S}(p))
be the distance from
p
to its closest point in
\mathcal{S}
.

We assume that the minimal distance between two points is
1
, and denote
\diam(P)
the maximum distance between two points of
P
. We also assume that
P\subset B(0,\diam(P))
. Using standard arguments for clustering problems, we can assume
\diam(P)=\mathrm{poly}(n)

[
14
]
.

A hierarchical decomposition
\mathcal{D}
is a sequence
\mathcal{B}_{1},\mathcal{B}_{2}...
such that for any
i
,
\mathcal{B}_{i}
is a partition of
P
, and
\mathcal{B}_{i}
is a refinement of
\mathcal{B}_{i+1}
: namely, every part of
\mathcal{B}_{i}
is fully contained in a part of
\mathcal{B}_{i+1}
(note that in this definition, parts are increasing with
i
).

2.2
The Hierarchical Decomposition and Its Properties

Lemma 2.2
.

For any set of points
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and any
\rho>0
,
there is a randomized hierarchical
decomposition
\mathcal{D}
such that the diameter of a part
B\in\mathcal{B}_{i}
is at most

2^{i+1}
,
|\mathcal{D}|\leq\lceil\log(\diam(P))\rceil
, each part
B\in\mathcal{B}_{i}
is refined in at most
2^{O(d)}
parts at level
i-1
, and:

1.

for any
p\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, radius
r
, and level
i
, we have

\Pr[\mathcal{D}\text{ cuts }B(p,r)\text{ at a level }i]\leq dr/2^{i}.

2.

for every part
B\in\mathcal{B}_{i}
of the decomposition, there is a set of at most
1/\rho^{d-1}
many

portals
such that for every points
p\in B,q\notin B
there is a portal
x\in\mathcal{P}_{B}
close to the line connecting
p
to
q
, i.e.,

\|p-x\|+\|x-q\|\leq\|p-q\|+\rho 2^{i+1}
.
Furthermore, any portal of level
i+1
that lies in

We denote

By extension, a solution to

Let

,
\mathcal{D}
be
a hierarchical decomposition of

\mathcal{D}

.

The following lemma bounds the probability of being badly cut, and is a direct consequence of Property

Lemma 2.4

Let

.

Let

3
Structure Theorem and the Approximation Algorithm

3.1
Roadmap

Now that all definitions are in place, we give a more precise high-level overview of the proof of
Theorem

1.2
.
Our goal is to show that the decomposition of
Lemma

2.2
with
\rho=\varepsilon/\log(1/\varepsilon)
is precise enough for a dynamic program to compute a portal-respecting solution that is a
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximation.

To do this, we show that one can find a solution
\mathcal{S}^{*}
such that: (1) it is a
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximation, and (2) the detour from each point to
\mathcal{S}^{*}
is at most the budget of the point. To conclude, it is enough to bound the budget, which is a mere application of
Lemma

2.2
and first-moment analysis.
To find such a solution
\mathcal{S}^{*}
, we start from the optimal solution (which respects (1) but not (2)), and transform it such that each client can be connected through the portals.

First, consider the easy case where a client
p
is not badly cut, and
B(p,3(\mathcal{A}_{p}+\text{OPT}_{p}))
is not badly cut. Then, the detour from
p
to
\text{OPT}_{p}
can be charged to
b_{2}(p,\varepsilon)
, as the closest optimal center to
p
lies in the latter ball.
When this ball is badly cut, instead of serving
p
by its closest center
\text{OPT}(p)
, we serve it by
\text{OPT}(\mathcal{A}(p))
. We show that this can always be charged to the budget of
p
. Indeed, in the sub-case where
\mathcal{A}(p)
itself is not badly cut, we can show that the detour between
p
and
\text{OPT}(\mathcal{A}(p))
is affordable compared to the distance between them; and this distance can be charged to the budget
b_{2}(p,\varepsilon)
. This sub-case is illustrated in
Fig.

1
.
However, in the sub-case where
\mathcal{A}(p)
is badly cut as well, we have no choice but to add the center to
OPT
. This results in a solution with slightly too many centers – in expectation
(1+\varepsilon)k
, since each center is badly cut with probability
\varepsilon
– and we will need to remove some centers of the optimal solution without increasing the cost too much. This step is already presented in
[
13
]
, and we recall it in
Section

3.2
.

Figure 1
:
Illustration of one case of the distinction.
B(p,3(\mathcal{A}_{p}+\text{OPT}_{p}))
is badly cut by the thick orange-dashed line, so
p
cannot be connected via portals to
\text{OPT}(p)
. However,
p
and
\mathcal{A}(p)
are not badly cut, so
p
can be connected to
\text{OPT}(\mathcal{A}(p))
instead, making a detour through the thin orange-dashed line. The cost of this reassignment is charged to
b_{2}(p)
.

This essentially concludes the case where
p
is not badly cut. When it is badly cut, the decomposition
\mathcal{D}
approximates its distances poorly; however, this happens with tiny probability, and we can afford to move
p
to
\mathcal{A}(p)
(charging this movement to
b_{1}(p,\varepsilon)
). A case analysis, similar to the previous one, then shows that the detour between
\mathcal{A}(p)
to
OPT
can be charged to
b_{2}(p,\varepsilon)
and
b_{3}(p,\varepsilon)
.

In the remainder of this section, we show how to remove some centers from the optimal solution (to then add the badly cut centers of
\mathcal{A}
), then prove the structure theorem that shows the existence of a solution satisfying (1) and (2).
We explain in Section
3.4
the dynamic program that relies on this structure theorem and enables the computation of a good approximation, to conclude the proof of
Theorem

1.2
.

3.2
Preparing the Instance

We consider the mapping of the
centers of
OPT
to
\mathcal{A}
defined as follows: for
any
f\in\text{OPT}
, let
\mathcal{A}(f)
denote the center of
\mathcal{A}
that is the closest
to
f
. Recall that for a client
c
,
\mathcal{A}(c)
is the center serving
c
in
\mathcal{A}
.

For any center
\ell
of
\mathcal{A}
, define
\psi(\ell)
to be the set of centers
of
OPT
that are mapped to
\ell
, namely,
\psi(\ell)=\{f\in\text{OPT}\mid\mathcal{A}(f)=\ell\}
.
Define
\mathcal{A}^{1}
to be the set of centers
\ell
of
\mathcal{A}
for
which there exists a unique
f\in\text{OPT}
such that
\mathcal{A}(f)=\ell
, namely

\mathcal{A}^{1}=\{\ell\in\mathcal{A}\mid|\psi(\ell)|=1\}
. Let
\mathcal{A}^{0}=\{\ell\in\mathcal{A}\mid|\psi(\ell)|=0\}
, and
\mathcal{A}^{\geq 2}=\mathcal{A}\setminus(\mathcal{A}^{1}\cup\mathcal{A}^{0})
.
Similarly, define

\text{OPT}^{1}=\{f\in\text{OPT}\mid\mathcal{A}(f)\in\mathcal{A}^{1}\}

and
\text{OPT}^{\geq 2}=\{f\in\text{OPT}\mid\mathcal{A}(f)\in\mathcal{A}^{\geq 2}\}
. Note that
|\text{OPT}^{\geq 2}|=|\mathcal{A}^{0}|+|\mathcal{A}^{\geq 2}|
, since

|\text{OPT}^{1}|=|\mathcal{A}^{1}|
and, w.l.o.g.,
|\text{OPT}|=|\mathcal{A}|=k
.

As shown in
[
13
]
, one can remove some of the centers from
\text{OPT}^{\geq 2}
to obtain the following result:

Lemma 3.1

(See Step 1. and Lemma 4.1 in
[
13
]
)
.

There exists a set
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\text{OPT}^{\geq 2}
such that:

•

|\mathcal{H}|=\lfloor\varepsilon\cdot|\text{OPT}^{\geq 2}|/2\rfloor
,

•

for any
\ell\in\mathcal{A}
, the center in
\psi(\ell)
closest to
\ell
is not in
\mathcal{H}
,

•

let
\text{OPT}^{\prime}=\text{OPT}\setminus\mathcal{H}
:
\text{OPT}^{\prime}
has cost at most
(1+O(\varepsilon))\mathrm{cost}(\text{OPT})+O(\varepsilon)\mathrm{cost}(\mathcal{A})
.

Furthermore, we transform the input set
P
to give it more structure, as follows.
Given a randomized hierarchical decomposition
\mathcal{D}
and a solution
\mathcal{A}
, we define the set
\widetilde{P}
as follows. For every point
p
,

\widetilde{p}=\begin{cases}\mathcal{A}(p)\text{ if $p$ is badly cut w.r.t.\ }\mathcal{D}\text{ and }\mathcal{A}\\
p\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

We note that
\widetilde{P}:=\{\widetilde{p}:p\in P\}
is a random variable that depends on the randomness of
\mathcal{D}
.

3.3
Construction of a Structured Solution

Using
Lemma

3.1
, we can now show the existence of a near-optimal portal-respecting solution.
Let
\mathcal{A}
be a fixed solution, and
\text{OPT}^{\prime}
the solution from
Lemma

3.1
for
P
.
Let
\mathcal{D}
be a randomized hierarchical decomposition, and
B_{\mathcal{D}}
be the set of centers of
\mathcal{A}
that are badly cut w.r.t.
\text{OPT}^{\prime}
, i.e.,
c\in B_{\mathcal{D}}
when the ball
B(c,3\text{OPT}^{\prime}_{c})
is cut at some level greater than
\log(3\text{OPT}^{\prime}_{c})+\tau(\varepsilon,d)
.

Definition 3.2
.

For
\varepsilon>0
, we say that
\mathcal{D}
has
\varepsilon
-
small distortion
if

1.

the total budget w.r.t.
\mathcal{A}
and
\text{OPT}^{\prime}
is bounded:
\sum_{p\in P}b(p,\varepsilon)\leq\varepsilon(\mathrm{cost}(P,\text{OPT})+\mathrm{cost}(P,\mathcal{A}))
.

2.

there exists a solution
\mathcal{S}^{*}
that contains
B_{\mathcal{D}}

with
\sum_{p\in P}\left(\dist(p,\widetilde{p})+\dist(\widetilde{p},\mathcal{S}^{*})\right)^{2}\leq(1+\varepsilon)\mathrm{cost}(P,\text{OPT})+\varepsilon\mathrm{cost}(P,\mathcal{A})
,

3.

for each point
p\in P
, its detour to
\mathcal{S}^{*}
is at most
b(p,\varepsilon)
, namely there is a center
s\in\mathcal{S}^{*}
cut from
\widetilde{p}
at level
i
such that
(\dist(p,\widetilde{p})+\dist(\widetilde{p},s)+\varepsilon 2^{i})^{2}\leq\left(\dist(p,\widetilde{p})+\dist(\widetilde{p},\mathcal{S}^{*})\right)^{2}+b(p,\varepsilon)
.

As explained in the introduction, we will use those properties as follows: a standard dynamic program will compute the best
portal-respecting
solution that minimizes
\widetilde{\mathrm{cost}}(P,\mathcal{S}):=\sum_{p}(\dist(p,\widetilde{p})+\dist(\widetilde{p},\mathcal{S}))^{2}
. Making the solution portal-respecting incurs an error
\varepsilon 2^{i}
for two points cut at level
i
: Property
3
therefore bounds this error by
b(p,\varepsilon)
. Property
1
of
Definition

3.2
ensures that the total budget is bounded, and Property
2
relates
\widetilde{\mathrm{cost}}
to
\mathrm{cost}
.

Our main structural theorem for
k
-means in low dimension is the following:

Theorem 3.3

(Structure Theorem)
.

For any
\varepsilon>0
,
\mathcal{D}
has
\varepsilon
-small-distortion with probability at least
2/3
.

The rest of the paper is dedicated to the proof of this theorem.

3.3.1
Bounding the Budget

Lemma 3.4
.

Fix two solutions
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{S}
, and let
b(p,\varepsilon)
be the budget of
p
w.r.t. solutions
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{S}
. With probability
2/3
(over the randomness of
\mathcal{D}
),
\sum_{p}b(p,\varepsilon)=O(d^{2}\log(1/\varepsilon)\cdot\varepsilon)(\mathrm{cost}(P,\mathcal{A})+\mathrm{cost}(P,\mathcal{S}))

Proof.

We bound the expectation of the budget and then use Markov’s inequality. Recall that any ball
B(x,r)
is cut at level
i
with probability
dr/2^{i}
, and is badly cut with probability
\varepsilon
. Furthermore, when
B(x,r)
is cut at level
i
, then
\deto_{\mathcal{D}}(x,r)=\varepsilon r2^{i}+\varepsilon^{2}2^{2i}
.

To simplify the analysis slightly, we first analyze the expectation of the variable

\beta(x,r):=\begin{cases}\deto_{\mathcal{D}}(x,r)\text{ when }\level(x,r)\leq\log(r)+\tau(\varepsilon,d)\\
0\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

To do so, we distinguish between the cases where
\level(x,r)
is at most
\log(r)
, between
\log(r)
and
\log(r)+\tau(\varepsilon,d)
, and at least
\log(r)+\tau(\varepsilon,d)
. This gives the following upper bound:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\beta(x,r))]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i\leq\log(r)}\deto_{\mathcal{D}}(x,r)+\sum_{i=\log(r)}^{\log(r)+\tau(\varepsilon,d)}\deto_{\mathcal{D}}(x,r)\cdot\Pr[\mathcal{D}\text{ cuts }B(p,r)\text{ at a level }i]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i\leq\log(r)}\left(\varepsilon 2^{i}r+\varepsilon^{2}2^{2i}\right)+\sum_{i=\log(r)}^{\log(r)+\tau(\varepsilon,d)}\left(\varepsilon 2^{i}r+\varepsilon^{2}2^{2i}\right)\cdot\frac{dr}{2^{i}}

\displaystyle\leq 3\varepsilon r^{2}+\sum_{i=\log(r)}^{\log(r)+\tau(\varepsilon,d)}\left(\varepsilon dr^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}2^{i}\cdot dr\right)

\displaystyle\leq 3\varepsilon r^{2}+\tau(\varepsilon,d)\cdot\varepsilon dr^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}\cdot dr\cdot 2^{\log(r)+\tau(\varepsilon,d)}

\displaystyle=O(d^{2}\log(1/\varepsilon))\varepsilon r^{2},

where the last line uses
2^{\tau(\varepsilon,d)}=2^{\log(d)+\log(1/\varepsilon)}=O(d/\varepsilon)
.

Since
b_{1}(p,\varepsilon)=\beta(p,3\mathcal{A}_{p})
and
b_{3}(p,\varepsilon)=\beta(\mathcal{A}(p),3\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{A}(p)})
, we directly get
\mathbb{E}[b_{1}(p,\varepsilon)+b_{3}(p,\varepsilon)]\leq O(d^{2}\log(1/\varepsilon)\varepsilon)(\mathcal{A}_{p}^{2}+\mathcal{S}_{p}^{2})
.
Bounding
b_{2}(p,\varepsilon)
is only slightly more complicated:
b_{2}(p,\varepsilon)=\beta(p,\mathcal{A}_{p}+\mathcal{S}_{p})+X(p,\varepsilon)
, where
X(p,\varepsilon):=36d\mathcal{A}_{p}^{2}+16d\mathcal{S}_{p}^{2}
when
\level(p,3(\mathcal{A}_{p}+\mathcal{S}_{p}))>\log(3(\mathcal{A}_{p}+\mathcal{S}_{p}))+\tau(\varepsilon,d)
.
Therefore, it is enough to compute
\mathbb{E}[X(p,\varepsilon)]
.

Properties of the decomposition in
Lemma

2.2
ensure that
\level(p,3(\mathcal{A}_{p}+\mathcal{S}_{p}))>\log(3(\mathcal{A}_{p}+\mathcal{S}_{p}))+\tau(\varepsilon,d)
with probability at most
\varepsilon
, and therefore
\mathbb{E}[X(p,\varepsilon)]\leq\varepsilon\cdot(36d\mathcal{A}_{p}^{2}+16d\mathcal{S}_{p}^{2}))=O(\varepsilon d(\mathcal{A}_{p}^{2}+\mathcal{S}_{p}^{2}))
.

Therefore,
\mathbb{E}[b_{2}(p,\varepsilon)]=\mathbb{E}[\beta(p,3(\mathcal{A}_{p}+\mathcal{S}_{p})]+\mathbb{E}[X(p,\varepsilon)]\leq O(d^{2}\log(1/\varepsilon)\cdot\varepsilon)(\mathcal{A}_{p}^{2}+\mathcal{S}_{p}^{2})
.

Summing these inequalities for
b_{1},b_{2},b_{3}
, and for all
p\in P
we get
\mathbb{E}[\sum_{p\in P}b(p,\varepsilon)]\leq O(d^{2}\log(1/\varepsilon)\cdot\varepsilon)(\mathrm{cost}(P,\mathcal{A})+\mathrm{cost}(P,\mathcal{S}))
. Markov’s inequality concludes the lemma.
∎

3.3.2
Construction of
\mathcal{S}^{*}

To construct
\mathcal{S}^{*}
, we start from the solution
\text{OPT}^{\prime}
obtained from

and apply the two following steps.

For any
\ell\in\mathcal{A}
, define
f_{\ell}
to be its closest center in

, breaking ties arbitrarily.
We
start with
\mathcal{S}^{*}=\text{OPT}^{\prime}
obtained from

. Note that for every
\ell\in\mathcal{A}^{1}\cup\mathcal{A}^{\geq 2}
, the second property of

ensures that
f_{\ell}
is a center of
\text{OPT}^{\prime}
.
\mathcal{S}^{*}
is then transformed as follows:

•

Step 1.

For each center
\ell\in B_{\mathcal{D}}\setminus\mathcal{A}^{0}
(i.e.,
\ell
is badly cut w.r.t.
\mathcal{D}
and
\text{OPT}^{\prime}
, and

by
\ell

\mathcal{S}^{*}
.

•

Step 2.

Add all centers of
\mathcal{A}^{0}
badly cut w.r.t.
\mathcal{D}
and
\text{OPT}^{\prime}
to
\mathcal{S}^{*}
.

The two key properties of that procedure are the following:

Fact 3.5

(Claim 4.3 and 4.4 in
[
13
]
)
.

With probability
4/5
,
|\mathcal{S}^{*}|\leq k
, so
\mathcal{S}^{*}
is a valid solution, and
\mathrm{cost}(P,\mathcal{S}^{*})\leq(1+\varepsilon)\mathrm{cost}(P,\text{OPT})+\varepsilon\mathrm{cost}(P,\mathcal{A})
.
Furthermore, for any point
p
,
it holds that
\dist(p,\mathcal{S}^{*})\leq 2\dist(p,\text{OPT}^{\prime})+\dist(p,\mathcal{A})
.

In addition, the construction of
\mathcal{S}^{*}
ensures that:

Fact 3.6
.

For any point
p
,

\displaystyle\dist(p,\widetilde{p})+\dist(\widetilde{p},\mathcal{S}^{*})

\displaystyle\leq 3\mathcal{A}_{p}+2\text{OPT}^{\prime}_{p}

\displaystyle\dist(p,\mathcal{S}^{*}(\mathcal{A}(p)))

\displaystyle\leq 3\mathcal{A}_{p}+2\text{OPT}^{\prime}_{p}.

Furthermore, for any center
\ell\in\mathcal{A}
, it holds that
\dist(\ell,\mathcal{S}^{*})\leq 2\dist(\ell,\text{OPT}^{\prime})
.

Proof.

The first inequality follows from the fact that
\dist(\widetilde{p},\mathcal{S}^{*})\leq\dist(\widetilde{p},\mathcal{S}^{*}(p))\leq\dist(p,\widetilde{p})+\dist(p,\mathcal{S}^{*}(p))
and
3.5
.
The second follows similar lines:

\dist(p,\mathcal{S}^{*}(\mathcal{A}(p)))\leq\mathcal{A}_{p}+\dist(\mathcal{A}(p),\mathcal{S}^{*}(\mathcal{A}(p))\leq\mathcal{A}_{p}+\dist(\mathcal{A}(p),\mathcal{S}^{*}(p))\leq 2\mathcal{A}_{p}+\dist(p,\mathcal{S}^{*})\leq 3\mathcal{A}_{p}+2\text{OPT}^{\prime}_{p}
.

For the third inequality, let
\ell
be a center of
\mathcal{A}
, and
\text{OPT}^{\prime}(\ell)
be its closest center in
\text{OPT}^{\prime}
. Then, either
\text{OPT}^{\prime}(\ell)\in\mathcal{S}^{*}
, in which case we are done since
\dist(\ell,\mathcal{S}^{*})=\dist(\ell,\text{OPT}^{\prime})
, or
\text{OPT}^{\prime}(\ell)
was removed in Step 1.
In that case, it was replaced by the center
\ell^{\prime}
that is closest to
\text{OPT}^{\prime}(\ell)
in
\mathcal{A}
: in particular,
\dist(\ell^{\prime},\text{OPT}^{\prime}(\ell))\leq\dist(\ell,\text{OPT}^{\prime}(\ell))
. Therefore, it holds that:

\displaystyle\dist(\ell,\mathcal{S}^{*})

\displaystyle\leq\dist(\ell,\ell^{\prime})\leq\dist(\ell,\text{OPT}^{\prime}(\ell))+\dist(\ell^{\prime},\text{OPT}^{\prime}(\ell))

\displaystyle\leq 2\dist(\ell,\text{OPT}^{\prime})\qed

We can now use this solution
\mathcal{S}^{*}
to show
\mathcal{D}
has
\varepsilon
-small distortion.

3.3.3
Bounding the cost of
\mathcal{S}^{*}

Lemma 3.7
.

\mathcal{S}^{*}
contains
B_{\mathcal{D}}
, and with probability at least
4/5
it holds that

\sum_{p\in P}\left(\dist(p,\widetilde{p})+\dist(\widetilde{p},\mathcal{S}^{*})\right)^{2}\leq(1+\varepsilon)\mathrm{cost}(P,\text{OPT})+\varepsilon\mathrm{cost}(P,\mathcal{A})

Proof.

By construction,
\mathcal{S}^{*}
does contain
B_{\mathcal{D}}
.
To simplify the equations, we write
p\text{ b.c.}
when
p
is badly cut w.r.t.
\mathcal{A}
(in which case
\widetilde{p}=\mathcal{A}(p)
). It holds that

\displaystyle\sum_{p\in P}\left(\dist(p,\widetilde{p})+\dist(\widetilde{p},\mathcal{S}^{*})\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{p\in P}\dist(p,\mathcal{S}^{*})^{2}+\sum_{p\text{ b.c.}}\left(\dist(p,\widetilde{p})+\dist(\widetilde{p},\mathcal{S}^{*})\right)^{2}.

(1)

By
3.5
, the first term is at most
(1+\varepsilon)\mathrm{cost}(P,\text{OPT})+\varepsilon\mathrm{cost}(P,\mathcal{A})

For this, we use

\displaystyle\sum_{p\text{ b.c.}}\left(\dist(p,\widetilde{p})+\dist(\widetilde{p},\mathcal{S}^{*})\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{p\text{ b.c.}}\left(3\mathcal{A}_{p}+2\text{OPT}^{\prime}_{p}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 18\sum_{p\text{ b.c.}}\mathcal{A}_{p}^{2}+{\text{OPT}^{\prime}}_{p}^{2}

Each point
p
is badly cut with probability
\varepsilon

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[18\sum_{p\text{ b.c.}}\mathcal{A}_{p}^{2}+{\text{OPT}^{\prime}}_{p}^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq 18\varepsilon\sum_{p\in P}\mathcal{A}_{p}^{2}+{\text{OPT}^{\prime}}_{p}^{2}

Using Markov’s inequality, it holds with probability

\displaystyle=(1+O(\varepsilon))\mathrm{cost}(P,\text{OPT})+O(\varepsilon)\mathrm{cost}(P,\mathcal{A}).\qed

The Algorithm: how to use the Structure Theorem

Suppose that

Furthermore, the algorithm from

Dynamic Program: proof of
Theorem

3.9

The algorithm used to show
Theorem

3.9
is a quite standard dynamic program (DP), described in detail in
[
13
]
. We give here a brief overview.
For the sake of the DP, we will represent the decomposition
\mathcal{D}
as a binary tree, instead of a
2^{d}
-ary tree.
3
3

3

Technically, in
\mathcal{D}
each cell is decomposed into
2^{d}
children, obtained by cutting it with
d
hyperplanes
H_{1},...,H_{d}
. Instead, we represent the decomposition by first cutting along
H_{1}
, creating two children, themselves cut along
H_{2}
, creating two children for each of them, etc.

A
configuration
consists of the following parameters:

•

a node of the quadtree; let
D
be its diameter,

•

for each portal
p_{i}
of the node, two values
\ell_{i}
and
s_{i}
, multiples of
\varepsilon D
in the range
\left[0,D/\varepsilon+1/\varepsilon\right]
,

•

a boolean flag, indicating whether the closest center to the cell is at distance at least
D/\varepsilon
or not,

•

a target cost
c_{0}
, power of
(1+\varepsilon/\log n)
in the range
\left[\mathrm{cost}(\mathcal{A})/n,(1+\varepsilon)\mathrm{cost}(\mathcal{A})\right]
.

The value
\ell_{i}
encodes to the distance from portal
p_{i}
to the closest center inside the part, and
s_{i}
the distance to the closest center outside of the part.

The DP computes, for each configuration, the smallest number of centers necessary to place in the cell to match the portal parameters and have a solution with
\widetilde{\mathrm{cost}}
at most
c_{0}
.

The first base case of the DP is when the node is a leaf: then, as explained in
[
13
]
, there is a single point from
\widetilde{P}
in the cell, and the two options are either to open a center in the cell to have
\widetilde{\mathrm{cost}}=0
, or to connect to a center outside via a portal-respecting path, whose distance is provided by the values of
s_{i}
.

The other base case of the DP is when the boolean flag indicates that the closest center is at distance more than
D/\varepsilon
, then every point in the cell can be assumed to be at the center of the cell, as if there was a single point. Then, the value of the configuration is computed as for a leaf.

In the non-base case, the recursion is also standard: for a given configuration,
the DP enumerates all possible compatible configurations for the two children of the node, namely: all configurations where
\ell_{i}
and
s_{i}
are the same when the portals are the same, respects the triangle inequality otherwise, and the sum of the two estimated costs is equal to
c_{0}
(up to the rounding).

The proof of correctness can be found in Section 4.1 of
[
13
]
. The complexity is improved, as we have fewer portals: by
Lemma

2.2
with
\rho=\varepsilon/\log(1/\varepsilon)
, there are
(\log(1/\varepsilon)/\varepsilon)^{d-1}
of them. For each portal, there are
1/\varepsilon
choices for the values of
\ell_{i}
and
s_{i}
. Furthermore, there are
O(\log^{2}n)
possible values for
c_{0}
.
Last, there are
O(nd\log n)
nodes in the tree, as each leaf contains at least one point from
\widetilde{P}
, and the depth of the tree is
O(d\log n)
, since
|\mathcal{D}|\leq\log(\diam(P))
,
\diam(P)=\mathrm{poly}(n)
, and the binary tree has
d
times more levels than the decomposition
\mathcal{D}
.
Therefore, the complexity of the DP is
(2/\varepsilon)^{O\left(\log(1/\varepsilon)/\varepsilon)^{d-1}\right)}dn\log^{3}n
.

Fine-Grained Hardness of Approximation in Low Dimensional Spaces

The goal of this section is to prove
Theorem

1.3
, which we restate here for convenience:
See
1.3

Our hardness result is under the Gap-ETH hypothesis:

Definition 4.1

(Gap Exponential Time Hypothesis (Gap-ETH)
[

]
)

There exist constants

such that any algorithm that, given as input a 3-CNF formula

In order to preserve the gap in our reduction, we need to ensure each variable appears in only few clauses. For this, 3-CNF formulas are not enough, but the following lemma shows that one can assume each variable appears in at most

that, given a

m

\phi

Next, the result we use from

is the following. For any (3, 3)-CNF formula

The construction replaces vertices and edges with specific components:

Each variable

•

•

This graph is embedded into the augmented

∎

Furthermore, the graph

Our main result is the following, which relates the cost of a discrete clustering solution to the formula

Proof.

We see the centers of
\mathcal{S}
as covering edges of the graph

\mathcal{S}

We say a part is

We can relate the number of destroyed parts with the number of non-covered edges, in order to link the cost of the clustering solution with a valid assignment. We classify the destroyed parts into three types:

•

Parts destroyed because
\mathcal{S}
places too few vertices on them (deficiency).

•

Type (B):
Parts destroyed because
\mathcal{S}

•

Parts that receive the exact correct number of vertices, but leave at least 1 internal edge uncovered.

Let
D
be the total vertex deficiency across all Type (A) parts, and

be the total surplus of vertices in Type (B) parts. Since exactly

. Let

Every part

be the number of internal edges of a part and

be its optimal local vertex cover size. If a Type (A) part is deficient by

We can evaluate this lower bound by checking
E_{P}
and
k_{P}
for each gadget type. For a variable gadget, which is a cycle of length eight, we have
E_{P}=8
and an optimal cover of
k_{P}=4
, yielding
2d+(8-8)=2d\geq d
uncovered edges. For a clause with three literals, the gadget is a cycle of length nine with an optimal cover of
k_{P}=5
, resulting in
2d+(9-10)=2d-1
uncovered edges. Because a Type (A) part is deficient by definition, we know
d\geq 1
, which guarantees
2d-1\geq d
. This exact same arithmetic applies to the remaining parts: the wire gadgets are simple paths of odd length (where
E_{P}=2t_{w}-1
and
k_{P}=t_{w}
), and the clauses with exactly two literals use an edge as a clause gadget (where
E_{P}=1
and
k_{P}=1
). In both of these cases, the formula again evaluates to
2d-1\geq d
.

Consequently, a Type (A) part missing
d
vertices always leaves at least
d
internal edges completely uncovered. Summing this globally, Type (A) parts contribute at least
D\geq k_{A}
uncovered internal edges. Furthermore, by definition, every Type (C) part does not induce a valid local vertex cover, so it must leave at least
1
internal edge uncovered, contributing at least
k_{C}
uncovered edges overall.

Since internal edges of different parts are mutually disjoint, we can sum them without double counting. The total number of uncovered edges
U
is bounded by:

U\geq D+k_{C}

Since
D=S
, we know
D=\frac{D+S}{2}\geq\frac{k_{A}+k_{B}}{2}
. Substituting this yields:

U\geq\frac{k_{A}+k_{B}}{2}+k_{C}=\frac{k_{A}+k_{B}+2k_{C}}{2}\geq\frac{\delta_{parts}}{2}

Thus, there are at least
\delta_{parts}/2
uncovered edges in
G
.

We say a clause is
bad
if it is destroyed, or one of the variables in the clause is destroyed, or one of its variables/wires is destroyed, or if there is a mismatch between them (which leaves a connecting edge uncovered).

Let
\beta
be the total number of bad clauses. We can partition these into
\beta_{\text{dest}}
clauses that contain at least one destroyed part, and
\beta_{\text{mis}}
clauses that have no destroyed parts but contain a mismatch (so
\beta=\beta_{\text{dest}}+\beta_{\text{mis}}
). Since a single destroyed part contributes to at most 3 bad clauses, the
\beta_{\text{dest}}
clauses correspond to at least
\beta_{\text{dest}}/3
destroyed parts. As seen earlier, this yields at least
\frac{1}{2}(\beta_{\text{dest}}/3)=\beta_{\text{dest}}/6
uncovered internal edges. The
\beta_{\text{mis}}
mismatched clauses have completely valid local parts but there must be at least one uncovered connecting edge per clause (yielding
\geq\beta_{\text{mis}}
uncovered connecting edges). Because internal edges and connecting edges are mutually disjoint, the total number of uncovered edges
U
is at least
\frac{\beta_{\text{dest}}}{6}+\beta_{\text{mis}}\geq\frac{\beta_{\text{dest}}+\beta_{\text{mis}}}{6}=\frac{\beta}{6}
.

By the embedding properties, an uncovered edge has its client (the edge midpoint) at a squared distance of at least
4
from any selected center. For the discrete
k
-means problem, if it were covered locally, the cost would be
1
(for
E_{1}
) or
2
(for
E_{2}
). Since the maximum standard cost of a locally covered edge is
2
, an uncovered edge introduces a strict additive penalty of at least
4-2=2
. Consequently, the total cost of the discrete
k
-means solution is bounded below by
|E_{1}|+2|E_{2}|+2\left(\frac{\beta}{6}\right)=|E_{1}|+2|E_{2}|+\frac{\beta}{3}
.

For the discrete
k
-median problem (which minimizes the sum of standard, un-squared distances), the analysis is entirely analogous. An uncovered edge midpoint is at a distance of at least
\sqrt{4}=2
from any selected center, whereas a locally covered edge has a cost of
1
(for
E_{1}
) or
\sqrt{2}
(for
E_{2}
). Since the maximum standard cost of a locally covered edge is
\sqrt{2}
, an uncovered edge introduces a strict additive penalty of at least
2-\sqrt{2}>0.58
. Consequently, the total cost of the discrete
k
-median solution is bounded below by
|E_{1}|+\sqrt{2}|E_{2}|+0.58\left(\frac{\beta}{6}\right)\geq|E_{1}|+\sqrt{2}|E_{2}|+\frac{\beta}{11}
.

Conversely, we are given a solution
\mathcal{S}
with cost at most
(1+\varepsilon)\cdot\text{OPT}
.
For
k
-means, this implies the extra penalty is bounded by
\frac{\beta}{3}\leq\varepsilon(2|E_{1}|+2|E_{2}|)=2\varepsilon|E|
, simplifying to
\beta\leq 6\varepsilon|E|
.
For
k
-median, the penalty is bounded by
\frac{\beta}{11}\leq\varepsilon(\sqrt{2}|E_{1}|+\sqrt{2}|E_{2}|)=\sqrt{2}\varepsilon|E|
, simplifying to
\beta\leq 11\sqrt{2}\varepsilon|E|
.
In both cases, we can bound the number of bad clauses by
c^{\prime}\varepsilon|E|
for a constant
c^{\prime}>0
(e.g.,
c^{\prime}=6
for
k
-means and
c^{\prime}=16
for
k
-median).

Finally, we can efficiently compute a satisfying assignment for all non-bad clauses. For this, we rely on the result of
[
24
]
, who showed how to decode a valid assignment for
\phi
given a valid vertex cover of
G
. Restricted to the clauses that are not bad, the subgraphs act as a valid local vertex cover, providing a valid assignment for all
m-c^{\prime}\varepsilon|E|
non-bad clauses.
∎

Using
Lemma

4.3
, we can conclude the proof of our
Theorem

1.3
.

Proof.

Let
\delta,\gamma
be the constants from Lemma
4.2
. Note that for a (3,3)-CNF formula, the number of clauses
m
is linearly related to the number of variables
n
(
m=\Theta(n)
).

Assume for the sake of contradiction the existence of an approximation scheme that takes
\varepsilon>0
as input and computes a
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximation to discrete
k
-means (or
k
-median) in time
2^{c(1/\varepsilon)^{d-1}}\mathrm{poly}(N)
, where
N=|V|=\Theta(n^{d/(d-1)})
is the size of the instance, and
c>0
is a sufficiently small constant to be determined to reach a contradiction.

Fix a (3,3)-CNF formula
\phi
with
n
variables and
m
clauses, and build the graph
G
and the corresponding clustering instance as described. We set our approximation parameter
\varepsilon
such that the number of bad clauses
c^{\prime}\varepsilon|E|
is exactly
\frac{\delta}{2}m
. Since
|E|=\Theta(n^{d/(d-1)})
and
m=\Theta(n)
, this dictates that we set:

\varepsilon=\Theta\left(\frac{n}{n^{d/(d-1)}}\right)=\Theta(n^{-1/(d-1)})

Note that this implies
(1/\varepsilon)^{d-1}=\Theta(n)
.

By
Lemma

4.3
, if we run our hypothetical approximation scheme with this specific
\varepsilon
, we can decode in polynomial time an assignment satisfying all clauses that are not bad.
Therefore, the embedding ensures the following: if
\phi
is satisfiable, the optimal discrete
k
-means (or
k
-median) cost is exactly
OPT
. If the scheme returns a solution of cost at most
(1+\varepsilon)\cdot\text{OPT}
, then we recover an assignment satisfying all but
\frac{\delta}{2}m<\delta m
clauses.

The time taken by the scheme to run with this
\varepsilon
is:

2^{c\left(1/\varepsilon\right)^{d-1}}\mathrm{poly}(N)=2^{c\cdot\Theta(n)}\mathrm{poly}(n).

By choosing the constant
c
in the algorithm’s running time to be sufficiently small, this overall time becomes strictly less than
2^{\gamma n}
, contradicting Gap-ETH.
∎

Appendix A
Algorithm for
k
-Median in Low Dimensions

For
k
-median, since distances are not squared, we need to slightly adapt our budget and argument. Given this modified budget, the structure of the algorithm is almost identical; thus, we only present the proof of the structure theorem for
k
-median here.

Definition A.1
.

Let
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\mathcal{D}
be
a hierarchical decomposition of
P
,
\varepsilon>0
,
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{S}
be two
solutions to
k
-median on
P
.

For a ball
B(x,r)
, its
detour
with respect to
B
and
\mathcal{D}
is
\deto_{\mathcal{D}}(x,r)=\varepsilon 2^{\level(x,r)}
.

A point
p\in P
has a
k
-median budget with respect to
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{S}
of
b(p,\varepsilon)=b_{1}(p,\varepsilon)+b_{2}(p,\varepsilon)+b_{3}(p,\varepsilon)
, with

\displaystyle b_{1}(p,\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\deto_{\mathcal{D}}(p,3\mathcal{A}_{p})\text{ if }\level(p,3\mathcal{A}_{p})\leq\log(3\mathcal{A}_{p})+\tau(\varepsilon,d)\\
0\text{ otherwise }\end{cases}

\displaystyle b_{2}(p,\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\deto_{\mathcal{D}}\left(p,3(\mathcal{A}_{p}+\mathcal{S}_{p})\right)\text{ if }\level(p,3(\mathcal{A}_{p}+\mathcal{S}_{p}))\leq\log(3(\mathcal{A}_{p}+\mathcal{S}_{p}))+\tau(\varepsilon,d)\\
3\mathcal{A}_{p}+2\mathcal{S}_{p}\text{ otherwise }\end{cases}

\displaystyle b_{3}(p,\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\deto_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathcal{A}(p),3\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{A}(p)})\text{ if }\level(\mathcal{A}(p),3\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{A}(p)})\leq\log(3\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{A}(p)})+\tau(\varepsilon,d)\\
0\text{ otherwise }\end{cases}

Our goal is to show, similar to
Theorem

3.3
, that
\mathcal{D}
has small distortion for
k
-median, defined as follows:

Definition A.2
.

For
\varepsilon>0
, we say that
\mathcal{D}
has
\varepsilon
-
small distortion
for
k
-median if

1.

the total
k
-median budget w.r.t.
\mathcal{A}
and
\text{OPT}^{\prime}
is bounded:
\sum_{p\in P}b(p,\varepsilon)\leq\varepsilon(\mathrm{cost}(P,\text{OPT})+\mathrm{cost}(P,\mathcal{A}))
.

2.

there exists a solution
\mathcal{S}^{*}
that contains
B_{\mathcal{D}}

with
\sum_{p\in P}\dist(p,\widetilde{p})+\dist(\widetilde{p},\mathcal{S}^{*})\leq(1+\varepsilon)\mathrm{cost}(P,\text{OPT})+\varepsilon\mathrm{cost}(P,\mathcal{A})
,

3.

for each point
p\in P
, its detour to
\mathcal{S}^{*}
is at most
b(p,\varepsilon)
, namely there is a center
s\in\mathcal{S}^{*}
cut from
\widetilde{p}
at level
i
such that
\dist(p,\widetilde{p})+\dist(\widetilde{p},s)+\varepsilon 2^{i}\leq\dist(p,\widetilde{p})+\dist(\widetilde{p},\mathcal{S}^{*})+b(p,\varepsilon)
.

The solution
\mathcal{S}^{*}
is constructed exactly as in
Section

3.3
, with the only difference that
\mathcal{A}
and
\text{OPT}^{\prime}
are now solutions to
k
-median instead of
k
-means.

Bounding the Budget.

As for
k
-means, we first show that the budget is bounded:

Lemma A.3
.

Fix two solutions
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{S}
, and let
b(p,\varepsilon)
be the budget of
p
w.r.t. solutions
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{S}
. With probability
2/3
(over the randomness of
\mathcal{D}
),
\sum_{p}b(p,\varepsilon)=O(d\log(1/\varepsilon)\cdot\varepsilon)(\mathrm{cost}(P,\mathcal{A})+\mathrm{cost}(P,\mathcal{S}))
.

Proof.

We bound the expectation of the budget and then use Markov’s inequality. Recall that any ball
B(x,r)
is cut at level
i
with probability
\sqrt{d}r/2^{i}
, and is badly cut with probability
\varepsilon
. Furthermore, when
B(x,r)
is cut at level
i
, then
\deto_{\mathcal{D}}(x,r)=\varepsilon 2^{i}
.

To simplify the analysis slightly, we first analyze the expectation of the variable

\beta(x,r):=\begin{cases}\deto_{\mathcal{D}}(x,r)\text{ when }\level(x,r)\leq\log(r)+\tau(\varepsilon,d)\\
0\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

To do so, we distinguish between the cases where
\level(x,r)
is at most
\log(r)
, between
\log(r)
and
\log(r)+\tau(\varepsilon,d)
, and at least
\log(r)+\tau(\varepsilon,d)
. This gives the following upper bound:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\beta(x,r))]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i\leq\log(r)}\deto_{\mathcal{D}}(x,r)+\sum_{i=\log(r)}^{\log(r)+\tau(\varepsilon,d)}\deto_{\mathcal{D}}(x,r)\cdot\Pr[\mathcal{D}\text{ cuts }B(p,r)\text{ at a level }i]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i\leq\log(r)}\varepsilon 2^{i}+\sum_{i=\log(r)}^{\log(r)+\tau(\varepsilon,d)}\varepsilon 2^{i}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{d}r}{2^{i}}

\displaystyle\leq 2\varepsilon r+\sum_{i=\log(r)}^{\log(r)+\tau(\varepsilon,d)}\varepsilon\sqrt{d}r

\displaystyle\leq 2\varepsilon r+\tau(\varepsilon,d)\cdot\varepsilon\sqrt{d}r

\displaystyle=O(\sqrt{d}\log(d/\varepsilon))\varepsilon r,

where the last line uses
\tau(\varepsilon,d)=\log(d)/2+\log(1/\varepsilon)
.

Since
b_{1}(p,\varepsilon)=\beta(p,3\mathcal{A}_{p})
and
b_{3}(p,\varepsilon)=\beta(\mathcal{A}(p),3\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{A}(p)})
, we directly get
\mathbb{E}[b_{1}(p,\varepsilon)+b_{3}(p,\varepsilon)]\leq O(\sqrt{d}\log(d/\varepsilon))\varepsilon(\mathcal{A}_{p}+\mathcal{S}_{p})
.

Bounding
b_{2}(p,\varepsilon)
is only slightly more complicated:
b_{2}(p,\varepsilon)=\beta(p,\mathcal{A}_{p}+\mathcal{S}_{p})+X(p,\varepsilon)
, where
X(p,\varepsilon):=3\mathcal{A}_{p}+2\mathcal{S}_{p}
when
\level(p,3(\mathcal{A}_{p}+\mathcal{S}_{p}))>\log(3(\mathcal{A}_{p}+\mathcal{S}_{p}))+\tau(\varepsilon,d)
.
Therefore, it is enough to compute
\mathbb{E}[X(p,\varepsilon)]
.
Properties of the decomposition in
Lemma

2.2
ensure that
\level(p,3(\mathcal{A}_{p}+\mathcal{S}_{p}))>\log(3(\mathcal{A}_{p}+\mathcal{S}_{p}))+\tau(\varepsilon,d)
with probability at most
\varepsilon
, and therefore
\mathbb{E}[X(p,\varepsilon)]\leq\varepsilon\cdot(3\mathcal{A}_{p}+2\mathcal{S}_{p})=O(\varepsilon)(\mathcal{A}_{p}+\mathcal{S}_{p})
.

Therefore,
\mathbb{E}[b_{2}(p,\varepsilon)]=\mathbb{E}[\beta(p,3(\mathcal{A}_{p}+\mathcal{S}_{p})]+\mathbb{E}[X(p,\varepsilon)]\leq O(\varepsilon)(\mathcal{A}_{p}+\mathcal{S}_{p})
.

Summing these inequalities for
b_{1},b_{2},b_{3}
and all
p\in P
, we get
\mathbb{E}[\sum_{p\in P}b(p,\varepsilon)]\leq O(\sqrt{d}\log(d/\varepsilon)\varepsilon)(\mathrm{cost}(P,\mathcal{A})+\mathrm{cost}(P,\mathcal{S}))
. Markov’s inequality concludes the lemma.
∎

Bounding the cost of
\mathcal{S}^{*}
.

Now, exactly as for
k
-means, the solution
\mathcal{S}^{*}
is near-optimal: we can use it to show the following lemma, similar to
Lemma

3.7
. We repeat the proof for completeness.

Lemma A.4
.

\mathcal{S}^{*}
contains
B_{\mathcal{D}}
, and with probability at least
4/5
it holds that

\sum_{p\in P}\dist(p,\widetilde{p})+\dist(\widetilde{p},\mathcal{S}^{*})\leq(1+\varepsilon)\mathrm{cost}(P,\text{OPT})+\varepsilon\mathrm{cost}(P,\mathcal{A})

Proof.

By construction,
\mathcal{S}^{*}
does contain
B_{\mathcal{D}}
.
To simplify the equations, we write
p\text{ b.c.}
when
p
is badly cut w.r.t.
\mathcal{A}
(in which case
\widetilde{p}=\mathcal{A}(p)
). It holds that

\displaystyle\sum_{p\in P}\dist(p,\widetilde{p})+\dist(\widetilde{p},\mathcal{S}^{*})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{p\in P}\dist(p,\mathcal{S}^{*})+\sum_{p\text{ b.c.}}\dist(p,\widetilde{p})+\dist(\widetilde{p},\mathcal{S}^{*}).

(2)

By properties of
\mathcal{S}^{*}
, the first term is at most
(1+\varepsilon)\mathrm{cost}(P,\text{OPT})+\varepsilon\mathrm{cost}(P,\mathcal{A})
: thus, we focus only on the second term.
For this, we use
3.6
:

\displaystyle\sum_{p\text{ b.c.}}\dist(p,\widetilde{p})+\dist(\widetilde{p},\mathcal{S}^{*})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{p\text{ b.c.}}3\mathcal{A}_{p}+2\text{OPT}^{\prime}_{p}

Each point
p
is badly cut with probability
\varepsilon
: therefore, in expectation, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[3\sum_{p\text{ b.c.}}\mathcal{A}_{p}+\text{OPT}^{\prime}_{p}]

\displaystyle\leq 3\varepsilon\sum_{p\in P}\mathcal{A}_{p}+\text{OPT}^{\prime}_{p}\leq 3\varepsilon(\mathrm{cost}(P,\mathcal{A})+\mathrm{cost}(P,\text{OPT}^{\prime}))

Using Markov’s inequality, it holds with probability
4/5
that

\displaystyle\sum_{p\text{ b.c.}}\dist(p,\widetilde{p})+\dist(\widetilde{p},\text{OPT}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leq 3\cdot 5\cdot\varepsilon(\mathrm{cost}(P,\mathcal{A})+\mathrm{cost}(P,\text{OPT}^{\prime})).

Plugging this bound into
Eq.

2
and using
Lemma

3.1
to bound the cost of
\text{OPT}^{\prime}
, we conclude:

\displaystyle\sum_{p\in P}\dist(p,\widetilde{p})+\dist(\widetilde{p},\mathcal{S}^{*})

\displaystyle\leq(1+\varepsilon)\mathrm{cost}(P,\text{OPT})+\varepsilon\mathrm{cost}(P,\mathcal{A})+3\cdot 5\cdot\varepsilon(\mathrm{cost}(P,\mathcal{A})+\mathrm{cost}(P,\text{OPT}^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=(1+O(\varepsilon))\mathrm{cost}(P,\text{OPT})+O(\varepsilon)\mathrm{cost}(P,\mathcal{A}).

∎

Bounding the detour.

As for
k
-means, we now conclude the structure theorem showing that the detour incurred by connecting
\widetilde{P}
to
\mathcal{S}^{*}
through portals of the decomposition
\mathcal{D}
is within the budget defined in
Definition

A.1
. Again, we repeat the proof only for completeness: the case disjunction is slightly simpler, as it does not involve squares.

Lemma A.5
.

Let
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\mathcal{A}
be a solution for
k
-median on
P
and
\mathcal{D}
be a randomized decomposition.
Let
\mathcal{S}^{*}
be the solution from
Lemma

3.7
.

Then, for any
p\in P
, there is a center
s\in\mathcal{S}^{*}
cut from
\widetilde{p}
at level
i
such that
\dist(p,\widetilde{p})+\dist(\widetilde{p},s)+\varepsilon 2^{i}\leq\dist(p,\widetilde{p})+\dist(\widetilde{p},\mathcal{S}^{*})+b(p,\varepsilon)
.

Proof.

We recall that any point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is badly cut w.r.t.
\mathcal{D}
and a set
S
when the ball
B(x,3\dist(x,S))
is cut at level higher than
\log(9\dist(x,S))+\tau(\varepsilon,d)
.
We recall our construction of
\widetilde{P}
and
\mathcal{S}^{*}
: When a point
p
is badly cut w.r.t
\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{A}
,
\widetilde{p}=\mathcal{A}(p)
; when a center
\ell\in\mathcal{A}
is badly cut w.r.t
\mathcal{D}
and
\text{OPT}^{\prime}
,
\ell\in\mathcal{S}^{*}
.

To show the lemma, we make a case distinction, first according to whether
p
is badly cut or not w.r.t.
\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{A}
.
In the case where it is badly cut, it holds that
\widetilde{p}=\mathcal{A}(p)
.

1.

If
\mathcal{A}(p)
is badly cut w.r.t.
\mathcal{D}
and
\text{OPT}^{\prime}
, then
\mathcal{A}(p)\in\mathcal{S}^{*}
: therefore,

\widetilde{p}=\mathcal{A}(p)=\mathcal{S}^{*}(p)
, and
\widetilde{p}
and
\mathcal{S}^{*}(p)
are not cut, e.g.
i=-\infty
. Therefore,
s=\mathcal{A}(p)
satisfies the lemma, with
\dist(p,\widetilde{p})+\dist(\widetilde{p},s)+\varepsilon 2^{i}=\dist(p,\widetilde{p})
.

2.

\mathcal{A}(p)
is not badly cut w.r.t.
\mathcal{D}
and
\text{OPT}^{\prime}
. Let
s=\mathcal{S}^{*}(\mathcal{A}(p))
, and
i
be the level at which
\mathcal{A}(p)
and
s
are cut: we write

\dist(p,\widetilde{p})+\dist(\widetilde{p},s)+\varepsilon 2^{i}=\mathcal{A}_{p}+\dist(\widetilde{p},\mathcal{S}^{*})+\varepsilon 2^{i}
.

By
3.6
,
s\in B(\mathcal{A}(p),2\text{OPT}^{\prime}_{\mathcal{A}(p)})
, and so

i\leq i^{\prime}:=\level\left(\mathcal{A}(p),3\text{OPT}^{\prime}_{\mathcal{A}(p)}\right)
.
Combined with the fact that
\mathcal{A}(p)
is not badly cut, it therefore holds that

\varepsilon 2^{i}\leq\varepsilon 2^{i^{\prime}}\leq b_{3}(p)
.

We now turn to the case where
p
is not badly cut. In that case,
\widetilde{p}=p
: we therefore only need to bound
\dist(p,s)+\varepsilon 2^{i}
.
Furthermore, the ball
B(p,3\mathcal{A}_{p})
is cut at level
\level(p,3\mathcal{A}_{p})\leq\log(3\mathcal{A}_{p})+\tau(\varepsilon,d)
.
We make a case distinction according to whether the ball
B(p,3(\mathcal{A}_{p}+\text{OPT}^{\prime}_{p}))
is badly cut or not. Note that, by
3.5
,
\mathcal{S}^{*}(p)\in B(p,3(\mathcal{A}_{p}+\text{OPT}^{\prime}_{p}))
.

1.

If
B(p,3(\mathcal{A}_{p}+\text{OPT}^{\prime}_{p}))
is not badly cut, then
p
and
s=\mathcal{S}^{*}(p)
are cut at level at most
i=\level(p,3(\mathcal{A}_{p}+\text{OPT}^{\prime}_{p}))
, and
s
satisfies the lemma:
\dist(p,s)+\varepsilon 2^{i}\leq\dist(p,\mathcal{S}^{*})+b_{2}(p)
.

2.

If
B(p,3(\mathcal{A}_{p}+\text{OPT}^{\prime}_{p}))
is badly cut, we show that
s=\mathcal{S}^{*}(\mathcal{A}(p))
satisfies the lemma. In particular,

(a)

if
\mathcal{A}(p)
is badly cut, then
s=\mathcal{A}(p)
; in that case, since
p
is not badly cut,
p
and
s
are cut at level at most
i=\level(p,3\mathcal{A}_{p})
and
\dist(p,s)+\varepsilon 2^{i}\leq\dist(p,\mathcal{S}^{*})+b_{1}(p)
.

(b)

if
\mathcal{A}(p)
is not badly cut, then one can assign
p
to
s=\mathcal{S}^{*}(\mathcal{A}(p))
. The key observation here is that they are cut at level
i=\max\left(\level(p,\mathcal{A}_{p}),\level(\mathcal{A}(p),3\text{OPT}^{\prime}_{\mathcal{A}(p)})\right)
: indeed, from
3.6

s
is in the ball
B(\mathcal{A}(p),3\text{OPT}^{\prime}_{\mathcal{A}(p)})
, therefore
s
and
\mathcal{A}(p)
are in the same cell at level
i
; similarly,
p
and
\mathcal{A}(p)
are at the same cell at level
i
, and, therefore,
p
and
s
are cut at level at most
i
. Additionally, from
3.6
,
p
and
s
satisfy
\dist(p,s)\leq 3\mathcal{A}_{p}+2\text{OPT}^{\prime}_{p}
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\dist(\widetilde{p},s)+\varepsilon 2^{i}

\displaystyle\leq 3\mathcal{A}_{p}+2\text{OPT}^{\prime}_{p}+\varepsilon 2^{i}

Now, since
B(p,3(\mathcal{A}_{p}+\text{OPT}^{\prime}_{p}))
is badly cut,
3\mathcal{A}_{p}+2\text{OPT}^{\prime}_{p}\leq b_{2}(p)
. Furthermore, by the upper bound on
i
we have
\varepsilon 2^{i}\leq b_{1}(p)+b_{3}(p)
, as both
p
and
\mathcal{A}(p)
are not badly cut.
Therefore,
\dist(\widetilde{p},s)+\varepsilon 2^{i}\leq b_{1}(p)+b_{2}(p)+b_{3}(p)
, which concludes the case and the lemma.

∎

k
-median algorithm.

Given this structural result for

-approximate solution.

Prize-collecting

In the prize-collecting

-median and
k
-means, every client
p\in P
is associated with a penalty
\pi(p)
that one can pay instead of connecting
p
to a center. A solution to the problem is a set of
k
centers
\mathcal{S}
, with a set of outliers
O\subseteq P
: the cost of that solution is
\sum_{p\in P\setminus O}\mathrm{cost}(p,\mathcal{S})+\sum_{p\in O}\pi(p)
.

The approach of
[
13
]
is the following: compute a constant-factor approximation
(\mathcal{A}_{S},\mathcal{A}_{O})
, and say a point
p\in\mathcal{A}_{O}
is badly cut if it is badly cut w.r.t.
\mathcal{D}
and
OPT
(i.e., the same definition as for centers of
\mathcal{A}_{S}
). An outlier is badly cut with probability
\varepsilon
. The set of badly cut centers is
B_{\mathcal{D}}\subseteq\mathcal{A}_{S}
, and the set of badly cut outliers is
BO_{\mathcal{D}}\subseteq\mathcal{A}_{0}
.
Now, an instance has
small distortion
when

1.

the total budget w.r.t.
\mathcal{A}
and
\text{OPT}^{\prime}
is bounded:
\sum_{p\in P}b(p,\varepsilon)\leq\varepsilon(\mathrm{cost}(P,\text{OPT})+\mathrm{cost}(P,\mathcal{A}))
.

2.

there exists a solution
(\mathcal{S}^{*},O^{*})
such that
\mathcal{S}^{*}
that contains
B_{\mathcal{D}}
,
O^{*}
contains
BO_{\mathcal{D}}
, and
with

\sum_{p\in P\setminus O^{*}}\left(\dist(p,\widetilde{p})+\dist(\widetilde{p},\mathcal{S}^{*})\right)^{2}\leq(1+\varepsilon)\mathrm{cost}(P,\text{OPT})+\varepsilon\mathrm{cost}(P,\mathcal{A})
,

3.

for each point
p\in P\setminus O^{*}
, its detour to
\mathcal{S}^{*}
is at most
b(p,\varepsilon)
, namely there is a center
s\in\mathcal{S}^{*}
cut from
\widetilde{p}
at level
i
such that
(\dist(p,\widetilde{p})+\dist(\widetilde{p},s)+\varepsilon 2^{i})^{2}\leq\left(\dist(p,\widetilde{p})+\dist(\widetilde{p},\mathcal{S}^{*})\right)^{2}+b(p,\varepsilon)
.

[
13
]
showed how to modify the construction of
\mathcal{S}^{*}
in order to account for outliers: essentially, every time a center of
\text{OPT}^{\prime}
is closed in Step 1 of the construction, all the clients it serves that are also in
\mathcal{A}_{0}
are added to
O^{*}
. Additionally, all badly cut outliers are added to
O^{*}
. Since each of these happens with probability at most
\varepsilon
, the cost of the outliers added this way is at most
\varepsilon\sum_{p\in\mathcal{A}_{O}}\pi(p)=O(\varepsilon)\mathrm{cost}(P,\text{OPT})
.

For the other properties, the proof of the structure theorem
Theorem

3.3
goes through exactly the same way, showing that with probability at least
2/3
the instance has small distortion. The dynamic program of
Section

3.4
can be easily adapted to solve the prize-collecting version of the problems.

B.2
k
-means and
k
-median with Outliers

In this clustering version, we are given an integer
z
, and the cost function ignores the
z
most expensive points. More formally, a solution to the problem is a set of
k
centers
\mathcal{S}
, with a set of
z
outliers
O\subseteq P
: the cost of that solution is
\sum_{p\in P\setminus O}\mathrm{cost}(p,\mathcal{S})
.

[
13
]
showed how to use the ideas from prize-collecting to get a bi-criteria solution, namely a solution with
(1+\varepsilon)z
outliers and cost
(1+\varepsilon)\mathrm{cost}(P,\text{OPT})
. The idea is that the previous argument for prize-collecting adds new outliers with probability only
\varepsilon
: starting with a set of
z
outliers, the number of such added outliers is only
O(\varepsilon z)
. The remaining part of the analysis – bounding detour for the non-outliers – stays unchanged compared to the standard
k
-median and
k
-means: hence, our improved analysis carries over directly to the outlier case.
