Dual sensory impairment and health equity: evidence and gaps from the Australian Eye and Ear Health Survey
Authors: Fan Jiang, Lei Xu, Shanquan Chen
Published: 2026

Dual sensory impairment (DSI) is more disabling than either impairment alone, because the senses can no longer compensate for one another. This has a significant impact on communication, mobility and independence. The Western Pacific region is facing a critical issue regarding sensory health. Several of its countries are ageing faster than almost anywhere else in the world. Yet populations bearing the greatest burden of sensory disease, including indigenous peoples and those with limited access to affordable healthcare, have lacked the necessary epidemiological data. Even for the general population, nationally representative estimates of objectively measured DSI have been scarce, and for Indigenous populations they have been almost entirely absent.1

The Australian Eye and Ear Health Survey (AEEHS) by Gopinath et al. fills this gap, reporting an age-standardized DSI prevalence of 2.5% in adults aged 50 and over, rising steeply to almost 10% in those aged 80 and over.2 DSI was more prevalent among Indigenous adults than non-Indigenous adults, at 5.2% and 2.8% respectively, with the excess prevalence concentrated before the age of 70, reflecting an earlier onset. Conventional sensory screening is aimed at older age groups and relies on individuals coming forward. However, this approach may be too late for these communities. This highlights the need for integrated, culturally safe, community-based services to be provided earlier to Indigenous people who need them.

Notably, the absence of private health insurance accounted for 86% of the DSI burden among Indigenous adults, compared to 34% among non-Indigenous adults. In Australia, access to specialist care and rehabilitation for sensory loss is often delayed in the public sector, carrying substantial out-of-pocket costs. Private insurance, on the other hand, provides faster and more affordable access. As Indigenous Australians are far less likely to hold private insurance, the absence of cover accounts for a far larger share of their burden. Indigenous adults face further barriers: specialist care is often far away; aboriginal community-controlled health organizations are chronically underfunded; and mainstream services often lack cultural safety. These immediate barriers arise from deeper structural causes, such as dispossession, restricted economic participation and exclusion from health systems across generations, as documented by the Lancet–Lowitja Institute Global Collaboration.3 Clinical care aimed at individual risk factors may not reduce inequity of this kind. The higher DSI burden among Indigenous Australians and those without private insurance suggests that sensory services are not being funded or located appropriately, with the groups least served by the health system bearing the brunt of this.

These structural deficits are widespread among the Indigenous peoples in the Western Pacific (Table 1).4, 5, 6, 7, 8 Sensory care depends on the ability to pay for it; specialists tend to cluster in a few urban centers, far from rural and island communities; and Indigenous and community services are chronically underfunded. Much of this loss is avoidable, such as hearing loss caused by preventable childhood ear disease and vision loss due to uncorrected refractive error, cataract, and diabetic retinopathy, yet little of it is caught or treated in time. Reducing this burden requires more than just effective clinic organization. As the burden falls on those least able to pay, publicly funded benefit packages could reduce the financial barrier to care. For the smallest states, pooled procurement of hearing aids, spectacles and intraocular lenses could reduce unit costs. As specialists tend to be concentrated in urban areas, screening and routine management could be transferred to primary care and to Indigenous and Pacific health workers. They could use validated portable and smartphone-based tools, as well as remote specialist support. This would ensure that capacity follows the burden. Existing maternal, child and school programs provide an opportunity to address ear and eye health before loss begins. However, community-governed services depend on ongoing funding, and a shared regional data platform would help to evaluate the effectiveness of these measures and gauge the scale of the burden.

The AEEHS provides Australia with a nationally representative baseline and a replicable model grounded in Indigenous partnerships and community governance. This model could be adapted by Indigenous communities across the Western Pacific to suit their own settings. Three priorities have emerged: the integration of vision and hearing screening into primary care and community services, particularly for Indigenous population due to the earlier onset of these conditions; the addressing of structural barriers in financing and locating care by incorporating sensory care into publicly funded benefit packages and providing services in areas where the burden is greatest; and the building of evidence through longitudinal studies of single-to-dual progression and trials of community-based delivery, supported by a shared regional data platform, which is something that current recommendations still lack. Achieving equitable sensory health will require investment in better measurement and reforming the systems that deliver care.
