Zero-couplings of infinite measures with cyclically monotone support and multivariate regular variation
A.Alexandre Reber, A.Anne Sabourin, J.Johan Segers, C.Cees de Valk

Abstract
We study cyclically monotone transport plans between measures in M 0 ​ ( ℝ d ) \mathrm{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d}) , the class of Borel measures on ℝ d ∖ { 0 } \mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\} that are finite on sets bounded away from the origin but may have infinite total mass. We avoid moment assumptions and allow the transport cost to be infinite. This framework naturally arises for exponent measures in multivariate regular variation and includes other examples such as Lévy measures. We introduce the notion of a zero-coupling and establish existence of cyclically monotone zero-couplings for arbitrary pairs of measures in M 0 ​ ( ℝ d ) \mathrm{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d}) . Under a Hausdorff-dimension condition on the first measure and when at least one of the two measures has infinite mass, we prove uniqueness of the cyclically monotone zero-coupling, yielding an analogue of the Brenier–McCann theorem in this infinite-measure setting. We further derive a representation of such couplings through gradients of closed convex functions and identify conditions under which the zero-coupling is proper in the sense that the second measure is equal to the restriction to the punctured space of the push-forward of the first measure by a cyclically monotone transport map. Finally, we apply these results to regularly varying probability measures. We show that a cyclically monotone coupling between two such distributions admits a tail limit that coincides with the unique proper cyclically monotone zero-coupling between the corresponding exponent measures.

Introduction

1.1
General motivation

We study measure transportation by cyclically monotone mappings between nonnegative Borel measures on Euclidean space with possibly infinite total mass that are finite on complements of neighborhoods of the origin. Such measures arise in the definition of multivariate regular variation; Lévy measures provide another example. Especially in the first setting, which appears naturally in extreme value theory, the usual moment assumptions that are key to classical results in optimal transport theory cannot be taken for granted.

Our goal is to strengthen the theoretical foundation for the use of cyclically monotone mappings in statistics, where such maps are of intrinsic interest—even when they cannot be interpreted as optimal transport plans due to infinite transport costs. A prominent example is the theory of center-outward multivariate quantiles
[
7
,
12
,
18
,
19
]
, which provides a notion of rank statistics for multivariate observations. Our results are intended to lay the groundwork for extensions to multivariate extreme quantile contours. A difference to the existing literature is that a natural reference distribution in an extreme value context is not spherical uniform, but would typically factorize into a product of a reference angular distribution and a heavy-tailed radial component. In another direction, monotone maps and optimal transport play a central role in generative modeling
[
24
,
1
]
. We anticipate that our results may help extend these methods to the generation of synthetic extreme samples.

Our focus is on transport maps and an appropriate notion of coupling between measures in the set
\mathrm{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, the set of Borel measures
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\smallsetminus 0}=\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\left\{0\right\}
that are finite on sets bounded away from the origin but may have infinite total mass
[
22
,
23
]
. The space
\mathrm{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is equipped with a notion of convergence in duality with the set
\mathcal{C}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
of real-valued bounded continuous functions whose support is bounded away from the origin. A typical assumption in multivariate extreme value theory is that observations are drawn from a probability distribution
Q
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
that is regularly varying: there exists a function
b
on the positive reals and a limit measure
\nu\in\mathrm{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that

t\,Q(b(t)\,\cdot\,)\to\nu,\qquad t\to\infty,

(1)

in
\mathrm{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. In practice,
\nu
can be used to approximate the distribution
Q
in regions of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
far from the origin, where few or no observations are available.

1.2
Related work, zero-couplings, proper couplings

Our interest lies in transport maps between measures
\mu,\nu\in\mathrm{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with arbitrarily heavy tails. In such cases, neither the Monge problem for the
L^{2}
cost,

\min_{T:T_{\#}\mu=\nu}\int\|x-T(x)\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x),

(2)

nor the Kantorovich problem,

\min_{\gamma\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\int\|x-y\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\gamma(x,y),

(3)

may admit solutions with finite objective, since
\mu
and
\nu
need not have finite second moments themselves. Here,
T_{\#}\mu
is the push-forward measure of
\mu
induced by
T
, see Section
1.5
below, while
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
denotes the set of coupling measures between
\mu
and
\nu
, i.e., measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
with margins
\mu
and
\nu
. As we will explain below, we will actually use a slightly weakened notion of couplings, called zero-couplings, to deal with the possible singularity at the origin.

The special case where
\mu,\nu
are probability measures with finite second moments falls within the classical framework of optimal transport, for which there are many excellent and extensive textbook introductions
[
4
,
34
,
37
, e.g.]
. If
\mu
places no mass on sets of Hausdorff dimension less than
d-1
, Brenier’s Theorem
[
9
,
10
]
(attributed also to
[
33
]
; see
[
37
]
, Chapter 9, and the bibliographic comments on page 212 on many other seminal papers) guarantees that the Kantorovich problem admits a unique solution
\pi
, which can be written as
\pi=(\operatorname{Id}\times T)_{\#}\mu
, where the map
T
is defined
\mu
-almost everywhere as
T=\nabla\psi
for some convex function
\psi
and which, up to a
\mu
-null set, also solves the Monge problem (
2
). A key property of
T
—or, equivalently, of the support
\operatorname{spt}\pi
of
\pi
—is cyclical monotonicity, the definition of which is recalled in
Eq.

6
below. Conversely, if a coupling measure
\pi
has cyclically monotone support, then it is necessarily optimal in the sense that it solves the Kantorovich problem (
3
), see
[
6
,
14
,
27
,
35
]
. This is why we focus on cyclically monotone transport plans (maps and couplings) even when the expected cost may be infinite. For simplicity, we focus on the notion of cyclical monotonicity with respect to the quadratic cost function
c(x,y)=\|x-y\|^{2}
, deferring the study of general cost functions
c(x,y)
to future research.

Brenier’s result has been extended in two distinct directions:

(i) Positive and potentially infinite Borel measures are considered in several articles, though typically under moment assumptions that allow for the study of the Kantorovich problem. For instance,
[
17
]
introduces a transportation metric between Lévy measures in the context of viscosity equations, a metric that
[
11
]
later uses to construct an index of dependence between random measures. In
[
16
]
, solutions of the Kantorovich problem are linked to gradient flows that solve the heat equation with Dirichlet boundary conditions. A common feature of these references is the presence of a specific subset of the domain—the origin in
[
17
,
11
]
or the boundary in
[
16
]
—which acts as a potential
“sink”
or
“reservoir”
of mass in the transport problem. These works also introduce extended notions of coupling, called
“admissible couplings”
in
[
16
,
17
]
and
“extended couplings”
in
[
11
]
, in which the first and second marginals are required to coincide with
\mu
and
\nu
only on the complement of the reservoir or sink. In our paper, we adopt the terminology
“zero-couplings”
to describe such couplings, reflecting the special role of the origin in
\mathrm{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

(ii)
In
[
25
]
, the condition that second moments exist is relaxed, and existence and uniqueness of a cyclically monotone coupling and the associated transport map are established under assumptions similar to those in
[
9
]
. Let
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
denote the set of probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\Pi_{\mathrm{cm}}(P,Q)
the set of coupling measures of
P,Q\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with cyclically monotone support. The subdifferential of a convex function
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
is denoted by
\partial\psi
(see Section
A.2
for preliminaries).

Theorem 1.1

(McCann,
[
25
]
)
.

Let
P,Q\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, with
P
vanishing on sets of Hausdorff dimension at most
d-1
. The set
\Pi_{\mathrm{cm}}(P,Q)
contains a single element
\pi
. For any closed convex function
\psi
such that
\operatorname{spt}\pi\subset\partial\psi
, the gradient
\nabla\psi
is a transport map,
(\nabla\psi)_{\#}P=Q
. This transport map is unique
P
-a.e.

In this context, the optimality of a transport map is not applicable if the Kantorovich problem has no solutions for which the objective is finite. Still, the focus on cyclical monotonicity is justified by Brenier’s theorem and the earlier cited results on the sufficiency of cyclical monotonicity for optimality in case second moments do exist.

1.3
Problem statement and main results

To our knowledge, the following questions have not been treated in the literature for infinite measures without finite-moment assumptions:

(Q1)

Existence, Uniqueness:
Given two potentially infinite measures
\mu,\nu\in\mathrm{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, is there a (unique) cyclically monotone zero-coupling (see Definition
2.2
) of
\mu,\nu

(Q2)

Transport map:
Given such a zero-coupling
\gamma

\psi
such that
\operatorname{spt}\gamma\subset\partial\psi
, is it necessarily the case that the map
\nabla\psi
pushes
\mu
forward to
\nu
?

(Q3)

Stability:
In case
\mu,\nu
are limit measures of
P,Q
, respectively, in the definition in
Eq.

1

\pi
is a cyclically monotone coupling between
P
and
Q
,
what are the connections between
(P,Q,\pi,\nabla\psi)
and
(\mu,\nu,\gamma,\nabla\bar{\psi})
, where
\nabla\psi
and
\nabla\bar{\psi}
are transport maps from
P
to
Q
and from
\mu
to
\nu
, respectively?

The answer to
(Q1)
, and in particular the uniqueness part, involves substantially more technicality than in
[
25
]
, due to the potentially infinite masses of sets.
A cyclically monotone zero-coupling always exists (Theorem
2.8
) and a sufficient condition for it to be unique is that one of the two measures vanishes on sets of Hausdorff dimension at most

and, moreover, one of the two measures has infinite mass (Theorem
4.1

4.2
).

Regarding
(Q2)
, if
P,Q
in

1.1

Example 1.2
.

Let
\mu,\nu\in\mathrm{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R})
and suppose that
\mu(\mathbb{R}_{>0})=\nu(\mathbb{R}_{<0})=\infty
while
\mu(\mathbb{R}_{<0})=\nu(\mathbb{R}_{>0})=0

\mu

\nu
[see Definition

\infty=\nu(\mathbb{R}_{<0})=\mu(\{z\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}\mid T(z)<0\})\leqslant\mu(\mathbb{R}_{<0})=0.

1.4
Outline of the paper

In Section
2
, zero-marginals and zero-couplings are introduced, and
sequences of zero-couplings and sets containing their supports are studied. The existence of zero-couplings whose supports are cyclically monotone is proved and a representation of such zero-couplings using gradients of closed convex functions is derived.

Proper zero-couplings are the focus of Section
3
. We provide conditions on the supports of the two measures to be coupled for the zero-coupling between them with (cyclically) monotone support to be proper. The latter property is crucial for the representation in Section
2
to yield a push-forward formula for one measure in terms of the other, precluding situations as in the counterexample in Example
1.2
.

In Section
4
, an extension of Brenier’s Theorem to measures in
\mathrm{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with potentially infinite total mass is provided. When at least one of the zero-marginals has infinite mass, conditions are given for the cyclically monotone zero-coupling to be unique and for a cyclically monotone transport map to exist.

Section
5
investigates the connections linking couplings between multivariate regularly varying probability distributions on the one hand and zero-couplings between their limit measures in the definition of regular variation on the other hand. A criterion is given for a cyclically monotone transport map to exist; it only concerns the support of the first zero-marginal.
An extended form of regular variation of the cyclically monotone coupling is proven, together with the other assertions in the commutative diagram in
Fig.

1
. A short discussion in Section
6
concludes the paper.

The proofs of the main results are given at the end of each section. The appendix contains background material on
\mathrm{M}_{0}
-convergence and convex analysis (Appendix
A
), together with auxiliary results on zero-couplings (Appendix
B
).

\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\smallsetminus 0}
, respectively, with

\displaystyle\varphi_{1}\gamma(A)=\gamma(A\times\mathbb{R}^{d})\quad\text{and}\quad\varphi_{2}\gamma(A)=\gamma(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times A)\quad\text{for $A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\smallsetminus 0})$.}

If
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\smallsetminus 0}
is bounded away from
0\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then
A\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times A
are bounded away from
0\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
. It follows that
\gamma\in\mathrm{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
implies
\varphi_{1}\gamma,\varphi_{2}\gamma\in\mathrm{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

A subset
T
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
is
monotone
if

\forall(x_{1},y_{1}),(x_{2},y_{2})\in T:\qquad\langle{x_{1}-x_{2},y_{1}-y_{2}}\rangle\geqslant 0.

(5)

In the following, we need a stronger notion: a set
T\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
is
cyclically monotone
if for every integer
n\geqslant 2
and all
(x_{1},y_{1}),\ldots,(x_{n},y_{n})\in T
with
y_{n+1}:=y_{1}
, we have

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\langle{x_{i},y_{i}}\rangle\geqslant\sum_{i=1}^{n}\langle{x_{i},y_{i+1}}\rangle.

(6)

The case
n=2
in
Eq.

6
leads back to
Eq.

5
, so that a cyclically monotone set is also monotone.

Definition 2.2

(Zero-couplings)
.

Let
\mu,\nu\in\mathrm{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. The set
\Gamma_{0}(\mu,\nu)
of zero-couplings between
\mu
and
\nu
consists of all nonnegative Borel measures
\gamma
on
(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})\setminus\{0\}
such that

\varphi_{1}\gamma=\mu\quad\text{and}\quad\varphi_{2}\gamma=\nu.

Let
\Gamma_{0,\mathrm{m}}(\mu,\nu)
and
\Gamma_{0,\mathrm{cm}}(\mu,\nu)
denote the sets of zero-couplings
\gamma
between
\mu
and
\nu
such that
\operatorname{spt}\gamma
is monotone and cyclically monotone, respectively.

In Definition
2.2
, we implicitly use the property that
\Gamma_{0}(\mu,\nu)\subset\mathrm{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
, which is justified by Lemma
2.4
. Clearly,
\Gamma_{0,\mathrm{cm}}(\mu,\nu)\subset\Gamma_{0,\mathrm{m}}(\mu,\nu)\subset\Gamma_{0}(\mu,\nu)
for
\mu,\nu\in\mathrm{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Remark 2.3
.

Definitions

2.1
and
2.2
are similar to the ones of the usual marginals and couplings of measures.
Indeed, for a measure
\pi
in the set
\mathrm{M}^{+}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
of nonnegative Borel measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the marginals
\phi_{1}\pi,\phi_{2}\pi
are defined like the zero-marginals with the difference that the set
A
is allowed to contain the origin, while the collection of (cyclically monotone) couplings
\Pi_{(\mathrm{cm})}(p,q)\subset\mathrm{M}^{+}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
between
p,q\in\mathrm{M}^{+}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is defined like the one of zero-couplings by substituting
\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}
with
\phi_{1},\phi_{2}
.

The following lemma ensures
\Gamma_{0}(\mu,\nu)\subset\mathrm{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
, so that for any zero-coupling
\gamma
, the expressions
\operatorname{spt}\gamma
,
\tilde{\gamma}
and
[\gamma]
are well-defined.

Lemma 2.4
.

For all
\mu,\nu\in\mathrm{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we have
\Gamma_{0}(\mu,\nu)\subset\mathrm{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

To avoid ambiguities, we indicate the dimension of a ball by a subscript. Let
r>0
. For
0<r^{\prime}\leqslant r/\sqrt{2}
, we have
\mathds{B}_{d}(0,r^{\prime})\times\mathds{B}_{d}(0,r^{\prime})\subset\mathds{B}_{2d}(0,r)
and thus

(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})\setminus\mathds{B}_{2d}(0,r)\subset\left(\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\mathds{B}_{d}(0,r^{\prime})\right)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)\cup\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\mathds{B}_{d}(0,r^{\prime})\right)\right).

For any
\gamma\in\Gamma_{0}(\mu,\nu)
, we have therefore

\gamma\left((\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})\setminus\mathds{B}_{2d}(0,r)\right)\leqslant\mu(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\mathds{B}_{d}(0,r^{\prime}))+\nu(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\mathds{B}_{d}(0,r^{\prime}))<\infty.\qed

We now define a special set of zero-couplings that will be of major interest in the remainder of this paper.

Definition 2.5

(Proper zero-couplings)
.

A zero-coupling
\gamma\in\Gamma_{0}(\mu,\nu)
is called
proper
if

\gamma\left(\left\{0\right\}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\smallsetminus 0}\right)=0.

Let
\Gamma_{0}^{\mathrm{p}}(\mu,\nu)
denote the set of proper zero-couplings and let
\Gamma_{0,\mathrm{m}}^{\mathrm{p}}(\mu,\nu)=\Gamma_{0}^{\mathrm{p}}(\mu,\nu)\cap\Gamma_{0,\mathrm{m}}(\mu,\nu)
and
\Gamma_{0,\mathrm{cm}}^{\mathrm{p}}(\mu,\nu)=\Gamma_{0}^{\mathrm{p}}(\mu,\nu)\cap\Gamma_{0,\mathrm{cm}}(\mu,\nu)
denote the sets of proper zero-couplings with monotone and cyclically monotone support, respectively.

Definition

2.5
is crucial because for applications involving transport maps between measures, only proper zero-couplings are relevant. Indeed, if
\gamma(\{0\}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\smallsetminus 0})
is non-zero, the origin
\{0\}
serves as an additional reservoir of mass when transporting
\mu
onto
\nu
. As shall become clear in Theorem
2.9
, this precludes the construction of a map
T
that pushes
\mu
forward to
\nu
.

Note that ‘being proper’ is a property which concerns equivalence classes of zero-couplings: If the zero-couplings
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
belong to the same equivalence class
[\gamma]
, then
\gamma_{1}
is proper if and only if
\gamma_{2}
is proper. Additional facts about zero-couplings and their supports are collected in Appendix
B
.

2.2
Stability

To deal with transport problems between infinite measures, a common approach is to consider transport problems between finite approximations for which there are already results in the literature. We refer to
Huesmann and Sturm 2013
,
Huesmann 2016
and
Catalano et al. 2021
where the authors are interested in vague convergence and weak* convergence respectively.
This approach is all the more natural in our setting since we aim at studying transport problems between infinite measures in
\mathrm{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
appearing as limits of rescaled probability measures in the definition of regular variation (Section
5
).

Let
\mathcal{C}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
denote the set of bounded and real-valued functions
f
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\smallsetminus 0}
that vanish on a neighbourhood of the origin, i.e., for which there exists
r>0
such that
f(x)=0
whenever
\left\|x\right\|\leqslant r
. The topology on
\mathrm{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is the weakest one for which the evaluation functionals
\mu\mapsto\mu(f)=\int f\,\mathrm{d}\mu
are continuous for all
f\in\mathcal{C}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. The space
\mathrm{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
equipped with this topology is Polish
[
22
, Theorem 2.3]
. For a sequence
(\mu_{n})_{n}
in
\mathrm{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, the convergence
\mu_{n}\raisebox{-0.5pt}{\,\scriptsize$\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{-0.5pt}{\mbox{\tiny$0$}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}$}\,\mu
in
\mathrm{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
as
n\to\infty
is equivalent to the convergence
\mu_{n}(f)\to\mu(f)
as
n\to\infty
for all
f\in\mathcal{C}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. For convergence of measures in
\mathrm{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, a version of the Portmanteau theorem and of Prohorov’s theorem are available in
[
22
]
and are quoted in
Section

A.1
.

Consider two sequences
\mu_{n},\nu_{n}\in\mathrm{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
n\in{\rm Nature}
, converging to
\mu,\nu\in\mathrm{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, respectively, and a sequence of zero-couplings
\gamma_{n}\in\Gamma_{0}(\mu_{n},\nu_{n})
and closed sets

satisfying
\operatorname{spt}\gamma_{n}\subset T_{n}
.
The asymptotic behaviour of
\gamma_{n}
and
T_{n}
is described below in
\mathrm{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
and in the Fell topology, respectively.

Theorem

2.6
is inspired by results from
Segers 2022
, treating weakly converging sequences of probability measures and their couplings.

We follow
[
26
]
for the description and properties of the Fell topology. For an open subset

of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, let
\mathcal{F}(\mathbb{E})
denote the set of all subsets of
\mathbb{E}
which are closed in the trace topology on

.
For a subset

of
\mathbb{E}

\mathcal{F}_{A}=\{F\in\mathcal{F}(\mathbb{E})\mid F\cap A\neq\varnothing\}
and

of sets that respectively hit and miss

.
The Fell hit-and-miss topology is defined by the sub-base consisting of

for all open subsets

of
\mathbb{E}
and
\mathcal{F}^{K}
for all compact subsets

of
\mathbb{E}
.
Let

,
n\in{\rm Nature}
. The sequence
F_{n}

\mathcal{F}
-converges to

as

in the Fell topology—we write

—if for every open

for all large

, and for every compact

for all large

.

We write
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{cm}}
for the collection of closed subsets of

satisfying the cyclical monotonicity property in

and
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{mcm}}
for the collection of maximal cyclically monotone sets, which are the cyclically monotone sets that are not strictly included in any other cyclically monotone set—obviously any maximal cyclically monotone set is closed. Likewise, we write
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{mm}}
for the collection of maximal monotone sets, that is, monotone sets as in

2.3
Existence and representation

When a moment assumption is made on both zero-marginals

Theorem 2.8

3
Proper zero-couplings and representations

The interest in the proper zero-coupling and the proper representation in

\forall B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\smallsetminus 0}):\qquad\nu(B)=(\nabla\psi)_{\#}\mu(B)=\mu\left((\nabla\psi)^{-1}(B)\setminus\left\{0\right\}\right),

is indeed a transport map pushing forward the reference
\mu

[
18

but then for Lévy measures and for certain infinite measures arising in multivariate regular variation, as developed in

below.

.

and
\nu
. First,

\displaystyle=\left(\operatorname{Id}\times\nabla\psi\right)_{\#}\mu\left(\left\{0\right\}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\smallsetminus 0}\right)

\displaystyle=\mu\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\smallsetminus 0}\cap\left(\left\{0\right\}\cap(\nabla\psi)^{-1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\smallsetminus 0})\right)\right)=\mu(\varnothing)=0.

Lemma 3.8

(monotonicity)

(b)

4
Brenier–McCann theorem for zero-couplings

In
McCann 1995
it is proven that, for a probability measure
P
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
vanishing on sets of Hausdorff dimension at most
d-1
and for closed convex functions
\phi
and
\psi
, the identity
\left(\operatorname{Id}\times\nabla\psi\right)_{\#}P=\left(\operatorname{Id}\times\nabla\phi\right)_{\#}P
implies
\nabla\phi=\nabla\psi

P
-a.e.
In our setting, we prove that the same conclusion holds under appropriate assumptions when an improper term is added as in the representation from
Theorem

2.9
.
The proof is inspired by the one of the uniqueness assertion of Main Theorem in
McCann 1995
, which relies in turn on a refinement of arguments from
Aleksandrov 1942
. However, the fact that we are only dealing with zero-couplings and that some quantities may be infinite makes the proof substantially more complicated.
Recall that we write
\operatorname{res}m
for the restriction of a Borel measure
m
on
\mathbb{R}^{k}
to
\mathbb{R}^{k}\setminus\left\{0\right\}
.

Theorem 4.1

(Uniqueness of zero-couplings)
.

Let
\mu,\nu\in\mathrm{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and assume
\mu
vanishes on sets of Hausdorff dimension at most
d-1
. Let
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\in\Gamma_{0,\mathrm{cm}}(\mu,\nu)
and let
\psi_{1},\psi_{2}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\left\{\infty\right\}
be closed convex functions such that
\operatorname{spt}\gamma_{i}\subset\partial\psi_{i}
for
i\in\left\{1,2\right\}
. If
(0,0)\in\partial\psi_{1}\cap\partial\psi_{2}
, then
\nabla\psi_{1}=\nabla\psi_{2}

\mu
-almost everywhere and thus
\gamma_{1}=\gamma_{2}
. In particular, if at least one of
\mu
or
\nu
has infinite mass, then
\Gamma_{0,\mathrm{cm}}(\mu,\nu)
is a singleton.

The consequence
\gamma_{1}=\gamma_{2}
stated in
Theorem

4.1
follows from the representation in
Theorem

2.9
. In the proof of
Theorem

4.1
, we actually show the slightly stronger statement that
\nabla\psi_{1}(x)=\nabla\psi_{2}(x)
for any
x\in\operatorname{spt}\mu
in the domains of both
\nabla\psi_{1}
and
\nabla\psi_{2}
.

In the proof of
Theorem

4.1
, we cannot proceed directly as in the proof of the Main Theorem in
[
25
]
and take any
p\in\operatorname{spt}\mu
such that
\nabla\psi_{1}(p)\neq\nabla\psi_{2}(p)
to obtain a contradiction, since the pathological set

Remark 4.2

In

Lemma 4.8

(Aleksandrov’s Lemma, extended)
.

Let
\phi,\psi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\left\{\infty\right\}

Define

Then

The inclusion

follows immediately from the definitions, since
\nabla\psi(x)=y

\partial\psi(x)=\left\{y\right\}
.

a.
In case

\nabla\psi(p)=0

25

that

b.
In the general case, define

\displaystyle\bar{\phi}(x)

\displaystyle=\phi(x)-\phi(p)-\langle\nabla\psi(p),x-p\rangle,

\displaystyle\bar{\psi}(x)

\displaystyle=\psi(x)-\psi(p)-\langle\nabla\psi(p),x-p\rangle.

Clearly,
\bar{\phi}(p)=\bar{\psi}(p)=0
. For all

we have
\partial\bar{\phi}(x)=\partial\phi(x)-\nabla\psi(p)=\left\{y-\nabla\psi(p)\mid y\in\partial\phi(x)\right\}
, and for all

and
\nabla\bar{\psi}(p)=0

to the functions

\displaystyle\tilde{X}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\left\{x\in\operatorname{dom}\partial\bar{\psi}\mid\partial\bar{\psi}(x)\cap\partial\bar{\phi}(M^{\prime})\neq\varnothing\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{x\in\operatorname{dom}\partial\psi\mid\left(\partial\psi(x)-\nabla\psi(p)\right)\cap\left(\partial\phi(M)-\nabla\psi(p)\right)\neq\varnothing\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{x\in\operatorname{dom}\partial\psi\mid\partial\psi(x)\cap\partial\phi(M)\neq\varnothing\right\}=\tilde{X}.

A similar reasoning yields

.
The desired result then follows.

5
Tails of transport plans between regularly varying distributions

In probability theory, regular variation underpins asymptotic theory for sample maxima and sums
[
8
,
13
,
28
,
29
,
30
]

as it provides a coherent framework to describe the behaviour of functions at infinity and thus for the tails of random variables and vectors. A Borel measurable function
f
defined in a neighbourhood of infinity and taking positive values is regularly varying with index
\tau\in\mathbb{R}
if
f(\lambda r)/f(r)\to\lambda^{\tau}
as
r\to\infty
for all
\lambda\in(0,\infty)
. A random variable
R
is said to have a regularly varying upper tail with index
\alpha\in(0,\infty)
if the function
r\mapsto\operatorname{\mathsf{P}}(R>r)
is regularly varying with index
-\alpha
. The function
t\mapsto b(t)=Q(1-1/t)
, with
Q
the quantile function of
R
, is then regularly varying at infinity with index
1/\alpha
, and we have
t\,\operatorname{\mathsf{P}}[R/b(t)>\lambda]\to\lambda^{-\alpha}
as
t\to\infty
for all
\lambda\in(0,\infty)
. The latter statement means that, in the space
\mathrm{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R})
, we have
t\,\operatorname{\mathsf{P}}[R/b(t)\in\,\cdot\,]\raisebox{-0.5pt}{\,\scriptsize$\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{-0.5pt}{\mbox{\tiny$0$}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}$}\,\nu_{\alpha}
as
t\to\infty
where
\nu_{\alpha}
is concentrated on
(0,\infty)
and is determined by
\nu_{\alpha}((\lambda,\infty))=\lambda^{-\alpha}
for all
\lambda\in(0,\infty)
.

More generally, a probability measure

is regularly varying (see
Hult and Lindskog 2006
,
Lindskog et al. 2014

defined in a neighbourhood of infinity and taking positive values such that

As a consequence, its support is a (closed) multiplicative cone, that is,

6
Discussion

We have studied measure transportation between infinite Borel measures on Euclidean space by cyclically monotone mappings. The measures under consideration are finite on sets bounded away from the origin, and so are their zero-couplings, which have the prescribed margins apart from a possible contribution at the origin. We have shown that a cyclically monotone zero-coupling always exists, and we have identified conditions under which it is unique and can be represented as the restriction to the punctured space of the push-forward of one measure by a cyclically monotone mapping, that is, the gradient of a closed convex function. In addition, we have established stability properties of cyclically monotone zero-couplings with respect to
\mathrm{M}_{0}
-convergence of measures and Fell convergence of subgradients.

The cost function was implicit in our work through the notion of cyclical monotonicity. In contrast to classical optimal transport theory, we did not assume finiteness of transport costs or moments, and no minimization of an expected cost was required. Allowing the measures to have potentially infinite mass leads to new phenomena compared to the case of probability measures. In particular, the possible presence of a sink or infinite reservoir of mass at the origin may preclude representations in terms of push-forward measures. This observation motivated the introduction of the notion of proper zero-couplings and the study of conditions ensuring their existence.

We have further connected these results to the theory of regular variation. Specifically, we have shown how cyclically monotone couplings between regularly varying probability measures converge at high levels to cyclically monotone zero-couplings between their limit measures. In this way, the present work provides a rigorous link between monotone transport for regularly varying distributions and monotone transport for their tail measures, thereby laying a theoretical foundation for the use of transport-based methods in multivariate extreme value analysis.

Several directions for further research remain open. One natural extension is the study of transport problems under cyclical monotonicity with respect to more general cost functions beyond the quadratic cost considered here. Another direction concerns the development of statistical methodology based on cyclically monotone transport for infinite measures, including inference and simulation methods for multivariate extremes and related stochastic models such as Lévy processes.

A.2

In this subsection we recall convex functions and cyclical monotonicity, which are connected to optimal transport.

A function

For any proper, convex function

denote the set containing all such

\left.\begin{array}[]{rcrcr}\operatorname{dom}\nabla f&\subset&\operatorname{int}(\operatorname{dom}\partial f)&=&\operatorname{int}(\operatorname{dom}f),\\

&&\operatorname{cl}(\operatorname{dom}\partial f)&=&\operatorname{cl}(\operatorname{dom}f).\end{array}\right\}

(47)

and
g(x)=\infty
if
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus D
. Then
\phi
is a closed convex function with the following properties:

(a)

\phi\geqslant\psi
;

(b)

\phi(x)=\psi(x)
for
x\in D
;

(c)

\partial\phi(x)=\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\forall z\in D,\,\psi(z)\geqslant\psi(x)+\langle{z-x,v}\rangle\}\supset\partial\psi(x)
for
x\in D
;

(d)

\operatorname{dom}\nabla\phi\subset\operatorname{dom}\partial\psi
;

(e)

D\cap\operatorname{dom}\nabla\phi\subset\operatorname{dom}\nabla\psi
and
\nabla\phi(x)=\nabla\psi(x)
for all
x\in D\cap\operatorname{dom}\nabla\phi
.

Since
\operatorname{epi}\phi=\operatorname{cl}(\operatorname{epi}(\operatorname{conv}g))=\operatorname{cl}(\operatorname{conv}(\operatorname{epi}g))
, the epigraph of
\phi
is closed and convex. Hence
\phi
is a closed and convex function.

(a) As
\operatorname{epi}g=\operatorname{epi}\psi\cap(D\times\mathbb{R})
, we have
\operatorname{epi}\psi\supset\operatorname{epi}g
and thus, since
\operatorname{epi}\psi
is convex and closed,
\operatorname{epi}\psi\supset\operatorname{conv}(\operatorname{epi}g)
and then
\operatorname{epi}\psi\supset\operatorname{cl}(\operatorname{conv}(\operatorname{epi}g))=\operatorname{epi}\phi
. Hence
\psi\leqslant\phi
.

(b) Since
\operatorname{epi}\phi\supset\operatorname{epi}g
, we must have
\phi(x)\leqslant g(x)=\psi(x)
for
x\in D
, and thus
\phi(x)=\psi(x)
for such
x
.

(c) Let
x\in D
and write
V=\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\forall z\in D,\,\psi(z)\geqslant\psi(x)+\langle{z-x,v}\rangle\}
.
By definition of the subdifferential,
V\supset\partial\psi(x)
. Likewise, in view of (b), also
V\supset\partial\phi(x)
. We need to show that
V\subset\partial\phi(x)
. Let
v\in V
. By definition,

\operatorname{epi}g=\operatorname{epi}\psi\cap(D\times\mathbb{R})\subset\{(z,\lambda)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}:\lambda\geqslant\psi(x)+\langle{z-x,v}\rangle\}.

The set on the right-hand side is a closed half-space in
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
. Therefore, it also contains

\operatorname{cl}(\operatorname{conv}(\operatorname{epi}g))=\operatorname{epi}\phi
.
But that means that
\phi(z)\geqslant\psi(x)+\langle{z-x,v}\rangle
for all
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. As
\psi(x)=\phi(x)
, we find that
v\in\partial\phi(x)
.
Therefore,
V\subset\partial\phi(x)
as required.

(d) From
\psi\leqslant\phi
we infer
\operatorname{dom}\phi\subset\operatorname{dom}\psi
.

Therefore, by (
47
),

\operatorname{dom}\nabla\phi\subset\operatorname{int}(\operatorname{dom}\phi)\subset\operatorname{int}(\operatorname{dom}\psi)=\operatorname{dom}\partial\psi.

(e) Let
x\in D\cap\operatorname{dom}\nabla\phi
and write
\nabla\phi(x)=y
. On the one hand, by (d),
x\in\operatorname{dom}\partial\psi
and thus
\partial\psi(x)\neq\varnothing
. On the other hand, by (c),
\partial\psi(x)\subset\partial\phi(x)=\{y\}
. We conclude that
\partial\psi(x)=\{y\}
, so that
x\in\operatorname{dom}\nabla\psi
and
\nabla\psi(x)=y=\nabla\phi(x)
.
∎

B
Zero-couplings and their support

This section relies heavily on the work in
Segers 2022
on the convergence of sequences of couplings
\pi_{n}\in\Pi(P_{n},Q_{n})
where
P_{n},Q_{n}
are sequences of probability measures on

satisfying
\operatorname{spt}\pi_{n}\subset T_{n}
.
Although being formulated for probability measures, most of the results in
[

]
are in fact given with a demonstration that extends straightforwardly to
\mathrm{M}_{0}

Indeed, thanks to Theorem 2.7 (Prohorov) and Theorem 2.4 (Portmanteau) for
\mathrm{M}_{0}
(which are quite different from the ones in the set of probability measures, see Section

) in
[
22
]
, most of the arguments can be reused almost directly. In what follows, we adapt the results given in
[
36
]
to our context.

Lemma B.1
.

The maps
\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}
in
Definition

2.1
are continuous as functions
\mathrm{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathrm{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

We only treat
\varphi_{1}
, as
\varphi_{2}
can be treated in exactly the same way.
It suffices to check that
\gamma_{n}\raisebox{-0.5pt}{\,\scriptsize$\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{-0.5pt}{\mbox{\tiny$0$}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}$}\,\gamma
in
\mathrm{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
implies
\varphi_{1}\gamma_{n}\raisebox{-0.5pt}{\,\scriptsize$\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{-0.5pt}{\mbox{\tiny$0$}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}$}\,\varphi_{1}\gamma
in
\mathrm{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
as
n\to\infty
.
To do so, we consider the Portmanteau Theorem 2.1(iii) in
Lindskog et al. 2014
(see
Theorem

A.1
) and apply it twice.
Let
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\smallsetminus 0}
be bounded away from the origin. Then
\operatorname{int}(A)
,
\operatorname{int}(A)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\operatorname{cl}(A)
,
\operatorname{cl}(A)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
are open and closeds set, respectively, and bounded away from the origin in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
or
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Since
\gamma_{n}\raisebox{-0.5pt}{\,\scriptsize$\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{-0.5pt}{\mbox{\tiny$0$}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}$}\,\gamma
as
n\to\infty
in
\mathrm{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we have

\gamma\bigl(\operatorname{int}(A)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\bigr)\leqslant\liminf_{n\to\infty}\gamma_{n}\bigl(\operatorname{int}(A)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\bigr)\leqslant\limsup_{n\to\infty}\gamma_{n}\bigl(\operatorname{cl}(A)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\bigr)\leqslant\gamma\bigl(\operatorname{cl}(A)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\bigr),

which we can rewrite as

(\varphi_{1}\gamma)(\operatorname{int}(A))\leqslant\liminf_{n\to\infty}(\varphi_{1}\gamma_{n})(\operatorname{int}(A))\leqslant\limsup_{n\to\infty}(\varphi_{1}\gamma_{n})(\operatorname{cl}(A))\leqslant(\varphi_{1}\gamma)(\operatorname{cl}(A)).

It follows that
\varphi_{1}\gamma_{n}\raisebox{-0.5pt}{\,\scriptsize$\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{-0.5pt}{\mbox{\tiny$0$}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}$}\,\varphi_{1}\gamma
as
n\to\infty
, as required.
∎

For our purpose, the main consequence of
Lemma

B.2
below is that if a sequence
(\gamma_{n},T_{n})\in\Gamma_{0}(\mu_{n},\nu_{n})\times\mathcal{F}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
satisfying
\operatorname{spt}\gamma_{n}\subset T_{n}
converges in the product topology to some

, then the limit pair satisfies
\operatorname{spt}\gamma\subset T
.
It is adapted from Lemma D.11 (Lower semi-continuity of the support map) in
[
36
]
. The
\liminf
of a sequence
F_{n}
in
\mathcal{F}(\mathbb{E})
is defined as the set of points
x\in\mathbb{E}
such that there exist
x_{n}\in F_{n}
such that
x_{n}\to x
in
\mathbb{E}
; the set
\liminf_{n}F_{n}
is necessarily closed in
\mathbb{E}
too.
We recall that a map
f
from a topological space
\mathbb{D}
into
\mathcal{F}(\mathbb{E})
is lower semi-continuous if
f^{-1}(\mathcal{F}_{G})
is open for any open set
G
in
\mathbb{E}

\mathcal{F}_{G}=\left\{F\in\mathcal{F}(\mathbb{E}):F\cap G\neq\varnothing\right\}
; which, in the case where the space
\mathbb{D}
is metrizable with metric
\rho
and
\mathbb{E}
is a locally compact Hausdorff topological space (LCHS), is equivalent to
f(d)\subset\liminf_{n}f(d_{n})
whenever
d_{n}\to d
in
\mathbb{D}

[
36
, lemma D.8]
.

Lemma B.2

(Lower semi-continuity of the support map)
.

Let
\mathbb{E}
be a non-empty open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{k}
for some
k\geqslant 1
.
If
\mu_{n}\raisebox{-0.5pt}{\,\scriptsize$\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{-0.5pt}{\mbox{\tiny$0$}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}$}\,\mu
in
\mathrm{M}_{0}(\mathbb{E})
—seen as a subset of
\mathrm{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
—as
n\to\infty
, then
\operatorname{spt}\mu\subset\liminf_{n\to\infty}\operatorname{spt}\mu_{n}
, that is, the map

\operatorname{spt}:\mathrm{M}_{0}(\mathbb{E})\to\mathcal{F}(\mathbb{E}):\mu\mapsto\operatorname{spt}\mu

is lower semi-continuous. In particular,
\left\{(\mu,F)\in\mathrm{M}_{0}(\mathbb{E})\times\mathcal{F}(\mathbb{E}):\operatorname{spt}\mu\subset F\right\}
is closed in the product topology.

The proof is adapted from the one of Lemma D.11 in
[
36
]
, with minor changes to deal with the
\mathrm{M}_{0}
space.
Let
G\subset\mathbb{E}
be open and suppose that
\operatorname{spt}\mu\cap G\neq\varnothing
. By Lemma D.8(ii) in
[
36
]
we need to show that
\operatorname{spt}(\mu_{n})\cap G\neq\varnothing
for all large
n
. For
\varepsilon>0
small enough, the set
G_{\varepsilon}=G\setminus\operatorname{cl}\mathds{B}_{0,\varepsilon}
is a non-empty open set bounded away from
0
.
Since
\mu\in\mathrm{M}_{0}(\mathbb{E})\subset\mathrm{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
we have
\operatorname{spt}\mu\neq\{0\}
as
\operatorname{spt}\mu
is by definition equal to
\operatorname{spt}\tilde{\mu}
and
\tilde{\mu}(\{0\})=0
, so, decreasing
\varepsilon
if needed, we can also suppose
G_{\varepsilon}\cap\operatorname{spt}\mu\neq\varnothing
.
It follows from the Portmanteau lemma for
\mathrm{M}_{0}
-convergence (
Theorem

A.1
, see Theorem 2.1 in
[
23
]
) that

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mu_{n}(G)\geqslant\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mu_{n}(G_{\varepsilon})\geqslant\mu(G_{\varepsilon})>0.

Since
\operatorname{spt}(\mu_{(n)})\cap G\neq\varnothing
if and only if
\mu_{(n)}(G)>0
(since
G
is open), the first part of the Lemma is proved.
The set
\{(\mu,F):\operatorname{spt}\mu\subset F\}
is closed in view of Lemma D.8(iv) in
[
36
]
since
\mathbb{E}
is a LCHS space.
∎

The following result is adapted from Lemma 4.1 in
[
36
]
and will be useful when dealing with converging sequences. Let
\mathrm{M}_{0,cm}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})

Lemma B.3

(Closure and compactness properties of sets of zero-coupling measures)
.

(a)

For
\mu,\nu\in\mathrm{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, the sets
\mathrm{M}_{0,cm}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\Gamma_{0,\mathrm{cm}}(\mu,\nu)
are closed in
\mathrm{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

(b)

The sets

\displaystyle\left\{(\mu,\nu,\gamma)\mid\mu,\nu\in\mathrm{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\gamma\in\Gamma_{0}(\mu,\nu)\right\}\qquad\text{and}

\displaystyle\left\{(\mu,\nu,\gamma)\mid\mu,\nu\in\mathrm{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\gamma\in\Gamma_{0,\mathrm{cm}}(\mu,\nu)\right\}

are closed in
\mathrm{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times\mathrm{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times\mathrm{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

(c)

If
K,L\subset\mathrm{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
are compact, then

\left\{(\mu,\nu,\gamma)\mid\mu\in K,\nu\in L,\gamma\in\Gamma_{0}(\mu,\nu)\right\}

(50)

is compact too.

We deal with the different assertions separately.

Proof of (a).

As
\Gamma_{0,\mathrm{cm}}(\mu,\nu)=\Gamma_{0}(\mu,\nu)\cap\mathrm{M}_{0,cm}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
, it suffices to show that
\Gamma_{0}(\mu,\nu)
and
\mathrm{M}_{0,cm}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
are closed.

According to
Lemma

B.1
, the maps
\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}:\mathrm{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathrm{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
sending a measure in
\mathrm{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
to its zero-marginals are continuous. As a consequence,
\Gamma_{0}(\mu,\nu)=\varphi_{1}^{-1}(\left\{\mu\right\})\cap\varphi_{2}^{-1}(\left\{\nu\right\})
is closed.

Next, we have
\mathrm{M}_{0,cm}=\operatorname{spt}^{-1}(\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{cm}})
where the map

\operatorname{spt}:\mathrm{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathcal{F}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
sends a measure to its
support. The set
\operatorname{spt}^{-1}(\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{cm}})
is closed by lower semi-continuity of
\operatorname{spt}

(Lemma
B.2
) and the fact that the complement of
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{cm}}

can be written as a union over finite intersections of sets of the
form
\mathcal{F}_{G}=\{F\in\mathcal{F}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})\mid F\cap G\neq\varnothing\}
for open
G\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}

[
36
, Lemma 3.2]
.

Proof of (b).

If
\gamma_{n}\in\Gamma_{0}(\mu_{n},\nu_{n})
for all
n
and if
\mu_{n}\raisebox{-0.5pt}{\,\scriptsize$\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{-0.5pt}{\mbox{\tiny$0$}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}$}\,\mu
,
\nu_{n}\raisebox{-0.5pt}{\,\scriptsize$\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{-0.5pt}{\mbox{\tiny$0$}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}$}\,\nu
and
\gamma_{n}\raisebox{-0.5pt}{\,\scriptsize$\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{-0.5pt}{\mbox{\tiny$0$}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}$}\,\gamma
, then also
\gamma\in\Gamma_{0}(\mu,\nu)
using arguments similar to those used for (a) and relying on the continuity of
\varphi_{i}
. It follows that
\left\{(\mu,\nu,\gamma)\mid\mu,\nu\in\mathrm{M}_{0},\gamma\in\Gamma_{0}(\mu,\nu)\right\}
is closed in
\mathrm{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times\mathrm{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times\mathrm{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
. The intersection of the former set with the closed set
\mathrm{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times\mathrm{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times\mathrm{M}_{0,cm}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
is the set

\left\{(\mu,\nu,\gamma)\mid\mu,\nu\in\mathrm{M}_{0},\gamma\in\Gamma_{0,\mathrm{cm}}(\mu,\nu)\right\},

which is thus closed as well.

Proof of (c).

Let
M=\bigcup_{\mu\in K,\nu\in L}\Gamma_{0}(\mu,\nu)
.
The set

\left\{(\mu,\nu,\gamma)\mid\mu\in K,\nu\in L,\gamma\in\Gamma_{0}(\mu,\nu)\right\}=\left\{(\mu,\nu,\gamma)\mid\gamma\in\Gamma_{0}(\mu,\nu)\right\}\cap\bigl(K\times L\times\mathrm{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})\bigr)

is closed by (b) and contained in
K\times L\times M
. Since
K,L
are compact subsets of
\mathrm{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, it suffices to prove that
M
is relatively compact in
\mathrm{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
to conclude that
K\times L\times M
is relatively compact, yielding the desired compactness of the set of interest.

Let
\epsilon>0
. Since
K
and
L
are compact in
\mathrm{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, the Prohorov-like characterization of relative compactness in
\mathrm{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
stated in
Theorem

A.2

[
22
, Theorem 2.7]
yields the existence of two positive sequences
(r_{i}^{K})_{,i\geqslant 1}
and
(r_{i}^{L})_{i\geqslant 1}
decreasing to zero, so that for each
i\geqslant 1
there exist compact sets
C_{i}^{K}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\mathds{B}(0,r_{i}^{K})
and
C_{i}^{L}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\mathds{B}(0,r_{i}^{L})
such that

\displaystyle\sup_{\mu\in K}\mu\bigl(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\mathds{B}(0,r_{i}^{K})\bigr)

\displaystyle<\infty,

\displaystyle\sup_{\mu\in K}\mu\bigl(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus(\mathds{B}(0,r_{i}^{K})\cup C_{i}^{K})\bigr)

\displaystyle\leqslant\epsilon/2,

\displaystyle\sup_{\nu\in L}\nu\bigl(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\mathds{B}(0,r_{i}^{L})\bigr)

\displaystyle<\infty,

\displaystyle\sup_{\nu\in L}\nu\bigl(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus(\mathds{B}(0,r_{i}^{L})\cup C_{i}^{L})\bigr)

\displaystyle\leqslant\epsilon/2.

It is easy to see that we can choose common sequences of numbers and sets by setting

\displaystyle r_{i}

\displaystyle=\max(r_{i}^{K},r_{i}^{L}),

\displaystyle C_{i}

\displaystyle=(C_{i}^{K}\cup C_{i}^{L})\setminus\mathds{B}(0,r_{i}),

to satisfy the above inequalities for
\mu
and
\nu
simultaneously.

For the Euclidean norm,
\left\|(x,y)\right\|\geqslant\sqrt{2}r
implies
\max(\left\|x\right\|,\left\|y\right\|)\geqslant r
, where
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
and

. As a consequence, we have, indicating the dimensions of the balls by a subscript,

\mathbb{R}^{2d}\setminus\mathds{B}_{2d}(0,\sqrt{2}r_{i})\subset\bigl((\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\mathds{B}_{d}(0,r_{i}))\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\bigr)\cup\bigl(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\mathds{B}_{d}(0,r_{i}))\bigr),

and thus, for every

,

\sup_{\gamma\in M}\gamma\bigl(\mathbb{R}^{2d}\setminus\mathds{B}_{2d}(0,\sqrt{2}r_{i})\bigr)\leqslant\sup_{\mu\in K}\mu\bigl(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\mathds{B}_{d}(0,r_{i})\bigr)+\sup_{\nu\in L}\nu\bigl(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\mathds{B}_{d}(0,r_{i})\bigr)<\infty.

Finally, define the compact set

K_{i}=\bigl(C_{i}\cup\operatorname{cl}\mathds{B}_{d}(0,r_{i})\bigr)^{2}\setminus\mathds{B}_{2d}(0,\sqrt{2}r_{i}),

which is obviously contained in
\mathbb{R}^{2d}\setminus\mathds{B}_{2d}(0,\sqrt{2}r_{i})
.
For all
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
, we have

\displaystyle(x,y)\notin K_{i}\cup\mathds{B}_{2d}(0,\sqrt{2}r_{i})

\displaystyle\implies(x,y)\notin\bigl(C_{i}\cup\operatorname{cl}\mathds{B}_{d}(0,r_{i})\bigr)^{2}

\displaystyle\implies x\notin C_{i}\cup\operatorname{cl}\mathds{B}_{d}(0,r_{i})\text{ or }y\notin C_{i}\cup\operatorname{cl}\mathds{B}_{d}(0,r_{i})

\displaystyle\implies x\notin C_{i}\cup\mathds{B}_{d}(0,r_{i})\text{ or }y\notin C_{i}\cup\mathds{B}_{d}(0,r_{i}),

whence, in
\mathbb{R}^{2d}
,

[K_{i}\cup\mathds{B}_{2d}(0,\sqrt{2}r_{i})]^{c}\subset[(C_{i}\cup\mathds{B}_{d}(0,r_{i}))^{c}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}]\cup[\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(C_{i}\cup\mathds{B}_{d}(0,r_{i}))^{c}].

It follows that

\displaystyle\sup_{\gamma\in M}\gamma\bigl([K_{i}\cup\mathds{B}_{2d}(0,\sqrt{2}r_{i})]^{c}\bigr)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sup_{\mu\in K}\mu\bigl([C_{i}\cup\mathds{B}_{d}(0,r_{i})]^{c}\bigr)+\sup_{\nu\in L}\nu\bigl([C_{i}\cup\mathds{B}_{d}(0,r_{i})]^{c}\bigr)

\displaystyle\leqslant\varepsilon/2+\varepsilon/2=\varepsilon.

Since
\varepsilon>0
was arbitrary, we have shown that the set
M
satisfies the two criteria in
Theorem

A.2
, confirming that
M
is relatively compact, as required.
∎

The following lemma is similar to Lemma C.1 in
[
36
]
which concerns probability measures. Recall that
\varphi_{1}\gamma
and
\varphi_{2}\gamma
are the zero-marginals of
\gamma\in\mathrm{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
while
\phi_{1}\gamma
and
\phi_{2}\gamma
are its ordinary marginals (
Definition

2.2
and
Remark

2.3
). Let
\operatorname{proj}_{1}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be the projection mapping
(x,y)\mapsto x
. Seeing a subset
T
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
as (the graph of) a multivalued mapping from
\mathbb{R}^{d}
into
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we also write
\operatorname{dom}T=\operatorname{proj}_{1}(T)=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mid\exists y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:(x,y)\in T\right\}
.

Lemma B.4

(Support of a zero-margin)
.

Let
\gamma\in\mathrm{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
and let
\mu=\varphi_{1}\gamma\in\mathrm{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
denote its left zero-marginal. Then
\operatorname{spt}\mu\subset\operatorname{spt}\phi_{1}\gamma=\operatorname{cl}(\operatorname{proj}_{1}(\operatorname{spt}\gamma))
.
As a consequence, if
\operatorname{spt}\gamma\subset T
for some
T\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then
\operatorname{spt}\mu\subset\operatorname{cl}(\operatorname{dom}T)
.
In particular, if
T
is maximal monotone, then
\operatorname{int}(\operatorname{spt}\mu)\subset\operatorname{dom}T
.

We consider the extension
\tilde{\gamma}
of
\gamma
to a positive Borel measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
and write
\bar{\mu}=\phi_{1}\tilde{\gamma}=\phi_{1}\gamma
for its left marginal. Since
\mu
is the restriction of
\bar{\mu}
to
\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\smallsetminus 0}
, we have
\operatorname{spt}\mu\subset\operatorname{spt}\bar{\mu}
.

We show that
\operatorname{spt}\bar{\mu}
is equal to
F=\operatorname{cl}(\operatorname{proj}_{1}(\operatorname{spt}\tilde{\gamma}))
. First, to show that
\operatorname{spt}\bar{\mu}\supset F
, it suffices to show that
\operatorname{spt}\bar{\mu}\supset\operatorname{proj}_{1}(\operatorname{spt}\tilde{\gamma})
, as
\operatorname{spt}\bar{\mu}
is closed. But the latter inclusion follows easily: if
x\in\operatorname{proj}_{1}(\operatorname{spt}\tilde{\gamma})
, then there exists
y
such that
(x,y)\in\operatorname{spt}\tilde{\gamma}
; for any open neighbourhood
U
of
x
, the set
U\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
is an open neighbourhood of
(x,y)
, and thus
\bar{\mu}(U)=\tilde{\gamma}(U\times\mathbb{R}^{d})>0
, which, as
U
was arbitrary, implies
x\in\operatorname{spt}\bar{\mu}
.

Second, to show that
\operatorname{spt}\bar{\mu}\subset F
, we need to show that
\bar{\mu}(F^{c})=0
, as
F
is closed. We have
\bar{\mu}(F^{c})=\tilde{\gamma}(F^{c}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
and

F^{c}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\subset(\operatorname{proj}_{1}(\operatorname{spt}\tilde{\gamma}))^{c}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\subset(\operatorname{spt}\tilde{\gamma})^{c},

which is a
\tilde{\gamma}
-null set.

If
\operatorname{spt}\gamma\subset T
for some
T\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then
\operatorname{proj}_{1}(\operatorname{spt}\gamma)\subset\operatorname{dom}T
and thus
\operatorname{spt}\bar{\mu}\subset\operatorname{cl}(\operatorname{dom}T)
.

Finally, assume
T
contains
\operatorname{spt}\gamma
and is maximal monotone. By Theorem 12.41 (near convexity of domains and ranges) in
[
32
]
, there exists a convex subset
C
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
C\subset\operatorname{dom}T\subset\operatorname{cl}C
.
As
\operatorname{spt}\mu\subset\operatorname{cl}(\operatorname{dom}T)
, we have
\operatorname{spt}\mu\subset\operatorname{cl}C
, hence
\operatorname{int}(\operatorname{spt}\mu)\subset\operatorname{int}\operatorname{cl}C
. Moreover, the convexity of
C
yields
\operatorname{ri}C=\operatorname{ri}\operatorname{cl}C
, where
\operatorname{ri}
is the relative interior of
C
with respect to the smallest affine subspace that contains
C
. As a consequence, we can write

\displaystyle\operatorname{int}(\operatorname{spt}\mu)\subset\operatorname{int}\operatorname{cl}C

\displaystyle\subset\operatorname{ri}\operatorname{cl}C

\displaystyle=\operatorname{ri}C\subset C\subset\operatorname{dom}T,

and the statement is proved.
∎

Remark B.5
.

Let
\mu,\nu\in\mathrm{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\gamma\in\Gamma_{0}(\mu,\nu)
, and write
\bar{\mu}=\phi_{1}\gamma
for its left margin (
Remark

2.3
). Since
\mu=\operatorname{res}\bar{\mu}
we have the inclusions

\operatorname{spt}\mu\subset\operatorname{spt}\bar{\mu}\subset\operatorname{spt}\mu\cup\{0\}
.
It follows by
Lemma

B.4
that
\operatorname{spt}\mu\setminus\left\{0\right\}=\operatorname{cl}(\operatorname{proj}_{1}(\operatorname{spt}\gamma))\setminus\left\{0\right\}
.

Recall that we write
T\llcorner V=T\cap(V\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
for
T\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
V\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The following lemma will be useful to prove the graphical convergence, relative to the interior
V
of the support of
\mu
, of a sequence of maximal cyclically monotone sets
T_{n}\in\mathcal{F}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
, for
n\in{\rm Nature}
, containing the support of
\gamma_{n}
by allowing one to prove that the limit points of
T_{n}\llcorner V
are all the same. It is adapted from Lemma 4.2 in
[
36
]
. For
T\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we write
T^{-1}=\left\{(y,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\mid(x,y)\in T\right\}
,
\operatorname{dom}T=\operatorname{proj}_{1}(T)
and
\operatorname{rge}=\operatorname{proj}_{2}(T)
.

Lemma B.6

(Uniqueness of maximal monotone extension on the interior of the support)
.

Let
\gamma\in\Gamma_{0}(\mu,\nu)
for
\mu,\nu\in\mathrm{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and let
S,T\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{mm}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
satisfy
\operatorname{spt}\gamma\subset S\cap T
. Put
V=\operatorname{int}(\operatorname{spt}\mu)
and
W=\operatorname{int}(\operatorname{spt}\nu)
. Then
V\subset\operatorname{dom}S\cap\operatorname{dom}T
and
S\llcorner V=T\llcorner V
, as well as
W\subset\operatorname{rge}S\cap\operatorname{rge}T
and
S^{-1}\llcorner W=T^{-1}\llcorner W
.

Let
\tilde{\gamma}
be the extension of
\gamma
to
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
defined by
\tilde{\gamma}(B)=\gamma(B\setminus\left\{(0,0)\right\})
for
B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
Let
\hat{\mu}=\phi_{1}\tilde{\gamma}
and
\hat{\nu}=\phi_{2}\tilde{\gamma}
be its left- and right-marginals; by definition,
\tilde{\gamma}
lies in
\Pi(\hat{\mu},\hat{\nu})
.
According to
Lemma

B.4
and Lemma C.1 in
Segers 2022
,
it is immediate that both
\hat{V}=\operatorname{int}\operatorname{spt}\hat{\mu}
and
V
are contained in both
\operatorname{dom}S
and
\operatorname{dom}T
, since
\operatorname{spt}\gamma=\operatorname{spt}\tilde{\gamma}
. The rest of the proof of Lemma 4.2 in
[
36
]
can then be reused directly since it exclusively relies on properties of maximal monotone maps and not on the finiteness of the measures considered there. It follows that
S(x)=T(x)
for all
x\in\hat{V}
.
Since
\operatorname{spt}\mu\subset\operatorname{spt}\hat{\mu}
, we have
S(x)=T(x)
for all
x\in V
too.

The statements about
W
follow by switching the roles of
\mu
and
\nu
, upon noting that
\operatorname{dom}S^{-1}=\operatorname{rge}S
.
∎

Lemma B.7
.

Let
\mu,\nu\in\mathrm{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\mu(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\smallsetminus 0})=\infty
or
\nu(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\smallsetminus 0})=\infty
(or both), and let
\gamma\in\Gamma_{0}(\mu,\nu)
be supported by
\partial\psi
for some closed convex function
\psi
. Then
(0,0)\in\operatorname{spt}\gamma\subset\partial\psi
and
\psi(0)=\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\psi(x)
.

The zero-coupling measure
\gamma
must have infinite mass: if, for instance,
\mu(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\smallsetminus 0})=\infty
, then also
\gamma(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\smallsetminus 0}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})=\mu(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\smallsetminus 0})=\infty
. By
Lemma

2.4
,
\gamma
belongs to
\mathrm{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Therefore, necessarily
\gamma(\mathds{B}_{d}(0,\varepsilon)\times\mathds{B}_{d}(0,\varepsilon))=\infty>0
for every
\varepsilon>0
, which means that
\operatorname{spt}\gamma
meets
\mathds{B}_{d}(0,\varepsilon)\times\mathds{B}_{d}(0,\varepsilon)
for every
\varepsilon>0
. As a consequence,
(0,0)\in\operatorname{spt}\gamma\subset\partial\psi
, the latter inclusion holding by assumption. But then also
0\in\operatorname{dom}\partial\psi\subset\operatorname{dom}\psi
in view of
Eq.

47
. By definition of the subdifferential we find, as required,

\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\qquad\psi(x)\geqslant\psi(0)+\langle{x-0,0}\rangle=\psi(0).\qed
