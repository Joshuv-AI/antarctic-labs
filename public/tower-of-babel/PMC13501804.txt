The clinical features, pathological assessment, and prognostic analysis of autoimmune hepatitis in Chinese persons living with HIV
Authors: Danqing Wang, Jiafa Liu, Sen Lin, Qiwen Zhou, Mi Zhang, Aimei Liao, Xingwen Yu, Xingqi Dong, Haiyan Min, Xinyi Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeAutoimmune hepatitis (AIH) is an uncommon condition among persons living with HIV (PLWH), and its clinical presentation may be influences by immune reconstitution and viral reactivation, potentially affecting disease course and prognosis. In this retrospective case series, we aimed to describe the clinical features, pathological findings, treatment approaches, and outcomes of PLWH diagnosed with AIH in Yunnan Province, China, with a focus on region-specific clinical features.MethodThis single-center retrospective case series included PLWH diagnosed with AIH at Yunnan Provincial Infectious Diseases Hospital between June 2017 and February 2025, based on electronic medical records. AIH was diagnosed according to the simplified International Autoimmune Hepatitis Group criteria (definite ≥ 7 points; probable ≥ 6 points), with cases lacking liver biopsy classified as probable AIH. PLWH with other chronic liver diseases, substantial missing data, or follow-up shorter than 15 months were excluded.ResultAmong the eight PLWH, seven were female, with a median age of 46.5 years (IQR 39–51). All PLWH had been living with HIV for more than 5 years and were receiving antiretroviral therapy with well-controlled viral loads. Elevated transaminase and immunoglobulin G (IgG) levels were observed in all cases, and four of them presented with jaundice. All cases tested positive for ANA and ASMA autoantibodies; three were positive for LKM-1, two for SLA, and five for EBV-DNA (62.5%), which appeared higher than the ~ 20% reported in the general AIH population. Three PLWH (37.5%) had concomitant autoimmune diseases, including rheumatoid arthritis, Sjögren syndrome, and hyperthyroidism. Serum IL-6 (median 18.9 pg/mL, IQR 14.7–27.8) and IL-7 (median 10.2 pg/mL, IQR 8.7–13.1) levels were elevated. Liver biopsies was performed in six cases, showing inflammatory activity grades A1-A3 and fibrosis stages F0-F4, all consistent with typical features of AIH. The remaining two cases were classified as probable AIH based on the simplified criteria. One PLWH with advanced disease (A3/F4) died early due to acute hepatic failure. Among the six PLWH who received immunosuppressive therapy, five achieved complete remission and one (complicated with Sjögren syndrome) had an incomplete response. During a follow-up of 15–24 months, no relapse was observed in PLWH who achieved complete remission, and SF-36 scores improved.ConclusionIn PLWH in Yunnan, China, AIH presents with complex clinical features and may be potentially associated with EBV reactivation and systemic inflammation. However, the small sample size and retrospective design preclude causal inference. Our findings support the value of early non-invasive assessment and generate a hypothesis regarding the potential role of EBV monitoring. Future multiventer prospective cohorts or randomized controlled trials are required to calidate the long-term safety and efficacy of immunosuppressive therapy.Keywords: HIV, Autoimmune hepatitis, Liver biopsy, Non-invasive fibrosis scores, EBV-DNA, Clinical features, Genetic features, Therapy, Prognosis

Introduction

AIH is a chronic inflammatory liver disease of unknown etiology, characterized by diverse clinical manifestations and marked heterogeneity. Without timely diagnosis and appropriate treatment, AIH may progress to liver cirrhosis or hepatic failure [1]. In PLWH, the recognition of autoimmune diseases is particularly challenging and often overlooked, owing to HIV-associated immune dysregulation and immune reconstitution inflammatory responses induced by antiretroviral therapy (ART) [2]. Although the annual incidence of AIH in the general population is relatively low (approximately 0.9-2 per 100,000 individuals) [3], it is even rarer among PLWH, with reported detection rates of < 0.5% [2, 4]. In this population, AIH is easily confused with viral hepatitis or drug-induced liver injury, substantially increasing the risk of missed or delayed diagnosis [4, 5]. This challenge is particularly pronounced in regions such as Yunnan Province, China, where healthcare resources are relatively limited and HIV prevalence is high. According to a 2023 report by the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention, newly reported HIV cases in Yunnan accounted for more than 10% of the national total [6].

Currently, the international diagnosis of HIV-AIH primarily follows the guidelines of “European Association for the Study of Hepatology (EASL)”, which involve autoantibodies, IgG levels, liver biopsy, and a simplified scoring system [7]. However, the presence of non-specific antibodies in PLWH and the interference of IRIS significantly reduce the specificity of these diagnostic criteria [4]. In addition, the clinical utility of non-invasive assessments, such as FibroScan and APRI, has not been adequately validated in PLWH with AIH [8]. In terms of treatment, prednisone combined with azathioprine is the primary therapeutic regimen. However, standardized therapeutic guidelines specifically tailored for PLWN are lacking. Moreover, immunosuppressive therapy in this population requires particular vigilance for EBV reactivation and other opportunistic infections [9]. In China, particularly in Yunnan Province, the high prevalence of HIV and the limited techniques of liver biopsy limited early diagnosis.

To address the issues, we conducted a retrospectively analysis on 5687 PLWH admitted to Yunnan Infectious Disease Hospital between June 2017 and February 2025, form whom eight cases of confirmed or suspected AIH were identified (detailed clinical characteristics are presented in the Results section). By analyzing their clinical, biochemical, immunological, and pathological features, we clarified the diagnostic and therapeutic features of AIH in the context of HIV. We further hypothesize that these findings may be associated with IRIS and variation in immune-related genes, such as HLA-DRB1 or those involved in IL-6/IL-17 pathways. Our results indicate that early identification and standardized inmmunosupressive therapy strategies may hold significant value in improving the prognosis of HIV-AIH patients, and also provides practical insights for the management of diagnosis and treatment in resource-limited regions. Despite the small sample size and singe-center design, which may have introduced selection biases (e.g., potential underdiagnosis) and regional biases (e.g., HIV epidemiological features in Yunnan, including the prevalence of intravenous drug use and ethnic composition), our study provides preliminary data on the regional features of HIV-AIH in China and offers direction for future multicenter, large-sample (with a recommendation of at least 30 cases) studies and genetic testing validation.

Methods

Registry, trial registration number, and data of registration

Since this study is a retrospective research and not a prospective clinical trial, this project has not been registered for clinical trials.

Research objects

We retrospectively collected 5687 PLWH admitted to Yunnan Infectious Disease Hospital from June 2017 to February 2025. Among them, eight cases were confirmed or highly suspected to be complicated with AIH. The diagnosis of HIV/AIDS followed the “Chinese AIDS Diagnostic and Treatment Guidelines (2024 edition)” [10]. The diagnosis of AIH is based on the “2015 European Society of Hepatology Clinical Practice Guidelines: Autoimmune Hepatitis”, “Chinese Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Autoimmune Hepatitis (2021 edition)”, and the “Simplified Diagnostic Scoring System” [7, 11, 12].

Ethics statement

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Yunnan Provincial Infectious Disease Hospital (Approval No. 2024050). All samples from PLWH were obtained from Yunnan Provincial Infectious Disease Hospital. Written informed consent was obtained from all PLWH and healthy donors prior to participation in the study. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Enrollment cohort

Clinical data of all enrolled PLWH were collected and analyzed, including gender, age, medical history, clinical features, laboratory test results, liver histology results, treatment regimen and efficacy assessment. Efficacy assessment was evaluated according to the “2021 APASL Clinical Practice Guidelines”, and categorized into complete response, incomplete response, or no response [13].

Follow-up data

Duration of maintenance therapy, occurrence of relapse, SF-36 quality-of-life score.

HIV ELISA

The fourth-generation ELISA kits (e.g., Abbott ARCHITECT HIV Ag/Ab Combo, USA) were used to detect HIV antibodies and p24 antigen. Samples are EDTA anticoagulated serum or plasma (5 mL). The sensitivity of the assay is > 99% and the results were reported as absorbance ratio (S/CO).

CD4+ T cells counts and CD4/CD8 ratio

Peripheral blood samples (5 mL) were collected in EDTA anticoagulant tubes. Flow cytometry was performed using a BD FACSCanto II cytometer (BD Biosciences, USA) with three-color monoclonal antibodies (CD3-FITC, CD4-PE, and CD8-APC). After red blood cell lysis, samples were analyzed on the instrument. Results were expressed as cells/µL, and the percentage of CD4+/CD8+ was calculated.

Aspartate aminotransferase and platelet ratio index (APRI)

Aspartate aminotransferase (AST) was measured by the enzyme kinetic method (IFCC recommended method) using a fully automated biochemistry analyzer (Beckman Coulter AU5800, USA). Platelet counts were obtained by resistance and optical scattering method using Sysmex XN-9000 hematology analyzer (Sysmex, Japan). APRI was calculated as [AST (U/L) / AST upper limit of normal (40 U/L)] × 100 / platelet count (10^9/L).

Fibrosis-4 index (Fib-4)

Fib-4 was calculated as [age (year) × AST (U/L)] / [platelet counts (10^9/L) × ALT (U/L)]. Reference values: <1.45 suggests low risk of fibrosis, 1.45–3.25 is moderate risk, and > 3.25 is high risk.

Transient elastography of the liver (FibroScan)

Liver stiffness was assessed using a FibroScan 502 Touch (Echosens, France). Grading criteria: <7.0 kPa was considered normal, 7.0-9.5 kPa was considered mild fibrosis (F1-F2), and > 9.5 kPa was considered moderately severe fibrosis (F3-F4).

Ferritin assay

Serum ferritin concentrations (µg/L) were quantified using a chemiluminescent immunoassay (Siemens Atellica IM, Germany) to detect the chemiluminescent signal generated by the reaction of serum ferritin with anti-ferritin antibodies.

Liver tissue biopsy

Under ultrasound guidance, liver tissue samples (length > 15 mm) were obtained using a 16G disposable puncture needle (Bard, USA), fixed in 10% neutral formaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and stained with H&E and Masson trichrome. Inflammatory activity (A) and fibrosis stage (F) were evaluated according to the METAVIR scoring system (A0-A3, F0-F4). For patients who did not undergo liver biopsy, “probably AIH” was diagnosed according to the international simplified scoring system for autoimmune hepatitis.

Bias control

Potential sources of bias were proactively identified and addressed. Selection bias was minimized by consecutively including all eligible cases identified from the electronic health records (EHRs). Information bias was reduced through standardized data extraction procedures combined with multi-source verification. To control for confounding, stratified analyses were performed for key variables, including CD4+ T-cell count (< 200 vs. ≥200 cells/µL) and age (< 40 vs. ≥40 years). To mitigate time-related bias, a unified follow-up starting point was defined as the date of AIH diagnosis. In addition, particular caution was exercised regarding potential flase-positive results due to HIV antigen epitope mimicry [14].

Variable definition and data collection

All variables were defined a priori and collected using a standardized Excel-based data collection template. Data extraction was independently performed by two investigators (one clinician and one statistician). Discrepancies were resolved through cross-checking and consensus discussion.

Data analysis

Data were collected and organized using Excel. Count data were expressed in number of cases. Quantitative data were expressed as median (range), and qualitative data were expressed as number and percentage.

Result

Baseline characteristics of the study population

Among the eight PLWH with AIH, seven were female. The median age was 46.5 years (IQR: 39.0–51). All individuals had been living with HIV for more than five years and had received regular antiretroviral therapy, with sustained virologic suppression. The median nadir CD4+ T-cell counts were 120 cells/µL (IQR: 80–180). At the time of AIH diagnosis, CD4+ T-cell counts were all > 300 cells/µL (IQR: 341–658). All cases presented with elevated aminotransferase and IgG levels, and four of them were accompanied by visible jaundice (Fig. 1). All eight patients were tested positive for ANA and ASMA autoantibodies, and three of them were positive for LKM-1 antibody, two were positive for SLA antibody (Table 1). EBV-DNA was detectable in five patients (62.5%), which was notably higher than the reported prevalence of approximately 20% in typical AIH populations [15]. In addition, three PLWH (37.5%) had concomitant autoimmune diseases, including rheumatoid arthritis, Sjögren syndrome, and hyperthyroidism, a proportion exceeding the 22–34% reported in the general AIH population. Serum IL-6 and IL-7 levels were elevated, with median values of 18.9 pg/mL (IQR: 14.7–27.8) and 10.2 pg/mL (IQR: 8.7–13.1), respectively, suggesting activation of immune-related inflammatory pathways.

Non-invasive liver fibrosis assessment

To explore the utility of non-invasive liver fibrosis indicators in diagnosing HIV-AIH, we evaluated corresponding indicators of the eight patients. The median aspartate aminotransferase-to-platelet-ratio index (APRI) was 1.2 (IQR: 0.6–3.5; range: 0.5–4.2), the median fibrosis-4 (Fib-4) index was 1.8 (IQR: 1.0-4.1; range: 0.9–4.8), the median liver stiffness measured by transient elastography (FibroScan) was 7.2 kPa (IQR: 5.8–15.2; range: 5.5–18.0), the median ferritin level was 390 µg/L (IQR: 280–450; range: 250–1500). The liver stiffness detected by Fibroscan in the deceased PLWH (Case 3 and 7) ranged from 15.2 to 18.0 kPa, exceeding the commonly used threshold for cirrhosis (> 12.5 kPa). Serum ferritin levels were also markedly elevated (1200–1500 µg/L), compared with the overall cohort median. The inflammatory activity of the six patients who underwent liver biopsies ranged from A1 to A3, and the fibrosis stage from F0 to F4. Case 7 was the only one with severe inflammation and fibrosis status (A3/F4, APRI 4.2, Fib-4 4.8, FibroScan 18.0 kPa), suggesting a high risk of mortality. Two patients did not undergo liver biopsy and were classified as having “probable AIH” according to the simplified diagnostic scoring system. Sensitivity analysis excluding these non-biopsied cases showed a similar pattern of concordance between non-invasive fibrosis scores and histological findings; however, the further reduction in sample size substantially limited the reliability of these observations. Although non-invasive indices apprared broadly consistent with biopsy-based staging, this agreement was descriptive in nature, lacked formal statistical validation, and may be affected by reduced sensitivity in the context of HIV infection (Table 3).

Treatment and prognosis

All PLWH had been regularly treated with ART for 3 to 15 years at the time of diagnosis as AIH, with CD4+ T cell counts above 300 cells/µL (IQR: 341–658), and HIV RNA levels below the limit of detection. The onset of AIH occurred 3–15 years after ART initiation, suggesting a delayed immune reconstitution-ralated effect rather than the immediate unmasking of subclinical AIH following ART initiation [16]. Six patients received induction therapy, which includes prednisone monotherapy or prednisone combined with azathioprine. Among them, five PLWH received complete response after 6 months of treatment, with liver function and IgG levels returning to normal. In contrast, one PLWH with concomitant Sjögren’s syndrome showed an insufficient response and achieved only an incomplete response to induction therapy. For maintenance therapy, two PLWH with rheumatoid arthritis (Case 2) and Sjögren syndrome (Case 8) continued treatment with prednisone combined with azathioprine after achieving remission. The remaining four PLWH who achieved remission after induction therapy were switched to azathioprine monotherapy for maintenance. case 3 and 7 presented with advancer AIH and acute liver failure, accompanied by multiple organ dysfunction at admission, and died rapidly before standardized immunosuppressive therapy could be initiated. Among the six PLWH who were followed up, five of them with complete response remained relapse-free during 15–24 months of maintenance therapy. Health-related quality of life improved, with a median SF-36 score of 85 (IQR: 80–90). Case 8 had relapse accompanied by pneumonia after 12 months of maintenance therapy, with an SF-36 score of 70. Discussion

Although the global incidence of AIH is on the rise [9], epidemiological data in PLWH populations remain scarce, which may be related to low clinical recognition rates [4]. Among the 5,687 PLWH admitted to our hospital from January 2017 to February 2025, only 8 cases (0.14%) were diagnosed or suspected as AIH, indicating an extremely low detection rate in the PLWH population. The majority of these PLWH were female (87.5%), with a median age of 46.5 years. The gender and age distribution were similar to that of the general AIH population [4], suggesting that HIV infection did not significantly alter the epidemiological characteristics of AIH. Among this group of PLWH, 3 cases (37.5%) had other autoimmune diseases (such as rheumatoid arthritis, Sjögren’s syndrome, and hyperthyroidism), which was higher than the comorbidity rate in ordinary AIH without HIV infection (22%–34%) [17]. This indicates that immune reconstitution after ART may induce or exacerbate various autoimmune responses. In terms of clinical manifestations, these PLWH exhibit diverse modes of onset, including persistent fatigue, recurrent liver function abnormalities, and even acute liver failure, reflecting the clinical heterogeneity of AIH [18]. Additionally, HIV infection can induce polyclonal B-cell activation, leading to hypergammaglobulinemia and nonspecific autoantibody positivity, which are commonly observed even in the absence of typical autoimmune diseases [19]. This significantly increases the diagnostic difficulty of AIH. Therefore, for PLWH with unexplained abnormal liver function after excluding viral hepatitis, drug-induced liver injury, and opportunistic infections, AIH should be considered as an important differential diagnosis. Simultaneously, vigilance against false-positive results is warranted, as HIV viral antigen epitopes may cross-react with autoantibodies, leading to false-positive ANA or ASMA [20–22]. Current studies indicate that the false-positive rate of autoantibodies such as ANA in PLWH can reach 20–30%, which may stem from molecular mimicry between viral antigens and human tissue epitopes, as well as the resemblance of multiple antigenic epitopes, thereby significantly increasing the diagnostic difficulty of AIH [21]. In addition, HIV-related systemic inflammation can increase susceptibility to infections, including the risk of opportunistic infections, and may exacerbate liver damage [23], which is also a key aspect requiring differentiation during diagnosis.

In addition to diagnostic challenges, the immunological mechanisms underlying the co-occurrence of AIH in PLWH populations have not yet been fully elucidated. HIV infection typically leads to depletion of CD4+T cells as viral load increases, manifesting as an immunosuppressed state [24], while AIH is triggered by T cell-mediated immune imbalance causing chronic liver inflammation [25], presenting an apparent contradiction between the two conditions. More notably, IRIS following ART is considered a significant trigger for AIH occurrence in PLWH populations [26]. ART can rapidly restore CD4+T cell function, leading to an increased proportion of Th17 cells and Treg/Th17 imbalance, thereby activating hepatic immune responses [27, 28]. The 13 cases of HIV-AIH reported by Chaiteerakij et al. also support the IRIS hypothesis, with an additional 10 case reports of IRIS-related AIH [4]. In this study, all PLWH were in a state of virological suppression, with generally elevated levels of IL-6 and IL-17, suggesting that the patients might be in a state of inflammatory response. In this study, the median CD4/CD8 ratio in PLWH was 1.2 (IQR 0.9–1.5), suggesting that immune dysregulation may be associated with the occurrence of AIH. However, its diagnostic specificity requires further investigation, particularly regarding its role in the context of IRIS. In addition, HIV-related systemic inflammation may increase susceptibility to infections such as EBV reactivation, leading to exacerbation of AIH [23].

EBV, as a pathogen closely associated with various autoimmune diseases (such as systemic lupus erythematosus and rheumatoid arthritis) [29], warrants attention for its potential relevance in PLWH with concurrent AIH. In this study, EBV-DNA positivity was observed in 5 (62.5%) PLWH cases. This observational finding suggests potential EBV reactivation concurrent with the disease. However, due to the extremely small sample size (n = 8), absence of control groups, lack of statistical testing, and retrospective design, random variation cannot be excluded nor can any association be established. To provide preliminary background reference, we retrieved data from literature on HIV-infected individuals without AIH: one Chinese study reported an EBV-DNA positivity rate of 56.0% among HIV-positive MSM [30], while an Ethiopian study reported 49.7% (higher than the corresponding HIV-negative control groups’ rates of 26.0% and 24.7% respectively) [31]. These literature data indicate that EBV reactivation is relatively common among HIV-infected individuals, while the slightly higher positivity rate observed in this group of HIV-AIH patients is merely a descriptive observation, and cannot be used to infer AIH-specific effects or increased risk. Meanwhile, current reports limited data on EBV reactivation in non-HIV AIH, and lacks directly comparable studies. No significant differences in hepatic inflammatory activity or fibrosis degree were observed between EBV-positive and EBV-negative cases in this study. Therefore, future studies should incorporate well-designed case-control studies (including HIV controls without AIH and AIH groups without HIV), multicenter prospective cohorts, and functional validation experiments (such as animal models) to evaluate the potential correlation and clinical significance of EBV reactivation in HIV-AIH.

As the diagnosis of AIH highly depends on liver biopsy, its invasive nature limits widespread use; therefore, non-invasive indicators are becoming clinical aids [8]. In the six cases where biopsy was performed, APRI, Fib-4 and FibroScan were highly consistent with the pathological findings (Cases 2, 4, 5, 6 and 7 were completely consistent, and Case 8 was basically consistent), and these indicators performed well in the prediction of severe fibrosis and cirrhosis (e.g., Case 7, with APRI of 4.2, Fib-4 of 4.8, and FibroScan of 18.0 kPa, which was consistent with A3/F4 ); The APRI and Fib-4 of Case 8 (A2/F2) were slightly below the fibrosis threshold, suggesting that the presence of HIV may affect the sensitivity of the indicators. Cases 1 and 3 could not be assessed for consistency because no biopsy was performed. However, Case 3, with APRI 3.5, Fib-4 4.1, and FibroScan 15.2 kPa, was suggestive of severe fibrosis, which was consistent with death from liver failure. Further statistical validation is required to confirm consistency across the entire cohort. In addition, serum ferritin, as a sensitive indicator of systemic inflammation and tissue injury, showed potential prognostic value in this cohort. Ferritin levels were significantly higher in the deceased patients (1200–1500 µg/L) compared to the other patients (median: 390 µg/L). In the future, non-invasive diagnostic models should be developed for use in the HIV setting, especially in resource-limited areas, to optimize thresholds of the indicators and reduce dependence on biopsy.

Currently, there is no standardized treatment guideline for HIV combined with AIH, but international guidelines recommend prednisone combined with azathioprine as the first-line treatment regimen [7, 11], although PLWH receiving immunosuppressive therapy carries potential infection risks [32]. However, it has been shown that PLWH with well-controlled viral loads did not have significantly increased risk of serious infections when treated with corticosteroids and azathioprine [26]. In our study, five of the six patients treated with corticosteroids combined with immunosuppressive agents achieved complete biochemical responses within 6 months, with a significant improvement in quality of life (median SF-36 score of 85), and only one patient with comorbid Sjögren syndrome (case 8) relapsed after 12 months of treatment due to an incomplete response, accompanied by mild pneumonia. This suggests that the concomitant presence of other systemic autoimmune diseases may affect response to treatment and increase the risk of complications. Regarding long-term management, the four patients achieving complete responses were treated with azathioprine monotherapy for maintenance therapy, and no relapse was observed at 15–24 months of follow-up. This shows good tolerability and efficacy in long-term maintenance. The key to maintenance therapy is to keep the immunosuppressant at a low dose, optimize treatment adherence, and closely monitor liver function and immunologic indicators to minimize the risk of relapse and cumulative toxicity. Additionally, deceased cases failed to receive timely treatment due to liver failure, suggesting the importance of early recognition and intervention for prognosis. With the advancement of AIH therapeutic research, novel targeted treatment (e.g., anti-BAFF, anti-TNF-α, and IL-2 agonists) are being explored for AIH, also providing a promising direction for HIV-AIH population [27], particularly in the cases where hormone-related adverse effects need to be avoided.

Although this study has yielded some preliminary conclusions, there are still certain limitations. First, our study, as a single-center retrospective case series, has inherent limitations that constrain the interpretation and generalizability of the results. These include: (1) In terms of design and sample, the small sample size (n = 8) severely limits statistical power and inferential analysis, precluding formal association or causal assessments; (2) The single-center origin may introduce selection and geographic biases (e.g., specific Yunnan population), restricting generalization to non-Chinese or broader PLWH cohorts; (3) The absence of control groups (e.g., PLWH without AIH or AIH patients without HIV) hinders contextualization of key findings, such as the EBV-DNA positivity rate (62.5%) or the specificity of autoimmune comorbidities. Second, Diagnostic heterogeneity was prominent in the diagnostic and data aspects: two PLWH were diagnosed as “probable AIH” without liver biopsies, potentially biasing pathological evaluation and prognostic interpretation; the data missing rate was 5% (imputed using median values, which may introduce information bias); the absence of nadir CD4 count data limited the assessment of immune recovery dynamics (such as IRIS timing), making it impossible to determine whether AIH occurrence was directly related to CD4 increase. The short-term follow-up (median 18 months, range 15–24 months) could not evaluate long-term outcomes, such as relapse rates after 2 years, fibrosis progression, or cumulative toxicity of immunosuppression. Third, Despite vigilance against false positives (such as HIV-induced nonspecific antibodies [22]) and systemic inflammation (e.g., elevated IL-6/IL-17 increasing infection susceptibility [23]), the lack of molecular-level validation (such as genomic sequencing or functional experiments) limits in-depth mechanistic exploration. Although the EBV involvement hypothesis has literature support [30, 31], this study provides no causal evidence, offering only observational suggestions that cannot substantiate etiological explanations. While noninvasive scores (e.g., APRI, Fib-4, FibroScan) show consistency with biopsies, this agreement is based on small-sample descriptions lacking statistical validation, and their sensitivity in HIV contexts may be compromised.

In conclusion, our study is the first to systematically describe the clinical features and treatment outcomes of HIV-AIH in China, filling the research gap of Chinese population cases in this field and providing regional data to optimize diagnosis and treatment in the context of immune reconstitution after ART. As for treatment, our study demonstrates the effectiveness and safety of the early prednisone combined with azathioprine treatment, and pointed out that patients with other autoimmune comorbidities need individualized adjustment of drug dosage and strict prevention and control of infection. Moreover, we provide a practical and feasible management strategy for primary healthcare institutions in Yunnan. In the future, multicenter, large-sample (with a recommendation of at least 30 cases) cohort studies, incorporating flow cytometry, single-cell RNA sequencing, and HLA typing should be conducted to systematically evaluate the epidemiological features, immune mechanisms (IL-6/IL-17 pathways, EBV effects), genetic features, and long-term efficacy of HIV-AIH. Such studies will promote the standardization of precision diagnosis and treatment for HIV-AIH.
