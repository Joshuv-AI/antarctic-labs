Re: Ludovico Maria Basadonna, Federica Passarelli, Giorgio Graps, et al. The Influence of Music on Perceived Pain and Levels of Anxiety While Undergoing Transperineal Prostate Biopsy: The VIVALDI Trial. Eur Urol Open Sci 2026;87:107–14
Authors: Francesca Vedovo
Published: 2026

We read with great interest the VIVALDI trial by Basadonna et al. [1], which addresses an important and timely topic: the use of music as a simple, low-cost, nonpharmacological strategy to improve patient comfort during transperineal prostate biopsy. Studies exploring interventions aimed at reducing pain, anxiety, and procedural distress in outpatient urology are certainly welcome, particularly as patient experience is increasingly recognized as a relevant outcome of care.

However, when music is evaluated as a clinical intervention, it should be subject to the same methodological standards applied to any other therapeutic or procedural intervention. The fact that music is nonpharmacological, safe, inexpensive, and easy to implement should not exempt it from the need for clear definition, standardization, reproducibility, and treatment fidelity. This concept is specifically addressed by dedicated reporting guidelines for music-based interventions, which recommend detailed reporting of the theoretical rationale, musical content, person selecting the music, mode of delivery, sound level, setting, delivery schedule, and fidelity procedures [2].

This issue is particularly relevant in the VIVALDI trial [1]. In the intervention arm, patients were allowed to freely select the music, which was delivered through speakers from the preoperative holding area until the end of the procedure. While this approach may enhance comfort and acceptability, it also introduces substantial heterogeneity in the intervention itself. Music is not a generic exposure: genre, tempo, rhythm, emotional valence, familiarity, presence or absence of lyrics, volume, distance from the speaker, ambient noise, and perceived audibility may all influence attention, arousal, anxiety, and pain perception.

Therefore, without standardized or at least fully reported information on these elements, it becomes difficult to determine what the intervention actually was and which component may have driven the observed effect. The reported benefit may reflect not only a specific effect of music but also a composite of individual preference, familiarity, expectancy, environmental modification, and contextual reassurance. This represents a major potential confounder and limits the causal interpretation and reproducibility of the findings.

This concern is not merely theoretical. Previous methodological work has shown that poor reporting of music interventions is common in health care research and directly limits interpretation, replication, cross-study comparison, and clinical translation [2]. In the field of urological procedures, systematic reviews and meta-analyses suggest a potential benefit of music but also highlight substantial heterogeneity in intervention protocols and generally low certainty of evidence [3], [4]. Moreover, while several smaller studies have reported positive results, larger randomized trials in prostate biopsy have been more cautious or neutral, raising the possibility that the benefit of music may be overestimated in the published literature, particularly if neutral or negative studies are less likely to be published [5].
