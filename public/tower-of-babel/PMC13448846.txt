Diabetic Corneal Neuropathy as a Window Into Diabetic Foot Risk: A Large‐Scale Association Study
Authors: Chang Liu, Mingyi Yu, Isabelle Xin Yu Lee, Gavin Patrick O'Donnell, Hong Chang Tan, Aya Takahashi‐Nakajima, Yixin Wang, Mui Hui Koh, Ho Sean Matthew Chung‐Hei, Hasika Wickrama Senevirathne, Hla Myint Htoon, Ching‐Yu Cheng, Yu‐Chi Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsTo evaluate the association between diabetic corneal neuropathy and diabetic foot risk by examining corneal nerve features, clinical characteristics, and tear mediators in patients with different diabetic foot risk categories.MethodsIn this cross‐sectional study, 710 participants underwent diabetic foot examination, in vivo confocal microscopy scans, ocular surface assessments, and tear mediators measurements. Ordinal regression analysis was applied to determine the associations between foot risk categories and corneal nerve metrics. ROC curve analysis assessed the ability of corneal nerve parameters to identify high‐risk diabetic foot.ResultsAll diabetic foot risk categories exhibited significantly impaired corneal nerve fibre length, density, fractal dimension, and more swollen width compared with controls (all p < 0.001). These patients also demonstrated significantly shorter tear break‐up time, more severe ocular symptoms, elevated tear MMP‐9, and reduced tear Substance P levels (all p < 0.05). Patients with high‐risk diabetic foot showed significantly more pronounced corneal nerve alterations than those in low and moderate‐risk groups (all p < 0.05). The more severe diabetic foot risk category was significantly associated with reduced nerve fibre length, density, fractal dimension, and increased width (all p < 0.05). A predictive model incorporating corneal nerve fibre length, width, and serum creatinine achieved an AUC of 0.80 for identifying high‐risk diabetic foot. A CNFL < 5.7 mm/mm2 was associated with 4.37‐fold higher odds of having a high‐risk diabetic foot.ConclusionCorneal nerve impairment is evident even in low‐risk diabetic foot patients and is associated with foot risk severity. Corneal nerve metrics may serve as early indicators for identifying high‐risk diabetic foot.Keywords: corneal nerves, diabetic corneal neuropathy, diabetic foot risk, ocular surface, tear mediators

Introduction

Diabetic peripheral neuropathy (DPN) is one of the most prevalent microvascular complications of diabetes, affecting up to 75% of individuals with type 2 diabetes [1], and represents a major contributor to diabetic foot disease. Approximately 16%–60% of diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs) are attributable to DPN [2]. If not managed early, around 15%–20% of patients with DFUs eventually require lower limb amputation [3]. Diabetes‐related lower extremity amputations are independent risk factors of premature mortality, with the 5‐year mortality rate following major amputation at 61.9% [4]. Furthermore, diabetic foot disease is a chronic condition that requires an interprofessional and multi‐disciplinary approach to management, posing a significant public health problem and economic burden. The cost of DFUs alone is estimated at $9–13 billion annually, resulting in expenditures 50%–200% higher than baseline diabetes‐related care [5]. Foot risk stratification is crucial for distinguishing patients with different prognoses. Patients identified as having high risk were given an associated 2‐fold increase in mortality risk [6]. For these reasons, the guidelines recommend a minimum of one comprehensive foot examination annually for all individuals with diabetes, with more frequent assessments for those classified as high risk [7, 8].

However, the current assessment of diabetic foot risk is time‐consuming and complex, requiring detailed history‐taking and comprehensive physical examinations of callus formation, foot deformities, peripheral arterial disease (PAD), and neuropathy. Consequently, the implementation of routine risk assessment in clinical practice remains highly variable, ranging from approximately 15.7%–64.8% across countries [9]. There remains an unmet clinical need for rapid, accurate, and objective tools for diabetic foot risk stratification [10]. Effective prevention of diabetic foot complications begins with systematic screening for loss of protective sensation. Nerve conduction studies are considered to be the gold standard for the diagnosis of DPN, but they rely on the functional measurement of large nerve fibres, leading to inaccurate detection and delayed diagnosis. There is a lack of imaging tools to visualise peripheral nerves directly. The assessment of diabetic corneal neuropathy (DCN) has been recognised as a surrogate marker for DPN [11]. Corneal in vivo confocal microscopy (IVCM) is a rapid, non‐invasive ophthalmic imaging modality that enables accurate quantification of early axonal damage in diabetes, with high sensitivity and specificity using validated image analysis software. DCN is characterised by reduced corneal nerve fibre density (CNFD) and length, increased nerve fibre tortuosity, and a higher density of dendritic cells on IVCM [12, 13]. Furthermore, 50% of DPN patients exhibited abnormal corneal subbasal nerve changes in advance of the manifestation of clinical signs, suggesting that the alterations in corneal nerve precede the progression of DPN [14]. Therefore, it is reasonable to investigate the association between DCN and diabetic foot risk.

Despite growing evidence linking corneal nerve alterations with DPN, less is known about the interconnection between DCN and diabetic foot risk stratification, which integrates both neurological and vascular assessments to guide preventive care. Zhivov et al. [15] evaluated diabetic foot syndrome and corneal nerve fibre changes in 28 Congolese patients and demonstrated that the grade of diabetic foot syndrome, as evidenced by Wagner scores, correlated with CNFD and corneal sensation. Another study involving 78 individuals with type 1 diabetes reported that corneal nerve fibre length (CNFL) and CNFD exhibited good discriminatory ability between patients with and without DFUs [16]. The generalizability of the findings may be limited, given the small sample size and the lack of representativeness of the study cohort. The association between DCN and diabetic foot has not been fully elucidated in a larger population.

In diabetes, impaired nerves disrupt ocular surface homeostasis, manifested by reduced tear secretion and tear film stability, accompanied by subjective ocular discomfort, with these abnormalities worsening in parallel with diabetes severity [17]. However, these manifestations have not been observed in patients stratified by diabetic foot risk severity. In addition, alterations in tear profiles have been identified as potential indicators of diabetes‐related changes in the ocular surface. Tear concentrations of tissue inhibitor of metalloproteinase‐1 (TIMP‐1), substance P (SP), nerve growth factor (NGF), and insulin‐like growth factor‐binding protein 3 (IGFBP3) are significantly associated with corneal nerve loss in type 2 diabetes [18, 19]. Increased tear MMP‐9 has also been found to be linked with ocular surface inflammation in patients with type 2 diabetes [19, 20]. Analysis of tear cytokines represents a readily accessible and non‐invasive approach for obtaining pathological insights into diabetic microvascular complications.

In this study, we investigated the association between DCN and diabetic foot risk by comparing the corneal nerve metrics, ocular surface integrity, and tear neuromediators in patients with different foot risk categories. We also attempted to identify a panel of corneal nerve parameters that could be indicators of high‐risk diabetic foot.

Materials and Methods

Subjects and Study Design

This cross‐sectional study included 710 participants recruited at Singapore Eye Research Institute and Singapore General Hospital between February 2022 and July 2025. Among them, 493 were diagnosed with type 2 diabetes by experienced endocrinologists according to the American Diabetes Association (ADA) diagnostic criteria: HbA1c ≥ 6.5% (48 mmol/mol), fasting plasma glucose ≥ 126 mg/dL (7.0 mmol/L), 2‐h plasma glucose ≥ 200 mg/dL (≥ 11.1 mmol/L) during oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT), or random plasma glucose ≥ 11.1 mmol/L [21]. Exclusion criteria included any other foot diseases not related to diabetes, neuropathy of non‐diabetic origin, systemic diseases affecting the cornea, active ocular surface or corneal pathology, prior corneal or intraocular surgery, and contact lens wear within the preceding month. The control group comprised 217 age‐matched healthy individuals without diabetes, systemic inflammatory diseases, ocular pathology, or prior ocular surgery. All the participants' demographics and clinical characteristics were collected, including age, sex, race, duration of diabetes, body mass index (BMI), fasting blood glucose, cholesterol, high‐density lipoprotein (HDL), triglycerides, low‐density lipoprotein (LDL), haemoglobin A1C (HbA1c), and serum creatinine. The study was approved by the SingHealth Institutional Review Board, Singapore (number 2022/2046), and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all the subjects.

IVCM Scan and Imaging Analysis

IVCM (Heidelberg Retina Tomography III. Rostock Cornea Module, Heidelberg Engineering GmbH) was utilised to assess the corneal subbasal nerve plexus with the published protocol [22]. In brief, patients were instructed to fix on a target light while scans were obtained at the subbasal nerve plexus layer. Images were acquired from the central cornea and 4 peripheral quadrants located approximately 3 mm from the apex. For each region, five well‐focused images were selected, yielding a total of 25 images per eye. Image analysis was performed using ACCMetrics software (University of Manchester). The following corneal nerve quantitative parameters were obtained, including CNFD (number of fibres/mm2, frame area = 0.16033 mm2), corneal nerve branch density (CNBD; branch points on the main fibres/mm2), CNFL (total length of fibre mm/mm2), corneal total branch density (CTBD; total branch points/mm2), corneal nerve fibre area (CNFA; total nerve fibre area μm2/mm2), corneal nerve fibre width (CNFW; mean nerve fibre width μm/μm2), and corneal nerve fractal dimension (CFracDim), a measure of spatial distribution of nerves, where higher values indicate more even uniform branching. All the scans were performed by an independent and masked ophthalmologist (C.L.).

Ocular Surface Assessments

Ocular surface assessments encompassed the Schirmer's I test (without anaesthesia), with results being recorded as the length of strip wetting (mm) over 5 min [23]. Tear break‐up time (TBUT) was recorded as the interval between the last blink and the appearance of the first dry spot. Corneal sensitivity was evaluated by a Cochet‐Bonnet esthesiometer (Luneau Ophthalmologia, Chartres, France) at five locations: the central cornea and each of the four quadrants. The length (0–6 cm) of filament that elicited a blink reflex or escape response was recorded for each location. The total corneal sensitivity score ranged from 0 to 30 cm [24]. Three measurements in the same visit were taken for the abovementioned tests, and the average value of each eye was used for analysis. Ocular surface fluorescein staining (Oxford score; 0: absent, 5: severe) and corneal fluorescein staining (National Eye Institute (NEI) scale; 0: minimal, 15: maximal) were used to assess the ocular surface integrity, with higher scores indicating worse ocular surface integrity. All assessments were conducted by an independent and masked ophthalmologist (C.L.). The subjective symptoms were assessed with the Ocular Surface Disease Index (OSDI) questionnaire, a 12‐item questionnaire designed to assess the severity of ocular surface symptoms and their impact on vision‐related functioning. Assessment of Diabetic Foot Risk

All the participants underwent a detailed medical history review, including documentation of prior foot ulcer or amputation and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR). A standardised physical examination was performed to evaluate callus formation, foot deformities, PAD, and DPN. Physicians conducted a thorough inspection of both feet, assessing skin integrity and identifying deformities that might increase friction or pressure in footwear. PAD was evaluated by palpating pedal pulses bilaterally, including dorsalis pedis and posterior tibial arteries. DPN was assessed using a 10 g Semmes‐Weinstein Monofilament, vibration perception with a 128 Hz tuning fork, nerve conduction studies, and quantitative sensory testing. Based on the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) guidelines [26], patients were stratified into 3 risk categories of diabetic foot: Low‐risk diabetic foot: no presence of risk factors, such as loss of sensation or absent or diminished pulses, or present a simple callus only; Moderate‐risk diabetic foot: presence of one risk factor, such as foot deformity, PAD, or neuropathy; or thick callus requiring treatment or deformity with callus; High‐risk diabetic foot: history of foot ulcer or amputation, eGFR < 15 mL/min/1.73 m2, callus with intradermal bleeding, or present two or more risk factors, including deformity, callus, PAD, or neuropathy.

Tear Cytokines and Mediators Analysis

Tear samples were collected from the wet Schirmer strips as previously described and stored at −80°C until analysis [23]. On the day of analysis, the strips were cut into small pieces and immersed into 200 μL ice‐cold tear elution buffer containing 0.55M NaCl (S3014‐500G, Sigma Life Science), 0.33% Tween‐20 (P1379‐500ML; Sigma Life Science), 0.55% bovine serum albumin (9998S; Cell Signalling Technology), and 1× protease inhibitor (78 425; Thermo Scientific). Samples were subjected to sonication and homogenisation at 20% amplitude for 20 s, followed by incubation at 4°C for 17 h with gentle agitation at 450 rpm. The homogenisation step was repeated the following day prior to centrifugation at 11000 rpm for 20 min at 4°C. The resulting clear supernatants were collected and analysed by enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) for SP (6× dilution), NGF (1.5× dilution), IGFBP‐3 (6× dilution), MMP‐9 (10× dilution), and TIMP‐1 (15× dilution) (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, USA). Optical density (OD) was measured at 450 nm using a Tecan Infinite M200 microplate reader (Tecan, Männedorf, Switzerland), with 540 nm used as a reference wavelength. Analyte concentrations were calculated by interpolation from standard curves using GraphPad Prism 8 software (San Diego, CA, USA).

Statistical Analysis

The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Variables with normal distributions were expressed as mean ± SD, and variables that were not normally distributed were expressed as median (interquartile range). The mean values of both eyes were used for the comparisons across four different risk categories and ordinal regression analysis. For the comparisons of the characteristics across different groups, analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Bonferroni post hoc tests were applied to normally distributed continuous variables. Kruskal‐Wallis test and Dunn's multiple comparisons test were used for non‐normally distributed continuous variables. To account for multiple testing, the Benjamini‐Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure was applied to the overall p values obtained from the demographic variables, risk factors, corneal nerve parameters, and ocular surface assessments. The resulting FDR‐adjusted p values are reported in the ‘Adjusted p value’. Ordinal regression analysis was used to determine the associations between diabetic foot risk category and corneal nerve parameters, ocular surface parameters, after adjusting for well‐established potential confounders, including age, BMI, HbA1c, HDL, and duration of diabetes. Sample size was determined based on Cohen's F test. A total sample size of 600 participants was calculated for a four‐group comparison, assuming a moderate effect size (Cohen's f = 0.25), a significance level at 5%, and a statistical power of 0.9987. We also applied a conservative adjustment to account for potential variance inflation in discrete ordinal scales, by increasing the estimated sample size by 15%, resulting in a final target sample size of 695 participants. All statistical analysis was performed using SPSS (version 30.0, IBM Corp., USA), and statistical significance was defined as a p < 0.05. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to evaluate the diagnostic performance of using corneal nerve parameters for the identification of high‐risk diabetic foot. The area under the curve (AUC) was calculated as a measure of overall accuracy. Sensitivity, specificity, optimal cut‐off values, positive predictive values, and negative predictive values were determined using the Youden index. The AUC were compared using the DeLong test. The analysis was done by R (ver. 4.2.1; R Core Team, 2022).

Results

Participant Characteristics

Among the 493 patients with type 2 diabetes, 370 (75.0%) patients belonged to the low‐risk category, 64 (13.0%) patients were in the moderate‐risk category, and 59 (12.0%) patients were in high‐risk diabetic foot. Two hundred fifteen patients (43.6%) were female. Three hundred ten patients (62.9%) were Chinese, 97 (19.7%) were Indian, 58 (11.8%) were Malay, and 28 (5.7%) were from other ethnicities. The mean age was 58.4 ± 11.9 years. Of the 217 controls, 110 patients (50.7%) were female. One hundred eighty individuals (82.9%) were Chinese, 16 (7.4%) were Indian, 14 (6.5%) were Malay, and 7 (3.2%) were from other ethnic backgrounds. The mean age of the controls was 59.5 ± 10.8 years, which was comparable to that of the diabetic cohort (p = 0.221; Table 1).

Corneal Nerve Changes in Different Categories of Diabetic Foot Risk

Corneal nerve images showed that in patients with type 2 diabetes, even in the group of low‐risk diabetic foot, corneal nerve density and length had been markedly reduced compared to controls. The corneal nerve morphology and quantity, including nerve density, length, and tortuosity, deteriorated with the progression of diabetic foot risk (Figure 1).

On the quantitative analysis, compared with controls, all the patients with type 2 diabetes, irrespective of their diabetic foot risk category, had significantly lower CNFL, CNFD, and CFracDim, and significantly more swollen CNFW (all p < 0.001). Patients with low and high‐risk diabetic foot also exhibited significantly worse CTBD, CNBD, and CNFA compared with controls (all p < 0.001). Additionally, compared to patients with low and moderate‐risk diabetic foot, the high‐risk group showed significantly reduced CNFD (p = 0.002 and p = 0.039, respectively), CNFL (p = 0.001 and p = 0.023, respectively), and CFracDim (p = 0.002 and p = 0.024, respectively), as well as significantly more swollen CNFW (p < 0.001 and p = 0.040, respectively. Table 1; Figure 1).

Comparisons of Subject Symptoms and Ocular Surface Assessments in Different Risk Categories of Diabetic Foot

Compared to controls, patients with low, moderate, and high‐risk diabetic foot had significantly more severe ocular surface symptoms, as evidenced by the higher total OSDI score (p < 0.001, p = 0.010, and p < 0.001, respectively) and lower TBUT (p < 0.002, p = 0.032, and p < 0.029, respectively). The high‐risk group had significantly worse corneal sensitivity (p = 0.026) compared to low‐risk diabetic foot and significantly reduced Schirmer's test values compared to controls (p = 0.022; Table 1; Figure 1).

Comparisons of Tear Neuromediators in Different Risk Categories of Diabetic Foot

Compared with controls, all the diabetic foot risk categories had significantly lower tear SP concentrations (p < 0.001, p < 0.001, and p = 0.003, respectively), and significantly increased tear MMP‐9 concentrations (p < 0.001, p < 0.001, and p = 0.003, respectively; Table 1; Figure 1). No significant differences were observed in tear NGF, IGFBP3, and TIMP‐1 concentrations across the groups.

Relationship Between Corneal Nerve Parameters, Ocular Surface Assessments, and Diabetic Foot Risk Category

In the ordinal regression model, more severe diabetic foot risk category was significantly associated with reduced TBUT (OR = 0.90, 95% CI: 0.83–0.98, p = 0.018), CNFD (OR = 0.90, 95% CI: 0.86–0.96, p < 0.001), CNFL (OR = 0.85, 95% CI: 0.77–0.93, p < 0.001), and CFracDim (OR = 0.002, 95% CI: 0.000–0.180, p = 0.007), after adjusting for patients' age, BMI, HDL, HbA1c, and duration of diabetes. Similarly, after adjusting for potential confounders, increased CNFW was independently associated with a higher diabetic foot risk category (OR = 1.65, 95% CI: 1.37–2.05, p < 0.001), indicating that for each 1 μm/mm2 increase in CNFW, the odds of being in a higher diabetic foot risk category increased by 1.65 (Table 2).

Performance of Corneal Nerve Parameters for Identifying High‐Risk Diabetic Foot

We further identified a panel of corneal nerve parameters that identify high‐risk diabetic foot using those variables significantly associated with diabetic foot risk category. The ROC curve analysis showed that CNFL had an AUC of 0.66 (95% CI: 0.56–0.77, sensitivity: 40.5%; specificity: 86.5%) to differentiate high‐risk diabetic foot from non‐high‐risk diabetic foot. When incorporating serum creatinine, the AUC was improved to 0.79 (95% CI: 0.69–0.88, sensitivity: 64.9%, specificity: 86.9%; p < 0.001 compared with that of the model with CNFL alone). When CNFW was combined with CNFL and serum creatinine, the AUC was 0.80 (95% CI: 0.71–0.89, sensitivity: 73.0%, specificity: 80.6%; p < 0.001 compared with that of the model with CNFL alone), indicating good accuracy in distinguishing high‐risk diabetic foot patients from non‐high‐risk patients (Table 3; Figure 2).

Discussion

In this large cohort study, we for the first time reported the relationship between corneal nerve morphology, ocular surface functionality, tear neuromediators, and diabetic foot risks. We found that several corneal nerve parameters, such as CNFD, CNFL, CNFW, and CFracDim, were significantly associated with diabetic foot risk classification, and the combination of CNFL, CNFW, and serum creatinine demonstrated good accuracy for identifying patients at high risk of diabetic foot. Notably, corneal nerve alterations were evident even when patients were categorised as low‐risk diabetic foot. As diabetic foot conditions deteriorated, progressive impairment in corneal nerve status, tear film stability, ocular surface integrity, and tear neuroinflammatory and inflammatory mediators were observed. These findings underscore the fact that DCN may precede the clinical manifestations of diabetic foot and worsen in parallel with increased foot risk severity. Thus, corneal nerve status may serve as an early indicator of diabetic foot risk.

Our ordinal regression analysis revealed that corneal nerve parameters, including CNFD, CNFL, CNFW, and CFracDim, were independently associated with diabetic foot risk categories. Patients with diabetes who exhibit impaired corneal nerve status are more susceptible to developing diabetic foot, with those at higher diabetic foot risk demonstrating greater degrees of corneal nerve impairment. The observed associations can be explained by the parallel progression of microvascular complications, such as DPN and DCN, which share common pathogenetic mechanisms. Chronic hyperglycaemia activates several interrelated metabolic pathways, including advanced glycation end products formation, polyol and hexosamine pathway flux, protein kinase C activation, and oxidative stress [12], all of which contribute to endothelial dysfunction and microvascular damage. In addition, inflammatory cell activation and dysregulated cytokine production further exacerbate vascular injury and neuronal degeneration [27]. Collectively, these pathways are central to the development of both DCN and diabetic foot. Our findings are consistent with previous studies demonstrating that corneal nerve metrics correlate with DPN severity and neuropathic symptoms [28, 29, 30]. The corneal nerve metrics were lower in patients with DFUs compared to those without [16], and the metrics further worsened with increasing disease severity of diabetic foot [15]. While prior work has primarily focused on the presence of DPN or diabetic foot, our present study extends these observations by linking corneal nerve alterations specifically to diabetic foot risk categories, a clinically meaningful and practical outcome associated with morbidity and healthcare burden. Taken together, the results strengthen the evidence that DCN is closely linked to diabetic foot risk stratification, supporting the use of corneal nerve assessment as a promising biomarker for diabetic foot.

Notably, compared with controls, we found that corneal nerve metrics had been impaired in those patients who were categorised as low‐risk, with the absence of loss of sensation or diminished pulses in the feet. These indicate that corneal nerve damage was observed even when the clinical manifestation of diabetic foot was absent. The findings align with the concept that neurodegeneration develops early in diabetes, preceding overt microangiopathy, and small nerve fibre damage emerges prior to large nerve fibre impairment. Chronic hyperglycaemia has been shown to reduce neuronal perfusion and slow nerve conduction velocity, ultimately leading to neural injury and denervation [31]. Unmyelinated C fibres are generally recognised as being affected earliest in DPN, due to the lack of protective myelin sheath [31, 32]. The corneal subbasal nerve plexus is composed predominantly of unmyelinated C fibres and thinly myelinated Aδ fibres, which can be visualised by IVCM, providing a window for assessing early nerve damage. Clinical assessment of the diabetic foot is typically based on palpation of pedal pulses, including dorsalis pedis and posterior tibial arteries, and neurological assessment of perception using a 10‐g monofilament, vibration perception with a 128‐Hz tuning fork, nerve conduction studies, and quantitative sensory testing. Compared with these functional changes, the structural and morphological damage of nerves occurred first. Our results indicate that the alterations of the corneal nerve could be used as an early marker for diabetic foot prior to clinical manifestation.

Small nerve fibres constitute 70%–90% of peripheral nerves and play an essential role in sensory perception, tissue perfusion, thermoregulation, sudomotor function, and sweating, and these processes are highly relevant to the clinical manifestations and adverse outcomes of DFUs [33]. In diabetes, reduced CNFD has been linked to an increased risk of cerebrovascular events, and small fibre denervation is associated with microvascular dysfunction [16]. Moreover, corneal nerve metrics have been found to be associated with cardiac autonomic neuropathy, myocardial dysfunction, myocardial ischemia, and ischemic stroke [34, 35]. Collectively, these findings indicate that corneal nerve pathology reflects widespread diabetic neuronal and vascular impairment, such as PAD, which is a key contributor to diabetic foot. These observations support the potential utility of corneal nerve status as a surrogate marker of neurovascular disturbances relevant to diabetic foot.

In our cohorts, the prevalence of high‐risk diabetic foot was 12.0%, which is aligned with the previously reported ranges. Limited studies have reported the prevalence of high‐risk diabetic foot across different countries, with estimates ranging from 1.4% to 20% [36, 37]. Using the large UK IQVIA Medical Research Data, Wang et al. presented that 8% of 225 787 individuals with type 2 diabetes were at moderate or high risk [6]. Accurate identification of patients at high risk for diabetic foot is critical to enable timely interventions and prevent severe sequelae. In a large cohort of adults with type 2 diabetes, high‐risk patients were associated with a two‐fold increase in mortality compared with low‐risk counterparts [6]. Furthermore, healthcare costs rise substantially as risk progresses from low to high, increasing from approximately USD $17 000–$58 000 annually [38, 39], and a single high‐risk diabetic foot episode may exceed $50 000 in cost [40]. In this study, we further demonstrated that a panel of corneal nerve parameters serves as a strong indicator for the identification of patients at high risk of diabetic foot. A CNFL value below 5.7 mm/mm2 was associated with a 4.37‐fold higher odds of having a high‐risk diabetic foot. The AUC of 0.80 suggests that 80% of patients with diabetes could be accurately diagnosed as high‐risk diabetic foot by the multimodal model incorporating CNFL, CNFW, and serum creatinine. Reduced CNFL has been reported in patients with subclinical DPN and decreased further with increasing neuropathic severity [41]. Furthermore, a subsequent systematic review and meta‐analysis demonstrated the utility of CNFL in the diagnosis of DPN [42]. Concerning CNFW, Brines et al. reported that increasing severity of diabetic neuropathy was associated with a shift in the frequency distribution of CNFW toward thicker fibres [43]. Lauria and colleagues showed that patients with painful neuropathy exhibited significantly higher intraepidermal nerve swelling ratios at the distal leg [44]. These results indicate that nerve fibre swelling or axonal hypertrophy is present in diabetic neuropathy. However, the causal relationship, whether these changes contribute to the pathophysiology of diabetic neuropathy or arise as a consequence of the neuropathy, remains to be elucidated. In our model, the AUC could be enhanced by incorporating serum creatine levels. Serum creatinine testing is inexpensive, widely available, and routinely performed in individuals with diabetic nephropathy. Chronic kidney disease is a known risk factor for PAD [45], supporting the rationale for including creatinine as an indicator of diabetic foot risk in the model.

The impaired nerve innervation in our diabetic cohort, particularly among individuals at high risk of diabetic foot, resulted in diminished corneal sensation, decreased tear production, unstable tear film, and ocular surface discomfort. It has been well‐established that corneal nerves function as the afferent limb of the lacrimal reflex arc, as well as regulate blink frequency and provide trophic support for the ocular surface [46]. We also found that TBUT was significantly associated with diabetic foot risk categories. This finding is consistent with the report by Misra et al., who demonstrated a moderate inverse correlation between non‐invasive TBUT and total neuropathy score in patients with type 1 diabetes [47], supporting the concept that ocular surface alterations may reflect systemic neuropathic involvement. Our results also suggest that ocular surface assessments are recommended in patients at risk of diabetic foot to prevent the development of diabetic keratopathy.

We found that tear MMP‐9 levels were significantly elevated and tear SP levels were significantly reduced in individuals with diabetes, irrespective of diabetic foot risk category, compared with controls. Furthermore, tear concentrations of MMP‐9 and SP exhibited consistent trends with increasing severity of diabetic foot risk. The findings are consistent with previous studies comparing individuals with diabetes to healthy controls [20, 48, 49]. Moreover, tear MMP‐9 concentrations have also been reported to show an upward trend in patients with DPN compared with those without DPN [20], and tear SP levels were significantly correlated with total neuropathy score in individuals with type 1 diabetes [49]. Both biomarkers are also closely associated with ocular surface inflammation and neurogenic inflammation in diabetes [19, 48]. These results suggest that measurements of tear biomarkers may have potential clinical utility in reflecting the severity of diabetic foot risk. The causal mechanisms underlying these associations remain uncertain. Whether dysregulated tear cytokines lead to corneal nerve injury and ocular surface subjective and objective presentations, or whether corneal denervation releases neuropeptide and subsequently disrupts the ocular surface environment, requires further investigation.

Assessment of diabetic foot typically requires multiple examinations, which are time‐consuming, operator‐dependent, and may yield inconsistent results [45]. Our results underscore the importance of detecting small fibre neuropathies, such as DCN, for the early identification of microvascular complications, including DPN and diabetic foot [50]. Evaluation of corneal nerve status offers value in detecting the denervation, monitoring nerve regeneration, and indicating the onset and progression of diabetic foot.

Nevertheless, our study has several limitations. Given its cross‐sectional design, we were unable to assess longitudinal changes in corneal nerves and ocular surface parameters for the progression of diabetic foot, as the clinical neuropathy may develop up to 10 years after diabetes onset [51]. Future multicenter studies with larger and external cohorts, together with artificial intelligence‐based algorithms, can be considered to test the generalizability and validate the performance of using corneal nerve metrics in predicting high‐risk diabetic foot. Additionally, further research is needed to elucidate the shared pathophysiological mechanisms underlying DCN and diabetic foot, particularly at the molecular level. Such investigations may facilitate the identification of novel biomarkers and therapeutic targets within common pathogenic pathways.

In conclusion, our study revealed a significant association between DCN and diabetic foot risk, suggesting the corneal nerve metric could serve as a surrogate marker for diabetic foot. Importantly, DCN may precede the clinical onset of diabetic foot, supporting the utility of corneal nerve assessment for early identification of patients at risk of diabetic foot. Furthermore, the multimodal model incorporating corneal nerve metrics shows promise in differentiating individuals at high risk for diabetic foot. Understanding this relationship could potentially enhance diagnostic strategies in patients with diabetic microvascular complications, as well as strengthen multi‐disciplinary approaches for diabetes management.
