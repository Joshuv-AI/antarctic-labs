The effect of communication skills-focused psychoeducation on subjective well-being of caregivers of individuals with schizophrenia: a three-arm randomized controlled trial
Authors: Şirin Harkın, Hülya Bilgin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCaregivers of individuals with schizophrenia face emotional and psychological challenges that affect their subjective well-being (SWB). Despite their vital role, caregiver-focused interventions are limited. Structured programs that promote SWB by strengthening communication and psychosocial skills are needed to support caregivers effectively. The aim of this study is to evaluate the effectiveness of the “Communication Skills-Focused Psychoeducation Program(CSFP)”,which was developed to improve the SWB of caregivers of individuals diagnosed with schizophrenia.MethodsIt was conducted in an experimental design with randomization, two intervention groups, and one control group, with pre-test, post-test, and follow-up measurements. First, caregivers completed assessments on Subjective Well-Being scale (SWBS), communication skills (CSS), and caregiving burden (ZBI). Afterward, 72 caregivers were randomly assigned to three groups: CSFP, General Psychoeducation (GEP), and control. The CSFP-group received an 8-week, the GEP-group received a 5-week psychoeducation program, and the control group continued routine treatment. The effects of the intervention were evaluated immediately after the intervention and at the 1st and 3rd months of follow-up.ResultsResults showed a statistically significant difference between the pre-intervention and post-intervention SWBS mean scores of CSFP participants, as well as between pre-intervention and follow-up scores. In comparisons between groups, CSFP participants showed significantly higher SWBS mean scores than GEP participants immediately after the intervention, but no significant differences were found in the follow-up stages. Additionally, CSFP participants had significantly higher SWBS mean scores than the control group both post-intervention and at the 3rd month follow-up stage, though no significant difference was found at the 1st month follow-up stage.ConclusionsThese findings show that both interventions are effective in enhancing caregivers’ SWB, with CSFP providing longer-lasting effects. Future studies should further examine the effectiveness of these interventions and explore how they can be adapted to different groups. Additionally, integrating CSFP into psychosocial and rehabilitation programs could be beneficial in reducing caregiver burden and improving care outcomes.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov İdentifier: NCT05068765, (Registered on 5 October 2021) (https://register.clinicaltrials.gov/prs/beta/records).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12888-026-08413-2.

Background

Schizophrenia is a mental disorder that hinders individuals from maintaining an autonomous life and affects not only the individual but also their family [1]. Individuals diagnosed with schizophrenia often experience difficulties in self-care and social participation, necessitating support from others. This support is primarily provided by family members [2–4]. Recent studies conducted in Turkey indicate that 50–90% of individuals diagnosed with schizophrenia return to their family homes after discharge from the hospital, with at least one family member assuming the role of caregiver [5, 6]. However, the caregiver role is complex and can lead to caregiver burden. Along with the caregiver role, caregivers may struggle to understand symptoms arising from the nature of the illness (e.g., delusions and hallucinations), hesitate to communicate with the patient, experience interpersonal difficulties, and require guidance and support throughout this process [7–9].

Studies have reported that caregivers experience various negative emotions such as stress, anxiety, depression, shame, guilt, fear, helplessness, distress, worry, and hopelessness due to the patient’s condition and their inability to provide sufficient support [2, 6, 10]. Additionally, caregivers report lower levels of self-efficacy, quality of life, and Subjective Well-Being (SWB) perceptions [11–15]. SWB is defined as a comprehensive concept encompassing individuals’ satisfaction with various life domains, such as marriage, work, and other aspects of life, their emotional responses to events, and their cognitive evaluations of positive and negative affect [16, 17]. Studies indicates that families of individuals diagnosed with schizophrenia are at risk in terms of SWB and that there is a negative relationship between caregiving burden and SWB levels [13, 18, 19]. In this regard, supporting caregivers’ SWB is crucial for reducing caregiver burden and ensuring the sustainability of quality care [19, 20].

According to Diener’s model of SWB, positive/negative emotions and life satisfaction are significantly influenced by one’s social relationships [16]. In this context, strong social bonds and effective communication skills can contribute to individuals’ greater satisfaction with their lives and enhance their overall well-being [21–24]. Specifically, strengthening caregivers’ communication skills enables them to establish healthier relationships with both patients and family members, which can have positive effects on their SWB levels [9, 14].

Research has shown that effective communication skills in social relation are positively associated with key components of SWB, including life satisfaction, self-efficacy, happiness, and quality of life [23, 24]. In this regard, improving and effectively utilizing communication skills not only enhances individuals’ SWB levels [1, 14] but also contributes to reducing stress related to caregiving burden [8, 9] and making the caregiver role more sustainable [25].

Although interventions addressing the challenges faced by caregivers of individuals diagnosed with schizophrenia exist in the literatüre [11, 25–28] the necessity to manage communication deficiencies, support coping with stress arising from interpersonal relationships, and enhance interpersonal functionality has necessitated the adoption of more effective and diverse approaches [8, 9, 25, 29]. To reduce depressive symptoms caused by communication deficiencies and prolonged exposure to a chronic stressor, assist in stress management, and improve interpersonal functionality among caregivers, the Communication Skills-Focused Psychoeducation (CSFP) model has been proposed as a practical intervention [8, 9, 29]. The primary goal of CSBP is to train caregivers in effectively utilizing communication and interaction skills while managing individuals who exhibit unusual behaviors due to the nature of their illness [9]. A randomized controlled trial conducted by Rezaei et al. [25] demonstrated that a communication skills-focused psychoeducation program for caregivers of individuals diagnosed with schizophrenia improved their overall health status and enhanced their communication skills. Integrating psychoeducation programs into routine clinical practice for caregivers of individuals with schizophrenia is crucial for enhancing their adaptation and well-being [14, 30, 31]. Using the CSFP model, it is anticipated that the SWB levels of caregivers of individuals diagnosed with schizophrenia can be enhanced by facilitating the development of healthy and positive relationships, improving their ability to cope with negative emotions and challenges, increasing their awareness of personal strengths, and promoting a meaningful and fulfilling life.

In this context, this study is expected to contribute to the literature by comparing general psychoeducation and CSFP interventions applied to caregivers, as well as by developing new approaches that generate outcomes within the same timeframe. Given that both interventions have the potential to improve SWB, it has become necessary to examine their effects separately.

This study was conducted with the aim of evaluating the impact of two different interventions on the SWB levels of caregivers of individuals diagnosed with schizophrenia, based on the premise that integrating existing evidence-based approaches with newly developed combinations may provide a broader range of treatment options.

Hypotheses

H1

The SWB Scale scores of caregivers who participate in the CSFP will be higher after the intervention compared to those who only participate in the General Psychoeducation Program.

H2

The SWB Scale scores of caregivers who participate in the CSFP will be higher during the follow-up phase compared to those who only participate in the General Psychoeducation Program.

H3

The SWB Scale scores of caregivers who participate in the CSFP will be higher after the intervention compared to those in the control group.

H4

The SWB Scale scores of caregivers who participate in the CSFP will be higher during the follow-up phase compared to those in the control group.

Method

Design

This study was conducted using a randomized, experimental design with two intervention groups and one control group, including pre-test, post-test, and follow-up measurements (Appendix 1). Every step of the randomised controlled trial was conducted according to the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines (Fig. 1).

Population and sample

This study was conducted between January 5, 2021, and November 15, 2023. The study population consisted of 279 family members who provided care for individuals diagnosed with schizophrenia and applied to the Psychiatry Outpatient Clinic of a university hospital between January 2021 and December 2021. An a priori power analysis was conducted using G*Power 3.1 during the study planning phase. The sample size estimation was informed by effect sizes reported in a comparable randomized intervention study conducted with family caregivers of individuals with schizophrenia [32, 33]. Based on this framework, a total sample of 60 participants was considered sufficient for the planned analyses. Considering that a 10–20% data loss might occur during the study process [25, 34], the target sample size was increased by 20%, resulting in a planned sample of 72 participants.

Inclusion Criteria; the care recipient must have been diagnosed with schizophrenia at least one year ago according to DSM-5-TR criteria, the caregiver must have been directly responsible for care for at least six months, be at least 18 years old, have at least a primary school education, have no hearing or comprehension impairments, be willing to participate in the study, and ensure regular attendance in the psychoeducation programs. Exclusion Criteria; A history of substance abuse (except for nicotine).

All caregivers included in the study were responsible for unique patients, and no multiple caregivers were recruited from the same household. Therefore, all data points are independent, and no within-household dependencies exist.

Randomization

Participants were assigned to the three study groups in a 1:1:1 ratio by an independent statistician using SPSS. A block randomization procedure with a block size of 9 was used to maintain balanced allocation across the three groups. During allocation, comparability across groups was considered for gender, age, educational level, marital status, income status, relationship to the care recipient, duration of caregiving, baseline Zarit Burden Interview (ZBI) score, and baseline Communication Skills Scale (CSS) score (see Table 1, ‡: Control variables), as these variables have been reported to be associated with caregivers’ subjective well-being [12, 19, 31, 35]. Allocation concealment was maintained by using coded participant numbers only; the statistician had no access to names or other direct identifiers. Baseline comparisons showed no statistically significant between-group differences in these variables (Table 1). Because the researcher delivered the intervention and participants were aware of group assignment, full blinding was not feasible.

The study sample consisted of a total of 72 caregivers of individuals diagnosed with schizophrenia, divided into three groups: Group 1 (n = 24): CSFP intervention, Group 2 (n = 24): General Psychoeducation Program (GEP) for caregivers of individuals with schizophrenia, and Group 3 (n = 24): control group. During the intervention and follow-up phases, three participants from the CSFP group, four participants from the GEP group could not attend the sessions regularly and two participants from the control group could not be reached during follow-up. Therefore, the study was completed with 63 participants (Fig. 1).

Data collection instruments

Personal information form

This form consists of 32 questions in total, designed to identify participants’ individual characteristics, caregiving-related factors, and communication issues with the care recipient. The form includes “Yes-No” questions, Visual Analog Scale (VAS) items, and multiple-choice questions.

Subjective Well-Being Scale (SWBS)

The reliability and validity study of the scale, initially developed by Tuzgöl-Dost for university students, was conducted for the adult population by Tülek [36]. The scale assesses personal judgments regarding life domains and expressions of positive and negative emotions. The scale consists of 46 items with a 5-point Likert-type response system, ranging from 1 (“Not Suitable at All”) to 5 (“Completely Suitable”). The scale is considered unidimensional, with higher scores indicating higher levels of SWB. Tülek reported a high level of internal consistency with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.95, and a validity coefficient (Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin) of 0.90, indicating the scale’s strong psychometric properties for use with adults [36]. This study, the Cronbach’s Alpha coefficient was found to be 0.76 in the pre-test measurement.

Zarit Caregiver Burden Index (ZBI) Developed by Zarit et al. (1980) and adapted into Turkish by İnci and Erdem [37], the ZCBS is used to assess the difficulties experienced by caregivers of individuals in need of care. The scale consists of 22 Likert-type items with response options: “Never” = 0, “Rarely” = 1, “Sometimes” = 2, “Often” = 3, “Almost Always” = 4. The items primarily focus on social and emotional aspects, with higher scores indicating higher caregiver burden. The Cronbach alpha internal consistency coefficient of the original scale was found to be 0.71, 0.95 in the Turkish adaptation, and 0.87 in this study [37]. This scale was administered to both the intervention and control group caregivers before group assignments to ensure group equivalence.

Communication Skills Scale (CSS) Developed by Buluş et al. [38], the CSS is designed to measure interpersonal communication skills in adults. The scale consists of 34 items, with responses rated on a 5-point Likert scale: “Not Suitable at All,” “Very Slightly Suitable,” “Somewhat Suitable,” “Mostly Suitable,” and “Completely Suitable.” Five of the scale items contain negative statements. The scale comprises five sub-dimensions: “Ego-Enhancing Language,” “Active/Participatory Listening,” “Self-Recognition/Disclosure,” “Empathy,” and “I-Statements.” In the reliability and validity analyses performed for the scale, it was observed that the item-total correlations ranged between 0.32 and 0.60.The internal consistency coefficient for adults was found to be 0.82, and in this study, it was 0.74 in the pre-test. This scale was also administered to both intervention and control group caregivers before group assignments to ensure group equivalence.

Detection bias was attempted to be reduced by using standardized and valid measurement tools consistently for all participants.

Participant group experience form

This semi-structured form consists of five open-ended questions and was prepared to evaluate the experiences and perceptions of CSFP participants regarding the program. In developing the form, relevant literature [25, 28] was reviewed and questions appropriate to the context of this study were designed.

Pre- and post-intervention assessments were conducted by trained raters. Due to logistical constraints, these raters were not blinded to group allocation.

Implementation process

Development of psychoeducation programs

CSFP Communication Skills-Focused Psychoeducation Program was developed by the researcher based on the relevant literature [8, 9, 27, 29]. To facilitate the transformation of targeted skills into behaviors, an activity-based teaching approach was adopted, incorporating various exercises. Activities within a psychoeducation group serve multiple purposes, such as explaining a concept, fostering self-reflection, increasing engagement, or concluding a session. These activities contribute to enhancing the retention of learned skills and strengthening information synthesis [39, 40]. An eight-session training program was developed, including the following topics: “Introduction and Effective Communication,” “Schizophrenia and Challenges in the Care Process,” “Self-Awareness and Active Listening,” “Nonverbal Communication and Effective Use of Body Language,” “Recognizing Emotions and Expressing Them Using I-language Statements,” “Empathy and Recognizing Communication Styles,” “Managing Anger and Conflict Resolution,” and “Evaluation” (Appendix 2). The content of the program was reviewed by two experts in the field (faculty members specializing in Mental Health and Psychiatric Nursing). Based on their recommendations, the language was simplified, unnecessary sections were removed, and an educational booklet was created for caregivers.

GEP The General Psychoeducation Program (GEP) for caregivers of individuals with schizophrenia was developed through a literature review focusing on the challenges and educational needs of caregivers [6, 10, 41]. The training topics were determined accordingly and structured into a five-session program, covering: “Introduction and Understanding Schizophrenia,” “Schizophrenia Treatment and Recovery,” “Communication and Related Challenges,” “Coping with Stress and Problem-Solving,” and “Evaluation”. The program content was reviewed by two experts (faculty members specializing in Mental Health and Psychiatric Nursing). Necessary revisions were made based on their feedback, and the final educational booklet for caregivers was prepared (Appendix 3).

In summary, while both interventions include communication content, CSFP emphasizes practical skill training and behavioral application, whereas GEP focuses on knowledge provision and psychoeducation, reflecting distinct mechanisms of change.

Pilot-implementation

On May 20, 2022, and May 26, 2022, the first and third sessions of CSFP were piloted with caregivers of individuals diagnosed with schizophrenia and psychosis who were hospitalized at the study center (these participants were not part of the main study sample but met the inclusion and exclusion criteria). The pilot-implemention was conducted with four female and three male caregivers. Following the evaluation of session duration and content, it was observed that longer sessions reduced concentration levels. Therefore, the number of activities was adjusted, and sessions were divided into two parts, each lasting 60 min. Additionally, 10-minute breaks were introduced between sessions, and refreshments were provided for participants.

Preparation for implementation Participants were individually contacted by phone prior to the appointment, and inclusion criteria were checked during the call. Only those who met the criteria and agreed to participate were invited to the institution. The recruitment process was open to all eligible caregivers within the institution, and reasons for declining participation were recorded (as shown in Fig. 1). As shown in the flow diagram, a total of 207 individuals were excluded for the following reasons: illiteracy (n = 22), providing care for a person diagnosed with schizophrenia for less than one year (n = 9), hearing or comprehension impairments (n = 11), and refusal to participate (due to time constraints and difficulties accessing the institution) (n = 165). Although a large number of potential participants declined to participate or were excluded, the final sample size met the requirement determined by the power analysis. After participants were provided with information about the study, they were asked to sign an informed consent form and then complete the Personal Information Form and scales (pre-test).

The meeting room designated for the sessions was located on the ground floor of a building, approximately 100 m from the outpatient clinic. The room was spacious enough for group sessions and was equipped with a whiteboard and a projector, ensuring a secure and interactive environment with no external interruptions. To facilitate effective communication, chairs were arranged in a U-shape.

It is recommended that group sizes be limited to 8–12 participants to promote healthy communication, emotional expression, and active participation in activities [42]. Therefore, the two intervention groups (CSFP and GEP), each consisting of 24 participants, were further divided into two subgroups of 12 participants each. Participants in the CSFP group were given the option to attend sessions on either Tuesdays or Wednesdays, while those in the GEP group could choose between Thursdays or Fridays, depending on their availability.

İntervention

In the week following the pre-test, the CSFP Program (8 sessions) was conducted between June and August 2022, while the GEP Program (5 sessions) was implemented between June and July 2022, with one session per week for each program. Participants were called one day before each session to remind them of the session times. Those who could not attend on their designated session day were allowed to join the other group’s session. Sessions were conducted according to the predefined topics and structured plan, with each group session lasting approximately 90–105 min, including 10-minute breaks during the session. At the end of the final sessions, participants in the CSFP, the Group Experience Form and SWBS were administered as post-tests. Participants in the GEP group, only the SWBS was administered as a post-test. Educational booklets were distributed to all caregivers. All interventions were conducted by the researcher. During the intervetion, no adverse effects of the interventions were reported by participants. Some participants were unable to attend all sessions (3 participant in the CSFP group and 4 participant in the GEP group) due to personal time constraints or scheduling conflicts (Fig. 1).

While the intervention groups actively participated in their programs, no intervention was applied to the control group; they were contacted only twice by phone throughout the study to inquire about their status and reminded of the survey while being invited to the outpatient clinic.

Follow-up

To assess the sustainability of the psychoeducation programs, follow-up measurements were conducted at the 1st and 3rd months after the interventions for all groups. Participants were contacted by phone and invited to the psychiatry outpatient clinic, where they completed the scales. During the follow-up phase, two participants from the control group could not be reached.

Data analysis

Statistical analyzes were performed with SPSS v26 and the level of statistical significance was set to 5%. (IBM Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). The normality of the data was checked using the Shapiro-Wilk test. For variables with normal distribution, ANOVA was used. For variables that did not follow a normal distribution, Mann-Whitney U and Kruskal-Wallis tests were applied, while Pearson Chi-square analysis was used for the comparison of categorical variables. To examine the changes in scale scores within groups, between groups, and over time, Kruskal-Wallis and Friedman tests were performed. The responses to the Participant Group Experience Form were reviewed by the researcher. Responses with similar wording and meaning were grouped descriptively, and the resulting response groups were summarized as quantitative data using frequencies and percentages. Statistical bias was attempted to be prevented through group assignments and analyses conducted by an independent statistician.

In randomized controlled trials, some participants may not complete the study for various reasons. In this study, two participants from the CSFP group dropped out after the first session, and one participant left after the third session without completing the final test. Two participants from the GEP group left after the first session, and two others dropped out after the second session without completing the final test. Two participants from the control group could not be reached during the follow-up phase and were removed from the study. Because post-test/follow-up outcome data were not available for these participants, the analyses were conducted using a per-protocol approach based on participants who completed the study assessments [43].

Ethics

For the study to be conducted, approval was obtained from the University Ethics Committee (Document Date and Number: 05.01.2021-2021/01) and permissions were granted by the institution where the research was carried out (Date and Number: 08.06.2021-E-39633678-300-46165). In the study, all ethical principles were followed, ensuring the protection of participants’ rights, including “Informed Consent”, “Confidentiality and Data Protection”, “Voluntary Participation Principle”, and “Autonomy”: To ensure privacy and confidentiality, each participant was assigned a unique code, all data were stored in password-protected files accessible only to the research team, and identifying information was kept separate from study data. Participants were informed that their participation was voluntary and that they could withdraw at any time without any consequence. This research was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Permission was obtained for the use of the scales included in the study. The researcher held a “Professional Communication Skills in Psychiatry” certification. After the study was completed, psychoeducation booklets were shared with participants in the control group. The study was registered in the ClinicalTrials.gov database (Identifier: NCT05068765; Registered on 4 October 2021).

Table 3 shows the SWBS scores of CSFP, GEP, and control group participants over time. The pre-test mean SWBS scores were not statistically significant among the groups (KW = 5.802, p = 0.079). In the post-test, the CSFP group’s mean score was significantly higher than both the GEP and control group scores (KW = 20.102, p < 0.001). Additionally, there was no statistically significant difference between the mean scores of the GEP and control groups. In the one-month Follow-up 1, the CSFP group’s mean SWBS score was higher than the control group, with a statistically significant difference (KW = 18.100, p < 0.001). Although the CSFP group’s mean score was higher than the GEP group, the difference was not statistically significant. In the three-month Follow-up 2, the CSFP group’s mean score was higher than the control group, with a statistically significant difference (KW = 13.633, p = 0.001). Additionally, the difference between the CSFP and GEP group mean scores favored the CSFP group, but it was not statistically significant.

In the within-group comparisons, the CSFP group’s pre-test mean score was lower than the post-test, Follow-up 1, and Follow-up 2 mean scores. When comparing the post-test, Follow-up 1, and Follow-up 2 mean scores, there was no statistically significant difference (p > 0.05), but a sustained upward trend was observed (F = 45.188, p < 0.001). In the GEP group, the pre-test mean score was lower than the post-test, Follow-up 1, and Follow-up 2 mean scores (F = 49.423, p < 0.001). In the control group, the Follow-up 1 mean score was higher than the pre-test and post-test mean scores, with a statistically significant difference (F = 18.558, p < 0.001).

Discussion

In this study, the most common communication problems experienced by caregivers were difficulty communicating when individuals diagnosed with schizophrenia exhibited unusual speech or repetitive sentences, struggles in expressing themselves and their emotions, loss of control over behaviors, and challenges in empathizing and active listening. These findings are consistent with previous studies demonstrating that caregivers frequently experience similar communication difficulties [8, 11, 44]. The consistency of our findings with prior research may reflect the common challenges inherent to the caregiver role. These findings are important because communication difficulties may affect not only the relationship between caregivers and individuals with schizophrenia, but also caregivers’ broader psychosocial well-being. Research has shown that negative interactions in this context may also influence relationships with other family members and friends [18], and that caregivers need information and guidance on how to cope with these challenges [44–46]. Increasing evidence suggests that effective communication skills can enhance subjective well-being and contribute to positive outcomes such as improved health and longevity [16, 47]. Therefore, psychoeducation programs aimed at strengthening caregivers’ communication skills may support the psychosocial health of both caregivers and care recipients and contribute to improvements in SWB [36, 32, 48, 49].

In this study, caregivers’ SWB levels were found to be low across all three arms. Similar findings have been reported in previous studies conducted with caregivers of individuals with various illnesses, where SWB levels were also observed to be low [20, 31, 50–52]. These results may have emerged as a consequence of the challenges experienced during the caregiving role [12].

In the CSFP group, caregivers’ post-intervention SWB scores increased significantly compared to pre-intervention scores; although no statistically significant difference was found between the post-test and follow-up tests, the mean scores continued to increase. These findings may be associated with improvements in SWB levels through the enhancement of caregivers’ communication skills and their increased ability to manage the challenges of the caregiving role [48, 49].

The results of the study indicated that the post-intervention mean SWBS scores of caregivers in the CSFP group were statistically significantly higher than those in the GEP group. This finding supports the first hypothesis. This difference may be explained by the mechanisms of change underlying each intervention. While the GEP primarily provides caregivers with information and psychoeducation, the CSFP emphasizes practical skill acquisition through activity-based exercises and active application of communication strategies. This skills-focused approach may have enabled caregivers to experience immediate improvements in their perceived well-being by applying new communication techniques in real-life caregiving situations. However, no significant difference was found between the Follow-up 1 and Follow-up 2 mean scores of the CSFP and GEP groups, although the scores in the CSFP group remained higher. This result does not confirm the second hypothesis. This may be due to the content addressed in the GEP program (e.g., definition and treatment of schizophrenia, communication with the patient, coping with stress, and problem-solving). Similarly, in a randomized controlled trial conducted with individuals caring for patients with dementia, no differences were observed in mood outcomes at follow-up stages between psychoeducation and CBT interventions [53]. Therefore, to better evaluate the scope of the programs’ effects and their applicability in different populations, it is recommended to replicate the study with larger and more diverse samples.

The CSFP and GEP programs differ in the number of sessions, which may require caution in interpreting the results. Moreover, the literature suggests that intervention duration, rather than session number, may be more decisive [54]. Additionally, psychoeducation programs with fewer than ten sessions are generally considered brief interventions. A systematic review examining twenty brief psychoeducation programs reported that they produced positive effects. Therefore, this difference can be considered a procedural variation [55].

The findings obtained from this study indicate that both interventions are effective in improving SWB. The similar outcomes may be partially explained by the overlap in communication content; however, the CSFP includes skill-based exercises intended to enhance real-life application, which theoretically differentiates it from GEP. A possible explanation for this outcome is the positive change brought about by shared experiences and the establishment of new relationships in psychosocial interventions [28]. Another potential explanation is that caregivers perceived both interventions as effective because they felt that the support and attention provided during the interventions were also directed toward their own needs and well-being [25]. Similarly, a previous study conducted with family members of individuals with chronic mental illness compared a psychoeducational intervention focused on providing information and support about the illness with a collaborative family intervention that emphasized interaction among family members. The results showed that both interventions had similar effects on family burnout and family well-being [56]. The same study emphasized the importance of designing psychosocial interventions that address interpersonal interaction and communication skills. In this regard, structuring psychoeducation programs in a way that encourages individuals to share their emotions and thoughts may strengthen caregivers’ coping mechanisms and contribute to expanding their psychosocial support systems.

A significant difference was found between the post-test, Follow-up 1, and Follow-up 2 mean SWBS scores of the CSFP group and those of the control group. The analysis revealed a difference in favor of the CSFP group, supporting the third and fourth hypotheses. Similarly, Duran and Barlas [14] implemented a psychoeducation program for parents of children with intellectual disabilities, focusing on family interaction, stress management, and recognizing and expressing emotions. Their findings showed a significant increase in SWB levels in the intervention group compared to the control group, supporting the results of this study.

One of the noteworthy findings of this study was that the Follow-up 1 mean SWBS scores of the control group were significantly higher compared to the pre-intervention, post-intervention, and Follow-up 2 measurements. This outcome may have resulted from minimal interactions, such as checking on participants’ well-being and reminding them about the survey while inviting them to the outpatient clinic during the two phone calls conducted with the control group. This finding indicates that even brief attention and concern provided to participants may lead to a temporary improvement in their SWB levels [25, 57, 58].

In the study, participants expressed that they found the CSFP program to be highly positive and beneficial. It was thought that these positive evaluations were due to the fact that the prepared program made caregivers feel that they were not alone and made them feel good by giving them the opportunity to share. Caregivers often feel inadequate and overwhelmed [11, 13, 15]. Showing interest in them and acknowledging their competence in caregiving helps them feel appreciated. Even a simple remark such as “You are doing a great job” can have significant meaning. It is important to ask caregivers about their specific needs, including whether they require any support or information [25, 28, 59]. Therefore, raising awareness among mental health nurses regarding the importance of supporting caregivers’ participation in caregiving and addressing their needs is of great significance. Batchelor et al. [25] conducted a study with caregivers of individuals diagnosed with psychosis and found that online psychoeducation combined with peer-supported intervention reduced feelings of loneliness by helping participants realize that others faced similar challenges and provided them with a sense of being understood through shared experiences. Additionally, the findings of this study are consistent with those of Cristancho-Lacroix et al. [60], who evaluated a web-based psychoeducation program for caregivers of individuals diagnosed with Alzheimer’s disease. The positive feedback may have been influenced by the fact that the program content was carefully designed to address the specific circumstances and needs of caregivers. Furthermore, the program was structured to reinforce newly acquired knowledge through practical activities. For example, each session began with a warm-up activities, and all learning objectives were delivered through activity-based teaching. These arrangements were also considered to have contributed to the positive outcomes observed in the study.

Caregivers emphasized the importance of increasing their knowledge about the illness, reporting that the program was effective in providing access to a rich and reliable pool of information while also boosting their confidence and understanding, making them feel better and more at ease. Previous studies have also highlighted that caregivers most frequently need information about the illness and its management [41, 44].

Some caregivers frequently reported difficulties in participating in the group sessions due to time and transportation constraints. This challenge was thought to stem from their inability to leave the individual they cared for with someone else and their additional household responsibilities. Furthermore, the location of the program, being far from the city center, may have created additional transportation difficulties. Similarly, several studies have shown that caregivers encounter similar barriers [57, 59, 61]. Considering these limitations in advance could enhance the accessibility of such programs for those in need, improve participation continuity, and maximize the benefits caregivers can gain from the program.

Strengths and limitations

The study’s strengths include its pre-test–post-test control group experimental design with repeated measurements, the use of stratified randomization for the intervention and control groups, the inclusion of two intervention groups and a control group, and the attempt to compare the intervention’s effectiveness against a known effective intervention. Additionally, this study is significant as it represents the first application of a communication skills-focused psychoeducation program for caregivers of individuals diagnosed with schizophrenia in Turkey.

However, there are notable limitations. The data collection tools relied on self-reports, which may have introduced response bias and influenced the results. A further limitation of the study is that the raters who conducted the pre- and post-intervention assessments were not blinded to group allocation. This may have introduced assessment bias, potentially affecting the observed magnitude of the intervention effects. The content of the psychoeducation programs was predetermined by the researcher, limiting flexibility to address participants’ individual needs. The sample was selected from a single center, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other populations or settings. An important limitation of the study is that the difference in the number of sessions between the groups may have affected the outcomes. Although the sample size was determined by power analysis, using an active control group and repeated measures may limit statistical power. The study design comparing the CSFP program to the GEP may not be fully equipped to determine whether communication skills-focused interventions are essential for caregivers. The analyses were conducted using a per-protocol approach rather than an intention-to-treat framework; therefore, the findings reflect outcomes among participants who completed the study assessments and should be interpreted with caution. In addition, responses to the Participant Group Experience Form were grouped descriptively rather than analyzed using a formal qualitative method with independent coders, which may limit the rigor and reproducibility of these findings. Finally, no external stakeholders, such as healthcare institutions or professional advisory boards, were involved in the study, which may limit the generalizability of the findings.

Recommendations for further research

Evaluating the CSFP intervention with different groups and over longer follow-up periods could provide valuable insights.

Future research could examine whether caregiver interventions have positive effects not only on caregivers but also on the health and SWB of family members diagnosed with schizophrenia.

In future studies, equalizing the number of sessions for both the CSFP and GEP programs may reduce the potential effects of session number differences on the outcomes.

Future studies could also explore the best ways to plan program locations and schedules in collaboration with caregivers to facilitate participation by reducing time and transportation difficulties.

Future research could examine the potential benefits of home visits for caregivers who are unable to attend programs due to logistical constraints.

Moreover, studies could investigate the integration of CSFP into both outpatient and inpatient psychiatric care services as a strategy to maintain continuity in the system and enhance caregivers’ SWB and communication skills.

Participant feedback in the current study highlights the personal and practical benefits of the program. Including stakeholders such as caregivers and individuals diagnosed with schizophrenia in planning future interventions could improve the quality of research. Additionally, gathering detailed feedback from participants on which aspects of the CSFP they found most helpful could guide the program.

Conclusion

In this study, while the CSFP intervention demonstrated a higher impact, both interventions resulted in significant improvements in SWB compared to the control group. Based on the self-reports of CSFP participants, it was found that their awareness of their communication with themselves and their surroundings increased, their social interactions strengthened, and their self-confidence improved. Additionally, the findings indicated that enhancing communication skills can empower caregivers and contribute to an increase in their SWB levels. Based on this information, an integrated model could be developed to ensure that caregivers benefit optimally from both interventions.

Relevance to clinical practice

Within the framework of the community-based mental health model, mental health nurses should recognize the importance of caregivers and establish care models that address their psychosocial needs. The findings of this study highlight the benefits of integrating the CSFP into care plans for caregivers of individuals with schizophrenia. Mental health nurses can use CSFP to enhance caregiver SWB, reduce burden, and improve communication skills, ultimately leading to better caregiving outcomes.

Additionally, this study underscores the importance of training mental health nurses in psychoeducation interventions. CSFP-based training equips nurses with the skills needed to support caregivers effectively. Integrating CSFP into mental health care fosters a holistic approach, addressing both caregivers’ psychological needs and patient outcomes.
