Lightning OPD: Efficient Post-Training for Large Reasoning Models with Offline On-Policy Distillation
Yecheng Wu, Song Han, Han Cai NVIDIA

Abstract
Abstract: On-policy distillation (OPD) has emerged as an efficient post-training paradigm for large language models, where a student is trained to match a stronger teacher’s token-level distribution using dense per-token advantage signals. However, standard OPD requires a live teacher inference server throughout training, resulting in substantial infrastructure overhead. In this work, we investigate whether on-policy distillation can be performed offline. A natural approach is to precompute teacher log-probabilities once over SFT rollouts and reuse them during training. In practice, however, this offline variant fails to reliably match the performance of standard OPD. To understand this discrepancy, we identify a previously overlooked condition that is critical for any OPD pipeline, which we term teacher consistency . This condition requires that the same teacher model be used for both supervised fine-tuning and OPD. We show that violating teacher consistency introduces an irreducible gradient bias, causing both offline and online OPD to converge to a suboptimal fixed point regardless of training duration. Building on this insight, we propose Lightning OPD , an offline on-policy distillation framework that enforces teacher consistency by precomputing teacher log-probabilities over SFT rollouts. This design eliminates the need for a live teacher server entirely. We further show that, under teacher consistency, Lightning OPD shares the same optimum as standard OPD, with bounded gradient discrepancy and an implicit regularization effect that helps prevent policy drift. Extensive experiments on mathematical reasoning and code generation demonstrate that Lightning OPD achieves state-of-the-art performance with significantly improved efficiency. Starting from an SFT-initialized Qwen3-8B-Base model, Lightning OPD reaches 69.9% on AIME 2024 in just 30 GPU hours , achieving a 4.0 × \times speedup over standard OPD and substantially lowering the barrier to entry for academic research on LLM post-training.

However, the very property that makes OPD effective—the requirement that the teacher score every student rollout during training—also introduces a persistent infrastructure bottleneck. Standard OPD relies on a dedicated multi-GPU teacher server running in parallel with the training job, leading to substantial compute overhead and making large-scale experiments costly and difficult to reproduce, particularly for academic researchers without access to extensive serving infrastructure.

A natural question is whether the benefits of on-policy supervision can be preserved while eliminating the need for a live teacher server. On-policy training is defined by the student’s current rollout distribution, which evolves at every gradient step, making the teacher appear indispensable. However, recent empirical studies suggest that RL-trained models remain surprisingly close to their SFT initialization: reasoning trajectories in RL models are largely a reweighted subset of those present in the SFT model
61
, and on-policy updates are inherently biased toward solutions that minimize KL divergence from the reference policy
42

This observation suggests a practical alternative: precompute the teacher’s log-probabilities once over SFT rollouts prior to training and reuse these values throughout the OPD process, thereby eliminating the need for a live teacher server.

In practice, however, naively applying this offline precomputation fails to reliably match the performance of standard OPD. Investigating the root cause, we find that the issue does not primarily stem from the offline approximation itself, but rather from a more fundamental condition that has been overlooked in prior OPD work, which we term
teacher consistency
. Unlike RLVR, where model behavior is shaped solely by a reward signal, OPD involves
two
distinct teachers: one used during the SFT stage to generate training trajectories, and another used during the OPD stage to provide the reference distribution. Teacher consistency requires these two teachers to be the same model. In practice, existing pipelines often violate this condition by following conventions inherited from RLVR, where SFT datasets are curated using whichever teacher produces the highest-quality demonstrations, without regard to the teacher used during OPD. For example, Thinking Machines Lab
29
trains a Qwen3-8B-Base model on OpenThoughts-3
11
, whose trajectories are generated by QwQ-32B, while using Qwen3-32B as the OPD teacher—a mismatch that our analysis predicts to be detrimental. We show that such teacher inconsistency introduces an irreducible bias into the training objective, causing both offline and online OPD to converge to a suboptimal fixed point regardless of training duration or data scale. This issue is further exacerbated in the offline setting, where the fixed rollout distribution additionally reflects the incorrect teacher. These findings establish teacher consistency as a necessary condition for any OPD pipeline, rather than a constraint specific to the offline setting.

Methodology

3.1
Preliminaries

Let
\pi_{T}
denote a fixed teacher model and
\pi_{\theta}
a trainable student. Given a prompt
q
, a response
x=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{T})
is generated autoregressively as

\pi_{\theta}(x\mid q)=\prod_{t=1}^{T}\pi_{\theta}(a_{t}\mid s_{t}),

(1)

where
s_{t}=(q,a_{1},\ldots,a_{t-1})
. Let
\pi_{\text{ref}}
denote the SFT-initialized student. The per-token OPD advantage is

A_{t}(\theta)=\log\pi_{T}(a_{t}\mid s_{t})-\log\pi_{\theta}(a_{t}\mid s_{t}),

(2)

which is positive where the teacher is more confident than the student and negative otherwise. Standard OPD
1
;
29
;
53
optimizes

J_{\text{on}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{q\sim p,\;x\sim\pi_{\theta}}\!\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}A_{t}(\theta)\right],

(3)

while Lightning OPD fixes the rollout distribution to
\pi_{\text{ref}}
and optimizes

J_{\text{off}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{q\sim p,\;x\sim\pi_{\text{ref}}}\!\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}A_{t}(\theta)\right].

(4)

Both objectives share the same advantage function and differ only in the response distribution. By an IS decomposition,
\nabla J_{\text{on}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\pi_{\text{ref}}}[w(x;\theta)\cdot\nabla J_{\text{off}}(\theta)]
where
w(x;\theta)=\pi_{\theta}(x)/\pi_{\text{ref}}(x)
, so the offline gradient is the special case
w\equiv 1
.

Following the common practice of LLM post-training

;

, Lightning OPD consists of two stages. We describe each stage in detail below and highlight where Lightning OPD departs from standard OPD.

Stage 1: Supervised Fine-Tuning.

Given a base model
\pi_{\text{base}}
, a teacher
\pi_{T}
, and a prompt dataset
\mathcal{Q}_{\text{SFT}}
, we collect a supervised dataset from teacher-generated trajectories:

\mathcal{D}_{\text{SFT}}=\bigl\{\,\bigl(q^{i},\;x^{i}\bigr)\;\bigm|\;x^{i}\sim\pi_{T}(\cdot\mid q^{i}),\;q^{i}\in\mathcal{Q}_{\text{SFT}}\,\bigr\}.

(5)

The base model is then fine-tuned on
\mathcal{D}_{\text{SFT}}
via maximum likelihood estimation to obtain the reference policy
\pi_{\text{ref}}
:

\pi_{\text{ref}}=\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{\theta}\;\mathbb{E}_{(q,x)\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{SFT}}}\!\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log\pi_{\theta}(a_{t}\mid s_{t})\right].

(6)

While standard OPD may use any high-quality SFT dataset regardless of its source, Lightning OPD requires that
\mathcal{D}_{\text{SFT}}
be generated by the same teacher
\pi_{T}
used in the OPD stage. This
teacher consistency
condition is a prerequisite for the offline approximation to be sound, as we formally establish in Section
3
.

Stage 2: Offline On-Policy Distillation.

Starting from a task-specific prompt dataset
\mathcal{Q}_{\text{OPD}}
, the OPD stage proceeds in two phases. In the
preprocessing phase
, rollouts are sampled from
\pi_{\text{ref}}
and the teacher is queried
once
to precompute and store per-token log-probabilities, forming the offline dataset:

\mathcal{D}_{\text{OPD}}=\Bigl\{\,\bigl(q^{j},\;x^{j},\;\{\log\pi_{T}(a_{t}^{j}\mid s_{t}^{j})\}_{t=1}^{T_{j}}\bigr)\;\Bigm|\;x^{j}\sim\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid q^{j}),\;q^{j}\in\mathcal{Q}_{\text{OPD}}\,\Bigr\}.

(7)

In contrast, standard OPD requires a live teacher server throughout training, querying it at every step for log-probabilities on freshly sampled rollouts from the current policy
\pi_{\theta}
. In the
training phase
, the student is initialized from
\pi_{\text{ref}}
and trained on
\mathcal{D}_{\text{OPD}}
with no teacher server required. At each step, the advantage is computed as
A_{t}(\theta)=\log\pi_{T}(a_{t}\mid s_{t})-\log\pi_{\theta}(a_{t}\mid s_{t})
, where the teacher term is read directly from
\mathcal{D}_{\text{OPD}}
and the student term is computed online. This eliminates the primary infrastructure bottleneck of standard OPD and makes high-quality on-policy distillation accessible with minimal compute overhead. The full procedure is given in Algorithm

3.3
Theoretical Analysis

We characterize when and why Lightning OPD matches standard OPD and identify the precise conditions under which this equivalence holds. All proofs are deferred to Appendix
A
. The analysis rests on three standard assumptions, with a fourth introduced for the teacher-mismatch analysis.

Assumption 3.1

(Bounded Second Moment of Advantage)
.

There exists
\sigma_{A}<\infty
such that for all
\theta\in\Theta
:

\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\pi_{\text{ref}}}\!\left[\bigl(\sum_{t=1}^{T}A_{t}(\theta)\bigr)^{2}\right]\leq\sigma_{A}^{2}
,
where
A_{t}(\theta)=\log\pi_{T}(a_{t}\mid s_{t})-\log\pi_{\theta}(a_{t}\mid s_{t})
.

Assumption 3.2

(Support Coverage)
.

For all
\theta
encountered during optimization and all prompts
q
:
\operatorname{supp}(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid q))\subseteq\operatorname{supp}(\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid q))
.

Assumption 3.3

(Bounded Score Function)
.

There exists
G<\infty
such that
\|\nabla\log\pi_{\theta}(a_{t}\mid s_{t})\|_{2}\leq G
for all
\theta\in\Theta
and all
t
.

Assumption 3.4

(Bounded Teacher Mismatch)
.

Let
\Delta_{t}=\log\pi_{T}^{\text{SFT}}(a_{t}\mid s_{t})-\log\pi_{T}^{\text{OPD}}(a_{t}\mid s_{t})
. There exists
\sigma_{\Delta}<\infty
such that

\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\pi_{\text{ref}}}\!\left[\bigl(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\Delta_{t}\bigr)^{2}\right]\leq\sigma_{\Delta}^{2}
.
When teacher consistency holds (
\pi_{T}^{\text{SFT}}=\pi_{T}^{\text{OPD}}
),
\Delta_{t}=0
everywhere and
\sigma_{\Delta}=0
.

Assumptions
3.1
–
3.3
are standard:
3.1
is an
L^{2}
condition that avoids vacuous sup-norm bounds,
3.2
holds naturally when
\pi_{\theta}
is initialized from
\pi_{\text{ref}}
, and
3.3
is standard in policy gradient analyses. Assumption
3.4
quantifies the teacher mismatch and is only needed for the teacher consistency results (Theorems
3.11
–
3.13
).

Theorem 3.5

(Gradient Discrepancy Bound)
.

Under Assumptions
3.1
–
3.3
,

\|\nabla J_{\text{on}}(\theta)-\nabla J_{\text{off}}(\theta)\|_{2}\leq G\cdot\sigma_{A}\cdot\sqrt{\chi^{2}(\pi_{\theta}\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}})},

(8)

where
G
bounds the score function norm,
\sigma_{A}
is the
L^{2}
norm of the cumulative advantage under
\pi_{\text{ref}}
, and
\chi^{2}(\pi_{\theta}\|\pi_{\text{ref}})=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\pi_{\text{ref}}}[w(x;\theta)^{2}]-1
.

Remark 3.6
.

At initialization
\pi_{\theta}=\pi_{\text{ref}}
, we have
\chi^{2}=0
and the two gradients coincide exactly. The bound grows as
\pi_{\theta}
drifts from
\pi_{\text{ref}}
, but remains small under standard KL regularization
39
;
58
, which keeps the student near its initialization throughout training.

Despite this gradient gap, both objectives share the same performance optimum.

Theorem 3.7

(Shared Fixed Point)
.

The stationary conditions of
J_{\text{on}}
and
J_{\text{off}}
over
\Theta
coincide. Every stationary point
\theta^{*}
minimizes
\mathrm{KL}(\pi_{\theta}\|\pi_{T})
over
\Pi_{\Theta}
and is shared by both standard OPD and Lightning OPD.

Remark 3.8
.

The performance ceiling of Lightning OPD is determined solely by model capacity
\Pi_{\Theta}
relative to the teacher, not by the choice of rollout distribution. Switching to online rollouts can only reduce the distribution-shift error bounded in Theorem
3.5
, but cannot lower the irreducible approximation error
\varepsilon_{\text{approx}}=\min_{\theta}\mathrm{KL}(\pi_{\theta}\|\pi_{T})
.

Moreover, the offline objective itself resists the drift that causes the bound in Theorem
3.5
to grow.

Theorem 3.9

(Implicit Regularization)
.

The offline gradient decomposes as

\nabla J_{\text{off}}(\theta)=\nabla J_{\text{on}}(\theta)-\mathrm{Cov}_{\pi_{\text{ref}}}\!\left[w(x;\theta),\;f(x;\theta)\right],

(9)

where
f(x;\theta)=\sum_{t}A_{t}(\theta)\cdot\nabla\log\pi_{\theta}(a_{t}\mid s_{t})
.

Remark 3.10
.

The covariance term vanishes when
\pi_{\theta}=\pi_{\text{ref}}
and grows as the student diverges, acting as an implicit trust-region penalty that opposes drift from
\pi_{\text{ref}}
. Unlike an explicit KL penalty, this regularization requires no hyperparameter and arises automatically from fixing the rollout distribution.

The three results above assume the SFT and OPD stages use the same teacher. We formalize this as
teacher consistency
and show that violating it introduces an irreducible bias in both paradigms.

Theorem 3.11

(Teacher Consistency and the Offline-Online Gap)
.

Let
\pi_{T}^{\text{SFT}}
and
\pi_{T}^{\text{OPD}}
denote the teachers used in the SFT and OPD stages. Under Assumptions
3.1
–
3.4
,

\|\nabla J_{\text{on}}(\theta)-\nabla J_{\text{off}}(\theta)\|_{2}\leq G\cdot\!\left(\sigma_{A}\sqrt{\chi^{2}(\pi_{\theta}\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}})}+\sigma_{\Delta}\right).

(10)

When
\sigma_{\Delta}>0
, the additive term
G\sigma_{\Delta}
is irreducible and persists even at initialization.

Remark 3.12
.

The bias
\sigma_{\Delta}
arises because
\pi_{\text{ref}}
concentrates mass on trajectories preferred by
\pi_{T}^{\text{SFT}}
, while the advantage reflects
\pi_{T}^{\text{OPD}}
. This mismatch injects a persistent gradient error into Lightning OPD that no amount of additional training can remove. The mismatch constant
\sigma_{\Delta}
is directly controlled by the divergence between the two teachers and vanishes exactly when they coincide.

Theorem 3.13

(Teacher Consistency and Standard OPD)
.

Let
\nabla J_{\text{on}}^{\delta=0}(\theta)
denote the standard OPD gradient under a consistent teacher (
\pi_{T}^{\text{SFT}}=\pi_{T}^{\text{OPD}}
). Then at any
\theta
reachable from
\pi_{\text{ref}}
,

\|\nabla J_{\text{on}}(\theta)-\nabla J_{\text{on}}^{\delta=0}(\theta)\|_{2}\leq G\cdot\sigma_{\Delta}.

(11)

Standard OPD therefore also converges to a suboptimal fixed point when
\sigma_{\Delta}>0
.

Remark 3.14
.

Theorems
3.11
–
3.13
together show that
G\sigma_{\Delta}
corrupts the gradient of
both
paradigms equally, independent of whether rollouts are online or offline. Teacher consistency is therefore a fundamental requirement shared by Lightning OPD and standard OPD. When
\sigma_{\Delta}=0
(i.e., the two teachers are identical), Theorems
3.5
–
3.7
apply in full and Lightning OPD provably matches standard OPD.

3.4
Discussion

We discuss how Lightning OPD relates to two superficially similar paradigms, offline RL and offline knowledge distillation, and clarify why neither subsumes our approach.

Relation to Offline RL.

Lightning OPD resembles offline RL in that both optimize over a fixed dataset, but the resemblance is superficial and a direct application of offline RL techniques to OPD would fail for reasons that offline RL methods are not designed to address.
The central challenge of offline RL is OOD action overestimation arising from sparse reward signals, which offline RL methods address through conservatism mechanisms such as value pessimism or policy constraint.
Neither problem exists in Lightning OPD, where the teacher supplies dense per-token log-probability supervision for all token sequences, leaving no OOD region and no sparse reward to cause high-variance estimation.
Conservatism is therefore neither necessary nor applicable.
The real obstacle to offline OPD is teacher inconsistency. When
\sigma_{\Delta}>0
, the irreducible bias
G\sigma_{\Delta}
is a structural property of the gradient field itself, not an estimation artifact from limited data coverage, and no importance sampling correction or conservatism mechanism can remove it.
Beyond the challenge, the two paradigms also differ in the nature of their solutions. Offline RL fixed points are data-limited, shaped by the coverage of the behavior policy, so better data coverage leads to better policies. Theorem
3.7
shows that Lightning OPD’s fixed point is instead capacity-limited, determined solely by model capacity
\Pi_{\Theta}
relative to the teacher and independent of the rollout distribution. Improving the rollout distribution cannot push past the teacher’s own capability, and the right lever is model capacity, not data coverage.
In summary, Lightning OPD is not a variant of offline RL applied to distillation. It is a principled offline approximation to an on-policy distillation objective, one whose unique challenge is teacher inconsistency rather than distributional coverage, and one that provably recovers standard OPD exactly when teacher consistency is enforced.

Relation to Offline Knowledge Distillation.

Lightning OPD also differs fundamentally from offline (off-policy) knowledge distillation
20
;
10
, despite the shared use of precomputed teacher signals.
Offline KD trains the student on teacher-generated sequences, so the student only receives supervision on trajectories the teacher would produce, never on its own mistakes. Lightning OPD instead collects rollouts from the student’s own policy
\pi_{\text{ref}}
and evaluates the teacher’s per-token log-probabilities on these student-generated sequences. This means the teacher provides corrective signals on exactly the distribution the student will encounter during inference, which is the core advantage of on-policy methods over off-policy ones
1
.
The distinction is preserved in Lightning OPD even though teacher log-probabilities are precomputed. Empirically, the OPD stage provides substantial gains over the SFT baseline across all benchmarks, confirming that on-policy supervision on student rollouts extracts significantly more from the teacher than offline KD on teacher-generated data alone. Theorem
3.7
explains this gap by showing that the fixed point of Lightning OPD is capacity-limited, depending on how well
\pi_{\theta}
can approximate
\pi_{T}
across the full sequence space, rather than being restricted to the support of teacher-generated data.

For mathematical reasoning, we evaluate on AIME 2024

4.5
Ablation Study

Teacher consistency.

To empirically verify the teacher consistency principle, we design an ablation that crosses SFT-stage and OPD-stage teacher choices. We introduce QwQ-32B
46
as an additional teacher alongside Qwen3-32B at the 8B scale, and QwQ-32B alongside Qwen3-8B at the 4B scale, yielding a full grid of SFT and OPD teacher combinations. All other settings follow the main experiments. We conduct this ablation for both standard OPD and Lightning OPD, reported in Tables
3
and
4
respectively. The results uniformly confirm our theoretical prediction that the teacher-consistent setting, on the diagonal, always achieves the best performance, while mismatched teachers consistently degrade accuracy across all model scales and both paradigms. Notably, teacher inconsistency imposes a larger penalty on Lightning OPD than on standard OPD. At the 8B scale, mismatching from Qwen3-32B SFT to QwQ-32B OPD drops Lightning OPD by 6.8 points, from 69.9% to 63.1%, but drops standard OPD by only 3.7 points, from 68.5% to 64.8%. A similar asymmetry appears in the reverse direction. Averaged across all inconsistent teacher combinations and both model scales, Lightning OPD falls 1 to 2 points below standard OPD. This is precisely the failure mode we identified, namely that naively applying offline precomputation without enforcing teacher consistency does not reliably match standard OPD, and the gap is not random noise but a systematic consequence of the teacher mismatch. These findings are consistent with Theorems
3.11
and
3.13
, which predict that mismatched teachers introduce an irreducible gradient bias of magnitude
G\sigma_{\Delta}
into both paradigms, where
\sigma_{\Delta}
measures the teacher mismatch under
\pi_{\text{ref}}
(Assumption
3.4
). The amplified penalty for Lightning OPD arises from a compounding effect not fully captured by the per-step bound: a mismatched SFT teacher corrupts
\pi_{\text{ref}}
in two roles simultaneously, both as the reference distribution and as the source of fixed rollouts, whereas standard OPD refreshes its rollout distribution from the current student at every step and can therefore partially recover from the reference bias over the course of training. Teacher consistency is consequently a more critical design requirement for Lightning OPD than for standard OPD.

5
Conclusion

We presented Lightning OPD, an offline on-policy distillation framework for efficient LLM post-training. By precomputing teacher log-probabilities once over SFT rollouts, Lightning OPD reduces the infrastructure of OPD to a single standard training job while preserving the dense per-token supervision that makes OPD effective. We further identified teacher consistency as a necessary condition for OPD to work well in general, proving that mismatched SFT and OPD teachers introduce an irreducible gradient bias that causes both paradigms to converge to a suboptimal fixed point, and that under consistency Lightning OPD provably shares the same optimum as standard OPD. Empirically, Lightning OPD trained an 8B reasoning model to 69.9% on AIME 2024 in just 30 GPU hours, achieving 4.0
\times
speedup over standard OPD while matching its performance across all benchmarks. We hope these results encourage broader adoption of on-policy distillation in settings where a persistent teacher server is impractical, and that the teacher consistency principle provides a useful design guideline for future OPD research.

Proofs

We provide complete proofs of all theoretical results stated in Section
3.3
. We use Assumptions
3.1
–
3.3
(and Assumption
3.4
for the teacher-mismatch results) as stated in Section
3.3
. We begin with a fundamental preliminary result and then prove each theorem in order.

Notation.

Let
\Omega
be a finite vocabulary. A prompt
q\in\Omega^{*}
is drawn from
p(q)
. A response
x=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{T})
is generated as
\pi_{\theta}(x\mid q)=\prod_{t=1}^{T}\pi_{\theta}(a_{t}\mid s_{t})
, where
s_{t}=(q,a_{1},\ldots,a_{t-1})
. The sequence-level IS ratio is
w(x;\theta):=\pi_{\theta}(x\mid q)/\pi_{\text{ref}}(x\mid q)
.

A.1
Preliminary: Importance-Sampling Decomposition

We begin with a fundamental identity relating the online and offline gradients via IS reweighting. This is used in all subsequent proofs.

Proposition A.1

(IS Decomposition)
.

Under Assumption
3.2
, the online gradient equals the IS-reweighted offline expectation:

\nabla J_{\text{on}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\pi_{\text{ref}}}\!\left[w(x;\theta)\cdot\sum_{t=1}^{T}A_{t}(\theta)\cdot\nabla\log\pi_{\theta}(a_{t}\mid s_{t})\right].

The offline gradient
\nabla J_{\text{off}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\pi_{\text{ref}}}[\sum_{t}A_{t}(\theta)\cdot\nabla\log\pi_{\theta}(a_{t}\mid s_{t})]
is the special case
w\equiv 1
.

Proof.

Since
\Omega
is a finite vocabulary, all expectations are finite sums. For any function
f(x)
:

\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\pi_{\theta}}[f(x)]=\sum_{x}\pi_{\theta}(x)\,f(x)=\sum_{x}\pi_{\text{ref}}(x)\cdot\frac{\pi_{\theta}(x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(x)}\cdot f(x)=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\pi_{\text{ref}}}[w(x;\theta)\,f(x)],

where the second equality requires
\pi_{\text{ref}}(x)>0
wherever
\pi_{\theta}(x)>0
, which holds by Assumption
3.2
. Applying this with
f(x)=\sum_{t}A_{t}(\theta)\cdot\nabla\log\pi_{\theta}(a_{t}\mid s_{t})
(stop-gradient applied to
A_{t}(\theta)
) gives the result.
∎

A.2
Proof of Theorem
3.5

Proof.

Define the per-trajectory gradient
f(x;\theta):=\sum_{t=1}^{T}A_{t}(\theta)\cdot\nabla\log\pi_{\theta}(a_{t}\mid s_{t})
. By Proposition
A.1
:

\nabla J_{\text{on}}(\theta)-\nabla J_{\text{off}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\pi_{\text{ref}}}\!\left[(w(x;\theta)-1)\,f(x;\theta)\right].

Applying the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality to the expectation:

\left\|\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\text{ref}}}[(w-1)\,f]\right\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\text{ref}}}\!\left[(w-1)^{2}\right]}\cdot\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\text{ref}}}\!\left[\|f\|_{2}^{2}\right]}.

First factor.

Since
\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\text{ref}}}[w(x;\theta)]=\sum_{x}\pi_{\theta}(x)=1
:

\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\text{ref}}}\!\left[(w-1)^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\text{ref}}}\!\left[w^{2}\right]-2\,\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\text{ref}}}[w]+1=\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\text{ref}}}\!\left[w^{2}\right]-1=\chi^{2}(\pi_{\theta}\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}).

Second factor.

By Assumption
3.3
,
\|\nabla\log\pi_{\theta}(a_{t}\mid s_{t})\|_{2}\leq G
for each
t
. By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality over tokens:

\|f(x;\theta)\|_{2}\leq\left|\sum_{t=1}^{T}A_{t}(\theta)\right|\cdot G,

and therefore:

\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\text{ref}}}\!\left[\|f(x;\theta)\|_{2}^{2}\right]\leq G^{2}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\text{ref}}}\!\left[\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T}A_{t}(\theta)\right)^{\!2}\right]\leq G^{2}\,\sigma_{A}^{2},

where the last inequality uses Assumption
3.1
.

Combining.

\left\|\nabla J_{\text{on}}(\theta)-\nabla J_{\text{off}}(\theta)\right\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{\chi^{2}(\pi_{\theta}\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}})}\cdot G\,\sigma_{A}.\qquad\square

∎

Corollary A.2

(Zero Gap at Initialization)
.

When
\pi_{\theta}=\pi_{\text{ref}}
,
\chi^{2}(\pi_{\theta}\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}})=0
, so
\|\nabla J_{\text{on}}(\theta)-\nabla J_{\text{off}}(\theta)\|_{2}=0
. Under standard smoothness conditions, the gap grows at rate
O(\eta\,k)
after
k
gradient steps with step size
\eta
.

Proof.

At
\pi_{\theta}=\pi_{\text{ref}}
,
w(x;\theta)=1
for all
x
, so
\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\text{ref}}}[w^{2}]=1
and
\chi^{2}=0
. The growth rate follows from a first-order Taylor expansion of
\chi^{2}(\pi_{\theta-\eta g}\|\pi_{\text{ref}})
around
\theta
.
∎

Remark on
\chi^{2}
versus KL.

A bound via Pinsker’s inequality yields
\|\nabla J_{\text{on}}-\nabla J_{\text{off}}\|_{2}\leq MTG\sqrt{2\,\mathrm{KL}(\pi_{\theta}\|\pi_{\text{ref}})}

M=\sup_{t}|A_{t}(\theta)|
. This is vacuous in practice because
M
diverges whenever
\pi_{\theta}(a_{t}|s_{t})\to 0

3.5
avoids this by using the

\sigma_{A}\ll MT

\chi^{2}

Proof of Theorem

Proof.

We show that the first-order stationarity conditions of
J_{\text{on}}
and
J_{\text{off}}
over
\Theta
are equivalent.

Define the weighted inner product on token-sequence functions:

\langle g,\,h\rangle_{\mu}:=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mu}\!\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}g(a_{t},s_{t})\,h(a_{t},s_{t})\right].

Let
\mathcal{S}_{\theta^{*}}=\operatorname{span}\!\left\{\partial_{v}\log\pi_{\theta^{*}}(a_{t}\mid s_{t}):v\in\mathbb{R}^{|\Theta|}\right\}
be the tangent space of
\Pi_{\Theta}
at
\theta^{*}
.

The stationarity condition
\nabla J_{\text{off}}(\theta^{*})=0
requires, for every direction
v\in\mathbb{R}^{|\Theta|}
:

\left\langle A(\theta^{*}),\,\partial_{v}\log\pi_{\theta^{*}}\right\rangle_{\pi_{\text{ref}}}=0,

where we write
A(\theta)=\sum_{t}A_{t}(\theta)
for shorthand. This is precisely the statement that
A(\theta^{*})
is
orthogonal to
\mathcal{S}_{\theta^{*}}
in
L^{2}(\pi_{\text{ref}})

\Pi_{\mathcal{S}_{\theta^{*}}}\!\left[A(\theta^{*})\right]=0,

where
\Pi_{\mathcal{S}_{\theta^{*}}}
denotes the
L^{2}(\pi_{\text{ref}})
-orthogonal projection onto
\mathcal{S}_{\theta^{*}}
.

For standard OPD, the stationarity condition
\nabla J_{\text{on}}(\theta^{*})=0
requires:

\left\langle A(\theta^{*}),\,\partial_{v}\log\pi_{\theta^{*}}\right\rangle_{\pi_{\theta^{*}}}=0.

By Assumption
3.2
,
\pi_{\text{ref}}
and
\pi_{\theta^{*}}
are mutually absolutely continuous (the IS weight
w=\pi_{\theta^{*}}/\pi_{\text{ref}}
is strictly positive almost everywhere). Therefore the two inner products
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\pi_{\text{ref}}}
and
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\pi_{\theta^{*}}}
define equivalent topologies on function space: a function orthogonal to
\mathcal{S}_{\theta^{*}}
under one measure is orthogonal under the other. Hence the two stationarity conditions are equivalent and
J_{\text{on}}

share the same set of stationary points
\Theta^{*}\subseteq\Theta
.

To characterise

: at any stationary point
\theta^{*}

\Pi_{\mathcal{S}_{\theta^{*}}}[A(\theta^{*})]=0
means no first-order update in

. Expanding

\|A(\theta^{*})\|^{2}_{L^{2}}=\mathrm{KL}(\pi_{\theta^{*}}\|\pi_{T})+\text{const}

\mathrm{KL}(\pi_{\theta}\|\pi_{T})
over
\Pi_{\Theta}
.

Proof.

Recall
f(x;\theta)=\sum_{t}A_{t}(\theta)\cdot\nabla\log\pi_{\theta}(a_{t}\mid s_{t})

\nabla J_{\text{on}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\text{ref}}}[w(x;\theta)\cdot f(x;\theta)].

Using the covariance decomposition
\mathbb{E}[wf]=\mathbb{E}[w]\,\mathbb{E}[f]+\operatorname{Cov}[w,f]

\operatorname{Cov}[w,f]=0

Proof of Theorem
3.11

\pi_{T}^{\text{SFT}}
and the OPD-stage reference teacher
\pi_{T}^{\text{OPD}}
differ. The SFT model
\pi_{\text{ref}}
was trained on data generated by
\pi_{T}^{\text{SFT}}

\pi_{T}^{\text{SFT}}

A_{t}(\theta)=\log\pi_{T}^{\text{OPD}}(a_{t}\mid s_{t})-\log\pi_{\theta}(a_{t}\mid s_{t})
reflects
\pi_{T}^{\text{OPD}}

Proof.

Define
\delta:=\chi^{2}(\pi_{T}^{\text{SFT}}\|\pi_{T}^{\text{OPD}})
and
\Delta_{t}:=\log\pi_{T}^{\text{SFT}}(a_{t}\mid s_{t})-\log\pi_{T}^{\text{OPD}}(a_{t}\mid s_{t})
. The advantage under teacher inconsistency decomposes as:

A_{t}(\theta)=\underbrace{\left(\log\pi_{T}^{\text{SFT}}(a_{t}\mid s_{t})-\log\pi_{\theta}(a_{t}\mid s_{t})\right)}_{\text{consistent advantage }A_{t}^{\text{cons}}(\theta)}-\Delta_{t}.

Correspondingly, define
f_{\text{cons}}(x;\theta)=\sum_{t}A_{t}^{\text{cons}}(\theta)\cdot\nabla\log\pi_{\theta}(a_{t}\mid s_{t})
and
f_{\Delta}(x;\theta)=-\sum_{t}\Delta_{t}\cdot\nabla\log\pi_{\theta}(a_{t}\mid s_{t})
, so
f=f_{\text{cons}}+f_{\Delta}
.

By Proposition
A.1
and the triangle inequality:

\left\|\nabla J_{\text{on}}(\theta)-\nabla J_{\text{off}}(\theta)\right\|_{2}=\left\|\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\text{ref}}}[(w-1)f]\right\|_{2}\leq\underbrace{\left\|\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\text{ref}}}[(w-1)f_{\text{cons}}]\right\|_{2}}_{\text{(I)}}+\underbrace{\left\|\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\text{ref}}}[(w-1)f_{\Delta}]\right\|_{2}}_{\text{(II)}}.

Term (I): Consistent part.

This is identical to the proof of Theorem
3.5
with the consistent advantage, giving:

\text{(I)}\leq G\,\sigma_{A}\cdot\sqrt{\chi^{2}(\pi_{\theta}\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}})}.

Term (II): Mismatch part.

Applying Cauchy–Schwarz:

\text{(II)}\leq\sqrt{\chi^{2}(\pi_{\theta}\|\pi_{\text{ref}})}\cdot G\cdot\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\text{ref}}}\!\left[\left(\sum_{t}\Delta_{t}\right)^{\!2}\right]}.

Beyond this, even at
\pi_{\theta}=\pi_{\text{ref}}
(where
w\equiv 1
and Term (II)
=0
), there is a residual bias in the offline gradient itself:

\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\text{ref}}}[f_{\Delta}]=-\sum_{t}\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\text{ref}}}\!\left[\Delta_{t}\cdot\nabla\log\pi_{\theta}(a_{t}\mid s_{t})\right]\neq 0\quad\text{when }\delta>0.

By Assumption
3.3
and Cauchy–Schwarz:

\left\|\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\text{ref}}}[f_{\Delta}]\right\|_{2}\leq G\cdot\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\text{ref}}}\!\left[\left(\sum_{t}\Delta_{t}\right)^{\!2}\right]}\leq G\,\sigma_{\Delta},

where the last inequality applies Assumption
3.4
directly. Adding this residual to Term (I):

\left\|\nabla J_{\text{on}}(\theta)-\nabla J_{\text{off}}(\theta)\right\|_{2}\leq G\cdot\!\left(\sigma_{A}\sqrt{\chi^{2}(\pi_{\theta}\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}})}+\sigma_{\Delta}\right).

When
\sigma_{\Delta}>0
, the term
G\,\sigma_{\Delta}
is irreducible: it persists even at
\pi_{\theta}=\pi_{\text{ref}}
(where
\chi^{2}(\pi_{\theta}\|\pi_{\text{ref}})=0
) and cannot be reduced by any amount of additional training or data.
∎

A.6
Proof of Theorem
3.13

Proof.

Let
\nabla J_{\text{on}}^{\delta=0}(\theta)
denote the standard OPD gradient under a consistent teacher (
\pi_{T}^{\text{SFT}}=\pi_{T}^{\text{OPD}}
), and let
\nabla J_{\text{on}}(\theta)
denote the gradient under the mismatched teacher
\pi_{T}^{\text{OPD}}\neq\pi_{T}^{\text{SFT}}
. For any iterate
\theta
reachable from
\pi_{\text{ref}}
, their difference is:

\displaystyle\nabla J_{\text{on}}(\theta)-\nabla J_{\text{on}}^{\delta=0}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\pi_{\theta}}\!\left[\sum_{t}\left(A_{t}^{\text{OPD}}(\theta)-A_{t}^{\text{SFT}}(\theta)\right)\cdot\nabla\log\pi_{\theta}(a_{t}\mid s_{t})\right]

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\pi_{\theta}}\!\left[\sum_{t}\Delta_{t}\cdot\nabla\log\pi_{\theta}(a_{t}\mid s_{t})\right],

where
\Delta_{t}=\log\pi_{T}^{\text{SFT}}(a_{t}\mid s_{t})-\log\pi_{T}^{\text{OPD}}(a_{t}\mid s_{t})
.

By Assumption
3.3
and the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality:

\left\|\nabla J_{\text{on}}(\theta)-\nabla J_{\text{on}}^{\delta=0}(\theta)\right\|_{2}\leq G\cdot\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\pi_{\theta}}\!\left[\left(\sum_{t}\Delta_{t}\right)^{\!2}\right]}.

Since
\theta
is reachable from
\pi_{\text{ref}}
, by Assumption
3.2

\pi_{\theta}\ll\pi_{\text{ref}}
. Applying the IS identity (Proposition
A.1
):

\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\pi_{\theta}}\!\left[\left(\sum_{t}\Delta_{t}\right)^{\!2}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\pi_{\text{ref}}}\!\left[w(x;\theta)\left(\sum_{t}\Delta_{t}\right)^{\!2}\right].

At or near initialisation (
\pi_{\theta}\approx\pi_{\text{ref}}
,
w\approx 1
), this reduces to
\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\text{ref}}}[(\sum_{t}\Delta_{t})^{2}]\leq\sigma_{\Delta}^{2}
by Assumption
3.4
. Substituting:

\left\|\nabla J_{\text{on}}(\theta)-\nabla J_{\text{on}}^{\delta=0}(\theta)\right\|_{2}\leq G\,\sigma_{\Delta}.

This bound is independent of the rollout distribution: the same irreducible term
G\,\sigma_{\Delta}
corrupts the gradient of standard OPD whenever
\sigma_{\Delta}>0
, causing it to converge to a fixed point shifted away from the capacity-limited optimum identified in Theorem
3.7
.
∎
