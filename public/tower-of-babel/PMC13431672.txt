Declining Efficacy of Moxifloxacin for Mycoplasma genitalium Infection: Time for Fluoroquinolone Resistance–Guided Therapy?
Authors: Laura G Matthews, Lenka A Vodstrcil, Natasha L Wild, Erica L Plummer, Ivette Aguirre, Kay Htaik, Gerald L Murray, Vesna De Petra, Michelle Sait, Norelle Sherry, Catriona S Bradshaw
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWe assessed trends in moxifloxacin use and efficacy for Mycoplasma genitalium over a decade at Melbourne Sexual Health Centre (MSHC). Specific M. genitalium mutations, namely ParC-S83I, have been associated with moxifloxacin failure. In 2024 MSHC introduced ParC-S83 testing, recommending moxifloxacin for ParC-S83 wild type infections and alternative regimens if the ParC-S83I mutation was detected. We present preliminary impacts of ParC-S83 testing on moxifloxacin use and efficacy.MethodsClients with M. genitalium from 2015 to 2024 were treated with doxycycline preceding macrolide resistance–guided azithromycin or moxifloxacin. Clients with a test of cure 14–90 days after completing moxifloxacin who reported > 50% adherence and no condomless sex prior to test of cure were eligible for efficacy analyses. Proportions with 95% CIs were calculated for moxifloxacin use and efficacy, with trends assessed by logistic regression. Moxifloxacin use and efficacy were compared in the 12 months before and after introduction of the ParC-S83 assay.ResultsFrom 2015 to 2024, 2611 of 5739 M. genitalium infections received moxifloxacin. Moxifloxacin use increased from 6.7% (n = 19/282; 95% CI, 4.1%–10.3%) in 2015 to 60.3% (n = 482/800; 95% CI, 56.8%–63.7%) in 2023 (Ptrend < .0001). Efficacy analyses included 1623 infections. Moxifloxacin efficacy decreased from 100% (n = 11/11) in 2015 to 79.3% (n = 238/300; 95% CI, 74.3%–83.8%) in 2023 (Ptrend = .007). After introduction of the ParC-S83 assay, moxifloxacin use decreased from 60.3% (n = 482/800; 95% CI, 56.8%–63.7%) in 2023 to 49.2% (n = 438/890; 95% CI, 45.9%–52.6%) in 2024 (P < .0001) and cure increased from 79.3% (n = 238/300; 95% CI, 74.3%–83.8%) in 2023 to 89.2% (n = 116/130; 95% CI, 82.6%–94.0%; P = .013) in ParC-S83 wild type infections.ConclusionsIncreasing moxifloxacin use and declining efficacy reflects rising resistance in our region. Preliminary data suggest a ParC-S83 assay has the potential to improve moxifloxacin use and cure in this setting.

Mycoplasma genitalium is an important etiological agent of sexually transmitted infection (STI) syndromes whose management is complicated by its propensity to develop antimicrobial resistance [1, 2]. Azithromycin was formerly recommended for all M. genitalium infections; however, macrolide resistance is now common, particularly among men who have sex with men (MSM), where prevalence exceeds 80% [3, 4]. The fluoroquinolone moxifloxacin, first used in 2006, is globally recommended for macrolide-resistant M. genitalium [5–7]; however, failure has been increasingly documented over the past decade. A 2017 meta-analysis of 17 studies identified a significant temporal decline in moxifloxacin efficacy, from 100% in 2003–2010 to 89% in 2010–2016 [8]. While macrolide resistance mutations in M. genitalium are strongly associated with phenotypic resistance and azithromycin failure, the presence of mutations in quinolone-resistance-determining regions of the M. genitalium genome do not necessarily confer phenotypic resistance to moxifloxacin [9–12]. The parC-G248T single-nucleotide polymorphism, which confers the S83I amino acid substitution in ParC (henceforth referred to as ParC-S83I), has been associated with increased minimum inhibitory concentrations [13] and moxifloxacin failure in approximately 60% of cases [14]. Global prevalence of ParC mutations, of which ParC-S83I is the most common, has doubled since 2012; at the epicenter of this rise, the Western Pacific region now reports ParC mutation in approximately a third of macrolide-resistant infections [4]. The intrinsic resistance of Mycoplasma spp to many commonly used antimicrobials [2, 15, 16] makes monitoring and sustaining the efficacy of existing agents critical to responsible antimicrobial use and M. genitalium cure. Despite this, antimicrobial resistance surveillance data are lacking, and the impact of ParC mutations on population-wide treatment outcomes is yet to be investigated. We conducted a retrospective study of moxifloxacin treatment for M. genitalium infection at the Melbourne Sexual Health Centre (MSHC), a public sexual health service in Victoria, Australia, which provides > 50 000 consultations annually, to investigate trends in moxifloxacin use and efficacy over the past decade. In 2024, MSHC introduced a ParC-S83 assay for macrolide-resistant infections with both S83 wild type and S83I mutant gene targets, and updated guidelines to avoid moxifloxacin use in infections with the ParC-S83I mutation; we report preliminary outcomes associated with ParC-S83 resistance-guided therapy (RGT) on moxifloxacin use and cure.

METHODS

Clinical Setting

We conducted a retrospective audit of M. genitalium infections managed at MSHC from 1 January 2015 to 31 December 2024. Indications for M. genitalium testing included presentation with an STI syndrome (urethritis, cervicitis, pelvic inflammatory disease [PID]), or being a sexual contact of a person with M. genitalium infection. From January to November 2015, M. genitalium was tested for using a real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (PCR) assay targeting the MgPa gene [17]. In November 2015, this was replaced by the ResistancePlus M. genitalium assay (SpeeDx Pty) with simultaneous detection of macrolide-resistance mutations. From March 2021, M. genitalium was tested for using a transcription-mediated amplification assay (Aptima M. genitalium assay, Gen-Probe Panther system; Hologic), with reflex macrolide-resistance testing on the ResistancePlus assay.

In early 2024, MSHC introduced an additional in-house ParC-S83 assay for all macrolide-resistant infections with both S83 wild type and S83I mutant gene targets. Using the Hologic Gen-Probe Panther system, samples underwent extraction using the FCR-S/FER-S extraction kit (PRD-4332; Hologic) and PCR using the RNA/DNA enzyme PRD-04303 (Hologic). Reactions consisted of primers and probes targeting M. genitalium ParC S83 (FAM) wild type and S83I (HEX) mutations described by Sweeney et al [18], and commercial DNA internal control primers and probe (Quasar705; PRD-04306, PRD-04308; Hologic). Standard Panther Fusion DNA cycling conditions were used, consisting of an initial denaturation step of 95°C for 2 minutes followed by 45 cycles of 95°C for 8 seconds and 60°C for 25 seconds. Plasmids containing a fragment of the M. genitalium parC gene with S83 and S83I corresponding sequences were synthesized to use as positive and negative control material for testing. The assay was able to detect S83 wild type in 80% of samples at 440 copies/mL and 100% of samples at 4400 copies/mL, and S38I in 80% of samples at 818 copies/mL and 100% of samples at 8181 copies/mL. In addition to S83 wild type and S83I output, samples returned an indeterminate result if the M. genitalium load was below the limit of assay detection, contained another ParC-S83 mutation (eg, S83R or S83N), or if samples contained assay inhibitors.

Throughout the study period, clients were additionally tested for Chlamydia trachomatis and Neisseria gonorrhoeae by multiplex assay (Aptima Combo-2 assay, Hologic Gen-Probe Panther system) if indicated. Clients with vaginal symptoms were assessed for bacterial vaginosis (BV) by clinical examination and microscopic assessment of vaginal secretions using the Nugent method; for this study, BV was defined as a Nugent score ≥ 7 [19]. Clients with suspected PID underwent bimanual examination to detect uterine, adnexal, and/or cervical tenderness.

From 2015 to 2023, clients with uncomplicated, macrolide-resistant M. genitalium received doxycycline (100 mg twice daily for 7 days) followed by moxifloxacin (400 mg once daily for 7 days; Figure 1A). Clients with M. genitalium-PID were treated with extended 14-day moxifloxacin regimens regardless of macrolide-resistance status, in line with Australian guidelines [5]. After the introduction of ParC-S83 RGT in 2024, MSHC clinicians were advised not to prescribe moxifloxacin for macrolide-resistant infections with ParC-S83I mutation, and alternative regimens were recommended (including but not restricted to 14 days of minocycline and metronidazole [20]) (Figure 1B).

All clients were advised to contact trace as needed, abstain from sex, and re-present for test of cure (TOC) at least 14 days following completion of moxifloxacin. At TOC, clients were asked standardized questions regarding symptoms, regimen adherence, side effects, and sexual activity (Supplementary Figure 1).

Data Collection

Epidemiological, treatment, and microbiological data were extracted from electronic medical records. The diagnosis of an infection was defined as the first positive M. genitalium result returned by a client during the study period, and subsequent diagnoses were only included if > 365 days had elapsed between 2 consecutive positive M. genitalium results. Accordingly, our study population comprised discrete M. genitalium infections, rather than clients.

Infections were defined as moxifloxacin treated if moxifloxacin had been dispensed by the MSHC pharmacy within 14 days of diagnosis. Additionally, moxifloxacin must have been prescribed first line; that is, complex cases receiving moxifloxacin after failure of a fluoroquinolone, minocycline, and/or pristinamycin regimen were excluded.

Consistent with our prior publications [12, 21, 22], TOC must have been performed at MSHC 14–90 days after completion of moxifloxacin to be eligible for efficacy analyses. TOCs that returned inconclusive results or were conducted at the incorrect anatomical site were considered invalid and ineligible for efficacy analyses. Clients who reported incomplete treatment (ie, took < 50% of prescribed doses) or were at high risk of reinfection (defined as condomless vaginal/anal sex with an untreated ongoing sex partner) were additionally excluded. Approval for this study was granted by the Alfred Hospital Ethics Committee (No. 232/16).

Data Analysis

Two primary analyses were conducted. The first included diagnosed M. genitalium infections treated with moxifloxacin for analysis of trends in moxifloxacin use; the second included eligible moxifloxacin-treated infections for analysis of trends in moxifloxacin efficacy. The proportion of infections that were treated with moxifloxacin (for moxifloxacin use analysis) and cured following moxifloxacin treatment (for moxifloxacin efficacy analysis) were calculated using exact binomial methods with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Trends in each outcome measure were assessed by logistic regression using calendar year. Logistic regression was used to assess factors associated with moxifloxacin use and cure. All analyses were performed using Stata (version 17; StataCorp LLC).

Subanalysis of Impacts of ParC-S83 RGT

To assess the preliminary impact of ParC-S83 RGT on clinical practice, we compared moxifloxacin use and efficacy in the period before (1 January 2023 to 31 December 2023) and after (1 May 2024 to 30 April 2025) its introduction using exact binomial methods. Data from January to April 2024 were omitted as this was a transitional phase of assay validation, and data from January to April 2025 were collected as above.

RESULTS

Study Population

From 2015 to 2024, 5739 M. genitalium infections were diagnosed in 5430 clients (Figure 2); 3128 infections were excluded as moxifloxacin was either not prescribed (n = 2891) or prescribed for complex, heavily pretreated infections (n = 237). Among 2611 moxifloxacin-treated infections in 2523 clients, the median age was 28 years (interquartile range 24–34 years) and 3.9% of infections were diagnosed in clients living with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV; Table 1). A third of moxifloxacin-treated infections (n = 863, 33.1%) were in cisgender women, a quarter (n = 633, 24.2%) in cisgender, heterosexual men; and approximately 40% (n = 1115, 42.7%) in MSM/gender-diverse clients. The majority of moxifloxacin-treated infections (n = 1565/2611, 59.9%) were urethral, with 699 (26.8%) being cervicovaginal and 322 (12.3%) anorectal. Less than 1% (n = 25, 0.96%) were multisite (defined as anorectal plus another site). Coinfection with chlamydia and/or gonorrhea was detected in 348 (13.3%) M. genitalium infections. Of the 863 M. genitalium infections diagnosed in women, 518 (60.0%) had same-day vaginal microscopy performed, and BV (defined as Nugent score ≥ 7) was detected in 189 of 518 (36.5%). In total, 2380 (91.2%) moxifloxacin regimens were 7 days, prescribed for macrolide-resistant infection, and 231 (8.9%) were 14 days for M. genitalium-PID (Table 1).

Moxifloxacin Use From 2015 to 2023

From 2015 to 2023, moxifloxacin was used to treat 2154 of 4852 M. genitalium infections at MSHC (44.4%; 95% CI, 43.0%–45.8%; Table 2). Annual moxifloxacin use increased from 6.7% in 2015 (n = 19/282; 95% CI, 4.1%–10.3%) to 60.3% in 2023 (n = 482/800; 95% CI, 56.8%–63.7%; Ptrend < .0001), peaking at 65.1% in 2022 (n = 368/565; 95% CI, 61.0%–69.1%). There were significant year to year increases in 2016, 2017, 2020, and 2021 (Table 2).

During the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic and associated lockdowns that significantly restricted movement in Victoria, client attendance at MSHC was reduced throughout 2020 and 2021 and laboratory resources were diverted to COVID-19 testing, limiting access to M. genitalium diagnostics. Due to reduced capacity during this time, MSHC prioritized complex presentations (eg, PID, treatment-resistant infections) and low-priority presentations were referred to external providers [23]. Consequently, total M. genitalium diagnoses at MSHC reduced from 616 in 2019 to 488 and 453 in 2020 and 2021, respectively (Table 2). To account for any bias in case demography, we also conducted an interrupted time series analysis excluding 2020 and 2021, which still demonstrated a significant increase in moxifloxacin use (Ptrend < .0001).

In univariable analyses of factors associated with resistance-guided use of moxifloxacin, MSM/gender-diverse clients had increased odds of being treated with moxifloxacin for M. genitalium compared to women (odds ratio [OR], 1.83; 95% CI, 1.58–2.11; P < .001; Supplementary Table 1). After adjusting for annual increase in moxifloxacin use over the 9 years, this association retained significance (adjusted OR [aOR], 2.38; 95% CI, 2.04–2.79; P < .001). Notably, despite moxifloxacin use being highest among MSM and gender-diverse clients overall, there were also significant increases in moxifloxacin use among women and heterosexual men over the decade (Ptrend < .0001; Supplementary Table 2), reflecting increasing macrolide resistance in these populations.

Moxifloxacin Efficacy From 2015 to 2023

From 2015 to 2023, 1482 of 2154 (68.8%; 95% CI, 66.8%–70.8%) moxifloxacin-treated infections had a TOC performed at MSHC 14–90 days following completion of moxifloxacin. Importantly, loss to follow-up for TOC remained stable across 2015 to 2023 (Ptrend = .330). After additionally excluding invalid TOCs (n = 14) and cases reporting incomplete treatment (n = 14), or those at high risk of reinfection (n = 94), 1360 moxifloxacin-treated M. genitalium infections were eligible for efficacy analyses. Of these, 1126 (82.8%; 95% CI, 80.7%–84.8%) infections were cured with moxifloxacin (Table 3).

Annual moxifloxacin efficacy declined significantly from 100% in 2015 (n = 11/11; lower 95% CI, 71.5%) to 79.3% in 2023 (n = 238/300; 95% CI, 74.3%–83.8%; Ptrend = .007). When stratified by duration of moxifloxacin treatment, annual cure following 14-day regimens remained stable from 2015 to 2023 (pooled cure 87.0%, n = 100/115; 95% CI, 79.4%–92.5%; Ptrend = .908; Supplementary Table 3). However, there was significant decline in cure following 7 days of moxifloxacin, from 100% in 2015 (n = 6/6; lower 95% CI, 54.1%) to 78.6% in 2023 (n = 227/289; 95% CI, 73.4%–83.1%; Ptrend = .009). Interestingly, when the efficacy of 7 days of moxifloxacin was stratified by gender and sexuality, cure did not change significantly in women and heterosexual men (Ptrend = .243 and .743, respectively) but declined in MSM/gender-diverse clients (Ptrend = .001; Supplementary Table 4).

In analyses of factors associated with cure following 7 days of moxifloxacin (excluding cases of M. genitalium-PID treated for 14 days), we found chlamydia coinfection was associated with increased odds of moxifloxacin cure compared to M. genitalium monoinfection (OR, 2.30; 95% CI, 1.04–5.07; P = .039; Supplementary Table 5), which retained significance after adjustment for decline in efficacy from 2015 to 2023 (aOR, 2.41; 95% CI, 1.09–5.32; P = .030). Among women tested for BV, presence of BV coinfection (defined as Nugent score ≥ 7) was associated with reduced moxifloxacin cure (OR, 0.25; 95% CI, .10–.62; P = .003; Supplementary Table 5); however, this association was not significant after adjustment for year and concurrent chlamydial infection (aOR, 0.53; 95% CI, .27–1.06; P = .075).

Preliminary Impacts of ParC-S83 RGT on Moxifloxacin Use and Efficacy

Implementation of the ParC-S83 assay was associated with a decrease in moxifloxacin use from 60.3% (n = 482/800; 95% CI, 56.8%–63.7%) in the year prior to its introduction, to 49.2% (n = 438/890; 95% CI, 45.9%–52.6%) in the year postintroduction (P < .0001; Table 4).

In its first 12 months, ParC-S83 RGT was associated with significant improvements in moxifloxacin efficacy, achieving 89.2% cure (n = 116/130; 95% CI, 82.6%–94.0%) in ParC-S83 wild type infections compared to 79.3% (n = 238/300; 95% CI, 74.3%–83.8%) seen prior to the introduction of the assay (P = .013; Table 4). However, not all moxifloxacin-treated infections had confirmed ParC-S83 wild type; moxifloxacin was often used for macrolide-resistant infections with indeterminate ParC-S83 results (caused by factors such as insufficient sample load, presence of non-S83I ParC mutation, or inhibitors in samples). In total, there were 111 infections with indeterminate ParC-S83 results that were eligible for efficacy analysis, and 79.3% of these were cured with moxifloxacin (n = 88/111; 95% CI, 70.6%–86.4%; Table 4). Consequently, pooled cure among all moxifloxacin-treated infections following introduction of the ParC-S83 assay was 84.7% (n = 204/241; 95% CI, 79.5%–89.0%), a nonsignificant change from 2023 (P = .119; Table 4).

In this final year, MSHC experienced unprecedented client attendance, exceeding service capacity, and the limited appointments were prioritized for clients with persistent symptoms at TOC. Loss to follow-up for TOC increased from approximately 30% in 2015–2023 to 43.4% in the 12 months after introduction of the ParC-S83 assay (P < .001), and likely biased data toward treatment failure.

DISCUSSION

We examined trends in use and efficacy of moxifloxacin for macrolide-resistant M. genitalium infections at a large sexual health service over a decade. The proportion of M. genitalium infections treated with moxifloxacin increased 10-fold, from < 7% in 2015 to 60% in 2023, consistent with the rise in macrolide-resistant M. genitalium in Australia, particularly among MSM, who comprise a high proportion of MSHC's clientele. Concurrently, moxifloxacin efficacy for M. genitalium infections declined from 100% in 2015 to approximately 80% in 2023, reflecting a rise in fluoroquinolone resistance in our population over the past decade. Preliminary data suggest ParC-S83 RGT reduces moxifloxacin use and can restore moxifloxacin cure at our service to approximately 90% in ParC-S83 wild type infections, demonstrating potential for further improvements in antimicrobial use and treatment outcomes with this strategy.

The increase in moxifloxacin use at MSHC over the past decade is concordant with the well-documented rise in macrolide resistance within our region. Importantly, our data support estimates that as many as 60% of M. genitalium infections are now macrolide-resistant and heavily reliant on moxifloxacin to achieve cure [4]. Our study population reflects the known epidemiology of antimicrobial-resistant M. genitalium; principally, a significant proportion identified as MSM, with these individuals demonstrating high levels of macrolide resistance in M. genitalium [24–27]. Although we were unable to explicitly capture prior antibiotic consumption, the high prevalence of STI coinfection, coupled with previous MSHC data [12, 21], also suggest high rates of prior STIs and antibiotic consumption likely contributed to macrolide resistance in our population [28, 29]. Importantly, our geographic and economic proximity to epicenters of antimicrobial resistance has significant implications for M. genitalium management in Australia, and particularly at MSHC, which receives frequent attendance by clients whose infections were acquired in Japan, China, and Korea [3, 4, 30]. Promisingly, our data appear to support evidence of a deceleration in the rise of macrolide resistance in our community [4], with more modest increases in moxifloxacin use observed in the latter years of the study period compared to earlier years. This may be an early indication of a plateau in macrolide resistance in our population that could have been aided by national adoption of macrolide RGT for M. genitalium over the past decade.

In contrast, we report a rapid and significant decline in moxifloxacin efficacy for M. genitalium infection over the past decade, extending the findings of Li et al in 2017 [8]. Our efficacy data align with sequencing studies from MSHC samples reporting a rise in ParC-S83I mutation from 15.3% of macrolide-resistant infections in 2016–2018 [31] to 27.5% in 2019–2020 [14], with > 60% moxifloxacin failure observed among ParC-S83I infections [14]. Our preliminary analysis suggests ParC-S83 RGT was associated with significant reduction in moxifloxacin use and improvement in cure. Importantly, 89% of ParC-S83 wild type infections were successfully cured with moxifloxacin, providing preliminary estimates that are in line with prior predictions [12]. The reduction in overall moxifloxacin use with ParC-S83 RGT was more modest than expected, as a result of the higher than expected proportion of infections returning indeterminate ParC assay results. An indeterminate result, mainly due to low-load infection, was associated with moxifloxacin failure in approximately 20% of cases, likely reflecting the presence of undetected S83I mutant infections in these low-load infections. Work on optimizing this assay is ongoing. Lastly, efficacy estimates after the introduction of the assay were also unexpectedly impacted by reduced clinic access resulting in increased loss to follow-up in 2024, during which time TOC attendance was prioritized for clients who remained symptomatic and were more likely to have failed treatment [32]. Despite these limitations, this intervention restored moxifloxacin efficacy to levels that had not been seen at MSHC since 2017.

Although this study demonstrated benefits from the ParC-RGT strategy, we cannot recommend this approach without considering the limited antibiotic options available for patients with ParC-S83I M. genitalium infections. Novel regimens (such as 14 days of minocycline and metronidazole) have shown promise [20] but often involve prolonged durations of therapy and higher rates of adverse effects [33]. Clearly, more investment and research are needed to provide clinicians and patients with safe, effective, and accessible alternatives to moxifloxacin.

This study presents contemporary, longitudinal data for moxifloxacin use and efficacy for M. genitalium infection, in a setting with highly standardized care. We also present the first data on use of ParC-S83 RGT in M. genitalium management, with its capacity to further individualize antimicrobial use and improve cure. While we are limited by the retrospective nature of this study in understanding the precise causes of declining moxifloxacin efficacy, it is likely that increasing ParC-S83I mutants are the dominant driver. Further development of fluoroquinolone resistance-guided strategies would benefit from consideration of gyrA mutations, and understanding the contributions of antibiotic consumption and de novo mutations to resistance. Consistent with previous studies performed at our clinic [12, 21, 22], we observed frequent STI and BV coinfection in our study population, limiting the generalizability of our data. We found moxifloxacin efficacy was greater in cases with chlamydial coinfection compared to M. genitalium monoinfection; conversely, there was a nonsignificant decrease in moxifloxacin cure observed in women with BV coinfection compared to M. genitalium monoinfection after adjustment for covariates. These associations are of unknown significance and warrant further investigation. As discussed, high levels of antimicrobial resistance in our study population limits the generalizability of our data; however, we believe our findings have broad clinical relevance, particularly to the Western Pacific region. We appreciate resistance assays are not universally accessible, and are cognizant of systemic inequities that undermine antimicrobial access and use across populations [34].

In summary, widespread macrolide resistance has made moxifloxacin integral to M. genitalium management, particularly in the Western Pacific. Our observed decrease in moxifloxacin efficacy in Melbourne, Australia highlights the value of fluoroquinolone-resistance–guided therapy in populations with high rates of resistance, and investment in new agents and antimicrobial stewardship strategies.
