Falcotentorial dAVF with Unusual Venous Drainage Presenting with Obstructive Hydrocephalus: A Case Report and Literature Review of Endoscopic Third Ventriculostomy Followed by Staged Transarterial Embolization
Authors: Yusuke Otsu, Hideaki Shigematsu, Kohei Miyagi, Takayasu Ando, Sosho Kajiwara, Yukihiko Nakamura, Kiyohiko Sakata, Takayuki Kawano, Shuichi Tanoue, Masaru Hirohata, Motohiro Morioka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to report a case of falcotentorial dural arteriovenous fistula (dAVF) presenting with obstructive hydrocephalus caused by an unusual venous drainage pattern and to describe a staged treatment strategy.Case PresentationA 38-year-old man presented with photopsia and was found to have obstructive hydrocephalus. MRI demonstrated compression of the cerebral aqueduct by a dilated draining vein. DSA revealed a falcotentorial dAVF with deep venous reflux, supplied by multiple arterial feeders. Venous drainage occurred through a dilated trans-mesencephalic venous pathway, which compressed the cerebral aqueduct, resulting in obstructive hydrocephalus. Because hydrocephalus was present and embolization was considered to carry a risk of worsening obstruction of the cerebral aqueduct, endoscopic third ventriculostomy (ETV) was performed as the initial treatment. After improvement of hydrocephalus, staged transarterial embolization was successfully performed. At 6-month follow-up, DSA showed a small recurrent shunt without clinical deterioration, and the patient remained symptom-free.ConclusionFalcotentorial dAVF can cause obstructive hydrocephalus depending on venous drainage patterns. When deep venous structures compress the cerebral aqueduct, careful evaluation of venous anatomy and a staged treatment approach, including initial ETV, may be useful for safe management.

Introduction

Dural arteriovenous fistulas (dAVFs) are abnormal connections between dural arteries and venous structures. Symptoms of dAVFs vary depending on the location of the shunt and the pattern of venous drainage.1) In cases with deep venous drainage, venous pressure can increase and lead to neurological symptoms.2) Falcotentorial dAVFs develop at a deep location near the junction of the falx and tentorium.3) These lesions often have several feeding arteries and complex venous drainage pathways involving deep veins. Owing to this complex anatomy, falcotentorial dAVFs are sometimes difficult to diagnose and treat, and careful assessment of venous drainage is essential before intervention. Clinical symptoms of falcotentorial dAVFs are mainly related to venous drainage rather than arterial supply.4) Changes in venous flow can produce different symptoms, and the venous anatomy may strongly influence both presentation and treatment strategy. Understanding these venous features is therefore important when planning treatment. Many falcotentorial dAVFs are non-sinus–type lesions, and transarterial embolization (TAE) using liquid embolic materials is commonly selected as the primary treatment strategy.5)

In this report, we present a case of falcotentorial dAVF with distinctive venous drainage that caused obstructive hydrocephalus. The patient was treated with a staged approach using endoscopic third ventriculostomy (ETV) followed by TAE. We describe the venous anatomy and explain the reasoning behind this treatment strategy.

Case Presentation

A 38-year-old man presented to a local ophthalmology clinic with photopsia. Increased intracranial pressure was suspected on funduscopic examination, and he was referred to our hospital for further evaluation. He had no relevant past medical history. MRI showed obstructive hydrocephalus. A dilated venous structure compressing the cerebral aqueduct was observed on heavily T2-weighted constructive interference in steady state (CISS) images (Fig. 1A). DSA revealed a falcotentorial dAVF with deep venous reflux, consistent with Cognard type IV (Fig. 1B–1G). The shunt was supplied by multiple feeders, including bilateral dural branches of the posterior cerebral arteries, a dural branch of the left superior cerebellar artery, the hypoglossal branches of the bilateral ascending pharyngeal arteries (APAs), the posterior convexity branch of the right middle meningeal artery (MMA), and the meningohypophyseal trunk. The straight sinus was occluded. Venous drainage from the shunt descended through a dilated draining vein corresponding to a trans-mesencephalic venous pathway, coursing through the mesencephalon, as demonstrated on a fusion image of MRI CISS and reconstructed DSA maximum intensity projection (MIP) images, which compressed the cerebral aqueduct, and then ascended to the vein of Galen with reflux into the deep venous system and cortical veins (Figs. 1H and 2).

On admission, the patient was alert but complained of headache and nausea. Neurological examination revealed no focal deficits. Funduscopic examination demonstrated papilledema, suggesting increased intracranial pressure. MRI demonstrated marked mechanical compression of the cerebral aqueduct by a dilated draining vein. Therefore, immediate relief of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) obstruction was considered necessary. Given this mechanism, ETV was performed 2 days after admission, as it provided a definitive solution for CSF diversion in the setting of aqueductal obstruction. External ventricular drainage was considered; however, it would provide only temporary CSF diversion without addressing the underlying obstruction. Ventriculoperitoneal shunting was also not selected, as CSF circulation could be restored by ETV alone, in addition to the potential risks associated with shunt placement, including infection. During ETV, the cerebral aqueduct was observed to be compressed by the dilated trans-mesencephalic venous pathway, and a dilated draining vein was also seen along the anterior surface of the cerebellum (Fig. 3A and 3B). CSF diversion was achieved without complications (Fig. 3C and 3D). Follow-up MRI performed 1 month after ETV showed improvement of hydrocephalus (Fig. 3E and 3F), accompanied by improvement of the patient’s headache and visual disturbance. After improvement of hydrocephalus was confirmed and the patient’s condition had stabilized, TAE was subsequently performed under stable clinical conditions. Although the patient remained neurologically stable after ETV, the lesion was classified as Cognard type IV and therefore carried an inherent risk of hemorrhage. In addition, the dilated draining vein causing aqueductal compression may reflect ongoing hemodynamic stress within the dAVF system. The 1st TAE was performed via the hypoglossal branch of the left APA using liquid embolic materials. A 90-cm 6-Fr FUBUKI Dilator (Asahi Intecc, Aichi, Japan) was introduced via the right femoral artery and positioned in the left common carotid artery. A coaxial system consisting of a 130-cm Guidepost (Asahi Intecc) and a 165-cm 1.5-Fr Marathon (Medtronic, Irvine, CA, USA) was used for arterial catheterization to selectively access the left APA hypoglossal branch (Fig. 4A and 4B). TAE was performed with Onyx 18 (Medtronic) at the distal segment of the APA hypoglossal branch, resulting in a reduction of shunt flow (Fig. 4C and 4D). A 2nd TAE was then performed through the posterior convexity branch of the right MMA using the same catheter system (Fig. 4E–4H). Although residual shunt flow was observed immediately after the second session, follow-up angiography performed 1 month later demonstrated spontaneous thrombosis and complete disappearance of the shunt flow (Fig. 4I and 4J). The postoperative course was uneventful, with no neurological deficits. The patient remained symptom-free after treatment. Follow-up MRI confirmed sustained improvement of hydrocephalus, and the patient had a modified Rankin Scale score of 0, with no recurrence of symptoms. The patient is currently being followed at another institution. There has been no recurrence of hydrocephalus; however, follow-up DSA performed 6 months after treatment showed a small recurrent shunt without clinical deterioration. The patient has remained clinically stable for at least 9 months after treatment based on available information, although detailed imaging follow-up was limited due to transfer to another institution.

Discussion

Obstructive hydrocephalus associated with falcotentorial dAVF is uncommon, and only a small number of cases have been reported.6–9) To clarify the underlying mechanisms and treatment considerations, we reviewed previously reported cases of falcotentorial dAVF complicated by obstructive hydrocephalus and compared them with the present case (Table 1).

In our review, obstructive hydrocephalus was caused by direct compression of the cerebral aqueduct by a dilated draining vein, sometimes accompanied by a varix, in 4 of 5 reported cases (80%).6,8,9) In the remaining case, hydrocephalus was related to cerebellar edema due to impaired venous outflow rather than direct compression of the cerebral aqueduct.7) These findings suggest that obstruction of the cerebral aqueduct by dilated venous structures is the most frequent mechanism of hydrocephalus in falcotentorial dAVF, while venous congestion–related parenchymal changes may also play a role in some patients. Regarding treatment, TAE was selected as the initial intervention in 4 of the 5 reported cases (80%), and embolization alone resulted in improvement of hydrocephalus in these patients.6,7,9) However, 1 reported case experienced worsening obstructive hydrocephalus after TAE and required urgent ETV.8) This clinical course suggests that embolization can, in selected cases, worsen hydrocephalus, possibly due to acute hemodynamic alterations in venous outflow, such as rapid thrombosis or transient enlargement of the draining vein.10,11) In the present case, obstructive hydrocephalus was already established at presentation, and MRI demonstrated marked direct compression of the cerebral aqueduct by a dilated draining vein. Although embolization was expected to reduce shunt flow, the timing and extent of subsequent decompression of the aqueduct could not be predicted with certainty. More importantly, given the presence of established obstructive hydrocephalus with significant aqueductal compression, immediate relief of CSF obstruction was considered necessary, and it was not appropriate to wait for potential delayed improvement following embolization. Therefore, we selected ETV as the initial treatment to promptly restore CSF circulation before definitive endovascular therapy. ETV was performed prior to embolization to relieve the hydrocephalus. Even if the shunt had been occluded with Onyx (Medtronic), the dilated vein might not have shrunk immediately, and the aqueduct could have remained obstructed. For this reason, we chose to perform ETV first. After the hydrocephalus was stabilized, staged TAE was performed safely. Although ETV was effective for immediate CSF diversion, the underlying dAVF represents the primary pathology and carries a risk of hemorrhage, particularly in Cognard type IV lesions. Therefore, definitive treatment should ideally be performed as early as possible after stabilization of hydrocephalus.

In the present case, the interval before TAE was longer than might be recommended in an ideal setting. In retrospect, earlier definitive treatment might have been preferable to reduce the potential hemorrhagic risk. Based on this experience, we would consider performing definitive endovascular treatment at an earlier stage following CSF diversion in similar future cases, provided that the patient’s condition allows.

Another important feature of this case was the involvement of the trans-mesencephalic venous pathway as the primary drainage route. The venous structure observed in this case was interpreted as a trans-mesencephalic venous pathway. Previous anatomical studies have demonstrated that mesencephalic veins exhibit considerable variability and that their nomenclature is not standardized.12–14) Therefore, the term “trans-mesencephalic venous pathway” is used here as a descriptive expression to indicate a venous channel traversing or surrounding the mesencephalon, rather than a formally established anatomical entity. In the present case, reconstructed MIP images clearly demonstrated that the draining vein coursed around and partially through the midbrain. Although the precise relationship between this vein and the lateral mesencephalic vein could not be directly confirmed on imaging, the angiographic findings suggest a close anatomical association between these structures, indicating a complex connection between deep venous pathways and tentorial drainage.13,14) In this patient, dilation of the trans-mesencephalic venous pathway resulted in direct compression of the cerebral aqueduct, leading to obstructive hydrocephalus. This venous drainage pattern differs from most previously reported cases, in which venous varix formation or enlarged Galenic drainage was responsible for obstruction of the cerebral aqueduct. Careful assessment of venous anatomy was therefore essential for understanding the pathophysiology and for selecting an appropriate treatment strategy. Because the patient was transferred to another institution, detailed long-term imaging follow-up data were limited; however, no clinical deterioration has been reported to date. This case has several limitations. First, the interval before definitive endovascular treatment was longer than might be recommended in an ideal clinical setting. Although this delay was influenced in part by institutional and logistical factors, it may not be appropriate to generalize such a treatment interval, particularly given the hemorrhagic risk associated with Cognard type IV lesions. Second, detailed long-term imaging follow-up was limited due to transfer of care to another institution.

Conclusion

Falcotentorial dAVFs can cause obstructive hydrocephalus depending on their venous drainage patterns. When deep venous structures, such as the trans-mesencephalic venous pathway, compress the cerebral aqueduct, a staged treatment approach, including initial ETV, may be considered in selected cases to achieve prompt CSF diversion. However, because the underlying dAVF represents the primary pathology and carries a hemorrhagic risk, particularly in high-grade lesions, definitive endovascular treatment should be performed as early as possible after stabilization, and prolonged deferral should be avoided.
