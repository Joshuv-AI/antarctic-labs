Repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation treatment for obsessive–compulsive disorder
Authors: Monika Klírová, Andrej Stančák, Tomáš Novák, Odile A van den Heuvel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurpose of reviewRepetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) and its modifications have emerged as promising interventions for obsessive–compulsive disorder (OCD). Although growing evidence supports the safety and clinical efficacy of rTMS, substantial heterogeneity in study designs and stimulation protocols continues to hinder the development of uniform, consensus-based guidelines.Recent findingsLarge-scale clinical trials capable of definitively establishing the effectiveness of existing rTMS protocols are still lacking. However, available findings from smaller randomized controlled trials and (network) meta-analyses suggest moderate efficacy for various rTMS targets and protocols. Accelerated protocols may shorten time to response, though comparative evidence of their efficacy remains limited. In the absence of clear clinical predictors, research is increasingly focused on personalized treatment protocols and biomarker-informed prediction of response. Also, integrated treatment approaches, with accumulating evidence supporting the combination of rTMS with psychotherapy or pharmacotherapy, seem promising.SummaryrTMS shows promise as an adjunctive treatment for OCD. Large, well designed clinical trials are required to establish the most effective target and stimulation parameters and to verify long-term outcomes. Personalized targeting guided by neural predictors, as well as the integration of rTMS into broader therapeutic strategies, may enhance treatment efficacy.Keywords: combined treatment, deep transcranial magnetic stimulation, obsessive–compulsive disorder, prediction, repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation

INTRODUCTION

Obsessive–compulsive disorder (OCD) is a disabling condition characterized by intrusive thoughts and repetitive behaviours. Latest global surveys estimate a lifetime prevalence of 4.1% and an annual prevalence of 3% [1]. Although many patients present with mild symptoms, a substantial proportion experience moderate to severe impairment. OCD arises from an interplay of genetic [2] and environmental factors [3], with neuroimaging studies elucidating a key role for cortico-striato-thalamo-cortical (CSTC) and interconnected circuit abnormalities [4]. Understanding these mechanisms guides the development of novel treatments [5].

First-line treatments, including cognitive–behavioural therapy (CBT) together with exposure and response prevention (ERP) or selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, provide adequate relief for only about half of patients [6]. For those with refractory symptoms, next-choice treatments, including clomipramine or antipsychotics, or in severe cases, invasive approaches like deep-brain stimulation [5] or ablative neurosurgery [7], may be considered. The significant treatment gap between first-line treatment options and invasive strategies underscores the urgent need for additional therapeutic options.

Repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) is a noninvasive brain stimulation technique with demonstrated efficacy in several neurological [8] and psychiatric disorders [8,9], including OCD. Its strongest evidence in psychiatry is for depression [10], with emerging data supporting its benefits across diverse populations [11–14] and its potential cost-effectiveness in the treatment of depression [15]. Despite its therapeutic potential, access remains limited in many countries. It is often restricted to severe, treatment-resistant depression (TRD) [16], highlighting the need for broadened clinical research and improved implementation, including for indications with level A evidence.

This review summarizes recent randomized control trials (RCTs) and meta-analyses on rTMS for OCD, discusses current limitations and outlines promising directions for future research.

OVERALL EFFICACY OF REPETITIVE TRANSCRANIAL MAGNETIC STIMULATION

The treatment effects of rTMS and associated transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) protocols in OCD were evaluated across a large number of RCTs published between 2001 and 2026. Table 1 summarizes selected meta-analyses examining overall and/or specific effects. Across recent meta-analyses, rTMS showed moderate efficacy, with effect sizes between active and sham rTMS ranging from 0.5 to 0.9 [17–19,20▪▪] and symptom reductions on the Yale-Brown Obsessive Compulsive Scale (Y-BOCS) just below the minimal clinically important difference of 4.9 points [21]. While all meta-analyses shown in Table 1 analysed RCTs, the evidence regarding the efficacy of rTMS is limited by the inclusion of trials with small sample sizes and an elevated risk of bias due to issues with allocation concealment, blinding, and randomization.

STIMULATION PARAMETERS

Against this background, existing guidance for rTMS protocols for OCD treatment is also inconsistent [8,9,22,23,24▪], which may also reflect substantial heterogeneity in stimulation parameters across studies, including differences in cortical target, frequency, dosing, and related factors [18,25,26]. The optimal combination of these parameters for individual patients or OCD subtypes remains unclear.

Cortical target and coil selection

The therapeutic effect of rTMS in OCD relies on modulating neural excitability and inducing neuroplastic changes [27] in cortical regions and their connections within the CSTC circuits [28] (Fig. 1). Traditional protocols target prefrontal cortex (PFC) areas, namely the right or bilateral dorsolateral PFC (DLPFC), orbitofrontal cortex (OFC), supplementary motor area (SMA) and pre-SMA (hereinafter referred to as SMA), using figure-of-eight coils, with several meta-analyses supporting the efficacy of some of these targets [19,25,29].

More recent work has focused on deeper structures, specifically the dorsomedial PFC (DMPFC) and anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), through deep TMS (dTMS) administered following symptom provocation, and growing evidence supports the efficacy of this approach [30▪,31,32]. These dTMS protocols [specifically those approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA)] employ H7 or double-cone coils, and recent meta-analyses in selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor-resistant [33] and treatment-resistant OCD (ROCD) [32] suggest that H7-based dTMS may serve as a promising treatment for this condition. Nevertheless, direct evidence demonstrating its superiority over conventional rTMS is lacking, and head-to-head comparisons between conventional figure-of-eight coils (targeting the DLPFC or SMA) and dTMS (targeting the DMPFC/ACC) have yet to be conducted.

Overall, further support for rTMS and dTMS comes from a network meta-analysis of 34 RCTs (1089 patients) reporting efficacy for targets including the bilateral DLPFC, DMPFC/ACC, and right DLPFC [34▪▪], and a systematic review of 47 studies endorsing rTMS as an adjunctive therapy, particularly when targeting the SMA and DLPFC [35].

Stimulation frequency

Stimulation frequency is another key determinant of rTMS efficacy alongside target location [18,25,29]. In OCD, frequency selection is guided by CSTC circuits dysfunction [28]. In general, low-frequency rTMS (LF-rTMS, 1 Hz) and continuous theta-burst stimulation (cTBS) are used to inhibit hyperactive regions (e.g. right DLPFC or SMA), whereas high-frequency rTMS (HF-rTMS, ≥10 Hz) and intermittent theta-burst stimulation (iTBS) aim to activate underactive structures, notably the left DLPFC.

Theta-burst stimulation (TBS) offers a shorter, more energy-efficient stimulation duration, and initial OCD studies point to its potential therapeutic benefit. Although a recent RCT [36▪] showed that the efficacy of cTBS and LF-rTMS, both targeting the DLPFC, is comparable, evidence across multiple combined cTBS studies suggests a lack of statistically significant decrease in OCD symptoms [37,38▪▪] (Table 1).

Meta-analyses report positive effects for LF-rTMS targeting the right DLPFC [18,25] or SMA [18,25], and for HF-rTMS applied to the bilateral DLPFC [18,25] and DMPFC/ACC [18,32], although no clear consensus exists on the optimal target–frequency combination (Table 1 and Fig. 2). Current guidelines differ: Australian/New Zealand guidelines [9] recommend high-frequency dTMS of the DMPFC/ACC, whereas European guidelines [8] support LF-rTMS of the right DLPFC for OCD treatment. A recent Dutch–Belgian consensus [23], as well as Canadian and international OCD guidelines [24▪], recommend a broader range of protocols targeting different cortical regions as adjunctive treatment for ROCD (Table 1). Also, newer evidence from network meta-analysis of 41 RCTs in 1333 patients [39▪] in broader clinical samples suggests that both LF-rTMS and HF-rTMS are associated with symptom improvement not only in OCD but also across anxiety disorders and posttraumatic stress disorder.

Number of pulses and treatment duration

rTMS effectiveness also depends on the number of pulses per session, the total number of sessions, and the inter-session interval. A recent meta-regression in TRD [40] found that treatment response was associated with the total number of sessions, but not the total number of pulses, suggesting that session count is a key driver of outcome. However, OCD research suggests that traditional rTMS protocols involving longer sessions and fewer overall treatment sessions predict superior clinical outcomes [17].

Analyses of 23 RCT studies suggest that accelerated TMS protocols, including twice-daily HF-rTMS or accelerated TBS involving multiple sessions per day, that are already FDA-approved for depression, can enhance efficacy and reduce overall treatment time [41]. Using these accelerated protocols can lower the treatment time per remission [42], maintaining safety while improving treatment accessibility. A recent meta-analysis of seven RCTs [43▪▪] reported that accelerated TMS protocols (compared to sham) significantly reduce OCD and depressive symptoms and are generally well tolerated, though follow-up periods remain short, and little is known about relapse risk. However, most evidence for accelerated protocols comes from studies on depression rather than OCD.

Newer combined protocols, such as iTBS plus HF-rTMS within accelerated dTMS, also show some potential, with a retrospective study of 378 patients reporting symptom improvement [30▪], though the evidence remains preliminary.

Stimulation intensity

As far as stimulation intensity and its role in rTMS efficacy are concerned, evidence suggests that fixed percentages of the resting motor threshold (RMT), commonly used in clinical practice, can produce variable or subthreshold electric fields in nonmotor and deeper targets, potentially limiting therapeutic effects [44]. However, a recent meta-analysis reported no significant differences in efficacy across various RMTs, suggesting that intensity alone does not fully determine outcome [17]. Therefore, to achieve consistent and effective stimulation, rTMS parameters such as intensity, frequency, duration, and total number of pulses and sessions must be considered together as interconnected components that collectively define the overall dose. This integrated approach, which accounts for the interrelationships among parameters, may help optimize clinical outcomes [45▪].

Stimulation combined with symptom provocation

Some trials have incorporated symptom provocation prior to stimulation to activate the stimulated network and enhance neuromodulatory effects by optimal target engagement [46]. However, RCTs directly comparing protocols with and without provocation are lacking. Nonetheless, a recent meta-analysis of 39 RCTs in OCD [20▪▪] found larger effect sizes for active versus sham rTMS when symptom provocation was included, but this enhancement was modest and not statistically significant, based on only four trials (Table 1), underscoring the need for further research.

EFFECT DURATION

Most rTMS studies in OCD have focused on short-term outcomes, with follow-up periods typically limited to a few weeks [29], making it difficult to assess the long-term durability of treatment effects. However, recent evidence suggests that the effects of rTMS, with sustained reductions in OCD symptoms, may persist for up to 3 months [38▪▪].

POPULATION CHARACTERISTICS

In general, findings suggest that baseline demographic characteristics and OCD profile do not significantly influence rTMS efficacy [17], although conclusions are limited by small, heterogeneous studies [47].

Age

Age effects on rTMS effectiveness in OCD have been investigated across diverse populations [38▪▪,48▪]. Data on children are particularly limited, although early findings appear promising [13,14]. Also in adolescents, where rTMS was recently approved for TRD [49], the evidence for its use in OCD remains limited: a retrospective uncontrolled study combining LF-rTMS with fluoxetine reported high response (85%) and remission rates (32.5%) in first-episode patients [48▪], while reviews highlight considerable heterogeneity, limited overall evidence [26,50], and unresolved neurodevelopmental risks [50].

In OCD adults, rTMS can generally be considered an effective treatment. While a recent study indicates that middle age and male gender may be associated with a better response to accelerated dTMS [30▪], no meta-analyses to date have demonstrated that age influences rTMS outcomes [38▪▪].

There is limited data on older adults with OCD, but data from other disorders suggest that rTMS may be effective and well tolerated in elderly populations, but requires appropriate monitoring [11,12].

Obsessive–compulsive disorder symptom characteristics

OCD is a heterogeneous disorder, with multiple dimensions (obsessions, compulsions) and symptom types (including contamination, checking and symmetry). Subtypes likely reflect distinct neuropsychological and pathophysiological profiles, providing a rationale for circuit-informed and individualized interventions [51].

Evidence on symptom-specific responses to rTMS is limited. However, current research suggests that rTMS is more effective in reducing obsessions than compulsions [19]. In addition, a recent meta-regression analysis [38▪▪] showed that greater baseline OCD severity predicted a reduced treatment effect of rTMS (linear regression coefficient = −0.46 (95% CI −0.88 to −0.04).

Comorbidities

Research generally supports a beneficial effect of rTMS in OCD on comorbid depressive symptoms [17,25,38▪▪] with evidence also suggesting reductions in comorbid anxiety [17], [38▪▪]. Notably, greater reductions in depressive symptoms are linked to stronger treatment responses in OCD, suggesting overlapping mechanisms [17,25].

Protocol-specific effects appear relevant. Recent research indicates that LF-rTMS and cTBS over the right DLPFC improve comorbid depression and anxiety [36▪], whereas dTMS targeting the DMPFC/ACC with H7 coils reduces OCD symptoms independently of depressive improvement [31], suggesting symptom-domain specificity. Additionally, recent meta-analysis on accelerated TMS has shown efficacy for depressive symptoms, with uncertain benefits for anxiety [43▪▪].

Sleep disturbances represent an additional clinically relevant factor. They are common in OCD and correlate with symptom severity [52]. Poor sleep may reduce the response to rTMS combined with psychotherapy [53], emphasizing the need to assess and improve sleep in rTMS studies.

Treatment resistance

No significant differences in treatment outcomes after rTMS have been observed between ROCD and nonresistant patients [17], suggesting rTMS remains effective in those with prior treatment failure. rTMS, including dTMS, may be a promising adjunctive treatment for ROCD, with meta-analyses showing moderate effects and higher response rates in real versus sham rTMS, especially with LF-rTMS over the DLPFC, SMA, or OFC [47]. Consistent with these findings, a recent RCT in ROCD found that LF-rTMS over the right SMA significantly reduced Y-BOCS scores compared to sham [54], supporting SMA-targeted rTMS for persistent symptoms.

Furthermore, an interesting additional perspective emerges from rTMS research in TRD, which suggests that response prediction using baseline clinical data, combined with structural MRI, functional MRI (fMRI), or electroencephalography, might improve clinical outcomes, for instance, by enabling personalized treatment parameters and patient stratification [55]. While direct evidence of these approaches in ROCD is limited, they may offer a promising direction for this population [55].

FUTURE DIRECTIONS

Given the limited predictive value of demographic and baseline clinical factors for OCD improvement after rTMS, patient-specific characteristics and biomarkers might inform more targeted, personalized treatment approaches. These could include behavioural predictors, such as cognitive flexibility [56], or neuronal predictors [35] derived from structural MRI and fMRI-based functional connectivity [57▪,58▪], which might enable patient stratification to symptom-related rTMS protocols and more precise targeting [5,59], to maximise clinical response. Similarly, combining rTMS with complementary interventions, particularly psychotherapy [35,59], appears to be a promising future direction.

Neural predictors of treatment response

Functional connectivity analyses from resting-state fMRI or task-based fMRI are pivotal methods for understanding neural mechanisms and guiding rTMS interventions. Evidence from resting-state fMRI studies shows that rTMS can modulate pathological connectivity; for example, SMA-targeted stimulation reduced functional connectivity within the CSTC circuit, which correlated with clinical improvement [60].

In the context of research on neural predictors of treatment response, three analyses [57▪,58▪,61▪] based on the same randomized ROCD cohort (58–61 patients) revealed that pretreatment neural activity, such as right amygdala activity during symptom provocation [57▪] and task-based network activation during planning or inhibitory control [58▪], can predict response to combined HF-rTMS and ERP, with patterns varying by stimulation target. Beyond these predictors, symptom improvement was closely linked to task-specific modulation of brain networks [61▪], highlighting how rTMS, when paired with ERP, reshapes neural activity to drive clinical benefit.

Although functional abnormalities in OCD are intensively studied, structural predictors of rTMS response have been less explored. A recent dTMS study [62▪] reported larger grey matter volumes in the left temporal lobe and medial temporal structures among responders. Although these findings suggest potential predictive value, they remain preliminary and require further validation.

Combinatory treatments

Recent research highlights the synergistic potential of combining rTMS with psychotherapy or pharmacotherapy in OCD. Initial evidence indicates that TMS-induced neuroplasticity, particularly when stimulation is paired with symptom-related exposure tasks, such as symptom provocation or ERP (a form of exposure-based psychotherapy) preceding or directly following stimulation, can enhance clinical outcomes [59]. Moreover, a recent RCT showed that rTMS combined with ERP led to 57.4% response rate, accompanied by target-specific neural modulation, with DLPFC stimulation affecting planning-related activity and SMA stimulation influencing error-relevant activation in clinical responders [61▪]. Supporting these findings, a recent meta-analysis of 44 OCD trials [20▪▪] (Table 1) suggests that symptom provocation may enhance clinical response to rTMS.

Similar benefits are seen when rTMS is combined with CBT, including exposure-based techniques. In an RCT of 80 patients, CBT combined with SMA-targeted rTMS produced greater symptom reduction and enhanced fronto-striatal and cerebello-prefrontal connectivity than CBT with sham stimulation [63▪].

rTMS has also been evaluated in combination with pharmacotherapy – in adolescents with first-episode OCD, LF-rTMS combined with fluoxetine resulted in higher response and remission rates than either intervention alone [48▪].

Collectively, these findings underscore the promise of combined rTMS interventions for OCD and support their feasibility; however, replication in well controlled studies is needed to establish their robustness, generalizability, and cost-effectiveness.

CONCLUSION

Recent evidence confirms that rTMS is safe and moderately effective for OCD. However, variability in study methodologies, stimulation protocols, and patient responses makes it difficult to establish clear, consensus-based guidelines. Future research should focus on large, rigorous trials to validate existing protocols (including head-to-head comparisons of stimulation protocols), identify patient subgroups most likely to benefit from rTMS, and develop reliable biomarkers to support personalized treatment strategies. Evaluating rTMS in combination with other treatment approaches is also important for optimizing outcomes, establishing cost-effectiveness, and clarifying its role in OCD management.
