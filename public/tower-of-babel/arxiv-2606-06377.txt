A new family of distances over partially ordered sets
Astrid A. Olave

Abstract
Order theory is increasingly relevant in applications where data is naturally structured as a partially ordered set (poset), often requiring meaningful notions of distance over posets. In this paper, we introduce a new family of extended metrics on path-connected and fence-connected posets that do not require additional structure. Unlike many existing distances, these metrics are not induced by valuations, but instead arise as a type of shortest-path distance determined by both path length and the number of alternations. For discrete posets, we show that these metrics converge to a type of shortest-fence metric. Our main result establishes that these metrics characterize most discrete path-connected posets up to isomorphism, and up to duality for modular posets. Finally, we prove that this family defines interleaving distances when posets are viewed as thin categories.

In many real-world settings, elements may be comparable only in certain respects, and partial orders offer a mathematical framework to model such situations. In particular, many problems require quantifying the dissimilarity between data points, that is, defining a notion of distance between elements of a poset. Several metrics defined on posets have been proposed for this purpose. For instance:

In statistics, graphs that encode structure among a set of variables define a poset. The distance between two graphs is given by the length of a shortest path between them on the poset of graphs

In environmental science and chemistry, chemicals can be partially ordered according to a set of attributes, in what is known as the partial ranking method

A partition of a set

The degree of kinship between individuals is crucial in legal contexts such as inheritance rules, prohibited degrees of marriage, and legal adoption. This degree of kinship can be modeled as a distance function defined on upper semilattices

•

In information theory, a generalization of Shannon entropy can be formulated in the context of lattices. Entropies on lattices are defined as a type of lower valuation; as such, they induce metrics on the lattices, thereby opening the possibility of new axiomatizations of entropies starting from axiomatizations of metrics on lattices

While several metrics have been proposed in these contexts, many of them rely on additional structural assumptions on the underlying poset such as the existence of a maximal element, modularity, or a lattice structure. These assumptions restrict the class of posets to which such metrics can be applied when analyzing data. Consequently, it is desirable to develop more general metrics that do not rely on these structural conditions, as this allows the analysis of a broader range of ordered sets and highlights different structural properties of the underlying poset

Motivated by the need for distance measures that apply to general posets, we introduce two new families of extended metrics defined over partially ordered sets, called the
climber

In contrast to many existing metrics, these distances are not induced by valuations and do not depend on additional structural assumptions beyond the order relation itself.

We then investigate the relationship between these metrics and structural properties of posets such as linearity, modularity, and lattice structure. In particular, we show that the

(3)

3
Calculation of distances and comparisons

In this section, we present explicit formulas for computing the climber and diver distances in some notable classes of posets, including linear orders and lattices. We also describe how these distances behave under standard constructions, such as direct product or lexicographic sums of posets. We conclude the section comparing these metrics with the classic shortest-path and shortest-fence metrics, the Chebyshev distance, as well as establishing that our metrics are not induced by any valuation on the poset. For this section, let
\delta_{x,y}
denote the Kronecker delta of
x
and
y
.

3.1
Distances on classes of posets

Linear posets

A poset
P
is called
linear
if
P
is itself a chain i.e. all elements in
P
are comparable. All linear posets are fence-connected posets. For any
x\leq y\in P
we have that
\{x,y\}
is a fence with zero alternations from
x
to
y
. Then, we obtain that for the fence distances:

\mathcal{E}(x,y)=\mathcal{B}(x,y)=1-\delta_{x,y}.

If
|[x,y]|<\infty
, this chain is the only path in
P
from
x
to
y
with zero alternations. Hence for any
k
:

\mathcal{E}_{k}(x,y)=\mathcal{B}_{k}(x,y)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\Big\lceil\frac{\ell([x,y])}{k}\Big\rceil&\text{ if }|[x,y]|<\infty\\
\infty&\text{else}.\end{array}\right.

Zig-zag posets

A poset is called a
zig-zag
poset if
P=\{x_{i}\}_{i\in A\subseteq\mathbb{Z}}
and for all
i
even,
x_{i-1}\prec x_{i}\succ x_{i+1}
or
x_{i-1}\succ x_{i}\prec x_{i+1}
and those are all covering relations of
P
. Notice that for
i\leq j
,
j-i-1
is the number of alternations of the path on
P
from
x_{i}
to
x_{j}
. Then we obtain for the climber distances:

\mathcal{E}_{k}(x_{i},x_{j})=\mathcal{E}(x_{i},x_{j})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}0&i=j\\
\frac{j-i+1}{2}&j-i\text{ odd}\\
\frac{j-i}{2}&j-i\text{ even},x_{i}\prec x_{i+1}\\
\frac{j-i+2}{2}&j-i\text{ even},x_{i}\succ x_{i+1}.\\
\end{array}\right.

We have a similar formula for
\mathcal{B}_{k}(x_{i},x_{j})=\mathcal{B}(x_{i},x_{j})
interchanging
x_{i}\prec x_{i+1}
and
x_{i}\succ x_{i+1}
on the last two conditions.

Crown posets

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
even. A poset is
P=\{x_{0},\dots,x_{2n-1}\}
is called a
n
-crown poset
if

x_{0}\prec x_{1}\succ x_{2}\prec\cdots\succ x_{2n-2}\prec x_{2n-1}\succ x_{0}

such that these are all covering relations of
P
. The diagram of a
n
-crown poset is the diagram of a zig-zag poset with the endpoints joined, in this way
P
is a path-connected poset. Let
i\leq j
and take
I,J\geq 0
the numbers between 0 and
2n-1
such that
I\equiv i-j\mod 2n
and
J\equiv j-i\mod 2n
. Then

\mathcal{E}_{k}(x_{i},x_{j})=\mathcal{E}(x_{i},x_{j})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\min\{\frac{I+1}{2},\frac{J+1}{2}\}&j-i\text{ odd}\\
\min\{\frac{I}{2},\frac{J}{2}\}&j-i\text{ even and }x_{i}\prec x_{i+1}\\
\min\{\frac{I+2}{2},\frac{J+2}{2}\}&j-i\text{ even and }x_{i}\succ x_{i+1}\\
\end{array}\right.

Similarly to zig-zag posets, the formula for
\mathcal{B}_{k}(x_{i},x_{j})=\mathcal{B}(x_{i},x_{j})
are the same interchanging
x_{i}\prec x_{i+1}
and
x_{i}\succ x_{i+1}
on the last two conditions.

Common bounds

For
x,y
in a poset
P
,
z
is a
common upper bound
of
x
and
y
if
x\leq z
and
y\leq z
. Dually,
w
is a
common lower bound
of
x
and
y
if
w\leq x
and
w\leq y
. A poset
P
has the upper (or lower) filtering property if any two elements have a common upper (or lower, respectively) bound.

Take
P
to have the upper filtering property. For any
x
and
y
distinct elements in
P
take
z
a common upper bound. Then, there exists the upward fence with one alternation
x<z>y
. Then
\mathcal{E}(x,y)=1
. Hence,
\mathcal{E}(x,y)=1-\delta_{x,y}
for any
x,y\in P
. In a similar way, if
P
has the lower filtering property for any
x
and
y
distinct elements, there exists the downward fence with one alternation
x>w<y
where
w
is a common lower-bound. Hence,
\mathcal{B}(x,y)=1-\delta_{x,y}
. If
P
has both properties this implies that for any
x,y\in P
we have

\mathcal{E}(x,y)=\mathcal{B}(x,y)=1-\delta_{x,y}.

Modular posets

A poset
P
is called
upper semi-modular
if and only if for all
x\neq y\in P
with
w
a common lower cover of
x
and
y
i.e

there exists

such that
x\prec z\text{ and }y\prec z
. Dually, a poset is
lower semi-modular
if the existence of a common upper cover implies the existence of a common lower cover. A poset is called

Theorem 3.3
.

If

is a modular poset then for all

and for any

\mathcal{E}_{k}(x,y)=\mathcal{B}_{k}(x,y)
. If

is also discrete then
\mathcal{E}(x,y)=\mathcal{B}(x,y)
.

Proof.

Take
P
a modular poset and
x,y
in
P
. If
x
and
y
are in a different path-connected component then for any
k
,
\mathcal{E}_{k}(x,y)=\infty=\mathcal{B}_{k}(x,y)
and we are done. Assume that
x,y
are in the same path-connected component and for a fixed
k
, let
\gamma
be a path from
x
to
y
such that

. Let us prove that
\mathcal{B}_{k}(x,y)\leq\mathcal{E}_{k}(x,y)
. We have three cases

1.

Suppose that
A(\gamma)
is even. Then, by definition of
L^{*}_{k}
, we have that
L^{*}_{k}(\gamma)=L_{k}(\gamma)
. Thus, as, desired,
\mathcal{B}_{k}(x,y)\leq L^{*}_{k}(\gamma)=L_{k}(\gamma)=\mathcal{E}_{k}(x,y)
.

2.

Suppose that
A(\gamma)
is odd and
\gamma
is an upward path with chain decomposition
\{C_{0},\dots,C_{A(\gamma)}\}
. Take the paths
\gamma_{i}=C_{2i}\cup C_{2i+1}
for each integer

between

and
\frac{A(\gamma)-1}{2}
.

Each path

is an upward path with exactly one alternation. Since

is lower semi-modular, Lemma

guarantees the existence of a downward path

connecting the same endpoints as

to

\gamma^{*}=\gamma^{*}_{1}\cup\gamma^{*}_{2}\cup\cdots\cup\gamma^{*}_{\frac{A(\gamma)-1}{2}}

Let

denote the chain decomposition of

. By construction of each
\gamma^{*}_{i}
,
A(\gamma^{*})=A(\gamma)
and for all

,
\ell(C^{*}_{2i})=\ell(C_{2i+1})\text{ and }\ell(C^{*}_{2i+1})=\ell(C_{2i})
. Then we obtain that

\displaystyle L_{k}(\gamma)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{A(\gamma)}\Big\lceil\frac{\ell(C_{i})}{k}\Big\rceil-\frac{A(\gamma)+1}{2}=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{\frac{A(\gamma)-1}{2}}(\Big\lceil\frac{\ell(C_{2i})}{k}\Big\rceil+\Big\lceil\frac{\ell(C_{2i+1})}{k}\Big\rceil-1)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{\frac{A(\gamma)}{2}-1}(\Big\lceil\frac{\ell(C^{*}_{2i+1})}{k}\Big\rceil+\Big\lceil\frac{\ell(C^{*}_{2i})}{k}\Big\rceil-1)=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{A(\gamma)}\Big\lceil\frac{\ell(C^{*}_{i})}{k}\Big\rceil-\frac{A(\gamma)+1}{2}=L^{*}_{k}(\gamma^{*}).

\mathcal{B}_{k}(x,y)\leq L^{*}_{k}(\gamma)=L_{k}(\gamma)=\mathcal{E}_{k}(x,y)
. Thus,
\mathcal{E}_{k}(x,y)
upper bounds
\mathcal{B}_{k}(x,y)
.

3.

Suppose that
A(\gamma)
is odd and
\gamma
is a downward path. Then, by definition of
L^{*}_{k}
, we have that
L^{*}_{k}(\gamma)=L_{k}(\gamma)-1
. Thus,
\mathcal{B}_{k}(x,y)\leq L^{*}_{k}(\gamma)<L_{k}(\gamma)=\mathcal{E}_{k}(x,y)
as desired.

In a dual way, we can prove that
\mathcal{E}_{k}(x,y)\leq\mathcal{B}_{k}(x,y)
. Take
\gamma^{*}
to be a path from

to

Suppose that

is odd and
\gamma^{*}

L^{*}_{k}

3.2
Distances under constructions of posets

In this subsection we describe how to create new posets from existing ones and characterize the climber and diver distances in these new posets. We will use the notation
\mathcal{E}^{P}
and
\mathcal{B}^{P}
and
\mathcal{E}_{k}^{P}
and
\mathcal{B}_{k}^{P}
to emphasize that each metric is computed with respect to a specific underlying poset
P
.

Subposets

We can create a new poset from an existing one by restricting to subsets. If
P
is a poset and
Q\subset P
, then
Q
becomes a poset by inheriting the order from
P
: whenever
x,y\in Q
satisfy
x\leq_{P}y
, we set
x\leq_{Q}y
. Because the order is inherited, every path or fence that lies in
Q
is contained in
P
as well.

Take
k
fixed but arbitrary. Let
\gamma
be a path in
Q
from
x
to
y
such that
L_{k}(\gamma)=\mathcal{E}^{Q}_{k}(x,y)
. Since
\gamma\subset P
we obtain that

\mathcal{E}^{P}_{k}(x,y)\leq L_{k}(\gamma)=\mathcal{E}^{Q}_{k}(x,y).

Thus, the
k
-climber distance can only increase. The same monotonicity holds for the fence-climber distance. If
F
is a fence from
x
to
y
in
Q
that minimizes
\mathcal{E}^{Q}
we have that

\mathcal{E}^{P}(x,y)\leq L(F)=\mathcal{E}^{Q}(x,y).

By the same argument we conclude as well that

\mathcal{B}^{P}_{k}(x,y)\leq\mathcal{B}^{Q}_{k}(x,y)\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{B}^{P}(x,y)\leq\mathcal{B}^{Q}(x,y).

Opposite posets

Let
P
be a poset. The
opposite poset
of
P
,
P^{\text{op}}
, has the same underlying set with the order reversed:
x\leq_{P^{\text{op}}}y
if and only if
y\leq_{P}x
. Then, the set identity function
Id
is a natural anti-isomorphism
\text{Id}:P\to P^{\text{op}}
from a poset to its opposite.

Consider
\gamma
a path in
P
from
x
to
y
. The path
\gamma^{\text{op}}
in
P^{\text{op}}
is obtained by taking the same elements of
\gamma
. Then,
\gamma^{\text{op}}
is a path from
x
to
y
that has the same length, the same number of alternations but with opposite orientation, i.e, if
\gamma
is an upward path, then,
\gamma^{\text{op}}
is a downward path and viceversa. Thus,

L_{k}(\gamma)=L^{*}_{k}(\gamma^{op})\quad\text{and}\quad L^{*}_{k}(\gamma)=L_{k}(\gamma^{op}).

Moreover,
\gamma
minimizes
L_{k}
in
P
if and only if
\gamma^{op}
minimizes
L^{*}_{k}
in
P^{\text{op}}
. In a similar way,
\gamma
minimizes
L^{*}_{k}
in
P
if and only if
\gamma^{op}
minimizes
L_{k}
in
P^{\text{op}}
. Hence, for any
x,y
in
P

\mathcal{B}^{P^{op}}_{k}(x,y)=\mathcal{E}^{P}_{k}(x,y)\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{E}^{P^{op}}_{k}(x,y)=\mathcal{B}^{P}_{k}(x,y).

The same correspondence holds for fences. If
F
is a fence from
x
to
y
in
P
, then the fence
F^{\text{op}}
in
P^{\text{op}}
obtained by taking same elements of
F
, has the same number of alternations but with opposite orientation. Consequently,

\mathcal{B}^{P^{op}}(x,y)=\mathcal{E}^{P}(x,y)\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{E}^{P^{op}}(x,y)=\mathcal{B}^{P}(x,y).

In particular, we have the following result for modular posets.

Corollary 3.4
.

Let
P
be a modular poset. For any
k
we have that for any
x,y\in P
,

\mathcal{E}^{P}_{k}(x,y)=\mathcal{E}^{P^{\text{op}}}_{k}(x,y)=\mathcal{B}^{P}_{k}(x,y)=\mathcal{B}^{P^{\text{op}}}_{k}(x,y).

If
P
is also discrete we have

\mathcal{E}^{P}(x,y)=\mathcal{E}^{P^{\text{op}}}(x,y)=\mathcal{B}^{P}(x,y)=\mathcal{B}^{P^{\text{op}}}(x,y).

Proof.

Let
P
be a modular poset. It is known that if
P
is a modular poset, then,
P^{\text{op}}
is also modular. Hence, by Theorem
3.3
we obtain that

\mathcal{E}^{P}_{k}(x,y)=\mathcal{B}^{P}_{k}(x,y)\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathcal{E}^{P^{\text{op}}}_{k}(x,y)=\mathcal{B}^{P^{\text{op}}}_{k}(x,y).

By the duality of
P
and
P^{\text{op}}
we conclude that

\mathcal{E}^{P}_{k}(x,y)=\mathcal{B}^{P}_{k}(x,y)=\mathcal{E}^{P^{\text{op}}}_{k}(x,y)=\mathcal{B}^{P^{\text{op}}}_{k}(x,y).

Assume that
P
is discrete. A similar argument proves that

\mathcal{E}^{P}(x,y)=\mathcal{B}^{P}(x,y)=\mathcal{E}^{P^{\text{op}}}(x,y)=\mathcal{B}^{P^{\text{op}}}(x,y).

∎

Direct product of posets

Take
\{P_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
to be a collection of posets. The
direct product
of this collection is the cartesian product
\displaystyle\bigtimes_{i=1}^{n}P_{i}=P_{1}\times P_{2}\cdots\times P_{n}
with the order relation

(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\leq_{\bigtimes_{i=1}^{n}P_{i}}(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})\;\text{ if and only if }\;x_{i}\leq_{P_{i}}y_{i}\text{ for all }1\leq i\leq n.

5
Concluding Remarks and Future directions

5.1
Summary

In this paper we have produced two new families of metrics for path connected posets called the
k
-climber distance
\mathcal{E}_{k}
and the
k
-diver distance
\mathcal{B}_{k}
, one dual to the other. In this context
k
is a natural number. Given a path-connected poset
P
, such distances are defined on the power set of the poset and then it induces a metric on the poset itself. We show that the k-climber distance of
x,y\in P
is equivalent to the minimal
k
-climber length over all paths from
x
to
y
and the
k
-climber length is a value that depend solely on the number of alternations and the length of the path (see Section
2
).

In the case that
k=1
, the metrics
\mathcal{E}_{1}
and
\mathcal{B}_{1}
determine, up to isomorphism, discrete path-connected posets that do not contain a subposet isomorphic to an infinite diamond–pentagon ladder, with a single exception: modular posets. More precisely, if
P
and
Q
are such posets,
f:P\to Q
is a bijection that preserves
\mathcal{E}_{1}
and
\mathcal{B}_{1}
if and only if
P\cong Q
. In the case that
P
is a modular poset, the posets
P
and
Q
may instead be duals of one another (see Section
4
).

In Section
2
we prove that when
k
goes to infinity
\mathcal{E}_{k}
converges to what we call the
\infty
-climber distance
\mathcal{E}_{\infty}
. Similarly, we have a
\infty
-diver distance,
\mathcal{B}_{\infty}
, defined as the limit of
\mathcal{B}_{k}
. The
\infty
-distances between
x
and
y
are realized by a minimal length over all paths from
x
to
y
that depends only on the number of alternations of the path. This observation lead us to define the fence-climber and the fence-diver distances for fence-connected posets. The distances coincide with
\mathcal{E}_{\infty}
and
\mathcal{B}_{\infty}
respectively when calculated over a discrete poset.

In Section
3
we present explicit formulas for computing the climber and diver distances in some notable classes of posets, including linear orders and lattices. Likewise, we describe how these distances behave under standard constructions, such as direct product or lexicographic sums of posets. Also, we compare the shortest path, the shortest fence metrics and the Chebyshev distance with the climber and diver metrics. Additionally, we prove that our metrics are not induced by real valued functions defined on the poset. We prove instead, that the climber and diver metrics are instances of interleaving distance, a metric defined over categories equipped with a flow (Section
5
).

Finally, in SageMath
[
20
]
, we implemented a collection of functions that, for any pair of elements in a poset, compute their
k
-climber and
k
-diver distance for arbitrary
k
as well as determine the corresponding shortest paths in this setting (see Appendix
A
).

Definitions and notations

We give basic definitions. For more categorical terminology; see

An order-preserving self map of

Given a poset

given by

and

\displaystyle=\min\{\lfloor\epsilon\rfloor\mid R\leq(\downarrow_{1}\uparrow_{1})^{\lfloor\epsilon\rfloor}(S)\text{ and }S\leq(\downarrow_{1}\uparrow_{1})^{\lfloor\epsilon\rfloor}(R)\}

5.3
Future directions

The climber and diver metrics address the theoretical and practical need for metrics that can be applied to general posets without structural constraints. Consequently, studying the computation and interpretation of these metrics on posets arising from real datasets constitutes a natural continuation of this project. An important question is what kinds of structural or hierarchical information these metrics are capable of detecting and highlighting within a dataset.

From a computational perspective, an important open problem is the analysis of the algorithmic complexity of computing
k
-climber and
k
-diver distances. The SageMath implementation (Appendix
A
) is intended primarily as a proof of concept and may require optimization for large-scale computations. Future research could focus on complexity estimates, optimized algorithms, and the identification of families of posets where these computations can be performed efficiently. The development of better implementations and computational experiments would also strengthen the practical applicability of these metrics.

In a similar vein, it is known that if a finite set with a partial ordering has known shortest-path and shortest-fence values, then both the partial order and its dual order can be recovered through a computable algorithm
[
4
]
. Since we have shown that finite posets are characterized by their 1-climber and 1-diver metrics, it is natural to conjecture that a similar reconstruction algorithm may be developed using these distance values. Such procedure could provide a new method for recovering a poset directly from its metric information.

Another natural direction is the study of the metrics in broader classes of posets. While this work establishes explicit formulas and properties for some families of posets like linear and modular posets, many important classes remain unexplored, including distributive lattices, interval posets and graded posets. Such studies may reveal additional simplifications, structural properties, and new relationships satisfied by these metrics.

Finally, it would be interesting to determine whether richer classes of posets admit more diverse notions of categorical flow. Such flows could give rise to new families of interleaving-type distances and, consequently, to new metrics associated with ordered sets.

A.1

We propose Algorithm

Take

and
y\in(\downarrow_{k}\uparrow_{k})^{n}(\{x\})
. By symmetry, is enough to find the minimum
n
satisfying the second condition.

Hence, our approach to computing

consists of iteratively calculating

n

\mathcal{E}_{k}(x,z)=n
for all

. Given that
P
is connected,
y

.

If we replace
\downarrow_{k}\uparrow_{k}
by
\mathcal{T}
it the process above, then, we can generalize this algorithm to any
\mathcal{T}:\mathcal{P}(P)\to\mathcal{P}(P)
and the distance defined to be the minimum

such that
x\in(\mathcal{T})^{n}(\{y\})
and
y\in(\mathcal{T})^{n}(\{x\})
. So, the metric is fully characterized by
\mathcal{T}
. In particular, this is true for the
k
-diver distance with
\mathcal{T}=\uparrow_{k}\downarrow_{k}
, the fence-climber distance with
\mathcal{T}=\downarrow\uparrow
and the fence-diver distance with
\mathcal{T}=\uparrow\downarrow
. Moreover, as
P
is finite, then the
\infty
-climber and
\infty
-diver distances coincide with the fence-climber and the fence-diver distances respectively.
