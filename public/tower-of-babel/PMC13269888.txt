Lightweight and Energy-Aware Intrusion Detection for Industrial IoT Using TinyML and Edge AI
Authors: Laila Nassef, Mohammed Ibrahim Alghamdi, Slim BEN CHAABANE, Qaisar Abbas, Wedad M. Alawad, Omar H Albalawi, Osamah I. Alqaisi, Bahjat Fakieh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Industrial Internet of Things (IIoT) ecosystems are expanding rapidly. Scalable and reliable intrusion detection systems (IDS) are needed to protect critical infrastructures from evolving cyber threats. This study proposes a hybrid IDS framework that combines Graph Attention Networks (GAT) and Bidirectional Gated Recurrent Units (BiGRU) for privacy‑preserving distributed detection. The model is optimized with the Grey Wolf Optimizer (GWO) and enhanced through Federated Learning (FL). In IIoT traffic, GAT captures complex structural links, while BiGRU analyzes bidirectional temporal patterns, enabling accurate anomaly detection. GWO automates hyperparameter tuning and offers faster convergence than traditional methods such as Ant Colony Optimization. FL trains models locally on distributed IIoT devices, preserving data privacy and supporting decentralized deployment. The framework demonstrates improved scalability potential through decentralized training and reduced communication overhead (20% lower in a 10-node simulation), achieving detection accuracies of up to 95% across diverse attack scenarios, including Distributed Denial of Service (DDoS), Advanced Persistent Threats (APTs), and Zero‑Day exploits. It has been evaluated on the Edge‑Industrial Internet of Things dataset (Edge‑IIoTset), Canadian Institute for Cybersecurity – Internet of Things 2023 Dataset (CICIoT2023), and Real‑Time Internet of Things 2022 (RT‑IoT2022) datasets. An Explainable AI (XAI) module further improves interpretability by leveraging GAT’s attention mechanism. Overall, this technology demonstrates competitive offline performance on the EDGE-IIoTset, CICIoT2023, and RT-IoT2022 benchmark datasets, achieving F1-scores of up to 0.94, and shows promising scalability potential through decentralized Federated Learning with 20% lower communication overhead in a 10-node simulation. However, inference latency on resource-constrained edge hardware remains a challenge (e.g., 120–180 ms per sample on Raspberry Pi 4), which limits its strict real-time feasibility in mission-critical environments. Therefore, further model compression, adversarial robustness testing, and real-world deployment validation are required before practical edge-level applicability can be confirmed.

Introduction

IIoT has transformed manufacturing, energy, and transportation through the integration of cyber-physical systems, efficient data exchange, predictive maintenance, and advanced automation1,2. Its large network of sensors, actuators, and edge devices enables smooth communication and faster decision-making, strengthening productivity and innovation in Industry 4.0 settings3,4. However, the rapid expansion of IIoT deployments has significantly increased cybersecurity risks, exposing critical infrastructures to sophisticated attacks5–7. In 2024, targeted attacks on IIoT systems rose by 30%, including DDoS, APTs, and zero-day exploits8,9. These threats are intensified by the heterogeneous nature of IIoT environments, where modern devices operate alongside outdated systems10,11.

Strong IDS are therefore essential. IDS monitors device and network behavior to detect anomalies that may indicate malicious activity12,13. Unlike traditional IT networks, IIoT systems run in real-time and under strict resource limits, where downtime can cause major financial losses. Gartner reported over $50 billion in global economic damage from cyber incidents in 202514,15. Effective IDS requires high accuracy, low latency, scalability, and adaptability to evolving threats1,16.

ML and DL methods have become key tools because they use pattern recognition to distinguish malicious from benign traffic17,18. Recurrent models handle sequential data well, while graph‑based models capture the topological structure of IIoT networks19,20. Yet integrating these models into IIoT environments introduces unique challenges that demand innovative solutions2,3.

Existing IIoT IDS frameworks still face many challenges. The IIoT’s distributed architecture—spanning edge, fog, and cloud layers creates latency and bandwidth issues that complicate centralized processing4,5. Many traditional IDS rely on centralized training, which requires aggregating private data from multiple devices and raises privacy concerns under regulations such as the California Consumer Privacy Act (CCPA) and General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR)6,7. Conventional convolutional and recurrent models also struggle to capture the complex spatio-temporal patterns in IIoT traffic. Graph Convolutional Networks (GCNs) often perform poorly against adaptive attacks because they treat all neighbors equally and overlook differences in node importance10,11. Similarly, unidirectional recurrent units like Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) miss subtle anomalies because they cannot model bidirectional context in traffic sequences12,13. Explainability is also a major concern, as black-box deep learning models limit trust and hinder forensic analysis, which industrial stakeholders consider essential16.

Generalization remains limited in many existing IIoT intrusion detection approaches, as outdated datasets often fail to capture emerging threats such as multi-vector attacks or AI-generated intrusions18–20. Recent studies emphasize energy-efficient federated learning through optimized Unmanned Aerial Vehicle (UAV) deployment and edge association to reduce resource consumption in distributed IIoT networks17. Energy-aware virtual machine allocation in cloud-based IoT data centers has also shown significant power savings for large-scale applications18. Furthermore, blockchain-based verifiable decentralized identity mechanisms enhance tamper-resistant security in intelligent manufacturing and heterogeneous IIoT ecosystems20. These constraints highlight the need for advanced, scalable IDS frameworks tailored specifically to IIoT environments. Improving accuracy against evolving threats while enabling privacy‑preserving detection in decentralized settings is a key priority.

This work addresses these needs by proposing a hybrid IDS framework that integrates GAT, BiGRU, GWO for hyperparameter tuning, FL for distributed privacy‑preserving training, and an XAI module based on GAT attention. The framework shows competitive offline performance on benchmark datasets while real-time feasibility on constrained edge devices remains to be validated. “Proposed methodology” section provides detailed descriptions of these components and their integration.

Related works

Paper21 introduces a federated learning framework for IoT intrusion detection that combines nature‑inspired hyperparameter optimization with TabTransformer’s ability to model tabular feature interactions. The study shows that privacy‑preserving training across clients is feasible, with competitive accuracy and strong generalization on heterogeneous data. It also offers a practical template for FL‑based IDS pipelines and demonstrates deployment viability on resource‑limited devices.

Research22 provides a detailed empirical comparison of CNN and CNN + BiLSTM models for federated IDS on IoT data CICIoT2023. The authors examine client heterogeneity and class imbalance, and they quantify trade‑offs between accuracy, computation per round, and communication cost. Their findings guide the selection of lightweight architectures that maintain performance in edge environments while controlling training latency.

A innovative event-based publish/subscribe communication protocol for the IIoT that incorporates traceable and reportable ring signatures was proposed by researchers in23. In distributed industrial networks, this method guarantees publisher authentication, anonymous communication, traceability for harmful activity, and robust privacy.

An explainable and trust-aware AI-based system for network slicing in 6G IoT networks is presented in24. It makes use of deep learning models to improve resource allocation while offering interpretability and decision transparency. In dynamic and resource-constrained IoT environments, this method improves stakeholder confidence through performance indicators and attention-based explanations.

An enhanced ransomware detection model was created in a different study25 using a hybrid classifier optimized by metaheuristic algorithms, sensitivity analysis using SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) values, and feature selection using F-criterion and recursive feature elimination (RFE). With great interpretability and resilience against zero-day threats, this method achieves high precision, accuracy, and recall on large-scale datasets.

Researchers presented VFL + in26, a low-connectivity vertical federated learning architecture that allows autonomous predictions at inference time while incorporating privileged information from other clients during training. This approach improves model independence without sacrificing performance, lowers parameter coupling between participants, and improves privacy in heterogeneous data environments.

A memory-guided multiscale network for unsupervised anomaly identification in IoT time series data is shown in research27. It incorporates spectral and spatiotemporal information using hierarchical attention and gating methods. The model achieves better detection performance in noisy and imbalanced IoT environments and successfully captures intricate patterns and dependencies in multivariate sequences.

Study28 applies GraphSAGE to IoT IDS by constructing graphs from attribute similarity (Top‑K) rather than physical links. This attribute‑based design creates strong connectivity for tabular telemetry and improves generalization on NF‑ToN‑IoT and NF‑BoT‑IoT. The paper also identifies key design factors—K, similarity metrics, and sampling—that most influence accuracy and inference cost.

Work29 combines deep models with post‑hoc interpretation to explore explainable AI for IoT IDS. Using feature attribution and rule‑level summaries, the authors expose the decision logic behind high‑risk alerts, improving auditability and operational trust. Their results show that XAI can guide feature engineering and reveal dataset issues without significantly reducing detection performance. Research30 proposes explainable, attention‑based methods for detecting zero‑day attacks in IoT networks. The combination of attention mechanisms and interpretability tools enables generalization to unseen threats and provides human‑readable explanations at the feature or flow level. This makes the approach suitable for post‑incident analysis and response in resource‑constrained IoT settings.

In31, CBRFL is introduced, a committee-based framework for Byzantine-resilient federated learning, which, through weighted aggregation, increases the resilience against malicious clients and achieves high accuracy in non-IID data scenarios, making it suitable for distributed IoT applications. Work32 optimizes IoT IDS components by combining the Whale Optimization Algorithm (WOA) with GWO. This hybrid approach achieves a better exploration–exploitation balance than either metaheuristic alone. By improving F1 scores and reducing false alarms across benchmarks, the method shows the value of complementary heuristics for navigating complex objective landscapes in IoT security.

Work42 proposes a hybrid blockchain-based IDS combining Genetic Algorithm Optimization (GAO)-optimized, Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) classification with Elliptic Curve Cryptography (ECC)-secured data transmission. It achieves high accuracy on CIC-IDS2017 and IoT-23 datasets with strong emphasis on secure storage and tamper resistance. While effective for centralized or semi-centralized setups, it lacks federated privacy preservation and graph-based topological modeling of device interactions. In43, the framework integrates blockchain for tamper-proof logging and consensus-based anomaly validation. It demonstrates robust security against data manipulation but incurs higher communication and computational overhead compared to pure ML/FL approaches. Study44 introduces an XAI-enhanced deep model tailored for Industry 5.0 Cyber-Physical Systems (CPS) environments, focusing on interpretability via SHAP/LIME , Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations (LIME), and high accuracy on CPS-specific datasets. It shares our emphasis on explainability but does not incorporate federated training or graph attention mechanisms. Research45 combines FL for privacy with blockchain for model integrity and auditability. It shows strong resilience to poisoning attacks but reports higher communication costs and lower scalability in very large networks compared to lightweight FL-only designs. Transfer-learning-based BiLSTM model leverages pre-trained weights for fast adaptation to new IoT datasets, achieving good generalization on limited labeled data. While efficient for transfer scenarios, it does not address graph-structured IIoT topologies or federated privacy46. Paper47 targets IIoT-specific threats with high accuracy on industrial datasets. It provides a strong baseline for centralized deep learning but lacks distributed training and attention-based explainability.

Proposed methodology

This study proposes a hybrid IDS framework. In this section, we outline the architecture of the proposed scalable IDS for IIoT environments. The framework integrates GAT with BiGRU, enhanced by GWO for hyperparameter tuning and supported by FL for distributed, privacy‑aware training. This hybrid design aims to address key IIoT challenges, including heterogeneous device interactions, temporal traffic patterns, and privacy considerations in distributed settings.

GAT models the complex topological dependencies in IIoT networks, where devices and communications form graph nodes and edges. BiGRU complements this by processing sequences in both forward and backward directions, capturing long‑term temporal behavior that helps detect subtle anomalies such as stealthy APTs or evolving DDoS variants. GWO, inspired by grey wolves’ hierarchical hunting strategies, automates hyperparameter optimization and ensures efficient convergence without manual tuning. FL decentralizes training across edge devices, reducing the risks of centralized data collection and improving scalability in large IIoT deployments. An XAI module further uses GAT attention weights to generate interpretable explanations, strengthening trust in mission‑critical industrial settings.

The overall workflow, shown in Fig. 2, includes data preprocessing, model construction, optimization, federated aggregation, and evaluation. Preprocessed IIoT traffic is passed to the GAT‑BiGRU model, which is optimized through GWO. FL manages local training on distributed nodes and coordinates global updates through secure aggregation. This design improves detection accuracy, lowers latency, and preserves data sovereignty.

The role of grey wolf optimizer in the framework

By automating hyperparameter selection, GWO strengthens the robustness and efficiency of the GAT-BiGRU model. It mimics leadership-driven search behavior, balancing local exploitation and global exploration through the social hierarchy of grey wolves (alpha, beta, delta, and omega)36. This is especially valuable for deep learning in IIoT, where hyperparameters such as learning rate, attention heads, hidden units, and dropout ratios strongly affect performance but are difficult to tune manually due to the high-dimensional search space.

The integration of GWO into the framework involves three main components: a data preprocessor for feature refinement, a GAT‑BiGRU core for anomaly analysis, and an FL coordinator for distributed optimization. This automated tuning reduces human bias, improves generalization across diverse attack types, and lowers training overhead, making the IDS suitable for resource‑constrained IIoT edge devices. Data preprocessing module

Effective preprocessing is essential for handling the noisy, high‑dimensional, and heterogeneous nature of IIoT traffic. Our module begins by ingesting data from the selected datasets (EDGE‑IIoTset, CICIoT2023, and RT‑IoT2022), which include a wide range of benign and malicious flows such as DDoS, Mirai botnets, Address Resolution Protocol (ARP) spoofing, and zero‑day attacks. Raw data is then cleaned by removing duplicates, addressing missing values, and normalizing numerical features using min‑max scaling, where each feature vector is scaled based on its original value. To preserve semantic meaning without introducing ordinal bias, categorical attributes—such as protocol types or IP addresses—are encoded using one‑hot encoding.1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x^{\prime } = \frac{x - \min (X)}{{\max (X) - \min (X)}},$$\end{document}

The next step is dimensionality reduction using Principal Component Analysis (PCA), which retains at least 95% of the variance while projecting the data onto a lower-dimensional subspace. PCA is applied globally on the entire dataset before partitioning for Federated Learning, ensuring a consistent reduced feature space across all edge nodes (e.g., 20–30 components from original attributes like flow duration, packet size, and protocol type). Although PCA is applied globally on the entire dataset before partitioning for Federated Learning, this constitutes a one-time offline preprocessing step performed by a trusted coordinator using only anonymized statistical summaries. No raw or sensitive data is shared among clients during this phase. Only the resulting low-dimensional principal components (20–30 features) are distributed to the edge nodes. This approach maintains the privacy-preserving nature of the subsequent Federated Learning training phase while ensuring a consistent feature space across all participants. This global application preserves semantic meaning by transforming raw features into principal components that capture variance in traffic patterns, without differentiation per-traffic type (e.g., benign vs. malicious) to avoid bias. The PCA-reduced features then serve as initial node attributes in the GAT module, where each node’s feature vector is the projected representation of its aggregated flow data (e.g., average packet size, total bytes), allowing GAT to learn structural relationships on a standardized, low-dimensional input. For IIoT, this stage is essential because datasets such as CICIoT2023 include more than 100 attributes; PCA usually reduces this to 20–30 principal components, easing the curse of dimensionality and speeding up further processing.

To support FL, the data is partitioned across simulated edge nodes so that each local subset reflects real‑world distributions without requiring centralized transmission. This preprocessing pipeline, shown in Fig. 4, also incorporates the Synthetic Minority Over‑sampling Technique (SMOTE) to strengthen robustness against class imbalance, which is common in IIoT datasets. In datasets such as CICIoT2023, attack samples—including ARP spoofing and zero‑day exploits—represent less than 10% of all instances, making oversampling essential.

In our experiments, this process increased the representation of attack classes in EDGE‑IIoTset by roughly 50%, which improved the GAT‑BiGRU model’s recall to 0.95 for rare threats such as APTs.

The SMOTE procedure used in this framework builds on the original method introduced by Chawla et al.34 and is adapted for IIoT security scenarios, as demonstrated by Ferrag et al.35.

Graph construction from IIoT traffic is performed as follows: Each unique source or destination IP address (or MAC address when available) represents a node. An undirected edge is created between two nodes if at least one bidirectional flow was observed between them within a 60-s time window. For each node, features are obtained by aggregating all flows involving that node (mean, standard deviation, minimum, maximum, and count of packet size, flow duration, and protocol-related statistics) and then applying the previously computed PCA transformation to reduce the feature vector to 20–30 principal components. Neighborhood relationships are defined by the 1-hop adjacency matrix derived from these edges, with self-loops added to include each node’s own aggregated features. This graph structure is built independently for each time window and partitioned across the simulated edge nodes for Federated Learning.

This centralized preprocessing step is performed only once and does not involve sharing of raw IIoT traffic data, thereby preserving the overall privacy guarantees of the FL framework.

Hyperparameter optimization via GWO

The GAT-BiGRU analyzer engine’s success is largely dependent on how well its hyperparameters, such as the number of attention heads in GAT, the number of hidden units in BiGRU, the learning rate, and the dropout rate, are configured. The GWO, which has a faster convergence and a better balance between exploration and exploitation than the ACO utilized in [article base reference], is used to do this. Using datasets like EDGE-IIoTset, the GWO algorithm effectively searches the hyperparameter space to maximize the model’s performance measures, such F1-score, and is inspired by the hierarchical hunting behavior of grey wolves. Objective function for optimization

In order to effectively adjust the GAT-BiGRU model’s hyperparameters, the GWO needs a clear goal function that strikes a compromise between several performance measures designed for IIoT intrusion detection. In contrast to the objective function in [article base reference], which gave priority to accuracy and detection rate for GCN-GRU optimization through ACO, the suggested framework includes an interpretability metric derived from GAT’s attention weights to improve XAI capabilities, as well as F1-score to account for class imbalance. The objective function, as shown in Fig. 6, is intended to optimize the model’s performance on datasets such as RT-IoT2022, CICIoT2023, and EDGE-IIoTset.

Experimental evaluation

This section evaluates the proposed IDS for IIoT using the integrated GWO, BiGRU, GAT, and FL framework. The assessment measures the model’s ability to detect a wide range of cyberthreats across three benchmark datasets: EDGE‑IIoTset, CICIoT2023, and RT‑IoT2022. These datasets include attacks such as DDoS, Mirai botnets, ARP spoofing, and zero‑day exploits. An ablation study was conducted to isolate component contributions.

The experiments are designed to test the effectiveness of the GAT‑BiGRU architecture in distributed IIoT settings, the benefits of GWO‑based hyperparameter tuning, and the scalability and privacy advantages provided by FL. Performance is measured using standard metrics—accuracy, precision, recall, F1‑score, and false positive rate (FPR)—with particular emphasis on the F1‑score due to the class imbalance common in IIoT datasets.

Experimental setup

The experiments were conducted in a distributed setup with multiple edge nodes simulating an IIoT environment. Each node processed a local subset of the EDGE‑IIoTset, CICIoT2023, and RT‑IoT2022 datasets, which together cover diverse attack scenarios and benign traffic patterns. The preprocessing pipeline from “Data preprocessing module” section was applied to clean, normalize, encode, and convert the data into graph structures.

As described in “The role of grey wolf optimizer in the framework” section, the GAT‑BiGRU model was configured using GWO‑optimized hyperparameters: 8 attention heads, 128 BiGRU hidden units, a learning rate of 0.005, and a batch size of 64. Results and analysis

GAT, BiGRU, GWO, and FL were all integrated in the proposed IDS, which was thoroughly tested on three benchmark datasets: EDGE-IIoTset, CICIoT2023, and RT-IoT2022. From innocuous flows to complex attack scenarios like DDoS, APTs, Mirai botnets, ARP spoofing, and zero-day exploits, these datasets cover a wide range of IIoT traffic patterns. Convolutional Neural Network (CNN), LSTM, and Graph Convolutional Network (GCN), which are frequently used in IIoT intrusion detection, were among the baseline techniques against which the performance of the suggested GAT-BiGRU model was evaluated. Five-fold cross-validation was used to achieve robust and dependable results for all models’ training and testing on preprocessed datasets.

The suggested model achieving improvements over baselines in F1-scores by 3–8%, as shown in Table 4. On the EDGE-IIoTset dataset, the proposed GAT-BiGRU model achieved a competitive offline F1-score of 0.94 on the EDGE-IIoTset, outperforming CNN (0.87), LSTM (0.89), and GCN (0.91) in five-fold cross-validation. (IIoT)This improvement of 3–7% in F1-score indicates better handling of class imbalance and rare attacks (e.g., ARP spoofing), as the higher recall (0.95) reduces missed detections while maintaining high precision (0.93), leading to fewer false alarms in resource-constrained IIoT environments. Discussion

The experimental findings confirm competitive offline performance of the proposed IDS on benchmark IIoT datasets, which makes use of the complementary integration of GWO, BiGRU, GAT, and FL. Table 4 and Fig. 10 indicate the higher performance of the suggested framework on the EDGE-IIoTset, CICIoT2023, and RT-IoT2022 datasets, with F1-scores of 0.94, 0.92, and 0.93, respectively. In dynamic IIoT environments where new threats appear frequently, these findings demonstrate the model’s strong generalization to a variety of attack scenarios, such as DDoS, APTs, Mirai botnets, ARP spoofing, and zero-day exploits. As shown in Fig. 10, the suggested GAT-BiGRU model shows improvements over selected baselines baseline techniques, such as CNN, LSTM, and GCN, with gains in F1-score ranging from 3 to 8% across datasets.

The secret to this performance lies in the architectural improvements of the proposed framework. In contrast to the static aggregation in GCNs employed in the baseline study, GAT’s attention mechanism dynamically weights node interactions, better capturing intricate topological linkages in IIoT networks36. For example, Fig. 9’s attention maps show that nodes engaged in DDoS attacks have high attention scores (e.g., 0.85), making it possible to identify crucial devices with accuracy. In the basic GCN-GRU model36, unidirectional GRUs have trouble identifying subtle irregularities such gradual data exfiltration in APTs. BiGRU’s bidirectional processing improves temporal analysis by modeling both forward and backward dependencies. Figure 11’s GWO algorithm reduces training time by around 15% by achieving faster convergence (within 50 iterations) than the baseline’s ACO, which required up to 65 iterations. This efficiency is essential for IIoT contexts with limited resources, as shown in Table. Furthermore, the FedAvg algorithm’s performance in Fig. 7 shows that the FL framework reduced communication overhead by 20% in our 10-node simulation compared to centralized training, indicating good scalability potential; however, real-world scalability on hundreds or thousands of heterogeneous devices remains to be validated. Because of its compliance with GDPR, the system can be used for decentralized IIoT deployments. Class imbalances were successfully reduced by using the SMOTE in the preprocessing pipeline. This relative robustness difference of ~ 3% highlights the framework’s better tolerance to noise, likely due to GAT’s attention mechanism focusing on critical structural features and BiGRU’s bidirectional temporal modeling, which helps preserve detection performance against perturbations common in real-world IIoT traffic. These results imply that the proposed model offers practical advantages in noisy or potentially tampered edge environments, though full adversarial robustness (e.g., targeted poisoning) requires further dedicated evaluation.

Although the model achieves high offline detection performance and reduced training/communication costs, the current evaluation does not include per-inference latency measurements or real-world deployment tests on edge devices. Thus, while the results indicate strong potential, claims of real-time capability require additional validation through latency benchmarks and hardware-specific experiments.

Real-world deployment considerations

While the proposed GAT-BiGRU framework exhibits competitive offline detection performance, several practical deployment constraints must be considered for real-world IIoT environments.

Inference latency and per-sample computational cost

The current evaluation focuses on training time and batch processing on Graphics Processing Unit (GPU)/CPU servers. Inference latency — critical for real-time anomaly detection in IIoT — was not directly measured. Preliminary profiling on a standard workstation (Intel Xeon CPU, NVIDIA RTX 3080 GPU) shows average per-sample inference time of ~ 12–18 ms for the full GAT-BiGRU model (batch size 1, sequence length 128), which is acceptable for many non-mission-critical IIoT applications but may exceed strict real-time requirements (< 5–10 ms) in high-frequency monitoring scenarios (e.g., industrial control systems with sub-10 ms cycle times). On more constrained edge hardware such as Raspberry Pi 4 (quad-core Cortex-A72 @ 1.5 GHz, no GPU acceleration), preliminary tests indicate inference times of 120–180 ms per sample, highlighting the need for model compression (e.g., quantization, pruning) or lightweight variants in future work.

Hardware compatibility and resource footprint

The model requires approximately 8–12 MB of model weights (after training with 128 BiGRU hidden units and 8 GAT heads) and ~ 300–500 MB RAM during inference (including graph construction overhead). This footprint is feasible on mid-range edge gateways (e.g., NVIDIA Jetson Nano/TX2 or industrial PCs with 4–8 GB RAM), but challenging on ultra-low-power devices (e.g., ESP32, Arduino-class nodes) without significant optimization. Federated Learning further reduces memory pressure on individual nodes by distributing training, but local inference still requires the full model.

The ablation results demonstrate that GAT and BiGRU are the primary drivers of detection accuracy, contributing the largest improvements in F1-score through enhanced structural and temporal modeling, respectively. GWO delivers the most significant efficiency gain by reducing training time while maintaining near-optimal performance. Federated Learning incurs only a minor performance penalty in this offline simulation but provides essential privacy preservation and scalability advantages not achievable in centralized mode. The XAI component introduces negligible degradation while enabling interpretability, fulfilling its design goal without compromising core detection capability.

These findings confirm the complementary and synergistic nature of the proposed components, with no single element dominating performance at the expense of others. The ablation study strengthens the claim that the full hybrid architecture is necessary to achieve the reported balance of accuracy, efficiency, interpretability, and privacy in IIoT intrusion detection.

The ablation results quantify the relative contribution of each component to the overall performance. GAT provides the largest improvement in detection accuracy by enabling dynamic weighting of important device interactions, contributing approximately + 3.5% to the F1-score compared to using a standard GCN. BiGRU further enhances performance by capturing bidirectional temporal dependencies, adding + 2.8% to the F1-score relative to a unidirectional GRU. GWO significantly improves efficiency, reducing training time by approximately 38% (from 68 to 42 min per fold) while causing only a minor 2.3% drop in F1-score compared to grid search. Federated Learning incurs a small performance penalty of 0.8% in F1-score but delivers essential privacy preservation and scalability advantages in distributed settings. The XAI module adds interpretability with virtually no impact on accuracy (0.3% drop). These findings demonstrate that the full hybrid GAT-BiGRU-GWO-FL-XAI architecture achieves the best trade-off between high detection performance, computational efficiency, privacy, and explainability, outperforming simpler alternatives through the complementary strengths of its components.

Statistical significance was assessed via paired t-tests on F1-scores across folds. Removal of GAT resulted in the most significant degradation (p < 0.001), followed by removal of BiGRU (p = 0.003). The performance penalty from removing FL was not statistically significant (p = 0.18), consistent with its primary role in privacy and scalability rather than raw accuracy. Removal of GWO yielded a significant but modest drop (p = 0.012), underscoring its efficiency benefit.

Conclusion

This paper presents a hybrid intrusion detection framework that achieves competitive offline detection performance on three contemporary IIoT benchmark datasets (EDGE-IIoTset, CICIoT2023, and RT-IoT2022), with ablation analysis and statistical tests supporting the complementary contributions of its components in controlled conditions. Despite the competitive offline performance, current inference latency on resource-constrained edge devices restricts immediate deployment in mission-critical environments. Therefore, future research must prioritize model compression and lightweight adaptations to ensure strict real-time feasibility for edge-level execution.

However, several critical limitations persist. Inference latency and resource demands on real edge hardware have not been thoroughly benchmarked, formal privacy guarantees and advanced federated adversarial robustness remain unevaluated, and the approach has not been tested in live, heterogeneous, streaming, or non-stationary industrial environments. Addressing these gaps through on-device profiling, privacy auditing, poisoning attack simulations, and physical deployment experiments will be essential to determine the framework’s true suitability for operational IIoT security applications.

Competitive performance is shown in evaluations on the EDGE-IIoTset, CICIoT2023, and RT-IoT2022 datasets, which positions it as an effective IIoT security solution based on the evaluated metrics and datasets, with F1-scores of 0.94, 0.92, and 0.93, respectively, and a low FPR of 0.02 on RT-IoT2022 (Table 4, Fig. 10). The system demonstrates robustness in simulated adversarial scenarios, retaining an F1-score of 0.90 on EDGE-IIoTset (only 4% lower than clean conditions, Table 5), which supports its potential reliability in noisy IIoT deployments compared to baselines that degrade more significantly (Figs. 11 and 12).

While the framework shows promising detection capabilities and efficiency gains in offline evaluation, further assessment of inference latency and deployment on resource-constrained edge hardware would be required to confirm its suitability for strict effective IIoT applications. Computational overhead for graph construction in large-scale networks and comprehensive adversarial robustness in FL remain areas for improvement. Future work will explore lightweight optimizations, sparse graph techniques, and advanced oversampling methods like ADASYN to enhance practicality36. Statistical tests confirm that the reported improvements over baselines are significant (p ≤ 0.002 across datasets), while ablation results (Table 6) and uncertainty estimates demonstrate the robustness and complementary contributions of each component.

While the framework demonstrates competitive offline performance and efficiency gains in controlled experiments on benchmark datasets, inference latency on real edge hardware (estimated at 120–180 ms per sample on Raspberry Pi 4) indicates that model compression or hardware acceleration will be necessary for strict real-time IIoT applications. Sophisticated FL poisoning and Byzantine attacks remain unevaluated despite the observed resilience to mild Gaussian noise. Future work will investigate Byzantine-resilient aggregation mechanisms such as Krum and Trimmed Mean, along with differential privacy techniques, to enhance robustness against model poisoning and membership inference attacks in federated settings. Formal privacy guarantees and validation under non-stationary industrial conditions will also be addressed.
