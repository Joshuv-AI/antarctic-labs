Early effects of HPV vaccination on high-grade cervical intraepithelial neoplasia in Brazil: Evidence from outpatient data
Authors: Ana Luiza Bierrenbach, Paula Cristina Pungartnik, Rodrigo Gonçalves Queijo, João Gabriel Rego, Cintia Irene Parellada
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCervical cancer remains a leading cause of disease burden among women, despite being largely preventable. High-grade cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN) is a key intermediate outcome for evaluating early population-level effects of HPV vaccination and is mainly managed in outpatient settings, which may provide a more timely signal than hospitalizations or cancer registry data. This study aimed to assess whether the introduction of quadrivalent HPV vaccination in Brazil in 2014 through its National Immunization Program was associated with changes in first outpatient consultations for high-grade CIN. We conducted a quasi-experimental interrupted time-series analysis using Brazil’s Outpatient Information System, including females aged ≥15 y with first eligible high-grade CIN records identified by ICD-10 codes and relevant procedures. Descriptive trends were assessed from 2011 to 2023, and interrupted time-series models were restricted to 2011–2019. Age-specific consultation counts and rates were calculated, and segmented regression models were fitted using generalized linear models. Negative controls included consultations for non-HPV sexually transmitted infections (STIs) and trends among older age groups not directly targeted by vaccination. Among adolescents aged 15–19 y, high-grade CIN consultation rates increased from 5.5 per 100,000 women in 2011 to a peak of 7.1 in 2015, followed by a decline to 1.8 in 2023, falling below pre-vaccination levels. Older age groups did not show comparable declines, while non-HPV STI consultations increased steadily across all ages. These contrasting trends support a vaccine-related effect in adolescents and indicate that the observed decline is unlikely to reflect changes in healthcare-seeking behavior or reporting practices.KEYWORDS: Human papillomavirus, vaccination, cervical intraepithelial neoplasia, outpatient visits

Introduction

Cervical cancer, caused by human papillomavirus (HPV), remains a major public health challenge in Brazil despite being almost entirely preventable.1 It is the third most common cancer among women, with an estimated 19,310 new cases annually between 2026 and 2028, corresponding to an age-standardized incidence rate of 14.8 per 100,000 women.2 In 2022, 6,983 deaths were reported, corresponding to an age-adjusted mortality rate of 4.8 per 100,000.3

In response, Brazil has adopted the WHO global strategy to eliminate cervical cancer as a public health problem, supported by the Pan American Health Organization (PAHO).1 This initiative sets the 90–70-90 targets for 2030: vaccinate 90% of girls against HPV by age 15, screen 70% of women at ages 35 and 45, and ensure that 90% of women diagnosed with precancer or cancer receive appropriate treatment.4 Given that persistent infection with high-risk HPV types is the necessary cause of cervical cancer, HPV vaccination is a cornerstone of elimination efforts.

Brazil introduced the quadrivalent HPV vaccine (targeting types 6, 11, 16, and 18) into its National Immunization Program in 2014, initially targeting girls aged 9–14 y. Eligibility was later expanded to boys between 2017 and 2018, initially aged 11–14 y and subsequently including younger ages (9–10 y) and, from 2015 onwards, also to high-risk populations up to 45 y, including individuals living with HIV or under immunosuppressive therapy.5,6 Vaccination coverage for at least one dose among girls by age 15 ranged from 75% to 83% between 2015 and 2019, with recovery observed in 2023 after pandemic-related declines.7

As vaccinated cohorts reach the age of sexual debut and enter cervical screening programs, early impacts on HPV‑associated outcomes are expected to emerge. International evidence consistently shows marked reductions in HPV prevalence, cervical cancer precursors, and, with longer follow-up, cervical cancer incidence in vaccinated cohorts.8–10 In Brazil, the POP-Brazil study reported an 80.6% reduction in HPV 6/11/16/18 prevalence among vaccinated girls aged 16–17 y compared with unvaccinated peers just three years after HPV vaccine introduction.11 Two additional Brazilian studies have shown declines in high-grade cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN) in young women after five and nine years of HPV vaccination implementation.12,13 However, these previous studies relied on hospitalization or cancer registry data, which capture more severe cases and exclude lesions managed exclusively in outpatient settings.

Since high-grade CIN is predominantly diagnosed and treated in ambulatory care, national outpatient records offer a more sensitive and timelier indicator of vaccine impact than hospital or cancer registry data. We therefore aimed to assess whether the introduction of quadrivalent HPV vaccination in Brazil in 2014 was associated with population-level changes in first outpatient consultations for high-grade CIN within the public healthcare system, using interrupted time-series (ITS) analysis. To strengthen internal validity, we incorporated negative control outcomes not expected to be affected by HPV vaccination, including non-HPV sexually transmitted infections and compared findings with high-grade CIN trends in older age groups.

Methods

Study design and setting

We conducted a quasi-experimental, population-based ITS analysis using secondary data from Brazil’s Outpatient Information System (Sistema de Informação Ambulatorial – SIA).14 This design enables assessment of temporal changes in high-grade CIN following HPV vaccine introduction in 2014. A priori, descriptive trends are presented from 2011 to 2023 to contextualize outpatient activity; however, causal inference from ITS models was restricted to 2011–2019 to avoid confounding from the COVID-19 pandemic, which substantially disrupted healthcare-seeking behavior and outpatient service availability.

Population

The study included females aged ≥15 y receiving outpatient care within the Brazilian Public Unified Health System (Sistema Único de Saúde – SUS) for high-grade CIN. Analyses were stratified by age (15–19, 20–29, 30–39, and ≥40 y), reflecting the primary vaccine target cohort (15–19 y) and older groups with minimal direct eligibility during rollout.

Cervical cancer screening in Brazil

During the study period, the Brazilian Ministry of Health recommended cervical cancer screening for women aged 25 to 64 y, with annual Pap smears initially, transitioning to every three years after two consecutive annual negative results.15 Although women under 25 were outside the recommended screening range, clinicians sometimes may screen sexually active adolescents based on clinical judgment and risk factors. In 2015, the Ministry of Health issued clinical and therapeutic guidelines for comprehensive sexually transmitted infections (STIs) care that recommended annual screening for sexually active adolescents, covering chlamydia, gonorrhea, syphilis, and HIV. Additionally, a Pap smear should be performed during these examinations if there is any suspicion of cervical or vaginal disease.16 Data from the national cytological screening program (2013–2019) indicate that approximately 20% of Pap smears were conducted in women <20 y.17

Data sources and case identification

Outpatient data were extracted from the SIA “Boletim Individual” module, which contains patient-level records for services delivered under the Brazilian Unified Health System. SIA primarily reflects care for individuals without private health insurance, representing approximately 75% of the Brazilian population. Each record includes an encrypted National Health Card identifier (Cartão Nacional de Saúde – CNS), enabling longitudinal linkage across consultations. We included records with a valid ICD-10 code in the main diagnosis field; records with missing or null diagnosis codes were excluded.

Annual population denominators by age group were obtained from the Brazilian Institute of Geography and Statistics (Instituto Brasileiro de Geografia e Estatística – IBGE), based on projections from the 2022 Census. HPV vaccination coverage by age 15 (first and second doses) for girls and boys was obtained from the WHO/UNICEF Joint Reporting Form for Brazil (2014–2023).18

Data System upgrades

Brazil implemented major outpatient information system upgrades through the e-SUS strategy (initiated in 2012–2013), including electronic health records and stricter CNS use.19,20 These changes improved data capture, integration, and traceability across the public primary care network. However, adoption varied by region and time, potentially introducing discontinuities in reporting that ITS models cannot fully adjust for. This context is important for interpreting observed trends, as increases in outpatient consultations may partly reflect enhanced reporting rather than true epidemiological changes. To mitigate this limitation, we incorporated negative internal controls, as described below.

Case definitions

High-grade CIN cases were identified from outpatient consultations that included specific ICD-10 diagnostic codes recorded as the main diagnosis: N87.1 (moderate cervical dysplasia), N87.2 (severe cervical dysplasia), D06 (carcinoma in situ of cervix uteri), D06.0 (carcinoma in situ of endocervix), D06.1 (carcinoma in situ of exocervix), D06.7 (carcinoma in situ of other parts of cervix), and D06.9 (carcinoma in situ of cervix, unspecified site). To improve specificity and avoid inclusion of unconfirmed cases, follow-up or miscoded visits, these diagnostic codes were only considered valid when accompanied by at least one relevant procedure, such as excision of the transformation zone or anatomopathological examination (Table S1). Only the first eligible outpatient consultation per patient within the study window (2011–2023) was included. To ensure that the record represents new clinical events rather than follow-up care, we applied a one-year look-back period: if a patient had a prior consultation for high-grade CIN within the previous 12 months, the subsequent visit was excluded. For the initial study year (2011), consultations were included unless a record from 2010 indicated a prior diagnosis, in which case the patient was excluded.

Negative controls definitions

To strengthen internal validity, two negative control strategies were implemented. Conditions were selected for their similar healthcare-seeking patterns but lack of expected association with HPV vaccination. The first strategy focused on STIs not preventable by HPV vaccines, given their shared transmission route, high prevalence among young women, and usual management in ambulatory care. Included conditions were primary and secondary syphilis (A51), gonorrhea (A54), lymphogranuloma venereum (A55), chlamydia (A56 and A74.9), chancroid (A57), granuloma inguinale (A58), trichomoniasis (A59), herpes simplex (A60), and other or unspecified STIs (A64), including all subcategories. A one-year look-back period was applied to ensure inclusion of new episodes only. For modeling, all STIs were grouped into a composite category. However, for descriptive purposes, individual STI time series were also examined and presented separately to illustrate trends over time.

The second strategy used high-grade CIN consultations among women aged ≥30 y, who were unlikely to receive HPV vaccination during program rollout. These older cohorts served as an internal comparison group with minimal anticipated direct impact, enabling the detection of secular trends and potential indirect effects. Although a small number of women in these age groups may have received the vaccine due to immunosuppressive conditions, this is unlikely to meaningfully influence overall patterns. Together, these two control strategies strengthen the study’s ability to distinguish vaccine-related changes from underlying background trends and temporal fluctuations in outpatient consultations.

Measures and descriptive analysis

We calculated annual consultation counts and rates per 100,000 females, stratified by age group from 2011 to 2023. HPV vaccination coverage by age 15 was summarized annually to contextualize temporal trends in outcomes.

Interrupted time-series modeling

To assess the effect of HPV vaccination on high-grade CIN consultations, we conducted ITS analyses using negative binomial generalized linear models, appropriate for overdispersed count data. Separate models were fitted for high-grade CIN and for the composite control series. Each model included the following coefficients: an indicator variable for vaccine introduction, a continuous time variable beginning in January 2011, and a categorical variable for age group. We used half-year rates to improve model stability. The pre-vaccination period was from January 2011 to July 2014, and the post-vaccination period from July 2014 to December 2019. A single intervention point corresponding to HPV vaccine introduction in 2014 was used for all ITS models, with no transition phase modeled. The natural logarithm of the female population was included as an offset to adjust for demographic changes. For interpretability, we report the exponentiated post-vaccination time coefficient as the incidence rate ratio per half-year (IRRpost = exp[slopepost]), with 95% confidence intervals obtained by exponentiating the slope’s confidence limits. IRRpost values greater than 1 indicate increasing trends in the post-vaccination period; values less than 1 indicate decreasing trends.

Model fit was evaluated using simulated residuals and pseudo-R2 statistics, with thresholds defined as poor (<0.5), moderate (0.5–0.6), or good (>0.6). Pseudo-R2 values were 0.994 for the high-grade CIN and 0.957 for the control series, indicating excellent fit.21 To assess the statistical significance of differences between observed and counterfactual counts, we applied a bootstrap procedure with 1,000 replications. This resampling approach generated a distribution of differences, enabling estimation of the total number of averted HPV-related first consultations. Results were summarized as median differences with corresponding 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles.

Data were obtained from the publicly accessible SIA/SUS database via DATASUS FTP, which provides anonymized patient-level records with encrypted identifiers. No personally identifiable information is included, and ethics approval was not required under Brazilian regulations (CNS Resolution 510/2016).14,22 Statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.4.2). Model diagnostics were conducted using the DHARMa package to assess residual patterns and overall fit, complemented by pseudo-R2 statistics and other standard metrics.

Results

Descriptive analysis

Vaccination coverage

HPV vaccination coverage by age 15 in Brazil showed rapid uptake among females after program introduction in 2014, with first-dose coverage exceeding 98% by 2016–2018, although two-dose series completion rates remained lower (64–77%). Male coverage increased following program expansion in 2017, reaching 65% for the first dose and 51% for the second dose by 2023 (Table S2).

High-grade CIN

Between 2011 and 2023, first outpatient consultations for high-grade CIN among women aged ≥ 15 y rose from 11,394 to 21,604 consultations, with corresponding rates increasing from 14.8 to 24.6 per 100,000 women (+66%) (Table 1). The largest absolute increase occurred among women aged 30–39 y, whose consultation rate rose from 21.1 to 41.7 per 100,000, followed by women aged 20–29 (16.1 to 23.8) and those aged ≥40 y (13.6 to 22.5). In contrast, adolescents aged 15–19 y showed a sustained decline from 5.5 to 1.8 per 100,000 (−67%), well below the 2011 baseline.

For non-HPV STIs, rates rose sharply across all age groups during the study period. For women aged ≥15 y, consultations increased from 586 to 5,214, with rates increasing from 0.8 to 5.9 per 100,000 women (+638%) (Table 1). Growth was most pronounced in younger cohorts: among adolescents aged 15–19 y, rates climbed from 0.6 to 10.8 per 100,000 women (+1,700%), while among those aged 20–29 y, rates increased from 1.0 to 11.9 per 100,000. After 2019, increases were substantial: for all ages, rates nearly tripled (2.0 to 5.9). Older age groups (≥40 y) experienced smaller but consistent increases.

Interrupted time-series analysis

Figure 1 illustrates observed and predicted trends in high-grade CIN first consultations by age group, comparing pre- and post-vaccination periods. Early in the series, first consultation counts increased sharply, producing a steep counterfactual slope (yellow line). Among adolescents aged 15–19 y, observed values declined visibly after vaccine introduction, and the post-vaccination trend (blue line) fell below the counterfactual. Importantly, the 95% confidence intervals of these two trends did not overlap in this age group, indicating a clear deviation from the expected counterfactual pattern. In contrast, for older age groups (20–29, 30–39, and ≥40 y), the blue line remained slightly below the yellow line, but confidence intervals overlapped throughout, indicating no clear deviation from expected trends in these non-targeted populations. Because the counterfactual trend is upward, its confidence interval naturally widens over time, which may contribute to the observed overlap in older age groups.

Figure 2 shows trends for non-HPV STIs, used as a control outcome. Unlike high-grade CIN, STI first consultations rose steadily across all age groups throughout the study period, with no evidence of decline following HPV vaccine introduction. Observed post-vaccination values consistently exceeded counterfactual projections, and the post-vaccination trend continued upward. Notably, the 95% confidence intervals of the counterfactual and post-vaccination models did not overlap after 2014. This divergence likely reflects limitations in pre-vaccination model fit, which underestimated the sustained upward trajectory of STI consultations. Consequently, counterfactual projections declined over time and were accompanied by relatively narrow confidence intervals, failing to capture the post-2014 increase.

Interrupted time-series estimates (Table 2) confirmed distinct post-vaccination patterns. Among adolescents aged 15–19 y, the trend reversed from positive pre-vaccination growth (slope 0.236; 95% CI 0.216; 0.257) to post-vaccination decline (slope −0.117; 95% CI −0.126; −0.107), with an IRRpost of 0.89 per half‑year (95% CI 0.88; 0.90). Observed first consultations (2,620) were well below the counterfactual (7,688), yielding a median difference of 5,068 cases (95% CI 3,408; 6,648). In older age groups, slopes after vaccine introduction remained positive, indicating continued but attenuated growth: for women aged 20–29 y the slope was 0.043 (95% CI 0.039; 0.048; IRRpost 1.04 [95% CI 1.04;1.05]), for those aged 30–39 y the slope was 0.374 (95% CI 0.361;0.388; IRRpost 1.45 [95% CI 1.43;1.47]) and for those aged ≥ 40 y was 0.482 (95% CI 0.451;0.514; IRRpost 1.62 [95% CI 1.57;1.67]). These cohorts exhibited large differences between observed and counterfactual estimates, 20,753, 25,821, and 29,441 consultations, respectively – indicating that although counts continued to rise, increases were substantially lower than projected under the no-vaccine scenario.

For non-HPV STIs, all age groups exhibited strong positive post-vaccination slopes, with the largest increase observed among women aged 20–29 y (0.088; 95% CI 0.082; 0.094; IRRpost 1.09 [95% CI 1.09; 1.10]). Supplementary Figures S1 and S2 extend the analysis through 2023, showing continued increase in STI consultations across all age groups and, among older women, modest declines in high-grade CIN followed by stabilization. Figure S3 shows changes in the age-specific distribution of high-grade CIN across periods, with a progressive decline in the proportion of adolescents aged 15–19 y, from 4.0% in 2011–2014 to 2.3% in 2015–2019 to 0.7% in 2020–2023. In contrast, Figure S4 presents age distribution over the entire study period, directly comparing high-grade CIN with non-HPV STIs. Non-HPV STIs exhibited an early and sharp peak around 20 y of age, whereas high-grade CIN increased more gradually after adolescence, reaching its highest levels in young adulthood, approximately between 25 and 35 y of age, followed by a progressive decline.

Discussion

Using national outpatient data, we observed an early, population-level signal of HPV vaccine impact on high-grade CIN in Brazil. Among adolescents aged 15–19 y, first consultation rates declined below pre-vaccination levels by 2019, with a negative post-vaccination trend (IRRpost < 1), indicating sustained reductions. In older age groups, post‑vaccination slopes remained positive (IRRpost > 1), yet observed counts were markedly lower than counterfactual projections, demonstrating attenuation rather than absolute decline. Concurrent sharp increases in non-HPV STIs reinforce that reductions in high‑grade CIN among adolescents are unlikely to be explained by secular trends in sexual behavior or healthcare utilization.

The detection of high‑grade CIN among adolescents in our study is biologically and epidemiologically coherent with the Brazilian context. Early sexual debut, with a mean age of 13.9 y for boys and 14.9 y for girls, increases the risk of early infection by high‑risk HPV types,23 and natural history studies show that most high‑grade CIN (mainly HPV 16) develops within 24 months of persistent infection.24 This combination of early HPV exposure and opportunistic cytology created the conditions for high-grade CIN to be detected in adolescents and enabled evaluation of vaccine impact in this age group within our study.

In addition, our findings align with early signals from other countries, where reductions in HPV prevalence, cervical cancer precursors and invasive cervical cancer have followed vaccine introduction.9,25–28 The two negative control strategies, non-HPV STIs and older age groups not directly targeted by vaccination, strengthened the robustness of our results. The absence of declines in these controls, together with continued rises in STI consultations, supports the interpretation that the adolescent CIN decline is unlikely to result from secular changes or reporting artifacts. Apparent improvements in older cohorts primarily reflect attenuation relative to the counterfactual; limited declines in specific subperiods could relate to vaccination of special groups eligible up to age 45 (e.g., individuals living with HIV) or indirect effects, but these patterns were inconsistent.

Interpretation of trends must consider Brazil’s e‑SUS rollout, which improved data completeness and traceability over the study period.19,20 While these enhancements strengthened surveillance, they may have contributed to increases in recorded outpatient consultations irrespective of disease risk. Our ITS specification cannot fully account for such structural changes, so upward secular trends should be interpreted cautiously. Counterfactual comparisons and a negative control outcome mitigate, though do not eliminate, the risk that reporting improvements explain the adolescent decline.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, ITS analysis cannot fully account for time-varying confounders (changes in screening practices, healthcare access, or evolving data systems) and operates at the population-level, raising the possibility of ecological fallacy.29 In addition, we did not model a transition or ramp-up phase after HPV vaccine introduction. Although population-level effects of HPV vaccination are expected to emerge gradually as vaccinated cohorts age and enter cervical screening pathways, we used a single intervention point to preserve model simplicity and statistical stability within the available time series. Consequently, the timing and magnitude of estimated effects should be interpreted with caution, as inclusion of a transition phase could potentially better reflect the real-world implementation and the gradual emergence of its effects. Second, outpatient administrative records lack histological confirmation and may include miscoded or follow-up visits.30 We mitigated this by requiring relevant accompanying procedures and by retaining only the first consultation within a one-year window. Third, our data reflected the public sector, which covers around 75% of the Brazilian population; therefore, findings may not be generalizable to private care settings.31,32 Fourth, the post-vaccination period may not fully capture the impact among girls vaccinated within the target age range, particularly those aged younger than 14 years at program introduction, who may have had insufficient follow-up time.33 Finally, the STI control model showed non‑overlapping confidence intervals between counterfactual and observed post‑2014 trends; we interpret this as pre‑period fit limitations rather than vaccine effects on STIs, consistent with steady increases across the full period.

Despite these limitations, the decline in high-grade CIN among adolescents provides an encouraging indicator of early population-level impact of HPV vaccination within Brazil’s routine public-sector care. Yet the broader epidemiological context underscores why sustained efforts are critical. HPV remains highly prevalent in Brazil, fueled by early sexual debut, multiple partners, and low condom use, especially among young adults.34–36 Recent data indicate that HPV infection affects over half (53.6%) of sexually active adolescents and young adults aged 16–25 y, with high-risk types detected in 38.6% of women and 29.2% of men in this age group.37 A systematic review estimated a cervical HPV prevalence of 25.4% in the general population, with even higher rates in high-risk groups and anatomical sites such as the penile and anal regions.38 These patterns underscore the vulnerability of unvaccinated cohorts and the urgent need for robust primary prevention strategies to reduce HPV transmission and long-term disease burden of all HPV-related cancers.

Advancing all three pillars of the WHO elimination strategy – vaccination, screening, and treatment – is essential to accelerate reductions in cervical cancer incidence and achieve the target of fewer than 4 cases per 100,000 women. Persistent challenges in Brazil’s historically opportunistic screening program, including limited access to early diagnosis, inadequate follow‑up, and delays in treatment, continue to sustain high incidence and mortality rates.39,40 With Brazil´s full implementation of nationwide HPV DNA testing for women aged 25–64 y by the end of 2026,15 alongside sustained high HPV vaccination coverage, catch‑up strategies for missed cohorts, and expansion of vaccination to older age groups, Brazil will substantially strengthen its cervical cancer prevention program. In this evolving context, vaccinated women will not only benefit from substantially reduced cervical cancer risk but also from fewer unnecessary interventions, minimizing overtreatment and reducing obstetric risks in future pregnancies.

Conclusions

The observed decline in high-grade CIN among adolescents provides an early indicator of HPV vaccine impact within Brazil’s public health system. Sustaining these gains will require maintaining high coverage in routine target ages, implementing catch‑up strategies for missed cohorts, and considering phased expansion to older age groups. Together with strengthened screening and treatment pathways, these efforts will accelerate progress toward WHO cervical cancer elimination goals. As vaccinated cohorts age, additional reductions are expected not only in high‑grade CIN and cervical cancer but also across the spectrum of HPV‑related cancers, underscoring the long‑term value of sustained investment in comprehensive prevention strategies.
