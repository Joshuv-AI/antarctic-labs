Harvesting Isotopically Pure Ac-225 from Ra-225 Produced in Thorium Target

Spallation Reaction

Todd N. Poe1*, Thomas Perry1, Declan Wallace1, Cathy S. Cutler1, Dmitri Medvedev1
1
 Isotope Research and Production Department, Brookhaven National Lab, Upton, NY 11973

*Corresponding Author: tpoe@bnl.gov

Abstract:

 A method for isolating Ra-225 from linear accelerator irradiated Th metal chemical

processing waste streams has been established. Separating Ra-225 from the complex matrix

following proton irradiation of Th metal provides a source of isotopically pure Ac-225 free of the

Ac-227 impurity that is present in accelerator-produced Ac-225. Ra isotopes are first separated

from the irradiated Th target via cation exchange methods. Importantly, the Ra-containing fraction

is cleaned from residual citrate via dilute nitric acid washes (0.5 M) on AG50W-X8 resin. The

mixed Ra isotopes are then further purified from Ba-140 impurity using a Sr resin column. Ra

elutes through the resin with successive washes of 2.5 M HNO3 while Ba-140 is retained. Ac-225

is then harvested by loading the Ra content onto an AG50W-X8 cation exchange column where

the Ra-225 parent is eluted with 2.5 M HNO3 before the Ac-225 daughter is eluted with 8 M HNO3.

 1. Introduction

 Medical advances in diagnosis and treatment of aggressive conditions like cancer have

become abundant in recent years due to a focus on radiopharmaceutical development. Progress in

targeted alpha therapy (TAT) research has experienced exceptional growth thanks to the

remarkable results achieved in treatment of many types of cancer with alpha emitters, such as Ac-

225 (t1/2 = 9.92 days) for example. TAT utilizes short-lived, alpha particle emitting radionuclides

 1

that are chemically bound to a complexing molecule conjugated with a biological linker that directs

the radionuclide in-vivo to the site of a cancerous tumor where alpha radiation then ablates the

diseased tissue.1 Studies have shown that the short range (50-100 µm) and high linear energy

transfer (80 keV/µm) of emission results in effective destruction of cancerous cells while also

minimizing the risk of damage to nearby healthy tissues.2, 3 While several candidates for TAT

exist, Ac-225 has attracted attention for many reasons. Perhaps most notably, the identification of

chelators such as macropa4 and DOTA5 that rapidly and effectively coordinate Ac3+ in aqueous

media. As a result, these chelators radiolabeled with Ac-225 have been the centerpiece of many

ongoing pre-clinical and clinical trials judging the efficacy of this radionuclide as a potential

radiopharmaceutical drug.6-10

 The increased interest in clinical research and the potential for a fully realized treatment

option on the horizon requires reliable means of procuring Ac-225 to satisfy demand. Initially, Ac-

225 was obtained from a U-233 (t1/2: 1.6 x 106 years) stockpile stored at Oak Ridge National Lab

that produces Th-229 (t1/2: 7,340 years) which then decays to the much shorter-lived Ra-225 (t1/2:

14.9 days). The beta decay of Ra-225 yields Ac-225 that can be isolated and purified by a series

of anion and cation exchange columns.11 Though a steady and reliable option, the stockpile leaves

little room for increasing supply of Ac-225 should demand increase.12 Accordingly, a concerted

effort to achieve Ac-225 through accelerator irradiated Th metal targets has been made to

supplement the growing interest in this radionuclide.13-15

 Acquiring Ac-225 through this method introduces several new challenges. Spallation

reactions during the proton irradiation of Th metal result in the generation of over 80 radionuclides

in addition to Ac-225 including production of both Ac-226 (t1/2: 29.37 hours) and Ac-227 (t1/2:

21.772 years).13, 16 The former is shorter lived than Ac-225 and decays more quickly, but the Ac-

 2

227 is longer lived leading to decreased purity of Ac-225 over time.17 Separation of Ac-225 and

Ac-227 is not straightforward because they are the same element from the perspective of their

chemical properties. Optimizing proton energy and irradiation time does not reduce Ac-227

impurity enough to consider it negligible.

 Separation of Th is simpler given the depth of literature precedent for separations of Ac

and Th. Early reports by Kirby18 and Sani19 proposed solid-state methods of separation involving

desorption of Ac from Ac/Th residues on Pt substrates using ammonium nitrate solution and

liquid-liquid extractions, respectively. Over time, this gave way to more sophisticated

chromatographic separations culminating in methods used to recover Ac-225 from the U-233

stockpile reported by Boll and coworkers.11 Building on this precedented variety of ion exchange

techniques, more recently Radchenko and coworkers developed a separation strategy consisting of

the cation exchange resin AG50W-X8 that exhibits high affinity for Th, Ac, Ba, and Ra in neutral

and low acidic environments.20, 21 Following the work of Chen and Wong,22 Radchenko and

coworkers processed proton-irradiated Th metal in a pH 2 citric acid media. Under these

conditions, the deprotonated citrate ions chelate the Th4+ in solution and form an anionic complex

that passes through the AG50W-X8 resin while the trivalent Ac and divalent Ba/Ra are retained.

After thoroughly debulking the Th content, the Ac, Ba, and Ra are then eluted with 6 M HNO3 and

a finer purification of Ac from the divalent isotopes is executed on DGA resin. These efforts

streamlined chemical recovery of accelerator-produced Ac-225 free of Th target material.

 Since 2020, three DOE national labs, including Brookhaven National Laboratory (BNL),

Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL), and Los Alamos National Laboratory (LANL), have

demonstrated successful production of Ac-225 from proton irradiated Th targets which has been

referred to as the tri-lab effort.23 While this bodes well for increasing the Ac-225 supply chain, the

 3

accelerator Ac-225 is inherently accompanied by the co-produced Ac-227 impurity. Researchers

note that the impurity becomes more pronounced when samples are used further from the end of

bombardment of the Th target.24

 One of the many radionuclides produced by the spallation reaction during proton-

irradiation of Th metal is Ra-225. Ra-225 isolation could provide a potential source of Ac-225 free

of the Ac-227 carrier and has been pursued by multiple groups.25, 26 Robertson and coworkers

proposed Th precipitation with peroxide before isolating Ra-225 from the irradiated Th matrix.26

This work did not, however, address the Ba-140 impurity that accompanies Ra throughout

accelerator-produced Ac-225 processing. With a half-life similar to that of Ra-225, Ba-140 (t1/2:

12.751 days) will accompany Ra through the separation process unless it is chemically removed.

In addition, since Ba decays to La-140 (t1/2: 1.68 days), and lanthanum being chemically similar

to Ac, the presence of Ba contaminates the Ac-225 product with La-140. The latter being in a

secular equilibrium with Ba will in-grow faster than Ac-225. One solution is to allow the shorter-

lived La-140 to decay to stable Ce-140 after a separation of Ac-225/La-140 from Ba-140/Ra-225,

but a considerable amount of Ac-225 will also be lost to decay. Hence, the better strategy is to

separate Ba-140 from the Ra-225 initially to eliminate ingrowth of La-140 from all future

generations of Ac-225.

 Thus, we explored alternative options for separating Ba-140 from the Ra isotopes present

in irradiated Th matrices that avoid the use of citrate altogether. In 1992, Horwitz, Chiaraza, and

Dietz published various metal retention data regarding a novel exchange resin colloquially known

as Sr resin.27 They report a retention for Ba(II) around 100 times greater than that of Ra(II) in nitric

acid solutions in the vicinity of 3 M, and more recent reports corroborate these findings in dilute

perchloric solution as well.28

 4

Intrigued by these results, we investigated Sr resin for the separation of Ba-140 from the Ra isotope

mixture obtained from accelerator irradiated Th matrices. Since the work of Horwitz and

coworkers utilized solutions containing dilute oxalic acid, it would need to be verified that the

column chemistry remains the same in the absence of a chelator. Herein, we report a procedure for

separating Ra-225 from proton irradiated Th targets where separation of Ra-225 (along with

residual Ra-223 and Ra-224) from Ba-140 was carried out using Sr resin in the absence of citrate

and other organic chelators. Additionally, we demonstrated that the Ra pool containing Ra-225

can be used for generation of isotopically pure Ac-225 using the AG50W-X8 column with 83.2%

recovery.

 2. Methods

 Warning! Some of these experiments were executed using highly radioactive materials.

Irradiated Th-232 (t1/2 = 1.40 x 1010 years1) metal targets contain 80+ radionuclide tracers, the

range of which are summarized elsewhere.13 Of note for this particular study, Ra-223 (t1/2 = 11.43

days) is an α-emitter that decays to Rn-219 (t1/2= 3.96 seconds) that is short-lived, but can be

volatilized. Ra-225 (t1/2= 14.9 days) is a β− emitter that decays to Ac-225 (t1/2= 9.9 days) a potent

α-emitter. Ba-140 (t1/2= 12.751 day) is a β− emitter that produces another potent α-emitter, La-

140 (t1/2= 1.68 days). Radioactive materials were handled with care in appropriately shielded hot

cells with mechanical manipulators. Small aliquots of samples with low activity were removed and

prepared in a radiological fume hood to prepare analytical samples.

 Materials. Nitric acid (Fisher Scientific, 67-70%, OptimaTM), ammonium hydroxide

solution (30% wt/wt, Sigma-Aldrich), and citric acid anhydrous (Fisher Scientific) were used as

obtained. DI H2O used in all experiments was procured from a MilliQ water purification system

(18 mΩ cm). Glass columns were purchased from Bio-Rad (Hercules, CA, USA). AG50W-X8

 5

resin (200-400 mesh) was purchased from Bio-Rad. Sr resin (50-100 µm) was purchased from

Eichrom. Ba and La ICP standards (1000 ppm) were purchased from Sigma Aldrich (St. Louis,

MO, USA) and diluted for ICP-OES analysis in 2% nitric acid solution as described.

 Handling Ra Fraction from the Ac-225 Production Batch. Th metal targets for

radiotracer experiments were irradiated onsite at the Brookhaven Linear-Accelerator Isotope

Producer (BLIP) facility. Target dissolution procedures were carried out as described previously.21

A brief overview of the irradiated target dissolution and processing is detailed elsewhere,29 but all

hot cell target manipulations were performed at BNL. In short, the separation of Th from Ac-225

on cation exchange column in citrate media was one of the critical steps of the process. Based on

internal production documentation it was established that Ra can be collected in the citrate column

wash step. During the first step, Ac, Ln and Ra are retained on a cation exchanger column in a

citrate matrix while bulk Th elutes. Once an experimental approach was verified with cold

experiments, the entire solution from processing of irradiated Th targets was used during the Ra

recovery experiments described herein.

 2.1 Separation of stable Ba/La on a Cation Exchange Column. A Bio-Rad glass column

(2.5 cm inner diameter) was slurry packed in DI H2O with a 30 mL AG50W-X8 resin bed. The

column was then washed with 60 mL of DI water for conditioning. A 750 mL solution of 1 M

citric acid in water was titrated to a pH of 2.00 with a few drops of 30% wt/wt ammonium

hydroxide, spiked with Ba (5 mg) and La (5 mg) from ICP calibration standards (Sigma-Aldrich).

The resulting solution was then passed through the AG50W-X8 column followed by 600 mL of 1

M citrate solution (pH = 2.00), and 150 mL of DI H2O. Finally, 240 mL of 2.5 M HNO3 was passed

through the column to elute Ba. The column loading solution was assayed before it was passed

through the column to detect initial quantities of Ba and La

 6

 2.2 Citrate Removal from Mock Target solutions. The Ba eluate was evaporated to

dryness on a hot plate. Resulting solids were redissolved in 150 mL of DI water and passed through

the AG50W-X8 column (freshly prepared as described in section 2.1). The column was washed

with 0.5 M HNO3 (300 mL) to remove citrate and to demonstrate retention of Ba on Sr resin.

Finally, Ba was recovered in 240 mL of 2.5 M HNO3. This solution was stored for future

manipulations.

 2.3 Testing Sr Column for Ba Retention. A Bio-Rad glass column (1.5 cm inner

diameter) was slurry packed with a 10 mL Sr resin bed. The column was conditioned by washing

first with 20 mL of DI water followed by 20 mL of 2.5 M HNO3. La (5 mg) was added to the 240

mL solution of 2.5 M HNO3 containing Ba from a La ICP calibration standard (Sigma-Aldrich) to

mimic Ra behavior as reported by Horwitz et al.27 Then, the solution was heated to dryness

resulting in a small quantity of colorless residue. The residue was reconstituted in 20 mL of 2.5 M

HNO3. The resulting solution was passed through the Sr resin column and collected for assay in 5

mL fractions. This was followed by 75 mL of 2.5 M HNO3 and collected in 5 mL fractions. Finally,

75 mL of 0.1 M HNO3 was passed through the column and collected in 5 mL fractions. The

resulting fractions were diluted with 2% HNO3 solution for ICP-OES analysis.

 2.4 Citrate Removal from irradiated Th Matrix. This process was carried out similarly

to section 2.2. The nitric solution (240 mL) containing Ra-225 from accelerator produced Ac-225

was heated to dryness in a hot cell yielding a white precipitate and a 30 mL AG50W-X8 column

was prepared as described in section 2.2. The precipitate was redissolved in 150 mL of DI water.

Next, the resulting solution was loaded the AG50W-X8 column. As in section 2.2, 300 mL of 0.5

M HNO3 was passed through the column to remove excess citrate. Finally, 240 mL of 2.5 M HNO3

was passed through the column to elute all activity.

 7

 2.5 Separation of Ra and Ba on Sr Resin. The 240 mL solution of 2.5 M HNO3 containing

all activity from section 2.4 was heated to dryness in a hot cell resulting in a small quantity of

colorless residue. A 10 mL Sr resin column was prepared as described previously. The residue was

reconstituted in 20 mL of 2.5 M HNO3. The resulting solution was washed through the Sr resin

column and fractions were collected for assay. An additional 35 mL of 2.5 M HNO3 was washed

through the column to recover all remaining Ra-225. Finally, 60 mL of 0.1 M HNO3 was washed

through the column to elute Ba-140. Fractions obtained from the Sr resin column were diluted in

DI H2O and analyzed by HPGe gamma spectroscopy to determine the distribution of Ra and Ba.

As a note, Ba-140 was tracked directly through the separation process via HPGe gamma

spectroscopy while Ra-223 was used to track the Ra containing fractions during this experiment.

Ra-223 can be measured by gamma spectroscopy immediately. Since Ra-223 and Ra-225 are

chemically the same element and will exhibit the same retention on the different resins used in this

study. The data we present reflects the activity of Ra-223 following all chromatographic

separations with the understanding that these fractions will also contain Ra-225. To reliably track

Ra-225 by gamma spectroscopy ion this mixture, one would have to wait several days for Ra-225

to decay to Ac-225, and then subsequently measure the daughters of Ac-225 before being able to

calculate the amount of Ra-225 present

 2.6 Ac-225/Ra-225 Generator. For these experiments, an 18-day period elapsed following

the completion of section 2.5 to allow for maximum in-growth of Ac-225. A Bio-Rad glass column

(2.5 cm inner diameter) was slurry packed in DI H2O with a 30 mL resin bed of AG50W-X8. The

column was conditioned by washing with 60 mL of DI water. Fractions from the Sr resin column

described in section 2.5 were collected in larger bulk fractions reported in Table 1 of section 3.6.

The Ra-225 containing fractions (25 mL loading fraction and 30 mL of 2.5 M HNO3 F1) were

 8

combined into a single 55 mL fraction of 2.5 M HNO3 and eluted through the AG50W-X8 column.

Then, an additional 240 mL of 2.5 M HNO3 was washed through the column to remove Ra-225.

Finally, 400 mL of 8 M HNO3 was washed through the column to elute Ac-225.

 High-Purity Germanium Gamma Spectroscopy. Gamma-ray spectroscopy was carried

out using HPGe detector GEM30P4-83 from ORTEC-AMETEK (Oak Ridge, TN, USA) with

efficiency of 30% at 1333 keV relative to NaI detector peak-to-Compton ratio 60:1. The response

function determination (energy and efficiency calibration) was performed using standard solution

of radionuclides (Eckert and Ziegler, Atlanta, GA, USA) containing 241Am, 109Cd, 57Co, 139Ce,
203
 Hg, 113Sn, 137Cs, 88Y and 60Co in quantities traceable to National Institute of Standards and

Technology (NIST, Gaithersburg, MD, USA). Counting dead time was kept at or below 15%.

Spectra acquisition and analysis was carried out using Gamma Vision Software (version 7.2,

AMETEK, Oak Ridge, USA). Ac-225 was measured using its daughter Fr-223 (t1/2 = 22.0 min)

gamma rays at 218.00 keV (11.44%) whereas Ra-223 (t1/2 = 11.4 days) was analyzed by its gamma-

rays at 324.10 keV (4.00%) and 445.50 keV (1.50%). Ba-140 was measured using its gamma rays

at 162.64 (5.07%), 304.82 keV (3.65%), and 537.38 keV (19.90%). Throughout the separation

process, both elements were detected using these gamma energies, with the same standardized

geometry as for the calibration of the detector. The material balance for Ra and Ac was followed

by measuring volumes of the starting, intermediate, and final solutions and taking exact aliquots

for gamma spectroscopy using hand pipettes. This method of assay yields errors in the activity

measurements of 5-7%, based on internal validation documentation of this assay method.

 ICP-OES Analysis. The metal content in solution for non-radioactive experiments was

measured using a Perkin Elmer Avio 560 Max Inductively Coupled Plasma Optical Emission

Spectrometer (ICP-OES). Aliquots from column fractions were diluted with 2% Optima Grade

 9

HNO3 for analysis. Calibration standard solutions were prepared by dilution of 1000 ppm Ba and

La standards to build a standard curve using the following concentrations of both metals: 0.05

ppm, 0.1 ppm, 0.25 ppm, 0.5 ppm, and 1 ppm. All samples were diluted such that measured metal

concentrations fell within the upper and lower limits of this calibration curve. Ba was detected at

a wavelength of 455.403 nm and La was detected at 408.672 and 384.902 nm (values obtained for

the La wavelengths were averaged). Vanadium was used as an internal standard (1 ppm) in all

solutions and measured at a wavelength of 290.880 nm.

 3. Results

 3.1. Separation of stable Ba/La on a Cation Exchange Column. The initial separation

mimicking the Th debulking cation exchange process was performed as described in section 2.1.

ICP-OES analysis of the pre-column solution yielded 5.35 mg Ba and 5.48 mg La. After

completing the column, the 2.5 M HNO3 wash was assayed to detect final quantities of Ba and La.

ICP-OES analysis of this solution yielded 5.09 mg Ba which corresponds to 95.1% recovery with

no detectable La.

 3.2. Citrate Removal from Mock Target solutions. Citrate removal process was carried out

as described in section 2.2. After completing the column, the 2.5 M HNO3 wash was assayed by

ICP-OES yielding 4.85 mg Ba (95.3 % recovery), an insignificant loss of Ba. Notably, upon dry

down, the Ba containing solution resulted in less solid residue present before the column procedure

indicating successful removal of citrate.

 3.3. Testing Sr Column for Ba Retention. Elution profile of Ba and La (Figure 1) shows that

elution of La starts in the Load and continues through the 2.5 M HNO3 wash. In the case of cold

surrogate studies, non-radioactive La was used as an analogue for Ra in this study as Horwitz and

coworkers reported the retention of Ra on Sr resin as nearly identical to that of the lanthanide

 10

elements.27 99% recovery of La (Ra surrogate) is observed by 40 mL total 2.5 M HNO3 eluted.

The considerable Ba retention on Sr resin during this phase confirmed adequate quantity of citrate

was removed in section 2.2. Nearly 80 mL of 2.5 M HNO3 eluted before Ba breakthrough was

observed resulting in 8% Ba contamination in the 2.5 M HNO3 wash.

 3.4. Citrate Removal from irradiated Th Matrix. Target processing was performed as

discussed in section 2.1. Upon initial dry down of the 2.5 M HNO3 fraction obtained from the Th

target processing a noticeable amount of white precipitate was observed (Figure 2). This solid was

reconstituted and treated to remove citrate (see Section 2.4). Significant reduction in quantity of

white precipitate (Figure 2) mirrored the decrease in solid precipitate observed during cold studies

(section 2.2) indicating successful removal of citrate.

 4. Discussion

 The processing of the larger irradiated Th metal matrix necessitates scale up of the process

than that described by Radchenko and coworkers.21 Most notably, the process (discussed in section

2.1) includes a washing of 2.5 M HNO3 (240 mL). It was determined that this fraction contains the

Ra produced by spallation reaction (radionuclides and activities at end of bombardment: 223.0

mCi Ra-223, 313.3 mCi Ra-224, 16.1 mCi Ra-225) this fraction also contains residual citrate.

Unfortunately, due to the nature of working with highly radioactive samples, the exact amount of

citrate can be difficult to quantify directly. Following the work of Horwitz,27 it was ascertained

that increasing the concentration of citrate in solutions put through Sr resin columns resulted in

decreased Ba retention (Figure S1). The work of Mastren and coworkers does include the use of

dilute nitric acid to remove excess citrate before recovering Ra/Ac.25 A step for removal of citrate

 14

was added for the separation of Ba and Ra on Sr resin to be successful, especially considering the

eventuality of target material scale-up from tens to hundreds of grams.

 A cation exchange column that mimicked the conditions of the original column proposed

by Radchenko and coworkers21 was chosen to debulk residual citrate for two reasons. First,

AG50W-X8 resin exhibits exceptional retention for metal ions at low concentrations of nitric acid

allowing for copious eluant to be passed through the column without breakthrough of activity.20

Second, establishing that citrate could be debulked on a column of the same size as proposed by

Mastren and coworkers25 meant that this step could be easily substituted for the DI H2O

transitionary step for media (see section 2.1) that would be more effective at citrate removal

without disrupting the isolation of accelerator produced Ac-225 from thorium target matrices. This

was investigated by first executing mock cation exchange columns as described in section 2.1.

Then, after drying down the mock 2.5 M waste fraction containing non-radioactive Ba as a tracer

for both Ba and Ra, the obtained solid was redissolved in DI H2O and loaded onto a fresh AG50W-

X8 column. This procedure was repeated several times to test citrate debulking solutions of DI

H2O, 0.1 M HNO3, and 0.5 M HNO3 of varying volumes before the Ba was stripped from the

column with 240 mL of 2.5 M HNO3. The solution was then dried and reconstituted in a smaller

volume of 2.5 M HNO3 (20-25 mL) to be loaded onto the Sr resin column to assess separation

efficiency and Ba retention. As mentioned previously, non-radioactive La was used as an analogue

for Ra given their similarity in behavior on Sr resin.27

 As was anticipated, DI H2O was not sufficient for removing citrate on AG50W-X8 resin.

Subsequent separations of La and Ba on Sr resin failed even for citrate debulking columns where

1.5 liters of DI water were passed through the column before recovering Ba (Figure S2). Increasing

the concentration of nitric acid wash solutions to 0.5 M HNO3 provided a more reliable removal

 15

of citrate with smaller wash volumes leading to good separation of La and Ba on Sr resin (section

3.3, Figure 1). In fact, following citrate debulking with 0.5 M HNO3, it was observed that after

metal content was loaded on the Sr resin column, an additional 45 mL of 2.5 M HNO3 could be

washed through the column before breakthrough of Ba was observed (Figure 1).

 Given the successful separation of La and Ba on Sr resin following removal of citrate via

a AG50W-X8 cation exchange column, it was necessary to verify that this method would be

sufficient for separation of Ba and Ra from accelerator produced Ac-225 matrices. This was

executed in a hot cell following the procedures outlined in section 2.4 and 2.5 of the Methods. The

procedures described in these sections were repeated on two separate irradiated Th matrices that

had first been subjected to the cation exchange procedures discussed in section 2.1.

 Photographs taken during the experiment described in section 3.4 show that a generous

amount of solid was removed during this step (Figure 2). The same observation was made during

cold experiments following the citrate removal column signifying successful removal of citrate in

the radiotracer experiment. This smaller quantity of residue was redissolved in 2.5 M HNO3 (25

mL) and loaded onto Sr resin columns of the parameters described in section 2.5. Figure 3 in

section 3.5 shows the elution of the two isotopes across the collected fractions. Like the surrogate

non-radioactive La studies that were previously discussed, Ra-223 was observed eluting in the

loading phase of the column, and continuing to elute as 2.5 M HNO3 was flowed through the

column. Ba-140 was more strongly retained by the resin under the flow of 2.5 M HNO3, and the

small quantity of breakthrough observed did not occur until Ra-223 had ceased eluting.

Furthermore, we note that the quantity of Ba-140 present was small enough that, unlike the non-

radioactive experiments performed previously, elution of Ba-140 under the flow of 0.1 M HNO3

was slow with some of the activity remaining stuck to the column. This observation was especially

 16

encouraging for the purposes of establishing a Ra-225/Ac-225 generator free of the Ba-140/La-

140 impurity.

 The experiment described previously was performed again as described in section 2.6.

Unlike the experiment in section 2.5, fractions were bulk collected, and the percent recovery of

Ra-223 and Ba-140 found in each fraction following separation on Sr resin are reported in Table

1 of section 3.6. Like in section 3.5, it was observed that nearly the entirety of the Ra-223 activity

elutes during the loading phase of the Sr column and the next 30 mL of 2.5 M HNO 3. In further

2.5 M HNO3 washes, Ba-140 begins to break through, but the Ra lost (0.1275%) is negligible in

comparison to what is recovered in the first 55 mL of 2.5 M HNO3 washes (98.58%). As with the

first experiment, high Ba retention was observed evidenced by the lack of activity recovered when

the column was washed with 60 mL of 0.1 M HNO3 acid. It is likely that the small amount of Ra

content lost to Ba breakthrough could be recovered in future experiments by collecting smaller

fractions, but for the purposes of this study, the measured 98.58% recovery of Ra-223 was

sufficient to follow through with establishing a method of recovering Ac-225 from the

accompanying Ra-225 in these fractions.

 For establishing a Ra-225/Ac-225 generator we used the AG50W-X8 cation exchange

column parameters established by Radchenko and coworkers for debulking Th.21 After waiting for

the in-growth of Ac-225 to reach equilibrium with the Ra-225 parent (~18 days), Ra-223

containing fractions were pooled as described in section 3.6, and the column procedure described

in section 2.6 was executed. The resulting distribution of activity as a percentage of the total

activity loaded on the column is reported in Table 2 of section 3.6. Using this method, 83.2%

recovery of Ac-225 is observed, with an impurity of Ra-223 in the first fraction of the 8 M HNO3

wash equaling 2.14% of the Ra-223 activity loaded on the column. Additionally, Ac-225 is

 17

observed in the fractions containing the Ra-223 recovered in 2.5 M HNO3, but the activity

observed in these fractions is consistent with the decay of Ra-225 rather than unanticipated

breakthrough.

 Figure 4 summarizes the proposed series of columns for isolating Ra-225 from accelerator-

irradiated Th matrices. This proposed procedure does not include a classical generator setup for

procuring Ac-225 where the Ra-225 parent remains fixed on the column resin. Instead, Ra-225 is

recovered in 2.5 M HNO3 before elution of the desired Ac-225 in 8 M HNO3. Still, the recovered

Ra-225 can be reloaded onto a fresh AG50W-X8 column when desired to obtain Ac-225 by the

same procedure. It should also be noted that the first AG50W-X8 column used for the removal of

citrate could be avoided in the future if the initial Th target processing column (section 2.1)

included a citrate washing step of 0.5 M HNO3 before Ba/Ra is recovered similar to what previous

reports have described.21, 25 This would further streamline the recovery of Ra-225, and thus

production of carrier free Ac-225.

 5. Conclusion

 Overall, the process we report indicates that isotopically pure Ac-225 can be harvested

from Ra-225 obtained from accelerator-irradiated Th matrices for the production of Ac-225.

Importantly, we show that the same AG50W-X8 column parameters reported for processing

irradiated Th matrices are sufficient for citrate debulking with 0.5 M HNO3 acid washes without

concern of activity breakthrough. The citrate debulking column described here could, in fact, be

targets.

 18

eliminated entirely from this process by washing the original cation exchange column with 0.5 M

HNO3 following Th debulking rather than DI H2O to ensure removal of citrate leading to a faster

recovery of Ra-225. Regardless, adequate removal of citrate from Ra-225 containing solutions

obtained from irradiated Th matrices leads to a straightforward separation of the Ba-140 impurity

using Eichrom’s Sr resin, with greater than 98% recovery of Ra. We note that the separation could

be optimized further by collecting smaller volumes of 2.5 M HNO3 washes to recover even greater

quantities of Ra without risking Ba-140 breakthrough. Similarly, AG50W-X8 cation exchange

resin, while sufficient for milking isotopically pure Ac-225 from the pooled Ra-225, did result in

a small contamination of Ra-223 in the Ac-225 containing 8 M HNO3 fractions. This is likely

mitigated by using a larger wash volume of 2.5 M HNO3 before stripping the Ac-225 from the

column with 8 M HNO3. Despite these minor discrepancies, the method we report is remarkably

reliable for production of isotopically pure Ac-225 from Ra-225 isolated during accelerator

production of Ac-225 via irradiated Th metal.
