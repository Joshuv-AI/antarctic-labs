The relationship between future anxiety and global climate change awareness among physiotherapy students in Turkey: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Esedullah Akaras, Uğur Sözlü
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe climate crisis is not just an environmental issue; it’s a profound public health emergency that is deeply affecting the mental well-being of young people, leading to widespread eco-anxiety. As the future frontline of healthcare, physiotherapy students will inevitably have to manage the health consequences of climate change. Yet, we know surprisingly little about how prepared they are specifically, how their own awareness of the crisis connects with their anxiety about the future. This exploratory study aimed to provide preliminary findings on the relationship between global climate change awareness and future anxiety among physiotherapy students in Turkey.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional study with 394 physiotherapy students (mean age 21.3 ± 3.0; 74.6% female) from the state universities. Using an online survey, we gathered data on their demographic and social characteristics, along with their scores on two validated scales: the Awareness Scale of University Students About Global Climate Change (ASUSGCC) and the Future Anxiety Scale for University Students (FASUS). We analyzed the data using Pearson correlation to examine the relationship between climate change awareness and future anxiety, and univariable linear regression analyses to explore factors associated with climate change awareness.ResultsOverall, students reported a moderate level of climate change awareness (ASUSGCC mean: 3.4 ± 0.7) and a notable level of future anxiety (FASUS mean: 61.6 ± 8.6). We found a statistically significant, positive, but weak correlation between total climate awareness and total future anxiety (r = 0.168, p < 0.01).The regression analyses showed that several variables were statistically associated with higher awareness scores, although the explained variance was low. Female gender (β = 0.273, p = 0.001), environmental volunteering (β = 0.180, p = 0.019), and more frequent use of social media for climate-related information (β = 0.167, p = 0.001) were associated with higher climate change awareness. Higher total future anxiety was weakly but statistically significantly associated with higher awareness scores (β = 0.013, p = 0.001). In subscale-level analyses, Future Fear was also weakly associated with awareness (β = 0.009, p = 0.015). Previous environmental training was not significantly associated with climate change awareness (p = 0.466).ConclusionsAmong physiotherapy students, climate change awareness was weakly but statistically significantly associated with future anxiety. Environmental volunteering and more frequent exposure to climate-related content on social media were also associated with higher awareness scores, whereas previous environmental training was not significantly associated with awareness. However, given the cross-sectional design and possible self-selection into volunteering, these findings should be interpreted as associations rather than evidence that volunteering increases awareness. Future longitudinal or intervention-based studies are needed to examine whether structured environmental engagement can improve climate literacy in physiotherapy education.

Introduction

Climate change is now widely recognized as a profound threat to human health and wellbeing. International health organizations warn that climate change will exacerbate disease, pollution, disasters and social upheaval, posing “the greatest threat to human health of the 21st century” [1]. Beyond these physical impacts, the climate crisis also generates substantial psychological distress. A growing literature describes eco-anxiety or climate anxiety as chronic worry, helplessness and grief about environmental decline [2]. Many young people report feeling distressed about an unstable climate future, even experiencing such anxiety without directly experiencing disasters. For example, large surveys have found that the majority of adolescents and young adults view climate change as frightening and report concern for the future [3]. In psychology, eco-anxiety is typically defined as subclinical anxiety, guilt or sadness aroused by thoughts of climate change or ecological loss [4]. In contrast, future anxiety is a broader construct – it denotes a cognitive–emotional state of apprehension or fear about one’s personal future and anticipated negative events [5]. Climate anxiety can thus be seen as a specific form of future-oriented concern tied to environmental issues, whereas future anxiety also encompasses worries about personal career, economic or social challenges. Climate change awareness, meanwhile, refers to one’s informed understanding of climate science, impacts, and mitigation efforts (e.g. causes of global warming, effects on health and ecosystems). These three concepts – climate awareness, climate/eco-anxiety, and future anxiety – intersect. In many studies, higher climate change awareness among young people or students has been linked paradoxically to greater anxiety about climate or the future [6, 7]. In other words, students who know more about climate threats often report more worry. At the same time, awareness may also motivate engagement: some authors note that climate anxiety can coexist with hope and pro-environmental intent [8]. Overall, the literature suggests a complex interplay: informed students may feel vulnerable about the future but could channel that concern into action.

Health profession students are a key group for this dynamic. Nursing and medical students have begun to show the same patterns. For instance, Turkish nursing students had only moderate climate-change knowledge but above-average climate anxiety, and higher awareness modestly predicted higher anxiety similarly found that climate awareness significantly predicted “eco-anxiety” in nursing undergraduates [7]. In another recent Turkish study, awareness of climate change was positively associated with hopelessness and climate anxiety among youth activists [6]. Correspondingly, Erkan and Budak reported that among nursing students in Turkey, higher scores on a climate change awareness scale (ASUSGCC) went hand-in-hand with higher scores on a future anxiety scale [9]. In short, emerging research shows that as students learn about climate risks, their anxieties often rise (while still being moderate in absolute level). These findings underscore that climate knowledge and psychological vulnerability are intertwined: educating students about global warming can inadvertently increase worry, unless paired with coping resources and empowerment.

Physiotherapy students warrant special attention in this context. As future health professionals, physiotherapists already occupy a unique place in prevention and rehabilitation. International bodies now urge physiotherapists to actively engage in planetary health and sustainability. World Physiotherapy’s 2022 policy statement declares the climate crisis an emergency and explicitly calls on physiotherapists to reduce the healthcare carbon footprint and build capacity for climate mitigation and adaptation [10]. It emphasizes that “physiotherapists have a role to play in addressing practices that support sustainability and health promotion,” including raising awareness and advocacy. Leading educators argue that physiotherapy curricula must be reoriented around planetary health, using active, service-learning approaches to teach environmental competence [11]. In this light, developing physiotherapy students’ climate literacy is seen as essential not only to leverage their potential in promoting active transport and healthy lifestyles, but also to foster hope and agency rather than helplessness. For example, Paz-Lourido and Ribeiro-Chaves urge a “reimagining of humanity’s relationship with the planet” in physiotherapy education, calling for “active engagement” and solution-oriented learning as part of an eco-social curriculum reform [11].

Despite these calls, there has been almost no empirical research on climate-related attitudes and anxieties in physiotherapy students. Empirical evidence on how global climate change awareness relates to future-oriented psychological concerns among physiotherapy students remains limited. This is a clear gap: physiotherapy students face the same climate future as other youth, yet their educational programs and professional focus are distinct from those of nursing or medicine. Preliminary surveys suggest that physiotherapy undergraduates already report substantial “future anxiety” related to socioeconomic pressures [12], but it is unknown how this overlaps with environmental concerns. Filling this gap is important. Understanding the levels of climate change awareness and future anxiety in physiotherapy students can inform how education programs integrate planetary health content and provide psychological support. Although eco-anxiety provides an important conceptual background, the present study specifically measured general future anxiety rather than climate-specific anxiety. By focusing on Turkish physiotherapy students specifically, the present study aims to document both their knowledge of climate change and their personal worries about the future, and to analyze the relationship between these variables. This will help identify whether more climate awareness is linked to greater anxiety in this population, and guide educators in balancing factual instruction with resilience-building for a sustainable professional role.

Methods

Study design

A cross-sectional, correlational study design was employed to examine the relationship between study variables.

Setting and participants

The study population comprised undergraduate students (Years 1–4) enrolled in physiotherapy and rehabilitation programs in Turkey. Participants were recruited from five state universities located in different geographical regions of Turkey: Erzurum Technical University, Tokat Gaziosmanpaşa University, Bursa Uludağ University, Gazi University, and Hatay Mustafa Kemal University. These universities represent the Eastern Anatolia, Black Sea, Marmara, Central Anatolia, and Mediterranean regions, respectively. A convenience sampling method was used. Data were collected via an online Google Forms survey between August and November 2025. Of the 560 students invited to participate, 425 agreed to take part, yielding an initial participation rate of 75.9%. After excluding 31 incomplete or incorrect responses, 394 students were included in the final analysis, corresponding to a final valid response rate of 70.4% among invited students.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the Erzurum Technical University Scientific Research and Publication Ethics Committee (Date: 10.07.2025; Decision No: 10/03). Participants were informed about the study’s purpose, their right to withdraw, and the anonymity of their data. Electronic informed consent was obtained from all participants before they could access the survey.

Data collection instruments

The survey consisted of three sections:

Participant Information Form: This form, developed by the researchers, collected sociodemographic data (age, gender, university year) and information on environmental engagement. Engagement was assessed through questions regarding prior environmental volunteering, receiving any environment-related training or academic courses, participation in climate campaigns, frequency of following climate change content on social media (5-point scale from “Never” to “Always”), and consideration of an environment-related career. Awareness Scale of University Students about Global Climate Change (ASUSGCC): Developed and validated in Turkish by Deniz et al. [13], this scale measures students’ awareness of climate change. It consists of 21 items rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = Strongly Disagree, 5 = Strongly Agree). The scale has four subscales: ‘awareness of effects on natural and human environment,’ ‘awareness of global organizations and agreements,’ ‘awareness of causes,’ and ‘awareness of energy consumption relation. The ASUSGCC can be scored using total or mean item scores. In the present study, mean item scores were used for the total scale and subscales, with higher scores indicating greater climate change awareness. The original study reported a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.83 for the total scale [13]. Future Anxiety Scale in University Students (FASUS): This scale measures the level of anxiety students feel about their future. Its use in similar Turkish student populations has been established [14]. The scale yields a total score and two subscale scores used in this study’s analysis: ‘Future Fear’ and ‘Despair of the Future.’ The total score ranges from 24 to 86, with higher scores indicating greater future anxiety. The original validation study reported a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.91 for the total scale [14].

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). Descriptive statistics (mean ± standard deviation [SD], frequency [n], and percentage [%]) were used to summarize participant characteristics and scale scores. The normality of data distribution was assessed. Cronbach’s alpha coefficients were calculated to assess the internal consistency reliability of the scales in the present sample. Pearson correlation coefficient (r) was used to determine the direction and strength of the linear relationship between the total scores of the ASUSGCC and the FASUS.

Univariable linear regression analyses were conducted to explore factors associated with the dependent variable, the total ASUSGCC score. Independent variables included demographic factors (age, gender), environmental engagement variables (training, social media use, etc.), and the two FASUS subscales (Future Fear, Despair of the Future) to avoid multicollinearity with the total FASUS score. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 394 physiotherapy students were included in the analysis (mean age 21.3 ± 3.0 years); 74.6% were female and the distribution across study years was relatively balanced (21.4% first year, 24.1% second, 27.9% third, 26.6% fourth). Just over a quarter reported previous environmental volunteering (27.4%), one fifth had received some form of environment- or climate-related training (20.8%), and only 4.8% had ever taken an academic course specifically on climate change. Participation in climate-change campaigns was low (12.4%). Almost half of the sample reported “sometimes” following climate-related content on social media (46.2%), whereas only a small minority did so “often” or “always” (7.6% and 1.3%, respectively). Regarding future plans, 15.5% stated that they would consider an environmental career, while 51.3% were undecided. Unemployment was the most frequently reported source of career anxiety (51.0%), and overall climate-change awareness levels were predominantly moderate (64.0%), with 30.5% classified as high and 5.5% as low (Table 1).

The internal consistency of the scales was acceptable to excellent in the present sample. Cronbach’s alpha coefficients were 0.909 for the Awareness Scale of University Students about Global Climate Change and 0.770 for the Future Anxiety Scale in University Students. On the ASUSGCC, the mean total awareness score was 3.4 ± 0.7 (range 1–5). Among its subscales, awareness of environmental effects showed the highest mean (3.9 ± 0.8), followed by energy-related awareness (3.7 ± 1.1), whereas awareness of organizations/agreements (2.8 ± 1.1) and especially awareness of causes (2.5 ± 1.7) were comparatively lower, indicating important knowledge gaps in etiological and policy-level aspects of climate change. The mean FASUS total score was 61.6 ± 8.6 (range 24–86), with subscale means of 41.6 ± 9.7 for Future Fear and 20.1 ± 3.7 for Despair of the Future, suggesting a generally elevated level of future-oriented concern in this cohort. Correlation analysis showed a weak but statistically significant positive association between total climate-change awareness and total future anxiety (r = 0.168, p < 0.01) and between awareness and Future Fear (r = 0.123, p < 0.05), whereas the correlation with Despair of the Future was negligible and not statistically significant (r = 0.068). All ASUSGCC subscales were strongly inter-correlated (r = 0.56–0.79 with the total score), and small but significant positive correlations emerged between several awareness subscales (particularly environmental effects and energy) and FASUS total scores (r up to 0.25, p < 0.01), further supporting a modest “awareness–anxiety” linkage (Table 2).

According to the univariable linear regression analysis, the explained variance remained low across all statistically significant models (R2 = 0.0137–0.0303). This indicates that while certain variables were statistically significant, their effect sizes were limited. Female students had significantly higher climate change awareness scores than male students (β = 0.2733, p = 0.001). Additionally, students with prior volunteering experience related to the environment or climate exhibited higher awareness levels than those without such experience (β = 0.1627, p = 0.019). More frequent use of social media for climate change-related content was also associated with higher awareness scores (β = 0.1674, p = 0.001). Furthermore, future anxiety showed a statistically significant but weak association with climate change awareness (β = 0.0133, p = 0.001), with limited explanatory power. Conversely, age, grade level, receipt of environmental or climate-related training, enrollment in academic courses on climate change, participation in climate campaigns, environmental career intentions, source of career anxiety, and future hopelessness were not statistically significantly associated with climate change awareness (all p > 0.05) (Table 3; Fig. 1).

Discussion

This exploratory study provides preliminary evidence on the relationship between future anxiety and global climate change awareness among physiotherapy students in Turkey. The primary finding was a weak but statistically significant positive correlation between climate change awareness and future anxiety (r = 0.168). This indicates that students with higher awareness scores tended to report slightly higher future anxiety scores; however, the magnitude of this association was small and should be interpreted cautiously.

This “awareness-anxiety” link, though weak, is consistent with a growing body of literature on the psychological burden of climate change. The finding directly mirrors recent studies conducted with Turkish nursing students, which also utilized the ASUSGCC and FASUS scales and found a similar positive, weak-to-moderate relationship [9, 15]. This emerging pattern across different health disciplines suggests that higher awareness of global crises may coexist with higher levels of future-oriented concern, although the direction and causality of this association remain unclear. In the regression analyses, Future Fear, but not Despair of the Future, was weakly associated with climate change awareness. This may indicate that awareness is more closely related to apprehensive concern than to hopelessness, a distinction that may be useful for future educational and psychological support strategies.

The regression findings also suggested that demographic characteristics, informal exposure to climate-related information, and environmental engagement were associated with climate change awareness. Female gender, environmental volunteering, and more frequent use of social media for climate change-related information were each associated with higher awareness scores. However, the explained variance was low, and these associations should not be interpreted as strong predictors or causal effects. In particular, the association between volunteering and awareness may partly reflect self-selection, as students who are already interested in environmental issues may be more likely to participate in volunteering activities. Together with the finding that only a small proportion of students had taken an academic course specifically on climate change, these results suggest that formal curricular exposure to climate-related content was limited in this sample.

Previous environmental training and having taken an academic course on climate change were not statistically significantly associated with climate change awareness in this sample, whereas environmental volunteering was associated with higher awareness scores. This pattern should be interpreted cautiously. It may suggest that experiential or practice-based engagement is relevant to climate awareness, but it may also reflect the possibility that students with higher pre-existing awareness are more likely to seek volunteering opportunities. Therefore, the present findings do not establish that volunteering increases awareness. Future longitudinal and intervention-based studies are needed to examine whether structured service-learning or environmental volunteering activities can improve climate literacy among physiotherapy students. Nevertheless, the broader literature supports the educational value of experiential and community-oriented planetary health learning approaches [16–18].

Implications for medical and health education

The findings of this study present a dual mandate for physiotherapy and broader health professions education.

First, the findings support the relevance of integrating planetary health and environmental sustainability into physiotherapy curricula [19–21]. Given the low proportion of students who had taken an academic course on climate change, many students may be obtaining climate-related information from informal sources such as social media. Educational institutions have a responsibility to fill this gap with evidence-based, solutions-oriented content.

Second, educators cannot simply add climate change information to an already demanding curriculum without addressing the psychological fallout. Our findings are consistent with the possibility of an awareness-anxiety link, although the weak association and cross-sectional design preclude causal interpretation. Therefore, future curricular initiatives may benefit from psychologically informed and solution-oriented approaches [22]. This involves embedding mental health support, teaching emotional regulation strategies, and fostering “constructive hope” [23]. The goal is to move students from anxiety to empowerment and agency [24], equipping them to be resilient leaders rather than burned-out providers.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precludes any inference of causality. Therefore, it is not possible to determine whether greater climate change awareness increases future anxiety, whether students with higher future anxiety are more likely to seek climate-related information, or whether these associations are bidirectional. Similarly, the positive association between environmental volunteering and awareness should be interpreted cautiously because students who self-select into volunteering may already have higher interest in, or awareness of, environmental issues. Second, the use of convenience sampling and the predominance of female participants may limit the generalizability of the findings. Third, all data were based on self-report, which may be subject to social desirability and recall bias. Fourth, this study measured general future anxiety rather than climate-specific anxiety or eco-anxiety; therefore, the findings should not be interpreted as direct evidence of eco-anxiety. Finally, the correlation between total future anxiety and total climate-change awareness was weak but statistically significant (r = 0.168), and the explained variance in the regression analyses was low, suggesting that future anxiety and climate change awareness are complex constructs influenced by multiple individual, educational, social, and socioeconomic factors [12].

Conclusions

Physiotherapy students in this study demonstrated a weak but statistically significant positive association between global climate change awareness and future anxiety. Environmental volunteering and more frequent exposure to climate-related content on social media were also associated with higher awareness scores, but these findings should be interpreted cautiously because of the cross-sectional design, low explained variance, and possible self-selection into volunteering. Overall, the results provide preliminary support for integrating planetary health and environmental sustainability topics into physiotherapy education. Such integration should be evidence-based, solution-oriented, and accompanied by strategies that promote constructive engagement rather than increasing students’ future-related concerns.
