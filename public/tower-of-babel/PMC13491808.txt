A deep learning approach for predicting antimicrobial resistance in urinary tract infections
Authors: Marian Shaker, Adel Zaki, Sara Lofty Asser, Iman El Sayed, Mohamed Moustafa Azmy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDrug resistance is a crucial issue in patients with urinary tract infection (UTI). This study aimed to detect drug-resistant (DR) UTI cases using suitable machine learning classifiers for early prediction of DR UTI.MethodsThree databases were used. The first database was from UTI patients at Alexandria University Hospital in Egypt. The second database was from a general clinic in Northern Mindanao, Philippines. The third was from Al-Rafa Laboratory, IBB University, IBB City, Yemen. Novel mathematical methods were compared for the detection of DR UTI patients in database 1, positive UTI patients or not in database 2, and multi-drug resistance (MDR) or not in database 3. These methods were compared to extract features from the used databases based on novel engineering calculation methods. After that, several classifiers were compared using these features to determine whether the UTI patients were DR to the antibiotics or not. The classifier used was based on a deep learning method, which was known as bidirectional long short-term memory (BILSTM). The logistic regression and gradient boosting classifiers were also compared. Each database was classified based on holdout or K-fold cross-validation methods.ResultsThe selected approach for extracting values of the UTI patients was based on the fractional discrete cosine decomposition method (FrdcDM). The obtained accuracy rate and the area under the curve (AUC) of this method were the highest when it was applied to the three databases, based on hold-out and K-fold cross-validation methods, using deep learning. For example, the accuracy rate and AUC after applying this method on database 1, using deep learning as a classification method based on the holdout validation method, were 97.4% and 95%, respectively. If the same model was applied to database 2, the accuracy and the AUC rates were 99.1% and 99.5%, respectively. The accuracy and AUC rates after applying the method for database 3 were 98.2%, 98.6%, respectively, based on the deep learning of the holdout cross-validation method. The classification parameter rates of the deep learning were the highest compared with the other classifiers, such as logistic regression and gradient boosting, especially using databases 1 and 2.ConclusionThe classification parameters of the selected method were the highest compared to other methods. Therefore, this method could be used to detect DR UTI in patients. This model showed potential for assisting in DR prediction. Therefore, physicians are helped to choose the right plan and improve patient health.Keywords: UTI, deep learning, FDM, FRFT, DCT

Introduction

Urinary tract infection (UTI) is common in most communities and hospitals due to bacterial infections [1]. Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is a crisis because of the inability to treat infections [2]. AMR occurs when bacteria, viruses, fungi, and parasites do not respond to medications. Therefore, AMR may cause several diseases and lead to death [3]. The increased patient morbidity and mortality are due to AMR to antibiotics [4]. Increasing AMR causes UTI inside and outside hospitals [5]. The most common cause of UTI is Escherichia coli. The symptoms of UTI patients often include a burning sensation during micturition and lumbar pain. Most patients with UTI can be treated with antibiotics. There are nearly 700,000 deaths annually due to AMR. There may be 10 million deaths per year by 2050 [6]. Although many studies have been conducted to predict drug-resistant UTI patients using machine learning, the classification metrics were low. Therefore, they may lead to poor diagnosis results for physicians. There are many factors of drug resistance in UTI patients, such as age, sex, history of UTI, diabetes mellitus (DM), prostate disease, urinary catheter used before hospitalization, residence in a nursing home, and prior antibiotic treatment [7]. There were few studies on multidrug-resistant UTI in EGYPT without presenting independent antimicrobial resistance predictors [8, 9].

There were several types of drug resistance: multidrug-resistant (MDR), extensively drug-resistant (XDR), and pan-drug-resistant (PDR). MDR has resistance to three or more classes of antibiotics. XDR is defined as resistance to all but one or two antibiotic classes. PDR is non-susceptible to all agents in all antimicrobial classes.

In this study, a drug-resistant UTI was defined microbiologically based on urine culture and antimicrobial susceptibility testing. All clinically significant uropathogens were included; the gram-negative bacteria were the primary cause, led by E.Coli and Klebsiella pneumonia. The applied machine learning model was used to predict multidrug resistance (MDR), which is defined as non-susceptibility to at least one agent in three or more antimicrobial categories, according to the definitions of the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control (ECDC) and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). The resistance was assessed across multiple antimicrobial classes rather than for a single drug. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed using standard disk diffusion and the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) methods. The results were interpreted according to the Clinical & Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines, which were updated annually and applied consistently throughout the study period (2018–2020).”

The main goal of this study was to detect DR UTI cases using machine-learning classifiers after extracting their features. The selected classifier was chosen because of its high classification parameters. In this study, the problem of low parameter classification rates for the prediction of DR UTI cases in previous studies was addressed, related to the used environments, as stated in the discussion section.

A deep learning (DL) algorithm was used for the detection of AMR. A large amount of data on AMR could be analyzed to identify different patterns related to AMR. Resistance could be predicted using ML algorithms based on previous data. Therefore, physicians could suggest decisions for the best treatment methods. New antibiotics could be discovered using ML algorithms by analyzing AMR data to obtain candidates that face resistance. Understanding the classification of antibiotic resistance in urinary tract infections is crucial for guiding effective treatment decisions and improving patient outcomes in clinical practice.

In clinical practice, risk factors for Antimicrobial Resistance (AMR) do not act in isolation; rather, they function as a complex, non-linear ‘signal’ of the patient’s biological and environmental history. Transforming structured clinical variables (such as duration of catheterization and history of antibiotic use) allows the model to capture multi-dimensional interactions. Just as signal processing identifies patterns in noisy data, our approach treats individual clinical variables as interrelated features. For example, the interaction between ‘age’ and ‘prior antibiotic use’ is not merely additive; it represents a cumulative physiological stressor that shifts the microbial baseline. By applying transformation techniques, we can extract ‘latent features’ from standard records that traditional linear models might miss. This is particularly relevant in urology, where the ‘noise’ of varying hospital stay durations and multiple interventions can obscure the primary predictors of MDR.

This study was divided into five sections. The materials and methods used were presented in the following sections. Then, the results were obtained. After that, discussions were presented. Finally, conclusions were discussed. As shown in Fig. 1, the pipeline for selecting a suitable model was represented. First, datasets were collected from Alexandria University, the Philippines Center, and IBB University. Second, the data were preprocessed by removing empty rows and unifying all variables for each attribute by converting them to numeric values. Third, several methods were proposed for extracting features from the three datasets. Fourth, features were extracted to reduce the dimensions of the datasets. Fifth, the obtained features for each patient were classified using various machine learning methods and evaluated using classification metrics, including accuracy, sensitivity, and precision. Finally, the method with the highest parameter rates was selected.

Materials and methods

The obtained novel methods were deduced from classical methods, as seen in the next subsections. They were used to extract features of each UTI patient. Therefore, the highest classification rates might be achieved.

Fractional fourier transform & fractional discrete cosine transform

The Fractional Fourier Transform (FRFT) represented a signal in the frequency and fractional domains [10, 11]. The signals were represented in the frequency domain only using the Fourier transform (FT). Therefore, FT was not practical for dealing with different types of signals, such as nonstationary signals. They varied in both the time and frequency domains. Using FRFT, signals were represented in frequency and fraction domains.

The fractional discrete cosine transform (FRDCT) was derived from the FRFT and discrete cosine transform (DCT) [12]. Therefore, the advantages of both DCT and FRFT were achieved using FRDCT. It overcame the drawbacks of both. The high-frequency components of signals were poorly addressed in the DCT. The time localization was not inherently available in the FRFT.

Fourier decomposition method (FDM)

The Fourier decomposition method (FDM) of a signal was obtained using the Fourier transform to make a set of Fourier intrinsic band functions (FIBFs). The obtained amplitude and frequency were functions of time. Therefore, the analysis of non-stationary signals using this method was suitable. Energy was preserved in the FIBFs. In addition, FIBFs were adaptive and local. A signal x[n] was decomposed into several FIBFs with suitable groups of frequencies. The energy of the signal was retained in the FDM. The signal x(n) was decomposed into b0 and the sum xl(n), as shown in Eq. (1). Here, b0 was the mean value of the signal. The lth orthogonal LINOEP (linearly independent, non-orthogonal yet energy preserving) was pl. Here, xl is the FIBFs. Each of the FIBFs could be obtained using a fast Fourier transform (FFT) and a zero-phase filtered bank (Hl(k)) of the input signal. The filter type used was a low-pass filter. The frequency response of the zero-phase filter Hl(k) equaled 1 in the desired frequency band. The Hl(k) equaled 0 to maintain the frequency response of the lth band [13–14]. It was referred to as FDM-dft. Here, x(n) was decomposed into FIBFs xlx2(n), ….xm(n). The FDM-dft could be obtained by calculating the FFT of x(n) to be X(k), where N was the FFT length of x(n), as shown in Eq. (2). N was equal to 136. Furthermore, FIBFs could be obtained from the inverse fast Fourier transform (IFFT) by multiplying Hl(k) and X(k), as shown in Eq. (3).

The response of each filter was defined by setting the FDM to use zero-phase filtering with non-negative real values. FDM was considered to provide good results in extracting the features of the signal by keeping its maxima and minima [13–14].

Fractional fourier decomposition method & Fractional discrete cosine decomposition method

The fractional Fourier decomposition method (FrFDM) and the fractional discrete cosine decomposition method (FrdcDM) were novel transforms. They were deduced from the FDM. All steps of the FrFDM and the FrdcDM were the same as FDM except replacing Eq. (2) with Eqs. (4,5), respectively. These novel transforms were obtained to extract features of UTI patients. They could be used to extract features and classify various biomedical signals. The optimum classification parameters might be obtained using these transforms, as shown in the results section.

Classification method

Deep learning

Deep learning is a type of machine learning. The number of layers in deep learning is large. Therefore, a “deep” word is used. The neurons are referred to as nodes. The recurrent neural network (RNN) is a type of deep learning. It can deal with previous data. RNN is widely used in the classification of time sequence signals such as the electrocardiogram (ECG). The long-short-term memory (LSTM) can overcome the previous problems of RNN, such as not dealing with long time-series data. LSTM can handle long-time interval signals perfectly. It has a memory cell. A memory cell contains input, output, and forget gates. A forget gate is used to exclude redundant data. The main disadvantage of LSTM is handling the input information in only one direction. Bidirectional long-short-term memory (BILSTM) is the next version of LSTM for handling information in forward and backward directions.

As shown in Fig. 2, the layers of deep learning in this study were presented. The first layer represented the input features from each patient’s data. The second layer presented the BILSTM layer, with 100 hidden units. The third layer consisted of a fully connected layer, where all input and output sublayers were interconnected. The fourth layer was a dropout layer, which was used to avoid overfitting by removing some connections between neurons in the hidden layers. The fifth layer was a Softmax layer, where the obtained numbers were converted to a probability distribution. The last layer was a classification layer, where UTI cases were detected, whether DR or not, in database 1, positive or negative UTI in database 2, or MDR or not in database 3 [15]. The maximum number of epochs was the maximum number of times the learning algorithm looped over the entire training dataset. It equaled 100. The batch size was the number of training samples used in a single iteration before the model’s weights were updated. The minimum batch size was 150. The learning rate was a hyperparameter that controlled the updating of the model weights. It was 0.01. These hyperparameters were selected according to the simulated annealing method (SAM). This optimization method was used to select hyperparameters based on the resulting highest accuracy rates after applying this method. SAM was a probabilistic optimization method built on finding the best solution by simulating the physical process of cooling a material [16]. This method started with initial values and gradually changed until reaching the optimal solution. The initial values of the hyperparameters of the maximum number of epochs, the hidden layer size, the minimum batch size, and the initial learning rate were 50, 50, 50, and 0.001, respectively. The upper values were 200, 200, 200, and 0.1, respectively. The algorithm started with initial values that gradually increased to the upper values until obtaining the highest accuracy rate. The obtained parameters from this optimization method were 100, 100, 150, and 0.01, respectively.

Layers of deep learning

Logistic regression

The second classifier used was logistic regression [17]. It was a statistical model. The probability of an observation was predicted for obtaining its class, as shown in Eq. 6. The weights of this classifier were w1, w2, w3, …, wn. The bias w0 of this study was zero. The features of this classifier were x1, x2, x3,…,xn. In this classifier, regularization was used to avoid overfitting by constraining the weights of the model using the Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator Regression (Lasso regression). The cost function was J. A regularization term was added to the mean square error MSE(w). The normalization of the weight vector w was applied. In this study, the learning rate was 0.01.

Gradient boosting classifier

Boosting is a kind of machine learning. It uses a combination of weak learners using ensemble learning to produce a strong learner. The predictive power is very strong for many classification methods, such as data mining. In boosting, programmers select a random sample of data. Then, they fit it into a model. After that, they train it in a sequential model. Each model is well-trained to overcome the weaknesses of the previous model. With each iteration, the weak models are merged to produce a strong model. Gradient boosting was first developed by J. Friedman et al. They sequentially added the predictors to an ensemble of predictors, which was used to correct the errors of the previous ones. The gradient boosting is trained on the residual errors of the previous predictor, rather than adjusting the weights of the data points. The gradient boosting is a combination of the boosting method and the gradient descent algorithm. The logistic regression boosting (logitBoost) learner reduces logistic loss using conditional Bernoulli likelihood [18].

In this paper, the weak learner used was a decision tree, where the number of splits was 10. Split was a question of whether the case was a DR UTI or not. Leaves were the answer to the question. Therefore, the number of leaves of each tree was 11, as shown in Fig. 3. The number of weak learners (or trees) used was 100.

Databases

Three databases were used from Egypt, the Philippines, and Yemen. The first database was collected from the faculty of medicine at Alexandria University Hospital in Egypt [19]. It was the largest hospital in Alexandria. It served patients in Alexandria and the North Delta. An observational retrospective record-based study was conducted on all patients admitted to the Urology Department from October 2018 to October 2020. In this database, cases were classified as having drug resistance (DR) to antibiotics or not. The names and IDs of the cases were excluded. The other variables were as follows: gender type, ages, having tumor or not, duration of hospital stay in days, urinary catheter insertion, duration of catheter use in days, history of antibiotics use in the previous three months, current urinary tract infection, type of UTI community or hospital, having E.Coli or not, recurrent UTI or not, previous UTI or not, having infection inflammation or not.

The original number of patients in this database was 1091. Some of these data were unclear whether a drug was a DR or not. When the dataset was preprocessed, the missing UTI data of 702 patients were excluded. If the missing data were replaced by an incorrect value, the obtained prediction process would be unrealistic. No class imbalance was found. The gender value for each patient was female or male, which was represented as 0 or 1, respectively. Effects of categorical predictors were frequently easier to implement than effects of continuous variables. Therefore, the values of each patient’s age were binary variables. The age value was one if the patient’s age was greater than 60 years and 0 if not. If there was infection or inflammation in the patient, this column was 1; otherwise, it was 0. If the patient had a tumor, this column is 1; otherwise, it was zero. If there was a history of antibiotics, this column was 1; otherwise, it was 0. This database could be treated as a time-series database, because it contained patients who shared the same characteristics at different periods.

Holdout validation was used by dividing the database into training, validation, and test sets. The testing data was separated from the training and validation data. The percentages of training, validation, and testing were 60%, 10%, and 30%, respectively. They were selected randomly from each class. The validation data was separated from the testing data [19]. The K-fold cross-validation method was also used. The testing data was separated from the training data in both holdout and k-fold cross-validation methods.

In database 1, MDR was dominant. This database was about whether the presented cases were drug resistance (DR) or not. The DR was defined as the presented case having at least one of the following: MDR, PDR, or XDR. The percentage of DR cases was 77%.

The second database was collected from a local clinic in Northern Mindanao, Philippines [20]. It was recorded from April 2020 to January 2023. The number of variables for each patient was 15. The number of patients was 1436. The number of females and males was 816 and 620, respectively. The females were referred to as zero. The males were referred to as one. The patients’ names were concealed. An ID number was assigned to each patient to prevent the leakage of sensitive information. The ID number was also removed from the used database. The variables used for each patient were their ages, urine color, presence of glucose, presence of protein, pH level, specific gravity, red blood cells, quantity of epithelial cells, quantity of Mucous Threads, Amorphous Urates, presence of bacteria, and diagnosis of UTI, either positive or negative. A negative UTI was referred to as zero. A positive UTI was referred to as one. The urine color ranged from light yellow, dark yellow, yellow, brown, amber, straw, reddish, light red, red, and reddish yellow. Each color was referred to by a number from 0 to 9, respectively. UTI transparency was changed to clear, cloudy, slightly hazy, hazy, or turbid case with variable numbers, such as 0,3,4,5, and 6, respectively. In this database, there was no missing data.

The third database used was obtained from Al-Rafa Laboratory, IBB University, Yemen. The clinical specimens were obtained from diverse patient populations from March 2023 to November 2024. The number of cases was 317. The data were about 52 patient records, such as age, gender, the existence of E. coli, gram-positive bacteria, crystal, sugar, blood, yeast, or not in UTI patients. The patient records also contained a count of epithelial cells, R.B.C., or W.B.C. The used data was classified as MDR or not. All patients’ names were anonymized before dealing with them. Patient confidentiality was maintained according to the Declaration of Helsinki principles [21].

The preprocessing cleaning method for databases 1, 2, and 3 involved converting all text values into numeric values to facilitate the use of feature extraction methods and machine learning techniques.

Evaluation metrics

In the deep learning classifier, the parameters of the confusion matrix can be obtained, such as the number of accurate and inaccurate predictions for positive and negative cases. The number of positive cases is the number of patients who have DR. The number of negative cases is the number of patients who didn’t have DR. The parameters of the confusion matrix are TP, TN, FP, and FN. TP represents the number of true positive cases accurately classified, TN represents the number of true negative cases accurately classified, FP represents the number of false positive cases inaccurately classified, and FN represents the number of false negative cases inaccurately classified. The classification parameter rates used are accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, precision, F-measure, G-mean, and AUC. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve describes the relationship between the sensitivity of the classification model on the y-axis and the probability of false alarm (1-specificity) of the classification model on the x-axis. It measures the performance of the classification model. When the ROC curve approaches 1 (i.e., the AUC (the area under the ROC curve) is near 1), the classifier used is the best. When the ROC curve is far from 1 (i.e., the AUC is small), the classifier used is the worst. These parameters can be calculated using Eqs. (8–19). These parameters are used to evaluate the suggested approaches for extracting features from the UTI databases.

A technique called Decision Curve Analysis (DCA) is also used to assess a predictive model’s clinical utility, particularly in classification problems like risk prediction or disease diagnosis. DCA is used to calculate the net benefit from threshold probability pt, as shown in Eq. (20). DCA is used to clarify if the used model has a benefit to be used (treated) or not. Three lines are plotted on this curve. The first line is the Treat All line, which indicates that all cases are positive. That means the sensitivity is maximum, and there are many false positives. The second line is the Treat None line, which indicates that no one is treated using this model; that is, no true positive cases and no true positive cases. The third line is the net benefit of the selected model as the y-axis versus the threshold probability as the x-axis. If the third model lies above the other two curves, the selected model has the benefit of being used in the treatment process.

The calibration curve is also used to assess the relationship between the predicted values and the true values of the classification process of a model. The mean predicted probability is considered as the x-axis, and the observed true rate is considered as the y-axis. If the plotted line is on the diagonal, the model is perfectly calibrated. When the curve is below the diagonal, the model predicts higher probabilities than are actually true. When the curve is above the diagonal, the model predicts lower probabilities than are actually true.

The Brier score is a metric for assessing probabilistic predictions’ accuracy in classification methods. It can be calculated from the mean square of the predicted and actual values. It will be one if the used model is incorrectly predicting unknown cases. It will be zero if the used model correctly predicts unknown cases.

Wilson’s confidence interval (CI) is the Wald interval, calculated from the data by adding two successes and two failures. The Wald interval is between . The Wilson CI [22] is suitable for small sample databases, because it provides better coverage rates. It is often calculated for the classification parameter rates, such as accuracy and sensitivity. It is applied when these rates are near or equal to 1. Wilson CI is used to determine whether the used model is stable or not. As shown in Eq. 21, if there is a sample number of accuracies n, the sample mean is Z/n. The score for confidence level is Z. In this manuscript, Z equals 1.96 for a 95% confidence level. CI is the Wilson CI of the classification parameter rate p.

The feature selection method of the five methods features based on the Shapley Value, which has become the gold standard for interpreting complex ‘black box’ class methods, such as random forests, by providing a mathematically rigorous framework to attribute a model’s prediction to its individual input features. Its core philosophy focuses on calculating exactly how much each specific feature, such as a mean or variance, contributed to a particular outcome relative to the average prediction. By treating features as players in a game, it offers a fair and consistent method to quantify each variable’s unique impact to achieve the maximum classification rate.

After applying the Shapley value to select the best features, which were extracted from method 5 based on bagging random forest, logistic boosting, the suggested features were mean, max, min, variance, kurtosis, skewness, entropy, and sum (i.e., x1, x2, x3, x4, x5, x6, x7, and x8). As shown in Fig. 10, the most effective features were mean, variance, sum, and max (i.e., x1, x4, x8, and x2), where the highest predictor values were achieved. Therefore, these features were selected.

Discussion

There is an important need to assign drug-resistant UTI patients early to avoid different diseases. The main goal of the proposed model is to work as a supplementary clinical decision-support tool within Antimicrobial Stewardship programs, rather than a replacement for clinical judgment. Its primary role is to provide early risk classification.

This study aimed to detect DR UTI patients using ML algorithms. These algorithms can be used as a complementary decision-support tool. The ML methods were used. Most of the ML methods facilitated the detection of several diseases. There were three databases used in this study. The first database was used to classify UTI patients as DR or not. The second dataset was used to classify whether UTI patients were positive or negative. The third database was used to detect MDR patients. It was used in this study to ensure the effect of feature extraction methods. There was no bias from excluding 702 patients with missing variables in database 1, because the same effect of feature extracting methods was approved to a different UTI database.

Several studies have been conducted on the use of machine learning to classify drug-resistant UTI patients and determine whether they have resistance to antibiotics or not. Mohamed et al. determined the antimicrobial resistance of pathogens isolated from cancer patients in the intensive care unit (ICU) of the Alexandria University Hospital. They estimated the probability of AMR using a prediction model in which previous antibiotics were used to rapidly identify higher-risk patients [23]. Rich et al. developed a prediction model, such as the DT, boosted logistic regression (BLR), and random forest models for predicting antibiotic resistance for UTI treatment [24]. Belete and Saravanan reviewed drug-resistant UTI in pregnant women in developing countries in Asia and Africa [25]. İlhanl et al. used machine learning for the prediction of antibiotic resistance in UTI patients [26]. Yang et al. predicted antibiotic resistance in UTI patients using machine learning with a 95% AUC [27]. Mancini et al. used machine learning methods such as SVM and NN to detect multidrug-resistant UTIs. They obtained a sensitivity rate of 90.4%, an F1-score rate of 80.9%, an AUC rate of 73.9%, and an accuracy rate of 71.7% [28]. Lee et al. used machine learning to predict ciprofloxacin resistance in UTI patients. They obtained an AUC rate of 82.9%, a precision rate of 55.5%, a sensitivity rate of 93.4%, and a specificity rate of 31.2% [29]. Shovein et al. used machine learning to predict antimicrobial resistance in uncomplicated UTIs. They compared the AUC values of random forest (RF) and least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO). They deduced that LASSO outperformed RF in AUC values [30]. Keith et al. predicted phage therapy for treating Escherichia coli UTI based on machine learning [31]. Al-Shaebiet al. detected antimicrobial resistance using artificial intelligence [32]. Brintz et al. used several classifiers to predict antibiotic resistance for the treatment of UTI [33]. Stracy et al. predicted antibiotic-resistant UTIs using machine learning [34].

In this study, BILSTM was chosen because it acted as a bidirectional LSTM in both directions, future and past. In the context of DR prediction, clinical events (such as duration of catheter and the history of antibiotics use in the previous three months) were not just independent occurrences but were often interrelated across time. The BILSTM architecture allows the model to access both past and future context within a patient’s history, providing a more holistic representation of the clinical trajectory. This bidirectional processing significantly improves the feature extraction from sequential electronic health records, leading to high sensitivity (100%) and accuracy rates, as demonstrated in this study.

The classification parameter rates were high based on the analyzed databases. After applying method 5 in database 1, the testing number of patients was 117. There were zero false negative cases. There were 3 incorrectly predicted cases as DR, while they were DR-negative. After applying method 5 in database 2, if the number of tested patients was 431, the number of false positive cases was 4. The number of false negative cases was zero.

The third database was used to verify the results of the suggested model for extracting features of UTI patients. The selected database was from IBB University in Yemen. It was about whether UTI patients were MDR or not. It was a suitable database because it was from another country to get the benefit of the diversity of patients under nearly the same records as database 1.

From all these demonstrated results, the selected model’s high sensitivity and specificity emphasize its reliability in clinical decision-making.

The 100% sensitivity reflects the model’s high capacity to identify every DR case in database 1 by using strong clinical predictors, such as prior antibiotic use and surgical history. This performance was an intended clinical priority for a decision-support tool, where ensuring patient safety by avoiding untreated resistant infections (zero False Negatives) is paramount. The trade-off is reflected in the slightly lower specificity (96.7%), which we consider an acceptable clinical bias in the context of empiric therapy selection.

The advantages of this study can be applied to rapidly detect DR in inpatient, outpatient, and emergency settings among UTI patients using AI techniques.

The set of patients AI model should be used for recurrent UTI. The AI model of clinical practice in urology has rapid classification results within seconds rather than 48–72 h. All input database variables, such as demographics and clinical history, were routinely captured during standard admission anamnesis and physical examination without laboratory processing. Therefore, the presented model was used in real-time DR or UTI assessment.

In clinical practice, the model serves as a point-of-care decision-support tool; by inputting routinely available data, such as prior antibiotic use and catheter history, clinicians can immediately identify high-risk patients for targeted empiric therapy and rapid molecular testing. This model had the potential to bridge the 48–72-hour gap before culture results were available, supporting antimicrobial stewardship by ensuring that broad-spectrum antibiotics are reserved for those with the highest predicted risk of DR or UTI. This approach should complement, not replace, microbiological testing.

The steps of Transparent Reporting of a multivariable prediction model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis–Machine Learning (TRIPOD-ML) were applied as in the following sentences [35]. The study aimed to detect DR UTI in patients. The title of this paper includes the deep learning approach to the prediction of DR. This study showed the ability of machine learning to predict the diagnosis of DR cases faster. There were three databases used. The first database used was from the hospital of the faculty of Medicine, Alexandria University, to predict whether the presented cases were DR or not. The second database used was from a general clinic in Northern Mindanao, Philippines, to predict whether the cases were positive UTI or negative. The third database from IBB university, Yemen, to predict whether cases were MDR or not. The number of cases in the first and second databases used was 389 and 1436, respectively. The number of cases in database 3 was 317. The Shap analysis was applied for feature selection in database 2. The selected features were statistical calculations of the five Methods for the input variables of the second databases. The extracted features of each case in the two databases were split into training, testing, and validation using two cross-validation methods (i.e. holdout and K-fold). The split rates of training, validation, and testing for the holdout cross-validation method were 60%, 10%, and 30%, respectively. The split rates of training and testing for the K-fold cross-validation method were 66.6% and 33.3%, respectively. The machine learning algorithms were the deep learning, gradient boosting, and Lasso logistic regression classifiers. The validation strategy was based on k-fold and holdout cross-validation. The K-fold cross-validation was used to ensure the obtained results and to avoid overfitting. The performance parameters were Accuracy, AUC, sensitivity, specificity, precision, g-mean, and F1-score. The modeling decisions were fully documented. There was a limitation of this study, in that the obtained results were not in real-time for the input UTI patients.

The AI model was described for two cases of database 1. The first case was a male who had a DR UTI. His age was more than 60 years old. He had a tumor. He had a history of antibiotics. He took the antibiotic amoxicillin clavulanate. He did not take the antibiotic meropenem. He took the antibiotic amoxicillin clavulanate previously. He had a hospital UTI. He didn’t have a community UTI. He didn’t have E.Coli. The second case was a female who did not have a DR UTI. Her age was less than 60 years old. She didn’t have a tumor. She didn’t take amoxicillin clavulanate antibiotic before. She didn’t have a hospital UTI, but she had a community UTI before. She was infected with E.Coli. The AI model intervention had a great effect for predicting DR at the admission time (zero hour). It was used to save cost and time.

The DR UTI may contribute to adverse outcomes; however, the findings were promising, and further validation was desirable by using several large datasets to confirm the obtained results and avoid bias. It would also be helpful to underline that this approach was meant to complement, rather than replace, standard microbiological testing.

The selected model of method 5 can be applied to time variant databases (as the applied databases) or time series data, such as ECG, EEG, and heart sounds, to achieve the highest classification parameter rates.

Conclusions

The DR UTI patients must be detected to avoid several diseases that can lead to death. The innovation of this study was methodological, using five mathematical techniques. They were compared to extract features of the variables of UTI patients for the three databases. The deep learning classifier was applied to classify the used databases. It was selected because it achieved the maximum classification parameter rates. The obtained accuracy rate of method 5 was 97.4%, 99.54%, and 98.2% in databases one, two, and three, respectively. The machine learning methods can be used as a complementary decision-support tool. This model had the potential to reduce the delay in initiating appropriate therapy by up to 48 h. On the other hand, there was a risk if DR cases were misclassified. In the future, it was suggested to use a large database of UTI patients, whether they are DR or not, from several hospitals throughout Egypt, not just Alexandria University. There will be a need for prospective, real-time validation in clinical workflows.
