Percutaneous Retrieval Strategies Following Left Atrial Appendage Occluder and Peri-Device Leak Closure Embolization
Authors: Wadie David, Ziad Affas, John Bajouka, Keyshla Pagan Morales, Herman Kado
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPercutaneous LAAO is an established stroke-prevention strategy in atrial fibrillation patients unsuitable for long-term anticoagulation. Device embolization is uncommon but can cause severe complications requiring urgent intervention.Abstract of the CaseWe report 2 related cases of device embolization associated with left atrial appendage occlusion and subsequent peri-device leak closure. The first involved early Amulet occluder embolization with mitral inflow obstruction and hemodynamic instability. The second occurred during peri-device leak closure after prior WATCHMAN implantation, when a 6 × 4-mm vascular occluder embolized upon release. In both cases, intraprocedural transesophageal echocardiography and fluoroscopy guided successful snare-based percutaneous retrieval with coaxial sheath-assisted extraction, avoiding surgery. Both devices were retrieved intact without major complications.ConclusionsPercutaneous retrieval of embolized LAAO devices is achievable with systematic planning and multidisciplinary preparedness. Growing LAAO volumes and leak reinterventions underscore the need for structured embolization rescue protocols.Key words: atrial fibrillation, device embolization, left atrial appendage occlusion, percutaneous retrieval, peri-device leak, snare technique

Stroke prevention remains a primary objective in the management of atrial fibrillation (AF), a leading cause of cardioembolic stroke. While oral anticoagulation (OAC) effectively reduces the risk of ischemic stroke, certain patients are unable to tolerate long-term therapy due to bleeding risk, frailty, unstable anticoagulation control, drug interactions, or poor adherence. Current guidelines recommend percutaneous left atrial appendage occlusion (LAAO) as an alternative for appropriately selected patients.

Take-Home Message

•Device embolization during left atrial appendage occlusion or peri-device leak closure is uncommon but potentially life-threatening; immediate multimodality imaging recognition (transesophageal echocardiography + fluoroscopy) and a rehearsed, coaxial snare-based percutaneous retrieval pathway can achieve complete recovery without surgical rescue.

LAAO can reduce embolic risk without lifelong anticoagulation. Peri-device leak (PDL) remains a contributor to residual stroke risk; even minor leaks are associated with increased thromboembolic and bleeding events, prompting ongoing debate regarding thresholds for reintervention and balance between antithrombotic therapy and mechanical closure.1,2 These concerns have led to an increase in transcatheter reinterventions for leak management, thereby introducing additional procedural complexity and risk.

Device embolization occurs less frequently than PDL or device-related thrombus but can result in acute hemodynamic compromise or systemic embolization, necessitating urgent retrieval. Embolization may arise immediately due to poor anchoring, device undersizing, inadequate compression, or challenging left atrial appendage (LAA) anatomy. Alternatively, it may develop later because of anatomical remodeling, insufficient endothelialization, suboptimal antithrombotic strategies, or mechanical interaction during repeat transseptal instrumentation.

The interventional community has adopted algorithmic approaches to embolization rescue centered on stabilization, localization, and retrieval based on embolism site and injury risk. A EuroIntervention consensus provides an operator-oriented triage and retrieval framework,3 while the Society for Cardiovascular Angiography and Interventions/Heart Rhythm Society consensus highlights programmatic needs including imaging, complication preparedness, and postprocedural surveillance.3

In cases of LAA closure device embolization, retrieval techniques are adapted to the specific occluder design. Extraction generally involves a large-bore sheath 2F to 4-F larger than the delivery catheter (14-F to 26-F) and a single-loop snare, with the objective of achieving complete ensheathment prior to removal to minimize vascular or intracardiac injuries.4 For Amplatzer Cardiac Plug devices, the distal screw serves as the preferred snare target, facilitating controlled collapse with hooks oriented away from the sheath; the proximal screw is an alternative but requires greater force. In Amulet devices, the recessed proximal screw restricts access, making the distal screw the primary target.4 When coaxial alignment is challenging, a “mother-in-child” technique using a steerable sheath (Agilis NxT Steerable Introducer, 8.5-F, Abbott) within a larger retrieval sheath enhances directional control. Adjunctive high-traction graspers or baskets may be necessary when snaring alone is insufficient.4

The location of device embolization determines the retrieval strategy. Aortic embolization is retrieved transfemorally with a large-bore sheath and snare, collapsing the device fully before withdrawal.3,4 Left atrial embolization is managed with transseptal retrieval, employing a pigtail catheter across the mitral valve to prevent migration into the left ventricle (LV).4 In cases of LV embolization, retrograde pigtail manipulation guides the device into the left ventricular outflow tract, followed by snare-assisted recapture with a large-bore sheath.3

This review presents 2 LAAO-related embolization events: one involving an Amulet device, and another involving a vascular occluder used to close a residual leak (Table 1). The focus is on procedural decision-making and snare-based retrieval strategies as they relate to LAAO program protocols.

Case Presentations

Case 1: acute embolization of an Amulet LAAO device with mitral inflow compromise

A 75-year-old woman with paroxysmal AF was referred for LAAO because long-term OAC was considered unsafe due to recurrent falls and easy bruising. Her CHA2DS2-VASc score was 5. The procedure was performed under intraprocedural imaging guidance with continuous fluoroscopy and transesophageal echocardiography (TEE).

After transseptal access, an initial 22-mm occluder was selected based on appendage measurements. Following standard device preparation, the device was advanced into the LAA and deployed. On assessment, the device appeared undersized, and it was exchanged for a 25-mm LAAO. The second deployment demonstrated stable positioning, including an appropriate relationship of the lobe to the circumflex artery, clear separation between the lobe and the disc, coaxial alignment at the LAA neck, and a concave disc profile on imaging.

Several minutes after deployment, TEE demonstrated device dislodgement into the left atrium with migration toward the mitral apparatus. The occluder became oriented perpendicular to the mitral valve, causing partial obstruction of transmitral flow (Figure 1). The patient developed acute hemodynamic instability with hypotension and tachycardia, requiring intravenous fluids and inotropic support.

A rapid percutaneous retrieval strategy was initiated. Additional femoral venous access was obtained, and the transseptal sheath was upsized to a 24-F large bore sheath to permit safe device recapture. A steerable sheath was advanced into the left atrium and directed toward the mitral annulus to optimize alignment, with careful navigation to avoid mitral apparatus. A snare system was then introduced, the device hub was engaged, and controlled traction was applied while maintaining coaxial orientation. Fluoroscopy demonstrated stable snare engagement and sheath-assisted recapture of the occluder prior to extraction (Figures 2A to 2C). The occluder was drawn fully into the large bore sheath and removed intact. Postretrieval assessment with fluoroscopy and TEE confirmed complete device removal, absence of new pericardial effusion, and no visible thrombus or residual foreign material. The patient recovered without recurrent instability, and a pre-discharge transthoracic echocardiogram showed no pericardial effusion or new structural abnormality. After retrieval, repeat LAAO was not attempted, and surgical ligation was not pursued. The risks and benefits of resuming OAC were discussed with the patient and her family. The patient decided the stroke risk outweighed the risks of bleeding, so a direct oral anticoagulant was restarted.

This percutaneous transseptal retrieval strategy, using a large-bore sheath, steerable support for coaxial alignment, and snare-based engagement followed by complete re-sheathing prior to extraction, is consistent with contemporary recommendations for left atrial retrieval.

Case 2: embolization during closure of a late PDL after prior WATCHMAN implantation

A 77-year-old man with persistent AF underwent successful LAAO with a WATCHMAN device. Early postimplant imaging confirmed appropriate seating, adequate compression, and no PDL. Four years later, surveillance imaging identified a PDL measuring 0.279 cm, and he was referred for transcatheter leak closure.

The procedure was performed under general anesthesia with TEE guidance. Right femoral venous access was obtained, and transseptal puncture was performed with a BRK needle; the sheath was exchanged for a steerable sheath to improve alignment with the mitral-sided defect. TEE identified a 3- to 5-mm communication adjacent to the previously implanted occluder. Under combined fluoroscopic and echocardiographic guidance, a multipurpose catheter and guidewire were advanced to cannulate the appendage. A smaller multipurpose catheter was substituted to improve support, and contrast injection confirmed positioning just beyond the original device's fixation region.

A 6 × 4-mm vascular occluder was delivered across the defect. After deployment of the distal component followed by the proximal component, TEE initially showed minimal residual flow. With minor adjustments of the sheath position to improve coaxial alignment, flow across the defect was no longer visualized, and contrast injection demonstrated no passage beyond the newly deployed occluder. The device was then released. Immediately after release, the occluder embolized into the left atrium.

Percutaneous retrieval was initiated without delay. A single-loop snare was advanced through the steerable sheath, and the embolized occluder was captured on the first attempt, but it disengaged during withdrawal. On a second try, the occluder was secured and pulled all the way into the steerable sheath, allowing for an in-tact removal. Hemostasis at the venous access site was achieved using a suture-mediated closure device. The patient was hemodynamically stable throughout and had no immediate complications of the procedure.

In case 2, the procedural response aligns with location-based retrieval strategies established by expert consensus which included urgent transseptal retrieval. As in both reports, a steerable sheath was used to optimize reach, alignment, and maneuverability within the left atrium, facilitating controlled engagement of the embolized device with a single-loop snare and maintaining coaxial orientation during traction. The retrieval technique also reflects the core safety principle emphasized in both papers, namely secure device fixation followed by complete re-sheathing prior to extraction, as the occluder was ultimately drawn fully into the sheath and removed intact, minimizing the risk of further embolization or structural injury. After retrieval, the patient remained stable with no immediate complications. Due to persistent PDL, she was scheduled for coil embolization. Her direct oral anticoagulant was continued. Pre-discharge echocardiogram showed no residual injury, regurgitation, or effusion.

Discussion

Together, these cases illustrate device embolization as a rare but serious complication of transcatheter LAAO, arising in 2 distinct procedural contexts. In case 1, acute embolization of an Amplatzer Amulet into the left atrium with impingement across the mitral inflow caused acute hemodynamic compromise and highlights how rapidly this complication can become clinically significant (Figures 1 and 2). In case 2, embolization occurred during an attempted percutaneous closure of a residual PDL, underscoring those secondary interventions, while often necessary, add mechanical complexity and introduce new embolization vectors. Together, these cases support a practical message: Embolization is uncommon, but every LAAO program needs an explicit prevention strategy, an early-recognition workflow, and a reproducible percutaneous retrieval plan, with predefined triggers for surgical escalation.1,3, 4, 5 An evidence-informed, stepwise percutaneous retrieval and escalation pathway, aligned with contemporary consensus guidance and registry experience, is summarized in Table 2.

Incidence, mechanisms, and preventable contributors

Device embolization centers around procedural and anatomic mismatches—suboptimal sizing, noncoaxial deployment, shallow landing zones, and difficult anatomies—and remains clinically significant because consequences depend on where the device travels. LV embolization or mitral interaction can cause obstruction or acute regurgitation, narrowing the window for controlled retrieval.1,3, 4, 5, 6

A second theme is that residual PDL is not benign. Recent evidence demonstrates that residual flow is associated with increased thromboembolic risk, with this association becoming more pronounced as leak size or persistence increases.1,7 Leak-directed reinterventions require additional catheter exchanges, sheath manipulation, and device-to-device contact, collectively increasing the risk of embolization during deployment. The implication is not that leak closure should be avoided, but that patient selection, device choice, and intraprocedural stability assessments should be conducted with greater rigor than in a new implant.7

Recognition and imaging: why speed and modality matter

Early recognition is what separates a manageable complication from a hemodynamic emergency. Fluoroscopy usually determines loss of appendage position; however, TEE is often critical to understanding valve interaction, obstruction physiology, and chordal risk. In case 1, mitral inflow compromise was demonstrated by TEE and helped guide the retrieval trajectory (Figures 1 and 2). This imaging-first approach is consistent with the recommendations of contemporary consensus practice that stress the importance of immediate multimodality evaluation in situations where the position of the device is questionable or embolization is suspected.8

Beyond acute rescue, cardiac computed tomography is increasingly being used to characterize sealing, device position, and mechanisms of residual leaks or device-related findings on follow-up.9 Cardiac computed tomography characterizes residual leak anatomy and guides reintervention planning.9

Percutaneous retrieval: procedural principles and escalation logic

Percutaneous retrieval follows a core sequence: stabilize, secure, align, sheath, and extract. Key steps include snaring a hub or stable strut, restoring coaxial alignment, and re-sheathing into a large-bore sheath to minimize valvular trauma.7 Technical principles and escalation triggers are summarized in Table 2.

Percutaneous attempts should be abandoned early if the device cannot be stabilized, chordal entanglement is suspected, or hemodynamic deterioration occurs, particularly with LV, aortic root, or mitral-compromising embolization.3,6

Clinical implications

These cases support 3 recommendations: standardized sizing and imaging-defined release criteria, a rehearsed embolization-rescue workflow with surgical notification for ventricular or valvular involvement, and individualized PDL reintervention decisions that acknowledge the embolization risk of secondary closure.7,10

Study Limitations

These 2 cases cannot define incidence or comparative effectiveness, and long-term follow-up was unavailable. They offer procedural lessons and imaging and rescue principles applicable across devices and institutions.

Conclusions

Device embolization during LAAO or leak closure is rare but potentially life-threatening. In both cases, continuous TEE, early sheath upsizing, and coaxial snare-assisted recapture enabled complete percutaneous retrieval without surgery. Leak closure reinterventions carry distinct mechanical risks requiring strict stability checks and immediate retrieval set availability. LAAO programs should maintain a standardized embolization response pathway with defined roles, equipment, attempt limits, and surgical escalation criteria. Future multicenter studies should refine embolization predictors and evaluate whether protocolized rescue drills reduce retrieval time and morbidity.
