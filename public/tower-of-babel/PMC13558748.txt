RUL-DBRS: A Novel Energy Efficient and Robust Protocol for Enhanced Communication in Underwater Wireless Sensor Networks
Authors: Abdul Salam, Shahab Haider, Noreen Khan, Fasee Ullah, Shahid Kamal, Arfat Ahmad Khan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUnderwater exploration is vital for understanding marine life and accessing natural resources, yet only a small fraction of the underwater world has been studied due to communication challenges. Underwater Wireless Sensor Networks (UWSNs) enable data collection through distributed sensor nodes, but existing routing protocols face critical issues including high energy consumption, frequent node failures, packet losses, long delays, and poor throughput. To address these limitations, we propose Robust Uneven Load Balancing–Direction Aware Best Route Selection (RUL-DBRS), a novel energy-efficient routing protocol for UWSNs. RUL-DBRS employs uneven load balancing based on the average residual energy of available paths, reducing dead nodes, lowering end-to-end delay, and enhancing throughput. By combining direction-aware routing with adaptive load distribution, the protocol minimizes congestion and packet losses, leading to more reliable communication. Simulation results demonstrate that on average RUL-DBRS reduces the packet loss rate by 20%, minimizes end-to-end delay by 40 s, enhances nodes’ life network by 5%, improves throughput by 20%, and consumes 29% lesser energy in comparison with E2MR-HOA, making it a promising solution for efficient and sustainable underwater communication.Subject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Earth is the only known planet that sustains life, and water is a fundamental necessity for all living organisms. Approximately 70% of the Earth’s surface is covered by water, including oceans, seas, rivers, lakes, ponds, and canals. Oceans and seas constitute the largest share, yet only around 10% of the underwater world has been explored, leaving vast areas unexplored1. We need underwater exploration because approximately one third of the earth is under water, yet much of it is still a mystery. By exploring beneath the surface, we learn how marine life survives and how our actions are affecting ocean health. It helps us find underwater resources, e.g., minerals, new species, and potential medicines etc., that could benefit humanity. Underwater research also keeps us safer by improving our understanding of earthquakes, tsunamis, and other natural hazards. It supports sustainable fishing and protects the livelihoods of coastal communities. Exploring the deep sea also helps secure vital underwater cables and pipelines. Most importantly, it deepens our connection with the planet and encourages us to protect what we are only just beginning to understand. This underscores the importance of underwater exploration for studying ecosystems and harnessing natural resources, such as petroleum and natural gas1.

The high cost and complexity of installing and maintaining wired communication systems underwater have led to the development of Underwater Wireless Sensor Networks (UWSNs)2. As illustrated in Fig. 1 of this paper, a UWSN consists of two main types of nodes: underwater sensor nodes and sink nodes. Sensor nodes are deployed underwater to gather data, which they transmit to sink nodes floating at the surface. The sink nodes then forward the collected data to a base station located onshore, either through a private network or via the Internet3. Because deterministic node placement is not feasible underwater, nodes are typically deployed randomly.

An UWSN having randomly deployed sensor and sink nodes.

Unlike terrestrial Wireless Sensor Networks (WSNs), UWSNs rely on acoustic signals instead of radio frequencies, which are severely attenuated underwater. While acoustic communication is more reliable in aquatic environments, it suffers from limited bandwidth, higher propagation delays, and increased bit error rates4. UWSNs also face additional challenges such as limited battery capacity, packet loss, long end-to-end delays, and reduced throughput, which collectively make communication less efficient.

Several routing protocols have been proposed to address these issues. For example, the Depth-Based Routing (DBR) protocol [35] efficiently forwards packets based on node depth but tends to drain nodes near the sink due to repeated use. The Energy-Efficient Multipath Routing (E2MR) protocol6 prolongs network lifetime using multipath communication but experiences high packet losses in long-distance transmissions. Cooperative schemes such as Co-UWSN7 reduce energy consumption but can suffer from imbalanced load distribution and early node failures. More advanced approaches like (E2MR- HOA)5 employ hybrid optimization to improve performance, yet still face throughput degradation and higher latency in dense deployments.

These limitations highlight the urgent need for routing protocols that are energy-efficient, robust, and capable of adapting to the dynamic behavior of underwater environments.

Related work

In this section, we critically review the key literature related to UWSNs. Such networks are typically built through the random deployment of nodes underwater. Sensor nodes are placed on the seabed, while sink nodes float on the water surface. The sensor nodes collect data from their surroundings and forward it to the sink nodes, which then transmit the information to a base station located onshore. The efficiency of these nodes, particularly in terms of energy consumption, is critical8–10 as it directly affects both the network’s lifetime and the overall communication process11–14.

The work in15 aims to improve communication reliability by employing cooperative routing with mobile sink nodes. Similarly, Vahabi et al.16 propose a novel routing protocol that leverages link quality among nodes to reduce energy consumption. Their protocol enhances network lifetime and remains efficient for both large- and small-scale networks. The authors in17 introduce a meta-heuristic relay node selection protocol that minimizes collisions in acoustic underwater networks, thereby reducing energy consumption. Yadav et al.18 present a greedy, energy-efficient load-balancing protocol that uses weight factors to identify nodes with minimal distance. Furthermore, Ullah et al.19 propose a protocol that divides the network into upper and lower subnetworks, with sinks placed in both layers to reduce node-to-sink distance. Forwarder selection in this approach is based on residual energy, bit rate, and distance. However, repeatedly using the same forwarder drains its battery quickly, which increases the number of dead nodes, causes network partitions, and negatively impacts communication20.

In addition, Lilhore et al.21 present a protocol that employs data fusion and a genetic algorithm to reduce energy consumption and minimize the number of dead nodes. Routing protocols in UWSNs can broadly be classified into location-free and location-based categories22. Further classifications are based on parameters such as vector-based, depth-based, and clustering approaches23. A metaheuristic clustering protocol proposed in24 uses the grasshopper optimization method to identify the optimum route.

Farwa et al.25 divide the network into smaller sub-networks to minimize interference and to determine the optimal path to the destination. Umar et al.15 also proposed the Depth and Energy Aware Dominating Set-Based (DEADS) protocol, which leverages sink mobility to ensure reliable routing. Xiao et al.26 present a clustered routing protocol that employs Ant Colony Optimization (ACO) for path discovery. Similarly, Lee et al.27 address the problem of fake message flooding, which consumes bandwidth and can lead to Denial of Service (DoS) attacks. The Depth-Based Routing (DBR) protocol28 uses a greedy approach to select forwarders, where static sinks float on the surface and randomly deployed sensor nodes remain on the seabed. Packets forwarded to the sink are then routed to a base station onshore. An enhanced version, Energy-Efficient DBR (EEDBR)29, selects intermediate nodes based on both residual energy and depth. Umar et al.30 propose another reliable routing protocol that also considers residual energy and depth to improve packet transmission. Energy-Efficient Grid-Based Routing (EEGBRP), proposed in31, divides the network into three-dimensional cells and selects gateways of these cells for routing. Another approach, E2MR-HOA, proposed in5, employs hybrid optimization. It uses modified chemical reaction optimization for clustering and modified bacterial foraging search to identify reliable routes to destination nodes.

The literature review demonstrates that existing UWSN routing protocols consume significant energy, which reduces node and network lifetime and leads to frequent link failures. Moreover, these protocols often suffer from high packet loss and long end-to-end delays, further degrading network throughput. To address these challenges, this paper proposes a novel routing protocol, which is described in the following section.

The Proposed RUL-DBRS Protocol

In this section, we present our proposed Robust Uneven Load balancing-Direction aware Best Route Selection (RUL-DBRS) protocol, which aims to enhance communication between different nodes, i.e., sensor and sink nodes, in UWSNs. Figure 2 depicts the procedural flowchart of RUL-DBRS, which enhances nodes’ lifetime. Moreover, RUL-DBRS reduces end-to-end delay and packet losses that yields improved throughput.

Procedural flowchart of the proposed RUL-DBRS scheme.

UWSNs are established by deploying nodes in a specified underwater area. Traditionally, there are two approaches to deploy nodes, i.e., deterministic and random. A deterministic deployment is preferred when area is known, whereas random approach deploys nodes in an unknown area having less chance of nodes’ replacement. This is why, a random approach suits the UWSNs32. Another important aspect in UWSNs is nodes’ replacement, which is almost impossible to perform. Therefore, efficient and balanced utilization of battery remains critical, which minimizes network partitioning by reducing the number of dead nodes. UWSNs comprises two kinds of nodes, namely, sensors and sinks. A sensor node gathers information from the surrounding and utilizes other sensor nodes to transmit this information to a sink node. A sink floats at the surface of sea water and communicates the gathered information to an onshore data centre or base station33. Communication among sensor nodes occur through acoustic waves, whereas a sink node employs acoustic waves for data transmission with underwater sensor nodes and radio waves for communication with a base station and34. Figure 1 depicts a scenario representing random deployment of nodes having sinks and relay nodes, which enable communication with a data center. Our proposed RUL-DBRS protocol, as shown in Algorithm 1, aims to enhance communication among nodes in UWSNs and is detailed below.

The proposed RUL-DBRS protocol.

A source node (s) intending data transmission to a specified destination node (d), s identifies all of its directly connected neighbours and populates the set N. A set R holds all the possible route (ri) to d. RUL-DBRS finds all the possible routes towards d using the algorithms proposed in our previously routing protocol, i.e., Direction Aware Best Forwarder Selection (DABFS)35. RUL-DBRS employs a direction-aware routing strategy, as nodes do not remain static in UWSNs and change their position due to water flow. Therefore, such a direction aware strategy also suites UWSNs to more efficiently identify routes. Selection of the best path among all the available routes is a challenging task in UWSNs. To this end, the existing literature provides several techniques that takes into account different parameters. These parameters include shortest path in terms of hops count, highest average bandwidth of a path, etc.36. However, repeated selection of a path on such parameters may cause a large amount of dead nodes. Since replacement of nodes is not possible, this results in frequent network partitioning.

To address this issue, our protocol enables s to compute average residual energy, i.e., ξ , of all available paths. Furthermore, a Max(.) function selects the path holding the highest average residual energy (rm). Selection on the aforementioned residual energy parameter enables usage of high energy nodes in the communication process. Moreover, the existing body of knowledge provides several load balancing techniques that distribute traffic on multiple paths to enhance communication efficiency. Load balancing is commonly kept even, where data is divided into equal parts. Each of the constituent paths enables transmission of its designated part, which improves communication as well as lives of nodes on the network37,38. However, perpetual use of the selected set of paths, drains off the battery rapidly, thereby, producing a number of dead nodes. Consequently, producing several network partitions, which impairs communication Therefore, selection of shortest paths is not always effectively. To achieve this aim, we propose a novel uneven load distribution technique, which distributes load with respect to the average residual energy of a route. This increases the communication efficacy and minimizes the dead nodes count on the network, hence, reducing network partitioning. Our proposed uneven load distributor enables transmission of packets on the selected path, which is determined with respect to normalized ξ using uniform statistical distribution39. It is worth mentioning that uneven load balancing enhances packet delivery ratio and reduces end-to-end delay. When packets are allowed to adopt the shortest path all the time, congestion on this path increases that results in higher packet drops. Moreover, retransmission of the lost packets increases the congestion further. Congestion also increases end-to-end delay, where queueing delay remains a major contributor. Additionally, battery remains a critical issue in UWSNs and repeated adoption of a certain path leads towards increased dead nodes. Replacement of dead nodes is not possible in such networks. Hence, load balancing becomes critical. To this end, our proposed protocol relies upon the average residual energy of the available paths and transmits increased traffic on the paths bearing higher average residual energy, thereby, performing uneven load balancing. This enhances network throughput by reducing packet loss ratio and end-to-end delay. The subsequent section includes evaluation of the efficacy of RUL-DBRS and compares it with state-of-the-art.

Evaluation and Analysis of Performance

This section consists performance analysis of our proposed Robust Uneven Load balancing-Direction aware Best Route Selection (RUL-DBRS) protocol in comparison with an eminent protocol from the literature, namely, Energy-Efficient Multi- path Routing- Hybrid Optimization Algorithm (E2MR-HOA)5. The following subsections include simulation parameters and evaluation metrics followed by results and their corresponding discussion.

Simulation Setup

The aforementioned protocols are simulated using parameters, which are presented in this section. Results are derived using a MATLAB-based simulator35,40 under the parameters listed in Table 1. Nodes’ density is kept in a range of 0 to 600 and are deployed randomly in an area of 50 x 50 meter2 (m2). Moreover, network is classified into three density levels, namely, low, medium, and high to cover low and high traffic rates. A low density network comprises nodes from 0 − 200, the medium density ranges from 201 − 400 nodes, and a high density lies between 401 − 600 nodes35. Sensors collect information and transmit it to the sinks that communicate the same to an onshore data center. Size of a packet is taken as 1000 bits. Initial node’s energy remains 2.5 joules (j), whereas transmission or communication range remains 100 m.

Performance Evaluation Metrics

This section details the metrics employed to compare the performance of the aforementioned protocols. Evaluation metrics comprises nodes’ life in terms of dead and alive nodes count, average residual energy, packets loss, end-to-end delay, and network throughput, which are used by state-of-the-art41–44. Simulation Results and Discussion

This section details metric-wise results yield after simulating the aforementioned protocols under the parameters defined in Table 1.

Average Node’s Life

As detailed in Section "The proposed RUL-DBRS protocol", life of a node depends upon the battery drainage. A nodeâ ™s lifetime refers to the duration it can actively participate in the communication process, which largely depends on its available energy. Life of a node decreases with its continuous participation in data dissemination process, which occurs as a result of battery depreciation. When a node exhausts all of its energy, it is referred to as dead node. Such nodes adversely affect the communication process. Since battery replacement remains impossible in UWSNs, node’s life is a critical metric to evaluate the performance of a certain protocol. To this end, our proposed RUL-DBRS protocol introduces a new load balancing criterion, which does not always rely upon the shortest path. As selection of a shortest path drains the nodes’ batteries on this path quickly, network communication performance gets deteriorated at a rapid pace. RUL-DBRS enables uneven distribution of data on the multiple available paths. Using ω, data is distributed on these paths depending upon its average residual energy. A path holding high residual energy transmit more data in comparison with a path having low residual energy, as detailed in Section "The proposed RUL-DBRS protocol". Performances of RUL-DBRS and E2MR-HOA are evaluated for low, medium, and high density networks. Results presented in Fig. 3 conform to our claim of achieving better performance, where RUL-DBRS reduces dead nodes considerably in comparison with E2MR-HOA.

Average Residual Energy

A node requires energy to participate in a communication process enabling it to transmit and receive data. This energy is provided by an attached battery, which remains limited. Since battery replacement is not possible in UWSNs, this component stands critical during communication. An early battery drainage of a node results in frequent network partitions, which adversely impact the network performance45. This demands a protocol to be capable of distributing network traffic on the paths exhibiting high residual energy, which is found lacking in the existing literature. To this end, our proposed RUL-DBRS protocol enables uneven network traffic load balancing on the available paths. Higher amount of data is transmitted over the paths having higher average residual energy to keep the nodes alive for a longer period of time, as discussed in Section "Average Node’s Life". Figure 4 presents the simulation results showing average residual energy of nodes during communication for low, medium, and high density networks. Considering these results, RUL-DBRS outperforms E2MR-HOA by a significant margin, which is due to uneven load balancing by RUL-DBRS on the selected paths. Reduced energy usage increases the number of alive node that enhances the communication process significantly.

Average Packet Losses

Packet loss rate defines the ratio of lost data packets to the amount of data packets sent during a given period of transmission. Since packet losses adversely impact performance, it is required to be minimized39. Packet losses increase with the increase in nodes’ density on the network. Figure 5 demonstrates the same behaviour for both protocols, i.e., RUL-DBRS and E2MR-HOA, for sparse, medium, and dense networks. However, the packet losses in our proposed protocol remains smaller than E2MR-HOA. This is due to uneven load balancing by RUL-DBRS, which transmit more packets on the path having the highest average residual energy. This prevents a certain path from congestion and reduces network partitions, thereby, enhancing communication performance.

Average packet losses during transmission of packets.

Average End-to-End Delay

End-to-end delay is another important metric that impacts network performance, which is the average time taken by a packet in its successful delivery to a destination node. End-to-end delay remains inversely proportional to the performance of a network, hence, it is required to be reduced46. This delay increases with increase in network traffic, thus, nodes’ density plays a pivotal role in communication. Increased number of nodes rises congestion on the network, thereby, escalating end-to-end delay during communication. Our novel uneven traffic distribution stands as a significant solution in this re- gard. Figure 6 compares RUL-DBRS and E2MR-HOA, where end-to-end delay increases, in all the three graphs, for both the protocols with the increase in number of nodes and congestion on the network. It is worth mentioning here that our unique uneven load balancing in RUL-DBRS fowards higher number of packets on paths exhibiting higher average residual energy. This reduces congestion on a single path. Moreover, it also decreases the number of dead nodes and network parti- tions, thereby, increasing the number of paths on the network to reach a certain destination node. This is why, RUL-DBRS shows lesser end-to-end delay, in comparison with E2MR-HOA, that confirms the efficacy of the proposed RUL-DBRS protocol.

Average end-to-end delay during transmission of packets.

Average Network Throughput

An improved packet delivery ratio directly contributes to higher network throughput, which largely depends on how efficiently packets are managed during transmission. Relying on a single path for forwarding all packets can create congestion, leading to increased packet drops and reduced throughput. Likewise, when congestion builds up across multiple paths, overall network performance is also degraded. To address this, the proposed RUL-DBRS protocol distributes the load intelligently across available paths. Paths that are congested or have lower average residual energy are assigned fewer packets, which not only extends the lifetime of the nodes but also reduces the risk of network partitions. This balanced approach ultimately enhances network throughput. Contrarily, E2MR-HOA always rely upon the shortest path, hence, producing inferior network throughput. Simulation results presented in Fig. 7 affirm our aforementioned claim by showing better performance of RUL-DBRS, in comparison with E2MR-HOA, in terms of network throughput for low, medium, and high density networks.

Limitation of the Proposed Protocol

In UWSNs, depth plays a major role in data routing. As sensors go deeper, communication becomes slower and weaker because acoustic signals lose strength and take longer to travel. The proposed RUL-DBRS protocol lacks the capability to cater for depth variance. Addition of depth-based density variations will be considered in future to further enhance the functionality of our proposed protocol.

Conclusion

Earth is the only planet in our solar system that supports life, where water is one of the major requirements. This planet is covered with majorly with water, approximately 70%, in the form of oceans, seas, rivers, lakes, ponds, and canals, etc. Investigation of this underwater world is challenging, which is why, only 10% of this underwater world is discovered so far. Further investigation remains important to explore life and natural resources underwater. In this regard, UWSNs deploy different sensors that gather precious data and share this data to a base station located on the land. To efficiently route gathered data, the existing body of knowledge provides numerous routing protocols. These protocols comprise several issues, such as short battery life of a node, increased packet losses and end-to-end delay, and inferior network throughput that makes the communication process inefficient. To this end, this work proposes a novel protocol, namely, RUL-DBRS, that aims to enhance communication among different nodes, which will improve underwater resources exploration. RUL-DBRS reduces energy consumption through uneven minimizes the number of dead nodes by enhancing a node’s life. Moreover, this work also improves network throughput by reducing packet losses and end-to-end delay. Simulation results show that on average RUL-DBRS reduces the packet loss rate by 20%, minimizes end-to-end delay by 40s, enhances nodes’ life network by 5%, improves throughput by 20%, and consumes 29% lesser energy in comparison with E2MR-HOA, thereby, confirming its efficacy. This work can further be extended to utilize energy, generated due to water waves, for battery recharge that will further enhance a node’s life. Integrating wave-based energy harvesting into underwater wireless sensor networks is feasible by using surface buoys that convert wave motion into electricity to recharge underwater sensor nodes. This can extend network lifetime and reduce battery replacement, though challenges like durability in harsh sea conditions and efficient power transfer still need careful design. Moreover, security and retransmission overhead are also critical to enable authenticity, reliability, and confidentiality of data. Additionally, a multi-factor route selection metric may further enhance the robustness of the proposed protocol, where metrics, such as transient acoustic fluctuations and spatial energy heterogeneity, can be considered. Also, metrics, such as drift velocity, current turbulence, depth-based density variations, can be employed to further enhance the mobility-based routing.
