Lightweight CNN-Based Anomaly Detection for High Voltage Converter Modulators in the Spallation Neutron Source
Alberto D. Cencillo albertodiazcen@ugr.es, Leonardo Concepción leonar2cp@ugr.es, Julián Luengo julianlm@decsai.ugr.es [1.0em], Isaac Triguero triguero@decsai.ugr.es

Abstract
Unscheduled trips of high-power pulsed converters are a leading source of downtime at large accelerator facilities. At the Spallation Neutron Source (SNS), the High Voltage Converter Modulators (HVCMs) are consistently the second-largest contributor to lost beam time. Each HVCM pulse is recorded across sensor channels spanning currents, voltages, and magnetic fluxes, whose mutual interactions encode the operating state of the system. Fault precursors do not manifest uniformly across these channels: depending on fault type, they may alter the temporal structure of individual signals, change the statistical dependencies among channels, or both. Existing deep-learning approaches typically process multi-channel signals with standard convolutional pipelines that entangle temporal and cross-channel operations from the first layer, giving the model no explicit mechanism to represent channel independence or structured inter-channel interaction. We hypothesise that architectural inductive bias, specifically the ordering of temporal filtering and cross-channel mixing, plays a central role in detection performance on this class of data. To test this, we vary the order in which these two operations are applied, and examine whether per-pulse adaptive channel reweighting further improves sensitivity. Evaluated on the public HVCM dataset across all four SNS subsystems (RFQ, DTL, CCL, SCL), our best variant achieves a pooled AUC-PR of 0.816 and AUC-ROC of 0.934, outperforming the state of the art on most subsystems and five of the six fault families. Ablations identify three dominant input channels and link per-fault-family performance to whether precursors manifest as amplitude shifts in individual channels or as subtler patterns requiring joint channel representations to surface.

Introduction

Particle accelerators underpin a broad range of scientific and industrial applications, from neutron scattering and high-energy physics to medical isotope production and materials irradiation
[
2
,
16
]
. As the average power and duty cycle of these facilities continue to grow, the cost of an unscheduled trip, measured in lost beam time, damaged hardware, and disrupted experimental schedules, has risen sharply. The SNS at Oak Ridge National Laboratory provides the most intense pulsed neutron beams in the world, and its long-term availability statistics show that the HVCMs powering its klystrons are consistently among the top sources of downtime, second only to the mercury target
[
1
,
22
,
33
]
.

Within the accelerator community, deployment-grade anomaly detection has generally relied on a small and more task-specific toolkit: recurrent and convolutional autoencoders
[
22
]
, conditional variational autoencoders
[
1
]
, and supervised classifiers built on 1-Dimensional Convolutional Neural Networks (1D-CNNs) and LSTMs with multi-sensor fusion
[
33
]
. Two practical constraints repeatedly shape these designs. First, faulty pulses are rare and not all fault types are equally represented, so models must generalise from limited and imbalanced data. Second, the statistical relationships between the 14 recorded channels are not constant across operating regimes. Cross-channel correlations that are stable and structured during normal operation may change substantially in the presence of a fault precursor. Treating channels as fully independent discards this cross-channel information entirely
[
18
]
. Conversely, a standard convolution that mixes all channels indiscriminately from the first layer entangles cross-channel structure with temporal structure, making it harder for the network to represent regime-dependent channel interactions. This motivates an explicit factorisation of the two operations: a cross-channel mixing stage that can learn which channel combinations carry discriminative information, and a temporal filtering stage that can exploit the resulting representations.

In this work, we operationalise this factorised inductive bias for HVCM pulse-level anomaly detection. Following the depthwise-separable decomposition of mobile-vision architectures
[
4
,
9
]
, we decompose the 1D convolutional operator into a per-channel temporal filter and a cross-channel mixing operation, and study three concrete variants that differ in the order and gating of these two stages: (i) DS, which extracts channel-level temporal features first and then mixes; (ii) PW-First, which mixes channels first and then applies per-channel temporal filtering; and (iii) PW-First+SE, which augments PW-First with a squeeze-and-excitation gate
[
10
]
that re-weights derived channels adaptively per pulse. All three factorised variants reduce the parameter count of the convolutional front-end relative to a standard joint-mixing baseline that serves as a controlled comparison point.

2
The High Voltage Converter Modulator

The SNS uses a linear accelerator (see Figure
1
) to bring negative hydrogen ions to roughly 90% of the speed of light before stripping them on a carbon foil and directing the resulting protons onto a liquid-mercury target
[
16
,
22
]
. The radio-frequency power required to accelerate the beam is produced by 92 klystrons distributed along the linac. Each cluster of klystrons is in turn fed by an HVCM: pulsed power supply that converts 13.8 kV three-phase alternating current into trains of up to 135 kV, 1.3-ms pulses at 60 Hz
[
22
,
24
,
27
]
. There are 15 HVCMs in total, distributed across four sub-systems organised by accelerating structure and operating voltage:

Figure 1:
Overview of SNS. Original image by
8
.

•

RFQ: one modulator at 115 kV, powering the radio-frequency quadrupole.

•

DTL: two modulators at 125 kV, powering the drift-tube linac.

•

CCL: four modulators at 135 kV, powering the coupled-cavity linac.

•

SCL: eight modulators at 74–75 kV, powering the superconducting linac.

Each HVCM contains a 13.8 kV switchgear stage, a six-pulse phase-controlled rectifier, an oil-insulated tank with pulse transformers and resonant capacitors, and an array of IGBT switches that chop the rectified DC at roughly 20 kHz. The chopped voltage is stepped up by the pulse transformers, rectified again, combined in parallel and filtered before being delivered to the klystron cathodes
[
20
,
24
]
. The full circuit topology is shared across the 15 modulators, but design parameters such as leakage inductance, transformer turns ratio and capacitor values differ between sub-systems, so the resulting waveforms have sub-system-specific morphology even under nominal operation
[
1
,
20
]
.

Reliability of the HVCMs is critical to SNS uptime. Historically, catastrophic failures in the resonant capacitors and driver circuits have caused single-event downtimes of a day or more
[
19
,
22
]
. The typical physical structure is a high-voltage pulsed converter feeding an radio-frequency amplifier through a resonant network. It is replicated, with variations, in essentially every modern high-power linac.

2.1
The public HVCM dataset

Throughout this paper, we use the public HVCM dataset released by
20
. The dataset contains waveform samples collected by the HVCM controller from all 15 modulators between 2020 and 2022. The native acquisition is performed by a PXI-based LabVIEW controller that digitises up to 32 channels at 50 MS/s. The released dataset retains the 14 channels that domain experts identified as the most informative for fault diagnosis
[
1
,
20
,
22
]
:

•

Six IGBT currents: phases A+, A+*, B+, B+*, C+, C+*.

•

Three magnetic-flux densities, one per phase (A, B, C), measured by Rogowski coils integrated by an op-amp integrator.

•

Modulator output voltage (Mod-V) and modulator current (Mod-I).

•

Cap-bank voltage (CB-V) and cap-bank current (CB-I).

•

Time derivative of the modulator voltage (DV/DT).

The public dataset of
20
is distributed in pre-processed form: each file already contains a single 4500-step macro-pulse selected by the dataset authors following the convention described above. For normal files, the central macro-pulse is used, while for faulty files, the pre-fault macro-pulse is used, forcing the model to detect precursors rather than the catastrophic event itself
[
1
,
22
]
. Each pulse is therefore a tensor of shape
(4500\times 14)
.

The dataset is organised into the four subsystems and contains the totals shown in Table
1
. For each pulse a binary label (Run/Fault) is provided, together with a fault-type sub-label such as DV/DT, FLUX, IGBT,
Driver, SCR, and SNS PPS. This follows the fault categorisation in
1
.

As depicted in Figure
2
, each subsystem exhibits a characteristic separation between the normal and pre-fault waveform regions for each recorded channel. In the RFQ subsystem the C-Flux and Mod-V channels show a pronounced shift in mean between normal and pre-fault pulses, providing a relatively clear discriminative signal. By contrast, the CCL subsystem displays a substantially smaller separation between the two classes across the same channels, presumably making fault precursors harder to distinguish from nominal operation. These subsystem-specific differences in class separability motivate training a dedicated model for each subsystem, as detailed in Section

Two empirical observations from prior work, corroborated by our own exploratory analysis, shape our methodology. First, the cross-channel statistical structure of HVCM pulses is not constant: correlations that are stable and characteristic during normal operation change in fault-type-specific ways during pre-fault pulses
[
1
,
33
]
. Some fault families manifest as a global disruption across all channels, while others produce subtler shifts concentrated in a subset; crucially, the pattern is not fixed and may vary even within the same fault family. This motivates an architecture that can learn which channel combinations carry discriminative information and adapt its reliance on them across different pulse regimes, rather than treating all channels uniformly or independently. Second, even within the same fault family, pre-fault pulses range from clearly anomalous to nearly indistinguishable from normal pulses
[
22
]
, reflecting different precursor lead times. Models must therefore tolerate considerable overlap between the normal and pre-fault distributions, which motivates the use of imbalance-aware metrics (Section
5
).

3
Related work

The literature relevant to this work spans two scopes. At the broader level, machine learning for anomaly detection in scientific accelerator facilities has grown substantially over the past decade, with methods ranging from classical feature engineering to recurrent and convolutional deep learning applied across a variety of beam diagnostics and radio-frequency systems. At the narrower level, a small but growing body of work has targeted the HVCM specifically, producing the public dataset and the supervised and unsupervised baselines against which this paper is evaluated. The two subsections below survey each scope in turn.

3.1
Anomaly detection in particle accelerators

Within the accelerator community, machine learning is now established as a complement to physics-based machine protection
[
5
,
17
]
.
28
applied random forests to classify faults in the C100 superconducting radio-frequency cavities at Jefferson Lab, providing a publicly archived dataset of 17 radio-frequency signals
\times
8 cavities.
29
extended this with deep LSTM/CNN baselines on the same data.
7
combined singular value decomposition with isolation forests to flag faulty beam-position monitors at the LHC (Large Hadron Collider).
6
developed feedforward AEs to predict magnet faults in the Advanced Photon Source storage ring.
15
used unsupervised feature-extraction methods on radio-frequency tuning data from the ALPI accelerator at LNL. At the LHC,
31
,
30
used LSTM forecasters to monitor superconducting magnets via post-mortem voltage traces. For tokamak disruption prediction,
11
showed that recurrent neural networks transfer between machines, and the open-source DisruptionPy framework
[
13
]
now standardises pipelines across Alcator C-Mod, DIII-D, and EAST. Most recently,
32
proposed a self-supervised spatial-temporal contrastive fusion (STCF) framework for anomaly detection at the China Spallation Neutron Source, jointly modelling temporal order and inter-sensor spatial proximity from 21 beam-loss and vacuum signals without requiring labelled anomalies, achieving an average F1 of 0.80 across six months of operational data.

Specifically at the SNS, beam-current data from the Differential Current Monitor (DCM) has been used by
25
,
26
to predict errant macro-pulses with binary classifiers (logistic regression, gradient boosting, random forests), achieving up to 96% precision after Fast-Fourier-Transform feature extraction and PCA dimensionality reduction.
3
further introduced uncertainty-aware Siamese networks for the same task, doubling the true-positive rate at zero false positives.
23
continued this line with conditional Siamese and conditional VAE architectures. None of these works targets the HVCM directly, but their methodological lessons on uncertainty awareness, conditional generative modeling, and careful evaluation under imbalance inform our design.

3.2
Anomaly detection for the HVCM

The HVCM-specific literature is more recent.
19
performed early anomaly detection on a single SNS HVCM (SCL09) using discrete cosine transform features on 83 magnetic-flux waveforms, identifying 11 of 16 faults despite the very limited training set.

22
presented the first deep-learning study on the same system, applying recurrent autoencoders (Bi-LSTM, Bi-GRU) and a Convolutional-LSTM (ConvLSTM) autoencoder to the C-Flux signal of the RFQ subsystem. Trained on 150 normal pulses and evaluated on 81 normal and 50 faulty pulses, all three RAEs reached precision up to 91%, recall up to 88%, false-omission rate as low as 20%, and AUC up to 0.90, outperforming a battery of classical baselines (IF, SVM, LOF, RF, DT, KNN) and feedforward / convolutional autoencoders. The same group later open-sourced the multi-channel dataset
[
20
]
and extended the analysis to multi-module variational and convolutional autoencoders
[
21
]
.

1
introduced a multi-module Conditional Variational Autoencoder (CVAE) that jointly models all 15 HVCM modules by conditioning the encoder and decoder on a one-hot module identifier. The CVAE uses three Conv1D blocks in each of the encoder and decoder, a 512-dimensional latent space, and a Gaussian prior. Training on all 14 channels and all modules, the authors report AUC values between 0.83 and 0.98 for six fault families on MOD-V reconstruction error, with the multi-module variant systematically outperforming a per-module VAE both in detection metrics and in the convexity of its loss landscape.

Most recently,
33
proposed a supervised model that combines 1D-CNN feature extraction, multi-sensor feature-level fusion, and an LSTM temporal aggregator. The architecture first applies a per-channel 1D-CNN with kernel size 16 and stride 2 to each of the 14 raw signals, concatenates the resulting feature maps into a
(14\times 1125)
tensor, and then passes the fused tensor through three Conv1D + BatchNorm + ReLU + MaxPool blocks before a single-layer unidirectional LSTM with 64 units and a softmax classifier. Trained on a 70/30 split of the public dataset across all four subsystems, their model reaches accuracies between 90.4% (RFQ) and 92.7% (DTL), with F1 around 69–77%. This results in a marked improvement over the KNN/RF/SVM/CNN/LSTM baselines they evaluate, particularly for the minority Fault class. They defend that multi-sensor fusion outperforms any single-channel input, motivating a closer architectural look at exactly how channels should be combined.

4
Proposed method

We frame HVCM anomaly detection as a binary classification problem: given a 1.8-ms pre-pulse tensor
x\in\mathbb{R}^{14\times 4500}
, predict whether the next macro-pulse will be Normal or Faulty. Following
33
, we train and evaluate the model on a mix of normal and faulty pulses with explicit binary labels, rather than in the unsupervised reconstruction regime of
1
,
22
.

4.1
Architectural intuition

The 14 HVCM channels are a heterogeneous mix of currents, voltages, magnetic fluxes, and a derivative signal, with markedly different units, dynamic ranges, and noise characteristics
[
20
]
. How fault precursors manifest across these channels is not uniform: some fault families produce a broad disruption that is simultaneously visible across most channels, while others leave a subtler signature concentrated in a smaller subset. Within a given fault family, individual pre-fault pulses can vary substantially in how prominently the anomaly appears
[
22
,
1
,
33
]
. This is well illustrated in Figure
3
, where we observe the correlation differences between channels in different RFQ and CCL subsystems. In DTL we notice a similar behaviour to CCL, and in SCL to RFQ. An architecture capable of detecting this full spectrum of manifestations must be able to represent both the temporal shape of individual channels and the cross-channel structure of the pulse, and to weight these two sources of information flexibly depending on the input. A standard 1D-CNN conflates these two axes from the very first layer by applying a joint spatio-temporal kernel, leaving the network no explicit mechanism to separate them.

Depthwise-separable convolutions
[
4
,
9
]
decouple this operator into two distinct steps. First, a depthwise convolution applies an independent temporal filter to each channel. Second, a pointwise (
1{\times}1
) convolution linearly recombines the resulting per-channel feature maps. This factorisation exposes the ordering of the two operations as an explicit design choice. We study three variants that explore this design space:
DS
, which extracts per-channel temporal features before cross-channel mixing and is therefore better positioned when the discriminative signal lives in the shape of individual channel traces.
PW-First
mixes channels before temporal filtering and is better positioned when the discriminative signal lives in cross-channel combinations. Lastly,
PW-First+SE
augments PW-First with a Squeeze-and-Excitation gate
[
10
]
between the two stages, producing a per-pulse adaptive weighting of the mixed channels that allows the network to shift its reliance across the two regimes depending on the input pulse. All three are contrasted against a
Standard
joint-mixing baseline. The depthwise-separable factorisation was popularised in mobile-vision architectures
[
4
,
9
]
and has more recently been applied to time-series modelling by ModernTCN
[
14
]
, which we follow in spirit. All four variants share an identical backbone, classifier head, and training protocol described in Section
4.5
; the only difference is the convolutional operation inside each block.

4.2
The DS variant: depthwise-separable extraction

DS extracts channel-level temporal features first and mixes channels afterwards. Inside each block, the input is first convolved channel-by-channel with a depthwise temporal kernel of size
k
(one independent filter per input channel) and then projected to the block’s output width by a pointwise
1\times 1
convolution that linearly mixes channels. The full network stacks four such blocks with widths
(32,64,128,64)
and kernel sizes
(7,5,3,3)
. Also, the first three blocks are followed by BatchNorm, ReLU and MaxPool(
2
), and the last block uses an AdaptiveAvgPool(
1
) to collapse the temporal axis. A small classifier head (Dropout(
0.3
) followed by a single linear layer) produces the binary output. The exact backbone, applied identically across all four variants, is described in Section
4.5
. A diagram illustrating the architecture is included in Figure
4
.

Figure 4:
DS architecture.

The DS ordering corresponds to the intuition that the most discriminative information at the lowest level is the shape of each channel’s pulse, taken in isolation: a flux trace that sags below its nominal envelope or an IGBT current that fails to commutate. Cross-channel coupling, the fact that a fault in phase A’s flux is often correlated with a perturbation in phase A’s IGBT current, is then exploited at higher abstraction levels by the pointwise mixers.

4.3
The PW-First variant: pointwise-then-depthwise

PW-First reverses the ordering of DS. Within each block, the input is first projected by a pointwise
1\times 1
convolution that mixes the input channels into the block’s output width (the layer can be interpreted as a learned linear combination of raw signals). The resulting tensor is then convolved with a depthwise temporal kernel that filters each mixed channel independently. Only the order of the depthwise and pointwise operations is swapped, but the block widths, kernel sizes, normalisation, activation, pooling, and head are identical to those of the DS variant (Section
4.2
).

Figure 5:
PW-First architecture.

PW-First is theoretically attractive when the discriminative signal lives in linear combinations of the raw channels, for example, when the relative phase between two IGBT currents, or the difference between modulator voltage and cap-bank voltage, is more informative than any single trace. By mixing first, the network is given the opportunity to construct such combinations before the temporal filters fire. The same total parameter budget is, by construction, very close to that of DS, but the inductive bias is genuinely different. PW-First architecture is illustrated in Figure
5
.

The cross-channel correlation shifts illustrated in Figure
3
provide empirical motivation for the PW-First family of variants. Because fault precursors alter the statistical dependencies between channels in subsystem-specific ways, an architecture whose first operation is a learned linear recombination of input channels is better positioned to capture these discriminative cross-channel patterns than one that filters each channel independently before mixing (DS) or entangles both operations from the outset (Standard).

4.4
The PW-First+SE variant: PW-First with channel attention

PW-First+SE augments PW-First with a Squeeze-and-Excitation (SE) block
[
10
]
inserted between the pointwise and depthwise stages of every block, so each block becomes PointwiseConv1D
\rightarrow
SE
\rightarrow
DepthwiseConv1D. The SE block adaptively re-weights the mixed channels produced by the preceding pointwise layer based on a global summary of their activations, allowing the network to emphasise channels that are most informative for a given input pulse and to suppress those that carry only noise.

Concretely, given a feature map
X\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times T}
at the output of the pointwise convolution, the SE block first applies a global average pooling along the temporal axis to obtain a per-channel summary
z\in\mathbb{R}^{C}
. The summary is then passed through a small fully-connected bottleneck of reduction ratio
4
(a linear layer of width
C/4
followed by a ReLU and a second linear layer back to width
C
), and a sigmoid activation produces a vector of per-channel attention weights
s\in(0,1)^{C}
. The block’s output is the input feature map rescaled channel-wise,
\tilde{X}_{c,t}=s_{c}\cdot X_{c,t}
, so the attention weights are computed independently for every input pulse. The SE parameters add a negligible cost to the overall budget but give the network a per-pulse channel gating mechanism. In our backbone,
C
takes the values
(32,64,128,64)
across the four blocks, and the SE attention vector
s
has different lengths in different blocks, and indexes derived channels rather than the 14 input channels.

Conceptually, PW-First+SE is intended to combine the cross-channel mixing flexibility of PW-First with a per-pulse selection mechanism: even after mixing, the SE gate can amplify or suppress derived channels depending on the input pulse, allowing the network to use different combinations of the 14 input channels under different fault precursors.

4.6
Parameter efficiency

The four variants differ substantially in parameter count despite sharing the same backbone widths
(32,64,128,64)
, kernel sizes
(7,5,3,3)
and classifier head. Standard uses a full 1D convolution that jointly mixes input channels and time at every block, so each block’s parameter cost scales as
C_{\text{in}}\cdot C_{\text{out}}\cdot k
. The factorised variants (DS, PW-First, PW-First+SE) replace this with a depthwise temporal filter (
C_{\text{in}}\cdot k
parameters, applied independently per channel) followed or preceded by a pointwise mixer (
C_{\text{in}}\cdot C_{\text{out}}
parameters, applied independently per timestep), which reduces the per-block cost by roughly a factor of
k
in the limit of large kernels. Concretely, the four variants instantiate this trade-off as follows: Standard uses
63,169
parameters, DS uses
20,355
, PW-First uses
20,641
and PW-First+SE uses
33,801
, which adds a small overhead over PW-First for the squeeze-and-excitation gates of reduction ratio four.

pre-pulse tensor described there, with binary Run/Fault labels. Additionally, we have the fault-type sub-labels: DV/DT, FLUX, IGBT,
Driver, SCR, and SNS PPS.

We treat each of the four subsystems (RFQ, DTL, CCL, SCL) as an independent benchmark and apply a separate 70/30 train/test partition at the pulse level within each subsystem, stratified by class. This preserves the proportion of faulty pulses in both the train and test splits of every subsystem. One model is trained per architectural variant per subsystem (four trainings per variant), and the per-subsystem detection results reported in Section
6
are obtained by evaluating each per-subsystem-trained model on its own held-out test split. Pooled metrics (’all subsystems’ rows in Tables
3
and onwards) are obtained by concatenating the four held-out test predictions. Thus, they are not produced by a single jointly-trained model. Within each subsystem’s training split,
15\%
is held out as an internal validation set used exclusively for early stopping and selecting the decision threshold (Section
4.5
), and the test split is used only once, at the final evaluation. The full procedure is repeated for 15 independent random seeds to capture the variance induced by initialisation and split randomness.

Each channel is normalised by z-score using the mean and standard deviation aggregated across the training pulses of the corresponding subsystem and all timesteps (one statistic per channel per subsystem). The same per-subsystem training-set statistics are applied to that subsystem’s validation and test pulses. The same training-set statistics are applied unchanged to the validation and test pulses. Channels with
\sigma<10^{-8}
are assigned unit variance to avoid numerical issues. In practice, this fallback never triggers on the public HVCM data. No data augmentation is used.

Unlike the unsupervised baselines of
22
and
1
, whose training sets contain only normal pulses, our Standard, DS, PW-First and PW-First+SE variants are trained on the joint pool of normal and faulty pre-pulses, following the supervised regime of
33
. The unsupervised baselines observe faulty pulses only at test time, in line with the original protocols.

5.2
Evaluation metrics

Because the HVCM Fault class accounts for only 12–17% of the pulses per subsystem, raw accuracy is a misleading metric, since a constant predictor that always outputs Run achieves 83–88% accuracy without detecting a single fault
[
33
]
. We therefore report a panel of seven metrics:

•

Area Under the Precision–Recall Curve (AUC-PR): the primary metric, robust to severe class imbalance and our recommended figure of merit, computed without thresholding.

•

Area Under the ROC Curve (AUC-ROC): for direct comparability with
1
,
22
; we follow
12
in reporting it as a secondary metric only, since AUC-ROC is known to inflate apparent performance on imbalanced anomaly-detection benchmarks.

•

Accuracy: reported only for completeness against
33
.

•

Precision
=TP/(TP+FP)
, evaluated at the threshold that maximises validation F1.

•

Recall
=TP/(TP+FN)
, evaluated at the threshold that maximises validation F1.

•

F1: the harmonic mean of precision and recall, evaluated at the threshold that maximises validation F1.

•

G-Mean: the geometric mean of recall and specificity, sensitive to performance on the minority class, evaluated at the same threshold.

The decision threshold for the four threshold-dependent metrics (F1, Precision, Recall, G-Mean) is selected once per seed on the validation precision–recall curve as the point that maximises validation F1, and is then applied unchanged to the test set. AUC-PR and AUC-ROC are computed directly from the predicted probabilities and require no threshold.

6.3
Cross-channel correlations

The choice between DS and PW-First is fundamentally a question of the input geometry. If the discriminative information lives in linear combinations of the 14 channels, PW-First should win. Conversely, if it lives within channel temporal patterns, DS should win. PW-First+SE attempts to be agnostic to that distinction by allowing the network to gate channels adaptively after mixing.

To probe this empirically, we run two complementary ablations on the same 15 trained seeds. We consistently distinguish between
input channels
(the 14 raw HVCM signals introduced in Section
2.1
) and
derived channels
(the
F
feature maps produced by the pointwise convolution inside each block, where
F\in\{32,64,128,64\}
across the four blocks of the backbone). Both ablations probe input-channel reliance, first at the level of physically related sensor groups and then at the level of individual channels. We report results for all four variants. The PW-First and PW-First+SE comparison is the relevant one for isolating the contribution of the SE block, while including Standard and DS provides additional context on how the joint-mixing baseline and the depthwise-temporal-first variant distribute their reliance across input channels.

7
Concluding remarks

In this paper, we present three lightweight CNN variants for pulse-level anomaly detection on High Voltage Converter Modulator data, and argue that the inductive bias encoded in a convolutional block is not a neutral design choice for multi-channel physical sensor data. Factorising the operator into a per-channel temporal filter and a cross-channel mixer, and varying their order, produces a monotonic performance ranking across all four SNS subsystems and most fault families. The channel-first variant augmented by per-pulse adaptive reweighting reaches an AUC-PR of 0.816 and AUC-ROC of 0.934, surpassing both supervised and generative baselines from the literature on the majority of evaluated metrics. The per-fault-family breakdown reveals that this gain is not uniform: fault types whose precursors produce large amplitude shifts in individual channels are detectable by all variants, while those requiring the model to integrate information across channels benefit most from explicit cross-channel mixing before temporal filtering.

Cross-channel ablations reinforce this picture. Three channels, C-Flux, Mod-V, and CB-I, account for the bulk of attribution across all variants. The DV/DT channel, derived directly from Mod-V, contributes the least and is largely redundant, suggesting that future data collection could safely prioritise primary physical quantities over derived ones.

Several directions remain open. The squeeze-and-excitation reweighting used here is global and symmetric; a spatial or temporal attention mechanism that conditions channel weights on the pulse trajectory could adapt more precisely to within-pulse transients. Graph-based architectures that encode the physical wiring topology of the modulator directly into the model offer a principled way to move beyond learned channel mixtures toward structured physical priors. Finally, full SHAP attribution across subsystems and fault families would enable a more systematic audit of which physical phenomena drive the model’s decisions and where residual misclassification is concentrated.
