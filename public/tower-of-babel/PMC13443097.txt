Migratory jackpot individuals fuel rapid ecotype shifts in Galaxias fishes
Authors: Ashleigh Iwikau, Jason Augspurger, Marc A Bailie, Graham A McCulloch, Mitra M Darestani, Tania M King, Gerard P Closs, Travis Ingram, P Mark Lokman, Bruce Deagle, Christopher P Burridge, Ludovic Dutoit, Jonathan M Waters
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdaptation is considered essential for species facing environmental change, but our understanding of the evolution of complex ecotypes remains limited. We show that a substantial genomic cluster of co-adapted alleles has repeatedly fuelled complex life-history shifts in fishes. Specifically, the majority of strong outlier loci underpinning repeated landlocking of formerly marine-migratory Galaxias lineages are co-located on a single chromosome. Phenotypic, behavioural and genomic comparisons reveal a strong parallel basis for multi-trait ecotype shifts (osmoregulation; musculature; locomotion; reproduction) across eight independently landlocked populations. Critically, migratory populations house ‘jackpot’ individuals carrying intact sequences essential for rapid multi-trait life-history switches. The observed high frequencies of these migratory jackpot carriers explain the rapid ecotype shifts detected in Galaxias populations isolated by recent catastrophic landslides in New Zealand. These findings represent a complete example of complex ecotype evolution, highlighting the key role of modular genomic variation in facilitating rapid adaptive change.Subject terms: Evolutionary genetics, Ecological genetics, Population genetics

Introduction

Scientists have long been captivated by the repeatability of evolution1–5. Iconic examples of such parallelism include repeated loss of pigmentation and vision in cave fauna6,7, myriad flight-loss events in birds and insects8–10, and rapid ecotype shifts in sticklebacks11–16. Some of these intriguing cases involve complex multi-trait shifts, underpinned by diverse loci9–16. While recent data suggest genomic architecture can potentially expedite such shifts17–21, evolutionary theory11 still struggles to explain how complex ecotypes can evolve so rapidly (e.g. over ecologically-relevant timeframes)22–26.

Globally, transitions from freshwater-marine migratory (diadromous) to freshwater-limited (landlocked) life-histories have been key drivers of fish diversification11–16,25–30. However, the mechanisms underpinning the repeated evolution of phenotypically similar landlocked lineages have remained contentious9, as adaptation has produced concordant shifts across diverse traits such as spawning behaviour, osmoregulation, reproductive investment, and predator avoidance11–16,25–28. Researchers initially proposed that such multi-trait shifts must be explained by selection at unlinked loci, with disparate freshwater-adapted alleles introgressed into migratory populations, and subsequently re-aggregated in newly landlocked populations via recombination (the ‘transporter hypothesis’; Fig. 1)11. However, emerging data call this model into question, suggesting instead that rapid parallel adaptation may be facilitated by rare migratory ‘jackpot’ individuals carrying co-located landlocking alleles within large recessive haploblocks28 (Fig. 1). Nonetheless, empirical studies have found scant (if any) evidence for such ‘landlocking’ haploblocks within migratory fish populations11–16,26.

Contrasting pathways to multi-trait adaptation in response to environmental change.

Landlocking of marine-migratory fishes drives parallel shifts in morphology, behaviour, and reproduction (Fig. 2). A The ‘transporter’ hypothesis11–14 proposes that multiple important ‘landlocking’ alleles are recruited independently across large numbers of marine-migratory founders. B In contrast, the ‘jackpot’ hypothesis26,28 involves the simultaneous recruitment of multiple landlocking alleles as haploblocks carried by key migratory individuals (purple). Unlike the ‘transporter’ hypothesis11, the ‘jackpot’ hypothesis provides a plausible mechanism for rapid ecotype shifts in response to sudden environmental change28, such as in numerous New Zealand lakes formed by catastrophic landslides107. Under both scenarios, ‘landlocking’ alleles are re-distributed back to the marine-migratory population via introgressive hybridisation (dashed arrow), but these are more greatly fragmented by recombination under the transporter than under the jackpot hypothesis. Our study of New Zealand Galaxias brevipinnis provides evidence for high frequencies of ‘jackpot’ individuals in marine-migratory populations, enabling rapid ecotype shifts even when lake founding sizes are small (Fig. 3) (images: DigitalGlobe; https://slidenz.net/).

New Zealand’s radiation of freshwater Galaxias fishes (Galaxiidae, Osmeriformes) (Fig. 2) represents a highly replicated system for understanding parallel evolution associated with landlocking30–38. Specifically, sudden loss of marine migration (e.g. following major landslides) has led to rapid and concordant shifts across diverse phenotypic characters (e.g. larval musculature and swimming ability; fecundity; spawning time; Fig. 2)30–38. While recent molecular data suggest a role for genetic parallelism underpinning these repeated Galaxias landlocking events33,34, the genomic distribution of alleles under selection remains unknown. Likewise, it remains unclear whether ‘landlocking’ alleles are recruited directly as haploblocks from marine-migratory jackpot carriers (Fig. 1), or independently.

Here we firstly demonstrate repeated morphological and behavioural divergence between migratory versus landlocked fish ecotypes. Second, we test whether modular ‘landlocked’ haploblocks have fuelled rapid evolution of complex ecotypes. Specifically, we map genome-wide SNPs from five marine-migratory and eight independently and post-glacially landlocked Aotearoa New Zealand Galaxias brevipinnis (kōaro) populations to a newly assembled reference genome. Because marine-migratory G. brevipinnis become increasingly rare with distance upstream34–38, remote inland lake populations (e.g. lakes formed by recent landslides; Fig. 1) were likely founded by just a few such individuals. Under this scenario, we predict parallel evolution of landlocked phenotypes would require readily detectable jackpot individuals within marine-migratory populations. Here we provide evidence for substantial frequencies of jackpot carriers fuelling rapid switches in fish life history.

Results and discussion

Landlocking drives dramatic shifts in larval morphology and locomotion

Comparisons between laboratory-reared larvae of landlocked versus marine-migratory populations (Fig. S1), under standardised conditions, revealed substantial differences in several morphological and locomotory measures (Supplementary Note 1). After accounting for their larger sizes at hatching, the larvae of migratory populations were substantially more muscular (Table S1) and faster swimming than larvae from landlocked populations (Figs. 2 and S2). Notably, 7-day-old migratory larvae were substantially stronger swimmers than even 28-day-old landlocked larvae, despite the greater size of the latter (Fig. S2). Likewise, ANCOVA revealed that migratory larvae exhibit substantially increased musculature (myomere depth) than landlocked larvae, after accounting for differences in total length (Table S1). We propose that, while strong swimming ability is essential for diadromous Galaxias larvae encountering strong waves and currents in coastal waters, such ability is unnecessary for landlocked larvae encountering relatively benign lacustrine conditions37. We further suggest that the energetic savings from reduced muscle development may facilitate survival in relatively oligotrophic upland lake systems. Our research also shows that landlocked populations are characterised by spring (rather than autumn) spawning38 (Fig. 2), and substantially increased female fecundity38 with reduced egg size38 (Fig. 2)

Independence of landlocking events

Phylogenetic analysis of 113,005 genome-wide SNPs revealed striking differentiation among landlocked populations, with each lake population (all from different drainages; Tables S2 and S3) represented by a distinct monophyletic group (Fig. 2). Moreover, demographic analyses support the hypothesis that each lake population was independently derived from the marine migratory population (Fig. S3). By contrast, the three marine migratory populations from mainland New Zealand together comprised a single ‘mixed’ clade, with no structure evident among populations. The geographically remote migratory populations of the Auckland and Chatham islands were each represented by a distinct clade, substantially divergent from the mainland migratory clade (Fig. 2).

Landlocking loci are co-located

Using a newly assembled draft Galaxias brevipinnis genome (22 chromosomes; 590.3 Mb; N50 = 28.2 Mb; L50 = 10; BUSCO = C:95.7% [S:94.7%, D:1.0%]; Figs. S4–S6), comparisons of New Zealand migratory (combined) and landlocked populations (eight populations; 114 individuals) revealed outlier loci on eleven chromosomes (Fig. 2). Rather than being individually dispersed, the majority of the strongest outliers are co-located on Chromosome 17, which has two distinctive outlier regions, each characterised by a SYNE-ESR gene complex (Table S4; Fig. 3A, B). Specifically, of the 71 outliers (Q < 0.01) differentiating diadromous versus landlocked samples, 43 (61%) are located on Chromosome 17 (23.4 Mb; Figs. 3 and S7), and the majority of these (33) are distributed within a 1.2 Mb region housing several strong candidate genes for rapid life-history evolution. Of the 43 outlier SNPs on this chromosome, 22 (51%) are located within exons, and half of these are non-synonymous (Table S4; Fig. 3).

Parallel genomic basis of reduced larval musculature and locomotion

The key outlier loci located on Chromosome 17 show dramatic, parallel allele frequency shifts associated with eight independent landlocking events (Fig. 4). The strongest outlier locus in the data set (FST = 0.98; BayeScan FST = 0.44) is a non-synonymous substitution (SNP 15,291,940) in AKAP-12b (Gravin) (Fig. 3B). Knockdowns of this gene in zebrafish produce larvae with poor swimming ability39, a distinctive phenotype shared by larvae of landlocked G. brevipinnis37 (Fig. 2). A further seven outlier SNPs differentiating migratory and landlocked populations within the 1.2 Mb region are located within the large (75 kb) gene SYNE-1 (Nesprin-1; Fig. 3B). Knockdowns of this cytoskeletal protein alter muscle development and function40,41, as well as cell shape42,43, and this gene has previously been strongly implicated in salmonid landlocking44. We propose that both AKAP-12b and Nesprin-1 underpin the repeated evolutionary reduction in muscle development and swimming performance (Fig. 2) that characterise landlocked G. brevipinnis larvae37.

Endocrine receptors implicated in life-history switches

An additional candidate gene located within the 1.2 Mb outlier region of Chromosome 17 is ESR-1 (oestrogen receptor), with three exonic SNPs substantially differentiated between migratory and landlocked G. brevipinnis (FST = 0.79–0.95; Fig. 3B). In zebrafish, ESR-1 mutant females exhibit significantly elevated fecundity45, mirroring the increased egg production of landlocked G. brevipinnis (Figs. 2 and 3)38. Additional candidate ‘landlocking’ genes found within this 1.2 Mb cluster of outlier loci include GRIK-2 and TRAM-2, both of which are implicated in the landlocking of other osmeriform fish lineages46. Indeed, recent studies suggest a key role for GRIK-2 (a glutamate-gated ion channel) in facilitating adaptation to osmotic stress in euryhaline species47.

Two additional chromosomes house clusters of outlier SNPs implicating a role for endocrine receptors in repeated Galaxias life-history evolution. Firstly, on Chromosome 13 (26.5 Mb; Fig. 2), the strongest (SNP 2,773,847) of seven outliers comprising a 45 kb island (FST = 0.92) is a non-synonymous mutation within the coding sequence of somatostatin receptor 2-like (SST-R). Somatostatin (SST) inhibits the release of growth hormone48, which plays a key role in energy re-distribution and development of hypo-osmoregulatory ability during smoltification (the physiological transformation required for fish migration between freshwater and marine habitats49). Indeed, SST has recently been proposed as a key endocrine switch for reallocating energy between growth and reproduction50, traits that are central to fish life-history evolution. Secondly, on Chromosome 16 (24.3 Mb), five strong outlier SNPs (including four with FST > 0.80), comprising a 76 kb island, tightly flank the endocrine gene thyroid hormone receptor alpha (TR-alpha). Thyroid hormone is the ubiquitous driver of larval-to-juvenile transitions in vertebrates51, and has long been implicated in fish life-history variation52, including key roles in phenology and behavioural shifts49,53. Moreover, a recent genomic study showed that TR-alpha is differentiated between migratory and landlocked forms of other osmeriform fishes (Neosalanx ecotypes within the Yangtze River system)46. We propose that this endocrine locus is involved in the repeated shift in spawning from autumn (marine-migratory) to spring (landlocked) in G. brevipinnis (Fig. 2)38.

Highly heterozygous migratory individuals support ‘jackpot’ hypothesis

The ‘transporter hypothesis’ (originally proposed for stickleback life-history transitions11) has been widely supported as a key evolutionary mechanism facilitating parallel evolution12–16. Under this hypothesis, a migratory population maintains and ‘transports’ the genetic ‘fuel’ needed for repeated evolution of non-migratory landlocked lineages, but does so via large numbers of individuals each contributing distinct alleles. Despite the broad acceptance of the transporter hypothesis, landlocking may have instead been facilitated by jackpot carriers26, each containing the full complement of necessary alleles as a large recessive haploblock (Fig. 1). However, direct evidence for the presence of such individuals has until now remained scant26.

Our analysis reveals that migratory G. brevipinnis populations carry significantly elevated heterozygosity at the key Chromosome 17 outlier regions relative to their genome-wide variability, whereas landlocked individuals exhibit the opposite pattern (Fig. 3C). More importantly, we show that approximately 5% of migratory specimens are jackpot carriers, being widely heterozygous across key outlier loci of this haploblock (Maori River #3 (Fig. 3B, D); Breccia Creek #3). This structuring of genomic variation within and among individuals implies that colonisation of upland lakes by even small numbers of migratory founders38 has potential to fuel rapid adaptation to landlocked freshwater habitats. This Southern Hemisphere Galaxias system thus contrasts with Northern Hemisphere Gasterosteus, in which founding populations of lowland lakes are large, and jackpot carriers (assuming they exist) have been sufficiently rare to inhibit detection11–16,26.

Under both ‘transporter’11 and ‘jackpot’ hypotheses26, the ‘landlocking’ haploblocks carried by migratory individuals are likely to have introgressed from landlocked into migratory G. brevipinnis populations through hybridisation (Fig. 1). Such hybridisation is perhaps most likely to have occurred in lowland lake settings (e.g. Lake Paringa; Fig. 2) that are readily accessible to marine-migratory individuals38,54. Despite the broadly distinct spawning periods of migratory versus landlocked G. brevipinnis ecotypes, the flexible and extended reproductive seasonality of landlocked populations suggests reproduction between ecotypes is feasible during early summer (Fig. 2)37,38. It is also possible that rare flooding events may occasionally sweep landlocked larvae downstream from lakes, with potential for subsequent introgression of non-migratory alleles into the migratory population. Regardless, introgressed recessive ‘landlocking’ alleles (Fig. 1) are likely maintained in the migratory population due to its large effective size11–16, with substantial haploblocks (Figs. 3B and S7) remaining intact likely due to structural genomic features (e.g. inversions18–21).

Concluding remarks

Our study provides key support for the importance of jackpot carriers in facilitating the rapid evolution of complex ecotypes. Such individuals likely play essential roles influencing the speed of complex adaptative shifts in response to sudden environmental change28 (Fig. 1). Our findings also highlight the significant role of modular genomic architecture in fuelling such complex ecotype shifts18. Indeed, these genomic features may be crucial in facilitating rapid evolution in a fast-changing world5, including adaptive responses to landslide events that are regular occurrences in geologically dynamic settings.

Methods

All specimen collection and animal handling were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of the University of Otago (permits AUP-19-64; AUP-59-2016; AUP-103-2013; AEC-64-14) and by the New Zealand Department of Conservation (permits 39843-RES; 51959-RES).

Larval rearing

We compared larval morphology, swimming performance and developmental differences between marine-migratory versus landlocked populations of Galaxias brevipinnis55. Reproductively mature fish were collected by backpack electrofishing, and later strip spawned in the laboratory. Specifically, landlocked adults were obtained from two tributaries of both Lakes Wanaka and Wakatipu, and migratory adults were collected from three streams along the west coast of New Zealand (Fig. S1). To account for known differences in spawning time38, ripe landlocked G. brevipinnis were collected on 23 October 2015, whereas ripe marine-migratory G. brevipinnis were collected on 30 March 2016.

Eight ripe females and four ripe males from each site were strip spawned following protocols previously developed for galaxiids56,57. Mature oocytes were strip-spawned into plastic petri dishes where they were combined with milt from all running ripe males collected from one site. If females were not ovulating, ovulation was induced by two intraperitoneal injections of Human Chorionic Gonadotropin (1000 IU kg-1) administered over 24 h57,58. Gametes were activated in freshwater (0 ppt) and then incubated submerged (simulated aquatic oviposition) in plastic petri dishes with twice daily water changes56. Fungal infections were minimised by removing all unfertilised and dead eggs twice daily. In addition, a methylene blue solution (1 ppm) was used during daily water changes. To simulate natural rearing conditions, eggs from land-locked fish were subjected to increasing day length associated with spring spawning, while eggs from marine-migratory populations were subject to decreasing day length due to autumn spawning. Potential differences in incubation time were accounted for by recording incubation time of each larva from fertilisation to hatching. Rearing of both landlocked and marine-migratory populations was completed in freshwater.

Following hatching, larvae were maintained at a high density for the first two days of life due to high mortality. Then, to minimise competition and maximise growth, larvae were transferred to rearing aquaria, at densities of no more than 10 larvae/L of water, and fed first instar Artemia and finely ground pellet food ad-libitum throughout rearing. Dead larvae and excess food were removed during daily water changes. Rearing temperatures of eggs and larvae varied with ambient air temperature, ranging from 12.2 ⁰C to 15.4 ⁰C. Due to seasonal differences in ambient air temperature, landlocked eggs and larvae were reared at a mean temperature of 14.5 ± 0.5 ⁰C while marine-migratory eggs and larvae were reared at a mean temperature of 14.9 ± 0.3 ⁰C.

Larval swimming and morphology

Swimming performance and rheotaxis of larvae were assessed throughout ontogeny (days 0, 4, 7, 14, 21 and 28) in at least 10 larvae from each site. To accommodate the rapid development associated with the depletion of endogenous feeding reserves and increase comparability with other larval galaxiid studies, trials were conducted more often during the first week of life than subsequent weeks57. Realistic swimming patterns and ability were assessed by critical speed (Ucrit) swimming trials, representing a combination of both burst and sustained swimming speed57. The potential effects of turbulence were minimised and a theoretical maximum Ucrit obtained using freshwater in a laminar flow flume. Trials were conducted by (i) allowing larvae a 2 min acclimatisation period to the flume, (ii) starting the flume at 10 mm/s, and (iii) increasing the speed by 20 mm/s increments every 2 min57 until the larva was exhausted. To assess response to current, larvae were considered positively rheotactic if they oriented upstream, negatively rheotactic if oriented downstream, and indifferent if no taxis to current was shown. To allow for morphological measurement, larvae were euthanized after the trial, and hence only used once. Larvae exhibiting laboured swimming were excluded from trials.

Morphological differences throughout ontogeny were measured at days 0, 4, 7, 14, 21 and 28 post swimming trial57. To allow for comparison of morphology and assessment of key larval provisioning characteristics, six key characteristics were measured57. As a measure of larval size, potential performance and growth, larval total length (TL) was measured. Larval robustness was measured via post anal myomere depth, measured as body depth immediately posterior to the anus. The range of prey sizes that larvae could consume was estimating by measuring gape size based on the length of the upper maxilla. Sight/foraging ability was accounted for by measuring eye diameter, which was calculated as the mean of the longest and shorted axes of the eye. To assess maternal provisioning, endogenous provisioning and resistance to starvation, yolk sac diameter was measured and calculated by averaging the longest and shortest axes of the yolk sac. As the eyes, yolk sac and oil globule were not perfectly spherical, measurement of the longest and shortest axes provided an average diameter which was used to estimate yolk volume57.

Phototaxis trials

Responses to photocues were quantitatively assessed at day 10 by placing 20 larvae per site in a black tray (300 × 200 mm), divided into six compartments, and allowed to acclimate for 10 min in darkness. After 10 min, a 1000 lumen light was turned on over one of the compartments, and the larvae present in the illuminated compartment versus the dark were counted after 30 s. To determine the dominance of rheo- or photo-positional cues, strength of phototaxis vs. rheotaxis trials were run on 10 of the 10-day old larvae per site. Trials were conducted by placing larvae in a laminar flow flume with a light located near the front of the flume. After a 2 min acclimation, the water velocity was gradually increased to a speed of 80 mm/s. Larvae were allowed to acclimate to this velocity for 2 min and then the light was moved to the back of the flume. If larvae followed the light, they were considered more phototactic than rheotactic.

Genome sequencing, assembly and annotation

A reference genome was obtained from a diadromous Tasmanian individual of Galaxias brevipinnis, a sister clade of New Zealand Galaxias brevipinnis30,31. A single male individual was sampled from Snug River for 10 µL of blood via caudal vein puncture and tissue samples. From that individual, RNA-Seq, PacBio Hifi and Hi-C data were generated. DNA was extracted using the Circulomics Nanobind HMW protocol for nucleated blood. The genome was sequenced at Novogene Singapore using one SMRT cell of a PacBio Revio to generate HiFi reads. Gill, gonad, skeletal muscle, and liver transcriptomes were sequenced for the same individual at the Ramaciotti Centre for Genomics, UNSW, using the Illumina Stranded mRNA kit on a Novaseq X Plus to generate 2 × 100 bp reads. Finally, frozen blood and fin was shipped to the Biomolecular Research Facility at the Australian National University for Hi-C library preparation using the Arima Hi-C kit, and sequencing on an Illumina Nextseq (2 × 150 bp).

Initial sequence quality was assessed with NanoComp v1.20.0, followed by CutAdapt v4.4 for adaptor sequences removal59,60. Genome assembly was then performed on the complete trimmed HiFi and Hi-C sequence using Hifiasm v0.19.7 with default parameters for Hi-C integration61. Gfastats v1.3.6 was used to assess size and quality metrics of the complete primary contig assembly62. To estimate completeness of the assembly, BUSCO v5.6.1 scoring was carried out using eukaryote auto-lineage option initially, and with the actinopterygii_odb10 dataset in successive runs63,64. Jellyfish v2.3.0 was then used to count canonical 21-mers and output a histogram of coverage in the sequence file, followed by input to GenomeScope2 to estimate genome expected size based on k-mer coverage65,66.

Purge_dups v1.2.5 was run to remove duplicate sequences and heterozygous haplotigs67. Sequence reads were first aligned to the primary assembly using minimap2 v2.2868. Aligned reads were processed to obtain relevant coverage statistics, and duplicate regions were removed. RepeatModeler v2.0.3 and RepeatMasker v4.1.0 were then used to mask repetitive sequence from the final assembly69,70. Finally, contigs were ordered from largest to smallest using SAMTools v1.2271.

Galaxias genome annotation

The Galaxias genome was annotated using short-read RNA-seq as the primary evidence. Paired-end reads were aligned to the soft-masked genome with STAR v2.7.11b72 and merged with SAMtools v1.22.173; splice junctions were curated with Portcullis v1.2.474. Protein alignments from actinopterygian/SwissProt datasets were generated with miniprot v0.1775 and converted into AUGUSTUS76 intron hints using prot_aln2hints.pl. BRAKER77–79 v1.9 (AUGUSTUS v3.5.0, GeneMark-ET80) was run with both RNA and protein hints to generate evidence-trained gene models. In parallel, transcripts were reconstructed using StringTie v3.0.081 and Scallop v0.10.582 from the merged RNA-seq BAM, while a de novo transcriptome was assembled with Trinity v2.15.283. Coding sequences and proteins were predicted with TransDecoder84, informed by Pfam85 and SwissProt homology86 searches.

All annotation evidence was integrated with Mikado v2.3.987 to produce a consensus gene set, scored using high-confidence Portcullis junctions. Outputs were tidied and validated with GenomeTools v1.6.588 and gffread v0.12.789. Structural refinement and sequence extraction (mRNA, CDS, proteins) were performed with AGAT, including longest-isoform selection and FASTA generation90. Functional annotation of predicted proteins employed InterProScan v5.59-91.091; Pfam, PANTHER92, SMART93, TIGRFAM94, SUPERFAMILY95, TMHMM96, Phobius97 and eggNOG-mapper v2 (DIAMOND mode, v2.1.13)98 to assign GO, KEGG, and COG terms. Functional attributes were propagated from mRNAs to gene features. Annotation completeness was assessed with BUSCO (actinopterygii_odb10 lineage, v5.7.1)99, and AGAT reports provided structural and functional summaries. All environments were pinned for reproducibility.

Long-read polishing

The draft genome assembly was polished using Pacific Biosciences (PacBio) high-fidelity (HiFi) long reads with approximately 40× coverage. Quality control and polishing were performed using a custom genome polishing pipeline implemented in Python. The HiFi reads were aligned to the draft assembly using Minimap2 v2.24100 with the ‘map-pb’ preset optimised for PacBio reads. The assembly was polished through two iterations of Racon v1.5.0101 using fragment-correction mode for memory efficiency. Each Racon iteration consisted of read mapping with Minimap2 followed by consensus polishing. The final polished assembly was used for subsequent scaffolding.

Hi-C scaffolding

Chromosome-level scaffolding was achieved using proximity-ligation Hi-C sequencing data. Hi-C paired-end reads were independently aligned to the polished assembly using BWA-MEM v0.7.19102 with the ‘-5SP’ flags to preserve supplementary and split alignments critical for Hi-C data. The R1 and R2 read pairs were aligned separately with 32 threads, then merged using SAMtools v1.1573. The combined alignment file was name-sorted, processed with SAMtools fixmate to add mate information and mark duplicate candidates (-m flag), position-sorted, and PCR/optical duplicates were marked and removed using SAMtools markdup with the -r flag.

Scaffolding was performed using YaHS v1.2.2103 with default parameters, which conducted iterative scaffolding rounds at increasing resolutions (10 kb, 20 kb, 50 kb, 100 kb, 200 kb, 500 kb, 1 Mb, and 2 Mb). YaHS identified 32 telomeric repeat motifs (AACCCT) at scaffold termini and performed three rounds of contig error correction, resulting in 13 assembly breaks to resolve misjoins. The scaffolding process reduced the assembly from 274 input contigs (590.2 Mb total) to 60 scaffolds with an N50 of 28.2 Mb, N90 of 19.3 Mb, and total assembly size of 590.3 Mb. The final scaffolded assembly showed substantial improvements in contiguity, with the largest scaffold reaching 42.8 Mb.

Contamination screening and contig filtering

Assembly contamination was assessed using BlobToolKit v3.0104 within a Singularity container (blobtoolkit.sif). A BlobDir database was created for the scaffolded assembly using the NCBI taxonomy ID 42526 (Galaxias genus) and the NCBI taxdump database. Sequence similarity searches were performed against the NCBI nucleotide database using BLASTN105, and taxonomic assignments were determined using the ‘bestsumorder’ rule. Coverage information was incorporated from both HiFi long-read alignments and Hi-C read alignments in BAM format. Interactive blob plots were generated to visualise GC content, coverage, and taxonomic assignments for contamination identification.

Following contamination screening, scaffolds were filtered to remove potential contaminant sequences and small fragments. Scaffolds shorter than 22 kb were excluded from the final assembly to reduce fragmentation while retaining biologically relevant sequences. This size threshold was selected to remove small contigs while preserving scaffolds likely to contain complete genes or functional elements, resulting in a high-quality reference genome for downstream analyses.

Genome annotation transfer

Gene annotations from the original draft assembly were transferred to the new scaffolded genome using Liftoff v1.6.3106. The transfer was performed with stringent parameters: sequence identity threshold of 0.95 (-s 0.95), alignment coverage threshold of 0.95 (-a 0.95), and child feature coverage threshold of 0.95 (-sc 0.95). The ‘-cds’ flag ensured proper handling of coding sequences, ‘-exclude_partial’ removed partially mapped features, and ‘-copies’ enabled detection of gene duplications or paralogs in the new assembly. The original annotation file (mikado.final.longest.gff3) from the Mikado v2.3.9 pipeline87 was used as input. Liftoff was run with 32 threads (-p 32), and unmapped features were logged to unmapped_features.txt for downstream review. The final lifted annotation was validated using gffread v0.12.789 with the -E flag to verify structural integrity and identify potential annotation errors. Feature counts were compared between original and lifted annotations to assess annotation completeness and identify genes that failed to transfer.

Software and computing resources

All computational analyses were performed on a high-performance computing cluster running Ubuntu Linux. Jobs were allocated 16-128 CPU cores and 64-128 GB RAM depending on computational requirements. Conda v25.3.1 was used for environment management, with specific environments created for polishing, scaffolding, and annotation.

Genomic comparisons of marine-migratory and landlocked life histories

A total of 184 G. brevipinnis specimens were collected from 13 New Zealand localities (9–16 fish per site) between 2007 and 2016 (Table S2). Specifically, diadromous (migratory) specimens were sampled from three coastal rivers of ‘mainland’ New Zealand (Fig. 2), and from the remote Chatham and Auckland Island groups. Landlocked populations were sampled from lakes or lake tributaries across eight distinct South Island catchments (Fig. 2). These lakes are all postglacial in age, and two were formed by catastrophic landslides107 (Table S2). The youngest, Lake Chalice, was dammed by a landslide in the Goulter River headwaters approximately 2000 years ago107, and has an underground outlet.

Whole genomic DNA was extracted from fin clips or muscle tissue using the Qiagen DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kit, with DNA quantified by NanoDrop spectrophotometer. Genotyping-by-sequencing (GBS) library preparation was performed by AgResearch Ltd, using a PstI-MspI double-digest108,109. Libraries underwent a Pippin Prep (SAGE Science) to select fragments in a size range of 193–318 bp (genomic sequence plus 123 bp of adaptors), with all 184 samples sequenced as single-end reads on one lane of an Illumina HiSeq 2500 using v4 chemistry.

FastQC v 0.11.7 was used initially to assess the quality of the sequence data. Raw reads were demultiplexed according to their barcode and filtered for quality using the process-radtags module in STACKS v.2.41110. Adaptors were removed using Cutadapt v.4.4 and reads shorter than 50 bp were discarded.

Variant calling was carried out using the refmap pipeline in STACKS v2.67 to align GBS data from 191 individuals to the reference genome73. SNPs were filtered using VCFtools v0.1.15 to retain loci present in at least 80% of samples and exclude SNPs with a minor allele frequency <0.01111. Further filtering was carried out to remove individuals with more than 50% missing data, retaining 154 individuals and 113,005 sites.

The final variant call file was converted to GESTE format using PGDSpider v3.0.0.0112. Individuals were grouped based on life history, and BayeScan v2.1 was run to identify FST outliers between the two groupings113. FST scores with a Q-value of ≤0.01 were considered significant and retained for analysis. LDBlockShow v1.39 was then used to calculate and visualise r2 values for co-located significant GWAS hits and determine the presence of linkage disequilibrium between these outlier SNPs114. We calculated the mean heterozygosity for each individual in TASSEL115, and compared genome-wide heterozygosity versus heterozygosity across the 1.2 Mb outlier region on Chromosome 17 using a two-tailed paired t-test.

Demographic analyses

We used DIYABC-RF v1.0.14116 to test whether the landlocked populations arose independently from migratory mainland ancestors. We compared two demographic scenarios: Scenario 1 assumed a single landlocking event (as suggested by the phylogeny), whereas Scenario 2 assumed eight independent landlocking events (Fig. S4). In both scenarios, the order of divergence among the landlocked populations was unconstrained. Although these scenarios do not encompass all possible histories of landlocking, they provide a framework for evaluating alternative diversification scenarios.

We removed SNPs with minor allele frequencies <0.05, and those not found in at least one of the sampled populations. The vcf file was converted to DIYABC format using the vcf2DITABC.py script (https://github.com/loire/vcf2DIYABC.py). As population sizes and divergence timeframes were not known we used broad, uninformative priors (101–106 for each parameter), with a uniform distribution.

We used the Random Forest module for model choice. For each scenario, 50,000 simulations were performed, and 500 trees were generated to identify the best-supported model. To evaluate model fit, we conducted a PCA comparing prior and posterior distributions of the summary statistics with the observed data, using the “model checking” option in DIYABC.
