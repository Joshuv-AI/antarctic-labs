 ABSTRACT

A structured decision metrics analysis was developed as one of the tasks under PROACTIVE’s
Focus Area 4 (FA4) and used structured decision metrics for processes, items, and facilities (PIF)
to determine the efficacy of an M&V system in meeting treaty goals and technical objectives.
The method starts with the functional decomposition of a “treaty” with the M&V system overlaid
on it. A Functional Decomposition Rubric (FDR) for each PIF is used to determine a score
ranging from 0 (weak) to 4 (strong) for assurance, security, and burden. The individual scores are
combined to provide an overall Verification System Score (VSS) which assesses the overall
verification system’s suitability given goals; scores for each step of the process are also given.
The outcome of the evaluation is to identify needs or modifications to the enterprise model,
verification system, or proposed testbed capabilities. VeriScore was used to evaluate FA4’s
Spiral 0 exercise; those results are included in this report. The VeriScore and FDR framework
can also be used in a non-treaty environment, as any goal/objective/method structure will work,
thus expanding its applicability beyond traditional arms control structures.

 1. INTRODUCTION

To address the challenges of future Arms Control agreements post New START, the Priority
Research Objectives for Arms Control Technology Innovation, Verification, and Evaluation
(PROACTIVE) venture was established. The goal of this venture was to enable the negotiation
and implementation of future nuclear arms limitation/reduction treaties with verification at the
level of individual warheads by advancing the state of the art in verification and monitoring
technical capabilities. The PROACTIVE venture had four focus areas to address technical
priorities one of which deals with verification systems. Focus Area Four (FA4) aims to model,
quantify, and connect elements of the confirmation and monitoring technologies, equipment, and
data trust to build verification confidence. The goal was to establish methodologies for the
integration of verification and monitoring technologies into verification systems to minimize
operational burdens, meet information security, and maximize confidence in conclusions [1].

 2. EFFICACY EVALUATION FOR VERIFICATION SYSTEMS

2.1 PROACTIVE FOCUS AREA FOUR: VERIFICATION SYSTEMS

FA4 was a system-of-systems effort to establish methodologies for the integration of monitoring
and verification (M&V) technologies into verification systems that support treaty objectives, in
ways that minimize operational burdens, meet information security, and maximize confidence in
conclusions. The individual tasks within FA4 were designed to be able to accept and feed
information among themselves. The information flow and other tasks within FA4 are described
in more detail in Reference [2]. Core information flow is represented by the functional
decomposition of the treaty, which includes the treaty goals and requirements, M&V objectives,
and compilation of technical approaches and activities that could be used to perform those tasks.
An enterprise model captures the context for the M&V systems, e.g., the local state of the

 1

nuclear enterprise and the activities being performed as part of the M&V objectives, modeled
using the Discrete Event Simulator (DES). Although models and components are designed to
address the high-level “verification system for the complete enterprise” questions, simplified but
expandable treaty functional decomposition models and DES are needed to model the application
of different evaluation approaches. These approaches are R&D methods for evaluating multi-
attribute performance over multi-scaled systems. Sensor system data characterization data is
injected into the DES and output from the DES is given to the efficacy evaluation teams to test
candidate M&V systems against the treaty goals via a set of metrics to determine how well the
system meets those goals. Gaps and vulnerabilities are also folded into the flow and evaluation
and provide feedback to the DES.

2.2 EFFICACY

Efficacy is an evaluation of how well an M&V system meets stated goals given a specific
enterprise definition or scenario. The M&V system includes a wide variety of components.
These components may include monitoring transportation or chain of custody measures,
confirmation or absence evaluation, or accountancy. It also includes the web of activities tying
the components together over both time and location for a given enterprise system.

To evaluate the efficacy for specific goals, four metrics of the system are determined:
 Assurance that the information gathered by the M&V system can provide confidence that the
 declarations made are true and correct. This can be as statistically rigorous as determining the
 expected accuracy of conclusions drawn based on statistical analysis of M&V data,
 computing and comparing information gains between candidate systems, and identifying
 potential deception targets, or as statistically simple as performing a systematic evaluation
 based on quantitative and qualitative information.

 Burden that the M&V system places on the host from facility, personnel, and/or resource
 allocations.

 Security of the M&V system due to the quantity and sensitive nature of the data collected.

 Vulnerability of the system to 1) opportunities where treaty objectives could be violated
 without being detected by the M&V infrastructure, or 2) vulnerability to other activities
 which have the potential to disrupt the first three efficacy metrics.

Efficacy evaluation was designed to assess how well a given M&V system may provide
information to ensure confidence that treaty goals are met by partners while also providing
information about the burden and security risks that would be imposed on hosts. For example, an
M&V system that passively collects low confidence data over an extended period may end up
with a higher overall efficacy evaluation than one that collects a single, highly invasive
measurement with higher confidence.

2.3 STRUCTURED DECISION METRICS ANALYSIS

A structured decision metrics analysis was developed as one of the tasks under FA4 and used
structured decision metrics for processes, items, and facilities (PIF) to determine the efficacy of
an M&V system in meeting treaty goals and technical objectives. The method starts with the
functional decomposition of a “treaty” with the M&V system overlaid on it. A Functional
Decomposition Rubric (FDR), described in Section 2.3.2, for each PIF is used to determine a
score ranging from 0 (weak) to 4 (strong) for assurance, security, and burden. The individual
scores are combined to provide an overall Verification System Score (VSS) which assesses the
overall verification system’s suitability given goals; scores for each step of the process are also
given. The process is shown in Figure 1. The outcome of the evaluation is to identify needs or
modifications to the enterprise model, verification system, or proposed testbed capabilities.

 Figure 1: Simplistic diagram of Structured Metrics process

Sensitivity analyses can be performed at any level of the functional decomposition. This is done
by varying the FDR score for a process, instrument, or facility from the highest score to the
lowest score and evaluating the change in the overall VSS. These sensitivity analyses can help a
user determine which items, processes, or facilities are most important to meeting treaty
objectives and which have little or no bearing.

2.3.1 Functional Decomposition and Scenario-Specific Input

The functional decomposition is a hierarchy of a set of goals and objectives (e.g. a treaty)
starting with the overall goal (called the “treaty goal” when considering a formal treaty) and
extending down to the possible measurement and monitoring technologies that could be used to
address those goals. A graphical example of a basic functional decomposition is shown in Figure
2.

 3

 Figure 2: Simple diagram of a basic functional decomposition and the FDRs that feed into it.

At the highest level of decomposition are the goals. In a formal arms control treaty, this would be
the “treaty goal,” e.g. “Limit total strategic warheads,” and the “strategic goal,” e.g. “Limit
strategic warheads in the storage location.” These are the why of the M&V construct. Below the
goals are the “treaty” objectives which start the process of determining how the treaty and
strategic goals will be accomplished. The “treaty” requirements, M&V objectives, and technical
activities break down the where, what, and what is needed to meet the treaty objectives. Finally,
the FDRs are not part of the decomposition but feed into it to provide the SME input necessary to
determine to what degree an M&V system meets the overarching goals.

2.3.2 Functional Decomposition Rubrics

The FDRs are based on pedigree matrices and pedigree analysis. A pedigree matrix is a tool to
systematically score parameter strength with respect to predefined quality indicators (called
pedigree criteria [3]). Pedigree assessment involves qualitative expert judgement. To minimize
the inherent subjectiveness of expert judgement, pedigree matrices are used to code qualitative
expert judgements into a discrete numerical scale from 0 (weak) to 4 (strong) with written
descriptions of each level on the scale. Because each type of information has its own key aspects,
different pedigree matrices are used with different criteria for each [4].

FDRs can be populated and scored based on input from the storyboards described in Section
2.3.1, which can be completed by technology developers and subject matter experts. FDRs also
rely on enterprise-specific conditions defined by the functional decomposition and the DES.
Each FDR is subdivided into scores for assurance, burden, and security. An example FDR for a
simple visual inspection of an item is shown in Figure 3. This FDR would be scored by subject

 4

matter experts (SMEs), and an average of scores used to determine a final score for the FDR that
is used in the structured metrics framework.

 Figure 3: FDR for a visual inspection.

Two general types of FDR pedigree matrices were used for FA4 purposes: (1) scenario agnostic
and (2) scenario dependent. Scenario-agnostic FDRs can be scored by the subject matter expert
(SME) for a technology, facility, or process being assessed in the absence of the context of a
treaty or a specific application within a treaty scenario. For example, an instrumentation FDR
(such as one for a radiation detector) can be scenario agnostic as it represents the assurance,
security, and burden of the instrument itself irrespective of the M&V system. In turn, scenario-
dependent FDRs depend on a specific scenario or state of the enterprise system, as in the case of
a radiation measurement, which would then rely on and pull in scores from the scenario-agnostic
radiation detector FDR. When there are linked scenario-agnostic and scenario-dependent FDRs,
the scores for each are averaged to obtain a final total FDR score.

A set of FDR templates were developed for this project. These can be found in Appendix A.
More needs to be done to vet these templates with other SMEs to ensure completeness.

2.3.3 Algorithm Structure and Output

A prototype code, written in python and called VeriScore, pulls together all the pieces of the
structured metrics process. VeriScore receives the graded functional decomposition of a
verification system as input, scores the verification system according to an importance-weighted
grade propagation method, and presents users with visualization and analysis options for results.

 5

The primary function of VeriScore is to quantitatively evaluate a proposed verification system
based on its assurance that treaty objectives are being met, the burden the system places on the
host institution, and the security of information and/or materials involved in the M&V activities.
As mentioned in Section 2.2, these three attributes (assurance, burden, and security) are
collectively referred to as the efficacy of the verification system.

The proposed verification system is broken out to produce a functional decomposition.
Conceptually, the functional decomposition is a tree structure starting with high level treaty
objectives that break down into individual M&V activities. Logistically, the functional
decomposition is stored as a csv file. Each line includes the level of the item in the tree, a unique
identifier used to simplify visualizations, a more detailed description for reference, the identifier
of its parent item, the importance weight (description below), and the individual item scores for
assurance, burden, and security, if applicable.

At the lowest level of the functional decomposition, there is a technology, process, or procedure
associated with the M&V activity. These are referred to as technical components. Each technical
component is graded according to the appropriate FDR on the assurance, burden, and security.
Scores are propagated bottom-up to achieve the overall verification system scores. The score of
each parent item in the tree is calculated using a weighted average of the scores of its
subcomponents. Figure 4 gives a graphical representation of this process.

 Figure 4: Simple example illustrating the weighted average process for assurance, burden, and security score
 propagation.

The weights used for calculating the average of subcomponent scores correspond to the
importance of that subcomponent on fulfilling the parent objective. Importance weights are
therefore dependent on both the facility the M&V system is being applied to and the overall
treaty goals. For instance, if one facility features multiple staging locations separated by large
spaces, then the importance of secure transportation will be higher than the same process being

 6

applied to a facility with only one working area. Similar to the process of generating the FDRs,
the importance weights scores are determined using an average of SME determinations. Overall
assurance, burden, and security scores for the verification system are displayed by VeriScore on
the “Overall” tab in the main window as a bar chart with a range of 0 to 4.

In addition to overall efficacy scores, VeriScore performs sensitivity analysis using the
functional decomposition. When evaluating a proposed verification system, it is useful to
identify which technical components present an outsized influence on the overall efficacy of the
system. The sensitivity of the overall efficacy to an individual technical component is defined as
the calculated efficacy score difference between the system with that component being a perfect
4 out of 4 and it being a 0 out of 4. This difference is recorded for each technical component, and
the results are presented on the “Overall” tab in the main window as a pie chart. Larger wedges
of the pie chart correspond to technical components whose efficacy scores bear an outsized
influence on the overall efficacy scores of the verification system. Figure 5 shows the entire
“Overall” tab in the VeriScore main window.

 Figure 5: Graphics displayed in the VeriScore “Overall” tab. (Left) Overall assurance, burden, and security scores
and (Right) the overall sensitivity pie plot including each technical component in the functional decomposition. The
 highest and lowest sensitivities in the M&V system are listed below the pie chart.

The next layer of granularity on the sensitivity analysis addresses the following two questions: 1)
Which technical component(s) does the system stand the most to gain from improving? and 2)
Which technical component(s) does the system stand the most to lose from downgrading? The
answers to these questions are revealed in the upside and downside sensitivity analysis,
respectively. Whereas the overall sensitivity to an individual technical component was calculated
by setting the individual efficacy scores to 4 and 0 and taking the difference, upside sensitivity
compares the proposed system “as is” to one with that technical component set to a perfect 4.
Likewise, the downside sensitivity is calculated by comparing the proposed system to one with

 7

that technical component set to 0. Assurance, burden, and security sensitivities are calculated
independently, and each are displayed as an upside pie chart and a downside pie chart in the
“Upside/Downside Sensitivity” tab as shown in Figure 6.

Figure 6: Pie charts displayed in the “Upside/Downside Sensitivities” tab showing the (Top) upside sensitivities and
 (Bottom) downside sensitivities.

The M&V system used to generate Figure 5 and Figure 6 represents a very simple M&V system.
Even using this system, it is evident that the sensitivity analysis for a more complicated (and
more realistic) M&V system will quickly become too convoluted to display. In order to produce
a result that is more useful to the end user, the concept of level sensitivity was introduced. In the
“Level Sensitivity” tab of VeriScore, the user can select a level to analyze. The functional
decomposition tree is truncated at this level, and the sensitivity analysis previously described is
performed on the truncated tree. The lowest level items are treated as new technical components.
To calculate their equivalent FDR scores, the scores from each sub-item are propagated up to the
items in the selected level. The results of the sensitivity analysis are displayed in an analogous
way as the overall sensitivity analysis. Fundamentally, the question this analysis answers is,
“Which sub-tree in the selected level has the most outsized influence on the efficacy scores of
the rest of the M&V system?” Rather than focusing on individual technical components (e.g. a
single radiation measurement), the focus is placed on higher level M&V objectives. An example
of the output generated by the level sensitivity analysis is presented in Figure 7.

 8

Figure 7: The upside and downside results for the level sensitivity analysis. In this example, level 3 was chosen out
of a 5-level functional decomposition. Note that the number of items analyzed was reduced from 23 to 7 by running
 the analysis on level 3 instead of the lowest level.

Once a particular M&V objective is identified using the level sensitivity analysis, the user may
be interested in identifying which technical component that item is most sensitive to. VeriScore
provides a modality to display this information as well. In the “Item Sensitivity” tab, the user
may select the level in the functional decomposition containing their desired item. A drop-down
menu is then populated with all items within that level. Upon selecting the desired item, the
sensitivity analysis is performed using the selected item as the root of the functional
decomposition. The result is a simplified display that is more useful for the user. An example of
the item sensitivity analysis is presented in Figure 8.

 Figure 8: Item sensitivity analysis output after the user selected level 3 and item 1.A.3.1 within level 3. "Update
Level" updates the drop-down menu of items within the chosen level and "Update Plots" redraws the pie charts for
 the item.

 3. SPIRAL ZERO SYSTEM FRAMEWORK

FA4 performed the Spiral 0 exercise over three days in July 2023. It consisted of six scenarios of
increasing complexity that were built from four treaty scenarios (and two variations of those
treaty scenarios). The exercise is described in greater detail in previous publications [2, 5].

based on NA-243 literature. They were designed to introduce increasingly complex verification
systems to demonstrate the tradeoff between confidence and sensitivity concerns. The MSEL
definitions are shown in Figure 9, and each MSEL had a specific script for host and inspector

3.4 ANALYSIS AND RESULTS

The aggregated FDR scores and functional decomposition information relevant to each MSEL
was read into VeriScore. The resulting output comparing the overall VSS for each MSEL is
shown in Figure 11 and Table 6. The results demonstrate the increase in the assurance score as
the scenarios become more complex but also shows an increase in burden and the stagnation of
the security score. Indicating as expected that as the scenarios increase in complexity, assurance
increases dramatically but so does burden.

 4. OUTLOOK

Going forward, the framework could be modified to be used with other exercises such as the
EMC2 and IT1 exercises hosted by the PROACTIVE venture. This would involve using the
storyboards that were completed by the venture tasks and notes taken during EMC2 and IT1 to
select and score the appropriate FDRs for the exercises and develop new FDRs as required. The
FDRs and functional decomposition that are relevant to EMC2 and IT1 can then be read into
VeriScore to obtain an analysis of the exercise and technologies used for it.

On a broader note, VeriScore can be utilized for any issue that involves a goal and methods
proposed to achieve that goal. This makes it a very useful tool for fields outside of a treaty/OSI
concept and even outside of arms control entirely.
