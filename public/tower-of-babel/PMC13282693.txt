Defining the Human Nucleus Pulposus Microenvironment and Its Impact on Cell Matrix Synthesis and Metabolic Activity
Authors: Niamh Wilson, Tara Ní Néill, Jake McDonnell, Emily McDonnell, Stacey Darwish, Joseph S Butler, Conor T Buckley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionIntervertebral disc (IVD) degeneration remains the leading cause of low back pain worldwide. Regenerative therapies focused on restoring extracellular matrix (ECM) composition and disc height often overlook the IVD microenvironment, which remains to be fully characterized. This study first aims to profile the microenvironment of human nucleus pulposus (NP) tissue across degeneration grades from discectomy procedures, quantifying glucose, oxygen, pH, lactate, osmolarity, and 13 cytokines (TNF, IL‐1β, IL‐6, MMP‐3, β‐NGF, BNDF, IL‐10, TIMP‐1, ‐2, ‐3, FGF, and ADAMTS4 and 5). Profiling within the same samples enabled correlation analysis between all parameters. Second, this study investigated how clinically relevant microenvironmental conditions influence NP cell matrix synthesis and metabolic activity.Materials and MethodsMicroenvironmental profiling: NP tissue was obtained via informed consent from patients undergoing discectomy. pH was measured using a fiber optic microsensor. Oxygen was quantified using Image‐iT green hypoxia reagent. Glucose and lactate were quantified via colorimetric assays, and osmolarity was measured using a vapor pressure osmometer. Cytokines were analyzed by multiplex and enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). Cellular response: NP cells were formed into microtissues and primed with TGF‐β3 for 7 days, followed by 14 days of clinically relevant microenvironmental insult with different combinations of glucose and pH or cytokine exposure and pH. Cell viability, DNA, GAG, collagen, and metabolic rates were assessed.Results and DiscussionAcross a broad donor cohort, microenvironmental parameters, cytokine concentrations, and ECM were maintained with increasing degeneration grades, despite notable donor variability. NP microtissues demonstrated resilience across clinically relevant ranges of glucose, pH, and cytokine exposure. This study establishes experimentally defined microenvironmental ranges that are representative of the human NP microenvironment and supported by donor‐specific in silico modeling. It further demonstrates that human NP cells within a native matrix are not highly sensitive to clinically relevant changes in microenvironmental conditions, an important consideration for cell‐based regenerative strategies.Keywords: cytokines, glucose, intervertebral disc, matrix synthesis, metabolism, microenvironment, nucleus pulposus, osmolarity, oxygen, pH

Introduction

Low back pain (LBP) is a global socio‐economic concern and remains the single leading cause of disability worldwide. LBP affected an estimated 619 million people in 2020, and this number is projected to increase to 843 million by 2050 due to global population aging [1, 2]. The condition affects individuals across all ages and demographics [3], contributing the highest global years‐lived‐with‐disability among all health conditions [1, 2]. The economic impact is considerable with both direct costs attributed to care and indirect costs due to lost wages and reduced productivity [4]. In Europe alone, the annual cost exceeds €240 billion per year [5], making it the third most expensive health condition, with work absences accounting for nearly two‐thirds of the total expenditure [6].

Although LBP is multifactorial in origin [7], degeneration of the intervertebral disc (IVD) is thought to play a crucial role through compression of the vertebrae and impingement of the nerve root endings [8]. IVDs are soft, fibrocartilaginous tissues located between the vertebrae of the spine, facilitating movement and load transmission [9]. Owing to their avascular nature, the IVD relies primarily on diffusion through the cartilage endplate (CEP) for the supply of nutrients and removal of metabolites [10]. Degeneration disrupts this process, altering nutrient gradients and giving rise to a harsh microenvironment that reduces cell viability and matrix synthesis [11].

Recent regenerative strategies aimed at restoring native disc function include cell‐based therapies, cytokine and growth factor delivery, and biomaterial‐based approaches [12]. These strategies promote extracellular matrix (ECM) synthesis by increasing cell number or activity as demonstrated in in vitro and preclinical models. They maintain cell viability by mitigating inflammatory factors within the disc microenvironment and providing a protective niche. Mechanical integrity is preserved by restoring proteoglycan‐rich matrix content and structural support necessary for physiological load bearing [13, 14]. However, the success of these approaches has been limited. Despite numerous preclinical studies demonstrating positive outcomes, there are currently no Food and Drug Administration (FDA) or European Medicines Agency (EMA) approved intradiscal cell‐based therapies [15]. However, several regenerative therapies, including mesenchymal precursor cell‐based approaches, are advancing through late‐stage clinical development, indicating potential for future clinical translation [16]. Previous studies and clinical trials have explored a variety of cellular therapies, ranging from mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) [17] to chondrogenic cell types [18]. Despite increasing clinical interest in regenerative therapies for IVD degeneration, relatively few clinical trials have reported complete outcomes or demonstrated consistent long‐term efficacy although several approaches are now progressing through advanced stages of clinical development [19, 20]. One of the major barriers to success is the limited consideration of the harsh NP microenvironment during the development of these treatments. Transplanted cells must endure an avascular, nutrient‐deficient, hypoxic, and acidic microenvironment. However, many in vitro experiments and ex vivo studies are conducted under optimal conditions such as normoxia, ample glucose supply, and neutral pH [21]. Consequently, cells that thrive under standard culture conditions may often fail to survive or function effectively in vivo due to these harsh conditions. Preclinical animal models partially address these limitations by allowing evaluation of transplanted cells within a living, mechanically loaded disc environment. However, most models involve young, healthy animals, greater nutrient availability, and limited degeneration, which fail to fully recapitulate the hostile microenvironment of the human degenerated IVD [22]. These discrepancies may partly explain the persistent gap between promising preclinical findings and the limited success observed in human clinical trials.

The microenvironment of the IVD has been characterized across various stages of degeneration, through a combination of human tissue measurements [23], in silico modeling [24], and animal studies [22], revealing gradients of oxygen, glucose, osmolarity, and pH [23, 24, 25]. Nutrients and metabolites diffuse through the CEP [26, 27], with concentration gradients becoming steeper as degeneration advances [24, 28, 29, 30] (Figure 1). Consequently, the innermost NP, which lies along the longest diffusion pathway, becomes the most nutrient‐deprived region [31]. In human NP tissue, oxygen levels have been reported to range from 4% to 10% [23, 32, 33], with most studies based on small donor cohorts and limited degeneration ranges, deteriorating as degeneration progresses [31, 34]. Glucose concentrations are estimated to lie between 1 and 5 mM [24, 29, 31] in NP tissue, decreasing in degenerated discs as nutrient transport becomes restricted due to endplate calcification [35]. Although glucose concentrations within human NP tissue have been experimentally quantified, existing measurements are limited to a small number of donors over the age of 55 [36], therefore heavy reliance is placed on in silico modeling to inform concentrations within the disc [24, 29, 37, 38]. Osmolarity in healthy human NP tissue averages 430–500 mOsm, decreasing to 300 mOsm in degenerated tissue [39, 40], and pH values fall from approximately 7.1 to as low as 6.5 in advanced degeneration [23, 24, 41]. Lactate accumulation accompanies these changes, further acidifying the microenvironment [23, 42]. Collectively, these alterations create a low oxygen microenvironment that is lacking in nutrients which can affect cell metabolism and matrix homeostasis [35, 43]. However, these parameters are typically measured independently and across small cohorts, limiting understanding of how microenvironmental factors interact within the same human disc. Thus, the current gap lies not only in donor number or degeneration range, but in the lack of integrated multi‐parameter profiling across individual human NP samples. Therefore, a clear understanding of the native NP microenvironment and its influence on cellular metabolism is essential for the design and optimization of regenerative strategies that aim to restore disc structure and function.

In addition to nutrient and metabolite changes, disc degeneration is accompanied by a shift in cytokine levels within the disc [44], with several studies reporting direct measurements in human IVD tissue [44, 45]. Pro‐inflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor (TNF) [44, 46], interleukin‐1β (IL‐1β) [44, 47], and interleukin‐6 (IL‐6) [45] have previously been quantified in intradiscal tissue, most commonly obtained during discectomy procedures for nerve root compression associated with disc herniation, primarily using immunoassay‐based approaches [48] with limited studies reporting on changes in these factors as a result of degeneration [44]. Catabolic enzymes including MMP‐3 and aggrecanases including a disintegrin‐like and metalloproteinase with thrombospondin motifs 4 (ADAMTS4) and ADAMTS5 are stimulated in the degenerative state, which accelerate ECM degradation [49, 50]. Anti‐inflammatory and regulatory cytokines including interleukin‐10 (IL‐10) [51], fibroblast growth factor (FGF), and the tissue inhibitors of metalloproteinases (TIMP‐1, TIMP‐2, TIMP‐3) [52] counteract these effects by promoting matrix preservation and repair [53]. Neurotrophic factors such as β‐nerve growth factor (β‐NGF) and brain‐derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) have also been reported in human disc tissue and may influence pain signaling and nerve ingrowth [54, 55]. However, these measurements are typically derived from small cohorts and reported in isolation, limiting the ability to understand the interactions between cytokines and other microenvironmental factors within the same human samples. The balance between these inflammatory and anti‐inflammatory mediators is critical for maintaining homeostasis, yet this complex cytokine network remains underexplored in the context of human NP degeneration [49].

While individual components of the NP microenvironment have been studied extensively, relatively few studies have explored how these parameters interact with one another and the combined effect they can have on influencing cellular behavior [56, 57]. Understanding their combined effects is essential for improving the translatability of in vitro models and ensuring that emerging regenerative strategies are compatible with the native disc environment.

The aim of this study was first to characterize the human NP microenvironment with respect to oxygen, glucose, pH, osmolarity, lactate, deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA), glycosaminoglycan (GAG), collagen, and a range of cytokines (TNF‐α, IL‐1β, IL‐6, IL‐10, MMP‐3, ADAMTS4, ADAMTS5, FGF, TIMP‐1, TIMP‐2, TIMP‐3, β‐NGF, BDNF) across varying grades of degeneration. Second, the impact of clinically relevant multi‐factor microenvironments on cellular matrix synthesis and metabolism was investigated. Overall, this work aims to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the human NP microenvironment and its implications for the development of regenerative and cell‐based therapies.

Materials and Methods

Tissue Procurement

Human NP tissue was collected through informed consent of patients undergoing discectomy procedures and approved by the Mater Misericordiae Hospital Institutional Review Board (Ref 1/378/2229) and Trinity College Dublin (TCDFSTEMSREC/15032021/Buckley). Tissue was collected along with Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) scans and previous patient medical history. Tissue samples were obtained from patients undergoing discectomy procedures, typically performed to relieve nerve root compression associated with lumbar disc herniation. As such, samples may include displaced or extruded nucleus pulposus tissue rather than intact central disc tissue. A total of 26 patients, both male and female between the ages of 20–72 with varying degrees of disc degeneration were included (Figure 2A). Samples were graded by three individual clinicians based on MRI scans using the Pfirrmann grading system. Donor specific age and degeneration grades are detailed in Table S1. Not all measurements were obtained for every sample due to technical and sample‐specific limitations. In some cases, oxygen measurements were not possible due to insufficient tissue volume. Osmolarity data were omitted for earlier samples due to temporary unavailability of the osmometer. Several pH readings were excluded due to visible blood contamination preventing reliable readings, and certain cytokines fell below the assay's limit of detection.

Experimental Design

Each NP sample was divided into two portions, one designated for microenvironmental profiling and the other reserved for subsequent cell isolation (Figure 2B). Profiling involved assessing the oxygen concentration, pH, glucose, lactate, osmolarity, and a panel of 13 inflammatory and regenerative cytokines to characterize the native NP microenvironment across mild and moderate degeneration. Among these factors, glucose and pH demonstrated the widest variability across samples and have previously been shown to strongly influence NP cell behavior [35, 58]. On this basis, three glucose levels and three pH levels were defined from the measured clinical ranges. Combinations of these factors were then applied within a 3D microtissue model to evaluate their effects on matrix synthesis and metabolism within a clinically relevant range. Microtissues were selected to better recapitulate cell–matrix interactions and nutrient diffusion characteristics of the native NP environment. Following identification of the effects of glucose and pH, a subsequent experiment was performed in which both parameters were maintained at their mean physiological values. Cultures were then exposed to the presence or absence of the profiled cytokines at mean measured concentrations to determine their relevance during in vitro stages of cell therapy development.

Oxygen Measurement

Initially, oxygen profiling was attempted using a needle‐type fluorescence microsensor (NTH‐PSt1, Presens Germany). Despite this method previously proving successful in profiling intact discs in our laboratory, it proved unreliable for small tissue samples. NP oxygen levels are reported to be notably below atmospheric oxygen [23], and once tissue is exposed during surgery, rapid diffusion from ambient air concentration alters local oxygen within the sample, compromising probe readings. As a result, obtaining confident measurements from small, excised NP fragments with this method was not feasible and therefore fluorescent‐based hypoxia labelling was employed.

Immediate hypoxia staining of freshly isolated tissue was not performed, as the staining protocol was not established at the time of collection for earlier donors. To maximize donor inclusion and ensure consistent methodology across the cohort, oxygen measurements were performed on OCT embedded NP samples. Accordingly, all tissues were kept at room temperature prior to being embedded in OCT and snap‐frozen in liquid nitrogen within 16 h of surgical removal. Although hypoxia‐dependent fluorescence intensity differed relative to immediate staining, this change was consistent and was incorporated into the oxygen calibration described below (Figure 2D). Cryosections (10 μm) were prepared using a Leica cryostat and incubated for 3 h with 10 μM Image‐iT Green Hypoxia Reagent. Sections were mounted using Fluoroshield containing DAPI (Sigma‐Aldrich) and imaged on a Leica SP8 confocal scanning microscope. The Image‐iT Green hypoxia signal was excited at 488 nm, while DAPI was excited at 405 nm. Corrected total cell fluorescence (CTCF) was quantified using ImageJ software. The Image‐iT reagent reports hypoxia via endogenous reductase enzyme activity that is upregulated under low oxygen. A membrane‐permeable precursor is reduced intracellularly to a green‐fluorescent product, yielding signal intensity proportional to local oxygen concentrations. Importantly, once activated under hypoxic conditions, the Image‐iT Green signal is not rapidly reversible upon reoxygenation. Therefore, calibration standards incorporating identical handling and processing conditions were used to ensure that fluorescence intensity could be reliably related to oxygen concentration across all samples. Although Image‐iT Green Hypoxia reagent is marketed for live‐cell applications, enzyme histochemistry is routinely performed on unfixed cryostat sections [59, 60, 61], which preserve sufficient enzymatic activity. Consistent with this principle, oxygen‐dependent Image‐iT green hypoxia signal was observed in snap‐frozen, unfixed NP cryosections. This approach was directly compared to that of live tissue samples stained under standard Image‐iT conditions.

To evaluate the impact of sample handling and processing on oxygen measurement, a series of validation experiments was performed as described below. To facilitate extrapolation of CTCF to native oxygen concentrations, a standard curve was generated from donor tissue biopsies of 2 mm diameter incubated for 72 h at 2%, 5%, 10%, or 20% oxygen. For each oxygen level, tissues were stained under three conditions after removal from the incubator to assess the effect of sample handling: (a) immediately while the tissue remained viable, (b) after 16 h at ambient conditions (to simulate live‐tissue handling during collection), and (c) after 16 h at ambient conditions followed by OCT embedding, snap‐freezing in liquid nitrogen and cryosectioning (to match the workflow used for the study samples) (Figure 2C,D). As the accuracy of these standards underpins all quantitative readings, we validated oxygen exposure by two methods: (i) in silico modeling to confirm minimal intra‐sample gradients under the culture setup, and (ii) real‐time well oxygen monitoring using an SDR plate reader (PreSens, Germany) (Figure 2E).

pH Measurement

pH was measured in tissue samples using the PreSens pH‐1 micro and accompanying needle‐type pH microsensor NTH‐HP5. Each needle‐type microsensor consists of a fiber optic sensor housed inside a 21G needle, mounted on a 1 mL syringe. To ensure the sensor remained within the housing at all times, a mark was placed on the plunger of the syringe to inform the maximum extension of the sensor. The sensor was connected to the pH‐1 micro fiber optic pH transmitter, which in turn was operated via the Presens Profiling Studio software (all PreSens, Germany). A custom holder was designed to ensure stability of the probes while taking measurements and facilitating a wide range of motion. Prior to each measurement, the probes were calibrated within the pH range of 4–9.2. A multipoint calibration was carried out using clear buffer solutions of pH 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, and 9.2. In each case, the needle‐type pH microsensor was washed with deionized water and immersed in each pH buffer until a stable plateau was reached, indicating a constant value for the phase corresponding to that pH.

Hydration and Osmolarity Measurements

To determine hydration, tissue samples were weighed pre and post overnight lyophilization using a Mettler Toledo XS205 DualRange analytical balance (Mettler Toledo, Switzerland) in a Labconco Triad freeze drier (Labconco Corporation, USA) using a standard procedure (500 mTorr, −10°C, 16–18 h). Hydration was calculated as the water removed during this process as a percentage of the total wet weight. Osmolarity was measured using a vapor pressure osmometer (Elitech, UK), calibrated according to the manufacturer's instructions before each use. Tissue samples were placed in the instrument's sealed chamber and allowed to equilibrate for 1 h, as determined through preliminary testing prior to measurement.

Glucose and Lactate Measurements

A biochemical assay for quantifying glucose and lactate in human NP tissue was developed using Glucose Liquid (Sentinel Diagnostics 17630H) and Lactate Dry Fast (Sentinel Diagnostics 17285) assays. As these assays had not previously been applied to human NP tissue, three standard preparation methods were evaluated to ensure that sample processing did not affect readings (Figure 2F,G). Method (1) Incubated tissue standards: For each standard, 100 mg of human NP tissue was placed in 10 mL deionized water (diH2O) and agitated for 48 h on an orbital shaker, with a complete water change performed after 24 h to remove native glucose and lactate. Tissues were then lyophilized for 24 h and rehydrated for 2 h in diH2O containing known glucose or lactate concentrations. Samples were subsequently digested in papain (125 μg/mL) (SLS Scientific Laboratory Supplies (Ireland) Ltd) with 5 mmol/L L‐cysteine HCL (Sigma–Aldrich, Merck Life Science Ltd.), papain buffered extract (PBE) (50 mM sodium phosphate dibasic anhydrous, 50 mM sodium phosphate monobasic anhydrous, 0.05 mol/L EDTA, all Sigma–Aldrich, Merck Life Science Ltd.) at 60°C for 18 h under constant rotation at 10 rpm. This digestion protocol is consistent with that used for biochemical analysis of NP tissue, including quantification of DNA, GAG, collagen, and metabolite content. For each sample, a single papain digest was used for all assays requiring enzymatic digestion, enabling multiple biochemical measurements to be obtained from the same tissue sample. Method (2) Spiked tissue standards: Tissue standards were prepared as before and digested in papain solutions containing known glucose or lactate concentrations, bypassing the incubation step. Method (3) Solution based standards: Papain or PBS solutions containing known glucose or lactate concentrations were generated to assess potential papain interference. A simple linear regression showed no significant differences among the standard preparation methods for either factor. Therefore, PBS‐based standards were used for all analyses. Samples were digested in papain solution as described above for 18 h. All glucose and lactate measurements were normalized to tissue hydration levels. The Glucose Liquid and Lactate Dry Fast reagents use an enzymatic colorimetric reaction based on glucose/lactate oxidase and peroxidase. Glucose is oxidized to gluconic acid and hydrogen peroxide, while lactate is oxidized to form pyruvate and hydrogen peroxide. In both cases, the byproducts react with peroxidase to form a colored compound: red for glucose, measured at 505 nm, and purple for lactate measured at 540 nm, using a plate reader.

Cytokine Measurement

Cytokine presence within human NP tissue was quantified using GeniePlex Multiplex Immunoassays and ELISA kits (AssayGenie). A multiplex plate was used to quantify IL‐1β, IL‐10, IL‐6, TNF, TIMP‐1, TIMP‐2, FGF, β‐NGF, BNDF, and MMP‐3 and read using a flow cytometer (CANTO). ADAMTS4, ADAMTS5, and TIMP‐3 were quantified using custom ELISA kits with absorbance measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Biotek). All samples were prepared through homogenization in cold tissue lysis buffer containing 200 mM sodium chloride, 10 mM Tris–HCL pH 7.4, 5 mM EDTA, 1% Triton X, 10% glycerol, 1 mM oxidized L‐glutathione, 100 μM PMSF, 2.1 μM Leupeptin, and 0.15 μM Aprotinin (all Sigma–Aldrich). Once prepared, the lysis buffer was kept on ice. For each donor, 100 mg of tissue was weighed and homogenized in 500 μL of ice‐cold tissue lysis buffer using the IKA T10 basic homogenizer. The 10 mm diameter dispersing tool was operated at the highest speed to minimize processing time required and consequently heat generation. All samples were normalized to tissue hydration to account for differences in water content between samples and thereby correct for variations in wet weight, with values expressed in pg/mL.

Quantitative Biochemical Analysis for Tissue and In Vitro Microtissue Samples

Lyophilized tissue was papain digested in 500 μL papain solution as described previously. Throughout the culture period, media was pooled from 4 microtissues per group. The pooled media and associated microtissues were lyophilized post culture and papain digested in 400 μL papain solution. DNA was quantified using the Quant‐iT Pico Green DNA kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with 10 μL of a 400 μL digest used to obtain a reading. Sulphated glycosaminoglycan (sGAG) levels were determined using a dimethylene blue dye‐binding assay, using a chondroitin sulfate standard (Shark) (all DMMB Blyscan, Biocolour Ltd) [58, 62, 63]. In this assay, binding of the dye to sGAGs generates a colorimetric signal following dissociation, with absorbance proportional to sGAG concentration. Total collagen content was assessed via hydroxyproline assay, which measures the collagen specific amino acid, hydroxyproline. Measured hydroxyproline values were converted to collagen content using a hydroxyproline to collagen ratio of 1:7.69 [58, 64, 65].

Cell Isolation and Monolayer Expansion

NP tissue was obtained from each donor, with each sample treated as an independent biological replicate. All donors were included in cell isolations where sufficient tissue volume was available, and the degree of degeneration was recorded. NP tissue was incubated overnight at 37°C to ensure sterility in low‐glucose Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (LG DMEM, Sigma‐Aldrich, Merck Life Science Ltd., U.K.) supplemented with 2% penicillin/streptomycin (Pen/Strep, Invitrogen, Bio‐Sciences Ltd., Ireland), 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Invitrogen, Bio‐Sciences Ltd., Ireland), 100 μg/mL kanamycin sulfate (Bio‐Sciences Ltd., Ireland), and 0.25 μg/mL amphotericin B (Sigma–Aldrich, Merck Life Science Ltd., U.K.).

Tissue digestion was performed enzymatically at a ratio of 50 mg NP tissue per mL digestion medium. Digestion was conducted in low‐glucose Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (LG DMEM) containing 2% penicillin/streptomycin, 300 U/mL collagenase type II (Gibco, Invitrogen, Dublin, Ireland), and 100 U/mL pronase (Sigma–Aldrich, U.K.). Samples were incubated for 4–6 h at 37°C with continuous rotation. Mechanical dissociation was carried out at hourly intervals using a gentleMACS tissue dissociator (Miltenyi Biotech) until complete digestion was achieved, confirmed by visual assessment and the presence of single cells when viewed under a microscope. The resulting cell suspension was filtered through a 70 μm cell strainer to remove any remaining debris. Cells were then seeded at a density of 5 × 103 cells/mm2 in T‐75 culture flasks and expanded to passage 2 in LG DMEM supplemented with 2% pen/strep and 10% FBS. Cultures were maintained at 37°C in a humidified incubator under 5% CO2 and 5% O2, with media exchanges performed every 3 days. The use of 10% FBS during the expansion phase aligns with current consensus guidelines [66], which recognize that limited serum supplementation is often necessary to achieve sufficient cell yields prior to experimental culture. Following expansion, cells were cultured in basal medium without FBS for the remainder of the study.

Microtissue Formation

To assess the effect of altering the culture microenvironment in a more relevant in vitro culture system, microtissues were generated. Under sterile conditions, 200 μL of molten agarose (2% [w/v] at ~80°C, Sigma‐Aldrich) was pipetted into wells of a 48‐well plate. A concave base formed within the culture well due to agarose contraction during cooling, promoting microtissue formation. Expanded NP cells were seeded into the wells to form microtissues with a density of 5 × 104 cells/microtissue After seeding, plates were incubated for 30 min at 37°C before centrifuging at 850 g for 5 min to condense cells at the bottom of each well. To facilitate full microtissue compaction, a baseline timepoint of 3 days post‐formation was designated as Day 0. Microtissues were primed for 7 days with TGFβ‐3 to provide a protective niche around the cells and promote anabolic cellular activity comparable to that of in vivo tissue [58, 67]. Microtissues were subsequently cultured without TGFβ‐3 in the range of microenvironmental factors (Tables 1 and 2) for an additional 14 days in a humidified atmosphere at 37°C with 5% O2, 5% CO2. In silico modeling, informed by previous work in our laboratory [57, 68], indicated that media should be refreshed every 2 days to maintain distinct microenvironmental conditions (Figure 2H). To account for gradients within the culture set up, the incubator oxygen concentration was set to 10% resulting in an approximate 6% oxygen level within the microtissue, corresponding with the mean value observed in our measurements.

Media Preparation and Stabilization

Glucose‐free DMEM was used as the basal medium across all experimental conditions. The medium was supplemented with penicillin (100 U/mL), streptomycin (100 μg/mL), and 1× ITS (Gibco, Invitrogen, Dublin, Ireland), along with amphotericin B (0.25 μg/mL), L‐proline (40 μg/mL), bovine serum albumin (1.5 mg/mL), linoleic acid (4.7 μg/mL), L‐ascorbic acid‐2‐phosphate (50 μg/mL), and dexamethasone (100 nM) (all Sigma‐Aldrich, Ireland).

The osmolarity of the base media was measured at 326.5 mOsm and was increased to 450 mOsm by the addition of 0.5 mM N‐methyl‐D‐glucamine‐hydrochloride/mOsm (Fluorochem, Ireland) to align with measured in vivo values. N‐methyl‐D‐glucamine hydrochloride is considered inert and does not interfere with cellular function [69]. Glucose levels were adjusted by supplementing the culture media with D‐glucose (Sigma‐Aldrich). Media pH was adjusted to three predefined values using 3 mM HCl (0.5 μL per 0.1 pH unit), as described previously [58] and equilibrated overnight in upright T‐75 flasks under humidified conditions at 37°C with 5% CO2 and 5% O2.

The initial cell culture experiment was designed to evaluate the combined effects of pH and glucose, as these parameters exhibited the greatest variability in our in vivo measurements. Experimental values were selected according to Pfirrmann grading, as this classification best reflects the clinical assessment of discs at the time of surgery. Three glucose concentrations derived from the human in vivo measurements were targeted corresponding to the mean GII value ± one standard deviation (0.7, 2.1, and 3.5 mM). Informed by in silico modeling, glucose concentrations within the media were increased by 0.55 mM above each target concentration to ensure the microenvironment within the microtissue matched the target value. In silico modeling predicted minimal glucose gradients across the microtissues (Figure 2H), indicating that the applied media concentration offset was sufficient to maintain targeted glucose values within the microtissues. Similarly, pH was set at three levels (6.5, 6.9, and 7.5) based on mean values observed across Pfirrmann grades. Based on in silico modeling, the culture media pH was maintained 0.1 units above the target to compensate for localized pH reduction within the microtissue. The lowest pH value (6.5) was included to represent a lower cluster of measurements observed in grade 3 discs. Clustering of donor samples at this pH has also been reported previously, supporting its inclusion [24]. In combination, these two factors resulted in nine distinct culture conditions (Table 1).

The second cell culture experiment was designed to assess whether the presence of cytokines at clinically relevant concentrations influences cell behavior. Cultures were maintained at a fixed glucose concentration of 2.1 mM, selected based on the outcome of the first experiment, and at three pH levels (6.5, 6.9, and 7.5), consistent with the previous experimental design. Groups were cultured either in the presence or absence of cytokines. Based on in vivo cytokine profiling described above, 13 cytokines were supplemented at concentrations corresponding to the mean values to define physiologically relevant culture conditions (Table 2).

Assessment of Cell Viability for In Vitro Microtissue Samples

Cell viability was assessed via a live/dead assay. Media was aspirated and microtissues were rinsed with phenol free DMEM prior to staining. Samples were then incubated for 1 h at 37°C in phenol free, low‐glucose DMEM containing 2 μM calcein (Sigma‐Aldrich, Merck Life Science Ltd., UK) and 4 μM ethidium homodimer‐1 (SLS Scientific Laboratory Supplies (Ireland) Ltd). Following incubation, the staining solution was removed and microtissues were washed three times with phenol free media. Imaging was performed using a Leica SP8 confocal scanning microscope, with excitation/emission wavelengths of 485/530 nm for calcein and 530/645 nm for ethidium homodimer‐1. Acquired z‐stacks were processed in ImageJ (version 1.53e) to generate maximum‐intensity projections, allowing qualitative assessment of cell viability throughout the microtissues.

Metabolic Consumption Rates In Vivo Microtissue Culture

Oxygen consumption rate (OCR) and lactate production rate (LPR) were assessed using a Seahorse XFe96 with 96‐well assay plates (Agilent Technologies, Ireland). One microtissue was placed in each well, with three microtissues analyzed per donor. Prior to use, Seahorse sensor cartridges were hydrated in deionized water and maintained in a non‐CO2 incubator at 37°C for a minimum of 8 h to eliminate residual CO2 that could interfere with measurements. The deionized water was replaced with XF calibrant 30 min before initiating the assay. For analysis, wells were filled with 200 μL of unbuffered Seahorse XF DMEM supplemented with 1 mM sodium pyruvate, 2 mM L‐glutamine, and glucose concentrations matched to the respective culture conditions (Agilent Technologies, Ireland). The pH of the media was adjusted accordingly to reflect experimental conditions. Media‐only control wells were included for each formulation to account for background OCR and extracellular acidification rate (ECAR).

Metabolic measurements were collected in triplicate at 20‐min intervals under basal conditions (without the addition of metabolic inhibitors or stimulators). Following the seahorse assay, microtissues were retrieved and digested using papain enzyme solution and analyzed for DNA content as described previously to enable normalization. DNA was converted to cell number using a predefined standard curve. Raw oxygen and pH data were exported from Wave software, and data analysis was conducted in Excel. Lactate concentrations were derived from pH values using a previously established linear relationship [35, 70]. All measurements were normalized by cell number per well.

Histological Staining and Evaluation

Histological staining for human tissue was conducted on the OCT embedded samples as prepared for oxygen profiling. Post slicing, tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 12 min at room temperature. Microtissues were fixed in 4% PFA overnight and dehydrated in increasing ethanol solutions and wax embedded in paraffin wax. Microtissues were sectioned at 10 μm on a microtome (Leica Microsystems, Germany). Both tissue samples and microtissues were stained for tissue structure (hematoxylin and eosin) [28], sulfated ECM components including sGAG (aldehyde fuchsin), and collagen (picrosirius red) [58, 71]. Slides were imaged at 20× magnification on a Leica Aperio AT2 slide scanner. Histological grading of donor tissue was conducted by three independent assessors following the standardized histopathology scoring system defined by Le Maitre et al. [72], which provides a consensus‐based framework for assessing the severity of human IVD degeneration based on region‐specific morphological features of the NP, annulus fibrosus, and CEP.

Vascular Endothelial Growth Factor (VEGF) Quantification

VEGF expression was assessed by immunohistochemistry conducted on OCT‐embedded NP tissue samples, prepared and fixed as described for histological staining. Cell membranes were permeabilized in 0.1% Triton X‐100 for 20 min, and nonspecific binding was blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS for 1 h (Sigma‐Aldrich, Merck Life Science Ltd., Ireland). Sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with a primary VEGF monoclonal antibody (VG1) (MA1‐16629, Invitrogen) diluted 1:100 in 1% BSA in PBS. A concentration‐matched IgG isotype control was used for all staining. Alexa Fluor 488‐conjugated goat anti‐mouse IgG secondary antibody (A12379, Invitrogen) was incubated for 1.5 h at room temperature, with Hoechst Bisbenzimide 33258 (Sigma–Aldrich, Merck Life Sciences Ltd., UK) for nuclear counterstaining. Images for each independent biological replicate were captured in triplicate using a Leica SP8 scanning confocal microscope (Leica Microsystems, Germany). Mean fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ (version 1.53e) and normalized to cell number.

In Silico Modeling of Nutrient and Metabolite Transport in Full Intervertebral Discs

In silico modeling was performed using COMSOL Multiphysics 6.2 (COMSOL Inc., Burlington, USA) to predict gradients of oxygen, glucose, and pH across the IVD. The models were based on previously reported work by McDonnell et al. [24, 73] employing the Transport of Diluted Species physics interface derived from Fick's law, which was applied to all domains and species. Idealized disc geometries [24] were used as the base model, with donor‐specific widths and heights employed for each grade. Owing to symmetry, modeling was performed on disc quadrants. Three donors were selected, one from each Pfirrmann grade (II, III, and IV). Disc height and width were measured from MRI data using ImageJ. Diffusion coefficients and active cell densities specific to each degeneration grade were applied as previously described [24, 28, 68]. Donor‐specific oxygen consumption rate (OCR) and lactate production rate (LPR), as experimentally determined in the microtissue culture system across a range of glucose and pH conditions, were applied using the values corresponding to the combination of glucose and pH that most closely matched those measured in the tissue samples. All parameters employed for each degeneration grade are outlined in Table S6.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism (version 10). Human NP profiling measurements were analyzed using a one‐way ANOVA with Tukey's post hoc test. In vitro microtissue culture results were analyzed via two‐way ANOVA with Šídák's multiple comparisons test. p values below 0.05 were considered statistically significant with data presented as mean ± standard deviation in both cases. Correlation analysis was performed using Pearson's correlation coefficient to assess relationships between microenvironmental parameters, cytokine concentrations, ECM components, and donor‐specific variables. The strength of correlations was interpreted based on established effect size conventions [74], with |r| ≥ 0.30 considered moderate, |r| ≥ 0.50 considered strong, and values between −0.30 and +0.30 considered weak or negligible.

Correlations Between Microenvironmental Parameters, Cytokine Presence, and ECM Components

Pearson correlation analysis revealed associations between microenvironmental parameters, cytokine expression, as well as ECM components (Figure 6). Correlation coefficients were categorized as positive (r ≥ 0.30), negligible (−0.30 < r < +0.30), or negative (r ≤ −0.30). Complete correlation coefficients for all variable pairs are provided in Table S5. Correlations were observed among microenvironmental parameters. Oxygen concentration showed strong positive correlations with osmolarity (r = 0.91) and pH (r = 0.73). pH and osmolarity were also positively correlated to one another (r = 0.76). In contrast, lactate demonstrated a moderate negative correlation with osmolarity (r = −0.39) and a negligible association with pH (r = −0.18). Glucose showed weak correlations with oxygen (r = 0.17) and pH (r = 0.29), indicating independence between these factors.

Correlations of microenvironmental parameters, cytokine presence, and ECM components. Heat map highlighting positive (r ≥ 0.30), negligible (−0.30 < r < +0.30), or negative (r ≤ −0.30) correlations for all factors.

Several microenvironmental factors were also associated with inflammatory cytokine expression. Lactate correlated positively with IL‐1β (r = 0.59) and MMP‐3 (r = 0.72) while also demonstrating strong positive correlations with matrix remodeling inhibitors TIMP‐1 (r = 0.54), TIMP‐2 (r = 0.72), and TIMP‐3 (r = 0.62). While the majority of correlations across glucose concentrations were negligible, glucose demonstrated moderate negative correlations with MMP‐3 (r = −0.56) and TIMP‐3 (r = −0.48). Other microenvironmental parameters showed more limited associations with cytokines. Oxygen and pH exhibited negligible correlations with most inflammatory markers (−0.25 < r < +0.25), suggesting that oxygen availability and pH alone did not strongly track with cytokine expression. Matrix‐degrading enzymes exhibited strong intercorrelations, with MMP‐3 showing significant associations with ADAMTS4 (r = 0.734) and ADAMTS5 (r = 0.490). Anti‐inflammatory cytokines similarly demonstrated correlated expression, with TIMP‐3 correlating strongly with both TIMP‐1 (r = 0.892) and TIMP‐2 (r = 0.628).

ECM components demonstrated stronger associations with cytokines than with most microenvironmental parameters. For example, GAG content correlated positively with IL‐1β (r = 0.574) and MMP‐3 (r = 0.475), while collagen content showed a strong positive correlation with IL‐1β (r = 0.829). DNA content demonstrated negligible correlations with microenvironmental parameters, cytokines, and ECM components (−0.30 < r < +0.30 in all cases), including oxygen (r = 0.071), glucose (r = 0.202), GAG (r = 0.438), and collagen (r = −0.290). This indicates that variation in matrix composition and inflammatory signaling occurred largely independently of changes in cell number.

Degeneration grade, age, and sex were also included in the correlation analysis. Pfirrmann grade demonstrated negligible correlations with oxygen (r = 0.01), glucose (r = 0.21), pH (r = −0.23), lactate (r = 0.03), and osmolarity (r = 0.09), supporting the absence of a strong association between MRI‐based degeneration grade and measured microenvironmental parameters. Pfirrmann grade showed a moderate positive correlation with TNF (r = 0.52), while correlations with ECM components were weak to moderate, including DNA (r = 0.34), GAG (r = 0.32), and collagen (r = −0.16). Histological grade showed stronger associations with some parameters, including negative correlations with pH (r = −0.49), β‐NGF (r = −0.44), collagen (r = −0.44), and sex (r = −0.56), and positive correlations with glucose (r = 0.46), TIMP‐1 (r = 0.46), IL‐10 (r = 0.38), VEGF (r = 0.36), and GAG (r = 0.35). Age showed moderate negative correlations with osmolarity (r = −0.54), oxygen (r = −0.40), IL‐10 (r = −0.33), and ADAMTS5 (r = −0.32), and moderate positive correlations with lactate (r = 0.49), TIMP‐1 (r = 0.36), TIMP‐2 (r = 0.32), and TIMP‐3 (r = 0.37). Sex was moderately associated with oxygen (r = −0.52), collagen (r = 0.41), age (r = 0.49), and histological grade (r = −0.56). Overall, these findings suggest that degeneration grade, age, and sex may contribute to donor variability.

These findings indicate that while some parameters exhibit coordinated relationships across samples, others vary more independently, suggesting that the observed variability in profiled factors reflects a combination of shared and parameter‐specific influences, as well as donor‐specific variation.

Glucose Concentration and pH Levels Do Not Significantly Alter Cell Viability, Matrix Composition, or Metabolism Rates

No significant differences were observed in any measured parameter in response to variations in glucose concentration or pH levels within a clinically relevant range, although considerable variability was observed among biological replicates across all conditions. Live/dead imaging demonstrated high viability across all experimental conditions (Figure 7A) with no significant differences observed as a result of altered pH or glucose levels. Mean viability was quantified at 57.85% ± 3.69% (Figure 7B). Consistent with these findings, DNA content per microtissue did not differ significantly across glucose concentrations or pH conditions. The mean DNA content across all groups was 3549 ± 343.1 ng DNA/microtissue (Figure 7C). GAG content normalized to DNA was maintained across conditions, with no significant effects of glucose concentration observed at any pH level. The average GAG/DNA across all groups was 0.79 ± 0.1 (Figure 7D). Similarly, collagen content normalized to DNA remained comparable across all glucose and pH conditions with a mean value of 14.8 ± 1.2 (Figure 7E). These findings were confirmed by histological staining. Hematoxylin and eosin staining revealed intact tissue morphology across all conditions (Figure 7F). Alcian blue staining demonstrated consistent sulfated GAG distribution throughout the microtissues (Figure 7G) while picrosirius red staining showed the presence of collagen across all groups (Figure 7H). Histological staining revealed lighter matrix‐associated staining toward the microtissue core, particularly in samples cultured at pH 6.5, while staining patterns were broadly comparable across other glucose and pH conditions. Oxygen consumption rate (OCR) was maintained across glucose concentrations at all pH levels, with no significant differences detected between groups. The average OCR across all groups was 23.6 ± 3.6 nmol/million cells/h (Figure 7I). Similarly, lactate production rate (LPR) remained stable across all glucose and pH conditions with a mean value of 320.8 ± 34.7 nmol/million cells/h (Figure 7J).

Donor‐Specific Scaling of Idealized In Silico Disc Models Reflects Physiologically Relevant Nutrient and pH Microenvironments

Idealized three‐dimensional models were generated based on donor specific widths and heights from each Pfirrmann grade. A common idealized disc geometry was used, with overall width and height scaled to match each donor. This resulted in disc widths of 36.0, 38.5, and 39.3 mm and disc heights of 9.3, 8.9, and 7.6 mm for Grade II, III, and IV discs, respectively (Figure 9A). Donor specific microenvironmental measurements and metabolic rates are outlined for each degeneration grade (Figure 9B). Microenvironmental profiles revealed pronounced gradients in oxygen, glucose, and pH across the anterior–posterior width of the disc (Figure 9C–E). Oxygen concentrations decreased from the disc periphery toward the center in all models, reaching minimum values between 0.87% and 1.56%. The experimentally measured oxygen concentrations for Grades II, III, and IV were 5.5%, 4.6%, and 7.4%, respectively and were generally consistent with the simulated spatial ranges. The profiled Grade IV value (7.4%) exceeded the boundary condition applied in the model, which was derived from literature‐reported values. Across donors, measured oxygen values corresponded to positions closer to the disc periphery, consistent with the anatomical location of tissue excision. Glucose exhibited a similar spatial gradient, decreasing from the outer disc (maximum 4.75 mM) to a central minimum of 0.97 mM. The experimentally measured glucose concentrations for grades II, III and IV were 2.8, 5.3, and 4.4 mM respectively. These values were within the simulated ranges, except for the Grade III concentration (5.3 mM), which exceeded the specified boundary condition. Similar to the oxygen results, the measured values corresponded to the peripheral regions of the simulated profiles. pH also decreased toward the disc center, reaching a minimum of 6.9. The profiled pH values of 7.5 (Grade II) and 7.1 (Grade IV) were within the predicted spatial range, located near the peripheral region of the simulated profiles, whereas the Grade III pH (6.7) was notably lower and mapped closer to the lower end of the predicted pH distribution. This accumulation of lactate coupled with the observation of higher glucose could indicate reduced diffusion in this donor compared to the rates employed from the literature. Despite differences in diffusion parameters, disc geometry, active cell density, and degeneration grade between donor‐specific models, the overall shape and magnitude of the oxygen, glucose, and pH gradients were comparable across all three simulations. The experimentally measured donor‐specific values were largely within the predicted spatial ranges and corresponded to the peripheral disc regions from which the tissue samples had been excised, thereby supporting the validity of the in silico modeling framework.

Discussion

The microenvironment of the IVD is increasingly recognized as a defining feature of disc health and degeneration, yet its key microenvironmental parameters, and their effects on disc cell matrix synthesis and metabolism, remain poorly characterized across degeneration severity. Therefore, this study aimed to characterize the human NP microenvironment across various grades of degeneration ranging from mild to moderate–severe. Subsequently, in vitro microtissue cultures were used to investigate whether clinically relevant pH, glucose, and cytokine levels within the culture media influence microtissue viability, matrix synthesis rates, and metabolic activity. Defining the range of microenvironments present during IVD degeneration in vivo coupled with analysis of the impact of these factors on in vitro culture provides important insight into the relevance of employing clinically relevant conditions when developing regenerative therapies. When stratified by either Pfirrmann or histological grade, microenvironmental parameters and cytokine concentrations were not significantly altered across degeneration grades. Correlation analysis incorporating Pfirrmann grade, histological grade, age, and sex further supported this finding, with predominantly weak or negligible correlations observed between degeneration severity and measured microenvironmental parameters. While some moderate correlations were identified for individual variables, these were not consistent across factors. Notably, histological grade demonstrated stronger associations with selected parameters compared to Pfirrmann grade, suggesting that MRI‐based grading may not fully capture biochemical variation within the disc. Age and sex were also associated with several parameters, indicating that donor‐specific factors contribute to variability independent of degeneration grade. ECM composition was similarly preserved across all groups. Consistent with these findings, NP microtissues demonstrated resilience to variations in glucose, pH and cytokine levels within clinically observed ranges. This contrasts with many previous in vitro studies reporting changes in NP cell behavior under altered glucose, pH, or cytokine presence. These studies commonly employ monolayer culture systems or expose cells directly to microenvironmental changes without prior priming, thereby lacking the protective niche that more closely reflects the in vivo situation. This protective effect of the native matrix has also been previously demonstrated comparing tissue explants with alginate bead cultures [76]. Differences in the chosen cell culture model as well as the absence of priming in many studies may contribute to the heightened sensitivity to microenvironmental changes previously reported [57, 77]. While the microenvironment of the IVD may both influence and respond to disc degeneration, the findings of this study indicate that differences in microenvironmental parameters are not directly associated with degeneration severity, highlighting the multifactorial nature of disc degeneration.

Microenvironmental profiling revealed that oxygen and lactate concentrations, as well as osmolarity in human NP tissue fall within ranges previously reported in the literature and predicted by in silico models [23, 24, 78]. No significant changes as a function of degeneration grade were observed in these parameters; however, high variability was noted between donors. Mean oxygen concentrations profiled in this study align with values previously reported by Bartels et al. [23], who demonstrated oxygen availability ranged between 2% and 10% in the human NP. This range of values is consistent with the avascular, hypoxic nature of the NP [26, 78] and falls within ranges commonly employed in NP cell culture studies [63, 71, 79, 80, 81]. Consistent with the tissue measurements, OCR remained stable across all microtissue culture conditions indicating that the reliance of human NP cells on oxygen remains consistent within the range of glucose, pH, and cytokine presence observed in human NP tissue.

Similarly, lactate concentrations measured in this study aligned with prior experimental measurements and in silico modeling that predicted NP lactate concentrations of 2–15 mM depending on disc geometry, diffusion distance, and cellular metabolic rates [23, 24, 30, 31, 37]. Despite the established link between degeneration, CEP permeability, and lactate accumulation [24, 31, 82], lactate concentrations in this study did not increase significantly with degeneration grade. It should be noted that tissue samples were obtained during discectomy procedures and therefore are most representative of the peripheral regions of the nucleus pulposus. Given the established spatial gradients within the disc, with higher lactate accumulation toward the disc center, the concentrations reported here may underestimate those present in the core region. LPR in NP microtissues remained stable across physiologically relevant glucose and pH conditions and in the presence or absence of cytokines. This indicates that cellular LPRs are maintained within the clinically relevant range of glucose, pH and cytokine microenvironments.

Osmolarity measurements in this study were consistent with previously reported values for human NP tissue which are reported to fall within the range of 350–500 mOsm [39]. No significant changes were observed as a result of degeneration grade. The large donor variability in this parameter has previously been linked to tissue hydration [39, 83]. The range observed in this study is comparable to cell culture studies where baseline media is approximately 330–360 mOsm dependent on supplier with additional supplements increasing osmolarity [84]. These findings indicate that oxygen, lactate, and osmolarity within the NP microenvironment vary between individuals but are maintained across degeneration grades. Furthermore, within a clinically relevant range they do not significantly impact NP matrix synthesis or metabolic rates. This supports the broad applicability of regenerative cell‐based therapies, as efficacy is unlikely to depend strongly on patient‐specific differences in these parameters.

To the best of our knowledge, this study provides the first experimental quantification of glucose concentrations in human NP tissue across a large donor cohort, enabling robust comparison with values previously predicted through in silico models. To date, only one prior study has reported direct glucose measurements in human NP tissue, with Yin et al. [36] measuring glucose concentrations of approximately 0.6 mM in degenerated samples from three donors, all over the age of 55. Historically, glucose availability in the human IVD has been estimated primarily through in silico models, which predict low glucose concentrations in the range of approximately 1–5 mM depending on disc height, CEP permeability, and cellular metabolic rates [24, 29, 38, 78, 85]. The experimentally measured glucose concentrations reported in this study fall within the ranges predicted by these models, providing important experimental validation [24, 29, 86]. Experimentally measured pH values also aligned closely with ranges previously reported for human IVDs and predicted by in silico models, with studies by Bartels et al. [23, 78] and Urban and Roberts reporting pH values of 6.5–7.2.

Notably, neither glucose nor pH changed significantly as a result of degeneration, despite large donor variability. Microtissue culture within these measured ranges maintained cell viability, matrix composition, and metabolic activity. Lighter histological staining was observed at the microtissue core, particularly at pH 6.5, consistent with the known sensitivity of NP cells to acidic conditions [31, 35, 41, 58]. Notably, this reduced staining was less pronounced than in comparable culture groups exposed to cytokines at corresponding pH levels. The lack of differences in cellular matrix synthesis and metabolic rates indicates that although glucose and pH levels differ between individuals, NP cell behavior remains stable across this physiologically relevant range. This is encouraging for development of regenerative therapies and clinical translation. It suggests that cell therapies may not need to be tailored to patient‐specific glucose or pH conditions, particularly given the practical difficulty of quantifying these parameters in a clinical setting. However, many previous in vitro studies have employed high glucose concentrations (25 mM) which are significantly higher than the range profiled in this study [56, 71, 87]. These studies reported altered NP cell behavior, including increased lactate production and metabolic activity, changes in matrix synthesis, and upregulation of inflammatory or catabolic markers [36, 56, 71]. In contrast, culturing within the physiologically relevant glucose range showed no significant changes in matrix synthesis or metabolic activity. This suggests that responses observed under high‐glucose culture conditions may not accurately reflect NP cell behavior in vivo, highlighting the importance of using clinically relevant microenvironmental conditions when developing regenerative therapies.

Support for the experimental findings is further provided by the donor specific in silico disc models, which predicted highly comparable oxygen, glucose, and pH gradients across Pfirrmann Grades II–IV despite the application of degeneration‐specific diffusion coefficients and cell densities. Given the metabolic and transport changes commonly associated with advanced degeneration, more pronounced depletion in Grade IV discs might be expected. However, the similarity of predicted profiles highlights the dominant role of disc width and height, rather than degeneration grade alone, in determining nutrient availability within the disc. This finding correlates with the lack of significant degeneration dependent differences observed experimentally and demonstrates that the measured microenvironmental parameters reflect physiologically relevant conditions within the human disc. Importantly, the experimentally measured oxygen, glucose, and pH values from human tissue were within the ranges predicted by the models and were located closer to the disc periphery, consistent with the anatomical location from which the tissue samples were obtained. In contrast, the predicted central regions exhibited lower nutrient availability and pH than initially anticipated. This is likely attributable to the relatively high OCR and LPRs applied in each model, which were experimentally derived under comparable culture conditions and exceed values used in some previously published models [28, 88]. While this may provide a more realistic representation of cellular metabolic demand, it is also possible that the model does not fully capture additional transport mechanisms or buffering effects present in vivo, leading to an overestimation of central depletion. Notably, the in silico models represent intact IVDs, whereas the experimental measurements were obtained from tissue collected during discectomy procedures. These samples included displaced or herniated NP, which may not reflect their original anatomical position within the disc. As such, the modeling was not intended to directly reproduce experimentally measured values, but rather to provide a framework to visualize potential spatial gradients within an intact disc geometry. Discrepancies between predicted and measured values may therefore arise from differences in tissue location, altered transport pathways, or disruption of the native disc structure. Together, these observations suggest that while the minimum values predicted centrally should be interpreted with caution, the strong agreement between predicted ranges and experimental measurements near the disc periphery reinforces the physiological relevance of both the experimental data and in silico modeling.

Profiling of cytokine presence within the human NP tissue revealed relatively low expression of both inflammatory and anti‐inflammatory cytokines with high variability between donors but no significant differences as a result of degeneration grade. Two of the most commonly used cytokines in in vitro studies, IL‐1β and TNF, were predominantly detected in the pg/mL range, consistent with prior reports in human NP tissue [46, 50, 89]. These concentrations were orders of magnitude lower than those of previous NP cell culture studies where IL‐1β is frequently used at 1–10 ng/mL and TNF at 5–50 ng/mL [47, 90, 91]. At these concentrations, changes in matrix synthesis, metabolic activity, and expression of catabolic markers have been reported in NP cells, which contrasts with the observations from the microtissue culture conducted in this study. It should also be considered that cytokine availability and stability within the 3D microtissue culture system may influence cellular responses. Previous studies have demonstrated that cells cultured in agarose and other hydrogel‐based systems remain responsive to cytokine stimulation, indicating that diffusion of soluble factors is not fully restricted within these environments [92, 93, 94]. However, hydrogel‐based culture systems may modulate the effective availability of cytokines through factors such as matrix binding, degradation, or altered transport dynamics within the ECM [94, 95]. While other cytokines were also quantified in this study, direct experimental comparisons between their native tissue concentrations and those commonly used in vitro remains limited. Quantitative measurements in human IVD tissue have been reported for IL‐1β [44, 45, 96], TNF [44, 45, 97], and IL‐6 [45, 96], most commonly obtained using immunoassays such as ELISA. IL‐10 [44, 45, 98], ADAMTS4 [99], ADAMTS5 [99], MMP‐3 [100], TIMP‐1 [101], TIMP‐2 [102], TIMP‐3 [99], β‐NGF [103], and BDNF [104] have also been quantified in IVD tissue but to a lesser extent. These studies are often stratified by degeneration or pain‐related pathology with many reporting values derived from small cohorts, explant‐conditioned media, or semi‐quantitative analyses. This study aligns with previously reported values while providing quantitative measurements across a broad panel of cytokines enabling comparison between factors and establishment of correlations.

Finally, histological analysis of microtissue cultures provided qualitative insight into spatial features of cytokine‐cultured microtissues. Although matrix synthesis rates were consistent across conditions, reduced staining intensity toward the microtissue core was observed across all culture groups. This effect was evident in both cytokine‐treated and control microtissues (without cytokines), the latter being otherwise comparable to glucose and pH cultures at 2.1 mM glucose. However, this effect of reduced staining intensity within the core was less pronounced under glucose and pH culture conditions. Biochemical analysis from both experiments revealed no statistically significant differences in DNA, GAG, and collagen content, although lower mean collagen values were observed in the cytokine experiment. Microtissues in this group developed a more pronounced peripheral matrix layer, which may have influenced transport to the microtissue core and contributed to the observed differences in collagen content as previously observed [105, 106].

Microtissue dimensions and culture conditions were informed by in silico modeling to minimize the development of such gradients. However, the less intense core staining observed in the cytokine experiment suggests that donor‐dependent factors or cellular priming prior to microtissue formation may influence transport properties or early tissue organization in ways not fully predicted by the in silico model. The donor‐dependent nature of this effect suggests that such histological features are not uniformly expressed. Importantly, the preservation of quantifiable matrix synthesis and metabolic activity demonstrates that these spatial differences do not translate into measurable changes in overall tissue function within the range of conditions tested. Therefore, this study forms an important reference for defining clinically relevant cytokine concentrations for in vitro NP cell culture models. Given the relatively low expression of cytokines and the maintenance of matrix synthesis and metabolic activity regardless of their presence, the supraphysiological cytokine levels commonly used in vitro may not accurately reflect the microenvironment of the IVD. Therefore, regenerative therapies validated under these levels may overestimate the impact of cytokine presence in vivo and may not accurately predict treatment efficacy.

Profiling of ECM composition demonstrated that DNA, GAG, and collagen content, as well as the GAG:collagen ratio were maintained across degeneration grades, regardless of stratification by Pfirrmann or histological grading. While loss of ECM components with progressing degeneration is widely reported [78, 107], prior work has also highlighted substantial donor variability [108, 109], consistent with the findings in this study. ECM content measured in this study was consistent across degenerative states and falls within the expected range from the literature for DNA, GAG, and collagen despite large variation observed between donors [107, 109, 110]. This was reflected in the NP microtissue culture model, where matrix synthesis was maintained across a physiologically relevant range of glucose, pH, and cytokine presence. These observations suggest that the matrix production rate of NP cells is maintained within the clinically relevant range of microenvironmental conditions.

Although microenvironmental parameters did not change significantly with degeneration grade, large variability was observed between donors. Correlation analysis provided additional insight by identifying relationships between microenvironmental parameters, cytokine concentrations, and ECM components that were not apparent from stratification by degeneration grade. Correlation analysis has previously been used in computational and experimental studies to describe relationships between oxygen, glucose, lactate, and pH in the IVD; however, these analyses have largely relied on in silico modeling or limited experimental datasets [24, 29, 30, 38, 70]. In this study, the observed correlations are consistent with predictions from in silico modeling. Correlations between lactate, IL‐1β, MMP‐3, and TIMPs suggest that lactate levels are linked to increased metabolic activity and matrix turnover, rather than pH alone. Notably, pro‐inflammatory and their anti‐inflammatory cytokines were also strongly correlated with one another, indicating coordinated regulation of these pathways within the NP, consistent with previous findings [49, 89, 99]. ECM components showed stronger correlations with cytokine presence than with most microenvironmental parameters, while DNA content showed little association with any factor. This indicates that differences in matrix composition are not directly linked to cell number but rather to changes in cellular behavior [107, 108]. At first glance, positive correlations between ECM components and inflammatory or matrix‐remodeling cytokines such as IL‐1β and MMP‐3 may appear counterintuitive given their established catabolic roles in vitro [47, 111]. However, in native human NP tissue, these factors are often co‐expressed at low levels alongside their inhibitors and may reflect ongoing matrix turnover rather than net matrix loss [78, 89, 99, 107]. The positive correlations observed between MMPs and TIMPs in this study support the presence of balanced remodeling activity [112] rather than unopposed ECM degradation, consistent with the maintenance of matrix synthesis observed in the microtissue culture experiments. The observed variation in the individual measurements further suggests that the NP microenvironment is not regulated as a single uniform system. While coordinated relationships between certain parameters, particularly among microenvironmental factors and subsets of cytokines, indicate elements of integrated regulation, the presence of more independent patterns of variation highlights the influence of heterogeneous, donor‐specific factors. Together, these findings suggest that the NP microenvironment reflects a combination of coupled biological processes and individual variability, highlighting relationships within the NP microenvironment that are maintained across degeneration grades.

Several limitations should be considered when evaluating these findings. Although this study profiled a large number of human donors spanning a range of degeneration grades, ages, and sexes, further increases in sample size would better inform donor variability and degenerative changes particularly for mildly and more severely degenerated groups. The availability of donors within these groups was limited by sample collection, as samples were obtained from patients undergoing discectomy procedures, where moderately degenerated tissue is most common. In addition, all samples were from herniated tissue and are likely to represent gradients in the peripheral NP which may not fully represent the intact disc microenvironment. Herniations may be subject to disruption of normal diffusion pathways and exposure to surrounding tissues could alter local microenvironmental conditions. The chronicity of the clinical condition was not evaluated in this study due to limited availability of consistent clinical data. This may influence the local microenvironment and should be considered in future investigations. It should also be considered that DNA content in discectomy‐derived samples may be influenced by the presence of infiltrating cell populations associated with inflammation or chronic degeneration. While histological assessment was performed, the fragmented nature of the samples limited the ability to definitively distinguish resident NP cells from infiltrating cells. As such, DNA measurements should be interpreted as total cellular content rather than a direct measure of NP cell number. Furthermore, it was not possible to quantify all parameters in every sample due to tissue availability and assay restrictions, resulting in reduced data points for some factors. This limitation was most pronounced in cytokine profiling, where several donors fell near or below the detection limits of the assay. Limitations in sensitivity were observed in oxygen profiling which was conducted using OCT‐embedded tissue sections to maintain consistency across donors. While this approach reduced measurement variability, it was shown to be less sensitive when compared to live‐tissue oxygen imaging, as indicated by lower corrected total cell fluorescence (CTCF) values. As a result, subtle differences in oxygen gradients may not have been detected. Future studies incorporating live‐tissue oxygen imaging immediately after collection would allow more sensitive and physiologically representative characterization of microenvironmental oxygen concentrations. In addition, the in vitro microtissue cultures were maintained under static conditions and did not incorporate biomechanical loading. Mechanical stimuli are known to play a critical role in regulating NP cell metabolism, matrix synthesis, and overall disc homeostasis. While the present study was designed to isolate the effects of microenvironmental factors, it is likely that interactions between biochemical and biomechanical cues influence cellular behavior in vivo. Therefore, the absence of mechanical loading should be considered when interpreting the translatability of these findings to the native IVD microenvironment. While T2‐weighted MRI data were available for all donors, quantitative T2 mapping was not performed and therefore could not be incorporated into the present analysis. The inclusion of quantitative MRI metrics in future studies may provide a more continuous assessment of degeneration severity and enable more refined evaluation of relationships between disc degeneration and microenvironmental parameters. Finally, NP cells isolated from degenerated human tissue may have adapted to harsh microenvironmental conditions, in contrast to cells derived from young or healthy animal models. Human NP cells have previously been shown to have phenotypic and metabolic adaptations to the degenerative microenvironment which may reduce sensitivity to short‐term variations in glucose, pH, and cytokine exposure in vitro, contributing to the limited impact of these factors observed in the microtissue cultures [76]. This is supported by previous studies that showed that NP cells maintained consistent hypoxia signaling under both normoxic and hypoxic conditions [113, 114, 115]. This adaptation may contribute to the limited impact of microenvironmental changes observed in the microtissue cultures in this study. This should be considered when translating these findings to preclinical animal models, where animal cells from non‐degenerated discs have been shown to be impacted by similar microenvironmental changes [58, 68]. Despite these limitations, the use of quantitative microenvironmental profiling conducted in this study across a range of varying donors provides a robust and clinically relevant profile for the development of cell based regenerative therapies.

In summary, this study characterized the microenvironment of the human NP across varying grades of degeneration, ages, and sexes. It establishes methods for quantifying a broad range of microenvironmental factors in human NP tissue while analyzing the impacts of the range of quantified factors on the matrix synthesis and metabolism of NP cells in vitro. This work bridges an important gap between in silico modeling, experimental measurements, and in vitro culture. Collectively, the findings indicate that NP cell behavior is largely maintained across clinically relevant microenvironmental conditions, supporting the broad applicability of regenerative cell‐based therapies, as efficacy is unlikely to depend strongly on patient‐specific differences in these parameters.

Conclusion

This study provides a comprehensive, experimentally derived characterization of the human NP microenvironment and evaluates its influence on cellular matrix synthesis and metabolism under physiologically relevant conditions. Across a broad donor cohort, key microenvironmental parameters, cytokine levels, and ECM composition were maintained across degeneration grades, despite substantial donor‐to‐donor variability. Consistent with these in vivo observations, human NP microtissues maintained viability, matrix production, and metabolic activity across physiologically relevant ranges of glucose, pH, and cytokine exposure. Together, these results indicate that clinically observed microenvironmental variation does not necessarily translate into altered cellular matrix synthesis or metabolic rates, highlighting the resilience of NP cells to microenvironmental change. Collectively, this study establishes experimentally defined microenvironmental ranges suitable for both in vitro culture and in silico modeling. It further demonstrates that human NP cells within a native matrix exhibit limited sensitivity to physiologically relevant microenvironmental variation, with important implications for cell‐based regenerative strategies.
