A collocation scheme that is equivalent to discontinuous Galerkin discretizations
Jason E. Hicken

Abstract
A spectral collocation operator with the summation-by-parts property was introduced by Chan to develop entropy-stable discontinuous Galerkin (DG) semi-discretizations ( DOI:j.jcp.2018.02.033 ). The present work shows that semi-discretizations based on this collocation operator produce solutions that are equivalent to solutions of a DG semi-discretization using the same underlying quadrature. The equivalence holds regardless of the number of degrees of freedom in the collocation scheme and when the quadrature is not strictly positive. Extraneous degrees of freedom in the collocation scheme are associated with the nullspace of the operator and remain zero throughout an unsteady simulation. If necessary, nullspace consistency can be recovered by introducing projection-based numerical dissipation that targets only the extraneous modes. The equivalence between collocation and DG solutions is verified for the constant-coefficient advection equation and Burgers’ equation on triangular meshes. The numerical results show that equivalence breaks down for entropy-stable semi-discretizations of Burgers’ equation based on a skew-symmetric splitting, but that equivalence can be recovered by projecting the collocation scheme’s residual onto the relevant polynomial space. In addition to investigating equivalence, the results demonstrate that the collocation operator produces semi-discretizations with favorable spectral radii compared with a commonly used summation-by-parts operator construction.

Introduction

By mimicking integration-by-parts at the discrete level, summation-by-parts (SBP) operators
Kreiss and Scherer 1974
provide a versatile foundation for constructing stable, high-order discretizations. This foundation has been exploited for many years, but interest in SBP operators grew significantly after 2013, when Fisher and Carpenter
Fisher and Carpenter 2013
demonstrated that SBP operators could be used to construct high-order, entropy-stable schemes on bounded domains.

SBP operators were originally developed as finite-difference discretizations
Kreiss and Scherer 1974
, but they have since been generalized to unstructured, element-based grids. This includes both tensor-product operators
Gassner 2013
;
Carpenter et al. 2014
and multidimensional SBP operators
Hicken et al. 2016
. Enabling SBP discretizations for unstructured grids has been a key development in allowing the framework to tackle more complex problems.

Multidimensional SBP operators can be difficult to use, and the goal that originally motivated this work was to identify a straightforward SBP operator with a closed-form expression. The search for such an operator brought me to the projection-based SBP operator introduced by Chan
Chan 2018
— see also
Chan 2019
. I will refer to Chan’s operator as a modal collocation (MC) operator for reasons that will become clear shortly. Chan did not use the MC operator in a collocation scheme, but as an intermediate step in a finite-element discretization. To the best of my knowledge, only Montoya and Zingg
Montoya and Zingg 2024
have used the MC operator in a collocation setting to compare their tensor-product simplex operators with multidimensional SBP operators.

The contribution of this work is to highlight a remarkable property of modal collocation: it produces a solution that is identical to a discontinuous Galerkin (DG) discretization based on the same quadrature. Thus, regardless of the number of quadrature points, a modal-collocation discretization is effectively a modal DG method. This is despite the fact — shown here — that MC operators are nullspace inconsistent
Svärd and Nordström 2019
, which is typically detrimental for finite difference operators; see, for example,
Glaubitz et al. 2026
.

Here is a brief outline of the remaining paper. In Section
2
, I present the MC operator definition and review some results from
Chan 2018
. The main theoretical contributions related to MC-DG equivalence are presented in Section
3
. I verify the theory in Section
4
by demonstrating the equivalence between a few MC and DG discretizations. Finally, I provide a summary and discussion of the paper’s results in Section
5

Definition and review of modal-collocation discretizations

2.1
Notation and preliminaries

Vectors are denoted with bold font, such as the spatial coordinates
\bm{x}=[x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}]^{T}
. Matrices use sans-serif font, e.g.,
\mathsf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times M}
is an
N\times M
matrix. Element domains will be represented as
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{D}
, where
D\geq 1
is the space dimension, and the notation
\Gamma=\partial\Omega
indicates the boundary of
\Omega
. I will use calligraphic type in order to distinguish functions from point values. For example,
\mathcal{U}(x,t)\in L^{2}(\Omega)
is often used to represent the solution to a partial-differential equation, whereas
\bm{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
is used to represent its discrete or semi-discrete counterpart.

I will use polynomials to specify the accuracy of various operators, so some notation is needed to describe these functions. Let
\mathbb{P}^{P}(\Omega)
denote the space of polynomials of total degree
P
on the domain
\Omega
. The dimension of this space is denoted
N_{P}=\binom{P+D}{D}
. The ordered set
\{\mathcal{V}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{P}}
represents an orthonormal basis for
\mathbb{P}^{P}(\Omega)
; that is, the basis functions are orthonormal with respect to the
L^{2}
inner product on
\Omega
:

\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{V}_{i}\mathcal{V}_{j}\,d\Omega=\delta_{ij},\qquad\forall\,i,j=1,2,\ldots,N_{P},

(1)

where
\delta_{ij}
is the Kronecker delta.

I will also need some notation for quadrature rules, since numerical integration plays a key role in the definition and analysis of MC operators. The nodes and weights for an
N
-point quadrature rule over the domain
\Omega
will be represented as
X_{\Omega}=\{\bm{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
and
W_{\Omega}=\{w_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
, respectively. Similarly, I will use
X_{\Gamma}=\{\hat{\bm{x}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{M}
and
W_{\Gamma}=\{\hat{w}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{M}
to denote the nodes and weights, respectively, for an
M
-point quadrature rule over
\Gamma
. Unless stated otherwise, I assume that the quadrature rules are positive-interior rules; for example, this implies that
X_{\Omega}\subset\Omega
, and
w>0
for all
w\in W_{\Omega}
.

Some matrices will arise frequently and warrant being defined here. The matrix
\mathsf{I}_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
denotes the
N\times N
identity matrix, and the matrix
\mathsf{0}_{N\times M}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times M}
is the
N\times M
matrix of zeros. The matrix
\mathsf{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N_{P}}
is reserved to represent the Vandermonde matrix consisting of the orthonormal basis
\{\mathcal{V}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{P}}
evaluated at the nodes
X_{\Omega}
. Similarly, the matrix
\mathsf{V}_{x_{d}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N_{P}}
contains the derivatives of the orthonormal basis, with respect to
x_{d}
, evaluated at the nodes
X_{\Omega}
. To be precise, the entries in these matrices are

[\mathsf{V}]_{ij}=\mathcal{V}_{j}(\bm{x}_{i}),\qquad\text{and}\qquad[\mathsf{V}_{x_{d}}]_{ij}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{V}_{j}}{\partial x_{d}}(\bm{x}_{i}),

(2)

where
i=1,2,\ldots,N
, and
j=1,2,\ldots,N_{P}
.

Finally, I review the definition of diagonal-norm, first-derivative multidimensional SBP operators
Hicken et al. 2016
, since this definition will be referenced several times in the paper.

Definition 1

(Diagonal-norm, first-derivative summation-by-parts operator)
.

The matrix
D_{x_{d}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
is a degree
P
, diagonal-norm, summation-by-parts operator approximating the derivative operator
\partial/\partial x_{d}
,
d=1,2,\ldots,D
, at the nodes
X_{\Omega}=\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
if it satisfies the following conditions.

Derivative Accuracy:

The matrix differentiates polynomials in
\mathbb{P}^{P}(\Omega)
exactly. Thus,

\mathsf{D}_{x_{d}}\mathsf{V}=\mathsf{V}_{x_{d}}

(3)

where
\mathsf{V}
and
\mathsf{V}_{x_{d}}
are the matrices defined in (
2
).

Factorization:

The matrix can be factored as
\mathsf{D}_{x_{d}}=\mathsf{W}^{-1}\mathsf{Q}_{x_{d}}
, where
\mathsf{W}
is a diagonal, positive-definite matrix.

Boundary Accuracy:

The symmetric matrix
\mathsf{E}_{x_{d}}=\mathsf{Q}_{x_{d}}+\mathsf{Q}_{x_{d}}^{T}
satisfies

\big[\mathsf{V}^{T}\mathsf{E}_{x_{d}}\mathsf{V}\big]_{ij}=\int_{\Gamma}\mathcal{V}_{i}\mathcal{V}_{j}\,n_{x_{d}}\,d\Gamma,

(4)

where
n_{x_{d}}
is the
x_{d}
component of the outward unit normal vector on
\Gamma
.

While the SBP definition does not mention quadrature, one can show that the nodes
X_{\Omega}
and the corresponding diagonal entries in
\mathsf{W}
constitute a degree
2P-1
exact quadrature rule over
\Omega

Hicken and Zingg 2013
;
Hicken et al. 2016
. Thus, my choice of notation for the nodes,
X_{\Omega}
, in the SBP definition reflects this connection to quadrature.

2.2
First-derivative modal-collocation operator

I begin this section by introducing the closed-form expression for the first-derivative operator used in modal-collocation discretizations. The definition below uses orthonormal basis functions, but it is otherwise identical to the projection-based derivative operator in Equation (24) of
Chan 2018
. I adopt an orthonormal basis to simplify the presentation and analysis, but it is not necessary in practice; see Section
3.4
.

Definition 2

(First-derivative modal-collocation operator)
.

Let
\{X_{\Omega},W_{\Omega}\}
be a positive-interior quadrature rule that is exact for polynomials of total degree
2P
. Then the matrix
\mathsf{D}_{x_{d}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
is a degree
P
modal-collocation operator corresponding to the first derivative
\partial/\partial x_{d}
,
d=1,2,\ldots,D
, at the quadrature nodes
X_{\Omega}
if it has the form

\mathsf{D}_{x_{d}}=\mathsf{V}_{x_{d}}\mathsf{V}^{T}\mathsf{W},

where
\mathsf{W}=\diag(w_{1},w_{2},\ldots,w_{N})
is a positive-definite, diagonal matrix whose nonzero entries are the quadrature weights in
W_{\Omega}
.

Chan
(
Chan 2018
, Lemma 1)
demonstrated that MC operators are degree
P
SBP operators. I restate and prove this result below in the context of orthonormal bases. While the result is not novel, I include it for completeness and to introduce some definitions needed later.

Theorem 1
.

All degree
P
first-derivative MC operators are also degree
P
, diagonal-norm SBP operators.

Proof.

The derivative-accuracy condition follows by multiplying the MC operator and the Vandermonde matrix
\mathsf{V}
:

\mathsf{D}_{x_{d}}\mathsf{V}=\mathsf{V}_{x_{d}}\mathsf{V}^{T}\mathsf{W}\mathsf{V}=\mathsf{V}_{x_{d}},

where I used the identity
\mathsf{V}^{T}\mathsf{W}\mathsf{V}=\mathsf{I}_{N_{P}}
, which follows from the orthonormality of the basis, c.f. (
1
), and the
2P
exactness of the quadrature used in the MC operator definition.

The factorization condition in Definition
1
is trivially satisfied by defining

\mathsf{Q}_{x_{d}}\equiv\mathsf{W}\mathsf{D}_{x_{d}}=\mathsf{W}\mathsf{V}_{x_{d}}\mathsf{V}^{T}\mathsf{W}.

(5)

This definition of
\mathsf{Q}_{x_{d}}
implies that the symmetric matrix

\mathsf{E}_{x_{d}}=\mathsf{Q}_{x_{d}}+\mathsf{Q}_{x_{d}}^{T}=\mathsf{W}\mathsf{V}_{x_{d}}\mathsf{V}^{T}\mathsf{W}+\mathsf{W}\mathsf{V}\mathsf{V}_{x_{d}}^{T}\mathsf{W},

(6)

which can be used to verify the boundary-accuracy condition, Equation (
4
), as follows:

\displaystyle\big[\mathsf{V}^{T}\mathsf{E}_{x_{d}}\mathsf{V}\big]_{ij}

\displaystyle=\Big[\mathsf{V}^{T}\Big(\mathsf{W}\mathsf{V}_{x_{d}}\mathsf{V}^{T}\mathsf{W}+\mathsf{W}\mathsf{V}\mathsf{V}_{x_{d}}^{T}\mathsf{W}\Big)\mathsf{V}\Big]_{ij},\qquad

(using (
6
))

\displaystyle=\big[\mathsf{V}^{T}\mathsf{W}\mathsf{V}_{x_{d}}+\mathsf{V}_{x_{d}}^{T}\mathsf{W}\mathsf{V}\big]_{ij},

(using
\mathsf{V}^{T}\mathsf{W}\mathsf{V}=\mathsf{I}_{N_{P}}
)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathcal{V}_{i}(\bm{x}_{k})w_{k}\frac{\partial\mathcal{V}_{j}}{\partial x_{d}}(\bm{x}_{k})+\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{\partial\mathcal{V}_{i}}{\partial x_{d}}(\bm{x}_{k})w_{k}\mathcal{V}_{j}(\bm{x}_{k})

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\Big(\mathcal{V}_{i}\frac{\partial\mathcal{V}_{j}}{\partial x_{d}}+\frac{\partial\mathcal{V}_{i}}{\partial x_{d}}\mathcal{V}_{j}\Big)\,d\Omega

(using quad. accuracy)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{V}_{i}\mathcal{V}_{j}\,n_{x_{d}}\,d\Gamma,

(using div. theorem)

as required. Thus, all three conditions are satisfied, which concludes the proof.
∎

The converse of Theorem
1
does not hold. Most first-derivative SBP operators are not first-derivative MC operators, and I will provide an example in Section
4
. However, it is possible to convert an existing SBP operator into an MC operator by projecting it onto the modal basis. Indeed, as I show below, this conversion is possible for any first-derivative operator that acts at the nodes of a sufficiently high-order quadrature rule.

Proposition 2
.

Let
\mathsf{D}_{x_{d}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
be a nodal operator that exactly differentiates degree
P
polynomials evaluated at the nodes
X_{\Omega}
; thus,
\mathsf{D}_{x_{d}}\mathsf{V}=\mathsf{V}_{x_{d}}
. If the nodes coincide with a degree
2P
exact quadrature rule, with positive weights
W_{\Omega}
, then
\mathsf{V}\mathsf{V}^{T}\mathsf{W}\mathsf{D}_{x_{d}}\mathsf{V}\mathsf{V}^{T}\mathsf{W}
is a degree
P
modal collocation operator.

Proof.

The result follows immediately from the accuracy of the nodal operator
\mathsf{D}_{x_{d}}
, since

\mathsf{V}\,\mathsf{V}^{T}\,\mathsf{W}\,\mathsf{D}_{x_{d}}\,\mathsf{V}\,\mathsf{V}^{T}\,\mathsf{W}=\mathsf{V}\,\mathsf{V}^{T}\,\mathsf{W}\,\mathsf{V}_{x_{d}}\,\mathsf{V}^{T}\,\mathsf{W}=\mathsf{V}_{x_{d}}\,\mathsf{V}^{T}\,\mathsf{W},

where I used
\mathsf{V}\,\mathsf{V}^{T}\,\mathsf{W}\,\mathsf{V}_{x_{d}}=\mathsf{V}_{x_{d}}
in the second step.
∎

Remark 1
.

Proposition
2
is an interesting observation, but it is not useful in practice: if one can perform the necessary projection
\mathsf{V}\mathsf{V}^{T}\mathsf{W}
, why not use the MC definition
2
directly?

2.3
Modal-collocation boundary operator

There is an equivalent representation of the boundary operator
\mathsf{E}_{x_{d}}
that can be used in practice to evaluate boundary integrals in the weak form. This is the subject of Lemma 1 from
Chan 2018
, which is restated below using the present notation. However, before restating the lemma, I need the following definition that relates volume and boundary quadrature rules. Equation (
7
), appearing in Definition
3
, is called the compatibility condition in the SBP literature
Del Rey Fernández et al. 2014
, and it is related to Assumption 1 in
Chan 2018
.

Definition 3

(Compatible volume and boundary quadratures)
.

Let
\{X_{\Omega},W_{\Omega}\}
be a quadrature rule over
\Omega
and let
\{X_{\Gamma},W_{\Gamma}\}
be a quadrature rule over its boundary
\Gamma
. We say that the quadrature rules are degree-
P
compatible if

\mathsf{V}^{T}\mathsf{W}\mathsf{V}_{x_{d}}+\mathsf{V}_{x_{d}}^{T}\mathsf{W}\mathsf{V}=\mathsf{V}_{\Gamma}^{T}\,\mathsf{W}_{\Gamma}\,\mathsf{N}_{x_{d}}\,\mathsf{V}_{\Gamma},

(7)

where
\mathsf{W}
,
\mathsf{V}
, and
\mathsf{V}_{x_{d}}
are defined as before,
\mathsf{W}_{\Gamma}
is a diagonal matrix whose nonzero entries are the quadrature weights
W_{\Gamma}
, and
\mathsf{N}_{x_{d}}
is a diagonal matrix holding the unit normal
n_{x_{d}}
evaluated at the quadrature nodes
X_{\Gamma}
.

Definition
3
states that compatible volume and boundary quadrature rules must mimic the integration-by-parts formula for degree
P
polynomials. Written out in detail, Equation (
7
) reads

\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathcal{V}_{i}(\bm{x}_{k})w_{k}\frac{\partial\mathcal{V}_{j}}{\partial x_{d}}(\bm{x}_{k})+\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{\partial\mathcal{V}_{i}}{\partial x_{d}}(\bm{x}_{k})w_{k}\mathcal{V}_{j}(\bm{x}_{k})\\
=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathcal{V}_{i}(\hat{\bm{x}}_{m})\mathcal{V}_{j}(\hat{\bm{x}}_{m})n_{x_{d}}(\hat{\bm{x}}_{m})\hat{w}_{m},\qquad\forall i,j=1,2,\ldots,N_{P}.

The above identity is easy to enforce when the boundary
\Gamma
consists of piecewise-linear facets, since
n_{x_{d}}
is then constant over each sub-boundary. In this case, one can choose a degree
2P
exact boundary quadrature for each sub-boundary and a degree
2P-1
exact volume quadrature. Therefore, compatibility is straightforward to attain for standard reference elements, such as quadrilaterals, hexahedrons, triangles, and tetrahedrons. Elements with curvilinear boundaries may require a higher than
2P
-exact boundary quadrature to achieve compatibility. On the other hand, quadratures that are only compatible for constant functions (
P=0
) are often adequate in practice for stable, high-order discretizations; see, for example,
(
Crean et al. 2018
, Sec. 5)
.

I can now restate Lemma 1 from
Chan 2018
.

Lemma 1

(
Chan 2018
)
.

Let
\mathsf{D}_{x_{d}}=\mathsf{V}_{x_{d}}\mathsf{V}^{T}\mathsf{W}=\mathsf{W}^{-1}\mathsf{Q}_{x_{d}}
be a degree
P
first-derivative MC operator. In addition, let
\{X_{\Gamma},W_{\Gamma}\}
be a positive-interior quadrature rule over the boundary
\Gamma
that is degree-
P
compatible with the volume quadrature used to define
\mathsf{D}_{x_{d}}
. Then

\mathsf{E}_{x_{d}}=\mathsf{R}_{\Gamma}^{T}\,\mathsf{W}_{\Gamma}\,\mathsf{N}_{x_{d}}\,\mathsf{R}_{\Gamma},

(8)

where
\mathsf{R}_{\Gamma}=\mathsf{V}_{\Gamma}\,\mathsf{V}^{T}\,\mathsf{W}
, and
\mathsf{V}_{\Gamma}
is the orthonormal basis evaluated at the boundary quadrature nodes
X_{\Gamma}
.

3
Analysis of modal-collocation discretizations

3.1
Equivalence of modal-collocation and modal DG

I will demonstrate the equivalence between MC and modal DG for a scalar, nonlinear hyperbolic equation with a source. Thus, I consider the initial boundary-value problem (IBVP)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{U}}{\partial t}=-\sum_{d=1}^{D}\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}_{d}(\mathcal{U})}{\partial x_{d}}+\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{U},t),

\displaystyle\forall\,\bm{x}\in\Omega,t\in[0,T],

(9)

\displaystyle\sum_{d=1}^{D}\mathcal{F}_{d}(\mathcal{U})n_{x_{d}}=\mathcal{F}_{n}^{*}(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{G}),

\displaystyle\forall\,\bm{x}\in\Gamma,t\in[0,T],

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}(\bm{x},0)=\mathcal{U}_{0}(\bm{x}),

\displaystyle\forall\,\bm{x}\in\Omega.

The source term,
\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{U},t)\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, is nonlinear and time dependent, in general. Boundary conditions are specified using the flux function
\mathcal{F}_{n}^{*}(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{G})
, which selects the boundary data
\mathcal{G}(\bm{x},t)
where the characteristics enter the domain and the solution
\mathcal{U}(\bm{x},t)
otherwise. The initial condition is specified using
\mathcal{U}_{0}(\bm{x})\in L^{2}(\Omega)
.

Modal DG semi-discretization

For simplicity, I will assume one element is used for the domain
\Omega
. The modal, discontinuous Galerkin semi-discretization of (
9
) begins with the weak formulation

\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{V}_{h}^{T}\,\frac{\partial\mathcal{U}_{h}}{\partial t}\,d\Omega=\sum_{d=1}^{D}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\partial\mathcal{V}_{h}}{\partial x_{d}}\,\mathcal{F}_{d}(\mathcal{U}_{h})\,d\Omega\\
-\int_{\Gamma}\mathcal{V}_{h}\,\mathcal{F}_{n}^{*}(\mathcal{U}_{h},\mathcal{G})\,d\Gamma+\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{V}_{h}\,\mathcal{S}\,d\Omega,\qquad\forall\,t\in[0,T],

(10)

where the test functions are elements of the orthonormal basis,
\mathcal{V}_{h}\in\{\mathcal{V}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{P}}
, and the DG solution is

\mathcal{U}_{h}(\bm{x},t)=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{P}}\mathcal{V}_{i}(\bm{x})\tilde{u}_{i}(t).

The initial values of the modal coefficients,
\{\tilde{u}_{i}(0)\}_{i=1}^{N_{P}}
, are found through an
L^{2}
projection of the initial condition
\mathcal{U}_{0}(\bm{x})
onto the modal basis. Thus,

\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{V}_{h}\,\mathcal{U}_{h}(\bm{x},0)\,d\Omega=\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{V}_{h}\,\mathcal{U}_{0}(\bm{x})\,d\Omega,\qquad\forall\,\mathcal{V}_{h}\in\{\mathcal{V}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{P}}.

(11)

Next, I approximate the integrals in (
10
) and (
11
) using positive-interior quadrature rules that are degree
2P
exact over
\Omega
and
\Gamma
. Substituting the matrices
\mathsf{V}
,
\mathsf{V}_{x_{d}}
,
\mathsf{V}_{\Gamma}
,
\mathsf{W}
, and
\mathsf{W}_{\Gamma}
defined previously, and using the identity
\mathsf{V}^{T}\mathsf{W}\mathsf{V}=\mathsf{I}_{N_{P}}
, the DG weak formulation becomes

\frac{d\tilde{\bm{u}}}{dt}=\sum_{d=1}^{D}\mathsf{V}_{x_{d}}^{T}\,\mathsf{W}\,\bm{f}_{d}(\mathsf{V}\tilde{\bm{u}})-\mathsf{V}_{\Gamma}^{T}\,\mathsf{W}_{\Gamma}\,\bm{f}^{*}_{n}(\mathsf{V}_{\Gamma}\tilde{\bm{u}},\bm{g}_{\Gamma})+\mathsf{V}^{T}\,\mathsf{W}\,\bm{s}(\mathsf{V}\tilde{\bm{u}},t),\qquad\forall\,t\in[0,T],

(12)

where I have introduced the vector of modal coefficients,
\tilde{\bm{u}}=[\tilde{u}_{1},\tilde{u}_{2},\ldots,\tilde{u}_{N_{P}}]^{T}
, as well as the vector-valued functions
\bm{f}_{d}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
\bm{f}_{n}^{*}:\mathbb{R}^{M}\times\mathbb{R}^{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{M}
, and
\bm{s}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. These functions are defined entrywise below:

\displaystyle\big[\bm{f}_{d}(\mathsf{V}\tilde{\bm{u}})\big]_{i}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}_{d}\big(\mathcal{U}_{h}(\bm{x}_{i},t)\big),

\displaystyle\forall\,i=1,2,\ldots,N,

(13)

\displaystyle\big[\bm{f}^{*}_{n}(\mathsf{V}_{\Gamma}\tilde{\bm{u}},\bm{g}_{\Gamma})\big]_{i}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}^{*}_{n}\big(\mathcal{U}_{h}(\bm{x}_{i},t),\mathcal{G}(\bm{x}_{i},t)\big),

\displaystyle\forall\,i=1,2,\ldots,M,

(14)

\displaystyle\big[\bm{s}(\mathsf{V}\tilde{\bm{u}},t)\big]_{i}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{U}_{h}(\bm{x}_{i}),t),

\displaystyle\forall\,i=1,2,\ldots,N,

(15)

where
\big[\bm{g}_{\Gamma}\big]_{i}=\mathcal{G}(\bm{x}_{i},t)
for all
i=1,2,\ldots,M
. In addition, the initial condition for the modal coefficients can be expressed as

\tilde{\bm{u}}(0)=\mathsf{V}^{T}\,\mathsf{W}\,\bm{u}_{0},

(16)

where
\big[\bm{u}_{0}\big]_{i}=\mathcal{U}_{0}(\bm{x}_{i})
, for all
i=1,2,\ldots,N
.

Modal collocation semi-discretization

Following the SBP literature, the strong-form discretization of the IBVP (
9
) based on the first-derivative MC operator is

\frac{d\bm{u}}{dt}=-\sum_{d=1}^{D}\mathsf{D}_{x_{d}}\,\bm{f}_{d}(\bm{u})-\mathsf{W}^{-1}\,\mathsf{R}_{\Gamma}^{T}\,\mathsf{W}_{\Gamma}\,\big(\bm{f}_{n}^{*}(\mathsf{R}_{\Gamma}\bm{u},\bm{g}_{\Gamma})-\bm{f}_{n}(\bm{u})\big)+\mathsf{V}\,\mathsf{V}^{T}\,\mathsf{W}\,\bm{s}(\bm{u},t),\\
\forall\,t\in[0,T],

(17)

where

\bm{f}_{n}(\bm{u})=\sum_{d=1}^{D}\mathsf{N}_{x_{d}}\,\mathsf{R}_{\Gamma}\,\bm{f}_{d}(\bm{u}).

(18)

The functions
\bm{f}_{d}
,
\bm{f}_{n}^{*}
, and
\bm{s}
appearing in the MC discretization (
17
) are the same as the ones defined in Equations (
13
)–(
15
) for the DG weak formulation; however, they are nominally evaluated using the MC solution rather than the DG solution.

Notice that the source term in Equation (
17
) has been projected onto the modal basis. Similarly, rather than taking
\bm{u}(0)=\bm{u}_{0}
as the initial condition, I adopt the projected initial condition

\bm{u}(0)=\mathsf{V}\,\mathsf{V}^{T}\,\mathsf{W}\,\bm{u}_{0}.

(19)

Remark 2
.

Projecting the source term and initial condition onto the polynomial space is not commonly done in SBP discretizations but is necessary to prove MC-DG equivalence.

The discretization (
17
) can be rewritten in an equivalent “weak” form by left multiplying by
\mathsf{W}
and using the properties of the MC operator, specifically Equations (
5
), (
6
), and (
8
). After some algebra I obtain

\mathsf{W}\frac{d\bm{u}}{dt}=\sum_{d=1}^{D}\mathsf{W}\,\mathsf{V}\,\mathsf{V}_{x_{d}}^{T}\,\mathsf{W}\,\bm{f}_{d}(\bm{u})-\mathsf{R}_{\Gamma}^{T}\,\mathsf{W}_{\Gamma}\,\bm{f}_{n}^{*}(\mathsf{R}_{\Gamma}\bm{u},\bm{g}_{\Gamma})+\mathsf{W}\,\mathsf{V}\,\mathsf{V}^{T}\,\mathsf{W}\bm{s}(\bm{u},t),\\
\forall\,t\in[0,T].

(20)

This form of the MC discretization will be useful in the proof of MC-DG equivalence.

Main result

Theorem 3

(MC-DG equivalence)
.

Assume that the initial-value problem corresponding to the modal DG semi-discretization (
10
) and (
16
) is well posed. Then the solution to the MC semi-discretization (
17
) and (
19
) is equivalent to the modal DG solution. That is,

\bm{u}(t)=\mathsf{V}\,\tilde{\bm{u}}(t),\qquad\forall\,t\in[0,T].

Proof.

Let
\mathsf{Z}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N_{Z}}
, where
N_{Z}=N-N_{P}
, be a matrix in the nullspace of
\mathsf{V}^{T}\,\mathsf{W}
and orthonormal with respect to
\mathsf{W}
; that is,

\mathsf{V}^{T}\,\mathsf{W}\,\mathsf{Z}=\mathsf{0}_{N_{P}\times N_{Z}},\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathsf{Z}^{T}\,\mathsf{W}\,\mathsf{Z}=\mathsf{I}_{N_{Z}}.

(21)

Based on the rank-nullity theorem, the MC solution can be decomposed using the modal and nullspace bases as follows:

\bm{u}(t)=\mathsf{V}\,\bm{u}_{V}(t)+\mathsf{Z}\,\bm{u}_{Z}(t),

where
\bm{u}_{V}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{P}}
and
\bm{u}_{Z}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{Z}}
. The proof proceeds by showing that
\bm{u}_{Z}(t)=\bm{0}
and
\bm{u}_{V}(t)=\tilde{\bm{u}}(t)
.

To show that
\bm{u}_{Z}(t)=\bm{0}
, left multiply the weak form of the MC discretization, Equation (
20
), by
\mathsf{Z}^{T}
. On the left-hand side this yields (see (
21
))

\mathsf{Z}^{T}\,\mathsf{W}\frac{d}{dt}\big(\mathsf{V}\,\bm{u}_{V}+\mathsf{Z}\,\bm{u}_{Z}\big)=\frac{d\bm{u}_{Z}}{dt},

while on the right-hand side we obtain

\sum_{d=1}^{D}\mathsf{Z}^{T}\,\mathsf{W}\,\mathsf{V}\,\mathsf{V}_{x_{d}}^{T}\,\mathsf{W}\,\bm{f}_{d}(\bm{u})-\mathsf{Z}^{T}\,\mathsf{R}_{\Gamma}^{T}\,\mathsf{W}_{\Gamma}\,\bm{f}_{n}^{*}(\mathsf{R}_{\Gamma}\bm{u},\bm{g}_{\Gamma})+\mathsf{Z}^{T}\,\mathsf{W}\,\mathsf{V}\,\mathsf{V}^{T}\,\mathsf{W}\,\bm{s}(\bm{u},t)=\bm{0},

since, again,
\mathsf{Z}
is orthogonal to
\mathsf{V}
with respect to
\mathsf{W}
; recall from Lemma
1
that
\mathsf{R}_{\Gamma}=\mathsf{V}_{\Gamma}\,\mathsf{V}^{T}\,\mathsf{W}
so
\mathsf{Z}^{T}\,\mathsf{R}_{\Gamma}^{T}=\mathsf{0}_{N_{Z}\times N_{M}}
. Furthermore, since
\bm{u}(0)=\mathsf{V}\,\bm{u}_{V}(0)+\mathsf{Z}\,\bm{u}_{Z}(0)=\mathsf{V}\,\mathsf{V}^{T}\,\mathsf{W}\,\bm{u}_{0}
from (
19
), it follows that the vector
\bm{u}_{Z}
is governed by the initial value problem

\frac{d\bm{u}_{Z}}{dt}=\bm{0},\qquad\forall\,t\in[0,T],\qquad\text{with IC}\qquad\bm{u}_{Z}(0)=\bm{0}.

Thus,
\bm{u}_{Z}(t)=\bm{0}
for all time
t\in[0,T]
. Consequently, the MC solution must be of the form
\bm{u}(t)=\mathsf{V}\bm{u}_{V}(t)
.

Next, I left multiply Equation (
20
) by
\mathsf{V}^{T}
and substitute
\bm{u}(t)=\mathsf{V}\,\bm{u}_{V}(t)
:

\frac{d\bm{u}_{V}}{dt}=\sum_{d=1}^{D}\mathsf{V}_{x_{d}}^{T}\,\mathsf{W}\,\bm{f}_{d}(\mathsf{V}\bm{u}_{V})-\mathsf{V}_{\Gamma}^{T}\,\mathsf{W}_{\Gamma}\,\bm{f}_{n}^{*}(\mathsf{V}_{\Gamma}\bm{u}_{V},\bm{g})+\mathsf{V}^{T}\,\mathsf{W}\,\bm{s}(\mathsf{V}\bm{u}_{V},t),\qquad\forall\,t\in[0,T],

(22)

where I used
\mathsf{R}_{\Gamma}\,\mathsf{V}\,\bm{u}_{V}=\mathsf{V}_{\Gamma}\,\bm{u}_{V}
. The initial condition for
\bm{u}_{V}
is found by left multiplying (
19
) by
\mathsf{V}^{T}\,\mathsf{W}
:

\bm{u}_{V}(0)=\mathsf{V}^{T}\,\mathsf{W}\,\mathsf{V}\,\mathsf{V}^{T}\,\mathsf{W}\,\bm{u}_{0}=\mathsf{V}^{T}\,\mathsf{W}\,\bm{u}_{0}.

(23)

Comparing Equations (
22
) and (
23
) with Equations (
12
) and (
16
), respectively, we see that
\tilde{\bm{u}}
and
\bm{u}_{V}
are governed by identical initial value problems. The modal DG semi-discretization is assumed to be well-posed, so its solution exists and is unique, which completes the proof.
∎

Remark 3
.

The proof can easily be generalized to multi-element meshes, provided the MC discretization uses the appropriate
\mathsf{R}_{\Gamma}
matrices to project the solution to the face quadrature nodes.

3.2
Spectral equivalence of modal-collocation and modal DG

Suppose the PDE in the IBVP (
9
) is linear. Then the modal DG semi-discretization (
12
) can be written in the form

\frac{d\tilde{\bm{u}}}{dt}=\tilde{\mathsf{A}}\tilde{\bm{u}}+\tilde{\bm{b}}(t),\qquad\forall\,t\in[0,T],

(24)

and the MC semi-discretization can be written as

\frac{d\bm{u}}{dt}=\mathsf{A}\bm{u}+\bm{b}(t),\qquad\forall\,t\in[0,T].

(25)

One of the consequences of Theorem
3
is that spectra of the matrices
\tilde{\mathsf{A}}
and
\mathsf{A}
in (
24
) and (
25
) are effectively equivalent.

Corollary 1
.

The spectrum of the matrix
\mathsf{A}
in the MC semi-discretization (
25
) can be partitioned into two sets. The first set consists of the
N_{P}
eigenvalues corresponding to the matrix
\tilde{\mathsf{A}}
in the DG semi-discretization (
24
). The second set consists of
N_{Z}=N-N_{P}
repeated zero eigenvalues.

Proof.

Substitute the decomposition
\bm{u}(t)=\mathsf{V}\tilde{\bm{u}}(t)+\mathsf{Z}\bm{u}_{Z}(t)
from the proof of Theorem
3
into (
25
):

\begin{bmatrix}\mathsf{V},\,\mathsf{Z}\end{bmatrix}\frac{d}{dt}\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\bm{u}}\\
\bm{u}_{Z}\end{bmatrix}=\mathsf{A}\begin{bmatrix}\mathsf{V},\,\mathsf{Z}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\bm{u}}\\
\bm{u}_{Z}\end{bmatrix}.

The inverse of the
N\times N
matrix on the left-hand side is

\begin{bmatrix}\mathsf{V},\,\mathsf{Z}\end{bmatrix}^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathsf{V}^{T}\,\mathsf{W}\\
\mathsf{Z}^{T}\,\mathsf{W}\end{bmatrix}.

Left multiplying by this inverse, the ODE above becomes

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\bm{u}}\\
\bm{u}_{Z}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\mathsf{V},\,\mathsf{Z}\end{bmatrix}^{-1}\mathsf{A}\begin{bmatrix}\mathsf{V},\,\mathsf{Z}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\bm{u}}\\
\bm{u}_{Z}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\mathsf{V}^{T}\,\mathsf{W}\,\mathsf{A}\,\mathsf{V}\quad&\mathsf{V}^{T}\,\mathsf{W}\,\mathsf{A}\,\mathsf{Z}\\[6.45831pt]
\mathsf{Z}^{T}\,\mathsf{W}\,\mathsf{A}\,\mathsf{V}\quad&\mathsf{Z}^{T}\,\mathsf{W}\,\mathsf{A}\,\mathsf{Z}\end{bmatrix}}_{\displaystyle\equiv\mathsf{B}}\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\bm{u}}\\
\bm{u}_{Z}\end{bmatrix}.

(26)

Note that the matrix
\mathsf{B}
, defined above, is similar to
\mathsf{A}
, so the two matrices have the same eigenvalues, i.e.
\sigma(\mathsf{A})=\sigma(\mathsf{B})
.

Now, from the DG semi-discretization (
24
) and the trivial ODE
d\bm{u}_{Z}/dt=\bm{0}
— the latter follows from Theorem
3
— we also have

\frac{d}{dt}\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\bm{u}}\\
\bm{u}_{Z}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\mathsf{A}}&\mathsf{0}_{N_{P}\times N_{Z}}\\[6.45831pt]
\mathsf{0}_{N_{Z}\times N_{P}}&\mathsf{0}_{N_{Z}\times N_{Z}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\bm{u}}\\
\bm{u}_{Z}\end{bmatrix}.

Equating the matrix on the right-hand side with the matrix
\mathsf{B}
in (
26
), we can conclude that
\sigma(\mathsf{A})=\sigma(\mathsf{B})=\sigma(\tilde{\mathsf{A}})\cup\{0\}_{i=1}^{N_{Z}}
, as desired.
∎

Corollary
1
implies that MC solutions are effectively independent of the choice of quadrature. In particular, the quadrature node distribution should have no impact on the spectral radius; I will illustrate this in Section
4
. The caveat suggested by the word “effectively” is that the projection is only approximate for functions that are not in
\mathbb{P}^{P}(\Omega)
, so the spectrum will differ between quadrature rules, in general; however, these differences will be on the order of the discretization.

3.3
Nullspace inconsistency and steady problems

A first-derivative operator
\mathsf{D}_{x_{d}}
is
nullspace consistent

Svärd and Nordström 2019
if its nullspace consists only of constant vectors; that is,
\mathsf{D}_{x_{d}}\bm{u}=\bm{0}
implies that
\bm{u}
is a constant vector. The operator is consistent in the sense that the differential operator
\partial/\partial_{x_{d}}
also has only constant functions in its nullspace.

MC operators are
nullspace inconsistent
whenever
N>N_{P}
. This is easy to see using the matrix
\mathsf{Z}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N_{Z}}
, which was defined in the proof of Theorem
1
as the nullspace of
\mathsf{V}^{T}\,\mathsf{W}
. It follows immediately that
\mathsf{D}_{x_{d}}\,\mathsf{Z}=\mathsf{V}_{x_{d}}\,\mathsf{V}^{T}\,\mathsf{W}\,\mathsf{Z}=\mathsf{0}_{N\times N_{Z}}
.

In the context of finite-difference discretizations, nullspace inconsistency can lead to inaccurate solutions
Svärd and Nordström 2019
;
Glaubitz et al. 2026
. However, the nullspace inconsistency of MC operators does not appear to present a problem, because the nullspace modes are initialized to zero and never grow; recall
d\bm{u}_{Z}/dt=\bm{0}
in the proof of Theorem
3
.

Remark 4
.

The projected initial condition (
19
) is responsible for setting the initial nullspace modes to zero. However, projecting the initial condition is unnecessary for smooth problems, because the nullspace modes would correspond to
\text{O}(h^{P+1})
errors that, while not damped, never grow.

While not an issue for unsteady problems, the nullspace inconsistency of MC operators is problematic for steady problems, because Theorem
3
implies that the Jacobian of the right-hand side of (
20
) will be singular. Similarly, Corollary
1
indicates that the system matrix for linear problems has zero eigenvalues and is therefore singular.

Fortunately, there is a simple solution to the nullspace inconsistency of MC discretization: local-projection stabilization (LPS). LPS was first proposed in
Becker and Braack 2001
and adapted to multidimensional SBP discretizations in
Hicken 2020
.

Definition 4

(Local-projection stablization operator)
.

Let
\{X_{\Omega},W_{\Omega}\}
be a positive-interior quadrature rule that is exact for polynomials of total degree
2P
on the domain
\Omega
. Then the matrix
\mathsf{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
is a degree
P
local-projection stabilization operator if

\displaystyle\mathsf{P}

\displaystyle=\big(\mathsf{I}_{N}-\mathsf{V}\mathsf{V}^{T}\mathsf{W}\big)^{T}\mathsf{W}\big(\mathsf{I}_{N}-\mathsf{V}\mathsf{V}^{T}\mathsf{W}\big)

\displaystyle=\mathsf{W}\big(\mathsf{I}_{N}-\mathsf{V}\mathsf{V}^{T}\mathsf{W}\big),

where the second line follows from the orthonormality of
\mathsf{V}
with respect to
\mathsf{W}
.

To remove the nullspace from the MC semi-discretizations, one subtracts
\mathsf{P}\bm{u}
from the right-hand side of the weak formulation (
20
), or
\mathsf{W}^{-1}\mathsf{P}\bm{u}
from the right-hand side of the strong formulation (
17
).

LPS does not affect polynomials in
\mathbb{P}^{P}(\Omega)
, since

\mathsf{P}\mathsf{V}=\mathsf{W}\mathsf{V}-\mathsf{W}\mathsf{V}\big(\underbrace{\mathsf{V}^{T}\mathsf{W}\mathsf{V}}_{\displaystyle\mathsf{I}_{N_{P}}}\big)=\mathsf{0}_{N\times N_{P}},

so it does not influence the polynomial exactness of the rest of the discretization. By contrast, LPS is nonzero when applied to
\mathsf{Z}
, since
\mathsf{P}\mathsf{Z}=\mathsf{W}\mathsf{Z}
. In addition to accuracy, one can show that LPS is conservative and energy/entropy stable
Hicken 2020
.

Remark 5
.

In practice, LPS is modified to make it dimensionally consistent and scaled to the magnitude of the local wave speed(s). This can be achieved by inserting a diagonal, positive definite matrix — or block-diagonal, positive-definite matrix for PDE systems — between the projections and the norm
\mathsf{W}
. If
\mathsf{\Lambda}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
is such a positive-definite matrix then the modified LPS operator is

\mathsf{P}=\big(\mathsf{I}_{N}-\mathsf{V}\mathsf{V}^{T}\mathsf{W}\big)^{T}\mathsf{W}\mathsf{\Lambda}\big(\mathsf{I}_{N}-\mathsf{V}\mathsf{V}^{T}\mathsf{W}\big)^{T}.

(27)

Since
\mathsf{\Lambda}
is positive definite, including this factor does not impact the stability of LPS. Moreover, polynomial exactness and conservation are maintained.

Remark 6
.

Consider the first-derivative operators

\mathsf{D}_{x_{d}}^{(+)}=\mathsf{H}^{-1}\big(\mathsf{Q}_{x_{d}}+\mathsf{P}\big),\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathsf{D}_{x_{d}}^{(-)}=\mathsf{H}^{-1}\big(\mathsf{Q}_{x_{d}}-\mathsf{P}\big).

It is straightforward to show that
\mathsf{D}_{x_{d}}^{(+)}
and
\mathsf{D}_{x_{d}}^{(-)}
are diagonal-norm upwind SBP operators
Mattsson 2017
. An attractive feature of upwind SBP operators is that they can be combined with flux-vector splitting to determine an appropriate scaling matrix
\mathsf{\Lambda}
in(
27
). See, for example,
Lundgren and Mattsson 2020
and
Hew et al. 2025
for examples of upwind SBP operators in the context of finite-difference methods.

3.4
Generalizations

I chose to use an orthonormal basis and a degree
2P
quadrature to simplify the presentation, but the theory can be adapted if we use a nonorthogonal basis for
\mathbb{P}^{P}(\Omega)
and a degree
2P-1
quadrature.

For a nonorthogonal basis
\{\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{P}}
and
Q\geq 2P-1
exact quadrature, the MC operator becomes
Chan 2018

\mathsf{D}_{x_{d}}=\hat{\mathsf{V}}_{x_{d}}\,\mathsf{M}^{-1}\,\hat{\mathsf{V}}^{T}\,\mathsf{W},

(28)

where
\mathsf{M}\equiv\hat{\mathsf{V}}^{T}\,\mathsf{W}\,\hat{\mathsf{V}}
is the mass matrix, and
\hat{\mathsf{V}}
and
\hat{\mathsf{V}}_{x_{d}}
hold the basis and its derivative evaluated at the quadrature nodes; these matrices are analogous to
\mathsf{V}
and
\mathsf{V}_{x_{d}}
in the orthonormal case. In addition, the boundary operator remains
\mathsf{E}_{x_{d}}=\mathsf{R}_{\Gamma}^{T}\,\mathsf{W}_{\Gamma}\,\mathsf{N}_{x_{d}}\,\mathsf{R}_{\Gamma}
, but the boundary-projection operator is now defined by
Chan 2018

\mathsf{R}_{\Gamma}=\hat{\mathsf{V}}_{\Gamma}\,\mathsf{M}^{-1}\,\hat{\mathsf{V}}^{T}\,\mathsf{W}.

(29)

Nonorthogonal basis with
2P
exact quadrature

If the volume quadrature remains
2P
exact, then the MC operator (
28
) is equivalent to one based on an orthonormal basis. To show this, I use the Cholesky decomposition of the symmetric, positive definite mass matrix, i.e.
\mathsf{M}=\mathsf{L}\mathsf{L}^{T}
. Then

\mathsf{D}_{x_{d}}=\hat{\mathsf{V}}_{x_{d}}\mathsf{L}^{-T}\mathsf{L}^{-1}\hat{\mathsf{V}}^{T}\mathsf{W}=\mathsf{V}_{x_{d}}\mathsf{V}^{T}\mathsf{W},

where I have made the identifications
\mathsf{V}=\hat{\mathsf{V}}\mathsf{L}^{-T}
and
\mathsf{V}_{x_{d}}=\hat{\mathsf{V}}_{x_{d}}\mathsf{L}^{-T}
. It is then easy to verify that
\mathsf{V}
is orthonormal with respect to
\mathsf{W}
:

\mathsf{V}^{T}\mathsf{W}\mathsf{V}=\mathsf{L}^{-1}\hat{\mathsf{V}}^{T}\mathsf{W}\hat{\mathsf{V}}\mathsf{L}^{-T}=\mathsf{L}^{-1}\mathsf{L}\mathsf{L}^{T}\mathsf{L}^{-T}=\mathsf{I}_{N_{P}}.

In other words, the role of the (inverse) mass matrix in (
28
) and (
29
) is to transform the nonorthogonal basis into an orthonormal one. Thus, the theory developed for the orthonormal basis applies to the nonorthogonal case by recognizing that there is an implicit orthonormal basis defined by
\{\mathcal{V}_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{i}[\mathsf{L}^{-1}]_{ij}\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{j}\}_{i=1}^{N_{P}}
and because
\mathsf{L}
is independent of the quadrature when
Q=2P
.

Degree
2P-1
volume quadrature

The theory for first-derivative SBP operators tells us that we need only
2P-1
exactness from the volume quadrature. This is also true for MC operators. In the following list, I explain how the various results must be adapted.

Theorem
1
:

Consider the MC operator defined by (
28
), but using a degree
2P-1
exact volume quadrature. It is straightforward to show that such an operator is also a diagonal-norm SBP operator by following the same steps as used in the proof of Theorem
1
.

Proposition
2
:

If
\mathsf{D}_{x_{d}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
is a degree
P
first-derivative operator that acts at the nodes of a degree
2P-1
quadrature rule, then
\hat{\mathsf{V}}\mathsf{M}^{-1}\hat{\mathsf{V}}^{T}\mathsf{W}\mathsf{D}_{x_{d}}\hat{\mathsf{V}}\mathsf{M}^{-1}\hat{\mathsf{V}}^{T}\mathsf{W}
is a degree
P
modal collocation operator of the form (
28
).

Lemma
1
:

A degree
2P-1
volume quadrature was already considered in Lemma 1 of
Chan 2018
. Indeed, to adapt Lemma
1
, we simply replace the boundary-projection operator with the one defined by (
29
). Furthermore, note that compatibility, Definition
3
, can be satisfied with a degree
2P-1
exact volume quadrature and
2P
exact boundary quadrature, at least for elements with piecewise linear facets.

Theorem
3
:

The main result continues to hold provided the source and initial condition are projected using
\hat{\mathsf{V}}\mathsf{M}^{-1}\hat{\mathsf{V}}^{T}\mathsf{W}
rather than
\mathsf{V}\mathsf{V}^{T}\mathsf{W}
. The matrix
\mathsf{Z}
is now the nullspace basis for
\hat{\mathsf{V}}^{T}\mathsf{W}

\mathsf{W}
.

Corollary
1
:

The spectrum of the MC semi-discretization is partitioned in the same manner described in Corollary
1
, provided the DG semi-discretization uses the same
2P-1

\mathsf{A}

\tilde{\mathsf{A}}
.

Nonpositive quadrature weights

The proof of Theorem
3

even if it uses a volume quadrature rule with negative weights

4.2
Constant-coefficient advection

IBVP and discretizations

The first study is based on the following constant-coefficient advection problem:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{U}}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{d=1}^{D}\alpha_{d}\frac{\partial\mathcal{U}}{\partial x_{d}},

\displaystyle\forall\,\bm{x}\in\Omega,t\in[0,2],

(30)

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}(\bm{x},t)

\displaystyle=\bm{G}(\bm{x},t),

\displaystyle\forall\,\bm{x}\in\Gamma^{-},t\in[0,2],

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}(\bm{x},0)

\displaystyle=\bm{G}(\bm{x},0),

\displaystyle\forall\,\bm{x}\in\Omega,

where, for simplicity, the spatial domain is the right triangle

\Omega=\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\,|\,-1\leq x_{1}\leq 1,-1\leq x_{2}\leq-x_{1}\},

[\mathsf{N}_{-}]_{mm}=\min\big(0,\alpha_{n}(\hat{\bm{x}}_{m})\big)
and
[\mathsf{N}_{+}]_{mm}=\max\big(0,\alpha_{n}(\hat{\bm{x}}_{m})\big)
, and I used the identity
\sum_{d=1}^{D}\alpha_{d}\mathsf{N}_{x_{d}}=\mathsf{N}_{-}+\mathsf{N}_{+}
to decompose
\bm{f}_{n}(\bm{u})
.

The spectrum for the semi-discretization (
31
) requires writing the equation in the form
d\bm{u}/dt=\mathsf{A}\bm{u}+\bm{b}
. To this end, I substitute (
32
) into (
31
) and find

\frac{d\bm{u}}{dt}=\underbrace{\Bigg(-\sum_{d=1}^{D}\alpha_{d}\mathsf{D}_{x_{d}}+\mathsf{W}^{-1}\mathsf{R}_{\Gamma}^{T}\mathsf{W}_{\Gamma}\mathsf{N}_{-}\mathsf{R}_{\Gamma}\Bigg)}_{\displaystyle\equiv\mathsf{A}}\bm{u}\;\;\underbrace{\rule[-12.91663pt]{0.0pt}{12.91663pt}-\mathsf{W}^{-1}\mathsf{R}_{\Gamma}^{T}\mathsf{W}_{\Gamma}\mathsf{N}_{-}\bm{g}(t)}_{\displaystyle\equiv\bm{b}(t)}.

(33)

The modal DG discretization is obtained by substituting
\bm{u}(t)=\mathsf{V}\,\tilde{\bm{u}}(t)
into Equation (
33
) and left multiplying the equation by
\mathsf{V}^{T}\mathsf{W}
.

Equivalence and negative quadrature weights

I conclude the constant-coefficient advection study with a short example of MC-DG equivalence when the quadrature has negative weights. I use the same semi-discretization (

(

The quadrature nodes include the vertices of the triangle, and their corresponding weights are negative; the weights are

Remark 8
.

Conventional SBP energy-stability theory cannot predict the stability of the MC semi-discretization in this case, since the matrix
\mathsf{W}
is indefinite. Instead, stability of the MC solution is a consequence of its equivalence to the DG solution, which is well-known to be energy-stable for constant-coefficient advection; see, for example,
Hesthaven and Warburton 2008

Nonlinear initial boundary value problem

IBVP and discretizations

Next, consider the following two-dimensional version of Burgers’ equation:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{U}}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial\mathcal{U}^{2}}{\partial x_{1}},

\displaystyle\forall\,\bm{x}\in\Omega,t\in[0,1],

(35)

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}(\bm{x},0)

\displaystyle=\bm{G}(\bm{x},0),

\displaystyle\forall\,\bm{x}\in\Omega,

where
\Omega=[0,2\pi]^{2}
is a square, periodic domain. The initial condition is specified using the exact solution,
\mathcal{G}(\bm{x},t)
, which is the root of the following nonlinear equation:

\mathcal{G}(\bm{x},t)-\sin\big(x_{1}-t\mathcal{G}(\bm{x},t)\big)=0.

(36)

It is easy to see that the Burgers’ IBVP (
35
) corresponds to the general IBVP (
9
) by making the identifications
\mathcal{F}_{1}(\mathcal{U})=\mathcal{U}^{2}/2
,
\mathcal{F}_{2}(\mathcal{U})=0
, and
\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{U},t)=0
.

I discretize the IBVP (
35
) on a multi-element mesh consisting of triangular elements. To generate the mesh, I first create an
N_{1D}\times N_{1D}
mesh of quadrilaterals and then split each quadrilateral into two triangles; thus, there are
K=2N_{1D}^{2}
elements. Figure
5
shows an example of a mesh with
N_{1D}=4
using degree
P=2
modal-collocation operators whose quadrature is
2P
exact.

I investigate two MC-based discretizations for Burgers’ equation. The first, which I refer to as the Standard discretization, satisfies the requirements of Theorem
3
. The second discretization is entropy conservative.

Standard:

The first discretization applies the MC semi-discretization (
17
) on each element with zero source (
\bm{s}=\bm{0}
). The
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
fluxes are given by
\bm{f}_{1}=\frac{1}{2}\bm{u}\circ\bm{u}
and
\bm{f}_{2}=\bm{0}
, respectively, where
\circ
denotes the entry-wise (Hadamard) product. The numerical flux function in the Standard semi-discretization is

\big[\bm{f}_{n}^{*}(\mathsf{R}_{\Gamma}\bm{u},\bm{u}_{\Gamma}^{+})\big]_{i}=\frac{1}{2}\bigg(\frac{u_{i}+u_{i}^{+}}{2}\bigg)^{2},\qquad\forall\,i=1,2,\ldots,M,

where
u_{i}=\big[\mathsf{R}_{\Gamma}\bm{u}\big]_{i}
is the projection of the MC solution on the element to face node
i
, and
u_{i}^{+}
is the projection of the solution to node
i
from the appropriate neighboring element.

Entropy Conservative:

The second discretization corresponds to a skew-symmetric split-formulation of Burgers’ equation that is entropy conservative. The discretization is given by Equation (8) in Crean et al.
Crean et al. 2018
, and it uses the entropy conservative flux

\mathcal{F}_{x_{1}}^{*}(u_{L},u_{R})=\frac{u_{L}^{2}+u_{L}u_{R}+u_{R}^{2}}{6}.

The discretization is entropy conservative, which means that the
L^{2}
norm of the numerical solution is time invariant:

\frac{d}{dt}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\bm{u}_{k}^{T}\mathsf{W}_{k}\bm{u}_{k}=0,

where the subscript
k
denotes the element index.

The results will demonstrate that the entropy-conservative discretization is not equivalent to modal DG, because it cannot be written in the form (
17
); nevertheless, I will show how to recover equivalence, if it is desired.

Investigation of MC-DG equivalence

In this section I compare the Standard and Entropy-Conservative MC discretizations with their modal DG counterparts. As with the constant coefficient advection problem, the modal DG discretizations are obtained by substituting
\bm{u}_{k}(t)=\mathsf{V}\tilde{\bm{u}}_{k}(t)

The results confirm that the Standard MC discretization is equivalent to the modal DG discretization, at least to machine precision. By contrast, the Entropy-Conservative discretization differs from its modal DG discretization, although the difference converges to zero with mesh refinement.

The Entropy-Conservative discretization produces a residual that cannot be represented in the polynomial basis, which is why MC-DG equivalence is lost in this case. Comparing the EC results in Figures

Summary and discussion

I have highlighted some properties of the modal collocation (MC) operator introduced by Chan
Chan 2018
. I showed that MC and DG semi-discretizations of a nonlinear hyperbolic IBVP produce equivalent solutions when they use the same underlying quadrature. One consequence of this result is that the spectrum of MC semi-discretizations is effectively identical to the spectrum of modal DG for linear initial-boundary-value problems.

MC operators are nullspace inconsistent, in general. I argued that this inconsistency is not an issue for unsteady problems, because the trivial modes remain zero. On the other hand, for steady problems, an MC discretization should include local-projection stabilization to avoid singular Jacobians.

I presented numerical studies to verify the equivalence between MC and DG discretizations in the case of the constant-coefficient advection equation and Burgers’ equation. Most of the results were based on a collapsed-coordinate tensor-product quadrature for the triangle; however, I also demonstrated MC-DG equivalence for the advection equation when the quadrature has negative weights. I showed that an entropy-conservative MC discretization of Burgers’ equation is not inherently equivalent to DG, because the residual is not spanned by the polynomial basis. However, if desired, equivalence can be restored by projecting the residual onto the basis.

If MC and DG are effectively equivalent, which one should we use? At this time I do not have a definitive answer. Like many such questions regarding the use of one method versus another the answer may depend on the application. For linear problems where the element mass and stiffness matrices can be precomputed, I suspect MC will provide no benefit and may be slower than a DG method. On the other hand, it may be worth considering MC for nonlinear problems that are solved with explicit time-marching schemes or matrix-free implicit schemes.

Unlike the MC versus DG question, I am more confident answering the following: how should one construct multidimensional SBP operators? Use modal collocation.
