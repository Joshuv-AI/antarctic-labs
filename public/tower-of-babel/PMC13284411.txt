Mental health disorders and suicide risk among autistic children, adolescents and adults: a population-based study using linked administrative data
Authors: Deepa Singal, Jennifer E Enns, Kevin Friesen, Nicholas Piroddi, Margherita Cameranesi, Jonathan Lai, Nathan C Nickel, Shahin Shooshtari, Lonnie Zwaigenbaum, Marni Brownell, Ana Hanlon-Dearman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundAutistic people face elevated mental health risks, yet few population-based studies have quantified these risks across developmental stages. We examined the prevalence and relative risk of physician-diagnosed mental health disorders and suicide attempts among autistic individuals in the province of Manitoba, Canada, using linked administrative health data.MethodsWe conducted a whole-population cohort study using provincial health records from 2018 to 2022. Autistic individuals were identified using a validated case definition and compared to all other Manitobans without autism. We assessed five-year prevalence and sex- and age-adjusted relative risks of attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), conduct disorder, mood/anxiety disorders, schizophrenia, substance use disorders, and suicide attempts across three age groups (6–12, 13–17, and 18–45 years) using regression models.FindingsAutistic people had substantially higher prevalence of all examined mental health disorders (except substance use disorder) and significantly elevated relative risks across every age group we examined. Compared to non-autistic individuals, autistic people had higher risks of ADHD (age- and sex-adjusted relative risk [aRR] 5.24, 95% confidence interval [CI] 4.82–5.70), conduct disorder (aRR 5.96, 95% CI 4.61–7.70), mood/anxiety disorders (aRR 2.00, 95% CI 1.89–2.12), schizophrenia (aRR 9.82, 95% CI 8.94–10.78), and attempted suicide (aRR 3.14, 95% CI 2.31–4.27). The study cohort included n = 317,073 males and n = 314,261 females. Autistic males (n = 4523) had higher risk of being diagnosed with ADHD (aRR 1.39, 95% CI 1.35–1.43), conduct disorder (aRR 1.54, 95% CI 1.34–1.78), and schizophrenia (aRR 1.83, 95% CI 1.71–1.96), whereas autistic females (n = 1377) had higher risk of mood/anxiety disorders (aRR 1.96, 95% CI 1.87–2.00) and attempted suicide (aRR 2.22, 95% CI 2.00–2.44).InterpretationAutistic individuals experience disproportionate mental health challenges, including suicide risk, across their life course. These findings demonstrate the urgent need for sex-specific, autism-informed mental health strategies, early routine screening, and specialized clinical training to improve diagnostic assessment and care delivery in universal health systems.FundingWe gratefully acknowledge the support of the Azrieli Foundation, the National Centre for Autism Collaboration, the Autism Alliance of Canada, and the Canadian Institutes of Health Research.Keywords: Autism, Mental health, Child, Adolescent, Adult, Administrative data

Research in context

Evidence before this study

We searched PubMed and Google Scholar from inception to January 31, 2025, using combinations of the terms “autism” or “autistic”, “mental health”, “psychiatric disorder”, “prevalence”, “epidemiology”, and “suicide” in English. We included population-based studies, meta-analyses, and large cohort studies examining mental health outcomes among autistic individuals in our review. Most existing evidence focuses on small clinical samples, cross-sectional designs, or single disorder outcomes, with limited stratification by age or sex. While several studies report elevated rates of depression, anxiety, and suicidality among autistic youth and adults, few have used whole-population administrative data to assess co-occurring psychiatric conditions across developmental stages, particularly within a universal healthcare system.

Added value of this study

To our knowledge, this is one of the largest population-based studies to examine the prevalence and relative risks of physician-diagnosed mental health disorders and suicide attempts among autistic individuals across childhood, adolescence, and early adulthood. Using linked administrative health data from a Canadian province with universal healthcare coverage, this study offers robust estimates of psychiatric comorbidity using real-world data. Our analysis provides evidence of the early emergence and persistence of mental health challenges in autistic people, with distinct sex-based patterns, and highlights elevated risks for suicide attempts, particularly for women.

Implications of all the available evidence

Our results reinforce the urgent need for autism-informed mental health strategies and targeted intervention across the lifespan. The high burden of psychiatric conditions and suicide risk among autistic individuals demands greater clinical awareness, improved screening, and training for mental health professionals in both pediatric and adult care. These findings can inform policy and health system planning to ensure that autistic people receive timely, appropriate, and equitable mental health supports.

Introduction

Autism is a lifelong neurodevelopmental condition characterized by differences in social communication and the presence of restricted and repetitive behaviours or interests.1 The clinical presentation of autism and the support needs of autistic individuals are highly variable,2,3 with many also experiencing co-occurring mental health disorders that complicate diagnosis and care delivery (see Panel 1).5, 6, 7 Mental health disorders contribute to elevated risks for morbidity,7 reduced quality of life,8 and premature mortality9 among autistic individuals, yet remain frequently overlooked and insufficiently addressed. Although an estimated 1–2% of the global population is autistic,1 healthcare systems continue to fall short in meeting their complex and evolving mental health needs.10,11

Language statement.

Language used to describe autism is evolving and reflects a range of personal and community preferences. While some autistic people choose to use person-first language (e.g., “person with autism”) to emphasize their personhood, many autistic self-advocates prefer identity-first language (e.g., “autistic person”) to affirm autism as a core aspect of their identity. In this paper, we use identity-first language in alignment with the preferences expressed by many self advocates, while recognizing that individual preferences vary. For more information, see the Autism Alliance of Canada Language Guide.4

While the importance of mental health in autistic individuals is increasingly acknowledged, previous studies on the prevalence of mental health disorders rely on clinical samples or self-reported data and are constrained by small sample sizes, narrow age ranges, and insufficient population coverage.12,13 As a result, they often miss population-level patterns in mental health diagnoses, including how risks vary by age and sex, how frequently co-occurring conditions are recognized within health systems, and which groups may be underserved or overlooked in routine care. Population-based administrative health data offer an opportunity to examine real-world mental health diagnoses at scale. These data reflect physician-identified conditions captured in routine care and can reveal age- and sex-specific trends in psychiatric diagnosis, service use, and risk for severe outcomes such as suicide and suicide attempts. Such system-level insights are critical to informing the design of mental health care models that are timely, equitable, and developmentally appropriate for autistic people.8,14

This study uses linked administrative health data from the province of Manitoba, Canada, to estimate the five-year prevalence and age- and sex-adjusted relative risks of physician-diagnosed mental health disorders and suicide attempts among autistic and non-autistic individuals aged 6–45 years. By generating population-based, clinically recorded estimates by age and sex, this study aims to improve understanding of the distribution of diagnosed mental health disorders among autistic people and to support the development of targeted, neuro-affirming mental health strategies across the life course.

Methods

Study setting

This study was conducted in Manitoba, a central Canadian province with a population of approximately 1.4 million.15 Approximately 65% of the population lives in urban centres, while the remainder live in smaller rural or remote communities. Like all Canadian provinces and territories, Manitoba has a publicly funded universal health insurance system that provides access to medically necessary primary care and hospital care without out-of-pocket costs at the point of use. However, many forms of advanced or specialized care are available only in the larger towns and cities, creating challenges in providing equitable care to all residents.

Manitoba is internationally recognized for its rich administrative data infrastructure, which allows for robust and innovative population-based research.16,17 Through the Manitoba Population Research Data Repository, researchers can access de-identified, linkable, individual-level data across health, education, social services, and justice sectors for nearly the entire provincial population. This comprehensive, longitudinal system enables the study of real-world health service use, diagnostic patterns, and outcomes across the life course. Leveraging these data, our study examines the prevalence and relative risk of clinically diagnosed mental health disorders and suicide attempts among autistic people compared with their non-autistic peers. The depth and quality of the data enable us to uncover powerful insights with relevance for broader health system planning and mental health service delivery in autism within Canada and internationally.

Data sources

The data for this study are from the Manitoba Population Research Data Repository housed at the Manitoba Centre for Health Policy (MCHP), University of Manitoba. The Repository contains administrative, registry, survey, and other types of data on virtually all (>99.9%) residents of Manitoba through contacts with the health system, social services, public education system, and justice system. A few groups are incompletely identified in the Repository, including Indigenous Peoples living on reserves, military personnel and individuals incarcerated in federal prisons. All of the Repository data records are de-identified (names and addresses removed). Individual-level linkage across databases is achieved through scrambled Personal Health Identification Numbers (PHINs) attached to each record.16,17 MCHP uses an established data quality framework to evaluate and document the accuracy, completeness, and reliability of administrative databases in the Repository, employing specialized macros to rapidly assess incoming data, identify missing values, invalid codes, and outliers, and ensure that all databases are research-ready.18

The specific databases used in this study include the Manitoba Health Insurance Registry, which contains sociodemographic information on all Manitobans eligible to receive health services and is the central registry used for linkage via the scrambled PHINs; the Hospital Discharge Abstract Database, which contains data on in-patient hospitalizations, diagnoses, and procedures; physician billing claims, which capture ambulatory visits to primary care providers and specialist physicians, such as psychiatrists; and data on clinical diagnoses of autism from the Rehabilitation Centre for Children (RCC), a community-based health centre in Winnipeg, Manitoba, providing specialized rehabilitation, developmental, and diagnostic programs and services to children and youth with a variety of health diagnoses and conditions, including autism. We also used social assistance and education databases to help identify people with intellectual or developmental disabilities. Lastly, we accessed public use Canada Census files on average neighbourhood income to examine small area-level income in the study population.15

Autism definition

Our case definition for autism was developed from the 9th and 10th revisions of the International Classification of Disease (ICD) coding system in collaboration with mental health clinicians and clinician researchers in Manitoba. It was also informed by the case definition published by the pan-Canadian group of experts who comprise Health Canada’s Chronic Disease Surveillance System (CCDSS).19 We defined autism as having one or more hospitalizations with an ICD-10-CA code of ICD-F84.0 (childhood autism), F84.1 (atypical autism), F84.2 (Rett’s syndrome), F84.3 (other childhood disintegrative disorder), F84.4 (overactive disorder associated with mental retardation and stereotyped movements), F84.5 (Asperger’s syndrome), F84.8 (other pervasive developmental disorders), or F84.9 (pervasive developmental disorder, unspecified), or two or more primary care or specialist physician visits within two years with an ICD-9 code of 299 (pervasive developmental disorder). Following the CCDSS’s guidance, we included all F84.X codes in our definition to ensure that conditions historically classified as pervasive developmental disorders are captured by the algorithm.19 We tested and validated this algorithm against the clinical diagnostic data from the RCC and obtained a sensitivity of 77.4%.20

Study population

The study population was derived from the Manitoba Health Insurance Registry (Fig. 1). We first identified all Manitobans who were aged 6–45 years from 2018 to 2022, which is the observation period within which we examined mental health diagnoses and covers the most recent years of administrative health data to which we had access at the time of analysis. Within this population, we identified two groups of people: 1) autistic people, i.e., anyone who met our definition of autism and/or had a clinical diagnosis of autism in the RCC data from 1984 until the end of the observation period; 2) non-autistic people, i.e., all other Manitobans, except for people diagnosed with an intellectual or developmental disability (see Supplemental Table S1 for more details). We excluded individuals with an intellectual disability from the non-autistic group, because although autism is distinct from intellectual disability, there can be overlap in the way these neurodevelopmental conditions present, such as having difficulty with verbal communication. We did not limit the study population to only those whose autism diagnosis occurred before a mental health diagnosis, because all autistic individuals are autistic from birth, regardless of when their diagnosis occurs. Finally, we excluded people who did not have health coverage for the entire observation period of 2018–2022 from both groups.

Variables

Covariates

We examined the following sociodemographic characteristics: age (assessed at the middle of the study period, on July 1, 2020), biological sex at birth (male/female), urban or rural residency (‘urban’ included the population centres of Winnipeg and Brandon; all other postal codes were deemed ‘rural’), and income quintiles (based on neighbourhood-level income).21

Outcome variables

Data algorithms to identify mental health disorders in child, adolescent, and adult populations were developed in our previous work.22 The administrative data definitions for these disorders (ADHD, conduct disorder, mood/anxiety disorders, schizophrenia, substance use disorder) and suicide attempts are presented in Supplemental Table S1. Mood/anxiety disorders were grouped together because of limited specificity in the ICD codes used to define them.

The outcomes were examined across three age strata according to the age groups in which each disorder was likely to be diagnosed: ADHD was assessed in individuals aged 6–45 years; conduct disorder in individuals aged 6–17 years; schizophrenia in people aged 13–45 years; and mood/anxiety disorders, substance use disorders, and attempted suicides in 13–45 year olds. We initially examined completed suicides as well, but rates were low enough in the autistic study group that all values would have been suppressed in accordance with provincial requirements for protecting personal health information, and so we did not include them in the study.

Handling of missing data

We did not remove any individuals from the cohort due to missing data. All of the sociodemographic variables (except for income quintile) were available for all members of the study population. The income quintile calculation is based on postal code and some postal codes were not available – this sometimes occurs when the postal code is associated with a Child Protection Services residence or if the individual is a ward of the province. These individuals represented 0.4% of our total study population; they were included in the mental health outcome analyses, but not the income quintile calculations. For the outcome variables, Ns depended on whether a mental health disorder was diagnosed or not; the denominators for each outcome are reported below.

Analysis

We generated descriptive sociodemographic characteristics for the study population, providing counts and percentages of autistic and non-autistic Manitobans’ age, sex, urban and rural residency, and income quintiles. We calculated crude five-year prevalence estimates and adjusted relative risks (aRR) with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) of mental health disorders and suicide attempts for autistic and non-autistic Manitobans. Confidence intervals were generated from a saturated generalized linear model with Poisson distribution and log link with the natural log of the population as an offset to ensure the standard error calculated was appropriate for rates of events. To confirm an independent association between autism and attempted suicide, we conducted a sensitivity analysis by calculating the relative risk of suicide attempts among autistic individuals, adjusting only for mood/anxiety disorders and ADHD as covariates.

To examine differences between age groups and males/females, we calculated crude five-year prevalence estimates by age group (6–12, 13–17, and 18–45 years) and sex (male/female). The aRR were generated from models controlling for autism diagnosis and age group and models controlling for autism diagnosis and sex, respectively. Adjusted relative risks were modeled using generalized linear regression models with a log link and either a Poisson or negative binomial distribution with the log of the population of each age or sex stratum as the offset. Canada Census subdivisions were included as a random effect to control for potential correlation between observations due geographical clustering of autism cases. We selected the type of model based on the dispersion of the data (Poisson if the variance was equal to the mean; negative binomial if the variance was greater than the mean to account for overdispersion) and assessed model fit using the Likelihood Ratio Test and the Akaike Information Criterion.

Potential confounders were identified using a Directed Acyclic Graph (Supplemental Fig. S1) and were addressed to the extent possible by adjusting for and/or stratifying by covariates (age, sex, urban/rural geography, income quintile), as well as by using the Canadian Index of Multiple Deprivation23 (CIMD) to examine prevalence and risk of the outcomes across quintiles. The CIMD is an area-based index composed of four dimensions: residential instability, economic dependency, ethno-cultural composition, and situational vulnerability. It uses data from the Canada Census to measure socioeconomic inequalities and deprivation across different geographical areas. All analyses were done in SAS Version 9.4 between the dates of January 1, 2024, and October 31, 2025.24

Ethics approval and consent for data

The study received approvals from the University of Manitoba’s Health Research Ethics Board (HS25757–H2022:364, initially approved November 18, 2022), the Provincial Health Research Privacy Committee (No. P2022-126), and the data providers, including Manitoba Health and Long-Term Care, Manitoba Education & Early Childhood Learning, Manitoba Families, and the Rehabilitation Centre for Children in Winnipeg, Manitoba. With regard to privacy and confidentiality, under provincial legislation, individual patients or participants must give consent for disclosure and use of their data in research when direct contact with these individuals is anticipated. However, the University of Manitoba’s Human Research Ethics Board waived the requirement for individual consent because the study employs secondary use of data, and therefore there is no direct contact with patients or participants; as well, several additional measures (such as de-identification of the data and both physical and digital limitations to accessing the data used in this study) have been taken to protect individual privacy. More details on privacy and protection measures at MCHP are available here.16

Results

The study population comprised 5900 autistic people and 625,434 non-autistic people aged 6–45 years during the five-year study period of Jan 1, 2018–December 31, 2022 (Fig. 1). Their sociodemographic characteristics are presented in Table 1. The group of autistic individuals had more 6–12 year olds than the non-autistic group (33.9% versus 17.6%), whereas the non-autistic group had more adults (70.5% versus 48.9%). The autistic group was predominantly male (76.7%) versus an even male-female distribution in the non-autistic group (50% male). A greater proportion of autistic than non-autistic individuals (70.2% versus 59.6%) lived in urban areas. Although income quintile distributions were similar across groups, a modest socioeconomic gradient was observed among autistic people when comparing lower versus higher income quintiles.

Table 2 presents the crude five-year prevalence and adjusted relative risks of mental health disorders among autistic and non-autistic people from 2018 to 2022. Across nearly every diagnostic category, autistic people had substantially higher prevalence and higher adjusted relative risks of mental health disorders. ADHD was diagnosed in 43.3% of autistic individuals aged 6–45 years compared to 8.11% of non-autistic individuals (age- and sex-adjusted relative risk [aRR]: 5.24, 95% CI: 4.82–5.70). Similarly elevated risks were observed for conduct disorder (11.0% versus 1.78%; aRR: 5.96, 95% CI: 4.61–7.70) in 6–17 year olds, mood/anxiety disorders (53.9% versus 26.6%; aRR: 2.00, 95% CI: 1.89–2.12) in 13–45 year olds, and schizophrenia (7.05% versus 0.71%; aRR: 9.82, 95% CI: 8.94–10.78) in 13–45 year olds. Suicide attempts occurred in 0.69% of autistic individuals aged 13–45 years versus 0.22% of their non-autistic peers, yielding an aRR of 3.14 (95% CI: 2.31–4.27). There were no statistically significant group differences for substance use disorders. In the sensitivity analysis examining whether there was an independent association between autism and suicide attempts, the adjusted relative risk of attempted suicide was 1.96 (95% CI 1.69–2.27), which reflects a smaller but still statistically significant difference between autistic and non-autistic Manitobans.

In Table 3, we present crude five-year prevalence estimates by sex and age group (6–12, 13–17, and 18–45 years) and aRRs in models controlling for age, sex and autism diagnosis. As an example, among children aged 6–12 years, ADHD was diagnosed in 39.6% of autistic males and 35.5% of autistic females, compared to 13.0% and 6.88% of their non-autistic peers, respectively. Autistic adolescents and adults also had a much higher prevalence of ADHD than those without autism. The aRRs of being diagnosed with ADHD demonstrate that the younger two age groups were at higher risk (aRR 1.47, 95% CI 1.38–1.56 for 6–12 year olds; aRR 1.68, 95% CI 1.59–1.79 for 13–17 year olds) compared to the older group of 18–45 year olds. After adjusting for age, autistic individuals had 5.45 times higher risk (95% CI 5.02–5.91) of ADHD diagnosis than their non-autistic counterparts. In the comparison between sexes, males were at higher risk of ADHD than females (aRR 1.39, 95% CI 1.35–1.43 for males), and after adjusting for sex, autistic individuals had 4.51 (95% CI 4.28–4.75) times higher risk of ADHD diagnosis than their non-autistic counterparts.

Across age and sex strata, autistic individuals had consistently elevated risks for nearly all of the other mental health disorders examined, with the greatest relative disparities in the age-adjusted aRRs for conduct disorder (aRR: 8.67, 95% CI: 7.07–10.64) and schizophrenia (aRR: 10.78, 95% CI: 8.73–13.32). Their risk of a suicide attempt was also markedly elevated, with autistic individuals experiencing twice the age-adjusted risk of non-autistic individuals (aRR: 2.16, 95% CI: 1.36–3.44) and 4.5 times the sex-adjusted risk (aRR: 4.54, 95% CI: 3.81–5.42). Males had greater risk of being diagnosed with ADHD (aRR 1.39, 95% CI 1.35–1.43), conduct disorder (aRR 1.54, 95% CI 1.34–1.78), schizophrenia (aRR 1.83, 95% CI 1.71–1.96), and substance use disorders (aRR 1.14, 95% CI 1.06–1.22), while females had higher risk of mood/anxiety disorders (aRR 1.96, 95% CI 1.87–2.00) and suicide attempts (aRR 2.22, 95% CI 2.00–2.44). These patterns underscore the need for age- and sex-responsive mental health strategies.

Supplemental Table S2a–d presents data on prevalence and risk of mental health disorders among autistic and non-autistic Manitobans using the CIMD.24 These tables are formatted similarly to Table 3 but present prevalence and relative risk across quintiles for each CIMD dimension. To briefly summarize the findings in these tables, in two of the CIMD dimensions (economic deprivation and ethno-cultural composition), lower deprivation among autistic people was associated with higher aRRs of ADHD, conduct disorder, and mood/anxiety disorders – that is, among those experiencing lower deprivation, there were more diagnoses of these disorders. Meanwhile, lower residential stability among autistic people was associated with lower aRRs of all six disorders. The aRRs for situational vulnerability were mixed. Notably, across all dimensions, the aRR of a mental health disorder diagnosis among autistic people was still several fold higher than among non-autistic people for all disorders except substance use disorder; the highest aRR among the disorders was schizophrenia (9–10 fold higher among autistic individuals).

Discussion

This population-based cohort study found that autistic children, adolescents, and adults face substantially elevated risks of mental health disorders and suicide attempts compared to their non-autistic peers. We observed pronounced age-adjusted disparities for ADHD, conduct disorder, and schizophrenia in autistic individuals. These conditions carry a high clinical burden and warrant efforts towards early detection and tailored intervention across the lifespan. Risk of a suicide attempt was also markedly elevated among autistic individuals, with more than three times the risk of non-autistic peers. Autistic females had more than double the risk of attempting suicide compared to autistic males. Males were more likely to receive diagnoses of ADHD, conduct disorder, schizophrenia, and substance use disorder, while females were more likely to receive diagnoses of mood/anxiety disorders. These findings underscore the importance of integrating age and sex considerations into mental health screening and care pathways for autistic individuals to better address the complexity and heterogeneity of psychiatric needs across the lifespan.

Several previous studies have reported elevated rates of mental health comorbidity among autistic individuals, particularly for anxiety, depression, and ADHD.13,26,27 However, most existing evidence comes from small clinical or self-selected samples that are limited in generalizability and may underrepresent autistic people who are not receiving specialist care.28,29 Our findings are consistent with this literature and contribute new population-based estimates using administrative health data from a universal healthcare system. Our use of linked administrative health records allows for whole-population ascertainment and avoids recall and selection bias, providing more representative estimates of diagnosed mental health disorders. The eight-fold higher risk of conduct disorder and ten-fold higher risk of schizophrenia among autistic individuals reflect substantial clinical burden that has not been well characterized in prior literature. While some earlier studies have suggested increased risk of suicidality among autistic females,6,10 few have quantified this risk using large, whole-population datasets with physician-recorded data. Our analyses build on the existing evidence base and suggest that mental health risks among autistic individuals are not uniform, but instead vary meaningfully by age and sex, with important implications for screening and service delivery.

Several different mechanisms may contribute to the elevated rates of mental health disorders observed in autistic individuals. Autistic people may experience chronic stress due to social exclusion, sensory overload, and unmet support needs, all of which can increase vulnerability to mental health disorders over time.30,31 Delays in diagnosis, lack of autism-informed care, and diagnostic overshadowing may further exacerbate distress or lead to undertreatment. Sex differences in diagnosis and service use are also relevant; for example, autism in females may be under-recognized in early development and distress may be masked with camouflaging behaviors until symptoms escalate.32 This mechanism may partly explain the significantly elevated suicide attempt risk among autistic females compared to males in our study. Often, behavioural factors related to regulation of emotions, peer dynamics, and executive functioning are treated as singular issues rather than evolving developmental concerns, but they can contribute to conditions such as conduct disorder and schizophrenia, particularly in adolescence and adulthood, which emphasizes the need for ongoing specialized care.33 As well, health system factors such as differences in clinical recognition and diagnostic practices likely shape patterns of recorded diagnoses and contribute to observed disparities between autistic and non-autistic people’s mental health.34

The elevated risk of suicide attempts observed in this study warrants particularly urgent clinical and policy attention. Autistic individuals face disproportionately high rates of suicidality, often stemming from experiences of bullying, trauma, marginalization, and unmet mental health support needs.11,35 Existing suicide prevention strategies frequently overlook autistic individuals or fail to account for the distinct stressors they face.36 In this study, suicide attempts were more than three times as common among autistic individuals than their non-autistic peers, and autistic females were at significantly higher risk than autistic males. The disparity in risk between males and females has been underexplored in research thus far and may reflect cumulative impacts of diagnostic delays, unsupported distress, and gendered expectations. Our findings indicate a need for targeted suicide prevention approaches that are both autism-informed and sex/gender-specific, with improved identification, screening, and therapeutic support tailored to the lived experiences of autistic individuals.

The results of this study have direct implications for clinical practice and mental health policy. The elevated risks observed across developmental stages point to the need for integrated, early, and ongoing mental health screening within autism services. Mental health concerns should be considered core components of autism care. Clinicians may require enhanced training to recognize and respond to psychiatric symptoms in autistic individuals, including non-traditional presentations and differences in emotional expression. The high prevalence of diagnoses such as ADHD, conduct disorder, and mood/anxiety disorders among adolescents highlights the need for proactive school- and community-based supports, especially during transition periods. Suicide prevention efforts must be inclusive of autistic individuals and incorporate approaches that address both individual risk factors and the broader social and systemic contributors to distress.

From a systems perspective, this study demonstrates the value of using administrative health data to generate whole-population insights that can inform service planning and policy development. Mental health and neurodevelopmental services must be better coordinated, with clinical pathways that reflect developmental stages and accommodate specific risks for males and females. Inclusive surveillance systems and performance indicators that track mental health outcomes in autistic populations would support more responsive, data-informed policy. In essence, addressing mental health needs in autism care will require not only clinical innovation but also structural reform to reduce the diagnostic barriers and promote equitable access to supports.

Although this study has several notable strengths, such as the use of physician-diagnosed data from a large, population-based cohort within a universal healthcare system, some limitations also warrant mention. These include reliance on diagnostic coding, which has limited specificity and can result in misclassification or omission of cases, particularly for individuals who are undiagnosed, underdiagnosed, or experience subclinical symptoms. Because this study includes only individuals who received formal diagnoses through the health system, it likely underestimates the true burden of mental health symptoms among autistic people. This may be particularly true among the older individuals in the cohort, whose autism and/or mental health symptoms may not have been readily recognized or diagnosed until more recent years. Suicide attempts were defined based on events resulting in hospitalization or medical intervention, which excludes ideation and non-medically treated events. Analyses were restricted to individuals aged 6–45 years due to important limitations in data comparability and completeness among older individuals. Earlier cohorts would have been subject to differential case ascertainment and potential misclassification (e.g., use of ICD-8, in which autism was classified differently than in later versions such as ICD-9) and incomplete coverage of mental health data over longer historical periods, limiting the validity of estimates in older populations. Our adjustment strategy was limited by the variables available in the Data Repository; as shown in Supplemental Fig. S1, it would have been helpful to include family or early life variables describing parental mental health and education levels, as these are likely to influence whether a child is diagnosed with autism and whether they receive additional supports. The study does not reflect diagnostic trajectories or timing of onset of mental health disorders, and the absence of data on race, ethnicity, or broader structural determinants of health limits our ability to examine equity-related disparities or intersectional risk patterns in detail. However, the data in Supplemental Table S2 (mental health prevalence and adjusted risks by the four components of the Canadian Index for Multiple Deprivation) provide some understanding of how autism and mental disorders are linked to residential instability, income, and ethnicity. Future research should explore longitudinal trajectories of mental health diagnoses among autistic individuals and examine the impact of early intervention, service access, and structural determinants of health. Integrating social and race/ethnicity data, along with lived experience perspectives, will be essential to better inform equitable, responsive mental health strategies.

This study provides robust, population-based evidence of elevated physician-diagnosed mental health disorders and suicide attempts among autistic individuals across childhood, adolescence, and early adulthood. Our findings underscore the urgent need for autism-informed, developmentally appropriate, and sex/gender-specific mental health strategies. Earlier identification, targeted prevention, and more accessible, integrated care across the life course are essential to addressing unmet needs and advancing equity in mental health care.
