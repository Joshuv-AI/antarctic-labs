Thermal Evolution of the Central Star in Pa 30
Anthony L. Piro, Yossef Zenati, Tin Long Sunny Wong

Abstract
Pa 30 has been identified as the nebular remnant of the historical SN 1181. It is host to a hot ( ≈ 200,000 ​ K \approx 200,000\,{\rm K} ) central star (WD J005311) with a fast wind ( ≈ 16,000 ​ km ​ s − 1 \approx 16,000\,{\rm km\,s^{-1}} ) radiating at roughly the Eddington luminosity for a solar mass ( ≈ 1.5 × 10 38 ​ erg ​ s − 1 \approx 1.5\times 10^{38}\,{\rm erg\,s^{-1}} ). We explore the thermal evolution of this star to understand how it progressed toward the state it is observed as today as well as to constrain its underlying physical properties. We develop a semi-analytic two-component model, which approximates the central star as a hot radiating envelope contracting and cooling above a relatively cool core. Comparing this model with the observed luminosity and radius requires a core mass M c ≈ 1.15 − 1.4 ​ M ⊙ M_{c}\approx 1.15-1.4\,M_{\odot} with a core radius R c ≈ ( 6 − 8 ) × 10 8 ​ M ⊙ R_{c}\approx(6-8)\times 10^{8}\,M_{\odot} , and a hot envelope mass Δ ​ M ≈ 0.02 − 0.04 ​ M ⊙ \Delta M\approx 0.02-0.04\,M_{\odot} . The small envelope mass is the best constrained of these parameters due to the need to reach the observed radius of ≈ 0.15 ​ R ⊙ \approx 0.15\,R_{\odot} in a timescale of ≈ 845 ​ yrs \approx 845\,{\rm yrs} . These results favor a picture where SN 1181 involved the merger of O/Ne and C/O white dwarfs, and where the majority of the latter was ejected in the explosion. We also explore which models ignite carbon burning at the base of the hot envelope, demonstrating that this is possible but not necessarily required to explain the current thermal state of the central star.



I
Introduction

IRAS 00500+6713 (Pa 30) was identified in the WISE mid-IR archive by Dana Patchick
(
Cutri & et al. 2012
;
Kronberger et al. 2016
)
and cataloged in HASH
(
Parker et al. 2017
)
. Follow-up observations revealed an extremely hot and luminous central source (WD J005311) devoid of H and He, with emission lines in C and O that imply a wind velocity of
\approx 16,000\,{\rm km\,s^{-1}}
and a mass loss rate of
\sim 10^{-6}\,M_{\odot}\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1}

(
Gvaramadze et al. 2019
)
. Subsequent work connected Pa 30 to the historical SN 1181, implying a current age of
\approx 845\,{\rm yrs}

(
Ritter et al. 2021
;
Lykou et al. 2023
;
Fesen et al. 2023
;
Schaefer 2023
)
. Mapping of filaments reveals ballistic ejecta at
\sim 10^{3}\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}
, a large inner cavity, and a sharply bounded, filamentary shell, largely consistent with the 1181 explosion date
(
Cunningham et al. 2024
)
.

Due to the relatively dim maximum brightness, together with the low expansion velocities, it has been argued that Pa 30 was the result of a subluminous class of Type Ia supernovae, called SNe Iax
(
Li et al. 2003
;
Foley et al. 2013
;
Jha 2017
)
. This is also consistent with X-ray studies, which reveal large neon, magnesium, silicon, and sulfur enrichment of the central star and the nebula
(
Oskinova et al. 2020
)
and requires a low explosion energy
(
Ko et al. 2024
;
Shao et al. 2025
)
. A further connection is that SNe Iax do not transition to a nebular phase like normal SN Ia
(
Foley et al. 2016
;
Kawabata et al. 2018
, e.g.,)
, likely because these explosions leave some sort of bound central star with a wind
(
Shen & Schwab 2017
, which could be driven by delayed radioactive decay for the first few years to decades,)
.

The nebula composition and near-Eddington luminosity favors thermonuclear burning in a roughly solar-mass system, but the outflow speeds of
\approx 16,000\,{\rm km\,s^{-1}}
far exceed the escape velocity of a white dwarf (WD). This disfavors a purely radiatively-driven wind, and points to a magnetocentrifugal flinging
(
Kashiyama et al. 2019
;
Zhong et al. 2024
)
. A natural way to produce such a fast spin
\sim 0.1\,{\rm s}^{-1}
and strong magnetic field
\sim 10^{8}\,{\rm G}
is via a double degenerate merger
(
García-Berro et al. 2012
;
Schwab et al. 2012
;
Kilic et al. 2021
, e.g.,)
. This fits with growing evidence that at least some SNe Iax occur from mergers
(
Karambelkar et al. 2021
, e.g.,)
, while others are associated with WDs with stellar companions
(
McCully et al. 2014
;
Foley et al. 2014
)
. In still other cases, only limits could be placed on a companion
(
Foley et al. 2015
;
Zimmerman et al. 2026
)
, so low-mass main-sequence or He-star stars companion, as well as double degenerate systems, are all possible.

From the above collection of properties for Pa 30, a general picture is developing. A double degenerate merger occurred, leading to a non-terminal explosion that ejected many tenths of a solar mass in a low energy outburst. The burning likely proceeded as a deflagration that initiated somewhere near the merged product of the two WDs. The current configuration is thus a hot layer consisting of some combination of the burned and unburned material from the less massive WD as well as fallback material that was not completely unbound in the low energy explosion
(e.g.,
Jordan et al. 2012
;
Kromer et al. 2013
;
Fink et al. 2014
;
Long et al. 2014
, which find that
\sim 0.01-0.2\,M_{\odot}
of burned material may be retained in a deflagration)
. This sits on top of a relatively cooler core of the more massive WD, although this may have been heated somewhat depending on the details of where and how the deflagration took place. Such configurations of mass and energy have been studied a number of times
(
Schwab et al. 2012
;
Schwab 2021
;
Wu et al. 2022
;
Yao et al. 2023
;
Ko et al. 2026
, e.g.,)
. Here we approach this problem with a semi-analytic framework
(
Eddington 1926
;
Hansen & Kawaler 1994
, similar to the so-called Eddington model but applied to a layer, e.g.,)
. This allows us to explore a wide parameter space of models and better understand what underlying physical characteristics of the central star are constrained by the observed properties.

In Section
II
, we present our semi-analytic model for the Pa 30 central star along with an exploration varying various properties of the envelope and core lead to different observational consequences. In Section
III
, we discuss whether carbon burning is expected to be ignited in the hot surface layers and what impact it may have on the evolution. In Section
IV
, we explore what properties of the Pa 30 central star can be best constrained through this modeling, and in Section
V
, we summarize our results as well as discuss extensions of this work for the future.

Hot Envelope Model

Our general picture of the central star of Pa 30 is summarized in Figure

II.1
Hot Envelope Model Framework

Flux limited diffusion through the envelope requires

\displaystyle L=-\frac{16\pi r^{2}acT^{3}}{3\kappa\rho}\frac{dT}{dr},

(1)

where
a
is the radiation constant and
\kappa
is the opacity. Hydrostatic equilibrium is given by
dP/dr=-\rho GM_{c}/r^{2}
, where we approximate
\Delta M\ll M_{c}
(this is found to be consistent with what we constrain for Pa 30). Combining this with Equation (
1
) results in

\displaystyle L=\frac{16\pi GM_{c}acT^{3}}{3\kappa}\frac{dT}{dP}.

(2)

The luminosity will generally be near the Eddington luminosity

\displaystyle L_{\rm Edd}=\frac{4\pi GM_{c}c}{\kappa},

(3)

so it is helpful to parameterize the envelope solutions by the Eddington ratio
\chi\equiv L/L_{\rm Edd}
1
1

1

Note that one can also interpret
\chi
as the ratio of radiation pressure to total pressure, so that
\chi=aT^{4}/(3P)
and
1-\chi=\rho k_{\rm B}T/(\mu m_{p}P)
.
. Integrating Equation (
2
) with
L
set constant results in a temperature profile

\displaystyle T(P)=\chi^{1/4}\left(\frac{3P}{a}\right)^{1/4}.

(4)

We assume that
\kappa
is constant because it is roughly given by electron scattering in the hot envelope.

For the equation of state, we consider contributions from both ideal gas and radiation, so that

\displaystyle P=\frac{\rho k_{\rm B}T}{\mu m_{p}}+\frac{aT^{4}}{3},

(5)

where
k_{\rm B}
is the Boltzmann constant,
\mu
is the mean molecular weight, and
m_{p}
is the proton mass. Combining this with Equation (
4
), results in pressure as a function of density of

\displaystyle P(\rho)=\left(\frac{k_{\rm B}}{\mu m_{p}}\right)^{4/3}\left(\frac{3}{a}\right)^{1/3}\frac{\chi^{1/3}}{(1-\chi)^{4/3}}\rho^{4/3}.

(6)

This demonstrates that the envelope is represented by an
n=3
polytrope. This means we can simply use
P=K\rho^{4/3}
, where

\displaystyle K\equiv\left(\frac{k_{\rm B}}{\mu m_{p}}\right)^{4/3}\left(\frac{3}{a}\right)^{1/3}\frac{\chi^{1/3}}{(1-\chi)^{4/3}},

(7)

is a factor (the entropy) that increases as the envelope gets near the Eddington limit.

We can also solve for the thermodynamic properties as a function of radius by using the radiative diffusion equation. Substituting

\displaystyle\rho(T)=\frac{\mu m_{p}}{k_{\rm B}}\frac{aT^{3}}{3}\frac{1-\chi}{\chi}.

(8)

into Equation (
1
) results in

\displaystyle\frac{dT}{dr}=-\frac{\mu m_{p}}{k_{\rm B}}\frac{GM_{c}}{4r^{2}}(1-\chi),

(9)

and integrating this results in

\displaystyle T(r)=\frac{\mu m_{p}}{k_{\rm B}}\frac{GM_{c}}{4}(1-\chi)\left(\frac{1}{r}-\frac{1}{R_{s}}\right).

(10)

Similarly,

\displaystyle\rho(r)=\frac{a}{3}\left(\frac{\mu m_{p}}{k_{\rm B}}\right)^{4}\left(\frac{GM_{c}}{4}\right)^{3}\frac{(1-\chi)^{4}}{\chi}\left(\frac{1}{r}-\frac{1}{R_{s}}\right)^{3}.

(11)

for the density profile within the envelope.

The envelope radius
R_{s}
is found by requiring the envelope to have the correct mass

\displaystyle\Delta M=\int_{R_{c}}^{R_{s}}4\pi r^{2}\rho dr.

(12)

Substituting
\rho(r)
from Equation (
11
) and integrating this expression results in the relationship

\displaystyle\Delta M=

\displaystyle 4\pi B(\chi)\left\{\ln\left(\frac{R_{s}}{R_{c}}\right)\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\frac{R_{c}}{R_{s}}\left[\frac{1}{3}\left(\frac{R_{c}}{R_{s}}\right)^{2}-\frac{3}{2}\left(\frac{R_{c}}{R_{s}}\right)+3\right]-\frac{11}{6}\right\},

where we define

\displaystyle B(\chi)\equiv\frac{a}{3}\left(\frac{\mu m_{p}}{k_{\rm B}}\right)^{4}\left(\frac{GM_{c}}{4}\right)^{3}\frac{(1-\chi)^{4}}{\chi},

(14)

which has units of mass. In practice, we solve Equation (
) numerically to find the surface radius
R_{s}
for a given
\chi
,
\Delta M
,
M_{c}
, and
R_{c}
. In the limit
R_{c}\ll R_{s}
, this simplifies to

\displaystyle R_{s}(\chi)\approx R_{c}\exp\left[\frac{\Delta M}{4\pi B(\chi)}\right],

(15)

so that the surface radius depends exponentially on the amount of mass in the layer. In the opposite limit, when
\Delta M/B(\chi)\ll 1
corresponding to a small envelope mass or
\chi\ll 1
, Equation (
) predicts
R_{s}\approx R_{c}
. This matches our expectation for when the layer has fully cooled and lost all of its pressure support.

Given the relations above, for any given Eddington ratio
\chi
, we can solve for all of the properties of the envelope. This corresponds to a snapshot in time during the envelope’s evolution, where
\chi
goes from high to low values from early to late times. The next important question to answer is, what time does this correspond to? We can estimate this by considering the energy of the layer.

The internal energy of the envelope, including both ideal gas and radiation components, is

\displaystyle E_{\rm int}=\int_{R_{c}}^{R_{s}}\left(\frac{3}{2}\frac{\rho k_{\rm B}T}{\mu m_{p}}+aT^{4}\right)4\pi r^{2}dr.

(16)

Substituting
T(r)
and
\rho(r)
from above, this is integrated to give, to first order in
R_{c}/R_{s}
,

\displaystyle E_{\rm int}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle 4\pi a\left(\frac{\mu m_{p}}{k_{\rm B}}\right)^{4}\left(\frac{GM_{c}}{4}\right)^{4}\frac{(1+\chi)(1-\chi)^{4}}{2\chi}

(17)

\displaystyle\times\frac{1}{R_{c}}\left[1+\frac{10}{3}\frac{R_{c}}{R_{s}}+4\frac{R_{c}}{R_{s}}\ln\left(\frac{R_{c}}{R_{s}}\right)\right],

for the internal energy.

Next, there is the gravitational potential energy of the surface layer, which is

\displaystyle E_{\rm grav}=-\int_{R_{c}}^{R_{s}}\frac{GM_{c}}{r}4\pi r^{2}\rho dr.

(18)

Again substituting
\rho(r)
, we integrate this to find to first order in
R_{c}/R_{s}
that

\displaystyle E_{\rm grav}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle-\frac{16\pi}{3}a\left(\frac{\mu m_{p}}{k_{\rm B}}\right)^{4}\left(\frac{GM_{c}}{4}\right)^{4}\frac{(1-\chi)^{4}}{\chi}

(19)

\displaystyle\times\frac{1}{R_{c}}\left[1+\frac{3}{2}\frac{R_{c}}{R_{s}}+3\frac{R_{c}}{R_{s}}\ln\left(\frac{R_{c}}{R_{s}}\right)\right].

Comparing this with Equation (
17
), in the limit of
R_{c}\ll R_{s}
we see that

\displaystyle E_{\rm grav}\approx-\frac{8}{3}(1+\chi)^{-1}E_{\rm int}.

(20)

In Appendix
A
, we provide a more detailed derivation of the virial relation of a hot layer and a more exact version of Equation (
20
). The total energy of the layer is therefore

\displaystyle E_{\rm tot}=E_{\rm int}+E_{\rm grav}\approx-\left[\frac{8}{3}(1+\chi)^{-1}-1\right]E_{\rm int}.

(21)

Energy conservation tells us

dE_{\rm tot}/dt=-L
, thus

\displaystyle\left[\frac{8}{3}(1+\chi)^{-1}-1\right]\frac{dE_{\rm int}}{dt}=L.

(22)

This expression shows how the internal energy increases with time as the layer compresses and gets hotter.

For the final step, we assume that the internal energy increases like a power law with time as the layer compresses, so that
E_{\rm int}\propto t^{\beta}
. If this is the case, then
dE_{\rm int}/dt=\beta E_{\rm int}/t
. Substituting this into the expression above, we can solve for the time

\displaystyle t\approx\left[\frac{8}{3}(1+\chi)^{-1}-1\right]\beta\frac{E_{\rm in}}{L}.

(23)

As we show below,
\beta\approx 1
is a fairly good approximation, which is what we use for all of our calculation.

The model described above allows us to easily explore the full space of envelope solutions. The main parameters for any given envelope evolution are
M_{c}
,
R_{c}
, and
\Delta M
, and we can explore the evolution by varying
\chi
and then solving for
t
using Equation (
23
). One additional issue is that in a realistic scenario,
R_{s}
will start with some value
R_{s,0}
at early times set by the initial conditions
(
Shen et al. 2012
;
Schwab 2021
, e.g.,)
, while Equation (
15
) implies the envelope radius can become arbitrarily large. This is because at early times (basically before a Kelvin-Helmholtz cooling timescale), the evolution is still dominated by initial conditions and not in a self-similar regime yet. Thus for times when
R_{s}(\chi)>R_{s,0}
, we instead use Equation (
15
) to solve for
\chi_{0}
such that
R_{s}(\chi_{0})=R_{s,0}
, and use this for the envelope profile at these early times.

III
Carbon Burning

As the C/O-rich envelope contracts, carbon burning can turn on abruptly, and act as an additional heat source that slows contraction and helps maintain a near-Eddington luminosity
(
Kashiyama et al. 2019
;
Ko et al. 2026
, e.g.,)
. Here, we explore which models are expected to initiate appreciable carbon burning at the base of the envelope and over what timescales.

For the C-C burning energy generation rate, we use
(
Caughlan & Fowler 1988
;
Kippenhahn et al. 2013
)

\displaystyle\epsilon_{\rm CC}

\displaystyle=1.86\times 10^{43}X_{12}^{2}T_{9}^{-3/2}T_{9a}^{5/6}

\displaystyle\times\exp[-84.165/T_{9a}^{1/3}-2.12\times 10^{-3}T_{9}^{3}]\,{\rm erg\,s^{-1}\,g^{-1}},

where
X_{12}
is the carbon mass fraction,
T_{9}=T/10^{9}\,{\rm K}
, and
T_{9a}=T_{9}/(1+0.0396T_{9})
. We do not include screening corrections due to the relatively low densities in the hot envelope, and we simply set
X_{12}=0.5
. Equation (
) is used to plot the profile of carbon burning energy generation rate in Figure
2
, demonstrating how strongly peaked the burning rate is at the base of the envelope due to its temperature sensitivity.

For each of our models, we also numerically integrate the carbon burning rate to get the total luminosity from carbon burning,

\displaystyle L_{\rm CC}=\int_{R_{c}}^{R_{s}}4\pi r^{2}\rho\epsilon_{\rm CC}dr.

(25)

We expect that once
L_{\rm CC}\approx L
, then carbon burning should pause the contraction of the hot envelope due on a timescale of how long it takes to burn a significant fraction of the carbon in the layer (which would be much longer than the age of Pa 30).

, increasing
M_{c}
leads to earlier carbon ignition because of the larger gravity and correspondingly large base temperature for the hot layer. The dependence on
\Delta M
is more subtle. At high
\Delta M
, the contraction of the envelope is slower because the cooling timescale of the layer becomes longer. At low
\Delta M
, the density at the base of the layer becomes smaller, again leading to a longer time to ignite carbon. The result of these effects is that there is an in-between region, roughly
\Delta M\approx 0.03-0.1\,M_{\odot}
depending on
M_{c}
, where carbon ignites fastest.

In Figure
8
, we fix
\Delta M
and vary the other parameters. The time contours in this case are simpler, indicating that the time to carbon ignition is primarily sensitive to
R_{c}
. This is because when the core is more compact, the layer is deeper in the gravitational potential well and has a hotter base temperature. Thus, determining whether burning occurs can be a sensitive constraint on the core’s thermal state.

In cases where carbon does ignite, we assume that the luminosity and radius
R_{s}
remain fixed for the remainder of the evolution that we are following. This will continue only as long as sufficient carbon fuel remains, but for the timescales we are more interested in, we do not follow the subsequent evolution after the carbon is depleted. Examples of this evolution are shown in Figure
9
, where the specific parameters were simply chosen to show some diversity in evolution.

An important takeaway from this exploration is that carbon burning is not a necessity to explain the thermal state of Pa 30 today. If the layer has a sufficiently small or large envelope mass, or if the core radius is sufficiently large, then we do not expect carbon burning to ignite during the
\approx 845\,{\rm yrs}
that Pa 30 has been evolving. Conversely, as more detailed observations are made of Pa 30, and if they indeed show (through the nucleosynthetic inferences, for example) that burning is necessary, then these types of models will help narrow the parameter space where this is possible.

IV
Constraints on Pa 30

Given the discussion above of how the evolution depends on
M_{c}
,
R_{c}
, and
\Delta M
, we next seek to constrain the values inferred from the current properties of Pa 30 of an age of
\approx 845\,{\rm yrs}
, a luminosity of
\approx 1.5\times 10^{38}\,{\rm erg\,s^{-1}}
, and a photospheric radius
\approx 0.15\,R_{\odot}

(
Gvaramadze et al. 2019
)
. Although we use these values for the fitting, this comes with some caveats. As noted in
Schaefer 2023
, the full spectral energy distribution of the central star in Pa 30 is not measured, so inferences must be made about the bolometric luminosity. Given that we see a dense wind
(
Kashiyama et al. 2019
, e.g.,)
, the observed photosphere may represent a radius within the wind and thus the true stellar photosphere is smaller. The advantage of the framework we present here is that, as these specific values are updated with future measurements and models, we can readily assess how the underlying physical properties of the central star change.

To explore what models fit Pa 30 best, we run a grid of
10^{6}
models varying
M_{c}
,
R_{c}
, and
\Delta M
. We resolve the full time evolution for each one so that we can track the luminosity of carbon burning at each time using Equation (
25
). If at some time
L_{\rm CC}=L
, we assume that the luminosity stops evolving and continue at a constant level for the remainder of time (as shown in the examples in Figure
9
). For each of these models, we then extract the parameters at
t=845\,{\rm yrs}
and calculate a fitting parameter

\displaystyle\chi^{2}=\left(1-R_{s}/R_{s,\rm obs}\right)^{2}+\left(1-L/L_{\rm obs}\right)^{2},

(26)

where
R_{s,\rm obs}
and
L_{\rm obs}
are the observed photospheric radius and luminosity, respectively. This
\chi^{2}
is meant to represent the goodness of fit while keeping both the radius and luminosity on roughly equal footing.

Calculating
\chi^{2}
across all
10^{6}
models that we run, we identify the model with the smallest
\chi^{2}
value, which is
M_{c}=1.3\,M_{\odot}
,
\Delta M=0.038\,M_{\odot}
, and
R_{c}=7.1\times 10^{8}\,{\rm cm}
. These values have interesting implications. First,
M_{c}
is on the higher side for what may be expected for a WD. This is in line with arguments that the primary WD in the event that made SN 1181 was probably an O/Ne WD rather than a C/O WD
(
Siess 2006
;
Doherty et al. 2015
, where a typical boundary between these compositions is
\sim 1.05\,M_{\odot}
,)
. Next, the amount of fallback and other burnt material still left in the hot surface layer is relatively small. Combining this with the
\approx 0.5\,M_{\odot}
of ejecta inferred from modeling the SN remnant X-rays
(
Ko et al. 2024
)
, argues that the secondary mass was
\approx 0.5-0.6\,M_{\odot}
, consistent with a typical C/O WD. Finally, the relatively large
R_{c}
we measure indicates that significant heat entered the core during the merger and the subsequent deflagration that produced SN 1181. These values we find for
M_{c}
,
R_{c}
, and
\Delta M
are roughly consistent with what is found by
Ko et al. 2026
. For their approach, they consider the temperature of the core rather than its radius, but we both agree that the core must be hotter than a zero-temperature WD.

and
R_{c}\approx(6-8)\times 10^{8}\,{\rm cm}
, consistent with a hot O/Ne core.

V
Discussion and Future Work

We explored the thermal evolution of the central star of SN 1181, which now sits at the center of Pa 30. We approximate it as a hot envelope sitting above a colder core. Using a semi-analytic model, we show that the thermal evolution of such a mass configuration mainly depends on three parameters, the mass of the core
M_{c}
, the radius of the core
R_{c}
, and the mass of the hot envelope
\Delta M
. Our semi-analytic framework simplifies our ability to explore the range of evolutions for different sets of parameters. We also discussed carbon burning within the layer and when it is expected to occur.

We fit our model to the current age and state of the Pa 30 central star, and we conclude that the envelope needs to be relatively low mass (
\Delta M\approx 0.02-0.04\,M_{\odot}
) for it to contract to the size that is observed today.
Combining our small envelope mass with the observed mass for the supernova remnant of
\approx 0.5\,M_{\odot}

(
Ko et al. 2024
)
suggests a C/O WD secondary that was mostly ejected in the explosion. There is some degeneracy in the core mass and radius, but the favored values of
M_{c}\approx 1.15-1.4\,M_{\odot}
and
R_{c}\approx(6-8)\times 10^{8}\,{\rm cm}
are consistent with an O/Ne WD primary that is somewhat expanded by heating during the merger and explosive event (deflagration) that produced SN 1181. Our best-fit model reproduces the current luminosity of the central star solely through thermal emission, without invoking carbon burning.

We acknowledge that this model has important caveats. For example, there are uncertainties in the true bolometric luminosity
(
Schaefer 2023
, e.g.,)
and the true radius given the dense wind
(
Kashiyama et al. 2019
, e.g.,)
. The strength of our approach, however, is that we can readily understand how the system’s basic parameters change as these observed properties are updated.

An important piece of physics that is missing from this model is how the radius adjusts when carbon ignites, since this presumably would generate convection, alter the envelope’s density profile, and require tracking the envelope’s entropy, all of which are outside the scope of our framework. We plan to address this shortcoming in future work using full numerical stellar models
(
Ko et al. 2026
, although see the investigation by)
. Our framework also does not include contributions to the pressure in the hot envelope from nonrelativistic degenerate electrons, although we consider this a small correction at these high temperatures.

Going forward, it will be important to understand how the merger, unstable ignition, and subsequent deflagration produced the mass distribution for the central star in Pa 30 observed today. While this will require detailed numerical simulations, the subsequent time evolution of such models can be addressed quickly using the intuition we have developed from the framework presented here. This may also help predict the diversity of hot stars left from SNe Iax that may exist elsewhere in the Milky Way or nearby galaxies.

Appendix A
Virial Relation for a Hot Surface Layer

An important step in deriving the time in Equation (
23
) is understanding the relation between the internal and gravitational energy, i.e., the virial relation. Typically one might assume that
E_{\rm grav}=-2E_{\rm int}
, but this is inconsistent with what we find in Equation (
20
) from direct integration of the energies for a constant luminosity profile. Here we present an alternative derivation that yields the same relation as Equation (
20
), thereby showing that this result is indeed robust.

Starting with hydrostatic balance,
dP/dr=-\rho GM_{c}/r^{2}
, multiplying both sides by
4\pi r^{3}
, and integrating over the hot surface layer,

\displaystyle\int_{R_{c}}^{R_{s}}4\pi r^{3}\frac{dP}{dr}dr=-\int_{R_{c}}^{R_{s}}\rho\frac{GM_{c}}{r}4\pi r^{2}dr.

(A1)

The right side is simply the gravitational potential energy
E_{\rm grav}
. The left side can be integrated by parts,

\displaystyle-4\pi R_{c}^{3}P(R_{c})-3\int_{R_{c}}^{R_{s}}4\pi r^{2}Pdr=E_{\rm grav}.

(A2)

The term on the far left side is a key difference between a hot layer and a full self-gravitating body like a star. In the latter case

\displaystyle\left.4\pi r^{3}P\right|_{R_{c}}^{R_{s}}=0,

(A3)

because
R_{c}=0
and
P(R_{s})\approx 0
. For the hot layer this term is non-zero, which we show ultimately leads to a modified virial relation.

Pressure can be related to energy by

\displaystyle\int_{R_{c}}^{R_{s}}4\pi r^{2}Pdr=\frac{2}{3}E_{\rm gas}+\frac{1}{3}E_{\rm rad},

(A4)

where
E_{\rm gas}
and
E_{\rm rad}
are the contributions to the internal energy from ideal gas and radiation, respectively. Using the analytic envelope solution in the limit
R_{c}\ll R_{s}
, the base-pressure term satisfies so Equation (
A2
) reduces to

\displaystyle-\frac{8}{3}E_{\rm gas}-\frac{4}{3}E_{\rm rad}\approx E_{\rm grav}.

(A5)

To find the energies, we integrate them directly from our profile

\displaystyle E_{\rm gas}=\int_{R_{c}}^{R_{s}}\frac{3}{2}\frac{\rho k_{\rm B}T}{\mu m_{p}}4\pi r^{2}dr\approx 4\pi a\left(\frac{\mu m_{p}}{k_{\rm B}}\right)^{4}\left(\frac{GM_{c}}{4}\right)^{4}\frac{(1-\chi)^{5}}{2\chi}\frac{1}{R_{c}}\left[1+\frac{10}{3}\frac{R_{c}}{R_{s}}+4\frac{R_{c}}{R_{s}}\ln\left(\frac{R_{c}}{R_{s}}\right)\right],

(A6)

and

\displaystyle E_{\rm rad}=\int_{R_{c}}^{R_{s}}aT^{4}4\pi r^{2}dr\approx 4\pi a\left(\frac{\mu m_{p}}{k_{\rm B}}\right)^{4}\left(\frac{GM_{c}}{4}\right)^{4}(1-\chi)^{4}\frac{1}{R_{c}}\left[1+\frac{10}{3}\frac{R_{c}}{R_{s}}+4\frac{R_{c}}{R_{s}}\ln\left(\frac{R_{c}}{R_{s}}\right)\right],

(A7)

where we only keep terms up to first order in the ratio
R_{c}/R_{s}
. Comparing these with
E_{\rm int}
from Equation (
17
), we see that

\displaystyle E_{\rm gas}=\left(\frac{1-\chi}{1+\chi}\right)E_{\rm int}

(A8)

and

\displaystyle E_{\rm rad}=\left(\frac{2\chi}{1+\chi}\right)E_{\rm int}.

(A9)

This matches our expectation that when
\chi=0
then
E_{\rm int}=E_{\rm gas}
and conversely when
\chi=1
then
E_{\rm int}=E_{\rm rad}
. Finally, substituting these into Equation (
A5
), we find

\displaystyle E_{\rm grav}\approx-\frac{8}{3}(1+\chi)^{-1}\left[\frac{1+\frac{10}{3}(R_{c}/R_{s})+4(R_{c}/R_{s})\ln(R_{c}/R_{s})}{1+\frac{3}{2}(R_{c}/R_{s})+3(R_{c}/R_{s})\ln(R_{c}/R_{s})}\right]E_{\rm int}\approx-\frac{8}{3}(1+\chi)^{-1}E_{\rm int},

(A10)

as derived in the main text. For a typical range of values of
\chi\approx 0.4-0.6
, this results in a prefactor on the right side of
(1+\chi)^{-1}8/3\approx 1.7-1.9
.
Thus the gravitational energy is proportionally smaller than the internal energy for a hot layer in comparison to a self-gravitating body (where this factor would be
2
).
