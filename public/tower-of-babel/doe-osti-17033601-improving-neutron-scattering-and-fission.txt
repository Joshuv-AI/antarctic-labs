 Abstract

We describe the development of a novel emulator for the fission fragment decay code,
CGMF, which calculates the emission of prompt neutrons and γ rays from fission fragments.
The emulator described in this work uses a combination of a noisy emission model and
mixture density network to model the neutron and γ-ray multiplicities and energies. In this
manuscript, we display the power of this type of emulator for not only modeling average
prompt fission observables but also correlations between fission fragment initial conditions
and these observables, using both neutron-induced and spontaneous fission reactions. We
find that neutron (γ-ray) multiplicities can be emulated to better than 0.5% (2%) across the
training and testing data sets, and neutron (γ-ray) energies can be emulated to better than
1% (10%). While there are improvements to be made in the emulation of γ-ray properties,
our emulator provides a three order of magnitude speed up compared to the original CGMF
calculations, paving the way for detailed uncertainty quantification studies.

We also develop a new technique for learning the nuclear potential from neutron scattering
data. Optical model potentials have long been used to model nuclear reactions and thereby
interpret scattering data. Common models have a Woods-Saxon form, where the depth,
radius, and diffuseness are parametrized as a function of reaction energy, mass, and charge of
the target nucleus. Here, we propose an alternate technique replacing the Woods-Saxon form
of the optical potential with a neural network. This network is trained on differential elastic
cross section data using differentiable programming. We show an improvement in excess of
two times on a cross section dataset compared to an existing phenomenological model.

Chapter 1

Introduction

1.1 Neutrons
Neutrons are electrically neutral subatomic particles which, along with protons, make up the
nucleus of atoms. Since their discovery in 1932 by James Chadwick[12], neutrons have served
both as informative byproducts of nuclear processes and as tools to probe the nucleus itself.
Nuclear processes such as fission, or the splitting of heavy nuclei, lead to the emission of
neutrons and γ rays, the quantity and energy of which carry information about the process
which released them. Furthermore, owing to its lack of charge, the neutron is capable of
penetrating deeply into matter since it is not deflected by electromagnetic fields. This electric
neutrality makes the neutron a uniquely useful means of investigating nuclei.

Of the four fundamental forces in nature–electromagnetic, strong, weak, and gravitational–
only the first three listed here are significant at the mass and length scales dictating nuclear
physics. There is a strong repulsive electromagnetic force between protons, which are positively
charged. This repulsion means that in order to form a nucleus with multiple protons, a strong
attractive force is required. This is provided by the strong force via neutrons. The strong
force, so named because it is stronger than the other three fundamental forces at subatomic
length scales, is the force that holds quarks together to form hadrons, including neutrons
and protons. The strong force is also responsible for holding neutrons and protons together
in the nucleus. Finally, the weak force allows quarks to change from one type into another
and thereby enables β decay, or the conversion of a neutron into a proton, electron, and
antineutrino.

1.2 Nuclear fission
Nuclear fission is a process in which a nucleus is split into two smaller nuclei. Multiple
energetic neutrons may be released during this process, as is a huge amount of energy. The
fission process may occur spontaneously, as with californium-252, or it may be induced by
the bombardment of a fissile material with neutrons or other particles. The neutrons ejected
by the splitting of the nucleus may induce fission in other nearby nuclei, leading to a chain
reaction.

Figure 1.1 is a representation of neutron induced fission. An incident neutron enters from left
and impinges on a nucleus. The neutron enters the nucleus, which becomes unstable, deforms,
and scissions (splits) into two or more smaller nuclei. In a very short time, on the order
of femtoseconds (10−15 s), so-called prompt neutrons are emitted from the resulting nuclei.
Prompt γ rays, represented as yellow arrows, are also emitted. The final stage, occuring
on longer timelines up to fractions of a second, includes a secondary or delayed emission of
products such as γ rays, neutrons, and neutrinos.

The modeling of prompt neutron and γ ray emission is a high priority when it comes to
understanding fission, both for fundamental physics and for applications such as nuclear
energy safety and stockpile stewardship. The code CGMF [89] was developed at Los Alamos
National Laboratory to model the de-excitation of fission fragments on a per-event basis.
CGMF uses experimental data on fission fragment yields and excitations to determine the
fission fragment initial conditions in mass, charge, total kinetic energy, spin, and parity. From
there, CGMF uses the Hauser-Feshbach statistical theory [23] of decay (see Section 2.1) to
probabilistically emit prompt neutrons and γ rays for each fission fragment until a stable

the optical model of neutron scattering, discussed in Section 1.3. The computation of γ-ray

1.3 Neutron scattering
One of the main ways in which the properties of nuclei are investigated is through scattering
experiments. In a scattering experiment, a beam of particles, often neutrons or protons, is
directed at a target of some element or isotope. As particles impinge on the target, some are
deflected. The way in which the incident particles interact with the target is revealed by the
particles’ final path and energy.

The primary measurement resulting from a scattering experiment is termed the cross section.
In nuclear physics, a cross section quantifies the probability that a reaction will occur in terms
of effective area. It is not a probability in the strict sense; rather, it represents the effective
area a nucleus presents to the incident beam, such that a larger cross section corresponds to a
higher reaction rate for a given flux of incoming particles. For example, the neutron scattering
total cross section, σT , measured in barns (1 barn being 10−28 m2 ), describes the area that a
neutron will ”see” when it scatters from a nucleus when fired at a target comprised of the
corresponding atom. A more granular measurement is the scattering differential cross section,
dσ/dΩ, in which the effective area of scattering per unit solid angle is measured. Differential

 8

cross sections provide more information about the scattering potential, since small changes
in the potential will more strongly influence dσ/dΩ than σT .

Many fundamental nuclear processes involve interactions between nuclei and incident particles.
For example, understanding neutron radiation requires knowledge about how neutrons scatter
when impinging on nuclei. While complex and computationally expensive models for such
processes exist [24, 10, 11, 2, 18, 111], the phenomenological optical model has been a
particularly successful and inexpensive model for the interaction of nucleons with nuclei [109].
The optical model describes the interaction between a projectile and a nucleus in terms
of a complex potential, the real component governing scattering of the projectile and the
imaginary component allowing for loss of flux to channels not explicitly captured by the
model. This model is analogous to the refraction and absorption of light waves by a medium
with a complex refractive index. Crucially, the optical model treats a nucleus as a single body
with one potential, simplifying the many-body scattering problem into a two body problem
by freezing degrees of freedom.

Virtually all variations of the optical model since its first use by Feshbach et al. in 1954 [17]
are phenomenological; that is, they rely on simple functional forms with a small number
of parameters which may be tuned to match scattering data. While the original optical
model used square well potentials, these were soon replaced by complicated forms built on
the Woods-Saxon shape [109]. Over the years, the complexity of these phenomenological
models grew, eventually encompassing a multi-term expansion intended to capture different
physical processes with the real and imaginary parts of the potential and their dependence
on tunable scale and shape factors. These parameters can be self-consistently fit across a
range of nucleon energies and target nuclei parameters, providing a quasi-global solution for
the scattering problem. However, even the best extant closed-form tunable models could not
be tuned to reproduce all known cross-section data within available error bars.

Background

2.1 Nuclear Fission
In a fission event, a heavy nucleus splits into two—or more—lighter fission fragments that are
highly excited and very quickly decay through neutron and γ-ray emission, so-called prompt
emission. The resulting fission products may further undergo β decay, followed by a sequence
of β-delayed neutron and γ-ray emissions, so-called delayed emission. Average quantities
from these decays, such as the multiplicities and energies of the neutrons and γ rays, along
with detailed distributions, are used to understand fission properties and in applications,
e.g. [28, 69, 88]. Correlations between fission observables can provide further insight into
the underlying fission process and can also be used in more detailed simulations of nuclear
technologies through Monte Carlo transport codes.

When studying fission theoretically, calculations are often broken down into three stages. The
first describes the initial deformation and splitting of the fissile nucleus1 , typically through
microscopic or macroscopic-microscopic models [80, 82, 103, 75, 51, 97, 26, 53, 104]. These
methods have been used to determine fission fragment masses, charges, and kinetic energies,
as well as to gain insight into the spins of the fission fragments immediately following scission
[70, 45, 9, 71, 83]. After scission, separate models are used to de-excite the fission fragments
through prompt neutron and γ-ray emission, [89, 102, 101, 38, 59, 41, 79, 96]. Finally,
the delayed emission calculations can be solved using time-dependent or time-independent
methods, often using available evaluated decay data, such as from the ENDF/B [7], JEFF
[66], or JENDL [27] nuclear data libraries. To date, there is no single model that describes

the entire fission process.

The Hauser-Feshbach fission fragment decay code CGMF, developed at Los Alamos National
Laboratory, models the second stage of the fission process as described above [89]: the
emission of the prompt neutrons and γ rays. Fission fragment initial conditions in mass,
charge, total kinetic energy, total excitation energy, spin, and parity, Y (A, Z, TKE, U, J, π),
are phenomenologically parametrized and fit to available experimental data. The mass
distribution is constructed to be a sum of Gaussians, and the charge distribution is taken
from the Wahl systematics [105]. The average TKE as a function of incident neutron energy
is linear, where the TKE for each mass is taken to be a Gaussian. Parities are assumed to be
distributed evenly between positive and negative while the spin distribution is proportional
to (2J + 1)exp [−J(J + 1)/2B 2 (Z, A, T )], where B 2 is the spin-cut off parameter. For more
details on the exact parametrization, see [89]. For each event, these five quantities are sampled
from their respective distributions, then the total excitation energy (TXE) is calculated for
the given fragmentation based on conservation of energy, TXE = TKE − Q, where Q is the
Q value for the fragment split. The TXE is shared between the fragments based on a ratio of
temperatures, RT , and the TKE is shared based on momentum conservation.

Once the initial conditions are determined for each fission fragment, the Hauser-Feshbach
statistical theory [23] is used to deexcite these fragments. Neutrons and γ rays are emitted
according to probability distribution functions for their emissions. In the Hauser-Feshbach
theory, probabilities for emitting a photon in a specific state are determined from γ-ray
strength functions. For neutrons, probabilities are determined from transmission coefficients
calculated from an optical potential for the neutron-nucleus interaction. For each fission
event sampled and computed by CGMF, the characteristics (number, energy, angle) of the
emitted neutrons and γ rays are collected for post-processing, providing average quantities as
well as more complex correlations.

To begin the Hauser-Feshbach calculation, each fission fragment is described by its mass, A,
charge, Z, kinetic energy, KE, excitation energy, U , spin, J, and parity, π. At each step in
the decay, energy, momentum, spin, and parity are conserved. Although these six values are
enough to describe the initial conditions of the fission fragments, the full decay—including
the probabilistic nature of CGMF—is much more complicated, depending on how these initial
conditions change as the neutrons and γ rays are emitted. We also note that CGMF can run in
two modes. In the first, the full Hauser-Feshbach decay is calculated. In the second, only the
fission fragment initial conditions are sampled, without performing the neutron and γ-ray
emission. This second mode runs significantly faster than the full Hauser-Feshbach decay.

 12

We use the second mode to generate the inputs for our emulator after it has been trained.

Although CGMF can be used with limited computing resources to study complex correlations
in fission observables, much larger computational resources are needed when performing
large-scale parameter sensitivity and optimization studies. Due to these computational
challenges across nuclear physics, emulators have become increasingly common, particularly
as the number of studies on parametric uncertainty quantification has grown. Gaussian
process (GP) emulators [57, 76, 47, 86] have recently been replaced with reduced basis
methods (RBMs) [19, 77, 31, 16, 20, 107]. Unlike the GPs, where the emulator is a non-linear
mapping from some set of inputs to one (or more) outputs, the RBMs use a small set of basis
functions to emulate the Schrödinger equation directly. Because the Schrödinger equation
itself is emulated, instead of the resulting observables such as energies or cross sections, the
extrapolations from RBMs tend to be much more robust than those from GPs, as in [31].
Neural networks have also been used for performing uncertainty quantification for theory
models [98, 99, 55, 106, 56]. However, in nuclear physics, emulators rarely, if ever, have been
used for event generators.

In this work, we aim to create an emulator for CGMF that significantly speeds up the Hauser-
Feshbach piece of the calculation while retaining correlations between fission fragment initial
conditions and prompt fission observables. Part of the calculation of the neutron energies
and the competition between neutrons and γ rays involves solving the Schrödinger equation
for the neutron transmission coefficients, so RBMs could be used to speed up this piece of
the calculation. We note that these methods have been used for cross section calculations
[15, 58]. However, just implementing this method would not speed up the initial condition
sampling or any calculations for the γ-ray properties. Additionally, because we want our
emulator to retain the correlations between prompt neutron and γ-ray observables and the
fission fragment initial conditions, we cannot just emulate average multiplicities or energies.
Therefore, we develop a novel emulator that provides event-by-event emulation.

In addition to being able to quickly explore fission fragment correlations with this novel
emulator, a main motivator of this work is to be able to perform robust uncertainty quantifi-
cation (UQ) without the use of high performance computing resources. Limited UQ studies
have been undertaken for fission observables, e.g. [46, 81, 28, 69]. The studies in [46, 81]
concern the uncertainties in the parameters of density functional theory, describing how the
parent nucleus fissions. In contrast, [28] shows how the low uncertainties on average prompt
neutron multiplicity measurements can be used to constrain the fission fragment average total
kinetic energy, due to strong anti-correlations between the two quantities, and [69] forward

 13

propagates fission fragment initial conditions to prompt neutron and γ-ray observables. Most
previous studies of fission fragment uncertainties have focused on the fission fragment initial
conditions that most directly impact a specific prompt neutron or γ-ray observable, due to
the computational demands of fission event generators. Finally, a long-standing challenge
for CGMF and other Hauser-Feshbach fission fragment decay models is the calculation of the
energy spectrum of the neutrons emitted from the fission fragments (the so-called prompt
fission neutron spectrum, PFNS). It is unlikely that there is enough uncertainty in the fission
fragment initial conditions to completely explain the discrepancy between the data and theory.
As studies such as [29] have shown, there is some impact on the PFNS from changing fission
fragment initial conditions. There are also known missing physics in CGMF and other fission
event generators, such as the omission of scission neutrons, unmeasured nuclear levels, and
optical model potentials only constrained at stability. However, with the development of an
emulator such as the one described in this work, we can begin to investigate one potential
source of this discrepancy.

2.2 The Optical Model
When Feshbach et al. first described the optical model for nuclear potential in 1954 [17], the
prevailing idea was that before scattering, an incident nucleon would enter the nucleus and
form a compound nucleus, meaning that the motions of all the nucleons would be strongly
coupled. One consequence of this strong-coupling model is that the total neutron cross
section should increase slowly with increasing atomic mass number A at a given incident
energy, and the total cross section’s dependence on incident energy should have generally the
same shape across the nuclear chart. Experiments measuring the total neutron cross section
did not fit this prediction of the strong-coupling model; rather, these cross sections showed
qualitative changes in the shape of cross section as a function of energy between different
nuclei. However, these qualitative changes varied slowly as a function of A, suggesting that
scattering depends not on the specific nuclear structure but on a slowly varying parameter
with A, such as the nuclear radius.

Experimental disagreement with cross section predictions of the strong-coupling model led
Feshbach et al. to propose a model with weaker interaction between the incident particle and
the nucleus: the optical model. In this weaker interacting model, a nucleon would enter a
nucleus and move within its boundaries without forming a compound nucleus. The impact
of the nucleus on the incoming nucleon’s path could thus be interpreted as a potential well.
Feshbach et al. also accounted for the chance that a compound nucleus might be formed by

To test their model, Feshbach et al. compare both total and differential elastic cross sections

sections are less sensitive to small differences in potential. Furthermore, Feshbach et al.
neglect spin orbit effects for simplicity, which has a much greater effect on the differential
cross sections than on the total. In short, Feshbach et al. showed the promise of a simple
optical model to capture the basic details of the physics involved with neutron scattering.
However, they did not attempt to capture the impact varying incident energy and nuclear
mass number and charge number might have on the form of the optical model’s potential.

Despite the general success of the square well potential in capturing trends in scattering
experiments, it was soon found to be insufficient. The square well optical model made
predictions for the reaction cross section which were smaller than experimentally measured.

higher cross sections than experiments revealed. Both of these shortcomings were addressed
by the addition of a tail to the potential by Woods and Saxon [109]:

In the following decades, as more experimental scattering data became available and computing

 18

power improved, more groups developed global models for the nuclear potential. In 1969,
Beccheti and Greenlees used a χ2 optimization routine to tune models for neutron and proton
scattering with up to 30 parameters each [4]. In 1979 Rapaport et al. created a global optical
model focusing specifically on nuclei known to be spherical, thus avoiding the unpredictable
effect nucleus deformation has on the potential [72]. In 1991 Varner et al. leverage a more
complete understanding of the physics implied by the optical model, as well as the growing
database relative to previous efforts, to create a single model describing potentials for both
neutron and proton scattering [100]. In 2003 Koning and Delaroche used an iterative process
of training both local (single-nucleus) models and a global model to converge to the best
possible global fit with their limited number of parameters [32]. They used the relativistic
Schrödinger equation, allowing them to have the widest energy coverage of the global models:
1 KeV to 200 MeV. Their work was built on by Pruitt et al. [67], who worked both to
reproduce the original dataset used for training, as well as to construct more reasonable error
bounds for the data and the model. Despite the advancements in computing power from
the 1950s to the 2000s, each of the optical potential models formulated was restricted in
functional form and total number of parameters partly by limitations in the computational
methods available for parameter estimation.

3.1 Methods
The true Hauser-Feshbach calculation for prompt neutrons and γ rays may be summarized
as a joint distribution conditioned on the fission fragments initial conditions, x:

                                      P (Nν , Eν , Nγ , Eγ |x)                              (3.1)

where Nν , Nγ are neutron and γ-ray multiplicities and Eν , Eγ are their respective energies.
While all of these quantities are correlated, creating a single model to predict all four presents
a number of challenges. We have four distinct data, two being discrete and two continuous.
The structure of a model capturing all four quantities would be complex, as would the training
process. To simplify, we split the problem into four distinct models. Since neutron (γ-ray)
energy is directly dependent on neutron (γ-ray) multiplicity, we will use the prediction of the
multiplicity emulators as input for their respective energy emulator.

 20

Our emulators for the neutrons and γ rays that are emitted during the decay of a single
fission fragment involve two stages. In the first stage, we predict the multiplicity of neutrons,
ν and γ rays, Nγ , that are emitted. The structure of these emulators is described in general
in 3.1.1. In the next stage, we use a conditional probabilistic emulator, described in Sec.
3.1.2, to predict the neutron and γ-ray energies, En and Eγ , given the neutron and γ-ray
multiplicities.

3.1.1 Multiplicity Emulator
Stochastic Emulator Design

In fission, the outcomes for multiplicity are effectively a multinomial distribution, a general-
ization of the binomial distribution to n outcomes. Concretely, a single set of fission fragment
initial conditions may lead to multiple discrete outcomes, e.g. 0, 1, or 2 neutrons emitted.
The data reflects this randomness. Since the inputs to our model are random draws from the
fission fragment initial condition distributions, we do not necessarily have multiple datapoints
for each unique set of inputs, especially considering that the kinetic energy and excitation
energy take continuous values. Practically speaking, this means that we do not have direct
access to the multinomial distribution at any point of the input space. The construction of
our emulator must capture the stochasticity inherent in the problem, while being trainable
on single multiplicity values at each datapoint.

We describe what we term the noisy emission model (NEM) for the neutron and γ-ray
multiplicities–emission for the emission of neutrons (γ rays) and noisy for the randomness
inherent to the physics of this emission. To simplify our discussion, in this methods section
we assume a maximum fragment multiplicity of ν = 4, suitable for neutrons, but we employ
the same algorithm with a larger range of multiplicities for γ rays. We begin by constructing
a probability density function for a continuous parameter, n, ranging from 0 to 4, which
covers the range for multiplicity outcomes we are emulating: 0, 1, 2, 3, or 4 neutrons emitted.

As discussed, the underlying multinomial distributions realized in the dataset are not directly
accessible. However, it is possible to make an approximation of these distributions by
clustering nearby datapoints together and counting outcomes, as outlined in Appendix A.

to be a discrete realization of a Gaussian distribution (see App. A). Therefore we begin by

Multiplicity training data

We focus our emulator on spontaneous fission and first-chance fission for neutron-induced
reactions (that is, below about 5 MeV incident neutron energy where a neutron has a near
zero probability of being emitted from the compound nucleus before it fissions). In our
training set, we include events from 252 Cf spontaneous fission as well as 235 U(n,f), 238 U(n,f),
and 239 Pu(n,f) at incident neutron energies of thermal, 1 MeV, 2 MeV, 3 MeV, 4 MeV, and 5

Multiplicity emulator design choices

The NEMs described in Sec. 3.1.1 provide a surrogate model, ν̂, for neutron (or similarly
γ-ray) multiplicity. While six physical quantities that define the initial conditions of the
fission fragments, A, Z, KE, U , J, and π, are available as inputs for our model, the prompt
observables do not have a strong sensitivity to all of these initial conditions. For example,
when modeling prompt decay products, the U available in a fission fragment determines
the number of neutrons and γ rays that can be emitted. Because the kinetic energy of the
fragments is highly anti-correlated with the excitation energy, including KE and U into the
input vector is likely redundent information. We confirmed that including kinetic energy as
an input is detrimental to performance by training and testing such a network.

In CGMF, the initial parity of each fission fragment is sampled randomly, split evenly between
positive and negative. For many of the γ-ray emissions, the J and π of the initial and
surrounding nuclear states determines whether a decay is accessible or not. Parity, however,
plays a much smaller role in the neutron emission, as the underlying equations average

 23

over the two parity states. Therefore, in our emulators we use π as an input for the γ-ray
multiplicity emulator but not for the neutron emulator.

In our two emulators, we therefore consider x = {A, Z, U, J} for the neutron multiplicity
emulator and x = {A, Z, U, J, π} for the γ-ray multiplicity emulator. Having selected
physically motivated fission fragment initial conditions from CGMF for the two emulators, we
now design a model ν̂ capable of representing the stochastic nature of the neutron and γ-ray
multiplicities.

To construct ν̂, we tested fully connected neural networks with ReLU activation functions
[54] of varying architectures: 2, 4, and 8 hidden layers with 16, 32, and 64 nodes each. For
neutrons the highest performing architecture was the 4 layer 64 node model, while for γ rays
an 8 layer 64 node model performed best. Inputs were normalized to have means of 0 and
standard deviations of 1. Two output nodes represented a Gaussian’s mean µ and standard
deviation σ for parameter n, as described in Sec. 3.1.1.

Having outlined a strategy for training a multiplicity emulator on the raw data available from

simply compute expected values for both the reference distribution from CGMF ν̄ and the

the much wider distribution of γ-ray multiplicities, their skew toward higher multiplicities,

values.

the input for the γ-ray MDN included all six fission fragment initial conditions, x =

the energies of the neutrons and γ rays in the laboratory frame. Although energy available
for the decay is given by the excitation energy, the kinetic energy is necessary to know the
conversion from the center of mass frame to the laboratory frame. Additionally, as discussed
in Sec. 3.1.1, we include the parity as an input for the γ-ray emulators due to its importance
in the selection of the decay mode (CGMF includes E1, E2, and M1 modes).

For the neutron MDN, our network was fully connected, consisting of 1 layer with 24 nodes
each and 6 Gaussian mixtures. The MDN for the γ-ray energy emulator consisted of 8
layers with 48 nodes each and 8 Gaussian mixtures. We trained for 1000 (2500) epochs for
the neutron (γ-ray) emulator. For both MDNs, we tested a variety of Gaussian mixtures,
layers, and nodes per layer. We did not find a significantly improved performance with larger
networks for either emulator.

3.2.3 Discussion
General performance for the neutron multiplicity emulator is excellent, with ∆ν̄/ν̄ falling
below 0.4% for trained reactions and 0.6% on reactions outside the training set. This error is
on the order of or lower than the deviation of CGMF from experimental data.

 31

The neutron NEM’s performance on individual fission events is what gives it the generality
to model whole fission reactions, including those outside the training set, with such fidelity.
One primary use case for this emulator is uncertainty quantification (UQ) of CGMF itself.
Performing UQ requires the adjustment of many parameters within CGMF and iterative testing
of how these adjustments change the outputs. If we were to test this emulator only on fission
reactions in the training data, it would not be clear whether the emulator would remain
valid under adjustments to CGMF’s parameters, because these adjustments lead to different
distributions for the post-scission conditions. However, by testing our emulator on fission
reactions outside the training set, we gain confidence that the emulator remains accurate
even when CGMF parameters are changed.

The γ-ray NEM, γ̂, has worse performance than the neutron emulator ν̂, with ∆N̄γ /N̄γ on
trained reactions rising about an order of magnitude. However, for the ultimate goal of
performing UQ with this CGMF emulator, we note that experimental measurements of ν̄ can
be at the 0.5–1% level where as measurements of N̄γ usually have an uncertainty of a few
percent. Therefore, both of these NEM emulators are within the tolerance of the experimental
measurements and should not bias any optimization that can be performed.

3.4 Full emulator
We finally discuss the importance of the full emulator for fission studies with CGMF. The
two NEMs and two MDNs have been interfaced with the model of CGMF that only calculates
the fission fragment initial conditions. We find about three orders of magnitude speed up
between the full CGMF calculation and the emulator described in this work. Currently, the
speed of our emulator is limited by the time needed to sample the fission fragment initial
conditions, which has recently been improved [87] compared to [89] for the incident energy
range considered here.

To show the power of the full emulator, we construct several models for the spontaneous
fission of 252 Cf that differ by varying a handful of parameters that determine the fission
fragment initial conditions. Namely, we vary the average total kinetic energy, TKE, the
width of the TKE distribution, σTKE , and the spin cutoff parameter, α. The default values
from CGMF [89] and the updated values are shown in the second, third, and fourth columns of
Table 3.1. We have chosen these three quantities to vary because previous studies have shown
their impact on prompt fission observables. Several previous studies have demonstrated that
TKE and ν̄ are strongly anti-correlated [28, 69]. The width of the TKE distribution has an
impact on the width of the neutron multiplicity distribution [88], often denoted as the second
moment of the distribution, ν2 . The spin cutoff parameter has a relatively small impact on
the neutron observables but does strongly impact the γ-ray properties [84].

3.5 Conclusion
Here, we have developed an emulator for event-by-event fission fragment decay code, CGMF,
using a combination of noisy emission models (NEMs) and mixture density networks (MDNs).
The NEMs are used to emulate the multiplicity of the neutrons and γ rays from a fission
fragment, given a subset of the fission fragment initial conditions in mass, charge, kinetic
energy, excitation energy, spin, and parity. The MDNs emulate the energies of each neutron
or γ ray emitted, given the multiplicities predicted by the NEM. The neutron emulators can
reproduce the full CGMF results to within a percent or better for both neutron energies and
multiplicities, while the γ-ray emulator suffers from larger discrepancies on the order of a few
percent. The emulator generalizes to reactions outside of the training set—which included
spontaneous fission of 252 Cf and neutron-induced fission up to 5 MeV for 235 U, 238 U, and

 37

239
 Pu—to an error within a factor of two compared to the training set. We find a speed up
of about three orders of magnitude using our emulator compared to a full CGMF calculation,
which is mainly limited by the calculation of the fission fragment initial conditions, not by
the implementation of the emulator.

While providing impressive accuracy and speed gains, our emulator has several limitations
that require further investigation. The γ-ray emulator in particular falls short of the accuracy
achieved by the neutron emulator, due to the more complex distributions to be learned and
the difference between fission fragment initial conditions and the fragment conditions when
the γ rays are emitted. Another limitation is event-by-event performance, particularly for the
NEM. Although, for example, the neutron multiplicity distribution from the neutron NEM
reproduces CGMF to better than 1% on average, when these multiplicities are used as inputs
to the neutron MDN, the discrepancy between the neutron energies from the emulator and
CGMF worsens.

However, the results shown in this paper open up one avenue into emulating event generators,
rather than the typical structure and reaction observables that have already been the focus
of a broad development of emulators for nuclear theory models. The pathway sets the stage
for future studies into robust uncertainty quantification (UQ) of the fission fragment initial
conditions, following work such as [28, 69]. Such robust and fast emulators enable large-scale
parameter sensitivity and optimization studies, probing the limits of validity of several physics
models implemented in CGMF.

Future work includes further emulator development, particularly looking at machine learning
methods that can emulate the partial differential equations that are solved within CGMF,
rather than constructing a blackbox mapping from inputs to outputs. Similar work has been
the focus of emulators such as [58] which builds an emulator for neutron scattering but has
not yet been implemented into CGMF or other event generators. The goal is to be able to
provide a similar speed up to the current emulator with better accuracy for both neutron
and γ-ray observables. Additionally, although the pytorch implementation of this emulator
provides a significant speed up, the separate call outside of CGMF could limit the emulator’s
use in transport codes, such as MCNP® [35]. Work to translate the emulator into C++ is
planned to more directly integrate it with CGMF.

predicated on the fact that most differentiable mathematical functions are composed of a
finite set of simple functions with known derivatives, namely, the four arithmetic operations,
the sign switch, and the exponential, logarithmic, and trigonometric functions. AD avoids
the shortcomings of gradient estimation methods because it is (a) fast, and scales well with
increasing number of model parameters, (b) free from truncation error, and accurate to
machine precision, and (c) generally applicable to code including control structures such as

4.2 History of automatic differentiation
The development of automatic differentiation began in the 1960s at the University of Wisconsin-
Madison with a simple application of forward mode AD [68]. AD did not begin to catch on
more generally, however, until the 1980s when Andreas Griewank and colleagues at Argonne
National Laboratory led a resurgence in an era of new programming techniques and the
introduction of reverse-mode AD, a more efficient algorithm for problems with many inputs
and few outputs.

The mid-2010’s saw the advent of deep learning, with researchers training networks many
layers deep and parameter counts running in the millions. With the curse of dimensionality1
weighing heavily on deep learning models using gradient estimates, the demand for efficient
gradient computation exploded. Software packages such as autograd [44], Chainer [95],
and PyTorch [63] which had AD capabilities became available. Around this time the more
general term differentiable programming was advocated by Olah, Dalrymple, and LeCun
[3]. Differentiable programming or diffprog extends the idea of automatic differentiation to
arbitrary blocks of code and data structures, making diffprog a very powerful and general
tool. Henceforth we shall refer to differentiable programming and automatic differentiation
interchangeably.

As AD grew in popularity and potency, its application in scientific machine learning grew.
One notable development was the development of Neural ODEs in 2018 [13]. In their work,
Chen et al. represent the derivative of a hidden state using a neural network and solve this
derivative with an existing ODE solver. They use differentiable programming to efficiently
compute the gradient of a loss function by propagating through the solver’s operations without
direct access to the solver’s code. This new and powerful class of neural network helped
popularize the use of AD in scientific fields, with [13] garnering over 7000 citations since 2018.
Neural ODEs, and consequently AD, have found application in scientific disciplines as diverse
as computational fluid dynamics, design optimization, and bioinformatics. As recently as
this year, diffprog has even been applied to global optical models. In the preprint [36], Lei
used AD to train an emulator which maps optical potential parameters to scattering wave

scattering, the neural potential model, and the differentiable programming approach to
optimize our model. Here we will also elucidate the mathematical and numerical methods
used to solve the scattering problem. In Section 5.2, we present the initial results of our
baseline model, demonstrating its flexibility and accuracy improvements to scattering data
over existing models. In Section 5.3 we introduce the changes to both the physics and the
numerical methods of the problem to extend our model to relativistic regimes. In Section
5.4 we present the fully relativistic version of our model and compare its performance to
an existing model. We conclude in Section 5.5 with a discussion of the applicability of our
model, as well as potential future directions for this method in the nuclear domain.

5.1.1 Phenomenological optical model for neutron scattering
The phenomenological optical model for low-energy (< 200MeV) neutron scattering on
nuclei simplifies the many-body neutron scattering problem into a two-body problem with
a single neutron scattering off a local and complex-valued potential, whose imaginary part
characterizes the loss of neutron flux to reactions not accounted for in the model, such as
inelastic scattering [17]. To be concrete, the neutron scattering cross section is computed by

Scattering with angular momentum effects

The above derivation was made under the assumption of zero angular momentum to simplify
the mathematics. However, in reality, there are three possible sources of angular momentum
which combine to impact scattering physics. First, the neutrons being scattered carry intrinsic
angular momentum known as spin. Each individual neutron may carry a spin of − 12 or + 12 .
Second, the nucleus itself may have spin, depending on the spins of its constituent protons
and neutrons. Since our problem involves averaging over many nuclei, and each nucleus is
comprised of particles which can take on spins only of − 21 or + 12 with equal likelihood, we
assume a nucleus spin of 0. Third, the relative motion between the neutron and nucleus

 50

introduces orbital angular momentum, labeled as ℓ. These three angular momenta couple to
form the total angular momentum J of the system.

described in Sec. 5.1.1 to a multi-channel scattering problem: one channel for each total

of ℓ ± 12 . The S-matrix, previously diagonal, now has non-zero values above and below the

of disparate experiments performed at different times in different facilities, leading to different

unpredictably between nuclei, making them impossible to constrain without regularly spaced
data. Koning and Delaroche also find that for nuclides known to deform strongly from a
spherical shape, the spherical optical model is insufficient to completely describe nucleon
scattering, and thus a purely numerical approach with a limited set of parameters struggles
to find a satisfactory state.

Koning and Delaroche address the difficulties enumerated above by implementing an involved
multi-stage training scheme. They begin with the construction of simpler, local optical
potential models for individual nuclei and energies fit directly to experimental data. They
train these ”best-fit” local models on elastic scattering cross section data across a wide energy
range (1 keV to 200 MeV) and nucleus range (Z = 12 to Z = 83), as well as on total cross
section data σ and analyzing power data Ay . They use a combination of three methods to
train these local models: χ2 optimization with simulated annealing, grid search, and visual
goodness-of-fit estimation.

 1. Simulated annealing [21] is an optimization scheme in which the function to be minimized
 is evaluated at randomized steps from a starting point. Steps are accepted or denied
 based on a hyperparameter termed ”temperature” and the change in the objective
 value’s function. Simulated annealing is a robust numerical method for non-convex
 optimization; however, as it relies on function evaluations to measure change and does
 not include gradient information, it suffers from the curse of dimensionality. That is, as
 the number of parameters in a model increases, the time for simulated annealing to
 converge increases exponentially.

 2. A grid search is used for those nuclides with sufficient data. Koning and Delaroche
 separate their model’s parameters into energy-independent (geometric) and energy-
 dependent (functional ) groups. By alternately setting subsets of these parameters to
 reasonable starting values and performing simulated annealing to minimize the χ2 value,
 they learn best fit parameters.

 3. Visual goodness of fit is the final word used by Koning and Delaroche when fitting
 local potential models. They use the software package ECISVIEW [34], a visualizer for
 the nuclear code ECIS [74], to rapidly plot the predicted cross sections for their model
 against experimental data and interactively update their model parameters accordingly.

 53

These local models are used to determine global trends for optical model parameters. Koning
and Delaroche plotted each parameter over atomic mass number A and hand-selected closed
form expressions which satisfactorily fit the trends.

Koning and Delaroche cover a wide range of energies with their model, from 1 keV to 200
MeV. Above several tens of MeV, relativistic effects become significant; therefore Koning
and Delaroche use a relativistic version of the calculations detailed in Section 5.1.1. At low
energies, typically below 10 MeV, compound nucleus effects become significant. Koning and
Delaroche account for these effects with nuclear physical strategies which are beyond the
scope of this writing. For the initial (non-relativistic) version of the model we will restrict
ourselves to above 5 MeV and neglect compound nucleus corrections. For our more robust
relativistic model we will perform an analysis to determine the isotope-dependent lower
energy bound as described in App. B. To avoid relativistic effects we will limit ourselves to a
maximum energy of 40 MeV for this version.

5.1.3 Neural optical model
In principle it is possible to represent each of the terms in Eqn. (5.16) as the output of neural
network. However, the solution of the scattering problem according to the methods in 5.1.1
does not distinguish between, for example, contribution to the imaginary component of the
potential by the volume-central potential or the surface-central potential; it is the sum of
these two terms which is used in calculations. Only the spin-orbit terms VSO and WSO are
treated differently in the optical model. For this non-relativistic version, we found that the
imaginary spin-orbit term was extremely small and made a negligible difference in the 5-40
MeV range. Therefore we choose to model the potential as a single real and single imaginary
quantity for non-spin terms, along with the real spin-orbit term:

                               U(x; ϕ) = V + iW + VSO · L · σ                             (5.20)

where x = [E, A, Z, r] is the input vector and ϕ are the trainable parameters of the neural
network.

Our initial version of the neural network begins with four input nodes, allowing the network
to take as input the incident energy of the neutron, E (MeV), radial distance from the
neutron, r (fm), atomic number Z, and mass number A. From there we have 4 hidden layers
comprised of 8 nodes each. The leaky ReLU activation function [43]

                                  ReLU(x) = max{0.01x, x}                                 (5.21)

 54

is used at each node. The network terminates in three nodes, each providing one of the three
terms in Eqn. (5.20). We apply a negative ReLU function to the output nodes to ensure the
potential is always negative.

5.1.4 Controlling spin-orbit potential
We found in practice that the spin-orbit term VSO was susceptible to non-physical behavior,
specifically forming a peak at the center of the nucleus and declining toward the nuclear
surface. While this behavior lead to better fits than K-D at backward angles, it is not
physically explicable under the optical model. Furthermore, this behavior was accompanied
by rapid oscillations in the other potential terms. To control the spin-orbit term, we adopt a
method often used with Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) to impose boundary
conditions on neural network outputs: the distance function [85]. In short, a distance function
Φ(x) may be used to linearly transform the output of a neural network to satisfy certain
requirements. As a simple example, if the output of a model M(r) is required to be zero at
the boundaries r = 0 and r = 1, a distance function Φ(r) with Φ(0) = 0 and Φ(1) = 0 may
be used:

                                     M(r) = Φ(r)NN(r)
                                      Φ(r) = r(1 − r)

where NN(r) is the output of a neural network. Nonzero boundary conditions may also be
imposed by including additive terms.

Our desired behavior–a spin-orbit potential which peaks near the nuclear surface and falls off
rapidly away from the surface–is already encoded in the K-D spin-orbit potential. Furthermore,
since the boundary conditions are zero, we can use the K-D VSO term as a multiplier of our
neural network’s output to constrain VSO without including an additive term.

One small change is required to VSO to make it suitable as a distance function. Note in
Eqn. (5.17) that VSO has an r term in the denominator. This means that as r → 0, VSO will
diverge. As we require a boundary condition of 0, our distance function must converge to 0
as r → 0. To ensure this we adjust VSO by replacing 1/r with r/(r + 0.1)2 :

5.1.6 Nuclear scattering data
Neutron elastic scattering data was downloaded from the EXFOR database [61], a collabora-
tion between the Internation Network of Nuclear Reaction Data Centres. Neutron differential
cross sections spanning incident neutron energies of 5 to 40 MeV and targets with mass
numbers from 24 to 209.

The above filtering process pulls data from experiments on two types of targets: isotopes
and natural elements. Isotopic data is fully defined for our problem, with a unique value for
mass number A and atomic number Z. However, elemental data involves targets which are
composed of two or more isotopes, depending on the element. This presents a challenge for
our neural network, which requires specific inputs for both A and Z. However, a cross section
is an averaging over many neutron scatter events; therefore we can take a weighted average
of the cross sections produced by the isotopes comprising an element in proportion to their
natural abundances to produce a single prediction.

The resulting dataset is comprised of about 28,000 individual data points, where a single
point represents the differential cross section for one target nucleus [A, Z] at one incident
neutron energy E at a single angle θ off the axis of the neutron beam leaving the target. It
is natural to group these datapoints by what we will call ”experiment”. An experiment is
a single measurement made in a laboratory of the complete differential cross section across
the full range θ for a single target nucleus at a single incident neutron energy. We have
approximately 1000 experiments in our dataset, most of which contain between 10 and 40
data points.

We note here that the dataset we describe in this section is not directly comparably to the
dataset used by Koning and Delaroche. They limited their model to near-spherical nuclei to
avoid the confounding effects of nuclear geometry; they also spanned energies from 1 keV
to 200 MeV. We are avoiding relativistic effects by cutting off our dataset at 40 MeV and
(attempting to) avoid compound scattering effects at low energies. A follow-up study to the
work in this section determined that a 5 MeV cutoff was not conservative enough to avoid
compound scattering effects for all nuclei. We wait to address these issues (relativistic effects,
insufficient low-energy cutoff, non-spherical nuclei) in the follow up section on the relativistic
version of the NOM, Section 5.3, by a process detailed in Appendix B.

5.1.8 Training
There are any number of potential curves which may give rise to a given differential cross
section. To ensure that our model is learning a physically plausible potential, we train in two
stages. First, we train directly on the Koning-Delaroche (K-D) global optical potential model
[32]. In effect the K-D model becomes our prior. We train our neural network to minimize
the mean absolute error between the K-D potential and our model’s potential:

 1 X
                                LKD =           |UKD − UNOM |                            (5.33)
 N A,Z,E

We train on the full range of intended input space: A from 24 to 209, Z from 12 to 83, E
from 5 to 200 MeV, and r from 0 to 15 fm.

Having pre-trained our model on K-D, we move to the second stage: training on differential
cross section data. For a given experiment, a prediction for the potential is made, which
is then used to predict a differential cross section according to Sec. 5.1. This cross section
is then used to compute the loss (Eqn. 5.29) vs the data for that experiment. The loss is
averaged over all experiments in the training set for each training step. We use the Adam
optimizer [30] implemented in the Julia package Optim.jl [49] with an adaptive learning rate
to minimize the loss function by adjusting our model parameters over 10000 training steps.
We utilize a standard test-train split with 80% of experiments falling into the training set
and 20% into the test set.

5.2 Results
5.2.1 Injected signal recovery
To demonstrate the validity of our method, we test the framework beginning with a known
(synthetic) potential. We use this synthetic potential to generate differential cross-section
data, then attempt to learn the original potential directly from this data. Testing our method
in this way gives us complete control over the geometry of the source potential, the location
of training data, and the amount of noise present in the data.

In order to test our method in a way which most closely emulates the real-world problem, we
construct a global potential which is a perturbed version of K-D. This allows us to perform
warm-up training on the original K-D as we do in the full model. We perturb K-D by adding
an interior hump to the real potential well beginning at the nuclear center, as well as a
shoulder to the region where the real potential begins increasing to 0. We add a secondary

5.3.1 Relativistic kinematics
A thoroughly principled treatment of the scattering problem at high energies would require
the application of the Dirac equation, Dirac’s generalization of the Schrödinger equation
which accounts for special relativity. However, solving the Dirac equation presents additional
challenges and high computational cost. The scope of optical models is not to provide the
most honest possible picture of scattering, but a ’good enough’ calculation which is flexible
enough to fit data and make predictions for low computational cost. Therefore, rather than
reframing the problem as a Dirac equation solution, designers of optical models use the
non-relativistic Schrödinger’s equation as described in Sec. 5.1.1 with relativistic corrections
to the kinematics.

5.3.2 Reintroducing imaginary spin-orbit
The initial version of this model discarded the imaginary spin-orbit term WSO due to the fact
that WSO stays very nearly zero under the limit of 40 MeV we set to avoid relativistic effects.
Now that we are implementing relativistic corrections and allowing our model to explore the
full range set by Koning and Delaroche (up to 200 MeV), we introduce imaginary spin-orbit.
The NOM is now a neural network terminating in four output nodes:

                        U(x; ϕ) = V + iW + VSO · L · σ + WSO · L · σ                     (5.54)

We elect to control the imaginary spin-orbit term WSO in the same way as VSO (Sec. 5.1.4)
due to the fact that this term remains very close to zero in most r across the nuclear chart
and for moderate energies. The structure of our model as described in Sec. 5.1.3 is effective
for enforcing the non-positive nature of the attractive potentials we are modeling; however,
when certain potentials are zero or very near zero for most inputs, this model has a tendency
to only predict zeros regardless of the input. This tendency is caused by the fact that gradient
descent is based on the sensitivity of the output to changing input, but a ReLU activation
function is insensitive to changes which in the ”flat” zone, or in our case, changes to input
values which would produce a positive potential. Using a distance function allows us to
initialize a network which produces output values of -1 rather than 0, ensuring we remain in
an input region which is sensitive to small changes.

5.3.4 Relativistic data
To ensure that we have a reliable and reproducible dataset on which to train, we follow
the work of Pruitt et al. [67] in reconstructing the dataset originally used by Koning and
Delaroche to train their optical model. Pruitt et al. assembled tables listing the original K-D
datasets by their isotope/element, incident neutron energy, and EXFOR subentry identifier.
Some datasets from the original paper were excluded due to inaccessability; others were

 71

datasets listed in papers but not recorded on EXFOR. The result is a dataset Pruitt et al.
dub the ”KDUQ” corpus: the most faithful possible modern reconstruction of the data used
by Koning and Delaroche. The development of the EXFOR Extractor tool to automate this
data collection is detailed in Chap. 6. We also use this tool to extract Pruitt et al.’s Test
corpus, a set of data available on EXFOR which matches the requirements of Koning and
Delaroche for use by their model, but postdates their work.

The EXFOR Extractor pulls data from experiments on two types of targets: isotopes and
natural elements. As we discussed in Section 5.1.6, our model needs a single value for A, which
is not straightforward for elements, being comprised of with varying fractions of isotopes
with different A values. Koning and Delaroche’s model faces the same challenge, but they do
not elucidate a strategy to treat elements fairly. We gather from Pruitt et al.’s work that
they assign a single value for the mass number of elements: the atomic weight. Whereas
for the preliminary non-relativistic model we chose to combine predicted cross sections from
each individual isotope making up a given element in proportion to their abundance, in this
section we adopt Pruitt et al.’s convention. This helps us create a more direct comparison
with K-D, but also sped up training by around 40%, since we no longer needed to simulate
(and back-propagate gradients through) multiple isotopes for elemental data.

Another important adjustment to the dataset was incorporated for this more refined relativistic
version of the NOM: a principled lower energy bound to avoid compound elastic effects on
the differential cross section. Previously we used a cutoff of 5 MeV, but we later determined
that this cutoff was not conservative enough for light nuclei. A full discussion of how a lower
energy bound was determined is presented in Appendix B. The resulting filtered version of
the KDUQ corpus is comprised of 10,525 individual data points spanning 340 experiments.
The filtered Test corpus contains 3431 points from 111 experiments.

5.3.5 Algorithm for choosing number of partial waves
For the non-relativistic version of the NOM, a fixed low value for the maximum number
of partial waves used in the scattering calculation is sufficient (for the model as presented,
Lmax = 15). However, at higher energies a larger number of partial waves is required. Using a
fixed large value such as Lmax = 60 imposes an unnecessarily high computation burden. The
full enhanced Numerov method as summarized in Eqns. (5.64) and (5.65) must be repeated
for each value ℓ = 0 → Lmax for every cross section computed by the code; furthermore,
derivatives must be back-propagated through each of these iterations. It is not possible to
precompute the required number of partial waves for each experiment because the convergence

 72

of the scattering calculation is heavily influenced by the potential itself, which is changing with
every iteration. Therefore we require an heuristic to determine during calculation whether
further partial waves are needed. For inspiration we turn to the open source FRESCOX code
[93] which includes such an heuristic.

FRESCOX is based on the Fresco code [91] developed by Ian Thompson for coupled-channel
nuclear reaction calculations. FRESCOX’s scope is much larger than the elastic neutron
scattering our model addresses, leading to notable differences in how calculations are actually
performed. Most importantly for the partial wave termination routine is the fact that
FRESCOX formulates the scattering problem in a more general ’J basis’, where the spin of
the target nucleus may take on non-zero values. A full explanation of the general scattering
problem which allows interactions as general as nuclei scattering from nuclei is outside our
scope. The crucial takeaway is that FRESCOX code is based on the conservation of angular
momentum blocks denoted J π , where π is parity. The analogous design decision in FRESCOX

5.4.1 Model identifiability
In order to determine whether the potentials being learned are uniquely determined from data,
we perform an ensemble training experiment. Concretely, we wish to determine whether, given
a set prior functional form of the Koning-Delaroche optical potential, do different parametric
realizations of the same model converge to the same inferred potentials once trained on cross
section data? To test this, we initialize 16 models with a single shared architecture of 8
layers with 64 nodes, each having different randomized initial parameters. We pre-train this
models to reproduce Koning-Delaroche across the relevant domain as described in Sec. 5.1.8.
Once these 16 models are appropriately converged, we train them on an identical test-train
split of experimental data. We note that this second training stage is deterministic; the only
disparity between these models is the prior parametrization. We note here that by prior we
refer to the model state prior to training on experimental data, not a statistical prior.

In Fig. 5.14 we examine the potentials recovered by the NOM for an experiment used in
training. There are noteworthy changes to the K-D prior in each of the four terms. In V (r) we
observe a slight upturn of the potential as we move to the center of the nucleus, accompanied
by higher model uncertainty. The well is slightly deeper at the surface, and tapers to 0 MeV
at a slightly smaller radius than does K-D. In W (r) we see that the center-peaked potential
is greater in magnitude than is K-D, along with a high model uncertainty internal to the
nucleus. For VSO we have remained very close to K-D, with a small shift to peaking at a
lower radius. Finally, WSO has collapsed to almost 0, despite being encouraged by design to
be similar to K-D (see Sections 5.1.4 and 5.3.2). Effectively the model has eliminated a term

5.5 Conclusion
Here we have developed a method for inferring a nuclear potential directly from elastic
neutron scattering data with a neural network. We leverage differentiable programming
to propagate the gradient of our loss with respect to model parameters through our code,
including an ODE solution of Schrödinger’s equation. We train on data across a wide range
of nuclei (Z=12 to Z=83) and energies (∼ 5 to 200 MeV), producing a global model of optical
potential for elastic neutron scattering. Our model demonstrated significantly improved
performance over the widely-used Koning-Delaroche optical potential model, beating it by
an average factor of 2.3-2.4 on the data originally used to train K-D. This model, with fast
evaluation time and wide coverage of the space of interest, as well as improved accuracy with
regards to scattering calculations, has the potential to be used within research codes such as
MCNP® [35] for more accurate scattering simulation. This model also displayed an ability
to interpolate between nuclei, making it a useful tool for scattering predictions where data is
sparse or non-existent.

There is a question regarding the unknowability of the quantity learned: how can we be
sure that our model has any meaning, given that direct measurement of the potential is
impossible? This is a valid criticism when viewing the potential as a fundamental quantity of
scientific interest; however, when constrained to the problem of neutron scattering, whether or
not our model is ”meaningful” is primarily determined by its ability to reproduce scattering
data accurately. Furthermore, we demonstrated the robustness of the learned potentials to
varying parametrizations, and were able to interpret the error between models of an ensemble
in physically explicable terms.

The methods we employed here are immediately applicable to learning improved optical
models for inelastic neutron scattering, as well as elastic and inelastic proton scattering.
Whereas elastic scattering models involve interactions where the scattered particle’s energy is

 86

unchanged, inelastic scattering includes the exchange of energy between the projectile and
the nucleus. This energy exchange encodes information about the way nuclei move, making
inelastic models more informative about nuclear behavior.

More important than the model itself is the demonstration of differential programming as a
means to learn from experimental data. While diffprog has seen widespread use in learning
from simulated data in the past decade, rarely has it been applied to an inverse learning
problem with real-world experimental data, and never in the field of nuclear reaction theory
to our knowledge. As detailed in Section 5.1.2, Koning and Delaroche resorted to a complex
and human-involved combination of methods to learn a reasonable global fit to scattering
data. Our method, while requiring a significant human investment in writing differentiable
code up-front, requires no human decision making or even domain expertise when it comes
to training. Furthermore, as new data becomes available, retraining with differentiable
programming already implemented is trivial.

We expect that the use of diffprog to solve scientific problems will grow exponentially with
the increasing number of tools supporting diffprog and papers making use of them. For
example, a parametrized nuclear equation of state could be learned from neutron star data
via gradient descent enabled by diffprog (see [110]). Models for the spread of disease could
be trained on data from previous or ongoing epidemics. Large scale climate science models
could be efficiently trained on climate data. The use of differentiable programming to solve
inverse problems using experimental data has promise in numerous scientific fields.

5.6 Future Work
The work presented here is only scratching the surface of the possibilities of differentiable
programming as applied to the nucleon scattering problem. Much near-term work remains,
including some boilerplate machine learning work such as exploring the space of neural
network architectures by training many different models, or employing a more robust cross
validation scheme. For example, in K-Fold Cross Validation a dataset is split into k ”folds” or
subsets, and the model is trained on k − 1 folds, leaving one out to evaluate for generalization.
This routine is repeated k times, leaving a different fold out of training each time. This
strategy allows a model to train on all available data while still providing robust validation.
Such a scheme would be powerful in our case where data can be quite sparse, and would
allow us to train on the full KDUQ and Test corpora for better coverage of the nuclear chart
without sacrificing validation.

 87

Another important step which may be undertaken immediately is an investigation into the
impact of priors on the learned potential. We elected to use Koning-Delaroche as our prior due
to its popularity and good performance. However, it is important to test whether other priors
of varying similitude to K-D converge to the same potential once trained on experimental
data. The first step in this line of inquiry was already undertaken in this work in Section
5.4.1, specifically determining whether different parametric realization of the same functional
prior lead to the same learned potential. We found that generally they did, with quantifiable
drift: uncertainties in the learned potential of the ensemble corresponded both to regions in
r which are harder to probe (internal to the nucleus), as well as to energy bands where data
is more sparse. This study sets the stage for the more difficult test of varying the functional
form of the prior by contextualizing how much drift in the potential can be attributed to
randomization of initial parameters.

Other near term work includes extension of the model to other observables, such as those
used by Koning and Delaroche in their training: total cross sections and analyzing power.
While total cross section data would likely not provide useful constraints to our model in
regions where we already have differental cross sections to train on, they could potentially fill
experimental gaps in the nuclear chart or energy range. More useful would be the analyzing
power, which is a function of the ratio between the spin-up and spin-down polarized cross
sections. The spin-orbit terms of the optical model have the strongest impact on analyzing
power; therefore this new data type would constrain the spin orbit terms of our model more
tightly than does the differential cross section. Including these observables would also address
a major limitation of our model: we have used Koning-Delaroche as a benchmark to evaluate
performance, but the comparison is made unfair by leaving out these observables which K-D
trained on. It may be ”easier” for our model to fit differential cross sections when it is not
constrained by these other data types.

An active area of research for nuclear modeling in general is that of uncertainty quantification.
Most relevant to our work here is the previously mentioned undertaking by Pruitt et al.
to modernize K-D with uncertainty bounds. Pruitt et al. relied on the commonly used
Markov-Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) method to estimate uncertainties on K-D parameters
and also on predictions made by K-D. Use of MCMC is industry standard, but expensive due
to the many repeated calculations required. Differentiable programming may offer a better,
more computationally efficient way to understand how experimental uncertainty propagates
to model parameters, and how uncertainty in model parameters propagates to observables.
We refer to the Fisher matrix, which quantifies how much information random variables (or
observables) carry about the parameters of a model. The inverse of the Fisher matrix is

 88

the covariance matrix, the diagonal of which can provide uncertainties on the parameters
themselves under certain conditions. A full derivation is outside the scope of this discussion;
however, computation of the Fisher matrix involves taking a second derivative, something
which our codebase is capable of thanks to autodifferentiability. Preliminary work to compute
the Fisher matrix and resulting uncertainties was unsuccessful due to degeneracies in the
model cause by the overparametrization inherent to neural networks. However, it would
be trivial to implement K-D (or a similar model) in the autodifferentiable framework and
calculate the Fisher matrix in a single pass. The much lower dimensionality of K-D lends
itself to this type of analysis, and the autodifferentiable calculation of the Fisher matrix
from experimental data offers a faster but still statistically principled manner of estimating
uncertainty bounds.

More long term work could extend autodifferentiability to much larger and more powerful
codebases. For example, codes like YAHFC [60] and TALYS [33] have the capability to
probe lower energy regimes by modeling the impact of Compound Elastic (CE) effects on the
cross section. By limiting ourselves to energy regions where the CE contribution is negligible
(see Appendix B), we eliminated 31% of the KDUQ corpus and 21% of the Test corpus.
While the idea of rewriting large codebases to add a feature like autodifferentiability seemed
untenable a short time ago, the advent of AI agent-assisted coding has made this sort of
undertaking feasible. In fact, this very problem was tested by an Anthropic researcher using
the Claude AI tool. In [48], Mishra-Sharma uses Claude Opus 4.6 to convert the cosmological
Boltzmann solver code CLASS [37] to Python JAX, enabling gradient-based cosmological
inference. Crucially, the human work involved was minimal: sub-percent accuracy to CLASS
was achieved in a matter of days.

EXFOR Extractor

Reproducing and building upon the work of Koning and Delaroche is made particularly
challenging by the state of the data they used. Most, but not all, of this data is stored
on the EXFOR nuclear reaction database [112]. The data are comprised of hundreds of
disparate experiments performed across decades by different groups at different laboratories,
stored in different formats and in different frames. Pruitt et al [67] undertook the task of
reproducing the original Koning-Delaroche dataset as completely as possible. In their work
they publish tables referring to the data as available today in terms of isotope or element,
energy, and EXFOR identifier. However, they do not publish the resulting dataset or a means
of automatically extracting it from EXFOR. We therefore build upon their work and describe
a tool to reproduce this dataset for our own use and use by future researchers.

6.1 Methods
For the design and testing of this tool, we will focus on the neutron differential elastic cross
sections of what Pruitt et al. dub the KDUQ corpus. We aim to retrieve all of the datasets
listed in this table and homogenize them into a single format and frame for downstream use
by our Neural Optical Model.

6.1.1 EXFOR Retrieval
Pruitt et al.’s tables specify datasets by listing the isotope/element, energy in the laboratory
frame in MeV, and EXFOR accession number. Also referred to as a subID, the accession
number serves as a persistent identifier for data presented in some published source, typically
a paper. Experimental sources often contain results for multiple incident energies; therefore,

 90

what we have termed in our work as an experiment (a single target at a single energy) is not
uniquely identified by EXFOR SubID. To index data by how we define experiment, we must
combine incident energy and SubID in our tool. We scrape the KDUQ table in Pruitt et al.
for these quantities to define a list of desired experiments.

Having obtained the unique identifiers of energy and SubID, we query EXFOR records by
constructing the associated URLs and scraping these web pages for data. Each retrieved
record is inspected for reaction channel, target nucleus, incident energy, measured angle,
differential cross section, and uncertainty. The handling of the various formats for these data
is a distinct task from retrieval, and we will document this effort below.

6.3 Results and Discussion
The product of this work is a robust and extensible EXFOR extraction tool targeted at
elastic neutron scattering differential cross sections. Beginning with the Pruitt et al. modern
reconstruction of the original data used by Koning and Delaroche, this tool retrieves selected
EXFOR subentries, extracts cross section data, standardizes units and frames, filters the
data to the required energies, and produces data tables suitable for training or analyzing
optical potential models.

Our final data corpus contains the data in the format described in Table 6.1. We also retain
information used for auditing our process such as the original frame that the data was stored
in. The source for each dataset is retained–either an EXFOR SubID or the paper source in
case of manually added data.

We note here some limitations to the tool as it stands. The EXFOR Extractor was designed
with the KDUQ dataset as a testbed; this means that there may be some formats of differential
cross section data on EXFOR which are unfamiliar to the tool and could cause errors in
retrieval or in standardization. Furthermore, there are several more data types commonly
used with optical models, namely proton differential cross sections, reaction cross sections,
and analyzing power. While we believe the extension of this tool to these datatypes is
straightforward, these data do represent a missing capability which would be of great use to
scientists working on optical models.

6.4 Conclusion
Here we have presented a tool we developed to enable our own access and standardization
of differential cross section data used by Koning and Delaroche as reconstructed by Pruitt
et al. This tool handles the numerous formats and unit systems encountered on EXFOR
and prepares the data for use by converting to appropriate frames. The tool is flexible to
definitions of EXFOR datasets by incident energy and SubID, as well as to manual addition
of data not available on EXFOR. Future revisions will extend the tool to handle more data
types (reaction cross section, analyzing power) as well as validate it for use on the Pruitt et
al. Test corpus.

Crucially, the existence of the exfor-json database was not discovered until this tool was
already developed. The most difficult and fragile parts of this tool are those designed for
scraping the EXFOR raw site and standardizing the units, both tasks being rendered obselete
by the exfor-json format. A future version of this tool need only interact with exfor-json as

a datasource, extract from it the desired datasets, and translate them to the correct frame
when necessary.

Appendix A

NEM event-by-event distribution
estimation

For our model to be useful and general enough for its intended uses, we require it to be
capable of representing distributions for multiplicities on an event-by-event basis. Concretely,
the model must reproduce distributions ν̂(x) based on the true distribution ν(x) at any given
point x in the parameter space. However, as discussed in Sec. 3.1.1, we do not have direct
access to these distributions ν(x); we only have discrete samples taken from the underlying
distribution at various points across parameter space.

To allow us to characterize multiplicity distributions on an event-by-event basis, we use a
strategy we will refer to as local clustering. In principle, if we group together many data
points centered around a given point x in parameter space, we can obtain an estimate of the
true distribution ν(x) by counting the relative frequency of multiplicity outcomes (0, 1, 2,
...) in that local cluster. By randomly selecting many points xv within the full dataset and
clustering the nearby datapoints xcv of each, we can construct an estimate for ν by assuming
ν(xv ) maps to the distribution defined by the outcomes for multiplicity associated with xcv .

We provide an example estimation of a local distribution in Fig. A.1. Data from CGMF was
filtered for 115 Ru from all reactions in the training set, and three bins for total kinetic energy
(TKE) were chosen. Within each of these three bins, neutron multiplicity outcomes were
counted and divided by the number of datapoints in that bin to produce an estimate of the
local distributions. Note how strongly dependent ν is on TKE. Additionally, P(ν) is unimodal;
unimodality was common across every local distribution we investigated, which enables us to
use a Gaussian distribution mapped to discrete outcomes to model the dependence of ν on

 99

 Figure A.1: An example validation distribution for 115 Ru.

the input space.

For summarizing the performance of the model across an entire data set, e.g. for the decay
reactions created by a specific fission event such as 252 Cf, it is useful to evaluate the model
on an entire dataset and tally the results. These results form a distribution of multiplicities
resulting from a given fission event and can be compared directly to the distribution observed
in the dataset. While such a method does not prove event-by-event performance, it is a useful
metric for many of the possible applications of the emulator.

Appendix B

Low energy cutoff for compound
scattering

To this point we have deferred discussion about the different physical processes which
contribute to the elastic scattering cross section data we are training on. What we have been
treating as a single quantity is typically modeled as having two distinct contributors: direct
scattering and compound-elastic (CE) scattering. Direct scattering has been described in
Section 5.1.1, but CE scattering is a more complex phenomenon mostly beyond the scope
of this work. Whereas direct scattering involves the fast movement of an incident particle
through a target, in CE scattering the incident particle is captured by the target. The
incident particle and target form a compound nucleus for a short period, after which the
incident particle is re-emitted. CE cross sections tend to be symmetric about 90◦ , in contrast
with direct cross sections which are forward-peaked. Experimental measurements have no
means of separating these two types of scattering, so the data discussed in this work are in
fact the sum:

CE scattering is most significant at low energies. Sources vary on energies below which
compound scattering effects may be neglected. For example, the Chapel Hill (CH89) [100]
global optical model uses data above 10 MeV to avoid CE effects. In Molina et al.’s work [50]
on an optical model tailored to 27 Al, a limit of 12 MeV is stated as appropriate. Practically
speaking, the impact of CE on the observed elastic cross section will be dependent on both
mass and the nuclear structure of a given isotope.
