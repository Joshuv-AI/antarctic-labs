ABSTRACT

Machine learning (ML)–based wildfire detection methods have been developed in recent years, primarily using deep learning (DL) models trained on large collections of wildfire images and videos. However, peatland fires exhibit distinct visual and physical characteristics—such as smoldering combustion, low flame intensity, persistent smoke, and subsurface burning—that limit the effectiveness of conventional wildfire detectors trained on open-flame forest fires. In this work, we present a transfer learning–based approach for peatland fire detection that leverages knowledge learned from general wildfire imagery and adapts it to the peatland fire domain. We initialize a DL-based peatland fire detector using pretrained weights from a conventional wildfire detection model and subsequently fine-tune the network using a dataset composed of Malaysian peatland images and videos. This strategy enables effective learning despite the limited availability of labeled peatland fire data. Experimental results demonstrate that transfer learning significantly improves detection accuracy and robustness compared to training from scratch, particularly under challenging conditions such as low-contrast smoke, partial occlusions, and variable illumination. The proposed approach provides a practical and scalable solution for early peatland fire detection and has the potential to support real-time monitoring systems for fire prevention and environmental protection.

Peatland Fires, Smoldering Fire, Peat Fire Detection, Transfer Learning, Deep Learning

Introduction

Wildfires pose a significant threat to ecosystems, infrastructure, and human health worldwide. In recent years, machine learning, and deep learning–based wildfire detection systems have emerged as effective tools for early fire identification using visual data from cameras, drones, and satellites

. These systems typically rely on convolutional neural networks (CNNs), vision transformers or video-based deep learning architectures trained on large datasets of wildfire images and videos, enabling rapid and automated fire detection.

While existing approaches perform well for open-flame forest and grassland fires, their effectiveness is substantially reduced when applied to peatland fires. Peatland fires differ fundamentally from conventional wildfires in both appearance and behavior. They often involve low-temperature smoldering combustion, minimal visible flames, persistent smoke plumes, and underground fire spread. As a result, visual cues commonly used by standard wildfire detectors—such as bright flames or high-contrast fire regions—are often absent or weak in peatland fire scenarios.

Peatland fires are particularly critical due to their long duration, difficulty of suppression, and severe environmental impact, including regional air pollution. Countries with extensive peatland regions, such as Malaysia and Indonesia, face recurring peat fires that demand early and reliable detection to mitigate damage. However, the scarcity of labeled peatland fire data presents a major challenge for training deep learning models from scratch.

To address this challenge, this paper proposes a transfer learning–based peatland fire detection framework. Transfer learning allows a model trained on a large and diverse wildfire dataset to transfer its learned feature representations to a related but distinct domain. In this work, we initialize a deep learning–based peatland fire detector using pretrained weights from a general wildfire detection model. The model is then fine-tuned using a curated dataset of Malaysian peatland fire images and videos, enabling adaptation to the unique visual characteristics of peat fires.

The contributions of this work are threefold:

(i) We demonstrate that transfer learning is an effective strategy for peatland fire detection under limited data conditions.

(ii) We present a peatland fire dataset derived from real-world imagery and videos captured in Malaysian peatland regions.

(iii) We show that the proposed approach improves detection performance and robustness compared to conventional wildfire detection models applied directly to peatland scenarios.

(iv) We use a Walsh Hadamard Transform (WHT)-based ResNet in this application and show that the WHT-based ResNet produces better results than a standard ResNet because the transform is orthogonal, and it learns better, with smaller number of parameters, than the standard ResNet model.

The proposed method offers a practical path toward deployable, real-time peatland fire monitoring systems, supporting early intervention, and contributing to wildfire prevention efforts not only in Malaysia and the United States, but also across diverse geographic regions worldwide. Also, low contrast wildfire signatures also arise in spotting, where lofted embers can be difficult to detect reliably, especially under smoke and variable illumination. The transfer learning and lightweight spectral feature ideas used here are also compatible with ember detection pipelines in beyond visible imagery

2.1
Transfer Learning

Transfer learning is a practical strategy that allows a model trained on one task to be rapidly adapted to a related task with minimal additional data and training time
[
9
]
.
In the context of wildfire and smoke monitoring, this approach is especially valuable because collecting and annotating large image datasets for every new environment or fire type can be costly and time–consuming.
Instead of training a new model from scratch, an already robust wildfire detection model can be reused as a strong starting point and then fine–tuned for a different but related scenario, such as peatland fire or peat smoke detection.

In this work, models that were first trained on the GWFP (Global Wildfire Prevention) dataset were subsequently fine–tuned on the target dataset composed of Malaysian peatland images and videos dataset.
Only a small amount of additional training was required for the model to adapt to the new visual characteristics, such as differences in terrain, vegetation density, and smoke appearance.
This process significantly reduced training effort while maintaining high performance.

Object detection tasks involve identifying the location and classification of objects in images. Usually, the bounding box-based method needs to manually label massive bounding boxes like the Yolo series algorithm

Resizing the images into the input size of neural networks would be a prevalent method, but it can cause the little smoke to go undetected. In order to minimize the loss of information in resizing, images are divided into 60 blocks in 224×224 and each four adjacent blocks will be stitched into one sub-images. Thus, each image will be divided into 45 sub-images that are sent into the neural network. As shown in Fig.
, the score in each block stands for the probability of fire in the sub-image composed of the block with the score and its right, bottom, and bottom-right block, instead of a single block. The effectiveness of this method for high-resolution image processing has been verified in this article. Using this approach, it is possible to detect and locate fires accurately and we do not need to label the bounding box of the wildfire.

Hadamard Transform based Deep Neural Network

The Walsh–Hadamard Transform (WHT) is an orthogonal linear transform that decomposes a signal into a set of binary-valued basis functions
[
1
]
. Owing to its computational simplicity and orthogonality, it has recently been explored in neural network architectures for efficient feature encoding and lightweight representation learning
[
11
]
.

The normalized Hadamard matrix of order
2^{m}
is defined recursively as

H_{m}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix}H_{m-1}&H_{m-1}\\
H_{m-1}&-H_{m-1}\end{bmatrix},\quad H_{0}=[1],

(2)

where
H_{m}H_{m}^{T}=I
, indicating orthogonality. Unlike conventional convolutional filters, Hadamard-based layers operate in the transform domain and replace multiplications with sign-based additions, thereby reducing computational complexity and improving energy efficiency. In practical implementations, we do not divide the matrix coefficients by
\sqrt{2}
, ant the WHT has a fast algorithm.

The main idea of our modified Walsh-Hadamard-transform (WHT)-based convolutional neural network is to perform convolutions in the transform domain, shown in Fig.
, and incorporated into the residual blocks of ResNet50
[
8
]
. It is a well-known theorem that spatial convolutions can be implemented in the Fourier transform domain as elementwise multiplications. However, the Fourier transform requires complex arithmetic, so we replaced it with the binary Hadamard transform
[
11
]
. This operation reduces the number of parameters to learn, when replacing all convolutioal layers with WHT layers as in HTMA-ResNet50
[
7
]
or some convolutioal layers with WHT as in WHT-ResNet50, and the resulting ResNet is more effective than the regular ResNet in small datasets such as the peatland wildfire dataset.

The resulting network is not a binary network
[
17
]
but it is a hybrid network involving real arithmetic and binary operations.

In practical wildfire monitoring systems, F1 is often more informative than accuracy alone, since both over–triggering alarms and failing to detect real smoke events carry operational risks.

Two backbone architectures are evaluated: the standard ResNet–50 and the proposed WHT-ResNet50 (Hadamard Transform Enhanced ResNet–50).
Each architecture is tested with and without transfer learning from the GWFP (Global Wildfire Prevention) dataset.
A value of “Yes” in the Transfer Learning column indicates that the model was first pretrained on the GWFP dataset and then fine–tuned on the target dataset, whereas “No” indicates training from scratch.

In our experiments, transfer learning provides a substantial improvement in performance.
For the baseline ResNet–50, accuracy increases from 71.4% to 88.9%, and F1 improves from 70.1% to 88.2% when GWFP pretraining is applied, demonstrating the benefit of exposure to diverse wildfire and smoke imagery.
Second, the proposed WHT-ResNet50 consistently outperforms the baseline configuration.
Even without transfer learning, WHT-ResNet50 achieves 77.2% accuracy and 77.1% F1, indicating that the Hadamard–based feature enhancement improves the model’s ability to distinguish smoke, flame, and visually similar background patterns.
Finally, the best overall performance is obtained when both enhancements are combined.
WHT-ResNet50 with transfer learning reaches 90.1% accuracy and 89.6% F1, representing the strongest balance between correct detections and reduced false alarms.
The close proximity between ACC and F1 values also indicates stable performance across classes rather than bias toward a dominant category.

From an operational wildfire surveillance perspective, the improvement in F1 is particularly significant, as it reflects a better balance between detecting real fire or smoke events and avoiding unnecessary alerts caused by clouds, fog, or lighting variations.
Overall, these results suggest that combining domain–specific pretraining with spectral feature enhancement yields more reliable and robust wildfire smoke detection performance in real–world scenarios.

4
Conclusion

This study demonstrates that transfer learning is an effective and practical strategy for adapting deep learning–based wildfire detection models to the more challenging task of peatland fire detection.
Unlike open–flame forest fires, peatland fires often exhibit low–intensity combustion, persistent smoke, and visually subtle cues that make them difficult to identify using models trained solely on conventional wildfire imagery.
By initializing the detector with weights learned from a large and diverse wildfire dataset and subsequently fine–tuning it on a smaller peatland–specific dataset, the proposed approach achieves strong detection performance while requiring significantly less data and training effort than building a model from scratch.
