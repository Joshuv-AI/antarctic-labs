Variation in Stabilization and Resuscitation Practices at Birth of Preterm Infants Among 12 National or Regional Neonatal Networks
Authors: Maria Fernanda de Almeida, Ruth Guinsburg, Kjell Helenius, Maximo Vento, Valerie Biran, Tomasz Szczapa, Malcolm Battin, Satoshi Kusuda, Mikael Norman, Simone Pratesi, Mark Adams, Dirk Bassler, Kei Lui, Laura SanFeliciano, Liisa Lehtonen, Gil Klinger, Tetsuya Isayama, Brian Reichman, Marc Beltempo, Prakesh S Shah, iNeo Investigators
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimNeonatal resuscitation follows national or regional guidelines, but uptake varies in clinical practice. This study aimed to examine variations in reported delivery room practices for infants < 29 weeks' gestation across neonatal units in 12 networks of the International Network for Evaluating Outcomes in Neonates.MethodsAn online pre‐piloted survey was sent to 608 neonatal units across 12 networks. Responses, based on 2022–2023 practices, were categorized as very frequent (90%–100%), often (50%–89%), sometimes (10%–49%), rarely (1%–9%), and never, and summarized as frequency of units per network.ResultsOverall, 382 units (63%) responded (37%–100% within network). Active resuscitation at 22, 23 and 24 weeks was reported as ‘very frequent/often’ by 22%, 53% and 76% of units, with variation. Delayed cord clamping, cord milking, and resuscitation with intact cord were ‘very frequent/often’ in 47%, 27% and 7% of units. Japan reported 75% cord milking. For initial respiratory support, 86% reported oxygen concentration < 40%. Intubation at birth for infants 27–28 weeks was uncommon except in Japan. Surfactant delivery room use was reported by 24% of units.ConclusionsDelivery room management of very preterm infants varies across networks. Future studies should analyse the impact of reported delivery room practices on neonatal outcomes.Keywords: delivery room, infant, intensive care units, neonatal premature, questionnaires, resuscitation, surveys

Introduction

Internationally, neonatal resuscitation procedures immediately after birth follow national or multinational guidelines [1, 2, 3, 4, 5], generally based on the International Liaison Committee on Resuscitation (ILCOR) Consensus on Science and Treatment Recommendations [6]. These recommendations are a result of continuous review of scientific literature in order to highlight evidence‐based practices [6]. However, the evidence base for recommendations is variable.

The acceptance and implementation of resuscitation guidelines into clinical practice are critical for improving consistency and sequencing of actions. As highlighted by Corson [7], integrating evidence‐based practices into clinical settings is a complex process. Acceptance is a cognitive construct reflecting the extent to which providers perceive a practice as appropriate [8], whereas implementation refers to the concrete actions undertaken to integrate the new practice into routine care [9].

Despite the existence of international recommendations, variation in reported neonatal care at birth has been reported in Europe. A survey of delivery room practices for infants < 33 weeks' gestation across 262 centres in 33 European countries revealed significant regional differences in umbilical cord management, strategies for thermal control, and ventilation practices, particularly regarding the initial oxygen concentration for infants < 28 weeks' gestation [10].

Examining differences in the neonatal resuscitation and stabilization practices across international networks may inform refinements and adaptations that ensure guidelines remain evidence‐based while also respectful of local contexts and values. Accordingly, the objective of this study was to examine reported variations in delivery room practices for infants < 29 weeks' gestation among neonatal units within 12 population‐based national or regional neonatal networks participating in the International Network for Evaluating Outcomes in Neonates (iNeo).

Method

As reported elsewhere [11], this observational study used a pre‐piloted online questionnaire to assess multiple aspects of care of preterm infants < 29 weeks' gestation, including infrastructure, staffing and neonatal practices. The survey, with 68 questions, was sent to participants of iNeo, which is a collaboration of neonatal networks aiming to provide a platform for comparative evaluation of outcomes of very preterm and very low birth weight neonates at the national and regional levels to generate evidence for improvement of outcomes in these infants [12].

The study was approved by the Research Ethics Board of Mount Sinai Hospital, Toronto, and by the directors of the iNeo collaboration. The participation in iNeo collaboration is approved by regulatory authorities in each country. The questionnaire was sent to the directors or representatives of 608 neonatal units participating in 12 networks: Australia/New Zealand (n = 30), Brazil (n = 20), Canada (n = 32), Finland (n = 5), France (n = 70), Israel (n = 26), Japan (n = 292), Poland (n = 56), Spain (n = 55), Sweden (n = 9), Switzerland (n = 9) and Tuscany, Italy (n = 4). The survey commenced in July 2023 and closed in March 2024. The responses were recorded anonymously as the code for each individual site was distributed by the Network Directors who received them from the iNeo informatics manager. The respondents were instructed to provide answers reflecting their unit‐specific data for 2022 or their protocols during 2022–2023, and not personal preferences. No questions were mandatory, and the time required to complete the survey was around 30–45 min.

Nine questions were relevant to management at birth and comprised the following domains: gestational age limits for active resuscitation (1 question), peripartum interventions (1 question), thermal care (2 questions), respiratory support at birth (5 questions). The complete list of questions with the possible answers are shown in Appendix S2. Briefly the questions related to: (1) Proportion of live‐birth infants who received active resuscitation for each gestation age of 22, 23 and 24 weeks in 2022; (2) Proportion of neonates who received different cord management practices in 2022; (3–4) Presence of written guidelines to avoid hypothermia at birth; (5) Initial oxygen concentration in delivery room; (6) Initial preferred mode of respiratory support and (7–9) Prophylactic surfactant use. The responses were reported as proportion of units in each network and percentages were calculated as appropriate. In the majority of responses, we summarized ‘often or very frequently’ to compare results between networks. We followed CROSS guidelines for reporting this survey results, and completed checklist is in Appendix S3.

Results

Overall, 382/608 units (63%) responded the survey, varying from 37% (108/292 units in Japan) to 100% (Brazil, Finland, Sweden, Switzerland and Tuscany), with a median of 98% of units responding the survey in each network.

Active resuscitation at 22, 23 and 24 weeks' gestation at birth was very frequently or often offered, respectively, by 22%, 53% and 76% of 347 units, with wide variation among networks (Figure 1).

Frequency of units in each network answering that often (50%–89%) or very frequently (90%–100%) live births without major congenital anomaly receive active resuscitation at birth, according to gestational age.

The question regarding cord clamping management was answered by 372 units (Figure 2). Delayed cord clamping was reported as routinely or often performed in 181 units (49%). More than 75% of units from the Australia/New Zealand, Canada, Finland, Israel, Sweden and Switzerland networks indicated that this practice is applied to over 50% of neonates delivered in their centers. Cord milking was reported as routinely or often performed in 101 units (27%). In Japan, 75% of the units reported cord milking as routinely or often performed. In contrast, Australia/New Zealand, Brazil, Canada, Finland, Sweden, Switzerland and Tuscany networks indicated that this practice was never performed. Resuscitation with intact cord was mentioned as routinely or often practiced in 25 units (7%).

Frequency of units in each network answering that often (50%–89%) or very frequently (90%–100%) for delayed cord clamping, cord milking, or initiation of resuscitation with intact cord.

Written guidelines to avoid hypothermia were referred by 280 (73%) of 382 units, with 100% of units reporting their availability in Israel, Sweden, Switzerland and Tuscany. Only 60% of the units of the Finish and Japanese networks responded that they have written guidelines to avoid hypothermia. Among the 280 units that reported having guidelines, the inclusion of the following components was mentioned: use of warm towel—76%, plastic wrap—90%, preheated warmer—92%, thermal mattress—55%, and heated gases—43%. Only in Sweden, at least 80% of the units reported that all thermal care procedures at birth were included in their guidelines.

Use of initial oxygen concentration less than 40% for respiratory support at birth was reported by 92% of 360 units. All units from Finland, France, Spain, Sweden and Tuscany mentioned that the most commonly initial oxygen concentration for resuscitation in the delivery room of preterm infants < 29 weeks' gestation was 21%–39%. One unit in Brazil, one in Poland and 4 units in Japan responded that their initial oxygen concentration for resuscitation at birth was 60% or more.

Tracheal tube as the preferred interface for ventilatory support at birth decreased as gestational age increased (chi‐square for trend; p < 0.0001). For infants born at 26–27 weeks, preference for intubation at birth was less frequent across networks, except for Japan (Table 1). Prophylactic surfactant use in the delivery room was reported by 25% of 374 units, varying from zero (Brazil) to 40%–43% (Finland and Poland) (Figure 3).

Discussion

Marked variations in all domains of delivery room management of infants < 29 weeks' gestation were reported across the 12 networks that participated in the iNeo survey. It should be noted that 11 networks represent high‐income countries with low neonatal mortality rates. In 2022, the human development index (HDI) [13] of these countries varied from 0.881 (Poland) to 0.967 (Switzerland) and the neonatal mortality rate (NMR) [14] ranged from 0.79 (Japan) to 3.33 (Canada) per thousand live births. Only the Brazilian Neonatal Network adds data from a middle income setting and, in 2022, the HDI was 0.760 and the NMR, 8.56. In this context, the participating networks represent countries where facilities, equipment, and human resources for neonatal care at birth are likely widely available. Moreover, in all networks studied, multinational or national neonatal resuscitation guidelines are regularly issued and updated [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]. Therefore, the variation observed in our results is unlikely to be explained by differences in the availability of delivery room infrastructure for preterm care, but rather by the degree of acceptance and incorporation of evidence‐based guidelines into routine clinical practice. In fact, examination of how ILCOR recommendations are interpreted within national or multinational guidelines adopted by the participating networks across the various topics addressed in the study questionnaire (Appendix S4) reveals subtle differences in the recommended management of preterm infants at birth. These differences are likely to be further magnified at the individual unit level within each network.

Survival of preterm infants of 22–24 weeks' gestation depends largely on ethical considerations, treatment policies, institutional expertise, and maternal and neonatal characteristics, as well as on the perinatal team's subjective judgement regarding viability and the willingness to pursue proactive management. Collectively, these variables may influence how different guidelines frame recommendations regarding proactive treatment of extremely immature infants (Appendix S4a). Consequently, survival rates are influenced by patient‐, caregiver‐, and institution‐level factors, resulting in wide variability across countries of different income levels and over time. A systematic review of 69 studies from 25 countries, encompassing 56 526 live births and 59 104 neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) admissions, reported survival rates among live births of 7%, 27% and 52% at 22, 23 and 24 weeks' gestation, respectively, whereas survival among NICU‐admitted infants at the same gestational ages was 31%, 45% and 62% [15]. Therefore, the differences found in our study among networks on intention to offer active resuscitation in these periviable infants have direct impact on chances of survival. In fact, an analysis of 11 neonatal networks participating in the iNeo collaboration, including 846 infants at 22 weeks and 4173 at 23 weeks' gestation without major congenital anomalies admitted between 2015 and 2021, demonstrated survival to discharge ranging from 0% to 64% at 22 weeks and 15%–80% at 23 weeks across networks [16].

ILCOR highlights the benefits of cord clamping for a minimum time of 30 s after birth for uncomplicated preterm births since 2010 [17]. However, providing delayed cord clamping requires deliberate strategies for implementation, especially when the practice does not have perceived benefit to practitioners or health systems in terms of time or money [18]. Chiruvolu et al. distributed an electronic survey to neonatologists working in the USA in order to identify and describe cord clamping practices. Among 517 respondents, they found that, although the majority of practitioners reported having a guideline/protocol, there were variations with regard to timing, method, and performance in higher‐risk deliveries. Demographic and professional factors played an important role in these variations [19]. These findings are in line with the results presented in our survey. Half of all studied units reported delayed cord clamping as the preferred strategy for cord management, but most Japanese units prefer cord milking. It is interesting to note that, since 2015, ILCOR [20] suggested against intact cord milking for infants < 28 weeks' gestation, considering that severe intraventricular haemorrhage may be increased compared to delayed cord clamping. In Japan, the recommended practice is a single milking or stripping of the cut umbilical cord with a length of about 30 cm [3], and there is insufficient data to recommend pro or against cut cord milking [6].

Neonatal hypothermia is a global health challenge, affecting infants in both resource‐limited and resource‐rich settings [21]. Since 2015, the ILCOR has highlighted admission temperature as a critical predictor of neonatal outcomes, advocating its documentation [20]. Availability of guidelines to standardize care can help reduce human error and ensure compliance with safety standards. This is particularly important in the unpredictable environment of the delivery room, where the composition of the multidisciplinary team and the availability of equipment may vary, and where noise and chaos are sometimes inherent to the clinical situation. Thirty seven percent of the units that responded the survey do not have written guidelines regarding bundles to maintain normothermia at birth. Among those units that have guidelines, the adoption of the recommended interventions to improve thermoregulations of preterm infants vary. There is thus room for improvement in the majority of units across the 12 networks.

Use of initial oxygen concentration < 40% in preterm infants who require resuscitation at birth was consistent among respondents. Since the survey was performed new data have been published [22] that may influence future guidelines and practice. An individual patient data network meta‐analysis with 1055 infants suggests that high initial oxygen concentration (≥ 90%) may be associated with reduced mortality in preterm infants < 32 weeks' gestation compared to low initial oxygen concentration [22], but more evidence is required.

As expected, the lower the gestational age, the greater the use of invasive respiratory support. According to Kusuda et al., the first step in postnatal stabilization is initiated by positive airway pressure with a bag and mask. Once the heart rate increases above 100 bpm, intratracheal intubation should be achieved because it is unusual for a micropreemie (defined as babies born below 500 g birthweight and/or below 24 weeks gestation) to breathe spontaneously or by non‐invasive respiratory support for a protracted duration [23]. It is interesting, however, that Japanese units, that have the lowest mortality rate of neonates with 22 and 23 weeks [16], also choose intubation as the preferred interface for ventilatory support at birth at more advanced gestational ages, such as 25–28 weeks. The same finding was noted in a recent survey that found limited adoption of the avoidance of mechanical ventilation in Japan [24]. It seems that priority is given to stabilizing the infant's respiratory condition over avoiding lung injury.

Finally, use of prophylactic surfactant in the delivery room for infants less than 29 weeks was reported by 25% of respondents. Recently, there has been an attempt to combine the benefits of surfactant therapy with the avoidance of tracheal ventilation, by using strategies that deliver surfactant with brief or no endotracheal ventilation, but, there is no consensus with regard to timing and location of surfactant therapy [25]. It is important to note that the question addressing prophylactic surfactant use was left open‐ended, as shown in Supplement 2, without providing a formal definition of prophylactic surfactant. Consequently, the observed variability in responses may, in part, reflect differences in how respondents defined or interpreted prophylactic surfactant use.

The results presented here stem from a large collaborative survey on reported delivery room practices for preterm infants born at < 29 weeks' gestation, encompassing 382 units across 12 international neonatal networks. As a survey, the findings provide a snapshot of intended practices at birth, but they do not necessarily reflect what actually occurs in clinical settings. Although participation was broad, the results mainly represent high‐income countries and cannot be considered a picture of global practice. It should be noted that data were not collected on which resuscitation guidelines were followed by each of the 382 neonatal units at the time of the survey, nor on how these units were audited with respect to the reported neonatal care practices at birth. The variations we observed could be due to the fact that some of the evidence base for recommendations issued by the different organizations is not strong.

In conclusion, substantial variation exists in the delivery room management of preterm infants across the networks participating in the iNeo collaboration. The acceptance and implementation of international resuscitation recommendations differ among networks. Future research should assess the impact of reported delivery room practices on neonatal mortality and morbidity.
