Detection of Spatio‐Temporal Dispersion Electrograms Using Complex Signal Identification‐Labeled Abnormal Potential Areas in Patients With Persistent Atrial Fibrillation
Authors: Kazuki Ito, Yoshihisa Naruse, Yuki Tokonami, Yutaro Kaneko, Makoto Sano, Yuichiro Maekawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionAblation targeting spatio‐temporal dispersion electrograms (STDEs) in addition to pulmonary vein isolation (PVI) has been reported to reduce atrial fibrillation (AF) recurrence in patients with persistent AF.BackgroundHowever, STDE identification remains subjective. Complex Signal Identification (CSI), integrated into the CARTO 3 mapping system, automatically quantifies electrogram characteristics. We evaluated whether CSI parameters can accurately identify STDEs.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed intracardiac electrogram points from five consecutive patients who underwent catheter ablation for persistent AF using the CARTO 3 version 8 mapping system (Biosense Webster, Diamond Bar, CA, USA) between October and December 2024 to evaluate the ability of CSI parameters to identify STDEs. STDEs were identified and marked by the operator using an OCTARAY catheter.ResultsA total of 4453 electrogram points obtained before ablation were analyzed. Among all CSI metrics, the fractionation score demonstrated the highest diagnostic accuracy. An optimal cutoff of 4.1 yielded an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.905 (95% CI: 0.892–0.918), with a sensitivity of 80.4%, specificity of 98.6%, positive predictive value of 94.1%, and negative predictive value of 93.1%. In contrast, complex duration (cutoff ≥ 50 ms), %cycle length (cutoff ≥ 15%), and complex onset (cutoff ≤ −32) showed AUCs of 0.613 (95% CI: 0.557–0.668), 0.599 (95% CI: 0.538–0.660), and 0.525 (95% CI: 0.461–0.589), respectively.ConclusionA CSI fractionation score cutoff of 4.1 identified STDEs with high accuracy, suggesting that CSI‐guided mapping may serve as a practical tool for adjunctive ablation beyond PVI.Keywords: CARTO 3 version 8, complex signal identification, persistent atrial fibrillation, spatio‐temporal dispersion electrograms

Pulmonary vein isolation (PVI) alone achieves an approximately 60% non‐recurrence rate in persistent atrial fibrillation (AF) [1], underscoring the need for additional substrate modification. Spatio‐temporal dispersion electrograms (STDEs) are proposed markers of the arrhythmogenic substrate, and STDE‐guided ablation improves the outcomes [2]. However, STDE identification relies on the subjective visual assessment of high‐density electrograms, promoting the demand for objective methods. Previously, we found the EnSite system's fractionation map (Abbott, Abbott Park, IL, USA) useful for detecting STDEs [3]. The complex signal identification (CSI) algorithm in the CARTO 3 system (Biosense Webster, Diamond Bar, CA, USA) automatically quantifies electrogram features using four parameters, potentially enabling the objective detection of fractionated signals. Therefore, we investigated whether CSI‐derived metrics can objectively identify the STDE regions in persistent AF.

We retrospectively analyzed 4453 intracardiac electrogram points from five patients who underwent catheter ablation for persistent AF between October and December 2024. The median age was 76 years (range, 40–81 years), and all patients were male. The median body mass index was 23.4 kg/m2 (range, 18.0–25.6), the median left atrial volume index was 44 mL/m2 (range, 38–77), and the median duration of atrial fibrillation was 10 months (range, 3–48 months). High‐density mapping was performed using an OCTARAY multipolar catheter (Biosense Webster). STDEs are defined as an ensemble of intracardiac electrograms forming a localized sequential activation in a distinct area, in which clusters of three or more adjacent bipolar electrograms show an intracardiac activation spanning the entire AF cycle length. This pattern suggests localized reentrant‐like conduction, indicating a role in initiating or maintaining AF [2]. During the ablation procedure, STDE determination was performed by two electrophysiology specialists—the operator and the assistant—reaching a consensus.

Mapping quality was managed: a location stability filter measuring 4 mm in size (no SMART INDEX). Due to AF variability, the waveform amplitude and local activation time filters were omitted. Bipolar electrograms were filtered at 16–500 Hz and unipolar at 2–240 Hz. Unstable catheter positions, noisy data, and unclear electrograms were excluded from the analysis.

The CSI parameters were automatically calculated using CARTO version 8 for each point under uniform conditions, without operator involvement. In AF, reference atrial waves with constant intervals were chosen. The coronary sinus catheter was used as a reference for CSI computation in all cases. AF cycle length stability was confirmed by measuring 30 consecutive atrial cycle lengths (A–A intervals) at the reference site in each patient, with a coefficient of variation below 15% (Supporting Information S1: Table S1). Supporting Information S1: Table S2 summarizes the A‐A interval stability during mapping. A coefficient variation of ≤ 15% was observed throughout, confirming stable intervals. CSI maps were reviewed with various window of interest (WOI) settings (100, 200, 400, 800 ms) to compare with STDE; the 200 ms WOI was the most consistent, and it was used for all cases. The CSI tag settings had a fixed score cutoff and a minimum wave duration of 50 ms, with no restrictions on amplitude or timing within the WOI. A receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was performed on individual and pooled data from five cases to assess the diagnostic performance of the CSI parameters.

Statistical analyses were conducted in EZR (version 1.68; Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University). The study was approved by the Ethics Review Committee of Hamamatsu University School of Medicine (Research Number: 24‐296). The authors declare no conflicts of interest. All scientific content and interpretations were reviewed and approved by the authors.

Among all CSI‐derived metrics, the fractionation score demonstrated the highest diagnostic accuracy for identifying STDE. An ROC analysis using pooled data revealed an optimal fractionation score cutoff of 4.1, yielding an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.905 (95% confidence interval [CI] 0.892–0.918), with a sensitivity of 80.4% and specificity of 98.6%. Diagnostic performance based on the fractionation score was generally consistent across individual cases, indicating reproducibility (Figure 1). In contrast, a complex duration (cutoff ≥ 50 ms), %cycle length (cutoff ≥ 15%), and a complex onset (cutoff ≤ −32) showed a substantially lower diagnostic performance, with AUC values of 0.613, 0.599, and 0.525, respectively. Bipolar voltage mapping during AF (< 0.2 mV) demonstrated minimal diagnostic value (AUC 0.301).

For the pooled data, at an optimal cutoff value of 4.1, the specificity, sensitivity, and AUC were 98.6%, 80.4%, and 0.905, respectively. A region‐based overlap heatmap demonstrated substantial spatial concordance between the STDE and CSI‐positive regions across the left atrium (Figure 2). After pooling the mapping points from five patients and defining CSI positivity as a fractionation score ≥ 4.1, the Dice coefficients were calculated for eight anatomical regions. High Dice values were observed in the pulmonary vein antral regions, posterior wall, and roof, whereas relatively lower values were noted in the anterior wall and the septum. These findings indicate regional variability in the concordance between STDE and CSI across the left atrium. Similar trends were observed in individual cases. Despite regional variability in mapping point counts, consistently high Dice coefficients across multiple regions indicate that CSI‐based fractionation mapping captures the key spatial features of the STDE. These findings support the use of CSI as an objective surrogate marker for atrial substrate complexity.

CSI was calculated under standardized conditions, ensuring reproducibility. The consistent diagnostic performance across individual cases and pooled analyses, along with the spatial overlap between STDE and CSI‐positive regions, supports the objectivity of CSI labeling. The fractionation score within the WOI is determined by the wave duration, sharpness, zero sensing, and voltage to capture electrogram fragmentation. Electrogram fractionation signals atrial remodeling and conduction heterogeneity [4] and may indicate an arrhythmogenic AF substrate unique to each patient [5]. Although STDE‐guided ablation can improve the outcomes [2], automated STDE tools are not widely available. In contrast, the CSI, built into the CARTO system, makes substrate assessment accessible in routine clinical practice.

This study has several limitations. First, STDE is not a definitive standard, and it depends on the operator's interpretation. Second, the proprietary CSI algorithm is not fully transparent; thus, this study evaluated only the clinical and electrophysiological relevance of its outputs. Third, this was a single‐center exploratory study with a small sample size. The point‐level analysis may have reduced the effective sample size. Furthermore, the limited number of patients restricts the assessment of inter‐patient heterogeneity, and it may limit generalizability. Finally, because only male patients were included, the applicability of our findings to female patients remains uncertain and requires further investigation.

This study shows that CSI‐derived parameters consistently align with independently annotated STDE regions in persistent AF, supporting the CSI as a reliable marker of AF substrate complexity. CSI‐guided fractionation mapping could help identify arrhythmogenic substrates and guide ablation beyond pulmonary vein isolation. Further research is needed to compare the CSI‐guided and conventional ablation strategies and assess their clinical impact.
