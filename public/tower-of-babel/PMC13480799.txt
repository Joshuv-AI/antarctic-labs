Strain Engineering via W–O–Ru Interfacial Coupling to Suppress Lattice Oxygen Activation for Stable Acidic Water Electrolysis
Authors: Yi Guan, Xiaozhang Yao, Ruiwen Qi, Xiangzhong Ren, Gaowa Liu, Zhongxin Song, Lei Zhang, Xueliang Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDeveloping highly active and durable acidic oxygen evolution reaction (OER) electrocatalysts remains a central challenge for proton‐exchange membrane water electrolysis (PEMWE). Here, we combine theory‐guided design, atomic‐layer engineering, and operando spectroscopy to create a structurally robust, mechanistically tuned Ru‐based catalyst. Density functional theory reveals that depositing W1O3 onto RuO2 maximizes Ru and O vacancy formation energies, outperforming other tested transition metals. Guided by this, we employ atomic layer deposition to construct atomically coupled W–O–Ru interfacial units on RuO2 (W–O–RuO2), generating a tensile‐stressed surface while preserving the rutile core. Comprehensive in situ spectroscopy and mass spectrometry demonstrate that this architecture effectively suppresses lattice–oxygen activation, shifting the reaction from a lattice–oxygen mechanism to a more reversible adsorbate evolution mechanism. Operando x‐ray absorption spectroscopy confirms the dynamic stability of the W–O–Ru interface during OER, which evolves into a resilient, mildly compressive (1%) state without degrading. Consequently, W–O–RuO2 demands a mere 168 mV overpotential at 10 mA cm− 2 and sustains 1 A cm− 2 in a PEMWE device for 1000 h with an ultra‐low degradation rate of 63.3 µV/h. This work establishes interfacial unit engineering as a generalizable blueprint for designing exceptionally stable acidic OER catalysts.Keywords: density functional theory screening, dynamic strain evolution, interfacial engineering, operando X‐ray absorption spectrum, proton‐exchange membrane water electrolysis

Introduction

Proton exchange membrane water electrolysis (PEMWE) represents a highly promising approach for green hydrogen production, offering high current densities, exceptional energy efficiency, and robust safety [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7]. Nevertheless, its broader implementation remains constrained by high operating voltages, sluggish anodic oxygen evolution reaction (OER) kinetics, and severe electrocatalyst degradation under harsh acidic conditions. Ruthenium (Ru) is recognized as a promising alternative to Ir due to its modest cost (US$9, 523 kg−1), earth abundance, and intrinsic OER activity [8, 9]. Although the moderate binding affinities of intermediates (O*, OH*, and OOH*) on Ru sites contribute to the high OER efficiency of Ru‐based catalysts, the formation of oxygen vacancies during oxygen evolution under acidic conditions leads to the overoxidation of Ru cations, resulting in the dissolution, rapid degradation of Ru active species (Run+, n > 4), and poor stability issues [10, 11, 12, 13, 14]. Therefore, the development of Ru‐based catalysts with outstanding operational stability is both necessary and highly challenging.

Strain engineering plays a pivotal role in optimizing Ru‐based acidic oxygen evolution reaction (OER) catalysts, yet achieving a balance between activity and stability remains challenging [15, 16, 17, 18]. While tensile strain enhances stability by weakening Ru–O covalency and suppressing the lattice–oxygen–mediated mechanism (LOM), it often compromises activity due to intermediate overbinding caused by an upshift in the Ru d‐band center [11, 19, 20]. Conversely, compressive strain optimizes adsorption energetics but typically fails to prevent thermodynamic instability of lattice oxygen [21, 22, 23, 24]. Therefore, investigation of strain regulation to simultaneously suppress the LOM and optimize diverse intermediate adsorption emerges as a superior alternative [25, 26, 27, 28, 29]. Surface decoration of M–O species on Ru‐based catalysts holds great promise in offering lattice stabilization and charge redistribution by localizing modification at the interface [6, 30, 31]. Notably, surface M–O–Ru moieties are not static, and their bond lengths can evolve during operation. This intrinsic adaptability creates the possibility of dynamic strain transition, which enables the catalyst to meet the competing demands of activity and stability. Therefore, constructing atomic M–O species on RuO2 and exploring the dynamic strain evolution are critical to effectively decouple the activity–stability trade‐off in acidic OER.

Here, we constructed atomically coupled W–O–Ru interfacial units to stabilize RuO2 and to enhance its OER catalytic function. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations show that W1O3 species markedly increase the formation energies of Ru and O vacancies, confirming their stabilizing potential. Subsequently, we developed a controlled atomic layer deposition (ALD) process to construct atomically dispersed W–O–Ru interfacial units with a W1O3‐like coordination environment on RuO2, thereby generating a tensile–strained interface. In situ Raman and differential electrochemical mass spectrometry (DEMS) reveal a pronounced suppression of lattice–oxygen involvement, and in situ attenuated total reflection surface‐enhanced infrared absorption spectroscopy (ATR–SEIRAS) confirms a dominant AEM pathway that contributes to the stable acid OER. Moreover, operando x‐ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS) combined with DFT analysis indicates that the OER occurs at the W–O–RuO2 interface through a W–Ru dual‐site cooperative mechanism, in which W sites enhance the reactivity of adjacent Ru centers via synergistic electronic and structural coupling. These W–O–Ru interfacial units remain robust under OER conditions, evolving a mildly compressive (∼1%) yet non‐degraded configuration after long‐term operation, indicating their capacity for dynamic interface strain accommodation during electrocatalysis. Benefiting from this stabilized electronic structure and mechanism regulation, W–O–RuO2 achieves 10 mA cm− 2 at an overpotential of only 168 mV and sustains 1000 mA cm− 2 in a PEMWE with a minimal degradation rate of 63.3 µV/h for 1000 h. These findings establish interfacial electronic and strain modulation as a powerful blueprint for designing ultra‐stable acidic OER catalysts and provide a practical route toward durable Ru‐based anodes for large‐scale green hydrogen production.

Results

Theoretical Investigation of M–O on RuO2

To gain insights into the effect of M–O decoration on the surface stability of RuO2 catalysts, we conducted DFT simulations. The RuO2(110) surface as the thermodynamically stable facet was considered. Under harsh acidic OER conditions, three surface sites are susceptible to vacancy formation on RuO2(110): the coordinatively unsaturated Ru site (RuCUS), the coordinatively unsaturated O site (OCUS), and the fully coordinated bridge O site (OBRI), as shown in Figure 1a,b, and Figure S1a [32, 33, 34].

(c) Configuration of M1O3 species anchored on RuO2 (M–O–RuO2); red, silver–white, and purple spheres denote O, Ru, and M atoms, respectively. (d) Calculated RuCUS vacancy formation energies for M–O–RuO2 and bare RuO2. (e) Oxygen vacancy formation energies at OBRI and OCUS sites for M–O–RuO2 and RuO2. (f) Projected density of states (PDOS) of O 2p and Ru 4d orbitals for W–O–RuO2 and RuO2. (g) Band centers of O 2p and Ru 4d orbitals for M‐O‐RuO2 and RuO2.

To strictly adhere to the control–variable principle and isolate the intrinsic electronic modulations of different metal species, a unified surface coordinate descriptor was required. Therefore, we constructed highly oxidized M1O3–modified RuO2 (M–O–RuO2, M = Fe, Co, Mn, Ni, Cu, Nb, Zr, and W) models to evaluate how M–O deposition influences the stability of Ru active sites (Figure 1c and Figure S1b–i). This M1O3 motif was purposefully selected to mimic the highly oxidized precatalytic surface states typically induced by the strong anodic potentials during the acidic OER. By systematically comparing eight different M1O3 species, we found that the introduction of M1O3 significantly suppresses the formation of Ru vacancies. The Ru vacancy formation energy of M–O–RuO2 is markedly higher than that of bare RuO2 (Figure 1d and Table S1). Notably, the W–O–RuO2 surface exhibits the highest RuCUS vacancy formation energy among the evaluated transition metals (4.14 eV), nearly twice that of pristine RuO2 (2.29 eV). Moreover, the incorporation of M1O3 species on the RuO2 surface substantially suppresses oxygen vacancy formation, as shown in Figure 1e and Table S1. Among all systems studied, W–O–RuO2 shows the highest oxygen vacancy formation energies for both OBRI (3.42 eV) and OCUS (3.45 eV) sites, significantly exceeding those of bare RuO2 (2.72 eV for OBRI, 2.70 eV for OCUS). These results clearly demonstrate that the introduction of W1O3 species effectively enhances surface stability by suppressing both Ru and O vacancy formation on RuO2(110).

To further elucidate the strong inhibition effect of M1O3 species on vacancy formation in RuO2, we analyzed the valence bond electronic structure. The projected density of states (PDOS) of the O 2p states, which are closely associated with lattice oxygen reactivity, are presented in Figure 1f,g, Figure S2, and Table S2 [35, 36]. Upon deposition of M1O3 species onto the RuO2 surface, a distinct downshift of the O 2p band center was observed. In particular, W–O–RuO2 exhibits the most negative O 2p band center (–3.52 eV), substantially lower than that of pure RuO2 (–3.25 eV). This downshift of the O 2p band center, together with the increased charge density of lattice oxygen, contributes to the enhanced stabilization of the lattice oxygen and the suppression of oxygen vacancy formation. Interestingly, while the Ru 4d band center in most M‐O‐RuO2 systems shifts downward relative to that of pure RuO2 (–1.90 eV), W–O–RuO2 displays a significant upshift to –1.59 eV, suggesting a possible shift of the redox‐active center from lattice oxygen to the metal site. Furthermore, the W 5d band center (–1.50 eV) is found close to that of Ru 4d in W–O–RuO2, implying strong d–d electronic coupling between W and Ru, as shown in Figure S3 and Table S2. Such coupling facilitates electron delocalization from Ru sites across the W–O–Ru framework, preventing excessive electron localization on individual Ru atoms and thus avoiding irreversible over‐oxidation [37]. Additionally, the electronic states of W can modulate the charge distribution at neighboring Ru sites, enabling synergistic catalysis during the reaction. The charge density difference analysis further confirms that W–O deposit substantially modifies the surface charge distribution of RuO2 (Figure S4). This modulation arises from electronic regulation through O bridge bonds, which tune the Ru electronic structure closer to the catalytically active configuration, consistent with the PDOS results.

While the above unified descriptor screening highlights W as the optimal electronic modifier, we further sought to validate the thermodynamic feasibility of this specific theoretical configuration. We calculated the formation energies of various surface W–Ox intermediate states (W SAC, W1O1, W1O2, and W1O3) on the RuO2(110) surface. As shown in Figure S5, the W1O3 configuration exhibits a stable low formation energy of –4.84 eV, sitting at the absolute thermodynamic energy minimum compared to their other oxygen–deficient states (+5.73 eV for W SAC–RuO2, +0.71 eV for W1O1–RuO2, and –0.013 eV for W1O2–RuO2). This stringent thermodynamic evaluation confirms that surface‐anchored W intrinsically favors the highly oxidized W1O3 coordination in the W–O–RuO2.

Synthesis and Characterization of W–O–RuO2

Guided by the aforementioned theoretical design, we developed a precise bottom‐up strategy to construct the targeted electrocatalyst. Initially, a wet impregnation method was employed to load the Ru precursor onto a carbon black support, followed by H2/Ar annealing to obtain Ru/C nanoparticles. Subsequently, the Ru/C material was thermally treated in air, converting the Ru nanoparticles into RuO2 while simultaneously removing the carbon support. To accurately replicate the theoretically predicted W1O3 motif, we employed ALD to graft a monolayer of atomically dispersed W–O–Ru interfacial units onto RuO2 crystals, achieving the targeted W–O–RuO2 material (Figure 2a).

X‐ray diffraction (XRD) analysis confirms the phase purity of the rutile RuO2 structure (ICSD: 43–1027) with no discernible peak shifts, corroborating that the modification was strictly confined to the surface without perturbing the bulk crystal lattice, as such highly localized strain heterogeneity is insufficient to shift volume‐averaged XRD patterns, as shown in Figure S6 [22, 38, 39, 40]. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and high‐angle annular dark‐field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF–STEM) reveal that the synthesized RuO2 nanoparticles are ~30.5 nm in diameter and exhibit a well–defined lattice spacing of 3.16 Å corresponding to the (110) plane (Figure S7). Following ALD treatment, SEM imaging reveals that the W–O–RuO2 nanoparticles retain a morphology and size distribution comparable to those of the pristine RuO2 (Figure S8a,b). At the atomic scale, HAADF–STEM further identifies a distinct amorphous W–O overlayer (< 1 nm) at the RuO2 surface, yielding the W–O–RuO2 catalyst featuring atomically dispersed W–O–Ru interfacial units, as shown in Figure 2b. The underlying RuO2 lattice remains intact, as evidenced by HAADF–STEM spacings of 3.16 Å and 2.24 Å for the (110) and (002) planes, respectively (Figure 2c,d). Notably, Figure 2e reveals a dense and uniform distribution of W atoms. The absence of any crystalline W–O lattice fringes rules out the formation of bulk WOx phases, instead indicating that W species are highly dispersed and confined to the surface, forming atomically dispersed W–O–Ru interfacial units. The intrinsic lattice mismatch between this local W1O3 coordination environment and the rutile RuO2 framework forces the deposited W1O3 layer to conform to the shorter Ru–O surface spacing. Consequently, this geometric constraint induces strong interfacial W–O–Ru bonds and generates a localized surface tensile strain of approximately 2%, as visualized by geometric phase analysis (GPA), a widely established method for quantitative lattice strain mapping in nanoscale systems (Figure 2f) [41, 42, 43]. Crucially, a localized lattice expansion is observed at the W–O–RuO2 interface, where the surface interplanar spacing increases from 3.16 to 3.24 Å (Figure S8c,d). Such atomic‐resolution evidence provides a consistent match with the ∼2% surface tensile strain revealed by GPA mapping. Besides, energy‐dispersive x‐ray spectroscopy (EDS) elemental mapping also confirms that the atomic W layer is confined to the surface of RuO2 without penetrating the lattice (Figure 2g).

Finally, to demonstrate the universality of this interfacial coupling strategy, we deposited the ALD W coating onto commercial RuO2 (Com–RuO2). The resulting W–O–Com–RuO2 surface exhibits a highly similar localized tensile strain (Figure S9), confirming that ALD–mediated W–O deposition is a robust and highly generalizable method for nanoscale RuO2 strain modulation.

Structure Identification of W–O–RuO2

The electronic structure of W–O–RuO2 was determined using x‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). The high–resolution W 4f spectrum displays two well‐defined peaks at 32.6 and 34.6 eV, corresponding to W 4f7/2 and W 4f5/2, respectively. These features are characteristic of W4 + species and indicate the successful formation of a W–O–Ru interfacial structure with strong electronic coupling between W and Ru (Figure 3a) [44, 45]. The high‐resolution Ru 3p XPS spectrum of W–O–RuO2 exhibits the characteristic doublets between 460 and 485 eV, attributed to Ru 3p3/2, 3p1/2, and their satellite peaks (Figure 3b) [46, 47]. While the overall spectral profile and peak positions confirm that the predominant oxidation state of Ru remains +4, a subtle yet distinct positive binding energy shift of 0.1 eV is observed relative to pure RuO2. This shift indicates a modified electronic environment around Ru sites, likely arising from electronic interaction with the neighboring W–O–Ru interfacial units, suggesting strong interfacial coupling without a formal change in the Ru oxidation state.

The inset in (c) shows an enlarged view of the white‐line intensity region. Fourier‐transformed EXAFS spectra of (d) the W L3 edge and (f) the Ru K edge. To confirm the successful construction of atomically dispersed W–O–Ru interfacial units with a W1O3–like coordination environment on the RuO2 surface via the ALD process, W–O–RuO2 was further characterized using XAS. As shown in Figure 3c, the W L3 edge XANES profile of W–O–RuO2 exhibits a distinct white line (WL) intensity positioned explicitly between those of the W foil (W0) and WO3 (W6 +) references. Furthermore, the first derivative of the XANES spectrum (Figure S10a) unambiguously places the absorption edge within the 0 to +6 range. Quantitative linear combination fitting (LCF) of the XANES data determines an average oxidation state of +3.3 ± 0.04 for the deposited W (Figure S10b), which is highly consistent with the W 4f XPS results. Notably, the stabilization of this subvalent W state, rather than the thermodynamically preferred bulk W6+, is driven by the kinetically controlled ALD process utilizing a zero–valent W(CO)6 precursor, combined with strong interfacial charge modulation between the surface W species and the RuO2 substrate [48]. The local structural environment of W was investigated by analyzing EXAFS spectra. For W–O–RuO2, the W EXAFS Fourier transform (FT) magnitudes reveal five distinct peaks within the 0–3 Å range, assigned to W–O1 (1.25 Å), W–O2 (1.66 Å), W–O3 (2.00 Å), W–O4 (2.45 Å), and W–Ru (2.88 Å), as shown in Figure 3d. Notably, the bond lengths of W–O1, W–O2, and W–O3 are all within 2.00 Å, indicating that the W atoms are directly bonded to three O atoms on the RuO2 support. The W–O4 peak at 2.45 Å suggests the presence of one O atom coordinated exclusively to the W atom. Furthermore, the W–Ru bond length (2.88 Å) in W–O–RuO2 is significantly longer than the W–W coordination in W foil (2.76 Å) and the W–O–W coordination in WO3 (2.58 Å). This distinct local environment strongly indicates the atomic dispersion of W species and unambiguously excludes the formation of aggregated WOx clusters or incorporation into the bulk lattice. Instead, it demonstrates that W atoms are atomically anchored to the RuO2 surface through W–O–Ru interfacial bonding. Based on these observations, we conclude that the synthesized W–O–RuO2 structure is highly consistent with the W1O3‐like coordination environment predicted computationally, which was previously identified as exhibiting optimal capability to suppress the formation of oxygen and Ru vacancies, as shown in Figures S1i and S11. The fitting curves for W elements with a coordination environment of W–O–RuO2 in both R–space (real and imaginary parts) and k–space closely match the experimental data for coordination numbers and bond lengths (Figure S12 and Table S3).

We also examined the electronic structure of Ru in the W–O–RuO2. As shown in Figure 3e and Figure S13a, the WL intensity and the first derivative of the Ru K edge XANES spectrum for W–O–RuO2 lie between those of RuO2 and Ru foil, indicating that the oxidation state of Ru ranges between 0 and +4. Moreover, linear fitting results reveal that the oxidation state of Ru in the W–O–RuO2 sample is +3.4 ± 0.06 (Figure S13b). The Ru EXAFS results reveal that in W–O–RuO2, the Ru–O bond lengths remain comparable to those in pristine RuO2 after the formation of W–O–Ru interfacial units on its surface. However, the formation of strongly coupled W–O–Ru interfacial units on the RuO2 lattice surface leads to a noticeable modulation of the Ru–Ru (metal–metal) coordination environment. As shown in Figure 3f, W–O–RuO2 and RuO2 exhibit a Ru–O1 peak at 1.46 Å, suggesting that the main lattice structure of RuO2 remains. However, W–O–RuO2 shows a significantly longer Ru–O2 bond at 2.0 Å, suggesting the presence of surface tensile strain associated with the formation of W–O–Ru interfacial units. Additionally, in the second shell region (2.1–3.3 Å), W–O–RuO2 features shorter Ru–O3 bonds and Ru–M (M = W, Ru) bonds compared to RuO2, further supporting the presence of strong electronic interactions between W and Ru atoms. To confirm this, we performed fitting using the same theoretically optimized model. As evidenced by the EXAFS data in Figure S14 and Table S4, the R‐space and k–space demonstrate the Ru–W with bond length consistent at 2.8 Å, irrespective of the tested atom (W or Ru). Moreover, EXAFS in Figure 3g–i show the W L3 edge in W–O–RuO2 with a maximum intensity at 8.0 Å− 1, which is distinctly different from the peaks in WO3 (6.7 Å− 1) and W foil (8.7 Å− 1). At the Ru K edge, W–O–RuO2 and RuO2 exhibit a similar Ru–O main peak position (5.5 Å− 1), which is notably distinct from Ru foil (10.1 Å− 1), as shown in Figure S15. The XAS results from both W and Ru edges confirm the successful synthesis of the W–O–RuO2 catalyst via controlled ALD, leading to the formation of atomically dispersed W–O–Ru interfacial units whose local coordination environment is consistent with the W1O3 motif. This interfacial configuration enhances the vacancy suppression capability without altering the bulk crystal lattice. The resulting tensile surface strain, associated with W–O–Ru interfacial bonding, is consistent with our AC–TEM measurements and supports the formation of a strongly coupled interface.

Electrocatalytic Performance

The acidic OER performance of W–O–RuO2 and contrast catalysts was evaluated using a rotating disk electrode in an argon‐saturated 0.5 M H2SO4 solution at room temperature. As shown in Figure 4a and Table S5, the linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) curves demonstrate that W–O–RuO2 exhibits superior OER performance compared to RuO2. Specifically, W–O–RuO2 achieves a current density of 10 mA cm− 2 at an overpotential of 168 mV (ɳ 10 = 168 mV), while RuO2 requires an overpotential of 240 mV (ɳ 10 = 240 mV) to reach the same current density. To further validate that ALD of W effectively enhances performance while maintaining the structural integrity of the RuO2 lattice, we also deposited an atomic W layer on commercial RuO2 (Com‐RuO2). The resulting W–O–Com–RuO2 catalyst (ɳ 10 = 200 mV) showed markedly improved performance over Com–RuO2 (ɳ 10 = 268 mV). Additional kinetic information was extracted from the Tafel plots. As shown in Figure 4b and Table S5, W–O–RuO2 exhibited the Tafel slope of 53.9 mV dec− 1, significantly lower than those of RuO2 (65.2 mV dec− 1), W–O–Com–RuO2 (65.5 mV dec− 1), and Com–RuO2 (77.5 mV dec− 1). Moreover, electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements at 1.4 V reveal that the W–O–RuO2 catalyst exhibits the charge transfer resistance of 2.8 Ω, significantly lower than that of RuO2 (6.5 Ω), W–O–Com–RuO2 (10.7 Ω), and Com–RuO2 (14.7 Ω), as shown in Figure 4c, Figure S16, and Table S5. The drastic decrease in resistance for W–O–RuO2 can be explained by a combination of factors. First, the atomically precise ALD coating creates an intimate and continuous interfacial contact, minimizing contact resistance. Second, the strong W–O–Ru bonding induces tensile surface stress and effectively lowers the charge transfer barrier at the interface. Finally, this electronic‐structure engineering enhances carrier mobility, collectively resulting in superior electrical conductivity. To evaluate the durability of W–O–RuO2, accelerated degradation tests (ADT) were conducted by performing 5000 cyclic voltammetry (CV) cycles at a scan rate of 100 mV s− 1 between 1.4 and 1.8 V (vs. RHE). As shown in Figure 4d, W–O–RuO2 demonstrated exceptional stability with no performance degradation after 5000 cycles, outperforming the other catalysts. Similarly, W–O–Com–RuO2 exhibited much better cycle stability than Com‐RuO2 (Figure S17). To further evaluate operational stability, we performed a long‐term chronopotentiometry (CP) test at a constant current density of 50 mA cm− 2. As shown in Figure 4e, the W–O–RuO2 electrode exhibited exceptional durability, with a minimal potential increase of only 4 mV after 100 h of continuous operation (decay rate = 40 µV/h). This performance stands in sharp contrast to that of the synthesized RuO2 reference, which began to degrade rapidly after only 20 h. Furthermore, the activity and stability of W–O–RuO2 surpass that of other recently reported state–of–the–art noble‐based acidic OER catalysts (Figure 4f and Table S6). Consistent with this trend, W–O–Com–RuO2 also demonstrated markedly improved stability compared to Com–RuO2 (Figure S18), underscoring the general efficacy of the W–O surface modification strategy.

To demonstrate the practical potential of W–O–RuO2 catalyst for water electrolysis, we further constructed a PEMWE system using W–O–RuO2 as the anode catalyst and Pt/C as the cathode (Figure S19a). The steady‐state polarization curves of a PEMWE cell operating in pure water at 80°C with an ultra‐low loading of W–O–RuO2 (100 µgRu cm− 2) are shown in Figure S19b, benchmarked against other RuO2‐based devices. Notably, the PEMWE with W–O–RuO2 anode required only 1.68 V to achieve industrial current densities of 1000 mA cm− 2, which is substantially lower than the voltages needed for W–O–Com–RuO2 (1.80 V), RuO2 (1.84 V), and Com‐RuO2 (2.33 V). To further validate the long‐term operational stability of W–O–RuO2 under practical PEMWE conditions, extended constant‐current electrolysis was conducted at a high current density of 1000 mA cm− 2 using a W–O–RuO2–based membrane electrode assembly (MEA) at 80°C. As shown in Figure 4g and Table S7, 63.3 mV voltage degradation was observed over more than 1000 h of operation (degradation rate = 63.3 µV/h), demonstrating outstanding stability that surpasses recently reported PEMWE systems employing Ru‐based electrocatalysts. In contrast, the PEMWE equipped with RuO2 showed rapid degradation, with the cell voltage increasing from 1.85 to 2.66 V within only 29 h, as shown in Figure S19c. These results demonstrate that ALD technology, which enables the efficient and rapid coating of atomic‐layer W onto RuO2, can lead to substantial improvements in the activity and stability of RuO2, showcasing strong potential for commercialization.

Discussion

Reaction Pathway Analysis

To further elucidate the distinct reaction pathways enabled by W–O modification of W–O–RuO2 during the OER process, in situ Raman spectroscopy was conducted over a potential range of 1.1–1.6 V versus RHE. As shown in Figure 5a and b, both W–O–RuO2 and RuO2 exhibit three characteristic Raman modes of rutile RuO2, assigned as the Eg (515–540 cm−1), A1g (627–630 cm−1), and B2g (700–720 cm−1) modes [49, 50]. Notably, no distinct W–O vibrational peaks (750–1000 cm−1) are observed in the Raman spectra (Figure S20), confirming the highly dispersed and amorphous nature of the ALD–deposited W species without forming crystalline WOx aggregates. A detailed analysis of the in situ spectra reveal pronounced differences in the evolution of the Eg mode with increasing potential (Figure S21), offering critical insight into lattice–oxygen behavior. For W–O–RuO2, the E g bending vibration associated with in–plane O–Ru–O motion shows minimal intensity loss and negligible peak shifting as the potential increases. Such stability indicates that the strongly coupled W–O–Ru interfacial units effectively suppress in–plane lattice distortion and inhibit activation of lattice oxygen. The A 1g symmetric stretching mode of axial Ru–O bonds remains comparably stable, while the B 2g asymmetric bending mode exhibits only slight changes, further confirming that W incorporation mitigates Ru–O bond and blocks vacancy formation under OER conditions [7, 51]. In contrast, RuO2 displays substantial potential‐induced suppression of the Eg mode, pronounced red shifts of the A 1g peak, and broadening of the B 2g vibration. Collectively, these vibrational fingerprints demonstrate that W–O–RuO2 stabilizes the RuO6 octahedral framework, suppresses LOM‐associated degradation, and thereby underpins its markedly enhanced stability during acidic OER.

To further verify that the W1O3 layer in W–O–RuO2 promotes AEM, we performed in situ DEMS with 1 8O isotopic tracing. Using isotopic labeling with 18O, we tracked the oxygen evolution during the OER process, where the appearance of labeled oxygen molecules directly reflects the involvement of the LOM pathway (with 18O originating from the catalyst lattice and 16O from H2 16O in the electrolyte) [30, 52]. As shown in Figure 5c, the oxygen signals are dominated by 32O2, with no detectable signal of 36O2, and only a minor presence of 34O2 for W–O–RuO2. Quantitative analysis by peak area integration reveals that only 0.65% of the evolved O2 contains labeled 18O, in stark contrast to conventional RuO2 catalysts, which typically exhibit 18O fractions in the range of 1.11% under similar conditions, as shown in Figure 5d and Figure S22. These DEMS results indicate that W–O–Ru interfacial units on RuO2 effectively suppresses lattice oxygen participation in the OER.

In situ ATR–SEIRAS was employed to identify oxygenated intermediates formed on the catalyst surface during the reaction [53]. To rigorously exclude the possibility of artifactual features or static electrolyte interference, all spectra were carefully background‐subtracted, and the dynamic evolution of the peaks was strictly evaluated against the applied potential, adhering to stringent spectroscopic standards for interfacial species [54]. As shown in Figure 5e and Figure S23, with increasing potential, several distinct absorption bands centered at 1033, 1200, and 3300 cm−1 progressively emerged exclusively within the Faradaic OER potential window. Consistent with recent well‐established in situ spectroscopic consensus on Ru–based systems [30, 55, 56]. The prominent peaks at 1033 and 1200 cm− 1 are assigned to the O–O stretching vibrations of the *OOH intermediate, a characteristic species associated with the AEM pathway. A weaker feature around 1122 cm− 1 is attributed to the *OO intermediate, commonly regarded as a signature of the LOM pathway [57, 58]. Interestingly, this *OO signal is only weakly observed between 1.1 and 1.4 V, but disappears at higher potentials, suggesting that progressive coordination reconstruction of the surface W layer suppresses LOM activity during the OER process. The band at ∼3300 cm− 1, corresponding to the *OH intermediate, further supports the dominance of the AEM pathway on W–O–RuO2, as shown in Figure S23. Crucially, this spectroscopic evolution perfectly corroborates the in situ DEMS isotopic analysis. A similar spectral evolution is observed on pristine RuO2 (Figure 5f), though the *OO signal at 1122 cm− 1 remains prominent throughout the OER region, while signals corresponding to *OOH and *OH intermediates are relatively weak. This observation indicates that the reaction on RuO2 predominantly follows the LOM pathway. Together, these results confirm that the atomic‐layer W overlayer in W‐O‐RuO2 effectively suppresses lattice oxygen participation and shifts the reaction mechanism toward AEM, consistent with the in situ DEMS results.

Mechanism of Enhanced Intrinsic Activity

To elucidate the origins of the high catalytic activity of W–O–RuO2, we conducted Operando XAS characterization of the W L3 edge and Ru K edge of W–O–RuO2 during acidic OER. These measurements were taken under open–circuit conditions and at applied potentials of 1.1 V, 1.4 V, and 1.7 V (vs. RHE). At the W L3 edge, the XANES spectra exhibit subtle but systematic potential‐dependent changes near the absorption edge (Figure 6a, inset), indicating moderate electronic modulation and oxidation‐state variation of W sites under operating conditions. The W L3 edge EXAFS spectra remain largely unchanged from open–circuit to 1.7 V (vs. RHE). However, a systematic shift of the W–M scattering peak from ∼2.9 Å (1.1 V) to ∼2.8 Å (1.7 V) is observed. Crucially, this potential–dependent bond contraction serves as direct operando evidence for the dynamic transition of the local W–O–Ru interface into a compressively strained state under realistic OER conditions. This dynamic structural evolution reflects a strong electronic interaction at the interface, consistent with the robust W–Ru orbital hybridization revealed by PDOS calculations (Figure 6b). This collective evidence confirms the formation of a robustly coupled W1O3–RuO2 interface. Furthermore, this potential‐induced shortening of the W–Ru bond optimizes the local electronic environment of the W sites, thereby fine‐tuning their adsorption affinity for key oxygenated intermediates and directly contributing to the enhanced OER kinetics.

(e) Free energy diagrams illustrating the OER pathway for the W site of W–O–RuO2, the Ru site of W–O–RuO2, and pristine RuO2, with additional simulations under an electrode potential of 1.23 V. (f) RDS barriers of M1O3 deposit on RuO2. (g) Schematic diagram of the W–Ru dual‐site synergistic mechanism.

In contrast, the Ru K–edge XANES spectra (Figure 6c, inset) show negligible edge displacement with increasing potential, and the Ru K–edge EXAFS spectra (Figure 6d) display no significant changes in the intensity or position of the dominant Ru–O and Ru–Ru coordination shells. The invariance of these Ru signatures indicates that the bulk RuO2 framework and the average Ru oxidation state remain largely unaffected during OER. This comparison establishes that while the W1O3 overlayer undergoes slight electronic tuning under bias, the underlying RuO2 lattice remains structurally intact, confirming that W modification primarily affects the surface electronic environment without perturbing the core crystalline structure.

To unravel the origin of the enhanced OER activity, we performed DFT calculations to determine the free energy pathways for the reaction at the distinct W and Ru sites in W–O–RuO2 via the AEM. As shown in Figure 6e and Table S8, the reaction profiles reveal a sophisticated dual‐site synergistic mechanism. In the initial stages of H2O activation and its conversion to *OH, the reaction barriers at W and Ru sites are comparable, with W sites serving as initial active centers. The critical differentiation occurs at the rate‐determining step (RDS) of *O to *OOH, where the Ru sites exhibit a significantly lower reaction barrier (1.88 eV) compared to both the W sites (2.16 eV) and pristine RuO2 (2.12 eV). In the final desorption step (*OOH to O2), the W sites again contribute by offering a lower barrier, thereby facilitating the completion of the catalytic cycle. This complementary action, where W sites assist in the initial and final steps while Ru sites dominate the potential‐determining step, is confirmed by simulations at an applied potential of 1.23 V. To rigorously validate the necessity of the W–Ru dual–site synergy, we conducted additional control calculations on a fully W–covered RuO2 model (Tungsten Atomic Layer on RuO2, denoted as WAL–RuO2). In this configuration, the adjacent Ru sites are completely blocked, eliminating the exposed W–Ru interfaces. As shown in Figure S24, the RDS energy barrier for WAL–RuO2 skyrockets to 3.43 eV, rendering it highly unfavorable for OER. This is in stark contrast to the significantly reduced RDS barrier of 1.88 eV achieved by the optimally dispersed sub‐monolayer W‐O‐RuO2 catalyst.

Furthermore, to contextualize the exceptional performance of the W–O–RuO2 system, we computed the RDS barriers for a series of M1O3 on the RuO2 surface. Figure 6f indicates that the W1O3 deposition on RuO2 leads to the most significant reduction in the RDS barrier among the studied systems, underscoring the unique efficacy of the W–O–Ru interface. The superior performance is due to W site as an integral enabler that optimizes the electronic structure and reaction environment of the adjacent Ru sites. This synergistic interplay is multi–faceted: W sites function as an electron buffer via the W–O–Ru interfacial bonds, mitigating excessive charge accumulation on Ru during intermediate adsorption and replenishing electron density during the critical *OOH formation. Concurrently, the dynamically contracting W–Ru bond, as observed by operando XAS, imposes a rigid framework that suppresses detrimental lattice distortion at the Ru sites, leading to smoother energy landscapes. Furthermore, W sites provide auxiliary adsorption, likely stabilizing key transition states through weak interactions. Therefore, we conclude that the OER on W–O–RuO2 proceeds via a cooperative W–Ru dual‐site mechanism. This mechanism, graphically summarized in Figure 6g, represents a paradigm where a site with modest intrinsic activity (W) can dramatically enhance the performance of a neighboring active site (Ru) through electronic and structural synergy.

Stability Analysis

To elucidate the origin of the markedly enhanced durability of W–O–RuO2 during acidic OER, we systematically correlated electrolyte acidity evolution, metal dissolution behavior, atomic–scale structural stability, and vacancy–formation energetics. As shown in Figure 7a, Operando pH measurements reveal that pristine RuO2 undergoes a pronounced decrease in electrolyte pH as the potential increases, indicating substantial Ru dissolution and progressive lattice degradation. In contrast, W–O–RuO2 maintains a significantly more stable pH profile throughout the potential range, suggesting suppressed surface corrosion. These observations are confirmed by ex situ inductively coupled plasma–optical emission spectrometer (ICP–OES) analysis measurements (Figure 7b), where RuO2 undergoes severe Ru leaching (26.12 µg L− 1), while W–O–RuO2 exhibits remarkably reduced dissolution (2.24 µg L− 1 for Ru and 0.74 µg L− 1 for W). The dissolution level is comparable to that of commercial RuO2 yet achieved without sacrificing intrinsic activity, highlighting the stabilizing role of the atomic W1O3 overlayer.

(b) Ex situ ICP analysis of W–O–RuO2, W–O–Com–RuO2, RuO2, and Com–RuO2 catalysts after long time stability test. (c, d) EXAFS spectra and XANES spectra of the Ru K edge, respectively. (e, f) XANES spectra and EXAFS spectra of the W L 3 edge, respectively. (g) HAADF–STEM image of W–O–RuO2–ADT. (h) Strain analysis profile of W–O–RuO2–ADT (∼1% compressive strain). The color scale represents relative strain, spanning from –1% (compressive) to 1% (tensile). (i) DFT–calculated vacancy –formation energies for RuCUS, OBRI, and OCUS sites before and after ADT.

The structural origin of this stability is revealed by comparing the XAS spectra of W–O–RuO2 before and after ADT (W–O–RuO2–ADT). The Ru K edge EXAFS (Figure 7c) retains nearly identical Ru–O and Ru–Ru scattering features following ADT, demonstrating that the bulk RuO2 framework remains intact and experiences minimal coordination collapse. Correspondingly, the Ru K edge XANES spectra (Figure 7d) exhibit negligible shifts in edge position or spectral shape, indicating that the oxidation state and average coordination environment of Ru are preserved during prolonged operation. These results imply that the W1O3 overlayer protects Ru sites against dissolution and prevents lattice oxygen redox‐induced structural decay. The W L3 edge XAS spectra reveal that the atomic W1O3 motif remains structurally well–preserved after long‐term ADT. The XANES profiles (Figure 7e) of W–O–RuO2 and W–O–RuO2‐ADT display nearly identical edge shapes and white‐line features, indicating that the local oxidation state and electronic configuration of W remain essentially unchanged during prolonged OER operation. This spectral invariance confirms that the W1O3 layer does not undergo oxidation, reduction, or dissolution, consistent with its role as a robust interfacial stabilizer. In the corresponding Fourier–transformed EXAFS spectra (Figure 7f), all major coordination peaks are retained after ADT, further demonstrating that the first‐shell W–O coordination and overall W1O3 geometry remain intact. The only noticeable change occurs at the W–Ru scattering peak, which shifts slightly from ∼2.90 to ∼2.85 Å after ADT. This subtle contraction mirrors the trend observed in the operando XAS measurements and reflects a dynamic tightening of the interfacial W–Ru linkage under electrochemical stress.

High‐resolution AC–TEM imaging further corroborates the exceptional structural integrity of W–O–RuO2 after ADT (W–O–RuO2–ADT), as shown in Figure 7g and Figure S25). Clear lattice fringes of rutile RuO2 are maintained, and no evidence of surface amorphization or WOx phase formation is observed. Moreover, geometric phase analysis (Figure 7h) reveals a transition from the initial tensile surface strain (∼2%) to a mild compressive strain (∼1%) after stability testing. While evaluating such nanoscale strain variations requires rigorous verification, this ∼3% relative structural contraction visually mapped by GPA is remarkably consistent with the quantitative operando EXAFS results. Specifically, EXAFS revealed a dynamic W–Ru bond contraction from ∼2.9 to ∼2.8 Å at high potentials, corresponding to a localized structural compression of ∼3.4%. Together, these operando and ex situ results confirm a self‐adjusting interfacial reconstruction. The catalyst dynamically accommodates interfacial compressive strain to optimize OER activity, while the robust W–O–Ru coupling preserves catalytic integrity and suppresses LOM, thereby successfully decoupling the traditional activity–stability trade–off. The vacancy formation energies derived from DFT calculations (Figure 7i) align with the experimental dissolution trends. Following the stress transition, the compressively strained W–O–RuO2–ADT exhibits elevated formation energies for RuCUS vacancies, OBRI, and OCUS vacancies relative to W–O–RuO2 (approximately 0.01 eV higher). Additionally, the reduced adsorption energies of intermediates at the active sites result in a significantly lower RDS energy barrier (1.81 eV) for W–O–RuO2–ADT (Figure S26 and Table S8). This finding indicates that the W1O3 layer enables dynamic modulation of surface stress during long–term stability testing, concurrently suppressing vacancy formation and optimizing the reaction process.

Collectively, these Operando and post–ADT analyses demonstrate that the atomically engineered W1O3 overlayer establishes a strongly coupled W–O–Ru interface that (i) suppresses lattice–oxygen activation, (ii) prevents Ru dissolution, (iii) maintains the structural integrity of the RuO2 framework, and (iv) dynamically accommodates interfacial strain during operation. This synergistic stabilization mechanism accounts for the remarkable long‐term durability of W‐O‐RuO2 under industrially relevant acidic OER conditions.

Conclusions

In this work, we establish a general strategy to stabilize RuO2, the most active yet intrinsically unstable acidic OER catalyst, by constructing atomically coupled W–O–Ru surface units. DFT screening reveals that W–O uniquely and substantially increases the formation energies of Ru and O vacancies on RuO2 through strong hybridization between W 5d and Ru 4d orbitals, contributing to the enhanced stability. Guided by theory, a controlled ALD process enables the introduction of W species to form atomically dispersed W–O–Ru interfacial units with a W1O3–like coordination environment, generating a tensile‐strained (∼2%) and strongly coupled interface. In situ Raman and DEMS show markedly suppressed lattice–oxygen involvement, and in situ ATR–SEIRAS indicates an AEM–dominated mechanism characterized by stabilized *OH and *OOH intermediates. Operando XAS confirms a cooperative W–Ru dual‐site mechanism, in which the W sites modulate and stabilize the neighboring Ru centers through synergistic electronic and structural coupling. Furthermore, the strongly coupled W–O–Ru interface can suppress lattice–oxygen activation, maintain the structural integrity of the RuO2 framework, and dynamically accommodate interfacial strain during operation. Benefiting from this stabilized electronic landscape and mechanism regulation, W–O–RuO2 catalyst achieves exceptional activity (ɳ 10 = 168 mV) and industrial–scale durability (only 63.3 µV/h degradation rate for 1000 h) in a proton–exchange membrane water electrolyzer. Overall, this work provides a generalizable blueprint for designing ultra‐stable acidic OER catalysts through targeted surface electronic modulation and offers a practical path toward durable Ru‐based anodes for large‐scale green hydrogen production.
