Asymptotics of ultra-high-dimensional generalized spiked sample covariance matrix
Wonjun Seo

Abstract




Abstract

This paper investigates the asymptotics of eigenstructure of sample covariance matrix under the spiked covariance matrix model in ultra-high-dimensional settings, where the dimensionality can grow much faster than the sample size with
p\asymp n^{\alpha}
,
\alpha>1
. We establish the first-order convergence limits of eigenvalue locations and eigenvector projections of properly scaled sample covariance matrix. Our results are extensions of
[
14
,
24
]
.

1
Introduction

The sample covariance matrix is of fundamental importance in multivariate statistics. In the classical regime, where the dimensionality
p
is fixed while the sample size
n
tends to infinity, it is well-known that the sample covariance matrix is a consistent estimator of the population covariance matrix. Consequently, the plug-in principle, replacing population covariance matrix by the sample covariance matrix, yields asymptotically optimal procedures for a wide range of methodologies, including principal component analysis, discriminant analysis, and Hotelling’s
T^{2}
test
[
2
,
37
]
.

However, this breaks down when
p
is no longer negligible compared to
n
, as the sample covariance matrix loses its consistency
[
43
]
. In particular, the eigenstrucure of the sample covariance matrix can deviate significantly from its population courterpart, leading to substantial distortions in statistical procedures that rely on the plug-in principle. The situation becomes even more severe when
p
is much larger than
n
, which is common in modern high dimensional settings, as procedures that rely on the precision matrix may fail completely.

To address this issue, a specific structural assumption is often imposed on the population covariance matrix
\Sigma
. One of the most widely studied frameworks in this context is the spiked covariance matrix model, first introduced by Johnstone
[
30
]
. Johnstone’s spiked covariance matrix model assumes that
p
and
n
are comparable and that the population covariance matrix is a finite-rank perturbation of the identity matrix as follows

\widetilde{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{Johnstone}}=I_{p}+\sum_{i=1}^{r}\mathsf{d}_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}^{\top},

(1.1)

where
r\in\mathbb{N}
is a fixed number,
\mathsf{d}_{i}>0
are some positive fixed numbers, and
\mathbf{v}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
are orthonormal vectors. The key idea of this model is that a few
spikes
in the spectrum represent dominant directions or factors, while the remaining eigenvalues are set to be 1. Over the past few years, many efforts have been made to analyze the asymptotic behavior of the eigenstructure of the sample covariance matrix under this model. Building on these results, a variety of statistical procedures have been developed. We refer to
[
38
,
29
,
3
,
43
,
17
]
and references therein for a comprehensive review.

Despite its success, Johnstone’s spiked covariance matrix model has certain limitations. In particular, the assumption that the bulk eigenvalues are all equal to 1 can be overly restrictive in practice, where the underlying covariance structure may be more complex. Moreover, most existing results are established in the regime where
p
is comparable to
n
, and do not directly extend to the regime where
p
is much larger than
n
.

In this paper, we study the asymptotic eigenstructure of sample covariance matrix under a more generalized spiked covariance matrix model and the regime where
p
is much larger than
n
. More specifically, we consider

\widetilde{Q}=\widetilde{\Sigma}^{1/2}XX^{\top}\widetilde{\Sigma}^{1/2},

(1.2)

where
X
is a
p\times n
random matrix whose entries are centered and properly scaled i.i.d real random variables, and
\widetilde{\Sigma}
is a
p\times p
positive definite matrix. Unlike the Johnstone’s spiked covariance matrix model in (
1.1
),
\widetilde{\Sigma}
is assumed to be a finite-rank perturbation of a general positive definite matrix
\Sigma_{0}
with eigenvalues
\{\sigma_{i}\}
and eigenvectors
\{\mathbf{v}_{i}\}
, given by

\widetilde{\Sigma}=\Sigma_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{r}\mathsf{d}_{i}\sigma_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}^{\top},

(1.3)

under mild conditions. For the dimensionality, we assume that

p\asymp n^{\alpha},\ \text{ for some constant }\alpha\in(1,\infty),

(1.4)

so that
p
is allowed to grow much faster than
n
. To distinguish this regime from the comparable regime where
\alpha=1
, we refer to it as the ultra-high-dimensional regime.

1.1
Related works

In this subsection, we briefly review existing related results on the spiked covariance matrix model. For the notational convenience, we denote

\phi\equiv\phi_{n}:=\frac{p}{n}.

(1.5)

The first major finding was the identification of the
outliers
in the spectrum of
\widetilde{Q}
, established by Baik, Ben Arous, and Peché
[
6
]
. They showed that if
\mathsf{d}_{i}>\phi^{1/2}
, the
i
th largest eigenvalue of
\widetilde{Q}
separates from the majority of the other eigenvalues, under the Johnstone’s spiked covariance matrix model in (
1.1
), for
\alpha=1
in (
1.4
), assuming complex Gaussian entries for
X
. This phenomenon is now widely known as the BBP transition. The behavior of outlier eigenvectors was established by Paul
[
39
]
. More precisely, the
i
th eigenvector of
\widetilde{Q}
is concentrated on a cone with the axis parallel to
\mathbf{v}_{i}
if
\mathsf{d}_{i}>\phi^{1/2}
, under the same model and regime, assuming real Gaussian entries. Since these foundational works, outlier eigenvalues and eigenvectors of sample covariance matrices have been extensively investigated under weaker assumptions and in various settings
[
7
,
4
,
12
,
5
,
15
,
14
,
42
,
24
,
8
]
.

The behavior of non-outlier eigenvalues and eigenvectors can be analyzed using the local laws. In
[
14
]
, the authors established the eigenvalue sticking and delocalization, which characterize the asymptotic behavior of non-outlier eigenvalues and eigenvectors, respectively. They also derived the large deviation bounds of the locations of outlier eigenvalues and the generalized components of outlier eigenvectors. These results are based on the isotropic local laws developed in
[
1
]
, and are proved under Johnstone’s model and for
\alpha\in(0,\infty)
. In a similar spirit,
[
24
]
derived the analogous results under the generalized spiked covariance matrix model in (
1.3
) for
\alpha=1
, using the anisotropic local laws established by Knowles and Yin
[
33
]
.

Our paper can be viewed as a synthesis of these two works, as we consider the generalized spiked covariance matrix model with
\alpha\in(1,\infty)
. While the outlier eigenvalues in this setting have recently been studied in
[
28
]
, our work also incorporates the behavior of non-outlier eigenvalues and both outlier and non-outlier eigenvector projections.

Finally, we mention that the BBP-type transitions have also been established for other classes of spiked random matrices arising in statistics, including signal-plus-noise matrices
[
11
,
13
,
23
,
9
,
27
]
, spiked separable sample covariance matrices
[
22
]
, spiked Fisher matrices
[
41
]
, and spiked correlation matrices
[
36
]
. Another important class in random matrix theory is the Wigner matrices, and the analogous results for the deformed Wigner matrices have been studied in
[
12
,
31
,
40
,
32
,
34
]
.

1.2
An overview of main results

In this subsection, a brief overview of main results is provided.

The first set of results concerns the asymptotic locations of non-trivial eigenvalues
\{\widetilde{\lambda}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
of
\widetilde{Q}
. Under the assumption that the spike magnitudes
{\mathsf{d}_{i}}_{i=1}^{r}
exceed certain thresholds given in (
2.9
), the first
r
largest eigenvalues
\{\widetilde{\lambda}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{r}
emerge as outliers. They detach from the rest eigenvalues and concentrate around deterministic locations determined by
\{\mathsf{d}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{r}
. On the other hand, the non-outlier eigenvalues
\{\widetilde{\lambda}_{i}\}_{i=r+1}^{n}
concentrate around the corresponding quantiles of the density
\varrho
, which is the limiting spectral distribution of the sample covariance matrix under the non-spiked model; see Section
2.2
for details.

Next, the results on the eigenvectors
\{\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
of
\widetilde{Q}
are summarized. To avoid the ambiguity of the phase of
\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}_{i}
and to quantify the alignment with the population eigenvectors
\{\mathbf{v}_{j}\}
, the squared spectral projections
\langle\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}_{i},\mathbf{v}_{j}\rangle^{2}
are considered. The first part of Theorem
3.5
shows that each outlier eigenvector
\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}_{i}
,
1\leq i\leq r
, concentrates on a cone with the axis parallel to corresponding population eigenvector
\mathbf{v}_{i}
, with a deterministic aperture depending on
\mathsf{d}_{i}
. On the other hand, the non-outlier eigenvectors are delocalized in any direction of
\mathbf{v}_{j}
.

Although Theorem
3.5
deals with individual eigenvector projections, weighted sums of projections are often of interest in statistical applications, such as principal component analysis (cf. Example
3.9
) and optimal shrinkage estimation
[
25
,
20
]
. In such applications, one is interested in the deterministic equivalent of

\sum_{j=1}^{p}l_{j}\langle\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}_{i},\mathbf{v}_{j}\rangle^{2},

where
\{l_{j}\}
is some positive deterministic sequence. For
1\leq i\leq r
, Theorem 3.4 (
3.6
) alone is insufficient for this purpose, as it only yields the bound
|\langle\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}_{i},\mathbf{v}_{j}\rangle|^{2}\leq p^{-1+\epsilon}
with high probability for
r+1\leq j\leq p
. To address this issue, a perturbation argument introduced in
[
20
]
is employed, leading to the first-order limit of the weighted projections in Theorem
3.7
.

Before proceeding, we discuss several possible future works. First, we assume the spikes are above the critical points by constant-order gap for simplicity. This means that we only consider the somewhat trivial supercritical regime. We believe that the phase transition occurs on the scale
n^{-1/3}
, as in other models. Second, the second-order limits (distributional limits) are also important to develop statistical inference procedures. We refer to
[
14
,
8
]
for those who are interested in joint distribution of outlier eigenvalues and eigenvectors under simpler settings. Lastly, the deterministic equivalent of
\sum_{j=1}^{p}l_{j}|\langle\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}_{i},\mathbf{v}_{j}\rangle|^{2}
for
r+1\leq i\leq p
is also important in optimal shrinkage estimation. However, we do not pursue this direction, as it requires establishing the quantum unique ergodicity (QUE) estimate, which involves more sophisticated techniques. The QUE estimates for sample covariance matrix under the comparable regime
\alpha=1
have been recently established in
[
20
]
. We believe similar argument with a slight modification will also work in the ultra-high-dimensional regime. We will pursue these generalizations in our future works.

Organzation

This paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, the non-spiked and spiked covariance matrix model, along with key assumptions, are introduced, and some preliminary results on the asymptotic laws of sample covariance matrices under the non-spiked covariance matrix model are summarized. Section
3
presents the main results of the paper, where the eigenvalue locations and eigenvector projections under the spiked covariance matrix model are derived. In Appendix
A
, we introduce the local laws, which serve as key inputs to the proofs of the main results. The proofs of the results are deferred to Appendix
B
.

Notation

In this paper, we follow the notational conventions commonly used in the random matrix theory and high-dimensional statistics literature. The sample size
n
is the fundamental parameter, and we often omit the explicit dependence on
n
. For instance, although (
1.4
) indicates that the dimensionality
p
depends on
n
, we write
p\equiv p_{n}
as
p
. A constant that does not depend on
n
is referred to as a
fixed
constant. Let
a_{n}
and
b_{n}
be positive real sequences. We write
a_{n}\asymp b_{n}
if there exists a positive fixed constant
C>0
such that
C^{-1}a_{n}\leq b_{n}\leq Ca_{n}
and
a_{n}\gtrsim b_{n}
if
a_{n}\geq Cb_{n}
for sufficiently large
n
. In addition, we write
a_{n}\gg b_{n}
if
a_{n}/b_{n}\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
. The Euclidean norm of a vector
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is denoted by
\|\mathbf{v}\|
. The operator norm of a matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
is denoted by
\|A\|
. The complex number will be written as
z=E+\mathrm{i}\eta
, where
E
is the real part and
\eta
is the imaginary part. We let
\mathbb{C}_{+}=\left\{z=E+\mathrm{i}\eta:\eta>0\right\}
. For any
i,j\in\mathbb{N}
with
i<j
, we denote
\llbracket i\rrbracket=\{1,2,\dots,i\}
and
\llbracket i,j\rrbracket=\{i,i+1,\dots,j\}
.

2
Model

In this section, we introduce our model, assumptions, and several notations required to state the main results.

2.1
Spiked covariance matrix model and assumptions

Throughout the paper, we consider the spiked covariance matrix model with general structures, as considered in
[
24
,
20
]
.
Let
\Sigma_{0}
be a deterministic
p\times p
positive definite matrix admitting the spectral decomposition

\Sigma_{0}=V\Lambda_{0}V^{\top}=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\sigma_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}^{\top},\quad 0<\sigma_{p}\leq\dots\leq\sigma_{1},

(2.1)

where
\{\sigma_{i}\}
are the eigenvalues and
\{\mathbf{v}_{i}\}
are the associated eigenvectors of
\Sigma_{0}
. For some fixed integer
r\in\mathbb{N}
, we introduce
r

spikes
to
\Sigma_{0}
, resulting in another
p\times p
positive definite matrix
\widetilde{\Sigma}
with spectral decomposition

\widetilde{\Sigma}=V\widetilde{\Lambda}V^{\top}=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\widetilde{\sigma}_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}^{\top},\quad 0<\widetilde{\sigma}_{p}\leq\dots\leq\widetilde{\sigma}_{1},

(2.2)

where the spiked eigenvalues
\{\widetilde{\sigma}_{i}\}
are defined as

\widetilde{\sigma}_{i}=\begin{cases}(1+\mathsf{d}_{i})\sigma_{i},&i\in\llbracket r\rrbracket\\
\sigma_{i},&i\in\llbracket r+1,p\rrbracket\end{cases},

(2.3)

where
0<\mathsf{d}_{r}\leq\dots\leq\mathsf{d}_{1}
are parameters used to characterize the spikes.
Note that when
\mathsf{d}_{i}\equiv 0
for all
1\leq i\leq r
or
r=0
,
\widetilde{\Sigma}
contains no spikes and coincides with
\Sigma_{0}
. In what follows, we refer to
\Sigma=\Sigma_{0}
as the
non-spiked covariance matrix model
and
\Sigma=\widetilde{\Sigma}
as the
spiked covariance matrix model
.

Under each model, we define the corresponding sample covariance matrix as

Q_{0}=\Sigma_{0}^{1/2}XX^{\top}\Sigma_{0}^{1/2},\quad\widetilde{Q}=\widetilde{\Sigma}^{1/2}XX^{\top}\widetilde{\Sigma}^{1/2},

(2.4)

where
X\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times n}
is a random matrix whose entries are i.i.d. centered random variables. By using the same realization of
X
for both
Q_{0}
and
\widetilde{Q}
, any discrepancy between these two sample covariance matrices originates solely from the difference between the population covariance matrices
\Sigma_{0}
and
\widetilde{\Sigma}
.

We impose the following assumptions on
X
,
\Sigma_{0}
, and
\widetilde{\Sigma}
.

Assumption 2.1
.

(i)

Dimensionality.
There exists a constant
\alpha\in(1,\infty)
such that

p\asymp n^{\alpha}.

(2.5)

(ii)

Entries of
X
.
Let
X=(x_{ij})
be a
p\times n
matrix whose entries are i.i.d. real random variables with

\mathbb{E}x_{ij}=0,\quad\mathbb{E}x_{ij}^{2}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{pn}}.

(2.6)

We further assume that for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
k
-th norm of
(pn)^{1/4}x_{ij}
is uniformly bounded, i.e., there exists a constant
C_{k}>0
such that

\mathbb{E}\left|(pn)^{1/4}x_{ij}\right|^{k}\leq C_{k}.

(2.7)

(iii)

Spectrum of
\Sigma_{0}
.
Let
\varsigma\in(0,1)
be a fixed small constant. We assume that
\{\sigma_{i}\}
in (
2.1
) satisfy

\varsigma\leq\sigma_{p}\leq\cdots\leq\sigma_{1}\leq\varsigma^{-1}.

(2.8)

(iv)

Spikes.
Let
\varpi\in(0,1)
be a fixed small constant. We assume that
\{\widetilde{\sigma}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{r}
in (
2.3
) satisfy that for all
i\in\llbracket r\rrbracket

-\phi^{1/2}(\mathsf{c}_{1}+\varpi)^{-1}<\widetilde{\sigma}_{i}<\phi^{1/2}\varpi^{-1},

(2.9)

where
\phi
is defined in (
1.5
), and
\mathsf{c}_{1}<0
with
|\mathsf{c}_{1}|\asymp 1
will be defined in Lemma
2.3
(i) below.
We further assume that

\min_{i\neq j\in\llbracket r\rrbracket}\left|\widetilde{\sigma}_{i}-\widetilde{\sigma}_{j}\right|>\phi^{1/2}\varpi.

(2.10)

Remark 2.2
.

Several remarks are in order. First, condition (i) indicates that we consider the ultra-high-dimensional regime, in which
p
grows much faster than
n
. Second, the scaling
(pn)^{-1/2}
in (
2.6
) is adopted to accommodate the ultra-high-dimensional regime. In contrast, in the classical low-dimensional regime
(\alpha<1)
and the comparable regime
(\alpha=1)
, the scaling
n^{-1}
is typically used. The moment assumption in (
2.7
) can be relaxed to finitely many moments by a truncation argument, but such generalizations are not pursued here for simplicity. Third, (
2.8
) is a mild condition ensuring that the eigenvalues of
\Sigma_{0}
are bounded from both above and below. Finally, the lower bound in condition (
2.9
) is imposed to ensure that
\mathsf{d}_{i}
represents a true spike in the sense that the associated eigenvalue of the sample covariance matrix
\widetilde{Q}
emerges as an outlier. For simplicity, we do not consider the case where
\widetilde{\sigma}_{i}
is smaller than
-\phi^{1/2}\mathsf{c}_{1}^{-1}
, in which case no outlier is created. Although this transition is not analyzed explicitly, the quantity
-\phi^{1/2}\mathsf{c}_{1}^{-1}
serves as the critical threshold distinguishing the subcritical and supercritical regimes, as in other BBP-type transitions. Moreover, we assume a constant-order gap between
\phi^{-1/2}\widetilde{\sigma}_{i}
and
-\mathsf{c}_{1}^{-1}
for clarity of exposition. This condition can be relaxed to gaps of order
n^{-1/3+\epsilon}
for any small constant
\epsilon>0
, following
[
14
,
22
]
. The upper bound in (
2.9
) is imposed only for notational convenience and can be readily removed without difficulty. Condition (
2.10
) ensures that the spiked eigenvalues
\{\widetilde{\sigma}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{r}
are well-separated, so that the corresponding outlier eigenvectors of
\widetilde{Q}
are uniquely defined. Nevertheless, this condition can be relaxed to include degenerate cases by considering the eigenspace as in
[
22
,
14
]
. We will explore these generalizations in future work.

2.2
Asymptotic laws under the non-spiked covariance matrix model

In this subsection, we summarize some results on the asymptotic laws of the eigenvalues of sample covariance matrix
Q_{0}
under the non-spiked covariance matrix model. Recall that for any
p\times p
symmetric matrix
A
, its empirical spectral distribution (ESD) is defined as the empirical probability measure
\mu_{A}=\frac{1}{p}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\delta_{\lambda_{i}(A)}
, where
\{\lambda_{i}(A)\}
are the eigenvalues of
A
. For a probability measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}
, its Stieltjes transform
m_{\mu}:\mathbb{C}^{+}\to\mathbb{C}^{+}
is defined by

m_{\mu}(z)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{1}{x-z}\mu(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x),\quad z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}.

We now characterize the asymptotics of the ESD of
Q_{0}
under Assumption
2.1
(i)-(iii). Since
Q_{0}
and its companion
\mathcal{Q}_{0}:=X^{\top}\Sigma_{0}X
share the non-trivial eigenvalues, we focus on the ESD of
\mathcal{Q}_{0}
. From
[
21
]
, it is known that the ESD of
\mathcal{Q}_{0}
has the asymptotic deterministic equivalent, denoted by
\varrho\equiv\varrho_{\Sigma_{0}}
. The probability measure
\varrho
can be best described through its Stieltjes transform
m\equiv m_{\Sigma_{0}}
. To be more specific, as given in
[
21
, Lemma 2.3]
, for
z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
,
m(z)
is characterized as the unique solution to the equation

z=f(m(z)),\quad m(z)\in\mathbb{C}^{+},

(2.11)

where, recalling that
\phi=p/n
in (
1.5
),
f\equiv f_{\Sigma_{0}}:\mathbb{C}^{+}\to\mathbb{C}^{+}
is defined as

f(x)=-\frac{1}{x}+\frac{\phi^{1/2}}{p}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\frac{\sigma_{i}}{1+\phi^{-1/2}\sigma_{i}x}.

(2.12)

Note that the domain of
f
can be readily extended to the real projective line
\overline{\mathbb{R}}=\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
except
0
and
\{\phi^{1/2}\sigma_{i}^{-1}\}
.

The following lemma established in
[
18
]
summarizes some properties of
f
and
\varrho
. Denote

\mathfrak{m}_{1}:=\int x\pi_{0}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x),

(2.13)

where
\pi_{0}:=\frac{1}{p}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\delta_{\sigma_{i}}
is the ESD of
\Sigma_{0}
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Under Assumption
2.1
(i)-(iii) holds, the following hold for sufficiently large
n
.

(1)

f
has two critical points, denoted by
\mathsf{c}_{1}<0
and
\mathsf{c}_{2}>0
, with
|\mathsf{c}_{k}|\asymp 1
.

(2)

Define
\gamma_{+}:=f(\mathsf{c}_{1})
and
\gamma_{-}:=f(\mathsf{c}_{2})
. Then, we have

\gamma_{+}\geq\gamma_{-}>0,\quad|\gamma_{\pm}-\phi^{1/2}\mathfrak{m}_{1}|=\mathrm{O}(1),

(2.14)

and

\operatorname{supp}\varrho\cap(0,\infty)=[\gamma_{-},\gamma_{+}].

(2.15)

(3)

\varrho
has the square-root behavior near the edge
\gamma_{\pm}
.

Proof.

See Lemma 2.5 and Section B.2 in
[
18
]
.
∎

Remark 2.4
.

From (
2.11
)-(
2.12
) and Lemma
2.3
, the asymptotics of the ESD
Q_{0}
can be obtained once we know
\Sigma_{0}
, more precisely its ESD
\pi_{0}
. In particular, the support of
\varrho
is given by an bounded interval containing
\phi^{1/2}\mathfrak{m}_{1}
.

Remark 2.5
.

When
\Sigma_{0}=I_{p}
, that is, when all
\{\sigma_{i}\}
are equal to one, (
2.12
) feduces to

f^{\mathrm{MP}}(x)=-\frac{1}{x}+\frac{\phi^{1/2}}{1+\phi^{-1/2}x}.

(2.16)

The corresponding asymptotic density, denoted by
\varrho^{\mathrm{MP}}
, is the celebrated Marchenko-Pastur law
[
35
]
, given by

\varrho^{\mathrm{MP}}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x):=\frac{\phi^{1/2}}{2\pi}\frac{\sqrt{\left[(x-\gamma_{-}^{\mathrm{MP}})(\gamma_{+}^{\mathrm{MP}}-x)\right]_{+}}}{x}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x,

(2.17)

where
\gamma_{\pm}^{\mathrm{MP}}=\phi^{1/2}+\phi^{-1/2}\pm 2
. See
[
21
, Remark 2.4]
and
[
1
, Section 2.1]
for details. Note that the critical points of
f^{\mathrm{MP}}
are given by

\mathsf{c}_{1}^{\mathrm{MP}}=-(1+\phi^{-1/2})^{-1}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathsf{c}_{2}^{\mathrm{MP}}=(1-\phi^{-1/2})^{-1}.

Remark 2.6
.

Under the comparable regime
(\alpha=1)
, the function
f
may have an even number of critical points, with convention that a degenerate critical point is counted twice, and that the support of
\varrho
can have multiple bulk components whose edges are the value of
f
at each critical point. See
[
21
, Remark 2.6]
and
[
33
, Lemma 2.4-2.6]
for details.

3
Main results

In this section, we present our main results. For a precise description, we use the notion of
stochastic domination
, first introduced in
[
26
]
. In random matrix theory literature, this has become a standard tool for presenting the statements of the form “
\mathsf{X}
is bounded with high probability by
\mathsf{Y}
up to a small power of
n
”.

Definition 3.1

(Stochastic domination)
.

Let

\mathsf{X}=\left(\mathsf{X}^{(n)}(u):n\in\mathbb{N},u\in\mathsf{U}^{(n)}\right),\quad\mathsf{Y}=\left(\mathsf{Y}^{n}(u):n\in\mathbb{N},u\in\mathsf{U}^{(n)}\right)

be two families of nonnegative random variables, where
\mathsf{U}^{(n)}
is a possibly
n
-dependent parameter set.
\mathsf{X}
is said to be stochastically dominated by
\mathsf{Y}
, uniformly in
u
, if for any small
\epsilon>0
and large
D>0
, we have

\sup_{u\in\mathsf{U}^{(n)}}\mathbb{P}\left(\mathsf{X}^{(n)}(u)>n^{\epsilon}\mathsf{Y}^{(n)}(u)\right)\leq n^{-D},

for all large enough
n\geq N_{0}(\epsilon,D)
, and is denoted by
\mathsf{X}\prec\mathsf{Y}
. For a complex family
\mathsf{X}
, we write
\mathsf{X}\prec\mathsf{Y}
if
|\mathsf{X}|\prec\mathsf{Y}
. For two deterministic nonnegative families
\mathsf{X}
and
\mathsf{Y}
, we write
\mathsf{X}\prec\mathsf{Y}
if
\mathsf{X}\leq n^{\epsilon}\mathsf{Y}
for any small
\epsilon>0
whenever
n
is sufficiently large. We also write
\mathsf{X}=\mathrm{O}_{\prec}(\mathsf{Y})
if
\mathsf{X}\prec\mathsf{Y}
.

We write the spectral decomposition of
\widetilde{Q}
as

\widetilde{Q}=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\widetilde{\lambda}_{i}\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}_{i}\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}_{i}^{\top},

where
\widetilde{\lambda}_{1}\geq\dots\geq\widetilde{\lambda}_{p}\geq 0
and
\{\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}_{i}\}
are the eigenvalues and the corresponding eigenvectors.

3.1
Eigenvalue locations

We start with the eigenvalues of
\widetilde{Q}
. We introduce some notations first. For
i\in\llbracket r\rrbracket
, let

\mathfrak{a}_{i}:=f\left(-\phi^{1/2}\widetilde{\sigma}_{i}^{-1}\right),

(3.1)

where
f
is defined in (
2.12
). Recall the asymptotic law
\varrho
introduced in Section
2.2
. We define its quantiles
\{\gamma_{i}\}
by

\int_{\gamma_{i}}^{\infty}\varrho(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x)=\frac{i}{n},\quad i\in\llbracket n\rrbracket.

(3.2)

Theorem 3.2
.

Suppose that Assumption
2.1
holds.

(i)

For
i\in\llbracket r\rrbracket
uniformly, we have

\left|\widetilde{\lambda}_{i}-\mathfrak{a}_{i}\right|\prec n^{-1/2}.

(3.3)

(ii)

For
i\in\llbracket r+1,n\rrbracket
uniformly, we have

\left|\widetilde{\lambda}_{i}-\gamma_{i-r}\right|\prec(\min\{i,n+1-i\})^{-1/3}n^{-2/3}.

(3.4)

Theorem
3.2
describes the asymptotic behavior of the non-trivial eigenvalues of
\widetilde{Q}
. More precisely, it identifies deterministic equivalents for their locations together with precise error bounds. These deterministic equivalents depend on whether the eigenvalues correspond to the spiked eigenvalues or to the remaining non-spiked eigenvalues.

For the first
r
eigenvalues, the deterministic equivalents are given by
\{\mathfrak{a}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{r}
. Under Assumption
2.1
(iv),
\{\mathfrak{a}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{r}
lie outside the support of
\varrho
of
Q_{0}
. In particular, they are greater than
\gamma_{+}
by constant-order gap; see (
B.8
) below. Thus, (
3.3
) shows that the first
r
eigenvalues
\{\widetilde{\lambda}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{r}
emerge as outliers and concentrate around
\{\mathfrak{a}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{r}
with error of order
n^{-1/2}
.

On the other hand, the deterministic equivalents of the remaining eigenvalues are given by the quantiles of
\varrho
. Specifically, (
3.4
) shows that
\widetilde{\lambda}_{i}
is close to
\gamma_{i-r}
for
i\in\llbracket r+1,n\rrbracket
. The convergence rate depends on the location of the quantile. It is of order
n^{-2/3}
near the edges and of order
n^{-1}
in the bulk. These rates match the typical spacing of the quantiles, which follows from the square-root behavior of
\varrho
at the edges, as stated in Lemma
2.3
(3).

Remark 3.3
.

In the proof of (
3.4
) below, we combine two estimates: the
eigenvalue sticking
and the
rigidity
. Heuristically, the eigenvalue sticking can be expressed as
\widetilde{\lambda}_{i}\approx\lambda_{i-r}
, where
\{\lambda_{i}\}
are the eigenvalues of
Q_{0}
, meaning that the non-outlier eigenvalues of
\widetilde{Q}
“stick” to the corresponding eigenvalues of
Q_{0}
. On the other hand, the rigidity implies that each
\lambda_{i}
concentrates around the quantile
\gamma_{i}
of
\varrho
. See (
B.13
) and Lemma
A.4
below for the precise statements.

Example 3.4
.

Consider Johnstone’s spiked covariance matrix model, that is,
\Sigma_{0}=I_{p}
as in Remark
2.5
. Then, the condition (
2.9
) in Assumption
2.1
is satisfied whenever
\mathsf{d}_{i}>\phi^{1/2}(1+\varpi)
. Under this case, (
3.1
) with
f^{\mathrm{MP}}
in (
2.16
) gives

\mathfrak{a}_{i}^{\mathrm{Johnstone}}=\phi^{1/2}+\phi^{-1/2}+\phi^{-1/2}\mathsf{d}_{i}+\phi^{1/2}\mathsf{d}_{i}^{-1},

which recovers Theorem 2.3 in
[
14
]
.

3.2
Eigenvector projections

In this subsection, we present the results on the eigenvector projections. For
i\in\llbracket r\rrbracket
, denote

\mathfrak{b}_{i}=\left(\phi^{1/2}\widetilde{\sigma}_{i}^{-1}\right)\frac{f^{\prime}\left(-\phi^{1/2}\widetilde{\sigma}_{i}^{-1}\right)}{f\left(-\phi^{1/2}\widetilde{\sigma}_{i}^{-1}\right)}.

(3.5)

Recall that
\{\mathbf{v}_{j}\}
are the eigenvectors of population covariance matrix
\widetilde{\Sigma}
.

Theorem 3.5
.

Suppose that Assumption
2.1
holds. Then,

(i)

For
i\in\llbracket r\rrbracket
uniformly, we have

\langle\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}_{i},\mathbf{v}_{j}\rangle^{2}=\begin{cases}\delta_{ij}\mathfrak{b}_{i}+\mathrm{O}_{\prec}(p^{-1/2}),\quad&j\in\llbracket r\rrbracket,\\
\mathrm{O}_{\prec}(p^{-1}),\quad&j\in\llbracket r+1,p\rrbracket.\end{cases}

(3.6)

(ii)

For
i\in\llbracket r+1,n\rrbracket
uniformly, we have

\langle\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}_{i},\mathbf{v}_{j}\rangle^{2}\prec\begin{cases}(pn)^{-1/2},\quad j\in\llbracket r\rrbracket,\\
p^{-1},\quad j\in\llbracket r+1,p\rrbracket\end{cases}

(3.7)

Theorem
3.5
shows that the first-order deterministic equivalents for individual eigenvector projections are available only for the outlier eigenvectors in their corresponding spike directions. More precisely, for
i\in\llbracket r\rrbracket
, the projection
\langle\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}_{i},\mathbf{v}_{j}\rangle^{2}
has the deterministic equivalent
\mathfrak{b}_{i}
, whose order is given by
\phi^{-1/2}
, only when
j=i
. This means that each outlier eigenvector
\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}_{i}
is asymptotically localized around the spike direction
\mathbf{v}_{i}
, in the sense that it concentrate on a cone with the axis parallel to
\mathbf{v}_{i}
with an aperture given by
\mathfrak{b}_{i}
. For all other projections, we only obtain high-probability error bounds instead. In particular, non-outlier eigenvectors are delocalized in any direction of
\{\mathbf{v}_{j}\}
. The theorem also shows that the order of the bound depends on the index
j
, according to whether
\mathbf{v}_{j}
is a spike direction or a non-spike direction.

Example 3.6
.

Similar to Example
3.4
, Theorem
3.5
recovers the results for Johnstone’s spiked covariance matrix model given in Example 2.13 in
[
14
]
when
\Sigma_{0}=I_{p}
. We omit the details.

Finally, we consider weighted sums of the projections of the non-spike directions
\{\mathbf{v}_{j}\}_{j=r+1}^{p}
onto an outlier eigenvector

\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}_{i}
, where
i\in\llbracket r\rrbracket
. Such quantities arise naturally in statistical applications; see Example
3.9
below for one such example. The individual projection estimate in Theorem
3.5
is not sufficient for this purpose. Indeed, (
3.6
) only gives

|\langle\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}_{i},\mathbf{v}_{j}\rangle|^{2}\prec p^{-1}

for each
j\in\llbracket r+1,p\rrbracket
. Therefore, summing this bound over

j
produces an error of order one, and does not yield a deterministic equivalent for quantities of the form
\sum_{j=r+1}^{p}\ell_{j}\langle\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}_{i},\mathbf{v}_{j}\rangle^{2}
, where
\{\ell_{j}\}
is some deterministic sequence. The following theorem provides such a deterministic equivalent directly at the level of the weighted sum. For
i\in\llbracket r\rrbracket
and a deterministic positive sequence
\{\ell_{j}\}_{j=1}^{p}
, define

\dot{\mathsf{m}}_{0,\mathfrak{a}_{i}}(\mathfrak{a}_{i})\equiv\dot{\mathsf{m}}_{0,\mathfrak{a}_{i}}(\mathfrak{a}_{i};\ell):=\frac{\phi m^{\prime}(\mathfrak{a}_{i})}{p\mathfrak{a}_{i}}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\frac{\ell_{j}/\sigma_{j}}{(\phi^{-1/2}m(\mathfrak{a}_{i})+\sigma_{j}^{-1})^{2}},

(3.8)

where
m^{\prime}(z)=\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z}m(z)
.

Theorem 3.7
.

Suppose that Assumption
2.1
holds. Let
\{\ell_{j}\}_{j=r+1}^{p}
be a deterministic positive sequence such that
\tau\leq\ell_{j}\leq\tau^{-1}
for some small constant
\tau
and
\ell_{1}=\dots=\ell_{r}=\ell_{r+1}
. Then, we have that

\left|\sum_{j=r+1}^{p}\ell_{j}\langle\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}_{i},\mathbf{v}_{j}\rangle^{2}-\phi^{-1}\mathfrak{a}_{i}\mathfrak{b}_{i}\widetilde{\sigma}_{i}\dot{\mathsf{m}}_{0,\mathfrak{a}_{i}}(\mathfrak{a}_{i})\right|\prec n^{-1/4}.

(3.9)

Remark 3.8
.

For
i\in\llbracket r\rrbracket
, the distinction between the case
j\in\llbracket r\rrbracket
and the case
j\in\llbracket r+1,p\rrbracket
for
\langle\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}_{i},\mathbf{v}_{j}\rangle^{2}
is natural in the spiked model. The direction
\mathbf{v}_{i}
corresponds to a spike, and therefore plays a distinguished role in the behavior of the associated outlier eigenvector
\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}_{i}
. Hence, the projection of
\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}_{i}
onto
\mathbf{v}_{i}
is individually tractable. In contrast, the directions

\{\mathbf{v}_{j}\}_{j=r+1}^{p}
are non-spiked directions. Their individual contributions are too small to be resolved separately. Nevertheless, their aggregate contribution admits a tractable deterministic equivalent, as described above.

Example 3.9

(Principal component analysis)
.

In this example, we illustrate how the preceding results can be used to analyze a quantity that arises in principal component analysis. For
i\in\llbracket r\rrbracket
,
\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}_{i}^{\top}\widetilde{\Sigma}\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}_{i}
measures the variance explained in the direction of the
i
th principal component. We use Theorems
3.5
and
3.7
to derive its deterministic equivalent.

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}_{i}^{\top}\widetilde{\Sigma}\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\widetilde{\sigma}_{j}\langle\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}_{i},\mathbf{v}_{j}\rangle^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{r}\widetilde{\sigma}_{j}\langle\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}_{i},\mathbf{v}_{j}\rangle^{2}+\sum_{j=r+1}^{p}\widetilde{\sigma}_{j}\langle\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}_{i},\mathbf{v}_{j}\rangle^{2}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\sigma}_{i}\mathfrak{b}_{i}+\mathrm{O}_{\prec}(\phi^{1/2}p^{-1/2})+\phi^{-1}\mathfrak{a}_{i}\mathfrak{b}_{i}\widetilde{\sigma}_{i}\dot{\mathsf{m}}_{0,\mathfrak{a}_{i}}(\mathfrak{a}_{i})+\mathrm{O}_{\prec}(n^{-1/4})

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\sigma}_{i}\mathfrak{b}_{i}+\phi^{-1}\mathfrak{a}_{i}\mathfrak{b}_{i}\widetilde{\sigma}_{i}\dot{\mathsf{m}}_{0,\mathfrak{a}_{i}}(\mathfrak{a}_{i})+\mathrm{O}_{\prec}(n^{-1/4}),

where

\dot{\mathsf{m}}_{0,\mathfrak{a}_{i}}(\mathfrak{a}_{i})=\frac{\phi m^{\prime}(\mathfrak{a}_{i})}{p\mathfrak{a}_{i}}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\frac{\sigma_{j}/\sigma_{j}}{(\phi^{-1/2}m(\mathfrak{a}_{i})+\sigma_{j}^{-1})^{2}}=\frac{\phi m^{\prime}(\mathfrak{a}_{i})}{p\mathfrak{a}_{i}}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\frac{1}{(\phi^{-1/2}m(\mathfrak{a}_{i})+\sigma_{j}^{-1})^{2}}.

Lemma A.1

(Basic estimates of

Proof.

∎

(A.8)

Proof.

(

18
]

∎

See

Lemma A.5

Suppose Assumption
