Live LTL Progress Tracking: Towards Task-Based Exploration
Noel C. Brindise, Cedric Langbort, Melkior Ornik

Abstract
Motivated by the challenge presented by non-Markovian objectives in reinforcement learning (RL), we present a novel framework to track and represent the progress of autonomous agents through complex, multi-stage tasks. Given a specification in finite linear temporal logic (LTL), the framework establishes a ‘tracking vector’ which updates at each time step in a trajectory rollout. The values of the vector represent the status of the specification as the trajectory develops, assigning true, false, or ‘open’ labels (where ‘open’ is used for indeterminate cases). Applied to an LTL formula tree, the tracking vector can be used to encode detailed information about how a task is executed over a trajectory, providing a potential tool for new performance metrics, diverse exploration, and reward shaping. In this paper, we formally present the framework and algorithm, collectively named Live LTL Progress Tracking, give a simple working example, and demonstrate avenues for its integration into RL models. Future work will apply the framework to problems such as task-space exploration and diverse solution-finding in RL.

Introduction

In this work, we consider a common setting in reinforcement learning (RL) where goal specifications are known a priori, such as pre-determined tasks to complete or constraints to follow. We pay special attention to
non-Markovian
tasks, which contain time or order dependencies. Though multi-stage, order-dependent tasks are quite common, a significant challenge arises when they are applied to the typical Markov decision process (MDP) model in RL, which can only capture Markovian dependencies. While it is possible to extend the system and explicitly encode past events in the state itself, this is prone to state-space explosion
(
5
)
and has prompted a wide variety of workarounds. The most prominent of these is the incorporation of replay buffers and memory
(
33
)
. A popular solution is long short-term memory (LSTM), which preserves information from recent time steps
(
20
;
18
)
. Unfortunately, even state-of-the-art LSTMs suffer in both performance and computation time as the memory size increases, in part due to the inability of the model to reliably identify which past events are most relevant
(
25
)
. In the special case where specifications are known a priori, it seems preferable to leverage this system knowledge to incorporate relevant events more directly into training.

Variants of
reward shaping
have incorporated memory in tailored ways to make non-Markovian tasks learnable, especially via recently-developed
reward machines
and LTL
(
24
)
. In general, reward shaping carefully engineers functions to distribute rewards for improved feedback
(
22
;
35
;
37
)
. In a reward machine (RM), the usual reward function is replaced by a finite state machine (FSM) whose internal states and transitions are structured according to the specification of interest, making it possible to assign rewards depending directly on a notion of progress
(
23
)
. Several recent RM approaches consider specifications in LTL, a convenient syntax for time- and order-dependent expressions like tasks and constraints
(
32
)
, and shown that RMs on these objectives have asymptotic learning guarantees
(
30
)
.

We present our framework, Live LTL Progress Tracking (LPT), and discuss its integration into existing RM methods; we also propose it as a starting point for less-explored capabilities in RL. In line with the suggestion by
9
that internal states of RMs be leveraged during learning, our framework decomposes LTL specifications into a novel multilayered structure, forming an enhanced, informative new tracking state. As shown in Figure
1
, we first decompose a specification into a common tree structure. This tree is used by the algorithm to structure its tracking state. To perform live tracking, trajectory updates are fed into the algorithm, which produces a corresponding tracking state vector. This vector may serve as a reference to identify solutions which are meaningfully different from each other in terms of the task of interest, potentially providing a guiding diagnostic during exploration and a basis for diverse solution finding.

Figure 1:

true
(1),
false

open
(-1) status for each tree node at each time step.

We intend this paper as a theoretical basis for future work and exclude exhaustive experimentation. We will present the algorithm structure and include a reference to a prototype Python implementation. We supplement the formalization with basic conceptual examples and discuss potential for integration and new capabilities in RL.

2
Previous Work

In this section, we situate our work more thoroughly within the literature. Firstly, a variety of methods exist to seek optimal policies for non-Markovian tasks in linear temporal logic (LTL). For stochastic systems, a common objective is to maximize the probability that a specification is satisfied by a strategy. In RL, this strategy is a
policy
, a function prescribing the choice of actions taken by the agent
(
11
)
. LTL synthesis, the most-explored framing for LTL specifications on transition systems, involves a two-step process. First, the LTL specification is converted into an automaton, such as a limit-deterministic Büchi automaton (LDBA). The original system is likewise converted, and the two are combined into a single system over a product state space
(
4
;
40
)
. Next, an optimal policy is found by attempting to maximize the probability that good (“accepting”) states of the automaton are reached via dynamic programming
(
17
)
.

Problematically for the first step, automaton generation is computationally intensive
(
28
)
. Moreover, the guarantees obtained in the maximization step are severely conditioned on sufficient sampling
(
14
;
15
)
. It is thus perhaps unsurprising that practical examples are typically limited to low-complexity tasks, such as simple reach-avoid problems
(
7
;
31
)
.

Partly in response to the challenges of classic LTL synthesis, some approaches in RL have instead shaped a reward function to correspond to progress through the specification. In many cases, these introduce a distance measure to calculate how far the current state is from an accepting state in terms of transitions; actions which take the agent ‘in the right direction’ are rewarded
(
26
;
27
;
29
)
,
36
. For complicated tasks, many approaches seek modularity: breaking TL specifications into simpler subtasks to be learned individually and then applied to multi-step or multi-objective problems
(
41
)
. Reward shaping has also been implemented by approximating a value function on an LDBA
(
3
)
.

Sometimes in combination with the aforementioned strategies, LTL reward machines have begun to gain popularity. Similarly to LTL synthesis, these machines typically begin by constructing an automaton from an LTL specification which is then used to assign rewards. These RMs have been developed for time-discounted LTL
(
1
)
, modularized versions of LTL tasks (“sequential” LTL)
(
42
)
, and finite LTL
(
8
)
. However, RMs structured in this way suffer from the same complexity hurdle as LTL synthesis during automaton generation.

To track a more explicit notion of progress, some approaches incorporate
LTL progression
, first proposed by
2
. LTL progression is a method to update a specification dynamically as a trajectory rolls out, simplifying the LTL specification as its individual requirements are satisfied. In this way, progression tracks the requirements ‘to go’ from a current timestep, i.e., which requirements remain to be met at that point. For example,
39
encode a specification as a Graph Neural Network (GNN) and assigns rewards based on LTL progression. Similarly to an application we will recommend in this paper, some methods use progression to identify subtasks and learn value functions for each of them
(
38
)
.

We avoid LTL progression because of the way it expresses progress, namely as a dynamic specification which compiles remaining requirements into one running ‘to-do.’ As is the case in the automata of LTL synthesis, the single representation obscures many of the finer details of how a specification is met. When applied to the exploration problem, such as by
19
, we propose that our framework will help to identify a wider variety of behaviors. We will explore the distinction in our example scenario.

Preliminaries

3.1
Reinforcement Learning and Reward Machines

We primarily propose the use of our LPT framework for RL. RL is a paradigm in machine learning in which agents learn behaviors using rewards or costs as feedback. The agent and its environment are generally expressed together as a Markov Decision Process (MDP). In our case, we will introduce a
labeling function
to the base MDP.

Definition 1

(Labeled Markov Decision Process)

(1)

is a finite set of actions,

is a reward function.
P
is a finite set of labels, and

is a labeling function assigning a set

to each state in
S
.

An agent prescribes a

\pi:S\rightarrow A
to map states to actions, aiming to optimize its
expected cumulative reward
on the MDP. This may incorporate a discount factor
\gamma\in[0,1]
:

V^{\pi}(s_{t})=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\big[\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{k}R(s_{t+k},a_{t+k})\big]

(2)

where
\mathbb{E}_{\pi}
denotes the expectation given that

.

Recently, some RL approaches have replaced or supplemented the simple reward function with a

. We consider a simplified version adapted from

Definition 2

(Reward Machine)
.

Let
\mathcal{M}=\langle S,A,T,R,P,\mathcal{L}\rangle
. A reward machine for

Note that the function

4
LTL Live Progress Tracking

4.1
Overview

LTL Live Progress Tracking (LPT) is a novel framework that uses LTL specifications to describe and track behaviors, such as an agent completing steps in a task. This is done by mapping agent trajectories to traces and providing the updated traces to the framework as the trajectory progresses. The framework ultimately encodes the trace as a ‘signature:’ a vector which serves as a unique identifier for behavior patterns.

In short, LTP signatures are generated from a ternary tracking vector which contains a value for each time step in the trace. The values correspond to a truth status for each argument of the specification (true, false, open), and they update continuously as the agent trace rolls out (hence the
live
descriptor). Intuitively, an argument is assigned
true
at
t
only if its formula must logically hold on any suffix from
t
, given what has already occurred. It is likewise
false
only if the formula must logically be false. The
open
assignment means that the algorithm has not identified conditions for
true
nor
false
. (We intentionally avoid a stronger statement; we reserve the discussion of this for the Definitions below.)

The example below gives a brief demonstration of how such signatures can describe system traces.

Example 7
.

Consider an agent which should eventually collect two keys, Key A and Key B, represented by formula

\varphi=\mathbf{F}\texttt{keyA}\wedge\mathbf{F}\texttt{keyB}.

We begin by considering the formula tree for
\varphi
, which has five nodes:

\mathbf{F}\texttt{keyA}\wedge\mathbf{F}\texttt{keyB}\quad\mathbf{F}\texttt{keyA}\quad\mathbf{F}\texttt{keyB}\quad\texttt{keyA}\quad\texttt{keyB}

(5)

The LPT framework establishes a tracking vector for each of these nodes, with each vector containing one entry per time step. The signature is simply a concatenation of these vectors with consecutive repeated values merged, e.g.
[0,0,1,-1,-1]
becomes
[0,1,-1]
, since this preserves the order of events while removing excess information about precise timing. For a fixed formula, trajectories can then be compared behaviorally in terms of their signatures.

The signatures of LPT track progress for every node in a rule tree, giving higher-fidelity information than the automata in LTL synthesis (discussed in section

4.2
Definitions

LPT tracks progress at a selected ‘current’ time step
t^{\prime}
during a trajectory rollout. The trajectory up to
t^{\prime}
is described by finite trace

\rho^{t_{0}...t^{\prime}}:=(L_{t_{0}},...,L_{t^{\prime}}).

(6)

For a rollout which terminates at a future time
T_{f}>t^{\prime}
, we call the future sequence of unknown values a
continuation:

Definition 8

(Trace Continuation)
.

Consider
\rho^{t...t^{\prime}}=(L_{t},...,L_{t^{\prime}})
, where
L_{t},...,L_{t^{\prime}}
are known and remain fixed. Then a continuation of
\rho^{t...t^{\prime}}
refers to any trace created by appending an arbitrary label set sequence to
\rho^{t_{0}...t^{\prime}}
, i.e.

\rho^{t...t^{+}}=(L_{t},...,L_{t^{\prime}},L^{+}_{t^{\prime}+1},...,L^{+}_{t^{+}})

(7)

where each
L^{+}
is any set of labels
L\subseteq P
.

Note that this definition does not require
\rho^{t...t^{+}}
to represent a feasible trajectory on
\mathcal{M}
.

The goal of LPT is to assign tracking values
true
(h),
false
(v), and
open
(o) to each formula argument appropriately at each time step given the known information in
\rho^{t...t^{\prime}}
. A value assignment is considered
valid
if it satisfies the following properties:

Definition 9

(Tracking value properties)
.

Consider an LTL formula
\varphi
on
\rho^{t...t^{\prime}}
with continuations
\rho^{t...t^{+}}
. Then a value assignment of (h),(v), or (o) is
valid
if:

\bullet

true (h)
:
\rho^{t...t^{+}}\models\varphi
for all
t^{+}\geq t^{\prime}
.

\bullet

false (v)
:
\rho^{t...t^{+}}\not\models\varphi
for all
t^{+}\geq t^{\prime}
.

\bullet

open (o)
: any of these conditions may or may not be met.

Definition
9
deliberately leaves flexibility in the assignment of (o), using it as a catch-all for cases where
\rho^{t_{0}...t^{+}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle?}}{{\models}}\varphi
is perhaps determinate but not resolved by the algorithm. For example, consider the formula
\varphi=\varphi_{0}\wedge\neg\varphi_{0}
. If the truth of

(Tracking value vector)

Consider

,

. For all entries

-1&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(8)

Definition 11

(Validity of tracking value vector)
.

A tracking value vector
\vec{\xi}
is called valid for LTL formula
\varphi

11

4.3
Algorithm

4.3.1
Definitions and Guarantees

For any specification, the LPT algorithm begins by constructing a formula tree. As in a standard LTL tree, the top-level node contains the original formula, which has child node(s) containing its arguments, and so on, such that the leaves of the tree are atomic formulas (labels). Each node is additionally assigned a
type
to identify its top-level LTL operator (e.g.
\mathbf{F},\wedge,\mathbf{U},...
). The algorithm then processes a current trajectory for the entire tree from the bottom, starting from the tree leaves (
\varphi:=\alpha
) and progressing upward. For each node, it assigns a tracking value for the argument in question based on its formula type and the tracking values of its children. Once the algorithm finishes at the top level, tracking value vectors have been populated for every node in the tree.

4.3.2
Complexity

The computational complexity of LPT is dependent on the length of the trajectory, the depth of the specification, and the specification operator types. The necessary number of computations is also affected by the sequence of events in any individual trajectory. The bound of Theorem
13
is the most general bound, but computation time may be much lower in practice.

Theorem 13

(Live LTL Progress Tracking Complexity)
.

For a formula tree with height L, base node at level
l=0
, and maximum 2 children per node evaluated on trace
\rho
, complexity has upper bound

\mathcal{O}(2^{L}|\rho|^{2}).

(10)

Proof.

Consider a trace with from
T_{0}
to
T_{f}
, i.e.
|\rho|=T_{f}-T_{0}
, for which an LPT update is performed at every time step. For each node
\varphi_{...x}
in a tree, the corresponding module is called once per update; each module produces a tracking value for every
t_{0}
in
\{T_{0},...,t^{\prime}\}
, resulting in
\leq t^{\prime}-T_{0}
evaluations per module call. At each
t_{0}
, the module performs
one evaluation per argument

\varphi_{...x.y}
. Thus, any node with a binary operator (two arguments) performs
n\leq 2(t^{\prime}-T_{0})
evaluations per update, yielding conservative bound
n<2|\rho|
.

A tree with height L, base node at level
l=0
, and maximum 2 children per node will have
n_{L}\leq 2^{L}
nodes at level L. In particular, since the final level
L
must consist of atomic nodes, this may be further bounded as
n_{L}\leq 2^{L-1}
. Furthermore, atomic nodes require only
one
evaluation per call. This means that the number of evaluations for a tree of depth
L
is of order
\mathcal{O}(2^{L-1}2|\rho|)
, or
\mathcal{O}(2^{L}|\rho|)
.

Now, if an update is performed at every time step, there are
|\rho|
total updates. The upper bound on complexity thus becomes
2^{L}|\rho||\rho|
, or

\mathcal{O}(2^{L}|\rho|^{2})

for the full tree.
∎

Conceptual Application: Reward Machines

Figure 5:
Transition function

In the introduction, we suggest that LPT be applied to guide exploration and incentivize diverse behaviors. A starting point is the application of LPT in a reward machine.

Taking tracking vectors as states and considering the dynamics by which they are updated, LPT fits easily into an RM framework. Recalling the Preliminaries, RMs serve as a replacement for simple reward functions in an MDP, assigning rewards based on a separate state space and transition function within the machine itself.

Consider Figure
5
. As shown in the figure, we first define an RM-internal state
(u,L)
. Here,
u
contains a current time as well as the tracking vectors for all nodes of a given specification.
L
is a set of labels as before. Just as in LPT, we take the previous tracking vector
u_{t^{\prime}-1}
as an input alongside the most recent trace step
L_{t^{\prime}}
. The updated tracking vector is then calculated modularly exactly as before, shown in the diagram as the output
u_{t^{\prime}}
. Since the original LPT algorithm does not require any additional or intermediate inputs beyond
(u_{t^{\prime}-1},L_{t^{\prime}})
as described, it may be treated as a self-contained transition function. In the diagram, we show it as
\delta_{u}
.

Given this internal state, a new reward function
\delta_{r}
may be established. In the general definition of RMs,
\delta_{r}
may map
U
to a function space, i.e. a function
R:S\times A\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is output for each
u\in U
.

Suppose all rewards are non-negative. Then, to incentivize a specific tracking state
u_{goal}
, for example, take

\delta_{r}=\begin{cases}R(s,a)&u_{t^{\prime}}=u_{goal}\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(14)

Consider the common case that an agent has learned a specific behavior, but different behaviors are desired. Collecting the tracking vectors observed during the first behavior in the set

:

\delta_{r}=\begin{cases}R(s,a)&u_{t^{\prime}}\not\in U_{old}\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

When applying an RM to an MDP, it is necessary to give the machine access to the RM state as well as its own, such that policies may have the form
\pi:S\times U\rightarrow A
.

6
Conclusion and Future Work

In this paper, we establish Live Linear Temporal Logic Progress Tracking (LPT), an algorithm for detailed tracking of autonomous agent trajectories. LPT produces vector representations of an agent’s progress through tasks that are specified in finite Linear Temporal Logic (LTL). These vectors encode behavioral information such as the order in which task-related events occur. By compressing vectors into a ‘signature’, it is possible to compare trajectories in terms of their behavioral features, independently of specific timing or time scales.

We suggest that LPT has promising applications in reinforcement learning and give a framework for an LPT-based reward machine. By providing progress-based non-Markovian rewards, the machine could be used to incentivize incremental progress through tasks or exploration of diverse behaviors. Future work will apply the conceptual framework of LPT to RL, both for exploration and agent performance diagnostics.

Lemma 15

(Dynamics of
\vec{\xi}
)
.

Consider
\vec{\xi}
which is a valid tracking value vector by Definition

at update

. Denote all entries at

. Then, for entries

\displaystyle(\xi[t]_{t^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=1\text{ valid})\Rightarrow(\xi[t]_{t^{\prime\prime}}=1\text{ valid})

\displaystyle(\xi[t]_{t^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=0\text{ valid})\Rightarrow(\xi[t]_{t^{\prime\prime}}=0\text{ valid})

(19)

for all

for some

,

.

) is true, it must also be true that

This is the required property of a valid assignment of (h) at
t
by Definition

, making
\xi[t]_{t^{\prime\prime}}=1

. Thus,

(\xi[t]_{t^{\prime}}=1\text{ valid})\Rightarrow(\xi[t]_{t^{\prime\prime}}=1\text{ valid})

.

Now suppose
\xi[t]=0
for

, corresponding to assignment (v). Then, by Definition

Lemma
15
yields a useful consequence for updates of
\vec{\xi}
:

Property of valid
\vec{\xi}

Given any entry
\xi[t]
which is valid by Definition
11
at update
t^{\prime}
, the entry
remains valid
at any later update
t^{\prime\prime}>t^{\prime}
if no evaluation is performed.

Proof.

Trivially by Definitions
11
and
9
, any assignment
\xi[t]=-1
is always valid (if unspecific). Thus, only entries with
\xi[t]=0,1
can be potentially be invalid and may
require
evaluation.

The algorithm initializes all entries with
\xi[t]=-1
. As the algorithm
never
evaluates entries with
t>t^{\prime}
given strictly increasing update time
t^{\prime}
,

\xi[t]=-1\quad\forall t>t^{\prime}.

Thus, entries for
\xi[t]
,
t>t^{\prime}
are uniformly valid at
t^{\prime}
and do not require evaluation. Now, for all entries
\xi[t]
with
t\leq t^{\prime}
,

•

if
\xi[t]=0
at the time of a previous update
t^{-}
, i.e.
\xi[t]_{t^{-}}=0
with
t^{-}<t^{\prime}
, then
\xi[t]_{t^{\prime}}
must remain 0 by Lemma

. Thus, no new evaluation is required for such
\xi[t]
at any update
t^{\prime}
.

•

if
\xi[t]=1
, the same principle applies.

•

if
\xi[t]=-1

Therefore, any entry
\xi[t]
which is a valid tracking value at update
t^{\prime}

remains valid
at any later update
t^{\prime\prime}>t^{\prime}
if no evaluation is performed.
∎

Lemma 16

(Terminal Update
T_{f}
)
.

Consider
\vec{\xi}
which is valid by Definition
11
at final update
T_{f}
. Denote entries of any
\vec{\xi}_{T_{f}}

. Then, if the following terminal update is performed for each tree node
\varphi

\displaystyle\xi[t]=\begin{cases}1&\rho^{t...T_{f}}\models\varphi\\

∎
