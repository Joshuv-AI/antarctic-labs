 Executive Summary of CRADA Work:

 Hydrogen stations today serve the emerging market of light duty fuel cell vehicles, primarily in
 California with over 30 public retail locations. There has been a steady increase in the number of
 stations open and hydrogen dispensed, especially in the last two years. From 2015 to 2016, the
 annual amount of hydrogen dispensed increased from 27,400 kg to 109,200 kg, a nearly fourfold
 increase in just one year. One station dispensed nearly 12,000 kg in the second quarter of 2017.
 Despite the significant progress, gaps exist between current infrastructure capabilities and future
 requirements. For example, fuel cell vehicle applications such as buses, medium-duty, and
 heavy-duty trucks will gain market share and this must be considered as future customers at
 hydrogen stations. The expected number of light duty fuel cell vehicles in California alone are
 expected to grow from approximately 4,000 to over 13,000 by 2020, and 37,000 by 2023. To
 serve the multiple mobile fuel cell technologies and increased demand, hydrogen stations will
 have to increase output, decrease cost, and improve reliability. To address these challenges, the
 project team will demonstrate a hydrogen-focused integrated renewable energy production,
 storage, and transportation fuel distribution/retailing system. The proposed R&D tasks address
 key challenges related to light duty station/component reliability and development and validation
 of high flow rate system models for new applications like medium and heavy-duty truck fueling.

 A high-flow hydrogen refueling test station was established at the National Renewable Energy
 Laboratory (NLR) in Golden, Colorado. NLR engineers completed construction of the facility,
 leveraging comprehensive modeling tools (Computational Fluid Dynamics, H2FillS, etc.) and
 existing hydrogen infrastructure at the site. For a heavy-duty fill, the team designed for a 60‒80-
 kilogram transfer from the station to the vehicle at an average flow rate of 10 kg/min throughout
 the duration of the fill. The design criteria assumed a H70, T40 hydrogen station that is similar to
 the current commercially available light-duty stations. The team initially used H2FillS to explore
 what it would take to achieve a fill of this magnitude. Using H2FillS, the team determined that
 the flow rate on a 10 kg/min average fill could go as high as 18–20 kg/min peak flow rate. This
 meant the team must design a system that can accommodate up to a 20 kg/min peak flow during
 the fill process. After determining potential flow rate characteristics, the team used internal
 capacity models to explore how much ground storage was necessary to meet the design criteria.
 As a result, NLR added an additional 288 kilograms of high-pressure (>700b bar) storage to its
 site to achieve a single heavy-duty (HD) fill. The final high-flow hydrogen refueling
 infrastructure design is comprised of a heavy-duty research dispenser (HDD), heavy-duty vehicle
 storage simulator (HDVS), high-capacity hydrogen chiller system (HDPC), and 665 kg of bulk
 gas hydrogen storage. The facility is capable of transferring precooled pressurized hydrogen gas
 (700 bar at -40°C) at average flow rates of 10 kg/min (20 kg/min peak), delivering over 60 kg in
 under 10 minutes to the vehicle storage simulator.

 After commissioning, demonstration fueling events were performed while meeting the
 requirements agreed upon in Task 1 of this project. NLR targeted average flow rates of 10
 kg/min, with 20 kg/min peak for a total mass transfer of 60-100 kg. On October 6, 2022, NLR
 completed a 82.3 kg fueling event in 6 minutes and 33 seconds with mass flow rates of 12.6
 kg/min average and 23 kg/min peak. 100-105% state-of-charge (SOC) was achieved in the
 HDVS and hydrogen gas was delivered at -40°C pre-cooled temperatures. This benchmark
 fueling event proved out the station and presented the largest fill the station can achieve without
 penalties on the system. Data for this fueling event is shown below in Figure 3.

 Figure 3: Benchmark Heavy-Duty Refueling Event. The top graph displays overall fueling metrics
 such as flow control valve (FCV) inlet and outlet pressure, hose delivery pressure to the vehicle,
 and a representative internal vehicle pressure. The bottom graphs displays the SOC progression
 of the vehicle simulator over the duration of the fueling event.

 After the benchmark fueling event, NLR completed several other fueling events at various
 ambient temperatures, totalized mass delivered, fueling temperatures, and vehicle starting
 pressure. Results from these fueling events helped validate modeling work completed in Task 2
 and improve the experiment fueling strategy and performance of the high-flow hydrogen
 refueling facility.

 Task 2: Heavy-Duty Fueling Model

 • Develop and validate high flow rate tank CFD model
 • Develop high flow rate fueling thermodynamic model
 • Validate high flow rate fueling thermodynamic model
 • Deliver publicly available, validated high flow rate fueling model

 NLR developed a high flow rate computational fluid dynamics model (CFD) to simulate thermal
 and flow fields inside and outside hydrogen components. The CFD tank model enables users to
 evaluate 1) how various geometrics impact the thermal and gas flow fields and 2) the
 significance of temperature gradients seen within the vehicle storage tanks, which help identify
 potential “hot spots” caused by stratification of internal tank liner. CFD simulations were also
 used to inform experimental design, test plans for this project to ensure safe operation, and even
 fuel protocol development.

 The CFD task completed both partial and full fill simulations to explore aspects like the effect of
 tank size, injector diameter, and injector angle on homogenous mixing of hydrogen gas. Below
 are CFD results and example of full and partial fill simulations.

 NLR built upon the publicly available light-duty (LD) H2FillS model to design and validate an
 HD version. H2FillS is a 1D physics-based thermal fluid model that simulates the real-world
 fueling process from the high pressure storage system through vehicle onboard storage tanks.
 Changes in temperature, pressure, and mass of components during the fueling process are
 evaluated by the model while also capturing the interaction between the station and vehicle (e.g.,
 how significantly the pre-cooling temperature impacts the temperature rise inside the vehicle
 tanks). Figure 5 overviews the holistic approach taken by H2FillS to model station and vehicle
 fueling interaction and performance.

 7

 Figure 5: H2FillS enables station and vehicle design optimization through individual components’
 influence on fueling performance (i.e., impacts on vehicle tanks caused by changes in
 temperature, pressure, mass, and hydrogen pre-cooling).

 The HD H2FillS models the NLR’s HDVS system (Figure 6), which consists of seven Type IV
 vehicle tanks and two Type III tanks. The model was validated using experiment data captured
 by NLR high-flow hydrogen refueling station. Results of the validation are demonstrated in
 Figure 7.

 Figure 6: 3D representation of NLR’s Heavy-Duty Vehicle Simulator. The design volume of this
 tank setup represents a single Class 8 truck’s potential onboard storage requirement.

 8

 Figure 7: Heavy-Duty H2FillS model validation using NLR experimental high-flow hydrogen
 refueling data. H2FillS demonstrates the ability to predict tank temperatures and pressures for the
 duration of the fueling event with strong accuracy.

 Two types of H2FillS model were developed under this task: Partial and Full Station. The partial
 station model is publicly available (https://www.nlr.gov/hydrogen/h2fills) and the full station
 model is currently under review as a beta-version. Public release of the full station model will
 occur after sufficient partner reviews and development.

 NLR experimentally quantified the performance of an ambient pre-cooling system to reject heat
 to the environment and reduce the energy and capacity required for hydrogen pre-cooling
 thermal systems. To do so, a diffusion-bonded, microchannel heat exchanger was installed on the
 light-duty NLR commercial dispenser at the Energy Systems Integration Facility (ESIF) at
 NLR’s Golden, Colorado campus. The system is activated when outlet temperature of the
 hydrogen from the pressure control valve is above the inlet temperature of the glycol coolant
 flowing through the heat exchanger.

 Figure 8: Flow and component diagram for the dispense flow path with the additional heat
 exchanger is shown on the left and on the right, a picture of the complete installation with NLR’s
 commercial dispenser.

 Using this heat rejection setup, NLR was able to demonstrate total fill heat transfer, peak heat
 transfer, and heat rejected by the heat exchanger at an efficiency of over 90%. Results are shown
 in Figure 9. Various starting SOCs of the vehicle storage with corresponding results are shown in
