Thalamic aphasia as an uncommon manifestation of thalamic stroke due to artery of Percheron occlusion: Case report with literature review
Authors: Aziz Ahizoune
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThalamic aphasia (TA) represents a subcortical form of acquired language disorder that is rarely reported in the literature and is characterized by semantic paraphasia with relatively preserved comprehension and repetition. Bilateral paramedian thalamic infarcts are typically due to the occlusion of the artery of Percheron (AOP). In right-handed patients; TA is mainly reported in the setting of isolated left thalamic lesion, whereas it's rare in the setting of bilateral or right thalamic lesions. The most common manifestations of bilateral paramedian infarction are decreased level of consciousness, cognitive impairment, behavioral disturbances, and vertical gaze abnormalities. Here we describe a 55-year-old right-handed man who developed symptoms of TA secondary to bilateral paramedian infarction of the thalami following occlusion of the AOP.Keywords: Artery of Percheron, Stroke, Thalamic aphasia, Infarction, Language

Introduction

Thalamic aphasia (TA) is a language disorder resulting from bilateral or unilateral thalamic lesions. The pattern of this aphasia is fluent and often with frequent paraphasic errors. However, comprehension and repetition are less impaired. The thalamus is a paired gray matter structure above the midbrain that connects nerve fibers to the cerebral cortex in all directions. Each thalamus is connected to the other by the interthalamic adhesion. The thalamus works as a relay station, filtering information between the brain and the body, and it contains nuclei that receive, process, and send information to associated cortical areas [1].

Cortical lesions in the setting of stroke are the most known cause of acquired cortical aphasia. Subcortical aphasias are infrequent and rarely reported in the literature. Experts classified subcortical aphasia into 3 subgroups: thalamic aphasia (TA), striatocapsular aphasia, and aphasia with paraventricular white matter lesions [2]. TA is the most reported aphasia among these subcortical language disorders and is characterized particularly by semantic paraphasia, with preserved comprehension and repetition [1].

Bilateral paramedian thalamic stroke accounts for about 0.1%-2% of all cerebral ischemic strokes, and 27% of thalamic infarcts [3]. The manifestations of thalamic stroke are various; loss of consciousness, cognitive impairment, coma, confusion, vertical gaze palsy, disturbance of language and behaviors. Bilateral paramedian strokes of thalami are seen especially in the setting of occluded artery of Percheron (AOP) with classic findings in brain MRI [4]. In right-handed patients, isolated lesion of the left thalamus was the most commonly reported to cause TA [5]. In contrast, bilateral thalamic lesions or isolated right thalamic lesion are rarely involved in TA with only few case reports and small series [5].

AOP is a rare anatomical variant that arises from the posterior cerebral artery (PCA) and supplies the paramedian regions of the thalami and also, in some cases, the rostral area of the midbrain and the anterior part of the thalamus [4]. When the AOP is occluded, the typical manifestations are related to bilateral paramedian stroke of the thalami, with or without involvement of the midbrain and anterior region of the thalamus [4]. Here we describe a right-handed patient with thalamic aphasia due to bilateral paramedian infarction without midbrain involvement in the setting of the AOP occlusion.

Case report

A 55-year-old right-handed man with a history of type 2 diabetes and smoking presented to our emergency department with a recent onset of speech disorder. This latter had started suddenly 3 days ago and was characterized by incoherent speech with an excessive number of mispronounced words and difficulty in naming things. These symptoms were not associated with convulsions, altered awareness, fever, or headache. On clinical examination, he was alert with symmetrical and reactive pupils. Hemodynamically, he was stable with a blood pressure of 120/70 mmHg, apyretic, and without nuchal rigidity.

Neurologic evaluation revealed no motor deficits in the limbs, no ataxic syndrome, no sensory impairment, and no cranial nerve involvement. Nevertheless, the assessment of his speech revealed fluent spontaneous speech with a lot of semantic paraphasia. He also had several phonemic errors, leading to distortion of words and jargon when speaking spontaneously or when naming objects. It is important to point out that the patient may stop talking for several minutes during the examination if he is not prompted to do so. Repetition and comprehension of spoken language were normal. As the patient is not literate, reading and writing skills were not tested. The cardiovascular examination and the remaining somatic evaluation were unremarkable.

Regarding this clinical presentation, a CT scan was performed on the day of the admission, and showed bilateral lesion of both thalami (Fig. 1). Consequently, a Brain MRI with contrast injection demonstrated bilateral ischemia of both thalami involving the paramedian territory without midbrain lesions as shown particularly in the coronal T2 sequence (Fig. 2D). The analyzes of TOF sequences and postcontrast T1 weighted images demonstrated the arising of a small branch from the P1 segment of the left PCA. This small branch ends rapidly after its emergence, indicating an occlusion at this level (Fig. 3). This small part of the affected artery corresponded to the emerging segment of the Percheron's artery which was occluded (Fig. 4).

Cardiovascular investigations including electrocardiogram (ECG), cardiac ultrasound, transesophageal echocardiography, cervical ultrasound of the supra-aortic arteries, and Holter ECG were unremarkable. Except for mild hyperglycemia, laboratory tests including complete blood count, C-reactive protein, blood ionogram, prothrombotic, renal and hepatic tests, lipid and thyroid panels were normal. The serological tests for HIV, hepatitis, and syphilis were negative. Screening for autoantibodies; antinuclear antibodies, ANCA, anti-DNA, and anti-extractable nuclear antigen antibodies (SSA, SSB, Jo-1, Scl-70, anti-Sm, anti-RNP), and anti-phospholipid antibodies were negative.

Given this, an atherosclerotic origin was considered in our patient. Therefore, he was started on aspirin 160 mg/d, rosuvastatin 10 mg/d, and speech therapy in addition to his diabetes treatment. Three months later, a significant improvement in his communication was noted. The frequency of semantic paraphasia was decreased, accompanied by occasional difficulty in finding some words.

Discussion

Thalamic stroke accounts for 2%-4% of all ischemic lesions [5]. The paramedian region of the thalamus is supplied by the thalamoperforating arteries arising from segment P1 of the PCA [4]. The P1 segment corresponds to the first segment of PCA before reaching the communicant posterior artery. There is considerable variability in the branches supplying the paramedian area of the thalamus [4]. When there is bilateral and simultaneous ischemia of both paramedian territories of thalami, the AOP is the classic artery obliterated [4]. Percheron's artery, first described by the French neurologist Gérard Percheron in 1973, is a rare anatomical variant of thalamic vascularization that arises from the P1 segment of PCA and irrigates the paramedian thalamic nuclei of both sides [4].

In addition to supplying the paramedian territory of the thalamus, the AOP is involved also in the irrigation of the rostral midbrain and sometimes the anterior region of the thalamus [4]. The symptoms of an AOP stroke are various and depend on which areas of the thalamus are affected. Manifestations of bilateral paramedian infarction of thalami are dominated by confusion, hypersomnolence, coma, memory impairment, and eye movement abnormalities [6,7]. TA is rarely reported in the literature and its phenotype was described firstly by Crosson in 1984 and is characterized by fluent output with frequent paraphasias, mainly of a semantic nature, associated with less impaired auditory-verbal comprehension; and the repetition is minimally affected [8]. In general, most patients present with mild features, with a prevalence of lexical-semantic deficits such as anomia and paraphasia, but comprehension and repetition are usually spared [5]. These features were observed in our patient, who also exhibited jargon aphasia, as already described in TA [1,9].

The thalamus is involved in many functions of the central nervous system and plays an important role through its connections with subcortical and cortical areas. Regarding the linguistic aspect, it is involved in language processes, with multiple operations and modulations on the circuits involved in communication [1]. Several studies have suggested that the left thalamus is primarily involved in TA, and this is supported by functional MRI data [5]. For the anatomoclinical correlation related to TA, it is difficult to restrict the involvement of the left thalamus, as there are studies that have shown bilateral or even right activation of the thalamus during language tasks [5]. In our case, the lateralization of language impairment is difficult to determine because the 2 thalami are involved, suggesting that both structures are the cause of aphasia in our patient.

According to the literature, reports of bilateral thalamic infarcts causing TA are very rare. Generally, TA is described in the context of a left thalamic infarct, or sometimes secondary to a right thalamic infarct [10,11]. TA has been reported following lesions in different thalamic subregions: anterior, paramedian, posterior as well as inferolateral thalamic territory [12]. Thus, it is not known exactly which thalamic subareas are associated with language processing [12].

Bilateral thalamic lesions are mainly seen in the setting of occlusion of the AOP. However, some infrequent conditions could provoke the same lesions such as; internal cerebral venous thrombosis, infiltrating neoplasms of thalami, upper basilar artery infarction, Wernicke's encephalopathy, infections, toxic, metabolic, and inflammatory diseases [3]. These causes were not considered in our patient, regarding our clinical context, results of laboratory tests, and neuroimaging study. Brain MRI is the method of choice for diagnosing the acute phase of AOP occlusion, as demonstrated in our observation.

According to Lazzaro's work, 4 ischemic profiles can be identified for AOP infarction based on the association of bilateral paramedian thalamic involvement with midbrain and/or anterior thalamic involvement [4]. The most prevalent pattern in his study was the bilateral paramedian thalamic with midbrain lesion (43%). In contrast, the pattern observed in our patient didn’t include midbrain involvement.

The most commonly reported causes of AOP infarction are cardioembolic origin [3]. Occlusion of the AOP is more common in men with a median age of 60 years [3]. There are several risk factors for AOP infarction such as; hypertension, smoking, diabetes mellitus, and dyslipidemia [13]. In order of frequency, after the cardioembolic causes, atherosclerosis, and small vessel disease have also been described to cause AOP infarction [13]. Our patient had a history of diabetes and heavy active smoking with a negative assessment for cardioembolic causes and other etiologies, making atherosclerosis the most probable cause in our setting.

Experts suggest that conventional angiography may not be indicated in the setting of AOP stroke, as the lack of visualization of the artery does not exclude its presence [11]. In our patient, occlusion of the initial part of the AOP was demonstrated on TOF and postcontrast T1 sequences on brain MRI. The outcome of TA in AOP occlusion depends on the size and location of the lesion. Thalamic aphasia often recovers rapidly, with little or no symptoms after days or weeks [5]. Our patient showed a significant regression of the semantic paraphasias, which is in accordance with the literature. However, persistent aphasic symptoms have also been described in some case series [5].

Conclusion

Thalamic aphasia is characterized by semantic paraphasias with fluent output, often due to an isolated left thalamic lesion in right-handed patients. It is rarely reported in the context of bilateral paramedian thalamic infarction in the setting of AOP occlusion, as seen in our report. The cardioembolic etiologies are the most involved in AOP stroke, whereas the atherosclerotic causes are rarely implicated. The initial clinical manifestation of TA may be mistaken with another type of aphasia or confusional syndrome if the thalamic lesions are not demonstrated on brain imaging.
