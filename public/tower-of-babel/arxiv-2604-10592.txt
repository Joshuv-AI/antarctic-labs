Post-Cut Metadata Inference Attacks on Quantum Circuit Cutting Pipelines
Samuel Punch, Krishnendu Guha, Utz Roedig

Abstract
Quantum circuit cutting enables near-term quantum devices to execute workloads exceeding their qubit capacity by decomposing circuits into independently runnable fragments. While this extends computational reach, it creates a previously unexplored confidentiality surface: the fragment-level execution transcript observable by a semi-honest cloud provider. We formalise this surface and demonstrate that post-cut transcripts constitute a practical metadata side channel. Operating solely on provider-visible compiled circuit metadata—fragment width, depth, and two-qubit gate count—we evaluate a structured inference attack across six classification objectives spanning algorithm identity, cut mechanism, and coarse Hamiltonian structure. Our simulated corpus comprises 1,200 circuit fragments across eight algorithm families transpiled against three hardware topologies. To anchor our threat model in physical reality, we validate these findings on a 156-qubit production quantum computer, confirming that true QPU execution time remains invariant across a 25 × \times variation in compiled depth. This renders traditional timing side-channels ineffective while verifying the deterministic scaling of the metadata routing tax. Under strict instance-disjoint generalisation, our attack recovers algorithm family with 0.960 accuracy (AUC 0.999), cut mechanism with 0.847 accuracy (AUC 0.924), and Hamiltonian k k -locality with 0.960 accuracy (AUC 0.998). Hamiltonian connectivity and geometry inference achieve AUC of 0.986 and 0.942 respectively, with strong stability under size-holdout. Topology inference is harder but remains consistently above chance (AUC 0.666). A matched-footprint control and channel ablation study confirm that leakage is not explained by trivial scale artefacts and is primarily carried by structural transcript statistics. These results demonstrate that circuit cutting is not confidentiality-neutral and that metadata leakage should be treated as a first-class security concern in quantum cloud systems that use circuit cutting.



I
Introduction

Quantum circuit cutting has emerged as an important technique for
executing workloads that exceed the qubit capacity of noisy
intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ)
devices
[
17
,
21
]
. By decomposing a large circuit
into smaller fragments, executing those fragments independently, and
classically reconstructing the target expectation values, cutting
extends the practical reach of near-term hardware. Recent work has
broadened the circuit-cutting toolbox through improved reconstruction
methods and richer cutting
primitives
[
14
,
18
,
20
]
, and cutting
is increasingly incorporated into wider near-term workflows such as
scalable estimation
pipelines
[
6
]
. Despite this growing importance,
fragmented execution also changes what a cloud platform can observe
about a user workload, creating a confidentiality surface that
remains insufficiently modelled.

The post-cut metadata leakage problem.

Circuit cutting changes the
execution interface
exposed to the
cloud. Instead of submitting a single monolithic job, the client
submits a structured collection of fragment jobs, often with
heterogeneous widths and depths, repeated executions, and non-trivial
ordering constraints. The resulting control-plane
artefacts—including fragment multiplicity, fragment-size profiles,
submission order, timing rhythms, and shot-allocation
patterns—form a high-dimensional
execution transcript
. Even
when quantum states and measurement outcomes are not directly revealed,
these transcript features can correlate with circuit topology,
partition strategy, and underlying problem structure. As a result, a
semi-honest cloud provider can attempt
workload-structure
inference
: recovering algorithmic or structural properties of the
hidden workload from the observable transcript alone.

Why this matters now.

Inference from execution-level side channels is no longer merely
hypothetical. Prior work has demonstrated timing-based inference
against cloud quantum
services
[
15
]
,
controller-level power side channels that support circuit
reconstruction
[
9
,
24
]
,
and queue- or scheduling-based metadata leakage in superconducting
cloud settings
[
3
]
. In shared hardware environments,
multi-tenant interference and crosstalk further amplify leakage and
may enable adversarial strategies beyond passive
observation
[
7
,
1
,
12
]
. However,
these studies do not isolate the distinct leakage surface induced by

fragment-based execution
, where the observable transcript is
shaped by partitioning and reconstruction constraints rather than by
a single circuit submission.

Why existing confidentiality frameworks do not close this gap.

Blind quantum computation (BQC)
[
5
]
and related
protocols provide strong privacy guarantees for
state-level

information against an untrusted server, while later refinements add
verifiability
[
10
]
or experimental
demonstrations
[
2
]
. Quantum homomorphic
encryption
[
8
]
and classical
verification
[
16
]

broaden the delegation landscape, and measurement-only approaches
offer alternative client-capability
profiles
[
11
]
. These frameworks primarily reason about
confidentiality for
monolithic
execution or interactive
protocols. They do not explicitly model the incremental,
structure-dependent leakage introduced by fragment multiplicity,
scheduling constraints, and transcript organisation in circuit-cutting
workflows.

Circuit Cutting and Knitting.

Quantum circuit cutting was formalised by Peng

Recent work has explored circuit cutting as a tool for algorithm

However, they do not isolate post-cut transcript leakage as an attack

(iii) generalisation-tight evaluation protocols.

Quantum Confidentiality and Delegated Computation.

[

, QHE
[

]
, verifiable

[

]

[

provide strong guarantees for monolithic execution or interactive
protocols but do not model the incremental, structure-dependent
leakage introduced by fragment multiplicity and scheduling patterns
intrinsic to circuit cutting.

Quantum Cloud Side Channels.

Timing-based attacks
[
15
]
,
controller-level power
analysis
[
9
,
24
]
, and
scheduling-based inference
[

]
show that execution metadata
can reveal circuit structure. Multi-tenant crosstalk further
demonstrates leakage via co-resident
effects
[
7
,
1
,
12
]
.

While the aforementioned side-channels primarily exploit physical-layer phenomena—such as hardware crosstalk, instruction-timing variations, or multi-tenant resource contention—they uniformly operate under the assumption that the provider executes a single, monolithic circuit. The advent of quantum circuit cutting fundamentally fractures this threat landscape. Instead of executing a coherent sequence of operations, the provider now orchestrates a disjointed stream of independent sub-circuits. Consequently, the confidentiality perimeter shifts from the physical execution hardware to the classical submission interface. This paper departs from physical side-channel analysis to formalise a newly exposed attack surface:
post-cut metadata inference
. We demonstrate that the statistical distributions of classical metadata (width, depth, and gate counts) are sufficient to reconstruct the original computational intent.

System Model

We model circuit-cutting execution as a two-domain pipeline comprising
a
Trusted Client Environment
and an
Untrusted Cloud
Service
, as illustrated in Fig.
1
. The client
is responsible for circuit construction, cutting, transcript shaping,
and post-processing, whereas the cloud controls job ingestion,
scheduling, and backend execution.

Fig. 1
:
System Model Architecture.
End-to-end
circuit-cutting workflow across the trusted client environment and

submission and client-side mitigation and stitching.

Execution Pipeline.

The workflow proceeds through ten stages: (1) workload specification,
(2) circuit partitioning and preparation, (3) transcript preparation,
(4) job submission across the trust boundary, (5) cloud ingest and
scheduling (where execution artefacts
\Pi
first become observable),
(6) backend execution, (7) result serialisation, (8) result
retrieval, (9) error mitigation and stitching, and (10) application
output. The stitching specification and reconstruction map remain
local to the client throughout.

We partition information into four domains:

•

Secret State (
\mathcal{S}
).
Exists solely within
the Trusted Client Domain: the original circuit
G
,
partitioning decisions, and the stitching specification.

•

Public Parameters (
\mathcal{P}
).
Known to both
parties: hardware gate sets and device noise characteristics.

•

Observable Transcript (
\mathcal{L}
).
The metadata
surface exposed at the Submission Interface and during Cloud
Ingest, comprising the prepared transcript
L
and the resulting
Execution Artefacts
\Pi
.

•

Ideal Assumptions.
We assume
Execution
Integrity
(faithful execution) and
Client Isolation

(no leakage outside the defined interface).

Threat Model

Fig. 2
:
Threat Model Analysis.
A semi-honest cloud

Adversary.

The provider is
semi-honest
(honest-but-curious): it executes
fragment jobs correctly but passively logs the classical metadata
available at the Submission Interface. Its advantage derives solely
from offline statistical inference. It does
not
observe
measurement outcomes, circuit payloads, compiled gate sequences,
qubit mapping, or physical-layer telemetry.

Observable Transcript.

The provider observes

\mathcal{T}=\{(w_{i},\,d_{i},\,q_{i},\,s_{i},\,t_{i},\,\pi_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N},

where
w_{i}
,
d_{i}
,
q_{i}
denote
compiled
fragment width,
depth, and two-qubit gate count respectively;
s_{i}
the shot
allocation;
t_{i}
the submission timestamp; and
\pi_{i}
the
execution order.

Inference Objectives.

The adversary pursues the following goal:

1.

Workload-Structure Inference:
Recovering
cutting mechanism (W1), algorithm family (A1), sub-family
variant (A2), and coarse Hamiltonian structure—connectivity
regime (H1), geometry class (H2), and
k
-locality
regime (H3).

The realism of this semi-honest model stems from the standard
operational practices of cloud quantum providers. Metadata such
as compiled circuit depth, two-qubit gate counts, and shot
allocations are routinely collected for billing, queue management,
and system telemetry. Consequently, the adversary requires no
malicious modifications to the physical control stack or
compilation pipeline to compile the observable transcript.
Because circuit cutting and fragmentation processes inherently
preserve certain topological features of the original monolithic
workload, the resulting statistical distribution of the
sub-circuits acts as a structural fingerprint. By applying
offline statistical learning to these routine logs, the provider
can passively reconstruct the high-level computational intent
without ever inspecting the underlying quantum states or
physical-layer measurements.

Workload-structure inference relies on exploiting these distinct
structural footprints across diverse computational domains. The
globally entangled operations of algebraic functions like the
Quantum Fourier Transform yield fragment profiles that differ
significantly from the parameterized layers of variational
hardware-efficient ansätze or the Trotter-like steps
characteristic of chemistry and physics simulations. These
structural differences are encoded in the compiled fragment
width, depth, and two-qubit gate count — the three
provider-visible observables that form the basis of the attack
evaluated in §
VI
.

Adversary Training Corpus.

The supervised classifiers evaluated in §
VIII
require labelled
training examples. We model this requirement as operationally realistic for a
large cloud provider via two complementary mechanisms. First, a provider can
construct a
calibration corpus
by submitting known open-source benchmark
circuits—drawn from established libraries such as QASMbench or MQT
Bench
[
13
,
19
]
—under controlled conditions and recording the
resulting compiled metadata. Second, a provider can leverage
retrospective
labelling
: a subset of historical jobs will correspond to published algorithms
whose identity is inferable from job names, user publications, or API context,
providing weakly supervised signal at scale. Crucially, the provider need not
label the workload it is actively trying to attack; the classifier is trained
offline on representative circuits and applied passively to new submissions.
This is consistent with the semi-honest model: no modifications to the execution
pipeline are required, only retention of the metadata logs that are already
collected for billing and telemetry.

Out of Scope.

Active attacks, result manipulation, and adaptive query strategies are excluded.

Before presenting the attack, we establish

overhead, and compiled width—and show that each carries
distinct, non-redundant information about the underlying workload.

Compiled Width: A Topology-Invariant Family Signal

While depth and 2Q overhead carry a joint family

Two observations from panel (a) are particularly informative.

The remaining families overlap substantially in the 6–14 qubit

V-C

Complementary Structure of the Three Observables

The three compiled observables carry complementary and
non-redundant information:

•

Compiled depth
encodes the family
\times
topology
interaction through the routing tax. It is the dominant signal
carrier (Gini importance 0.44–0.51 across all tasks,
Fig.
6
).

•

Compiled 2Q count
captures routing demand
independently of depth, providing additional discriminative
power for families with similar depth profiles but different
gate densities.

•

Compiled width
provides topology-invariant family
information, contributing most to topology inference and
providing the primary signal for isolating Oracle and Sim
from the rest of the family taxonomy.

This complementary structure explains why removing any single
feature degrades performance and why all three observables are
necessary for robust leakage across the full task set.

Attack Design and Methodology

VI-A

Attacker Models

•

Random Forest (RF)
— headline attacker. Robust to
feature scaling, strong on tabular data.

•

ExtraTrees (ET)
— stronger non-linear baseline.

•

HistGradientBoosting (HGB)
— gradient boosting
baseline.

All training is offline and computationally feasible (under 5 minutes
on commodity hardware), consistent with a provider who caches
metadata and trains models asynchronously.

VI-B

Evaluation Protocols

•

Instance-disjoint split:
No cutting job appears in
both train and test sets. Prevents memorisation of specific
circuit instances.

•

Size-holdout split:
The largest-width quartile is
held out as test. Tests generalisation to unseen workload scales.

•

Matched-footprint control:
A caliper of 0.20 in
normalised (width, depth, fragment count) space restricts
evaluation to footprint-matched subsets. Removes coarse scale
cues and tests whether residual leakage survives equalisation.

We report Accuracy (ACC), Macro-F1, and Macro-AUC (OvR) with 95%
bootstrap confidence intervals (
B{=}1000
). For binary W1 cut
mechanism, AUC uses the positive-class probability directly.

Headline Leakage Under Instance-Disjoint Evaluation

Table

The size-holdout results reveal an important scope boundary. A1 accuracy drops from

Based on these results, a clear performance hierarchy emerges:

\text{H3}\approx\text{A1}>\text{W1}_{\text{cut}}>\text{H1}\approx\text{H2}\gg\text{W2}_{\text{backend}}

This ordering reflects how directly each hidden property maps onto the structural compiled features. Tasks where the routing tax produces unambiguous, structure-only signatures achieve near-ceiling performance. Conversely, backend topology inference (W2) sits at the bottom of the hierarchy; because restricted hardware topologies impose overlapping inflation penalties on all complex algorithms, isolating the hardware itself from the algorithm running on it proves significantly more difficult, though performance remains well above the random baseline (AUC

Metadata vs. Timing: A Critical Distinction

The most significant finding for our threat model is the stark invariance of
QPU execution time across all 32 physical workloads. As shown in
Table

1.895

18

Strikingly, the HEA circuit at
n{=}5

2.408

n{=}12

slower

This timing invariance occurs because, in modern superconducting quantum architectures, the raw physical execution duration of microwave pulses (measured in nanoseconds) is dwarfed by macroscopic control-plane latencies. System overheads such as waveform generation, cryogenic control line multiplexing, active qubit reset protocols, and batch readout integration times dominate the physical execution envelope. Consequently, traditional timing side-channels—which rely on microarchitectural execution differences—are effectively blinded by this systemic noise floor.

Conversely, the classical metadata logged by the provider for job orchestration, billing, and system telemetry remains perfectly descriptive, deterministic, and high-resolution across the full scale of submitted circuits. This empirically demonstrates that in modern quantum cloud architectures, the primary confidentiality perimeter shifts away from the physical hardware layer and into the classical control plane. An adversary with access to classical orchestration logs can cleanly bypass the physical-layer noise that thwarts traditional side channels, cementing post-cut metadata inference as a distinctly superior and more reliable attack vector.

X-D

Implications

These results demonstrate that circuit cutting is not
confidentiality-neutral. Several findings warrant specific
attention for practitioners:

Hamiltonian structure leakage is particularly sensitive.

H1–H3 results show that a provider can infer coarse properties of the
underlying problem Hamiltonian — not just the algorithm type. In
competitive research environments, this represents a meaningful
intellectual property risk beyond what algorithm-family inference
alone would suggest.

The routing tax is the mechanism and cannot easily be
eliminated.
Mitigating structural leakage would require either
standardising compiled circuit footprints (computationally
expensive) or injecting dummy fragments to obfuscate size
distributions. Both approaches trade confidentiality for
execution cost.

Topology inference is limited but non-trivial.
A provider
cannot reliably distinguish Linear from Heavy-Hex, but can reliably
identify whether a backend is ideal (all-to-all) or restricted.
This leaks information about the user’s hardware access pattern.

XI
Conclusion

We formalised post-cut execution transcripts as a confidentiality
surface and demonstrated that a semi-honest cloud provider can
infer meaningful structural properties of quantum workloads from
compiled fragment metadata alone. Under strict instance-disjoint
generalisation across an 8-family, 1,200-fragment empirical corpus,
our attack achieves AUC of 0.999 for algorithm family, 0.924 for
cut mechanism, and 0.998 for Hamiltonian
k
-locality, using only
three provider-visible compiled circuit features.

The leakage hierarchy — structural properties before topological
properties — reflects the physical routing tax mechanism:
topology-dependent compilation overhead makes compiled circuit
footprints informative about the algorithms and Hamiltonians that
produced them. This is empirically confirmed by near-perfect
structure-only channel performance on five of six tasks, and
validated on a 156-qubit production backend where QPU execution
time remains invariant across a
25{\times}
variation in compiled
depth, rendering timing side-channels ineffective.

These results demonstrate that circuit cutting is not
confidentiality-neutral. Classical control-plane artefacts
generated by fragmentation leak algorithmic and Hamiltonian-level
information in a statistically robust manner, persisting under
matched-footprint controls and across unseen workload scales.
Transcript-level metadata should be treated as a first-class
security concern in quantum cloud system design, and mitigations
must target the compilation footprint — not only the circuit
payload — to constitute a complete quantum cloud security posture.

Cross-run linkability — determining whether separate transcripts
originate from the same workload instance — represents a natural
extension of this attack surface and is left for future work.
