Gut fungal signatures in colorectal cancer and their potential for supporting diagnosis: a multi-cohort metagenomic analysis
Authors: Zhihang Zhang, Tong Lu, Bo Dong, Jin Liu, Yue Zhang, Shenghui Li, Hengming Liu, Xin Li, Tianyu Guan, Hongxing Guo, Qiulong Yan, Zhixin Lei, Xiaofei Yu, Leyi Wang, Jian Kang, Linlin Li, Deyu Zhao

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundColorectal cancer (CRC) is influenced by host factors and environmental exposures that shape gut microbial ecosystems. Although bacterial and viral alterations in CRC have been widely investigated, the role of gut fungi remains underexplored, partly because of their low biomass and the limited availability of well-curated fungal reference genomes.MethodsWe conducted a large-scale metagenomic analysis across 9 publicly available cohorts comprising 1,433 fecal samples to characterize CRC-associated fungal alterations and fungal-bacterial co-abundance patterns. The predictive value of microbial signatures was assessed using LASSO and random forest models, with external validation performed in 6 independent cohorts comprising 272 samples.ResultsMulti-cohort analysis revealed CRC-associated alterations in gut fungal community structure and selected diversity measures. Differential abundance analysis identified 15 fungal species with recurrent changes across cohorts. Among them, Saccharomyces cerevisiae c86 and Trichophyton rubrum c61 showed predominant enrichment in healthy controls, whereas Barnettozyma c122 and Pseudopithomyces c302 showed predominant enrichment in CRC. However, integrating fungal features with bacterial biomarkers modestly improved CRC prediction performance compared with bacterial-only models. Background

Colorectal cancer (CRC) represents a major global health burden, causing more than 500,000 deaths annually and ranking as the third most common cancer worldwide [1]. Epidemiological projections suggest that CRC incidence will continue to rise and may become one of the most prevalent cancers in the coming decades [2]. Although multiple risk factors have been associated with CRC, including environmental exposures [3], genetic predisposition [4], chronic inflammatory conditions [5], metabolic disorders (diabetes and obesity) [6], and lifestyle factors (excessive alcohol consumption, tobacco use, and dietary patterns characterized by high red/processed meat intake and low fruit/vegetable consumption) [6, 7], the precise etiological mechanisms remain incompletely understood due to the disease’s remarkable heterogeneity and complex pathogenesis.

The gut microbiota, comprising bacteria, fungi, and viruses, serves as a critical interface between environmental exposures and host physiology, playing a key role in maintaining human physiological homeostasis and has been implicated in various disease states [8]. Accumulating evidence suggests that gut microbial dysbiosis is associated with a range of pathological conditions, including obesity [9], Parkinson’s disease [10], hypertension [11], renal diseases [12, 13], inflammation, and atherosclerosis [14]. Furthermore, there is growing evidence indicating a mechanistic association between specific bacterial taxa and the pathogenesis of CRC [15]. For instance, an enrichment of Fusobacterium spp. [16], pathogenic strains of Escherichia coli, and enterotoxigenic Bacteroides fragilis has been consistently observed in CRC patients [17]. Moreover, distinct bacterial signatures including Fusobacterium nucleatum, Porphyromonas asaccharolytica, Gemella morbillorum, and Clostridium difficile have been identified as potential diagnostic biomarkers for CRC [18], highlighting the clinical relevance of microbial profiling in oncology.

Equally important are the contributions of viruses to CRC development. The gut virome exhibits CRC-associated alterations [19], with oncolytic viruses not only inducing the body’s anti-tumor immune response through the presentation of tumor antigens but also promoting CRC cell death via selective replication in malignant cells. Furthermore, Brunovirus and Hpunavirus show positive correlations with CRC proliferation, whereas Lubbockvirus displays an inverse association [20]. These findings, corroborated by our previous research as well as studies conducted by Yang et al. [21, 22], position the gut virome as a promising diagnostic tool for CRC detection.

In contrast to the well-established research on gut bacteriome and virome, studies focusing on fungi in CRC are remain underexplored. This disparity is primarily due to the relatively low abundance of fungi in the human gut, which accounts for approximately 1% of the total microbial biomass, as well as the limited availability of well-annotated fungal reference genomes that are essential for accurate sequence alignment. These technical limitations have frequently led to the neglect of fungal communities in conventional metagenomic studies [23]. However, emerging evidence emphasizes the significant impact of the imbalance in fungal community composition on different disease status. For example, Aspergillus sydowii has been shown to play a pivotal role in shaping the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment in lung adenocarcinomas, despite its low biomass. This fungal species promotes tumor progression and is associated with markedly poorer clinical outcomes [24]. Similar oncogenic roles have been identified for Malassezia species in the development of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma and for Blastomyces species in breast cancer pathogenesis [25, 26]. Collectively, these observations suggest that alterations in the gut mycobiome may influence the initiation and progression of CRC.

Another major challenge in CRC microbiome research is the inconsistency across studies caused by technical and biological heterogeneity, including differences in sample processing, sequencing platforms, analytical pipelines, geography, diet, and host background [27, 28]. Large-scale integrative analyses across multiple cohorts are therefore needed to identify more reproducible microbial features and evaluate their diagnostic relevance. In this study, we systematically analyzed nine publicly available shotgun metagenomic datasets comprising 1,433 fecal samples from CRC patients and healthy controls. Using a standardized analytical pipeline and a curated human-associated fungal genome catalog, we characterized CRC-associated fungal alterations, evaluated fungal-bacterial co-abundance patterns, and assessed the diagnostic value of fungal signatures alone and in combination with bacterial biomarkers. Our findings provide a multi-cohort view of CRC-associated gut mycobiome alterations and highlight exploratory fungal signatures that may complement existing microbiome-based CRC detection models.

Methods

Publicly available metagenomic datasets from CRC patients and healthy controls

We systematically identified CRC studies published between 2014 and 2024 that utilized shotgun metagenomic sequencing of human fecal samples from large cohorts through the NCBI database. Our primary analysis included 9 eligible cohorts comprising metagenomic datasets from 1,433 samples, including 641 CRC patients (the CRC group) and 792 healthy controls (the CTR group). The following accession IDs: PRJNA706060 (Gao_2022 et al.) [29], PRJNA961076 (Grion_2023 et al.) [30], PRJNA731589 (Liu_2022 et al.) [31], PRJNA758208 (Xu_2022 et al.) [32], PRJDB4176 (Yachida_2019 et al.) [33], PRJNA763023 (Yang_2024 et al.) [34], PRJEB10878 (Yu_2015 et al.) [35], PRJEB6070 (Zeller_2014 et al.) [36] and PRJEB7774 (Feng_2015 et al.) [37] were used to download datasets from NCBI Sequence Read rchive (SRA) database. In addition, the 6 validation cohorts included 272 samples, comprising 146 CRC patients and 126 healthy controls. The following accession numbers PRJEB12449 (Vogtmann_2016 et al.) [38], PRJNA389927 (Hannigan_2018 et al.) [39], PRJNA447983 (Thomas_a_2019 et al.) [40], PRJNA447983 (Thomas_b_2019 et al.) [40], PRJEB70916 (Sadeghi_2024 et al.) [41] and CNP0002641 (Chen_2023 et al.) [21] were also downloaded. Sample size and details of sample collection of retrieved cohorts is reported in (Table S1). Detailed information on gender, age, BMI, country, DNA extraction method, and sequencing platform for all samples is summarized in (Table S2).

Construction of gut fungal genome catalog

We established a high-quality human-associated fungal genome catalog through rigorous multi-step curation of NCBI resources. Initially, we downloaded 16,634 fungal genomes in February 2025, removing 1,384 low-quality (N50 length < 2,000 bp or number of scaffolds > 10,000) or a mixture of multiple genomes, remaining 15,250 genomes retained as a reference for further analyses. Meanwhile, to ensure accurate fungal identification and the purity of the reference genomes, we adapted methods from published articles [42]. Specifically, all fungal candidate sequences were aligned against the NCBI bacterial nucleotide (NT) database using BLASTN, and any sequences exhibiting significant homology to prokaryotic genomes (identity > 95%) were removed. We identified a total of 1,490 fungi species associated with humans. These strains were then clustered into 317 non-redundant human-associated fungal species based on an average nucleotide identity (ANI) threshold of 95%.

Processing of metagenomic sequencing data

We performed rigorous quality control on each sample using fastp v0.20.164 [43], implementing the following filtering criteria: (1) removal of reads < 90 bp; (2) exclusion of reads with mean Phred score < 20; (3) removal of reads with an average complexity < 30%; (4) removal of reads where > 30% of their bases have a Phred quality score < 20; and (5) removal of unpaired-end reads. The detailed statistics for raw reads, filtered reads, and final fungal counts for each sample are provided in (Table S2).

For comprehensive characterization of the gut bacteriome, we aligned metagenomic reads from both CRC patients and healthy controls against the Unified Human Gastrointestinal Genome (UHGG) reference database [44]. Following alignment, we systematically excluded reads mapping to the bacterial rRNA/tRNA gene sequences, resulting in the identification of 2,354 prokaryotic species. Relative abundance of these species was calculated by normalizing each sample, with phylum and genus level relative abundances obtained by summing the abundances of species within the same taxonomic group.

To ensure specific detection of fungal communities, we implemented a rigorous filtering method specifically to minimize the impact of potential contaminants. Specifically, quality-controlled reads were sequentially aligned against three reference databases: (1) the MetaPhlAn4 bacterial genome database [45], (2) the complete chm13 v2.0 human genome, and (3) the SILVA rRNA gene database [46]. Through this multi-step alignment strategy that effectively eliminated reads of human or prokaryotic origin. Subsequently, we mapped the remaining reads to our curated gut fungal genome catalog using Bowtie2 with optimized parameters, employing the settings ‘--end-to-end’ [47]. To ensure high-confidence taxonomic assignment, only alignments with a sequence identity of at least 90% were retained, ultimately identifying 273 fungal species. To eliminate potential batch effects arising from different sequencing platforms, we performed batch effect correction using the MMUPHin pipeline. Subsequently, to estimate fungal relative abundance, mapped reads assigned to each fungal genome were first normalized by genome length to reduce bias caused by differences in genome size. The resulting length-normalized counts were then rescaled within each sample to a TPM-like scale by dividing each value by the total length-normalized counts and multiplying by one million. Taxon-level fungal abundances were calculated by summing the relative abundances of all species assigned to the corresponding taxon.

Statistical analyses and visualization

All statistical analyses were performed in R software version 4.1.2, and visualizations were generated using the ggplot2 package.

Microbial diversity was assessed at the species level. Species richness was defined as the number of detected microbial species with relative abundance > 0 in each sample. Alpha diversity was evaluated using Shannon’s index calculated with the diversity function in the vegan package. Beta diversity was assessed using principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) based on Bray–Curtis dissimilarity matrices, and group differences were tested by permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) using the adonis2 function in the vegan package.

Differential microbial signatures between CRC patients and healthy controls were identified separately within each of the 9 discovery cohorts using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. For fungal species, features with p < 0.05 and fold change (FC) > 1.2 were considered significant. For bacterial species, features with FDR-adjusted q < 0.05 and FC > 1.2 were retained. P-values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg method. To improve reproducibility across cohorts, only microbial species showing significant differential abundance in at least 3 cohorts were retained for downstream analyses. For meta-analysis, fungal relative abundances were first transformed using the arcsine square-root transformation. Differences between CRC patients and healthy controls were then summarized using Hedges’ g effect sizes calculated with the escalc function. Between-study heterogeneity was evaluated using Cochran’s Q test and I² statistics with the rma function.

Bacterial-fungal co-abundance relationships were evaluated using both Spearman correlation and SparCC analysis. For Spearman analysis, correlations with coefficient > 0.3 and q < 0.05 were considered significant. SparCC analysis [48] was performed on bacterial and fungal read count profiles using fastspar v0.0.10 with --iterations 20 [49]. Statistical significance was assessed using 1,000 bootstrap iterations, and correlations with coefficient > 0.3 and p < 0.05 were considered significant.

CRC prediction models were constructed using least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression and random forest (RF). Three validation strategies were applied: intra-cohort validation, cross-cohort validation, and leave-one-cohort-out (LOCO) validation. For intra-cohort validation, 10 repeats of stratified 10-fold cross-validation were performed within each cohort, and feature selection was conducted strictly within the training folds. LASSO models were fitted using the cv.glmnet function with alpha = 1 and family = “binomial”, and the optimal λ was selected using lambda.min. RF models were trained with ntree = 999 and seed = 123. For cross-cohort validation, models were trained on one cohort and tested on another. For LOCO validation, models were trained on 8 cohorts and tested on the held-out cohort, with feature selection repeated within each training set. External validation was performed by training models on all 9 discovery cohorts and testing them on 6 independent validation cohorts. Model performance was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) calculated with the pROC package. Model calibration was evaluated in the external validation cohorts by comparing predicted probabilities with observed CRC outcomes. For both the bacterial-only and bacterial-fungal mixed models, predicted probabilities were divided into five equally sized groups, or quintiles, using the ntile function from the dplyr package. Quintile-based grouping was used because the external validation cohorts had relatively small sample sizes, and five groups provided a practical balance between interpretability and stability while avoiding overly sparse bins. For each quintile, the mean predicted probability was plotted against the observed proportion of CRC cases.

Results

Collection of datasets

In this study, we analyzed fecal microbiota profiles from 9 publicly available cohorts (total samples = 1,433; 641 CRC patients and 792 healthy controls) using standardized whole-metagenome shotgun sequencing (Table S1). To identify reproducible microbial biomarkers for diagnosing CRC, we further established an independent validation dataset comprising 6 cohorts (total samples = 272; 146 CRC patients and 126 healthy controls) (Table S1). To minimize technical biases in bioinformatics processing and ensure methodological consistency, all sequencing data were analyzed using a uniform pipeline, yielding approximately 15.3 Tbp of high-quality data for downstream analysis. Key cohort-level metadata, including sample size, age, sex, BMI, country, DNA extraction method, sequencing platform, and sequencing quality statistics, are summarized in Table S2.

Alterations in gut microbiota diversity and structure in CRC patients

To assess within-sample (α) diversity of the gut microbiota community across the 9 cohorts, we evaluated two key indicators: the Shannon index and the observed number of microbial species. Statistical differences in the Shannon index between the CRC and CTR groups were observed only in the Yang_2024 cohort (Fig. 1A). Additionally, significant differences in the number of observed fungal species were found in only 2 cohorts (Wilcoxon rank-sum test, p < 0.01) (Fig. S1A). Furthermore, when all cohorts were combined, the Shannon index did not show significant differences between groups, but the observed number of fungal species was significantly higher in the CRC group compared to the control group (Fig. S1B-C). Additionally, the bacterial Shannon index and the number of observed bacterial species showed difference in only 2 cohorts, demonstrating a high consistency with fungal diversity (Fig. S1D, E). When cohorts were combined, a significant Overall difference in the Shannon index was observed, with a higher number of observed bacterial species in the CRC group (Fig. S1F, G).

The PCoA revealed significant variation in gut mycobiome composition across the 9 study populations (PERMANOVA R2 = 12.7%, p = 0.001), indicating that cohort effects geographical location, DNA extraction method, and inter-project differences dominate the overall mycobiome variation. Despite that, the disease status of subjects still exerts a partial influence on the overall fungal composition (PERMANOVA R2 = 0.4%, p = 0.001) (Fig. 1B), though its explanatory power Was much lower than that of cohort-related factors. Subsequent cohort-specific analyses demonstrated that CRC status significantly influenced mycobiome composition in nearly all studies, except for the Grion_2023 and Yachida_2019 cohorts (Fig. 1C). Finally, we did a compositional analysis of CRC and CTR groups at the subphylum level. Result consistently identified Saccharomycotina, Pezizomycotina, Mucoromycota, and Basidiomycota as the predominant fungal lineages across all study cohorts, including CRC group and CTR group (Fig. 1D).

CRC-associated fungal signatures and their predictive performance CRC

Wilcoxon rank-sum tests revealed significant differences in fungal abundances between CRC and CTR groups. Notably, 15 fungal species showed predominant directional alterations in at least 3 of 9 independent cohorts (Fig. S2A; Fig. 2A; Table S3). We further quantified effect sizes using log2 fold change (log2FC) and Cohen’s d across individual cohorts (Table S3). Although several taxa exhibited moderate log2FC alterations, the overall Cohen’s d effect sizes Were mostly small consistent with the low biomass and high inter-individual variation of gut fungi. These fungal taxa may suggest potential associations with CRC and could serve as candidate exploratory biomarkers. Among them, Barnettozyma c122 and Saccharomyces cerevisiae c86 were significantly enriched in 5 cohorts, with occasional discordant patterns in individual datasets (Fig. 2B) despite modest overall effect sizes. Specifically, Barnettozyma c122 showed significance in the Yu_2015, Zeller_2014, Gao_2022, Feng_2015, and Liu_2022 studies, being enriched in CRC group except for Feng_2015 (Fig. 2C). Furthermore, Saccharomyces cerevisiae c86 was significant in five cohorts and showed a predominant control-enriched pattern, although one cohort displayed a discordant direction. (Fig. 2D). Meta-analysis further supported the cross-cohort differential patterns of CRC-associated fungi. A total of 16 fungal taxa showed significant differences between CRC patients and healthy controls, including Barnettozyma c122 and Saccharomyces cerevisiae c86. However, I² statistics indicated varying degrees of inter-cohort heterogeneity, supporting the interpretation of these taxa as exploratory candidate signatures rather than universal CRC biomarkers (Table S4). Similarly, Wilcoxon rank-sum tests identified 17 bacterial species showing a consistent trend between the CRC and CTR groups across at least 3 of the 9 independent cohorts (Fig. S2B; Fig. S2C; Table S5).

To evaluate the diagnostic potential of gut mycobiome signatures in CRC, we analyzed 15 characteristic fungi based on CRC and CTR groups, employing LASSO and RF machine learning algorithms for intra-cohort and cross-cohort modeling and validation. Feature importance analysis from RF models identified Schizophyllum c291, Candida albicans c116, and Kazachstania c80 exhibited the highest importance (Fig. 2E). In intra-cohort validation, diagnostic performance varied substantially, with LASSO algorithm achieving AUCs of 0.53 to 0.68 (average = 0.59) and RF algorithm yielding AUCs of 0.57 to 0.82 (average = 0.70). Notably, the Yachida_2019 cohort demonstrated the lowest predictive accuracy in both algorithms, likely attributable to its comparatively modest microbial variation between cases and controls. Cross-cohort validation AUCs ranged from 0.5 to 0.79 (average = 0.6) for LASSO and from 0.55 to 0.76 (average = 0.66) for RF.

To overcome the sample size limitations on single cohorts, we performed a leave-one-cohort-out (LOCO) analysis, in which models were built on 8 cohorts combined and validated on the left-out cohort, for each cohort in turn. The LOCO AUCs ranged from 0.54 to 0.77 (average = 0.65) and from 0.61 to 0.76 (average = 0.68) for 9 cohorts using the LASSO and RF algorithms, respectively (Fig. 2F and G). Taken together, our intra-cohort, cross-cohort, and LOCO analyses indicate that the gut mycobiome signatures have limited ability to distinguish CRC and CTR groups.

Correlation of gut bacteriome and mycobiome

To explore potential co-abundance relationships between CRC-associated fungal and bacterial signatures, we systematically analyzed co-occurrence patterns of 15 fungal species and 17 bacterial species. Spearman correlation analysis revealed that only Pseudopithomyces c302 and (Hungatella hathewayi, Enterocloster bolteae) as well as Kazachstania c80 and (Gemella morbillorum, Peptostreptococcus stomatis) exhibited correlation coefficients greater than 0.3 with q < 0.05. Likewise, when all bacterial were combined, no fungi exhibited correlation coefficients greater than 0.3 (Fig. 3A; Table S6). Furthermore, to further validate these findings, we conducted a SparCC analysis, revealing that only Pseudopithomyces c302 and Barnettozyma c122 showed strong correlations with certain bacteria (Fig. S3; Table S6). Consistently, we found that the fungi diversity parameters of samples were largely inconsistent with their bacterial diversities (Fig. 3B). Together, these results suggest that CRC-associated fungal and bacterial signatures showed limited co-abundance relationships in our dataset. However, these correlation-based analyses cannot establish direct ecological interactions or causal dependencies between bacteria and fungi.

Fungal signatures enhance bacterial-based CRC prediction

To improve diagnostic potential, we developed an integrated biomarker panel incorporating both fungal and bacterial signatures. We first conducted LASSO and RF modeling and testing on 17 bacterial signatures (Fig. 4A and B). In intra-cohort validation, we observed AUCs performances ranging from 0.67 to 0.89 (average = 0.74) for the LASSO algorithm and from 0.7 to 0.9 (average = 0.8) for the RF algorithm. Cross-cohort validation AUCs ranged from 0.61 to 0.91 (average = 0.77) for LASSO and from 0.67 to 0.94 (average = 0.81) for RF. The LOCO AUCs ranged from 0.68 to 0.86 (average = 0.78) and from 0.68 to 0.93 (average = 0.81) for 9 cohorts using the LASSO and RF models, respectively.

(E) Ranking of the mix signatures by importance. We then constructed LASSO and RF models based on combined bacterial and fungal signatures (Fig. 4C and D). After combining bacterial and fungal signatures, both algorithms showed improved AUC scores within the intra-cohort validation, the average AUC for LASSO increased from 0.74 to 0.75, while the average AUC for RF rose from 0.8 to 0.83. In addition, the average values for both algorithms in cross-cohort validation were each improved by 0.1. In the LOCO analysis, the average AUC of the LASSO algorithm did not improve, while the AUCs of the RF algorithm increased from 0.81 to 0.83.

We selected the top 6 fungi and 6 bacteria with the highest enrichment rankings across all projects to form a reduced mixed model. Feature importance analysis from the RF model showed that the top contributors were mainly established CRC-associated bacterial taxa, including Fusobacterium nucleatum, Parvimonas micra, and Peptostreptococcus stomatis (Fig. 4E). Among the fungal features, Barnettozyma c122 and Pseudopithomyces c302 showed relatively higher importance than the other fungi, but their importance values were lower than those of the leading bacterial markers. These results suggest that the reduced mixed model was primarily driven by bacterial signatures, with selected fungal taxa contributing to a lesser extent. In the LOCO algorithm, LASSO results indicated that, compared to the bacterial-only model, the AUCs for the Feng_2015, Xu_2022 and Grion_2023 cohorts remained unchanged in the mixed model, while Liu_2022 cohort showed a slight decrease. In contrast, the AUCs for the other 5 projects significantly increased, with the average AUC rising from 0.75 to 0.76. RF results showed a similar trend, with only the Zeller_2014 cohort maintaining its AUC value unchanged; Liu_2022 cohort showed a slight decrease, while the AUCs for the other 7 cohorts significantly improved in the mixed model, resulting in an average AUC increase from 0.77 to 0.79 (Fig. 4F). Hence, our results demonstrated that a combined fungal and bacterial panel may improve diagnostic performance relative to bacterial-only signatures.

Validation of CRC signatures in external dataset

To validate the diagnostic capability of the selected 12 microbial signatures, we applied the RF and LASSO models trained on the 9 discovery cohorts to 6 independent external validation cohorts, comprising 146 CRC patients and 126 healthy controls. To quantify the additional contribution of fungal features, we calculated the absolute AUC difference between the bacterial-only and bacterial-fungal models. Comprehensive model performance metrics are provided in Table S8.

In the LASSO model, the AUC for the Sadeghi_2024 cohort remained unchanged after adding fungal features, whereas the other 5 cohorts showed improved predictive performance. Overall, the mean AUC increased from 0.71 in the bacterial-only model to 0.75 in the bacterial-fungal model. Similarly, the RF model showed a generally consistent trend. Except for the Hannigan_2018 cohort, which showed a slight decrease, the bacterial-fungal model improved AUCs in the other 5 external validation cohorts. The mean RF AUC increased from 0.722 to 0.762, corresponding to an average ΔAUC of + 0.041. These results suggest that fungal signatures provide a modest but generally positive complementary contribution to bacterial-based CRC prediction models in external validation cohorts (Fig. 5).

We further evaluated the calibration performance of the RF model in the 6 independent external validation cohorts (Fig. S4A–F). In Hannigan_2018 and Thomas_b_2019, the bacterial-only and bacterial-fungal models showed comparable calibration performance with reasonable monotonic trends. In Thomas_a_2019, Vogtmann_2016, Chen_2023, and Sadeghi_2024, the bacterial-fungal model showed a closer correspondence between predicted probabilities and observed CRC proportions across most of the mid-to-high predicted risk range. Discussion

Although a series of studies indicated intestinal dysbacteriosis in CRC patients, most of them focused on the characterization of the gut bacteriome and gut viruses, often neglecting fungi due to their relatively low abundance in the gut microbiota. Furthermore, a single metagenomic data set may not have sufficient sequencing reads and sample size to obtain a comprehensive profile of the enteric microbiota, which similarly affects the study of the relationship between fungi and CRC. By combining results from comparable studies, multicohort analysis could improve statistical power and allow reduction of cohort-specific bias. In this study, we retrieved metagenomic datasets from 9 previous CRC microbiota studies and established an additional small sample cohort for validation to uncover the landscape of fungal microbiota in CRC patients from different populations. Our results suggest an association between CRC and gut fungal dysbiosis.

Consistent with previous findings on the gut mycobiome, the gut mycobiome in CRC patients differs from that of healthy controls. Meanwhile, regarding fungal taxa composition, Saccharomycotina, Pezizomycotina and Mucoromycota were the predominant subphyla, collectively accounting for over 90% of total abundance across groups. We then determined signature fungal species that are correlated to CRC. Among the 15 CRC-associated differential fungi identified, Barnettozyma c122, Pseudopithomyces c302, Schizophyllum c291, Saccharomyces cerevisiae c86, Trichophyton rubrum c61, and Kazachstania c80 were enriched in more than 4 datasets, with Barnettozyma c122 and Saccharomyces cerevisiae c86 being the most enriched across the datasets. We further quantified the biological effect size of key taxa using log2 fold change and Cohen’s d across individual cohorts. Although several taxa exhibited moderate log2FC alterations, Cohen’s d effect sizes were generally small. Such modest effect sizes and cohort discordance are inherent characteristics of the human gut mycobiome, driven by low fungal biomass, high sparsity, geographical divergence, and heterogeneous dietary and lifestyle habits across populations. These factors inevitably lead to variable statistical performance and effect sizes across different cohorts, highlighting the necessity of cross-cohort evaluation and meta-analysis. Our meta-analysis partially consolidated these cross-cohort trends and confirmed that Barnettozyma c122 and Saccharomyces cerevisiae c86 presented consistent associative patterns with CRC. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that these fungal signatures represent exploratory candidate biomarkers with moderate biological effect sizes, rather than universally robust diagnostic signatures with substantial impact. We further explicitly acknowledge that technical batch effects, geographic differences, and inter-project variation are the dominant drivers of gut fungal community variation, explaining far more compositional variance than disease status itself — a common observation in multi-cohort gut mycobiome studies. Additionally, as this study relies on publicly available retrospective datasets, detailed metadata (including dietary habits, medication use, and specific tumor stages) was not uniformly available across all cohorts, which represents an unavoidable limitation related to residual confounding. Previous studies have shown that Saccharomyces cerevisiae c86 enriched in CRC reduces colorectal tumor progression by promoting epithelial cell apoptosis and modulated gut microbial structure and intestinal immunity [50, 51]. Additionally, Liu’s study demonstrates that a Fungi-based acetaldehyde generator was prepared by modifying alcohol dehydrogenase-loaded metal-organic framework nanocarriers onto the surface of Saccharomyces cerevisiae c86, which can target the tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) by mannose, induce tumor cell apoptosis and promote TAMs polarization to anti-tumor phenotype, and further enhance macrophage-mediated immunotherapy by combining anti-CD47 antibody, significantly inhibit the growth of CRC cell [52]. The persistent increase of Barnettozyma c122 in CRC patients suggests its potential pathogenic role. Phylogenetically, Barnettozyma c122 belongs to the order Saccharomycetales, along with Saccharomyces cerevisiae c86, which further supports its possible beneficial function. Saccharomyces cerevisiae c86 contains genes associated with short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) metabolism [53], suggesting that it, along with Barnettozyma c122, may exert immunomodulatory effects like butyrate-producing bacteria, thereby contributing to CRC protection. While our analysis identified 4 key fungi, excluding Saccharomyces cerevisiae c86 and Barnettozyma c122, as significantly altered in CRC patients, direct evidence linking these taxa to colorectal carcinogenesis remains limited. However, existing evidence has linked Barnettozyma c122, Pseudopithomyces c302, Schizophyllum c291, Trichophyton rubrum, and Kazachstania c80 to nonalcoholic fatty liver disease [54], allergic bronchopulmonary mycosis [55], dermatophytosis [56], and ulcerative colitis [57], respectively, suggesting their broader involvement in various pathological states. Their established roles in other diseases suggest potential mechanistic pathways in CRC that are worthy of further exploration.

Additionally, we performed diversity analysis on bacterial communities using the same method. The results revealed significant dysbiosis in the gut bacteriome of CRC patients, highlighting the role of microbial imbalance in the occurrence of colorectal cancer. Among the 17 CRC-associated differential microbial taxa identified, 6 exhibited consistent enrichment across more than 5 independent datasets: Dialister pneumosintes [58], Peptostreptococcus stomatis [59], Fusobacterium nucleatum [60], Parvimonas micra [61], Gemella morbillorum [62], and Clostridium symbiosum [63]. Notably, these species have previously been linked to the development of CRC, further supporting their potential role in its development.

The gut microbiome is a tightly interconnected community of microorganisms that interact to from a complex ecological network. While bacteria and fungi are known to co-exist and dynamically interact in the mammalian gut—either through mutualistic symbiosis or competitive exclusion, their relationship in the context of CRC remains poorly understood. Previous studies have demonstrated extensive bacterial-fungal interactions in various disease states. For instance, in metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease, fungi can influence bacterial colonization patterns, while bacteria, can regulate fungal morphological changes [64]. Similarly, our earlier work revealed significant bacterial-virus interactions in CRC patients [21], suggesting that inter-microbial relationships may contribute to disease pathogenesis.

To explore this further, we systematically analyzed bacterial-fungal co-occurrence patterns and diversity changes in CRC. Interestingly, unlike in other diseases, we observed minimal statistically significant correlations between fungi and bacteria. Spearman analysis revealed that only Pseudopithomyces c302 and Kazachstania c80 exhibited positive correlations with specific bacterial taxa, indicating limited interactions. To elucidate the compositional characteristics of the microbiome data and avoid spurious correlations, SparCC analysis were employed. The results similarly indicated a general absence of strong associations between bacteria and fungi, with few significant correlations detected. Furthermore, project-wise comparisons revealed no consistent trends in bacterial and fungal Shannon index, suggesting that microbial community stability may be differentially regulated in CRC. These findings imply that bacterial and fungal ecological networks in CRC are relatively independent, potentially reflecting distinct disease-specific microbial drivers. It should be noted that these correlation analyses only reflect statistical co-abundance patterns and cannot establish direct ecological interactions or causal relationships between bacteria and fungi. Given the sparse and inconsistent bacterial-fungal correlations observed in our study, our results should not be interpreted as evidence for strong cross-kingdom ecological interactions in CRC. Instead, they suggest that bacterial and fungal signatures may provide partially independent disease-associated information.

Furthermore, previous studies have shown that gut bacteria-based CRC prediction models have demonstrated high diagnostic potential; however, the models are not available in all cohorts [40, 65], implying that the identification of new potential predictors is necessary. In this study, we employed fungal biomarker-based random forest and LASSO models with LOCO validation. Although both models indicated that fungi have a limited capacity to distinguishing CRC patients from healthy controls, combining fungal and bacterial signatures significantly improved model accuracy. The poor predictive performance of fungal-only models may reflect their lower abundance or higher inter-individual variability in the gut compared to bacteria. Alternatively, fungi might play an indirect role in the pathogenesis of CRC, requiring bacterial assistance to manifest detectable signals. Such as, previous studies have shown that fungi can also enhance bacterial predictive efficiency in diseases such as atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease and osteoarthritis [14, 66]. The incremental improvement upon adding fungi suggests that bacteria-only models may overlook critical biological information. Combining fungal and bacterial signatures improved model performance in nearly all cohorts. To further validate these findings, we constructed a model incorporating the 6 most differentially abundant fungal and bacterial taxa. The results demonstrated that fungal features significantly enhanced the predictive performance of bacterial-only signatures, support the exploratory complementary value of cross-kingdom microbial signatures.

The robustness of our findings was further confirmed by external validation in 6 independent cohorts, where fungal-bacterial models showed a tendency to outperform bacterial-only models. This partial reproducibility underscores the generalizability of cross-kingdom microbial signatures for CRC diagnosis, even across populations and sequencing platforms. Moreover, despite sample size limitations potentially affecting the performance of the LASSO model, the bacterial-fungal mixed model demonstrated better calibration than the bacterial-only model in most cohorts, characterized by smoother curves that were closer to the ideal reference line. While previous CRC microbiome studies primarily focused on bacteria, our work demonstrates that incorporating fungi, a long-overlooked component, can optimize predictive models.

Beyond fecal signatures, the spatial distribution of microbes is an emerging theme in CRC research. Recent studies have demonstrated that certain CRC associated microbes, such as Solobacterium moorei, are not only enriched in patient feces but also significantly accumulate within tumor tissues. These intratumoral microbes can directly interact with overexpressed host surface receptors to activate oncogenic signaling cascades like the PI3K-AKT-mTOR pathway, thereby driving tumor cell proliferation [67]. This suggests that the conserved fungal signatures we identified, such as Barnettozyma c122, may similarly participate in this gut-tumor axis and influence host genomic stability or activate specific intracellular pathways. Future studies can integrate multi-omics approaches and experimental validation to confirm the causal role of these candidate fungi in driving host genomic alterations and CRC progression.

Despite the multi-cohort design, external validation, and identification of recurrent CRC-associated fungal signatures, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, our diagnostic models were purely microbe-based. Although the integration of fungal and bacterial signatures modestly improved predictive performance, CRC is a multifactorial disease influenced by host genetics, immune responses, systemic inflammation, and clinical risk factors. Because FIT results, fecal hemoglobin levels, colonoscopy findings, and other standardized clinical screening variables were not uniformly available across the public cohorts analyzed here, we were unable to directly benchmark our microbial models against established CRC screening tools. Future studies integrating microbial signatures with host-derived data, clinical findings, and conventional screening indicators may yield more clinically useful multi-modal diagnostic models. Second, this study was based on publicly available retrospective metagenomic datasets, and we were therefore unable to directly control for contamination during sample collection, DNA extraction, and sequencing. In addition, detailed information on dietary habits, medication use, antibiotic exposure, tumor stage, and other clinical or lifestyle variables was not uniformly available across cohorts. Although we used a standardized bioinformatic pipeline, batch correction, cohort-wise analyses, and external validation to reduce technical and population-specific bias, residual confounding cannot be fully excluded. Third, fungal abundance profiles were generated using genome-length normalization followed by TPM-like rescaling, and we further performed CLR-based Wilcoxon and ALDEx2 sensitivity analyses to assess robustness under compositional data frameworks. These analyses showed generally consistent enrichment directions for the main candidate fungal signatures, although ALDEx2 produced more conservative significance results, likely reflecting the low biomass and high sparsity of gut fungal profiles. Nevertheless, compositional analyses of low-prevalence fungal taxa remain sensitive to zero handling and pseudo-count selection. Therefore, the identified fungal signatures should still be interpreted as exploratory candidate features with generally modest effect sizes, rather than definitive CRC biomarkers. Finally, our findings are primarily based on cross-sectional fecal metagenomic associations. Although taxa such as Barnettozyma c122 and Saccharomyces cerevisiae c86 showed recurrent CRC-associated patterns across multiple cohorts, these associations do not establish causality or confirm mechanistic roles in colorectal carcinogenesis. Moreover, we were unable to integrate host immune profiles, tumor transcriptomic data, tumor tissue microbiome data, or spatial microbial localization. Therefore, the potential links between candidate fungi and CRC-related immune regulation, tumor biology, or gut-tumor interactions remain hypothesis-generating. Future studies combining longitudinal sampling, tissue-level microbiome profiling, host multi-omics data, and experimental models are required to evaluate the functional relevance of these fungal signatures.

Conclusions

Our multi-cohort analysis suggests that CRC is associated with gut fungal dysbiosis, characterized by recurrent enrichment of Barnettozyma c122 and relative depletion of Saccharomyces cerevisiae c86, despite moderate cross-cohort heterogeneity. Fungal-only signatures showed limited standalone predictive capacity and exhibited limited co-abundance relationships with bacterial signatures. However, integrating fungal features with established bacterial biomarkers modestly improved CRC prediction performance, suggesting that gut fungi may provide complementary information for microbiome-based CRC detection. Collectively, these findings highlight the exploratory value of fungal signatures as adjunctive candidate features for refining CRC-associated microbiome models.
