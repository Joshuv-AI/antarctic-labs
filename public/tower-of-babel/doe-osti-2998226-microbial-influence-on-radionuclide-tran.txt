EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

The potential release of radionuclides into the Culebra dolomite formation is modeled
extensively in the Waste Isolation Pilot Plant’s Performance Assessment (WIPP PA) calculations.
Uranium is of chief concern in these modeled scenarios, as it shows the greatest release.
Because of this, a series of experiments was undertaken to determine if microbial activity in the
Culebra formation could transform uranium, either directly or indirectly, to a state that could be
immobilized.

Two WIPP groundwaters were sampled—Sandia National Laboratories Well 10 (SNL-10) and
the Water Quality Sampling Program Well 6 (WQSP-6)—and enriched for iron- and sulfate-
reducing microorganisms. These organisms were then inoculated into eight sample sets: filtered
and amended SNL-10 ± crushed dolomite; simulated and amended SNL-10 ± crushed dolomite;
filtered and amended WQSP-6 ± crushed dolomite; and simulated and amended WQSP-6 ±
crushed dolomite. Acetate was provided as a carbon source and electron donor, and uranyl
chloride was added as a potential electron acceptor. Uranium (U), acetate, and sulfate
concentrations were measured at initial and final time points.

Uranium concentrations decreased in all samples over time by 40-77%. Loss of uranium was
greatest in filtered groundwaters and in WQSP-6. A black precipitate was noted in all samples,
especially in those with dolomite. This solid is hypothesized to be either sulfide from the
reduction of sulfate or uraninite from the reduction of uranium or both. Although sulfate levels
only slightly decreased, the sulfide generated would have been enough to cause the odor that was
noted in all samples and enough to induce uranium reduction. Uraninite could not be verified by
x-ray diffraction (XRD).

Oddly, acetate concentrations increased in samples without dolomite and decreased in all
dolomite samples, including the negative controls. Neither scenario points toward acetate
utilization coupled with sulfate or uranium reduction, and the reason for the changes in acetate
concentration are currently under investigation.

Further analyses are required to confirm uranium oxidation state and/or mineralization and/or
sorption to help determine the actual mechanism of uranium loss from solution and its potential
for resolubilization. Regardless of mechanism, significant amounts of uranium were removed
from both natural and simulated groundwaters in the presence of indigenous groundwater
microorganisms. Should uranium be released into the Culebra, it is highly probable that it will
be immobilized by these organisms, whether via reductive precipitation and/or biomineralization.

1.0 INTRODUCTION
According to calculations for radionuclide transport through the Culebra, 234Uranium is projected
to account for the single largest release, by mass, in the event of an intrusion into the Waste
Isolation Pilot Plant (WIPP) horizon. This occurs via a release point in the westernmost panels
of the repository in full mining scenario simulations (Bethune, 2023). Should uranium be
released into the Culebra, there are many factors that will affect its transport, one of which is
microbial activity.

Many iron-reducing and sulfate-reducing microorganisms are capable of immobilizing oxidized
uranium via reduction to its +4 state and/or incorporation into phosphate and carbonate minerals
(Spear et al., 2000; Abdelouas et al., 2000; Wall and Krumholz, 2006; Newsome et al., 2014).
Whether this is a result of cellular respiration using uranium as an electron acceptor, reduction
without coupled cell growth, indirect reduction of U via reduced iron or sulfide, or merely a
detoxification strategy is not relevant to Culebra transport scenarios, as long as the uranium has
been immobilized. Iron- and sulfate-reducing bacteria have been detected in Culebra
groundwaters via deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) sequencing analysis and isolated through
enrichment incubations (Swanson et al., 2013).

Uranium solubility, speciation, and redox state in natural groundwaters are dependent on the
groundwater composition (especially the presence of complexing ligands, such as carbonate or
sulfate), ionic strength, pH, and reducing capacity (Eh). Culebra groundwaters vary in these
aspects depending on their location around the land withdrawal boundary (Lofton, 2021; WIPP
ASER, 2022). All the groundwaters sampled to date by the Actinide Chemistry team have been
high in sulfate, and because these waters are situated in a fractured dolomite formation,
concentrations of carbonate can also be elevated. Almost all enrichment incubations of these
groundwaters have resulted in biogenic sulfide formation and when iron is added, iron-sulfide
precipitates. Even transitional enrichment incubations, where the atmosphere is sealed while
oxic, result in a sulfide precipitate. This suggests that the organisms present in the Culebra could
also reduce uranium, if not directly, then indirectly via sulfide formation (Lee et al., 2013; Bargar
et al., 2013; Pidchenko et al., 2023).

The experiments described in this report were designed to determine whether Culebra-
indigenous microorganisms are capable of immobilizing uranium through some form of
biotransformation—i.e., reductive precipitation or biomineralization. While biosorption of
uranium onto cell surfaces was not the focus of this work, it is possible that sorption can
contribute to uranium loss from solution and even subsequent mineralization (Kelly et al., 2001;
Bernier-Latmani et al., 2010).

Culebra wells SNL-10 and WQSP-6 were chosen for sampling based on the projected

headspace was left. The water was clear, but a strong sulfide smell was noted during sampling.

Incubations were monitored daily for any changes in appearance, such as turbidity (indicating
possible cell growth), color change (indicating possible iron reduction), and dark particulate
matter (indicating possible sulfide formation from sulfate or iron reduction). When signs of
growth were evident, subcultures were plated onto anoxically prepared marine agar to isolate
organisms from these incubations. Subcultures from the incubations utilizing acetate as the
carbon source/electron donor were maintained over time on anaerobic marine agar until the
uranium experiments could be started.

2.3 Uranium experiments set-up

The compositions of each sampled groundwater were analyzed by inductively-coupled plasma-
mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) and ion chromatography (IC), and from these results, simulated
versions of SNL-10 and WQSP-6 were derived (Appendix 1). Both the simulated groundwaters
and 0.2 micron-filtered groundwaters were used as diluents for the uranium reduction
experiments. These incubations were set up to achieve final concentrations of 10 mM acetate, 10
mM sodium bicarbonate, 0.5 mM uranyl chloride, and growth supplements at 0.1 g/L.
Bicarbonate was added to adjust the pH of the media to match that of the groundwaters, to
account for the presence of dolomite carbonate in the Culebra, and to provide a ligand for
uranium, other than acetate which was intended as the electron donor and carbon source.
Organisms were washed 3-5 times with sterile normal saline (~0.85%) and then inoculated (3%
by volume) into the amended groundwaters.

In general, anaerobic cultures are maintained in glass tubes or serum bottles sealed with butyl
rubber, gas-tight stoppers, and are sampled via needle introduction. Due to safety concerns,
media were dispensed into 30 ml polypropylene bottles sealed within secondary jars. Broth
cultures can maintain anaerobicity in this way.

Samples were withdrawn at two timepoints for analysis: t = 0 and t = final (2 weeks). Both 100
kD-filtered/static and unfiltered/mixed samples were withdrawn and analyzed by ICP-MS for
uranium and by IC for acetate and sulfate. Filtered/static samples were meant to measure
uranium, acetate, and sulfate in solution. Unfiltered/mixed samples were diluted in 2 M nitric
acid to reoxidize any U(IV) and are considered measurements of total uranium (Ganesh et al.,
1997). Microscopic cell counts were performed on mixed, unfiltered samples. Absorbance
spectra for uranium were obtained on the Ultraviolet-Visible (UV-Vis) spectrometer before
inoculation and at the final time point. If solid precipitates were observed, these were collected
for analysis by XRD, if sufficient mass was present.

A subset of experiments was also set up using the same bottles and medium but with 0.5 g of
crushed dolomite added. This was intended to establish if the presence of dolomite had any
effect on uranium disposition, such as sorption, mineralization, or enhanced dissolution.
Uranium, acetate, and sulfate concentrations were not measured at the initial time point. Values
for the samples without dolomite were used to approximate the initial values. At the final time
point for these samples, an aliquot of the solids was removed, dried, ground, and analyzed by
XRD. Cell counts were not monitored for this set due to time constraints and dolomite
interference.

3.1.1 No dolomite samples

Acetate concentrations in the biotic samples without dolomite appeared to increase by 3-5 mM to
concentrations higher than what was originally added to the incubations (Figure 2). Sulfate
concentrations in the biotic samples without dolomite decreased between 4-30% over time (up to
10 mM loss; Figure 3). Because the abiotic concentrations of acetate match the actual targeted
concentration, the biotic measurements are presumed to be valid. The majority of sulfate
measurements fall within the instrument uncertainty (10%), with the exception of SNL-10-A,
SNL-10-Sim-B, and WQSP-6-Sim-B. However, given the distinct odor of sulfide in the
samples, it is believed that these concentrations are also valid. The changes in sulfate
concentration appear minor, but the starting concentrations were also very high, so it is feasible
that these decreases are meaningful.

3.1.2 Dolomite samples

Acetate concentrations in the dolomite samples decreased between 4 to 7 mM in both biotic
samples and negative controls (Figure 2), and sulfate concentrations decreased between 14-27
mM (48-60% change; Figure 3). The dolomite samples also contain more black precipitation,
arguing for sulfate reduction in these samples. However, there appears to be no meaningful
difference between biotic and abiotic final samples in the presence of dolomite, except possibly
in SNL-10 simulated groundwater incubations, so the argument for biotic sulfate reduction is
tenuous. Microscopy was performed on random negative controls after the fact, and no cells
were observed.

3.2.1 No dolomite samples

Uranium concentrations in solution decreased by about 76% in the SNL-10 filtered groundwater
samples and ~55% in the simulated groundwater samples. In contrast, 72-77% of uranium was
lost in all WQSP-6 samples over the course of two weeks (Figure 4).

3.2.2 Dolomite samples

In samples with dolomite, uranium concentrations in solution decreased by about 51% in the
SNL-10 filtered groundwater samples, ~43% in the SNL-10 simulated groundwaters, and 72-
75% in the WQSP-6 samples over the course of two weeks (Figure 4).

The presence of dolomite does not appear to affect uranium removal from WQSP samples. In
contrast, in SNL-10, less uranium was removed from dolomite samples than in samples with no
dolomite: approximately 25% less from the filtered samples and ~12% less from the simulated
groundwater samples.

It was not possible to track cell numbers over time in all samples. WQSP-6 samples (WQSP-6-

4.0 DISCUSSION

In natural groundwaters, uranium mobility is dependent upon the pH and Eh of the groundwaters
and the presence of complexing ligands, either dissolved or on solid surfaces. Possible
interactions of uranium with the groundwaters and simulants in these experiments included: 1)
complexation with dissolved sulfate, carbonate or acetate; 2) complexation with phosphate
released by microorganisms; 3) incorporation into carbonate minerals, such as calcite but also
including dolomite; and 4) sorption onto available surfaces, such as biomass or dolomite, with
possible subsequent mineralization. UV-Vis spectra confirmed U-carbonate complexes in all
initial samples, and these were likely to be in the +6 state. Peaks diminished over time in almost
all samples, as did the uranium concentrations measured by ICP-MS.

It was hypothesized that the iron- and sulfate-reducing microbial cultures would be able to
reduce uranium in the incubations, either directly concomitant with acetate oxidation or
indirectly via sulfate reduction, or alternatively, that they would induce U mineralization, via
phosphate release. Although there are more supporting analyses to be done and anomalies to be
investigated, the results presented herein show uranium removal from solution in the presence of
microorganisms, in either natural or simulated groundwaters, and in the presence or absence of
dolomite. At this time, a definitive mechanism cannot be identified, and it would not be unusual
for there to be interplay between different mechanisms. These are discussed below.

4.1 Bioreduction

Most microbiology studies on uranium reduction consider all uranium precipitated from solution
to be U(IV). However, given the pH and composition of the Culebra groundwaters, especially
the presence of carbonate and calcium, which are thought to inhibit uranium reduction (Brooks et
al., 2003; Wan et al., 2005; Hua et al., 2006), we do not make this assumption.

As is, the data cannot support respiration on uranium as the cause for uranium reductive
precipitation, since there was no apparent utilization of acetate, the electron donor and carbon
source. Although some sulfate was reduced, the data also cannot support respiration by sulfate
reduction at the expense of acetate oxidation. It is possible that the concentration of uranium in
these experiments was toxic, but this was not tested (Francis et al., 2002). Nevertheless, uranium
was removed from solution, and enzymatic U(VI) reduction can occur regardless of whether
organisms are growing and respiring (Lovley and Phillips, 1992; Lovley et al., 1993; Francis et
al., 1994).

In addition to uranium reduction in a non-growth scenario, it is also likely that indirect reduction
of U(VI) occurred as a result of sulfide. Presumably, the sulfide was biotically generated, given
that higher temperatures are required for abiotic sulfidogenesis. However, it is unclear if the
sulfide was generated during these incubations or if it was already present. Both are likely.

To distinguish U(IV) from U(VI), some have used nitric acid to oxidize all uranium and give a
total concentration from which the concentration of U(IV) could be calculated: [U]total = [U(VI)]
+ [U(IV)] (Ganesh et al., 1997). Because of the possibility that uranium sorption onto cells or

Microbial Influence on Radionuclide Transport Through the Culebra Page 21 of 36

dolomite occurred, we do not assume that all uranium lost from solution is U(IV). Operationally,
total uranium was defined as [U]total = [U]in 100 kD filtrate + [U]reduced, precipitated, sorbed and the
contribution of each to the mass balance was determined (Figure 12).

Figure 12 shows that the presence of dolomite inhibits uranium removal from most SNL-10
samples. However, dolomite did not affect uranium behavior in WQSP-6 nearly as much as in
SNL-10, although a slight difference was noted between filtered and simulated WQSP samples.

4.2 Sorption

Uranium sorption onto either cell surfaces or onto dolomite is another possibility for uranium
disposition in these experiments. Dolomite has been screened for its ability to remove numerous
heavy metals, as well as uranium (Irani et al., 2011; Khoshraftar et al., 2023; Yin et al., 2023).
Sorption onto dolomite is a pH-dependent process for most metal cations, with greater removal
at higher pH when the overall charge on the sorbent (i.e., dolomite) is negative. In contrast,
uranium removal was found to decrease with increasing pH, due to the more complex speciation
at slightly alkaline pH and in the presence of carbonates (Diwan et al., 2020). In studies with
dolomite and humic substances, the presence of certain functional groups on fulvic acid was
hypothesized to bridge dolomite and uranium to result in overall removal (Yin et al., 2023).
Nevertheless, Figure 12 shows little effect of dolomite.

Microbial biomass is another possible sorbent for uranium. Uranium can complex with
functional groups on cell surfaces and biosorption has repeatedly been proposed as a remediation
strategy, especially if subsequent mineralization and precipitation occurs (Wall and Krumholz,
2006; Newsome et al., 2014). Although the biomass concentrations were relatively low in these
experiments, it is possible that limited biosorption occurred.

4.3 Induced precipitation and/or mineralization

Uranium can complex with phosphate groups on cell surfaces, providing a nucleation site for
mineral growth and stabilizing the U(IV) precipitates (Boyanov et al., 2011; Bargar et al., 2013).
Alternatively, organisms can release phosphate into the surrounding medium to form a sparingly
soluble complex with uranium and remove it from their environs. This would be especially true
if uranium was exerting a toxic effect on the organisms.

Uranium can exchange with either calcium or magnesium in the dolomite lattice (Steffanowski
and Banning, 2017). However, natural weathering can cause its release again, if the surrounding
medium is undersaturated with respect to uranium. Uranium incorporation into other carbonate
minerals, such as calcite, also occurs under microbial influence. In anoxic groundwaters from
the Äspö underground laboratories, uranium was reduced and incorporated into calcite, when
iron-sulfides were present (Pidchenko et al., 2023).

4.4 Condition-specific mechanisms for uranium removal

Studies have shown that different mechanisms used by different organisms under different
conditions can result in different solubility-controlling uranium phases. These include U(IV)-
phosphate precipitates when media are amended with phosphates, U(IV)-carbonate, uraninite,
nanoparticulate uraninite, amorphous uraninite and non-uraninite U(IV) (Bernier-Latmani et al.,
2010; Boyanov et al., 2011; Bargar et al., 2013; Xie et al., 2018). In some cases, this is a two-
step process—sorption onto biomass or mineral followed by reduction—while in other cases
reduction is direct or indirect in solution (Wersin et al., 1994). Given that the Culebra
groundwaters contain little to no measurable phosphate, phosphate-controlled removal would be
organism-dependent (Lofton, 2021; WIPP ASER, 2023).

For these experiments, we hypothesize different uranium removal mechanisms that depend on
sample conditions, specifically the presence of certain organisms and dolomite. It is not
uncommon for more than one redox pathway to exist in natural systems. However, without
corroborating evidence from advanced analytical techniques, this would be speculative.

In all samples, amino acids were added as nutritional supplements (e.g., found in yeast extract
and casamino acids). Fermentation of certain amino acids can generate acetate, as well as other
products such as hydrogen, H2 (Barker, 1981; Wildenauer and Winter, 1986). However, the
concentrations of these supplements were low and only meant to provide organisms with
required growth factors. Thus, although possible, fermentation seems unlikely in these samples.
Acetate can also be produced lithotrophically with H2/CO2. For this to occur, a source of
molecular hydrogen is needed. Fermentation, as noted above, could provide this source.
Hydrogen can then be used as an electron donor for either sulfate or uranium reduction by sulfate
reducers. This would explain the increase in acetate concentrations in the samples without
dolomite, the lack of significant sulfate reduction (sulfide reduces uranium in turn) and the lack
of significant cell proliferation. Again however, fermentation is an uncertainty.
At best, we can say uranium reduction occurred but was decoupled from growth, or that limited
sulfate reduction (or biogenic sulfide already present in the filtered groundwaters) led to the
indirect reduction of uranium. It is also possible that uranium adsorbed onto cells or that
uranium complexed with acetate sorbed onto dolomite.

4.5 Future work

4.5.1 Determine the mechanism(s) of uranium loss

The goal of this work was to show that microorganisms present in the Culebra could remove
uranium from the groundwater. Although this has been successfully shown, we cannot provide a
definitive mechanism for its removal at this time. Future analyses will help to determine which
of the possible mechanisms discussed in this report is responsible.

Advanced analytical techniques are needed to determine the oxidation state and speciation of
uranium in these samples (e.g., X-ray absorption spectroscopy, extended X-ray absorption fine
structure spectroscopy). These analyses will confirm whether loss from solution was due to
reductive precipitation, whether direct, indirect, or abiotic. Further, scanning electron
microscopy with energy-dispersive X-ray diffraction (SEM-EDS) analyses can help determine if
uranium is localized at cell surfaces or with precipitated solids. Finally, genomic analyses will
be conducted from which metabolic pathways can sometimes be inferred.

4.5.2 Potential for reoxidation of uranium

Reoxidation and remobilization of uranium is a significant problem at contaminated sites,
especially when other oxidants, such as oxygen or nitrates, are present and when high carbonate
concentrations stabilize the mobile U(VI) oxidation state (Wan et al., 2005). If uranium is
complexed with phosphate, this is less of an issue. Some have argued that the presence of sulfate

Microbial Influence on Radionuclide Transport Through the Culebra Page 25 of 36

reducers and biogenic iron-sulfide minerals, such as mackinawite, can lead to uranium reduction
but can also lead to its release into solution again (Lee et al., 2013). In contrast, more studies
have shown that the presence of mackinawite not only promotes the formation of uraninite but
also helps stabilize it by scavenging oxygen and providing a physical barrier to reoxidation (Bi et
al., 2013; Bargar et al., 2013). The Äspö study confirmed U(IV) stabilization and
immobilization and additionally showed that even very low micromolar concentrations of iron-
sulfides prevented uranium reoxidation (Pidchenko et al., 2023).

The Culebra groundwaters have little to no measurable nitrates (Lofton, 2021; WIPP ASER,
2023), suggesting that uranium reoxidation coupled to nitrate reduction should not occur. Iron
concentrations are higher in these tested groundwaters than in the Äspö waters, suggesting that
reduced uranium should be stable. Phosphate control on solid phase uranium will depend on
microbial activity.
