Beyond survival: empowering adults with congenital heart disease through communication and co-designed education
Authors: Hajar Habibi, Margarita Brida, Michael A Gatzoulis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdults with congenital heart disease (ACHD) live with lifelong clinical uncertainty, recurrent interventions, and complex decision-making demands. As survival improves, optimal ACHD care increasingly requires approaches that extend beyond biomedical management to support patient empowerment and engagement. This review examines contemporary strategies to support patient empowerment and engagement in ACHD, with a particular focus on communication-based interventions, nurse-led education, and co-designed tools that facilitate shared decision-making. We synthesise contemporary evidence alongside clinical experience from specialist ACHD services. Key approaches include structured communication strategies such as agenda-setting, teach-back, and Question Prompt Lists (QPLs), including the “Ask Me Anything” (AMA) digital tool developed within a tertiary ACHD centre. These interventions may improve patient confidence, facilitate question-asking behaviour, and support more effective consultations. Nurse-led education programmes, transition-focused interventions, and routine psychosocial screening are discussed as integral components of an empowerment framework, particularly during periods of heightened vulnerability such as adolescence, pregnancy, and progression to heart failure. The role of patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) is also considered, not only as outcome assessment tools but also as mechanisms to facilitate structured communication and shared reflection between patients and clinicians. Integrating educational, communicative, and psychosocial strategies within multidisciplinary ACHD care may strengthen continuity of care, improve patient engagement, and support shared decision-making across the lifespan.Keywords: Adult congenital heart disease (adult CHD), patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs), shared decision-making, patient empowerment, nurse-led education

Introduction

Advances in paediatric cardiology and cardiac surgery have transformed congenital heart disease (CHD) from a condition historically associated with high childhood mortality into a lifelong chronic illness, with over 97% of affected individuals in developed countries now surviving into adulthood (1).

As a result, the global population of adults with congenital heart disease (ACHD) is growing and ageing, with increasing clinical complexity related to heart failure, arrhythmias, reproductive counselling, pregnancy-related risk assessment re-intervention, and psychosocial burden (2). While improvements in survival represent a major success, they have also shifted the focus of care from mortality alone to long-term quality of life, patient engagement, and partnership with healthcare professionals.

ACHD care differs from many acquired cardiovascular conditions because patients frequently require lifelong specialist follow-up, repeated interventions, and complex decision-making across different stages of life. Many patients also experience ongoing uncertainty regarding prognosis, future procedures, self-management and long-term functional status within complex healthcare systems (3).

ACHD care is characterised by recurrent decision points across the lifespan, often involving uncertainty, trade-offs, and evolving patient priorities. These decisions are embedded within complex health systems and require sustained interaction between patients and multidisciplinary teams (4). Therefore, traditional clinician-led models of care are increasingly recognised as insufficient to meet the needs of patients living with a lifelong condition. Contemporary ACHD services therefore require structured approaches that support patients as active participants in their care through communication-based education, psychosocial support, and shared decision-making (5).

Structured patient education and engagement strategies are increasingly recognised as core components of patient-centred cardiovascular care and align with international priorities for value-based healthcare delivery (6). For ACHD populations, these principles are particularly relevant given the need for long-term self-management, adherence, and informed participation in complex care pathways. This review explores communication-based and nurse-led strategies that support patient engagement in ACHD care, with a particular focus on structured communication techniques, co-designed educational tools, transition support, and integrated psychosocial approaches that facilitate decision-making in routine clinical practice (7).

Unlike acquired cardiovascular disease, ACHD care is characterised by lifelong specialist follow-up, recurrent interventions, and developmental transitions occurring across adolescence and adulthood. These evolving care needs require engagement strategies that remain adaptable to changing clinical status, psychosocial priorities, and repeated decision-making across the lifespan (8).

Conceptual framework: empowerment and engagement in ACHD

Patient empowerment is a multifaceted concept that extends beyond information provision or education alone. It encompasses the ability of individuals to understand their condition, reflect on their values and priorities, communicate effectively with healthcare professionals, and participate meaningfully in decisions about their care (7,9). In long-term conditions such as ACHD, empowerment is not a static state but a dynamic process that evolves across the lifespan and in response to changing clinical and personal circumstances (2,10).

ACHD patients frequently remain under lifelong specialist follow-up, requiring repeated adaptation to changing clinical status, interventions, and personal priorities over time (11).

Engagement refers to the observable behaviours and interactions that arise from empowerment, including question-asking, shared goal setting, and active participation in consultations. Importantly, empowerment and engagement are relational rather than solely individual attributes. They are shaped by the quality of communication, continuity of care, trust, and the extent to which healthcare systems create space for patient voice and partnership (12).

ACHD patients present unique challenges to empowerment. Patients often acquire medical knowledge from an early age, yet may experience uncertainty, anxiety, and decisional fatigue due to repeated interventions and prognostic ambiguity (13).

Transition from paediatric to adult services, reproductive counselling, pregnancy-related decision-making, and progression to heart failure represent periods of heightened vulnerability where patient engagement may be disrupted if communication and support are inadequate (14).

Transition from paediatric to ACHD services represents a particularly vulnerable period in long-term ACHD care. During transition, patients are expected to assume increasing responsibility for understanding their condition, attending appointments, communicating with healthcare professionals, and navigating complex healthcare systems. However, interruption of follow-up remains common in ACHD populations and may contribute to poorer engagement with specialist care. Structured transition programmes, communication-based education, and sustained multidisciplinary support are therefore essential components of empowerment-oriented ACHD care (8,15).

Within this context, nurses play a pivotal role in operationalising empowerment through relational continuity, education, and advocacy. Nurse-led models are well positioned to integrate empowerment into routine practice by reinforcing understanding and facilitating dialogue within multidisciplinary teams.

Framing empowerment as an embedded clinical process rather than a discrete intervention provides a practical foundation for implementing structured engagement strategies within ACHD services (14).

Question Prompt Lists (QPLs) and structured communication tools

QPLs are structured sets of questions designed to help patients identify and raise issues they may otherwise hesitate to ask. In oncology and chronic disease settings, QPLs have been shown to improve patient participation, increase question-asking, and enhance satisfaction without increasing consultation length (16).

Recent work by Habibi et al. extended the application of QPLs to cardiology by evaluating a co-designed digital QPL, the “Ask Me Anything” (AMA) tool, in young people and adults with CHD. The AMA tool was developed in partnership with patients to reflect real-world concerns, including prognosis, future interventions, mental health, lifestyle, fertility, and long-term planning. Early evaluation demonstrated high acceptability, strong patient engagement, and improved confidence in preparing for consultations (17).

Longitudinal qualitative evidence indicated that QPLs may enhance confidence and normalize question-asking behaviour (16). Similarly, patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) are increasingly recognised as tools that support care planning and patient-clinician dialogue rather than functioning solely as measurement instruments (18). Patient engagement in ACHD care is shaped by continuity of care and the broader healthcare environment.

Nurse-led education and empowerment models in ACHD

Nurses play a central role in implementing strategies that support patient empowerment within ACHD services. Through sustained patient contact, continuity across care transitions, and expertise in both clinical and psychosocial domains, specialist nurses are uniquely positioned to integrate education, engagement, and support into routine care. Nurse-led education in ACHD extends beyond information delivery to encompass coaching, advocacy, and facilitation of shared decision-making across the lifespan (4).

In specialist ACHD centres, nurse-led models often provide the relational continuity that enables patients to develop confidence, articulate concerns, and navigate complex care pathways. Education is often delivered longitudinally, and embedded throughout care, supporting patients at key milestones such as transition from paediatric to adult services, pregnancy counselling, heart failure progression, and consideration of advanced therapies (2). This may be particularly important during transition to adult care, where interruption of medical follow-up remains a recognised challenge in ACHD populations. Structured transition education, transition readiness assessment, and communication strategies that support health literacy and self-management may help reduce disengagement from specialist follow-up and improve long-term continuity of care (19). This longitudinal approach recognises that informational and psychosocial needs evolve over time and require ongoing reinforcement through clinical support and communication (20).

Nurse-led education also supports self-management by addressing medication understanding, symptom monitoring, lifestyle adaptation, and psychosocial coping. These educational interactions often occur outside formal consultations, through follow-up contacts, virtual clinics, or structured education sessions, complementing medical decision-making. Evidence from chronic cardiovascular care suggests that nurse-led interventions can improve patient confidence, adherence, and satisfaction with care (21).

Beyond individual patient encounters, nurses contribute to empowerment at a system level by shaping care pathways, developing educational resources, and supporting multidisciplinary collaboration. Their role in bridging biomedical information and patient experience allows complex clinical concepts to be discussed in a more meaningful and understandable way for patients. This relational and interpretive function is increasingly recognised as a core component of high-quality ACHD care (4).

While nurse-led education models are well established in ACHD, their principles are transferable to other long-term conditions characterised by uncertainty and complex self-management demands. Embedding education within everyday clinical interactions, rather than relying solely on discrete educational programmes, offers a sustainable and adaptable approach across diverse healthcare settings. In ACHD services, such models may contribute to improved preparedness for interventions, enhanced adherence to surveillance, and more effective shared decision-making in complex clinical contexts.

Psychological support and mental health integration

Psychological challenges including anxiety, depression, and illness-related uncertainty, are highly prevalent in CHD and may directly affect engagement with care. International data show that approximately one-third of ACHD patients report clinically elevated symptoms of anxiety and/or depression, which are associated with poorer quality of life and health status (22). Integrating routine psychological screening and access to psychosocial support is therefore essential to empowerment. Embedding mental health assessment within routine cardiology visits, supported by clinical nurse specialists and psychologists, can normalise emotional discussion, reduce stigma, and foster earlier identification and referral for care (23).

PROMs as engagement and reflection tools

PROMs are increasingly used in cardiology to assess quality of life and symptom burden. Emerging evidence suggests that PROMs may also act as engagement tools by prompting reflection, surfacing unspoken concerns, and guiding conversations during consultations (23).

For ACHD patients, PROMs developed through patient co-design may be particularly effective in supporting empowerment, as they capture the concerns and priorities that matter most to patients rather than relying solely on clinician-defined measures (17). Integrating PROMs into routine clinical practice therefore offers a practical means of enhancing patient engagement, reflection, and shared decision-making.

Discussion: toward integrated empowerment in long-term ACHD care

This review emphasises that structured interventions and care strategies that support patient empowerment, engagement and shared decision-making should be embedded into routine ACHD care rather than treated as optional additions. Communication strategies, co-designed tools, and nurse-led models support participation in care across the lifespan, while reflecting the lived experience of ACHD population (23).

A key insight from both research and clinical practice is that empowerment is relational and context-dependent shaped by patients’ emotional engagement and the quality of professional support (26). Patients’ capacity to engage fluctuates in response to clinical status, life stage, and psychosocial stressors (26). Structured communication tools such as agenda-setting, teach-back, and QPLs help normalise uncertainty and legitimise patient concerns, enabling clinicians to respond more effectively. When combined with continuity of care and nurse-led education, these approaches strengthen therapeutic partnerships and promote more meaningful consultations (25,27).

Although this review draws primarily on ACHD experience, the principles described are relevant to other long-term conditions requiring sustained self-management and patient-clinician collaboration.

Educational and engagement strategies must be practical and adaptable across healthcare system, with tools such as QPLs and structured communication techniques supporting participation and shared decision-making even in setting with limited specialist access (25,27).

Emerging evidence suggests that PROMs can complement communication strategies by prompting reflection and dialogue during consultations. When used thoughtfully, PROMs may help patients identify priorities, articulate concerns, and participate more actively in care decisions. This aligns with broader shifts toward person-centred and value-based healthcare (8,26). Future research should evaluate how empowerment strategies influence long-term outcomes, clinician experience, and service delivery across diverse ACHD populations. Empowerment should not be viewed as an additional burden on services, but as a core component of high quality, sustainable care. Embedding empowerment within ACHD service design reflects the evolving needs of a growing and ageing congenital population.

Implementing empowerment-oriented ACHD care may present practical challenges, including limited clinical time, variation in access to specialist psychology services, and differences in transition resources across healthcare systems. Sustained implementation therefore requires organisational support, multidisciplinary collaboration, and staff training in communication and psychosocial care (8,25).

Implications for clinical practice

Empowerment strategies should be systematically integrated into ACHD services rather than delivered in an ad hoc manner. Achieving this requires clear organisational commitment, staff training in communication techniques, and formal recognition of the central role of specialist nursing leadership (25). Patient co-design should be considered a methodological and practical strength, ensuring that educational tools, PROMs, and QPLs reflect real patient priorities and support meaningful engagement in care (28).

Future directions

Future research should evaluate the long-term impact of empowerment- focused interventions on clinical outcomes, adherence, psychological wellbeing, and healthcare utilisation. In addition, studies should examine patient-reported outcomes and clinician experience to better understand how empowerment influences consultation quality and shared decision-making (23).

Digital platforms offer opportunities for scalable and flexible patient education, however, their development must prioritise equity, accessibility, and alignment with patient preferences. Implementation research is also needed to determine how digital and communication-based interventions can be sustainably embedded within routine ACHD services (25).

Conclusions

Empowering young people and adult with CHD requires intentional, structured approaches to communication, education, and psychosocial support. Evidence from QPLs, nurse-led programmes, co-designed digital tools, and PROMs demonstrates that patients can be supported to participate actively in care when given the appropriate frameworks. Integrating these strategies into routine cardiology practice represents an important step toward person-centred care, strengthening patient confidence and shared decision-making, with the potential to improve long-term engagement, continuity of care, and patient experience.
