Accomplishments
Accomplishments: What was done? What was learned? (Response for public)
This project used neutron scattering as a unique tool to investigate magnetic chiral structures. Neutron
scattering is a powerful technique in which the neutron wavepacket scatters from nuclear or magnetic
structures, providing insight into the structure of a material and its magnetic features. In this work, we
focused on chiral magnetic structures. Most magnetic materials are colinear ferro- or antiferromagnets,
where the spin moments align parallel or anti-parallel with their neighbors. In some materials, the magnetic
moments instead curl and can form closed loops. These structures, with the additional feature that the core
and perimeter are oriented in opposite out-of-plane directions, exhibit a property called topology; these
structures are known as skyrmions. Topology is a broadly used term, but here it refers to a magnetic
configuration that cannot be created or destroyed through any continuous transformation. For these looped
structures, the closed loop cannot be destroyed continuously, giving rise to unique properties such as
collective dynamics and particle-like behavior. This also raises fundamental questions about how such
structures form and evolve.
 Few techniques can resolve nanoscale magnetic features, especially within a material, making
skyrmion research challenging. Neutrons provide a unique solution. Neutrons were the first tool used to
identify magnetic skyrmions and remain a gold standard for their study. We demonstrated that neutrons can
be used to investigate skyrmions in [Gd/Fe] multilayers; this is the only clear example of magnetic
skyrmions in thin films measured with neutrons. Most neutron studies have focused on bulk materials
because neutrons are weakly interacting and require large sample volumes to achieve adequate signal.
Furthermore, most studies have used neutrons in relatively simple ways: transmission SANS geometry,
looking for diffraction patterns associated with ordered skyrmion lattices. Neutron scattering can reveal
much more about these systems, but the techniques must be extended - or in some cases developed - to
extract this information. This was the purpose of the current research.
 As a first step beyond steady-state measurements, we asked how skyrmions form and order into a
lattice. An ordered skyrmion lattice produces a characteristic hexagonal diffraction pattern, corresponding
to the Fourier transform of the real-space structure. By rapidly stepping the magnetic field out of and back
into the skyrmion stability region, we measured how this pattern evolved in time. This allowed us to
determine how the lattice forms. We found that skyrmions emerge as ordered domains with a distribution
of sizes and orientations, which then merge and equilibrate. This process occurs over «60 ms, much slower
than typical magnetic dynamics (nanoseconds), leading us to describe it as kinetics rather than dynamics.
These results were published in Nanoscale 16, 10715 (2024).
 We next investigated the three-dimensional structure of skyrmions in Gd/Fe multilayers. The
topology is a direct result of the spin configuration, making it critical to precisely determine, but measuring
that configuration is challenging because it is embedded within the material. The conventional picture
stacks the looped structure to form a cylindrical skyrmion tube. We proposed that near the surface, dipolar
fields reorient the moments from azimuthal (Bloch-like) to radial (Neel-like) wrapping. Using GISANS,
we measured diffraction patterns that encode both in-plane and out-of-plane structure. By combining these
data with micromagnetic modeling, we converged on a structure that is grounded in the physics of
magnetism and reproduces the observed scattering. The results show a continuous rotation of the magnetic
moment along the skyrmion length and variation in the wrapping width with depth. These features
collectively define the topology. These results were published in Physical Review B 107, 184412 (2023).
 The third major experiment used ferromagnetic resonance to excite magnetic dynamics while
simultaneously measuring the skyrmion lattice with in operando SANS. The initial expectation was that

stochastic variation in the lattice would be accentuated during dynamic gyration and broaden the diffraction
pattern. Instead, we observed a large increase in low-angle (low-q) scattering at resonance and no change
to the diffraction from the lattice. The low-q signal was consistent with a fractal-like structure arising from
interference of spin waves generated during excitation. From this, we extracted characteristic length scales
and a fractal dimension, providing a new view of dynamic magnetic structure. These results were published
in Advanced Materials 2300416 (2023).
 We later attempted this experiment in a single crystal of Cu2OSeO3 and observed no dynamic signal
in the scattering. This can be understood from the underlying physics: in Cu2OSeO3, skyrmions are set by
the DMI, which enforces a uniform handedness. As a result, all skyrmions have the same chirality, gyrate
with the same phase and frequency, and in the same direction, and thus generate identical spin wave
emissions. The resulting spin-wave interference is thus periodic across the lattice. This produces a
conventional diffraction feature at the same q-position as the skyrmion lattice, rather than the distributed
signal observed in Fe/Gd. The absence of signal therefore reflects a fundamental difference in the role of
stochasticity between these systems.
 We also investigated coupling between magnetic skyrmions and superconducting vortices in
[Gd/Fe]/Nb heterostructures. Transport measurements showed no evidence of commensurate locking. This
suggests that coupling requires precise matching of skyrmion and vortex densities and may be limited by
interfacial competition between superconducting and magnetic order.
 Near the end of the project, we developed the capability to grow MnSi thin films, an archetypal
DMI skyrmion system. Using MnSi/Nb bilayers, we re-attempted this experiment and observed coupling
in the magnetometry measurements, which will be reported in an upcoming publication.
 In addition to these results, this work produced 25 publications and 23 conference presentations.
Two graduate students earned their Ph.D.s during the course of this grant, both of which are part of the U.S.
industrial workforce at technology-focused Fortune 500 companies in roles directly aligned with their
doctoral research.
 A challenge we faced was limited access to neutron beamtime. Experiments are conducted at
national user facilities through competitive proposals, and extended downtime at the NIST reactor led to
oversubscription at other U.S. sources. The reality is there is currently only one instrument in the western
hemisphere which could support our research. This resulted in delays between experiments and required
prioritization of key measurements. Despite this, we were able to attempt all of the proposed experiments,
achieve meaningful, publishable results on several of the projects, and graduate two Ph.D. students.
 Beyond these specific results, this work established new neutron scattering methodologies for
probing non-equilibrium and dynamic magnetic textures, extending the capability of SANS and
reflectometry beyond conventional static measurements. These approaches are broadly applicable to other
mesoscale and quantum materials systems where buried structure and dynamics are otherwise inaccessible
and strengthen the role of neutron scattering as a unique probe of emergent magnetic phenomena.
What are the major goals and objectives of the project? (Response for DOE)
We proposed a series of experiments spanning multiple systems and measurement modalities, including:
(1) determination of the three-dimensional structure using GISANS, (2) measurement of skyrmion depth
profiles in hybrid, B20, and spin-orbit coupled systems, (3) investigation of lattice formation and ordering
using time-resolved SANS (TR-SANS), (4) probing dynamic structure using FMR-SANS, (5) exploration
of charge-current-driven skyrmion motion and Doppler-shift SANS, (6) comparative studies of dynamics
in DMl-based systems such as CmOSeOs, (7) investigation of coupling between skyrmions and

superconducting vortices, and (8) exploratory efforts toward imaging and characterization of skyrmions in
antiferromagnetic and related systems.
 We carried out each of these experimental directions, with clear success in (1), (3), (4), (6), and (7),
resulting in publications or ongoing manuscripts. Experiments (5) and (8) were attempted but did not yield
measurable signals in our systems and were subsequently deprioritized, although related approaches have
since been demonstrated by other groups. Experiment (2) was delayed as we developed MnSi thin film
growth techniques, but we are currently planning to perform this work. In the course of this work, we
developed new capabilities within our lab—such as growth of B20 MnSi thin films—extended existing
neutron techniques into new regimes, including GISANS and TR-SANS, and developed new experimental
approaches such as FMR-SANS.

What was accomplished under these goals? (Response for DOE)
We successfully carried out the proposed experimental program, with clear success in (1), (3), (4), (6), and
(7), developing new understanding of the three-dimensional structure of magnetic skyrmions, the kinetics
of skyrmion lattice formation, and their dynamic behavior under excitation. In (6) we gained clear insight
into the underlying physics of our system, while (7) provided insight into vortex coupling. In performing
these experiments, we advanced neutron scattering techniques including GISANS and time-resolved SANS
(TR-SANS) into new regimes for magnetic systems, and developed new experimental approaches such as
FMR-SANS to directly couple magnetic excitation with structural probes.
 Experiments (5) and (8) were attempted but did not yield measurable signals in our systems, and
were subsequently deprioritized. These outcomes informed our understanding of the limits of our
approaches. Collectively, these advances significantly expand the capability of neutron scattering to study
non-equilibrium and driven magnetic systems.

What opportunities for training and professional development has the project provided?
This project supported two Ph.D. students throughout most of their time in our lab. They were trained in
thin-film growth and characterization techniques, including sputtering, magnetometry, transport, X-ray
diffraction, electron microscopy, neutron diffraction, and micromagnetic simulations. In carrying out these
experiments, they also developed complementary skills in Python and LabVIEW programming, CAD
drafting, and PCB design, as well as more broadly applicable skills such as technical writing and
presentation development.
 These experiences prepared the students to enter the U.S. industrial workforce in roles closely
aligned with their doctoral research. One is now a research staff member at Western Digital in San Jose,
developing hard drive materials. The other worked at First Solar on thin-film photovoltaic growth by
sputtering and now works at Intel in Portland, advancing nanofabrication processes.
 Trainees also gained experience as users at national neutron facilities (HFIR, SNS, NCNR),
including proposal writing, beamtime execution, and large-scale data analysis.

Technologies or Techniques
 1 Ferromagnetic Resonance SANS (FMR-SANS): This entirely new technique uses
 ferromagnetic resonance - a magnetic spectroscopy technique - to excite magnetic dynamics.
 During this excitation, spin waves are generated, encoding the fundamental energy modes in
 the system. By simultaneously performing SANS, we capture the collective structure of these
 dynamic modes, providing a first-of-its-kind insight into the structure of these dynamic
 magnetic structures. We developed custom sample holders and ex situ instrumentation to
 perform these measurements, as well as appreciable work to interpret the resulting scattering.
 2 Time-Resolved SANS (TRSANS): This technique attaches a time-stamp to each counted
 neutron, then uses a lock-in type cycling to develop a time-resolved diffraction pattern that
 follows an evolving structure. This is very new, especially to the condensed matter community,
 where many of the dynamics are much faster than SANS is able to resolve. We were among the
 first groups to use this technique at NIST (on NG7SANS) and the first to my knowledge at

 GPSANS (ORNL). We developed custom sample holders and ex-situ equipment for these
 measurements and worked with ORNL software personnel to perform the post-experiment
 binning.
 3 Grazing-Incidence SANS (GISANS): This technique illuminates a thin-film sample at grazing
 incidence, establishing a reflected beam that encodes features along the film thickness, as-well
 as off-specular features resulting from the in-plane structure. This is complementary to off-
 specular neutron reflectometry in many ways, but unique in its ability to resolve low-q
 structures, which is the core strength of SANS. Using this, we measured, for the first time, the
 3D structure of magnetic skyrmions in thin films. We developed an analysis sequence based
 which couples the neutron results to micromagnetic simulations.
 4 Ferromagnetic Resonance Spectrometer (New to our lab, fairly unique): Ferromagnetic
 resonance spectroscopy uses microwave excitation to drive magnetic dynamics. Using a
 nanostripline, the absorption of these microwaves can be detected allowing the dynamic modes
 to be mapped versus frequency and field. We already had the major pieces of equipment (the
 network analyzer and the cryogenic magnet), but now we have integrated them to perform
 magnetic spectroscopy across a wide range of temperatures and magnetic fields (0-9T, 2K-
 300K).

Impact
Impact: What is the impact of the project? How has it contributed? (Response for public)
This project advanced our ability to study complex magnetic materials by developing new neutron
scattering methods that can observe how magnetic structures form, evolve, and respond to external stimuli.
These capabilities enable scientists to probe buried structures and dynamic processes that are not accessible
with conventional techniques. The results improve our understanding of topological magnetic systems
being explored for next-generation information technologies. In addition, the project trained graduate
researchers who have transitioned into the U.S. technology workforce, contributing to national
competitiveness in advanced materials and manufacturing.

What is the impact on the development of the principal discipline(s) of the project?
This work advances the field of nanomagnetism and neutron scattering by enabling direct measurement of
dynamic and depth-resolved magnetic textures, addressing long-standing limitations in probing buried and
non-equilibrium spin structures. These measurements provide new insight into the formation, stability, and
dynamic behavior of skyrmions, including the role of domain evolution, stochasticity, and excitation-driven
structure in determining their properties. The methodologies developed here extend neutron scattering from
primarily static structural measurements to dynamic and driven systems, with broad applicability to
emergent magnetic and quantum materials.

What is the impact on other disciplines?
The development of TR-SANS and GISANS is directly translatable to other programs in the neutron
community, including soft matter (e.g., polymers), energy materials such as batteries, and con-elated
electron systems. These techniques enable time-resolved and depth-sensitive measurements of mesoscale
structure and dynamics, which are central challenges across a wide range of materials classes. In soft matter,
they provide new access to non-equilibrium processes such as self-assembly and deformation; in energy
materials, they offer pathways to probe ion transport, phase evolution, and interfacial structure during
operation. More broadly, these approaches extend neutron scattering into dynamic and driven regimes,
providing new experimental capabilities that can impact fields well beyond magnetism.

What is the impact on the development of human resources?
The impact on human resources is most evident in the two PhD students that this program supported. Each
of these graduates have entered the industrial workforce, working at high-technology, research-driven US¬
based corporations (Western Digital and Intel) where the directly apply the skills learned during their PhD
work to advancing domestic technology leadership. The program has also supported a handful of
undergraduate students who are at a variety of places at this time, including PhD programs of their own.
While developing the measurement and analysis techniques for GISANS, TRSANS, and FMR-SANS we
have also enhanced the research capabilities of the instrument scientists we worked with, allowing them to
engage and expand the neutron users program.

What is the impact on physical, institutional, and information resources that form infrastructure?
The techniques developed in the course of this work (GISANS, TRSANS, and FMR-SANS) are now
demonstrated for magnetic thin-film systems. Through that process we developed custom measurement

configuration, sample holders, measurement procedures, and analysis and interpretation approaches which
are now made avalible to other users with other systems, guided by our publications and inspired by our
presentations. We are committed to assisting future users in these endeavors through collaborative support,
if they solicit us. Furthermore, these approaches are also enshrined in our instrument scientist collaborators
who have a much closer connection with future users and so are able to better support them.

What is the impact on technology transfer?
We did not generate any technology from this work, however it has led to engagement with a small company
proposing to develop skyrmion logic devices.

What is the impact on society beyond science and technology?
This work contributes to society through workforce development and long-term advances in materials
understanding. The project trained graduate researchers who have transitioned into the U.S. technology
workforce, supporting industries in electronics and advanced manufacturing. The results also contribute to
the broader knowledge base underlying future energy-efficient information technologies and strengthen
U.S. capabilities in neutron scattering and materials research.

 Changes
 Changes in approach and reasons for change
 The primary objectives were completed as proposed. In parallel, new experimental directions (e.g., FMR-
 SANS) emerged that were not part of the original proposal but significantly expanded the scope and impact
 of the work. Several additional proposed directions evolved based on experimental feasibility, beamtime
 constraints, and emerging results:
(1) The skyrmion-superconductor in [Gd/Fe]/Nb coupling experiment did not yield evidence of commensurate
 locking, likely due to mismatch in lattice densities or interfacial competition.
(2) Work on B20 thin-film skyrmions was successfully initiated, including development of MnSi thin-film
 growth. However, this effort occurred late in the project and was limited by neutron beamtime availability,
 preventing full execution of the planned SANS and GISANS measurements. We have since performed
 neutron work, observed significant scattering which we are currently analyzing and will be published in the
 near future.
(3) The proposed studies of spin-orbit coupled heterostructures were not completed. Tn practice, achieving
 sufficient skyrmion ordering and neutron scattering signal in these systems proved more challenging than
 anticipated, and effort was instead focused on systems with demonstrated long-range order.
(4) Charge-current-driven skyrmion motion and Doppler-shift SANS measurements were attempted, but no
 appreciable signal was observed. With the limited availability of neutron time due to the oversubscription,
 we chose to not repeat this experiment. Subsequent work using time-resolved SANS methodologies
 demonstrated this experiment was in-fact plausible (Phys. Rev. B 1 10, 014431 (2024)).
(5) Dynamic SANS measurements in CmOSeOs were also explored but did not show a measurable signal.
 These results suggest that the fractal-like spin-wave networks observed in Fe/Gd arise from stochastic
 variations in skyrmion wrapping and gyration - made random by the lack of DMI in the FeGd system. In
 the more ordered DMI system (Cu^OSeOj) the stochasticity is suppressed because the DMI sets the
 orientation of each skyrmion, and the spin-wave response is thus identical unit-cell by unit-cell, appearing
 as a conventional diffraction feature at the same q-position as the lattice.
(6) Efforts to image antiferromagnetic skyrmions using PEEM were not successful. We attempted this three
 time at the Advanced Light Source without success. In hybrid systems, interdiffusion (e.g., Mn from the
 AFM into Gd/Fe) destroyed the skyrmion ordering. Alternative oxide-based AFMs reacted with the Gd also
 destroying the skyrmion phase, preventing formation of a stable structure suitable for imaging. We tried
 using a Pt break-layer, but this decoupled the films and we saw no ordering. Another group demonstrated
 our approach during the second year of our project (Applied Physics Letters 119, 192407 (2021)) and so
 this work was deprioritized.
(7) Complementary SAXS scattering measurements were performed at the COSMIC beamline at the Advanced
 Light Source, late in the project. Dynamic scattering broke one of their detectors, by inadvertently
 amplifying the broadcasted microwaves. We were able to resolve the hexagonal diffraction pattern and saw
 a collapse of the skyrmion phase under strong dynamic excitation. We are currently scheduled to look at
 MnSi thin films with COSMIC. These results will also be published in the near future.

 Actual or anticipated problems or delays and actions or plans to resolve them
 The primary challenge encountered during this project was limited access to neutron beamtime, particularly
 during extended outages at NIST and oversubscription at other U.S. neutron facilities. This resulted in
 delays between experiments and required prioritization of the most promising systems and measurement
 approaches. To mitigate these limitations, we adapted the research plan to focus on experiments that could
