Improvements in chemical reaction pathway exploration algorithms and dataset generation
Authors: Zhaojia Dong, Hanwen Zhang, Bowen Li, Sixuan Mi, Jiabin Yin, Jingbo Wang, Jianyi Ma, Tong Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chemical reaction networks provide a comprehensive framework for understanding complex reaction systems, in which reaction path exploration is a critical component. In this study, molecular structures are represented as bond-electron matrices, and reaction candidates are systematically enumerated through matrix transformations. Starting from more than 1,000 reactant molecules, diverse reaction pathways were generated and validated using DFT calculations, resulting in OrgReact, a dataset comprising 9,649 reactions. The dataset includes reactant, product, and transition-state structures, together with associated energetic information, and is intended to support data-driven studies of organic reaction pathways and machine learning models for molecular energies and forces.

Background & Summary

The significance of chemical reaction networks lies in their ability to provide a comprehensive understanding of complex chemical reaction systems. By constructing reaction networks, we can explore relationships between different reactions, discover novel reaction pathways and intermediates, elucidate reaction mechanisms, and predict product formation1,2. Understanding reaction networks facilitates optimization of synthetic routes, design of new catalysts, and prediction of chemical reaction behaviors, thus offering critical guidance and support in fields such as material design, drug development, and environmental protection. Chemical reaction networks contain numerous known and unexplored reaction pathways. Traditionally, research relies primarily on manual exploration methods, combining experimental observations and quantum chemical calculations. Researchers must utilize chemical intuition to propose reaction intermediates and transition-state structures. However, as the number of potential reactants in the chemical space increases by hundreds or thousands, manually designing reaction pathways involves enormous workloads and low efficiency. This limitation has driven the rapid advancement of algorithms for automated reaction path exploration3.

Research on the automatic search of chemical reaction pathways began quite early. In 1994, Klein et al.4 proposed a computational system named NETGEN for automatically generating pyrolysis reaction models. Molecules were represented as molecular graphs—vertices as atoms and edges as bonds—with adjacency matrices and bond-electron matrices used to describe molecular connectivity and electronic information. Chemical reactions were then automatically executed by manipulating these matrices to generate product structures. In 2000, V. Warth et al.5 introduced EXGAS, a software system for automatically generating gas-phase oxidation mechanisms. It employed specific pattern-matching rules and structural transformation operations for each reaction type, enabling the automatic construction of detailed kinetic mechanisms for alkanes and ethers. In 2010, Rangarajan et al.6 developed a tool named RING (Rule Input Network Generator), an automated chemical network generation tool that takes SMILES strings as input and converts them into graph structures. It was specifically designed to simulate complex thermochemical reaction networks in biomass conversion. In the same year, Satoshi Maeda’s group7 proposed a novel method for reaction path exploration called AFIR (Artificial Force Induced Reaction), which represented a breakthrough in potential energy surface (PES) exploration. By introducing artificial force fields to drive directional interactions between molecular fragments, AFIR enabled automatic reaction path discovery. In 2013, Zhipan Liu’s team8 proposed a new PES exploration method named SSW (Stochastic Surface Walking), which supports both global minimum structure prediction and reaction path search, offering a balanced capability between structure and reaction exploration. That same year, Paul M. Zimmerman introduced a systematic approach named ZStruct9 for automatically identifying plausible reaction paths in main-group chemical systems. Starting from a given initial structure, ZStruct generates potential intermediates, screens thermodynamically feasible species, and automatically locates transition states to construct full reaction pathways. In 2014, Dmitrij Rappoport’s group10 proposed HAQC (Heuristics-Aided Quantum Chemistry), an automated reaction network construction approach combining heuristic rules and quantum chemistry. It is efficient for exploring complex systems such as cellular metabolic pathways. Also in 2014, Lee-Ping Wang et al.11 proposed a method named ab initio nanoreactor, which utilizes ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) to construct reaction networks. It can automatically discover reaction pathways without prior knowledge of the mechanism, and was applied to investigate, for example, glycine synthesis. In 2015, Kai Leonhard et al.12 introduced an automated method based on ReaxFF reactive molecular dynamics to discover pathways in complex chemical reactions. That same year, Maike Bergeler et al.13 proposed a reaction mechanism exploration method guided by heuristic rules. By locating reactive sites from electronic structure theory and introducing reactants (e.g., protons) near these sites to form high-energy complexes, geometrically close structure pairs were searched and treated as possible elementary reactions. In 2016, William H. Green’s group14 introduced the open-source modeling software RMG (Reaction Mechanism Generator). It uses graph theory, rule templates, and rate-based screening algorithms to automatically generate reaction mechanisms, widely applied in combustion chemistry. In 2017, Markus Reiher’s team developed the Chemoton program15 and upgraded it in 202216. Chemoton is extensible, automated, and based on first-principles calculations for exploring reaction networks. Its updated version employs a Newton Trajectory algorithm, where selected atoms or groups are pulled closer by artificial gravitational forces and biased energetically to cross barriers and reveal reaction paths. Also in 2017, Shuhua Li’s group17 proposed an automatic reaction path search method combining molecular dynamics (MD) and coordinate driving (CD). MD is used to sample conformational space, and CD to search paths. The newly generated structures from each MD/CD round serve as inputs for the next, enabling multi-step reaction network construction. In 2023, the team introduced updates18 using GFN2-xTB (for radicals) and GFN-FF (for closed-shell species) for MD, and adding IRC verification and single-electron transfer exploration to CD. In 2018, the Korean team led by Kim19 proposed a method combining molecular graph theory and reaction network analysis. Molecules were converted into graphs encoding bond information, and by controlling the number of bond breaks, product structures were generated efficiently with low computational cost. In the same year, Steven E. Wheeler’s group20 developed AARON, an open-source tool for automating transition state (TS) construction, conformer sampling, optimization, screening, and selectivity prediction. In 2019, Stefan Grimme21 proposed an efficient method to explore chemical space by combining RMSD-biased meta-dynamics with the tight-binding quantum chemistry method GFN2-xTB, enabling systematic reaction path searches. Lan’s team22 further extended this approach by incorporating implicit Markov models to identify reaction events and build reaction networks. Also in 2019, Dmitrij Rappoport and Alán Aspuru-Guzik23 proposed a framework also called HAQC (Heuristically-Aided Quantum Chemistry), which predicts organic reaction paths without requiring explicit transition state searches. It uses heuristic rules and energy evaluation to automatically identify thermodynamically and kinetically feasible paths. That same year, Judit Zádor’s group24 introduced KinBot, a tool developed for automatically constructing key stationary points (especially transition states) on the PES for unimolecular reactions (isomerization and dissociation). It is particularly suitable for combustion and radical gas-phase reaction networks, offering a fully automated pipeline from structure to TS and rate calculation. In 2020, Zhao’s group25 developed YARP (Yet Another Reaction Program), an automated reaction exploration tool that uses bond-electron matrices for reaction enumeration and the Growing String Method (GSM) for transition state path generation. YARP features low computational cost and enables rapid discovery of large numbers of reactions. In 2021, Emilio Martínez-Núñez’s team released AutoMeKin 202126, which integrates multiple algorithms and interfaces with various quantum chemistry packages. It supports highly automated, scalable reaction path exploration, includes four search algorithms, builds reaction networks with NetworkX, and is accessible via a web interface. In 2022, Evgeny A. Pidko’s team27 developed ReNeGate, a graph-based automated reaction network analysis tool designed for mechanism discovery in homogeneous catalysis. It addresses challenges in pathway discovery, network construction, and key species identification, using RMSD-biased metadynamics simulations at the GFN2-xTB level (via CREST) to explore reaction space.

In addition to the methods mentioned above, the rise of artificial intelligence has led to promising approaches for predicting chemical reaction pathways through machine learning models28,29. Samha M. H. and colleagues30 reported an end-to-end data-driven workflow that employs machine learning techniques to effectively predict how structural characteristics of coupling partners and ligands influence copper-catalyzed C-N coupling reactions31–33. Sun and colleagues introduced, for the first time, a first-principles machine learning (FPML) approach to study graphene-based atomic catalysts (GDY-ACs)34,35, directly predicting reaction trends of key C-C-C coupling processes and the transformation of various C3 products36. The effectiveness of machine learning methods relies heavily on a rich and accurate chemical reaction database containing detailed information about reactants, transition states, and products. By learning the PES of numerous reactions, machine learning models can more accurately predict novel reactions. In 2020, Colin A. Grambow37 proposed a dataset containing 11,961 chemical reactions, involving species with seven heavy atoms, including carbon (C), oxygen (O), and nitrogen (N). Transition states were obtained via the Growing String Method38 (GSM) and subsequently calculated using the Q-Chem39 software at the ωB97X-D340/def2-TZVP41 level of theory. Building upon Grambow’s dataset, Mathias Schreiner employed the Climbing Image Nudged Elastic Band42 (CI-NEB) method to re-explore reaction pathways and transition states, ultimately generating a dataset of 10,073 reactions named Transition1x43. This dataset was subsequently used to train an equivariant graph message-passing neural network model and cross-validated against widely-used ANI1x44 and QM945 datasets. Akihide Hayashi46 also utilized the Transition1x dataset to train a neural network model aimed at predicting reaction pathways. Despite its extensive use in validating machine learning models, questions remain regarding the diversity and comprehensiveness of the reactions included in the Transition1x dataset. For instance, whether additional new reactions can be enumerated from the reactants contained in this dataset still warrants further investigation. In recent years, large-scale datasets for molecular reactivity and transition-state prediction have also advanced rapidly. Levine et al. introduced the Open Molecules 2025 (OMol25) dataset47, which contains more than 100 million DFT calculations performed at the ωB97M-V/def2-TZVPD48–50 level of theory. The dataset covers diverse chemical systems, including small molecules, biomolecules, metal complexes, electrolytes, and reactive structures, thereby providing an important data foundation for the development of general-purpose machine-learning potentials and reaction-related models. In addition, Nikitin et al. recently proposed RitS, a stereochemistry-aware generative model for molecular transition states51, and constructed a dataset comprising approximately two million transition-state structures at the GFN2-xTB level.

The goal of this study is to apply automated reaction pathway search algorithms to construct a high-accuracy chemical reaction dataset. First, the automated reaction pathway exploration program YARP was selected, and its bond-electron matrix method was supplemented and refined through additional enumeration rules. Subsequently, starting from the reactants included in the Transition1x dataset, multiple enumerations were carried out using different reaction enumeration strategies to generate a larger number of novel products. The transition states of these reactions were first located using the climbing image nudged elastic band (CI-NEB) method at the xTB level of theory. Subsequently, transition state geometries were refined and validated through intrinsic reaction coordinate (IRC) calculations at the density functional theory (DFT) level using the ωB97X-D functional in combination with the def2-TZVP basis set. This procedure resulted in a new dataset consisting of 9,649 reactions, designated OrgReact. A comparative analysis between the OrgReact and Transition1x datasets was conducted to further enrich and improve the reaction database. Finally, the DPA3 model52—a foundation machine-learned interatomic potential (MLIP) based on the Deep Potential (DeePMD) architecture and designed for modeling reactive molecular systems—was trained using the integrated Transition1x and OrgReact datasets. The resulting model was subsequently evaluated, with particular focus on its accuracy in predicting energies and atomic forces along reaction pathways. This work aims to refine reaction pathway exploration algorithms and provide more accurate and reliable data support for machine learning applications.

Methods

Overview

Figure 1 illustrates the technical workflow used in this study to generate a high-quality chemical reaction dataset. The overall process consists of four key steps. Step one is reaction enumeration and initial structure optimization. Based on the reaction enumeration module of YARP version 2.053 and the concept of bond-electron matrices, we further expanded and refined the reaction generation rules. Specifically, we allowed up to three bond-breaking and three bond-forming events during the transformation from reactants to products, thereby enabling a broader coverage of reaction types. Additionally, we designed separate enumeration strategies for closed-shell and radical systems to ensure the electronic structure rationality and diversity of the reaction pathways. Using the 1,155 reactant molecules from the Transition1x dataset as starting species, multiple candidate product structures were generated via these rules. To improve the efficiency of subsequent pathway searches, we applied GFN2-xTB54,55, a highly efficient semiempirical quantum chemical method, to perform geometry optimization on the initially generated product structures, obtaining reasonable molecular conformations. Step two is transition state path search and energy barrier filtering. After obtaining the preliminarily optimized reactant and product structures, we employed the CI-NEB method at the GFN2-xTB level to search for reaction pathways and locate approximate transition state structures. This method accurately captures the pathway close to minimum energy path from reactants to products. Upon completion of the path search, we extracted energy profiles and filtered the results based on activation barriers. To ensure chemical feasibility and data quality, only reactions with energy barriers below 100 kcal/mol were retained, while high-barrier reactions were discarded. This yielded an initial collection of plausible reaction pathways. Step three is transition state optimization and validation transition state optimization and validation are performed at the DFT level using the ωB97X-D functional in combination with the def2-TZVP basis set. For the filtered reaction pathways, we conducted high-precision optimization of the transition states at the DFT level. Specifically, each transition state was optimized using the Gaussian 16 software, followed by an imaginary frequency analysis to confirm that the structure corresponds to a true transition state. Subsequently, IRC calculations were performed to verify that the transition state indeed connects the correct reactants and products. Step four is construction of the final dataset. For reactions successfully validated, we re-optimized the geometries of the corresponding reactants and products at the same DFT level to ensure consistency across all structures in the dataset. To remove redundancy, we converted the structures of reactants and products into canonical SMILES strings and performed a deduplication process. In the end, we retained all reaction pairs that passed the pathway search, energy barrier filtering, transition state optimization, and IRC validation, and organized them into a unified dataset. This final dataset includes a total of 9,649 valid reaction pathways and is named OrgReact56.Fig. 1Overview of the dataset generation workflow.

Supplementation and improvement of reaction rules

In YARP version 2.0, four types of reaction enumerations are available: b1f1, b2f1, b2f2, and b3f3 (where b denotes the number of bond-breaking events and f denotes the number of bond-forming events). To enable comprehensive reaction enumeration within the b3f3 scope, we extended the bond-electron matrix-based framework to include three additional reaction types: b1f2, b2f3, and b3f2. Moreover, we enhanced the enumeration rules for the b3f3 category to include reactions involving radical species. After completing the code improvements, the modified version now supports full enumeration for all seven types: b1f1, b1f2, b2f1, b2f2, b2f3, b3f2, and b3f3. Transition state search

After generating products through reaction enumeration using the improved YARP program, we used the structures of the reactants and products to search for transition states. Currently, commonly used methods for locating transition states include end-to-end approaches such as the GSM and the Nudged Elastic Band (NEB) method. These methods initiate the search from both the reactant and product sides to construct a reaction pathway and then identify the highest-energy point along this path as an initial guess for the transition state structure. The NEB method involves creating a series of discrete images to represent the possible path from reactants to products. These images are connected by virtual springs to maintain an even distribution along the path. By iteratively adjusting the positions of these images, the NEB method gradually converges toward the minimum energy path. However, the NEB method does not guarantee that this highest-energy point corresponds to the true transition state, as it may not represent the actual maximum on the reaction pathway. The Climbing Image NEB57 (CI-NEB) method improves upon the standard NEB by designating the highest-energy image as the “climbing image.” During subsequent iterations, the climbing image is optimized only in the direction opposite to the atomic forces (that is, toward higher energy). This allows the image to more accurately ascend to the true saddle point, thereby improving the precision of transition state identification. Therefore, we ultimately selected the CI-NEB method for locating transition states in our study.

Initial reactant data source

We utilized a dataset composed of molecules containing seven heavy atoms, including carbon (C), nitrogen (N), and oxygen (O). This dataset58, developed by Colin A. Grambow, employed the GSM to search for reaction pathways and transition states, and quantum chemical calculations were performed using Q-Chem at the ωB97X-D3/def2-TZVP level of theory. As a result, 11,961 reactions involving reactants, transition states, and products were obtained. The reactants in the Transition1x dataset were derived from this dataset. Therefore, we extracted 1,155 reactants from Grambow’s dataset, performed new reaction enumerations, and conducted transition state searches and validations, ultimately generating a new dataset named OrgReact. We will compare this new dataset with the Transition1x dataset to analyze the differences in their properties. The dataset can be downloaded from: https://zenodo.org/records/3715478.

Transition1x dataset

The Transition1x dataset59 was proposed by Mathias Schreiner and is derived from the dataset introduced by Colin A. Grambow, which contains 11,961 reactions. Mathias Schreiner recomputed the dataset at the ωB97X/6-31 G(d)60 level of theory and employed the CI-NEB method to search for transition states, ultimately obtaining 10,073 successfully converged reactions. The dataset can be downloaded from: https://gitlab.com/matschreiner/Transition1x.

Geometric optimization of reactants, transition states, and products

After completing reaction enumeration, we first applied xTB to quickly optimize the geometries of the reactants and products. We then used the CI-NEB method at the xTB level to generate initial guesses for transition states. Subsequently, the transition states were subjected to geometry optimization and imaginary frequency validation at the DFT level, followed by IRC verification. Reactions that passed the IRC verification were retained, and their corresponding reactant and product structures were further optimized at the DFT level. Throughout this process, DFT-level optimizations were performed using the Gaussian 16 software61, with the ωB97XD62 functional and the def2-TZVP basis set.

Reaction verification and extraction

During the construction of the OrgReact dataset, multiple filtering steps were applied. First, after performing geometry optimization of the enumerated reactant-product pairs using xTB, the structural validity of the products was evaluated. The filtering criterion was based on the maximum allowed number of chemical bonds for each atom (for example, a carbon atom is allowed to form no more than four bonds). Next, after applying the CI-NEB method to search for transition states, reactions were filtered based on the energy profiles generated by CI-NEB. Only reactions with a single-peaked energy curve and a reaction barrier less than 100 kcal/mol were retained. Subsequently, at the DFT level, geometry optimization of the transition states, imaginary frequency calculations, and IRC verifications were performed. Reactions that failed any of these steps were excluded. Finally, the reactants and products of the remaining reactions were further optimized at the DFT level, and any reactions that failed at this stage were also removed. The final reaction dataset was obtained after completing all of these filtering steps.

Model and training

To validate the effectiveness of the dataset, we trained models using DeePMD-kit v3 on both the Transition1x and OrgReact datasets and evaluated their accuracy in predicting energies and atomic forces. DeePMD-kit63,64 is a high-efficiency, open-source tool specifically designed for molecular dynamics simulations based on machine learning methods. Its latest version supports multiple deep learning frameworks, including TensorFlow, PyTorch, JAX, and PaddlePaddle, making it well-suited for constructing advanced neural network architectures and descriptors for complex systems, thereby significantly improving flexibility and performance.

In the training stage, we divided the experiments into three groups: the first group used the Transition1x dataset, the second group used the OrgReact dataset, and the third group used the combined dataset. For the first two groups, 80% of the data was used for training, 10% for validation, and 10% for testing. For the combined dataset, we directly merged the training, validation, and test sets from the first two groups.

Prior to training, we used the dpdata tool to preprocess the raw data, extracting atomic coordinates, potential energies, and atomic forces, and converting them into a format compatible with DeePMD-kit. In the input.json configuration file, we specified the neural network architecture, training parameters, and data paths.

During the model training process, we adopted the DPA-3 architecture supported by DeePMD-kit v3. Compared with previous models, DPA-3 incorporates higher-order structural features, effectively enhancing the representation of interatomic interactions and significantly improving the accuracy of energy and force predictions in deep potential models. For the training parameters, we set the initial learning rate to 0.001 and the batch size to 16, aiming to balance training stability and convergence efficiency. Based on the characteristics of the datasets, we designed two distinct training strategies:

The first is the comprehensive training strategy. We first trained the model independently on the Transition1x and the newly constructed OrgReact datasets for 1,000,000 steps each. This was followed by a further 1,000,000 steps of training on the combined dataset of Transition1x and OrgReact, allowing the model to fully learn the structural diversity and reaction types present in both datasets. The second is the low-barrier-focused strategy, targeting reactions with energy barriers below 75 kcal/mol. In this strategy, we first trained the model on the low-barrier reactions from Transition1x and OrgReact separately for 1,000,000 steps each. We then conducted another 1,000,000 steps of training on the merged low-barrier data from both datasets, with the goal of improving the model’s ability to characterize low-barrier reaction pathways.

Through these two training strategies, we aim to systematically evaluate the impact of incorporating the OrgReact dataset in enhancing structural diversity and enriching reaction types, thereby improving the model’s predictive accuracy and generalization ability. By comparing the performance of models trained with each strategy in terms of energy and force predictions, we provide a detailed analysis of the contribution of the OrgReact dataset and offer valuable guidance for future dataset construction and model optimization.

After training, we evaluated the models using the combined test set from both Transition1x and OrgReact, comparing the RMSE and mean absolute error (MAE) for energy and force predictions across the three models to assess their generalization ability and predictive performance.

Data Records

The dataset is available at Figshare: 10.6084/m9.figshare.31668682. The released dataset contains two compressed files: OrgReact_xyz.zip and energy_and_bonds_changes_files.zip. After decompression, OrgReact_xyz.zip contains subfolders corresponding to individual reactions. Each subfolder provides the reactant, product, and transition-state structure files for the corresponding reaction, named RS.xyz, PS.xyz, and TS.xyz, respectively.

The file energy_and_bonds_changes_files.zip contains two CSV files. The first file, OrgReact_energy.csv, summarizes the reaction barriers. Its first column, Reaction, corresponds to the folder name of each reaction, while the second column, Reaction energy barrier (kcal/mol), reports the reaction barrier in kcal/mol. The second file, OrgReact_bond_changes.csv, summarizes the bond-change information. Its first column, Reaction, corresponds to the folder name of each reaction, while the second column, Types of chemical bond changes, describes the types of chemical bond changes involved in each reaction. These files allow users to associate each reaction ID with the corresponding energetic information and bond-breaking/bond-forming features, thereby facilitating subsequent searching, filtering, and reuse.

Technical Validation

Starting from the reactants in the Transition1x dataset, we conducted a systematic reaction enumeration process based on the supplemented and refined reaction rules. The defined reaction types included b1f1, b1f2, b2f3, b2f2, and b3f3, covering various combinations of bond-breaking and bond-forming events. Through a series of filtering steps, including structural validity checks, CI-NEB reaction path energy analysis, DFT-level transition state optimization, and IRC verification, we ultimately constructed a new dataset containing 9,649 reactions, which named OrgReact.

To evaluate the quality and characteristics of the OrgReact dataset, we conducted a systematic comparison with the widely used Transition1x dataset. First, we compared the distribution of reaction barriers in the two datasets. As shown in Fig. 2, the reaction barriers in the OrgReact dataset are primarily concentrated in the 0–100 kcal/mol range. This is closely related to our filtering criterion based on CI-NEB energy profiles, where only reaction pathways with barriers below 100 kcal/mol were retained. It is worth noting that the CI-NEB calculations were performed at the xTB level, which may introduce deviations compared to DFT-level results, leading to a small number of reactions with barriers exceeding 100 kcal/mol. In contrast, the Transition1x dataset exhibits a more dispersed distribution of reaction barriers, mainly concentrated in the 50–125 kcal/mol range, with significantly fewer reactions in the 0–50 kcal/mol and above 125 kcal/mol regions.Fig. 2Comparison of reaction energy barrier distributions between the OrgReact and Transition1x datasets.

Comparison of reaction energy barrier distributions between the OrgReact and Transition1x datasets.

To further improve the accuracy of the energy labels, we employed the Deep post-Hartree-Fock (DeePHF)65 framework proposed by Xiao et al. This method constructs a neural network model that takes as input the eigenvalues of the local electron density matrix obtained at the DFT level (e.g., M06-2×66 or B3LYP67), and learns the mapping to high-level correlation energies (e.g., CCSD(T)-F12a/cc-pVDZ-F1268–70). Once trained, the model can extrapolate DFT-level energies to near-CCSD(T)71–73 accuracy, enabling high-precision energy barrier predictions for large-scale reaction systems at a fraction of the computational cost of ab initio methods.

We applied this extrapolation method to all reaction pathways in the OrgReact dataset to obtain approximate CCSD(T)-level energy estimates for each reaction, including reactants, transition states, and products. The extrapolated results show that the overall barrier distribution in the OrgReact_ approximate _CCSD(T) dataset is consistent with the original OrgReact dataset, with a slight upward shift in the 50–75 kcal/mol range and a modest increase in the proportion of low-barrier reactions (0–25 and 25–50 kcal/mol).

To evaluate the diversity and uniqueness of reactions in the OrgReact and Transition1x datasets, we conducted a systematic analysis of reaction overlap between the two. Specifically, we first used Open Babel74 to convert the Cartesian coordinates of reactants and products into SMILES representations. These SMILES were then standardized to obtain unique canonical SMILES strings. By comparing the SMILES combinations of reactant–product pairs from both datasets, we identified the set of overlapping reactions. The results showed that 944 reactions are common to both datasets, indicating that approximately 90% of the reactions in OrgReact are novel. Notably, many of these new reactions fall within the low energy barrier range, suggesting that by extending the reaction enumeration rules and implementing rigorous filtering procedures, we successfully constructed a chemical reaction dataset enriched with low-barrier reactions. To further validate the reaction enumeration capabilities of the YARP program, we randomly selected a subset of reactions from the Transition1x dataset that share the same reactant and filtered out those of the b2f2 type. We then applied the improved YARP program to perform b2f2 type reaction enumeration using the same reactant and compared the enumerated products with those in the selected subset of Transition1x. The results demonstrated that the products generated by YARP fully covered the products present in the corresponding group from Transition1x, confirming the program’s comprehensive enumeration capability for specific reaction types. The relatively low overall overlap between the OrgReact and Transition1x datasets can be attributed to two main factors. First, during the CI-NEB transition state search, we strictly filtered out high-energy reactions with energy differences greater than 100 kcal/mol, making OrgReact more focused on feasible reactions with low energy barriers. Second, the YARP program incorporates built-in rules for filtering chemically unreasonable products during enumeration, particularly with respect to ring structures. For instance, it excludes complex polycyclic structures, bridged rings, and fused rings that share two or more atoms, or a single atom in cases where one of the rings is a three-membered ring. Although these constraints limit the diversity of product structures, they help eliminate chemically unstable or computationally challenging species, thereby improving the physical reliability and computational tractability of the dataset.

We conducted a systematic comparison of the number of chemical bonds broken and formed in reactions from the OrgReact and Transition1x datasets. Specifically, we transformed both reactants and products into bond-electron matrices, where the off-diagonal elements represent the bond order between atoms, for instance, 0 denotes no bond, 1 denotes a single bond, and 2 denotes a double bond, and so on. By comparing the values at corresponding positions in the matrices of reactants and products, we were able to accurately determine the number of chemical bonds broken and formed during each reaction. Note that, in this work, the numbers of broken and formed bonds are defined as the numbers of unique atom pairs that undergo connectivity changes, rather than the sum of bond-order differences. As shown in Fig. 3, in both datasets, reactions involving the breaking and formation of two chemical bonds constitute the majority, accounting for over 50% of the data. However, further analysis reveals a clear imbalance in the Transition1x dataset: the proportion of reactions involving the breaking of two bonds is significantly higher than that of forming two bonds. This may indicate asymmetries in reaction pathways or incomplete pairing in some reactions. In contrast, the OrgReact dataset exhibits a more balanced distribution, with nearly equal proportions of reactions involving the breaking and formation of two bonds. This symmetry reflects the rigorous filtering and curation criteria applied during the construction of OrgReact, ensuring more chemically reasonable and complete reaction pathways.Fig. 3Comparison of the counts of broken bonds and formed bonds between the OrgReact and Transition1x datasets.

Comparison of the counts of broken bonds and formed bonds between the OrgReact and Transition1x datasets.

The types of chemical bond changes involved in the reactions from the OrgReact and Transition1x datasets are shown in Fig. 4. It is evident from the figure that, even after undergoing multiple stringent filtering steps-including energy barrier screening, structural validation, and transition state verification-the OrgReact dataset still retains a rich variety of chemical bond transformations. A comparative analysis of the bond change distributions in the two datasets reveals that carbon (C) and hydrogen (H) are the most frequently involved elements in bond changes, while oxygen (O) and nitrogen (N) participate in bond transformations less frequently. This trend not only reflects the inherently higher reactivity and involvement of carbon and hydrogen in organic reactions but also correlates with the elemental composition of the molecules in the datasets. Since C and H atoms are more abundant in the selected reactants, they are more likely to participate in bond-breaking and bond-forming events during reactions. In contrast, O and N atoms are present in smaller quantities, leading to fewer corresponding bond changes. We conducted a systematic comparative analysis of interatomic distance distributions in the OrgReact and Transition1x datasets to further assess the structural diversity and chemical soundness of the reactions. As shown in Fig. 5, the x-axis represents the ratio of interatomic distance to the standard single bond length for each atom pair (using the corresponding single bond length as a reference), while the y-axis denotes the normalized frequency distribution. The normalization was performed by setting the most frequent distance interval for each atom pair type to 1, and dividing the frequencies of all other intervals by this maximum value. This allows for an intuitive comparison between different atom pairs and datasets within the same coordinate framework. The distribution of interatomic distances reflects both the diversity of bond orders (e.g., single, double, or transitional bonds) and the variations in three-dimensional structural conformations. To analyze the reaction types in the OrgReact dataset, we performed a systematic classification based on the Bond-Electron Matrix approach. This method transforms the 3D structures of reactants and products into their respective bond-electron matrices. By comparing the product matrix with that of the reactant, we can accurately identify the atomic pairs that undergo bond dissociation or bond formation, thus capturing the atomic rearrangements that occur during the reaction.

Specifically, we first identify the atomic pairs in the reactant whose bonds are broken (e.g., A-B), and then track whether these atoms form new bonds in the product (e.g., A-C or B-D). For classification, each reaction is characterized by the transformation of atomic pairs, such as A-B → A-C or A-B → B-D, which effectively captures the nature of chemical changes.

As shown in Fig. 6, we identified 55 distinct types of bond transformations in the OrgReact dataset. Among them, C–C and C–H bond cleavage and recombination are the most prevalent, accounting for over 50% of all reactions. This may be attributed to the high abundance of carbon and hydrogen atoms in the dataset, as well as the relatively weaker bond strengths that make these bonds more prone to breakage. In addition, transformations involving C–N and C–O bonds account for over 25%, indicating that these bonds also exhibit notable reactivity within the reaction system.Fig. 6Reactions classification of OrgReact dataset.

On the full test set, the model trained on the Transition1x dataset exhibited relatively good performance in energy prediction, with an RMSE of 3.98 kcal/mol and an MAE of 2.17 kcal/mol. It also achieved a force MAE of 1.37 kcal/mol·Å, indicating overall robustness. In contrast, the model trained solely on the OrgReact dataset showed poor performance in energy prediction (RMSE = 8.18 kcal/mol, MAE = 4.22 kcal/mol) but demonstrated better performance in force prediction, achieving a force MAE of 2.05 kcal/mol. This suggests that the OrgReact dataset, likely due to its richer structural diversity and reaction types, provides more effective training data for learning interatomic forces. Notably, the model trained on the combined Transition1x and OrgReact datasets achieved the best performance in force prediction, with a MAE reduced to 0.79 kcal/mol·Å and an RMSE of only 1.84 kcal/mol·Å. Meanwhile, its energy prediction errors also decreased to 3.35 kcal/mol (RMSE) and 1.73 kcal/mol (MAE), demonstrating enhanced generalization ability and improved stability.

For further evaluation of model performance on low-barrier reactions, we conducted an additional analysis on the subset of reactions with barriers below 75 kcal/mol. On this subset, the model trained only on Transition1x achieved an energy prediction RMSE and MAE of 6.61 and 3.52 kcal/mol, respectively, while the corresponding force prediction RMSE and MAE were 3.15 and 1.75 kcal/mol·Å. The model trained only on OrgReact showed improved performance, with energy RMSE/MAE values of 5.85/2.61 kcal/mol and force RMSE/MAE values of 2.74/1.25 kcal/mol·Å. In contrast, the model trained on the combined Transition1x and OrgReact dataset delivered the best results, reducing the energy prediction error to 3.06 kcal/mol in RMSE and 1.48 kcal/mol in MAE, while also achieving the lowest force prediction errors, with RMSE and MAE values of 1.36 and 0.63 kcal/mol·Å, respectively. These results demonstrate that training on the combined dataset substantially improves both the accuracy and generalization ability of the model in low-barrier reaction scenarios.
