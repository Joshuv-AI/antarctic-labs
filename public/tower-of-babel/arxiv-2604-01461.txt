Reducing Hallucinations in LLM-based Scientific Literature Analysis Using Peer Context Outlier Detection
Daniel Xie, Maxwell J. Jacobson, Adil Wazeer, Haiyan Wang, Xinghang Zhang, Yexiang Xue

Abstract
Reducing hallucinations in Large Language Models (LLMs) is essential for improving the accuracy of data extraction from large text corpora. Current methods, like prompt engineering and chain-of-thought prompting, focus on individual documents but fail to consider relationships across a corpus. This paper introduces Peer Context Outlier Detection (P-COD), a novel approach that uses the relationships between documents to improve extraction accuracy. Our application domain is in scientific literature summarization, where papers with similar experiment settings should draw similar conclusions. By comparing extracted data to validated peer information within the corpus, we adjust confidence scores and flag low-confidence results for expert review. High-confidence results, supported by peer validation, are considered reliable. Our experiments demonstrate up to 98% precision in outlier detection across 6 domains of science, demonstrating that our design reduces hallucinations, enhances trust in automated systems, and allows researchers to focus on ambiguous cases, streamlining the data extraction workflows.

Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) have transformed the landscape of automated information retrieval by enabling rapid synthesis of vast scientific literature. However, their propensity for generating hallucinations – plausible but incorrect information – presents a serious challenge, particularly in scientific research where accuracy is paramount. Hallucinations occur because LLMs generate responses based on statistical patterns rather than verified facts, leading to unreliable extractions that can mislead researchers.

While LLMs provide significant improvements in efficiency and adaptability, their errors can cause issues when incorrect information is given in automated workflows. This is especially problematic in scientific literature, where data accuracy is crucial for future research. Without robust validation mechanisms, LLM-driven knowledge extraction risks amplifying misinformation, making it difficult to distinguish between genuine scientific conclusions and errors.

Several strategies have been developed to improve the accuracy of LLM outputs. One widely used approach is prompt engineering
(
13
;
17
)
– prompts are optimized to guide the model toward more reliable extractions. This includes zero-shot prompting, where the model is directly asked to generate an answer without prior examples, and few-shot prompting, where the model is given a small set of examples to better understand the task. While these techniques improve performance, they operate only at the individual document level and rely on indirect approaches to modify LLM behavior. They do not introduce any mechanism to verify the correctness of extracted data against external sources, making them prone to hallucinations when factual grounding is weak.

Another technique is Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting
(
16
;
20
)
, which encourages the model to reason step-by-step before making a final decision. While this method improves logical consistency, it does not inherently validate extracted data against external sources or flag potential inconsistencies within a corpus. It remains limited in its ability to cross-reference information across multiple documents.

A more recent approach is Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)
(
7
)
, which enhances LLMs by incorporating external knowledge from a retrieval system. This method grounds model outputs in authoritative sources, reducing hallucinations. However, RAG depends entirely on the completeness and reliability of its retrieval system. If the retrieved documents are incomplete, outdated, or lack experimental data, RAG cannot ensure the correctness of extracted scientific information.

Despite these advancements, existing methods do not actively compare extracted data against related scientific literature. They fail to account for
inter-document relationships
– missing an important and useful idea for validation. Existing approaches do not validate whether an answer retrieved by the LLM is consistent with trends established by other documents. This leads to a fundamental gap in reliability: models may produce plausible but incorrect outputs without mechanisms to detect inconsistencies across multiple sources.

The absence of cross-document validation means that researchers must manually verify extracted data, reducing the efficiency of automated extraction systems. Without a system that can flag anomalies given the context of similar literature, erroneous information can go unnoticed, potentially skewing scientific analyses and conclusions. To ensure trust in LLM-generated data, a method is needed that cross-checks extracted values against similar studies.

To address this gap, we introduce
Peer Context Outlier Detection (P-COD)
, a novel approach that enhances extraction accuracy by leveraging inter-document relationships. Unlike traditional methods that process each document in isolation, P-COD evaluates extracted data in the context of semantically related and peer-reviewed papers. The intuition is that papers summarizing experiments with similar conditions should conclude with similar results. Our approach allows for confidence scoring, where extracted data points are evaluated based on alignment with existing knowledge, anomaly detection, where numerical inconsistencies between related papers are flagged for review, and human-in-the-loop validation, where only low-confidence results require manual assessment.

P-COD functions as an advanced form of outlier detection
(
1
;
8
)
but incorporates additional layers of validation. Standard outlier detection primarily relies on statistical measures such as Z-score analysis, Mahalanobis distance, or density-based clustering methods like k-Nearest Neighbors (k-NN) anomaly detection. These techniques work by identifying data points that deviate significantly from a central tendency, often based on a predetermined threshold. For example, Z-score detection flags values that lie a certain number of standard deviations away from the mean, while Mahalanobis distance considers the correlation between variables to detect outliers in multidimensional datasets. In the context of structured numerical data, these methods can be highly effective.

However, when applied to scientific data extraction, traditional outlier detection techniques face significant limitations. Scientific studies are not uniform; they differ in methodology, reporting formats, and experimental conditions, meaning that what appears to be an outlier numerically may actually be a valid deviation due to a different experimental approach. Furthermore, statistical outlier detection assumes that an outlier is an inherently incorrect or anomalous value, but in scientific research, differences in methodologies can lead to naturally high variance in reported data. This makes it difficult to determine whether a flagged value is truly an error or a meaningful deviation based on the study’s experimental design.

Our method extends traditional anomaly detection by utilizing semantic embeddings to group comparable studies and establish peer relationships, comparing extracted numerical values against those of semantically similar papers, adjusting confidence scores based on alignment with established research, and automatically flagging results that deviate significantly from expected trends, reducing false positives in anomaly detection. By incorporating peer validation, our approach provides a more nuanced and reliable assessment of data correctness than existing techniques.

To evaluate this approach, we conducted experiments on scientific document corpora across 6 domains (Computer Science, Physics, Biology, Chemistry, Materials Science, and Environmental Science), demonstrating up to
98%
precision in identifying anomalous data points. The key outcome of our method is a ranked list of data points, scored based on how surprising they are relative to their peers. By generating these surprising scores, our system provides a clear and quantifiable measure of confidence, allowing researchers to prioritize their review process. Instead of manually verifying all extracted data, researchers can focus on the subset of flagged points that exhibit the most unexpected deviations. This dramatically narrows down the scope of human review, significantly reducing effort while improving the overall reliability of automated data extraction.

By incorporating corpus-wide validation into automated data extraction, P-COD increases trust in LLM-driven research workflows. This allows researchers to focus on genuinely ambiguous cases while streamlining large-scale information synthesis, reinforcing LLMs as powerful tools for scientific discovery.

Problem Definition

The challenge of extracting accurate information from scientific literature using LLMs lies in their tendency to generate plausible but incorrect data. Automated extraction methods lack robust mechanisms for verifying extracted values, leading to potential inaccuracies in high-stakes domains such as scientific research. To ensure reliability, a system must not only extract information efficiently but also identify which extracted data points require human verification. This is the fundamental problem P-COD seeks to address – a human-in-the-loop validation system that highlights uncertain extractions for targeted review rather than relying on exhaustive manual verification.

The system takes as input a collection of scientific papers along with a query about their contents. Each paper is processed to extract relevant information, and rather than treating each extracted value in isolation, the system evaluates them in relation to other papers in the dataset. This ensures that outputs are assessed not just based on individual accuracy but also on how well they align with broader trends in the field.

The output of our method is a list of flagged papers and data points that are likely to contain hallucinated or inconsistent information extracted by the LLM. Each flagged point is associated with a surprising score, which quantifies how unexpected a data point is based on two primary factors: semantic similarity and experimental consistency. If a numerical value extracted from a paper deviates significantly from values in semantically related papers – those that discuss similar methodologies and experiments – its surprising score increases. Similarly, if an experimental result diverges from established trends within a comparable set of studies, it is flagged as a likely outlier. While these scores do not explicitly form a ranking, they can be used as a ranking system, where the most surprising points (those with the highest scores) should be prioritized for human review. Researchers can also define a threshold to control how much deviation is tolerable before a paper is flagged, allowing flexibility in balancing automated extraction with human oversight.

The key trait of the surprising score is its ability to rank data points by their deviation from expected values. Instead of requiring human reviewers to manually verify every extracted value, the system pinpoints the most anomalous results, directing human attention toward potential errors or novel findings. This significantly reduces the burden of manual verification while ensuring that genuinely uncertain or unexpected data points receive appropriate scrutiny.

By structuring the problem this way, P-COD transforms an unmanageable task – manually validating all extracted data – into a more efficient process. Rather than discarding uncertain extractions or blindly trusting LLM outputs, the system integrates human expertise into an automated workflow, allowing researchers to focus their efforts where it matters most. This problem formulation serves as the foundation for the methodology that follows, detailing how surprising scores are computed and used to refine scientific data extraction.

The goal of our method is to identify value outliers within a peer group by comparing extracted data points against semantically similar and methodologically related scientific papers. Traditional outlier detection methods struggle with contextual variations across different studies, but our approach ensures data reliability by validating it against its peers. Figure

To achieve this, our method outputs a
Surprising Score

The surprising score captures situations where a paper’s content is very similar to others in the field (high semantic similarity) but reports experimental results that deviate significantly from established norms (high experimental difference). This scenario is critical to flag because it reveals potential inconsistencies in results.

Semantic Text Embedding of Papers

In natural language processing (NLP), a text embedding is a high-dimensional vector representation of a given text that captures its semantic meaning. These embeddings transform text into vectors that can be compared to one another in a structured way. By converting words, sentences, or even entire scientific papers into vector representations, we can analyze and compare their content based on meaning rather than just surface-level word similarities.

LLMs generate these embeddings using transformer-based architectures with multi-head self-attention mechanisms that encode positional and contextual relationships between tokens. Specifically, we use OpenAI’s text-embedding-3-large model
(
14
)
. The underlying mechanism behind text embeddings relies on transformer-based neural networks, which process input text by encoding contextual relationships between words through multiple layers of attention mechanisms. This allows the model to represent words not only based on their individual meanings but also in relation to their surrounding context. Unlike traditional word frequency-based approaches (such as TF-IDF
(
12
)
), text embeddings capture deeper meanings by understanding word relationships at different levels – such as sentence structure, phrase composition, and even document-level semantics.

In our approach, we represent scientific papers as embeddings in a high-dimensional space to facilitate their comparison. Each paper is converted into a vector using a pretrained language model (GPT4o). As shown in Figure
2
, these embeddings are generated through the Text Embedding LLM, which converts scientific literature into a shared vector space where semantic similarities can be analyzed. Once vectorized, papers with similar research topics, methodologies, and terminologies will have embeddings that are closer together in this space. This allows us to quantify how related two papers are based on their semantic similarity rather than merely comparing keyword overlap. To measure semantic similarity, we use cosine similarity
(
18
)
, a standard for semantic vector comparisons.

The ability to analyze text through embeddings enables us to enhance the reliability of scientific data extraction. When assessing an extracted numerical result, we do not just examine the document it came from in isolation; instead, we compare it to semantically related documents to determine whether the extracted data aligns with established findings in the field. This embedding-based approach allows for a more context-aware evaluation, reducing the risk of relying on isolated or potentially misleading information.

This score is designed to identify data points that require human review by assessing their alignment with established scientific trends.

P-COD on Multiple Domains

Setup.
To evaluate P-COD’s generalizability across scientific disciplines, we conducted experiments spanning 6 domains: Computer Science, Physics, Biology, Chemistry, Materials Science, and Environmental Science. We selected papers from the scientific_papers dataset
(
3
)
, which contains structured documents from ArXiv and PubMed repositories. Using OpenAI’s text-embedding-3-large model to generate semantic vector representations that allow us to plot paper similarity in latent dimensional space, we created a controlled experimental dataset as follows: For each domain, we started with real papers from the dataset as seeds and generated 168 synthetic papers (28 per domain) that maintain semantic similarity to their respective seeds while varying experimental values. These synthetic papers serve as our ground truth dataset, organized into 4 clusters per domain based on methodological similarity. Our experiment tests P-COD’s ability to detect anomalous experimental values by strategically corrupting a subset of these papers and measuring detection accuracy. We identified domain-appropriate experimental metrics and their typical ranges through systematic analysis of the original scientific_papers dataset.

For each domain, we selected specific metrics that represent common experimental outcomes: accuracy (0.70-0.95) for Computer Science, wavelength (400-800 nm) for Physics, protein levels (2-1000 ng/mL) for Biology, yield (50-100%) for Chemistry, strength (100-2000 MPa) for Materials Science, and CO2 levels (380-450 ppm) for Environmental Science. We strategically corrupted 25% of papers by introducing values 3-5 times outside normal ranges to create obvious outliers for validation.

Baseline Methods.
To establish P-COD’s superiority, we compared against two common methods for detecting problematic data: prompt engineering and chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting. The
prompt engineering baseline
uses direct GPT-4 classification with domain-specific engineered prompts, combining quantitative range checking with title-based heuristics. The
Chain-of-Thought baseline
implements structured multi-step reasoning including range assessment, deviation severity calculation, contextual analysis, evidence integration, and domain-specific validation.

Results.
P-COD significantly outperformed both baseline methods across all six domains. P-COD flagged 53 papers as potential outliers, of which 42 were actually corrupted papers, achieving 79.2% precision (42/53) in detecting corrupted papers. The prompt engineering approach achieved precision ranging from 45.7% to 68.4% across domains, with an average precision of 55.9%. The Chain-of-Thought method achieved precision ranging from 58.3% to 76.8% across domains, with an average precision of 66.9%. In contrast, P-COD achieved precision ranging from 66.7% to 100.0% across domains, with an average precision of 88.7%. This consistent performance across diverse scientific fields validates our approach’s domain-independent effectiveness and demonstrates the value of semantic-experimental integration over purely statistical or prompt-based approaches.

The semantic clustering effectively grouped papers by methodology within each domain, while the surprising score mechanism correctly flagged papers with anomalous experimental values. Analysis of the clustering results confirmed that papers discussing similar experimental approaches were positioned closely in semantic space, while corrupted papers were identified as clear outliers by P-COD.

P-COD on Computer Science Domain

Setup.
To test scalability and within-domain precision, we conducted a large-scale experiment focused on Computer Science with 200 papers organized into 8 specialized clusters of 25 papers each: Deep Learning Optimization, Reinforcement Learning, Graph Neural Networks, Blockchain Technology, Data Mining Systems, Cloud Computing, Information Retrieval, and Bioinformatics. Each cluster was built around one seed paper from the original dataset, with 24 additional synthetic papers generated to maintain methodological similarity while varying experimental results. This diverse set of CS subfields allows us to test P-COD’s ability to detect anomalies within closely related research areas that share the common metric of model accuracy (0.70-0.95 range), demonstrating how the method performs when semantic similarity is high but experimental values deviate from expected norms. We corrupted 25% of papers with accuracy values outside the 0.70-0.95 range.

Results.
This experiment achieved outstanding results with 98.0% precision in detecting corrupted papers, successfully detecting 49 out of 50 corrupted papers. This demonstrates P-COD’s effectiveness at scale and its ability to maintain high precision within specialized sub-domains.

Conclusion

This paper presents a novel approach for field-wide validation of scientific data extraction using surprising scores. By integrating semantic similarity and experimental consistency, our method provides a robust mechanism to enhance trust in automated data extraction.

Peer Context Outlier Detection enables cross-document validation, ensuring that extracted data aligns with established trends within a given research domain. Through this process, we reduce the likelihood of hallucinations and improve the reliability of information synthesized from large language models (LLMs).

Our experimental results demonstrate the effectiveness of this approach in identifying anomalous data points with up to 98% precision. By using semantic embeddings and inter-document relationships, P-COD enables researchers to focus on ambiguous cases while minimizing unnecessary manual review –streamlining large-scale information synthesis, and reinforcing LLMs as reliable tools for scientific discovery. From the accuracy, it can be deployed in the scientific community to improve the reliability of automated literature analysis.

Although P-COD demonstrates strong performance in diverse scientific domains, several areas present opportunities for future enhancement. Future work will explore domain-specific adaptation through fine tuned embedding models for individual scientific fields. Multi-modal integration represents another promising direction, extending P-COD to analyze figures, tables, images, videos, and supplementary data alongside textual content, enabling comprehensive validation of experimental claims across all forms of information storage.

Furthermore, integration with existing scientific databases and real-time validation pipelines could enable P-COD deployment in automated peer review systems and literature review workflows, providing confidence for researchers and flagging potentially problematic data points before publication. These extensions build upon our validated approach to further enhance accuracy, confidence, reliability, and belief in the adoption of automated literature review systems across the broader scientific research ecosystem.
