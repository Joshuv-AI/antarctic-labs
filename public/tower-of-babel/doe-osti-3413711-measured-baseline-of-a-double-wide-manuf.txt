 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

A 1990 double-wide manufactured home (MH) dimensioned 26 ft × 40 ft with an area of 1,040 ft2 and total
volume of 8,320 ft3 was installed and instrumented on Oak Ridge National Laboratory’s (ORNL) main
campus in Oak Ridge, Tennessee to establish baseline (“as-is”) performance for envelope, airtightness, and
space-conditioning energy use. The unoccupied test facility was instrumented with more than 200 sensors
to quantify hygrothermal behavior across the four wall orientations and major assemblies (walls, floor, and
ceiling/roof), including temperature (thermistors/thermocouples), relative humidity, and heat-flux
measurements. Boundary conditions were recorded using an on-site weather station (air temperature,
relative humidity, pressure, precipitation, solar radiation, wind speed and direction). Electrical consumption
of space-conditioning equipment was monitored with circuit-level power metering, and additional
instrumentation supported condensate measurement and mean radiant temperature characterization.

Field-measured, monthly integrated thermal resistance (R-value) was calculated from December 2025
through May 2026 using surface temperatures and in-situ heat-flux data. Wall thermal performance was
stable and consistent with calculated values: measured integrated R-values ranged from 18.5 to 19.5
hr·ft²·°F/Btu versus a calculated value of ~19.2. In contrast, the floor assembly underperformed
substantially with measured integrated R-values ranging from 10.1 to 12.8 hr·ft²·°F/Btu compared to a
calculated value of ~20.4. This reduction is consistent with observed degradation of the belly cavity system
(sagging/failed membrane and displaced fiberglass insulation), which likely increased convective bypass
and reduced effective insulation performance. Ceiling integrated R-values were most reliable during winter
(12.0–12.7 hr·ft²·°F/Btu from December–February), aligning with the calculated ceiling value (10.8 ± 2)
given measured variability in loose-fill thickness; spring results became highly variable as temperature
differentials weakened due to solar effects.

Sheathing temperature and relative humidity (RH monitoring showed broadly similar behavior across all
wall orientations, with periods above 80% RH occurring primarily during colder conditions and generally
declining as temperatures increased, indicating seasonal drying potential and no clear, persistent mold-risk
signature in the monitored wall locations. The floor sheathing exhibited wider RH swings, consistent with
the compromised belly condition. Roof OSB sheathing experienced pronounced solar-driven temperature
peaks (maximum recorded ~160.5°F in spring), with modest RH differences between slopes, underscoring
the importance of summer overheating considerations for any future above-sheathing insulation retrofits.

Airtightness testing using multipoint depressurization blower door measurements found the home to be
extremely leaky in its baseline condition at 18.1 ACH50. Sealing the vents and central HVAC distribution
system reduced leakage to 14.8 ACH50 (an ~18% reduction), demonstrating that duct/distribution pathways
contribute materially to whole-house leakage. Two off-the-shelf tape-based “DIY” duct-sealing approaches
produced smaller reductions (to 17.3 and 16.8 ACH50), indicating limited whole-house benefit from simple
register/duct sealing measures alone.

Space heating during winter baseline operation was provided by three variable-speed window space
conditioner (WSC) units (two from Manufacturer A and one from Manufacturer B). Based on monitored
inlet/outlet air temperatures and power measurements for the most instrumented unit, average WSC winter
performance (December 2025 and January 2026) ranged from coefficient of performance (COP) ~2.5 to
3.08, with uncertainty driven primarily by airflow measurement limitations on one unit. Using conservative
assumptions for building-level comparison, WSC operation was estimated to reduce heating energy use by
roughly 55% and peak demand by roughly 40% relative to distributed electric resistance (ER) heating
providing equivalent delivered heat.

Planned and unplanned power interruption tests further highlighted peak-demand implications during
recovery. After an unplanned ~4-hour outage (average outdoor ~22°F), three WSCs restored indoor

 1

conditions to setpoint in under 2 hours with a measured peak of 2,224 W. In a planned 9-hour shutdown at
~20°F using four 1,500 W resistance heaters, recovery required ~3 hours with a 5,424 W peak. A planned
9-hour outage using three WSCs recovered in ~3 hours with a 4,440 W peak. A mixed strategy (two WSCs
plus two 1,500 W resistance heaters) recovered in ~2.5 hours at ~30°F with a 4,236 W peak and reduced
recovery energy use by 46% relative to resistance-only heating. Collectively, these baseline results quantify
key envelope deficiencies (notably the floor), document very high infiltration, and demonstrate the potential
of WSC heating and cooling systems to reduce energy use and peak electrical demand in older, leaky MHs.

 1. INTRODUCTION

There are approximately 8.7 million manufactured homes (MH) in the U.S., accounting for 6.3% of total
US housing stock. About 79% of MHs are inhabited, providing housing for roughly 22 million people [1,2].
Manufactured housing is mostly concentrated in the southern, northwestern, and southwestern United
States, with about half of these homes located in rural areas, where they constitute 13% of the housing
stock, compared with a 5% national average. In some rural counties, MHs comprise more than half of all
housing units [3].

Manufactured homes have the highest energy use intensity of all residential building types, with energy
costs roughly 50% higher per square foot than site-built single-family homes and 20% higher than
apartments in large buildings [3]. This high energy use is influenced by the differences in building envelope
efficiency requirements in the Manufactured Home Construction and Safety Standards (the HUD Code),
which serves as the primary federal building code for MHs. Housing age is another critical factor, with over
40% of homes at least 40 years old, 22% predating the HUD Code, and 66% built before the 1994 HUD
Code update [4]. The HUD Code, first established in 1976 and revised in 1994 to improve safety and
introduce modest thermal efficiency requirements, provides minimal energy performance compared with
modern building standards [3].

Residents of MHs are predominantly low- to moderate-income households, with a median household
income of approximately $35,000 USD, compared with $70,000 USD for residents of site-built homes
[5,6]. Energy affordability is therefore a critical concern. About 50% of MH households are energy
burdened, and 25% are severely burdened, with a median energy burden of 5.3%, nearly double the U.S.
median of 2.9%. Of the 8.4 million MH households, 44% spend more than 6% of their income on energy,
highlighting the social benefit for cost-saving retrofits [6].

Electricity is the primary fuel in MH, followed by natural gas and propane. About 60% of MH use electricity
for space heating and 77% for water heating, while propane use is higher in rural areas, lacking piped gas.
The reliance on electricity and propane, which are generally more expensive than natural gas, contributes
to higher utility costs in MHs [4].

Due to current home affordability concerns in the US, both the total number and market share of MHs are
expected to grow in coming years [7]. When normalized by living area, MHs have significantly higher
energy costs than any other major U.S. housing type. Previous studies reported that energy costs per square
foot for MHs are more than 50% higher than those of single-family detached homes [8,9].

To address the high energy burden associated with this growing segment of the housing market, it is
important to first establish the baseline performance of a typical MH and then evaluate cost-effective
envelope and equipment retrofit strategies aimed at reducing energy consumption and utility costs for
occupants living in MHs. This report presents the baseline (“as-is”) performance of a MH testing facility.
Specifically, it summarizes the hygrothermal performance of the building envelope systems, airtightness
characteristics, energy consumption, and peak electricity demand of the test home. The results provide an

in-situ assessment representative of the real-world performance of a typical MH under unoccupied
conditions.

 2. LITERATURE REVIEW

A comprehensive study by the Northwest Energy Efficiency Alliance (NEEA) examined 341 MHs across
four U.S. sub-regions (Idaho, Western Montana, Oregon, and Washington) [10]. The study revealed that
new construction of MHs increased sharply from the 1970s until 2000, after which there was a noticeable
decline. Over 98% of the homes were built between 1961 and 2010, with less than 3% constructed after
2010. The sample included various types of homes: 22% single-wide, 71% double-wide, 4% triple-wide,
and 3% modular/prefab. For the building envelope, 19% of the homes had attic or ceiling insulation with
an R-value of R0-R8, 37% had R9-R14, 3% had R15-R21, 31% had R22-R30, and 11% had R31-R40. In
terms of wall insulation, 81% of the homes featured at least R9 insulation (with 57% between R9 and R14,
and 25% between R15 and R21), while 19% had insulation values between R0 and R8. Air leakage testing
using a blower apparatus indicated that older MHs generally exhibited higher air leakage. The average air
change per hour at a 50 Pa differential pressure (ACH50) was 16.2 for homes built between 1961 and 1970,
compared to 5.3 for homes built after 2001. Table 1 summarizes the vintage and airtightness of the MHs
investigated. Similarly, Baylon et al. [11] conducted a study for the Northwest Energy Efficient
Manufactured Homes (NEEM) initiative. Testing 74 double-wide and triple-wide MHs in Idaho, Montana,
Oregon, and Washington from 1992 to 2007 revealed a decrease in ACH50 from a maximum of 6.1 in 1992
to a minimum of 3.7 in 2007, indicating that the homes have become progressively air-tight over this 15-
year period (1992-2007).

A study conducted by Building America Industrialized Housing Partnership (BAIHP) for the U.S.
Department of Energy (DOE) analyzed 105 MHs built between 2001 and 2002, which were then tested in
2004 [12]. The results showed that homes are becoming tighter, as evidenced by an average air leakage rate
of 4.2 ACH50. This represents nearly a 25% improvement over the original Manufactured Home Association
(MAP) home average [12]. In addition, the median equivalent leakage area (EqLA) for double-section
homes decreased by about 12% compared to average MHs, even though the overall house size increased.
Approximately half of the homes in the study used central cooling, and of these, more than half were
equipped with a heat pump. Efficiency losses attributable to centralized duct supply leaks in the tested
homes ranged from 10% to 20%, depending on local weather and the type of heating equipment.
Additionally, a 2022 study by Slipstream evaluated the building tightness of 200 newly factory-tested MHs,
primarily single-wide, from multiple plants in the Southeast. The average measured air leakage was 4.7
ACH50, ranging from 2 ACH50 to over 7 ACH50. The study also noted that leakage rates are expected to
increase somewhat after the homes are installed on-site [13]. Similarly, Ek et al. conducted blower door
depressurization tests on 93 double-wide MHs in the U.S. Northwest region during 1986. They reported an
average leakage rate of 8.40 ACH50 [14].

In a separate study, Saidi and Tabares-Velasco performed blower door tests on 15 rural MHs constructed
in different years [15]. They reported an ACH50 of 13.8 for homes built post-1994, compared to 18.3 for
those built pre-1976. Additionally, Davis et al. assessed nine MHs built to Model Conservation energy-
efficiency standards in the Pacific Northwest during 1994 and 1995. These homes featured high levels of
underfloor insulation (R-33), duct insulation, and minimal duct leakage. They found that new, electrically
heated MHs participating in a regional program had a field-assessed air leakage of 4.5 ACH50 [16].

Finally, Pigg et al. [17] performed depressurization blower door tests on 95 residential homes (both
manufactured and non-manufactured) in Minnesota. They found that air leakage rates varied from 4 to 49
ACH50, with a mean value of 12.9 ACH50; specifically, MHs had a mean of 15.5 ACH50 compared to 10.9
ACH50 for non-manufactured homes.

 3. STUDY OBJECTIVES

The primary objectives of this baseline study are to:

 1. Assess the hygrothermal performance of building envelope systems (walls, floor, and ceiling) of
 the MH testing facility.

 2. Evaluate the airtightness of the MH testing facility under two conditions: with the central HVAC
 system vents sealed and unsealed.

 3. Analyze initial performance data from winter space-heating tests using the WSC units.

 4. Determine the impact of the WSC system on reducing energy consumption and peak demand.

 5. Assess the performance (COP) of using the WSC system as an alternative to centralized heating
 and cooling systems.

 4. MANUFACTURED HOME TESTING FACILITY DESCRIPTION

The MH installed at the ORNL facility is a double-wide unit measuring 26 ft by 40 ft, constructed in the
early 1990s (see Figure 1a). It features three bedrooms, two bathrooms, a living room, and a kitchen. The
home has a cathedral ceiling without an attic. Ceiling heights range from 7 ft along the long sides (east and
west) to 9 ft at the center. All measurements are taken from the interior. The home's total area is 1,040 ft2
with a volume of 8,320 ft3. It has two exterior entry doors, located on the west and east sides. The house
was imported from Michigan to ORNL’s site in Tennessee.

Figure 1b displays the North-West view of the home, which features the west side exterior entry door and
two WSC units. The central air conditioning system is a Ruud RA13 Series unit and Coleman natural gas
furnace for heating. The home includes seven windows and one skylight: four windows are positioned on
the west side, one window is located on the south side of Bedroom 2, one is on the east side of the kitchen,
and one window is on the north side of the master bedroom toilet (see Figure 1a).

The building envelope of the MH consists of the following assemblies: (1) Exterior walls (outside to inside):
0.04 in. vinyl siding, 0.20 in. XPS sheathing, 5.5 in. fiberglass batt insulation with kraft facer, 2 × 6 in.

 4

wood studs spaced 16 inches on center, and 5/16 in. interior gypsum board; (2) Interior walls: ½ in. gypsum
board, 3.5 in. air cavity, and ½ in. gypsum board; (3) Roof (top to bottom): 0.3 in. asphalt roof shingles,
felt underlayment, ½ in. OSB sheathing, 4 in. vented air space, an average of 2.2 in. insulation, and 5/8 in.
gypsum board ceiling; (4) Floor (top to bottom): Carpet and pad, 5/8 in. OSB subfloor, 6 in. fiberglass batt
insulation (unfaced), and a plastic membrane; (5) Crawlspace wall (skirt): vinyl siding; and (6) Exterior
windows (outside to inside): 3 mm clear glass, 4 in. air gap, and 3 mm clear glass (storm window).

It is worth noting that although the floor originally incorporated 6 in. of fiberglass batt insulation with a
moisture-resistant plastic membrane, the membrane has sagged over time, causing the insulation to drop 1–
2 ft below the OSB sheathing. As a result, the floor insulation currently provides reduced thermal benefit.

On the equipment side of the MH testing facility, the MH includes a 2-ton, SEER 13, centralized air
conditioning system, a 62 kBtu/hr (output) natural gas furnace and a 40 gallon 32 kBtu/hr natural gas water
heater. The ducting system was found to be very leaky and the air conditioner undersized for the Tennessee

refrigerator was also provided and the baseline energy consumption was about 1.13 kWh per day. Since
60% of the space heating for MHs is electric and 77% of the water heating is electric, the typical baseline
energy consumption for the MH was estimated by back-calculating the energy consumption from the
window space conditioners at a COP = 1 for two months of the heating season – essentially equivalent to
the electrical usage for this same time period.

The WSCs used to baseline the system are manufactured by two different manufacturers, henceforth called
manufacturer A and manufacturer B. The technology fits over and is set down onto the windowsill, is
completely installable from inside the dwelling and has a variable speed compressor and variable speed
fans to allow good part-load energy performance. The refrigerant used in both units is R32 which also helps
achieve high performance at very low outdoor temperatures. In cooling mode, units from manufacturer A
pump condensed water to the outside part of the unit and use a piezoelectric to spray the water into the
ambient. Manufacturer B uses a simple spray nozzle to atomize condensate water. In total, 3 window space
conditioners were installed, 2 from manufacturer A (WSC A) and one from manufacturer B (WSC B). The
central AC and furnace indoor components are labeled in Figure 2, and the condenser (CND) is outside the
building envelope on the North corner of the house.

 5. ON-SITE WEATHER CONDITIONS MEASUREMENT

Outdoor environmental conditions were monitored from November 2025 through May 2026 using an on-
site Campbell Scientific weather station. Measurements included outdoor air temperature, RH,
precipitation, wind speed and direction, solar irradiance, vapor pressure, and barometric pressure. Indoor
environmental conditions were monitored concurrently using temperature and RH sensors installed in
multiple rooms of the MH test facility (Table 2). The outdoor and indoor datasets establish the thermal and
moisture boundary conditions acting on the building enclosure and will be used as inputs for the
hygrothermal simulation analyses in the next phase of this study.

Figure 3 presents the daily average outdoor and indoor air temperatures during the monitoring period, along
with the ASHRAE 55 [18] heating-season preferred indoor temperature range of 68°F to 75°F. Outdoor
temperatures showed pronounced seasonal variation, with the coldest conditions occurring in January and

 6

February 2026 and warmer conditions during the spring months. The maximum daily outdoor temperature
recorded was 76.5°F, and the minimum was 17.1°F. Indoor temperatures remained comparatively stable
due to operation of WSCs in the three bedrooms. The maximum and minimum indoor temperatures were
recorded in the master bedroom toilet (79.1°F and 56.7°F, respectively). In the remaining rooms of the
house, maximum indoor temperatures were generally near 76°F, while minimum indoor temperatures
ranged from 57.3°F to 59.0°F. For most of the monitoring period, indoor temperatures were within the
ASHRAE 55 preferred range. Short departures below the comfort range occurred on November 10, 2025;
January 27, 2026; and February 25, 2026. The November event was associated with an unplanned ORNL
power outage during a cold snap. The late-January drop coincided with subfreezing outdoor conditions;
during that period, two WSC units were operating, indicating that two units were insufficient to maintain
indoor temperature under extreme cold conditions. The late-February drop corresponded to intentional
power-outage testing. Overall, the sustained indoor–outdoor temperature differential during the winter
months provided favorable conditions for evaluating the thermal resistance of the building enclosure
systems.

Daily average outdoor and indoor RH measurements are shown in Figure 4, together with the ASHRAE 55
preferred indoor RH range of 30% to 60% for the heating season. Outdoor RH fluctuated substantially with
changing weather conditions and precipitation events, with a maximum recorded RH of 100% and a
minimum of 28%. Indoor RH was comparatively stable and generally remained within the 30%–60% range
until April 21. After that date, a humidifier was added to represent occupancy-related moisture generation.
Because the manufactured home has three bedrooms, we assumed four occupants living in the home
following ASHRAE 62.2 standard. The latent moisture load from four occupants was modeled as equivalent
to 1.2 gallons (10 lb) of water per day. Accordingly, the humidifier was set to add 1.2 gallons of water per
day. Although temperature and RH preferences vary among occupants depending on factors such as
clothing and activity level, maintaining indoor RH between 30% and 60% year-round is commonly
recommended to reduce conditions conducive to mold, bacterial growth, and dust mites that can aggravate
allergies and asthma at elevated RH levels. The relatively consistent indoor environmental conditions
during most of the monitoring period provided stable interior boundary conditions for assessing enclosure
thermal and moisture performance.

Solar irradiance (solar flux density) increased from winter into spring (Figure 5), ranging from 3 to 102
Btu/ft2·h. Higher solar exposure contributes to daytime warming of roof assemblies and can increase daily
roof-sheathing temperature swings during late winter and spring, with potential impact on increasing
building energy demand. Wind speed and precipitation were also monitored to characterize exterior
exposure conditions (Figure 6). Periodic high-wind and precipitation events may influence air leakage and
moisture transport mechanisms within the building enclosure systems, particularly in areas with
compromised air barriers or insulation conditions such as the damaged floor belly cavity. Daily rainfall
totals ranged from 0 to 2.5 in./day, and average daily wind speed ranged from 1.0 to 6.3 mph.

Tables 2 and 3 summarize monthly outdoor and indoor conditions for the monitoring period, highlighting
the seasonal variability in outdoor temperature, RH, solar irradiance, and precipitation, and the
comparatively stable indoor conditions maintained by operation of the test facility.

 7. INSTRUMENTATION AND EXPERIMENTAL SETUP

The data acquisition system (DAQ) for this experimental testbed is built around two Campbell Scientific
CR1000Xe dataloggers operating in parallel. The CR1000Xe supports a wide range of sensor types and
executes CRBasic programs for on-board signal processing and multi-rate data storage. Each CR1000Xe is
equipped with an external rechargeable battery backup that sustains continuous measurement and data
logging during power interruptions lasting several days, ensuring no data loss during utility outages or
short-term power fluctuations. Five Campbell Scientific AM16/32B relay multiplexers and one Campbell
Scientific AM25T 25-channel solid-state thermocouple multiplexers are distributed across the two

 13

dataloggers to accommodate the high sensor count of the instrumented testbed. Each AM16/32B provides
32 differential or 64 single-ended input channels in a 1- or 2-wire configuration [25], enabling systematic
cycling through a large number of temperature sensor (thermistor and thermocouple), heat flux transducer
(HFT) and RH sensor arrays within a single scan cycle.

Both dataloggers are programmed to execute a synchronized scan at a 30-second interval, during which all
connected sensors are sequentially polled. Raw measurements are processed on-board and written to
multiple output data tables at averaging intervals of 1, 15, 60, and 1,440 minutes. This hierarchical storage
architecture preserves high-resolution transient data while simultaneously producing the time-averaged
records required for steady-state and daily energy analysis.

Meteorological data from the on-site weather station and precipitation sensors are recorded at 60-minute
intervals, consistent with the polling constraints of the SDI-12 bus protocol used by those instruments.

Electrical energy data is acquired by an eGauge EG4015 power meter operating independently at the main
electrical panel, the eGauge logs at one-minute intervals and shares a network modem with the CR1000Xe
units, synchronizing all system clocks to a common NTP time source. Table 7 summarizes the types,
quantities, measuring ranges, and accuracy ranges of the sensors used in the MH.

The MH testing facility was instrumented with over 200 sensors. The instrumentation includes: (1)
temperature sensors (thermistors and thermocouples), RH sensors, and HFTs to characterize the
hygrothermal performance of building envelope systems (walls, floor, ceiling, roof, and windows) across
all four orientations; (2) an on-site weather station to monitor outdoor atmospheric conditions, including
air temperature, barometric pressure, precipitation, relative humidity, solar radiation, tilt, wind speed, wind
direction, and lightning strike count; (3) power meters to measure electrical energy consumption of the
central HVAC system and WSC system; (4) rain collection systems to quantify moisture removal from the
central HVAC and window units; and (5) black globe temperature sensors to measure mean radiant
temperature in two indoor rooms.

8.1 THERMAL PERFORMANCE

Monthly integrated thermal resistance (R-value) values were calculated for the wall, ceiling, and floor
assemblies from December 2025 through May 2026 using ASTM C1155 [26] sum method based on
measured field data from over 200 sensors. The purpose of this analysis was to evaluate the seasonal thermal
performance of the building envelope systems under real operating conditions and to investigate the
variation in thermal resistance across different months and assembly types. In the sum method, the
temperature difference across the assembly and the corresponding heat flux are integrated over the
measurement period (one month in this study), and the thermal resistance is calculated as the ratio of the
cumulative temperature difference to the cumulative heat flux. By integrating the measurements over time
rather than averaging instantaneous R-values, the sum method reduces the effects of transient heat storage
within the assembly and measurement noise, providing a more reliable estimate of the effective steady-state

 15

thermal resistance under dynamic field conditions. Because of these advantages, the ASTM C1155 sum
method is widely used for in-situ determination of building envelope integrated R-values.

Accordingly, the integrated R-values were determined using hourly measured interior and exterior surface
temperatures (Tin and Tout) together with hourly measured heat flux (q) data as shown in Equation 3.
Monthly values were calculated by integrating the hourly thermal response over each month to reduce short-
term fluctuations and improve the stability of the calculated thermal resistance values.

8.1.1 Wall System

Figure 9 summarizes monthly integrated wall R-values along with the calculated (expected) R-value
without the framing effect. The integrated R-values were relatively stable over the monitoring period,
ranging from 18.5 to 19.5 hr·ft²·°F/Btu. The highest values occurred in April and May, while the lowest
value was observed in February 2026. The calculated R-value of the wall assembly is calculated to be 19.2
hr·ft²·°F/Btu.

Overall, the integrated R-values show satisfactory agreement with the calculated value, indicating that the
wall is performing as intended. Minor seasonal fluctuations likely reflect changes in outdoor conditions,
transient wall behavior, and measurement uncertainty in the heat flux and temperature sensors as shown in
Table 7.

It is also important to note that the calculated R-value is constant throughout the monitoring period because
it is based on material properties and assembly configuration under standardized steady-state conditions. In
contrast, the measured monthly integrated R-value varies under actual in-situ environmental and operating
conditions. Factors such as outdoor temperature, solar radiation, wind, moisture content, air leakage, and
thermal mass within the building envelope can affect the measured heat flux and temperature difference
across the wall.

8.1.2 Floor System

The floor assembly exhibited month-to-month variation in thermal resistance, with measured R-values
ranging from 10.1 to 12.8 hr·ft²·°F/Btu (Figure 10). By comparison, the calculated R-value of the assembly

Field observations revealed tears in the belly cavity materials resulting in damages to the floor fiberglass

the cavity, increasing convective heat transfer and reducing the insulation's effective thermal resistance to
about 50% of the calculated R-value. Unlike conductive heat transfer through a well-sealed insulated cavity,
internal air movement can create thermal bypass pathways that increase heat loss and lead to lower and
more variable measured R-values. Consequently, the measured floor R-values are representative of the in-

8.1.3 Ceiling System

The ceiling assembly produced consistent, representative integrated R-values during the winter months
(December through February), ranging from 12.0 to 12.7 hr·ft²·°F/Btu (Figure 12). During this period, large

in reliable thermal resistance measurements. However, the measured R-values became increasingly variable
from March through May ranging between 9.1 to 18.5 hr·ft²·°F/Btu (Figure 12). This variability is likely
attributable to the ventilated attic configuration combined with seasonal weather changes. As outdoor
temperatures increased, the temperature gradient across the ceiling decreased, resulting in lower heat flux
levels that are near zero and increased sensitivity to measurement uncertainty. In addition, attic ventilation
and increased solar radiation in the spring season introduced significant fluctuations in attic air
temperatures, creating transient heat transfer conditions that are not fully captured by R-value calculations.
Consequently, the spring measurements are considered less representative of the actual thermal resistance
of the ceiling assembly than those obtained during the winter monitoring period.

range reflects variability in the loose-fill insulation thickness, which fluctuated by approximately 0.5 in
(about R-2) around the average measured thickness of 2.2 in., based on samples collected at six locations
in the ceiling. Considering this thickness variability, the winter integrated R-values (December 2025 -
February 2026) show satisfactory agreement with the calculated value.

slopes. Daily average values were analyzed to assess seasonal trends, moisture accumulation potential, and
the influence of enclosure conditions on moisture behavior of sheathing material layers.

Measured XPS wall-sheathing temperature and RH exhibited similar daily and seasonal trends across all
four orientations. The highest recorded sheathing temperatures were 78.8°F (south), 80.3°F (west), 77.9°F
(east), and 78.9°F (north). The lowest recorded daily sheathing surface temperatures were 29.4°F (south),
26.1°F (west), 26.5°F (east), and 26.7°F (north) as shown in Figure 13. For RH at the XPS wall-sheathing,
the maximum daily values were 88.9% (south), 94.5% (west), 99.8% (east), and 93.3% (north), while the

accumulation or lower surface temperatures than the others (Figures 13 and 14). This relatively uniform
behavior suggests comparable thermal resistance among the wall assemblies. Figure 14 also indicates an
80% RH threshold commonly associated with mold growth on material surfaces. While wall-sheathing RH
exceeded 80% during certain periods (specifically November 2025 to February 2026), elevated RH
generally coincided with lower temperatures, and RH decreased as temperatures increased indicating drying
potential during the monitoring period. The data does not show potential mold-growth risk.

RH varied more widely over the monitoring period (Figures 13 and 14). The highest and lowest recorded
temperatures were 73.6°F and 49.3°F, respectively, and RH ranged from a high of 86.8% to a low of 38.1%.

the floor. The compromised belly material likely increased air movement within the cavity, exposing the

sheathing surface.

Roof OSB sheathing temperatures showed clear seasonal differences between the two roof slopes (Figure

differences in solar exposure and solar-driven warming effects between the roof orientations during winter
conditions. After February, the maximum daily surface temperatures of both roof slopes became
considerably more consistent as seasonal conditions transitioned into spring.

To determine the leakiness of the home and ducts, blower door and duct blaster tests were conducted.

9.1 BLOWER DOOR TESTING

In this study, depressurization, multi-point blower door tests were conducted using a The Energy
Conservatory blower door system (see Figure 15) to quantify building air leakage in accordance with ASTM
International Standard ASTM E779 [28].

The blower door system specifications are as follows: (1) fan model: Energy Conservatory Model 3 (110V),
serial number 29773; and (2) digital pressure gauge: The Energy Conservatory DG-1000, serial number
20038. The manufacturer certifies that this equipment complies with the requirements of ASTM E779-19
for building airtightness testing.

To measure air leakage in the MH, depressurization tests were conducted according to the following
procedure, ensuring compliance with ASTM E779-19: (1) creating a single zone for this test by opening all
interconnecting doors in the conditioned space such that a uniform pressure is maintained within the
conditioned space to within ±10% of the measured inside/outside pressure difference, (2) closing exterior
door(s), windows, fireplace, and other operable dampers unless they are used to depressurize the building,
(3) measuring and recording the indoor and outdoor temperatures at the beginning and the end of the test
and averaging the values, (4) connecting the blower door assembly to the building envelope, using an
exterior door opening and installing the pressure measuring device across the building envelope, (5) setting
the zero flow position for pressure measurements if a damper is used to control airflow, (6) measuring zero
flow pressures with the fan opening blocked, both before and after the flow measurements, (7) collecting
eight data points with an induced pressure difference ranging from 10 to 60 Pa, using increments of 5 - 10
Pa across the range, (8) measuring airflow rate (CFM) and the pressure differences across the envelope at
each pressure difference, and (9) calculating the Air Changes per Hour at 50 Pascal pressure difference.
Zero-flow pressures were recorded before and after the test with the fan opening sealed. Eight data points
were collected over an induced pressure range of 10 to 60 Pa, with approximately 6 Pa increments,
consistent with the ASTM-required 5–10 Pa spacing. In addition, ANSI/RESNET/ICC 380-2019 specifies
a minimum induced pressure difference of 15 Pa for a valid test, which was achieved in all measurements.

9.2 DUCT BLASTER TESTING

Duct leakage testing was performed in accordance with ASTM E1554-13 [29]. Prior to testing, all supply
registers and return grilles were temporarily sealed using tape and plastic film to isolate the duct system. A
calibrated Series B Minneapolis Duct Blaster fan, manufactured by The Energy Conservatory, was
connected to the duct system at the air handler cabinet. Airflow and pressure measurements were recorded
using a digital manometer (Model DG-3). Pressure taps were installed to measure duct-to-house and house-
to-outdoor pressure differentials (Figure 16).

ASTM E1554 defines four alternative test methods depending on the measurement objective. Method A
(pressure subtraction method) determines duct leakage to the outdoors by combining duct pressurization
with whole-building blower door testing and subtracting envelope leakage effects. Method B (split-system
fan pressurization) measures leakage to the outdoors by simultaneously pressurizing the duct system and
controlling the house pressure relative to outdoors, such that the ducts and indoor space are maintained at
the same pressure; under this condition, measured airflow represents leakage only to the outside. Method
C is a modification of Method B that incorporates additional pressure measurements to improve accuracy
under certain field conditions. Methods A, B, and C are intended to quantify leakage to the outdoors, which
directly affects energy performance and system efficiency. Method D measures total duct leakage by
pressurizing the duct system to 25 Pa without distinguishing between leakage to indoors and leakage to
outdoors. This method is commonly used for code compliance and comparative studies because of its
simplicity and repeatability.

In this study, D was employed, and results are reported as airflow at 25 Pa (CFM₂₅). The 25 Pa reference
pressure is specified by ASTM E1554 to ensure repeatable and comparable leakage measurements. Baseline
pressures were recorded prior to fan operation to correct for ambient pressure differences.

10.3 POWER OUTAGE ASSESSMENT

Planned power outage studies were conducted on the unoccupied MH to determine the impacts of losing
power. For this assessment, peak energy use and energy consumption were measured during building
temperature recovery.

High-efficiency HVAC upgrades such as WSCs are of interest because they can reduce peak demand and
improve grid stability. A particularly challenging grid condition occurs immediately after power restoration
during extreme cold, when many buildings simultaneously call for heat. High-efficiency HVAC upgrades
like WSCs can reduce post-outage peaks. When many homes restore power at once and call for full heating
simultaneously, a much higher coincident peak is produced.

An unplanned outage at the Oak Ridge test site on November 10 lasted just over 4 hours and allowed indoor
temperatures to drop to approximately 56°F. Three installed WSCs restored indoor conditions to setpoint
in under 2 hours, with a measured peak apparent power of 2,224 W.

Next, a planned 9-hour heat shutdown at an average outdoor temperature near 20 ∘ F used four 1,500 W
electric resistance heaters to represent a non-upgraded home. The heaters returned the space to setpoint in
3 hours, with a peak power draw of 5,424 W, as illustrated in Figure 22 and Table 9.

 11. CONCLUSION

Baseline testing of the 1990 MH facility shows that envelope performance is highly assembly-dependent
and that air leakage is a dominant deficiency. Field-integrated R-values indicate the walls performed near
calculated (18.5–19.5 hr·ft²·°F/Btu vs. ~19.2 calculated), while the floor performed far below calculated
(10.1–12.8 vs. ~20.4), consistent with observed belly/membrane failure and displaced fiberglass insulation
that likely enabled convective bypass. Ceiling R-values were most representative during winter (12.0–12.7
hr·ft²·°F/Btu) and became variable in spring as temperature gradients weakened and transient attic/solar
effects increased. Sheathing RH trends across wall orientations were broadly similar and generally showed
drying as temperatures increased, while floor sheathing exhibited wider RH swings consistent with the
compromised underfloor assembly.

 30

Blower door testing confirmed the home is extremely leaky at 18.1 ACH50. Sealing vents and the central
HVAC distribution system reduced leakage to 14.8 ACH50 (about an 18% reduction), indicating
duct/distribution pathways contribute meaningfully to overall infiltration, whereas tape-based DIY duct
fixes produced only a modest whole-house improvement (16.8–17.3 ACH50). Winter operation and outage-
recovery testing also suggest WSC units can materially reduce heating energy and peak demand compared
to electric resistance heating, with measured winter COPs of roughly 2.5–3.08 and substantially lower
recovery peaks in outage scenarios. Overall, central-system-related leakage was significant, but simple
tape-based fixes had limited impact on whole-house airtightness.

Winter outage-recovery tests indicate WSCs can restore comfort with lower peak demand and lower energy
use. Three WSCs recovered the home in under 2 hours after an outage with a 2,224 W peak, and a mixed
setup (two WSCs plus two resistance heaters) restored setpoint in 2.5 hours while using 46% less energy in
recovery than resistance-only heating – the most common space heating method in MHs at 60% of the
market. The results establish a clear baseline for retrofit evaluation, highlighting underfloor repairs and
comprehensive air-sealing as key envelope opportunities and WSCs as a practical alternative or supplement
to ducted systems in leaky MHs, with potential benefits for both occupants and grid peak loads.

 12. FUTURE WORK

The next phase of this study will continue to collect summer measured data, develop a detailed EnergyPlus
model of the MH, compare simulated outputs with the measured dataset, and calibrate the model to ensure
it reproduces observed envelope and HVAC performance. Once calibrated, the model will be extended to
evaluate performance across multiple weather conditions (e.g., Baltimore, Tampa, Houston) and alternative
heating configurations, including natural gas, electric resistance, and window space conditioners.

In parallel, the calibrated model will be used to assess a set of DIY and contractor-installed retrofit
strategies, including window films, added roof/ceiling insulation, and roof-coating options with varying
solar reflectance. These measures will be evaluated across the selected weather conditions to quantify
energy and peak-demand impacts and to identify the most cost-effective retrofit packages with the shortest
payback periods for each location. After identifying cost-effective retrofit packages for equipment and the
building envelope, we will experimentally test them at the facility
