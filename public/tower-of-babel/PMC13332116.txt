Reprogramming macrophage metabolism for cardiovascular therapy: From molecular pathways to precision nanomedicine
Authors: Huijun Guo, Yuji Xie, Yihui Wang, Yuan Song, Tian Peng, Junmin Zhang, Jing Liu, Ying Xu, Wenqian Wu, Jing Wang, Yali Yang, Li Zhang, Tang Gao, Mingxing Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCardiovascular diseases (CVDs) remain the leading cause of mortality worldwide, largely driven by chronic inflammation and inadequate tissue repair. As central orchestrators of immune homeostasis, macrophages undergo profound metabolic reprogramming that dictates whether inflammation is sustained or resolved. Glycolysis fuels the pro-inflammatory phenotype, whereas oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) and fatty acid oxidation (FAO) support the reparative state. These metabolic transitions are governed by signaling pathways such as mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR), nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB), peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor γ (PPAR-γ)/liver X receptor (LXR), and nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor (NRF2), and further stabilized by epigenetic modifications that imprint long-term immunometabolic memory. This review integrates advances in signaling, metabolic, and epigenetic regulation of macrophage reprogramming in CVDs, with a focus on therapeutic strategies that modulate these axes. Particular attention is given to nanomedicine-based delivery systems that enable precise, controlled, and multi-level regulation of macrophage metabolism, overcoming the limitations of conventional therapies. By bridging macrophage immunometabolism with nanoscale intervention, this work outlines a framework for developing macrophage-centered therapeutics aimed at resolving inflammation, stabilizing atherosclerotic lesions, and enhancing cardiac repair, thereby advancing the translation of precision immunometabolic therapies in cardiovascular medicine.Keywords: Macrophage metabolic reprogramming, Cardiovascular diseases therapy, Immunometabolism, Epigenetic regulation

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) remain the leading cause of global morbidity and mortality, with deaths projected to exceed 23.6 million by 2030 [1]. Standard therapies, including antiplatelet, lipid-lowering, and anti-inflammatory treatments, have improved cardiovascular care. However, outcomes in atherosclerosis, myocardial infarction (MI), and heart failure (HF) remain suboptimal due to recurrent ischemic injury and maladaptive remodeling [2].

Macrophage-driven inflammation is a central feature of these disorders. Macrophages accumulate in atherosclerotic plaques and injured myocardium, where their functional plasticity influences inflammation resolution and tissue repair [3]. Dysregulated macrophage responses exacerbate adverse remodeling and contribute to heart failure [2,3]. Consequently, macrophages represent an attractive therapeutic target in CVDs.

A key determinant of macrophage plasticity is metabolic reprogramming [4], which links local cues to effector functions by reshaping energy utilization and biosynthesis. Core pathways include glycolysis, the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), fatty acid oxidation (FAO), and glutamine metabolism [5,6]. Traditional frameworks distinguish M1 macrophages (e.g., CD80, CD86, CD16/32) from M2 macrophages (e.g., Arg1, CD206; IL-10) [[7], [8], [9]]. In vivo, macrophage states form a dynamic continuum governed by integrated metabolic, signaling, and epigenetic programs, which also contribute to immunometabolic memory and trained immunity [10].

To illustrate this continuous spectrum of in vivo macrophage activation, atherosclerosis provides a prime example. Recruited monocytes internalize oxidized low-density lipoproteins (oxLDL) and inflammatory signals within the arterial intima, undergoing metabolic adaptation as they transition to lipid-laden foam cells [11]. Early-stage cells rely heavily on glycolysis for acute inflammatory responses, while later-stage foam cells adjust their metabolism to manage lipid toxicity and oxidative stress, producing mixed phenotypes such as Mox and Mhem macrophages [12,13].

Conventional drugs often lack the spatial and temporal precision required to effectively modulate these pathways, and systemic administration may lead to off-target toxicity or limited macrophage specificity [14,15]. Nanomedicine platforms enable targeted delivery, controlled release, and combination payloads, providing a translationally actionable framework for stage-specific and macrophage-focused interventions.

In this review, we summarize recent advances in macrophage metabolic reprogramming for cardiovascular therapy (Fig. 1). We first outline key mechanisms as drivers, executors, and stabilizers, subsequently discussing pharmacological and nanomedicine-based strategies that target these nodes, before finally highlighting translational challenges and future directions. The drivers-executors-stabilizers framework proposed in this review serves not merely as a conceptual classification scheme, but as a practical blueprint for therapeutic design. Unlike the useful but simplified M1/M2 model, which mainly describes macrophage phenotypes, this framework organizes macrophage metabolic reprogramming according to causal and therapeutic hierarchy. Specifically, drivers represent upstream signaling pathways that initiate reprogramming; executors denote the metabolic fluxes and redox programs that implement macrophage function; and stabilizers comprise the epigenetic and metabolic memory mechanisms that dictate the durability of these states. This structure effectively links molecular mechanisms with optimal therapeutic timing, target selection, and delivery strategies. Ultimately, we propose that macrophage metabolic reprogramming represents a clinically actionable axis, and that nanomedicine provides the essential tools to translate this concept into next-generation cardiovascular immunotherapies.

Mechanistic basis of macrophage metabolic reprogramming

Macrophage metabolic reprogramming is governed by coordinated signaling, metabolic, and epigenetic networks that collectively shape pro-inflammatory versus reparative functions in CVDs. Core signaling axes include mTOR [10], NF-κB [16], peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor γ (PPAR-γ)/liver X receptor (LXR) [17], and nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor (NRF2) [18]. These interact with metabolic circuits such as glycolysis, the TCA cycle [19], OXPHOS, and FAO [20]. Concurrently, epigenetic regulators, including DNA and histone modifiers, can stabilize transcriptional programs and contribute to immunometabolic memory [21]. Together, these interconnected layers form the mechanistic basis of macrophage metabolic reprogramming in CVDs.

To clarify the added value of this framework, we benchmarked it against commonly used macrophage classification models (Table S1). The M1/M2 model provides a simple phenotypic orientation but cannot capture the continuous and mixed macrophage states prevalent in cardiovascular lesions. Similarly, pathway-centered models thoroughly describe glycolysis, the TCA cycle, OXPHOS, FAO, and ROS handling, yet they often conceptually isolate these metabolic routes from their upstream signals and downstream memory formation. While single-cell transcriptomic models effectively reveal macrophage heterogeneity, they frequently remain descriptive rather than providing directly actionable therapeutic targets. In contrast, the drivers-executors-stabilizers framework links microenvironmental cues, signaling initiation, metabolic execution, and epigenetic memory. It also helps match therapeutic strategy to biological purpose: rapid signaling modulation through drivers, metabolic flux correction through executors, and durable memory resetting through stabilizers.

These drivers-executors-stabilizers tiers do not function as isolated pathways. Instead, they interact through feedback and feed-forward regulation. For example, upstream drivers such as mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) and nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) increase glycolytic flux and promote succinate accumulation [22,23]. Succinate then enhances mitochondrial reactive oxygen species generation and hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α) stabilization, forming a succinate-reactive oxygen species-HIF-1α amplification loop that reinforces inflammatory signaling [24,25]. Executor metabolites also influence the stabilizer layer. Acetyl-CoA supports histone acetylation, α-ketoglutarate (α-KG) acts as a cofactor for DNA and histone demethylation, whereas succinate and fumarate can inhibit α-ketoglutarate-dependent dioxygenases [26]. Lactate can also contribute to histone lactylation. Through these interactions, short-term metabolic changes can reshape chromatin accessibility and support the persistence or reversibility of macrophage functional states [27]. Acknowledging these multidimensional interactive loops is imperative, as successful therapeutic interventions must anticipate and modulate these interconnected cascades rather than targeting single nodes in isolation.

Drivers: Signal pathways

Signaling cascades drive macrophage metabolic reprogramming and provide actionable targets in CVDs (Fig. 2). Among them, mTOR signaling integrates phosphoinositide 3-kinase/protein kinase B (PI3K/Akt) inputs and engages downstream nodes including TNF receptor associated factor 6 (TRAF6) and NF-κB. Through these effectors, mTOR promotes glycolytic programs and pro-inflammatory activation [22,[28], [29], [30]]. Conversely, PPAR-γ and LXR can dampen mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling and support lipid-handling programs [31], whereas the AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) restrains mTOR [28], while enhancing extracellular signal-regulated kinase 5 (ERK5)-NRF2-linked antioxidant responses [28,32], and signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) favors oxidative metabolism and reparative polarization [33]. Collectively, mTOR, NF-κB, PPAR-γ/LXR, and NRF2 represent major signaling axes that connect microenvironmental cues to macrophage metabolism and function in CVDs.

mTOR pathway

mTOR integrates upstream inputs to coordinate macrophage metabolism and inflammation, and its effects are context-dependent across cardiovascular lesions. In atherosclerotic plaques, macrophages exposed to oxidized lipids, cholesterol crystals and hypoxia often show enhanced mTOR activity, which supports glycolytic bias and pro-inflammatory programs and can contribute to trained immunity [22,[34], [35], [36]]. In contrast, after myocardial infarction, reparative macrophage functions are supported by mitochondrial metabolism, including OXPHOS and FAO [[37], [38], [39]], together with lysosomal and autophagy programs regulated by transcription factor EB (TFEB) [40]. These observations indicate that mTOR network can promote inflammatory activation in plaques while supporting repair-associated functions in post-infarction settings, depending on lesion stage and niche signals.

Mechanistically, PI3K/Akt signaling engages the mTOR pathway and downstream mediators including TRAF6 and NF-κB to promote glycolysis and limit oxidative metabolism, thereby favoring pro-inflammatory activation [22,[28], [29], [30]]. In plaques, this program is reinforced by hypoxia and lipid stress, driving epigenetic remodeling that sustains inflammatory gene expression [22,[34], [35], [36]]. Interferon regulatory factor 5 (IRF5) is a key transcription factor for pro-inflammatory macrophages [41]. The pro-inflammatory phenotype induced by IRF5 is tightly coupled with the Akt2-mammalian target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1)-glycolytic axis. In vivo, targeted suppression of IRF5 shifts recruited monocyte-derived macrophages toward reparative programs, consistent with reduced inflammatory output and improved tissue repair after myocardial infarction [42]. Additionally, pharmacological inhibition of dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP-4) by gliptins (e.g., sitagliptin) has also been reported to attenuate inflammatory macrophage programs and promote plaque-stabilizing phenotypes [43].

Conversely, TFEB acts as a downstream effector to restore lipid metabolism and limit inflammation, thereby attenuating post-infarction remodeling [40]. The activation of TFEB within macrophages enhances the capacity of resident and infiltrating cardiac macrophages to clear necrotic cardiomyocytes and lipid debris [39,40], while simultaneously driving a metabolic shift toward mitochondrial oxidation. In atherosclerosis and myocardial infarction, excessive mTORC1 activation drives glycolysis [22], inhibits autophagy [44], and promotes pro-inflammatory macrophage polarization; whereas its suppression enhances reparative macrophage polarization, angiogenesis, and tissue repair [29,40]. Thus, targeting mTOR signaling provides a promising metabolic strategy for cardiovascular therapy.

NF-κB pathway

NF-κB is a central transcriptional regulator that couples inflammatory signaling to metabolic reprogramming in CVDs. Sustained NF-κB activation promotes cytokine production, foam-cell formation, and plaque instability across multiple CVD contexts [[45], [46], [47]]. In atherosclerotic plaques, RNA methylation machinery such as methyltransferase-like 14 (METTL14), can enhance NF-κB/interleukin-6 (NF-κB/IL-6) signaling efficiency, thereby reinforcing inflammatory transcriptional programs and associated metabolic remodeling [45]. This regulatory layer supports a shift away from mitochondrial oxidative metabolism toward glycolysis-dominant inflammatory states and contributes to lesion progression.

At the mechanistic level, NF-κB directly controls macrophage metabolic fate. Upon activation, p50/p65 heterodimers bind κB elements to induce interleukin-1β (IL-1β), IL-6, and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), while concurrently regulating genes involved in glucose and glutamine metabolism [48]. NF-κB activation favors glycolytic flux by upregulating rate-limiting enzymes such as 6-phosphofructo-2-kinase/fructose-2,6-bisphosphatase 3 (PFKFB3) and hexokinase 2 (HK2), while suppressing OXPHOS and FAO programs. This process is metabolically reinforced by glycolysis-derived ATP, which facilitates IκBα degradation, and by lactate accumulation, which further amplifies NF-κB activity, creating a feed-forward inflammatory metabolic loop [49].

In cardiovascular lesions, receptor-mediated inputs converge on this axis. CD36-dependent lipid uptake and Toll-like receptor 4-myeloid differentiation primary response 88 (TLR4-MyD88) signaling activate NF-κB and perturb fatty acid metabolism in plaque-resident macrophages [[50], [51], [52], [53], [54]]. Upstream, inhibitor of κB kinase β (IKKβ) and TRAF6-dependent signaling promotes mitochondrial and NOX-derived reactive oxygen species production, further sustaining inflammatory programs [55,56]. Similar NF-κB-dependent glycolytic activation is observed in ischemia-reperfusion injury, where damage-associated molecular patterns induce transient glycolytic bursts that enhance chemokine expression and monocyte recruitment. Conversely, NF-κB inhibition reduces monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), intercellular adhesion molecule-1 (ICAM-1), and vascular cell adhesion molecule-1 (VCAM-1) expression. It also restores oxidative metabolism and biases macrophages toward reparative phenotypes in both plaque and myocardial settings [57]. Collectively, these findings establish NF-κB as a key metabolic inflammatory integrator in cardiovascular macrophages and a rational therapeutic target.

PPAR-γ pathway

The STAT family cooperates with nuclear receptors to coordinate macrophage metabolism and functional polarization. Signal transducer and activator of transcription 1 (STAT1), activated downstream of interferon-γ (IFN-γ), promotes glycolytic reprogramming by increasing lactate production, stabilizing HIF-1α, and inducing key glycolytic enzymes such as PFKFB3 and lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA), thereby supporting pro-inflammatory macrophage states [56,58]. In contrast, STAT3 activation by interleukin-6 (IL-6) and STAT6 activation by interleukin-4 (IL-4) or interleukin-13 (IL-13) favor a reparative metabolic program characterized by enhanced FAO and OXPHOS [38,[59], [60], [61], [62]]. Mechanistically, STAT6 induces the expression of carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1A (CPT1A), acyl-CoA oxidase 1 (ACOX1), and the transcriptional coactivators PPAR-γ coactivator-1 (PGC-1α and PGC-1β), which synergize with PPARα and nuclear respiratory factor-1 to promote mitochondrial biogenesis and oxidative metabolism [38].

Downstream of STAT signaling, the PPAR-γ/LXR axis plays a central role in lipid handling and foam-cell biology. PPAR-γ, together with LXR, transcriptionally regulates the cholesterol transporters ATP-Binding Cassette Transporter A1 (ABCA1) and ATP-Binding Cassette Transporter G1 (ABCG1), thereby enhancing cholesterol efflux, limiting lipid accumulation, and restraining foam-cell formation in atherosclerotic macrophages [63]. This lipid-centric metabolic program counterbalances pro-inflammatory glycolytic signaling and supports macrophage phenotypes associated with plaque stability and tissue repair.

Importantly, the balance among these STAT-driven metabolic programs is highly context-dependent across CVDs. In the acute hypoxic and necrotic environment of myocardial infarction, transient STAT1-HIF-1α-PFKFB3-mediated glycolytic activation facilitates rapid pro-inflammatory responses and debris clearance [64]. By contrast, in the chronic lipid-rich milieu of atherosclerosis, sustained STAT6-PGC-1-PPARα signaling and intact OXPHOS/FAO act as metabolic constraints that limit foam-cell formation and promote reparative macrophage functions [38,62,65]. These observations highlight how cardiovascular macrophages dynamically adapt their metabolic trajectories in response to disease-specific microenvironments.

Pharmacological modulation further supports the therapeutic relevance of this axis. Agents that influence this axis can increase ABCA1 expression, suppress MAPK signaling, reduce glycolytic flux, and attenuate MCP-1 production, thereby limiting atherosclerotic progression [66,67]. At the transcriptional level, CCAAT/enhancer-binding protein delta (C/EBPδ) and triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 2 (Trem2) represent opposing regulators of foam-cell metabolism. C/EBPδ (simvastatin) enhances CD36 and scavenger receptor expression while suppressing mitochondrial FAO and cholesterol efflux pathways, driving lipid accumulation and pro-inflammatory metabolism [66]. In contrast, Trem2 activation restores mitochondrial oxidative capacity, enhances ATP-binding cassette transporter A1/ATP-binding cassette sub-family G member 1 (ABCA1/ABCG1)-mediated cholesterol efflux via LXR signaling, and engages Syk-PI3K-Akt-mTOR pathways to support macrophage survival under lipid stress [67]. Together, these findings indicate that PPAR-γ/LXR-centered metabolic programs provide a druggable framework to redirect macrophages from glycolytic, lipid-laden states toward oxidative, reparative phenotypes in CVDs.

NRF2 pathway

NRF2 is a central regulator of redox balance and metabolic adaptation in macrophages within CVDs. In lipid- and cholesterol-rich environments such as the necrotic core of atherosclerotic plaques, oxidized lipids and cholesterol crystals disrupt the kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1-cullin 3 (Keap1-Cul3) complex, permitting sustained NRF2 nuclear translocation [32,68]. NRF2 activation induces antioxidant and glutathione biosynthesis programs, suppresses p38/c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK)-MAPK-driven glycolytic gene expression, including PFKFB3 and HK2, and supports mitochondrial oxidative metabolism.

NRF2 function is closely coupled to AMPK signaling. AMPK activation promotes reparative macrophage polarization by enhancing OXPHOS and FAO [69], while metformin-induced AMPK activation upregulates heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1) and improves lipid handling. Under ischemic or hypoxia-reperfusion stress, NRF2 may cooperate with the AMPK-PGC-1α axis to induce CPT1A, ACOX1, and mitochondrial biogenesis genes. This synergy enables macrophages to maintain OXPHOS/FAO-dominant metabolic programs in infarct border zones and during heart failure remodeling [[70], [71], [72]]. In contrast, miR-33 suppresses ABCA1/ABCG1 expression, weakening cholesterol efflux and antioxidant defenses within the NRF2-LXR-PPAR-γ network, which predisposes macrophages to glycolysis-driven inflammatory amplification during atherosclerosis [18,73].

NRF2 activity is further constrained by regulatory checkpoints such as ERK5 phosphorylation. Conversely, pharmacological activators, including curcuminoids and dimethyl fumarate, can enhance mitochondrial oxidative metabolism and antioxidant capacity [32,71,74]. NRF2 also mediates the protective effects of neuropeptide Y and HO-1-modified macrophages during ischemia-reperfusion injury by limiting oxidative stress and preserving mitochondrial integrity [75,76].

Importantly, NRF2 function exhibits tissue- and lineage-specific effects. Cardiac resident macrophages may display intrinsically higher oxidative and antioxidant capacity under homeostasis, where NRF2 activation further supports mitochondrial stability [72,77]. In contrast, monocyte-derived macrophages infiltrating plaques or infarcted myocardium rely more heavily on NRF2 to counter lipid overload and hypoxic stress [72,78]. In these pro-inflammatory environments, excessive ROS not only causes cellular injury but also impairs cholesterol efflux and disrupts glycolytic dependence, forming a positive feedback loop that promotes foam-cell formation and pro-inflammatory persistence [18,79].

Despite its broadly protective roles, NRF2 exerts context-dependent effects in atherosclerosis. In oxLDL-rich plaques, NRF2 activation can upregulate scavenger receptors such as CD36, thereby enhancing lipid uptake and potentially contributing to foam-cell accumulation [80,81]. These observations indicate that NRF2-mediated antioxidant programs do not uniformly promote plaque stabilization. Consequently, therapeutic targeting of NRF2 requires precise modulation rather than indiscriminate activation, with outcomes determined by activation intensity, metabolic background, and lesion stage.

Context-dependent “double-edged” effects and comparative therapeutic relevance of major signaling pathways

While modulating macrophage metabolic pathways presents a promising therapeutic avenue for CVDs, major signaling targets should not be interpreted as uniformly beneficial or harmful. The therapeutic outcomes of activating or inhibiting a specific pathway are highly context-dependent and vary according to disease stage, lesion composition, macrophage lineage, intervention timing, and delivery route (Table S3). Therefore, therapeutic relevance should be assessed through a context-specific framework rather than a universal rank order.

NRF2 illustrates this principle clearly. As discussed above, NRF2 activation can enhance antioxidant defense, reduce oxidative stress, and support reparative macrophage programs [82]. However, in oxidized lipid-rich atherosclerotic lesions, NRF2 may also promote the expression of scavenger receptors such as CD36, thereby facilitating oxidized low-density lipoprotein (oxLDL) uptake and potentially contributing to foam-cell formation [80,81]. Similarly, PPAR-γ/LXR activation promotes the resolution of inflammation and cholesterol efflux through ABCA1/ABCG1, supporting lipid clearance and plaque stabilization [83]. Nevertheless, in advanced lipid-engorged plaques, excessive or nonselective activation of lipid-handling pathways may become maladaptive if lipid uptake exceeds efflux capacity, increasing the risk of intracellular lipid accumulation, lipotoxic stress, and macrophage dysfunction [84,85].

A similar spatiotemporal dichotomy applies to mTOR signaling. In atherosclerotic plaques, inhibition of excessive mTORC1 activity can enhance autophagy, reduce glycolysis-driven inflammatory activation, and limit lipid stress, thereby supporting plaque stabilization [86]. Preclinical models also suggest that suppressing mTORC1-driven inflammatory glycolysis may improve post-myocardial infarction repair under selected conditions [87,88]. However, systemic or poorly timed mTOR inhibition may impair macrophage survival, lysosomal function, efferocytosis, and repair-phase immune remodeling. Consistent with this translational complexity, systemic everolimus did not reduce infarct size in the CLEVER-ACS trial [89], and nonselective mTOR inhibition may also compromise mTORC2-dependent protective functions [90]. Thus, mTOR modulation is therapeutically attractive but requires lesion-specific and stage-matched control.

NF-κB and HIF-related pathways also exhibit double-edged properties. NF-κB inhibition can suppress inflammatory cytokine production, reduce glycolytic amplification, and attenuate macrophage-driven tissue injury [91,92]. However, broad or long-term NF-κB suppression may compromise host defense, phagocytosis, apoptotic cell clearance, and the early inflammatory response required for debris removal after myocardial injury [93]. HIF-1α inhibition may reduce glycolysis-driven inflammatory activation under hypoxic or lipid-stressed conditions, but excessive suppression of HIF signaling could impair macrophage adaptation to hypoxia and disturb the delicate balance between the initial pro-inflammatory response and the subsequent reparative transition [94].

These examples indicate that major signaling pathways differ not only in molecular function but also in their therapeutic windows and translational feasibility. For short-term control of excessive inflammatory activation, NF-κB may be a high-priority target if inhibition is transient and locally restricted [92,93]. For chronic plaque progression, mTOR may be particularly relevant because it links inflammatory glycolysis, autophagy, lysosomal function, and lipid stress, but its clinical utility depends on lesion-selective delivery and avoidance of systemic suppression [86,90,95,96]. NRF2 and PPAR-γ/LXR are especially relevant in redox- and lipid-stressed macrophage niches [97,98], but their effects require careful calibration in advanced oxidized lipid-rich plaques where CD36-mediated lipid uptake and foam-cell formation may dominate [80,99]. HIF-related signaling is most relevant in hypoxic plaques and infarct border zones, but isoform specificity and intervention timing are critical [91,94].

Therefore, the therapeutic priority of each pathway depends on the clinical objective. Acute inflammatory flares may require transient, localized control of NF-κB or HIF-related signaling; chronic plaque stabilization may benefit from stage-matched modulation of mTOR, NRF2, and PPAR-γ/LXR; and post-infarction repair requires preservation of macrophage plasticity, efferocytosis, and mitochondrial recovery. This context-dependent hierarchy supports the need for spatiotemporally controlled delivery systems. Nanomedicine-based platforms may be particularly valuable because they can restrict metabolic interventions to defined pathological microenvironments, reduce systemic exposure, and align pathway modulation with disease stage and macrophage functional state.

Executors: Core metabolic programs

While signaling pathways initiate macrophage polarization, metabolic programs execute these instructions by reallocating carbon fluxes, modulating redox balance, and adjusting mitochondrial output to meet functional demands within cardiovascular lesions. In practice, macrophages do not switch “on or off” a single pathway; instead, they rewire coupled modules including glycolysis, the TCA cycle, OXPHOS, FAO, and ROS handling to match local constraints such as hypoxia, lipid overload, and tissue damage. These executors determine the immediate phenotype by controlling cytokine production, efferocytic capacity, and biosynthetic supply. Furthermore, they generate metabolic intermediates that feed forward to reinforce signaling cascades and prime the epigenetic layer for long-term immunometabolic memory. Therefore, comprehensively understanding macrophage metabolic reprogramming in CVDs requires defining not only which pathways change, but also how metabolic fluxes are redistributed and coordinated across mitochondria-centered checkpoints within disease-specific microenvironments.

Macrophage metabolic pathways

Macrophage functions are executed through distinct but interconnected metabolic programs (Fig. 3). In inflammatory settings, macrophages preferentially increase aerobic glycolysis, pentose phosphate pathway (PPP) activity, and lipid synthesis. At the same time, the TCA cycle becomes functionally rewired, with altered flux through isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH) and succinate dehydrogenase (SDH)-linked steps [100]. This rewiring promotes citrate and succinate accumulation. Succinate stabilizes HIF-1α and sustains IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α production, thereby aggravating vascular injury [11,101,102]. Mechanistically, glucose transporter 1 (GLUT1)-dependent glucose influx and succinate-driven reverse electron transport (RET) amplify ROS generation and NF-κB activation in C-C motif chemokine receptor 2 (CCR2+) macrophage-enriched contexts, creating a feed-forward loop that reinforces inflammatory transcriptional output [[103], [104], [105]]. Consistent with this logic, blocking glycolytic control nodes (e.g., PFKFB3) limits inflammatory activation and biases macrophages toward reparative programs across cardiovascular-relevant disease models [25,106]. In contrast, macrophages engaged in resolution and repair tend to preserve an intact TCA cycle and maintain OXPHOS, with FAO providing sustained substrates for energy production and reparative functions [107,108].

A key functional implication of this immunometabolic wiring is that metabolic preference and phenotype usually move in the same direction: increased glycolytic flux and lactate production often accompany heightened pro-inflammatory cytokine output, whereas strengthening mitochondrial oxidation supports reparative behaviors. In advanced plaques, glucose is inefficiently routed into mitochondrial ATP production, with increased glucose uptake and lactate generation reflecting a glycolysis-biased state [101]. In cardiomyopathy and related stress contexts, macrophages can also increase glycolysis to meet rapid energy and biosynthetic demands, which frequently coincides with a pro-inflammatory transcriptional program [11]. In vivo, HIF signaling links metabolic state to disease outcome: HIF-1α promotes aortic dissection progression and facilitates Mer tyrosine kinase (MerTK) cleavage via glycolytic reprogramming [109]. In contrast, HIF-2α suppresses anti-inflammatory mitochondrial metabolism, supporting the concept that HIF-1α-centered glycolytic control represents a tractable target in vascular inflammation [102,109].

Redox output is a major executor linking metabolic flux to inflammatory signaling in macrophages. In acute myocardial infarction, mitochondrial electron leakage from complexes I/III contributes to superoxide generation, whereas in atherosclerotic plaques, NADPH oxidase 2 (NOX2) is a major reactive oxygen species (ROS) source in inflammatory macrophages. NADPH oxidase 4 (NOX4) and nitric oxide synthase (NOS) uncoupling are more closely associated with fibrotic remodeling, ischemic myocardium, and heart failure contexts [105,110]. These ROS signals are not merely injurious byproducts. In glycolysis-biased macrophages, GLUT1-dependent glucose uptake and PFKFB3-supported glycolytic flux can amplify NOX2 activity and mitochondrial stress, thereby enhancing nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) activation and interleukin-1β/interleukin-6/tumor necrosis factor-α (IL-1β/IL-6/TNF-α) expression. Endogenous antioxidant systems, including superoxide dismutase (SOD), glutathione-dependent detoxification, and nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (NRF2)-driven programs, restrain this inflammatory redox amplification [105,110].

Among mitochondrial nodes, the succinate-SDH-Complex I axis is particularly relevant to macrophage inflammatory metabolism. Inflammatory stimuli disrupt tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle flux, resulting in a profound accumulation of succinate [25]. Oxidation of succinate by SDH increases electron pressure within the ubiquinone pool and can drive RET at Complex I, leading to mitochondrial ROS (mtROS) generation [111]. This mtROS output stabilizes HIF-1α and reinforces NF-κB/HIF-1α-dependent inflammatory cytokine programs. In contrast, reparative macrophages tend to preserve forward electron transport, OXPHOS, and FAO-OXPHOS coupling, thereby limiting excessive RET-mtROS signaling while supporting ATP generation, efferocytosis, and tissue repair [112]. Thus, SDH-linked succinate oxidation and Complex I RET represent a functional immunometabolic checkpoint rather than merely part of baseline electron transport chain physiology.

Succinate reinforces inflammatory signaling, whereas α-ketoglutarate supports reparative epigenetic remodeling to reparative programs by facilitating the epigenetic induction of anti-inflammatory genes. Inflammatory “TCA rewiring” is also reinforced by altered anaplerosis. Specifically, the disruption of IDH leads to reduced α-ketoglutarate (α-KG) formation, while the shunting of glutamine-derived carbons results in succinate accumulation, which intensifies pro-inflammatory signaling through augmented SDH activity and subsequent HIF-1α stabilization [113]. Conversely, the nuclear receptor Nur77 orchestrates an anti-inflammatory metabolic profile by maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis and limiting the excessive accumulation of succinate and other TCA-derived inflammatory intermediates [19]. Furthermore, impaired mitochondrial NAD+ transport can create a metabolic 'breakpoint' that favors the production of itaconate. In the context of myocardial remodeling, metabolic cues released by TREM2-expressing macrophages can influence fibroblast activation, whereas therapeutic enhancement of TREM2 signaling promotes a switch toward oxidative metabolism, thereby improving cardiac functional recovery [114].

OXPHOS regulation also interacts with arginine metabolism and RNA-mediated metabolic control. Although mitochondrial respiration depends on coordinated electron transport chain activity, inflammatory and reparative macrophages use this system differently. Inflammatory macrophages show reduced mitochondrial oxidative efficiency and increased glycolytic dependence, whereas reparative macrophages maintain FAO-driven OXPHOS to support sustained repair functions [[115], [116], [117]]. In inflammatory macrophages, inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS)-derived nitric oxide can inhibit Complex IV activity and further suppress OXPHOS, whereas reparative macrophages channel arginine metabolism toward tissue repair. Restoring mitochondrial electron transport capacity and correcting arginine metabolic imbalance may therefore help shift macrophages away from inflammatory programs. MicroRNAs can also reshape this balance by promoting OXPHOS and limiting glycolysis, such as through the regulation of Complex I or III subunits in chronic allograft vasculopathy models [[118], [119], [120]].

FAO is a key fuel source for reparative macrophages and supports sustained anti-inflammatory functions [121]. Beyond ATP supply, FAO derived acetyl-CoA can fuel histone acetylation, providing a direct metabolic route to stabilize reparative transcriptional programs [122]. Consistent with this concept, stimuli that enhance lipolysis and fatty-acid availability support FAO and reparative reprogramming. Hydrogen sulfide (H2S) has been reported to exert cardioprotective effects via such metabolic mechanisms [104], while 13-series resolvin 4 (RvT4) promotes cholesterol efflux and reprograms lipid-laden macrophages toward tissue-protective states [123]. Collectively, inflammatory programs are commonly supported by glycolysis/PPP and TCA rewiring (with succinate-ROS-NF-κB/HIF-1α coupling), whereas intact TCA-OXPHOS-FAO coupling favors resolution and repair, highlighting actionable metabolic nodes for cardiovascular therapy.

Integrative immunometabolic framework

Macrophage metabolism operates as a coupled network rather than isolated pathways. Glycolysis, the TCA cycle, OXPHOS, redox control, and epigenetic regulation continuously influence each other through metabolite availability, cofactor balance, and signaling feedback. This coupling allows macrophages to rapidly adapt to hypoxia, lipid overload, and tissue injury, and to transition between inflammatory and reparative states in a context- and time-dependent manner. Accordingly, mechanistic interpretation in CVDs should focus on how carbon flux and redox output are redistributed across modules, rather than assigning phenotypes to a single pathway.

Because the available studies differ in experimental models, disease stages, stimulation protocols, and metabolic assays, direct numerical pooling of absolute flux values is not feasible in this review. Nevertheless, several recurring readouts, including glucose uptake, extracellular acidification rate (ECAR), oxygen consumption rate (OCR), lactate production, succinate accumulation, mtROS, and FAO/OXPHOS activity allow a comparative interpretation of macrophage metabolic flux across cardiovascular contexts (Table S4).

A central axis in this network is the glycolysis-TCA interface [124]. In inflammatory macrophages, increased glycolysis together with pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase 1 (PDK1) activation and pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH) inhibition reduces pyruvate entry into mitochondria [125], creating a functional “break” in the TCA cycle. This rewiring favors the accumulation of citrate and succinate, particularly when flux linked to IDH and succinate dehydrogenase (SDH) is altered [24,126,127]. Succinate is not only a TCA intermediate but also an inflammatory amplifier: it drives RET at complex I to increase mtROS, stabilizes HIF-1α, and promotes interleukin-1β (IL-1β) expression [124]. In parallel, citrate can be converted into acetyl-CoA via ATP-citrate lyase, thereby supplying substrates for histone acylation reactions that reinforce inflammatory gene accessibility and transcription. Thus, glycolysis-TCA coupling directly links metabolic flux to inflammatory gene programs.

These flux patterns differ across cardiovascular conditions. In early atherosclerotic lesions, recruited monocytes and macrophages exposed to oxidized lipids and inflammatory cytokines commonly show increased glucose uptake and glycolytic activity, together with TCA rewiring and redox stress. As plaques progress and become more lipid-rich and hypoxic, macrophages increasingly face impaired mitochondrial oxidative capacity, lipid overload, defective efferocytosis, and persistent inflammatory signaling. In this setting, the balance between lipid uptake, cholesterol efflux, FAO, and OXPHOS becomes particularly important, because excessive lipid accumulation can reinforce foam-cell formation and macrophage dysfunction rather than resolution.

The flux dynamics in MI are strongly shaped by spatial metabolic heterogeneity. The infarct core and border zone do not provide equivalent metabolic niches for macrophages. In the highly ischemic infarct core, severe oxygen and substrate deprivation may limit mitochondrial respiration and restrict sustained TCA-dependent flux. By contrast, the partially perfused border zone retains residual mitochondrial activity under high oxidative stress [128], which may permit regional succinate-linked redox signaling and inflammatory macrophage accumulation. This spatial distinction provides a tissue-level rationale for why inflammatory activity often peaks in the infarct border zone and suggests that metabolic interventions require spatial and temporal precision to limit persistent inflammation without disrupting early debris clearance [128].

During the post-MI repair phase, macrophage metabolism shifts toward programs that support efferocytosis, extracellular matrix remodeling, angiogenesis, and tissue repair. Compared with acute inflammatory macrophages, reparative macrophages tend to show reduced glycolytic dependence, more intact TCA cycle activity, and increased reliance on FAO-OXPHOS coupling. FAO-derived acetyl-CoA can also support histone acetylation, providing a metabolic route to stabilize reparative transcriptional programs. Thus, the transition from acute inflammation to repair is not simply a switch from one pathway to another, but a coordinated redistribution of glycolytic, mitochondrial, lipid, and epigenetic flux.

TCA metabolites further regulate macrophage state through an epigenetic-metabolic interface. α-Ketoglutarate (α-KG) acts as a co-substrate for ten-eleven translocation (TET) dioxygenases and Jumonji domain-containing protein (JMJD) family demethylases. By promoting these demethylation reactions, α-KG enhances chromatin accessibility and supports anti-inflammatory gene programs [26]. In contrast, succinate and fumarate can competitively inhibit these enzymes, favoring persistence of pro-inflammatory epigenetic states. Itaconate produced during inflammatory activation can promote antioxidant and anti-inflammatory responses by inhibiting Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (KEAP1) and stabilizing NRF2, illustrating how TCA-derived metabolites can either reinforce inflammation or support resolution depending on context [129].

In parallel, impaired OXPHOS elevates mtROS and can activate nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB)-dependent inflammatory transcription, whereas antioxidant programs such as NRF2-driven defenses restrain ROS and indirectly dampen NF-κB output [130]. Finally, FAO-OXPHOS coupling provides durable energy and acetyl-CoA supply to support efferocytosis, tissue repair, and the stabilization of reparative transcriptional programs [26]. Together, these interactions support a unified view: macrophage phenotypes in CVDs are executed by a network in which metabolic flux, mitochondrial redox output, and chromatin state co-evolve, creating both rapid functional shifts and longer-lived immunometabolic memory.

This comparative flux framework has therapeutic implications. In acute injury, excessive early suppression of inflammatory metabolism may impair debris clearance, whereas in chronic plaques, persistent glycolytic and lipid-overload programs may sustain non-resolving inflammation. Therefore, metabolic interventions should be tuned according to disease stage, lesion niche, and macrophage functional state. Spatiotemporally responsive delivery systems, including macrophage-targeted nanomedicines, may be particularly useful because they can restrict metabolic modulation to defined pathological microenvironments.

Macrophage heterogeneity, metabolic programs, and implications for subset-selective delivery

Recent single-cell RNA-sequencing studies have refined the traditional binary classification models by revealing multiple macrophage subpopulations in cardiovascular lesions [39,[131], [132], [133]]. These subsets differ not only in lineage and anatomical niche, but also in metabolic state and therapeutic vulnerability.

Tissue-resident cardiac macrophages, often enriched for markers such as LYVE1 and TIMD4, are generally associated with homeostatic surveillance, efferocytosis, and tissue repair. Their functions are supported by relatively preserved mitochondrial metabolism and oxidative programs [39,134,135]. In contrast, recruited CCR2-positive monocyte-derived macrophages are prominent during early post-infarction inflammation and in active atherosclerotic lesions. These cells are commonly linked to glycolysis-dominant, NF-κB/HIF-1α-associated inflammatory programs and may be suitable targets for transient, lesion-restricted delivery systems designed to attenuate pro-inflammatory output [131,135,136].

TREM2-positive lipid-associated macrophages, often expressing markers such as TREM2, CD9, GPNMB, and SPP1, have been identified in advanced plaques and injured myocardium [131,137,138]. These cells are associated with lipid handling, lysosomal activity, cholesterol metabolism, and context-dependent reparative or stress-adaptive functions. Proliferating macrophages, marked by cell-cycle genes, may contribute to the local expansion of macrophage burden in chronic lesions and are potentially linked to mTOR-dependent anabolic metabolism [96,131,139]. In advanced plaques, lipid-engorged foam-cell macrophages with defective efferocytosis show impaired mitochondrial fitness, oxidative stress, endoplasmic reticulum stress, and lipid overload, which contribute to necrotic core expansion and plaque vulnerability [140,141].

These subset-specific features have direct implications for nanomedicine design (Table S5). Pan-macrophage targeting may not be optimal, because depletion or nonspecific modulation of protective resident macrophages may impair repair. More precise delivery strategies can be built along three complementary axes. First, surface markers such as CCR2, TREM2, CD9, LYVE1, and TIMD4 enable subset-selective targeting. Second, microenvironmental triggers, low pH, reactive oxygen species, or lipid-rich niches, confer lesion specificity. Third, payloads can be matched to metabolic vulnerabilities, including glycolysis restraint, cholesterol efflux enhancement, mitochondrial rescue, and efferocytosis promotion [142].

Taken together, single-cell omics has reframed cardiovascular macrophage biology from a binary M1/M2 model into a multidimensional landscape of co-existing subsets, each with distinct metabolic vulnerabilities and therapeutic opportunities. The next generation of cardiovascular nanomedicine should move beyond pan-macrophage targeting toward subset-selective delivery platforms. By exploiting subset-specific surface markers (CCR2, TREM2, CD9, LYVE1, TIMD4), microenvironmental triggers (pH, ROS, lipid abundance), or metabolic dependencies (mTORC1, FAO, efferocytic clearance), these platforms can enrich therapeutic action in pathogenic pro-inflammatory populations while preserving the crucial functions of reparative resident macrophages.

Stabilizers: Epigenetic and metabolic memory

Epigenetic programs stabilize macrophage metabolic states and provide a mechanistic basis for innate immune memory. Through DNA methylation and histone modifications, macrophages modulate chromatin accessibility and transcriptional output, thereby sustaining inflammatory or reparative gene programs beyond the initiating stimulus. Histone lysine marks are among the best-characterized regulators: histone H3 lysine 4 trimethylation (H3K4me3) is enriched at active promoters, H3K4me1 marks enhancers, and histone H3 lysine 27 acetylation (H3K27ac) is associated with active, accessible chromatin [27]. These states are written and erased by histone methyltransferases/demethylases and acetyltransferases/deacetylases, creating tractable nodes for therapeutic intervention.

During activation, innate immune cues induce chromatin remodeling and DNA methylation changes that facilitate inflammatory gene expression [143,144]. Importantly, some epigenetic changes persist after stimulus withdrawal. For example, sustained H3K4me3 at promoters and H3K4me1 at enhancers can prime loci for faster transcription factor binding and stronger gene induction upon re-challenge [145]. This “primed” chromatin landscape underlies trained immunity and helps explain why transient inflammatory insults may translate into prolonged macrophage hyper-responsiveness in cardiovascular lesions.

A defining feature of this stabilizing layer is its direct dependence on metabolism. Key metabolites act as substrates or cofactors for chromatin-modifying enzymes, thereby coupling nutrient flux to transcriptional memory [145]. Acetyl-CoA supports histone acetylation, NAD+ tunes sirtuin (SIRT) activity, and α-KG is required for multiple demethylases, whereas succinate and itaconate can restrain demethylation reactions [146]. By shifting the availability of these metabolites, macrophage metabolic states can impose durable yet reversible chromatin configurations that favor either persistent inflammation or resolution-oriented programs [144].

Therefore, this epigenetic-metabolic coupling functions as a stabilizer that controls the persistence and reversibility of functional states in macrophages. As summarized in Fig. 4, it encodes innate immune memory and sets the “set point” for subsequent responses, shaping whether cardiovascular inflammation resolves or evolves into chronic pathology.

DNA methylation and demethylation

DNA methylation stabilizes macrophage transcriptional states by controlling promoter and enhancer accessibility, thereby shaping inflammatory tone in cardiovascular lesions. In atherosclerosis, aberrant methylation at disease-relevant loci can skew cytokine programs and lipid-handling pathways. For example, hypermethylation at the PPAR-γ promoter reduces its transcriptional activity, weakening anti-inflammatory and lipid-resolving functions and favoring plaque progression. Therefore, reversing maladaptive methylation patterns represents a potential lever to rebalance macrophage programs in CVDs [27].

DNA methylation is also a core component of innate immune memory. In macrophages, β-glucan-induced training can override lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced tolerance through stimulus-dependent remodeling of DNA methylation, enabling the reactivation of pro-inflammatory genes (e.g., IL-6, TNF-α) while maintaining repression at selected regulatory loci [147]. This selective “resetting” supports a heightened secondary response without globally releasing all brakes, which helps explain why trained states can be robust yet context-sensitive.

Mechanistically, DNA methylation rarely acts alone; it cooperates with histone marks to lock in permissive or repressed chromatin. Increased deposition of active histone marks (e.g., H3K4me3 and H3K27ac) together with locus-specific demethylation sustains open chromatin and accelerates transcriptional re-induction upon restimulation [146]. Through this coordinated epigenetic logic, methylation and demethylation dynamics establish durable but adjustable gene programs that tune macrophage reactivity across repeated challenges [143].

Histone modification

Histone modifications provide a fast and scalable way to tune macrophage gene expression by reshaping chromatin accessibility. During inflammatory activation, active marks such as H3K4me3 accumulate at promoters of cytokine genes (e.g., IL-1β and TNF-α), and H3K27ac increases at enhancers, together amplifying inflammatory transcriptional output in cardiovascular tissues. In parallel, repressive marks such as H3K27me3 limit transcription at selected anti-inflammatory loci (e.g., IL-10, Arg1), thereby reinforcing state-dependent gene programs [21]. The demethylase Jumonji Domain-Containing Protein 3 (JMJD3) modulates H3K27me3 dynamics and is required for appropriate polarization programs; perturbing this axis restores repressive marks and constrains inflammatory transcription [21]. These principles also underpin vaccine-trained states such as bacillus calmette-guérin (BCG), in which histone landscapes are reconfigured to support broader innate responsiveness.

Histone remodeling is tightly coupled to metabolism. H3K4me3 is enriched at the promoters of glycolysis-related genes [145,148], and the TLR-driven deposition of this mark supports glycolytic flux and pro-inflammatory outputs. β-Glucan similarly enhances glycolytic enzyme expression through histone remodeling, a process that can be attenuated by methylation-modulating interventions (e.g., methylthioadenosine) or by targeting specific histone methyltransferase pathways (e.g., Set7) with agents like cyproheptadine. Histone deacetylases (HDACs) form another key bridge: resveratrol can restrain β-glucan-trained glycolysis and cytokine production in part through HDAC-related chromatin control [22]. Consistent with this, miR-452-3p and miR-328-5p modulate HDAC-linked transcriptional programs, enhancing ABCA1 activity and exerting anti-atherosclerotic effects [21].

Additional chromatin enzymes integrate metabolic cues with macrophage energetics. Set7-dependent H3K4 methylation has been linked to SDH expression and OXPHOS tuning [149]. In contrast, G9a (EHMT2) promotes H3K9me2-associated chromatin compaction; its downregulation during BCG training facilitates inflammatory gene accessibility [150], whereas pharmacological inhibition of G9a can limit pro-inflammatory macrophage programs [151]. HDAC4 has also been implicated in lipid and glucose metabolism; loss of HDAC4 is associated with enhanced glycolysis and inflammatory polarization in atherosclerosis.

Beyond methylation/acetylation, histone lactylation has emerged as a metabolic imprint that can favor reparative transcriptional programs. Lactate-derived histone lactylation promotes expression of repair-associated genes and supports functional recovery after myocardial infarction [152,153]. Together, these findings emphasize a bidirectional logic: metabolites (e.g., acetyl-CoA, lactate) supply chemical substrates for histone modifications, while chromatin regulators (e.g., HDAC4) reshape metabolic gene networks.

Epigenetic-metabolic reprogramming underlying trained immunity

Trained immunity refers to the heightened responsiveness of innate immune cells upon secondary stimulation after a brief primary challenge, characterized by a sustained capacity for cytokine production and considered a form of innate immune memory [154]. In experimental settings, human primary monocytes primed with BCG, LPS, or β-glucan, followed by a resting phase and restimulation with TLR agonists, display markedly increased cytokine release [154]. While beneficial for host defense, such durable reprogramming can also sustain low-grade inflammation in CVDs. For example, oxidized LDL (oxLDL) can imprint trained features in macrophages and thereby accelerate atherosclerosis progression [151,155].

Mechanistically, trained immunity is established by coordinated metabolic rewiring and chromatin remodeling. β-glucan-primed monocytes activate the Akt/mTOR axis, shift toward glycolysis, and deposit active histone marks such as H3K4me3 and H3K27ac [22,27]. Similarly, oxLDL primes inflammatory programs, and subsequent TLR2/TLR4 stimulation enhances TNF-α and IL-6 production via PI3K and MAPK pathways while suppressing ABCA1/ABCG1, linking inflammatory signaling to lipid-handling dysfunction in plaques [35,151,156]. Importantly, blocking histone methylation with methylthioadenosine abrogates oxLDL-induced training, supporting a causal requirement for epigenetic remodeling [151]. Maintenance of the trained state relies on persistent glycolytic bias together with stable histone configurations, which collectively amplify secondary responses to TLR ligands (e.g., LPS) and increase lipid uptake capacity [[157], [158], [159]]. Consistent with this logic, glycolysis inhibition (2-deoxy-D-glucose), mTOR modulation (metformin), and histone methyltransferase-targeted strategies suppress β-glucan-induced training responses [22].

A key feature is bidirectional coupling between metabolites and epigenetic enzymes, which allows durability without irreversibility. Acetyl-CoA supports histone acetyltransferases, fumarate inhibits lysine demethylase 5B (KDM5B), and α-ketoglutarate serves as a cofactor for the demethylase JMJD3, together shaping locus-specific transcriptional outcomes [160]. This metabolite-chromatin coupling stabilizes functional memory and influences whether macrophages maintain pro-inflammatory programs or transition toward reparative trajectories. In CVDs, trained immunity is therefore a double-edged mechanism: it can fuel chronic inflammation, yet it also offers actionable metabolic and epigenetic nodes to “reset” maladaptive macrophage memory.

Druggability and clinical translation of epigenetic modulators

While the epigenetic-metabolic interface provides important mechanistic insight into macrophage memory, its therapeutic value depends on whether these regulatory nodes can be modulated safely and selectively. Epigenetic enzymes and chromatin-associated proteins are pharmacologically tractable because many function as “writers,” “erasers,” or “readers” of DNA and histone marks. Relevant target classes include DNA methyltransferases (DNMTs), histone acetyltransferases (HATs), HDACs, histone methyltransferases and demethylases, sirtuins, and bromodomain and extra-terminal (BET) proteins [161,162]. These proteins can theoretically reshape macrophage inflammatory memory, trained immunity, and metabolic preference by altering chromatin accessibility at cytokine, lipid-handling, and mitochondrial metabolic genes (Table S6).

Other epigenetic drug classes are also relevant but remain less advanced in cardiovascular macrophage applications. HDAC inhibitors and DNMT inhibitors have been developed primarily for oncology and other non-cardiovascular indications; therefore, their potential cardiovascular applications are currently best considered exploratory or repurposing-oriented [163,164]. In macrophages, HDAC-related pathways can influence glycolysis, inflammatory cytokine production, cholesterol handling, and reparative transcriptional programs. Similarly, DNMT- and TET-related pathways may reshape trained immunity and inflammatory memory. However, direct clinical evidence that systemic HDAC or DNMT inhibition can safely and selectively reprogram macrophage metabolism in cardiovascular lesions remains limited.

A major translational bottleneck is tissue and cellular specificity. Epigenetic regulators control broad transcriptional networks across many cell types, and systemic modulation may affect hematopoietic cells, endothelial cells, vascular smooth muscle cells, fibroblasts, and cardiomyocytes in addition to macrophages. Such pleiotropy can generate off-target toxicity, including bone marrow suppression, immune dysregulation, and the unintended remodeling of non-target tissues [165]. This concern is particularly important in CVDs, where the same epigenetic pathway may support macrophage inflammatory resolution in one context but regulate cardiomyocyte survival, endothelial barrier function, or vascular smooth muscle phenotype in another.

Therefore, epigenetic modulation is best positioned as a strategy for durable macrophage reprogramming only when paired with appropriate selectivity. Future translation will require macrophage- or lesion-targeted delivery, time-restricted dosing, and pharmacodynamic biomarkers that verify on-target chromatin remodeling in cardiovascular lesions. Nanomedicine-based platforms may be especially valuable in this setting because they can enrich epigenetic cargos in lesion macrophages, reduce systemic exposure, and potentially confine chromatin reprogramming to defined pathological microenvironments.

Therapeutic interventions in macrophage reprogramming

Therapeutic strategies that rewire macrophage metabolism are emerging as a tractable route to limit inflammation and improve repair across CVDs. Guided by the multilevel framework above, current interventions can be organized into three mechanistic tiers: targeting upstream signaling drivers, modulating core metabolic executors, and reshaping epigenetic or other stabilizing regulators (Fig. 5, Fig. 6, Table 1). This structure helps connect molecular targets to functional outcomes and clarifies whether the evidence is direct mechanistic evidence from descriptive or associative evidence.

Existing approaches mainly include small molecules, bioactive proteins/peptides, and gene-based modalities. Small molecules are the most mature class and can access intracellular pathways, but systemic exposure often leads to off-target effects and narrow therapeutic windows in CVDs. Bioactive proteins/peptides offer high specificity and can reprogram macrophage behavior by altering extracellular cues or receptor signaling, yet their stability, delivery efficiency, and immunogenicity require careful control. Gene-based strategies enable direct and potentially durable correction of pathogenic programs, but raise additional challenges related to delivery, tissue selectivity, and safety monitoring.

Importantly, the benefit of metabolic reprogramming is context-dependent: the same pathway may support host defense and debris clearance early after injury but promote persistent inflammation or maladaptive remodeling when sustained. Therefore, a key translational goal is not “global” macrophage suppression, but stage- and tissue-tailored modulation with defined endpoints. In this regard, delivery technologies, particularly macrophage-oriented nanomedicine provide an enabling layer to improve targeting, reduce systemic toxicity, and support combination or time-controlled interventions, which we discuss in the sections below.

Targeting signaling pathways (Drivers)

Targeting upstream signaling drivers is a direct way to reshape macrophage metabolism and inflammatory output in CVDs. Among candidate nodes, NF-κB is most frequently leveraged because it couples inflammatory transcription to glycolytic commitment and redox stress. Therefore, inhibition of NF-κB-linked programs can shift macrophages away from a glycolysis/ROS-dominant state and toward reparative functions. For example, mitochondrial division inhibitor-1 (Mdivi-1) inhibits dynamin-related protein 1 (Drp1)-dependent mitochondrial fission and suppresses complex I activity. This intervention thereby reduces mitochondrial ROS, restricts succinate accumulation, and decreases glycolytic flux. Consequently, this metabolic shift is accompanied by reduced NF-κB/CCL2-CCR2 signaling, attenuated monocyte recruitment, and the subsequent prevention of neointimal hyperplasia [[174], [175], [176]]. Consistently, mitochondria-targeted catalase (mCAT) reduces mitochondrial ROS and suppresses NF-κB activation in atherosclerotic macrophages [16]. Genetic modulation can converge on the same axis. For example, deficiency of copper metabolism murr1 domain-containing 1 (COMMD1), a regulator of NF-κB and lipid handling, increases glycolysis and inflammatory output. These effects are partially reversed by 2-deoxy-D-glucose (2-DG), supporting a functional link between glycolytic dependence and inflammatory tone [177]. Traditional formulations such as Yang-Xin-Shu-Mai granule (YXSMG) and Qishen granule (QSG) have also been reported to alleviate coronary disease and heart failure via suppression of TLR-MyD88-NF-κB signaling [178,179]. Taken together, these studies position NF-κB as an actionable driver connecting inflammatory signaling with metabolic rewiring in CVDs.

A second major driver is the PPAR-γ/LXR hub, which coordinates lipid trafficking with reparative programming. Several interventions appear to promote a macrophage state characterized by enhanced cholesterol efflux and reduced inflammatory signaling. Complement C1q supports reparative polarization in part by suppressing NF-κB and increasing IL-10 [180]. The carbohydrate-responsive element binding protein (ChREBP) downregulation during TLR activation alters redox-associated metabolism and dampens inflammatory output [167]. RNA epigenetic regulation intersects with these pathways: Mettl14 increases MyD88 mRNA stability through m6A modification, amplifying NF-κB/IL-6 signaling and accelerating atherosclerosis, whereas Mettl14 knockdown reduces pro-inflammatory output [45]. Multiple small molecules and natural products-cyclodextrin, naringin, pomegranate peel, and curcumin-have been linked to improved cholesterol handling through PPAR-γ/LXR-dependent transcriptional programs [31,63,158,168,181,182]. In addition, exosomes from IL-4-stimulated macrophages (THP1-IL4-exo) deliver miRNAs that enhance PPAR-γ-GLUT4 signaling and favor reparative polarization [17]. Collectively, these examples support the concept that engaging PPAR-γ/LXR can couple lipid metabolism to macrophage reprogramming, although the strength of mechanistic evidence varies across studies.

Other signaling nodes provide complementary entry points into metabolic control. Sorting Nexin 10 (SNX10) deficiency enhances TFEB nuclear translocation and FAO, biasing macrophages toward reparative polarization [29]. Angiotensin II promotes inflammatory reprogramming through Erk1/2 and p38/Stat3, while saracatinib counteracts these effects and delays atherosclerosis [183,184]. Cannabinoid CB1 receptor signaling has been associated with macrophage recruitment and inflammatory reprogramming, and its inhibition confers vascular protection through MAPK modulation [185]. These studies broaden the driver landscape beyond NF-κB and PPAR-γ/LXR, and highlight that distinct upstream cues can converge on shared metabolic endpoints.

In summary, signaling pathways represent actionable drivers of macrophage metabolism and polarization. Interventions ranging from small molecules (Mdivi-1, saracatinib) and biologics (Neuropeptide Y) to gene-based strategies (Mettl14 knockdown, siRNA against LXRα) converge on these nodes to reorient macrophages toward reparative phenotypes. This highlights signaling modulation as a clinically relevant axis to attenuate inflammation, restore lipid homeostasis, and promote repair in CVDs.

Although signaling axes such as NF-κB and PPAR-γ/LXR are central to macrophage metabolic reprogramming, their functions in cardiovascular tissues exhibit significant cell type-specific and time-dependent variations. NF-κB inhibition can reduce macrophage glycolysis and restrain the succinate-HIF-1α inflammatory loop, yet NF-κB also contributes to stress adaptation and mitochondrial homeostasis in cardiomyocytes, raising concerns that sustained systemic inhibition may compromise myocardial protective responses [186]. Likewise, PPAR-γ activation can enhance cholesterol efflux and FAO, but in advanced lesions it may also increase lipid droplet accumulation and lipotoxic stress in macrophages, potentially blunting benefit or shifting risk profiles [136]. For m6A regulators such as Mettl14, broad transcriptome-level effects make off-target consequences difficult to predict without tissue-selective delivery and careful safety profiling [45]. Therefore, although signaling-targeted approaches can rapidly rewire macrophage metabolism, pathway redundancy, pleiotropy, and systemic exposure often limit their use as stand-alone strategies, highlighting the need for delivery designs that improve lesion selectivity and temporal control.

Targeting metabolic enzymes (Executors)

Targeting metabolic enzymes offers a direct route to reprogram macrophage function because these enzymes sit at rate-limiting steps that determine carbon flow, redox tone, and mitochondrial engagement. In CVDs, pro-inflammatory macrophage states are typically sustained by high glycolytic flux with disrupted mitochondrial oxidation, whereas reparative states rely more on intact mitochondrial metabolism and controlled redox signaling. Enzyme-level interventions therefore aim to (i) reduce pathological glycolysis and inflammatory amplification, (ii) restore pyruvate entry into mitochondria and OXPHOS capacity, and (iii) rebalance ROS production and lipid handling in lesion macrophages.

Glycolysis as an inflammatory amplifier. Several glycolytic entry and control points have been linked to macrophage inflammatory output in vascular disease. Hexokinase (HK) controls glucose commitment to glycolysis; kruppel-like factor (KLF7) reduces glycolytic activity by downregulating GLUT1, lowering glucose uptake and lactate production, and attenuating atherosclerosis progression [187]. Pyruvate kinase m2 (PKM2) supports glycolytic throughput and inflammatory polarization. In disease models, PKM2 suppression, achieved either through miR-124 or via knockdown of polypyrimidine tract-binding protein 1 (PTBP1), dampens hyper-glycolysis and inflammatory programs in both pulmonary hypertension and coronary artery disease [107]. PKM2 has also been implicated in myocarditis via lncRNA AK083884-exosome signaling, highlighting that metabolic enzyme control can be coupled to intercellular communication within inflammatory niches [169]. PFKFB3 is a prominent glycolytic checkpoint that increases fructose-2,6-bisphosphate and sustains high glycolytic flux. Consistent with its position upstream of multiple inflammatory outputs, inhibition with AZ26 or AZ67 reduces cytokine production, lowers HIF-1α/NF-κB activity, and improves cardiac function in reported models [46,188]. Collectively, these studies suggest that limiting glycolytic flux at key regulatory steps is often accompanied by reduced HIF-1α/NF-κB pathway activity and lower inflammatory cytokine output in disease-relevant macrophage settings.

Restoring mitochondrial pyruvate oxidation. PDK inhibits PDH, diverting pyruvate away from mitochondria and reinforcing glycolysis. Interventions that reduce PDK activity can therefore re-open pyruvate entry into mitochondria, restore OXPHOS, and limit lactate accumulation. In ischemia-reperfusion contexts, dexmedetomidine-associated downregulation of PDK4 or direct PDK1 inhibition by dichloroacetic acid (DCA) has been reported to improve metabolic balance and tissue protection [3,189]. Traditional medicine Nuanxinkang, a Chinese herbal decoction, has likewise been linked to enhanced OXPHOS through the suppression of PDK1. Under hypoxia, phosphoglycerate kinase 1 (PGK1) can modulate pyruvate routing via PGK1-PDK1 coupling; tanshinone IIA has been reported to promote reparative macrophage polarization and improve cardiac function through this axis [190]. Conceptually, these approaches target a shared executor logic: shifting macrophages from a lactate-dominant program toward mitochondrial oxidation to support resolution and repair.

Redox and lipid-handling enzymes as phenotype “set points”. Because ROS species and sources are not equivalent, enzyme targeting must distinguish mitochondrial ROS from NOX-derived ROS and consider antioxidant capacity. NOX4-driven oxidative stress has been associated with CD80+ macrophage polarization and atherosclerosis progression [191]. By contrast, antioxidant strategies such as the mitochondria-targeted antioxidant mitoquinone (MitoQ) or thioredoxin-2 have been reported to reduce LPS-induced cytokine output and support plaque stability, consistent with mitigation of excessive oxidative signaling [16]. Additional regulators connect redox tone to tissue remodeling: in pulmonary hypertension, C-terminal binding protein 1 (CtBP1) has been linked to a metabolic shift toward aerobic glycolysis, and inhibition with pharmacologic inhibitor 4-methylthio-2-oxobutyric acid (MTOB) reduces proliferation and inflammation in the reported setting [192]. LDHA has been associated with reduced ROS and cardiomyocyte proliferation and with a reparative macrophage bias in related models, supporting the idea that lactate/pyruvate handling can feed back to redox balance and repair programs [170]. Glutaredoxin 1 (Grx1) provides another layer of redox control through feedback on NOX, NOS, and Arg2, and its overexpression has been reported to protect against diet-induced atherosclerosis [171,193]. Lipid metabolic enzymes add a second “executor tier” by controlling fatty-acid entry, mitochondrial quality control, and cholesterol efflux. Apolipoprotein A-I binding protein (AIBP) localizes to mitochondria and has been linked to PTEN-induced kinase 1 (PINK1)- dependent macrophage polarization; AIBP deficiency promotes inflammatory activation and accelerates atherosclerosis in reported models [194]. Conversely, acyl-CoA synthetase 1 (ACSL1) increases fatty acid uptake and has been associated with reduced ABCA1 expression and amplified inflammation; silencing ACSL1 mitigates atherosclerosis in adoptive transfer models [172]. These examples illustrate how redox and lipid-handling enzymes can tune macrophage inflammatory thresholds and foam-cell dynamics, in addition to classic glycolytic executors.

Translational considerations and boundary conditions. Enzyme targets can offer improved mechanistic specificity, but translation is limited by metabolic network coupling and cell-type dependency. For example, PFKFB3 inhibition can reduce macrophage glycolysis and inflammatory output, and PFKFB3 also supports endothelial barrier function, raising the possibility of vascular side effects with systemic inhibition [195]. Similarly, DCA can re-balance pyruvate oxidation, but neurotoxicity and broader metabolic effects constrain long-term use [196]. Emerging approaches targeting the succinate-SDH-HIF-1α axis are currently under investigation to reduce IL-1β and stabilize plaques. However, succinate and inflammatory metabolism can be spatially and temporally patterned (e.g., peri-infarct regions), and excessive suppression may blunt early inflammatory responses required for debris clearance and effective repair [197]. These considerations argue for lesion-selective delivery, time-windowed intervention, and careful separation of macrophage-directed effects from impacts on endothelium, smooth muscle cells, and cardiomyocytes when advancing metabolic enzyme targets toward clinical application.

Targeting epigenetic regulators (Stabilizers)

Epigenetic regulation stabilizes macrophage metabolic states and helps sustain inflammatory or reparative phenotypes in CVDs. By tuning DNA/histone modifications and chromatin-associated enzymes, these mechanisms can prolong transcriptional programs that would otherwise be transient, making epigenetic regulators attractive but potentially higher-risk targets for macrophage reprogramming.

Loss of nucleophosmin 1 (NPM1) shifts macrophages away from glycolysis toward OXPHOS by recruiting the histone demethylase KDM5B to the promoter of an mTOR inhibitor; and this remodeling has been linked to enhanced angiogenesis, improved cardiac function, and reduced tissue damage [88]. Nuclear receptor corepressor 1 (NCOR1), which is regulated by miR-148b-3p [198], also constrains inflammatory reprogramming. HDAC4 supports this restraint by limiting histone acetylation and maintaining NCOR1-dependent transcriptional repression, which reduces macrophage glucose metabolism and attenuates atherosclerosis [187]. In the setting of cardiac repair, mechanosensors Yes-associated protein/transcriptional coactivator with PDZ-binding motif (YAP/TAZ) can engage the HDAC-NCOR1 repressive complex, promote Arg1 expression, and support reparative polarization [173].

Sirtuins provide another entry point into epigenetic-metabolic coupling. SIRT1 activation by SRT1720 increases deacetylation activity and dampens focal adhesion kinase (FAK)-NF-κB-associated inflammatory programs in macrophages [199]. SIRT3, induced by estradiol (E2), helps preserve mitochondrial function and limits inflammatory polarization, thereby delaying inflammation-related cardiac injury [200]. Collectively, these examples suggest that targeting chromatin modifiers, co-repressors, and sirtuins can maintain or reinforce macrophage metabolic states and extend therapeutic effects beyond the dosing window.

A key limitation is specificity. Epigenetic regulators often control broad gene networks across multiple cardiovascular cell types. For example, while SRT1720 can restrain macrophage inflammatory activity, SIRT1 also regulates mitochondrial biogenesis and metabolic adaptation in cardiomyocytes, raising the possibility of unintended remodeling with systemic activation [201]. Likewise, although the NCOR1-HDAC4 axis can suppress macrophage glycolysis and atherosclerosis, HDAC-directed interventions generally lack cell-type selectivity and may affect cardiomyocytes and vascular smooth muscle cells [202]. Therefore, epigenetic targeting is best positioned as a strategy to achieve durable macrophage reprogramming, but translation will likely require macrophage-selective delivery or context-restricted modulation to limit off-target effects.

Other emerging strategies

Beyond canonical signaling drivers, metabolic executors, and epigenetic stabilizers, several additional strategies can reshape macrophage metabolism and function in cardiovascular diseases (CVDs). To clarify their classification, these approaches can be organized according to their target hierarchy: extracellular or cell-surface regulatory cues, intracellular metabolic-flux modulators, and post-transcriptional regulators that impose more durable changes in metabolic preference.

At the extracellular and cell-surface regulatory level, conditioning-related interventions can reshape macrophage behavior through upstream microenvironmental cues. Galanin combined with occlusion preconditioning (OP) reduces fibrosis and inflammation and increases CD206 expression, consistent with a shift toward reparative macrophage programs in ischemia/reperfusion injury and myocarditis [166,203]. Although this strategy does not directly inhibit a single metabolic enzyme, it illustrates how tissue-conditioning cues can influence macrophage redox balance, mitochondrial integrity, and downstream immunometabolic state.

At the intracellular metabolic-flux level, several bioactive compounds and non-canonical metabolic modulators directly affect macrophage energy metabolism or metabolite-linked inflammatory signaling. Quercitrin (Que) shows antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activity and has been reported to restrain glycolytic metabolism while supporting oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), reprogram arginine metabolism, increase CD206+ macrophages, improve ejection fraction, and limit adverse ventricular remodeling [204]. These examples indicate that emerging small-molecule strategies may act beyond the classic glycolysis-OXPHOS axis by targeting amino acid metabolism, redox balance, and TCA-derived immunoregulatory metabolites.

At the post-transcriptional regulatory level, non-coding RNAs can impose longer-lasting shifts in macrophage metabolic preferences. For instance, miR-10a has been demonstrated to promote fatty acid oxidation (FAO) by suppressing ligand-dependent nuclear receptor co-repressor (LCOR); this action reinforces reparative programming and supports a broader role for Dicer-dependent miRNAs in governing cardiovascular inflammation [205]. Compared with direct metabolic enzyme inhibition, RNA-based regulation may provide a more durable but also more delivery-dependent means of reprogramming macrophage metabolism.

Despite these advances, most agents in this category still face familiar barriers, including systemic exposure, limited lesion selectivity, and difficulty sustaining reprogramming within dynamic cardiovascular microenvironments. These constraints provide a clear rationale for nanomedicine-based solutions. Nanoplatforms can couple cell targeting, optimized pharmacokinetics, and controlled release within a single design, enabling more localized and sustained modulation of macrophage metabolism.

Nanomedicine-based approaches to macrophage reprogramming

Why nanomedicine?

Conventional drugs show limited efficacy in reprogramming macrophages for CVDs because of nonspecific distribution, poor bioavailability, and restricted penetration into fibrotic or ischemic tissues [206,207]. In addition, many agents act on a single pathway (e.g., mTORC1 inhibition or AMPK activation) [28], which is frequently insufficient in macrophages because metabolic pathways are highly interconnected and readily compensate. As a result, in myocardial infarction and atherosclerosis, these drugs may reduce inflammatory signals but rarely achieve sustained, lesion-localized changes in macrophage immunometabolic programs. In this context, nanomaterial-enabled macrophage reprogramming has emerged as a practical strategy to improve lesion delivery, enable multi-pronged metabolic modulation, and enhance tissue access [4].

Nanomedicine improves selectivity primarily through two design levers: surface targeting and physicochemical tuning. Surface ligands (such as peptides, antibodies, and glycosylated structures) can be designed to engage CD36, CD206, or CCR2 on lesion macrophages. By enriching nanoparticles within plaques or inflamed myocardium, these ligands raise the effective dose at the target site while reducing systemic exposure [208]. In parallel, size, shape, and surface charge determine circulation behavior, vascular margination, and interactions with macrophages; these parameters also influence transport through extracellular matrix in plaque cores or infarct border zones, where diffusion is otherwise restricted [208]. Building on these design principles, multiple studies in CVD models have shown that nanoparticle delivery of metabolic modulators, including AMPK activators, ROS scavengers, and mitochondria-targeted agents, can reprogram macrophages from a highly glycolytic inflammatory state toward oxidative, reparative metabolism. These shifts are accompanied by reduced plaque inflammation and improved cardiac function.

At the level of distribution and uptake, nanomedicine adds another layer of control. Administration route (intravenous, local vascular delivery, or intramyocardial injection) can shape the initial lesion exposure in myocardial infarction, atherosclerosis, and inflammatory cardiomyopathies [208]. At the cellular level, receptor-mediated endocytosis can be leveraged to favor uptake by specific macrophage subsets (e.g., CCR2+ inflammatory macrophages or CD206+ reparative macrophages), supporting more selective regulation of glycolysis, TCA intermediates (including succinate), and mitochondrial OXPHOS. In contrast, phagocytosis is well suited for particulate cargos and mitochondria-directed payloads. In myocardial infarction and myocarditis models, nanomedicines optimized for these uptake routes have been shown to lower macrophage ROS, reduce HIF-1α/IL-1β signaling, and improve lipid handling and mitochondrial homeostasis within lesions [209].

From a platform perspective, four major classes of nanosystems are commonly used in this field. Polymeric carriers (e.g., vesicles, dendrimers, micelles, nanospheres, and nanofibers) support controlled release and have been used to deliver AMPK activators, mTOR inhibitors, and mitochondria-targeted modulators, thereby shifting macrophage metabolism toward oxidative programs in myocardial infarction and atherosclerosis models [4]. Inorganic nanoparticles (e.g., silica, iron oxide, gold, metal-organic frameworks) offer high loading capacity, and the catalytic functions can modulate mitochondrial ROS, the succinate-HIF-1α axis, or iron-related redox processes in some cases, indirectly reshaping macrophage inflammatory metabolism [208]. Lipid-based systems (e.g., liposomes, lipid nanoparticles, and lipid emulsions) are currently the most established vehicles for nucleic acid delivery and have been applied to deliver siRNA/mRNA to lesion macrophages to regulate metabolic enzymes or inflammatory pathways [208]. Biomimetic carriers (e.g., microvesicles and protein nanocarriers) exploit endogenous structures to improve immunocompatibility and biodegradability and have shown potential for macrophage reprogramming in myocardial infarction and atherosclerosis [4].

Taken together, nanomedicine provides a coherent solution to key limitations of traditional therapies by combining lesion enrichment, multi-target modulation, and controllable release (Fig. 7, Table 2). Surface functionalization can increase macrophage uptake and reduce off-target exposure, whereas responsive or sustained-release designs help maintain effective concentrations in inflamed tissue over time [219,223,240]. Platforms such as mesoporous and biomimetic nanoparticles also enable co-delivery of metabolic modulators and anti-inflammatory agents with tunable dosing and favorable biocompatibility profiles [227,241]. Collectively, these features can position nanomedicine as a leading approach for achieving more precise and durable macrophage metabolic reprogramming in CVDs.

Targeting metabolic axes

Macrophage functions are supported by different metabolic programs that shift with disease context and stage. In general, inflammatory macrophage activities often coincide with increased glycolytic throughput and accumulation of glycolysis-linked intermediates, whereas reparative functions are more compatible with intact mitochondrial respiration and FAO. Nanomedicine provides a practical way to translate these concepts into lesion-focused interventions: it can concentrate metabolic modulators in plaques or injured myocardium, limit systemic exposure, and sustain local drug levels long enough to remodel macrophage bioenergetics. Importantly, metabolic intervention is not “one-size-fits-all”: in acute injury, excessive early suppression of inflammatory metabolism may impair debris clearance, whereas in chronic lesions, persistent glycolytic bias can sustain non-resolving inflammation. Therefore, stage-matched tuning (rather than maximal inhibition or activation) is typically the most defensible therapeutic goal.

Inhibiting glycolysis

Heightened glycolytic flux is frequently observed in inflammatory macrophage programs and is associated with increased lactate production, altered TCA cycling (including succinate accumulation), and sustained inflammatory transcriptional activity. Accordingly, glycolysis restraint has been explored as a way to reduce inflammatory output and facilitate a shift toward reparative programs in CVDs. Nanomedicine is particularly useful here because it can (i) localize inhibitors to inflammatory lesions, (ii) reduce dose-limiting systemic effects, and (iii) provide sustained exposure that is often required for durable metabolic remodeling.

Several representative nanoplatforms illustrate this strategy. Artificial hybrid nanozymes built from nanomaterials (NMs) have been developed to modulate oxidative stress and inflammatory metabolism [210]. A zeolitic imidazolate framework nanozyme (ZIF-8zyme) with antioxidative and anti-inflammatory properties was reported to lower intracellular ROS and attenuate glycolysis, thereby biasing macrophages toward a reparative phenotype and improving cardiomyocyte survival [8,210]. Beyond enzyme-mimetic designs, lipid-mimicking carriers can intervene upstream in metabolite-generating pathways. Recombinant HDL nanoparticles loaded with statins (statin-rHDL) deliver 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-coenzyme A (HMG-CoA) reductase inhibitors into plaque macrophages, thereby suppressing the mevalonate pathway and reducing plaque inflammation. This platform is positioned to support either prolonged low-dose suppression or short-course high-dose control in advanced lesions [211].

Metabolite-inspired payloads provide another entry point. Dimethyl itaconate (DMI) can attenuate inflammatory gene programs while enhancing antioxidant responses, and electrospun poly-ε-caprolactone (PCL)/DMI nanofibers were designed to recruit macrophages and provide local DMI exposure (Fig. 8a). In myocardial infarction models, PCL/DMI scaffolds reduced infarct size and improved myocardial protection, consistent with a macrophage shift toward reparative polarization (Fig. 8b–e) [212].

Together, these diverse platforms, including nanozymes, HDL-based statin delivery systems, and DMI-releasing scaffolds, support a shared principle: glycolysis-targeted nanomedicine is best framed as a lesion-localized metabolic “de-intensification” designed to attenuate persistent pro-inflammatory output, rather than a systemic or temporally indiscriminate suppression of glycolysis.

Enhancing OXPHOS

Promoting mitochondrial fitness and OXPHOS is a complementary strategy to glycolysis restraint, especially when the therapeutic goal is to support resolution, efferocytosis, and tissue repair. A common design logic is to reduce excessive oxidative stress that compromises electron transport, while concurrently delivering anti-inflammatory agents or regulatory cargos that stabilize reparative programs.

ROS-scavenging nanomaterials are frequently used to support mitochondrial recovery. Hollow Prussian blue (PB) nanoparticles encapsulating betamethasone sodium phosphate (BSP) and further modified with dextran sulfate sodium (DSS), termed DSS/PB@BSP, were designed for scavenger receptor-mediated targeting (Fig. 8f); In myocardial infarction/reperfusion injury (MI/RI) models, this platform integrated ROS scavenging with anti-inflammatory drug delivery to reduce pro-inflammatory cytokines, increase IL-10 levels, and promote reparative polarization (Fig. 8g–h) [213]. Similarly, black phosphorus nanosheets (BPNSs) can function as ROS scavengers in plaques, suppress macrophage inflammatory cytokines, reduce lesion area, and improve plaque stability in ApoE-deficient mice [214].

Biomimetic designs can further improve lesion enrichment and intracellular delivery. Platelet-membrane-camouflaged nanoparticles (CsA@PPTK) were reported to accumulate in the ischemic myocardium and improve cyclosporin A (CsA) delivery, attenuating oxidative stress and apoptosis while reducing fibrosis following MI/RI. Furthermore, this formulation was associated with enhanced reparative macrophage accumulation at injury sites and the expansion of regulatory T (Treg) cells, consistent with coordinated immunometabolic remodeling (Fig. 9a–g) [215].

Natural vesicles provide an additional route for mitochondrial-oriented reprogramming. Extracellular vesicles (EVs) can deliver miRNAs and proteins that reshape macrophage metabolism and reduce mitochondrial ROS [216]. Cortical bone stem cell-derived EVs (CBSC-EVs), enriched with miR-182/183, improved cardiac function after ischemic injury and were linked to enhanced macrophage OXPHOS and reduced mitochondrial ROS, alongside reduced cardiomyocyte apoptosis and infarct size [217]. Platelet membrane-modified EVs (P-EVs) were also reported to facilitate endosomal escape and cytosolic miRNA release after macrophage uptake, driving inflammatory-to-reparative polarization and promoting cardiac repair [218].

Finally, some nanomedicine studies extend metabolic reprogramming beyond macrophages to other cardiac cell types, which can indirectly reshape the immune microenvironment. A neutrophil-mimicking mesoporous silica nanoparticle system carrying a cardiac miRNA cocktail (miR-1, 133, 208, 499) targeted fibroblasts and promoted regenerative remodeling with reduced fibrosis after MI/RI (Fig. 9h–l) [242]. While not a macrophage-targeted OXPHOS intervention, this example underscores that metabolic and phenotypic remodeling of stromal compartments can complement macrophage-directed strategies during repair.

Overall, OXPHOS-supporting nanomedicines (e.g., ROS-scavenging particles, biomimetic carriers, and EV/miRNA-based systems) may serve as useful repair-phase tools to restore mitochondrial function, alleviate oxidative injury, and consolidate reparative immune programs. Importantly, their delivery timing and dose must be precisely matched to the disease stage, ensuring that essential early inflammatory functions are not prematurely dampened.

Nano-therapeutic targeting of inflammatory signaling pathways

Inflammatory signaling pathways, especially NF-κB and PPAR-γ/LXR, connect local danger cues to macrophage metabolic choices and effector functions in CVDs. Persistent pathway bias supports cytokine production, foam-cell persistence, and maladaptive remodeling, which together sustain lesion progression. Nanomedicine improves the therapeutic window of pathway modulation by enriching payloads in lesion macrophages, enabling stimulus-responsive release, and reducing systemic exposure. Rather than “turning off” inflammation globally, the goal of nano-therapeutic signaling control is context- and stage-matched tuning: limiting non-resolving activation in chronic lesions while preserving essential early host-defense and clearance functions.

Nanomedicine inhibits inflammation by targeting NF-κB pathway

NF-κB integrates diverse upstream inputs and coordinates inflammatory transcriptional programs in macrophages, making it an attractive node for nano-enabled intervention in both atherosclerosis and ischemic heart injury. Recent work supports multiple delivery logics, EV engineering, nucleic acid delivery, antioxidant carriage, and multifunctional co-delivery, each aiming to rebalance macrophage programs in a lesion-restricted manner.

EV-based approaches. Laminar shear stress-induced EVs (LSS-EVs) enriched in miR-34c-5p were reported to reprogram macrophages toward a reparative phenotype through transforming growth factor-β-Smad3 (TGF-β-Smad3) signaling and slow atherosclerotic progression. Hyaluronic-acid decoration further enabled CD44-dependent targeting and improved delivery efficiency in vitro and in vivo (Fig. 10a–g) [219]. EVs delivering miR-181b-5p reduced mitochondrial ROS, dampened NF-κB signaling, and lowered glycolytic activity in macrophages, which correlated with reduced infarct size [2]. By contrast, PH fibroblast-derived EVs (PH-Fib-EVs) activated mTOR/NF-κB signaling and promoted inflammatory polarization with a glycolysis-biased metabolic pattern [243]. Additional EV cargos, including miR-139-3p, were linked to enhanced reparative-marker expression and cardioprotection; atorvastatin-pretreated MSC-derived EVs (MSCATV-EVs) were proposed to act through the miR-139-3p/STAT1 axis, with miR-139-3p manipulation modulating reparative polarization and cardiac outcomes (Fig. 11i–l) [58].

Superparamagnetic polymeric micelles (SPMs) co-delivering celecoxib and a miR-155 antagonist targeted inflammatory mediators and NF-κB-related gene programs simultaneously [223]. A neutrophil-membrane-camouflaged polydopamine nanoplatform delivering puerarin (NM@PDA@PU) promoted reparative polarization at infarct sites and reduced cardiomyocyte pyroptosis [224]. Other hybrid designs, including supramolecular hydrogels (PLDX-PMI) and mannose-modified liposomes carrying IKKβ siRNA (siIKKβ-ML), further demonstrate that NF-κB can be modulated across distinct carrier types and disease settings [[244], [245], [246]].

Carrier-intrinsic immunometabolic effects and selective uptake. Immunomodulatory nanoparticles (iNPs) composed of polylactic acid (PLA) with polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) or poly(ethyl methacrylate) (PEMA) surfactants showed composition-dependent suppression of LPS-induced NF-κB p65 and p38 MAPK activation via lactate-regulated mechanisms, lowering pro-inflammatory cytokine release [225]. In parallel, a mannose receptor targeting ligand (MRTL) was designed to enhance intracellular accumulation of polymeric nanocarriers and showed selective targeting toward reparative macrophages, supporting the feasibility of phenotype-biased delivery [226].

Collectively, these studies indicate that nano-enabled NF-κB modulation demonstrates the greatest translational potential when it (i) achieves targeted therapeutic enrichment within lesion macrophages, (ii) integrates signaling control with redox or metabolic support, and (iii) avoids the systemic or prolonged NF-κB suppression that could compromise host defense.

Nanomedicine promotes reverse cholesterol transport by targeting PPAR-γ pathway

The PPAR-γ/LXR axis promotes lipid handling programs in macrophages, including ABCA1/ABCG1-dependent cholesterol efflux, and can reduce foam-cell burden and improve plaque stability. Nanomedicine helps operationalize this pathway by improving plaque macrophage delivery of agonists or modulators and reducing off-target effects in liver or other tissues.

Macrophage-directed delivery of LXR ligands and PPAR-γ agonists. Mannose-functionalized dendrimeric nanoparticles (mDNPs) using a PAMAM scaffold and polyethylene glycol (PEG) spacers to conjugate the LXR ligand T0901317 were efficiently internalized by macrophages, upregulated ABCA1/ABCG1, and enhanced cholesterol efflux [227,228]. Targeted delivery of pioglitazone to inflammatory monocytes similarly promoted reparative polarization through PPAR-γ activation while limiting NF-κB signaling, reducing recruitment and inflammation and improving remodeling outcomes [229].

Protecting efflux machinery and modulating its regulators. During regression, foam-cell lipid clearance largely depends on ABCA1/ABCG1-mediated efflux [230]. Nanoparticles that engage LXR signaling have therefore been positioned as plaque-focused delivery systems [247,248]. Calpain activation can cleave ABCA1/ABCG1; mannose-modified selenium nanoparticles carrying a calpain inhibitory peptide (MSeNP@CIP) accumulated in plaques, inhibited calpain, reduced plaque formation, and decreased proteolytic loss of ABCA1/ABCG1 in ApoE−/− models [231]. Chitosan nanoparticles (ChNPs) were also reported to deliver exogenous miR-33 to macrophages, altering ABCA1 expression and reducing cholesterol efflux capacity, demonstrating the feasibility of miRNA-based control over efflux pathways. However, because reduced efflux would be expected to be pro-atherogenic, this strategy requires careful therapeutic framing rather than direct application [232]. Epsins can inhibit cholesterol efflux by promoting ABCG1 lysosomal degradation; targeting Epsins in diseased macrophages was proposed to simultaneously reduce CD36-mediated uptake and preserve ABCG1-mediated efflux [233].

Biomimetic membrane-coated and multifunctional platforms. Inflammatory macrophage membrane-coated nanoprodrugs were developed to improve circulation and targeting ability while evading immune clearance. MM@CD-PBA-RVT was reported to promote plaque stabilization and inhibit atherosclerosis by activating reverse cholesterol transport and promoting inflammatory-to-reparative polarization [234]. MM@DA-pCD@MTX combined β-cyclodextrin-assisted cholesterol handling with methotrexate, which was linked to reduced foam-cell formation and increased ABCA1 expression [235]. Col@HMnO2-MM was reported to mitigate inflammation, lipid deposition, and plaque formation in ApoE−/− mice by enhancing cholesterol efflux and limiting foam-cell formation [236].

Additional plaque-/macrophage-targeted designs. A bionic nanoparticle system (HA-ML@(H+R) NPs) was designed to target plaques and macrophages, reduce plaque area, and improve stability; mechanistically, it downregulated CD36 and was linked to autophagy activation and improved lipid handling [237]. Molecularly imprinted cerium dioxide nanocomposites (CeO2@MIP) promoted cholesterol efflux via an artificial receptor mechanism and improved anti-inflammatory activity [238]. The CEZP platform CpG-encapsulated PLGA nanoparticles with an MOF shell coordinated with Zn2+ and epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG) was proposed to enhance endocytosis, accelerate lipolysis, and promote cholesterol efflux through ABCA upregulation [239].

In summary, nano-therapeutic engagement of PPAR-γ/LXR signaling aims to strengthen cholesterol efflux programs (notably ABCA1/ABCG1) and reduce foam-cell persistence, thereby improving plaque stability [249]. The strongest translational rationale arises when nanocarriers (i) enrich in plaque macrophages, (ii) avoid systemic lipid-metabolism disruption, and (iii) integrate efflux enhancement with concurrent control of oxidative stress and inflammatory signaling within the lesion microenvironment.

Conclusions

Macrophage metabolic reprogramming provides a coherent lens to interpret cardiovascular immunopathology. In cardiovascular lesions, macrophages adjust glycolysis, the TCA cycle, FAO, and OXPHOS to match local demands, and these shifts shape inflammation kinetics, clearance capacity, and repair quality. Across models, interventions that engage immunometabolic regulators (mTOR, NF-κB, PPAR-γ/LXR, NRF2) can rebalance macrophage function and improve myocardial repair or plaque stability. In parallel, epigenetic regulation through acetylation, lactylation, and m6A modification consolidates these states and supports trained or tolerant phenotypes after injury.

Despite rapid progress, translation remains limited by three issues. First, macrophage metabolism operates as a coupled network; single-node interventions may be transient or provoke compensation, and the same manipulation can be beneficial or harmful depending on lesion stage and tissue context. Second, metabolic requirements differ across cardiovascular disease entities and phases: after myocardial infarction, macrophages typically transition from early inflammatory programs to later reparative programs [250], whereas in atherosclerosis, chronic lipid stress can entrench foam cells in maladaptive states characterized by impaired efflux and persistent oxidative burden [250]. Third, spatial and lineage heterogeneity (resident versus monocyte-derived macrophages, core versus border zones, hypoxic versus reperfused areas) is still insufficiently mapped into therapeutic design, making it difficult to define dose-time windows and reliable biomarkers of on-target reprogramming.

Nanomedicine offers a practical route to address these constraints. By combining lesion-targeting, stimulus responsiveness, and controlled release, nanoplatforms can enrich metabolic/epigenetic modulators in lesion macrophages and enable multi-layer regulation with reduced systemic exposure. Key priorities for the field include defining spatial immunometabolic states in human CVDs, aligning interventions with disease stage and macrophage lineage, and establishing long-term safety, immunogenicity, and manufacturability standards for nanosystems.

Overall, macrophage metabolic reprogramming is both a mechanistic framework and a design principle for next-generation CVD therapy. With tighter integration of spatial omics, causal metabolism studies, and clinically scalable nanoplatforms, macrophage-centered metabolic interventions may become realistic strategies to limit chronic inflammation, stabilize plaques, and improve myocardial healing.

Future perspectives

Biological hurdles: Navigating the complexity of macrophage metabolic reprogramming

The next frontier in cardiovascular therapy lies in decoding and ultimately reprogramming the metabolic program of macrophages in vivo. Three advances are likely to be decisive. First, patient stratification and on-treatment monitoring should move from systemic markers to macrophage-focused readouts. Inflammation imaging is starting to provide this layer: CXCR4-or SST2-targeted PET tracers (e.g., 68Ga-Pentixafor, 68Ga-DOTATATE) and long-axial-field PET/MR enable real-time assessment of macrophage-rich activity within plaques and peri-infarct regions, creating a practical pharmacodynamic window for immunometabolic interventions [251]. Second, targets are expanding from canonical inflammatory signaling toward programs that couple lipid handling and efferocytosis to inflammation resolution. Modulating PPAR-γ/LXR, engaging TREM2-associated lipid programs, and restoring MerTK-dependent efferocytosis can support macrophage states that favor lipid clearance and tissue repair, with benefits for plaque stabilization and post-injury healing [252]. Third, delivery has become a co-equal determinant of efficacy. Biomimetic vesicles, ligand-decorated nanoparticles, and ROS-responsive systems can enrich payloads in lesion macrophages and support stage-matched modulation of glycolysis, oxidative metabolism, and inflammatory output; clinical translation will require tight control over ligand density, release kinetics, and biodistribution [253].

Two additional directions may accelerate impact. Integrating immunometabolic targeting with host risk architecture including clonal hematopoiesis (e.g., TET2, DNA Methyltransferase 3 α (DNMT3A)) and IL-6-linked inflammatory biology may identify subgroups most likely to benefit and enable rational combinations. In this context, IL-6 blockade with ziltivekimab has entered outcomes testing and could be conceptually complementary to approaches aimed at reducing durable inflammatory “memory” [254]. Endpoints also need to evolve. Beyond plaque size, repair-centered measures resolution of inflammation, fibrous-cap stabilization, and post-infarct remodeling are better aligned with macrophage reprogramming biology. Agents that improve mitochondrial fitness and redox resilience, including NRF2 activators such as dimethyl fumarate, already show macrophage-centered cardioprotection in preclinical settings [71].

Concurrently, several constraints must be addressed to make this strategy clinically realistic. Metabolic nodes are shared across cell types, so off-target effects are an inherent risk. Core pathways that govern macrophage fate also sustain cardiomyocyte, endothelial, and smooth muscle energetics, and single-cell profiling after myocardial infarction supports spatially coupled metabolic changes between macrophages and cardiomyocytes [255]. This raises the possibility that systemically delivered metabolic modulators whether small molecules, nucleic acids, or nanotherapies could perturb myocardial energetics, vascular barrier function, or hematopoiesis outside the lesion, particularly with long-term dosing.

Timing is equally critical. After myocardial infarction, early inflammatory programs are required for debris clearance and repair initiation; pushing macrophages too early toward oxidative, reparative-like states may impair clearance and bias healing toward excessive fibrosis. Single-cell studies support a phased macrophage trajectory after myocardial infarction in which early glycolysis-linked inflammatory activity precedes later reparative programs [39]. Conversely, myocarditis studies suggest that mitochondrial dysfunction can amplify inflammatory activity through metabolite-linked stress programs, emphasizing that reducing inflammation without preserving essential immune control may be counterproductive in some contexts [256].

Finally, prior clinical experience cautions against assuming a wide safety window for systemic metabolic modulation. PPAR-γ agonists can improve lipid handling and inflammatory tone, but large trials highlighted adverse effects such as fluid retention and increased heart failure risk, illustrating how benefits can be offset by on-target liabilities in complex patients. In parallel, work on TREM2+ lipid-associated macrophages underscores that lipid-program activation is context-dependent and may not uniformly stabilize lesions if overstimulated [257]. Immunosuppression is another nontrivial risk. Pro-inflammatory metabolic programs contribute to host defense and the clearance of danger signals. Sustained suppression of these programs, particularly when combined with cytokine blockade such as IL-1β or IL-6 targeting, may reduce resilience in high-risk groups, including older adults and patients with diabetes, chronic kidney disease, or clonal hematopoiesis.

In summary, macrophage metabolic reprogramming is moving toward image-guided, programmable therapy (Table S7), but clinical success will depend on (i) macrophage-specific monitoring, (ii) stage-matched intervention rather than fixed polarization goals, and (iii) delivery systems that enforce lesion selectivity and reversibility. Addressing off-target metabolism, fibrosis risk, prior safety lessons, and long-term immune consequences will be essential to convert this concept into durable clinical benefit.

Clinical translation: Current status and translational gaps

While preclinical studies have defined multiple pathways governing macrophage metabolic reprogramming, translation into cardiovascular clinical practice remains at an early stage. Currently, no therapy is approved specifically to reprogram macrophage metabolism in cardiovascular diseases. Nevertheless, several clinically used drugs or advanced clinical candidates intersect with macrophage immunometabolism and therefore provide useful reference points for translation. These agents should be interpreted cautiously, because most human evidence is derived from systemic cardiovascular outcomes, metabolic biomarkers, or inflammatory markers rather than direct measurements of macrophage metabolic flux within human cardiovascular lesions (Table S8).

Metformin, a first-line therapy for type 2 diabetes, has well-documented cardiovascular benefits. Preclinical and translational models demonstrate that metformin activates AMPK, which subsequently inhibits mTOR signaling and glycolysis, thereby favoring a shift toward a reparative macrophage phenotype [258,259]. Despite robust clinical data supporting its cardioprotective role, definitively separating its systemic glucose-lowering benefits from its direct immunomodulatory effects on plaque-resident macrophages in human trials remains a significant hurdle [260].

Similarly, thiazolidinediones such as pioglitazone act as PPAR-γ agonists. In murine and in vitro models, PPAR-γ activation enhances FAO and OXPHOS, strongly skewing macrophages toward a reparative state [38]. While clinical trials have shown that pioglitazone can reduce major adverse cardiovascular events in specific patient populations [261], its broader translation is limited by systemic adverse effects, including fluid retention and an increased risk of heart failure, underscoring the risks of non-targeted metabolic modulation [262].

Beyond traditional metabolic drugs, dimethyl fumarate, an FDA-approved agent for the treatment of multiple sclerosis, has emerged as a potent immunometabolic regulator [263]. Dimethyl fumarate succinates the active site of glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH), effectively suppressing aerobic glycolysis and preventing the activation of pro-inflammatory macrophages [264]. Preclinical models of CVDs have shown that dimethyl fumarate mitigates atherosclerosis and ischemic injury [265,266]. However, clinical trials targeting dimethyl fumarate specifically for cardiovascular immunometabolism remain to be established, largely because of the challenge of repurposing an immunosuppressive drug for chronic cardiovascular conditions.

Another strategy approaching clinical translation involves targeting downstream inflammatory consequences of macrophage immunometabolism. Glycolysis-biased pro-inflammatory macrophages contribute to the release of interleukin-1β and interleukin-6. Ziltivekimab, a fully human monoclonal antibody targeting the interleukin-6 ligand, reduced systemic inflammatory and thrombotic biomarkers in patients with chronic kidney disease in the RESCUE trial and is being evaluated in the Phase III ZEUS cardiovascular outcomes trial [267]. Although ziltivekimab does not directly alter intracellular macrophage metabolic flux, its clinical development supports the translational relevance of targeting inflammatory pathways linked to macrophage immunometabolic activation.

The clinical advancement of macrophage metabolism-targeting strategies faces several major obstacles. First, significant discrepancies exist between human and murine immunology and metabolic rates, limiting the predictive value of rodent models [268]. Second, intermediate metabolites (e.g., succinate, itaconate) and metabolic enzymes are ubiquitous across various cell types. Systemic modulation of these targets inevitably leads to off-target effects, potentially compromising physiological immune defense or systemic organ function. Future clinical translation will heavily depend on the development of advanced delivery platforms, such as targeted nanomedicine, to specifically deliver metabolic modulators to tissue-resident or recruited macrophages without disturbing systemic metabolic homeostasis.

A further translational priority is subset-aware macrophage targeting. Single-cell omics indicates that cardiovascular lesions contain protective resident macrophages, inflammatory CCR2-positive macrophages, TREM2-positive lipid-associated macrophages, proliferating macrophages, and lipid-engorged foam-cell populations. These subsets differ in metabolic state and therapeutic vulnerability. Therefore, future nanomedicine design should move beyond pan-macrophage delivery and incorporate subset-enrichment markers, lesion microenvironmental triggers, and payloads matched to metabolic vulnerabilities. Such strategies may allow pathogenic macrophage programs to be modulated while preserving protective resident populations, although their safety and specificity require validation in human cardiovascular lesions.

Technological frontiers: Overcoming nanomedicine translational barriers

Beyond these clinical gaps, several technical barriers must be addressed. Although nanoplatforms offer specific targeting and the ability to reprogram macrophages from pro-inflammatory to reparative phenotypes, multiple challenges remain for clinical translation.

A major clinical concern is the off-target accumulation of nanoparticles. While nanomedicines are designed to target atherosclerotic plaques or ischemic tissues, a significant fraction is often sequestered by the reticuloendothelial system (RES), primarily in the liver and spleen. This off-target biodistribution necessitates rigorous long-term toxicity profiling to ensure that continuous systemic clearance does not induce hepatic or splenic toxicity [269]. The physicochemical properties of nanocarriers (e.g., surface charge, coating materials) can inadvertently trigger immune responses. Opsonization by plasma proteins can lead to rapid clearance or unintended complement activation, which may exacerbate systemic inflammation rather than resolving it [270].

Furthermore, the complex bi-directional interplay between macrophage metabolic reprogramming and the cardiovascular lesional microenvironment [124] makes the precise in vivo targeting of nanomedicines highly challenging. Addressing off-target toxicity and ensuring the reproducibility of these highly specialized, metabolism-modulating nanomedicines under Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) standards remain critical hurdles for clinical translation [271]. Ensuring consistent nanoparticle size, drug-loading efficiency, and surface functionalization across large-scale batches is crucial for clinical reliability [272].

While engineered nanoplatforms show great potential in repurposing macrophage metabolism towards reparative phenotypes, their transition from bench to bedside is heavily regulated. Many targeted nanomedicines are classified as complex biological or combination products. The regulatory pathways for these therapeutics demand extensive preclinical validation of pharmacokinetics, pharmacodynamics, and safety profiles, significantly increasing the time and cost required for clinical approval [272]. Addressing these translational gaps will be essential for realizing the full clinical potential of macrophage-targeted nanotherapies.

Ultimately, bridging the gap from bench to bedside for these metabolism-modulating nanomedicines will require a rigorous effort to address the critical bottlenecks of long-term in vivo safety, batch-to-batch reproducibility, and complex regulatory compliance.
