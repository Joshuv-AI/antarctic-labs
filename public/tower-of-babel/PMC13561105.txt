Physiological Demands and Contributors to Variance of Core Temperature in 162 Recreational Runners during Distance Running in a Warm-Humid Environment
Authors: YU SAKAMOTO, SHARIFAH BADRIYAH ALHADAD, XIAOHAN ZHANG, BING YANG TAN, WEE HON ANG, LYDIA YU LI LAW, SURIYA PRAKAASH, SEBASTIEN RACINAIS, BORJA MUNIZ-PARDOS, DAVID BANDIERA, ELENA COMADRAN DE BARNOLA, DANI PEÑA IGLESIAS, YANNIS PITSILADIS, JASON KAI WEI LEE
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Purpose:To determine the physiological demands and their associations with body core temperature (Tc) of tropical recreational runners during mass participation distance running in a warm-humid environment (Dry bulb temperature: 27.2°C ± 0.4°C, relative humidity: 87 ± 2%).Methods:One hundred sixty-two individuals participated in a 21 km (n = 84) or 10 km (n = 78) race. Participants’ demographics were recorded in a prerace questionnaire, and in-race measurements of environmental conditions, heart rate (HR, n = 115), Tc (n = 102), four-site skin temperature (Tsk, n = 34), and fluid balance (n = 36) were assessed. Real-time monitoring of HR, Tc, and geolocation was conducted via a multiuser dashboard. Race split and finishing times were extracted from official results. Correlation and multiple linear regression analyses were performed between various parameters and peak Tc.Results:Participants achieved peak HR (21 km: 183 ± 9 [154–209] bpm, 10 km: 180 ± 10 [156–204] bpm), Tc (21 km: 39.4 ± 0.6 [38.3–40.8]°C, 10 km: 39.3 ± 0.6 [38.2–41.1]°C), and Tsk (21 km: 34.0 ± 0.6 [32.8–35.4]°C, 10 km: 33.7 ± 0.8 [31.9–35.3]°C). Mean Tc was higher (P < 0.05) in 21 km (38.7 ± 0.5 [37.9–39.8]°C) than in 10 km (38.5 ± 0.4 [37.2–39.9]°C). Tsk exhibited an “inverted-U” profile in the 21 km but plateaued in the 10 km race. Body mass loss was −2.5 ± 1.1 (−5.5 to −0.7)% and −1.3 ± 0.7 (−2.4 to +0.5)% for the 21 km and 10 km participants, respectively. Starting Tc (18%), mean HR (13%), body surface area (11%), and average speed (9%), but not age, estimated maximal aerobic capacity, nor finishing time, contributed to peak Tc.Conclusions:Recreational runners experienced high cardiovascular and thermal demands. We observed an “inverted-U” Tsk profile in the 21 km race in contrast to a plateau commonly described in laboratory-based findings. Starting Tc, mean HR, body surface area, and average speed were independently associated with interindividual differences in peak Tc. Real-time monitoring and contributors of peak Tc may inform future development of targeted strategies to optimize the safety of recreational populations competing in the heat.

The body’s thermal equilibrium is challenged during vigorous exercise due to increased metabolic heat production (1). In response, heat dissipation mechanisms are initiated. However, when heat production exceeds heat dissipation, core temperature (Tc) will rise uncontrollably, leading to heightened thermoregulatory and cardiorespiratory strain (2,3), increasing the risk of adverse medical conditions such as heat-related injuries and illnesses (4). Heat dissipation, and the ability to tolerate heat can be influenced by multiple factors such as environmental conditions (e.g., ambient temperature and humidity), hydration status, task (e.g., exercise intensity and duration), and personal (e.g., body morphology and aerobic fitness) characteristics (5). As these factors vary between individuals, mass profiling of real-world events is essential to capture the range of physiological responses, identify individuals at potentially greater risk, and ensure timely interventions.

Previous work has demonstrated that elite athletes in competitive cycling, running, or race-walking events have reported that individuals can tolerate Tc as high as 41.7°C (6–10). However, while thermal responses have been effectively characterized in field-based events, few studies have characterized cardiovascular responses concurrently (11) despite our understanding of the concomitant increase in cardiovascular strain that develops as heart rate (HR) increases and circulating blood volume and cardiac output decline, impairing oxygen delivery to working muscles and brain (12,13).

Indeed, most previous studies in similar environments only assessed Tc, finishing times, and/or fluid balance measures (14,15) but did not include measures of HR or skin temperature (Tsk). Additionally, existing studies conducted in laboratory conditions suggest Tsk to be a prominent factor determining aerobic exercise performance and capacity (16,17). However, to our knowledge, only one other study has characterized Tsk response during a mass participation event in elite populations (6,9). Racinais et al. (9) demonstrated that Tsk and the Tc-Tsk gradient, rather than Tc, were highly related to performance and may be important indicators of thermal tolerance for elite athletes during outdoor events. A separate field study, though not during a mass participation event, had previously collected chest Tsk data adding to evidence suggesting that Tsk and Tc-Tsk gradient, alone or in combination with Tc, contribute to fatigue development in the heat (18). However, it must be acknowledged that Tsk measures in these studies are not without limitations, having been obtained from thermal cameras (6,9) or skin temperature patches on the chest (18). Thermal cameras allowed only snapshots of Tsk data at given locations, and the reliability of Tsk measured could be impaired by clothing worn and the sweat layer on the skin surface (19,20). Meanwhile, measurements from a single-site skin temperature patch placed on the chest may not be representative of overall Tsk (21) as thermal distribution and convectional heat loss can differ by body parts, and measurements could be affected by the technique used to attach the patches (22,23).

Further, despite the increasing number of recreational runners internationally, a comprehensive physiological profile (Tc, with HR, and Tsk) of recreational runners in field-based events remains scarce. Aerobic fitness can affect heat dissipation mechanisms and subsequent thermal responses (5,24). Trained individuals can withstand greater cumulative heat strain before fatigue development compared with less-fit, recreational athletes (25). Recreational runners generally form the majority of participants in mass participation distance running events. Other than their lower aerobic fitness and thermoregulatory efficiency, they may also differ in body composition and movement efficiency (26) and may also be poorly informed regarding heat-related challenges compared with elite athletes, posing additional risks during warm/hot weather endurance events.

With global warming and growing popularity of amateur sports participation, there is also growing interest in utilizing wearable sensors to assess physiological parameters (e.g., HR and Tc) in real time during races, providing insights into the physiological demands (27) of recreational runners. Systems that integrate continuous real-time physiological and geolocation data can be utilized for on-site informed decision-making, which facilitates targeted and timely interventions (28), possibly augmenting current safety protocols established for mass participation events. For instance, implementing a dashboard that provides real-time physiological and geolocation tracking of recreational runners during annual endurance events hosted in similar conditions (e.g., Rio de Janeiro marathons, etc.), or even in cooler environments (e.g., Boston and London marathons, etc.), where exertional heat illnesses (EHI) have been reported (29), may allow early detection and interventions that can prevent the development of serious medical complications. Studies to date have explored this concept of real-time monitoring under controlled conditions, with different cohorts and on a smaller scale (28,30). However, its ability to be deployed at scale and field feasibility has not yet been demonstrated, particularly in a mass participation distance running event.

Thus, we utilized a real-time monitoring system to profile the cardiovascular and thermal demands (including continuous Tsk monitoring) of tropical recreational runners during mass participation distance running in a warm-humid environment at the 2023 SAFRA Singapore Bay Run and Army-Half Marathon. A secondary aim was to investigate the relationship between peak Tc and various physiological and personal characteristics to determine their associations with peak Tc in tropical recreational populations competing in the heat, through an exploratory and hypothesis-generating approach.

METHODS

Study Design

Ethical approval was obtained from the National University of Singapore Institutional Review Board (Reference code: NUS-IRB-2023-414) in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Participants were recruited from the pool of Singapore Armed Forces regulars who signed up for the 2023 SAFRA Singapore Bay Run and Army-Half Marathon and were tropical natives of Singapore (mean dry-bulb temperature: 27.4°C, relative humidity: 83%) (31,32). The event offered two race distances, 21 km and 10 km, starting at 4:30 am and 6:30 am, respectively. Individuals were excluded from the study if they had any history of gastrointestinal surgery, inflammatory and motility bowel disorders, or were scheduled to undergo magnetic resonance imaging within 7 d from consumption of the telemetric capsule. There were no restrictions on gender and ethnicity. 162 participants (7% female) provided written informed consent to participate in the study and successfully completed the race. Participants reported in running attire consisting of a T-shirt or singlet and shorts or tights. Environmental conditions, HR, and Tc were continuously measured for all participants. Prerace, but not morning, urine specific gravity (USG), continuous Tsk measurements, estimated fluid intake during the race, and pre- and postrace nude body mass were additionally measured in a subsample of 36/162 participants.

Prerace Measurements

Prerace questionnaire

All participants completed a prerace questionnaire, which surveyed their sex, age, height, and body mass. Participants also provided their most recent 2.4 km run time recorded as part of their yearly physical fitness test to allow estimation of maximal aerobic capacity (VO2max) (33).

Prerace hydration status

Prerace mid-stream urine samples were collected, and USG was assessed using a digital refractometer (UG-alpha, Atago, Japan). As these were not first void, rapid high-volume fluid intake prerace could have transiently lowered USG despite ongoing dehydration, due to delayed water absorption. Further, any fluid consumed between the assessment and the start of the race would not have been accounted for.

In-Race Measurements

Environmental conditions

Environmental conditions were measured using three portable climate monitoring devices (QUESTemp 36/44, TSI, Shoreview, MN) mounted on a tripod at a height of ~1.4 m located at the 0 km, 4 km and 6 km mark, respectively, along the near loop 21 km course with an out-and-back extension and near loop-10 km race route. The racecourse spanned an approximate longitudinal extent of 10.5 km and a lateral extent of 5 km. Data were recorded at 5 min intervals, and average values were derived from the three devices.

Heart rate

HR was recorded using a smartwatch (Galaxy Watch5 LTE, Samsung Electronics Co., Ltd., Yeongtong-gu, South Korea) with an optical photoplethysmography sensor fitted on participants’ wrists. Photoplethysmography-derived HR from the Samsung Galaxy Watch series has demonstrated acceptable agreement with electrocardiogram-derived HR, even during vigorous activities (34) performed in hot conditions, resulting in wet skin surfaces in some (35). HR was recorded at 15 s intervals and averaged over 60 s for analysis. HRmax and %HRmax were calculated from the available HR data (36).

Body core temperature

Tc was recorded via a telemetric capsule (e-Celsius, BodyCap, Caen, France) ingested 8–10 h before the respective race start times. Tc data were relayed to a wrist-based gateway device (ePerf Connect gateway, BodyCap, Caen, France) fitted on the opposite wrist from the HR watch. Tc was recorded at 30 s intervals and averaged over 60 s for analysis. Linear interpolation was employed to obtain missing Tc values of individuals (n = 23) who had <20% of data missing intermittently during the race (37).

Skin temperature

Tsk was recorded with skin thermistors (iButton, Maxim Integrated Products, Inc., San Jose, CA). The bottom side of the skin thermistors was attached to the skin on the mid-belly of the chest, arm, thigh, and shin on the right-hand side of the body using hypoallergenic polyacrylate adhesive tape (Fixomull, Smith and Nephew Ltd., Auckland, New Zealand), made of breathable and vapor-permeable material. Skin thermistors on the chest, arm, and thigh were fully or partially covered depending on the participants’ attire, while skin thermistors on the shin were uncovered. Tsk was recorded at 15 s intervals. Weighted Tsk was derived by Ramanathan’s four-site equation (38) and averaged over 60 s for analysis.

Fluid Balance Parameters

Pre- and postrace nude body mass were measured to the nearest 0.02 kg using a precision weighing scale (BBA211-5CA150, Mettler Toledo, Greifensee, Switzerland). Postrace, participants reported the number of 100 mL cups consumed during the race to estimate fluid ingestion. Sweat rate was estimated as the difference in pre- and postrace nude body mass and total fluid ingested during the race divided by race duration. Fluid loss through urination was not accounted for in this estimation. Pre- and postrace nude body mass loss was also used to calculate the percentage of body mass loss (% body mass loss).

Race Metrics

Race start time, split times (21 km: Split 1 [0–3.2 km], Split 2 [3.2–6.5 km], Split 3 [6.5–12.2 km], Split 4 [12.2–18.0 km], and Split 5 [18.0–21.0 km], 10 km: Split 1 [0–4.8 km], Split 2 [4.8–7.0 km], and Split 3 [7.0–10.0 km]) and finishing times were extracted from the official race results.

Real-Time Monitoring

The smartwatch, coupled with an electronic SIM fitted on participants’ wrists, was connected via Bluetooth to a wrist-based gateway device (e-Gateway, BodyCap, Caen, France). This allowed for real-time monitoring of Tc, HR, and geolocation profiles. These variables were displayed on a dashboard. Further details about this transmission in real-time have been discussed previously (28,30).

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism version 10 (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA). HR, Tc, Tsk, and fluid balance data excluded from and included in the analysis are outlined in Table 1. The differences in participant characteristics, physiological responses, and fluid balance parameters were analyzed using two-tailed unpaired t tests for a descriptive and observational comparison between the two race distances (21 km and 10 km) and analysis of variance when comparing physiological responses across splits within each race distance (e.g., Tc differences between splits in the 21 km). Bonferroni adjustments were applied for multiple comparisons. Eta-squared (ƞ2) was reported as a measure of effect size with the following classification of small (0.01), medium (0.06), and large (≥0.14) (39). A three-step approach was employed in the selection of independent variables for the multiple linear regression model. First, Pearson product-moment correlation coefficient (r) was used to examine the relationships between various parameters and peak Tc. The strength of the relationships was classified accordingly: very weak (<0.20), weak (0.20–0.39), moderate (0.40–0.59), strong (0.60–0.79), and very strong (≥0.80) (40). Second, variance inflation factor collinearity analysis was used to assess for multicollinearity and exclude redundant contributors. Third, stepwise regression was used to finalize the independent input variables. Hierarchical multiple regression analysis was employed to account for starting Tc as a covariate in the first model, followed by the inclusion of other physiological and performance variables in the second layer to determine significant independent contributors for peak Tc. All data were presented as mean ± standard deviation unless otherwise stated. Statistical significance was accepted as P < 0.05.

Cardiovascular and Thermal Responses During the Race

Mean (P = 0.812) and peak HR (P = 0.119) did not differ between races, with the highest peak HR attained during 21 km and 10 km races being 209 and 204 bpm, respectively. 98% (n = 51) of 21 km and 95% (n = 60) of 10 km participants achieved %HRmax > 77%, indicative of vigorous to maximal effort (36). Mean Tc was higher in 21 km than 10 km (38.7 ± 0.5 vs 38.5 ± 0.4°C, P = 0.048, ƞ2 = 0.04). There was no difference in peak Tc (P = 0.713) between both races. 19% from 21 km (n = 9) and 9% from 10 km (n = 5) had peak Tc > 40°C (Table 3). The highest peak Tc recorded in the 21 km and 10 km races was 40.8°C and 41.1°C, respectively. No EHI cases were reported. Mean (P = 0.735) and peak Tsk (P = 0.274) also did not differ between races, with the highest peak Tsk of 35.4°C and 35.3°C obtained during 21 km and 10 km, respectively. Figure 1 illustrates the individual Tsk profiles. There was no difference in mean Tc-Tsk gradient (P = 0.225). However, the peak Tc-Tsk gradient was higher in 21 km than in 10 km (7.3 ± 1.2 vs 6.2 ± 1.0°C, P = 0.022, ƞ2 = 0.06).

Cardiovascular and Thermal Responses Between Splits

In the 21 km race, mean HR and Tc progressively increased and were elevated above start and split 1, from split 2 (3.2 km~) onwards (all P < 0.001; Fig. 2A, B). Mean Tsk was elevated above start at split 2 (P = 0.002) before declining, with mean Tsk lower in split 4 (P = 0.024; 12.2 km~) and 5 (P < 0.001; 18.0 km~) than split 2 (Fig. 2C). Average speed was faster in split 2 compared with the other splits (P < 0.001; Fig. 2D). In the 10 km race, mean HR was elevated above start from split 1 onwards (P < 0.001) and above split 1 at split 2 (P = 0.004; 4.8 km~) and 3 (P = 0.003; 7.0 km~) (Fig. 2E). Mean Tc progressively increased and was elevated above baseline and split 1 at split 2 and 3, respectively (all P < 0.001; Fig. 2F). Fluid Balance Parameters During the Race

Estimated fluid ingestion (P = 0.017, ƞ2 = 0.16) and % body mass loss (P = 0.002, ƞ2 = 0.26) were higher in the 21 km than 10 km, although sweat rate was similar (P = 0.435). A >2% body mass loss was observed in 60% (n = 12) of 21 km and 12% (n = 2) of 10 km participants. However, participants with >2% body mass loss did not have higher peak Tc (21 km: 39.5 ± 0.6°C vs 39.2 ± 0.4°C, P = 0.339; 10 km: 39.2 ± 0.6°C vs 38.9 ± 0.5°C, P = 0.582).

Correlation and Multiple Linear Regression Analysis

Correlational and multiple linear regression analysis were performed on 77 participants who had included HR and Tc data. Average speed, starting Tc, mean HR, and sweat rate had moderate positive correlations, while % body mass loss had a moderate negative correlation with peak Tc. Starting HR had a weak positive correlation with peak Tc (Fig. 3A). After variable selection based on Pearson correlation, variance inflation factor collinearity analysis, and stepwise regression, only body surface area (BSA), average speed, starting Tc, and mean HR were retained in the final model. Hierarchical regression was conducted with starting Tc entered in the first block, which alone accounted for approximately 27% of the variance in peak Tc (Adjusted R2 = 0.268). In the second block, the addition of BSA, mean HR, and average speed increased the total explained variance to approximately 51% (Adjusted R2 = 0.506; Fig. 3B), with a moderate relationship between fitted and actual peak Tc (Fig. 3C). This indicates that these physiological and performance variables significantly contributed to peak Tc beyond starting Tc. Specifically, mean HR accounted for approximately 13% of the explained variance, BSA for 11%, and average speed for 9%, with all predictors emerging as statistically significant (P < 0.05). The model had high tolerance values, indicating acceptable collinearity and model stability. The relative contribution of each independent variable to peak Tc was quantified using standardized regression coefficients (41), with starting Tc as the largest contributing variable (Table 4).

DISCUSSION

We profiled the physiological demands and determined their associations with Tc of tropical recreational runners in two endurance race distances during mass participation distance running in a warm-humid environment (dry-bulb temperature: 27.2 ± 0.4°C, wet-bulb globe temperature: 26.0 ± 0.3°C, relative humidity: 87 ± 2%). To the authors’ knowledge, this study is the first to incorporate large-scale (n = 162), continuous real-time monitoring of HR and Tc measurements on a dashboard during a real-life mass participation event, extending prior proof-of-concept works that were conducted with different cohorts in smaller-scale settings (28,30). This enabled us to obtain a holistic understanding of cardiovascular and thermal demands experienced by 162 tropical recreational runners during endurance events of multiple distances. Overall, tropical recreational runners running 21 km and 10 km races at near maximal efforts (>77%HRmax) (36) in a warm-humid environment attained elevated HR (21 km: 183 ± 9 bpm, 10 km: 180 ± 10 bpm), Tc (21 km: 39.4 ± 0.6°C, 10 km: 39.3 ± 0.6°C), Tsk (21 km: 34.0 ± 0.6°C, 10 km: 33.7 ± 0.8°C), and body mass loss (21 km: −2.5 ± 1.1%, 10 km: −1.3 ± 0.7%). Though descriptive, the differences of these cardiovascular and thermal demands across two distances reflect the combined influence that exercise duration, intensity, and pacing strategies may have on physiological responses during distance running in real-world conditions. Tsk profile in the 21 km race exhibited an “inverted-U” shape in contrast to a plateau commonly described in laboratory-based studies, demonstrating real-world Tsk responses are influenced by environmental variability and movement dynamics not fully captured in laboratory settings. Further, this study also utilized 77 individual datasets in a hierarchical multiple regression to identify potential contributors to the variance of peak Tc. To our knowledge, this represents one of the largest datasets used in this exploratory manner to date, and we found that starting Tc, mean HR, BSA, and average speed were significantly associated with peak Tc during endurance events. Finally, although the real-time monitoring dashboard was not utilized to make in-race medical decisions, this study demonstrated the scalability and feasibility for broader implementation in future mass participation events.

Cardiovascular and Thermal Demands during Race

We observed a progressive increase in HR upon the onset of exercise, followed by a sustained plateau until the cessation of exercise. Peak HR as high as 209 bpm in 21 km and 204 bpm in 10 km were recorded, in line with participants running at 89 ± 6% HRmax. Previous studies on recreational runners reported similar mean HR (156 ± 11 [132–178] bpm) during a 10 km run under heat stress (42) and peak HR (192 ± 12 [179–209] bpm) during a half-marathon in thermoneutral conditions (43). This indicates that the combined effects of heat stress and low aerobic fitness can result in elevated HR and a heightened cardiovascular strain in recreational runners, despite running at a relatively modest pace of 10 km·h−1 compared with elite runners who can sustain speeds of 15–22.5 km·h−1 (6,44).

Cardiovascular and thermal demands observed in the current study cohesively support existing notions of Tsk-mediated blood distribution (45) and the consequent strain imposed on the cardiovascular system, characterized by increased HR as a result of reduced cardiac filling and stroke volume (46). Such an increase in cardiovascular strain observed in recreational runners may be caused by the redistribution of blood flow to the skin (12,13), consequent to elevated Tsk. In both races, Tsk declined upon the onset of exercise, likely contributed by the increased air velocity generated by movement, increased sweat production (47), and prioritization of blood flow to working skeletal muscles (45). The initial decline was followed by a progressive rise in Tsk, suggesting a reflex afflux of warm blood and cutaneous vasodilation for heat dissipation. We speculate that the progressive rise in Tc and Tsk during the races triggered more blood flow to the skin to facilitate heat dissipation from the skin surface, compromising venous return and cardiac filling, causing a marked increase in cardiovascular strain (48). Interestingly, Tsk profile of the first 10 km of the 21 km race closely resembled the Tsk response in the 10 km race. However, Tsk profiles differed between the race distances after the rise, whereby Tsk exhibited an “inverted-U” profile in the 21 km (Fig. 1A–C) but plateaued in the 10 km race (Fig. 1D–F). This suggests that the responses were similar in absolute time but different in relative time. The “inverted-U” profile in the 21 km differs from existing laboratory studies, where Tsk typically increases at the onset of exercise and subsequently plateaus (16,49). However, this difference between laboratory- and field-based Tsk profiles has been discussed by Aylwin et al. (6) and may be partially explained by fluctuations in relative air velocity induced by variations in running speed, which may not be fully recreated in laboratory settings (7). The increase in running speed led to an increase in air velocity, which could facilitate convective heat loss (50,51). However, as recreational runners may not be as fast or as efficient at offloading heat compared with elite runners (26), increasing running speed and intensity may simply result in a concomitant rise in Tc and a corresponding increase in Tsk. Thus, fluctuations in relative air velocity may be insufficient to reduce Tsk for recreational runners. Therefore, one possible explanation is that the combined influence of air velocity and running speed underlies the complementary trends in average speed and Tsk profile observed in the 21 km event. In fact, the fastest average speed coincided with the peak Tsk in split 2, after which both average speed and Tsk progressively declined (Fig. 1). However, this interpretation remains speculative as direct measurements of airflow or pacing-related aerodynamics were not obtained in the present study. In contrast, the relative stability of average speed coincided with the relative stability of Tsk observed in the 10 km race.

Prior research has demonstrated that real-time changes in Tsk are highly sensitive to environmental variation and movement dynamics (50,52). Our study builds on this by providing field-based validation in a large cohort of tropical recreational runners during a real-world endurance event. Continuous, ecologically valid Tsk data of scale in the field remain scarce in the literature (6,9) and provide insight into the complex thermoregulatory responses that laboratory models often fail to fully emulate. Furthermore, skin thermistors were utilized to continuously monitor Tsk as compared with alternative techniques such as infrared thermal cameras or intermittent thermography. Infrared methods typically do not capture continuous data during movement and can be confounded by external factors such as clothing and environmental reflections (19,20). Thus, by employing skin thermistors, Tsk fluctuations associated with changes in blood flow, sweating responses, and environmental exposures could be observed. This strengthens the novelty of our findings, highlighting Tsk as a highly dynamic marker of thermoregulatory strain (21) that responds sensitively to both intrinsic cardiovascular changes and extrinsic factors, such as airflow resulting from running speed and fluctuating environmental conditions. Such nuanced thermoregulatory patterns have not been previously documented in large cohorts, particularly among tropical recreational runners who may be partially heat acclimatized due to their habitual environmental heat exposure (32).

Nevertheless, taken together, our findings characterized the cardiovascular, thermal strain, and Tsk dynamics of recreational runners during mass participation endurance events in warm-humid environments. While such physiological responses may be expected during exercise in the heat, our study builds on previous literature demonstrating that these responses (e.g., peak Tc [21 km: 39.4 ± 0.6 [38.3–40.8]°C, 10 km: 39.3 ± 0.6 [38.2–41.1]°C) can be tolerated by nonelite recreational runners while remaining asymptomatic of EHI at the end of the race (7,8,14). This suggests a greater degree of thermal tolerance in recreational populations than previously assumed, particularly among those who may be partially heat acclimatized (32), benefiting from reduced sensitivity to heat (53) and more efficient thermoregulatory abilities (54,55) despite potentially lower aerobic capacity. Furthermore, our continuous monitoring of Tsk revealed complex fluctuations that reflect the interplay of cardiovascular changes, environmental factors, and exercise intensity. These Tsk responses observed underscore the intricate balance of heat dissipation mechanisms operating under real-world conditions, which laboratory studies may not fully capture. This thus highlights the importance of incorporating continuous Tsk monitoring in future real-world field events.

Body Mass Loss

Progressive dehydration results in reduced blood volume, causing a reduction in blood flow and oxygen distribution to exercising muscles during prolonged exercise, thereby accelerating fatigue (5,56). The recent consensus statement recommends drinking to minimize body mass loss >2% and to encourage sufficient fluid intake before the event to minimize the inevitable dehydration that occurs while exercising in the heat (57). However, elite athletes commonly experience >2% body mass loss while competing in marathons or race walks in heat (58–60). Our findings provide insight that recreational populations can also withstand high levels of dehydration (up to 5.5%, equivalent to 5.1 kg body mass loss) without excessive increases in peak Tc when competing in a warm-humid environment. Dehydration during endurance events is not unexpected, and our findings demonstrate similar estimated sweat rates (1.3 ± 0.4 L·h−1) to previous endurance events in similar environments (15). Notably, mean fluid replenishment rates across both the 21 km and 10 km races were ~50 (0–277)% of sweat loss, considerably higher than in previous studies conducted in similar environments (~15% of sweat loss) (15) and under compensable heat stress (~30% of sweat loss) (61). One individual in the 10 km race also gained body mass postrace (+0.52%). Although marginal, the potential for individuals to overhydrate, with >100% fluid replenishment rates and an increase in postrace body mass, increases the risk of exercise-associated hyponatremia (62,63). This highlights the need to educate less-informed recreational runners on the dangers of improper hydration when participating in endurance events in warm-humid environments.

Potential Contributors to Variance of Peak Tc

To add to existing literature, which explored modulators of Tc (15,42) and performance (6), we sought to explore potential contributors to the variance of peak Tc of tropical recreational runners and provide contextual insight into interindividual variability in peak Tc of recreational populations competing in the heat, an area where field data still remains limited. While exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than mechanistic, to our knowledge, this is among the largest regression analyses of in-race thermophysiological responses (77 individual datasets) in the literature, strengthening the reliability and generalisability of our findings. Starting Tc accounted for the highest variance (18%) in peak Tc in our model. In support, participants with elevated starting Tc had exacerbated hyperthermia, attaining higher peak Tc of 39.6 ± 0.6°C in both 21 km and 10 km races, as compared with individuals with lower starting Tc who attained peak Tc of 39.1 ± 0.5°C in both races. However, no differences in finishing times were observed. This extends current evidence that elevated Tc is not always associated with accelerated fatigue and adverse outcomes, especially in heat-acclimatized tropical natives (14,24) who reside in consistently warm-humid environments (mean Tdb: 27.4°C, RH: 83%) (31,32). Mean HR and BSA each accounted for 13% and 11% of the variance in peak Tc, respectively, in line with prior evidence demonstrating that BSA influences heat exchange processes along with the skin-ambient temperature gradient (64,65). However, greater BSA relative to body mass may also coincide with other factors influencing thermal strain. Larger individuals typically produce more absolute metabolic heat during exercise (65), which may offset the advantage of increased surface area for heat dissipation. Differences in morphology, such as limb length and body shape, further affect airflow exposure and evaporative cooling efficiency (66). Additionally, BSA often covaries with body size and exercise intensity, meaning that individuals with greater BSA may experience higher absolute workloads (67), amplifying heat production and core temperature responses. Finally, average speed only accounted for 9% of the variance in peak Tc (Fig. 3B), in contrast with a previous study where the average speed of 25 elite runners during a 21 km road race accounted for 42% of the variance in Tc change (15). These differences may be explained by the sample size and the heterogeneous study population of less aerobically fit recreational runners compared with an elite population. Notably, previous work conducted in laboratory conditions (dry-bulb temperature: 33.0 ± 0.2°C, wet-bulb temperature: 26.8 ± 2.2°C, relative humidity: 61 ± 9%) identified that Tc of recreational runners (n = 46) after a self-paced 10 km run was modulated by similar parameters including HR (Standardized β coefficients (β) = 0.46), sweat rate (β = −0.40), Tc-Tsk gradient (β = 0.36), wet bulb globe temperature (WBGT) (β = 0.33), initial Tc (β = 0.28), running speed (β = 0.24) and VO2max (β = 0.23), in descending order of importance (42). The convergence in predictors strengthens our confidence in the relevance of these potential contributors for peak Tc within a recreational population. Differences in the relative contributions of these factors likely reflect differences in study design (laboratory vs field-based study), run distance (10 km only vs 10 km and 21 km), and the fact that our population consisted of tropical natives (32). In addition, 49% of the variance in peak Tc remained unexplained in our final model. This could be due to parameters that were assessed but could not be included in the regression (i.e., sweat rate, Tc-Tsk gradient, WBGT) (42) and factors not determined in our study, such as clothing insulation (68). Although we corroborate Tsk as a dynamic marker of thermoregulatory strain in the section above, we were unable to include Tsk or sweat rate-related parameters in the regression analysis as they were only measured in a subset of our participants. Thus, conclusions drawn from the regression analysis are associative, and mechanistic conclusions are limited by these unmeasured variables. Similarly, environmental parameters such as WBGT were not included as they were reported as course-level averages (0, 4, and 6 km) rather than participant-specific exposures. This may have attenuated observed associations between heat load and physiological responses. Nonetheless, these task-specific (i.e., exercise intensity−mean HR and average speed) and personal (i.e., BSA and starting Tc) characteristics represent potential contributors to the variance of Tc that can be considered when determining thermal tolerance of recreational individuals. Furthermore, our findings complement and extend the prevailing literature on factors associated with Tc and highlight the importance of field-based validation of laboratory-derived predictors due to the heterogeneous nature of real-world environments.

Real-Time Monitoring as Proof-of-Concept

In the context of rising global temperatures and increasing participation in amateur endurance events, the ability to augment existing safety protocols and potentially prevent serious medical complications in real-time is crucial. As such, in the current study, Tc and HR were monitored live by the research team during the race via a central dashboard, which also included GPS tracking. The system allowed researchers to track participants’ physiological responses throughout the event, serving as a proof-of-concept for scalable, real-time surveillance during future mass participation events. Furthermore, the identification of personal- and task-specific factors associated with peak Tc can enhance the value of these real-time systems. For example, based on our regression analysis results, elevated starting Tc or lower BSA may be used to flag individuals at greater risk prerace. Additionally, integrating potential predictive markers of thermoregulatory strain such as Tsk, into future live dashboards may allow race officials and medical staff to dynamically assess thermal strain, stratify risks, and initiate targeted interventions before clinical symptoms develop. Nonetheless, building upon prior proof-of-concept work (28,30), the present study demonstrates the feasibility and scalability of such a system in a mass participation context and a large cohort (n = 162).

However, translating real-time physiological monitoring into clinical practice requires careful consideration of both clinical and ethical challenges. In this study, the purpose was to assess its feasibility and scalability in a mass participation context, rather than clinical application, and thus, real-time interventions were not triggered. Although we demonstrated the ability to effectively capture and relay physiological data in real time during a mass participation event, the lack of clear, validated thresholds for safety intervention limits its immediate clinical applicability. Recreational runners in this study achieved elevated Tc without displaying symptoms of EHI, suggesting that reliable predictors of EHI still remain to be determined. Without robust, evidence-based clinical decision pathways, the use of real-time monitoring for clinical decision-making during mass participation events could risk potential false positives, technological failures, and inconsistent decision-making. In addition, the adoption of real-time monitoring introduces notable ethical considerations, including participant data privacy, the establishment of validated thresholds for intervention, and potential liability when decisions are based on live physiological data. These issues may be less pronounced in military contexts but represent critical barriers to widespread implementation in civilian endurance events.

Thus, our findings should be interpreted primarily as a proof-of-concept demonstration of the technological feasibility of a real-time monitoring system in a mass participation event. Finally, further research is still needed to establish clear and robust clinical decision-making frameworks and ethical safeguards to support the safe and effective implementation of real-time monitoring systems in mass-participation settings.

Limitations

Given the opportunistic and field‑based nature of this study, several methodological constraints should be noted. First, there was limited control over pre- and mid-race participant‑related factors (i.e., starting Tc, clothing, and mid-race fluid intake). Prerace Tc was also not experimentally regulated, resulting in elevated starting Tc at the start of the race (e.g., as high as 38.9°C), potentially from participants’ self-directed warm-ups, movement from the study site to start line (approximately 1.5 km), and/or the crowding of participants at the start line. Participants’ clothing was also dissimilar (e.g., sleeved and sleeveless shirts, shorts, and long tights), which may affect the comparability of the Tsk measurements. Although such variability is expected and increases the ecological validity of our findings, the analysis of Tsk data should be interpreted as exploratory, as the magnitude and direction of bias remain unknown. Further, mid-race fluid intake was self-reported, and urine volumes were unavailable, limiting the accuracy of individual fluid balance calculations. Although such uncontrolled elements are inherent to large-scale field profiling, future studies could account for individual variability (i.e., warm-up routine or race attire) where possible, and implement structured approaches (e.g., place designated water bottles at each water station) to reduce and report bias.

Further, there were unmeasured variables (i.e., course-level average environmental exposure but not individual environmental conditions, Tsk, sweat rate, and Tc-Tsk gradient were only available for a subset of participants) that were not included in the regression analysis or the monitoring dashboard. These unmeasured variables can influence Tc response during exercise heat stress and, therefore, in their absence, the associations presented should be interpreted as highly exploratory and hypothesis-generating. To strengthen the evidence base, future work should incorporate comprehensive monitoring of variables that influence Tc across all participants to better characterize the thermoregulatory strain of recreational runners.

Additionally, the participant cohort consisted of military personnel. Based on their estimated VO2max, their aerobic fitness levels fall within the expected range for recreational runners (69,70). However, their greater exposure to structured training regimens may have conferred a greater degree of heat acclimatization, which could limit the extent to which this cohort fully represents the broader recreational running population.

This study also initially intended to investigate EHI cases among tropical recreational runners and the physiological demands faced by symptomatic individuals to compare against asymptomatic individuals, and against prior research on elite populations. However, there were no reported EHI cases, preventing such comparisons. Finally, as an observational study, clinical decision-making based on the data was not within the scope of our work. Hence, no clinical interventions were initiated for any observed elevated Tc. Future studies on determining and validating thresholds for clinical interventions are critical for the actual implementation and usage of real-time monitoring systems in mass participation events.

CONCLUSIONS

This is the first study to integrate continuous Tsk monitoring with real-time Tc and HR measurements, to assess both cardiovascular and thermal demands of tropical recreational runners at this scale during mass participation distance running in a warm-humid environment, adding to existing literature that typically focuses on elite populations. Specifically, our findings suggest that tropical recreational individuals, despite possessing lower physical fitness levels compared with elite athletes, can achieve similar elevated HR, Tc, Tsk, and high levels of dehydration while running in 21 km and 10 km races in hot environments without adverse consequences. This finding suggests that existing temperature-based safety limits during endurance events can no longer be easily applied to elite versus recreational populations, which emphasizes the importance of individualized monitoring in the future. To add, we observed an “inverted-U” Tsk profile in the 21 km race in contrast to a plateau commonly described in laboratory-based findings, suggesting intricacy of Tsk dynamics influenced by both environmental variability and movement dynamics that are not fully emulated in laboratory settings. We also found starting Tc, mean HR, BSA, and average speed to be independently associated with interindividual differences in peak Tc, providing insight into factors determining individual thermal tolerance of tropical recreational populations. The real-time monitoring dashboard utilized in the study has the potential to be scaled and implemented in other mass participation events, augmenting safety protocols in place. Incorporating factors associated with peak Tc to such a dashboard, combined with physiological and GPS tracking, may inform future development of targeted interventions to optimize the safety of recreational populations competing in the heat.
