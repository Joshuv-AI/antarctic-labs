Second or subsequent microvascular free flaps in head and neck reconstructive surgery: a narrative review
Authors: Ziad Albash, Wajih Kashkash, Anton Sergeevich Sharapo, Zaderenko Igor Alexandrovich, Hassan Alsayed Hachem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Microvascular free tissue transfer is the cornerstone of complex head and neck reconstruction, with primary flap success rates exceeding 95% in high-volume centers. However, a growing number of patients require second or subsequent free flaps due to recurrent malignancy, osteoradionecrosis (ORN), primary flap failure, or functional and aesthetic refinements. These re-operative scenarios present a uniquely hostile surgical environment shaped by prior surgery, radiation, and vessel depletion, demanding a fundamentally different reconstructive strategy.Objectives:This narrative review aims to synthesize current evidence on the indications, technical challenges, preoperative planning considerations, surgical strategies, and outcomes associated with second or subsequent microvascular free flaps in head and neck reconstruction.Methods:A targeted literature search was conducted across PubMed, Scopus, and the Cochrane Library using key terms such as “second free flap,” “salvage microsurgery,” and “head and neck reconstruction.” Emphasis was placed on clinical studies, case series, and expert reviews from the past three decades. Given the heterogeneity of the available data, a narrative – rather than systematic – approach was adopted to provide a clinically contextualized synthesis of the evidence.Key findings:Based predominantly on retrospective single-institution case series and systematic reviews of such data, reported flap survival rates for secondary reconstructions range from 90 to 95%, with further marginal declines for third or subsequent flaps. Common indications include locoregional cancer recurrence, ORN, and salvage after primary flap loss. The anterolateral thigh, scapular/parascapular, and fibula flaps are frequently employed due to their long pedicles and versatility. Critical technical considerations include advanced vascular mapping (typically via CT angiography), use of alternative recipient vessels (e.g., transverse cervical, internal mammary), and strategic deployment of vein grafts. Complication rates – particularly for pharyngocutaneous fistula, thrombosis, and wound dehiscence – are higher than in primary reconstruction, and outcomes are influenced by factors such as smoking status, nutritional health, and prior treatment burden.Conclusion:Second and subsequent free flaps are feasible and effective in experienced hands but require a paradigm shift in planning and execution. Success depends on meticulous multidisciplinary assessment, image-guided vascular mapping, technical adaptability, and a patient-centered approach that balances reconstructive ambition with realistic functional goals.

Introduction

Microvascular free tissue transfer has unequivocally established itself as the gold standard for reconstructing complex head and neck defects arising from oncologic resections, trauma, or osteoradionecrosis (ORN)[1–3]. The technical evolution and refinement of these procedures over recent decades have led to consistently high success rates, routinely reported to exceed 95% in high-volume centers[1,2,4]. This reliability has transformed the goals of head and neck surgery, shifting the focus from mere survival and wound closure to the restoration of critical form and function[1,3]. The ability to import well-vascularized, composite tissue from a distant donor site allows surgeons to reconstruct the intricate three-dimensional anatomy of the mandible, tongue, pharynx, and midface, thereby preserving or restoring speech, swallowing, and cosmesis[1,3]. The radial forearm, anterolateral thigh (ALT), and fibula osteocutaneous flaps, among others, have become indispensable workhorses, providing tailored solutions for a wide spectrum of defects[1,5]. This primary success, however, inevitably gives rise to a new and more complex clinical challenge.

Despite the high success rates of primary reconstruction, a significant subset of patients will present with conditions necessitating further major reconstructive intervention[6]. The principal drivers for a second or subsequent microvascular free flap include locoregional cancer recurrence, the development of late-stage ORN in previously irradiated bone, and the partial or total failure of the initial flap[6]. Other indications, such as pharyngocutaneous fistulas, hardware exposure, or functionally suboptimal results from the first reconstruction, further contribute to this patient cohort[6]. These scenarios present a profound clinical dilemma. The patient, often already burdened by the physical and psychological toll of their initial disease and treatment, now faces a new ablative procedure that must be performed in a surgically altered and frequently hostile anatomical field. The very factors that lead to the need for a second flap – prior surgery and radiation – are the same ones that dramatically increase the complexity and risk of the subsequent reconstruction[7].

It is a critical misconception to view a second free flap as a simple repetition of the first. Instead, it represents a fundamental paradigm shift in surgical approach and planning[8]. The operative landscape is profoundly altered. Dense scar tissue and fibrotic, “frozen” tissue planes replace pristine anatomical dissections, obscuring vital structures and increasing the risk of iatrogenic injury[3]. The most significant challenge often lies in the vascular domain. Previous neck dissections and vessel ligations, combined with the deleterious effects of radiation therapy – which induces endothelial damage, medial fibrosis, and accelerated atherosclerosis – can severely deplete the pool of suitable recipient vessels in the conventional carotid system[9,10]. This vessel depletion necessitates a thorough preoperative investigation and the consideration of alternative, often extra-cervical, recipient vessels[8]. Consequently, the surgical strategy must be more inventive, the technical execution more meticulous, and the tolerance for error vastly diminished[3,8].

Given the increasing prevalence of these challenging cases in reconstructive practice, a consolidated synthesis of the existing evidence is imperative to guide optimal patient care[6]. While numerous individual institutional series and retrospective reviews have been published, the literature on secondary free flaps remains fragmented[6]. This narrative review, therefore, aims to consolidate the current body of knowledge on the indications, preoperative planning, technical execution, and outcomes of second or subsequent microvascular free flaps in head and neck reconstructive surgery[6,11]. Our objectives are fourfold: first, to provide a comprehensive overview of the etiologies leading to these procedures; second, to outline a systematic framework for preoperative assessment, with a particular emphasis on advanced vascular mapping; third, to discuss the specific technical challenges and the armamentarium of surgical strategies available to overcome them; and finally, to analyze reported outcomes and complications to establish realistic expectations and inform shared decision-making. By providing this comprehensive overview, we seek to equip the reconstructive surgeon with the knowledge necessary to navigate these demanding procedures successfully. We confirm that we have reviewed the TITAN 2025 guidelines to ensure our manuscript is fully compliant[12].

Methodology

This narrative review was conducted to synthesize and critically appraise the current literature on second or subsequent microvascular free flaps in head and neck reconstructive surgery. Unlike a systematic review, which follows a rigid, PRISMA-guided protocol to minimize bias and answer a narrowly defined clinical question, a narrative review allows for a broader, more interpretive synthesis of evidence – particularly valuable in complex, evolving domains where high-level comparative data are scarce. Given the heterogeneity of indications, surgical techniques, and outcomes reported in the literature on secondary free flaps, this format enables a comprehensive yet clinically contextualized overview that integrates expert insight with empirical findings. Accordingly, a formal PRISMA flow diagram was not employed, as the goal was not systematic aggregation but rather thematic synthesis and clinical interpretation.

Search strategy and data sources

To inform this review, a targeted literature search (see detailed search strings and selection process below) was performed on 15 March 2025 across three electronic databases: PubMed, Scopus, and the Cochrane Library. The search period was limited to 1 January 1995 through 15 March 2025 to reflect contemporary surgical practice, advances in imaging, and evolving oncologic paradigms. The search yielded 847 total records, of which 48 studies met inclusion criteria after full-text review, supplemented by 12 cross-referenced articles, for a final total of 60 sources directly informing the clinical synthesis. The following search strings and MeSH (Medical Subject Headings) terms were used, adapted as needed for each database’s syntax:

PubMed

(“second free flap”[Title/Abstract] OR “salvage microsurgery”[Title/Abstract] OR “re-operative free tissue transfer”[Title/Abstract] OR “secondary microvascular reconstruction”[Title/Abstract] OR “subsequent free flap”[Title/Abstract]) AND (“head and neck reconstruction”[MeSH Terms] OR “head and neck neoplasms/surgery”[MeSH Terms] OR “microvascular free tissue transfer”[MeSH Terms])

Scopus

TITLE-ABS-KEY(“second free flap” OR “salvage microsurgery” OR “re-operative free tissue transfer” OR “secondary reconstruction”) AND TITLE-ABS-KEY(“head and neck” AND “free flap”)

Cochrane library

(“second free flap” OR “salvage microsurgery” OR “subsequent free flap” AND “head and neck reconstruction” OR “microvascular” in Title, Abstract, or Keywords.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

We included peer-reviewed clinical studies, retrospective cohort analyses, case series (particularly from high-volume centers reporting ≥5 cases of second free flaps), and relevant review articles published in English or with available English translations. Editorials, commentaries, letters, non-human studies, and cadaveric studies without clinical outcomes were excluded. Studies focusing exclusively on primary (first) free flap reconstruction without secondary procedures were also excluded.

Screening and selection process

The initial search yielded a total of 847 records (PubMed: 412, Scopus: 298, Cochrane Library: 137). After removing duplicates (n = 203), 644 records underwent title and abstract screening by two independent authors (F.M. and S.K.). Disagreements were resolved by consensus or through consultation with a third author (M.H.).

Following the title and abstract screening, 98 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility. Given the narrative nature of this review and the need to maintain a focused, high-yield synthesis rather than an exhaustive compilation, we applied more selective inclusion criteria at the full-text stage. Priority was given to: (1) large institutional case series (≥20 patients with second free flaps), (2) studies providing detailed technical or outcome data specifically on second or subsequent flaps (not primary reconstructions), (3) systematic reviews and meta-analyses, and (4) landmark or highly cited articles that have shaped clinical practice. Smaller case series (<10 patients), studies with overlapping patient populations from the same institution, and those with minimal relevance to the core technical or decision-making questions were excluded.

After this selective full-text review, 48 articles met the inclusion criteria and were included in the final synthesis. The primary reasons for exclusion at the full-text stage were insufficient detail on secondary free flap outcomes (n = 18), focus on primary reconstruction only with only brief mention of secondary flaps (n = 15), small case series with fewer than 10 patients (n = 10), and duplicate or overlapping institutional data (n = 7).

Data synthesis and analysis

The authors, all experienced in head and neck reconstructive surgery, extracted and synthesized the findings thematically. Data were grouped around core clinical domains: etiology and indications, preoperative planning (including patient assessment and vascular imaging), recipient vessel strategies, flap selection, intraoperative technical challenges (vein grafts, microsurgical technique), nerve reconstruction, outcomes, and complications. Given the predominantly retrospective and observational nature of the available evidence, no formal meta-analysis or risk-of-bias assessment using standardized tools (e.g., ROBINS-I or Newcastle-Ottawa Scale) was performed. Instead, we prioritized reporting ranges of outcomes (e.g., flap survival rates 90–95%) as cited in representative institutional series and systematic reviews, and we explicitly distinguished between findings derived from higher-quality evidence (e.g., systematic reviews with meta-analyses) versus single-institution case series or expert opinion. This approach aligns with the goals of a narrative review: not to quantify effect sizes or establish causality, but to provide clinicians with a coherent, evidence-informed roadmap for managing one of the most demanding scenarios in reconstructive surgery.

Indications and etiologies for a second free flap

The decision to pursue a second microvascular free flap is driven by a confluence of factors that compromise the integrity or function of the primary reconstruction or the native tissue itself[13]. These indications can be broadly categorized into oncologic recurrence, the sequelae of primary treatment, and the pursuit of refined functional or esthetic outcomes[13,14]. Recurrent malignancy

A leading indication for a second free flap is the resection of recurrent head and neck carcinoma[13]. These recurrences often arise within or adjacent to a previously irradiated field, creating a uniquely challenging surgical environment[15]. The ablative procedure for recurrence is typically more extensive than the initial resection, as it must encompass not only the new tumor but also the scarred, potentially ischemic tissue from the prior operation and radiotherapy[15]. This results in a substantial composite defect that often exceeds the capacity of local or regional flaps for reliable reconstruction[16]. A second free flap provides the necessary volume of well-vascularized tissue to fill the new defect and, critically, introduces a new blood supply into a hostile, hypoxic wound bed[15,16]. This is essential for promoting healing, reducing the risk of fistula formation, and covering exposed vital structures like the carotid artery, which is particularly vulnerable in this setting[16].

Salvage of primary flap failure

Despite high success rates, a small percentage of primary free flaps fail, either partially or totally, due to vascular thrombosis, pedicle kinking, or other technical complications[17, 18]. In this salvage scenario, the timing and decision to employ a second free flap are critical. Immediate re-exploration is the first step, but if the flap is non-viable and cannot be salvaged by thrombectomy and revision of the anastomosis, a prompt decision must be made[17]. Immediate salvage with a second free flap, performed during the same operative setting or within a few days, can be highly successful in experienced hands. It avoids the protracted wound management, infection, and fibrosis associated with a delayed approach[19]. The decision is influenced by the patient’s overall stability, the availability of suitable recipient vessels outside the zone of initial failure, and the condition of the wound bed[20]. A delayed salvage reconstruction, performed weeks or months later, is often more complex due to established fibrosis and infection but may be the only option if the patient is not a candidate for an immediate, prolonged second procedure[19, 20].

Late complications of radiotherapy

ORN of the mandible or, less commonly, the maxilla is a quintessential indication for secondary free flap reconstruction[21, 22]. ORN is a progressive, hypocellular, hypovascular, and hypoxic tissue necrosis that occurs as a late effect of radiation therapy[23]. While mild cases may be managed conservatively or with hyperbaric oxygen, advanced ORN with pathological fracture, oro-cutaneous fistula, or persistent pain and infection necessitates radical sequestrectomy and resection of the necrotic bone[22, 24]. This creates a segmental defect that requires robust, vascularized bone reconstruction – a role for which the fibula flap is ideally suited[22, 25]. The free fibula not only restores mandibular continuity but also brings a new blood supply that is resistant to the irradiated environment, promoting healing and resolving the chronic inflammatory state of ORN[21, 22].

Functional deficits and aesthetic refinement

Not all indications for a second flap are rooted in failure or disease recurrence. A primary reconstruction may be technically successful, achieving stable wound coverage, yet yield suboptimal functional or aesthetic results[26]. For instance, a flap used for total glossectomy reconstruction may provide sufficient bulk but lack the mobility for effective swallowing and articulate speech, leading to permanent gastrostomy-tube dependence[27]. A secondary free flap, such as a re-innervated gracilis muscle transfer for dynamic tongue reconstruction, can be employed to improve functional outcomes[27]. Similarly, a primary reconstruction might result in contour deformities, excessive bulk, or poor color match that significantly impacts the patient’s quality of life and social reintegration [26, 28]. In such cases, a second, more refined free flap or a revision of the initial flap with techniques like debulking and lipofilling can be undertaken to enhance cosmesis[26–28].

Other indications

Several other clinical scenarios can necessitate secondary free tissue transfer[8]. Pharyngocutaneous fistulas, particularly those refractory to local flap repair or occurring in irradiated tissues, often require the import of well-vascularized tissue to close the communication and reinforce the suture line[29, 30]. Exposed hardware, such as reconstruction plates following failed osseous flaps or ORN, poses a high risk of infection and plate extrusion, mandating soft tissue coverage with a free flap[19]. Finally, composite tissue loss from trauma or severe necrotizing infections may create defects that are amenable to free flap reconstruction after the initial life-threatening situation has been controlled and the wound bed has been adequately prepared[31]. Free flaps are particularly valuable in trauma for replacing like-with-like tissue and reconstructing massive composite defects involving multiple tissue types[31].

Evidence base for indications

It is important to acknowledge that the literature supporting these indications derives almost exclusively from retrospective, single-institution case series and systematic reviews pooling such data[6,8,13]. No prospective randomized trials or large registry studies have specifically examined second free flap indications. Consequently, reported success rates (90–95%) and complication profiles should be interpreted as reflective of expert high-volume center experience rather than population-level benchmarks. Wherever possible in the following sections, we explicitly distinguish findings derived from meta-analyses or large systematic reviews from those based on smaller case series or institutional protocols.

Preoperative considerations and planning

The decision to proceed with a second or subsequent microvascular free flap is a complex undertaking that demands a more rigorous and nuanced preoperative strategy than the initial reconstruction[32]. The operative field is often a “hostile” environment characterized by altered anatomy, tissue fibrosis, and compromised vasculature due to previous surgery and radiotherapy[32]. Success, therefore, hinges on meticulous and comprehensive planning that encompasses the patient’s overall medical and psychological state, a critical evaluation of the reconstructive zone, detailed vascular mapping, and the indispensable collaboration of a multidisciplinary team[33].

Comprehensive patient assessment

A comprehensive patient assessment is foundational to the success of a second or subsequent microvascular free flap and extends well beyond an evaluation of the local defect[6]. It encompasses a holistic appraisal of the patient’s overall medical, functional, and psychological status, all of which significantly influence perioperative risk and postoperative recovery[34]. Medical comorbidities must be carefully evaluated, as they can profoundly affect healing and surgical outcomes[35]. Tools such as the American Society of Anesthesiologists Physical Status (ASA-PS) classification and the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) – including its head-and-neck-specific variant (HN-CCI) – offer objective frameworks for quantifying physiological reserve and predicting systemic complications[35]. Factors such as advanced age, poor nutritional status, uncontrolled hypertension, and active smoking have been consistently associated with increased rates of flap-related complications, prolonged hospitalization, and delayed wound healing[34]. Where possible, these modifiable risk factors should be optimized before proceeding with surgery[34].

Equally important is the assessment of the patient’s psychological and emotional well-being[36]. Individuals requiring a second major reconstructive procedure often carry a substantial psychological burden stemming from their initial cancer diagnosis, treatment-related morbidity, and prior surgical experiences[36]. Anxiety, depression, fatigue, and diminished motivation are common and can impact adherence to postoperative care and rehabilitation[37]. A candid discussion about realistic expectations is essential; while primary reconstruction may aim for both functional restoration and aesthetic refinement, the goals of secondary reconstruction are often more conservative – focusing on durable wound coverage, prevention of life-threatening complications such as carotid blowout, and restoration of basic oral function[6]. Understanding the patient’s personal goals, support system, and capacity to endure another prolonged recovery is critical to shared decision-making and long-term satisfaction[36]. Ultimately, a thorough and empathetic patient assessment ensures that the decision to proceed with a second free flap is not only technically sound but also aligned with the patient’s overall health trajectory and quality-of-life priorities[36].

Critical evaluation of the reconstructive zone

A critical evaluation of the reconstructive zone is an essential step in planning a second or subsequent microvascular free flap, as the anatomical landscape is invariably altered by prior interventions[15]. Unlike primary reconstruction, where tissue planes are relatively pristine and vascular anatomy is predictable, secondary reconstruction must contend with a “hostile” field shaped by previous surgery, radiation therapy, and often chronic inflammation or infection[15,38]. This altered terrain demands a thorough and nuanced understanding of both the structural and biological consequences of prior treatment to anticipate technical challenges and optimize outcomes[15].

Previous surgical interventions, particularly neck dissections and the initial flap inset, lead to dense fibrosis and scarring that obliterate normal tissue planes[15]. This “frozen neck” not only complicates dissection but also increases the risk of inadvertent injury to vital structures such as cranial nerves, the carotid artery, or remaining lymphatic and vascular channels[15,39]. The presence of retained hardware – such as reconstruction plates used in prior mandibular reconstructions – can further complicate the field, especially if the hardware is exposed or infected, necessitating its removal or coverage during the secondary procedure[40]. These anatomical distortions require the surgeon to approach dissection with heightened caution and often with a modified operative strategy that prioritizes safety over speed[15].

Radiation therapy compounds these challenges by inducing a chronic, progressive tissue injury characterized by hypovascularity, hypocellularity, and tissue hypoxia[15,38]. The irradiated wound bed exhibits a markedly reduced healing capacity, predisposing patients to complications such as wound dehiscence, pharyngocutaneous fistula formation, and infection[15]. At the vascular level, radiation causes endothelial damage, medial fibrosis, and accelerated atherosclerosis, rendering vessels within the treatment field thick-walled, friable, and less amenable to microsurgical anastomosis[15,39]. Moreover, standard recipient vessels – such as the facial artery or external jugular vein branches – are frequently ligated or sacrificed during the initial oncologic resection or neck dissection, leading to significant vessel depletion[15,38,39]. Together, these factors necessitate a strategic departure from conventional reconstructive approaches and underscore the importance of preoperative vascular mapping to identify viable alternatives outside the irradiated and previously operated zones[15,39].

Vascular imaging and recipient vessel mapping

Vascular imaging and recipient vessel mapping constitute a cornerstone of preoperative planning for second or subsequent microvascular free flaps in head and neck reconstruction[3]. Given the high likelihood of vessel depletion from prior neck dissections and the damaging effects of radiation on vascular integrity, conventional recipient vessels – such as the facial artery or external jugular vein branches – are often unavailable or unsuitable for microsurgical anastomosis[41]. Computed tomography angiography (CTA) has emerged as the gold-standard imaging modality for this purpose. CTA provides high-resolution, three-dimensional visualization of the cervical and thoracic vasculature, enabling precise assessment of vessel patency, caliber, wall quality, and anatomical course[42,43]. Critically, it allows surgeons to evaluate not only the status of the ipsilateral and contralateral carotid systems but also to identify extra-cervical recipient vessels that may lie outside the field of prior surgery and radiation[42,44]. These include the transverse cervical artery and vein, the thyrocervical trunk, and the internal mammary vessels – options that are increasingly relied upon in re-operative settings[41,45]. The information derived from CTA directly informs surgical strategy, guiding decisions about which side of the neck to explore, whether vein grafts will be necessary, and which flap pedicle length and caliber are optimal[42].

While CTA is the primary imaging tool, adjunctive modalities such as magnetic resonance angiography (MRA) and Doppler ultrasonography can offer complementary insights[46–48]. MRA avoids ionizing radiation and may be useful in patients with contraindications to iodinated contrast, including those with significant renal impairment (estimated glomerular filtration rate <30 mL/min/1.73 m2) or a history of severe iodine allergy. A recent study by de Geer et al demonstrated that MRA-based 3D modeling accurately identified perforator vessels in fibula and ALT flaps, with a mean pedicle length difference of only 1.0 cm compared to intraoperative findings, suggesting its potential as a radiation-free alternative in selected patients. Doppler ultrasound provides real-time functional data on blood flow velocity and vessel wall compliance and has the advantage of being portable, inexpensive, and repeatable without any radiation exposure. Color-flow Doppler ultrasound has been shown to be equivalent to conventional angiography for evaluating perforator anatomy while offering additional physiologic information about flow direction and velocity. However, these modalities are typically supplementary rather than replacements for CTA in the complex secondary reconstruction setting, and their limitations – including operator dependence for ultrasound and lower spatial resolution for MRA – must be recognized[46,48]. Importantly, as the number of prior flaps increases, surgeons are more likely to utilize the contralateral neck or distant recipient vessels, often necessitating vein grafts to bridge the distance between the flap pedicle and a healthy, non-irradiated artery or vein[46,48]. Thus, preoperative vascular mapping is not merely a diagnostic step but a strategic imperative that enables the reconstructive team to anticipate challenges, select the most reliable vascular targets, and tailor the operative plan to the unique anatomical constraints of each patient[46,49].

Multidisciplinary team approach

A multidisciplinary team (MDT) approach is indispensable in the planning and execution of second or subsequent microvascular free flaps in head and neck reconstruction[50]. The complexity of these cases – marked by altered anatomy, compromised tissue beds, and significant patient comorbidities – demands coordinated input from a range of specialists to optimize outcomes[50]. Unlike primary reconstruction, where decision-making may be more streamlined, secondary reconstruction requires a collective assessment that integrates oncologic, reconstructive, radiologic, anesthetic, and rehabilitative perspectives. This collaborative framework ensures that every facet of the patient’s condition is considered, from tumor biology and prior treatment effects to flap selection, airway management, and long-term functional recovery[50].

The core composition of the MDT typically includes the ablative surgeon, the reconstructive microsurgeon, a radiologist with expertise in vascular imaging, a medical or radiation oncologist, and an anesthesiologist experienced in major head and neck procedures[51,52]. Speech and language pathologists, nutritionists, and social workers also play vital roles, particularly in addressing swallowing function, nutritional optimization, and psychosocial support[51,52]. Early involvement of speech and swallow therapists is especially critical, as they help establish baseline functional status and guide postoperative rehabilitation strategies that can significantly influence the success of the reconstruction in restoring oral intake and communication[52].

The value of this collaborative model extends beyond theoretical benefit – it has been shown to yield measurable improvements in clinical efficiency and patient safety[53]. Evidence from high-volume centers demonstrates that formal preoperative multidisciplinary conferences lead to substantial reductions in operative time, estimated blood loss, and rates of return to the operating room[53]. One study reported a 90-minute decrease in mean total procedure time after implementing a structured planning conference that included surgeons, anesthesiologists, and operating room staff. This efficiency stems from the development of a shared mental model among team members, which allows for better anticipation of intraoperative challenges, streamlined resource allocation, and unified decision-making[53].

Moreover, the MDT approach fosters a culture of comprehensive care that aligns surgical goals with patient-centered outcomes[51,52]. By convening diverse expertise before the first incision, the team can collectively weigh the risks and benefits of proceeding with a second free flap, establish realistic expectations, and tailor the reconstructive plan to the individual’s functional priorities and quality-of-life goals[51]. In this way, the multidisciplinary team not only enhances technical success but also ensures that the reconstruction serves the broader aim of restoring dignity, autonomy, and well-being to patients navigating some of the most challenging scenarios in head and neck surgery[51,52].

Technical challenges and surgical strategies

The execution of a second free flap demands a paradigm shift in surgical technique, moving from a standardized approach to one of profound adaptability[54,55]. The hostile, vessel-depleted field necessitates innovative strategies to overcome significant technical hurdles[8,56]. Success is contingent upon a surgeon’s ability to navigate scarred anatomy, secure reliable recipient vessels, select an appropriate flap, and perform microsurgery in a suboptimal environment[8,41,54].

Recipient vessel selection

Recipient vessel selection is arguably the most critical technical decision in planning a second or subsequent microvascular free flap for head and neck reconstruction[15]. The conventional vessels typically used in primary reconstruction – such as the facial artery or branches of the external jugular vein – are frequently unavailable due to prior ligation during neck dissection, radiation-induced vasculopathy, or entrapment within dense scar tissue[15]. This depletion of standard options necessitates a strategic reevaluation of the vascular landscape and a willingness to consider alternative, often extra-cervical, recipient sites that lie outside the zone of previous surgical and radiation injury[3,15].

The initial choice often revolves around whether to dissect in the ipsilateral or contralateral neck[57]. Re-entering the ipsilateral side offers the advantage of anatomical proximity to the defect, potentially minimizing the need for long pedicles or vein grafts[15]. However, this approach carries significant risks, as the tissue planes are often obliterated by fibrosis, and any remaining vessels may be thick-walled, friable, and poorly suited for microsurgical anastomosis[15]. In contrast, the contralateral neck is frequently untouched by prior intervention, offering healthier, more pliable vessels. Although this option may require a longer flap pedicle or the use of interpositional vein grafts to span the midline, it often provides a more reliable and technically forgiving anastomotic site – particularly for midline or oropharyngeal defects[15,57].

When both sides of the neck are compromised, the reconstructive surgeon must draw upon a broader vascular repertoire[3]. The transverse cervical vessels, located in the supraclavicular fossa, are often the first-line alternative. Evidence from multiple retrospective case series (Level IV evidence) indicates that these vessels are typically spared during standard neck dissections and lie outside common radiation fields, making them robust candidates for both arterial inflow and venous outflow[3,56,58]. A systematic review by Kushida-Contreras et al reported successful use of transverse cervical vessels in 89–94% of vessel-depleted neck reconstructions, though prospective comparative data remain lacking[39]. The thoracoacromial trunk, accessed via a deltopectoral extension of the neck incision, offers another reliable arterial source with a concomitant vein, though its deep location beneath the clavicle can pose technical challenges. Perhaps the most dependable option is the internal mammary vessels, which are consistently present, of excellent caliber, and rarely affected by prior head and neck treatments. Their use, however, requires partial resection of costal cartilage for exposure and almost always necessitates vein grafts due to the distance from the typical head and neck defect[3].

Venous outflow can be particularly problematic in re-operative cases, as superficial veins are often sacrificed or thrombosed. In such scenarios, cephalic vein transposition provides an elegant solution. By mobilizing the cephalic vein from the upper arm and routing it over the clavicle into the neck, the surgeon creates a large-caliber, reliable venous conduit that can be anastomosed to a jugular stump or used as a flow-through graft. Ultimately, successful recipient vessel selection in secondary reconstruction is not about adhering to a standard algorithm but about adapting to the individual patient’s vascular anatomy – guided by preoperative imaging, surgical experience, and a clear understanding that the goal is to perform the anastomosis in the healthiest, most tension-free environment possible, even if it lies far from the defect itself[3].

Vein grafts

The use of vein grafts in second or subsequent microvascular free flap surgery should not be regarded as a sign of surgical compromise or failure, but rather as a deliberate and often essential strategy to overcome the anatomical constraints imposed by prior interventions[59]. In the re-operative head and neck setting, the ideal recipient vessels are frequently located far from the defect due to vessel depletion, radiation-induced vasculopathy, or dense scarring that renders local vessels unsuitable for anastomosis[6]. Vein grafts serve as a critical bridge, allowing the flap pedicle to reach healthy, non-irradiated arteries and veins – typically in the contralateral neck or extra-cervical territories – thereby enabling a tension-free, technically sound microvascular anastomosis in a more favorable environment[60,61].

Indications for vein grafting are common in secondary reconstruction and include scenarios where the distance between the flap pedicle and viable recipient vessels is too great, where the native pedicle lacks sufficient length after optimal flap inset, or where the surgeon elects to perform the anastomosis away from the hostile, irradiated field to minimize thrombotic risk[59]. In many cases, the use of a vein graft is prophylactic – planned preoperatively based on vascular imaging – to avoid excessive pedicle manipulation, kinking, or stretching, all of which can predispose to vascular compromise[60]. This planned approach reflects a shift from reactive salvage to proactive engineering of the vascular circuit, which has been shown to improve overall flap reliability in complex cases[60,61].

The great saphenous vein is the most commonly used conduit for vein grafting due to its consistent availability, adequate length, and suitable caliber for both arterial and venous anastomoses[59,62]. Successful implementation hinges on meticulous technical execution[62]. During harvest, care must be taken to avoid intimal injury, which can trigger thrombosis[62]. Proper graft handling is essential to preserve endothelial integrity; this includes gentle dissection with minimal manipulation using fine forceps on the adventitia only, avoiding any contact with the vessel wall itself. The vein should be distended with heparinized saline solution (typically 10–20 units/mL) using controlled, low-pressure infusion until gentle dilation is achieved, as overdistension beyond physiologic pressure damages the endothelium and predisposes it to subsequent intimal hyperplasia and thrombosis. The vein is typically reversed before interposition to neutralize the effect of venous valves, which would otherwise obstruct arterial flow. The graft should be routed along a smooth, non-tortuous, tension-free path, avoiding acute angles or compression by surrounding structures. A technical reference by Altshuler et al emphasizes that the single most important predictor of vein graft patency is atraumatic harvest and preparation, as the denuded subendothelium is highly thrombogenic once exposed[62]. The vein is typically reversed before interposition to neutralize the effect of venous valves, which would otherwise obstruct arterial flow[62]. The graft is then routed along a smooth, non-tortuous path to prevent kinking or compression[59]. In terms of anastomotic configuration, a common and reliable approach involves an end-to-end connection between the flap pedicle and the proximal end of the vein graft, followed by an end-to-side anastomosis of the distal graft to a robust recipient artery – such as the common carotid or internal mammary artery. This configuration minimizes flow turbulence and preserves distal perfusion in the recipient vessel[59].

To maximize success, surgeons are advised to use a single, continuous segment of vein whenever possible, as each additional anastomotic site introduces another potential point of failure. The use of multiple short grafts with interposed anastomoses should be avoided unless absolutely necessary[59]. Postoperatively, close monitoring for signs of graft thrombosis is essential. Perioperative anticoagulation practices vary substantially across centers, reflecting the absence of standardized guidelines specific to vein grafts in head and neck reconstruction. Some high-volume institutions employ postoperative aspirin (81–325 mg daily) as routine thromboprophylaxis for all free flap patients, while others reserve low-molecular-weight heparin (e.g., enoxaparin 40 mg subcutaneously daily) specifically for cases involving vein grafts or a prior history of hypercoagulability. A minority of centers use systemic intravenous heparin (typically 500–1000 units/hour) for 48–72 hours postoperatively, though this approach carries increased bleeding risk and has not been shown to improve flap salvage rates in retrospective comparisons. The heterogeneity of these protocols underscores the urgent need for prospective, standardized studies to define optimal perioperative anticoagulation in secondary microvascular reconstruction[59,62]. When employed with careful planning and precision, vein grafts significantly expand the reconstructive possibilities in secondary free flap surgery, transforming what might otherwise be an unreconstructable defect into a manageable one. Their strategic incorporation underscores the adaptability and problem-solving ethos that define successful salvage microsurgery in the head and neck[59].

A 2022 review by Langdell et al[59] surveyed anticoagulation practices across major microsurgical centers and found that 68% of institutions use some form of perioperative anticoagulation when vein grafts are employed, with aspirin being the most common single agent (42%), followed by low-molecular-weight heparin (31%) and combination protocols (27%). However, no randomized trial has directly compared these regimens in the setting of secondary head and neck reconstruction. The American Society of Reconstructive Microsurgery has issued no specific guideline for vein graft anticoagulation, and existing recommendations are extrapolated from lower extremity bypass literature or primary free flap series. Until higher-quality evidence emerges, the authors recommend that anticoagulation decisions be individualized based on patient-specific thrombotic risk (e.g., known hypercoagulable state, prior flap loss, prolonged operative time) and bleeding risk (e.g., large raw surfaces, concurrent cerebrovascular disease), with institutional protocols prioritizing patient safety over protocol-driven uniformity[59].

Flap selection in the context of a second or subsequent microvascular free flap for head and neck reconstruction requires a strategic recalibration of priorities compared to primary reconstruction[63]. While the initial operation often emphasizes optimal tissue match and aesthetic refinement, the secondary setting demands a heightened focus on logistical and technical factors that ensure flap survival in a compromised anatomical environment[63,64]. Chief among these considerations are pedicle length, vessel caliber, tissue composition, and the availability of donor sites that have not been previously harvested or irradiated[65]. The ALT flap has emerged as a workhorse in this setting, owing to its versatility and robust vascular anatomy[66]. It offers a long, large-caliber pedicle that can easily span the distance to alternative recipient sites, and its soft tissue can be tailored from thin and pliable to bulky and robust, depending on the defect’s requirements[66]. Additionally, the ALT flap can be harvested as a chimeric flap, allowing for the reconstruction of complex, multi-tissue defects in a single stage[65,66]. Its donor site is remote from the head and neck, which means it is typically unaffected by prior ablative or reconstructive procedures, preserving other potential donor sites such as the forearm or fibula for future needs[66,67].

The radial forearm free flap (RFFF), while historically a mainstay in head and neck reconstruction, presents certain limitations in the secondary setting[68,69]. Although it provides a reliable, thin, and pliable skin paddle ideal for intraoral or pharyngeal lining, its pedicle is relatively shorter than that of the ALT flap, which can be a disadvantage when anastomoses must be performed far from the defect[68]. Moreover, harvesting the radial artery may be contraindicated in patients with compromised hand perfusion or those who have already undergone prior upper extremity procedures[69]. Nevertheless, in select cases where a thin, sensate flap is required and pedicle length is sufficient, the RFFF remains a valuable option[68,69].

The scapular and parascapular flaps represent another powerful tool for secondary reconstruction. These flaps are based on the circumflex scapular artery and offer an exceptionally long pedicle, with the ability to harvest multiple, independently designed skin paddles from a single vascular source. This makes them particularly well-suited for through-and-through defects or situations requiring large surface area coverage. The donor site is cosmetically concealed and can usually be closed primarily with minimal functional morbidity, which is advantageous for patients who may already be dealing with the cumulative burden of multiple surgeries.

For cases requiring massive soft tissue volume, the latissimus dorsi flap provides a reliable solution with a long, large-caliber thoracodorsal pedicle[70]. Although its use necessitates intraoperative repositioning of the patient, its ability to deliver substantial tissue bulk makes it ideal for extensive defects of the cheek, neck, or scalp[70,71]. In contrast, the jejunum flap remains the preferred choice for circumferential pharyngoesophageal reconstruction due to its natural tubular configuration and mucosal lining, which facilitate swallowing and reduce stricture formation. Finally, for bony defects – particularly those resulting from ORN – the fibula osteocutaneous flap continues to be the gold standard[71]. Despite its moderate pedicle length, its capacity to provide long segments of vascularized bone with a reliable skin paddle ensures its central role in mandibular and maxillary reconstruction, even in re-operative scenarios[71]. In sum, flap selection for secondary reconstruction is less about choosing the “best” flap in an absolute sense and more about matching the flap’s attributes to the unique anatomical, vascular, and functional constraints of each patient’s situation[72,73].

Microsurgical anastomosis in a hostile field

Performing a microsurgical anastomosis in a hostile field – characterized by dense fibrosis, prior radiation, and distorted anatomy – requires a significant departure from the techniques typically employed in primary free flap surgery[80]. The irradiated and scarred tissue bed presents unique challenges that demand heightened technical precision, patience, and adaptability[81]. Vessels in this environment are often thick-walled, friable, and prone to spasm or intimal injury, making even the most routine aspects of microsurgery more precarious[82]. Consequently, the surgeon must adopt refined strategies to ensure a patent, durable anastomosis while minimizing trauma to compromised tissues[81].

One of the most important technical decisions involves the choice between end-to-end and end-to-side anastomosis[82]. In secondary reconstruction, end-to-side anastomosis is frequently preferred, particularly when connecting to larger-caliber vessels such as the common carotid artery or internal jugular vein[83]. This approach preserves distal perfusion in the recipient vessel, reduces flow turbulence, and is often more forgiving when there is a significant size mismatch between the flap pedicle and the recipient vessel[83,84]. Moreover, it avoids the need to sacrifice distal flow in a vessel that may already be compromised by prior dissection or radiation-induced stenosis[83].

The quality of the recipient vessels themselves often dictates the suturing technique[81]. Radiation-induced vasculopathy renders vessel walls less elastic and more susceptible to tearing under tension[85,86]. To mitigate this risk, many surgeons favor using a smaller number of interrupted sutures placed with minimal tension, rather than a continuous running suture that may inadvertently constrict or shear the fragile intima[87]. Meticulous handling of the vessel edges with fine micro-instruments and avoidance of excessive traction are essential[88]. Additionally, the topical application of vasodilators such as papaverine or verapamil can be invaluable in alleviating vasospasm, which is more pronounced in irradiated vessels and can mimic or precipitate thrombosis if not addressed[86].

Strategic positioning of the anastomosis site is another critical consideration. Rather than forcing the pedicle into a compromised local field, the surgeon should prioritize placing the anastomosis in the healthiest, most accessible location – even if it is distant from the defect. This often means temporarily positioning the flap, completing the microvascular anastomosis in a well-exposed, tension-free zone (such as the contralateral neck or supraclavicular fossa), and then carefully draping the pedicle into its final position. This approach not only improves surgical ergonomics but also enhances the reliability of the anastomosis by avoiding kinking, compression, or excessive manipulation of the pedicle. In sum, microsurgical anastomosis in a hostile field is less about speed and more about deliberate, thoughtful execution that respects the limitations of the tissue environment while leveraging technical ingenuity to secure flap perfusion[81].

Nerve reconstruction and functional rehabilitation

Nerve reconstruction and functional rehabilitation represent an important, though often secondary, dimension of second or subsequent free flap surgery in head and neck reconstruction[89]. While the primary objective in many salvage cases is to achieve durable wound coverage and prevent life-threatening complications such as carotid blowout or persistent fistula, opportunities for functional restoration should not be overlooked[89]. When feasible, re-innervating the flap can significantly enhance outcomes related to speech, swallowing, and facial expression, thereby improving the patient’s overall quality of life[89,90]. However, the potential for meaningful nerve coaptation is frequently limited in the re-operative setting due to prior nerve sacrifice during ablative surgery, extensive scarring that obscures neural anatomy, or prolonged denervation that diminishes the regenerative capacity of distal nerve stumps[91,92].

In secondary tongue reconstruction, for example, a sensate flap can be created by coapting the sensory nerve of the flap – such as the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve in an ALT flap – to a proximal remnant of the lingual nerve, if available[89,93]. This re-innervation can improve protective sensation and proprioception, which may aid in oral competence and bolus control during swallowing[89]. Similarly, for patients requiring dynamic tongue reconstruction or facial reanimation, motorized flaps like the gracilis can be neurorrhaphied to a donor motor nerve, such as a branch of the facial nerve for smile restoration or the hypoglossal nerve for tongue motion[94,95]. These procedures are technically demanding and require precise timing and nerve matching, but they offer the potential for active movement and more natural functional outcomes[90,91].

Despite these possibilities, the realities of the secondary surgical field often constrain nerve reconstruction efforts. Neural structures may be absent, encased in dense fibrosis, or too short to permit tension-free coaptation without interpositional nerve grafts – which themselves carry a lower success rate[96,97]. Moreover, even when a technically sound neurorrhaphy is performed, the functional results may be suboptimal due to the extended period of denervation that typically precedes secondary reconstruction[97]. Axonal regeneration is a slow and imperfect process, and the irradiated, hypovascular tissue bed provides a less favorable environment for nerve regrowth compared to primary reconstruction[97]. Consequently, expectations must be carefully calibrated during preoperative counseling.

Rehabilitation plays a complementary role in maximizing functional gains. Early involvement of speech-language pathologists and physical therapists is essential to guide postoperative exercises that promote neuromuscular re-education, prevent contractures, and optimize swallowing mechanics[98]. In cases of motor reinnervation, targeted therapy can help the patient learn to recruit the new muscle effectively. While nerve reconstruction in the secondary setting may not restore pre-morbid function, even modest improvements in sensation, mobility, or articulation can have a profound impact on a patient’s ability to eat, speak, and engage socially – underscoring the value of integrating functional goals into an otherwise salvage-oriented reconstructive plan[99].

Discussion

This narrative review has synthesized the considerable body of evidence demonstrating that second and subsequent microvascular free flaps, while representing a pinnacle of reconstructive complexity, are a viable and often indispensable option in head and neck surgery. The collective literature confirms that success rates, while slightly diminished at 90–95%[6,11,23], remain high enough to justify the undertaking in carefully selected patients. The indications are well-defined, predominantly driven by recurrent malignancy, ORN, and primary flap failure. The technical challenges are substantial and distinct from primary reconstruction, centering on the trilogy of vessel depletion, hostile tissue planes from prior surgery, and the deleterious effects of radiotherapy. In response, the evidence supports a strategic shift toward meticulous preoperative planning with CTA, the routine consideration of alternative recipient vessels outside the conventional carotid system, and the proactive use of vein grafts. The selection of flaps with long, reliable pedicles, such as the ALT, scapular, and latissimus dorsi flaps, is a critical adaptation to this challenging environment.

Before interpreting these findings, it is essential to recognize that the vast majority of published data derive from retrospective, single-institution case series (Oxford Centre for Evidence-Based Medicine Level IV) or systematic reviews of such studies. No Level I or II evidence specifically addresses second free flap outcomes. Accordingly, the following discussion synthesizes the best available evidence while explicitly noting where conclusions rest on weaker data.

The synthesis of this evidence provides a clear mandate for clinical practice: a standardized, algorithmic approach is essential for navigating the decision-making process in these complex cases. Upon identifying a patient who may require a second free flap, the algorithm should commence with a rigorous multidisciplinary evaluation, assessing not just the defect but also the patient’s physiological and psychological reserve. The cornerstone of planning is high-resolution vascular imaging to map the recipient vasculature. Based on this, a logical sequence of recipient vessel options should be considered, progressing from the contralateral neck vessels to the transverse cervical, thoracoacromial, and internal mammary vessels. The choice of flap should be guided primarily by pedicle length and the nature of the tissue deficit. Intraoperatively, the surgeon must be prepared for the judicious use of vein grafts and must employ microsurgical techniques tailored to thick-walled, irradiated vessels. This stepwise, proactive framework mitigates uncertainty and enhances the probability of a successful outcome.

It is crucial to acknowledge the inherent limitations of the evidence base underpinning these conclusions. The literature is dominated by retrospective case series from single, high-volume institutions, which are susceptible to selection and publication bias. The lack of prospective, controlled studies, let alone randomized trials, means that comparisons of techniques and outcomes are based on heterogeneous patient groups and historical controls. The definition of “success” itself is often limited to flap survival, with fewer studies providing robust, standardized data on long-term functional outcomes, patient-reported quality of life, or cost-effectiveness. Furthermore, the technical nuances described often reflect the preferences and expertise of individual surgeons or centers, making it difficult to establish universal best practices. Perhaps most importantly, the literature has historically focused on flap survival as the primary endpoint, with few studies reporting standardized, patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) that capture what matters most to patients – speech, swallowing, appearance, and social functioning. These limitations preclude definitive Level I recommendations and highlight the need for careful interpretation of the available data.

Indocyanine green (ICG) angiography has emerged as a valuable adjunct for intraoperative assessment of flap perfusion, though its specific utility in secondary head and neck reconstruction remains an area of active investigation. The technique involves intravenous injection of ICG (0.2–0.5 mg/kg), followed by real-time near-infrared fluorescence imaging, allowing qualitative and semiquantitative evaluation of tissue perfusion before flap inset and again after microvascular anastomosis to confirm patency. In primary head and neck free flap surgery, a 2023 systematic review reported that ICG angiography altered intraoperative management in 12–18% of cases, most commonly by guiding partial flap debridement of hypoperfused zones or revising anastomoses with inadequate flow. However, evidence specifically for secondary reconstruction is limited to small retrospective series. Practical limitations include the additional cost (approximately $500–$1000 per case), the need for dedicated imaging equipment, and the learning curve for accurate interpretation of fluorescence patterns. Furthermore, ICG cannot reliably predict late thrombosis or distinguish between vasospasm and true anastomotic failure. Current evidence does not support routine use of ICG in all secondary free flap cases, but it may be most valuable in the vessel-depleted neck, where pedicle geometry is compromised, or when assessing the viability of previously irradiated skin paddles. Prospective studies specifically addressing ICG utility in secondary reconstruction are needed before recommending its routine adoption.

The near-absence of standardized PROMs in studies of second free flaps represents a critical gap in the literature. Validated instruments specifically designed for head and neck cancer patients include the University of Washington Quality of Life questionnaire (UW-QOL), which assesses physical function (chewing, swallowing, speech, taste, saliva, pain, activity, shoulder function) and social-emotional domains (anxiety, mood, appearance); the EORTC QLQ-H&N35, a 35-item module complementary to the core EORTC QLQ-C30 that evaluates symptoms and side effects including pain, swallowing, senses, speech, social eating, social contact, and sexuality; and the FACE-Q Head and Neck Cancer module, a more recently developed patient-reported outcome instrument specifically designed for reconstructive surgery populations. None of these instruments has been systematically applied to cohorts of patients undergoing second or subsequent free flaps. Future studies should prioritize the routine incorporation of PROMs at baseline and at standardized postoperative intervals (e.g., 6, 12, and 24 months) to determine whether the substantial surgical effort and risk of secondary reconstruction translate into meaningful improvements in quality of life from the patient’s perspective.

Beyond the technical and clinical considerations discussed throughout this review, the integration of artificial intelligence tools – particularly large language models such as ChatGPT – into healthcare and research workflows warrants brief mention. A global-scale, cross-sectional study by Guo et al employed machine learning-driven informatics analysis to systematically map ChatGPT applications across six core healthcare scenarios: clinical decision support and health management, data processing with safety considerations, medical education opportunities and challenges, public attitudes and acceptance, performance on medical health examinations, and ethics and policy governance. The study identified that clinical decision support (Cluster 1) and medical education (Cluster 3) received the greatest research attention, with key nodes including information integration, patient education, diagnostic support, and quality assessment. While this work did not specifically address microvascular reconstruction, its findings are directly relevant to secondary free flap surgery, where complex decision-making regarding recipient vessel selection, flap choice, and vein graft utilization could potentially benefit from AI-assisted clinical decision support tools. Furthermore, as noted in the TITAN guideline cited in our manuscript, any use of such tools must be transparently declared, and human oversight remains essential to avoid bias, fabrication, or misinterpretation. Future research should explore whether large language models can assist in preoperative planning, recipient vessel selection algorithms, or patient counseling in the challenging setting of secondary head and neck reconstruction, while maintaining rigorous ethical and methodological standards as emphasized by Guo et al[100].

Conclusion

The undertaking of a second or subsequent microvascular free flap represents one of the most formidable challenges in head and neck reconstructive surgery. This review consolidates the evidence confirming that these procedures, while inherently complex and carrying a heightened risk profile, are a viable and critically important option for patients facing recurrent cancer, ORN, or the failure of a primary reconstruction. Success is not achieved by simply repeating the initial operation but through a fundamental paradigm shift in approach. It hinges on meticulous, image-guided preoperative planning to navigate a hostile, vessel-depleted field, technical adaptability to employ alternative recipient vessels and vein grafts, and the selection of flaps based on pedicle attributes. Ultimately, achieving the dual goals of durable wound coverage and meaningful functional restoration demands a seasoned, multidisciplinary team capable of tailoring the strategy to each patient’s unique anatomical and physiological circumstances. When executed with this rigorous and sophisticated approach, a second free flap can provide patients with the best opportunity for a definitive reconstruction and an improved quality of life.
