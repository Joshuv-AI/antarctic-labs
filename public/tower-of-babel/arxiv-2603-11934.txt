Decoding universal cycles for t t -subsets and t t -multisets by decoding bounded-weight de Bruijn sequences
Daniel Gabrić, Wazed Imam, Lukas Janik-Jones, Joe Sawada

Abstract
A universal cycle for a set 𝐒 \mathbf{S} of combinatorial objects is a cyclic sequence of length | 𝐒 | |\mathbf{S}| that contains a representative of each element in 𝐒 \mathbf{S} exactly once as a substring. Despite the many universal cycle constructions known in the literature for various sets including k k -ary strings of length n n , permutations of order n n , t t -subsets of an n n -set, and t t -multisets of an n n -set, remarkably few have efficient decoding (ranking/unranking) algorithms. In this paper we develop the first polynomial time/space decoding algorithms for bounded-weight de Bruijn sequences for strings of length n n over an alphabet of size k k . The results are then applied to decode universal cycles for t t -subsets and t t -multisets.

Decoding universal cycles.

Let
\mathcal{U}=u_{1}u_{2}\cdots u_{m}
be a universal cycle for a set
\mathbf{S}
of length-
n

The

is found in

\mathbf{s}

O(|{\bf S}|)
time. Similarly, a look-up table can be applied to store the position of a given string, but this requires
\Theta(|{\bf S}|)
space. We say a decoding algorithm is
efficient
if the ranking and unranking algorithms run in polynomial time and require polynomial space, with respect to the size of the alphabet and the length of each string
\mathbf{s}
. The efficient decoding of a universal cycle is necessary when it is applied to robotic position sensing (vision) applications
[
3
,
8
]
.

Main results.

The main results of this paper are as follows:

1.

We demonstrate that the lexicographically smallest universal cycle for
\mathbf{S}_{k}(n,w^{\uparrow})
(a bounded-weight de Bruijn sequence) is efficiently decodable.

2.

We demonstrate a universal cycle for
t

3.

We demonstrate a universal cycle for
t
-multisets that is efficiently decodable.

As we mention later in the paper, a decodable universal cycle for

can be easily applied to decode a universal cycle for the strings in

The analysis of all algorithms described in this paper assume the unit-cost RAM model of computation. A C implementation of our decoding algorithms is available at

Recall that
\mathbf{S}_{k}(n)

A

Let

is said to be

; otherwise
\mathbf{s}
is

Let
\mathbf{N}_{k}(n)=(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\ldots,\sigma_{t})
denote the tuple consisting of all necklaces in
\mathbf{S}_{k}(n)
listed in lexicographic order. For example,

\mathbf{N}_{2}(4)=(\mathtt{1111,1112,1122,1212,1222,2222}).

.

denote this lexicographically smallest de Bruijn sequence for

For example,

2.1
Bounding the weight

The
weight
of a string
\mathbf{s}
, denoted by
\wt(\mathbf{s})
, is the sum of it symbols. Recall that
\mathbf{S}_{k}(n,w^{\uparrow})
denotes the subset of
\mathbf{S}_{k}(n)
containing strings with weight
at least

w
.
Let
\mathbf{N}_{k}(n,w^{\uparrow})=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{m})
denote the tuple consisting of all necklaces in
\mathbf{S}_{k}(n,w^{\uparrow})
listed in lexicographic order. For example,

\mathbf{N}_{2}(4,6^{\uparrow})=(\mathtt{1122,1212,1222,2222}).

Remarkably, the Granddaddy construction can be generalized to
\mathbf{S}_{k}(n,w^{\uparrow})
.
Let

\mathcal{G}_{k}(n,w^{\uparrow})=\ap(\alpha_{1})\ap(\alpha_{2})\cdots\ap(\alpha_{m}).

The sequence
\mathcal{G}_{k}(n,w^{\uparrow})
is the lexicographically smallest bounded-weight de Bruijn sequence for
\mathbf{S}_{k}(n,w^{\uparrow})

[
29
,
30
]
.
For example,

\mathcal{G}_{2}(4,6^{\uparrow})=\mathtt{1122\cdot 12\cdot 1222\cdot 2}.

Unfortunately, adapting the Granddaddy for strings with an upper bound on the weight does not necessarily lead to a universal cycle. For example, the sequence
\mathtt{1\cdot 1112\cdot 1122\cdot 12}
is not a universal cycle for
\mathbf{S}_{2}(4,6_{\downarrow})
: the string
\mathtt{2211}
is missing.

There are a number of other known constructions for bounded-weight de Bruin sequences (for instance, see
[
29
,
30
,
13
,
27
]
); however, no efficient decoding algorithms for them was previously known. Efficient universal cycles are also known for the subsets of
\mathbf{S}_{k}(n)
with both a lower and upper bound on weights
[
28
]
.

2.2
Necklace properties

The following five properties of necklaces can be derived from the definition of a necklace.

Property 1
.

If
\alpha=\mathtt{a}_{1}\cdots\mathtt{a}_{j-1}\mathtt{x}\mathtt{k}^{n-j}
is in
\mathbf{N}_{k}(n)
for some
j\geq 1
and symbol
\mathtt{x}\neq\mathtt{k}
, then
\mathtt{a}_{1}\cdots\mathtt{a}_{j}(\mathtt{x}+1)\mathtt{k}^{n-j}
is also a necklace.

Property 2
.

Let
n,k>1
and
k<w<kn
. The first necklace in
\mathbf{N}_{k}(n,w^{\uparrow})
is
\mathtt{1}^{n-j-1}\mathtt{x}\mathtt{k}^{j}
,
where
j=\lfloor\frac{w-n}{k-1}\rfloor
and
\mathtt{x}=w-(n{-}j{-}1)-kj
; it has weight
w
and is aperiodic.

Property 3
.

Let
n,k>1
and
w<kn
. The last two necklaces in
\mathbf{N}_{k}(n,w^{\uparrow})
are

(\mathtt{k{-}1})\mathtt{k}^{n-1}
and
\mathtt{k}^{n}
.

The next property follows from the definition of a periodic necklace.

Property 4
.

Let
\alpha_{i}
and
\alpha_{i+1}
be consecutive necklaces in
\mathbf{N}_{k}(n,w^{\uparrow})
where
n,k>1
and
w<kn
. If
\alpha_{i}
is periodic then
\ap(\alpha_{i})\cdot\ap(\alpha_{i+1})
has prefix
\alpha_{i}
. Moreover
\alpha_{i+1}
is aperiodic.

Proof.

Since
\alpha_{i}
is periodic, it can be written as
\alpha_{i}=(\mathtt{a}_{1}\cdots\mathtt{a}_{p})^{j}
for some
j>1
. Let
\ell
be the largest index in
[1,p]
such
\mathtt{a}_{\ell}\neq\mathtt{k}
. Such an index exists since
\alpha_{i}
is not the last necklace
\mathtt{k}^{n}
in
\mathbf{N}_{k}(n,w^{\uparrow})
. By Property
1
, there exists a necklace with prefix
(\mathtt{a}_{1}\cdots\mathtt{a}_{p})^{j-1}
that is lexicographically larger than
\alpha_{i}
and also in
\mathbf{N}_{k}(n,w^{\uparrow})
. Thus
\alpha_{i+1}
must also have prefix
(\mathtt{a}_{1}\cdots\mathtt{a}_{p})^{j-1}
since
\mathbf{N}_{k}(n,w^{\uparrow})
is lexicographically ordered. Thus
\ap(\alpha_{i})\cdot\ap(\alpha_{i+1})
has prefix
\alpha_{i}
. The fact that
\alpha_{i+1}
is aperiodic is proved in
[
30
, Lemma 5]
.
∎

The following property is proved in
[
31
]
.

Property 5
.

Let
\alpha=\mathtt{a}_{1}\mathtt{a}_{2}\cdots\mathtt{a}_{n}
be a necklace and let
1\leq i\leq j\leq n
.
Then
\mathtt{a}_{i}\mathtt{a}_{i+1}\cdots a_{\mathtt{j}-1}\mathtt{x}
is lexicographically larger than
\alpha
if
\mathtt{x}>\mathtt{a}_{j}
.

Ranking the Granddaddy de Bruijn sequence
\mathcal{G}_{k}(n)

In this section we review how the Granddaddy de Bruijn sequence
\mathcal{G}_{k}(n)
can be efficiently decoded based on the groundbreaking results of Kociumaka, Radoszewski, and Rytter
[
21
]
. Then in Section
4
, we adapt the approach to decode the bounded-weight de Bruijn sequence
\mathcal{G}_{k}(n,w^{\uparrow})
.

Let
\mathbf{s}
be a string in
\mathbf{S}_{k}(n)
. It can be written as
\mathbf{s}=\mathbf{p}\mathbf{q}
where
\mathbf{q}
is the longest suffix of
\mathbf{s}
such that
\mathbf{q}\mathbf{p}
is a necklace. For example, if
\mathbf{s}=\mathtt{221221}

then
\mathbf{p}=\mathtt{22}
and
\mathbf{q}=\mathtt{1221}
.
Let
\rank(\mathbf{s})
denote the rank of
\mathbf{s}
in
\mathcal{G}_{k}(n)
.
The last
n
length-
n
substrings of
\mathcal{G}_{k}(n)
are all strings
\mathbf{s}=\mathtt{k}^{n-j}\mathtt{1}^{j}
for
0\leq j<n
. For these strings
\rank(\mathbf{s})=k^{n}-(n-j)+1
.
For all other strings, there are two key steps involved in the efficient computation of
\rank(\mathbf{s})
.

Step 1:
Determine consecutive necklaces
\beta_{1}
,
\beta_{2}
, and
\beta_{3}
in
\mathbf{N}_{k}(n)
such that
\ap(\beta_{1})\ap(\beta_{2})\ap(\beta_{3})
contains
\mathbf{s}
as a substring.
The following remark summarizes results from
[
10
]
.

Remark 6
.

There exist unique consecutive necklaces
\beta_{1}
,
\beta_{2}
, and
\beta_{3}
in
\mathbf{N}_{k}(n)
(considered cyclically) such that

\mathbf{p}
is a suffix of
\beta_{1}
and
\mathbf{q}
is a prefix of
\beta_{2}
, i.e.,
\mathbf{s}
is a substring of
\beta_{1}\beta_{2}
. Moreover,
\mathbf{s}
is a substring of
\ap(\beta_{1})\ap(\beta_{2})\ap(\beta_{3})
.

The necklaces
\beta_{1},\beta_{2}
, and
\beta_{3}
from the above remark can be determined in
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
time using
\mathcal{O}(n)
space
[
21
]
.

Step 2:
Compute
T_{k}(n,\sigma_{i})=|\ap(\sigma_{1})\ap(\sigma_{2})\cdots\ap(\sigma_{i-1})|
which counts the number of strings in
\mathbf{S}_{k}(n)
whose necklaces are lexicographically smaller than
\sigma_{i}
. For example,

T_{2}(4,\mathtt{1122})=|\{\mathtt{1111}\}|+|\{\mathtt{1112,1121,1211,2111}\}|=5.

The value
T_{k}(n,\sigma_{i})
can be computed in
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
time using
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
space
[
21
]
.

Putting these two steps together,

\rank(\mathbf{s})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}k^{n}-(n{-}j)+1,&\ \ \mbox{if $\mathbf{s}=\mathtt{k}^{n-j}\mathtt{1}^{j}$ for some $0\leq j<n$;}\\
T_{k}(n,\beta_{2}),-|\mathbf{p}|+1,&\ \ \mbox{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

Example 1

Recall
\mathcal{G}_{2}(4)=\mathtt{1111\underline{2112}21212222}
. Consider
\mathbf{s}=\mathtt{2112}
. It is a substring in the concatenation of
\beta_{1}=\mathtt{1112}
and
\beta_{2}=\mathtt{1122}
, where
\mathbf{p}=\mathtt{2}
and
\mathbf{q}=\mathtt{112}
. Since
T_{2}(4,\mathtt{1122})=5
, the rank of
\mathbf{s}
is
5-1+1=5
. The rank of
\mathtt{2211}
is
2^{4}-2+1=15
.

The string at rank
r
in
\mathcal{G}_{k}(n)
can be determined in
\mathcal{O}(n^{3}\log k)
time by applying a binary search and repeatedly applying the ranking algorithm
[
21
]
. See also a reinterpretation of these results in
[
31
]
.

Decoding the bounded-weight de Bruijn sequence
\mathcal{G}_{k}(n,w^{\uparrow})

In this section we adapt the decoding algorithm for the Granddaddy to decode the bounded-weight de Bruijn sequence
\mathcal{G}_{k}(n,w^{\uparrow})
. If
w=k
, then
\mathcal{G}_{k}(n,w^{\uparrow})=\mathcal{G}_{k}(n)
. If
w=kn
, then
\mathcal{G}_{k}(n,w^{\uparrow})
is the single symbol
\mathtt{k}
.
Throughout the remainder of this section assume that
k<w<kn
and let:

•

\alpha_{1}=\mathtt{a}_{1}\mathtt{a}_{2}\cdots\mathtt{a}_{n}
be the first necklace in
\mathbf{N}_{k}(n,w^{\uparrow})
,

•

\mathbf{s}=\mathbf{p}\mathbf{q}
be a string in
\mathbf{S}_{k}(n,w^{\uparrow})
, where
\mathbf{q}
is the longest suffix of
\mathbf{s}
such that
\mathbf{q}\mathbf{p}
is a necklace, and

•

\beta_{1}
and
\beta_{2}
be the necklaces defined in Remark
6
for
\mathbf{s}
.

Recall that
\beta_{1}
has suffix
\mathbf{p}
and
\beta_{2}
has prefix
\mathbf{q}
.

To adapt
Step 1
from the previous section, observe that necklaces
\beta_{1}
and
\beta_{2}
may not satisfy the required weight constraint. This is illustrated in the following example.

Example 2

Consider
n=3
,
k=4
, and
w=9
. Since

\mathbf{N}_{4}(3,9^{\uparrow})=\mathtt{144,~234,~243,~244,~333,~334,~344,~444},

we have

\mathcal{G}_{4}(3,9^{\uparrow})=\mathtt{14\underline{4\cdot 23}4\cdot 243\cdot 244\cdot 3\cdot 334\cdot 344\cdot 4}.

Consider
\mathbf{s}=\mathtt{423}
where
\mathbf{p}=\mathtt{4}
and
\mathbf{q}=\mathtt{23}
. In
\mathcal{G}_{4}(3)
,
\mathbf{s}
is found as a substring of
\beta_{1}\cdot\beta_{2}=\mathtt{22\underline{4\cdot 23}3}
. However, in
\mathcal{G}_{4}(3,9^{\uparrow})
it is found as a substring of
14\underline{4\cdot 23}4
.

To address this challenge, first consider the strings found in the wraparound of
\mathcal{G}_{k}(n,w^{\uparrow})
.
Recall from Property
2
that
\alpha_{1}
is aperiodic. Thus,
\alpha_{1}
is a prefix of
\mathcal{G}_{k}(n,w^{\uparrow})
.
From Property
3
,
\alpha_{m-1}=(\mathtt{k{-}1})\mathtt{k}^{n-1}
and
\alpha_{m}=\mathtt{k}^{n}

positions can be filled in

Thus, for
0\leq j\leq t\leq n

w\geq 0

B(t,j,w)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&\ \ \ \mbox{if \ \ $j=t=0$ and $w\leq 0$, }\\
0&\ \ \ \mbox{if \ \ $j=t$,}\\
B(t,j+1,w)+\displaystyle{\sum_{\mathtt{x}=\mathtt{a}_{j+1}+1}^{\mathtt{k}}B(t-j-1,0,w-\wt(\mathtt{a}_{1}\cdots\mathtt{a}_{j}\mathtt{x}))}&\ \ \ \mbox{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

Now consider the set
\mathbf{P}(t,j,w)
which denotes the subset of
\mathbf{S}_{k}(t)
where each string has weight
exactly

w
and prefix
\mathtt{a}_{1}\mathtt{a}_{2}\cdots\mathtt{a}_{j}
such that every non-empty suffix is greater than
\alpha
. Let
P(t,j,w)=|\mathbf{P}(t,j,w)|
. Applying the same recursive strategy applied for computing

, but with different base cases, we obtain the following recurrence for

:

\small{P}(t,j,w)=\begin{cases}1&\ \ \ \text{if }j=t=w=0,\\
0&\ \ \ \text{if }j=t\mbox{ or }w\leq 0,\\
{P}(t,j+1,w)+\displaystyle{\sum_{\mathtt{x}=\mathtt{a}_{j+1}+1}^{\mathtt{k}}P(t-j-1,0,w-\wt(\mathtt{a}_{1}\cdots\mathtt{a}_{j}\mathtt{x}))}&\ \ \ \text{otherwise}.\\
\end{cases}

7.2
Computing
T_{k}(n,w,\alpha)

Let
\textbf{T}_{k}(n,w,\alpha)
denote the subset of strings in
\mathbf{S}_{k}(n,w^{\uparrow})
whose necklaces are lexicographically smaller than a given necklace
\alpha
. Note that
T_{k}(n,w,\alpha)=|\textbf{T}_{k}(n,w,\alpha)|
. To compute
T_{k}(n,w,\alpha)
, we partition
\textbf{T}_{k}(n,w,\alpha)
into subsets following the approaches used in
[
31
,
14
]
.

Let
\textbf{A}(t,j)
denote the subset of strings in
\textbf{T}_{k}(n,w,\alpha)
of the form
\mathbf{s}=\mathtt{s}_{1}\mathtt{s}_{2}\cdots\mathtt{s}_{n}
where
t
is the smallest integer such that
\mathbf{s}^{\prime}=\mathtt{s}_{t}\mathtt{s}_{t+1}\cdots\mathtt{s}_{1}\mathtt{s}_{2}\cdots\mathtt{s}_{t-1}<\alpha
and
j
is the largest integer such that
\mathtt{a}_{1}\cdots\mathtt{a}_{j}
is a prefix of
\mathbf{s}^{\prime}
. Let
A(t,j)=|\textbf{A}(t,j)|
. Then

T_{k}(n,w,\alpha)=\displaystyle{\sum_{t=1}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n}A(t,j).}

7.3
Analysis

By applying dynamic programming, all necessary
B(t,j,w)
and

\mathcal{O}(n^{3}k)

time for

and
O(k)
time for
Case 2
, and thus

can be computed in

time.
This proves Lemma
