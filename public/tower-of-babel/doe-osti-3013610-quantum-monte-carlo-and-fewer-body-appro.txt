1 Accomplishments

1.1 Major Goals of the project

The overall goals of this project were to advance understanding of reactions and scattering
states in light nuclei, as well as related properties of bound states, and to facilitate application
of that understanding to astrophysics and cosmology. These goals were pursued mainly using
quantum Monte Carlo methods to compute nuclear properties using protons and neutrons
as the basic degrees of freedom.

By budget, the main focus was on extending the quantum Monte Carlo methods to address
coupled-channel scattering problems, using wave functions with particle-in-a-box boundary
conditions. In the short term, this meant developing methods to compute cross sections for
scatterings that change the orbital or spin angular momentum. These cases of coupled angu-
lar momentum channels serve as a stepping stone to the longer-term goal of treating particle
rearrangement reactions. The main application chosen to drive this development under this
project was nucleon scattering from a mass-three nucleus: neutron-triton scattering, initially
using the variational Monte Carlo (VMC) computational method, with a goal of eventually
adapting the work to the trickier but more accurate Green’s function Monte Carlo (GFMC)
method.

A second major thrust in the proposals was the development of “integral methods,” wherein
scattering properties (which are nominally properties of the outer parts of the wave function)
are computed by integrals over the short-range parts of the wave function. I anticipated that
this would essentially “correct” problems with the less-accurate outer parts of the quantum
Monte Carlo wave functions, and also that it could be used in an approximation method
where a single wave function at one engery stands in for wave functions at many energies, with
changes in the energy introduced entirely through the “kernel” of the integral method. This
second thrust ended up being largely merged with the coupled-channels work and applied to
the neutron-triton system by Ph.D. student Abraham Flores.

In addition to direct application of integral methods to true scattering wave functions, a
significant part of the plan was to develop integral methods as a tool for approximation within
the quantum Monte Carlo methods, possibly allowing cross section calculations of reasonable
accuracy using lower-quality wave functions that still capture essential aspects of systems.
As originally envisioned, much of this work would involve application of “pseudobound”
approximations to resonance states, in which unbound resonance states are computed with
bound-state boundary conditions. Many such states had previously been computed and
found to provide reasonable estimates of resonance energies.

The original proposal envisioned continuation of my previous work with collaborators on
application of halo effective field theory to astrophysically significant cross sections in light
nuclei. The main application envisioned at that time was to the capture of 3 He and 4 He nuclei
to form 7 Be in the Sun and the Big Bang. It also included bringing some undergraduates

1.2 What was accomplished under the goals

Nearly all of the Ph.D. thesis work of student Abraham Flores occurred during the project
period, which began at the start of his second year of graduate school. His was initially
assigned the work on coupled-channel scattering in the neutron-triton system using the
VMC method.

Although the original plan for Flores’ thesis work was to develop the basic framework of
particle-in-a-box treatment of coupled-channels problems, practical considerations eventually
forced the merger of this work with the second main thrust of the project as described in
both the original and the renewal proposals: development of numerical methods that extract
properties of the box surface (which determine scattering outcomes) by performing integrals
over the box interior. These methods enabled an approximation method that we refer to
in our publications as the “fixed interior wave approximation,” which reduces the amount
of computation needed to find scattering phase shifts over a range of low energies, and
solves difficulties in obtaining pairs of independent wave functions with matched energies.
Application of these methods was published in Physical Review C and made up a large
fraction of Flores’ thesis.

Much of the time required to bring the work up to publishable standards was spent on figuring
out how to properly regularize a singularity in the integration kernels; Flores obtained rough
but disappointing scattering phase shifts, and figuring out how to achieve reasonable accuracy
was a multi-year process of figuring out how to do the regularization properly. The key was
to find a procedure that allows unambiguous distinction between good and bad values of the
regularizer’s scaling-length input parameter. The procedure is based on examination of the
radial overlap function characterizing projection of our four-body states onto the neutron-
triton channel. With these improvements, the approach became successful in matching
essentially exact calculations done by other groups using the same potential, even though
our actual wave functions using the nuclear VMC ansatz are significantly less precise.

We had originally envisioned Flores’ Ph.D. thesis including GFMC calculations in the neutron-
triton system, building on his VMC wave functions. However, integral relations had devel-
oped into an essential ingredient of his VMC calculations, especially for coupled channels,
making the incorporation of the integral relations into the GFMC codebase an essential task.
By the time this complicated task was completed, Flores faced a deadline for completion of
his degree, and there was no time to complete the extension of the GFMC code to include

 2

the spin-isospin basis setup for neutron-triton scattering states. Instead, Flores applied the
integral relations in GFMC to neutron scattering from 4 He nuclei. He succeeded in re-
producing existing benchmarks based on “exact” particle-in-a-box calculations, but with a
method that is easier to carry out accurately (given existing code). This work was included
in his thesis, defended in August 2023. However, complications arose during preparation
for journal publication after Flores left and started a postdoctoral appointment, leaving us
with some questions about the accuracy of the original results in the thesis. The issues were
not resolved until about a year after Flores graduated, and publication finally occurred in
Physical Review C a few months after the end of the project period.

Also during the project, exploratory work on applications of integral methods to VMC
wave functions was carried out mainly by Master’s students, but did not reach the point
of ripeness for journal publication. Master’s student Satish Chandran carried out an initial
exploration of integral methods for alpha-emission channel amplitudes (whereas all of my
other work on integral relations focused on states that emit single nucleons). He found bound-
state asymptotic normalization coefficients for states of 7 Li and 7 Be, as well as resonance-
state analogues of those amplitudes that he used for crude estimates of resonance energy
widths. That work was promising, but it also presented some puzzles, and it will need
a more careful second pass before anything publishable results (although they have been
presented as preliminary results at meetings of the American Physical Society and American
Astronomical Society). Chandran finished his thesis after many covid-related delays and
graduated in August 2022.

Master’s student Arik Mahbub carried out an extension of the fixed interior wave approxima-
tion originally developed by Abraham Flores, in which he used single fixed “pseudobound”
approximations to resonance states but scanned over the energies input to the integral
method as a way of computing approximate scattering phase shifts. By the time that he
needed to finish his thesis and graduate in fall 2023, he had applied the method to low-lying
resonances in the 5 He and 5 Li systems, with mixed results that were not yet done to high
enough standards for journal publications. At the end of the project, Master’s student Alec
Clark had started extending Mahbub’s work into larger nuclei.

This project has been associated with (but not directly supported) a series of undergraduate
projects directed toward learning stellar evolution models and eventually developing models
of lithium-rich giant stars. During this period those students were Nicholas Kinzel (graduated
in Spring 2022) and Esteban Canizales (grduated in Spring 2023). The high point so far has
been some actual models that produced lithium-rich stars, and significant training of the PI
in how the code works, but nothing publishable.

The PI’s collaboration with Daniel Philips of Ohio University and with Xilin Zhang, who
occupied verious postdoctoral positions during the project period, resulted in one publication
during the period funded here. Application of halo effective field theory to the astrophysical
reaction 3 He(α, γ)7 Be had been started prior to the project period.

Finally, DOE support under this project facilitated PI participation in the Solar Fusion III
review of nuclear rates for solar-neutrino work. The initial workshop to organize the work

1.3 Opportunities for training and professional development pro-
 vided

The students working on this project learned aspects of quantum mechanics, many-body
physics, particle physics, and astrophysics that are not covered in their coursework. They
also gained experience working independently on relatively long-term project-oriented work.
Each student met with me approximately weekly to report progress and to receive instruction
and guidance on next steps, as well as general mentorship. Students gained significant coding
skills and experience, and in particular learned methods of scientific programming beyond
the instruction received in the SDSU physics department’s two courses on computation. The
Ph.D. student gained experience in parallel programming. All of them gained substantial
experience with technical writing.

4 Impact

Impact on development of the principal discipline of the project

Quantum mechanical systems of many interacting particles remain very difficult to model
due to their mathematical complexity. This is especially true for atomic nuclei, due to
the complexity of the forces among neutrons and protons and due to the Pauli exclusion
principle that forbids any two of these particles from being in the same state at the same
time. Collisions of nuclei involve all of the difficulties associated with calculations of a
single nucleus, but additional complication arises because the particles are not confined to

 5

a small region of space. Consequently, physicsists have only made a start on developing
methods that can start from neutron and proton properties and predict what happens in a
collision involving even most small nuclei accurately – a so-called ”ab initio” calculation of
the collision. During this award period, we made progress toward extending the application
of the quantum Monte Carlo computational methods to these kinds of problems, mainly for
cases where the collision changes the colliding nuclei in some way.

The quantum Monte Carlo methods constitute one of a few different computational ap-
proaches that might allow ab initio calculation of collisions. The ability to predict collision
properties accurately for a wide range of nuclear systems would be a feat in itself. It would
also help to determine the nature of the strong nuclear force that binds atomic nuclei to-
gether, and it would allow more accurate calculations of the nuclear reaction rates that are
important inputs to models of stars and the Big Bang.

Under this project, we developed the use of integral relations to extract information about
scattering observables from quantum Monte Carlo wave functions. In the variational Monte
Carlo approach, we found that it can produce quite accurate results even from relatively
inaccurate (compared with the exact Green’s function Monte Carlo) variational Monte Carlo
wave functions, and we adapted the integral methods to the more complicated (but more
accurate) Green’s function Monte Carlo setting. This opens a path to calculations in a
wide range of light systems, and to resolving difficulties in Green’s function Monte Carlo
calculations of collisions. We also developed extensions of the integral methods to collisions of
alpha particles (instead of nucleons) with nuclei, and explored its application to pseudobound
calculations of resonance states.

Impact on other disciplines

Through PI participation in the Solar Fusion III review of cross sections for solar neutrino
physics, this work supports improvements in interpretation of experimental nuclear data, and
in the application of those data to stellar astrophysics, particle physics, and geochemistry.

Impact on the development of human resources

Participation in this award trained one Ph.D. in the area of computational science, three
Master’s students in physics, and five undergraduates in physics. The Ph.D. student con-
tinued on to postdoc in a nuclear theory group; his training places him at the forefront of
computer applications to science. One of the Master’s students (Chandran) is currently a
mathematics Ph.D. student at the Uniersity of California, Riverside, and the other (Mahbub)
has gone into industry. The undergraduates gained experience with coding and in pursuing
independent projects in science. One of them (Kinzel) reports that his experience in the
group helped place him into his first job out of college.
