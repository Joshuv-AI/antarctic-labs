Challenges of caring for complex, vulnerable patients: how psychiatric nurses experience caring for people with co-occurring psychiatric and addiction disorders
Authors: Natalie Finch
Published: 2026

Commentary on: Bertrand E, Morvillers J.M. Nurses perceptions of patients with co-occurring psychiatric and substance use disorders in psychiatric settings: a grounded theory study. Journal of Advanced Nursing. 2025 Jan; 0:1–14.1

Implications for practice and research

Nursing staff who care for people with co-occurring psychiatric and substance use disorders should be offered high-quality supervision and be trained and supported to use evidence-based behaviour change strategies in their clinical practice.

Context

Where psychiatric disorders co-occur with substance use disorders, patients have poorer outcomes across global domains of health and wellbeing.2 Prevalence rates of this presentation are high,3 4 yet studies show that nurses frequently hold stigmatised views of this group.5 This presents a major barrier to the provision of high-quality nursing care and has an impact on staff wellbeing.

Methods

The authors conducted 11 semi-structured interviews with psychiatric and mental health nurses who came into frequent contact with patients who experienced co-occurring psychiatric and substance use disorders. While all participants worked at one public psychiatric hospital in the Paris area, some were based in the inpatient facility and others in the connected community mental health service. A theoretical sampling strategy was used as data collection progressed to support deeper enquiry into the emerging themes.

Data were collected in face-to-face interviews using a flexible interview guide which was refined based on the emerging data. The guide sought to draw out how nurses’ perspectives on this patient group shaped nursing care. Multiple rounds of coding were conducted to arrive at key themes, in accordance with grounded theory methodology.

Findings

Continuous analysis and refinement of the data concluded with the finding of a central theme of ‘waiting for a patient’s will to change’. Here, the concept of change is hinged around substance use behaviour modification. The authors noted that while this waiting occurred, participants deployed two main strategies: clinical management of the patient from an illness perspective and a focus on relationship building. Achieving balance in responding to these symbiotic areas was identified by participants as crucial in caring for this group of patients; however, it was considered difficult to achieve when behaviours relating to drug use were perceived as intolerable. This negatively impacted both the nurses’ sense of agency, confidence and trust in the patient. The findings highlight both the clinical and emotional complexity of this work.

Commentary

This study highlights how nursing care in this setting is dictated by the perception of waiting for the patient to be ready to change. The paper argues that staff are not generally passive in the wait period, focusing on clinician management and/or relationship building, which are both valuable. The paper does highlight, however, the inaction of staff in deploying evidence-based interventions that may have a direct impact on the duration of the wait time that is viewed as so crucial. The paper makes clear suggestions with regard to addressing the nursing skills deficit identified with evidence-based behaviour change interventions. Upskilling staff in areas such as motivational interviewing is likely to improve patient outcomes and positively impact nursing job satisfaction and an increased sense of agency. The paper also highlights the need for high-quality clinical supervision to attend to the challenging emotional labour that participants express is a fundamental part of this role.

The sample size and geography of the study limit the generalisability of the findings, particularly in locations where care is more integrated than the parallel model used in France. Regardless of this, the rich qualitative data are likely to be of interest to all practitioners with a focus on caring for people with co-occurring psychiatric and substance use disorders as it adds something new to the literature by looking at how nurses’ perceptions of the patient group impact on care delivery.

The paper sought to include the experiences of nurses across both hospital and community settings; however, 9 out of the 11 participants worked in community roles. This is acknowledged as a limitation in terms of applicability of findings to inpatient settings. Notably, the two hospital-based nurses were very newly qualified, while the nine community-based nurses had at least a decade of nursing experience. This too may have had an impact which was not explored. Further exploration of the proposed model in both hospital and inpatient locations as discreet locations would be beneficial to support its future applicability across settings where care may occur.
