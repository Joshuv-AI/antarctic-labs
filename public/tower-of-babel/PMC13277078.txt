Strengthening women’s leadership in health systems through innovation and intersectionality
Authors: Augustus Osborne, Morie Vandi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Women comprise most of the global health and care workforce, yet they remain underrepresented in leadership across health systems, including in ministries of health, hospitals, academic institutions, humanitarian agencies, and health financing bodies. This imbalance is not only a matter of fairness; it shapes whose expertise is recognised, which priorities are funded, and how health systems respond to inequity, crisis, and exclusion. The problem is particularly acute in fragile and conflict-affected settings, where insecurity, displacement, interrupted education, shrinking civic space, and institutional breakdown further narrow women’s pathways into leadership. This viewpoint argues that strengthening women’s leadership should be treated as a core component of health systems strengthening rather than as a peripheral diversity agenda. It further argues that leadership initiatives must move beyond numerical inclusion to address how authority, legitimacy, and opportunity are distributed across institutions and communities. Drawing on an intersectional lens, the paper examines how gender interacts with ethnicity, caste, class, disability, age, geography, and displacement status to shape who is excluded from leadership pathways and why. It then outlines four practical and adaptable strategies for change: digital mentorship and leadership training platforms, community-based leadership incubators, policy and institutional reform fellowships, and participatory research and advocacy networks. The paper emphasises that leadership development will remain limited unless it is accompanied by reforms to workplace culture, sponsorship, promotion systems, safety, and institutional accountability. Strengthening women’s leadership in health systems is therefore essential not only for representation, but also for building more equitable, trusted, and resilient systems capable of advancing universal health coverage and responding effectively to diverse community needs.

Introduction

Women comprise the majority of the global health and care workforce, yet they remain markedly underrepresented in leadership across health systems, including in ministries of health, hospitals, research institutions, humanitarian agencies, and financing bodies [1, 2]. This contradiction is well documented. Women deliver care, sustain frontline services, coordinate community responses, and absorb much of the social and emotional strain generated by weak systems, but they are significantly less likely than men to occupy senior decision-making roles. This imbalance is not only a question of fairness. It shapes which priorities are funded, whose expertise is recognized, and how institutions respond to exclusion, crisis, and everyday inequity. The scale of the imbalance is striking. Globally, women account for approximately 70% of the health and social care workforce but hold fewer than 25% of senior leadership positions [3]. In sub-Saharan Africa, women constitute the majority of community and frontline health workers yet represent a minority of facility managers, district health officers, and ministerial decision-makers [4]. In Ethiopia, despite women comprising the majority of health extension workers, national health programme directorates are led overwhelmingly by men [4]. Similar patterns are documented across South Asia, the Pacific, and Latin America. These data reveal that numerical workforce representation does not translate automatically into institutional authority, and that targeted effort is required to close the leadership gap.

The problem is particularly acute in conflict-affected and fragile settings, where violence, displacement, insecurity, interrupted education, and institutional breakdown further narrow women’s pathways into leadership [5, 6]. In such settings, power is often consolidated in militarized and male-dominated structures, even while women continue to hold communities together through formal and informal care work. Yet the argument for strengthening women’s leadership in health systems extends beyond symbolic inclusion. Evidence from health governance and political representation suggests that, in some contexts, women leaders may be more likely to prioritize social welfare, primary care, accountability, and inclusive service delivery, although these associations are shaped by institutions and cannot be treated as universal traits [7, 8]. In health emergencies and fragile settings, women also often occupy trusted positions in communities and act as intermediaries between households and formal services. When their leadership is supported, health systems may become more responsive to maternal health, child health, gender-based violence, and barriers affecting access to care among marginalized groups [9, 10].

An intersectional lens is essential to this agenda because women do not experience health systems or leadership pathways in the same way. Gender interacts with ethnicity, caste, class, disability, age, displacement status, religion, geography, and educational opportunity to shape who is seen, heard, protected, and promoted. Intersectionality, as developed through Black feminist scholarship, helps explain why formal commitments to gender equality often benefit relatively privileged women while leaving others behind [11]. A rural woman from a minority ethnic group, a refugee nurse, a lower-caste health worker, or a woman with disability may face layered barriers that are not captured by gender categories alone. Without attention to these overlapping structures of advantage and disadvantage, leadership initiatives risk reproducing the hierarchies they claim to challenge.

This viewpoint argues that strengthening women’s leadership in health systems requires more than adding women to existing structures. It requires interventions that widen access to authority, redistribute opportunity, and recognize multiple forms of leadership, including formal institutional leadership, operational and professional leadership, and community-based leadership. It also requires these interventions to be designed and tested in ways that are attentive to context, especially across fragile and non-fragile settings. The central claim is that women’s leadership should be treated as a core component of health systems strengthening, universal health coverage, and resilience rather than as an optional diversity agenda [12, 13].

Why women’s leadership matters for health systems

The case for women’s leadership in health systems is both normative and instrumental. Normatively, the underrepresentation of women in decision-making roles is inconsistent with principles of equity and democratic inclusion, especially in a sector where women make up most of the workforce [1, 2]. Instrumentally, leadership matters because it shapes policy priorities, organizational culture, workplace conditions, and the legitimacy of institutions. When decision-making spaces are socially narrow, blind spots become embedded in how health systems are governed.

A health system’s leadership affects more than high-level strategy. It influences budgeting decisions, staffing models, professional hierarchies, patient safety, accountability mechanisms, and relationships with communities. Exclusion from leadership therefore has system-wide consequences. It affects whether maternal and reproductive health are treated as central or peripheral; whether health worker safety is taken seriously; whether harassment is challenged; whether rural, minority, disabled, and displaced populations are considered in service design; and whether local knowledge is integrated into planning and response [7, 10, 12].

At the same time, it is important to avoid essentialist assumptions. The argument is not that women are inherently more caring, more ethical, or more community oriented than men. Rather, the claim is that leadership diversity matters because lived experience, social positioning, and differential exposure to exclusion can shape institutional priorities and problem recognition. In some contexts, women leaders have been associated with stronger attention to social welfare, child health, and public accountability, but these effects depend on political opportunity, organizational culture, and the degree of authority women are able to exercise in practice [7, 8]. A woman occupying a nominal leadership role within a hostile institution may have little real capacity to shift decisions. Leadership, therefore, is not simply about occupying a seat. It is about being able to act from that seat with legitimacy, protection, resources, and influence.

For this reason, women’s leadership should be understood in broad health systems terms. It includes formal institutional roles such as ministers, hospital directors, district managers, regulatory leaders, and deans. It also includes operational and professional leadership among nurse supervisors, programme managers, and technical leads. In many settings, it also includes community and civic leadership through health committees, community health worker networks, women’s groups, and local advocacy structures. These forms of leadership are not identical, but all shape how health systems function and whom they serve.

Barriers to women’s leadership across diverse contexts

The barriers to women’s leadership are widely recognized, but they do not operate in the same way across settings or across groups of women. In conflict-affected and fragile settings, women’s exclusion is intensified by insecurity, targeted violence, displacement, interrupted education, weak salaries, institutional breakdown, and shrinking civic space [5, 6]. Travel may become unsafe, workplaces may be militarized, and women may be pushed out of visible roles due to threats, stigma, or direct attacks. Restrictions on women’s mobility, education, and employment can have devastating effects on both service delivery and the long-term pipeline of female leaders, especially where women health workers are essential for care provision among women patients [14]. In countries such as Yemen, Afghanistan, Somalia, and South Sudan, prolonged instability has narrowed women’s ability to remain in or advance within the health sector by disrupting training, supervision, wages, and professional continuity [5, 9, 14].

These pressures are reinforced by gender norms that often harden during crisis rather than soften. Women may be accepted as caregivers but not as decision makers. Leadership can be framed as inappropriate, unsafe, or culturally disruptive, particularly when it requires public visibility, mobility, or authority over resources. Women who seek leadership roles may face reputational harm, family pressure, exclusion from negotiation spaces, or backlash from local elites [6, 15]. Humanitarian governance structures can also reproduce exclusion. Although international agencies frequently use the language of participation and inclusion, recruitment criteria often privilege English fluency, urban networks, formal credentials, and short-term deployability. Security procedures and centralized programming may further distance local women, especially those from rural, minority, or displaced communities, from institutional power. The humanitarian system itself faces profound disruption. Dramatic funding cuts, renewed calls for localisation, and sweeping reform agendas are reshaping how international health and humanitarian organisations operate [16]. Yet the proposals for reform emerging from this process are largely gender-blind [16]. Localisation, in principle, should empower local actors including local women’s organisations; in practice, however, the operationalisation of localisation frameworks frequently fails to distinguish between local male-dominated structures and locally embedded women’s civil society [17]. Reform packages that downsize international presence without transferring resources, authority, and protection to local women’s groups risk removing the scaffolding without building the floor. Women in fragile and conflict-affected settings therefore face a compound threat: not only do persistent structural barriers limit their advancement, but the reform processes intended to improve the system may actively erode the limited spaces they have managed to occupy [18–20].

In more stable settings, barriers are often less visible but remain deeply entrenched. Patriarchal organizational cultures shape who is recruited, promoted, mentored, paid, and recognized in health institutions across low-, middle-, and high-income settings [2, 21]. Women are often clustered in lower-paid and lower-status professions, while top management, academic leadership, and high-level policy roles remain male dominated. Informal sponsorship networks frequently favor men. Career progression is interrupted by unequal care burdens, weak parental support policies, gendered expectations around time and availability, and workplace norms that associate leadership with masculine styles of authority [1, 21, 22]. Exclusion also occurs through softer but highly consequential mechanisms: authorship patterns, funding access, board appointments, conference visibility, and informal professional networks.

These barriers are intensified by intersectional disadvantage. Women from marginalized ethnic or racial groups, lower caste groups, poor households, rural areas, refugee communities, or those living with disability often experience cumulative exclusion. Their language, accent, place of training, or embodied difference may be treated as markers of inferiority. Refugee and displaced women may possess substantial practical health experience but lack recognized documentation, legal status, or pathways into formal employment. Women with disability may face inaccessible transport, inaccessible training venues, discriminatory assumptions about competence, and technologies not designed for their use. Younger women may encounter age-based discounting of authority, while unmarried women may face gendered scrutiny over public visibility. These are not peripheral dynamics. They shape who is recognized as leadership material in the first place [11, 22, 23].

The consequences for health systems are substantial. When leadership is socially narrow, institutions are more likely to overlook practical barriers facing those who cannot travel safely, cannot afford care, do not speak dominant languages, or experience discrimination within services. In fragile settings, this weakens resilience by reducing the system’s ability to draw on local trust, contextual knowledge, and diverse adaptive capacity. In more stable settings, it narrows reform agendas and reduces accountability to underserved populations. A health system that sidelines women, especially women facing multiple forms of disadvantage, not only wastes talent but also undermines equitable and legitimate governance [7, 10].

An intersectional framework for strengthening women’s leadership

Any serious strategy to strengthen women’s leadership in health systems must begin with equity rather than numbers alone. Numerical representation matters, but parity by itself can obscure who benefits and who remains excluded. Programmes that reach only highly educated, urban, socially connected women may improve institutional optics without changing underlying inequities. An intersectional framework shifts attention from women as a single category to women located differently within systems of power [11, 23]. The guiding question is not only how to increase the number of women leaders, but which women are systematically excluded from leadership pathways and why.

A second principle is context specificity. Leadership development cannot be transplanted wholesale across settings. In fragile environments, safe mobility, trusted local partnerships, remote delivery, and flexible timelines may matter more than conventional institutional training. In relatively stable settings, formal organizational reform, promotion policies, parental support, anti-harassment enforcement, and accountability systems may be more feasible and necessary [5, 6]. The same intervention may work differently depending on whether women are navigating armed checkpoints, caste hierarchies, inaccessible transport, care burdens, or male-dominated professional associations.

A third principle is agency with authority. Women’s leadership should not be reduced to participation in structures that remain hostile or symbolic. Leadership requires decision-making power, not merely visibility. This means strengthening women’s ability to influence budgets, staffing, policy priorities, and accountability processes, while also ensuring that institutions do not punish women for exercising authority [22, 24]. Training in isolation is not enough if women remain excluded from networks, denied sponsorship, or prevented from acting on what they know.

A fourth principle is sustainability. Short-term initiatives may generate compelling stories without shifting structures. Enduring change depends on reforms to recruitment, promotion, workplace safety, remuneration, childcare support, mentorship, and legal protections from discrimination and harassment [12, 21, 22]. Leadership pathways require continuity. Without institutional anchoring, women may be celebrated during a programme but marginalized once donor attention moves on.

A fifth principle is collective legitimacy and accountability. Women’s leadership becomes more durable when communities, professional associations, policymakers, and male allies recognize its value and share responsibility for enabling it. Norms do not shift through instruction alone. They change when visible examples, practical benefit, and local legitimacy begin to align [6, 24]. This is especially important in settings where women’s authority is publicly contested.

Innovative approaches to develop and test women’s leadership

If women’s leadership is to be strengthened in meaningful ways, interventions must do more than offer short courses or symbolic panels. They need to alter access to skills, networks, legitimacy, and decision-making space. Four approaches are especially promising because they can be adapted across contexts and can be tested using implementation and systems research.

Digital mentorship and leadership training platforms

Digital mentorship and training platforms offer one route for reaching women whose mobility is constrained by insecurity, geography, disability, caregiving responsibilities, or restrictive social norms. Their value lies not in technology itself but in the possibility of connecting women to training, peers, and mentors without requiring repeated travel to central institutions. In conflict-affected settings such as Syria or Afghanistan, where in-person training may be unsafe or inaccessible, low-bandwidth mobile platforms could host modular training on health governance, budgeting, negotiation, advocacy, and staff management, combined with mentorship from women leaders who have navigated similar challenges [14, 25]. In more stable but geographically unequal settings such as rural India, the same model could support district nurses, community health supervisors, and early-career public health professionals who are often excluded from national leadership programmes concentrated in urban centers [26].

To be equitable, such platforms must be designed for real constraints rather than idealized users. That means multilingual and low-literacy options, voice-based learning, accessibility for women with disability, offline functionality where possible, data support, safe access points, and safeguards against digital surveillance or harassment. These features matter because digital interventions can easily reproduce exclusion if they assume stable connectivity, private device ownership, English fluency, or unrestricted online participation. Their effectiveness should therefore be assessed not only through completion rates but through changes in confidence, professional network strength, retention in the workforce, promotion, and perceived capacity to influence decisions.

Community-based leadership incubators

Community-based leadership incubators offer a more grounded strategy for recognizing and supporting women who are already leading informally but remain invisible to formal institutions. In many settings, women lead through mutual aid, referral support, health committees, community health work, crisis response, and local problem-solving, yet they are rarely recognized as legitimate health leaders. Incubators could provide structured support through training in public speaking, negotiation, evidence use, local planning, conflict mediation, and engagement with district or facility authorities. Crucially, they could also provide the material supports necessary for participation, including stipends, childcare, transport, and scheduling that acknowledges women’s unpaid labor burden.

The effectiveness of this approach depends heavily on local legitimacy. Partnerships with trusted local women’s groups, civil society organizations, and community institutions are therefore central. Recruitment should deliberately include women from marginalized groups, including lower caste communities, minority ethnic groups, displaced populations, indigenous groups, and women with disability. Without deliberate attention to who is invited and supported, incubators may be captured by locally powerful women and reproduce existing hierarchies. Evaluation should examine whether participants gain formal committee membership, increased influence over local planning, stronger advocacy skills, and greater recognition by health authorities, while also documenting risks such as backlash, unpaid labor intensification, or elite capture [24, 26].

Policy and institutional reform fellowships

Policy and institutional fellowships can address a recurring weakness in leadership development efforts: women may receive training but remain excluded from the spaces where policy is drafted, budgets are negotiated, and institutional norms are set. Fellowships can help bridge that gap by funding placements within ministries of health, district health offices, hospital management teams, regulatory councils, parliamentary health committees, or public health agencies. In fragile settings, such fellowships may help cultivate women leaders able to navigate politically complex systems while bringing local priorities into formal governance. In relatively stable settings, they may create pathways into senior administrative and technical roles from which women have long been informally excluded [2, 21].

The design of fellowships is critical. Selection criteria should not privilege only polished, elite, English-speaking candidates from prestigious institutions. They should also recognize rural service, nontraditional leadership experience, community-based work, and barriers overcome. Fellows require not only technical opportunities but fair remuneration, mentorship, anti-harassment protections, and explicit pathways into future posts. Without this, programmes risk tokenism: women are showcased temporarily but do not gain durable authority. Evaluation should therefore assess not only individual career progression but also whether fellows influence policy language, budget priorities, recruitment practices, committee deliberations, or organizational culture.

Participatory research and advocacy networks

Participatory research and advocacy networks offer a fourth pathway, particularly in settings where women are rarely treated as producers of policy-relevant knowledge. Too often, women in affected communities are consulted as respondents while analysis and agenda setting remain elsewhere. Supporting women to lead inquiry into health system problems in their own settings can begin to reverse that pattern. Women-led research groups could document barriers to maternal care, refugee access, disability inclusion, health worker safety, gender-based violence within services, or transport failures, and then use these findings to engage with policymakers and managers [15, 24].

This approach is especially useful from an intersectional perspective because it expands what counts as expertise. It values experiential, relational, and community-embedded knowledge alongside formal credentials. With training, modest funding, ethical support, and institutional links, such networks can increase women’s visibility as credible actors in public decision-making. Their success should not be judged only through publications, but through whether evidence informs local plans, facility changes, district budgets, or public debate. In contexts where formal authority is difficult to access, evidence-backed advocacy may itself become a route into leadership.

Across all four approaches, the central issue is not innovation for its own sake. It is whether interventions widen access to authority, account for differences among women, and shift leadership from rhetoric to lived institutional change. Comparative testing across conflict-affected, fragile, and more stable settings is essential because an intervention that works in one context may fail or produce unintended harms in another.

Implementation challenges and mitigation strategies

Even well-designed programmes will encounter resistance. One major challenge is social and cultural backlash. In many settings, women’s authority may be accepted in caregiving roles but contested in decision-making roles, especially where leadership requires public visibility, travel, or resource control. Such resistance may come from families, professional colleagues, employers, local authorities, or community leaders. Programmes that ignore this terrain risk exposing women to personal and professional harm. Mitigation requires sustained engagement with communities, professional bodies, local leaders, and male allies who can help build legitimacy and reduce backlash [6, 24]. Framing women’s leadership in terms of service quality, trust, and community benefit may sometimes increase local acceptability, although this should not replace a rights-based commitment to gender justice. It is important in this context to engage explicitly with the argument that gender equality should not only be a means to health system improvement, but an output and goal of health system reform in its own right. The Lancet Commission on Gender Equality, Peace and Health has articulated this case with particular clarity, arguing that health systems themselves must be redesigned to produce gender-equitable outcomes, not merely to incorporate gender-sensitive processes [27, 28]. This framing has important implications for how backlash is addressed. Rather than positioning women’s leadership as instrumentally valuable, something that improves outcomes and therefore merits tolerance, the rights-based argument insists that gender-equal leadership is a normative requirement of any system that claims to serve all people fairly. Building community and professional support for this argument requires investment in narrative, evidence, and visible examples: communities that see women health leaders producing better maternal outcomes, safer facilities, and more trusted referral networks are more likely to develop positive norms around women’s authority in health systems. Health institutions that treat leadership diversity as an organisational performance commitment, rather than a public relations exercise, are better placed to sustain the reforms needed to make such change durable.

A second challenge is security risk, especially in conflict and politically unstable settings. Travel to training sites may be unsafe, public affiliation with certain institutions may increase exposure, and digital participation can create risks related to surveillance, online harassment, or household control over technology [5, 15]. Safety therefore needs to be built into programme design from the outset. Local women’s organizations should play a central role in risk assessment and safeguarding because they often have the best understanding of context-specific threats. Security protocols should address physical travel, communications, data protection, anonymity where needed, and the domestic risks that women may face when joining visible leadership initiatives.

A third challenge is resource fragility and donor dependence. Many women’s leadership initiatives are small, pilot-driven, and short-lived. They generate activity but not durable pathways. Sustainable financing from governments, multilateral agencies, and philanthropic funders is needed to support stipends, childcare, devices, transport, mentorship, and longitudinal follow-up [1, 21]. Digital tools can improve reach, but they cannot substitute for institutional commitment. Without changes to hiring, promotion, workplace protection, remuneration, and organizational accountability, programmes may train women for leadership roles that remain structurally inaccessible. The current moment presents a structural threat of particular severity. The rapid withdrawal of development assistance for health (DAH), accelerated by geopolitical shifts and donor country budget pressures, is creating a crisis for gender-responsive health programming globally. Many of the global health reform proposals circulating at the time of writing, including those from the Wellcome Trust [16], are substantially gender-blind: they address structural inefficiencies, funding architecture, and multilateral coordination without meaningfully engaging with gender equality as either a process or an outcome [29]. The risk is not merely one of reduced funding; it is that the normative gains of two decades, the recognition of gender equity as central to universal health coverage, resilience, and effective governance, may be quietly abandoned as the agenda is reframed around financial sustainability alone. Women’s leadership initiatives are disproportionately exposed to this retrenchment because they are often housed in discrete gender programmes rather than mainstreamed into core health system financing. Any serious response to this challenge must therefore advocate for the integration of women’s leadership into the core architecture of health system investment, not as an add-on that survives only when discretionary funds permit.

A fourth challenge lies in intersectional blind spots. Gender-focused initiatives often overlook how caste, race, ethnicity, disability, language, class, age, or displacement shape participation and outcomes. As a result, programmes often benefit women who are easiest for institutions to reach and reward. Baseline assessment is therefore essential. Programmes should identify who is excluded, why, and at what point in the pipeline. Selection, curriculum, delivery, and evaluation should be co-designed with women from affected groups rather than merely adjusted after implementation begins [11, 22, 23]. Data should be disaggregated beyond sex wherever ethically and practically possible, or else programmes will not be able to see whom they are failing.

A fifth challenge is premature or narrow evaluation. Leadership does not always produce immediate title changes. Women may first gain influence informally through agenda setting, coalition building, expanded visibility, and increased credibility in community or institutional spaces. Evaluation should therefore include short- and long-term indicators, measuring confidence, network growth, retention, decision-making influence, policy effects, and formal promotions where relevant. Longitudinal and mixed-methods designs are especially important in fragile settings, where progress may be interrupted by displacement, renewed violence, or political change.

Research and evaluation priorities

If leadership interventions are to be more than symbolic, they must be studied with methodological seriousness. The evidence base on how to strengthen women’s leadership in health systems remains limited, especially in fragile settings. Future research should therefore move beyond descriptive accounts of underrepresentation toward testing mechanisms, implementation conditions, and outcomes over time. Critically, this research agenda should be directly linked to the four innovative approaches outlined in the preceding section. Each approach raises distinct and answerable questions. For digital mentorship and training platforms, research should test whether low-bandwidth, multilingual, and disability-accessible platforms improve leadership self-efficacy, network expansion, and career progression compared to in-person or no-intervention controls, particularly in fragile settings where mobility is constrained. For community-based leadership incubators, priority questions include whether incubator participation translates into durable formal and informal authority, whether elite capture can be prevented through deliberate targeting, and how material support (stipends, childcare, transport) affects participation and completion among the most marginalised women. For policy and institutional reform fellowships, research should assess whether fellows influence policy language, staffing practices, or budget priorities beyond their own careers, and which fellowship design features, selection criteria, remuneration, mentorship, and post-fellowship pathways, are most important for sustained impact. For participatory research and advocacy networks, evaluation should examine whether women-led evidence generation increases local recognition of women as credible health actors, and whether evidence produced through these networks changes facility-level practice, district planning, or national policy. Across all four approaches, comparative testing across fragile, transitional, and stable settings is essential: understanding which contextual conditions make an intervention viable, scalable, and safe is as important as demonstrating that it works under ideal circumstances.

Several methodological approaches are well suited to this agenda. Mixed-methods implementation research can help assess feasibility, acceptability, reach, and early outcomes across different settings. Realist evaluation may be particularly useful because it asks what works, for whom, in what contexts, and through which mechanisms. Comparative case studies can illuminate how similar interventions operate differently across conflict-affected and stable settings. Longitudinal cohort tracking can follow women over time to examine whether leadership programmes affect retention, promotion, authority, and influence. Where feasible, quasi-experimental designs could be used to compare outcomes between participants and matched nonparticipants.

Evaluation should also move beyond simple head counts. Useful indicators include progression into formal and informal leadership roles, participation in governance spaces, changes in self-efficacy and negotiation ability, professional network expansion, influence over planning and budgets, workplace safety, retention in the health sector, and perceived legitimacy among peers and communities. Crucially, these outcomes should be analyzed across social groups, including rural or urban location, caste or ethnicity, disability status, age, and displacement status where ethically appropriate. Without this, intersectionality remains rhetorical rather than operational.

Research should also ask whether strengthening women’s leadership affects health systems more broadly. Over time, this may include effects on workforce stability, community trust, responsiveness to marginalized groups, organizational culture, accountability, and selected service outcomes. Such relationships are unlikely to be simple or linear, but they are important. If women’s leadership is to be framed as central to health systems development, the field must build stronger evidence on how and under what conditions leadership matters.

Policy implications

Strengthening women’s leadership requires action at multiple levels. Governments should embed gender-equitable leadership pathways within workforce and governance reforms, including transparent recruitment and promotion systems, anti-harassment enforcement, parental support policies, and investments in rural and underserved pipelines. Health institutions should examine who is mentored, sponsored, protected, and promoted, and should treat leadership diversity as part of organizational performance rather than public relations. Donors and multilateral agencies should move beyond short project cycles and support locally grounded, women-led, and context-adapted initiatives, particularly in fragile settings that are often written off as too difficult. Professional associations and academic institutions should address exclusion in authorship, training, conference visibility, and board participation. Researchers should co-produce evidence with affected women and local organizations rather than treating them only as study subjects.

It is also important that investments do not focus only on exceptional women who are already near elite spaces. The more difficult but more consequential task is to build pathways for women who are structurally least likely to be seen as leaders: rural women, minority women, lower caste women, refugee and displaced women, women with disability, and women working far from capital cities and prestigious institutions. If these groups remain excluded, leadership programmes may improve representation without improving equity.

Conclusion

Women’s leadership in health systems should be treated as a core concern of health systems strengthening, not as an optional addition to a gender agenda. Where women are excluded from leadership, health systems lose knowledge, legitimacy, and responsiveness. Where diverse women are able to lead with real authority, institutions are better positioned to reflect community needs, respond to inequity, and function with resilience under pressure [6, 12, 13].

However, strengthening women’s leadership requires more than inviting women into unchanged structures. It requires confronting how power is distributed across institutions, professions, and communities. It requires recognizing that women do not face the same barriers, and that interventions designed without attention to caste, class, disability, ethnicity, geography, age, and displacement may reproduce exclusion rather than reduce it [11, 23]. It also requires moving beyond symbolic inclusion toward practical strategies that build access to skills, networks, institutional legitimacy, and decision-making authority.

The future worth aiming for is not one in which a small number of exceptional women are praised for overcoming hostile systems. It is one in which diverse women can lead without having to clear unnecessary obstacles at every stage, and in which leadership reflects the populations most affected by health policy decisions. Building such systems will require sustained investment, political will, institutional reform, and evidence that is attentive to context. But the stakes are clear. Strengthening women’s leadership is not only about representation. It is about building health systems that are fairer, more trusted, and more capable of protecting life.
