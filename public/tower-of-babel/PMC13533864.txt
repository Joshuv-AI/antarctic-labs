Cousin Syndrome Due to TBX15 Gene Variants: Three Novel Cases and Review of the Literature
Authors: Wafaa Alharbi, Abdul A. Peer‐Zada, Abeer S. Alaqidi, Corinne Collet, Capucine Coulon, Aziza Mushiba, Florence Petit
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCousin syndrome (MIM#260660) is a rare genetic disorder characterized by short stature, pelvi‐scapular dysplasia and craniofacial dysmorphism, due to biallelic pathogenic variants in the TBX15 gene. So far, only six molecularly confirmed cases have been reported in the literature. Herein, we report three novel individuals from two unrelated families carrying homozygous novel variants in the TBX15 gene causing Cousin syndrome with detailed phenotypic description. We review the literature and discuss differential diagnoses. This report confirms that Cousin syndrome is a clinically recognizable condition, with typical dysmorphic features, short stature, hypoplastic scapula and iliac wings, a high prevalence of hip dislocation and humero‐radial synostosis, transmission deafness and strabismus. More case descriptions are needed to precise the prevalence of intellectual disability in this condition, a feature that seems strikingly variable among reports.

Introduction

Cousin syndrome (MIM#260660) is a genetic disorder that causes congenital skeletal deformities in the shoulders, pelvis, and limbs, as well as short stature and a facial dysmorphism with dysplastic ears.

Jacques Cousin, a pediatrician in Lille, France, and colleagues described the disorder as “familial pelvic‐scapular dysplasia with dwarfism and dysmorphisms” in 1982 [1]. They reported two sibs with congenital dwarfism, dysmorphism, agenesis of the scapula wings and major hypoplasia of the alae of the ilium and acetabulum, resulting in hip dislocation. Dysplastic ears and cervical skin tag were illustrated, together with extended facial naevus flammeus. Moderate psychomotor retardation was also present.

In 2008, the group of Andrea Superti‐Furga showed that Cousin Syndrome is caused by biallelic inactivating variants in the TBX15 gene (MIM#604127), after the identification of two additional unrelated individuals [2]. Because the phenotype was similar to that of the Droopy ear mouse, a Tbx15‐deficient mouse model with abnormal external ears and severe bone abnormalities [3], this candidate gene was screened in these individuals, allowing the identification of homozygous variants. TBX15 maps to chromosome 1p13 in humans. It is a member of the T‐box gene family, encoding transcription factors that regulate a variety of developmental processes [4].

So far, only six cases of Cousin syndrome with molecular confirmation have been reported [2, 5, 6, 7]. Here, we describe two novel variants in the TBX15 gene causing Cousin Syndrome in three individuals from two families, broadening the phenotypic description of the syndrome.

Results

We observe highly homogeneous craniofacial and skeletal features among patients affected with Cousin syndrome, among our series and the literature (Table 1, Figure 1, and Appendix S1). Short long bones are present from birth and may be diagnosed prenatally. Growth retardation and short stature develop during early infancy, leading to dwarfism. No microcephaly is observed. Skeletal defects include a short neck with constant hypoplastic scapula and elbow mobility defects usually due to humeroradial synostosis, constant hypoplastic ilia with frequent hip dislocation. Dysmorphism is recognizable, especially the dysplastic ears, hypertelorism with narrow palpebral fissures and frontal prominence with large anterior fontanelle. “Cousin ears” are small, low‐set and posteriorly rotated, with dysplasia of conchae, ear lobe and antitragus, narrow auditory canals, sometimes associated with preauricular skin tags. Most patients harbor skin features such as a large naevus flammeus of the fronto‐orbital area, or a posterior cervical tag and redundant skin folds on the cervical or frontal area, together with a low posterior hairline. Sensorineural deficits with hearing loss, strabismus or limitation of eye movements up to ophthalmoplegia are very common. Finally, nearly half of the patients exhibit developmental delays. In some cases, gross motor delay is at least partly related to hip dysplasia, but also observed in patients who have not been immobilized, in connection with hypotonia. In addition, speech delay is common in patients with hearing loss. However, global developmental delay may also be a symptom of intellectual disability, observed in the outcome of four out of eight patients.

Altogether, seven homozygous variants have been reported in the TBX15 gene so far (Figure 2B). Among them, six are predicted to lead to the absence of the protein (Table 1) and are associated with a severe clinical phenotype. Only one missense variant has been reported, associated with a milder but still recognizable phenotype [7]. This is the only case with normal height and no sensorineural deficit.

Discussion

We report two novel TBX15 gene variants, NM_001330677.2(TBX15):c.883C>T; p.(Arg295*) and c.926 + 3A>T; p.(?), in three patients who presented with clinical features typical for Cousin syndrome, a rare and clinically recognizable autosomal recessive genetic disorder.

Only 8 cases of Cousin syndrome have been reported in the literature so far, in consanguineous individuals (Table 1) [1, 2, 5, 6, 7]. While molecular studies have not been performed in the princeps report [1], homozygous variants in TBX15 have been found in the remaining cases (Figure 2B). Cousin syndrome is characterized by recognizable skeletal defects with craniofacial deformity, scapular hypoplasia, pelvic dysplasia, limited elbow mobility, and short stature. The growth retardation is usually severe, leading to dwarfism. So far, only two adult cases are described with final heights of 115 and 118 cm in females, corresponding to −8 SD [2, 5]. Of note, the only one case harboring a homozygous missense TBX15 variant has normal height at 9 years old [7]. The severity of pelvic dysplasia and hip dislocation has considerable influence on orthopedic management and the age of walking. Patient 1, as detailed in the princeps report, experienced a particularly delayed onset of walking at the age of 6 years [1]. Speech delay is also common in cases of apparent constant hearing loss. Ear CT‐scan in case 2 revealed marked hypoplasia of the external and median ear as the cause of the hearing loss, instead of an internal ear defect. The frequency of intellectual disability in Cousin syndrome is unclear so far, as it has not been precisely documented in previous reports and the cases in this series are still young. However, interfamilial variability appears to be strikingly high, and mild phenotypes may be observed. We report moderate intellectual disability in the two siblings from the second family. Given that the coexistence of two recessive disorders is not uncommon in consanguineous individuals, we examined the genomic sequencing data from Case 2 in detail, but did not identify any other diagnoses. Intellectual disability was also reported in the two cases described by Cousin et al. [1]. Therefore, additional case reports and follow‐up of the young patients described here are needed to assess the prevalence of intellectual disability in Cousin syndrome.

With only a few cases reported to date, Cousin syndrome seems to be an ultra‐rare condition and remains relatively unknown despite being easily recognizable. However, it is possible that some sporadic patients with “pelvis‐shoulder dysplasia” (MIM#169550) or other similar diagnoses in the medical literature may in fact be affected with Cousin syndrome. Pelvis‐shoulder dysplasia likely encompasses different clinical entities characterized by hypoplasia of the scapula and pelvis but no craniocervical malformation [9]. The apparently recessive disorder reported by Elliott et al. [10] as scapulo‐iliac dysostosis may be the same disorder as that reported by Cousin et al. [1]. Also, Al‐Hassnan and Teebi, reported two sibs affected with craniofacial anomalies, humero‐radial synostosis and rhizomelic limb shortness [11], who were highly suggestive of Cousin syndrome ever since the diagnosis was not suspected and no molecular testing was performed.

Campomelic dysplasia (MIM#114290) due to monoallelic variations in and near the SOX9 gene [12], is an important differential diagnosis, since hypoplasia of the scapulae and of the iliac bones with hip dislocation may also occur. However, the facial features help to differentiate between conditions.

Finally, in both families we report, Antley‐Bixler syndrome (MIM#207410, MIM#201750) was initially considered. This rare disorder is characterized by skeletal abnormalities including craniosynostosis, joint contractures, and humeroradial synostosis [13]. Humeroradial synostosis is a quite infrequent skeletal malformation, which is specific for only a few syndromic conditions, including Antley‐Bixler and Cousin syndromes (reviewed in [14]).

Interestingly, de novo heterozygous missense variants in TBX15, affecting highly conserved residues (c.370A>C;p.(Lys124Gln) and c.1549C>A;p.(Leu517Met)), have been reported in two patients affected with unilateral defect of soft palate with intact uvula and velopharyngeal insufficiency [15]. In addition, a nonsense TBX15 variant (c.1231C>T;p.(Gln411*)) was identified in a three‐generation family with submucous cleft palate (SMCP) [16]. This phenotype, different from Cousin syndrome, is consistent with Tbx15 expression in the posterior palatal mesenchymal cells during soft palate development. Since no similar palatal phenotype is observed in patients affected with Cousin syndrome, nor in their heterozygous carrier parents, the cellular consequences of TBX15 monoallelic variants associated with SMCP may be different.

The nonsense heterozygous variant associated with SMCP p.(Gln411*) [16] lies within the last exon (exon 8), therefore likely escaping the nonsense‐mediated decay (NMD) [17, 18]. In vitro functional testing demonstrated the presence of a truncated TBX15 protein, responsible for cytoplasmic mislocalization. In Cousin syndrome, four truncating biallelic variants are reported (Figure 2B). Regarding its location in exon 6/8, which is 65 bp long, the p.(Arg295*) variant likely triggers the NMD [17, 18], therefore leading to haploinsufficiency. Both variants p.(Ile450fs) and p.(Pro451fs) lye within the last exon. In vitro functional testing indicated that cellular levels of TBX15 were abolished because of proteasome‐associated degradation, therefore leading to haploinsufficiency [2]. The p.(Arg387*) variant also lies in the last exon, but no functional testing has been performed to determine if a truncated protein is produced and study its stability.

The cellular consequences are harder to hypothesize for missense variants. The heterozygous de novo variants identified in patients with SMCP affect residues that are not conserved among TBX proteins in humans (Figure 2C). Conversely, the homozygous p.(Met237Val) variant identified in Cousin syndrome affects a highly conserved residue of the T‐Box domain (Figure 2C), suggesting DNA‐binding alteration and most likely loss‐of‐function, given the absence of phenotype in heterozygous parents and similarity with the phenotype due to haploinsufficiency. Functional tests performed for the missense variants associated with palate anomalies do not show loss of transactivation activity [15]. Together, these findings may suggest that Cousin syndrome is due to biallelic variants responsible for haploinsufficiency of loss‐of‐function, while SMCP are due to monoallelic variants with different cellular consequences. We believe that our report adds to the limited literature on Cousin syndrome and emphasizes the importance of early recognition, accurate diagnosis, and comprehensive management of this rare genetic disorder. Cases follow‐up is needed to describe the neurodevelopmental outcomes, to explore whether growth hormone therapy can enhance patient stature, and confirm the suspected genotype–phenotype correlations.
