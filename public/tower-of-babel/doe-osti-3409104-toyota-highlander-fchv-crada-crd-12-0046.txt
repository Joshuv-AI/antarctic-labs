 Summary of Research Results:

 This project relates to the loan of four Toyota Mirai FCEV-adv vehicles to NLR to provide a
 load (vehicles to fill with hydrogen) to our fueling station research facility to study hydrogen
 fueling infrastructure performance using 700 bar precooled hydrogen at ESIF’s Hydrogen
 Infrastructure Testing and Research Facility (HITRF) facility.

 Task 1: Hydrogen Fueling System Equipment Interaction

 a) Determine how the Vehicles and its high-pressure Hydrogen storage system interact
 with NLR's non-communicative fueling at the wind site. In particular, important
 validation of SAE Standard J2601 regarding vehicle tank temperature and fill rate
 correlations will occur. The daily activities of the Vehicles and its fueling needs will
 represent anticipated real-world applications. NLR researchers will collect and report
 data from the Vehicles and the fueling dispenser and compare the two for accuracy.

 i) These data gathered by NLR will be used to create information products on reliability
 and performance of the refueling infrastructure (tanks, sensors, safety devices,
 compressors, dispenser, generators). The infom1ational products will be fed back to
 DOE and equipment suppliers to help address infrastructure reliability, which is the
 leading economic and technical challenge facing the widespread adoption of fuel cell
 vehicle in the US.

 Task 1 Results:

 The Toyota vehicles were filled throughout during their use at NLR at the HITRF. Fueling the
 vehicles enabled technology innovation to reach and validate station protocols for Societ yof
 Automotive Engineers (SAE) J2601. The tank capacity of the Toyota Mirai is approximately 122
 liters. The maximum weight of hydrogen that can be stored in this fuel tank is about 5 kilograms,
 translating to a total storage volume of around 122.4 liters (32.3 gallons). During the vehicle’s
 use at NLR, four fills were recorded from NLR’s dispenser, specific to the Mirai. The dispenser
 only recorded the largest fill for the day. The average filled mass of hydrogen was 3.135 kg.
 These fills allowed the NLR team to evaluate the efficiency and range capabilities, which were
 linked to miles traveled and mass of hydrogen dispensed.

 These vehicles provided NLR the opportunity to enhance its research, development, and testing
 capabilities related to renewable hydrogen production, fueling infrastructure, and vehicle
 performance and durability.

 Task 2: Integrated Renewable Energy Station (IRES)

 a) NLR will use the wind and the vehicle fleet to validate the concept of hydrogen-based
 Integrated Renewable Energy Station, capable of servicing many different needs
 beyond vehicle fueling. These include providing short term (seconds) ancillary support
 services (frequency and voltage regulation) to electric grid operators and longer term (hours)
 firming of wind and PY-based renewables by using hydrogen as an energy storage medium.
 Finning renewables with hydrogen will help alleviate stranded wind, provide power when
 renewable resources are not plentiful, and the need for spinning reserves running on fossil
 fuels. No modifications will be made to the Vehicle's configuration or software by NLR.

 Task 2 Results:

 The hydrogen-based integrated Renewable Energy Station, capable of servicing many different
 needs beyond vehicle fueling was not validated under this project. During the project
 performance period, it was determined that there was no standard methodology or process to
 make this evaluation and that doing so would require work to be performed outside of the agreed
 upon statement of work.

 Task 3: Commuter Influence on Vehicle Operations

 a) Determine the effects of the environment and driving patterns on Vehicle’s energy
 storage system and propulsion system. HTSC engineers will record and analyze
 operational data of the Vehicle while it is being driven on variable road conditions and in
 variable weather conditions (temperature, humidity, wind, etc). Drive cycles will include use
 of the Vehicle on public roads, both to and from various locations, and returning to NLR or
 Test Facilities each evening.

 i) These patterns are chosen with the intent to analyze the effects of these anticipated real-
 world factors on the Vehicle's electric drive system. Testing the Vehicle between NLR
 and other destinations along the Colorado Front Range will significantly improve the
 efficiency, economy, and robustness of these studies by providing applicable data while
 eliminating the need to obtain and reproduce the same driving conditions in a laboratory
 environment with limited time and financial resources.

 Task 3 Results:

 Each vehicle was assigned to a group within NLR for use and made available to NLR staff upon
 completion of driver training and agreement to the terms and conditions of use. The vehicles
 were assigned to the Security, Sustainability, Wind and Transportation groups to provide a broad
 base of drivers on campus. Over 100 people were trained to drive the vehicles and used them
 over the past two years. The drivers included NLR staff from executive management, innovation
 partnering and outreach, laboratory operations, energy analysis and decision support, materials
 and chemical sciences, mechanical and thermal systems engineering, energy systems integration,
 bioenergy science and technology, and technology program development and management. They
 used the vehicles for activities such as commuting to meetings, hosting VIP visitors, showcasing
 the technology and renewable fuel, and participating in public and educational events. Many of
 these activities were reported to Toyota on a quarterly basis and when high profile events or
 visitors were scheduled that involve the vehicles.

 User feedback for the Toyota Mirai was excellent. One NLR driver expressed “The driving
 experience with the Mirai was the smoothest and quietest driving experience I’ve ever had”.
 Other drivers characterized their driving experience as follows:

 • Very good power, the vehicle would start up and move smoothly and quickly
 • Good range under winter conditions
 • Tank refueling took only 5 minutes
 • Heated seats were a bonus
 • Great internal temperature control

 Although originally planned under this project, a comprehensive analysis of the effects of the
 environment and driving patterns on each vehicle’s energy storage system and propulsion system
 was not completed.

 Task 4: Driver Behavior

 a) Demonstrate driver behavior and associated Vehicle use as it relates to the constraints
 of a hydrogen fuel cell electric system. Colorado currently has limited hydrogen fueling
 availability; NLR has the only hydrogen fueler in the region. Range and driver behavioral
 changes are of interest to the DOE program, HTSC, and the industry, and will be studied by
 NLR in the context of NLR's limited fueling availability. A number of NLR employees will
 sign-out and drive the Vehicle during normal business hours for research and analysis.

 4

 Task 4 Results:

 Over the multi‑year loan period from Toyota, the vehicles were driven on daily business trips
 and NLR security routes, allowing researchers to observe how driving habits, route selection,
 passenger and cargo load, and environmental factors such as high‑altitude operation affected fuel
 economy, range, and overall performance. Data were collected to document the driver
 experience and how drivers interacted with the hydrogen fuel cell fueling infrastructure under
 real‑world conditions. NLR evaluated driver behavior within the context of limited regional
 hydrogen fueling availability and the integration of renewable hydrogen production.

 Many of the participating drivers were unfamiliar with hydrogen and fuel cells until the vehicles
 were on campus, providing an opportunity for education and hands-on experience with hydrogen
 technology. The fueling station was about 20 miles away from Denver, and there was a 50%
 reduction in range due to the use of 350 bar fueling. This provided an opportunity to assess the
 impact of limited fueling capability on vehicle use. The drivers reported having range anxiety
 with increasing distance from the only hydrogen fueling station; however, the participating
 drivers had good experiences with the vehicles and enjoyed using them during business activities
 to demonstrate renewable energy in action.

 Task 5 Results:

 Each vehicle was assigned to a group within NLR for use and made available to NLR staff upon
 completion of driver training and agreement to the terms and conditions of use. The vehicles
 were assigned to the Security, Sustainability, Wind and Transportation groups to provide a broad
 base of drivers on campus. Over 100 people were trained to drive the vehicles and used them
 over the past two years. The drivers included NLR staff located in executive management,
 innovation partnering and outreach, laboratory operations, energy analysis and decision support,
 materials and chemical sciences, mechanical and thermal systems engineering, energy systems
 integration, bioenergy science and technology, and technology program development and
 management.

 The vehicles were used for activities such as commuting to meetings, hosting VIP visitors,
 showcasing the technology and renewable fuel, and participating in public and educational
 events. Many of these activities were reported to Toyota on a quarterly basis and when high
 profile events or visitors were scheduled that involve the vehicles. The vehicles were also used
 for educating and raising awareness of hydrogen technologies by hosting state government
 officials, local elected officials, industry representatives, media representatives, NLR employees
 and individuals whose support could assist NLR in carrying out its mission. The travel area
 included the Denver Metro area, specifically the Denver West Office Park and South Table
 Mountain site in Golden, and the National Wind Technology Center near Boulder. Additional
 travel included trips to the Denver International Airport as well as the Lab’s ReFUEL Facility in
 downtown Denver.

 High altitude conditions were tested by completing trips to various locations such as Estes Park
 (7,522 ft.). The drives were conducted during summer months without incident or noticeable
 power loss while driving on an incline at higher altitudes. The vehicles were used in and around
 the Denver, Colorado area regularly and performed well.

 The vehicles were a great educational and outreach tool. These vehicles enabled our staff to
 understand that these vehicles are ready for commercial use and to share this information with
 others. Representatives from the Denver Sustainability Office came to visit and rode in the fuel
 cell vehicles to the wind site for a tour of the facility. The Denver Post requested a ride in one of
 the vehicles along with information about the technology. The interest and excitement generated
 by these vehicles created many new opportunities to spread the word about hydrogen technology.
