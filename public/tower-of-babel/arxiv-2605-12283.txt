Ordering governs magnetic tunability in FePt-based Janus particles independent of curvature
Natalia Gonzalez-Vazquez, Eylül Suadiye, Eberhard Goering, Ruben O. Miranda-Rosales, Hilda David, Frank Thiele, Julia Unangst, Andrew K. Schulz, Gunther Richter

Abstract
Magnetic Janus particles enable remote actuation across biomedical, microfluidic, and materials applications. While curvature-driven magnetic effects are well established at the nanoscale, their influence on magnetization reversal at micrometer-scale particle sizes remains unresolved. Here, we combine experiment and micromagnetic simulation to investigate curvature-dependent magnetism (i.e., diameter-dependent changes in magnetic response) in FePt-coated Janus particles across diameters of 3–10 μ \mu m, and extended to 1–20 μ \mu m via micromagnetic simulations. Structural and crystallographic characterization confirms continuous coatings with near-equiatomic composition and partial L ​ 1 0 L1_{0} ordering. Magnetometry measurements reveal nearly invariant hysteresis behavior as a function of particle diameter ( μ 0 ​ H c = 1.13 ± 0.05 \mu_{0}H_{c}=1.13\pm 0.05 T, pooled n = 8), with no statistically significant dependence of coercivity or remanence on diameter (statistical analysis, p = 0.61 p=0.61 for H c H_{c} , p = 0.85 p=0.85 for M r / M s M_{r}/M_{s} ). To interpret these results, we introduce FunMaP , an open-source micromagnetic simulation framework enabling direct comparison between experiment and idealized FePt caps. Simulations show that curvature does not significantly influence magnetization reversal in this regime, consistent with a locally planar limit (exchange length ℓ ex ≪ R \ell_{\mathrm{ex}}\ll R ). In contrast, variations in chemical ordering produce substantial changes in hysteresis shape and coercivity. These results show that, at micrometer scales, magnetic behavior in FePt Janus particles is governed primarily by material ordering rather than curvature. This work defines a quantitative regime boundary for curvature-dependent magnetism and provides design guidelines for programmable magnetic micro-systems.

Iron–platinum (FePt) based coatings are especially promising in this context due to their high magnetocrystalline anisotropy (

In curved magnetic shells, curvature is expected to introduce effective anisotropies and curvature-induced exchange terms, which can modify domain wall structure, switching fields, and chirality

In parallel, the field of curvilinear magnetism has demonstrated that curvature can directly reprogram magnetic behavior in nanoscale systems

Results and Discussion

We establish a reproducible workflow for the synthesis of FePt-coated Janus particles, followed by structural and magnetic validation using SEM, XRD, and SQUID magnetometry. As summarized in Figure
1

This integrated approach allows a direct comparison between ensemble-averaged experimental behavior and idealized single-particle simulations, enabling intrinsic curvature effects to be separated from processing-induced structural variations

Throughout this work, “curvature effects” refer specifically to diameter-dependent changes in magnetic response, distinguishing them from curvature-imposed anisotropy configurations that are invariant across diameter.

2.1
Workflow for FePt Janus caps across curvatures

To probe curvature-dependent behavior, monodisperse SiO
2

All samples consist of FePt caps with a nominal thickness of 60 nm, ensuring that thickness remains constant while curvature (via particle diameter) is varied.

SEM imaging confirms that this protocol produces uniform particle monolayers with predominantly hexagonal packing across all diameters (Figure

These competing mechanisms define a narrow processing window in which continuous FePt caps can be obtained while enabling partial chemical ordering. 2.3
Morphological evolution: curvature influences processing, not magnetism

SEM analysis after annealing (Figure
4

Coupled role of ordering and morphology

Even at the highest simulated disorder level (20% A1,
\mu_{0}H_{c}=3.81\pm 0.10

Dewetting kinetics follow
\tau_{n}\propto h^{4}/D_{s}

the surface diffusion coefficient. The fourth-power dependence on thickness implies that the rim region (where

Consistent with this, SEM analysis localizes morphological changes (roughening, grain-boundary grooving, and partial edge retraction) at the cap perimeter while the central region remains comparatively continuous. These morphological features can influence magnetic behavior through two mechanisms. First,
exchange decoupling
: grain-boundary grooving may reduce effective exchange coupling when characteristic feature sizes approach or exceed the exchange length, rather than as a direct consequence of structural discontinuity. This distinction is important, as structural fragmentation alone does not necessarily alter hysteresis behavior if exchange connectivity is preserved

localized soft regions
: the rim thickness gradient creates regions where the cap is sufficiently thin for finite-size effects (e.g., reduced anisotropy due to surface contributions) to lower

, generating additional nucleation sites for reversal.

Within this framework, curvature does not act as a direct tuning knob. Rather, it influences magnetic behavior indirectly by setting the thickness distribution
t(\theta)=t_{0}\cos\theta

R\gg\ell_{\mathrm{ex}}
) with fixed film thickness (

3
Conclusion

We present a combined experimental and micromagnetic investigation of FePt Janus caps across micrometer-scale curvatures (3–10
\mu
m), integrating synthesis, structural characterization, SQUID magnetometry, and simulation within a unified framework (Figure
1
). This approach enables a direct separation of intrinsic micromagnetic behavior from processing-induced structural effects. Contrary to expectations derived from nanoscale curvilinear magnetism, we find that curvature does not constitute an independent tuning parameter for magnetization reversal in this regime. Both SQUID measurements (Figure
3
, Table
1
) and micromagnetic simulations (Figure
5
) show that coercivity, remanence, and switching behavior remain effectively invariant with particle diameter. This invariance is consistent with the locally planar limit (
\ell_{\mathrm{ex}}/R\ll 1
), where curvature-induced contributions to the micromagnetic energy are suppressed.

Instead, the magnetic response is governed by material properties, specifically chemical ordering (which sets the magnetocrystalline anisotropy) and microstructural heterogeneity (which modifies exchange coupling and local anisotropy). Simulations demonstrate a continuous and order-of-magnitude dependence of coercivity on A1 phase fraction (Figure
6
, Table
1
), establishing that even small deviations from full
L1_{0}
order strongly modify hysteresis behavior. The experimental response is consistent with a partially ordered system, confirming that phase composition, rather than curvature, governs the intrinsic reversal mechanism. The remaining discrepancy between idealized simulations and experimental measurements is explained by diffusion-driven morphological evolution. SEM analysis (Figure
4
) reveals thickness gradients and grain-boundary grooving consistent with early-stage solid-state dewetting. These features introduce spatial heterogeneity in anisotropy and exchange coupling, leading to reduced coercivity and broadened switching. Within this framework, curvature influences magnetic behavior indirectly by modifying the thickness distribution and therefore the local diffusion kinetics and ordering pathways during annealing, rather than through direct modification of the micromagnetic energy landscape.

Taken together, these results establish a clear hierarchy of control parameters for FePt Janus caps at micrometer length scales: (i) chemical ordering defines the intrinsic magnetic response, (ii) morphology modulates switching through structural heterogeneity, and (iii) geometry configures the easy-axis distribution (radial vs. uniaxial), which fundamentally shapes the hysteresis loop but does not introduce a diameter-dependent tuning parameter in this regime. This defines a curvature-insensitive but processing-sensitive regime for magnetic Janus systems. The results provide practical design guidelines for magnetic microrobots and functional colloids, shifting the focus from geometric engineering toward precise control of phase transformation, diffusion kinetics, and thin-film morphology. More broadly, this work establishes a quantitative boundary for curvature-dependent magnetism, demonstrating that geometric effects become negligible when the radius of curvature greatly exceeds intrinsic magnetic length scales (
\ell_{\mathrm{ex}}/R\ll 1
).

4
Methods

4.1
Particle Monolayer Preparation and FePt Deposition

Monodisperse SiO
2
particles (3–10
\mu
m diameter, microParticles GmbH) were assembled into near-monolayers on Si substrates (
5\times 5
mm
2
) via drop-casting of diluted aqueous suspensions. Dilution factors were determined from geometric packing considerations to achieve close-packed coverage (Supporting Information). Substrates were cleaned by sequential sonication in deionized water and ethanol/acetone (1:1), followed by oxygen plasma treatment (30 W, 10 min) to enhance wettability. A 25
\mu
L droplet was deposited immediately after plasma activation and dried under ambient conditions for at least 12 h.

FePt thin films were deposited by molecular beam epitaxy via co-evaporation of Fe and Pt under high vacuum (
<1\times 10^{-8}
mbar). Fluxes were adjusted to obtain near-equiatomic composition (50:50
\pm
5 at.%), and a nominal thickness of 60 nm was monitored in situ using a quartz crystal microbalance. Continuous substrate rotation ensured uniform azimuthal coverage.

4.2
Thermal Processing and Structural Characterization

Post-deposition annealing was performed at
500^{\circ}
C for 1 h under argon atmosphere to promote partial A1
\rightarrow
L1
0
phase transformation while limiting excessive solid-state dewetting of the FePt caps. Preliminary experiments indicated that FePt films on curved/spherical substrates exhibited the onset of diffusion-driven morphological evolution at lower temperatures than comparable planar films, particularly near the cap perimeter where the local film thickness decreases. These conditions therefore represent a compromise between chemical ordering and preservation of cap continuity during annealing. Structural and morphological characterization was carried out using scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Zeiss GeminiSEM500, 2–8 kV) in both top-view and tilted configurations to assess cap continuity and curvature-dependent morphology. Elemental composition was verified by energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX, 25 kV, area-averaged mapping), confirming near-equiatomic FePt. Phase identification was performed by X-ray diffraction (XRD) using Cu K
\alpha
radiation with cross-beam optics.

4.3
Magnetic Characterization

Magnetic hysteresis loops were measured using a Quantum Design MPMS3 SQUID magnetometer in vibrating sample mode at 300 K with applied fields up to
\pm
7 T. Diamagnetic background contributions from the substrate were subtracted using linear fits to the high-field regions (Supporting Information), and magnetization was normalized to the saturation value. The coercive field (
H_{c}
), remanence ratio (
M_{r}/M_{s}
), and hysteresis loss (
W_{\mathrm{hyst}}
) were extracted from the corrected loops.

4.4
Micromagnetic Simulations

Micromagnetic simulations were performed using OOMMF via the Ubermag Python interface. FePt caps were modeled as hemispherical shells with inner radius
R=d/2
and thickness
t=60
nm, using material parameters representative of L1
0
FePt (
M_{s}=1.0\times 10^{6}
A/m,
A=1.0\times 10^{-11}
J/m,
K_{u}=6.6\times 10^{6}
J/m
3
). Unless otherwise stated, the easy axis was aligned along the local surface normal to represent a radial anisotropy configuration. Hysteresis loops were computed via quasi-static energy minimization by sweeping the applied field between
\pm
18 T with a small transverse bias field to avoid metastable states. Additional numerical details and geometry implementation are provided in the Supporting Information.
