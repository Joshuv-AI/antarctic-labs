Enhancer-mediated DDIT4 activation by SMYD2-dependent H3K4me1 promotes pazopanib resistance in clear cell renal cell carcinoma
Authors: Yun Sun, Mengyao Wang, Haoran Song, Yi Wu, Yingxia He, Fumin Ma, Yuxuan Liang, Lishuo Zhang, Jianan Gong, Minghua Ren, Zhaohua Zhong, Wenran Zhao, Beichen Ding
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe progression and resistance to targeted therapy, including pazopanib, frequently lead to poor prognosis in clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC) patients. However, the underlying molecular mechanisms of these processes remain unclear.MethodsIn this study, we first performed RNA-seq to identify genes that were differentially expressed in both SMYD2-knockdown and pazopanib-resistant cells, indicating their potential role in SMYD2-mediated drug resistance. We analyzed TCGA-KIRC data and 150 patient samples to identify the relationship between SMYD2 and DDIT4 expression levels, as well as the prognostic significance of DDIT4. In vitro functional assays and murine models were applied to evaluate the effects of SMYD2 and DDIT4 on tumor growth and on pazopanib resistance. CUT&Tag and chromosome conformation capture (4 C) assays were applied to identify enhancers associated with SMYD2-mediated regulation of DDIT4, while the JASPAR database was utilized to predict transcription factors involved in the enhancer regulation. CRISPR-mediated enhancer deletion and ChIP–qPCR were subsequently performed to validate the regulatory roles of the identified enhancer and the transcription factor SPI1 in DDIT4 expression.ResultsOur study revealed that the expression level of DDIT4 is positively correlated with SMYD2. DDIT4 is highly expressed in renal cell carcinoma and is associated with poorer survival outcomes. Further research revealed that SMYD2 regulates H3K4me1 in a DDIT4 distal enhancer (chr10:72830412–72830891), promoting the recruitment of the transcription factor SPI1, thereby activating DDIT4 expression. We found that DDIT4 promotes the proliferation, metastasis, and pazopanib resistance of ccRCC, and DDIT4 knockdown enhances drug sensitivity in both in vitro and in vivo experiments. Furthermore, the SMYD2–DDIT4 axis activates the downstream STAT3 signaling pathway, thereby promoting tumor progression. In addition, DDIT4-related prognostic features showed potential associations with patient survival and predicted drug sensitivity in computational analyses.ConclusionsOur study identifies a previously unrecognized SMYD2–enhancer–DDIT4 regulatory axis, which promotes tumor progression and pazopanib resistance in ccRCC. These findings may provide potential therapeutic implications to overcome pazopanib resistance and improve treatment outcomes in ccRCC by targeting the SMYD2–enhancer–DDIT4 axis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12967-026-08714-w.Keywords: ccRCC, SMYD2, DDIT4, Enhancer regulation, STAT3 signaling, Pazopanib resistance

Introduction

Renal cell carcinoma (RCC) is a prevalent malignant tumor in the urinary system, accounting for approximately 3%-5% of adult malignancies [1]. Clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC) is the most common pathological subtype of renal carcinoma, comprising about 75–80% of all cases [2]. Although targeted therapies, including tyrosine kinase inhibitors such as pazopanib, a first-line treatment for advanced and metastatic ccRCC, have improved clinical outcomes in ccRCC, acquired resistance inevitably develops, and the underlying epigenetic mechanisms remain poorly defined [3].

SMYD2 is a member of the SET and MYND domain (SMYD) family of lysine methyltransferases that plays an important role in epigenetic regulation and transcriptional control [4]. SMYD2 has been reported to catalyze H3K4 methylation, including H3K4me1 and H3K4me3 [5]. H3K4me3 is widely recognized as a marker of promoter activation, whereas H3K4me1 is associated with enhancer activity. Enhancers are key regulatory elements that control gene transcription, and the co‑enrichment of H3K4me1 and H3K27ac defines transcriptionally active enhancer regions [6, 7]. Accumulating evidence has demonstrated that SMYD2 is frequently upregulated in various malignancies, including lung, breast, and colorectal cancers, where it functions as an oncogenic regulator [8–10]. Previous studies have shown that SMYD2 contributes to enzalutamide resistance in castration-resistant prostate cancer (CRPC) [11]. Notably, our previous study demonstrated that SMYD2 overexpression promotes tumor progression and resistance to chemotherapy and targeted therapy in ccRCC [12]. While SMYD2-mediated H3K4me3 at promoters is known to activate transcription, it remains poorly understood whether SMYD2 could drive resistance by depositing H3K4me1 at enhancers [13].

DNA damage-inducible transcript 4 (DDIT4), also known as REDD1, is a stress-responsive protein that plays an important role in cellular homeostasis [14]. DDIT4 has been implicated in tumor progression by promoting cell proliferation, migration and inhibiting apoptosis [15, 16]. Notably, accumulating evidence suggests that DDIT4 contributes to therapeutic resistance in various cancers, such as brain, lung, and gastric cancers [17–19]. In addition, elevated DDIT4 expression has been associated with poor prognosis in multiple malignancies [20, 21]. But the upstream regulators that control DDIT4 transcription—especially through epigenetic mechanisms in ccRCC—have not been fully defined. We therefore hypothesized that SMYD2 might activate DDIT4 via enhancer-associated H3K4me1 to drive ccRCC progression and pazopanib resistance, filling the dual gap of “how SMYD2 confers resistance” and “how DDIT4 is epigenetically activated” in this disease.

Here, we identified a SMYD2–enhancer–DDIT4 regulatory axis that promotes SPI1 recruitment and transcriptional activation, driving ccRCC progression and pazopanib resistance. This axis further activates downstream STAT3 signaling. These findings reveal a novel pathogenic mechanism and highlight potential therapeutic targets for ccRCC.

Methods

Cell culture

All cell lines used in this study were obtained from the China Center for Type Culture Collection (China) and cultured following the recommended protocols. Normal renal epithelial cells (HEK-293T) were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 2 mmol/L L-glutamine. The renal carcinoma cell lines 786-O and OSRC-2 were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute-1640 (RPMI-1640) medium, also supplemented with 10% FBS and 2 mmol/L L-glutamine. All cell lines were grown and maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2.

Antibodies and reagents

Antibodies against the following proteins were used: SMYD2 (ab195365), DDIT4 (ab191871), SPI1 (ab302623) and Goat anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L) Cross-Adsorbed Secondary Antibody, Alexa Fluor™ 488 (A-11008) from Abcam (Cambridge, UK); Mono-Methyl-Histone H3 (Lys4) (#5236), Acetyl-Histone H3 (Lys27) (#8173) and Phospho-mTOR (#5536S) from Cell Signaling Technology (Beverly, MA, USA); Phospho-STAT3 (CY 6566) from Abways (Shanghai, China); AKT (10176-2-AP), Phospho-AKT (28731-1-AP), Phospho-MAPK (28733-1-AP), Phospho-p53(28961-1-AP), GAPDH (60004-1-lg), STAT3 (10253-2-AP), BAX (50599-2-Ig), Bcl-2 (60178-1-Ig), c-MYC (10828-1-AP), Cyclin D1 (60186-1-Ig), mTOR (20657-1-AP), Histone H3 (17168-1-AP) and IgG control antibody (30000-0-AP) from Proteintech Group (Wuhan, Hubei, China). The following reagents were used in this research: pazopanib (HY-10208), stattic (10 mM in DMSO, HY-13818) and AZ505 (HY-15226) from MedChemExpress (Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA). Pazopanib, stattic, and AZ505 were dissolved in DMSO to prepare a 10 mM stock solution, which was aliquoted and stored at -20 °C until use.

Cell viability and proliferation assay

The viability of the ccRCC cells was detected using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Dojindo), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, the cells were seeded in a 96-well plate. After treatment, 10 µl of CCK-8 solution was added to each well, and the cells were incubated for 1.5 h at 37 °C. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader and normalized to the control. Cell proliferation was evaluated using the Cell-Light EdU Apollo 568 in Vitro Kit (RiboBio, #C10310-1), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Cell viability and proliferation data were derived from three independent experiments.

Colony formation assay

Transfected cells were seeded in 6-well plates (1,000 cells/well) or 60 mm dishes (100 cells/dish) for colony formation. After 10 days of incubation, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with crystal violet. Colonies were then photographed and counted. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

786-O pazopanib-resistant cell line

786-O cells were continuously exposed to pazopanib via a concentration escalation method, starting with an initial concentration of 2 µM. After two days of culture, the medium was replaced with fresh medium, and cells were either subcultured or the medium was replaced, depending on their status. The concentration was gradually increased by 0.5 µM each time until a final concentration of 20 µM was reached. Cells were maintained at 20 µM pazopanib for about 4 weeks before subsequent experiments. To assess the stability of the resistant phenotype, 786-O PR cells were cultured in pazopanib-free medium for 7 days and then subjected to pazopanib sensitivity testing using the CCK-8 assay (Fig. S4B).

Data acquisition

DDIT4 mRNA levels was analyzed based on TCGA (KIRC dataset, http://www.tcga-data.nci.nih.gov). Survival analysis of DDIT4 in the TCGA-KIRC dataset was performed using the GEPIA database (http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn/). ChIP-seq of H3K27ac was analyzed based on the NCBI GEO databases (GSM7509632).

Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) and chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays

Total cellular RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), which was then reverse transcribed to create cDNA using the Takara reagent kit (Takara Biotechnology, Shiga, Japan), followed by qPCR amplification using SYBR Green master mix (Takara Biotechnology, Shiga, Japan). Relative gene expression was normalized against GAPDH.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) was performed via the BeyoChIP™ Enzymatic ChIP Assay Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, P2083S) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The DNA fragments were extracted and analyzed by RT-qPCR. The primer sequences are shown in Table S1.

4 C-seq analysis

4 C-seq libraries were sequenced on an Illumina PE150 platform. DpnII/BfaI were used as the first and second restriction enzymes. Raw reads were filtered using fastp v0.23.2 and analyzed with pipe4C v1.1.6. Viewpoint-specific reads were trimmed, aligned to the human reference genome using Bowtie2 v2.4.5, assigned to uniquely mappable restriction fragments, normalized, and smoothed to generate 4 C-seq coverage profiles. Near-cis interaction peaks were called using peakC v0.2 and annotated with ChIPseeker v1.32.0.

Cleavage under targets and tagmentation (CUT&Tag)

CUT&Tag assays were performed mainly according to the protocol previously described by Kaya-Okur et al. (Nat Commun, 2019) [22]. CUT&Tag libraries were sequenced on an Illumina PE150 platform. Raw reads were trimmed using Trimmomatic, and clean reads were quality-checked using FastQC. Reads were aligned to the human GRCh38.p13 reference genome using Bowtie2. Low-quality alignments (MAPQ < 30), PCR duplicates, and organelle-mapped reads were removed. Peaks were called using MACS2 with a q-value cutoff of 0.05 and annotated to genomic features and nearest TSS-associated genes. Differential CUT&Tag peaks between groups were identified using DESeq2 with thresholds of |log2FoldChange| ≥ 1 and p-value ≤ 0.05.

Tissue microarrays (TMA)

Human RCC tissue microarray (TMA, HKidE180Su0) was obtained from Outdo Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The array included 30 cases of paired adjacent renal tissues and 120 cases of primary RCC tissues. The use of these specimens was approved by the Ethics Committee of Outdo Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) (approval number: SHYJS-CP-1510001), and informed consent was obtained from all patients. Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining was performed using anti-SMYD2 and anti-DDIT4 antibodies according to the manufacturers’ protocols.

siRNA, plasmids and lentivirus

SiRNAs were obtained from WZ Biosciences (Wuhan, China). We transfected siRNAs into cells with Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen). HEK293T cells were transfected with a lentiviral plasmid plus psPAX2 packaging plasmid and pMD2. G envelope plasmid. The cells were harvested after lentiviral particle infection for 5 days and analyzed by western blot or RT-qPCR to examine the efficiency of knockdown, knockout or overexpression. The sequences of the siRNAs, shRNAs and sgRNAs are shown in Table S2.

Animal studies

All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines and were approved by the Animal Care and Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Harbin Medical University (IACUC approval number: 2023141).

For the establishment of the subcutaneous xenograft model, 786-O cells were suspended with PBS and injected subcutaneously into the 5-week-old BALB/c nude mice. The tumor size was measured every 3 days starting 10 days after injection and the tumor volume was calculated as V = (width2 × length)/2. For this evaluation, the tumors were removed, weighed and photographed. The tumor tissues were fixed in formalin for immunohistochemical detection.

To examine renal cancer cell metastasis, 786-O pazopanib-resistant cells, stably expressing luciferase, were injected into the tail vein of BALB/c nude mice. D-luciferin (75 mg/kg) (122799, PerkinElmer) was intraperitoneally administered, and an IVIS Imaging System (IVIS 200, PerkinElmer) was used to acquire images of mice 15 min after injection. 28 days later, the mice were sacrificed, and the lungs were harvested and analyzed for metastatic nodules.

Random forest analysis

Random Forest analysis was performed using the randomForest package in R to rank the candidate genes obtained from the overlap between SMYD2 knockdown-downregulated genes and pazopanib resistance-upregulated genes. For model construction, samples were divided into High and Low groups based on both SMYD2 activity and pazopanib resistance status. shNC and pazopanib-resistant 786-O PR samples were assigned to the High group, whereas shSMYD2 and parental 786-O samples were assigned to the Low group. The model was constructed using the randomForest package in R with 1,000 trees (ntree = 1000), the default mtry parameter, and a fixed random seed of 123. Gene importance was assessed using the Mean Decrease Gini index.

Construction of the DDIT4-related prognostic signature

The DDIT4-related prognostic signature was constructed based on TCGA-KIRC transcriptome and clinical data. Candidate genes were screened using univariate Cox regression analysis and further analyzed using LASSO Cox regression with the glmnet R package [23]. A risk score was calculated for each patient according to the expression levels of selected genes and their corresponding coefficients. Patients were divided into high- and low-risk groups using the median risk score. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis and time-dependent ROC curve analysis were used to evaluate the predictive performance of the model.

Drug sensitivity prediction

Drug sensitivity prediction analysis was performed using the GDSC database and the oncoPredict R package, and the predicted IC50 values were compared between the high-risk and low-risk groups.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed via GraphPad Prism software (v8.0, https://www.graphpad.com/scientific-software/prism/). Comparisons of DDIT4 expression levels between ccRCC tumor tissues and the paired peritumor tissues were performed using a paired Student’s t-test. Significant associations between DDIT4 expression and clinicopathological parameters were assessed using a χ2 test. Survival curves were plotted using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared with the log-rank test. Cox regression analysis was performed to assess the significance of the variables for survival. Comparisons between two groups were performed via an unpaired Student’s t test. Differences among the groups were determined by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). The data were presented as the means ± standard deviations (SDs), and P < 0.05 was considered to reflect a statistically significant difference.

Results

SMYD2 transcriptionally upregulates DDIT4 in ccRCC

SMYD2 has been reported to be frequently overexpressed in ccRCC and promote tumor progression, which is consistent with our observations (Fig. S1A-H). Moreover, our previous study showed that SMYD2 contributes to chemotherapy and targeted therapy resistance in renal cancer. To identify SMYD2-regulated genes that may contribute to pazopanib resistance in ccRCC, we established a pazopanib-resistant 786-O cell line and integrated RNA-seq analysis of 786-O cells with SMYD2 knockdown and pazopanib resistance. RNA-seq analysis of 786-O-control/shSMYD2 revealed 2812 differentially expressed genes (DEGs), including 902 upregulated and 1910 downregulated genes upon SMYD2 knockdown, indicating that reduction in SMYD2 levels led to significant transcriptome alterations (Fig. S2A-B). In comparison, the 786-O pazopanib-resistant cell line showed 867 upregulated genes and 531 downregulated genes relative to the control group (Fig. S2C-E). Next, we analyzed all DEGs with P < 0.05, generated a volcano plot, and labeled the top 30 (Fig. 1A) and top 20 (Fig. 1B) significant genes, respectively.

To identify candidate mediators that are positively regulated by SMYD2 and associated with pazopanib resistance, we intersected genes downregulated by SMYD2 knockdown with genes upregulated in pazopanib-resistant ccRCC cells, resulting in 379 overlapping candidate genes (Fig. 1C). We reasoned that this gene set may be enriched for SMYD2-dependent, resistance-associated transcriptional effectors.We performed random forest analysis on the 379 overlapping candidate genes to prioritize key feature genes associated with SMYD2-dependent pazopanib resistance transcriptional processes. The results revealed that DDIT4 ranked within the top 10% of the genes prioritized by the Random Forest analysis (Fig. 1D). Further analysis of the correlation between candidate genes and SMYD2 expression using the TCGA-KIRC cohort revealed that DDIT4 was one of the genes most significantly positively correlated with SMYD2 expression (Fig. 1E), suggesting that DDIT4 may serve as a candidate downstream effector linking SMYD2 to ccRCC progression and pazopanib resistance.

We next validated these findings experimentally, showing that SMYD2 knockdown significantly reduced DDIT4 expression in ccRCC cell lines, as confirmed by RT–qPCR and western blot (Fig. 1F-G).

Our findings indicate that DDIT4 is a SMYD2-regulated gene and may contribute to SMYD2-associated pazopanib resistance in ccRCC.

DDIT4 is highly expressed and is associated with poor prognosis and aggressive clinicopathological characteristics in ccRCC

We first analyzed DDIT4 expression and its prognostic features using the data from TCGA-KIRC cohort. The results showed that DDIT4 was significantly upregulated in 526 ccRCC tumor samples compared to 72 normal samples (Fig. 2A). Kaplan–Meier survival analysis indicated that higher DDIT4 expression levels were significantly associated with poorer disease-specific survival (DSS) and progression-free survival (PFS) in ccRCC patients (Fig. 2B-C). This upregulation of DDIT4 was further confirmed by RT–PCR and western blot analyses in two distinct ccRCC cell lines (Fig. 2D-E).

We further assessed the significance of DDIT4 expression in 150 ccRCC clinical tumor specimens. IHC analysis of tissue microarrays (TMAs) demonstrated significantly higher DDIT4 expression in tumor tissues compared to paired peritumor tissues (Fig. 2F-G). Kaplan–Meier survival analysis revealed that elevated DDIT4 expression was significantly associated with poorer overall survival (OS) in ccRCC patients (Fig. 2H).

Further analysis showed that high DDIT4 expression was significantly correlated with age (P = 0.014) and higher tumor Fuhrman grade (P < 0.001), but not with AJCC stage or gender (Table 1). Therefore, the independent prognostic value of DDIT4 remains to be validated in future studies.

DDIT4 promotes ccRCC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion

To investigate the biological function of DDIT4 in ccRCC, we established stable DDIT4 knockdown cell lines using 786-O and OSRC-2 cells.

The effectiveness of DDIT4 knockdown was confirmed through RT-qPCR and western blot analysis (Fig. 3A-C). Functional assays, including CCK-8, EdU, and colony formation assays, demonstrated that DDIT4 knockdown significantly reduced the viability and proliferation of renal cancer cells (Fig. 3D-I). Additionally, wound-healing and Transwell assays showed that DDIT4 knockdown inhibited cell migration and invasion in both 786-O and OSRC-2 cells, suggesting that DDIT4 also contributes to the metastatic potential of ccRCC cells (Fig. 3J-M).

SMYD2 regulates DDIT4 transcription through enhancer remodeling

To investigate the epigenetic mechanism by which SMYD2 regulates DDIT4 expression, we performed CUT&Tag sequencing to identify H3K4me1-associated regulatory regions and integrated these data with RNA-seq analysis. Since histone modification marks are often enriched near regulatory regions such as promoters and enhancers, we analyzed the distribution of CUT&Tag signals around transcription start sites (TSS) (Fig. 4A-B). In addition, H3K27ac ChIP-seq data from the 786-O cell line from the GEO database (GSM7509632) were analyzed to generate TSS enrichment heatmaps and signal distribution profiles (Fig. 4C-D). RNA-seq analysis identified differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and overlap analysis between CUT&Tag and RNA-seq datasets revealed a subset of 4 candidate target genes (Fig. 4E).

To further investigate the spatial chromatin interactions underlying DDIT4 regulation, we performed 4 C-seq of DDIT4 in 786-O cells. Genome-wide analysis revealed prominent interaction signals on chromosome 10 (Fig. 4F), with DDIT4 showing the strongest interaction peak (Fig. 4G-H). Integrative analysis of CUT&Tag and 4 C data identified a 480-bp candidate regulatory region as a putative DDIT4 enhancer (chr10:72830412–72830891) (Table S3).

We next assessed the role of SMYD2 in enhancer activation. Western blot analysis showed that SMYD2 knockdown significantly reduced global H3K4me1 and H3K27ac levels (Fig. 5A-B). Active enhancers were identified as DNA sequences overlapping with H3K4me1 and H3K27ac signals, whereas the density of the H3K27ac signal was further used to evaluate the activity of enhancers. IGV visualization demonstrated co-enrichment of H3K4me1 and H3K27ac signals at the candidate enhancer region (Fig. 5C). ChIP–qPCR further confirmed significant enrichment of SMYD2, and active enhancer markers H3K4me1/H3K27ac at this locus in control cells, which was markedly reduced upon SMYD2 knockdown (Fig. 5D-F).

To determine the functional relevance of this enhancer (designated EDDIT4, chr10:72830412–72830891), we performed CRISPR–Cas9-mediated deletion of the EDDIT4 region. PCR-based genotyping confirmed the expected deletion of the targeted DDIT4 enhancer region in single-cell clone 1, which was subsequently used for DDIT4 expression analysis (Fig. S4A). RT–qPCR analysis revealed that deletion of EDDIT4 significantly decreased DDIT4 mRNA levels (Fig. 5G), indicating that this enhancer is essential for DDIT4 transcription. Functionally, deletion of EDDIT4 led to a marked reduction in the IC50 of pazopanib, reflecting increased drug sensitivity, and significantly suppressed cell proliferation (Fig. 5H-I).

Collectively, these results demonstrate that SMYD2 promotes DDIT4 transcription by maintaining enhancer activity, suggesting that enhancer remodeling is a key mechanism underlying DDIT4 upregulation.

SPI1 contributes to enhancer-associated DDIT4 transcriptional regulation

We next sought to identify transcription factors potentially involved in enhancer-associated regulation of DDIT4. Enhancer activation is often associated with transcription factor binding to specific DNA motifs. A ranked list of predicted transcription factor binding motifs within the DDIT4 enhancer region is provided in Table S5. Among these candidates, SPI1 was selected for further validation because it was identified as a candidate transcription factor corresponding to the enriched motif in the CUT&Tag-enriched region (Fig. 5J).

Next, we examined DDIT4 mRNA expression in 786-O control cells and EDDIT4-deleted cells by RT-qPCR. In 786-O control cells, SPI1 interference led to a decrease in DDIT4 mRNA expression (Fig. 5K), suggesting that SPI1 contributes to DDIT4 transcriptional regulation. However, in EDDIT4-deleted 786-O cells, SPI1 interference had no obvious effect on DDIT4 mRNA levels (Fig. 5L), suggesting that the regulatory effect of SPI1 on DDIT4 is at least partially dependent on the presence of the DDIT4 enhancer.

To further examine the association between SPI1 and the DDIT4 enhancer, we performed ChIP-qPCR, which showed reduced SPI1 enrichment in the EDDIT4-deleted cells compared with the controls (Fig. 5M). This suggests that the DDIT4-associated enhancer may facilitate SPI1 enrichment at this locus during DDIT4 transcriptional regulation. Based on these findings, we propose that SMYD2-mediated H3K4me1 modification is associated with activation of the DDIT4 enhancer and SPI1 enrichment, thereby contributing to DDIT4 transcription.

SMYD2 promotes ccRCC progression in a DDIT4-dependent manner

To determine whether SMYD2 promotes ccRCC progression through DDIT4, we performed rescue experiments in vitro and in vivo. The experimental results demonstrated that the reduction in cellular proliferation, migration and invasion of ccRCC cells induced by SMYD2 knockdown was reversed by DDIT4 overexpression (Fig. 6A-H).

We further verified the effects of SMYD2 and DDIT4 on tumorigenesis and development of ccRCC in the xenograft mouse model. 786-O cells were subcutaneously injected into the nude mice. These findings suggest that SMYD2 not only enhances the activation of the STAT3 signaling pathway but also relies on DDIT4 to mediate its tumor-promoting effects. This establishes DDIT4 as a crucial functional component in the SMYD2-driven progression of ccRCC.

SMYD2 regulates DDIT4 and cooperatively promotes pazopanib resistance in ccRCC

To investigate the effect of DDIT4 on pazopanib sensitivity, we established 786-O pazopanib-resistant cell line (786-O PR) (Fig. 7A). Compared with the 786-O cells, the migration and invasion abilities of the 786-O PR cells were significantly stronger (Fig. 7B-C). The IC50 values of the 786-O pazopanib-resistant cells were significantly higher than those of the control cells (Fig. 7D). Additionally, DDIT4 mRNA and protein levels were elevated in the pazopanib-resistant cells (Fig. 7E, G). SMYD2 protein expression also increased (Fig. 7G), but there was no significant change observed in mRNA levels (Fig. 7F). Upon knocking down DDIT4 in the pazopanib-resistant cell lines, the IC50 values were significantly reduced compared to the negative control, demonstrating that DDIT4 knockdown enhanced the cells’ sensitivity to pazopanib (Fig. 7H). Colony formation assays further revealed a significant reduction in cell proliferation following DDIT4 knockdown, with a dose-dependent response to pazopanib treatment (Fig. 7I-J).

In the 786-O pazopanib-resistant cell lines, the reduced IC50 values and colony formation observed after SMYD2 knockdown were reversed by DDIT4 overexpression (Fig. 7K-N), suggesting that DDIT4 overexpression can counteract the effects of SMYD2 knockdown by restoring pazopanib resistance. We also established a nude mouse lung metastasis model by injecting different 786-O pazopanib-resistant cells into the tail vein to evaluate the ability of cells to metastasize in vivo. As demonstrated in Fig. 7O-Q, knockdown of SMYD2 or DDIT4 significantly reduced lung metastatic nodules compared with the control group, while the number of tumor nodules was significantly increased in mice injected with cells with SMYD2 knockdown and DDIT4 overexpression. The above results indicate that SMYD2 plays a key role in regulating DDIT4, which is essential for pazopanib resistance and lung metastasis in renal cancer.

DDIT4 drives STAT3 activation and functional phenotypes in a STAT3-dependent manner

To further explore the downstream signaling pathways involved, we performed overlap analysis on differentially expressed genes from RNA-seq datasets and identified four gene sets (Fig. S3A). KEGG enrichment analysis of the gene set containing DDIT4 revealed potential involvement of multiple signaling pathways (Fig. S3B). Western blot analysis showed that SMYD2 knockdown did not significantly affect PI3K/AKT, MAPK, or p53 signaling pathways (Fig. S3C). However, DDIT4 knockdown reduced both total and phosphorylated mTOR levels (Fig. S3D). We next examined STAT3 signaling. SMYD2 knockdown inhibited STAT3 pathway activation, whereas DDIT4 overexpression partially restored STAT3-related protein expression, indicating that DDIT4 mediates SMYD2-dependent STAT3 activation (Fig. 8A).

To test whether the functional output of DDIT4 requires STAT3 activity (functional epistasis), we performed rescue experiments using the STAT3-specific inhibitor Stattic (5 µM) in both cell lines. As shown in Fig. 8B, Stattic effectively suppressed p-STAT3 in all groups. Critically, under Stattic treatment, DDIT4-OE cells maintained significantly higher p-STAT3 levels than Stattic-treated vector controls, demonstrating that DDIT4 overexpression partially rescues STAT3 phosphorylation from Stattic-mediated inhibition. Consistently, CCK-8 proliferation assays (Fig. 8C-D) showed that DDIT4-OE significantly enhanced cell proliferation, while Stattic treatment partially but significantly attenuated this advantage. These results suggest that DDIT4-driven proliferative advantage is at least partially dependent on STAT3 activity.

Next, we examined STAT3 signaling in 786-O PR cells. Western blot analysis revealed that 786-O PR cells exhibited markedly elevated p-STAT3 levels compared to parental cells, with no significant change in total STAT3 expression (Fig. 8E), indicating selective activation of STAT3 signaling in the resistant context. To determine whether this activation is functionally required for resistance, we performed rescue experiments using the STAT3-specific inhibitor Stattic. Before assessing the effect of STAT3 inhibition on pazopanib sensitivity, we first confirmed that the selected Stattic concentration (5 µM) did not significantly affect 786-O PR cell viability over the 24 h treatment period (Fig. 8F), excluding potential confounding from non-specific cytotoxicity. The co-treatment with Stattic significantly resensitized 786-O PR cells to pazopanib (Fig. 8G).

Collectively, our results demonstrate that DDIT4 promotes tumor cell proliferation and drug resistance through activation of the STAT3 pathway, with STAT3 serving as an essential downstream effector of DDIT4-driven phenotypes.

Construction of a DDIT4-related prognostic model and anti-tumor drug response prediction in ccRCC

To further evaluate the prognostic significance of DDIT4-associated genes in ccRCC, pearson correlation analysis was performed to identify genes correlated with DDIT4 expression in the TCGA-KIRC cohort. Subsequently, univariate Cox regression analysis was used to screen candidate genes related to prognosis. LASSO-Cox regression analysis was then applied to construct a DDIT4-related prognostic signature consisting of 16 genes using the optimal λ value (Fig. 9A-B, Supplementary Table S4).

Based on the median risk score, patients were divided into high-risk and low-risk groups. The distribution of risk scores and survival status demonstrated that patients in the high-risk group exhibited a higher mortality rate (Fig. 9C). The expression patterns of the 16 signature genes are presented in a heatmap (Fig. 9D). The hazard ratios and prognostic contributions of the 16 signature genes are shown in Fig. 9E. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis showed that the overall survival of patients in the high-risk group was significantly worse than that in the low-risk group (Fig. 9F). Time-dependent ROC curve analysis revealed that the prognostic model exhibited robust predictive performance, with AUC values of 0.769, 0.744 and 0.764 for 1-year, 3-year, and 5-year overall survival, respectively (Fig. 9G). Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses further confirmed that the risk score serves as an independent prognostic factor for patients with ccRCC (Fig. 9H-I). Furthermore, a nomogram was constructed by integrating the risk score with clinicopathological features to predict the 1-, 3-, and 5-year overall survival probabilities for patients with ccRCC (Fig. 9J).

The volcano plot revealed that various anti-tumor drugs exhibited significantly different predicted drug sensitivities between the high-risk and low-risk groups (Fig. 9K). The heatmap further illustrates the overall picture of different drug responses among representative targeted therapies and chemotherapy drugs (Fig. 9L).

Notably, high-risk patients were predicted to be more resistant to several targeted therapeutic agents, particularly EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitors, including gefitinib and osimertinib. In contrast, high-risk patients appeared to be more sensitive to several conventional chemotherapeutic drugs, such as gemcitabine and topotecan. Representative boxplots of predicted drug sensitivity are shown in Fig. 9M.

Based on these findings, we propose a model in which SMYD2 activates DDIT4 transcription by enhancer remodeling. Specifically, SMYD2-mediated H3K4me1 deposition maintains the activity of a distal DDIT4 enhancer, facilitating the recruitment of the transcription factor SPI1. This enhancer-dependent mechanism promotes DDIT4 transcription and activates the STAT3 signaling pathway, thereby driving ccRCC progression and contributing to pazopanib resistance (Fig. 10).

Discussion

Clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC) is characterized by high heterogeneity, which contributes to therapeutic resistance. Epigenetic dysregulation, including enhancer activation, is increasingly recognized as a key driver of tumor progression and drug resistance [21]. Enhancers are key noncoding regulatory elements that control gene transcription; however, their roles in ccRCC remain incompletely understood [24]. In this study, we identify a novel tumor-promoting enhancer regulating DDIT4 in ccRCC (chr10:72830412–72830891) and demonstrate that SMYD2 activates this enhancer to upregulate DDIT4, thereby promoting STAT3 signaling, ccRCC progression, and pazopanib resistance.

Approaches commonly used to identify enhancers include profiling histone modifications such as H3K4me1 and H3K27ac, as well as chromatin profiling approaches including ChIP-seq/CUT&Tag [25]. Although these methods can yield numerous candidate regulatory regions, functional validation is required to define real enhancers. In this study, we performed integrative analyses of CUT&Tag and public ChIP-seq datasets to identify candidate regulatory regions associated with DDIT4. In parallel, 4 C-seq analysis was used to capture chromatin interactions, enabling the identification of a distal regulatory region with strong interaction signals at the DDIT4 locus. By combining these approaches, we defined a candidate enhancer region located at chr10:72830412–72830891. To validate the functional relevance of this enhancer, we performed ChIP–qPCR and CRISPR–Cas9-mediated deletion assays. Our results demonstrate that deletion of this enhancer significantly reduces DDIT4 expression at both the mRNA and protein levels, supporting its functional role in DDIT4 transcription. Notably, we further show that this enhancer is associated with SMYD2-mediated H3K4me1 and facilitates SPI1 recruitment, thereby supporting its potential role as a functional DDIT4-associated enhancer that may contribute to tumor-promoting phenotypes in ccRCC.

SMYD2 is a member of the SET and MYND domain-containing family of lysine methyltransferases [26]. Previous studies, including our own, have shown that SMYD2 is overexpressed in multiple cancers, including ccRCC, where it functions as an oncogenic regulator [12]. SMYD2 regulates multiple biological processes in tumor progression by catalyzing the lysine methylation of H3K36, H3K4, and non-histone proteins [11]. Notably, SMYD2 has been reported to activate gene expression through H3K4me3-mediated regulation of promoter regions [13]. Given its role in H3K4 mono-methylation, we hypothesized that SMYD2 may also promote tumor progression through enhancer activation. Consistent with this, our findings demonstrate that SMYD2 activates a distal enhancer of DDIT4 via H3K4me1 deposition, thereby driving tumor-promoting transcription.

The function of enhancers has high cell/tissue specificity, which is due to the different landscapes of transcription factors (TFs) in varying cells [27]. As a member of the ETS family of TFs, SPI1(also known as PU.1) is generally thought to regulate gene expression in myeloid cells in leukemia but has also been identified as a metastatic suppressor in hepatocellular carcinoma, and an activator of DPYD expression in colon cancer [28–30]. By motif prediction using the JASPAR database, we identified a putative SPI1-binding motif within the DDIT4 enhancer region. Accordingly, we demonstrate that SPI1 binding at the DDIT4 enhancer region was detected by ChIP–qPCR analysis. Furthermore, depletion of the enhancer by CRISPR–Cas9 significantly reduces SPI1 occupancy, confirming that SPI1 recruitment depends on the integrity of this regulatory element.

DDIT4 exhibits context-dependent roles in tumorigenesis, with both tumor-suppressive and pro-tumorigenic functions reported. As a well-known negative regulator of mTORC1, DDIT4 is induced under various stress conditions, including oxidative stress, nutrient deprivation, and hypoxia [31], and suppresses mTOR signaling through activation of the TSC1/2 complex, thereby exerting tumor-suppressive effects [32]. In contrast, emerging evidence indicates that DDIT4 can also promote tumor progression. For instance, DDIT4 has been reported to promote tumor cell proliferation and tumorigenesis in gastric and colon cancers through modulation of the p53 and MAPK pathways, highlighting its dual roles in cancer [33]. In our study, we identify a previously unrecognized mechanism by which DDIT4 promotes tumor progression. We demonstrate that SMYD2 upregulates DDIT4 to activate STAT3 signaling, thereby driving ccRCC growth and pazopanib resistance. STAT3 is a well-established oncogenic transcription factor that promotes tumor cell proliferation, survival, migration, and therapeutic resistance in multiple cancers [34]. Notably, the regulation of STAT3 signaling by DDIT4 represents a novel finding, further expanding the functional landscape of DDIT4 in cancer. Clinically, elevated DDIT4 expression is associated with poorer survival outcomes and more aggressive clinicopathological features in ccRCC, supporting its relevance to disease progression.

Given that pazopanib remains a first-line therapy for advanced ccRCC, the identification of the SMYD2–enhancer–DDIT4 axis offers a potential mechanism underlying drug resistance. Targeting SMYD2/DDIT4 or disrupting enhancer-mediated transcription may represent a promising strategy to overcome drug resistance. Furthermore, the involvement of STAT3 signaling suggests that combination therapies targeting both epigenetic regulators and downstream signaling pathways may enhance therapeutic efficacy.

In addition to the mechanistic findings, we also constructed a DDIT4-related prognostic model based on TCGA-KIRC data through Cox and LASSO analysis, which showed good prognostic predictive ability in ccRCC patients. Drug sensitivity prediction analysis further suggested distinct therapeutic response patterns between high- and low-risk groups, indicating the potential utility of this model in guiding personalized treatment strategies for ccRCC.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Although our findings support a model in which SMYD2 promotes DDIT4 expression through enhancer-associated regulation and contributes to ccRCC progression and pazopanib resistance via downstream STAT3 signaling, additional studies are needed to further define the causality of each step. For example, catalytic-dead SMYD2 rescue experiments would help determine whether SMYD2 methyltransferase activity is directly required for enhancer regulation, and complementary approaches such as enhancer reporter assays or CRISPR interference would further validate the regulatory properties of the identified DDIT4-associated enhancer. In addition, the pazopanib-resistant model used in this study was generated from a single 786-O-derived resistant lineage. Although the resistant phenotype was maintained after short-term drug withdrawal, this model may not fully reflect the molecular heterogeneity of pazopanib resistance in patients. Furthermore, key findings were validated in two ccRCC cell lines; however, further validation in patient-derived models, independent resistant models, and external clinical cohorts will be important to assess the generalizability of this mechanism. Finally, although statistical thresholds and adjusted P values/FDR were reported where applicable, the DDIT4-related prognostic signature was developed from a single public cohort and lacks independent validation, creating a potential risk of overfitting. Therefore, the prognostic signature and drug sensitivity analysis should be interpreted as exploratory and require independent clinical and experimental validation.

Conclusions

In summary, we identify a previously unrecognized regulatory enhancer associated with DDIT4 expression in ccRCC, located at chr10:72830412–72830891. SMYD2-mediated H3K4me1 deposition is associated with activation of this enhancer, SPI1 recruitment, and increased DDIT4 transcription. Disruption of this enhancer significantly reduces DDIT4 expression and impairs tumor cell proliferation and metastatic potential. We further demonstrate that DDIT4 is associated with activation of STAT3 signaling and contributes to tumor progression and pazopanib resistance. These findings provide new insights into the epigenetic regulation of ccRCC and suggest that targeting the SMYD2–DDIT4 axis may represent a potential therapeutic strategy, supporting the development of epigenetic-based precision medicine approaches.
