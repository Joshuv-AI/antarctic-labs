Psychotherapy, Antidepressants, and Combined Treatment for Depression: A Network Meta-Analysis on Social Functioning Outcomes
Authors: Clara Miguel, Lingyao Tong, Aran Tajika, Mathias Harrer, Nino de Ponti, Keisuke Yoshimura, Marketa Ciharova, Paul van Haaren, Claudia Buntrock, Andrea Cipriani, Georgia Salanti, Edoardo G. Ostinelli, Ioana A. Cristea, Pim Cuijpers, Eirini Karyotaki, Toshi A. Furukawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionSocial functioning is a key component of recovery in depression, yet most research on first-line treatments focuses on symptom reduction, and comparative evidence on social functioning is limited. We aimed to evaluate the comparative effects of psychotherapy, antidepressant medication, and their combination on social functioning in adults with major depression.MethodsWe conducted a systematic review and network meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials up to May 1, 2025. Eligible trials compared psychotherapy, antidepressant medication, combined treatment, and control conditions (placebo, care as usual, waitlist, or no/minimal treatment) in adults with major depression. Effects were calculated as standardized mean differences (SMDs) in social functioning instruments (e.g., Sheehan Disability Scale) at posttreatment (mean 12 weeks) and follow-up (mean 32 weeks). Random effects models were used. We assessed risk of bias and conducted sensitivity analyses. Certainty of evidence was evaluated using CINeMA.ResultsOf 490 identified trials, 94 (19%; 23,874 participants) reported social functioning and were included. All active treatments outperformed control conditions. Effects varied substantially by control condition type, with waitlist comparisons yielding the largest estimates and comparisons against placebo or care as usual yielding more conservative effects. Combined psychotherapy and pharmacotherapy showed the largest effects versus placebo at posttreatment (SMD 0.75, 95% CI: 0.57–0.94) and follow-up (1.08, 0.62–1.54). Psychotherapy and pharmacotherapy did not differ at posttreatment (SMD 0.10, 95% CI: −0.05 to 0.25) or follow-up (0.27, 95% CI: 0.00–0.54). Certainty of the evidence ranged from moderate to low.ConclusionFirst-line depression treatments support not only symptom reduction but also meaningful participation in daily life. However, only one-fifth of trials assessed this outcome, highlighting the need to better integrate patient-valued outcomes into future trials.

Introduction

Depression is a leading cause of disability worldwide [1]. This prevalent and recurrent disorder severely impacts quality of life and functioning, causing impairments in work, relationships, school, and leisure [2]. Psychotherapy and antidepressants are the two first-line treatment options for depression. Both therapeutic approaches have shown superiority over control conditions such as placebo or treatment as usual in reducing depressive symptoms [3, 4]. Most previous examinations of the absolute and relative effectiveness of depression treatments have primarily focused on the reduction of depressive symptom severity. However, patients with depression consider improvements in social functioning (e.g., performance at work, home, or school) as important as the absence of symptoms [5, 6]. Social functioning is thus a central outcome from patients’ perspective [7], and it can be conceptualized as the functioning of individuals in several social environments and domains, such as work or study, interpersonal relationships, and leisure activities [8, 9].

Some prior pairwise meta-analyses have reported small effects of antidepressants on social functioning [10, 11], small-to-moderate effects of psychotherapy [9], and larger effects of combined treatment [12]. However, direct comparisons between psychotherapy and antidepressants remain inconclusive [12], largely due to the lack of head-to-head trials. Existing syntheses, which rely primarily on pairwise meta-analyses, cannot establish the relative effects of first-line treatments on social functioning. Consequently, available evidence provides a limited basis for comparative treatment guidance, despite the recognition of functioning as a critical outcome in clinical guidelines [13] and its inclusion in common measurement initiatives [14, 15]. To address this gap, we systematically reviewed randomized clinical trials of first-line depression treatments to characterize the availability and measurement of social functioning outcomes, and conducted a network meta-analysis to examine the comparative effects of psychotherapy, antidepressants, and their combination on social functioning.

Methods

Search Strategy and Selection Criteria

The methods of this systematic review and network meta-analysis are detailed in our preregistered OSF protocol (https://osf.io/sfcx8) and further described in online supplementary material 1 (for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000553252). The reporting follows the PRISMA guidelines (see PRISMA checklist in online supplementary material 1). Studies were identified from two existing databases of RCTs on depression treatment [3, 16]. One database focuses on psychotherapy (Metapsy) [16], the other on antidepressant medication (GRISELDA) [3]. Both were built through systematic searches in major bibliographic databases (e.g., PubMed, Embase, PsycINFO) and additional sources (e.g., trial registries), with at least two independent researchers screening titles and abstracts, and subsequently full texts. We used the latest available update of the Metapsy database (May 1, 2025) and updated GRISELDA with a new search up to the same date. No language restrictions were applied. Given that social functioning is not usually a primary outcome, we conducted supplementary screenings of secondary reports or registrations from the trials deemed eligible, as well as previous relevant meta-analyses. When multiple reports referred to the same trial, data were extracted once. Further details about the searches, search strings, and study selection are described in online supplementary material 1.

Included studies compared any of the following treatment or control nodes: psychotherapy (psy), antidepressant medication (pha), combined psychotherapy and antidepressant medication (com), pill placebo (pla), care-as-usual (cau), waitlist (wl), and minimal-to-no treatment (nt). Table 1 describes the included study nodes in detail. Any type of psychotherapy was included, delivered through any format that involves human guidance related to the therapeutic content. We included fixed or flexible licensed dose regimens of new generation antidepressants that have shown greater efficacy than placebo in acute treatment (SSRIs, SNRIs, etc.) [3]. Participants were ≥18 years old, met the criteria for a depressive disorder as diagnosed by clinicians or by structured interviews, and were recruited from clinical or community settings. We excluded trials focusing on another primary psychiatric disorder, somatic illness, perinatal or treatment-resistant depression, or inpatients. These characteristics could act as effect modifiers and violate the transitivity assumption in the network meta-analysis.

Data Extraction and Risk of Bias Assessment

Some data were previously collected during the development of these meta-analytic databases [3, 16], including study, participant, and intervention characteristics, as well as risk of bias (RoB) assessments based on Cochrane’s tool (version 1) [20]. RoB assessments were reevaluated for the purposes of the present analysis and for the specific outcome of interest (social functioning at posttreatment). More details about the RoB assessments are provided in online supplementary material 1. For this study, we additionally extracted data related to social functioning outcomes from published trial reports (type of measure, data for calculating effect sizes, scoring direction of the instrument, etc.) as this information had not been previously collected as part of the existing databases. All data were collected independently by pairs of reviewers, with discrepancies resolved through discussion or involving a third reviewer. Trial authors were not contacted for additional data (e.g., to request unpublished outcomes or missing statistics).

Outcome

Social functioning was defined as the measurement of an individual’s ability to participate and perform effectively in key social roles and contexts, including interpersonal relationships, family or partner relationships, work or study, and leisure or recreational activities, consistent with prior operationalizations in the psychotherapy literature [9]. These domains are captured by the most commonly used instruments in the treatment literature, such as the Sheehan Disability Scale (SDS) and the Work and Social Adjustment Scale. Subscales measuring one of these areas within a broader instrument (e.g., SF-36 social functioning subscale) were included as social functioning outcomes. We included scales focused on a single, highly specific domain within social functioning (e.g., work engagement) and examined their influence in sensitivity analyses. We excluded measures assessing relationship quality (e.g., marital adjustment) and global indices that combine symptom severity and functioning (e.g., the Global Assessment Scale) to preserve construct specificity and avoid conceptual overlap with depressive symptoms.

When multiple measures were available within a study, we prioritized the instrument most frequently used across eligible studies. Whenever possible, we extracted total scores. When only subscales reflecting subdomains of social functioning were reported (e.g., the “Work,” “Social,” and “Family” subscales in the SDS), we pooled them within study arms, assuming a correlation of 0.5. Multiple arms belonging to the same node were pooled using the same approach.

For each included measure of social functioning, a standardized mean difference (SMD; Hedges’ g) was calculated to estimate the difference between arms within a trial at posttreatment (first assessment following acute-phase treatment). When available, effect sizes were also calculated at longer term follow-ups (>20 weeks post-randomization) as secondary analyses. Effect sizes were calculated using endpoint means, standard deviations, and sample sizes. If these were unavailable, we used change scores or converted other statistics (e.g., F value, t value) into Hedges’ g. The scoring direction of each instrument (e.g., higher scores indicating better functioning) was independently verified by two researchers, and effect sizes were recoded accordingly.

Statistical Analysis

The comparative efficacy (defined as SMDs in social functioning outcomes at posttreatment) between psychotherapy, antidepressants, combined treatment, and control conditions (cau, pla, wl, nt) was estimated through a frequentist network meta-analysis [21], with a random effects model [22]. We reported comparative SMDs with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Treatments were ranked using P-scores [23] (online suppl. material 7). Heterogeneity was assessed through I2, tau2, and prediction intervals. Between-study heterogeneity was estimated using restricted maximum likelihood. Pairwise meta-analyses for direct comparisons were also computed. Small-study effects were examined using Egger’s test (for comparisons with >10 studies) and visually through contour-enhanced and comparison-adjusted funnel plots [24]. The same analyses were repeated at long-term follow-ups.

The transitivity assumption was assessed by comparing the distribution of potential effect modifiers across comparisons (age, baseline depression severity, and sex). Consistency of the network was assessed via the design-by-treatment interaction test [25, 26], net heat plots [26], and SIDE tests [27] (separating indirect and direct evidence), interpreted following the Confidence in Network Meta-Analysis (CINeMA) guidance [28]. One study [29] was excluded from all primary analyses because it was a high-RoB study that disproportionately contributed to substantial inconsistency in the network, which was largely improved upon removal with no meaningful impact on overall findings (see online suppl. material 4 for further explanations about this decision, as well as several analyses with the study).

We conducted the following sensitivity analyses to examine whether our findings varied across different methodological decisions and sample characteristics: (1) restricting to studies reporting global instruments assessing multiple domains of social functioning (e.g., total scores of the SDS), excluding very specific measures (e.g., work-specific scales); (2) excluding trials targeting chronic depression as these populations may present with greater and more enduring functional impairment than those in typical acute depression trials, potentially introducing heterogeneity in outcomes; (3) excluding studies at high RoB; (4) limiting the analyses to cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) and SSRIs; (5) excluding studies focused on older adults (mean age >65 years), given that some aspects of social functioning might not apply to this group (e.g., work functioning); (6) limiting to studies reporting outcomes between 6 and 16 weeks post-randomization, the most homogeneous measurement points across studies; (7) excluding arms classified as “suboptimal pharmacotherapy” (following Cuijpers et al. [30], defined as mixed agents, unclear or nontherapeutic dosing, or inadequate titration schedules); and (8) including only active comparisons. More details about these analyses are reported in online supplementary material 8.

Certainty of the evidence was assessed through the CINeMA framework and application (https://cinema.ispm.ch) [28, 31]. Analyses were conducted in R (version 4.5.0), using the netmeta [21] package.

Results

Study Selection

The study selection process is displayed in Figure 1 (PRISMA flow diagram) and in online supplementary material 2. A total of 490 RCTs were eligible for this study and were screened for social functioning outcomes (205 and 284 RCTs from the psychotherapy and antidepressants databases, respectively, with 1 additional RCT identified through screening of previous meta-analyses). Out of the eligible studies, 94 (19%) reported sufficient data on a measure of social functioning, 41 from the Metapsy database (20% reporting, out of 205) and 53 from GRISELDA and its updates (19% reporting, out of 284) (references of included studies in online suppl. material 2).

PRISMA flow diagram describing the inclusion process.

Study Characteristics

Table 2 presents the aggregated study characteristics, which are available for each individual study in online supplementary material 3. The 94 RCTs (23,874 participants) had 198 arms included in the analysis, with k = 104 comparisons between these arms. These arms included pharmacotherapy (n = 72 arms), psychotherapy (n = 37), combined treatment (n = 8), pill placebo (n = 55), care as usual (n = 13), waitlist (n = 9), and minimal-to-no treatment (n = 4). Trials were mostly conducted in high-income countries (62 trials, 66%, in North America and Europe), with middle-aged adults (mean age 45 years; SD = 10), and with female participants (average 68%). The most commonly examined antidepressant classes were SNRIs (17 studies, 18%) and SSRIs (15 studies, 16%), and CBT was the most frequently studied psychotherapy (13 studies, 14%). Exact types of antidepressants and psychotherapies examined in each study are listed in online supplementary material 3. A total of 54 trials (57%) were considered at low RoB.

Measurement of Social Functioning

Twenty-three different instruments or subscales were used to assess social functioning across included trials, indicating substantial heterogeneity in outcome measurement. The most frequently used instrument was the SDS (48 studies), followed by the SF-36 social functioning subscale (8 studies) and the Work and Social Adjustment Scale (4 studies). Instrument use differed substantially by trial type. In pharmacotherapy versus placebo trials, 7 instruments were used to measure social functioning, but measurement was predominantly conducted using the SDS (85%; 41 of 48 studies). In contrast, across 26 studies comparing psychotherapy with its most common control conditions (waitlist, care as usual, or no treatment), 14 different instruments were used. The most frequently used instrument in these trials was the SDS, but it was used in only 5 studies (19%). All instruments identified are described in detail in eTable 2 (online suppl. material 3).

Figure 2 presents the network plot, showing the number of direct existing comparisons between the nodes. The most frequently studied comparisons were pharmacotherapy vs. placebo (k = 54 comparisons), psychotherapy vs. care-as-usual (k = 13), and psychotherapy vs. pharmacotherapy (k = 10). Pairwise meta-analyses are reported in online supplementary material 6.

The results of the network meta-analysis are presented in Table 3 and Figure 3. Throughout the results, SMDs are reported such that positive values favor the first-named treatment in each comparison. Combined treatment was the most effective intervention, outperforming all other interventions and control conditions, i.e., vs. placebo (SMD 0.75, 95% CI: [0.57–0.94], PI [0.44–1.07]); vs. pharmacotherapy alone (SMD 0.52, 95% CI: [0.34–0.70], PI [0.21–0.84]); vs. psychotherapy alone (SMD 0.42, 95% CI: [0.20–0.64], PI [0.09–0.76]). There was no evidence for a difference between psychotherapy and pharmacotherapy (SMD 0.10, 95% CI: [−0.05 to 0.25], PI [−0.20 to 0.39]). Psychotherapy was more effective than all control conditions, ranging from SMD 0.24 vs. care-as-usual to SMD 0.61 vs. waitlist. Pharmacotherapy was more effective than placebo, no treatment and waitlist (SMD ranges: 0.23 vs. placebo to 0.51 vs. waitlist).

Assessment of Heterogeneity, Transitivity, and Inconsistency

Potential effect modifiers were relatively balanced across pairwise comparisons, providing no evidence of violating the transitivity assumption (online suppl. material 3). There was evidence of moderate heterogeneity across the network, with I2 = 53% (95% CI: 41%–63%) and tau2 = 0.0165, and inconsistency within designs (Q = 187, df = 86, p < 0.001). The design-by-treatment interaction model did not indicate evidence of inconsistency across study designs (Q = 8.59, df = 7, p = 0.283). SIDE did not identify meaningful local inconsistency in five out of seven loops; however, two comparisons (com vs. psy; pha vs. nt) were judged as having major concerns for inconsistency according to CINeMA (online suppl. material 5).

Sensitivity Analyses

Sensitivity analyses (online suppl. material 8) broadly confirmed the main findings, including analyses restricted to low-RoB studies. However, two analyses showed different results. When limited to studies evaluating only CBT, SSRIs, or their combination (k = 26 comparisons), combined treatment was no longer superior to monotherapies, outperforming only waitlist and no treatment. Psychotherapy no longer outperformed care-as-usual or placebo, while SSRIs were superior to all control conditions. Excluding k = 11 comparisons involving “suboptimal” pharmacotherapy arms (removing 50% of direct active comparisons) also resulted in no differences between combined treatment and monotherapies.

Long-Term Outcomes

Long-term outcomes (20–52 weeks post-randomization) were reported in 19 studies (k = 25 comparisons) (online suppl. material 9). Combined treatment remained the most effective intervention, showing greater effects than pharmacotherapy (SMD 0.67, 95% CI: [0.38–0.97], PI [0.15–1.2]), psychotherapy (SMD 0.40, 95% CI: [0.06–0.74], PI [−0.15 to 0.96]), and all control conditions (SMDs 0.54–1.08). Both monotherapies were superior to placebo: psychotherapy vs. placebo (SMD 0.67 [95% CI: 0.23–1.12], PI [0.04–1.31]); pharmacotherapy vs. placebo (SMD 0.40 [95% CI: 0.05–0.75], PI [−0.16 to 0.96]). There was no evidence of a difference between psychotherapy and pharmacotherapy (SMD 0.27, 95% CI: [0.00–0.54], PI [−0.23 to 0.78]). There was considerable inconsistency in these analyses (Q = 17.76, df = 4, p = 0.002).

Certainty of the Evidence and Small-Study Effects

No evidence for the presence of funnel plot asymmetry was found (online suppl. material 10). The confidence of the evidence was moderate (11 out of 21) to low (10 out of 21 comparisons) (online suppl material 11).

Discussion

This study systematically identified 490 eligible randomized controlled trials of first-line depression treatments and examined the availability and measurement of social functioning outcomes across this complete cohort. Ninety-four trials (23,874 participants) reported social functioning outcome data and were synthesized in a network meta-analysis. Across these studies, we identified 23 different instruments or subscales used to measure social functioning, providing a comprehensive and systematic overview of how this outcome is operationalized in depression trials. All active treatments showed greater improvements in social functioning compared to control conditions at posttreatment and at long-term follow-ups. Combined treatment showed the largest effects, performing better than both psychotherapy and pharmacotherapy alone.

Evaluating the clinical relevance of these effects is not straightforward. The smallest worthwhile difference, defined as the smallest beneficial effect of an intervention that patients deem worthwhile given the associated burdens, is the conceptually appropriate benchmark for interpreting between-group intervention effects from a patient perspective. While the smallest worthwhile difference estimates have recently been derived for antidepressants and psychotherapies for depressive symptom outcomes [32, 33], equivalent estimates for social functioning do not yet exist. The effect of combined treatment versus placebo (SMD 0.75) is large and comparable in magnitude to effects observed for depressive symptoms in prior meta-analyses [30], while the monotherapy effects of psychotherapy (SMD 0.33) and pharmacotherapy (SMD 0.23) are more modest. Whether these effects meet the threshold of what patients would consider worthwhile, given the burdens of treatment, remains an open question that future research should address.

These findings confirm and consolidate prior pairwise meta-analyses examining the effects of depression treatments on social functioning [9–12]. The network meta-analysis framework used in the current study additionally allowed simultaneous comparisons of all first-line treatments, including indirect estimates for psychotherapy versus pharmacotherapy. Our findings on social functioning outcomes also partially mirror those seen for depressive symptoms in previous syntheses, where combined treatment has been found to be the most effective option in the short term [30] and psychotherapy alone or in combination superior to pharmacotherapy for sustaining effects over the long term [34]. However, our long-term results did not reach statistical significance and should be interpreted with caution given the limited number of studies and considerable inconsistency in these analyses. Also consistent with prior findings, our results suggest that waitlist control may exert a nocebo effect on social functioning [35]. Participants on waitlist showed worse outcomes than placebo, with a likely meaningful difference (SMD = 0.28, 95% CI: [0.04–0.52]) [36].

Our main findings were supported by multiple sensitivity analyses, including those restricted to studies with low RoB. Two sensitivity analyses, however, showed different patterns: restricting the network to trials of CBT, SSRIs, or their combination, and excluding arms classified as suboptimal pharmacotherapy. In the first analysis, combined treatment no longer outperformed monotherapies and psychotherapy no longer outperformed placebo or care as usual. In the second, excluding suboptimal pharmacotherapy arms also led to no clear advantage for combined treatment. These findings contrast with broader meta-analyses focusing on depressive symptoms, which consistently identify combined treatment as the most effective option [30, 34, 37]. Both sensitivity analyses relied on weaker networks that excluded much of the direct evidence, particularly for combined treatment. For example, in the CBT and SSRI-restricted analysis, only one study included a combined treatment arm. Accordingly, these results should be interpreted with caution and may reflect differences in the composition of the restricted networks rather than true differences in treatment effects.

Notably, unlike the effects on depressive symptoms, which often attenuate over time [30], improvements in social functioning were maintained at follow-up and, in some, comparisons were more pronounced than at posttreatment, although long-term evidence was limited and inconsistent. Prior research suggests that social functioning and depressive symptoms, although related, do not improve in parallel [38–40]. Symptom improvement typically occurs earlier and more strongly, whereas functional improvement tends to emerge later and may play an important role in sustained recovery [39]. Consistent with this, impaired functioning has also been observed to persist even after full symptomatic remission across mood disorders beyond depression [41]. Declines in social functioning during the post-remission phase have also been associated with an even greater increase in relapse risk and recurrence of depression than residual symptoms [42, 43]. Distinct profiles of social dysfunction have additionally been associated with differences in suicidal ideation and work participation [44]. These findings reinforce the conceptualization of social functioning as a distinct outcome and a core component of recovery in depression, highlighting the clinical importance of assessing it. Definitions of mental health increasingly recognize that recovery extends beyond symptom reduction and includes living a meaningful and fulfilling life, sometimes referred to as social recovery [2, 45–47]. As health sciences move toward a broader and more person-centered understanding of recovery, outcomes like social functioning become essential for evaluating the full impact of treatment. Importantly, most commonly used depression rating scales do not directly assess functioning, meaning that symptom outcomes alone provide limited information about how treatments translate into everyday role performance and participation [48–51].

Evidence on patient-relevant outcome domains warrants more prominent consideration in the evaluation of competing depression interventions [52, 53]. However, many patient-relevant domains, including social functioning, are still underrepresented in clinical trials [50, 51, 54]. In our review, only 19% of trials on first-line depression treatments reported social functioning outcomes. Although functioning is increasingly recognized as a candidate domain for a core outcome set in depression [14, 15, 50], uptake in current trial practice is limited. This selective availability of outcome data limits confidence that current estimates reflect treatment effects across the full trial literature. In addition, the wide range of instruments used across trials highlights the lack of standardization in how social functioning is measured in depression research. Furthermore, it is worth acknowledging that social functioning is not solely an outcome of individual treatment. As a construct encompassing work, interpersonal relationships, family life, and leisure activities, social functioning is also shaped by the behavior of others and broader environmental and contextual factors. The measures used in the included trials do not allow us to disentangle the individual and contextual determinants of any observed improvements.

This study has several potential limitations. First, the trials differed markedly in design across intervention types. Control conditions were rarely comparable, which complicates interpretation and may contribute to overestimation of effects. Effects varied substantially depending on the type of comparator: waitlist comparisons yielded the largest estimates (e.g., vs. pharmacotherapy SMD 0.51, vs. psychotherapy SMD 0.61), while comparisons against placebo or care as usual showed considerably smaller effects (e.g., vs. pharmacotherapy SMD 0.23 and 0.14, respectively, vs. psychotherapy SMD 0.33 and 0.24, respectively). Pill placebo controls are only available in trials that include a pharmacotherapy arm. Instruments used to assess social functioning also varied, especially between pharmacotherapy and psychotherapy trials. Most of the studies on pharmacotherapies used the SDS (41 out of 48), whereas the psychotherapy studies relied on a wide variety of outcome measures (23 different outcome measures). Second, while trials focusing on somatic disorders were excluded, comorbid physical conditions may have been present among participants, varying across trials or interventions. All these factors may have affected comparability and contributed to the observed heterogeneity in SMD estimates. In addition, while the studies selected from both underlying databases primarily target MDD, some trials also included participants with dysthymia or other depressive disorders, which may have introduced some diagnostic heterogeneity across the included studies. Moreover, aggregate trial-level data, as used in meta-analyses, do not allow reliable investigation of individual-level moderators of treatment effects, such as potential gender, age, or socioeconomic differences in social functioning outcomes. Individual participant data would be needed to examine such questions appropriately. Third, the full dataset showed substantial inconsistency, largely driven by a single outlier study. We excluded this study from the main analysis to provide more consistent and interpretable estimates. This did not affect the overall conclusions. Fourth, only 19 trials reported long-term outcomes on social functioning, so these findings should be interpreted with caution. Similarly, comparisons between CBT and SSRIs were based on a small, weakly connected subset of studies. Fifth, comparisons between pharmacotherapy and placebo were limited to agents with established efficacy, using strict definitions of dose and schedule [3]. In contrast, some head-to-head comparisons with psychotherapy included trials using suboptimal pharmacotherapy (e.g., mixed agents, unclear dosing). This choice allowed us to retain direct comparisons, which would otherwise have been too limited. At the same time, such trials may also better reflect real-world clinical practice. However, differences in how pharmacotherapy is defined across study designs, particularly between trials comparing pharmacotherapy with psychotherapy and those comparing pharmacotherapy with placebo, may have introduced additional variability and potential bias into the findings. Sixth, the certainty of evidence was moderate to low. Seventh, because only a minority of eligible trials reported social functioning outcomes, our analyses may be susceptible to selection bias related to outcome availability. We did not contact all the authors of eligible trials to request any potential unreported data. Finally, because most trials were conducted in high-income countries and enrolled predominantly middle-aged adults, generalizability to low-resource settings, older populations, and more clinically complex samples, such as participants with somatic disorders, may be limited.

Conclusion

In this comprehensive synthesis of the comparative effects of first-line depression treatments on social functioning, all active interventions were more effective than control conditions, and combined psychotherapy and pharmacotherapy showed the largest benefits. The relative effectiveness of psychotherapy and pharmacotherapy as monotherapies remains uncertain. Only one in five trials reported social functioning outcomes, highlighting a mismatch between what patients consider important and what is routinely measured in clinical research. Our study contributes to a growing evidence base informing treatment choices based not only on symptom relief but also on broader outcomes that capture meaningful participation in daily life, including work, relationships, and pleasurable activities.
