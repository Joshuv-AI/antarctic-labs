Reducing healthcare emissions in the United States in the era of federal environmental deregulation: established and emerging mitigation opportunities and concomitant benefits
Authors: Erica Mascarenhas, Oluseyi A Fayanju, Anna Chen Arroyo, Jyothi Tirumalasetty
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe United States (US) healthcare sector leads the world in per capita greenhouse gas emissions, contributing disproportionately to global climate change. The recent reversal of federal climate regulations has increased the need for voluntary and urgent action from the US healthcare sector. In 2022, the US healthcare sector emissions resulted in over 425,000 disability-adjusted life years lost, reflecting the direct human cost of healthcare-related greenhouse gas emissions. Climate change worsens health outcomes and disrupts access to care — these impacts fall heavily on vulnerable populations including low-income communities, indigenous populations, the elderly, and children. Three key areas for decarbonization include infrastructure, supply chains, and operations and can be accounted for and addressed through the Greenhouse Gas Protocol framework. Some established mitigation strategies include transitioning to renewable energy, reducing medical waste, adopting lower emissions anesthetics and inhalers, and implementing sustainable food management. However, current greenhouse gas inventories utilized by health systems may not yet account for the rapidly expanding landscape of technology in healthcare and its growing contribution to emissions. Emerging priorities for mitigation that are often overlooked include reducing emissions associated with medical conferences and the growing energy demands of artificial intelligence. Here, we discuss how decarbonization not only reduces emissions but also provides important co-benefits, including better air quality, climate resilience, reduced costs, and improved workforce well-being.Keywords: Healthcare sustainability, Climate change and health, Healthcare decarbonization, Greenhouse gas emissions, Global warming

Background

The US has the highest per capita healthcare-related greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions in the world and accounts for a disproportionate share of global healthcare emissions [1–3]. As of 2022, per capita US healthcare emissions were 1191 kilograms carbon dioxide equivalent (kg CO2e) – more than double the 549 kg CO2e per capita emissions reported in the United Kingdom (UK) that year [2]. On April 22, 2022, the US Department of Health and Human Services’ Office of Climate Change and Health Equity announced the Health Sector Climate Pledge — a call-to-action inviting healthcare organizations to voluntarily reduce emissions by 50% by the year 2030 [4]. Over 100 healthcare organizations joined the pledge and established decarbonization programs within their institutions [4]. In 2025, these sustainability initiatives, along with the Office of Climate Change and Health Equity, were defunded and dismantled secondary to shifting federal priorities, leaving many organizations without the incentive structures needed for successful decarbonization [5].

In 2022, US health sector emissions resulted in 425,400 disability-adjusted life years lost [2]. Climate change, driven by GHG emissions and global warming, impacts health outcomes through multiple mechanisms: extreme heat has been associated with increased mortality secondary to cardiovascular events and exacerbation of chronic respiratory diseases, air pollution has been associated with asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease exacerbations, the geographic ranges of infectious diseases such as coccidioidomycosis are expanding, and warming temperatures have contributed to longer and more intense pollen seasons [6–10]. The resulting increase in healthcare utilization from climate-related disasters further drives healthcare-related emissions. The health impacts of climate change fall disproportionately on vulnerable populations, often those who already face the greatest barriers to healthcare access [2]. Climate-related disasters can cause unanticipated and prolonged national supply chain disruptions. In 2024, Hurricane Helene led to the shutdown of an intravenous fluid manufacturing plant in North Carolina, leading to a 10-month shortage of intravenous fluids across the country [11].

Given the growing impacts of climate change on health, the dismantling of federal climate protections due to political headwinds, and the urgency of limiting further global surface temperature increase to 1.5 degrees Celsius long-term, individual states, local governments, and the US health sector must take swift, voluntary action to prevent further harm [5, 12]. In addition to reducing GHG emissions, healthcare decarbonization provides concomitant benefits including cost savings, health system resilience, and decreased waste of resources [13]. Current emissions accounting strategies may fail to capture several important sources of emissions and mitigation opportunities – including the emissions associated with medical conferences, artificial intelligence (AI), and expanding telehealth services. Addressing these emerging sources of emissions will require both increased institutional awareness and the expansion of the GHG accounting frameworks currently used by healthcare systems. Here, we outline established and emerging areas for mitigation, discuss recommendations for implementation, and describe the broader benefits stemming from healthcare decarbonization, beyond emissions reduction alone.

Key areas and strategies for healthcare-related emissions reduction

In 2021, the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change 26th Conference of the Parties (COP26) outlined ways to decrease healthcare-related emissions. The COP26 Health Program outlines plans to establish health systems that are climate resilient, sustainable, and net-zero [14]. Establishing climate resilient healthcare systems will allow for more effective anticipation, responses, coping, recovery, and adaptation to climate-related events [15]. Building sustainable and resilient health systems requires clinically effective care that is responsible in its use of resources [16]. In addition to the COP26 Health Program and subsequent World Health Organization sponsored group Alliance for Transformative Action on Climate and Health, several organizations including Healthcare Without Harm, and the US based National Academy of Medicine have also outlined individual hospital and system level actions to further sustainability. Below, we outline the latest recommendations from these and other key sources. Healthcare emissions can be accounted for and addressed using the widely accepted framework of the Greenhouse Gas Protocol which classifies emissions into three scopes [1]. Scope 1 represents emissions from items that are directly owned or operated by healthcare organizations such as facilities, fleet of vehicles, and anesthetic gases and accounts for approximately 7% of healthcare sector emissions. Scope 2 includes all purchased energy and accounts for about 11% of healthcare sector emissions [1]. Scope 3 is the largest category, making up an estimated 82% of healthcare sector emissions and includes the supply chain, medications, food, waste, water, and business travel among other components [17]. While many of the strategies discussed below involve institution-level decisions, point-of-care clinical decisions made by individual clinicians, such as choosing inhalers and anesthetics with lower environmental impacts, minimizing unnecessary tests, and offering telehealth visits can still contribute to meaningful reductions in emissions [18].

Scope 1

Facilities

Hospitals are amongst the top energy-consuming buildings in the US. Heating, cooling and ventilation systems within hospital-owned facilities are a major driver of emissions, especially in older buildings with poor insulation and heating systems dependent on fossil fuels. Hospitals have higher standards for air filtration and ventilation to prevent transmission of infectious diseases. These systems can be optimized to maximize energy efficiency. Electrifying facilities by switching from natural gas heating to heat pumps would significantly reduce onsite emissions. Investment in energy efficient retrofits of existing buildings and net-zero building design at new construction sites are other strategies that could reduce direct emissions [19].

Fleet of vehicles

Electrification of ambulances, patient transport vehicles, and other hospital owned vehicles is a key strategy to reduce vehicle-related Scope 1 emissions. For example, the US Veterans Health Administration has taken major steps by purchasing zero-emissions vehicles [18].

Anesthetic gases

High global warming potential (GWP) volatile anesthetics such as desflurane and nitrous oxide are a significant source of operating room emissions [20] - 95% of these gases are vented into the atmosphere [21]. Switching to lower GWP volatile anesthetics such as sevoflurane or replacing volatile anesthetics altogether with intravenous anesthesia has been shown to reduce emissions [22, 23].

Scope 2

Energy

Purchased energy is the primary source of Scope 2 emissions under the Greenhouse Gas Protocol [1]. Practical impediments like the cost of replacing older buildings, as well as a confusing array of different state and local mandates for updating energy generation and insulation, could make decarbonization in this area challenging. Reducing reliance on fossil fuel energy sources should be a priority under any decarbonization plan. Viable options include onsite renewable energy generation through installation of photovoltaics on building rooftops and procurement of renewable energy sources through purchase agreements [24]. Success stories include Ohio’s Cleveland Clinic, which launched a $7.5 million Green Revolving Fund in 2016 to finance energy efficient investments, and Utah-based Intermountain Health, which launched a 40-megawatt solar farm capable of offsetting electricity use at 17 healthcare facilities in 2024 [25, 26].

Scope 3

Supply chain

The supply chain is by far the largest emissions category and the most challenging to account for within healthcare organizations since standardized accounting methods have not been implemented widely. Scope 3 healthcare emissions have been described as all purchased goods and services, including but not limited to food, medications, and waste [27]. Reducing supply chain emissions requires action on two fronts: supply-side measures that engage vendors and manufacturers to produce goods with lower emissions, and demand-side measures that improve how health systems select, use, and dispose of the products they purchase.

On the supply side, healthcare organizations have significant leverage as large institutional purchasers. The National Health Service (NHS) Net Zero Supplier Roadmap demonstrates this approach at scale, prioritizing procurement from businesses committed to net-zero goals and transparent emissions reporting, and identifying lower emissions alternatives [28]. US health systems can adopt analogous strategies by requiring emissions disclosures from vendors, incorporating sustainability criteria into procurement decisions, and choosing products with lower lifecycle emissions. Organizations that aggregate procurement across multiple health systems are well-positioned to drive supply-side change by setting sustainability standards with large vendors [29].

On the demand side, health systems can reduce Scope 3 emissions by using their purchases more efficiently. Strategies include standardizing formularies and supply lists to eliminate redundancy, shifting from single use to reusable devices when possible, and implementing inventory management systems that help to decrease waste from expired or unused supplies. Consolidating orders to reduce shipping frequency and using locally sourced or regionally manufactured goods can further reduce freight-related emissions [30].

Waste

US hospitals generate enormous amounts of waste – about 6 million tons annually [31]. Out of the approximately 14,000 tons of waste generated daily by healthcare facilities in the US, roughly one-quarter of that consists of plastic, most of which is not recycled [32]. While reuse is sometimes limited for evidence-based reasons such as infection control, careful steps to recycle, compost, and reimagine supply chain mechanics can be effective steps towards waste and emissions reductions. For example, health systems have explored the use of biodegradable plastic and efforts to reuse materials such as gowns used for personal protective equipment [33, 34]. In 2021, self-reported information from 345 hospitals showed about $157 million in savings when supply waste reduction initiatives were enacted [30].

Medications

Pharmaceutical manufacturing, packaging, and waste represent a significant and growing component of Scope 3 healthcare emissions. The production of active pharmaceutical ingredients is energy-intensive and often involves chemical synthesis processes which create substantial GHG emissions [35]. Packaging can include single-use plastics, blister packs, cold-chain containers, and injection devices which contribute to additional waste and GHG emissions. Manufacturing and distribution have been identified as significant sources of emissions for many drugs, often exceeding emissions associated with administration and disposal [36]. Healthcare organizations can reduce medication-related emissions through formulary stewardship, choosing lower-emissions alternatives when clinically appropriate, and by advocating for sustainable manufacturing and packaging [37].

Metered dose inhalers (MDIs), the most commonly prescribed inhalers in the world, have been identified as a significant source of healthcare-related GHG emissions [38]. The Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality’s Primer on Measures and Actions for Healthcare Organizations to Mitigate Climate Change identifies reducing MDI-related emissions as a high priority measure for healthcare decarbonization in a 2024 report [18]. Because of their high GWP propellants known as hydrofluorocarbons, MDIs have, on average, up to 30 times the emissions of their non-propellant counterparts, dry-powder inhalers (DPI) and soft-mist inhalers (SMI) [39]. Switching to DPIs and SMIs when clinically appropriate, as well as switching to new propellants with lower GWPs, can reduce inhaler-related emissions [39, 40]. Inhaler recycling programs and reusable SMIs are available in England and could serve as a blueprint for the US [41, 42]. While several high-impact studies on inhaler-related GHG emissions have increased awareness in the US over the past several years, there is no cohesive national plan in place for reducing inhaler-related emissions [38, 40].

Biologics have the potential to reduce emissions by improving disease control, but little is known about the environmental impact of these drugs. Manufacturing and administration of monoclonal antibodies has been associated with significant emissions, regardless of administration route [43]. Another largely overlooked area is emissions associated with cold chain shipping of medications such as biologics.

The rapid expansion of the glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) and glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide agonist market is a growing source of plastic waste related to single-use injection pens. Multi-use injection pens that reduce the environmental impact of these drugs are currently available in Europe but not in the US [44]. Of note, Novo Nordisk has started a single-use injectable pen recycling pilot program in select countries across the globe, including the UK, Japan, and Brazil [45]. An oral version of the GLP-1 agonist semaglutide was approved by the US Food and Drug Administration in 2025, providing a lower waste alternative to single-use injection pens [46].

These examples highlight the need for broader pharmaceutical industry commitment to sustainability, such as providing more transparent product emissions data, investing in sustainable packaging solutions, and implementing take-back and recycling programs as standard practice [37].

Transportation

Efforts to reduce transportation emissions could have substantial impacts on reducing the US healthcare sector’s carbon footprint. For example, Stanford Health Care has encouraged green commuting for employees by providing environmentally friendly options such as electric buses, encouraging biking to work, and providing free electric train passes [18, 47].

Food

Food sourcing and management can have tangible impacts on health system GHG emissions and bring concomitant cost and energy savings. Machines such as beverage chillers and cafeteria dishwashers that can enter sleep mode outside of peak hours have led to documented savings for some health systems [48]. Additionally, local ingredient sourcing, made-to-order food processing, culturally conscious promotion of plant-based options, and technology-based applications to facilitate safe repurposing of leftover ingredients have reduced organic waste outflow to landfills [48]. State-level policy changes from Connecticut to California have translated into organic waste management mandates that have further lowered GHG emissions. For example, as of 2025, Section 22a-226e of Connecticut’s Commercial Organics Recycling Law mandates that hospitals and other institutions generating more than 26 tons of source-separated organic material each year must implement efficiency improvements, donate surplus food or collect and divert food waste from the waste stream to composting, anaerobic digestion, or to other organic waste processing facilities [49, 50].

Emerging mitigation opportunities

Telehealth

Telehealth is an area poised for rapid impact on operations-related emissions, however since patient travel is currently considered to be outside of the GHG protocol scopes, its impact may not be captured within GHG inventories. Telehealth has been shown to be most effective at reducing emissions when it replaces in-person visits that require long-distance travel [51]. During the COVID-19 pandemic, Stanford Health Care reduced emissions by an estimated 17,000 metric tons (MT) CO2e when compared to in-person visits [52]. This is equivalent to approximately 4000 gasoline powered vehicles driven for one year [53]. In a separate study from Ontario, Canada, telemedicine was shown to reduce costs associated with gasoline, parking, and public transit in the province by anywhere from $465 to $600 million over a 22-month period [54]. The role of telehealth has contracted somewhat post-pandemic, but it now occupies a noticeable niche within healthcare [55]. While barriers to access also exist with in-person care, telehealth may worsen disparities in those without access to digital tools such as smart phones or consistent internet access [56]. Some initiatives that may help reduce barriers to telehealth include providing digital support, device lending programs, and improving internet access to those in rural and underserved areas [57].

Medical conferences

Annual medical professional conferences are a significant but largely overlooked source of healthcare sector GHG emissions. These emissions are driven primarily by air travel. In 2019, the annual conference of the American Society of Tropical Medicine and Hygiene generated approximately 8650 MT CO2e from air travel alone [58]. Given the hundreds of professional society meetings that occur annually across the US, the aggregate emissions from conferences are likely substantial and typically not accounted for within GHG emissions inventories. These emissions would fall into the Scope 3 category, under business travel [1]. During the COVID-19 pandemic, many organizations successfully moved to virtual formats. While many organizations still offer hybrid formats, in-person attendance is now encouraged or required. While in-person attendance encourages collaboration and allows for meaningful interpersonal connection, organizations can still encourage hybrid formats to reduce emissions associated with long-haul air travel [59]. Decentralizing conferences may help to reduce emissions and costs, while alternating conference sites can encourage in-person attendance for local attendees and decrease long-haul travel. Carbon offset programs can be encouraged and offered during registration for necessary travel [59]. Several strategies can be considered to cut onsite emissions unrelated to travel. This includes choosing recyclable and reusable aluminum or glass bottles alongside water stations to replace single-use plastic water bottles. Offering plant-based, locally sourced meals, eliminating single-use coffee cups, and switching from plastic utensils to compostable or bamboo alternatives can help reduce waste associated with conference meals. Recycled materials can be used for badges and lanyards, with collection bins for recycling after use. Paper handouts and posters can be replaced with digital versions as standard practice. Organizations can also prioritize conference venues that demonstrate environmentally friendly practices [60]. Medical organizations can consider annual GHG emissions inventories for conferences, employ targeted strategies to reduce conference emissions and waste, and report emissions annually to facilitate transparency.

Artificial intelligence implementation

Healthcare systems are rapidly integrating AI applications into electronic health records and clinical workflows, including clinical decision support tools, test result communications, documentation services, and diagnostic imaging interpretation. While AI will likely improve efficiency, reduce burnout, and improve diagnostic accuracy, its energy demands are a largely unquantified source of emissions that have the potential to set back health system decarbonization efforts in the coming years. The environmental impact of AI comes from training AI models and inference or running AI models. Training AI models is energy intensive in comparison to inference, however as AI is deployed more broadly, cumulative inference-related emissions will grow rapidly [61]. AI data centers consume large amounts of water for cooling, which is another drain on resources in addition to energy consumption. As healthcare organizations continue to expand their use of AI, careful consideration can be given to emissions associated with AI. The Sustainably Advancing AI framework offers guidance on managing AI-related emissions. Recommendations include optimizing application design, selecting more efficient models, choosing data centers that use renewable energy, and establishing oversight over AI integration. Moving forward, healthcare organizations can consider including AI-related emissions in their GHG inventories primarily under Scope 2 [62].

Implementation

Implementation of decarbonization programs requires establishing leadership, governance, workforce education, and engaging various stakeholders within healthcare organizations [13]. Standardized emissions measuring, tracking, and reporting will allow for comparison of emissions across institutions and may eventually serve as a means for regulation and mandatory reporting [17, 27]. From a policy perspective, decarbonization efforts could be linked with US Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services reimbursements, providing financial incentives for health systems to implement sustainability programs [63]. Investing in research to improve healthcare delivery and quality while reducing emissions will provide the evidence base needed for health systems to achieve their decarbonization goals. Some US healthcare systems have established sustainability programs which include annual emissions inventories, and some have started to implement decarbonization strategies [63, 64]. (Fig. 1).

Clinicians interested in planetary health represent a wide range of medical specialties and are taking the lead in starting initiatives at their respective institutions. For example, Massachusetts General Hospital’s Sustainability Anesthesia Committee has successfully reduced the use of several high GWP anesthetic gases, decreasing emissions by 75% [22]. Workforce and medical trainee education can include topics like healthcare sustainability, climate and health, and environmental justice in its curricula. The VITALS (visualize, incorporate, thread, advance, localize, sustain) framework is an example of how to guide graduate medical programs in integrating sustainable care pathways into their education [65]. The framework further advocates incorporating a culture of workplace sustainability, standardized evaluation of climate-related items, climate health education, specialty specific initiatives and regionally relevant climate concerns into already existing curricula [65]. Teaching future generations of healthcare providers patient-centered but environmentally sound care could lead to GHG reductions, especially since many are unaware of the environmental impact associated with diagnosis and treatment.

Support from established leadership is also necessary for successful, system-wide initiatives to address climate change and its effects. To further address climate change and the healthcare sector’s role in it, healthcare systems should invest in leadership training in climate change preparedness and sustainability [66].

Costs of deploying decarbonization programs, lack of state and federal programs and incentives such as tax breaks, as well as de-prioritization of sustainability goals secondary to competing foci such as AI implementation may prove to be significant challenges to implementation. However, international health systems such as England’s NHS can serve as examples of successful domestic decarbonization. Through the Greener NHS program, England’s NHS aims to achieve net-zero for its direct emissions by 2040 and indirect emissions by 2045 by mandating emissions reporting, reducing supply chain emissions, and curbing inhaler-related emissions [28]. NHS’s initiative, which was implemented in 2021, shows that national programs that prioritize decarbonization and require mandatory emissions reporting are more effective than fragmented and voluntary health sector action [28].

Co-Benefits of decarbonization

Decarbonization provides returns beyond emissions reductions alone. Many of these concomitant benefits are specific to the intervention and GHG protocol scope it addresses (Fig. 2). For example, lowering Scope 2 emissions by switching to renewable energy sources generates air quality improvements by reducing fossil fuel combustion. Some Scope 1 interventions, such as switching to lower emissions anesthetic gases, can result in immediate cost savings. Other Scope 1 interventions may require investment up front, such as modernizing facilities and fleet electrification, however these interventions lead to cost savings over a longer time horizon [2]. Understanding the resultant short and long-term co-benefits of decarbonization efforts can help health systems prioritize interventions that align with institutional goals.

Health equity and air quality

Reducing healthcare emissions can improve public health through improved air quality and decreased exposure to fine particulate matter (PM2.5) [67]. Vulnerable populations such as children and the elderly, indigenous groups, and low-income communities are disproportionately impacted by exposure to PM2.5, which is associated with increased morbidity and mortality from various conditions, including cardiovascular disease, respiratory conditions such as asthma, and mental health disorders [30, 68].

Climate resilience

Improving healthcare system resilience through decarbonization also improves access to care for vulnerable communities by preventing disruptions in healthcare delivery during climate-related disasters. Sustainable programs often improve climate resilience by decreasing reliance on the external energy grid, leading to fewer power outages, and strengthening local supply chains. Building resilience requires that health systems rely less on supply chains that are vulnerable to disruptions from climate-related impacts [69].

Economic benefits

Healthcare decarbonization can decrease operating expenses through improved energy efficiency, decreased waste of resources, decreased waste disposal costs, reduced transportation costs, and improved infrastructure resilience [30, 70]. One study estimated that transitioning one hospital system in the state of New York to 100% solar energy would bring up to $800,000 to $1.5 million in annual health savings [71].

Workforce well-being

Creating a culture of sustainability can engage and unite the workforce around shared values. Commitment to decarbonization programs requires long-term investment and accountability from health systems which can improve staff and public trust. A recent survey study found that 72% of staff perceived that hospital sustainability initiatives would allow them to feel more satisfied with their job and work culture, while 90% felt that these measures would improve the hospital’s reputation [72]. In a 2023 survey of over one thousand US physicians, roughly four-fifths of the respondents agreed that it was important for their organizations to address climate change, a proportion that was similar across different demographic variables such as gender, organizational tenure, and region [73].

Conclusions

Despite the landmark 2015 Paris Agreement and the worldwide call to action to reduce GHG emissions by the United Nations Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change in 2018, global GHG emissions reached a record high in 2024, the hottest year thus far in recorded history [74–76]. Growing GHG emissions warm the planet and increase the frequency and intensity of climate change-driven disasters which adversely affect human health. The detrimental health impacts of climate change heighten health inequities and disproportionately affect underserved populations in the US which include low-income communities, indigenous populations, the elderly, and children [77]. Climate-related disasters also adversely impact health system operations and ability to deliver care, making resilience a top priority.

Decarbonization efforts are urgently needed from the US health sector, which produces an outsized amount of annual GHG emissions. The US contains approximately 4% of the world’s population yet its total annual healthcare emissions are the second highest in the world at 407 million metric tons (MMT) CO2e in 2022, with China’s total healthcare emissions for that year coming in at 954 MMT [1, 2]. The health sector contributes approximately 6.5% of annual domestic GHG emissions – more than three times the contribution of domestic commercial aviation which accounts for approximately 2% [2, 78]. The recall of the US Environmental Protection Agency’s 2009 GHG Endangerment Finding in February 2026, the legal basis for reducing GHG emissions, is indicative of the federal government’s diminishing role in regulating climate pollution [79]. This shift in policy has resulted in an urgent call for state and local governments and the health sector to take a more active and voluntary role in advocacy and expanding sustainability initiatives [5]. While a framework for successful decarbonization has already been outlined by numerous sources, continuous reassessment is needed to identify and address new and changing sources of GHG emissions such as AI. In addition to reducing emissions, healthcare decarbonization provides measurable returns: lower costs, resilience, workforce well-being, and improved health outcomes for communities that are most vulnerable to the impacts of climate change [80].
