Highly conductive and ultrarobust elastic conductors for stretchable electronics
Authors: Tong Zheng, Shengxin Xiang, Qiongfeng Shi, Jianwei Li, Xiao Wei, Lei Liu, Chenhui Xu, Shengshun Duan, Pinzhen Chen, Xinkai Xie, Litao Sun, Jun Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Elastic conductors are vital for flexible electronics, but the high filler concentrations conventionally required to achieve metallic conductivity severely degrade mechanical properties. Here, we report a poor solvent–induced interfacial self-assembly strategy to fabricate robust elastic conductors. This approach yields a resilient top polymer domain and a bottom liquid metal (LM) polymer interpenetrating conductive domain. Consequently, the conductors achieve exceptional conductivity (3.33 × 106 siemens per meter), extreme stretchability (>1400% strain), and high toughness (>30 megapascals) at a low LM loading (~15% volume proportion). By regulating the self-assembly behavior of LM nanoparticles in elastomers, our method overcomes the traditional trade-off between electrical and mechanical performance. Demonstrating its practical utility, we constructed a wireless stretchable system for monitoring the temperature and motion of living organisms, highlighting its broad applicability in high-performance wearable and implantable electronics.

INTRODUCTION

Developing stretchable, lightweight, and stable conductors is crucial for advancing flexible electronics from traditional flexibility to enhanced stretchability (1–9). The extraordinary properties of intrinsically elastic conductors, such as metallic conductivity (3), strain-insensitive performance (7), and skin-like flexibility (10, 11), exhibit notable advantages in fabrication for next-generation flexible electronic devices, including soft robotics (12, 13), deformable integrated circuits (14, 15), stretchable human-machine interfaces (16, 17), epidermal electronics (18, 19), and implanted devices (20, 21). At the system level, stretchable electronic systems consist of rigid electronic components, stretchable vias, solder pads, circuits, and substrates (22–24). In particular, stretchable circuits based on intrinsically elastic conductors [for example, conductive polymer (10), ionic conductor (25), metal/elastomer composite materials (26), and room-temperature liquid metal (LM) (27)] and fabricated by advanced processing technology [such as 3D printing (28), laser processing (29), stamp (19), omnidirectional printing (28), lithography (4), and transfer printing (30)] have enabled the evolution of conventional rigid/flexible circuits into stretchable circuits on elastomer substrates. Among these intrinsically elastic conductors, LM and LM nanoparticles (LM NPs) have received substantial attention because of their strain-insensitive properties, even under large deformations (31, 32).

To achieve elastic conductors with desirable electrical performance, an effective strategy is to incorporate a large number of LM NPs into the elastomer, forming a continuous bulk conductive network (33). However, an excessive amount of LM is highly susceptible to leakage under mechanical deformation due to its intrinsic fluidity of LM (34, 35), resulting in short-circuit of adjacent wires and waste of LM (36), which limits its stability, reliability, and uniformity for real-world application in wearable electronics. To address this fundamental bottleneck, constructing a biphasic or hierarchically porous architecture within the elastomer has emerged as a promising solution to accommodate fluidic LM and mitigate leakage (7, 37, 38). However, these structural modifications conventionally severely degrade the intrinsic mechanical toughness of the matrix. Achieving metallic conductivity alongside superior mechanical resilience with a minimized LM NP content remains a formidable challenge (table S1).

In this study, we engineer high-performance elastic conductors via a thermodynamically regulated interfacial self-assembly between thermoplastic polyurethane (TPU) and LM fillers. Introducing a poor solvent induces polymer precipitation and wrinkling, which intercepts the gravity-driven sedimentation of LM NPs to yield an asymmetric architecture: a resilient polymer top domain and a dense polymer-LM interpenetrating conductive bottom domain. This structure optimizes interfacial coupling; under minor strain, the wrinkled backbone facilitates chain slip, whereas under severe strain, its controlled localized rupture dissipates stress and preserves continuous fluidic LM pathways. By decoupling electrical and mechanical trade-offs, these conductors achieve ultrahigh stretchability (>1400%), formidable toughness (>30 MPa), and metallic conductivity (3.33 × 106 S/m) at a low LM loading of 5% (v/v). Leveraging these properties, we constructed robust, wireless, and passive stretchable electronic systems for advanced epidermal health care monitoring and human-machine interfaces.

RESULTS

Schematic illustration of highly conductive and ultrarobust elastic conductors for stretchable electronics

Figure 1A compares the differences of three methods to fabricate liquid metal conductor (LM-C). The preparation of elastic conductors in high-relative humidity environments facilitates the replacement of water molecules, solvents, and nonsolvents, leading to TPU polymer precipitation locally. LM NPs settle slowly and preferentially adhere to the porous polymer matrix, forming a porous polymer/LM NP structure [Fig. 1A (a)]. Achieving bulk conductivity in elastic conductors requires a substantial amount of LM NPs, but the breaking strain is constrained by the porous structure and LM NP content (fig. S57, A to C). In contrast to the thermodynamically driven polymer/LM NP structure, gravity-dominated processes cause most LM NPs to settle rapidly, forming highly conductive pathways. The dense polymer layer at the top imparts superior mechanical properties to the conductors. However, weak interfacial bonding, such as between polymer/LM NPs and LM NPs/LM NPs, limits the effectiveness of gravity-driven LM NP settlement [Fig. 1A (b) and fig. S57, D to F]. The introduction of poor solvent into the solvent system induces thermal kinetics and gravity coupling effects [Fig. 1A (c) and fig. S57, G to I], forming a polymer-LM interpenetrating structure. The elastic conductor constructed on the basis of gravity and thermodynamics coupled technology has achieved a fracture strain greater than 1400%, a fracture strength greater than 30 MPa, and a conductivity of 3.33 × 106 S/m (Fig. 1B). These characteristics enable the electronic system constructed by the elastic conductor to withstand various deformations (Fig. 1C) and achieve conformal contact with the skin (Fig. 1D).

Thermodynamic process of solution systems and the formation of LM-C

To fabricate a poor solvent–induced LM-C with a polymer-LM interpenetrating structure, the effect of the poor solvent on the solvent system was examined. Figure 2A illustrates the effect of poor solvents on polymer chains in a polymer solution system. The solution, consisting of tetrahydrofuran (THF) as the solvent and TPU as the solute, forms a homogeneous mixture due to the stronger interaction between THF and TPU, which disrupts the hydrogen bonds within the polymer chains. The addition of poor solvents [mainly ethanol (EA) in this case] promotes the entropy in the solvent system, which regulates the interactions between solvents/solvents and solvents/polymers in the solvent system (Fig. 2B), causing TPU microsegments to aggregate and precipitate (fig. S58, A and B, and note S1). However, these aggregates remain smaller than the visible light wavelength, preventing light scattering and keeping the solution transparent until the solubility limit [~40% (v/v) EA] is reached (fig. S58, C to D). Centrifugation of a 35% (v/v) EA solution further confirmed this, showing a small amount of flocculent precipitate (fig. S58E).

During the solvent evaporation and film formation process in solvent systems, the relative humidity in the environment has a strong impact on the morphology of the film. The film with a high relative humidity environment (~75%) generates a dense, large porous structure, attributed to the rapid evaporation of THF and the complete exchange of THF with surrounding water molecules. A film was fabricated in a high-humidity environment (~75%) for 1 min, followed by placement in a low-humidity environment (<20%). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images confirmed solvent exchange occurring quickly (fig. S1), which were also supported by fluid-phase field simulations (fig. S2). In the presence of poor solvent, the film surface showed a small porous structure. The structural differences between with (w/) and without (w/o) poor solvent are ascribed to the introduction of EA with lower vapor pressure (~7.8 kPa) reduces the overall evaporation rate of the mixture, leading to a structure with smaller pores was formed (fig. S58, F and G). High-magnification SEM images confirmed that the wrinkled polymer microsegments self-assembled to form wrinkled polymers (fig. S58H). The appearance of this microwrinkled structure enhanced the films’ mechanical performance, exhibiting improved maximum strain compared to films without the poor solvent (fig. S3). Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was performed to determine whether a chemical reaction occurs during the formation of the elastic conductors. The results indicated that no notable external functional groups were introduced during the molding process (fig. S4). Furthermore, thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) and differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) were conducted to examine shifts in the glass transition temperature (figs. S5 and S6). The findings revealed that all four sample sets displayed nearly identical characteristics, suggesting that the TPU elastomer molding process does not introduce other substances or functional groups.

Figure 2C illustrates the fabrication process of poor solvent–induced LM-C with a polymer-LM interpenetrating structure, the sedimentation of LM NPs under gravity can be influenced by thermodynamic changes induced by the poor solvent in the solution. The atomic force microscopy (AFM) imaging revealed structural differences during the film forming: Films w/o the poor solvent showed a uniform distribution of hard and soft segments (Fig. 2D), whereas poor solvent addition led to microwrinkled formation (Fig. 2E). Without controlled environmental humidity, LM NPs undergo uneven settlement, hindering the formation of stable conductive pathways (Fig. 2F). The relationship between the proportion of poor solvents (from 0 to 40%) and the formed LM-C is shown in fig. S7, confirming the regulatory effect of poor solvents on the morphology of LM-C forming process. By controlling the proportion of poor solvents and maintaining a relative humidity below 20% during solvent evaporation, the LM-C composite with an interpenetrating polymer-LM structure can be successfully fabricated (fig. S8).

The AFM height image clearly reveals a height difference between the polymer backbone and the LM NPs, highlighting the interconnected structure (Fig. 2G). Cross-sectional images (Fig. 2, H and I) and bottom-view structures (Fig. 2, J and K) of the LM-C composite, together with its corresponding structural architecture, confirm the formation of a robust upper polymer layer and a bottom conductive domain featuring an interpenetrating polymer-LM network. This interconnected network of LM NPs ensures high conductivity, whereas the polymer matrix stabilizes the structure, preventing LM leakage and enhancing stability under deformation. Furthermore, the proportion of LM NPs enriched regions in LM-C after molding was quantitatively described. LM-C constructed with 5 % (v/v) LM accounted for ~15%. Increasing the content of LM NPs can further regulate the proportion of LM NPs enriched regions in LM-C (fig. S9). In addition, by modulating environmental conditions during the evaporation process, LM-C with a patterned conductive path beneath the surface can be fabricated (fig. S10). Optical images (fig. S59A), initial resistance measurements (fig. S59B and movie S1), and leakage performance evaluations (fig. S59C) were used to assess the properties of the conductors. To further investigate structural differences under various processes, LM-C was subjected to brittle breaking and immersion in sodium hydroxide solution to remove the LM NPs (fig. S11).

The LM-C, constructed through the coupling of gravity and thermodynamics, demonstrates a dense polymer/LM NP–coated structure with minimal LM leakage at the conductor edges. Figure S12 and fig. S60 compare the construction processes for elastic conductors and patterned circuits, highlighting the notable advantages of this approach in reducing the content of conductive fillers while simultaneously achieving superior conductivity and mechanical properties. Figure S13 illustrates the silicon elastomer–based LM-C constructed using this process, exhibiting a distinct interpenetrating structure between LM NPs and the silicon elastomer at the bottom, demonstrating that the construction process used in this study can be extended to other elastomers. On the basis of the previous discussion, the process combining gravity and thermodynamic coupling is selected for fabricating elastic conductors in subsequent steps.

Performance and strain-insensitive mechanism of LM-C

The LM-C is prepared using the method of poor solvent induction with varying concentrations of LM NPs. Increasing the concentration of LM NPs leads to a rapid decrease in the initial resistance of LM-C and a reduction in the ultimate strain (fig. S14). It is found that an optimal concentration range of LM NPs [3 to 6 % (v/v)] offers both a favorable polymer zone and a coupling zone between the mechanical and conductive phases, achieving both low initial resistance and high ultimate strain (Fig. 3A). Figure 3B shows the initial resistance and relative resistance variations of LM-C at different LM NP concentrations. LM-C with a high concentration of LM NPs exhibits lower resistance change (fig. S15). The LM-C with LM NPs [5 % (v/v)] demonstrates advantageous mechanical and electrical properties (fig. S16 and movie S2), including high electrical conductivity, large elongation (over 1400%) (fig. S17), and outstanding strain-sensitive performance (R/R0 under different stretching rates further confirm this performance) (Fig. 3C, fig. S18, and note S2). Figure S19A shows a dumbbell-shaped LM-C (with a channel width of 0.5 mm), which exhibits a resistance of 0.2 Ω (measured using a multimeter, with loop resistance also at 0.2 Ω; fig. S19B), whereas the resistance of LM-C with a low content of LM NPs [5 % (v/v)] showed a small reduction (fig. S19C). On the basis of the test results of fig. S19 and the cross-sectional characteristics of Fig. 3I, the conductivity of LM-C was calculated. The conductivity of the LM enrichment region and the overall film conductivity were 3.33 × 106 and 7.69 × 105 S/m, respectively (fig. S20 and note S3). To eliminate the impact of contact resistance on the testing process, dynamic gauge-length scaling measurements based on the transmission line method were performed (details can be found in note S4 and fig. S21). These results confirm that the strain-insensitive characteristics of LM-C is dominated by the intrinsic bulk properties of the composite, not an artifact of the contacts. Figure S22 (A and B) and movie S3 further demonstrate the remarkable toughness of LM-C. The LM-C (40 mm by 5 mm by 60 μm) exhibits exceptional toughness, successfully lifting a 1.5-kg weight. Figure 3D demonstrates the strain-insensitive properties of an LM-C conductor (0.6 mm by 30 mm), and the green light-emitting diode (LED) hardly dims (movie S4) before the wire breaks under a strain greater than 1300%.

Figure 3E illustrates the strain-insensitive mechanism of LM-C, and the initial conductive trace of LM NPs is formed due to the interconnected LM NPs. Stretching the LM-C at small strains causes the connected LM NPs to change shape from spherical to ellipsoidal (fig. S23) and slight wrinkles, whereas the polymer backbone shows minimal changes (fig. S24). This indicates that the Ga2O3 shell of LM NPs bears the stress at small strains by adaptively adjusting its structure, preventing the leakage of LM due to the naturally formed oxide layer, which is ~2.6 nm in thickness (fig. S25). In contrast, repeated stretching of LM-C at large strains (1200%) leads to the rupture of the Ga2O3 shell, forming stable conductive paths (fig. S26). Multiple identical LM-C were subjected to different strains to further confirm the strain-insensitive mechanism. The oxide shell of LM NPs deformed at 25% strain, and some of the outer shells of LM NPs broke at 100% strain, which is consistent with a previous report (33). Further stretching released flowing LM to maintain conductivity (fig. S27).

In situ SEM images capture the entire stretching process of LM-C, and the LM-C retains its original structure under strains of less than 100% (fig. S28). At large strains (~500%), the polymer network undergoes rupture, with the resulting cracks acting as stress dissipation zones, thereby preventing local stress concentration (fig. S29). In the case of cracked polymer networks, the presence of cracks creates sufficient space to accommodate the flowing LM, facilitating its movement under strain. Conversely, an intact polymer network serves as a containment structure for the LM NPs, effectively preventing leakage even when the LM composite is subjected to up to 800% strain (fig. S30). As strain applied to LM-C increases, a more pronounced separation between the two regions occurs. At strains above 800%, the upper TPU polymer primarily bears the stress, whereas the maintenance of conductivity is mainly attributed to the fluidity of the LM. High-strain stretching leads to the complete fragmentation of the Ga2O3 shell (fig. S31), resulting in the formation of uniform conductors upon the recovery of the initial strain (fig. S32). The noticeable wrinkles in LM-C are attributed to partial plastic deformation caused by the inherent properties of the TPU polymer.

Mechanical and electrical stability of the LM-C

Figure 4A shows the loading-unloading curves at increasing strains from 50 to 1400%, with LM-C’s stress showing a stable increase before rupture. Figure 4B presents the corresponding electrical properties, noting a slight fluctuation in the strain range of 50 to 200%. This may be attributed to the ruptured Ga2O3 shell and cracked polymer network. These results align well with the in situ SEM images at strains from 100 to 300% [Fig. 3E (iii)]. Furthermore, LM-C can withstand stress over 20 MPa even when cracks are present in the initial state (fig. S33).

The LM-C demonstrates excellent stability, including environmental stability [restored in water and phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) solution for 8 weeks, respectively] (fig. S34), cyclic stability (loading-unloading strain of 100% for 5000 cycles and strain of 500% for 1200 cycles, respectively) (Fig. 4C), pressure stability (over 20,000 presses) (Fig. 4D, figs. S35 and S36, and movie S5), and mechanical-electrical stability. The latter includes cyclic loading-unloading strain of 600% with a 10-min hold time (fig. S37), cyclic strain from 100 to 600% for 50 cycles (fig. S38), and cyclic loading-unloading strain of 300% at varying stretching speeds (fig. S39). In addition, the signal transmission stability of LM-C is characterized by a change in signal frequency from 0.05 to 10 kHz (Fig. 4E). The corresponding fast Fourier transform (FFT) images are shown in fig. S40, confirming the accurate signal transmission. Waveform data (fig. S41) and the strain exerted on the LM-C (fig. S42) are also provided. The performance of the elastic conductors is evaluated by comparing the mechanical and electrical properties (Fig. 4F and table S1).

The real-world application of LM-C involves its use as a conductor for transmitting audio signals, where the audio signal shows minimal disruption under conditions of strike, press, and stretch (fig. S43 and movie S6). In addition, we demonstrate the feasibility of using LM-C to acquire physiological electrical signals, including electrocardiogram (ECG) and electroencephalography (EEG) signals. ECG signals from a volunteer are continuously recorded using a custom circuit (fig. S44), with stable recording of key features such as the p-wave, QRS complex (Q wave, R wave, and S wave), and t-wave. Thanks to the softness and excellent strain-insensitive performance of LM-C, steady physiological electrical recordings with high signal-to-noise ratios (SNRs) are achieved even under dynamic deformations (fig. S45). Similarly, EEG signals are recorded in real time with a custom circuit (fig. S46A), monitoring the volunteer’s EEG in two states: deeply relaxed and deeply focused (fig. S46B). Obvious differences in alpha and beta activities are observed as the working states change, even under a strain of 60% (fig. S46C). Meanwhile, the skin irritation assessment of LM-C proves that it can be worn on the human epidermis for a long time. (fig. S47).

Taking advantage of the LM-C’s exceptional stability, strain-insensitive performance, and compatibility with laser patterning, we demonstrate the feasibility of constructing stretchable electronics. The circuit can be patterned using LM-C and maintains a sharp outline (fig. S48). LM-C ensures stable electrical and mechanical connections with a surface-mounted LED (fig. S49), even under cyclic loading-unloading strain of 300% (fig. S50A). The device exhibits almost no obvious darkening before being stretched to fracture (fig. S50B and movie S7). In addition, a stretchable circuit was fabricated and demonstrates stable performance under bending, twisting, and stretching (fig. S51 and movie S8).

Radio frequency properties and wireless powering device for stretchable electronics

Wireless power transmission is gaining attention in the field of wearable devices as it removes the need for frequent charging (39–41). In this study, we use a femtosecond laser to pattern LM-C and construct stretchable spiral coils, antennas, and integrated systems.

For the stretchable spiral coils used in the wireless power transmission system (WPTS), the operational mechanism of the two-port WPTS is depicted in Fig. 5A. The magnetic field distribution and dynamic parameters of both the transmitter and receiver coils under radio frequency (RF) excitation are simulated (detailed simulation procedures are provided in note S5), as shown in fig. S61 (A to E). In addition, measurements of key parameters (details shown in note S6), including resistance (R), inductance (L), scattering parameters S11 (reflection coefficient) and S21 (transmission coefficient), as well as the transmission efficiency (η) of the WPTS at varying distances, are presented in fig. S61 (F to I) and Fig. 5B. Notably, a low-magnetic-field intensity is observed at distances below 5 mm, which can be attributed to the overcoupling effect between the coils (42). Furthermore, the WPTS fabricated with LM-C demonstrates excellent stability (Fig. 5C) and exhibits minimal frequency shift (less than 8 MHz) across strain ranges from 0 to 100% (fig. S61, J to M, and Fig. 5D), outperforming silver nanowire-based WPTS (fig. S52). Theoretical calculations, based on Maxwell’s equations for electromagnetic fields, confirm the stable current density within the coil under strain [Fig. 5E(a)]. In addition, the coil, when integrated with a surface-mounted LED, successfully illuminates under stretching [Fig. 5E(b)]. In contrast, most wearable devices exhibit limited stretchability (<30%) due to the use of intrinsically rigid materials, which are insufficient for maintaining conformal contact and accommodating strains (>80%) at the device-skin interface (43). We developed an NFC (near field communication)-based wearable device using LM-C, which maintains stable operation under mechanical deformation. Multifield simulations were conducted to validate the feasibility of the NFC-based device (fig. S61, N and O). The device was subsequently positioned on a volunteer’s chest (Fig. 5F), demonstrating its potential for health care applications (Fig. 5G and movie S9).

Furthermore, a wireless passive stretchable electronic system based on LM-C was developed for body temperature monitoring. This system includes a temperature sensor, a microcontroller, a far-field antenna for data transmission, and a near-field energy harvesting unit (fig. S53). The stretchable system exhibits excellent toughness against mechanical deformations, such as poking, twisting, and stretching, allowing it to be securely attached to human skin and form a conformal wrap (Fig. 5K). To enable long-term continuous monitoring, we redesigned the system for miniaturization (fig. S54 and movie S12; details in Materials and Methods). As a result, we were able to continuously record the volunteer’s body temperature and physical activity during sleep for 5 hours (fig. S55). The fluctuations in body temperature, sleeping positions, and physical activities were tracked in real time, as shown in the corresponding thermal imagery (Fig. 5L).

DISCUSSION

We have successfully developed polymer-LM interpenetrating robust elastic conductors using a poor solvent–induced interfacial self-assembly strategy. The settlement process of LM NPs in elastic conductors is analyzed through the thermodynamic behavior of the solution system. The introduction of poor solvent hinders the evaporation and exchange of solvents, causing polymer microsegments to wrinkle and precipitate. This results in the polymer wrapping around the LM NPs, inhibiting their rapid settlement and forming an interpenetrating structure between the LM NPs and the polymer skeleton. The resulting LM-C demonstrates excellent mechanical properties and metallic conductivity, even at a low LM NP content of 5 % (v/v). Furthermore, the strain-insensitive properties of LM-C ensure high stability under various mechanical deformations, enabling the reliable recording of electrophysiological signals during deformation. We also highlight the potential of this method for fabricating stretchable electronics, with wireless passive stretchable devices based on LM-C that enable continuous body temperature and motion monitoring. This work presents an approach to enhance both the mechanical and electrical performance of LM elastic conductors, with broad applications in stretchable electronics for health care and human-machine interfaces.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Materials

For the preparation of the TPU film and LM-C, EA (Aladdin), THF (Aladdin), N,N′-dimethylformamide (DMF; Aladdin), TPU (BASF), and LM (75.5% Ga and 24.5% In; Sigma-Aldrich) were used. For conducting the stability test, deionized water (Macklin) and PBS (1×, Aladdin) were used. For the fabrication of stretchable LED, Ecoflex is used, which serves as a stretchable substrate. All raw materials were used as received without further purification.

Fabrication of TPU solutions

For the fabrication of pure TPU solutions, 1 g of TPU particles was added to 10 ml of THF solution and stirred continuously at 60°C for 12 hours to form a uniform TPU solution. For preparing the EA/THF/TPU mixture, the EA [0 to 50 % (v/v)] was added to the pure TPU solution.

Fabrication of TPU films

For the preparation of TPU films with large porous, an evenly mixed TPU solution is poured into a culture dish and placed in a fume hood (average temperature: 22° to 26°C; average humidity: 65 to 85%). The sample is left to stand for more than 12 hours, and the solvent evaporates naturally to form TPU films. For TPU films with a small porous structure, 35 % (v/v) anhydrous EA was added to a stirred THF solution of TPU films prepared on the basis of the pure TPU film mentioned above. Then, it was placed back on the constant temperature magnetic stirring table and stirred at 50°C for 3 hours to ensure the formation of a homogeneous mixture. Then, the mixture was poured into a culture dish and placed in a fume hood (average temperature: 22° to 26°C; average humidity: 65 to 85%), allowing the solvent to evaporate naturally for more than 36 hours, ultimately forming a porous TPU film.

For TPU films with dense structure, the solvent evaporation process was strictly controlled. The mixture was placed in a closed constant temperature and humidity drying oven, controlling the temperature at 25°C and humidity at 20% and slowly evaporating for about 36 hours or more to form a thin film. Next, the sample was taken out and further heated on an 80°C heating table for 6 hours to remove the solvent.

Fabrication of the EA/THF/TPU/LM NP mixture

For the preparation of LM-C with LM NPs/polymer interpenetrating structure, we first take 10 ml of THF solution and different volume ratios of LM [5 % (v/v) LM represents that the volume of LM accounts for 5% of the global THF volume], add them to a 20-ml glass bottle, and use ultrasonic (65% power, working for 2.5 s, and stopping for 1 s) to disperse the bulk LM at the bottom of the solution for about 10 min, forming a uniformly mixed LM microsphere/THF dispersion. By studying the particle size distribution of LM NPs under different ultrasound times (fig. S56), LM-C was ultimately constructed using a 10-min ultrasound time. One gram of TPU particles and a 35% volume ratio of EA were added to the dispersion. The, we placed it on a constant temperature stirring table at 50°C and continued heating and stirring for 6 hours to form a homogeneous solution.

Fabrication of LM-C with the porous structure

The EA/THF/TPU/LM NP mixture was poured into a culture dish and placed in a fume hood (average temperature: 22° to 26°C; average humidity: 65 to 85%) to allow the solvent to slowly evaporate for solvent exchange. After standing for 36 hours, the sample was taken out and placed on a heating table for annealing to form a porous LM-C structure.

Fabrication of LM-C with the LM NPs/polymer interpenetrating structure

The EA/THF/TPU/LM NP mixture was poured into a culture dish and sonicated the bottom for about 30 s to remove some bubbles, and then the residual moisture was absorbed at the bottom of the culture dish with mirror paper. The sample was carefully placed in a sealed drying oven, and the temperature and humidity inside the oven were strictly controlled (25°C and humidity at 20%). The solvent was evaporated naturally for more than 36 hours. The sample was taken out and annealed on a heating table to form LM-C with the LM NPs/polymer interpenetrating structure. For constructing LM-C using PDMS and Ecoflex as elastomers, first, PDMS and Ecoflex are mixed in a planetary mixer and diluted with n-hexane, a good solvent. The LM is ultrasonically dispersed in EA, and after being evenly dispersed, it is added to the diluted PDMS and Ecoflex. Then, the mixture is stirred on a stirring platform, and after being evenly mixed, it is poured into a petri dish and placed in a sealed environment to evaporate the solvent.

Fabrication of the patterned LM-C

Various patterns constructed by LM-C are patterned on the basis of a femtosecond laser. The laser parameters are set to a frequency of 50 kHz and a processing speed of 100 mm/s. For achieving high-precision patterning, such as customized circuits, the processing speed is set to 800 mm/s and repeated six times.

Fabrication of the LM NPs/carbomer ink for printing

A 0.7 wt % carbomer solution was prepared by dissolving carbomer in deionized water. The mixture was placed on a magnetic stirring platform and stirred continuously. A 0.1 M sodium hydroxide solution was gradually added to the mixture, monitoring the pH until it reaches 7.0. Next, 95 wt % LM was added to the mixture. The mixture was transferred to a planetary mixer and blended thoroughly until the carbomer gel and LM are uniformly combined into a homogeneous ink. The prepared ink was loaded into the chamber of the printing device, and the chamber was evacuated to ensure complete ink filling without any bubbles. Last, the printing device was used to perform the printing process, with the extrusion volume and speed being controlled programmatically. The printed circuit is placed on a heating platform at 80°C for solvent evaporation.

Fabrication of the LM NPs/oxidized LM ink for screen printing

The LM was poured onto a sheet of paper, and then a glass sheet was used to repeatedly rub the LM across the paper surface. During this rubbing process, semisolid oxides of the LM will be formed. A sufficient amount of these LM oxides was collected to be used as ink for the subsequent screen printing process. For screen printing, a laser-patterned mask plate was used. The mask plate was secured tightly onto a layer of Ecoflex, then the ink was applied evenly over the mask plate using a squeegee. Once the ink has been applied, the mask plate was carefully peeled off to reveal the printed pattern.

Fabrication of the LM NPs/TPU film for imprinting

A mixed solution of THF and DMF was prepared in a volume ratio of 1:3. Ten grams of this mixed solution was weighed, and then 6 g of LM was added. The mixture was subjected to ultrasonic treatment using a cell disruptor for 15 min. Next, 2 g of TPU was introduced into the solution and stir at 60°C until a uniform solution is obtained. The resulting mixed solution was transferred into an electrospinning syringe. The electrospinning parameters were set as follows: a spinning voltage of 18 kV, a spinning rate of 1 ml/hour, and a roller speed of 300 r/min. The electrospinning process was continued until a total of 10 ml of the solution has been spun. Once the electrospun film is removed, a force of 100 N was applied using an imprint mold to perform patterned circuit imprinting.

Fabrication of the stretchable LED based on LM-C

First, a layer of Ecoflex was prepared as a substrate layer (thickness: 400 μm) using spin coating technology. The LM-C film was placed on top, the LM-C was patterned using a femtosecond laser, and two wires were cut for connection with surface surface-mounted LED. The additional LM-C was removed, and the LED was placed between the two wires using tweezers. Then, the self-made LM/TPU ink was used to package the surface-mount pads of LM-C and LED. The device was placed on the heating table for about 6 hours to evaporate the solvent in the ink. Stretchable LEDs were formed with stable electrical and mechanical connections. Last, the stretchable LED was removed for tensile testing.

Fabrication of the stretchable LED circuit, NFC and RFID tags, and wireless passive stretchable system

First, a layer of Ecoflex with a thickness of about 500 μm is prepared using spin coating technology as the substrate, and then LM-C is placed and the circuit is patterned using a femtosecond laser. The additional LM-C was removed, the patch LEDs were placed one by one on the patterned circuit, the LEDs and LM-C were connected with LM/TPU ink, and then they were placed on a heating table for about 6 hours to form a stable connection. Last, a layer of Ecoflex is spin coated on top of the entire circuit as a packaging layer. The circuit was cut into rectangles and connected to an external power source for electrical connection to light up the LED circuit.

For the preparation of stretchable NFC and RFID tags, first, Ecoflex with a thickness of 400 μm was spin coated as the substrate, then LM-C was patterned, and the chip was placed and connect with the LM/TPU ink.

In the evaluation of the rf characteristics of the dual-port WPTS, the coil parameters designed are as follows: The coil has four turns, with a single wire width of 4.5 mm and a spacing of 1 mm between each turn. The designed coil is patterned on an Ecoflex substrate using laser patterning technology for the LM-C conductor, and then a layer of Ecoflex is spin coated on top to encapsulate the LM-C. When testing the rf characteristics, after calibrating the vector network analyzer, an impedance-matched coaxial cable is used to connect the coil to the vector network analyzer, and then the rf characteristics are tested.

For the preparation of a wireless passive stretchable system, the process of the circuit part is consistent with that of the stretchable circuit, but the ink in the stretchable system uses conductive silver paste. The circuits in this system do not require vias, and the connection between the two layers of lines is directly connected to the wires on the other layer through large preplaced pads (due to the single-sided conductivity of LM-C). After connecting the components and chips in the circuit, a layer of Ecoflex was poured on top to achieve encapsulation.

Characteristics and measurements

SEM images were captured with a Zeiss Gemini 300 equipped with an energy-dispersive spectrometer. AFM images were captured with a Bruker Dimension Icon. TG data were obtained with a Netzsch DSC214 Polyma. DSC data were obtained with a NezschTGA209F1. FTIR spectroscopy was obtained with a Thermo Fisher Scientific Nicolet iS20. In situ SEM images were taken with a Phenom XL equipped with a high-precision sample stretching table. Electrical characterizations of conductors were measured with source meters Keithley 2400 and 7510, and a multimeter (VICTOR VC9805A) was used for the measurement of resistance. The mechanical properties were measured with a universal tensile testing machine (ZQ-990LA, ZHIQU). The audio signals were obtained with an oscilloscope (SDS3000X, SIGLENT). The s-parameters were measured with a vector network analyzer (SNA5032A, SIGLENT).

Experiments on human participants

The on-body evaluations of stretchable conductors and system on human participants were conducted according to the National Institutes of Health Guidelines under the protocols approved by the ethics committee at the Jiangsu Institute of Parasitic Diseases [SYXK(Su)2022-0047]. All human participants gave written and informed consent before participation in the studies.
