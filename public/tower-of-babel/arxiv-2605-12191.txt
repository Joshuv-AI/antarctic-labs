Sure-almost-sure and Sure-limit-sure Window Mean Payoff in Markov Decision Processes
Pranshu Gaba, Shibashis Guha

Abstract
Given rationals α \alpha and β \beta , the sure-almost-sure problem for a quantitative objective φ {\varphi} in a Markov decision process (MDP) asks if one can simultaneously ensure that all outcomes of the MDP have φ {\varphi} -value at least α \alpha (i.e., sure α \alpha satisfaction), and with probability 1 1 the outcome has φ {\varphi} -value at least β \beta (i.e., almost-sure β \beta satisfaction). The sure-limit-sure problem asks if for all ε > 0 \varepsilon>0 , one can simultaneously ensure that all outcomes have φ {\varphi} -value at least α \alpha , and with probability at least 1 − ε 1-\varepsilon the outcome has φ {\varphi} -value at least β \beta . Moreover, if simultaneous satisfaction of objectives is possible, then one would also like to construct a strategy (for sure-almost-sure) or a family of strategies (for sure-limit-sure) that achieves this. Even if both sure satisfaction and almost-sure (resp., limit-sure) satisfaction for an objective are known, combining the two is often non-trivial and requires novel techniques and approaches. In this paper, we solve the sure-almost-sure and sure-limit-sure problems for window mean-payoff objectives . While it is known that almost-sure satisfaction and limit-sure satisfaction for window mean-payoff coincide in MDPs, we show that sure-almost-sure satisfaction is distinct from sure-limit-sure satisfaction. The window mean-payoff objective strengthens the standard mean-payoff objective by requiring that the average payoff of a finite window that slides over an infinite run be greater than a given threshold. We study two variants of window mean payoff: in the fixed variant, the window length ℓ \ell is given, while in the bounded variant, the length is not given but is required to be bounded throughout the run. We show that the sure-almost-sure problem and the sure-limit-sure problem are both in P for the fixed variant (if ℓ \ell is given in unary) and are both in 𝖭𝖯 ∩ 𝖼𝗈𝖭𝖯 \mathsf{NP}\cap\mathsf{coNP} for the bounded variant, matching the computational complexity of sure satisfaction and almost-sure satisfaction when considered separately for these objectives. We also give bounds for the memory requirement of winning strategies for all considered problems.

Introduction

Beyond worst-case synthesis.

Classical two-player zero-sum games
[
1
,
22
]
involve decision-making against a purely antagonistic environment where a threshold performance or a specific behaviour of the system needs to be ensured against
all possible strategies of the environment
, that is even in the worst case.
On the other hand, Markov decision processes (MDPs)
[
24
]
model uncertainty, and decision-making involves ensuring a
specified behaviour with sufficient probability
or a
high expected performance
against a stochastic environment.
Such a stochastic model of the environment, however, does not provide any guarantee on the worst-case performance, and a strategy that is adequate against an adversarial environment may provide a suboptimal performance against a stochastic environment.

In practice, both might be desired simultaneously: A system needs to provide guarantee in the worst-case, as well as ensure some threshold performance with a high probability against a stochastic environment.
The beyond worst-case (
\mathsf{BWC}
) framework was introduced in
[
9
]
to provide strict worst-case guarantee as well as good expected performance for quantitative specifications.
Subsequently, in
[
5
]
, the beyond worst-case setting was studied for qualitative omega-regular objectives encoded as two parity objectives, where the first one needs to be satisfied surely, while the second one needs to be satisfied almost-surely or with a high threshold probability.

Window mean payoff.

For objectives such as long-run limits of reward functions
[
20
,
25
]
, a play may satisfy the objective while still exhibiting undesired behaviours over arbitrarily long finite segments
[
13
,
11
]
.
Finitary (or window) objectives enforce that undesired behaviour persist only within intervals of bounded length (windows) along the play.
In this work, we focus on
window mean-payoff objectives

[
11
]
, which are finitary versions of the classical mean-payoff objective.
Given a window length
\ell\geq 1
and a threshold
\lambda\in\mathbb{Q}
, the fixed window mean-payoff objective
\textsf{FWMP}(\ell,\lambda)
holds if, except for a finite prefix, from every position
i
in the play there exists a window starting at
i
of length at most
\ell
whose mean payoff is at least
\lambda
.
The bounded window mean-payoff objective
\textsf{BWMP}(\lambda)
holds for a play if there exists some
\ell\geq 1
for which
\textsf{FWMP}(\ell,\lambda)
holds for the play.

Related work.

The beyond worst-case framework under expectation and worst-case semantics was introduced in
[
9
]
for quantitative objectives.
The problem where the players are restricted to finite-memory strategies was shown to be in
\mathsf{NP}\cap\mathsf{coNP}
for the mean-payoff objective.
The case of infinite memory strategy for the
\mathsf{BWC}
synthesis problem was left open in
[
9
]
and was solved in
[
16
]
.
Further, in
[
16
]
, a natural relaxation of the
\mathsf{BWC}
problem, the beyond almost-sure synthesis (
\mathsf{BAS}
) problem was introduced that asks for a threshold performance almost surely, that is, with probability
1
, while maximizing expectation and was shown to be in
\mathsf{PTIME}
for the mean-payoff objective.
The beyond probability threshold synthesis problem that asks for a threshold performance with a given probability
p
while maximizing expectation was studied for mean-payoff objective in
[
14
]
.
In
[
5
]
, the beyond worst-case synthesis problems were studied for qualitative omega-regular objectives encoded as parity objectives, and the problems were shown to be in
\mathsf{NP}\cap\mathsf{coNP}
; in
[
15
,
19
]
, these problems were studied in the context of stochastic games which are a generalization of MDPs where the environment is both stochastic and adversarial.
In
[
3
]
, Boolean combinations of omega-regular objectives that need to be enforced either surely, almost surely, existentially, or with non-zero probability were studied, and it was shown that both randomization and infinite memory may be required by an optimal strategy.
In
[
4
]
, a combination of parity objective and multiple reachability objectives along with threshold probabilities were considered where the parity objective needs to be satisfied surely and each reachability objective is to be satisfied with the corresponding threshold probability.

Mean-payoff objectives were studied initially in two-player games without stochasticity
[
20
,
25
]
, and finitary versions were introduced as window mean-payoff objectives
[
11
]
and have been studied extensively since the last decade.
For finitary mean-payoff objectives, the satisfaction problem
[
8
]
and the expectation problem
[
7
]
were studied in MDPs.
Both problems were shown to be in
\mathsf{PTIME}
for the
\textsf{FWMP}(\ell)
objective and in
\mathsf{NP}\cap\mathsf{coNP}
for the
BWMP
objective.
The satisfaction problem and the expectation problem for window mean-payoff objectives have recently been studied in
[
18
]
and
[
17
]
respectively for the more general setting that are stochastic games.
In
[
21
]
,
\mathsf{BWC}
synthesis with the expectation semantics has been studied for window mean-payoff objectives in MDPs.

We study two problems for the fixed and bounded window mean-payoff objectives each.

For the sure-almost-sure satisfaction of quantitative objectives such as mean payoff
[

and parity

Preliminaries

Probability distributions.

A
probability distribution
over a finite set
A
is a function
\mathsf{Pr}\colon A\to[0,1]
such that
\sum_{a\in A}\mathsf{Pr}(a)=1
.
We denote by
\mathcal{D}(A)
the set of all probability distributions over
A
.
The
support
of the probability distribution
\mathsf{Pr}
on
A
is
\mathsf{Supp}(\mathsf{Pr})=\{a\in A\mid\mathsf{Pr}(a)>0\}
.
For algorithm and complexity reasons, we assume that probability distributions take rational values.
Probability distributions may also be defined over an infinite set
A
, see e.g.
[
6
]
.

Markov decision processes.

A finite
Markov decision process
(MDP) is a tuple
\mathcal{M}=((V,E),(V_{\bigcirc},V_{\Diamond}),\mathbb{P},w)
, where:

•

(V,E)
is a directed graph with
a finite set
V
of vertices and a set
E\subseteq V\times V
of directed edges such that
for all vertices
v\in V
,
the set
N^{+}(v)=\{v^{\prime}\in V\mid(v,v^{\prime})\in E\}

of out-neighbours of
v
is non-empty, i.e. no deadlocks.

•

(V_{\bigcirc},V_{\Diamond})
is a partition of
V
. The vertices in
V_{\bigcirc}
belong to the system or Player
\bigcirc
(denoted
\mathcal{P}_{\bigcirc}
), and the vertices in
V_{\Diamond}
belong to the environment and are called
probabilistic vertices
.

•

\mathbb{P}\colon V_{\Diamond}\to\mathcal{D}(V)
is a
probability function
that describes the behaviour of probabilistic vertices in the game.
It maps each probabilistic vertex
v\in V_{\Diamond}
to a probability distribution
\mathbb{P}(v)
over the set
N^{+}(v)
of out-neighbours of
v
such that
\mathbb{P}(v)(v^{\prime})>0
for all
v^{\prime}\in N^{+}(v)
(i.e., all out-neighbours have non-zero probability).
We denote by
\mathbb{P}_{\min}
the minimum non-zero probability in
\mathcal{M}
, that is,
\min\{\mathbb{P}(v)(v^{\prime})\mid v,v^{\prime}\in V,\ v^{\prime}\in N^{+}(v)\}
.

•

w\colon E\to\mathbb{Q}

is a
payoff function
assigning a rational payoff to every edge in the game.
We denote by
w_{\max}
the maximum absolute payoff on the edges of
\mathcal{M}
, that is,
\max_{e\in E}\lvert w(e)\rvert
.

A play in an MDP
\mathcal{M}
begins by putting a token on a designated vertex
v_{0}
.
The token proceeds in rounds as follows for all
i\geq 0
.
At the beginning of round
i
, if the token is on a vertex
v_{i}
, and
v_{i}\in V_{\bigcirc}
, then
\mathcal{P}_{\bigcirc}
chooses an out-neighbour
v_{i+1}\in N^{+}(v)
;
otherwise
v_{i}\in V_{\Diamond}
, and an out-neighbour
v_{i+1}\in N^{+}(v)
is chosen with probability
\mathbb{P}(v_{i})(v_{i+1})
.
Player
\mathcal{P}_{\bigcirc}
receives the amount
w(v_{i},v_{i+1})
given by the payoff function and the token moves to the chosen
v_{i+1}
for the next round.
This continues ad infinitum resulting in a
play

\pi=v_{0}v_{1}v_{2}\dotsm\in V^{\omega}
that is an infinite sequence of vertices such that
(v_{i},v_{i+1})\in E
for all
i\geq 0
.
For
i<j
, we denote by
\pi(i,j)
the
infix

v_{i}v_{i+1}\dotsm v_{j}
of
\pi
.
Its length is
\lvert\pi(i,j)\rvert=j-i
, the number of edges.
We denote by
\pi(0,j)
the finite
prefix

v_{0}v_{1}\dotsm v_{j}
of
\pi
, and by
\pi(i,\infty)
the infinite
suffix

v_{i}v_{i+1}\ldots
of
\pi
.
We denote by
\mathsf{Plays}_{\mathcal{M}}
and
\mathsf{Prefs}_{\mathcal{M}}
the set of all plays and the set of all finite prefixes in
\mathcal{M}
respectively.
We denote by
\mathsf{Last}(\rho)
the last vertex of the prefix
\rho\in\mathsf{Prefs}_{\mathcal{M}}
.
We denote by
\mathsf{Prefs}^{i}_{\mathcal{M}}
(
i\in\{\bigcirc,\Diamond\}
) the set of all prefixes
\rho
such that
\mathsf{Last}(\rho)\in V_{\bigcirc}
.

\Cref
fig:naive-strategy-fail shows an example of an MDP; vertices of
\mathcal{P}_{\bigcirc}
are shown as circles and probabilistic vertices as diamonds.
The payoff for each edge is shown in
red
and probability distribution over out-neighbours of probabilistic vertices is shown in
blue
.

Objectives.

An objective
{\varphi}
is a Borel-measurable subset of
\mathsf{Plays}_{\mathcal{M}}

[
23
]
.
A play
\pi\in\mathsf{Plays}_{\mathcal{M}}

satisfies
an objective
{\varphi}
if
\pi\in{\varphi}
.
For a target set
T\subseteq V
, we define some objectives:

•

reachability
, denoted
\textsf{Reach}(T)=\{\pi=v_{0}v_{1}v_{2}\cdots\mid\exists i\geq 0,\,v_{i}\in T\}
, is the set of all plays that visit a vertex in
T
;
we also define some variants of
\textsf{Reach}(T)
:

–

For a subset
F\subseteq E
of edges,
edge-restricted reachability
, denoted
\textsf{Reach}_{F}(T)=\{\pi=v_{0}v_{1}v_{2}\cdots\mid\exists i\geq 0,\,\forall j\geq 0,v_{i}\in T\wedge(j<i\implies(v_{j},v_{j+1})\in F)\}
, is the set of all plays that visit a vertex in
T
and use only edges in
F
until the first visit to
T
;

–

For an integer
k\geq 0
,
bounded reachability
, denoted
\textsf{Reach}_{\leq k}(T)=\{\pi=v_{0}v_{1}v_{2}\cdots\mid\exists i\geq 0,\,i\leq k,\,v_{i}\in T\}
, is the set of all plays that visit a vertex in
T
in at most
k
steps from the start of the play;

•

safety
, denoted
\textsf{Safe}(T)=\{\pi=v_{0}v_{1}v_{2}\cdots\mid\forall i\geq 0,\,v_{i}\in T\}
, is the set of all plays that never visit a vertex not in
T
;

•

Büchi
, denoted
\textsf{B\"{u}chi}(T)=\{\pi=v_{0}v_{1}v_{2}\cdots\mid\forall i\geq 0,\,\exists j\geq i,\,v_{j}\in T\}
, is the set of all plays that visit
T
infinitely often;
and

•

coBüchi
, denoted
\textsf{coB\"{u}chi}(T)=\{\pi=v_{0}v_{1}v_{2}\mid\exists i\geq 0,\,\forall j\geq i,\,v_{j}\in T\}
, the set of all plays that eventually visit only vertices in
T
.

The objectives
\textsf{Reach}(T)
and
\textsf{Safe}(V\setminus T)
are duals of each other, that is, a play satisfies
\textsf{Reach}(T)
if and only if it does not satisfy
\textsf{Safe}(V\setminus T)
.
Similarly, the objectives
\textsf{B\"{u}chi}(T)
and
\textsf{coB\"{u}chi}(V\setminus T)
are duals of each other.

For a rational threshold
\lambda
, we define some more objectives:

•

total payoff
, denoted
\textsf{TP}(\lambda)=\{\pi=v_{0}v_{1}v_{2}\cdots\mid\limsup_{n\to\infty}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}w(v_{i},v_{i+1})\geq\lambda\}
, is the set of all plays with (supremum) total payoff at least
\lambda
; and

•

mean payoff
, denoted
\textsf{MP}(\lambda)=\{\pi=v_{0}v_{1}v_{2}\cdots\mid\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}w(v_{i},v_{i+1})\geq\lambda\}
, is the set of all plays with (supremum) limit-average payoff at least
\lambda
.

In this work, we consider finitary versions of the mean-payoff objective called
window mean-payoff
objectives, which are defined in
\Cref
sec:window-mean-payoff-objectives.

Prefix independence.

An objective
{\varphi}
is
closed under suffixes
if for all plays
\pi
satisfying
{\varphi}
, all suffixes of
\pi
also satisfy
{\varphi}
, that is,
\pi(j,\infty)\in{\varphi}
for all
j\geq 0
.
An objective
{\varphi}
is
closed under prefixes
if for all plays
\pi
satisfying
{\varphi}
, for all prefixes
\rho
such that the concatenation
\rho\cdot\pi
is a play in
\mathcal{M}
(that is
\rho\cdot\pi\in\mathsf{Plays}_{\mathcal{M}}
), we have that
\rho\cdot\pi
satisfies
{\varphi}
.
An objective
{\varphi}
is
prefix-independent
if it is closed under both prefixes and suffixes.
In other words, an objective
{\varphi}
is prefix-independent if it is independent of finite prefixes of the play, that is, it depends only on the infinite suffixes of a play.
For a prefix-independent objective
{\varphi}
and for all plays
\pi
and
\pi^{\prime}
with a common suffix (that is,
\pi^{\prime}
can be obtained from
\pi
by removing and adding finite prefixes), we have that
\pi\in{\varphi}
if and only if
\pi^{\prime}\in{\varphi}
.
The objective
\textsf{Safe}(T)
is closed under suffixes,
\textsf{Reach}(T)
is closed under prefixes, and
\textsf{B\"{u}chi}(T)
,
\textsf{coB\"{u}chi}(T)
, and
\textsf{MP}(\lambda)
are prefix-independent objectives (closed under both suffixes and prefixes).

\textsf{Reach}_{F}(T)
,
\textsf{Reach}_{\leq k}(T)
, and
\textsf{TP}(\lambda)
are closed under neither suffixes nor prefixes.

Strategies.

A (deterministic)
strategy
for
\mathcal{P}_{\bigcirc}
in an MDP
\mathcal{M}
is a function
\sigma\colon\mathsf{Prefs}^{\bigcirc}_{\mathcal{M}}\to V
that maps prefixes
\rho
with
\mathsf{Last}(\rho)\in V_{\bigcirc}
to an out-neighbour of
\mathsf{Last}(\rho)
.
Strategies can be realized as the output of a (possibly infinite-state)
Mealy machine
, that is a deterministic transition system with transitions labelled by input/output pairs.
Intuitively, in each step, if the token is at a vertex
v
, then
v
is given as input to the Mealy machine.
If
v\in V_{\bigcirc}
, then the Mealy machine outputs an out-neighbour of
v
that
\mathcal{P}_{\bigcirc}
must choose, and otherwise if
v\in V_{\Diamond}
, then the Mealy machine outputs an empty string.

The
memory size
of a strategy
\sigma
is the smallest number of states a Mealy machine defining
\sigma
can have.
A strategy
\sigma
is
memoryless
if for all prefixes
\rho,\rho^{\prime}\in\mathsf{Prefs}^{\bigcirc}_{\mathcal{M}}
such that
\mathsf{Last}(\rho)=\mathsf{Last}(\rho^{\prime})
, we have
\sigma(\rho)=\sigma(\rho^{\prime})
.
Memoryless strategies can be defined by Mealy machines with only one state.
A play
\pi=v_{0}v_{1}\dotsm
is an
outcome
of a strategy
\sigma
if for all
j\geq 0
with
v_{j}\in V_{\bigcirc}
, we have
v_{j+1}=\sigma(\pi(0,j))
.
Fixing a strategy
\sigma
and an initial vertex
v
in an MDP
\mathcal{M}
yields a distribution
\mathsf{Pr}^{\sigma}_{\mathcal{M},v}(\cdot)
over plays in
\mathcal{M}
(see e.g.
[
2
]
).
For an objective
{\varphi}
, we have that
\mathsf{Pr}^{\sigma}_{\mathcal{M},v}({\varphi})
is the probability that an outcome of
\sigma
from
v
in
\mathcal{M}
satisfies
{\varphi}
.

Winning conditions.

A strategy
\sigma
of
\mathcal{P}_{\bigcirc}
from
v
in
\mathcal{M}
is
winning for positive-
{\varphi}
if
\mathsf{Pr}^{\sigma}_{\mathcal{M},v}({\varphi})>0
,
winning for probability
(p)
-
{\varphi}
if
\mathsf{Pr}^{\sigma}_{\mathcal{M},v}({\varphi})\geq p
,
winning for almost-sure-
{\varphi}
if
\mathsf{Pr}^{\sigma}_{\mathcal{M},v}({\varphi})=1
, and
winning for sure-
{\varphi}
if all outcomes of
\sigma
from
v
in
\mathcal{M}
satisfy
{\varphi}
(that is, the edge probabilities are ignored and the environment is considered adversarial instead of probabilistic).
If such a winning strategy
\sigma
from
v
exists, then the vertex
v
is said to be winning for positive-
{\varphi}
, probability
(p)
-
{\varphi}
, almost-sure-
{\varphi}
, or sure-
{\varphi}
respectively in
\mathcal{M}
.
In addition, we also say that a vertex
v
is winning for
limit-sure-
{\varphi}
in
\mathcal{M}
if for all
\varepsilon>0
, we have that
v
is winning for probability
(1-\varepsilon)
-
{\varphi}
.
Similar to winning vertices, an edge
e
is winning for positive-
{\varphi}
(resp., probability
(p)
-
{\varphi}
, limit-sure-

Window mean-payoff objectives

In this section, we recall window mean-payoff objectives introduced in
[
11
]
in the context of two-player games.
We see two types of window mean-payoff objectives:

fixed
, in which a window length
\ell\geq 1
is given, and

bounded
, in which for every play we need a bound on the window length.
First, we recall the notion of windows in a play.

Windows.

We extend the definition of
\lambda
-windows
as given in
[
11
]
for arbitrary thresholds
\lambda\in\mathbb{Q}
.
Given a play
\pi=v_{0}v_{1}\cdots
, for all
i\geq 0
, a
\lambda
-window
starts
at position
i
of the play.
The
\lambda
-window starting at position
i

closes
at a position
j
if
j
is the first position after
i
such that the mean payoff of the infix
\pi(i,j)
is at least
\lambda
.
Equivalently, if for all
i<k\leq j
it is the case that the mean payoff of
\pi(i,k)
is less than
\lambda
, then the window starting at
i
is
open
at
j
.
Given
j>0
, we say a
\lambda
-window is open at
j
if there exists an open
\lambda
-window
\pi(i,j)
starting at
i
for some
i<j
.
An interesting and useful observation is the
inductive property of
\lambda
-windows
: if the
\lambda
-window starting at position
i
closes at position
j
, then for all
i<k<j
, the
\lambda
-window starting at
k
is closed at or before
j
.

Fixed window length.

Let
\ell\geq 1
be a given window length and
\lambda\in\mathbb{Q}
be a given threshold.
A play
\pi=v_{0}v_{1}\dotsm
in
\mathcal{M}
satisfies the
good window
objective
\textsf{GW}_{\mathcal{M}}(\ell,\lambda)
if the
\lambda
-window that opened at
v_{0}
in
\pi
closes in at most
\ell
steps.

\textsf{GW}_{\mathcal{M}}(\ell,\lambda)=\{\pi\in\mathsf{Plays}_{\mathcal{M}}\mid\exists j\in\{1,\ldots,\ell\},\ \frac{1}{j}\sum_{k=0}^{j-1}w(v_{k},v_{k+1})\geq\lambda\}

A play
\pi
in
\mathcal{M}
satisfies the
direct fixed window mean-payoff
objective
\textsf{DirFWMP}_{\mathcal{M}}(\ell,\lambda)
if every
\lambda
-window in
\pi
closes in at most
\ell
steps.

\displaystyle\textsf{DirFWMP}_{\mathcal{M}}(\ell,\lambda)=\{\pi\in\mathsf{Plays}_{\mathcal{M}}\mid\forall i\geq 0,\ \pi(i,\infty)\in\textsf{GW}_{\mathcal{M}}(\ell,\lambda)\}

The prefix-independent version of this objective is the
fixed window mean-payoff
objective
\textsf{FWMP}_{\mathcal{M}}(\ell,\lambda)
.
A play
\pi
satisfies
\textsf{FWMP}_{\mathcal{M}}(\ell,\lambda)

if there exists a suffix of
\pi
that satisfies
\textsf{DirFWMP}_{\mathcal{M}}(\ell,\lambda)
.
In other words, the play
\pi
satisfies
\textsf{FWMP}_{\mathcal{M}}(\ell,\lambda)

if from some point on in the play, all
\lambda
-windows close in at most
\ell
steps.

\displaystyle\textsf{FWMP}_{\mathcal{M}}(\ell,\lambda)

\displaystyle=\{\pi\in\mathsf{Plays}_{\mathcal{M}}\mid\exists k\geq 0,\ \pi(k,\infty)\in\textsf{DirFWMP}_{\mathcal{M}}(\ell,\lambda)\}

We omit the subscript
\mathcal{M}
when it is clear from the context.
Note that
\textsf{FWMP}(\ell,\lambda)\subseteq\textsf{FWMP}(\ell^{\prime},\lambda)
for
\ell\leq\ell^{\prime}
as a smaller window length is a stronger constraint.
We also have that
\textsf{FWMP}(\ell,\lambda)\supseteq\textsf{FWMP}(\ell,\lambda^{\prime})
if
\lambda\leq\lambda^{\prime}
, since closing of
\lambda^{\prime}
-windows implies closing of
\lambda
-windows.

Bounded window length.

We also consider bounded versions of
\textsf{DirFWMP}(\ell,\lambda)
and
\textsf{FWMP}(\ell,\lambda)
.
A play
\pi
satisfies the
direct bounded window mean-payoff
objective if there exists a window length
\ell\geq 1
such that
\pi
satisfies
\textsf{DirFWMP}_{\mathcal{M}}(\ell,\lambda)
.

\displaystyle\textsf{DirBWMP}_{\mathcal{M}}(\lambda)=\{\pi\in\mathsf{Plays}_{\mathcal{M}}\mid\exists\ell\geq 1,\ \pi\in\textsf{DirFWMP}_{\mathcal{M}}(\ell,\lambda)\}

Similarly, a play
\pi
satisfies the bounded window mean-payoff objective
\textsf{BWMP}(\lambda)
if there exists a window length
\ell\geq 1
such that
\pi
satisfies
\textsf{FWMP}_{\mathcal{M}}(\ell,\lambda)
.
The
\textsf{BWMP}(\lambda)
objective is also the prefix-independent version of the
\textsf{DirBWMP}(\lambda)
objective.

\displaystyle\textsf{BWMP}_{\mathcal{M}}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\{\pi\in\mathsf{Plays}_{\mathcal{M}}\mid\exists\ell\geq 1,\ \pi\in\textsf{FWMP}_{\mathcal{M}}(\ell,\lambda)\}

\displaystyle=\{\pi\in\mathsf{Plays}_{\mathcal{M}}\mid\exists k\geq 0,\ \pi(k,\infty)\in\textsf{DirBWMP}_{\mathcal{M}}(\lambda)\}

Note that
\textsf{DirFWMP}(\ell,\lambda)
and
\textsf{DirBWMP}(\lambda)
are closed under suffixes, and
\textsf{FWMP}(\ell,\lambda)
and
\textsf{BWMP}(\lambda)
are prefix-independent objectives.
We also note that all of these objectives strengthen the standard mean-payoff objective
\textsf{MP}(\lambda)

[
20
]
.
In particular, we have the following relation between the objectives

\textsf{DirFWMP}(\ell,\lambda)\subseteq\textsf{FWMP}(\ell,\lambda)\subseteq\textsf{BWMP}(\lambda)\subseteq\textsf{MP}(\lambda),

\textsf{DirFWMP}(\ell,\lambda)\subseteq\textsf{DirBWMP}(\lambda)\subseteq\textsf{BWMP}(\lambda)\subseteq\textsf{MP}(\lambda),

and all of these inclusions are strict in general.

)

•

.

, player

\textsf{FWMP}(\ell,\beta)

\textsf{FWMP}(\ell,\alpha)
-almost-sure-
\textsf{FWMP}(\ell,\beta)

.

-almost-sure-

\alpha
-windows in at most
\ell

Description of
IsGoodEdge
procedure.

Given an MDP
\mathcal{M}
, the procedure
\textup{{IsGoodEdge}}(e=(s,t),E_{g})

determines if an edge
e
is good with respect to the set
E_{g}
of edges, that is, the procedure
checks if the edge
e
is winning for sure-
\textsf{DirFWMP}(\ell,\alpha)
-positive-
\textsf{Reach}_{\{e\}\cup E_{g}}(T)
in
\mathcal{M}
.
In other words,
IsGoodEdge
checks if, starting from
e
, it is possible with positive probability to reach
T
through only edges in
\{e\}\cup E_{g}
and also simultaneously satisfy sure-
\textsf{DirFWMP}(\ell,\alpha)
.
The procedure constructs an MDP
\mathcal{M}_{e}
by augmenting some vertices and edges to
\mathcal{M}
, and returns true if and only if a designated vertex
\hat{s}
is winning for sure-
\textsf{DirFWMP}(\ell,\alpha)
in
\mathcal{M}_{e}
.
This reduces the problem of satisfaction of
sure-
\textsf{DirFWMP}(\ell,\alpha)
-positive-
\textsf{Reach}_{E_{g}}(T)
in
\mathcal{M}
to that of satisfaction of sure-
\textsf{DirFWMP}(\ell,\alpha)
in
\mathcal{M}_{e}
, which we know how to solve from
[
11
, Algorithm 2]
.
The correctness of this reduction follows from
\Cref
lem:sdfpr-isgoodedge-correctness.
We describe the construction of
\mathcal{M}_{e}
in two steps.

In the first step, we construct an intermediate MDP
\mathcal{M}_{e}^{\prime}
.
The set of vertices of the MDP
\mathcal{M}_{e}^{\prime}
is
V\cup\widetilde{R}\cup\{\hat{s}\}
, where

•

the set
V
is the set of all the vertices of
\mathcal{M}
;

•

the set
\widetilde{R}
contains a copy
\tilde{v}
of every vertex
v
in
R

(we denote by
\widetilde{T}
the set
\{\tilde{v}\in\widetilde{R}\mid v\in T\}
, that is, the set of all vertices in
\widetilde{R}
in
\mathcal{M}_{e}^{\prime}
that are copies of vertices in
T
, and we have that
\widetilde{T}\subseteq\widetilde{R}
);

•

the set
\{\hat{s}\}
contains a copy of the vertex
s
.

We note that the copy of a vertex that is controlled by
\mathcal{P}_{\bigcirc}
is also controlled by
\mathcal{P}_{\bigcirc}
, and the copy of a probabilistic vertex is a probabilistic vertex.

The set of edges of the MDP
\mathcal{M}_{e}^{\prime}
is
E\cup\widetilde{E}_{g}\cup\widetilde{E}_{\Diamond}\cup\{(\hat{s},\tilde{t})\}\cup\widehat{E}_{\Diamond}
, where

•

the set
E
is the set of all the edges of
\mathcal{M}
;

•

the set
\widetilde{E}_{g}
contains a copy
(\tilde{u},\tilde{v})
of every edge
(u,v)
in
E_{g}

(recall that for every edge
(u,v)
belonging to
E_{g}
, we have that
u\in R\setminus T
and
v\in R
, and thus,
(\tilde{u},\tilde{v})\in(\widetilde{R}\setminus\widetilde{T})\times\widetilde{R}
);

•

the set
\widetilde{E}_{\Diamond}
contains, for every probabilistic vertex
u
in
R\setminus T
, for every out-edge
(u,v)
of
u
that is not in
E_{g}
, a copy
(\tilde{u},v)
of
(u,v)
;

•

the set
\{(\hat{s},\tilde{t})\}
contains a copy of the edge
e=(s,t)
,

•

if
s
(and hence also
\hat{s}
) is a vertex belonging to
\mathcal{P}_{\bigcirc}
, then
\widehat{E}_{\Diamond}
is empty, and
if
s
is a probabilistic vertex, then
\widehat{E}_{\Diamond}
contains a copy
(\hat{s},v)
of every out-edge
(s,v)
of
s
such that
v\neq t
.

When we make a copy of an edge, we preserve its payoff and probability.
Briefly, the set
E
contains edges that start and end in
V
.
The set
\widetilde{E}_{g}\cup\widetilde{E}_{\Diamond}
contains edges starting in
\widetilde{R}\setminus\widetilde{T}
.
The set
\{(\hat{s},\tilde{t})\}\cup\widehat{E}_{\Diamond}
contains edges starting at
\hat{s}
.
The set
\widetilde{E}_{\Diamond}\cup\widehat{E}_{\Diamond}
contains edges starting in probabilistic vertices in
(\widetilde{R}\setminus\widetilde{T})\cup\{\hat{s}\}
and ending in
V
.
We note that if
s
belongs to
R
, then in addition to
s
and
\hat{s}
, the MDP
\mathcal{M}_{e}^{\prime}
also contains a copy
\tilde{s}
of
s
.

In the second step, we obtain
\mathcal{M}_{e}
from
\mathcal{M}_{e}^{\prime}
by identifying, for all
v\in T
in
\mathcal{M}_{e}^{\prime}
, the vertex
\tilde{v}
with
v
.
That is, we merge
\tilde{v}
and
v
into one vertex, and the set of in-edges (resp., out-edges) of
v
in
\mathcal{M}_{e}
is the union of the in-edges (resp., out-edges) of
v
and in-edges (resp, out-edges) of
\tilde{v}
in
\mathcal{M}_{e}^{\prime}
.
We have that
\widetilde{T}
and
T
become identical in
\mathcal{M}_{e}
, and belong to both
V
and
\widetilde{R}
in
\mathcal{M}_{e}
.
In other words, vertices in
\widetilde{T}
in
\mathcal{M}_{e}^{\prime}
are merged into vertices in
T
, and thus, each vertex in
\mathcal{M}_{e}
belongs to exactly one of the three sets:

,
\widetilde{R}\setminus T
, or
\{\hat{s}\}
.

•

If

If

For each probabilistic vertex

For all vertices

\mathbb{P}_{\min}
, and
\varepsilon
such that
x_{N}\geq 1-\varepsilon
holds.
It can be seen that the probability
x_{N}
of reaching
u_{m+1}
starting from
u_{1}
in
\mathcal{M}_{m,p}
after visiting probabilistic vertices at most
N
times is equal to the following:
given a coin that lands heads with probability
p
and tails with probability
1-p
, the probability that a sequence of
N
tosses of this coin contains a streak of at least
m
consecutive heads.
We can compute
x_{N}
using the recurrence relation

\displaystyle x_{N}

\displaystyle=p^{m}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}p^{i-1}\cdot(1-p)\cdot x_{N-i}

\displaystyle=p^{m}+(1-p)\cdot x_{N-1}+p\cdot(1-p)\cdot x_{N-2}+\cdots+p^{m-1}\cdot(1-p)\cdot x_{N-m}

for
N\geq m
, and with initial values
x_{0}=x_{1}=\cdots=x_{m-1}=0
.
Intuitively, a sequence of
N
coin tosses that contains a streak of
m
heads either does not contains a tail in the first
m
tosses (which happens with probability
p^{m}
), or the first tail appears at the
i^{\text{th}}
position (this happens with probability
p^{i-1}\cdot(1-p)
, following which there is a streak of
m
heads in the remaining
N-i
tosses with probability
x_{N-i}
) for
1\leq i\leq m
.
This recurrence relation, however, does not have a nice closed form, so we find approximate lower and upper bounds for
N
such that
x_{N}\geq 1-\varepsilon
.

•

Upper bound:

We divide the sequence of
N
coin tosses into blocks of length
m
each (we assume that
m
divides
N
).
We define
q
to be probability that at least one of the
N/m
blocks consists of all heads.
If one of the blocks is all heads, then this is a witness for the streak of at least
m
consecutive heads in the sequence of
N
coin tosses, and thus, we have that
x_{N}\geq q
.
For each block, the probability that the block contains at least one tail is
(1-p^{m})
, independent of the other blocks.
The probability
q
that at least one of the blocks is all heads is
1-(1-p^{m})^{N/m}
.
We get that
x_{N}\geq 1-(1-p^{m})^{N/m}
.
Further, if
1-(1-p^{m})^{N/m}\geq 1-\varepsilon
, then we have the following:

\displaystyle 1-(1-p^{m})^{N/m}

\displaystyle\geq 1-\varepsilon

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle(1-p^{m})^{N/m}

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(1-p^{m})^{N/m}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\frac{N}{m}\log(1/(1-p^{m}))

\displaystyle\geq\log(1/\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle N

\displaystyle\geq\frac{m\cdot\log(1/\varepsilon)}{\log(1/(1-p^{m}))}

Moreover, for values of
p
not close to
1
(say
0<p\leq 0.5
), we have that
\frac{1}{\log(1/(1-p^{m}))}
is closely bounded above by
\frac{2.4}{p^{m}}
.
Thus, if
N\geq\frac{2.4\cdot m\cdot\log(1/\varepsilon)}{p^{m}}
, then
N\geq\frac{m\cdot\log(1/\varepsilon)}{\log(1/(1-p^{m}))}
, which in turn implies
1-(1-p^{m})^{N/m}\geq 1-\varepsilon
, and the desired inequality
x_{N}\geq 1-\varepsilon
holds since
x_{N}\geq 1-(1-p^{m})^{N/m}
.
Since
m=\frac{\lvert V\rvert-1}{2}
and
p=\mathbb{P}_{\min}
for
\mathcal{M}_{m,p}
, we have that for all MDPs with
\lvert V\rvert
vertices and minimum probability
\mathbb{P}_{\min}
, choosing a value of
N
that is at least
\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\lvert V\rvert\cdot\log(1/\varepsilon)}{(\mathbb{P}_{\min})^{\lvert V\rvert}}\right)
is sufficient to ensure that
W_{\mathsf{S}\beta}
is reached with probability at least
1-\varepsilon
after
N
visits to probabilistic vertices, and this gives us an upper bound for
N
.

•

Lower bound:

If there is a streak of at least
m
consecutive heads in the sequence of
N
coin tosses, then the streak must begin at the
i^{\text{th}}
coin toss for some
1\leq i\leq N-m+1
.
Moreover, the probability of starting a streak of
m
heads at the
i^{\text{th}}
coin toss is
p^{m}
, independent of
i
.
Using the union bound, we get that
x_{N}
is bounded above by
(N-m+1)\cdot p^{m}
, which is bounded above by
N\cdot p^{m}
.
That is, we have
N\cdot p^{m}\geq x_{N}
.
Thus, in order to satisfy
x_{N}\geq 1-\varepsilon
, it is necessary that
N\cdot p^{m}\geq 1-\varepsilon
holds, that is
N\geq\frac{1-\varepsilon}{p^{m}}
.
Substituting
m=\frac{\lvert V\rvert-1}{2}
and
p=\mathbb{P}_{\min}
gives us a lower bound
\Omega\left(\frac{1-\varepsilon}{(\mathbb{P}_{\min})^{\lvert V\rvert}}\right)
for
N
.

Complexity of SLS-
\textsf{FWMP}(\ell)
.

Since sure satisfaction and almost-sure satisfaction of the
\textsf{FWMP}(\ell)
objectives can both be checked in polynomial time
[
11
,
8
]
, we have that
\Cref
alg:sure-fwmp-limitsure-fwmp runs in polynomial time, and we have the following result.

Theorem 29
.

Sure-limit-sure satisfaction of
\textsf{FWMP}(\ell)
objectives is in
\mathsf{PTIME}
.
The memory required is at least
\Omega\left(\frac{1-\varepsilon}{(\mathbb{P}_{\min})^{\lvert V\rvert}}+\ell\right)
and at most
\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\lvert V\rvert\cdot\log(1/\varepsilon)}{(\mathbb{P}_{\min})^{\lvert V\rvert}}+\ell\right)
.

Complexity of SLS-
BWMP
.

Since sure satisfaction and almost-sure satisfaction of the
BWMP
objectives are in
\mathsf{NP}\cap\mathsf{coNP}

[
11
,
8
]
, and the algorithm for sure-limit-sure satisfaction for
BWMP
makes a polynomial number of calls to algorithms for sure satisfaction and almost-sure satisfaction of
BWMP
objectives, we have that sure-limit-sure satisfaction of
BWMP
objectives is in
\mathsf{PTIME}^{\mathsf{NP}\cap\mathsf{coNP}}
, which is the same as
\mathsf{NP}\cap\mathsf{coNP}
.
Moreover, an
\varepsilon
-optimal strategy for the sure-limit-sure satisfaction of
BWMP
objectives follows a similar two-phase structure as that of sure-limit-sure satisfaction of
\textsf{FWMP}(\ell)
objectives.
The first phase requires a counter of size
N
(with the same bounds as before), and memoryless strategies exist for the satisfaction of sure-
\textsf{BWMP}(\alpha)
or sure-
\textsf{BWMP}(\beta)

[
11
, Theorem 19]
in the second phase.
Thus, we have that memory of size
N+1
suffices for an
\varepsilon
-optimal strategy.

Theorem 30
.

Sure-limit-sure satisfaction of
BWMP
objectives is in
\mathsf{NP}\cap\mathsf{coNP}
.
The memory required is at least
\Omega\left(\frac{1-\varepsilon}{(\mathbb{P}_{\min})^{\lvert V\rvert}}\right)
and at most

\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\lvert V\rvert\cdot\log(1/\varepsilon)}{(\mathbb{P}_{\min})^{\lvert V\rvert}}\right)
.

6
Discussion

In the context of reactive synthesis, beyond worst-case synthesis has received considerable attention since the last decade.
In this paper, we continued this study for window mean-payoff objectives which strengthen the classical mean-payoff objective by removing the drawback that there may exist arbitrarily long windows with suboptimal mean payoff.
Our algorithms use techniques that are significantly different from existing studies on beyond worst-case synthesis and our results are positive in the sense that the complexities of solving these problems are no more than that of solving the corresponding sure satisfaction problem and the almost-sure satisfaction problem separately.

As future work, some interesting problems to study include the combination of satisfying a window mean payoff
\alpha
surely (resp. almost surely) and a window mean payoff
\beta
with some threshold probability
p
, or a combination of sure, almost sure, and threshold probability satisfaction, or even a combination of satisfaction with different threshold probabilities, that is,
\alpha_{1}
with probability
p_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
with probability
p_{2}
.
It would also be interesting to see if some of these techniques can be used for the study of other finitary objectives like finitary parity or Streett conditions.
Finally, various combinations of sure, almost-sure, and threshold probability satisfaction can also be studied for classical parity (some of which exist in
[
5
]
and
[
3
]
) and classical mean-payoff objectives.
