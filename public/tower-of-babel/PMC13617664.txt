Quantitative imaging analysis in hypertrophic cardiomyopathy: phenotypic differentiation to prognostic stratification
Authors: S Pradeep Kundur, A Rai, A Kandala, A Malik, S Sivalokanathan, F Jamal, S Jha, A Owens
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) is the most common inherited cardiac disorder and remains associated with sudden cardiac death (SCD), heart failure, and diagnostic uncertainty due to overlap with other causes of left ventricular hypertrophy, including hypertensive heart disease and cardiac amyloidosis. This review aimed to evaluate the evolving applications of radiomics in the context of related quantitative imaging techniques. A review was conducted of studies applying radiomics, texture analysis, machine learning, and deep learning to cardiac imaging in HCM. The evidence base derives predominantly from CMR, with a smaller and more recent body of work in echocardiography and CT. Current evidence suggests that radiomics can extract quantitative features of myocardial texture, shape, and signal heterogeneity beyond visual assessment. These approaches have shown promise in differentiating HCM from phenocopies, identifying myocardial fibrosis without contrast administration, and improving the prediction of adverse outcomes, including SCD and heart failure. Several studies reported incremental value over conventional imaging markers such as wall thickness, global T1 values, and binary late gadolinium enhancement. However, the literature remains limited by retrospective study design, small cohorts, heterogeneity in imaging acquisition and segmentation, and insufficient external validation. Radiomics has emerged as a promising adjunct to conventional cardiac imaging in HCM, with the potential to be adapted into a clinical decision support tool. Its future clinical role will depend on methodological standardization and robust external validation.Keywords: hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, radiomics, cardiac imaging, machine learning, artificial intelligence, sudden cardiac death

Introduction

Background

Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) is the most prevalent inherited cardiac disease worldwide, affecting approximately 1 in 200 to 500 adults.1 Despite its prevalence, HCM is frequently underdiagnosed and remains the leading cause of sudden cardiac death (SCD) in young adults in the USA.2–4

Definition and pathology

Current consensus from the European Society of Cardiology (ESC), American Heart Association (AHA), and American College of Cardiology (ACC) guidelines defines HCM as unexplained left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH) with echocardiography or cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR) showing a maximal end-diastolic wall thickness of ≥15 mm within the left ventricle,5–7 except in relatives of diagnosed patients or individuals with pathogenic variants for whom thresholds of 13–14 mm may suffice. Histologically, HCM is characterized by cardiomyocyte hypertrophy, myofibrillar disarray, and interstitial fibrosis, which underpins diastolic dysfunction and arrhythmogenic risk.6

Current diagnostic pathways and limitations

Diagnosis of HCM is multimodal, integrating clinical assessment, imaging, and genetic testing.7 Transthoracic echocardiography remains the primary tool for detecting LVH and left ventricular outflow tract obstruction, whereas CMR is the reference standard for tissue characterization and morphology.8 Late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) quantifies focal myocardial fibrosis, a surrogate of arrhythmogenic risk,9 while genetic testing supports diagnosis and family screening.10

Despite these tools, important diagnostic and prognostic gaps remain. Hypertensive heart disease (HHD), cardiac amyloidosis (CA), and athlete's heart can mimic HCM11 yet require different management, making differentiation essential. Histology with biopsy remains the definitive method of characterization, highlighting the need for non-invasive alternatives.

The role of SCD risk

Current guidelines prioritize the prevention of SCD in HCM management, recognizing fibrosis as an incremental marker of arrhythmic risk, although its role in implantable cardioverter defibrillator (ICD) decision-making, which remains the most effective strategy to mitigate the risk of malignant arrhythmias,5,12 differs across guidelines. ACC/AHA considers extensive LGE (>15% LV mass) a Class IIb indication for ICD implantation,13 whereas ESC guidelines recommend shared decision-making in intermediate-risk individuals.12 Despite its widespread use, LGE lacks a strong negative predictive value. Although contemporary risk-prediction strategies have reduced SCD rates,14 residual risk persists. Functional markers such as myocardial strain detect early mechanical dysfunction but not tissue composition. Likewise, binary LGE and maximal wall thickness do not capture the diffuse microstructural abnormalities that contribute to arrhythmogenesis, reinforcing the need for complementary structural imaging biomarkers.

The scope for radiomics

Current diagnostic frameworks rely on limited descriptors such as wall thickness, ejection fraction, and binary LGE. Radiomics addresses this by extracting quantitative features capturing myocardial texture, shape, and signal heterogeneity beyond visual assessment. Although the evidence base remains weighted towards CMR, where myocardial tissue characterization is most mature, this review synthesizes radiomics applications across cardiac imaging,15 incorporating the emerging but still limited literature in echocardiography and CT. By contrasting engineered radiomics with traditional analysis and data-driven deep learning (DL), we evaluate its capacity to bridge diagnostic gaps, reduce the burden of SCD, and overcome the standardization barriers requisite for clinical translation.

Methods

This structured review was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA-ScR guidelines (Figure 1). A comprehensive literature search was performed in PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, and Scopus from database inception to September 2025. The search strategy combined MeSH and free-text keywords across three domains: clinical condition, computational methodology, and imaging modalities. Studies were screened using predefined inclusion criteria, including peer-reviewed original studies applying radiomics, texture analysis, or machine learning to cardiac imaging in HCM with reported diagnostic or prognostic performance metrics. Reference lists of relevant articles were screened to identify additional eligible studies. Two reviewers independently screened studies and extracted data, resolving discrepancies by consensus. Extracted data included study characteristics, sample size, imaging modality, feature extraction methods, classification approaches, and key diagnostic or prognostic outcomes. A description of the included studies can be found in Tables 1–3.

PRISMA ScR flowchart. Records were identified through searches of Embase, MEDLINE, and Scopus. Following removal of duplicate records, titles and abstracts were screened, with eligible full-text articles assessed against the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Reasons for full-text exclusion included incorrect study population, absence of quantitative radiomics methodology, inappropriate publication type, and insufficient data. A total of 23 studies met the eligibility criteria and were included in the final systematic review.

Radiomics: concepts and pipeline

Image acquisition

The radiomics workflow begins with image acquisition. Consistency in acquisition parameters is essential to minimize technical variability. Reviews emphasize that image acquisition parameters (e.g. contrast, voxel size, reconstruction) remain the most influential sources of variance in radiomics, and that quality checklists should ensure strict standardization before features are extracted.39 Pre-processing improves feature comparability.

Segmentation

Segmentation defines regions of interest for feature extraction, typically the hypertrophied myocardium or fibrotic regions. While manual segmentation is widely used, it introduces inter-observer variability and limits scalability.39 Automated deep learning approaches, particularly U-Net architectures, provide improved reproducibility and efficiency,40 achieving near-expert performance.41–43 However, segmentation in HCM remains challenging due to asymmetric hypertrophy and complex ventricular geometry, necessitating robust validation for downstream analysis.

Feature selection and extraction

Radiomic features include first-order statistics (mean, variance, entropy) and higher-order texture features from gray-level matrices.39 PyRadiomics is commonly used for feature extraction. Given the high dimensionality, feature selection helps prevent overfitting.44 Dimensionality reduction techniques, such as Principal Component Analysis (PCA), can be applied to transform the dataset by mathematically combining correlated features into a smaller set of artificial components. While effective at reducing data size, PCA sacrifices clinical interpretability, as the resulting components cannot be mapped back to specific biological textures. This restricts analysis to the most robust and meaningful descriptors, retaining only features with a high intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) across repeated scans.45

Conversely, feature selection algorithms retain the original, interpretable features by isolating the most predictive subset. Common techniques include filter methods, such as minimum redundancy maximum relevance (mRMR) and embedded methods like the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO), which applies an L1 regularization penalty to shrink the coefficients of uninformative features to zero. Modern radiomics pipelines strongly favour feature selection over pure dimensionality reduction to maintain biological transparency. Yet recently, Bayesian feature selection is gaining traction for its utility in estimating feature inclusion and quantifying uncertainty.46

Statistical models

Following feature selection, models are applied to classify aetiologies or predict outcomes. While logistic regression and support vector machines remain common, recent studies increasingly favour ensemble classifiers. Notably, Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) utilizes gradient-boosted decision trees to manage complex, non-linear feature interactions, frequently outperforming traditional classifiers. To counteract the inherent opacity of these advanced models, modern workflows integrate SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP). Grounded in cooperative game theory, SHAP quantifies the exact contribution of each radiomic feature to a patient's specific prediction,47 evolving complex computations into transparent clinical decision-support tools.

Concurrently, deep learning frameworks such as Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) can extract spatial texture directly from images,48 while U-Net architectures streamline automated boundary segmentation. Model efficacy is rigorously evaluated using conventional metrics, such as the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), sensitivity, and specificity, alongside calibration and decision curve analyses.49 A schematic framework of these processes is illustrated in Figure 2.

Radiomics pipeline in hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. Cardiac images (CMR, TTE or CT) are segmented to delineate the myocardial region of interest (Step 1), from which high-throughput quantitative features are extracted, either hand-crafted radiomic features or deep learning representations (Step 2). Feature selection or dimensionality reduction mitigates overfitting (Step 3) before classification with conventional or ensemble machine learning models (Step 4). Outputs may inform clinical decisions, such as contrast-sparing imaging or ICD implantation, and interpretability methods such as SHAP identify the features that drive each prediction (Step 5).

Deep learning vs. hand-crafted radiomics

A recurring methodological question across the literature is the distinction in the diagnostic performance between workflows incorporating hand-crafted radiomic features compared to end-to-end deep learning. Hand-crafted radiomics is derived from a predefined library of mathematical descriptors that can be traced to a specific first-order, shape, or higher-order textural feature. When these are paired with subsequent statistical methods of selection, the contribution of individual features to a given prediction output can be interrogated and retraced to known pathophysiological mechanisms. However, deep learning, by contrast, learns hierarchical representations directly from the image, frequently achieving superior discrimination at the cost of interpretability, as the resulting features map less closely to explainable concepts and underlying mechanisms of disease.50,51 Studies have evaluated these studies against each other as well as combining them across the range of applications outlined in this review.

Phenotypic differentiation of left ventricular hypertrophy

Differentiation of HCM from phenocopies such as hypertensive heart disease (HHD), cardiac amyloidosis (CA), and other cardiomyopathies remains a clinical problem,52 with misclassification rates of up to one-third reported in cohort studies.53,54 Furthermore, in older adults, aortic stenosis represents a common further phenocopy that must be excluded. CMR and transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) reveal hypertrophy and fibrosis but remain limited by substantial phenotypic overlap in gross imaging findings.55 Even global T1 mapping from CMR images to quantify diffuse interstitial fibrosis offers modest discriminatory ability28 compared to radiomics models, which quantify myocardial texture and shape (Table 1).

Differentiating HCM from HHD

HCM and HHD both feature cardiomyocyte hypertrophy and interstitial fibrosis, but only HCM demonstrates myofibrillar disarray and patchy replacement fibrosis.56 These microstructural differences are difficult to resolve with standard imaging. Misclassification has important consequences. Underrecognition of HCM delays family screening and SCD risk mitigation, whereas mislabelling HHD as HCM burdens the patient with unnecessary evaluation of a non-inherited condition.57

Echocardiography

Echocardiography (TTE), the first-line investigation for left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH), is limited by operator dependence and poor tissue characterization.58 Radiomics mitigates this by capturing myocardial textural differences, thereby improving aetiological classification without requiring additional imaging.

In a pilot study of four-chamber apical TTE, Yu et al.26 identified the interventricular septum (IVS) as a region of interest (ROI) and applied a Support Vector Machine (SVM) classifier to differentiate HCM, HHD, and uraemic cardiomyopathy. Six of 77 features proved significant (P < 0.05) and reproducible (ICC > 0.50). HCM exhibited the most homogeneous texture, with EtBrt, Std, and CoV yielding AUCs up to 0.87 and accuracies of 83–84%. Advancing to cine loops with automated ResUNet-based IVS segmentation, Xu et al.22 extracted temporal and frequency features. Their Boltzmann and SVM models achieved AUCs of 0.84 ± 0.05 (HHD vs. HCM) and 0.87 ± 0.04 (HCM vs. UCM), alongside >82% accuracy and up to 91% specificity, demonstrating the viability of automated pipelines.

In the largest multi-centre study to date (n = 1486), Moon et al.16 deployed an AI-automated LightGBM model, achieving 96% overall accuracy and AUCs of 0.96 (HCM), 0.89 (cardiac amyloidosis [CA]), and 0.86 (HHD), outperforming conventional parameter-based logistic regression. Dominant predictive features included harmonization-driven textural heterogeneity and myocardial thickness (HCM), run-length non-uniformity (CA), and shape (HHD). Although external validation was limited to a single centre and lacked cross-modality comparisons, these findings establish echocardiographic radiomics as a robust, low-cost diagnostic tool.

Cardiac MRI

Neisius et al. evaluated native T1 radiomics in patients with HCM, HHD, and control patients compared with mean T1 values alone.28 In 232 patients (53 HHD, 108 HCM, and 71 controls) scanned with free-breathing native T1 mapping CMR at 1.5T, the entire LV myocardium was segmented manually by one primary reader and repeated by two others to select over 200 texture features. The final model achieved a diagnostic accuracy of 80.0% (c-statistic: 0.89; 95% CI: 0.77 to 1.00) in the validation set, outperforming global T1 mapping with a modest accuracy of 64% (c-statistic: 0.549; 95% CI: 0.452 to 0.640). Although this study was limited by its single-centre approach and manual segmentation, it provided early proof-of-concept evidence that T1 radiomics captures disease-specific textural patterns.59,60

Zhang et al. combined cine and LGE radiomics in a large cohort of 421 HCM and 200 HHD patients. Semi-automated segmentation using DL-based commercial software (cvi42 version 5.11.2) was applied to short-axis cine and LGE images from 3T CMR.17 PyRadiomics was used to extract a total of 1409 initial features with iterative selection identifying 20 optimal features by multivariate logistic regression. Logistic regression models incorporated clinical findings, cine and LGE-derived radiomics scores (radscores), and conventional imaging findings, achieving an AUC of 0.98 (CI: 0.97–0.99) in a training group and 0.98 (0.97–1.00) in a validation group, significantly outperforming models using CMR findings alone. The need for labour-intensive LGE segmentation was highlighted as a primary barrier to clinical translation.

Conversely, another trial by Liu et al.19 considered whether ROIs beyond the myocardium, such as the papillary muscle, should be considered for differentiation. Utilizing cine CMR at 3T, end-diastolic images were analysed to derive two distinct ROIs, the myocardium (MYO) and the papillary muscles (PM), for two tasks: detection of LVH and differentiation between HCM and HHD. Following redundancy reduction and feature selection with an SVM classifier, six features were used: gradient gray-level co-occurrence matrix (GLCM) correlation, original shape sphericity, original shape elongation, wavelet-LHL GLCM Imc1, log-sigma-5-0-mm-3D glszm, ZoneEntropy, and wavelet-LHH GLCM MCC. For detection of LVH, the model developed with the MYO features achieved the highest AUC (0.966 vs. 0.944 (MYO + PM) and 0.772 (PM), P = 0.924 and P < 0.001, respectively); but precision and recall were slightly higher in the MYO + PM group. However, for differentiation of LVH aetiology, the MYO + PM model achieved an AUC of 0.94, significantly outperforming conventional CMR (P = 0.002) and radiomics with the myocardium alone (AUC 0.91).

Building on these advances, Wang et al.38 shift the methodological focus from hand-crafted radiomic features to deep learning (ResNet-32) with native T1 maps. A CNN using a myocardial-ring ROI achieved the best performance (AUC 0.83, 95% CI: 0.70–0.96), surpassing global T1 (AUC 0.55; CI: 0.35–0.74) and radiomics classifiers (AUC 0.80; 95% CI: 0.66–0.94), remaining among the few studies that compare deep learning to radiomics.

HCM vs. cardiac amyloidosis

CA is another key phenocopy, which can present with similar imaging findings of concentric LVH. Moon et al. recently demonstrated the feasibility of echocardiography radiomics for the differentiation of this condition,16 but CMR radiomics has equally shown promise.

In a 216-patient cohort study (50 HCM, 52 CA, 68 aortic stenosis, 15 HHD, 31 controls), a histogram-based technique was applied to cine CMR mid–short axis frames.29 Reproducibility remained high in this study with an ICC of 0.9, and comparisons revealed that kurtosis and skewness were key identifiers for CA, due to underlying myocardial infiltration. Whereas entropy best represented HCM due to a heterogeneous distribution of myocardial disarray. Building on these results, Jiang et al.20 studied a larger cohort of 251 patients (82 HCM, 85 CA, 84 controls) with 3.0T cine MRI. The key improvement was the use of a high-dimensional radiomic pipeline integrated with machine learning classifiers. A total of 275 features were extracted from each ROI, defined as the LV myocardium and manually segmented by two radiologists. After LASSO was applied to prevent overfitting, nineteen features were selected for analysis. The best-performing model, with radiomics and CMR, achieved a superior discriminatory performance (AUC = 0.89) over the CMR findings alone (AUC = 0.79). Again, in this study, SVM stood out as the best classifier, and run-length non-uniformity (GLevNonU) emerged as a powerful texture-based discriminator.

These studies converge on the insight that cine radiomics can capture subtle histological differences between the two conditions. Clinically, aside from the feasibility of cine MRI as a contrast-free diagnostic tool, the microstructural insight offered by radiomics enables CMR to highlight the possibility of these techniques acting as a surrogate ‘virtual biopsy’ to visualize the signature patterns of disease at a tissue level.

Classification of other cardiomyopathies

A 118-patient study by Izquierdo et al. further sought to distinguish HCM from other cardiomyopathies (HCM = 25, DCM = 37, LV non-compaction = 35, and healthy = 21), whilst providing valuable context for refining use cases for quantitative imaging methods.23 All participants were scanned on 1.5T Siemens systems with a standard short-axis cine stack. In total, 420 radiomics features were extracted from the LV myocardium and LV cavity at end diastole (ED) and end systole (ES). A machine learning approach included classifiers such as one-vs.-rest SVMs, multinomial logistic regression, and random forest, yielding the best-performing model (random forest), which reached an overall AUC of 0.96 and a one-vs.-rest AUC of 0.95 for HCM. Remarkably, in this study, the performance of the radiomics model was similar and statistically insignificant when compared to standard CMR indices for differentiation, yet the authors maintain the relevance of their model as it achieved considerable AUC without the effort of manual delineation of the trabeculae, simplifying segmentation to the LV cavity and compact myocardium only. However, this equivalent result may be an intrinsic fallacy of the quantitative models when differentiating morphologically distinct diseases. For example, when the gross morphological changes between the asymmetric LV hypertrophy in HCM compared to the dilatation and non-compaction in DCM are already evident, the macroscopic images may already capture the majority of differences between the aetiologies, leaving little residual variance for intricate textural features to extricate. This raises the issue of high-dimensionality methods offering liability rather than added value in such situations where classifiers risk modelling scanner-specific noise or artefacts in place of biologically meaningful differences, discussed in the literature as ‘the curse of dimensionality’.44,45

Larger datasets have optimized the use of radiomics algorithms in cardiomyopathy differentiation. Zhang et al. analysed 238 subjects (HCM = 48, dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) = 52, healthy controls = 123).18 Imaging with standard short-axis cine CMR, comprising ED and ES frames, was used for feature extraction. ROIs comprised the LV cavity, RV cavity, and LV myocardium. A 9-feature model achieved 91.2% accuracy with per-class AUCs of 0.94 (healthy controls), 0.97 (DCM), and 0.94 (HCM). Notably, this study also found that features derived from the right ventricle contributed sparingly to the distinction between DCM and HCM, with myocardial and LV cavity features dominating the radiomic signature, identifying specific ROIs offering valuable information beyond macroscopic indices. Despite a multi-centre approach, the authors highlighted limitations such as reliance on pre-provided contours in public datasets for ROI delineation, preventing the calculation of intraobserver variability, and a lack of patient-based features such as age, gender, or weight, restricting further analysis. Overall, these findings encourage the consideration of an optimized diagnostic workflow for patients with LVH incorporating radiomics to determine the true underlying etiology (Figure 3).

Clinical integration pathway for radiomics in left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH) assessment. This figure illustrates a proposed workflow for incorporating radiomics into the diagnostic evaluation of patients with left ventricular hypertrophy or unexplained myocardial wall thickening. Following initial clinical assessment with electrocardiography, transthoracic echocardiography, and routine blood tests, patients undergo comprehensive cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) imaging, including cine sequences, T1/T2 mapping, and late gadolinium enhancement (LGE). Detection of fibrosis and gadolinium-sparing imaging

Myocardial fibrosis is a central component of the arrhythmogenic risk in HCM, quantified by LGE-CMR. Nearly 50% of patients with HCM are LGE-negative,61 but international guidelines suggest 2–5 yearly contrast-enhanced CMR scans for SCD risk stratification as a class IIb recommendation.7,12 Safety concerns have been raised with gadolinium, such as the risk of nephrogenic systemic fibrosis in patients with renal dysfunction, deposition in tissues such as the brain with repeated exposure.62 Whilst the insights offered by LGE are unparalleled, studies have investigated whether radiomics can identify fibrosis without contrast, enabling safer imaging strategies (Table 2).

Cine MRI as a surrogate for LGE

Cine CMR is widely available and may contain tissue-level information detectable through radiomics. Mancio et al. were among the first to demonstrate this, applying machine learning to develop a model from a large multi-center dataset, externally validated on a second cohort.32 Images were automatically segmented to delineate LV endocardial and epicardial borders (cvi42V R DL method) with manual adjustment by an expert cardiologist. Following feature extraction, dimensionality reduction and classification (XGBoost), a seven-feature model was tested, yielding an AUC of 0.83 (95% CI: 0.77–0.89) with a sensitivity of 91% and specificity of 62%.

In a comprehensive head-to-head study, Fahmy et al.30 compared radiomics, deep learning, and hybrid models in a multi-centre cohort of 759 patients scanned with 1.5T MRI. This study also employed automatic segmentation of the LV (cvi42 image analysis package) with manual validation. Using the Pyradiomics feature extraction library, a total of 944 radiomic features were extracted from each image, with LASSO identifying the ten best performers. A deep learning model comprising convolutional neural networks (CNN) and a fully connected network (FCN) was trained end-to-end and combined or compared with the radiomics approach. The combined deep learning and radiomics model achieved the highest AUC (0.83 vs. 0.77 (deep learning), P = 0.006 and 0.78 (radiomics), P = 0.05). Furthermore, it correctly identified 43% and 28% of patients without scar in the internal and external datasets.

Recently, Pu et al.31 refined the approach by trialling LV-wide cine radiomics. 273 HCM patients scanned with 1.5T CMR were included in training and test sets at a ratio of 7:3. One radiologist manually segmented LV myocardium with assessment of interclass variability by an independent expert. Slices of the LV myocardium and maximum wall thickness (MWT) were considered, with 1688 and 1130 features extracted from the entire LV myocardium and MWT slice, respectively. Following dimensionality reduction with the Boruta algorithm and machine learning-aided classification (XGBoost), three features were selected from the MWT slice and five from the LV myocardium. Results showed that the integrated model combining conventional CMR parameters and radiomic features performed the best for detecting fibrosis (AUC of 0.90, accuracy 89.02%, sensitivity 92.54%). Despite the retrospective design and manual segmentation, this established that considering the whole LV myocardium is more reliable than slice-based models.

Native T1 radiomics to identify scar-negative patients

While cine radiomics leverages motion and intensity patterns, native T1 mapping evaluates a direct tissue signal. Neisius et al.33 investigated whether radiomic texture analysis of native T1 maps could detect scar-specific heterogeneity invisible to mean T1 values, hence identifying patients with a low probability of scar in whom gadolinium could be avoided. Using native T1 mapping at 1.5T, the scans of 188 HCM patients were segmented to delineate the LV myocardium with LGE-positive areas labelled for comparison. Four texture descriptors, namely, histogram, run-length, co-occurrence, and local binary patterns, were extracted, and selection revealed five features with the best discriminatory capacity for LGE, which were entered into a decision-tree ensemble classifier. This model achieved a considerable c-statistic of 0.75 in cross-validation and 0.74 in an independent cohort, but most crucially, it was able to correctly identify 100% of LGE-positive patients and classified 37% of LGE-negative patients as scar-free. Avoiding contrast in up to one-third of patients whilst ensuring no scar-positive patients are missed, ascertains T1 as a powerful triage tool.

Fibrosis assessment beyond CMR

Qin et al. investigated CT as an alternative to CMR by retrospectively enrolling 161 HCM patients who underwent coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA) and CMR scanning.34 The LV myocardium was segmented according to the AHA 17-segment models, with each ROI yielding 1591 features. After excluding features with poor reproducibility and mitigating overfitting, CNNs and random forests were compared to develop models using combinations of radiomic signatures, clinical features, and echocardiography data. The results showed that the radiomic model achieved an AUC of 0.78 in external testing for the detection of LGE-positive segments, whereas the integrated model, comprising clinical and TTE findings as well, achieved great discrimination for extensive fibrosis (LGE/LV mass ≥ 15%) with an AUC of 0.92. The authors noted SumEntropy, Maximum (wavelet), and ClusterProminence as the top three predictive features capturing the heterogeneity of CT pixel values.

Echocardiography has also been studied for detecting fibrosis. Apical five-chamber views can be studied to train deep convolutional neural networks (DCNN) to classify the presence of LGE. In one study, a logistic regression model built from clinical and echocardiography parameters was integrated with the DCNN-derived possibility of fibrosis.63 This integrated model achieved an AUC of 0.86, with the authors including decision curve analysis to confirm the net clinical benefit of their model.

SCD risk prediction and prognostic modelling

Considering that SCD remains the most feared complication of HCM, advanced risk stratification strategies remain in continuous development. Whilst current guideline-recommended risk calculators rely on limited imaging metrics with poor sensitivity, radiomics holds the potential to quantify the heterogeneity of scar in LGE-positive areas and simultaneously evaluate microstructural textural patterns, providing more sensitive biomarkers for integration into prognostic models (Table 3).

Wang et al. showed that radiomic texture features from LGE images independently predicted SCD events in 379 patients over long-term follow-up over 29 months even after adjusting for extensive LGE burden (≥15%).24 11/157 features were identified as significant predictors, with local binary patterns (LBP-19) and Moment (1) emerging as the strongest, with a hazard ratio of 1.21 and 1.08, respectively. Patients with high values (LBP ≥ 4.277, Moment ≥ 4.262) experienced markedly higher rates of SCD events, identifying individuals who would otherwise have been overlooked and proving that scar heterogeneity and not just extent must be considered in risk.

Fahmy et al. expand this observation to a multi-centre cohort, extracting features from 1229 HCM patients’ LV LGE images.37 1888 radiomic features were identified, undergoing principal component analysis to shortlist the top 3 components (PrinRads), maintaining a ratio of 1 predictor to every 10 SCD events in the data set. In their Cox regression model, PrinRads remained an independent predictor of ICD therapies and arrhythmic events, achieving a higher c-statistic (0.69) than the ACC/AHA (0.67, P = 0.75), and the ESC risk models (0.57, P = 0.02) over a follow-up period of 49 ± 28 months. Combined models incorporating the parameters of either the ESC or ACC/AHA model and radiomics were further developed, with the PrinRads-ESC model demonstrating a net reclassification index of (0.25 [95% CI: 0.08–0.43]; P = 0.005). This study further highlighted LGE heterogeneity as a significant predictor of SCD risk (HR: 0.07 [95% CI: 0.01–0.75]; P = 0.03). The authors noted that decision curve analysis revealed a net clinical benefit from this approach, especially in the intermediate-risk population for whom management is the most uncertain.

Smaller studies, such as Alis et al.64 investigated the link between texture analysis of LGE images and ventricular tachyarrhythmias. In 64 patients, also undergoing Holter monitoring, texture features were extracted from LGE-positive regions. After dimensionality reduction and machine learning classifiers, they achieved 94.1% accuracy. Durmaz et al.35 combined cine and LGE radiomics in 60 patients, of whom 17 developed sustained ventricular tachyarrhythmias. More than 1600 radiomics features were identified, and after LASSO selection and TabPFN classifier, the model achieved an AUC of 0.88. Run-length non-uniformity and wavelet-GLCM features, both reflecting textural heterogeneities, were identified as the main contributors. The translational promise remains clear that radiomics may identify and mitigate SCD risk in those patients invisible to current algorithms.

Beyond SCD, patients with HCM can experience disease progression and complications of heart failure. One model developed from a 516-patient cohort from cine CMR images found that the radiomics score served as the strongest predictor for the development of HF events in univariable and multivariable analysis. A combined model, with clinical and conventional CMR parameters, achieved a 3-year AUC of 0.77 in a validation set.36 Radiomics may therefore enable identification of patients with at-risk features for progressive ventricular remodelling and other major complications.

Challenges and future perspectives

Despite encouraging results, translation of radiomics into clinical workflows remains limited by methodological and practical challenges.

Clinical utility and incremental value

Although the majority of radiomics studies in HCM report high discrimination, often yielding AUCs >0.85, AUC alone is insufficient to justify clinical integration. Clinical decisions in HCM, such as ICD implantation, rely on absolute risk thresholds, making calibration and accurate probability estimation essential. Currently, few studies report calibration metrics or decision curve analyses to demonstrate a net clinical benefit over standard-of-care. Without these analyses, it remains unclear whether a radiomics model simply reiterates information already captured by conventional parameters. A few studies nevertheless provide a template for how future imaging biomarkers should be evaluated. Fahmy et al., in their sudden cardiac death model,37 and Akita et al., in echocardiographic fibrosis detection,63 both incorporated decision curve analysis to demonstrate net clinical benefit beyond discrimination alone, whilst Qin et al. reported calibration for their integrated CT model.34 These studies are highlighted as the current methodological benchmark, as a quantitative imaging tool intended to influence significant decisions such as ICD implantation must offer calibration and positive net benefit across clinically relevant probability thresholds. Routine integration of these analyses and a recommendation from contemporary prediction-model reporting guidelines for the inclusion of these metrics would increase the credibility of these tools for clinical adoption.

Bias, overfitting, and generalizability

An appraisal of the current HCM radiomics literature reveals significant vulnerabilities regarding study quality and risk of bias. The most pervasive limitation is the reliance on retrospective, single-centre datasets with small sample sizes. In radiomics, the number of extracted features frequently exceeds the number of adverse clinical events, creating a high risk of overfitting. In such scenarios, machine learning classifiers may simply memorize scanner-specific noise or local acquisition protocols rather than identifying true biological signals. Consequently, while internal cross-validation often yields high discrimination, these models frequently fail when applied to new populations. Broadening the perspective to general medical imaging guidelines (such as CLEAR and CLAIM), the absolute requisite for clinical translation is independent, external validation. Future prospective studies must ensure adequately powered cohorts and openly report calibration metrics to prove true generalizability across diverse hospital systems and scanner vendors.

Feature reproducibility and segmentation

Given the high feature-to-sample ratio typical of radiomics studies in HCM, robust feature selection is essential to reduce overfitting. Supervised approaches such as LASSO or mRMR are generally preferable when clinical interpretability is required, whereas unsupervised dimensionality reduction methods such as principal component analysis may obscure feature meaning and limit reproducibility. Transparent, cross-validated selection procedures with stability assessment are therefore recommended.

Similarly, reproducibility remains a significant concern as radiomics features are sensitive to acquisition and processing variability. Some studies acknowledge that the reproducibility of radiomic features with different manual segmentations was not tested.30 Similarly, the mapping of T1 radiomic features has been shown to demonstrate wide variability depending on imaging acquisition and preprocessing steps. Studies have reported that only 40–50% of features met acceptable reproducibility thresholds (ICC > 0.75) on repeat imaging or with alternative pre-processing.65 Reproducibility in cardiac radiomics is further complicated by cardiac motion and phase selection variability. This underscores the need for robust harmonization strategies, such as ComBat, to reduce scanner-related variation.

Campello et al. studied harmonization techniques across five centres in HCM patients vs. healthy controls, demonstrating that without harmonization,66 there was unprecedented heterogeneity in feature distribution. Downstream, manual segmentation, used by many studies, also confounds results, with reproducibility dependent on the experience of the operator. The highly trabeculated nature of the left ventricle, coupled with the variable geometry of the hypertrophied septum, makes manual segmentation highly subjective. Even minor deviations in contouring the endocardial or epicardial borders can drastically alter higher-order texture matrices. Positive steps to address this issue include the increased adoption of automated segmentation tools (e.g. ResUNet)67 and expert validation. Reproducibility testing must be embedded into the pipeline of future radiomics studies with test-retest sampling and sensitivity analysis.

Biological interpretability

Whilst LGE reflects areas of fibrosis, higher-order texture features extracted by radiomics, such as LBP and Moment,24 are not currently mapped to known histopathological processes.68 Current literature focuses heavily on adult cohorts with classic asymmetrical septal hypertrophy, leaving significant phenotypic ‘blind spots.’ For example, Apical HCM, characterized by a ‘spade-like’ left ventricular cavity and dense apical crowding, presents distinct imaging challenges. The apex is notoriously vulnerable to near-field artefacts in echocardiography and partial volume effects in CMR. Consequently, radiomics models trained predominantly on septal hypertrophy are highly likely to misclassify or fail when applied to apical variants. Similarly, paediatric HCM is absent from current radiomic validation studies. Paediatric patients exhibit vastly different baseline heart rates, body surface areas, and progressive phenotypic penetrance compared to adults. The genetic and histological underpinnings in paediatric cohorts often manifest with different temporal imaging signatures.27 To be clinically viable, future radiomic algorithms must explicitly include training data from these under-represented subgroups to ensure equitable and accurate diagnostic performance.69–71

Furthermore, most classification models are limited by the underexplored assumption of mutually exclusive diagnoses. In practice, a considerable proportion of patients could have concurrent pathologies such as HHD and HCM in the setting of comorbid arterial hypertension or transthyretin amyloidosis in elderly patients with existing aortic stenosis.72 Classifiers trained to assign a diagnosis label may fail to comprehend the complexity of these mixed phenotypes. Similarly, the diagnostic accuracy of these studies is relative to the reference standard of the models. Most models are trained on a clinical or imaging-based diagnosis, which is imperfect and may fail to encompass borderline or atypical presentations such as apical HCM with a maximum wall thickness below 12 mm or grade 1 cardiac amyloidosis, which carries a significant challenge of diagnostic uncertainty from imaging.73,74 Validating models against these standards risks the inheritance of uncertainty with seemingly accurate result outputs reproducing the errors of current diagnostic limitation.

Concerns with data sharing

Development of generalizable radiomics models requires large multi-center datasets, yet data sharing is limited by privacy and governance restrictions.75,76 Federated learning (FL) has emerged as a potential solution to mitigate the issues surrounding patient-sensitive data. In FL, a central server aggregator receives model updates instead of raw data, allowing inter-institutional data sharing with fewer concerns.76 Linardos et al. explored this concept in a simulation of FL across four centres using CMR images to classify healthy vs. HCM patients.21 Performances remained equivalent to a centralized approach of sharing raw data, without the risk of data leakage. A recent large cohort federated learning study using cardiac CT images further supported that these models can be implemented as a scalable solution for radiomics research.77

Reporting and clinical implementation

Ultimately, clinical translation of radiomics in HCM workflows depends on transparent reporting and alignment with guideline-driven standards. Several current studies fall short on these expectations.78 Critical methodological details for reproducibility are inconsistently reported, while calibration and decision-curve analysis to justify clinical value are sparsely assessed. Consensus bodies have defined what constitutes good practice in radiomics studies. The Image Biomarker Standardisation Initiative (IBSI) defines feature nomenclature, as well as providing reporting guidelines and recommendations for image pre-processing.79 Whereas the CLEAR checklist (CheckList for EvaluAtion of Radiomics) sets a list of mandatory reporting items, from segmentation to data sharing. Furthermore, as radiomics is rapidly advancing into the field of AI, broader guidelines such as TRIPOD AI,80 and CLAIM81 emphasize the need for calibration, pre-registration, and transparent reporting. Recent AHA statements underscore the need for fairness and monitoring of AI-based cardiovascular imaging tools,82 paving the way for clinical decision support.
