ReSS: Learning Reasoning Models for Tabular Data Prediction via Symbolic Scaffold
Chenlang Yi, Gang Li, Zizhan Xiong, Tue Minh Cao, Yanmin Gong, My T. Thai, Tianbao Yang

Abstract
Tabular data remains prevalent in high-stakes domains such as healthcare and finance, where predictive models are expected to provide both high accuracy and faithful, human-understandable reasoning. While symbolic models offer verifiable logic, they lack semantic expressiveness. Meanwhile, general-purpose LLMs often require specialized fine-tuning to master domain-specific tabular reasoning. To address the dual challenges of scalable data curation and reasoning consistency, we propose ReSS , a systematic framework that bridges symbolic and neural reasoning models. ReSS leverages a decision-tree model to extract instance-level decision paths as symbolic scaffolds. These scaffolds, alongside input features and labels, guide an LLM to generate grounded natural-language reasoning that strictly adheres to the underlying decision logic. The resulting high-quality dataset is used to fine-tune a pretrained LLM into a specialized tabular reasoning model, further enhanced by a scaffold-invariant data augmentation strategy to improve generalization and explainability. To rigorously assess faithfulness, we introduce quantitative metrics including hallucination rate, explanation necessity, and explanation sufficiency. Experimental results on medical and financial benchmarks demonstrate that ReSS-trained models improve traditional decision trees and standard fine-tuning approaches up to 10 % 10\% while producing faithful and consistent reasoning.

Introduction

In high-stakes domains such as healthcare
(
12
)
, finance
(

)
where tabular data is common, predictive accuracy alone is often insufficient. Models are also expected to provide faithful and human-understandable reasoning that enables practitioners to inspect, trust, and act upon their predictions. Despite decades of progress in tabular learning
(
7
;
10
;
26
)
, constructing models that jointly achieve strong performance and meaningful reasoning remains a fundamental challenge.

Traditional machine learning methods have long dominated tabular prediction, with tree-based approaches such as decision tree
(
7
)
, random forests
(
8
)
, XGBoost
(
10
)
, and LightGBM
(
20
)
achieving strong empirical performance. From a reasoning perspective, however, these models remain fundamentally limited. Tree ensemble methods do not expose an explicit, instance-level decision process, while single decision trees express reasoning solely through symbolic rules defined over low-level feature thresholds. Although such representations faithfully reflect the model’s internal logic, they lack semantic grounding in domain concepts and therefore cannot be readily interpreted by non-expert users, limiting their practical utility in real-world, high-stakes decision-making settings. Recent advances in deep learning (DL) for tabular data have produced models that are able to match or even surpass the performance of tree-based methods
(
18
;
3
;
1
;
26
)
. However, these models usually provide limited or no interpretability regarding its decision process. Even when post-hoc explanations are available, they often fail to constitute truly human-understandable reasoning chains.

Recently, studies have leveraged LLMs for tabular data prediction
(
13
;
15
)
. These methods transform tabular rows into natural language prompts to utilize the LLM’s prior knowledge for prediction. However, they essentially function as direct-prediction models rather than reasoning models, as they map inputs directly to final labels while bypassing the intermediate decision-making process. Consequently, they fail to provide a transparent, step-by-step logical chain to justify their predictions. This raises an important question:

How to efficiently train a faithful reasoning model for tabular data prediction?

There are two key challenges to be addressed: (i) how to curate high-quality reasoning data for fine-tuning a LLM; and (ii) how to ensure that the reasoning traces generated by the learned model are both faithful and explainable. Curating high-quality reasoning data for high-stakes domains such as healthcare and finance typically requires domain experts and is therefore expensive, time-consuming, and difficult to scale. Moreover, while current LLMs are capable of producing chain-of-thought (CoT)-like outputs, these explanations are not guaranteed to be faithful and causal to the tabular data prediction
(
19
;
5
)
.

To address these challenges, we propose
ReSS
, a systematic framework that curates high-quality reasoning data via symbolic scaffolds and trains efficient reasoning models for tabular prediction. ReSS first trains a decision tree on tabular data and extracts instance-level decision paths from samples that are correctly predicted by the decision tree, which serve as explicit symbolic scaffolds capturing the model’s underlying decision logic. These scaffolds, together with input features and labels, are used to guide an LLM to generate grounded, step-by-step natural-language reasoning that strictly adheres to the logical constraints encoded in the decision paths, yielding a reasoning dataset that is both faithful to the original decision process and interpretable by humans. To further enhance generalization and explainability, ReSS incorporates a scaffold-invariant data augmentation strategy that expands the reasoning dataset and introduces out-of-distribution examples by perturbing input features while preserving the symbolic decision paths and labels. 1
.

We summarize our contributions as follows:

•

We propose ReSS, a systematic framework that leverages decision-tree paths as symbolic scaffolds to guide an LLM using its inherent domain knowledge to curate high-quality grounded reasoning data, and trains efficient reasoning models for tabular prediction.

•

We introduce a scaffold-invariant data augmentation strategy that preserves symbolic decision logic while generating both in-distribution and out-of-distribution reasoning data, enabling improved generalization and more robust explainability.

•

We propose a set of metrics to measure the faithfulness and explainability of generated CoT-like outputs by the trained reasoning model, including hallucination rate, explanation sufficiency, and explanation necessity.

•

We conduct experiments on four real-world datasets, including two from medicine and two from finance. The results demonstrate the effectiveness of ReSS and the scaffold-invariant data augmentation, and validate the faithfulness and causal impact of the generated rationales.

Related work

While tabular data prediction has long been dominated by tree-based ensemble models, recent years have seen the emergence of new approaches based on DL and LLMs.

Deep Learning Methods for Tabular Prediction.

Early DL approaches for tabular prediction typically rely on generic architectures such as MLPs, which lack inductive biases aligned with the structure of tabular decision manifolds and often struggle to match the performance of tree-based methods
(
3
)
. To overcome this limitation, subsequent work introduced tabular-specific inductive biases into neural architectures. TabNet
(
3
)
injects inductive bias through sequential, instance-wise feature selection, enabling competitive performance with tree-boosting models on a wide range of benchmarks. DNF-Net
(
1
)
incorporates a logical inductive bias by aligning model structure with disjunctive normal form, encouraging localized and rule-like decision behaviors. TabTransformer
(
18
)
leverages self-attention to model complex feature interactions, demonstrating that transformer-based architectures can match or surpass gradient-boosted trees in tabular prediction tasks. TabPFN
(
16
)
formulates tabular prediction as in-context inference with a prior-data fitted transformer network and achieves competitive or superior performance on small to medium-sized datasets.
InterpreTabNet
(
26
)
attempts to provide intrinsic interpretability through instance-wise feature selection mechanisms, by
employing an LLM to map from the learned feature mask onto natural language text describing the learned signal. Despite these advances, they do not aim to produce explicit, human-understandable reasoning processes.

LLMs for Tabular Prediction.

Recent work has explored applying LLMs to tabular prediction by serializing tabular records into natural-language descriptions, allowing LLMs to operate in their native textual space. Depending on whether model parameters are updated, these approaches can be broadly categorized into inference-only and fine-tuning–based methods. TABLET
(
27
)
studies inference-only prediction by prompting pretrained LLMs such as Flan-T5
(
11
)
, GPT-J
(
6
)
, and ChatGPT, but reports that simple tree-based baselines consistently outperform LLMs under comparable settings. Fine-tuning–based approaches, including LIFT
(
13
)
and TabLLM
(
15
)
, demonstrate that language pretraining and meaningful feature semantics can improve tabular prediction, particularly in low-shot regimes. However, these gains are inconsistent across datasets, and overall performance still struggles to reliably surpass strong tree-based models. Moreover, most existing methods focus solely on final predictions and do not generate explicit reasoning.

Large Reasoning Models.

Recently, Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) trained by RL have demonstrated strong capabilities in solving complex tasks
(
14
;
28
)
, positioning them as a promising paradigm for tabular prediction. Beyond generating final predictions, LRMs can produce explicit reasoning chains. Many RL algorithms have been developed including, GRPO
(
14
)
, REINFORCE++
(
17
)
, DisCO
(
23
)
, to name just a few. Two recent works, including TabReason
(
35
)
and TabR1
(
9
)
have applied RL directly to a base model for tabular prediction. However, reasoning capabilities of existing LRMs do not readily transfer to tabular reasoning, which requires both semantic and numerical understanding
(
9
)
. As a result, directly generating reasoning traces from existing LRMs without fine-tuning on the tabular reasoning data may lead to hallucinations, undermining the faithfulness and trustworthiness of the outputs.

33
considered medical question-answering tasks and proposed an approach for curating reasoning traces from tabular data by constructing contrastive triplets of an example including the sample itself, the nearest samples with the same and different labels, and prompting an generalist LLM to transform the triplets into contrastive decision processes, which are then used to supervise fine-tuning of a target LLM.

Faithfulness of Reasoning Models.
The reasoning traces generated from LRMs, although provide explicit explanation for the final prediction, could be unfaithful
(
29
;
2
;
5
)
.
These works show that the reasoning chains of a model can be misleading or suffer from subtle biases that are not reflected in the traces, hampering the trustworthiness of the prediction. Thus, to ensure that the answer is trustworthy, evaluating the faithfulness of model reasoning explanation is imperative for any task, including tabular prediction.
An important quality of a faithful reasoning is consistency, where important sentences or features in the reasoning trace have strong causal impact
(
25
;
4
)
and replacing them with their counterfactual version should greatly affect the output of the model.
However, existing works that use LRMs in tabular prediction
(
35
;
9
;
33
)
only focus on evaluating prediction accuracy, lacking a comprehensive test to quantify the faithfulness quality of their models’ reasoning.

3
ReSS: Reasoning via Symbolic Scaffolds

3.1
Notations and Baselines

We consider classification problems on tabular data. Each sample can be represented by a tuple
(\mathbf{x},y)
, where
\mathbf{x}
is a feature vector
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
y\in\mathcal{Y}
is the class label. The features in
\mathbf{x}
may be numerical, ordinal, or categorical. Given an example
\mathbf{x}
, our goal is to fine-tune an LLM that given input features
\mathbf{x}
predicts the label
y
, along with a reasoning trace
\mathbf{z}=(\mathbf{z}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{z}_{k})
to support and explain the final prediction, where each
\mathbf{z}_{k}
represents a reasoning step consisting of a sequence of reasoning tokens.

To input each tabular row into an LLM, we serialize the feature vector
\mathbf{x}
into a sequence of natural-language tokens. Let us consider an example of diabetic prediction problem, where features may include age, BMI (Body Mass Index), DBP (Diastolic Blood Pressure), etc. Given a feature vector {Age: 31, BMI:10.5, DBP: 66, …}, it is serialized to a text, e.g.,
“‘The age is 31, the body mass index is 10.5, the diastolic blood pressure is 66, …”
.

Without causing any confusion, let
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}
denote the serialized texts for the input features and the output label, respectively. Let
\pi_{\mathbf{w}}
denote an LLM with parameters
\mathbf{w}
. We denote by
\pi_{\mathbf{w}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})
the probability of generating
\mathbf{y}
given
\mathbf{x}
.

Before delving into the proposed approach, we first present two straightforward approaches for fine-tuning an LLM for tabular data prediction and discuss their limitations.

:

An illustration of the ReSS pipeline applied to the diabetes prediction problem.

Baseline: Direct SFT.
Given serialized data
\{(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{y}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
, a straightforward approach to fine-tune an LLM is to perform the supervised fine-tuning (SFT) by minimizing the following objective:

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{w}}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\pi_{\mathbf{w}}(\mathbf{y}_{i}|\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{t}),

(1)

where
\mathbf{t}
denote the task-related prompt. A limitation of this approach is that the model does not output any reasoning for the final prediction.

Baseline: Direct Reasoning Curation and SFT.

One way to address the above limitation is to first curate a reasoning data and then conduct SFT. However, curating high-quality reasoning data from domain experts in high-stakes domains is expensive, time-consuming, and hard to scale. A simple way is to leverage the knowledge of a general-purpose LLM. To this end, we can prompt an LLM with all features and the label
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
and a guidance prompt
\mathbf{t}_{c}
to generate a rationale for how a label
\mathbf{y}
is predicted given the features in
\mathbf{x}
. We let
\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
denote the reasoning tokens generated by the LLM. We refer the readers to Figure
9
and Appendix
A.2.1
for an example of output and the guidance prompt
\mathbf{t}_{c}
, respectively. Then, SFT is conducted by solving the following problem:

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{w}}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\pi_{\mathbf{w}}(\{\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{y}_{i}\}|\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{t}),

(2)

where
\mathbf{z}_{i}=\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{y}_{i})
,
\{\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{y}_{i}\}
denotes the concatenation of reasoning tokens and the final label tokens, and
\mathbf{t}
denotes the task-related prompt.

3.2
Using Decision Tree Paths as Symbolic Scaffolds

Motivation of Using Symbolic Scaffolds.

A consequence of the direct curation approach discussed above is that the generated rationale may contain many non-useful features. An example given in Figure
9
shows that the rationale generated by ChatGPT could iterate through all eight features on the diabetes dataset. To explain its limitation and motivate the proposed approach, we consider a simple example below. Let us consider the input data
\mathbf{x}\in\{1,-1\}^{2d}
, which contains a sequence of
\{1,-1\}
tokens. Suppose the
y
is given by
y=\prod_{i=1}^{d}x_{P(i)}
, where
P
is a fixed (but unknown) permutation of
\{1,\ldots,2d\}
. Hence,
y
is just the parity of
d
fixed tokens in
\mathbf{x}
. The existing work
(
34
)
has shown that if we directly learn a model
\pi_{\mathbf{w}}(y|\mathbf{x})
to predict
y
given
\mathbf{x}
, the learning requires an exponential number of samples in the order of
O(\exp(d))
. Hence, it is impossible to learn the correct model for predicting
y
given
\mathbf{x}
with limited data. The challenge of the problems lies that finding the correct permutation
P
out of all permutations is exponentially hard. However, if we tell the learner that the final prediction
y
only relies on the features in
x_{P(1)},\ldots,x_{P(d)}
, it could dramatically simplify the learning process.

This motivates the proposed approach of using
symbolic scaffolds
to guide the feature selection and decision process. A symbolic scaffold refers to an explicit external structure such as rules, diagrams, decision trees, or step-by-step procedures that humans use to organize and guide their reasoning processes. In developmental psychology
(
31
)
, symbolic scaffolds are known to support problem solving by breaking complex tasks into interpretable intermediate steps, thereby reducing cognitive load and enabling systematic inference. Rather than replacing reasoning, these symbolic aids shape how reasoning unfolds by constraining the space of possible actions and making intermediate states explicit. It is important to note that the concept of a
symbolic scaffold differs from that of chain-of-thought (CoT)

(
32
)
in reasoning models. CoT concerns how reasoning is expressed, whereas a symbolic scaffold determines how the reasoning process is structured and constrained.

To construct a symbolic scaffold for a tabular data, it is natural to use a decision tree model. To this end, we first train a decision tree with a grid search of its depth on the given tabular data. For each input instance
\mathbf{x}
, the tree induces a unique root-to-leaf symbolic decision path, which we formalize as an instance-specific symbolic
scaffold denoted by
S(\mathbf{x})
. In particular, the scaffold
S(\mathbf{x})
is an ordered set of feature-level logical constraints that characterizes the sufficient conditions leading to the prediction for
\mathbf{x}
. Importantly,
S(\mathbf{x})
is induced in a data-dependent manner: both the selected features and their associated thresholds arise from the empirical data distribution. As a result, the scaffold provides a compact, verifiable constraint space that can be directly used to guide rationale generation. An example of a scaffold
S(\mathbf{x})
from the decision tree is given in Figure
1
, “age
>
26.5, BMI
>
9.625, … ”. Similar as before, we serialize
S(\mathbf{x})
into a sequence of tokens.

3.3
Symbolic Scaffold Informed Reasoning Dataset Curation

Given a symbolic scaffold that specifies the constraints of the decision process, we leverage the input features, the output label, and the corresponding symbolic scaffolds of correctly predicted instances to generate reasoning data. To this end, we prompt a generalist LLM (e.g., ChatGPT) by asking it to provide the rationale how the label
\mathbf{y}
is predicted from the input features
\mathbf{x}
following the symbolic decision path
S(\mathbf{x})
that leads to the correct label. Generalist LLMs encode rich domain knowledge acquired from large-scale pretraining, which can be leveraged to translate symbolic rules into coherent natural language explanations. In our setting, we use a generalist LLM as a constrained generator that injects domain knowledge while strictly adhering to symbolic constraints as specified in the symbolic scaffold. We give some examples of curated reasoning traces following this procedure in Appendix
B.1
.

For each example
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
that is correctly predicted by the decision tree model, we let
\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},S(\mathbf{x}))
denote the curated reasoning tokens. As a result, we collect a set of reasoning data
\{\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{y}_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{C}}
, where
\mathbf{z}_{i}=\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{y}_{i},S(\mathbf{x}_{i}))
, and
\mathcal{C}\in[1,\ldots,n]
denotes the subset of data that is correctly predicted by the decision tree model.

3.4
Scaffold-Invariant Data Augmentation

One might note that we only use the data that is correctly predicted by the decision tree model. This is important for LLM to learn consistent decision rules. However, it will shrink the size of training data for SFT. Below, we present an effective data augmentation strategy.

A data augmentation is usually performed by perturbing the input features. However, a major challenge in data augmentation for tabular prediction is ensuring the consistency between perturbed features and the label, as unconstrained perturbations may cause an instance to no longer correspond to its original label. Our framework addresses this issue by enforcing
scaffold invariance
during augmentation.
Specifically, for each correctly predicted training instance by the decision tree model, its symbolic scaffold
S(\mathbf{x})
characterizes the decision logic leading to its label. This scaffold defines a conjunction of feature-level constraints and serves as an explicit invariance during data augmentation.

To generate an augmented sample, we randomly sample some features (e.g., by flipping a coin for each feature) and for each feature we randomly perturb its value such that it does not violate the decision rule specified by the scaffold
S(\mathbf{x})
. Perturbed values are sampled within a feature-specific valid range. To improve the out-of-distribution generalizability, we also allow for a certain probability (e.g., 0.2) to perturb a sampled feature that do not appear in the decision path to the value
unknown
. This not only simulates out-of-distribution data but also facilitates the evaluation of explainability presented in next section. As a result, all perturbations satisfy the original decision-path constraints, ensuring that the symbolic scaffold remains unchanged. By preserving the decision scaffold while allowing controlled feature-level variation, our augmentation procedure generates diverse yet label-scaffold consistent samples.

For a perturbed sample
(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\mathbf{y})
corresponding to an original data point
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
, we follow the same procedure described in the previous subsection to generate the reasoning traces
\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\mathbf{y},S(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}))
. Let
\mathcal{D}=\{(\mathbf{x}_{j},\mathbf{z}_{j},\mathbf{y}_{j})\}_{j=1}^{m}
denote the final set of reasoning data including the original data and the perturbed data, we conduct SFT by solving the following problem with
t
being the task-related prompt:

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{w}}-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}|}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{D}}\log\pi_{\mathbf{w}}(\{\mathbf{z}_{j},\mathbf{y}_{j}\}|\mathbf{x}_{j},\mathbf{t}).

(3)

4
Evaluation of Faithfulness and Explainability

Since the ReSS-trained reasoning model is derived from an explainable and faithful decision tree model, a natural question is whether the learned reasoning model preserves the faithfulness and explainability of the underlying decision tree. To answer this question, we need to evaluate whether the model-generated reasoning is faithful and explainable. Without systematic evaluation, reasoning traces may appear plausible while relying on nonexistent features or implausible feature values, and including artificial rationales that are not causal for the final predictions. To this end, we consider the following three complementary criteria: hallucination, explanation sufficiency, and explanation necessity, which together characterize whether the reasoning is grounded in the input, complete, and causally relevant to the prediction.

Hallucination.
We measure hallucination by identifying reasoning steps that violate the input evidence.
Specifically, we consider three types: (i)
feature existence hallucination (FEH)
, where the reasoning refers to features absent from the input;
(ii)
feature value hallucination (FVH)
, where incorrect or unsupported feature values are asserted;
and (iii)
comparison hallucination (CH)
, where invalid numerical or categorical comparisons are made.
The hallucination rate is computed at the step level: for each reasoning step, we mark it as hallucinated if it contains a violation, and report the fraction of hallucinated steps among all steps.

Explanation Sufficiency.
To assess explanation sufficiency, we mask features that are not referenced in the generated reasoning traces by setting their values to
unknown
in the input, and prompting the model to re-generate reasoning and prediction, and evaluating the prediction accuracy.
Specifically, we progressively mask the number of such features from
k=1
to
4
. If the accuracy remains stable under these perturbations, the original reasoning traces are considered to be sufficient.

Explanation Necessity.
To assess explanation necessity, we mask the features explicitly appearing in the generated reasoning traces by setting their values to
unknown
in the input, and measure the resulting performance degradation.
Similarly, the number of masked features is varied from
k=1
to
4
. A significant drop in accuracy suggests that the identified features are necessary for the prediction, while a minor change indicates redundant or spurious reasoning.

5.2
Comparison with Baselines

Table
2
summarizes the classification accuracy of different methods across four tabular datasets. We can observe that Direct SFT generally exhibits weaker performance than traditional methods. Introducing additional reasoning supervision (DRC+SFT) improves performance on some datasets but yields inconsistent gains, indicating the limitations of direct unconstrained reasoning curation. In contrast, with symbolic scaffold informed reasoning boosted by the scaffold-invariant data augmentation, ReSS consistently outperforms all baselines, with particularly notable gains on Diabetes and AD datasets.
We also see that Direct RL approach shows high variance across datasets, reflecting the difficulty of optimizing reasoning behavior without explicit structural guidance. These results collectively demonstrate that benefits of structured reasoning supervision grounded in symbolic scaffolds, underscoring the importance of high-quality, structure-aligned reasoning data for effective tabular decision-making.

As discussed in Section

Hallucination Analysis.

Comparison Hallucination occurs only rarely, with rates below

These results demonstrate that the generated explanations are highly faithful to the underlying input features,
with only occasional errors arising from incorrect relational comparisons.

Explanation Sufficiency and Necessity.

Figure
2
illustrates the results of explanation sufficiency and necessity evaluation
by progressively masking features with an
unknown

We observe that across all datasets, masking unused features in reasoning has only a marginal impact on performance,
indicating that the explanations are sufficient: they already capture the core information needed for prediction.
In contrast, masking used features in reasoning leads to a substantial and monotonic drop in accuracy, where performance degrades sharply as more explanatory features are removed.
This clear divergence between the two curves demonstrates that the explanations rely on features that are genuinely

We further analyze explainability under the absence of symbolic guidance by comparing ReSS with DRC+SFT, and observe a substantial degradation in explanation necessity without symbolic scaffolds; detailed results are provided in Appendix

5.5
Ablation studies

Domain Knowledge Matters.

To quantify the role of domain knowledge, we conduct an ablation study that delexicalizes all feature names and values during reasoning data curation. Specifically, we preserve the original input structure—including the input features, symbolic scaffolds, and the label—but anonymize all feature names and categorical values into abstract placeholders (e.g.,
Feature A
,
Category B
). The LLM is strictly instructed to follow the symbolic scaffold while being prohibited from providing any semantic or domain-specific explanations, resulting in reasoning that purely imitates the symbolic structure without introducing external domain knowledge. Prompts for reasoning data curation and fine-tuning, as well as curated reasoning examples, are provided in Appendix
A.3
and
B.3
, respectively.

Figure
13
reports the results. Models trained on delexicalized reasoning achieve performance nearly identical to the decision tree baseline across all datasets, but consistently underperform ReSS. This result indicates that delexicalized reasoning mainly captures the symbolic structure of the decision tree, while the additional performance gains of ReSS arise from domain knowledge introduced by a general LLM.

We further include a cherry-picked analysis to illustrate how domain knowledge contributes to the performance gains of our ReSS-trained model over decision trees (Appendix
E.1
).

SFT vs RL.

Since RL is usually regarded as an effective approach to improve the generalization of SFT, we conduct RL on ReSS-trained models. To this end, we conduct RL based on DisCO from the model initialized from ReSS-trained model without augmentation. We compare this approach, denoted by ReSS (w/o aug) + RL, with the direct RL approach in Figure
15
, which shows that performing RL after ReSS consistently outperforms directly applying RL to the base model across all datasets, demonstrating that RL benefits substantially from structured reasoning supervision.

We also compare the approach ReSS (w/o aug) + RL with ReSS and ReSS (w/o aug.) in Figure
15
. We can observe while RL generally improves its SFT counterparts, it is not necessarily better than ReSS-trained model on augmented data. This indicates that our scaffold-invariant data augmentation is an effective approach for improving generalization.
We additionally apply RL on top of ReSS with augmented data. However, this setting does not yield further consistent improvements. This suggests that scaffold-invariant augmentation already supplies a strong learning signal, diminishing the marginal benefits of additional RL.

6
Conclusions

We proposed ReSS, a framework for learning faithful reasoning models for tabular data by using decision-tree paths as symbolic scaffolds. ReSS enables scalable reasoning data curation and trains LLMs to produce structured, causally grounded explanations. Experiments on medical and financial datasets demonstrate improved predictive performance and strong faithfulness and explainability. While ReSS shows promising results in financial and medical domains, future work will explore its effectiveness in other domains where LLMs possess strong intrinsic domain knowledge.

Impact Statement

This work focuses on improving the faithfulness and consistency of model-generated reasoning for tabular prediction tasks. By grounding natural-language explanations in symbolic decision logic, the proposed approach may support greater transparency and interpretability in machine learning systems, particularly in domains where tabular data is prevalent.

While our experiments include datasets from healthcare and finance, the proposed method is intended as a decision-support tool rather than an autonomous decision-making system. Potential downstream impacts depend on how such models are deployed in practice. In high-stakes settings, improper use or over-reliance on model outputs could lead to adverse outcomes if not accompanied by appropriate human oversight and domain expertise.

This work does not address deployment, governance, or regulatory considerations directly, and we encourage practitioners to carefully evaluate domain-specific risks when applying the proposed framework.

Appendix E
Supplementary Experimental Results

E.1
Knowledge helps our ReSS-trained LLM to correct mistakes of Decision tree

Decision trees are purely data-driven models and do not encode domain knowledge. As a result, some predictions may reflect dataset-specific artifacts or spurious correlations rather than applying domain-plausible rules (see an example given below). While this can introduce a small fraction of noisy or counterintuitive reasoning examples for training, the majority of decision-tree-derived reasoning paths remain consistent with domain commonsense. When trained on such data, the language model does not simply memorize individual paths, but instead learns implicit explanatory structures and domain-level regularities underlying the paths. Consequently, during inference, the model may generate predictions that deviate from specific decision-tree leaf outcomes while remaining aligned with learned domain-level regularities.

To illustrate this phenomenon, we present a qualitative case study on a testing data from the Diabetes dataset shown in Figure
12
. Along this decision path, the decision tree assigns a
non-diabetic
label, which reflects the empirical training label distribution observed in this localized region of the feature space but is wrong. However, every condition along the path corresponds to a well-established risk factor for diabetes according to the domain knowledge. In contrast, our fine-tuned LLM generates a rationale that faithfully follows the same decision path conditions, explicitly referencing each split and providing medically plausible interpretations for them. The generated reasoning emphasizes the cumulative risk implied by high elevated 2-hour plasma glucose levels, extreme obesity, elevated diabetes pedigree function, and high blood pressure, and finally predicts the correct
diabetic
label.

E.4
Explainability without Symbolic Guidance.

We further analyze the explainability of the generated reasoning by comparing ReSS,
trained without augmented reasoning data, with a DRC+SFT baseline,
whose reasoning data is generated without symbolic scaffold constraints.
This analysis focuses on explanation sufficiency and necessity,
using the same feature-masking protocol as in the main experiments.

Figure
16
shows the sufficiency and necessity curves
on Diabetes and AD.
For ReSS, masking unused features results in only minor accuracy changes,
while masking explanation-referenced features leads to a sharp and monotonic
performance drop, indicating strong explanation necessity.
In contrast, DRC+SFT consistently exhibits substantially weaker necessity.
On Diabetes, masking features referenced by the explanation causes a much smaller
accuracy degradation compared to ReSS.
The issue becomes more pronounced on AD, where the accuracy drop under necessity
masking is minimal and, in some cases, even smaller than that observed when masking
unused features, revealing a clear violation of explanation necessity.

This behavior can be attributed to the absence of symbolic guidance during
reasoning data generation.
Without symbolic scaffold constraints, the LLM tends to incorporate a large number
of input features into the explanation, rather than selectively identifying
decision-critical evidence.
This over-inclusive behavior is particularly detrimental for high-dimensional
datasets such as AD, which contains 25 input features.
Under DRC+SFT, the generated explanations reference an average of 14 features,
many of which are redundant or only weakly correlated with the prediction outcome.

From an explainability perspective, such reasoning fails to distinguish truly
influential features from incidental information, leading to substantially
degraded explanation necessity.
In contrast, the symbolic scaffolds in ReSS explicitly encourage structured
reasoning, ensuring that the features highlighted by the explanation are genuinely
relevant to the model’s decision.
