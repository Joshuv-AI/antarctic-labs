Strategies to improve health service quality in Indonesia: a qualitative study
Authors: Suprapto, Yuriatson, Maria Kurni Menga, Darmi Arda, N L Nur Syamsi, Trimaya Cahya Mulat, Yohan Trayanus Djaha, Tirta Asprimi Angreani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundImproving the quality of health services remains a significant challenge in Indonesia, particularly due to variations in workforce capacity, uneven adoption of digital health systems, and limited stakeholder engagement. Understanding how these factors influence service quality from the perspectives of key stakeholders is essential to inform effective and sustainable improvement strategies.MethodsThis study employed a qualitative descriptive design across multiple healthcare settings in Indonesia, including primary health centers, clinics, and district hospitals. Data were collected through in-depth, semi-structured interviews with healthcare providers, facility managers, policymakers, and community representatives. Participants were selected purposively based on their involvement in service delivery and quality improvement activities. Interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and analyzed using thematic analysis. An inductive coding approach was applied to identify recurring patterns and themes. Analytical rigor was enhanced through peer debriefing, source triangulation, and member checking.ResultsThe analysis identified several interconnected themes influencing health service quality. Workforce capacity emerged as a central challenge, characterized by limited access to continuous training, high workloads, and gaps in clinical and managerial competencies. Digital health implementation varied widely across facilities, with barriers including low digital literacy, poor infrastructure, and limited system integration. Participants also highlighted gaps in policy implementation at the facility level, often related to bureaucratic constraints and insufficient organizational support. Community engagement was generally limited, with few structured mechanisms for capturing and responding to patient feedback. Participants emphasized that effective quality improvement requires coordinated strategies addressing human resources, digital readiness, governance, and community participation.ConclusionsHealth service quality improvement in Indonesia is shaped by complex interactions between workforce capacity, digital health readiness, organizational support, and community engagement. Sustainable improvement requires integrated, context-sensitive strategies that strengthen human resources, support effective use of digital systems, enhance local policy implementation, and promote patient-centered care. These findings provide practical insights for policymakers and health system leaders seeking to strengthen service quality in resource-constrained settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12913-026-14221-x.Keywords: Community engagement, Digital health, Health service quality, Policy implementation, Workforce capacity

Background

The quality of health services is a central determinant of population health outcomes and health system performance [1]. High-quality health services are essential for ensuring patient safety, improving clinical outcomes, enhancing patient satisfaction, and promoting equity in access to care. In many low- and middle-income countries, including Indonesia, efforts to expand health service coverage have progressed substantially over the past decade [2]. However, improvements in service quality have not always kept pace with increased access, resulting in persistent gaps between service availability and effective, patient-centered care [3]. These challenges underscore the importance of examining not only whether services are delivered, but also how well they meet communities’ needs [4].

Indonesia has implemented several major health system reforms aimed at strengthening service delivery, including expanding primary healthcare facilities, introducing a national health insurance scheme, and investing in digital health infrastructure [5]. Despite these initiatives, variability in service quality remains evident across regions and levels of care [6]. Disparities are particularly pronounced between urban and rural areas, where differences in resource availability, workforce capacity, and infrastructure continue to influence service performance [7]. Health facilities in remote and underserved settings often face shortages of trained personnel, limited access to continuing professional development, and inadequate technological support, all of which constrain their ability to deliver consistent and high-quality care [8].

Existing literature highlights workforce capacity as a critical factor influencing health service quality. Healthcare providers play a central role in determining clinical effectiveness, patient safety, and service responsiveness [9]. However, many studies report that healthcare workers in resource-constrained settings experience high workloads, insufficient training opportunities, and limited managerial support [10]. These conditions can lead to reduced motivation, burnout, and variability in clinical practice, ultimately affecting patient outcomes [11]. While capacity-building initiatives have been widely promoted, evidence suggests that traditional training approaches alone are often insufficient to produce sustained improvements in service quality without supportive organizational structures and leadership [12].

The integration of digital health technologies has been increasingly promoted as a strategy to enhance efficiency, coordination, and quality of care [13]. Tools such as electronic medical records, telemedicine, and health information systems can improve clinical decision-making, strengthen referral systems, and support data-driven management [14]. In Indonesia, digital health adoption has expanded rapidly; however, its implementation remains uneven [15]. Prior research indicates that limited digital literacy, inadequate infrastructure, poor system interoperability, and resistance to organizational change frequently undermine the effectiveness of digital health initiatives [16]. As a result, technology may add to administrative burden rather than improving service quality if it is not accompanied by adequate training, technical support, and alignment with local workflows [17].

Policy frameworks and organizational governance also play a significant role in shaping health service quality [18]. National policies often provide clear standards and guidelines for quality improvement, yet their implementation at the facility level depends heavily on local capacity, leadership, and contextual adaptability [19]. Studies have shown that rigid or top-down policy approaches may fail to address the realities of frontline service delivery, particularly in settings with limited resources [20]. Effective quality improvement requires supportive leadership, clear communication, and mechanisms for monitoring and feedback that enable facilities to adapt policies to their specific contexts while maintaining accountability [21].

Another important dimension of health service quality is community engagement and patient experience [22]. Patient-centered care emphasizes responsiveness to individual needs, preferences, and values, and recognizes patients and communities as active partners in health service delivery [23]. However, evidence suggests that community involvement in planning, monitoring, and evaluating health services remains limited in many settings [24]. Feedback mechanisms are often informal, poorly documented, or not systematically integrated into quality improvement processes [25]. The lack of structured community engagement can weaken trust in health services and reduce opportunities to identify and address service gaps from the user perspective [26].

Although previous studies have provided an understanding of health service quality in Indonesia, much of the existing evidence is derived from quantitative assessments, policy analyses, or accreditation-focused evaluations [27]. While these approaches are valuable, they may overlook the contextual and experiential factors that influence how quality improvement strategies are understood and implemented at the facility level [28]. There is a relative lack of qualitative research that captures the perspectives of diverse stakeholders, including healthcare providers, managers, policymakers, and community representatives, within a single analytical framework [29].

This study addresses this gap by providing an in-depth qualitative exploration of strategies to improve health service quality in Indonesia. By examining stakeholder experiences across multiple healthcare settings, the study aims to generate contextually grounded insights into the human, technological, organizational, and community-related factors that shape service quality. The findings contribute to the field by highlighting practical opportunities to strengthen workforce capacity, improve digital health readiness, enhance policy implementation, and promote meaningful community engagement. These insights are intended to inform more integrated and sustainable approaches to health service quality improvement in Indonesia and other resource-constrained settings.

Theoretical framework

This study was guided by an integrative theoretical framework developed from a review of the health service quality and health systems strengthening literature, complemented by expert consultation. The framework conceptualizes health service quality as a multidimensional outcome shaped by the interaction of workforce capacity, digital health readiness, policy implementation and governance, organizational support, and community engagement. These domains are consistently highlighted in established quality-of-care and health systems models, which emphasize that sustainable service quality emerges from the alignment of human resources, system infrastructure, governance mechanisms, and patient-centered processes.

Within this framework, workforce capacity is positioned as a central determinant influencing clinical competence, service responsiveness, and patient safety. Digital health readiness functions as an enabling factor that can enhance efficiency, coordination, and data-driven decision-making when supported by adequate infrastructure and skills. Policy implementation and organizational support define the governance environment that shapes how national quality standards are translated into routine practice at the facility level. Community engagement represents the relational and social dimension of service quality, ensuring that healthcare delivery is responsive to patient needs, expectations, and local contexts.

This theoretical framework informed the development of the interview guide and guided the data collection process. Key domains of the framework were used to structure the interview questions, ensuring systematic exploration of factors influencing health service quality across diverse healthcare settings. At the same time, the qualitative design allowed for inductive insights beyond the initial framework, enabling a comprehensive and context-sensitive understanding of health service quality in Indonesia. Guided by this theoretical framework, the present study aimed to explore stakeholder perspectives on strategies to improve health service quality in Indonesia across diverse healthcare settings.

Methods

Aim, design, and setting of the study

This study adopted a qualitative multiple-case study design to explore strategies for improving health service quality in Indonesia across diverse healthcare settings. Each healthcare facility (public primary health centers, private clinics, and district hospitals) was treated as an individual case, enabling in-depth examination of contextual factors influencing service quality within and across cases. The multiple-case study approach was selected to allow systematic comparison of similarities and differences across cases, thereby strengthening analytical depth and transferability of findings. The study design was informed by established methodological literature on case study research, which emphasizes the value of multiple cases for identifying robust patterns and theoretical replication across contexts. This approach was particularly appropriate for examining complex health service quality issues shaped by organizational, professional, and contextual variability. The multiple-case design also complemented the study’s theoretical framework by enabling exploration of how workforce capacity, digital health readiness, policy implementation, and community engagement interact differently across healthcare settings.

Participants and characteristics

Participants were selected using purposive sampling to ensure inclusion of individuals with relevant experience and direct involvement in health service delivery or quality improvement processes. Eligible participants included healthcare providers (such as doctors, nurses, and midwives), facility managers, local health policymakers, and community representatives. Inclusion criteria required participants to have at least 1 year of experience in their current role and familiarity with operational, managerial, or quality-related activities in their respective settings.

A total of 22 participants were recruited using purposive sampling, with the final sample size determined by data saturation, defined as the point at which no new themes or substantive insights emerged from successive interviews. Healthcare providers constituted the largest participant group, reflecting their central role in frontline service delivery. Facility managers, policymakers, and community representatives were included to provide complementary system-level and user perspectives. Participants varied in age, professional background, and years of experience, thereby enhancing the depth, credibility, and diversity of the qualitative data. The sample size in this qualitative multiple-case study was determined using the principle of data saturation rather than statistical calculation. Data saturation was defined as the point at which additional interviews no longer generated new themes, insights, or meaningful variations relevant to the study objectives.

Several considerations informed the final sample size, including the complexity of the research topic, the inclusion of multiple healthcare settings as distinct cases, and the need to capture diverse stakeholder perspectives within and across cases. Recruitment continued until thematic saturation was achieved at both the within case and cross-case levels, resulting in a total of 22 participants. This approach ensured sufficient depth and analytical richness to support credible and transferable qualitative findings.

Participants were included in the study if they met the following criteria: (1) healthcare providers, facility managers, policymakers, or community representatives; (2) at least one year of experience in their current role; and (3) direct involvement in health service delivery, management, policy implementation, or community engagement related to service quality. These criteria were applied to ensure that participants had sufficient experiential knowledge to contribute meaningfully to the study objectives.

Participants were excluded if they had less than one year of experience in their role, had no direct involvement in quality-related activities, or were temporarily assigned to the facility during the data collection period. The exclusion criteria were intended to minimize the inclusion of participants with limited contextual understanding of organizational processes.

Several potential sources of bias were considered in this qualitative multiple-case study, including selection bias, response bias, and researcher bias. Selection bias was addressed through purposive sampling with maximum variation across healthcare settings, geographic contexts, and stakeholder groups to capture diverse perspectives. Response bias, including social desirability bias, was mitigated by ensuring participant confidentiality and anonymity, emphasizing voluntary participation, and using open-ended, non-leading interview questions. To reduce bias related to study design and instrumentation, the interview guide was developed based on a literature review and expert consultation and was reviewed by the research team prior to data collection.

During data analysis, researcher bias was addressed through investigator triangulation, with multiple researchers independently coding transcripts and discussing interpretations to reach consensus. Reflexive discussions were conducted throughout the analytical process to critically examine assumptions and enhance credibility. Member checking was also used to validate key interpretations and reduce the risk of misrepresentation.

Data collection procedures

The interview guide was developed using the study’s theoretical framework as a guiding structure. Key domains of the framework informed the formulation of open-ended questions to ensure systematic exploration of workforce, technological, policy, organizational, and community-related factors influencing health service quality. At the same time, the semi-structured design allowed participants to introduce perspectives and experiences beyond the predefined domains, supporting an inductive qualitative approach.

File 1

Interviews were conducted either face-to-face or via secure online platforms, depending on participant availability and logistical considerations, and each interview lasted approximately 45–60 min. All interviews were conducted in Bahasa Indonesia, audio-recorded with participant consent, and supplemented by field notes documenting contextual information and initial analytical impressions. Audio recordings were transcribed verbatim, and transcripts were reviewed for completeness and accuracy prior to analysis.

Processes, interventions, and comparisons

This study employed a qualitative exploratory design and did not involve the implementation or evaluation of experimental interventions. Instead, the analysis focused on documenting existing practices, perceived challenges, and stakeholder-identified strategies related to health service quality improvement. Participants described routine processes such as staff training, supervision, use of digital health tools, policy implementation practices, and mechanisms for community feedback. Comparisons were conducted analytically across participant groups and healthcare settings to identify similarities and differences in perspectives and experiences. For example, views expressed by healthcare providers were compared with those of managers, policymakers, and community representatives, as well as across urban and rural service contexts. These analytical comparisons supported an in-depth understanding of contextual variation in health service quality.

Data analysis

The unit of analysis in this study was the healthcare facility, defined as an organizational setting in which health services are delivered and managed. Each facility public primary health centers, private clinics, and district-level hospitals was treated as an individual case. This unit of analysis allowed examination of how organizational context, workforce capacity, digital health readiness, policy implementation, and community engagement interact to influence health service quality within and across cases.

Cases were selected using purposive sampling to achieve maximum variation across healthcare settings. Selection criteria included facility type (primary health center, private clinic, or district hospital), geographic location (urban, peri-urban, or rural), and level of service complexity. Facilities were required to have been operational for at least two years and to be actively involved in routine service delivery and quality improvement activities. These criteria ensured that each case had sufficient organizational experience to provide meaningful insights into health service quality processes. Within each selected facility, participants were recruited based on predefined inclusion criteria. Eligible participants included healthcare providers, facility managers, policymakers, and community representatives with at least one year of experience in their current role and direct involvement in service delivery, management, policy implementation, or community engagement. Individuals with less than one year of experience or limited involvement in quality-related activities were excluded. The final sample size was determined by data saturation, defined as the point at which no new themes or substantive insights emerged from successive interviews.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of Politeknik Sandi Karsa, Indonesia (approval number: B-582/PT19.2.1/PP/VI/2025). This study was conducted in full compliance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants were provided with detailed information about the study prior to participation, and written or verbal informed consent was obtained. Participation was voluntary, and confidentiality and anonymity were strictly maintained throughout the research process.

Results

Guided by a multiple-case study design, the analysis examined similarities and differences across healthcare settings and stakeholder groups. Cross-case comparison revealed both shared challenges and context-specific strategies for improving health service quality. Guided by the theoretical framework, the analysis identified several interrelated themes corresponding to the key domains of workforce capacity, digital health readiness, policy implementation and organizational support, and community engagement. While these domains provided an initial analytical orientation, the findings also revealed cross-cutting interactions and contextual factors that extended beyond the original framework, reflecting the complexity of health service quality in real-world settings. This section presents the findings derived from the thematic analysis of in-depth interviews with healthcare providers, facility managers, policymakers, and community representatives across multiple healthcare settings in Indonesia. The study generated a set of interrelated themes that describe key challenges, enabling factors, and strategic opportunities for improving the quality of health services. Results are presented in tabular form to summarize themes and subthemes, followed by a narrative synthesis and a visual representation of thematic prominence.

Overview of participants

A total of 22 participants were included in the analysis, representing diverse roles within the health system. Participants included healthcare providers (doctors, nurses, and midwives), facility managers, district-level policymakers, and community representatives. They were drawn from public primary health centers, private clinics, and district hospitals located in urban, peri-urban, and rural areas. This diversity enabled comparison across professional roles and service contexts.

The study generated several major themes that reflect participants’ perspectives on the current state of health service quality, the challenges they encounter, and the strategies they believe are essential for meaningful and sustainable improvement. These themes capture both structural and operational issues, including workforce capacity, digital health integration, policy implementation, organizational support, and community involvement. The results are organized according to these thematic areas, with illustrative quotations from participants included to provide depth, context, and authenticity to the findings. Together, these insights provide a comprehensive understanding of the complex factors that shape health service quality and highlight opportunities for strengthening the health system.

Workforce capacity and competency

Workforce-related issues emerged as the most prominent theme across all participant groups. Healthcare providers consistently described high workloads, limited opportunities for continuing professional development, and insufficient mentoring or supervision. Participants emphasized that training opportunities were often sporadic and not aligned with practical service demands. Managers highlighted the need for structured capacity-building programs that integrate clinical skills, leadership development, and teamwork. Differences were observed between urban and rural settings, with rural facilities reporting greater challenges related to staffing shortages and access to training. These limitations were perceived as directly affecting service responsiveness, patient safety, and the consistency of care.

Digital health readiness

Digital health implementation varied substantially across facilities. Some participants reported routine use of electronic medical records and teleconsultation tools. In contrast, others described limited or inconsistent use due to poor internet connectivity, insufficient equipment, and low staff digital literacy. In several cases, digital systems were perceived as increasing the administrative burden due to duplicate data entry and poor system integration. Facility managers and policymakers acknowledged the potential of digital health to improve efficiency and coordination. However, they emphasized that technology alone was insufficient without adequate training, technical support, and alignment with existing workflows.

Policy implementation and organizational support

Participants noted that national policies and quality standards were generally well defined but challenging to implement consistently at the facility level. Bureaucratic procedures, limited operational autonomy, and insufficient resources were commonly cited barriers. Facility managers emphasized the importance of supportive leadership and flexible implementation approaches that allow adaptation to local contexts. Policymakers acknowledged these challenges and highlighted the need for improved monitoring, supervision, and communication between policy and service delivery levels.

Community engagement and patient experience

Community engagement was described as limited and largely informal. While some facilities collected patient feedback, mechanisms were often unstructured and rarely integrated into systematic quality improvement processes. Community representatives reported communication gaps and expressed a desire for greater involvement in service planning and evaluation. Participants agreed that strengthening patient feedback systems and community participation could improve service responsiveness, trust, and accountability.

Themes that were consistently described by participants as having a direct and substantial influence on service delivery and quality outcomes were categorized as having a high perceived impact. Themes discussed with moderate emphasis or primarily as enabling or contextual factors were classified as having moderate or moderate-to-high perceived impact.

Discussion

This study provides an in-depth qualitative examination of factors influencing health service quality in Indonesia by incorporating perspectives from healthcare providers, managers, policymakers, and community representatives across diverse service settings. Overall, the findings demonstrate that a complex and interrelated set of factors, including workforce capacity, digital health readiness, policy implementation, organizational support, and community engagement, shapes health service quality. Taken together, these results reinforce the argument that quality improvement in health systems requires integrated, system-level approaches rather than isolated or single-focus interventions [30].

First and foremost, workforce capacity and competency emerged as the most prominent and influential theme across participant groups [31]. Participants consistently highlighted challenges related to limited access to continuous professional development, high workloads, staffing shortages, and gaps in managerial and leadership skills [32]. Importantly, these challenges were perceived as directly affecting service responsiveness, care consistency, and patient experience [33]. This finding is consistent with existing research emphasizing that human resources are the backbone of health system performance, particularly in low- and middle-income countries [34]. When healthcare workers lack adequate training, supervision, and institutional support, the quality of care becomes uneven and difficult to sustain [35]. Consequently, the strong emphasis on workforce issues in this study underscores the need to prioritize capacity-building strategies that go beyond short-term training and incorporate mentoring, supportive supervision, and leadership development [36].

In addition to workforce capacity, digital health readiness was identified as a key factor influencing service quality [37]. Participants acknowledged the potential of digital technologies to improve efficiency, coordination, and data-driven decision-making [38]. However, this potential was often constrained by uneven infrastructure, limited digital literacy, poor system interoperability, and increased administrative burden [39]. Notably, participants described situations in which digital systems added complexity rather than improving workflows [40]. These findings align with broader evidence suggesting that digital health initiatives may fail to deliver meaningful improvements when they are implemented without adequate attention to user capacity, local context, and organizational readiness [41]. Therefore, the results of this study suggest that digital health should be positioned as an enabling component of quality improvement, rather than a stand-alone solution [42]. To be effective, technological investments must be accompanied by training, technical support, and governance mechanisms that ensure alignment with routine service delivery [43].

Furthermore, policy implementation and organizational support were identified as critical determinants of health service quality, although they were discussed less frequently than workforce and digital health issues [44]. Participants generally perceived national quality policies and standards as well-designed; nevertheless, they emphasized persistent challenges in translating these policies into practice at the facility level [45]. Bureaucratic procedures, limited operational autonomy, and insufficient leadership support were commonly cited barriers [46]. This finding reflects a well-documented gap between policy formulation and implementation, particularly in decentralized health systems [47]. In this context, the study highlights that effective quality improvement depends not only on the existence of policies but also on local organizations’ capacity to interpret, adapt, and operationalize them [48]. Strengthening facility-level leadership and enabling greater local adaptation may therefore enhance the practical impact of national quality initiatives [49].

Similarly, community engagement and patient experience emerged as critical enablers of sustainable quality improvement, even though they were discussed less frequently [50]. Participants reported that mechanisms for capturing patient feedback were often informal, poorly documented, or not systematically used for improvement purposes. Meanwhile, community representatives expressed a desire for more meaningful involvement in service planning and evaluation [51]. These findings are consistent with growing recognition that patient-centered and community-engaged approaches are essential for improving service responsiveness, trust, and accountability [52]. Without structured mechanisms to incorporate patient and community perspectives, health services risk overlooking critical dimensions of quality, such as communication, respect, and cultural appropriateness [53]. As such, strengthening community engagement may serve as an essential lever for improving both perceived and actual service quality [54].

Taken together, an essential contribution of this study lies in its holistic perspective on health service quality. Rather than examining individual factors in isolation, the findings illustrate how workforce, technology, policy, and community-related elements interact to shape healthcare delivery [55]. Participants repeatedly emphasized that improvements in one area are unlikely to be effective unless complementary changes in others support them [56]. For example, digital health systems cannot function optimally without trained staff, supportive leadership, and clear implementation guidelines, while organizational and policy environments constrain workforce capacity-building efforts [57]. This systems-oriented perspective aligns with contemporary health system strengthening frameworks and reinforces the importance of coordinated, multi-level interventions [58].

From a practical standpoint, these findings have several implications for health system stakeholders. Policymakers should prioritize sustained investment in workforce development, including continuous professional education, mentoring, and leadership training [59]. At the same time, health system leaders should ensure that digital health initiatives are implemented in a context-sensitive and user-centered manner [60]. Facility managers may benefit from greater autonomy and organizational support to adapt quality improvement strategies to local conditions. Finally, structured and formalized mechanisms for community engagement should be integrated into routine service management to ensure that patient perspectives meaningfully inform quality improvement efforts [61].

Nevertheless, this study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the qualitative design and purposive sampling approach limit the generalizability of the findings. The results reflect the experiences of participants from selected healthcare settings and may not fully represent all regions or facility types in Indonesia. Second, the reliance on self-reported data introduces the possibility of recall bias or social desirability bias. Participants may have emphasized certain challenges or underreported others based on personal or organizational considerations. Third, although thematic prominence was used to illustrate the relative importance of themes, this approach does not imply quantitative measurement or causal relationships. Therefore, the visual representations should be interpreted as conceptual summaries rather than statistical comparisons [62].

In addition, this study did not include direct observation of service delivery or analysis of routine service data, which could have provided complementary insights into actual practice. Future research could address these limitations by combining qualitative approaches with quantitative measures, longitudinal designs, or intervention studies to assess the effectiveness of specific quality improvement strategies. This study enhances understanding of health service quality in Indonesia by demonstrating how workforce capacity, digital health readiness, policy implementation, and community engagement interact to influence service delivery. The findings emphasize the need for integrated, context-sensitive approaches to quality improvement and offer valuable insights for policymakers, health system leaders, and practitioners working to strengthen health services in resource-constrained settings.

Strengths and limitations

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, as a qualitative multiple-case study employing purposive sampling, the findings are not intended for statistical generalization. Rather, they reflect in-depth insights from selected healthcare facilities and stakeholder groups and may not fully capture the diversity of all regions or facility types in Indonesia. Nevertheless, the use of multiple cases and maximum variation sampling enhances the analytical transferability of the findings.

Second, the study relied on self-reported data, which may be subject to recall bias or social desirability bias. Participants may have emphasized certain challenges or underreported others due to personal, professional, or organizational considerations. To mitigate this limitation, data were collected from diverse stakeholder groups and analyzed using triangulation across roles and settings.

Third, thematic prominence was used to illustrate the relative salience of themes; however, this does not imply quantitative measurement, statistical comparison, or causal inference. Visual representations should therefore be interpreted as conceptual summaries that reflect the depth and frequency of qualitative discussion rather than numerical importance.

In addition, the study did not include direct observation of service delivery or analysis of routine service data, which could have provided complementary insights into actual practices and system performance. Future research could address these limitations by integrating qualitative findings with quantitative indicators, longitudinal designs, or intervention-based studies to assess the effectiveness of specific quality improvement strategies over time.

Despite these limitations, this study contributes to a deeper understanding of health service quality in Indonesia by demonstrating how workforce capacity, digital health readiness, policy implementation, and community engagement interact across organizational contexts. The findings underscore the importance of integrated and context-sensitive approaches to quality improvement and provide relevant insights for policymakers, health system leaders, and practitioners working in resource-constrained settings.

Conclusion and future directions

This study highlights that health service quality in Indonesia is shaped by the combined influence of workforce capacity, digital health readiness, policy implementation, and community engagement. Workforce capacity emerged as the most critical determinant, underscoring that healthcare workers’ skills, motivation, and support are fundamental to delivering consistent, patient-centered care. Without strengthening human resources, improvements in other areas are unlikely to be sustained. The findings also show that digital health initiatives and quality improvement strategies can enhance service delivery when they are implemented in ways that align with local capacity and organizational context. Adequate infrastructure, staff training, and supportive leadership are essential for ensuring that digital systems contribute positively to care processes. Furthermore, effective policy implementation and meaningful community engagement function as enabling factors that support sustainable quality improvement and strengthen accountability within health services.

The contribution of this study lies in its integrated, system-level perspective, which captures the interactions between human, technological, organizational, and social dimensions of service quality. By incorporating diverse stakeholder perspectives, the study provides contextually grounded evidence relevant to health system strengthening in resource-constrained settings. Future research should build on these findings through mixed-methods or longitudinal studies to evaluate the effectiveness of specific quality improvement interventions. Greater integration of routine service data, direct observation, and patient-reported outcomes would further strengthen the evidence base and inform scalable, context-sensitive strategies for improving health service quality.
