Sulfhydrated TFEB alleviates blast-induced lung injury by maintaining epithelial barrier integrity through the IGF2R/MMP-2/9 pathway
Authors: Jian-kui Du, Cheng-jian Luo, Jun-hui Zhan, An-xin Xiang, You-yi Zhang, Peng Wang, Wei-na Liu, Miao Wang, Wen-qing Bao, Cheng-yu Liu, Sen-yuan Kang, Bo-wen Jia, Jing-yu Wang, Jia-ju Zou, Ou-ou Gao, Lei Zhang, Liang Chen, Chang-nan Wang, Jian Xiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Blast-induced lung injury (BLI) is a life-threatening complication of explosive shock waves, yet its molecular mechanisms remain largely unclear. This study aimed to elucidate the key molecular mechanisms governing epithelial barrier integrity during BLI progression and define the core regulatory function of Transcription Factor EB (TFEB) and its downstream signaling axis in BLI pathogenesis. We also evaluated the therapeutic effect and underlying mechanism of the hydrogen sulfide (H2S)-releasing nanozyme (Pt@Pd–S), intending to offer novel targets and translational therapeutic strategies for BLI. Bioinformatic analysis of single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data revealed activation of adherens junction, tight junction, and multiple stress-related signaling pathways in epithelial cells. Transcription factor analysis identified TFEB as a pivotal regulator in these pathological processes. Functional experiments demonstrated that TFEB overexpression via lentiviral delivery significantly alleviated BLI, as evidenced by reduced pulmonary edema, inflammation, oxidative stress and improved barrier integrity. Insulin-like growth factor 2 receptor (IGF2R) was identified as a novel target of TFEB and participates in the protection against shock wave-induced epithelial barrier damage by regulating the release of matrix metallopeptidase 2 (MMP-2) and matrix metallopeptidase 9 (MMP-9). Furthermore, the Pt@Pd–S nanozyme promoted S-sulfhydration of TFEB at the Cys212 residue, thereby enhancing its transcriptional activity, upregulating autophagy, improving lysosomal function, and maintaining epithelial barrier integrity and mitigating lung injury through modulating the IGF2R/MMP-2/9 signaling pathway. Collectively, our findings uncover a previously unrecognized TFEB/IGF2R/MMP-2/9-autophagy and lysosomal axis in the pathogenesis of BLI and highlight the promise of engineered H2S-releasing nanozymes as a therapeutic strategy for pulmonary trauma.

Introduction

Explosions, a pervasive and devastating phenomenon in military conflicts, terrorist attacks, and civilian practices [1], cause catastrophic blast injuries through the rapid onset of intense overpressure and the subsequent propagation of powerful shock waves [[2], [3], [4]]. Blast waves predominantly affect air-tissue interfaces, such as the lungs, gastrointestinal tract, and brain, with pulmonary blast injury being the most probable cause of severe casualties and long-term debilitation [2,5,6]. Blast lung injury (BLI) affects more than 60% of civilian casualties in conflict scenarios and constitutes 12.8% to 33% of combat-related military injuries [7,8]. Pathologically, BLI is marked by alveolar septal dysfunction, severe pulmonary hemorrhage, interstitial edema, and the infiltration of inflammatory cells. These pathological changes may progress to acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), which not only poses a significant threat to patient survival but also adversely affects their quality of life [5,6]. Current management approaches for lung injuries primarily emphasize supportive care, intensive treatment, mechanical ventilation [5], and no specific alternative therapy exists. Thus, it is essential to investigate the mechanism of BLI to identify further effective treatment approaches.

An increasing number of studies focused on the role of autophagy in acute lung injury (ALI) [[9], [10], [11], [12]]. Autophagy serves as a crucial defensive and protective mechanism in the body, ensuring the maintenance of metabolism and homeostasis by removing protein aggregates and damaged organelles, and is closely associated with the progression and onset of lung diseases [[13], [14], [15]]. Among them, upregulation of autophagy alleviates lipopolysaccharide (LPS) or cecal ligation and puncture (CLP)-induced ALI by inhibiting inflammation and/or oxidative stress [15,16]. Enhanced autophagy also attenuates silica-induced mitochondrial-dependent apoptosis and suppresses cytokine production in macrophages [17], which potentially elucidates a specific molecular mechanism underlying the therapeutic intervention for ALI and silicosis. Therefore, triggering autophagy may be an effective therapeutic target for BLI.

Hydrogen sulfide (H2S) stands out for its important regulatory functions in both health and disease, particularly within the respiratory system. Endogenously, H2S is mainly produced through the enzymatic action of cystathionine β-synthase (CBS) and cystathionine γ-lyase (CSE) on l-cysteine and has been identified as a protective gaseous mediator in models of ALI caused by different triggers [[18], [19], [20], [21]]. Its biological activities include reducing oxidative stress, modulating inflammatory pathways, and supporting cellular survival and tissue regeneration, potentially attributable to the inhibition of the toll-like receptor 4/nuclear factor-κB/NOD-like receptor pyrin domain-containing 3 (TLR4/NF-κB/NLRP3) signaling pathway, activation of the signal transducer and activator of transcription 3/interleukin-10 (STAT3/IL-10) system, and suppression of the protein kinase RNA-like endoplasmic reticulum kinase/eukaryotic initiation factor 2α/activating transcription factor 4/growth arrest and DNA damage-inducible protein 34 (PERK/eIF2α/ATF4/GADD34)-mediated endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress. Despite the therapeutic promise of H2S, conventional H2S donors are often limited by their rapid and short-lived release profiles, which can necessitate higher dosages and potentially increase side effects. This challenge highlights the need for advanced delivery systems that can provide a more controlled and sustained H2S release.

At present, no specific pharmacological therapy has been approved exclusively for BLI, and its clinical management largely follows the principles used for trauma-associated ALI and acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) [4,22]. Current treatment mainly relies on supportive strategies, including oxygen therapy, lung-protective mechanical ventilation, conservative fluid management, antibiotics when infection is suspected, corticosteroids in selected patients, bronchodilators, anticoagulation when clinically indicated, and extracorporeal membrane oxygenation for refractory hypoxemia [23,24]. Although these therapies can improve oxygenation, maintain ventilation, control infection, and support organ function, they have important limitations, such as ventilator-induced lung injury, fluid overload, secondary infection, immunosuppression, bleeding risk, limited availability, and lack of specific effects on oxidative stress, inflammatory amplification, autophagy-lysosomal dysfunction, matrix metalloproteinase-2/9 (MMP-2/9) activation, and epithelial barrier disruption. These limitations highlight the need to develop new mechanism-based therapeutic strategies for BLI. To address this, a novel nanozyme (Pt@Pd–S) with a core-shell architecture has been developed for the management of BLI. This nanozyme is constructed from platinum and palladium cores with a sulfur-enriched shell, allowing for the continuous and localized generation of H2S gas. The uniform distribution of S on the surface of Pt@Pd evidenced by element mapping images and the XPS analysis collectively indicated that sulfur is physically adsorbed on the surface of Pt@Pd nanocrystal in the form of S2−. Intriguingly, recent findings suggest that H2S can mitigate ventilator-induced lung injury (VILI) by influencing autophagic processes [21], pointing to a potential mechanism of action in lung protection. However, whether the Pt@Pd–S nanozyme confers protection against BLI through the activation of autophagy, or via alternative pathways, remains to be fully elucidated.

In this study, we integrated single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) analysis with experimental validation to systematically investigate the mechanisms underlying BLI. Our bioinformatics analysis revealed a significant enrichment of gene sets related to apoptosis, inflammation, oxidative stress and barrier dysfunction in epithelial cells. Further analysis identified transcription factor EB (TFEB) as a central regulator potentially mediating these pathological changes through regulating the insulin-like growth factor 2 receptor/matrix metallopeptidase 2/9 (IGF2R/MMP-2/9)-mediated autophagy-lysosome biogenesis response. Building on these findings, we developed a novel inhalable H2S-releasing nanozyme (Pt@Pd–S) and demonstrated its protective effects against BLI, primarily through promoting TFEB activation and autophagy via S-sulfhydration. Collectively, our work uncovers a previously unrecognized TFEB-IGF2R-MMP-2/9-autophagy and lysosomal axis through modulating epithelial barrier integrity in BLI pathogenesis and highlights the therapeutic potential of engineered nanozymes for pulmonary trauma.

Material and methods

Animal experiment

Male C57BL/6 mice aged 6-8 weeks (20-25 g body weight) were acquired from the Experimental Animal Center at the Shanghai Institute of Planned Parenthood Research, China. The mice were maintained in standardized conditions (22 ± 2 °C ambient temperature, 40% relative humidity) under a 12/12-h photoperiod with free access to autoclaved feed and water. All experimental procedures strictly complied with the ethical standards ratified by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Naval Medical University (20240704052). After a one-week acclimation period, the shock tube-induced BLI model was employed to subject experimental animals to pulmonary blast injury using a specialized shock tube device as shown in Fig. 1A, thereby simulating the lung damage that occurs in real-world explosive environments [5]. Prior to the experiment, animals were anesthetized with sodium pentobarbital (administered intraperitoneally at a dose of 30 mg/kg) to ensure they remained pain-free throughout the procedure. The preliminary parameters for blast injury induction (shock wave pressure) were 200, 330, 390, and 500 kPa to achieve the desired degree of injury. The most stable and reliable shock wave pressure was selected for subsequent experiments. The treatment experiments, NaHS was administered intraperitoneally at a dose of 10 mg/kg, a well-established dose in murine models of acute lung injury [25,26]. Pt@Pd and Pt@Pd–S nanozymes were administered via inhalation nebulization at a dose of 5 mg/kg. The dose of Pt@Pd–S was selected based on two considerations: GYY4137 was administered intraperitoneally at 50 mg/kg according to previously reported effective doses in acute lung injury models [27,28], and the H2S release profile of Pt@Pd–S was quantified and shown to be comparable to that of GYY4137 at its effective dose in terms of cumulative H2S output over the treatment window [29]. All treatments were initiated 1 h after blast. At 48 h after blast exposure, mice were anesthetized again with sodium pentobarbital at 30 mg/kg via intraperitoneal injection. After adequate anesthesia was confirmed by the absence of pedal withdrawal reflex, bronchoalveolar lavage fluids (BALFs) were collected first, followed by terminal cardiac blood collection under anesthesia. Cell culture and model building

HPAEpiCs (type II alveolar epithelial cells) were obtained from the ScienCell Research Laboratory (San Diego, CA) and cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 cell incubator. The cultured condition and procedure were described as previous published reference [30,31]. HPAEpiCs were used between passages 3 and 8. Cell model building was based on previous research as shown in Fig. 4A [32]. Briefly, the medium was removed at 70-80% cell confluence, and cells were washed with PBS. Cells were detached using trypsin-EDTA, neutralized with culture medium, and resuspended gently. A cell suspension of 1 × 105 cells/mL in 1 mL volume was prepared and placed in a 5 mL tube. The tube was sealed, inverted on a shock wave device with coupling gel, and treated at 10 Hz. Treated cells were seeded into culture plates for subsequent experiments.

Measurement of lung wet/body and wet/dry (W/D) weight ratio

Following anesthesia of the mice, the lungs were perfused via the right heart with 20 mL of heparinized saline. The right lung was rapidly excised, blotted dry to remove excess surface fluid, and immediately weighed to obtain the wet weight while calculating the wet-to-body weight ratio. The tissue was then dried in an oven at 60 °C for 72 h. Afterward, the dry weight was measured, and the wet-to-dry (W/D) weight ratio was calculated.

Histological analysis

For histopathological analysis, the left lung tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde solution, embedded in paraffin, and cut into 4 μm-thick sections. Two valid sections were randomly selected from each lung tissue specimen, and five non-overlapping visual fields were captured and analyzed for each section. The tissue sections were subsequently stained using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining kits according to the manufacturer's instructions. Morphological alterations in pulmonary architecture and the extent of lung injury were assessed by light microscopy. The lung injury score was evaluated by two pathologists specializing in pulmonary pathology. The scoring system was based on previously established criteria [33], which are as follows: 0 = normal lung tissue; 1 = minimal inflammatory alterations; 2 = mild to moderate inflammation without significant disruption of lung structure; 3 = moderate inflammation characterized by thickened alveolar septa; 4 = moderate to severe inflammatory changes with the presence of nodules or areas of pneumonitis; and 5 = severe inflammation leading to complete loss of normal lung architecture. An average score was calculated for each tissue section.

Immunofluorescence analysis

Paraffin sections (4 μm) of lung tissues were rehydrated and microwaved in citric acid buffer to retrieve antigens. Two valid sections were randomly selected from each lung tissue specimen, and five non-overlapping visual fields were captured and analyzed for each section. Subsequently, the sections were exposed overnight at 4 degrees Celsius to primary antibodies against CD31 (1:1000, Abcam, ab222783), E-cadherin (1:200, Proteintech, 20874-1-AP), ZO-1 (1:200, Proteintech, 21773-1-AP), Occludin (1:500, Proteintech, 27260-1-AP), TFEB (1:200, Proteintech, 13372-1-AP), LC3 (1:200, Proteintech, 14600-1-AP) and LAMP1 (1:200, Proteintech, 20253-1-AP). Following washing, the sections were treated with an Alexa Fluor-594 conjugated donkey anti-mouse (1:500, Abcam, ab150080) or Alexa Fluor-488 (1:500, Abcam, ab150073) conjugated donkey anti-rabbit secondary antibody solution at room temperature for 1 h in a dark environment. Finally, the sections were washed three times with PBS for 10 min each and stained with DAPI for mounting. Images were captured using fluorescence microscopy (Olympus, Japan). Quantitative analysis of fluorescence was used ImageJ software [34].

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) detection

Pulmonary tissue sections were incubated with 2,3-Dimethoxy-1,4-naphthoquinone (cat. no. D5439; Merck, USA) at a 1:100 dilution for 15 min. Stained specimens were subsequently analyzed by fluorescence microscopy (Olympus, Japan) [35].

ELISA

Levels of IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-10, SOD, and MDA in serum, cell supernatant or bronchoalveolar lavage fluids (BALFs) were quantified using ELISA kits sourced from Lifespan Bioscience (Seattle, WA, USA) or Westang Bioscience (Shanghai, China), in accordance with the manufacturers’ instructions [36].

Permeability assay

Evans blue-albumin (EBA) was administered via the right jugular vein and permitted to circulate for 45 min. Following this, intravascular Evans blue was removed by perfusing the right ventricle with PBS for 2 min. The lungs were then harvested, homogenized in 1 mL of PBS, and incubated in 2 mL of formamide at 60 °C for 72 h. The concentration of Evans blue in the lung tissue was quantified by measuring the optical density at 620 nm (OD620) of the formamide extract and normalized relative to body weight. The amount of Evans blue retained in the lung tissue was calculated using a standard curve, serving as an indicator of pulmonary vascular permeability-higher dye retention corresponding to increased tissue permeability [33].

Total protein levels and counts of inflammatory cells in BALFs

BALFs were obtained through tracheal cannulation and subsequently centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatants were preserved at −80 °C, while the cell pellets were resuspended and stained using Wright-Giemsa stain (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Total protein concentrations in the cell-free supernatants were determined using the Bicinchoninic Acid (BCA) Protein Assay Kit [36].

Western blotting analysis

Lung tissue samples were homogenized in lysis buffer provided by a total protein extraction kit (Beyotime, China) to isolate proteins. For each sample, 20 μg of total protein was loaded per lane, separated by SDS-PAGE gels (Yamei, China), and subsequently transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA). Following blocking, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies specific to E-cadherin (1:1000, Proteintech, 20874-1-AP), ZO-1 (1:1000, Proteintech, 21773-1-AP), Occludin (1:1000, Proteintech, 27260-1-AP), TFEB (1:200, Proteintech, 13372-1-AP), LC3 (1:1000, Proteintech, 14600-1-AP), IGF2R (1:1000, Proteintech, 26593-1-AP), LAMP1 (1:1000, Proteintech, 20253-1-AP), and β-actin (1:10000, Sigma, A5441). After thorough washing with TBST, the membranes were treated with horseradish peroxidase-labeled secondary antibodies for 1.5 h at room temperature. Immunoreactive bands were detected using an ECL chemiluminescence kit (Biosharp, USA) and visualized via a chemiluminescent imaging system. Densitometric analysis of the protein bands was carried out using ImageJ software, with β-actin serving as the loading control for normalization purposes [37].

Lentivirus construction and transfection

The lentiviral expressing the TFEB gene and TFEB with cysteine mutation into serine at the 212 site were provided by Genechem (Shanghai, China). For intra-pulmonary adenovirus transfection, mice were intratracheally instilled with 1 × 108 plaque-forming units (PFU) of lentivirus carrying TFEB gene (Lv-TFEB) and TFEB mutation (Lv-TFEB Mutation) in 30 μL enhanced infection solution. The dosage was determined based on previous reports to achieve overexpression of the TFEB gene [33]. After transfection, lung tissue samples were collected, and the expression of TFEB protein was examined using WB and immunofluorescence methods to verify the transfection efficiency.

Determination of IGF2R promoter activity

A genomic DNA fragment corresponding to the upstream region of the human IGF2R gene transcriptional start site, including three TFEB binding motif (site1: 5′-GTCACGTGAC-3′, site2: 5′-CTCACGTGAC-3′, site3: 5′-GTCACGTGAG-3′), was synthesized and inserted into the pGL3-basic vector to generate the construct designated as pGL3-IGF2R. To generate the mutant plasmid pGL3-IGF2RΔTFEB, which lacks functional TFEB responsiveness, the core sequences were performed the following mutations: site1 5′-GTCACGTGAC-3′ to 5′-GTCACGTGAG-3′, site 2 5′- CTCACGTGAC-3′ to 5′-CTGTGCTTG-3′, site3 5′-GTCACGTGAG-3′ to 5′-GTCACGTGAG-3’. HPAEpiCs were seeded in 48-well plates and maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with an atmosphere of 5% CO2 and 95% air. After 18 h of culture, the cells were co-transfected using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen) with either the pGL3-luciferase reporter constructs (100 ng per well) or the pRL-TK-Renilla luciferase control plasmid (10 ng per well; Promega). Subsequently, transfected cells were exposed to shock wave stimulation. At 48 h post-transfection, luciferase activity was measured using the dual-luciferase reporter assay system (Promega), and firefly luciferase signals were normalized to Renilla luciferase values to ensure accurate data comparison [38].

Preparation and inhalation of Pt@Pd–S

10 mg of Pt@Pd and 1 mg of Na2S were dissolved into 10 mL of water with continuous stirring overnight, dialyze to remove free Na2S and collect the Pt@Pd–S nanozymes. For further use, the obtained Pt@Pd–S was re-dispersed in sterile water by ultrasonic assistance and diluted with complete medium for biological experiments. A Pt@Pd–S solution was administered to animals via inhalation using a nebulizer to expose them to a nano-H2S environment. Briefly, Pt@Pd or Pt@Pd–S was dissolved in PBS to prepare the solution and administered at a dose of 5 mg/kg. The solution was placed in a nebulizer, and the nebulization parameters were adjusted to produce droplets of appropriate size that could effectively reach the animals’ lungs, with the atomization process lasting approximately 15 min. After anesthesia, animals were placed in a closed inhalation chamber and subjected to nebulized treatment [[39], [40], [41]].

scRNA - seq

Single-cell RNA sequencing data analysis was conducted using the Seurat package (v5.3.0) within the R programming environment. Following quality control filtering, data normalization was performed using the NormalizeData function. To address batch effects across datasets, the Harmony algorithm was applied for integrated analysis. Dimensionality reduction was carried out using the RunUMAP function under default parameter settings. Cellular heterogeneity was resolved through the FindClusters function at a resolution of 0.6, while differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the FindMarkers function with an adjusted p-value threshold of <0.05. Cell clustering was performed using the FindClusters function with a resolution parameter set to 0.6. Cell type annotation was performed using a predefined marker gene panel for murine pulmonary cells: endothelial cells were identified by PECAM1, TEK, Cdh5, Mcam, and Vcam1; epithelial cells by CDH1 and EPCAM; monocytes by Cd14 and Itgam; macrophages by Itgax, Cd68, Mrc1, and Marco; dendritic cells (DCs) by Aif1, H2-DMb1, H2-Eb1, H2-Aa, IRF7, PLD4, FLT3, CLEC9A, ZBTB46, and CLEC10A; neutrophils by Gsr, Pglyrp1, S100a8, and Ly6g; B cells by Ms4a1, Cd79a, Igkc, and Cd19; natural killer cells by Ncr1 and Nkg7; T cells by Cd3e, Cd4, Foxp3, Il7r, Cd8a; smooth muscle cells (SMCs) by Acta2, Cnn1, and Tagln; adventitial fibroblasts by Serpinf1, Pi16, and Entpd2; alveolar fibroblasts by Fgfr4, Slc7a10, and Slc38a5 and mesothelial cells by Msln, Wt1, and Upk3b. For transcriptional regulatory network analysis, processed data were converted into loom format and analyzed using the pySCENIC pipeline in Python 3.10. Regulatory networks were constructed using species-specific motif annotations from the RcisTarget database, and results were reported as mean regulation activity scores.

MMP2/9 activity assays

16. Activity assays for MMP-2 and MMP-9 were performed using the MMP-2 and MMP-9 Biotrak Activity Assay kits (GE Healthcare Life Sciences, Buckinghamshire, UK), which enable accurate measurement of the active forms of these enzymes in biological samples such as plasma, urine, and tissue homogenates. The procedures were conducted following the manufacturer's protocol. Where appropriate, the levels of active MMP-2 and MMP-9 were normalized to the total protein content to ensure reliable quantification [42].

Statistical analysis

All histological staining, immunofluorescence detection, image acquisition, quantitative analysis, and lung injury scoring were performed under blinded conditions. Investigators responsible for animal modeling, sample collection, and experimental procedures were independent from those who performed image analysis, quantitative measurements, and histological scoring. All samples and images were labeled with randomly generated numerical codes without group or treatment information. During image acquisition, quantitative assessment, and histopathological evaluation, the investigators were blinded to group allocation and treatment conditions. The blinding codes were revealed only after completion of all data acquisition and analyses, followed by statistical analysis [43]. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 20 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, USA). All data are expressed as mean ± SD. Graphical representations and statistical evaluations were carried out using GraphPad Prism version 9.0. For pairwise comparisons, Student's t-test was applied, while one-way or two-way ANOVA was employed for assessing differences across multiple groups. Statistical significance was defined as a P value < 0.05.

Results

Establishment of a mouse model of BLI and pathological characterization

To accurately determine the blast overpressure thresholds that induce pathological lung injury while maintaining a relatively low mortality rate, mice were exposed to escalating doses of blast overpressure (200, 330, 390, and 500 kPa). As illustrated in Fig. 1B, the survival rates of mice 48 h post-blast were as follows: 0% for 500 kPa (15/0), 7.7% for 390 kPa (13/1), 38.1% for 330 kPa (21/8), and 100% for 200 kPa (15/15). Based on these results, the 200 kPa dose was selected for subsequent experiments. As depicted in Fig. 1C, mice subjected to blast exposure exhibited pronounced pulmonary hyperemia compared to controls. Additionally, both the lung weight-to-body weight ratio and the wet-to-dry (W/D) weight ratio were significantly elevated in the blast group relative to controls (Fig. 1D and E). Histological analysis by H&E staining revealed severe lung injury characterized by hemorrhage, edema, rupture of alveolar septa, and marked infiltration of inflammatory cells (Fig. 1F and G).

Single-cell transcriptomic profiling reveals epithelial cell injury in BLI

To elucidate the complex mechanisms underlying BLI, single-cell suspensions from control and blast-exposed mouse lungs (n = 3 per group) were subjected to single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq). Following rigorous quality control, a total of approximately 41,510 cells were obtained for downstream analysis, including 23,157 cells from control mice and 18,353 cells from blast-injured mice. Unsupervised clustering analysis identified 26 major cell populations after excluding dissociation-induced gene signatures based on the expression profiles of canonical marker genes [44,45]: adventitial fibroblast (285 cells, 0.69%), alveolar fibroblast (60 cells, 0.14%), B cell (6705 cells, 16.15%), T cell (4204 cells, 10.13%), DC cell (2875 cells, 6.93%), epithelial cells (3264 cells, 7.86%), endothelial cells (1401 cells, 3.38%), mesothelial (56 cells, 0.13%), monocyte (6769 cells, 16.31%), neutrophil (152 cells, 0.37%), smooth muscle cell (123 cells, 0.30%), macrophage (13741 cells, 33.10%) and NK cell (1875 cells, 4.52%) (Fig. 2A). To assess the integrity of the pulmonary barrier, we examined the expression and localization of junctional proteins using immunofluorescence staining and immunoblotting. In control lungs, E-cadherin, ZO-1, and Occludin were robustly expressed and displayed continuous, well-defined patterns along cell borders. In contrast, blast exposure led to a pronounced reduction in fluorescence intensity, with disrupted and fragmented staining of these proteins (Fig. 3I and supplementary Fig. S1 E-H). Immunoblotting confirmed decreased levels of E-cadherin, ZO-1, and Occludin in blast-injured tissues (Fig. 3J). Furthermore, Evans blue assay indicated significantly increased vascular permeability in the blast group compared to controls (Fig. 3K). Fig. 3L and M shows that the IgM content and total cell count in the BALFs were also significantly increased in the blast group compared with the control mice.

At the cellular level, we employed type II alveolar epithelial cells to investigate the functional impact of shock waves delivered via a blast wave therapy device. As previous research indicated, an in vitro model of BLI was developed by exposing alveolar epithelial cell cultures, seeded at a density of (1-5) ×105 cells, to 1000 pulses of a shock wave (8 bar, 10 Hz) generated by a blast wave therapy device as shown in Fig. 4A [32]. As shown in Fig. 4B–G, the levels of IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α and MDA in the culture medium of shock wave-injured epithelial cells were significantly elevated, whereas the levels of IL-10 and SOD were significantly decreased relative to those in the control group. Immunoblotting analysis further confirmed a significant reduction in the expression levels of E-cadherin, ZO-1, and Occludin in shock wave-injured epithelial cells (Fig. 4H). Moreover, the level of IgM in the culture medium of shock wave-injured epithelial cells was also significantly elevated relative to those in the control group (Fig. 4I).

Taken together, single-cell transcriptomic and molecular analyses revealed that BLI in mice is characterized by enhanced epithelial inflammation, oxidative stress and disruption of pulmonary epithelial barrier integrity.

IGF2R is required for aberrant TFEB-mediated autophagy and lysosomal dysfunction in the context of BLI

Next, we explored the exact mechanism of TFEB in regulating autophagy in the context of BLI. We integrated scRNA-seq data with a publicly available transcription factor database (Gene Transcription Regulation Database, GTRD) and employed TFEB as the transcription factor of interest to identify its downstream target genes. Following interaction analysis of the prediction results, we found that IGF2R might be as a potential key gene implicated in TFEB-mediated autophagy dysfunction contributing to BLI (Fig. 8A). Further analysis demonstrated significant downregulation of IGF2R in epithelial cells in the blast-exposed group compared to controls (Fig. 8B and C). Bioinformatic analysis revealed multiple TFEB consensus sequence located within the proximal region of the IGF2R promoter, and the three binding sites with the highest ratings are selected for further analysis. Several studies have demonstrated that IGF2R regulates autophagy by modulating lysosomal function [49,50]. Therefore, the present study investigated whether IGF2R is required for TFEB dysregulation-induced autophagy and lysosomal dysfunction in lung epithelial cells exposed to shock wave. As shown in Fig. 8D and E, shock wave exposure led to a significant decrease in IGF2R expression at both the mRNA and protein levels. TFEB activator partially reversed the shock wave-induced downregulation of IGF2R expression. Moreover, TFEB siRNA not only reduced IGF2R protein expression but also exacerbated the suppressive effect of blast injury on IGF2R levels (Fig. 8F). We then generated an IGF2R reporter gene by cloning the promoter region of the mouse IGF2R into the pGL3 vector, thereby further confirming that the TFEB activator enhances IGF2R expression through the TFEB binding site within the IGF2R gene. As shown in Fig. 8G, shock wave treatment of cells significantly reduced the luciferase activity of the IGF2R reporter gene, which was significantly reversed by TFEB activator. We then constructed the three IGF2R reporter gene containing a mutated TFEB binding site and transfected it into epithelial cells. The results showed that treatment of cells with shock wave significantly decreased luciferase activity of the IGF2R reporter gene and the inhibitory role was dramatically reversed by TFEB activator. The basal luciferase activity of the mutant1 reporter gene was also lower than that of the wild-type IGF2R reporter gene. However, shock wave and TFEB activator have no obvious effect on luciferase activity of the IGF2R reporter gene as compared to the basal luciferase activity of this mutant1 (−29 to −20) reporter gene. Notably, the basal luciferase activity of the mutant2 and mutant3 reporter genes were no significant difference than that of the wild-type IGF2R reporter gene. Whereas the inhibitory effect of shock wave on luciferase activity of the IGF2R mutant2 (−75 to −66) and mutant3 (−133 to −124) reporter genes were both mitigated by TFEB activator (Fig. 8H). Interestingly, TFEB siRNA have no obvious effect on luciferase activity of the IGF2R reporter gene as compared to the basal luciferase activity of this mutant1 (−29 to −20) reporter gene (Fig. 8I). Synthesis and characterization of H2S-releasing nanozyme Pt@Pd–S

Recent studies have highlighted the pivotal role of H2S as a gaseous signaling molecule in various physiological and pathological processes. Notably, emerging evidence indicates that H2S can regulate TFEB activity through post-translational modification, specifically via persulfidation (also known as S-sulfhydration). Given the potential of H2S to modulate TFEB and thereby influence autophagy and cellular homeostasis, we sought to explore the regulatory relationship between H2S and TFEB in the context of BLI.

To test this hypothesis, we synthesized a novel H2S-releasing nanozyme, Pt@Pd–S. TEM analysis revealed that both Pt@Pd and Pt@Pd–S nanoparticles exhibited a highly uniform, flower-like porous morphology. High-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) images further confirmed that platinum (Pt) and palladium (Pd) were predominantly localized within the core of the nanoparticles, establishing a well-defined core-shell structure (Pt@Pd). Elemental mapping demonstrated a homogeneous distribution of sulfur on the nanoparticle shell, verifying successful sulfur modification.

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was employed to further characterize the surface chemistry of the nanoparticles. A characteristic absorption peak at approximately 1020 cm−1, corresponding to the S–O bond, was observed in Pt@Pd–S but not in Pt@Pd, confirming the incorporation of sulfur. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis showed binding energies of 72.5 eV for Pt (Pt0) and 335 eV for Pd (Pd0), indicating the presence of metallic platinum and palladium, respectively. Additionally, sulfur exhibited two distinct peaks at 162 eV (S2−) and 168 eV (S6+), suggesting the coexistence of multiple valence states.

Surface charge analysis indicated that the zeta potential of Pt@Pd–S (−15 mV) was lower than that of Pt@Pd (−10 mV), likely due to the introduction of sulfur, which may enhance the hydrophilicity of the nanoparticles. Finally, the H2S release kinetics of Pt@Pd–S were evaluated at pH 6.5. Discussion

The lung's extensive air-tissue interface makes it particularly vulnerable to blast injuries, which can severely compromise survivors' quality of life and long-term prognosis. Recent studies have begun to unravel the complex cellular and molecular mechanisms underlying BLI, implicating processes such as inflammation, oxidative stress, immune dysregulation, apoptosis, and barrier dysfunction. In our study, by integrating single-cell transcriptomics with experimental validation, we demonstrate that BLI is characterized by pronounced inflammation, and disruption of barrier integrity. Importantly, we identify impaired autophagy-driven by dysregulated TFEB/IGF2R/MMP-2/9 pathway-as a central pathogenic mechanism. Notably, we show that the nanozyme Pt@Pd–S facilitates the S-sulfhydration of TFEB at the Cys212 residue, thereby enhancing its transcriptional activity, promoting autophagy and lysosomal biogenesis, preserving epithelial barrier integrity and ultimately mitigating BLI.

Nowadays, H2S has emerged as a crucial endogenous modulator in the pathogenesis of ALI, exerting protective effects by regulating inflammation, mitigating oxidative stress, and inhibiting both apoptosis and pyroptosis. Previous studies using ALI rodent models-such as LPS-induced or ventilator-associated injury-have consistently shown that H2S donor compounds (e.g., GYY4137, NaHS) significantly reduce inflammation and cell death [21,28,53]. Building on these findings, our study introduces a novel sulfur-anchored, inhalable Pt@Pd–S nanozyme, created by chemically conjugating H2S donors to Pt@Pd nanoclusters. This nanozyme offers several advantages over conventional H2S donors: (1) sustained H2S release, (2) potent catalase-mimetic activity, (3) greater cost-effectiveness compared to GYY4137, and (4) an inhalable formulation with enhanced lung-targeting specificity. Notably, Pt@Pd–S nanozyme demonstrates significantly superior therapeutic efficacy against BLI compared to NaHS. Future work will focus on thoroughly evaluating the long-term toxicity, immunogenicity, and metabolic fate of nanozymes to ensure their safety for clinical application, as well as exploring their synergistic potential with current therapies such as mechanical ventilation and anti-inflammatory drugs.

Autophagy is a vital endogenous process that maintains cellular homeostasis under both physiological and pathological conditions. Numerous studies have demonstrated that H2S acts as an inducer of autophagy, providing protection in a variety of diseases-including those affecting the endocrine and cardiovascular systems, as well as the lungs [52,54]. H2S activates several signaling pathways, such as PI3K-AKT-mTOR and AMPK-mTOR, to induce autophagy and protect against conditions like hepatic ischemia/reperfusion injury and myocardial fibrosis [55,56]. Consistent with these findings, our study shows that the Pt@Pd–S nanozyme exerts its protective effect against BLI primarily by promoting autophagy. Interestingly, some studies have reported that H2S can also suppress autophagy in certain ALI models [21,57], suggesting that its regulatory effects may depend on the specific experimental context and disease model. Autophagy exerts diverse and context-dependent roles in ALI. Both protective and detrimental effects have been observed across different experimental models of lung injury in response to various pathological stimuli, indicating the complex and dual-function nature of autophagy in the progression and regulation of ALI. In the context of LPS-induced ALI, autophagy within the epithelial layer demonstrates a protective function. Studies have shown that mice with Mtor deficiency in either bronchial or alveolar epithelial cells exhibit reduced airway inflammation and less disruption of the epithelial barrier [58]. Furthermore, following bleomycin administration, mice lacking Atg4b exhibit markedly increased inflammatory activity and enhanced apoptosis of bronchial epithelial cells [59]. However, the function of autophagy in lung endothelial cells is multifaceted and not yet fully understood. Domigan and colleagues reported that the reduction of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) within the endothelium triggers elevated autophagy, ultimately contributing to cell death [60]. Additional research has indicated that autophagy activation is involved in the development of hepatopulmonary syndrome, and suppressing autophagy can alleviate pathological microvascular dilation in the lungs [61]. Studies using models of ischemia-reperfusion-induced lung injury have also demonstrated that autophagy plays a crucial protective role in preserving endothelial function through the ITGAV-ITGB5/integrin αvβ5 signaling pathway [62]. Moreover, the therapeutic potential of mesenchymal stem cells in mitigating the severity of LPS-induced lung damage has been shown to rely on autophagy, which is mediated by the phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K) signaling pathway [63,64]. Dong et al. found that blocking autophagy-either pharmacologically or through siRNA knockdown of ATG5 or ATG7-worsens endothelial barrier dysfunction, suggesting a protective role for autophagy in maintaining EC function during endotoxin exposure [65]. In the current study, the protective role of nanozyme might be attributed to the unique dual functionality of our nanozyme, which not only releases H2S but also mimics catalase activity on the coordinated regulation and functional significance of autophagy in epithelial cells, thereby providing a synergistic therapeutic effect. Further research is therefore necessary to clarify the double-edged sword of autophagy in the context of pulmonary disorders.

TFEB serves as a master transcriptional regulator of the autophagy-lysosomal pathway, orchestrating autophagic flux by binding to the promoter regions of autophagy- and lysosome-related genes. Its role in mediating cytoprotective effects-particularly through antioxidant and anti-inflammatory mechanisms-has been extensively documented in cardiovascular and neurodegenerative diseases [52,[66], [67], [68], [69], [70], [71], [72], [73], [74], [75], [76], [77]]. In pulmonary disease models, upregulation or overexpression of TFEB has been shown to alleviate LPS-induced ALI by enhancing mitophagy [48,78,79]. In line with these observations, our study demonstrates that TFEB overexpression significantly attenuates blast-induced pulmonary injury by reducing oxidative stress, suppressing inflammation, and restoring both structural and functional barrier integrity.

The activity and subcellular localization of TFEB are tightly regulated by various post-translational modifications, including phosphorylation, ubiquitination, PARsylation, S-sulfhydration, and alkylation [52,[80], [81], [82], [83]]. Phosphorylation by mTORC1 and MAPK1/ERK2 at specific serine residues inhibits TFEB nuclear translocation [[84], [85], [86]]. Recent research has identified S-sulfhydration-a redox-sensitive modification-at Cys212 as a critical determinant of TFEB's nuclear accumulation and transcriptional activity. In our study, we demonstrate that the Pt@Pd–S nanozyme promotes S-sulfhydration of TFEB at Cys212, which is essential for its protective effect against BLI. Mutation of this residue abolishes the therapeutic benefits of H2S, confirming the importance of this modification. Additionally, H2S-mediated inhibition of mTORC1 activity may facilitate TFEB dephosphorylation and nuclear translocation, suggesting a dual regulatory mechanism. Further investigation is needed to fully elucidate how Pt@Pd–S modulates mTORC1 signaling and TFEB activity in pulmonary tissues.

IGF2R, the primary receptor for IGF2, primarily participates in the transport of lysosomal enzymes from the trans-Golgi network to lysosomes via early and late endosomes, facilitating their subsequent internalization. Previous research demonstrated loss of IGF2R disrupts the intracellular trafficking of M6P-tagged cathepsins from the Golgi apparatus to lysosomes, resulting in diminished lysosomal activity, abnormal accumulation of these proteases, and impaired autophagy and mitophagy, thereby contributing to the accumulation of misfolded proteins and elevated production of reactive oxygen species [87]. Here, we report for the first time that IGF2R is a novel transcriptional target of TFEB. An aberrant TFEB/IGF2R signaling pathway contributes to lysosomal dysfunction and impaired autophagy in shock wave-treated epithelial cells and lung tissues. Furthermore, downregulation of IGF2R led to lysosomal damage and the release of MMP-2 and MMP-9, which may mediate the degradation and redistribution of ZO-1 and Occludin through proteolytic cleavage.

Based on previous nanoparticle- and nanozyme-based studies in acute lung injury models, the pharmacokinetic behavior of Pt@Pd–S may be influenced by the injured pulmonary microenvironment. BLI is characterized by alveolar-capillary barrier disruption, increased vascular permeability, inflammatory infiltration, oxidative stress, and pulmonary edema, which may facilitate nanoparticle deposition, retention, and cellular uptake in injured lung tissue [88]. Thus, Pt@Pd–S may provide relatively localized nanozyme activity and sustained H2S release in the damaged lung, potentially extending cytoprotective signaling compared with rapidly diffusible small-molecule H2S donors93. From a pharmacodynamic perspective, Pt@Pd–S may exert therapeutic effects through the integration of Pt/Pd-mediated catalytic redox regulation and H2S-mediated signaling. Specifically, Pt@Pd–S reduces oxidative stress and inflammation, promotes TFEB sulfhydration at Cys212 and nuclear activation, restores autophagic-lysosomal function, upregulates IGF2R, suppresses MMP-2/9 activity, preserves ZO-1 and Occludin, and maintains epithelial barrier integrity. These pharmacodynamic effects may be reflected by improvements in ROS burden, inflammatory cytokines, MMP-2/9 activity, tight junction protein expression, BALF protein leakage, lung W/D ratio, and histological injury scores. Nevertheless, the precise blood half-life, pulmonary accumulation, H2S release kinetics, biodistribution, biodegradation, clearance pathway, dose-response relationship, therapeutic window, and PK/PD correlation of Pt@Pd–S remain to be systematically investigated in future studies [89].

From a translational perspective, Pt@Pd–S nanozymes may represent a promising therapeutic strategy for blast-induced lung injury by integrating controlled H2S release with Pt/Pd-based nanozyme activity. This dual-functional strategy may reduce oxidative stress and inflammation, promote TFEB sulfhydration and activation, enhance IGF2R expression, inhibit MMP-2/9-mediated degradation of tight junction proteins, and preserve epithelial barrier integrity. However, it should be emphasized that the effectiveness and safety of Pt@Pd–S in humans have not yet been established. Future clinical translation requires systematic evaluation of H2S release kinetics, therapeutic window, pharmacokinetics, biodistribution, clearance pathways, and long-term biosafety [90]. Potential adverse effects should be carefully considered. Excessive H2S exposure may impair mitochondrial respiration, disturb oxygen utilization, and induce vasodilation-related hypotension [91]. In addition, Pt/Pd-based nanomaterials may raise concerns regarding pulmonary retention, tissue accumulation, immunogenicity, hemocompatibility, complement activation, platelet activation, and coagulation disturbance. Possible drug interactions should also be evaluated, particularly in patients receiving vasodilators, antihypertensive drugs, nitrates, anticoagulants, antiplatelet agents, antioxidants, anti-inflammatory drugs, antibiotics, sulfur-containing drugs, or metal-chelating agents. Therefore, before future human application, comprehensive preclinical toxicology, PK/PD profiling, dose-escalation studies, immunotoxicity assessment, reproductive and genotoxicity evaluation, and interaction studies with standard-of-care medications are required.

This study has several limitations. First, although the murine BLI model recapitulates major pathological features of human blast lung injury, including pulmonary edema, inflammatory infiltration, oxidative stress, and epithelial barrier disruption, it cannot fully mimic the clinical complexity of human blast trauma, which is frequently accompanied by hemorrhagic shock, burn injury, infection, traumatic brain injury, or mechanical ventilation. Second, our study mainly focused on the acute phase of BLI; therefore, the long-term effects of Pt@Pd–S on lung function, post-traumatic fibrosis, and chronic epithelial barrier repair remain unknown. Third, the pharmacokinetics, biodistribution, biodegradation, clearance pathways, repeated-dose toxicity, immunogenicity, hemocompatibility, and long-term biosafety of Pt@Pd–S require systematic evaluation, particularly given the metallic nature of Pt/Pd-based nanomaterials. Fourth, although Pt@Pd–S was designed to release H2S in a controlled manner, the optimal release kinetics, therapeutic window, and dose-response relationship of H2S in the injured lung need further clarification. Finally, validation in human primary alveolar epithelial cells, lung organoids, ex vivo human lung models, or clinical BLI specimens is needed to further support the translational relevance of this strategy.

In summary, our findings reveal that blast-induced pulmonary injury is primarily driven by disruption of TFEB/IGF2R/MMP-2/9-dependent autophagic and lysosomal flux, leading to epithelial cell damage. Stabilization of TFEB or treatment with the Pt@Pd–S nanozyme effectively mitigates BLI by suppressing inflammation and repairing barrier integrity. Notably, the Pt@Pd–S nanozyme activates TFEB through S-sulfhydration, thereby enhancing autophagic and lysosomal flux and providing robust protection against lung injury. These results establish a novel TFEB/IGF2R/MMP-2/9-autophagy and lysosomal axis in the pathogenesis of BLI and highlight the translational potential of engineered nanozymes as innovative therapies for pulmonary trauma. Future studies should further evaluate the long-term safety and clinical applicability of this approach, as well as its synergistic effects with existing treatments.
