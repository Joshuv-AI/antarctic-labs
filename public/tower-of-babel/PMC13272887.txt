Prognostic Value of the C‐Reactive Protein × Platelet‐to‐Lymphocyte Ratio for 28‐Day Mortality in Patients With Sepsis: A Retrospective Cohort Study
Authors: Yuping Duan, Yuxuan Zhang, Jiujia Xiao, Mengjiao Yang, Yi Wen, Dan Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsInflammatory and hematologic biomarkers are commonly used for early risk assessment in sepsis. The C‐reactive protein × platelet‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (CPL) integrates systemic inflammation, platelet‐related thromboinflammation, and lymphocyte‐associated immune suppression. This study aimed to evaluate the association between log‐transformed CPL (lnCPL) and 28‐day mortality in patients with sepsis and to assess its potential value as an adjunctive risk‐stratification marker.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included 630 adult patients with sepsis admitted to the intensive care unit of a tertiary hospital between January 2022 and March 2025. CPL was calculated as CRP × platelet‐to‐lymphocyte ratio and then log‐transformed. The primary outcome was 28‐day all‐cause mortality. Patients were stratified according to lnCPL quartiles, and lnCPL was additionally analyzed as a continuous variable. Multivariable logistic regression, restricted cubic spline analysis, receiver operating characteristic curve analysis, bootstrap internal validation, and admission‐year sensitivity analysis were performed.ResultsAmong 630 patients, 115 died within 28 days, corresponding to a mortality rate of 18.3%. Mortality increased progressively across lnCPL quartiles, from 8.9% in Q1 to 31.6% in Q4. When modeled as a continuous variable, lnCPL was associated with increased 28‐day mortality after adjustment for age, sex, SOFA score, GCS score, mechanical ventilation, CRRT, and AKI (OR = 1.716, 95% CI: 1.402–2.101, p < 0.001). Restricted cubic spline analysis showed a significant non‐linear association between lnCPL and mortality. Bootstrap internal validation showed an optimism‐corrected AUC of 0.685 for lnCPL alone and 0.795 after adding lnCPL to the clinical model. Additional adjustment for admission year did not materially change the association.ConclusionHigher lnCPL was associated with increased 28‐day mortality in patients with sepsis and provided modest incremental discrimination beyond conventional clinical variables. However, because lnCPL overlaps with its inflammatory and hematologic components, it should be interpreted as an adjunctive risk‐stratification marker rather than a standalone independent prognostic tool. Prospective multicenter validation is warranted.Keywords: C‐reactive protein, inflammatory biomarkers, mortality prediction, platelet‐to‐lymphocyte ratio, prognostic factors, sepsis

Introduction

Sepsis is a life‐threatening syndrome caused by a dysregulated host response to infection and remains a major global health burden. A Global Burden of Disease analysis estimated that sepsis accounted for tens of millions of cases and a substantial proportion of infection‐related deaths worldwide [1]. According to the Third International Consensus Definitions for Sepsis and Septic Shock, sepsis is characterized by life‐threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection [2]. Despite advances in antimicrobial therapy, hemodynamic support, organ function monitoring, and critical care management, early identification of patients at high risk of deterioration remains challenging [3]. The pathophysiology of sepsis involves complex and dynamic interactions among systemic inflammation, immune dysregulation, endothelial injury, coagulation abnormalities, and progressive organ dysfunction. Therefore, readily available biomarkers that reflect different dimensions of the host response may help improve early risk stratification in patients with sepsis.

Clinical severity scores, such as the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score and the Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II), are widely used to assess disease severity and predict outcomes in critically ill patients [4, 5]. However, these scoring systems require multiple physiological and laboratory variables and mainly reflect established organ dysfunction rather than the full spectrum of early inflammatory and immunologic alterations. In this context, routine laboratory biomarkers have attracted increasing attention because they are inexpensive, rapidly available, and easily applicable in daily clinical practice. Hematologic inflammatory indices, including the neutrophil‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (NLR), platelet‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (PLR), and systemic immune‐inflammation index (SII), have been investigated as prognostic markers in sepsis [6, 7, 8, 9, 10]. These indices may reflect the balance between innate immune activation, platelet‐related inflammatory responses, and lymphocyte‐associated immune suppression. Nevertheless, their discriminatory performance varies across studies, and their clinical utility remains limited when used alone.

C‐reactive protein (CRP) is a classical acute‐phase reactant and is widely used as a marker of systemic inflammation in infectious and inflammatory diseases. In patients with sepsis, CRP, procalcitonin, and immune‐cell‐derived ratios have been evaluated for their associations with disease severity and prognosis, although the prognostic value of individual biomarkers remains inconsistent [11]. Platelets are increasingly recognized not only as mediators of hemostasis but also as active participants in inflammation, immunity, endothelial interaction, and thromboinflammatory responses [12]. In parallel, lymphopenia is a common feature of sepsis‐associated immune suppression and has been associated with adverse clinical outcomes [13]. Therefore, combining CRP with hematologic indices may provide a more integrated representation of systemic inflammation, thromboinflammatory activity, and immune dysregulation than single biomarkers alone.

The C‐reactive protein × platelet‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (CPL), calculated as CRP multiplied by PLR, is a simple composite inflammatory‐hematologic index. Its biological rationale is based on the integration of systemic inflammatory burden, platelet‐related thromboinflammation, and lymphocyte‐associated immune status. Recent studies have shown that composite indices derived from routine inflammatory, nutritional, and hematologic variables, such as the CRP‐albumin‐lymphocyte index and combined NLR‐related indices, may have prognostic value in critically ill patients with sepsis [14, 15]. In septic shock, combined inflammatory‐marker strategies such as SII plus procalcitonin have also shown potential value for short‐term prognostic assessment [16]. These findings support the broader concept that integrated biomarker strategies may better capture the multidimensional host response than isolated laboratory parameters. However, the prognostic value of CPL specifically calculated as CRP × PLR remains insufficiently characterized in patients with sepsis.

Importantly, CPL is mathematically derived from CRP and PLR, while related indices such as NLR and SII share overlapping hematologic components. Therefore, the interpretation of CPL requires careful consideration of potential collinearity and overlapping biological information. In addition, categorizing continuous biomarkers into quartiles may lead to information loss and may obscure non‐linear dose‐response relationships. Accordingly, analyses evaluating CPL both as a categorized and continuous variable, assessing potential non‐linearity, and examining model stability are needed to clarify its prognostic relevance more rigorously.

In this retrospective cohort study, we aimed to evaluate the association between log‐transformed CPL (lnCPL) and 28‐day all‐cause mortality in patients with sepsis. We further examined the dose‐response relationship between lnCPL and mortality, assessed whether lnCPL provided adjunctive prognostic information beyond conventional clinical variables, and evaluated the robustness of the findings through multicollinearity diagnostics, internal validation, subgroup analyses, and admission‐year sensitivity analysis.

Methods

Study Design and Data Source

This retrospective cohort study was conducted using data from patients admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) of The First Affiliated Hospital of Chengdu Medical College, a tertiary teaching hospital, between January 2022 and March 2025. Data were extracted from the hospital's electronic medical record (EMR) system. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of The First Affiliated Hospital of Chengdu Medical College, which waived the need for informed consent due to the retrospective and anonymized nature of the data (Approval No. CYFYIRB‐SQ‐28). This study is reported in accordance with the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines.

Participant Selection

Adult patients (≥ 18 years) diagnosed with sepsis according to the Third International Consensus Definitions for Sepsis and Septic Shock (Sepsis‐3) were eligible for inclusion. Sepsis was defined as a suspected or documented infection accompanied by an acute increase of ≥ 2 points in the SOFA score. Exclusion criteria were: (1) ICU stay < 24 h; (2) multiple ICU admissions during the same hospitalization; (3) presence of malignant tumors; (4) underlying hematologic diseases; (5) liver cirrhosis; (6) Acquired Immunodeficiency Syndrome (AIDS); (7) hypersplenism; (8) pregnancy; and (9) incomplete records for any of the variables required for the primary analysis.

Data Collection and Variable Definitions

Baseline demographic and clinical data were collected upon ICU admission, including age and sex. All physiological and laboratory data were extracted from the first 24 h of the ICU stay. If multiple laboratory measurements were available within the first 24 h after ICU admission, the first available value was used. Laboratory parameters included white blood cell (WBC) count, neutrophil (NEU), lymphocyte (LYM), platelet (PLT), monocyte (MONO) counts, CRP, and red cell distribution width (RDW). The SOFA and Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) scores were recorded as measures of organ dysfunction and consciousness level, respectively.

Acute kidney injury (AKI) was defined according to the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes criteria based on changes in serum creatinine. Mechanical ventilation (MV) and continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) were recorded as organ support variables. Hospital length of stay and ICU length of stay were described as clinical outcomes but were not included as covariates in the mortality regression models to avoid reverse causation and overadjustment.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was 28‐day all‐cause mortality after ICU admission. Secondary descriptive variables included hospital length of stay, ICU length of stay, AKI, MV use, CRRT use.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.1). A two‐sided p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro‐Wilk test. Normally distributed continuous variables were summarized as means with standard deviations, whereas non‐normally distributed variables were summarized as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs). Categorical variables were summarized as counts and percentages. Between‐group comparisons according to 28‐day survival status were performed using the Student's t test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables, and the Pearson Chi‐square test or Fisher's exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate.

The primary analysis was defined as the evaluation of the association between lnCPL and 28‐day all‐cause mortality. Patients were initially divided into four groups according to lnCPL quartiles. Differences in 28‐day mortality across lnCPL quartiles were evaluated using the Pearson Chi‐square test. The Cochran‐Armitage trend test was used to assess the trend in mortality across increasing lnCPL quartiles. When the overall comparison was statistically significant, post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed with Bonferroni correction.

To avoid information loss caused by categorization, lnCPL was additionally analyzed as a continuous variable in logistic regression models. Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. Sequential logistic regression models were constructed to assess the robustness of the association between lnCPL and 28‐day mortality. Model 1 was unadjusted. Model 2 was adjusted for age and sex. Model 3 was further adjusted for SOFA and GCS scores. Model 4 was additionally adjusted for MV, CRRT, and AKI. Covariates were selected based on clinical relevance, prior literature, temporal availability, and avoidance of post‐baseline outcome variables.

Because lnCPL is mathematically derived from CRP and PLR, and because PLR, NLR, and SII share overlapping hematologic components, models simultaneously including lnCPL, CRP, PLR, NLR, and SII were considered exploratory component‐adjusted sensitivity analyses rather than primary inferential models. Multicollinearity among variables included in multivariable logistic regression models was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs). A VIF greater than 5 was considered indicative of potentially problematic multicollinearity.

Restricted cubic spline analysis was performed to explore the dose‐response relationship between lnCPL and 28‐day mortality. Three knots were placed at the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles of lnCPL. The median lnCPL value was used as the reference. The spline model was adjusted for age, sex, SOFA score, GCS score, MV, CRRT, and AKI. The overall association and non‐linearity were assessed using likelihood ratio tests by comparing nested logistic regression models.

The discriminatory performance of lnCPL, SOFA score, CRP, NLR, PLR, SII, and other laboratory indices for predicting 28‐day mortality was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. The area under the ROC curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity, and optimal cut‐off value based on the Youden index were calculated.

Internal validation of model discrimination was performed using bootstrap resampling. Specifically, 1000 bootstrap samples were generated with replacement from the original dataset. For each bootstrap sample, the model was refitted, and the AUC was calculated in both the bootstrap sample and the original dataset. The average difference between these two AUC values was defined as optimism. The optimism‐corrected AUC was calculated by subtracting the mean optimism from the apparent AUC.

Subgroup analyses were performed to evaluate the consistency of the association between lnCPL and 28‐day mortality across clinically relevant strata, including age, AKI status, SOFA score, and MV status. lnCPL was modeled as a continuous variable in subgroup analyses. Interaction terms between lnCPL and subgroup variables were included in logistic regression models to test for effect modification. These subgroup and interaction analyses were considered exploratory.

To assess potential temporal changes in ICU mortality during the study period, 28‐day mortality was compared across admission years using the Pearson Chi‐square test. The Cochran‐Armitage trend test was used to evaluate temporal trends in annual mortality. In addition, admission year was included as a categorical covariate in a sensitivity analysis to assess whether the association between lnCPL and 28‐day mortality was robust after accounting for calendar‐year effects.

Results

Baseline Characteristics of the Study Population

A total of 630 patients diagnosed with sepsis were included in the final analysis, with an overall 28‐day mortality rate of 18.3% (115/630). The baseline demographic, clinical, and laboratory characteristics of the study population, stratified by 28‐day survival status, are presented in Table 1. Compared to survivors (n = 515), non‐survivors (n = 115) were significantly older (median [IQR]: 71.0 [65.5, 81.0] vs. 64.0 [52.0, 73.0] years, p < 0.001) and presented with a higher severity of illness, as indicated by a greater SOFA score (8.0 [5.5, 10.0] vs. 5.0 [4.0, 8.0], p < 0.001). Non‐survivors also exhibited a more pronounced inflammatory and immune response, with significantly higher CRP levels (141.5 [61.3, 225.7] vs. 86.2 [36.0, 159.7] mg/L, p < 0.001) and lower lymphocyte (LYM) counts (0.67 [0.34, 1.09] vs. 1.02 [0.62, 1.58] × 109/L, p < 0.001). Although the median GCS score was 15 for both groups, a statistically significant difference was found (p = 0.033), indicating a difference in score distribution. Non‐survivors had a shorter length of hospital stay but a longer ICU stay. No significant between‐group differences were observed for sex, neutrophil (NEU), platelet (PLT), RDW, or monocyte (MONO) counts (all p > 0.05).

Association Between lnCPL Quartiles and 28‐Day Mortality

A significant dose‐response relationship was observed between increasing lnCPL quartiles and 28‐day mortality (Table 2 and Figure 1). The mortality rates were 8.9% in Q1, 13.4% in Q2, 19.1% in Q3, and 31.6% in Q4. The overall difference in mortality across quartiles was statistically significant, and the trend test also showed a significant dose‐response relationship between higher lnCPL quartiles and increased 28‐day mortality. Post‐hoc pairwise comparisons showed that the mortality rate in the highest lnCPL quartile was significantly higher than that in Q1 and Q2 after Bonferroni correction. Other pairwise comparisons did not remain statistically significant after multiple‐comparison adjustment.

In the crude model, each 1‐unit increase in lnCPL was associated with a higher risk of 28‐day mortality. This association remained statistically significant after adjustment for age and sex, and further adjustment for SOFA score and GCS score. In the clinical support‐adjusted model, which additionally included MV, CRRT, and AKI, lnCPL remained significantly associated with 28‐day mortality. In the exploratory component‐adjusted sensitivity model, which simultaneously included lnCPL, CRP, PLR, NLR, and SII, the association between lnCPL and 28‐day mortality was attenuated and was no longer statistically significant (OR = 1.231, 95% CI: 0.880–1.722, p = 0.224). These findings suggest that lnCPL captures prognostically relevant inflammatory and hematologic information, but its effect overlaps with its component and related inflammatory indices.

Multicollinearity Diagnostics

Multicollinearity diagnostics were performed using variance inflation factors. In the primary multivariable model, all VIF values were low, ranging from 1.018 to 1.375, indicating no evidence of problematic multicollinearity. In contrast, in the exploratory component‐adjusted model that simultaneously included lnCPL, CRP, PLR, NLR, and SII, VIF values increased for the inflammatory indices, with the highest VIF observed for SII (VIF = 4.916). These results indicate moderate mathematical and biological overlap among lnCPL and related inflammatory indices. Therefore, the component‐adjusted model was interpreted as an exploratory sensitivity analysis rather than the primary model for estimating the prognostic association of lnCPL (Supporting Information S1: Table S1).

Restricted Cubic Spline Analysis

Restricted cubic spline analysis was performed to further explore the dose‐response relationship between lnCPL and 28‐day mortality (Figure 2). Three knots were placed at the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles of lnCPL, and the median lnCPL value was used as the reference. In the clinical support‐adjusted spline model, lnCPL showed a significant overall association with 28‐day mortality (p < 0.001), with evidence of non‐linearity (p = 0.004). The spline curve suggested that mortality risk tended to increase more prominently at higher lnCPL levels.

Discriminatory Performance and Internal Validation

The discriminatory performance of lnCPL and other clinical or laboratory indicators for predicting 28‐day mortality was evaluated using ROC curve analysis. lnCPL alone showed modest discriminatory ability, with an apparent AUC of 0.685. This was numerically similar to the AUC of the SOFA score alone, which was 0.686. The AUCs of CRP, NLR, PLR, and SII were 0.636, 0.667, 0.643, and 0.632, respectively. These findings indicate that lnCPL had modest discrimination and was numerically higher than several conventional inflammatory indices, but the magnitude of discrimination remained moderate. Bootstrap internal validation using 1000 resamples showed limited optimism. The optimism‐corrected AUC was 0.685 for lnCPL alone. The clinical model including age, sex, SOFA score, GCS score, MV, CRRT, and AKI had an apparent AUC of 0.774 and an optimism‐corrected AUC of 0.761. After adding lnCPL to this clinical model, the apparent AUC increased to 0.811, and the optimism‐corrected AUC increased to 0.795. These results suggest that lnCPL provided a modest incremental improvement in discrimination beyond conventional clinical variables. Subgroup Analysis

Subgroup analyses were performed to evaluate the consistency of the association between lnCPL and 28‐day mortality across clinically relevant strata, including age group, AKI status, SOFA score group, and MV status. The positive association between lnCPL and 28‐day mortality was generally consistent across subgroups. Specifically, higher lnCPL was significantly associated with increased 28‐day mortality in both age subgroups, in patients with AKI, in both SOFA score subgroups, and in patients with or without MV. The association did not reach statistical significance only in the relatively small non‐AKI subgroup. No significant interactions were observed for age group, AKI status, SOFA score group, or MV status, suggesting that the association between lnCPL and 28‐day mortality was not significantly modified by these clinical factors. Because these subgroup analyses were exploratory and may have been limited by reduced statistical power within some strata, the findings should be interpreted cautiously (Supporting Information S1: Table S3).

Admission‐Year Sensitivity Analysis

To assess potential temporal changes in ICU mortality during the study period, 28‐day mortality was compared across admission years. The 28‐day mortality rates were 19.6% in 2022, 16.3% in 2023, 19.3% in 2024, and 15.9% in 2025. No statistically significant difference in mortality was observed across admission years (Pearson Chi‐square test, p = 0.803), and no significant temporal trend was detected (Cochran‐Armitage trend test, p = 0.817). In the sensitivity analysis additionally adjusted for admission year as a categorical covariate, the association between lnCPL and 28‐day mortality remained materially unchanged (OR = 1.713, 95% CI: 1.400–2.097, p < 0.001). These results indicate that the observed association between lnCPL and 28‐day mortality was not substantially influenced by calendar‐year effects during the study period.

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort study of 630 ICU patients with sepsis, higher lnCPL was associated with increased 28‐day all‐cause mortality. Mortality increased progressively across lnCPL quartiles, and lnCPL remained significantly associated with mortality when modeled as a continuous variable after adjustment for demographic characteristics, illness severity, and early organ support variables. Restricted cubic spline analysis further suggested a non‐linear dose‐response relationship, with mortality risk increasing more prominently at higher lnCPL levels. However, this association was attenuated after simultaneous adjustment for CRP, PLR, NLR, and SII in the exploratory component‐adjusted model. These findings indicate that lnCPL captures prognostically relevant inflammatory and hematologic information, but it should be interpreted as an adjunctive risk‐stratification marker rather than a standalone independent prognostic tool.

The observed association between lnCPL and mortality is biologically plausible in the context of sepsis pathophysiology. Sepsis is not a simple hyperinflammatory condition but a heterogeneous syndrome involving concurrent inflammation, immune suppression, endothelial dysfunction, coagulation disturbance, and progressive organ injury [17, 18, 19]. The prognostic information carried by a single biomarker is, therefore, usually limited, because no individual marker can fully represent the multidimensional host response. lnCPL may be clinically relevant because it integrates three routinely available dimensions:CRP‐related systemic inflammation, platelet‐related thromboinflammatory activity, and lymphocyte‐associated immune dysfunction. This integrated structure may explain why lnCPL was associated with mortality in our study, particularly at higher values.

CRP is a widely used acute‐phase reactant and remains one of the most accessible inflammatory biomarkers in routine clinical practice. Previous ICU‐based studies have reported that elevated CRP at admission may be associated with increased mortality and prolonged ICU stay in patients with sepsis [20]. However, the prognostic value of CRP alone is inconsistent and may be influenced by infection source, timing of measurement, liver function, antimicrobial treatment, and the phase of the host inflammatory response. Studies evaluating CRP and procalcitonin during the course of sepsis have also suggested that static single‐time‐point measurements may not fully capture the dynamic inflammatory trajectory of septic patients [21]. In the present study, lnCPL outperformed CRP numerically in discrimination, but this should not be interpreted as evidence that lnCPL is a superior biomarker in isolation. Rather, the result suggests that combining CRP with hematologic information may provide a broader but still incomplete representation of risk.

The PLR component of CPL may also carry biologically meaningful information. Platelets are increasingly recognized as active immune and inflammatory cells in sepsis, participating in leukocyte recruitment, endothelial interactions, microvascular dysfunction, coagulation activation, and immunothrombosis [22]. Clinical studies have shown that platelet abnormalities, particularly thrombocytopenia, are associated with a dysregulated host response and adverse outcomes in critically ill patients with sepsis [23]. At the same time, lymphocyte depletion reflects an important aspect of sepsis‐associated immune suppression. Recent reviews have emphasized that lymphopenia in sepsis may represent an acquired immunodeficiency‐like state and may contribute to persistent immune dysfunction and poor outcomes [24, 25]. Therefore, a higher PLR may reflect, at least in part, an imbalance between platelet‐related inflammatory activation and lymphocyte‐associated immune competence. By multiplying CRP and PLR, CPL may amplify the combined signal of systemic inflammation and immune‐hematologic dysregulation.

A central issue raised by our findings is the non‐independence of lnCPL from its components and related inflammatory indices. CPL is mathematically derived from CRP and PLR, whereas PLR, NLR, and SII share overlapping hematologic components, particularly lymphocyte, platelet, and neutrophil counts. In our multicollinearity diagnostics, the primary clinical model showed low VIF values, indicating no evidence of problematic collinearity. However, VIF values increased in the exploratory component‐adjusted model that simultaneously included lnCPL, CRP, PLR, NLR, and SII. More importantly, the association between lnCPL and mortality was attenuated and no longer statistically significant after these related biomarkers were included together. This finding should not be viewed simply as a statistical weakness; rather, it clarifies the nature of lnCPL. lnCPL appears to summarize overlapping inflammatory and hematologic information, instead of providing completely independent prognostic information beyond its mathematical components.

The relationship between lnCPL and established clinical severity variables should also be interpreted cautiously. SOFA reflects established organ dysfunction, which may lie downstream of inflammatory and immune dysregulation. Therefore, adjustment for SOFA can partly adjust for the clinical consequences of the same biological process that lnCPL is intended to capture. In the present study, lnCPL remained associated with mortality after adjustment for SOFA, GCS, MV, CRRT, and AKI. Nevertheless, this does not imply that lnCPL can replace SOFA or other clinical severity assessments. A more appropriate interpretation is that lnCPL may provide complementary information derived from routine laboratory tests and may serve as an adjunctive risk marker, particularly when rapid early risk stratification is needed.

The restricted cubic spline analysis provided additional insight into the pattern of association between lnCPL and mortality. Rather than showing a purely linear relationship, the curve suggested that mortality risk increased more prominently at higher lnCPL levels. This pattern is clinically reasonable, because marked elevation of lnCPL may reflect the coexistence of intense systemic inflammation, platelet‐related thromboinflammatory activation, and lymphocyte‐associated immune suppression. However, the RCS result should be considered exploratory. It should not be used to define a definitive clinical threshold or decision‐making cut‐off without external validation. The purpose of the spline analysis in this study was to reduce information loss caused by categorization and to better describe the dose‐response pattern.

Internal validation using bootstrap resampling suggested that the discriminatory performance of models incorporating lnCPL was reasonably stable, with limited optimism. Adding lnCPL to a clinical model modestly improved the optimism‐corrected AUC. This finding supports the potential incremental value of lnCPL but also emphasizes the need for cautious interpretation. In prediction research, internal validation can assess optimism and model stability, but it cannot substitute for external validation in independent populations [26, 27]. Given the single‐center retrospective design of the present study, the clinical utility of lnCPL should be considered hypothesis‐generating until confirmed in multicenter cohorts.

We also addressed the potential influence of temporal changes in ICU management across the study period. The 28‐day mortality rates did not differ significantly across admission years, and no significant temporal trend was observed. Additional adjustment for admission year did not materially change the association between lnCPL and mortality. These findings suggest that the observed association was unlikely to be driven primarily by calendar‐year effects. However, because the number of patients admitted in 2025 was smaller than in previous years, the temporal sensitivity analysis should still be interpreted cautiously.

This study has several limitations. First, the retrospective single‐center design limits causal inference and generalizability. Second, although we performed multivariable adjustment, multicollinearity diagnostics, restricted cubic spline analysis, bootstrap internal validation, subgroup analyses, and admission‐year sensitivity analysis, residual confounding cannot be excluded. Third, lnCPL was calculated using laboratory values obtained within the first 24 h after ICU admission. Dynamic changes in CRP, platelet count, lymphocyte count, and lnCPL were not evaluated. Given the time‐dependent nature of sepsis biology, serial biomarker trajectories may provide more clinically informative risk assessment than baseline values alone [21]. Fourth, important inflammatory, immune, and coagulation biomarkers, such as procalcitonin, interleukin‐6, monocyte HLA‐DR, D‐dimer, fibrinogen, and endothelial injury markers, were not systematically available in the source database. Fifth, because CPL is mathematically derived from CRP and PLR, its incremental value beyond its components requires further validation. Finally, the study lacked external validation, and the RCS‐derived risk pattern should not be interpreted as establishing a clinically actionable cut‐off.

In summary, lnCPL was associated with 28‐day mortality in ICU patients with sepsis and showed a non‐linear dose‐response relationship with mortality risk. Adding lnCPL to conventional clinical variables modestly improved model discrimination. However, its prognostic information overlapped with CRP, PLR, NLR, and SII, and it should, therefore, be interpreted as a readily available adjunctive marker rather than a standalone independent prognostic tool. Future prospective multicenter studies should validate these findings externally, evaluate serial lnCPL trajectories, and determine whether lnCPL can improve dynamic risk stratification when combined with established clinical severity scores and other immune‐inflammatory biomarkers.

Conclusion

In this retrospective cohort study of ICU patients with sepsis, elevated lnCPL was associated with increased 28‐day all‐cause mortality and demonstrated a non‐linear dose‐response relationship, with risk rising more prominently at higher lnCPL levels. As a composite index integrating CRP‐related systemic inflammation, platelet‐associated thromboinflammatory activity, and lymphocyte‐related immune dysfunction, lnCPL may provide clinically relevant information that is readily available from routine laboratory tests. However, its prognostic signal overlapped with CRP, PLR, NLR, and SII, and the association was attenuated after adjustment for these related inflammatory and hematologic indices. Therefore, lnCPL should not be interpreted as a standalone independent prognostic biomarker or a substitute for established severity scores. Rather, it may serve as an adjunctive marker for early risk stratification in sepsis. Future prospective, multicenter studies with external validation and serial biomarker measurements are warranted to determine whether lnCPL can improve dynamic prognostic assessment and support individualized clinical decision‐making in patients with sepsis.
