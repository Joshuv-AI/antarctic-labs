Knowledge, Attitudes and Practices Toward Hepatitis B Virus Among Transfusion‐Dependent Thalassemia Patients in Jordan
Authors: Nader Alaridah, Haneen O. Abuhani, Rahaf A. Jereisat, Sara Abu‐Mutaw, Raba'a F. Jarrar, Rayan M. Joudeh, Hasan Nassr, Layan Ismail, Haneen Al‐Abdallat, Anas Y. El‐Massad, Heba Mahmmoud, Anas H. A. Abu‐Humaidan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundKnowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) studies provide essential insight into public awareness, perceptions, and behaviors toward health issues. Hepatitis B virus (HBV) remains a major public health problem in Jordan. Transfusion‐dependent patients are at higher risk of infection due to frequent exposure to blood products. This study aimed to assess KAP toward HBV among transfusion‐dependent patients in Jordan.MethodsA cross‐sectional study was conducted among 407 transfusion‐dependent patients attending hematology clinics in governmental hospitals in Jordan. A validated questionnaire assessed socio‐demographic data and KAP regarding HBV. Knowledge, attitude, and practice were each scored out of their respective items; participants scoring ≥ 75% correct were classified as having good knowledge, positive attitude, or safe practices, respectively. Statistical analyses included descriptive statistics, chi‐square tests, and logistic regression to determine associations, with p‐values < 0.05 considered significant.ResultsOf the 407 participants, 114 (28%) had good knowledge, 170 (41.8%) had positive attitudes, and 156 (38.3%) demonstrated safe practices toward HBV. Key predictors of good knowledge included older age (AOR 3.81, 95% CI 2.09–6.94) and higher education (AOR 3.05, 95% CI 1.80–5.16). Safe practice was significantly associated with family history of HBV infection. Significant associations were found between knowledge scores and educational level, employment status, and history of HBV vaccination (p < 0.05).ConclusionHBV knowledge, attitudes, and practices among transfusion‐dependent patients in Jordan were suboptimal. These findings highlight the need for targeted health education programs and HBV vaccination campaigns for this high‐risk group.

Introduction

Hepatitis B infection is recognized as a serious worldwide health problem, with rising rates of morbidity and mortality [1]. In the Eastern Mediterranean Region (EMR), HBV seroprevalence ranges from 2% to 7%, placing countries such as Jordan in the intermediate endemicity bracket [2, 3]. Significant liver‐related conditions such as cirrhosis, hepatocellular carcinoma, and acute‐chronic hepatitis can be caused by Hepatitis B infection. [4, 5]. Despite the implementation of preventive screening, immunization measures, and effective therapies [1], approximately one‐third of individuals infected with Hepatitis B Virus (HBV)in the United States remain unaware of their condition, as estimated by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) [5]. It is crucial to maintain and enhance awareness‐raising initiatives in a culturally sensitive and non‐stigmatizing manner among the population. In addition to providing information about HBV infections, modes of transmission, screening, and vaccines [5]. HBV transmission varies by age and occurs perinatally, in early childhood, and in adulthood. High‐risk adult populations include blood recipients, healthcare workers, hemodialysis patients, intravenous drug users, and individuals with infected sexual partners [1, 5]. Transfusions of infected blood or blood products are a recognized route of transmission, as is percutaneous exposure [6]. HBV is also notable for its environmental stability, remaining infectious on surfaces for up to 7 days at room temperature [7].

In countries with moderate endemicity (2%–7% HBsAg), such as those in Europe and the Middle East, all the abovementioned routes of HBV transmission have been reported [2, 3].

The estimated carrier prevalence of thalassemia in Jordan is between 2% and 4% [8]. According to the Thalassemia Department in the Ministry of Health, there are approximately 1450 patients diagnosed with Beta thalassemia [9]. Patients with beta‐thalassemia are typically classified based on their clinical characteristics and their need for blood transfusions. Those with transfusion‐dependent beta‐thalassemia (TDT) experience severe anemia during childhood and require lifelong, frequent blood transfusions once a month or more to maintain hemoglobin levels within the range between 9 and 10 g/dL [10]. The risk of transfusion‐transmitted hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection has gradually decreased in recent decades. However, it is important to note that HBV transmission still remains the most prevalent viral infection transmitted through blood transfusions [11]. Donations of products that test negative for HBsAg and were obtained either in the pre‐seroconversion stage or in the late phases of the infection are the main factors associated with the risk of HBV transmission [4]. When HBV DNA is found outside of the window period (WP), which is the time interval between infection and the identification of a viral antigen or antibody marker, this is identified as occult HBV infection or carriage (OBI) [12]. It is well known that infections that occur during the window period preceding seroconversion have the highest potential to transmit HBV. The possibility for transmission of occult HBV infections is still being debated; nevertheless, as evidenced by the existence of HBV DNA in the absence of detectable HBsAg [13]. Indeed, it is crucial to explore the level of awareness regarding HBV infection among high‐risk groups, particularly among thalassemia patients in Jordan who are vulnerable to transfusion‐transmitted hepatitis B virus due to their regular blood transfusions. Public awareness toward HBV infection among TDT patient, will play major role in controlling the infection and preventing its complications affecting the patients; a lack of reasonable understanding about HBV would results in poor detection rates, delay in treatment, and stigma. For HBV in Jordan, KAP findings can reveal misconceptions, stigmas, and unsafe behaviors that limit vaccination uptake and timely testing. Among transfusion‐dependent patients, addressing these gaps is essential to reduce preventable exposure and improve care. The objective of the current study is to assess the knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) related to HBV among patients with TDT in Jordan. While KAP gaps toward HBV have been documented among healthcare workers and community groups across the EMR [14, 15], data on transfusion‐dependent thalassemia patients in Jordan remain scarce. This understudied population warrants dedicated investigation given their chronic, repeated exposure to blood products and the implications for infection control [16]. This research aims to provide valuable insights into the understanding, perspectives, and behaviors of this specific group towards HBV, which can subsequently contribute to the development of targeted interventions and educational programs.

Methodology

A multicenter, cross‐sectional descriptive study was conducted among thalassemia patients. It was conducted in three different governmental thalassemia transfusion centers in Jordan: AL Bashir Hospital and New Zarqa Governmental Hospitals in the middle region, and Princess Rahmeh Hospital in the north of Jordan. Data collection for the study was carried out between May and October in the year 2023. A total population (census) sampling approach was used, in which all eligible registered patients attending the three participating centers during the data collection period were invited to participate. The three participating centers had approximately 700 registered thalassemia patients in total. During the data collection period, 30 patients were identified as under 18 years of age at the time of interview and were therefore excluded by the eligibility criteria. Of the remaining eligible adult patients, 15 declined to participate. Patients who did not attend their scheduled transfusion sessions during the data collection period were not captured, which is an inherent characteristic of clinic‐based census sampling over a fixed time window. A total of 407 patients were successfully enrolled, representing ~66.7% of the eligible adult patients who attended during the study period. The inclusion criteria for participants were as follows: (1) thalassemia patients aged 18 years and above, (2) patients who undergo regular blood transfusion sessions, and (3) patients who consented to participate in the study. Exclusion criteria included patients below the age of 18 or those unable to provide informed consent. Informed consent was obtained from all participants who agreed to participate, and those individuals were invited to complete an interview‐based survey. A total of 407 thalassemia patients participated in the study, exceeding the minimum required sample size of 385. The sample size calculation was based on a 5% marginal error and a 50% estimated prevalence.

Measurement Tool

The survey questionnaire was administered in an interview‐based manner and consisted of 35 items. It was developed based on a previously published and validated KAP survey on HBV [17]. The questionnaire was translated from English to Arabic using a forward‐backward translation procedure carried out by bilingual medical professionals to ensure linguistic and conceptual consistency. Four sections made up the questionnaire: one for demographic information, twenty questions for knowledge, seven questions about attitudes, and eight questions about practices. Each correct response was assigned one point, with all items equally weighted. A score of ≥ 75% correct responses was used to define “good” knowledge, positive attitude, or safe practice, based on prior KAP studies in similar settings, where this threshold is commonly applied to distinguish between adequate and inadequate outcomes [17]. Attitude and practice items were scored based on public health‐recommended responses as defined in the original validated questionnaire [17]. The practice item regarding avoidance of HBV patients was retained as part of the original validated questionnaire, reflecting awareness of transmission risk rather than social stigma. The questionnaire was piloted with 30 respondents before conducting the study in order to evaluate its consistency and acceptability. The reliability of the tool was assessed during pilot testing; Cronbach's alpha exceeded 0.7 across all sections, indicating acceptable internal consistency. Based on the pilot testing, a few modifications were made to the questionnaire. However, the final analysis did not incorporate data from pilot research. The tool was made accessible for data collection after the research questionnaires' validity and consistency were validated. Participants were subsequently classified into two binary categories: those scoring ≥ 75% were classified as having good knowledge, positive attitude, or safe practice, while those scoring below 75% were classified as poor ethical considerations.

In compliance with the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki, this study was carried out ethically. The study's procedure was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) at AL Bashir Hospital, Amman, the Hashemite Kingdom of Jordan (Reference number: M.B.A/3208) in meeting No. 2/2022. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants who agreed to take part in the study, and the inclusion and exclusion criteria are described above. Acceptance or refusal to participate did not affect the treatment provided to patients. Informed consent was obtained through a clear, verbal explanation of the study purpose, procedures, risks, and benefits, with opportunities for questions before signing the consent form. For participants unable to read or write, the form was read aloud in the participant's native language, and consent was documented using a witnessed thumbprint signature. Data collection was conducted in a private area within the transfusion unit, either prior to or after the blood transfusion session, to ensure privacy and comfort.

All collected data were anonymized and stored securely to protect confidentiality. Because the interview included sensitive items related to fear, shame, stigma, and avoidance of hepatitis B patients, interviewers were trained to recognize signs of emotional distress and to remind participants that they could withdraw from the survey at any time without penalty. If distress was observed, participants were offered immediate emotional support and, if needed, referral to counseling services available at the hospital.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were performed to analyze the data, and the results were reported as either frequency and percentage for categorical variables or mean and standard deviation for numerical variables. The Chi‐square test was utilized to examine the relationship between demographic factors, knowledge, attitude, and practices. Multivariate regression analysis was conducted to evaluate each independent variable while controlling for potential confounders. Each correct response in the questionnaire was assigned one point. Participants who answered 75% or more of the questions correctly in each section of knowledge, attitude, and practices were considered to have a good score, while those who answered less than 75% correctly were classified as having a poor score (Figure 1). For the purpose of logistic regression analysis, knowledge and attitude outcomes were binary coded, with good scores coded as 1 and poor scores coded as 0. Adjusted odds ratios (AOR) with 95% confidence intervals were reported for each variable in the knowledge and attitude models. Microsoft Excel 2016 was used for data entry, and the data were subsequently imported into IBM SPSS version 25 for analysis.

Table 3 summarizes participants’ attitudes toward hepatitis B infection. 64.4% (n = 262) thought that they could get infected with the virus. Reactions to infection varied among participants, 42.8% (n = 174) would be afraid, 32.7% (n = 133) would be sad, 23.6% (n = 96) would be surprised and only 1% (n = 4) would be ashamed. However, 56% (n = 228) mentioned that they would talk to a physician when infected, and 33.2% (n = 135) would talk to their parents. The remaining 10.8% (n = 44) would talk to their spouse, child or another relative. However, none of the participants mentioned talking to a friend or not talking at all. The majority of participants (85%, n = 346) would visit a doctor if they got infected, 13% (n = 53) would go to a health facility, 7 participants mentioned that they would not visit a doctor and only 1 participant would visit a traditional healer. When asked about the stage of infection at which they would see a physician, 86% (n = 350) answered as soon as they realized the symptoms, 13% (n = 53) would go after 3–4 weeks, 4.7% (n = 19) would go when their own treatment failed, and only 7 participants would not see a physician at all. Almost half of the participants (53.1%, n = 216) had no idea about the costs of diagnosis and treatment of hepatitis B, whereas 27.3% (n = 111) thought it would be expensive or somewhat expensive, 10.6% (n = 43) thought it would be reasonable, and 9.1% (n = 37) thought it would be free. When asked about the main concerns if they got infected, 64.9% (n = 264) mentioned the fear of spreading the infection to their families, 17.2% (n = 70) mentioned the fear of death, 10.6% (n = 43) mentioned the fear of isolation from society and 7.4% (n = 30) mentioned the costs of treatment.

Discussion

Among TDT patients, the elevated risk of HBV infection stems from repeated exposure to blood products, potential contact with contaminated equipment, and compromised immune function [18]. Despite improvements in blood donor screening, occult HBV infection and window‐period donations continue to pose a transmission risk, underscoring the importance of patient awareness [14]. To the best of our knowledge, this study represents the first attempt to investigate the knowledge, attitudes, and practices of TDT patients toward HBVin the Hashemite Kingdom of Jordan. A multicenter cross‐sectional study was conducted, involving a total of 407 participants. The study population predominantly consisted of individuals in the young to middle‐aged range, with an equal distribution between genders. The overall findings indicate that the knowledge, attitudes, and practices related to HBV were generally unsatisfactory among the study population, similar finding have been reported on high‐risk group of HBV [19]. These suboptimal KAP findings are consistent with patterns observed across the broader Eastern Mediterranean Region. Similar knowledge gaps have been documented among healthcare workers and high‐risk community groups such as barbers in the region, collectively underscoring the need for integrated HBV education programs targeting both patients and providers [14, 15, 16]. However, there were some notable patterns observed. Participants aged 28 years and above, particularly those aged ≥ 38 years, and individuals with a higher level of education demonstrated higher levels of knowledge and more positive attitudes. Notably, patients with a family history of HBV showed a higher level of adherence to recommended practices.

In terms of general knowledge about HBV, approximately half of the participants were already familiar with the virus, recognizing it as a viral infection and associating it with symptoms such as jaundice. However, their knowledge about other symptoms of HBV was limited. Moreover, only around 50% of the participants agreed that using contaminated blades, blood, surgical needles, unsterilized syringes, and blood products might spread HBV. It is worth noting that the level of knowledge observed in this study falls below that of the general population, as reported in previous research [20]. These findings highlight the need for targeted education and awareness campaigns to enhance knowledge and understanding of HBV transmission and symptoms among the study population. This study also showed that only around one‐third of individuals were aware that HBV may spread vertically from mother to child and through unsafe sexual contact. Approximately sixty percent of the respondents were aware that HBV can affect liver function. However, only one‐fifth of them were aware of the risk of developing liver carcinoma, which is crucial information for transfusion‐dependent ‐thalassemia patients who are at a higher risk of developing this condition due to recurrent transfusions [21]. Moreover, the respondents’ understanding of HBV treatment was poor. Despite the implementation of the HBV vaccine program in Jordan since 1995 [22]. Only around fifty percent of the participants were aware of HBV vaccination. This finding aligns with a previous study conducted in Jordan among the general population [20]. The findings suggest that while participants are generally aware of the seriousness of HBV, they lack sufficient knowledge and confidence in the treatment process to effectively address the disease. This knowledge gap and lack of confidence can significantly increase the burden of the disease on patients with regular transfusions, impacting various aspects of their daily lives, social interactions, and financial situation [23]. The stigma surrounding HBV is often rooted in poor knowledge about its mode of transmission and the availability of vaccinations, and this stigma has been observed not only among patients but also among healthcare providers and students [24, 25]. However, the study findings indicate a positive attitude towards vaccination among the participants, with approximately half of them being aware of the existence of the HBV vaccine. Around two‐thirds of respondents, believe that they could get HBV infection, which is significantly higher than study in India [26], this may reflect differential access to health information by education level and area of residence, though causal interpretation requires further study. This was reflected in our respondent, as the majority of participants expressed a willingness to undergo further investigations and adhere to safety practices. This demonstrates a proactive approach towards managing the disease and minimizing its transmission. More research is required to determine how prevalent HBV is in people with TDT. More investigation is required to evaluate the long‐term health effects for individuals infected with the HBV, particularly the influence on their liver function, death rates, and general quality of life, as well as the prevalence of HBV and immune status of TDT patients. To gain a more comprehensive understanding of the overall well‐being of TDT patients and to tackle any aversion towards preventive practices and seeking treatment, it would be helpful to also investigate the psychological and emotional effects of living with HBV infection, which includes any associated stigma or social challenges. To our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate the KAP of HBV among TDT patients, which is the strength of our research. This is especially important since it directs attention from healthcare providers to a higher‐risk population. One limitation of the study is its cross‐sectional nature, which relies heavily on self‐reported data. It's possible that there has been inadequate or excessive reporting, and a bias toward social desirability is possible. Additionally, the wide confidence interval observed for the medical field subgroup in the regression analysis (AOR = 7.136, 95% CI: 1.315–38.720) is attributed to the small subgroup size (n = 14), which limits the precision of this estimate and should be interpreted with caution. The Hosmer‐Lemeshow test was not performed; however, model adequacy was supported by the inclusion of only statistically significant variables from the bivariate analysis, consistent with standard practice in KAP studies of similar design. This study has several limitations. First, its cross‐sectional design precludes causal inference. Second, findings may have limited generalizability beyond the three included centers. Third, the interviewer‐administered format may have introduced social desirability bias, potentially causing participants to over‐report positive attitudes despite low safe practice scores. Fourth, the ≥ 75% threshold used for classification may have introduced misclassification, particularly in the attitude and practice domains where item‐level responses varied considerably.

Conclusion

In summary, only 28% of participants demonstrated good knowledge and 38.3% demonstrated safe practices — the lowest‐scoring domain — toward HBV. Attitudes were mixed, and preventive practices including vaccination uptake (33.9%) and participation in health education programs (7.6%) were inconsistent and below recommended levels. Structured HBV educational programs should be implemented at transfusion centers, vaccination strategies should be strengthened particularly for younger and less‐educated patients, and infection control education should be integrated into routine follow‐up care.
