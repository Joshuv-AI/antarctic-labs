‘Today, I’ll manage, but not all days!’ Adult refugees’ meaning-making of learning and coping with trauma and other emotional distress while in language training programmes
Authors: Janita Flem Tomren, Marianne Opaas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: Adverse life experiences evoke thoughts and emotions that may negatively affect adult refugees’ language learning. This may significantly impact the ability to establish their lives in the resettlement country. However, little attention has been paid to adult refugee learners’ own understandings of managing distress following trauma and forced migration while in language learning contexts.Objective: This qualitative study aims to explore how adult refugee learners deal with distressing memories, emotions, and thoughts, and how they make meaning of their coping and learning efforts, situated within a context increasingly shaped by political integration objectives.Methods: We interviewed 22 adult learners from diverse refugee backgrounds (12 men), aged 19–57, attending the Introduction Programme in Norway. We conducted a reflexive thematic analysis of the participants’ narratives.Results: We developed three connected themes offering insight into adult learners’ meaning-making and coping. Theme 1: Preferred learning strategies – tools for managing psychological distress. Theme 2: Pushing aside trauma-related memories, thoughts and feelings to focus on learning. Theme 3: Making temporary breathing spaces to recuperate.Conclusion: Adult refugee learners employ several strategies to cope with their distress while in the Introduction Programme. Teachers’ understanding of their students’ needs and acknowledgement of their strategies could help students to better navigate emotional distress. Insights into trauma-affected adult refugee learners’ experiences and views may guide adult education and other stakeholders in laying the ground for refugees’ coping and learning, ultimately supporting them to establish their lives in the country of resettlement.KEYWORDS: Adult learners from refugee backgrounds, coping, trauma, psychological distress, second language learning, learning strategies, adult education, reflexive thematic analysis

Introduction

Language learning plays a major role in adult refugees’ efforts to establish their lives in the resettlement country (e.g. Ghadi et al., 2019), which also involves engaging and restoring their adaptive coping resources (Herman, 2022, p. 297). However, traumatic experiences can impair concentration, attention, and working memory, all of which are crucial for language learning (van der Kolk, 2014, p. 160). Previous research suggests that successful second language acquisition (SLA) among traumatised refugees depends on opportunities to deal with their traumas while attending language training programmes (Iversen et al., 2014). In this study we explore adult refugees’ own perspectives on coping with psychological distress in order to learn Norwegian and participate in class.

Representations of the voices of trauma-affected adult refugee learners are limited (Tomren & Opaas, 2024). However, the available studies provide valuable information about the resources refugees and other adversity-stricken learners possess and could also draw upon in language learning situations. In a systematic literature review examining the coping strategies among conflict-affected civilians, including refugees, Seguin and Roberts (2017, p. 823) found that the most frequently reported strategies across the articles were support-seeking (e.g. from their social network – including professionals), positive cognitive restructuring (e.g. changing one’s perspectives), problem-solving (e.g. taking concrete actions), escape-avoidance (e.g. mental and physical withdrawal), and distraction (e.g. engage in alternative pleasurable activities). The review also indicated that gender significantly affected the use of coping strategies, as did previous traumatic experiences, albeit to a lesser extent. Other studies of efforts to deal with distress and adapt to the new situation among refugees and forcibly displaced individuals include using humour and storytelling (Tippens, 2017), using hope to cope with prolonged uncertainty during displacement (Brun, 2015), maintaining communication with family, social contact with members of the resettlement country, engaging in internal practices like prayer (Oviedo et al., 2022), and dealing with being in exile by expressing gratitude for surviving and finding meaning (Erdener, 2017). Coping and meaning-making are closely related concepts (Frounfelker et al., 2020; Lazarus & Folkman, 1984; Park & Folkman, 1997; Puvimanasinghe et al., 2014). Research indicates that in times of crisis, in order to adapt to the situation, people tend to actively seek meaning from their adverse experiences (Antonovsky, 2002; Puvimanasinghe et al., 2014; Yang et al., 2021). In a literature review of the reciprocal relationship between coping and learning, problem-focused coping, in particular, seemed to unlock adult students’ capacity to learn by reducing anxiety and enhancing stress management (Lapina, 2018). Research suggests that having effective coping strategies is a positive predictor of adult refugees’ motivation to participate in language training (Iversen et al., 2014).

Coping strategies are diverse, encompassing a wide range of responses, and can differ significantly between individuals, in different situations, and across various life stages (Brennan et al., 2012). The authors highlight that the strategies are not mutually exclusive; rather, they interact. Moreover, unhealthy coping strategies can be modified, and healthy strategies cultivated and improved (Nielsen & Knardahl, 2014). While problem-focused coping strategies, such as planning and problem-solving, are characterised by directly addressing a stressor, emotion-focused coping strategies focus on managing the emotional responses to a stressor rather than confronting it directly (Lazarus & Folkman, 1984, p. 62). These strategies are mutually influential and may both facilitate and impede each other. Lazarus and Folkman (1984, p. 140) highlight that ‘coping should not be equated with mastery over the environment; many sources of stress cannot be mastered, and effective coping under these conditions is that which allows the person to tolerate, minimize, accept, or ignore what cannot be mastered.’

In Benseman’s (2014) interview study with adult refugee learners and teachers, several factors challenged the coping resources of both teachers and students. For the teachers, these ranged from managing large, multilingual, and multi-level classes to assisting students with limited prior schooling in ‘learning to learn’, such as following instructions or using school materials. In the interviews with the students, all with limited schooling, several appeared to be unable to identify whether they had specific challenges to deal with, and some reported they did not experience any difficulties. However, some did mention challenges related to aspects of the language learning process, including spelling and speaking, and other factors such as depression, exhaustion, visual impairments, and coping with an extensive caregiver burden (Benseman, 2014).

Recent research indicates that struggling with language barriers negatively affects newcomers’ integration and well-being (Kikulwe et al., 2021). Lower host-country language skills have been associated with a risk of depression among long-settled war-refugees (Bogic et al., 2012), with higher prevalence and severity of mental illness among migrants (Montemitro et al., 2021), and to exacerbate feelings of shame and anxiety as well as hinder employment opportunities and access to social interactions among Syrian refugees in Canada (Ghadi et al., 2019), all of which may strain an individual’s coping capacity. In an Australian study examining the relationship between trauma exposure, SLA, and mental health among long-term resettled Bosnian refugees (76m, 62f), learning English was shown to reduce symptoms of anxiety and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), but did not have the same effect on depression (Kartal et al., 2019). Another study exploring Syrian refugees (14m, 12f) who had lived in Irland for one to two years, found language acquisition to be related to fewer feelings of isolation and depression (Ćatibušić et al., 2021). In their systematic review of the association between language proficiency and mental health outcomes among migrants, Montemitro et al. (2021) found that early interventions to improve language proficiency may have a protective effect on migrants’ mental health. Finally, research suggests that acquiring the resettlement country’s language fundamentally influences refugees’ employment opportunities (Budría et al., 2019; Ćatibušić et al., 2021) and is ‘a prerequisite for economic, political, social and cultural integration’, crucial for refugees’ well-being (Hou & Beiser, 2006, pp. 137, 155).

Do past trauma and ongoing adversities impact learning negatively? Being able to learn depends largely on intact cognitive functioning. According to Maja et al. (2022), early trauma and extreme life stress can negatively impact cognitive development and functioning (such as attention, concentration and memory). However, in a study among trauma-affected adolescent learners with post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), Mirabolfathi et al. (2022) found that trauma-affected adolescents performed poorly compared to other adolescent learners, but only when traumatic memories were triggered. This suggests that learners who experience many triggers during the school day, such as anxieties around ongoing violence, stressors in the home, or worries about family and friends left behind in a violence-stricken country, may experience frequent challenges in their ability to learn. In a published interview study of adult learners from refugee backgrounds, preceding the current study, adult refugee learners’ responses varied from no effect of trauma-related distress on learning to a strong effect (Tomren & Opaas, 2024). The study also showed that learning an additional language could, in itself, be associated with feelings of stress and anxiety, and a reduced capacity to concentrate, remember, and learn. However, attending school and focusing on learning can also contribute to coping with past adversity. Zbidat et al. (2020) found that Syrian refugees used, among other strategies, second language acquisition as a means to cope with war-related traumatic experiences and associated health issues. Concentration on learning afforded them a much-needed break from mental and physical pain and invasive thoughts and memories.

Aims and research question

Existing research offers limited insights into how adult refugee learners experience and navigate psychological distress in educational settings, and how they make sense of these experiences. Such knowledge is essential for strengthening adult education programmes and (inter-)national efforts aiming to support refugees in establishing their lives in the country of resettlement. The current study investigates adult refugees’ meaning-making and perspectives on learning and coping with trauma and other emotional distress while attending the Norwegian Introduction Programme (NIP). The NIP is a mandatory full-time training programme designed to facilitate early integration, where language learning is an integral part (Integration Act, 2020, § 1). The training programme, as well as the adult education setting in which it is offered, is shaped by political integration objectives. This broader context provides an important backdrop for the study. However, although the Norwegian or the Scandinavian introductory programmes for refugees may be different from those of other countries receiving refugees, we believe refugees in other countries also experience many of the same obstacles to learning.

This study aims to gain insight into an area that has received limited scholarly attention by investigating the following research question: In what ways do trauma-affected adult refugee learners cope with psychological distress and manage their learning while participating in language classes, and how do they make sense of their coping and learning efforts?

Methods

In a qualitative study, we conducted in-depth interviews with 22 adult learners from refugee backgrounds attending the Norwegian Introduction Programme (NIP). The study was approved by the Norwegian Regional Committees for Medical and Health Research Ethics (REK, 2018/1186). Criteria for inclusion encompassed refugees possessing the right and obligation to participate in the NIP (Integration Act, 2020, §§ 8–9), and who were currently enrolled in or had previously participated in the programme. Those taking part in the NIP were entitled to receive financial support (introductory benefit) from the Norwegian government (§ 19), and undocumented absence entailed deductions in the economic support accordingly.

A previous publication (Tomren & Opaas, 2024) presented the participants’ adverse experiences, symptoms of mental distress, and their experiences and views of the impact on learning of past trauma and post-migration difficulties. In the present study, we explore the adult refugee learners’ experiences and perspectives of coping with psychological distress while learning or trying to learn the Norwegian language, and how they constructed meaning from these efforts.

Participants

Twenty-two learners with refugee backgrounds, 10 women and 12 men, aged 19 to 57, agreed to participate. The participants originated from six countries across the Middle East, Central Asia, and Africa, and their educational backgrounds varied from having no formal education to having completed six years of university. At the time of the interview, participants were either currently enrolled or had previously been enrolled in the programme. The sample was non-clinical. Still, as documented in the previous publication (Tomren & Opaas, 2024), almost half of the participants’ self-assessments aligned with the questionnaire-based criteria for a preliminary diagnosis of PTSD on the Posttraumatic Stress Disorder Checklist (PCL-5; Weathers et al., 2013), and on the Stressful Life Events Screening Questionnaire (SLESQ-Revised; Goodman et al., 1998), men reported having experienced an average of 6.5 different types of traumatic events, while women reported having experienced 5.8. Female learners frequently associated their traumatic experiences with intimate relationships, whereas male learners often associated their experiences with war and escape. In the interviews, participants also associated adverse life experiences with education and learning in the past, involving strict, demanding, and insensitive teachers and caregivers.

Procedure

Participants were recruited from adult education centres in Norway. No participant was approached by the researchers directly, and the interviewer (the first author) had no knowledge of the participants prior to the interviews. We contacted school principals via email with a brief explanation of the study and requested assistance in connecting with teachers who could help recruit participants. After a school’s agreement to help recruit participants to the study, invitation letters and consent forms were emailed to the principal who subsequently forwarded them to the designated contact person. The contact persons invited participants to the study, handed them written invitations, and upon an adult learner’s agreement to participate, provided consent forms and questionnaires about stressful life events and post-traumatic stress symptoms to complete before or at the face-to-face interview. All participants provided written informed consent. Participation was voluntary, and anonymity and confidentiality were ensured.

The participants were interviewed face-to-face by the first author in the participants’ respective schools in 2019. To enable participation across different levels of Norwegian language skills, participants were offered a professional telephone interpreter with expertise in similar subject areas. Telephone interpreters were used in six of the interviews. The interviews conducted without an interpreter were marked by a stronger sense of closeness and openness about painful personal experiences, and some participants also displayed more emotions, including tears. Still, participants contributed with rich responses relevant to this study’s aims and research question.

The interviewer used visual aids (photos) and two empty picture frames of different sizes to provide conversational support. The frames represented the ‘emotional window of tolerance’ (Nordanger & Braarud, 2017, pp. 85–87; Siegel, 2012, pp. 281–285, 315). The interviews lasted between one and two hours, were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim shortly afterwards. All participants agreed to a follow-up phone call by the interviewer two days after the interview. The participants were also offered the opportunity to speak with a mental health professional (the second author) following the interviews, but all reported that no additional support was needed (Tomren & Opaas, 2024).

Additionally, during the COVID-19 lockdown in April 2020, we managed to contact eleven of the twenty-two participants. We invited them for a telephone interview to learn how they managed during these unprecedented circumstances. Two declined without any further explanation. The ensuing nine men and women, aged 24 to 49, with six to over sixteen years of education, were interviewed by telephone, also by the first author. None of them wanted an interpreter. The interviews lasted between 14 to 67 min. Eight interviews were recorded and transcribed immediately afterwards. As one participant declined audio recording, notes were taken during the interview and written up immediately afterwards. All participants agreed to a follow-up call two days after these interviews as well. Unless otherwise specified, the data presented are generated from both datasets.

The first author conducted all interviews, the analyses, and the main drafts of the article, while the second author contributed with discussions of analyses and themes, critical readings, and revisions throughout the writing process.

A more detailed description of the procedure, including recruitment, data collection, ethical considerations related to vulnerable participants, and limitations that apply to the entire study, can be found in Tomren and Opaas (2024).

Researcher reflexivity

Throughout this project, the first author maintained a research diary to reflect on – and document – both the analytic process and the broader research process. This encompassed critical engagement with prior knowledge, assumptions, and values which allowed her to examine how her positioning and understanding (e.g. of the NIP context) shaped the analysis and engagement with the data. It also involved ongoing reflections on her evolving perspectives and interpretations of the data, particularly during code and theme development.

The first author has many years of teaching experience in the NIP, which positioned her as both an outsider and a partial insider, allowing her to draw on her years of experience from conversations with immigrants (Tomren & Opaas, 2024). Thus, both interviewees and the interviewer were all well acquainted with the NIP. This familiarity, encompassing both terminology and how such training programmes function, appeared to enhance communication, facilitate trust, and establish common ground more quickly, as well as shape how coping and learning were narrated, what was considered worth telling, and how narratives were made sense of. The second author has worked with traumatised refugees as a researcher and a clinical psychologist for more than 20 years.

The interview situation – a temporary community of shared meaning

We see the interview situation as a temporary community of shared meaning, where the interviewer and the interviewee engage in co-construction of meaning and where the conversation is influenced by and situated within a cultural context (Gergen, 2023). Moreover, both parties in the interview situation adjust or ‘recipient-design’ (Silverman, 2017, p. 149) their communication, such as what they choose to say, the questions they ask, and how they communicate it, based on how they perceive each other.

The analytic process

Informed by the reflexive thematic analysis (reflexive TA) outlined by Braun and Clarke (2006; 2019), we analysed the data, using a primarily inductive approach to generate patterns of meaning across our dataset, which is well suited to generating knowledge applicable in practice (Braun & Clarke, 2021). Within this approach, analysis is conceptualised as ‘a process of meaning-making, at the intersection of the researcher, the dataset, and analytic and data context’ (Braun & Clarke, 2022, p. 45). Furthermore, in reflexive thematic analysis, quality ‘is not about following procedures ‘correctly (or about ‘accurate’ and ‘reliable’ coding, or achieving consensus between coders), but about the researchers’ reflective and thoughtful engagement with their data and their reflexive and thoughtful engagement with the analytic process’ (Braun & Clarke, 2019, p. 594).

Throughout the prolonged engagement with the data and the iterative analytic process, the first author kept detailed notes in the research diary, documenting analytic decisions, including coding, theme development, and reflections on interpretive choices. Experiences of discomfort, frustration and challenges were reflected upon and interrogated as part of the analytic process. The research diary provided a structured, chronological account of reflections and developing ideas as they evolved through the iterative phases of analysis. Regular dialogues between the authors and discussions with colleagues and a mentor provided further opportunities for reflexive engagement with the analysis.

At the core of reflexive TA is the researcher’s subjective, situated engagement with the data and with knowledge production (Braun & Clarke, 2019, 2023). The present authors view our different perspectives as a resource, while also acknowledging that our positionalities, including our (almost) lifelong engagement with people from refugee backgrounds, our middle-class upbringings, education, and (professional) experiences and values, influence and shape the analysis and research process throughout. Thus, the insights generated from the analysis can only ever be partial and situated (Braun & Clarke, 2022, Chapter 8, p. 241; Finlay, 2002; e.g. Haraway, 1991, pp. 3, 187, 192–193).

The analysis was informed by a relational social-constructionist framework, assuming that people co-construct and negotiate meaning through interactions with others within cultural and social contexts at a given time (Gergen, 2023). This understanding partially aligns with a sociocultural perspective, positing that knowledge is being socially constructed through interactions within a specific cultural-historical context (Valsiner, 1984; Vygotsky, 1978, pp. 19, 56–57). Sensitivity to the reciprocal interaction among the individual, culture, and context also resonates with Lazarus and Folkman (1984, p. 179) process-oriented approach to coping, suggesting that an individual’s coping responses vary based on the circumstances. In this study, we draw selectively from these perspectives without strictly adhering to every aspect of the theories.

The choice of using reflexive TA allowed us to (1) deeply engage with the participants’ diverse and complex accounts, attending to both semantic (explicit/surface) and latent (implicit/underlying) meanings across the dataset, (2) move from an initially participant-centred analytic focus to, in later phases of the analytic process, also include (3) a more critical orientation in the sense of recognising the context-dependent nature of meaning-making and coping. The flexibility of reflexive TA also allows for creativity (e.g. Finlay, 2021). As with flexibility, creativity is ‘situated within a framework of rigour’ (Braun & Clarke, 2022; Chapter 1, p. 9). During the analytic process, conceptualising creativity as ‘framed creativity’ (our term) has been beneficial. The ‘framing’ aspect mitigated feelings of overwhelm associated with the rich dataset, which subsequently unleashed and facilitated creativity as well as (re-)directed attention to the analytic focus at a given moment.

The initial analysis included repeated readings of the interview transcripts and listening to the recorded interviews for familiarisation. The transcripts also encompassed non-verbal language, such as laughter, tears, gestures, tone of voice, brief pauses, and the interviewer’s notes from each of the 22 interviews. This provided contextual cues and meanings to the data that supported and enriched the interpretation of the participants’ accounts during analysis. Reflections, comments, questions, and ideas from the familiarisation phase were compiled in a digital feature called ‘Comments to code’ (F4analyse software). Coping-related features across the dataset, relevant to the research question, were coded systematically and organised into clusters, first manually, then digitally for a better overview. The codes were printed, cut into strips, and organised into clusters with sticky tack, or ‘teacher’s gum’, allowing easy rearrangement throughout the analytic process. We reworked the code clusters several times, while generating the initial themes.

Likewise, the themes were mapped by hand and then digitally visualised; the latter allowed creative freedom as the boxes could be moved around swiftly. In generating initial themes, questions such as ‘Does this provisional theme capture something meaningful?’, and ‘Is it coherent, with a central idea?’ (Braun & Clarke, 2022; Chapter 4, p. 85) were helpful in assessing whether a pattern constituted a candidate theme that addressed the research question or was ‘just’ interesting. Terry et al. (2017, p. 14) suggest that, along with e.g., thematic maps, questions can help researchers in defining and naming the themes.

Throughout the iterative phases of theme development, through ongoing revisions and refinement, including the writing up, we also revisited modified versions of the questions above. Rather than generating answers, the questions encouraged reflections (e.g., ‘Why do I think this theme capture something meaningful?’), curiosity and openness for further engagement with the data. This process was often characterised by ‘slow wondering’ – slow, reflective thinking that also included necessary pauses to examine a patterned meaning, assessing whether it required further refinement, should be kept, or discarded. Braun and Clarke (2022, Chapter 4, p. 90) warn against a ‘question and answer orientation’ in data engagement as it may lead to superficial interpretations and limit the researcher’s abilities to identify and explore less evident yet valuable insights. However, as addressed above, questions can serve various purposes when used with consideration. For example, throughout our analysis, the question ‘What happens here?’ allowed us to move beyond what was explicitly expressed, facilitating interpretations beyond the surface meanings. Through the analytic process, we generated three themes that collectively provide insight to this study’s aims, unified by an overarching theme, Perseverance – to stay the course.

In the following, to ensure anonymity and recognise the individual participant’s contributions, fictitious names and approximate age, such as ‘30s’ or ‘40s’, are added to the quotations. However, names are not used for all extracts, as gathering all extracts from the same individual could pose a risk of identification. Quotes are, according to convention, in italics or marked by indentation. Some of the quotes have been slightly edited for readability. Brief pauses in speech are indicated by parentheses, indicated by (…). Text in [square brackets] has been added for clarification or explanation, as well as to indicate the interviewer’s questions. The page numbers in references to Braun and Clarke (2022) correspond to the Kindle edition and may differ from the print editions.

Results

We generated three themes that collectively provide insight to this study’s aims, unified by the overarching theme, Perseverance – to stay the course. The final developed themes were as follows: Theme 1: Preferred learning strategies – tools for managing psychological distress. Theme 2: Pushing aside trauma-related memories, thoughts and feelings to focus on learning. Theme 3: Making temporary breathing spaces to recuperate. See Figure 1. While each theme provides distinct insights and understandings about the participants’ perspectives, together they convey the kinds of actions they considered useful to successfully complete the NIP, and how they made meaning of their coping efforts as necessary steps towards establishing their lives in Norway.

The three final themes related to refugees’ meaning-making and coping with psychological distress while in language learning contexts, connected by the overarching theme, Perseverance – to stay the course.

As contextual background for the themes developed, we will briefly recount some of what the participants had to contend with. The twenty-two adult learners were required to handle multiple challenges, including (but not limited to) a different educational system, distressing memories, emotions and thoughts associated with previous traumatic experiences, feelings of loneliness, and being gossiped about by their peers. Additionally, some learners faced ongoing challenges in close relationships while simultaneously striving to acquire the unfamiliar language (Tomren & Opaas, 2024).

Perseverance – to stay the course

Perseverance – to stay the course serves as an overarching theme (Braun & Clarke, 2022, Chapter 4, p. 86) that connects the three themes, situating them within a broader sociocultural context and a politicised field of practice. It encompasses adult learners’ ongoing efforts to adapt to and handle psychological distress as they navigate between personal resources and the external demands. Language acquisition and economic self-sufficiency within a limited time frame, which are considered crucial for establishing their lives in Norway, were the principal external demands they had to contend with. We found the notion of ‘perseverance’ to be particularly useful as it appeared essential for ‘staying the course’, not only in the literal sense of completing the training course but also in the metaphorical sense of persisting towards one’s goals despite challenges.

Theme 1: preferred learning strategies – tools for managing psychological distress

This theme explores how participants’ preferred learning strategies, such as reading, writing and combining words with visual aids, not only supported their language learning, but also helped them navigate emotional distress. Across differences in formal educational background, participants frequently constructed – both verbally and non-verbally (e.g. through tone of voice and laughter) – learning and learning strategies as meaningful activities that evoked positive emotions including joy and enthusiasm. Commonly, participants’ accounts demonstrated an awareness of what learning strategies worked best for them, how these strategies worked, and sometimes why they preferred one strategy over the other. For example, Salma (30s), who during the interview systematically assessed each of her many learning strategies, stated:

[happy tone] But I’m also having my own method! If I don’t understand a certain word, it’s a little easier to use pictures. I look for pictures that I use and they help me remember. (…) – Such as the picture of the animal I showed you [a scorpion]. [happy voice]: It helps me!

In this extract, Salma constructed learning as challenging but manageable, framing herself as a capable adult learner drawing on personalised strategies to facilitate her own learning. While Salma’s positive account of learning strategies above did not directly express such strategies as a deliberate choice to manage emotional distress, our interpretation of her account was that it indicated enhanced well-being, conducive to learning. Anwar (30s), who, similarly to Salma, described a range of strategically used learning strategies, expressed joy and gratitude for managing his own learning while recognising that learning is an individual process: ‘I think every person has their way of studying. What suits me, may not suit someone else. [happy voice]: So, I’ve found a way that works for me!’ Both Salma and Anwar positioned adult learners as knowledgeable actors drawing on different strategies to facilitate their own learning. Scholars, such as Oxford (2014, p. 492), suggest that knowledge about, access to, and use of effective language learning strategies are considered essential to facilitate learning and well-being.

While the above excerpts illustrate an implicit sense of increased well-being, Dima (40s) explicitly connected writing – one of her favoured learning strategies – to a positive shift in emotional state and perhaps also in cognitive functioning. She stated: ‘Writing words [in Norwegian], for example, when I’m writing a lot – my head gets positive! (…) For example, I write a word ten times, then next ten times.’ Several participants described similar experiences while using their preferred learning strategies. For example, Majed (30s) foregrounded his feelings of pleasure and achievement associated with reading and understanding: ‘I use many different things to learn. (…) For example, I use Klar Tale [a Norwegian easy-to-read newspaper aimed at language learners]. I get so excited when I read and understand the news! This is really good for me.’ Farah (40s), one of several participants who reported repeated, disturbing, and unwanted memories in response to stressful experiences (Tomren & Opaas, 2024), added nuance to Majed’s perspective by framing her love for reading both as a way to learn – a learning strategy she had been using since childhood – and as recreation from difficult memories. She noted:

I like to read [small laugh]. When I read, I forget about some things. I just read. Sometimes I try to knit, but it doesn’t help. And I cry a lot and think a lot (…) I knit and think a lot – and cry and cry [small laugh].

Taken together, these excerpts suggest a shift in emotional regulation when using their preferred learning strategy, which may enhance the individual’s capacity to navigate affective and cognitive distress and focus on the activity.

Some narratives suggested an overlap between, for example, reading or writing for pleasure and reading and writing as learning strategies. For others, reading and writing instead reminded them of something painful. Thus, access to valued learning strategies varied among the participants, affecting outcomes both academically and emotionally. For example, Hakeem (30s) described writing as a previously cherished strategy for managing learning. He recalled the positive feelings writing previously evoked: ‘[Before fleeing] I wrote down everything I felt. (…) I relaxed. Because I could talk to my “friend” – the paper. I felt so good and relaxed when I was writing.’ Before being forced to flee, Hakeem – like some of the other participants – had been politically active, and for years, he utilised what he described as a code language to record events and opinions – material he perceived as life-threatening if discovered by the authorities. Hakeem later realised that everything he had written was lost – as he had been forced to entrust it to another person while fleeing. He concluded: ‘They lost everything I had written – three years of work. So, I will not write again.’ [Interviewer: ‘Do you write today?’] ‘No. I will not, I will not write again! (…) That’s how it is.’ Hakeem’s metaphor of his ‘friend, the paper’ can be understood as having relational and therapeutic qualities. The subsequent loss of his ‘friend’ was framed as a turning point in life and in learning. The above narrative highlights the devastating outcome of losing access to strategies constructed as essential for learning and for managing emotional distress as well as for providing purpose and direction during a period of considerable strain.

On a few occasions, the (emotional) outcome of engaging in preferred learning strategies was constructed as context dependent, largely depending on perceived safety. For example, Habibi (30s), who initially described how emotions of fear and shame limited her ability to read aloud in class, later constructed reading (aloud) as eliciting positive emotions in one situation while inducing intense negative emotions in another: ‘I look down at the book, and everything is black. Everything is black (…) [happy tone]: But when I read to my friend in Norwegian – I read to her when we’re at work – then I’m not afraid!’ The excerpt illustrates the complexity of adult learners’ experiences and points to an ongoing negotiation of how a learning strategy that facilitates feelings of coping in one context can be experienced as overwhelming in another.

While some accounts associated access to learning strategies with previous trauma – as illustrated in Hakeem’s narrative – others contextualised access to preferred learning strategies with structural factors, such as changes in teachers and classes. For example, one of the many learning strategies that William (30s) used involved working systematically with verbs on a computer-based verb programme. When we asked about his preference for working with verbs, he explained:

I think I understand the [verb] system. I’m not sure, but I almost understand what’s coming. For example: ‘explain – explained – explained’ [happy voice]: I really like verbs the best! I prefer learning on the computer. I don’t know why, but I don’t get tired. When I work on paper, I get tired really fast. Then my ‘window’ is just narrow, perhaps. [Points to the middle of the available large picture frame illustrating ‘the window of tolerance’ and raises his voice]: When using the computer, I’m here! [small laugh].

William’s narrative suggests that focusing on a specific language area, like conjugating verbs, provides structure, predictability, and calmness, indicating a positive change in emotional state, something he refers to as being inside the ‘window.’ Regaining calm is crucial for adult learners with traumatic experiences, as distressing emotions can adversely affect attention, concentration, and the acquisition of new knowledge (van der Kolk, 2014, p. 70), and also deprive individuals of personal agency and control (van der Kolk, 2014, p. 205), all considered vital for successful learning.

As illustrated in the excerpt above, William constructed the outcome of using preferred learning strategies as dependent on the (writing) utensil. When asked whether his teachers were aware of his narrow emotional ‘window’ when writing by hand, William confirmed this awareness as shared knowledge: ‘Yes, I’ve told them.’

Interviewer: Do the teachers say that you are welcome to use the computer, then?

William: No.. Well, in primary education for adults, [teachers say] you must write on the computer, but when you’re going to learn [they say] you must write on paper.

Taken together, the above extracts underscore the importance of teachers’ knowledge of their students’ preferred learning strategies and, as articulated by William, that some strategies are experienced as ineffective and even narrow their ‘window’.

Some narratives framed learning and use of learning strategies in terms of uncertainty, suggesting teacher guidance as essential for students to benefit from learning strategies, academically and emotionally. For example, one woman (30s) who was currently enrolled in literacy training – noted: ‘The teacher talks a lot and sometimes he’s using pictures. We’re talking quite a bit too.’ Next, when she was asked what worked best for her, she responded: ‘What teachers do is teach, and then we learn (…) I don’t know what other methods – what other ways to go.’ Another participant, Darius (20s), who had previously completed secondary education, simply stated: ‘[raising his voice] I want to learn Norwegian, but I don’t know where to begin!’ The uncertainty expressed in both excerpts suggests that some students may benefit from guidance on different kinds of learning strategies, as well as which ones work best for them.

Some participants reported various forms of repetitive learning, such as memorisation and repeated writing, as helpful for managing learning and navigating emotional distress, such as Dima, cited above, who explicitly connected writing ‘a word ten times, then next ten times’ to a positive shift in emotional and cognitive state. Another woman in her 30s expressed a similar emotional shift, though more implicitly through an enthusiastic and positive tone of voice. When asked whether she thought about her painful experiences when in class, she answered:

No. Well, today – perhaps just like small glimpses [small laugh], and then quickly, quickly it disappears. [Interviewer: ‘What do you do, to make it disappear?’] [The participant takes a breath, speaks in a firm tone]: I write texts! [happy voice]: I read Norwegian! I go to my room, and I write assignments or texts, and I focus one hundred percent. It really helps me. [Shortly after, she added]: I enjoy writing words on my own and then [trying to] remember them correctly – like, twice, three times. Actually, I fill the whole page, I write and write. And I’m like: ‘That’s a correct word, good!’ I like writing! Repeat, write a lot! [Eager tone]: And sometimes, if I like a sentence, I rewrite it many times!

Here, writing, particularly repeated writing, is framed as a learning strategy that may make thoughts about her painful experiences disappear. Writing seems for her to have therapeutic qualities, eliciting positive emotions that may enhance both emotional and academic well-being. However, another participant (in their 20s), when talking about learning strategies, emphasised: ‘I don’t like to repeat! No-no-no! I’m really bad at repeating! [small laugh]. And, especially in science, I need to repeat things.’

Several participants stated that they had been socialised within teacher-centred educational traditions, which involved the use of corporal punishment to discipline students (Tomren & Opaas, 2024), and where, for instance, rote learning was an educational practice. Compared to these practices, the Norwegian educational context was described by the participants as highly different. While they appreciated the absence of harsh disciplining in the Norwegian school system, some participants articulated the expectations to engage in critical thinking as particularly challenging, which is considered an important learning strategy in the present context.

Theme 2: pushing aside trauma-related memories, thoughts and feelings to focus on learning

The second theme relates to participants’ prioritisation of focusing on learning and pushing aside unwanted and painful memories, thoughts and emotions about their past and present trauma and adversities. These priorities were frequently constructed as necessary to navigate the training programme, commonly expressed as something they ‘tried to’ or ‘had to.’ For example, one young woman (20s), who cried while describing an abusive relationship in which she was repeatedly ridiculed, ignored and put down by her partner at home and at school (they were placed in the same class), noted: ‘Although I have big problems now, I have to focus on the language, and I have to learn.’ Such accounts may indicate an adherence to societal expectations and political visions regarding SLA, as well as reflect their own academic aspirations and identity as dedicated students. Some participants framed their efforts to meet expectations and requirements in more implicit ways. One woman (40s) stated: ‘I hope the refugee service knows me, why I learn Norwegian that slow. Because learning goes really slow for me.’ Another woman, Mary (20s), who had previously witnessed fellow refugees being abandoned and left to die in the desert, stated: ‘I have to forget everything! Because now, I go to school. I don’t want to think about it! Just think: What to do right now. I almost manage to forget it.’ The excerpts suggest a tension between wanting to attend to their academic responsibilities and experiences of being derailed by invasive memories and emotions. Like Mary, many participants framed adopting a present- and future-oriented perspective, and pushing away memories of traumatic experiences as beneficial. Still, they frequently reported how disturbing and vivid memories of traumatic experiences brought up a range of emotions, including fear, sorrow, and sadness while at school. For example, one male participant (30s) stated: ‘Sadness is the feeling that influences my learning the most.’ A highly educated woman (30s) who had survived severe intimate partner violence initially described school days marked by repetitive negative thinking, exhaustion, pain, and fear that her ex-husband might appear at school: ‘All the time, I’m thinking negative, negative, negative. And [deep breath], at night it’s very difficult because I can’t sleep. And at school, I’m very tired (…) and very scared [small laugh].’ Yet, throughout the interview, she emphasised the personal value of language learning and attending school despite these challenges. When we later asked her about the reason for her Norwegian proficiency, she simply responded: ‘I have to learn, I have to write, and I have to read to become better in Norwegian.’ Across accounts, participants expressed a strong wish to forget their traumatic experiences in order to concentrate and remember what they learned in the training programme, while also recognising that these disruptive memories remained inescapable. For example, despite strong efforts to maintain his focus on learning, Jamal (30s) reported significantly slower learning subsequent to experiences he described as traumatic. He framed forgetting the traumatic events as necessary, yet impossible to accomplish.

You have to forget, but it’s impossible. Some things you just can’t forget because they’re with you. You can’t say: ‘I must forget it’. It just comes. [Raising his voice]: I want to forget everything, I don’t want to remember, but it just comes!

Speaking with emphasis, Jamal depicted his traumatic experiences as an inescapable part of his life. This highlights the challenges of prioritising learning a new language and familiarise oneself with a new culture when other concerns appear equally pressing. This was echoed by Rami (30s) who recounted how intrusive and unwanted memories of a traumatic experience that had significantly affected his mother’s health – something he felt responsible for – were accompanied by experiences of mentally ‘zooming out’ during school sessions. He stated:

These things, of course, are quite painful for a person when you’re going to learn. It’s my family – it’s everything I sssgot. So, of course, sometimes you zoom out during lessons, but then you forget about it for a while, and you regain your focus and continue. But you’re feeling a bit unstable.

As illustrated in the above excerpts, participants, despite efforts and determination to concentrate on learning, were preoccupied with other concerns, which at times limited their ability to be mentally present during school lessons. While Rami described occasionally ‘zooming out’ during class, some shared how unwanted and intrusive memories and concerns persistently disturbed their concentration and learning. For example, Darius (20s), who expressed ongoing concerns about his concentration and memory, appeared discouraged when he stated: ‘It’s really hard to follow the teaching because I’m occupied with other things.’ Later, he reflected: ‘[speaking faster] But I’m human [small laugh], I’m not a machine (…) If only there were something that could help me remember [what he had learned]. Maybe there is a medication that could help?’ Like Darius, Shukri (20s) disclosed experiences of inner turmoil and disturbing memories and dreams about his life-threatening escape. Shukri however, argued that when at school, he was determined – and successful – in not letting these experiences interfere with his learning. He stated:

If you have problems, you can isolate your problems at home. At school, you should just learn. [Interviewer: Do you think that’s possible?] Yes, it’s possible, because I do that. (…) Even if I’m young and I’m having a very difficult life, I try to leave my problems at home. When I’m at school, I’m another person.

Shukri constructed school as primarily a space for academic learning, positioning himself as a strong learner who – despite his young age – possessed the capacity to push aside his traumatic experiences and concentrate on learning. Still, he admitted that he could get easily upset, perhaps indicating that he still brought with him some inner turmoil to school.

The participants commonly positioned themselves as hard-working and goal-oriented students who, despite traumatic experiences, post-migration hardships and concentration issues (Tomren & Opaas, 2024), were determined to maintain their learning progress. Nadia (20s), for example, framed her concentration problems as challenging, yet manageable. She noted: ‘On hard days it’s difficult concentrating on reading and things like that. It’s often like that. But afterwards, I still read anyway [small laugh].’ Karim (30s) described that he, with time, had learned to better deal with psychological distress, though he experienced that his ability to cope fluctuated. He simply stated: ‘Today, I’ll manage, but not all days!’ Not all attempts to set aside painful emotions appeared to achieve their intended outcome. One male (30s) explained:

In a way, I drink a lot. Maybe a lot of alcohol, – not that I get drunk, but I like to drink to perhaps forget about [the sudden loss of] dad or about this pain-pain [loneliness and torture]. [Interviewer: Does it help?] No. It doesn’t [small laugh].

To avoid focus on their difficulties, participants frequently emphasised their efforts to appear confident and capable in class. Some participants described how they used laughter and smiles while at school as a means to mask emotional distress, and to draw fellow students’ attention away from verbal cues and bodily expressions that could reveal the psychological distress they experienced. Unwanted attention from fellow students could either appear as personal and intrusive comments and questions, as gossip, or as overly sympathetic remarks. Habibi, for example, stated:

At school, I’m very happy; I put on make-up, and I’m happy and stuff. I don’t want the others to know that I have problems. Everyone thinks I don’t have any problems and that I’m very happy, that everything is normal. That’s enough for me. If they understand that I have trauma, then they’ll say: ‘Oh, poor, poor you’ [clicking her tongue]. There’s so much pain in my heart. ‘Oh, poor you’ doesn’t help.

Appearing cheerful on the outside to create an impression of normality is depicted by participants as a socially acceptable strategy essential for navigating social situations such as school. One man, (20s), who had experienced severe and multiple (childhood) traumas, expressed a similar viewpoint. He argued:

A small problem – and people will start crying. And I [deep breath], when I talk about my problems, I’m about to cry, but I try to stop myself. When I smile, it doesn’t mean that I’m happy [pointing at his heart]. It means that I have something painful in my heart and that that smile is just a fake. It means nothing, I’ll just smile because I don’t want others to understand the kind of pain I have. I don’t want others to know. If they had the same life as me, they would’ve killed themselves.

In this excerpt, the young participant positioned himself as vulnerable yet strong while comparing his situation with others more fortunate than himself. Masking painful emotions with a smile seemed to function as a protective strategy to avoid appearing vulnerable or different from others. He stated: ‘No matter how I feel, I try to learn!’ Another participant, Haris (30s), responded promptly when asked whether he showed his feelings in class:

[Small laugh]: It’s very difficult to show feelings, and that makes me put on another type of face in class. In class, I’m actually another person. (…) When I’m in class, I can’t show others my true emotions, that’s why I put on a mask. People who don’t know me will think that I’m this person. I’m wearing a mask to hide what I actually feel.

The excerpt describes considerable efforts to conceal his emotional pain from peers – thereby keeping them unaware of his distress – positing concealment as a coping strategy for managing difficult emotions. Adopting a brave face and attempt to be perceived as ‘another person’ – seemed to help him remain composed and maintain a sense of control and privacy. This was echoed by Anwar who emphasised: ‘No one.. yes, no one knows what happened to me [small laugh], I don’t tell anyone [about the torture he endured in prison].’ As several other participants, Anwar described a kind of ‘self-silence’ as a way to protect psychological well-being. Taken together, these accounts highlight the participants’ considerable efforts to push aside psychological distress and present a smiling and confident façade, aimed to protect themselves from unwanted attention from others, promote social acceptance, and allow for feelings of normalcy. Also, at its best, keeping a smiling face promoted their self-confidence, prevented them from delving into distressing feelings, and helped them keep their focus on learning.

Often, the participants’ descriptions of refraining from discussing their distress were framed as a desire to protect fellow students’ well-being. For example, one participant (50s) who had been working within healthcare for years before resettlement, portrayed his traumatic experiences as contagious. He stated: ‘Anyway, what happened to me is private. I don’t want to infect people with my pain. I just want to make others happy and spread good vibes to others!’ Similarly, the wish to protect others alongside oneself was evocatively expressed by Karim (30s), who compared sharing emotional pain with people, including peers, to car scrapping:

If I give you something negative, you will feel like [making a collapsing gesture while mimicking] ‘Oh … ’. – It’s almost like that place where they crush cars. [Pretending to draw a smiley face in the air, speaking slowly] I paint smiles for others. But my heart is crying.

These excerpts depict how participants engaged also in prosocial behaviour to support fellow learners, rather than engaging with their own difficulties, suggesting a positive contribution to the learning environment.

One strategy adopted by some, was to downplay their concerns and uncertainties regarding their learning progression while simultaneously emphasising their persistent efforts to learn. For example, Farah stated: ‘I’m a little worried if I understand or not. If I learn or not. And I read and read – many times.’ Another participant (20s), who repeatedly articulated his concern regarding learning, noted: ‘It’s not that I’m not taking in the information, I do. I understand, but what happens is that I forget so fast! It happens all the time.’ Others minimised their own suffering and the significance of their adverse life experiences by contrasting it with the more severe experiences of others. Some participants normalised violence and loss by contextualising their experiences within a broader framework of war and other refugees’ suffering. For instance, one of the women (40s) stated: ‘What’s difficult is the loss of my husband [her husband was killed in war]. But in my country, there’s generally a lot of war.’ Similarly, another participant (20s), who had been sexually abused for years, concluded: ‘For others, it’s difficult. But for me, it isn’t because I was born into it.’ Their accounts suggest normalisation and acceptance as a way to cope and move forward. Employing minimising as a strategy to avoid pain also extended to more immediate distress, such as conflicts at school. Some participants portrayed fellow student’s negative actions towards them as insignificant in order to prevent potential conflicts. Salim (30s), for example, recalled an instance where a fellow student ruined a school project he had completed – an act he deemed politically motivated. He explained: ‘I said to the headmaster: Don’t think about it. No problem. Conflicts are normal. I have a lot of knowledge [laughing]. I’m a well-educated man.’ Salim’s account positioned conflicts as common experiences. His reference to higher education may reflect an attempt to protect self-esteem and to justify his way of handling the situation. Nevertheless, previous research suggests that education can better equip people for managing conflicts in a peaceful way (Afshaneh, 2023).

Although rarely explicitly stated by the participants, minimising adverse life experiences as well as hurtful experiences at school appeared to function as an adaptive strategy for regulating emotional responses and prevent unwanted conflicts, enabling them to remain composed and focused on learning. However, ‘minimising’ as a strategy may inadvertently lead to unresolved emotions and a ‘narrow window of tolerance’, leaving them vulnerable in the face of new challenges.

Participants moreover identified positive self-talk and positive thinking as ways to reduce emotional distress, regain their composure, and draw attention to learning. For example, Alice (30s), who frequently engaged in self-talk, noted: ‘Catastrophic thoughts always come with stress. To calm down, I talk to myself: I’m in Norway. Focus! Focus! You’re not back home! Focus! It’s safe here. [Waves her hand as if brushing something away from her head]: Don’t think!’ This excerpt illustrates how self-instruction can be used to direct attention away from distressing thoughts and emotions, and towards the present. On some occasions, participants explicitly described engaging in comforting and encouraging self-talk to redirect painful thoughts and emotions into learning. For example, Yasmine (40s) noted: ‘[deep breath] I think a lot. And I talk to myself, like, ‘Yasmine, you can’t think that much. You better.. you have to learn Norwegian.’ When we asked whether self-talk worked, she replied: ‘No! [small laugh]. Right now, a negative thought appeared. So, it doesn’t help much.’ Later, she questioned whether self-talk made sense: ‘I’m talking to myself (…) Perhaps it’s stupid [small laugh].’ In this account, positive self-talk did not appear effective in shifting attention away from negative thoughts, which may suggest she needed additional ways to deal with psychological distress.

Some made sense of their experiences by reframing them as opportunities for personal growth, engaging in positive thinking and focusing on moving forward. The subsequent extract from a highly educated participant (40s) captures these aspects, even if he had been imprisoned and experienced tortured:

I’m just an average adult who perhaps sometimes break down and then get back up on my feet. And I learn from my experiences. If it wasn’t like that, I’ll go into depression. I will not - will not – think about depression or things like that, or my life will be over. I just have to move on!

The excerpt constructs getting back on one’s feet and learning from one’s difficulties as paramount to maintaining psychological health and well-being. Moreover, determination not to think of depression and adverse life experiences was framed as necessary to move on. The account emphasises that creating some meaning out of adverse life experiences, may hold significance for well-being and health. Psychological well-being has been found to be a facilitator in learning (Aydın & Tekin, 2023; Oxford, 2014).

Some participants mentioned being encouraged by others to ignore distressing feelings and persistent, negative thoughts, and concentrate on learning instead. This was commonly constructed as expressions of encouragement and care. For instance, Nadia who reported experiencing distressing thoughts and emotions in the initial period after resettlement, stated: ‘My husband helped me a lot. He said: ‘You have to read! You must learn! Don’t think!’ Later, she noted: ‘My teacher said: ‘You learn Norwegian! Don’t think about it!’ These encouragements seemed to provide motivation and support to persist in the participant’s efforts to learn. However, such encouragements may suggest an underlying belief that it is possible to ‘not think’ or to ‘forget’ about trauma, and that to successfully manage learning, adult learners should set aside painful emotions and thoughts altogether. Intentional forgetting and pushing aside traumatic experiences are understood differently across cultures; while some cultures encourage verbalising and sharing, others value silence and intentional forgetting (Wang, 2008).

Our analysis suggests that avoiding thinking about personal trauma while at school was considered necessary to maintain focus on learning. To the extent that it is possible, temporarily setting aside distress can serve as a constructive, adaptive and useful coping strategy (Sleijpen et al., 2016).

Taken together, the participants’ deliberate attempts to forget, to minimise the severity of their experiences, to hide their feelings, and to put on a smiling face helped them sustain a learning-oriented focus. Moreover, Tomren and Opaas (2024) noted that triggers during the school day, including distressing news from close relatives or the media, can make it difficult to push these memories aside. They also pointed out that a few participants reported being unable to set aside their traumatic experiences during lessons and engage in learning until they received help by therapy.

Theme 3: making temporary breathing spaces to recuperate

This theme relates to participants’ descriptions of self-care revolving around activities framed as personalised breathing spaces that allowed them to vent their psychological distress or just get a mental rest. These activities appeared to indirectly facilitate learning by providing mental breaks that supported participants in being present and able to concentrate when in class. The theme has a temporal quality as the activities were time limited, sometimes only taking a few minutes, sometimes more.

Scheduled breaks. Some of the participants’ schools offered brief, frequent breaks, while others provided two extended breaks throughout the school day. The breaks seemed highly valued by the participants. Some argued that breaks characterised by a relaxed, humorous and not too serious tone was better for academic comprehension and learning in class. For example, one participant stated (30s): ‘You need to joke around a bit during breaks. Not always being serious and talking about assignments and stuff. If you are always like that, you don’t understand. If you sometimes joke around, it’s better to learn.’ The excerpt highlights how such breaks allow for a mental rest that can help students return to class with refreshed focus and energy.

Self-initiated, (il)legitimate breaks. Some participants reported taking brief pauses to recuperate during class – some of which may be considered acceptable – or legitimate – by the school, while others may not. Legitimate acts of self-care to deal with emotional distress, was commonly described as leaving class for some minutes under the pretext of using the restroom. For example, one of the participants (30s) who had endured severe domestic violence, which escalated following arrival, explained: ‘Sometimes, I go to the toilet and cry a little, and then I return to class.’ Asking for permission to go to the toilet complies with established classroom rules and behavioural expectations, thereby avoiding unwanted attention and inquiries concerning the reason for leaving the classroom. However, it was not always the case that they visited the restroom. One woman (30s), who shortly after attending the NIP received what she described as ‘really bad news’ from a home country affected by war, recalled: ‘I heard the teacher talking but I was really, really spaced out!' In this period, she frequently left class for a few minutes to alleviate emotional tension. She noted: ‘And many times I walked up the stairwell at school (…) and I cried a lot.' After a violent, armed attack in his country of origin, another student (30s) described how he dealt with feelings of anger by using cold water to calm down and shift his perspective. He explained:

If I wash my face with cold water, it disappears! And then I’m back, I’m normal. If I get angry, I have to wash my face first, and then I can talk. (…) You can imagine that the water is very cold and then think a little about the water. [Interviewer: Does it shift your focus?] Yes. Water does. It really helps.

Other participants described occasionally leaving the classroom without asking for permission. One man (30s) described sometimes leaving the classroom in response to fellow students’ political utterances in Arabic – never in Norwegian – which he framed as attempts to provoke emotional reactions. He argued that such discussions occurred ‘all the time.’ He compared their impact on his well-being to being involved in a bicycle accident. He stated: ‘For example, when we’re discussing – in class or outside of class – it’s like ‘riding a bike and someone puts a stick in your wheel. That’s how I experience such discussions.’ On such days, he went straight to a friend at a residential care home nearby for a while. He went on to say: ‘Because there I feel treated like a king. (…) I can’t talk with my peers or teachers like that. I talk with old Norwegians, and I feel better. Calmer.’ These brief pauses appeared to help him gather himself and eventually return to class. These accounts depicted that to deal with psychological distress, and thus take care of themselves, the participants attempted to create personal breathing spaces within acceptable classroom norms. Although not explicitly stated, leaving early and going home did not appear to be an option due to a potential reduction in introduction course salary. Nabila (30s), for example, noted: ‘It was a very difficult time at school [when learning about her family being bombed]. But I had to come to school because it was the Introduction Programme.’

For some learners, brief breaks of self-care gradually turned into prolonged periods of non-participation. One of these students (20s) left the classroom after being rejected and belittled by someone he referred to as ‘what I thought was a close friend.’ He recalled that after disclosing experiences of sexual abuse to the friend in private, the friend later made repeated, hurtful remarks in front of fellow students: ‘And he spoked a bit harshly to me. (…) You are nothing! You can’t be [friends] with me!’ At the time of the interview, he reported that he rarely attended school.

Several participants expressed concerns regarding the teachers’ perceptions of them (Tomren & Opaas, 2024). From an educational perspective, it is a concern that even during short, self-initiated breaks participants may miss valuable instruction, which might negatively affect academic outcomes and their ability to succeed in the training programme. This applied to accepted as well as less accepted ways to take a break. Only in rare cases, it appeared that teachers possessed insight into the reasons behind their students’ self-initiated breaks. The participants’ accounts further suggest that the need for brief, personal breathing spaces was rarely articulated in the training programmes.

Daily school routines as a potential breathing space. One type of breathing spaces pertained to the school’s role. Several of the participants’ accounts positioned school as not only a place for learning, but as a safe space offering a temporary break from psychological distress and personal concerns. Shukri (20s), for example, who initially described strong feelings of distress when at home, reflected:

I don’t really know how to explain it. I feel it through my whole body, like the blood is pumping from my heart and running faster [Interviewer: Do you experience it when you’re at school?] No! (…) When we have class, I concentrate, and then I become calmed [happy voice].

Many participants portrayed school as a space in which they engaged in meaningful activities. One woman noted: ‘When I’m at school, I forget a little about that other thing [experiences of domestic violence], and I learn Norwegian. It’s important to me.’ Some depicted school as offering experiences of connectedness and belonging. For example, Anwar concluded: ‘I think about the traumas only when I’m alone. You know, when you’re home alone, you start thinking about bad things. But, here at school, together with other people, I don’t think about trauma. Those memories don’t come then.’ These excerpts emphasise that for several participants the school itself served as a provider of a breathing space and break from distress affecting them at home. When asked whether he was thinking about traumatic experiences and concerns when at school, Shukri (20s) stated: ‘I just think about positive things, because when you worry, you learn nothing.’ This does not imply, however, that the participants did not think about their adverse experiences at all during the school day. Mary, for example, explained: ‘I don’t want to think about it when I’m at school. (…) But sometimes I do. Sometimes. I think about it after two o’clock [after school].’ Similarly, Jamal remarked: ‘I want to concentrate at school, but sometimes it is really difficult, but sometimes you can defeat it. And I can do that! When I’m at school, I can manage to forget about it [traumatic experiences].’

Some participants expressed their appreciation for small breaks during lessons – for example, when the teacher incorporated singing, songs and music into language teaching. Research indicates that singing can facilitate adult learners’ language learning, particularly during the early phases of language acquisition (Alisaari & Heikkola, 2016; Ludke et al., 2014). The participants in our study typically constructed singing as positive because it provided a cognitive break. For example, one participant (20s), who told that teachers sometimes used singing as a part of their lessons, concluded: ‘It helps a little to take a break, not be reading and writing all the time and such.’ Another participant, Alice, framed singing as a wake-up activity: ‘This spring, two of the teachers have been using singing during class to wake us up a little.’ This account suggests that teachers offer brief distractions such as singing to help students regain their focus. Habibi, when asked whether the teachers used song and music to support language learning, argued that singing should be contingent upon the stage of the learning progress: ‘Well [started laughing], we used to sing during the first year of language training. – We’re adults attending adult basic education. But in basic language learning singing is fine.’ Others, such as Mary, expressed their doubt whether singing facilitated language learning but positioned song and music as respite moments facilitating calmness and relaxation. She explained: ‘Every Tuesday one of the teachers sings and plays the piano.’ When asked whether she believed that singing in Norwegian helped her language learning, Mary stated: ‘No! [laughing]’ (…). Well, I don’t know. I listen. And sometimes, at home, when I want to relax, I listen to Norwegian songs. (…) But I don’t want to listen to music from my home country!’ This account suggests that certain types of music functioned as a trigger of distress while other types functioned as a refreshing and relaxing break.

Others emphasised attending school as providing structure and predictability during the early years of resettlement. For example, in the telephone interview with Salim during COVID-19, he stated: ‘I miss school! What I miss most is learning face-to-face. No more emails or digital teaching or anything like that! When we go to school, it regulates our time and provides a daily rhythm.’ The excerpt suggests a longing for the predictability and structure of everyday life previously afforded by in-person learning. This aligns with findings that educational institutions can serve as potential providers of support in young refugee learners’ daily lives (Pastoor, 2015; Şeker & Sirkeci, 2023). Regardless of education and previous traumatic experiences, participants – both men and women – described the sudden loss of predictability and daily routines as increasing their psychological distress. One woman, who reported having no friends in class or close relationship with her teacher, shared that being at school helped her manage difficult emotions and reduce rumination. She stated: ‘I sleep very badly, and … I’m very tired. I’m so tired all day, and it’s hard to breathe. When I was at school, it was easier. I didn’t think so much.’ These excerpts suggest that disruption of daily school routines and face-to-face learning posed challenges to the participants’ self-management skills and their ability to cope with daily life.

A need for breaks from school, despite a love for learning

During the face-to-face interviews, participants commonly portrayed learning as joyful and attending the NIP as largely beneficial to their language learning and well-being. Nabila stated: ‘[At school], I feel safe. Being at school – most of the time – really helps me. I learn Norwegian, talk with the teacher, and … It’s positive for me [happy voice]: Attending the Norwegian language course makes me feel great.’ Nabila still recognised that being at school was not always experienced as positive. Later, in a telephone interview during COVID, Nabila described how she seised the COVID-19 lockdown as an opportunity to recover after an intense period of Norwegian language training. She concluded: ‘However, there are some positive effects of the COVID-19 pandemic. For the first time since resettlement, I could take a break, exhale – somehow.’ Although she, as several others, described the pandemic as eliciting feelings of persistent stress, her positive reframing of the COVID-19 lockdown pointed to another type of breathing spaces: the need for a longer rest.

On a few occasions, participants cautiously addressed the need to take a few days off to deal with their distress. One woman (30s) emphasised the need for a time away from school upon receiving distressing news from home. She stated:‘[tearful voice] I’m thinking that when the situation is really difficult, maybe the student could have three days off. Perhaps we should [try to] understand students who are in such situations.’ The participant appeared to not realise that psychological distress could be a reason for using self-certification for sick leave. The Norwegian system permits employees, including NIP participants, to take three days of sick leave at a time, up to four times annually, without a physician’s note (Regulation Relating to the Integration Act, 2020). The excerpt illustrates what the participant considered a reason for sick leave, suggesting that using self-certification was limited to physical health issues.

Out-of-school activities to recuperate. Participants commonly associated their need for mental breaks to emotionally demanding days. Jesse (30s), for instance, stated:

When you’re having a difficult day, you must try to find an activity, think about pleasant things, be around people, listen to music and exercise. (…) And, I’m running, I enjoy jogging. I haven’t exercised since I moved here, but I used to jog a lot.

Similarly, when asked how he managed after school, when alone, Anwar illustrated his response by lifting the large picture frame above his head and gazing into it – his eyes barely peeking over the bottom edge, and stated:

I do like this; I try to go back inside. (…) I try to do anything that can take away my worries. For example, if I’m very, very sad, I go for a walk, exercise, read, or play video games. So, there isn’t only one particular way.

We previously referred how Farah was helped by reading, but not by knitting. Many participants articulated that they knew themselves well enough to know what could work as a breathing space or not.

Several accounts described engaging in temporary and pleasurable activities outside school to recuperate. Some provided detailed descriptions of how they distracted themselves with hobbies including knitting, painting, gaming, singing, dancing, and hiking. The value they placed on these mental pauses was evident throughout the dataset. One participant portrayed his many hobbies as means for physical and mental recovery after severe trauma. He concluded:

I’ve used all my professional experience to rebuild my body. Through yoga, and other hobbies, such as painting, carving, and listening to music. To me, these hobbies are support tools. And sometimes I drink a little [small laugh]. I’m not addicted, but I drink to enjoy myself.

Others reported a loss of interest in hobbies and meaningful activities they used to enjoy before they arrived in Norway (Tomren & Opaas, 2024).

Physical activities. An aspect of ‘breathing space’ related to physical activity. Several male participants identified their habits of working out as offering immediate and noticeable benefits for their overall health. Jamal, for example, described how he regularly exercised, and when asked whether he believed it affected his learning, he promptly stated:

Yes, of course! It really helps me. For example, this week, I haven’t exercised and I haven’t been eating as usual, so I feel tired. And when I’m tired, I can’t concentrate or remember. When I exercise, I feel happy. (…). If I’m happy, I can better learn.

Anwar made a similar comment, recalling the reason why he initially started working out:

Last summer, I started working out more seriously – then I exercised for my illnesses. [Interviewer: Do you think working out affects your learning?] Yes, very much! Very, very much! Because after working out, I have more energy and I sleep better.

While some of the men framed physical activity as crucial for both learning and well-being, women appeared more hesitant about the benefits of physical activities. Some female participants in our study addressed how concerns about personal safety prevented them from engaging in outdoor physical activities such as going for a walk to deal with distress. One participant who had been equipped with a personal safety alarm for protection against intimate partner violence, repeatedly stated: ‘I don’t like going for a walk [small laugh].’ When the interviewer asked if she thought it was too dangerous, she replied: ‘Yes! – I just don’t want to do that!’ One woman (40s) challenged the health care professionals’ advice to engage in physical activity to improve mental health. She noted: ‘I talked with psychologists, and they just said: ‘You must go for a walk.’ – It’s not – not enough!’ Another participant (30s) simply stated: ‘I don’t think it [physical activity] helps when you have a difficult time.’ When asked about whether she exercised, one woman (30s) started to laugh, stating: ‘No! But I dance.’ She depicted dancing as an opportunity for engaging in positive social interactions outside school. She went on, saying: ‘[small laugh] Sometimes, I take my daughters to my neighbour, who is in the same class as me, and we dance together. I feel so happy!’

Seeking solitude to recuperate. A few participants explicitly framed seeking solitude as a means to recuperate, choosing to withdraw from others for a while. One participant in her 20s stated:

For example, when I’m sad [starts laughing, speaks faster], I sit by myself and drink coffee! That’s nice! [laughing]. I listen to music or go for a walk in the forest for like half an hour. Then I return home. It really helps. After, I feel well! I relax [small laugh].

Seeking solitude fundamentally differed from accounts describing experiences of (deep) loneliness (Tomren & Opaas, 2024). For some, it appeared to serve as a sanctuary where they could let go of their emotions, restore their energy, and help them re-engage with others and their language studies. For example, another woman (20s) shared how she occasionally used to cycle to a specific spot in a nearby forest, allowing herself to let go of painful feelings and thoughts. She concluded: ‘I use to sit there, in the forest. I just sit there, breathing for a while. Then I return home.’ When she was asked how she felt afterwards, she simply noted: ‘It is difficult. But afterwards it is normal. ( … ) Then, I’ll want to talk and laugh a little again.’ This highlight seeking solitude as an act of selfcare that may support individual’s in restoring a sense of emotional well-being. Previous research suggests that spending time outdoors, particularly out in nature, may alleviate mental distress (Bratman et al., 2015).

The participants’ accounts suggest that attending school provided meaning, structure and predictability during the early years of resettlement, offering participants a temporary break and a sense of emotional distance from mental distress that was more present when left to themselves at home. At the same time, being fully present and attentive at school, when emotionally loaded thoughts and memories were intruding upon them, was described as hard. Therefore, both scheduled breaks and self-initiated small breaks were at times necessary to regain their composure at school. However, the limited number of acceptable reasons for leaving class may leave adult learners without opportunities to compose themselves. Outside school, in their spare time, activities like going for walks, exercising, dancing and reading were means to recuperate, which could prepare them for learning on their next day at school.

On learning the language

Not surprisingly, participants typically constructed language acquisition and higher education as crucial for achieving future aspirations. For example, when asked about future hopes, Sabika’s (30s) conclusion illustrates a shared emphasis on the value of education among participants: ‘It’s still a dream – taking education. Because, then I’m someone here in Norway, not only a zero. I can rebuild myself, do something. Perhaps buy a car or eventually a house. Get a job. Establish a good life here.’ Here, Sabika constructed education as a place for healing after trauma, pointing to how knowledge can empower individuals beyond formal education. Moreover, she articulated a distinction between those with and without (higher) education, described as shaping an individual’s self-esteem and the opportunities they have to establish themselves as independent citizens. Similarly, Salim framed refugees’ independence as the ability to manage without the assistance from interpreters: ‘Sometimes, I think that getting help with translation isn’t good. We need to manage on our own ( … That’s very important to me. That’s what I think.’ Nadia made a similar comment, outlining future aspirations made possible by learning Norwegian: ‘When I learn Norwegian, I can talk with others, find work, and, for example when I’m going to meetings in kindergarten, I can speak without an interpreter.’ Taken together, the participants’ accounts depict becoming independent as crucial for establishing their lives after trauma. The aspirations to become independent shaped their priorities on learning and how they approached emotional distress when in a learning situation. It may be worth considering whether adult refugee learners’ priorities and determination to focus on learning were also shaped by pressures from the Norwegian authorities, which emphasise early employment, self-sufficiency, and societal contribution. However, the participants’ positive attitudes towards acquiring a new language and engaging with a new culture shaped their priorities and encouraged them to invest time and efforts to prioritise learning.

Discussion

In this study, we aimed to explore (trauma-affected) adult refugee learners’ meaning-making of learning and coping with distressing memories, emotions and thoughts, situating their experiences, efforts, and priorities within the framework of the Norwegian Introduction Programme. We developed three connected, yet distinctive themes, unified by an overarching theme, Perseverance – to stay the course: (1) Preferred learning strategies – tools for managing psychological distress; (2) Pushing aside trauma-related memories, thoughts and feelings to focus on learning; and (3) Making temporary breathing spaces. Each theme offers insight into a specific aspect of adult refugee learners’ meaning-making and coping with psychological distress. Taken together, the themes provide a deeper understanding of adult learners’ persistent determination and aspirations to complete the NIP, despite the burden of trauma and forced migration. The insights from this study thus add to earlier research on trauma-affected adult learners from refugee backgrounds by offering novel perspectives from adult refugees engaged in language learning.

Our analysis suggests a dual benefit of learning strategies that may be overlooked by educators and adult learners alike. Some strategies not only served as tools for learning but also appeared to help participants mitigate psychological distress. Thus, we suggest refugee learners’ preferred learning strategies may have therapeutic qualities. To our knowledge, there is no former study on the potential therapeutic qualities of learning strategies for adult learners. However, previous research suggests that teachers’ pedagogical actions may have therapeutic qualities (Hayward, 2017). Such actions include creating supportive, inclusive and safe learning environments through culturally responsive pedagogy (Gay, 2002; Housel, 2020), as well as recognising the impact of adversity on learning (Horsman, 2000). Our finding that adult refugees use learning strategies to acquire knowledge and also to regulate emotional distress, direct novel attention to the resources and actions of the individual learner. Attention towards adult learners’ resources, aligns with educational policies and legislation that promote user-centred practices and emphasise students’ voice and participation (Integration Act, 2020, §§ 1, 15; UNHCR, 2018). However, although intended to empower, emphasising adult refugees’ resources may unintentionally shift the responsibility from those who hold the structural power, such as educational and public institutions responsible for integration, and place the burden to succeed on the refugees themselves (Støren, 2024).

Research indicates that pedagogical differences between school systems that prioritise critical thinking and those that put more emphasis on repetitive learning and memorisation (Li, 2022, pp. e.g. 134–135, 148–149) pose challenges to adult refugee learners who are more familiar with the latter approach. Similarly, educators may find it difficult to recognise the potential benefits of repetitive learning, if it conflicts with their understandings of what constitutes ‘good’ learning. We note, however, that learning strategies described as effective during an initial language training phase may not necessarily remain effective in later stages of the integration process. Our study highlights the importance of educators recognising their students’ learning strategies as resources that not only can facilitate language learning but also elicit positive feelings and a sense of calmness, helping them navigating emotional distress.

Across our data, pushing aside painful memories, emotions and thoughts was framed as necessary coping strategies to maintain focus on learning and being able to meet the expectations and requirements in the limited time of the NIP. To reach a specific language proficiency within a set timeframe appeared urgent to the participants, who frequently constructed language acquisition as crucial for their future aspirations. It seems useful to understand the 22 refugee learners’ efforts, intentions, and agency as strategic actions situated within a particular context and timeframe, aimed at achieving educational goals. For most of the participants in our study, it appeared more manageable and readily rational to prioritise learning and focus on future goals than to work on processing the emotional and cognitive aftermath of trauma. In the context of learning, despite that close to half of our study’s participants had met the questionnaire-based criteria for a provisional PTSD diagnosis, pushing emotional distress aside and re-direct attention into grammar or reading and rereading seemed useful for most of the adult learners in this study. However, pushing past and present trauma aside to focus on learning also leaves a potential harm in the form of the unaddressed adverse life experiences. Previous research suggest that refugees’ mental health problems and needs are frequently under-prioritised by themselves due to competing needs, and also by healthcare services, whether in reception countries (van der Boor & White, 2020) or in humanitarian field settings (Ventevogel, 2018). Studies have found that confronting and working through painful or traumatic memories in a safe therapeutic relationship, can facilitate refugees’ psychological improvement (Opaas & Hartmann, 2021). However, as therapists may often not be available, guidance may potentially be provided by a trained school counsellor or nurse (Pastoor, 2015). Furthermore, insights from our study suggest that adult learners make sense of their experiences and efforts in various ways important to navigate emotional distress, support learning and, eventually, establish a new life in Norway.

The participants’ strategies could be understood as processes of active coping and emotional self-regulation (consciously using learning strategies to reduce emotional distress and inner turmoil), acceptance (letting all the grief and troublesome thoughts come forth after school) or as cognitive restructuring or positive reframing (comparing to others’ more severe trauma, or to a population where everyone have suffered such trauma), both parts of approach-based coping, linked to adaptation and health (Solberg et al., 2021). The participants in our study also engaged in avoidant coping strategies, described as deliberate attempts to avoid painful thoughts and feelings during class and attempts to forget, often with varying degrees of success. Avoidant coping strategies are commonly associated with negative mental health outcomes (Solberg et al., 2021). For example, consistently pushing aside past and present adversities has been linked to maladaptive avoidant behaviour, which may hinder emotional processing and worsen mental health problems among traumatised refugees (Yuval & Bernstein, 2017). Further evidence (e.g. Solberg et al., 2021; Woltin et al., 2018) suggest that pushing psychological distress aside is associated with increased psychological distress later on. In contrast, a study on former child refugees suggested that strategic use of avoidance (e.g. pushing aside traumatic memories to prevent being overwhelmed) served an adaptive function across their lifespan (McCormack & Tapp, 2019). Similarly, another study suggested that the capacity to push aside intrusive trauma-related memories may contribute to positive post-traumatic adaptation (Mary et al., 2020). Nevertheless, while participants constructed avoidance as beneficial in the present, our analysis raises concerns about the future potential individual and societal costs of prioritising dealing with learning and less with health.

Other coping strategies deemed helpful by the participants included taking a break during school hours to recuperate. The participants’ accounts provided insights into the tension between the need to remain present in the classroom – and a need for brief mental breaks. However, engaging in daily school routines was itself found to provide a temporary break from emotional distress. Classroom structure and behavioural norms offer structure and predictability amidst life’s chaos, and scheduled breaks allow for mental recovery and social engagement. The participants’ accounts suggested that scheduled breaks insufficiently address adult learners’ needs for airing painful emotions and thoughts in a private space. In our data, the need for informal, self-initiated breaks in addition to the formal breaks was evident. According to Baines and Blatchford (2023), systematic evidence regarding implications of breaks remains limited. Their findings suggest that educators frequently undervalue the positive impact of breaks on students’ wellbeing, and that there is a ‘widespread practice of withholding breaks’ (p. 943) to modify students’ behaviour.

In addition to recuperating by breaks within the school-day and by attending school itself, participants reported various activities outside school, such as physical exercise, dancing, reading or seeking solitude that made them restore their calm, renew their energy, and be ready for the next school-day. In a survey on physical activity among newly arrived refugees in Sweden (461 men and 204 women), Sjögren Forss et al. (2021) found that physical activity reduced mental distress and increased adult refugees’ well-being. The study suggests a need to increase knowledge among newly arrived refugees about the benefits of physical activity, by informing them on its positive effects on such as sleep quality and reduced stress. In a Danish study examining the perceptions of physical activities and health among newly arrived refugees, women reported a wish to engage more in physical activities. However, they often faced barriers to doing so, including lack of time due to family obligations, cultural restrictions and fear of social judgment (Ryom et al., 2022).

Adult education, like hospitals and community centres, may reflect a community’s shared vision, where even the buildings themselves embody structure, order and the underlying representation of the community’s values and worldview (Martinsen, 2021, p. 17). The participants’ accounts suggest that schools can, and need to be, more than a ‘house of productivity’. Adult education’s interpretations of whether language- and other training programmes provide a ‘house’ in which adult students can ‘live’, experience success, and feel safe, supported, and challenged – is essential for a positive educational experience and for perseverance, irrespective of achieving the intended outcomes. Adult refugee learners’ own coping actions and how they interpret and construct their efforts to deal with adverse life experiences and learning significantly impact their establishing in the resettlement country. How they manage this process will have lasting effects on their lives and on the society in which they are a part.

We acknowledge our analysis as subjective, and that the insights generated from this study are both enriched and constrained by the intersection of a small dataset, the context of the NIP – along with its requirements and possibilities – and our positionalities as researchers, encompassing our theoretical assumptions and our experience with refugees and migrants. Within the scope of this analysis, we focused on developing themes across the dataset rather than examining variation between subgroups. However, we acknowledge that variation in gendered trauma exposure, educational background, and age may significantly shape how adult refugee learners cope with and make sense of their experiences. Such differences could meaningfully be pursued in future research.

Implications

While the suggested implications may be self-evident to practitioners working in adult education and integration contexts, we believe it is useful to articulate them, as they might otherwise remain implicit.

The dual qualities of learning strategies: In a hectic school day, teachers may assume, rather than ask their students which learning strategies they consider most effective. Scheduled teacher-student conversations are opportunities to address adult refugees’ preferred learning strategies and invite them to notice how they feel when employing these strategies in various contexts. This may raise adult refugees’ awareness of their preferred learning strategies as resources to facilitate learning, and as a means of navigating emotional distress. To effectively utilise this dual benefit, educators should acknowledge and support their students’ preferred strategies – even when they doubt their effectiveness. Adult learners would benefit from teachers’ knowledge of the potential impact of accessible learning strategies on both learning and emotional health.

Pushing aside trauma-related memories to focus on learning: Considering the participants’ efforts to hide their feelings and put on a smiling face, teachers need to be aware of the deep distress that may be hidden under this mask. Therefore, the implications highlighted above and below must be applied also to those who appear cheerful. Some participants managed to learn despite severe traumatic experiences. However, support is needed for those who struggle so hard with traumatic memories that it extensively hinders their learning.

A need for brief breaks to recuperate: It may be uncomfortable and burdensome for distressed adult students to repeatedly request permission to leave the classroom or use the restroom during a school day to recuperate. One way to provide flexible, dignity-preserving alternatives for such short breaks without imposing unintended burdens is to create a sense of understanding and openness, both in class and in formal student-teacher conversations. This can be done by acknowledging that students may occasionally need brief breaks and by encouraging them to communicate such needs without being required to justify them in front of everyone. For example: ‘If you need it, feel free to take a brief five-minute break from my lessons to “breathe”. You don’t need to ask for permission. Let’s talk again after class on Monday in two weeks to see how it goes. What do you think?’ It is equally important that such conversations are followed up on. In our experience, receiving understanding and being regarded seriously can reduce a student’s need for leaving the classroom. Finally, while physical exercise may not be sufficient to restore (mental) health after trauma, it may still be helpful. However, female refugees’ worries about safety and social sanctions must be counteracted, and safe spaces for physical activity should be established.

Conclusion

Our analysis connects adult refugee learners’ meaning-making and coping to the wider discourse in adult education, emphasising the impact of political-educational demands on these learners’ priorities, adaptation and efforts to establish a new life. The insights from this study suggest that the participants’ use of preferred learning strategies to alleviate psychological distress, their efforts to push aside psychological distress to focus on learning, and allowing themselves (il)legitimate ‘breathing spaces’ to recuperate helped them persevere in the training programme. While some coping efforts, such as using available learning strategies to calm down, may be understood as creative or even innovative by educators, taking brief pauses can easily be misread as ‘taking liberties’ rather than as acts of self-care. The insights from our study offer teachers alternative explanations for student behaviour that does not meet classroom behaviour expectations.

The insights from this study could be helpful for educators, stakeholders, and students who have experienced trauma, as well as those who ‘just’ feel overwhelmed. Taken together, these insights can inform future pedagogical practices, strengthen efforts to ensure refugees’ right to education (United Nations General Assembly, 1951, Article 22), and ultimately support refugees in establishing their lives in the resettlement country.
