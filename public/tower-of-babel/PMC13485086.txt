Commentary on: Is a visual field test really needed for a driving license?
Authors: Smita Narayanan
Published: 2026

The call for applying stringent criteria for obtaining a driving license to decrease the numbers of Road Traffic Accidents (RTA) has been on and off since many decades. This is a cross-sectional study to determine the prevalence of visual field defects among participants requiring a driving license.[1] Of the 1900 participants studied, 86 (4.5%) drivers had binocular visual field defects and would have failed the United Kingdom Driver and Vehicle Licensing Authority UK DVLA criteria for Group 2 (buses and lorries). Thirty-one among them would have failed the DVLA criteria for Group 1 (cars and motorcycles) too. The authors found glaucoma to be the leading cause of the visual field defects. They have suggested the use of Esterman Test for visual field assessment for driving license purposes and to establish an accredited panel of ophthalmologists to enable uniformity of examination.

Driving is a composite skill which needs multiple visual functions including the visual fields. A good reaction time, hearing ability, concentration, cognition, and many known and unknown factors besides the obvious hands–feet and eye coordination are also required. The visual field for driving purposes is binocular and is heavily affected by ocular and head movements. This means that the Esterman test is a poor substitute for the actual fields used during driving.[2]

In patients with visual field defects, the cognitive status and duration of visual field loss help to compensate. Monocular defects are easily “filled in” by the visual field of the other eye. Neuroplasticity and the ability to compensate vary between individuals. On the other hand, age can compound the ability to cause accidents. To determine which RTA occurred due to lapse of concentration and which occurred due to a visual field defect is very difficult.

No single visual field test can encompass all these variables and determine a person’s ability to drive safely. Unlike autorefractometry, visual field testing on the automated perimeter does not carry the same experience of ease for all test takers.

In this era of self-driving vehicles, greater flexibility is needed in the application of visual field standards during driving. The strategies for detection of glaucomatous or neurological or any other causes of visual field defects do not reflect the complexity of the actual driving environment.[3] A practical road test of sufficient duration in the real world is the best way to know if a person with visual field defects is fit to drive or not, but it carries a significant risk to life and limb.[4] Instead, a driving test in a simulated environment or an artificial intelligence system will help to know if a driver with visual field defects is safe or not.
