Cervical cancer screening predictors in Tanzania: a variance component models’ approach of national survey 2022
Authors: Jovin R. Tibenderana, Sanun Ally Kessy, Jomo Gimonge, Innocent Peter, Fabiola Moshi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Despite the potential for prevention through early detection and treatment, cervical cancer remains the most prevalent cancer among women of reproductive age (WRA). There is a paucity of evidence on the predictors influencing cervical cancer screening nationwide. Thus, this study aimed to determine the predictors associated with cervical cancer screening among WRA in Tanzania using the 2022 Tanzania Demographic and Health Surveys. This study was an analytical cross-sectional study that utilized nationally representative secondary data sourced from the Tanzania Demographic and Health Surveys of 2022. A weighted sample of 15,254 WRA (15–49 years) was included in the study. A multilevel binary logistic regression analysis was used to examine predictors associated with the uptake of cervical cancer screening services. These results were presented using adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Predictors that were found to be significantly associated with cervical cancer screening among WRA in Tanzania were age (AOR = 7.12, 95% CI: 5.42–9.37), education (AOR = 2.58, 95% CI: 1.91–3.48), occupation (AOR = 1.3, 95% CI: 1.10–1.54), having visited a health facility in the past 12 months (AOR = 1.30, 95% CI: 1.13–1.51), the use of contraceptives (AOR = 1.23, 95% CI: 1.06–1.42), human immunodeficiency virus status (AOR = 5.56, 95% CI: 4.33–7.14), and residence (AOR = 0.64, 95% CI: 0.52–0.78). Overall, cervical cancer screening uptake is low in Tanzania. These findings underscore the importance of tailored interventions to improve screening rates, particularly among younger, less educated, rural-dwelling, and socioeconomically disadvantaged women. Efforts to enhance access to healthcare services, increase health literacy, and address barriers to screening participation are essential for reducing cervical cancer disparities and promoting women’s health.

1. Introduction

Cervical cancer remains one of the most common gynecological cancers worldwide. According to current data, it is regarded as the fourth most prevalent cancer among women globally.[1,2] It is primarily caused by persistent infection with the human papillomavirus (HPV), which ranks among the most common sexually transmitted infections (STIs). There are over 130 identified HPV serotypes, categorized as either high-risk or low-risk. High-risk types, particularly HPV-16 and 18, are linked to the development of cervical cancer, whereas low-risk types typically lead to the formation of noncancerous warts.[3,4]

In 2022, there were around 660,000 newly diagnosed cases and 350,000 deaths attributed to cervical cancer worldwide.[4] Without taking additional action, cases are expected to rise to 700,000 by 2030.[5] In low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), the incidence rate is almost double, and the mortality rate is 3 times higher compared with high-income countries.[3,6]

Almost 85% of all diagnosed cases and 90% of all cervical cancer deaths occur in LMICs.[7–9] Moreover, only 44% of women in LMICs have undergone cervical cancer screening, with the lowest rates observed among women in sub-Saharan Africa.[10]

Cervical cancer is the most frequent cancer among women aged 15 to 44 years in Tanzania,[5,11] and this high burden is attributed to several factors, including the high prevalence of HPV in the region,[12] which is the primary cause of cervical cancer. Other reasons include lack of awareness and logistical challenges.[13]

East Africa has been identified as one of the regions with the highest prevalence of HPV infections.[12] It is estimated that approximately 3.3% of women in the general population carry the cervical HPV-16/18 infection at any given time, with 68.0% of invasive cervical cancers being caused by HPV types 16 or 18.[14]

Screening has the potential to significantly decrease mortality, illness, and the negative impact on quality of life caused by cervical cancer.[15] Cervical cancer screening is designed to identify precancerous alterations that could progress to cancer if left untreated.[7] Routine screening can detect abnormal cervical cells before they transform into cancer, enabling timely intervention to halt disease progression. This is especially crucial because cervical cancer typically remains asymptomatic until it reaches a more advanced stage, making treatment more difficult and less successful.[16]

Tanzania places a strong focus on vaccinating young girls against HPV to prevent infections that can lead to cervical cancer. Public health campaigns and community education, backed by the government, NGOs, and international organizations, work to boost awareness and screening participation.[11] Outreach programs bring services to remote regions, and training healthcare providers ensures proficient screening and follow-up care.[11] These combined efforts aim to enhance screening rates and lower the incidence and mortality of cervical cancer. The effectiveness of cervical cancer screening programs in detecting precancerous conditions and early-stage cancers is well established elsewhere.[2,17]

Cervical cancer is considered nearly completely preventable because of the highly effective primary prevention (HPV vaccine), which significantly reduces infection risk when administered to young individuals before sexual activity. Additionally, regular screening methods can detect precancerous changes that can be treated early.[4]

Previous studies reported that age,[18,19] education level,[19,20] distance to a health facility,[18,21] media exposure,[22,23] contraceptive use,[24] wealth index,[18–20] residence,[19,20] number of living children,[19] occupation,[25] health insurance,[19,26] human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) status,[20] visiting a health facility in the past 12 months,[19] and a history of STI in the past 12 months[24] were factors significantly associated with cervical cancer screening.

Most of the studies in Tanzania have assessed knowledge of cervical cancer screening[8,27–29] as well as associated factors, but their findings cannot be generalized to the whole country.[26,30] Research on cervical cancer screening in Tanzania remains scarce, and a noticeable lack of published studies exists on the factors influencing cervical cancer screening nationwide. Furthermore, previous studies did not account for the hierarchical or nested structure of the Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) data to address the predictors associated with cervical cancer screening. Hence, to address this gap and provide a comprehensive understanding of how individual- and community-level predictors interact to influence outcomes, the present study aims to determine the predictors associated with cervical cancer screening among women of reproductive age (WRA) in Tanzania in 2022 using multilevel analysis. The results of this study provide important information for health policymakers, program designers, and healthcare providers, laying the groundwork for improving financial support and healthcare access for WRA.

2. Methods and materials

The reporting of this study has followed the STROBE guideline.[31]

2.1. Study design and period

This analytical cross-sectional study was carried out using information gathered from the Tanzania Demographic and Health Survey (TDHS) in 2022. Tanzania, the largest nation in East Africa, spans 940,000 square kilometers, including 60,000 square kilometers of inland water. With an annual population growth rate of 3.2%, as of 2022, the population of Tanzania was estimated at 61,741,120, and WRA are estimated to be about 16 million.[32,33]

2.2. Data source and study population

The TDHS is carried out every 5 years to update health and health-related indicators. The DHS program provided the data. For further details on the surveys, please refer to the respective national reports.[33] After obtaining authorization through an online request that outlined the research’s purpose, the data were accessed from the MEASURE DHS website via https://dhsprogram.com/. The study included 15,254 WRAs aged 15 to 49 years who participated in the DHS 2022.

2.3. Sampling technique

The TDHS used a 2-stage probability sampling method. In the first stage, primary sampling units, which are census enumeration areas (EAs), were selected with a probability proportionate to size within each stratum. These EAs serve as survey clusters. In the second stage, households within each EA are selected proportionally using a systematic sampling method. Detailed information on the sampling technique was previously published.[32,33]

2.4. Variables and measurements

2.4.1. Outcome variable

The dependent variable for this study was cervical cancer screening, which was measured by the question, “Ever tested for cervical cancer by health care provider?” The binary response was Yes/No, following the approach employed by other researchers who utilized DHS data.[18,19]

2.4.2. Independent variables

The independent variables were classified as individual- and community-level variables. The individual-level variables included maternal age, marital status, educational status of women, media exposure, number of living children, HIV status, contraceptive use, health insurance coverage, history of STI, and employment status. The place of residence, distance to a health facility, and wealth index of the study participants were considered as community-level variables. We categorized and recategorized the independent variables in accordance with the literature.[13,17,19,26,30]

2.5. Statistical analysis

We used Stata software, version 18.0 (Stata Corporation, College Station) for all statistical analyses. Initially, we applied cross-tabulation to explore the distribution of cervical cancer screening across the various background characteristics, and chi-square tests were used to calculate the P-values. Before conducting the regression analysis, we assessed potential collinearity among the variables using the variance inflation factor. Results indicated minimal collinearity, with variance inflation factor values ranging from 1.50 to 1.86, suggesting no significant multicollinearity concerns. To investigate the predictors of cervical cancer screening, we employed a multilevel binary logistic regression analysis. This approach was chosen due to the complex survey design of the DHS, which involved 2-stage cluster sampling. Four models were utilized for analysis: Model 0, an empty model without any explanatory variables; Model I, including individual-level factors; Model II, including community-level predictors; and Model III, considering all independent variables together. Crude and adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated for each model. Both fixed and random effects were included in all models. Random effects represented the variation in cervical cancer screening based on the primary sampling unit, measured by the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). Fixed effects were employed to demonstrate the relationship between explanatory variables and the outcome. The fitness of the models was assessed using the Akaike information criterion, enabling us to gauge the goodness of fit of each model to the data. A significance level of P < .05 was utilized for all statistical tests. Additionally, we applied weighting to all analyses to correct for any disproportionate sampling and nonresponse in the data.

2.5.1. Random effects

Table 2 presents the results for random effects. We observed that the ICC value in Model III was 0.1002, indicating that approximately 10.02% of the variability in cervical cancer screening was attributed to differences between the clusters. The cluster-level variation, as indicated by the ICC values, was 20.14%, 12.38%, 13.10%, and 10.02% for Model 0, Model I, Model II, and Model III, respectively. We opted to focus on interpreting the results of Model III for the discussion, as it had the lowest Akaike information criterion value (6547.33) among the models, indicating the best goodness of fit.

2.6. Ethical consideration

This study analyzed secondary data from the 2022 TDHS. Permission to use the dataset was granted by MEASURE DHS/ICF International. The DHS Program follows strict industry standards to ensure respondent privacy, and ICF International confirms that the survey complies with the US Department of Health and Human Services’ Human Subjects Protection Act. Ethical approval for the survey was obtained in advance, and informed consent was obtained from all participants. For respondents under 18 years of age, written consent was provided by their parents or guardians. Additional details on the data and ethical guidelines are available on the DHS program website (https://dhsprogram.com/methodology/Protecting-the-Privacy-of-DHS-Survey-Respondents.cfm). Consequently, no further ethical approval was necessary for this study.

3.3. Predictors of cervical cancer screening

3.3.1. Fixed effect

Table 2, Model III, presents the predictors of cervical cancer screening among Tanzanian WRA. Women aged 35 to 49 years were more likely to be screened for cervical cancer compared with those aged 15 to 24 years (AOR = 7.12, 95% CI: 5.42–9.37). The odds of cervical cancer screening were higher among educated women (secondary/higher) compared with uneducated women (AOR = 2.58, 95% CI: 1.91–3.48). Notably, WRA who visited a health facility in the past 12 months had a higher likelihood of cervical cancer screening compared with those who did not (AOR = 1.30, 95% CI: 1.13–1.51). Compared with those who had not used contraceptives, women who used contraceptives had higher odds of cervical cancer screening (AOR = 1.23, 95% CI: 1.06–1.42). WRA who were HIV-positive were more likely (AOR = 5.56, 95% CI: 4.33–7.14) to screen for cervical cancer compared with HIV-negative women. Women with a history of STI in the past 12 months were more likely to screen for cervical cancer compared with those who did not (AOR = 1.33, 95% CI: 1.01–1.75). Additionally, participants residing in rural areas had 36% lower odds of screening for cervical cancer compared with their counterparts (AOR = 0.64, 95% CI: 0.52–0.78). We found that participants from rich households were more likely to screen for cervical cancer compared with their counterparts (AOR = 2.09, 95% CI: 1.60–2.73).

4. Discussion

The study aimed to determine the predictors of cervical cancer screening among WRA in Tanzania. Predictors that were significantly associated with cervical cancer screening in Tanzania were age, educational level, residence, occupation, having a history of STIs, previous health facility contact within the past 12 months, HIV status, and a history of contraceptive use.

It was observed that a very small proportion (7.2%) of WRA underwent cervical cancer screening in Tanzania. This finding aligns with those of other sub-Saharan African countries, such as Nigeria[34] and Ghana,[35] which reported cervical cancer screening proportions of 8% and 8.3%, respectively, but it was higher than that reported in Ethiopia,[10] which was 4%. Moreover, the proportion was lower than that reported in Thailand[36] and Dessie, Ethiopia,[37] which were 13.27% and 12%, respectively. Unlike Tanzania, the early introduction and implementation of universal health coverage since 2010 in developed countries have resulted in cervical cancer screening and treatment being free of charge, and subsequently, more women have presented for diagnosis and treatment.[25] Additionally, the availability of screening centers with different approaches in both rural and urban areas has significantly improved cervical cancer screening in these countries.[38]

Older women (35–49 years) were more likely to be screened for cervical cancer compared with those aged 15 to 24 years. This public health emphasis is anticipated, given that research has indicated a higher incidence of cervical cancer among older women.[39] Consequently, healthcare providers tend to prioritize recommending cervical cancer screening for older women over younger ones, as recommended by the World Health Organization.[40] However, this approach may inadvertently create gaps in public awareness of cervical cancer screening, disadvantaging younger women who are more susceptible to HPV infection and subsequent premalignant lesions due to their higher likelihood of engaging in risky sexual behavior.[41] The World Health Organization has also reported that the target age group in low-resource countries should be between 30 and 49 years of age since the disease is uncommon in females under 30 years of age.[42] This might have informed the screening center’s healthcare staff, who therefore focused on encouraging this age group to undergo screening. This might also explain why this population was the largest group utilizing the screening center. This finding is in line with another study conducted in Ethiopia.[10] However, the findings of this study were contrary to those of the study conducted in the Mara Region in Tanzania, which showed that older women were 76% less likely to undergo cervical cancer screening,[43] and those of another study conducted in Turkey,[44] which revealed that young adults were more likely to be screened for cervical cancer than older adults. These disparities can be attributed to differences in sample size, study population, and study period.

Educated women (secondary education and above) were 3 times more likely to screen for cervical cancer than uneducated women. Possible reasons for this could be that educated women tend to possess greater awareness and knowledge regarding health issues, including preventive measures such as screening. Another reason could be that access to information through various sources, coupled with improved health literacy, enables them to navigate the healthcare system more effectively and comprehend the screening process. Economic stability, which is often associated with higher education levels, further facilitates their ability to afford healthcare expenses and allocate resources for screening. These findings are consistent with other studies conducted in Ghana,[45] northwestern Ethiopia,[25] Kenya,[20] and Bangladesh.[46] This is not surprising, as it is expected that women who are educated have an understanding of the causes, risk factors, prevention mechanisms, and treatment of the disease and, as such, can demand screening services. Also, better-educated women have a higher efficiency in the production of health and education, as well as greater self-efficacy, confidence, motivation, and social inclusion in the search for health interventions.[15] Additionally, a possible explanation could be that women with some level of education are more likely to encounter information regarding cervical cancer screening and its significance. This exposure, often gained through reading materials such as books, leaflets, and articles, enhances their knowledge of the subject. Consequently, possessing greater knowledge from these sources is more likely to raise their awareness about the importance of cervical cancer screening, leading them to utilize such services more than less-educated women.

Employed women were more likely to screen for cervical cancer compared with those who were not, possibly due to access to healthcare through employer-provided health insurance. Additionally, employed women have a stable income that enables them to afford medical services, including preventive services, and these women benefit from extensive social networks at work, where they share information and encourage each other to participate in health screening. This finding is in line with other studies conducted in South Africa,[47] Uganda,[48] and Canada.[38] A study conducted in Ghana revealed that market women who were self-employed had enough time to participate in screening activities compared with government workers and students, whose time was restricted due to their activities and who needed more time to engage in screening activities.[49] However, another study conducted in England reported that women who were unemployed screened for cervical cancer compared with employed women.[50] Employment status improves individual economic status, which increases the likelihood of participating in health and screening activities.

Notably, WRA who visited health facilities in the past 12 months had a higher likelihood of cervical cancer screening compared with those who did not. This finding is in line with other studies conducted in Northern Tanzania, Northern Ethiopia,[17] and Bangladesh.[46] This might indicate that health workers provided awareness and encouraged women to undergo cervical cancer screening. It might also indicate that women who had visited health facilities had an opportunity to gain awareness about the cervical cancer screening service, which leads to better utilization of the screening service.[10]

Our study revealed that women with a history of STIs in the past 12 months were more likely to undergo cervical cancer screening compared with those who did not. This may be because cervical cancer screening is sometimes advised as part of medical care when certain STIs are present.[51] This finding is in line with an African multi-country study.[24]

Additionally, participants residing in rural areas were less likely to be screened for cervical cancer compared with women residing in urban areas. This finding has been seen in previous studies conducted in Namibia,[19] Ghana,[45] and Nigeria, which reported that more than one-third of the screened population was from rural areas. This can be explained by the fact that access to healthcare differs between women in rural and urban areas, largely due to transportation and financial constraints that prevent screening attendance.[52] This is of paramount importance, as it highlights the need for investing in healthcare accessibility and equity, which will greatly impact screening services for these women.

We found that participants from rich households were more likely to screen for cervical cancer compared with their counterparts; this finding is consistent with studies conducted in Kenya,[20] Rwanda,[47] and Turkey.[44] This finding is inconsistent with a study conducted in England, which found that WRA from all social classes screened for cervical cancer.[50] This has been attributed to adjustments in healthcare policy, which include the extension of HPV vaccination to boys born after 2006 and men who have sex with men. In addition, the pandemic rapidly changed the landscape of healthcare, whereby 90% of general practitioner consultations were conducted remotely as of April 2020, compared with 31% in the year prior.[50]

WRA who were HIV-positive were 6 times more likely to screen for cervical cancer; this finding was consistent with studies conducted in Ethiopia[17] and Ghana.[53] Healthcare professionals frequently advise routine cervical cancer screening for HIV-positive women as part of their comprehensive treatment, and HIV-positive women are more likely to screen for the disease for a number of reasons. HIV-positive women are encouraged to prioritize cervical cancer screening due to their 6-fold higher risk of developing cervical cancer,[4] increased awareness, and a proactive approach to managing their health. Additionally, many HIV clinics provide comprehensive healthcare services, such as cervical cancer screening, which makes routine screening easier to obtain and more convenient for women living with HIV.

The study revealed that contraceptive users had a higher likelihood of cervical cancer screening than nonusers. This finding aligns with a prior study by Ba et al,[19] which found that those who use contraception are more likely to visit the hospital regularly for their contraceptive requirements. Therefore, as they attend the health facility for their needs, they are more likely to be exposed to education on the dangers of cervical cancer and the significance of screening for early diagnosis and treatment. Healthcare practitioners are more likely to recommend cervical cancer screening to their patients because studies have shown that users of oral contraceptives have an increased risk of several malignancies, including cervical cancer.[54–57] This is another possible explanation, which could help to explain why, in comparison to nonusers, those who use contraceptives have higher odds of developing cervical cancer.

4.1. Strengths and limitations

We used data from the most recent DHS, which is known for its rigorous methodology and validation across multiple studies. The use of nationally representative data ensures that our findings are broadly applicable to Tanzanian WRA and women in other African nations. However, our study has limitations; the reliance on secondary data limited our analysis to the variables accessible in the datasets, excluding important factors such as cultural influences and patriarchal norms that may influence cervical cancer screening among WRA. Furthermore, the cross-sectional nature of the DHS prohibits causal inferences. While our study provides insights into predictors of cervical cancer screening uptake in Tanzania, it is important to note that reliance on self-reported data from the TDHS raises the likelihood of recall bias and limits our ability to assess test type, diagnostic accuracy, and subsequent outcomes. This has implications for understanding the potential harms of screening, particularly overdiagnosis and overtreatment. Evidence from other cancer types demonstrates how such challenges can differ across regions. For example, Chang et al[58] highlighted distinct East-West variations in lung cancer screening, where the prevalence of subsolid nodules and their risk of overdiagnosis differed markedly between populations, with higher rates observed in East Asian cohorts compared with Western settings. These findings emphasize that screening outcomes are not only dependent on uptake but also on the biological characteristics of the disease, test accuracy, and health system context similar to that for cervical cancer. However, lessons from other regions and cancer types suggest that without standardized test quality, clear follow-up protocols, and attention to context-specific risks, expanded screening programs could inadvertently expose women to unnecessary interventions for transient or regressing lesions. While a large sample size improves statistical power, it can also make tiny associations statistically significant. Even with these limitations, our findings offer useful insights and serve as a platform for future studies and targeted interventions.

5. Conclusion

This study found that cervical cancer screening uptake was low. The findings highlight that there is a need to strengthen SBCC (Social and Behavioral Change Communication) programs, including a wide range of interventions that fall into 3 broad categories: mass media, interpersonal communication, and community mobilization. Therefore, healthcare providers and other stakeholders should conduct interventions continuously to develop women’s health-seeking behavior towards the uptake of cervical cancer screening. There is also a need to strengthen the health system to allow accessibility and affordability for women of all economic statuses, education levels, and residences to access screening services.
