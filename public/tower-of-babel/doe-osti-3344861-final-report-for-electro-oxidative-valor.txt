Final Technical Report for DE-SC0023322 “Electro-oxidative valorization of biomass:
 design strategies for selective and stable catalysis”
Overview
 This project aimed to develop a fundamental understanding of the factors that dictate activity,
selectivity, and deactivation during electrochemical partial oxidation of multi-carbon organic
molecules derived from biomass. Demonstrative efforts involved the furanic chemical platform
(oxidations of furfural and hydroxymethylfurfural to value-added compounds), while the broader
goal was extraction of generalizable reactivity principles. The team advanced understanding of
the requirements for controlling the progression between oxygenate functional groups (alcohol to
aldehyde, aldehyde to carboxylic acid) while also avoiding undesired pathways that lead to
surface poisoning—for example, C-C cleavage steps that lead to CO* formation or overoxidation
of acids to bound carboxylate (R-COO**) species (“*” indicating a surface site).
 Prior research in electro-oxidation had disproportionately focused on alkaline conditions
(where more materials are stable), whereas a majority of feedstock preparation methods (e.g.,
pyrolysis, acid hydrolysis) result in low pH mixtures. Thus a specific focus of this project was also
on acid-stable materials. The work involved well-defined catalyst material synthesis, comparative
kinetics, and operando spectroscopies (PI Holewinski), supported by quantum chemical
simulations (co-PI Janik), enabling progress toward efficient, acid-stable oxidation catalysis for
biomass upgrading.
Background
 Several of our early studies (motivating preliminary work, expanded through the early stages
of this project) laid a foundation in understanding oxidation pathways for organic molecules over
common acid-stable electrocatalysts. We performed extensive characterization of furfural
oxidation on polycrystalline and supported-nanoparticle Au and Pt electrodes and established
factors that determine selectivity among various reaction paths. Figure 1A illustrates critical
reaction pathways that are encountered. On more inert surfaces such as Au, oxidation of
aldehydes to carboxylic acids is selective but slow, and the resulting carboxylate species inhibit
the rate by forming bidentate R-COO** species that can further passivate surface through pi-
bonded networks. On the other hand, the more reactive Pt-group metals perform rapid
decarbonylation leading to limitation by CO* poisoning at low potentials. At higher potentials, Pt
can oxidize off the CO* but is relatively unselective amongst a number of C4 and C5 oxygenates.

Objectives
 In the above context, the goals of the project were to find mechanisms to enhance selectivity
and rate by promoting the removal of CO* at lower potentials while simultaneously avoiding the
accumulation of carboxylates (RCOO**) at higher potentials. This led to several hypotheses as
follow (and are illustrated in Figure 1B): (i) Adsorbate isotherm regulation with specifically-
adsorbing electrolyte anions could suppress flat-lying adsorption and C-C cleavage, (ii)
Bifunctional OH-donation introduced with surface site heterogeneity (either alloy structures or
metal/metal oxide interfaces) could remove strongly adsorbed species, and (iii) Reducing active
metal ensemble size toward the limit of single atoms could geometrically circumvent the
carboxylate inhibition pathway. Publication products related to these lines of study are listed and
then highlighted with details below.

Significant Results

Isotherm regulation effects
 Some highlights from focus area (i) are shown in Figures 2-3. Fig. 2A shows two high-
conversion electrolyses using a conventional nanoparticle Pt/C electrocatalyst (20% wt). At the
lower potential (0.9 V RHE), selectivity toward FA (a desirable product early in the serial pathway)
actually increases over time. The promotional mechanism was found to be product self-assembly:
a high coverage of FA stabilizes upright binding configurations through 𝜋 − 𝜋 interactions (Fig.
2C). The increase to selectivity comes with a penalty in rate, however. Upon increasing to 1.2 V
RHE these species decompose but selectivity shifts toward C4 products.
 Figure 3 shows selectivities from electrolysis using both non-adsorbing ClO4– and specifically-
adsorbing H2PO4– for adsorbate-isotherm tuning. In both cases, increasing the pH to 4 is found to
raise selectivity to FA. This was suggested in part to relate to the deprotonation of furoic acid at
elevated pH causing it to be drawn more strongly to the electrode surface and suppress furfural
binding configurations that would promote C4 products. Notably however, in the presence of
phosphate buffer (H3PO4/ NaH2PO4) selectivity toward FA is enhanced to over 70% as a result of
the adsorbed H2PO4– interfering with the ability of ring structures to lie flat on the surface and
access C-C cleavage paths (Fig. 3C). This interpretation was supported with attenuated total
reflectance surface-enhanced infrared spectroscopy (ATR-SEIRAS) as seen in Fig 3B, where the
disappearance of a CO* feature in pH 4 phosphate suggests a role for H2PO4– in suppressing a
CO*-forming pathway. Spectral assignments were aided by DFT calculations from Janik. Different
pH and ion conditions can thus lead to significantly different product selectivities via dictated
adsorption modes and subsequent reaction paths. These and related findings are elaborated in
Publications [1]-[3].

Controlling Bifunctionality for C-O coupling
 Given the prevalence of CO* poisoning as a deactivation pathway, some deeper focus was
given to understanding the nature of surfaces that are tolerant to CO. For clearer interpretability,
we used steady-state CO oxidation kinetics as a high-throughput probe to categorize the
emergence of “bifunctional” cooperative oxidation behavior between different active sites, both
within the catalyst itself (metal alloys) as well as between catalyst particles and support materials.
CO oxidation has the advantage of generating a single product (CO2), such that kinetic signatures
can inform on the predominant reaction pathway as a function of material properties. While CO
oxidation is not a fully-transferable model reaction for furanic oxidation, it reveals the nature of
site cooperativity on different groups of materials. Two resulting Publications [4]-[5] discuss novel
kinetic analyses used to attribute bifunctionality or lack thereof on metal alloys. In contrast to
popular belief in the literature, Pt-Ru alloy catalysts were found not to exhibit bifunctionality in CO
oxidation (do not function through OH-donation from Ru to CO* bound on Pt). Conversely Ag-Pd
alloys were found to exhibit bifunctionality and to facilitate the reaction through a unique partial
discharge process (fractional electrosorption valency) that we were able to identify through a new
kinetic analysis on the second-derivatives of rate as well as potential-dependent DFT calculations
(Figure 4).

 Building on the capacity to analyze CO* removal pathways from multi-site materials, we
extended the approach to metal/metal-oxide (M/MO) catalysts, which (looking forward to larger
organic molecule oxidation) could simultaneously take advantage of the single-atom dispersion
effects highlighted in Figure 1B. Pt supported on antimony-doped tin oxide (ATO) was
investigated, as the ATO support allows anchoring of isolated Pt sites, in contrast to conventional
carbon supports that bind Pt weakly and are susceptible to particle ripening.
 We built an automated reactor system (Fig. 5A) to facilitate these characterization studies
and were able to identify differences in CO-oxidation pathways on single-atoms or few-atom
clusters (low Pt weight loading) relative to larger Pt nanoparticles (high weight loading). Analysis
of high-throughput kinetic data highlighted in Fig. 5B-C showed that the isolated sites can actually
co-adsorb OH and CO, followed by rapid CO removal, but they cannot accommodate two CO’s,
pointing to enhanced poisoning tolerance. These results are also supported by calculations from
Janik and are forthcoming in Publication [6]

characteristics of different particle sizes of Pt on different supports. Figure 6A shows that
supporting Pt on ATO improves its ability (relative to Pt/C) to activate water to OH*, while moving
