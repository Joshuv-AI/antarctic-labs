Genome-wide identification and characterization of the RNAi gene families in Brassica rapa L. highlighting their regulatory components and underlying functions involving crop improvement
Authors: Zobaer Akond, Sheikh Hasna Habib, Fee Faysal Ahmed, Md Nurul Haque Mollah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBrassica rapa is an oilseed crop used for vegetable oil production, with applications in both human consumption and industrial purposes. RNAi-guided gene families play crucial roles in plant growth and development by regulating gene expression in response to various pathogens and abiotic stressors. However, these gene families in this species have not yet been studied in detail. Therefore, this research aims to explore the major RNAi genes, including their characterization and underlying functions for crop improvement.ResultsOur analysis identified 4 BrDCL, 13 BrAGO, and 6 BrRDR genes from the Brassica rapa genome. Phylogenetic analysis revealed that the identified genes were evolutionarily related to the RNAi genes in Arabidopsis. The domain, exon-intron, and motif structures of genes and proteins were very similar to those of A. thaliana. Moreover, sequence logo and relative frequency analysis showed that lysine (K), serine(S), valine (V), leucine (L), and glutamic acid (E) were highly significant (p < 0.01) amino acids in motifs. The genes were distributed across the 10 chromosomes, and segmental duplication was observed in BrAGO1 and BrAGO4. GO analysis showed that genes were involved in important biological processes such as defense response to virus (p < 0.01), post-transcriptional gene silencing (p < 0.01), etc., and molecular pathways, for example, RNA polymerase activity (p < 0.01), siRNA binding(p < 0.01), etc. The largest number of genes was associated with the nucleus, chloroplast, and cytosol. Trans-regulatory analysis showed that the top-ranked TF families accounted for 393 (78%) of 502 regulators, including Dof 115 (23%), bZIP 58(11.55%), C2H2 49(9.76%), ERF 30(6%), BBR-BPC 29(5.77%), MICK-MADS 23(4.58%), MYB, TCP, and WRKY each 23 (4.58%), with AP2 regulating 21(4.18%) TFs. Gene-TF network analysis showed that eight key TF families were highly connected to RNAi genes. The associated cis-acting elements were classified as hormone, light, and various stress-responsive. 10 key RNAi genes interacted with most of the TFs, comprising 302 TFs (~61%), among which BrAGO1a (48 TFs~10%) and BrAGO1b (40 TFs~8%) were influential. Expression analysis demonstrated that most genes showed expression in the root, seeds, flowers, leaves of seedlings, silique, and seed coats.ConclusionOverall results would provide valuable resources for improving stress-tolerant transgenic lines by initiating knockdown of negative regulators of stress responses, increasing defense-related gene expression, or generating host-induced gene-silencing molecules that target pathogens and pests. Also, by manipulating BrRNAi genes, one can significantly improve crop traits such as disease resistance, abiotic stress tolerance, and crop yield with quality in B. rapa.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12863-026-01441-7.Keywords: Brassica rapa, RNAi genes, Characterization, Regulatory elements, Expression analysis, Bioinformatics analysis

Introduction

RNA interference (RNAi) or RNA/gene silencing is an important mechanism conserved in most multicellular eukaryotic organisms. This molecular mechanism occurs by the 19–24 nt non-coding small RNA molecules (miRNA and siRNA) to regulate gene expression at the transcriptional and post-transcriptional (PTS) level by targeting complementary RNAs and protecting cells from deadly exogenous and endogenous genetic materials [1], ]. RNAi plays a significant role in plants’ various biological, molecular, and cellular processes, such as growth and development, epigenetic modifications, maintaining genomic stability, and different pathogenic and environmental stress responses [2, 3].

Dicer-like (DCL), Argonaute (AGO), and RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RDR) are the three major RNA-silencing machinery gene families [4]. RNA silencing is first triggered by the double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) or partially double-stranded stem-loop RNA that is cleaved by the activity of RNaseIII-type DICER-LIKE proteins (DCL) into small RNA (sRNA) molecules of length 21–24 nucleotide termed as micro-RNA (miRNA) or short-interfering RNA (siRNA) [1, 5]. Secondly, one strand of these miRNAs or siRNAs is connected to AGO protein to establish a pre-RNA-induced silencing complex(pre-RISC) that requires the molecular chaperone Heat shock protein 70 (Hsp70)/90(Hsp90) [1, 6]. The sRNA is merged by the function of the N-domain of AGO, and only the guide RNA strand exists in the complex to produce a complete RISC [1, 7, 8]. The RISC binds to complementary mRNAs guided by single-stranded sRNA to restrict translation during post-transcriptional gene silencing (PTGS), RNA degradation, and produce heterochromatin during transcriptional gene silencing (TGS), leading to gene silencing. Host cell-encoded RNA-dependent RNA polymerases (RDRs) produce these sRNAs from the targeted RNA to generate more dsRNAs to process miRNAs or siRNAs and start a new round of RNA silencing [2]. The proteins of these three families contain some specific domains that measure their important characteristics (structure and function). The proteins of the DCL family are identified for the presence of six domains, viz., a DEAD domain, a helicase conserved C-terminal (Helicase C) domain, a Dicer dimerization domain (Dicer dimer/Duf283), a PAZ (Piwi Argonaute and Zwille) domain, a Ribonuclease III domain (Ribonuclease 3), and a double-stranded RNA-binding domain (DSRM) [5]. The proteins of the AGO family are located in the RISC that cleaves the target mRNAs, and are characterized and identified for the presence of variable MID and N-terminal domains and well-conserved PAZ and PIWI (P-element-induced wimpy testis) domains [3, 9]. The proteins of the RDR family are vital to continue the RNA silencing process. The proteins of this gene group represent a particular conserved RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp) catalytic domain [5]

Studies of the DCL, AGO, and RDR gene families have been done on several plant species. Research showed that some varying protein members of these three groups were present among the species such as in the Arabidopsis (4DCL, 10AGO and 6RDR), rice (5DCL,19AGO and 8RDR), maize (5DCL,18AGO and 5RDR), tomato (7DCL, 15AGO and 6RDR), wheat (7DCL, 39AGO and 16RDR), soybean (7DCL, 21AGO and 7RDR), quinoa (8DCL, 21AGO and 11RDR), sunflower (5DCL, 15AGO and 10RDR), banana (3DCL, 13AGO and 5RDR), cowpea (7DCL, 14AGO and 7RDR) etc. [2, 5, 9–15]. Previous studies have provided substantial evidence that these gene families are highly conserved among plant species, preserving important structural and functional characteristics. However, large-scale analyses of these genes have not yet been done in Brassica rapa. In this study, evolutionarily, nearly the same number of RNAi genes and proteins were identified in the Brassica rapa genome as in Arabidopsis thaliana, indicating similar gene and protein structures, physicochemical properties, and identical biological and molecular functions performed by these RNAi genes in the plant genome. Arabidopsis thaliana DCL1 (AtDCL1), however, showed the properties of miRNA biogenesis, whereas AtDCL2/DCL3/DCL4 mediate siRNA processing [16]. AtDCL3/DCL4 are also important for RNA-directed DNA methylation of the FWA transgene, which is related to histone H3 lysine 9 (H3K9) methylation [2, 16]. Although AtDCL2 generates siRNAs to develop defense against viral infection and a cis-acting antisense transcript, AtDCL4 controls the vegetative period change by producing siRNAs from the trans-acting transcript [17]. Interestingly, AtDCL3 targets short dsRNAs, and AtDCL4 cuts the long dsRNA substrates [9, 16]. DCL genes in plants, such as AtDCL1 and AtDCL3, increase flowering [18]. Recently, a study identified three proteins, viz., Pt-DCL1, Pt-DCL2, and Pt-DCL3, that are strongly related to the leaf rust pathogen (Puccinia triticina), which is known as a very destructive disease of wheat [19].

Argonaute proteins also play key roles during the RNA-guided gene-silencing process for plants’ growth and development [20, 21]. For example, the AtAGO1 is connected to the transgene-silencing pathways [22] and the AtAGO4 to the epigenetic silencing [23]. The AtAGO7 and AtAGO10 proteins control growth [24] and meristem functions in plant cells [25]. Recently, a study showed that some AGO genes (TaAGO2a/2b) of wheat are predicted to play a role in wheat growth and development, specifically in grain quality improvement [9]. Two AGO genes (TaAGO1b/TaAGO4) identified from wheat are predicted to play a key role in the vegetative and reproductive phase in cold environments at the vernalizing stage [26]. RDR proteins in plants (Arabidopsis and maize) are also engaged in RNAi-related processes, viz., co-suppression, protection of pathogen infection, chromatin modification, and post-transcriptional gene silencing actions [27–29].

Brassica rapa, known as field mustard, is a potential oilseed crop for vegetable oil and is used for human consumption and industrial purposes. It is also used as fodder and biodiesel; young leaves are used as human food and green vegetables. Canola oil is popular for its low erucic acid and glucosinolate content in its meal protein derived from the Brassica rapa [30]. Brassica rapa (B. rapa) is a diploid species (A genome; n=10) that may have been domesticated about 3000 years ago and has experienced a whole-genome triplication since it deviated from Arabidopsis [31–33]. B. rapa shows deep morphological diversity, viz., leafy vegetables, turnips, and oil types, which may be due to genetic and epigenetic differences [32]. The genome sequencing of B. rapa has now created scope for large-scale investigations of the sequenced genes. A comprehensive bioinformatics analysis of the physicochemical properties, gene and protein structures, and their roles in key biological and molecular pathways—as well as their functions at the cellular organelle level—of RNAi genes in B. rapa remains to be fully explored for sustainable crop improvement. In particular, large-scale studies investigating the regulatory components of RNAi genes in Brassica rapa are lacking, which limits our ability to identify stress-associated genes, proteins, and regulators. High-throughput approaches are crucial for developing more stress-tolerant transgenic lines with enhanced nutritional quality. The genomic findings from this study will provide researchers with a broad foundation for conducting advanced molecular experiments, such as functional profiling, validation studies, and targeted RNAi gene manipulations—including gene knockdown or knockout using CRISPR/Cas9—for the development of oilseed varieties with improved resistance to biotic and abiotic stresses and superior agricultural traits. Materials and methods

The data sources of DCL, AGO, and RDR gene families in the A. thaliana and B. rapa genomes

First, the protein sequences of DCL, AGO, and RDR gene families of Arabidopsis thaliana were taken from the TAIR database (http://www.arabidopsis.org/). Next, following the HMMER method, the conserved domains of the 4AtDCL, 10AtAGO, and 6AtRDR proteins were searched using the EnsemblePlants database. Then the peptide sequences of this species were used in BLASTP 2.11.0+ [34] search in the Phytozome database (https://phytozome-next.jgi.doe.gov/blast-search) to predict the RNAi-related genes against the Brassica rapa genome (Phytozome genome ID: 277). Cross-checking was also performed in the NCBI (NCBI taxonomy ID: 3711) for additional confirmation of the genes/proteins. The specific bioinformatics parameters used in the BLASTP process are: E-value = 0.05, Matrix = BLOSUM62. BLOSUM62 (Blocks Substitution Matrix) is the default scoring matrix used in the Protein BLAST (BLASTP) process to align and compare protein sequences. It is a 20 × 20 (or 23 × 23) matrix where each cell represents a log-odds score for a particular amino acid substitution. This matrix helps determine the similarity between query and database sequences. All collected protein sequences were again explored for conserved domains from EnsemblePlants. Finally, the 23 RNAi-related genes (4 BrDCL, 13 BrAGO, and 6BrRDR) were predicted according to the percentage of identity (>90.0%), maximum score, and the number of conserved domain compositions similar to A. thaliana, and the redundant sequences were omitted. Some genomic info about these genes, including accession number, genomic location, genome, CDS, and encoded protein lengths, was recorded in Table 1. Then, all newly identified genes from the B. rapa genome were designated as BrDCL, BrAGO, and BrRDR gene families using taxonomy based on the phylogenetic relationship of the corresponding family members of the Arabidopsis thaliana genes. Prediction of the theoretical pI (isoelectric point) and Mw (molecular weight) of each gene was also recorded from the ExPASyComputepI/Mwtool (http://au.expasy.org/tools/pitool.html).

Multiple sequence alignments and phylogenetic tree construction

The protein sequences of DCL, AGO, and RDR genes from the Arabidopsis thaliana and Brassica rapa genomes were used for multiple sequence alignment using the ClustalW method in MEGA 11.0 [35]. To observe the evolutionary closeness of B. rapa DCL, AGO, and RDR proteins with those of their Arabidopsis counterparts, a phylogenetic tree was constructed using MEGA 11.0, following the Neighbor-Joining (NJ) method. The phylogeny test was performed using the bootstrap method with 1000 replications to measure statistical support for nodes [36] and the evolutionary distances were computed using the p-distance substitution method with complete deletion of the gaps/missing data [35]. The candidate genes were given new names under the group names BrDCL, BrAGO, and BrRDR in line with the phylogenetic resemblance and sequence homologies of parallel A. thaliana homologs.

Conserved functional domains and catalytic residues of the protein families

The conserved functional domain analysis was carried out from the EnsemblePlants database following the profile Hidden Markov Model (HMMER) method to check if the domain composition of the predicted RNAi proteins in B. rapa is consistent with their Arabidopsis orthologs. Here, HMMER is a powerful software tool that uses probabilistic models to predict protein and DNA sequence domain families from sequence databases. HMMER actually underlies many comprehensive collections of alignments and profiles of known protein and DNA sequence domain families, including the Pfam database. HMMER also uses ensemble algorithms that consider all possible alignments, weighted by their relative likelihood. Six functional domains, namely DEXDc, HELICc, DUF283, PAZ, RIBOc, and DSRM, were considered for identifying gene members of the BrDCL family. The gene elements of the BrAGO group possessing DUF1785, PAZ, and Piwi functional domains are termed AGO gene members, which are also consistent with the AtAGOs. Gene members of the BrRDR family were also identified for two common RdRP and RRM functional domains for protein structure. Additionally, to identify the two most potentially conserved metal-chelating catalytic triad regions or residues (DDH/H and DLDGD) in the PIWI and RdRp domains, the BrAGO and BrRDR protein families were considered. The alignment profiles were performed using the ClustalW program in MEGA and GENEDOC v2.6.002 software [37].

Conserved motif structure

Multiple Expectation Maximization for Motif Elicitation (MEME) [38] was used to predict the conserved motifs of the 4BrDCL, 13BrAGO, and 6BrRDR proteins by keeping the parameter settings (i) optimum motif width of ≥ 6 and ≤50, (ii) maximum number of motifs of 20, (iii) minimum sites per motif is 2 and maximum sites per motif is 8, and motif site distribution is ZOOPS. Any Motifs that did not match the structural domains in each protein family were rejected.

Three-dimensional protein structure and nonsynonymous (Ka)/synonymous (Ks) ratio

The three-dimensional (3D) protein structure analysis was also performed using SWISS-MODEL [39] for the BrDCLs, BrAGOs, and BrRDRs. The best protein model was selected based on the global model quality estimation (GMQE) score and maximum sequence identity (% coverage) index. Additionally, the synonymous substitutions/mutations (Ks), nonsynonymous substitutions/mutations (Ka), and the nonsynonymous/synonymous mutation ratio (Ka/Ks) were calculated using MEGA11. The results were subsequently visualized with TBtools v2.136 [40]. When the ratio Ka/Ks = 1, it was predicted for neutral selection. If Ka/Ks > 1, it was considered for positive selection (adaptive evolution). Conversely, if Ka/Ks < 1, it was suggested for purifying/negative (conserved) selection.

Gene structure and chromosomal mapping

The prediction of the gene(exon-intron) structure of the 4 BrDCL, 13 BrAGO, and 6 BrRDR genes was analyzed using Gene Structure Display Server GSDS 2.0 [41] by taking their respective CDS and DNA sequences. Additionally, the genomic locations and duplication events of the genes in B. rapa were studied using the MapGene2Chromosome (MG2C) V2 [42]. The parameters used in MG2C are SVG container, single chromosome (chr) container, chromosome ID, chromosome, gene lines, gene ID, connection, and scale.

GO analysis and prediction of the sub-cellular localization

Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was performed using PlantTFDB v4.0 [43] and QuickGO [44] to explore the involvement of RNAi genes in B. rapa across different biological processes (BPs), molecular functions (MFs), and cellular components (CCs). The nine key bioinformatics parameters and filtering options used in QuickGO, a web-based tool, include: Protein/Gene Identifiers (ID), Evidence codes, Qualifier data, Taxonomic data (QuickGO uses the UniProtKB taxonomic hierarchy), GO terms/aspect, GO Slims, Targeted Search, Reference type, and Advanced querying (Boolean operators such as AND, NOT, OR). Fisher’s exact test was applied to determine p-values for the overrepresentation of functional categories in GO enrichment analysis. To reduce the false positive rates, p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg test procedure. Enrichment outcomes with adjusted p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. To predict the cellular locations of the genes, a web-based tool, WoLF PSORT [43] was used. The protein sequences of the corresponding BrRNAi genes were used to predict the cellular locations. Next, the results were visualized by constructing a heatmap using TB tools [40].

Trans-regulatory elements and gene networking

The PlantTFDB database was used to predict the RNAi-associated transcription factors (TFs) in B. rapa. We used HMMER’s hmmsearch to search against the protein sequences of B. rapa to predict TFs. We used an E-value cutoff of 0.01, which is widely adopted for HMMER searches. However, to extract the TF list of the 4 BrDCL, 13 BrAGO, and 6BrRDR genes from Plant Transcriptional Regulatory Map (PlantRegMap) (http://plantregmap.gao-lab.org/tf_enrichment.php), the gene symbols of the corresponding BrRNAi proteins were first collected from the NCBI database following the BLASTp process. Then, the gene symbol lists for BrDCL, BrAGO, and BrRDR were submitted separately to predict the TFs that show significantly over-represented targets in the input genes. To elucidate the plant transcriptional regulatory system and its association with predicted RNAi proteins, a Gene-TF network was constructed between the identified transcription factors (TFs) and RNAi-related proteins in B. rapa using Cytoscape 3.10.0 [44]. And the network layout was manually adjusted to improve visualization quality.

Prediction of the cis-regulatory elements and in silico expression analysis of the genes

Cis-regulatory or cis-acting components are an important portion of a gene located in the promoter region to regulate gene activity with the help of a specific transcription factor (TF). For a comprehensive analysis of the RNAi genes in Brassica rapa species, the cis-acting regulatory components in the promoter region were collected and analyzed using a web-based prediction bioinformatics tool, PlantCARE [44]. The extracted bundle of cis-acting components was classified into three groups based on their association with many activities, such as hormone-responsive, light-responsive, and stress-responsive. The results were visualized by TBtools v2.136. The DNA sequences of each identified gene corresponding to the gene group BrDCL, BrAGO, and BrRDR were used to extract expressed sequence tag (EST) information from PlantGDB (http://www.plantgdb.org) for various tissues/organs of B. rapa. The default parameter with e-value = 1e-04 was used for the BLASTN search, and the results were visualized using TBtools v2.136.

Results and discussion

Genome-wide identification and characterization of the RNAi-related genes in the B. rapa genome

We have finally selected a total of 23 genes, including 4 BrDCL, 13 BrAGO, and 6 BrRDR, from the B. rapa genome based on high-scoring aligned sequences for subsequent analyses (Table 1). Four DCL genes in B. rapa were identified by the presence of six domains, including DExD-helicase-C, helicase-C, Dicer-dimmer/Duf283, PAZ, RNase III (Ribonuclease-3), and a double-stranded RNA-binding motif (dsRB/DSRM), which are also characteristic of AtDCLs. Domains search of these 4 BrDCL from the three databases such as Pfam, NCBI-CDD, and SMART analysis revealed that almost all BrDCL protein families contained these domains, which are known as the full characteristics of plant DCL protein structure from the DCL family (class 3 RNase III family) (Additional file 1). Domain identification using SMART analysis revealed that the Dicer-dimer (DUF283) domain is absent; however, Pfam and NCBI-CDD database analyses indicated its presence (Additional file 1). We observed that BrDCL1 and BrDCL4 possess a second DSRM domain, as noted in the SMART and NCBI-CDD databases (Additional file 1). The SMART analysis revealed that BrDCL2 possesses a transmembrane region preceding the DExDc domain. The DNA length of the BrDCL proteins, however, varied from 9764 bp for BrDCL4 to 6688 bp for BrDCL3, with their corresponding protein-coding lengths of 1698 aa and 1517 aa (Table 1).

Based on the characteristics of two domains (PAZ and PIWI), 13 BrAGOs were identified (Table 1). A comparative study using Pfam, SMART, and NCBI-CDD also showed that all putative BrAGO protein elements tend to contain an N-terminus PAZ domain and a C-terminus PIWI superfamily domain (Additional file 1). The length of the BrAGO protein families varied from 6,529 bp for BrAGO1b to 3,538 bp for BrAGO3, with their corresponding coding lengths of 1,111 aa and 1,009 aa (Table 1). The third most important RNAi-related protein is the RDR protein. The 6 BrRDRs were identified for the presence of the key RdRp domain. The genomic length of the BrRDR proteins varied from 5,089 bp for BrRDR3 to 3,654 bp for BrRDR1, with their corresponding peptide lengths of 999 aa and 1,109 aa (Table 1). The pI values in the table implied that all putative BrDCL genes hold acidic (5.93~6.77) characteristics. On the other hand, the predicted BrAGOs (8.82~9.61) and BrRDRs (6.44~9.42) possess higher basic characteristics according to their pI values (Table 1).

Classification and phylogenetic analysis of the RNAi genes in B. rapa

Phylogenetic analysis was conducted for all 23 BrRNAi proteins alongside 10 AtRNAi proteins to investigate the evolutionary relationships and functional diversity among 4 BrDCL, 13 BrAGO, and 6 BrRDR genes. The BrDCL proteins exhibited high sequence similarity to their Arabidopsis counterparts, with the four BrDCLs clustering into four distinct groups, mirroring the pattern observed for AtDCLs. Each cluster contained a BrDCL gene closely grouped with its AtDCL ortholog (Fig. 2A). Based on their phylogenetic relationships and sequence homology with AtDCLs, the BrDCLs were designated BrDCL1, BrDCL2, BrDCL3a, and BrDCL4.

For the 13 BrAGO genes, phylogenetic analysis divided them into nine subclasses (Fig. 2B), with class names reflecting their identity to AtAGOs. Several BrAGO subgroups, including BrAGO1, BrAGO4, and BrAGO8/9, each contained two members (e.g., BrAGO1a and BrAGO1b; BrAGO4a and BrAGO4b; BrAGO9a and BrAGO9b), while BrAGO2, BrAGO3, BrAGO5, BrAGO6, BrAGO7, and BrAGO8 had no sub-members. The six BrRDRs (BrRDR1, BrRDR2, BrRDR3, BrRDR4, BrRDR5, and BrRDR6) were also named after their AtRDR orthologs, displaying close evolutionary relationships on the phylogenetic tree and exhibiting the highest levels of protein sequence homology (Fig. 2C).

Domain composition and catalytic residue analysis

The physicochemical characteristics and domain composition of the B. rapa DCL, AGO, and RDR proteins were nearly identical to those of their Arabidopsis counterparts. The B. rapa genome contained the same number of DCL and RDR gene members as A. thaliana, except that the BrAGO gene group had three more AGOs than AtAGO (Table 1). Four DCL genes were selected as BrDCL from the Phytozome for six common conserved domains, such as DExDc (DEAD-like helicases superfamily), HELICc (helicase superfamily C-terminal domain), PAZ (Piwi, Argonaute, Zwille), RIBOc (Ribonuclease III family), and DSRM (Double-stranded RNA binding motif) corresponding to A. thaliana DCL genes. While all members of the BrDCL family contain the six domains, BrDCL3 lacks the C-terminal DSRM domain in SMART analysis (Fig. 3 and Additional file 1). The four BrDCLs showed a molecular weight of 157–208 kDa.

Conserved domains of BrDCL, BrAGO, and BrRDR protein families. Domains are indicated in various colour boxes with the corresponding name inside the box

AGO proteins commonly comprise four domains: DUF1785 (N-terminal function-unknown domain), PAZ, MID, and C-terminal PIWI domains. Based on HMMER analysis of PAZ and PIWI domains from the predicted peptide sequence, 13 BrAGOs were selected. Protein domain prediction by Pfam, SMART, and NCBI-CDD revealed that all BrAGOs shared an N-terminus PAZ domain and a C-terminus PIWI domain, vital characteristics of plant Argonaute proteins. SMART analysis showed that 13 BrAGOs contained an N-terminal DUF1785 domain before each PAZ domain (Fig. 3 and Table 2). In addition to these two important domains, BrAGOs possessed other functional domains similar to those of A. thaliana, including ArgoN, ArgoL1, Gly-rich_Ago1, DUF1785, ArgoL2, and ArgoMid (Additional file 1). The BrAGOs, however, displayed a molecular weight of 97.21 ~ 122.88 kDa. The 6 BrRDR proteins possess a unique domain named RdRP, which is the key characteristic of this protein family. However, SMART and NCBI-CDD analyses revealed another domain, RRM (Additional file 1). The molecular weight of these proteins varies from 105.18 to 137.07 kDa.

Three-dimensional (3D) protein structure and evolutionary Ka/Ks ratio analysis

The predicted 3D structural analysis indicated that all BrDCL and BrRDR proteins exhibited nearly identical structures; however, some differences were observed in BrAGO (Additional files 7A–C and 8). The average alpha-helix and β-folding for BrDCL proteins were 22 and 16, respectively. The BrAGOs have an average of 11 and 14 alpha helices and beta sheets, respectively. The highest alpha-helices 13 were found in BrAGO9a, followed by 12 in BrAGO1a/4b/9/10, and the highest β-sheets 19 were found in BrAGO1b, followed by 18 in BrAGO1a and 17 in BrAGO4b (Additional file 8). The highest α-helices 20 belonged to BrRDR3, followed by 18 in BrRDR2 and 17 in BrRDR4/6, and the highest β-folding 23 was obtained by BrRDR6, followed by BrRDR1.

The ratio (Ka/Ks) calculation results were (<1) for all gene pairs of BrDCL, BrAGO, and BrRDR groups, indicating that there were more synonymous changes than nonsynonymous changes. Additionally, the duplication and time of divergence for the BrDCLs, BrAGOs, and BrRDRs genes were measured in million years ago (MYA) using the formula T = Ks/2x, where ×was equal to 6.56 × 10−9 and MYA = 10−6 (Additional file 9).

In Brassica rapa, gene duplications generate several homologs that, through processes of loss, retention, and divergence at the coding, regulatory, and epigenetic levels, give rise to new or partitioned functions. These changes might have fueled the metabolic, developmental, and adaptive diversity observed in B. rapa. Gene duplications—especially the recent Whole Genome Triplication (WGT) event—have played a pivotal role in driving the functional diversity, morphological variation, and environmental adaptation of the Brassica rapa genome. After divergence from Arabidopsis thaliana, this triplication event (which may have occurred between 13 and 17 million years ago) created extensive genetic redundancy, enabling duplicated genes (homologs) to evolve distinct patterns of expression, regulation, and function.

Analysis of trans-regulatory elements and gene networking

To predict the TF for the identified gene, we used the corresponding gene symbols extracted from the B. rapa genome from the NCBI database. In this work, 502 trans factors were finally predicted for the 23 B. rapa RNAi genes. The predicted TFs were classified into 31 groups/families (Additional file 13). The top-ranked 10 TF families (Dof, bZIP, C2H2, ERF, BBR-BPC, MICK-MADS, MYB, TCP, WRKY and AP2) comprise 393 (78%) regulators, of which Dof 115(23%), bZIP 58(11.55%), C2H2 49(9.76%), ERF 30(6%), BBR-BPC 29(5.77%), MICK-MADS 23(4.58%), MYB, TCP, and WRKY each 23 (4.58%), and AP2 regulate 21(4.18%) TFs.

A comprehensive relationship between the predicted RNAi genes of B. rapa and associated TFs was calculated using a gene-networking technique. To construct the whole network, the open-source software Cystoscope 3.10.0 was used. The network produced 236 nodes and 502 edges between the genes and TFs. Based on the ‘Degree’ node criteria, the relationship was established. The higher the degree, the stronger the connection of a particular RNAi gene with the TFs. The network analysis showed that the Dof TF family was found in the highest number in BrRNAi genes except for BrAGO1b, BrRDR2, and BrRDR5 (Additional file 13 and Fig. 9). Among the 4BrDCL genes, BrDCL4 was linked to the highest number of Dof TF family. Among 13 BrAGO genes, BrAGO3/4a/4b/8/9b/10 were connected with a higher number of Dof. Six Dof TFs were associated with the BrRDR6. Interestingly, no bZIP TF was associated with the 4 BrDCLs. This TF family was found in higher numbers in BrAGO1a/1/1b/4a and BrRDR1. The C2H2 family was associated with almost all RNAi genes of B. rapa except BrAGO4a/4b/5/9a and BrRDR4. The ERF family was associated with a specific number of RNAi genes (BrDCL1/2, BrAGO5/9a/10, and BrRDR6). The analysis showed that BrAGO2/5/6/9a and BrRDR1/3/6 were not connected with any BBR-BPC TF (Additional file 13 and Fig. 9). There was a lower link between the MIKC_MADS TF and all RNAi genes of B. rapa, except for BRDCL2, BrAGO2/8, and BrRDR1/4/6. The MYB TF was found to have the highest number in BrRDR5, followed by BrAGO8/4/4b/3 and BrDCL3/2. The TCP was associated with only three RNAi genes (BrAGO5, BrAGO8, and BrRDR1). The important TF family AP2 was also connected with almost all RNAi genes except BrDCL2, BrAGO6, BrRDR1, and BrRDR6. Analysis of cis-regulatory components of BrDCL, BrAGO, and BrRDR gene families

Cis-regulatory element (CRE) analysis was done to find their functional involvement and divergence for BrDCLs, BrAGOs, and BrRDRs using the web-based PlantCARE database. It was observed from Fig. 10B that 18, 24, and 16 cis-regulatory sequences/motifs were diversely possessed by most of the 4 BrDCL, 13 BrAGO, and 6 BrRDR gene members, respectively, in the promoter regions that were predicted to be involved in light response (LR) activities in B. rapa. Among the LR CREs, 18 CREs (G-Box, GATA-motif, GT1-motif, AE-box, 3-AF1 binding site, TCT-motif, Box-4, TCCC-motif, chs-CMA2a, ACE, ATC-motif, MRE, GA motif, ATCT-motif, AAAC-motif, I-box, Gap-box, LAMP-element) were common among 4 BrDCL and 13 BrAGO gene members, and 13 CREs (G-Box, GATA-motif, GT1-motif, AE-box, TCT-motif, Box 4, TCCC-motif, chs-CMA2a, ACE, MRE, ATCT-motif, AAAC-motif, I-box) were common among 4 BrDCL, 13 BrAGO, and 6 BrRDR gene members (Fig. 10B and Additional files 11–13). A study on the U’s triangle Brassica species also identified almost the same CREs [52]. It was, however, noticeable that among all the predicted LR CREs, G-box was carried by all members of DCL, AGO, and RDR gene families, followed by GATA-motif and TCT-motif in 16 BrRNAi genes (Fig. 10B). Though the AE-box was present in all BrDCLs, it was absent from BrGAO1/3/5/6/7 and BrRDR2/4/5.

The TC-rich repeats (involved in defense and stress responsiveness), MBS (MYB binding site involved in drought inducibility), and LTR elements (involved in low-temperature responsiveness), ARE (cis-acting regulatory element essential for the anaerobic induction for waterlogging), GC-motif (enhancer-like element involved in anoxic-specific inducibility plays a role for heat tolerance) were commonly found as various stress-responsive CREs among the RNAi gene families in B.rapa (Fig. 10C and Additional files 14–16).

Out of these, CGTCA-motif and TGACG-motif (involved in the MeJA-responsiveness), CAT-box (related to meristem expression), ABRE (ABA-responsive cis-acting element involved in the abscisic acid responsiveness) [52]. The O2-site (involved in regulating zein metabolism), the TGA-element (an auxin-responsive element), and the ABRE function are involved in responses to several adverse environmental situations [52]. TCA-element (involved in salicylic acid responsiveness), GARE-motif (gibberellin-responsive element), and P-box (gibberellin-responsive element) [52] are possessed by the DCL, AGO, and RDR gene elements in B. rapa for specific gene expression. These CREs, however, are retained by the three BrDCL, BrAGO, and BrRDR protein families, which are termed phytohormone CREs.

Discussion

Oilseed crops are an important source of edible oil. However, these crops often face various stresses that result in greater loss of oilseed production. RNA interference, known as gene silencing in eukaryotes, is a key mechanism controlled by three gene families: Dicer-like, Argonaute, and RNA-dependent RNA polymerase [10, 53]. They regulate gene expression at the transcriptional or post-transcriptional level, playing a crucial role in plant growth and development in response to diseases and abiotic stresses [9, 12]. This mechanism is predicted to be maintained by 19–24 nt non-coding small RNA (ncRNA) molecules.

Similar to other plants, in B. rapa, RNAi gene families (DCL, AGO, and RDR) are predicted to play crucial roles in regulating gene expression in response to various stresses, thereby supporting healthy and timely crop growth and development [2, 15]. Genome-wide identification and broad-scale characterization of the major set of RNAi genes/proteins have not yet been completed to uncover their structures, functions, and the underlying regulatory components in B. rapa.

Our comprehensive study identified 4 DCL, 13 AGO, and 6 RDR genes in Brassica rapa. The evolutionary conservation of BrRNAi proteins closely resembled that of AtRNAi proteins, consistent with patterns observed in other plant species [13, 54, 55]. The domain structure of each protein type is crucial for its precise function, and the identified proteins exhibited well-conserved domain compositions, similar to AtRNAi proteins. Comparative analysis across three databases confirmed the presence of key domains, corroborated by previous research [9].

DCL proteins play a vital role in fragmenting double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) to produce microRNAs (miRNAs), thereby initiating the RNAi cycle. Multiple studies have demonstrated that plant DCL genes are well characterized by the presence of six specific domains: DEXDc, HELICc, DUF283, PAZ, RIBOc, and DSRM [3, 12, 55]. The model dicot plant Arabidopsis possesses four DCL genes that generate small RNAs (sRNAs) [56, 57]. In B. rapa, BrDCL1 and BrDCL4 are likely responsible for generating sRNAs of 21 to 24 nucleotides from dsRNAs. AtDCL1 and AtDCL4 are essential for producing 21 nt sRNAs, which function as miRNAs and siRNAs [57], while AtDCL3 generates 24 nt sRNAs involved in de novo DNA methylation, gene silencing, and chromatin modification [58]. DCL genes have also been shown to play a central role in plant viral defense mechanisms [59]. DCL4 is a key producer of 21 nt siRNAs associated with antiviral responses [60, 61], while DCL4 and DCL2 both exhibit functional properties against viral infections. Additionally, several studies indicate that DCL3 genes contribute to antiviral defense by generating abundant 24-nt siRNAs in response to invading viruses [60–62].

In A. thaliana, the DCL3 gene also directs cleavage of positive-sense RNA viruses such as Cucumber mosaic virus (cucumovirus), Oilseed rape mosaic virus (tobamovirus), and Turnip crinkle virus (carmovirus) [17, 45]. Bozorov et al. [63] demonstrated that Nicotiana attenuata DCL influences Manduca sexta larval performance, with DCL3 and DCL4 involved in regulating genes associated with signaling and defense metabolite activation against herbivore attack [60]. Recent research on cotton DCL genes further indicated that DCL3 contributes to viral defense [60]. AGO proteins are central components of RNAi effector complexes, playing primary roles in RNA silencing [2]. AGO family members are predicted to significantly contribute to RNA-directed gene suppression and are actively involved in developmental processes across various organs and tissues in Brassica rapa, as seen in other plants [21, 26, 64, 65].

RDR genes reinforce RNAi silencing signals by generating additional aberrant RNA molecules [66]. In A. thaliana, RDR genes are implicated in diverse biological functions, including viral defense, chromatin modification, and post-transcriptional gene silencing (PTGS) [10]. Among the AtRDRs, AtRDR1 is induced by salicylic acid (SA) or viral infection, functioning in antiviral defense mechanisms in several plant species [2, 67]. AtRDR2 plays a crucial role in RNA-directed DNA methylation and repressive chromatin modifications on specific transgenes, endogenous genes, and centromeric repeats, which are linked to the generation of 24-nt interfering RNAs [2, 68, 69]. AtRDR6 amplifies aberrant RNAs derived from transgenes or inverted repeats to initiate degradation of complementary RNA species [70].

The presence and replacement of a particular amino acid in the DDH/H residue in the PIWI domain were predicted to play a significant role in plant growth, development, and biotic and abiotic responses in B. rapa. The active position of one PIWI domain responsible for RNA cleavage generally conveys a well-preserved metal-chelating Asp–Asp – His (DDH) motif [10, 54]. This catalytic triad was originally discovered in Arabidopsis AGO1, and a preserved histidine at location 798 (H798) was also found to be crucial for AGO1 for in vitro endonuclease action [2, 55]. Histidine(H) is one of the key amino acids that plays a significant role in plant growth and development [56]. More physiological studies found that H provides unique functions in plants as chelators and transporters of metal ions [57]. Earlier investigations showed that the higher presence of D in the proteins implied that it plays crucial role to keep the nutritional characteristics in maize like other plants especially in seeds/grains since it acts to synthesize of four vital amino acids (aa) such as lysine/Lys (K), threonine/Thr (T), methionine/Met (M), and isoleucine/Ile (I) [58–60]. The aa R was found in the residues of four proteins and was predicted to provide important functions in plants during various physiological and biochemical activities, growth, and development, and it may also help adapt to different biotic and abiotic issues [61]. Serine (S) is an important amino acid in plants that plays a role in metabolism and various signalling pathways, for example, the phosphorylated pathway, which helps remove defects in embryos, male gametophytes, and roots [62]. Additionally, the serine(S) rich proteins play a pivotal role in plant responses to both biotic and abiotic stresses [45]. This substitution may be predicted to play important roles in B. rapa, as in other plants, in fighting against various stressors, including metal chelators, antioxidative defense, and signalling molecules [63, 64]. Thus, the amino acid alterations/mutations at the catalytic residue level in BrAGO and BrRDR proteins suggest that these proteins play vital roles in metabolism, growth, and development, as well as in responses to various stresses in B. rapa.

A motif is a recurring sequence pattern found in related protein sequences. These motifs, often referred to as small active sites of an enzyme, are essential for precise protein folding and molecular evolution [9]. Motif analysis is crucial for understanding the similarities and differences among related proteins within an organism, as it helps elucidate their regulatory roles in gene expression. The observation of nearly identical motif characteristics indicates that BrRNAi proteins may perform functions similar to those of AtRNAi proteins. To gain deeper insights into their structural evolution and functional similarities, the gene and exon-intron structures of BrDCLs, BrAGOs, and BrRDRs were examined. This analysis revealed that BrRNAi and AtRNAi genes share similar structural patterns, consistent with trends observed in other plant species [10, 13, 15].

The alpha helix (α-helix) and the beta-sheet(β-sheet)/beta-strand are the two main motifs of the secondary/3D structures of proteins and are predictable from protein sequence [65, 66]. An alpha-helix structure may be required for protein sequences to perform various functions, and a β-strand is a 3 to 10-amino acid-long polypeptide chain [67]. It can be inferred that they were well-preserved and continued to perform the same functions. The predicted results showed that the RNAi-related proteins in B. rapa owned α-helix and β-folding, displayed a hybrid protein structure, and formed the proper place to complete activities for synthesizing the specific binding pocket, and these pockets anchor the characteristics of two-nucleotide 3’ overhang that results from the breakdown of RNAs by RNase III for creating miRNA/siRNAs [8]. A previous study presented that the AGO proteins of 32 plants owned varying numbers of alpha-helices and β-sheets, which suggested a hybrid protein structure [8]. A recent study also conveyed the same results [9]. The synonymous substitution/mutation rates (Ks), nonsynonymous substitution/mutation rates (Ka) of DNA base pairs, and the ratio Ka/Ks were estimated to determine the rates of molecular evolution for each pair of paralogous genes, providing insights into the selective pressures acting on these duplicated sequences [9, 68]. Gene duplication (tandem/segmental) is characterised by continuously distributed homologous genes (BLAST e-value < 1e-20) and is not interrupted by non-homologous sequences [69]. Evolutionarily, the RNAi genes exhibited some segmental and tandem duplication actions, leading to the expansion of these genes in wheat, maize, Sorghum bicolour, and rice [2, 9, 10, 70].

GO analysis suggested that the BrRNAi genes were involved in a range of important biological and molecular pathways, including response to virus, immune effector process, post-transcriptional gene silencing, siRNA binding, RNA polymerase and ribonuclease activities. Among the BPs, the response to the virus (GO:0009615, p-value: 1.00E-30), defence response to the virus (GO:0051607, p-value: 1.00E-30), immune effector process (GO:0002252, p-value: 1.00E-30) and posttranscriptional gene silencing (GO:0016441, GO:0016441) were connected to as many as 8 BrAGO, 4 BrDCL and 2 BrRDR genes and the association of these BPs were also suggested by the previous studies (Mourrain et al., 2000b; Deleris et al., 2006b; Margis et al., 2006b; Faysal et al., 2021; Mosharaf et al., 2021a; Naim et al., 2024). Our results, therefore, suggested that in B. rapa, several RNAi genes were associated with numerous important biological processes and molecular actions. Some polymerase activity and double-stranded RNA-specific ribonuclease activity were controlled by the 5BrAGO and 4BrDCL genes, similar to AtRNAi [2, 9]. Determination of the cellular compartment (CC) of the genes would help to comprehend their functions at different cellular levels [71, 72]. The reduction of specific mRNA to decrease certain gene expression in plants typically occurs in cytoplasmic cellular locations, implying that the identified RNAi genes/proteins are intimately involved in the PTGS. [73]. Cytosol is one of the vital CC in plants where the highest level of metabolism occurs [74]. From the analysis, we observed that most of the proteins were also located in this component. Hence, it can be assumed that the predicted genes in B. rapa located in cytoplasmic organelles are responsible for performing various chemical actions and energy transformations associated with rapeseed plant growth and development. [9]. The genes placed in mitochondria might have the role of engagement in developmental stages and different stress-responsive pathways [75]. The CC chloroplast is key in oxygenic photosynthesis, generating energy and initial metabolism in plants. The chloroplast in plants is the most vital organ that plays a crucial role as an energy source, and the leaves remain green due to the presence of this organ. The chloroplast also has a vast role in regulating plant responses to abiotic and biotic stress conditions [76].

A transcription factor (TF), also called the trans-acting element, combined with the cis-acting element in the promoter region of a gene, regulates gene expression. The DNA-binding one finger (Dof) is a plant-specific TF gene family found in green algae to higher plants, which showed bifunctional binding characteristics with DNA and proteins to control transcriptional machinery in plant cells [77, 78]. Dof is involved in regulating genes related to seed maturation and germination, phytohormone and light-mediated regulation, and plant tolerance to biotic and abiotic stresses [77–79]. This TF is predicted to act as a molecular switch in response to environmental challenges by regulating signaling pathways for abscisic acid (ABA), jasmonates (JA), and other hormones. Overexpression of specific Dof genes enhances resistance to drought, cold, and salt stress, as seen in MdDof54 in apple and various StDof genes in potatoes [80]. The TFs belonging to this family regulate plant growth from germination to maturity, including root growth, hypocotyl elongation, leaf development, and vascular tissue development. Dofs participate in regulating carbon and nitrogen metabolism, photosynthesis, and flavonoid biosynthesis. It also has links to flower development and maturation, including in floral transition, flower organ development, and fruit ripening.

The Basic leucine zipper (bZIP) transcription factors play a role in gene regulation for development and resistance response to various environmental stressors [81]. TFs of this family regulate plant adaptation to salt, cold, and drought stress by controlling genes responsible for ROS scavenging, proline accumulation, and membrane stability. For example, CibZIP43 improves salt and heat tolerance in Arabidopsis [82].

Many bZIPs, specifically those in the A and S subgroups, are heavily involved in ABA-dependent signal transduction in response to drought.

C2H2, a zinc finger-type protein, is also an influential TF family with finger-like structures and can bind Zn2+ [83]. This TF plays a vital role in plant growth, development, and stress signal transduction [84]. A study showed that the genes of this TF family play a critical role in salt, osmotic, drought, cold, oxidative, and high-light stress [83]. Some stress-associated plant hormones, such as abscisic acid (ABA), salicylic acid (SA), jasmonic acid (JA), and ethylene (ET), play a crucial role in many environmental stressors (pathogens and abiotic) mediated by C2H2 proteins [83]. The TF family ERF (ethylene response factor) is one of the largest subfamilies of the APETALA2/ERF family. It includes ethylene signalling and the response pathways in plants, and was characterized by a single AP2 domain [85]. This TF family also responds to plant hormones, improving plant survival during stress conditions. For example, several AP2/ERF families react to the plant hormones abscisic acid (ABA) and ethylene (ET), which help stimulate ABA and ET-dependent and independent stress-responsive genes [86, 87]. An experimental investigation showed that an ethylene response factor (SlERF5/ERF5) helps to increase adaptation to drought and salt tolerance in tomatoes [88].

Barley B recombinant-basic Penta-Cysteine (BBR/BPC) TFs are conserved plant-specific regulators that manage development and stress response by binding to GAGA-motif cis-elements and recruiting Polycomb Repressive Complexes (PRC1/PRC2) to repress target genes [89]. They regulate homeotic genes, leaf development, and plant immunity/stress responses to salinity, drought, and cold [89]. BBR/BPC showed essential functions for proper plant growth and development [90, 91]. The proteins of this family regulate flower development and circadian rhythm, the size of the stem cell niche, and seed development through transcriptional regulation of homeotic transcription factor genes [91]. BBR/BPC also works in signaling brassinosteroid hormones in plants, such as Arabidopsis. BPC6 targets the promoters of all key brassinosteroid signaling elements [91]. The TFs of this family are activated by salt, osmotic, and cold stress. For instance, AtBPC2 acts as a key regulator in salt tolerance, with its expression changing in response to environmental fluctuations [92]. The TFs are linked to abscisic acid (ABA) signaling and other hormonal responses, controlling how plants respond to environmental challenges. They repress specific downstream genes, such as LEA (Late Embryogenesis Abundant) genes, regulating seed maturation and germination during osmotic stress. Some BPCs are identified as regulators of immunity and responses to biotic challenges, helping manage the trade-off between growth and defense.

The MIKC-type MADS-box transcription factors (TFs) are critical regulators in plants, acting as master switches for developmental processes—including flower, fruit, and root development—and as integrators of abiotic stress responses, such as drought, salinity, and temperature fluctuations. They often function through complex protein-protein interaction networks and modulate hormonal signaling pathways (e.g., ABA, auxins) to enhance resilience and adapt growth under environmental stress [93, 94]. This TF family is popular to act as a regulatory network for rapid and simultaneous functional divergence in vegetative and reproductive stages in plants for regulating gene expression in flowers, pollen, endosperm, guard cells, roots, and trichomes [95]. The TFs of this family act within ABA signaling pathways (e.g., OsMADS31, GhFYF) to enhance stress tolerance, notably in seed germination and seedling growth under salt stress [96, 97]. These TFs also regulate downstream genes that manage reactive oxygen species (ROS) and maintain metabolic stability under osmotic stress [98]. This family was also responsible for the transcription of OsRDR1 genes to increase the resistance power against the rice stripe virus (RSV) in rice [53, 99].

In plants, MYB TF was also found in higher numbers. In A. thaliana, this TF family accounts for nearly 9% of the total TFs [100]. This TF was identified for its conserved MYB domain (a 52 amino acid motif) at the N-terminus, and was an evolutionarily conserved domain found in almost all eukaryotes [100, 101]. The TFs of this family in plants are strongly linked to various biological processes, namely, the circadian rhythm, defense and stress responses, cell fate and identity, seed and floral development, and regulation of primary and secondary metabolism [100, 102]. MYB proteins act as key mediators in environmental stress signaling, improving resistance to drought, salt, and temperature extremes. For example, ZmMYB-CC10 improves maize drought tolerance, and MYB60/MYB124 manage stomatal movement [103]. They serve as, or trigger, molecular defenses against fungi, bacteria, and insects [104]. Moreover, MYB TFs are crucial for development processes, including cell differentiation, flower formation, and stomatal development. They interact with plant hormones such as abscisic acid (ABA), auxin (IAA), and gibberellin (GA) to coordinate responses to stress and guide growth, notably in root development [104].

In various plant species, the WRKY family is believed to be involved in i) the modulation of antagonistic interaction between salicylic acid and jasmonic acid signalling and ii) the enhancement of defense against neurotropic pathogens. WRKYs regulate genes related to ROS (reactive oxygen species) scavenging, ion homeostasis, and hormone signaling (abscisic acid, jasmonic acid) to manage stress from drought, salinity, and heat. For instance, OsWRKY63 negatively regulates cold tolerance, while SlWRKY3 improves heat resistance by controlling ROS accumulation [105]. They play a central role in plant immunity, acting as regulators in pathogenesis-related (PR) gene expression, particularly in response to fungal or bacterial infections. In addition, specialized WRKYs, such as GmWRKY21 or WRKY75, modulate plant responses to aluminum stress and phosphate deficiency, respectively [106]. This TF family plays a significant role in plant growth and development, including leaf senescence: WRKY TFs are often highly expressed during leaf senescence, promoting this process to allow nutrient reallocation. They are involved in embryogenesis, trichome (leaf hair) development, and seed coat development. It has a vital role in metabolic regulation, that is, WRKYs can modulate secondary metabolism and sugar metabolism, influencing plant growth and development in tandem with environmental stress responses [105].

AP2/ERF (Apetala2/Ethylene Responsive Factor) transcription factors (TFs) are large plant-specific families vital for adapting to environmental stress (drought, cold, salt, pathogens) and regulating developmental processes like flowering and leaf growth [87]. Through subfamilies such as DREB and ERF, they bind specific DNA elements (e.g., GCC-box, DRE/CRT) to regulate downstream gene expression in response to phytohormones like ethylene, jasmonic acid, and ABA [107]. DREB subfamily members recognize DRE/CRT cis-acting elements to initiate the expression of protective genes, such as RD29A or COR15A. These TFs are essential for cold acclimation and mitigating osmotic stress. ERF subfamily members typically regulate defense genes against fungi, bacteria, and insects by interacting with GCC-box elements. During plant development, the subfamily is critical for determining flower organ identity and regulating leaf development, notably APETALA2 (AP2) in Arabidopsis. In hormone signaling, these TFs regulate developmental processes in response to ethylene, affecting stem and leaf growth, root development, and vascular tissue development [108]. Earlier investigations also reported that the AP2 family contributed to gene regulation for development and resistance in response to various environmental stressors [81, 109]. Our results, however, are supported by recent studies indicating the vital role of the predicted TFs in plant growth and development, particularly in response to hormonal cues [110, 111]. The regulatory network revealed that the RNAi process of predicted putative genes in B. rapa represents a diagrammatic evolutionary model that can be explored in detail through the characterization of these predicted genes.

Cis-regulatory elements (CREs) are sequences that act as enhancers, promoters, insulators, and silencers for particular gene expression in living organisms. CREs are linear fragments of non-coding DNA (6–10 bp) [53]. These elements play essential roles in growth, development, and other important physiological activities by regulating specific gene expression in plants [112]. Typically, these cis-acting components contain non-coding DNA and retain binding sites for transcription factors (TF) and/or other regulatory molecules for generating gene transcription [112, 113]. Photosynthesis in plants is important, and it has a high link to light responses that take place in plants’ green leaves and certain other organisms to transform light energy into chemical energy in the form of oxygen and energy-rich organic compounds. Like many plants, photosynthesis is a significant physiological parameter in B. rapa plants that relates to many aspects of increasing grain quality and crop productivity [114]. An increased rate of photosynthesis can exploit the solar radiation suitably, leading to an early flowering time because flowering signals are produced in leaves [115, 116]. Therefore, the identified CREs related to LR are assumed to retain direct and indirect connections for high photosynthesis levels in B. rapa plant leaves.

It was well-studied that various plant hormones or phytohormones are essential during plant growth and developmental timing [117]. Abscisic acid-responsive elements (ABREs), also referred to as AREs, are cis-acting DNA sequences found in plant promoters that function as molecular switches and serve as significant cis-regulatory elements (CREs) for abiotic stress responses. These elements are bound by specific transcription factors (e.g., AREB/ABF) to activate gene expression under conditions such as drought, salinity, and cold stress [118]. ABREs are primary mediators in the abscisic acid (ABA)-dependent signaling pathway, initiating responses when ABA levels rise during environmental stress [119, 120]. They serve as binding sites for basic leucine zipper (bZIP) transcription factors (AREB1, AREB2, AREB3), which in turn activate stress-protective genes, leading to physiological changes like stomatal closure and reduced water loss [118].

The MYB Binding Site (MBS) is another crucial CRE found in the promoter regions of plant genes, typically consisting of sequences such as 5′-AACTAAA/C-3′ [103]. MBS motifs play important roles in plant stress responses, particularly in drought and osmotic stress tolerance. During drought conditions, MYB transcription factors bind to MBS sites to trigger the expression of stress-responsive genes (such as RD22, ADH1), thereby enhancing tolerance. Beyond drought, MBS motifs contribute to responses to low temperature (cold) and salt stress by activating antioxidant enzyme systems like SOD and CAT [119]. Furthermore, MBS motifs are involved in root development, regulating the biosynthesis of epidermal waxes—crucial for limiting non-stomatal water loss—and influencing flower development and cell cycle regulation within root meristems [121].

Long Terminal Repeat (LTR) cis-elements serve as molecular switches within the 5′ LTR of LTR-retrotransposons (LTR-RTs), primarily activating transcription in response to stress conditions such as heat, cold, salt, and drought [122]. Certain LTR-cis elements also drive gene expression in specific tissues, like roots or wound sites, highlighting their role in normal developmental adaptation [122]. GC motif-binding transcription factors (TFs), which interact with GC-rich elements, GC boxes, or as a part of larger GC-rich anaerobic response elements (AREs), are key regulators of plant responses to abiotic stresses and developmental processes [53]. These TFs function as molecular switches, modulating gene expression in response to environmental cues including waterlogging, low oxygen, drought, salinity, and cold [123]. Additionally, endoplasmic reticulum (ER) or heat stress responses are triggered by specific membrane-bound TFs (MTFs) [124]. GC-rich motifs also play essential roles in plant development, such as embryogenesis, cell fate determination, apical-basal patterning, and the formation of shoot and root meristems [125]. As growth-regulating factors (GRFs) containing GC-rich regions, they further regulate organ development, leaf formation, and seedling morphology [126].

GCN4_motif (core sequence: TGAGTCA) is a CRE that plays a central role in controlling endosperm-specific expression of the genes in B. rapa [53, 127]. TGA-element (auxin-responsive element) and ABRE functions were involved in responses to several adverse environmental situations, such as drought, high salt, and cold/freezing [15, 53, 127, 128]. Previous studies also suggested that the five most important plant hormones: auxin, gibberellin, cytokinin, ethylene, and abscisic acid act collectively or individually to affect plant growth and development [117, 129–132]. The mechanisms of environmental adaptation in terms of major abiotic stresses (drought, salinity, temperature, light) in crops are also connected to chromatin accessibility, which has recently been shown to be dynamic at CREs near stress-responsive genes [133]. A recent study by Shahzad et al. (2025) also showed similar results on CRE analysis of U’s triangle Brassica species.

In silico expression analysis was performed for all 23 B. rapa RNAi genes to extract in-depth insights into the biological and molecular involvement of the DCL, AGO, and RDR gene families in B. rapa, including evidence of expression of any gene of these three families in different tissues or organs. Expression analyses of RNAi-related genes in several plant species were completed, and the results suggested that these genes showed considerable and varied expression in flower/flower buds, followed by seeds, leaves, roots, siliques, and seed coats, and other important organs or tissues. Flowers, roots, leaves, and siliques are the key organs of oilseed crops for improved yield; therefore, the expression of more genes belonging to BrDCL, BrAGO, and BrRDR families implied that these organs would play a significant role in oilseed crop production by addressing various environmental factors [2, 8, 10, 11, 134–138].

In plants, RNA interference (RNAi) genes—including those encoding miRNAs and siRNAs—form a dynamic regulatory system that controls gene expression at the post-transcriptional (via mRNA degradation) or transcriptional (via DNA methylation) level [139]. Under stress or during specific developmental stages, the expression of these small RNAs is modulated, leading to the downregulation of target genes such as transcription factors, enzymes, or hormone receptors, thereby enabling plants to adapt their physiology and metabolism [140]. For instance, in maize, increased miR172 levels target APETALA2-like genes to initiate flowering as the plant matures [141, 142]. The miR166/165-mediated regulation of Homeodomain-Leucine Zipper III (HD-ZIP III) transcription factors is crucial for vascular development, leaf polarity, and lateral root formation. In hormone signaling, miR160 and miR167 modulate auxin response factors (ARFs), which influence lateral root development and floral organ identity [143]. Plants rapidly adjust the expression of certain RNAi genes (especially miRNAs) in response to abiotic stresses (drought, salinity, cold) or environmental changes to perfect metabolism, such as reducing growth and conserving energy. For example, under drought stress, miR169g in A. thaliana and miR393 in rice are upregulated to suppress growth-related signaling [144], while miR396 is downregulated in some contexts to facilitate root growth adaptation. In response to osmotic stress, miR474 is upregulated to target proline dehydrogenase, leading to enhanced proline accumulation [145, 146]. Additionally, miR156 is implicated in heat stress responses in Arabidopsis [145]. During pathogen attacks by viruses, fungi, or bacteria, plants activate RNAi mechanisms to degrade invading RNA or silence host genes that may facilitate infection. Virus-derived siRNAs are generated and loaded into Argonaute proteins (AGO1) to target and degrade viral mRNAs. Fungal infections can induce miR159 and miR166 in cotton, which then target virulence-related fungal proteins, thereby enhancing plant resistance [147].

RNA interference (RNAi) genes play key roles in enhancing plant traits by enabling precise, sequence-specific silencing of target genes, which can be harnessed to improve disease resistance, increase yield, and modify nutritional content. The introduction of double-stranded RNAs (dsRNAs) corresponding to specific genes activates the plant’s natural RNAi defense mechanism, leading to the degradation of the matching messenger RNA (mRNA) and preventing protein production. RNAi serves as an immune system against biotic stressors by silencing genes vital for the survival or virulence of pests and pathogens. Engineered plants expressing dsRNA targeting viral sequences—such as coat proteins or replication enzymes—can disrupt viral life cycles, conferring broad-spectrum resistance to viruses like Cucumber Mosaic Virus and Banana Bract Mosaic Virus [148]. RNAi can also directly improve yield by altering plant metabolic pathways and physiological responses, particularly under stress. For example, silencing genes involved in hormone signaling (such as fatty acid amide hydrolase, AtFAAH) or modifying miRNA expression can enhance resilience to drought, salinity, and extreme temperatures [149]. Silencing auxin response factors (e.g., SlARF7) can induce the development of seedless fruits in crops like tomatoes [150]. Further, RNAi technology can enhance the nutritional and industrial value of crops by reducing toxic compounds or boosting beneficial nutrients. For instance, silencing SBEII (starch-branching enzyme) in wheat and maize increases amylose content, promoting digestive health [151, 152] while silencing FAD2 or SAD1 raises oleic acid levels in oilseeds [153]. Additionally, silencing ethylene-producing genes (e.g., ACC oxidase) can delay fruit ripening and extend tomato shelf life [154].

The expansions and duplication events of the RNAi machinery genes in Brassica rapa are predicted to show significant roles, particularly enhancing the plant’s ability to respond to environmental stresses. These occurrences may allow this species to have functional redundancy, reducing the negative impact of mutations in a single gene, while also enabling the evolution of new functions. Duplicated RNAi genes often diverge in expression patterns and functions. For example, specific BrAGO and BrRDR genes (e.g., BrAGO1, BrAGO4, BrAGO9, BrRDR5) have expanded and are often differentially expressed under stress, indicating tailored regulatory functions. It is also predicted that duplicated genes in B. rapa might exhibit extensive divergence in their miRNA binding compared to Arabidopsis and Oryza sativa, suggesting that RNAi-mediated regulation has evolved to be more complex to manage a greater number of gene copies. RNAi genes in B. rapa might remain active as a “stress memory” mechanism, regulating gene expression to manage environmental challenges [155]. For example, knockdown of GIGANTEA (GI) via RNAi improves salt tolerance, suggesting that the expansion of the RNAi machinery permits finer regulation of flowering time and stress pathways [156]. Moreover, it is assumed that the two RDRs (BrRDR1 and BrRDR6), which are involved in siRNA biogenesis, are upregulated under drought and salinity stress, thereby facilitating adaptive responses [157]. In case of pathogenic resistance, the expansion of AGO and DCL genes is predicted to enhance B. rapa‘s ability to produce specific small RNAs (siRNAs/miRNAs) to silence pathogen genes [158].

Linking the identified RNAi genes to practical crop improvement strategies involves moving from functional validation to the engineered downregulation of target genes, either within the plant (transgenic) or in pests/pathogens (exogenous application). Key strategies include enhancing biotic/abiotic stress resistance, improving nutritional quality, and modifying agronomic traits by utilizing techniques like hairpin RNA-expressing vectors (hpRNAi), Host-Induced Gene Silencing (HIGS), and Spray-Induced Gene Silencing (SIGS) [159]. Transgenic plants are engineered to express double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) targeting essential genes in pests or pathogens. Upon feeding, the pests ingest this dsRNA, leading to silencing of their vital genes. For example, resistance to root-knot nematodes via silencing 16D10 [118], cotton bollworm (Helicoverpa armigera) control by targeting CYP6AE14 [119], and resistance to Fusarium fungus via targeting chitin synthase genes [120]. A non-transgenic, “sprayable” approach where dsRNA or siRNA is applied directly to plant surfaces to silence pathogen genes, offering a sustainable alternative to pesticides. For instance, controlling Botrytis cinerea (grey mold) on fruits using the topical application of dsRNA targeting pathogen silencing machinery [160].

RNAi can downregulate endogenous genes to remove undesirable compounds or increase beneficial ones, providing a more precise alternative to conventional breeding [121, 122]. For nutritional improvement, the increasing amylose content in wheat by suppressing starch-branching enzymes (SBEIIa/SBEIIb) [123] and the high-lysine maize was produced by knocking out the 22-kD zein storage protein [124]. In case of reduction of toxins/allergens, the production of “tearless” onions is possible by suppressing the lachrymatory factor synthase gene [125]. To enhance abiotic stress tolerance, RNAi is used to knock down negative regulators of stress responses or fine-tune metabolic pathways to improve tolerance [126]. For example, silencing the RACK1 (Receptor for Activated C-kinase 1) gene in rice improved drought tolerance [161] and suppression of farnesyl transferase has been used to develop drought-tolerant canola [162]. RNAi can manipulate plant growth habits, such as height and flowering, to improve yield [163]. Knockdown of OsDWARF4 in rice resulted in shorter plants with erect leaves, improving photosynthesis and allowing higher planting density [164].

Enhancing crop stress tolerance and yield requires manipulating genes involved in stress sensing, signaling, metabolic adjustment, and growth regulation. Potential experimental approaches range from traditional transgenics to advanced genome editing and non-genetic priming techniques [165]. RNA interference (RNAi) is best used for silencing negative regulators of stress tolerance, such as stress-sensitive genes or ABA-signaling repressors [166]. Overexpression is most effective for positive regulators of stress tolerance (e.g., transcription factors, antioxidant enzymes) or enzymes that increase yield-related metabolic pathways [167].

Transcription factors (TFs) and cis-regulatory elements (CREs) interact to regulate RNAi genes under stress by acting as molecular switches that control the expression of silencing machinery genes (DCLs, AGOs, and RDRs). Under stress, specific stress-responsive TFs bind to overrepresented CREs (e.g., ABRE, G-box, MBS, TGA-elements) in the promoters of RNAi components, leading to rapid transcriptional reprogramming [168]. Synergistic interactions exist between TF families (e.g., HSFs and AP2/ERF) to amplify this response [169]. Studies have shown that Heat Shock Factors (HSFs), such as HaHSFA9, can act synergistically with Drought-Responsive Element Binding factors (DREB2, an AP2/ERF family member) to regulate gene expression under stress [170].

Conclusion

In this research, we have performed the first genome-wide characterization of RNAi gene families in B. rapa, identifying 4 DCL, 13 AGO, and 6 RDR genes that play significant roles in gene regulation. Comparative genomic analysis revealed extensive syntenic conservation with A. thaliana, highlighting evolutionary preservation of the RNAi pathways. The protein domain, gene, and motif structures provided strong evidence of similarity with those of the A. thaliana RNAi proteins. The predicted genes were distributed across 10 chromosomes, with a segmental duplication observed in BrAGO1 and BrAGO4. GO analysis suggested that the BrRNAi genes were involved in a range of important biological and molecular pathways, including responses to viruses, immune effector processes, post-transcriptional gene silencing, siRNA binding, RNA polymerase activity, and ribonuclease activity. The higher number of genes was nucleus-centric, chloroplast-centric, and cytosol-centric. Dof, bZIP, C2H2, ERF, BBR-BPC, MICK-MADS, MYB, TCP, WRKY, and AP2 were top-ranking TF families, accounting for 393 (78%) out of 502 TFs. The gene-TF network analysis showed that eight key TF families were highly connected with the identified RNAi genes in B. rapa. CREs were highly likely to be related to the hormone, light, and various stress-responsive elements. The expression analysis showed that most genes were predicted to be expressed in flower/flower buds, followed by seeds, leaves, roots, siliques, and seed coats, which are important organs of B. rapa. The identified BrRNAi genes can be leveraged to improve stress resistance by creating transgenic lines that knock down negative regulators of stress responses, enhance defense-related gene expression, or produce host-induced gene silencing molecules that target pests and pathogens. Expression profile analysis, including RT-qPCR and RNA-seq, can be resorted to validate whether the identified genes are active during specific developmental stages or stress conditions. Finally, to establish function, knockdown/knockout experiments can be convenient to reduce the expression of the identified genes and observe phenotypic changes. Manipulating RNAi genes, however, can significantly enhance crop traits such as disease resistance, abiotic stress tolerance, and crop yield with quality in B. rapa.
