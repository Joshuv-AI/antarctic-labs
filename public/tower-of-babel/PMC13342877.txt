Nekropolis: a cross-domain dataset of computer-science retractions and AI-governance lifecycle records (2018–2026)
Authors: Anton Sokolov
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNekropolis is a curated public-record corpus of 14,923 artifacts (2018–2026) that were retracted, withdrawn, revoked, archived, or discontinued across computer-science research and adjacent digital-governance infrastructure, under one entry schema. The corpus is dominated by computer-science/AI-related journal retractions: the Retraction Watch slice is 90.5% of the release and is described throughout as an AI/ML/CS-related candidate slice, not an adjudicated AI-only set. Source families include journal retractions, preprint withdrawals, public retraction-flag metadata, trusted-service lifecycle changes, archived governance repositories, withdrawn benchmarks, closed European Digital Identity Wallet pilots, and one rescinded public model demo. Each record carries an artifact category, lifecycle dates, a source-stated reason where available, a conservative project-inferred cause label, and hashed source pointers. The corpus is descriptive; it is not an assessment of researchers, institutions, publishers, repositories, or service providers. The dataset can be reused for source-stratified study of negative governance evidence, temporal-awareness tests in retrieval systems, and evaluation of systems that extract evidence from public lifecycle records while preserving abstention when sources are silent. A versioned Zenodo package provides the release file, candidate-holdout file, schema, pipelines bundle, build notes, and manuscript source. The counts describe one curated release slice built under named gates and are not prevalence estimates.Keywords: Research integrity, Lifecycle evidence, Temporal awareness, Public records, Source stratification, Evidence extraction, Reproducibility

Background

The Nekropolis v0.3 dataset is deposited as a versioned Zenodo package [1]. AI-governance research usually preserves positive artifacts: standards, guidance, benchmarks, model cards, reference implementations, and compliance roadmaps. Lifecycle records for artifacts that were retracted, withdrawn, revoked, archived, paused, or otherwise discontinued are more dispersed. In scholarly publishing, retraction research spans misconduct analysis [2], paper-mill reporting [3], algorithmically generated nonsense papers [4], and authorship-for-sale markets [5]. Beyond publishing, public lifecycle records appear across preprint servers, trusted-service lists, software repositories, benchmark pages, public pilot pages, and model-demo notices. Nekropolis was compiled to describe these records under one public schema without converting lifecycle events into blame or legal conclusions. This data article describes the v0.3 release package, its schema, source-family gates, validation checks, and responsible reuse boundaries.

Data Description

The deposited v0.3 release contains 14,923 records in entries-v0.3-release.jsonl. Each line is a JSON object with the same ten fields: id, category, title, actors, first_appeared, withdrawn_at, stated_reason, inferred_cause, sources, and notes. The schema has no individual-person blame field. Actors are recorded at the organization or artifact level where the source supports them. stated_reason preserves source wording where available; inferred_cause is a coarse project label for aggregate grouping.

Every record in every source family carries the identical ten-field schema; there are no family-specific columns and no missing dimensions, so the corpus is a single homogeneous table rather than a join of differently shaped sub-datasets. What differs across families is evidence density, not the field set. As to annotation, stated_reason is verbatim source text (or empty) and is not annotated; inferred_cause is a deterministic single-annotator label (assignment described below); and category and the source-family label are fixed at capture. No field is human-coded under an inter-annotator process.

The inferred_cause label is assigned by a deterministic, rule-based program rather than by human reading, and is reproducible from the deposited pipeline. For the Retraction Watch family an ordered set of nine case-insensitive regular expressions maps the verbatim stated_reason to the first matching label (an empty reason returns a no-information state); non-Retraction Watch families receive a single lifecycle/status label from their own fetcher. A release-time normalization step then maps every working-corpus label onto a fixed thirteen-label canonical taxonomy (authorship-or-affiliation; compromised-peer-review; computer-generated-content; data-results-or-methods-integrity; duplication-or-redundant-publication; editorial-or-publisher-process; ethics-consent-or-human-subjects; opaque-or-limited-public-record; paper-mill-or-third-party-involvement; plagiarism-or-attribution; preprint-readiness-or-revision; trust-service-status-withdrawn; unclassified-public-record), with reason-free records falling back to a low-information label. Because the project is single-author, the appropriate reliability claim is reproducibility — identical inputs yield identical labels — not inter-rater agreement; inferred_cause is never presented as a finding of fault, and stated_reason is never overwritten.

Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

Nekropolis uses a three-layer construction process: raw source capture, append-only working corpus, and curated release slice.

Raw source capture stores public responses or source-near records with access time, URL or local path, and local SHA-256 hash. Scripted collection was used for the large public-record families: Retraction Watch/Crossref [6], arXiv [7], OpenAlex [8], EU LOTL/EUTL [9], and GitHub repository metadata [10]. Source-near manual review was used for the small lifecycle families: withdrawn benchmarks, closed European Digital Identity Wallet pilots, and the rescinded public model demo.

The deposit redistributes the curated layer — the release file, schema, candidate-GitHub file, v0.2 lineage files, changelog, build summary, and pipeline archive — but not the raw upstream payloads, which are retained in the working pipeline and not redistributed. Recoverability is anchored at two levels: the release file's SHA-256 fixes the published record set byte-for-byte, and the build summary records, per source family, the snapshot SHA-256, the access date, and the deduplication counts captured at raw-capture time, so the upstream snapshot each family was built from is identifiable even though the live sources continue to change.

The append-only working corpus maps captured sources into the unified schema. This layer preserves candidate provenance even when later curation decides that a row should not enter the public release. The GitHub family illustrates this design. A repository-search pass produced 2201 candidate records, but three spot-check passes put the family above the release noise threshold. A 2026–05–31 manual review of 100 sampled GitHub candidates rejected all 100 from the release because the rows failed the inactivity gate, governance-scope review, or both. Full-family metadata triage found 1469 candidate rows failing the 548-day inactivity gate. The release therefore keeps only six previously adjudicated GitHub records and preserves the remaining 2195 records in a candidate-only holdout file.

Curated release slices are built through four gates. Schema validation checks all rows against the JSON Schema. Curation overrides exclude rows under versioned, source-anchored rules. Review gates apply to source families whose discovery method is weaker than the release rule. The release package then records a changelog, working-corpus build summary, pipeline archive, and checksums so that the build can be inspected after publication.

For reproducible reruns, a reuser starts from the Zenodo record [1], downloads entries-v0.3-release.jsonl, entry.schema.json, entries-v0.3.jsonl.summary.json, and nekropolis-pipelines-v0.3.tar.gz, unpacks the pipeline archive, installs the Python dependencies recorded with the pipeline, rebuilds the release slice with the deposited release-builder scripts, validates the output against entry.schema.json, runs the deposited pytest and byte-compilation checks, and compares the resulting record count, category totals, source-family totals, and SHA-256 hashes with the build summary and Table 2. Because the upstream services are mutable, the reproducibility target is the byte-stable deposited slice and its recorded dated source-snapshot hashes, not the live state of each upstream service.

The v0.3 build passed schema validation for all 14,923 released records. The pipeline test suite reported 141 passing tests, and Python byte-compilation passed across the deposited pipeline scripts. Identifier and count checks found 14,923 unique IDs, with category totals and source-family totals reconciling to the same total.

The Retraction Watch slice is the dominant source family and uses a recall-oriented AI/ML/CS selection heuristic. Each Retraction Watch row carries an evidence tier. The tier distribution is: 7134 broad computer-science metadata records, 3243 high-precision title-level AI/ML records, 2629 method- or system-adjacent records, 475 venue or metadata AI/ML records, and 21 review-needed records. Reusers who need high precision can filter to the title-level tier; reusers studying candidate discovery can keep the full slice.

The selection heuristic is a substring-presence gate, not a numeric threshold: a row is admitted if any of twelve case-insensitive AI/CS terms — artificial intelligence, machine learning, deep learning, neural network, computer vision, natural language processing, large language model, foundation model, algorithmic, algorithm, data mining, computer science — appears anywhere in its Title, Subject, Journal, Publisher, Reason, or Notes fields. Each admitted row is then assigned exactly one evidence tier by first match in a fixed precedence: a high-precision title-level AI/ML term (the eight AI/ML terms above plus neural targeting); a method/system-adjacent term in the title; a high-precision term in the venue or subject; a method/system-adjacent term in title or subject; a broad computer-science metadata term; or review-needed. No score or cut-off is applied.

Limitations

Nekropolis is not a prevalence dataset. It describes a curated release slice under named gates and does not estimate how often retraction, withdrawal, repository archival, trusted-service status change, pilot closure, benchmark withdrawal, or demo rescission occur in the world. It also does not establish misconduct, fault, legal non-compliance, or institutional quality. A lifecycle event is a public record, not a verdict.

The source families are uneven. Retraction Watch records are often reason-rich, while OpenAlex retraction flags are thin in this snapshot. arXiv withdrawal metadata is brief; trusted-list records expose status transitions; repository archival metadata is often ambiguous; and pilot or demo closure pages are source-near but small in number. Analyses that pool these families without stratification will misread the dataset.

Ethics Statement

The author has read and follows the ethical requirements for publication in Data in Brief. This work does not involve human subjects, animal experiments, or data collected from social media platforms. The dataset uses public secondary records and does not reproduce the text of retracted papers, private communications, or personal data beyond what appears in public source metadata. Nekropolis records actors at organization or artifact level, separates source-stated reasons from project-inferred causes, and is not designed for ranking researchers, maintainers, institutions, publishers, repositories, or service providers. Responsible reuse requires source-stratified reporting, preservation of the GitHub candidate-slice caveat, and avoidance of individual-level blame or legal conclusions.
