The Role of Wisdom and Sense of Coherence in Predicting Quality of Life Among Hospitalized Elderly: A Cross‐Sectional Study From Eastern Guilan, Northern Iran
Authors: Azar Darvishpour, Masoumeh Rahimibashar, Shiva Mahdavi Fashtami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsAging is accompanied by various physical, psychological, and social changes that can adversely affect the quality of life (QoL). Psychological constructs such as wisdom and sense of coherence (SOC) are believed to serve as important protective and promotive factors in maintaining QoL in older adults. This study aimed to examine the relationship between wisdom, SOC, and QoL among elderly individuals referred to hospitals in eastern Guilan, northern Iran.MethodsThis cross‐sectional and correlational study was conducted in 2024 with a sample of 200 elderly patients selected through multistage cluster sampling from general hospitals. Data collection tools included a demographic questionnaire, the Abbreviated Mental Test (AMT), the CASP‐19 Quality of Life Scale, the Antonovsky SOC‐13, and the Wisdom Questionnaire. Data were analyzed using SPSS version 21, employing descriptive statistics, partial correlation analyses, and multivariate linear regression (p < 0.05).ResultsThe majority of participants were male (66.5%) and aged 60–74 years (66.5%). The mean scores for wisdom, SOC, and QoL were 75.35 ± 8.81, 52.88 ± 10.84, and 38.10 ± 6.58, respectively. Mean scores for wisdom, SOC, and QoL were 75.35 ± 8.81, 52.88 ± 10.84, and 38.10 ± 6.58, respectively. Wisdom (r = 0.267, p < 0.001) and SOC (r = 0.253, p < 0.001) positively correlated with QoL. The regression model was statistically significant (F = 7.59, p < 0.001) and explained 41.5% of the variance in QoL (R 2 = 0.415). Higher levels of wisdom (β = 0.270, p < 0.001) and SOC (β = 0.301, p < 0.001) were significantly associated with higher QoL.ConclusionWisdom and sense of coherence are significant psychological predictors of quality of life among hospitalized elderly individuals. Designing interventions to enhance these internal resources may help promote psychological well‐being and life satisfaction in this population.Keywords: aged, older adults, quality of life, sense of coherence, wisdom

Introduction

The world's elderly population is growing rapidly [1]. According to the World Health Organization, by 2030, one in six people worldwide will be aged 60 years or older, with the total number of individuals in this age group projected to rise from 1 billion in 2020 to 1.4 billion. By 2050, this figure is expected to reach 2.1 billion, while the population aged 80 years and above is anticipated to triple, reaching 426 million [2]. In Iran, there is also evidence of a significant increase in the elderly population in the near future [3], with forecasts indicating that over the next 35 years, about 33% of the country's total population will be elderly [4]. According to the latest census conducted by the Statistical Center of Iran in 2016, 13.2% of the population of Guilan province consists of older adults, which is the highest proportion in the country [5].

Aging is a natural and inevitable process of human life [6], but if not accompanied by an acceptable quality of life, it can lead to negative physical, psychological, and social consequences [7]. The increasing elderly population imposes numerous economic, social, and health‐related challenges on societies [8]. Addressing these challenges requires the formulation of effective policies to improve the physical, mental, and social well‐being of the elderly. Today, the goal is not only to extend life expectancy; the quality of those extended years has also become essential [9]. In other words, the aim of geriatric care is not solely to add years to life, but also to enhance comfort, physical and mental health during these years [7].

Quality of life (QoL) is a multidimensional and subjective concept [10] that can hold different meanings depending on personal and cultural contexts [11]. Quality of life is a multidimensional, subjective construct that encompasses physical, psychological, social, and spiritual domains, as well as autonomy and environmental context [12]. Its conceptualization has evolved from a primarily health‐focused perspective to a more holistic framework that considers emotional well‐being, social participation, and life satisfaction as core components [11]. According to the World Health Organization, QoL refers to an individual's perception of their position in life in the context of cultural and value systems, relative to personal goals, expectations, and priorities [13]. Recent research highlights that QoL in older adults is influenced not only by health status but also by psychological resources and adaptive capacities, underscoring the need to examine cognitive, emotional, and social factors in addition to physical health [14, 15, 16]. In the elderly, QoL is a broader concept than physical health alone [17], encompassing psychological, social, and spiritual dimensions, as well as independence, security, adaptability, and interpersonal relationships [11, 18, 19]. Accordingly, some researchers have suggested that 21st‐century healthcare should focus not just on disease control, but on the enhancement of life quality [20]. In recent decades, numerous studies have been conducted on QoL of the elderly [18, 20]. In a systematic review by Kazemi et al., about 92% of the studies reported that QoL of Iranian older adults was at a low or moderate level, and only 7% reported high levels [18]. Farajzadeh et al. also concluded that the overall QoL among Iranian elderly is generally moderate [20].

Emerging evidence indicates that psychological factors such as wisdom and sense of coherence (SOC) play a crucial role in maintaining QoL in older adults. Wisdom is a complex, multidimensional construct [21] that is positively associated with subjective well‐being among the elderly [22]. Contrary to popular belief, wisdom is not merely the result of high intelligence scores but reflects a set of personal traits, including insight, sound social decision‐making, emotional balance, reflection, assertiveness in uncertain situations, tolerance for differing values, and prosocial behaviors such as empathy and compassion [23, 24]. Research has shown that wisdom is associated with greater well‐being, happiness, life satisfaction, resilience, and a reduction in suffering during times of crisis [25, 26]. Wise individuals are more likely to respond calmly to adversity, engage in healthier lifestyles, and enjoy better psychological stability [27, 28, 29]. For example, a study by found that individual wisdom was positively correlated with QoL among Chinese elderly people, regardless of whether they lived in urban or rural areas [30]. Similarly, Zadworna et al. found that wiser older adults tended to have a more positive outlook on life and were more engaged in health‐promoting behaviors, especially those supporting mental health [31]. These findings suggest that wisdom is not only a cognitive‐emotional trait but also a practical resource for enhancing QoL in older adults.

Another important psychological factor associated with QoL is the sense of coherence (SOC) [32]. Antonovsky defines this construct as a personal orientation toward life, grounded in three core components: comprehensibility, manageability, and meaningfulness [33, 34]. These three dimensions respectively reflect the cognitive, behavioral, and motivational aspects of SOC. People with a strong SOC believe that life events are understandable, manageable, and meaningful, and thus, they possess a greater capacity to cope with stress [33]. Particularly in later life, this orientation helps individuals adapt more effectively to both physiological and psychological changes [35]. Several studies have identified a positive association between SOC and QoL. A study by Sawma et al. in hemodialysis patients found a significant relationship between SOC and most dimensions of QoL [36]. Similarly, Galletta et al. demonstrated that SOC not only improved psychological well‐being but also physical health among individuals with chronic conditions [34]. In Iran, Asgari et al. found that SOC significantly predicted QoL among older adults [37], and Mahmoodi et al. reported that those with a stronger SOC experienced a higher level of life quality [38].

Despite existing research on QoL among the elderly, studies examining the combined influence of wisdom and SOC on QoL are scarce. Most prior research has investigated these variables independently or in non‐Iranian populations, leaving a gap in understanding their interactive effect in culturally distinct contexts. However, understanding the interaction of these two variables can offer a more comprehensive perspective on the psychological mechanisms underpinning well‐being in later life. Exploring their combined explanatory power may support the development of more effective interventions aimed at promoting elderly health and resilience [27, 31]. Furthermore, while Guilan province has the highest proportion of elderly individuals in Iran, no prior study has directly investigated the simultaneous relationship between wisdom, SOC, and QoL in this unique demographic, highlighting the need for culturally and regionally specific evidence. Therefore, the present study aims to determine the relationship between wisdom and SOC with QoL in elderly individuals attending general hospitals in the eastern region of Guilan province. This study's novelty lies in its simultaneous examination of these psychological factors in a previously understudied Iranian population, providing insights that can inform targeted interventions and healthcare planning.

Methods

This cross‐sectional analytical study with a correlational design was conducted between November 2024 and April 2025.

Population, Sample, and Sampling Method

The study population consisted of all individuals aged 60 years and above who visited public hospitals in eastern Guilan. From the six public hospitals in this region, three were randomly selected as clusters. Subsequently, participants were recruited from each selected hospital using convenience sampling. Eligible elderly individuals were consecutively included until the sample size was fulfilled.

The sample size was determined using a dual‐approach strategy to ensure adequate statistical power for both the correlational analyses and the multivariable regression analyses. First, a correlation‐based power analysis was conducted. Based on the findings of Asgari and Shafiee [37], who reported a correlation coefficient of r = 0.251 between SOC and QoL, the sample size was calculated using Fisher's Z transformation method, which is standard for correlation studies. Using a significance level of α = 0.05 (Z1−a2 = 1.96), a statistical power of 0.90 (Z1−B = 1.28), and an effect size of r = 0.251, the required sample size was estimated at 163 participants. A statistical power of 0.90 was selected to reduce the probability of Type II error, particularly because psychosocial studies commonly involve small‐to‐moderate effect sizes and heterogeneous populations [39, 40].

Second, because the study also included multiple linear regression analyses with 11 independent variables, an additional regression‐based approach was applied to ensure model stability. Following the conservative recommendation of Halinski and Feldt [41], which suggests 10–15 observations per predictor variable, the required sample size ranged from 110 to 165 participants. To maximize statistical stability, the upper bound of this recommendation (165 participants) was adopted.

Notably, the two approaches yielded highly similar estimates (163 and 165 participants), indicating convergence between the correlation‐based and regression‐based sample size considerations. Therefore, the larger estimate (165 participants) was retained as the minimum required sample size. Finally, to compensate for potential attrition, non‐response, and incomplete questionnaires among hospitalized older adults, a 20% attrition rate was considered [42, 43]. Accordingly, the final target sample size was increased from 165 to 200 participants.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Participants were eligible for inclusion if they were aged 60 years or older, expressed willingness to participate in the study, and were capable of completing the questionnaires independently or with the assistance of an interviewer. Given the necessity of intact cognitive function for accurate responses, cognitive screening was conducted using the Abbreviated Mental Test (AMT) at the outset. Individuals scoring below 8 on the AMT were considered cognitively impaired and excluded from the study [44]. Furthermore, participants with severe hearing or visual impairments that interfered with answering questions, those in critical health conditions, or experiencing acute unstable illnesses were also excluded. The only exclusion criterion during the study was voluntary withdrawal at any stage of data collection.

Data Collection Procedure

After obtaining necessary ethical approvals and permissions from relevant authorities, the researcher visited the selected public hospitals. The study objectives were explained to eligible participants, and written informed consent was obtained. The questionnaires were completed either through self‐report or, if needed, by interviewing the participants, conducted by one of the researchers. Given this data collection method, there is a possibility of response bias, social desirability bias, recall bias, and interviewer bias. To minimize these biases, participants were assured that their information would remain confidential and anonymous, and validated instruments were used, and the researcher endeavored to create a calm and neutral environment to encourage honest and accurate responses.

Research Instruments

In addition to demographic data (age, gender, marital status, number of children and grandchildren, place of residence, education, occupation, and income), the following instruments were used.

Abbreviated Mental Test Score (AMTs)

This tool, originally developed by Hodkinson [45], consists of 10 items designed to assess orientation, attention, and short‐ and long‐term memory in older adults. Each correct response is awarded one point, yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 10.

A score of < 8 is widely used to indicate potential cognitive impairment, particularly in screening settings where higher sensitivity is required. Methodological guidance derived from Hodkinson's work indicates that the two most discriminating cut‐off points are 7 and 8, with a cut‐off of 8 demonstrating higher sensitivity (97%), making it suitable when the goal is early detection and minimizing false negatives [45].

In Iran, the Persian version of the AMT has undergone psychometric validation. Foroughan et al. confirmed the scale exhibited high internal consistency, with a Cronbach's alpha coefficient of 0.90. Additionally, the cut‐off scores of 6 and 7 provided an optimal balance between sensitivity (99% and 94%, respectively) and specificity (85% and 86%, respectively) [44]. Furthermore, a validation study among residents of the Kahrizak nursing home supported the use of the < 8 cut‐off, demonstrating a sensitivity of 92%, specificity of 81%, internal reliability of 0.76, and inter‐rater reliability of 0.89 [46]. Although optimal cut‐off values may vary by cultural context [44, 47], the use of locally validated thresholds is essential for ensuring accurate screening performance. Therefore, in the present study, individuals scoring < 8 on the AMT were excluded to ensure adequate cognitive ability for valid questionnaire completion. In the present study, internal consistency of the AMT was confirmed with a Cronbach's alpha of 0.84.

CASP‐19 Quality of Life Questionnaire

This questionnaire is a valid and reliable instrument designed to assess QoL in the elderly. It consists of 19 items across four dimensions: Control (4 items), Autonomy (5 items), Self‐realization (5 items), and Pleasure (5 items) [48]. This questionnaire was originally developed in the United Kingdom based on the needs satisfaction model and has since been utilized in various European countries. It has been translated into 12 languages, and numerous studies have been conducted to standardize and evaluate its psychometric properties across different countries and cultural contexts [49]. Overall, previous research has demonstrated that the CASP‐19 is a useful tool for measuring QoL in older populations [50, 51]. The questionnaire is scored on a four‐point Likert scale, ranging from 0 (never) to 3 (often), with total scores ranging from 0 to 57. Higher scores indicate a higher level of QoL [52].

The validity and reliability of the questionnaire were assessed by Nalathamby et al. in a study among Malaysian elderly individuals. The results indicated that the instrument demonstrated strong internal consistency and test–retest reliability, as well as acceptable construct validity when compared to the 12‐item Short Form Health Survey (SF‐12) [51]. The Persian version of the CASP‐19 has undergone extensive psychometric evaluation among Iranian older adults. In particular, Heravi‐Karimooi et al. [53] conducted a comprehensive validation study that provided strong evidence for the instrument's reliability and validity. Construct validity was supported through exploratory factor analysis, with sampling adequacy confirmed by acceptable to excellent Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) values and a statistically significant Bartlett's test of sphericity, indicating the suitability of the data for factor extraction [49, 53]. The extracted factors demonstrated appropriate factor loadings and a structure consistent with the theoretical dimensions of the original questionnaire [49]. Further validity evidence was established through convergent validity, demonstrated by significant correlations between the Persian CASP‐19 and the SF‐36, and through known‐groups validity, which showed that the measure successfully distinguished older adults with differing levels of functional independence [49, 53]. Reliability indices were also excellent: Heravi‐Karimooi et al. [49] reported a Cronbach's alpha of 0.97 for the total scale and values ranging from 0.93 to 0.97 across the four subscales, as well as test–retest reliability coefficients between 0.87 and 0.94.

Overall, these findings demonstrate that the Persian CASP‐19 possesses robust psychometric properties, including strong reliability and multiple forms of validity, while maintaining straightforward scoring procedures and wide applicability across clinical, home‐based, and research settings [53]. In the present study, internal consistency of the CASP‐19 was confirmed with a Cronbach's alpha of 0.82.

Sense of Coherence Scale (SOC‐13)

This questionnaire was developed by Antonovsky in 1993 [33]. This instrument contains 13 items rated on a seven‐point Likert scale and is designed to assess three subscales: comprehensibility (cognitive perception), manageability (perceived control), and meaningfulness (sense of purpose). The total score ranges from 13 to 91, with higher scores indicating a stronger SOC. Specifically, scores between 13 and 63 represent low coherence, 64–79 indicate moderate coherence, and 80–91 reflect high coherence [54]. The subscale of comprehensibility consists of items 2, 6, 8, 9, and 11; manageability includes items 3, 5, 10, and 13; and meaningfulness encompasses items 1, 4, 7, and 12 [55, 56]. Items 1, 2, 3, 7, and 10 are reverse‐scored [57].

The reliability of this instrument was assessed by Alharbi et al. in Saudi Arabia, reporting a Cronbach's alpha of 0.82, indicating good internal consistency [55]. In the study by Mafla et al., the ω, α, and CR coefficients confirmed the high internal consistency of the instrument [58].

The Persian version of the SOC‐13 was psychometrically evaluated in an Iranian sample by Mahammadzadeh et al. [59]. In that study, exploratory factor analysis supported the construct validity of the scale, and all 13 items showed strong correlations with the total score. The scale demonstrated acceptable internal consistency (Cronbach's α = 0.77), and significant correlations were observed between the subscales and the total score, confirming the reliability of the Persian SOC‐13. In the present study, the internal consistency of the SOC‐13 was calculated with a Cronbach's alpha of 0.79.

Wisdom Scale

This scale was developed by Schmit et al. It is a multidimensional measure conceptualized to assess wisdom across several dimensions, including reflective, openness, interactional aptitude, practical, paradoxical tolerance, and experience [60]. It consists of 21 items grouped into six dimensions: reflective (3 items), openness (3 items), interactive (3 items), practical (3 items), contradictory (4 items), and experiential (5 items). Items are rated on a five‐point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree) [61, 62].

For scoring, two items from the openness dimension (“I don't like art” and “I don't enjoy going to art museums”) and all items from the contradictory dimension are reverse‐scored, while the remaining items are scored positively [63]. Score ranges vary by subscale based on the number of items; for example, the contradictory subscale ranges from 4 to 20, and the experiential subscale ranges from 5 to 25. The total score for the scale ranges from 21 to 105, with higher scores indicating greater wisdom [61].

In the original study by Schmit et al., the six‐factor structure of the scale was confirmed through confirmatory factor analysis, and the Cronbach's alpha coefficient was reported at 0.75 [60]. In Iran, the psychometric properties of the Persian version of the scale were evaluated by Akbari et al. (2016), using factor analysis, convergent validity, and discriminant validity methods. The factor analysis confirmed the existence of six distinct factors. Reliability for the total scale and its subscales was confirmed using Cronbach's alpha coefficients ranging from 0.71 to 0.80, and test‐retest reliability coefficients ranging from 0.71 to 0.78 [63]. Another study by Azadmanesh et al. (2020) reported Cronbach's alpha coefficients ranging from 0.68 to 0.79 across the subscales [62], while Khalaj‐Amiri et al. (year not specified) reported an overall Cronbach's alpha of 0.928 [61]. In the present study, internal consistency was confirmed with a Cronbach's alpha of 0.82.

Data Analysis

All statistical tests were two‐tailed, and the a priori significance level was set at p < 0.05. Descriptive statistics (mean, SD, frequency, percentage) were used to summarize the sample characteristics. Pearson correlation was used to assess bivariate associations between continuous variables, and partial correlation was applied to examine these associations while controlling for socio‐demographic covariates. Multiple linear regression analysis (enter method) was conducted to identify independent predictors of QoL. Assumptions of regression, including normality, homoscedasticity, linearity, and multicollinearity (VIF and tolerance values), were examined to ensure model adequacy. All analyses were pre‐specified, and no exploratory subgroup or post‐hoc analyses were performed. Data analysis was performed using SPSS version 21.

Ethical Considerations

This research was approved by the ethics committee of Guilan University of Medical Sciences (ethics ID IR.GUMS.REC.1403.323, approval date: 2024‐10‐9) in Iran. The study was performed under the ethical standards as laid down in the Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments or comparable ethical standards. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants included in the study.

Discussion

This study investigated how two important psychological constructs wisdom and SOC influence the QoL among elderly individuals hospitalized. The findings revealed that the mean QoL score of participants was 38.10 ± 6.58, indicating a moderate level of life quality in this group. This moderate QoL may reflect a complex interplay of chronic health conditions, functional decline, and limited psychosocial resources commonly experienced by hospitalized older adults. Similar studies have demonstrated that elderly individuals with chronic illnesses or prolonged hospital stays tend to report lower QoL due to increased dependency and diminished autonomy. For instance, a 12‐month study on post‐discharge elderly patients found that those without prolonged hospitalization showed improvements in health‐related QoL, while extended stays were linked to gradual deterioration [64]. This underscores the impact of hospitalization duration on patients' recovery trajectories. Additionally, Lee et al., using data from the Korean National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey, reported significantly lower HRQoL in elderly individuals with multimorbidity, especially when cardiovascular diseases coexisted with arthritis [65]. These patterns are aligned with our findings and highlight the need to consider disease clustering and functional impairment in QoL assessments.

Although some international studies report relatively favorable QoL scores among community‐dwelling older adults, such differences may stem from variations in sample characteristics (hospitalized vs. non‐hospitalized), cultural contexts, and the availability of social support. Hospital‐attending older adults are inherently more vulnerable due to physical limitations, mental health challenges, and reduced social engagement. Despite these limitations, the moderate QoL observed in our sample suggests the potential presence of psychological resilience, which may partially buffer against the detrimental effects of illness.

The average wisdom score was 35.75 ± 8.81, suggesting a moderate level of wisdom. This is consistent with findings that indicate wisdom may decline slightly with age, particularly in the presence of hospitalization, cognitive strain, and reduced social roles. Some research suggests that wisdom peaks in midlife and diminishes thereafter [66, 67, 68], whereas others argue that specific components such as emotional regulation and empathy may increase in late adulthood [69]. These discrepancies likely stem from differences in measurement tools and the cognitive health status of participants. Neurodegenerative processes may impair aspects of wisdom like mental flexibility, but other facets may be preserved [70].

The mean SOC score was 88.52 ± 10.84, which is relatively low compared to other elderly populations. According to Antonovsky's salutogenic theory, SOC includes comprehensibility, manageability, and meaningfulness—all of which can be undermined during hospitalization. The illness experience may challenge one's ability to make sense of health changes, cope effectively, and find meaning, particularly when independence is reduced. International reviews also support this theory; for example, a meta‐analysis indicated that lower SOC is associated with higher levels of pain, depression, and poor sleep [71]. A Dutch longitudinal study confirmed that SOC generally remains stable but may slightly decline in late life, particularly under health stressors [72].

Our study found significant positive correlations between both wisdom and SOC with QoL, even after controlling for demographic confounders (e.g., age, gender, marital status, residence, education, income, and history of illness). This reinforces the idea that these psychological resources serve as protective factors for well‐being. Previous research supports these associations, emphasizing the mediating role of variables such as resilience, depression, self‐esteem, and life purpose. For example, Chen et al. reported a significant association between wisdom and QoL among Chinese older adults, with self‐esteem and depression acting as mediators [73]. Indati et al. identified wisdom, particularly its emotional component as a strong predictor of life satisfaction [74]. Similarly, Hayat et al. demonstrated the mediating role of resilience in the relationship between wisdom and life satisfaction [75]. Ardelt and Edwards emphasized the importance of reflective wisdom and the sense of life purpose in enhancing QoL among older adults in care settings [27].

Regarding SOC, several independent studies have confirmed its predictive value for QoL. Komeili et al. found that SOC significantly predicted QoL among the elderly [76]. Tan et al. showed that manageability and meaningfulness, two key components of SOC, were positively associated with QoL in institutionalized older adults [77]. Asgari et al. also reported a positive relationship between internal coherence and QoL in elderly individuals [37]. Dymecka et al. extended these findings to patients with multiple sclerosis, identifying SOC as a buffer against reduced QoL [78]. However, Gerasimcik‐Pulko et al. did not find a significant association between SOC and QoL among breast cancer patients, suggesting that disease type and social support may influence this relationship [79].

The multiple regression model in the present study showed that SOC, wisdom, and demographic variables together explained 41.5% of the variance in QoL, indicating a substantial psychological contribution. These results support the idea that psychological constructs such as SOC and wisdom could be systematically included in geriatric assessments as predictors of QoL and potential intervention targets. Rakizadeh and Hafezi demonstrated that all SOC components significantly correlated with various QoL dimensions in students [80]. Alcantara et al. found that psychological well‐being was a stronger predictor of SOC than physical functioning [81]. Kristofferzon et al. reported that SOC had both direct and indirect effects on psychological QoL in chronically ill patients [82]. Vähäaho et al. also confirmed a strong relationship between SOC and QoL in breast cancer survivors [83]. Wisdom, likewise, has been consistently recognized as a contributor to health, social competence, and psychological well‐being. Chen et al. emphasized that enhancing wisdom can lead to better QoL in older adults [73]. Indati et al. concluded that emotional aspects of wisdom play the most crucial role in satisfaction with life in late adulthood [74].

The results of the present study showed that homemakers and employees reported significantly lower QoL compared to retired individuals. This finding may be attributed to several factors. For homemakers, the lack of formal social integration outside the home and potential isolation in old age could negatively impact well‐being. For employees, the stress associated with continuing to work in old age, or perhaps the contrast between their current hospitalized state and their previous active professional roles, might contribute to lower QoL. Conversely, the retired group, having already transitioned out of the workforce, may have adapted better to their current health status, enjoying the benefits of retirement income and leisure time. This highlights the importance of considering the psychosocial transition associated with retirement versus the burdens of other roles when designing geriatric care interventions.

Given the findings of this study, several practical recommendations can be proposed. First, psychosocial interventions aimed at enhancing SOC should be integrated into geriatric care plans. These interventions may focus on increasing the elderly's sense of comprehensibility through clear communication, strengthening manageability by teaching coping and problem‐solving strategies, and reinforcing meaningfulness by facilitating social, spiritual, or creative engagement. Second, structured programs aimed at cultivating wisdom, such as life review therapy, emotional regulation training, and decision‐making skills workshops, may also help older adults cope more effectively with the stressors of illness and hospitalization. By simultaneously addressing both constructs, such interventions can foster psychological resilience and improve perceived QoL in elderly individuals.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be taken into account when interpreting the results. First, its cross‐sectional design prevents the establishment of causal relationships between wisdom, SOC, and QoL. Longitudinal studies are required to track how these psychological variables evolve over time and how they may influence one another in a causal framework. Second, the sample consisted exclusively of elderly individuals who were referred to a hospital setting. As such, the findings may not be generalizable to community‐dwelling older adults, those residing in nursing homes, or other subpopulations. Future research should explore these variables across diverse contexts to enhance external validity. Third, data collection relied on self‐reported questionnaires, which are subject to potential measurement biases, including social desirability, recall errors, and interviewer influence. Although measures were taken to support participants with limited literacy or health conditions, incorporating mixed methods such as qualitative interviews, behavioral assessments, or informant reports could strengthen the credibility and richness of the data. Fourth, while the regression model explained 41.5% of the variance in QoL, a substantial proportion remained unaccounted for. Including additional psychological (e.g., coping styles), social (e.g., perceived support), and contextual (e.g., income level, cultural background, hospital type, illness severity) variables in future models may yield a more comprehensive understanding of QoL determinants in the elderly. Lastly, the study was conducted within a specific cultural and regional context in northern Iran, which may limit the transferability of findings. Cross‐cultural research is recommended to determine whether the observed relationships hold in other settings.

Conclusion

The findings of this study indicate that wisdom and SOC, as two key psychological factors, play a significant role in predicting the QoL among elderly individuals referred to the hospital. Moreover, the results of multiple regression analysis demonstrated that both SOC and wisdom have a positive and significant impact on QoL. In addition, demographic factors such as occupation, history of illness, age, and gender were also found to contribute meaningfully to the explanation of QoL among older adults. These results highlight the importance of interventions aimed at enhancing wisdom and SOC in the elderly as a critical step toward improving their QoL. Furthermore, consideration of demographic characteristics and physical health status is essential in designing effective intervention programs. Given the specific challenges faced by elderly individuals dealing with health issues, strengthening psychological factors and providing social support can lead to improvements in their overall well‐being. Ultimately, this research suggests that wisdom and SOC not only help older adults cope with life's challenges but also serve as protective factors in promoting their QoL. Therefore, investing in programs that foster these psychological resources may contribute significantly to the health and well‐being of the elderly population.
