Unveiling α‐MoO3 as a High‐Index, Low‐Loss Anisotropic Material: Bridging the Gap From Mid‐Infrared to Ultraviolet Nanophotonics
Authors: Adilet N. Toksumakov, Dmitriy V. Grudinin, Georgy A. Ermolaev, Alexey P. Tsapenko, Anton A. Minnekhanov, Konstantin V. Kravtsov, Nikolay V. Pak, Arslan B. Mazitov, Andrey A. Vyshnevyy, Aleksandr S. Slavich, Ivan A. Kruglov, Gleb I. Tselikov, Aleksey V. Arsenin, Kostya S. Novoselov, Valentyn S. Volkov
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTModern nanophotonics demands light manipulation at a subwavelength scale, a capability historically constrained by the isotropic dielectric response of conventional optical semiconductors. Here, we establish α‐MoO3 as a high‐index, low‐loss anisotropic material that bridges the gap between mid‐infrared polaritonics and short‐wavelength nanophotonics. Integrating spectroscopic ellipsometry, first‐principles calculations, and near‐field optical microscopy, we determine its broadband 250–1700 nm anisotropic optical constants and combine them with a phonon model in mid‐infrared to 30 µm. While the mid‐infrared phonon polariton properties of α‐MoO3 are well established in the literature, the present work provides the first experimental characterization of its complete anisotropic dielectric tensor in the ultraviolet‐to‐near‐infrared regime, conceptually bridging these two spectral domains in which this material finds application. The pronounced out‐of‐plane birefringence 0.35 suppresses evanescent crosstalk beyond competing van der Waals (vdW) waveguide materials, and the high in‐plane anisotropy enables zero‐order waveplates at wavelengths as short as 325 nm. As a practical demonstration, we prepared nanowaveguide which exhibits wavelength‐selective light guidance at visible range. Together, these results reposition α‐MoO3 from a mid‐infrared specific material to a versatile wide‐bandgap platform bridging mid‐infrared polaritonics and ultraviolet‐visible nanophotonics.

Introduction

The emergence of van der Waals (vdW) materials has significantly influenced the development of modern photonics. While cubic semiconductors like silicon [1], gallium phosphide [2], and titanium dioxide [3] serve as foundational platforms for modern optics, their isotropic dielectric response restricts the degree of freedom in controlling light polarization and electromagnetic field localization. Early vdW photonics focused on high‐symmetry hexagonal lattices [4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12], which, despite excellent integration capabilities [13, 14], remain optically isotropic within their basal planes. The scope of van der Waals photonics has expanded to encompass low‐symmetry crystal systems (orthorhombic, monoclinic, triclinic), where a dielectric tensor (εxx ≠ εyy ≠ εzz ) facilitates the realization of hyperbolic phonon polaritons [15, 16, 17] and direction‐dependent refractive indices [18, 19, 20]. This intrinsic anisotropy enables various functionalities, such as biosensing [21, 22], waveplates [23, 24, 25, 26], chirality‐preserving mirrors [7, 27, 28], and sub‐diffractional light guiding [7, 29, 30, 31, 32] without the requirement for complex lithographic patterning. However, many highly anisotropic materials are characterized by a fundamental trade‐off between dissipative losses in the visible (VIS) spectral range and relatively low refractive indices, which can restrict effective optical confinement [33].

While alpha‐phase of molybdenum trioxide (α‐MoO3) is well‐documented as a platform for mid‐infrared (MIR) hyperbolic phonon polaritons [34, 35], its optical behavior in the short‐wavelength limit has received less systematic attention. Despite its wide bandgap (E g > 2.9 eV) and recent advancements in the large‐scale synthesis of thin films with out‐of‐plane anisotropy [36, 37, 38], comprehensive experimental data on its anisotropic complex refractive index remain limited. Consequently, currently available optical constants often rely on isotropic thin‐film approximations [36, 37] or theoretical predictions [39], which constrain the precise development of α‐MoO3‐based polarization‐sensitive devices in the visible regime [40, 41, 42].

In this work, we present α‐MoO3 as a high‐index platform that connects mid‐infrared polaritonic functionalities with visible‐range all‐dielectric nanophotonics. By integrating spectroscopic ellipsometry on exfoliated flakes with first‐principles calculations and micro‐reflection spectroscopy, we resolve the broadband (250–1700 nm) anisotropic optical response of α‐MoO3. The material exhibits a high refractive index (n > 2.4 in the VIS and n > 3.8 in the ultraviolet (UV) regions) alongside a broad transparency window and pronounced in‐ and out‐of‐plane birefringence reaching 0.1 and 0.35, respectively. These results are further corroborated by scattering‐type scanning near‐field optical microscopy (s‐SNOM), which allows for the direct visualization of directional waveguide modes. A comparative analysis with other van der Waals materials confirms that α‐MoO3 enables ultra‐compact waveguide architectures with suppressed crosstalk. Thus, we experimentally cover the 250–1700 nm range and show that the same material possesses exceptional optical properties across a far broader spectral window, from the ultraviolet to the near‐infrared, thereby connecting its established MIR identity with a class of applications—visible and UV integrated nanophotonics.

Results

Structural Anisotropy of α‐MoO3

To establish a rigorous framework for the broadband optical characterization of α‐MoO3, we first analyze its crystal structure and determine the crystallographic orientation of the exfoliated flakes. α‐MoO3 crystal lattice has the orthorhombic space group Pmcn (No. 62) and lattice parameters a = 3.70 Å, b = 3.96 Å, and c = 13.86 Å [43, 44, 45]. The crystal structure consists of distorted MoO6 octahedra, where each Mo6+ ion is coordinated by six oxygen atoms [46]. These octahedra share corners and edges to form sheets stacked along the c‐axis, held together by weak van der Waals interactions that facilitate mechanical exfoliation. In our experimental configuration, the a‐ and b‐axes lie within the plane of the flake, while the c‐axis is perpendicular to the layers (Figure 1a).

This atomic‐scale structural anisotropy manifests macroscopically as a birefringence, clearly visible in polarization‐resolved optical microscopy. Figure 1b displays optical micrographs of a mechanically exfoliated α‐MoO3 flake on a Si/SiO2 substrate. As the sample is rotated from 0° to 90° relative to the incident linear polarization, the flake exhibits a dramatic color shift, providing visual evidence of the stark contrast in the refractive index along the principal in‐plane axes.

To unambiguously verify the crystallographic orientation and phase purity, we performed angle‐resolved polarized Raman (ARPR) spectroscopy measurements and ab initio calculations (see Methods for details). The Raman spectrum is dominated by the high‐frequency modes associated with terminal Mo─O bonds, while the lower‐frequency region reflects collective vibrations of the octahedral framework and interlayer interactions [14, 47]. These modes correspond to the Raman‐active vibrations allowed by the D2h point group symmetry. The experimental and theoretical ARPR spectroscopy heatmaps are shown in Figure 1c,d, together with corresponding spectra along the a and b axes (Figure 1e,f). The experimental results show excellent agreement with our theoretical predictions, confirming the high crystalline quality of the samples. Consistent with Raman spectroscopic characterization and previous reports [14, 47, 48], A g vibrational modes at 156 and 818 cm−1 and B 1g mode at 282 cm−1 are used to identify the crystallographic orientation of α‐MoO3. The intensity of these modes depends on the alignment of the crystallographic axes, enabling lattice indexing for the integration of α‐MoO3 into anisotropic optoelectronic structures.

Anisotropic Dielectric Function of α‐MoO3

Having established the crystallographic orientation of the flakes, we next quantify the anisotropic dielectric function ɛ = ɛ 1 + iɛ 2 of α‐MoO3 across the ultraviolet‐near infrared (NIR) spectral range (250–1700 nm), where ɛ 1 and ɛ 2 denote the real and imaginary parts of the dielectric function, respectively. Moreover, the principal optical axes [18] have been confirmed utilizing Mueller matrix measurements (see Methods for details). We performed spectroscopic ellipsometry on mechanically exfoliated flakes transferred onto a silicon substrate. The samples were rotated to align the incident plane of the light parallel to the crystallographic a and b axes. Configuration allows to minimize cross‐polarization, therefore leading to the measurement of standard ellipsometric parameters Ψ and ∆ instead of the Mueller matrix. The resulting parameters of the flake (thickness = 483 nm) at different incident angles are presented in Figure 2a,b. The validity of Ψ, Δ parameterization was further confirmed by performing Mueller matrix measurements for 11 elements (M12, M13, M14, M21, M22, M23, M24, M31, M32, M33, M34) at visible range and verifying that the off‐diagonal elements M13, M14, M23, M24, M31, M32 were close to zero at the assigned axis orientations (details are presented in Supporting Note S1). The solid lines denote the measured data, while the dashed lines represent the fit curves (see Methods for details). Distinct oscillatory behavior is observed in both Ψ and ∆ spectra, indicating strong optical anisotropy and multiple interband transitions.

The optical profile of α‐MoO3 places it within a class of high‐index, low‐loss layered materials exhibiting a refractive index comparable to widely utilized and potentially scalable benchmarks such as MoS2 [9, 10], hBN [7], TiO2 [3, 51], calcite [51], and gallium chalcogenides (GaP [2], GaS [52]), as shown in Figure 3d. While α‐MoO3 does not claim record‐breaking absolute refractive index values, its true distinction, and the motivation for its use in nanophotonics emerges from in‐plane and out‐of‐plane anisotropies. Particularly, it demonstrates the largest in‐plane anisotropy among transparent layered materials within the 425–500 nm spectral range as shown in Supporting Note S5. The pronounced in‐plane optical anisotropy renders α‐MoO3 a highly promising platform for waveplate applications. The use of strongly anisotropic materials in waveplates enables unique functionality yet results in polarization‐dependent transmittance (linear dichroism) and wavelength selectivity. Therefore, to convert linearly polarization into circular, the incident beam's polarization plane should form an angle of arctanTyTx with the optical principal axis y. The optical losses and dichroism of α‐MoO3 waveplates with various thicknesses were systematically evaluated in Supporting Note S6. Notably, the waveplates considered here are substantially thinner than those reported previously [53]. In addition, α‐MoO3 exhibits a large out‐of‐plane birefringence, reaching values up to 0.35 in the visible spectral range, which highlights its strong potential for waveguiding and integrated photonic applications.

To provide a comprehensive view of the dielectric response from the UV to the MIR (250–30 000 nm), the measured data were combined with previously reported [54] infrared dielectric function. The two datasets were merged solely for broadband visualization. Care was taken to ensure spectral consistency and physical continuity at the stitching boundary (at 1700 nm), where excellent agreement was observed. No artificial fitting procedures were involved beyond ensuring smooth spectral overlap (details in Supporting Note S7). This broadband representation enables systematic analysis of dispersion, optical anisotropy, and polaritonic behavior across multiple spectral regimes. The consolidation of these datasets establishes α‐MoO3 as a versatile platform for nanophotonics. Moreover, we compared optical constants from different reports, including ab initio calculations [39] and thin films [36, 37] in Supporting Note S8.

The confluence of a high refractive index, negligible dissipative losses, and dual‐axis birefringence transforms α‐MoO3 into a unique medium for light manipulation. This combination is advantageous for waveguiding applications, where the material's anisotropy can be leveraged to achieve direction‐dependent cut offs of fundamental TE and TM modes [29]. When coupled with the recent progress in the large‐scale synthesis of α‐MoO3 films [36, 37, 38], these properties position the material as a promising candidate for short‐wavelength integrated photonic circuits.

Waveguiding in α‐MoO3 was first visualized at λ = 710 nm using far‐field optical imaging (Figure 4c). In this experiment, a focused laser beam was directed onto the edge of elongated α‐MoO3 flakes, while scattered light was monitored at the opposite end of the crystal. The appearance of a bright scattering spot at the distant flake edge indicates that the incident light couples into modes propagating along the flake before being scattered back into the far field.

To probe the optical response in greater detail, we performed mapping of the complex near‐field signal at longer wavelengths (see other wavelength images and flakes measurements are presented in Supporting Note S9). Figure 4d shows representative near‐field amplitude and phase images acquired over 260 nm thick flake. Multiple sets of interference fringes emanate from flake edges, with spatially periodic phase fronts clearly visible across both polarizations and thickness regimes. The mechanism underlying fringe formation was further elucidated via line profile extraction perpendicular to the flake boundary (Figure 4e). Both amplitude and phase traces exhibit oscillatory decay with well‐defined spatial frequencies, consistent with interference between counter‐propagating in‐plane guided waves. Figure 4f shows the result of complex Fourier transforms of these profiles. The zero‐frequency peak in the FFT corresponds to the background signal, and other smaller peaks correspond to the transverse magnetic and transverse electric modes scattered on opposite edges of the sample. In the s‐SNOM experiment, the spatial frequencies of the observed fringes are shifted from modal wavenumbers. Consequently, the TM0 modes propagating in opposite directions produce fringes of two very different apparent effective indices as seen from their respective FFT peaks in Figure 4f. We compared the extracted effective indices to optical mode dispersion relations computed via transfer‐matrix simulations incorporating the full dielectric tensor of α‐MoO3 (Figure 4g,h). Experimental data points, extracted across the two flake thicknesses and three excitation wavelengths, align closely with simulated TE0 and TM0 branches. The agreement between experimental and theoretical dispersion characteristics underscores the reliability of our near‐field approach for characterizing waveguiding in α‐MoO3. Moreover, it also verifies the dielectric tensor retrieved from far‐field experimental methods. Waveguide Simulations

To evaluate the potential of α‐MoO3 for dense vdW photonic integration, we performed numerical simulations of waveguides based on the extracted optical constants. While the refractive index n of α‐MoO3 is lower than that of other high‐index materials such as GaS, MoS2, and GaP (as shown in Figure 3d), its pronounced out‐of‐plane birefringence offers advantages for light manipulation. We compared α‐MoO3 with transparent, anisotropic high‐index vdW materials, including MoS2, GaS, and hBN. All simulations were performed at the shortest wavelength operation in a low‐loss regime. A schematic illustration is presented in Figure 5a. Waveguide confinement was analyzed using square‐cross‐section waveguides fully embedded in SiO2 (Figure 5b). For each material, the waveguide width (w) was chosen such that the exponential decay constant (χ) of the evanescent field into the SiO2 cladding was fixed at χ = 2.22 µm−1, providing a consistent basis for comparing modal confinement across materials operating at different wavelengths. The mentioned decay constant χ is defined as neff2−ns2/λ, where n eff is the effective index of the mode, n s is the refractive index of the waveguide cladding, and λ is the operating wavelength.

To corroborate these simulations experimentally, we prepared a proof‐of‐concept device: an α‐MoO3 nanowaveguide with a width of 530 nm, a height of 70 nm, and a length of L = 20.1 µm on a SiO2 (285 nm)/Si substrate (Figure 5c–e). A laser beam was focused onto the input facet of the waveguide, and the scattered light was collected at the opposite facet. At λ = 532 nm, a bright output spot appears at the distant facet (Figure 5f), evidencing guided propagation along the entire structure. By contrast, at λ = 785 nm no output is observed (Figure 5g). This wavelength‐selective response is reproduced by transmission simulations of the studied geometry (Figure 5h): at 532 nm, the waveguide supports a well‐confined TE0 mode, whereas at longer wavelengths the mode approaches cutoff and delocalizes from the waveguide core, suppressing the transmission at 785 nm. Thus, even a sub‐100‐nm‐thick α‐MoO3 nanowaveguide routes visible light over tens of micrometers, providing a device‐level validation of the extracted optical constants and waveguide simulations.

Although α‐MoO3 possesses a lower refractive index than GaS and MoS2 (Figure 3d), its high intrinsic birefringence facilitates enhanced light manipulation. For comparison, the out‐of‐plane anisotropy of these materials in their respective transparency regions is summarized in Figure 5c. GaS exhibits a higher refractive index, but lower anisotropy compared to α‐MoO3, whereas hBN displays a lower refractive index alongside higher anisotropy. MoS2 surpasses α‐MoO3 in both parameters, but its operation is restricted to longer wavelengths. The integration density of photonic circuits is ultimately limited by evanescent coupling between neighboring waveguides. To quantify this effect, the full power transfer (crosstalk) length (L ct) was calculated as a function of the separation distance (d) between adjacent waveguides (Figure 5j). In these simulations, the waveguides were positioned on a SiO2 substrate with air cladding, and both the width and height of the waveguides were optimized for each separation distance to achieve the highest crosstalk distance in the single‐mode regime. All calculations were performed at wavelengths where the extinction coefficient satisfies k < 0.01, corresponding to the onset of absorption for each respective material.

The results demonstrate that for a fixed acceptable crosstalk threshold, α‐MoO3 allows for smaller waveguide separations and higher achievable integration density. These findings highlight the suitability of α‐MoO3 for compact, low‐loss, and densely integrated planar vdW photonic circuits. This capability allows for a substantial reduction in the physical footprint of waveguide structures while maintaining minimal signal interference, positioning α‐MoO3 as a building block for ultra‐compact, high‐density planar photonic circuits in the visible regime.

Discussion

The central finding of this work is a fundamental repositioning of α‐MoO3 within the photonic landscape. Prior to this study, the material was considered almost through its mid‐infrared identity [42, 54]. The ultraviolet and visible ranges had remained uncharacterized despite being the spectral window where α‐MoO3 reaches its highest refractive indices and strongest birefringence. Our results reveal that the same crystal supporting phonon polaritons at 10 µm simultaneously delivers n > 3.9 in the UV, n > 2.6 in the visible, and the largest in‐plane birefringence among transparent layered materials at 425–500 nm. GW first‐principles calculations, applied here for the first time to α‐MoO3 with spin‐orbit coupling, correctly reproduce the optical constants. The unified broadband dielectric tensor spanning 250 nm to 30 µm, constructed by combining our new UV‐to‐NIR data with established MIR phonon models [54], is the first continuous optical portrait of this material from the ultraviolet to the terahertz.

These findings open application domains inaccessible without the UV optical constants. The monotonically increasing in‐plane birefringence enables zero‐order quarter‐wave plates at 325–375 nm using sub‐200‐nm‐thick flakes, extending visible‐frequency demonstrations into the near‐UV. As a practical demonstration we prepared nanowaveguide (70 nm × 530 nm) which demonstrates wavelength‐selective guidance at visible range with quantitative agreement between experiment and simulation, confirming that the extracted dielectric tensor is accurate at the device level. From an integration standpoint, the large out‐of‐plane birefringence (0.35) suppresses evanescent crosstalk beyond competing van der Waals waveguide materials at equivalent waveguide separations, making α‐MoO3 architecturally advantageous for densely integrated visible photonic circuits. Together with recent advances in large‐scale α‐MoO3 synthesis [36, 37, 38], these results demonstrate the feasibility of wide‐bandgap van der Waals photonic circuits spanning the ultraviolet through the near‐infrared.

Experimental Section

Sample Preparation

Samples with α‐MoO3 flakes were prepared by mechanically exfoliating the bulk α‐MoO3 crystal (acquired from 2D Semiconductors, Inc.) onto the desired substrate (silicon, Si/SiO2, glass). An optical microscope Nikon ECLIPSE LV150N was employed to visually identify exfoliated flakes of suitable size.

First‐Principles Calculations

Calculations of optical and dynamical properties were performed using the Vienna ab initio package (VASP) [55, 56]. For these calculations, we used experimental crystal structure parameters of α‐MoO3 crystal [45]. To calculate optical properties, we first obtained the one‐electron ground‐state wavefunctions from a single‐shot DFT calculation. Crucially, spin‐orbit coupling (SOC) was explicitly included in this step to accurately account for relativistic effects associated with the heavy transition metal (Mo) atoms. These wavefunctions were then used to initialize the GW run. Next, we calculated the imaginary and real parts of the frequency dependent dielectric function within the many‐body GW approximation and derived the refractive indices and extinction coefficients of the material. The cutoff energy for the plane wave basis was set to 550 eV, while the first Brillouin zone was sampled with a 𝛤‐centered 6 × 2 × 6 grid. The exchange correlation effects were described with a generalized gradient approximation (Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof functional, PBE‐xc), and the behavior of the wavefunctions in the core region was reconstructed with the projector augmented wave pseudopotentials of a GW type. We used 504 energy bands in the DFT and GW calculations.

The second‐order force constants were calculated for a 3 × 3 × 1 (144 atoms) supercell with an atomic displacement value of 0.01 Å using a non‐analytical term correction to the dynamical matrix. K‐meshes of 13 × 12 × 4 and 2 × 2 × 2 divisions, respectively, were used for the primitive cell and the α‐MoO3 supercell. The convergence parameter of the self‐consistent electron cycle (EDIFF) was 10−8 eV. The PBE xc‐functional, together with Grimme‐D3 correction, was used for all calculations of Raman spectra. The kinetic energy cutoff for the basis set of plane waves was chosen to be 550 eV. DFPT was used to calculate the static dielectric tensor. Intensities were computed based on the Phonopy‐Spectroscopy code with our modifications [57]. The widths of all computed peaks were chosen to be 5 cm−1.

Angle‐Resolved Polarized Raman Spectroscopy

ARPR‐spectroscopy measurements of the samples were performed using an alpha300 RA confocal Raman‐AFM microscope (WITec, Ulm, Germany). Raman spectra were acquired with the 100× Zeiss EC Epiplan‐Neofluar objective (0.9, Differential Interference Contrast (DIC)). A 532 nm laser with a power of 1 mW was used for sample excitation, yielding a power density of approximately 14 mW/µm2 (a spot diameter of ∼300 nm). ARPR spectra were recorded at 5° intervals over a complete 360° rotation (72 angles were recorded in total) using the parallel configuration of the ARPR system (i.e., with the polarizer aligned parallel to the analyzer). A grating with 1200 lines/mm was employed, and the backscattered light was detected using a back‐illuminated deep‐depletion Charge‐Coupled Device (CCD) cooled to −60°C, yielding a registered spectral resolution of approximately 0.5 cm−1. Each spectrum was acquired with an integration time of 5 s per angle. Spectral processing was performed using WITec Project 7 software.

Spectroscopic Ellipsometry

To analyze the anisotropic in‐plane optical constants of α‐MoO3 flakes, we utilized the Accurion EP4 imaging spectroscopic ellipsometer (Park Systems GmbH, Göttingen) in the rotating compensator mode. The measurements of the flakes were performed with the plane of incidence aligned with the crystallographic a‐axis and b‐axis, respectively, which allowed for quasi‐isotropic optical recording of Ψ and Δ. The orientation of crystallographic axes of the flakes was determined and confirmed using Mueller matrix measurements at 400, 650, and 900 nm, done with a ∼5° step over a complete 360° rotation of the table where the sample with the flake was installed. Once, for the table rotation value, the measured Mueller indices: (M13, M14), (M23, M24), (M31, M32) are equal to 0 than any other table rotation value, that angle was assigned as the first axis (and that axis orientation was additionally confirmed with the axis orientation using ARPR spectroscopy). The second axis was assigned by rotating the table 90° clockwise (or anticlockwise). The final assignment of the crystallographic a‐axis and b‐axis to the determined axes is done during the modeling step, after the spectroscopic ellipsometry measurements are done. Ellipsometry spectra of Ψ and Δ were recorded in the spectral range from 250 nm to 1700 nm for three incident angles: 45°, 50°, and 55°.

Polarization‐Resolved Reflectance

The experimental setup for polarization‐resolved reflectance mapping was based on a trinocular microscope (RX50M), which was modified by the polarizer and an analyzer [58]. Data were recorded across the 550–850 nm wavelength range. An 20× magnification objective with a numerical aperture of 0.5 (SOPTOP MPlanFL) was utilized to collect the reflected signal from a spot size of <20 µm. The collected signal was directed from one of the oculars to a splitting system that coupled the light to a camera and a spectrometer (Optosky ATP5020P). An optical fiber with a 100 µm core diameter (Thorlabs M96L02) was used to connect the spectrometer. All measurements were conducted at room temperature. The spectral data were processed, and the in‐plane principal optical axes were identified according to the methods [59].

Atomic Force Microscopy

The thicknesses of the studied α‐MoO3 flakes were determined using atomic force microscopy. Measurements were performed in tapping mode with a WITec alpha300 RA system equipped with a NanoWorld ARROW‐FMR probe (resonant frequency 75 kHz; spring constant 2.8 N/m). The scanning speed, scan size, and resolution were tailored to each study area to ensure optimal visualization quality. AFM image processing was conducted using Gwyddion software.

Scattering‐Type Scanning Near‐Field Optical Microscopy

Near‐field optical measurements were performed using a commercial s‐SNOM system (NeaSNOM, Neaspec GmbH, Germany) operated in reflection mode. The system utilizes an atomic force microscopy (AFM) tip illuminated by a focused illumination source, collecting the scattered near‐field signal for amplitude and phase‐resolved imaging with a spatial resolution of ∼20 nm. A single off‐axis parabolic mirror was employed for both focusing the incident light onto the probe and collecting the backscattered near‐field signal. The probe consisted of platinum‐coated silicon tips (ARROW‐NCPt‐50), oscillating vertically at a frequency of approximately 280 kHz with an amplitude of ∼80 nm. Two distinct illumination sources were used as the excitation sources in two separate experimental series: (1) a tunable monochromatic NIR‐laser (1475–1600 nm) operated at six discrete wavelengths with 25 nm step was utilized for the sample with thickness of ∼850 nm, and (2) a Ti:sapphire (titanium‐doped sapphire) femtosecond laser system (TiC, AVESTA) tunable from 700 nm to 1000 nm, and operated at discrete wavelengths of 710, 850, and 1000 nm, was used for measurements of the sample with thickness of ∼260 nm. Near‐field signals were extracted by demodulating the detector output at higher harmonics (1 Ω–4 Ω) of the tip oscillation frequency; all reported data correspond to the third harmonic (3Ω), effectively suppressing far‐field background contributions. Signal detection was carried out using a pseudo‐heterodyne interferometric scheme, in which the reference beam was phase‐modulated by an oscillating mirror to enable phase‐resolved acquisition of the near‐field amplitude and phase.

Numerical Calculations for Waveguides

We performed numerical calculations using a previously established method [60], combining COMSOL Multiphysics and MATLAB through a live link. All simulations used a simple 2D geometry in COMSOL as the solver, while MATLAB handled geometry optimization. Specifically, we used a binary search algorithm to determine the waveguide width for a constant χ, and a modified gradient descent algorithm to optimize waveguide widths for maximum crosstalk length at a fixed core spacing.
