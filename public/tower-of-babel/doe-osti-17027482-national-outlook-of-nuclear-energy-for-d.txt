 SUMMARY
 This report assesses the potential for nuclear energy to support the rapidly growing electricity demand
of U.S. data centers, particularly hyperscale and artificial-intelligence facilities. It updates the FY25
national outlook by examining data center demand projections, nuclear capacity availability, deployment
timelines, fuel requirements, and the technical, and socioeconomic implications of nuclear-powered data
centers applied to four case studies.
 U.S. data center average electricity demand is projected to increase by 12–84 GW by 2030 according
to various sources. Existing nuclear plants, power uprates, reactor restarts, and announced new reactor
projects could provide approximately 6 GW of capacity in the near term, with potentially 33 GW by 2035
and 50 GW by 2040 if current plans and announcements are realized. These estimates indicate that
nuclear energy could make an important contribution to data center expansion, although new nuclear
construction is generally unlikely to satisfy the earliest demand: interim power from the existing grid,
natural gas, energy storage, and demand-side flexibility will therefore be required during phased nuclear
deployment.
 The report also evaluates the potential fuel-cycle implications of nuclear deployment driven by data-
center demand. Under the modeled light-water reactor power-uprate scenarios (most of them, but not all,
are currently motivated by energy demand from data centers), additional annual requirements could reach
approximately 1,900 metric tons of natural uranium and 1,850 metric tons of separative work units,
representing increases of approximately 9% and 12%, respectively, relative to the 2024 U.S. fleet
baseline. A substantial portion of this increase would require uranium enriched above the conventional
5% threshold, or LEU+. Announced advanced-reactor deployment scenarios associated with Amazon,
Google, and Meta could require approximately 380 metric tons of high-assay low-enriched uranium
(HALEU normalized to 19.75% enrichment) by 2040.
 Four representative case studies are analyzed: brownfield deployment at the Rockport coal plant site
in Indiana, greenfield deployment in Texas, a light-water reactor uprate at Palo Verde, and a light-water
reactor restart at Three-Mile Island Nuclear Generating Station. It is noted that the sites and power plants
used in this work were selected for case study only, not intended to represent planned deployment of
nuclear plants or data centers at those sites. The cases illustrate a tradeoff between deployment speed,
capacity scale, capital requirements, and project risk. Uprates and restarts could provide hundreds of
megawatts within approximately two to six years but are constrained in terms of site and project size by
the existing reactor fleet. Brownfield and greenfield projects offer greater potential for gigawatt-scale
expansion but require longer development periods, larger investments, and more extensive infrastructure.
Modeled cost-based power purchase agreement prices range from approximately $37 to $92 per
megawatt-hour across the cases. These values represent cost floors under specified assumptions, rather
than expected market prices, and do not fully include transmission and distribution charges, interim-
power costs, or value-based premiums associated with contract negotiation.
 The case studies also demonstrate the importance of site-specific conditions, including water
availability, cooling technology, contiguous land, transmission capacity, fiber connectivity, transportation
infrastructure, workforce availability, and regional grid conditions. The socioeconomic assessment
indicates substantial potential employment and economic benefits during both construction and operation
of the nuclear and data center facilities in all case studies. Overall, the results support continued
evaluation of nuclear energy as one component of a broader, flexible energy strategy for data centers.

 SYSTEMS ANALYSIS & INTEGRATION

 NATIONAL OUTLOOK OF NUCLEAR ENERGY FOR
 DATA CENTERS AND CASE STUDY ANALYSES

1. INTRODUCTION
 In 2025, the U.S. Department of Energy’s Office of Nuclear Energy (DOE-NE) Systems Analysis and
Integration (SA&I) campaign initiated an assessment of the compatibility of nuclear energy with
powering the digital infrastructure and more specifically, datacenters (DC). This initial study was
documented in a technical report [1] that examined projected energy demand from digital infrastructure,
various types of grid connections and their economic implications, as well as siting and socioeconomic
aspects. The present report builds on that foundation by providing a more comprehensive outlook for
nuclear-powered data centers and by analyzing a range of deployment case studies. It is noted that
specific sites and power plants used in this work were selected for case study only, without specific plans
for deployment of nuclear plants and data centers at the sites. The primary purpose of this report is to
inform a wide range of policy, scientific, and industrial stakeholders on the technical, economic, and
socioeconomic considerations relevant to such or similar projects.

1.1 Context
 DCs are increasingly integral to our digital lives and the broader economy, particularly as artificial
intelligence (AI) expands rapidly. Their electricity demand is rising sharply, with U.S. data center load
projected [1] to more than double by 2030. This growth is already having immediate impacts on the U.S.
economic outlook—driven by the increased importance of AI—on the nation’s strategic ambition to lead
in AI, and on society. The surge in electricity needs is reshaping utility planning, grid interconnection
queues, and the market for reliable power nationwide.
 Nuclear energy is well suited to data center requirements, providing scalable and reliable, 24/7
electricity with stable long-term costs. Modern hyperscale DC campuses can require hundreds of
megawatts for Information Technology (IT) equipment and cooling, and many applications demand
extremely high uptime. At the same time, leading hyperscalers have aggressive commitments to use clean
power sources that limit reliance on fossil generation. Nuclear power plants (NPPs) can meet these
requirements while reducing exposure to fossil fuel price volatility and transmission constraints, and by
potentially alleviating pressure on the grid that could otherwise result in higher electricity prices for
surrounding communities. The main challenges for nuclear energy remain deployment speed, particularly
uncertainties in cost and construction duration.
 Recognizing these opportunities and challenges, several significant recent nuclear investments have
been announced by Meta, Amazon, Alphabet, and others, including direct purchase commitments and
investments in TerraPower, Oklo, X-energy, and the current fleet through Light Water Reactor (LWR)
license extension, restart, and uprate [2] [3] [4] [5] [6]. A comprehensive list of ongoing projects and
expected capacity is discussed in Section 2.2. These announced projects demonstrate that hyperscale
companies are willing to make substantial financial commitments, confident that the challenges
associated with nuclear energy can be addressed.

1.2 Insight from Recent Publications
 Several noteworthy publications were published in the past few months that add to the literature and
complement our report.
 The IAEA held an “AI Symposium” in December 2025 and released a summary [7] that provides
useful insights on the convergence of artificial intelligence and nuclear energy, policy and regulatory
implications, and convening/partnership roles. The symposium stressed that the main challenges are not
technical, but institutional, organizational, and systemic—such as workforce readiness, regulatory
alignment, supply-chain resilience, and public trust. The highlights note that the AI–nuclear relationship
is reciprocal: nuclear energy supplies low‑carbon, reliable power for AI, while AI can be applied to
improve nuclear competitiveness.
 A 2026 report from the Carnegie Endowment for International Peace entitled “Beyond the Hype:
Assessing Hyperscaler Nuclear Commitments Against U.S. Energy Realities” [8] documents mounting
private-sector moves, major U.S. policy and funding shifts supporting nuclear (loans, executive orders,
DOE awards), evolving regulatory frameworks (NRC Part 53), and rising public and bipartisan political
support for nuclear as data-center demand surges. It also frames nuclear energy challenges—reputational
risk, fuel and nonproliferation questions, spent‑fuel management, regulatory and workforce constraints,
supply‑chain bottlenecks, and high delivery/cost risk—as material concerns that make many hyperscalers
cautious. The authors emphasize the mismatch in timescales and investment profiles—hyperscalers run a
short, competitive “sprint” to build AI capacity while nuclear is a long, capital‑intensive “marathon”—so
signing up for nuclear does not solve near‑term capacity needs (which this report discusses and quantifies
in Section 2) and introduces strategic and executional risks.
 Several recent Electric Power Research Institute (EPRI) reports [9] [10] were issued on this topic.
The “Advanced Nuclear Technology: A Guide for Co-Locating Data Centers with Nuclear Plants” [9]

data centers to consume 9-17% of U.S. electricity by 2030, up from 4% today, significantly increasing
their previous forecast. EPRI emphasizes the importance of fleet deployment and standardization
(especially for Small Modular Reactors or SMRs) to control costs and schedules, and the role of AI in
accelerating nuclear project delivery, construction, and supply-chain optimization.
 Finally, U.S. national laboratories led by INL have issued a “Data Center Playbook” [12] which
provides operational and technical guidance for powering and integrating data centers with nuclear
reactors. This report aggregates recent technical sources and emphasizes practical implementation issues
such as microgrid approaches, interconnection, operational reliability for large loads, and the role of
advanced nuclear deployments to meet rapid data‑center scale-up. The playbook also addresses the need
for robust safety, security, and nonproliferation frameworks, and the importance of collaboration between
hyperscalers, nuclear operators, and regulators to ensure reliable, scalable, and low-carbon power for data
centers.

2.1.4 DC Energy Requirements
 Energy requirements from data centers were extensively discussed in [1]. Computational hardware
processes the primary workloads, while cooling systems regulate the substantial heat dissipation
generated by high-density servers. Within a modern DC, roughly 60–70% of site electricity flows to IT
equipment, 25–35% to cooling, and the balance to power conditioning and lighting [13] [30]. Rising GPU
thermal design power is pushing the IT share upward while direct-to-chip liquid cooling and higher
chilled-water setpoints reduce cooling energy per unit of compute [10] [30]. Different types of data
centers follow reliability requirements from Tier 0 to Tier IV, with the most demanding facilities
requiring 99.995% [1]. Load factors vary equally by workload type: AI training clusters sustain utilization
above 90%, while inference and general-purpose facilities carry lower and more variable electrical
demand [13].
 The current trajectory of digital infrastructure reveals three foundational macroeconomic trends
governing this sector. Data centers are operating at higher power intensities while concurrently advancing
systemic efficiency. Furthermore, they fundamentally require continuous baseload power to ensure
operational reliability. Finally, developers are increasingly adopting load flexibility to accelerate market
entry and overcome local utility constraints.

 Operational efficiency is increasing alongside the exponential rise in computing power density.
The industry standardizes around Power Usage Effectiveness (PUE) and Water Usage Effectiveness
(WUE) to measure the ratio of total facility power to active computational equipment power [32]. Fleet-
average PUE for hyperscale builds has declined from about 1.65 in 2013 to 1.15–1.20 in 2026, achieved
through hot-aisle containment, elevated supply-air temperatures, and, increasingly, direct-to-chip cooling
for GPU racks operating above 100 kW [13] [30]. WUE remains heterogeneous and is now a leading
permitting concern; several of the largest AI sites, including xAI's Colossus 2 and Microsoft's Fairwater
Atlanta campus, have adopted closed-loop air-cooled or refrigerant-based designs specifically to avoid
evaporative water withdrawals [33]. Rack-level power density has climbed from 10–20 kW to 100–130
kW for GB200-class systems, shrinking the mechanical footprint per unit of compute [34].
 Continuous baseload electricity remains the most critical requirement for DC operations. Data
centers must maintain rigid uptime standards to support global digital services, necessitating
uninterrupted power availability across all operational hours. AI training clusters are the clearest case:
synchronized training jobs are intolerant of interruption† because a single stalled node halts the entire
cluster, and inference workloads sustain load factors above 90% of nameplate—conditions that vary by
hour and season [35]. Battery storage and on-site power conditioning systems (STATCOM) deployed at
the facility level can absorb these short-term demand swings, buffering the electrical load seen by the
reactor or the grid [36]. Nvidia's system reference designs favor utility grid interconnection where firm
capacity is available, with on-site generation used to bridge queue delays rather than to replace grid
service [37]. Utility surveys report developer demand for five-nines availability at connection points that
historically served three- or four-nines industrial loads [10].

 System operators are integrating flexible power strategies to achieve faster deployment.
Regional transmission constraints frequently delay traditional utility interconnections by four to seven
years across most independent system operators (ISOs) [10]. For example, the Electric Reliability Council
of Texas (ERCOT) recently paused data center grid connections due to an overwhelming queue of over
1,800 projects representing 474 GW of requests [38]. Operators are compressing time-to-power through
three complementary measures. First, co-located battery energy storage smooths transient training-driven
load steps and reduces contracted peak demand [39]. Second, developers signal willingness to curtail non-
training workloads during grid stress events, a shift from load-flat operation to bounded flexibility [40].
Third, on-site generation (natural gas turbines, fuel cells, and increasingly SMRs) is sized to bring first
megawatts online 12–24 months after groundbreaking, well ahead of full grid interconnection [32]. Grid
operators and federal regulators responded to interconnection queue pressure by creating dedicated large-
load pathways. SPP's high impact large load (HILL) framework [41], approved by FERC in January

completes within 90 days, and conditional curtailable service agreements (e.g., conditional high impact
large load service, CHILLS) that allow facilities to draw power before network upgrades are finished
[42]. FERC extended this effort system-wide in June 2026, directing all six major grid operators to justify
or reform large-load tariff provisions [43]. These orders build upon proceedings in Docket No. AD24-11
[44], which explored cost-allocation principles to ensure behind-the-meter facilities bear appropriate
transmission costs. Collectively, these measures signal a shift in DC energy architecture from grid-only
operation to flexible operation with hybrid sourcing.

2.2.3 Power Uprates
 A power uprate is the process of increasing the licensed electrical generating capacity of an existing
commercial nuclear power plant beyond its original design output while maintaining compliance with
nuclear safety, operational, and environmental requirements. Rather than constructing a new reactor or
restarting a retired facility, an uprate improves the performance of an operating reactor by increasing its
thermal power output and enhancing the efficiency of the balance-of-plant systems. Depending on the
magnitude of the increase, uprates may involve modifications to reactor fuel design, steam generators,
turbines, condensers, pumps, transformers, instrumentation and control systems, and cooling water
systems. All uprate projects require detailed engineering analyses and approval by the U.S. Nuclear
Regulatory Commission (NRC) to demonstrate that the reactor can safely operate at the higher power
level under both normal and accident conditions.
 The currently operating nuclear fleet in the U.S. is generally considered to be under-utilized, and its
power could be boosted through “uprates.” There are different types of power uprates, ranging from
smaller uprates requiring minor upgrades to larger uprates requiring major balance-of-plant (BOP)
upgrades, as detailed in [64]. The DOE Light Water Reactor Sustainability (LWRS) Program has
investigated the uprate potential of the U.S. nuclear fleet [64]. The fleet has already increased its power
capacity by 8 GWe when compared to its initial licensing capacity through uprates [65], and the DOE
liftoff report mentions 2‒8 GWe of uprate potential for the current fleet [66]. There are several types of
uprates that are currently being considered, with time frames and potential fleet capacities shown in Table
2-3. Thermal power uprates include [67]:

 • Measurement Uncertainty Recapture (MUR): Increases licensed power level by less than 2%
 through improved techniques for calculating reactor power by more accurately measuring
 feedwater flow. This is generally a 1-3 year project.

 • Stretch Power Uprate (SPU): Increases licensed power level by between 2% and 7% through
 changes in instrument settings and without major other plant modifications. This is generally
 a 3-6 year project.

 • Extended Power Uprates (EPU): Increases licensed power level by 7% and as much as 20%.
 These require significant modifications to major pieces of non-nuclear equipment (HP
 Turbine, Condensate Pumps, Main Generator, Transformers, Moisture Separator Reheaters).
 Those are 5-10 year projects. The LWRS program is investigating potential EPU+ scenarios
 involving uprates up to 25% for PWRs and 30% for BWRs.
 According to a 2025 Nuclear Energy Institute survey [47], “Greater than 73% of sites surveyed have
a level of interest in power uprates for their site units.” At the time of this writing, there are no expected
or pending NRC uprate applications [65], but 31 applications are expected by 2032, including 10 MUR
and 19 EPU [68], for a total of 2.4 GW of added capacity. A stretched goal of uprating all BWRs to 30%
and PWRs to 25% would see a total fleet addition of 17.7 GWe (upper boundary of what is realistically
achievable), with the state distribution shown in Figure 2-7. However, another 2 GW of capacity could be
potentially added through another type of uprate involving electrical capacity increases (without an
increase in thermal power), these are achieved through 1-5 year projects:

2.3.1 NPP and Data Center Coupling Options
 The FY2025 report provided a detailed evaluation of alternative configurations for coupling NPP and
DC [1]. Five electrical coupling options were identified, as shown in Figure 2-11, and their corresponding
electrical architectures are illustrated in Figure 2-12. These options are distinguished primarily by whether
the NPP and DC are colocated, whether they have a direct electrical connection, and the role of the bulk
power system (BPS). The grid may serve as the primary source of electricity, providing backup power
when the NPP is unavailable, or receiving excess electricity generated from the NPP when DC demand is
lower than reactor output. Those different coupling options can be considered to meet the different DC
energy requirements (such as reliability and availability) mentioned in Section 2.1.4.

Figure 2-11. Decision tree for NPP/DC coupling options.

Figure 2-12. Schematic for different NPP/DC coupling options.

 The first option involves an NPP and DC that are not colocated and are connected independently to
the BPS. In this configuration, the DC may purchase nuclear-generated electricity through a physical or
virtual PPA, but the NPP does not supply the DC through a dedicated electrical connection. This
approach offers the greatest flexibility in siting the DC and avoids many of the regulatory and technical
issues associated with direct NPP/DC connections. However, it remains dependent on the availability and
reliability of the transmission and distribution network, and it may require significant grid-interconnection
upgrades.

August 31, 2026                                                                                             35

 The second option involves colocated NPP and DC facilities with a direct electrical connection, while
both facilities retain full connections to the BPS. The NPP can supply electricity directly to the DC and
export excess generation to the grid, while the DC can draw power from the grid when the NPP is offline
or unable to meet its full demand. This configuration can improve power quality, reduce transmission
losses, and provide greater operational flexibility. It may also facilitate contractual arrangements between
separate NPP and DC owners. However, it requires additional electrical infrastructure, including
dedicated transmission lines, transformers, protection systems, and potentially separate interconnection
agreements.
 The third option is a colocated, behind-the-meter (BTM) configuration. The NPP and DC are directly
connected, but the DC does not have a separate, fully metered connection to the high-voltage transmission
system. The NPP may export excess electricity to the grid, while the DC receives power directly from the
NPP and may have limited access to the grid for backup purposes. Compared with the second option, this
arrangement may reduce the amount of duplicate interconnection equipment and could shorten the grid-
interconnection process. Nevertheless, BTM arrangements introduce important regulatory, market, and
metering considerations. If grid backup is not permitted, the DC may need to curtail its load or rely on
onsite generation whenever the NPP is unavailable.
 The fourth option consists of a fully islanded microgrid in which the NPP and DC are directly
connected but neither facility maintains a meaningful connection to the BPS. This configuration can
reduce dependence on transmission infrastructure and may enable development in locations where grid
access is limited. It also provides the greatest degree of direct control over the NPP/DC system. However,
it requires sufficient redundant generation and energy storage to maintain DC operations during reactor
outages, refueling, maintenance, or unexpected load changes. In addition, the configuration may face
significant nuclear safety and regulatory challenges because existing requirements generally require
nuclear plants to maintain one or more connections to offsite power systems [1].
 The fifth option represents an intermediate configuration: the NPP and DC operate as a colocated
microgrid, while a limited grid connection is retained for backup power and safety-related NPP loads.
Unlike the fully islanded configuration, this arrangement can use the grid to support essential plant
systems and provide an additional source of power to the DC during NPP outages. At the same time, the
reduced-size NPP grid connection may lower the cost and complexity of high-voltage interconnection
equipment. This option could expand the range of potential sites while retaining some of the reliability
benefits of grid access; however, it would still require careful coordination of protection, control,
metering, and regulatory requirements.
 The principal considerations in selecting among these options are the required DC reliability level, the
availability and cost of transmission capacity, the desired degree of electrical independence from the grid,
the ability to export excess nuclear generation, and the availability of backup generation. The selected
configuration also affects reactor sizing. When the NPP is connected to the grid, the grid can provide
power during reactor outages, and the NPP generally does not need to be sized to meet the DC’s highest
reliability requirement independently. In contrast, configurations without grid backup require additional
reactor modules or other onsite generation to maintain service during planned and unplanned outages. As
a result, these configurations may favor multiple smaller reactor units rather than a single large unit [1]. In
addition to electrical integration, future designs may consider thermal integration, including the use of
nuclear steam or waste heat to drive absorption chillers, shared cooling systems, and other energy-
management approaches.
 Recent federal regulatory actions have formalized transmission service pathways that correspond
closely to these five configurations. Across six show cause orders (Dockets E-7 through E-12) issued
June, 2026, Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (FERC) directed all six regional transmission
organizations (RTOs)/ISOs to adopt expedited 60-to-90-day interconnection study timelines, mandatory

36                                                                                 August 31, 2026

cost recovery agreements, and structured non-firm transmission options for large co-located loads [93].
The resulting framework defines five service types — Traditional Front-of-Meter Network Integration,
Firm Contract Demand, Non-Firm Contract Demand, Interim Network Integration, and Electrically
Proximate Load and Generation — that map directly onto Options 1, 2, 3, 5, and (conditionally) 4 of the
deployment taxonomy [93].

 • Option 1 (virtual / grid-only) corresponds to traditional front-of-meter Network Integration
 Transmission Service, under which the transmission provider plans both generation dispatch and
 delivery to load.

 • Option 2 (colocated, direct link, both grid-tied) aligns with Firm Contract Demand service,
 dedicating generation to the colocated facility while preserving the generator's right to export
 surplus energy into wholesale markets.

 • Option 3 (behind-the-meter, generator grid-tied maps) to Non-Firm Contract Demand and
 behind-the-meter arrangements, in which the generator retains market access, but the colocated
 customer draws power upstream of grid injection.

 • Option 4 (fully islanded microgrid) has no corresponding FERC transmission-service pathway,
 since it operates entirely outside federal tariffs. Commercial implementation may additionally
 face the NRC's requirement for independent offsite AC power circuits to nuclear safety systems
 [94].

 • Option 5 (colocated microgrid with backup grid tie) corresponds to Interim Network Integration
 Transmission Service, providing conditional, non-firm grid support for plant safety systems and
 backup power during phased commissioning.
 FERC also formalized an Electrically Proximate category (generation and load studied jointly when
located within two electrical buses) which extends the operational and cost benefits of Option 2 to non-
colocated facilities and may be relevant for NPP/DC projects where strict physical colocation is not
feasible [93]. The implications of these coupling options were summarized qualitatively in Table 5-3 of
the FY2025 report [1] and are further evaluated in Section 5 of this report for the selected case studies.

2.3.2 Ratepayer Impact
 Data center development has generated significant community concern, particularly regarding
potential increases in electricity bills, water consumption, noise, property values, and land use [95]. These
concerns have contributed to the cancellation or postponement of proposed projects and have prompted
several states to adopt or consider moratoria on new data center development [95]. This section focuses
specifically on the potential effects of data center deployment on electricity rates, grid-infrastructure
costs, reliability, and the allocation of costs between new data center customers and existing ratepayers.
 Whether large DC loads raise retail electricity rates for existing customers has become the central
political question facing utilities, regulators, and host communities. The five coupling configurations
described in Section 2.3.1 differ not only in technical architecture but in how they allocate transmission
upgrade and replacement-capacity costs between the new load and the current ratepayer base. The choice
of NPP–DC coupling configuration determines whether transmission and replacement-capacity costs are
socialized across existing ratepayers or borne by the new load. This creates a ratepayer impact profile
where the same reactor plant or DC can produce very different distributional outcomes depending on how
it is connected to the grid [96]. The five coupling options [1] span a spectrum from fully grid-integrated,
geographically separated facilities to completely islanded microgrids. Three characteristics distinguish

August 31, 2026                                                                                           37

them: (1) whether the nuclear plant and data center are directly wired, (2) whether the data center has its
own independent grid interconnection, and (3) whether the nuclear plant has its own grid interconnection:

 • Option 1 (both grid-connected): baseline; DC pays full retail transmission for its load; minimal
 net rate-payer impact. However, transmission congestion and associated congestion fees may
 increase ratepayer costs during the grid upgrade process.

 • Option 2 (both on grid with direct connection): The DC pays full transmission charges, and at
 sufficient scale, the added load can dilute per-kW transmission costs for all ratepayers, making
 this the most equitable configuration. The nuclear plant's generation remains visible to the RTO's
 capacity and energy markets, preserving market price signals and avoiding the supply-withdrawal
 effects.

 • Option 3 (NPP on Grid, DC off Grid): Neutral to slightly negative for ratepayers on the
 transmission cost dimension if the nuclear plant is newly built. The DC contributes nothing to
 existing fixed transmission costs recovery but also imposes no transmission burdens. The NPP
 continues to provide firm capacity and energy, so resource adequacy is maintained. However, the
 DC's consumption is invisible to the grid operator, potentially distorting load forecasts and
 capacity planning. A recent study found that behind-the-meter configurations allow data centers
 to curtail grid consumption during peak hours, reducing their demand charges and shifting
 infrastructure costs to other ratepayers [97] .

 • Option 4 (Islanded microgrid): No impact on ratepayers. A fully islanded configuration
 eliminates ratepayer exposure entirely but also removes grid access for backup, requiring the
 nuclear fleet to be sized with sufficient redundancy to meet the data center's reliability
 requirements without external support. This configuration is most relevant for remote or security-
 sensitive installations [1] where grid access is unavailable or undesirable.

 • Option 5 (DC on Grid, NPP off Grid): Isolating the NPP behind-the-meter while leaving the
 DC dependent on the transmission network for backup power creates an inequitable cost-shifting
 mechanism most problematic for existing ratepayers. The utility must maintain costly spinning
 reserves and replacement capacity to guard against sudden data center outages, while the nuclear
 plant's firm capacity remains unavailable to the public grid— shifting replacement capacity and
 transmission shortfall costs onto existing ratepayers.
 For any of these options, if the NPP is an existing facility, the outcome may differ. Diverting an
existing nuclear plant's output behind-the-meter removes that low-cost generation from the wholesale
market pool. This supply withdrawal mechanically drives up market clearing prices for ratepayers on the
grid. Beyond the rate aspect, large loads bring additional risks of sudden grid disruptions. For instance, a
grid event that caused a 3GW data center load to disconnect abruptly on July, 2026 at a Virginia data
center facility [98] caused flickering throughout the Eastern half of the U.S. Recognizing this threat,
FERC instructed the North American Electric Reliability Corporation (NERC) to develop new or updated
reliability standards and registration criteria for computational loads [99].

2.3.3 Contract Types and Partnerships
 DCs are generally not planning to own or operate NPP, as hyperscalers are highly interested in
investing in generation but remain leery of exposure to long-term nuclear liabilities. To address this, some
NPP vendors (such as Oklo or NuScale) are offering alternative contracting structure, such as the Build-
Own-Operate (BOO) model, where the vendor retains equity ownership and operational responsibility
[100]. By clearly separating who owns and operates the plant from who consumes the power, vendors can
sell electricity through a PPA. This could shield offtakers from utility-like obligations.

38                                                                                August 31, 2026

 To date, hyperscaler efforts reflect a preference to act as energy offtakers (customers) over direct
nuclear ownership or project development. Long-term Power Purchase Agreements (PPAs) represent the
dominant contracting mechanism. This approach represents a fundamental departure from traditional
nuclear development. Historically, commercial nuclear projects were driven by regional utilities relying
on rate-base cost recovery or government loan guarantees, tethered to slow, decades-long integrated
resource planning. Conversely, hyperscaler nuclear procurement sits outside the retail rate base, driven by
an intense speed-to-power urgency. Corporate offtakers are now dictating multi-unit orderbooks and
utilizing bespoke bilateral partnerships—including behind-the-meter colocation and facility restarts—to
bypass congested grid interconnection queues and shift financial risk away from public ratepayers.
 However, even with robust PPAs in place, nuclear and data center contracting risk occupies two
distinct layers across the project lifecycle: pre-operational project-development risks and post-operational
contract-delivery risks as illustrated in Figure 2-13. Project-development risks determine whether a
facility physically reaches commercial operation. These upstream factors include construction cost
overruns, regulatory licensing delays, first-of-a-kind (FOAK) technology failures, and severe timeline
mismatches between rapid data-center demand and decade-long reactor construction schedules.
Conversely, contract-delivery risks emerge during the operations phase and dictate how hourly
generation-to-consumption mismatches are financially allocated between the developer and the offtaker.
These downstream, operational factors include volume shortfalls, hourly shape profile mismatches, nodal-
to-zonal basis differentials, and fixed-price competitiveness against future spot markets.

3. PROJECTION OF NUCLEAR FUEL REQUIREMENTS FROM DATA
 CENTER DEMAND
 The nuclear fuel supply chain represents a major potential barrier to the deployment of upcoming
nuclear projects and is therefore a central area of focus for the SA&I program. This section quantifies the
potential fuel requirements associated with prominent announced nuclear projects driven by data center
energy demand and identifies potential solutions to address related supply constraints. Section 3.1
examines LEU+ requirements, including fuel needed to support LWR uprates. Section 3.2 examines
HALEU requirements for announced advanced reactor projects by Oklo, TerraPower, Kairos Power, and
X-energy.

3.1 LEU+ for LWR Uprates
 The currently operating U.S. light-water reactor fleet is fueled with LEU, with typical refueling cycle
lengths of about 18 months for PWRs and 24 months for BWRs. In the near term, power uprates are
increasingly considered as a pragmatic option to increase electricity production from the existing fleet (as
discussed in Section 2.2.3). However, because many U.S. cores already operate near the practical
enrichment ceiling of ~4.95 wt% U-235, power uprates that preserve cycle length and fuel-management
structure require higher fissile loading per batch than can be supplied with conventional LEU. In that
context, LEU+—here defined as uranium enriched in the 5–10 wt% U-235 range—becomes relevant as a
means to increase the available reactivity and enable higher discharge burnup from a neutronics
standpoint. The key system-level implication is that higher reactivity inventory can support higher energy
extraction and/or reduce the number of fresh assemblies required per reload, which can improve fuel-
cycle economics and reduce outage fuel-handling burden if other constraints (e.g., peaking, boron limits,
burnup limits) are also satisfied.
 Prior work has evaluated extended enrichment concepts in PWRs over the ~5–7 wt% U-235 range,
including impacts on discharge burnup capability, reactivity coefficients, and other neutronics parameters
[111] [112]. Industry deployment has also begun to move beyond conceptual evaluation: Westinghouse
has reported development and insertion of LEU+ fuel pellets as lead test assemblies at Vogtle Unit 2 in
April 2025 [113] [114]. On the enrichment-supply side, NRC authorization of Urenco USA’s Eunice,
New Mexico facility to produce uranium enriched up to 10 wt% U-235 represents an important step
toward commercial availability of LEU+ supply in the United States [115].
 Based on this background, this section estimates fleet-level LEU+ demand under representative
uprate scenarios to provide an order-of-magnitude uranium demand signals that can inform enrichment
and fuel-fabrication infrastructure planning. Not all those uprates may be motivated by data centers
demand – so this section will provide the amount of fuel to accommodate all the fleet uprates. It should be
noted that most of the announced large uprates are associated with PPAs or partnership from data center
companies (especially Meta).

3.1.2 Estimation of Annual LEU+ Demands
 For each uprate case, the enrichment required to sustain the assumed operating cycle is estimated
using the Linear Reactivity Model [117]. The model is used as a screening-level tool to link uprate
magnitude to the additional reactivity that must be carried in fresh fuel when cycle length and batch
strategy are held fixed. Because the refueling interval, fresh-fuel batch fraction, and fuel heavy-metal
loading are held constant, the energy generated per fuel batch—and therefore the target discharge
burnup—is assumed to increase approximately in proportion to reactor power. Accordingly, the 8% and
20% uprate cases correspond to approximately 8% and 20% increases in batch burnup, respectively. In
this analysis, it is assumed that uprate-related plant modifications are completed before implementation,
so that operation can step from baseline power to uprated power at the start of the uprated cycle. In
practice, fuel management may include phased transitions and iterative adjustments to enrichment and
batch fractions; however, these complexities are simplified here to maintain clarity and ensure a
consistent accounting basis across the fleet. Readers interested in details of the Linear Reactivity Model
can refer to Appendix A.
 Two depletion states are explicitly distinguished because they have different enrichment implications:
 1. Equilibrium cycle – the equilibrium cycle is the steady operating condition after several
 cycles at specific conditions such as baseline operation or power uprate condition, when all
 batches in the core have been loaded under the same uprated assumptions. In that condition,
 the reactivity burden is distributed across all batches operating under the uprated design basis.
 2. Transition cycle – the transition cycle is the first cycle after the uprate in which the core
 contains a mixed inventory: some fuel was loaded under the baseline conditions, while the
 newest batch is loaded for uprated conditions. In conditions with constant batch fraction and
 cycle length, the uprate increases energy extracted per cycle and therefore increases the
 reactivity requirement. In the transition cycle, the fresh batch must carry a higher enrichment,
 because the previously loaded baseline fuel was not enriched for the uprated duty.

48                                                                                August 31, 2026

 Consequently, the transition cycle generally requires a higher enrichment than the subsequent
 uprated equilibrium cycles.
 To translate reactor reloads into annual supply signals, uranium demand is distributed over a
procurement lead time equal to twice the refueling cycle length — for instance, three years for 18-month
cycles (in PWRs) and four years for 24-month cycles (in BWRs).

3.1.4 Fleet-Level Demand Trend
 Using the assumed uprate adoption schedule, Figure 3-4 shows the annual enriched uranium
procurement requirement for the fleet over time, separating baseline operation from uprated transition and
equilibrium contributions. As uprates are phased in, demand from baseline declines and demand from
uprated equilibrium cycle grows, approaching a new steady state by the early 2040s under the assumed
schedule. Figure 3-5 converts this into incremental demand relative to baseline, reporting additional NU
and SWU requirements and distinguishing the LEU+ share. Overall, the analysis shows that power
uprates require higher enrichment levels (5-6 wt%) and increase front-end fuel-cycle requirements, with
the largest impact arising from PWR EPU+ cases. Under the assumptions of this study, PWR EPU+
reloads require LEU+ fuel, whereas BWR enrichments remain close to 5 wt% even under EPU+, although
this could change if lower batch fractions or longer cycle lengths are adopted. Under the assumed uprate
implementation schedule, power uprates result in additional steady-state requirements of approximately
1,900 metric ton of natural uranium per year (tNU/yr) and 1,850 tSWU/yr — increases of roughly 9%
and 12% relative to the 2024 U.S. fleet baseline [118], respectively. The increment attributable to the
use of LEU+ fuel is approximately 1,400 t(NU)/yr and 1,350 tSWU/yr, representing about 74% of the
total increase.
 This result is particularly important because the additional demand is not merely a matter of scale
within the existing fuel supply chain; rather, a large fraction of the increase depends on the availability of
LEU+ enrichment and fuel fabrication capacity. Supplying LEU+ fuel requires enrichment services above
the conventional 5 wt% enrichment threshold, and the facilities and processes needed to provide that
capability are subject to regulatory licensing and, in some cases, facility modification or qualification. As
a result, the expansion of LEU+ supply cannot be assumed to occur automatically in response to reactor
demand. Moreover, fuel procurement and fabrication decisions are made several years before reactor
loading, meaning that utilities and suppliers must secure enrichment capacity, conversion services,
fabrication capability, and regulatory approvals well in advance of the actual uprate implementation date.
Consequently, if LEU+ supply infrastructure is not prepared early enough, it could become a practical
bottleneck to uprate deployment even when the reactor-side uprate itself is technically and economically
justified.

3.1.5 Notes on Potential Variations in LEU+ Estimation
 The demand estimates depend on baseline design assumptions and on simplified fuel-management
rules. The baseline reactor models were selected as Westinghouse four-loop for PWR and General
Electric Type 4 for BWR, with assumed core sizes, assembly heavy-metal loadings, batch fractions,
enrichments, and cycle lengths [119] [120]. Real plants differ from these simplified baselines, and
deviations in uranium loading, batch strategy, and operating targets will alter the estimated enrichment
and mass requirements.
 In addition, the analysis holds batch fraction constant between baseline and uprated cases, and
assumes a fixed batch fraction across all cycles, neglecting the cycle-to-cycle variations that arise during
the transition from an initial core to equilibrium conditions. In practice, increased enrichment and stronger
reactivity control measures may require use of burnable absorbers (e.g., gadolinia), which displace heavy
metal and can reduce uranium loading per assembly; such effects could require higher enrichment than
estimated here. Likewise, regulatory constraints on rod average burnup and power peaking can drive
batch fraction changes that feed back into enrichment and feed/SWU requirements.
 Finally, power-uprate scenarios were constructed using historical uprate experience by reactor type
[121]. For each reactor class, EPU and EPU+ were treated as feasible only if uprates of similar magnitude
have been achieved previously in that same reactor type. Based on this screening, the uprate magnitudes
were set to 8% (EPU) and 20% (EPU+). The annual number of uprates was anchored to the NRC’s
expected license amendment request schedule through 2032 [68] and extended beyond 2032 using
scenario-driven adoption rates consistent with the study’s planning objectives. Collectively, these
assumptions motivate the need for dedicated uncertainty and sensitivity studies that systematically vary
batch fraction, transition cycle length, power uprate magnitude, and reactor type to bound the range of
enrichment and fuel mass requirements under realistic operating conditions.

3.2 HALEU for SMR Deployments
 Currently operating NPPs and LWR-type SMRs use low-enriched uranium (uranium enriched lower
than 5 wt% in uranium-235), which is commercially available. Several hyperscalers announced
partnerships with advanced nuclear reactor developers and investments in SMR technologies that utilize
HALEU [2] [5] [83]. It is noted that HALEU refers to uranium enriched greater than 5 and less than 19.75
weight percent of the uranium-235 isotope, but in this work, HALEU is considered greater than 10 wt%
and less than 19.75 wt% enriched uranium that is needed to support advanced SMRs. There is currently
no domestic commercial large-scale supplier for HALEU, and DOE is providing limited amounts of
HALEU to support immediate needs to demonstrate advanced reactors [122] through the HALEU
Availability Program (which supports the development of HALEU for civilian domestic research,
development, demonstration, and commercial use). This HALEU is produced through downblending of
DOE’s highly enriched uranium (HEU) stockpile, from initial HALEU enrichment production started by
Centrus (900 kg/year achieved so far) [123]. DOE has also awarded several companies (Urenco USA,
Orano USA, General Matter) to help scale up domestic HALEU capacity.

and the third one from Meta represents the main update of this section.

4. METHODOLOGY FOR CASE STUDIES
 The objective of the case study analysis is to illustrate what a nuclear-powered DC project could look
like in terms of timing, siting, techno and socioeconomics. The projects considered are hypothetical
and not intended to represent an announced or otherwise proposed project. However, they are meant
to be realistic. A list of possible types of NPP/DC projects was shown in Table 2-6. For this analysis, four
project types were selected for more detailed analysis: brownfield, greenfield, LWR restart, and LWR
uprate. Those provide a diverse selection of potential NPP/DC projects, with short- and medium-term
deployment potential.
 For each case study, a specific site location is selected. For brownfield/greenfield, specific sites were
chosen based on ORSAGE analysis that ranked viable sites according to their attractiveness for nuclear
and DC projects [1]. To help down-select viable sites, several criteria were considered including grid
price projections, availability of nearby fiber networks, on-site power, or grid capacity to accommodate
short-term DC deployment, and state policy incentives for SMR and DC. In addition, this report
attempted to showcase a diversity of U.S. regions, together with both rural and urban areas for
socioeconomic impact.

 Cooling Water Requirements
 Cooling water availability is one of the primary criteria considered in the siting assessment because it
directly influences the technical and operational feasibility of nuclear power plant deployment. The siting
study completed in FY25 (see Table 7-3 of [1]) clearly identified the swift reduction in NPP and DC
suitable lands in every State based on reliance on fresh water (river, lake, underground sources, sea)
versus brought-in “city-water” sources (which would also be representative of dry-cooled). Cooling water
requirements were estimated using industry rules of thumb for water demand per megawatt of electrical
generation [134]. Those requirements were confirmed based on several relevant environmental analyses
supporting nuclear power plant site evaluations submitted to the NRC. In addition, the analysis assumes
that cooling water makeup withdrawals should not exceed 10% of the available stream flow for closed
cycle plants, consistent with previous ORSAGE studies and established siting practices (e.g., [133]). This
assumption restricts candidate sites to locations in the vicinity of rivers and streams with sufficient flow
capacity to support long-term reactor operation while minimizing impacts on aquatic ecosystems and
competing water uses.
 For this study, a 20-mile maximum piping distance from a freshwater source was adopted as a
practical planning assumption for supplying cooling water through pipeline infrastructure [133]. While

August 31, 2026                                                                                              59

cooling water could technically be transported over greater distances, doing so would increase capital
costs, pumping energy requirements, and operational complexity. Consequently, sites located beyond the
20-mile threshold were considered less favorable but were not categorically excluded from further
consideration. For sites located near coastal regions, seawater may provide a viable alternative cooling
source, thereby reducing dependence on inland freshwater resources. In water-constrained inland regions,
other nontraditional water supplies, such as reclaimed municipal wastewater (as successfully
demonstrated at the Palo Verde Generating Station), may also provide a sustainable alternative for reactor
cooling. These alternative cooling strategies can substantially expand the range of suitable candidate sites
while reducing pressure on limited freshwater resources. Finally, SMRs and DCs relying on dry cooling
technology are also being developed and deployed to reduce water requirements and extend siting
options. The tradeoff between water withdrawal and economics implications for different SMR cooling
options are discussed in Reference [135].
 However, the reduction in water dependence involves important economic and performance tradeoffs
[136]. This consideration is particularly relevant under future extreme weather conditions. Increasing
temperatures, more frequent heat waves, drought, and changes in precipitation and streamflow could
reduce the long-term reliability of conventional water-based cooling at some candidate sites. Dry cooling
reduces exposure to water scarcity but increases sensitivity to high ambient temperatures. Consequently,
future SMRs and DCs siting should evaluate water availability and temperature simultaneously: sites
experiencing declining water availability may favor dry cooling, while locations projected to experience
extreme increases in temperature may incur greater dry-cooling performance penalties.
 Proximity to Existing Infrastructure
 Proximity to existing infrastructure is a critical siting consideration for both NPPs and gigawatt DCs
because it directly affects construction costs, project schedules, operational reliability, and long-term
economic viability. For nuclear facilities, access to high-voltage transmission lines is essential for
efficiently delivering generated electricity to the bulk power system while minimizing the need for costly
transmission expansion. Likewise, proximity to railways and major roadways facilitates the transportation
of large reactor components, construction materials, heavy equipment, nuclear fuel, and maintenance
supplies throughout the plant's lifecycle. Existing transportation infrastructure also enhances emergency
preparedness and improves access for plant personnel and supporting services. Consequently, candidate
sites located near established transmission corridors and multimodal transportation networks generally
offer significant economic and logistical advantages over more remote greenfield locations.
 For gigawatt data centers, digital connectivity is as important as electrical infrastructure. In addition
to reliable power and transportation access, data centers require proximity to high-capacity fiber-optic
networks to provide the low-latency, high-bandwidth communications necessary for the various data-
intensive applications. The availability of existing fiber infrastructure can substantially reduce
deployment costs and accelerate project implementation by minimizing the need for new
telecommunications construction. Conversely, sites lacking nearby fiber connectivity may require
significant investment in new fiber routes, increasing both capital costs and deployment timelines.
 Therefore, evaluating the proximity of candidate sites to transmission lines, transportation
infrastructure, and fiber-optic networks provides a comprehensive assessment of infrastructure readiness
and is an essential component of integrated siting analyses for co-located nuclear power plants and
gigawatt data centers.

4.2 Socioeconomics
 An input-output model was utilized to quantify the impacts of nuclear reactor and data center
operations scenarios. The results of the model are specific to the counties for sites that were selected for
this report. Input-output models are developed by integrating regional economic data with industry-level
transaction data for a specific period, typically one year. By applying mathematical formulas, the impact
of new economic activity observed in a particular industry can be traced as it permeates other industries
throughout the region. These inter-industry transactions create opportunities for increased revenue, job
creation, and income growth. Such models can either be calculated manually or processed using advanced
applications available from multiple software developers. The model employed in this report was
produced using the IMPLAN input-output modeling application [137].
 Input-output model results are driven by three primary factors: employment, revenue, and labor
income. For this analysis, revenue from electricity generation was calculated using annual megawatt
hours (MWh) multiplied by the electricity price dictated by the estimated power purchase agreement price
as described in Section 4.3.2.
 Employee compensation for both data centers and the nuclear reactor scenarios is based on the default
values in the most recent IMPLAN datasets without adjustment. Employment numbers for the nuclear
reactor and data center scenarios were determined through a meta-analysis of news reports and previous
economic impact studies.
 The nuclear reactor scenarios are averages based on the availability of data necessary for the
operationalization of the input-output model. Various public reports from reactor vendors have provided
employment estimates for small modular reactors (SMRs). The SMR vendor data used in this analysis
include estimates from NuScale Power [138], X-energy [139], and TerraPower [140]. These vendors have
published or announced employment estimates that enhance the accuracy of model results. Accurate
employment and wage information are critical components for effective input-output modeling. Large
reactor employment estimates were based on findings from the C2N Guidebook [141].
 Figure 4-1 illustrates the components included in estimates of the total economic impact of operating
data centers or nuclear reactors. Economic impacts are separated into the following four categories:

 • Facility Operations Impact: Otherwise known as the “Direct Impact”. These values are
 based on facility operations which include employment, labor costs, and wholesale revenue
 from electricity produced by the generating facilities or annual operations in the case of a data
 center.

 • Supply Chain Impacts: Otherwise known as “Indirect Impacts”. The result of supply chain
 activity between the generating stations or a data center and suppliers of goods and services
 within the region.

 • Community Impact: New economic activity caused by households spending income earned
 directly or indirectly from generating station or data center operations. These are otherwise
 known as “Induced Impacts”.

 • Total Impact: The combination of all three impact categories.

4.3 Techno-Economics
 The techno-economic framework evaluates nuclear-powered data center viability by isolating
electricity generation costs and modeling how specific grid integration strategies dictate project
economics. The assessment relies on a sequential methodology that first optimizes reactor sizing to meet
strict reliability constraints and then applies a financial model to generate power purchase agreement
(PPA) pricing.
 Site selection discussed in Section 4.1 influences these economic outcomes. Modeled locations align
with either publicly announced commercial projects or highly promising environments driven by existing
geographic and infrastructure advantages. For prospective deployments, such as coal-to-nuclear (C2N)
conversions, regional market dynamics could introduce an additional evaluative layer. The wholesale
electricity price forecasting tool A-LEAF [143] captures local grid conditions to estimate potential excess
generation revenues or backup power costs.
 Section 4.3.1 outlines the sizing optimization logic used to determine the precise number and capacity
of reactor units needed to achieve minimum levelized cost of electricity (LCOE) while satisfying high-
availability targets. Section 4.3.2 introduces the Argonne PPA Explorer (APEX) model, which bridges the
gap between base LCOE and bilateral contract structures.

4.3.1 Sizing Optimization
 One of the key questions in designing a nuclear-powered data center is the selection of the reactor
unit size and the number of units to be deployed. This choice has significant economic implications:
larger reactors generally benefit from economies of scale, but smaller reactors deployed in multiple units
offer operational redundancy and can better match specific demand levels. The optimal configuration also
depends on how the NPP and the DC are electrically coupled, since the coupling option determines both
the reliability requirements imposed on the nuclear fleet and the revenue streams available to offset
capital and operating costs. This techno-economic optimization framework will be applied in the different
case studies of this report to estimate the reactor size and number of units that minimize the Levelized

64                                                                                 August 31, 2026

Cost of Electricity (LCOE) for a DC under different coupling options, and for representative data center
demand levels.
 The methodology applied here was originally developed by Hanna et al. [144] and further described
in [1]. The reader is referred to those references for a full description of the framework; a brief overview
is provided here for context.
 The framework estimates the LCOE of nuclear electricity delivered to a DC as a function of reactor
unit capacity (ranging from 50 to 1,000 MWe), for a given DC demand and coupling configuration.
LCOE is computed from the overnight capital cost (OCC), operation and maintenance (O&M) costs
(including fuel), and construction duration, all expressed as functions of reactor power P through
regression curves fitted to published cost data. A learning rate is applied to account for cost reductions
associated with the deployment of multiple units of the same design, along with a reduction in O&M
costs per unit as the number of co-deployed units increases. When multiple units are deployed, refueling
outages are staggered across units to minimize the total downtime of the fleet and maximize the effective
capacity factor of the combined system. The number of reactor units N is optimized jointly with the unit
capacity, subject to constraints imposed by the coupling option. The optimal reactor configuration is
defined as the one that minimizes LCOE over the feasible design space. Two enhancements were
introduced in this work relative to the original methodology:

 • Uncertainty quantification: The cost correlations for OCC, O&M cost, learning rate, O&M
 reduction with multi-unit deployment, and construction duration are each derived from regression
 fits to scattered empirical data, and therefore carry inherent parametric uncertainty. Rather than
 using single-point best-fit estimates, this work propagates that uncertainty through Monte Carlo
 sampling: at each evaluation, the regression parameters are randomly drawn from their fitted
 probability distributions, and the calculation is repeated over a large number of samples to
 produce output distributions for LCOE. The resulting +/- 1 standard-deviation uncertainty bands,
 shown as shaded regions in the figures, reflect the scatter in the underlying cost data and provide
 a more realistic representation of the range of plausible outcomes.

 • Grid connection costs: For coupling configurations that include a physical connection between
 the NPP and the bulk transmission grid (Options 2 and 3), a grid connection cost of $100/kW
 (2022 USD) is added to the capital cost of the NPP. This value is taken from the 2024 Annual
 Technology Baseline (ATB) and covers typical costs for a spur line, point of interconnection, and
 nominal network upgrades [145]. While this addition represents a methodological improvement,
 the grid connection cost is relatively small compared to NPP capital costs and therefore has a
 limited effect on the LCOE results.
 In this work, all results are reported in 2025 USD. The key financial and operational assumptions
used in this analysis are as follows: Weighted Average Cost of Capital (WACC) of 6%, levelization
period of 60 years, Debt-to-equity ratio of 1:1, refueling downtime of 4 weeks/year, and grid wholesale
electricity price (for exported power, Options 2 and 3) of 30 $/MWh. Policies such as ITC, PTC and coal-
to-nuclear infrastructure savings are not considered in this sizing optimization analysis but will be
considered in the PPA pricing study.

4.3.2 Power Purchase Agreement Pricing Model
 Translating optimal reactor fleet configurations (Section 4.3.1) into what a DC would actually pay
requires a separate financial model that captures capital recovery, operating costs, and contract structure.
To bridge this gap, the APEX tool was leveraged [146]. This is a spreadsheet-based pricing engine that
computes multiple structured PPA rates for nuclear generation delivered to a DC off taker [147]. APEX is
aligned with the 2024 ATB financial methodology [145] and uses Hanna et al. (2024) [144] cost

August 31, 2026                                                                                           65

correlations (i.e., OCC and O&M) for greenfield reactors. Reflecting the latest publicly available project-
specific reports, APEX also provides cost estimates for alternative deployment scenarios, including coal-
to-nuclear (C2N) conversions, LWR uprates, and nuclear plant restarts. Detailed cost adjustments across
each case study deployment pathway are described in their respective techno-economic assessment in
Section 5.
 Long-term PPAs execute three financial functions that are essential in the nuclear context but absent
from a standard LCOE calculation. First, PPAs provide the revenue certainty required to finance capital-
intensive nuclear assets. With overnight capital costs of 4,000–9,000/kWe (2025 USD) and construction
timelines of five to seven years, lenders require a creditworthy offtaker committed to purchasing output
over a defined term [148]. Second, PPAs allocate risk between the generator and the offtaker through
contractual structure. Unlike a static LCOE calculation, PPA terms—such as capacity charges, take-or-
pay provisions, and residual value clauses—distribute cost, volume, and market-price risk between
parties, producing different electricity prices for the same underlying plant [149]. Third, PPAs define a
capital recovery horizon that may differ from the plant's physical lifetime. A reactor licensed for 60 years
may be contracted under a 20- or 30-year PPA. The offtaker pays only for capital consumed during the
contract period (not the full plant cost) a lease-equivalent logic central to the PPA formulations.
 The grid connection option directly dictates PPA pricing: Options 1–3 carry a grid connection cost of
$110/kWe (2025 USD; scaled from 2022 USD [145]), while Option 4 eliminates this cost entirely. The
impact of NPP/DC options on the ratepayers are not quantified in this report (beyond the qualitative
assessment in Section 2.3.2). Future work may consider use of A-LEAF [143] or other power market
model to project how regional electricity prices evolve under each interconnection scenario.
 APEX establishes a cost-based revenue floor, rather than a value-based ceiling. Solving for a PPA
price that yields zero net present value (NPV) at the developer's target weighted average cost of capital
(WACC) ensures developers earn exactly their hurdle rate while offtakers capture all residual commercial
upside [150]. PPA pricing executed above this baseline generate positive NPV, capturing the commercial
premium available to early developers. Quantifying this value-based ceiling, where hyperscalers under
scarcity pressure pay a premium above physical cost recovery, requires a dedicated willingness-to-pay
assessment specific to each transaction and falls outside the scope of this study.
 Risk allocation within that cost-based framing is asymmetric across volume, shape, availability, and
delivery. Volume risk is neutralized: nuclear assets deliver continuous baseload generation at 92-95%
capacity factor, and the reactor fleet in each case study is sized to match the contracted data center load.
Shape and profile risk is not modeled because APEX operates on annual and lifetime averages rather than
hourly matching, consistent with the capacity/baseload delivery scheme of Zhao et al. (2026) [103] rather
than a 24/7 hourly-matched product. Availability risk from planned refueling and unplanned reactor
outages is treated implicitly as a deterministic input (e.g, 2% unplanned outage rate for Greenfield).
Broader delivery risk, meaning the fleet's ability to serve a firm data center load through single-unit
outages and refueling overlap, is addressed selectively by the sizing optimization in Section 4.3.1 and the
reliability calculator, not by APEX itself; the C2N case (Section 5.1.4) discusses this in more detail.
 Bilateral contract structure determines how capital and operating costs are partitioned between
counterparties. The resulting PPA rates represent the post-incentive cost floor required for project
bankability, establishing a transparent baseline before any commercial scarcity premium is negotiated.

 • LCOE Benchmark: Full 60-year cost recovery under uniform market assumptions assigns all
 cost, volume, and market-price risk to the developer. This is the theoretical price floor: no
 bilateral contract can price below it without transferring risk the developer will not accept without
 additional compensation.

 • Two-Part PPA (20- or 30-year Terms): Splits procurement into a fixed capacity charge to cover
 debt service and fixed operations and maintenance (O&M) costs, paired with a variable energy
 charge; the offtaker takes on volume risk through a take-or-pay obligation on contracted MWh,
 and the developer takes on operational availability risk on delivery. Accelerating capital
 amortization increases the gross capacity charge by $7.6/MWh relative to the LCOE baseline. A
 $4.4/MWh residual value credit partially offsets this compression by compensating the offtaker
 for the NPP asset life in years 31 through 60.

 • Shared-Risk PPA: Establishes a baseline cost target and settles the difference between nuclear
 generation and DC consumption at the wholesale market price between the asset owner and the
 consumer. Excess generation is exported to the wholesale market at an assumed price of
 $45/MWh [151]; both parties share market-price exposure on residual generation. Since the
 reactor fleet is closely sized to the 2.6 GW DC and the DC load ramps up alongside phased
 commissioning, the export mechanism does little work in Figure 4-2. Shared-Risk becomes more
 valuable when either (1) the offtaker load is uncertain and the developer needs the export path as
 a hedge, or (2) wholesale export prices are meaningfully above the $45/MWh placeholder used
 here.

 • Mankala PPA: Functions as a cooperative joint-investment structure where corporate consumers
 provide upfront equity and purchase generated electricity at actual cost proportional to their

 equity at a zero cost, while the developer conventionally finances the remaining 60%, yielding a
 lower WACC. Thus, the $1.3/MWh discount relative to LCOE is a structural artifact of the zero-
 cost data center equity. Bilateral counterparty risk is eliminated because the offtaker is an equity
 owner. Residual value mechanics are inapplicable since the partners retain the asset for its full 60-
 year life.

5. CASE STUDY COLLECTION
 This section presents four illustrative/hypothetical case studies of nuclear-powered data center
deployment: brownfield (5.1), greenfield (5.2), LWR uprate (5.3), and LWR restart (5.4). The cases were
selected to represent diverse deployment timelines, site conditions, reactor technologies, and grid-
coupling configurations that were discussed in Section 2.4. Each case evaluates siting, techno-economic,
and socioeconomic considerations using the methodology and modeling assumptions described in Section
4.
 The case studies presented in this section represent exploratory analytical projections rather than
deterministic forecasts or project proposals. Therefore, the selected geographic sites and technologies
serve purely to establish baselines and do not constitute an endorsement from any reactor vendor or local
community. The total data center requested power level (comprising IT and cooling power) is matched by
nuclear power plant capacity and grid connection to meet availability requirements: this is a clearly
simplified setting as commercial projects could use a mix of demand flexibility, battery storage, and
natural gas onsite production to reduce nuclear capacity in hybrid energy infrastructures. However,
adopting a direct-matching assumption isolates the fundamental capital costs, siting challenges, and
socioeconomic impacts of nuclear integration, providing a baseline independent of proprietary developer
specifications.
 Given that deployment outcomes are highly sensitive to these proprietary configurations (e.g., project
cost, siting constraints and socioeconomic impact estimates), the results are intended to support
comparative analysis and are not recommendations for specific projects. Section 5.5 compares the cases
using common metrics, such as deployment timeline, PPA price, capital cost, employment, and local
economic impact. Relevant justifications, contextualization, and areas for future work are subsequently
detailed within the interpretation of each case study's results.

5.1.2 NPP Unit Sizing Optimization
 In this case study, the first step involves selection of NPP unit size and number of units that would
meet project requirements for the different behind-the-meter types of connections. The range of NPP size
considered here covers from small-type SMR (50 MWe) to giga-watt scale NPP. For this case study, the
power coupling options are either option 2 or 3 or 5 as follows§:

 • Option 2 (Colocation with Direct Connection and Full Power Export): Both the NPP and the DC
 maintain grid connections. The grid provides full backup power to the DC, so the NPP fleet is not
 required to guarantee extremely high level of availability (up to 99.995%, depending on DC
 requirements) on its own. To qualify as a nuclear-powered DC, it is assumed that the NPP must
 nonetheless satisfy at least 90% of the DC's demand. Since any generation more than DC demand
 can be exported to the grid at the assumed wholesale price of 30 $/MWh, there is a revenue
 incentive to build additional units beyond the minimum needed to cover the load.

 • Option 3 (Colocation with Direct Connection, Behind-the-meter): The DC has no independent
 grid connection and relies entirely on the NPP fleet for its power supply. The NPP must therefore
 be sized and configured to achieve a plant-level availability of at least 99.9% (assumed in this
 scenario), accounting for planned refueling outages (staggered across units) and unplanned
 downtime. This requirement drives the deployment of redundant units, making N substantially
 larger than the minimum number needed to meet average demand. Any generation in excess of
 DC demand may be exported to the grid.

 • Option 5 (Colocation, Microgrid with Backup Interconnection): The DC maintains a grid
 connection for backup power, but the NPP has no grid export capability. There is therefore no
 economic incentive to build units in excess of what is needed to serve the DC. As with Option 2,
 the NPP must satisfy at least 90% of DC demand on average. Option 5 is structurally equivalent
 to Option 2, with the single difference that revenue from exporting surplus electricity to the grid
 is always zero.

5.1.3 Site Assessment
 In the site and techno-economic assessment, it is decided to move forward with 330 MW capacity,
which would fit well for both 1 GW deployment (3 units with grid backup available in connection #2 and
#5) and 2.6 GW (8 units). Even though this power size was not identified as providing an optimum
solution by the cost optimization framework, several commercial SMRs provide power level in this range
(AP300, BWRX-300, Holtec SMR-300, Natrium, etc.) and the SMR unit size is close to optimal in all
scenarios considered. Thus, this SMR size represents a realistic candidate for the proposed case study.
 The siting analysis uses requirements of the SMR-300 reactor from Holtec. This is a 300-MWe
(approximately 1,050-MWth) advanced pressurized water SMR. The reactor is based on proven light-
water reactor technology while incorporating a simplified design that relies extensively on passive safety
systems. Depending on site characteristics and available infrastructure, multiple SMR-300 units can be
deployed in a modular configuration to achieve utility-scale generating capacity while allowing phased
investment and incremental expansion. The design is intended for deployment at both greenfield and
brownfield locations, including retired coal-fired power plant sites, where existing transmission
infrastructure, cooling water resources, transportation access, and skilled workforce can be leveraged to
reduce redevelopment costs.
 This analysis evaluates the feasibility of co-locating newly developed DCs with SMRs at a retiring
CPP site using the behind-the-meter coupling configuration using the ORSAGE siting criteria
summarized in Table 4-1. The siting assessment was conducted with the ORSAGE tool using 1-mile and
5-mile evaluation radii centered on the centroid of the existing CPP site (green dot in Figure 5-1). For
this analysis, each Holtec SMR-300 unit is assumed to require approximately 8 acres of land.
Accordingly, deployment scenarios involving three to eight SMR-300 units would require an estimated
24 to 64 acres of suitable land.
 The cooling water requirements summarized in Table 4-1 are reported as minimum resource flow on
a per-unit basis for each technology. The existing Rockport Generating Station withdraws cooling water
from the Ohio River using a closed-cycle recirculating cooling system with two natural-draft cooling
towers. Under this configuration, the majority of the cooling water is continuously recirculated, with only
makeup water withdrawn to compensate for evaporation and blowdown losses. Consequently, the facility
requires substantially lower water withdrawals than an equivalent once-through cooling system.
Consistent with this approach, the proposed NPP is assumed to employ a similar closed-cycle cooling
configuration. For a deployment scenario of up to eight Holtec SMR-300 units, the analysis assumes a
maximum cooling water requirement of approximately 288,000 gpm of available stream flow, based on
an estimated 3,600 gpm of consumptive water use per reactor unit (equivalent to approximately 36,000
gpm of available stream flow per unit under the assumed 10% withdrawal criterion). Given the Rockport
site's direct access to the Ohio River, the available water resource is expected to be substantially greater
than the projected makeup water requirements for the proposed SMR deployment, indicating that cooling
water availability is unlikely to be a limiting factor for reactor siting. Furthermore, the additional cooling
water demand associated with a 2.6-GW hyperscale data center would be relatively small compared with
the total water requirements of the nuclear facility and is therefore not expected to greatly affect the site's
overall cooling water adequacy.

5.1.4 Techno-Economic Assessment
 Brownfield C2N conversions may benefit from initial capital cost savings through infrastructure re-
use and higher ITC but may involve higher long-term operating costs [91]. Deploying at existing coal
sites provides structural cost advantages, notably the elimination of grid connection costs and a reduction
in OCC through the reuse of existing infrastructure and water rights. These benefits can be partially offset
by O&M premium to manage legacy BOP and electrical equipment (assumed 10% in this work,
consistent with [91]). The APEX financial model evaluates 60-year LCOE and two-party PPA price for
C2N configurations across varying OCC and O&M assumptions, fleet scales (1.0 GW across 3 units of
330 MW SMR versus 2.6 GW across 8 units of 330 MW SMR), and contract structures. The results are
compared in Figure 5-7. Results indicate that 30-year Two-Part PPA prices range from $55/MWh under
moderate assumptions to $66/MWh under conservative estimates for a 2.6 GW data center load. These
figures fall within or below the competitive threshold of new natural gas combined cycle (NGCC)
generation ($66-$96/MWh [153]).
 Three primary cost drivers determine C2N project viability:

 • Scale Economies: Expanding the data center load from 1.0 GW to 2.6 GW reduces the 30-year
 PPA price by $8/MWh under moderate assumptions. This cost reduction is achieved through
 batch-level construction learning across sequential units (see Appendix B-2) and the dilution of
 fixed O&M costs over a larger operational fleet.

 • Cost Assumption Variance: The shift from conservative to moderate OCC and O&M
 projections represents the single largest impact on project economics. For a 1.0 GW deployment
 under a 20-year PPA, adopting moderate rather than conservative assumptions reduces the total
 price by $12.64/MWh. Validating the 10% C2N O&M premium against empirical conversion
 data is a critical requirement for future analyses (see Table B-2).

 • Contract Term and Residual Value: A sub-lifetime (i.e., < 60 yr) PPA requires a lease-
 equivalent pricing correction. A 30-year PPA generates a residual value credit between -$3.54
 and -$5.32/MWh, reflecting the asset value retained by the supplier at contract expiration. For an
 accelerated 20-year PPA, this credit increases to a range of -$8.72 to -$13.12/MWh. Without this
 structural credit, the 20-year PPA price would exceed $75/MWh across all evaluated scenarios.

5.1.6 Discussion
 The Rockport case illustrates the potential advantages of brownfield deployment when the site
already contains substantial transmission, water, and industrial infrastructure. The site also provides
immediate qualification for energy-community tax incentives. These features may reduce capital costs
relative to comparable greenfield projects.
 The site assessment shows that the retiring coal plant location has sufficient suitable land for a multi-
unit SMR deployment within the immediate plant area, The principal siting limitation is not reactor
placement but co-locating the data center close to the reactor site while preserving access to fiber and
other supporting infrastructure. Accommodating a full 2.6 GWe data center necessitates careful
verification of (1) historical coal-plant water withdrawal limits against the combined cooling requirements
of the new reactors and data center, and (2) applicable regulatory prerequisites.
 The PPA estimates are sensitive to assumptions about infrastructure reusability, brownfield OCC and
O&M needs and the value of surplus generation under different coupling options. Repurposing the legacy
site intends to minimize grid exposure and avoids large, socialized transmission expenditures. This
approach ensures local grid dispatch is not forced onto steeper, higher-cost thermal supply curves (e.g.,
gas power plant). Nevertheless, contingent liabilities remain if the interconnection capacity of the retired
site cannot fully support the expanded footprint of the new nuclear fleet.
 The socioeconomic assessment indicates that the local benefits would be very significant across
construction and operations. At the same time, the composition of these impacts differs across facilities:
nuclear operations are more direct-labor intensive, while data center impacts are more concentrated in
supply-chain activity.
 Future work should quantify transition costs while the coal plant is retired and the nuclear fleet is
constructed, as well as the resulting effects on grid reliability, wholesale prices, and existing ratepayers.

 The PPA modeling in Figure 5-19 retains a $110/kWe interconnection cost because Option 4

primary offtaker is behind-the-meter. Operationally, this tie imports grid power during the phased
commissioning to bridge DC demand ahead of the 1 GW per year sequential reactor deployments. After 6
GW full commissioning, the interconnection assumes a dedicated standby safety role, ensuring the
commercially islanded campus insulates local ratepayers from ongoing transmission allocations. It should
be noted that the LCOE and PPA price reported in Figure 5-19 reflects steady-state 6×1 GW operation
only and does not include availability redundancy or transition-period supply costs.
 Three primary cost drivers determine project viability:

 • Scale Economies: Expanding fleet capacity from 1.0 GW to 6.0 GW acts as the dominant cost
 reduction mechanism, driving a $26.41/MWh decrease (20-year PPA). This leverages capacity-
 dependent learning rates and sequential multi-unit construction efficiencies.

 • Cost Assumption Variance: Capital and operational assumptions represent the second largest

 $9/MWh. OCC strictly dominates the cost structure, comprising nearly half of the total price.

 • Contract Term and Residual Value: Extending an accelerated 20-year PPA to a 30-year term
 reduces the required PPA price by an average of $1.88/MWh.
 NPP infrastructure accounts for a fractional share of total capital required for multi-gigawatt DC sites.

5.2.5 Discussion
 The greenfield case illustrates the potential for large-scale nuclear-powered data center deployment
on a new site, with sufficient land to support multiple large LWR units and a gigawatt-scale data center.
The approach provides the greatest long-term capacity expansion potential among the cases considered
but also requires the highest capital investment, the longest construction timeline, and the development of
new supporting infrastructure.
 The selected Texas site was found to have favorable land characteristics and access to transportation,
transmission, water, and fiber infrastructure. However, parcel continuity (not aggregated land area), water
availability, transmission capacity, and workforce access remain the binding siting constraint for large
reactors in Texas. Additional site characterization will be needed to confirm the suitability of the selected
location for the full reactor and data center configuration. The need to serve the DC before the full nuclear
fleet is online creates a significant interim-power challenge. In this case, the campus must initially rely on

August 31, 2026                                                                                           93

grid power or gas-fired generation, which introduces additional costs, emissions, reliability implications,
and contractual complexities in project financial models.
 The PPA results are also sensitive to capital cost assumptions and construction duration. With
projected power purchase agreement prices ranging from $54.2 to $91.8/MWh, greenfield economics rely
heavily on achieving economies of scale across multiple sequential units. A remaining challenge with this
scenario is managing the variability of the DC load: this will require on-site battery storage and power
conditioning system (e.g., STATCOM) to buffer these short-term demand swings. These specific load-
smoothing components are currently not costed in this scenario and should be evaluated in more detailed
work. New deployments carry the highest systemic ratepayer exposure, heavily relying on subsidized
regional grid expansion. Greenfield projects absorb direct connection liabilities (i.e., $110/kWe of grid
connection cost) but frequently trigger broader regional transmission buildouts. In theory, adding large
new electricity demand can lower average fixed grid costs if enough spare capacity exists [161].
However, clustering greenfield projects in congested areas could force utilities to run more expensive
power plants, driving up regional wholesale electricity prices. Recent studies highlight these market
pressures: Modeling [162] shows wholesale energy prices in constrained regions have already risen 2% to
6%; Spatial market analysis identifies Texas as one of the three most exposed regions in the nation,
alongside Virginia and the Carolinas [163].
 Future work should incorporate transition-period electricity supply, detailed regional grid modeling,
quantify combined water requirements under alternative cooling configurations, further evaluate the
construction and operating impacts of the DC itself.

 A LWR power uprate generally results in an increase in cooling water requirements because higher
reactor thermal power produces additional waste heat that must be rejected to the environment. As reactor
output increases, the condenser and cooling systems must dissipate a correspondingly larger thermal load,
leading to increased cooling water withdrawals and consumptive water use. The magnitude of the
increase depends on the type of uprate performed. Measurement Uncertainty Recapture (MUR) uprates
(typically up to 1.7%) generally produce only marginal increases in water demand and often require little
or no modification to existing cooling systems. Stretch Power Uprates (SPUs) (approximately 2–7%) may
require modest increases in cooling water capacity, while Extended Power Uprates (EPUs)
(approximately 7–20%) frequently require significant upgrades to cooling towers, circulating water
pumps, condensers, heat exchangers, and associated intake and discharge systems to accommodate the
additional thermal load. Consequently, water availability becomes an increasingly important
consideration for larger uprates, particularly at inland facilities that rely on rivers or reservoirs for cooling
[121] [164] [165].

 The Palo Verde site does not rely on a river, lake, or ocean for reactor cooling, but instead uses a

with the Palo Verde site. Although an LWR uprate would provide additional generating capacity that
could directly support increased electrical demand from data centers, it would also increase cooling water
requirements for the nuclear plant. Simultaneously, large data centers require substantial water for
evaporative cooling unless alternative dry cooling technologies are employed. Consequently, the
combined water demand of an uprated nuclear power plant and a gigawatt-scale data center could become
the primary limiting factor for future development at the site. Any integrated expansion strategy should
therefore include a comprehensive assessment of reclaimed wastewater availability, opportunities for
increasing wastewater reuse, implementation of hybrid or dry cooling technologies for data centers, and
optimization of water use across the co-located facilities to ensure long-term operational sustainability.

5.3.4 Socioeconomic Assessment
 Due to the lack of occupational data available to model uprate of LWR with IMPLAN, a quantitative
modeling analysis could not be completed. Instead, the following reviews the information available from
various uprate programs documented in the literature.
 A typical U.S. nuclear refueling outage — which occurs every 18 to 24 months and now lasts roughly
25 to 32 days — requires a substantial but temporary influx of workers on top of a plant's normal full-
time staff. At Arkansas Nuclear One, a two-unit Entergy site, routine refueling outages have been
supported by more than 1,100 full-time station employees plus an additional 1,200 to 1,300 contract
workers brought in specifically for the outage [169] [170]. A single-unit refueling outage at the D.C.
Cook plant similarly drew about 1,000 additional temporary workers [171]. This scale of staffing is
needed because outages compress a large volume of work into a short window — Duke Energy notes that
a typical outage involves roughly 9,000 discrete work activities, averaging about 343 completed per day
[172].
 When an outage also includes an EPU — installing the larger turbines, condensers, pumps, and
transformers needed to safely handle higher output — the temporary workforce grows well beyond a
routine refueling outages 1,000-to-1,500-worker range. The clearest U.S. example is Florida Power &
Light's Turkey Point 3 & 4 EPU, where construction employment reached roughly 3,200 workers in the
year before completion and was projected to peak at about 5,000 by early 2012, layered on top of the
plant's roughly 800 permanent employees [173] [174]. International EPU projects show the same
magnitude of increase: Spain's Almaraz plant, which normally employs about 400 to 600 workers, saw its
on-site workforce grow to roughly 3,000 workers during its power uprate outage [175]. In short, a routine
refueling outage roughly doubles a plant's on-site headcount for a few weeks, while an outage combined
with an EPU can multiply it several times over — 2,000 to 5,000 additional workers, sustained for
months rather than weeks — reflecting the far larger scope of physical plant modifications an uprate
requires.

5.3.5 Discussion
 Power uprates at existing LWR facilities represent a relatively near-term, cost-effective option for
serving incremental DC growth. The site assessment shows that Palo Verde (AZ) has sufficient land and
supporting infrastructure to host a gigawatt-scale DC. Cooling remains an important consideration in the
desert setting, which relies on reclaimed municipal wastewater. The available supply would need further
analysis to verify capacity availability to accommodate increased needs from uprate and potential DC
operation.
 Unlike new generation sources seeking interconnection, power uprates at existing NPPs typically
operate within the plant's already-established transmission interconnection agreements and grid access
rights [176]. Capacity additions from MUR and SPU typically operate within the historical capacity
headroom of existing Large Generator Interconnection Agreements, requiring no new transmission queue
filings [177]. For EPU that exceed existing interconnection limits, transmission providers evaluate
capacity increases through expedited modification studies rather than full greenfield queue processes
[178]. This distinction allows uprated nuclear capacity to reach commercial operation on a timeline
substantially shorter than newly constructed generating facilities.

100                                                                               August 31, 2026

 Relative to new build options, the capital requirement is much smaller, and the deployment timeline is
shorter. However, remaining useful lifetime of the plant is also shorter than that of a new built, reducing
return on investment. The main uncertainties concern the uprate class, plant-specific engineering scope,
and outage planning dictating uprate timeline. The economic results are sensitive to the allocation of the
capital-cost of the new generation between the DC and all other ratepayers (those may also benefit from
that new generation). Incremental capacity additions avoid structural grid investments, while the baseline
plant output continues to serve the grid.
 Executing power uprates leverages specialized, temporary labor surges rather than requiring the
sustained workforce mobilization of new reactor construction. While this generates localized economic
activity, existing literature provides limited detail on permanent and temporary employment associated
with uprates, so future analysis should include local investment, employment and revenue effects of the
NPP and the DC.

5.4 Case Study 4: LWR Restart
5.4.1 Description
 This scenario evaluates the LWR restart scenario, which involves returning a previously retired
commercial nuclear power plant to operational service after an extended period of shutdown. Unlike the
development of a new nuclear power plant, an LWR restart capitalizes on an existing nuclear site and
infrastructure, including the reactor and containment buildings, cooling water systems, electrical
switchyard, transmission interconnections, security facilities, and supporting infrastructure. By leveraging
these existing assets, the restart approach has the potential to significantly reduce project costs, shorten
deployment timelines, and mitigate many of the licensing and construction risks associated with new
nuclear development.
 This scenario focuses on the Three-Mile Island Nuclear Generating Station (TMI) also named the
Crane Clean Energy Center, located on Three-Mile Island in the Susquehanna River near Middletown,
Pennsylvania, approximately 10 miles southeast of Harrisburg. The site originally consisted of two
pressurized water reactors (PWRs): Unit 1, an 837-MWe generating unit, and Unit 2, a 906-MWe unit
that experienced a partial core meltdown in 1979. While Unit 2 was permanently shut down following the
accident, Unit 1 continued safe and reliable commercial operation for approximately four decades before
retiring in 2019.
 The TMI site remains one of the most strategically valuable nuclear energy assets in the United States
because of its existing high-capacity transmission infrastructure, licensed nuclear site characteristics,
abundant cooling water supplied by the Susquehanna River, and proximity to major electricity demand
centers within the PJM Interconnection region. These characteristics make the site particularly well suited
for supporting emerging high-demand applications, including AI, cloud computing, and hyperscale data
centers. The availability of existing nuclear infrastructure also creates opportunities for co-locating
energy-intensive facilities or supplying dedicated baseload power through direct power purchase
agreements.
 Reflecting these advantages, Constellation Energy announced in 2024 its intention to restart TMI Unit
1, which has been renamed the Christopher M. Crane Clean Energy Center. The proposed restart is
supported by a long-term power purchase agreement with Microsoft, under which the plant's electricity
output will help supply the company's expanding data center operations. This initiative represents one of
the first large-scale examples of an existing commercial nuclear reactor being recommissioned
specifically to meet the rapidly growing electricity demand associated with AI-driven digital

5.4.3 Techno-Economic Assessment
 This techno-economic assessment is applied to the TMI site as a representative LWR restart project.
Palisades cost data are included to establish a generalized financial baseline for commercial reactor
restarts. While the preceding spatial assessment isolates the TMI geography, integrating parallel financial
data from Palisades provides essential validation against the scarcity of domestic restart precedents. Two
complementary approaches estimate the plausible price range. The first, a cost-based forward calculation,
takes publicly announced capital investments directly as OCC inputs. For Three Mile Island Unit 1 (TMI-
1), a $1.6 billion [179] capital investment translates to $1,590/kWe, producing a required 20-year PPA of
$36.84/MWh under the PTC30 and $54.46/MWh under the ITC30. For Palisades, a $1.82 billion [180]
funding package establishes a $2,010/kWe capital basis, yielding a 30-year PPA of $39.31/MWh under
the PTC30 and $50.46/MWh under the ITC30††‡‡. Both sets of cost-based results fall below equivalent
greenfield and C2N deployments.
 The second approach reverses this calculation: taking publicly reported PPA estimates and solving for
the OCC that produces each price under standard financing assumptions. Independent analyst estimates
project the TMI-1 corporate contract settling between $98 and $115/MWh. This reverse calculation yields
implied OCC values of $5,000–$9,700/kWe depending on the assumed credit structure, four to six times
higher than the $1,590/kWe implied by the announced capital investment (i.e., $1.6 billion). Figure 5-30
shows the APEX-calculated PPA price decomposition for TMI-1 and Palisades across three credit
structures (no credit, ITC30, and PTC30).
 As illustrated in Figure 5-30, the gap between APEX estimates and public market estimates likely
signals a value-based pricing environment. The APEX framework prices only the cost-based revenue
floor and does not quantify this value-based premium; the scope of the model and its treatment of volume,
shape, availability, and delivery risk are documented in Section 4.3.2. Hyperscale offtakers require
continuous emission-free reliable generation at volumes that cannot readily be met at the required speed
and scale by renewables and storage alone. This scarcity shifts pricing power substantially toward the
developer [150], allowing restart asset owners to capture economic rent above pure cost recovery. The
premium also internalizes execution risks, including component degradation uncertainty and regulatory
licensing pathways.

5.4.5 Discussion
 LWR restarts offer a shorter timeline than new reactor construction by leveraging existing LWR
reactor plant and experienced workforce while avoiding the full capital burden of a greenfield project.
However, the restarted plant will have a shorter remaining operating life than a newly built plant,

106                                                                                August 31, 2026

reducing future return on investment. The TMI case illustrates both the opportunity and the complexity:
the site has suitable land and established transportation and transmission infrastructure for locating a
gigawatt-scale DC. The main site limitation identified in this case is fiber availability within the
immediate study radius requiring extension beyond the current five-mile study boundary.
 Existing plant infrastructure does not necessarily guarantee immediate grid availability, since regional
transmission constraints and reconnection requirements may affect the timing and cost of delivering
power to the data center. The TMI-1 restart preserved existing PJM hardware yet triggered a documented
reconnection delay (initially to 2031) due to regional transmission capacity constraints [194]. This
illustrates the broader finding that regional wholesale prices can rise sharply when large-load additions
outpace transmission investment [162]. Restart ratepayer exposure is intermediate between Uprate and
Greenfield: the physical grid connection is already in place, but the reactor comes back online into
congested regional grids where transmission investment costs are being socialized across ratepayers [97].
 APEX’s cost-based estimates establish a revenue floor under the assumed financing, capital-cost, and
tax-credit conditions, whereas observed or negotiated prices may include premiums for timely delivery,
reliable low-carbon power, refurbishment uncertainty, and project execution risk. Comparing the two
provides a first-order proxy for the risk premium markets assign to project-development risks that APEX
does not model. Restart economics are therefore highly sensitive to the condition of major plant systems,
relicensing requirements, refurbishment scope, financing terms, and remaining operating lifetime. Similar
sensitivities likely also exist in other case studies but could not be highlighted due to the lack of PPA data
to benchmark our model.
 Unlike uprates, which temporarily boost employment during outages, restarts permanently
reconstitute a facility's operational workforce. Future work should clearly distinguish between permanent
operating employment, temporary restart and outage employment, and broader construction and supply-
chain effects. In addition, the implications of dedicating restarted generation to a new large load on
regional grid reliability, wholesale prices, and ratepayers should be assessed.

configurations. The selected coupling configuration ultimately determines the magnitude of financial risk

 Across the case studies, the results indicate a tradeoff between deployment speed, capacity scale, and
project risk. Uprates and restarts can provide comparatively rapid incremental capacity but are
constrained by the existing fleet, while brownfield and greenfield projects offer greater expansion
potential but require more capital, infrastructure, and development time. In all cases, the results are
sensitive to coupling configuration and interim-power requirements (especially for new unit construction).
Additional work should focus on whether each configuration adds transmission needs, withdraws firm
generation from the grid, or shifts backup and infrastructure costs to existing ratepayers.

6. CONCLUSIONS AND SUMMARY

6.1 Summary
6.1.1          Outlook
 This report provides a comprehensive analysis of the potential for nuclear energy to meet the growing
energy demands of data centers. Their energy demand is projected to increase sharply in the U.S., with an
estimated additional requirement of approximately 12–84 GW of average production by 2030. This is
fueled by huge and ever-increasing investment in data centers of 700B$ in 2026. This power demand is
currently being met by the grid, with quick rise in solar power, battery storage, and natural gas. Data
centers have been observed to evolve quickly, with larger projects being built (GW scale and >$30B),
with onsite production, demand-side curtailment available to accelerate access to grid power.
 Nuclear energy can provide reliable, low-carbon, long-duration power for data centers, but new
nuclear construction generally cannot satisfy the earliest demand increases. Existing nuclear resources—
including operating reactors, reactor restarts, and power uprates—provide the most practical near-term
opportunities, although their potential is limited by fleet size, plant condition, licensing requirements, and
available uprate capacity. Announced nuclear projects and data center agreements could provide
approximately 6 GW of capacity in the near term – this plays a small but noticeable part in supporting
short-term DC demand. Longer-term (2035–2040) nuclear deployment has substantially greater potential
with around 50 GW nuclear capacity that could be deployed or dedicated to DCs.
 Such data center demand will have an impact on the overall fuel cycle with increased uranium and
enrichment services needed. The EPU+ uprates of the current LWR fleet (announced uprates are currently
mostly motivated by data center demand) could require the totality of the PWR fuel (1,250 t(EU)/year) to
increase their enrichment level slightly above the current 5% limit, which will increase annual natural
uranium and separative work unit requirements by approximately 9% and 12%, respectively, relative to
the 2024 U.S. fleet baseline. The deployment of SMRs considered under various NPP/DC agreements
(Meta/Oklo&TerraPower, AWS/Xenergy, Google/KairosPower) could require approximately 380 metric
tons (normalized to 19.75% enrichment) of HALEU by 2040, with an annual peak around 60
t(HALEU)/year.

6.1.2 Case Study
 The four NPP/DC case studies were developed to analyze the siting, socio and techno-economic
aspects of realistic but hypothetical projects. Those demonstrate a tradeoff between deployment speed and
expansion potential: LWR restarts and uprates can provide hundreds of MW of capacity within
approximately two to six years with only a few sites available throughout the US. The new reactor
constructions whether brownfield and greenfield projects offer greater capacity but require longer
development periods (7-10 year).
 The modeled cost-based PPA ranges vary by deployment pathway, all fitting in a 37-92 $/MWh
range, with the lowest modeled values generally associated with restart projects and the largest expansion
potential associated with greenfield and brownfield projects. Those PPA ranges appear competitive with
the ones currently observed from NGCC (LCOE*** around $66-$96/MWh [197]). Those projects relying
on existing LWRs require generally less capital but provide lower power level and for shorter lifetime

 The socioeconomic results indicate substantial potential employment and economic impacts during
both construction and operation. Estimated peak nuclear-only construction employment ranges from
approximately 3,400 jobs for the LWR restart to 17,500 jobs for the six LWR unit greenfield deployment,
with the brownfield 8-unit SMR case reaching approximately 4,500–5,100 jobs. Estimated annual
operating employment ranges from approximately 600 permanent direct nuclear jobs for the restart to
10,500 nuclear jobs for the greenfield case. Preliminary DC annual employment for a 6 GW size project
would scale up to 38,000 permanent direct and indirect jobs for operations. During construction, a 6 GW
data center could support up to 103,000 temporary construction jobs spread over 2 years, or less jobs if
the project timeframe was stretched over a longer time period.

6.2 Recommended Next Steps
 This report provides insight into the energy requirements of the rapidly expanding data center
industry and the potential role of nuclear energy in meeting those requirements. However, the data
center/nuclear landscape is evolving quickly: new hyperscaler announcements, technology developments,
regulatory changes, and supply-chain conditions could substantially alter the economic, siting, and
technical considerations for nuclear-powered data centers. The national nuclear/data center outlook
presented in Section 2 should therefore be updated periodically. Similarly, the fuel-demand estimates in
Section 3 should be revised as deployment plans and reactor-specific fuel requirements develop, with
future fleet-level assessments using detailed fuel-cycle tools such as Cyclus and DYMOND.
 The case study analysis should be expanded to include emerging deployment pathways, including
microreactors for data center power or backup, projects on federal lands, and more prospective concepts
such as nuclear-powered data centers on ships or in space. Other innovative options should be considered
in case studies, with tight coupling of nuclear power plants with data centers using nuclear heat in
absorption chillers [198] or nuclear/data center waste heat utilization in other industrial applications as
considered by EPRI [199]. Where appropriate, future studies should evaluate realistic energy systems that
combine nuclear generation with batteries, demand-side flexibility, natural-gas generation, and grid
backup. Analysis of specific non-hypothetical projects should be conducted in response to industry or
government needs and based on project-specific technical and financial information.
 The techno-economic analysis should be strengthened by incorporating nuclear cost estimates from
the ACCERT framework [200] and by extending APEX to evaluate value-based contract premiums,
transmission and distribution charges, interim-power costs, the risk allocation associated with different
contracts, and to perform uncertainty propagation. Additional analysis should quantify water
requirements, grid reliability effects, wholesale electricity-price impacts, and potential costs borne by
existing ratepayers. The siting methodology should be enhanced by incorporating water availability,
ambient temperature, cooling technology, and climate-related conditions into ORSAGE. This would
allow candidate sites to be evaluated not only on whether cooling water is currently available, but also on
which cooling configuration provides the most resilient and economically viable solution over the
facility’s operating life. The socioeconomic assessment should be expanded to further research data center
construction and operation jobs and revenue impact.

A-3. Limitations
 The present model is a batch-averaged linear-reactivity predictor and has the following limitations.
First, intermediate transition cycles are not calculated. Only the first transient cycle and the final target
equilibrium cycle are evaluated. Second, assembly-wise loading patterns are not explicitly modeled.
Therefore, detailed spatial leakage, local power peaking, and assembly shuffling effects are lumped in 𝑒0 .
The present model is therefore best suited for rapid scoping calculations and simplified screening
analyses. Finally, changes in batch number are restricted to the cases M=N-1 or N or N+1. Third, subtle
enrichment-dependent variations in reactivity and breeding behavior are not explicitly considered.

 The sizing optimization (Section 4.3.1) and PPA pricing model (Section 4.3.2) share a common
technoeconomic foundation but serve distinct analytical purposes. The sizing optimization identifies the
reactor fleet configuration (unit count, capacity, and phasing) that minimizes LCOE for a given data
center load and coupling option. The APEX PPA model then translates that engineering specification into
contract-grade pricing under realistic financing structures. Divergences are intentional: the sizing model
uses technology-neutral, scale-parametric correlations to evaluate the tradeoff between unit scale and fleet
redundancy, while APEX applies project-finance assumptions specific to each deployment pathway.

approximations [166]. Conversely, the INL (2025) [167] estimates derive from recent U.S. uprate project
histories, reflecting observed cost escalation that diverges substantially by reactor architecture and
modification scale. The framework categorizes modifications into discrete capacity categories:

B-2. Nuclear Capital Cost - Learning and Multi-Unit Assumptions
 Accurate capital cost estimation for advanced nuclear reactors requires structurally separating
chronological learning effects from multi-unit economies of scale. Both mechanisms reduce the OCC of
Nth-of-a-kind (NOAK) deployments but operate on fundamentally distinct economic drivers.
 Chronological learning curves capture the cost reductions achieved through sequential manufacturing
and construction experience. This accumulation of experience improves supply chain and workforce
efficiency over successive deployment phases. Following Hanna et al. (2024) [144], the learning rate (𝐿𝑅)
exhibits capacity dependence, where smaller reactor capacities (𝑃) achieve steeper learning trajectories
due to factory fabrication and rapid deployment cycles:
                                 𝐿𝑅(𝑃) = 0.145 × 𝑃−0.083                                          B. 1
 Learning effects manifest strictly at the batch level rather than among simultaneously constructed
units. Concurrent construction schedules preclude the immediate transfer of sequential experience across
concurrent projects. Consequently, batch-level learning only reduces the base cost of subsequent batches
(𝑏) according to Wright’s Law:
 𝑂𝐶𝐶𝑏𝑎𝑡𝑐ℎ (𝑏) = 𝑂𝐶𝐶𝐵𝑂𝐴𝐾 × 𝑏log2 (1−𝐿𝑅) B. 2
 Deploying multiple units at a single site yields immediate physical and logistical savings independent
of chronological learning. Abou-Jaoude et al. (2023, 2024) [201] [202] identify these economies of
multiples as arising from the amortization of fixed costs. Shared site preparation, common administrative
facilities, centralized control rooms, and bulk-discounted factory equipment generate immediate cost
advantages prior to the accumulation of construction experience.
 An exponential scaling factor quantifies the capital efficiency of co-locating multiple reactor
modules. This structural cost reduction applies to the cumulative unit count (𝑢) within a given batch (𝑏𝑢).
