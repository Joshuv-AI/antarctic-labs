ABSTRACT
Accurate short-term forecasting of lightning occurrence is important for a range of operational
applications, including to help discriminate lightning from signals of interest for remote sensing
and explosion detection applications. In this work, we investigate a convolutional U-net approach
for probabilistic nowcasting of lightning on a global latitude–longitude grid. The model predicts
the probability of lightning occurrence in the next 15-minute interval at 0.5 degree spatial
resolution using the previous hour of gridded lightning activity as input. Specifically, four lagged
input fields of log(count + 1) lightning counts are stacked as channels and used to forecast a
binary lightning-occurrence target at each grid cell. We train and evaluate the model separately
using two lightning data products— the World Wide Lightning Location Network (WWLLN), and
the Earth Networks Total Lightning Network (ENTLN)—with chronological train, holdout test,
and validation year splits.
We assess forecast quality using complementary probabilistic and threshold-based metrics.
Across both data products, the U-net produces useful short-term probabilistic forecasts and
demonstrates strong calibration, indicating that predicted probabilities are broadly consistent with
observed event frequencies. Event-discrimination skill is more modest, as expected for a
rare-event global forecasting problem, but remains operationally meaningful. Among the data
sources considered, ENTLN generally yields stronger results than WWLLN, consistent with its
higher detection efficiency and more complete representation of lightning occurrence. Spatial
analyses further show that forecast skill is strongest in regions with frequent lightning activity and
weakest in regions where lightning is sparse.
In addition to lightning-only inputs, we evaluate an extension that incorporates satellite-derived
cloud optical information, with the goal of improving skill beyond what can be inferred from
recent lightning history alone. We evaluate this using a smaller dataset consisting of lightning
data from the GOES Geostationary Lightning Mapper (GLM) and cloud data from the Advanced
Baseline Imager (ABI) over CONUS only. We also evaluate the ability of a simpler, Generalized
Linear Model (GeLM) approach to predicting lightning count. Finally, we present case studies
illustrating the model’s ability to capture storm evolution, highlight characteristic false positive
and false negative behavior, and demonstrate the practical utility of global gridded probability
forecasts. Overall, the results indicate that a U-net provides a credible and computationally
practical framework for near-real-time global lightning nowcasting, with potential value for
downstream decision support and event-screening applications.

1. INTRODUCTION

1.1. Motivation

Lightning has a significant impact on society, ranging from direct property damage to
environmental hazards such as wildfires and human fatalities [31]. While there are a number of
methods to mitigate the impact of lightning, prediction remains a vital but challenging avenue.
Lightning also impacts many of Sandia’s missions directly, including those pertaining to treaty
monitoring and explosion detection systems. Distinguishing natural phenomena like lightning
from signals of interest is fundamental to supporting these mission areas, but can be difficult in
data starved environments.
Long term forecasting is desired to predict lightning as a “background” signal, but is not currently
possible due to the complicated environmental conditions required to generate lightning, the
highly varied phenomenology and stochastic nature of lightning, and the lack of data available to
develop predictive models. As a result, rather than focus on long-term forecasting, “nowcasting”
provides a more practical approach by generating weather forecasts on a short time scale, ranging
from less than a minute to one or two hours into the future.
Nowcasting is often used for major weather events such as thunderstorms. Models that generate
nowcast predictions can include both numerical weather prediction models, which leverage
complex mathematics and physics, and data-driven models using machine learning and artificial
intelligence. Many such systems rely on radar, satellite, or other environmental data streams, but
these are not uniformly available worldwide; in particular, radar coverage is dense only in some
regions and remains sparse over oceans, complex terrain, and many lower-resource areas.
These limitations motivate the present effort. This LDRD develops a nowcasting model of
lightning activity for lightning-risk-mitigation applications. In this work, we focus specifically on
whether recent lightning observations alone can support accurate short-term lightning nowcasts,
with the longer-term goal of enabling a globally deployable and operationally simple forecasting
system.

1.2. Related Work

Lightning is a natural large-scale atmospheric electrical discharge generated by electrified clouds.
As with any electrical discharge through air, lightning consists of both initial channel development
via dielectric breakdown of the air [54] and electric current flow through the established channels
[6]. Predicting when and where lightning strikes remains one of the most difficult technical
challenges in weather prediction [28, 21, 72]. Both dielectric breakdown [30] and development of

 13

deep convection (i.e., clouds capable of producing lightning) [32, 3] contain such a multitude of
variables and uncertainties that direct physics-based simulation cannot currently predict lightning
at adequate scales, although repeat simulations with slightly different initial conditions can offer a
spread of useful possible outcomes (cf. ensemble weather forecasting). Thunderstorms are among
the shortest-lived and smallest-footprint high-impact weather phenomena and are sensitive to
microscale perturbations of initial conditions [72, 67]. Often, for a given location, severe weather
conditions like lightning, flooding, or tornadoes that are generated by thunderstorms can only be
accurately predicted once a thunderstorm already appears in proximity on radar or through
lightning detections, usually with tens of minutes of warning or less [62, 78, 81, 82].
Predicting lightning is of interest not only for the lightning hazard itself, which is important for the
general public, ground operations, power utilities, sporting events, aviation, and spaceports
[45, 10, 66, 67], but also for the severe weather hazards associated with lightning-producing
thunderstorms, such as flooding, high winds, hail, and tornadoes, that more generally affect people
and property. These hazards equate to billions of dollars each year in the United States alone
[64, 65], with some individual lightning-damage events costing millions of dollars, primarily due
to wildfire ignitions and the resulting loss of property and crop damage [14]. Near-real-time
lightning information can help forecasters predict high-impact weather hazards, including tornado
genesis and severe hail that are associated with a sudden lightning flash rate increase (“lightning
jump"), giving tens of minutes of lead time [62, 81, 62]. Researchers have also noticed a dramatic
increase in rainfall (“rain gush”) immediately following overhead lightning [46], which has more
recently been quantitatively investigated [49, 63]. Larger rainfall rates are strongly correlated with
lightning flash density, and both instantaneous rainfall and lightning flash rates are strongly
correlated with larger precipitation ice water content in the cloud.
For a numerical weather prediction (NWP) model to predict lightning activity based on physical
principles, a model of cloud charging must be implemented. This involves, at the least, an ability
to resolve cloud microphysics, i.e., the evolution and interaction of hydrometeors found in a cloud,
including cloud droplets, rain, ice crystals, hail/graupel pellets, snow, and aerosols [47]. It is
known that charge separation in clouds happens during deep convection whereby physically deep
cumulonimbus clouds grow to enormous heights, spanning roughly from the ground up to the edge
of the troposphere (∼ 10 km at the poles to ∼ 20 km at the equator) [38], and charge of different
sign is often vertically separated from one another in stratified layers of like charge [79, 68, 69].
Despite what we know about thunderstorm charging, a full physics-based model describing ice
particle collisions and charge transfer as a function of cloud liquid water content, altitude, and
temperature is currently intractable, and so cloud electrification models must be parameterized to
some extent. While these methods produce realistic thunderstorm charging rates and lightning
flash rates, there is admittedly significant sensitivity based on just how the collisional charging is
parameterized [42, 43], which is problematic because there are only a handful of laboratory studies
from which parameterizations are determined, with notable disagreements among the studies.
While NWP models generally produce lightning at onset times and rates consistent with
observations of storms, they “lack important timing and location specificity that observations can
provide in the very near-term” [12]. These NWP storm-electrification models are also
computationally expensive. Thus, for decades forecasters have looked to observations and data,
rather than or in tandem with NWP models, to help predict lightning. In particular, covariates of

 14

lightning [39] relating environmental conditions that are well observed in space and time to the
weather event to be forecast, include precipitation, cloud ice, wind, atmospheric moisture, and soil
moisture, to name a few [63, 22].

Using data to predict deep convection (and lightning) has long been the purview of nowcasting.
The goal of nowcasting is to predict and track severe weather events in near-real-time (e.g.,
lightning, winds, precipitation), to give advanced warning on the order of tens of minutes to hours
to ground-based and airborne stakeholders. Nowcasting often involves watching the development
and motions of already-existing storms via a variety of sensing modalities, including radar,
satellite imagery, and lightning detections. Although radar is among the most informative of these
data sources, its coverage is regionally uneven and far from global. Historically, nowcasting has
involved the use of NWP storm-electrification models like those mentioned above, combined with
near-real-time data assimilation. Convective Available Potential Energy (CAPE) in theory could
be used for data assimilation, but has obvious drawbacks because of the sparsity of upper-air
measurements, so that near-real-time updates to the models would not be feasible. However,
satellite observations can pick up some of the slack.

Much of nowcasting severe weather (and lightning) takes place once deep convection is already
under way. While ground-based Doppler radar data provide unprecedented information about
cloud convective structure and hydrometeor content, one of the most frequently updated datasets
with both wide coverage and precise timing and location information, even over oceans, is
lightning data [33, 80]. Because lightning activity is so strongly correlated with mixed-phase deep
convection, assimilating lightning data into NWP models can provide proxy updates to variables
important for convection. For example, lightning data were assimilated into WRF-ELEC and
COMMAS in [4, 24] and [41], respectively, to adjust cloud moisture and hydrometeor content.
Specifically, assimilating lightning data into WRF-ELEC significantly improved forecasting skill
over use of the model alone, which tends to overpredict the spatial footprint and severity of
convection, including overestimates of lightning flash counts.

Other data types are indicative of lightning activity and convective intensity. High reflectivity
from single- and dual-polarization radars indicates likely onset of lightning [62]; using observed
reflectivity as a lightning predictor gives on the order of minutes of lead time to lightning
initiation. Storm updraft speed and volume, another dual-polarization radar product, correlate
strongly with lightning flash rate [16] and give similar lead times. Other variables that influence
lightning initiation and flash rates include local terrain and topography [25]. The motivation for
data-driven machine learning (ML) techniques to tease out other correlative relationships between
weather observables and lightning activity, especially in response to knowledge gaps in lightning
physics, is clear.

In just the last couple of decades, ML has started to be used to try to forecast lightning better than
existing methods [48, 11, 7, 19, 12, 37, 34, 36, 81, 40, 44]. This direction of research and
development has become more and more opportune, considering the large amounts of
Earth-observing data that are currently available. For lightning alone, there are various methods
and platforms for data retrieval. Every lightning detection scheme has some detection efficiency
associated with it, with no one detection scheme reaching 100% because of physical limitations.
That said, the more types of lightning training and validation data one can introduce into an ML

 15

model, the more likely that the model will perform well under different circumstances, locations,
and seasons.
Lightning emits a broad spectrum of electromagnetic radiation, from a few hertz [8] up to optical
[76, 77, 15], and generates even more energetic processes that produce X-rays and gamma rays
[20, 35, 1, 52]. However, lightning detection and localization generally come in three broad
flavors: optical (nearly always spaceborne), low-frequency (LF) and below radio (e.g.,
ground-based national and global networks), and high-frequency (HF) and above radio (typically
ground-based local networks). Spaceborne optical sensors do not have 100% detection efficiency
because not all light from lightning in a cloud can reach the sensor. Two examples include the
Geostationary Lightning Mapper (GLM) and the Advanced Baseline Imager (ABI), which are
further described in Section 2.
Legacy ground-based lightning detection networks that complement space-based observations
include LF/VLF systems such as the World Wide Lightning Location Network (WWLLN) [18],
the National Lightning Detection Network (NLDN) [13], and the Earth Networks Total Lightning
Network (ENTLN) [83]. These networks differ in frequency range, spatial coverage, and the
degree to which they detect cloud-to-ground versus intracloud lightning, and comparisons with
space-based optical systems do not generally yield one-to-one agreement in observed flash rates
[13, 71, 55, 50, 80]. Lightning Mapping Arrays (LMAs) [70] are VHF systems that provide more
detailed views of lightning channel development in three dimensions, but their coverage is
regional rather than global.
Every type of lightning detection/observation system has some type of limitation, but using them
together or together with other weather and Earth observations can yield new information about
lightning, and lightning forecasting in particular. ML models to predict lightning are starting to
appear, but have so far showcased mostly modest or unclear improvement in lightning nowcasting
prediction compared to NWP models and data assimilation. There are a handful of different types
of ML schemes that are regularly used for lightning nowcasting, including decision-tree models,
such as random forest models [48, 11, 7], and gradient boosting models [19], that use the
combination of multiple decision trees to arrive at a prediction. Specialized types of Deep Neural
Network (DNN) models used for lightning prediction include convolutional neural networks
(CNNs) [12, 37, 34, 36, 81], specifically designed for image processing [58], where the ML model
is fed not only with local information from within a given cell of a fixed grid, but also with
information from adjacent cells in the grid; recurrent neural-network (RNN) models [40, 44] are
designed specifically for processing time-series data, such that they maintain memory of previous
time steps to predict the next step, and for lightning and weather imagery specifically they
typically include CNN layers within each time step.
For example, in [12], the LightningCast model used GLM lightning data and ABI cloud imagery
from the GOES-16 satellite to train a specialized type of CNN called a residual U-Net to predict
lightning occurrence from ABI imagery. The authors treated the lightning nowcasting problem as
a video-processing problem, predicting lightning within the next hour at each pixel in an ABI
image. In [12] specifically, the authors focused on predicting lightning initiation, as opposed to
presence of lightning in and following already-mature storms. In some cases, LightningCast was
able to produce an additional lead time of 10 to 30 minutes for lightning onset compared to using
radar observables alone, and in general LightningCast was able to produce 10 to 30 minutes of

 16

lead time before first lightning in a pixel. Overall, this was done with a critical success index
(CSI) of ∼ 0.4. While the scheme seemed to work fairly well, since deep convection as indicated
by ABI imagery is generally associated with lightning production, there were notable exceptions.
Geography impacted model performance, with worse skill over oceans than over land, which the
authors attributed to differences in oceanic versus continental cloud content. It was also noted that
inherent limitations in GLM, e.g., light not escaping cloud top for especially deep convection [60],
meant that training datasets had to be chosen carefully to omit these special scenarios, or else the
deepest convective storms might erroneously predict no lightning. Accurate lightning prediction
also declined in regions of lower lightning occurrence.

In [37], the authors likewise used a residual U-Net model to predict lightning, but used only GLM
lightning data, converting the data into 15-minute sequential images that they could feed into the
model to predict future image outputs in 15-minute increments, up to an hour into the future. The
model performed modestly well, and was compared to a simplified persistence model that
assumed that the state of the prediction in the next time step was the same as the state in the
current time step. Compared to the persistence model, the ML model performed comparably or in
some cases worse for short lead times (i.e., 15 minutes), but prediction accuracy was improved for
longer lead times. It is unclear how these ML models would compare to existing state-of-the-art
NWP lightning nowcasting models.

In [11], the authors used three separate ML models — namely, an NN, a random forest, and a
CNN — to predict lightning stroke density in each 1◦ × 1◦ grid cell over CONUS using WWLLN
lightning data and an assortment of other atmospheric variables (including precipitation and
CAPE, among others) from various sources as training data. 2010–2015 WWLLN data were used
as a training dataset, whereas 2017–2019 WWLLN data were used for validation of the models.
Similar to other studies, the models all performed worst when data were sparse, e.g., on the U.S.
west coast where there are low lightning flash rates. The models performed best in the
southeastern U.S. during the warm season, where lightning is typically frequent. The ML models
were compared to a simplified lightning parameterization scheme where the lightning flash rate
per area was set proportional to the precipitation rate 𝑃 times CAPE, which itself had a correlation
coefficient between its model output and truth of 𝑟 = 0.5 for most of CONUS. Note that both the
NN and random forest models were considered local models, where the inputs and outputs
corresponded to the same grid cell, in contrast to the CNN model (a nonlocal model), where input
could also come from adjacent cells. In [11] it was shown that the local NN and random forest
models had higher 𝑟 scores than the simplified parameterized model for most of CONUS, and
were better than the parameterized model by 𝑟 = 0.3 in some regions. Interestingly, the nonlocal
CNN model performed better than the local models in the southeastern U.S., but with worse skill
over the western U.S. The authors noted that these differences can possibly be attributed to the
different types of weather patterns that generate lightning in the west versus southeast, with more
micro- and mesoscale (local) forcing in the west, versus synoptic-scale forcing more typical in the
southeast. It is unclear how the ML models would have performed compared to more
sophisticated NWP models.

In [44], the authors used a complex RNN model, coupled with a generative adversarial network
(GAN), to make lightning-density forecasts using only an NLDN-type lightning dataset as input.
At each time step, the model took as input ten 6-minute images generated from the lightning data,

 17

and output the next ten 6-minute image predictions, enabling up to an hour of lead time; the model
was validated using data collected in the latter hour. The prediction accuracy of the model was
94.1%, 79.4%, and 65.8%, respectively, for 6-minute, 18-minute, and 36-minute lead times.
Despite this progress, an important operational gap remains. Many existing lightning-nowcasting
systems rely on multiple heterogeneous data streams, such as satellite imagery, radar products,
environmental reanalyses, or NWP output, which increases both system complexity and
vulnerability to missing data. Other approaches that use lightning alone have generally been
developed for more limited geographic domains or specific observing systems. Thus, there
remains a need for a globally applicable nowcasting approach that uses only recent lightning
observations as input, while still learning the spatiotemporal structure needed to predict
near-future lightning occurrence.

1.3. Contributions and Overview

Many existing lightning-nowcasting models require measurements from systems other than
lightning detectors, such that an operational forecast system must ingest and process multiple data
streams. In practice, however, some of these auxiliary data sources may be unavailable, delayed,
or inconsistent across regions. We therefore pursue a different approach: a lightning nowcasting
model and pipeline that rely only on lightning observations from the immediate past. This is a
nontrivial forecasting problem, because useful skill requires learning more than simple
persistence; the model must capture storm growth, decay, propagation, splitting, and merging
from recent lightning patterns alone. By requiring only a single stream of lightning detections,
this approach offers a minimal forecasting capability that can remain active even when other data
feeds are unavailable.
This motivation is especially important at global scale, where the availability, quality, and latency
of auxiliary data products vary substantially by region. A lightning-only approach is therefore
attractive not only for operational simplicity, but also for consistent deployment across diverse
geographic settings. In addition, we expect such a model to learn scientifically meaningful
spatiotemporal relationships that may help empirically test physical models and generate new
hypotheses about lightning evolution.
We also anticipate that this modeling approach will be relevant to Sandia monitoring-system
mission spaces, particularly those that must distinguish lightning-generated signals from signals
of interest. The contextual information provided by a nowcasting model can help mission
stakeholders make more confident decisions, improve event analysis, and support assurance of
mission objectives.
To this end, we develop a spatiotemporal deep-learning model based on a U-Net architecture,
similar in broad spirit to [12], to predict the probability of near-future lightning occurrence. The
key distinction is that our model uses only lightning data to predict future lightning, rather than
relying on cloud imagery or other auxiliary observations. Although a head-to-head comparison is
not feasible because of differences in data sources and preprocessing, our results suggest that
lightning-only input can achieve performance in the same general range as that reported in [12],

 18

despite relying on a more minimal input set. We also examine cloud optical density (COD) as an
additional predictor and find that, in the context considered here, it provides only modest
improvement beyond lightning-only input. Overall, this work evaluates whether recent lightning
observations alone can support accurate short-term global nowcasts, with the longer-term goal of
enabling a globally deployable and operationally simple forecasting system.
The main contributions of this work are:
 1. a global lightning nowcasting framework that uses only recent lightning observations as
 input;
 2. a spatiotemporal deep-learning model based on a U-Net architecture for predicting
 near-future lightning probability;
 3. an evaluation of the extent to which lightning-only input can approach the performance of
 more data-rich approaches; and
 4. an assessment of whether adding cloud optical density provides measurable benefit beyond
 lightning-only input.
The remainder of this report is organized as follows. Section 2 describes the datasets used in this
study, including WWLLN, ENTLN, GLM, and ABI, along with a discussion of data quality,
detection efficiency, bias, and the common gridding and preprocessing framework used to make
the datasets comparable. Section 3 presents the modeling methodology, including the
U-Net-based lightning probability prediction framework, the incorporation of cloud optical
density as an auxiliary predictor, and the validation metrics used to assess forecast skill and
calibration. Section 3 also introduces two complementary modeling efforts: a conformal binary
classification approach for uncertainty-aware event prediction and a separate framework for
forecasting lightning counts.
Section 4 presents the results of these approaches, including validation of the U-Net model on
WWLLN and ENTLN data, assessment of the impact of cloud data, selected case studies, results
for the conformal classification framework, and evaluation of lightning count prediction models.
Finally, Section 5 summarizes the main findings, discusses implications for globally deployable
lightning nowcasting and Sandia mission applications, and outlines directions for future work.

2. DATA

We train and test our model using three different sources of lightning data: World Wide Lighting
Location Network (WWLLN), Earth Networks Total Lightning Network (ENTLN), and
Geostationary Lightning Mapper (GLM). By building models and testing them on different
lightning detection systems, we can understand the generalizability of our model and identify
empirical insights into the relative strengths and weaknesses of the detection systems. We also
explore utilizing Advanced Baseline Imager (ABI) data to enhance lightning predictions by
supplementing with cloud-based data. The following is a brief description of each of these data
products and the data we extract from them.

2.1. World Wide Lightning Location Network (WWLLN)

WWLLN is a global network of very low frequency (VLF) radio lightning sensors hosted by
universities and research centers around the world. Their website claims a detection efficiency for
strokes 30 kA to be approximately 30% globally. Various studies have looked at the detection
efficiency of WWLLN in specific regions such as Spain (38%) [51] and Southeast Asia (32.9%
and 36.5% in two years) [56]. Studies have indicated that WWLLN can detect both cloud to
ground lightning and intra-cloud lightning [57]. Throughout the course of this project, we have
processed WWLLN data from the years 2015-2024. For the purposes of this report, our results
are taken from the years 2021-2023 for model training and testing. For each year in this period,
the WWLLN data yielded between 227 and 259 million extracted lightning strikes.
This dataset has been selected because it provides a consistent, long term, global dataset for model
development and training that is readily available for both Sandia and mission partners. The use
of WWLLN data, even with its apparent lower detection efficiency, allows this project to explore
potential nowcasting solutions that are directly applicable to our partners while still allowing for
flexibility during the research and development process.

2.2. Earth Networks Total Lightning Network (ENTLN)

ENTLN comprises 1800 global sensors for the detection of electrical wideband signals [84],
detecting electromagnetic pulses generated by lightning and reporting both the location and time
of the pulse [84]. ENTLN detects both intra-cloud (IC) and cloud to ground (CG) lightning.
Overall, ENTLN claims a stroke classification accuracy of 94 % for natural lightning and 88 % for
rocket-triggered lightning [84]. We use ENTLN data from 2017-2019 for model training and
testing. Over this period, we extracted between 812 and 990 million lightning strikes per year

 21

from the ENTLN records, corresponding to roughly four times the annual data volume of the
WWLLN product
ENTLN allows for both confirmation of our model approach in comparison to WWLLN, and
provides an opportunity to evaluate the use of a higher quality dataset. These data are not widely
distributed and are not readily available to mission partners, but could be made so with sufficient
justification.

2.3. Geostationary Lightning Mapper (GLM)

The Geostationary Lightning Mapper (GLM) [29, 59] is a space-based, single-channel,
near-infrared optical sensor designed to detect lightning. First operational in 2017, GLM contains
a 1372 × 1300 pixel instrument sensitive to 777.4 nm light (1-nm bandwidth) produced by atomic
oxygen in hot lightning plasma, with a pixel footprint of 8 km × 8 km at nadir and a frame rate of
2 ms (500 Hz). As of 2018, it was operational onboard two satellites covering the western
hemisphere between 54◦ N and 54◦ S latitude. GLM is deployed on the Geostationary Operational
Environmental Satellites (GOES-R) series, including GOES-16 through GOES-19, with a field of
view spanning the Americas and adjacent ocean regions. Optical imagers at 777 nm observe total
lightning, including both in-cloud and cloud-to-ground lightning.
Detailed technical information on the sensor can be found in [29]. Studies of GLM detection
efficiency report values ranging from the low 70% range [74] to the mid 90% range [5], depending
on time window and location. In this work, we use GLM data from 2021–2023, coupled with ABI
cloud data (described below), to train and test models and evaluate the utility of including features
derived from cloud data as additional predictors. Across these years, we extracted between 346
and 449 million lightning strikes annually from the GLM data. Although these totals are not
directly comparable to those from WWLLN and ENTLN because the spatial domains differ, they
nonetheless indicate that GLM detects lightning with high efficiency, comparable to ENTLN.
GLM provides a second modality of lightning detection (optical, in contrast to the RF focused
WWLLN and ENTLN) which aligns closer to the sensing modality of Sandia’s mission areas.
Unfortunately, GLM is restricted geographically, and is unable to completely support our desired
ouput for a global nowcasting model, but applying this data will allow the project to evaluate the
use of on-orbit optical datasets within the modeling framework.

2.4. Advanced Baseline Imager (ABI)

The Advanced Baseline Imager (ABI) [61], onboard the same GOES satellites as GLM, captures
spectral imagery of Earth in 16 bands — 2 visible, 4 near-infrared, and 10 infrared — at spatial
resolutions ranging from 0.5 to 2 km. Whole-disk scans occur at a 5- to 15-minute cadence, while
the shortest-duration mesoscale scans occur at a 30-second cadence. ABI generates both Level-1b
radiance data and Level-2 data products. Particularly relevant for anticipating lightning activity,
cloud-top microphysics can be inferred from spectral imagery spanning the visible to infrared,
enabling discrimination among liquid water, ice, and mixed-phase cloud tops [23].

 22

ABI data files were accessed directly from a NOAA Amazon Web Services (AWS) S3 bucket and
stored as NetCDF-4 (Network Common Data Form) files, the standard format for GOES-R series
satellites, including GOES-16 through GOES-19. ABI data products are categorized as Full Disk
(FD), Mesosector, and Contiguous United States (CONUS), each with distinct sampling rates.
CONUS imagery is produced every 5 minutes, and Full Disk imagery every 10 to 15 minutes.
Mesosector data provide higher-temporal-resolution imagery, with observations as frequently as
every 60 seconds, but are limited to small regions of the full field of view selected according to
NOAA operational needs.
The ABI data product of interest in this project is Cloud Optical Depth (COD), derived from ABI
band 2 at 0.64 𝜇m in the visible range and ABI band 6 at 2.2 𝜇m in the near-infrared range.
Separate daytime and nighttime algorithms are used for this product. COD captures cloud
scattering and radiative properties, which are influenced by both cloud thickness and cloud
particle size. For the U-Net model, we use COD data at 0.5 degree resolution to align with the
resolution of the lightning data products. Additional ABI data products, including Cloud Top
Height (CTH) and Cloud Particle Size (CPS), were also considered, but conclusive results were
not reached at the time of writing.

2.5. Data Quality, Bias, and Detection Efficiency

Of the three lightning data sources considered in this project, WWLLN and ENTLN are
ground-based networks that use time-of-group-arrival (TOGA) methods to estimate the locations
of lightning events. These networks report a latitude, longitude, and time for each event classified
as lightning, rather than the detailed time evolution of an individual pulse or sequence of pulses.
The locations and density of the ground stations used by each network directly influence regional
detection efficiency. As a result, detection efficiency can vary geographically and temporally over
many years. In addition, ground-based radio-frequency networks may have different sensitivities
to cloud-to-ground (CG) and intra-cloud (IC) lightning, with some networks preferentially
detecting higher-current CG strokes.
In contrast, the Geostationary Lightning Mapper (GLM) is a space-based optical sensor that
observes transient optical emissions associated with lightning. GLM records optical events in
pixels whose radiance exceeds a dynamically varying background threshold. Because GLM
detects optical emissions from above the cloud, its detection characteristics differ from those of
ground-based radio-frequency networks and can be affected by cloud optical depth, illumination
conditions, sensor viewing geometry, and background thresholding.
Due to these differences in detection modality and efficiency, none of the datasets should be
interpreted as a complete or unbiased record of all lightning activity. Rather, each dataset provides
a measurement of lightning activity conditioned on its observing modality, network geometry,
detection threshold, and processing algorithms. For example, an analysis comparing WWLLN
detections with observations from the space-based Lightning Imaging Sensor (LIS) aboard the
International Space Station found that approximately 30% of WWLLN detections were not
detected by LIS. This result suggests that optical detection systems can miss some
cloud-to-ground flashes or strokes that may be detected by ground-based systems [9].

2.6. Data Processing

To make the multiple datasets comparable and to support a generalized model-training framework,
the lightning observations are binned onto a common space-time grid that the authors refer to as a
“thundermesh.” This gridding scheme is inspired by historical lightning “thunderhour” datasets,
which have been used to provide more consistent evaluations of lightning activity across multiple
datasets and to support long-term statistical comparisons. Aggregating lightning observations in
space and time can help reduce the impact of dataset specific detection efficiency differences and
provides a robust representation of lightning activity at the thunderstorm scale, rather than at the
individual flash, stroke, or sub-flash level [73]. This approach is appropriate for the Striker project
because the objective is not to resolve individual flash dynamics, but instead to estimate the
probability of lightning activity within a specified area and time interval.
A thundermesh is a space-time grid constructed using consistent latitude, longitude, and temporal
spacing across the globe. For each dataset, lightning reports are grouped into discrete,
non-overlapping time intervals measured in minutes. Within each time interval, reported lightning
events are assigned to the corresponding latitude-longitude grid cell, and the total number of
events in each cell is accumulated. The resulting dataset contains the center latitude and longitude
of each occupied grid cell, the associated time interval, the grid-cell area, and the number of
lightning events reported in that cell.

3. METHODS

3.1. U-Net-Based Lightning Probability Prediction

We modeled near-real-time lightning forecasts on a global 0.5 degrees latitude–longitude grid
using a type of convolutional neural network called a U-Net. The forecasting task was to estimate,
at every grid cell, the probability of observing at least one lightning event during the next
15-minute interval based on the recent history of gridded lightning activity. This directly supports
the intended external need of providing lightning activity context for near-real-time applications,
particularly in situations where data are not readily available or timely enough to support rapid
decision making.

In this setting, each analysis time is represented by a spatial map of lightning counts, so the
problem can be viewed as learning how lightning patterns evolve over space and time on short
lead times. A U-Net is well suited to this task because lightning nowcasting can be posed as a
spatial prediction problem on evolving gridded fields, where both fine-scale storm morphology
and broader spatial context matter. Its encoder–decoder structure learns multiscale features
associated with storm initiation, growth, decay, and propagation, while skip connections preserve
the spatial detail needed for accurate cell-by-cell probability forecasts on the original grid. As a
result, the architecture is well matched to learning how recent lightning patterns evolve into
near-future lightning occurrence. The remainder of this section describes the selection of spatial
and temporal resolution, the construction of input sequences and binary targets, the choice of
network architecture, and the method used to split multi-year datasets into subsets for model
training, holdout testing, and final validation.

In addition to the 0.5 degree, 15-minute space–time grid configuration used for the reported
results, we also evaluated a finer spatial grid at 0.1 degree resolution with 15-minute time
windows, and a finer temporal discretization using 0.5 degree spatial resolution with 5-minute
time windows. Among the combinations we tested, the 0.5 degree grid with 15-minute time
windows produced the most accurate predictions, and it was therefore adopted for the final
analysis. The prediction domain was defined on a regular 0.5◦ × 0.5◦ latitude–longitude grid with
longitude spanning [−180◦ , 180◦ ) and latitude spanning [−60◦ , 60◦ ). This resulted in a 240 × 720
grid at each time step. We restricted the latitude range to [−60◦ , 60◦ ) in order to avoid the polar
regions, where lightning is even more sparse and where a standard latitude–longitude grid is less
favorable geometrically.

For each forecast time 𝑡, the model input consisted of lightning count fields from the previous four
15-minute intervals, corresponding to the previous hour:

                      𝑡 − 60 min,    𝑡 − 45 min,        𝑡 − 30 min,   𝑡 − 15 min.

A small number of entire days were missing from some yearly records because the underlying
lightning data product was unavailable. This affected the WWLLN and GLM data sets, but not
ENTLN. Any such missing days were removed before model fitting and evaluation so that
training, holdout testing, and final validation were all carried out using only valid samples. Aside
from these source-specific data availability issues, the same overall modeling and preprocessing
procedure was applied across all three lightning data sources considered in the study.

The U-Net design was guided by the spatial organization of lightning and by the need to issue a
forecast at every grid cell. Lightning occurrence is structured by storm systems rather than by
independent cellwise events, so the model must represent both localized dependence and
larger-scale spatial coherence. In this implementation, the encoder comprised four convolutional
blocks with 16, 32, 64, and 128 channels, followed by a 256-channel bottleneck. Each block used
two 3 × 3 convolutions with ReLU activations, with 2 × 2 max pooling for downsampling. The
decoder then restored spatial resolution using transposed convolutions and skip connections from
the corresponding encoder levels, producing the final gridded lightning probability forecast in the
output layer.

Our initial efforts explored a spatial point process formulation because lightning is fundamentally
event-based, but in practice that approach did not provide the level of predictive accuracy we
wanted and was substantially more computationally expensive. The U-Net therefore offered a
more effective balance among forecast skill, computational efficiency, and ease of deployment on
full global grids. It is also consistent with prior lightning nowcasting work; for example,
LightningCast uses a U-Net with a broadly similar encoder–decoder structure.

We did not impose special cyclic handling at the longitude seam, nor did we apply specialized
treatment near the domain boundaries beyond the standard convolutional operations used by the
network. Although periodic longitude handling could in principle be incorporated, the simpler
rectangular treatment did not appear to introduce substantial edge effects in our experiments and
was adequate for the present study.
For each lightning data source, three years of data were used in chronological order. One year was
used for training, the following year served as a holdout test set for monitoring loss and assessing
convergence, and the subsequent year was reserved for final validation using the performance
metrics described later in the report. This chronological split was chosen to reflect the intended
forecasting setting and to reduce the possibility of overly optimistic performance estimates that
could arise from mixing temporally adjacent samples between model development and final
evaluation. The specific calendar years depended on the data source, as documented in the Data
Section.
Training was performed on full global images rather than on spatial patches or tiles. Each sample
corresponded to a single forecast time and included the full 240 × 720 target grid together with the
four preceding 240 × 720 input grids. Training samples were shuffled across time to improve
stochastic optimization, while the temporal ordering within each four-step input sequence was
preserved. The model was trained in PyTorch using the Adam optimizer with learning rate 10−3
and binary cross-entropy loss as the objective function, applied at every grid cell. Because
lightning occurrence is sparse over the global domain, we also experimented with weighted
versions of the loss in an attempt to compensate for class imbalance. In practice, however, the
resulting fitted models were nearly identical to those obtained without weighting, so no explicit
class-imbalance correction was used in the final configuration. No additional regularization
methods such as dropout, weight decay, or gradient clipping were used. After each training epoch,
we computed the mean loss over the entire holdout test year. The loss decreased monotonically
during the first 10 epochs but had begun to level off by the end of that period, suggesting that
additional training would provide limited improvement. On that basis, training was stopped after
10 epochs.
The model was implemented in PyTorch, and the U-Net code used in this study is available in the
project repository:

 https://cee-gitlab.sandia.gov/lightning-forecasting/striker-model.

In this section, we extend the U-Net model inputs to include recent cloud optical depth (COD)

aggregated to 0.5 degree spatial resolution by computing the mean COD value from the ABI data
within each grid cell. To reduce the impact of extensive missingness in the COD product and to
focus training on a region with more consistent coverage, the analysis was restricted to the
CONUS domain, using latitude bounds of 25 degrees to 50 degrees and longitude bounds of -100

 29

degrees to -80 degrees. The U-Net architecture was modified to accommodate the added COD
input channels while otherwise preserving the general modeling framework described earlier.

We also evaluated the effect of feature scaling on model performance. Z-score normalization was
applied to the ABI-derived COD data as well as the GLM and WWLLN inputs, and performance
was compared between scaled and unscaled versions of each model. Normalization improved
performance for the combined model that used both lightning and COD inputs, whereas the
GLM-only and WWLLN-only variants showed little change. Model training used the U-Net

learning rates up to 0.005. For each model variant, the best-performing learning rate identified
during tuning was used for the final model fit.

3.3. Validation Metrics

Lightning probability forecasts directly support end users by providing contextual information that
is robust to data latency and dropout issues. Probability outputs allow analysts to incorporate that
information into their decisions without being overly prescriptive. Because our models output a
probabilistic forecast of lightning occurrence within a prescribed space–time window, it is
important that the validation metrics assess both probabilistic validity and discrimination
ability.

Probabilistic validity measures whether predicted probabilities correspond to observed event
frequencies. For example, among all space–time windows for which the model predicts
𝑝ˆ𝑖, 𝑗,𝑡 = 0.4, lightning should ideally be observed 40% of the time. Discrimination, by contrast,
measures how well the model distinguishes between space–time windows in which lightning
occurs and those in which it does not. For threshold-based discrimination metrics, we use the
notation 𝑌ˆ𝑖,𝜏𝑗,𝑡 = 1[ 𝑝ˆ𝑖, 𝑗,𝑡 ≥ 𝜏] to denote a binary lightning prediction at spatial grid cell (𝑖, 𝑗) and
time 𝑡 under decision threshold 𝜏. Both of these properties are important for supporting our
external objectives.

3.3.6.       Summary

Taken together, these metrics provide a complementary evaluation framework for probabilistic
lightning forecasting, and also allow for evaluation of different datasets in the model training
process. Precision–recall and CSI characterize event detection performance under thresholding,
with CSI additionally providing a principled basis for selecting an operational threshold.
Calibration curves and uniform ECE assess whether predicted probabilities are valid across the
forecast range. Finally, the inverse-prevalence-weighted Brier Score measures overall
probabilistic accuracy while accounting for severe class imbalance. This combination enables
assessment of both the decision-making value and the statistical validity of near-real-time
lightning probability forecasts.

3.4. A Conformal Binary Classification Approach

Conformal prediction (CP; [75]) is a distribution-free uncertainty procedure with a finite sample
coverage guarantee under only mild dependency assumptions. The original method requires
retraining the model for each new test case, but for large machine learning models with many
parameters such as neural networks, including our U-net, the computational burden is too high.
CP, therefore, became more widely used following the adoption of “inductive conformal
prediction" (ICP) [53] which only requires training the model once. ICP proceeds by partitioning
the full training dataset into a proper training set, which is used to train the predictive model, and a
calibration set, which is used to calibrate uncertainty to obtain the desired coverage level. Given a
nonconformity score, 𝑆 and an type-I error rate 𝛼, the (1 − 𝛼) prediction region is guaranteed to
cover the true label 𝑌𝑖, 𝑗,𝑡 with a probability of at least (1 − 𝛼) if the calibration set and the test set
are exchangeable (see [53] for details on method assumptions). As pointed out in [17], although
ICP sets satisfy marginal coverage, class-specific coverage may be poor, particularly for
underrepresented classes. We find this is the case for lightning forecasts, as lightning strikes
account for a small minority of the observations in our dataset. Therefore, it is preferable to apply
a class-wise conformal procedure, often referred to as Mondrian conformal prediction [75, 26].
We calculate a class-wise conformal p-value for each class {0, 1} by spatial location using the

3.5. Forecasting Lightning Counts

In this section, we model lightning counts directly, rather than modeling only the probability that
one or more lightning strikes will occur. The objective is to use recent lightning activity to
forecast the number of strikes in the next 15-minute window at each grid cell. Our ultimate goal is
not exact point prediction of the count, which is inherently difficult in this context, but rather to
provide a statistically principled estimate of storm magnitude together with valid uncertainty
quantification. To that end, we use a generalized linear model (GeLM) framework, which is more
interpretable than the neural-network approach used elsewhere in this report and provides a
natural basis for prediction intervals. We specifically utilize models in the zero-inflated GeLM
framework to properly account for the overwhelming number of zero counts in our dataset.

3.5.6. Spatially Varying Coefficients (SVC)

As described above, the objective of this section is to predict lightning count on a global grid. A
natural baseline approach is to fit a single zero-inflated GeLM using all available training data. In
that formulation, the regression coefficients are fixed globally, so the effects of recent local and
neighboring lightning activity are assumed to be the same everywhere on Earth. Although this
global model provides a useful reference, it is unlikely to fully capture local and regional
differences in storm behavior and climatology.
To allow for more localized behavior, we also consider a spatially varying coefficient (SVC)
approach motivated by the broader SVC literature [27]. In the classical formulation of spatially
varying coefficient models, regression coefficients are themselves modeled as spatial processes, so
that nearby locations have related coefficient values through an explicit spatial dependence
structure. We use a simpler approach for computational reasons given our large dataset. Rather
than imposing a hierarchical spatial prior over the coefficients, we instead fit a separate
zero-inflated GeLM at each grid cell. Thus, each grid cell (𝑖, 𝑗) receives its own set of estimated
parameters, and coefficients are estimated independently across cells. In this way, the relationship
between recent lightning activity and future count is allowed to vary according to local storm
patterns.
Because the SVC approach relies on fitting a separate model at each grid cell, a minimum amount
of local signal is needed for stable estimation. When using an entire year of data to train our
models at the 15-minute, 0.5 degree resolution, many grid cells provide a sufficiently large
number of observations, including both zero and nonzero outcomes. However, some locations
have too few nonzero lightning events to support reliable estimation of the count component. We
therefore impose an empirical inclusion criterion: a grid cell is included in the SVC analysis only
if the number of nonzero observations at that location exceeds 40, i.e., if

4.2. Performance with Cloud Optical Density

To assess whether cloud information improves short-term lightning nowcasts, we incorporated
ABI cloud products into the U-Net framework. Among the ABI variables we examined, cloud
optical depth (COD) showed the strongest correlation with observed lightning counts and was
therefore selected as the cloud predictor used in these experiments. We paired COD primarily
with GLM data, since GLM has lower sparsity and higher detection efficiency than WWLLN and
because GLM and ABI are observed over the same spatial domain. We also paired COD with
WWLLN as a reference case for comparison with the lightning-only WWLLN results presented in
the previous section.
As part of our analysis, we also examined whether the slower evolution of clouds relative to
lightning justified a longer input history for COD. Specifically, we compared models using either
𝑛 = 4 or 𝑛 = 12 previous time points as input. Although a longer context window was expected to
provide additional cloud-evolution information, the resulting predictions were nearly unchanged.
We therefore used 𝑛 = 4 previous time points in the final ABI-integrated models.
We also compared models trained on 5-minute and aggregated 15-minute windows. Because
15-minute windows have a higher probability of containing at least one lightning event, they were
expected to yield improved classification performance. In practice, however, the 5-minute models
performed comparably to the 15-minute models. We therefore prioritized the 5-minute
configuration for training and evaluation of the ABI-integrated models.
A major practical issue in using COD was missing data. For example, in June 2023, the processed
COD data initially contained 6,635,720 missing values. Figure 4-15 illustrates this issue for a
single time point, where white regions indicate missing values. Most of the missingness occurred
at night, so we applied daylight masking to retain only daylight observations. After daylight
masking, the number of missing values was reduced to 2,149,711, corresponding to an
approximately threefold reduction for that month. This reduction in missingness improved the
quality of the model training data and led to stronger performance in the ABI-based models.
Table 4-1 and Figure 4-16 summarize model performance. We used the area under the
precision–recall curve (AUC) to assess discrimination across thresholds, and selected a
representative decision threshold using the maximum critical success index (CSI). The strongest
performance was obtained with the combined ABI + GLM model, which slightly outperformed
the GLM-only model, increasing AUC from 0.771 to 0.782 and maximum CSI from 0.576 to
0.594. In contrast, COD alone performed poorly as a lightning predictor, as seen in the ABI-only
models for both GLM and WWLLN targets. For WWLLN, adding ABI data did not improve
overall skill: the combined WWLLN + ABI model performed nearly the same as, and slightly
worse than, the WWLLN-only model. Overall, these results suggest that COD provides modest
added value when paired with the higher-quality GLM lightning observations, but limited benefit
when paired with the sparser WWLLN data.
These experiments indicate that cloud context can improve lightning nowcasting, but the benefit
depends strongly on the quality of the accompanying lightning observations. In the setting
considered here, COD provided only a modest improvement beyond lightning-only input, and only
when paired with GLM. This suggests that much of the predictive information contained in COD

States from the upper north-western corner to Florida in the south-east. There is additionally

respectively, predict the full set {0, 1} around these locations of known lightning, indicating

lightning in the data leads to high uncertainty.

These results confirm that our conformal prediction methodology can achieve nominal coverage
rates, but the case study raises questions about the informativeness of the resulting prediction sets.
Although these sets may represent uncertainty appropriately in a formal sense, we ultimately seek
uncertainty quantification that is more actionable for the end user. A more useful approach would
be to quantify uncertainty in the lightning probability estimate itself. However, traditional
conformal prediction methods require knowledge of the true lightning probability in order to
calibrate such intervals properly, and that quantity is not available in practice. A potential way
around this limitation would be to construct conformal prediction intervals for lightning
probability directly using a model-trained heuristic, as suggested in [2]. In that work, pixelwise
quantile regression performed best in their setting, although its implementation for discrete classes
is less straightforward than some alternative approaches, such as heuristics based on residual
magnitude or softmax scores. Because the U-Net treats each lightning map as an image, these
image-based conformal methods appear to be a natural fit here, but applying them would require
additional development, including custom loss functions to determine the interval endpoints.

5. CONCLUSION

This work investigated a U-net-based approach for short-term probabilistic forecasting of
lightning occurrence on a global latitude–longitude grid. Using the previous hour of gridded
lightning activity as input, the model produced forecasts of lightning occurrence probability for
the subsequent 15-minute interval at 0.5 degree spatial resolution. Across the three lightning data
products considered in this study—WWLLN, ENTLN, and GLM—the results indicate that this
approach provides a practical and scientifically credible framework for global lightning
nowcasting. In particular, the model consistently produced well-calibrated probabilistic forecasts,
while also demonstrating operationally useful discrimination skill in a forecasting problem that is
both highly imbalanced and intrinsically difficult.
One of the clearest findings of the study is that forecast quality depended in part on the lightning
product used to define the target. The models trained on the ENTLN and GLM datasets generally
outperformed those trained on the WWLLN data. These results are consistent with the roughly
fourfold greater detection efficiency of those products. By contrast, WWLLN still supported
useful prediction, but its lower detection efficiency likely limited both model fitting and skill
measured during validation. With that in mind, if a nowcasting model is to be considered to
support mission systems, regular access to a higher detection efficiency data product should also
be a consideration.
A second major finding is that probabilistic calibration was a particular strength of the U-net
forecasts. Across the evaluated data sets, calibration curves were close to the identity line, and the
corresponding uniform ECE values were remarkably low. This is important because in many
operational settings the forecast probabilities themselves, rather than binary thresholded decisions,
may be used for downstream screening or risk communication. At the same time, the
threshold-based metrics show that discrimination remained imperfect, which is expected for
15-minute-ahead global lightning forecasting. The optimal CSI thresholds yielded a reasonable
balance between missed detections and false alarms.
The spatial analyses further clarified where the model performed best and where limitations
remained. Across products, skill tended to be strongest in regions with frequent lightning
occurrence and weakest in regions where lightning was rare or highly intermittent. Where events
are common, the model has more examples from which to learn recurring spatial structure, and
the corresponding verification metrics are estimated with greater stability. We also found little
seasonal dependence in the validation results.
An important practical conclusion is that a relatively compact U-net can capture meaningful
short-term evolution of global lightning activity without the computational burden associated with
more elaborate point-process-based formulations. We initially considered a spatial point process
approach because lightning is fundamentally event-based, but the U-net provided a better balance

 71

of accuracy and computational efficiency for the gridded forecasting task considered here. The
fact that useful skill was obtained using only recent lightning history suggests that substantial
predictive information is already contained in the recent spatial organization of lightning itself.
Further, it supports the idea that a near-real-time nowcasting capability can be applicable and
useful to operational systems.
The experiments incorporating cloud information were intended to test whether satellite-derived
cloud optical properties could improve forecasts beyond what was available from recent lightning
observations alone. Cloud inputs provided only limited gains over the lightning-only model. One
possible explanation is that COD contributes limited new information once recent lightning
activity is included, so any additional variation it captures may be only weakly related to
subsequent lightning. We note that the value of cloud information may depend on region, storm
regime, and data source, but we did not examine this in the report. In future work, a more in-depth
exploration of which cloud data features are most informative should also be conducted.
The case studies provide an additional, qualitative perspective on model behavior. They show that
the U-net forecasts are not merely reproducing static climatological patterns, but instead respond
to the evolving spatial structure of recent lightning activity in ways that are often operationally
meaningful. In several examples, the model successfully captured storm motion, while the
false-positive and false-negative examples help illustrate the situations in which the model
remains vulnerable. These examples are important because aggregate skill scores alone do not
fully reveal how the model behaves in specific meteorological scenarios or how useful the
probability fields may be for a human analyst.
Several promising directions for future work could further refine the binary classification
approach. First, additional predictor fields beyond recent lightning history and cloud optical data
may improve performance, particularly in regions where lightning is sparse. Second, the
evaluation could be extended to decision-focused use cases relevant to downstream applications,
including screening for explosive events, background characterization for event detection, and
operational hazard monitoring. Finally, future work could explore whether a single jointly trained
model across multiple observational products offers advantages over the source-specific models
considered here.
Using location-specific conformal prediction methods, we also generated set-valued lightning
forecasts with class-conditional coverage to explicitly account for uncertainty in this highly
imbalanced setting. Results show near-nominal coverage across multiple 𝛼-levels, with false
negative and false positive rates closely matching 𝛼 for both classes. The method identifies
substantial uncertainty near active storm regions and over oceans, frequently assigning the full set
{0, 1} and only rarely the singleton set {1}. Overall, the approach provides reliable uncertainty
quantification, but the rarity of lightning events limits the sharpness of confident lightning-only
predictions. Future research should investigate methods to improve the sharpness of
class-conditional conformal sets for rare events, for example through alternative nonconformity
scores or approaches that better incorporate temporal and spatial dependence.
In addition to probability forecasts, we explored generalized linear models for direct prediction of
lightning counts using zero-inflated Poisson and zero-inflated negative binomial formulations.
These models were designed to handle the large number of zeros in gridded lightning data while

 72

incorporating simple spatial and temporal lag effects, including a spatially varying coefficient
extension. This framework proved promising from both interpretability and modeling
perspectives, as it provides a transparent way to relate recent local lightning activity to future count
forecasts while naturally accommodating excess zeros. The results suggest that these count-based
models are a useful complement to the U-net-based probabilistic forecasts and provide a strong
foundation for future work on uncertainty quantification and spatially informed extensions.
Future work on predicting lightning counts can proceed in several directions. First, estimation of
the location-specific model coefficients in the SVC method currently depends only on the
observations at each location, with no spatial effects or correlation incorporated. However, as seen
in Figures 4-22 and 4-23, there appears to be substantial spatial structure in the 𝛾 𝐸 and 𝛾𝑊
coefficient estimates. Incorporating spatial dependence, for example through coefficient
smoothing, may therefore help reduce outlier predictions and yield a more parsimonious model.
Another natural direction is the inclusion of uncertainty quantification, such as calibrated
prediction intervals for both the ZiP and ZiNB models. In addition, extending conformal
prediction methods to spatial count data would be a valuable continuation of this work.
In summary, the results show that U-net-based global lightning nowcasting is feasible,
computationally practical, and capable of producing well-calibrated and operationally useful
short-term probability forecasts. The strongest performance was obtained when the target
lightning product provided more complete event detection, but even the more limited WWLLN
product yielded meaningful predictive value. Taken together, these findings support the use of
deep convolutional encoder–decoder models as a promising foundation for global lightning
forecasting and for related applications in which lightning must be characterized, anticipated, or
screened in near real time.
