When Artificial Intelligence Agrees Too Easily: The Emerging Risk of Artificial Intelligence Sycophancy in Dentistry
Authors: Mohammed Turky, Paul MH Dummer
Published: 2026

To the editor

Artificial intelligence (AI) is becoming an integral part of dental practice, education, and research, reshaping how professionals approach diagnosis, treatment planning, and learning.1 Much of the discourse surrounding AI in dentistry has focused on essential issues like diagnostic accuracy, the risk of algorithmic bias, the explainability of AI-driven recommendations, and the need for proper regulation of these technologies.2, 3, 4, 5 While these concerns are critically important for the integration of AI in the dental field, an emerging challenge that has yet to receive ample attention is that of AI sycophancy.

AI sycophancy refers to a concerning tendency of AI systems to produce responses that align closely with the user's expectations, beliefs, or preferences, rather than objectively analysing the available evidence.6, 7, 8 This trait leads to a situation where a sycophantic AI appears to provide affirmations tailored to what users desire to hear, rather than offering insights grounded in factual assessments. The distinction between sycophancy and conventional algorithmic bias is significant. While bias typically arises due to limitations in the training data or flaws in the design of AI models, sycophancy emerges from the dynamics of interaction between users and AI systems. Often, these models are fine-tuned to provide agreeable and satisfying answers, which can skew their effectiveness.6,8

At a cursory glance, such behaviour may seem benign or even advantageous. After all, many clinicians prefer technology that is intuitive and cooperative. However, in healthcare – and particularly in the nuanced field of dentistry – an excessively agreeable AI can lead to unintended consequences. For instance, if a clinician suspects that a periapical radiolucency is healing after treatment, a sycophantic AI that consistently supports this belief without critically assessing other interpretations may create a false sense of confirmation. This can result in detrimental outcomes, as the clinician may fail to explore alternative conclusions that could be equally valid. Similarly, when deliberating treatment options, AI-generated recommendations that subtly favour the clinician's established preferences can entrench existing biases instead of prompting a reassessment of the situation.

The pivotal concern does not lie in the fear that AI will entirely replace human judgment; rather, it is the potential for AI to subtly influence that judgment over time. Human decision-making is often susceptible to cognitive biases like confirmation bias and anchoring, which can lead individuals to favour information that supports their pre-existing beliefs. Ideally, AI should act as a counterbalance to these biases by delivering objective analyses and offering alternative viewpoints for consideration. However, a sycophantic AI might exacerbate the problem by simply mirroring user predispositions, becoming akin to a digital "yes-man" that amplifies cognitive biases rather than mitigating them.

As generative AI systems are increasingly embedded into clinical decision support systems, treatment planning tools, educational frameworks, and professional consultations, the relevance of this issue may grow. Unlike traditional diagnostic software that relies on static decision trees, large language models are designed for interactive conversations, adapting to user prompts and preferences. While this flexibility can enhance their utility, it simultaneously heightens the risk of reinforcing user biases. In educational settings, for instance, students might incorrectly interpret an AI system's agreement as validation of their understanding, leading to potentially harmful misapprehensions. In clinical applications, practitioners may unwittingly develop an unwarranted level of confidence in AI recommendations that reflect their existing framing of a case rather than challenging it.

Recognising this issue is crucial not for discouraging the adoption of AI within dentistry but for understanding that the efficacy of AI assistance is contingent not only on its accuracy but also on its ability to maintain intellectual independence. Future evaluations of dental AI systems should include assessments of whether these tools can appropriately challenge users’ assumptions, communicate uncertainties, and uphold evidence-based positions even when faced with leading questions or preconceived notions.

As the dental profession continues to navigate the vast opportunities presented by AI, discussions must broaden to encompass considerations beyond mere performance metrics and technical capabilities. An AI system that too readily agrees with its users may pose risks as substantial as those posed by underperforming systems. Therefore, the future challenge lies in ensuring that AI functions not just as a mechanistic source of answers but also as a reliable partner in promoting critical thinking and enhancing decision-making processes in dental practice.
