Sequestration of methyl blue dye from aqueous solution onto raw and modified Banana (Musa paradisiaca) flower wastes
Authors: Oluwakemi O Onabanjo, Najeem Abiola Adesola Babarinde, Ali El-Rayyes, Amnah Mohammed Alsuhaibani, Moamen S Refat, Kholoud K Alzahrani, Edwin Andrew Ofudje
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSynthetic dyes like methylene blue (MB) persist in aquatic environment and threaten human and animal health. Agricultural by-products like banana flower (BF) wastes can be converted into low-cost adsorbent for sustainable dye uptake. This study reveals the conversion of discarded banana flower (Musa paradisiaca) waste into an efficient and sustainable biosorbent for MB elimination from aqueous media. Acid modification was utilized to enhance the adsorption performance of raw banana flower (RBF), producing modified banana flower (MBF) with enhanced surface functionality and adsorption affinity. Surface and structural validation using XRD, SEM, FT-IR, and BET analyses confirmed the predominantly amorphous mesoporous structure and the presence of oxygen-containing functional groups such as –OH, C = O, and C–O responsible for dye binding. BET study revealed a surface area of 106.3 m2/g with a mesoporous structure (average pore diameter ≈ 6.1 nm), favorable for dye uptake. Batch adsorption experiments showed that MB uptake was strongly influenced by temperature, pH, contact time, and adsorbent dosage, with optimum adsorption occurring under alkaline conditions. Langmuir isotherm analysis showed maximum adsorption capacities of 29.9 mg/g for RBF and 48.6 mg/g for MBF, confirming the significant enhancement achieved through acid modification. The Pseudo-first-order model best described MB uptake by RBF (R2 = 0.977), while pseudo-second-order model fits perfectly the kinetic data of MBF (R2 = 0.988), while thermodynamic parameters confirmed a spontaneous (ΔG: –3.23 to –39.72 kJ/mol) and endothermic process (ΔH: + 23.38 and + 97.88 kJ/mol). The findings highlight the potential of acid-modified banana flower waste as a low-cost, eco-friendly, and high-performance adsorbent for sustainable wastewater treatment applications.Keywords: Adsorption, Banana flower, Mechanism, Sequestration, Synthetic dyesSubject terms: Chemistry, Environmental sciences, Materials science

Introduction

The degradation of the environment has worsened greatly in recent times, largely due to the discharge of different industrial effluents. Untreated solid and liquid sewage are being recklessly discharged in large quantities into rivers and streams, especially those that flow through urban and industrial areas1,2. These contamination of water bodies with hazardous compounds, like synthetic dyes, poses a severe risk to the aquatic ecosystem, and has caused great danger to humans, aquatic organisms, and plants3.

Dyes are widely used to modify the characteristics and color of various substrates, like paper, leather, and fabric4–6. Methyl blue (MB) is one of the widely used dyes as a disinfectant and coloring agent in dyestuffs, pharmaceuticals, rubber, varnishes, and pesticides7,8. Like most synthetic dyes, the structural stability of MB made it resistant to biodegradation, thus causing severe damage to aquatic organisms9. The turbidity of water can be increased due to the presence of MB in water which lessens light penetration and disturbs photosynthesis in aquatic plants10. It is equally toxic to aquatic organisms and, via bioaccumulation in the food chain, MB poses severe dangers to human health, capable of causing impairment to the human respiratory liver and tract11,12. Given its adverse influences, there is a need to seek for sustainable and effective approaches for removing MB dye from wastewater before it is discharged into water bodies13.

The removal of dyes from wastewater requires a range of techniques like physical, chemical, and biological methods14,15. Among the effective methods that have been recognized are coagulation, photodegradation, chemical oxidation, membrane filtration, adsorption, and natural oxidation (10,16). Despite the effectiveness of these methods, they have substantial drawbacks because they often require large quantities of chemicals, complex process, lack of sustainability, and they often lead to the production of secondary waste17. These restrictions underscore the need for more environmentally friendly, and efficient alternatives. Adsorption has appeared to be gaining much interests due to its high elimination performance, easy and simple operation procedure, very cheap, and the widespread availability of various adsorbent biomass16,18. In spite of its advantages, a key limitation with conventional adsorption method is the high cost of many commercial adsorbents, like activated carbon in developing countries. This economic barricade has led to the need to seek for cost-effective carbon sources to address wastewater pollution treatment, which has arisen interest in the use of agricultural waste materials19. The utilization of agricultural waste as adsorbents for dye sorption has gained immense popularity due to their efficiency, low cost, availability, and environmental non-toxicity20. Some studies have used different agricultural wastes for removing dyes from aqueous media. For instance, corn husk21, coffee waste22, rice husk23, coconut husk24, oil palm trunk25, walnut shell26 and many more have been used for this purpose.

Bananas are widespread crops, with over 25 billion banana or plantain trees cultivated worldwide27. The banana flower (BF) is a male bud, which is a leafy, maroon-colored cone at the end of the banana bunch with cream-colored florets layered inside28,29. While this blossom is an edible flower rich antioxidants and in numerous supplements providing some health benefits, it is typically disposed as waste during banana cultivation thus, creating nuisance in the environment27,30.

Despite the fact that different agricultural wastes have been studied as low-cost adsorbents for dye removal, many reported materials still show important limitations, such as inadequate surface functionality, low adsorption capacities, limited structural stability, poor pore accessibility, and insufficient mechanistic understanding of adsorption behavior. Also, most previous findings have primarily focused on conventional biomasses including coconut husk, rice husk, and coffee waste with only little attention given to banana flower waste, particularly for methylene blue removal from aqueous solutions.

Banana flower waste possesses various intrinsic characteristics favorable for adsorption applications, such as porous lignocellulosic framework enriched with hydroxyl, phenolic, and carboxyl functional groups capable of interacting with cationic dye molecules. These offer multiple active sites for ion hydrogen bonding, chelation, exchange, and electrostatic interactions9,31. Its abundance, biodegradability, low cost, and susceptibility to chemical modification further enhance its potential as a sustainable biosorbent. However, the adsorption behavior of banana flower waste remains underexplored, particularly regarding the influence of acid modification on its adsorption efficiency, surface properties, and adsorption mechanism.

The activation of banana flower via acid treatment is expected to improve the physicochemical properties of banana flower waste by increasing surface area, enhancing pore development, exposing additional active binding sites, and strengthening electrostatic interactions with methylene blue molecules31. These modifications may most likely enhance adsorption performance relative to the raw biomass and several previously reported agricultural adsorbents. Disdain these benefits, limited studies have systematically examined the relationship between acid-induced surface modification and adsorption behavior of banana flower-derived adsorbents.

Thus, this study examines the adsorption performance of raw and acid-modified banana flower waste for methylene blue uptake from aqueous solution. The study systematically examines the adsorption equilibrium, the effects of acid treatment on surface characteristics and adsorption behavior through physicochemical characterization, kinetics, thermodynamics, and mechanistic analyses. It is hypothesized that acid modification improves adsorption efficiency by increasing pore accessibility, surface area, and the availability of oxygen-containing functional groups responsible for dye binding.

Unlike many preceding works that focused mainly on adsorption efficiency alone, this study establishes a structure–property relationship between acid-induced surface modification and adsorption performance via comparative evaluation of raw and modified adsorbents. By converting a discarded material into a sustainable and an effective adsorbent, this study offers an economically and environmentally friendly approach for wastewater treatment. The influence of experimental parameters like temperature, MB concentration, contact time, adsorbent dose, and pH were examined to determine the optimal conditions. Different isotherms and kinetic models were used to model the adsorption data to gain insight into the adsorption mechanism.

Materials and methods

Materials

The banana flowers (Musa paradisiaca) wastes used as adsorbent in this study were collected from a farm at Ogboogbo, Ijebu-Ode, Ogun State, Nigeria and is shown in Fig. 1a. Though, the banana flowers used in this study are wastes that fell off the buds, permission was taken from the authority of the farm owner prior to the collection. Methyl blue dye (Fig. 1b) with molecular formula C37H27N3Na2O9S3 and molecular weight 799.814 gmol-1 which is of analytical grade was obtained from Merck, India.

(a) Raw banana flower (RBF) and (b) r Methylene blue dye.

Methods

Adsorbent preparation

The collected banana flower wastes cut into smaller pieces and thoroughly washed with distilled water in order to remove impurities. They were sun dried for two weeks and later dried in an oven at 80 °C for 3 h and thereafter crushed into powder form and referred to as raw banana flower (RBF). The concentration of H₂SO₄ (0.1 M) used for acid modification was carefully selected based on preliminary experimental trials and previous studies on lignocellulosic biomass modification, where mild acid treatment was adjudged effective in enhancing surface porosity and exposing oxygen-containing functional groups without causing severe degradation of the biomass structure. Thus, the acid modification of the adsorbent was carried out using H2SO4 by mixing 10 g the powder banana flower sample with concentrated H2SO4 (0.1 M) in specimen bottles (250 ml). The reaction mixture was kept in the reagent bottle for 12 h to achieve good penetration. Thereafter, the mixture was separated, washed with distilled water to neutral pH and oven dried for 2 h at 80 °C. The product formed were labeled as modified banana flower (MBF) and stored in an airtight sample bottle.

Biosorbent characterization

Shimadzu Fourier transform Infrared Spectrophotometer (FT-IR, 8400S, Japan) was used for the functional groups determination. Samples were homogenized with KBr anhydrous by mortar agate, compressed to form pellets and scanning was done in the in the range of 400 to 4000 cm-1. Scanning electron microscope Hitachi SU3500 (Japan) was utilized to determine the morphology of the adsorbent. The crystallinity property of the biomass was investigated with a PANalytical X’Pert PRO diffractometer, (Netherlands). The surface area, pore size and pore volume of the adsorbent were measured using Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET, Quantachrome NOVA 2200C, USA).

Point of zero charge (pHPZC) determination

The point of zero charge (pHPZC) of the material was performed using the pH-drift method. Briefly, 0.01 M NaCl solution was made and pour in different test-tubes. Thereafter, the initial pH (pH₀) was adjusted in the range of 2.0 to 10.0 using 0.1 M HCl or 0.1 M NaOH. 0.01 g of the adsorbent was introduced into each solution, and the mixtures were agitated at 150 rpm at 25 °C for 12 h. The final pH (pHf) was measured, and the change in pH (ΔpH = pHf − pH₀) was evaluated.

Preparation of adsorbate solution

Stock solution (1000 mg/L) of methyl blue was prepared by dissolving accurately weighed 0.3 g of the dye in 250 ml of distilled water. Working standards of MB dye (10 to100 mg/L) used for the adsorption study were prepared by diluting appropriate volume of the stock solution with distilled water. The pH of the dye solutions was adjusted using EcoSense pH100A, (China). By applying UV–Visible spectrophotometer, the dye solution absorbance was quantified, and a standard calibration plot was drawn for methyl blue dye at λmax 569 nm.

Batch adsorption study

The batch adsorption was done by varying different adsorption parameters like biosorbent dose, adsorbate pH, contact time, initial concentration and temperature. The analysis was done by measuring 50 mL of the MB solution of different concentrations (10–100 mg/L) into a series of 250 mL Erlenmeyer flasks. The pH of the MB was varied from (1–12) with 0.1 M NaOH/HCl, while the temperature ranged from 25 to 55 °C. Various doses of banana flower (0.1 g to 1.0 g) were weighed into each flask containing the MB solution. The mixture was placed on an orbital shaker and agitated at 200 rpm. At predetermined time, the residual concentration of MB in the supernatant was measured on a JENWAY 6305 UV–Visible spectrophotometer (UK) at wavelength of 569 nm. Results and discussion

Characterization of Banana flower

XRD patterns

The XRD spectra of modified banana flower biomass before and after adsorption are presented in Fig. 2. Both spectra depict a broad diffraction peak centered around 2θ = 20°–25°, which is common characteristics of amorphous cellulose and lignocellulosic materials40. This broad hump suggests a semi-crystalline to amorphous nature of the biomass. A notable reduction in peak intensity is observed in the XRD pattern after methylene blue sorption, compared to the spectrum before MB adsorption. This reflects that the sorption process alters the internal structure of the biomass, which may be due to the interactions between the dye molecules and functional groups present in the biomass matrix (hydroxyl, carboxyl, and methoxy groups)40. The reduced intensity and slight shift in the diffraction peak position after adsorption indicate a decrease in the crystallinity or structural ordering of the biomass. Noticeably, no new peaks are seen after adsorption, indicating that the methylene blue does not crystallize within the biomass or form any new crystalline phases. This reflects a mechanism characterized by physisorption or weak chemical interactions, rather than intercalation or chemical bonding that would result in new crystalline domains.

XRD patterns of MBF before and after the uptake of MB showing the predominantly amorphous structure of the lignocellulosic adsorbents and structural modifications induced by acid treatment.

FT-IR analysis of banana flower biomass

Figure 3 shows the FT-IR spectra of the banana flower before and after the adsorption of methyl blue. Before the sorption of MB, the FT-IR spectrum shows a broad and intense absorption band around 3368–3459 cm⁻1, which is attributed to O–H stretching vibrations of hydroxyl groups, often found in cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin. After adsorption, this peak becomes less intense with slightly shifts, suggesting that the hydroxyl groups may have interacted with the methylene blue molecules, likely through hydrogen bonding or electrostatic interactions. Another vital feature is the band observed at 2922 cm⁻1, corresponding to C–H stretching vibrations of –C-H groups. Though, this peak appears in both spectra but no shift in peak intensity or position was seen after adsorption, indicating the non-involvement in the sorption process. A strong peak seen at 1686 cm⁻1 in the adsorbent before adsorption is attributed to C = O stretching vibrations of carboxyl or conjugated carbonyl groups and aromatic C = C stretching in lignin. After the uptake process, the peak’s intensity drastically decreases and the peak’s position shifted to 1669 cm⁻1 indicating its interaction with the dye molecules. Additionally, the presence of peaks observed 1205 and 1034 cm⁻1 are related to C–O stretching vibrations of alcohols, esters or ethers. The intensities of these peaks were also noticed to have decrease, while shift in peak positions to 1186 and 1026 cm⁻1 after MB adsorption was observed, which further corroborate the involvement of polar groups in the adsorption process.

FT-IR spectra of MBF before and after the uptake of MB, illustrating the presence of various oxygen-containing functional groups responsible for methylene blue binding.

Scanning electron microscope (SEM) analysis

The surface morphology of the banana flower adsorbent before and after MB adsorption was examined using scanning electron microscopy (SEM), as displayed in Fig. 4. The raw banana flower before the uptake (Fig. 4a) displays a rough, irregular, and highly porous surface structure with visible cavities and channels. These natural pores are features of lignocellulosic biomass and provide potential active sites for sorption of the contaminant. The presence of such uneven and heterogeneous surface enhances the accessibility of adsorbate molecules. After the uptake of MB (Fig. 4b), the surface of the raw banana flower becomes relatively smoother and more compact, with observed reduction in pore visibility. The partial blockage of pores indicates successful adsorption of MB onto the adsorbent surface.

SEM images of raw banana flower before adsorption (a), raw banana flower after MB adsorption (b), acid modified banana flower before adsorption (c) and acid modified banana flower after MB adsorption (d).

The morphology of the acid-modified banana flower prior to adsorption (Fig. 4c) displays a more developed and cleaner porous structure relative to the raw material. The surface appears more exfoliated and fragmented, signifying that acid treatment has resulted in the removal of impurities and opened up additional pores which enhances the surface area and exposes more active binding sites, and may be attributed to the improved adsorption performance observed in chemically treated biomass materials.

Upon MB adsorption, the acid-modified sample (Fig. 4d) shows a significantly smoother and more uniform surface, indicating a masked surface likely by adsorbed dye molecules. The decline in surface roughness coupled with the disappearance of porous further confirm effective adsorption. It was further observed that the extent of surface coverage in the acid-treated adsorbent after adsorption is more pronounced when compare with the raw sample, signifying that acid modification improves adsorption capacity.

BET surface area and porosity analysis

The obtained pore size distribution using the BJH method (Fig. 5a), revealed a pore diameter of 4.2 nm, confirming the mesoporous nature (2–50 nm) of the material, which is ideal for adsorption of larger molecules such as dyes and organic pollutants41,42. Also, the calculated average pore diameter of 6.1 nm supports the mesoporous classification. Mesoporosity of adsorbent material plays a vital role in facilitating diffusion and improving adsorption kinetics, as it reduces mass transfer resistance within the adsorbent structure and this is particularly advantageous for wastewater treatment applications involving bulky contaminants. The nitrogen adsorption–desorption analysis was used to investigate the textural properties of the banana flower–derived adsorbent. The obtained isotherm (Fig. 5b) exhibited a Type IV isotherm with a hysteresis loop, which is characteristic of mesoporous materials. A recent study noted that such isotherm behavior confirms the presence of capillary condensation within mesopores and suggests a well-developed porous structure43. The BET surface area was computed from the linear region of the BET plot (Fig. 5c), yielding a value of 106.3 m2/g, and suggests that the banana flower adsorbent possesses sufficient active sites for adsorption while maintaining structural stability. The total pore volume was estimated to be 0.184 cm3/g, signifying a well-developed pore network. The observed relatively high BET surface area and mesoporous structure of the modified banana flower may have significantly contributed to its enhanced adsorption performance by increasing the accessibility of adsorption sites and facilitating intraparticle diffusion of methylene blue molecules as evidence from the larger adsorption capacity observed for MBF compared with. This demonstrates a clear structure–property relationship between textural characteristics and adsorption behavior.

Impact of pH

The of pH solution has a considerable impact on the removal efficiency of MB, as it governs the surface charge of the adsorbents and the ionization behavior of the dye molecules44. This relationship is strongly influence by the point of zero charge (pHpzc) of the adsorbent, which defines the pH at which the surface carries no net charge. The obtained values of pHpzc for the RBF and MBF are 5.6 and 4.2, respectively. Notably, the lower pHpzc value of MBF (4.2) relative to RBF (5.6) suggests that MBF attains a negatively charged surface at a lower pH, which explains its superior adsorption performance across the studied pH range.

At pH values lower than the pHpzc, the surface of the adsorbent is expected to be positively charged, while at pH values greater than the pHpzc, the surface becomes negatively charged44,45. With methylene blue being a cationic dye, its adsorption is highly favored under conditions where the adsorbent surface is negatively charged because of electrostatic attraction. The impact of pH on the removal of MB using RBF and MBF was investigated across a pH range of 2—10, while other parameters were kept constant. As depicted in Fig. 6, the maximum removal efficiency for MB occurred at pH 8.0, achieving 67.3% percentage removal for RBF and pH 10 with 80.4% percentage removal for MBF.

Effect of solution pH on methylene blue adsorption by RBF and MBF, showing enhanced adsorption under alkaline conditions due to favorable surface charge interactions with the cationic dye molecules.

At lower pH values (pH < pHpzc), both RBF and MBF demonstrated low sorption capacity which can be attributed to the electrostatic repulsion between the cationic MB molecules and positively charged adsorbent surface, which limits adsorption46. However, with the solution pH rising above the pHpzc, the surface of both adsorbents becomes increasingly negatively charged, thus enhancing the electrostatic attraction between the MB molecules, and the adsorbent which results in a considerable increase in adsorption capacity with increasing pH47.

Likewise, possible π–π interactions between the aromatic rings of methylene blue and lignin-derived aromatic domains in the biomass structure may also contribute to adsorption42. These interactions occur at high pH when the delocalized π electrons in the aromatic rings of the MB molecules interact with the π electrons in the aromatic rings of the adsorbents, causing a stronger affinity between them42. This rise in affinity contributes to the improved sorption of MB molecules from the solution. These reports align with the results from Muskan et al.48, where modified walnut shells were used as adsorbents for the uptake of MB dye. Lower uptake was observed at a low pH, while higher sorption efficiency took place at higher pH which may be attributed to the π-π interactions between the adsorbate molecules and the adsorbent surface.

Effect of adsorbent dosage

Adsorbent dosage is a crucial parameter affecting the sorption process. Figure 7 reflects the removal of MB dye when the amount of RBF and MBF was varied from 0.1 g to 1.0 g, while other parameters were kept constant. The maximum MG removal was observed at 0.7 g, with 73.3% removal for RBF, and at 0.6 g, with 79.2% removal for MBF. An increase in removal efficiency is due to the availability of more active sites for adsorption, enabling more effective MB removal. However, increasing the amount of both adsorbents beyond these values caused a slight decrease in removal efficiency. This decline at higher dosages is probably due to the agglomeration of adsorbent particles49. When more adsorbent is added, particles tend to clump together, decreasing the total surface area and the available accessible active sites50. At this point, the system reaches saturation, and dye molecules compete for inadequate active sites. In spite the increased number of particles, the effective surface area does not expand, resulting in reduced removal efficiency51. El-Rayyes et al.16 noted a similar trend where the rise in the adsorbent dosage improved Cresol red removal efficiency from 54.9 to 85.6% while using hot water-treated cow waste as adsorbent.

Effect of adsorbent dosage on methylene blue removal efficiency by RBF and MBF.

Effect of contact time

The efficiency of the adsorbents was examined as a function of the contact time between 5 to 240 min for both RBF and MBF. This was performed under constant conditions regarding other parameters as illustrated in Fig. 8. The removal efficiency demonstrated a steady rise until it plateaued at an equilibrium contact time of 120 min for both adsorbents. This plateau suggests that the sorption sites on the surfaces of the adsorbents were nearly saturated with MB molecules, causing the rate of adsorption to decrease49,52. The optimum adsorption capacities were recorded as 31.3 mg/g for RBF and 40.5 mg/g for MBF at 120 min. Beyond this period, no further increase in sorption was observed. The initial high rate of sorption can be because of the abundance of available unoccupied active sites on the surfaces of the biomass. But as these sites become saturated with molecules of MB, the rate of adsorption declines53. Alike results were documented by Parlayici and Aras54 with beads made from biopolymers as adsorbent for the uptake of MB from aquatic environments. Ofudje et al.71 also reported that the maximum percentage removal of MB while using sugarcane peel ash took place at 60 min for unmodified banana peel ash and at 80 min for its modified version, which again highlights the importance of active sites on the adsorbent surface.

Effect of contact time on methylene blue adsorption onto RBF and MBF, illustrating rapid initial uptake followed by gradual attainment of equilibrium due to progressive occupation of active adsorption sites.

Impact of concentration

The influence of MB dye initial concentration on the sorption process was examined, and the result was given in Fig. 9. The results revealed that the amount of MB sorbed at equilibrium rose with dye concentrations. The maximum removal efficiencies were obtained to be 74.2% for RBF and 81.4% for MBF at an initial concentration of 80 mg/L. This could be due to the stronger driving force that higher concentration provides, which enhances the rate of the uptake (Ofudje et al. 2024). But, as the concentration rose above this point, the sorption efficiency declined which is likely due to the rapid saturation of the available sorption sites on the adsorbent surface55. These reports were supported by Cheruiyot et al.55 with crystal violet dye removal by coffee husks. A comparable observation was made by Ogundiran et al.18 in their study on the adsorption of cationic dye by eggshell waste, where they stated that the sorption capacity rose as the concentration increases, which was equally attributed to the enhancement of the driving force available in the solution, which enables the molecules to diffuse faster.

Effect of initial methylene blue concentration on adsorption capacity of RBF and MBF, indicating enhanced driving force for mass transfer at higher dye concentrations.

Effect of temperature

Temperature is considered an important factor and can be attributed to the efficiency of MB adsorption. The effect of temperature on MB removal was evaluated by contacting a mixture of MB with RBF and MBF at different temperatures ranging from 25 to 55 °C while keeping other parameters constant. As shown in Fig. 10, the rate of adsorption of MB onto RBF increased with the temperature. At 25 °C, the percentage removal of MB was 37.4% and maximum adsorption capacity was observed at 45 °C with 67.7% removal efficiency. MBF also follows the same trend. The percentage removal increased from 48.4 at 25 °C to 78.4 at 50 °C, and beyond this optimum temperature, the adsorption capacity of the adsorbents decreased. As the removal efficiency increased with temperature, it is evident that the adsorption process was endothermic. A comparable trend was reported by Ofudje et al. (2024) for methylene blue removal using sugarcane peel ash, where the percentage removal increased to 67.4% with unmodified sugarcane peel ash and 81.2% with modified sugarcane peel ash when the temperature was raised from 45 to 55 °C, respectively.

Effect of temperature on methylene blue adsorption by RBF and MBF, demonstrating enhanced adsorption capacity at elevated temperatures consistent with the endothermic nature of the adsorption process.

Certainly, the surge in percentage removal observed is due to improved kinetic energy of the molecules as the temperature increases, and the quicker molecules disperse faster on the surface of the adsorbent45. In addition, rise in temperature will cause the internal structure of the adsorbent to swell, permitting large dyes to pierce the adsorbent56. At higher temperatures, however, desorption of MG molecules from the surface of the adsorbent takes place due to potential bond breakage, which eventually reduce the adsorption efficiency57.

Kinetic modeling of MB adsorption onto RBF and MBF

The PFO model gave good correlation for RBF, with the calculated adsorption capacity (29.01 mg/g) closely matched the experimental value (31.30 mg/g) with minimal error (SSE = 0.006) and a high coefficient of determination (R2 = 0.977). However, for MBF, the PFO model gave a lower R2 value of 0.849 with a significant deviation between experimental and calculated qe values, suggesting poor suitability.

On the other hand, the PSO model provides best fit for MBF adsorption, where the calculated qe (43.01 mg/g) was consistent with the experimental qe (40.50 mg/g), with lower error (SSE = 0.003) and followed by higher R2 (0.988). In the case of IPD model, R2 values are high for both RBF (0.981) and MBF (0.986), while Kid values of 27.92 and 31.09, respectively, inferred that intraparticle diffusion contributes significantly to the adsorption process. However, the non-zero intercepts (C = 1.595 for RBF and 3.646 for MBF) indicates that intraparticle diffusion is not the sole rate-controlling step; but with boundary layer resistance and film diffusion also play a role58. Thus, the uptake process onto RBF and MBF cannot be explained by a single model, but rather by a combination of surface adsorption and intraparticle diffusion, with the dominant mechanism being system-dependent.

The results indicate that the Langmuir model provided a good fit for RBF adsorbent when compared with the MBF, with high correlation coefficients (R2 = 0.997 for RBF and 0.956 for MBF) and low error values (%SSE = 0.013 and 0.061). The maximum adsorption capacities (Qmax) obtained are 29.9 mg·g⁻1 and 48.61 mg·g⁻1 for RBF and MBF respectively, confirming that modification enhanced the monolayer adsorption capacity. The Langmuir constant (KL) was higher for MBF (1.75 L·mg⁻1) compared to RBF (0.38 L·mg⁻1), suggesting stronger adsorptive affinity towards MB by MBF surface sites. The separation factor (RL) values for both adsorbents (0.06 for MBF and RBF for MBF) lie between 0 and 1, indicating favorable adsorption processes particularly towards MBF59. The Freundlich model gave lower R2 values (0.844 for RBF and 0.989 for MBF), suggesting that multilayer adsorption on heterogeneous surfaces controlled the uptake of MB by MBF. The Freundlich constants (KF) were 12.81 mg·g⁻1·(mg/L)⁻1/2 for RBF and 26.58 for MBF, consistent with greater adsorption intensity for MBF. The Freundlich intensity parameter (n) was greater than 1 in both cases (2.98 for RBF and 3.63 for MBF), inferring favorable adsorption60.

Thermodynamic studies

Thermodynamic analysis (Table 4 and Fig. 13) reveals that MB adsorption onto both raw banana flower and modified banana flower is endothermic and spontaneous16. Gibbs free energy changes values are negative over the temperature range studied and become more negative as temperature increases (RBF: − 3.23 to − 9.34 kJ·mol⁻1 from 298 to 328 K; MBF: − 10.03 to − 39.72 kJ·mol⁻1 from 298 to 328 K), indicating that moderately elevated temperatures favour adsorption18. The positive enthalpy (ΔH: + 23.38 kJ·mol⁻1 for RBF; + 97.88 kJ·mol⁻1 for MBF) and positive entropy (ΔS: 9.2 × 10–2 and + 3.99 × 10-1 J·mol⁻1·K⁻1, respectively) reveals that the process is endothermic and accompanied by increased disorder at the solid–liquid interface, which could be due to the release of water/counter-ions or desolvation of dye molecules67. The positive enthalpy values further suggest that adsorption involved progressively stronger adsorbate–adsorbent interactions after modification. The lower ΔH value for RBF is characteristic of predominantly physisorption processes, whereas the substantially higher ΔH observed for MBF indicates the contribution of stronger surface interactions (chemisorption) arising from acid-induced functionalization and improved active site accessibility10. This improved interaction strength (based on higher ΔH value for MBF) may arise from acid-induced surface functionalization, increased exposure of oxygen-containing groups, and improved accessibility of active adsorption sites.

Adsorption mechanism

The uptake of methylene blue by RBF and MBF is a multifaceted process governed by a combination of physical and chemical interactions, and a schematic illustration is shown in Fig. 14. The initial adsorption stage is facilitated by the physical properties of the adsorbents as the mesoporous structure offers a large surface area and abundant pathways for MB molecules to effortlessly access and be drawn to the surface41. This early uptake is mainly driven by physical forces, like van der Waals forces, and weak π − π interactions between the aromatic structures on the adsorbents and the aromatic rings of the MB molecules. The presence of functional groups such as hydroxyl, and carboxyl, as confirmed by FT-IR analysis, further supports these connections by providing sites for hydrogen bonding and other physical attractions. The aromatic structure of MB also contributes to the sorption process by interacting with the non-polar regions of the adsorbents68.

Adsorption mechanism of methylene blue onto banana flower.

The pH of the solution is a vital parameter that meaningfully influences the sorption efficiency. At low pH, the surface of the adsorbents becomes positively charged and this positive charge produces a strong repulsion force with the positively charged MB cationic dye, causing reduction in removal efficiency. On the other hand, as the pH rises and the adsorbents’ surface charge becomes more negative, and the electrostatic attraction between the MB molecules and the surface is greatly enhanced. This, along with continued π − π interactions, vividly improves the removal efficiency at higher pH values between 8 and 10. Thus, the successful uptake of MB is due to the synergistic effect where the functional groups, adsorbents’ surface area, the pH-dependent surface charge, and pore structure, all work together. From thermodynamic assessment, the substantially higher ΔH value noticed for MBF (97.88 kJ/mol) suggests the participation of stronger adsorbate–adsorbent interactions (chemisorption) after acid modification. Whereas adsorption on RBF appears to be dominated primarily by physisorption mechanisms including electrostatic attraction and pore diffusion which contribute significantly to dye uptake. Furthermore, the coexistence of multiple adsorption mechanisms is consistent with the heterogeneous nature of lignocellulosic biomass adsorbents, where electrostatic interactions, hydrogen bonding, π–π interactions, and possible surface complexation may occur simultaneously. It’s important to note that the contribution of electrostatic attraction was inferred from the pHpzc results and the strong dependence of adsorption efficiency on solution pH. The enhanced adsorption observed above the pHpzc values supports the development of negatively charged surface sites favorable for interaction with cationic methylene blue molecules.

Regeneration study

The regeneration study (Fig. 15) revealed that both adsorbents retained appreciable adsorption performance after repeated adsorption–desorption for five cycles, signifying acceptable structural stability and reusability. But, gradual decrease in adsorption capacity was experienced with increasing cycle number. The reduction in adsorption performance may be because of partial blockage of active adsorption sites, incomplete desorption of methylene blue molecules, and possible deterioration of surface functional groups during repeated acid regeneration69. It was observed that MBF adsorbent displayed superior regeneration stability compared with RBF, retaining approximately 82.7% of its initial adsorption efficiency after five cycles. This enhanced stability may be as a result of improved surface functionality and structural robustness introduced through acid modification70. The findings suggest that acid-modified banana flower possesses promising potential as a reusable and sustainable adsorbent for wastewater treatment applications which further supports the practical applicability and economic feasibility of the modified adsorbent for large-scale wastewater remediation.

Limitation/Future perspective

Although the proposed adsorption mechanisms were supported by FT-IR shifts, pHpzc analysis, and thermodynamic behavior, advanced techniques such as zeta potential measurements, XPS analysis, and computational interaction-energy studies would provide deeper insight into the nature and strength of adsorbate–adsorbent interactions. Future studies should therefore focus on these approaches to further elucidate the adsorption mechanism at the molecular level.

Also in this study, the adsorption experiments were performed under controlled laboratory conditions using synthetic dye solutions; thus, future studies should evaluate the adsorbent performance in real wastewater systems containing competing ions and complex contaminant matrices. Moreover, large-scale applicability should be investigated through fixed-bed column adsorption and continuous-flow systems to better simulate industrial wastewater treatment conditions. Despite these limitations, the present findings establish acid-modified banana flower waste as a promising sustainable biosorbent and provide a valuable foundation for future advanced adsorption studies.
