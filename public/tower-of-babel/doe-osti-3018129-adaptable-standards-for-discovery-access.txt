 1. OVERVIEW

Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) is leveraging its established capabilities and subject matter
expertise in data curation, governance, management, national security, and risk assessment and mitigation
to support the US Department of Energy (DOE) Grid Modernization Initiative. Using standards modeled
by the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST), the Data Curation Network (DCN), the
Oak Ridge Leadership Computing Facility (OLCF), and other leading organizations in the fields of
energy research, high-performance computing, and national and homeland security, ORNL seeks to
provide a federated approach to research data discovery, use, and interoperability.

1.1 ADDRESSING THE PROBLEM SET: HOW TREATMENT OF DATA GUIDES USE FOR
 ANALYSIS AND DECISION-MAKING

Before standards, rules, and amendments set by the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission’s (FERC),1
the Department of Homeland Security’s Protected Critical Infrastructure Information Program,2 DOE’s
Office of Cybersecurity, Energy Security, and Emergency Response (CESER),3 there was minimal
guidance for data security, export control, and distribution. Today, there are persistent challenges related

to the management, control, and guidance for energy infrastructure and grid data—where governance
policies have varied across data types, organizations, states, and sectors.

Researchers continue to contend with an ad hoc approach to these energy data governance and
management backlogs, with simultaneous and compounding challenges related to minimal guidance for
the security, export control, classification, and distribution of other types of energy and grid data. Coupled
with the increasing volume of data of varied quality, validity, and reliability, and where risk assessment
and mitigation to US infrastructure is based on data type rather than data visibility and interconnectivity,
inherent risks of linked datasets are often misidentified, unclearly defined, or overlooked.

Some consequences to this haphazard structuring of critical infrastructure and energy data may lead to
over- or under-protection, data misidentification or classification, and inconsistencies in collection,
storage, and use standards that may ultimately expose US grid infrastructure and associated DOE assets to
potential threats and vulnerabilities.

Therefore, it is critical to create an anticipatory, overarching, and integrated data governance and
management scheme upon project initiation and kick-off in alignment with federal regulations and known
standards, setting the stage for improved discovery, accessibility, long-term usage, and possible
redefinitions and repositioning of the data.

1.2 SOLUTIONS-ORIENTED INFRASTRUCTURE FOR OPTIMAL DATA DISCOVERY,
 USE, AND PROTECTION

ORNL is establishing a standard for promoting open data as a federated resource, posing limited risk to
US grid infrastructure. By developing dynamic and searchable linked dataset databases, including
comprehensive open data sharing taxonomies and naming conventions, we aim to enhance data
discoverability and usability. For all projects, we will

   Assess the use of synthetic datasets and explore the consolidation of existing catalogs and data
 information centers, potentially using a data mesh solution.

   Employ NIST Research Data Framework conventions, enforcing DOE policies, other federal
 regulations, and nested standards and policies for controlled unclassified information and critical
 energy infrastructure information.

   Follow DCN structured steps—check, understand, request, augment, transform, evaluate, and
 document (CURATE(d))—and ensure appropriate storage and backups will be made via a best-in-
 class research data management platform for all metadata.

   Employ need-based role-based access controls (RBACs) to ensure mitigation and abatement of
 potential threats to data assets, personnel security, and vulnerabilities to national security.

 2. FEDERATED DATA PORTAL REQUIREMENTS

A federated data discovery layer, or data portal, primarily operates as a data catalog, with professional
data stewards harvesting and augmenting metadata from a variety of sources, while providing attribution
and resolvable links back to the original datasets, creators, and organizations.4 Therefore, the platform’s

focus is on increasing discoverability. Records are checked and updated on a regular, scheduled basis for
continuity and accuracy, and steps are taken to ensure that the enriched metadata is understandable to
human end users, computation engines, and machines.

Notably, data catalogs and portals, unlike data repositories, do not archive datasets themselves. Instead,
they provide a means of access to them, describing high-value datasets that may have strict access
conditions or whose creators are reluctant to store the data in a repository but may provide access upon
request.5 Additionally, a data catalog can collocate multiple datasets of interest spread across the entire
DOE complex, providing links to the raw data, the published data, and any code necessary to reproduce
the results. In this sense, a data catalog is complementary to existing infrastructure, providing an
additional index point while maintaining the important attribution of the original host institution.6

2.1 OPEN DATA POLICIES AND SENSITIVE INFORMATION

While many datasets used across the DOE complex are categorized as “open data” and intended for
general users, applying best practices for sharing, a smaller set require granular RBACs. Following
curation, datasets undergo risk assessment and evaluation,7 adding additional layers of security to ensure
usage is aligned with the intended purpose of the infrastructure’s development.

Energy grid data is presumably sensitive information, particularly when collated into like-datasets. These
collections increase risk to national security, with elevated exposure to potential vulnerabilities to US
infrastructure, simply by the nature of their connectivity.

Therefore, data catalogs play an important role in maintaining energy and national security because they
can be designed to provide structure around specific user communities, enabling access to datasets based
on data governance policies. Consequently, metadata must be created around established policies,
purposes, and readable documentation.8 Nevertheless, even for critical infrastructure datasets that are
open, the impact of their inclusion must be considered within the context of the larger portal’s aggregated
use, purpose, and analysis. Should artificial intelligence be applied, this need becomes particularly acute.

At every stage of the data lifecycle, we will consider the cascading impacts of a dataset’s inclusion within
 the overall context of the portal9.

2.2 EMBRACING EXISTING DATA SOLUTIONS

Through standardized metadata for both energy-specific and publicly available data harvested from
existing open portals, we encourage a federated approach and advance the concept of “Connect vs.
Collect.” Incorporating data strategy methodologies from University of California (UC), Berkeley,
modules, we promote data democratization through openness, with appropriate safeguards in place to
promote national security, protecting the United States, its assets, and its citizens.

 3. HIGH-LEVEL FRAMEWORK FOR DATA CATALOG SOLUTIONS

Modeled after existing data catalog solutions such as Data.gov,10 OpenEI,11 and the Duke Energy
Initiative, ORNL’s approach encourages the creation of functioning data ecosystems. For energy grid and
DOE-complex data, we operationalize a high-level framework that collects and combines datasets from
institutional or organizational providers, adding data access controls via holistic governance models, and
encouraging interoperability and standardization. These collated datasets then undergo risk assessment
and evaluation12 to understand needed implementation of privacy-preserving techniques and safeguards
ahead of “data consumer” access and utilization.13

3.1 MOTIVATIONS FOR DATA GOVERNANCE AND MANAGEMENT

Within this framework are embedded conventions and standards that enable both current and long-term
use, providing standard operating procedures for personnel and decision-making processes on mission,
purpose, use cases, and maintenance across the data life cycle. These established procedures are
implemented before the development of use cases and data collection to enable pivots, expansion, and
sunsetting of elements or entire catalogs, regardless of time or life cycle stage, personnel changes, or
shifting administrative and mission priorities.

Like physical buildings and laboratory equipment, research data and its associated metadata are DOE
assets. Consequently, research data should be documented for maximum reuse, promoting open
collaborations that enhance positive impacts on the US energy sector and society. As such, research data
should be appropriately managed (i.e., collected, stored, protected, and used) throughout its life cycle.
This requires that research data should include named personnel roles and specific responsibilities.
Therefore, a fundamental tenet of the project is that research data should be as accurate and complete as
possible within the constraints of the project, requiring commitment to the following core principles:

  Curation and monitoring are implemented to ensure trust, accessibility, and reusability among
 parties.

  Standards (e.g., metadata standards) are consistently applied to promote a common understanding,
 purpose, and use.

  National security and compliance with nested regulations and standards ensure that data sharing and
 anonymization policies, including those for standalone and aggregated data, are enforced to protect
 against unwanted or unauthorized access and use.

  Data is acquired, used, stored, and disposed of in compliance with the law and applicable standards,
 regulations, and contractual obligations.

3.2 DISTINCTIONS BETWEEN DATA GOVERNANCE AND DATA MANAGEMENT

 Figure 3. Distinctions between data governance and data management.

The data governance structure is inherent to its management because it can be implemented at the launch
of any collection or integration project and is used to define workflows and streamline processes by which
standards, policies, and operations, among others, are implemented, evaluated, and operationalized for
intended use cases and user communities. Data governance seeks to clarify roles, responsibilities, and
accountabilities for the development of data portals/catalogs and to provide a decision tree upon which
management decisions, including pivots in use cases or RBACs (for example) can be made.

This governance structure has procedural benefits and enables trust and transparency in big data efforts,
like Robert’s Rules of Order for standard parliamentary procedures. Data governors are tasked with
upholding standards and controls that are inherent to the data portal/catalog’s context and purpose and
with determining risk and mitigation strategies. For DOE research data assets, these personnel are critical
to assuring robust practices, federal regulations, and lab complex guidelines for the maintenance of
national and homeland security.

3.3 ROLES AND RESPONSIBILITIES FOR DATA GOVERNANCE

Data governance personnel are assigned roles and responsibilities under three distinct but interconnected
categories. These categories define the hierarchy of decision-making related to the portal and provide
checks and balances for other personnel categories. Documentation of challenges, issues, and decision
pathways are shared among all individuals to ensure trust, validity, and repeatability. This documentation
not only promotes streamlined on-boarding and communication between groups but also provides record-

keeping in the event of an audit and ensures alignment with most-risk-averse national security practices,
regulatory, and standards adherence throughout the platform’s life cycle.14

3.3.1 Strategic Personnel

Strategic personnel are responsible for high-level decision-making, including quality assurance of the data
portal’s contents. Strategic personnel ensure that datasets, user communities, and management activities,
among others, are aligned with the overall objectives of the project and portal. Additionally, they are
accountable for the activities of tactical and operational personnel, ethical considerations, use of artificial
intelligence, and adherence to security, privacy, and regulatory standards.15

Data governance managers: Lead the overall data governance framework for the program, establishing
the strategic vision, and ensuring scientific and technical integrity.

Data owners and data creators: Encompass the personnel at the data’s origin, who have first collected,
stored, or managed the data in question. Data owners and creators may be considered the developers of
the data itself, or they may play other roles in its creation or curation at origin. Ultimately, these personnel
are responsible for the data’s continued updating, dispensation, and security—particularly in instances of
portal linkages to these original datasets.

3.3.2 Tactical Personnel

Tactical personnel are responsible for recognizing and recording issues that require the input of the data
owner. They coordinate with other data stewards during the weekly data steward meeting. They provide
input to the larger project data governance council each week.

Project data stewards: While each member may have responsibilities specific to their subject matter
expertise, responsibilities, such as identifying datasets, are interchangeable. Each member of the Project
Data Governance Council is responsible for data stewardship.

Data curator: Creates metadata for each dataset, checks datasets to ensure they are complete and do not
contain any potential PII or critical infrastructure issues. Communicates any issues found, or their
readiness for publication to other stewards and the data manager for final review and release.

Data portal manager: Identifies new datasets to include in the portal. Checks published data cards with
datasets linked periodically to stay up to date. Responds to new user requests. Troubleshoots data portal
schema changes. Is responsible for any changes made to the backend administration of the portal and
communicates to all relevant parties. Works with the data curator to determine workflows for updates,
system integration, and backups.

Security steward: Develops guidelines, standards, and protocols for ingress and egress information
security. Provides guidance for operations, including implementation and use. Provides iterative guidance
and documentation based on risk assessment and scenario analysis. (Guidance subject to national security
standards, federal and state regulations.)

3.3.3 Operational Personnel

Finally, the operational personnel provide further support for the stewards in their management functions
and inform the strategic personnel of any issues that may impact policies and any implementation issues.
Operational personnel include team members that have clearly defined scopes and expertise in ensuring
the success of the project.

Information technology lead: Is responsible for storage, access controls, overall architecture and disaster
recovery. Works with the security steward to deploy cybersecurity risk response across personnel and
platform.

Legal: Reviews any legal issues that arise, such the terms of agreement between multi-organizational
project data portals and ORNL’s legal standards and policies. Reviews any licensing issues. Helps
determine whether any privacy concerns need to be addressed. Is responsible for any nondisclosure
agreements and is tasked with communicating needed qualifications or constraints for individual and/or
aggregate datasets.

Privacy/auditors: Determine data portal compliance with federal and state regulations and provide cross-
checks and external reviews of portal protection of private energy infrastructure information, critical
energy infrastructure information, PII, and controlled unclassified information.

Cybersecurity: Is responsible for implementation of protection measures for overall system as well as
response and periodic audits of cybersecurity measures.

 4. PRINCIPLES OF RISK ASSESSMENT AND MITIGATION

Energy data and other critical infrastructure data (such as transportation, water, emergency
services data, etc.) are collected and managed by a vast range of agencies, organizations, and
research institutions across fields, sectors, and scales. The intention behind data-sharing
platforms to increase data accessibility and enhance research and decision-making, may amplify
risks to cyber-physical systems, particularly across sectoral boundaries.i Further challenges to
national security emerge particularly when human interpretation of regulatory practices and
privacy-preserving techniques influence the accessibility of this data, or lead to intentional non-
transparencies as mitigation techniques to guard against unwanted exposure and
vulnerabilities.iiiiiiv

Today, there is no shared standard for scalable cross-agency critical infrastructure data sharing,
workflow connections, security settings, nor for tagging, denoting dataset anomalies, naming
conventions, or use cases. Scattered approaches to data governance infrastructure – when present
– produce further fragmentation and incompleteness of resulting data usage and inconsistencies
in aggregating, cataloging, and storing datasets for research purposes. Given a lack of existing
tools for data governance and management across agencies, applying uniform risk assessment
and mitigation techniques result in inconsistent security and compliance with scaffolded rules
and regulations – leading to inefficiencies in utilizing this information for data-driven decision-
making.

4.1 DEMONSTRATED SOLUTIONS AT OAK RIDGE NATIONAL LABORATORY

To promote security and uniformity of critical infrastructure data in the development of these
platforms, Oak Ridge National Laboratory develops anticipatory lifecycle data governance and
management workflows, which are authored and adopted prior to a project’s launch. These
workflows enable flexibility, pivots, and refinements across the data lifecycle, and the nature of
their data and metadata curation techniques promote more discoverability and usability of this
information for research and decision-making purposes.

4.1.2 Standards and Policies Across all Research Data Platforms and Catalogs

ORNL’s security of critical infrastructure data in the development and refinement of data-sharing
platforms and research data catalogs is informed by consistent challenges and trends across organizations
and use cases that influence risk mitigation decision-making measures that are built into data governance
and management models. These include:

  Data volume of varied quality, validity, reliability, collection,
  Risk to infrastructure security based on data type, as opposed to visibility,
  Management backlogs for critical infrastructure information controls,
  Minimal guidance for security, export control, distribution of critical infrastructure data; and
  Varied data governance policies across jurisdictions, sectors, types and resulting overprotection,
 under-protection, misidentified data, or challenges with “shared language” among entities.

 11

  Because of these consistent observations related to the fragmentation of this data, we employ the
  following standards and policies for all our architectures as inherent in their design:

 The National Institute of Standards & Technology (NIST) Research Data Framework (RDaF)
  provides structure for the lifecycle management and curation of research data. Our team uses this
  framework to guide ingestion/linking, aggregation, maintenance, and dissemination of critical
  infrastructure data. This framework also enables our team to easily adopt DOE and individual
  laboratories’ policies for data use and sharing – cataloging, documenting, and maintaining standardized
  metadata for reuse and research collaboration.

 Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable (FAIR) Principles enable discoverability through
  standardization and auto-tagging with consistent identifiers, accessibility through security protocols,
  interoperability across tools, platforms, and entities, and documentation for reuse and collaboration.

 Findable, Accessible, Reproducible, Repeatable (FARR) Principles build on FAIR principles through
  version control, preserved analytical methods, and repeatable workflows for research reproducibility.

 The Data Curation Network’s (DCN) CURATE(d) Model is used to structure data acquisition,
  cleaning, curation, documentation, and storage in a secure, consistent manner. File-level curation ensures
  early risk assessment and mitigation through systematic metadata creation and subsequent access controls
  for the protection of sensitive and controlled data – both individually and in aggregate.

 Controlled Unclassified Information (CUI) Standards are embedded throughout the project lifecycle,
  beginning with classification reviews during file-level review. All datasets undergo classification and
  CUI-Specific reviews for information type, purpose, and sensitivity – both individually and in aggregate.
  The Security Steward implements access controls and risk mitigation protocols according to federal
  policies and DOE regulations, Executive Order 13556 for Controlled Unclassified Information, and
  subsequent guidance and standards.

 Ontology and Semantic Standards are adopted based on project purpose and use cases to provide
  shared definitions and vocabulary across tools and entities. Ontologies like the Open Energy Ontology
  (OEO), QUDT, and W3C Semantic Web Standards (RDF, OWL, SPARQL) enable machine-readable
  metadata, and support automation of workflows, interoperability, and audit defensibility through
  standardized. documentation.

  Additional standards and protocols are added based on the platform’s purpose, data ownership, type,
  collection, and cross-and-interorganizational standards, preferences, and securities. Data owners serve as
  the ultimate determinants of their willingness to undergo risk; however, file-level curation and security
  stewardship inherently adopts the most risk-averse stance for each dataset and adapts controls and
  accessibility according to scaffolded and nested policies, conventions, and changes in standards.

4.2 POST-CURATION RISK ASSESSMENT PROCESS

Finally, risk is evaluated through cascading scoring matrices developed during curation. Samples are
extracted from the data and examined, just as in curation. Sample review enables dataset scoring on risk
matrices, where criterion is set based on the purpose, use cases, and proposed user communities for the
project. While curation generates 20 samples from the data/metadata, more evaluation may be needed
based on tagging, CUI designation, presence of PII, or flags during curation. Geospatial visualizations aid
in the evaluation of risk and vulnerabilities to national security.

Raw scores are then determined based on assigned tags, exposure, attack capability and impact,
information sensitivity, and ease of access or exploitation – and compared against security threshold

 13

scores for relative risk. Datasets are scored individually according to these thresholds and flagged for
scores that exceed “Low Risk.” Then, composite scores for aggregated datasets are examined against
established thresholds for aggregated data, and security controls are then developed based on intended
user communities. Further action may be required if Tactical Personnel are undecided, or if there is
identified immediate risk or concern (i.e. the presence of PII). These actions follow processes outlined in
the project’s Data Governance Framework.

 5. ROLE-BASED ACCESS CONTROLS

When developing controls for which users have access to which data, it is important to refer to the use
case for which the portal has been developed and for which datasets have been integrated. Role-Based
Access Controls (RBACs) enable groupings of like-users – referred to as “user communities” – to ensure
that these individuals are accessing, viewing, and using individual or aggregated datasets for their
intended purpose and as aligned with their organizational roles, responsibilities, and accountabilities.

To develop RBACs prior to platform launch, we revisit the inherent purpose behind the creation and
curation of the project platform, its data, and its intended audience. We develop user personas to
determine our user communities, characterizing their roles, data needs, and relative organizational
policies for data-sharing and inherent national security. User personas enable Tactical Personnel to
synthesize the motivations, conditions, and accountabilities that any one user type may have – both in
their individual organizational role (job/position) and within their respective organization. User personas
are then assigned to like user communities – where similar access needs, security controls, and available
datasets (individual or aggregated) are determined according to the project’s Data Management Plan
(DMP) and risk assessments.

While users without an account may be able to browse open data sets, as exemplified in Open Energy
Data Portal (OEDP), national security risk is evaluated for individual and aggregated datasets. Here,
accounts and access for downloads and localized use are considered according to the project’s inherent
purpose, the account requestor’s organization, role, and purpose, and their contact information – which
can often be determined using portal access request forms to enable Tactical Personnel to assign RBACs
for the requestor prior to their conditional confirmation and registration.

 8. ALIGNMENT WITH STANDARDS AND TAXONOMIES

Helenmary Sheridan succinctly summarizes the need for a data catalog to align with standards and
taxonomies:

 “As nexuses for discovery, data catalogs describe data products with standards-compliant
 metadata that creates linkages among related records and serves structured data to search

 20

 engines. They are searchable and browsable, although the “how” varies according to each
 platform’s architecture.”22

Therefore, a data catalog that aligns with national and domain-specific standards increases the precision
of a search by using controlled access points as defined by a taxonomy. Taxonomies generally work by
creating a preferred term along with broader, narrower, and related terms. Recall can be increased by
leveraging existing key terms, also known as a folksonomy.

Because most existing energy grid taxonomies do not contain all the terms that may get to the
“aboutness” of a dataset, Ofgem has suggested using other controlled vocabularies and indicating the
source in brackets. Consequently, we see this as an area of further research. Done well, effective file-level
curation helps manage risk to the entirety of the governance structure (i.e., the data catalog) and can
actively contribute to scientific achievement across the entirety of the DOE laboratory complex.

 9. CONCLUSION

As we identify, explore, and catalog datasets for simultaneous use, we can maximize the longevity and
intended use cases through detailed governance and management schema that provide decision-making
processes and are iterative in nature. Although additional time for strategy and planning at the front end is
essential to design and implement these frameworks, demonstrating communication about the intended
use, audience, and purpose for curating data can ensure long-term efficiencies, reuse, and collaboration.

These frameworks enable seamless training and onboarding, and we can operationalize them across
catalogs, datasets, and repositories across projects and programs. For DOE research data, moving toward
a shared vision for data security across the national laboratory complex can assure proprietary data is
governed by standards, procedures, and established rules and regulations that ensure interoperability,
support for future integration, visualization, and automation.

Finally, we enable research data to be used for its intended purpose, which in turn builds trust among all
personnel and influences science communication. We provide information about how we collect, store,
and protect data—such as controlled unclassified information, critical energy infrastructure information,
and private energy infrastructure information—and we can demonstrate successes more easily to our
sponsors and alignment with the agency’s mission, vision, and objectives.
