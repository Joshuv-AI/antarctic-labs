On single-frequency asymptotics for


Abstract
We consider damped driven Maxwell–Bloch equations for a single-mode Maxwell field coupled to a two-level molecule. The equations are used for semiclassical description of the laser action. Our main result is the construction of solutions with single-frequency asymptotics of the Maxwell field in the case of quasiperiodic pumping. The asymptotics hold for solutions with harmonic initial values which are stationary states of averaged reduced equations in the interaction picture. We calculate all harmonic states and analyse their stability. Our calculations rely on the Hopf reduction by the gauge symmetry group U ⁡ ( 1 ) U(1) . The asymptotics follow by an extension of the averaging theory of Bogolyubov–Eckhaus–Sanchez-Palencia onto dynamical systems on manifolds. The key role in the application of the averaging theory is played by a special a priori estimate.

Introduction

The Maxwell–Bloch equations (MBE) were introduced by Lamb
[
20
]
for semiclassical
description of the laser action
[
1
,
6
,
13
,
27
,
28
,
29
,
30
]
.
The equations
are obtained as the Galerkin approximation of the Maxwell–Schrödinger system

[
5
,
12
,
15
,
17
,
18
,
19
,
22
,
25
]
.
Our main goal is construction of solutions with single-frequency asymptotics of the Maxwell amplitude.
The asymptotics provisionally correspond to
the laser coherent radiation, which
remains a key mystery of laser action since its discovery around 1960.
The damped driven MBE for pure states read

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}\dot{A}(t)&\!\!=&\!\!B(t),\quad\dot{B}(t)=-\Omega^{2}A(t)\!-\!\gamma B(t)+cj(t)\\
\\
i\hbar\dot{C}_{1}(t)&\!\!=&\!\!\hbar\omega_{1}C_{1}(t)+ia(t)\,C_{2}(t),\quad i\hbar\dot{C}_{2}(t)=\hbar\omega_{2}C_{2}(t)-ia(t)\,C_{1}(t)\end{array}\right|,\qquad t\geq 0.

(1.1)

Here
A(t),B(t)\in{\mathbb R}
, while
C_{1}(t),C_{2}(t)\in{\mathbb C}
;

\Omega>0
is the resonance frequency,
\gamma>0
is the dissipation coefficient,

c
is the speed of light,
\hbar
is the Planck constant,
and

\hbar\omega_{2}>\hbar\omega_{1}
are the energy levels of active molecules.
The current
j(t)
and the function
a(t)
are given by

j(t)={2}\varkappa\,{\rm Im{\hskip 1.42262pt}}[\overline{C}_{1}(t)C_{2}(t)],\quad a(t):=\frac{\varkappa}{c}[A(t)+A^{e}(t)],\qquad\varkappa=p\,{{\omega}},\quad{\omega}=\omega_{2}-\omega_{1}>0,

(1.2)

where
p\in{\mathbb R}
is proportional to the molecular dipole moment.
We
suppose that the external field
A^{e}(t)
(the pumping) is a quasiperiodic function:

A^{e}(t)={\rm Re{\hskip 1.42262pt}}[{\bf A}^{e}e^{-i\Omega t}+\sum_{1}^{N}{\bf A}^{e}_{k}e^{-i\Omega_{k}t}],\qquad{\rm where}\qquad{\bf A}^{e},{\bf A}^{e}_{k}\in{\mathbb C},\quad\Omega_{k}\in{\mathbb R}\quad{\rm and}\quad\Omega_{k}\neq\Omega.

(1.3)

We use the Heaviside–Lorentz units, and in Appendix
C
we
comment on the introduction of the equations.

For the MBE,
the charge conservation holds
|C_{1}(t)|^{2}+|C_{2}(t)|^{2}=\mathop{\rm const}\nolimits
. We will consider solutions with
\mathop{\rm const}\nolimits=1
:

|C_{1}(t)|^{2}+|C_{2}(t)|^{2}=1,\qquad t\geq 0.

(1.4)

Accordingly, the phase space of the system is

{\mathbb X}={\mathbb R}^{2}\times{\rm S}^{3},

where
{\rm S}^{3}
is the unit sphere in
{\mathbb C}^{2}
. For
any
r>0
,
solutions
X(t)=(A(t),B(t),C_{1}(t),C_{2}(t))
to (
1.1
) with
|p|/\gamma=r

satisfy the following a priori bounds:

|X(t)|\leq D_{r}(|X(0)|),\qquad t\geq 0,

(1.5)

which is proved in Appendix
A
.
The bound implies
the well-posedness of the MBE
in the phase space
{\mathbb X}
.

Our main goal is asymptotics of the Maxwell amplitudes
A(t)
and
B(t)

for solutions
X(t)
to the MBE
as

p,\gamma\to 0
.
Define complex Maxwell amplitudes by
M(t)=A(t)+iB(t)/\Omega
.
The first two equations of (
1.1
) are equivalent to

\dot{M}(t)=-i\Omega M(t)-i\gamma M_{2}(t)+2icp\omega\,{\rm Im{\hskip 1.42262pt}}[\overline{C}_{1}(t)C_{2}(t)]/\Omega,\quad{\rm where}\quad M_{2}(t)={\rm Im{\hskip 1.42262pt}}M(t).

(1.6)

Note that the parameters
|p|,\gamma
are very small for many types of lasers, see Appendix
D
.
For
p=\gamma=0
, all solutions to (
1.6
)
are single-frequency:
M(t)=e^{-i\Omega t}M(0)
.
For small
|p|
and
\gamma
, equation (
1.6
) implies that

M(t)=e^{-i\Omega t}M(0)+\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}R(s)ds,

where
\sup\limits_{s\geq 0}|R(s)|={\cal O}(|p|+\gamma)
by (
1.5
). Hence,
M(t)=e^{-i\Omega t}M(0)+{\cal O}(|p|t)

for
p/\gamma=r
with arbitrary fixed
r\neq 0
.
In particular, for solutions with any fixed initial states
M(0)
,

\max_{t\in[0,|p|^{-1/2}]}|M(t)-e^{-i\Omega t}M(0)|={\cal O}({|p|^{1/2}}),\qquad{p\to 0},\quad{p}/\gamma=r.\quad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad

(1.7)

Our main results show that in the resonance case, when

\Omega=\omega
,
the time scale
|p|^{-1/2}
in this asymptotics can be extended
to
|p|^{-1}
and even more in the case of special
harmonic states

of the dynamics (
1.1
).

The harmonic states are defined via
the reduction of dynamics (
1.1
) by the symmetry gauge group
U(1)
with the action

g(\theta)(A,B,C_{1},C_{2})=(A,B,e^{i\theta}C_{1},e^{i\theta}C_{2}),\qquad\theta\in[0,2\pi].

(1.8)

The action turns the sphere
{\rm S}^{3}
, corresponding to
(
1.4
), into the Hopf fibration with the base
{\rm S}^{2}
.
We introduce suitable “global” coordinate
Q\in{\mathbb C}
in the base,
using
the stereographic projection
{\rm S}^{2}\to{\mathbb C}
:

Q=\frac{Z}{\frac{1}{2}-{\bf Z}_{3}},\qquad{\bf Z}_{3}=\pm\sqrt{\frac{1}{4}-|Z|^{2}},\qquad Z=\overline{C}_{1}C_{2},

(1.9)

where
|Z|\leq 1/2
by (
1.4
), and

Z=|C_{1}C_{2}|e^{i\delta}
with
\delta=\theta_{2}-\theta_{1}
, where
\theta_{k}=\arg C_{k}
.
The base
{\rm S}^{2}
is identified with the graph of the two-valued function
Q(Z)
on the disk
{\bf D}:=\{Z\in{\mathbb C}:|Z|\leq 1/2\}
.
The coordinates
Z
and
Q
are invariant with respect to the action (
1.8
).
The population inversion
I:=|C_{2}|^{2}-|C_{1}|^{2}
admits the representation

I=\frac{|Q|^{2}-1}{|Q|^{2}+1},\qquad Q\in{\mathbb C}.

(1.10)

Thus, the reduction map
\Pi

transforms the system (
1.1
) with the phase space
{\mathbb X}={\mathbb R}^{2}\times S^{3}
, into the system of
ODEs with the factor-phase space
{\mathbb Y}=\Pi{\mathbb X}={\mathbb R}^{2}\times{\rm S}^{2}
.
Accordingly, the reduced dynamics becomes the system of ODEs for the complex Maxwell amplitude

M(t)\in{\mathbb C}

and the complex variable
Q(t)\in{\mathbb C}

which represents the amplitudes
(C_{1}(t),C_{2}(t))\in{\rm S}^{3}
.
The reduced
system can be written as

\dot{Y}(t)=F(Y(t),t),\qquad t\geq 0;\qquad Y(t)=(M(t),Q(t)).

(1.11)

It is crucially important that the Maxwell amplitudes
M(t)
are not changed
by the reduction, so their properties for the Maxwell–Bloch dynamics
(
1.1
) and for the reduced dynamics (
1.11
) are identical.
We consider the

interaction picture
of the reduced dynamics (
1.11
)
and
the corresponding
averaged equations
with the structure

\dot{\bf Y}(t)=p{\bf F}_{r}({\bf Y}(t)),\qquad t\geq 0,\qquad r=p/\gamma.

(1.12)

We define
the harmonic states
{\bf Y}\in{\mathbb Y}
of the reduced dynamics
(
1.11
) as stationary solutions of (
1.12
).
We call
X\in{\mathbb X}
as harmonic states of the MBE if

{\bf Y}=\Pi X
is the harmonic state of (
1.11
).
We show that the harmonic states depend only on the quotient
p/\gamma
.

Our main results are asymptotics for solutions
X(t)
to equations (
1.1
) with a
fixed quotient
p/\gamma=r
. Everywhere below we consider the case
p>0
only since the extension
to
p<0
is obvious.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\Omega=\omega
and the pumping be quasiperiodic.
Then for any
r>0
,
the following asymptotics hold.

i) Let
X(0)=X^{r}
be a harmonic state of (
1.1
), and

{\bf Y}^{r}=({\bf M}^{r},{\bf Q}^{r}):=\Pi X^{r}
be the corresponding stationary solution to (
1.12
).
Then for solutions
(M(t),Q(t))=\Pi X(t)
to the reduced system (
1.11
),
the adiabatic asymptotics holds:

\max_{t\in[0,p^{-1}]}\Big[|M(t)-e^{-i\Omega t}{\bf M}^{r}|{+|Q(t)-e^{-i\Omega t}{\bf Q}^{r}|}\Big]={\cal O}({p^{1/2}}),\qquad{p\to 0}.\quad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad

(1.13)

ii) Let, additionally,

{\bf Y}^{r}=({\bf M}^{r},{\bf Q}^{r})

be an
asymptotically stable stationary solution to (
1.12
),
and
D\subset{\mathbb C}^{2}
be a bounded domain of attraction.
Then for initial states
X(0)
with
\Pi X(0)\in D
,

\max_{t\in[0,p^{-1}]}\Big[|M(t)-e^{-i\Omega t}{\bf M}(t)|{+|Q(t)-e^{-i\Omega t}{\bf Q}(t)|}\Big]={\cal O}({p^{1/2}}),\qquad{p\to 0},\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad

(1.14)

where
{\bf M}(t)
and
{\bf Q}(t)
satisfy

({\bf M}(t),{\bf A}(t))\to({\bf M}^{r},{\bf Q}^{r}),\quad t\to\infty;\qquad\sup_{t\geq 0}[|\dot{\bf M}(t)|+|\dot{\bf Q}(t)|]={\cal O}(p),\qquad{p\to 0}.

(1.15)

iii) Let, additionally,
{\bf Y}^{r}=({\bf M}^{r},{\bf Q}^{r})

be a linearly stable
stationary solution to (
1.12
).
Then for initial states
X(0)=X^{r}
with
\Pi X^{r}={\bf Y}^{r}
,
the uniform in time asymptotics holds,

\quad\sup_{t\in[0,\infty)}\Big[|M(t)-e^{-i\Omega t}{\bf M}^{r}|{+|Q(t)-e^{-i\Omega t}{\bf Q}^{r}|}\Big]={\cal O}(p),\qquad{p\to 0}.

(1.16)

Moreover, for initial states
X(0)
sufficiently close to
X^{r}
,
the following asymptotics with attraction holds: for sufficiently
small
\varepsilon>0
, and some
\mu>0
,

|M(t)-e^{-i\Omega t}{\bf M}^{r}|{+|Q(t)-e^{-i\Omega t}{\bf Q}^{r}|}\leq C[p+d_{0}e^{-p\mu t}],\qquad t\geq 0,\qquad{p\leq\varepsilon},

(1.17)

where
d_{0}=|X(0)-X^{r}|
.

Remark 1.2
.

By (
1.10
), the asymptotics of type (
1.13
)–(
1.17
) hold also for
I(t)-I^{r}
,
where
I^{r}=({\bf Q}^{r}|^{2}-1)/(|{\bf Q}^{r}|^{2}+1)
.

Remark 1.3
.

Without
the constraint
p/\gamma=r
, the
a priori bounds (
1.5
) and the asymptotics (
1.13
)–(
1.17
) do not hold.
For example, the bounds must be different for small and big
|p|
for a given dissipation
\gamma>0
.
The asymptotics
do not hold without this restriction since the limiting amplitudes
{\bf M}^{r}
depend on
r
.

Let us comment on
our approach.
To prove the asymptotics (
1.13
), we show
that the reduced system (
1.11
)
admits solutions

M(t)=e^{-i\Omega t}{\mathbb M}^{r}(t)
,
Q(t)=e^{-i\omega t}{\mathbb Q}^{r}(t)

with
slowly varying
enveloping amplitudes
{\mathbb M}^{r}(t)
and
{\mathbb Q}^{r}(t)

for small
p
and
\gamma
with
p/\gamma=r
.
The amplitudes are solutions to the corresponding dynamical system
which is the
interaction picture
(or “rotating frame representation”) of (
1.1
).
The slow variation of the amplitudes
is equivalent to the fact
that
the initial state
({\mathbb M}(0),{\mathbb Q}(0))=(M(0),Q(0))

is a harmonic state, i.e., a stationary state of (
1.12
).

We
calculate all harmonic states
{\bf Y}^{r}=({\bf M}^{r},{\bf Q}^{r})\in{\mathbb Y}

which are stationary solutions to (
1.12
).
We show that
the states with
{\bf M}^{r}\neq 0
exist only
in the resonance case
\Omega=\omega
and only if
{\bf A}^{e}\neq 0
.
In particular, for single-frequency pumping

A^{e}(t)={\rm Re{\hskip 1.42262pt}}[{\bf A}_{p}e^{-i\omega_{p}t}]
,
the asymptotics (
1.13
)–(
1.17
) with
{\bf M}^{r}\neq 0
hold only in the case

{\bf A}_{p}\neq 0
and
triple resonance

\Omega=\omega=\omega_{p}.

(1.18)

We linearise the averaged dynamics (
1.12
) at the harmonic states and
calculate the spectrum of the linearisations.
In particular, we show that in the case
cr>|{\bf A}^{e}|
,
there exist

linearly stable

harmonic states
{\bf Y}^{r}=({\bf M}^{r},{\bf Q}^{r})
of (
1.12
).

For the proof of
the asymptotics (
1.13
)–(
1.17
), we extend
the averaging theory of Bogolyubov type
[
26
, Theorem 4.3.6]

and the averaging theory of Eckhaus and Sanchez-Palencia

[
26
, Theorem 5.5.1]
onto dynamical systems on manifolds.
The extension is done in Appendix
B
. The key role
in the application of the averaging theory
is played by the special a priori estimate (
1.5
).

Remark 1.4
.

i)
The map
\Pi:{\mathbb X}\to{\mathbb Y}
reduces one degree of freedom in the MBE.
However, the role of the reduction is more significant. Namely,
the
single-frequency asymptotics of type (
1.13
)–(
1.17
) apparently
do not hold

for
C_{1}(t)
and
C_{2}(t)
.
This conjecture is suggested by the Rabi perturbative solution
[
28
, (5.2.14)–(5.2.16)]

since it can
contain incommensurable frequencies.

ii) Our approach relies on the averaging theory which
neglects oscillating terms. So, it gives a justification of the “rotating wave approximation”, which is widely used in Quantum
Optics
[
1
,
13
,
27
,
28
,
29
,
30
]
.
The asymptotics (
1.13
)–(
1.17
)
specify the time scale and the error of such approximations.

Let us comment on related results.
The problem of existence of time-periodic solutions
to the MBE
has been
discussed since 1960s.
The first results in this direction were obtained recently in

[
10
]
and
[
31
]

for various versions
of the MBE.
In
[
31
]
,
the N-th order time-periodic
solutions
were constructed by perturbation techniques.
For the phenomenological model
[
2
,
3
]
,
time-periodic solutions
were constructed in
[
10
]
in the absence of
time-periodic pumping for small interaction constants.
The solutions are obtained as the result of a bifurcation
relying on homotopy invariance of the degree
[
8
]
and developing the
averaging arguments
[
9
]
.
The period is determined by the bifurcation.

In
[
16
]
, we have established the existence of
solutions with
T
-periodic Maxwell amplitude
for any
T
-periodic pumping
without smallness conditions.

Up to our knowledge,
the single-frequency asymptotics for the MBE were
not constructed till now.

U(1)
-symmetry and the Hopf fibration

Recall that
the phase space of the MBE
is

{\mathbb X}={\mathbb R}^{2}\times{\rm S}^{3}
due to
the charge conservation (
1.4
).
The system
is

U(1)
-invariant with respect
to the action (
1.8
).
This means that the function
g(\theta)X(t)
is a solution if
X(t)
is. This is obvious
from
(
1.1
) and (
1.2
).
Let us denote the factor space
{\mathbb Y}={\mathbb X}/U(1)

which is the space of all orbits
X_{*}=\{g(\theta)X:\theta\in[0,2\pi]\}
with
X\in{\mathbb X}
.
Thus, the reduction map
\Pi:{\mathbb X}\to{\mathbb Y}
is defined by

\Pi:X=(A,B,C)\mapsto X_{*}=(A,B,C_{*})\in{\mathbb Y},\qquad X\in{\mathbb X},

(2.1)

where
C_{*}=PC
, and
the map
P:C\mapsto C_{*}
is the Hopf fibration
{\rm S}^{3}\to{\rm S}^{2}
.
So,
{\mathbb Y}={\mathbb R}^{2}\times{\rm S}^{2}
.
The MBE induce the corresponding reduced dynamics in the factorspace

{\mathbb Y}
which can be written as (
1.11
).

Lemma 2.1
.

The reduced dynamics (
1.11
) admits a unique global solution

Y(t)=(A(t),B(t),C_{*}(t))
for every initial state
Y(0)\in{\mathbb Y}
.

Proof.

The uniqueness follows from the smoothness and continuity of
F(\cdot,t)
.
To prove the existence, take any point
X_{0}\in\Pi^{-1}Y(0)
and set

Y(t)=\Pi X(t)
, where
X(t)
is the solution to the MBE with the initial state
X_{0}
.
Then
Y(t)
is the solution to (
1.11
) by definition of the reduced dynamics,
and the initial state
Y(0)=\Pi X(0)=Y_{0}
.
∎

The model of the Hopf projection.

The Hopf projection

P

can be represented by the map

P:C=(C_{1},C_{2})\mapsto Z=\overline{C}_{1}C_{2}

(2.2)

since it is constant on the fibers
\{e^{-i\theta}(C_{1},C_{2}):\theta\in[0,2\pi]\}
.
The orbit populations
|C_{1}|^{2}
and
|C_{2}|^{2}
can be expressed
in
Z
. Indeed,
|C_{1}|^{2}+|C_{2}|^{2}=1
and
|Z|=|C_{1}||C_{2}|
. Hence,
the populations and the population inversion are given by

|C_{1}|^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\mp\sqrt{\frac{1}{4}-|Z|^{2}},\quad|C_{2}|^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\pm\sqrt{\frac{1}{4}-|Z|^{2}},\quad I:=|C_{2}|^{2}-|C_{1}|^{2}=\pm\sqrt{1-4|Z|^{2}}.

(2.3)

All functions
Z
,
|C_{1}|
,
|C_{2}|
and
the population inversion
I=|C_{2}|^{2}-|C_{1}|^{2}
are
constant on the fibers, so they are functions on
{\mathbb Y}
.
Hence,
the function (
2.2
)
defines the map
{\rm S}^{2}\to{\bf D}
onto
the disk
{\bf D}:=\{Z\in{\mathbb C}:|Z|\leq\frac{1}{2}\}
. So,
Z

is a local coordinate on the base
{\rm S}^{2}
outside the points with
|Z|=\frac{1}{2}
.
On the other hand, the formulas (
2.3
)
show that the map is not a homeomorphism
{\rm S}^{2}\to{\bf D}

since the inverse map

{\bf D}\to{\rm S}^{2}
is two-valued.
In particular,
Z=0
for the Hopf projection of ground state
(1,0)
and for the
projection of the excited state
(0,1)
.

Let us introduce an appropriate model
{\bf S}^{2}
of the base
{\rm S}^{2}
with a two-folded projection

{\bf S}^{2}\to{\bf D}
.

Denote by
{\bf B}
the ball
\{|{\bf Z}|\leq 1/2\}\subset{\mathbb R}^{3}
.
We identify the base
{\rm S}^{2}
with
the sphere
{\bf S}^{2}=\{{\bf Z}\in{\mathbb R}^{3}:|{\bf Z}|=\frac{1}{2}\}

and
{\bf D}
with the equatorial section
{\bf B}\cap\{{\bf Z}_{3}=0\}
.
Denote by
{\bf S}^{2}_{\pm}
the hemispheres

{\bf S}^{2}_{\pm}=\{{\bf Z}\in{\bf S}^{2}:{\bf Z}_{3}\gtrless 0\},

(2.4)

so
the orthogonal projections of
{\bf S}^{2}_{\pm}
onto the disc
{\bf D}

coincide.
Let us identify

Z={\bf Z}_{1}+i{\bf Z}_{2}
, see Fig.
1
.

To fix the model, we identify the North Pole
(0,0,1/2)
with
P(0,1)

and the South Pole
(0,0,-1/2)
with
P(1,0)
.
Equivalently,

{\bf S}^{2}_{\pm}=\{{\bf Z}\in{\bf S}^{2}:I\gtrless 0\}.

(2.5)

Remark 2.2
.

In the next section, we will construct a “global coordinate” on the base
{\bf S}^{2}

except one point.

The reduced dynamics.

Let us construct suitable representation for the reduced dynamics
(
1.11
) in the coordinate

.
Differentiating

, we obtain

\dot{Z}(t)=\dot{\overline{C}}_{1}C_{2}+\overline{C}_{1}\dot{C}_{2}=\overline{(-i\omega_{1}C_{1}+\frac{a}{\hbar}C_{2})}C_{2}+\overline{C}_{1}(-i\omega_{2}C_{2}-\frac{a}{\hbar}C_{1})=-i{\omega}\,\,Z+\frac{a}{\hbar}(|C_{2}|^{2}-|C_{1}|^{2}).

(2.6)

Using (

) and (

), we rewrite equation (

) as

\dot{Z}(t)=-i{\omega}\,\,Z\pm b[A(t)+A^{e}(t)]\sqrt{1-4|Z|^{2}},\qquad b=\frac{\varkappa}{c\hbar}.

(2.7)

.

)

) and (

(

) and

Resolution of singularity and the reduced dynamics

(B.1)

where
V(Y)
is a vector field on
{\cal M}
. The corresponding

\dot{\bf Y}(t)=\varepsilon\overline{V}({\bf Y}(t)),\quad t\geq 0;\qquad\overline{V}({\bf Y})=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}V({\bf Y},t)dt,\quad{\bf Y}\in{\cal M}.

(B.2)

Definition B.1
.

i) (
[

)

[

classical Theorem 4.3.6 of
[

]
admits the following extension.
Let the vector field

be Lipschitz continuous uniformly in time:

B.5
) implies (
B.3
).

Theorem B.2
.

Let
{\cal M}
be a compact manifold, (
B.5
) hold,

)

.

Proof.

The compact manifold

Appendix D
On possible treatment of the laser action

Here we discuss possible treatment of the laser action
relying on the obtained results.

On the smallness of the parameters.

Note that the dipole moment
p
and dissipation coefficient
\gamma
are very small
for many types of lasers. In particular,
the dissipation coefficient for the Ruby laser is the electrical conduction of corundum
which is

\gamma\sim 10^{-14}
in the Heaviside–Lorentz
units

[
11
,
29
,
30
]
.
For the dipole moment typically

|p|\sim 10^{-18}
according to

[
21
]

that agrees with the classical dipole moment
ed/2
, where

d\sim 10^{-8}
cm is the molecular diameter, and

e\sim 10^{-10}
is the elementary
charge in the same units.

On the laser threshold.

The asymptotics (
1.13
) and (
1.16
) hold for solutions with the harmonic initial states
({\bf M}^{r}_{\pm},{\bf S}^{r}_{\pm})
. On the other hand,
(
1.14
) and (
1.17
) hold for solutions with initial states
from an open domain of attraction in the phase space.
Hence,
the asymptotics (
1.14
) and (
1.17
) appear with a “nonzero probability” in contrast to
(
1.13
) and (
1.16
). This fact, provisionally,
shed new light on the “laser threshold” which is necessary
to ignite the laser action: the intensity of random pumping must be sufficiently large to bring the solution to
the domain
of attraction, and then the solution is captured in the domain with the single-frequency asymptotics.

On the laser amplification.

The equations (
1.1
) describe one molecule coupled to
the Maxwell field.
The limiting amplitudes of the Maxwell field
in all asymptotics
(
1.13
)–(
1.16
)
do not depend on non-resonance harmonics in the pumping (
1.3
)
with the frequencies
\Omega_{k}\neq\omega
.
This means that the dynamics (
1.1
) acts as a filter, selecting only the resonant harmonics, that itself cannot explain the
amplification of the Maxwell field in laser devices.
The amplification could be explained by a large number of active molecules,
typically
N\sim 10^{20}
, under the traditional assumption that the molecules
interact with the Maxwell field but do not interact with each other
[
23
]
.
In this case, the amplitude of the total Maxwell field is multiplied by
\sqrt{N}\sim 10^{10}
by the Law of Large Numbers.
The amplification is possible only due to the single-frequency asymptotics of individual contributions.

The amplification also depends significantly on the fact that
the phases of the amplitudes
{\bf M}^{r}

in asymptotics (
1.13
)–(
1.17
)
for all molecules are uniformly distributed.
The uniformity takes place
if it holds for phases of
{\bf A}^{e}
.
This follows from the asymptotics and
Remark
5.4
since
e^{i\phi}e^{-i\Omega t}{\bf M}
differs from
e^{-i\Omega t}{\bf M}

by a shift of time.

Self-induced transparency.
The harmonic states (
5.6
) means that the incident wave

{\bf A}^{e}e^{-i\Omega t}
results in the outgoing wave
M(t)\approx-{\bf A}^{e}e^{-i\Omega t}
with the same
amplitude and frequency, but with the phase jump
\pi
. This phenomenon resembles
the self-induced transparency
[
29
]
.
