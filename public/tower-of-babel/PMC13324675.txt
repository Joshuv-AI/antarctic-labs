Longitudinal methylated ctDNA increases predict immunotherapy progression across solid tumors

ABSTRACT
Timely identification of treatment failure during immune checkpoint blockade remains a critical unmet need in oncology. We evaluated a tissue-free, methylation-based circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) assay applied serially across 142 patients with advanced solid tumors receiving immune checkpoint inhibitor monotherapy or chemo-immunotherapy in two independent prospective cohorts. Blood was collected pre-treatment, C2D1, and C3D1. Molecular progression, mPD, was defined as an increase in ctDNA while on therapy. mPD stratified progression-free and overall survival across both cohorts (PFS HR 5.8 95% CI 3.4-9.9; OS HR 4.1 95%CI 2.3-7.0). Notably, incorporating ctDNA at C2 identified more molecular rebounders, and mPD was significant for OS in RECIST 6-month BOR stable disease subgroup where imaging is least informative (HR 8.4, 95% CI 1.4 to 48.7). Monitoring with ctDNA continued to predict progression after C3 and identified progression with median lead time of 62 days. These findings confirm prior studies that have shown that ctDNA is predictive for immunotherapy response and furthermore demonstrate the utility of a clinically practical molecular progression rule across the multiple cancer types treated by immune checkpoint inhibitors.

Introduction

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI) have transformed outcomes across many advanced solid tumors, yet durable benefit remains confined to a subset of patients [[1], [2], [3]]. Critically, prior studies have primarily focused on predicting initial treatment efficacy at early landmark timepoints. In the advanced cancer setting, however, treatment failure is common even among patients who demonstrate initial molecular or radiographic response, and acquired resistance frequently develops during the course of therapy [4,5]. Radiographic evaluation by RECIST criteria, while the established response assessment standard, has well-recognized limitations in this setting: scan frequency is constrained by cost and workflow; anatomic change frequently lags behind underlying biology; and the distinction between pseudoprogression and true progression during immunotherapy can be clinically challenging [[6], [7], [8], [9], [10], [11], [12]]. Conversely, stable disease on imaging does not reliably exclude early molecular treatment failure. These constraints collectively underscore the need for earlier, more continuous biological assessment of treatment response.

On-treatment circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) dynamics have consistently demonstrated the ability to predict immune checkpoint blockade efficacy across multiple tumor types [[13], [14], [15], [16], [17], [18], [19], [20]]. However, several unresolved challenges limit clinical translation. Most prior ctDNA studies in this setting have not addressed whether serial molecular monitoring can detect emerging resistance in patients who initially respond, leaving the question of longitudinal surveillance during ongoing treatment incompletely addressed. Fixed percentage decline thresholds (e.g., 50% or 90% reductions from baseline) have been calibrated to specific assay architectures, tumor types, and treatment classes, limiting generalizability [14,[21], [22], [23]]. In the chemo-immunotherapy context, transient ctDNA decreases from cytotoxic tumor debulking can confound single-timepoint landmark assessments [24,25]. Additionally, ctDNA shedding is inherently heterogeneous across tumor types, making universal population-level thresholds difficult to establish [26,27]. These limitations argue for an approach anchored to within-patient directional change analogous to how RECIST defines radiographic progression from a nadir, and PSA-based recurrence monitoring relies on serial increase independent of absolute value [6,28,29], rather than fixed thresholds requiring disease-specific calibration.

Tissue-free ctDNA assays offer practical advantages for serial monitoring in routine oncology practice, as they do not require tumor tissue sequencing and can be applied repeatedly without dependence on specimen availability [[30], [31], [32], [33], [34]]. Northstar Response is a tissue-free, methylation-based ctDNA assay that quantifies a tumor methylation score (TMS) across hundreds of hypermethylated loci with paired buffy coat subtraction to minimize non-tumor signal [32,[34], [35], [36]]. Its molecular counting design provides high quantitative precision, enabling detection of statistically significant serial directional change, a critical property necessary for deriving a within-patient increase rule. Prior studies have already demonstrated clinical activity of Northstar Response in immunotherapy-treated non-small cell lung cancer [32,34]. Beyond immune checkpoint blockade, serial methylation-based ctDNA monitoring is increasingly being explored in real-world NSCLC patients receiving molecularly targeted therapy, where dynamic methylation profiling has been applied to longitudinally track tumor burden over the course of treatment [37]. These efforts support the broader relevance of serial methylation dynamics as a tumor-agnostic monitoring strategy across treatment classes.

To evaluate the heterogeneity of treatment response during immune checkpoint blockade and to determine whether serial ctDNA measurements can provide a practical indicator of emerging treatment failure, we designed a longitudinal study of patients with advanced solid tumors receiving ICI monotherapy or chemo-immunotherapy. In two independent cohorts, using dense serial blood sampling collected throughout treatment, we assessed tumor methylation based ctDNA dynamics to characterize the timing and direction of molecular change and to define a within-patient rule to better identify molecular progression. This approach was intended to capture early biological signals of resistance, complement radiographic assessment, and establish a robust framework for continuous molecular monitoring during immunotherapy.

Methods

Patient cohorts, study design and inclusion criteria

The AHN Cohort comprised adult patients with advanced cancer receiving ICI with or without chemotherapy at Allegheny Health Network Cancer Institute (AHNCI). Patients were prospectively enrolled under an IRB-approved protocol (2020-258: Oncology Sample Biobank and Data Repository), with blood collections obtained pre-treatment and at every treatment cycle where feasible in routine clinical practice. Ten patients were excluded for absence of an evaluable on-treatment blood draw or maintenance therapy status, yielding an evaluable cohort of n = 72. Sample collection and processing for the AHN cohort have been described previously [38,39].

The NORTH Cohort (n = 70) was established by prospectively enrolling advanced cancer patients treated with immunotherapy with or without chemotherapy across solid tumor types from a large hospital network and 12 community oncology sites in the USA under a single protocol (WCG IRB No. 20231272). In this observational cohort, blood collections were taken at every cycle until the first standard of care imaging event, then every 6 weeks thereafter for up to one year or until progression or death. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, recognized ethical guidelines, and Institutional Review Boards.

Blood collection and ctDNA processing

Whole blood was collected in Streck Cell-Free DNA BCT tubes. Blood samples were either shipped directly to BillionToOne or processed to plasma per manufacturer recommendations and stored at −80C prior to shipment.

Samples were processed using the Northstar Response assay, a tumor-naive, methylation-based laboratory-developed test (CAP/CLIA approved) that precisely quantifies ctDNA in order to provide longitudinal assessments of ctDNA increase or decrease. The assay interrogates over 500 differentially methylated regions that are specifically hypermethylated in tumors and sequences both plasma and buffy coat to further remove non-tumor sources of DNA methylation. Results are reported as a Tumor Methylation Score and sequential increase/decrease calls. Analytical results have been reported previously across 12 different solid tumor types, in which the assay demonstrated <10% coefficient of variation at 1% tumor fraction and reliable ability to distinguish 0.25% absolute changes in contrived tumor fraction (AUC >0.94) across solid tumor types [32]. Clinicians treating the patients were blinded to all ctDNA results. A subset of subjects was orthogonally evaluated for assay performance on a 2000-locus assay revision using the same samples as available (Fig. S1).

Molecular progression definition

The primary analytical endpoint was molecular progressive disease (mPD), defined prospectively as a clinically reportable increase in TMS between consecutive timepoints. The bioinformatics methods for determining a TMS increase have been described previously [32]. Briefly, a clinically reportable increase (or decrease) in TMS is returned when the change in TMS exceeds a predefined statistical likelihood ratio based on the absolute quantification of methylated DNA molecules in the sample; otherwise, a no change call is issued on the clinical report. For the C3 landmark analysis, patients were classified as mPD if a TMS increase was observed at either cycle 2 day 1 (C2D1) or cycle 3 day 1 (C3D1) compared to previous timepoints. Similarly, for the longitudinal surveillance analysis, mPD was set at the first timepoint for which a ctDNA “increase” was reported. Radiographic assessment

Blinded independent centralized review for RECISTv1.1 was performed for a subset of patients for whom DICOM images were available (MERIT CRO, Madison WI). Radiographic scans were collected retrospectively. An independent radiologist reviewed the patient's medical chart for RECIST feasibility, such as the availability of appropriate pretreatment scans. RECISTv1.1 reads were performed by a pool of three board-certified radiologists. To ensure consistency for each patient, all scans pertaining to an individual case were reviewed by a single radiologist from this pool.

For C3 landmark assessments, RECIST assessments were taken as the most recent radiographic result at 120 days after treatment start, which corresponds to a typical restaging scan performed as part of standard of care. Six month best overall response (BOR) was assessed from patients with at least one radiography report or RECIST assessment in the first 6 months of treatment.

As a secondary analysis, radiographic objective response assessments were derived from clinical radiology medical notes and curated by SMHH and MA, who were blinded to the ctDNA results. Radiographic assessments based on curated radiographic reports were considered in the same fashion as RECIST with regard to six month BOR and 120 days initial radiographic restaging assessment.

Survival analysis

Overall survival (OS) was set as death or last contact. Progression-free survival (PFS) was derived using clinically documented progression, radiographically confirmed progression, cancer-related death, or censoring at last contact. Data were censored at 12 months (PFS) and 24 months (OS) to maintain comparability across both cohorts. Survival analyses, i.e. Kaplan-Meier and Cox proportional hazard calculations, were performed against the C3 landmark as the time origin.

Covariate selection for multivariable Cox models was based on clinical a priori reasoning. To identify possible clinical confounders, univariate analyses for each variable were performed in each cohort. Of the variables considered, only smoking history was identified as a potential confounder in the NORTH cohort (HR = 3.6 95% CI 0.83-15, p = 0.09) with further univariate significance when taken across both cohorts (HR = 2.8 95% CI 1.2-6.7, p = 0.02). In all analyses utilizing either the NORTH cohort or combined cohorts, Cox PH ratios were adjusted for smoking status. In combined cohort analyses, Cox models were also stratified by cohort (AHN vs. NORTH).

Lead time analysis

Lead-time was calculated as the interval in days between the date of first longitudinal mPD and the date of progression (radiographically-confirmed or clinical). Only patients with both events were included. To ensure temporal alignment, a ctDNA assessment was required within ±90 days of the conventional progression date. Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were used for paired comparisons.

Data availability

Raw sequencing data for this study were generated at BillionToOne and are not publicly available due to their proprietary nature and patient privacy concerns. De-identified patient-level clinical and methylation data required to recapitulate study results are available in Supplementary Table S1. Additional details may be available through the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Patients with baseline TMS above the cohort median demonstrated significantly worse outcomes compared to those below the median for both progression-free survival (HR 2.2, 95% CI 1.3–3.8, p = 0.003) and overall survival (HR 1.9, 95% CI 1.1–3.4, p = 0.019) (Supplementary Figure S2). These data confirm that baseline ctDNA level carries independent prognostic value. However, given the substantial heterogeneity in ctDNA shedding across tumor types (Supplementary Fig. S2, Table S2), baseline TMS alone does not constitute a broadly applicable monitoring rule. These observations motivated the primary analytical focus on within-patient longitudinal TMS dynamics in subsequent analyses.

Tumor methylation score dynamics at each cycle of therapy better predicts immune checkpoint inhibitor benefit than a single on-treatment timepoint by the C3D1 landmark assessment

Although it is well established that ctDNA dynamics are associated with outcomes during immune checkpoint blockade across diverse tumor types [[13], [14], [15], [16], [17], [18], [19], [20],22], numerous response thresholds have been proposed and no single operational rule has achieved broad adoption. We first sought to reproduce established ctDNA response definitions using TMS in the AHN Cohort.

Stratification by commonly referenced landmark thresholds at cycle 3, day 1 (C3D1), including any on-treatment decrease [13], >50% decrease [14,21], or >90% decrease [23] from baseline, was performed in 72 AHN cohort patients. Across all three thresholds, patients who achieved a decrease demonstrated comparable and favorable outcomes regardless of the magnitude of that decrease (Fig. 2a and b, dashed lines). Among patients who did not meet each molecular response threshold, a gradient in survival was observed as threshold stringency increased (Fig. 2a and b, solid lines). All three fixed thresholds reached statistical significance for PFS stratification (Any: HR 2.8, 95% CI 1.3–5.8, p = 0.006, C-index 0.63; 50%: HR 2.9, 95% CI 1.4–6.2, p = 0.005, C-index 0.64; 90%: HR 2.4, 95% CI 1.1–5.5, p = 0.032, C-index 0.61). For OS, the any-decrease and 50% thresholds reached significance (Any: HR 3.0, 95% CI 1.4–6.6, p = 0.006, C-index 0.65; 50%: HR 2.5, 95% CI 1.1–5.6, p = 0.024, C-index 0.63), while the 90% threshold did not (HR 1.9, 95% CI 0.8–4.3, p = 0.148, C-index 0.59). These findings demonstrate that as threshold stringency increases, the non-responder group becomes progressively contaminated with patients who have favorable outcomes. This dilution of the high-risk signal undermines the ability of fixed percentage thresholds to reliably identify patients at greatest risk of ICI failure.

To address these limitations, we examined individual patient TMS trajectories and observed three clinically distinct patterns (1) durable responders with sustained TMS decrease maintained over 12+ months (Fig. 2e); (2) molecular progressors with clear and sustained TMS increases from the earliest on-treatment timepoint (Fig. 2f); and (3) a rebound pattern in which an initial TMS decrease at C2D1 was followed by rebound by C3D1 (Fig. 2g), a kinetic signature that a single baseline-to-landmark comparison would misclassify as a favorable response. Dense serial sampling renders this rebound pattern visible and appropriately reclassifies such patients as mPD. This kinetic pattern was not exclusive to any single treatment class, occurring in patients receiving both ICI monotherapy and chemo-immunotherapy combinations, and was observed in 39 of 142 (27%) patients across both cohorts, underscoring the importance of frequent serial sampling for capturing the full diversity of on-treatment ctDNA dynamics regardless of treatment regimen.

mPD classifier performance in AHN cohort

We therefore defined molecular progressive disease (mPD) as a TMS increase between timepoints, a feature of the Northstar Response assay in which molecular counting technology is used to distinguish true changes in ctDNA from statistical sampling noise inherent to scarce ctDNA material [32]. Critically, unlike the fixed threshold comparators which rely on a single C3D1 measurement relative to baseline, the mPD classifier integrates information from both the C2D1 and C3D1 timepoints, with patients classified as mPD if a TMS increase was observed at either timepoint compared to the preceding timepoint. This integration of serial dynamics from two early on-treatment timepoints is a key distinguishing feature of the approach relative to fixed single-timepoint thresholds for early landmark assessment.

When applied to the AHN Cohort, the mPD classifier demonstrated substantially stronger predictive performance than any single-timepoint fixed threshold comparator across both endpoints. In univariate analysis, mPD was significantly associated with worse PFS (HR 7.0, 95% CI 3.3–14.9, p = 4.4 × 10−7, C-index 0.69) and OS (HR 6.2, 95% CI 2.8–13.8, p = 7.8 × 10−6, C-index 0.70), with the mPD C-index of 0.69 for PFS exceeding all fixed threshold comparators (range 0.61–0.64) (Fig. 2a and b red lines). Full at-risk tables, multivariate Cox proportional hazard analyses, and combined cohort analyses are provided in Supplemental Figure S3.

Validation in the NORTH cohort and subgroup analyses

The mPD classifier significantly stratified both PFS (HR 5.9, 95% CI 2.7–13.0, p = 9.8 × 10−6, C-index 0.69) and OS (HR 3.2, 95% CI 1.4–7.2, p = 0.004, C-index 0.63) in the independent NORTH cohort without modification (Fig. 2c and d). To assess the generalizability of the mPD classifier across clinically relevant subgroups, we also examined the association between mPD and survival outcomes stratified by treatment class and tumor type in the combined cohort. In patients receiving ICI monotherapy, mPD was significantly associated with PFS (HR 13.3, 95% CI 4.2–42.4) and OS (HR 4.4, 95% CI 1.4–13.7) (Supplemental Fig S4).Consistent findings were also observed in chemo-immunotherapy patients (PFS HR 11.2, 95% CI 4.3–29.1, p<0.001; OS HR 5.6, 95% CI 2.2–14.3, p<0.001)(Supplemental Fig. S4). In tumor type-specific analyses, mPD demonstrated significant predictive stratification in patients with lung cancer (Supplementary Fig. S5a and b; PFS HR 7.7, 95% CI 2.4–24.3, p < 0.001 and OS HR 7.4, 95% CI 2–27.1, p = 0.002) and in patients with melanoma (Supplementary Fig. S5c and d; PFS firth-corrected HR 34, p < 0.01 and OS HR 11, 95% CI 1–122.9, p = 0.051), and gastrointestinal tumors (Supplemental Fig. S5e and f; PFS HR 16.7, 95% CI 3.3-84.8 p = 6.9x10−4and OS HR 3.9, 95% CI 1.1-14.6, p = 0.041) with effect sizes directionally consistent with those observed in the overall cohort.

Molecular progression provides independent and incremental predictive information beyond radiographic assessment

Having established and independently confirmed the predictive utility of the mPD classifier, we next asked whether ctDNA-based mPD assessment could provide clinically meaningful information beyond or in advance of conventional radiographic evaluation. A common clinical challenge arises when a patient on immunotherapy is assessed as having stable disease, as it is often unclear whether the treatment is working or whether early progression is occurring below the threshold of radiographic detection. Pseudoprogression presents a related interpretive challenge, whereby initial radiographic assessments may show apparent tumor enlargement as a consequence of effective immune infiltration rather than true cancer progression. In a multivariable model incorporating ctDNA and radiographic assessment simultaneously, mPD demonstrated strong independent significance (HR 5.3, 95% CI 2.2–12.8, p = 1.9 × 10−4) while RECIST-defined radiographic progression did not retain independent significance (HR 1.2, 95% CI 0.5–2.9, p = 0.606), with a combined C-index of 0.71 (Fig. 3a). This pattern demonstrates that the mPD signal captures the predictive information shared between the two modalities and contributes independent information beyond radiographic assessment alone, whereas radiographic progression does not provide significant incremental value once mPD status is accounted for.

Among the 25 patients whose six-month best overall BICR RECIST response was stable disease, a population in which imaging cannot reliably distinguish treatment benefit from early treatment failure, mPD remained significantly associated with OS (HR 8.4, 95% CI 1.4–48.7, p = 0.018, C-index 0.82) (Fig. 3b). The wide confidence interval reflects the limited size of this subgroup (n = 6 mPD patients) and this finding should be considered hypothesis-generating pending replication in larger cohorts.

To further characterize the complementary relationship between mPD and radiographic assessment, we performed a four-arm analysis stratifying patients simultaneously by RECISTv1.1 defined radiographic response and landmark mPD status. Using non-PD (defined by RECIST) combined with non-mPD (defined molecularly) as the reference group, this analysis revealed four clinically distinct outcome groups (Fig. 3c). Molecular progression without radiographic progression (non-PD, mPD) carried HR 3.7 (95% CI 1.1–12.5, p = 0.03). Radiographic progression without mPD (PD, non-mPD) did not significantly worsen outcomes (HR 0.5, 95% CI 0.1–2.4, p = 0.41). Concordant molecular and radiographic progression (PD, mPD) demonstrated the worst outcomes (HR 13.8, 95% CI 3.9–48.8, p = 4.4 × 10−5).

Overall, these analyses establish that mPD and radiographic assessment provide complementary rather than redundant information, and that their integration yields the highest discrimination (C-index 0.76) of any single or combined approach evaluated. These C3 landmark findings were further corroborated in an expanded cohort of 88 patients with clinically annotated radiographic assessment available, but for whom RECISTv1.1 criteria could not be met (Fig. S6). mPD similarly retained independent significance in multivariable analysis (HR 3.5, 95% CI 1.4–8.4, p = 0.006; radiographic PD HR 1.6, 95% CI 0.6–4.5, p = 0.338; combined C-index 0.71) and achieved an HR of 5.3 (95% CI 1.4–20.1, p = 0.014) in the 33-patient stable disease subgroup. These data confirm that the predictive signal of mPD within radiographically stable patients is not an artifact of the radiographic annotation method employed.

Longitudinal ctDNA monitoring identifies molecular progression with a clinically meaningful lead-time advantage over conventional progression endpoints

Having established that landmark mPD assessment provides independent predictive information beyond radiographic assessment, we next examined whether continuous longitudinal TMS monitoring beyond the C3D1 landmark timepoint could identify emerging treatment failure in advance of conventional clinical and radiographic progression detection. This is clinically relevant as some patients may demonstrate initial response or stability but subsequently exhibit rising TMS during treatment, representing the early molecular signature of acquired resistance that may precede overt radiographic or clinical deterioration by a clinically actionable interval.

To evaluate the predictive utility of longitudinal mPD, we applied the mPD classifier to all available serial TMS measurements at any timepoint (before and after C3 landmark) among the 142 patients in the combined cohort. Longitudinal mPD (any increase compared to prior timepoint) was significantly associated with worse PFS (HR 4.1, 95% CI 2.4–7.0, p = 2.0 × 10−7, C-index 0.69) and OS in univariate analysis (HR 3.3, 95% CI 1.9–5.8, p = 2.4 × 10−5, C-index 0.65) (Fig. 4a and b), while radiographic progression associated to a slightly lesser extent (HR 2.7, 95% CI 1.4–5.3, p = 0.004, C-index 0.66). In multivariable analysis combining both longitudinal mPD and radiographic progression, the pattern established at the landmark timepoint in Fig. 3a was maintained: mPD retained independent significance (HR 3.2, 95% CI 1.6–6.4, p = 0.001) while radiographic progression did not (HR 1.9, 95% CI 0.9–3.8, p = 0.082), with a combined model C-index of 0.72. The consistency of this finding across both the early landmark and the extended longitudinal monitoring analyses reinforces that the mPD signal captures independent and clinically meaningful predictive information across the full treatment course, not only at the early C3D1 assessment window. We next applied the same 4-arm analysis among RECIST evaluable patients using the longitudinal mPD classifier and observed that molecular progression without radiographic progression (non-PD, mPD) carried HR 6.5 (95% CI 2.1–19.7, p = 9.2x10−4). Radiographic progression without mPD (PD, non-mPD) did not significantly worsen outcomes (HR 1.4, 95% CI 0.3–5.3, p = 0.65). Concordant molecular and radiographic progression (PD, mPD) demonstrated the worst outcomes (HR 19.7, 95% CI 4.3–89.6, p = 1.1 × 10−4). Discussion

Early molecular response assessment during immune checkpoint blockade has been an active area of investigation, with prior studies demonstrating that on-treatment ctDNA dynamics are associated with survival outcomes across multiple solid tumor types [[13], [14], [15], [16], [17], [18], [19], [20],22]. The principal contribution of the present study is the evaluation of a clinically pragmatic serial monitoring strategy utilizing a tissue-free, quantitatively precise methylation-based assay applied at frequent intervals, with evaluation of a within-patient longitudinal TMS increase rule (mPD), as a pan-cancer monitoring strategy during immune checkpoint blockade. This approach addresses an important unmet need in converting a biologically informative ctDNA signal into an interpretable decision rule applicable across heterogeneous tumor types, treatment regimens, and care settings. Critically, the mPD classifier serves a dual clinical role. At the early C3D1 landmark, mPD provides a rapid and actionable assessment of initial treatment efficacy, identifying patients unlikely to derive durable benefit from immune checkpoint blockade within the first two to three cycles of therapy. Beyond the landmark timepoint, the same within-patient increase rule applied to serial longitudinal sampling enables continuous monitoring for emerging treatment failure throughout the treatment course, preceding conventional radiographic and clinical progression endpoints by a clinically meaningful interval. This dual utility, spanning early efficacy prediction and longitudinal progression surveillance within a single unified molecular rule, distinguishes the present approach from prior studies that have focused predominantly on a single assessment window and represents a meaningful step toward integrating ctDNA monitoring into routine oncology practice.

A recurring challenge in the field is the substantial variability in published molecular response definitions, which have included ctDNA clearance, any measurable decrease, 50% or 90% decline thresholds, and composite molecular response rules derived from multiple timepoints [14,22,23,40]. This heterogeneity likely reflects genuine differences in patient populations, treatment regimens, ctDNA sampling frequency, and assay platform. As a result, molecular response algorithms dependent on tumor lineage, baseline shedding level, treatment class, or platform-specific calibration may perform well in selected contexts but remain difficult to implement into routine clinical practice.

The mPD rule anchors progression classification to within-patient directional TMS increase rather than a population-level decline threshold which is conceptually analogous to RECIST-based imaging progression (defined from a nadir) and PSA-based biochemical failure monitoring (defined by serial increase independent of absolute level) [6,28,29]. This design intention enables broadly applicable classification across tumor types and treatment classes without disease-specific recalibration, and the present findings across 12 tumor types and two distinct treatment regimens provide early support for this premise.

Dense serial sampling at every treatment cycle was necessary to capture the full diversity of early ctDNA dynamics. A subset of patients exhibited a C2D1 decrease followed by molecular rebound to or above baseline by C3D1, a kinetic signature that a single baseline-to-landmark comparison would misclassify as favorable. Integration of both C2D1 and C3D1 enabled appropriate reclassification of these patients as mPD. In an independent cohort, the classifier confirmed directionally consistent effect sizes despite differences in cohort composition and treatment distribution, providing further evidence for the generalizability of the mPD classifier. These data are in alignment with a recent cohort of patients with advanced solid tumors receiving chemotherapy, where any on-treatment increase in methylation-based ctDNA tumor fraction was similarly associated with inferior clinical outcomes, suggesting that the directional molecular signal carries predictive weight independent of the degree of change [41,42].

The clinical value of serial molecular monitoring was particularly evident in patients with radiographically stable disease, where conventional imaging cannot distinguish genuine response from early treatment failure below the threshold of anatomic detection. Within this subgroup, mPD remained significantly associated with worse overall survival, identifying outcome heterogeneity that imaging alone could not resolve. These data suggest that a meaningful proportion of patients classified as stable by imaging are experiencing molecular progression, with potential implications for earlier clinical intervention.

The four-arm combination analysis further supports an integrating ctDNA with radiography in the clinic. Molecular progression in the absence of radiographic progression identified a clinically high-risk group that imaging alone would have classified as non-progressors, while radiographic progression without a concordant molecular signal did not significantly worsen outcomes relative to the reference group. This discordance likely reflects biological heterogeneity in the relationship between anatomic and molecular disease evolution, and underscores the complementary rather than redundant nature of these two assessment modalities [43]. Combined assessment outperformed either modality alone, capturing distinct dimensions of treatment response. A similar analysis had previously demonstrated the improved predictive power of combining ctDNA and radiographic response at the C3 landmark in heavily pretreated patients (median 2 previous lines of therapy) undergoing PD-1 monotherapy [15]. Importantly, our findings now extend to a real-world population of primarily first-line chemo-immunotherapy combinations now representing standard of care across multiple tumor types [44,45].

The lead-time advantage of mPD over conventional progression detection is among the most clinically actionable findings of this study. In paired analyses, mPD preceded progression by a median of 62 days, representing a potentially actionable window for intensified surveillance, advancement of imaging, consideration of therapeutic modification, or preservation of alternative treatment lines while performance status remains favorable.

Effective serial monitoring requires both analytical precision and operational feasibility. Tissue-informed assays that track a limited number of patient-specific variants may require large changes before directional conclusions can be drawn with confidence, constraining the sensitivity with which serial dynamics can be interpreted. By contrast, interrogating hundreds of methylated loci through a molecular counting framework, the Northstar Response assay enables detection of statistically significant directional change even at low tumor fraction, a property critical for identifying early molecular rebound or subtle progression onset. The tissue-free design further supports repeated deployment across the treatment course without dependence on tumor tissue availability. Tumor-informed approaches require sequencing of adequate, representative tissue that accurately reflects current disease biology, a requirement frequently unmet in advanced metastatic setting where tissue may be unavailable, exhausted, or biologically outdated relative to dominant circulating clones [15,46,47]. Clonal architecture continues to evolve under therapeutic selection pressure, and variant-based assays anchored to archival tissue may fail to capture the molecular landscape of active disease [[48], [49], [50], [51], [52]]. A methylation-based tissue-free approach avoids these constraints, enabling serial assessment that reflects evolving tumor biology at the timepoints when monitoring decisions are most consequential [[30], [31], [32],34].

While our findings largely validate the utility of TMS in predicting imaging findings in a concordant manner, it must be acknowledged that isolated cases of divergence were observed. Indeed, discrepancies between TMS and imaging findings can be due to a myriad of tumor- and patient-related variables stemming from the fact that circulating tumor DNA reflects molecular and cellular activity [53], while imaging measures physical macroscopic tumor burden. The medical history of patients with discrepant TMS and imaging findings were generally explained by pseudoprogression, gaps in therapy, ctDNA shed rates of different anatomical sites, and confounding factors of localized therapy (Supplementary note 1). For example, a treatment break can lead to a transient increase in ctDNA. Tumors with anatomic barriers, such as brain metastases, often release little DNA into circulation due to the blood-brain barrier. Radiation therapy can lead to a transient increase followed by precipitous decline in ctDNA. Finally, immunotherapy can lead to inflammation that is misinterpreted as progression on imaging [8,12]. Interpreting ctDNA dynamics in clinical settings should account for the interacting effects of individual clinical factors such as therapy timing, localized therapy, and the anatomic sites and sizes of lesions. Consideration of the relative strengths and limitations of complementary modalities can be a powerful tool for physicians to evaluate the effectiveness of therapy.

This study has several notable strengths. Enrollment of two independent prospective cohorts allowed for validation of the mPD classifier without modification, strengthening confidence in its generalizability. Blinded independent centralized RECIST review by board-certified radiologists provides a rigorous radiographic comparator for evaluating the independent predictive contribution of mPD. The combined cohort is broadly representative of current immunotherapy practice, encompassing both ICI monotherapy and chemo-immunotherapy combinations predominantly in the first-line setting across 12 tumor types. Key limitations include limited sample sizes within individual tumor type subgroups due to the heterogeneity of the enrolled population, variability in scan timing as imaging was performed per standard of care rather than a protocol-mandated cadence, and the susceptibility of longitudinal survival analyses to time-dependent bias as patient stratification by ctDNA and radiography is assigned after the start of the survival analysis.

Collectively, these findings support the clinical utility of a tissue-free, methylation-based ctDNA monitoring strategy for early detection of molecular progressive disease during immune checkpoint blockade. Across two independent cohorts spanning 12 tumor types and both ICI monotherapy and chemo-immunotherapy, the mPD classifier outperformed fixed threshold comparators, retained independent predictive significance beyond radiographic assessment, identified high-risk patients within the radiographically stable disease population, and preceded conventional progression endpoints by a median of 62 days. Integration of mPD with radiographic assessment provides the greatest discriminatory power and represents the most complete approach to non-invasive treatment response monitoring currently available. Based on these data, we propose a practical ctDNA monitoring protocol of assessment at every treatment cycle through C3D1 followed by every six weeks thereafter, which captures the full diversity of early on-treatment molecular dynamics while providing a clinically meaningful lead-time window over conventional progression detection. Future work should examine whether disease-specific adaptations to the monitoring framework are warranted, as most tumor types have established imaging modifications within the broader RECIST paradigm that could be integrated with molecular progression assessment to improve subgroup-level precision. Extension of the mPD classifier to other systemic therapy classes, including cytotoxic chemotherapy, tyrosine kinase inhibitors, and antibody-drug conjugates, represents a further priority for establishing the generalizability of frequent molecular monitoring across the broader landscape of advanced cancer treatment.

Statement for studies in humans

Patient cohorts, study design and inclusion criteria

The AHN Cohort comprised adult patients with advanced cancer receiving ICI with or without chemotherapy at Allegheny Health Network Cancer Institute (AHNCI). Patients were prospectively enrolled under an IRB-approved protocol (2020-258: Oncology Sample Biobank and Data Repository), with blood collections obtained pre-treatment and at every treatment cycle where feasible in routine clinical practice. Ten patients were excluded for absence of an evaluable on-treatment blood draw or maintenance therapy status, yielding an evaluable cohort of n = 72. Sample collection and processing for the AHN cohort have been described previously [38 39].

The NORTH Cohort (n = 70) was established by prospectively enrolling advanced cancer patients treated with immunotherapy with or without chemotherapy across solid tumor types from a large hospital network and 12 community oncology sites in the USA under a single protocol (WCG IRB No. 20231272). In this observational cohort, blood collections were taken at every cycle until the first standard of care imaging event, then every 6 weeks thereafter for up to one year or until progression or death. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, recognized ethical guidelines, and Institutional Review Boards.
