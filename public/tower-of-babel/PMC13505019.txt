Effect of iTBS on suicidal ideation and emotion regulation in major depressive disorder: a randomized clinical trial
Authors: Li Pu, Yan Wang, Zhengzhi Deng, Jiang Wu, Zhihong Chen, Rui Fu, Dezhong Yao, Shu Yu, Guojian Yan, Hongmei Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMajor depressive disorder (MDD) with suicidal ideation (SI) is a psychiatric emergency characterized by high risk and a critical need for timely adjunctive intervention. Traditional antidepressants often exhibit a significant therapeutic lag. This study evaluated the efficacy of a 10-day intermittent theta burst stimulation (iTBS) protocol as an adjunctive neuromodulation strategy to alleviate depressive symptoms and suicidal ideation by restoring emotion regulation capacities.MethodsIn this prospectively registered, randomized, double-blind, sham-controlled clinical trial using a pre-specified 2:1 allocation ratio, 78 patients with MDD and SI were assigned to active iTBS plus stable medication management (n = 52) or matched sham stimulation plus stable medication management (n = 26) for 10 consecutive treatment days. The primary outcome was the Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD-17) score. Secondary outcomes included the Beck Scale for Suicide Ideation (BSSI), Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale (HAMA), and Emotion Regulation Questionnaire (ERQ).ResultsThe iTBS group demonstrated a greater reduction in HAMD-17 scores than the sham group, with a significant Group x Time interaction (F(1, 76) = 117.055, p < .001, partial eta-squared = 0.606). For suicidal ideation, the active iTBS group also showed a larger reduction in BSSI scores (post-treatment between-group comparison, p = .003). iTBS treatment significantly enhanced cognitive reappraisal (F(1, 76) = 51.214, p < .001, partial eta-squared = 0.403), and improvement in cognitive reappraisal was negatively correlated with reduction in depressive severity (r = − .455, p = .001). Improvements in anxiety and sleep quality were also greater after active iTBS, whereas digit-span performance remained comparable between groups.ConclusionA 10-day protocol of active iTBS added to stable medication management was associated with greater baseline-to-post-treatment reductions in depressive symptoms and suicidal ideation, together with improvement in adaptive emotion regulation. Because assessments were performed only at baseline and after treatment, these findings should be interpreted as short-term post-treatment effects rather than evidence of a measured time-to-response trajectory.Trial RegistrationClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT06417437; registered before participant recruitment (registration date: 23 April 2024).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-026-08283-8.

Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a leading cause of disability and premature mortality worldwide, characterized by high prevalence, frequent recurrence, and substantial socioeconomic burden [1]. Among its clinical manifestations, suicidal ideation (SI) represents one of the most severe and clinically consequential features, serving as a robust predictor of suicidal behavior and completed suicide [2]. Despite the widespread use of pharmacotherapy as the first-line treatment, approximately one-third of patients exhibit treatment-resistant depression [3]. Moreover, conventional antidepressants typically require several weeks to achieve therapeutic effects, creating a critical period during which patients with acute suicidal risk remain insufficiently protected [4]. This temporal gap highlights an urgent need for adjunctive interventions that can be deployed promptly alongside pharmacological treatment to improve symptom control, while recognizing that treatment response timing requires serial assessment.

From a neurobiological perspective, MDD—particularly when accompanied by SI—is increasingly conceptualized as a disorder of large-scale brain network dysregulation [5]. Converging evidence implicates dysfunction within the prefrontal-limbic circuitry, characterized by hypoactivity of the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (dlPFC) and impaired top-down regulatory control over limbic regions such as the amygdala [6]. This imbalance is thought to underlie deficits in executive control and maladaptive emotional processing, thereby contributing to persistent negative cognitive bias and heightened vulnerability to suicidal ideation [7, 8]. In particular, impaired emotion regulation—especially reduced capacity for adaptive strategies such as cognitive reappraisal—has been identified as a key psychological mechanism linking prefrontal dysfunction to suicidal risk [9, 10].

In this context, neuromodulation techniques have emerged as promising adjunctive strategies to enhance treatment response. Transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS), a non-invasive brain stimulation modality, modulates cortical excitability by inducing electromagnetic fields over targeted brain regions and has demonstrated efficacy across a range of psychiatric disorders [11]. Increasingly, TMS is being integrated with pharmacotherapy as part of a combined treatment approach, aiming to complement antidepressant treatment during the early clinical window [12]. Among TMS protocols, intermittent theta burst stimulation (iTBS) has gained particular attention due to its unique neurophysiological and practical advantages. By mimicking endogenous hippocampal theta rhythms and inducing long-term potentiation (LTP)-like plasticity, iTBS can efficiently enhance cortical excitability [13]. Notably, it achieves comparable or superior therapeutic effects to conventional TMS while dramatically reducing stimulation time (from approximately 30 min to 3 min), making it especially suitable for time-efficient clinical protocols [14]. Mechanistically, iTBS is well positioned to restore dlPFC function and rebalance prefrontal-limbic interactions, thereby potentially improving both cognitive control and emotional regulation [15].

Substantial clinical evidence supports the efficacy of iTBS in treating depressive symptoms, with studies consistently demonstrating its ability to enhance dlPFC activity and strengthen functional connectivity within prefrontal networks [14]. However, despite these advances, its application in the high-risk subgroup of MDD patients with co-occurring SI remains insufficiently investigated. In particular, there is a lack of rigorously designed randomized controlled trials examining whether iTBS can reduce suicidal ideation across a defined short treatment course and through which psychological mechanisms these effects may occur. Given its capacity to modulate prefrontal function, it is plausible that iTBS may enhance executive control and facilitate adaptive emotion regulation processes, such as cognitive reappraisal, thereby attenuating the emergence and persistence of suicidal ideation.

Materials and methods

Study subjects

The 78 subjects with MDD were recruited from July 2024 to October 2025 in Chengdu, China. The trial was prospectively registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06417437; 23 April 2024) before the first participant was enrolled. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Chengdu Fourth People’s Hospital and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and CONSORT principles for randomized trials. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before any study procedure.

The eligibility criteria for patients with depressive disorders were defined as follows [16]: (1) fulfillment of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5) criteria for MDD; (2) Han Chinese ethnicity and age of 18 years or older; (3) attainment of at least a junior high school education; (4) right-handedness; (5) a 17-item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD-17) score of 17 or higher; (6) current treatment with a stable selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor (SSRI) regimen for at least 3 months before enrollment, with no dose adjustment during the neuromodulation intervention period; (7) suicidal ideation during the previous week, operationally defined as endorsing either ‘weak’ or ‘moderate to strong’ on item 4a or item 5a of the Beck Scale for Suicide Ideation (BSSI); and (8) provision of written informed consent with willingness to comply with study procedures. None of the enrolled participants fulfilled criteria for treatment-resistant depression. No participant was medication-free or drug-naive at randomization; current drug-naive/no-SSRI status is reported by group in Supplementary Table S1.

Concomitant treatment was controlled clinically. All participants continued the same SSRI agent and dose throughout the trial, and initiation or adjustment of antidepressants, antipsychotics, mood stabilizers, benzodiazepines, hypnotics, structured psychotherapy, electroconvulsive therapy, or other neuromodulation was not permitted during the 10-day intervention. Supplementary Table S1 summarizes, by treatment group, stable SSRI exposure, current drug-naive/no-SSRI status, specific SSRI agents, daily dose ranges, treatment duration prior to enrollment, dose stability, and confirmation that no medication changes occurred during the intervention.

The exclusion criteria for all individuals were as follows [16, 17]: (1) a current or previous history of neurological disorders, severe traumatic brain injury, or a personal/familial history of epilepsy; (2) serious dysfunction of major organs, including the heart, liver, or kidneys; (3) severe systemic medical illnesses; (4) current or prior substance dependence or abuse, including alcohol or illicit drugs; (5) comorbid psychiatric diagnoses other than depressive disorders; (6) pregnancy or breastfeeding; (7) receipt of electroconvulsive therapy, transcranial magnetic stimulation, or other neuromodulatory/physical therapies within the previous 6 months; (8) implantation of autonomic nervous system stimulators; (9) presence of implanted metallic or electronic devices, such as cardiac pacemakers, defibrillators, vascular stents, orthopedic fixation devices, or ventriculoperitoneal shunts; and (10) marked visual or hearing impairment that could interfere with completion of neuropsychological evaluations or clinical rating scales.

Clinical and Psychological Evaluation

Baseline demographic characteristics were obtained prior to treatment initiation, while clinical and psychological evaluations were carried out both before and after the 2-week intervention period. Psychological assessments were independently completed by two senior clinical psychologists with more than 10 years of professional experience, both of whom were blinded to participants’ diagnostic information. The assessment outcomes were subsequently verified by an experienced psychiatrist.

The evaluation battery included Chinese-language or Chinese-adapted versions of the following instruments: the 17-item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD-17), 14-item Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale (HAMA), Zung Self-Rating Depression Scale (SDS), Zung Self-Rating Anxiety Scale (SAS), Beck Scale for Suicide Ideation (BSSI), 14-item Snaith-Hamilton Pleasure Scale (SHAPS), 10-item Emotion Regulation Questionnaire (ERQ), Social Support Rating Scale (SSRS), Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), and Digit Span Test (DST) [19–27]. Higher HAMD-17, HAMA, SDS, SAS, BSSI, SHAPS, and PSQI scores indicate greater symptom burden, whereas higher cognitive reappraisal scores indicate more frequent use of an adaptive emotion-regulation strategy.

Randomization and blinding

Eligible participants were allocated to either the iTBS intervention group (n = 52) or the sham stimulation group (n = 26) at a pre-specified 2:1 ratio according to a computer-generated random assignment schedule. This allocation was chosen to increase precision for estimating the clinical and tolerability profile of active iTBS in this high-risk suicidal population while retaining a concurrent sham-controlled comparator and reducing the number of participants exposed only to sham stimulation. The randomisation sequence was generated by an investigator not involved in treatment implementation or outcome evaluation, and group assignments were concealed using consecutively numbered, sealed opaque envelopes.

The study was designed as a randomized, double-blind, sham-controlled trial. Participants and clinical evaluators were blinded to group allocation. Outcome assessors were independent of the stimulation team and were instructed to avoid discussions of coil type, scalp sensations, or treatment assignment with participants and treating technicians. Data analysts received de-identified group codes until the primary analyses had been completed.

Blinding was reinforced by using identical treatment-room procedures, positioning, session duration, and auditory cues in both groups. Participants were informed that two forms of TMS were being compared but were not told which condition represented active stimulation.

TMS intervention protocol

A YRD CCY-1 transcranial magnetic stimulation system (Wuhan Yiruide Medical Equipment Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China) was used. Stimulation targets were identified using the international 10–20 EEG positioning system, with the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) localized at the F3 scalp site. Before treatment initiation, the resting motor threshold (RMT) was determined over the hand motor cortex contralateral to the stimulated hemisphere using electromyography (EMG)-recorded motor-evoked potentials (MEPs). Surface EMG was recorded from the abductor pollicis brevis muscle while the target muscle was relaxed. RMT was defined as the minimum stimulator output that elicited MEPs with a peak-to-peak amplitude of at least 50 uV in at least 5 of 10 consecutive trials. This procedure followed published consensus recommendations for TMS threshold determination and safety monitoring.

Participants were assigned to active iTBS or matched sham stimulation. Active stimulation was delivered using a figure-of-eight cooled stimulation coil supplied with the YRD CCY-1 system. The coil was placed tangentially to the scalp with the handle oriented posteriorly and laterally, and positioning was checked before each session. The matched sham coil reproduced the external appearance, handling procedures, clicking sound, and scalp somatosensory cues of the active coil while minimizing effective cortical stimulation, thereby supporting participant blinding. According to manufacturer documentation, detailed internal engineering parameters, including winding turns and material composition, are proprietary and not publicly available.

For the active protocol, iTBS was delivered over the left DLPFC at 90% of the individual RMT. Each burst contained three pulses at 50 Hz, with bursts repeated at 5 Hz intervals (every 200 ms). A stimulation train lasted 2 s and was followed by an 8-second inter-train interval. Sixty trains were delivered per session, yielding 1800 pulses per session, and each treatment session required approximately 10 min. Participants assigned to sham stimulation underwent the same schedule, positioning, session duration, and operator contact as the active group.

Patients assigned to the iTBS group received two sessions daily with an inter-session interval of approximately 50 min. Treatments were administered five days per week over a 2-week period, resulting in 20 sessions. The sham group received the identical schedule. All stimulation procedures were conducted by trained technicians under supervision of licensed physicians. During each session, participants remained seated comfortably and were continuously observed for tolerability, emergent suicidal risk, headache, scalp pain, dizziness, fatigue, anxiety/agitation, and other adverse events [18, 19].

SSRI exposure was fully characterized by group. In the iTBS group, the prescribed agents were escitalopram oxalate (n = 42, 10–15 mg/day), paroxetine (n = 8, 10–30 mg/day), and sertraline (n = 2, 50–150 mg/day). In the sham group, the corresponding agents were escitalopram oxalate (n = 21, 10–15 mg/day), paroxetine (n = 4, 10–30 mg/day), and sertraline (n = 1, 50–150 mg/day). All participants had received a stable SSRI regimen for at least 3 months before enrollment, all remained on the same agent and dose throughout the 10-day neuromodulation intervention, and no participant was drug-naive or medication-free at randomization (Supplementary Table S1).

Outcome measures

The primary endpoint was depressive symptom severity measured by HAMD-17 after completion of the 2-week intervention. Secondary outcomes included post-treatment suicidal ideation, cognitive reappraisal and expressive suppression, anxiety severity, anhedonia, sleep quality, working memory performance, perceived social support, and self-reported depressive and anxiety symptoms.

Safety measures

Safety monitoring was conducted continuously throughout the intervention phase. At each treatment visit, physicians actively assessed discomfort or newly emerging symptoms, while participant-reported events were documented in the adverse-event log. Common stimulation-related reactions, including headache, scalp pain, dizziness, fatigue, nausea, insomnia, anxiety/agitation, manic symptoms, seizure, and worsening suicidal ideation, were specifically queried. Serious adverse events, premature treatment discontinuations, and emergency interventions were reviewed by the responsible study physician and classified by severity and potential relationship to the intervention.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software (version 21.0; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Continuous variables were examined for distribution normality and variance homogeneity. Variables conforming to a normal distribution are summarized as mean +/- standard deviation (SD), whereas skewed data are presented as median with interquartile range. Categorical data are described using frequencies and percentages. Exact p values are reported where appropriate; values less than 0.001 are reported as p < .001, and zeros are omitted before decimal points for p values and correlations.

Changes in clinical outcomes across the intervention period were analyzed using repeated-measures general linear models. Measurement time point (baseline and post-treatment) was the within-subject factor, and treatment condition (active iTBS versus sham stimulation) was the between-subject factor. Main effects for time and group, together with the Group x Time interaction, were examined. ANOVA results are reported as F(df_effect, df_error) = value, p value, and partial eta-squared. When significant interaction effects were identified, post hoc comparisons clarified the direction and magnitude of between-group and within-group differences.

Correlation analysis was performed to test the prespecified mechanistic hypothesis that clinical improvement after left-DLPFC iTBS would be associated with restoration of adaptive emotion regulation. Pearson correlations were therefore restricted to change scores (post-treatment minus baseline) for variables showing significant between-group or interaction effects in the primary models, particularly HAMD-17, BSSI, HAMA, PSQI, and ERQ cognitive reappraisal. This strategy was intended to evaluate whether improvement in depressive severity and suicidal ideation was clinically linked to enhanced cognitive reappraisal rather than to nonspecific symptom fluctuation. All tests were two-sided, and p < .05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Characteristics of participants

A total of 78 participants were enrolled, randomized, completed the intervention, and were included in the final analysis (active iTBS, n = 52; sham, n = 26). The iTBS and sham groups were well matched with respect to demographic characteristics and baseline psychobehavioral scale scores. There were no statistically significant baseline differences between groups in age, sex, depression severity, anxiety, sleep quality, social support, suicidal ideation, or emotion-regulation measures (all p > .05) (Table 1). Concomitant SSRI exposure was also balanced: all participants in both groups were receiving stable SSRI treatment at randomization, the proportional distributions of escitalopram oxalate, paroxetine, and sertraline were identical across the two allocation arms, no participant was drug-naive or medication-free at baseline, and no medication changes occurred during the 10-day intervention (Supplementary Table S1).

Efficacy outcomes

Primary outcome

In the analysis of the primary outcome, HAMD-17 scores demonstrated a significant Group x Time interaction (F(1, 76) = 117.055, p < .001, partial eta-squared = 0.606). In the iTBS group, the mean (SD) HAMD-17 score decreased from 24.71 (4.137) at baseline to 8.96 (5.237) post-intervention (p < .001). In the sham group, the score decreased from 24.81 (3.774) to 15.95 (5.714) (p < .001). Post hoc analysis indicated significantly lower post-treatment depressive severity in the active iTBS group than in the sham group (p < .001) (Fig. 1).

Secondary outcomes

Self-rating depression scale

The SDS showed a significant Group x Time interaction (F(1, 76) = 32.713, p < .001, partial eta-squared = 0.301). Bracketed values represent SDs throughout the Results. Both groups showed within-group reductions (iTBS: 73.81 [7.316] to 57.19 [11.714]; sham: 74.50 [9.530] to 60.81 [10.994]; both p < .001). The post-treatment between-group comparison reached statistical significance (p = .016), although the magnitude was smaller than that observed for clinician-rated HAMD-17 (Fig. 1).

Self-rating anxiety scale

A significant Group x Time interaction was found for SAS scores (F(1, 76) = 20.456, p < .001, partial eta-squared = 0.212). Both the iTBS group (65.27 [12.175] to 49.52 [12.066]) and the sham group (65.58 [11.021] to 51.57 [12.596]) improved from baseline (p < .001). The post-treatment between-group difference was not statistically significant (p = .51) (Fig. 1).

Cognitive reappraisal score

For cognitive reappraisal, the Group x Time interaction was significant (F(1, 76) = 51.214, p < .001, partial eta-squared = 0.403). The iTBS group demonstrated a marked increase from 18.48 (4.570) at baseline to 29.17 (5.458) post-intervention (p < .001), and its post-treatment score was significantly higher than that of the sham group (24.33 [6.800]; p < .001) (Fig. 1).

Expressive suppression score

Expressive suppression showed a significant Group x Time interaction (F(1, 76) = 29.340, p < .001, partial eta-squared = 0.279). Both groups improved from baseline (iTBS, p < .001; sham, p = .003). At post-treatment, the iTBS group had a mean (SD) score of 13.29 (4.509), which did not differ significantly from the sham group (15.19 [4.389]; p = .091) (Fig. 1).

Snaith-Hamilton pleasure scale

The Snaith-Hamilton Pleasure Scale demonstrated a significant Group x Time interaction (F(1, 76) = 6.769, p = .011, partial eta-squared = 0.082). Post hoc analysis revealed improvement in the iTBS group, with the mean (SD) score decreasing from 34.37 (5.314) at baseline to 30.31 (6.057) post-intervention (p < .001). The sham group also improved from 34.35 (4.690) to 30.90 (3.780) (p = .026). No significant post-treatment between-group difference was observed (p = .664) (Fig. 1).

Pittsburgh sleep quality index

The Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index demonstrated a significant Group x Time interaction (F(1, 76) = 5.535, p = .021, partial eta-squared = 0.068). The iTBS group showed improved sleep quality, with mean (SD) scores decreasing from 13.50 (3.888) to 10.46 (4.304) (p < .001). Improvement in the sham group did not reach statistical significance (12.81 [3.990] to 10.48 [5.400]; p = .066), and the post-treatment between-group difference was not significant (p = .989) (Fig. 1).

Beck scale for suicide ideation in the past week

The BSSI showed a significant Group x Time interaction (F(1, 76) = 25.263, p < .001, partial eta-squared = 0.249). Suicidal ideation decreased in the iTBS group from 12.65 (5.854) to 4.15 (5.849) (p < .001) and in the sham group from 14.50 (5.515) to 8.76 (6.767) (p = .001). The active iTBS group had significantly lower post-treatment BSSI scores than the sham group (p = .003), indicating greater reduction in suicidal ideation (Fig. 1).

Social support rating scale

The Social Support Rating Scale showed no significant Group x Time interaction (F(1, 76) = 0.557, p = .458, partial eta-squared = 0.007). Scores remained stable in both groups (iTBS: 28.25 [7.087] to 28.71 [8.332]; sham: 26.50 [5.694] to 28.29 [6.389]), indicating that perceived social support did not account for the observed symptom changes (Fig. 1).

Hamilton anxiety rating scale

The Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale demonstrated a significant Group x Time interaction (F(1, 76) = 41.275, p < .001, partial eta-squared = 0.352). Anxiety severity decreased in the iTBS group from 18.33 (6.434) to 7.54 (5.326) (p < .001) and in the sham group from 20.08 (4.630) to 14.67 (6.167) (p = .002). The active iTBS group had significantly lower post-treatment anxiety severity than the sham group (p < .001) (Fig. 1).

Cognitive performance (Digit Span)

No significant Group x Time interactions were observed for Digit Span Forward (F(1, 76) = 0.563, p = .455, partial eta-squared = 0.007) or Digit Span Backward scores (F(1, 76) = 2.070, p = .154, partial eta-squared = 0.027). Forward scores remained stable in both the iTBS group (7.58 [1.673] to 7.96 [1.726]) and sham group (7.88 [1.275] to 7.67 [1.560]). Backward scores changed comparably in the iTBS group (4.12 [1.843] to 4.96 [2.142]) and sham group (4.23 [1.818] to 4.90 [1.947]) (Fig. 1).

Correlation analysis

In the iTBS group, Pearson correlation analysis of change scores (post-treatment minus baseline) showed that greater improvement in cognitive reappraisal was associated with greater reduction in HAMD-17 scores (r = − .455, p = .001). Changes in HAMD-17 were also positively correlated with changes in HAMA scores (r = .665, p < .001), suggesting that reductions in depressive and anxiety symptoms covaried during active iTBS treatment. These analyses were performed to evaluate the mechanistic hypothesis that symptom reduction after left-DLPFC iTBS is clinically linked to restoration of adaptive emotion regulation (Fig. 2).

Safety

No serious adverse events were reported in either group during the study period. No seizure, manic switch, suicide attempt, emergency hospitalization, or treatment discontinuation related to stimulation occurred. Mild stimulation-related discomfort, if present, was actively monitored at each session and summarized in Supplementary Table S2.

Discussion

In this prospectively registered, randomized, double-blind, sham-controlled trial, a 10-day intensive intervention of active iTBS added to stable medication management led to greater reductions in depressive severity (HAMD-17) and suicidal ideation (BSSI) than matched sham stimulation. The active iTBS group also showed greater improvement in anxiety symptoms and sleep quality. Importantly, iTBS enhanced cognitive reappraisal capacity, and improvement in cognitive reappraisal correlated with reduction in depressive severity. These findings support the interpretation that active iTBS may provide multidimensional symptom relief by engaging higher-order emotion-regulation mechanisms, while pharmacological treatment remained clinically stable across the intervention window.

The significant reduction in the primary outcome of HAMD-17 scores and the between-group divergence in BSSI scores support the short-term clinical efficacy of active iTBS added to stable medication management over the 10-day treatment course [20]. Pathophysiologically, MDD patients with SI often exhibit functional impairment in the left dlPFC. The magnetic pulses of active iTBS may induce LTP-like effects in this region, strengthening its top-down executive control over downstream limbic structures such as the amygdala [21, 22]. This circuit-level interpretation remains mechanistic and should be confirmed with neuroimaging or electrophysiological biomarkers in future work [23, 24].

The significant increase in cognitive reappraisal scores carries profound clinical significance. Supporting research indicates that the essence of depression is a failure of emotional regulation [25]. Our study demonstrates that iTBS specifically enhances cognitive reappraisal—a positive emotional reframing strategy—rather than merely affecting expressive suppression, which is a passive emotional inhibition strategy. This suggests that iTBS does not simply mask negative emotions but enhances cortical processing efficiency, allowing patients to reassess and reinterpret negative events [26, 27]. The correlation between improved cognitive reappraisal and HAMD reduction further proves that the optimization of emotional regulation strategies is a core driver of depressive symptom remission [28–31].

The significant reduction in HAMA scores and its strong correlation with depressive improvement reflect the generalized effect of iTBS on emotional circuit regulation. Notably, while PSQI (sleep) showed between-group differences, there were no significant differences in Digit Span (cognition) or Social Support scores between the two groups. This suggests that the effect of iTBS in this protocol is “emotion-targeted,” primarily aiming to regulate neural networks associated with emotional stability rather than inducing broad-spectrum cognitive enhancement in the short term. Such specificity provides important guidance for treatment selection in the context of precision medicine [32–34].

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although concomitant pharmacological treatment was clinically stabilized and Supplementary Table S1 now reports SSRI agent, dose range, treatment duration threshold, drug-naive/no-SSRI status, and medication stability by group, residual confounding from individual antidepressant exposure cannot be fully excluded. Second, because the study included only baseline and post-treatment assessments, precise time-to-response, time-to-remission, daily symptom trajectories, and number-needed-to-treat estimates could not be calculated; all efficacy findings should therefore be interpreted as baseline-to-post-treatment changes after a 10-day intervention rather than as evidence of measured response speed. Third, the 2:1 allocation increased information about active iTBS tolerability but reduced the size of the sham comparator. Fourth, although the study used a matched sham coil and blinded assessors, formal post-treatment assessment of blinding credibility was not collected. Fifth, although the stimulation device, coil type, target, intensity, pulse structure, session schedule, sham procedures, and RMT determination were described, detailed internal engineering parameters of the active and sham coils, including winding turns and material composition, are proprietary and not publicly available; this limits complete technical reproducibility at the internal coil-design level. Finally, this study did not use neuroimaging modalities such as functional MRI to directly observe changes in prefrontal-limbic circuitry. Future multicenter trials should include complete medication stratification, serial symptom assessments, objective blinding checks, full device-reporting where available, and neuroimaging biomarkers.

In summary, active iTBS added to stable medication management was associated with greater baseline-to-post-treatment improvement in depressive symptoms and suicidal ideation than sham stimulation in patients with MDD and SI. The association between improved cognitive reappraisal and reduced depressive severity suggests that enhancement of adaptive emotion regulation may be one clinically meaningful pathway through which iTBS exerts benefit. These findings justify further adequately powered trials with detailed pharmacological characterization, objective blinding assessment, repeated interim symptom assessments, and longer follow-up.
