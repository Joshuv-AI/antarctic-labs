Functional mitral valve regurgitation outcomes by treatment modality: a systematic review and meta-analysis of reconstructed survival data
Authors: Parham Dastjerdi, Kiarash Tavakoli, Roozbeh Narimani-Javid, Nazanin Anaraki, Hedieh Shayesteh, Negin Sadat Hosseini Mohammadi, Saghar Momeni, Reza Nikfar, Soheil Rahmati, Amr Mohsen, Kaveh Hosseini, Hamidreza Soleimani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFunctional mitral regurgitation (FMR) carries poor prognosis, and optimal management remains debated. Mitral Transcatheter Edge-to-Edge Repair (MTEER) is an alternative to surgical repair (SMVR) or optimized medical therapy (OMT), but long-term outcomes are uncertain.MethodsPubMed, Embase, Scopus, and the Cochrane Library were searched for studies reporting Kaplan–Meier curves of MTEER, SMVR, or OMT in FMR. Individual patient data (IPD) were reconstructed using the Liu method with accuracy checks. Pooled IPD were analyzed with Cox and time-varying Cox models, landmark, time-restricted, and restricted mean survival time analyses.ResultsSixteen studies (n = 4,570) were included. Patients undergoing MTEER were older and higher risk, with greater prevalence of atrial fibrillation, prior myocardial infarction, chronic kidney disease, higher EuroSCORE, and more frequent NYHA class ≥ III compared with SMVR. Over up to 60 months, both MTEER (HR: 0.65; 95% CI: 0.57–0.75; p < 0.001) and SMVR (HR: 0.39; 95% CI: 0.33–0.46; p < 0.001) significantly reduced all-cause mortality versus OMT, whereas MTEER carried higher mortality risk than SMVR (HR: 1.68; 95% CI: 1.43–1.97; p < 0.001). MTEER was less effective than SMVR in reducing recurrent MR > 2 (HR: 3.31; 95% CI: 1.62–6.75; p < 0.001) and reoperation (HR: 4.62; 95% CI: 2.22–9.61; p < 0.001). In contrast, MTEER reduced hospitalization risk by 30% compared to OMT (HR: 0.70; 95% CI: 0.64–0.77; p < 0.001) and decreased MACE by 21% (HR: 0.79; 95% CI: 0.73–0.84; p < 0.001).ConclusionsMTEER improves survival and reduces hospitalization and MACE compared with OMT but is less durable than SMVR, with higher risks of recurrent MR, reoperation, and late mortality.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12872-026-06106-y.

Introduction

Functional mitral regurgitation (FMR) is a common and complex disorder that occurs in patients with cardiomyopathy and heart failure and is associated with increased morbidity and mortality [1, 2]. Unlike degenerative (primary) mitral regurgitation, which results from intrinsic abnormalities of the mitral valve leaflets, FMR develops in the absence of leaflet pathology and is primarily related to ventricular or atrial remodeling, most often due to dilated cardiomyopathy of ischemic or nonischemic origin [3]. Several prospective studies suggest that mitral valve repair alleviates symptoms in patients with FMR, highlighting the critical role of treatment selection in management [4–6].

Mitral Transcatheter Edge-to-Edge Repair (MTEER), most commonly performed with the MitraClip device and mimicking the surgical edge-to-edge technique, has emerged as a less invasive alternative for patients with persistent symptoms despite optimized medical therapy [7–9]. Guideline recommendations differ between societies. The European Society of Cardiology recommends MTEER in hemodynamically stable patients with severe ventricular secondary MR and impaired LVEF with no concomitant coronary artery disease who remain symptomatic despite optimized guideline-directed medical therapy to reduce HF hospitalizations and improve quality of life, whereas the American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association recommends surgery only when transcatheter repair is not anatomically feasible [10, 11]. Evidence from clinical trials further reflects this uncertainty. The EVEREST II trial, conducted primarily in degenerative MR, demonstrated superior freedom from death, surgery, or severe MR at five years with surgical mitral valve repair, although beyond the first year, both approaches achieved durable MR reduction and comparable survival [12]. In terms of secondary MR, the MATTERHORN trial showed that, among patients with heart failure and secondary MR, MTEER was noninferior to mitral-valve surgery with respect to a composite of death, rehospitalization for heart failure, stroke, reintervention, or implantation of an assist device in the left ventricle at 1 year [13]. Furthermore, the MITRA-FR [14], COAPT [7], and RESHAPE-HF2 [15] trials compared MTEER combined with optimal medical therapy against medical therapy alone, yet reported contrasting outcomes: MITRA-FR found no significant benefit, whereas COAPT and RESHAPE-HF2 showed substantial reductions in mortality and heart failure hospitalizations.

To address these discrepancies and provide cumulative evidence, this systematic review and meta-analysis aims to compare clinical outcomes in patients with FMR undergoing MTEER, surgical mitral valve repair (SMVR), or optimized medical therapy (OMT).

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines [16]. The review protocol was registered in the Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; registration ID: CRD420251116637).

Literature search

A comprehensive literature search of PubMed, Embase, Scopus, and the Cochrane Library was conducted in June 2025 to identify studies reporting outcomes of transcatheter valve repair and surgical and/or medical therapy in patients with functional mitral valve regurgitation. Database-specific search terms included variations of (“transcatheter mitral valve repair” OR “MitraClip”) AND (“surgical therapy” OR “medical therapy”). No restrictions on language, study design, or publication type were applied during the search. Detailed, database-specific search strategies are provided in Supplemental Material.

Eligibility criteria and study selection

All retrieved articles were imported into Rayyan, a web-based tool for systematic review screening [17]. Duplicate entries were removed, after which four independent reviewers screened the titles and abstracts to identify potentially eligible studies. Subsequently, two independent researchers assessed the full-text articles to ensure inclusion criteria were met. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion or consultation with a senior reviewer.

Studies were eligible if they included at least two comparative arms assessing outcomes of transcatheter valve repair versus surgical and/or medical therapy in patients with functional mitral valve regurgitation. Both clinical trials and observational studies were included, provided they reported Kaplan–Meier (KM) survival curves for the desired outcomes. The following criteria were used to exclude studies: single-arm study designs, patients with primary mitral valve regurgitation, and studies involving patients under 18 years of age. Conference abstracts, case reports, case series, reviews, and non-English language studies were also excluded.

Data extraction

Two reviewers independently extracted relevant data from all eligible studies using a predefined, standardized spreadsheet. Extracted information included, but was not limited to: (1) study-level characteristics such as year of publication, study design, and inclusion and exclusion criteria; and (2) baseline patient characteristics, including mean or median age, sex distribution, body mass index (BMI), and the prevalence of cardiovascular risk factors such as hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and history of myocardial infarction. In addition, anatomical and clinical features specific to mitral valve regurgitation were collected (e.g., MR class).

Outcomes

The primary outcome evaluated in this meta-analysis was all-cause mortality. Secondary outcomes included recurrent MR, reoperation, hospitalization for heart failure (HF), and major adverse cardiac events (MACE). Recurrent MR was defined as MR class 3 or 4, and MACE was defined according to each individual study’s criteria.

Quality assessment

Methodological quality was assessed using the ROBINS-I tool (Risk Of Bias In Non-randomized Studies – of Interventions) for observational studies and the RoB 2 tool (revised tool for Risk of Bias in randomized trials) for randomized trials [18, 19].

The ROBINS-I tool is specifically designed to evaluate bias in observational studies that compare the effects of interventions. It encompasses seven key domains: confounding factors, classification of interventions, participant selection, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, outcome measurement, and selection of reported results. The RoB 2 tool is tailored to assess bias in randomized trials and is structured into five domains: randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of the outcome, and selection of the reported result.

Two reviewers conducted the assessments independently using the appropriate tool for each study type, with any disagreements resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer.

Statistical analysis

To enable time-to-event analysis across studies, an individual patient data (IPD) meta-analytic framework was used by reconstructing survival data from published Kaplan–Meier (KM) curves according to the method proposed by Liu et al. [20]. KM plots from each eligible study were digitized to extract survival probabilities over time, which were then combined with corresponding numbers at risk to reconstruct IPD datasets. Whenever included studies reported matched cohorts for baseline characteristics (e.g., via propensity score matching or other adjustment methods) and published Kaplan–Meier plots for those matched cohorts, we preferentially used the adjusted plots instead of the unadjusted plots for reconstruction. All reconstructions were performed using the functions available in the IPDfromKM package in R.

The accuracy of each reconstructed dataset was verified by calculating the root mean square error (RMSE), mean absolute error (MAE), and the maximum absolute error between the original and reconstructed survival curves. Following reconstruction, IPD from all studies were pooled to generate composite survival curves. Cox proportional hazards models were applied to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) comparing transcatheter valve repair versus other treatment strategies. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using the Grambsch–Therneau test and by visual inspection of Schoenfeld residuals. In cases where this assumption was violated, flexible time-varying Cox models were fitted to characterize changes in the hazard ratio over time, allowing the estimation of dynamic risk patterns across the follow-up period.

In addition to the overall Cox models, landmark survival analyses were conducted for each outcome at outcome-specific time points. These time points were chosen based on clinical relevance, observable inflection points in the primary KM curves, and exploratory intervals to test the robustness of results across different periods. Landmark analyses assessed conditional survival benefits beyond early mortality risks by comparing subsequent survival among patients alive at each selected time point.

Complementary time-restricted survival analyses were also performed for outcome-specific intervals selected using the same criteria. This approach evaluated potential shifts in risk profiles over time and confirmed whether effects persisted across alternative intervals. Finally, restricted mean survival time (RMST) was calculated as an alternative treatment effect measure, providing an interpretable estimate of survival benefit over the longest available follow-up [21] .

For descriptive purposes, baseline characteristics were also synthesized across treatment groups. Continuous variables (e.g., age, BMI, LVEF, EuroSCORE) and categorical variables (e.g., hypertension, diabetes, atrial fibrillation, chronic kidney disease) were pooled from each study that reported them using random-effects models to derive means or proportions with 95% CIs.

A network meta-analysis was not performed due to concerns regarding violation of the transitivity assumption, as patient populations across treatment groups differed substantially in baseline risk profiles (e.g., age, comorbidities, and surgical candidacy). In addition, the primary objective of this study was not to directly compare treatment strategies, but rather to evaluate outcomes among patients undergoing each modality in real-world settings. Given these considerations, indirect comparisons and treatment ranking were deemed potentially biased, and we therefore focused on pairwise analyses to preserve the validity and interpretability of the reconstructed IPD results.

All analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.5.0; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and RStudio (version 2025.05.0 + 496; Posit PBC, Boston, MA, United States) [22, 23]. Two-sided p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

The initial search identified 6,131 records across PubMed, Scopus, Embase, and Cochrane. After removal of 1,653 duplicates, 4,478 unique records were screened by title and abstract. Of these, 124 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility, resulting in 16 studies that met the inclusion criteria (Fig. 1). The included studies, published between 2012 and 2025, encompassed a broad range of geographic regions, with the largest representation from Italy (n = 6), followed by Germany (n = 3), multinational cohorts (n = 3), and single studies from Switzerland, Belgium, the Netherlands, and the United States. In total, 4570 patients were analyzed across MTEER, SMVR, and OMT groups. Study designs consisted of randomized controlled trials (n = 3), prospective observational studies (n = 2), and retrospective observational cohorts (n = 11) (Table 1). When pooling baseline characteristics across studies, the mean age was highest in the MTEER group (71.6 years) compared with SMVR (68.6 years) and OMT (71.1 years). The mean EuroSCORE was also markedly higher with MTEER (12.4) than with SMVR (7.6) or OMT (6.6). MTEER patients had higher pooled prevalences of atrial fibrillation (49.0%), chronic kidney disease (39.0%), and prior myocardial infarction (44.3%) than comparator groups. NYHA class ≥ III was most frequent in MTEER (83.6%). Overall, pooled estimates indicate that MTEER patients represented an older, more comorbid, and higher-risk cohort relative to SMVR and OMT (Supplementary Table 1).

Quality assessment of included studies

Among the three randomized controlled trials (RCTs), one was judged to have an overall “Low” risk of bias, while the remaining two were rated as having “Some concerns,”. Across the 13 observational studies assessed with ROBINS-I, seven were classified as having a “Moderate” risk of bias, and six were judged as “Serious” risk. Most observational studies demonstrated low risk in domains related to the classification of interventions, deviations from intended interventions, measurement of outcomes, and selection of reported results. Higher risks were predominantly observed in the confounding and participant selection domains.

A detailed domain-level assessment for each study is presented in Supplementary Figs. 1 (RoB 2) and Supplementary Fig. 2 (ROBINS-I).

All-cause mortality

A total of 3,980 patients from 14 studies, including 2,105 treated with MTEER, 1,113 with SMVR, and 762 with OMT, were included in the IPD-reconstructed meta-analysis for all-cause mortality. Over a follow-up of up to 60 months, both SMVR (HR: 0.39; 95% CI: 0.33–0.46; p < 0.001) and MTEER (HR: 0.65; 95% CI: 0.57–0.75; p < 0.001) were associated with a significantly lower risk of all-cause mortality compared with OMT (Fig. 2A). Furthermore, when directly compared with SMVR, MTEER was associated with a higher risk of all-cause mortality (HR: 1.68; 95% CI: 1.43–1.97; p < 0.001).

Fig. 2A Main KM plot for the all-cause mortality outcome; B 24-month landmark KM plot for all-cause mortality

A Main KM plot for the all-cause mortality outcome; B 24-month landmark KM plot for all-cause mortality

When assessing proportionality, no violation was observed for OMT versus MTEER (p = 0.56; Supplementary Fig. 3). In contrast, violations were detected for OMT versus SMVR (p = 0.008; Supplementary Fig. 4) and MTEER versus SMVR (p = 0.009; Supplementary Fig. 5). Flexible time-varying Cox models demonstrated that the mortality benefit of SMVR compared with OMT became progressively more pronounced with longer follow-up, as hazard ratios continued to decline over time (Supplementary Fig. 6). Conversely, for MTEER versus SMVR, the excess risk of mortality associated with MTEER increased throughout follow-up, although the rate of divergence slowed in later periods (Supplementary Fig. 7).

In the landmark analyses—restricted to patients alive at each time point (6, 12, and 24 months)—SMVR maintained a durable survival advantage over OMT: HR 0.28 (95% CI: 0.22–0.35) at 6 months, 0.30 (95% CI: 0.23–0.39) at 12 months, and 0.42 (95% CI: 0.29–0.61) at 24 months (all p < 0.001). MTEER also showed benefit versus OMT at 6 months (HR 0.62; 95% CI: 0.53–0.73; p < 0.001) and 12 months (HR 0.65; 95% CI: 0.54–0.78; p < 0.001), but this effect was no longer evident among survivors at 24 months (HR 0.87; 95% CI: 0.67–1.12; p = 0.274). When directly compared with SMVR, MTEER was associated with higher conditional mortality at each landmark through 24 months—HRs 2.24 (95% CI: 1.80–2.79), 2.15 (95% CI: 1.67–2.75), and 2.07 (95% CI: 1.49–2.89), respectively (all p < 0.001) (Fig. 2B, Supplementary Tables 2–4).

In time-restricted survival analyses, SMVR was associated with an early excess hazard compared with OMT within the first two weeks (HR: 3.59; 95% CI: 1.50–8.59; p = 0.004), which dissipated by one month (HR: 1.50; 95% CI: 0.89–2.51; p = 0.128) and three months (HR: 0.91; 95% CI: 0.64–1.31; p = 0.619). Beyond six months, SMVR demonstrated a significant conditional survival benefit over OMT (HR: 0.68; 95% CI: 0.51–0.91; p = 0.010). MTEER followed a similar pattern, with an early excess hazard at two weeks (HR: 2.36; 95% CI: 1.00–5.57; p = 0.050) that resolved by one and three months (both nonsignificant), transitioning to a significant survival benefit at six months (HR: 0.74; 95% CI: 0.58–0.94; p = 0.016). In contrast, comparison of MTEER versus SMVR revealed no significant differences during the first six months, with hazard ratios consistently close to unity. However, by 12 months, MTEER was associated with a higher risk of mortality compared with SMVR (HR: 1.36; 95% CI: 1.10–1.69; p = 0.005) (Supplementary Tables 5–7).

In RMST analyses up to 60 months, both SMVR and MTEER demonstrated survival advantages compared with OMT. Patients undergoing SMVR had an average survival gain of 10.07 months (95% CI: 6.93–13.21; p < 0.001) relative to OMT (Fig. 3A). Similarly, MTEER was associated with a mean survival gain of 6.61 months (95% CI: 4.50–8.73; p < 0.001) compared with OMT (Fig. 3B). Direct comparison of MTEER versus SMVR revealed inferior long-term outcomes for MTEER, with a mean survival deficit of − 3.46 months (95% CI: − 6.27 to − 0.65; p = 0.016; Fig. 3C).

Fig. 3All-cause mortality RMST: (A) SMVR vs. OMT; (B) MTEER vs. OMT; (C) MTEER vs. SMVR

All-cause mortality RMST: (A) SMVR vs. OMT; (B) MTEER vs. OMT; (C) MTEER vs. SMVR

To evaluate the robustness of the MTEER versus SMVR comparison, a sensitivity analysis was performed restricted to the nine studies that included both treatment arms directly, excluding five studies without a surgical comparator. In this subset, MTEER remained associated with a significantly higher risk of all-cause mortality compared with SMVR (HR: 1.83; 95% CI: 1.54–2.18; p < 0.001), fully consistent with the findings of the primary analysis.

Reoperation

A total of 1,174 patients from 2 studies, comprising 588 treated with MTEER and 586 with SMVR were included in the IPD-reconstructed meta-analysis for reoperation. Over a follow-up of up to 30 months, MTEER was associated with a significantly increased risk of reoperation compared with SMVR (HR: 4.62; 95% CI: 2.22–9.61; p < 0.001; Fig. 4A). No violation of the proportional hazards assumption was observed for this comparison (p = 0.29; Supplementary Fig. 8).

Fig. 4A Main KM plot for the reoperation outcome; B 12-month landmark KM plot for reoperation

A Main KM plot for the reoperation outcome; B 12-month landmark KM plot for reoperation

At 12 months, landmark analysis restricted to patients alive and event-free at the start of the interval demonstrated no significant difference between MTEER and SMVR (HR: 2.16; 95% CI: 0.61–7.65; p = 0.24; Fig. 4B). In contrast, time-restricted survival analysis up to 12 months showed a markedly higher risk of reoperation following MTEER (HR: 5.04; 95% CI: 1.10–23.0; p = 0.037), indicating that excess reoperations occurred predominantly in the early post-procedural period.

RMST analysis up to 30 months further confirmed shorter reoperation-free survival with MTEER compared with SMVR. The mean reoperation-free survival time was 26.25 months (95% CI: 25.18–27.33) in the MTEER group versus 28.05 months (95% CI: 26.93–29.16) in the SMVR group, corresponding to an average deficit of − 1.79 months (95% CI: − 3.34 to − 0.24; p = 0.023; Supplementary Fig. 9).

MR grade > 2

A total of 475 patients from 4 studies, comprising 285 treated with MTEER and 190 with SMVR were included in the IPD-reconstructed meta-analysis for the incidence of MR grade > 2. Over a follow-up of up to 48 months, MTEER was associated with a significantly higher risk of recurrent MR > 2 compared with SMVR (HR: 3.31; 95% CI: 1.62–6.75; p < 0.001; Supplementary Fig. 10). No violation of the proportional hazards assumption was detected (p = 0.89; Supplementary Fig. 11).

Landmark analyses consistently demonstrated higher hazard of recurrent MR > 2 with MTEER compared with SMVR across all time points. At 3 months, MTEER was associated with an HR of 2.63 (95% CI: 1.22–5.67; p = 0.014), at 6 months with an HR of 3.46 (95% CI: 1.34–8.92; p = 0.010), and at 12 months with an HR of 6.63 (95% CI: 1.56–28.17; p = 0.010) (Supplementary Table 8). Time-restricted survival analysis showed no significant difference between MTEER and SMVR within the first month, but a sharply increased risk of recurrent MR > 2 with MTEER over the first 3 months (HR: 8.84; 95% CI: 1.16–67.5; p = 0.036; Supplementary Table 9).

RMST analysis up to 48 months further highlighted the durability gap between interventions. The mean MR-free survival was 25.28 months (95% CI: 21.15–29.40) with MTEER compared with 42.61 months (95% CI: 40.30–44.96) with SMVR, corresponding to an average deficit of − 17.33 months (95% CI: − 22.1 to − 12.6; p < 0.001; Supplementary Fig. 12).

Hospitalization

A total of 839 patients from 4 studies, (MTEER: n = 467; OMT: n = 372) were included in the IPD-reconstructed meta-analysis for hospitalization. Over a follow-up period of up to 60 months, MTEER was associated with a significantly lower risk of hospitalization compared with OMT (HR: 0.70; 95% CI: 0.64–0.77; p < 0.001) (Fig. 5). Proportional hazards testing revealed no violation in this comparison (p = 0.89; Supplementary Fig. 13).

Fig. 5Main KM plot for the hospitalization outcome

Main KM plot for the hospitalization outcome

Landmark analyses showed a consistent benefit of MTEER compared with OMT up to 24 months, with HRs of 0.71 (95% CI: 0.63–0.81; p < 0.001) at 6 months, 0.65 (95% CI: 0.56–0.75; p < 0.001) at 12 months, and 0.64 (95% CI: 0.53–0.78; p < 0.001) at 24 months (Supplementary Table 10). At 36 months, the association was no longer statistically significant (HR: 0.96; 95% CI: 0.67–1.37; p = 0.803) (Supplementary Fig. 14). Time-restricted survival analyses confirmed the consistent reduction in hospitalization risk with MTEER compared with OMT, with HRs ranging from 0.68 to 0.75 across 6- to 36-month intervals, all statistically significant (p < 0.001) (Supplementary Table 11).

RMST analysis further supported these results, showing that during 60 months of follow-up, the mean hospitalization-free survival was 37.16 months with MTEER versus 24.04 months with OMT, corresponding to an absolute gain of 13.12 months (95% CI: 9.68–16.56; p < 0.001) in favor of MTEER (Supplementary Fig. 15).

MACE

A total of 1,157 patients from 5 studies, including 666 treated with MTEER and 491 with OMT, were included in the IPD-reconstructed meta-analysis for MACE. Over a follow-up of up to 60 months, MTEER was associated with a significant reduction in MACE compared with OMT (HR: 0.79; 95% CI: 0.73–0.84; p < 0.001) (Fig. 6). Testing of the proportional hazards assumption suggested a violation (p = 0.023; Supplementary Fig. 16), indicating time-varying effects. Flexible time-varying Cox models revealed that the relative benefit of MTEER over OMT became increasingly pronounced over time, with the hazard ratio declining steadily, indicating a growing separation in risk. However, the slope of decline flattened at later follow-up (Supplementary Fig. 17).

Fig. 6Main KM plot for the MACE outcome

Main KM plot for the MACE outcome

Landmark analyses demonstrated a persistent conditional benefit of MTEER, with significantly lower hazards at 6 months (HR: 0.58; 95% CI: 0.47–0.70; p < 0.001), 12 months (HR: 0.44; 95% CI: 0.34–0.57; p < 0.001), and 24 months (HR: 0.44; 95% CI: 0.32–0.61; p < 0.001, Supplementary Table 12), whereas the effect was no longer significant at 36 months (HR: 0.70; 95% CI: 0.41–1.21; p = 0.202) (Supplementary Fig. 18). Complementary time-restricted survival analyses confirmed the consistent reduction in MACE with MTEER compared with OMT across predefined intervals, with hazard ratios of 0.67 at 6 months, 0.72 at 12 months, 0.67 at 24 months, and 0.61 at 36 months (all p < 0.001; Supplementary Table 13).

Finally, RMST analysis over 60 months confirmed a durable absolute benefit with MTEER, with mean MACE-free survival of 28.16 months versus 18.22 months with OMT, corresponding to a between-group difference of + 9.94 months (95% CI: 7.25–12.63; p < 0.001) (Supplementary Fig. 19).

IPD reconstruction accuracy assessment

Overall, the accuracy of IPD reconstruction was high across outcomes and treatment arms. Nearly all study arms met the recommended thresholds for reliability, with RMSE values ≤ 0.05, MAE values ≤ 0.02, and maximum absolute error values ≤ 0.05. A few isolated study arms showed only slight deviations from these thresholds, but overall reconstruction performance remained robust and was supported by consistently low error metrics across studies. Full details of error metrics for each study arm are presented in Supplementary Tables 14–18.

Discussion

By combining data from 16 studies including more than 4500 patients with FMR, our study yielded several important findings: (I) In the short-term, both SMVR and MTEER demonstrated better survival outcomes compared to OMT, with no significant difference between the two interventional approaches. However, after six months, surgical treatment was associated with better survival outcomes, emerging as the superior modality over MTEER and OMT, with patients gaining an additional 3.5 and 10 months of survival free from events, respectively, during 60 months of follow-up. (II) MTEER was also less effective than SMVR in reducing MR grade to ≤ 2, with more than a threefold higher risk and about 17 months less MR-free survival over 4 years of follow-up. In addition, MTEER carried a 4.6-fold higher risk of reoperation and 1.8 months less reoperation-free survival within 30 months. (III) MTEER was associated with a 30% lower risk of hospitalization compared with OMT, corresponding to 13.1 additional hospitalization-free months over 60 months of follow-up. Similarly, MTEER reduced the risk of MACE by about 20%, yielding an average gain of 9.9 MACE-free months.

The present study demonstrated that patients undergoing MTEER experienced a 35% reduction in all-cause mortality compared to OMT over 60 months of follow-up. This finding aligns with results reported by Kaddoura et al., who observed improved survival with MTEER (RR: 0.55, 95% CI: 0.40–0.75) over 24 months [38]. Our meta-analysis expands upon these findings by incorporating a larger number of contemporary studies with longer follow-up durations. In contrast, Ghaly et al. reported no significant difference between MTEER and OMT (RR: 0.80, 95% CI: 0.63–1.02) [39]. While it should be acknowledged that RCTs are currently the strongest evidence so far, limiting the analysis to RCTs as they did might exclude a substantial body of real-world evidence. Another recent meta-analysis of RCTs found that death or first hospitalization due to heart failure at 24 months was significantly lower in the MTEER plus OMT group than in the OMT-alone group, as assessed by one-stage analysis (HR 0.72, 95% CI: 0.56–0.92; p = 0.010). However, the two-stage analysis only narrowly did not confirm this result (HR 0.72, 95% CI: 0.51-1.00; p = 0.052). This study also showed that All-cause death and cardiovascular death at 24 months were not significantly different between treatment groups, but became significant after excluding MITRA-FR in the leave-one-out analysis [40].

RCTs evaluating MTEER versus OMT have yielded conflicting results. The Cardiovascular Outcomes Assessment of the MitraClip Transcatheter Therapy for Heart Failure Patients with Functional Mitral Regurgitation (COAPT) trial demonstrated that MTEER significantly reduced all-cause mortality compared to OMT (HR: 0.62, 95% CI: 0.46–0.82) [7]. The RESHAPE-HF2 [15] involved patients with heart failure and moderate-severe functional MR who were assigned to either MTEER and OMT (device group) or OMT alone (control group). At 24 months, the rate of first or recurrent hospitalization for heart failure or cardiovascular death was 37.0 events per 100 patient-years in the device group and 58.9 in the control group (rate ratio, 0.64; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.48 to 0.85; P = 0.002). The rate of first or recurrent hospitalization for heart failure was 26.9 events per 100 patient-years in the device group and 46.6 in the control group (rate ratio, 0.59; 95% CI, 0.42 to 0.82; P = 0.002). While the Transcatheter Repair with the MitraClip Device for Severe Functional/Secondary Mitral Regurgitation (MITRA-FR) trial reported no significant difference in survival between the two groups (HR: 1.16, 95% CI: 0.73–1.84) [41]. The more favorable outcomes observed in COAPT and RESHAPE-HF2 may be attributed to higher procedural success, stricter patient selection, better adherence to guideline-directed medical therapy, and lower prevalence of MR grade > 2 at one year. By contrast, MITRA-FR better reflected a real-world population, suggesting that MTEER may not provide survival benefit across all patients with FMR [42].

Conversely, our analysis revealed that patients treated with MTEER had a 68% higher risk of all-cause mortality compared with those undergoing conventional surgery over the same follow-up period. Importantly, survival outcomes between MTEER and surgery were not significantly different during the first six months; after this period, surgical intervention demonstrated superiority. These findings differ from those reported in prior meta-analyses. Kaddoura et al. found MTEER to be non-inferior to surgery at 12 months (OR: 1.86, 95% CI: 0.97–3.56) and 24 months (OR: 0.95, 95% CI: 0.45–2.02) [38]. Felbel et al. also reported similar one-year mortality rates between MTEER (18%, 95% CI: 0.15–0.21) and surgery (11%, 95% CI: 0.07–0.18) [43]. Notably, neither study accounted for the effect of time, whereas hazard ratios provide a more precise assessment by incorporating time-to-event dynamics [44].

To date, the Multicenter Mitral Valve Reconstruction for Advanced Insufficiency of Functional or Ischemic Origin (MATTERHORN) trial remains the only RCT directly comparing MTEER with surgical mitral valve repair in patients with functional MR [13]. In contrast to our findings, MATTERHORN reported comparable mortality risks between the two treatment strategies. Similarly, the Endovascular Valve Edge-to-Edge Repair Study (EVEREST II) trial found no significant difference in long-term survival between MTEER and surgery; however, only 27% of participants had functional MR, and most presented with limited comorbidity aside from heart failure [12]. Interestingly, a recent meta-analysis demonstrated that patients enrolled in observational studies were typically older, had more comorbidities, presented with more advanced symptoms, and exhibited higher MR severity compared to those included in randomized trials [42].

Several factors may explain our observation of poorer survival outcomes with MTEER compared with surgical intervention. Patients selected for MTEER are typically older, at higher surgical risk, and present with greater frailty. They often have multiple comorbidities such as hypertension, chronic kidney disease, and sarcopenia, in addition to a history of prior cardiovascular interventions. These patients also tend to have more severe MR and lower left ventricular ejection fraction, conditions that heighten vulnerability to sudden health deteriorations even under minor physiological stressors. Such factors have been consistently associated with increased mortality in prior studies [38, 43, 45, 46]. In line with these considerations, our pooled baseline estimates showed that patients undergoing MTEER were indeed older, had higher EuroSCOREs, more prior myocardial infarction and atrial fibrillation, lower LVEF, more severe MR, and a higher proportion in NYHA class ≥ III compared with those treated surgically. This pattern strongly suggests that MTEER was preferentially offered to higher-risk patients, providing a logical explanation for why long-term survival outcomes were less favorable in this group. Moreover, there is an initial learning curve for using MitraClip due to the lack of operator experience for mitral interventions, which can be associated with poor outcomes in MTEER patients [47].

This study also demonstrated that MTEER was associated with reduced durability of mitral repair compared with conventional surgery, resulting in a higher risk of residual MR and reoperation. Notably, the 12-month landmark estimate was associated with a relatively wide confidence interval, likely reflecting a limited number of events at later time points. However, thresholds for reintervention may differ between surgical and transcatheter populations and can be influenced by factors such as patient frailty, life expectancy, and treatment preferences. As a result, reoperation rates may not be directly comparable between groups and should be interpreted with caution as a potential source of bias. Nonetheless, these findings are consistent with prior studies, which have shown that MTEER is less effective in achieving sustained MR reduction to grades ≤ 2 [12, 38]. We anticipate, however, that this limitation may be mitigated over time with increasing operator experience and the introduction of newer-generation devices and imaging technologies. The included studies span more than a decade, during which substantial advances have occurred in MTEER devices, procedural techniques, imaging, and guideline-directed medical therapy for heart failure. These temporal differences may have influenced procedural success rates and long-term outcomes, potentially contributing to heterogeneity across studies. Although formal subgroup analyses by study era were not feasible due to data limitations, this factor should be considered when interpreting the results.

The increased risk of recurrent MR > 2 after MTEER compared with SMVR may be due to several factors. It is mainly caused by inadequate initial MR reduction and by specific device failure mechanisms. Residual MR following MTEER has been consistently linked to future recurrences and adverse outcomes, indicating that many cases labeled as “recurrent” MR may actually result from progression due to an incomplete initial procedure. Additionally, TEER has specific structural failure modes, such as single-leaflet device attachment or partial clip detachment, which can cause sudden recurrence of significant MR [48, 49]. In contrast, SMVR typically includes annuloplasty and more complete restoration of leaflet coaptation, providing greater structural stability; when recurrence occurs, it is more often related to progressive ventricular remodeling or late degenerative changes, a comparatively gradual process [50].

In addition, our analysis showed that MTEER was associated with fewer hospitalizations for heart failure and a lower incidence of MACE compared with OMT, which is in agreement with previous reports [39, 51]. These benefits, however, must be weighed against procedural costs, peri-procedural risks, and the patient’s overall health status and prognosis [41]. These findings highlight the need for reliable risk stratification tools to guide patient selection and optimize outcomes with MTEER. Previous studies have evaluated the MitraScore for predicting rehospitalization and mortality following MTEER, reporting modest discriminatory performance (c-statistics of 0.70 in the derivation cohort and 0.66 in the validation cohort) [52]. Nonetheless, further research is needed to compare its predictive ability against widely used risk models, such as the CHA₂DS₂-VASc score.

Limitations

There are some limitations to our study. First, because evidence from RCTs remains limited, most of the data in our analyses were derived from real-world observational studies, which are inherently prone to selection bias and residual confounding. In addition, the small number of available RCTs—primarily comparing MTEER with OMT and lacking surgical comparator arms—precluded a meaningful RCT-only sensitivity analysis. Second, the IPD used in this analysis was reconstructed from published KM curves rather than obtained at the patient level, which precluded adjustment for individual-level comorbidities or other confounders. In addition, formal assessment of between-study heterogeneity was not feasible within this reconstructed IPD framework, although clinical and methodological heterogeneity across studies should be acknowledged. Third, we used Kaplan–Meier curves from matched cohorts in all studies that reported them, with the single exception of one study that performed matching but did not publish matched survival curves. Despite this approach, many included studies did not adjust or match treatment arms, which is reflected in our pooled baseline characteristics showing residual imbalances. Future studies should prioritize adjusted and matched analyses to better account for baseline differences. Finally, many included studies evaluated first- or second-generation MitraClip devices. Given the substantial technical advances in newer-generation systems, it is plausible that more recent devices may achieve improved outcomes, although their long-term durability remains to be demonstrated in large-scale RCTs.

Conclusion

In this meta-analysis of 16 studies, MTEER was associated with improved survival and reduced risk of heart failure hospitalization and MACE compared to OMT. When compared to SMVR, MTEER demonstrated comparable short-term survival, but less favorable long-term survival, which may reflect both differences in treatment durability and baseline patient risk profiles. In addition, MTEER was linked to reduced durability of valve repair, with higher rates of residual MR and reoperation. These findings underscore both the benefits and limitations of MTEER, highlighting the importance of careful patient selection to maximize benefits while balancing potential risks and costs. Future investigations with extended follow-up and evaluation of newer-generation MitraClip devices are warranted to clarify their efficacy and durability.
