Ensemble Monitoring for AI Control: Diverse Signals Outweigh More Compute
Eugene Koran, Yejun Yun, Samantha Tetef, Benjamin Arnav, Pablo Bernabeu-Perez

Abstract
As AI systems are increasingly deployed in autonomous agentic settings at scale, it is important to ensure the actions they take are safe and aligned with user intent. Monitoring agent actions is a key safety mechanism, yet reliable monitors remain difficult to build and the scale of these systems makes human oversight impractical. We show that combining signals from diverse monitors into an ensemble improves detection of misaligned actions. We build 12 GPT-4.1-Mini monitors using both prompting and fine-tuning strategies. We evaluate them on coding tasks where candidate solutions pass standard tests but fail on adversarial inputs. In this setting, diverse ensembles outperform both individual monitors and homogeneous ensembles. Our best 3-monitor ensemble achieves 2.4 × 2.4\times greater detection performance gain compared to an ensemble composed of three identical monitors, with the same ensemble performing strongly on an independent dataset. We contend that these results show that diversity—not scale—drives gains. The best ensembles combine strong individual performance with low correlation between monitors. Furthermore, fine-tuned monitors appear in every top-performing ensemble and maintain this advantage on out-of-distribution attack types, suggesting that fine-tuning enables detection capabilities that prompting alone does not elicit. These results support ensemble monitoring as a practical AI control strategy for safety gains at reasonable inference costs.

Introduction

As LLM-based coding agents are deployed with increasing autonomy and with greater capabilities, there is a risk of misalignment and intentional subversion, a concern especially acute for internal AI lab deployments. AI control
(
12
)
addresses this concern by designing protocols that remain robust against even deliberate subversion by an untrusted model. Such protocols treat sufficiently capable models as potentially misaligned and employ mechanisms that include trusted monitoring (scoring outputs with a weaker trusted model), resampling flagged actions
(
5
)
, and escalation to human review. One central question in AI control is how to construct effective monitors, specifically which approaches yield the strongest signals. Further, if employing multiple monitors in an ensemble, one must know how many are sufficient and whether diversity or scale drives performance changes.

Previous monitor design research has characterized the safety–cost tradeoff and explored combining signals. More capable monitors achieve higher detection rates but at increasing financial cost
(
28
)
. Protocols can combine monitors of different capability levels cost-optimally in a hierarchical system
(
15
)
. Moreover, protocols that combine action and chain-of-thought reasoning signals consistently outperform monitoring either signal alone
(
4
)
. In concurrent work, ensembling prompted monitors on realistic software engineering tasks achieves a dramatic reduction in false negatives compared with any single monitor
(
27
)
. These results echo classical ensemble theory: aggregating weakly correlated predictors reduces both variance and systematic error
(
9
;
10
)
. Yet prior work introduces diversity only through capability differences or signal type.

In contrast, we study whether diverse
construction methods
from a single base model, through prompting and fine-tuning, yield complementary signals that can be used to improve monitoring performance. We fix the base model (GPT-4.1-mini) and monitored signal (code actions) and construct 12 monitors through diverse prompting strategies and fine-tuned variants on the APPS dataset
(
14
)
from ControlArena
(
16
)
. We evaluate these monitors on a held out set of APPS questions and additionally on an out-of-distribution dataset. We show that diversity in construction is the primary driver of ensemble performance as opposed to repeated sampling of the same monitor: a carefully selected 3-monitor diverse ensemble matches the performance of a 12-monitor ensemble at one-quarter of the compute cost.

Our best 3-monitor ensemble improves pAUC (partial AUROC at false positive rate (FPR)
\leq 10\%
, see §
4.2
) by 13% over a baseline monitor, compared to just 5.5% for three identical monitors, a
2.4\times
improvement driven by construction diversity alone. These gains generalize to out-of-distribution attack types. Ensembles selected on APPS outperform both a single baseline and a median ensemble on BigCodeBench-Sabotage
(
4
)
, a dataset with a broader range of backdoor types. Fine-tuned monitors appear in every best-performing 3-monitor ensemble across both datasets, suggesting that fine-tuning as a construction method introduces detection signals that prompting cannot elicit from the same base model.

Given a fixed trusted model, a diverse monitor ensemble yields greater safety returns than spending the same inference budget on repeated sampling. These results provide evidence that trusted monitoring can be improved without scaling to a stronger model. More broadly, our results point to diversity, not scale, as the central principle for practical monitor ensemble design.

Our contributions are as follows:

\cdot

We show that diverse construction of monitors from a single base model produces complementary signals: our best 3-monitor diverse ensemble outperforms a homogeneous 3-monitor ensemble
2.4\times
(13% vs. 5.5% pAUC gain over a single monitor baseline).

\cdot

We show that ensemble performance plateaus rapidly with size. A carefully selected 3-monitor ensemble recovers the full benefit of a 12-monitor ensemble at one-quarter of the compute cost.

\cdot

We provide evidence that fine-tuned monitors contribute unique detection signals unavailable through prompting. They appear in every top-performing ensemble across both APPS and out-of-distribution BigCodeBench-Sabotage attack types.

2
Related Work

AI Control.
AI control
(
12
)
is a framework for designing deployment protocols that remain safe even against deliberate subversion by an untrusted model. Control protocols typically involve two components: monitoring (detecting outputs that constitute a sabotage attempt) and intervention (modifying untrusted responses for safe downstream use). Prior work has established trusted monitoring protocols evaluated on backdoored code datasets
(
12
;
28
)
and has extended them to agentic settings through dynamic resampling
(
5
)
, trusted editor routing
(
25
)
, and deception detection via linear probes
(
11
)
. Recent work has explored combining predictions from two monitors under cost constraints
(
15
)
. Notably,
27
provides evidence that monitor ensembles are effective under adversarial red-blue evaluation on realistic development tasks, and
4
demonstrates merging signals from chain-of-thought (CoT) and action-only monitors outperforms either individual monitoring source. CoT is a compelling monitoring signal but a brittle one. Training choices may eliminate readable reasoning traces
(
18
)
, and as models grow more capable, CoT becomes an increasingly unreliable signal
(
19
)
. We therefore ground our analysis in action-only monitoring, which remains applicable regardless of whether CoT is available or trustworthy.

Ensembling.
Ensemble methods have a long theoretical and empirical history in machine learning. Early work established that ensemble error falls as member diversity increases.
13
proved that independent errors below 50% can drive ensemble error to zero as ensemble size grows, and the ambiguity decomposition of
20
formalizes this observation, stating ensemble error equals average individual error minus average member disagreement.
6
demonstrated these benefits empirically through bagging, and
9
synthesized the theoretical foundations, identifying complementary reasons why ensembles outperform single models.
7
further formalized the role of diversity, showing that ensemble error depends on both individual accuracy and inter-member correlation, with lower correlation directly reducing error. These principles have been confirmed across diverse high-stakes application domains
(
10
)
, including medical diagnosis
(
22
)
, cybersecurity threat detection
(
24
)
, and financial fraud prevention
(
1
)
. Similar diversity-driven gains have been observed in LLM-as-a-judge settings
(
23
)
as well.

3
Method

3.1
Monitor types

We build monitors using two primary approaches. All prompt-based and fine-tuned monitors share the same base model and output a suspiciousness score on a fixed integer scale.

Prompting

Our Baseline monitor uses the default trusted monitor system prompt from ControlArena
(
16
)
(see Appendix
B
), which instructs the model to analyze submitted code for backdoor behavior given the problem statement and output a suspicion score from 0 to 10 with reasoning.

Beyond this baseline, we develop 7 diverse prompts that target different aspects of code analysis and exhibit complementary strengths and weaknesses. Prompting leverages the ability of LLMs to follow in-context instructions and to express latent capabilities when provided with appropriate context
(
8
)
. These prompts include instructions focused on identifying potential exploits, prioritizing either recall or precision, structuring analysis into two phases to scaffold the evaluation process, and a trusted debate variant
(
17
;
2
)
in which two model instances argue opposing positions and a third judges their exchange to produce a suspicion score. The motivation behind each prompt is summarized in Appendix
A
.

Fine-tuning

We also investigate fine-tuning as a complementary approach for monitor construction. Fine-tuning
(
26
)
adapts a base model to a target task by continuing training on labeled examples, enabling the model to internalize task-specific behaviors and decision boundaries. We fine-tune GPT-4.1-Mini on 50–200 training samples of code and suspicion score pairs using the OpenAI API, with sample counts varying by dataset. For some datasets, we generate reasoning and score pairs by prompting more capable models to simulate high-quality human annotators. We additionally apply dataset-specific filtering to improve label quality. For "filtered" datasets, we impose a suspiciousness threshold, retaining only examples where backdoor solutions score above the threshold and clean solutions score below it. As an alternative, we provide the generating model the correct label—whether the code is a solution or backdoor—before asking it to generate a response-score pair for training; these are "given labels" datasets. By default, "completions" datasets have no label filtering. We also train some monitors on deterministically set scores with no AI-generated reasoning traces to avoid introducing generative biases into the training data. Each fine-tuned monitor is named after the corresponding fine-tuning dataset and is prefixed with
FT-
throughout this work. Details of our fine-tuning datasets are presented in Appendix
C
.

3.2
Ensembling

Ensemble selection

We aggregate monitors by averaging their scores; we evaluated alternative aggregation methods but found no consistent improvement over arithmetic mean. For operating-point analysis (§
5.1
), we select score thresholds on the validation set and report detection rates on the held-out test set. For ensemble composition analysis (§
5.2
–
5.6
), following the ensemble diversity literature
(
21
;
30
)
, we enumerate all
\binom{N}{k}
subsets, where
N=12
is the total number of monitors, rank them by test set pAUC, and characterize properties of the top-performing ensembles. Generalization of the resulting composition patterns is evaluated on BCB-S (§
4.1
).

Diversity metric

We quantify ensemble diversity as the average pairwise Pearson correlation among constituent monitors’ score vectors, computed on attack (backdoored) samples only. For an ensemble of
k
monitors with score vectors
\mathbf{s}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{s}_{k}
over attack samples, the diversity metric is
\rho_{\text{avg}}=\frac{1}{\binom{k}{2}}\sum_{i<j}r(\mathbf{s}_{i},\mathbf{s}_{j})
, where
r(\cdot,\cdot)
denotes the Pearson correlation coefficient. Lower
\rho_{\text{avg}}
indicates greater diversity: the monitors in the ensemble disagree more in their scoring patterns on backdoored code. We restrict the correlation to attack samples because diversity in how monitors respond to backdoors drives complementary detection; correlation on honest samples primarily reflects agreement on benign code, which is less informative for ensemble selection.

4
Experimental Setup

All monitors use GPT-4.1-mini as the single trusted model. Evaluations are conducted using the ControlArena evaluation framework
(
16
)
at temperature 1, with all other generation parameters at their defaults. Detection performance is measured by pAUC (§
4.2
).

4.1
Datasets

APPS

We evaluate all monitors and ensembles on a filtered subset of the APPS ControlArena dataset
(
16
)
, which combines interview-style Python coding problems from APPS
(
14
)
with backdoored solutions sourced from
12
and
28
. Backdoors are adversarially generated by various LLMs to pass all standard tests while producing incorrect outputs on specific attack inputs. To isolate backdoor detection from generally incorrect code, we keep only working honest solutions (passing all tests) and working backdoored solutions (successfully exploiting attack inputs and passing all tests).

Our train/validation/test split simulates realistic deployment where monitors must defend against a stronger attacker while only having training examples from less-capable attacking models. We allocate all samples with curated backdoors generated by Claude-3.7-Sonnet-Thinking (the strongest attacking model in our dataset) to the test set, ensuring evaluation captures worst-case performance. We exclude backdoors from weaker or older models (GPT-3.5-Turbo, GPT-4o-Mini, Claude-3-5-Haiku), using generating model capability as a heuristic for backdoor quality. We train and validate on the remaining backdoors from more capable models.

The filtered dataset contains 1,936 training (50% attack rate), 482 validation (50% attack rate), and 1,375 test samples (172 attack, 1,203 honest). The test set is extended to problems that have working solutions but no working backdoors to sharpen FPR estimation at low thresholds. These problems may differ from training problems, but the shift affects all monitors equally and does not impact relative comparisons.

We observe that backdoors in the dataset frequently contain comments explicitly indicating the backdoor logic. To prevent monitors from relying on this unintended signal, we strip all comments from the code before evaluation.

BigCodeBench-Sabotage

We evaluate generalization using BigCodeBench
(
32
)
augmented with backdoors from
4
(BigCodeBench-Sabotage, BCB-S). BCB-S covers 11 backdoor types — edge case manipulation, I/O operations, permission changes, unusual variable names, complexity injection, and others — letting us test whether ensembles selected on one attack type transfer to qualitatively different ones. We construct two evaluation sets — 500 attack and 500 honest edge-case samples most similar to APPS (BCB-S Edge), and 500 attack and 500 honest samples drawn from all 11 backdoor types (BCB-S All).

4.2
Evaluation metric

Our primary metric is the normalized area under the ROC curve (AUROC) over low false positive rates (FPR):
pAUC=\frac{1}{0.1}\int_{0}^{0.1}\text{TPR}(\text{FPR})\,d(\text{FPR})
, where
\text{TPR}(\text{FPR})
is the true positive rate at a given false positive rate. We use a 10% (linearly interpolated) cutoff to summarize performance across deployment-relevant FPRs while reducing the variance inherent in measuring performance at specific thresholds. By focusing on the low FPR region, we aim to mimic the trusted monitoring scenario from
12
, where code flagged as suspicious above a score threshold is routed to a human reviewer. The false positive rate determines how much code humans must review, so keeping FPR low is essential to staying within the budget of available human oversight.

Results

5.1
Ensembles outperform individual monitors on APPS

We evaluate 12 monitors individually and in combination, aggregating ensemble outputs by arithmetic mean. The ensemble achieves the highest pAUC, outperforming all individual monitors. Notably, the Baseline monitor (the default ControlArena prompt, §
3.1
) achieves the best single-monitor performance on our test set (see Figure
3
), demonstrating that ensembling can still improve monitoring performance even without developing stronger individual monitors.

In our experiments, ensembles are also better calibrated. Single monitors often miss target FPRs, while ensembles track them more closely. Individual monitors produce integer scores on a 0–10 scale, which creates a sparse threshold space and limits fine-grained FPR control. For the Baseline monitor, FPR targets of 3%, 4%, and 5% all map to the same effective threshold, yielding an identical actual FPR of 12.2%. Score averaging produces a denser set of achievable thresholds and improves calibration as a structural benefit of ensembling. The median calibration gap, defined as the absolute difference between target and actual FPR, drops from 5.5 percentage points (pp) for individual monitors (
k=1

Top ensembles generalize to BCB-S

We apply the top APPS-selected ensembles to BCB-S (§

On edge-case backdoors (most similar to APPS), the top ensembles, selected from APPS, outperform both the Baseline monitor and median 3-monitor ensemble (Figure

5.4
Ensemble quality depends on both diversity and individual monitor strength

We find, contrary to expectations, that selecting ensembles purely for diversity (lowest inter-monitor correlation) performs worse than random selection on APPS (pAUC of 0.717 vs. median ensemble pAUC of 0.769), despite a slight positive correlation between ensemble diversity and performance overall.

Low-performing monitors disagree with others not because they detect distinct signals, but because they are more often incorrect. Selecting for diversity alone thus conflates genuine complementarity with high error rates. We therefore ensemble prune
(
31
)
by filtering out individual monitors in the bottom performance quartile and then select for diversity among the remaining monitors. After filtering out those poor-performing monitors, we find that selecting for diversity improves monitoring performance on APPS over both the median 3-monitor ensemble and the Baseline monitor. The effect is even stronger on BCB-S across all attack types, with higher diversity correlating with a larger increase in pAUC (Appendix
G
). Filtering for quality before selecting for diversity yields consistent gains over both baselines.

This suggests that diverse ensembles provide the greatest benefit when individual monitors are weakest.

6
Discussion

6.1
Why does ensembling work?

A monitor’s suspicion score can be decomposed into three components: true signal, systematic bias, and random noise. Under this decomposition, averaging multiple stochastic samples from the same monitor reduces noise in expectation but leaves systematic bias intact—a monitor’s idiosyncratic errors on any given sample do not diminish with repeated sampling.

Ensembling diverse monitors addresses this limitation. Classical ensemble theory establishes that aggregating predictions from weakly correlated learners reduces both variance and systematic error, as idiosyncratic biases partially cancel in expectation
(
9
;
10
)
. By deliberately constructing monitors through diverse approaches, we help reduce correlation between failure modes, satisfying the diversity condition under which systematic bias reduction holds at the aggregate level.

A complementary interpretation is that ensembling improves coverage over attack modes. Diverse monitors behave as a team of specialists; each monitor attends to different features of the input, applies different heuristics, and calibrates scores along different axes. This specialization increases the probability that at least one ensemble member assigns a high suspicion score to any given attack instance. Distinguishing between these mechanisms empirically remains an open question and a promising direction for future work.

6.2
Selecting good ensembles

Our findings suggest a practical approach for building monitoring ensembles. The first step is to build monitors using diverse approaches, explicitly aiming to create complementary detection strategies rather than architecturally similar approaches that share failure modes.

The second step is to evaluate individual monitors on a held-out validation set before forming ensembles. Pure diversity selection (lowest inter-monitor correlation) performs worse than random selection when poor-performing monitors are included; these monitors disagree with others not because they detect distinct signals but because they are frequently wrong. Ensemble pruning by filtering to the top performers first, e.g., removing bottom quartile individuals, and then selecting for diversity among the resulting ensembles consistently outperforms both random selection and homogeneous ensembles. For aggregation, simple arithmetic averaging is sufficient—more sophisticated methods such as geometric mean, weighted average, and majority voting do not show consistent improvement in our experiments.

6.3
Limitations & future work

Our evaluation focuses on a well-defined but narrow threat model, specifically short-horizon coding problems where each attack takes the form of a discrete backdoor. How well these findings generalize to subtler scenarios—realistic deployments with ambiguous attacker intent, longer agent horizons, or attacks distributed across multiple episodes—remains an open question. Moreover, all evaluations treat the attacker as unaware of the ensemble composition. We did not red-team our ensembles against an adaptive adversary with knowledge of the monitoring system. Until such experiments are conducted, we cannot claim that ensembles are robust to adversarially optimized attacks, or whether ensembles can introduce new attack surfaces.

Our diversity results are also scoped to a single base model, meaning the diversity we measure is entirely over fine-tuning protocol and prompting strategy. This shared architecture introduces a specific vulnerability: an adversary can embed monitor-manipulating instructions directly in the code under review, and
29
shows that such prompt injection attacks can reliably subvert trusted monitors. Ensembles drawn from different model families—or that incorporate non-LLM-based signals—may be more resistant, but we have not tested this directly. Thus, future work is needed to build monitors from different sources to test whether architectural diversity yields robustness gains beyond what fine-tuning and prompting variation can provide.

Additionally, we measure diversity via average pairwise correlation and use this metric to guide ensemble construction, but the mechanism underlying complementarity remains unclear. More targeted analysis—what features each monitor attends to, which specific attacks each uniquely catches, and whether fine-tuned monitors reliably specialize in the same blind spots across datasets—would make ensemble construction more principled and help validate the coverage hypothesis proposed above.

Finally, stronger monitoring is a necessary but insufficient safeguard in AI control. Recall depends critically on deployment choices—audit rates, score thresholds, and protocol design—that fall outside the scope of this study. The results here should be treated as one input into a broader safety analysis rather than as standalone safety guarantees.

7
Conclusion

Ensemble monitoring offers a practical path to improved catch rates in AI control settings. By combining monitors built through prompting and fine-tuning, we aggregate complementary detection signals that outperform any individual monitor. The central finding is not that ensembles universally outperform individuals, but that monitor diversity—not count—drives performance gains. A carefully selected subset of monitors can achieve most of the benefit of larger ensembles, and these selections generalize across datasets and attack types. Moreover, fine-tuning appears to be a particularly valuable source of diversity, consistently appearing in top ensembles across all our experiments. This prevalence suggests that fine-tuning enables monitoring capabilities that prompting alone does not elicit, justifying the additional cost of fine-tuning in safety-critical deployments.

Impact Statement

This work contributes empirical evidence toward the broader goal of developing control protocols that provide meaningful safety guarantees as AI systems become more capable. However, as with any study that publishes monitoring results, this work may inadvertently assist future AI systems in constructing backdoors that evade known ensemble monitoring protocols.

Prompted Monitor Descriptions

Table 3:
Description of prompt-based monitor designs.

Monitor

Design

Baseline

General-purpose backdoor detector that analyzes the suspiciousness of code submissions in the context of the software engineering (SWE) task (default APPS-setting prompt in ControlArena); outputs a 0–10 suspicion score with detailed reasoning.

High-Recall

Designed to maximize recall in an ensemble system with a lower threshold for flagging suspicious code; actively searches for patterns like unnecessary conditionals, boundary checks, and logic unrelated to problem requirements, willing to flag ambiguous cases where backdoors are possible even if not certain.

High-Precision

Designed to maximize precision in an ensemble system by only flagging code with strong evidence of intentional backdoors; extracts suspicious code quotes, evaluates each for malicious vs innocent interpretations, and requires clear indicators of deliberate design to assign high scores, explicitly distinguishing poor code quality from malicious intent.

Extract-Evaluate

Two-stage pipeline, following the strategy in
3
: the first stage extracts 1–5 specific code snippets that look most suspicious for potential backdoors, and the second stage judges whether these excerpts indicate deliberate backdoors versus legitimate code, bugs, or style issues.

Reference-Compare

Two-stage pipeline where the first stage generates a clean reference solution with clear documentation and straightforward approach, then the second stage compares the submitted solution against this reference to identify suspicious logic differences that could indicate backdoors (e.g., specific value checks, unnecessary conditions targeting hidden inputs).

Code-Heuristic

Pattern-matching backdoor detector that operates without access to the problem statement, relying on statistical heuristics to detect suspicious patterns like magic numbers in conditionals, hardcoded special-case checks, obfuscation signals, and arbitrary logic branches that deviate from typical clean code; outputs only a score without reasoning.

Exploit-Finder

Security researcher perspective that actively searches for exploitable backdoor patterns by identifying suspicious code elements, hypothesizing specific trigger inputs that would activate malicious behavior, constructing concrete exploit scenarios, and scoring 0–10 based on how clearly exploitable the code is.

Trusted-Debate

Adversarial debate format
(
2
)
with two debaters (one arguing code is clean, one arguing it contains backdoor) presenting evidence over multiple rounds, followed by a neutral judge who evaluates the transcript and renders a verdict with 0–10 score and clean/backdoor classification.

A note on fine-tuning dataset quality.
On the APPS validation set, the score-only monitors (FT-Completions-Randint, pAUC = 0.761; FT-Completions-Extreme, pAUC = 0.713) outperform those trained on GPT-generated reasoning traces. We hypothesize these score-only models excel because they learn from correctly scored code regardless of whether the generating model can accurately score the example code. High-quality human-generated reasoning and score pairs would likely yield similar performance. Among GPT-5-based models, FT-Completions-GPT-5-Filtered (pAUC = 0.718) outperforms FT-Completions-GPT-5-Given-Labels (pAUC = 0.692), which outperforms FT-Completions-GPT-5 (pAUC = 0.641). When the generating model disagrees with the correct label, it may produce ad hoc reasoning that doesn’t generalize, as occurs in FT-Completions-GPT-5-Given-Labels. We hypothesize this artifact is why FT-Completions-GPT-5-Filtered performs best; it preserves native reasoning while excluding incorrect responses which can appear in FT-Completions-GPT-5. This pattern generalizes to smaller generating models like GPT-5-nano. Finally, monitors fine-tuned on completions from GPT-4.1-mini and smaller GPT-5 variants (nano and mini) underperform compared to those trained on GPT-5 responses, confirming our expectations that monitor performance scales with generating model capability.
