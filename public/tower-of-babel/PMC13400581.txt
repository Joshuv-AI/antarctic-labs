Economic benefits of robotic-assisted ventral hernia repair in India: an analysis of health utility, productivity, and operational gains from the real-world ASPIRE INDIA study
Authors: Randeep Wadhawan, Vivek Bindal, Pradeep Jain, Ashwinikumar Kudari, Sreedhara V. Setty, Kanika Jain, Udipta Ray, Vijaykumar C. Bada, Vishal Soni, Biswabasu Das, Ramkaran Chaudhary, Naveen Sharma, Sudhir Sharma, Akhil Dahiya, Rajveer Bhaskar, Isha Sharma, Prabhakar Pandey, Pradeep Chowbey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study estimated the economic benefits of robotic-assisted ventral hernia repair (RVHR) using individual patient data (IPD) from a prospective multicenter study, the ASPIRE INDIA. The 30-day quality-adjusted life-years (QALY) gain was estimated from the EuroQol five-dimension three-level questionnaire scores (EQ-5D-3L), with uncertainty quantified via non-parametric bootstrap resampling. Productivity gains were estimated by wages drawn from a lognormal distribution fitted to Periodic Labour Force Survey (PLFS) 2023-24 data, adjusted to FY2026-27. A joint Monte Carlo (MC) simulation propagated uncertainty across all stochastic inputs. The results were scaled to an annual cohort of 300,000 cases. All assumptions were tested through sensitivity analyses. At 300,000 annual cases, the mean total benefit of RVHR over laparoscopic VHR (LVHR) was INR 2,799 million (95% confidence interval [CI]: INR 846–5,001) at 1x willingness-to-pay (WTP; INR 210,000/QALY) and INR 2,959 million (95% CI: INR 1,010−5,154 million) at 2x WTP. Post-anesthesia care unit (PACU) time saved represented the largest component of estimated economic benefit, which accounted for 65.8% of the total benefit at 1x WTP, followed by patient productivity (19.0%), caregiver productivity (9.5%), and QALY gain (5.7%). These findings identify the principal economic benefit dimensions for RVHR and provide a quantitative basis for structured value discussions at the institutional level regarding robotic-assisted surgery (RAS) adoption that should be considered alongside cost data in future comprehensive economic evaluations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11701-026-03721-x.

Introduction

Ventral hernias are abdominal wall defects at prior surgical sites, which typically present as a visible or palpable abdominal protrusion, often with exertional discomfort, and maybe complicated by incarceration or strangulation [1]. In the United States, ventral hernia repairs (VHR) have doubled from 350,000 procedures annually in 2006 to 611,000, incurring direct healthcare costs exceeding United States dollar (USD) 9.7 billion [2]. In India, VHR is among the most frequently performed elective abdominal procedures, however, a credible burden estimate is not available [3, 4].

Minimally invasive approaches, initially laparoscopic and later robotic-assisted surgery (RAS), have largely supplanted open surgeries, driven by favorable perioperative outcomes [5]. However, evidence comparing robotic and laparoscopic approaches in VHR remains inconclusive. A randomized trial of 175 patients demonstrated that 2-year recurrence and reoperation rate with RAS was lower versus laparoscopic VHR (LVHR) [6]. A 5-year analysis from the Abdominal Core Health Quality Collaborative (ACHQC) database showed that patients with robotic-assisted VHR (RVHR) had higher operative time while there was no difference in 30-days readmission rates, recurrence, reoperation, postoperative complications versus LVHR [7]. Another prospective multicenter study from India demonstrated that RVHR was associated with lower operative pain scores, earlier return to activities of daily living (ADL), reduced analgesic consumption, and superior short-term quality of life (QoL) versus LVHR [8].

In India, RAS adoption has increased over the past decade, driven by surgeon training programs and institutional investment in robotic platforms [9], however, the cost of RAS remains a concern for broader adoption. Existing economic evidence on RVHR is geographically concentrated in high-income settings, with most studies not relying on cost-per-quality-adjusted life-years (QALY) analyses and choosing a variety of outcomes or broader value-assessments [5, 10–19]. Healthcare financing in India is fragmented across insurers, employer-sponsored health benefits, government schemes, and substantial out-of-pocket expenditure [20]. Consequently, adoption and financing decisions are influenced by multiple stakeholders including hospitals, insurers, surgeons, and self-paying patients, each of whom may value operational efficiency, resource utilization, recovery, productivity restoration, and caregiver burden, rather than relying exclusively on a single cost-per-QALY metric. Therefore, the present study quantifies these stakeholder-relevant economic benefit dimensions with RVHR using individual patient data (IPD) from the ASPIRE INDIA study – a prospective, real-world study conducted across 12 Indian centers [8].

Methods

Study design and analytical framework

This analysis is a quantification of economic benefits and does not seek to determine whether RVHR is cost-effective relative to LVHR. The following four components were analyzed: (1) a 30-day QALY gain derived from EQ-5D-3L utility trajectories, (2) a productivity gain from earlier return to ADL, 3) productivity gain for caregivers due to patients’ earlier return to ADL, 4) and shorter length of post-anesthesia care units (PACU) time. These components were combined in a unified Monte Carlo (MC) simulation and the result scaled to national annual surgical cohorts [8]. A multi-stakeholder perspective and these components were chosen considering different value drivers for patients, employers, households, private insurers, and hospital administrators.

Data sources

Clinical data

The relevant IPD used in the present analysis that was sourced from the ASPIRE INDIA [8] included EQ-5D-3L index scores at Day 14 and Day 30 post-surgery (RVHR: n = 100; LVHR: n = 95 with complete utility data), time to return to ADL in days (RVHR: n = 101; LVHR: n = 98); and PACU duration in minutes (RVHR: n = 101; LVHR: n = 98). All analyses were performed on existing IPD under the data use provisions of the original ASPIRE INDIA ethics approvals, which was registered with the Clinical Trials Registry of India (CTRI/2024/03/064267) [8]. Complete-case analysis was used and only patients with non-missing data were included.

Wage data

Wage data for the productivity gain estimation were sourced from the Periodic Labour Force Survey (PLFS) 2023-24 conducted by the Ministry of Statistics and Programme Implementation, Government of India [21]. The analytical sample comprised regular salaried workers in urban areas. To adjust PLFS 2023-24 wages to the FY2026-27 reference period, a Consumer Price Index for Industrial Workers (CPI-IW) inflation factor of (1.055)2 was applied, consistent with the average annual all-India CPI-IW increase of approximately 5.5% over the two-year period.

QALY gain (component 1)

Utility trajectory and area under the curve (AUC) computation

The per-patient QALY gain attributable to RVHR over LVHR was computed using the trapezoid AUC method. This method treats the utility trajectory as a piecewise linear function connecting observed values at successive time points and computes the enclosed area. Day 0 utility (surgery day) was not measured in the ASPIRE INDIA study; hence was assumed as a shared value for both RVHR and LVHR, consistent with the statistically comparable pre-operative utility scores between groups (RVHR: 0.79 ± 0.18, LVHR: 0.79 ± 0.19; p = 0.90). The pre-operative utility score was not used directly as the Day 0 value because it reflects the patient’s health state prior to surgery, not the immediate post-surgical state. The trapezoid AUC was computed as the sum of two segments: Day 0 to Day 14 (width 14 days) and Day 14 to Day 30 (width 16 days), divided by 365 to convert day-utility units to QALYs. The incremental QALY gain (ΔQALY) was defined as AUCRVHR - AUCLVHR per patient over 30 days.

Uncertainty quantification

The Day 30 utility distribution in the RVHR group exhibited a pronounced ceiling effect as approximately 94% of patients attained the maximum EQ-5D-3L index score of 1.0. This renders standard parametric confidence intervals (CI) inappropriate. Therefore, uncertainty in ΔQALY was quantified using non-parametric bootstrap resampling of IPD (10,000-iterations). At each iteration, RVHR and LVHR patients were resampled independently with replacement, mean utilities were computed, and ΔQALY was derived via the trapezoid formula. The empirical 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the resulting bootstrap distribution constitute the 95% CI.

Patient productivity gain (component 2)

Productivity loss and gain were calculated using the Human Capital Approach (HCA), whereby the monetary value of working time saved is estimated as the product of working days recovered earlier and the prevailing market daily wage rate [22].

Productivity endpoint

Return-to-work (RTW) estimate in ASPIRE INDIA was not statistically significant between arms (p = 0.06), which precluded their use as the primary productivity endpoint. Time to return to ADL was therefore used as the primary productivity endpoint (RVHR versus LVHR, median: 5 days versus 7 days; p < 0.001; n = 199) [8]. ADL served as a proxy for the functional restoration of work capacity, on the grounds that the ability to resume daily physical activities is a prerequisite for return to productive employment while bad health and physical limitations are a significant impediment to employment and directly reduce participation rates [23].

ADL difference distribution

Uncertainty in the ADL recovery differential was propagated stochastically via bootstrap resampling. At each iteration, RVHR and LVHR ADL times were resampled independently with replacement, and the per-iteration mean difference was used as the productivity input. This yielded a bootstrapped mean ADL advantage of 3.01 days (95% CI: 1.79–4.26), consistent with the right-skewed distribution of recovery times.

Wage parameterization

Daily wages were sampled at each MC iteration from a lognormal distribution (µ = 9.81, σ = 0.78) fitted to PLFS 2023-24 data [21]. A 26-working-day month was assumed to convert monthly earnings to daily rates, consistent with standard Indian labor accounting [24]. Productivity gain per employed patient at each iteration was computed as the bootstrapped ADL difference multiplied by the sampled daily wage.

Employment fraction

The productivity gain applies to patients in paid employment at the time of surgery. An employment fraction of 55% was applied to the analytic cohort as the base case, derived from the proportion of ASPIRE INDIA patients in employment (those with non-missing RTW data [RVHR: 57%; LVHR: 49%; weighted average conservatively rounded to 55%]) [8]. The same employment fraction was applied separately to the caregiver productivity component; both fractions were set identically at base case and varied jointly in sensitivity analyses.

Caregiver productivity gain (component 3)

An informal caregiver productivity component was incorporated to capture the indirect economic cost imposed on working caregivers during the patient’s recovery period. It was assumed that 50% of patients are supported by a working-age primary caregiver during the recovery period, who, when in paid employment, takes time away from work equal in duration to the patient’s ADL recovery advantage. The caregiver’s daily wage was sampled at each MC iteration from the same lognormal wage distribution applied to the patient component. Both patient and caregiver employment fractions were varied across sensitivity scenarios (base: 55%; low: 45%; high: 65%). The raw per-iteration caregiver productivity gain was computed as the bootstrapped ADL difference multiplied by the sampled caregiver daily wage; the caregiver prevalence and employment fraction were then applied to yield the per-case caregiver component. No QALY gain was attributed to caregivers.

PACU time saved (component 4)

RVHR was associated with a significantly shorter PACU time compared with LVHR in the ASPIRE INDIA study (median; Interquartile Range [IQR]: 80 min; 55–120 versus 103; 65–360; p = 0.003) [8]. The PACU time difference was bootstrapped from IPD at each MC iteration (resampling RVHR and LVHR PACU durations independently with replacement). The base case rate was INR 5,000 per hour; sensitivity analyses tested INR 3,000 per hour (low) and INR 7,000 per hour (high). The base case estimate was informed by informal consultations with hospital administrators and is representative of the cost in tertiary care facilities in India. The sensitivity bounds span the range from public and trust-hospital costs to higher-acuity private metropolitan settings.

Simulation structure

A joint MC simulation of 10,000 iterations (random seed 2026) was used to propagate uncertainty across all model inputs. ΔQALY was drawn by resampling with replacement from the empirical bootstrap distribution derived in the QALY analysis. The ADL recovery advantage was estimated by independently bootstrapping RVHR and LVHR ADL data and calculating the mean difference for that iteration. A daily wage was drawn from the fitted lognormal wage distribution. The ADL draw was applied jointly to both the patient and caregiver productivity components at each iteration, consistent with the assumption that caregiver time released maps directly to the patient’s recovery advantage. PACU time saved was derived at each iteration by independently bootstrapping PACU duration IPD from both arms and multiplying the resulting per-iteration difference by the applicable PACU rate. All four stochastic inputs were treated as mutually independent, consistent with the absence of empirical correlation data across the distinct populations and data sources from which each is derived.

Willingness-to-pay (WTP) threshold

In the absence of a formally adopted WTP threshold for health interventions in India, four scenarios were tested based on India’s FY2026 gross domestic product (GDP) per capita (approximately INR 210,000): 1×, 1.5×, 2×, and 3× GDP per capita, corresponding to INR 210,000; INR 315,000; INR 420,000; and INR 630,000 per QALY, respectively [25]. This is consistent with the approach recommended by WHO for settings without established WTP thresholds [26].

Cohort scaling

The composite benefit was computed for annual surgical cohort volumes of 100,000 cases, 200,000 cases, and 300,000 VHR cases per year, with 300,000 representing the most plausible national estimate (please refer to Online Resource 1 for the justification). QALY gains and PACU savings scale with total case volume while patient and caregiver productivity gains scale with case volume multiplied by the applicable employment fraction.

Sensitivity and scenario analyses

One-way sensitivity analyses were conducted at 300,000 cases per year and 1× WTP scenario, varying the patient and caregiver employment fraction jointly (45% and 65%) and the PACU rate (INR 3,000/hr and INR 7,000/hr), with all other parameters held at base case values. The Day 0 utility assumption (0.50, 0.60, 0.65) was tested as a secondary sensitivity confined to the QALY component. Because Day 0 utility was assumed identical for both groups, it shifts both AUC trajectories equally, leaving ΔQALY driven entirely by the observed Day 14 and Day 30 divergence between groups (Online Resource 2).

All analyses were conducted in R (version 4.4) using the tidyverse, data.table, readxl, ggplot2, and scales packages. A fixed random seed (2026) was applied prior to all stochastic operations to ensure reproducibility.

Results

QALY gain

The 30-day postoperative per-patient QALY gain attributable to RVHR over LVHR was 0.0046 (95% CI: 0.0026–0.0066) (Fig. 1).

The Day 0 utility assumption had a negligible influence on ΔQALY as the conservative scenario (Day 0 = 0.50) yielded a mean of 0.00462 and the optimistic scenario (Day 0 = 0.65) yielded 0.00461, with overlapping CIs across all three scenarios (Online Resource 2).

Patient productivity gain

The bootstrapped mean ADL recovery advantage for RVHR over LVHR was 3.01 days (95% CI: 1.79–4.26 days). Applying national urban regular salaried wage draws from the PLFS 2023-24, the mean per-employed-patient productivity gain was INR 3,229 (95% CI: INR 470−11,443) per iteration of the MC simulation.

Caregiver productivity gain

Applying the 50% caregiver prevalence assumption and a 55% caregiver employment fraction, the mean per-case caregiver productivity gain was INR 884 (95% CI: INR 129–3,160).

PACU time saved

Bootstrapping the PACU duration IPD across 10,000 iterations, the mean per-case PACU time saved with RVHR over LVHR was 73.7 min (95% CI: 2.4–142.5). Valued at the base case rate of INR 5,000 per hour, the mean per-case PACU saving was INR 6,139 (95% CI: INR 202−11,874).

Composite benefit

At the base case (1x WTP, INR 210,000 per QALY; employment fraction 55%; PACU rate INR 5,000/hr), the mean composite benefit per case was INR 9,331 (95% CI: INR 2,821−16,669). At the reference cohort of 300,000 annual cases and 1x WTP, the mean total benefit was INR 2,799 million (95% CI: INR 846–5,001). At 1.5x WTP, the estimate was INR 2,879 million (95% CI: INR 930–5,078); at 2x WTP, INR 2,959 million (95% CI: INR 1,010−5,154), and at 3x WTP, INR 3,119 (95% CI: INR 1,176-5,317) (Fig. 2).

Sensitivity analyses

All sensitivity analyses were conducted at 300,000 annual cases and 1x WTP. Varying the employment fraction from the base case of 55% to 45% reduced total composite benefit to INR 2,625 million (95% CI: INR 729–4,668), a decrease of INR 174 million. Increasing the employment fraction to 65% raised total composite benefit to INR 2,973 million (95% CI: INR 974–5,331). Variation in the PACU rate had a more pronounced effect on total composite benefit, consistent with this component’s dominant share. At the low rate of INR 3,000 per hour, total composite benefit was INR 2,063 million (95% CI: INR 753–3,815), a reduction of INR 736 million from the base case. At the high rate of INR 7,000 per hour, total composite benefit increased to INR 3,536 million (95% CI: INR 923–6,274), an increase of INR 737 million (Fig. 3, Online Resource 3).

Discussion

This analysis estimates economic benefits of RVHR in India using patient-level data from a prospective multicenter study [8]. The present analysis is neither a justification for the incremental cost of RAS, nor is evidence supporting reimbursement or coverage decision. The methodological rationale for this approach reflects the contemporary Indian healthcare environment, in which financing and adoption decisions are distributed across multiple stakeholders rather than governed by a single national payer. The analysis therefore provides a portfolio of economic benefits, which may inform institutional and payer discussions regarding RAS. Conceptually, the present framework is similar in its approach with broader value assessments, which recognize multiple dimensions of value [27]. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that our analysis does not consider the incremental cost of RVHR, and a comprehensive CEA remains necessary to inform reimbursement decision from a public payer’s perspective [27].

The principal strengths of this analysis are its grounding in prospective Indian patient-level data, its multi-component structure, its use of probabilistic methods that propagate uncertainty from all stochastic inputs, and its use of Indian wage and volume parameters. The assumptions are transparently stated and tested in sensitivity analyses, all of which preserved the directional conclusion. Uncertainty in model parameters was quantified via non-parametric bootstrap resampling rather than parametric CIs. This ensured internal consistency across all stochastic inputs and avoided imposing distributional assumptions on data with small sample sizes. Wages were the sole component for which a lognormal parametric distribution was fitted, as wage distributions are well established to follow lognormal behavior, and the large sample size supporting the fit (approximately 300,000 observations nationally) justifies the parametric approach.

Earlier return to functional activity and the caregiver component generates a direct economic benefit to employed individuals and their households. The combined patient and caregiver productivity gain amounts to approximately INR 2,660 per case, which maybe modest at the individual level but accumulates meaningfully at population scale.

The PACU component was the largest benefit contributor. Although the median PACU difference observed in ASPIRE INDIA was 23 min per case (80 versus 103 min), the mean reduction estimated in the present analysis was larger (73.7 min). This reflects the markedly right-skewed distribution of PACU duration in the LVHR group, where a subset of patients experienced substantially prolonged recovery times. Consequently, the mean difference, which drives resource utilization and cost estimation, exceeded the median difference. At the base case PACU rate, RVHR was associated with a mean per-case PACU saving of approximately INR 6,139, which is about two-thirds of the total benefit per case. This highlights, beyond clinical outcomes, operational efficiency represents an important value driver for institutions considering RAS platforms. Additionally, in India’s predominantly private healthcare system, reductions in PACU utilization may generate downstream financial benefits through lower out-of-pocket expenditure and reduced insurer reimbursement costs.

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the four value components were treated as independent in the probabilistic analysis because data describing their interrelationships were unavailable. Consequently, uncertainty may have been conservatively estimated if positive correlations exist between components. Second, although, the trapezoid AUC is a common method for estimating QALYs from repeated utility measurements [28], the absence of utility measurements between Day 0 and Day 14 limits the precision of the AUC estimation in the early postoperative period, when recovery trajectories maybe nonlinear. Third, the use of ADL recovery as productivity proxy may have overestimated productive days regained for patients in physically demanding occupations whose work requirements exceed ADL functional thresholds. The wide CI around the per-patient productivity gain reflects uncertainty in this component. Fourth, caregiver prevalence, their employment fraction, and time-loss mapping were modeling assumptions; in absence of any Indian surgical caregiving data. While the base case caregiver employment fraction (55%) was assumed equal to the patient’s employment fraction from ASPIRE INDIA IPD, and closely approximated India’s employment-to-population ratio (~53%), the caregiver prevalence (50%) was a conservative assumption. This assumption, though justifiable, was still cautious for the Indian setting, where informal family caregiving is the cultural norm following surgical procedures. The assumption nevertheless allows for the substantial minority of patients who are elderly, live alone, or supported by a non-working-age caregiver. The caregiver time foregone was also assumed equal to the duration of patient functional dependency, which is another modeling simplification used in this analysis. We acknowledge that actual caregiver time commitments may differ from this assumption, and we may have overestimated caregiver productivity. The employment fraction (55%) may restrict the productivity gain, partially correcting for overestimation. Fifth, productivity derived from PLFS wages was also a conservative approach since PLFS may not fully represent the socioeconomic profile of patients accessing RAS at urban tertiary care centers, where incomes are likely above the national urban salaried median. Lastly, the PACU cost estimate also carries uncertainty as it was informed by informal consultation with hospital administrators since no unit cost estimates for PACU time are publicly available. The estimate of INR 5,000 per hour, while representative of private tertiary facilities, may not be accurate for all settings. Therefore, the sensitivity range of INR 3,000–7,000 per hour was designed to span the cost gradient from public facility to high-acuity private metropolitan hospital rates; all conclusions hold directionally across this range.

Nonetheless, in absence of a single payer in India [25], this analysis makes the findings interpretable to several decision-makers. The health utility component is relevant to patients and advocates; productivity gains are relevant to individuals, employers, and households; and PACU time savings are relevant to hospitals evaluating operational efficiency, which also likely have downstream patient benefits. The aggregate estimate should therefore be interpreted as a composite representation of economic benefits across stakeholders and not as value accruing to any individual decision-maker. Since, the analysis was conducted using ASPIRE INDIA data, the findings are most applicable to patients accessing RVHR at similar institutions and should not be generalized to public-sector, secondary-care, or rural settings, where demographics, costs, and access differ.

Conclusion

This analysis suggests that the benefits of RVHR in India are attributed to gains in recovery, caregiver burden, and operational efficiency. The dominant contributor is the reduction in PACU time. These findings provide a quantitative basis for structured value discussions at the institutional level, rather than a definitive cost-effectiveness determination.
