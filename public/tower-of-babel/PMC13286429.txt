Safety Evaluation of the RibXcar Technique Using the Piezotome: An Analysis of Surgical Complications
Authors: Mauricio E. Perez Pachon, Alfredo E. Hoyos Ariza
Published: 2026

This letter aims to express significant methodological and statistical concerns regarding the article, “Safety Evaluation of the RibXcar Technique Using the Piezotome: An Analysis of Surgical Complications,” by Cipriani et al.1 Although we appreciate the authors’ attempt to investigate this novel procedure, the study’s design and interpretation of data lead to conclusions that are, in our professional opinion, misleading and scientifically unfounded.

STUDY DESIGN, TITLE, AND SCOPE

We find the article title misleading. “Analysis of Surgical Complications” implies a cohort or case-series design with inferential statistics, yet the authors explicitly used a retrospective survey of 113 plastic surgeons. A surgeon survey is not an appropriate methodology to establish a procedure’s safety profile and is inherently vulnerable to recall and self-reporting bias.2 The authors themselves acknowledge that the retrospective design precludes causal inference; accordingly, the title should be revised to reflect a descriptive survey rather than an analytical complications study. Statistical claims are also unsupported. Table 2 indicates that 100% of respondents used a piezotome; with no comparison group, tests of association (eg, chi-square, logistic regression) cannot be performed. A reported P value of 1.0 is therefore not meaningful in a monotone dataset. Moreover, although the Methods section mention chi-square tests and logistic regression, their application is not demonstrated in the Results, apart from the unsubstantiated P value in the abstract. Finally, many tables do not state the denominator (n), and percentages in Tables 1–3 often fail to sum to 100%, suggesting multiple responses and/or missing data that are not explained. Clear denominators, response-option footnotes (single versus multiple choice), and explicit handling of missingness are required for statistical validity. Until these issues are corrected—and the title, methods, and claims aligned—the article’s conclusions about safety are not supported by its own data.

STATISTICAL ANALYSIS AND INTERPRETATION OF COMPLICATION RATES

Although the text cites a “low incidence of serious complications (2.6%)” and a “favorable safety profile,” summing major complications in Table 3 yields 9.0% (see table 1, Supplemental Digital Content 1, which shows an analysis of complications using data drawn from Table 3 of the article, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/F26); combined with the “None” category (90.03%), totals reach only 99.03%, leaving 0.97% unaccounted for. Moreover, a 9%–10% major complication rate (including 6.1% pneumothorax and 0.8% hemothorax) cannot reasonably be characterized as favorable, and describing these as “isolated cases” is misleading. Methodologically, the attribution scheme conflates life-threatening events (hemo/pneumothorax) with non–life-threatening outcomes (eg, prolonged pain), confounding risk interpretation, and the finding that 24.5% of complications were attributed to “surgeon factors” warrants deeper analysis rather than reassurance. In scientific reporting, tables and figures are the primary evidence; the narrative must align with them. Here, the mismatch between the 2.6% claim and the tabulated 9.0% rate undermines the credibility of the safety conclusion and necessitates correction and reanalysis with transparent denominators and appropriately stratified outcomes.

AESTHETIC RESULTS CLAIMS

The claim that “surgeons achieved aesthetically favorable and satisfactory results for patients” is not supported by the presented data, and is not a clear purpose of the article. The survey captured no patient-reported outcome measures or validated satisfaction scales; all assessments were surgeon self-reports, introducing recall and random bias. The only related item, “lack of good aesthetic result” (11.5%), appears as a minor complication and cannot substitute for a validated aesthetic or satisfaction measure. No standardized, blinded photographic assessment or objective scale (eg, global aesthetic improvement scale, BREAST-Q, anchored Likert) was used. Accordingly, conclusions about efficacy (aesthetic outcomes) and safety are unsupported. We recommend revising the title to reflect a descriptive surgeon survey and reanalyzing results with clear denominators, defined endpoints, and, prospectively, validated patient reported outcomes and blinded objective assessments.

We respectfully believe that PRS Global Open should solicit a formal rebuttal from the authors and, if the discrepancies are confirmed, publish an erratum clarifying the complication rates and methodological limitations. Alternatively, acceptance should be contingent on a revised title, transparent denominators, and reanalysis aligned with the tabulated data.

DISCLOSURE
