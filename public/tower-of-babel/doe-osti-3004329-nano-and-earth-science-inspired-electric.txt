ABSTRACT
With the expected growth of electric vehicles (EV) in the transportation sector, recycling or upcycling
spent battery materials will be required to reduce EV costs, lessen waste, and ease critical material
supply chain pressure for EV battery applications. “Direct” recycling or upcycling is an alternative to
current industrial approaches. Many challenges remain for viable direct battery upcycling, including
upgrading the transition metal ratio in the costliest component (cathode oxide); separation of
transition metal ions in the exit stream; battery disassembly; improving cathode exfoliation which can
lead to a new way of direct recycling; and lithium metal anode recycling -- including safety concerns.
We devise and deploy a suite of methods to deal with these challenges. The results pave the way to
future work on battery recycling, in areas of technological/commercial readiness level ranging from
basic research to large-scale commercialization.

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
This project focused on four major areas associated with battery recycling research: (1) battery
disassembly (separating cathode active material from binders and conductive carbon); (2) cathode
active material upcycling via exfoliation into nanosheets and cation exchange; (3) transition metal
cation separation in the exit stream; and (4) lithium metal anode recycling and related safety concerns.
(1)For disassembly, a froth floatation instrument was acquired, and a protocol that included
soaking/heating overnight was devised. The method currently works better for pristine than cycled
battery cathodes. We note that protocols published in the literature either do not fully work or require
converting the cathode into black mass at high temperature. (Chapter 5)
(2)For cation exchange, ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) free energy calculations were used to
demonstrate that Ni(II) <-> Co(II) cation exchange in LixCoO2 nanosheets in aqueous media is
kinetically viable and only slightly thermodynamically unfavorable. ~10% exchange of Ni into
exfoliated LixCoO2 is demonstrated experimentally, and the resulting material sintered into cathode
active material was demonstrated to exhibit reasonable cycling behavior, although the capacity, cycle
life, and % Ni intake all need to be improved upon. Ni insertion into exfoliated NMC(111) nanosheets
was also demonstrated experimentally. Preliminary, static DFT calculations suggested that Mn
exchange for Co in LixCoO2 nanosheets should be as viable as Ni exchange, but experimental results
have so far yet to demonstrate Co replacement with Mn. This suggests other factors (e.g., Mn(II)
solubility in solution at a given pH range) should be considered. A Sandia-specific, microwave-based
exfoliation approach was demonstrated. (Chapters 1-2, 6)
(3)Using functionalized zirconium-based metal-organic frameworks (MOF) MOF-808, Ni(II) was
selectively adsorbed from aqueous media over Co(II) by a factor of three. The selectivity of Mn(II),
relevant to NMC upcycling., was also considered. (Chapters 3-4)
(4)In a SUPN seed project, recycling of lithium metal anode in Li-S batteries is studied, using reactions
of N2 and O2 gases with Li metal. Li + N2 goes to completion in a few hours at T=180 oC and a few
days at T=300 oC. In contrast, O2 passivates Li metal under ambient conditions and is unsuitable for
recovering lithium. Density Functional Theory calculations were conducted to accompany this
experimental work. (Chapter 7)

1. COBALT-NICKEL EXCHANGE IN EXFOLIATED BATTERY LAYERED
 CATHODE SHEETS – DFT CALCULATIONS

1.1. Introduction
 Battery recycling and upcycling will help secure a domestic supply chain for battery materials and
help lower electric vehicle (EV) costs [1-5]. In terms of materials cost, the most valuable parts of EV
batteries are the cathode active materials made from transition metal oxides, which contain not only
lithium, but also cobalt and other transition metals, namely manganese (Mn) and nickel (Ni). Currently
industrial scale battery recycling relies on pyro- and hydro-metallurgical methods, which use high
temperature and acid leaching processes, respectively, to break down the entire oxide and recover the
elemental metal contents [1-4]. These processes tend to be energy intensive and/or produce significant
waste products. A recent promising direct recycling approach replenishes the lost lithium content in
spent battery materials [6-15]. It drastically reduces the energy input to reusing spent battery cathode
materials, but does not change the transition metal stoichiometry in the cathode oxides, unless further
reactions with additional transition metal reagents are performed [16-20]. This is an issue because in
LiNixMnyCozO2 (NMC), the cathode material used in >50% of EV battery cathode worldwide in
2023, the transition metal ratio Ni:Mn:Co is continually changing [21]. Currently NMC622 (60% Ni,
20% Mn, 20% Co) [21] and possibly NMC811 are supplanting NMC111 and NMC532 previously
installed in EV’s, while the older, Co-rich cathodes, including consumer electronics batteries that used
LiCoO2 (LCO), are reaching end-of-life. The reduction of cobalt content helps lessen the reliance on
a critical material element currently mined in conflict regions [22], while the increase of Ni-content
raises lithium utilization and the operating voltage, thus increasing the energy density [23]. Finally,
doping NMC with metals like Al3+ to further improve cycling properties is the subject of active
research [24]. Such dopants may be incorporated into future cathodes at industrial scale, making it
important to include them in upcycling schemes. This suggests that there is room for basic science
research into alternate recycling routes [6,7,25,26], particularly if the transition metal oxide
stoichiometry can be altered in the process. Specifically, in the case of LCO or Co-rich NMCs, this
means approaches to exchange the Co with Ni and other metal elements may allow for upcycling of
the cathode materials and recovery of Co. Fundamental science studies can further lead to the design
of future battery cathodes with built-in recycling pathways.

1.2 Results
 First we use electronic structure modeling methods to show that cation exchange is favored after
exfoliation of LCO or NMC materials into nanosheets. This primarily computational work was
published in Batteries and Supercaps 2025 [27], by LDRD members Kevin Leung, Clare Davis-
Wheeler Chin, and Candace K. Chan; here we give a brief description.
 Motivated by the layered oxide exfoliation work in the literature, we perform first principles
calculations to show that LixCoO2, if exfoliated into nanosheets, can readily undergo transition metal
cation exchange in aqueous media. The methods used are static DFT and dynamic AIMD plus
potential-of-mean-force free energy calculations (Figure 1.1).
 The substitution of Co(III) cations inside the sheet by Ni(II) is associated with modest reaction
barriers (G*~0.3 to 0.6 eV) and is at most mildly endothermic (G~0.2 to 0.3 eV). See Figure 1.2.
In contrast, previous battery degradation studies have shown that cobalt diffusion is strongly inhibited
inside bulk layered oxides. This suggests a potentially low-energy-cost, exfoliation-enabled pathway
to altering the transition metal and/or dopant stoichiometry of spent layered oxides using room-
temperature upcycling route without complete destruction of the oxide structure.

 Finally, we note that the original motivation for moving transition metal ions into and out of

2. DEMONSTRATION OF CO/NI EXCHANGE IN LICOO2 NANOSHEETS:
 A NEW APPROACH FOR UPCYCLING SPENT LI-ION BATTERY
 CATHODES

2.1 Abstract
In response to growing concerns over the limited availability of cobalt sources for lithium-ion battery
cathodes, this study explores a novel method for upcycling LiCoO2 (LCO) materials. Traditional
approaches for LCO recycling, such as pyrometallurgy and hydrometallurgy, are known for their
energy-intensive processes. Here, we propose a new direct upcycling approach that leverages the high
surface area of LCO nanosheets to facilitate cobalt extraction and cation exchange. The method
involves exfoliation of the LCO into nanosheets, where are then mixed with aqueous solutions to
promote cation exchange. The effectiveness of cobalt extraction and its replacement by nickel is
investigated using various characterization techniques, including Raman spectroscopy, X-ray
photoelectron spectroscopy, scanning electron microscopy, transmission electron microscopy, X-ray
powder diffraction and soft X-ray absorption spectroscopy. In addition, the reassembly of the
nanosheets into functional cathode materials is also accomplished using heat treatment. The results
show the successful synthesis of Ni-doped LCO (LiCo1-xNixO2, x = 0.11) using this approach.
Moreover, Mn ion exchange into the exfoliated LCO is also investigated with the same method. This
study establishes how nanosheet processing can be leveraged for recycling critical materials used in
batteries and establishes a new pathway that can be used for converting LCO or low-Co materials to
Ni-rich cathodes.

2.2 Introduction
 Lithium cobalt oxide (LiCoO2, LCO), the first intercalation cathode (reported by Goodenough
et al. in 1980) [1] as well as the cathode used in the first Li-ion batteries (commercialized by Sony, in
1991) [2] is still widely utilized today in portable electronic devices such as smartphones, tablets,
laptops, and drones [3]. However, the performance of LCO cathodes can diminish over time due to
issues such as cobalt dissolution and structural instability [4]. Additionally, the high cost and limited
supply of cobalt [5] drives the search for alternatives, particularly in applications such as transportation
where large format batteries are required.
 In recent years, the recovery of cathode materials from spent batteries has gained attention as
an alternative to massive landfilling of batteries that have reached end-of-life or new mining of critical
minerals, since both scenarios have significant impacts to human health and the environment [6, 7].
Conventional recycling strategies involve extraction of cobalt ions after decomposing the LCO using
high temperature (pyrometallurgy) or acid leaching (hydrometallurgy) treatments, followed by their re-
synthesis into cathode precursors and ultimately the LCO itself. In contrast to these destructive
processes, direct recycling [8] aims to preserve, as much as possible, the LCO materials while
implementing processing steps that replenish the lithium content and heal structural defects.
Compared to these recycling approaches, which aim to regenerate the spent LCO, upcycling attempts
to convert LCO into a different compound with added value. Some example products of LCO
upcycling that have been reported include electro/photocatalysts, solid lubricants, pseudocapacitor
electrodes, and magnetic materials [9].
 Since the cost of LCO is prohibitive for its use in electric vehicle (EV) applications, upcycling
LCO to other layered cathode compounds such as LiNiMnCoO2 (NMC) or LiNiCoAlO2 (NCA) is an
attractive way to deal with spent LCO waste while providing Co that can be used in for NMC (NCA).
The upcycling of LCO electrodes through nickel doping represents a significant advancement in

 14

battery technology, with substantial implications for both performance enhancement and
sustainability [10, 11]. Introducing nickel into the LCO structure to form LiNi1/3Mn1/3Co1/3O2 leads to
higher structural stability and specific capacity [12], while Mn dopants enhance the thermal stability
[13], which is essential for the safety and reliability of lithium-ion batteries used in EVs and large-scale
energy storage systems.
 Several studies have reported LCO upcycling to NMC (NCA) using various strategies. Yu et
al. [14] studied a mechanochemical approach for upcycling spent LCO, using ball-milling and then
acetic acid treatment to leach cobalt and then co-precipitate it with Ni and Al ions to form NCA.
Zheng et al. [15] used pyrolysis (chlorination roasting) and then water leaching to extract the cobalt,
then added Ni dopants in during the re-synthesis of the LCO. However, these approaches are
modifications of the pyro/hydro-metallurgical recycling methods, which are more energy intensive
and costly compared to direct recycling methods. Non-destructive upcycling approaches involve the
addition of dopants to the LCO particles directly. Zhang et al. [16] presented a strategy of Ni and Mn
co-doping to convert the degraded LCO particles into NMC with a hydrothermal process followed
by short annealing. Solid phase sintering described by Liu et al. [17] was also found to be effective for
Li, Mn, N, and S doping into spent LCO. However, the disadvantage of both approaches is the
possibility of non-uniform distribution of dopants or the formation of impurity phases, which may be
an issue in large particles.
 In this work, we report on a new method to upcycle LCO to Ni-doped LCO using a nanosheet
processing route. Nanosheets provide a high surface area, increasing the likelihood of reactions such
as ion exchange or doping. Therefore, using nanosheet structures may allow for more uniform doping
of nickel into the LCO matrix compared to traditional bulk synthesis methods, which often result in
inhomogeneous distribution of dopants [18, 19] that can lead to phase separation and reduced
performance. Herein, we demonstrate that exfoliated LCO nanosheets exposed to an aqueous nickel
solution followed by a heat treatment step will form Ni-doped LCO particles. Using characterization
including Raman spectroscopy, X-ray powder diffraction (XRD), scanning electron microscopy
(SEM), transmission electron microscopy (TEM), X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), and soft
X-ray absorption spectroscopy (sXAS), we confirm the formation of newly formed Ni-O bonds,
indicating the approach allows for successful insertion of Ni into the LCO lattice while retaining the
layered crystal structure. The results also show good distribution of Ni across the nanosheet materials
before and after calcination, with only a short annealing time required at 700 °C to achieve phase
purity. Preliminary investigations also show that the process is effective for increasing the Ni content
in NMC111 to a composition close to NMC532. The findings show that the nanosheet processing
route has the potential to enable uniform upcycling of LCO to Ni-doped LCO, which may help to
create a new pathway for converting LCO or low-Co materials to Ni-rich content cathodes.

2.3 Experimental Methods
 Exfoliated LCO nanosheets were prepared through intercalation of tetramethylammonium
(TMA) ions into protonated LCO, as reported previously [20, 21]. Ni cations were introduced to the
nanosheet suspensions from an aqueous solution of NiCl2 across a dialysis membrane. Subsequently,
the Ni-exchanged materials were recovered and dried. The dried powder is from here on referred to
as ‘Ni-ex-LCO.’
 The Ni-ex-LCO sample was mixed with LiOH and subjected to calcination for 4 hours in air at
400 °C (referred to as H400) or 700 °C (referred to as H700). For comparison, bulk CoOOH was
synthesized using the method described by Huang et. al [22]. The CoOOH was then subsequently
reacted with LiOH to form LCO as described in prior work [23].

 15

 The materials properties of the prepared samples were characterized using Raman spectroscopy,
scanning electron microscopy (SEM) with energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX), X-ray
photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), soft X-ray absorption spectroscopy (sXAS), and scanning
transmission electron microscopy (TEM). To assess the electrochemical properties of H700,
composite electrodes made from H700, polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) binder, and carbon black
(CB) conducting additive were prepared at weight ratio of 90:5:5. Half cells using Li metal anodes were
evaluated in 2032-type coin cells using a conventional nonaqueous electrolyte. For comparison,
electrodes were prepared in a similar manner using commercial LCO and LiNi1/3Mn1/3Co1/3O2
(NMC111) powders.

2.4 Results and Discussion

 The entire materials preparation process is shown in Figure 2.1 and can be separated into the
following steps. First, LCO powder is mixed with HCl so that some of the Li+ ions in the interlayer
space of the LCO structure are replaced by protons. The protons are subsequently exchanged with
bulky TMA cations that decrease the interlayer interactions and facilitate exfoliation of the bulk
structure with the aid of sonication. After removing the excess TMA from the suspension, the
nanosheet suspension is then exposed to an aqueous Ni solution to enable the exchange of Ni cations
for Co within the nanosheet structure. The final step involves mixing the Ni-doped nanosheets with
LiOH to replenish the depleted lithium, followed by heat treatment to ensure proper crystallinity of
the powder.

Materials and structural characterization
 The as-exfoliated LCO nanosheets form a colloidal suspension that displays the Tyndall effect
(Figure 2.2a), which is aided by the presence of TMA cations still absorbed on the surfaces that
prevent nanosheet agglomeration or precipitation. TEM analysis of the as-exfoliated LCO showed
that the nanosheets were mostly crumpled thin multilayers (likely two to three layers); some particles
~1 µm in size that could be LCO particles that did not fully exfoliate (Figure 2.2b) as well as
monolayers could also be observed (Figure 2.2c). The lattice fringes observed in high resolution TEM
imaging of the as-exfoliated nanosheets matched the (003) planes for CoOOH [24].
 Introduction of Ni cations by dripping a NiCl2 solution into the nanosheet suspension was
found to result in quick precipitation of the LCO nanosheets due to instability of the colloids. Thus,
to try to improve the exchange of Ni for Co, slower diffusion of Ni2+ ions into the suspension was
carried out across a semi-permeable membrane in a dialysis cassette. To provide an excess of Ni, the
concentration of Ni in the water was 1-2 wt%, depending on the amount of <101> nanosheets in the
suspension, to keep a molar ratio of Ni/Co = 3. The precipitation of nanosheets took place within 60
min using the dialysis-based Ni exchange process. TEM imaging of the nanosheets after Ni exchange
showed evidence of nanosheet agglomeration, which is consistent with the observed precipitation,
and the lattice fringes could still be matched to the (012) planes for CoOOH.

 SEM with EDX analysis showed uniform distribution of Ni throughout the precipitated
clusters after the Ni exchange. After heat treatment, the H700 materials comprised clusters ranging
from 5 to 10 µm in size that were made from smaller particles measuring approximately 500 nm to 1
µm in diameter. The plate-like, lamellar morphology of the H700 particles is consistent with the
restacking of the nanosheets, potentially facilitated by the Ni ions in the dialysis solution. EDX analysis
also showed uniform distribution of Ni throughout the particle clusters after heat treatment, with no
detectable chloride signal. The observed Ni/Co molar ratio measured in SEM EDX for H700 was
approximately 0.096, which is much lower than the molar ratio of 3 used in the dialysis solution.
 The TEM characterization of Ni-ex-LCO before calcination revealed the sample had a layered
structure, SAED pattern matching the (012) planes, and some non-uniform distribution of Ni from
the EDX mapping analysis. After heat treatment at 700 °C, the TEM image indicates the restacking
of the nanosheets into particles (Figure 2.2d). The lattice fringes and selected area electron diffraction
(SAED) pattern in (Figure 2.2e,f) corresponds to the (101) planes in LCO [25]. TEM EDX mapping
(Figure 2.2g) of a H700 particle revealed the uniform distribution of Co and Ni. Detailed composition
analysis of the EDX spectral image showed the distribution of Ni within several H700 particles and
determined the final composition as LiNixCo1-xO2, where x = 0.11. This is similar to the SEM EDX
results showing x ~ 0.1.
 XRD analysis was carried out on the samples after Ni-exchange and heat treatment (Figure
2.3). The XRD pattern of the precipitated Ni-ex-LCO sample remains similar to that of CoOOH [26],

 17

indicating the tetragonal (R3m) crystal structure was preserved despite the Ni/Co exchange process.
After heat treatment at 700 °C, the XRD pattern for H700 was like that of the LCO reference. Peak
splitting was observed for the (018) and (110) reflections (at 2θ = 65 and 65.9°, respectively),
suggesting the layered structure was successfully reformed [27] after the heat treatment. The peak
intensity ratio for (003)/(104) is 1.33, which is higher than the threshold of 1.2 mentioned in previous
literature, indicating the absence of cation mixing between Ni2+ and Li+ [28]. Conversely, in the case
of heat treatment at 400 °C (H400), some of the reflections remain merged, such as those at 2θ =37.2°
and 67.3°, which correspond to the (101) and (110) planes of LCO. This suggests that the temperature
of 400 °C may be insufficient to induce the formation of a fully layered structure, potentially allowing
the structure to persist as low temperature LCO spinel under these conditions [26].

Figure 2.3. XRD patterns of nanosheets after Ni exchange (Ni-ex-LCO) and after heat treatment with LiOH at 400 °C
(H400) or 700 °C (H700) compared to reference patterns for CoOOH and LCO.
 Raman spectroscopy was carried out to better understand the changes in the LCO structure
after exfoliation, Ni exchange, and heat treatment (Figure 2.4). Raman spectra were also obtained of
commercial LCO and NMC111, and in-house synthesized CoOOH as reference materials. In the
Raman spectrum of the dried nanosheets, one distinct peak is observed at ~501 cm⁻¹ and a broader
one centered at ~600 cm⁻¹, which resemble the modes reported for amorphous cobalt oxyhydroxide
(CoOOH) [29, 30]. The peak at 500 cm⁻¹ can be attributed to the bending vibration of Co-O-H, while
600 cm⁻¹ is associated with the stretching vibration of the Co-O bond in the CoOOH structure [24].
These results indicate that the sample after drying has a structure resembling CoOOH, which is
consistent with the removal of most of the interlayer Li+ during the exfoliation process. After Ni-
exchange, the Raman spectrum exhibited only the broad peak between 450 and 650 cm-1, which could
be indicative of incorporation of Ni into the nanosheet structure. A similar peak broadening upon Ni
doping into crystalline LCO was observed in previous studies [31]. Interestingly, the spectrum for the
heat-treated sample H400 shows two distinct modes at 486 and 592 cm-1, while that for H700 also
only shows one broad peak. The Raman spectrum for LCO also exhibits two distinct vibrations at
483 cm⁻¹ (corresponding to the O-Co-O bending along the a-b basal plane or Eg mode) and 592 cm⁻¹
(from Co-O stretching along the c-axis or A1g mode) [32, 33]. On the other hand, the Raman spectrum
for NMC111 displays merged peaks forming a broad feature between 450 and 650 cm⁻¹, attributed to
mixing of the Co, Ni, and Mn on the same cation sites and overlap of the respective Eg and A1g modes
[34]. The similarity of the Raman spectra for H700 and NMC111 provides evidence of Ni
incorporation into the structure. In contrast, the Raman spectrum for H400 retains two separate peaks,

 18

like that seen in LCO, indicating incomplete mixing of Ni within the LCO crystal structure due to the
too low of a heat treatment temperature.

Figure 2.4. Raman spectra of nanosheets as exfoliated, after Ni exchange (Ni-ex-LCO), and after heat treatment with
LiOH at 400 °C (H400) or 700 °C (H700). Raman spectra of CoOOH, NMC111, and LCO powders are shown for
comparison.

 The Raman and XRD results show that higher calcination temperatures favor the formation
of a more uniform layered structure with better Ni integration, as evidenced by the distinct changes
observed in the H700 sample compared to the H400 sample. Additionally, the use of nanosheets
appears to be key for obtaining structures with well mixed Ni distribution and phase pure layered
structure. As a control test, a mixture of CoOOH, LiOH, and NiCl2 was prepared and subjected to
the same 700 °C heat treatment. The Raman and XRD analysis showed the resulting product was a
mixture of LCO and Co3O4. In particular, the Raman modes match those expected for LCO and
Co3O4 and the lack of expansion and distortion of the peaks suggests that there is no substantial Ni
insertion in the comparison sample. There are also some reflections from impurities that cannot be
identified in the XRD pattern. These findings indicate that the nanosheet-based Ni exchange process
can successfully introduce Ni ions into the LCO structure more effectively than using bulk powders,
leading to more uniform structure and dopant distribution.

Electronic and surface structure characterization
 To better characterize the 3d electron states of the Ni and Co and p electron states of the
oxygen anions at different probing depths, sXAS was performed using fluorescence yield (FY)

 19

detection mode to obtain bulk information (~50 nm) and total electron yield (TEY) for surface
sensitive (1-20 nm) [35, 36]. In Figure 2.5a, the oxygen K-edge spectra using FY mode for reference
samples LiCoO₂, NMC111, and NiO are presented along with those for Ni-ex-LCO, H400, and H700.
For LCO, a prominent peak appears at ~530 eV corresponding to the transitions from the O1s to
hybridized O 2p-Co3+ 3d orbitals. Additionally, a shoulder is observed around 532 eV, which is also
associated with these interactions [30, 37]. The broader peaks at higher energies correspond to the O
2p → Co3+ 4sp transitions [37]. The spectrum for the NMC111 reference resembles that reported for
NMC compounds by others [38-40]. There are two distinct peaks at 529 and ~531.5 eV, with the first
peak attributed to the transition from O 1s → O 2p-Mn4+ and Co3+ 3d hybridized orbitals, and the
second with the O 1s → Ni2+ 3d-O 2p hybridized orbitals [38]. The latter assignment is confirmed
with the comparison to the NiO spectrum.

Figure 2.5. sXAS analysis of (a) Oxygen K edge, (b) Ni L3 edge, and (c) Co L3 and L2 edge in FY and TEY mode.

 When comparing these spectral features with those in Ni-ex-LCO, the features associated with
oxygen-cobalt (~530 eV) and oxygen-nickel (~531 eV) are both observed, confirming the introduction
of Ni into the nanosheets during the dialysis procedure. The oxygen-nickel feature is also present in
the spectra of H400 and H700 samples, indicating the successful insertion of nickel into the crystal
structure. The H400 and H700 samples also exhibit a new peak at ~533 eV that is more prominent in
the O K-edge TEY spectra. This suggests that this peak originates from surface species, likely LiOH,
which has a peak at this energy in the O K-edge [41, 42], that may not have fully reacted in the heat
treatment. The other O K-edge TEY spectra are similar to the FY spectra, with the exception of a
new peak at 534 eV attributed to the presence of surface Li2CO3 species [38] seen in the Ni-ex-LCO,
NMC111, and LCO samples.
 Examination of the Ni L₃-edge spectra (Figure 2.5b) provides additional evidence for the
successful incorporation of Ni into the exfoliated nanosheets and heat treated samples, with a
pronounced peak at 851.7 eV (labelled as A1) observed across all samples, indicative of nickel-to-
oxygen bonding [43]. The ratio between the shoulder at 853 eV (A2) and A1 can be used to assess the
Ni oxidation state, with a higher ratio indicating a higher oxidation state [38, 44]. Given that the nickel
in the reference materials NiO and NMC111 should be found as Ni2+, the results suggest that the
average valence state of Ni is around 2+ in the Ni-doped nanosheets. After heat treatment, the A2
peak becomes more pronounced compared to the A1 peak, which leads to a high A2/A1 ratio and
indicates oxidation of nickel from Ni²⁺ to Ni³⁺. This change suggests an increase in the oxidation state

 20

of nickel during the heat treatment process, which was carried out in air. Comparison of the Ni L3-
edge spectra from FY (bulk) and TEY (surface) modes shows the Ni oxidation state in H700 is higher
in the bulk compared to the surface, which is consistent with other studies on Ni-based cathodes [43,
44]. The Co L-edge spectra (Figure 2.5c) show no significant differences among all of the samples,
with prominent peaks at 779.6 and 794.5 eV for the L3 and L2-edges, respectively [37]. The results
indicate that the cobalt remains in the Co3+ state post-ion exchange and heat treatment.

Electrochemical characterization
 To assess the impacts of Ni-doping, the electrochemical properties of H700, NMC111, and
pristine LCO were evaluated with lithium metal as the counter electrode. The charge-discharge curves
were measured between 3.0 and 4.3 V vs. Li/Li+ using a 0.1C rate at room temperature. The voltage
plateaus of the samples differed significantly (Figure 2.6a), prompting further analysis using
differential capacity (dQ/dV) plots (Figure 2.6b). In the dQ/dV plot for LCO, a single peak at 3.94
V during charging corresponds to the O3-LCO phase transition [45]. In contrast, due to the
incorporation of nickel and manganese into NMC111, the dQ plot exhibits a broader peak range from
3.6 to 3.8 V, indicative of multiple phase transitions, including the H1 to M phase [46]. Xu et al. also
reported that the peak at 3.76 V corresponds to the Ni2+ to Ni4+ transition [47-49]. Interestingly, the
H700 sample shows not only a peak at 3.91 V (similar to LCO) but also an additional peak at 3.72 V.
This peak could be from the Ni3+ to Ni4+ transition, similar to what has been previously reported for
Ni-doped LCO prepared by direct solid-state synthesis [50, 51], providing further evidence of nickel
incorporation into the LCO structure, consistent with previous characterization results. The initial
discharge capacity for H700 was only ~80 mAh/g and dropped to 66 mAh/g after 50 cycles. In
comparison, the LCO and NMC111 electrodes showed much better capacity retention. The reason
for the low specific capacity and capacity retention in H700 is not understood yet, as prior studies on
Ni-doped LCO have shown good reversibility [50, 51]. The performance most likely could be
improved by optimizing the particle microstructure, slurry coating quality, Li content, and calcination
conditions, as those were not primary objectives of this study. We also note that different methods
for restacking and reassembly of LCO and NMC nanosheets have already been reported by Cheng et
al. [45, 52] and could be implemented in the future to further improve the cycling behavior.

Prospects for further doping and ion-exchange
 With the above results indicating that Ni-doped LCO could be achieved using the nanosheet
exfoliation and Ni/Co exchange process, a similar method was implemented to see if Mn-doping
could take place. However, the materials characterization results (Raman, XRD, SEM/EDX, XPS,
and sXAS) show that Mn exchange cannot be achieved as easily as Ni doping. While there may be
some evidence of Mn incorporation into the LCO according to the newly formed Mn-O bonding seen
by sXAS, the results also suggest that MnOOH is more likely to form.
 The comparative ease with which Ni can be exchanged into the LCO nanosheets is supported
by first principles calculations showing relatively low energy barriers for the process due to
stabilization of the cobalt coordination environment by solvent molecules [53]. However, the low
content of Ni in H700 points to other issues that could be inhibiting the exchange process. We found
that increasing the concentration of the NiCl2 solution (Ni/Co = 9) used for the exchange was not
effective for greatly increasing the amount of Ni in the Ni-ex-LCO, since the SEM-EDX analysis
showed a Ni/Co molar ratio of ~0.16. This is probably a result of the competition between the
precipitation of the nanosheets from the suspension and the Ni/Co ion exchange. Since the ion
exchange reaction is proposed to be more facile in the nanosheets, the rate of exchange may slow
once the nanosheets have agglomerated and undergone precipitation. Because the nanosheets are
negatively charged in aqueous solution [45], the addition of Ni2+ cations into the suspension will result
in the reassembly and precipitation of the nanosheets, which likely impedes the Ni/Co exchange
reaction. However, given that NMCs can also be exfoliated to form nanosheets [52], the Ni-doping
concept reported herein may be also be effective for the upcycling of low Ni-content NMCs to higher
Ni-content compounds.

exchange process as described above for LCO. For Ni-ex-NMC, the process was similar, but Li2CO3
was selected as lithium source to replace LiOH and the molar ratio of Li/Co was 1.5. Another

2.5 Conclusions

 This study explores an innovative method for upcycling LCO and low-Ni NMC materials,
leveraging the high surface area of the exfoliated nanosheets to facilitate cobalt extraction and
exchange with nickel ions. Several comprehensive characterization techniques were used to analyze
the effectiveness of cobalt extraction and the properties of the synthesized materials. XRD analysis
confirmed the recovery of the layered structure after Ni-exchange and heat treatment of the materials,
with minimal Li/Ni site mixing, while Raman spectroscopy showed peak broadening after the
exchange process, attributed to cation mixing and lattice expansion resulting from nickel substitution
at cobalt sites. Investigation of the electronic structure, bonding, and valence states in the materials
using sXAS and XPS confirmed the presence of Ni-O bonding and presence of Ni3+, which is also
supported by the redox behavior observed in electrochemical measurements. In summary, this study
successfully demonstrates the proof-of-concept and effectiveness of nickel doping into LCO using a
nanosheet exfoliation and ion exchange process. Preliminary characterization results also revealed that
NMC111 could be another candidate for this upcycling method. Further understanding of the method
and optimization of the materials may pave the way for the development of high-performance, cost-
effective, and sustainable lithium-ion battery technologies, addressing both environmental and
economic challenges associated with cobalt usage.

3. THE SELECTIVE ADSORPTION OF NI2+ OVER CO2+ FROM AQUEOUS
 SOLUTIONS IN SURFACE FUNCTIONALIZED METAL-ORGANIC
 FRAMEWORKS

3.1 Introduction

After cobalt-to-nickel cation exchange in exfoliated nanosheets (Chapters 1-2), residual Ni2+ reactants
and Co2+ products become intermixed in the near-neutral pH or slightly basic aqueous media in the
reactor. Selective capture of one of these divalent cations is necessary to purify the exiting cobalt
which can then be used as raw materials for new batteries.

In this study, two metal-organic frameworks (MOFs), zirconium (IV)-based MOF-808 and chromium
(III)-based MIL-101 were investigated for the selective adsorption of nickel (Ni2+) over cobalt (Co2+),
and the role of the nitrogen (N)-containing functional groups was evaluated using pyrazole (PyC) and
amine (-NH2) functional groups. Selective adsorption and adsorption rates were quantified using batch
adsorption experiments. The post-reaction MOFs after Co2+ and Ni2+ adsorption was characterized
with attenuated total reflectance Fourier transform infrared (ATR-FTIR) and X-ray photoelectron
(XPS) spectroscopies to identify the active sorption sites and to infer the adsorption mechanism. The
overall adsorption of Co2+ and Ni2+ by MOF-808 and MIL-101 increased after their surface
functionalization with PyC and -NH2. However, selective Ni2+ adsorption was only observed with
MOF-808-PyC. The pseudo-second-order model fit to the kinetic adsorption data suggests chemically
driven adsorption of Co2+ and Ni2+ onto all examined MOFs. The ATR-FTIR and XPS analyses of
MOFs with adsorbed Co2+ and Ni2+ indicate that the N sites in PyC and -NH2 are the reactive sites.
ATR-FTIR analyses of aqueous PyC solutions with metal cations show a greater shift in the N-H
vibrational band of PyC when interacting with Ni2+ compared to Co2+, which is linked to a stronger
interaction between Ni2+ and PyC, resulting in the Ni2+ selectivity by PyC-functionalized MOF-808.
We propose that PyC, as a π-acceptor ligand, exhibits greater affinity for Ni2+ compared to Co2+ due
to i) the greater electronegativity of Ni2+ and ii) the greater stability of Ni2+complex with PyC ligand
based on the Irving-Williams series and ligand field stabilization energy based on the electron
configuration differences between Co2+ (3d7) and Ni2+ (3d8).

This work was published in the journal Separation and Purification Technology 2025, 355, 129379, with
authors B.Y. Song, R.E. Sikma, M.L. Meyerson, K. Leung, K.M.M. Sanchez, J.G. Smith, D.F.S. Gallis,
and A.G. Ilgen [1]. More details can be found in that paper and in Chapter 4, which applies related
methods to further analyze the selective adsorption of Ni2+ over Co2+, and adds aqueous Mn2+ -- highly
relevant to batteries – to the mix. Here we excerpt some key results from this published work.

4. SELECTIVE ADSORPTION OF MN2+, CO2+, AND NI2+ BY MOF-808
 AND PYRAZOLE-FUNCTIONALIZED MOF-808: MECHANISTIC
 INSIGHTS VIA SPECTROSCOPIC ANALYSES

Abstract

The increasing demand for critical minerals such as Mn, Co, and Ni in lithium ion batteries necessitates
efficient recycling methods due to limited supplies and environmental concerns. Current recycling
practices are inadequate, with less than 5% of materials being recycled, leading to significant waste and
potential environmental hazards. This study explores the use of MOFs, particularly Zr-based MOF-
808, for the selective adsorption of these metals from aqueous solutions, focusing on the effects of
surface functionalization with pyrazole. This study is a continuation of the published work described
in Chapter 3. Here, we expand the use of ion-selective MOFs to metal mixtures that also include Mn2+.
1. Introduction
We investigate the selective adsorption of ions of interest in battery recycling Mn²⁺, Co²⁺, and Ni²⁺
using MOF-808 and pyrazole-modified MOF-808, and include XAS, expanded vibrational
spectroscopy characterization, and flow microcalorimetry to understand the thermodynamics of
selective adsorption. The research aims to further enhance the recycling of LIB waste by developing
efficient methods for the recovery of these transition metals. Using competitive batch adsorption
experiments, flow microcalorimetry, and spectroscopic techniques, the study elucidates the factors
influencing metal ion selectivity and binding mechanisms. Results indicate that pyrazole
functionalization significantly enhances Ni²⁺ adsorption while the preferential adsorption of Mn²⁺ via
partial precipitation in unmodified MOF-808. The findings provide insights into the design of
advanced materials and the recovery scheme of critical minerals.
2. Methods
Analytical-grade chemicals were used, including manganese nitrate tetrahydrate, nickel nitrate
hexahydrate, cobalt nitrate hexahydrate, and pyrazole. MOFs were synthesized at Sandia National
Laboratories, and their structures were characterized using PXRD, BET surface area analysis, TGA,
DLS, and ATR-FTIR spectroscopy. Competitive batch adsorption experiments were conducted to
evaluate the selective adsorption of Mn²⁺, Co²⁺, and Ni²⁺ by MOF-808 and pyrazole-functionalized
MOF-808. The adsorption capacity was determined by measuring the concentration of metal ions
before and after the adsorption process using ICP-MS. An operando flow microcalorimetry technique
was employed to measure the heat evolved during metal ion adsorption, providing insights into the
thermodynamics of the adsorption process. ATR-FTIR spectroscopy was used to observe changes in
the vibrational spectra of pyrazole upon metal binding, while XAS was utilized to investigate the local
coordination environment of adsorbed Ni²⁺.
3. Results
The synthesized MOF-808 and PyC-functionalized MOF-808 exhibited similar PXRD patterns,
indicating successful synthesis without significant structural changes. BET analysis revealed a decrease
in specific surface area from 1680 m²/g for MOF-808 to 1460 m²/g for MOF-808-PyC, attributed to
the volume occupied by the PyC groups. In competitive adsorption experiments, MOF-808 showed
the highest adsorption for Mn2+ (23.8 µmol/g), followed by Co2+ (19.2 µmol/g) and Ni2+ (20.2
µmol/g) (Figure 4.1). In contrast, pyrazole-functionalized MOF-808 exhibited enhanced adsorption

 33

for Ni²⁺ (31.8 µmol/g), with a 56% increase compared to unmodified MOF-808. The results suggest
that the pyrazole groups selectively enhance Ni²⁺ binding while having a minimal effect on Co²⁺ and
a slight increase in Mn2+ adsorption.
Flow microcalorimetry revealed that Mn2+ adsorption on both MOFs was exothermic, which is
indicative of partial precipitation of Mn2+ in MOF pores, while Co2+ and Ni2+ undergo surface
complexation only. Microcalorimetry indicated that Co2+ and Ni2+adsorption was endothermic for
both MOF-808 and MOF-808-PyC. The calorimetric data supported the preferential adsorption
trends observed in batch experiments, indicating that the adsorption behavior is metal-specific and
influenced by the functionalization of the MOF.
ATR-FTIR analysis demonstrated significant changes in the vibrational bands of pyrazole upon
interaction with Ni²⁺ and Co²⁺, indicating strong binding interactions. XAS results confirmed the
coordination environment of Ni²⁺ in both MOFs, revealing complexation with oxygen and potential
interactions with nitrogen in pyrazole.

Conclusion
This study highlights the potential of MOF-808 and its PyC-functionalized variant for the selective
recovery of critical minerals from LIB waste. The findings underscore the importance of ligand
functionalization in enhancing metal ion adsorption and provide a foundation for developing
advanced materials for sustainable resource recovery.

5. ELECTRODE SEPARATION AND FROTH FLOATATION FOR THE
 RECOVERY OF LI-ION BATTERY CATHODE MATERIALS

5.1 Abstract

Increasing use of Li-ion batteries (LIBs) will significantly increase the quantity of LIB waste generated
from end-of-life batteries. Spent LiCoO2 (LCO) and Li(NiMnCo)O2 (NMC) battery cathodes contain
significant quantities of valuable metals, making them good candidates for recycling. Typical recycling
processes rely on high temperatures (pyrometallurgy) and chemical leachants (hydrometallurgy) to
completely decompose the cathode and enable material recovery. Direct recycling processes and
recovery of active material from electrode scrap do not benefit from such destructive recycling, and
an efficient means of separating the cathode active material from the inactive components (e.g.,
polyvinylidene fluoride binder, conductive carbon) is needed. Here, we explore PVDF removal from
LCO cathodes by two separate methods, evaluating their suitability for coupling to subsequent
separation by froth floatation (FF). We show that washing in the solvent PolarClean (PC) dissolves
PVDF away from LCO cathodes, enabling separation of the remaining LCO and conductive carbon
by FF. The process is suitable for use with both pristine and cycled LCO electrodes, and the pristine
recovered material can be used to prepare electrodes capable of cycling at 140 mAh/g. This
demonstrates the suitability of coupled PC washing and FF separation for recycling both end-of-life
batteries and scrap electrode material.

5.2 Introduction

Lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) are an integral part of modern life, providing power for applications
ranging from personal electronics to electric vehicles to grid storage.1 Reliance on LIBs is expected to
grow as global electrification increases and, as such, global manufacturing capacity of LIBs is expected
to reach as high as 7 TWh by 2030.2 Increased use and production of LIBs comes with increased
generation of waste when the LIBs reach the ends of their useful lives, projected to be as much as 8
Mtons by 2040.3 Although these spent LIBs can no longer be used for electrochemical energy storage,
they still contain large quantities of valuable elements, including Li, Co, Ni, Cu, and P, and are expected
to be an increasingly important source of materials for the production of new batteries.4 As a result,
significant effort has been invested in the development and maturation of techniques to recycle used
LIBs, allowing the valuable materials to be recovered while limiting the negative impacts from the
toxic components in the batteries.4-9
 Most LIB recycling is accomplished through indirect processes, such as pyrometallurgy and
hydrometallurgy, whereby the battery components are broken down through physical and chemical
means to allow for the recovery and purification of the valuable components.4, 9 Both pyrometallurgy
and hydrometallurgy begin with preprocessing of the as-received batteries, typically consisting of
discharge, disassembly, and mechanical grinding. The mechanical grinding separates the structural and
inactive components of the battery (e.g., casing, current collectors, separators) from the valuable
“black mass” which consists of the metal oxide (cathode), graphite (anode), conductive carbon, and
binder.10 After separation, the black mass then undergoes additional processing to isolate the desired
recycling products. During pyrometallurgic processing, the black mass is subjected to high temperature
(< 1000 °C) reductive smelting, which burns away inactive battery components to produce transition
metal alloys; unfortunately, the process tends to be energy intensive, is ineffective at recovering lighter
elements like Li, and can release harmful byproducts during combustion.11 Hydrometallurgy, in
contrast, relies on chemical leaching to fully dissolve the active material within the black mass followed

 35

by selective precipitation to remove metals of interest from the resulting solution. Hydrometallurgy
has reduced energy costs and enables Li to be recovered from the LIBs in addition to transition metals,
instead presenting a different set of burdens.4, 9 Despite being typically less expensive than
pyrometallurgy and lacking emissions, the process the hydrometallurgy process can produce large
quantities of acidic waste that must be remediated and increases complexity. In some cases, separate
pyrometallurgy and hydrometallurgy processes are combined to improve the overall efficiency of the
LIB recycling process.4
 Recently, there has been increased interest in an alternative, less destructive route to LIB
recycling through what is known as direct recycling.7, 12 The broad goal of direct recycling is to preserve
as much of the chemistry and structure of the starting material (typically the metal oxide cathode) as
possible, and direct recycling can proceed through a variety of different methods. With respect to
metal oxide cathodes, direct recycling tends to use a variety of chemical processes, including
hydrothermal lithiation and high temperature sintering, to heal damaged material (e.g., Li loss, particle
cracking, etc.) and return the cathode particles to their pristine, pre-cycled state.6, 12 By preserving as
much of the original material as possible, direct recycling aims to reduce the cost of LIB material
recycling by minimizing the additional processing needed to transform transition metal alloys or salts
back into LIB cathodes. Moving beyond merely repairing old materials, there is also interest in
upcycling existing cathodes into more valuable forms, such as adding Ni and Mn to LiCoO2 (LCO) to
produce Li(NiMnCo)O2 (NMC).13 Several challenges remain for industrial scale adoption of direct
recycling/upcycling; one of these challenges is the need for improved cathode active material
separation/extraction methods.
 As the active materials in battery waste used in direct recycling will not necessarily undergo
destructive processing though acid leaching or pyrolysis, it is more important that the active materials
are separated from the other components in the black mass prior to processing. Roy et al. present a
useful overview of the variety of means for achieving this, featuring techniques like pyrolysis, cryogenic
grinding, dissolution, and others.12 Of note, even in cases where a full pyrometallurgic process is not
performed, the cathode materials are sometimes still exposed to high temperatures (>500 °C) to re-
lithiate and repair the cathode materials.7, 14 Although exposure to high temperatures has the additional
benefit of removing any residual binder or carbon in the sample, it is less suitable for recycling
processes where an annealing step is not needed. For example, recycling scrap cathode material or
preparing cycled cathodes for processes like upcycling13, 15 can both be accomplished without the need
for high temperature processes, as long as the active material can be efficiently separated from the
inactive components. Among the more promising techniques for achieving this separation is froth
floatation (FF), which has been shown to be an effective means of separating battery materials through
differences in the surface polarity of the various components (e.g., hydrophobic carbon/graphite from
hydrophilic metal oxides).16, 17 While FF has been successfully used to both separate anode and cathode
active materials from each other,18 as well as different cathode chemistries from physical mixtures,19
its efficacy decreases significantly if the organic binder (typically polyvinylidene fluoride, PVDF) in
the electrode is not removed prior to separation.20 The most common means of removing chemically-
resistant PVDF from a cathode without pyrolysis typically relies on dissolution in expensive and toxic
solvents like N-methyl-2-prrolidone (NMP).21 As a result, there is value in identification of alternative
means of removing PVDF binder from LIB black mass, thereby enabling more efficient FF separation
of valuable cathode materials from other components.
 In this work, we explore two different methods of removing PVDF binder from LCO
cathodes prior to material separation via FF. The first method involves the use of a 5M KOH solution
to simultaneously defluorinate the PVDF binder and dissolve the Al foil current collector at relatively
low temperatures (<100 °C).22 The second method explores the use of a low-toxicity polar aprotic
solvent, PolarClean (pentanoic acid, 5-(dimethylamino)-2- methyl-5-oxo-,methyl ester, PC)23 to wash

 36

away the PVDF binder, allowing separation of the residual LCO and conductive carbon (SuperP)
from the current collector. Using a variety of physiochemical characterization techniques, including
thermogravimetric analysis (TGA), Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), scanning electron
microscopy (SEM), energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX), and others, we show that while
KOH treatment effectively defluorinated and decomposed the PVDF binder, the process left a
residual carbon matrix that prevented FF separation of the cathode materials. As such, the KOH
treatment is unsuitable as a PVDF removal technique for LIB cathode recycling. In contrast, PC
washing is found to be an effective means of removing the binder, enabling collection of LCO from
commercial cathodes with >95% efficiency. Subsequent electrochemical cycling of the collected
material indicates that the processes are suitable for the direct recycling of scrap electrode material
into new electrodes. Coupling of PC washing and FF separation also enables collection of LCO from
cycled cathodes, demonstrating the process is a promising method for recovering active material from
the cathodes of both pristine (i.e., scrap) and recycled LIB cathodes.

5.3 Experimental

Materials
All materials were used as received, unless otherwise noted. LiCoO2 powder (LCO, 99.8%), KOH
(90%), 1-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP, ACS reagent), tetrabutylammonium bromide (TBAB, ACS
reagent), 4-methyl-2-pentanol (MIBC, synthesis), and 1 M LiPF6 EC/DEC (50/50 v/v, battery grade)
were purchased from Sigma Aldrich. Poly(vinylidene fluoride) (PVDF) was purchased from Solef.
SuperP conductive carbon was purchased from Hitachi. 2-propanol (IPA) was purchased from
Avantor. Kerosene (odorless) was purchased from ThermoFisher. PolarClean (pentanoic acid, 5-
(dimethylamino)-2- methyl-5-oxo-,methyl ester, PC) was purchased from Syensqo. Single-sided
commercial LCO electrodes (11.2 mg/cm2 LCO loading) were purchased from MTI Corporation.
Celgard 2400 and 2325 was purchased from Celgard. Li foil (0.5 mm thick) was purchased from MSE
Supplies. 20 μm thick Li foil on 20 μm Cu foil was purchased from Albermarle.

Electrode Preparation
To prepare homemade LCO electrodes with PVDF binder for KOH treatment, a slurry of LCO
powder, SuperP, and PVDF in NMP was prepared. The final slurry contained 38.8, 2.2, and 2.2 wt.%
of LCO, SuperP, and PVDF, respectively, with the remaining mass belonging to NMP solvent. The
slurry was mixed in a tube drive mixer (ULTRA-TURRAX, IKA) with fifteen 6 mm glass spheres for
30 minutes at 8000 rpm. After mixing, the slurry was coated onto a 25 μm thick Al foil using a 275
μm doctor blade and dried under vacuum at 100°C overnight. This produced a cathode sheet with an
LCO loading of 9.27 mg/cm2. Commercial LCO electrodes were likewise dried under vacuum
overnight at 100°C.

KOH Treatment of LCO Electrodes
An aqueous 5 M KOH solution using 18 MΩ water (Millipore) was prepared in an ice bath with
magnetic stirring. After preparation, 181.67 g of the solution was chilled in a 100 mL PTFE beaker in
an ice bath before 3.875 g of the above LCO electrode (in 1x1 cm pieces) was slowly added with
magnetic stirring. It is important to add the electrode pieces slowly and in a fume hood to prevent
heating and allow for removal of H2 generated during dissolution of the Al foil. After the Al foil was
dissolved, 0.45 g of TBAB was dissolved into the solution and the resulting mixture was subsequently
heated to 80 °C in an oil bath and held there for 2 hrs to defluorinate the PVDF. After cooling, the
solution was centrifuged using a 5804 centrifuge (Eppendorf) at 10,000 rpm for 10 minutes to isolate

 37

the solids; these were then repeatedly resuspended in water and centrifuged until the supernatant
reached a pH of 7.0. Following neutralization, the solids were dried overnight under vacuum at 70 °C.
Pure PVDF powder was processed in a similar manner.

PC Treatment of LCO and LFP Electrodes
Commercial LCO electrodes were cut into eight 4.2 x 5 cm pieces and soaked overnight at 65 °C in
40 mL of PC solvent. Following soaking, a razor blade was used to scrape the loosened film from the
surface of the Al foil current collector. This solid material was resuspended in 80 mL of fresh PC and
stirred for 1 hr at 85 °C before being vacuum filtered through a 0.2 μm nylon filter overnight. The
recovered solid was then stirred in 80 mL of fresh PC at 85 °C overnight before being sonicated for
10 min and subsequently vacuum filtered through a 0.2 μm nylon filter. The recovered solids were
resuspended in 80 mL of PC and stirred at 85 °C for 1 hour and vacuum filtered through a 0.2 μm
nylon filter one final time, before being rinsed and vacuum filtered in 80 mL of IPA to remove any
residual PC; the solid was dried overnight at 70 °C.

Froth floatation of electrode material
Prior to froth floatation (FF), 500 mL of 18 MΩ water was mixed with approximately 2-3 g of
electrode powder in a 500 mL mixing pan at 500 rpm for 10 min without air flow using a 911MPE-
D12-A Laboratory Floatation Machine (911 Metallurgist). Air flow was stopped by closing the valve
to the air supply of the instrument. 7.5 μL of MIBC and 7.5 μL of kerosene were then added to the
suspension as a frothing agent and collector, respectively, and allowed to mix at 500 rpm for an
additional 10 min. After mixing, the air valve was opened to ambient pressure and the mixing rate was
increased to 1200-1800 rpm, with the final speed chosen to produce a stable, grey-black foam that did
not overflow the mixing pan. A plastic spatula was used to scrape the foam from the surface of the
suspension into a collection beaker. Scraping continued over approximately 15 min, stopping when
the foam was a consistent white color. The FF process produced two separate products, the
foam/froth scraped from the surface (containing primarily hydrophobic carbon) and the remaining
solution/slurry (containing primarily hydrophilic LCO). After separation, both portions were spun
separately at 10,000 rpm in a centrifuge to separate the suspended solids from the supernatant. The
collected solids were washed/separated sequentially with water and IPA, then allowed to dry under
vacuum overnight at 70 °C.

Battery Construction and Electrode Harvesting
For pouch cells, commercial LCO electrodes were punched into 4.2x5 cm electrodes with a 1 x 0.25
cm tab, while 20 μm thick Li on Cu anodes were punched into 4.4 x 5.2 cm electrodes with 0.5 x 0.25
cm tabs. Al and Ni leads were spot welded to the Al and Cu tabs, respectively. Two 4.6 x 5.6 cm pieces
of Celgard 2325 were placed between the electrodes and heat sealed with 1 mL of 1 M LiPF6 electrolyte
inside a polymer coated Al pouch. Pouch cells were allowed to rest at open circuit for 20 hrs prior to
cycling. Following cycling, the electrodes were opened inside a glovebox and the cathode collected
and rinsed with 1 mL of anhydrous DEC solvent and allowed to dry prior to further processing.
 For coin cells, homemade LCO electrodes were punched into 15 mm diameter disks and dried
overnight at 100 °C before cycling into an Ar-filled glovebox with H2O/O2 < 0.1 ppm. A 16 mm
diameter disk of 0.5 mm Li foil was punched to serve as the anode. To build a typical coin cell, the
following components were placed in order inside of a stainless steel CR2032 coin cell case: wave
spring, 0.2 mm thick 16 mm diameter stainless steel disk, 0.5 mm thick 16 mm diameter stainless steel
disk, Li anode, two 19 mm diameter pieces of Celgard 2400, 70 μL of 1M LiPF6 EC/DEC electrolyte,
and an LCO cathode. Coin cells were allowed to rest at open circuit for 20 hrs prior to cycling.

Electrochemical Analysis
After construction, both pouch cells and coin cells were cycled on an LBT-20084 battery cycler (Arbin)
at a rate of C/10 (relative to theoretical LCO capacity, 273 mAh/g) between 3.0-4.3 V. Pouch cells
were cycled 25 times and then stopped at 3.0 V (discharge) or 4.3 V (charge) for disassembly and
separation, as described above.

Instrumental Analysis
X-ray diffraction spectroscopy (XRD) was performed using a D2 Phaser (Bruker) spectrometer using
a Cu kα radiation source. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was performed to collect images of
the electrode samples, using both a Vega (Tescan) microscope at an accelerating voltage of 30 kV and
equipped with a secondary electron detector, and with a JSM-IT800 Schottky Field Emission
microscope (JEOL). Energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) was performed using an Oxford
Instruments detector at an accelerating voltage of 20 kV. Samples for SEM were prepared by
sonicating the powders in IPA and then casting the suspension onto a Si wafer for imaging.
Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was performed using an SDTQ600 (TA Instruments), with
samples heated at 10 °C/min to 800 °C under continuous air flow at 400 mL/min. Fourier transform
infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) was performed using a Nicolet iS20 FTIR Spectrometer (ThermoFisher)
equipped with a Smart iTX Diamond Attenuated Total Reflectance (ATR) accessory and a KBr beam
splitter. Spectra were acquired between 750-4000 cm-1 with an average of 32 scans at 0.482 cm-1
resolution. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was performed using a Kratos AXIS Supra
operated at a base pressure better than 5x10-9 Torr, using a monochromatic Al Kα X-ray source
(1486.6 eV). Surveys and high resolution for elements of interest were taken on each sample. Spectra
were obtained using a large analyzer spot size and a pass energy of 160 eV for all surveys and 20 eV
for the high-resolution elements, with a step size of 0.1 eV. Charge compensation was done using a
charge neutralizer with a filament current of 0.42 A. All spectra were corrected to an adventitious (sp3)
carbon peak at 284.8 eV. Auger electron spectroscopy (AES) was performed using a 680 Scanning
Auger Nanoprobe (PHI), and spectra were collected at 5 keV using a 10 nA electron beam (d = 200
Å) at 0.2 eV/step under charge neutralization. The samples were sputtered for 30 s with Ar+ at 2 kEv
to clean the surface and better expose the underlying material.

separations (described in the Experimental section) were performed as outlined in Figure 5.1a using
MIBC as the frothing agent and kerosene as a collector and demonstrated the suitability of the
technique for separating even small quantities of carbon from the LCO active material.

KOH Removal of PVDF
PVDF is the most common binder for LIB electrodes, due to its chemical stability, plasticity, and
insolubility in carbonate-based electrolyte solutions, among other beneficial properties.21
Unfortunately, the same properties that make PVDF desirable also make it difficult to remove during
battery recycling and battery recycling typically requires pyrolysis or dissolution of the binder before
further processing can proceed.9 The drawback of pyrolysis is that it requires significant thermal energy
and produces both CO2 and toxic F-containing products (HF) as the PVDF decomposes.8 Conversely,
while PVDF dissolution can be performed at much lower temperatures and does not produce as many
harmful byproducts, the most commonly-used and effective solvent is 1-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP),
which has notable health hazards that have resulted in significant usage restrictions.24 An alternative
to these techniques that enables removal of the binder without the need for harmful tradeoffs would
be a boon to the field of LIB recycling.
 Recent work from Chen and coworkers has identified one such alternative in the form of
hydrothermal defluorination and decomposition of the PVDF using a LiOH solution.7, 25, 26 Rather
than relying on toxic NMP, the researchers reacted cathode black mass with a concentrated aqueous
LiOH solution under heat and pressure indie a hydrothermal reactor to both decompose the PVDF
and relithiate the active material for direct recycling. Earlier work from Brewis et al. showed that PVDF
could be de-fluorinated at even less harsh conditions, with optimized conditions producing a
defluorinated sp2 carbon product at temperatures as low as 80 °C under ambient pressure.22 Based on
this work, we demonstrated similar results using a commercial PVDF powder commonly used in
battery electrodes. Following reaction, the powder changed from off-white to black color and FTIR
spectra showed an almost complete loss of C-F moieties in the post-treatment powder (Figure 5.2a).
Given this successful PVDF decomposition, we next performed KOH treatment on a homemade
LCO cathode (described in the Experimental section). Although black mass is typically physically
removed from Al current collectors prior to reaction, we hypothesized that it may be possible to
couple the dissolution of the Al foil current collector and the decomposition of the PVDF binder into
a single step, given the well-known solubility of Al in strongly alkaline conditions.27 The strong 5M
KOH solution effectively dissolved the Al current collector from the electrodes and, following
reaction and washing, we recovered a black powder which XRD analysis confirmed contained
undamaged LCO (Figure 5.2b). XPS analysis of the powder surface showed both a near total loss of
C-F (~289-290 eV)28 moieties from the surface and significant carbon oxidation (286-288 eV)29 in the
C 1s spectra following reaction (Figure 5.2c). Likewise, the Co 2p 3/2 spectra showed an increase in
Co-OH species (~781 eV)30 at the surface following reaction (Figure 5.2d), suggesting that while the
bulk structure is likely unaffected by the 5M KOH solution the surface of the particles becomes more
hydroxylated in the alkaline conditions needed to dissolve the Al current collector and defluorinate
the PVDF binder.

processing is pure (>99%), the collection efficiency is only 10%. Thus, most of the desired LCO
product remains trapped in the froth product contaminated with carbon.

 SEM analysis of the three samples shows that both the unseparated, KOH-treated material

collection efficiency observed for this sample. Indeed, although Chen and coworkers reported

their process still requires a high temperature (> 550 °C) anneal to both crystalize the oxide product
and burn away the carbon produced during defluorination.26

PC Removal of PVDF
Turning from the alkaline defluorination, we next explored the use of PolarClean (PC) as a greener
NMP substitute for dissolving the PVDF binder prior to FF separation. PC has been identified as a
promising solvent with lower toxicity than NMP,23 and has previously shown promise in both casting
and recycling LIB electrodes.31, 32 In one example, Cattaneo et al. demonstrated that PC could liberate
the cathode film from the current collector by partially dissolving the PVDF binder. This allowed the
conductive carbon and active material to be separated using column separation, with subsequent

 44

hydrometallurgical processing collecting the active material for resuse.31 Given these promising results,
we chose to study the suitability of PC for integration with FF separation of LIB cathodes.

concentrations as high as 5 mgPVDF/mL achieved at 65 °C. Unfortunately, attempts to dissolve LCO

not successful. Previous reports have shown that PVDF has more limited solubility in PC than NMP,32
and multiple 1 hr PC washings at 65 °C similar to those performed by Cattaneo el al31 resulted in a
poor separation of LCO from the carbonaceous electrode material. TGA analysis showed that
although the LCO collection efficiency (43%) was much greater than that of the KOH-processed
electrode material (10%), a significant portion of the LCO (54.3 wt.%) remained trapped in the froth
product. Given the more limited solubility of PVDF from the cathodes, we increased the soaking
temperature to 85 °C and added an additional overnight soaking step to improve the PVDF

efficiency of 95.6% when used to wash pristine, uncycled commercial LCO electrodes (Figure 5.4a,

 SEM images and EDX mapping of the collected electrode powder before frothing (Figure
5.4b,c) show that, unlike the KOH-treated material, the PC-treated materials demonstrated separation
of the carbon and LCO particles even before separation by FF. Further SEM images and EDX
mapping of the froth and slurry products confirm that the froth product is much more carbon-rich
than the slurry product. Although the SEM imaging supports the relatively large quantity of LCO in
the froth product (roughly 80 wt.%) as measured by TGA, the low quantity of the material collected
from these small scale experiments (< 100 mg after FF) made attempts to perform additional
separations unsuccessful; we anticipate that further LCO separation could be achieved from
sufficiently large quantities of the froth product (> 1 g), similar to that observed in the earlier PVDF-
free separations. Regardless, these results show that the optimized PolarClean wash at 80 °C was an
effective means of removing PVDF from the electrodes and enabling separation of LCO and SuperP
by froth floatation.

Impact of Cycling on FF Separation
While the collection of LCO and other LIB cathode active materials from pristine battery electrodes
would help to address the nearly 1 Mton of battery manufacturing scrap expected to be produced
globally via battery manufacturing by 2030,15 recycling end-of-life LIBs is will be much more

 47

important. As of 2022 global interest in the operation or new construction of LIB recycling facilities
using hydrometallurgical, pyrometallurgical, and direct recycling processes was only expected to grow.4
Given this, we next explored the ability of the PC to remove PVDF from lightly cycled LCO electrodes
with a native cathode electrolyte interphase (CEI) as an analogue for understanding the suitability of
this process would for the recycling of end-of-life LIBs.
 Following limited cycling (25 cycles at C/10, pouch cells were stopped in the charged (Vcell =
4.3 V) or discharged (Vcell = 3.0 V) states and the cathodes harvested for PC washing and subsequent
separation by FF. As shown in Figure 5.6a,d and Table 5.2, while both the discharged and charged
electrodes show relatively high LCO collection efficiencies (92 and 71.1%, respectively) neither is as
high as that of the pristine electrodes (95.6%). Likewise, while the SuperP in the cathode powder prior
to separation does not appear tightly bound to the LCO particles themselves in either charge state
(Figure 5.6b,c,e,f), it does appear more clumped than in the untested electrodes (Figure 5.4). The
clumping may indicate that the presence of CEI may have hindered PVDF removal, or the CEI itself
may prevent the breakup of SuperP agglomerates. The same behavior is observed in both powders
following separation, indicating that the agitation from the froth floatation process was insufficient to
disrupt these structures.
 Auger electron spectroscopy (AES) of SuperP-rich areas on both untreated electrodes and
washed powders shows a significant decrease in the F content relative to C after washing, providing
additional evidence of the efficacy of PolarClean as a solvent for removal of PVDF from LCO
electrodes. However, we note that while the cycled electrodes do have significantly less F than the
commercial electrode (~600:1 and ~40:1, respectively), F was still detectable in both cases. In contrast,
the pristine washed electrode showed no detectable F following washing. This suggests that changes
to the electrodes following cycling may have slightly inhibited the dissolution of PVDF into the
PolarClean solvent, preventing the breakup of these larger SuperP agglomerates during washing and
FF. Earlier results have shown that the presence of CEI can have a notable impact on separation by

 48

FF,20 thus, we next chose to explore the electrodes’ surface chemistry more closely using XPS to gain
a better understanding any differences between the samples, particularly in the CEI.

 Quantification of the surface chemistry using XPS shows that both charged and discharged
electrodes exhibit an increase in the quantity of lithium, carbon, oxygen, and phosphorus on the
surface. This is concomitant with a decrease in the fluorine and cobalt signals, and so we conclude a
C- and O-rich CEI forms on the electrode surface with cycling, consistent with earlier reports.34, 35 The

5.5 Conclusions

In this work, we explored two methods of treating common LCO cathodes to prepare them for
material separation via froth floatation. Our results showed that while KOH treatment effectively
decomposed both the Al foil current collector and the PVDF binder in the cathode film without
significantly damaging the active material itself, the defluorination process left behind a carbon matrix
that inhibited the separation of the LCO particles from the SuperP in the sample. In contrast, repeated
washing of the electrodes in PolarClean at elevated temperatures allowed removal of the binder and
enabled highly efficient separation of the LCO from the conductive carbon via froth filtration with
95% LCO collection efficiency. LCO recovered from these electrodes was shown to cycle at a C/10
rate for more than 25 cycles at 140 mAh/g, indicating that there was minimal impact on the active
material itself from the washing and FF process. Electrochemically cycled LCO cathodes with a native
CEI layer could also be separated using the FF process, albeit with lower LCO collection efficiencies
(92% and 71.1% for discharged and charged electrodes, respectively). SEM analysis showed that,
unlike the pristine LCO cathodes, the SuperP in these samples remained clumped together after
washing and separation, while AES confirmed that most of the PVDF was successfully removed
during the washes. This suggests that the presence of the CEI may have itself inhibited
deagglomeration and separation of SuperP. XPS analysis of the cycled cathodes themselves showed
an increase in C and O content relative to the uncycled electrodes. We hypothesize that the more
carbon-rich CEI on the surface of the charged cathodes results in LCO particles that are more organic
and thus easier to separate into the hydrophobic froth product. In contrast, following washing the
pristine LCO electrodes feature CEI-free LCO particles with more hydrophilic surfaces, leading to
the excellent separation efficiency observed. Overall, these results indicate that the coupling of PC-
enabled binder removal with FF separation of cathode components represents a promising avenue to
more efficient, less intensive, and more environmentally friendly recovery of valuable battery materials
from both scrap and end-of-life cathodes.

6. MICROWAVE-ASSISTED DIRECT UPCYCLING OF LITHIUM ION
 BATTERY CATHODES

6.1 Summary

Lithium-ion batteries are a popular choice for many applications that utilize rechargeable batteries due
to their high charge capacity. A current challenge surrounding the influx of these batteries is the issue
of their disposal. Existing recycling methods are costly, energy intensive, destructive, and can create
toxic metal waste. Direct upcycling has been introduced as a more sustainable method that can mitigate
these challenges. In this chapter, a new facile one-pot microwave-assisted exfoliation method has been
developed to turn spent lithium-ion battery cathode materials (LCO and NMC 111) into 2D
nanosheets. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM)
images were used to observe the exfoliation from bulk material to few- and monolayer nanosheets. X-
ray diffraction was used to confirm the crystallinity of nanosheets produced. High quality cobalt oxide
nanosheets were produced in a shorter reaction time with higher yields compared to previously
established methods.

6.2 Introduction
 Our need for unlimited access to power sources has spawned a multitude of research into the
world of batteries and their storage capabilities. A popular choice for these types of batteries has been
lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) due to their high energy density per unit weight and unit volume. 1 Rapid
market growth of electric vehicles, high-capacity rechargeable devices, such as smartphones and
medical devices, has spurred exponential growth in the demand for LIBs. Disposal methods for LIBs,
however, are not as sufficient as most batteries end their life cycle in landfills or stockpiles. 2,3 Different
recycling methods have been suggested as most of the metals, such as lithium and cobalt, used in
cathode materials are becoming scarcer. Current recycling methods include pyrolysis, hydrometallurgy,
or disassembly and separation of cathode materials. 4,5 Pyrolysis involves melting at high temperature,
while hydrometallurgy uses acid leaching. Both processes destroy the cathode oxide material, extract
pertinent elements, and resynthesize the cathode from scratch from the regenerated metal salts. These
methods of recycling are costly, energy-intensive, and lead to toxic metal leaching and unwanted
environmental impact. The push to alleviate these concerns is based on a direct method of recycling
LIBs via disassembly. 6
 Direct recycling or direct upcycling aims at recovering and reusing LIB components for other
devices by breaking down the cathodes to turn them into value-added materials. 7 Conventional
cathode materials consist of layered metal oxides, such as LiCoO2 (LCO) or LiNi0.33Mn0.33Co0.33O2
(NMC 111), that allow the intercalation and deintercalation of Li+ ions during the charging and
discharging of the battery. LCO and NMC have high energy density, 150-250 Wh/kg, strong structural
stability, and are easy to use as battery cathodes, making them ideal for use in LIBs. 8 In terms of
recycling, it is known that once such cathodes are broken down to retrieve the layered metal oxides
powders, they are not receptive to further processing. 9
 The most sought-after method to ease post processing of the bulk layered metal oxide
powders, is to turn the material into nanosheets. Two dimensional (2D) materials, i.e. nanosheets, are
attractive because they can possess unique physiochemical properties, such as high surface area,
electronic properties, magnetic properties, etc. This can be advantageous in applications such as
transistors, energy storage systems, biomedical research, and more. 10 Exfoliation, the separation of
few layer nanosheets from bulk material by the insertion of bulky organic cations, is most effective at

 53

creating nanosheets. Layered materials from graphene 11, clays 12, metal chalcogenides 13, perovskites
14
 have been exfoliated into nanosheets by this approach allowing researchers to take advantage of
their nanoscale features and create new nanomaterials.
 There are many methods of exfoliation that exploit chemical, electrochemical, and mechanical
routes. A novel exfoliation procedure from Cheng et al. describes an electrochemical exfoliation
procedure that removes 1/3 of the Li+ ions from the bulk material by electrochemical oxidation in
order to enable insertion of bulky organic cations into the layered metal oxide interlayer, forcing apart
the oxide slabs to generate nanosheets (NSs). 15,16 An example of mechanical exfoliation method is
ball-milling. 17 Chemical methods using various strong chemical oxidizing agents including halogens
and dilute acids to extract Li+ ions have been shown to facilitate exfoliation. 18,19 While these methods
successfully generate layered metal oxide NSs, the bulk-to-nanoscale conversion yield remains very
low and makes the production of bulk nanosheets difficult. Furthermore, many methods either destroy
layered metal oxide integrity or require complex, multistep reaction schemes that take days to
complete, making the process very labor intensive and costly. This in turn makes scalability of direct
upcycling difficult or even unattainable. 2,4,20,21
 Herein we present a novel reaction pathway to cost-efficient, large-scale LIB cathode recycling
that enhances recovery yield while significantly reducing energy and cost intensity relative to the
current commercial methods. Our approach uses mild conditions and reagents in a facile “one-pot”
reaction that generates high-quality mono- and bilayer LCO and NMC111 NSs in high yields (> 95%)
by exploiting microwave (MW) dielectric heating. Exfoliation time is greatly decreased and is easily
scaled to generate multi-gram yields of NSs. These NSs are then available for further processing by
ion exchange or restacking, subsequently leading to reassembly into functional battery cathode
materials. This approach offers an important advance in the recycling of LIBs.

6.3 Experimental

6.3.1 Materials
Lithium cobalt oxide (“LCO,” LiCoO2, >99.5%) powder, tetrabutylammonium hydroxide 30-hydrate
(TBAOH, ≥98%), and sulfuric acid (> 95.0%, ACS standard) were obtained from Sigma Aldrich.
Nickel manganese cobalt oxide (“NMC111 or NMC,” LiNi0.33Mn0.33Co0.33O2, 98%) powder was
purchased from MSE Supplies. Concentrated sulfuric acid was diluted to 0.35 M using DI water.

6.3.2 Sample Preparation
Acid Delithiation (pre-treatment)
 To facilitate TBA+ intercalation and subsequent exfoliation, lithium ions were removed from
the bulk oxide interlayer via treatment with dilute acid. 22 A 0.1 g sample of as-obtained LCO and
NMC powders were placed in a glass scintillation vials along with 8 mL of 0.35 M H2SO4. The sample
was magnetically stirred on a hot plate at 90 °C for 48 hours. After cooling to room temperature, the
solid was washed repeatedly with deionized (DI) water via centrifugation at 4000 rpm until the pH of
the sample became neutral. The sample was dried in an oven at 100°C overnight.
Microwave Exfoliation
 The newly delithiated LCO/NMC samples were exfoliated using a microwave process
previously reported by Akbarian-Tefaghi et al.14 Syntheses were performed using a CEM Discover 2.0
microwave reactor with a single magnetron generator and rotating diffuser running at 2.45 GHz. The
instrument was controlled by a microprocessor power output of up to 1200 W. Temperature was
controlled using input from a contactless internal infrared sensor. The “dynamic heating” protocol, in
which ramp and reaction time, reaction temperature, and maximum applied power were programmed

 54

by the user, while the instrument varied actual applied power to meet user specifications, was used for
the following reactions.
 A 0.1 g sample of delithiated LCO or NMC was combined with 0.15 g of tetrabutylammonium
hydroxide (TBAOH), 15mL of DI water, and a Teflon-coated magnetic stir bar in a Pyrex microwave
vessel with a 30 mL capacity. The mixture was stirred briefly to combine, then fitted with a flexible
vented cap as an overpressure safety precaution. Under continuous stirring on “high,” the mixture
was ramped for 5 minutes and the held at the reaction temperature of 90 °C for 1 hour (NMC) or 2
hours (LC) at a maximum applied power of 150 W; samples were then cooled to room temperature
before being removed from the reactor.
 The cooled product was washed with DI water via centrifugation in two steps. The first
centrifugation was run at 4000 rpm to separate unexfoliated bulk material from exfoliated nanosheets.
The supernatant containing the nanosheet suspension was then centrifuged at 9000 rpm and
redispersed in DI water for further washing. The separated purified nanosheets and unexfoliated bulk
material were dried at 100°C in an oven overnight. Yields of each product were calculated based on
the mass difference between dried exfoliated product and unexfoliated bulk material, with results
showing exfoliated NSs yields of 99% (NMC) and 95% (LCO). NSs were stored as dry powders for
further characterization.
Characterization
 Powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) data was collected at room temperature on a Bruker D2
Phaser diffractometer utilizing Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å) at an accelerating voltage of 45 kV and
a current of 40 mA. Room temperature diffraction data were collected over a range of 10–80° 2θ at a
scan rate of 0.05 s−1. Diffraction patterns were analyzed using Jade 9 software (Materials Data Inc.,
Livermore, CA). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was conducted using a ThermoFisher Scientific
Verios 5 UC; to image the nanosheets, the dried powder was placed on carbon tape attached to a SEM
specimen holder. For TEM imaging, the nanosheet suspensions were centrifuged for 5 min at 9000
rpm and redispersed in ethanol after the supernatant was discarded. Samples were then drop cast onto
Ted Pella lacy carbon/Formvar TEM grids. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images of the
nanosheet suspensions were performed using a JEOL 2010 TEM with an accelerating voltage of 200
kV or Titan G2 Environmental Scanning TEM (STEM) with an accelerating voltage of 300 kV.

6.4 Results
6.4.1 XRD
 Powder X-ray diffraction patterns were obtained for NMC and LCO before any treatment,
after acid delithiation, and after microwave exfoliation (Figures 2.1 and 2.2). The high-intensity peak
at 18.7˚ and 19.1˚ 2θ for NMC and LCO, respectively, corresponds to the (003) plane of the crystal
structure, which indicates the Li+ ion rich interlayer spacing between the 2D oxide slabs. Upon
delithiation, the 003 peak shifts to lower angles (18.6 and 18.1˚ 2θ, respectively). There is also an
increase in interlayer spacing associated with this peak shift from 4.74 Å to 4.77 Å for NMC and 4.63
Å to 4.90 Å for LCO. The peak shift to lower angles in conjunction with the increase in d-spacing is
s a result of Li+ removal and subsequent expansion due to electrostatic repulsion. Both post-
delithiation spectra show that the peaks at 37.0° and 44.7°, and 37.3° and 45.2° (NMC and LCO,
respectively) are unchanged in sharpness and intensity, indicating preservation of layer crystallinity and
long-range order. 23
 Interestingly, in the case of LCO (Figure 2.2), the removal of Li+ ions from the interlayer space
results in a crystal structure change from O3-type of LCO to a mixture of O1- and P3-type structures.
XRD spectra of LCO displays a peak at 18.1˚ and 19.3˚ indicates the presence of the P3-type and O1-
type crystal structures, respectively (ref). The P3-type structure is a metastable structure with a stacking

 55

arrangement of ABBCCA due to some Li+ ions remaining in the structure. There are no Li+ ions
present in the O1-type structure, resulting in stacking arrangement of ABABAB where there’s a single
CoO2 sheet per unit cell.24 The phase transition from the original P3-type to the O1-type occurs due
to a change in symmetry from rhombohedral to monoclinic to achieve higher stability.24,25 A loss of
crystallinity is observed after both acid delithiation and microwave exfoliation due to crystal phase
changes and reordering of crystal stacking arrangements.
 After microwave exfoliation, the presence and intensity of the (003) in both NMC and LCO
indicate that the 2D oxide lattice slab remains intact (Figures 2.1 and 2.2). In contrast, the
disappearance of the higher angle reflections indicates a loss of long-range order that corresponds to
successful exfoliation of the bulk material into NSs. 15,16

6.4.2 Electron Microscopy
 Electron microscopy was used to obtain information about the morphology of the bulk
layered oxide material, as well as exfoliated nanosheets. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images
of as-obtained commercial NMC show spheroidal particles that display a broad range of sizes; the
average size of the NMC particles in the bulk are 6 ± 2.2 µm. (Figure 2.3 a) SEM images obtained for
as-obtained commercial LCO show large agglomerates of particles with large variation in the sizing of
particles throughout the bulk sample. The average size of the particles measured were 11 ± 6.5 µm
(Figure 2.3 b). For both NMC and LCO, the nanosheet layers of the bulk particles begin to separate
after acid delithiation, however, most of the particles are still intact. Exfoliated nanosheets are not yet
present in the sample. As Li+ ions are removed during delithiation, holes or channels are formed
creating spaces between the stacked nanosheets in the particle (Figures 2.3 c-d). After exfoliation,
sheets can be seen throughout both samples (Figures 2.3 e-f). SEM imaging indicated a nearly
complete conversion of both NMC and LCO, with no areas of bulk material observable.

6.5 Discussion
 Effective methods have been established for the bulk production of exfoliated NMC and LCO
nanosheets via MW solvothermal treatment. Production of high-quality mono/bilayer NMC (111)
and LCO NSs with near-unity conversion from bulk were achieved with a significantly simplified

 58

processing scheme and dramatically reduced reaction times compared to current state-of-the-art
battery cathode exfoliation techniques. Established literature (cite) methods can produce nanosheets
in 2 days at best with low yields, while our method can produce nanosheets in 1-2 hours for NMC
and LCO with high yields, respectively. The reduction in time, coupled with lower energy
consumption, renders this method both cost-effective, environmentally sustainable, and more suitable
for direct upcycling.
 Previously established exfoliation methods are well established but can be inefficient for
nanosheet formation. These methods, such as hydrothermal/solvothermal, electrochemical, and
mechanical exfoliation methods, lend themselves to low yield, decreased nanosheet quality, and long
reaction times. High temperatures and pressures, as well as the use of harsh solvents can also negatively
affect nanosheet formation and quality. Conventional heating methods such as solvothermal Parr
reactors and high-temperature organic-phase synthesis limit control over crystal structure and
composition by creating inefficient heat transfer throughout the sample.14

 The microwave exfoliation procedure presented in our study addresses the synthetic
challenges posed by these conventional methods. The method is a facile “one-pot” technique that is
fast, reproducible, and uniquely viable for scaling to commercial use. These enhancements are enabled
by directly coupling MW energy to MW-active reaction species, which rapidly transfers energy directly
to the reaction through a microwave transparent vessel allowing for localized super heating of the
solvent and direct interaction with NMC/LCO and bulky TBA+ cations.26 There is also better chemical
distribution within the microwave reaction vessel during the reaction, as compared to solvothermal
Parr reactors, due to continuous stirring within the MW reactor.27
 Homogeneous distribution of heat and reaction species allows for better intercalation of the
TBA+ ions into the interlayer space of the layered metal oxides. A higher degree of layer separation
occurs with better loading of TBA+ cations and facilitates more complete exfoliation.28 Quality of
exfoliation, defined by the generation of mono- and bilayer NSs rather than thicker or partially
agglomerated structures, is largely promoted by high surface energies during the reaction. In contrast,
low surface energy tends to generate larger (micron-scale) nanosheet aggregates that do not achieve
full separation.29 The rapid and homogeneous heat distribution generated by MW dielectric heating
promotes these high surface energies.
 Common exfoliation methods can include long periods of sonication or shear force to achieve
the same level of intercalation and exfoliation. While not just being time consuming, these techniques
damage the nanosheets from constant force, leading to poor nanosheet quality. Microwave exfoliation
eliminates the need for such force, thus maintaining nanosheet integrity and quality. Very few
nanosheets observed contained defects or undesirable phase changes (see Figure 2.4c and 2.4d).
Reaction times for exfoliation are significantly reduced from up to 7 days required for electrochemical
exfoliation techniques to 1 or 2 hours for NMC and LCO, respectively. 15,16 In this time, approximately
95% of the bulk layered metal oxide material is exfoliated into nanosheets. This is significantly greater
than the highest reported exfoliation yields of 60% from electrochemical and soft-chemical
conventional heating methods.28,29
 While being good sources of alternative energy and storage, lithium-ion battery production is
labor intensive, costly, and contains precious and increasingly scarce minerals, such as lithium, nickel,
cobalt, and manganese. In 2019, it was estimated that in the United States alone, only about 5% of
lithium-ion batteries made it to recycling plants; the other 95% are improperly discarded in the trash.
Once in landfills, such metals are leached into the soil and water systems, causing environmental
contamination. Current recycling methods, while necessary, tend to be destructive and waste these
critical metals. The focus is now shifting to direct upcycling methods that allow for the reuse of

 59

materials. Methods are being developed to aid in direct upcycling approaches to save these precious
resources and reduce environmental pollution.

6.6 Conclusions
 A new low temperature microwave method was developed to obtain thin-layer cobalt oxide
nanosheets from the exfoliation of LCO and NMC battery cathode materials. This method utilizes
the insertion of bulky TBA+ cations after a proton exchange step, followed by microwave heating to
achieve increased yields of high quality nanosheets at low temperatures and reduced reaction times
compared to previously published methods. MW irradiation interacts directly with reaction species to
promote homogeneous heat distribution and instantaneous localized superheating, accelerating
exfoliation rates and increasing conversion from bulk oxides to nanosheets. The newly obtained
nanosheets can be further manipulated by such processes as ion-exchange or restacking to create new
nanomaterials and nanocomposites with potentially new electronic, optical, and mechanical properties
to be used as new LIB cathodes or new applications. This facile method will directly contribute to the
ease of direct upcycling of spent cathode materials by being cost effective, energy efficient, and low
energy.

7. SEED PROJECT: LITHIUM METAL RECYCLING AND THE DIRTY
 ELECTRODE PARADIGM

7.1 Introduction

 While this LDRD project has focused on NMC cathode electrodes in lithium-ion batteries,
which are the most economically costly and critical-material-intensive parts of widely used electric
vehicle batteries, the recycling of future-oriented batteries is also considered in a seed project funded
by the SUPN and led by Prof. Fang Liu at the University of Wisconsin at Madison. One proposed
next-generation high energy battery is lithium-sulfur (Li-S). Sulfur is an earth-abundant cathode
material, and recycling the cathode would not be a priority. However, to achieve high energy density,
lithium metal anodes are required for Li-S batteries. Lithium metal is extremely reactive and is a
potential safety hazard; therefore, safe recycling and recovery methods need to be devised. Many all-
solid-state battery and other next-generation lithium battery designs also involve Li-metal anodes.
Hence the work described in this chapter is highly relevant to multiple future energy storage devices.
Sandian Joshua Lamb (07446) consulted and collaborated with Liu in this work.
 The experimental work involves the chemical reaction between electrochemically deposited
lithium electrode and gases like nitrogen and oxygen. Converting lithium with nitrogen at 180 oC is
found to be superior and more efficient than reacting lithium with oxygen, which must be conducted
at lower temperatures due to safety concerns.
 Interestingly, even at room temperature, N2 gas is known to react with fresh lithium metal to
completion in at a several-day time scale. Despite that fact that O2 is more reactive than N2, pure O2
or air (which has ~20% O2) forms white passivation layers on Li metal surfaces that prevent
uncontrolled reactions yielding complete conversion to Li2O. The reason for the N2 reaction going
to completion is often attributed to the cracking of LixN surface films [1]. However, this does not
transparently explain why the LixO surface film does not appear to crack.
 Motivated by these observations, we also conducted DFT calculations on O2 and N2 reactions
on lithium metal surfaces coated with thin films of Li2O and Li3N. Such calculations constitute an
example of the “dirty electrode paradigm” relevant to not only batteries [2], but structural metal
corrosion protection [3], and emerging electro-catalysis approaches that involve protective surface
films (“SEI”), e.g., lithium-mediated ammonia synthesis [4].
 This combined experimental/theoretical work has not been submitted for publication.

7.2 Experimental Methods and Results

 Lithium metal batteries are among the most promising candidates for next-generation energy
storage, particularly in transportation applications. However, the rising cost of lithium and
uncertainties in its supply chain pose significant challenges. Recycling lithium from lithium metal
anodes in spent batteries offers a potential pathway to address these issues. In this study, we
demonstrate a gas-mediated process to convert deposited lithium into lithium nitride (Li3N), followed
by recovery through a water-based chemical reaction. This approach achieves a conversion efficiency
of approximately 93%, highlighting its promise for sustainable lithium recycling.

 63

 In lithium metal batteries, lithium metal is the most reactive component. When exposed to air
or water, it can readily ignite, posing significant safety risks. This issue is particularly pronounced in
spent batteries, which often contain substantial amounts of inactive or “dead” lithium. The goal of
this work is to develop a safe and efficient process by first converting metallic lithium into lithium
nitride under nitrogen gas, and subsequently into lithium hydroxide (LiOH) through its mild reaction
with water. To understand lithium utilization, we first evaluated the Coulombic efficiency (CE) of
lithium in four common electrolytes and examined the time required for CE to reach equilibrium.
Among these, LP57 exhibited the lowest average CE of 36.4% and equilibrium around 15 cycles
(Figure 7.1a). High-concentration electrolytes, including 4 M LiTFSI and 4 M LiFSI in DME,
achieved CEs of 89.9% and 98.2%, respectively, and equilibrium around 10 cycles (Figure 7.1b-c). A
typical electrolyte for Li–S batteries, 1 M LiTFSI in DOL/DME, reached 96.1%, and the addition of
2 wt% LiNO3 further boosted the CE to 98.7% (Figure 7.1d-f). For subsequent experiments, we
selected 1 M LiTFSI in DOL/DME with 2 wt% LiNO3 as the electrolyte, as it delivers a high CE and
is also compatible with Li–S batteries.

7.3 Computational Methods and Results
 The computational work applies DFT methods similar to those discussed in Chapter 1
published in Ref. [5]. The base simulation cells for Li metal (111) facet coated with Li2O (111) have
dimensions 17.04x9.84x40.00 A3 with a Li207O36 stoichiometry. The surface is perpendicular to the z-
direction. An O2 or N2 molecule is further added to either the base cell or 1x2, 1x3, or 2x2 surface
supercells. The Li3N-coated Li metal base simulation cell is hexagonal in shape, with dimensions
14.26x14.26x36 A3 and a Li210N32 stoichiometry. An N2 molecule and several Li atoms are then added,
as discussed below. We also consider a simulation cell with only a Li3N surface film and no Li metal
layers, with a Li204N72 stoichiometry. Since some O2- and N2- species may have net spins, spin-
polarized DFT method is applied unless otherwise stated.

Figure 7.3 (a) Physisorbed O2, E=+2.01 eV; (b) chemisorbed O2, E=0.00 eV; (c) O-O bond broken, E= -3.96 eV.
Pink and red spheres are Li and O, respectively. The O2 species are partially charged.
 Figure 7.3 depicts the initial physisorbed O2 on Li2O-coated Li metal, the chemisorbed O2
intermediate, and the decomposed product with the O-O bond broken. The energies relative to the
intermediate are E=+2.01 eV, 0.00 eV, and -3.96 eV, respectively, computed using the 2x2 supercell.
The reason large supercells are needed to converge E is as follows. The reactions involve electron
transfer from the Li metal Fermi level to the O2 through the dielectric Li2O film. This leads to large
dipole moments in the simulation cell, the lateral periodic images of which repel each other. If this
were a reaction at constant applied potential, a different way to think of it is the following. The change
in dipole moment strongly modifies the electrode potential () in the simulation cell via  = 4
d/A. where d is the change in dipole moment and A is the lateral area of the simulation cell. “A”
needs to be sufficiently large to render the reaction-induced  small to mimic constant potential
conditions. The use of large supercell is a common feature of “dirty electrode paradigm” calculations
[1]; it can be alleviated by the use of constant-electrochemical-potential DFT codes which are however
still in their infancy [6].

 The reaction barriers, computed using the smallest (base) simulation cells, are E*=0.17 eV
and 0.30 eV going from the physisorbed species to the intermediate, and the from the intermediate to
the final structures, respectively. They may not be fully converged with respect to simulation cell sizes,
but since increasing the cell size decreases E* and the barriers are already small, it is unnecessary to
fully converge these quantities; the calculations indicate that reactions are fast and exothermic. It is
however worth visualizing the reaction pathway. The first reaction step involves transport of two Li+
on the oxide surface to coordinate to the physisorbed O2; these Li+ are replaced by Li+ deeper in the

surface film (Figure 7.4). In fact, the intermediate (Figure 7.3b) was discovered during an initial
barrier-finding calculation which attempted to find a single barrier between Figure 7.3a and 7.3c. In
both Figure 7.3a and 7.3b, the O2 is partially electrochemically reduced, and exhibits a longer O-O

 The Li2O surface film on Li metal has been shown to yield, by coincidence, almost the
equilibrium 0 V vs. Li+/Li(s) without the need to include solvent molecules [1]. Under battery charging
conditions when the anode is at 0 V, the calculations above suggest that O2 gas molecule
electrochemical reduction is viable and fast, at least when the surface film is thin.
 Next we switch the O2 to an adsorbed N2 (not shown in figures). The decomposition and
N-N bond breaking are far less favorable, with E=+1.0 eV instead of -3.9 eV when comparing the
N-equivalent of Figure 7.3b (the “intermediate”) and 7c. This is despite the fact that the N-N bond
length in the intermediate is 1.42 A, which indicates a single bond in a N24- species. This charge state
assignment is confirmed by maximally localized Wannier function analysis. The unfavorable E is
likely caused by insufficient Li+ surrounding the two N3- ions. Indeed, one of the N ions is only
coordinated to 5 Li+, unlike the 6 Li+ expected in Li3N crystal. This illustrates the significantly lower
reactivity of N2 against Li metal compared with O2.

Figure 7.5. (a) Physisorbed N2 on Li3N on Li(s). (b) Chemisorption to form N3- species. (c) Breaking a N-N bond to
form N plus a different N2. Pink, blue, and green spheres represent Li, N, and N (from N2) atoms.
 Finally, we consider N2 reacting with Li3N-coated Li metal (Figure 7.5). The results are less
clear-out than on Li2O surfaces. First, attempts at breaking the N-N- multiple bond leads to the
configuration reverting back to an adsorbed N2 species. Li+ ions do not spontaneously diffuse
through the Li3N film, unlike in Figure 7.4. Instead, we discovered that a different reaction pathway,
which involves the initial formation of N3 species via N2 addition to a surface N3-, is both
energetically favorable and kinetically viable (low reaction barriers). Therefore four N2 are attached
to four of the eight surface N anions in the base simulation cell to create a new baseline surface model.
However, further bond-breaking to generate separate N3- anions is unfavorable. To proceed further,
we added 11 Li atoms to the surface to coordinate to the N3 species. The reaction energies to form a
N2 and a N species from one of the N3 moiety is now an extremely favorable E=-2.35 eV, with a
low barrier of E*=0.13 eV. The last step to break up the N2 buried inside the Li3N film is exothermic,
E=-0.47 eV. However, the barrier for this last step is E*=1.40 eV.
 The reason for this high barrier is apparently that the N-N bond order is 1.5, appropriate to a
N21.5- species as suggested by our Wannier analysis. Despite the significant number of Li atoms added

 67

to the surface coordinated to what initially were N3 species, the resulting N2 is not sufficiently reduced,
electrochemically speaking. The reason is most likely that the Fermi level of the lithium metal is too
high. We estimate that the systems in Figure 9 is at ~0.5 V vs. Li+/Li(s), significantly higher than that
for Li2O. We confirm this point by removing the Li metal, using a thicker Li3N slab, and redoing the
above calculations. This forces the electrons from the added Li surface atoms to reside in the nitrogen
species instead of escaping to the lithium metal reservoir. The final N-N breaking barrier is now
E*=0.9 eV, which is attainable in 1-hour reaction time frames. Wannier analysis confirms that, in
this case, the N-N bond is a single bond, pertinent to N24- species.
 Here we summarize what we have learned from Figure 7.5. First, like many “dirty electrode”
models, the reaction with the surface passivating film can generate complex, amorphous-solid-like
configurations. Such calculations may yield insight, but without extensive sampling of the
reactant/product configuration space, the E and E* values obtained should considered preliminary.
Second, we have not solved the mystery regarding why O2 passivates Li metal while N2 doesn’t.
However, one possible conjecture may be the diffusion of N2- species inside the Li3N film towards
the anode surface. When these dinitrogen species reach the Li metal, they can be further reduced,
generating new species accompanied by mechanical stress that may crack a film of sufficient thickness.
Third, electron transport through the surface film may yield a non-adiabatic kinetic barrier which is
not captured by ground-state DFT calculations. Such electron tunneling barrier should be minimal in
models with thin (<10 A) surface films, however. Finally, the predicted presence of N3- and N2-
species in the nominally Li3N stoichiometric surface films originating from N2-Li metal reactions may
be highly relevant to lithium-mediated ammonia (LMA) synthesis. Would LMA involve chemical
intermediates such as H2N2-? Or would such intermediates be counter-productive and yield
undesirable side products such as hydrazine? Such questions, motivated by our finding, will be
investigated using DFT methods in the future.

8. OUTLOOK

 In addition to the technical accomplishments, Davis-Wheeler-Chin and Leung were selected
to participate in the DOE Energy I-Corps entrepreneurial training program based on Davis-Wheeler-
Chin’s exfoliation work. She also filed a provisional patent application. This training allowed us to
establish contacts with many industrial and non-government organization partners, and will be helpful
towards securing potential future funding opportunities.
 As of 2025, the battery recycling industry is experiencing significant challenges, with a number
of high-profile bankruptcies of North American companies which were in the process of building
expensive (>=$100 million) hydrometallurgy factories. We believe this suggests basic research that
can enable non-traditional (i.e., non-hydro- and pyro-metallurgy) methods which can reduce recycling
cost, including capital equipment cost, has become even more urgent.
 The different thrusts of this project, dealing with successive steps in battery recycling, were
designed to work together as a cohesive whole. However, individually they can also fit into existing,
commercialized recycling schemes, or in the case of froth floatation, future direct recycling schemes.
Looking even further into the future, the recycling of lithium-sulfur and all-solid-state batteries which
use lithium metal as anodes have seldom been discussed. Our SUPN-funded seed project will shed
light on lithium metal recovery, and help inject ease-of-recycling insights into the design of such
batteries.
 Finally, this project can also contribute to non-battery-recycling related research areas. The
selective adsorption/separation of multivalent metal ions will benefit the extraction/purification of
other critical materials/elements, including lanthanides. The exfoliation/ion-exchange/sintering
route can potentially lead to new synthetic approaches for creating new materials which are difficult
to synthesize in other ways. The study of Li +N2 reactions will benefit not just recycling, but also Li-
passivation/safety issues for next-generation lithium batteries as well as lithium-mediated ammonia
synthesis.
