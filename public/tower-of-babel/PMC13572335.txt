Spontaneous miscarriage and later lifestyle in predicting hazard of premature mortality: a cohort study in UK Biobank
Authors: Ying Hu, Rui Tang, Xiang Li, Xuan Wang, Hao Ma, Yoriko Heianza, Lu Qi, Zhaoxia Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo prospectively assess the association of spontaneous miscarriage with the hazard of premature mortality and to examine the interaction between spontaneous miscarriage and lifestyle in relation to premature mortality.MethodsA total of 231,148 participants from the UK Biobank were included. The unhealthy lifestyle was defined as an unhealthy diet, smoking, irregular physical activity, and an abnormal body mass index. Cox proportional hazards models were used to evaluate the associations.ResultsDuring a median follow-up time of 12.3 years, 11,672 premature mortalities were documented. Compared to women who did not have a spontaneous miscarriage, the multivariable-adjusted hazard ratios (aHRs) for all-cause premature mortality according to the number of spontaneous miscarriages were 0.98 (95% confidence interval [CI] 0.93–1.04) for one, 1.16 (1.06–1.27) for two, and 1.24 (1.10–1.39) for three or more, respectively (P-trend < 0.001). In contrast, the ever-versus-never comparison (any miscarriage) was not significant after adjustment for lifestyle factors. We found a significant interaction on an additive scale between a greater number of spontaneous miscarriages and the unhealthy lifestyle score (P for additive interaction = 0.03) on the risk of premature mortality.ConclusionsOur findings indicate that recurrent spontaneous miscarriage (≥ 2 events) serves as a risk marker for higher hazard of all-cause premature mortality, particularly death from cardiovascular disease, whereas a single miscarriage and the ever-versus-never comparison showed no significant association after full adjustment. A postnatal unhealthy lifestyle may partly account for the observed association, suggesting that lifestyle modification could be a focus for future preventive strategies in this high-risk population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00404-026-08471-7.

Introduction

Spontaneous miscarriage, occurring in 12–24% of clinically recognized pregnancies, is one of the most prevalent adverse pregnancy outcomes. [1, 2] Notably, the prevalence among US women increased by a relative 1% per year from 1990 to 2011 [3]. Previous studies have shown that a history of spontaneous miscarriage is associated with higher risks of preeclampsia, preterm birth and other adverse obstetric and perinatal outcomes. [4–6] Moreover, the potential impact of spontaneous miscarriage on women is not limited to perinatal outcomes. Previous studies have also shown that spontaneous miscarriage is significantly associated with the risk of mental illness later in life [7–9], the risk of chronic metabolic diseases [10–16], and even the risk of premature mortality. [17–20] Spontaneous miscarriage displays a robust pattern of recurrence, with the odds ratio for occurrence escalating from 1.5 after one miscarriage to 2.2 after two, and 4.0 after three. [21] Notably, conflicting results were observed in previous studies investigating the association of spontaneous miscarriage with premature mortality risk; both positive and inverse associations were found [17–20].

Certain lifestyle factors, such as smoking, obesity, and physical inactivity, are established risk factors for premature mortality [22]. Recent evidence has shown the relation between adverse pregnancy outcomes and later-life risk of disorders may be modified by lifestyle factors. Previous research has reported that a healthy diet can offset the higher risk of type 2 diabetes later in life due to gestational diabetes. [23, 24] Adopting a heart-healthy diet and increasing physical activity among women with adverse pregnancy outcomes are important lifestyle interventions to decrease cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk in later life [25]. We therefore hypothesized that the association between spontaneous miscarriage and the hazard of premature mortality may be modified by later lifestyle. To date, no study has assessed the joint association between spontaneous miscarriage and unhealthy lifestyle in relation to the hazard of premature mortality.

In this study, we prospectively investigated the association between spontaneous miscarriage and the hazard of premature mortality in women from UK Biobank. We particularly assessed the joint association of spontaneous miscarriage and established unhealthy lifestyle scores with the hazard of premature mortality.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

The UK Biobank is a large population-based prospective cohort in the United Kingdom, and the details of the study design have been previously described [26]. Briefly, over 0.5 million participants (aged 40–69 years) were recruited in the baseline survey between 2006 and 2010 with ongoing follow-up. All the enrolled participants provided written informed consent. The study was approved by both the National Health Service National Research Ethics Service and the Institutional Review Board of Tulane University.

We included 231,148 participants after excluding 41,924 participants without pregnancy experience or with missing information about gravidity, and 46 participants who did not have a history of spontaneous history before enrollment but were subsequently diagnosed with spontaneous miscarriage after enrollment (Supplemental Fig. 1).Fig. 1Cumulative incidence for all-cause premature mortality. Exposure assessment

Spontaneous miscarriage was defined by self-report from participants at enrollment or by the International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes (Supplemental Table 1). At enrollment, all the female participants were asked “Have you ever had any stillbirths, spontaneous miscarriages or terminations?” by a touchscreen questionnaire at baseline (2006–2010). Participants selected one answer from four options: (i) yes; (ii) no; (iii) do not know; (iv) prefer not to answer. Participants who indicated their answers of “yes” on the above questionnaire were then asked, “How many spontaneous miscarriages?” Participants selected one answer from the following options: (i) provided integer data (except zero); (ii) zero; (iii) do not know; (iv) prefer not to answer. Those who did not know or preferred not to answer were assigned a missing value. Participants who provided integer data (except zero) were defined as self-reporting spontaneous miscarriage. In addition, spontaneous miscarriage was also defined by self-report from participants when they were verbally interviewed by trained staff at baseline. Outcomes ascertainment

Information on death and death date was obtained by reviewing the death certificates held by the National Health Service Information Centre for participants in England and Wales and the National Health Service Central Register Scotland for participants from Scotland. Deaths that occurred at ages younger than 80 were defined as premature in our study, as analysis for all non-communicable disease mortality in the report of United Nations [27]. Cause-specific mortality was classified as CVD with International Classification of Diseases-10th Revision (ICD-10) code of I00-I99, cancer with code of C00-D48, and all other causes. Participants who died at or after age 80 were censored as non-events. Follow-up for all participants started at the date of enrollment, ended when death was recorded, loss to follow-up, or the administrative censoring date (31 May 2021), whichever came first.

Assessment of covariates

Sociodemographic factors including age, ethnicity, education level, average household income, and Townsend deprivation index (based on the participant’s postcode, higher scores indicate a higher level of social deprivation) were obtained by a touchscreen questionnaire at baseline. Reproductive factors including number of live births, early menopause, and hormone treatment were also obtained by a touchscreen questionnaire at baseline. History of gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) or hypertensive disorder in pregnancy (HDP) was captured either from self-report at baseline or from diagnosis before baseline using ICD codes. Lifestyle factors, such as alcohol intake frequency and smoking status, were also obtained by a touchscreen questionnaire at baseline. Body weight and height were measured at baseline during the initial assessment center visit, and body mass index (BMI) was then calculated as body weight (Kg) divided by height squared (m2). Physical activity was calculated from the following items obtained by the touchscreen questionnaire: duration of moderate activity, number of days per week of moderate physical activity, duration of vigorous activity, and number of days per week of vigorous physical activity. Following equations were used to calculate physical activity: Moderate-intensity activity (minutes/week) = duration of moderate activity (minutes/day) × number of days per week of moderate physical activity (days/week); Vigorous-intensity activity (minutes/week) = duration of vigorous activity (minutes/day) × number of days per week of vigorous physical activity (days/week). Regular physical activity was defined as physical activity ≥ 150 min of moderate-intensity activity per week or ≥ 75 min of vigorous-intensity activity per week or an equivalent combination per week according to the Physical Activity Guidelines [28]. A healthy diet score was established according to the American Heart Association Guidelines and determined by the following items: 1) Total fruits: ≥ 4.5 pieces per week; 2) Total vegetables: ≥ 4.5 servings per week (3 tablespoons of vegetables considered as 1 serving); 3) Total fish: ≥ 2 servings per week; 4) Processed meat: ≤ twice per week; 5) Red meat ≤ 5 times per week [29]. Details on the covariates, their definitions, and functional form are included in (Supplemental Table 2). Assessment of unhealthy lifestyle score

We included four lifestyle factors in our unhealthy lifestyle score: diet, smoking, physical activity, and body mass index. For each lifestyle factor, the participant received a score of 0 if she met the criterion for low risk and 1 for high risk. For diet, we defined low risk as the diet score in the top 40% of the cohort distribution. For smoking, we defined low risk as being a non-smoker. For physical activity, we classified low risk as regular physical activity. We defined low-risk BMI as between 18.5 and 24.9 kg/m2.

Statistical analysis

The baseline characteristics were presented as n (%) for categorical variables, mean (SD) for normally distributed continuous variables, and median (P25, P75) for non-normally distributed continuous variables. Cox proportional hazard models were used to estimate the hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for spontaneous miscarriage with hazard of all-cause and cause-specific premature mortality. The proportional hazards assumption was tested using Schoenfeld residuals. Several potential confounders were adjusted in Cox models. Model 1 was adjusted for age, ethnicity, education level, average household income, and Townsend deprivation index. Model 2 was further adjusted for number of live births, GDM, HDP, early menopause, and hormone treatment. Model 3 was further adjusted for BMI, alcohol intake frequency, diet score, physical activity, and smoke status based on Model 2.

We performed stratified analysis by the following factors: age (below versus at or above the mean), ethnicity (white versus non-white), education level (categorized as college versus non-college), average household income (< £18,000, £18,000–30999 or ≥ £31,000), Townsend deprivation index (below versus at or above the median), number of live births (0, 1 or ≥ 2), GDM (no versus yes), HDP (no versus yes), early menopause (no versus yes), hormone treatment (no versus yes), alcohol intake frequency (daily/almost daily, less than three or four times a week or never), BMI (≤ 24.9, 25–29.9 or ≥ 30 kg/m2), diet score (below versus at or above the mean), physical activity (regular versus irregular), and smoking status (never, previous or current). To evaluate interactions between the number of spontaneous miscarriages and these factors, multiplicative interaction was assessed by adding interaction terms to the Cox models. In addition, we performed a joint analysis of the number of spontaneous miscarriages and unhealthy lifestyle factors on the hazard of all-cause premature mortality.

We evaluated whether the associations between spontaneous miscarriage and premature mortality differed by later unhealthy lifestyle by using multiplicative interaction and additive interaction analyses. We coded variables with the lowest risk as reference (without spontaneous miscarriage and unhealthy lifestyle score < 3) and considered the number of spontaneous miscarriages as a categorical variable and the unhealthy lifestyle score as a dichotomous categorical variable to assess the additive interaction. We assessed the relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI) as an index of additive interaction, and examined the decomposition of the joint effect, which is the attributable proportion (AP) to spontaneous miscarriage alone, to unhealthy lifestyle alone, and to their interaction.

In this study, lifestyle factors (BMI, smoking, diet, physical activity, and alcohol intake) served dual roles depending on the analytical objective. In multivariable-adjusted models (Model 3), they were included as confounders. In interaction analyses, they were treated as effect modifiers to examine whether the association between miscarriage and mortality differed by lifestyle level. These are complementary analytical goals. A directed acyclic graph (DAG) illustrating the assumed causal relationships is provided in Supplementary Fig. 2. In sensitivity analyses, we first conducted analyses with further adjustments for hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, CVD, and cancer at baseline based on Model 3. Second, we excluded participants with diabetes, CVD or cancer at baseline and repeated the main analyses. Third, we excluded deaths due to complications of pregnancy, childbirth, and the puerperium. Fourth, we conducted sensitivity analyses using a stricter definition of premature mortality (< 70 years). We also used attained age as the time scale with delayed entry at age at recruitment.

Statistical analyses were performed with SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc, Cary, NC, USA). All P values were two-sided and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics of participants according to the number of spontaneous miscarriages

Among 231,148 women who had a history of pregnancy, 24.2% (n = 55,938) had at least one spontaneous miscarriage. Baseline characteristics of participants according to the number of spontaneous miscarriages are shown in Table 1. Compared with participants without a history of spontaneous miscarriage, participants who experienced more spontaneous miscarriages were more likely to be younger, of non-White ethnicity, to have a college degree, and to have a greater Townsend deprivation index. They were more likely to experience early menopause, had higher prevalence of a history of GDM or HDP, and to be current smokers.

Association between spontaneous miscarriage and hazard of premature mortality

During a median follow-up time of 12.3 years, 11,672 incident cases of all-cause premature death were documented, including 1677 deaths from cardiovascular disease and 6909 from cancer. Women who experienced at least one spontaneous miscarriage were 6% (1–11%) more likely to die prematurely than women who did not have a spontaneous miscarriage after adjustment for sociodemographic factors and reproductive factors. However, the hazard ratio (1.04, 95% CI 1.00–1.09) became non-significant after further adjustment for lifestyle factors (Supplemental Table 4).

Figure 1 displays cumulative incidence for all-cause premature mortality. These curves show an increasing trend in the premature mortality incidence along with the increasing number of spontaneous miscarriages. The multivariable-adjusted hazard ratios for all-cause premature mortality according to the number of spontaneous miscarriages were 0.98 (0.93–1.04) for one spontaneous miscarriage, 1.16 (1.06–1.27) for two spontaneous miscarriages, and 1.24 (1.10–1.39) for three or more spontaneous miscarriages relative to none (P-trend < 0.001) (Table 2). These results remained consistent after further adjustments for hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, CVD, and cancer based on model 3 (Supplemental Table 5); or excluding participants with diabetes, CVD, or cancer at baseline (Supplemental Table 6); or excluding participants whose death was caused by complications of pregnancy, childbirth, and the puerperium (Supplemental Table 7). In sensitivity analyses using a stricter definition of premature mortality (< 70 years), the associations were consistent in direction and magnitude with the primary analyses using the < 80-year cutoff (Supplemental Table 8). In a sensitivity analysis using attained age as the time scale with delayed entry, the results were consistent with the primary analysis (Supplementary Table 9).

Moreover, we found no evidence of effect modification of the association between spontaneous miscarriage and all-cause premature mortality by potential risk factors using multiplicative interaction (Supplemental Table 10).

For cause-specific premature mortality, we found that a greater number of spontaneous miscarriages was significantly associated with a higher hazard of cardiovascular disease mortality (P-trend = 0.04), but not for cancer mortality or all other causes of mortality (P-trend = 0.07 and P-trend = 0.08, respectively) (Supplemental Table 11).

Joint association between the number of spontaneous miscarriages and unhealthy lifestyle in relation to hazard of all-cause premature mortality

We found a significant additive interaction between the number of spontaneous miscarriages (categorized as 0, 1, 2, and ≥ 3) and the unhealthy lifestyle score (P for additive interaction = 0.03). The relative risk of premature mortality associated with a combination of each additional spontaneous miscarriage category and higher unhealthy lifestyle score was 1.59 (1.54–1.64), which was more than the addition of the risk associated with each individual factor (1.05 [1.02–1.08] and 1.47 [1.40–1.55]), with a RERI of 0.07 (0.01–0.13). The APs of joint effect were 7.9% (95% CI 3.0–12.7%) to the number of spontaneous miscarriages alone, 80.1% (68.5–91.8%) to the unhealthy lifestyle score alone, and 12% (1.7–22.3%) to their interaction (Table 3). The test for multiplicative interaction was not significant (P for multiplicative interaction = 0.26). Discussion

The results from this large cohort study from the UK Biobank, indicated that a greater number of spontaneous miscarriages was significantly associated with a higher hazard of all-cause premature mortality, after adjusting for socioeconomic level, reproductive factors, and lifestyle factors. Notably, a single miscarriage was not associated with increased risk after full adjustment, whereas recurrent miscarriage (two or more events) showed progressively higher hazards. We found a significant additive interaction between later unhealthy lifestyles and a greater number of spontaneous miscarriages on the risk of all-cause premature mortality. The joint effect could be decomposed to 7.9% attributable to the number of spontaneous miscarriages alone, 80.1% to unhealthy lifestyle score alone, and 12% to their additive interaction.

Few studies have assessed the association of spontaneous miscarriage with mortality risk and the results were inconsistent. A recent study from the USA reported the number of spontaneous miscarriages was significantly associated with a greater risk of all-cause premature death, particularly death from CVD [17]. Similar results were reported in a Danish register-based study. [18] Our results found that recurrent spontaneous miscarriage (two or more spontaneous miscarriages) [30] was significantly associated with a higher hazard of all-cause premature death, whereas experiencing it only once did not increase the risk of premature death. Conversely, two previous studies have reported an inverse association or no significant association between self-reported spontaneous miscarriage and cardiovascular disease mortality. [19, 20] Possible reasons for the inconsistent results include differences in characteristics of participants, inconsistent definitions of pregnancy loss (e.g., stillbirth, spontaneous and induced abortions were combined in the Japanese cohort), and lack of detailed data on various relevant confounders.

To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to investigate how prenatal risk factors and later unhealthy lifestyle are jointly related to the hazard of premature mortality. In our study, we found that the association between spontaneous miscarriage and the hazard of premature mortality attenuated after adjustment for BMI and lifestyle factors (alcohol intake frequency, diet score, physical activity, and smoke status) in the multivariable model. Moreover, our joint analysis found that the graded increasing hazard of premature mortality with increasing unhealthy lifestyle score was consistent across all the spontaneous miscarriage categories. These findings suggested an additive interaction between spontaneous miscarriage and unhealthy lifestyle score on the hazard of premature mortality. However, whether the higher risk of premature mortality associated with spontaneous miscarriage differs by a later healthy lifestyle is not clear. Our study provides evidence of an additive interaction between spontaneous miscarriage and later unhealthy lifestyle on the hazard of premature mortality. These findings suggested that the association between unhealthy lifestyle and premature mortality may be more pronounced in women with experience of spontaneous miscarriage.

The underlying mechanisms linking spontaneous miscarriage and premature mortality remain unclear. The metabolic changes in spontaneous miscarriage may contribute to premature mortality. For instance, insulin resistance, cardiometabolic disorder and unhealthy lifestyles (such as high BMI or smoking) all have been implicated in both spontaneous miscarriage [31–34] and premature mortality [22, 27, 35]. It is also plausible that spontaneous miscarriage triggers a cascade of events culminating in premature mortality, supported by the positive association between spontaneous miscarriage and a higher risk of cardiovascular disease, [10–14] type 2 diabetes [10, 15, 16] and psychiatric disorders, [9] all major contributors to premature mortality. [22, 36] Our findings, emphasizing an increased risk between spontaneous miscarriage and death from cardiovascular disease, also lend support to this notion. However, we lacked direct measurements of biomarkers such as inflammatory indices, endothelial function, or metabolic markers to test these mechanistic hypotheses. Therefore, these interpretations remain speculative, and further studies incorporating relevant biomarker data are warranted to comprehensively understand the underlying mechanisms linking spontaneous miscarriage to premature mortality.

Our findings provide important implications for public health. We found a significant additive interaction between spontaneous miscarriage and unhealthy lifestyle on the hazard of premature mortality, despite a modest RERI of 0.07. Additive interaction holds greater significance for public health interventions compared to multiplicative interaction [37]. In this large cohort, the attributable proportion due to interaction (AP = 12%) indicated that 12% of the joint risk among a women’s population with both a greater number of spontaneous miscarriages and an unhealthier lifestyle could be attributed to the synergistic effect of these two factors. [38, 39] This means that although the absolute RERI is small in a low-risk population, a meaningful proportion of the combined risk is driven by the interaction itself. The significant additive interaction also indicates that the public health consequence of unhealthy lifestyle may be larger in women with a history of spontaneous miscarriage than in those without.

Strengths of our study include the large sample size, long-term follow-up, prospective cohort design, and consistent results across various sensitivity analyses. An important strength is the availability of various reproductive and lifestyle factors for controlling confounders and examining effect modification. Our study had several limitations. First, the observational nature of our study imposed limitations on our ability to establish causal relationships. However, given the impracticality of randomizing women to experience spontaneous miscarriage, high-quality observational studies with long-term follow-up may provide the most robust evidence for investigation. Second, several selection biases should be considered. Because enrollment occurred at ages 40–69 (after the exposure), women who died before recruitment were never included (left truncation/survivor bias). Additionally, the UK Biobank is subject to “healthy volunteer” bias, as participants are healthier than the general population. Both biases would likely underestimate the true associations, making our estimates conservative. Third, lifestyle behaviors were assessed only at baseline using a questionnaire, and we could not account for potential changes over time during follow-up. This may introduce exposure misclassification and bias the interaction estimates. Fourth, spontaneous miscarriage history was self-reported, which may introduce recall bias, particularly for early pregnancy losses. Additionally, we lacked clinical subclassification of miscarriage etiologies (e.g., genetic, endocrine, or thrombotic factors), limiting our ability to infer specific biological mechanisms. Fifth, we lacked direct measurements of inflammatory, endothelial, or metabolic biomarkers, which prevented us from determining whether miscarriage reflects an underlying systemic vulnerability versus an independent contributor to subsequent disease. Sixth, because most of the UK Biobank cohort was non-Hispanic white women, our findings may not be applicable to other racial or ethnic groups. Seventh, we defined premature mortality as death before age 80 years. Although sensitivity analyses using a < 70-year cutoff yielded similar results, we acknowledge that a broader age cutoff may dilute associations. Additionally, we used follow-up time from enrollment as the time scale rather than attained age. Any resulting bias would likely be toward the null, making our estimates conservative. A sensitivity analysis using attained age as the time scale yielded consistent results. Last, despite our comprehensive control for traditional risk factors and potential confounding variables, residual confounding cannot be fully excluded. We lacked data on subclinical cardiometabolic disease, mental health, and healthcare access. If positively associated with both miscarriage and mortality, these unmeasured factors would bias our estimates toward the null.

In conclusion, a greater number of spontaneous miscarriages serves as a risk marker for a higher hazard of premature mortality, with unhealthy lifestyles showing an additive interaction. Although the association was attenuated after lifestyle adjustment, suggesting shared pathways, our findings identify women with recurrent miscarriage (≥ 2 events) as a high-risk population in whom lifestyle factors may partly account for the increased mortality risk.
