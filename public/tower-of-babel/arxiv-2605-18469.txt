Picard bundles and the degree of irrationality of Jacobians and Pryms
Federico Moretti and Andrés Rojas

Abstract
. For a smooth projective curve of genus g g , we study some positivity properties of (twisted) Picard bundles on the symmetric product. As applications, we prove that the degree of irrationality of any genus g g Jacobian is bounded from above by 2 g 2^{g} , and the degree of irrationality of any ( g − 1 ) (g-1) -dimensional Prym variety is bounded from above by 2 2 ​ g − 3 2^{2g-3} .

Let

in terms of the Fourier–Mukai transform. Beyond their natural appearance in the determinantal realization of the Brill–Noether loci

[
14

11

, cohomological regularity and continuous global generation
[
24

25
]
, or potential approaches to the Schottky problem
[
10
]

In this note we focus on the rank-
g
Picard bundle
F
whose fiber over
\alpha\in JC
admits a canonical identification

F|_{\alpha}\cong H^{1}\!\big(C,\mathcal{O}_{C}(-p_{0})\otimes\alpha\big),

where

denotes a fixed base point
1

1

For the purposes of this work, the line bundle

can be replaced by any line bundle of degree
-1
.

by passing to the symmetric product

.
More precisely, consider the Abel–Jacobi map

\pi:C^{(g)}\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}\Pic^{0}(C)=JC,\qquad p_{1}+...+p_{g}\longmapsto\mathcal{O}_{C}(p_{1}+...+p_{g}-gp_{0})

and denote by

We prove that the vector bundle

The main geometric application of our analysis of

The

6

However, an essentially complete solution is known in only very few cases, such as hypersurfaces in projective space

7

16
]

For abelian varieties of dimension

The key point in the proof of

with positive top Chern class produces, via a determinantal construction,

whose degree is bounded by the top Chern class of

Notably, the rational maps

In the last part of the paper we exploit a similar viewpoint, namely the use of suitable Picard type vector bundles, to find an upper bound for the degree of irrationality of Prym varieties. Recall that, for a smooth curve

For any
(g-1)

\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces ev}

For varieties of dimension
\geq 3
, computing the degree of a rational map (even understanding if a map is generically finite) using the description above can be difficult. However, the situation gets simpler when dealing with globally generated vector bundles:

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
X
be a smooth projective variety of dimension
n
, and let
E
be a globally generated vector bundle of rank
n
with
c_{n}(E)>0
. Then for a general
V^{\vee}\in\mathrm{Gr}(n+1,H^{0}(E))
the induced rational map
\varphi_{V}:X\dashrightarrow\mathbb{P}(V^{\vee})
is generically finite of degree
c_{n}(E)
.

Proof.

First note that
H^{0}(E^{\vee})=0
; indeed, if
H^{0}(E^{\vee})\neq 0
then by global generation
\mathcal{O}_{X}
must be a direct summand of
E
, which implies
c_{n}(E)=0
. Furthermore,

It is well known that a general
V^{\vee}\in\Gr(n+1,H^{0}(E))

E

W\in\Gr(n,H^{0}(E))

. For a general section

E

\Gamma_{W}
), the claim follows.

Therefore,
(E,V^{\vee})
is a good pair for a general

has 0-dimensional zero locus (of length

), according to

the induced rational map

is dominant of degree

. To prove

is disjoint from the (codimension

) locus

does not generate

Proof.

The morphism

Proof.

4.
Application to the degree of irrationality of Jacobians

With the study performed in the previous section, we are now in a position to prove
Theorem A
:

Proof of
Theorem A
.

In view of
subsection 2.1
and
section 3
, to establish that
\irr(JC)=\irr(C^{(g)})\leq 2^{g}
it suffices to show that
c_{g}(\widetilde{F})=2^{g}
.

To perform this computation, observe that
c_{i}(F)=[W_{g-i}(C)]
in the Chow group of
JC
by
[
17
,
27
]
, where
W_{g-i}(C)=C+\overset{g-i}{...}+C
. In particular,

c_{i}(\pi^{*}F)=\frac{(\pi^{*}\theta)^{i}}{i!}\in H^{2i}(C^{(g)},\mathbb{Z}).

On the other hand, denoting also by
x
the cohomology class
c_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{C^{(g)}}(x))\in H^{2}(C^{(g)},\mathbb{Z})
, it is well known that

x^{k}(\pi^{*}\theta)^{g-k}=\frac{g!}{k!}

(see
[
15
, Lemma 1]
). Applying the splitting principle (as in
[
12
, Remark 3.2.3]
) yields

c_{g}(\widetilde{F})=c_{g}(\pi^{*}F\otimes\mathcal{O}_{C^{(g)}}(x))=\sum_{k=0}^{g}x^{k}\frac{(\pi^{*}\theta)^{g-k}}{(g-k)!}=\sum_{k=0}^{g}\binom{g}{k}=2^{g},

which completes the proof.
∎

In other words, via the correspondence described in
subsection 2.1
, we have shown that a general element
V^{\vee}\in\mathrm{Gr}(g+1,H^{0}(\widetilde{F}))
induces a rational map
\varphi_{V}:C^{(g)}\dashrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{g}
which is dominant of degree
2^{g}
. In view of
section 3
, we can regard
\varphi_{V}
more geometrically as follows. We have the embedded curve

j:C\hookrightarrow|\omega_{C}((g+1)p_{0})|^{\vee}=\mathbb{P}^{2g-1}

and we have fixed a general
g
-dimensional linear space
\mathbb{P}(V^{\vee})\subset\mathbb{P}^{2g-1}
.
Then for a general
p_{1}+...+p_{g}\in C^{(g)}
, the
(g-1)
-dimensional span
\langle j(p_{1}),...,j(p_{g})\rangle
intersects
\mathbb{P}(V^{\vee})
at exactly one point; we define
\varphi_{V}(p_{1}+...+p_{g})\in\mathbb{P}(V^{\vee})
to be this intersection point.

It is natural to ask whether for every choice of the subspace
V^{\vee}
, the map
\varphi_{V}
remains dominant. More interestingly, one can also ask whether
\deg(\varphi_{V})
may drop for special choices of
V^{\vee}
(which would result in a better upper bound for
\irr(JC)
). The following lemma answers these questions in the case
g=2
:

Lemma 4.1
.

For every
V^{\vee}\in\mathrm{Gr}\left(g+1,H^{0}(\widetilde{F})\right)
,
(\widetilde{F},V^{\vee})
defines a good pair. Furthermore, if
g=2
then
(\widetilde{F},V^{\vee})
induces a rational dominant map
\varphi_{V}
of degree
4
.

Proof.

For the first claim, simply observe that any
V^{\vee}\in\mathrm{Gr}\left(g+1,H^{0}(\widetilde{F})\right)
generates
\widetilde{F}
in codimension 1, as a direct application of
section 3
for
k=g+1
.

Now assume
g=2
and consider any
V^{\vee}\in\mathrm{Gr}(3,H^{0}(\widetilde{F}))
. In view of
section 3
, a section of
\mathbb{P}V^{\vee}\setminus C
has vanishing locus of dimension
0
. Hence, the fiber computation in
subsection 2.1
shows that
\varphi_{V}
is dominant, and
\deg(\varphi_{V})<c_{2}(\widetilde{F})=4
if and only if all sections of
V^{\vee}
vanish at a common point; namely, if and only if
V^{\vee}\subset H^{0}(\widetilde{F}\otimes\mathcal{I}_{\zeta})

for some
\zeta\in C^{(2)}
.

But note that the latter condition cannot happen: since
h^{0}(\widetilde{F})=4
and
\widetilde{F}
is globally generated (recall

), we have
h^{0}(\widetilde{F}\otimes\mathcal{I}_{\zeta})=2
for every
\zeta\in C^{(2)}
. It follows that
\varphi_{V}
has degree

for every

.
∎

5.
The case of Prym varieties

In this section we apply a similar formalism to the study of Prym varieties, which will result in the upper bound of
Theorem B
.

5.1.
Basics on Prym varieties

Let us first recall some standard facts on Prym varieties; the reader may consult the seminal work
[
21
]
or
[
4
, Chapter 12]
for details. Let
f:\widetilde{C}\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}C
be an irreducible étale double cover of a smooth projective curve
C
of genus
g
. The curve
\widetilde{C}
has genus
2g-1
, and comes with an involution
\sigma:\widetilde{C}\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}\widetilde{C}
having no fixed point. The kernel of the norm map

\Nm_{f}:J\widetilde{C}\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}JC

has two connected components; the one containing the origin of
J\widetilde{C}
is called the
Prym variety

\Pr:=\Pr(f)
. It has dimension
g-1
and is naturally equipped with a principal polarization
\xi\in\NS(\Pr)
such that
2\xi
equals the restriction of the canonical principal polarization
\widetilde{\theta}
on
J\widetilde{C}
.

The Prym variety can also be realized as the image of the endomorphism
\Id-\sigma
of
J\widetilde{C}
; denoting by
j
the inclusion
\Pr\hookrightarrow J\widetilde{C}
, one has
j^{\vee}\circ\varphi_{\widetilde{\theta}}=\varphi_{\xi}\circ(\Id-\sigma)
. In other words, for every
\alpha\in J\widetilde{C}
:

(5.1)

P_{\alpha-\sigma(\alpha)}={{P_{\alpha}}_{|}}_{\Pr}\in{\Pr}^{\vee}.

If the curve
C
is hyperelliptic, then
\Pr
is a product of Jacobians (see
[
21
, Section 7]
) and the upper bound
\irr(\Pr)\leq 2^{2g-3}
follows immediately from
Theorem A
. For this reason, in the sequel we will assume that
C
is not hyperelliptic.

For a chosen point
p_{0}\in\widetilde{C}
, we consider the
Abel–Prym map

b:\widetilde{C}\hookrightarrow\Pr,\;\;\;p\mathchoice{\longmapsto}{\mapsto}{\mapsto}{\mapsto}\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{C}}\left(p-\sigma(p)-p_{0}+\sigma(p_{0})\right)

which embeds
\widetilde{C}
in
\Pr
as a curve of class
2\frac{\xi^{g-2}}{(g-2)!}\in H^{2g-4}(\Pr,\mathbb{Z})
. Note that
b
is the composition of the Abel–Jacobi embedding
a:\widetilde{C}\hookrightarrow J\widetilde{C}
(with base point
p_{0}
) and
\Id-\sigma:J\widetilde{C}\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}\Pr
.

For arbitrary
d
, the norm map extends to a map
\Pic^{d}(\widetilde{C})\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}\Pic^{d}(C)
, which we also denote by
\Nm_{f}
. In particular, the fiber
\Nm_{f}^{-1}(\omega_{C})
splits as a disjoint union of two natural torsors of
\Pr
:

\displaystyle{\Pr}^{+}:=\{L\in\Pic^{2g-2}(\widetilde{C}):\;\Nm_{f}(L)=\omega_{C},\;\text{$h^{0}(\widetilde{C},L)$ is even}\},

\displaystyle{\Pr}^{-}:=\{L\in\Pic^{2g-2}(\widetilde{C}):\;\Nm_{f}(L)=\omega_{C},\;\text{$h^{0}(\widetilde{C},L)$ is odd}\}.

These torsors provide realizations of two important loci: a canonical theta divisor
\Xi\subset{\Pr}^{+}
, and the
Prym–Brill–Noether locus

V^{2}(f)\subset{\Pr}^{-}
(see
[
28
]
). The latter is well known to have codimension 3, see for example
[
5
, Theorem 2.2]
.

We choose, once and for all, an element
M\in\Pr^{-}
. In this way, via tensor product with
M
(resp. with
M(\sigma(p_{0})-p_{0})
) we will freely identify
\Pr
with
\Pr^{-}
(resp. with
\Pr^{+}
), allowing us to regard both the theta divisor
\Xi
and
V^{2}(f)
as subvarieties of
\Pr
.

Consider the variety
X^{-}
defined by the cartesian diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X^{-}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\widetilde{C}^{(2g-2)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\Pr}^{-}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Pic^{2g-2}(\widetilde{C}),\ }

we will denote by
\pi
the corresponding birational morphism

.
It is an isomorphism over the complement of

, and will play the role of the Abel–Jacobi map

in the previous sections. Indeed,

will be deduced from a globally generated vector bundle of rank

on (a desingularization of)

, obtained as the twist of the pullback of a Picard type bundle on

For any line bundle
L\in\Pic^{g-1}(\widetilde{C})
satisfying the condition

, let us consider the

G:=\iota^{*}\varphi_{\xi}^{*}\left(R^{1}\Phi_{\Pr}(b_{*}L)\right)
on
\Pr
.

Proposition 5.1
.

The Prym–Picard sheaf
G
is locally free of rank
g-1
, with Chern classes

c_{i}(G)=2^{i}\frac{\xi^{i}}{i!}\in H^{2i}(\Pr,\mathbb{Z})

for every
1\leq i\leq g-1

the following hold:

h^{i}(\Pr,G\otimes P_{\beta})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{c l}\binom{g-2}{i}&\text{if $0\leq i\leq g-2$ and $\beta\in\widetilde{C}$}\\

\end{array}\right.

Proof.

Cohomology and base change, combined with Jacobi inversion for Pryms (see e.g.
[

Since the norm of an effective line bundle must be effective,

h^{0}(\widetilde{C},L\otimes\beta)=0
for every

Since

one obtains the Chern character

from which the Chern classes of

For the cohomological assertion, a similar argument to the one in
[

can be applied.
∎

Proof of

Given a point

, we consider

. The following two lemmas are probably well known to the experts, but we add some details since we could not find a reference.

Lemma 5.3
.

The intersection
x_{p}\cap X^{-}
is reduced for every point
p\in\widetilde{C}
.

Proof.

First we check the assertion for a general
p\in\widetilde{C}
. Let
\zeta=p_{1}+\cdots+p_{2g-2}\in X^{-}
be a smooth point, such that the points
p_{i}\in\widetilde{C}
are all distinct. The intersection of Zariski tangent spaces

T_{\zeta}(x_{p_{1}})\cap\cdots\cap T_{\zeta}(x_{p_{2g-2}})
inside
T_{\zeta}(\widetilde{C}^{2g-2})
is
0
, and so there exists an index

such that
T_{\zeta}(X^{-})\not\subset T_{\zeta}(x_{p_{i}})
. It follows that, for a general

and
\zeta\in x_{p}\cap X^{-}

is transverse at
\zeta
, and so
x_{p}\cap X^{-}
is reduced.

To derive the assertion for any

, note that the map
x_{p}\cap X^{-}\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}\mathrm{Pr}^{-}
is generically one-to-one onto its image, so
x_{p}\cap X^{-}
is reduced if and only if its schematic image
\pi(x_{p}\cap X^{-})
is reduced.

The image
\pi(x_{p}\cap X^{-})
in

can be identified set-theoretically with Beauville’s special subvariety
[

]
defined by the complete linear system
|\omega_{C}(-f(p))|
on

loc. cit.
, its cohomology class is
\xi
(which is non-divisible). Since
{x_{p}}_{|_{X^{-}}}
is reduced for general

, the identification holds scheme theoretically (for every

), and the lemma follows.
∎

By abuse of notation, from now on we write
x_{p}
for
{{x_{p}}_{|}}_{X^{-}}
.
We will also write
x:=x_{p_{0}}
.

Lemma 5.4
.

For every

and

\pi

Proof.

Under the previous identifications, one can readily check that, set-theoretically,
\pi^{*}(\Xi_{\beta})
coincides with the divisor

\Bigl\{p_{1}+\cdots+p_{2g-2}\in X^{-}\subset\widetilde{C}^{(2g-2)}:\;h^{0}\!\left(\widetilde{C},\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{C}}(p_{1}+\cdots+p_{2g-2}+\sigma(p)-p)\right)\geq 2\Bigr\}.

Since
\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{C}}(p_{1}+\cdots+p_{2g-2}+\sigma(p)-p)\in{\Pr}^{+}
for every
p_{1}+\cdots+p_{2g-2}\in X^{-}
, it follows that
x_{p}\subset\pi^{*}(\Xi_{\beta})
by reducedness of
x_{p}
. And since
\pi^{*}\Xi_{\beta}
is reduced away from the exceptional divisor of
\pi
, we deduce that
\pi^{*}(\Xi_{\beta})-x_{p}
is effective and does not contain
x_{p}.

Now we consider a point

q_{1}+\cdots+q_{2g-2}
in
\pi^{*}(\Theta_{\beta})\setminus x_{p}
.
Since it does not lie in
x_{p}
, we must have
p\neq q_{i}
for all
i
, whereas certainly
p\neq\sigma(p)
. It follows that

h^{0}\!\left(\widetilde{C},\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{C}}(q_{1}+\cdots+q_{2g-2}+\sigma(p)-p)\right)<h^{0}\!\left(\widetilde{C},\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{C}}(q_{1}+\cdots+q_{2g-2}+\sigma(p))\right),

where the left-hand side is
\geq 2
. Therefore,

h^{0}\!\left(\widetilde{C},\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{C}}(q_{1}+\cdots+q_{2g-2})\right)\geq 2

and since
q_{1}+\cdots+q_{2g-2}\in X^{-}
, the parity condition along
{\Pr}^{-}
implies that

h^{0}\!\left(\widetilde{C},\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{C}}(q_{1}+\cdots+q_{2g-2})\right)\geq 3.

Consequently
\pi(q_{1}+\cdots+q_{2g-2})\in V^{2}(f)
, that is,
q_{1}+\dots+q_{2g-2}
belongs to the exceptional divisor of
\pi
.
∎

Now we consider the vector bundle
(\pi^{*}G)(x)
on
X^{-}
and the determinant map

\det:\bigwedge^{g-1}H^{0}\!\bigl(X^{-},(\pi^{*}G)(x)\bigr)\longrightarrow H^{0}\!\left(X^{-},\det\bigl((\pi^{*}G)(x)\bigr)\right),

whose image is the key point to establish the following:

Proposition 5.5
.

The vector bundle
(\pi^{*}G)(x)
on
X^{-}
is globally generated.

Proof.

The proof applies the same strategy of
section 3
(
2
). First, using
subsection 5.1
we see that for every
\beta\in\widetilde{C}
there exists a (unique) global section
s_{\beta}\in H^{0}(G(\Xi))
such that
\Xi_{\beta}\subset Z(s_{\beta})
. The Picard sheaf
G
is again generated by
\{\widetilde{C}\}
in the sense of
[
25
]
, and so for general points
\beta_{1},...,\beta_{g-1}\in\widetilde{C}
, one has

Z\left(\det(s_{\beta_{1}}\wedge...\wedge s_{\beta_{g-1}})\right)=\Xi_{\beta_{1}}+\cdots+\Xi_{\beta_{g-1}}+R(\beta_{1},...,\beta_{g-1})

for a suitable residual divisor
R(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{g-1})
of class
2\xi
.
Then, in view of
subsection 5.2
, considering
(\pi^{*}G)(x)
, it follows that the linear system
|\mathrm{Im}(\mathrm{det})|
on
X^{-}
contains the divisors

x_{\beta_{1}}+x_{\beta_{2}}+\cdots+x_{\beta_{g-1}}+\pi^{*}R(\beta_{1}+\cdots+\beta_{g-1}),

for general
\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{g-1}\in\widetilde{C}
. Hence the base locus of
|\mathrm{Im}(\mathrm{det})|
is contained in
\pi^{*}B
, where

B:=\bigcap R(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{g-1})

(the intersection running over general points
\beta_{1},...,\beta_{g-1}\in\widetilde{C}
). It follows that
(\pi^{*}G)(x)
is globally generated away from
\pi^{*}B
, so to finish the proof it suffices to check that
B
is empty.

To this end observe that, as pointed out in
subsection 5.1
, the vector bundle
G
is the restriction to
\mathrm{Pr}
of the Picard sheaf
K_{L}
on
J\widetilde{C}
, whose determinant is the divisor
\Theta^{L}
.

For any
p\in\widetilde{C}
, we set
\alpha=a(p)
and
\beta=b(p)
. Then the unique (up to scalar) map
P_{\beta}^{-1}\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}G
on
\mathrm{Pr}
is the restriction of the map
P_{\alpha}^{-1}\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}K_{L}
on
J\widetilde{C}
(recall (
5.1
)). If
\beta_{1},...,\beta_{g-1}\in\widetilde{C}
are general points, it follows that the residual divisor
R(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{g-1})
—which is the locus where
\bigoplus_{i=1}^{g-1}P_{\beta_{i}}^{-1}\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}G
drops rank—is the restriction to
\mathrm{Pr}
of the corresponding degeneracy locus for
\bigoplus_{i=1}^{g-1}P_{\alpha_{i}}^{-1}\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}K_{L}
. The latter is, for determinant reasons, the translate
\Theta^{L}_{\iota(\alpha_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{g-1})}
of
\Theta^{L}
. Hence

R(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{g-1})=\Theta^{L}_{\iota(\alpha_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{g-1})}\cap\mathrm{Pr},

which implies

B=\bigcap_{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{g-1}\in\widetilde{C}}\Bigl(\Theta^{L}_{\iota(\alpha_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{g-1})}\cap\mathrm{Pr}\Bigr)=\left(\bigcap_{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{g-1}\in\widetilde{C}}\Theta^{L}_{\iota(\alpha_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{g-1})}\right)\cap\mathrm{Pr}.

An elementary computation shows that any
\alpha\in\Pic^{0}(\widetilde{C})=J\widetilde{C}
in this intersection has trivial norm and satisfies that
L\otimes\alpha
is effective. If such an
\alpha
existed, then
\mathrm{Nm}_{f}(L)=\Nm_{f}(L\otimes\alpha)
would be effective, contradicting our assumption on
L
; therefore,
B
is empty and the proof is complete.
∎

We let
\nu:\widetilde{X^{-}}\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}X^{-}
be a desingularization of
X^{-}
, and denote
\psi:=\nu\circ\pi
. Consider the vector bundle
\widetilde{G}=\nu^{*}((\pi^{*}G)(x))
.

Proposition 5.6
.

The vector bundle
\widetilde{G}
is globally generated and satisfies
c_{g-1}(\widetilde{G})=2^{2g-3}
.

Proof.

The global generation of
\widetilde{G}
follows directly from the previous proposition.

For the computation of
c_{g-1}(\widetilde{G})
, first observe that, in virtue of
subsection 5.1
,

c_{i}(\psi^{*}G)=2^{i}\frac{\psi^{*}\xi^{i}}{i!}\in H^{2i}(\widetilde{X^{-}},\mathbb{Z}).

On the other hand, denoting also by
x
the cohomology class
c_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{X^{-}}}(\nu^{*}x))\in H^{2}(\widetilde{X^{-}},\mathbb{Z})
, we claim that
\psi_{*}x^{k}=2^{k-1}\frac{\xi^{k}}{k!}
. Indeed, fix general points
p_{1},\dots,p_{k}\in\widetilde{C}
. By a similar argument to that of the proof of
subsection 5.2
the intersection
x_{p_{1}}\cap\dots\cap x_{p_{k}}\subset X^{-}
is reduced, and maps generically 1:1 onto its image in
\mathrm{Pr}
. This image can in turn be identified with Beauville’s special subvariety defined by the complete linear system
|\omega_{C}(-f(p_{1})-...-f(p_{k}))|
on
C
, and so it has cohomology class
2^{k-1}\frac{\xi^{k}}{k!}
(see
[
3
]
).

Therefore, a direct application of projection formula yields

x^{k}\cdot(\psi^{*}\xi)^{g-1-k}=\psi_{*}x^{k}\cdot\xi^{g-1-k}=2^{k-1}\frac{(g-1)!}{k!}.

Applying the splitting principle (again, as in
[
12
, Remark 3.2.3]
) yields

c_{g-1}(\widetilde{G})=c_{g-1}({\psi}^{*}G\otimes\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{X}^{-}}(\nu^{*}x))=\sum_{k=0}^{g-1}2^{g-1-k}x^{k}\frac{(\psi^{*}\xi)^{g-1-k}}{(g-1-k)!}=2^{g-2}\sum_{k=0}^{g-1}\binom{g-1}{k}=2^{2g-3},

which completes the proof.
∎

Observe that at this point,
Theorem B
follows directly by combining
subsection 5.2
with
subsection 2.1
.
