p53 overexpression counteracts the pro-survival effect of Bcl-2 by restoring MAMs function in cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma
Authors: Yupeng Huang, Jing Liu, Xiao Shao, Chunyan Xu, Xianpeng Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCutaneous squamous cell carcinoma (cSCC) is a prevalent type of skin cancer, and its development is strongly associated with impaired regulation of apoptosis. The anti-apoptotic protein B-cell lymphoma 2 (Bcl-2) and the tumor suppressor p53 play pivotal roles in controlling apoptotic signaling. Although Bcl-2 expression in cSCC has been reported to be heterogeneous, its role in mitochondria-associated membranes (MAMs)-mediated apoptosis remains unclear. This study aims to clarify how Bcl-2 and p53 regulate cSCC cell apoptosis by modulating the structure and function of MAMs.MethodsWe constructed stable Bcl-2 overexpression (oe-Bcl-2) and Bcl-2/p53 co-overexpression (oe-Bcl-2 + oe-p53) A431 cell lines using lentiviral transduction. Transcriptome sequencing (RNA-seq) was performed, followed by pathway enrichment and protein–protein interaction (PPI) network analyses to screen for critical signaling routes. Cell apoptosis, growth, migratory capacity, and invasive potential were evaluated using flow cytometry, CCK-8, EdU incorporation, colony formation, and Transwell-based assays. Key MAMs functions, including Ca2+ flux, mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP), ROS, and ATP levels, were measured. Subcutaneous tumor models in nude mice were used for in vivo validation.ResultsRNA-seq analysis revealed that Bcl-2 regulates multiple apoptosis- and calcium signaling-related pathways, significantly affecting key MAM-associated proteins, including VDAC2, IP3R, SERCA2, as well as the p53 signaling pathway. Bcl-2 overexpression markedly enhanced proliferation and migration of A431 cells while reducing apoptosis. It also modulated mitochondrial membrane potential, ATP production, and ROS generation, suggesting that Bcl-2 exerts anti-apoptotic effects through MAMs. In contrast, Bcl-2 knockdown significantly suppressed proliferation and invasion while inducing apoptosis in A431 cells, further supporting its oncogenic role. Notably, p53 overexpression reversed these effects and significantly inhibited tumor growth in vivo.ConclusionOur findings demonstrate that Bcl-2 promotes cSCC progression by modulating MAMs structure and function to inhibit p53-mediated mitochondrial apoptosis. The study identifies the novel Bcl-2-MAMs-p53 signaling axis that plays a pivotal role in determining cSCC cell fate, highlighting a promising target for therapeutic intervention.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at10.1186/s13008-026-00190-4.Keywords: Cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma, B-cell lymphoma 2, p53, Mitochondria-associated membranes, Apoptosis, RNA sequencing

Introduction

Cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma (cSCC), derived from epidermal keratinocytes, is one of the most prevalent skin cancers and ranks second to basal cell carcinoma in incidence [1, 2]. Its global burden has risen continuously in recent years, especially in groups characterized by chronic ultraviolet exposure, impaired immune status, and older age [3]. cSCC not only exhibits local invasive growth but also has the potential to metastasize via lymphatic and hematogenous routes to distant organs, resulting in significant mortality and severely impacting patients’ quality of life [2–4]. The development and progression of cSCC involve a multifactorial and multistep process, including DNA damage induced by ultraviolet radiation, decreased immune surveillance, exposure to chemical carcinogens, and genetic susceptibility [1, 2].

Anomalies in apoptosis represent a critical pathological mechanism in the progression of cSCC [5–7]; when apoptosis is inhibited, damaged cells evade immune clearance, accumulate genetic mutations, and promote the persistent survival and dissemination of tumor cells. Although early-stage cSCC can be effectively treated with surgical excision and radiotherapy, current therapeutic options are limited for recurrent, aggressive, or metastatic cases [3, 4]. In recent years, molecularly targeted agents and immunotherapies have demonstrated clinical benefit in a subset of patients; nevertheless, limited response rates, pronounced resistance, and inadequate durability of efficacy remain major obstacles [4, 8, 9]. Thus, a mechanistic understanding of cSCC focused on cell-death signaling, particularly survival-death homeostasis, is critical for discovering novel intervention targets and refining therapeutic approaches.

B-cell lymphoma-2 (Bcl-2), one of the earliest identified anti-apoptotic genes, is highly expressed in various malignancies. Its protein primarily localizes to the mitochondrial outer membrane, endoplasmic reticulum, and nuclear membrane [6, 7]. However, in clinical cSCC specimens, Bcl-2 expression exhibits marked heterogeneity, with some cases showing high expression while others display low or even negative expression [10, 11]. This heterogeneity implies that Bcl-2 function in cSCC is likely influenced by cellular context, particularly within subsets showing disruption of pivotal regulators such as p53. Mechanistically, Bcl-2 restrains Bax/Bak-mediated mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization, thereby limiting cytochrome c release and suppressing the intrinsic apoptotic pathway. Additionally, Bcl-2 regulates mitochondrial membrane permeability and calcium ion (Ca2+) homeostasis, maintaining a metabolic advantage for tumor cell growth and survival [6, 12]. Multiple genomic studies have consistently demonstrated that TP53 is the most frequently mutated driver gene in cSCC, with a mutation rate of approximately 50%–60% in primary cSCC and as high as 85%–95% in invasive or metastatic cases [13, 14]. The vast majority of these mutations exhibit characteristic ultraviolet (UV)-induced mutational signatures (C>T or CC>TT transitions). Most alterations are missense mutations concentrated in the DNA-binding domain, leading to impaired tumor-suppressor activity, increased genomic instability, and reduced apoptotic susceptibility, thereby facilitating the accumulation of additional oncogenic mutations. Such alterations are considered early pivotal events in cSCC pathogenesis and can even be detected in precancerous lesions, such as actinic keratosis [13, 14]. At the protein level, because most TP53 mutations are missense mutations, the mutant p53 protein adopts an abnormal conformation that escapes MDM2-mediated ubiquitin-proteasome degradation. This leads to protein stabilization and abnormal nuclear accumulation, which is typically observed as strong nuclear positivity in immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining. Multiple IHC studies have shown that p53 protein expression is markedly elevated in cSCC tissues compared with normal skin or benign atypical squamous lesions, where wild-type p53 is usually weakly expressed or undetectable [14, 15]. A recent preliminary study further reported that p53 positivity was approximately 63.6% in low-risk cSCC and increased to 90.5% in high-risk cSCC, significantly higher than in benign atypical squamous lesions (10%) [15]. Although truncating or nonsense mutations may result in complete loss of p53 protein expression (IHC-negative), such mutations are relatively uncommon in cSCC. The abnormal accumulation of mutant p53 is therefore widely regarded as a surrogate marker of TP53 dysfunction and has been associated with tumor invasion depth, histological grade, recurrence risk, and poor prognosis. Functionally, this loss of tumor-suppressive activity promotes genomic instability, enables damaged cells to evade apoptosis, and facilitates the accumulation of additional oncogenic mutations, representing a critical early event in cSCC development and progression. In contrast, p53 is a prototypical tumor suppressor widely known as the “guardian of the genome.” It becomes activated in response to cellular insults, including DNA damage, oxidative stress, and hypoxic stress. Through transcription-dependent pathways, p53 upregulates pro-apoptotic genes like Bax, p53 Upregulated Modulator of Apoptosis (PUMA), and NOXA or directly interacts with Bcl-2 family proteins at the mitochondrial membrane to promote changes in outer membrane permeability and initiate apoptosis signaling [16–18]. Despite extensive research on the antagonistic roles of Bcl-2 and p53 along the nucleus-mitochondria axis, there remains a lack of comprehensive studies exploring their direct or indirect interactions within other cellular substructures such as mitochondria-associated membranes (MAMs) [12, 19, 20]. In cSCC, reports on the expression patterns and interactions between Bcl-2 and p53 and their specific effects on apoptosis regulation are even scarcer. This gap limits our comprehensive understanding of their molecular mechanisms [3, 9].

MAMs are specialized membrane structures where the endoplasmic reticulum and mitochondria are close, typically separated by a distance of 10–30 nanometers. These structures are maintained by various transmembrane proteins and bridging protein complexes [21]. MAMs play critical roles not only in lipid synthesis and transport, mitochondrial dynamics regulation, and redox balance, but also serve as central hubs for cellular calcium signaling [22, 23]. Through Ca2+ flux mediated by proteins such as IP3R and Voltage-Dependent Anion Channel (VDAC), MAMs can directly influence mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP), adenosine triphosphate (ATP) synthesis, and reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, thereby modulating the balance between cell survival and apoptosis [12, 24]. Evidence indicates that impaired MAM function can trigger Ca2+ overload, exacerbate oxidative stress, disrupt mitochondrial homeostasis, and ultimately promote apoptosis [25, 26]. Additionally, as platforms for metabolic and signaling integration, MAMs are crucial in tumor cell energy reprogramming, apoptosis resistance, and chemoresistance [22, 26]. Although Bcl-2 has been found to localize at MAMs and interact with IP3R to inhibit calcium release, and p53 may promote apoptosis through cytoplasmic signaling targeting mitochondria, the direct regulatory relationships of these proteins within the MAM structure remain unexplored systematically, with a lack of experimental evidence specifically in the context of cSCC [12, 22].

Some sporadic reports suggest that the role of Bcl-2 in MAMs extends beyond its anti-apoptotic function. It may also contribute to creating a metabolic advantage for tumor cells by stabilizing membrane contact structures, regulating calcium flux, and inhibiting mitochondrial stress responses [12, 27]. In addition to its classic nuclear transcriptional activity, p53 can bind with Bcl-2 or Bcl-xL in the cytoplasm, thereby relieving their inhibition on Bax/Bak and accelerating apoptosis via the mitochondrial pathway [17, 28]. Within MAMs, p53 may disrupt the binding of Bcl-2 with IP3R and VDAC, altering calcium signaling and energy metabolism states, thus counteracting the anti-apoptotic effects of Bcl-2 [12, 19, 22]. However, there is currently a lack of multi-layered evidence from transcriptomics, proteomics, and functional experiments to elucidate the action mode of the Bcl-2-MAMs-p53 signaling axis. Additionally, few studies have validated its biological function in vivo using tumor models, highlighting a significant research gap that needs to be addressed.

This study aims to elucidate the regulatory interplay between Bcl-2 and p53 at the MAMs and its impact on apoptosis in cSCC. It seeks to determine whether Bcl-2 can suppress p53-mediated mitochondrial apoptosis by modulating MAM function, and whether p53 can counteract the anti-apoptotic influence of Bcl-2, thereby exerting a decisive role in the balance between cell survival and death. By dissecting the molecular mechanisms underlying the Bcl-2-MAMs-p53 signaling axis, this work endeavors to expand the conceptual framework of tumor cell apoptosis regulation and address the current knowledge gap regarding Bcl-2-p53 interactions at MAMs. Clinically, these insights may yield novel molecular biomarkers and precision therapeutic strategies for cSCC, offering new avenues of intervention for refractory and metastatic disease, with the ultimate goal of improving patient survival and quality of life.

Results

RNA-seq reveals potential mechanism of Bcl-2 in modulating mitochondrial apoptotic signaling via MAMs

To systematically investigate how Bcl-2 influences MAM regulation, we performed RNA-seq analysis as summarized in Fig. 1A. Initially, we successfully established a Bcl-2 overexpression A431 cell line (oe-Bcl-2 group) via lentivirus-mediated gene overexpression technology. Reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) and Western blot (WB) analyses confirmed the efficiency of gene overexpression. RT-qPCR showed that Bcl-2 mRNA levels were markedly higher in the oe-Bcl-2 group than in the oe-NC control (Fig. 2A). WB analysis further corroborated the upregulation of Bcl-2 protein levels (Fig. 2B), indicating the successful establishment of a stable Bcl-2 overexpression model.

RNA-seq results revealed extensive transcriptional changes in A431 cells due to Bcl-2 overexpression, identifying 560 differentially expressed genes (DEGs), with 263 upregulated and 297 downregulated genes (|log2FC| > 1, p < 0.05) (Fig. 1B). A heatmap visualized the expression patterns of these DEGs (Fig. 1C). GO enrichment analysis indicated that Bcl-2 overexpression predominantly affected mitochondrial outer membrane Ca2+ transport and apoptotic signaling pathways (Fig. 1D). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis indicated that Bcl-2 overexpression preferentially impacted the Calcium Signaling Pathway as well as apoptosis-associated pathways (Fig. 1E). Moreover, protein–protein interaction network (PPI) analysis showed that Bcl-2 directly regulated key apoptotic proteins such as p53 (TP53), BAX, and PUMA (BBC3), and influenced MAMs-related proteins including VDAC2, IP3R (ITPR1), and SERCA2 (ATP2A2) (Fig. 1F). Following Bcl-2 overexpression, the expression levels of p53 (TP53), BAX, PUMA (BBC3), VDAC2, IP3R (ITPR1), and SERCA2 (ATP2A2) were significantly downregulated (Fig. 1G), further supporting the critical regulatory role of Bcl-2 in MAMs-mediated apoptotic signaling pathways.

In summary, by establishing a stable Bcl-2-overexpressing A431 model and performing RNA-seq profiling, we demonstrate that Bcl-2 serves as a key regulator of MAMs-dependent mitochondrial apoptosis.

Bcl-2 promotes proliferation and invasion in cSCC cells, with p53 co-expression reversing this effect

We constructed cell models using A431 cells to overexpress Bcl-2 (oe-Bcl-2) and co-overexpress Bcl-2 and p53 (oe-Bcl-2 + oe-p53), confirming the efficiency of overexpression (Fig. 3A). RT-qPCR analysis showed that Bcl-2 mRNA expression was significantly higher in the oe-Bcl-2 group than in the oe-NC control, whereas the oe-Bcl-2 + oe-p53 group exhibited marked increases in both Bcl-2 and p53 transcripts (Fig. 2C). WB confirmed elevated Bcl-2 protein levels in oe-Bcl-2 cells relative to oe-NC, and demonstrated significant upregulation of both Bcl-2 and p53 proteins in the oe-Bcl-2 + oe-p53 group (Fig. 3D). Apoptosis measured by flow cytometry decreased in oe-Bcl-2 cells relative to oe-NC, whereas p53 co-overexpression restored/increased apoptosis compared with oe-Bcl-2 alone (Fig. 3B). This trend was further corroborated by TUNEL staining (Fig. 3C).

All cell experiments were conducted in triplicate. Statistical significance is indicated as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001

CCK-8 results showed elevated viability in oe-Bcl-2 cells versus oe-NC, while viability declined markedly in the oe-Bcl-2 + oe-p53 group (Fig. 3D). EdU staining and colony formation further confirmed that Bcl-2 overexpression strengthened proliferative potential, whereas p53 co-overexpression significantly weakened proliferation (Fig. 3E, F). Wound-healing assays revealed enhanced migration in the oe-Bcl-2 group but reduced migration in the oe-Bcl-2 + oe-p53 group (Fig. 3G). Transwell invasion assays showed the same pattern, with increased invasion in oe-Bcl-2 cells and a pronounced decrease following p53 co-overexpression (Fig. 3H).

These results indicate that Bcl-2 overexpression enhances cSCC cell survival and strengthens proliferative and invasive behaviors, whereas co-overexpression with p53 reversed these effects. This implies that Bcl-2 may enhance cSCC cell growth and migration by inhibiting p53-mediated apoptosis signaling pathways. To further validate the generality of the above findings across different cSCC cellular contexts, we selected another human cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma cell line, HSC-1, and repeated the key experiments. Stable HSC-1 models overexpressing Bcl-2 alone (oe-Bcl-2) or co-overexpressing Bcl-2 and p53 (oe-Bcl-2 + oe-p53) were generated, and target-gene expression was verified by RT-qPCR and WB (Figure S1A, B). Functional analyses yielded findings consistent with those in A431 cells: Bcl-2 overexpression suppressed apoptosis as determined by flow cytometry and TUNEL staining (Figure S1C, D), increased proliferative capacity based on CCK-8 and EdU assays (Figure S1E, F), and promoted migration and invasion as assessed by wound healing, colony formation, and Transwell assays (Figure S1G–I). Notably, co-expression of p53 effectively reversed the oncogenic phenotypes induced by Bcl-2 overexpression. Collectively, these data suggest that Bcl-2 promotes cSCC cell growth and migration by suppressing p53-mediated apoptotic signaling, and that this mechanism is conserved across different cSCC cell subtypes.

Bcl-2 inhibits p53-mediated apoptosis signaling via MAMs, and co-expression of p53 reverses this effect

To further investigate the roles of Bcl-2 and p53 in the regulation of MAMs, we assessed levels of Ca2+, MMP, ROS, and ATP (Fig. 4A). Intracellular Ca2+ levels were assessed using the Fluo-4 fluorescent indicator. Compared with the oe-NC group, free Ca2+ was significantly reduced in oe-Bcl-2 cells, whereas a significant elevation was detected in the oe-Bcl-2 + oe-p53 group (Fig. 4B). JC-1 analysis demonstrated elevated MMP in the oe-Bcl-2 group compared with oe-NC, while MMP was significantly reduced in the oe-Bcl-2 + oe-p53 group (Fig. 4C). The 2′,7′-Dichlorodihydrofluorescein Diacetate (DCFH-DA) fluorescent probe showed a significant decrease in intracellular ROS in oe-Bcl-2 cells relative to oe-NC, but ROS levels were significantly increased following p53 co-overexpression (oe-Bcl-2 + oe-p53) (Fig. 4D). ATP levels, measured by the luciferin-luciferase method, were significantly higher in the oe-Bcl-2 group than in the oe-NC group, whereas ATP levels were markedly reduced in the oe-Bcl-2 + oe-p53 group (Fig. 4E).

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays verified the physical association between Bcl-2 and p53. Relative to the oe-NC group, the Bcl-2-p53 interaction signal was significantly stronger in oe-Bcl-2 cells, whereas it was markedly reduced in the oe-Bcl-2 + oe-p53 group (Fig. 4F). Fluorescence colocalization experiments demonstrated a significantly increased colocalization signal of Bcl-2 and p53 in oe-Bcl-2 group cells, which was notably reduced in the oe-Bcl-2 + oe-p53 group (Fig. 4G). WB of MAM-related proteins (VDAC2, IP3R, and SERCA2) and apoptosis-associated proteins (Caspase-3, Bax, and PUMA) showed that VDAC2, IP3R, and SERCA2 were downregulated in oe-Bcl-2 cells compared with oe-NC but were significantly upregulated in the oe-Bcl-2 + oe-p53 group. A similar trend was observed for apoptotic markers: Caspase-3, Bax, and PUMA decreased in oe-Bcl-2 cells and increased significantly in the co-overexpression group (Fig. 4H, I).

We further investigated the underlying molecular mechanisms in HSC-1 cells. Co-IP and immunofluorescence co-localization assays robustly demonstrated that Bcl-2 physically interacts with p53 in HSC-1 cells (Figure S1M, O). Functional assessment of MAMs revealed that the regulatory effects of Bcl-2 and p53 on intracellular Ca²⁺ concentration, MMP, ROS levels, and ATP production were entirely consistent with those observed in A431 cells (Figure S1J–N). WB demonstrated that alterations in MAM integrity-related proteins (VDAC2, IP3R, and SERCA2) and downstream apoptotic effectors (Caspase-3, Bax, and PUMA) mirrored the patterns observed in A431 cells (Figure S1B). Collectively, these additional data obtained from the HSC-1 cell line strongly support our central conclusions.

These findings suggest that Bcl-2 potentially suppresses p53-mediated calcium transport and mitochondrial apoptosis signaling pathways via MAMs. The co-overexpression of p53 can reverse the anti-apoptotic effects of Bcl-2, further elucidating the mechanism by which Bcl-2 regulates p53’s influence on cSCC apoptosis through MAMs.

Bcl-2 promotes cSCC tumor growth, while p53 Co-expression inhibits its tumor-promoting effect

An in vivo cSCC xenograft model was generated by subcutaneous inoculation of A431 cells into BALB/c nude mice to evaluate the effects of Bcl-2 and p53 on tumor growth (Fig. 5A). Serial measurements showed that tumors in the oe-Bcl-2 group grew significantly larger than those in the oe-NC group, whereas the oe-Bcl-2 + oe-p53 group showed a notable decrease in tumor volume (Fig. 5B). On day 21, tumors were harvested for imaging and weighing; consistent with the volume data, tumor mass increased significantly in the oe-Bcl-2 group relative to oe-NC, but was substantially reduced in the oe-Bcl-2 + oe-p53 group (Fig. 5C, D). Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining showed that tumors from the oe-Bcl-2 group displayed increased cellular density and more evident nuclear atypia, consistent with an actively proliferative phenotype. In contrast, the oe-Bcl-2 + oe-p53 group presented expanded necrotic areas, disrupted tissue architecture, and prominent nuclear pyknosis (Fig. 5E). Immunohistochemistry further demonstrated significantly higher Bcl-2 expression in the oe-Bcl-2 group relative to oe-NC. While Bcl-2 levels were not markedly altered in the oe-Bcl-2 + oe-p53 group, p53 staining was notably enhanced (Fig. 5F).

Regulation of MAMs functions by Bcl-2 inhibits p53-mediated apoptosis, reversed by co-expression of p53

To explore the impact of Bcl-2 and p53 on MAMs functionality, we assessed levels of ROS and ATP in tumor tissues, alongside the expression of key MAM proteins and apoptosis-related proteins. DCFH-DA staining revealed that ROS levels were significantly lower in the oe-Bcl-2 group than in the oe-NC group, whereas ROS was markedly increased in the oe-Bcl-2 + oe-p53 group (Fig. 6A). ATP content, measured using a luciferin–luciferase assay, was significantly elevated in oe-Bcl-2 tumors but decreased substantially in the oe-Bcl-2 + oe-p53 group (Fig. 6B). WB further showed downregulation of MAM-related proteins (VDAC2, IP3R, SERCA2) in oe-Bcl-2 tumors compared with oe-NC, while these proteins were significantly upregulated in the co-overexpression group (Fig. 6C). A similar pattern was observed for apoptotic markers: Caspase-3, Bax, and PUMA were reduced in oe-Bcl-2 tumors and increased in oe-Bcl-2 + oe-p53 tumors (Fig. 6D).

These findings suggest that Bcl-2 inhibits ROS production and promotes ATP synthesis via MAMs, thereby suppressing the p53-mediated mitochondrial apoptosis signaling pathway. However, the co-overexpression of p53 can reverse this anti-apoptotic effect of Bcl-2.

Discussion

This study systematically elucidates the mechanism by which Bcl-2 and p53 influence Apoptosis in cSCC through the regulation of MAMs. It introduces, for the first time, the functional paradigm of the Bcl-2-MAMs-p53 signaling axis in cSCC progression. Our findings reveal that Bcl-2 overexpression significantly promotes A431 cell proliferative and motile/invasive behaviors while suppressing apoptosis. This anti-apoptotic effect is mediated through the maintenance of MMP, increased ATP synthesis, and reduced ROS production. Co-overexpression of p53 effectively reverses these effects, restoring Apoptosis levels and inhibiting tumor growth in vivo. RNA-seq and PPI analysis indicate that Bcl-2 regulates multiple pathways associated with Apoptosis and calcium signaling, significantly impacting the expression of key MAMs proteins such as VDAC2, IP3R, and SERCA2. These findings are highly correlated with their localization and function at MAMs. Consistent results were obtained in both in vitro models and in vivo xenograft models using two independent cSCC cell lines (A431 and HSC-1), thereby further strengthening the reliability and generalizability of our conclusions. These discoveries not only enhance our understanding of the roles of Bcl-2 and p53 in cSCC but also provide a theoretical basis for exploring new targets for Apoptosis regulation.

Although Bcl-2 is widely recognized as a classical anti-apoptotic protein and is highly expressed in multiple solid tumors, its expression exhibits marked heterogeneity in cSCC [10, 11]. In light of this variability, the present study did not aim to establish Bcl-2 as a universal driver of all cSCC cases. Rather, our objective was to elucidate how Bcl-2 exerts oncogenic effects through a novel subcellular mechanism in the subset of cSCC characterized by functionally active Bcl-2. To clearly dissect the complex PPIs occurring at the level of MAMs, we adopted a gain-of-function strategy to amplify the signaling cascade and implemented rigorously designed p53 rescue experiments. Our findings demonstrate that restoration of p53 expression markedly reversed all Bcl-2 overexpression-induced oncogenic phenotypes as well as the associated alterations in MAMs function. This “gain-of-function-rescue” experimental framework provides strong evidence supporting the specificity of the molecular mechanism we propose. Furthermore, previous studies have reported that inhibition of Bcl-2 function—either through genetic knockdown or pharmacological blockade—in cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma or other epithelial-derived cancer cell lines generally results in suppressed cell growth and increased apoptosis [29, 30]. Earlier investigations have primarily focused on the role of Bcl-2 within the nuclear-mitochondrial signaling axis. Accumulating evidence suggests that Bcl-2 is frequently upregulated across multiple solid malignancies, such as breast cancer, lung cancer, and melanoma, where it inhibits mitochondrial apoptotic pathways to prevent cell death [6, 7]. Existing studies have mainly focused on Bcl-2 function along the nuclear–mitochondrial signaling axis, particularly in preventing cytochrome C release by inhibiting Bax/Bak activation [6, 7]. However, our research expands the scope of Bcl-2’s function by revealing, for the first time in a cSCC model, how Bcl-2 modulates apoptotic signaling through its regulation of MAMs. This mechanism has been scarcely reported in prior skin cancer studies. Specifically, Bcl-2’s regulation of VDAC2, IP3R, and SERCA2 suggests it maintains mitochondrial homeostasis by influencing Ca2+ transmembrane flux. This observation aligns with phenomena noted in liver cancer and neuronal apoptosis models; however, the localization of Bcl-2 to MAMs and its downstream effects within the specific context of cSCC are novel findings. Furthermore, our results concur with some studies indicating that Bcl-2 promotes tumor cell metabolic advantage and inhibits ROS production, but emphasize that its effects are contingent upon the integrity of MAMs structure and function. This insight significantly supplements existing understanding.

p53, as a classical tumor suppressor protein, plays a central role in DNA damage response, cell cycle arrest, and the induction of apoptosis [18]. Most studies have focused on its transcriptional activity within the nucleus or its direct action on mitochondrial membranes to promote apoptosis in the cytoplasm [18, 19]. Our study found that co-overexpression of p53 significantly restores apoptosis levels and inhibits proliferation and migration in A431 cells against a background of Bcl-2 overexpression, suggesting that p53 can counteract the anti-apoptotic effects of Bcl-2 at MAMs. Mechanistically, p53 may interfere with the binding of Bcl-2 to MAM proteins such as IP3R and VDAC2, thereby enhancing Ca2+ transfer to mitochondria. This process induces a decrease in MMP, reduces ATP synthesis, and increases ROS production. These results are consistent with reports from colorectal and breast cancer models showing that p53 can counteract Bcl-2-mediated suppression and facilitate Bax activation. Notably, our study is the first to map this antagonistic relationship to MAMs, a specialized membrane microdomain, underscoring its spatially restricted regulation in cSCC. This finding expands current knowledge of p53-driven pro-apoptotic mechanisms and supports a broader functional spectrum of p53 across distinct subcellular compartments.

As a physical and functional interface between the endoplasmic reticulum and mitochondria, MAMs play a multifaceted role in calcium signaling, lipid metabolism, redox balance, and the regulation of Apoptosis [26, 31]. Previous studies have demonstrated that MAMs can facilitate metabolic adaptability and participate in the modulation of apoptotic sensitivity in tumor cells [22, 32]. Our study reveals that the Bcl-2-MAMs-p53 signaling axis significantly influences Ca2+ flow, MMP, ATP synthesis, and ROS levels, further substantiating the pivotal role of MAMs in energy metabolism and the balance of cell survival and death. In contrast to the regulatory mechanisms of MAMs reported in breast cancer and liver cancer, our findings highlight a specific regulatory pattern in cSCC, wherein Bcl-2 stabilizes MAMs to inhibit apoptosis, while p53 disrupts this stability to restore apoptotic signaling. This discovery offers a novel perspective for understanding the functional differences of MAMs across various tumor types.

RNA-seq combined with PPI network analysis has revealed significant changes in several DEGs associated with Apoptosis, calcium signaling, and oxidative stress, such as VDAC2, IP3R, and SERCA2. These changes are closely related to the expression levels of Bcl-2 or p53. While previous literature has frequently reported these molecules in mitochondrial function or calcium signaling, their specific roles in cSCC have been scarcely studied [33, 34]. Collectively, our data not only underscore the essential contribution of these molecules to the Bcl-2-MAMs-p53 signaling axis but also highlight their potential as candidates for future mechanistic studies and therapeutic exploitation. For example, VDAC2 has been implicated in controlling mitochondrial membrane permeability and apoptotic susceptibility across multiple tumor types; here, we provide the first evidence linking VDAC2 functionally to the Bcl-2-MAMs-p53 axis in a cSCC model. These observations offer a conceptual framework for developing small-molecule modulators, either inhibitors or activators, targeting key MAM-associated proteins.

A significant strength of this study is the consistency between in vitro and in vivo experiments. The cytological experiments demonstrate that Bcl-2 overexpression significantly promotes cell survival, migration, and invasion, whereas co-overexpression of p53 effectively suppresses these oncogenic characteristics. The nude mouse xenograft model corroborates the phenomenon that Bcl-2 promotes tumor growth while p53 reverses this effect. This consistency not only bolsters the reliability of our conclusions but also suggests that the Bcl-2-MAMs-p53 signaling axis holds genuine biological significance within the actual tumor microenvironment. Based on histological analysis, we hypothesize that this signaling axis may drive cSCC progression by concurrently influencing tumor cell metabolism and apoptosis sensitivity, aligning with mechanisms observed in certain tumors where metabolic and apoptotic signals are intertwined.

The potential clinical translation of the study’s findings warrants attention. The Bcl-2-MAMs-p53 signaling axis presents multiple feasible intervention strategies as a therapeutic target. For instance, Bcl-2 inhibitors like Venetoclax can directly diminish their anti-apoptotic capacity, while p53 activators such as APR-246 can enhance pro-apoptotic signaling. Additionally, MAMs function as modulators that can alter cell fate at the level of calcium signaling or energy metabolism. Furthermore, given the ubiquitous role of Bcl-2 and p53 in various tumors, this signaling axis may represent a therapeutically relevant target across cancer types. However, the specific regulatory patterns in cSCC provide a basis for developing differentiated treatments. Nonetheless, potential mechanisms of resistance, impacts on normal tissues, and drug specificity issues require thorough evaluation in future studies.

In conclusion, our study provides the first systematic evidence that Bcl-2 inhibits the p53-mediated mitochondrial apoptosis pathway by regulating MAMs functionality, thereby promoting cSCC cell growth and tumor progression. The co-overexpression of p53 can reverse this effect, introducing the novel concept of the Bcl-2-MAMs-p53 signaling axis. Scientifically, this research enhances the theoretical framework of apoptosis regulation in cSCC and underscores the critical role of MAMs in tumor biology. Clinically, this axis may represent a candidate molecular marker and therapeutic target for cSCC, offering new intervention avenues for patients with refractory and metastatic cases. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. Although our findings were validated in two independent cSCC cell lines (A431 and HSC-1) and further supported by a nude mouse xenograft model, large-scale validation using clinical tissue samples is still lacking. In addition, the downstream signaling pathways were not comprehensively investigated. Finally, the impact of different p53 mutational statuses on MAMs regulation was not explored and warrants further investigation. Future research should expand clinical sample sizes and employ techniques such as single-cell sequencing and live-cell imaging to analyze dynamic changes in MAMs in greater detail. Additionally, exploring combined therapeutic strategies targeting Bcl-2 inhibition, p53 activation, and MAMs modulation could facilitate the clinical translation of this signaling axis.

Materials and methods

Ethical statement

All procedures involving animals complied with relevant guidelines for animal experimentation and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC). Mice were housed in accordance with humane standards, and efforts were made throughout to reduce pain and discomfort. Following completion of the experiments, animals were euthanized under ether anesthesia.

Cell culture and lentivirus infection

Human cSCC cell lines A431 cells (CRL-1555, ATCC, USA) and HSC-1 (JCRB1015, JCRB Cell Bank, Japan), as well as HEK293T cells (CRL-11268, ATCC, USA) were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; 12491015, Gibco, USA) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; A5256701, Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (SV30010, HyClone, USA) at 37 °C in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO2 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). A lentiviral packaging system was employed to generate overexpression constructs for Bcl-2 (oe-Bcl-2) and p53 (oe-p53) and the corresponding empty vector control (oe-NC), as well as shRNA-mediated Bcl-2 knockdown lentivirus (sh-Bcl-2) and its negative control (sh-NC). Lentiviral plasmids were co-transfected into HEK293T cells using Lipofectamine 2000 (11668030, Thermo Fisher, USA). Viral supernatants were collected at 48 h post-transfection, passed through a 0.45 μm filter, and centrifuged to obtain viral particles. Additional supernatants were collected at 72 h, concentrated by centrifugation, pooled with the initial preparation, and titrated. For transduction, A431 cells were infected at an MOI of 20. Puromycin selection (2 µg/mL; 5.08838, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was initiated 48 h after infection and continued for 7 days to establish stable lines. Overexpression efficiency was verified by RT-qPCR and WB. The experiments were divided into two parts: an overexpression group and a knockdown validation group. The overexpression experiments included the oe-NC (control), oe-Bcl-2 (Bcl-2 overexpression), and oe-Bcl-2 + oe-p53 (Bcl-2 and p53 co-overexpression) groups; The knockdown validation experiments included sh-NC (negative control) and sh-Bcl-2 (Bcl-2 knockdown). Each experiment was independently repeated three times [35].

RT-qPCR

Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol reagent (15596018CN, Invitrogen, USA). RNA quantity and quality were assessed with a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), and samples with an A260/A280 ratio of 1.8–2.0 were used for subsequent analyses. cDNA was synthesized using the PrimeScript™ RT reagent Kit (RR037A, Takara, Japan). qPCR was performed with SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (1176202 K, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) on an ABI QuantStudio 5 instrument (Applied Biosystems, USA). Each reaction was carried out in a 20 µL volume containing 10 µL SYBR Green Mix, 1 µL forward primer, 1 µL reverse primer, 1 µL cDNA template, and 7 µL nuclease-free water. Cycling parameters were: 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s and 60 °C for 30 s. GAPDH was used as the reference gene, and expression changes were determined using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences are provided in Table S1.

WB

Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer (P0013B, Beyotime, China) containing a protease inhibitor cocktail (P1005, Beyotime, China). After lysis for 30 min, the samples were centrifuged at 12,000 g for 15 min to collect the supernatant. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA protein assay kit (P0009, Beyotime, China). Thirty micrograms of protein per well were loaded and separated by 10% Sodium Dodecyl Sulfate-Polyacrylamide Gel Electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), followed by transfer onto Polyvinylidene Fluoride (PVDF) membranes (03010040001, Millipore, USA). Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk (P0216, Beyotime, China) for 1 h and then incubated overnight with primary antibodies (details in Table S2). After three washes with TBST (ST671, Beyotime, China) (5 min each), membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (A0208 or A0216; 1:1000; Beyotime, China) for 1 h. Detection was performed using an ECL kit (P0018S, Beyotime, China), and imaging was conducted with a ChemiDoc XRS+ system (Bio-Rad, USA). GAPDH served as a loading control. Densitometric analysis of protein bands was carried out using ImageJ 6.0 software (NIH, USA).

Bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq)

Stable oe-NC and oe-Bcl-2 A431 cells were prepared with three biological replicates per group. Total RNA was isolated, and RNA purity was evaluated using a NanoDrop ND-1000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher) by measuring the OD260/280 ratio to exclude protein and organic contamination. RNA concentration was additionally quantified with a Qubit RNA Assay Kit (Q33221, Thermo Fisher, USA). Only RNA samples meeting the following quality criteria were used for library preparation: RNA integrity number (RIN) ≥ 7.0 and a 28 S:18 S ratio ≥ 1.5.

Library preparation and sequencing were conducted by CapitalBio Technology (Beijing, China). For each sample, 5 µg RNA was input and rRNA was removed using the Ribo-Zero Magnetic Kit (MRZG12324, Epicentre, USA). Libraries were generated with the NEBNext Ultra RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (E7760S, NEB, USA), including fragmentation (~ 300 bp), first- and second-strand cDNA synthesis (dUTP-based), end repair, poly(A) tailing, adapter ligation, and USER enzyme digestion (M5508, NEB, USA) to obtain strand-specific libraries. After PCR enrichment, libraries were evaluated on an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer and quantified with KAPA (kk3605, Merck, USA), followed by paired-end sequencing on the Illumina NextSeqCN500.

Analysis of RNA-seq data

Raw paired-end sequencing reads were first evaluated with FastQC (v0.11.8). Adapter sequences and poly(A) tails were trimmed using Cutadapt (v1.18). Reads containing > 5% ambiguous nucleotides (N) were removed with a custom Perl script. Quality filtering was further performed with FASTX Toolkit (v0.0.13), retaining reads in which at least 70% of bases had Phred scores > 20. Paired-end read files were subsequently repaired using BBMap, and the resulting high-quality reads were aligned to the human reference genome with HISAT2 (v0.7.12).

Differential expression was analyzed in R using the limma package (v3.52.2) with thresholds of |log2FC| > 1 and p < 0.05. Volcano plots were generated with ggplot2 (v3.3.6). Functional annotation and enrichment analyses were conducted using ClusterProfiler (v4.4.4). GO enrichment was performed across biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and molecular function (MF) categories, and KEGG pathway enrichment was considered significant at p < 0.05. PPI networks were built using the STRING database (v11.5, https://string-db.org/) by selecting interactions with a high-confidence combined score > 0.9.

Detection of apoptosis via flow cytometry

Apoptosis was quantified using Annexin V-FITC/propidium iodide (PI) double staining. A431 cells were harvested, washed twice with PBS (C0221A, Beyotime, China), and resuspended at 1 × 106 cells/mL. Staining was performed with the Annexin V-FITC/PI Apoptosis Detection Kit (C1383L, Beyotime, China). Briefly, 100 µL of the cell suspension was incubated with 5 µL Annexin V-FITC and 5 µL PI for 15 min at room temperature in the dark. Subsequently, 400 µL of 1× Annexin V binding buffer was added, and samples were analyzed immediately on a flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, USA). Annexin V+/PI⁻ cells were defined as early apoptotic, and Annexin V+/PI+ cells were defined as late apoptotic. Data were processed using FlowJo (v10, BD Biosciences, USA).

TUNEL staining for apoptosis detection

DNA fragmentation associated with apoptosis was evaluated by TUNEL staining. A431 cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA; P0099, Beyotime, China) for 20 min, rinsed with PBS, and permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 (P0096, Beyotime, China) for 10 min. TUNEL labeling was performed using a commercial kit (C1090, Beyotime, China). After a 1-hour incubation, nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (C1002, Beyotime, China). TUNEL-positive cells were visualized under a fluorescence microscope (Leica SP8, Germany), and the percentage of positive cells was quantified using ImageJ.

CCK-8 assay

Cell viability was determined using the CCK-8 assay (C0037, Beyotime, China). A431 cells were plated in 96-well plates at 5000 cells/well with six technical replicates per group and cultured for 12, 24, 36, or 48 h. Subsequently, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well and incubated for an additional 1.5 h. The absorbance (OD value) was measured at a wavelength of 450 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek, USA). Changes in cell viability were analyzed using GraphPad Prism.

EdU staining

Cell proliferation was assessed by EdU incorporation. A431 cells were seeded into 6-well plates at 1 × 105 cells per well and allowed to adhere for 24 h, followed by incubation with EdU reagent (C0078L, Beyotime, China) for 2 h. Cells were then fixed with 4% PFA, permeabilized, and EdU-labeled DNA was detected using Apollo reaction solution. Nuclei were stained with DAPI, and EdU-positive cells were observed by fluorescence microscopy. The proportion of EdU-positive cells was calculated using ImageJ to quantify proliferative activity.

Colony formation assay

Colony formation was used to evaluate long-term proliferative potential. A431 cells were plated in 6-well plates at 1000 cells per well and maintained for 14 days, with fresh medium replaced every 3 days. Colonies were fixed with 4% PFA for 15 min and stained with 0.05% crystal violet (32675, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) for 30 min. After washing with PBS, colonies were imaged using an inverted microscope, and colony numbers were quantified using ImageJ.

Wound healing assay

Cell migratory capacity was evaluated by a wound-healing (scratch) assay. A431 cells were grown to full confluence in 6-well plates, and a linear scratch was created across the monolayer using a 200 µL pipette tip. Floating cells and debris were removed by PBS washing, after which the medium was replaced with serum-free DMEM. Images were captured at 0 h and 24 h using an inverted microscope, and wound closure (scratch area/width) was quantified with ImageJ to determine the migration rate.

Transwell assay

Cell invasion was evaluated using Matrigel-coated Transwell chambers. Transwell inserts (8 μm pore size; 3374, Corning, USA) were pre-coated with 60 µL Matrigel (356234, BD Biosciences, USA) and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h to allow gelation. A431 cells (6 × 104 cells in 200 µL serum-free DMEM) were added to the upper compartment, and 500 µL DMEM supplemented with 15% FBS was placed in the lower chamber as a chemoattractant. After 24 h, inserts were washed with PBS and non-invaded cells on the upper surface were gently removed with a cotton swab. Cells that traversed the membrane were fixed with 4% PFA for 20 min and stained with 0.5% crystal violet for 20 min. Invaded cells were imaged and counted under an inverted microscope, and results were quantified using ImageJ.

Ca2+ detection

Intracellular Ca2+ concentration changes were assessed using the Fluo-4 AM fluorescent probe (S1060, Beyotime, China). A431 cells at logarithmic growth were washed twice with PBS and then loaded with 2 µM Fluo-4 AM diluted in serum-free DMEM for 60 min at 37 °C in the dark. After dye loading, cells were rinsed three times with PBS, and fluorescence was acquired by flow cytometry with 488 nm excitation. Data were analyzed using FlowJo.

Detection of MMP

MMP was assessed using the JC-1 dye (C2005, Beyotime, China). A431 cells in logarithmic growth were rinsed twice with PBS and incubated with JC-1 working solution for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark. After incubation, the cells were washed three times with PBS, and the red/green fluorescence ratio was measured using a fluorescence microscope. Changes in MMP were quantified using ImageJ software.

Detection of ROS levels

Intracellular ROS were quantified using the DCFH-DA probe (S0035S, Beyotime, China). A431 cells were incubated with 10 µM DCFH-DA for 30 min, followed by three PBS washes. Fluorescence was acquired by flow cytometry with 488 nm excitation, and signal intensities were analyzed using FlowJo.

For ROS assessment in tumor tissues, samples were homogenized in ice-cold PBS and the homogenates were incubated with DCFH-DA for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark. After three PBS washes, fluorescence was measured using a microplate reader (Ex 488 nm, Em 525 nm). ROS levels were calculated from a standard curve and normalized to total protein content.

Adenosine triphosphate (ATP) quantification

Intracellular ATP content was determined using an ATP Fluorescence Assay Kit (S0026, Beyotime, China). A431 cells at logarithmic growth were lysed with RIPA buffer, and the lysates were centrifuged at 12,000×g for 10 min to collect the supernatants. ATP working reagent was added following the manufacturer’s protocol, and luminescence was recorded at 570 nm using a microplate reader. ATP concentrations were calculated from a standard curve and normalized to total protein levels.

For ATP measurement in tumor tissues, samples were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and pulverized prior to extraction using the same kit. Tissue lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min, and supernatants were used for analysis. ATP standards were prepared as serial dilutions, and signals were measured at 570 nm. Tissue ATP levels were derived from the standard curve and normalized to protein content.

Co-IP

The protein interaction between Bcl-2 and p53 was validated using Co-IP assays. A431 cells were lysed on ice in RIPA buffer containing protease inhibitors, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 15 min to obtain the supernatant. To reduce nonspecific binding, the lysates were pre-cleared with Protein A/G magnetic beads (88802, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) for 1 h. The cleared lysates were then incubated with either an anti-Bcl-2 antibody (1:100; 12789-1-AP, Proteintech, China) or an anti-p53 antibody (1:100; 60283-2-Ig, Proteintech, China) overnight at 4 °C with rotation. Subsequently, Protein A/G magnetic beads were added and incubated for an additional 4 h to capture immune complexes. Bead-bound complexes were washed three times with PBS, eluted, resolved by SDS-PAGE, and analyzed by WB to detect Bcl-2-p53 interactions.

Fluorescence colocalization

The subcellular colocalization of Bcl-2 and p53 was assessed using immunofluorescence assays. A431 cells were plated on coverslips and cultured for 24 h, then fixed with 4% PFA for 15 min. Cells were permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 for 20 min and blocked with bovine serum albumin (BSA; ST023, Beyotime, China) for 30 min. Primary antibodies against Bcl-2 (1:200; 12789-1-AP, Proteintech, China) and p53 (1:200; 60283-2-Ig, Proteintech, China) were applied overnight at 4 °C. After PBS washes, Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated (A0423, Beyotime, China) and Cy3-conjugated (A0521, Beyotime, China) secondary antibodies were incubated for 1 h at 37 °C. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, and fluorescence images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope. Colocalization signals were quantified using ImageJ.

Establishment of subcutaneous tumor models

BALB/c nude mice (4–6 weeks old, 18–22 g; D000521, GemPharmaTech, China) were maintained under barrier conditions and acclimated for 1 week prior to experiments. A431 cells in logarithmic growth were harvested, washed twice with PBS, and resuspended in serum-free DMEM. Each mouse was subcutaneously injected in the right dorsal flank with 6 × 106 cells in a total volume of 200 µL. All animal procedures were conducted in accordance with protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (Approval No.: 2025021801). Mice were randomly assigned to three groups (n = 6 per group): oe-NC (control; unmodified A431 cells), oe-Bcl-2 (A431 cells with lentivirus-mediated Bcl-2 overexpression), and oe-Bcl-2 + oe-p53 (A431 cells co-expressing Bcl-2 and p53). Animals were housed under standard conditions (12 h light/dark cycle, 22 ± 2 °C, ~ 50% humidity) with free access to food and water, and general activity was monitored daily.

Tumor size was measured every 5 days over a 30-day period using an electronic caliper (Mitutoyo, Japan). The longest (L) and shortest (W) tumor diameters were recorded, and volume was calculated as V = 0.5 × L × W2. On day 21, mice were euthanized by CO2 asphyxiation, and tumors were excised, weighed, and photographed [36].

H&E staining

Tumor specimens were fixed in 4% PFA for 48 h and dehydrated through a graded ethanol series. The tissues were then cleared with an eco-friendly deparaffinizing agent (ST975, Beyotime, China) and embedded in paraffin (39601095, Leica, Germany). Paraffin Sect. (4 μm) underwent deparaffinization and were stained with hematoxylin (C0107, Beyotime, China) for 5 min. Differentiation was performed using hydrochloric acid ethanol (C0163S, Beyotime, China), followed by eosin staining (C0109, Beyotime, China) for 2 min. After dehydration, slides were mounted with neutral balsam (C0173, Beyotime, China) and examined under a light microscope. Histopathological characteristics, including tumor-cell nuclear atypia and necrotic areas, were documented.

IHC

Paraffin sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated, followed by heat-mediated antigen retrieval in citrate buffer (P0081, Beyotime, China) at 95 °C for 15 min. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked using hydrogen peroxide solution (P0100A, Beyotime, China) for 10 min. After blocking with 5% BSA for 30 min, sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against Bcl-2 (1:200; 12789-1-AP, Proteintech, China) or p53 (1:200; 60283-2-Ig, Proteintech, China). Following three washes with PBS, sections were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h. DAB was used as the chromogen (P0202, Beyotime, China), and nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin. Protein expression was observed under a light microscope and quantified using ImageJ to analyze the proportion of positive cell area.

Statistical analysis

Data from at least three independent experiments are expressed as mean ± SD. Differences between two groups were assessed using an unpaired (independent-samples) t-test. For three or more groups, one-way ANOVA was applied, and Tukey’s HSD test was used for multiple comparisons when overall significance was detected. When assumptions of normality or homogeneity of variance were not satisfied, nonparametric tests (Mann–Whitney U for two groups or Kruskal–Wallis H for multiple groups) were performed. Analyses were carried out using GraphPad Prism 9.5.0 (GraphPad Software, Inc.) and R 4.2.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). All tests were two-sided, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Conclusion

This study systematically elucidates the pivotal role of Bcl-2 in regulating apoptosis in cSCC. It was discovered that Bcl-2 promotes tumor cell proliferation, migration, and growth by modulating MAMs to inhibit p53-mediated mitochondrial apoptotic signaling. Transcriptomic analysis and a series of functional assays further confirmed that Bcl-2 significantly suppresses apoptosis by downregulating MAMs-related proteins such as VDAC2, IP3R, and SERCA2, thereby reducing intracellular calcium flux, ROS production, and caspase family protein expression. Co-overexpression of p53 can reverse this process, restoring the activity of the mitochondrial apoptotic signaling pathway and inhibiting tumor progression.

This research not only uncovers a novel mechanism by which Bcl-2 and p53 synergistically regulate apoptosis in cSCC but also provides theoretical support for targeting MAMs as a potential intervention point. This holds significant scientific importance and potential clinical translational value.

Regarding limitations, the current study primarily focuses on in vitro cell experiments and subcutaneous xenograft models, lacking an in situ skin-derived cSCC model and clinical tissue validation. Future studies should further explore its practical application prospects in cSCC diagnosis and treatment through multi-omics combined with clinical sample analysis, as well as expand research into immune regulation and resistance mechanisms.
