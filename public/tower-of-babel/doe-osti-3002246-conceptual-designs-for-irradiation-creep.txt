 SUMMARY
 Understanding irradiation creep of nuclear fuel cladding is important to properly
 size the initial fuel-cladding gap and understand when pellet-cladding contact is
 expected to occur due to a combination of fuel swelling and cladding creep-
 down. Irradiation creep also plays a role in relaxing stresses that develop in-pile.
 Silicon carbide fiber–reinforced silicon carbide matrix (SiC/SiC) composites are
 the leading long-term accident-tolerant fuel cladding concept for light-water
 reactors (LWRs). Although some limited data are available regarding irradiation
 creep of the individual constituents (fibers, matrix), data regarding irradiation
 creep of SiC/SiC composites are currently insufficient. Additional data regarding
 irradiation creep compliance and the rupture lifetime (combination of creep and
 slow crack growth) are needed to understand material limitations.
 This work describes the design and development of two irradiation vehicles that
 are being pursued for testing SiC/SiC concepts in the High Flux Isotope Reactor
 (HFIR). The first is a passive experiment, referred to as the PRECISE
 experiment, that leverages the constant coolant pressure of HFIR to compress a
 metallic bellows and provide a well-characterized load to drive creep in a
 SiC/SiC dog bone specimen. The total creep strain would be quantified post-
 irradiation by measuring dimensional changes of the specimen length as well as
 local dimensional changes within the gauge region. Non-stressed specimens
 would also be irradiated under the same conditions to provide an indication of
 dimensional changes due to radiation-induced swelling in the absence of creep.
 A second, more complex experiment, referred to as the INSITE experiment, is
 being designed in parallel that would use pneumatics to pressurize a metal
 bellows and linear variable differential transformers (LVDTs) to measure the
 specimen displacement in situ during irradiation. Such an experiment would
 provide significantly more data regarding the evolution of the creep compliance
 as a function of dose and applied stress within a single experiment but would
 require significantly more development time and cost to execute. The primary
 concern with the INSITE experiment is the accuracy, reliability, and expected
 lifetime of the LVDTs during irradiation at elevated temperatures. Efforts are
 being made to adjust the experiment design and operating procedure to limit
 LVDT temperatures and mitigate or otherwise compensate for uncertainties due
 to factors such as temperature fluctuations, creep in the surrounding structural
 materials, and drift of the LVDTs.
 This work describes the experiment designs, thermal and structural analysis that
 were performed to ensure that the desired temperature and stress conditions can
 be achieved, some initial sensitivity analyses to predict the evolution of the
 radiation-induced specimen displacements, and potential sources of uncertainty
 in the measurements. Out-of-pile testing is being performed in parallel to confirm
 that the test trains achieve the expected stress states in the specimens and do not
 result in prohibitive stress concentrators (e.g., in the grip regions) that might risk
 pre-mature failure. The PRECISE experiments are proceeding toward fabrication
 and assembly with HFIR insertion planned during fiscal year 2026. The INSITE
 experiment is progressing toward out-of-pile demonstrations, which will provide
 more conclusive evidence regarding the feasibility of executing these tests in
 HFIR or whether alternative displacement monitoring techniques may need to be
 considered.

 CONCEPTUAL DESIGNS FOR IRRADIATION CREEP
 TESTING OF SIC IN HFIR
1. INTRODUCTION
Following the Fukushima nuclear accident in Japan, the US and the broader nuclear energy community
have been pursuing advanced nuclear fuel concepts with enhanced accident tolerance. Since that time, Cr-
coated zirconium alloys have emerged as a near-term candidate with multiple US fuel vendors having
already completed irradiation of lead test rods. Longer term concepts such as iron-based alloys are also
being considered, primarily for boiling water reactor applications. However, the material that offers the
most significant improvements in accident tolerance is silicon carbide fiber-reinforced, silicon carbide
matrix composites (SiC/SiC). These materials offer enhanced resistance to steam oxidation during accident
scenarios, reduced hydrogen generation, and improved high-temperature strength compared with
conventional Zr alloys [1-4]. In addition, SiC/SiC composites offer favorable neutronic properties (i.e., low
neutron absorption) and could enable fuel burnup extension, power uprates, and fuel cycle economic
benefits. An important aspect of these composite materials is the pseudo-ductility and enhanced damage
tolerance compared with monolithic SiC ceramics. The composite architecture is particularly effective at
mitigating crack propagation.

For any light water reactor (LWR) fuel system, a critical phenomenon that must be well-understood is the
evolution of the fuel-cladding gap and the potential for fuel cladding interactions upon gap closure [5]. For
Zr alloy fuel cladding, gap closure is a complex process that involves fuel densification early in life followed
by subsequent fuel swelling and cladding creep down (in addition to growth for Zr alloys) due to the
compressive stresses that result from the external coolant pressure. A thorough understanding of the fuel-
cladding gap is required to properly size the initial fuel-cladding gap and inform the fuel specification.
Irradiation creep is also important in understanding the evolution of stress relaxation and in understanding
rupture lifetimes, which for SiC/SiC would involve a combination of creep as well as potential slow crack
propagation under a combination of irradiation and stress.

Irradiation creep data for SiC/SiC composites are limited [6]. The materials that make up the composites
(e.g., the fiber and matrix) have been studied, and some coarse estimates of creep compliance have been
reported [6-10]. However, there is a wide range of fiber materials and matrix densification approaches that
are being considered, and perhaps more importantly, the creep and/or slow crack propagation of the
composite system has not been well studied. It is reasonable to expect that slip at the fiber-matrix interface
(responsible for the pseudo-ductility), including the relatively weak pyrolytic carbon interphase material,
would accelerate creep of the composite relative to monolithic SiC fabricated by chemical vapor deposition
(CVD).

This report summarizes two concepts for evaluating the irradiation creep and slow crack propagation in
SiC/SiC composites, leveraging the high neutron flux and displacement damage rates that are available in
the High Flux Isotope Reactor (HFIR). The first concept is a rapid, low-cost approach to measuring
irradiation creep using a passive test in which SiC/SiC samples would be irradiated under a constant
mechanical load (stress) and the creep strain would be measured post-irradiation, known as PRECISE. The
second concept is a more complex and expensive experiment that would involve monitoring and controlling
the applied load and measuring the displacement in situ during irradiation, referred to as INSITE. The
benefit to this approach is that the dynamics of the creep compliance, including its dependence on dose,
temperature, and stress, could in principle be measured within a single experiment. Given the increasing
interest in accelerating the process of fuel qualification, such an instrumented test may be worth the
increased cost if it can reduce the number of experiments and provide more data for developing material
models.

2. BACKGROUND
 2.1 Previous SiC Irradiation Creep Data
One of the earliest studies on the irradiation creep of SiC was completed by Price in 1977, with several
other such as Scholz et al. and Shankar et al. following [8-10]. However, the creep compliance values
determined in these studies, and others, tended to disagree with one another, with variance in several orders
of magnitude reported. Many of these studies were found to be unreliable with several authors stating a lack
of confidence in their results. Further, many of these studies relied on ion beam irradiation rather than in-
reactor irradiation.

Of the studies to date, Katoh et al. and Koyanagi et al. seem to provide the most reliable results for SiC
irradiation creep covering the initial transient and steady state creep regimes [6, 7]. These studies employed
bend stress relaxation (BSR) tests in HFIR that included multiple forms of SiC, including SiC fibers, CVD
SiC, sintered SiC, and SiC/SiC composites. Katoh et al. quantified the initial transient stage of irradiation
creep compliance, up to about 1 displacement per atom (dpa), and showed it was related to the to the
transient swelling of SiC which resulted in a swelling-coupled model of irradiation creep compliance [7].
Koyanagi et al. continued this work to demonstrate a stress exponent of unity and a steady state creep
compliance of approximately 1×10-7 MPa-1dpa-1 from 1 to 30 dpa [6]. This study resulted in a suggested
SiC creep compliance model that simply adds the steady state creep to the previous swelling-coupled model.
This is the model used throughout Section 5.

Other notable studies of SiC irradiation creep include the instrumented experiment conducted in the Halden
reactor reported by Teranni [11, 12]. This experiment used an LVDT-bellows based creep capsule to collect
continuous data from CVD SiC specimens maintained at 300 °C. One specimen was stressed uniaxially at
100 MPa while the other was considered unstressed at approximately 5 MPa to maintain contact with the
specimen. The data set collected from the unstressed specimen was used to correct the stressed specimen’s
data for swelling effects. Due to the low flux of the Halden reactor, the specimens were only irradiated to
approximately 0.06 dpa. However, the creep strain determined with the in situ LVDT data was shown to be
approximately an order of magnitude larger than that determined by direct measurement of engraved
markings during post-irradiation examination (PIE) [13]. Similarly, the creep compliance determined in
situ was approximately one to two orders of magnitude larger than that determined by Katoh et al. and
Koyanagi et al. discussed previously. A possible explanation for the erroneous in-situ results is discussed
in Section 5.5.1.

 2.2 Previous In-Pile Creep Measurement Concepts
Passive irradiation creep experiments have long been used to evaluate time-dependent deformation in
structural materials exposed to neutron flux under applied stress. These methods incorporate self-contained
mechanical or pressure-based loading mechanisms within sealed capsules, enabling irradiation testing
without active feedback systems. Their simplicity, compatibility in test reactor environments, and cost-
effectiveness make them an indispensable tool for screening material behavior, generating creep data, and
informing predictive models.

One of the earliest and most widely used techniques is bend stress relaxation, in which specimens are pre-
bent and constrained in a fixed curvature, such as cams or four-point benders. During irradiation, stress
relaxation occurs due to creep, and the post-irradiation curvature change is used to calculate the deformation
response, typically with support from finite element modeling or analytical approaches. This method
enables simultaneous testing of multiple specimens within compact capsules and has been applied since the
1960s in reactors such as EBR-II and HFIR [6, 14, 15]. However, stress and strain are non-uniform through
the specimen thickness, and accurate modeling is required for data interpretation.

August 2025                                                                                                    3

Uniaxial loading using internal pressurized bellows offers a more uniform and controlled stress state. In
this approach, a gas-charged bellows applies a constant axial load to a train of specimens [16, 17]. This
setup is well suited for extracting key creep parameters such as the irradiation creep rate and stress exponent.
However, thermal management of the bellows poses a challenge. Because the bellows must be thermally
isolated via a gas gap to prevent obstruction when load is applied, the bellows temperature can be quite
high (gas gap acts as a thermal insulator), particularly when implemented in test reactors with a high neutron
flux (for accelerated damage accumulation) and associated high nuclear heating rates. As a result, the
bellows can overheat during irradiation, potentially leading to gas leakage, structural degradation, or a
change in the applied spring force—ultimately altering or invalidating the applied load [16].

A related concept is uniaxial loading via externally pressurized bellows, in which the reactor’s coolant
pressure is transmitted to the specimen stack through a diaphragm or bellows [18]. This design eliminates
the need for internal gas systems and naturally applies the load dependent on reactor operating coolant
pressure. However, because the applied load is coupled to reactor pressure, it is susceptible to fluctuations
from power transients, making the exact stress history difficult to reconstruct if there is unstable operation
during the course of the experiment. This limitation is particularly important in test reactors with variable
power schedules or those used for transient experiments. Fortunately, one advantage of conducting passive
creep tests in HFIR is that it operates at constant full power during irradiation cycles, which helps ensure
stable coolant pressure and mitigates concerns related to load variability from power transients.

A simpler and mechanically robust alternative to pressurized loading is gravity-driven uniaxial creep
testing. In this concept, a dense weight, typically made of tungsten or another high-density material, is
positioned vertically above the specimen and transfers a constant axial load through a rigid or semi-flexible
load train. This approach eliminates the need for internal pressurization or thermal isolation, reducing
mechanical complexity and the risk of gas leakage or bellows degradation. Gravity-based loading is
inherently self-regulating and compatible with reactors that have a vertical irradiation position, such as
HFIR. However, achieving relevant stress levels for irradiation creep often requires a substantial mass,
which can introduce design challenges. In some cases, the applied load must extend beyond the capsule
body and penetrate through the reactor’s upper bulkhead, complicating integration and safety qualification.
A gravity-loaded creep capsule was previously implemented at HFIR [19], demonstrating the feasibility of
this method despite its engineering challenges. While the load is fixed and cannot be actively adjusted
during irradiation, gravity-driven designs provide a viable passive alternative.

Pressurized tube testing directly replicates the hoop stress experienced by fuel cladding and pressure tubes.
In this configuration, sealed tubes are internally pressurized with gas to generate circumferential (hoop)
stress [20, 21]. Creep deformation is measured out of pile after irradiation by examining dimensional
changes such as diameter or wall thinning. While this method captures realistic service conditions, it
requires the ability to seal the specimen at high internal pressure, which, while relatively straightforward
for metal alloys, is more challenging for SiC/SiC composites. The accuracy is also limited due to challenges
in performing precise diameter measurements of small tubes, at least compared with the precision that can
be achieved when measuring the length of longer dog bone specimens following a uniaxial creep test.
Nonetheless, it remains the most representative method for validating uniaxial creep models under
operational stress states for cladding materials.

Because most reactor components are subject to multiaxial stress states, particularly hoop stress, it is critical
to relate uniaxial test results to real-world conditions. Uniaxial tests offer clean, interpretable data used to
calibrate constitutive creep models. These models are then applied in simulations to predict multiaxial
deformation behavior. Pressurized tube experiments serve to validate these predictions, ensuring that the
material response under actual service conditions aligns with uniaxial-based expectations.

The previous experiment concepts discussed so far all require measurements of specimen deformations
during PIE. As a result, each specimen either provides only one data point or it must be repetitively removed
from the reactor and capsule, measured, and placed back in the reactor in a new capsule. This process can
become expensive, time-consuming, and complex. Instrumented capsules provide an alternative to this
process by recording specimen deformations in situ in real time. The most common concept for an in situ
experiment uses a LVDT for displacement measurement and a gas pressurized bellows to apply uniaxial
stress to a specimen [11, 12, 22, 23]. This method typically compares displacement data from a stressed
specimen with a second capsule with an unstressed specimen to isolate the irradiation creep component of
deformation. Often, these LVDT-bellows capsules also contain temperature monitoring and on-line control
of specimen stress and temperature.

 2.3 High Flux Isotope Reactor (HFIR)
Both the PRECISE (passive) and INSITE (instrumented) experiment designs are tailored for deployment
within HFIR irradiation facilities, as shown in Figure 1. The PRECISE irradiation experiment is configured
for use in the Flux Trap Target Rod Rabbit Holder (TRRH) positions, while the INSITE experiment is
designed for placement in the large Removable Beryllium (RB) facilities.

The TRRH positions offer a peak thermal neutron flux of 2.5×1015 n/cm²·s and a peak fast neutron flux of
1.2×1015 n/cm²·s. In comparison, the RB positions provide a peak thermal flux of 1.2×1015 n/cm²·s and a
peak fast flux of 4.5×1014 n/cm²·s [24].

3. ANALYSIS METHODOLOGY
 3.1 Thermal Finite Element Analysis (FEA)
Thermal analysis of the experiment designs was performed using the ANSYS finite element software.
Steady-state thermal simulations were performed to calculate temperature distributions throughout the
experiment during irradiation. The simulation approach accounts for complex geometries, heterogeneous
material configurations, and position-dependent heating rates characteristic of the HFIR environment.

The ORNL Irradiation Engineering Group (IEG) maintains a curated database of design and analysis
calculations (DACs) that include temperature-dependent and, in some cases, radiation dose–dependent

6                                                                                          August 2025

thermophysical properties [26]. These DACs incorporate data from recognized sources such as CINDAS,
MatWeb, and peer-reviewed literature. Material properties from this database are accessed through custom
macros to ensure consistency and traceability across multiple capsule designs. These macros provide the
required inputs for temperature-dependent thermal conductivity, specific heat, and density values, etc.,
providing consistent modeling of the heat transfer behavior of HFIR irradiation experiments.

Internal heat generation within the capsule was modeled using volumetric heat source terms based on
gamma and neutron heating predictions from detailed radiation transport simulations. These predictions
account for spatial variations in flux, material-dependent properties, and interaction cross sections, with the
dominant contributions arising from neutron-induced gamma heating, along with other secondary nuclear
interactions. Heating rates were derived from Monte Carlo N-Particle (MCNP) and SCALE simulations for
the capsule’s specific radial position within HFIR and were applied to all relevant components accordingly.

Temperature control is achieved through engineered gas gaps between the internal structures and the outer
housing. These gaps function as thermal insulators and are tuned to regulate internal temperatures relative
to the surrounding housing in contact with the reactor coolant. The effective thermal conductance of these
gaps is modified within the simulation using parameterized macros. In addition, thermal expansion of
capsule components is included directly in the thermal analysis to accurately capture changes in contact,
gap width, and material alignment that influence heat transfer pathways under irradiation conditions.

Convective boundary conditions are applied to the capsule exterior to model heat transfer into the HFIR
coolant. These boundaries use empirically validated heat transfer coefficients and coolant temperature
profiles specific to the irradiation location. All boundary conditions are implemented using standardized
methods defined in the DACs to ensure consistency with prior HFIR experiment analyses.

 3.2 Structural FEA
After completing the thermal analysis, the temperature results from the thermal model are mapped onto a
static structural model in ANSYS. Because the previous thermal modeling accounted for the effects of
thermal expansion on the gas gap conductance, the thermal and structural models can be decoupled for
computational efficiency. Boundary conditions and loads are applied as needed for the PRECISE (passive)
or INSITE (instrumented) experiments. Components unrelated to the load train are suppressed to further
improve computational efficiency. Material properties were retrieved from the same DAC library described
previously for the thermal models.

In addition to traditional elastic stresses and strains, the INSITE experiment model also calculates
irradiation creep strain and swelling strain for all load train components. The passive creep rabbit design
would not have benefitted significantly from the inclusion of creep and swelling since creep is measured
directly during PIE, so it was not used in the interest of time and simplicity. The stainless steel creep and
swelling are modeled as linear with respect to fast neutron fluence, which is appropriate for doses up to
approximately 20 dpa based on the available data in the literature [27, 28]. The small portion of titanium in
the load train is assumed to follow the same creep and swelling models as stainless steel. The SiC creep
and swelling were modeled according to the equations given by Katoh et al. and Koyanagi et al. for
monolithic SiC [6, 7, 29]. It is hypothesized that creep of SiC/SiC composites will be greater than that for
monolithic SiC, but assuming values for monolithic SiC should provide a conservative estimate for the
resolvable creep compliance in this experiment due to the reduced creep in the specimen. Because of the
form of the SiC creep and swelling equations, custom user programmable features (UPFs) were required to
implement the irradiation creep and swelling models for SiC. It should be noted that thermal creep was
assumed to be negligible in this analysis, due to the relatively small target temperature of 300 °C compared
with the melting temperature of the materials used.

The inclusion of creep and swelling strains in the INSITE finite element modeling allowed the model to
predict the deformation of all capsule components. This functionality was used to plot the elongation of the
different load train components and ultimately the LVDT displacement against neutron fluence, as
described in detail in Section 5.4. These results were leveraged to calculate, understand, and optimize creep
compliance uncertainties, as described in Section 5.5.

4. PRECISE: PASSIVE RIG FOR EVALUATING COMBINED
 IRRADIATION AND STRESS EFFECTS
 4.1 Inherent Challenges of Passive Approaches
Passive in-pile creep experiments offer a practical and cost-effective means for evaluating irradiation creep,
particularly in early-stage material screening. However, these systems also present several inherent
limitations that must be carefully addressed to ensure meaningful data collection.

A critical challenge in all passive in-pile experiments is the reliance on passive thermometry, such as melt
wires or silicon carbide dilatometry to estimate irradiation temperature [30, 31]. These methods only
provide an indication of the maximum (in the case of melt wires) or end-of-irradiation (in the case of passive
SiC thermometry) temperatures and may not properly indicate the specimen temperature, depending on the
experiment design and resulting spatial temperature variations. Without in situ temperature monitoring, any
changes in the thermal environment due to reactor power fluctuations, coolant flow variations, or localized
effects from neighboring experiments cannot be detected or corrected during irradiation. These factors may
result in unanticipated temperature distributions that affect the experiment’s conditions and data quality.

Accurate thermal modeling is therefore essential to predict the actual specimen temperatures. Similarly,
load application in passive systems depends on capsule mechanics or pressure differentials and typically
cannot be adjusted or monitored in real-time. Whether stress is imposed via a sealed bellows, an external
coolant pressure interface, or an internal pressurization system, it must be inferred through pre-irradiation
calibration and post-test interpretation. Any unaccounted-for change in specimen geometry, bellows
condition, or internal pressure, particularly due to thermal effects, can introduce uncertainty into the applied
stress history. Passive experiments are also vulnerable to reactor operation perturbations, such as power
transients, flow anomalies, or unplanned shutdowns. These events may alter temperature and pressure
conditions within the capsule, impacting the stress and creep response.

An additional consideration is the impact of specimen geometry on creep response. Due to spatial
constraints within irradiation capsules, specimens used in passive creep experiments often deviate from
ASTM standard dimensions. Shorter gauge lengths and reduced cross-sectional areas can increase
sensitivity to geometric variability, end effects, and alignment tolerances. While two specimens under
identical stress may experience similar nominal loading, differences in gauge length or constraint conditions
can alter strain distributions and deformation mechanisms. Therefore, proper evaluation of sub-sized
specimen geometries and the associated loading mechanisms is essential to ensure that measured behavior
can be meaningfully interpreted or extrapolated to represent bulk material response. Comparative data
across experiments must be considered within the context of specimen geometry, load alignment, and the
specific mechanical boundary conditions imposed by the capsule design.

For composite materials such as SiC/SiC, additional constraints arise when selecting sub-sized geometries.
These composites exhibit anisotropic and heterogeneous behavior, with mechanical performance strongly
influenced by fiber architecture, orientation, and local fiber–matrix interactions. Maintaining a sufficient
gauge volume is critical to ensure the specimen captures representative bulk behavior rather than localized

8                                                                                          August 2025

effects dominated by just a few fiber bundles. In particular, reduced specimen cross sections may lead to
variability due to statistical differences in fiber alignment or architecture across specimens, especially in
woven configurations. While the passive capsule design varies the applied stress by adjusting the specimen
cross section, this must be carefully balanced to ensure the gauge section retains enough volume to reflect
the composite's designed fiber architecture.

Lastly, passive methods require expensive, time-consuming PIE to thoroughly characterize the deformation
of the specimens, ultimately yielding irradiation creep data at only a single dose per specimen. Techniques
such as profilometry, dimensional metrology, and optical microscopy are often used to measure local and
global deformations, and modeling is often required to reconstruct the stress-strain history.

In summary, the simplicity of passive creep experiments allows them to be quickly and cheaply assembled
and irradiated to rapidly screen new materials at the cost of increased time for PIE and limited data regarding
evolutions with dose. They also require detailed thermomechanical modeling and a well-designed
experimental capsule to complement the PIE results, understand potential sources of uncertainty, capture
potential variations in environmental conditions and stress evolution, and ultimately ensure reliable and
meaningful results.

 4.2 Current Irradiation Concept
The Passive Rig for Evaluating Combined Irradiation and Stress Effects (PRECISE) experimental
capability being pursued here is a coolant pressure-driven creep capsule design that uses a stainless steel
304 (SS304) housing with an integrated (welded) SS304 bellows to generate and transfer load to a tensile
specimen through a dedicated load path, as shown in Figure 2. The applied load results from coolant
pressure acting over the effective thrust area of the bellows, compressing its convolutions and generating
an axial force. This force is transferred through a quartz block and driver component into the tensile frame
assembly. The tensile frame holds both the filler and specimen retainers in alignment, ensuring that the
axial load is directed onto the gauge section of the creep specimen. This assembly converts coolant pressure
into a well-defined tensile stress within the specimen.

Positioned symmetrically around the central creep specimen are reference specimens and SiC thermometry
specimens, as illustrated in the exploded view in Figure 2. These components are stacked in parallel and
housed within the same tensile frame to ensure they experience nearly identical thermal and radiation
conditions. A filler component sits between the capsule housing and specimen stack to control heat flow
and stabilize the temperature distribution along the gauge length.

 4.3 Design Stress Selection and Load Application
The tensile load applied to the specimen in this capsule design is driven by the pressure differential across
the external bellows. This differential is generated by the reactor primary coolant pressure acting on the
outside of the bellows and the helium fill gas sealed within the capsule. As shown in Figure 4(a), this
pressure differential (ΔP = Pout – Pin) translates to a uniaxial force on the tensile specimen.

The fill gas pressure (Pin) is initially set near atmospheric conditions during capsule assembly. However,
thermal expansion of the gas at the target irradiation temperature (300 °C) is included in the design
calculation, which results in a slight increase in internal pressure. The coolant pressure (Pout) is calculated
from known inlet pressures in HFIR at the target location, with the expected pressure drop across the target
region considered.

To visualize the mechanical response, Figure 4(b) presents the predicted applied stress on the specimen as
a function of thickness for the axial positions within the HFIR TRRH experiment facilities. The shaded
region defines the stress envelope across the experimental positions. Based on the design objective of
applying ~100 MPa of stress during irradiation, a specimen thickness of 1.0 mm was selected. At this
dimension, the applied stress at Position 7 is approximately 103 MPa.

To minimize variability in the applied load, it is recommended that the capsule be placed in Position 7(top)
with the bellows oriented upward. This configuration avoids flow disturbances from adjacent capsules and
ensures the bellows are exposed to predictable pressure conditions.

4.4.2 Structural FEA
Another finite element model with a mesh identical to that of the one used for thermal analysis was used to
conduct a static structural analysis. The steady-state temperature distribution described in Section 4.1.1 was
mapped onto the structural mesh as an initial boundary condition. This analysis was conducted to evaluate
the mechanical response of the passive capsule under the combined effects of irradiation temperatures,
thermal expansion, and component interaction.

To reduce the computational size of the model, some unneeded components were ignored for the purpose
of the structural analysis. The bellows itself was omitted from the structural analysis, and force and fixed
displacement boundary conditions were applied to the faces where the bellows is welded to the endcap and
housing, respectively. This applied load is calculated based on the hydraulic pressure profile within the
TRRH stack. If the capsule is modeled near the top of the stack, the full inlet pressure is applied. For
positions lower in the stack, the load is adjusted to account for the estimated pressure drop due to coolant
flow resistance and system geometry. This approach allows the model to replicate the mechanical loading
experienced by the bellows and capture the resulting stress and strain in the load train and specimen for
experiment design. Additionally, all the unloaded parts within the assembly (filler, reference specimen,
thermometry, and retainer spring) were excluded from the mesh used for structural analysis to further reduce
unnecessary computational cost.

The mechanical properties used for this analysis were assigned using the same material library as the
preceding thermal analysis. These material property assignments were used to accurately capture in-pile
mechanical performance. Thermomechanical properties at the design temperature were also used to assess
whether differential thermal expansion between components could interfere with the intended loading path.
This analysis ensures that thermal mismatch does not induce constraint or binding that would inhibit proper
load transfer to the specimen. The results confirm the capsule delivers the target axial stress of
approximately 100 MPa in the gauge section and help identify any localized stress concentrations or
mechanical vulnerabilities in the assembly under HFIR operating conditions.

5. INSITE: Instrumented Capsule for In Situ Thermo-Mechanical

The irradiation experiment concept described within this section is referred to as the Instrumented Capsule
for In-situ Thermo-Mechanical Experiments (INSITE). The experiment is based on an LVDT-bellows
concept for instrumented, in situ measurements of irradiation creep of SiC. Similar to the passive creep
rabbit, the INSITE experiment is targeting LWR temperatures (300 °C) and stresses up to 100 MPa. As
described later in this report, a range of stresses will be used to reduce uncertainties related to creep in the
load train.

 5.1 Inherent Challenges in LVDT-Bellows Creep Experiments
Historically, the most common approach to measuring and controlling the applied load and displacement
during irradiation is to use a pneumatically pressurized bellows to apply load and a LVDT to measure
displacement. An in-situ experiment based on an LVDT-bellows capsule, such as INSITE, presents multiple
challenges inherent to the complexity of the experiment and the extreme in-pile environment. More
specifically, the simultaneous requirement for displacement monitoring, load application, and temperature
control necessitates a complex system of components that must operate reliably to generate accurate results.
Further, the experiment must operate in a harsh in-pile environment, which drives high temperatures and
undesirable material behaviors in the components of this complex system. Several of the challenges
encountered during the feasibility study of INSITE, which are generally applicable to any in situ LVDT-
bellows based experiment, will be discussed here. The approaches developed during this feasibility study
to address these challenges are discussed throughout this report.

5.1.2 Load Application
Several challenges also exist to apply the correct load to the specimen. A typical load train consists of an
assembly of multiple components housed within a capsule, in which the clearances maintained between
components and the capsule must be on the order of tens to hundreds of microns to maintain the desired
temperatures within the experiment. As the specimen elongates and the free end of the load train displaces,
it is possible for its components to contact the capsule or other fixed components, which causes friction. As
a result, the load transferred to the specimen may be reduced, despite the pressure actuating the bellows,
and thus the total force transferred to the load train, staying constant. Similarly, creep within the bellows
during irradiation may lead to a change in the effective area of the bellows, causing a change in load train
force for a given input pressure. In extreme cases, the bellows may rupture and eliminate the load train
force entirely [16].

Common challenges associated with creep and tensile testing with a load frame in a lab environment also
apply to an in situ LVDT-bellows experiment, although they are typically exaggerated by the extreme
irradiation environment. For example, the misalignment of the load train and bowing of the specimen can
induce bending stress in the specimen despite the stress being assumed to be uniaxial. Differential thermal
expansion and/or swelling in components, like the capsule or specimen, can add to the nominal
misalignment, inducing more error.

5.1.3 Temperature Control
Several challenges also exist regarding the temperature control of the experiment. In most cases, in situ
temperatures are measured by thermocouples placed within the experiment, which inform adjustments to
the gas composition within the experiment that in turn affects temperatures. In contrast to a furnace

August 2025                                                                                                 21

environment where the thermocouple and all other components will reach an equilibrium at the
environment’s temperature, the temperature of components in-pile are driven by internal heat generation
and will not reach a uniform steady-state temperature throughout the entire experiment. Moreover, the
temperature through the cross section of the thermocouple itself can vary in-pile with a high enough internal
heat generation rate and/or heat flux. As a result, the measured temperature of the thermocouple is sensitive
to its own geometry and materials, its location within the experiment, and the location and style of its
attachment to the experiment.

This is further complicated by the fact that the thermocouple often must be placed some distance from the
specimen to prevent any mechanical interference between the sample and the thermocouple. As a result,
the temperature measured by the thermocouple must be related to the temperature of interest, often across
multiple components and gas gaps each with their own uncertainties. Therefore, the measured temperature
of interest will have an uncertainty that is sensitive to the details of the experiment design and the analysis
completed to relate its temperature to that of the thermocouple.

5.2.1 Experiment Hardware
The INSITE experiment concept, shown in Figure 10, Figure 11, and Figure 12, is designed to provide
continuous, in situ displacement monitoring, load control, and temperature control during irradiation in the
large removable beryllium (RB) experiment facilities in HFIR. The experiment consists of six LVDT-
bellows based capsules, with three capsules located above the centerline of HFIR and the other three
mirrored below the centerline. The groups of capsules are surrounded by aluminum holders that locate the
capsules within HFIR and provide heat dissipation. The temperature limitations of the LVDTs requires the
capsules to be located away from the midplane of HFIR to reduce heat generation. As a result, a large
volume is available between the capsule groups, currently filled by a solid aluminum spacer, which can be
used for passive testing such as bend stress relaxation or pressurized tube experiments. This configuration,
with an empty middle spacer region and symmetric (about the HFIR midplane) upper and lower capsules,
allows the upper and lower sets of capsules to accumulate similar fast neutron fluences while maintaining
acceptable LVDT temperatures.

5.2.2 Experiment Operation

After insertion into HFIR, the INSITE experiment will follow the basic process shown in Figure 15. At the
beginning of a cycle in HFIR, the capsule will be pressurized to apply stress to the specimen, and data
collection will be initiated. Throughout the cycle, the temperature measurements of the thermocouples and
fiber optics can be used to locally adjust the fill gas composition within the experiment to maintain the
target temperatures as the neutron flux and gamma heating profiles change throughout the cycle.
Corrections may also be required due to radiation-induced dimensional changes in the experiment
components. After the end of the cycle when the temperatures have returned to ambient, two displacement
measurements will be taken from each LVDT. First the capsule will be depressurized to a low pressure that
just ensures contact at the grips to permit measurements of the swelling and creep deformations without
any effects of differential thermal expansion. Then, the capsule will be depressurized completely, and a
small pressure would be applied externally to the capsules, and thus internally to the bellows, by the fill gas
lines to return the LVDT to a reference position where a collar on the LVDT core contacts the capsule end
cap. This second measurement will be compared with an identical measurement taken prior to the cycle to
determine the LVDT drift throughout the cycle, which can be applied as a correction during the creep
compliance calculation as described in Section 5.5.1. This data collection process will be repeated for
additional cycles until the desired material damage is achieved.

The data collected can be analyzed after each cycle to determine creep compliance curves up to that point
with the method described in Section 5.5.1. In summary, the LVDT data would first be corrected for the
LVDT drift during the cycle by subtracting the drift from the original data, assuming a linear drift rate
throughout the cycle. The displacement rate with respect to dpa would then be calculated for all three
capsules within a group, each of which would contain a different specimen cross-sectional area and axial
stress. The creep compliance can then be calculated according to Eq. (4). This creep compliance calculation
method is an adaptation of the typical method of comparing a stressed specimen’s deformation directly with
an unstressed specimen, which is modified to reduce the uncertainty in the measured compliance, primarily
due to the effects of load train creep as described in more detail in Section 5.5.

After removal from HFIR, the final creep strain of each specimen can be determined in PIE according to a
similar process described for the passive creep rabbit previously. Specifically, the displacement of fiducial
scribes engraved along the specimen gauge section can be measured and compared with pre-irradiation
measurements to determine the total specimen strain. Similarly, the total strain of the unloaded reference
specimens can be determined. The creep strain in the loaded specimen can be isolated by subtracting the
unloaded reference specimen strain from the loaded specimen strain, which will offset the effects of other
irradiation-induced deformations such as swelling. Additionally, the passive thermometry can be evaluated
to estimate the specimen irradiation temperature during PIE. These PIE evaluations can be used to improve
the understanding of how the specimen temperature and deformation during irradiation compares with the
temperatures and displacements measured by the thermocouples and LVDT, respectively.

 5.3 Analysis Results
The analysis results described here for the INSITE experiment were used to inform decisions in the
experiment geometry and operation described previously. As a result, the results discussed here are not
entirely representative of the most current experiment geometry described in Section 5.2.1. However, only
minor modifications were made to the geometry, primarily concerning the exterior capsule geometry and
the inclusion of thermocouples, LVDT cables, gas lines, and fiber optics, that are not expected to
significantly change the results presented here or the conclusions of INSITE’s feasibility.

5.3.1 Thermal FEA
The thermal FEA followed the process described in Section 3.1. The 120° symmetry of the capsules about
the experiment’s centerline and the symmetry of the experiment about the midplane of HFIR were leveraged
to create a one-sixth symmetry model to increase computational efficiency. A convective boundary
condition was applied to the external surface of the housing that is in contact with HFIR’s coolant. Contacts
within the model were set up on a case-by-case basis, primarily with frictionless contacts that permit the
accurate representation of gas gap conductance as described in Section 3.1. Meshing was completed
primarily with hex-dominant methods, with tetrahedral elements being applied to complex geometries like
the LVDT body. Shell elements were applied to the membrane-like bellows.

The heat generation rates (HGRs) for the different materials were primarily taken from previous HFIR
safety calculations and are given in Table 5, along with the components modeled with each HGR. Because
most HFIR safety calculations are for the flux trap rather than the RB facilities, the HGR for aluminum and
titanium were scaled from the documented values for the flux trap using a factor of 0.402 based on the
results from Sims and Swanks which provides HGRs for materials in both positions [38].[38]. This is a
common technique that has been approved in other previous HFIR safety calculations. Furthermore, no
previous HGR data was available for the silver alloy that makes up the LVDT windings, so its value was
taken as the average of the scaled flux trap values of molybdenum and tungsten. These materials have an
atomic number lower and higher, respectively, than silver, and it has been shown that HGRs, or at least
those dominated by gamma heating, generally scale with atomic number. This approach also has precedent
in estimating the HGRs of new materials. Because these windings are modeled as a representative annulus
instead of individual wires, this average was multiplied by an additional factor of 0.65 to account for the
void space around the wires and the ceramic insulation on the wires.

5.3.2 Structural FEA
The structural FEA completed during this feasibility study had as its objectives of both conducting a stress
analysis of the load train components and predicting the displacement measured by the LVDT as a function
of fluence. The structural FEA process followed the process outlined in Section 3.2. Due to the complexity
of the INSITE experiment, the static structural model was independently linked to the thermal model. This
directly transfers the geometry and temperature results from the thermal model, while allowing the contact
definitions and suppression of components to be defined independently of the thermal model. As a result,
the computational efficiency was improved by suppressing all components that were not a part of the
load train or directly connected to the LVDT and by replacing the bellows with a spring element with the
spring rate published by the manufacturer. Meshing was again achieved primarily with hex-dominant
methods, with tetrahedral elements being applied to complex geometries like the LVDT body.

Hand calculations determined that a pressure differential of 5 MPa between the interior and exterior of the
capsule would produce the targeted stress of 100 MPa in the specimen. The pressure differential was
simulated by applying a 5 MPa pressure normal to all surfaces that would be subject to capsule
pressurization. This captured the effects of hydrostatic compression on the specimen and load frame. A
3-2-1 method was used to apply displacement boundary conditions at the bottom of the capsule to prevent
rigid body motion without restraining the deformation of any components. The force reactions at the
boundary conditions were checked to verify that the pressure loads were applied correctly and that they did
not produce a net force on the assembly.

As described in Section 3.2, the material properties were sourced from the DACs maintained by the IEG at
ORNL with ANSYS user programmable features (UPFs) being used to implement custom creep and
swelling models. The ANSYS APDL script used to apply the creep and swelling models allowed the
definition of the end fluence, defined in dpa, and the number of equal spaced substeps desired throughout
the analysis. This allowed ANSYS to produce results as a function of dpa. The results discussed here were
solved with 240 substeps up to 6 dpa to provide sufficient resolution in the initial transient swelling and
creep regimes of the SiC. It should be noted that thermal creep was assumed to be negligible in this analysis,
due to the relatively small target temperature of 300 °C compared with the melting temperature of the
materials used.

 5.5 Creep Compliance
5.5.1 Historical Creep Compliance Calculation Method
Previous instrumented creep experiments calculate creep compliance with a similar method employed for
passively stressed experiments that rely solely on PIE, where the deformation of a stressed specimen is
compared with the deformation of an unstressed specimen. More specifically, two identical capsules and
specimens are irradiated with one specimen at a target stress and the other unstressed, while LVDTs
measure displacement for both capsules. The LVDT measurements are converted to strain rates with respect
to fluence in dpa based on the specimen gauge length. The creep compliance is then calculated according
to Eq. (3) by subtracting the unstressed strain rate from the stressed strain rate, then dividing by the stress
of the stressed specimen [11, 12].

5.5.2 New Creep Compliance Calculation Method
A new creep compliance calculation method has been developed during this study to address the limitations
of the historical methods described above. The new method relies on the assumption that the specimen’s
irradiation creep strain is directly proportional to the applied stress, or, in other words, that the stress
exponent is unity, which has been verified for SiC in previous BSR testing and holds for most metallics [7].

The primary difference in the new calculation method is that the capsule of interest with the nominal
specimen stress would be compared with another capsule containing a specimen with a different cross-
section but an identical capsule pressure and load train force. As a result, the deformations of all components
besides the specimens themselves would be identical, with only the elastic and irradiation creep
deformations of the specimens varying due to their different stresses. To continue, taking the displacement
rate with respect to dpa for each capsule would eliminate the contribution of the specimens’ constant elastic
deformation, isolating the difference in the irradiation creep deformation between each specimen.

corrected for the LVDT drift throughout irradiation by subtracting the drift measured between each cycle
from the original data for the cycle as described in Section 5.2.2, assuming a linear drift rate. The known

5.5.3 Creep Compliance Uncertainty
The method described above addresses several major sources of error in the creep compliance calculation,
namely the load train creep and LVDT drift. However, several other sources of uncertainty remain which
will be discussed here. Figure 26 shows the uncertainty bands around the calculated creep compliance
discussed previously for the uncertainty sources considered here, with the total uncertainty also plotted as
a curve. Again, the cross-sectional area of the larger cross section specimen is taken as twice the area of the
smaller specimen, which is the practical geometrical limit for the capsule. The bands are calculated by
manipulating the ANSYS deformation data to reflect the effects of the uncertainty source for one capsule,
while leaving the second capsule displacements unchanged. The uncertainty sources discussed here are

42                                                                                         August 2025

likely even with a correctly operating experiment; however, other sources of error may arise if the
experiment does not operate correctly in situ.

 Figure 26. Comparison of the input and predicted SiC/SiC specimen irradiation creep compliance
 with uncertainty bands.

To begin, uncertainty in the stress applied to each specimen can arise from multiple sources such as friction
between the movable components of the load train and the capsule, uncertainty in the applied pressure, and
creep in the bellows leading to changes in its effective area. The combined uncertainty in the applied stress
to the specimen was taken as ±1% of the nominal specimen stress, which is reflected as a ±1% change in
the force transferred throughout the load train. This variance in load train force directly affects the elastic
and creep deformations of all components, which manifests in the creep compliance uncertainty primarily
in the steady-state stage after approximately 1 dpa, as shown in Figure 26. Prior to this, the larger creep of
the specimen reduces these effects.

Another source of uncertainty stems from temperature variance between the two capsules compared for the
creep compliance calculation. Because the calculation is based on displacement rates, the effects of constant
differences in displacement between the capsules due to thermal expansion or saturated swelling are
eliminated. However, the swelling rate and total swelling of the SiC specimen is strongly dependent on its
temperature [29]. As a result of the different swelling rates, differences in the specimen temperature
between the compared capsules will result in error in the compliance calculations. Based on previous
experience with HFIR irradiations, a temperature variance of ±20 °C was used in the creep compliance
uncertainty calculations. To quantify this uncertainty, the SiC swelling model used in the ANSYS modeling,
described in Section 3.2, was used to modify the specimen swelling deformations given by the ANSYS
model for one capsule in the compliance calculation. The uncertainty from the temperature variance
primarily affects the transient stage prior to 1 dpa, when the swelling of the specimen saturates, as shown
in Figure 26.

August 2025                                                                                                 43

The last source of uncertainty considered here stems from the LVDT drift discussed previously. While a
procedure has been developed to account for the drift in situ, there is still some uncertainty involved in this
process although it is an order of magnitude smaller than the uncertainty if the process is not used. The
remaining uncertainty results from the small amount of deformation in the LVDT components resulting
from thermally induced stress and swelling, which will cause a small error in the drift measurement when
at the reference position. This deformation is predicted to accumulate at a rate of approximately 0.5 µm/dpa
in the ANSYS model, so this value is used in the creep compliance uncertainty calculation. Figure 26 shows
that this uncertainty primarily affects the steady-state stage after approximately 1 dpa.

So far, the analysis presented here has taken the cross-sectional area of the larger cross section specimen as
twice the area of the smaller cross section specimen, which is the practical geometrical limit for the capsule.
However, the ratio of cross sections of the compared specimens, which can also be expressed as the ratio
of the specimen stresses, will affect the uncertainty in the creep compliance measurement. To explore this,
the ANSYS data set was used to determine the steady-state creep compliance uncertainty for several ratios
of specimen stress. The results are plotted in Figure 27.

 Figure 27. Plot of steady-state creep compliance uncertainty for different ratios of specimen stress
 used for compliance calculation.

The results demonstrate that the uncertainty in the creep compliance calculation is strongly dependent on
the cross-sectional areas of the two specimens used in the calculation. When considering the geometrical
constraints of the INSITE capsule presented here, the practical lower limit of the stress ratio is 0.5,
corresponding to one specimen’s cross-sectional area being twice that of the other specimen. As a result,
the INSITE experiment presented here can be expected to measure the creep compliance of a SiC specimen
with an uncertainty down to approximately ±2.5×10-7 dpa-1MPa-1 in the steady state stage.

 5.6 Cross Cutting Capabilities
In addition to the initial transient and steady state creep stages discussed here, INSITE shows promise for
measurement of slow crack growth and rupture following the steady state creep stage. Because the
displacement rate would be higher than that compared to steady state creep, the associated uncertainty in
creep measurements would be expected to decrease as rupture is approached. Additionally, instrumented,
in situ experiments are uniquely capable to capture this rupture behavior due to their real-time monitoring
capabilities. In contrast, it would be nearly impossible to capture this behavior with passive experiments
because the creep must be measured out of pile during PIE, which would not be possible with a ruptured
specimen.

The INSITE experiment presented here can quantify the irradiation creep compliance of nearly any material
of interest to the nuclear community. There is significant interest in the testing of irradiation creep of metals
at high temperatures, up to 700 °C, which has been shown to be possible with preliminary thermal modeling
in ANSYS. Specifically, the specimen temperature can be raised to over 700 °C by changing the capsule’s
internal gaps and the materials of the specimen filler and grips. Because heat is primarily rejected radially
from the capsule to the coolant in HFIR, this increase in specimen temperature only results in an increase
of approximately 20 °C in the temperature-sensitive LVDT windings, LVDT core, and bellows. As a result,
determining irradiation creep compliance of metals at temperatures up to 700 °C with INSITE seems to be
feasible. Furthermore, most other materials of interest, including metals, have irradiation creep compliances
that are expected to be an order of magnitude larger than that analyzed here, which would reduce the
associated uncertainty discussed here for SiC.

Further, the online measurement and control capabilities of temperature and load, while intended here for
creep testing, can be applied for other testing methods such as tensile, fatigue, and fracture toughness
testing. As such, the INSITE experiment presented here may function as a substitute for an out-of-pile load
frame that is typically used during PIE, allowing multiple types of possible mechanical testing while the
specimen is simultaneously undergoing irradiation.

 5.7 Potential Improvements
The INSITE experiment described here is expected to adequately measure SiC irradiation creep compliance
with uncertainty down to ±2.5×10-7 dpa-1MPa-1 in the steady state stage. However, there are several
challenges and limitations that are associated with the LVDT-bellows style capsule, which are discussed in
Section 5.1 and throughout the report. Some potential improvements for these are discussed below.

Many of these challenges and limitations are consequences of high LVDT temperatures, which are driven
primarily by relatively large internal gaps in the LVDT. Changes to the LVDT geometry to reduce these
gaps may be possible. Alternatively, it may be possible to fill some of these gaps with a thermally
conductive filler material to improve heat rejection. These potential modifications could be proposed to the
LVDT manufacturer, IFE, for consideration and possible implementation in the INSITE experiment. If
successful, the lower temperatures would allow higher temperatures within the experiment, allow greater
flexibility for LVDT placement within HFIR, reduce LVDT drift, and ease calibration of the LVDT.

An alternative experimental concept to measure deformation directly at the specimen would also eliminate
many of the challenges and limitations encountered in this study. For example, the change in diameter or
length of a pressurized tube or the deflection of a pressurized diaphragm can be measured to directly isolate
the strains in the stressed regions of the material of interest. A concept that involves directly measuring the
specimen also lends itself well to measurements made with fiber optics, which have numerous inherent
advantages over LVDTs including smaller size, improved displacement resolution, higher possible
operating temperatures, and the ability to simultaneously measure strain or temperature.

August 2025                                                                                                  45

One concept of particular interest involves measuring the deflection of a diaphragm loaded by either directly
pressurizing with a gas or by pressurizing a bellows pneumatically. A fiber optic-based Fabry-Perot cavity
would be established by placing a fiber a distance away from the inner surface of the diaphragm. A portion
of the light injected into the fiber would be reflected at the end of the fiber while the remainder would at
least partially reflect from the inner surface of the diaphragm and couple back into the fiber [42, 43]. Various
interferometric approaches can be used to demodulate changes in the cavity length, and therefore the
diaphragm displacement [44, 45]. This concept would directly measure deflection of the material of interest
with nanometer-scale resolution, potentially reducing the creep compliance uncertainty greatly. This
concept would also be much smaller than the LVDT-bellows based experiment and require fewer leads,
which would allow more specimens to be tested in a given facility in HFIR. The smaller size, together with
the expected improved temperature resistance compared with the LVDT-bellows concept, would allow this
concept to have more flexibility in its placement in HFIR, resulting in the potential for faster fluence
accumulation. There is precedent for operating fiber optic sensors in these same HFIR irradiation positions
at relevant dose and temperature conditions [46, 47]. Finally, this concept is much simpler with fewer
potential failure points. All these characteristics are consistent with the goals of the Advanced Fuels
Campaign to accelerate the process for developing and qualifying new fuel cladding materials.

6. CONCLUSIONS
Together, the passive and instrumented irradiation creep capsule concepts address complementary needs
for evaluating irradiation creep performance of SiC/SiC composites and have broader cross-cutting
applications for other fuel cladding materials. The PRECISE passive capsule offers a rapid, low-cost path
to screening material behavior under fixed conditions and gather valuable post-irradiation creep data, while
the INSITE experiment is aimed at high-fidelity, in-situ measurements that can capture the evolution of
creep compliance during irradiation. Both platforms are being developed with the goal of leveraging HFIR’s
high flux and damage rates to accelerate materials qualification and improve modeling fidelity for SiC/SiC
composite systems.

The passive irradiation creep experiment has been designed to evaluate the mechanical response of SiC/SiC
composites under high-flux irradiation in HFIR. The capsule applies tensile stress using a welded bellows
integrated into the capsule housing and externally pressurized by reactor coolant, which applies load
through a dedicated internal load train. The current design targets irradiation at 300 °C and 100 MPa, with
the flexibility to accommodate temperatures from 300 °C to over 700 °C and stress levels from 60 MPa to
300 MPa by adjusting internal configuration and specimen geometry. Finite element analyses have been
used to assess the target thermal and mechanical conditions, and out-of-pile tests are underway to verify
load transfer performance and structural containment. All activities supporting HFIR insertion, including
design verification, safety testing, capsule assembly, and preparation of required documentation, are
expected to be completed in fiscal year 2026.

The analysis presented here for the INSITE concept demonstrates the feasibility to provide real-time, in
situ measurements of SiC irradiation creep compliance with an uncertainty down to ±2.5×10-7 dpa-1MPa-1
during steady state creep. Further, INSITE shows promise for investigating slow crack growth and creep
rupture of SiC in-situ. Several sources of uncertainty have been identified and quantified, resulting in a new
creep compliance calculation method presented here to address the major sources. This work identified a
functional experiment geometry that is suitable to begin out-of-pile testing. The general conclusions from
the INSITE feasibility study are summarized as follows:

  A functional geometry has been generated that consists of six creep capsules, each capable of online
 displacement monitoring with an LVDT, specimen stress control with a pressure-actuated bellows,
 and temperature control with thermocouples and fill gas composition adjustment. These capabilities

46                                                                                         August 2025

 make the INSITE experiment cross-cutting for many in situ thermo-mechanical tests, such as
 tensile, fatigue, and fracture toughness testing, for many materials. This capsule geometry is
 suitable for future out-of-pile testing to evaluate capsule performance.

  The large internal gaps of the LVDT drive high temperatures of the LVDT internal components,
 such as the windings and core. The temperatures are within, but are approaching, the limits of the
 LVDT. These temperatures limit the placement of the LVDT within HFIR.

  Inclusion of creep and swelling strains in the structural FEA quantified the complexity in isolating
 the irradiation creep deformation from the deformation measured by the LVDT, which measures
 the difference in the sum of deformations of the inner and outer train of components connected to
 the LVDT core and body, respectively. For the INSITE capsule, the irradiation creep deformation
 of the specimen is shown to be just 2% of the total displacement measured by the LVDT.

  Irradiation creep in the load train measured by the LVDT is demonstrated to be 8.9 times larger
 than the specimen irradiation creep. Further, the method historically used to calculate creep
 compliance is not able to differentiate between the specimen’s irradiation creep deformation and
 other sources of time dependent deformation, such as the load train creep or LVDT drift. This is
 possibly the source of the large error reported in a previous instrumented creep experiment testing
 of SiC material.

  The large error in calculated creep compliance introduced by the load train creep necessitated the
 development of an improved creep compliance calculation method. In this new method, two
 capsules with specimens of different cross-sectional areas are compared after irradiation with an
 identical load train force applied. This produces identical load train creep between the capsules to
 allow the specimen irradiation creep compliance to be isolated directly using the LVDT
 displacement rates.

  A technique to account for the LVDT drift has also been conceptualized, which actuates the LVDT
 rod to a reference position by forcing a collar on the LVDT rod to contact the capsule end cap in-
 between cycles when temperatures have returned to ambient. Prior to calculating the displacement
 rate for the creep compliance calculation, this measured drift can be subtracted from the original
 data for the cycle, assuming a linear drift rate, to reduce the effects of the LVDT drift.
