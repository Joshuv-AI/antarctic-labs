On reversing the Simon–Lieb inequality in high-dimensional percolation
Romain Panis, Bruno Schapira

Abstract
We study Bernoulli percolation on ℤ d \mathbb{Z}^{d} in dimensions d > 6 {d>6} . We prove that a classical consequence of the van den Berg–Kesten inequality, often referred to as the Simon–Lieb inequality in the context of the Ising model, admits a partial reversal. As a main application, we show that the quantity φ p c ​ ( S ) \varphi_{p_{c}}(S) , introduced by Duminil-Copin and Tassion (Comm. Math. Phys., 2016), is uniformly bounded over all S ⊂ ℤ d S\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d} . This partial reversal further yields a short and self-contained route to several key results, including near-critical estimates on the two-point function and sharp bounds on the critical one-arm probability.

Correlation inequalities play a central role in the study of percolation models. They underpin much of the progress in the field, and the discovery of new ones may well prove decisive in resolving major open problems

The purpose of this work is to study a specific consequence of the BK inequality, commonly referred to as the

This result is of independent interest, but its full strength emerges in applications, where it provides a new structural tool for the analysis of the mean-field regime of Bernoulli percolation. We state the main results of the paper after introducing some notation and recalling relevant classical results.

We consider percolation on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}

by independently

(resp.

\bullet

\bullet

Spread-out model with

We often analyse the restriction of

It is classical (see

An important quantity in the analysis of Bernoulli percolation is the so-called

The FKG inequality
.

The FKG inequality states that, for every
p\in[0,1]
and every
increasing
events (i.e. events that are stable under the action of opening edges)
E
and
F

\mathbb{P}_{p}[E\cap F]\geq\mathbb{P}_{p}[E]\cdot\mathbb{P}_{p}[F].

(FKG)

The BK inequality
.

If
E
and
F
are two percolation events, we write

for the event of
disjoint

and
F

(resp.

(resp.
F
) occurs. The BK inequality states that, for every
p\in[0,1]
and every increasing events
E
and
F

\mathbb{P}_{p}[E\circ F]\leq\mathbb{P}_{p}[E]\cdot\mathbb{P}_{p}[F].

(BK)

1.1
A partially reversed Simon–Lieb inequality

The central object of this paper is the Simon–Lieb inequality, which provides a way to compare restricted two-point functions involving different sets.

Lemma 1.1

(Simon–Lieb inequality)
.

Let
d\geq 2
. Let
S\subset\Lambda\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and
p\in(0,1)
. For every
o\in S
and
x\in\Lambda
,

\tau_{\Lambda,p}(o,x)\leq\tau_{S,p}(o,x)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in S\\
v\notin S\\
u\sim v\end{subarray}}\tau_{S,p}(o,u)\cdot p\cdot\tau_{\Lambda,p}(v,x).

(1.4)

This result is an easy consequence of the BK inequality (see
[
20
,
21
]
). A related inequality (proved by exploration arguments) was derived by Hammersley
[
26
]
, see the discussion below. We include its proof in Section
2
for completeness.

Lemma
1.1
plays a central role in the seminal work of Duminil-Copin and Tassion
[
20
]
(see also
[
45
]
in the context of the Ising model). There, it is used to give an alternative proof of the
sharpness
of the phase transition
[
38
,
2
]
, which asserts that
p_{c}
(as defined above) also corresponds to the threshold beyond which exponential decay of
\tau_{p}(0,x)
stops holding. The Simon–Lieb inequality provides a
finite-size criterion
for exponential decay: if there exists a
finite

S\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
containing
0
such that

\varphi_{p}(S):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in S\\
v\notin S\\
u\sim v\end{subarray}}\tau_{S,p}(0,u)\cdot p<1,

(1.5)

then, iterations of Lemma
1.1
imply that exponential decay holds. Remarkably, such an argument can already be found in one of the earliest contributions to percolation theory, due to Hammersley
[
26
]
. Since the quantity
\varphi_{p}(S)
is continuous in
p
, this gives that the set of
p
for which exponential decay holds is an
open
subset of
[0,1]
. As a consequence, this inequality is not expected to hold for more general percolation models
1
1

1

For instance, it cannot hold for the free measure of the two-dimensional random-cluster model with cluster weight
q>4

[
16
]
, since exponential decay of correlations fails to be an open property in that setting.
. The quantity
\varphi_{p}(S)
is also interesting on its own, and we return to it below.

In view of the importance of Lemma
1.1
, it is natural to ask how sharp this inequality is. Our first result provides a partial answer.

Theorem 1.2

(Partially reversed Simon–Lieb inequality)
.

Let
d\geq 2
. Let
S\subset\Lambda\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and
p\in(0,1)
. For every
o\in S
and
x\in\Lambda
,

\tau_{\Lambda,p}(o,x)\geq\tau_{S,p}(o,x)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in S\\
v\notin S\\
u\sim v\end{subarray}}\mathbb{E}_{p}\Big[\mathds{1}\{o\xleftrightarrow{S\>}u\}\cdot p\cdot\tau_{\Lambda\setminus\mathcal{C}(u;\Lambda),p}(v,x)\Big],

(1.6)

where
\mathcal{C}(u;\Lambda)
denotes the cluster of
u
in
\Lambda
.

To the best of our knowledge, this result is new. We present its (short) proof in Section
2
.
The terminology “partially reversed” refers to the fact that
\tau_{\Lambda\setminus\mathcal{C}(u;\Lambda),p}
appears in place of
\tau_{\Lambda,p}
on the right-hand side of (
1.6
). To make this bound
effective
, one must understand the impact of restricting the two-point function to the complement of
\mathcal{C}(u;\Lambda)
. As it turns out, this can be achieved in dimensions
d>6
. The key intuition is that, in this regime, the cluster of
u
is typically not too large near
u
, so that
\tau_{\Lambda\setminus\mathcal{C}(u;\Lambda),p}(v,x)
is comparable to
\tau_{\Lambda,p}(v,x)
. Establishing this directly is generally restricted to the case
\Lambda=\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and
p=p_{c}
thanks to (

∗

‣
1
). Beyond this regime, however, suitably averaged versions of Theorem
1.2
allow one to exploit the same intuition. We provide more details in Section
1.5
.

The following result gives a more effective version of Theorem
1.2
in the case
\Lambda=\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and
p=p_{c}
.

Proposition 1.3
.

Let
d>6
and assume that (

∗

‣
1
) holds. Let
\varepsilon>0
. There exists
c=c(\varepsilon,d)>0
such that, for every
S\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
finite and every
o\in S
and
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
,

\tau_{p_{c}}(o,x)\geq c\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in S:\>|x-u|\geq\varepsilon|x|\\
v\notin S\\
u\sim v\end{subarray}}\tau_{S,p_{c}}(o,u)\cdot p_{c}\cdot\tau_{p_{c}}(v,x).

(1.7)

Proposition
1.3
may not be entirely surprising. Indeed, many correlation inequalities are expected to admit a reversal (at least at the critical point) in the mean-field regime. A more detailed discussion of this phenomenology can be found in
[
10
, Section 2]
.

1.2
Uniform boundedness of
\varphi_{p_{c}}(S)

Understanding
\tau_{S,p_{c}}
for a general set
S
is extremely challenging. The general belief is that in the mean-field regime one should have
\tau_{S,p_{c}}(0,x)\asymp\mathbb{G}_{S}(0,x)
, where
\mathbb{G}_{S}
is the Green function of the simple random walk killed upon exiting
S
. More precisely, letting
\mathbf{P}_{0}
be the law of the simple random walk on
(\mathbb{Z}^{d},\mathcal{E})
started at
0
, we define for
x\in S
,

\mathbb{G}_{S}(0,x)=\sum_{k\geq 0}\mathbf{P}_{0}[X_{k}=x,\>T_{S}>k],

(1.8)

where
T_{S}:=\inf\{k\geq 0:X_{k}\notin S\}
. By
[
47
]
and the discussion below
(

∗

‣
1
), this prediction is verified for

S=\mathbb{Z}^{d}
in certain high-dimensional settings. Under the assumption
that
d>6
and that (

∗

‣
1
) holds, it was recently
proved
[
42
]
that an analogous result holds for the half-space
two-point function: setting
\mathbb{H}=\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{Z}^{d-1}
,
one has
\tau_{\mathbb{H},p_{c}}\asymp\mathbb{G}_{\mathbb{H}}
(see also

[
11
,
12
]
). Beyond these two cases, no other complete results are available. Even the case
S=\Lambda_{n}
is not fully understood (see however
[
11
, Theorem 2]
). We refer to the discussion below
[
34
, Lemma 1.1]
for more information.

Nevertheless, one can study more averaged versions of these restricted two-point functions. A very natural candidate is the quantity
\varphi_{p_{c}}(S)
defined in (
1.5
). This quantity is comparable to the expected number of
pioneers
. Recalling that
\partial S=\{x\in S:\exists\,y\notin S,\,x\sim y\}
, we call a point
u\in\partial S
a
pioneer
if
0\xleftrightarrow{S\>}u
. We also define
\mathcal{P}_{S}:=|\{u\in\partial S:0\xleftrightarrow{S\>}u\}|
. One can check that, for
p\in(0,1)
,

(|\{x:0\sim x\}|p)^{-1}\varphi_{p}(S)\leq\mathbb{E}_{p}[\mathcal{P}_{S}]=\sum_{u\in\partial S}\tau_{S,p}(0,u)\leq p^{-1}\varphi_{p}(S).

(1.9)

In the case of the simple random walk, it is an easy consequence of Markov’s property that

\varphi^{\textup{RW}}(S):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in S\\
v\notin S\\
u\sim v\end{subarray}}\mathbb{G}_{S}(0,u)\mathbf{P}_{0}[X_{1}=v-u]=\mathbf{P}_{0}[T_{S}<\infty]\leq 1.

(1.10)

In particular, the
\varphi^{\textup{RW}}(S)
’s, where
S
runs over subsets of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
containing
0
, are uniformly bounded. Our main result extends this fact to high-dimensional Bernoulli percolation.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
d>6
and assume that (

∗

‣
1
) holds. There exists
C>0
such that, for every
S\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
containing
0
,

\varphi_{p_{c}}(S)\leq C.

(1.11)

Note that it is not even clear that
\varphi_{p_{c}}(S)<\infty
when
S
is
infinite. Prior to this work, the only results in this direction were obtained
for
S=\mathbb{H}_{n}:=\mathbb{H}-(n,0,\ldots,0)
in

[
33
,
17
]
(see
also
[
42
]
). Theorem
1.4
may come as a
surprise, as it extends the comparison to the random walk to settings
where the boundary of
S
is rough. Let us also mention that the result is

optimal
: we expect
\varphi_{p_{c}}(S)
to take arbitrarily large values
in dimensions
2\leq d\leq 6
. We explain how to derive Theorem
1.4

from Theorem

One of the main difficulties in the analysis of
\varphi_{p}(S)
lies in its lack of monotonicity in

. Nevertheless, Theorem

provides the following result.

Corollary 1.5
.

Let

and assume that (

∗

‣
1

such that, for every

, every

1.3
Near-critical bounds

Various natural length scales can be defined in the subcritical regime. We focus on the so-called
sharp length
which was initially introduced in
[
31
,
40
]
, and more recently studied in various contexts of statistical mechanics
[
17
,
18
,
19
,
22
,
23
]
. The sharp length
L(p)
is the first scale
k
at which we can find a set
S\subset\Lambda_{k}
containing
0
for which
\varphi_{p}(S)<\tfrac{1}{2}
. More precisely,

L(p):=\inf\Big\{k\geq 1:\exists S\subset\Lambda_{k},\>0\in S,\>\varphi_{p}(S)<\tfrac{1}{2}\Big\}.

(1.13)

It is not hard to see that
L(p)
diverges as
p
approaches
p_{c}
(see for instance
[
40
]
).
Our next result quantifies this divergence.

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
d>6
and assume that (

∗

‣
1
) holds. There exists
c,C>0
such that, for every
p\in[p_{c}/2,p_{c})
,

c(p_{c}-p)^{-1/2}\leq L(p)\leq C(p_{c}-p)^{-1/2}.

(1.14)

Combining Theorems
1.2
,
1.4
, and
1.6
, we obtain the following near-critical estimate on
\varphi_{p}(S)
for a general set
S
.

Theorem 1.7
.

Let
d>6
and assume that (

∗

‣
1
) holds. There exist
c,C>0
such that, for every
p<p_{c}
sufficiently close to
p_{c}
, and every
S\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
containing
0
,

c\exp\Big(-C(p_{c}-p)^{1/2}\mathrm{diam}(S)\Big)\leq\varphi_{p}(S)\leq C\exp\Big(-c(p_{c}-p)^{1/2}\mathrm{d}(0,\partial S)\Big),

(1.15)

with the convention that
\textup{diam}(S)=\infty
when
S
is infinite.

Theorem
1.7
is mostly useful in settings where
\textup{diam}(S)\asymp\mathrm{d}(0,\partial S)
, as is the case for
instance when
S=\Lambda_{n}
,
n\geq 1
. Let us mention that this result also implies a lower bound on
\varphi_{p}(\mathbb{H}_{n})
: indeed, by symmetry, one has
\varphi_{p}(\mathbb{H}_{n})\geq\tfrac{1}{2d}\cdot\varphi_{p}(\Lambda_{n})
.

Building on Theorem
1.7
, we also obtain the following near-critical bounds on the two-point function in the full-space.

Theorem 1.8
.

Let
d>6
and assume that (

∗

‣
1
) holds. There exist
c,C>0
such that, for every
p<p_{c}
sufficiently close to
p_{c}
, and every
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
,

\frac{c}{(1\vee|x|)^{d-2}}\exp\Big(-C(p_{c}-p)^{1/2}|x|\Big)\leq\tau_{p}(0,x)\leq\frac{C}{(1\vee|x|)^{d-2}}\exp\Big(-c(p_{c}-p)^{1/2}|x|\Big).

(1.16)

As mentioned above, there are other natural length-scales to consider in the subcritical regime. The most commonly studied one is the so-called
correlation length
, which measures the inverse of the exponential rate of decay of the two-point function. It is defined, for
p<p_{c}
and
u\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, by

\xi(\mathbf{u},p):=\left(-\frac{1}{n}\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\log\tau_{p}(0,\lfloor n\mathbf{u}\rfloor)\right)^{-1}.

(1.17)

Another natural distance is given by the
correlation length of order
\phi
where
\phi>0
: for
p<p_{c}
,

\xi_{\phi}(p)^{\phi}:=\frac{1}{\chi(p)}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}|x|^{\phi}\tau_{p}(0,x),

(1.18)

where
\chi(p):=\mathbb{E}_{p}[|\mathcal{C}(0)|]=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\tau_{p}(0,x)
is the expected size of the cluster of the origin
\mathcal{C}(0)
(also called the
susceptibility
).
Theorem
1.8
immediately implies the following result.

Corollary 1.9
.

Let
d>6
and assume that (

∗

‣
1
) holds. There exist
c,C>0
such that, for every
p<p_{c}
sufficiently close to

, for every
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
,

c(p_{c}-p)^{-1/2}\leq\xi(\mathbf{u},p)\leq C(p_{c}-p)^{-1/2},

(1.19)

and for every

c(p_{c}-p)^{-1/2}\leq\xi_{\phi}(p)\leq C(p_{c}-p)^{-1/2}.

(1.20)

1.4
The one-arm exponent

We now turn to our last application, beginning with some definitions. If

, we define the

\theta_{n}(p):=\mathbb{P}_{p}[0\xleftrightarrow{}\partial\Lambda_{n}].

It is expected that

decays algebraically in

[

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}p}\theta_{n}(p)=\frac{1}{p(1-p)}\mathbb{E}_{p}[\varphi_{p}(\mathcal{S}_{n})\mathds{1}_{0\in\mathcal{S}_{n}}],

. Combining (

yields the following result.

Corollary 1.10
.

Let

and assume that (

∗

‣
1
) holds. There exists

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}p}\theta_{n}(p)\leq C.

Combined with Theorem

yields a (very) short route to the computation of the one-arm exponent, thereby reproving a celebrated result of Kozma and Nachmias

Theorem 1.11
.

Let

and assume that (

∗

‣
1
) holds. There exist

such that, for every

\frac{c}{n^{2}}\leq\theta_{n}(p_{c})\leq\frac{C}{n^{2}}.

(1.24)

The argument of Kozma and Nachmias
[

relies on the analysis of

. We will introduce them more precisely in Section

below. Roughly, a point

is regular if its cluster is not too dense around it. The definition in
[

is a bit more involved, which makes their analysis quite intricate. As we glimpsed above, this notion is relevant to make Theorem

effective. We use a more geometric notion of regular points (introduced in
[

) to prove Theorem

. More details are provided in the next subsection.

Recently, two alternative proofs of Theorem

have appeared. To place our work in context, we briefly review these approaches. In
[
5
]
, the authors introduce a simpler notion of regular points, which simplifies part of the argument of Kozma and Nachmias. However, the overall strategy remains unchanged, and technical difficulties limit the approach to dimensions

. A different method is developed in

\theta_{n}(p_{c}-K/n^{2})\leq\frac{1}{10}\theta_{n/2}(p_{c}).

As we explain below (see Lemma

), this bound follows readily from the sharp length estimate
L(p)\asymp(p_{c}-p)^{-1/2}
. Consequently,
[

]
obtains (

[

]
. This is the case for sufficiently spread-out percolation in dimensions
{d>6}

[

]
, but not the case in the current more general setting (indeed
[
33
]
relies on
[

]
).
In contrast, our approach provides a short and essentially self-contained proof of the sharp length estimate, but also bypasses the use of the entropic bound.

1.5
Strategy of proofs

We now briefly discuss the main ideas of this paper. The novelties lie in the proofs of Theorems

,
1.4
,
1.6
, and in the lower bounds of Theorems

Let us first discuss how to obtain bounds on
\varphi_{p_{c}}(S)
for a finite

containing

\varphi_{p_{c}}(S)
. Indeed, by Lemma

, if

\tau_{p_{c}}(0,x)\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in S\\
v\notin S\\
u\sim v\end{subarray}}\tau_{S,p_{c}}(0,u)\cdot p_{c}\cdot\tau_{p_{c}}(v,x).

If
|x|\geq 2\cdot\textup{diam}(S)
, (

∗

‣
1
) gives that, for every

as above,
\tau_{p_{c}}(v,x)\asymp\tau_{p_{c}}(0,x)
, so that

\varphi_{p_{c}}(S)\gtrsim 1.

(1.27)

Let us stress, however, that obtaining this bound does not require (

∗

‣
1
): the bound
\varphi_{p_{c}}(S)\geq 1
holds in any dimension

, see
[
20
]
.

It is thus rather natural to try to obtain the inequality
\varphi_{p_{c}}(S)\lesssim 1
by deriving a “reversed” Simon–Lieb inequality. This is exactly what is performed in Proposition
1.3
. Using again (

∗

‣
1
), we see that Theorem
1.4
and Proposition
1.3
are equivalent. We will in fact obtain Proposition
1.3
as a corollary of Theorem
1.4
. Our purpose is to advertise that a good strategy to derive our main results involves trying to show that, in high dimensions, the application of the BK inequality to connectivity events is
sharp
(up to a multiplicative constant). Such an idea is also at the core of our recent paper
[
42
]
.

The strategy of reversing (
1.26
) to upper bound
\varphi_{p_{c}}(\mathbb{H}_{n})
, where we recall that
\mathbb{H}_{n}=\mathbb{H}-(n,0,\ldots,0)
, was pursued in
[
17
]
(see also
[
18
]
in the context of weakly self-avoiding walks). However, (
1.7
) is within a
multiplicative
constant from the upper bound of (
1.26
), while in
[
17
]
it is within an
additive
“error term”. Estimating the latter for a general

is extremely difficult as it involves a priori estimates on
\tau_{S,p_{c}}
(which are usually not available). Let us also mention that
[
33
]
obtains an upper bound on
\varphi_{p_{c}}(\mathbb{H}_{n})
by proving the existence of

such that, for every

\varphi_{p_{c}}(\mathbb{H}_{k+\ell+n_{0}})\geq c\cdot\varphi_{p_{c}}(\mathbb{H}_{k})\cdot\varphi_{p_{c}}(\mathbb{H}_{\ell}),

(1.28)

see
[

, Proposition 3.2]
.
Such an inequality can be viewed as an averaged reversed Simon–Lieb inequality. However, their analysis uses translation invariance and requires bounds on
\tau_{\mathbb{H},p_{c}}
, and hence cannot be used to bound
\varphi_{p_{c}}(S)
for a general set

.

We now discuss the main intuition behind the derivation of Theorems
1.2
and
1.4
. We start by looking at the counterpart of Lemma
1.1
in the context of random walks. Exploring the random walk until it exits
S
for the first time, and applying Markov’s (strong) property, yields, for every
x\notin S
,

\mathbb{G}(0,x)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in S\\
v\notin S\\
u\sim v\end{subarray}}\mathbb{G}_{S}(0,u)\cdot\mathbf{P}_{0}[X_{1}=v-u]\cdot\mathbb{G}(v,x).

(1.29)

In the setting of Bernoulli percolation, the situation is considerably more delicate. Two main obstacles prevent a direct adaptation of the random-walk argument:

(a)

Since we are considering a percolation model rather than a path model, the “first edge exiting
S
” is not necessarily well-defined.

(b)

Ignoring
(a)
and letting
\{u,v\}
be such that
0\xleftrightarrow{S\>}u
and
v\xleftrightarrow{}x
, we observe that the two connections are not independent. Indeed, it is possible that the connection between
v
and
x
visits the cluster that has been explored to reveal the connection between
0
and
u
in
S
. This difficulty arises from the non-Markovian nature of the cluster exploration.

We solve these problems as follows.

First, to mark a
special
edge on the boundary of
S
, we observe that we may create a connection from
0
to
x
by asking, for an edge
\{u,v\}
with
u\in S
and
v\notin S
, that the following event occurs:

\mathcal{E}_{uv}:=\{0\xleftrightarrow{S\>}u\}\cap\{\{u,v\}\textup{ is open}\}\cap\{v\xleftrightarrow{}x\}\cap\{0\xleftrightarrow{\omega^{[uv]}\>}x\}^{c},

(1.30)

where
\omega^{[uv]}
is the percolation configuration obtained from
\omega
by closing the edge
\{u,v\}
. In words, this event means that the edge
\{u,v\}
is open and pivotal for
\{0\xleftrightarrow{}x\}
,
and
that
0\xleftrightarrow{S\>}u
. We observe that a very similar event appears in the analysis of the susceptibility of the model
[
3
]
. As we prove below, the events
\mathcal{E}_{uv}
are incompatible, thus providing a unique way to mark an edge
\{u,v\}
on the boundary of
S
. At this stage, we may be losing a lot since this construction may not capture the true structure of a connection from
0
to
x
. Nevertheless, this is enough to get Theorem
1.2
, and we will see retrospectively that such a picture is true in the high-dimensional setting.

Second, to solve
(b)
, we rely on an extra ingredient due to Aizenman
[
1
]
:
in dimensions

, it holds (with high probability) that
|\mathcal{C}(u)\cap\Lambda_{n}(u)|\lesssim n^{4+o(1)}
for every

large enough (where

tends to

satisfying these bounds. Then, by (

∗

‣

\mathbb{E}_{p_{c}}\big[|\mathcal{C}(v)\cap A|\big]\lesssim\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{(2^{k})^{4+o(1)}}{2^{k(d-2)}}\lesssim 1,

(1.31)

where we used that

in the last inequality. This computation suggests (but does not prove) that, conditionally on its existence, the connection from

to

has a positive probability to occur in the complement of

. See Figure

for an illustration.

To prove Theorem
