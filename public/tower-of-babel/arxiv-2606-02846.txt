Cheeger Inequalities for the Persistent Laplacian
Magnus Bakke Botnan, Rui Dong

Abstract
We study Cheeger-type inequalities for persistent Laplacians associated with inclusions of simplicial complexes 𝒦 ↪ ℒ \mathcal{K}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{L} . We introduce a persistent up p p -Laplacian Δ q , p , up 𝒦 , ℒ \Delta_{q,p,\mathrm{up}}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{L}} for p ≥ 1 p\geq 1 . For p = 2 p=2 , this recovers the usual persistent up Laplacian, while for p = 1 p=1 it yields a nonzero persistent Cheeger constant φ q 𝒦 , ℒ \varphi_{q}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{L}} . We prove a Cheeger-type inequality relating φ q 𝒦 , ℒ \varphi_{q}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{L}} to the smallest nonzero eigenvalue of Δ q , up 𝒦 , ℒ \Delta_{q,\mathrm{up}}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{L}} . This gives a persistent extension of recent work by Jost and Zhang [ 25 ] . We then study two more structured settings. Under a locally complete q q -skeleton assumption on 𝒦 \mathcal{K} , we extend the complete-skeleton isoperimetric inequality of Parzanchevski–Rosenthal–Tessler [ 32 ] to the persistent setting. For orientable ( q + 1 ) (q+1) -dimensional pseudomanifolds, we prove a Kron-type reduction of the persistent up Laplacian to a vertex- and edge-weighted graph Laplacian, possibly with Dirichlet boundary terms, and obtain two-sided Cheeger inequalities; this is related to the dual-graph perspective in the work of Steenbergen–Klivans–Mukherjee [ 37 ] . We also describe the nonzero persistent Cheeger constant φ q 𝒦 , ℒ \varphi_{q}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{L}} explicitly in terms of the dual graph in the non-branching pseudomanifold case. Finally, for graph inclusions H ↪ G H\hookrightarrow G , we compare the persistent Cheeger constants introduced here with the Kron-reduction Cheeger constants of Mémoli et al. [ 30 ] .



1
Introduction

Cheeger inequalities play a fundamental role in graph theory, relating the combinatorial expansion properties of a graph to the smallest nonzero eigenvalue of its Laplacian. Several works have extended such inequalities to simplicial complexes, typically under additional assumptions such as orientability or completeness of the
q
-skeleton
[
25
,
18
,
32
,
37
]
. In this paper, we aim for a further generalization to the
persistent
Laplacian.

To recall the graph-theoretic starting point, let
G=(V,E)
be a finite graph. For
S\subseteq V
, write
S^{c}:=V\setminus S
, and let

E(S,S^{c}):=\{\,\{u,v\}\in E:u\in S,\ v\in S^{c}\,\}

be the set of edges crossing from
S
to its complement.

Definition 1.1

(
[
10
, Section 2.5]
)
.

The Cheeger constant
\varphi(G)
associated with
G
is defined by

\varphi(G)=\min_{0<|S|\leq|V|/2}\frac{|E(S,S^{c})|}{|S|}.

(1)

Thus
\varphi(G)
measures how efficiently one can separate the graph into two parts. This combinatorial quantity is intimately linked with the eigenvalues of the (unnormalized)
graph Laplacian

L=II^{T}
, where
I
is the
incidence matrix
defined by

I_{ij}=\begin{cases}-1,&\text{if edge }j\text{ leaves vertex }i,\\
1,&\text{if edge }j\text{ enters vertex }i,\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Theorem 1.1

(
[
10
, Section 2.5]
)
.

Let
G
be a connected graph and let
\lambda(G)
be the second smallest eigenvalue of
L
. Then

\frac{\varphi^{2}(G)}{2\max\limits_{v}\deg(v)}\leq\lambda(G)\leq 2\varphi(G).

(2)

Analogous bounds exist for higher eigenvalues in terms of how efficiently the graph can be partitioned into
k
pieces
[
28
]
. Cheeger inequalities are therefore a cornerstone of spectral graph theory; see, for example,
[
10
,
31
]
.

From graphs to complexes.

Extending Cheeger inequalities from graphs to simplicial complexes is subtle. In degree zero, a cut is simply a partition of vertices. In higher degree, the relevant objects are no longer only subsets of vertices: orientations, multiplicities, homology, and the interaction between adjacent simplices all enter into the Laplacian. Consequently, there is no unique higher-dimensional analogue of the graph Cheeger constant, and different definitions are appropriate under different assumptions. Recent work of Jost and Zhang
[
25
]
gives a systematic approach to this problem using the
1
-Laplacian and, more generally, nonlinear
p
-Laplacians. The
1
-Laplacian plays a distinguished role: while the ordinary Hodge Laplacian is an
\ell^{2}
-object, Cheeger constants are
\ell^{1}
-type quantities, and in the case
p=1
the corresponding Cheeger-type inequalities become equalities. This provides a bridge between the spectral theory of the
2
-Laplacian and the combinatorics of cuts. Based on this framework, Jost and Zhang obtain Cheeger-type inequalities for simplicial complexes, although the abstract nature of the resulting Cheeger constants makes their geometric interpretation difficult in full generality. We return to this setup in
Section

4
. For a broader overview of Cheeger inequalities for simplicial complexes, we refer the reader to the introduction of
[
25
]
.

Pairs of complexes.

In this paper, our focus is the persistent Laplacian
[
40
,
30
,
17
]
, a generalization of the classical Laplacian to pairs of spaces. It was introduced independently by Lieutier et al.
[
29
]
and Wang et al.
[
40
]
, and was later studied systematically by Mémoli et al.
[
30
]
, who gave algorithms for computing it via Gaussian elimination and Schur complements.

For an inclusion of simplicial complexes
\mathcal{K}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{L}
, the
q
-th persistent Laplacian
\Delta_{q}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{L}}
is defined as the sum of the persistent up Laplacian
\Delta_{q,\mathrm{up}}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{L}}
and the standard down Laplacian
\Delta_{q,\mathrm{down}}^{\mathcal{K}}
on
\mathcal{K}
. A key property is that Eckmann’s discrete Hodge theorem
[
15
]
extends to the persistent setting: the nullity of
\Delta_{q}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{L}}
is equal to the
q
-th persistent Betti number of the inclusion
\mathcal{K}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{L}
. Thus the barcode of persistent homology is encoded in the kernels of persistent Laplacians. Beyond the kernel, the spectra of persistent Laplacians contain finer geometric information about the underlying filtered space. The aim of this paper is to understand such spectral information through Cheeger-type inequalities.

The persistent Laplacian has become an active area of research, with recent developments in theoretical, applied, and algorithmic directions
[
12
,
42
,
3
,
23
]
. We refer the reader to
[
41
]
for a recent overview.

The goal of this paper is to bring Cheeger inequalities for simplicial complexes into the persistent setting. We focus on three existing approaches to Cheeger theory for simplicial complexes
[
25
,
32
,
37
]
and show that each admits a natural persistent extension. Similar extensions are likely to exist for other versions of Cheeger inequalities. We leave the development of a more unified framework for spectral inequalities of persistent Laplacians as an open direction in the discussion.

1.1
Outline and contributions

We begin in
Section

2
by recalling the graph-theoretic material needed later, including edge- and vertex-weighted graph Laplacians, incidence matrices, graph Cheeger constants, and Dirichlet boundary conditions. We also prove a Cheeger inequality for weighted graph Laplacians with Dirichlet boundary conditions. This result is used in the pseudomanifold setting, where the persistent Laplacian naturally reduces to a vertex- and edge-weighted graph Laplacian with possible boundary terms.

In
Section

3
, we recall the combinatorial Hodge Laplacian and the persistent Laplacian associated with an inclusion
\mathcal{K}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{L}
. We also recall the Schur-complement formula for the persistent up Laplacian and show that this operator may be viewed as a discrete Dirichlet-to-Neumann map, providing a novel interpretation of the persistent Laplacian, which plays an integral role in
Section

2
.

The main contributions of the paper are organized around four themes.

Theme 1: a general framework.

In
Section

4
, we extend the
1
-Laplacian approach to Cheeger inequalities of Jost–Zhang
[
25
]
to the persistent setting. We show that the persistent up Laplacian can be written as
BPB^{T}
, where
P
is an orthogonal projection. This projection formula allows us to define a persistent
p
-Laplacian and, for
p=1
, a nonzero persistent Cheeger constant
\varphi_{q}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{L}}
.

Result A
.

Let
\lambda_{\min}^{+}
denote the smallest nonzero eigenvalue of the persistent up Laplacian
\Delta_{q,\mathrm{up}}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{L}}

associated with an inclusion
\mathcal{K}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{L}
of simplicial complexes. Then

\frac{(\varphi_{q}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{L}})^{2}}{|S_{q+1}^{\mathcal{L}}|}\leq\lambda_{\min}^{+}\leq|S_{q}^{\mathcal{K}}|(\varphi_{q}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{L}})^{2},

where
|S^{\mathcal{L}}_{q+1}|
denotes the number of
(q+1)
-simplices in
\mathcal{L}
, and similarly for
|S_{q}^{\mathcal{K}}|
.
When
\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{L}
, this gives a Cheeger inequality for the ordinary up Laplacian; see
Corollary

4.1
.

Our arguments are similar to those in
[
25
]
although we work with a slightly different eigenvalue; the approach applies to both settings
Remark

4.1
.

The strength of this result is its generality: it holds without additional assumptions on the simplicial complexes. At this level of generality, however, the bounds retain some coarse features, since the constants involve the number of simplices in the complexes, and the Cheeger constant itself is not always easy to interpret geometrically. In
Section

6
, we show that in the pseudomanifold setting the same quantity admits a concrete dual-graph interpretation.

That said, it is natural to ask whether additional constraints on the simplicial complexes lead to Cheeger inequalities closer to those familiar from graph theory. This is the motivation behind the next two themes.

Theme 2: complete skeleta.

In
Section

5
, we extend the isoperimetric inequality of Parzanchevski–Rosenthal–Tessler
[
32
]
for complexes with complete skeleta to inclusions
\mathcal{K}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{L}
, under the assumption that each connected component of
\mathcal{K}
has a complete
q
-skeleton. The corresponding Cheeger constant is a persistent version of the complete-skeleton constant from
[
32
]
which is a generalization of the graph Cheeger constant:

h_{q}^{\mathcal{L}}=\min_{V=\coprod_{i=0}^{q+1}A_{i}}\frac{|V|\cdot|F(A_{0},\ldots,A_{q+1})|}{|A_{0}|\cdots|A_{q+1}|}.

Here
F(A_{0},\ldots,A_{q+1})
counts the number of
(q+1)
-simplices with vertices in different sets in the partition of the vertices
V
in
\mathcal{L}
. Our persistent Cheeger constant
h_{q}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{L}}
is rather involved in general, but it has a clear description when
\mathcal{K}
is connected; see
Eq.

10
.

Result B
.

Let
\mathcal{K}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{L}
be an inclusion of simplicial complexes such that each connected component of
\mathcal{K}
has a complete
q
-skeleton. Let

\lambda_{+}

be the smallest
non-trivial
eigenvalue of the persistent up Laplacian
\Delta_{q,\mathrm{up}}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{L}}
. Then,

\lambda_{+}\leq h_{q}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{L}};

see
Theorem

5.1
. When
\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{L}
, this recovers the non-persistent complete-skeleton upper Cheeger inequality.

Note that, in contrast to
\lambda_{\min}^{+}
,
\lambda_{+}
can be
0
, and this happens precisely when the induced map
H_{q}(\mathcal{K};\mathbb{R})\to H_{q}(\mathcal{L};\mathbb{R})
of homology vector spaces is non-trivial. If the induced map is trivial, the two eigenvalues coincide.

Theme 3: pseudomanifolds.

In
Section

6
, inspired by the dual-graph viewpoint in the work of Steenbergen–Klivans–Mukherjee
[
37
]
, we study persistent Laplacians over orientable pseudomanifolds. In this setting, the persistent up Laplacian admits a Kron-type reduction to an edge- and vertex-weighted graph Laplacian on the dual graph associated with the
(q+1)
-chains of
\mathcal{L}
relative to
\mathcal{K}
. This Kron reduction is significantly easier to interpret than the Kron reduction to a hypergraph Laplacian for (potentially unoriented) pseudomanifolds in
[
3
]
.

Importantly, our Kron reduction leads to two natural Cheeger constants:
h_{\mathrm{vol}}
in the boundaryless case, and
h_{\mathrm{vol}}^{\mathrm{Bdy}}
when boundary terms are present. The term
\Delta
is the maximal weighted degree (in the dual graph), normalized by the corresponding vertex weight.

Result C
.

Let
\mathcal{K}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{L}
be an inclusion of simplicial complexes, where
\mathcal{L}
is an orientable
(q+1)
-dimensional pseudomanifold. Let
\lambda_{\min}^{+}
denote the smallest nonzero eigenvalue of
the persistent up Laplacian
\Delta_{q,\mathrm{up}}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{L}}
.

1.

If
\mathcal{L}
is boundaryless, then

\frac{h_{\mathrm{vol}}^{2}}{2\Delta}\leq\lambda_{\min}^{+}\leq 2h_{\mathrm{vol}}.

2.

Otherwise,

\frac{(h^{\mathrm{Bdy}}_{\mathrm{vol}})^{2}}{2\Delta}\leq\lambda_{\min}^{+}\leq h^{\mathrm{Bdy}}_{\mathrm{vol}}.

See
Theorem

6.2
.

We also show that the orientability assumption is essential for the lower bound.

Theme 4: graphs.

In
Section

7
, we compare the different persistent Cheeger constants in the special case of graph inclusions. Mémoli et al. introduced persistent Cheeger constants for graphs using Kron reduction
[
30
, Definition 4.18]
; we compare these constants with the ones arising from the general persistent definitions above.

Result D
.

For an inclusion of graphs
H\hookrightarrow G
, we have

h_{\operatorname{Kron}}^{H,G}\leq h_{0}^{H,G},\qquad\varphi_{\operatorname{Kron}}^{H,G}\leq\varphi_{0}^{H,G};

see
Propositions

7.1
and
7.2
.

These inequalities are not equivalences in general. Under additional assumptions on the inclusion
H\hookrightarrow G
, namely that
H^{c}
is independent and every vertex of

has exactly one neighbor in

\varphi_{\operatorname{Kron}}^{H,G}\leq\varphi_{0}^{H,G}\leq 2\varphi_{\operatorname{Kron}}^{H,G};

see
Proposition

7.4
.

2
Spectral graph theory

Throughout this section, let
G=(V,E)
be a finite connected graph with vertex set
V=\{1,\ldots,m\}
. We allow symmetric edge weights
w_{ij}=w_{ji}\geq 0
for
i\neq j
, with
w_{ij}=0
whenever
i
and
j
are not adjacent.

2.1
Edge- and vertex-weighted graph Laplacians

Definition 2.1
.

The
edge-weighted graph Laplacian
of
G
is the matrix

L^{\mathrm{edge}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
with entries

L^{\mathrm{edge}}_{ij}=\begin{cases}-w_{ij},&i\neq j,\\[4.0pt]
\displaystyle\sum_{k\neq i}w_{ik},&i=j.\end{cases}

Equivalently,
L^{\mathrm{edge}}
is the unique symmetric matrix whose quadratic form is the Dirichlet energy

g^{T}L^{\mathrm{edge}}g=\sum_{i<j}w_{ij}(g_{i}-g_{j})^{2},\qquad g\in\mathbb{R}^{m}.

In particular, all row sums vanish:

\sum_{j=1}^{m}L^{\mathrm{edge}}_{ij}=0\qquad\text{for all }i.

In terms of the weighted incidence matrix
B=I\Omega^{1/2}
, where
w_{e}=w_{ij}
, this becomes

L^{\mathrm{ev}}=W^{-1/2}BB^{T}W^{-1/2}.

We now introduce vertex weights by conjugating
L^{\mathrm{edge}}
with a positive diagonal matrix.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
V_{1},\ldots,V_{m}>0
be
vertex weights
, and set

W:=\operatorname{diag}(V_{1},\ldots,V_{m}).

The
edge- and vertex-weighted graph Laplacian
associated with

(G,(w_{ij}),(V_{i}))
is the symmetric matrix

L^{\mathrm{ev}}:=W^{-1/2}L^{\mathrm{edge}}W^{-1/2}.

To interpret this definition, let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and write
g:=W^{-1/2}x
. Then

x^{T}L^{\mathrm{ev}}x=g^{T}L^{\mathrm{edge}}g=\sum_{i<j}w_{ij}(g_{i}-g_{j})^{2},\qquad x^{T}x=g^{T}Wg=\sum_{i=1}^{m}V_{i}g_{i}^{2}.

Thus the energy is still the usual edge-based Dirichlet energy, while the norm is weighted by the vertex measure. This differs from the standard normalized graph Laplacian, where the vertex weights are chosen to be the degrees; here the vertex weights are arbitrary positive numbers.

2.2
Dirichlet boundary conditions

Let

B\subseteq V
be a nonempty set of
boundary vertices
.
The complementary set
I:=V\setminus B

is called the set of
interior vertices
. In addition to
[
10
]
,
[
26
, Chamber 0]
is an excellent resource for graphs with Dirichlet boundary conditions.

Definition 2.3
.

A function
f\colon V\to\mathbb{R}
is said to satisfy a

Dirichlet boundary condition
on
B
if

f(i)=0\qquad\text{for all }i\in B.

Equivalently, in vector form, the components of
f
indexed by
B

are fixed to zero.

Given any (weighted or unweighted) Laplacian matrix
L
on
V
, the
Dirichlet
Laplacian
is obtained by restricting
L
to the interior vertices:

L_{II}\quad\in\mathbb{R}^{|I|\times|I|},

namely, the principal submatrix of
L
with both rows and columns indexed
by
I
.

Definition 2.4
.

The Dirichlet Laplacian associated with the boundary set
B
is
the matrix
L_{II}
.
It acts on functions
f
satisfying the Dirichlet condition

f|_{B}=0
via

(L_{II}f_{I})_{i}=(Lf)(i),\qquad i\in I.

The proof of the following proposition is straightforward. We refer the reader to
[
26
]
for a classification of matrices arising from various types of graph Laplacians.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
X
be an
m\times m
symmetric matrix with strictly positive diagonal entries and non-positive off-diagonal entries, and let
W
be a diagonal matrix with positive entries. Assume that all row sums of
X
are non-negative. Construct a weighted graph
G
on vertices
\{1,\dots,m\}
by assigning an edge between
i
and
j
of weight
w_{ij}:=-X_{ij}
whenever
i\neq j
and
X_{ij}<0
.

1.

If all row sums of
X
are equal to
0
, then
X
is the (edge-weighted) graph Laplacian
L_{G}
of
G
(i.e., the
0
-th up Laplacian).

2.

If all row sums of
X
are non-negative and at least one is strictly positive, then
X
is the graph Laplacian
L^{\mathrm{Dir}}_{G}
of the weighted graph
G
with Dirichlet boundary conditions at the vertices whose row sums are positive.

In both cases, the matrix
W^{-1/2}XW^{-1/2}
, is a
vertex and edge
weighted graph Laplacian (with boundary conditions), where the vertex weight at index
i
is
W_{ii}
; see
Definition

2.2
.

We see that
any symmetric matrix with positive diagonal entries and non-positive
off-diagonal entries can be realized as the Dirichlet Laplacian of a
weighted graph. Indeed, given such a matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
,
one may construct an extended weighted graph by adding a set of dummy
boundary vertices and assigning suitable edge weights so that every row sum
of the full Laplacian vanishes. The original matrix
A
is then obtained
precisely as the Dirichlet restriction of this Laplacian to the interior
vertices.

2.3
Cheeger bounds for edge- and vertex-weighted graphs

Let
X
be an
m\times m
symmetric matrix with strictly positive diagonal entries, non-positive off-diagonal entries, and non-negative row sums. Let
W=\operatorname{diag}(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{m})
be a diagonal matrix with
\mu_{i}>0
. From
Proposition

2.1
, we regard
X
as a weighted graph Laplacian, potentially with Dirichlet boundary conditions. Thus, for
i\neq j
, the (interior) edge weight between
i
and
j
is

w_{ij}:=-X_{ij},

whenever
X_{ij}<0
, and
w_{ij}=0
otherwise. The row-sum
b_{i}:=\sum_{j=1}^{m}X_{ij}
records the total boundary weight attached to the interior vertex
i
. Equivalently,

X_{ii}=\sum_{j\neq i}w_{ij}+b_{i}.

The edge- and vertex-weighted Dirichlet Laplacian associated with this data is

M:=W^{-1/2}XW^{-1/2}.

We now introduce the relevant Cheeger constants.

Definition 2.5
.

For
S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,m\}
, define

\operatorname{Vol}(S):=\sum_{i\in S}\mu_{i},\qquad\operatorname{Cut}(S):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in S\\
j\notin S\end{subarray}}w_{ij},\qquad\operatorname{Bdy}(S):=\sum_{i\in S}b_{i}.

If all row sums of
X
vanish, set

h_{\mathrm{vol}}:=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\varnothing\neq S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,m\}\\
\operatorname{Vol}(S)\leq V_{\mathrm{tot}}/2\end{subarray}}\frac{\operatorname{Cut}(S)}{\operatorname{Vol}(S)}.

If at least one row sum of
X
is positive, set

h_{\mathrm{vol}}^{\mathrm{Bdy}}:=\min_{\varnothing\neq S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,m\}}\frac{\operatorname{Cut}(S)+\operatorname{Bdy}(S)}{\operatorname{Vol}(S)}.

Remark 2.1
.

Geometrically, the constant
h_{\mathrm{vol}}
corresponds to the usual Cheeger problem for a graph without Dirichlet boundary: it measures how efficiently one can separate the vertex set
S
from its complement using only
interior
edges. In contrast,
h_{\mathrm{vol}}^{\mathrm{Bdy}}
is the appropriate quantity when the graph has Dirichlet boundary contributions, equivalently when at least one row sum of
X
is positive. In that case,
S
can be separated not only along edges connecting it to its complement, but also along the boundary encoded by the row-sum surplus. Hence, different Cheeger constants are appropriate in the two cases.

We now state a two-sided Cheeger inequality for edge- and vertex-weighted graphs, possibly with Dirichlet boundary conditions. Many related results appear in the literature, including Cheeger inequalities for graphs with Dirichlet boundary conditions
[
10
]
. However, we were unable to find a statement in precisely the form needed here. Since this estimate is used in the proof of the pseudomanifold result below, we include the details in
Section

C.1
.

Let
V_{\mathrm{tot}}:=\operatorname{Vol}(\{1,\ldots,m\})
, and set

\Delta:=\max_{1\leq i\leq m}\frac{X_{ii}}{\mu_{i}},

which is the maximal weighted degree, normalized by the vertex weight.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
X,W,M,\Delta
be as above, and assume that the interior graph associated with
X
is connected.

1.

(Boundaryless) If all row sums of
X
are equal to
0
, then

\frac{h_{\mathrm{vol}}^{2}}{2\Delta}\leq\lambda_{1}(M)\leq 2h_{\mathrm{vol}},

where
\lambda_{1}(M)
denotes the smallest nonzero eigenvalue of
M
.

2.

(Dirichlet) If at least one row sum of
X
is positive, then
M
is positive definite and

\frac{(h_{\mathrm{vol}}^{\mathrm{Bdy}})^{2}}{2\Delta}\leq\lambda_{1}(M)\leq h_{\mathrm{vol}}^{\mathrm{Bdy}},

where
\lambda_{1}(M)
denotes the smallest eigenvalue of
M
.

3
Combinatorial Laplacians

The graph Laplacian is the degree-zero instance of the Hodge Laplacian on a simplicial complex. Precisely, for
\mathcal{L}
a simplicial complex, we denote by
C_{q}^{\mathcal{L}}
the
q
-chains of
\mathcal{L}
over real numbers
\mathbb{R}
,and equip each
C_{q}^{\mathcal{L}}
an inner product structure. While the definitions carry through for arbitrary inner products, we shall focus on the unweighted case, i.e., when
\langle\sigma_{i},\sigma_{j}\rangle=\delta_{ij}
for
q
-simplices
\sigma_{i}
and
\sigma_{j}
.

The
combinatorial Hodge
q
-Laplacian
is defined by

\Delta_{q}^{\mathcal{L}}=\underbrace{\partial_{q+1}^{\mathcal{L}}(\partial_{q+1}^{\mathcal{L}})^{*}}_{\text{up}}+\underbrace{(\partial_{q}^{\mathcal{L}})^{*}\partial_{q}^{\mathcal{L}}}_{\text{down}}.

We call the first part the
up Laplacian
and the second part the
down Laplacian
, and denote them by
\Delta_{q,\text{up}}^{\mathcal{L}}
and
\Delta_{q,\text{down}}^{\mathcal{L}}
respectively. These operators are often called Eckmann Laplacians, after Eckmann’s discrete Hodge theorem
[
15
]
, and were studied extensively for different choices of inner products in
[
19
]
. Their kernels encode homology, while their nonzero eigenvalues reflect finer quantitative information about the way
q
-simplices are connected through
(q+1)
-simplices.

Decomposing the spectrum

For a self-adjoint operator
A
on a finite-dimensional inner product space, we write
\spec(A)
for the multiset of eigenvalues of
A
, counted with algebraic multiplicity. We write
\spec_{\neq 0}(A)

for the multiset obtained by removing the zero eigenvalues.

It is well known (see e.g., the introduction in
[
19
]
) that the nonzero spectra of
\Delta_{q}^{\mathcal{L}}
,
\Delta_{q,\mathrm{up}}^{\mathcal{L}}
, and
\Delta_{q,\mathrm{down}}^{\mathcal{L}}
are positive and satisfy

\spec_{\neq 0}(\Delta_{q}^{\mathcal{L}})=\spec_{\neq 0}(\Delta_{q,\mathrm{up}}^{\mathcal{L}})\sqcup\spec_{\neq 0}(\Delta_{q,\mathrm{down}}^{\mathcal{L}}),

where
\sqcup
denotes disjoint union of multisets. Moreover,

\spec_{\neq 0}(\Delta_{q,\mathrm{up}}^{\mathcal{L}})=\spec_{\neq 0}(\Delta_{q+1,\mathrm{down}}^{\mathcal{L}}).

(This is also a direct consequence of
Proposition

A.1
.)

Therefore, it is enough to study one of the up or down families. In particular, much of the earlier work focuses on bounding the nonzero eigenvalues of the up Laplacian
\Delta_{q,\mathrm{up}}^{\mathcal{L}}
.

3.1
The persistent Laplacian

The persistent Laplacian was first independently introduced by Lieutier et al. in
[
29
]
and by Wang et al. in
[
40
]
,
and then systematically studied by Mémoli et al. in
[
30
]
.
We recall the persistent Laplacian with respect to a pair of simplicial complexes
\mathcal{K}\subset\mathcal{L}
.
We refer to
[
40
,
30
]
for more details.
Let
C_{q}^{\mathcal{K}}
(resp.
C_{q}^{\mathcal{L}}
) be the
q
-chain space with coefficients in
\mathbb{R}
of
\mathcal{K}
(resp.
\mathcal{L}
),
and

3.3
Energy-minimizing extensions and Dirichlet-to-Neumann

In this section, we explain how the persistent up Laplacian arises from a Dirichlet-to-Neumann construction and how its Schur-complement form encodes an energy-minimizing extension principle. The boundary/interior notation
\mathcal{B},\mathcal{I}
is the one introduced in
Section

3.2
; it is analogous to the graph notation used in
Section

2.2
.

Lemma 3.1
.

For every
u_{\mathcal{B}}\in C_{q}^{\mathcal{K}}
, one has

u_{\mathcal{B}}^{T}\Delta_{q,\mathrm{up}}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{L}}u_{\mathcal{B}}=\inf_{u_{\mathcal{I}}}\begin{bmatrix}u_{\mathcal{B}}\\
u_{\mathcal{I}}\end{bmatrix}^{T}\Delta_{q,\mathrm{up}}^{\mathcal{L}}\begin{bmatrix}u_{\mathcal{B}}\\
u_{\mathcal{I}}\end{bmatrix},

where the infimum is taken over all interior
q
-chain values indexed by
\mathcal{I}
.

Proof.

This is the quadratic-minimization characterization of the Schur complement, applied to the block decomposition in
Section

3.2
; see
[
4
, Appendix A.5.5]
.
∎

Spelled out using the adjoint property,

\begin{bmatrix}u_{\mathcal{B}}\\
u_{\mathcal{I}}\end{bmatrix}^{T}\Delta_{q,\mathrm{up}}^{\mathcal{L}}\begin{bmatrix}u_{\mathcal{B}}\\
u_{\mathcal{I}}\end{bmatrix}=\left\|(\partial_{q+1}^{\mathcal{L}})^{*}\begin{bmatrix}u_{\mathcal{B}}\\
u_{\mathcal{I}}\end{bmatrix}\right\|_{2}^{2},

which measures how the
q
-chain values vary across
(q+1)
-simplices, with orientation taken into account. Hence, once the boundary values are prescribed, Schur-complement elimination determines the energy-minimizing extension to
\mathcal{L}
(see
[
4
, Appendix A.5.5]
):

u_{\mathcal{I}}^{\min}=-L_{\mathcal{I}\mathcal{I}}^{\dagger}L_{\mathcal{I}\mathcal{B}}u_{\mathcal{B}}+r,\qquad r\in\ker L_{\mathcal{I}\mathcal{I}},

and the quadratic form of the persistent up Laplacian gives the corresponding minimal energy. For a graph, this reduces to extending function values from boundary vertices to interior vertices so that the total variation along edges is minimized. The lemma above extends this same principle to simplicial complexes. More generally, the same argument applies to any symmetric positive semidefinite operator on a finite-dimensional vector space.

3.3.1
Dirichlet-to-Neumann

This subsection is not needed for the main arguments of the paper and may be skipped. It is included because the Dirichlet-to-Neumann perspective on persistent Hodge theory may be of independent interest.

The
Dirichlet-to-Neumann map
on
q
-chains is defined by

\Lambda:C_{q}^{\mathcal{K}}\longrightarrow C_{q}^{\mathcal{K}},\qquad u_{\mathcal{B}}\longmapsto\left(\Delta_{q,\mathrm{up}}^{\mathcal{L}}u\right)\big|_{\mathcal{B}},

where
u\in C_{q}^{\mathcal{L}}
solves

\Delta_{q,\mathrm{up}}^{\mathcal{L}}u(\sigma)=0\quad\text{for }\sigma\in\mathcal{I},\qquad u(\sigma)=u_{\mathcal{B}}(\sigma)\quad\text{for }\sigma\in\mathcal{B}.

Thus the interior
q
-chain values are
harmonic
, i.e. trivial under the up Laplacian, while the boundary values are prescribed. With respect to the block decomposition from
Section

3.2
, the Dirichlet problem is equivalently described by

\begin{bmatrix}L_{\mathcal{B}\mathcal{B}}&L_{\mathcal{B}\mathcal{I}}\\
L_{\mathcal{I}\mathcal{B}}&L_{\mathcal{I}\mathcal{I}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}u_{\mathcal{B}}\\
u_{\mathcal{I}}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}z\\
0\end{bmatrix},

(4)

where
z\in C_{q}^{\mathcal{K}}
is the resulting boundary flux, so
\Lambda u_{\mathcal{B}}=z
. From
Lemma

3.1
and the ensuing discussion,

\inf_{u_{\mathcal{I}}}\begin{bmatrix}u_{\mathcal{B}}\\
u_{\mathcal{I}}\end{bmatrix}^{T}\Delta_{q,\mathrm{up}}^{\mathcal{L}}\begin{bmatrix}u_{\mathcal{B}}\\
u_{\mathcal{I}}\end{bmatrix}=u_{\mathcal{B}}^{T}\Delta_{q,\mathrm{up}}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{L}}u_{\mathcal{B}},

with minimizing interior values

u_{\mathcal{I}}^{\min}=-L_{\mathcal{I}\mathcal{I}}^{\dagger}L_{\mathcal{I}\mathcal{B}}u_{\mathcal{B}}+r,\qquad r\in\ker L_{\mathcal{I}\mathcal{I}}.

This minimizing extension is harmonic on the interior. Indeed, since
\Delta_{q,\mathrm{up}}^{\mathcal{L}}
is positive semidefinite,
[
2
, Theorem 1]
gives
\ker L_{\mathcal{I}\mathcal{I}}\subseteq\ker L_{\mathcal{B}\mathcal{I}}
. If
h\in\ker L_{\mathcal{I}\mathcal{I}}
, then
L_{\mathcal{B}\mathcal{I}}h=0
, and hence

h^{T}L_{\mathcal{I}\mathcal{B}}u_{\mathcal{B}}=u_{\mathcal{B}}^{T}L_{\mathcal{B}\mathcal{I}}h=0.

Thus
L_{\mathcal{I}\mathcal{B}}u_{\mathcal{B}}\in(\ker L_{\mathcal{I}\mathcal{I}})^{\perp}=\operatorname{im}L_{\mathcal{I}\mathcal{I}}
, and therefore

L_{\mathcal{I}\mathcal{I}}L_{\mathcal{I}\mathcal{I}}^{\dagger}L_{\mathcal{I}\mathcal{B}}u_{\mathcal{B}}=L_{\mathcal{I}\mathcal{B}}u_{\mathcal{B}}.

It follows that

L_{\mathcal{I}\mathcal{B}}u_{\mathcal{B}}+L_{\mathcal{I}\mathcal{I}}u_{\mathcal{I}}^{\min}=L_{\mathcal{I}\mathcal{B}}u_{\mathcal{B}}-L_{\mathcal{I}\mathcal{I}}L_{\mathcal{I}\mathcal{I}}^{\dagger}L_{\mathcal{I}\mathcal{B}}u_{\mathcal{B}}+L_{\mathcal{I}\mathcal{I}}r=0.

Multiplying out the boundary row gives

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle=L_{\mathcal{B}\mathcal{B}}u_{\mathcal{B}}+L_{\mathcal{B}\mathcal{I}}u_{\mathcal{I}}^{\min}

\displaystyle=L_{\mathcal{B}\mathcal{B}}u_{\mathcal{B}}-L_{\mathcal{B}\mathcal{I}}L_{\mathcal{I}\mathcal{I}}^{\dagger}L_{\mathcal{I}\mathcal{B}}u_{\mathcal{B}}+L_{\mathcal{B}\mathcal{I}}r

\displaystyle=\left(L_{\mathcal{B}\mathcal{B}}-L_{\mathcal{B}\mathcal{I}}L_{\mathcal{I}\mathcal{I}}^{\dagger}L_{\mathcal{I}\mathcal{B}}\right)u_{\mathcal{B}}=\Delta_{q,\mathrm{up}}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{L}}u_{\mathcal{B}},

where
L_{\mathcal{B}\mathcal{I}}r=0
follows from
r\in\ker L_{\mathcal{I}\mathcal{I}}
and
\ker L_{\mathcal{I}\mathcal{I}}\subseteq\ker L_{\mathcal{B}\mathcal{I}}
. We conclude the following.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
\mathcal{K}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{L}
be an inclusion of simplicial complexes. Then the
q
-th persistent up Laplacian coincides with the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map; that is,

\Lambda=\Delta_{q,\mathrm{up}}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{L}}.

Dirichlet-to-Neumann maps and their eigenvalue problems have also been studied for graphs; see
[
20

36

. The study of persistent Laplacians on simplicial complexes is thus a natural generalization of that line of work.

Remark 3.1

In the continuous setting, where

The associated eigenvalue problem is the

[

for a historical survey. In physical terms, if

4
A Cheeger inequality for the persistent Laplacian

In this section, we prove a Cheeger-type inequality for the persistent up Laplacian associated with an inclusion

\mathcal{K}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{L}
of simplicial complexes. The result is essentially an extension of
[
25
, Proposition 2.1]
. One difference is that
[
25
]
focuses on the first non-trivial eigenvalue, whereas we restrict our attention to the first nonzero eigenvalue. Nevertheless, a Cheeger-type inequality for the persistent Laplacian in that setting can be obtained by essentially the same argument; see
Remark

4.1
.

The key observation is that one can give a well-defined definition of the
p
-persistent Laplacian in the persistent setting. We then introduce a persistent Cheeger constant and prove
Proposition

4.1
.

We believe that the introduction of a
p
-Laplacian in the persistent setting is of independent interest and will be considered in subsequent work.

4.1
A persistent
p
-Laplacian

Following
[
25
]
, the
q
-th up
p
-Laplacian on
\mathcal{L}
, defined on chains, is

\Delta_{q,\text{up}}^{\mathcal{L}}\coloneqq B_{q+1}^{\mathcal{L}}\circ\alpha_{p}\circ B_{q+1}^{T},

where

\alpha_{p}:(t_{1},t_{2},\ldots)\mapsto\begin{cases}\bigl(|t_{1}|^{p-2}t_{1},|t_{2}|^{p-2}t_{2},\ldots\bigr),&p>1,\\[5.69054pt]
\{(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\ldots):\xi_{i}\in\,\operatorname{Sgn}(t_{i})\ \text{for all }i\},&p=1,\end{cases}

and
\,\operatorname{Sgn}(\cdot)
is defined by

\,\operatorname{Sgn}(t)=\begin{cases}\{1\},&t>0,\\
[-1,1],&t=0,\\
\{-1\},&t<0.\end{cases}

This definition is given with respect to the standard bases of
q
- and
(q+1)
-simplices. Since the
p
-Laplacian is nonlinear, its action is not invariant under an arbitrary change of basis. Moreover, the persistent Laplacian is closely tied to the choice of a basis of chains, namely the columns of
Z
. Thus, one might expect that any definition of a persistent
p
-Laplacian depends on the computation of
Z
. We now show that this is not the case.

Let
S_{q}^{\mathcal{K}}
and
S_{q}^{\mathcal{L}}
be the ordered sets of
q
-simplices in
\mathcal{K}
and
\mathcal{L}
, respectively. We assume that the ordering of the
q
-simplices

S_{q}^{\mathcal{L}}=\{[\sigma_{i}]\}_{i=1}^{n_{q}^{\mathcal{L}}}

is chosen so that

S_{q}^{\mathcal{K}}=\{[\sigma_{i}]\}_{i=1}^{n_{q}^{\mathcal{K}}}.

When
n_{q}^{\mathcal{K}}<n_{q}^{\mathcal{L}}
, we can write the matrix
B_{q+1}^{\mathcal{L}}
in block form as

B_{q+1}^{\mathcal{L}}=\begin{bmatrix}B\\
D\end{bmatrix},

where the row indices of
B
and
D
correspond to
S_{q}^{\mathcal{K}}
and

S_{q}^{\mathcal{L}}\setminus S_{q}^{\mathcal{K}}
, respectively.

When
\dim(C_{q+1}^{\mathcal{L},\mathcal{K}})>0
,
[
30
, Lemma 3.4]
gives

B_{q+1}^{\mathcal{L},\mathcal{K}}=BZ.

Therefore, the persistent up Laplacian can be written as

\Delta_{q,\mathrm{up}}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{L}}=B_{q+1}^{\mathcal{L},\mathcal{K}}(Z^{T}Z)^{-1}(B_{q+1}^{\mathcal{L},\mathcal{K}})^{T}=BZ(Z^{T}Z)^{-1}Z^{T}B^{T}.

Let

P=Z(Z^{T}Z)^{-1}Z^{T}.

Then
P
is a linear transformation
P\colon C_{q+1}^{\mathcal{L}}\to C_{q+1}^{\mathcal{L}}
satisfying

P=P^{T}=P^{*}\qquad\qquad\text{and}\qquad\qquad P=P^{2}.

Hence

\Delta_{q,\mathrm{up}}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{L}}=BPB^{T}=(BP)(PB^{T}).

(5)

Moreover,
P
is the orthogonal projection from
C_{q+1}^{\mathcal{L}}
onto the subspace

C_{q+1}^{\mathcal{L},\mathcal{K}}
. It is therefore independent of the choice of basis for

C_{q+1}^{\mathcal{L},\mathcal{K}}
, and we thus have obtained a basis-free definition of the persistent Laplacian. With respect to the standard basis of
q
-simplices we get the following definition.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
\mathcal{K}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{L}
be an inclusion of simplicial complexes. The

q
-th up persistent
p
-Laplacian associated with this inclusion is
defined for
x\in C_{q}^{\mathcal{K}}
by

\Delta_{q,p,\mathrm{up}}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{L}}(x)=BP\alpha_{p}(PB^{T}x).

As in the case of the
2
-Laplacian, one can use Rayleigh quotients (
Theorem

A.1
) to
define variational eigenvalues. Concretely, set

\lambda_{i}\bigl(\Delta_{q,p,\mathrm{up}}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{L}}\bigr)=\inf_{\gamma(S)\geq i}\sup_{x\in S}\frac{\|PB^{T}x\|_{p}^{p}}{\|x\|_{p}^{p}},\qquad i=1,2,\ldots,|S_{q}^{\mathcal{K}}|.

(6)

Here

\gamma(S)=\begin{cases}\min\left\{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}:\text{there exists an odd continuous map }\varphi:S\to\mathbb{S}^{k-1}\right\},&S\neq\varnothing,\\[5.69054pt]
0,&S=\varnothing,\end{cases}

denotes the Krasnoselskii genus of a centrally symmetric set

S\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
.

For
p=2
, the variational eigenvalues defined in

Eq.

6
are precisely the eigenvalues of the persistent up
Laplacian. For
p\neq 2
, these variational eigenvalues form only a subset
of the eigenvalues satisfying the generalized eigenvalue problem

\Delta_{q,p,\mathrm{up}}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{L}}(x)=\lambda\alpha_{p}(x),\qquad p>1.

(For
p=1
, the eigenvalue problem involves the Clarke subdifferential
[
11
]
; see
[
25
]
.)
Determining whether the total number of such generalized eigenvalues is
finite remains an open problem in graph theory; see, for example,

[
13
]
for a recent overview.

4.2
A persistent Cheeger constant

For the purposes of this paper, we focus on the
p
-Laplacian only in the cases
p=1
and
p=2
. Our goal is to define the persistent Cheeger constant in terms of the smallest non-zero variational eigenvalue for
p=1
. This definition then leads naturally to a Cheeger-type inequality. Moreover, in the case of graphs, the resulting Cheeger constant agrees with the Cheeger constant appearing in
Definition

.

First, observe that

\ker\bigl(\Delta_{q,p,\mathrm{up}}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{L}}\bigr)=\ker(PB^{T}).

Since
PB^{T}
vanishes on
\ker(PB^{T})
, and since

\gamma(\ker(PB^{T})\cap S_{p})=\dim\ker(PB^{T}),\qquad S_{p}:=\{x:\|x\|_{p}=1\},

we have

\lambda_{i}\bigl(\Delta_{q,p,\mathrm{up}}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{L}}\bigr)=0\qquad\text{for all }i\leq\dim\ker(PB^{T}).

In fact, the smallest non-zero variational eigenvalue is

\lambda_{\dim\ker(PB^{T})+1}\bigl(\Delta_{q,p,\mathrm{up}}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{L}}\bigr).

This result is well-known; see, for instance
[
38
, Section 2]
.

The next result shows that, in the case
p=1
, this first non-zero
variational eigenvalue admits an equivalent formulation in terms of

\ell^{1}
-minimal representatives.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let

i=\dim\ker(PB^{T})+1.

Then

\lambda_{i}\bigl(\Delta_{q,1,\mathrm{up}}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{L}}\bigr)=\inf_{\gamma(S)\geq i}\sup_{x\in S}\frac{\|PB^{T}x\|_{1}}{\|x\|_{1}}=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\neq 0\\
x\perp^{1}\ker(PB^{T})\end{subarray}}\frac{\|PB^{T}x\|_{1}}{\|x\|_{1}}.

Here
x\perp^{1}Y
means that

\|x+y\|_{1}\geq\|x\|_{1}\qquad\text{for every }y\in Y.

Equivalently,
x
is an
\ell^{1}
-minimal representative of the affine
space
x+Y
.

Proof.

Set

T:=PB^{T},\qquad K:=\ker T.

We apply
[
24
, Theorem 2.1]
with

F(x)=\|Tx\|_{1},\qquad G(x)=\|x\|_{1},\qquad\Pi=K.

The functions
F
and
G
are even, nonnegative, and
1
-homogeneous.
Moreover,

F(x+y)=F(x)\qquad\text{for every }y\in K,

and

F(x)=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad Tx=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x\in K.

Thus the zero space appearing in the min–max theorem is precisely

\Pi=K
. Hence
[
24
, Theorem 2.1]
gives

\inf_{\gamma(S)\geq\dim K+1}\sup_{x\in S}\frac{\|Tx\|_{1}}{\|x\|_{1}}=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\neq 0\\
\partial\|x\|_{1}\cap K^{\perp}\neq\varnothing\end{subarray}}\frac{\|Tx\|_{1}}{\|x\|_{1}}.

It remains to identify the subdifferential condition with the stated

\ell^{1}
-orthogonality condition. By

[
24
, Proposition 2.3]
, the condition

\partial\|x\|_{1}\cap K^{\perp}\neq\varnothing

is equivalent to
x
being an
\ell^{1}
-minimal representative of its

K
, that is,

\|x+y\|_{1}\geq\|x\|_{1}\qquad\text{for every }y\in K.

This is precisely the condition
x\perp^{1}K
. Since
T=PB^{T}
, the claim
follows.
∎

This motivates the following definition.

Definition 4.2
.

Let
\mathcal{K}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{L}
be an inclusion of simplicial complexes. The

\varphi_{q}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{L}}=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\neq 0\\
x\perp^{1}\ker(PB^{T})\end{subarray}}\frac{\|PB^{T}x\|_{1}}{\|x\|_{1}}.

When
\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{L}
, we have

(the identity matrix), and therefore

\varphi_{q}^{\mathcal{L}}=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\neq 0\\
x\perp^{1}\ker\left((B_{q+1}^{\mathcal{L}})^{T}\right)\end{subarray}}\frac{\|(B_{q+1}^{\mathcal{L}})^{T}x\|_{1}}{\|x\|_{1}}.

(7)

In the case of graphs, this expression agrees with the graph Cheeger constant
appearing in

Below, we study this Cheeger constant for oriented pseudomanifolds
(

4.3
A persistent Cheeger-type inequality

Proposition 4.1

\Delta_{q,\mathrm{up}}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{L}}
. Then

\frac{\bigl(\varphi_{q}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{L}}\bigr)^{2}}{|S_{q+1}^{\mathcal{L}}|}\leq\lambda_{\min}^{+}\leq|S_{q}^{\mathcal{K}}|\bigl(\varphi_{q}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{L}}\bigr)^{2}.

(8)

Proof.

Let
x\in C_{q}^{\mathcal{K}}
be nonzero. We use the elementary norm inequalities

1\leq\frac{\|x\|_{1}^{2}}{\|x\|_{2}^{2}}\leq|S_{q}^{\mathcal{K}}|,\qquad 1\leq\frac{\|PB^{T}x\|_{1}^{2}}{\|PB^{T}x\|_{2}^{2}}\leq|S_{q+1}^{\mathcal{L}}|,

whenever
PB^{T}x\neq 0
. Hence

\frac{1}{|S_{q+1}^{\mathcal{L}}|}\frac{\|PB^{T}x\|_{1}^{2}}{\|x\|_{1}^{2}}\leq\frac{\|PB^{T}x\|_{2}^{2}}{\|x\|_{2}^{2}}\leq|S_{q}^{\mathcal{K}}|\frac{\|PB^{T}x\|_{1}^{2}}{\|x\|_{1}^{2}}.

Set

i=\dim\ker(PB^{T})+1.

By the discussion above, this is precisely the index of the smallest non-zero variational eigenvalue.By
Lemma

4.1
and
Definition

4.2
,

\varphi_{q}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{L}}=\lambda_{i}\bigl(\Delta_{q,1,\mathrm{up}}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{L}}\bigr)=\inf_{\gamma(S)\geq i}\sup_{x\in S}\frac{\|PB^{T}x\|_{1}}{\|x\|_{1}}.

On the other hand,

\lambda_{i}\bigl(\Delta_{q,\mathrm{up}}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{L}}\bigr)=\inf_{\gamma(S)\geq i}\sup_{x\in S}\frac{\|PB^{T}x\|_{2}^{2}}{\|x\|_{2}^{2}}.

Applying the preceding norm comparison to the Rayleigh quotients gives

\frac{1}{|S_{q+1}^{\mathcal{L}}|}\left(\inf_{\gamma(S)\geq i}\sup_{x\in S}\frac{\|PB^{T}x\|_{1}}{\|x\|_{1}}\right)^{2}\leq\lambda_{i}\bigl(\Delta_{q,\mathrm{up}}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{L}}\bigr)\leq|S_{q}^{\mathcal{K}}|\left(\inf_{\gamma(S)\geq i}\sup_{x\in S}\frac{\|PB^{T}x\|_{1}}{\|x\|_{1}}\right)^{2}.

Using the identity above with
\varphi_{q}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{L}}
, we obtain

\frac{\bigl(\varphi_{q}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{L}}\bigr)^{2}}{|S_{q+1}^{\mathcal{L}}|}\leq\lambda_{i}\bigl(\Delta_{q,\mathrm{up}}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{L}}\bigr)\leq|S_{q}^{\mathcal{K}}|\bigl(\varphi_{q}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{L}}\bigr)^{2},

as claimed.
∎

When
\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{L}
, we obtain the following corollary.

Corollary 4.1
.

Let
\lambda_{\min}^{+}
be the smallest non-zero variational eigenvalue of
the up Laplacian
\Delta_{q,\mathrm{up}}^{\mathcal{L}}
. Then

\frac{\bigl(\varphi_{q}^{\mathcal{L}}\bigr)^{2}}{|S_{q+1}^{\mathcal{L}}|}\leq\lambda_{\min}^{+}\leq|S_{q}^{\mathcal{L}}|\bigl(\varphi_{q}^{\mathcal{L}}\bigr)^{2}.

(9)

,

5
Locally complete skeleta

One of the first instances of a Cheeger inequality for simplicial complexes was given in
[
32
]
under the assumption that the simplicial complex has a complete
q
-skeleton. Concretely, they considered
\Delta^{\mathcal{L}}_{q,\mathrm{up}}
under the assumption that all possible
q
-dimensional simplices were included in
\mathcal{L}
. For
q=0
, this assumption is void and applies to any graph. For
q\geq 1
, however, this is a fairly strong assumption.

In this section, we extend this result to inclusions
\mathcal{K}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{L}
of simplicial complexes, under the assumption that
\mathcal{K}
every connected component of
\mathcal{K}
has a complete
q
-skeleton. This allows one to, e.g., consider
\mathcal{K}
to be a collection of triangles (
1
-cycles), and then the inclusion into
\mathcal{L}
measures how these cycles are connected in the ambient space.

Remark 5.1
.

Subsequent to
[
32
]
, Gundert and Szedlák
[
18
]
extended this framework to general simplicial complexes without the requirement of a complete skeleton. We conjecture that their result naturally extends to the persistent setting; however, due to the technical nature of such an extension, we limit our discussion to the simpler setting.

5.1
The Cheeger constant

Let
\mathcal{K}\subseteq\mathcal{L}
be locally
q
-complete. That is,

\mathcal{K}=\coprod_{\alpha=1}^{N}\mathcal{K}^{\alpha},

where each
\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}
is a connected component of
\mathcal{K}
with complete

q
-skeleton. We denote the vertex sets of
\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}
by

V^{\mathcal{L}}
and
V^{\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}}
, respectively.

We will consider labelings of the vertices of
\mathcal{L}
by the labels

0,\ldots,q+1
. For a subcomplex
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{L}
, let

V_{i}^{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}
denote the set of vertices of
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
with label
i
. The following condition ensures that each connected component of
\mathcal{K}

contains every label.

Definition 5.1
.

A partition

\mathcal{P}=\coprod_{i=0}^{q+1}V_{i}^{\mathcal{L}}

of
V^{\mathcal{L}}
is called
effective
if

V_{i}^{\mathcal{L}}\cap V^{\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}}\neq\varnothing

for every
0\leq i\leq q+1
and every
1\leq\alpha\leq N
.

In particular,
Definition

5.1
implies that each component

\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}
has at least
q+2
vertices.

Example 5.1
.

We illustrate the setup in
Fig.

2
. In this example,
\mathcal{L}
is a

2
-dimensional simplicial complex, and

\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{K}^{1}\coprod\mathcal{K}^{2}

has two connected components, each with complete
1
-skeleton. These components are colored red.

We partition the vertices of
\mathcal{L}
into three color classes, colored
blue, yellow, and green. This partition is effective because each
component
\mathcal{K}^{1}
and
\mathcal{K}^{2}
contains vertices of all three colors, as
shown in
Fig.

2
.

\mathcal{K}^{1}
\mathcal{K}^{2}

Figure 2
:
Example of locally
q
-complete
\mathcal{K}\subset\mathcal{L}
, with
\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{K}^{1}\coprod\mathcal{K}^{2}
.

Before defining the Cheeger constant, we introduce some additional notation.
Since each component
\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}
has a complete
q
-skeleton, any set of

q+1
vertices in
V^{\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}}
spans a
q
-simplex of
\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}
.
Thus, if a
(q+1)
-simplex
\tau\in S_{q+1}^{\mathcal{L}}
has exactly

q+1
vertices in
V^{\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}}
, then these vertices span a unique

q
-face of
\tau
contained in
\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}
.

We define the
(q+1)
-frontier of
\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}
, denoted

\operatorname{Ft}(\mathcal{K}^{\alpha})
, to be the set of
(q+1)
-simplices of
\mathcal{L}
that have
exactly one vertex outside
V^{\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}}
and whose remaining
q+1

vertices span a
q
-simplex in
\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}
. Equivalently,

\operatorname{Ft}(\mathcal{K}^{\alpha})=\left\{\tau\in S_{q+1}^{\mathcal{L}}:\begin{array}[]{l}\exists v\in\tau\text{ such that }\tau\setminus\{v\}\in S_{q}^{\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}},\\
\text{and }v\notin V^{\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}}\end{array}\right\}.

These are the pink
2
-simplices in
Fig.

2
.

We then define the set of
interior

(q+1)
-simplices of
\mathcal{L}
by

\mathcal{L}^{\circ}:=S_{q+1}^{\mathcal{L}}\setminus\left(S_{q+1}^{\mathcal{K}}\cup\bigcup_{\alpha=1}^{N}\operatorname{Ft}(\mathcal{K}^{\alpha})\right).

These correspond to the purple
2
-simplices in
Fig.

2
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be an effective partition. If

\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathcal{L}
is a subcomplex, we write

S_{q+1}^{\mathcal{X}}
for its set of
(q+1)
-simplices. If
\mathcal{A}\subseteq S_{q+1}^{\mathcal{L}}
is a set of
(q+1)
-simplices,
we define

F_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathcal{A})=\left\{\tau\in\mathcal{A}:\tau\text{ has }q+2\text{ distinct labels with respect to }\mathcal{P}\right\}.

For a subcomplex
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathcal{L}
, we use the shorthand

F_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathcal{X}):=F_{\mathcal{P}}(S_{q+1}^{\mathcal{X}}).

Next, for each
0\leq i\leq q+1
, let
G_{\mathcal{P}}(\operatorname{Ft}(\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}),i)
be the set of
(q+1)
-simplices in
\operatorname{Ft}(\mathcal{K}^{\alpha})
whose vertices have
q+2
distinct labels and whose unique vertex outside
\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}
has label
i
; that is,

G_{\mathcal{P}}(\operatorname{Ft}(\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}),i)=\left\{\tau\in\operatorname{Ft}(\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}):\begin{array}[]{l}\exists v\in\tau\text{ such that }v\in V_{i}^{\mathcal{L}}\setminus V_{i}^{\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}},\\
\tau\setminus\{v\}\in S_{q}^{\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}}\text{ has }q+1\text{ distinct labels, all different from }i\end{array}\right\}.

We also let
H_{\mathcal{P}}(\operatorname{Ft}(\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}),i)
be the set of
(q+1)
-simplices in
\operatorname{Ft}(\mathcal{K}^{\alpha})
whose
q
-face in
\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}
has
q+1
distinct labels, all different from
i
, and whose unique vertex outside
\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}
has the same label as one of the vertices in this
q
-face; equivalently,

H_{\mathcal{P}}(\operatorname{Ft}(\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}),i)=\left\{\tau\in\operatorname{Ft}(\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}):\begin{array}[]{l}\exists v\in\tau\text{ such that }\tau\setminus\{v\}\in S_{q}^{\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}},\\
\tau\setminus\{v\}\text{ has }q+1\text{ distinct labels, all different from }i,\\
v\in V^{\mathcal{L}}\setminus V_{i}^{\mathcal{L}}\end{array}\right\}.

Definition 5.2
.

Let
\mathcal{K}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{L}
be an inclusion of simplicial complexes, and assume that
\mathcal{K}
is locally
q
-complete with decomposition

\mathcal{K}=\coprod_{\alpha=1}^{N}\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}.

Let

m_{i}=\min_{1\leq\alpha\leq N}|V_{i}^{\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}}|,\qquad m=\sum_{i=0}^{q+1}m_{i},

and define

\epsilon_{i}^{\alpha}=|V_{i}^{\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}}|-m_{i},\qquad s_{i}^{\alpha}=m+\epsilon_{i}^{\alpha}.

For an effective partition
\mathcal{P}
, define the
interior, boundary, and frontier contributions
by

\displaystyle I_{\mathcal{P}}

\displaystyle=m^{2}|F_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathcal{L}^{\circ})|,

\displaystyle B_{\mathcal{P}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N}|V^{\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}}|^{2}|F_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathcal{K}^{\alpha})|,

\displaystyle R_{\mathcal{P}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{q+1}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N}\left((s_{i}^{\alpha})^{2}|G_{\mathcal{P}}(\operatorname{Ft}(\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}),i)|+(\epsilon_{i}^{\alpha})^{2}|H_{\mathcal{P}}(\operatorname{Ft}(\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}),i)|\right).

The
persistent Cheeger constant
is defined by

h_{q}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{L}}=\min_{\mathcal{P}}\frac{I_{\mathcal{P}}+B_{\mathcal{P}}+R_{\mathcal{P}}}{\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N}|V^{\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}}|\prod_{i=0}^{q+1}|V_{i}^{\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}}|},

where the minimum is taken over all effective partitions
\mathcal{P}
.

When
\mathcal{K}
contains only one component (
N=1
), then
\epsilon_{i}^{1}=0
and
s_{i}^{1}=m=|V^{\mathcal{K}}|
for each
i\leq q+1
. Hence, the Cheeger constant reduces to

h_{q}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{L}}=\min_{\mathcal{P}}\frac{|V^{\mathcal{K}}|^{2}\cdot\big|F_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathcal{L}^{\circ})\cup F_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathcal{K})\cup\big(\bigcup_{i=0}^{q+1}G_{\mathcal{P}}(\operatorname{Ft}(\mathcal{K}),i)\big)\big|}{|V^{\mathcal{K}}|\cdot\prod_{i=0}^{q+1}|V^{\mathcal{K}}_{i}|}.

Observe that in the numerator the set
F_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathcal{L}^{\circ})\cup F_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathcal{K})\cup\big(\bigcup_{i=0}^{q+1}G_{\mathcal{P}}(\operatorname{Ft}(\mathcal{K}),i)\big)=F_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathcal{L})
,
hence we obtain the following persistent Cheeger constant:

h_{q}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{L}}=\min_{\mathcal{P}}\frac{|V^{\mathcal{K}}|\cdot|F_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathcal{L})|}{\prod_{i=0}^{q+1}|V^{\mathcal{K}}_{i}|}.

(10)

If
\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{L}
, this recovers the Cheeger constant in
[
32
, Theorem 1.2]
.

Furthermore, note that this Cheeger constant can be zero; e.g., if
\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{L}
is the
1
-skeleton of a tetrahedron with one added
2
-simplex. One can then partition the vertices so that the labels of the vertices of the
2
-simplex are not distinct.

5.2
A (one-sided) Cheeger inequality

In this section, we let
\lambda_{+}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{L}}
be the smallest non-trivial eigenvalue of
\Delta_{q,\text{up}}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{L}}
,
i.e.,

\lambda_{+}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{L}}:=\min_{0\neq x\in\ker(\partial_{q}^{\mathcal{K}})}\frac{\langle x,\Delta_{q,\text{up}}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{L}}x\rangle}{\|x\|^{2}}.

The additional constraint that
x\in\ker(\partial_{q}^{\mathcal{K}})
allows us to deduce precisely when this eigenvalue is non-zero; see also
Remark

4.1
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
\mathcal{K}\subseteq\mathcal{L}
. Then
\lambda_{+}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{L}}=0
if and only if the map

H_{q}(\mathcal{K};\mathbb{R})\longrightarrow H_{q}(\mathcal{L};\mathbb{R})

induced by the inclusion is nonzero.

Proof.

By the Hodge decomposition theorem, we have the orthogonal decomposition

\ker(\Delta_{q,\mathrm{up}}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{L}})=\ker\bigl((\partial_{q+1}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{L}})^{*}\bigr)=\mathrm{im}\bigl((\partial_{q}^{\mathcal{K}})^{*}\bigr)\oplus\ker(\Delta_{q}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{L}}).

Since

\mathrm{im}\bigl((\partial_{q}^{\mathcal{K}})^{*}\bigr)=\ker(\partial_{q}^{\mathcal{K}})^{\perp},

it follows that

\ker(\Delta_{q,\mathrm{up}}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{L}})=\ker(\partial_{q}^{\mathcal{K}})^{\perp}\oplus\ker(\Delta_{q}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{L}}).

Moreover,
\dim\ker(\Delta_{q}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{L}})
is equal to the rank of the map

H_{q}(\mathcal{K};\mathbb{R})\longrightarrow H_{q}(\mathcal{L};\mathbb{R}).

Therefore, there exists a nonzero element

x\in\ker(\partial_{q}^{\mathcal{K}})
such that

\Delta_{q,\mathrm{up}}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{L}}x=0

if and only if

\ker(\Delta_{q}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{L}})\neq 0.

Hence,
\lambda_{\min}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{L}}=0
if and only if the induced map is nonzero.
∎

We now prove a one-sided persistent Cheeger-type inequality. In
[
32
, Section 4.2]
, the authors give an example using the Möbius strip showing that a lower Cheeger inequality is not possible in general.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
\mathcal{K}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{L}
be an inclusion of simplicial complexes, and assume that
\mathcal{K}
is locally
q
-complete with decomposition

\mathcal{K}=\coprod_{\alpha=1}^{N}\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}.

We have the following inequality:

\lambda_{+}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{L}}\leq h_{q}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{L}}.

(11)

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{P}=\coprod_{i=0}^{q+1}V^{\mathcal{L}}_{i}
be an effective partition that realizes the minimum in
h_{q}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{L}}
. For simplicity, we write
\mathrm{label}(v)=j
if
v\in V_{j}^{\mathcal{L}}
.

We put a total order on the vertices of
\mathcal{L}
such that
v<w
if
v\in V_{i}^{\mathcal{L}}
and
w\in V_{j}^{\mathcal{L}}
for
i<j
. Then, we orient the higher-order simplices according to this ordering. In particular, for a
q
-simplex
\sigma
in
\mathcal{L}
, we write
\sigma=[v_{0}v_{1}\cdots v_{q}]
where
v_{r}<v_{s}
for
r<s
.

For each such simplex, we define

f([v_{0}v_{1}\cdots v_{q}])=\begin{cases}(-1)^{i}|V^{\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}}_{i}|&\text{if }\sigma\in\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}\text{ and }\bigcup_{j=0}^{q}\mathrm{label}(v_{j})=\{0,\ldots,q+1\}\setminus\{i\};\\
(-1)^{i}m_{i}&\text{if }\sigma\notin\mathcal{K}\text{ and }\bigcup_{j=0}^{q}\mathrm{label}(v_{j})=\{0,\ldots,q+1\}\setminus\{i\};\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

and define the associated
q
-chains
f_{\mathcal{L}}:=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{q}^{\mathcal{L}}}f(\sigma)\sigma\in C_{q}^{\mathcal{L}}
and

f_{\mathcal{K}}:=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{q}^{\mathcal{K}}}f(\sigma)\sigma\in C_{q}^{\mathcal{K}}
, respectively.

The
q
-chain
f_{\mathcal{K}}
will serve as our test chain to give an upper bound on the eigenvalue. To that end, we first need to show that
f_{\mathcal{K}}\in\ker(\partial_{q}^{\mathcal{K}})
. This is immediate by noting that restricted to each
\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}
,
f_{\mathcal{K}}
coincides with the
q
-chain (or
(q-1)
-chain in their language) constructed for a simplicial complex with a complete skeleton in
[
32
]
.

Furthermore, following the construction in the same paper, it is straightforward to verify that

\sum_{\sigma\in S_{q}^{\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}}}f_{\mathcal{K}}(\sigma)^{2}=|V^{\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}}|\cdot\prod_{i=0}^{q+1}|V^{\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}}_{i}|.

To see this: a
q
-simplex
\sigma
contributes to the sum
\sum_{\sigma\in S_{q}^{\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}}}f_{\mathcal{K}}(\sigma)^{2}
only if the vertices of
\sigma
have
q+1
different labels. That is, there is a label
i
such that
V_{i}^{\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}}\cap\sigma=\varnothing
, and thus
\sigma
contributes
|V_{i}^{\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}}|^{2}
to the sum. Since
\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}
is
q
-complete, there are
\prod_{j\neq i}|V_{j}^{\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}}|
such
q
-simplices whose vertices have different labels and do not intersect with
V_{i}^{\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}}
. Therefore,

\sum_{\sigma\in S_{q}^{\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}}}f_{\mathcal{K}}(\sigma)^{2}=\sum_{i=0}^{q+1}\left(\prod_{j\neq i}|V_{j}^{\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}}|\right)|V_{i}^{\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}}|^{2}=|V^{\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}}|\cdot\prod_{i=0}^{q+1}|V^{\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}}_{i}|.

Summing over each connected component of
\mathcal{K}
, we obtain

\|f_{\mathcal{K}}\|^{2}=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{q}^{\mathcal{K}}}f_{\mathcal{K}}(\sigma)^{2}=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{q}^{\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}}}f_{\mathcal{K}}(\sigma)^{2}=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N}\left(|V^{\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}}|\cdot\prod_{i=0}^{q+1}|V^{\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}}_{i}|\right).

According to
Lemma

3.1
,

\langle f_{\mathcal{K}},\Delta_{q,\text{up}}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{L}}\,f_{\mathcal{K}}\rangle\leq\langle f_{\mathcal{L}},\Delta_{q,\text{up}}^{\mathcal{L}}\,f_{\mathcal{L}}\rangle
,
hence

\lambda_{+}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{L}}=\min_{0\neq x\in\ker(\partial_{q}^{\mathcal{K}})}\frac{\langle x,\Delta_{q,\text{up}}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{L}}x\rangle}{\|x\|^{2}}\leq\frac{\langle f_{\mathcal{K}},\Delta_{q,\text{up}}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{L}}\,f_{\mathcal{K}}\rangle}{\|f_{\mathcal{K}}\|^{2}}\leq\frac{\langle f_{\mathcal{L}},\Delta_{q,\text{up}}^{\mathcal{L}}\,f_{\mathcal{L}}\rangle}{\|f_{\mathcal{K}}\|^{2}}.

Hence, it suffices to show that

\frac{\langle f_{\mathcal{L}},\Delta_{q,\text{up}}^{\mathcal{L}}\,f_{\mathcal{L}}\rangle}{\|f_{\mathcal{K}}\|^{2}}=h_{q}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{L}},

or, equivalently,

\langle f_{\mathcal{L}},\Delta_{q,\text{up}}^{\mathcal{L}}\,f_{\mathcal{L}}\rangle=I_{\mathcal{P}}+B_{\mathcal{P}}+R_{\mathcal{P}}.

Evaluating the chain
f_{\mathcal{L}}
.

Recall that the coefficient of
(\partial_{q+1}^{\mathcal{L}})^{*}(f_{\mathcal{L}})
at the simplex
\tau\in S^{\mathcal{L}}_{q+1}
is given by

\langle(\partial_{q+1}^{\mathcal{L}})^{*}(f_{\mathcal{L}}),\tau\rangle=\langle f_{\mathcal{L}},\partial_{q+1}^{\mathcal{L}}(\tau)\rangle.

Hence,

\langle f_{\mathcal{L}},\Delta_{q,\text{up}}^{\mathcal{L}}f_{\mathcal{L}}\rangle=\|(\partial_{q+1}^{\mathcal{L}})^{*}(f_{\mathcal{L}})\|^{2}=\sum_{\tau\in S_{q+1}^{\mathcal{L}}}|\langle f_{\mathcal{L}},\partial_{q+1}^{\mathcal{L}}(\tau)\rangle|^{2}.

We compute this quantity through a case-by-case analysis.

Case 0a: repeated labels.

If
\tau
contains three vertices sharing the same label (i.e.,
v_{i_{1}},v_{i_{2}},v_{i_{3}}\in V^{\mathcal{L}}_{i}
), or two pairs of vertices sharing the same labels (i.e.,
v_{i_{1}},v_{i_{2}}\in V^{\mathcal{L}}_{i}
and
v_{j_{1}},v_{j_{2}}\in V^{\mathcal{L}}_{j}
), then removing any single vertex leaves a
q
-face that still contains duplicate labels. Thus,
\langle f_{\mathcal{L}},\partial_{q+1}^{\mathcal{L}}(\tau)\rangle=0
.

Case 0b: repeated labels outside the frontier.

Assume that
\tau\in\mathcal{L}^{\circ}\cup\mathcal{K}
, and that
\tau
contains exactly one pair of vertices with the same label. That is, we can write
\tau=[v_{0}v_{1}\cdots v_{q+1}]
where
\mathrm{label}(v_{i})=\mathrm{label}(v_{i+1})
for some index
i
. For
j\neq i,i+1
, the face
\tau\setminus\{v_{j}\}
still contains duplicated labels, so
f(\tau\setminus\{v_{j}\})=0
. Hence

\langle f_{\mathcal{L}},\partial_{q+1}^{\mathcal{L}}(\tau)\rangle=(-1)^{i}f(\tau\setminus\{v_{i}\})+(-1)^{i+1}f(\tau\setminus\{v_{i+1}\})=0,

since
f
only depends on the labels of the vertices, meaning
f(\tau\setminus\{v_{i}\})=f(\tau\setminus\{v_{i+1}\})
.

Case 1 (interior):
\tau\in F_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathcal{L}^{\circ})
.

For
\tau\in F_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathcal{L}^{\circ})
, all
q+2
labels are present. Removing the vertex
v_{i}
with label
i
yields a face that misses label
i
. Because
\tau
is in the interior, this face evaluates to

f(\tau\setminus\{v_{i}\})=(-1)^{i}m_{i}.

Hence,

\langle f_{\mathcal{L}},\partial_{q+1}^{\mathcal{L}}(\tau)\rangle=\sum_{i=0}^{q+1}(-1)^{i}f(\tau\setminus\{v_{i}\})=\sum_{i=0}^{q+1}(-1)^{i}(-1)^{i}m_{i}=m,

and therefore

\sum_{\tau\in F_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathcal{L}^{\circ})}|\langle f_{\mathcal{L}},\partial_{q+1}^{\mathcal{L}}(\tau)\rangle|^{2}=m^{2}|F_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathcal{L}^{\circ})|=I_{\mathcal{P}}.

Case 2 (boundary):
\tau\in F_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathcal{K}^{\alpha})
.

Similarly, for
\tau\in F_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathcal{K}^{\alpha})
,

f(\tau\setminus\{v_{i}\})=(-1)^{i}|V^{\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}}_{i}|.

Following the argument from the previous case,

\langle f_{\mathcal{L}},\partial_{q+1}^{\mathcal{L}}(\tau)\rangle=\sum_{i=0}^{q+1}(-1)^{i}(-1)^{i}|V^{\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}}_{i}|=|V^{\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}}|.

Summing over all such simplices yields

\sum_{\tau\in F_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathcal{K}^{\alpha})}|\langle f_{\mathcal{L}},\partial_{q+1}^{\mathcal{L}}(\tau)\rangle|^{2}=|V^{\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}}|^{2}|F_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathcal{K}^{\alpha})|,

and thus summing over all components gives
\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N}|V^{\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}}|^{2}|F_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathcal{K}^{\alpha})|=B_{\mathcal{P}}
.

Case 3 (frontier with distinct labels):
\tau\in G_{\mathcal{P}}(\operatorname{Ft}(\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}),i)
.

Recall that
i
denotes the label of the unique vertex in
\tau
not contained in
\mathcal{K}
. If we remove this vertex
v_{i}
, the remaining face is in
\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}
and misses label
i
, giving

f(\tau\setminus\{v_{i}\})=(-1)^{i}|V_{i}^{\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}}|.

If we remove any other vertex
v_{j}
(where
j\neq i
), the remaining face is not in
\mathcal{K}
and misses label
j
, yielding

f(\tau\setminus\{v_{j}\})=(-1)^{j}m_{j}.

Therefore,

\langle f_{\mathcal{L}},\partial_{q+1}^{\mathcal{L}}(\tau)\rangle=\sum_{j=0}^{q+1}(-1)^{j}f(\tau\setminus\{v_{j}\})=|V^{\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}}_{i}|+\sum_{j\neq i}m_{j}=s_{i}^{\alpha},

and

\sum_{\tau\in G_{\mathcal{P}}(\operatorname{Ft}(\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}),i)}\big|\langle f_{\mathcal{L}},\partial_{q+1}^{\mathcal{L}}(\tau)\rangle|^{2}=(s_{i}^{\alpha})^{2}|G_{\mathcal{P}}(\operatorname{Ft}(\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}),i)|.

Case 4 (frontier with distinct labels in
\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}
):
\tau\in H_{\mathcal{P}}(\operatorname{Ft}(\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}),i)
.

By definition, the
q
-face of
\tau
in
\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}
misses label
i
. The unique vertex outside
\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}
, say
v_{out}
, has the same label as exactly one vertex
v_{in}\in\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}
. Let this duplicated label be
k
(where
k\neq i
).

Because
v_{out}
and
v_{in}
share the same label, they are adjacent in our ordered vertices of
\tau
. Let their indices be
a
and
a+1
. If we remove any vertex other than
v_{out}
or
v_{in}
, the remaining
q
-face still contains duplicate labels, meaning
f
evaluates to zero. We thus only consider removing
v_{out}
or
v_{in}
:

•

Removing
v_{in}
yields a face not in
\mathcal{K}
that misses label
i
. Thus,
f(\tau\setminus\{v_{in}\})=(-1)^{i}m_{i}
.

•

Removing
v_{out}
yields a face in
\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}
that misses label
i
. Thus,
f(\tau\setminus\{v_{out}\})=(-1)^{i}|V^{\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}}_{i}|
.

Therefore, the inner product is

\displaystyle\langle f_{\mathcal{L}},\partial_{q+1}^{\mathcal{L}}(\tau)\rangle

\displaystyle=(-1)^{a}f(\tau\setminus\{v_{in}\})+(-1)^{a+1}f(\tau\setminus\{v_{out}\})

\displaystyle=(-1)^{a+i}m_{i}-(-1)^{a+i}|V^{\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}}_{i}|

\displaystyle=(-1)^{a+i+1}(|V^{\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}}_{i}|-m_{i})

\displaystyle=\pm\epsilon_{i}^{\alpha}.

Squaring this yields

\sum_{\tau\in H_{\mathcal{P}}(\operatorname{Ft}(\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}),i)}\big|\langle f_{\mathcal{L}},\partial_{q+1}^{\mathcal{L}}(\tau)\rangle|^{2}=\sum_{\tau\in H_{\mathcal{P}}(\operatorname{Ft}(\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}),i)}(\epsilon_{i}^{\alpha})^{2}=(\epsilon_{i}^{\alpha})^{2}|H_{\mathcal{P}}(\operatorname{Ft}(\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}),i)|.

Case 5 (frontier with duplicate labels in
\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}
).

The remaining case is that
\tau\in\operatorname{Ft}(\mathcal{K}^{\alpha})
, where the
q+1
vertices in
\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}
span at most
q
labels. This means there are two vertices in
\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}
sharing a label. Following the precise argument from Case 0b, we obtain
\langle f_{\mathcal{L}},\partial_{q+1}^{\mathcal{L}}(\tau)\rangle=0
.

Bringing it all together.

Combining Cases 3 and 4 over all choices of
i
and
\alpha
, we get

\sum_{i=0}^{q+1}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N}\left(\sum_{\tau\in G_{\mathcal{P}}(\operatorname{Ft}(\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}),i)}\big|\langle f_{\mathcal{L}},\partial_{q+1}^{\mathcal{L}}(\tau)\rangle\big|^{2}+\sum_{\tau\in H_{\mathcal{P}}(\operatorname{Ft}(\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}),i)}\big|\langle f_{\mathcal{L}},\partial_{q+1}^{\mathcal{L}}(\tau)\rangle\big|^{2}\right)=R_{\mathcal{P}}.

Combining this with Cases 1 and 2, we thus obtain

\displaystyle\langle f_{\mathcal{L}},\Delta_{q,\text{up}}^{\mathcal{L}}f_{\mathcal{L}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{\tau\in S_{q+1}^{\mathcal{L}}}|\langle f_{\mathcal{L}},\partial_{q+1}^{\mathcal{L}}(\tau)\rangle|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\tau\in F_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathcal{L}^{\circ})}|\langle f_{\mathcal{L}},\partial_{q+1}^{\mathcal{L}}(\tau)\rangle|^{2}+\sum_{\tau\in F_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathcal{K})}|\langle f_{\mathcal{L}},\partial_{q+1}^{\mathcal{L}}(\tau)\rangle|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{i=0}^{q+1}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N}\left(\sum_{\tau\in G_{\mathcal{P}}(\operatorname{Ft}(\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}),i)}\big|\langle f_{\mathcal{L}},\partial_{q+1}^{\mathcal{L}}(\tau)\rangle\big|^{2}+\sum_{\tau\in H_{\mathcal{P}}(\operatorname{Ft}(\mathcal{K}^{\alpha}),i)}\big|\langle f_{\mathcal{L}},\partial_{q+1}^{\mathcal{L}}(\tau)\rangle\big|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=I_{\mathcal{P}}+B_{\mathcal{P}}+R_{\mathcal{P}}.

∎

6
Pseudomanifolds

We assume throughout that
\iota\colon\mathcal{K}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{L}
is an inclusion of simplicial complexes, and that
\mathcal{L}
is a
(q+1)
-dimensional
orientable pseudomanifold
(
non-branching
): every
q
-simplex is in the boundary of at most two
(q+1)
-simplices, and the
(q+1)
-simplices can be oriented such that they induce opposite orientations on shared
q
-dimensional faces. Equivalently,
the boundary matrix

\partial_{q+1}^{\mathcal{L}}\colon C_{q+1}^{\mathcal{L}}\longrightarrow C_{q}^{\mathcal{L}}

with respect to the oriented simplex bases has at most two nonzero entries in each row, and the product of two non-zero elements (
\pm 1)
in the same row is
-1
. Note that the assumption on
\mathcal{L}
is satisfied for any
(q+1)
-dimensional complex embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{q+1}
, but not for e.g., projective spaces.

The
dual graph
of
\mathcal{L}
is the graph with vertex set of
(q+1)
-simplices, and an edge connecting two
(q+1)
-simplices with a shared face.

The goal of this section is twofold. First, we show that for orientable pseudomanifolds the persistent up Laplacian admits a Kron-type reduction to a graph Laplacian, possibly with Dirichlet boundary conditions. Second, we use this reduction and
Theorem

2.1
to obtain a two-sided Cheeger inequality.

We then specialize
Proposition

4.1
to the setting of pseudomanifolds (but orientable and not) and show that the resulting persistent Cheeger constant has a concrete geometric interpretation on the dual graph.

Remark 6.1
.

Spectra for (non-persistent) Laplacians of planar pseudomanifolds
appear in
[
16
]
. We believe that the Dirichlet
viewpoint in this section, together with the standard graph Laplacian interpretation for the
1
-down Laplacian, offers a transparent and natural explanation for their results concerning the first Laplacian; see, in particular,
Example

6.2
.

6.1
A Kron reduction for pseudomanifolds

As shown in
[
3
]
, if
\mathcal{L}
is a pseudomanifold (non-branching in the terminology of that paper), we may choose a basis

c_{1},\dots,c_{m}
of
C_{q+1}^{\mathcal{L},\mathcal{K}}
with an appropriate ordering such that
(i) each
c_{i}
is a
0/1
indicator chain of
(q+1)
-simplices,
(ii)
\mathrm{supp}(c_{i})\cap\mathrm{supp}(c_{j})=\varnothing
for

(
\mathrm{supp}
being the simplices on which the chain has non-zero coefficients), and
(iii)
\partial_{q+1}^{\mathcal{L}}c_{i}\in C_{q}^{\mathcal{K}}
.
For simplicity, we use the standard inner product on chains. Define

V_{i}:=\langle c_{i},c_{i}\rangle_{q+1},\qquad\mathrm{sf}_{ij}:=\#\{\text{$q$-faces shared by $c_{i},c_{j}$}\}

\mathrm{free}_{i}:=\#\{\text{$q$-faces of $c_{i}$ not shared with any $c_{j}$}\},

A_{i}:=\mathrm{free}_{i}+\sum_{j\neq i}\mathrm{sf}_{ij},\qquad W:=\mathrm{diag}(V_{1},\dots,V_{m}).

Here
V_{i}
counts
(q+1)
-simplices in
c_{i}
,
\mathrm{sf}_{ij}
counts shared
q
-faces,

\mathrm{free}_{i}
counts boundary faces not shared with another
c_{j}
, and
A_{i}
is the total

q
-boundary “area” of
c_{i}
, and
W
is the Gram matrix (
Appendix

A
) of the basis for
C_{q+1}^{\mathcal{L},\mathcal{K}}.
In particular, letting
Z
have columns the chains
c_{i}
, we get
Z^{T}Z=W
the following expression for the persistent up Laplacian:

\Delta_{q,\mathrm{up}}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{L}}=B_{q+1}^{\mathcal{L},\mathcal{K}}(Z^{T}Z)^{-1}(B_{q+1}^{\mathcal{L},\mathcal{K}})^{T}=B_{q+1}^{\mathcal{L},\mathcal{K}}W^{-1}(B_{q+1}^{\mathcal{L},\mathcal{K}})^{T},

where
B^{\mathcal{L},\mathcal{K}}_{q+1}
be the boundary matrix of
\partial_{q+1}^{\mathcal{L}}
restricted to
\mathrm{span}\{c_{i}\}
.

6.1.1
The Kron reduction

Proposition 6.1
.

We have,

\bigl((B^{\mathcal{L},\mathcal{K}}_{q+1})^{T}B^{\mathcal{L},\mathcal{K}}_{q+1}\bigr)_{ii}=A_{i},\qquad\bigl((B^{\mathcal{L},\mathcal{K}}_{q+1})^{T}B^{\mathcal{L},\mathcal{K}}_{q+1}\bigr)_{ij}=-\,\mathrm{sf}_{ij}.

Proof.

Each
q
-face in
\partial_{q+1}^{\mathcal{L}}c_{i}
appears with coefficient
\pm 1
.
By non-branching, a face is either free (contributing
+1
to the diagonal) or shared with exactly one
c_{j}
(contributing
-1
to the
(i,j)
entry since orientations are opposite). Summing these contributions gives the stated formulas.
∎

Hence, from

2.1

(B^{\mathcal{L},\mathcal{K}}_{q+1})^{T}B^{\mathcal{L},\mathcal{K}}_{q+1}
is the graph Laplacian of a graph with Dirichlet boundary conditions. We illustrate the need for phantom vertices in the non-persistent setting.

.

Let

(i)

the non-zero spectrum of

(ii)

the non-zero spectrum of

Proof.

, the non-zero spectrum of

This matrix is similar to the symmetric matrix

6.1.2
A persistent Cheeger inequality

Given the reduction to a graph with Dirichlet boundary conditions, a Cheeger inequality for the pseudomanifold case is near-immediate from
Theorem

2.1
. However, for the sake of clarity, we recall the definitions in the notation used here.

For any nonempty subset
S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,m\}
, where
\{1,\ldots,m\}
indexes the chains
c_{i}
, set

\operatorname{Vol}(S):=\sum_{i\in S}V_{i},\qquad\operatorname{Cut}(S):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in S\\
j\notin S\end{subarray}}\mathrm{sf}_{ij},\qquad\operatorname{Bdy}(S):=\sum_{i\in S}\mathrm{free}_{i}.

We also write
V_{\mathrm{tot}}:=\operatorname{Vol}(\{1,\ldots,m\})
.

Definition 6.1
.

Define

h_{\mathrm{vol}}:=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\varnothing\neq S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,m\}\\
\operatorname{Vol}(S)\leq V_{\mathrm{tot}}/2\end{subarray}}\frac{\operatorname{Cut}(S)}{\operatorname{Vol}(S)},\qquad h^{\mathrm{Bdy}}_{\mathrm{vol}}:=\min_{\varnothing\neq S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,m\}}\frac{\operatorname{Cut}(S)+\operatorname{Bdy}(S)}{\operatorname{Vol}(S)}.

Theorem 6.2
.

Let
\Delta:=\max_{1\leq i\leq m}A_{i}/V_{i}
, and let
\mathcal{K}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{L}
, where
\mathcal{L}
is a
(q+1)
-dimensional orientable pseudomanifold whose dual graph is connected. Let
\lambda_{\min}^{+}
denote the smallest non-zero eigenvalue of
the persistent up Laplacian
\Delta_{q,\mathrm{up}}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{L}}
.

1.

If
\mathcal{L}
is boundaryless, then

\frac{h_{\mathrm{vol}}^{2}}{2\Delta}\leq\lambda_{\min}^{+}\leq 2h_{\mathrm{vol}}.

2.

Otherwise,

\frac{(h^{\mathrm{Bdy}}_{\mathrm{vol}})^{2}}{2\Delta}\leq\lambda_{\min}^{+}\leq h^{\mathrm{Bdy}}_{\mathrm{vol}}.

Proof.

From
Theorem

6.1
, the smallest nonzero eigenvalue of
\Delta_{q,\mathrm{up}}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{L}}
agrees with the smallest relevant eigenvalue of

M:=W^{-1/2}(B_{q+1}^{\mathcal{L},\mathcal{K}})^{T}B_{q+1}^{\mathcal{L},\mathcal{K}}W^{-1/2},

where
W=\operatorname{diag}(V_{1},\ldots,V_{m})
. The result now follows from
Theorem

2.1
by noting that for
X:=(B_{q+1}^{\mathcal{L},\mathcal{K}})^{T}B_{q+1}^{\mathcal{L},\mathcal{K}}
,

X_{ii}=A_{i},\qquad X_{ij}=-\mathrm{sf}_{ij}\quad(i\neq j).

and

b_{i}=\sum_{j}X_{ij}=A_{i}-\sum_{j\neq i}\mathrm{sf}_{ij}=\mathrm{free}_{i}.

Moreover, the vertex weights in the graph theorem are
\mu_{i}=V_{i}
, and hence

\operatorname{Vol}(S)=\sum_{i\in S}V_{i},\qquad\operatorname{Cut}(S)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in S\\
j\notin S\end{subarray}}\mathrm{sf}_{ij},\qquad\operatorname{Bdy}(S)=\sum_{i\in S}\mathrm{free}_{i},

which are exactly the quantities in
Definition

6.1
. Also,

\Delta=\max_{i}\frac{X_{ii}}{\mu_{i}}=\max_{i}\frac{A_{i}}{V_{i}}.

The interior graph associated with
X
is the dual graph on the chains
c_{i}
, with edge weights
\mathrm{sf}_{ij}
, and is connected by assumption. Therefore
Theorem

2.1
applies. If
\mathcal{L}
is boundaryless, then
\mathrm{free}_{i}=0
for all
i
, so all row sums of
X
vanish. Hence

\frac{h_{\mathrm{vol}}^{2}}{2\Delta}\leq\lambda_{\min}^{+}\leq 2h_{\mathrm{vol}}.

If
\mathcal{L}
has boundary, then at least one
\mathrm{free}_{i}
is positive, so at least one row sum of
X
is positive. In this case
Theorem

2.1
gives

\frac{(h_{\mathrm{vol}}^{\mathrm{Bdy}})^{2}}{2\Delta}\leq\lambda_{\min}^{+}\leq h_{\mathrm{vol}}^{\mathrm{Bdy}}.

∎

We show that no analogue of the Cheeger-type lower bound in

We shall use the following result on

(

There exist a constant

of

tends to infinity, and

for all

, and

denotes the dual graph of

Note that in the non-persistent setting, partitioning the

For the purposes of our example, we slightly modify the construction.
Let
\mathbb{U}_{k}

with the triangulation of the

Let

.

There exists a constant

Let

be the dual graph of

6.2
The Cheeger constant
\varphi_{q}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{L}}

Let
\mathcal{K}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{L}
be an inclusion where
\mathcal{L}
is a
(q+1)
-dimensional orientable pseudomanifold. Since
\mathcal{L}
is an orientable non-branching pseudomanifold, the transpose of
B_{q+1}^{\mathcal{L},\mathcal{K}}
can be identified, after choosing orientations, with the signed incidence matrix of an associated dual graph. Thus
B_{q+1}^{\mathcal{L},\mathcal{K}}
is totally unimodular; see
Appendix

.

Leveraging this observation, we obtain a transparent description of the nonzero persistent Cheeger constant
\varphi_{q}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{L}}
(see
Definition

4.2
) in this setting. At the end of the section, we shall also consider the case where
\mathcal{L}
has boundary.

6.2.1
Some results from graph theory

We first recall that the distance between two vertices
u,v\in V
in a graph
G=(V,E)
, denoted by
\operatorname{dist}(u,v)
, is defined as the length of the shortest path joining
u
and
v
. The diameter of
G
, denoted by
\operatorname{diam}(G)
, is defined as the maximum distance over all pairs of vertices in
G
(see
[
10
, Chapter 3]
).

A well-known result bridging linear programming and spectral graph theory demonstrates that the diameter of a connected graph
G
can be deduced via the incidence matrix
I
(see
Section

1
).
We present the result as follows.

Lemma 6.2
.

We have the equality

\min_{x\perp^{1}\ker(I)}\frac{\|Ix\|_{1}}{\|x\|_{1}}=\frac{2}{\operatorname{diam}(G)}.

(12)

Since the graph
G=(V,E)
is connected by assumption,
therefore,
the incidence matrix
I
has rank
|V|-1
,
and if we remove one row from
I
,
the remaining rows of this matrix have the full row rank.
We refer to the node corresponding to the removed row as the
ground vertex
and this remaining matrix as the
reduced
incidence matrix of the graph
G
.
Similarly to
Lemma

6.2
,
the reduced incidence matrix gives rise to the maximum distance between the ground vertex and other vertices.

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
u\in V
be the ground vertex and
R
be the reduced incidence matrix.
Then

\min_{x\perp^{1}\ker(R)}\frac{\|Rx\|_{1}}{\|x\|_{1}}=\min_{i\in V\setminus\{u\}}\frac{1}{\operatorname{dist}(i,u)}.

(13)

We were unable to find good references for these results in the literature, and thus we provide the proofs of
Lemma

6.2
and
Lemma

6.3
, along with further details in
Section

C.2
.

Remark 6.2
.

The salient point is that the (reduced) incidence matrix of a graph is totally unimodular (an observation made by Poincaré
[
33
]
) and thus by the framework of linear programming,
the flow vector that reaches the minimum is integral, corresponding precisely to a discrete shortest path, giving rise to
\operatorname{diam}(G)
or
\operatorname{dist}(i,u)
.

6.2.2
Pseudomanifolds without boundary

Recall the block form

B_{q+1}^{\mathcal{L}}=\begin{bmatrix}B\\
D\end{bmatrix},

where the row indices of

and

correspond to

and

of

Let

be the column matrix representation of this basis,
i.e.,

and thus

In the following lemma, we do not assume that

is without boundary.

Lemma 6.4
.

We have the equality
\|PB^{T}x\|_{1}=\|Z^{T}B^{T}x\|_{1}
for an arbitrary
x\in C_{q}^{\mathcal{K}}
.

Proof.

Recall from
Section

6.1
that each
c_{i}
is a
0/1
indicator vector,
and
\mathrm{supp}(c_{i})\cap\mathrm{supp}(c_{j})=\varnothing
for
i\neq j
. A well-known result in linear algebra states that the
\ell^{1}
-norm of a matrix is equal to the maximum
\ell^{1}
-norm of all its column vectors,
hence
\|Z(Z^{T}Z)^{-1}\|_{1}=1
.

Thus we have the following inequality

\|PB^{T}x\|_{1}=\|Z(Z^{T}Z)^{-1}Z^{T}B^{T}x\|_{1}\leq\|Z(Z^{T}Z)^{-1}\|_{1}\cdot\|Z^{T}B^{T}x\|_{1}=\|Z^{T}B^{T}x\|_{1}.

On the other hand,
the equality
Z^{T}PB^{T}=Z^{T}B^{T}
implies that
\|Z^{T}B^{T}x\|_{1}\leq\|Z^{T}\|_{1}\cdot\|PB^{T}x\|_{1}
.
Since
\|Z^{T}\|_{1}=1
,
we have

\|Z^{T}B^{T}x\|_{1}\leq\|PB^{T}x\|_{1}.

Therefore

\|PB^{T}x\|_{1}=\|Z^{T}B^{T}x\|_{1}.

∎

We can then give a description of the nonzero persistent Cheeger constant

7
Persistent Cheeger constants for graph inclusions

In this section, we focus on persistent Cheeger constants for graphs. Throughout, let
G=(V^{G},E^{G})
be a connected graph and let
H=(V^{H},E^{H})
be a subgraph of
G
. Note that
\Delta_{0}^{H,G}
depends only on the vertex set
V^{H}
, and not on the edge set
E^{H}
.

As observed in
Proposition

3.2
, the persistent Laplacian
\Delta_{0}^{H,G}
can be identified with the Laplacian
\Delta_{0}^{\widetilde{H}}
of a connected weighted graph
\widetilde{H}=(V^{H},E^{\widetilde{H}},w^{\widetilde{H}})
, where
E^{\widetilde{H}}
and
w^{\widetilde{H}}
denote its edge set and edge weights, respectively. Using this identification and the standard Cheeger constants for weighted graphs
[
9
,
10
]
, one obtains the following definitions from
[
30
, Definition 4.18]
.

Definition 7.1
.

For
A\subseteq V^{H}
, let
\chi_{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{V^{H}}
denote its indicator vector, i.e.,

(\chi_{A})_{v}=\begin{cases}1,&v\in A,\\
0,&v\notin A.\end{cases}

The Kron-reduction versions of the persistent Cheeger constants are defined by

h_{\operatorname{Kron}}^{H,G}=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}V^{H}=H_{1}\sqcup H_{2}\\
H_{1},H_{2}\neq\varnothing\end{subarray}}\frac{|V^{H}|\cdot(\chi_{H_{1}})^{T}\Delta_{0}^{H,G}\chi_{H_{1}}}{|H_{1}|\cdot|H_{2}|},

(18)

and

\varphi_{\operatorname{Kron}}^{H,G}=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\varnothing\neq H_{1}\subseteq V^{H}\\
|H_{1}|\leq|V^{H}|/2\end{subarray}}\frac{(\chi_{H_{1}})^{T}\Delta_{0}^{H,G}\chi_{H_{1}}}{|H_{1}|}.

(19)

While these persistent Cheeger constants do not extend to higher-degree simplicial complexes, it is natural to compare them with the persistent Cheeger constants that do. This comparison is the goal of the remainder of this section.

7.1
Relating
h_{0}^{H,G}
to
h_{\operatorname{Kron}}^{H,G}

In the case of graphs, for which the condition on a complete skeleton is trivially satisfied,
the non-trivial Cheeger constant
Eq.

10
simplifies to the form

\displaystyle h_{0}^{H,G}=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}V^{H}=H_{1}\sqcup H_{2}\\
V^{G}=G_{1}\sqcup G_{2}\\
\varnothing\neq H_{i}\subset G_{i}\end{subarray}}\frac{|H|\cdot|E(V^{G}_{1},V^{G}_{2})|}{|H_{1}|\cdot|H_{2}|}.

(20)

Proposition 7.1
.

Let
H\hookrightarrow G
be an inclusion of graphs where
G
is connected. Then

h_{\operatorname{Kron}}^{H,G}\leq h_{0}^{H,G}.

Proof.

Let
V^{G}=G_{1}\sqcup G_{2}
and
V^{H}=H_{1}\sqcup H_{2}
be an admissible pair of partitions achieving
h_{0}^{H,G}
, so that
\varnothing\neq H_{i}\subseteq G_{i}
.

Since
H_{i}\subset G_{i}
, the restriction of
\chi_{G_{1}}
to
H
is
\chi_{H_{1}}
. By the variational characterization in
Lemma

3.1
,

(\chi_{H_{1}})^{T}\Delta_{0}^{H,G}\chi_{H_{1}}\leq(\chi_{G_{1}})^{T}\Delta_{0}^{G}\chi_{G_{1}}.

Finally,

(\chi_{G_{1}})^{T}\Delta_{0}^{G}\chi_{G_{1}}=|E(G_{1},G_{2})|.

Therefore

(\chi_{H_{1}})^{T}\Delta_{0}^{H,G}\chi_{H_{1}}\leq|E(G_{1},G_{2})|.

Multiplying by
|H|/(|H_{1}|\cdot|H_{2}|)
gives
h_{\operatorname{Kron}}^{H,G}\leq h_{0}^{H,G}
.
∎

The Cheeger constants
h_{0}^{H,G}
and
h_{\operatorname{Kron}}^{H,G}
are in general not equivalent,
i.e.,
there is not a constant
c

8
Conclusion and outlook

In this paper, we have shown that several Cheeger constants for
simplicial complexes admit natural extensions to the persistent setting.
This raises the question of whether there is a more unified approach to
this problem. For an inclusion
\mathcal{K}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{L}
, there is no
Kron reduction for which
\Delta_{q,\mathrm{up}}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{L}}
coincides
with the (non-persistent) Laplacian of a simplicial complex. It is therefore
natural to ask whether such a replacement object can be found in a
broader category, for instance among
\Delta
-complexes or
CW-complexes. Cheeger inequalities proved in that setting would then
translate directly to the persistent setting studied in this paper.

Furthermore, we introduced the persistent
p
-Laplacian. Given the
prominence of
p
-Laplacians for graphs, we are currently exploring
their role in the persistent setting of simplicial complexes.

Lastly, it might be the case that the upper bounds of
Theorem

6.2
also apply in the non-orientable setting. We leave that to future research.

Facts from linear algebra

These facts are well-known and can be found in many textbooks on linear algebra or spectral graph theory.
The following
Courant–Fischer theorem
is well-known.

Theorem A.1
.

Let
A
be a symmetric positive semidefinite
n\times n
matrix with respect to
a given inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Denote

and assume

has at least one positive eigenvalue.
For
x\neq 0
, the Rayleigh quotient of
A
is

R_{A}(x):=\frac{\langle Ax,x\rangle}{\langle x,x\rangle}.

Then the smallest non-zero eigenvalue
\lambda_{2}
satisfies

\lambda_{2}=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\perp\ker A\\
x\neq 0\end{subarray}}R_{A}(x)=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\perp\ker A\\
x\neq 0\end{subarray}}\frac{\langle Ax,x\rangle}{\langle x,x\rangle}.

A non-zero vector
x\perp\ker A
attains this minimum if and only if it is an
eigenvector of
A
with eigenvalue
\lambda_{2}
.

Similar characterizations can be given for the higher eigenvalues, see, e.g.,
[
19
]
.

.

1.

Let

Totally unimodular matrices and linear programming

Definition B.1
.

We say a matrix
M
is totally unimodular (TU) if the determinant of every squared submatrix of
M
is equal to
1
,
-1
or
0
.

It can be proved that the incidence matrix with respect to a directed graph is totally unimodular,
This fact was first stated by Poincaré
[
33
]
;
see
[
35

.

Lemma B.1
.

Let
e_{i},e_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{|V|}
be two standard vectors,
and define the operator
I^{+}:\mathrm{im}(I)\to\mathbb{R}^{|E|}
as:

I^{+}:w\mapsto\argmin_{x}\{\|x\|_{1}:Ix=w\}.

Note that this minimizer is characterized by the orthogonality condition
\|x+y\|_{1}\geq\|x\|_{1}
for any
y\in\ker(I)
.
We then have that

I^{+}(e_{i}-e_{j})\in\{-1,0,1\}^{|E|},

and
\|I^{+}(e_{i}-e_{j})\|_{1}
is equal to the length of the shortest path between
i,j\in V
.

Proof.

We observe that computing
I^{+}(e_{i}-e_{j})
is equivalent to solving the optimization problem:

\min_{x}\|x\|_{1}\quad\text{subject to}\quad Ix=e_{i}-e_{j}.

If we depart
x=x^{+}-x^{-}
with
x^{+},x^{-}\geq 0
,
this optimization problem is equivalent to the following Linear Program (LP):

\min\mathbf{1}^{T}x^{+}+\mathbf{1}^{T}x^{-}\quad\text{subject to}\quad\begin{bmatrix}I&-I\\
\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x^{+}\\
x^{-}\end{bmatrix}=e_{i}-e_{j}.

(22)

Since
I
is TU (
[
35
]
),
it is straightforward to verify that the block matrix

\begin{bmatrix}I&-I\end{bmatrix}
is TU as well.
According to Hoffman-Kruskal theorem
[
35
, Corollary 19.2a]
,
the optimal solution to
Eq.

22
is integral,
i.e.,

\begin{bmatrix}x^{+}\\
x^{-}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2\times|E|}.

Thus,
the minimizer
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{|E|}
is an integral flow.

We now prove that
x\in\{-1,0,1\}^{|E|}
.
According to the Flow Decomposition Theorem
[
1

, the integral flow
x
satisfying
Ix=e_{i}-e_{j}
can be decomposed into exactly one simple path
p
from
i
to
j
,
plus a set of simple closed cycles
C
:

x=p+\sum_{c\in C}c,

where
p\in\{-1,0,1\}^{|E|}
and the cycles satisfy
\sum\limits_{c\in C}c\in\ker(I)
.
Since
x
was chosen to minimize the
\ell^{1}
-norm,
the set of cycles
C
must be empty.
If
C
were non-empty,
removing the cycles would yield the flow
p
,
which still satisfies

but has a strictly smaller norm (

),
contradicting the minimality of

.
Therefore,

and

Consequently,

,
and its

\ell^{1}
-norm,

must be the shortest path between
i
and
j
.
∎

X
is positive, and let
S
achieve
h_{\mathrm{vol}}^{\mathrm{Bdy}}
. Taking
g=\chi_{S}
(the indicator vector on
S
) and
x=W^{1/2}g
, we get

\frac{x^{T}Mx}{\|x\|^{2}}=\frac{\operatorname{Cut}(S)+\operatorname{Bdy}(S)}{\operatorname{Vol}(S)}=h_{\mathrm{vol}}^{\mathrm{Bdy}},

so
\lambda_{\min}(M)\leq h_{\mathrm{vol}}^{\mathrm{Bdy}}
by
Theorem

A.1
noting that
\ker M=0
.

Now suppose that all row sums of
X
vanish. Let
S
achieve
h_{\mathrm{vol}}
, with
\operatorname{Vol}(S)\leq V_{\mathrm{tot}}/2
. Set
\alpha=\operatorname{Vol}(S)/V_{\mathrm{tot}}
, and define

g_{i}=\begin{cases}1-\alpha,&i\in S,\\
-\alpha,&i\notin S.\end{cases}

Then
x=W^{1/2}g
is orthogonal to
\ker M=W^{1/2}\mathbf{1}
. Since
b_{i}=0
for all
i
, we have

x^{T}Mx=\operatorname{Cut}(S),

while

\|x\|^{2}=\operatorname{Vol}(S)(1-\alpha)^{2}+(V_{\mathrm{tot}}-\operatorname{Vol}(S))\alpha^{2}=\frac{\operatorname{Vol}(S)(V_{\mathrm{tot}}-\operatorname{Vol}(S))}{V_{\mathrm{tot}}}.

Since
\operatorname{Vol}(S)\leq V_{\mathrm{tot}}/2
, we have
\|x\|^{2}\geq\operatorname{Vol}(S)/2
. Hence

\lambda_{1}(M)\leq\frac{x^{T}Mx}{\|x\|^{2}}\leq 2\frac{\operatorname{Cut}(S)}{\operatorname{Vol}(S)}=2h_{\mathrm{vol}},

by
Theorem

A.1
.

Lower bounds.

We now prove the lower bounds.

(1) Dirichlet case.

Assume that at least one row sum of
X
is positive, i.e.
M
is positive definite and
\ker M=0
.

Let
x
be an eigenvector for
\lambda_{\min}(M)
, let
g=W^{-1/2}x
, and set
h_{i}=g_{i}^{2}
. Introduce an auxiliary “ground” vertex, indexed by
0
, with fixed value
\widetilde{g}_{0}=0
. For
i\geq 1
, set
\widetilde{g}_{i}=g_{i}
and
\widetilde{h}_{i}=\widetilde{g}_{i}^{2}
, while
\widetilde{h}_{0}=0
. Define augmented weights by
\widetilde{w}_{ij}=w_{ij}
for
i,j\geq 1
, and
\widetilde{w}_{i0}=b_{i}
. Then

x^{T}Mx=\sum_{i<j}w_{ij}(g_{i}-g_{j})^{2}+\sum_{i}b_{i}(g_{i}-\widetilde{g}_{0})^{2}=\sum_{0\leq i<j\leq m}\widetilde{w}_{ij}(\widetilde{g}_{i}-\widetilde{g}_{j})^{2}.

Moreover,

|\widetilde{h}_{i}-\widetilde{h}_{j}|=|\widetilde{g}_{i}^{2}-\widetilde{g}_{j}^{2}|=|\widetilde{g}_{i}-\widetilde{g}_{j}|\,|\widetilde{g}_{i}+\widetilde{g}_{j}|.

Hence, applying Cauchy–Schwarz to
a_{ij}:=\sqrt{\widetilde{w}_{ij}}|\widetilde{g}_{i}-\widetilde{g}_{j}|
and
c_{ij}:=\sqrt{\widetilde{w}_{ij}}|\widetilde{g}_{i}+\widetilde{g}_{j}|
, we obtain

\left(\sum_{0\leq i<j\leq m}\widetilde{w}_{ij}|\widetilde{h}_{i}-\widetilde{h}_{j}|\right)^{2}\leq\left(\sum_{0\leq i<j\leq m}\widetilde{w}_{ij}(\widetilde{g}_{i}-\widetilde{g}_{j})^{2}\right)\left(\sum_{0\leq i<j\leq m}\widetilde{w}_{ij}(\widetilde{g}_{i}+\widetilde{g}_{j})^{2}\right),

and therefore

x^{T}Mx\geq\frac{\left(\sum_{0\leq i<j\leq m}\widetilde{w}_{ij}|\widetilde{h}_{i}-\widetilde{h}_{j}|\right)^{2}}{\sum_{0\leq i<j\leq m}\widetilde{w}_{ij}(\widetilde{g}_{i}+\widetilde{g}_{j})^{2}}.

The denominator is bounded by

\sum_{0\leq i<j\leq m}\widetilde{w}_{ij}(\widetilde{g}_{i}+\widetilde{g}_{j})^{2}\leq 2\sum_{0\leq i<j\leq m}\widetilde{w}_{ij}(\widetilde{g}_{i}^{2}+\widetilde{g}_{j}^{2})=2\sum_{0\leq i\leq m}\left(\sum_{j\neq i,0}w_{ij}+b_{i}\right)g_{i}^{2}=2\sum_{0\leq i\leq m}X_{ii}g_{i}^{2}\leq 2\Delta\|x\|^{2}.

For
t>0
, let
S_{t}:=\{i:h_{i}>t\}
. By the “layer-cake formula”,

\sum_{0\leq i<j\leq m}\widetilde{w}_{ij}|\widetilde{h}_{i}-\widetilde{h}_{j}|=\int_{0}^{\infty}\sum_{0\leq i<j\leq m}\widetilde{w}_{ij}|\chi_{S_{t}}(i)-\chi_{S_{t}}(j)|\,dt.

where
\chi_{A}
denote the indicator vector on
A
.

Since the vertex
0
is never in
S_{t}
, the integrand is
\operatorname{Cut}(S_{t})+\operatorname{Bdy}(S_{t})
. Thus

\sum_{0\leq i<j\leq m}\widetilde{w}_{ij}|\widetilde{h}_{i}-\widetilde{h}_{j}|=\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\operatorname{Cut}(S_{t})+\operatorname{Bdy}(S_{t})\right)\,dt\geq h_{\mathrm{vol}}^{\mathrm{Bdy}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\operatorname{Vol}(S_{t})\,dt.

Finally,

\int_{0}^{\infty}\operatorname{Vol}(S_{t})\,dt=\sum_{i}\mu_{i}h_{i}=\sum_{i}\mu_{i}g_{i}^{2}=\|x\|^{2}.

Substituting the numerator and denominator estimates gives

x^{T}Mx\geq\frac{\left(h_{\mathrm{vol}}^{\mathrm{Bdy}}\|x\|^{2}\right)^{2}}{2\Delta\|x\|^{2}}=\frac{(h_{\mathrm{vol}}^{\mathrm{Bdy}})^{2}}{2\Delta}\|x\|^{2}.

Dividing by
\|x\|^{2}
gives
\lambda_{\min}(M)\geq(h_{\mathrm{vol}}^{\mathrm{Bdy}})^{2}/(2\Delta)
.

(2) Boundaryless case.

Assume that all row sums of
X
vanish. Then
b_{i}=0
for all
i
, and

x^{T}Mx=\sum_{i<j}w_{ij}(g_{i}-g_{j})^{2}.

Since the interior graph associated with
X
is connected,
\ker M=\operatorname{span}(W^{1/2}\mathbf{1})
. Let
x\perp W^{1/2}\mathbf{1}
be an eigenvector for
\lambda_{1}(M)
, let
g=W^{-1/2}x
, and set
h_{i}=g_{i}^{2}
. The orthogonality condition is
\sum_{i}\mu_{i}g_{i}=0
. Thus
g
has both nonnegative and nonpositive parts. Replacing
g
by
-g
if necessary, we may assume that its positive support
S_{+}:=\{i:g_{i}>0\}
satisfies
\operatorname{Vol}(S_{+})\leq V_{\mathrm{tot}}/2
. Define
g_{i}^{+}:=\max(g_{i},0)
. Edgewise,
|g_{i}^{+}-g_{j}^{+}|\leq|g_{i}-g_{j}|
, so the energy of
g^{+}
is at most the energy of
g
. Thus it suffices to prove the desired lower bound for a nonnegative function
g
whose support
S_{+}
satisfies
\operatorname{Vol}(S_{+})\leq V_{\mathrm{tot}}/2
.

For such a nonnegative
g
, we use

|h_{i}-h_{j}|=|g_{i}^{2}-g_{j}^{2}|=|g_{i}-g_{j}|\,|g_{i}+g_{j}|.

By Cauchy–Schwarz,

\left(\sum_{i<j}w_{ij}|h_{i}-h_{j}|\right)^{2}\leq\left(\sum_{i<j}w_{ij}(g_{i}-g_{j})^{2}\right)\left(\sum_{i<j}w_{ij}(g_{i}+g_{j})^{2}\right).

Thus

x^{T}Mx\geq\frac{\left(\sum_{i<j}w_{ij}|h_{i}-h_{j}|\right)^{2}}{\sum_{i<j}w_{ij}(g_{i}+g_{j})^{2}}.

The denominator is bounded by

\sum_{i<j}w_{ij}(g_{i}+g_{j})^{2}\leq 2\sum_{i<j}w_{ij}(g_{i}^{2}+g_{j}^{2})=2\sum_{i}\left(\sum_{j\neq i}w_{ij}\right)g_{i}^{2}=2\sum_{i}X_{ii}g_{i}^{2}\leq 2\Delta\|x\|^{2}.

For
t>0
, let
S_{t}:=\{i:h_{i}>t\}
. Since
S_{t}\subseteq S_{+}
, we have
\operatorname{Vol}(S_{t})\leq V_{\mathrm{tot}}/2
. By the layer-cake formula,

\sum_{i<j}w_{ij}|h_{i}-h_{j}|=\int_{0}^{\infty}\sum_{i<j}w_{ij}|\mathbf{1}_{S_{t}}(i)-\mathbf{1}_{S_{t}}(j)|\,dt=\int_{0}^{\infty}\operatorname{Cut}(S_{t})\,dt.

By the definition of
h_{\mathrm{vol}}
,

\int_{0}^{\infty}\operatorname{Cut}(S_{t})\,dt\geq h_{\mathrm{vol}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\operatorname{Vol}(S_{t})\,dt=h_{\mathrm{vol}}\sum_{i}\mu_{i}h_{i}=h_{\mathrm{vol}}\|x\|^{2}.

Combining the numerator and denominator estimates gives

x^{T}Mx\geq\frac{(h_{\mathrm{vol}}\|x\|^{2})^{2}}{2\Delta\|x\|^{2}}=\frac{h_{\mathrm{vol}}^{2}}{2\Delta}\|x\|^{2}.

Dividing by
\|x\|^{2}
gives
\lambda_{1}(M)\geq h_{\mathrm{vol}}^{2}/(2\Delta)
.
∎

Lemma C.1

Let

be an undirected graph with adjacency matrix
A

A_{ij}=1
if
\{i,j\}\in E

A_{ij}=0
otherwise. Let
D
be the diagonal degree matrix,
D_{ii}=\sum_{j}A_{ij}
, and let
\Delta_{0}^{G}=L:=D-A

,

\|\Delta_{0}^{G}\chi_{S}\|_{1}=2\chi_{S}^{T}\Delta_{0}^{G}\chi_{S}=2|E(S,S^{c})|.

(L\chi_{S})_{i}=\begin{cases}\sum_{j\notin S}A_{ij},&i\in S,\\
