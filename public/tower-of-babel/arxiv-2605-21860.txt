Robust Statistical Estimators with Bounded Empirical Sensitivity Thanks: Authors are listed in alphabetical order.
Valentio Iverson, Gautam Kamath, Argyris Mouzakis, Adam Smith

Abstract
We introduce a new measure of robustness for statistical estimators, which we call empirical sensitivity . An estimator θ ^ \hat{\theta} has bounded empirical sensitivity if, with high probability over a dataset X = ( X 1 , … , X n ) ∼ 𝒟 ⊗ n X=(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})\sim\mathcal{D}^{\otimes n} , for any dataset Y Y obtained by modifying at most η ​ n \eta n points in X X , we have that θ ^ ​ ( Y ) \hat{\theta}(Y) is close to θ ^ ​ ( X ) \hat{\theta}(X) . We study bounds on this quantity for the prototypical problem of Gaussian mean estimation. We prove new lower bounds, showing that for any estimator μ ^ \hat{\mu} which achieves an optimal ℓ 2 \ell_{2} -error bound of O ⁡ ( d / n ) O(\sqrt{d/n}) , the empirical sensitivity is at least Ω ⁡ ( η + η ​ d / n ) \Omega(\eta+\sqrt{\eta d/n}) . The two terms arise due to obstructions on the mean and variance (via an Efron-Stein argument) of such an estimator. We show that this bound is tight up to logarithmic factors, by employing recent results for robust empirical mean estimation.



1
Introduction

Robustness is a classic topic of study within Statistics
[
17
]
.
It tries to understand and bound how much an estimator can fluctuate when data diverges from our assumptions.
Such divergences could arise for innocuous reasons such as model misspecification, or more malicious reasons like an adversary manipulating or poisoning the dataset.

A popular style of robustness ensures that an estimator maintains accuracy after an adversary modifies a portion of the input dataset.
In more detail, suppose we have an estimator
\hat{\theta}
for a parameter
\theta^{*}
of some family of distributions
\mathcal{D}_{\theta}
.
Let
X=(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})\sim\mathcal{D}_{\theta^{*}}
, and let
Y
be any dataset obtained by modifying
\eta n
points in
X
.
Robustness of the estimator
\hat{\theta}
is then measured in terms of
\|\hat{\theta}(Y)-\theta^{*}\|
, the distance between the estimator on the contaminated dataset and the true parameter of interest.

While algorithms satisfying this definition are quite powerful, the notion does not capture some natural properties we might hope for an estimator to possess.
We illustrate by considering the estimator
\hat{\mu}
to be the median, and the dataset
X
to be sampled from a univariate Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}(\mu,1)^{\otimes n}
.
For an uncontaminated dataset
X
, with high probability, the median is well known to achieve the near optimal statistical rate:
|\hat{\mu}(X)-\mu|\leq O(1/\sqrt{n})
.
Furthermore, the median is the canonical example of a robust statistic.
For any dataset
Y
, formed by changing
\eta n
entries of
X
, we have that
|\hat{\mu}(Y)-\mu|\leq O(\eta+1/\sqrt{n})
—a mild (but inherent) degradation of the rate from the uncontaminated case.
However, we consider how much the estimator can shift due to contamination.
For simplicity, focus on the case when
\eta=1/n
, i.e., a single point is contaminated.
The aforementioned robustness guarantee allows us only to say that
|\hat{\mu}(Y)-\hat{\mu}(X)|\leq O(1/n+1/\sqrt{n})
.
This is a rather large radius for only a single contamination, with the dominant term being the parametric rate of
1/\sqrt{n}
.
In fact, as we show in Section
B
, the median enjoys a quadratically stronger guarantee on this quantity:
|\hat{\mu}(Y)-\hat{\mu}(X)|\leq O(1/n)
.
To summarize, the conventional measures of robustness may not give insight into how
sensitive
the estimator may be.

Motivated by this deficiency, we introduce a new measure of robustness for statistical estimators, which we call the
empirical sensitivity
.
Informally, we would like for an estimator
\hat{\theta}
to simultaneously have the following two properties.

•

Accuracy: Given a dataset
X\sim\mathcal{D}_{\theta^{*}}
, we have that
\|\hat{\theta}(X)-\theta^{*}\|
is small.

•

Bounded empirical sensitivity:
For
X\sim\mathcal{D}_{\theta}
, and for any dataset
Y
obtained by modifying
\eta n
entries of
X
, we have that
\|\hat{\theta}(Y)-\hat{\theta}(X)\|
is small—either in expectation over
X
or with high probability.

Similar to traditional robustness, we focus on the case where the dataset
X
is drawn stochastically, but
Y
is an arbitrary or worst-case contamination of
X
.
As usual, obtainable guarantees will depend on properties of the class of distributions
\mathcal{D}_{\theta}
.
On the other hand, this can be seen as a
stronger
type of robustness: by triangle inequality, an accurate estimator with bounded empirical sensitivity will also enjoy a bound on
\|\hat{\theta}(Y)-\theta^{*}\|
, the traditional object of study in robust estimation.

The robustness of the estimated value to corruption has been considered before as a
worst-case
constraint on a function, notably in the design of differentially private algorithms. To our knowledge, ours is the first work that studies the interaction of empirical sensitivity with statistical efficiency, and also the first to explicitly study the average-case notion. See Related Work (
Section

1.2
) for more detail.

1.1
Results and Techniques

We initiate study of empirical sensitivity focusing on one of the most fundamental statistical tasks:
Gaussian mean estimation
.
Our main result is the following lower bound on the empirical sensitivity of optimal estimators.

Theorem 1.1
.

Consider any estimator
\hat{\mu}
such that

\sup_{\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu,\mathbb{I}_{d})^{\otimes n}}\|\hat{\mu}(X)-\mu\|_{2}^{2}\lesssim\frac{d}{n}.

(1)

For every
\eta\in(0,1)
,
1

1

Throughout, it is implicit that
\eta\geq 1/n
, i.e., there is at least one contaminated point.
we have that

\sup_{\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu,\mathbb{I}_{d})^{\otimes n}}\left[\sup_{Y\colon d_{H}(X,Y)\leq\lfloor\eta n\rfloor}\|\hat{\mu}(Y)-\hat{\mu}(X)\|_{2}^{2}\right]\right)^{1/2}\geq\Omega\left(\eta+\sqrt{\frac{\eta d}{n}}\right).

This lower bound is tight up to logarithmic factors.
Very recent work by Chen, Ding, Majid, and McKelvie
[
11
]
studies robust
empirical
mean estimation.
An easy argument shows that their estimator satisfies near-optimal accuracy and also average-case
empirical sensitivity (Theorem
3.2

1.1
above.

We recall that, for robust Gaussian mean estimation, the optimal rate for
\|\hat{\mu}(X)-\mu\|_{2}
is
\tilde{\Theta}\left(\eta+\sqrt{\frac{d}{n}}\right)
.
By triangle inequality, this implies that one may have hoped for an empirical stability bound as small as
\tilde{\Theta}(\eta)
.
2
2

2

Note that triangle inequality only implies a lower bound of
\Omega(\eta)
in the regime where
\eta=\Omega\left(\sqrt{d/n}\right)

Indeed, proving the lower bound of
\eta

Instead, the larger bound of
\tilde{\Theta}\left(\eta+\sqrt{\frac{\eta d}{n}}\right)

Technically, the two terms in the lower bound are proven by showing obstructions on the mean and variance of the estimator.

The mean obstruction.

We first reduce a
d

\eta\lesssim\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}
, we compare a valid local corruption, which shifts
k
random samples, to a statistically indistinguishable global dataset corruption that the estimator is forced to track due to its accuracy guarantees. For
\eta\gtrsim\frac{\log n}{n}
, a coupling argument compares two samples from two nearby Gaussian means whose total variance distance is of order
\eta
, so that the coupled samples differ in at most
\lfloor\eta n\rfloor
coordinates with high probability. The two regimes overlap for large
n
, covering the full range of values for
\eta
.

In Section

The variance obstruction.

The
\sqrt{\eta d/n}
lower bound is driven by clean-sample variability. Indeed, we show that any uniformly MSE-accurate estimator must have output variance
\Omega(d/n)
at some parameter. Using this, a block-resampling argument and vector Efron-Stein inequality convert this clean output variance into sensitivity to replacing an
\eta

Related Work

[

and

As already described, empirical sensitivity is stronger than

One recent work of Chen, Ding, Majid, and McKelvie
[

studies differentially private Bayesian estimation, which they reduce to robust estimation of an empirical mean.
While not originally conceived as such, their robust algorithm provides an estimator with bounded empirical sensitivity for a particular statistic that satisfies the desired accuracy guarantee (i.e., the empirical mean).

Stability has long been an object of study in statistics and machine learning, see, e.g.,
[
21
,
8
]
.
This generally focuses on understanding how the error
of an algorithm changes if one removes a single point.
Our focus instead is on the effect of modifying a
collection
of points (and also on the entire output, not just the error).
The recent, contemporaneous work of Chakraborty, Luo, and Barber
[
9
]
explores minimax risk when an estimator must
have low

—that is,
worst-case

Empirical stability also relates to concepts considered in the literature on differential privacy (DP)
[
14
]
.
Many DP mechanisms operate by adding noise calibrated to the worst-case sensitivity (called the
global sensitivity
) of a function.
(This differs from our notion in that it is for worst-case neighboring datasets and considers only a single corruption.)
The literature also considers the
local sensitivity

[
25
]
: the sensitivity of the function at a particular dataset.
In particular, our definition can be viewed as an average-case bound on

s
(Definition 4.4 of the full version of
[

]
) for

.

A similar notion is the

at a data set
X
, employed in the inverse sensitivity mechanism
[
24
,
19
,
2
,
1
]
. This measures how many corruptions to
X
would be needed to change the value of an estimator from

to a given target value
z
.
Empirical sensitivity provides an average-case bound on this quantity. That connection was used implicitly in some works relating robustness to privacy, such as
[

,
3
]
.

Several other “local” variants of sensitivity have been considered, for example

[

(related to

Finally, the recent work of Trillos, Jaffe, and Sen

introduces a new notion of sensitivity, which measures change in a statistic when infinitesimal Gaussian noise is added to each datapoint.
Similar to our notion, it is an average-case (as opposed to worst-case) quantity, but the different contamination models appear to capture different phenomena.

Problem Setup and Definitions

General Notation.

We use the notation
A\lesssim B
to mean that
A\leq CB
for a universal constant
C>0
, and
A\gtrsim B
analogously. We write
A\asymp B
if both
A\lesssim B
and
B\lesssim A
hold. We also note that
\widetilde{O}(\cdot)
suppresses polylogarithmic factors in the relevant problem parameters. Throughout the whole of this work, we assume that
d,n\geq 1
are fixed, while all norms are the standard Euclidean norms.

For two datasets
x,x^{\prime}\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{n}
, let
d_{H}(x,x^{\prime})\coloneqq\#\{i\in[n]\colon x_{i}\neq x_{i}^{\prime}\}
denote the Hamming distance at the sample level. Thus,
d_{H}(x,x^{\prime})\leq k
means that
x^{\prime}
can be obtained from
x
by replacing at most
k
samples by arbitrary vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Given a sample space
\mathcal{X}
, we denote the set of all distributions over
\mathcal{X}
by
\Delta(\mathcal{X})
. For any distribution
\mathcal{D}\in\Delta(\mathcal{X})
,
\mathcal{D}^{\otimes n}
denotes the product distribution over
\mathcal{X}^{n}
, where each marginal is
\mathcal{D}
. Given a dataset
X=(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
drawn i.i.d. from
\mathcal{D}
, we write
X\sim\mathcal{D}^{\otimes n}
. Whenever using the symbols of probability and expectation, we use a subscript to denote what the randomness is over in cases where it might not be clear from the context, e.g.,
\mathbb{E}_{X}[\cdot]
.

For
\eta\in(0,1)
, we consider the
\eta
-corruption model where the adversary is allowed to contaminate any of
k\coloneqq\lfloor\eta n\rfloor
points from the given
n
sample points.

Definition 2.1

(Uniform MSE Accuracy)
.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be a distribution class parameterized by
\theta\in\Theta
, and let
D_{\theta}\in\mathcal{D}
.
We assume that
f
is an estimator that takes
n
samples drawn i.i.d. from
D_{\theta}
and outputs an estimate of
\theta
.
We say that
f
is
(C_{\mathsf{MSE}},\gamma)
-uniformly MSE-accurate
if

\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathbb{E}_{X\sim D_{\theta}^{\otimes n}}\left[\left\lVert f(X)-\theta\right\rVert^{2}\right]\leq C_{\mathsf{MSE}}\gamma.

(2)

In the above definition, the error rate includes two parameters. These are
C_{\mathsf{MSE}}
and
\gamma
. To give a bit more context about this,
C_{\mathsf{MSE}}
should be thought of as an absolute constant, whereas
\gamma
should be treated as a component of the rate that may depend on various aspects of the class of distributions under consideration (e.g., the dimension of the data), as well as the number of samples used by our estimator.

Definition 2.2

(Pointwise empirical sensitivity
[
25
]
)
.

For
\eta\in(0,1)
, we define the
\eta
-corruption pointwise empirical sensitivity of an estimator
f
at dataset
X
as:

S_{\eta}^{f}(X)\coloneqq\sup_{X^{\prime}\colon d_{H}(X,X^{\prime})\leq\lfloor\eta n\rfloor}\left\lVert f(X)-f(X^{\prime})\right\rVert.

The pointwise definition of empirical sensitivity was considered previously, under the name
local sensitivity at step size
\lfloor\eta n\rfloor
.

Definition 2.3

(Distributional empirical sensitivity)
.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be a family of distributions parameterized by
\theta\in\Theta
.
For
D_{\theta}\in\mathcal{D},q\in\{1,2\}
, and
\eta\in(0,1)
, we define the
\eta
-corruption
L^{q}
-distributional empirical sensitivity of an estimator
f
at distribution
D_{\theta}
as

\mathsf{ES}_{\eta,q}(f;\theta)\coloneqq\biggl(\mathbb{E}_{X\sim D_{\theta}^{\otimes n}}\bigl[(S_{\eta}^{f}(X))^{q}\bigr]\biggr)^{1/q}.

Thus
\mathsf{ES}_{\eta,1}
is expected pointwise sensitivity and
\mathsf{ES}_{\eta,2}
is root-mean-square pointwise sensitivity.

2.1
Adversary Models

The robustness literature frequently studies the relative power of different adversaries
[
6
,
7
,
5
,
4
,
23
]
.
We briefly discuss various adversary models, listed here in increasing amount of strength.

Definition 2.4
.

Let a dataset
X=(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})\sim\mathcal{D}^{\otimes n}
, where
\mathcal{D}
is a probability distribution.
Fix a corruption fraction
\eta\in(0,1)
and define
k\coloneqq\lfloor\eta n\rfloor
to be the number of contaminated points.
4
4

4

One could consider broader classes of adversaries.
For example, an adversary who adaptively chooses
k
points adversarially, and then resamples them obliviously. Or an adversary who obliviously chooses
k
points and then replaces them arbitrarily. We omit discussion of such adversaries to streamline the presentation.

1.

Resampling adversary
. A subset
I\subseteq[n]
of size
k
is chosen uniformly at random, and the samples
\{X_{i}\colon i\in I\}
are replaced by fresh independent samples from the same clean distribution as
X
.
This is the weakest adversary: both the removed points and the inserted points are stochastic and drawn from the clean distribution.

2.

Stochastic adversary
. A subset
I\subseteq[n]
of size
k
is chosen uniformly at random, and the samples
\{X_{i}\colon i\in I\}
are replaced by independent samples from a fixed replacement distribution
\mathcal{Q}
chosen before seeing
X
. The replacement distribution may differ from the clean distribution, but it is not chosen adaptively after observing the realized dataset.

3.

Adaptive adversary
. After observing the clean dataset
X
, the adversary may choose both the corrupted indices and the replacement values arbitrarily, subject only to the constraint
d_{H}(X,Y)\leq k
. This is the adversary encoded by
S_{\eta}^{f}(X)
.

Our discussion thus far has focused on the adaptive adversary.
This is reflected in our main result.
The result focuses on the class of Gaussian distributions with identity covariance
\{\mathcal{N}(\mu,\mathbb{I}_{d})\}_{\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}
, and shows a lower bound on

\sup_{\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathsf{ES}_{\eta,2}(f;\mu)=\sup_{\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu,\mathbb{I}_{d})^{\otimes n}}\bigl[(S_{\eta}^{f}(X))^{2}\bigr]\right)^{1/2}.

The two terms in the lower bound of Theorem
1.1
come from different adversaries.
The variance-obstruction term of
\Omega(\sqrt{\eta d/n})
arises even for the weakest adversary, the resampling adversary.
On the other hand, our proof for the mean-obstruction term of
\Omega(\eta)
requires a stronger adversary. More precisely, the low-corruption proof uses a random-subset mean-shift adversary. While this mean-shift adversary and the standard stochastic adversary are technically distinct – as they induce different joint distributions between the clean and corrupted samples – they are identical in their marginal distributions. Because our argument relies solely on these marginal distributions, the proof applies directly to both the mean-shift and stochastic adversaries. By contrast, the high-corruption proof uses a coordinatewise TV-coupling adversary, where each sample is coupled independently and the corrupted indices are those coordinates on which the coupling fails; this is adaptive to the realized sample, but only coordinate-by-coordinate. As we show in Section
C
, more empirically stable estimators exist under the (weakest) resampling adversary.
In this case, the empirical mean achieves an empirical stability of
O(\sqrt{\eta d/n})
, avoiding the
\Omega(\eta)
term that is necessary under the adaptive adversary.

As the empirical sensitivity is a quantity measured with respect to a realized dataset, all our adversaries are defined as modifying a dataset, rather than a distribution (as is sometimes done in the conventional robustness literature).
Our adaptive adversary is the same as what is usually called the
strong contamination
model in the robustness literature
[
13
]
.
The stochastic adversary can be seen as the moral equivalent of Huber contamination
[
18
]
.
The resampling adversary has no equivalent, as, by construction, the underlying distribution is uncontaminated.

3
Main Results

In this section, we give the bulk of our results. We will be working with the class of Gaussian distributions with unknown mean
\mu
and known covariance matrix
\Sigma
which, without loss of generality, we assume to be equal to the identity matrix
\mathbb{I}
. We will consider mean estimators with bounded empirical sensitivity for this class, and give both upper and lower bounds.

The following theorem is the main result of this paper: a tight lower bound of the empirical sensitivity of accurate estimators, complemented by a recent upper bound result in
[
11
]
:

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
f\colon(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be measurable and
\left(C_{\mathsf{MSE}},\frac{d}{n}\right)
-uniformly MSE-accurate for the class of Gaussians
\{\mathcal{N}(\mu,\mathbb{I})\}_{\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}
. Then for every
\eta\in(0,1)
and all sufficiently large
n
depending only on
C_{\mathsf{MSE}}
, we have

\sup_{\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathsf{ES}_{\eta,2}(f;\mu)\geq\Omega_{C_{\mathsf{MSE}}}\left(\eta+\sqrt{\frac{\eta d}{n}}\right).

The lower bound comprises two independent components: a mean obstruction, which gives the
\eta
term, and a variance obstruction, which gives the
\sqrt{\eta d/n}
term. Each part captures the estimator’s vulnerability to a different adversarial mechanism. The mean obstruction reflects the estimator’s sensitivity to small shifts in the underlying distribution’s mean, whereas the variance obstruction isolates the estimator’s inherent variability when resampled from the same clean distribution.

3.1
Upper bound from robust empirical-mean recovery

Our lower bound is complemented by a high-probability upper bound, which is a sensitivity consequence of an estimator that remains close to the clean empirical mean simultaneously over all allowed corruptions.
The following is a consequence of a result of Chen, Ding, Majid, and McKelvie
[
11
]
.

Theorem 3.2
.

Fix
\eta\in(0,1/3)
and
\beta\in(0,1/2)
, there exists a measurable estimator
f_{\eta,\beta}:(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that, for every
\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with probability at least
1-\beta
over
X\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu,\mathbb{I}_{d})^{\otimes n}
, both of the following hold:

\displaystyle\|f_{\eta,\beta}(X)-\mu\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq O\left(\frac{d+\log(1/\beta)}{n}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad S_{\eta}^{f_{\eta,\beta}}(X)\leq\widetilde{O}\left(\eta+\sqrt{\eta\cdot\frac{d+\log(1/\beta)}{n}}\right)

Proof sketch.

The primitive from
[
11
]
gives a high-probability event on which, simultaneously for every
\eta
-corruption
X^{\prime}
of the clean sample
X
, every feasible cleaned candidate has empirical mean close to the empirical mean
\overline{X}
. We modify the estimator so that, on clean input, it chooses the feasible candidate with minimum Hamming distance to the input. Therefore, one can show that on the same good event,
f_{\eta,\beta}(X)=\overline{X}
. The complete proof can be found in
Appendix

E
.

\blacksquare

Remark 3.3

(Efficient versus inefficient upper bounds)
.

While the estimator of Theorem
3.2
should be viewed as the statistically sharp upper bound needed to match our lower bound up to logarithmic factors, it is not computationally efficient.
The same work also gives an efficient estimator with a worse empirical-mean recovery rate.
Applying the same triangle-inequality argument as above yields an efficient estimator for our empirical-sensitivity problem with sensitivity of order
\widetilde{O}\left(\eta+\sqrt{\eta\cdot\sqrt{\frac{d}{n}}}\right)
with high probability, together with clean-data MSE accuracy.

Their efficient estimator is complemented by a computational lower bound against restricted class of algorithms.
Informally, they show that one can not do better than this weaker
O\left(\sqrt{\eta\sqrt{\frac{d}{n}}}\right)
rate with any algorithm implemented by low-degree polynomials.
We note that this does
not
imply a computational lower bound for our setting, as their setting is specific to the empirical mean, whereas the problem we are considering allows any estimator which is MSE accurate.

It remains open whether efficient, uniformly accurate estimators can achieve the statistically optimal empirical-sensitivity scale
\eta+\sqrt{\frac{\eta d}{n}}
, or whether the worse efficient rate
\eta+\sqrt{\eta\sqrt{\frac{d}{n}}}
is inherent for computationally-efficient estimators under our empirical sensitivity condition.

3.2
Mean obstruction

We establish the mean obstruction by separating the analysis into two distinct regimes that collectively cover the entire spectrum of corruption levels. In the low-corruption regime, where
\eta=O(1/\sqrt{n})
, our argument relies on bounding the
\chi^{2}
-divergence between two distinct adversaries: a local shift of exactly
k
random samples by a constant
\delta
(which represents a valid adversarial
\eta
-contamination), and a global shift of all samples by
\eta\delta
(which represents a genuine change in the underlying parameter). Because the
\chi^{2}
-divergence between these two distributions scales as
O(k^{2}\delta^{4}/n)
, choosing a sufficiently small
\delta
renders the adversarial contamination statistically indistinguishable from a true parameter shift. Since any MSE-accurate estimator is mathematically forced to track the global shift to maintain its low average risk, this indistinguishability ensures the estimator is similarly displaced by the local corruption, yielding the
\Omega(\eta)
lower bound.

For the high-corruption regime, where
\eta=\Omega(\log n/n)
, we shift to a coordinate-wise maximal coupling argument. We couple two Gaussian distributions whose means are separated by exactly
\eta
. By showing that samples drawn from these coupled distributions differ in at most
k
coordinates with high probability in this regime, we show that an
\eta
-bounded adversary can seamlessly bridge the two datasets. Since the estimator’s accuracy forces its mean response to be displaced by
\Omega(\eta)
between these two parameter values, the empirical sensitivity is similarly bounded below. Crucially, these two regimes are not isolated; they overlap for all sufficiently large
n
, ensuring that our combined lower bound fully covers the entire spectrum of corruption allowances without any gaps.

To prove both high-dimensional theorems, we reduce them to the single-dimensional case using a projection and conditioning lemma. Informally, any uniformly MSE-accurate estimator
f\colon(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
can be projected along a carefully chosen direction to yield a one-dimensional estimator
g\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
with parameter
\mu^{\prime}
that inherits the
O(1/n)
Bayes risk, allowing us to lower-bound the high-dimensional sensitivity
\mathsf{ES}_{\eta,2}(f;\mu)
via the scalar empirical sensitivity
S_{\eta}^{g}(X)
.

Theorem 3.4

(Mean obstruction: low-corruption regime)
.

Let
f\colon(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be measurable and
\left(C_{\mathsf{MSE}},\frac{d}{n}\right)
-uniformly MSE-accurate for the class of Gaussians
\{\mathcal{N}(\mu,\mathbb{I})\}_{\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}
. There exist constants
\kappa=\kappa(C_{\mathsf{MSE}})>0
and
C_{\ref{const:meanlowLower}}=C_{\ref{const:meanlowLower}}(C_{\mathsf{MSE}})>0
such that, for every
\eta\in(0,1)
such that
1\leq\lfloor\eta n\rfloor\leq\kappa\sqrt{n}
and for all sufficiently large
n
, we have

\sup_{\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathsf{ES}_{\eta,2}(f;\mu)\geq C_{\ref{const:meanlowLower}}\eta.

Proof sketch.

We establish the low-corruption lower bound through an indistinguishability argument between two adversaries. As previously stated, we may assume that
\mu^{\prime}\in[0,1]
and thus it suffices to analyze a clipped scalar estimator
h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to[0,1]
.

1.

First, because the estimator inherits a low
O(1/n)
integrated risk under a smooth prior, its expected output is forced to accurately track the true parameter. Specifically, if the entire data distribution is subjected to a global mean shift of
\eta\delta

3.3
Variance obstruction

The variance obstruction is rooted in a fundamental statistical trade-off: any estimator achieving uniform MSE accuracy must exhibit an output variance of order
\Omega(d/n)
at some parameter point. An adversary can thus exploit this inherent variability, leveraging the estimator’s own structural fluctuations to induce empirical sensitivity. This is made precise by the following theorem.

Theorem 3.6

(Variance obstruction)
.

Let
f\colon(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be measurable and
\left(C_{\mathsf{MSE}},\frac{d}{n}\right)
-uniformly MSE-accurate for the class of Gaussians
\{\mathcal{N}(\mu,\mathbb{I})\}_{\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}
. There is a universal constant
C_{\ref{const:varLower}}>0
such that for every
\eta\in(0,1)
, we have

\sup_{\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathsf{ES}_{\eta,2}(f;\mu)\geq C_{\ref{const:varLower}}\sqrt{\frac{\eta d}{n}}.

Proof sketch.

The proof of the variance obstruction proceeds in three stages: establishing a scalar variance bound, lifting it to high dimensions, and converting variance into empirical sensitivity.

1.

First, we show that any one-dimensional uniformly MSE-accurate estimator must have output variance
\Omega(1/n)
at some parameter. Assume that the one-dimensional estimator is
g
with parameter
\mu^{\prime}
. Let
a(\mu^{\prime})\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{X}[g(X)]
. Uniform MSE accuracy thus implies
|a(\mu^{\prime})-\mu^{\prime}|\lesssim n^{-1/2}
. We consider an interval of large length which we assume the parameter
\mu^{\prime}
lies in, and we partition this interval such that each part is roughly of size
n^{-1/2}
, i.e.,
\mu^{\prime}_{j}=jn^{-1/2}
. Then
a(\mu^{\prime})
must move by a constant fraction of the interval length across the grid. For adjacent grid points, the two distributions
\mathcal{N}(\mu^{\prime}_{j},1)^{\otimes n}
and
\mathcal{N}(\mu^{\prime}_{j+1},1)^{\otimes n}
have bounded
\chi^{2}
-divergence. One can then use Hammersley-Chapman-Robbins inequality (
Theorem

A.4
) to get

\mathrm{Var}_{\mu^{\prime}_{j}}(g)\gtrsim(a(\mu^{\prime}_{j+1})-a(\mu^{\prime}_{j}))^{2}.

Summing over
j
and applying Cauchy-Schwarz forces the average scalar variance over the grid to be
\Omega(1/n)
.

2.

Second, we lift this scalar variance lower bound to
d
dimensions by applying this one-dimensional finite-difference argument along each coordinate direction, with all other mean coordinates fixed on a common finite grid. Doing this give us some parameter
\mu^{\ast}
such that
\mathrm{Var}_{\mu^{\ast}}(f(X))\gtrsim\frac{d}{n}
.

3.

Finally, we convert this clean-sample variance bound into empirical sensitivity by block resampling. Work on parameter
\mu^{\ast}
. Partition the sample into blocks of size
k\coloneqq\lfloor\eta n\rfloor
. Let
X^{(i)}
be obtained from
X
by replacing the
i
th block by an independent fresh block from the same distribution
\mathcal{N}(\mu^{\ast},\mathbb{I}_{d})
. Each
X^{(i)}
differs from
X
in at most
k
samples, and so pointwise

S_{\eta}^{f}(X)\geq\|f(X^{(i)})-f(X)\|.

Using Vector Efron-Stein Inequality (
Theorem

A.3
) would gives us
\mathsf{ES}_{\eta,2}(f;\mu^{\ast})\gtrsim\sqrt{\frac{\eta d}{n}}
, as desired.

The complete proof can be found in
Appendix

H
.

\blacksquare

4
Related Bernoulli Results

The same empirical-sensitivity question can be asked for Bernoulli mean estimation. We record a simple one-dimensional analogue of the mean obstruction.

Indeed, for
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, we let
|x|\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}
denote its Hamming weight. For a scalar estimator
f\colon\{0,1\}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
, define

S_{\eta}^{f}(x)\coloneqq\sup_{y\in\{0,1\}^{n}\colon d_{H}(x,y)\leq\lfloor\eta n\rfloor}|f(y)-f(x)|.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
f\colon\{0,1\}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be measurable and suppose that

\sup_{p\in[0,1]}\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mathsf{Bern}(p)^{\otimes n}}|f(X)-p|\leq\frac{C_{\mathsf{Bern}}}{\sqrt{n}}.

Then for every
\eta\in(0,1)
such that
k=\lfloor\eta n\rfloor\geq 1
, and for all sufficiently large
n
depending on
C_{\mathsf{Bern}}
, we have

\sup_{p\in[0,1]}\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mathsf{Bern}(p)^{\otimes n}}[S_{\eta}^{f}(X)]\geq C_{\ref{const:bernoulli}}\eta.

Proof sketch.

We will show the empirical sensitivity lower bound through a layer-telescoping style argument on the Boolean hypercube. As shown previously, clipping preserves accuracy and only decreases sensitivity, so it suffices to analyze a bounded scalar estimator
f\colon\{0,1\}^{n}\to[0,1]
.

1.

We exploit the estimator’s required accuracy. By decomposing the uniform prior on the Bernoulli parameter into discrete Hamming-weight layers, we can evaluate the estimator’s expected output conditionally per layer. Because
f
maintains
O(1/\sqrt{n})
expected risk, binomial anti-concentration bounds guarantee that its expected output at widely separated boundary layers must closely track the true proportion of ones. This forces an
\Omega(\eta)
gap in the estimator’s mean between these boundaries.

2.

We then distribute this global gap to local neighborhoods using a transport coupling. We couple elements between layers separated by a distance
\ell=\Theta(\eta n)
by uniformly flipping exactly
\ell
zero coordinates to ones, ensuring the coupled instances are strictly within the allowed Hamming radius
\lfloor\eta n\rfloor
. This then shows that the required boundary gap must be bridged by significant intermediate jumps. Averaging this forced variation across the discrete layers establishes the strict
\Omega(\eta)
lower bound on expected empirical sensitivity.

The complete proof can be found in
Appendix

I
.

Appendix A
Standard Facts

We collect the standard facts used throughout the paper.

Definition A.1
.

Let
P,Q
be probability measures on a common measurable space. If
Q\ll P
, we define the
\chi^{2}
-divergence by
\chi^{2}(Q\|P):=\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[\left(\frac{dQ}{dP}-1\right)^{2}\right]
. The total variation distance is defined by
\operatorname{TV}(P,Q):=\sup_{A}|P(A)-Q(A)|
.

Definition A.2
.

Let
g:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be a measurable scalar estimator. For each of the following,
\mu^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}
will be fixed and we assume the data is drawn as
X=(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu^{\prime},1)^{\otimes n}
.
We use the following notation throughout:

•

a(\mu^{\prime}):=\mathbb{E}_{X}[g(X)]
is the mean of the estimator.

•

b(\mu^{\prime}):=a(\mu^{\prime})-\mu^{\prime}
is the bias of the estimator.

Theorem A.3

(Efron–Stein inequality)
.

Suppose that
X_{1},\dots,X_{n},X_{1}^{\prime},\dots,X_{n}^{\prime}
are independent, with
X_{i}^{\prime}
distributed identically to
X_{i}
for every
i
. Let
X=(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
and

X^{(i)}=(X_{1},\dots,X_{i-1},X_{i}^{\prime},X_{i+1},\dots,X_{n}),\quad i=1,\dots,n.

Then, for every scalar function
f
with finite variance,

\mathrm{Var}(f(X))\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[(f(X)-f(X^{(i)}))^{2}\right].

More generally, if
f
is vector-valued and
\mathbb{E}\|f(X)\|_{2}^{2}<\infty
, then

\mathbb{E}\left[\|f(X)-\mathbb{E}f(X)\|_{2}^{2}\right]\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\|f(X)-f(X^{(i)})\|_{2}^{2}\right].

Theorem A.4

(Hammersley–Chapman–Robbins inequality)
.

Let
P,Q
be probability measures with
Q\ll P
and
\chi^{2}(Q\|P)<\infty
. Let
T
be a statistic such that
T\in L^{2}(P)
and
T\in L^{1}(Q)
. Then

\mathrm{Var}_{P}(T)\geq\frac{\left(\mathbb{E}_{Q}T-\mathbb{E}_{P}T\right)^{2}}{\chi^{2}(Q\|P)}.

Theorem A.5

(Cramér–Rao inequality)
.

Let
\{P_{\mu^{\prime}}\}_{\mu^{\prime}\in\Theta}
be a one-dimensional parametric family with densities
p_{\mu^{\prime}}
. Assume the family and a given statistic
T(X)
satisfy the following regularity conditions:

•

The support
\{x:p_{\mu^{\prime}}(x)>0\}
is independent of the parameter
\mu^{\prime}
.

•

The score function
s_{\mu^{\prime}}(X)\coloneqq\frac{\partial}{\partial\mu^{\prime}}\log p_{\mu^{\prime}}(X)
exists almost everywhere.

•

The Fisher information
I(\mu^{\prime})\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\mu^{\prime}}[s_{\mu^{\prime}}(X)^{2}]
satisfies
0<I(\mu^{\prime})<\infty
.

•

The statistic
T
has finite variance, its mean response
m_{T}(\mu^{\prime})\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\mu^{\prime}}[T]
is differentiable, and it satisfies the score identity
m_{T}^{\prime}(\mu^{\prime})=\mathbb{E}_{\mu^{\prime}}[T(X)s_{\mu^{\prime}}(X)]
.

Then, the variance of the statistic is lower bounded by

\mathrm{Var}_{\mu^{\prime}}(T)\geq\frac{(m_{T}^{\prime}(\mu^{\prime}))^{2}}{I(\mu^{\prime})}.

In particular, if
X=(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu^{\prime},1)^{\otimes n}
, then
I(\mu^{\prime})=n
, and hence
\mathrm{Var}_{\mu^{\prime}}(T)\geq\frac{(m_{T}^{\prime}(\mu^{\prime}))^{2}}{n}
.

Theorem A.6
.

For any two probability measures
P,Q
on a common measurable space, there exists a coupling
(X,Y)
with
X\sim P
and
Y\sim Q
such that
\mathbb{P}(X\neq Y)=\operatorname{TV}(P,Q)
. Consequently, if
(X_{i},Y_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
are independent maximal couplings of
P
and
Q
, then

\#\{i\in[n]:X_{i}\neq Y_{i}\}\sim\Bin(n,\operatorname{TV}(P,Q)).

Theorem A.7

(Chernoff bounds)
.

Let
Z\sim\Bin(n,p)
and write
\lambda=np
. Then, for every
\delta>0
,
\mathbb{P}(Z\geq(1+\delta)\lambda)\leq\left(\frac{e^{\delta}}{(1+\delta)^{1+\delta}}\right)^{\lambda}
. Equivalently, for every
t>\lambda
,
\mathbb{P}(Z\geq t)\leq\left(\frac{e\lambda}{t}\right)^{t}
. In particular, for every fixed
\rho\in(0,1)
, if
\lambda\leq\rho k
, then
\mathbb{P}(Z\geq k)\leq\exp(-c_{\rho}k)
, where
c_{\rho}>0
is a constant depending only on
\rho
.

Lemma A.8
.

There exists a universal constant
c>0
such that for every integer
n\geq 1
and every
r\in\{0,1,\dots,n\}
,
\mathbb{P}\left(\Bin\left(n,\frac{r}{n}\right)=r\right)\geq\frac{c}{\sqrt{r+1}}
.

Appendix B

Throughout this section, we assume for simplicity that
n
is odd. Write
n=2m-1
and for
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

x_{(1)}\leq\dots\leq x_{(n)}
denote the order statistics. Therefore,
\operatorname{med}(x)\coloneqq x_{(m)}
and

S_{\eta}^{\mathrm{med}}(x)\coloneqq\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:d_{H}(x,y)\leq k}|\mathrm{med}(x)-\mathrm{med}(y)|

where
k\coloneqq\lfloor\eta n\rfloor
.

Lemma B.1
.

Let
0\leq k\leq m-1
. Then, for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

S_{\eta}^{\operatorname{med}}(x)\leq\max\{x_{(m+k)}-x_{(m)},x_{(m)}-x_{(m-k)}\}.

Proof.

Fix
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
d_{H}(x,y)\leq k
. Thus
y
is obtained from
x
by changing at most
k
coordinates. We claim that
\operatorname{med}(y)\in[x_{(m-k)},x_{(m+k)}]
. Indeed, there are
m+k
entries of
x
that are at most
x_{(m+k)}
. Since at most
k
coordinates are changed, at least
m
of these entries remain in
y
. Hence at least
m
entries of
y
are at most
x_{(m+k)}
, which implies
\operatorname{med}(y)\leq x_{(m+k)}
. Similarly, there are
m+k
entries of
x
that are at least
x_{(m-k)}
. After changing at most
k
coordinates, at least
m
of these entries remain in
y
. Hence at least
m
entries of
y
are at least
x_{(m-k)}
, which implies
\operatorname{med}(y)\geq x_{(m-k)}
. Therefore
\operatorname{med}(y)\in[x_{(m-k)},x_{(m+k)}]
. Since
\operatorname{med}(x)=x_{(m)}
, we obtain
|\operatorname{med}(y)-\operatorname{med}(x)|\leq\max\left\{x_{(m+k)}-x_{(m)},x_{(m)}-x_{(m-k)}\right\}
. Taking the supremum over all
y
with
d_{H}(x,y)\leq k
proves the claim.
∎

We will also need some results from uniform spacings, see, e.g.,
[
26
]
.

Theorem B.2
.

Let
U_{1},\dots,U_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{iid}}}{{\sim}}\mathsf{Unif}[0,1]
, and let
0=U_{(0)}\leq U_{(1)}\leq\cdots\leq U_{(n)}\leq U_{(n+1)}=1
denote the order statistics with endpoints added. Define the spacings
D_{i}\coloneqq U_{(i)}-U_{(i-1)}
for
i=1,\dots,n+1
. Then
(D_{1},\dots,D_{n+1})\sim\operatorname{Dirichlet}(1,\dots,1)
. Equivalently, if
E_{1},\dots,E_{n+1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{iid}}}{{\sim}}\mathsf{Exp}(1)
and
T\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}E_{i}
, then
(D_{1},\dots,D_{n+1})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\left(\frac{E_{1}}{T},\dots,\frac{E_{n+1}}{T}\right)
.

We can now state the Gaussian empirical sensitivity of the median.

Theorem B.3
.

Let
n=2m-1
be odd, and let
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{iid}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}(\mu,1)
. Fix
\eta\in(0,1/3)
and set
k=\lfloor\eta n\rfloor
. There exists universal constants
c,C>0
such that, for every
\beta\in(0,1/2)
, with probability at least
1-\beta-Ce^{-cn}
, we have

S_{\eta}^{\mathrm{med}}(X)\leq C\left(\eta+\frac{\log(1/\beta)}{n}\right).

Proof.

By translation invariance, it suffices to prove the result for
\mu=0
. The case
k=0
or
\eta=0
directly gives us
S_{\eta}^{\mathrm{med}}(X)=0
. So we will assume
k\geq 1
. Let
\Phi
denote the standard Gaussian distribution function and define
U_{i}\coloneqq\Phi(X_{i})
. Then
U_{1},\dots,U_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{iid}}}{{\sim}}\mathsf{Unif}[0,1]
. Since
\Phi
is strictly increasing,
X_{(j)}=\Phi^{-1}(U_{(j)})
for every
j\in[n]
. We first localize the relevant order statistics away from the tails. Since
\eta<1/3
and
k=\lfloor\eta n\rfloor
, we have
m-k\geq\frac{n}{6}
and
m+k\leq\frac{5n}{6}+1
.

Let
N_{-}\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}\{U_{i}\leq 1/8\}
and
N_{+}\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}\{U_{i}\leq 7/8\}
. Then
N_{-}\sim\operatorname{Bin}(n,1/8)
and
N_{+}\sim\operatorname{Bin}(n,7/8)
. By the binomial Chernoff bound in
Theorem

A.7
, there exist universal constants
c,C>0
such that
\mathbb{P}\left(N_{-}\geq\frac{n}{6}\right)\leq Ce^{-cn}
and
\mathbb{P}\left(N_{+}\leq\frac{5n}{6}\right)\leq Ce^{-cn}
. Therefore, with probability at least
1-Ce^{-cn}
,
U_{(m-k)}\geq\frac{1}{8}
and
U_{(m+k)}\leq\frac{7}{8}
. On this event, all the order statistics
U_{(m-k)},U_{(m)},U_{(m+k)}
lie in
[1/8,7/8]
.

The inverse Gaussian CDF is Lipschitz on
[1/8,7/8]
. Indeed,
\frac{d}{du}\Phi^{-1}(u)=\frac{1}{\varphi(\Phi^{-1}(u))}
, where
\varphi
is the standard Gaussian density, and the right-hand side is bounded on the compact interval

. Hence there exists a universal constant
L<\infty

X_{(m+k)}-X_{(m)}\leq L\bigl(U_{(m+k)}-U_{(m)}\bigr)

\mathbb{P}(A\geq 2(k+t))\leq e^{-(k+t)}\mathbb{E}e^{A/2}=e^{-(k+t)}2^{k}=\exp\{-t+k(\log 2-1)\}\leq e^{-t}.

Also, another Chernoff bound gives
\mathbb{P}(T\leq n/2)\leq e^{-cn}

\frac{A}{T}\leq 4\frac{k+t}{n}
. Applying this bound to the two spacings and taking a union bound gives

\max\left\{X_{(m+k)}-X_{(m)},\,X_{(m)}-X_{(m-k)}\right\}\leq C\frac{k+\log(1/\beta)}{n}\leq C\left(\eta+\frac{\log(1/\beta)}{n}\right).

Finally, by
Lemma

,
S_{\eta}^{\operatorname{med}}(X)\leq\max\left\{X_{(m+k)}-X_{(m)},\,X_{(m)}-X_{(m-k)}\right\}
. Therefore, with probability at least

, we conclude
S_{\eta}^{\operatorname{med}}(X)\leq C\left(\eta+\frac{\log(1/\beta)}{n}\right)
, which proves the theorem.

Appendix C
Resampling Adversary Upper Bound

In this section, we record a simple observation showing that the linear-in-
\eta
term in our corruption lower bound should not be expected under the resampling adversary.

Recall the resampling adversary:

Definition C.1

(Resampling Adversary)
.

Fix
\eta\in(0,1)
and let
k\coloneqq\lfloor\eta n\rfloor
. Given
X=(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
with
X\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu,\mathbb{I}_{d})^{\otimes n}
. Let
I\subset[n]
be a uniformly random subset of size
k
, independent of
X
. Let
(X_{i}^{\prime})_{i\in I}
be independent fresh samples from
\mathcal{N}(\mu,\mathbb{I}_{d})
, also independent of
(X,I)
. The resampled dataset
X^{\mathrm{res}}
is defined by

X_{i}^{\mathrm{res}}\coloneqq\begin{cases}X_{i}^{\prime},&\text{if }i\in I,\\
X_{i},&\text{if }i\notin I.\end{cases}

The following proposition quantifies how much the empirical mean shifts under a resampling adversary.

Proposition C.2
.

Let
\overline{X}\coloneqq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
and
\overline{X}^{\mathrm{res}}\coloneqq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}^{\mathrm{res}}
. Then

\left(\mathbb{E}_{X,I,X^{\prime}}\|\overline{X}^{\mathrm{res}}-\overline{X}\|_{2}^{2}\right)^{1/2}\asymp\sqrt{\frac{\eta d}{n}}.

Consequently, under the resampling adversary, the empirical mean has empirical stability of order
\sqrt{\eta d/n}
, and does not exhibit an
\eta
term.

Proof.

By definition, we have

\overline{X}^{\mathrm{res}}-\overline{X}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in I}(X_{i}^{\prime}-X_{i}).

Conditional on
I
, the random vectors
(X_{i}^{\prime}-X_{i})_{i\in I}
are independent, and each has law
\mathcal{N}(0,2\mathbb{I}_{d})
. Therefore,

(\overline{X}^{\mathrm{res}}-\overline{X})\mid I\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,\frac{2k}{n^{2}}\mathbb{I}_{d}\right)

Hence,

\mathbb{E}_{X,X^{\prime}\mid I}\|\overline{X}^{\mathrm{res}}-\overline{X}\|_{2}^{2}=\operatorname{tr}\left(\frac{2k}{n^{2}}\mathbb{I}_{d}\right)=\frac{2kd}{n^{2}}.

Since RHS is deterministic in
I
, averaging over
I
gives the same value. Therefore, taking square root yields

\left(\mathbb{E}_{X,I,X^{\prime}}\|\overline{X}^{\mathrm{res}}-\overline{X}\|_{2}^{2}\right)^{1/2}=\sqrt{\frac{2kd}{n^{2}}}\asymp\sqrt{\frac{\eta d}{n}}.

∎

Appendix D
Lemmas

In this section, we isolate the main lemma that allow us to prove the high-dimensional theorem for the mean obstruction by reducing the problem to a scalar setting. Ultimately, we claim that it suffices to prove the lower bound for a bounded, one-dimensional estimator
h\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to[0,1]
that inherits
O(1/n)
integrated risk.

This restriction to compact interval
[0,1]
is rigorously justified by the following reduction: first, we restrict the parameter space of the projected 1D problem to the interval
\mu^{\prime}\in[0,1]
. Because the original high-dimensional estimator
f
is uniformly MSE-accurate over all
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, its 1D projection must maintain low average risk over this specified bounded sub-interval under a Gaussian prior. Because the true parameter
\mu
is now restricted to
[0,1]
, any output of the estimator outside this interval is strictly suboptimal, and thus we may clip the estimator’s output to
[0,1]
which strictly decreases or maintains both its estimation and its empirical sensitivity. Consequently, we may establish an empirical sensitivity lower bound for the clipped version of the estimator
h
to guarantee a valid lower bound for the original unrestricted high-dimensional estimator
f
.

Let
V=\mathsf{span}(u)
. If
\lambda\in u^{\perp}
and
Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbb{I}_{d})
, then
\lambda+(\mathbb{I}_{d}-uu^{\top})Z
is drawn from a Gaussian distribution on the affine subspace
\lambda+V^{\perp}
with mean
\lambda
.

Lemma D.1
.

Let
f\colon(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be measurable. Fix
u\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
and
\lambda\in u^{\perp}
. For
t=(t_{1},\dots,t_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, define

g_{\lambda}(t)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{Z}\left[\left\langle u,f(t_{1}u+V_{1},\dots,t_{n}u+V_{n})\right\rangle\right],\qquad V_{i}\coloneqq\lambda+(\mathbb{I}_{d}-uu^{\top})Z_{i},

where
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{iid}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbb{I}_{d})
. Then for every
\eta\in(0,1)
, every
q\in\{1,2\}
, and every
\mu^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}
,

\left(\mathbb{E}_{T\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu^{\prime},1)^{\otimes n}}\bigl[(S_{\eta}^{g_{\lambda}}(T))^{q}\bigr]\right)^{1/q}\leq\mathsf{ES}_{\eta,q}(f;\mu^{\prime}u+\lambda).

Moreover,

\mathbb{E}_{T\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu^{\prime},1)^{\otimes n}}[(g_{\lambda}(T)-\mu^{\prime})^{2}]\leq\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu^{\prime}u+\lambda,\mathbb{I}_{d})^{\otimes n}}\bigl[\langle u,f(X)-(\mu^{\prime}u+\lambda)\rangle^{2}\bigr].

Proof.

Set
k\coloneqq\lfloor\eta n\rfloor
. Fix
t,t^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
d_{H}(t,t^{\prime})\leq k
, and use the same orthogonal noise to lift both
scalar samples:
x_{i}=t_{i}u+V_{i}
and
x_{i}^{\prime}=t_{i}^{\prime}u+V_{i}
. Then
d_{H}(x,x^{\prime})\leq k
and so

\displaystyle|g_{\lambda}(t^{\prime})-g_{\lambda}(t)|

\displaystyle=\left|\mathbb{E}_{Z}\langle u,f(x^{\prime})-f(x)\rangle\right|

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{Z}\left\lVert f(x^{\prime})-f(x)\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{Z}S_{\eta}^{f}(x).

Taking the supremum over all such
t^{\prime}
gives

S_{\eta}^{g_{\lambda}}(t)\leq\mathbb{E}_{Z}S_{\eta}^{f}(t_{1}u+V_{1},\dots,t_{n}u+V_{n}),

which implies the sensitivity comparison we want by Jensen’s inequality and averaging over
T\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu^{\prime},1)^{\otimes n}
.

For the risk comparison, Jensen gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{T}[(g_{\lambda}(T)-\mu^{\prime})^{2}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{T}\left[\left(\mathbb{E}_{Z}[\langle u,f(T_{1}u+V_{1},\dots,T_{n}u+V_{n})\rangle-\mu^{\prime}]\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{T,Z}\bigl[(\langle u,f(T_{1}u+V_{1},\dots,T_{n}u+V_{n})\rangle-\mu^{\prime})^{2}\bigr].

For each
i
,

T_{i}u+V_{i}=T_{i}u+\lambda+(\mathbb{I}_{d}-uu^{\top})Z_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu^{\prime}u+\lambda,\mathbb{I}_{d}).

Also
\langle u,\mu^{\prime}u+\lambda\rangle=\mu^{\prime}
, since
\lambda\in u^{\perp}
. Therefore the last term above is just

\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu^{\prime}u+\lambda,\mathbb{I}_{d})^{\otimes n}}\bigl[\langle u,f(X)-(\mu^{\prime}u+\lambda)\rangle^{2}\bigr],

which proves the claim.
∎

Lemma D.2
.

Let
f\colon(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be measurable and
\left(C_{\mathsf{MSE}},\frac{d}{n}\right)
-uniformly MSE-accurate. Fix
\rho>0
. Then there exist
u\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
and
\lambda_{*}\in u^{\perp}
such that the scalar estimator
g_{\lambda_{*}}
from
Lemma

D.1
satisfies

\mathbb{E}_{\mu^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\rho^{2})}\mathbb{E}_{T\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu^{\prime},1)^{\otimes n}}[(g_{\lambda_{*}}(T)-\mu^{\prime})^{2}]\leq\frac{C_{\mathsf{MSE}}}{n}.

Proof.

Let
M\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\rho^{2}\mathbb{I}_{d})
and let

U\sim\Unif(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
, independently of all other randomness.
For every fixed
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\mathbb{E}_{U}[\langle U,z\rangle^{2}]=\frac{\left\lVert z\right\rVert^{2}}{d}.

Hence, by uniform MSE accuracy,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{U}\mathbb{E}_{M}\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mathcal{N}(M,\mathbb{I}_{d})^{\otimes n}}[\langle U,f(X)-M\rangle^{2}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d}\mathbb{E}_{M}\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mathcal{N}(M,\mathbb{I}_{d})^{\otimes n}}\left\lVert f(X)-M\right\rVert^{2}\leq\frac{C_{\mathsf{MSE}}}{n}.

Therefore there exists
u\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
such that

\mathbb{E}_{M}\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mathcal{N}(M,\mathbb{I}_{d})^{\otimes n}}[\langle u,f(X)-M\rangle^{2}]\leq\frac{C_{\mathsf{MSE}}}{n}.

Decompose

M=\mu^{\prime}u+\lambda,\qquad\mu^{\prime}\coloneqq\langle M,u\rangle,\qquad\lambda\coloneqq M-\langle M,u\rangle u.

Then
\mu^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\rho^{2})
,
\lambda\in u^{\perp}
, and

\mu^{\prime}
is independent of
\lambda
. Applying

Lemma

D.1
and averaging over
\lambda
gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\lambda}\mathbb{E}_{\mu^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\rho^{2})}\mathbb{E}_{T\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu^{\prime},1)^{\otimes n}}[(g_{\lambda}(T)-\mu^{\prime})^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{M}\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mathcal{N}(M,\mathbb{I}_{d})^{\otimes n}}[\langle u,f(X)-M\rangle^{2}]\leq\frac{C_{\mathsf{MSE}}}{n}.

Thus some
\lambda_{*}\in u^{\perp}
satisfies the desired bound.
∎

Lemma D.3
.

Let
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be a closed interval and let
\mathrm{clip}_{K}(t)
denote Euclidean projection onto
K
. For any scalar estimator
g
, define
h\coloneqq\mathrm{clip}_{K}\circ g
. Then, for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we have
S_{\eta}^{h}(x)\leq S_{\eta}^{g}(x)
. Moreover, if
\mu^{\prime}\in K
, then for every
x
,
|h(x)-\mu^{\prime}|\leq|g(x)-\mu^{\prime}|
.

Proof.

The projection map
\mathrm{clip}_{K}
is
1
-Lipschitz and fixes every point of
K
. Thus, if
\mu\in K
, we have

|h(x)-\mu^{\prime}|=|\mathrm{clip}_{K}(g(x))-\mathrm{clip}_{K}(\mu^{\prime})|\leq|g(x)-\mu^{\prime}|.

Similarly, for any

, we obtain
|h(x^{\prime})-h(x)|\leq|g(x^{\prime})-g(x)|
. Taking the supremum over all
x^{\prime}
with
d_{H}(x,x^{\prime})\leq\lfloor\eta n\rfloor
proves the sensitivity bound we want.
∎

Lemma D.4
.

Let
f\colon(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be measurable and
\left(C_{\mathsf{MSE}},\frac{d}{n}\right)
-uniformly MSE-accurate. There exists

\eta\in(0,1)
, every

, and every

Proof.

Apply
Lemma

D.2
with

\lambda_{\ast}

F.1
Statistical Indistinguishability of the Local and Global Shift

WLOG we may choose
\eta
such that
\eta=\frac{\lfloor\eta n\rfloor}{n}
, i.e.,
\eta n\in\{1,\dots,n\}
. For
\delta\geq 0
and
\mu^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}
, we define the local-shift distribution
Q_{\mu^{\prime},\delta}
via the following generative process: draw
X\sim P_{\mu^{\prime}}
, draw a
k
-subset
I\subset[n]
uniformly at random (independent of
X
), and output
X^{(I,\delta)}\coloneqq X+\delta e_{I}
, i.e., this means that

X_{i}^{(I,\delta)}=\begin{cases}X_{i}+\delta,&\text{if }i\in I,\\
X_{i},&\text{if }i\notin I.\end{cases}

The key observation is to show that this local adversarial corruption is statistically indistinguishable from a global mean shift
P_{\mu^{\prime}+\eta\delta}
.

Lemma F.1
.

For every
\mu^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}
and every
\delta\geq 0
,

\chi^{2}(Q_{\mu^{\prime},\delta}\|P_{\mu^{\prime}+\eta\delta})\leq\exp\left(\frac{k^{2}}{n}(e^{\delta^{2}}-1-\delta^{2})\right)-1.

To prove
Lemma

F.1
, we require two elementary auxiliary lemmas.

Lemma F.2

(Gaussian likelihood-ratio identity)
.

For any
\theta,\mu,\nu\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, if
X\sim\mathcal{N}(\theta,\mathbb{I}_{n})
, then

\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mathcal{N}(\theta,\mathbb{I}_{n})}\left[\frac{d\mathcal{N}(\mu,\mathbb{I}_{n})}{d\mathcal{N}(\theta,\mathbb{I}_{n})}(X)\cdot\frac{d\mathcal{N}(\nu,\mathbb{I}_{n})}{d\mathcal{N}(\theta,\mathbb{I}_{n})}(X)\right]=\exp(\langle\mu-\theta,\nu-\theta\rangle).

Proof.

Let
a\coloneqq\mu-\theta
and
b\coloneqq\nu-\theta
. If
X\sim\mathcal{N}(\theta,\mathbb{I}_{n})
, then
X=\theta+G
with
G\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbb{I}_{n})
. The likelihood ratio of
\mathcal{N}(\mu,\mathbb{I}_{n})
with respect to
\mathcal{N}(\theta,\mathbb{I}_{n})
is
\frac{d\mathcal{N}(\mu,\mathbb{I}_{n})}{d\mathcal{N}(\theta,\mathbb{I}_{n})}(X)=\exp\left(\langle a,X-\theta\rangle-\frac{1}{2}\|a\|_{2}^{2}\right)=\exp\left(\langle a,G\rangle-\frac{1}{2}\|a\|_{2}^{2}\right)
. Similarly,
\frac{d\mathcal{N}(\nu,\mathbb{I}_{n})}{d\mathcal{N}(\theta,\mathbb{I}_{n})}(X)=\exp\left(\langle b,G\rangle-\frac{1}{2}\|b\|_{2}^{2}\right)
.
Therefore, the expected product of the likelihood ratios is

\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mathcal{N}(\theta,\mathbb{I}_{n})}\left[\frac{d\mathcal{N}(\mu,\mathbb{I}_{n})}{d\mathcal{N}(\theta,\mathbb{I}_{n})}(X)\frac{d\mathcal{N}(\nu,\mathbb{I}_{n})}{d\mathcal{N}(\theta,\mathbb{I}_{n})}(X)\right]=\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\|a\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\|b\|_{2}^{2}\right)\mathbb{E}_{G}\exp\left(\langle a+b,G\rangle\right).

Using the standard Gaussian moment-generating function
\mathbb{E}\exp(\langle t,G\rangle)=\exp(\frac{1}{2}\|t\|_{2}^{2})
with
t=a+b
, the expectation evaluates to
\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\|a\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\|b\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|a+b\|_{2}^{2}\right)
. Expanding the squared norm gives the identity
\frac{1}{2}\|a+b\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\|a\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\|b\|_{2}^{2}=\langle a,b\rangle
. Substituting back
a=\mu-\theta
and
b=\nu-\theta
yields the final expected value
\exp(\langle\mu-\theta,\nu-\theta\rangle)
, as claimed.
∎

Lemma F.3
.

Let
A
and
B
be independent uniformly random
k
-subsets of
[n]
, and let
H=|A\cap B|
. For every
\lambda\geq 0

\mathbb{E}\exp\left(\lambda\left(H-\frac{k^{2}}{n}\right)\right)\leq\exp\left(\frac{k^{2}}{n}(e^{\lambda}-1-\lambda)\right).

Proof.

Condition on
A
. For

, write

. Then

. Set

e^{\lambda H}=\prod_{i\in A}(1+cX_{i})=\sum_{R\subseteq A}c^{|R|}\mathbf{1}\{R\subseteq B\}.

If

\mathbb{P}(R\subseteq B)=\frac{\binom{n-s}{k-s}}{\binom{n}{k}}\leq\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)^{s}.

Consequently, we have

\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda H}\mid A]\leq\sum_{s=0}^{k}\binom{k}{s}c^{s}\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)^{s}=\left(1+\frac{k}{n}(e^{\lambda}-1)\right)^{k}.

Since RHS does not depend on

\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda H}]
by averaging over all possible

Multiplying by
\exp(-\lambda k^{2}/n)
and using

for

, we thus obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\exp\left(\lambda\left(H-\frac{k^{2}}{n}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\lambda\frac{k^{2}}{n}+k\log\left(1+\frac{k}{n}(e^{\lambda}-1)\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(\frac{k^{2}}{n}(e^{\lambda}-1-\lambda)\right),

∎

Proof of

.

By translation invariance, it suffices to set

Corollary F.4
.

Let
h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to[0,1]
be any bounded measurable estimator. Then

\left|\mathbb{E}_{Q_{\mu^{\prime},\delta}}[h]-\mathbb{E}_{P_{\mu^{\prime}+\eta\delta}}[h]\right|\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{P_{\mu^{\prime}+\eta\delta}}[(h(X)-(\mu^{\prime}+\eta\delta))^{2}]\cdot\chi^{2}(Q_{\mu^{\prime},\delta}\|P_{\mu^{\prime}+\eta\delta})}.

Proof.

Let
P\coloneqq P_{\mu^{\prime}+\eta\delta}
and let
R(X)=h(X)-(\mu^{\prime}+\eta\delta)
. Applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}_{Q_{\mu^{\prime},\delta}}[h]-\mathbb{E}_{P}[h]\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[R(X)\left(\frac{dQ_{\mu^{\prime},\delta}}{dP}(X)-1\right)\right]\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{P}[R(X)^{2}]\cdot\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[\left(\frac{dQ_{\mu^{\prime},\delta}}{dP}(X)-1\right)^{2}\right]},

which perfectly matches the desired statement.
∎

F.2
Tracking the Global Shift

Having established indistinguishability, we will now show that the estimator must track the global shift to maintain its uniform MSE accuracy assumption. Indeed, let
t\coloneqq\eta\delta
, where
\delta>0
is a small constant chosen later. We let
a(\mu^{\prime})
and
b(\mu^{\prime})
be the expected output and bias of
h
respectively.

To lower-bound the supremum over
\mu^{\prime}
, we lower-bound the average over a carefully chosen prior. Let
w\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
be a continuously differentiable probability density function supported strictly on
[\epsilon,1-\epsilon]
for some fixed
\epsilon>0
. We assume
t<\epsilon
so that the shifted density
w(\cdot-t)
remains entirely supported within
[0,1]
.

By our risk assumption, the average squared bias over the interval satisfies
\int_{0}^{1}b(\mu^{\prime})^{2}\,d\mu^{\prime}\leq\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P_{\mu^{\prime}}}[(h(X)-\mu^{\prime})^{2}]\,d\mu^{\prime}\leq C_{\mathsf{int}}/n
. Because
w
is smooth and compactly supported, its derivative
w^{\prime}
has finite
L^{2}
norm, ensuring
\|w(\cdot-t)-w(\cdot)\|_{L^{2}}\leq t\|w^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}}
.
Therefore, by change of variables, we have

\displaystyle\int w(\mu^{\prime})(a(\mu^{\prime}+t)-a(\mu^{\prime}))\,d\mu^{\prime}

\displaystyle=t+\int b(v)(w(v-t)-w(v))\,dv

\displaystyle\geq t-\|b\|_{L^{2}}\|w(\cdot-t)-w(\cdot)\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\geq t-\sqrt{\frac{C_{\mathsf{int}}}{n}}\cdot t\|w^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle=t-O\left(\frac{t}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\geq t-o(t).

Thus, on average over the prior
w
, the estimator’s mean output is mathematically forced to shift by approximately
t=\eta\delta
.

To finish the proof, notice that because
Q_{\mu^{\prime},\delta}
is generated by shifting exactly
k=\lfloor\eta n\rfloor
coordinates of
X\sim P_{\mu^{\prime}}
, the resulting dataset
X^{(I,\delta)}
is a valid
\eta
-corruption. Therefore, we have
S_{\eta}^{h}(X)\geq|h(X^{(I,\delta)})-h(X)|
. By Triangle Inequality, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P_{\mu^{\prime}}}[S_{\eta}^{h}(X)]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P_{\mu^{\prime}},I}[|h(X^{(I,\delta)})-h(X)|]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P_{\mu^{\prime}},I}[h(X^{(I,\delta)})]-\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P_{\mu^{\prime}}}[h(X)]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}_{X\sim Q_{\mu^{\prime},\delta}}[h(X)]-\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P_{\mu^{\prime}}}[h(X)]

\displaystyle=(\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P_{\mu^{\prime}+t}}[h(X)]-\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P_{\mu^{\prime}}}[h(X)])+(\mathbb{E}_{X\sim Q_{\mu^{\prime},\delta}}[h(X)]-\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P_{\mu^{\prime}+t}}[h(X)])

\displaystyle\geq a(\mu^{\prime}+t)-a(\mu^{\prime})-\left|\mathbb{E}_{X\sim Q_{\mu^{\prime},\delta}}[h(X)]-\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P_{\mu^{\prime}+t}}[h(X)]\right|

We now average this inequality over the prior
w(\mu^{\prime})
to establish the final lower bound. The first term satisfies
\int w(\mu^{\prime})(a(\mu^{\prime}+t)-a(\mu^{\prime}))\,d\mu^{\prime}\geq\eta\delta-o(\eta\delta)
, where the
o(\eta\delta)
term vanishes as
n\to\infty
due to the smoothness of
w
. For the second term, we apply
Corollary

F.4
and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality over the prior integral to decouple the estimator’s accuracy from the statistical distance between distributions:

\displaystyle\int w(\mu^{\prime})

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}_{Q_{\mu^{\prime},\delta}}[h]-\mathbb{E}_{P_{\mu^{\prime}+t}}[h]\right|\,d\mu^{\prime}

\displaystyle\leq\int w(\mu^{\prime})\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{P_{\mu^{\prime}+t}}[(h(X)-(\mu^{\prime}+t))^{2}]\cdot\chi^{2}(Q_{\mu^{\prime},\delta}\|P_{\mu^{\prime}+t})}\,d\mu^{\prime}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\int w(\mu^{\prime})\mathbb{E}_{P_{\mu^{\prime}+t}}[(h(X)-(\mu^{\prime}+t))^{2}]\,d\mu^{\prime}}\cdot\sqrt{\int w(\mu^{\prime})\chi^{2}(Q_{\mu^{\prime},\delta}\|P_{\mu^{\prime}+t})\,d\mu^{\prime}}.

We evaluate the two components under the square root independently:

1.

By the uniform MSE assumption on the scalar estimator
h
, we have that variance is bounded by MSE, and thus

\int w(\mu^{\prime})\mathbb{E}_{P_{\mu^{\prime}+t}}[(h(X)-(\mu^{\prime}+t))^{2}]\,d\mu^{\prime}\leq\int w(v-t)\mathbb{E}_{P_{v}}[(h(X)-v)^{2}]\,dv\leq\frac{C_{\mathsf{int}}}{n}\|w\|_{\infty}.

2.

Applying Lemma
F.1
for the regime
k\leq\kappa\sqrt{n}
, the divergence satisfies

\chi^{2}(Q_{\mu^{\prime},\delta}\|P_{\mu^{\prime}+t})\leq\exp\left(\frac{k^{2}}{n}(e^{\delta^{2}}-1-\delta^{2})\right)-1\lesssim\frac{k^{2}\delta^{4}}{n}.

Substituting these bounds into the Cauchy-Schwarz product yields an error term of:

\sqrt{\frac{C_{\mathsf{int}}}{n}\|w\|_{\infty}\cdot\frac{k^{2}\delta^{4}}{n}}=\sqrt{C_{\mathsf{int}}\|w\|_{\infty}}\cdot\frac{k\delta^{2}}{n}=O(\eta\delta^{2}).

Putting this together, the average empirical sensitivity is bounded by:

\int w(\mu^{\prime})\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P_{\mu^{\prime}}}[S_{\eta}^{h}(X)]\,d\mu^{\prime}\geq\eta\delta-o(\eta\delta)-O(\eta\delta^{2}).

By choosing the absolute constant
\delta>0
to be sufficiently small, the
O(\eta\delta^{2})
term is made strictly smaller than
\eta\delta/2
. This guarantees an average expected sensitivity of
\Omega(\eta\delta)=\Omega(\eta)
. Since the average sensitivity is lower-bounded by
c_{\mathrm{low}}\eta
, the supremum over the interval must also satisfy

\sup_{\mu^{\prime}\in[0,1]}\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu^{\prime},1)^{\otimes n}}[S_{\eta}^{h}(X)]\geq c_{\mathrm{low}}\eta.

This establishes the scalar claim, which transfers to the high-dimensional estimator
f
via the orthogonal projection, completing the proof of
Theorem

3.4
.

Appendix G
Proof of
Theorem

3.5

Throughout this section, for a scalar parameter
\mu^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}
, we write
P_{\mu^{\prime}}\coloneqq\mathcal{N}(\mu^{\prime},1)^{\otimes n}
. By the reduction established in
Lemma

D.4
, to prove the high-dimensional theorem, it suffices to prove the following scalar claim: if the induced one-dimensional estimator
h\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to[0,1]
satisfies the integrated risk bound

\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P_{\mu^{\prime}}}[(h(X)-\mu^{\prime})^{2}]\,d\mu^{\prime}\leq\frac{C_{\mathsf{int}}}{n},

then, for a corruption budget
k=\lfloor\eta n\rfloor\geq C_{\mathrm{high}}\log n
, its average empirical sensitivity must be large:

\sup_{\mu^{\prime}\in[0,1]}\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P_{\mu^{\prime}}}[S_{\eta}^{h}(X)]\geq c_{\mathrm{high}}\eta.

To see exactly why this scalar claim implies the full high-dimensional theorem, recall that
Lemma

D.4
guarantees the existence of a "good" unit direction
u\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
and an orthogonal shift
\lambda_{\ast}\in u^{\perp}
. When we evaluate the original high-dimensional estimator
f
along the one-dimensional affine line parameterized by
\mu^{\prime}u+\lambda_{\ast}
, its empirical sensitivity strictly upper-bounds the sensitivity of the induced scalar estimator
h
. Together with Jensen’s inequality, this gives us

\mathsf{ES}_{\eta,2}(f;\mu^{\prime}u+\lambda_{\ast})\geq\left(\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P_{\mu^{\prime}}}[S_{\eta}^{h}(X)^{2}]\right)^{1/2}\geq\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P_{\mu^{\prime}}}[S_{\eta}^{h}(X)].

Taking the supremum over
\mu^{\prime}\in[0,1]
on both sides demonstrates that establishing the
c_{\mathrm{high}}\eta
lower bound for
h
immediately yields the required lower bound for
f
, completing the reduction.

We start with the following lemma about endpoint average bound for scalar estimator bound with integrated MSE bound.

Lemma G.1
.

Assume
\eta\in\left(0,\frac{1}{10}\right)
. If the scalar estimator
h\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to[0,1]
satisfies the integrated MSE bound

\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P_{\mu^{\prime}}}[(h(X)-\mu^{\prime})^{2}]\ d\mu^{\prime}\leq\frac{C_{\mathsf{int}}}{n}

Assume moreover that
n
is large enough such that
C_{\mathsf{high}}\log n\geq 100C_{\mathsf{int}}
. Then we have the following endpoint average bounds:

\frac{1}{\eta}\int_{0}^{\eta}a(\mu^{\prime})\ d\mu^{\prime}\leq\frac{1}{3}\ \text{and}\ \frac{1}{\eta}\int_{1-\eta}^{1}a(\mu^{\prime})\ d\mu^{\prime}\geq\frac{2}{3}.

Proof.

By the integrated MSE assumption,

\frac{1}{\eta}\int_{0}^{\eta}\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P_{\mu^{\prime}}}[(h(X)-\mu^{\prime})^{2}]\ d\mu^{\prime}\leq\frac{C_{\mathsf{int}}}{\eta n}\leq\frac{1}{100},

by our choice of
n
large enough so that
\eta n\geq k\geq C_{\mathsf{high}}\log n\geq 100C_{\mathsf{int}}
. Similarly, we obtain

\frac{1}{\eta}\int_{1-\eta}^{1}\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P_{\mu^{\prime}}}[(h(X)-\mu^{\prime})^{2}]\ d\mu^{\prime}\leq\frac{1}{100}.

Now, by noticing that for
\mu^{\prime}\in[0,\eta]
, we have
a(\mu^{\prime})\leq\mu^{\prime}+\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P_{\mu^{\prime}}}[|h(X)-\mu^{\prime}|]
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\eta}\int_{0}^{\eta}a(\mu^{\prime})\ d\mu^{\prime}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\eta}\int_{0}^{\eta}\mu^{\prime}\ d\mu^{\prime}+\frac{1}{\eta}\int_{0}^{\eta}\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P_{\mu^{\prime}}}[|h(X)-\mu^{\prime}|]\ d\mu^{\prime}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\eta}{2}+\left(\frac{1}{\eta}\int_{0}^{\eta}\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P_{\mu^{\prime}}}[(h(X)-\mu^{\prime})^{2}]\ d\mu^{\prime}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\eta}{2}+\frac{1}{10}\leq\frac{1}{3}.

Similarly, for
\mu^{\prime}\in[1-\eta,1]
, we have
a(\mu^{\prime})\geq\mu^{\prime}-\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P_{\mu^{\prime}}}[|h(X)-\mu^{\prime}|]
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\eta}\int_{1-\eta}^{1}a(\mu^{\prime})\ d\mu^{\prime}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\eta}\int_{1-\eta}^{1}\mu^{\prime}\ d\mu^{\prime}-\frac{1}{\eta}\int_{1-\eta}^{1}\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P_{\mu^{\prime}}}[|h(X)-\mu^{\prime}|]\ d\mu^{\prime}

\displaystyle\geq 1-\frac{\eta}{2}-\left(\frac{1}{\eta}\int_{1-\eta}^{1}\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P_{\mu^{\prime}}}[(h(X)-\mu^{\prime})^{2}]\ d\mu^{\prime}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\geq 1-\frac{\eta}{2}-\frac{1}{10}\geq\frac{2}{3}.

This proves the lemma.
∎

G.1
Average Mean Displacement

Take
n
sufficiently large such that the condition in
Lemma

G.1
holds. We first quantify the global displacement of the estimator’s expected mean under a shift of
\eta
. Indeed, we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1-\eta}(a(\mu^{\prime}+\eta)-a(\mu^{\prime}))\ d\mu^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\int_{\eta}^{1}a(\mu^{\prime})\ d\mu^{\prime}-\int_{0}^{1-\eta}a(\mu^{\prime})\ d\mu^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\int_{1-\eta}^{1}a(\mu^{\prime})\ d\mu^{\prime}-\int_{0}^{\eta}a(\mu^{\prime})\ d\mu^{\prime}\geq\frac{\eta}{3}.

Let
\mathcal{U}
denote the uniform probability measure over the interval
[0,1-\eta]
. We have

\mathbb{E}_{\mu^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{U}}[a(\mu^{\prime}+\eta)-a(\mu^{\prime})]=\frac{1}{1-\eta}\int_{0}^{1-\eta}(a(\mu^{\prime}+\eta)-a(\mu^{\prime}))\ d\mu^{\prime}\geq\frac{\eta}{3}.

G.2
Coordinate-wise Maximal Coupling

For any fixed
\mu^{\prime}\in[0,1-\eta]
, we construct a joint distribution over
(X,X^{\prime})
via independent coordinatewise maximal couplings as in
Theorem

A.6
of the marginals
\mathcal{N}(\mu^{\prime},1)
and
\mathcal{N}(\mu^{\prime}+\eta,1)
respectively. In this case,
X\sim P_{\mu^{\prime}}
and
X^{\prime}\sim P_{\mu^{\prime}+\eta}
. Let
D\coloneqq\#\{i\in[n]\colon X_{i}\not=X_{i}^{\prime}\}
be the number of coordinates where the samples differ. By
Theorem

A.6
,
D\sim\operatorname{Bin}(n,p)
where the success probability
p
satisfies
p<\eta
. Let
k\coloneqq\lfloor\eta n\rfloor
, we see that there exists an absolute constant
\rho\in(0,1)
such that
\mathbb{E}[D]\leq\rho k
for all
\eta\in(0,1/10]
. Therefore, a standard Chernoff bound argument as in
Theorem

A.7
gives us a constant
c_{\mathsf{Ch}}>0
such that
\mathbb{P}(D>k)\leq\exp(-c_{\mathsf{Ch}}k)
. We may choose
n
and
C_{\mathsf{high}}
large enough such that in the regime
k\geq C_{\mathsf{high}}\log n
, we are guaranteed that
\mathbb{P}(D>k)\leq\eta/6
for all
n
.

On the event
\{D\leq k\}
, the datasets
X
and
X^{\prime}
differ by at most
k
points. Therefore,
X^{\prime}
is a valid
\eta
-corruption of
X
. By definition,
S_{\eta}^{h}(X)\geq|h(X^{\prime})-h(X)|\cdot\mathbf{1}\{D\leq k\}
. Since
h\in[0,1]
, we have
|h(X^{\prime})-h(X)|\leq 1
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P_{\mu^{\prime}}}[S_{\eta}^{h}(X)]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}\left[|h(X^{\prime})-h(X)|\cdot\mathbf{1}\{D\leq k\}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}[(h(X^{\prime})-h(X))\cdot\mathbf{1}\{D\leq k\}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[h(X^{\prime})-h(X)]-\mathbb{E}[(h(X^{\prime})-h(X))\cdot\mathbf{1}\{D>k\}]

\displaystyle\geq a(\mu^{\prime}+\eta)-a(\mu^{\prime})-\mathbb{P}(D>k).

To conclude the proof, we average this final inequality over the uniform prior
\mathcal{U}
. This gives us

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mu^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{U}}\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P_{\mu^{\prime}}}[S_{\eta}^{h}(X)]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}_{\mu^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{U}}[a(\mu^{\prime}+\eta)-a(\mu^{\prime})]-\mathbb{P}(D>k)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\eta}{3}-\frac{\eta}{6}=\frac{\eta}{6}.

This implies
\sup_{\mu^{\prime}\in[0,1]}\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P_{\mu^{\prime}}}[S_{\eta}^{h}(X)]\geq\frac{\eta}{6}
, which is what we wanted. By
Lemma

D.4
, we thus have
\sup_{\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathsf{ES}_{\eta,2}(f;\mu)\geq\frac{\eta}{6}
, which proves
Theorem

3.5
.

Appendix H
Proof of
Theorem

3.6

The proof of
Theorem

3.6
can be split into two parts. First, we show that uniform MSE accuracy forces the estimator to have output variance
\Omega(d/n)
at some parameter point. Then we convert this clean-sample variance into a corruption strategy via block resampling.

H.1
Existence of large variance parameter

We first prove the statistical part of the variance obstruction. To do this, we give two proofs. The first is the shorter Cramer-Rao proof, which assumes a regularity condition on the mean map. The second is the Hammersley-Chapman-Robbins proof, which may be applied to broader distribution classes, without using similar regularity assumptions.

Throughout, for
X=(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu,\mathbb{I}_{d})^{\otimes n}
, we write

\mathrm{Var}_{\mu}(f)\coloneqq\mathrm{Var}_{\mu}(f(X))\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{X}\|f(X)-\mathbb{E}_{X}f(X)\|^{2}.

Proposition H.1
.

Let
f\colon(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be measurable and suppose that

\sup_{\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbb{E}_{X}\|f(X)-\mu\|^{2}\leq C_{\mathsf{MSE}}\frac{d}{n}.

Then there exists a universal constant
c_{\mathrm{var}}>0
and a parameter
\mu^{\ast}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

\mathrm{Var}_{\mu^{\ast}}(f)\geq c_{\mathrm{var}}\frac{d}{n}.

We will first show two different proofs for the scalar version of
Proposition

H.1
, then we will use the same argument to lift this to the
d
-dimensional version. Indeed, for the scalar version: consider
g=g(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n})
under
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{iid}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}(\mu^{\prime},1)
, where we write
a(\mu^{\prime})\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{Z}[g(Z)]
and
\mathrm{Var}_{\mu^{\prime}}(g)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{Z}[(g-a(\mu^{\prime}))^{2}]
.

H.1.1
Proof I of Scalar Version of
Proposition

H.1

This proof is the Cramer-Rao proof, which requires the standard regularity condition that the mean map
a(\mu^{\prime})
is absolutely continuous and satisfies the Gaussian score identity.

Define the bias
b(\mu^{\prime}):=a(\mu^{\prime})-\mu^{\prime}
. By Jensen’s inequality and the uniform MSE accuracy bound, we have

|b(\mu^{\prime})|=|\mathbb{E}_{Z}[g(Z)-\mu^{\prime}]|\leq\left(\mathbb{E}_{Z}[(g(Z)-\mu^{\prime})^{2}]\right)^{1/2}\leq\sqrt{\frac{C_{\mathsf{MSE}}}{n}}

for every
\mu^{\prime}
. Therefore, for
L\coloneqq 4\max\left\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}},\sqrt{\frac{C_{\mathsf{MSE}}}{n}}\right\}
, we have

a(L)-a(0)=L+b(L)-b(0)\geq L-2\sqrt{\frac{C_{\mathsf{MSE}}}{n}}\geq\frac{L}{2}.

By
Theorem

A.5
, applied to the Gaussian location family

P_{\mu^{\prime}}=\mathcal{N}(\mu^{\prime},1)^{\otimes n}
and the statistic
T=g
, the Fisher information is

I(\mu^{\prime})=n
. Therefore, under the stated regularity assumptions on the mean map

a(\mu^{\prime})=\mathbb{E}_{\mu^{\prime}}g
, we have

\mathrm{Var}_{\mu^{\prime}}(g)\geq\frac{(a^{\prime}(\mu^{\prime}))^{2}}{n}.

Averaging over
[0,L]
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{L}\int_{0}^{L}\mathrm{Var}_{\mu^{\prime}}(g)\ d\mu^{\prime}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{nL}\int_{0}^{L}a^{\prime}(\mu^{\prime})^{2}\ d\mu^{\prime}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{n}\left(\frac{1}{L}\int_{0}^{L}a^{\prime}(\mu^{\prime})\ d\mu^{\prime}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\left(\frac{a(L)-a(0)}{L}\right)^{2}\geq\frac{1}{4n}.

This proves the claim for
d=1
.

H.1.2
Proof II of Scalar Version of
Proposition

H.1

We now give the Hammersley-Chapman-Robbins proof. This proof avoids any differentiability assumptions. Let us consider
h\coloneqq\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}
,
L\coloneqq 4\max\left\{\sqrt{\frac{C_{\mathsf{MSE}}}{n}},h\right\}
and
J\coloneqq\left\lceil\frac{L}{h}\right\rceil
.

Now, we consider the grid points
\mu_{\ell}^{\prime}\coloneqq\ell h
for
\ell=0,\dots,J
. Since
\mu_{J}^{\prime}=Jh\geq L
and
|b(\mu^{\prime})|\leq\sqrt{\frac{C_{\mathsf{MSE}}}{n}}
, it follows that

a(\mu_{J}^{\prime})-a(\mu_{0}^{\prime})=\mu_{J}^{\prime}-\mu_{0}^{\prime}+b(\mu_{J}^{\prime})-b(\mu_{0}^{\prime})\geq L-2\sqrt{\frac{C_{\mathsf{MSE}}}{n}}\geq\frac{L}{2}.

For each
\ell=0,\dots,J-1
, we let
P_{\ell}
and
Q_{\ell}
denote the probability distribution
\mathcal{N}(\mu_{\ell}^{\prime},1)^{\otimes n}
and
\mathcal{N}(\mu_{\ell+1}^{\prime},1)^{\otimes n}
respectively. Because
\mu_{\ell+1}^{\prime}-\mu_{\ell}^{\prime}=h=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}
, we have

\chi^{2}(Q_{\ell}\|P_{\ell})=\exp(nh^{2})-1=e-1.

Applying
Theorem

A.4
with
P=P_{\ell}=\mathcal{N}(\mu_{\ell}^{\prime},1)^{\otimes n},Q=Q_{\ell}=\mathcal{N}(\mu_{\ell+1}^{\prime},1)^{\otimes n}
and
T=g
, we obtain

\mathrm{Var}_{\mu_{\ell}^{\prime}}(g)\geq\frac{(a(\mu_{\ell+1}^{\prime})-a(\mu_{\ell}^{\prime}))^{2}}{e-1}.

Summing over
\ell=0,\dots,J-1
and apply Cauchy Schwarz gives us

\sum_{\ell=0}^{J-1}\mathrm{Var}_{\mu_{\ell}^{\prime}}(g)\geq\frac{1}{e-1}\sum_{\ell=0}^{J-1}(a(\mu_{\ell+1}^{\prime})-a(\mu_{\ell}^{\prime}))^{2}\geq\frac{1}{(e-1)J}(a(\mu_{J}^{\prime})-a(\mu_{0}^{\prime}))^{2}\geq\frac{L^{2}}{4(e-1)J}.

Consequently, the average variance satisfies

\frac{1}{J+1}\sum_{\ell=0}^{J}\mathrm{Var}_{\mu_{\ell}^{\prime}}(g)\geq\frac{L^{2}}{4(e-1)J(J+1)}\geq\frac{2}{15(e-1)}\cdot\frac{1}{n}

This proves the claim for
d=1
.

H.1.3
Proof of
Proposition

H.1

We will now state the following general lemma that allows one to lift our argument from one dimension to higher dimension, as long as one can get an averaging measure over scalar parameters under which the expected output variance is
\Omega(1/n)
.

Lemma H.2
.

Fix
C_{\mathsf{MSE}}>0
and set
C_{0}:=C_{\mathsf{MSE}}d
. Suppose there is a probability measure
\nu_{C_{0}}
on
\mathbb{R}
and a universal constant
c_{\mathsf{sc}}>0
with the following property: For every auxiliary random element
W
whose law does not depend on
\theta
, and every real-valued statistics
g=g(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n},W)
, with
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{iid}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}(\theta,1)
independent of
W
, then

\sup_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{E}[(g-\theta)^{2}]\leq\frac{C_{0}}{n}\implies\int\mathrm{Var}_{\theta}(g)\ d\nu_{C_{0}}(\theta)\geq\frac{c_{\mathsf{sc}}}{n}

holds, where the variance is over both
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n}
and
W
. Then every measurable estimator
f:(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfying

\sup_{\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbb{E}\|f(X)-\mu\|_{2}^{2}\leq C_{\mathsf{MSE}}\frac{d}{n}

has a parameter
\mu^{\ast}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\mathrm{Var}_{\mu^{\ast}}(f)\geq c_{\mathsf{sc}}\frac{d}{n}
.

Proof.

Let
C_{0}\coloneqq C_{\mathsf{MSE}}d
, and let
\nu\coloneqq\nu_{C_{0}}
be the scalar averaging measure supplied by the assumption. We average the parameters
\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
under the product measure
\nu^{\otimes d}
.

Fix a coordinate
j\in[d]
, and fix the remaining coordinates
\mu_{-j}=(\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{j-1},\mu_{j+1},\dots,\mu_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
. Consider the scalar submodel
\mu(\theta)\coloneqq(\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{j-1},\theta,\mu_{j+1},\dots,\mu_{d})
for
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
. For
X\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu(\theta),I_{d})^{\otimes n}
, let
Z_{i}\coloneqq X_{i,j}
for
i\in[n]
, and let
W\coloneqq(X_{i,\ell})_{1\leq i\leq n,\ \ell\neq j}
denote all other coordinates of the sample. Then
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{iid}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}(\theta,1)
and
W
is independent of
Z
. Crucially, because
\mu_{-j}
is fixed, the law of
W
does not depend on
\theta
.

Define the scalar statistic
g_{j,\mu_{-j}}(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n},W)\coloneqq f_{j}(X)
. For every
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
, the uniform MSE assumption implies that the error of this single coordinate is bounded by the total vector error:
\mathbb{E}[(g_{j,\mu_{-j}}-\theta)^{2}]\leq\mathbb{E}\|f(X)-\mu(\theta)\|_{2}^{2}\leq C_{\mathsf{MSE}}\frac{d}{n}=\frac{C_{0}}{n}
. Therefore, the scalar averaging principle applies, yielding
\int\mathrm{Var}_{\mu(\theta)}(f_{j}(X))\,d\nu(\theta)\geq c_{\mathrm{sc}}/n
for every fixed
\mu_{-j}
. Averaging this inequality over
\mu_{-j}\sim\nu^{\otimes(d-1)}
via Fubini’s theorem yields
\mathbb{E}_{\mu\sim\nu^{\otimes d}}[\mathrm{Var}_{\mu}(f_{j}(X))]\geq c_{\mathrm{sc}}/n
.

Summing over all
d
coordinates gives the total expected variance:

\mathbb{E}_{\mu\sim\nu^{\otimes d}}[\mathrm{Var}_{\mu}(f)]=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}_{\mu\sim\nu^{\otimes d}}[\mathrm{Var}_{\mu}(f_{j}(X))]\geq c_{\mathrm{sc}}\frac{d}{n}.

Since the expected variance under
\mu\sim\nu^{\otimes d}
is at least
c_{\mathrm{sc}}d/n
, there must exist at least one parameter
\mu^{\ast}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\mathrm{Var}_{\mu^{\ast}}(f)\geq c_{\mathrm{sc}}d/n
.
∎

To finish this, we may instantiate the above lifting principle theorem with two different scalar proof that we have established. The Cramer-Rao scalar proof proves the scalar averaging principle using the continuous measure
\nu_{C_{0}}=\mathrm{Unif}([0,L])
,
c_{\mathrm{sc}}=\frac{1}{4}
, where
L=4\max\{\sqrt{C_{0}/n},1/\sqrt{n}\}
, provided that the scalar mean map satisfies the Cramer Rao regularity assumptions.

Alternatively, the Hammersley–Chapman–Robbins scalar proof establishes the same principle using the discrete measure
\nu_{C_{0}}=\mathrm{Unif}\{0,h,2h,\dots,Jh\}
and
c_{\mathrm{sc}}=\frac{2}{15(e-1)}
, where
h\coloneqq 1/\sqrt{n}
,
L=4\max\{\sqrt{C_{0}/n},h\}
, and
J\coloneqq\lceil L/h\rceil
.

This fully establishes
Proposition

H.1
.

H.2
Conversion into Corruption Strategy

Fix a parameter
\mu^{\ast}
from
Proposition

H.1
, and let
X=(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu^{\ast},\mathbb{I}_{d})^{\otimes n}
. Let
k=\lfloor\eta n\rfloor\geq 1
. Partition
[n]
into
M\coloneqq\lceil n/k\rceil
disjoint nonempty blocks
B_{1},\dots,B_{M}
of sizes at most
k
. Let
Y_{i}=(X_{\ell})_{\ell\in B_{i}}
denote the data in block
B_{i}
. Equivalently, we may view
X
as the collection
(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{M})
. Let
Y_{i}^{\prime}
be an independent fresh copy of
Y_{i}
, independent of all other variables, and let
X^{(i)}
denote the dataset obtained from
X
by replacing block
Y_{i}
by
Y_{i}^{\prime}
.

We note that by definition, each
X^{(i)}
differs from
X
in at most
k
sample positions. Therefore, pointwise, we have

S_{\eta}^{f}(X)^{2}\geq\|f(X)-f(X^{(i)})\|^{2},\quad\forall i=1,2,\dots,M.

By vector Efron-Stein inequality in
Theorem

A.3
: for independent blocks
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{M}
and independent replacements
Y_{i}^{\prime}
, we have

\mathrm{Var}_{\mu^{\ast}}(f(X))\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\mathbb{E}\|f(X)-f(X^{(i)})\|^{2}.

Hence, we see that

\max_{1\leq i\leq M}\mathbb{E}\|f(X)-f(X^{(i)})\|^{2}\geq\frac{2}{M}\mathrm{Var}_{\mu^{\ast}}(f(X)).

Combining this with
Proposition

H.1
, we see that there exists some constant
c_{0}>0
such that

\mathbb{E}_{X}[S_{\eta}^{f}(X)^{2}]\geq c_{0}\frac{\eta d}{n}.

Therefore, we have

\sup_{\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathsf{ES}_{\eta,2}(f;\mu)=\sup_{\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\mathbb{E}_{X}[S_{\eta}^{f}(X)^{2}]\right)^{1/2}\geq\sqrt{c_{0}}\sqrt{\frac{\eta d}{n}},

and taking
C_{\ref{const:varLower}}\coloneqq\sqrt{c_{0}}
proves
Theorem

3.6
.

Appendix I
Proof of
Theorem

4.1

Throughout this section, for
t\in\{0,\dots,n\}
, define the Hamming-weight layer
\mathsf{Layer}(t)\coloneqq\{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}:|x|=t\}
. Let
U_{t}
denote a uniformly random element of
\mathsf{Layer}(t)
.

Lemma I.1
.

Let
P\sim\mathsf{Unif}[0,1]
and, conditional on
P=p
, sample
X\sim\operatorname{Bern}(p)^{\otimes n}
. Let
T\coloneqq|X|=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
. Then
\mathbb{P}(T=t)=\frac{1}{n+1}
for all
t\in\{0,\dots,n\}
. Therefore, conditional on
T=t
,
X
is uniform on
\mathsf{Layer}(t)
.

Proof.

For
t\in\{0,\dots,n\}
, we have

\mathbb{P}(T=t)=\int_{0}^{1}\binom{n}{t}p^{t}(1-p)^{n-t}\ dp=\binom{n}{t}\frac{t!(n-t)!}{(n+1)!}=\frac{1}{n+1}.

∎

Lemma I.2
.

Fix integers
t,\ell
with
0\leq t\leq n-\ell
. Sample
U_{t}
uniformly from
\mathsf{Layer}(t)
. Conditional on
U_{t}=x
, choose an
\ell
-subset
I
uniformly among the zero coordinates of
x
and flip those coordinates to
1
. Let
V
be the resulting vector. Then
V\sim\mathsf{Unif}(\mathsf{Layer}(t+\ell))
and
d_{H}(U_{t},V)=\ell
.

Proof.

The Hamming-distance claim is immediate because exactly
\ell
zero coordinates are flipped to one. It remains to prove uniformity. Fix
y\in\mathsf{Layer}(t+\ell)
. A pair
(x,I)
maps to
y
exactly when
I
is an
\ell
-subset of the one coordinates of
y
, and
x
is obtained from
y
by changing the coordinates in
I
back to zero. Thus, every
y\in\mathsf{Layer}(t+\ell)
has exactly
\binom{t+\ell}{\ell}
preimages. Since the sampling probability of each valid pair
(x,I)
is the same,
V
is thus uniform on
\mathsf{Layer}(t+\ell)
.
∎

We are now ready to prove
Theorem

4.1
. Indeed, we may replace
f=\operatorname{clip}_{[0,1]}\circ f
by
Lemma

D.3
.

Let
\varepsilon\coloneqq\frac{C_{\mathsf{Bern}}}{\sqrt{n}}
. Choose a constant
\alpha=\alpha(C_{\mathsf{Bern}})\in(0,1/4]
small enough that
2\alpha+\frac{2C_{\mathsf{Bern}}}{c_{*}}\sqrt{\alpha}\leq\frac{1}{2}
. Let
k\coloneqq\lfloor\eta n\rfloor
and define
\ell\coloneqq\min\{k,\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor\}
. For all sufficiently large
n
, depending only on
\alpha
, the assumptions
k\geq 1
and
n
sufficiently large imply
1\leq\ell\leq k
and
\ell\leq\frac{n}{2}
.

For each
t\in\{0,\dots,n\}
, define
a_{t}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[f(U_{t})]
. Fix
t\in\{0,\dots,n-\ell\}
. By
Lemma

I.2
, we can couple
U_{t}
and
U_{t+\ell}
so that
d_{H}(U_{t},U_{t+\ell})=\ell\leq k
. Hence
\mathbb{E}[S_{\eta}^{f}(U_{t})]\geq\mathbb{E}[|f(U_{t+\ell})-f(U_{t})|]\geq|a_{t+\ell}-a_{t}|
. Therefore, we have

\sum_{t=0}^{n-\ell}\mathbb{E}[S_{\eta}^{f}(U_{t})]\geq\sum_{t=0}^{n-\ell}|a_{t+\ell}-a_{t}|.

Decompose RHS into arithmetic progressions modulo
\ell
. For each residue
r\in\{0,\dots,\ell-1\}
, write
r,r+\ell,\dots,r+T_{r}\ell
, where
T_{r}\coloneqq\lfloor\frac{n-r}{\ell}\rfloor
and
j_{r}\coloneqq r+T_{r}\ell
. Then
j_{r}\in\{n-\ell+1,\dots,n\}
. By the triangle inequality along this arithmetic progression,
\sum_{q=0}^{T_{r}-1}|a_{r+(q+1)\ell}-a_{r+q\ell}|\geq|a_{j_{r}}-a_{r}|
. Summing over
r=0,\dots,\ell-1
yields

\sum_{t=0}^{n-\ell}|a_{t+\ell}-a_{t}|\geq\sum_{r=0}^{\ell-1}|a_{j_{r}}-a_{r}|.

We now lower-bound the boundary gaps. Fix
r\in\{0,\dots,\ell-1\}
and set
p=\frac{r}{n}
. If
X\sim\operatorname{Bern}(p)^{\otimes n}
and
T=|X|
, then conditional on
T=r
,
X
is uniform on
\mathsf{Layer}(r)
. Therefore,

\varepsilon\geq\mathbb{E}[|f(X)-p|]\geq\mathbb{P}(T=r)\mathbb{E}[|f(U_{r})-p|]\geq\mathbb{P}(T=r)|a_{r}-p|.

We know that since
T\sim\mathsf{Bin}(n,p)
,
Lemma

A.8
gives us
\mathbb{P}(T=r)\geq\frac{c_{\ast}}{\sqrt{r+1}}\geq\frac{c_{\ast}}{\sqrt{\ell}}
for some positive constant
c_{\ast}
. Therefore,
|a_{r}-r/n|\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{c_{\ast}}\sqrt{\ell}
. Since
r\leq\ell-1
, we have
a_{r}\leq\frac{\ell}{n}+\frac{\varepsilon}{c_{\ast}}\sqrt{\ell}
. Similarly, fix
j\in\{n-\ell+1,\dots,n\}
and write
j=n-s
with
0\leq s\leq\ell-1
. Set
p=1-\frac{s}{n}
. By the same argument, we have
|a_{j}-j/n|\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{c_{\ast}}\sqrt{\ell}
, and thus we get
a_{j}\geq 1-\frac{\ell}{n}-\frac{\varepsilon}{c_{\ast}}\sqrt{\ell}
.

Combining both of these, we see that for every
r\in\{0,\dots,\ell-1\}
,
|a_{j_{r}}-a_{r}|\geq 1-\frac{2\ell}{n}-\frac{2\varepsilon}{c_{\ast}}\sqrt{\ell}
. Because
\ell\leq\alpha n
and
\varepsilon=\frac{C_{\mathsf{Bern}}}{\sqrt{n}}
, our choice of
\alpha
guarantees that
|a_{j_{r}}-a_{r}|\geq\frac{1}{2}
for all residues
r
. Substituting this back into our telescoping sums give us

\sum_{t=0}^{n-\ell}\mathbb{E}[S_{\eta}^{f}(U_{t})]\geq\frac{\ell}{2}.

To conclude, let
P\sim\mathsf{Unif}[0,1]
and, conditional on
P=p
, sample
X\sim\operatorname{Bern}(p)^{\otimes n}
. By
Lemma

I.1
,

\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\operatorname{Bern}(p)^{\otimes n}}[S_{\eta}^{f}(X)]\ dp=\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{t=0}^{n}\mathbb{E}[S_{\eta}^{f}(U_{t})]\geq\frac{\ell}{2(n+1)}.

Therefore this also gives us

\sup_{p\in[0,1]}\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\operatorname{Bern}(p)^{\otimes n}}[S_{\eta}^{f}(X)]\geq\frac{\ell}{2(n+1)}.

It remains to compare
\frac{\ell}{n+1}
to
\eta
. Indeed, suppose
k\leq\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor
. Then
\ell=k
. In this case,
\frac{k}{n}\geq\frac{\eta}{2}
, and thus
\frac{\ell}{2(n+1)}\geq\frac{\eta}{8}
. Otherwise, in the case
k>\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor
, then
\ell=\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor
. For all sufficiently large
n
, we have
\ell\geq\frac{\alpha n}{2}
which therefore gives us the bound
\frac{\ell}{2(n+1)}\geq\frac{\alpha}{8}\geq\frac{\alpha}{8}\eta
. This shows us

\sup_{p\in[0,1]}\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\operatorname{Bern}(p)^{\otimes n}}[S_{\eta}^{f}(X)]\geq\frac{\alpha}{8}\eta.

Therefore, this theorem holds with
C_{\ref{const:bernoulli}}\coloneqq\frac{\alpha}{8}
.
