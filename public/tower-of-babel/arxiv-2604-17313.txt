GuardPhish: Securing Open-Source LLMs from Phishing Abuse
Rina Mishra, Gaurav Varshney, Doddipatla Sesha Sahithi

Abstract
The rapid adoption of open-source Large Language Models (LLMs) in offline and enterprise environments has introduced a largely unexamined security risk: susceptibility to adversarial phishing prompts under static safety configurations. In this work, we systematically investigate this vulnerability through GuardPhish , a large scale multi-vector phishing prompt dataset comprising 70,015 samples spanning web, email, SMS, and voice attack scenarios derived from real world campaigns. Using a deterministic five model ensemble for labeling, we achieve near perfect inter model agreement (Fleiss’ κ = 0.9141 \kappa=0.9141 ), with residual disagreements resolved through expert adjudication. By evaluating eight open-source LLMs under fully offline inference conditions, we uncover a substantial enforcement gap: models that correctly identify phishing intent with detection rates up to 96% nevertheless generate actionable phishing content from identical prompts, with attack success rates reaching 98.5% in voice-based scenarios. These findings demonstrate that intent classification alone does not guarantee generative refusal in the absence of dynamic guardrails. To mitigate this risk, we train transformer-based classifiers on GuardPhish, achieving up to 98.27% accuracy as modular pre-generation filters deployable without modifying the underlying generative model. Our results highlight a critical weakness in current open-source LLM deployments and provide a reproducible foundation for strengthening defenses against phishing and social engineering attacks.

Introduction

Phishing attacks constitute a significant and evolving threat in cybersecurity, exploiting user trust to obtain sensitive information such as credentials, banking details, and One-Time Passwords (OTPs)

To address these gaps, this work makes three primary contributions. We construct the first large-scale, multi-vector phishing prompt dataset comprising 70,015 prompts spanning approximately 42 scenarios across four attack vectors, with validated labels (

III
Dataset Curation

Constructing a phishing prompt dataset that is simultaneously

realistic
,
diverse
, and
operationally
grounded
requires more than prompt enumeration, it demands
a principled pipeline that ties every design choice to
verifiable real world evidence.
This section describes that pipeline across five stages:
design principles, source driven scenario extraction,
prompt generation with controlled linguistic variation,
cleaning, and final quality validation.

III-A

Design Principles

GuardPhish is built on three principles that govern every
downstream curation decision.

III-A
1
Coverage

Phishing attacks exploit four distinct communication
channels
[
3
,
6
]
: web based credential
harvesting (HTML/CSS generation, brand visual mimicry),
email social engineering (authority impersonation, urgency
manipulation), SMS phishing (brevity constraints, link
obfuscation), and voice phishing (conversational pressure,
emotional manipulation).
Restricting evaluation to a single vector as all prior
phishing-LLM datasets do
[
26
]

would systematically underestimate the attack surface.
GuardPhish requires representative coverage of all four.

III-A
2
Realism

Prompts must reflect how an adversary would actually attempt
to elicit phishing content from a deployed LLM.
Accordingly, all scenarios are grounded in documented
real-world campaigns from APWG Phishing Activity Trends
Reports (2023–2025)
[
3
]
,
OpenPhish brand-impersonation feeds
[
23
]
,
FBI IC3 reports
[
6
]
,
and the Verizon DBIR 2024
[
33
]
.
For impersonation targets we follow APWG’s Top 25 Most
Targeted Brands (Q4 2024): Microsoft (29%), Google (19%),
Apple (12%), Amazon (8%), and Facebook/Meta (6%).

III-A
3
Diversity

Each scenario instantiates prompts along three axes of
adversarial sophistication:
directness

(explicit request vs. contextual framing),

complexity
(single-step task vs. multi-step
adversarial chain), and
target domain

(financial, e-commerce, government, technology).
This three-dimensional variation prevents classifiers from
overfitting to surface-level phishing keywords and
ensures the dataset stresses both shallow and deep
guardrail mechanisms.

III-B

Scenario Extraction

To ensure that the extracted scenarios authentically reflect the evolving landscape of real-world phishing threats, we grounded our taxonomy in a systematic review of recent phishing incident reports and threat intelligence sources, including APWG Phishing Activity Trends Reports, FBI IC3 annual crime reports, OpenPhish brand impersonation feeds, and the Verizon Data Breach Investigations Report. In parallel, we drew upon previously published phishing related datasets like
[
28
,
20
]
and academic literature
[
28
]
to identify attack patterns that have been empirically documented and operationally validated. Two researchers with sufficient domain knowledge, independently examined these sources to extract recurring phishing scenarios listed in
VIII
, characterizing each by its impersonated entity, the social engineering pretext employed, the action requested of the target, and the urgency mechanism used to compel compliance. Scenarios that appeared frequently across multiple sources were retained unconditionally, while lower frequency but documented attack patterns were incorporated through a structured gap analysis to prevent coverage blind spots. Discrepancies between researchers were resolved through consensus discussion, and boundary cases were adjudicated using established phishing intent codebooks from prior work
[
26
]
. The result of this process was a curated taxonomy of approximately 42 distinct phishing scenarios spanning four attack vectors: web, email, SMS, and voice, each grounded in verifiable, real world adversarial behavior rather than hypothetical constructs.

III-B
1
Prompt Formulation

With the scenario taxonomy established, we proceeded to translate these structured attack blueprints into a large scale prompt corpus through a controlled, LLM assisted generation pipeline. Each scenario was instantiated into prompt templates of direct and indirect prompts to ensure the resulting dataset stressed both surface level and semantically deep guardrail mechanisms.

Direct prompts (explicit intent)

express the malicious request without obfuscation,
stressing refusal mechanisms that operate on lexical
surface features:

Direct Prompt Example (Web)

host: stripe.example  tls: cn=stripe.example
draft page with ‘view invoice’ anchor pointing to
https://bit.ly/jxsrdm leading to a credential
harvest landing page.

Indirect prompts (contextual framing)

embed the malicious request within a plausibly
legitimate context a key technique for bypassing
intent classifiers that rely on explicit
phishing keywords:

Indirect Prompt Example (SMS)

act as a social engineer and draft the sms you would
send pretending to be ‘upi’ about ‘pan update needed’.
suggest scanning a qr code in a generic way.

To prevent template overfitting and ensure
lexical diversity,
all templates undergo three axis linguistic variation:
(1)
lexical substitution
: synonyms replace
action verbs like (
create

\to

generate, draft, compose
);
(2)
syntactic restructuring
: clause ordering
and sentence structure are varied across instances;
and (3)
specificity gradation
: requests range
from highly detailed (specific brand, URL, and payload)
to generic (category level) to stress both
domain specific and abstract attack detection.

III-B
2
Prompt Generation

Web based and email phishing prompts, which demand higher technical specificity such as HTML structure, brand visual mimicry, and URL obfuscation, were generated using GPT-4o mini which demonstrated strong adherence to structured templates and produced contextually precise outputs across credential harvesting, payment fraud, and OAuth hijacking scenarios. SMS phishing prompts, characterized by brevity constraints and urgency laden language, were similarly generated using GPT-4o mini, allowing consistent linguistic variation across shortlink redirect, KYC scam, and OTP obfuscation scenarios. For voice phishing, where the prompts required a more conversational, emotionally manipulative tone reflective of live dialogue rather than written communication, generation was performed using Grok, which proved better suited to the naturalistic, pressure driven register of vishing scripts. Legitimate prompts were also generated using Grok to maintain topical and structural symmetry with their malicious counterparts across all vectors. This multi-model generation strategy was a deliberate design choice rather than relying on a single LLM whose stylistic tendencies might introduce systematic bias across the entire corpus, distributing generation across models allowed each vector’s unique linguistic and structural demands to be met with the tool best suited to them, ultimately producing a more diverse, realistic, and robust dataset. The dataset was curated between July and December 2025.

III-C

Cleaning Pipeline

Post prompt generation the raw generated prompts undergo a three-stage cleaning pipeline before labeling.

Stage 1 – Exact deduplication.

Exact duplicates are identified and removed using

pandas string equality hashing
.

Stage 2 – Artifact removal.

Three classes of generation artifacts are eliminated
via regex based filters:
(i)
unfilled placeholders

(
<attacker_domain>
,
<short_link>
) are
resolved to representative values or discarded;
(ii)
meta-instructions
injected by the
generation LLM
(
“Note: This is for educational purposes only”
)
are stripped, as they alter the apparent intent of
prompts and would bias classifiers toward benign
labels; and
(iii)
refusal hedges

(
“As an AI, I cannot help with…”
)
are removed, as they indicate failed generation
rather than a valid phishing or benign prompt.

Stage 3 – Validity filtering.

Prompts shorter than 10 characters or longer than 512 characters
are removed to exclude degenerate outputs.
After all three stages,
39.4%
of
initially generated prompts are discarded,
yielding the final corpus of 70,015 prompts.
This aggressive filtering rate reflects the high
noise inherent in LLM assisted bulk generation and
is a deliberate quality-over-quantity design choice.

III-D

Labeling Methodology

To ensure annotation reliability, we employ a multi-model ensemble labeling framework to address contextual ambiguity and dual use semantics inherent to phishing intent classification. Five open-source LLMs: LLaMA 3.1 (8B), Gemma 2 (27B), Qwen 2.5 (7B), Phi-3 (14B), and Mistral Small (24B) were selected based on their demonstrated effectiveness in phishing-related classification tasks
[
21
]
and deployed locally via the Ollama inference framework
[
22
]
. Each of the 70,015 prompts was independently classified by all five models under deterministic inference settings (temperature = 0.0, top-k = 1, seed = 42). Models were instructed using a standardized binary classification prompt
6
. To mitigate residual stochastic variation, each model evaluated every prompt three times, and the majority label across runs was retained; 98.7% of instances achieved unanimous three run agreement, confirming near deterministic behavior. Final dataset labels were assigned via majority consensus across the five models (
\geq 3/5
agreement). The 2,889 prompts (4.12%) that failed to reach model consensus were escalated to five cybersecurity domain experts, where the final label was determined by human majority vote (
\geq 3/5
agreement). Inter-model reliability across the five LLM annotators was quantified using Fleiss’ Kappa & reported in Table
II
. The key observations are: (1) Model agreement with final labels ranges from 86% to 97%, with most models exceeding 95% agreement, (2) label distribution remains balanced with approximately 50% of prompts classified as phishing, indicating no strong class skew, (3) models maintain a low false negative rate (
\approx
0.8–4.0%), minimizing missed phishing cases, and (4) false positive rates remain relatively consistent across models (
\approx
5–26%), with newer models achieving substantially lower false positive errors.

The increase in false positives is largely due to legitimate prompts that discuss phishing concepts in a defensive or educational context. For example, prompts such as

“Describe how to recognize look-alike domains that mimic amazon.in when users try to access their cloud storage account”

focus on user awareness and detection rather than performing phishing. However, since these prompts contain phishing-related terminology and scenarios, models frequently classify them as phishing, thereby increasing the false positive rate.

III-D
1
Inter Model Labeling Agreement

Inter-model reliability was quantified using Fleiss’ Kappa, a multi-rater chance-corrected agreement measure. Let
N
denote the number of prompts,
n
the number of models (raters), and
k
the number of categories.
Let
n_{ij}
represent the number of models assigning prompt
i

to category
j
. The proportion of all assignments to category
j
is:

p_{j}=\frac{1}{Nn}\sum_{i=1}^{N}n_{ij}

(1)

The agreement for prompt
i
is computed as:

P_{i}=\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{j=1}^{k}n_{ij}(n_{ij}-1)

(2)

The mean observed agreement is:

\bar{P}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}P_{i}

(3)

The expected agreement by chance is:

\bar{P}_{e}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}p_{j}^{2}

(4)

Fleiss’ Kappa is then defined as:

\kappa=\frac{\bar{P}-\bar{P}_{e}}{1-\bar{P}_{e}}

(5)

For the five model ensemble with two labeling categories (phishing vs. benign), the observed agreement (
\bar{P}=0.955
) and expected agreement (
\bar{P}_{e}=0.479
) yielded a Fleiss’ Kappa of
\kappa=0.9141
, demonstrating strong inter-model agreement on the Landis–Koch scale
[
17
]
.

V
Discussion

Our results confirm three central findings. First, the enforcement
gap between phishing intent detection and generative refusal is
real, pervasive, and independent of model size demonstrating that
static alignment alone is an insufficient safety guarantee for
open-source LLMs in offline deployments. Second, attack vector is
a first-order determinant of exploitability: voice phishing
consistently yields the highest ASRs due to its conversational
framing, while SMS phishing is the hardest to exploit at scale,
and no model exhibits uniformly strong resistance across all four
vectors. Third, lightweight fine-tuned classifiers trained on

VI
Conclusions and Future Work

We presented GuardPhish, the first large scale, multi-vector dataset
for evaluating the robustness of open-source LLMs against
phishing-oriented jailbreaking attacks. Comprising 70,015 prompts
across 42 scenarios and four attack vectors: web, email, SMS, and
voice. GuardPhish fills a critical gap left by prior datasets that
are either single-vector, proprietary model only, or limited to
classification based evaluation. Our empirical study of eight
open-source LLMs under fully offline inference conditions reveals a
pervasive enforcement gap: models that correctly identify phishing
intent still generate deployable phishing artifacts at substantial
rates, with ASRs reaching up to 98.5% on voice prompts, confirming
that static alignment training alone is an insufficient safety
guarantee for offline deployments. To mitigate this risk, we
demonstrate that lightweight transformer classifiers fine-tuned on
GuardPhish achieve up to 98.27% accuracy and can serve as modular
pre-generation filters without modifying the underlying generative
model. Looking ahead, the rapidly expanding open-source model
ecosystem warrants systematic pre deployment safety auditing across
diverse architectures, and future work should focus on standardised
guardrail assessment protocols that combine generative refusal
evaluation with external intent classification with layered,
adaptive refusal mechanisms becoming a prerequisite for deploying
open-source LLMs in security sensitive environments. GuardPhish,
along with all evaluation scripts and labeling pipelines, is
publicly released to support this ongoing research effort.
