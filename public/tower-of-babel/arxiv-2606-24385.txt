Gradient Mean-Field Dynamics with Measure-Valued States: Well-Posedness, Chaos, and Long-Time Stability
Anderson Melchor Hernandez

Abstract
. We study a stochastic mean-field interacting particle system whose state space is 𝒴 = ℝ d × 𝒫 ⁡ ( U ) {\mathscr{Y}}={\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathscr{P}}(U) , where the first component represents a spatial variable and the second one is a probability measure over a compact metric space U U . The dynamics are driven by locally Lipschitz drift operators: the spatial component evolves according to a Brownian diffusion, while the measure-valued component is perturbed by a projected cylindrical noise acting in the Arens–Eells space. We first establish existence and uniqueness of strong solutions for both the N N -particle system and the associated nonlinear McKean–Vlasov equation under locally Lipschitz and linear growth assumptions on the drift coefficients. We then prove propagation of chaos: as N → ∞ N\to\infty , the empirical measure converges in expectation in Wasserstein–1 distance towards the unique McKean–Vlasov solution. Further, we investigate exponential convergence of the nonlinear McKean–Vlasov dynamics towards a unique invariant measure.



1.
Introduction

Mean-field interacting particle systems provide a powerful framework for describing the collective behavior of large populations of agents subject to deterministic interactions and stochastic fluctuations. Such models arise naturally in kinetic theory, statistical mechanics, interacting diffusions, and in the mathematical analysis of socio-economic systems, crowd dynamics, and multi-agent systems. In particular, kinetic and mean-field approaches have provided a systematic framework for the derivation of macroscopic models from microscopic interaction rules in crowd and swarming dynamics
[
11
]
. More recently, mean-field techniques have also played a central role in the analysis of learning dynamics and neural-type systems
[
13
,
28
,
31
]
.
From a modeling perspective, our approach is conceptually related to the active particle framework proposed by

[
12
]
. In that setting, the dynamics of a large population is derived from interaction rules prescribed at the level of individual agents. A statistical description of the system is then introduced,
leading to a kinetic equation governing the evolution of the population density. Similarly, in our framework, we start from a system of interacting stochastic differential equations describing the microscopic behavior
of each agent. The collective dynamics emerges by considering the empirical measure of the system and passing to the limit as the number of agents tends to infinity, which yields a nonlinear McKean–Vlasov equation. However, unlike the classical kinetic PDE formulation, our model involves a measure-valued internal state for each particle and evolves in an infinite-dimensional functional space.

In this work, we investigate a stochastic interacting particle system whose state space is
{\mathscr{Y}}={\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathscr{P}}(U)
, where the first component represents a spatial state variable evolving on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, and the second component is a probability measure over a compact set
U
. The dynamics couple a spatial diffusion with a measure-valued component evolving in a tangent-like space of
{\mathscr{P}}(U)
and perturbed by a generalized noise in the sense of Mamporia
[
23
,
24
]
. The interaction between particles is of mean-field type and depends on the empirical distribution of the full system
[
33
,
17
,
16
,
34
]
.

More precisely, we consider a system of
N
particles
(X^{i}(t),\lambda^{i}(t))_{i=1}^{N}
solving a coupled stochastic differential system with locally Lipschitz drift fields satisfying linear growth conditions. The associated empirical measure is defined by

(1.1)

\displaystyle\mu^{N}(t)\coloneqq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{(X^{i}(t),\lambda^{i}(t))}.

The corresponding nonlinear McKean–Vlasov limit equation reads

(1.2)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}{\rm d}Y(t)=H(Y(t),\Lambda(t),\mu(t))\,{\rm d}t+\sqrt{2\sigma_{x}}\,{\rm d}B(t),\\
{\rm d}\Lambda(t)={\mathcal{R}}(Y(t),\Lambda(t),\mu(t))\,{\rm d}t+\sqrt{2\sigma_{\lambda}}\,\Pi_{\Lambda(t)}\,{\rm d}W^{Q}(t),\end{cases}

where
\mu(t)={\rm Law}(Y(t),\Lambda(t))
. The first equation describes the spatial evolution on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, driven by the velocity field
H
and a
d
-dimensional Brownian motion. The second equation governs the evolution of the measure component
\Lambda(t)
and involves the velocity field
{\mathcal{R}}
together with a generalized cylindrical noise
W^{Q}(t)
acting in the Arens–Eells space
{\mathscr{F}}(U)
which is defined as the span of
{\mathscr{P}}(U)
with respect to the bounded Lipschitz norm
[
5
,
2
,
19
]
. Several applications have been proposed for systems of the form (
1.2
). One example is the follower–leader model, aimed at describing the collective behavior of a controlled population
Y(t)
influenced by a leader population distributed according to
\Lambda(t)

[
1
,
6
]
. In
[
18
]
, this framework was further developed to analyze a neuronal system in which the random variable
Y(t)
represents the evolution of the membrane potential of a neuron receiving inputs from other neurons in the network. We emphasize that the model (
1.2
) differs from those considered in
[
18
,
9
,
6
]
due to the presence of the generalized noise
W^{Q}(t)
. Roughly speaking, this object mimics the properties of a Wiener process when
Q
is a nuclear operator; however, its definition is formulated via duality, allowing us to consider cylindrical operators
[
29
,
22
]
. Unlike the previously studied models, the introduction of this term may destroy the positivity of
\Lambda(t)
. To overcome this issue, we project the stochastic perturbation onto the tangent space at
\Lambda(t)
through a suitable operator, denoted by
\Pi_{\Lambda(t)}
. This projection mechanism, combined with structural growth conditions on
Q
and a martingale argument, ensures that
\Lambda(t)
remains in
{\mathscr{P}}(U)
almost surely
[
30
]
.
We stress that the functional framework underlying the measure component relies on the structure of
{\mathscr{F}}(U)
, which is the canonical predual of the space of Lipschitz functions vanishing at a reference point. This dual characterization clarifies the compatibility between the bounded-Lipschitz norm on
{\mathscr{F}}(U)
and the Wasserstein-
1
distance (see (
2.5
) below). In particular, duality with Lipschitz functions naturally leads to estimates in
{\mathcal{W}}_{1}
, which can in turn be controlled by the
{\rm BL}
norm
[
26
,
2
,
9
,
18
]
.

Contributions.

Our analysis contributes to the mathematical theory of mean-field limits in a infinite-dimensional setting
[
6
,
7
,
8
,
18
]
. On the one hand, the measure-valued component of each particle evolves in the Arens–Eells space, and is driven by a projected cylindrical noise which is not assumed to be trace-class as usually done for the construction of a stochastic integral
[
22
,
29
]
. This goes beyond classical McKean–Vlasov dynamics with measure-dependent drifts, where the noise acts only on a finite-dimensional state and positivity is preserved by construction
[
9
,
18
]
. In our setting, a key technical difficulty stems from the fact that the noise does not preserve positivity a priori. In contrast to classical McKean–Vlasov models, where the law of each particle is automatically a probability measure and the stochastic perturbation does not act directly on the measure-valued coordinate, here the noise may drive
\Lambda(t)
out of
{\mathscr{P}}(U)

[
35
]
. A central contribution of this work is to show that, despite this, the combination of the projection onto a suitable tangent space, structural conditions on the covariance operator
Q
, and a martingale argument yields almost-sure preservation of positivity for
\Lambda(t)

[
15
,
30
]
. This allows us to treat a broader class of covariance operators than in previous works, while keeping the dynamics measure-valued. On the other hand, we obtain both well-posedness and propagation of chaos under purely local Lipschitz and linear growth conditions, and we establish exponential convergence to equilibrium under a natural gradient and uniform convexity structure, without invoking curvature or displacement convexity assumptions in the sense of the metric-gradient-flow theory
[
4
,
34
,
25
]
. We summarize our three principal results below.

Theorem 1.1

(Informal statement of
Theorem 4.1
)
.

Under locally Lipschitz and linear growth assumptions on the drift fields
H
and
{\mathcal{R}}
, together with suitable structural conditions on the operator
Q
, the nonlinear McKean–Vlasov system (
1.2
) admits a unique càdlàg strong solution
(Y(t),\Lambda(t))\in{\mathscr{Y}}
for all
t\in[0,T]
. Moreover, the
N
-particle system admits a unique strong solution on
[0,T]
.

This result provides the foundation for the analysis of propagation of chaos.

Theorem 1.2

(Informal statement of
Theorem 4.2
)
.

Let
\mu^{N}(t)
denote the empirical measure of the particle system and let
\mu(t)
be the unique solution of the McKean–Vlasov equation. Then, as
N\to\infty
,

(1.3)

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\mathbb{E}\big[{\mathcal{W}}_{1}(\mu^{N}(t),\mu(t))\big]\longrightarrow 0.

In particular, the particle system is chaotic with limit law
\mu(t)
.

The previous result holds on finite time intervals. However, under stronger structural assumptions on the velocity fields
H
and
{\mathcal{R}}
, it is possible to extend the analysis to the long-time regime.

Theorem 1.3

(Informal statement of
Theorem 4.3
)
.

Under additional assumptions imposing a gradient structure and uniform convexity of the drift fields, the nonlinear McKean–Vlasov dynamics admits a unique invariant measure
\mu_{\infty}
. Moreover, the flow converges exponentially fast:

(1.4)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{W}}_{1}(\mu(t),\mu_{\infty})\leq e^{-\kappa t}{\mathcal{W}}_{1}(\mu(0),\mu_{\infty}),

where
\mu(0)
denotes the law of the initial condition of (
1.2
) and
\kappa>0
.

As a consequence, we obtain uniform-in-time stability estimates for the mean-field limit, which transfer to the particle system.

2.1.
Probability measures

Let
(X,d_{X})
be a metric space, we denote by
{\mathcal{M}}(X)
the space of signed Borel measures with finite total variation, by
{\mathcal{M}}_{+}(X)
and
{\mathscr{P}}(X)
the convex subsets of nonnegative measures and probability measures, respectively. For
\sigma\in{\mathcal{M}}(X)
,
|\sigma|\in{\mathcal{M}}_{+}(X)
denotes the total variation measure of
\sigma
. We shall also use the notation
{\mathcal{M}}_{0}(X)
for the subset of measures with zero mean. Given a metric space
(X,d_{X})
, we consider the Lipschitz space

\displaystyle{\rm Lip}(X,d_{X}):=\left\{\phi:X\rightarrow{\mathcal{R}}|\exists\hskip 2.84544ptL>0:\forall x,y\in X\hskip 2.84544pt|\phi(x)-\phi(y)|\leq Ld_{X}(x,y)\right\}.

For a continuous function
\phi\in C(X)
we denote by

\displaystyle{\rm Lip}(\phi):=\sup_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle x,y\in Y}}{{x\neq y}}}\frac{|\phi(x)-\phi(y)|}{d_{X}(x,y)}

its Lipschitz constant. In a complete and separable metric space
(X,d_{X})
, we shall use the Kantorovich-Rubinstein distance
{\mathcal{W}}_{1}(\cdot,\cdot)
in the class
{\mathscr{P}}(X)
. For
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{1}(X)
, the
1
-Wasserstein distance
{\mathcal{W}}_{1}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})
is defined by

\displaystyle{\mathcal{W}}_{1}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2}):=\inf\left\{\left.\int_{X\times X}d_{X}(x_{1},x_{2})\gamma(dx_{1},dx_{2})\right|\gamma\in\Gamma(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})\right\}

where
\Gamma(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})
is the set of admissible coupling between
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
. It is worth to recall that due to Kantorovich duality, one can also consider the following definition

\displaystyle{\mathcal{W}}_{1}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2}):=\sup\left\{\int_{X}\phi{\rm d}(\mu_{1}-\mu_{2})\Big|\hskip 2.84544pt\phi\in{\rm Lip}(X,d_{X}),{\rm Lip}(\phi)\leq 1\right\}.

Notice that
{\mathcal{W}}_{1}(\mu_{1},\nu_{1})
is finite if
\mu_{1},\nu_{1}
belong to the space

(2.1)

\displaystyle{\mathscr{P}}_{1}(X):=\left\{\mu\in{\mathscr{P}}(X)\Big|\int_{X}d_{X}(x,\overline{x}){\rm d}\mu(x)<\infty\hskip 5.69046pt\text{for some $\overline{x}\in X$}\right\}.

Note that
({\mathscr{P}}_{1}(X),{\mathcal{W}}_{1})
is complete if
(X,d_{X})
is complete. Moreover, by
[
27
, Theorem 2.2.1]
the following holds true: a sequence
(\mu_{n})\subset{\mathscr{P}}_{1}(X)
converges to
\mu\in{\mathscr{P}}_{1}(X)
with respect to the Wasserstein distance
{\mathcal{W}}_{1}
if and only if,
for all
\phi\in{\rm Lip}(X,d_{X})
,

\displaystyle\int_{X}\phi{\rm d}\mu_{n}\overset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}\int_{X}\phi{\rm d}\mu,\qquad\int_{X}{\rm d}_{X}(\cdot,\bar{x}){\rm d}\mu_{n}\overset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}\int_{X}d_{X}(\cdot,\bar{x}){\rm d}\mu.

Let
T>0
. In what follows, we denote by
C([0,T],X)
the space of continuous curves
{\boldsymbol{y}}:[0,T]\rightarrow X
endowed with the metric

(2.2)

\displaystyle{\rm d}_{T,X}({\boldsymbol{y}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{y}}_{2})\coloneqq\sup_{t\in[0,T]}{\rm d}_{X}({\boldsymbol{y}}_{1}(t),{\boldsymbol{y}}_{2}(t)),\hskip 5.69046pt\text{for all ${\boldsymbol{y}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{y}}_{2}\in C([0,T],X)$.}

2.2.
Our setting

In what follows, we are interested in studying SDEs on
{\mathscr{Y}}
, where
{\mathscr{Y}}\coloneqq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathscr{P}}(U)
. The state space of our system is given by pairs
(x,\lambda)=y\in{\mathscr{Y}}
. Here

denotes the spatial component of an agent, whereas the element
\lambda\in{\mathscr{P}}(U)
denotes a probability distribution over the space
U
, which we assume to be a compact metric space. It can be interpreted as a space of strategies
[
2
,
26
]
.
Let
(U,d_{U})
be a compact metric space, and consider the Arens–Ells space
{\mathscr{F}}(U)
defined as

(2.3)

\displaystyle{\mathscr{F}}(U)\coloneqq\overline{{\rm span}({\mathscr{P}}(U))}^{{\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}_{{\rm BL}}}

as the closure in the dual space
({\rm Lip}(U,d_{U}))^{\ast}
with respect to the dual norm

\displaystyle{\left\lVert\ell\right\rVert}_{{\rm BL}}:=\sup\left\{\left\langle\ell,\phi\right\rangle:\phi\in{\rm Lip}(U,d_{U}),\left\lVert\phi\right\rVert_{{\rm Lip}}\leq 1\right\},

where

(2.4)

\displaystyle\left\lVert\phi\right\rVert_{{\rm Lip}}\coloneqq\left\lVert\phi\right\rVert_{\infty}+{\rm Lip}(\phi)

and

is the Lipschitz constant of
\phi
. Introduced in
[

]
, the space

is a separable Banach space containing

. Furthermore, for a measure

, the

-norm is equivalent to the norm induced by the dual formulation of the

(2.5)

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}\right\rVert_{{\rm BL}}\leq{\mathcal{W}}_{1}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})\leq(1+D_{U})\left\lVert\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}\right\rVert_{{\rm BL}},

where

(2.6)

\displaystyle D_{U}\coloneqq\min_{x_{0}\in U}\max_{x_{1}\in U}{\rm d}_{U}(x_{0},x_{1})\leq{\rm diam}(U),

and
{\rm diam}(U)
denotes the diameter of
U
. In the next we need to consider the space
{\mathfrak{S}}\coloneqq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathscr{F}}(U)
endowed with the norm
\left\lVert y\right\rVert_{{\mathfrak{S}}}=\left\lVert(x,\sigma)\right\rVert_{{\mathfrak{S}}}:=|x|+\left\lVert\sigma\right\rVert_{{\rm BL}}
, which is a separable Banach space. For a given

, we denote by

the closed ball of radius

in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and by

the ball of radius

in

, that is,
B_{r}^{{\mathscr{Y}}}\coloneqq\{y\in{\mathscr{Y}}:\left\lVert y\right\rVert_{{\mathscr{Y}}}\leq R\}
. We point out that since

is assumed compact, by
[
27
, Corollary 2.2.5]

{\mathscr{P}}(U)
is compact and thus
{\mathscr{Y}}
is a locally compact space. Therefore
B_{r}^{{\mathscr{Y}}}
is a compact set. Since
{\mathscr{Y}}\subset{\mathfrak{S}}
, for any
\Psi\in{\mathscr{P}}({\mathscr{Y}})
we may define

\displaystyle m_{p}(\Psi)\coloneqq\int_{{\mathscr{Y}}}\left\lVert y\right\rVert_{{\mathfrak{S}}}^{p}{\rm d}\Psi,

for
1\leq p<+\infty
. In particular, for
p=1
, one gets

\displaystyle{\mathscr{P}}_{1}({\mathscr{Y}})=\{\Psi\in{\mathscr{P}}({\mathscr{Y}}):m_{1}(\Psi)<+\infty\},

where
{\mathscr{P}}_{1}({\mathscr{Y}})
as introduced in (
2.1
).

2.3.
The stochastic integral

In this part, we suppose that
X
is a real separable Banach space,
X^{\ast}
its dual. A random variable
\xi:\Omega\rightarrow X
is called a Gaussian random variable if
\left\langle x^{\ast},\xi\right\rangle:\Omega\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
is a real-valued Gaussian random variable for all
x^{\ast}\in X^{\ast}
. Then, its distribution is uniquely determined by its mean
{\mathbb{E}}(\xi)
and its covariance operator
Q:X^{\ast}\rightarrow X
,
\left\langle Qx^{\ast},y^{\ast}\right\rangle\coloneqq{\mathbb{E}}(\left\langle\xi-{\mathbb{E}}(\xi),x^{\ast}\right\rangle\left\langle\xi-{\mathbb{E}}(\xi),y^{\ast}\right\rangle)
which is a symmetric and positive linear operator. Let us recall that by
Theorem A.1
, symmetric and positive linear operators which are covariance operators of Gaussian measures, are then referred to as Gaussian covariances.

Definition 2.1
.

A family of random elements
(W(t))_{t\in[0,T]}
,
W(t):\Omega\rightarrow X
, is called a Wiener process if

•

W(0)=0
almost surely (a.s.);

•

W(t_{i+1})-W(t_{i})(i=0,1,\ldots,n-1)
are independent random elements for every
0\leq t_{0}<t_{1}<\cdots t_{n}\leq T
;

•

for every
t\in[0,T]
,
W(t)
is a Gaussian random element with mean zero and covariance operator
tQ
, where
Q:X^{\ast}\rightarrow X
is a fixed Gaussian covariance.

In what follows, we assume that

(2.7)

\displaystyle c_{Q}\coloneqq\sup_{\left\lVert x^{\ast}\right\rVert\leq 1}\left\langle Qx^{\ast},x^{\ast}\right\rangle<\infty.

This condition is related to the well-posedness of the definition of stochastic integral in Banach spaces. In the next, our formulation of the stochastic dynamics follows the measure-valued approach introduced in
[
23
]
. Let us first recall some preliminary concepts.

Definition 2.2

(
[
23
, Definition 1]
)
.

Let
(\Omega,{\mathscr{B}},\mathbb{P})
be a probability space endowed of a complete filtration
({\mathscr{F}}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
. A function
\phi:[0,T]\times\Omega\rightarrow X^{\ast}
is called non-anticipating with respect to
({\mathscr{F}}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
if the function
(t,\Omega)\rightarrow\left\langle\phi(t,\omega),x\right\rangle
from
([0,T]\times\Omega,{\mathscr{B}}([0,T])\times{\mathscr{B}})
into
({\mathbb{R}},{\mathscr{B}}({\mathbb{R}}))
is measurable for all
x\in X
, and the function
\omega\rightarrow\left\langle\phi(t,\omega),x\right\rangle
from
(\Omega,{\mathscr{B}})
into
({\mathbb{R}},{\mathscr{B}}({\mathbb{R}}))
is
{\mathscr{F}}_{t}
-measurable for all
t\in[0,T]
and
x\in X
.

The concept of a non-anticipating function with respect to a complete filtration
({\mathscr{F}}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
can be generalized to functions of the form
\phi:[0,T]\times\Omega\rightarrow L(X,X)
, where
L(X,X)
denotes the space of linear bounded operators from
X
to
X
. In the next, we set
{\mathscr{H}}_{Q}(L(X,X))
the class of all non-anticipating functions
\phi:[0,T]\times\Omega\rightarrow L(X,X)
such that

(2.8)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\left\langle\phi(t,\omega)Q\phi^{\ast}(t,\omega)x^{\ast},x^{\ast}\right\rangle{\rm d}t{\rm d}{\mathbb{P}}<\infty\hskip 5.69046pt\text{for all $x^{\ast}\in X^{\ast}$.}

Here we use
\phi^{\ast}
to denote the dual map of
\phi
. We now recall what a stochastic integral of a non-anticipating function is.

Definition 2.3
.

Given
\phi\in{\mathscr{H}}_{Q}(L(X,X))
, and
t\in[0,T]
we consider
T_{\phi}^{t}:X^{\ast}\rightarrow L^{2}(\Omega,{\mathscr{B}},{\mathscr{P}})
,
T_{\phi}^{t}x^{\ast}\coloneqq\int_{0}^{t}\left\langle\phi^{\ast}(x^{\ast}),{\rm d}W(s)\right\rangle
. We call
T_{\phi}^{t}
a generalized stochastic integral of the operator-valued random function
\phi
with respect to
(W(s))_{s\in[0,t]}
if
T_{\phi}^{t}
defines a bounded linear operator. Furthermore, we say that
\xi:\Omega\rightarrow X
is the stochastic integral of
\phi
(if such an element exists) if
\left\langle\xi,x^{\ast}\right\rangle=T_{\phi}^{t}x^{\ast}
for all
x\in X^{\ast}
.

Notice that Definition
2.3
is meaninful when
\sup_{\left\lVert x^{\ast}\right\rVert\leq 1}\left\lVert T_{\phi}^{t}x^{\ast}\right\rVert^{2}<\infty
. In fact, we have by an abstract Itö isometry that for each
\phi\in{\mathscr{H}}_{Q}(L(X,X))
, and
t\in[0,T]

\displaystyle\sup_{\left\lVert x^{\ast}\right\rVert\leq 1}\left\lVert T_{\phi}^{t}x^{\ast}\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\left\lVert x^{\ast}\right\rVert\leq 1}\int_{\Omega}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\phi^{\ast}x^{\ast}{\rm d}W(s)\right)^{2}{\rm d}{\mathbb{P}}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\left\lVert x^{\ast}\right\rVert\leq 1}\int_{\Omega}\int_{0}^{t}\left\langle Q\phi^{\ast}x^{\ast},\phi^{\ast}x^{\ast}\right\rangle{\rm d}s{\rm d}{\mathbb{P}}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\left\lVert x^{\ast}\right\rVert\leq 1}\int_{\Omega}\int_{0}^{t}\left\langle\phi Q\phi^{\ast}x^{\ast},x^{\ast}\right\rangle{\rm d}s{\rm d}{\mathbb{P}}.

Because
\phi\in{\mathscr{H}}_{Q}(L(X,X))
we then conclude that the last quantity is finite, and we are done. As pointed out in
[
23
]
, for all
\phi\in{\mathscr{H}}_{Q}(L(X,X))
the generalized stochastic integral
T_{\phi}^{t}x^{\ast}
exists, but the stochastic integral
\xi
does not always exist. In what follows, if
\xi
exists, we write
\xi=\int_{0}^{t}\phi{\rm d}W_{t}^{Q}
.

3.
Stochastic dynamics with projected
Q
–noise

In this part, we introduce the model that we aim to analyze. We need the following space:

Definition 3.1
.

We define

(3.1)

\displaystyle T(U)\coloneqq\{h\in{\mathscr{F}}(U):\left\langle h,1\right\rangle=0\}.

In particular, notice that
T(U)
is a vectorial space, and thus with the induced norm of
{\mathscr{F}}(U)
, it has the structure of a Banach space. Furthermore, we define its projector as follows:

Definition 3.2
.

Let
\Pi_{\lambda}:{\mathscr{F}}(U)\to T(U)
be the bounded linear projection
defined by

(3.2)

\displaystyle\Pi_{\lambda}(h)\coloneqq h-\left\langle h,1\right\rangle\lambda,

In this definition, we substract the component parallel to
\lambda
to enforce zero total mass:

(3.3)

\displaystyle\left\langle\Pi_{\lambda}(h),1\right\rangle=0.

In what follows, we consider the system of SDEs

(3.4)

\begin{cases}&{\rm d}X^{i}(t)=H(X^{i}(t),\lambda^{i}(t),\mu^{N}(t))\,{\rm d}t+\sqrt{2\sigma_{x}}\,{\rm d}B^{i}(t),\\
&{\rm d}\lambda^{i}(t)={\mathcal{R}}(X^{i}(t),\lambda^{i}(t),\mu^{N}(t))\,{\rm d}t+\sqrt{2\sigma_{\lambda}}\,\Pi_{\lambda^{i}(t)}\,{\rm d}W^{Q,i}(t),\end{cases}

where
i=1,\ldots,N
,
B^{i}
is a standard Brownian motion in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
W^{Q,i}
is the generalized noise on
{\mathscr{F}}(U)
,
\Pi_{\lambda^{i}(t)}
ensures that the stochastic increment of
\lambda^{i}(t)
lies in
T_{\lambda^{i}(t)}(U)
,
\sigma_{x},\sigma_{\lambda}>0
are the diffusion coefficients, and
\mu^{N}(t)
is the empirical measure defined as

(3.5)

\displaystyle\mu^{N}(t)\coloneqq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{(X^{i}(t),\lambda^{i}(t))}.

Here, we suppose that
\{B^{i},W^{Q,i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
are independent random variables. An important fact is that the second equation is intended in the following sense:

Definition 3.3
.

Suppose that
(\Omega,{\mathscr{B}},{\mathbb{P}})
is a complete probability space endowed with the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
(B^{i}(t)\otimes W^{Q,i}(t))_{t\in[0,T],i=1\ldots,N}

6.
Future Directions

The present work opens several natural directions for further investigation. In this paper, the long-time and mean-field analysis is carried out under structural assumptions on the covariance operator
Q
ensuring well-posedness of the projected stochastic integral.
A natural and challenging extension concerns the study of the associated nonlinear continuity (or Fokker–Planck) equation satisfied by the law
\mu(t)
when the operator
Q
is arbitrary and not necessarily of finite trace. In this more general setting, the measure-valued component is driven by a cylindrical noise that may generate genuinely infinite-dimensional diffusion effects. Understanding the well-posedness of the corresponding continuity equation requires a careful analysis of the induced second-order operator in the space of probability measures, as well as suitable coercivity or dissipativity properties.
In particular, it would be of interest to establish existence, uniqueness, and stability results for the limiting equation without compactness or trace-class assumptions on
Q
. Another natural question concerns the intrinsic well-posedness of the nonlinear evolution equation satisfied by
\mu(t)
in the absence of strong structural assumptions on the drift fields. This includes identifying minimal conditions ensuring global existence, preservation of positivity, and stability in Wasserstein spaces.
A variational or gradient-flow formulation in the space of probability measures could provide a promising framework for such an analysis. A further direction consists in replacing the Gaussian cylindrical perturbation with a Lévy-type noise. In this case, the measure-valued component would evolve according to a jump-driven stochastic dynamics projected onto the tangent space of
{\mathscr{P}}(U)
. Such a setting would lead to a nonlinear nonlocal equation at the mean-field level, combining transport, diffusion, and jump terms. Establishing well-posedness, propagation of chaos, and long-time behavior in this framework represents a substantial analytical challenge and would significantly broaden the applicability of the present model.
