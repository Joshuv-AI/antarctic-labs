Finite-Time Regret Analysis of Retry-Aware Bandits
Bingkui Tong, Junpei Komiyama, Soichiro Nishimori, Paavo Parmas, [2ex] Mohamed bin Zayed University of Artificial Intelligence, [0.5ex] RIKEN AIP, [0.5ex] The University of Tokyo[2ex] bingkui.tong@mbzuai.ac.aejunpei@komiyama.info [0.5ex] nishimori@ms.k.u-tokyo.ac.jppaavo.parmas@weblab.t.u-tokyo.ac.jp

Abstract
We study a stochastic bandit algorithm motivated by retry-aware objectives that value the best outcome among multiple attempts, such as pass@ k k and max@ k k . Given a posterior over arm values, ReMax chooses a sampling distribution that maximizes the posterior expected maximum reward over M M virtual draws. Although this objective was introduced in reinforcement learning as an exploration mechanism under uncertainty, its regret properties in bandit problems have remained unclear. For Gaussian rewards and the first nontrivial case M = 2 M=2 , we characterize the optimal ReMax distribution through an expected-improvement balance condition and prove the first sublinear regret bound for ReMax. Our analysis separates the usual saturation behavior of suboptimal arms from a ReMax-specific underestimation effect, in which the optimal arm may be sampled too rarely after an unfavorable estimate. This explains why ReMax can be more exploitative than Thompson sampling (TS) and why its regret analysis is technically delicate. Experiments support this picture: ReMax often outperforms KL-UCB and Thompson sampling under mild underestimation, while posterior-variance scaling empirically mitigates severe underestimation.

Introduction

The stochastic multi-armed bandit problem is a fundamental framework for studying the trade-off between exploration and exploitation in sequential decision making. In the classical formulation, the learner chooses one arm per round and aims to maximize cumulative reward. Accordingly, most bandit algorithms select actions according to quantities that estimate, upper-bound, or sample from the value of a single pull. Canonical examples include optimism-based methods such as upper confidence bound (UCB)
(
Lai and Robbins 1985
;
Auer et al. 2002
;
Cappé et al. 2013
)
, empirical divergence methods
(
Honda and Takemura 2010
;
Maillard 2011
;
Bian and Jun 2022
)
, and Thompson sampling
(
Thompson 1933
;
Chapelle and Li 2011
;
Kaufmann et al. 2012
;
Komiyama et al. 2015
;
Agrawal and Goyal 2017
)
.

A different perspective arises when performance is evaluated by the best outcome among multiple attempts. This retry-aware view appears in language-model evaluation through pass@
k
and, more broadly, in best-of-
k
or max@
k
evaluation. Empirical studies suggest that improvements in pass@
1
do not necessarily translate into corresponding gains in pass@
k

(
Yue et al. 2025
)
, motivating objectives that directly value the best outcome over several attempts. Recent work has also begun to optimize pass@
k
or max@
k
criteria directly
(
Tang et al. 2025
;
Chen et al. 2025
;
Walder and Karkhanis 2025
)
. These objectives differ from the classical single-pull view: they value the best result across several attempts rather than the payoff of one draw. This raises a natural theoretical question: what kind of exploration behavior is induced by optimizing a best-of-
k
objective?

We study this question through the ReMax objective of
Nishimori et al. 2026
, which evaluates a policy by the expected maximum return over multiple virtual trials under reward uncertainty. In the bandit setting, ReMax leads to a simple posterior-based algorithm: given the current posterior over arm values, choose a sampling distribution that maximizes the posterior expected maximum reward over
M
virtual draws. Although prior experiments suggested that this rule can explore effectively, its regret properties in bandit problems were not understood.

This motivates a closer theoretical study of ReMax in a bandit setting. We analyze the Gaussian case with
M=2
, the first nontrivial setting. We first characterize the optimal ReMax sampling distribution through an expected-improvement balance condition: all arms assigned positive probability make the same posterior-averaged marginal contribution to the best-of-two objective, and arms outside the support make a smaller contribution. This condition explains how ReMax uses posterior uncertainty and highlights its difference from Thompson sampling. While Thompson sampling randomizes according to the posterior probability that an arm is best, ReMax also accounts for the magnitude of the possible improvement in the maximum reward. Building on this structure, we prove a first sublinear regret bound for ReMax. The analysis shows that ReMax can be more exploitative than Thompson sampling: when the optimal arm is underestimated, ReMax may return to it too slowly. This underestimation effect is the main bottleneck for ReMax, and we provide heuristic and empirical evidence that posterior-variance scaling can help.

Contributions

1.

We characterize the optimizer of the ReMax objective through a Bayesian expected-improvement balance condition, which clarifies the structure of the ReMax sampling rule.

2.

For Gaussian rewards in the first nontrivial case
M=2
, we provide the first regret analysis for ReMax, identify underestimation of the optimal arm as the main technical bottleneck, and empirically investigate
posterior-variance scaling as a mitigation strategy.

3.

We complement the theory with bandit experiments, demonstrating that ReMax achieves strong empirical performance and illustrating how retry-aware objectives induce exploratory behavior.

Preliminaries

We consider a
K
-armed stochastic bandit. We reserve
\mu_{i}
for the true mean reward of arm
i
,
\hat{\mu}_{i}(t)
for its empirical mean at round
t
, and
\theta_{i}
for a posterior sample of arm
i
. At each round
t\in\{1,\dots,T\}
, the learner chooses an arm
I(t)\in[K]:=\{1,\dots,K\}
and observes a reward
R_{t}
with mean
\mu_{I(t)}
. We assume that the means are ordered as
\mu_{1}>\mu_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\mu_{K}
, so arm
1
is uniquely optimal. Of course, the algorithm itself does not exploit this ordering. For each
i>1
, let the suboptimality gap be
\Delta_{i}:=\mu_{1}-\mu_{i}
, and define

N_{i}(t):=\sum_{s=1}^{t}\mathbf{1}\{I(s)=i\},\qquad\mathrm{Reg}(T):=\sum_{t=1}^{T}(\mu_{1}-\mu_{I(t)})=\sum_{i=2}^{K}\Delta_{i}N_{i}(T).

A good algorithm should balance exploration and exploitation to minimize the regret
\mathrm{Reg}(T)
.
It is known that
(
Lai and Robbins 1985
;
Burnetas and Katehakis 1996
)
any algorithm suffers at least

\liminf_{T\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\mathrm{Reg}(T)}{\log T}\geq\sum_{i=2}^{K}\frac{\Delta_{i}}{D(\mu_{i}||\mu_{1})},

(1)

where
D(p||q)
is the Kullback–Leibler divergence between two distributions with parameters
p,q
. Popular algorithms such as UCB and TS have a regret that matches Equation (
1
).

Throughout the paper, we let
\Pi_{t}
denote the posterior distribution over arm values after round
t
, and we reserve
\theta_{i}
for a posterior sample of arm
i
. We write
\bm{\theta}=(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{K})
for a sample drawn from the posterior.

3
The ReMax bandit algorithm

3.1
Objective and sampling rule

Let
\pi\in\Delta^{K-1}
be a distribution over the
K
arms, and let
A_{1:M}\sim\pi
denote
M
i.i.d. draws from
\pi
. The ReMax objective is

J_{\mathrm{ReMax}}^{(M)}(\pi;\Pi):=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\theta}\sim\Pi}\left[\mathbb{E}_{A_{1:M}\sim\pi}\left[\max_{m\in[M]}\theta_{A_{m}}\,\middle|\,\bm{\theta}\right]\right].

(2)

When the same arm appears multiple times in
A_{1:M}
, we reuse the same posterior sample
\theta_{i}
for that arm. Thus, repeated occurrences of an arm correspond to repeated action selection rather than re-sampling its posterior value.

At round
t
, the ReMax bandit algorithm selects a distribution maximizing the ReMax objective under the current posterior:

\pi_{t}:=\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}_{\pi\in\Delta^{K-1}}J_{\mathrm{ReMax}}^{(M)}(\pi;\Pi_{t-1}),\qquad I(t)\sim\pi_{t}.

(3)

After observing the reward
R_{t}
, the posterior is updated to obtain
\Pi_{t}
.

3.2
Expected-improvement balance

A key structural property of ReMax is that its optimizer equalizes a posterior-averaged notion of marginal improvement across arms in the support.

For a fixed posterior sample
\bm{\theta}
and a distribution
\pi
, let

W_{M-1}:=\max_{m\in[M-1]}\theta_{A_{m}},\qquad A_{1:M-1}\sim\pi.

(4)

We define the marginal expected improvement of arm
i
by

s_{t,i}(\pi,\bm{\theta}):=\mathbb{E}_{A_{1:M-1}\sim\pi}\bigl[(\theta_{i}-W_{M-1})_{+}\bigr],

(5)

and its posterior average at the optimizer
\pi_{t}
by

\bar{s}_{t,i}:=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\theta}\sim\Pi_{t-1}}\bigl[s_{t,i}(\pi_{t},\bm{\theta})\bigr].

(6)

Proposition 1

(Expected-Improvement Balance)
.

Fix a round
t
and let
\pi_{t}
satisfy Equation (
3
). Then:

1.

if
i,j\in\mathrm{supp}(\pi_{t})
, then
\bar{s}_{t,i}=\bar{s}_{t,j}
;

2.

if
j\in\mathrm{supp}(\pi_{t})
and
k\notin\mathrm{supp}(\pi_{t})
, then
\bar{s}_{t,j}\geq\bar{s}_{t,k}
,

where
\mathrm{supp}(\pi_{t})=\{i\in[K]:\pi_{t,i}>0\}
is the set of arms with non-zero weight.

The proof follows by differentiating the ReMax objective with respect to the sampling weights and applying the KKT conditions on the simplex. See Appendix
A
for the full proof.

For arms
i
and
j
, define

G_{ij}(t):=\mathbb{E}\left[(\theta_{i}-\theta_{j})_{+}\right]

where
\theta_{i}
and
\theta_{j}
are sampled from
\Pi_{t-1,i}
and
\theta_{j}\sim\Pi_{t-1,j}
independently. Then the expected improvement of arm
i
can be represented as

\bar{s}_{t,i}=\sum_{j=1}^{K}\pi_{t,j}G_{ij}(t).

(7)

Thus, arms in the support equalize weighted sums of pairwise positive-part gaps.

Discussion 1

(Difference from Conventional Expected Improvement)
.

Note that the expected-improvement quantity in Proposition
1
differs from the expected improvement algorithms studied in prior work, such as
Ryzhov 2016
, which are widely used in global optimization
(
Mockus 1989
;
Vazquez and Bect 2010
;
Brochu et al. 2010
;
Bull 2011
;
Snoek et al. 2012
)
. Proposition
1
measures how adding the
M
-th arm improves the maximum over the other
M-1
arms, whereas classical expected improvement algorithms measure how a single sample improves upon the maximum of the previous
t-1
samples. In fact, the analysis in
Ryzhov 2016
implies that expected improvement leads to sublinear regret, although its regret guarantees are not as strong as those of other popular bandit algorithms.

3.3
Reward-variance adaptation

Assume Gaussian posteriors:

\Pi_{t-1,i}=\mathcal{N}\!\left(\hat{\mu}_{i}(t),\frac{1}{N_{i}(t)}\right)

for arm
i
and

\Pi_{t-1,j}=\mathcal{N}\!\left(\hat{\mu}_{j}(t),\frac{1}{N_{j}(t)}\right)

for arm
j
.
Then
\theta_{i}-\theta_{j}
is Gaussian with mean

\delta_{ij}(t):=\hat{\mu}_{i}(t)-\hat{\mu}_{j}(t)

and variance

\sigma_{ij}^{2}(t):=\frac{1}{N_{i}(t)}+\frac{1}{N_{j}(t)}.

Writing
\gamma_{ij}(t):=\delta_{ij}(t)/\sigma_{ij}(t)
, we obtain

G_{ij}(t)=\sigma_{ij}(t)\phi\left(\gamma_{ij}(t)\right)+\delta_{ij}(t)\Phi\left(\gamma_{ij}(t)\right),

(8)

where
\phi
and
\Phi
are the standard normal density and CDF (the proof is provided in Appendix
C
).

Figure 1:
Colors show the optimal sampling probability
\pi_{2}^{\star}
as the posterior mean gap and arm 2’s posterior variance vary.

The first term is explicitly proportional to the posterior standard deviation. ReMax is therefore naturally variance-adaptive: even a mean-suboptimal arm can remain valuable if its upper tail is sufficiently informative for the best-of-two objective. As a concrete illustration, Figure
1
shows a two-arm Gaussian example with
M=2
. Holding arm 1 fixed, the optimal probability of sampling arm 2 increases with its posterior variance and decreases with the posterior mean gap
\hat{\mu}_{1}-\hat{\mu}_{2}
.

This feature distinguishes ReMax from posterior-sampling rules such as Thompson sampling. Thompson sampling randomizes according to the posterior probability that an arm is optimal, whereas ReMax also accounts for the magnitude of the potential improvement through
G_{ij}(t)
.

3.4
Intuitive optimality

In the two-arm case, Equation (
7
) yields the useful heuristic

\frac{\pi_{t,2}}{\pi_{t,1}}=\frac{G_{21}(t)}{G_{12}(t)}.

(9)

Assume that
\mu_{1}\geq\mu_{2}
and that the empirical quantities are close to the true values, namely,
\hat{\mu}_{1}(t)\approx\mu_{1}>\hat{\mu}_{2}(t)\approx\mu_{2}
.
Then
\pi_{t,1}
and
G_{12}(t)
are both
O(1)
, and arm
2
is drawn until
\pi_{t,2}
is approximately
1/t
, at which point its draws are saturated.
We can show that, under
\hat{\mu}_{2}(t)<\hat{\mu}_{1}(t)
,

G_{21}(t)\approx\mathrm{poly}(N_{2}(t))\exp\left(-N_{2}(t)D(\hat{\mu}_{2}(t)\|\hat{\mu}_{1}(t))\right)=O\left(\frac{1}{t}\right),

(10)

or, ignoring the polynomial term,

N_{2}(t)\sim\frac{\log t}{D(\hat{\mu}_{2}(t)\|\hat{\mu}_{1}(t))}\sim\frac{\log t}{D(\mu_{2}\|\mu_{1})},

which matches the asymptotically optimal regret lower bound in Equation (
1
).

In summary, the saturated

Regret analysis

In this section, we establish the regret upper bound. Following traditional bandit analysis, we derive the frequentist regret of the algorithm.

Bayesian regret can be derived by integrating the frequentist regret over the parameter distribution.

After presenting the regret bound, we provide a proof sketch for the ReMax algorithm in the first nontrivial case where
M=2
and each arm gets an initial pull.

.

4.1
Main result

Theorem 1

(Regret Bound of ReMax)
.

Let
\Delta_{\max\!}=\!\max_{i\geq 2}\Delta_{i}
and
\Delta_{\min}\!=\!\min_{i\geq 2}\Delta_{i}
. Under the assumption that the reward of each arm
i
is sampled from a Gaussian distribution with mean
\mu_{i}
and unit variance, and adopting an improper uniform prior such that the posterior is represented by
\Pi_{t,i}=\mathcal{N}(\hat{\mu}_{i}(t),\frac{1}{N_{i}(t)})
after each arm is pulled once initially, ReMax satisfies

\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{Reg}(T)]\leq\sum_{i=2}^{K}O\left(\frac{\Delta_{i}\log T}{D(\mu_{i}+\varepsilon\,\|\,\mu_{1}-\varepsilon/2)}\right)+\Delta_{\max}\tilde{O}\left(\frac{K^{1/3}T^{2/3}}{\varepsilon^{3}}\right),

(11)

for any
\varepsilon\in(0,\Delta_{\min}/5]
.
In particular, by choosing
\varepsilon=\Delta_{\min}/5
, we have

\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{Reg}(T)]\leq\sum_{i=2}^{K}O\left(\frac{\log T}{\Delta_{i}}\right)+\Delta_{\max}\tilde{O}\left(\frac{K^{1/3}T^{2/3}}{\Delta_{\min}^{3}}\right)=o(T).

(12)

The constants hidden in the Landau notation are explicit in Appendix
B
; the proof is non-asymptotic. The
\tilde{O}(\log T)
term arises from the overestimation of suboptimal arms (the “saturation” term). This term has the same order as in standard bandit analyses, but its proof is also more delicate than the corresponding Thompson sampling argument: after a suboptimal arm is saturated, the ReMax EI comparison still requires the optimal arm to be sufficiently sampled, and hence our analysis needs a lower bound on
N_{1}(t)
. However, the
O\left(T^{2/3}\right)
term (the “underestimation” term) stems from a challenge unique to ReMax; it is highly exploitative. If the optimal arm is initially underestimated, it may take a long time before the algorithm pulls the optimal arm again, leading to a protracted recovery period before its empirical mean is corrected.

4.2
Proof sketch

The central structural property of ReMax is the expected improvement (EI) condition: under the optimal sampling distribution
\pi_{t}
, all arms in the support must achieve an identical, maximal posterior-averaged marginal gain. Since regret accumulates only when suboptimal arms are pulled, and the EI condition strictly dictates which arms receive positive probability, our regret analysis reduces to understanding when EI can still justify pulling a suboptimal arm. To formalize this, we decompose the regret into an underestimation term and a saturation term, depending on whether the optimal arm is currently underestimated when a suboptimal arm is pulled.

Underestimation term

We first consider the regime in which the optimal arm is underestimated, and ask how long this situation can persist, namely how to control

\sum_{t}\mathbf{1}\!\left\{\hat{\mu}_{1}(t)<\mu_{1}-\varepsilon\right\}.

(13)

Even when the optimal arm is underestimated, it still retains a nontrivial posterior chance of improving beyond the threshold
\mu_{1}-\varepsilon
, which yields a lower bound
E\asymp\exp(-nD(\mu_{1}-\varepsilon\|\hat{\mu}_{1}(t)))
up to polynomial factors on its expected improvement. Based on this quantity, we define a set
\mathcal{S}
consisting of arms that have been pulled sufficiently many times, and a subset
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\mathcal{S}
consisting of those arms whose empirical means still exceed
\mu_{1}-\varepsilon
despite being sufficiently sampled. Using these two sets, we introduce the event
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathrm{u}}
to measure how much probability mass is assigned to sufficiently sampled arms (i.e., arms in
\mathcal{S}
), and the event
\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{u}}
to measure how much of that mass is further allocated to sufficiently sampled arms that still appear overly optimistic (i.e., arms in
\mathcal{H}
).

We first consider the case where a significant amount of probability mass is assigned to arms in
\mathcal{H}
. Since
\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{u}}\cap\mathcal{Y}_{\mathrm{u}}\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{u}}
, it suffices to bound the contribution from event
\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{u}}
. By definition, every arm in
\mathcal{H}
is already sufficiently sampled, yet still has empirical mean exceeding
\mu_{1}-\varepsilon
. For any suboptimal arm, this can only happen if its empirical mean exhibits a substantial upward deviation from its true mean. Such events are rare by concentration, and a Chernoff bound shows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t}\mathbf{1}\left\{\hat{\mu}_{1}(t)\leq\mu_{1}-\varepsilon,\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{u}}\right\}\right]=O\left(\frac{K\log K}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right).

(14)

We next consider the complementary case where either the sufficiently sampled arms or the optimistic sufficiently sampled arms do not carry enough probability mass, namely when
\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{u}}^{c}\cup\mathcal{Y}_{\mathrm{u}}^{c}
holds. If
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathrm{u}}^{c}
occurs, then the total probability mass assigned to
\mathcal{S}
is itself too small. If instead
\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{u}}^{c}
occurs while
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathrm{u}}
still holds, then most of the mass within
\mathcal{S}
must be assigned to arms that have already been sampled sufficiently often but whose empirical means are not large. For such arms, the pairwise improvements are too small to account for the lower bound
E
on the expected improvement of the optimal arm, even though the optimal arm is currently underestimated. In either case, the expected-improvement condition forces the algorithm to place a nontrivial amount of probability mass on arms outside
\mathcal{S}
. This in turn forces exploration outside
\mathcal{S}
and quickly drives those arms into
\mathcal{S}
, after which the event
N_{1}(t)=n
can no longer persist. Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t}\mathbf{1}\left\{\hat{\mu}_{1}(t)\leq\mu_{1}-\varepsilon,\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{u}}^{c}(t)\right\}\right]=\tilde{O}\left(\frac{K^{1/3}T^{2/3}}{\varepsilon^{3}}\right).

(15)

Under the natural condition
T\geq K
, which is anyway required since each arm is pulled once initially, the extra
O\left(\frac{K\log K}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)
term is lower-order in the regime of interest and we suppress it for readability.

Saturation term

We now turn to the complementary regime where the optimal arm is not underestimated.
This term is also challenging because, unlike the analysis of TS, it requires a lower bound on
N_{1}(t)
(i.e., pull count of the best arm).
Fix any suboptimal arm
j\neq 1
, and consider

\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{1}\!\left\{\hat{\mu}_{1}(t)\geq\mu_{1}-\varepsilon,\ I(t)=j\right\}.

(16)

We first exclude the rounds in which arm
j
itself is overestimated, since these can be controlled directly by concentration. In particular,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{1}\!\left\{I(t)=j,\ \hat{\mu}_{1}(t)\geq\mu_{1}-\varepsilon,\ \hat{\mu}_{j}(t)\geq\mu_{j}+\varepsilon\right\}\right]=O\!\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right).

(17)

We next analyze the remaining rounds, where arm
j
is not overestimated. For a fixed suboptimal arm
j
, define the saturation threshold
T_{j}=O\!\left(\frac{\log T}{D(\mu_{j}+\varepsilon\,\|\,\mu_{1}-\varepsilon/2)}\right)
. We further introduce the events

\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{s}}(t):=\left\{\hat{\mu}_{1}(t)\geq\mu_{1}-\varepsilon,\ \hat{\mu}_{j}(t)\leq\mu_{j}+\varepsilon\right\}
,
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathrm{s}}(t):=\left\{N_{j}(t)\geq 3T_{j}\right\}
, and

\mathcal{Z}_{\mathrm{s}}(t):=\left\{N_{1}(t)\geq 3T_{j}\right\}
.
We bound the number of pulls of arm
j
after
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathrm{s}}(t)
(i.e., saturation of arm
j
) occurs.
We further divide this regime according to whether the optimal arm is also saturated.

If both the optimal arm and arm
j
are sufficiently sampled, then under the concentration event the expected-improvement comparison strongly favors the optimal arm. As a result, the probability of drawing arm
j
is of order
T^{-1}
, and the total contribution of this regime is only
O(1)
. More precisely, once both arm
1
and arm
j
are saturated,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{1}\!\left\{I(t)=j,\ \mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{s}}(t),\ \mathcal{Y}_{\mathrm{s}}(t),\ \mathcal{Z}_{\mathrm{s}}(t)\right\}\right]=O(1).

(18)

The only remaining case is when arm
j
is already saturated while the optimal arm is not. On this event, a one-step EI comparison shows that arm
j
is no more likely to be selected than arm
1
. A direct counting argument then bounds the total contribution of this regime by
O(T_{j})
, namely

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{1}\!\left\{I(t)=j,\ \mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{s}}(t),\ \mathcal{Y}_{\mathrm{s}}(t),\ \mathcal{Z}_{\mathrm{s}}^{c}(t)\right\}\right]=O\left(\frac{\log T}{D(\mu_{j}+\varepsilon\,\|\,\mu_{1}-\varepsilon/2)}\right).

(19)

The bounds above hold for each fixed suboptimal arm
j
and the regret is the summation of this term over
j
. The full proof of Theorem
1
is provided in Appendix
B
.

4.3
Discussion on regret bound

The regret bound in Theorem
1
is likely not tight, and its looseness appears to come mainly from the underestimation term rather than the saturation term.
Nevertheless, the saturation analysis is also more delicate than in Thompson sampling: after a suboptimal arm
j
is saturated, our proof still requires a lower bound on
N_{1}(t)
so that the expected-improvement comparison between arm
1
and arm
j
becomes effective. This additional step is not needed in the standard Thompson-sampling analysis, where bounding the underestimation of the posterior suffices (e.g.,
Honda and Takemura 2014
;
Agrawal and Goyal 2017
).

That said, similarly to the standard bandit analyses of popular bandit algorithms such as Thompson Sampling, the more challenging term is the underestimation term.
The core issue in the case of underestimation of the optimal arm is to show that the algorithm returns to a normal estimation sufficiently quickly by drawing the optimal arm. For ReMax, this part of the argument is more subtle.

A useful instance in understanding the challenge in ReMax is the following three-arm example. Suppose that
\mu_{1}>\mu_{2}=\mu_{3}
,
\hat{\mu}_{2}=\hat{\mu}_{3}=\mu_{2}
, and the optimal arm is underestimated, i.e.,
\hat{\mu}_{1}<\mu_{2}
. Then the expected improvement (EI) of arm
2
(and similarly arm
3
) includes the term
\pi_{3}\,\mathbb{E}[(\theta_{2}-\theta_{3})_{+}]
, where
\mathbb{E}[(\theta_{2}-\theta_{3})_{+}]=O\!\left(\max\left\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{2}}},\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{3}}}\right\}\right)
. If
N_{2}
and
N_{3}
are both of order
t
, this term decays as
t^{-1/2}
. Balancing it with the EI of the underestimated optimal arm gives the relation
t^{-1/2}\approx\exp\!\left(-N_{1}D(\hat{\mu}_{1},\mu_{1})\right)
, and hence
t\approx\exp\!\left(2N_{1}D(\hat{\mu}_{1},\mu_{1})\right)
. This yields an extra factor of
2
in the exponent, and therefore a longer recovery time than desired.

This example also explains why ReMax can be more exploitative than Thompson sampling.
When the optimal arm is underestimated, mutual comparisons among suboptimal arms can yield larger posterior EI, so ReMax may delay returning to the optimal arm, unlike TS which samples by the posterior probability of being best.
This effect is reflected in the
\tilde{O}(T^{2/3})
term in our regret bound.
It also suggests variance inflation as a mitigation strategy: larger Gaussian posterior variance increases upper-tail probability and positive-part EI, helping the underestimated optimal arm re-enter the support.
In Appendix
F
, we give an informal discussion suggesting that suitable variance inflation could substantially reduce the underestimation contribution, with a heuristic calculation that shows the
\tilde{O}(T^{2/3})
can be reduced to
\tilde{O}(1)
; experiments further indicate that this is a promising direction.

To evaluate performance with more arms, we also consider a ten-arm

\mu=(0.1,~0.05,~0.05,~0.05,~0.02,~0.02,~0.01,~0.01,~0.01,~0.01)
,
reward noise
\sigma_{\eta}=0.05
, and
\Delta_{2}=0.05
.
This instance adds several nearly tied suboptimal arms to the lower-reward
setup, following
(
Komiyama 2016
)
.
We choose these instances because their gap structure stresses the freeze
regime studied in our underestimation analysis: the gap between the best
and second-best arms is small or comparable relative to the reward noise,
so an underestimated best arm can be difficult to distinguish from the
runner-up.

Baselines and protocol.

We compare ReMax (
M=2
) against TS and KL-UCB.
For
M=2
, we can analytically compute the ReMax optima in Equation (
3
) as in Appendix

.
All three methods use the same

the selection rule is applied.
For each instance, we set the horizon to

T=20{,}000
and average over
1{,}000

realizations with the same fixed mean vector. We report the
across-run mean of each cumulative statistic together with a one
standard-error band.
Further details are provided in Appendix

.

Metrics.

We track two quantities: (i) cumulative regret

\mathrm{Reg}(T)=\sum_{t=1}^{T}(\mu_{1}-\mu_{I(t)})
, and (ii) cumulative

\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{1}\{\hat{\mu}_{1}(t)<\mu_{2}\}

5.2
Real-world datasets

We further evaluate ReMax on bandit instances derived from real-world datasets.
Following
Komiyama et al. 2026
, we use the Open Bandit Dataset (OBD)
(
Saito et al. 2021
)
and the MovieLens 1M dataset
(
Harper and Konstan 2015
)
.
These datasets allow us to test whether the empirical advantage of ReMax extends beyond the synthetic instances in Section
5.1
.

Open Bandit Dataset.

OBD is a logged bandit dataset collected from a fashion e-commerce platform,
containing click feedback for multiple advertisements.
We treat each advertisement as an arm and use its empirical click-through
rate (CTR) as the arm’s mean reward, yielding a bandit instance with

K=80
arms.
To obtain a Gaussian bandit instance, we normalize the CTRs by the average
standard deviation of click outcomes across arms.
Following
Komiyama et al. 2026
, we model one pull as an aggregate of

10^{3}
impressions, which scales the Gaussian mean by
\sqrt{10^{3}}
.
Thus, each arm becomes a unit-variance Gaussian arm with mean proportional
to its normalized CTR.
This transformation preserves the ordering and relative gaps of the
empirical CTRs while converting the logged click data into a standard
Gaussian bandit instance.

MovieLens 1M.

We also construct a Gaussian bandit instance from the MovieLens 1M dataset.
MovieLens 1M contains user ratings for movies.
Following
Komiyama et al. 2026
, we keep movies with sufficiently many ratings and regard each remaining movie as an arm.
The mean reward of each arm is defined as the average rating of the corresponding movie, normalized by the average standard deviation of ratings across movies.
This yields a Gaussian bandit instance with
K=31
arms.
Compared with OBD, this benchmark provides a complementary real-world setting in which rewards are derived from user ratings rather than click feedback.

Experimental protocol.

We use the same baselines and the same optimization procedure for ReMax as in Section
5.1
.
For OBD, we set the horizon to
T=3{,}000
.
For MovieLens 1M, we set the horizon to
T=10{,}000
.
For each method and each dataset, we report the mean and standard error of cumulative regret over
100
independent runs.
More detailed settings are provided in Appendix
D
.

Results

Figure

ReMax outperforms the baselines on both OBD and MovieLens 1M, indicating that its empirical advantage is not limited to the synthetic instances.
These results are especially relevant in real-data-derived bandit instances, where the gaps between competitive arms can be small and accurate exploration is important.
The OBD results reflect online advertising scenarios with small CTRs and many similar advertisements, while the MovieLens results show that the same pattern appears in a distinct user-rating domain.

.

Remark on the results

The frequentist experiments support the theoretical picture: when underestimation is not severe, ReMax performs well because of its highly exploitative nature.
Its favorable results on real-world data-derived instances further suggest that this behavior may be practically beneficial.
However, as discussed in Section
4.3
, there should exist a failure mode in which underestimation is highly likely and ReMax consequently incurs larger regret, as indicated by the
T^{2/3}
bound.
In Appendix
F
, we investigate such a failure mode and show that variance inflation can mitigate it.
The main experiments cover
M=2
, which is the case analyzed in this paper.
The ReMax objective

J_{M}(\pi)

is well-defined for any
M\geq 2
, and larger
M
is expected to induce
more exploratory optimal policies. Although computing the exact optimal
policy is difficult for
M>2
, it can be approximated by gradient ascent following
(
Nishimori et al. 2026
)
;
we call this approximation
ReMaxGrad
and report its results in
Appendix
E.2
.

6
Discussion

Limitations

Our regret analysis currently covers only the
M=2
case, where the underestimation term scales as
\tilde{O}(T^{2/3})
, which is looser than the
\tilde{O}(1)
underestimation regret of popular algorithms like UCB and Thompson sampling.
This highlights the main bottleneck of our ReMax analysis: when the optimal arm is underestimated, we could not exclude a substantially longer period to recover, which we illustrate with an example in Section
4.3
. However, our experiments in Section
5
show that the regret due to underestimation grows very slowly in
T
, suggesting that the
\tilde{O}(T^{2/3})
term can be significantly reduced by refining the analysis. We also note that the underestimation term can be further empirically reduced by choosing
M>2
; see Appendix
E.2
.

Conclusion

We have studied the ReMax bandit algorithms through the lens of retry-aware exploration.
In simulations, ReMax often outperforms existing asymptotically optimal algorithms such as KL-UCB and Thompson sampling.
The main structural insight is an expected-improvement balance condition that balances the marginal improvement by the
M
-th arm. The main theoretical result is a first sublinear regret guarantee for the Gaussian
M=2
setting. More broadly, the analysis suggests that best-of-
k
objectives can induce exploration for principled reasons.
Pass@
k
and max@
k
objectives, which are widely used in the field of large language models, can be justified in view of exploration and exploitation balance
(
Lai and Robbins 1985
)
.
Despite its strong performance, the regret bound we have derived is looser than that of asymptotically optimal algorithms, such as KL-UCB
(
Cappé et al. 2013
)
, DMED
(
Honda and Takemura 2010
)
, and TS
(
Kaufmann et al. 2012
;
Agrawal and Goyal 2013
)
.
Since the ReMax algorithm exploits more aggressively than TS, its analysis is more challenging (Section
4
), and refining the regret bound is an interesting direction of future work.

Appendix A
Proof of Proposition
1

Proof of Proposition
1
.

Fix a round
t
and abbreviate

J(\pi):=J_{\mathrm{ReMax}}^{(M)}(\pi;\Pi_{t-1}).

For a fixed posterior sample
\theta
, define

v(\pi\mid\theta):=\mathbb{E}_{A_{1:M}\sim\pi}\left[\max_{m\in[M]}\theta_{A_{m}}\right].

Expanding over all realizations of
a_{1},\dots,a_{M}\in[K]
, we have

v(\pi\mid\theta)=\sum_{a_{1}=1}^{K}\cdots\sum_{a_{M}=1}^{K}\left(\prod_{m=1}^{M}\pi_{a_{m}}\right)\max\{\theta_{a_{1}},\dots,\theta_{a_{M}}\}.

We differentiate
v(\pi\mid\theta)
with respect to
\pi_{i}
. By the product rule,

\frac{\partial}{\partial\pi_{i}}\left(\prod_{m=1}^{M}\pi_{a_{m}}\right)=\sum_{k=1}^{M}\mathbf{1}\{a_{k}=i\}\prod_{m\neq k}\pi_{a_{m}}.

Substituting this into the expansion gives

\displaystyle\frac{\partial v(\pi\mid\theta)}{\partial\pi_{i}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{a_{1},\dots,a_{M}}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{M}\mathbf{1}\{a_{k}=i\}\prod_{m\neq k}\pi_{a_{m}}\right)\max\{\theta_{a_{1}},\dots,\theta_{a_{M}}\}

(20)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{M}\sum_{a_{1},\dots,a_{M}}\mathbf{1}\{a_{k}=i\}\left(\prod_{m\neq k}\pi_{a_{m}}\right)\max\{\theta_{a_{1}},\dots,\theta_{a_{M}}\}.

(21)

By exchangeability of the
M
i.i.d. draws, each term in the outer sum is identical. For any fixed position
k
, the constraint
a_{k}=i
fixes the
k
-th sample to arm
i
, while the remaining
M-1
indices are distributed according to
\pi
. Hence each term equals

\mathbb{E}_{A_{1:M-1}\sim\pi}\left[\max\{\theta_{i},W_{M-1}\}\right],

where

W_{M-1}:=\max_{m\in[M-1]}\theta_{A_{m}}.

Therefore,

\frac{\partial v(\pi\mid\theta)}{\partial\pi_{i}}=M\,\mathbb{E}_{A_{1:M-1}\sim\pi}\left[\max\{\theta_{i},W_{M-1}\}\right].

Using the identity
\max(a,b)=(a-b)_{+}+b
, we obtain

\max\{\theta_{i},W_{M-1}\}=(\theta_{i}-W_{M-1})_{+}+W_{M-1},

and hence

\displaystyle\frac{\partial v(\pi\mid\theta)}{\partial\pi_{i}}

\displaystyle=M\left(\mathbb{E}_{A_{1:M-1}\sim\pi}[(\theta_{i}-W_{M-1})_{+}]+\mathbb{E}_{A_{1:M-1}\sim\pi}[W_{M-1}]\right)

(22)

\displaystyle=M\left(s_{t,i}(\pi,\theta)+\mathbb{E}_{A_{1:M-1}\sim\pi}[W_{M-1}]\right).

(23)

Averaging over
\theta\sim\Pi_{t-1}
, we get

\displaystyle\frac{\partial J(\pi)}{\partial\pi_{i}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim\Pi_{t-1}}\left[\frac{\partial v(\pi\mid\theta)}{\partial\pi_{i}}\right]

(24)

\displaystyle=M\left(\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim\Pi_{t-1}}[s_{t,i}(\pi,\theta)]+\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim\Pi_{t-1}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{A_{1:M-1}\sim\pi}[W_{M-1}]\right]}_{=:C_{t}(\pi)}\right).

(25)

Evaluating this at the optimizer
\pi_{t}
gives

\frac{\partial J(\pi_{t})}{\partial\pi_{i}}=M\left(\bar{s}_{t,i}+C_{t}(\pi_{t})\right),

where

\bar{s}_{t,i}:=\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim\Pi_{t-1}}[s_{t,i}(\pi_{t},\theta)].

The KKT conditions on the simplex imply that

\frac{\partial J(\pi_{t})}{\partial\pi_{i}}=\frac{\partial J(\pi_{t})}{\partial\pi_{j}}\quad\text{for }i,j\in\mathrm{supp}(\pi_{t}),

and

\frac{\partial J(\pi_{t})}{\partial\pi_{j}}\geq\frac{\partial J(\pi_{t})}{\partial\pi_{k}}\quad\text{for }j\in\mathrm{supp}(\pi_{t}),\ k\notin\mathrm{supp}(\pi_{t}).

Since
C_{t}(\pi_{t})
depends only on
\pi_{t}
and
M
, but not on the arm index, this proves the proposition.
∎

Appendix B
Full proof of Theorem
1

We prove Theorem
1
by decomposing the expected regret into two parts:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{Reg}(T)]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=2}^{K}\Delta_{i}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{P}\left[\hat{\mu}_{1}(t)\leq\mu_{1}-\varepsilon,I(t)=i\right]+\mathbb{P}\left[\hat{\mu}_{1}(t)>\mu_{1}-\varepsilon,I(t)=i\right]\right]

\displaystyle\leq\Delta_{\max}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{P}\left[\hat{\mu}_{1}(t)\leq\mu_{1}-\varepsilon\right]+\sum_{i=2}^{K}\Delta_{i}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{P}\left[\hat{\mu}_{1}(t)>\mu_{1}-\varepsilon,I(t)=i\right]

The first term corresponds to the underestimation regime, while the second term corresponds to the regime in which the optimal arm is properly estimated.
We bound these two terms separately in the following subsections.

B.1
Bounding the underestimation term

We now bound the contribution from rounds in which the optimal arm is underestimated.
Let
\hat{\mu}_{i,n}
denote the empirical mean of arm
i
after
n
pulls.
For any
\hat{\mu}_{1,n}\leq\mu_{1}-\varepsilon
, define

E:=\frac{1}{4}\,\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{1}\sim\mathcal{N}(\hat{\mu}_{1,n},\,1/n)}\left[\max\left(0,\theta_{1}-(\mu_{1}-\varepsilon)\right)\right].

(26)

Intuitively,
E
will serve as a lower bound on
\bar{s}_{t,1}
. Note that
E
depends only on
n
and
\hat{\mu}_{1,n}
, and is conditionally independent of the other quantities given these.

To describe the structure of the posterior sampling distribution under underestimation, we define

\mathcal{S}(t):=\left\{i\in[K]:N_{i}(t)\geq\max\left(\frac{4}{\pi E^{2}},\,n+1\right)\right\},

(27)

\mathcal{H}(t):=\left\{i\in\mathcal{S}(t):i\neq 1,\ \hat{\mu}_{i}(t)>\mu_{1}-\varepsilon\right\},

(28)

and

\mathcal{B}(t):=\mathcal{S}(t)\setminus\mathcal{H}(t).

(29)

We also write

\pi_{t}^{\mathcal{S}}:=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}(t)}\pi_{t,i},\qquad\pi_{t}^{\mathcal{H}}:=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{H}(t)}\pi_{t,i},

(30)

and define the events

\mathcal{X}_{u}(t):=\left\{\pi_{t}^{\mathcal{H}}\geq\frac{1}{4}\right\},\qquad\mathcal{Y}_{u}(t):=\left\{\pi_{t}^{\mathcal{S}}\geq\frac{3}{4}\right\}.

(31)

Based on these events, we decompose the underestimation rounds as

\sum_{t}\mathbf{1}\{\hat{\mu}_{1}(t)\leq\mu_{1}-\varepsilon\}=\underbrace{\sum_{t}\mathbf{1}\{\hat{\mu}_{1}(t)\leq\mu_{1}-\varepsilon,\ \mathcal{X}_{u}(t)\}}_{\mathrm{(L_{u,1})}}+\underbrace{\sum_{t}\mathbf{1}\{\hat{\mu}_{1}(t)\leq\mu_{1}-\varepsilon,\ \mathcal{X}_{u}^{c}(t)\}}_{\mathrm{(L_{u,2})}}.

(32)

Here,
\mathrm{(L_{u,1})}
corresponds to the case where a nontrivial amount of probability mass is assigned to
\mathcal{H}(t)
, that is, to arms that are already sufficiently sampled but still appear overly optimistic. The term
\mathrm{(L_{u,2})}
is the complementary case where sufficiently sampled arms do not carry enough probability mass.

We begin by lower-bounding the auxiliary quantity
E
.

Lemma 1

(Lower Bound on
E
)
.

For any
\hat{\mu}_{1,n}\leq\mu_{1}-\varepsilon
, we have

E\geq\frac{1}{4\sqrt{2\pi n}}\frac{1}{3+n(\mu_{1}-\varepsilon-\hat{\mu}_{1,n})^{2}}\exp\left(-nD(\mu_{1}-\varepsilon\|\hat{\mu}_{1,n})\right).

(33)

Proof of Lemma
1
.

Let
\phi
and
\Phi
denote the PDF and CDF of
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
, and let
\delta:=\mu_{1}-\varepsilon-\hat{\mu}_{1,n}>0
. Then

\displaystyle E

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\,\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{1}\sim\mathcal{N}(\hat{\mu}_{1,n},\,1/n)}\left[\max\left(0,\theta_{1}-(\mu_{1}-\varepsilon)\right)\right]

(34)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\sqrt{n}}\left(\phi(\delta\sqrt{n})-\delta\sqrt{n}\,\Phi^{c}(\delta\sqrt{n})\right)

(35)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{4\sqrt{n}}\frac{1}{3+\delta^{2}n}\phi(\delta\sqrt{n})

(by
\Phi^{c}(x)\leq\frac{1}{x}\cdot\frac{2+x^{2}}{3+x^{2}}\cdot\phi(x)

[
Mukherjee 2016
]
)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\sqrt{2\pi n}}\frac{1}{3+\delta^{2}n}\exp\left(-nD(\mu_{1}-\varepsilon\|\hat{\mu}_{1,n})\right).

(36)

∎

Lemma
1
shows that even when the optimal arm is underestimated, it still retains a positive and quantifiable expected improvement.

We next upper-bound pairwise expected improvements between sufficiently sampled arms.

Lemma 2

(Bound on Expected Improvement with Large
n
)
.

Let
i,j\in[K]
satisfy
N_{i}(t),N_{j}(t)\geq n
and
\hat{\mu}_{i}(t)\geq\hat{\mu}_{j}(t)
. Then

G_{j,i}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi n}}.

(37)

Proof of Lemma
2
.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\hat{\mu}_{j}(t)-\hat{\mu}_{i}(t),\,\frac{1}{N_{i}(t)}+\frac{1}{N_{j}(t)}\right)}[\max\{0,X\}]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,\,\frac{1}{N_{i}(t)}+\frac{1}{N_{j}(t)}\right)}[\max\{0,X\}]

(38)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,\,\frac{2}{n}\right)}[\max\{0,X\}]

(39)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{2}{n}}\,\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)}[\max\{0,Z\}]=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi n}}.

(40)

∎

Applying Lemma
2
to arms in
\mathcal{S}(t)
gives the following corollary.

Corollary 1

(Further Bound for Sufficiently Sampled Arms)
.

Let
i,l(t)\in\mathcal{S}(t)
, where

l(t):=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{i\in\mathcal{S}(t),\,\pi_{t,i}\neq 0}\hat{\mu}_{i}(t).

(41)

Then

G_{l(t),i}\leq\frac{E}{2}.

(42)

Proof of Corollary
1
.

By the definition of
\mathcal{S}(t)
, both arms
i
and
l(t)
satisfy
N_{i}(t),N_{l(t)}(t)\geq 4/(\pi E^{2})
. Since
l(t)
has the smallest empirical mean in
\mathcal{S}(t)
, we have
\hat{\mu}_{i}(t)\geq\hat{\mu}_{l(t)}(t)
. Applying Lemma
2
with
n=4/(\pi E^{2})
yields

G_{l(t),i}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi n}}=\frac{E}{2}.

(43)

∎

We will also need a crude range-based upper bound.

Lemma 3

(Bound on Expected Improvement with Bounded Range)
.

Let
\hat{\mu}_{i},\hat{\mu}_{j}\in[\mu_{\min},\mu_{\max}]
. Then

G_{j,i}\leq\mu_{\max}-\mu_{\min}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}.

(44)

Proof of Lemma
3
.

Let
X\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. By definition and the subadditivity
\max(a+b,0)\leq\max(a,0)+\max(b,0)
,

\displaystyle G_{j,i}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\max\left(\hat{\mu}_{j}-\hat{\mu}_{i}+\sqrt{\frac{1}{N_{j}}+\frac{1}{N_{i}}}\,X,\,0\right)\right]

(45)

\displaystyle\leq\max(\hat{\mu}_{j}-\hat{\mu}_{i},0)+\sqrt{\frac{1}{N_{j}}+\frac{1}{N_{i}}}\,\mathbb{E}[\max(X,0)]

(46)

\displaystyle\leq(\mu_{\max}-\mu_{\min})+\sqrt{2}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\leq\mu_{\max}-\mu_{\min}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}.

(47)

∎

We now show that
E
indeed lower-bounds the expected improvement of arm
1
.

Lemma 4

(Lower Bound on Expected Improvement of Arm
1
)
.

Let

G_{i,j}:=\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim\Pi_{t-1}}\big[\max(\theta_{i}-\theta_{j},0)\big].

(48)

For
M=2
, the expected improvement of arm
j
can be written as

\bar{s}_{t,j}=\sum_{i\neq j}\pi_{t,i}G_{j,i}.

(49)

Moreover, under the event
\{\hat{\mu}_{1}(t)<\mu_{1}-\varepsilon\}\cap\mathcal{X}_{u}^{c}(t)\cap\mathcal{Y}_{u}(t)
, we have

\bar{s}_{t,1}\geq E.

(50)

Proof of Lemma
4
.

Since
\mathcal{X}_{u}^{c}(t)
and
\mathcal{Y}_{u}(t)
imply
\pi_{t}^{\mathcal{B}}=\pi_{t}^{\mathcal{S}}-\pi_{t}^{\mathcal{H}}\geq 3/4-1/4=1/2
, the set
\mathcal{B}(t)
carries probability mass at least
1/2
. For any arm
i\in\mathcal{B}(t)
, we have
\theta_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(\hat{\mu}_{i}(t),1/N_{i}(t))
and
\hat{\mu}_{i}(t)\leq\mu_{1}-\varepsilon
. By symmetry of the Gaussian distribution,

\mathbb{P}\big[\theta_{i}\leq\mu_{1}-\varepsilon\big]\geq\frac{1}{2}.

(51)

Therefore,

\displaystyle G_{1,i}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\max(\theta_{1}-\theta_{i},0)\right]

(52)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}\left[\max(\theta_{1}-\theta_{i},0)\mathbf{1}\{\theta_{i}\leq\mu_{1}-\varepsilon\}\right]

(53)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}\left[\max\left(\theta_{1}-(\mu_{1}-\varepsilon),0\right)\mathbf{1}\{\theta_{i}\leq\mu_{1}-\varepsilon\}\right]

(54)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\max\left(\theta_{1}-(\mu_{1}-\varepsilon),0\right)\right]\mathbb{P}\big[\theta_{i}\leq\mu_{1}-\varepsilon\big]

(55)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\,\mathbb{E}\left[\max\left(\theta_{1}-(\mu_{1}-\varepsilon),0\right)\right]=2E.

(56)

Hence,

\displaystyle\bar{s}_{t,1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\neq 1}\pi_{t,i}G_{1,i}\geq\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}(t)}\pi_{t,i}G_{1,i}\geq 2E\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}(t)}\pi_{t,i}\geq E.

(57)

∎

The previous lemma implies that, even during underestimation, arm
1
still carries a nontrivial expected improvement. We next control the empirical-mean range uniformly over time. The following lemma provides a high-probability upper bound on
L
, which will be used in the Freeze Lemma and the subsequent main-term argument.

Lemma 5

(Empirical Mean Range Bound)
.

Define the empirical-mean range

L:=\max_{i\in[K]}\hat{\mu}_{i}(t)-\min_{i\in[K]}\hat{\mu}_{i}(t),

(58)

and the good event

\mathcal{G}_{T}:=\left\{\forall i\in[K],\ \forall n\in[T],\ \left|\hat{\mu}_{i,n}-\mu_{i}\right|\leq\sqrt{\frac{2\log(2KT^{2})}{n}}\right\}.

(59)

Then

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{G}_{T})\geq 1-\frac{1}{T}.

(60)

Moreover, on the event
\mathcal{G}_{T}
, for all
t\in[T]
,

L\leq\max_{i\in[K]}\mu_{i}-\min_{i\in[K]}\mu_{i}+2\sqrt{2\log(2KT^{2})}.

(61)

Proof of Lemma
5
.

Fix any arm
i\in[K]
and any
n\in[T]
. Since the rewards are Gaussian with unit variance, the empirical mean satisfies
\hat{\mu}_{i,n}-\mu_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}\!\left(0,\frac{1}{n}\right)
. Therefore, for any
a>0
,

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\left|\hat{\mu}_{i,n}-\mu_{i}\right|>a\right)\leq 2\exp\!\left(-\frac{na^{2}}{2}\right).

(62)

Now choose
a:=\sqrt{\frac{2\log(2KT^{2})}{n}}.
Substituting this into the Gaussian tail bound gives

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\left|\hat{\mu}_{i,n}-\mu_{i}\right|>\sqrt{\frac{2\log(2KT^{2})}{n}}\right)\leq\frac{1}{KT^{2}}.

(63)

We now apply a union bound over all
K
arms and all
n\in[T]
. This yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{G}_{T}^{c})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{K}\sum_{n=1}^{T}\mathbb{P}\!\left(\left|\hat{\mu}_{i,n}-\mu_{i}\right|>\sqrt{\frac{2\log(2KT^{2})}{n}}\right)

(64)

\displaystyle\leq KT\cdot\frac{1}{KT^{2}}=\frac{1}{T}.

(65)

Hence,

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{G}_{T})\geq 1-\frac{1}{T}.

(66)

Now assume that the event
\mathcal{G}_{T}
holds. Fix any
t\in[T]
, and let

i_{t}^{+}\in\arg\max_{i\in[K]}\hat{\mu}_{i}(t),\qquad i_{t}^{-}\in\arg\min_{i\in[K]}\hat{\mu}_{i}(t).

(67)

Since
\hat{\mu}_{i}(t)=\hat{\mu}_{i,N_{i}(t)}
and
N_{i}(t)\in[T]
, the definition of
\mathcal{G}_{T}
implies

\hat{\mu}_{i_{t}^{+}}(t)\leq\mu_{i_{t}^{+}}+\sqrt{2\log(2KT^{2})},\qquad\hat{\mu}_{i_{t}^{-}}(t)\geq\mu_{i_{t}^{-}}-\sqrt{2\log(2KT^{2})}.

(68)

Therefore,

\displaystyle L

\displaystyle=\hat{\mu}_{i_{t}^{+}}(t)-\hat{\mu}_{i_{t}^{-}}(t)

(69)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\mu_{i_{t}^{+}}+\sqrt{2\log(2KT^{2})}\right)-\left(\mu_{i_{t}^{-}}-\sqrt{2\log(2KT^{2})}\right)

(70)

\displaystyle=(\mu_{i_{t}^{+}}-\mu_{i_{t}^{-}})+2\sqrt{2\log(2KT^{2})}

(71)

\displaystyle\leq\max_{i\in[K]}\mu_{i}-\min_{i\in[K]}\mu_{i}+2\sqrt{2\log(2KT^{2})}.

(72)

∎

On the event
\mathcal{G}_{T}
, we may take
L=\max_{i\in[K]}\mu_{i}-\min_{i\in[K]}\mu_{i}+2\sqrt{2\log(2KT^{2})}
according to Lemma
5
.

The next lemma uses this fact to show that if sufficiently sampled arms do not absorb enough probability mass, then the algorithm must keep assigning noticeable mass to arms outside
\mathcal{S}(t)
.

Lemma 6

(Freeze Lemma)
.

Assume that
\mathcal{X}_{u}^{c}(t)
holds at round
t
.
Then

\sum_{j\notin\mathcal{S}(t)}\pi_{t,j}\geq\min\left(\frac{1}{4},\,\frac{E}{2(L+1)}\right).

(73)

Proof of Lemma
6
.

If
\mathcal{Y}_{u}^{c}(t)
holds, then
1-\pi_{t}^{\mathcal{S}}\geq 1/4
, so Equation (
73
) follows immediately. It therefore remains to consider the case
\mathcal{X}_{u}^{c}(t)\cap\mathcal{Y}_{u}(t)
, that is,

\pi_{t}^{\mathcal{H}}\leq\frac{1}{4},\qquad\pi_{t}^{\mathcal{S}}\geq\frac{3}{4}.

(74)

Let

l(t):=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{i\in\mathcal{S}(t),\,\pi_{t,i}\neq 0}\hat{\mu}_{i}(t),

(75)

which is well-defined under Equation (
74
). Then

\displaystyle E

\displaystyle\leq\bar{s}_{t,1}

(by Lemma
4
)

\displaystyle\leq\bar{s}_{t,l(t)}

(by the expected-improvement condition, since
l(t)\in\operatorname{supp}(\pi_{t})
)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\notin\mathcal{S}(t)}\pi_{t,j}G_{l(t),j}+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}(t)}\pi_{t,i}G_{l(t),i}

(by Equation (
49
))

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j\notin\mathcal{S}(t)}\pi_{t,j}G_{l(t),j}+\frac{E}{2}.

(by Corollary
1
)

Hence,

\sum_{j\notin\mathcal{S}(t)}\pi_{t,j}G_{l(t),j}\geq\frac{E}{2}.

(76)

By Lemma
3
,

G_{l(t),j}\leq\mu_{\max}-\mu_{\min}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\leq L+1.

(77)

Therefore,

\sum_{j\notin\mathcal{S}(t)}\pi_{t,j}\geq\frac{E}{2(L+1)}.

(78)

Combining the two cases proves Equation (
73
).
∎

We next convert the Freeze Lemma into a bound on how long the process can remain in the regime
N_{1}(t)=n
while
\mathcal{X}_{u}^{c}(t)
persists.

Lemma 7

(Underestimation Main Term Bound)
.

Assume that the event
\mathcal{G}_{T}
holds. Then there exists a constant
C_{N}>0
such that

\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{P}\left(\hat{\mu}_{1,n}\leq\mu_{1}-\varepsilon,\ N_{1}(t)=n,\ \mathcal{X}_{u}^{c}(t)\,\middle|\,\hat{\mu}_{1,n}\right)\leq C_{N}K(L+1)E^{-3}.

(79)

Proof of Lemma
7
.

By definition, every arm outside
\mathcal{S}(t)
requires at most

\max\!\left(4/(\pi E^{2}),\,n+1\right)
additional draws to enter

\mathcal{S}(t)
. Whenever
N_{1}(t)=n
and
\mathcal{X}_{u}^{c}(t)
holds,
Lemma
6
guarantees that the total probability mass assigned
to arms outside
\mathcal{S}(t)
is at least
E/(2(L+1))
. Therefore, the
expected time needed for such an arm to enter
\mathcal{S}(t)
is at most

\frac{\max\left(4/(\pi E^{2}),\,n+1\right)}{E/(2(L+1))}

.

While
\hat{\mu}_{1,n}\leq\mu_{1}-\varepsilon
, let

\delta=\mu_{1}-\varepsilon-\hat{\mu}_{1,n}\geq 0
and

\theta_{1}=\hat{\mu}_{1,n}+Z/\sqrt{n}
, where
Z\sim N(0,1)
. Then

(\theta_{1}-(\mu_{1}-\varepsilon))_{+}=\left(\frac{Z}{\sqrt{n}}-\delta\right)_{+}\leq\left(\frac{Z}{\sqrt{n}}\right)_{+}.

(80)

Therefore,

\displaystyle E

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\,\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{1}\sim\mathcal{N}(\hat{\mu}_{1,n},\,1/n)}\left[(\theta_{1}-(\mu_{1}-\varepsilon))_{+}\right]

(81)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{4}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\mathbb{E}[Z_{+}]

(82)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\sqrt{2\pi n}}.

(83)

Hence,

\frac{4}{\pi E^{2}}\geq 128n\gg n+1.

(84)

Thus, the first term inside the maximum dominates
n+1
. Therefore, the
expected time needed for all such arms to enter
\mathcal{S}(t)
is at most

\frac{\max\left(4/(\pi E^{2}),\,n+1\right)}{E/(2(L+1))}\times K\leq\frac{8(L+1)K}{\pi E^{3}}.

(85)

Once all arms enter
\mathcal{S}(t)
, the event
N_{1}(t)=n
can no longer
persist, since arm
1
must eventually be drawn again and we then have

N_{1}(t)\geq n+1
. This proves the claim.
∎

We are now ready to bound
\mathrm{(L_{u,1})}
and
\mathrm{(L_{u,2})}
separately.

Proposition 2

(Non-Overestimation of Arms in
\mathcal{X}_{u}(t)
)
.

\mathbb{E}[(L_{u,1})]=\sum_{t}\mathbb{P}\left(\hat{\mu}_{1}(t)\leq\mu_{1}-\varepsilon,\ \mathcal{X}_{u}(t)\right)=O\left(\frac{K\log(K/\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right).

(86)

Proof of Proposition
2
.

Let

N_{\mathcal{H}}(t):=\sum_{s=1}^{t}\mathbf{1}\{I(s)\in\mathcal{H}(s),\ I(s)\neq 1\}

(87)

be the number of times a non-optimal arm in
\mathcal{H}(s)
has been drawn up to time
t
.
Then

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}\{\hat{\mu}_{1}(t)\leq\mu_{1}-\varepsilon,\ \mathcal{X}_{u}(t)\}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathbf{1}\{\hat{\mu}_{1}(t)\leq\mu_{1}-\varepsilon,\ \mathcal{X}_{u}(t),\ N_{\mathcal{H}}(t)=n\}

(88)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathbf{1}\{\hat{\mu}_{1}(t)\leq\mu_{1}-\varepsilon,\ \pi_{t}^{\mathcal{H}}\geq 1/4,\ N_{\mathcal{H}}(t)=n\}.

(89)

Under
\mathcal{X}_{u}(t)
, the total probability mass assigned to
\mathcal{H}(t)
is at least
1/4
,
so the expected waiting time until
N_{\mathcal{H}}(t)
increases is at most
4
.
Hence, for each fixed
n
,

\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\{\hat{\mu}_{1}(t)\leq\mu_{1}-\varepsilon,\ \pi_{t}^{\mathcal{H}}\geq 1/4,\ N_{\mathcal{H}}(t)=n\}\leq 4.

(90)

Moreover, the event

\{\hat{\mu}_{1}(t)\leq\mu_{1}-\varepsilon,\ \pi_{t}^{\mathcal{H}}\geq 1/4\}

implies that there exists at least one arm
i\neq 1
such that

\hat{\mu}_{i}(t)\geq\mu_{1}-\varepsilon
.
Let

N_{i}^{\mathrm{last}}:=\sup\{n:\hat{\mu}_{i,n}\geq\mu_{1}-\varepsilon\}.

(91)

Since
\mu_{i}\leq\mu_{1}-2\varepsilon
, we obtain

\mathbb{P}(N_{i}^{\mathrm{last}}\geq m)\leq\sum_{n=m}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}[\hat{\mu}_{i,n}\geq\mu_{1}-\varepsilon]\leq\frac{e^{-m\varepsilon^{2}/2}}{1-e^{-\varepsilon^{2}/2}}.

(92)

Therefore,

\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{i}N_{i}^{\mathrm{last}}\geq m\right)\leq\sum_{i}\mathbb{P}(N_{i}^{\mathrm{last}}\geq m)\leq K\frac{e^{-m\varepsilon^{2}/2}}{1-e^{-\varepsilon^{2}/2}}.

(93)

We now combine these bounds:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[(L_{u,1})]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\{\hat{\mu}_{1}(t)\leq\mu_{1}-\varepsilon,\ \pi_{t}^{\mathcal{H}}\geq 1/4,\ N_{\mathcal{H}}(t)=n\}

(by Equation (
89
))

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left\{\hat{\mu}_{1}(t)\leq\mu_{1}-\varepsilon,\ \pi_{t}^{\mathcal{H}}\geq 1/4,\ N_{\mathcal{H}}(t)=n,\ \max_{i}N_{i}^{\mathrm{last}}\geq\lfloor n/K\rfloor\right\}.

(94)

The last step holds because by the time
N_{\mathcal{H}}(t)=n
, at least one non-optimal arm
must have satisfied
\hat{\mu}_{i,n^{\prime}}\geq\mu_{1}-\varepsilon
for at least

\lfloor n/K\rfloor
different values of
n^{\prime}
, which implies

N_{i}^{\mathrm{last}}\geq\lfloor n/K\rfloor
.
Using Equation (
92
),

(
94
)

\displaystyle\leq 4\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\min\left(K\frac{e^{-\lfloor n/K\rfloor\varepsilon^{2}/2}}{1-e^{-\varepsilon^{2}/2}},1\right)

(95)

\displaystyle\leq 4\left(K+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\min\left(\frac{Ke^{\varepsilon^{2}/2}}{1-e^{-\varepsilon^{2}/2}}e^{-n\varepsilon^{2}/(2K)},1\right)\right),

(
\lfloor n/K\rfloor\geq n/K-1
)

Let

a:=\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2K},\qquad C_{\varepsilon}:=\frac{Ke^{\varepsilon^{2}/2}}{1-e^{-\varepsilon^{2}/2}}.

(96)

We split the summation at the threshold
b
, where the term inside the minimum drops below
1
:

b:=\max\left(0,\left\lceil\frac{\log C_{\varepsilon}}{a}\right\rceil\right).

(97)

Then

\mathbb{E}[(L_{u,1})]\leq 4\left(K+b+\sum_{n=b+1}^{\infty}C_{\varepsilon}e^{-na}\right).

(98)

By the definition of
b
, we have
C_{\varepsilon}e^{-ba}\leq 1
.
Thus,

\displaystyle\sum_{n=b+1}^{\infty}C_{\varepsilon}e^{-na}

\displaystyle=C_{\varepsilon}\frac{e^{-(b+1)a}}{1-e^{-a}}

(99)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{\varepsilon}e^{-ba}}{1-e^{-a}}

(100)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{1-e^{-a}}=O\left(\frac{1}{a}\right)=O\left(\frac{K}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right).

(101)

For the threshold
b
, noting that

1-e^{-\varepsilon^{2}/2}\geq\varepsilon^{2}/2
for small
\varepsilon
,
we have

\log C_{\varepsilon}=O\left(\log(K/\varepsilon^{2})\right).

(102)

Therefore,

b=O\left(\frac{\log C_{\varepsilon}}{a}\right)=O\left(\frac{K\log(K/\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right).

(103)

Thus,

\mathbb{E}[(L_{u,1})]=O\left(K+\frac{K\log(K/\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon^{2}}+\frac{K}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)=O\left(\frac{K\log(K/\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right).

(104)

∎

We now turn to the complementary term
\mathrm{(L_{u,2})}
, which is the main technical part of the underestimation analysis.

Proposition 3

(Underestimation Main Bound)
.

\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{(L_{u,2})}]=\sum_{t}\mathbb{P}\big[\hat{\mu}_{1}(t)\leq\mu_{1}-\varepsilon,\ \mathcal{X}_{u}^{c}(t)\big]=\tilde{O}\left(\frac{K^{1/3}T^{2/3}}{\varepsilon^{3}}\right).

(105)

Proof of Proposition
3
.

We first decompose the event according to the value of
N_{1}(t)
:

\sum_{t}\mathbb{P}\big[\hat{\mu}_{1}(t)\leq\mu_{1}-\varepsilon,\ \mathcal{X}_{u}^{c}(t)\big]=\sum_{n=1}^{T}\sum_{t}\mathbb{P}\big[\hat{\mu}_{1}(t)\leq\mu_{1}-\varepsilon,\ \mathcal{X}_{u}^{c}(t),\ N_{1}(t)=n\big].

(106)

For each fixed
n
,

\displaystyle\sum_{t}\mathbb{P}\big[\hat{\mu}_{1,n}\leq\mu_{1}-\varepsilon,\ \mathcal{X}_{u}^{c}(t),\ N_{1}(t)=n\big]

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{n}{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\mu_{1}-\varepsilon}\min\left(T,\ C_{N}K(L+1)E^{-3}\right)\exp\left(-\frac{n(\hat{\mu}_{1,n}-\mu_{1})^{2}}{2}\right)\,d\hat{\mu}_{1,n}=:(C_{n}),

(107)

where we used Lemma
7
. Summing over
n
gives

\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{(L_{u,2})}]\leq\sum_{n=1}^{T}(C_{n}).

(108)

Let
\delta:=\mu_{1}-\hat{\mu}_{1,n}>0
. Since the integration domain is
\hat{\mu}_{1,n}\leq\mu_{1}-\varepsilon
, we have
\delta\geq\varepsilon
. We split the domain into

\text{Region A: }\delta\geq\sqrt{\frac{4\log T}{n}},\qquad\text{Region B: }\varepsilon\leq\delta<\sqrt{\frac{4\log T}{n}}.

(109)

Accordingly, we write
(C_{n})=(C_{A})+(C_{B})
, and hence

\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{(L_{u,2})}]\leq\sum_{n=1}^{T}(C_{A})+\sum_{n=1}^{T}(C_{B}).

(110)

For Region A, since
\delta\geq\sqrt{4\log T/n}
, we have
n\delta^{2}/4\geq\log T
, and therefore

T\exp\left(-\frac{n\delta^{2}}{2}\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{n\delta^{2}}{4}\right).

(111)

Hence,

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{T}(C_{A})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=1}^{T}\sqrt{\frac{n}{2\pi}}\int_{\sqrt{4\log T/n}}^{\infty}\exp\left(-\frac{n\delta^{2}}{4}\right)\,d\delta

(112)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{T}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\sqrt{4\log T}}^{\infty}\exp\left(-\frac{u^{2}}{4}\right)\,du

(with
u=\sqrt{n}\delta
)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=1}^{T}\frac{2}{\sqrt{2\pi}}T^{-1}=O(1).

(113)

For Region B, using
\min(a,b)\leq a^{2/3}b^{1/3}
and Lemma
1
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{T}(C_{B})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=1}^{T}\sqrt{\frac{n}{2\pi}}\int_{\varepsilon}^{\sqrt{4\log T/n}}T^{2/3}C_{N}^{1/3}K^{1/3}(L+1)^{1/3}E^{-1}\exp\left(-\frac{n\delta^{2}}{2}\right)\,d\delta

(114)

\displaystyle\leq C(L+1)^{1/3}T^{2/3}K^{1/3}\sum_{n=1}^{T}n\int_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}\left(3+n(\delta-\varepsilon)^{2}\right)\exp\left(\frac{n(\delta-\varepsilon)^{2}}{2}\right)\exp\left(-\frac{n\delta^{2}}{2}\right)\,d\delta

(115)

\displaystyle=C(L+1)^{1/3}T^{2/3}K^{1/3}\sum_{n=1}^{T}n\int_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}\left(3+n(\delta-\varepsilon)^{2}\right)\exp\left(-\frac{n(2\delta-\varepsilon)\varepsilon}{2}\right)\,d\delta

(116)

\displaystyle\leq C(L+1)^{1/3}T^{2/3}K^{1/3}\sum_{n=1}^{T}n\exp\left(-\frac{n\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\right)\left[\int_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}3e^{-n(\delta-\varepsilon)\varepsilon}\,d\delta+\int_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}n(\delta-\varepsilon)^{2}e^{-n(\delta-\varepsilon)\varepsilon}\,d\delta\right]

(117)

\displaystyle=C(L+1)^{1/3}T^{2/3}K^{1/3}\sum_{n=1}^{T}n\exp\left(-\frac{n\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\right)\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}3e^{-ny\varepsilon}\,dy+\int_{0}^{\infty}ny^{2}e^{-ny\varepsilon}\,dy\right]

(118)

\displaystyle=C(L+1)^{1/3}T^{2/3}K^{1/3}\sum_{n=1}^{T}n\exp\left(-\frac{n\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\right)\left(\frac{3}{n\varepsilon}+\frac{2}{n^{2}\varepsilon^{3}}\right)

(119)

\displaystyle=C(L+1)^{1/3}T^{2/3}K^{1/3}\left(\frac{3}{\varepsilon}\underbrace{\sum_{n=1}^{T}e^{-n\varepsilon^{2}/2}}_{S_{1}}+\frac{2}{\varepsilon^{3}}\underbrace{\sum_{n=1}^{T}\frac{1}{n}e^{-n\varepsilon^{2}/2}}_{S_{2}}\right).

(120)

Now

S_{1}\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-x\varepsilon^{2}/2}dx=\frac{2}{\varepsilon^{2}}=O\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)

(121)

and

\displaystyle S_{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{T}\frac{1}{n}e^{-n\varepsilon^{2}/2}

(122)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n}\left(e^{-\varepsilon^{2}/2}\right)^{n}

(123)

\displaystyle=-\ln\left(1-e^{-\varepsilon^{2}/2}\right)

(
-\ln(1-x)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{x^{n}}{n}
for
x\in(0,1)
)

\displaystyle=O\left(\log\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)\right)

(since
1-e^{-x}=\Theta(x)
as
x\rightarrow 0^{+}
)

Therefore,

\sum_{n=1}^{T}(C_{B})=O\left(\frac{K^{1/3}T^{2/3}(L+1)^{1/3}\log(1/\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon^{3}}\right).

(124)

By Lemma
5
, on
\mathcal{G}_{T}
,

(L+1)^{1/3}=O\left((\log(KT)^{1/6})\right)

(125)

Combining the bounds for Regions A and B yields

\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{(L_{u,2})}]\leq\sum_{n=1}^{T}(C_{A})+\sum_{n=1}^{T}(C_{B})=O\left(\frac{K^{1/3}T^{2/3}\left(\log(KT)\right)^{1/6}\log(1/\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon^{3}}\right)=\tilde{O}\left(\frac{K^{1/3}T^{2/3}}{\varepsilon^{3}}\right).

(126)

∎

Above, we work on the event
\mathcal{G}_{T}
. On the complementary event
\mathcal{G}_{T}^{c}
, the contribution is at most

T\cdot\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{G}_{T}^{c})\leq T\cdot\frac{1}{T}=O(1),

which can be absorbed into the bound for
\mathrm{(L_{u,2})}
. Therefore, combining Propositions
2
and
3
, we obtain

\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t}\mathbf{1}\{\hat{\mu}_{1}(t)\leq\mu_{1}-\varepsilon\}\right]=\sum_{t}\mathbb{P}[\hat{\mu}_{1}(t)\leq\mu_{1}-\varepsilon]=O\left(\frac{K\log K}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)+\tilde{O}\left(\frac{K^{1/3}T^{2/3}}{\varepsilon^{3}}\right).

(127)

B.2
Bounding the saturation term

In this section, we bound the contribution from rounds in which the optimal arm is not underestimated. Fix any suboptimal arm
j\neq 1
, and consider

\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{1}[\hat{\mu}_{1}(t)\geq\mu_{1}-\varepsilon,\ I(t)=j].

(128)

We begin by isolating the rounds in which arm
j
itself is overestimated.

Lemma 8

(Arm
j
Overestimation Bound)
.

We have

\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{1}\{I(t)=j,\ \hat{\mu}_{1}(t)\geq\mu_{1}-\varepsilon,\ \hat{\mu}_{j}(t)\geq\mu_{j}+\varepsilon\}\right]=O\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right).

(129)

Proof of Lemma
8
.

By dropping the condition on arm
1
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{1}\{I(t)=j,\ \hat{\mu}_{1}(t)\geq\mu_{1}-\varepsilon,\ \hat{\mu}_{j}(t)\geq\mu_{j}+\varepsilon\}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{1}\{I(t)=j,\ \hat{\mu}_{j}(t)\geq\mu_{j}+\varepsilon\}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathbf{1}\{\hat{\mu}_{j,n}-\mu_{j}\geq\varepsilon\}.

(130)

Taking expectations and applying the Gaussian Chernoff bound
\mathbb{P}(\hat{\mu}_{j,n}-\mu_{j}\geq\varepsilon)\leq\exp(-n\varepsilon^{2}/2)
, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{1}\{I(t)=j,\ \hat{\mu}_{1}(t)\geq\mu_{1}-\varepsilon,\ \hat{\mu}_{j}(t)\geq\mu_{j}+\varepsilon\}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(\hat{\mu}_{j,n}-\mu_{j}\geq\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\exp\left(-\frac{n\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\right).

(131)

Since this is a convergent geometric series,

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\exp\left(-\frac{n\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\right)\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\left(-\frac{x\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\right)\,dx=\frac{2}{\varepsilon^{2}}.

(132)

This proves the claim.
∎

Using Lemma
8
, we can write

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{1}[I(t)=j,\ \hat{\mu}_{1}(t)\geq\mu_{1}-\varepsilon]

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{1}[I(t)=j,\ \hat{\mu}_{1}(t)\geq\mu_{1}-\varepsilon,\ \hat{\mu}_{j}(t)<\mu_{j}+\varepsilon]}_{\text{(L)}}+\underbrace{O\!\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)}_{\text{Lemma~\ref{lem_armj_overestimated}}}.

(133)

It therefore remains to bound the expected value of term (L).

Let

T_{j}:=\frac{\log T}{D(\mu_{j}+\varepsilon\parallel\mu_{1}-\varepsilon/2)}

(134)

be the saturation threshold. We also define the events

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}_{s}(t)

\displaystyle:=\{\hat{\mu}_{1}(t)\geq\mu_{1}-\varepsilon,\ \hat{\mu}_{j}(t)\leq\mu_{j}+\varepsilon\},

(135)

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}_{s}(t)

\displaystyle:=\{N_{j}(t)\geq 3T_{j}\},

(136)

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{s}(t)

\displaystyle:=\{N_{1}(t)\geq 3T_{j}\}.

(137)

We then split (L) according to whether arm
j
and arm
1
have reached the threshold:

(L)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{1}[I(t)=j,\ \mathcal{X}_{s}(t)]

\displaystyle\leq 3T_{j}+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{1}[I(t)=j,\ \mathcal{X}_{s}(t),\ \mathcal{Y}_{s}(t)]

\displaystyle=3T_{j}+\underbrace{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{1}[I(t)=j,\ \mathcal{X}_{s}(t),\ \mathcal{Y}_{s}(t),\ \mathcal{Z}_{s}(t)]}_{\mathrm{(L_{s,1})}}+\underbrace{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{1}[I(t)=j,\ \mathcal{X}_{s}(t),\ \mathcal{Y}_{s}(t),\ \mathcal{Z}_{s}^{c}(t)]}_{\mathrm{(L_{s,2})}}.

(138)

We first treat the regime
\mathrm{(L_{s,2})}
, where arm
j
is already saturated but arm
1
is not. The key point is that, on
\mathcal{X}_{s}(t)\cap\mathcal{Y}_{s}(t)\cap\mathcal{Z}_{s}^{c}(t)
, the EI comparison implies that arm
j
is no more likely to be drawn than arm
1
. This allows us to charge each expected pull of arm
j
in this regime to an expected pull of arm
1
before arm
1
reaches the saturation threshold.

Lemma 9

(Bound for
\mathrm{L}_{s,2}
)
.

For any fixed suboptimal arm
j\neq 1
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{1}\{I(t)=j,\ \mathcal{X}_{s}(t),\ \mathcal{Y}_{s}(t),\ \mathcal{Z}_{s}^{c}(t)\}\right]\leq O\left(\frac{\log T}{D(\mu_{j}+\varepsilon\parallel\mu_{1}-\varepsilon/2)}\right).

(139)

Proof of Lemma
9
.

Let

\mathcal{A}(t):=\mathcal{X}_{s}(t)\cap\mathcal{Y}_{s}(t)\cap\mathcal{Z}_{s}^{c}(t).

We assume
\varepsilon<\Delta_{j}/2
. On
\mathcal{A}(t)
, we have

N_{1}(t)<3T_{j}\leq N_{j}(t),

and hence
N_{1}(t)<N_{j}(t)
. Moreover,

\hat{\mu}_{1}(t)-\hat{\mu}_{j}(t)\geq(\mu_{1}-\varepsilon)-(\mu_{j}+\varepsilon)=\Delta_{j}-2\varepsilon>0.

Therefore, by the EI dominance comparison in Lemma
13
,

R_{t}:=\sum_{k\notin\{1,j\}}\pi_{t,k}\bigl(G_{1k}(t)-G_{jk}(t)\bigr)\geq 0.

We now show that this implies
\pi_{t,j}\leq\pi_{t,1}
. If
\pi_{t,j}=0
, the claim is immediate. Suppose
\pi_{t,j}>0
. First, we claim that
\pi_{t,1}>0
. If not, then arm
j
is active and arm
1
is inactive, so the EI KKT condition gives
\bar{s}_{t,j}\geq\bar{s}_{t,1}
. However,

\displaystyle\bar{s}_{t,1}-\bar{s}_{t,j}

\displaystyle=\pi_{t,j}G_{1j}(t)-\pi_{t,1}G_{j1}(t)+\sum_{k\notin\{1,j\}}\pi_{t,k}\bigl(G_{1k}(t)-G_{jk}(t)\bigr)

(140)

\displaystyle=\pi_{t,j}G_{1j}(t)+R_{t}>0,

(141)

which is a contradiction. Hence
\pi_{t,1}>0
.

Since both arms
1
and
j
are active, the EI KKT condition gives
\bar{s}_{t,1}=\bar{s}_{t,j}
. Thus

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\bar{s}_{t,1}-\bar{s}_{t,j}

(142)

\displaystyle=\pi_{t,j}G_{1j}(t)-\pi_{t,1}G_{j1}(t)+R_{t}

(143)

\displaystyle\geq\pi_{t,j}G_{1j}(t)-\pi_{t,1}G_{j1}(t).

(144)

Moreover,

G_{1j}(t)-G_{j1}(t)=\mathbb{E}[\theta_{1}-\theta_{j}]=\hat{\mu}_{1}(t)-\hat{\mu}_{j}(t)>0.

Therefore,

\pi_{t,j}\leq\pi_{t,1}\frac{G_{j1}(t)}{G_{1j}(t)}<\pi_{t,1}.

Since
\mathcal{A}(t)
is
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
-measurable and
I(t)
is sampled from
\pi_{t}
conditional on
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathbf{1}\{\mathcal{A}(t),I(t)=j\}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{1}\{\mathcal{A}(t)\}\pi_{t,j}

(145)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{1}\{\mathcal{A}(t)\}\pi_{t,1}

(146)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathbf{1}\{\mathcal{A}(t),I(t)=1\}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right].

(147)

Taking total expectations and summing over
t\leq T
gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{1}\{\mathcal{A}(t),I(t)=j\}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{1}\{\mathcal{A}(t),I(t)=1\}\right].

(148)

The sum on the right is deterministically at most
\lceil 3T_{j}\rceil
, because every counted term pulls arm
1
while
\mathcal{Z}_{s}^{c}(t)
holds. Once arm
1
has been pulled enough times so that
N_{1}(t)\geq 3T_{j}
, the event
\mathcal{Z}_{s}^{c}(t)
can no longer hold. Hence

\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{1}\{I(t)=j,\ \mathcal{X}_{s}(t),\ \mathcal{Y}_{s}(t),\ \mathcal{Z}_{s}^{c}(t)\}\right]\leq 3T_{j}+1.

Substituting

T_{j}=\frac{\log T}{D(\mu_{j}+\varepsilon\parallel\mu_{1}-\varepsilon/2)}

yields the claimed order.
∎

We next turn to the fully saturated regime
\mathrm{(L_{s,1})}
, where both arm
j
and arm
1
have already reached the threshold. We first record several auxiliary comparisons used to control
\pi_{t,j}
.

Lemma 10

(Lower Bound on
G_{1,j}
)
.

Assume that
\hat{\mu}_{1}(t)\geq\mu_{1}-\varepsilon
,
\hat{\mu}_{j}(t)\leq\mu_{j}+\varepsilon
, and
\varepsilon<\Delta_{j}/2
. Then

G_{1,j}\geq\frac{1}{4}\Delta_{j}.

(149)

Proof of Lemma
10
.

Let

\mu_{Z}:=\hat{\mu}_{1}-\hat{\mu}_{j},\qquad\sigma_{Z}^{2}:=\frac{1}{N_{1}}+\frac{1}{N_{j}}.

(150)

Then
Z:=\theta_{1}-\theta_{j}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{Z},\sigma_{Z}^{2})
, and

G_{1,j}=\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim\Pi_{t}}[\max(\theta_{1}-\theta_{j},0)]=\mathbb{E}_{Z}[\max(Z,0)].

(151)

Using the standard Gaussian identity,

G_{1,j}=(\hat{\mu}_{1}-\hat{\mu}_{j})\Phi\left(\frac{\hat{\mu}_{1}-\hat{\mu}_{j}}{\sqrt{\frac{1}{N_{1}}+\frac{1}{N_{j}}}}\right)+\sqrt{\frac{1}{N_{1}}+\frac{1}{N_{j}}}\,\phi\left(\frac{\hat{\mu}_{1}-\hat{\mu}_{j}}{\sqrt{\frac{1}{N_{1}}+\frac{1}{N_{j}}}}\right).

(152)

Under the assumptions,

\hat{\mu}_{1}-\hat{\mu}_{j}\geq(\mu_{1}-\varepsilon)-(\mu_{j}+\varepsilon)=\Delta_{j}-2\varepsilon>0.

(153)

Dropping the nonnegative
\phi
term and using
\Phi(\cdot)>1/2
for positive arguments, we obtain

\displaystyle G_{1,j}

\displaystyle\geq(\hat{\mu}_{1}-\hat{\mu}_{j})\Phi\left(\frac{\hat{\mu}_{1}-\hat{\mu}_{j}}{\sqrt{\frac{1}{N_{1}}+\frac{1}{N_{j}}}}\right)

(154)

\displaystyle\geq(\Delta_{j}-2\varepsilon)\cdot\frac{1}{2}\geq\frac{1}{4}\Delta_{j}.

(155)

∎

Lemma 11

(Upper Bound on
G_{j,1}
)
.

Assume that
\hat{\mu}_{1}(t)\geq\mu_{1}-\varepsilon
and
\hat{\mu}_{j}(t)\leq\mu_{j}+\varepsilon
. Then

G_{j,1}\leq C\exp\left(-\min_{x\in[\hat{\mu}_{j},\ \hat{\mu}_{1}]}\big(N_{1}(t)D(\hat{\mu}_{1}(t)\|x)+N_{j}(t)D(\hat{\mu}_{j}(t)\|x)\big)\right)

(156)

for some constant
C>0
.

Proof of Lemma
11
.

Let
Z=\theta_{j}-\theta_{1}
. Since
\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{j}
are independent Gaussians, we have
Z\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{Z},\sigma_{Z}^{2})
with

\mu_{Z}=\hat{\mu}_{j}-\hat{\mu}_{1},\qquad\sigma_{Z}^{2}=\frac{1}{N_{1}}+\frac{1}{N_{j}}.

(157)

Because
\hat{\mu}_{1}>\hat{\mu}_{j}
, we have
\mu_{Z}<0
. Therefore,

\displaystyle G_{j,1}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim\Pi_{t}}[\max(\theta_{j}-\theta_{1},0)]

(158)

\displaystyle\leq C\exp\left(-\frac{\mu_{Z}^{2}}{2\sigma_{Z}^{2}}\right)=C\exp\left(-\frac{(\hat{\mu}_{1}-\hat{\mu}_{j})^{2}}{2\left(\frac{1}{N_{1}}+\frac{1}{N_{j}}\right)}\right).

(159)

Now define

f(x):=N_{1}D(\hat{\mu}_{1}\|x)+N_{j}D(\hat{\mu}_{j}\|x)=\frac{N_{1}(\hat{\mu}_{1}-x)^{2}}{2}+\frac{N_{j}(\hat{\mu}_{j}-x)^{2}}{2}.

(160)

A direct calculation shows that
f(x)
is minimized at

x^{\star}=\frac{N_{1}\hat{\mu}_{1}+N_{j}\hat{\mu}_{j}}{N_{1}+N_{j}},

(161)

and the minimum value is

f(x^{\star})=\frac{(\hat{\mu}_{1}-\hat{\mu}_{j})^{2}}{2}\frac{N_{1}N_{j}}{N_{1}+N_{j}}=\frac{(\hat{\mu}_{1}-\hat{\mu}_{j})^{2}}{2\left(\frac{1}{N_{1}}+\frac{1}{N_{j}}\right)}.

(162)

Comparing Equations (
159
) and (
162
), we see that the exponent is exactly
-f(x^{\star})
. Since
x^{\star}
is a convex combination of
\hat{\mu}_{1}
and
\hat{\mu}_{j}
, under
\mathcal{X}_{s}(t)
it lies in
[\hat{\mu}_{j},\ \hat{\mu}_{1}]
. Hence

G_{j,1}\leq C\exp(-f(x^{\star}))=C\exp\left(-\min_{x\in[\hat{\mu}_{j},\ \hat{\mu}_{1}]}\big(N_{1}(t)D(\hat{\mu}_{1}(t)\|x)+N_{j}(t)D(\hat{\mu}_{j}(t)\|x)\big)\right).

(163)

∎

Lemma 12

(Expected Improvement Margin from Other Arms)
.

Assume that
\hat{\mu}_{1}(t)\geq\mu_{1}-\varepsilon
and
\hat{\mu}_{j}(t)\leq\mu_{j}+\varepsilon
. Then

\sum_{k\notin\{1,j\}}\pi_{t,k}(G_{j,k}-G_{1,k})\leq C\exp\left(-\min_{x\in[\hat{\mu}_{j},\ \hat{\mu}_{1}]}\big(N_{1}(t)D(\hat{\mu}_{1}(t)\|x)+N_{j}(t)D(\hat{\mu}_{j}(t)\|x)\big)\right).

(164)

Proof of Lemma
12
.

By definition,

\displaystyle G_{j,k}-G_{1,k}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim\Pi_{t}}[\max(\theta_{j}-\theta_{k},0)-\max(\theta_{1}-\theta_{k},0)]

(165)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim\Pi_{t}}[\max((\theta_{j}-\theta_{k})-(\theta_{1}-\theta_{k}),0)]

(166)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim\Pi_{t}}[\max(\theta_{j}-\theta_{1},0)]=G_{j,1}.

(167)

Since
\pi_{t,k}\geq 0
and
\sum_{k\notin\{1,j\}}\pi_{t,k}\leq 1
, we have

\sum_{k\notin\{1,j\}}\pi_{t,k}(G_{j,k}-G_{1,k})\leq G_{j,1}.

(168)

Applying Lemma
11
gives the claim.
∎

Lemma 13

(Dominance)
.

Assume that
\mathcal{X}_{s}(t)
holds and
N_{1}(t)\leq N_{j}(t)
. Then

\sum_{k\notin\{1,j\}}\pi_{t,k}(G_{1,k}-G_{j,k})\geq 0.

(169)

Proof of Lemma
13
.

For any pair of arms
i
and
c
, define

G_{i,c}=(\hat{\mu}_{i}-\hat{\mu}_{c})\Phi\left(\frac{\hat{\mu}_{i}-\hat{\mu}_{c}}{\sqrt{\frac{1}{N_{i}}+\frac{1}{N_{c}}}}\right)+\sqrt{\frac{1}{N_{i}}+\frac{1}{N_{c}}}\,\phi\left(\frac{\hat{\mu}_{i}-\hat{\mu}_{c}}{\sqrt{\frac{1}{N_{i}}+\frac{1}{N_{c}}}}\right).

(170)

Introduce the auxiliary function

g(\Delta,\sigma):=\Delta\Phi\left(\frac{\Delta}{\sigma}\right)+\sigma\phi\left(\frac{\Delta}{\sigma}\right).

(171)

Using
\phi^{\prime}(x)=-x\phi(x)
, we compute

\frac{\partial g}{\partial\Delta}=\Phi\left(\frac{\Delta}{\sigma}\right)>0,\qquad\frac{\partial g}{\partial\sigma}=\phi\left(\frac{\Delta}{\sigma}\right)>0.

(172)

Thus,
g(\Delta,\sigma)
is increasing in both arguments.

Now compare
G_{1,k}
and
G_{j,k}
for any
k\notin\{1,j\}
. Let

\Delta_{i,k}:=\hat{\mu}_{i}-\hat{\mu}_{k},\qquad\sigma_{i,k}:=\sqrt{\frac{1}{N_{i}}+\frac{1}{N_{k}}}.

(173)

Under
\mathcal{X}_{s}(t)
, we have
\hat{\mu}_{1}\geq\hat{\mu}_{j}
, so
\Delta_{1,k}\geq\Delta_{j,k}
. Also,
N_{1}(t)\leq N_{j}(t)
implies
1/N_{1}(t)\geq 1/N_{j}(t)
, hence
\sigma_{1,k}\geq\sigma_{j,k}
. By monotonicity of
g
,

G_{1,k}=g(\Delta_{1,k},\sigma_{1,k})\geq g(\Delta_{j,k},\sigma_{j,k})=G_{j,k}.

(174)

Since
\pi_{t,k}\geq 0
, summing over
k\notin\{1,j\}
gives

\sum_{k\notin\{1,j\}}\pi_{t,k}(G_{1,k}-G_{j,k})\geq 0.

(175)

∎

Lemma 14

(Upper Bound on
\pi_{t,j}
)
.

Assume that
\hat{\mu}_{1}(t)\geq\mu_{1}-\varepsilon
and
\hat{\mu}_{j}(t)\leq\mu_{j}+\varepsilon
. Then

\displaystyle\pi_{t,j}\frac{\Delta_{j}}{4}

\displaystyle\leq\pi_{t,1}C\max_{x\in[\hat{\mu}_{j},\ \hat{\mu}_{1}]}\exp\!\Big(-N_{1}(t)D(\hat{\mu}_{1}(t)\|x)-N_{j}(t)D(\hat{\mu}_{j}(t)\|x)\Big)

\displaystyle\qquad+C\max_{x\in[\hat{\mu}_{j},\ \hat{\mu}_{1}]}\exp\!\Big(-N_{1}(t)D(\hat{\mu}_{1}(t)\|x)-N_{j}(t)D(\hat{\mu}_{j}(t)\|x)\Big).

(176)

Moreover, if
N_{1}(t)\leq N_{j}(t)
, then

\pi_{t,j}\frac{\Delta_{j}}{4}\leq\pi_{t,1}C\max_{x\in[\hat{\mu}_{j},\ \hat{\mu}_{1}]}\exp\!\Big(-N_{1}(t)D(\hat{\mu}_{1}(t)\|x)-N_{j}(t)D(\hat{\mu}_{j}(t)\|x)\Big).

(177)

Proof of Lemma
14
.

When
\pi_{t,j}=0
, the result is trivial, so assume
\pi_{t,j}>0
.

The expected improvement condition gives
\bar{s}_{t,1}\leq\bar{s}_{t,j}
. Expanding this inequality, we get

\pi_{t,j}G_{1,j}+\sum_{k\notin\{1,j\}}\pi_{t,k}G_{1,k}\leq\pi_{t,1}G_{j,1}+\sum_{k\notin\{1,j\}}\pi_{t,k}G_{j,k},

(178)

or equivalently

\pi_{t,j}G_{1,j}\leq\pi_{t,1}G_{j,1}+\sum_{k\notin\{1,j\}}\pi_{t,k}(G_{j,k}-G_{1,k}).

(179)

Substituting the bounds from Lemma
12
, Lemma
11
, and Lemma
10
into Equation (
179
), we obtain

\displaystyle\pi_{t,j}\frac{\Delta_{j}}{4}

\displaystyle\leq\pi_{t,1}C_{1}\exp\left(-\min_{x\in[\hat{\mu}_{j},\ \hat{\mu}_{1}]}\big(N_{1}(t)D(\hat{\mu}_{1}(t)\|x)+N_{j}(t)D(\hat{\mu}_{j}(t)\|x)\big)\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+C_{2}\exp\left(-\min_{x\in[\hat{\mu}_{j},\ \hat{\mu}_{1}]}\big(N_{1}(t)D(\hat{\mu}_{1}(t)\|x)+N_{j}(t)D(\hat{\mu}_{j}(t)\|x)\big)\right).

(180)

If
N_{1}(t)\leq N_{j}(t)
, then Lemma
13
implies that the difference term is non-positive, and hence

\pi_{t,j}\frac{\Delta_{j}}{4}\leq\pi_{t,1}C\max_{x\in[\hat{\mu}_{j},\ \hat{\mu}_{1}]}\exp\!\big(-N_{1}(t)D(\hat{\mu}_{1}(t)\|x)-N_{j}(t)D(\hat{\mu}_{j}(t)\|x)\big).

(181)

∎

Lemma 15

(Bound for
\mathrm{L_{s,1}}
)
.

Assume that
\mathcal{X}_{s}(t)
,
\mathcal{Y}_{s}(t)
, and
\mathcal{Z}_{s}(t)
hold. Then
\pi_{t,j}\leq O(T^{-1})
. Consequently,

\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{1}\{I(t)=j,\ \mathcal{X}_{s}(t),\ \mathcal{Y}_{s}(t),\ \mathcal{Z}_{s}(t)\}\right]=O(1).

(182)

Proof of Lemma
15
.

Under
\mathcal{X}_{s}(t)=\{\hat{\mu}_{1}(t)\geq\mu_{1}-\varepsilon,\ \hat{\mu}_{j}(t)\leq\mu_{j}+\varepsilon\}
, the empirical gap is lower bounded by

\hat{\mu}_{1}(t)-\hat{\mu}_{j}(t)\geq\Delta_{j}-2\varepsilon>0.

(183)

By Lemma
14
and Lemma
11
, we obtain

\displaystyle\pi_{t,j}\frac{\Delta_{j}}{4}

\displaystyle\leq\pi_{t,1}C\exp\left(-\frac{(\hat{\mu}_{1}(t)-\hat{\mu}_{j}(t))^{2}}{2(1/N_{1}(t)+1/N_{j}(t))}\right)+C\exp\left(-\frac{(\hat{\mu}_{1}(t)-\hat{\mu}_{j}(t))^{2}}{2(1/N_{1}(t)+1/N_{j}(t))}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2C\exp\left(-\frac{(\hat{\mu}_{1}(t)-\hat{\mu}_{j}(t))^{2}}{2(1/N_{1}(t)+1/N_{j}(t))}\right),

(184)

where we used
\pi_{t,1}\leq 1
.

Since
N_{1}(t)\geq 3T_{j}
and
N_{j}(t)\geq 3T_{j}
, the effective sample size satisfies

\frac{1}{1/N_{1}(t)+1/N_{j}(t)}=\frac{N_{1}(t)N_{j}(t)}{N_{1}(t)+N_{j}(t)}\geq\frac{3T_{j}\cdot 3T_{j}}{3T_{j}+3T_{j}}=1.5\,T_{j}.

(185)

Substituting this lower bound and the empirical gap lower bound yields

\pi_{t,j}\leq\frac{8C}{\Delta_{j}}\exp\left(-1.5\,T_{j}\frac{(\Delta_{j}-2\varepsilon)^{2}}{2}\right).

(186)

Recalling that
T_{j}=\frac{\log T}{D(\mu_{j}+\varepsilon\parallel\mu_{1}-\varepsilon/2)}
, and using the Gaussian identity

D(\mu_{j}+\varepsilon\parallel\mu_{1}-\varepsilon/2)=\frac{(\Delta_{j}-1.5\varepsilon)^{2}}{2},

(187)

we obtain an exponent of the form
-c\log T
, where

c=1.5\left(\frac{\Delta_{j}-2\varepsilon}{\Delta_{j}-1.5\varepsilon}\right)^{2}.

(188)

For sufficiently small
\varepsilon
, we have
c\geq 1
, and hence

\pi_{t,j}\leq O(T^{-1}).

(189)

Summing over
t
gives

\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{1}\{I(t)=j,\ \mathcal{X}_{s}(t),\ \mathcal{Y}_{s}(t),\ \mathcal{Z}_{s}(t)\}\right]\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{1}\{\mathcal{X}_{s}(t),\ \mathcal{Y}_{s}(t),\ \mathcal{Z}_{s}(t)\}\pi_{t,j}\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}O(T^{-1})=O(1).

(190)

∎

We can now combine the previous steps to conclude the bound for the saturation term. Using Equation (
133
) together with Lemmas
9
and
15
, we obtain, for each fixed suboptimal arm
j
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{1}\{\hat{\mu}_{1}(t)\geq\mu_{1}-\varepsilon,\ I(t)=j\}\right]

\displaystyle\leq O\!\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)+3T_{j}+\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{(L_{s,1})}]+\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{(L_{s,2})}]

\displaystyle\leq O\!\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)+3T_{j}+O(1)+3T_{j}

\displaystyle=O\!\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}+\frac{\log T}{D(\mu_{j}+\varepsilon\parallel\mu_{1}-\varepsilon/2)}\right).

(191)

In particular, the logarithmic contribution is governed by the explicit scale

T_{j}=\frac{\log T}{D(\mu_{j}+\varepsilon\parallel\mu_{1}-\varepsilon/2)}.

B.3
Completion of the proof of Theorem
1

We are now ready to complete the proof of Theorem
1
. Recall that

\mathrm{Reg}(T)=\sum_{j=2}^{K}\Delta_{j}N_{j}(T)=\sum_{j=2}^{K}\Delta_{j}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{1}\{I(t)=j\}.

We decompose the regret according to whether the optimal arm is underestimated:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{Reg}(T)]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=2}^{K}\Delta_{j}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{P}\left[I(t)=j\right]

(192)

\displaystyle\leq\Delta_{\max}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{P}\left[\hat{\mu}_{1}(t)\leq\mu_{1}-\varepsilon\right]+\sum_{j=2}^{K}\Delta_{j}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{P}\left[\hat{\mu}_{1}(t)\geq\mu_{1}-\varepsilon,\ I(t)=j\right].

(193)

By the underestimation bound proved in the previous subsection,

\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{P}(\hat{\mu}_{1}(t)\leq\mu_{1}-\varepsilon)=O\!\left(\frac{K\log K}{\varepsilon^{2}}+\frac{K^{1/3}T^{2/3}}{\varepsilon^{3}}\right).

On the other hand, for each fixed suboptimal arm
j\neq 1
, the saturation analysis shows that

\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{P}\left[\hat{\mu}_{1}(t)\geq\mu_{1}-\varepsilon,\ I(t)=j\right]=O\!\left(\frac{\log T}{D(\mu_{j}+\varepsilon\|\mu_{1}-\varepsilon/2)}\right),

Combining the two bounds, and under the natural condition
T\geq K
(which is anyway required since each arm is pulled once initially), the additive term
K\log K/\varepsilon^{2}
is lower-order in the regime of interest, so we suppress it for readability. Thus,

\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{Reg}(T)]\leq\sum_{j=2}^{K}O\!\left(\frac{\Delta_{j}\log T}{D(\mu_{j}+\varepsilon\|\mu_{1}-\varepsilon/2)}\right)+\Delta_{\max}\,\tilde{O}\left(\frac{K^{1/3}T^{2/3}}{\varepsilon^{3}}\right).

It remains to specify the admissible range of
\varepsilon
. To apply Lemma
15
, we need the exponent
c
in the bound
\pi_{t,j}\leq O(T^{-c})
to satisfy
c>1
. Since

c=1.5\left(\frac{\Delta_{j}-2\varepsilon}{\Delta_{j}-1.5\varepsilon}\right)^{2},

a sufficient condition is

\varepsilon<\left(\frac{2}{5}-\frac{\sqrt{6}}{15}\right)\Delta_{j}

for every suboptimal arm
j
. Therefore, it is enough to impose the uniform condition

\varepsilon\in(0,\Delta_{\min}/5],

which guarantees
c>1
simultaneously for all
j\neq 1
. In particular, taking
\varepsilon=\Delta_{\min}/5
gives

\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{Reg}(T)]\leq\sum_{j=2}^{K}O\!\left(\frac{\log T}{\Delta_{j}}\right)+\Delta_{\max}\,\tilde{O}\!\left(\frac{K^{1/3}T^{2/3}}{\Delta_{\min}^{3}}\right),

which proves Theorem
1
.

.

If

ReMax exact optimization with primal–dual active–set method

For
M=2
and a Gaussian posterior
\theta_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(\hat{\mu}_{i},\sigma_{i}^{2})
, the ReMax objective
J_{2}(\pi)=\pi^{\top}G\pi
is a quadratic on the simplex with the pairwise expected-max matrix

G_{ij}=\mathbb{E}[\max(\theta_{i},\theta_{j})]=\hat{\mu}_{i}\,\Phi(z_{ij})+\hat{\mu}_{j}\,\Phi(-z_{ij})+s_{ij}\,\phi(z_{ij}),\qquad z_{ij}=\frac{\delta_{ij}}{s_{ij}},

(194)

where
\delta_{ij}=\hat{\mu}_{i}-\hat{\mu}_{j}
and
s_{ij}^{2}=\sigma_{i}^{2}+\sigma_{j}^{2}
. Equation (
194
) would compute
\mathbb{E}\max(\theta,\theta^{\prime})
for two independent copies on the diagonal, which is too large by
\sigma_{i}/\sqrt{\pi}
; we therefore overwrite
G_{ii}\leftarrow\hat{\mu}_{i}
to match
\mathbb{E}[\theta_{i}]
. For the Gaussian pairwise matrix above,
J_{2}
is concave on the simplex, so computing the exact ReMax policy is equivalent to solving a small convex quadratic program. Rather than calling a generic convex solver at every bandit round, we solve the KKT equations of this QP directly. Eliminating
\pi_{1}=1-\sum_{i>1}\pi_{i}
gives the linear system

A\,\pi_{2:K}=b,\qquad A_{ij}=G_{ij}+G_{11}-G_{i1}-G_{1j},\quad b_{i}=G_{11}-G_{i1},\quad i,j\in\{2,\ldots,K\},

(195)

which we solve in closed form.

The solution of Equation (
195
) is the unconstrained KKT point after enforcing only the simplex equality, and it can return
\pi_{i}<0
whenever the optimum lies on the boundary (this happens with nontrivial probability when several arms are nearly tied; see Appendix
E.2
). We handle these inequality constraints with a primal-dual active-set procedure. For a candidate active set
\mathcal{A}
, we solve the equality part of the KKT system

\begin{bmatrix}G&-\mathbf{1}\\
\mathbf{1}^{\top}&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\pi\\
\lambda\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{0}\\
1\end{bmatrix}\;\;\text{(active rows)},\qquad\pi_{i}=0\;\;\text{(inactive rows)},

then update
\mathcal{A}
using both KKT inequalities: drop active arms with
\pi_{i}<0
(primal infeasibility), and re-activate inactive arms with
(G\pi)_{i}>\lambda
(dual infeasibility). At convergence the policy satisfies stationarity on the support,
\pi_{i}\geq 0
,
\sum_{i}\pi_{i}=1
, and
(G\pi)_{i}\leq\lambda
off the support; since the problem is convex after sign reversal, these KKT conditions certify the global QP optimum.

The full procedure is summarized in Algorithm

E.1
Regret decomposition by underestimation

To make the connection between underestimation and regret more concrete, we decompose the cumulative regret into two parts according to whether the optimal arm is currently underestimated below the second-best arm:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Reg}^{\mathrm{under}}(T)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}(\mu_{1}-\mu_{I(t)})\cdot\mathbf{1}\{\hat{\mu}_{1}(t)<\mu_{2}\},

\displaystyle\mathrm{Reg}^{\neg\mathrm{under}}(T)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}(\mu_{1}-\mu_{I(t)})\cdot\mathbf{1}\{\hat{\mu}_{1}(t)\geq\mu_{2}\}.

Figures
5
and
6
plot these two components separately (dashed line: underestimation rounds; solid line: non-underestimation rounds). For ReMax and the baselines, the dashed component dominates the solid one in every panel, so almost all the cumulative regret is incurred during rounds where the optimal arm has been pushed below the second-best mean.

E.2
ReMax with larger
M
(ReMaxGrad)

The previous subsections focus on
M=2
, which is the case our theoretical analysis covers. The ReMax objective
J_{M}(\pi)=\mathbb{E}_{A_{1},\dots,A_{M}\sim\pi}[\mathbb{E}\max_{m}\theta_{A_{m}}]
is well-defined for any
M\geq 2
, and we expect larger
M
to produce more exploratory optimal policies because repeated draws of the same arm carry diminishing marginal benefit (the per-arm contribution saturates as
1-(1-\pi_{i})^{M}
).

Setup

We sweep
M\in\{2,3,4\}
on the three synthetic frequentist instances of Section
5.1
(two-arm, three-arm, and ten-arm; horizon
T=20{,}000
;
1{,}000
reward-noise replications), against the TS and KL-UCB baselines and ReMax (
M=2
). All methods share the one-pull-per-arm initialization of Algorithm
1
; for
M=2
both ReMax and ReMaxGrad are reported, so that any difference reflects only the optimization procedure (closed-form QP vs. gradient ascent) rather than the choice of
M
. For
M>2
the optimal policy has no closed form, so we approximate it by stochastic gradient ascent on the sample-average objective

\widehat{J}_{M}(\pi)=\frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S}\mathbb{E}_{A_{1:M}\sim\pi}\!\Big[\max_{m\in[M]}\theta^{(s)}_{A_{m}}\Big],\qquad\theta^{(s)}\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}\Pi_{t},

with
S=50
posterior samples per round and at most
L=20
Adam steps at learning rate
\eta=0.05
. We call this gradient-based variant
ReMaxGrad
and use it for every
M\in\{2,3,4\}
.

ReMax optimization for
M>2

For
M>2
, the optimal policy is no longer available in closed form, so we approximate it by stochastic gradient ascent on the sample-average ReMax following
[
Nishimori et al. 2026
]
. Concretely, we draw
50
posterior samples and take up to
20
Adam gradient steps with learning rate
0.05
at each round, following
[
Nishimori et al. 2026
]
. The policy parameters from round
t-1
are reused as the initialization at round
t
, but the Adam moment estimates are reinitialized to zero at every round to prevent stale momentum from carrying over. We summarize the per-round procedure in Algorithm
2
.

We parameterize the policy by softmax logits
z\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
,
\pi(z)=\mathrm{softmax}(z)
, and use the sampled-mean estimator
\widehat{J}_{M}
of the previous display. The gradient with respect to the logits is the simplex-projected push-forward of the policy gradient:

\nabla_{z}\widehat{J}_{M}(\pi(z))=\pi(z)\odot\bigl(g-\langle g,\pi(z)\rangle\bigr),\qquad g=\nabla_{\pi}\widehat{J}_{M}(\pi),

(198)

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the inner product over arms and
\odot
is elementwise. Because
\widehat{J}_{M}
depends on the discrete order statistics of
\theta^{(s)}
,
\nabla_{\pi}\widehat{J}_{M}
admits the explicit per-sample form

\bigl(\nabla_{\pi}\widehat{J}_{M}\bigr)_{(r)}^{(s)}=\sum_{r^{\prime}\geq r}M\bigl(\theta^{(s)}_{(r^{\prime})}-\theta^{(s)}_{(r^{\prime}+1)}\bigr)\bigl(1-\textstyle\sum_{j\leq r^{\prime}}\pi_{(j)}\bigr)^{M-1},

(199)

where
(\cdot)_{(r)}
indexes the
r
-th-largest element of
\theta^{(s)}
within the sample (ties broken arbitrarily) and the
r=K
terms vanish; we average (
199
) over the
S
posterior samples to obtain
g
.

Adam update and KKT-gap stopping criterion

Each inner step applies the Adam update
[
Kingma and Ba 2015
]
to the logits with the stochastic gradient (
198
), using the standard
(\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\varepsilon)=(0.9,0.999,10^{-8})
. With moment estimates
(m_{\ell},v_{\ell})
initialized to zero,

\displaystyle m_{\ell+1}

\displaystyle=\beta_{1}m_{\ell}+(1-\beta_{1})\,\nabla_{z}\widehat{J}_{M},\quad

\displaystyle\hat{m}_{\ell+1}

\displaystyle=m_{\ell+1}/(1-\beta_{1}^{\ell+1}),

(200)

\displaystyle v_{\ell+1}

\displaystyle=\beta_{2}v_{\ell}+(1-\beta_{2})\,(\nabla_{z}\widehat{J}_{M})^{\odot 2},\quad

\displaystyle\hat{v}_{\ell+1}

\displaystyle=v_{\ell+1}/(1-\beta_{2}^{\ell+1}),

\displaystyle z_{\ell+1}

\displaystyle=z_{\ell}+\eta\,\hat{m}_{\ell+1}\big/\bigl(\sqrt{\hat{v}_{\ell+1}}+\varepsilon\bigr).

(201)

Appendix F
Discussion: variance inflation

Figure 10:
We report the average and standard error of (left) cumulative regret, (center) cumulative underestimation, and (right) separated regret by the occurrence of underestimation.
In the designed failure mode, standard ReMax suffers from much larger underestimation, which is the direct cause of its worse regret compared with the baselines.
Underestimation is mitigated by variance inflation, as suggested by the theory.

The example in Section
4.3
illustrates that, when the optimal arm is underestimated, ReMax may require a long recovery period before the empirical estimate of the optimal arm is corrected. The failure case shown in Figure
10
provides an empirical illustration of this phenomenon. This observation is consistent with our regret analysis, where the underestimation term contributes a
\tilde{O}(T^{2/3})
factor to the regret bound.

A natural way to mitigate this underestimation effect is to inflate the variance of the posterior distribution used by ReMax. Specifically, instead of the standard Gaussian posterior, we consider the variance-inflated posterior

\Pi^{(c)}_{t,i}=\mathcal{N}\left(\hat{\mu}_{i}(t),\frac{c^{2}}{N_{i}(t)}\right),\qquad c\geq 1.

(202)

The original ReMax posterior analyzed in Theorem
1
is recovered as the special case
c=1
.

Intuition on variance inflation

Roughly speaking, our underestimation analysis for the optimal arm shows that, by Lemma
1
,

E\asymp\exp\left(-nD(\mu_{1}-\varepsilon\|\hat{\mu}_{1,n})\right),

up to polynomial factors. Moreover, by Lemma
7
, in the worst case ReMax may wait on the order of
E^{-3}
rounds until the next, i.e., the
(n+1)
-th, draw of the optimal arm. With variance inflation, we expect instead that

E\asymp\exp\left(-\frac{n}{c^{2}}D(\mu_{1}-\varepsilon\|\hat{\mu}_{1,n})\right),

again up to polynomial factors. Thus, at the level of exponential rates, when
c^{2}\geq 3
, the factor
E^{-3}
is at most of order

\exp\left(nD(\mu_{1}-\varepsilon\|\hat{\mu}_{1,n})\right).

By Lemma
7
, the expected waiting time until the next draw of the optimal arm is about
O(LE^{-3})
. On the other hand, the large-deviation probability of observing an empirical mean around
\hat{\mu}_{1,n}
is of order

\exp\left(-nD(\hat{\mu}_{1,n}\|\mu_{1})\right).

Therefore, with variance inflation, the fixed-
n
contribution to the underestimation term can be heuristically bounded as

\displaystyle LE^{-3}\times\exp\left(-nD(\hat{\mu}_{1,n}\|\mu_{1})\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim L\exp\left(nD(\mu_{1}-\varepsilon\|\hat{\mu}_{1,n})\right)\exp\left(-nD(\hat{\mu}_{1,n}\|\mu_{1})\right)

\displaystyle=L\exp\left(n\frac{(\mu_{1}-\varepsilon-\hat{\mu}_{1,n})^{2}}{2}\right)\exp\left(-n\frac{(\hat{\mu}_{1,n}-\mu_{1})^{2}}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=L\exp\left(n\left[\frac{(\mu_{1}-\varepsilon-\hat{\mu}_{1,n})^{2}}{2}-\frac{(\hat{\mu}_{1,n}-\mu_{1})^{2}}{2}\right]\right)

\displaystyle\leq L\exp\left(-n\frac{\varepsilon(\mu_{1}-\hat{\mu}_{1,n})}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq L\exp\left(-n\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\right),

(203)

where the last two inequalities use
\hat{\mu}_{1,n}\leq\mu_{1}-\varepsilon
.

Here, by Lemma
5
, we have

L=\max_{i,j,t}\left(\hat{\mu}_{i}(t)-\hat{\mu}_{j}(t)\right)=O(\sqrt{\log(KT)})

on the high-probability event
\mathcal{G}_{T}
. Therefore, summing over
n=1,2,\dots
gives a geometrically decaying series:

\sum_{n\geq 1}L\exp\left(-n\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\right)=O\left(\frac{L}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right).

In particular, this contribution is of order
O(\sqrt{\log T})=o(\log T)
, and hence is expected to be a non-leading term in the distribution-dependent regret bound.

Validation experiments

Inspired by the example in Section
4.3
, we constructed an intentional failure mode with a ten-armed bandit instance where underestimation is likely to occur.
Specifically, except for the best arm, whose mean is
\mu_{1}=1.5
, all other arms have the same mean
\mu_{i}=1
for
i=2,\dots,10
, and the arm standard deviation is
\sigma_{\eta}=1
.
We swept the variance inflation scale over
c^{2}=\{2,3,4\}
and compared it with standard ReMax, TS, and KL-UCB.
For the number of rounds and statistics, we used the same setting as in the main synthetic bandit experiments.
Figure
10
shows the results.
As expected, canonical ReMax suffers from larger underestimation than the baselines, which is the main contributor to its larger regret, as suggested by the separated-regret plot.
At the same time, variance inflation empirically mitigates underestimation, thereby supporting the intuition developed above.
We emphasize, however, that the discussion in this section is heuristic and does not constitute a formal regret proof.
Developing a rigorous analysis of ReMax with variance inflation is an interesting direction for future work.

. Specifically, the OBD instance uses the 80 click-through-rate values reported in their Table 5, and the MovieLens instance uses the 31 normalized movie-rating values reported in their Table 6. We set the reward variance of each arm to one, so each benchmark instance is fully specified by these published arm means.
