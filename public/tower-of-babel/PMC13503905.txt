Development of chemically modified clinoptilolite granules for enhanced ammonia removal
Authors: Armin Behfrouz, Maryam Tahmasebpoor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractZeolites are widely studied and applied in water and wastewater treatment owing to their favorable adsorption properties. Among the various types of zeolites, clinoptilolite (hereafter referred to as Clin) is one of the most abundant natural forms. However, despite its abundance, natural Clin relatively low efficiency for ammonia removal, which has motivated chemical modification to improve its performance. In this study, a chemically modified granular adsorbent with improved adsorption performance was developed through the chemical modification of Clin using sodium chloride (NaCl), magnetite (Fe3O4), sodium aluminate (NaAlO2) and sodium hydroxide (NaOH) to improve ammonia uptake. Among the tested modifications, NaAlO2‑modified Clin (NaAlO2/Clin) exhibited the highest adsorption capacity under the examined conditions. It reached up to 39.84 mg/g at pH 8, an adsorbent dosage of 4 g/L, a contact time of 120 min, and an initial ammonia concentration of 100 mg/L. To enhance practical applicability, NaAlO2/Clin was granulated using polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) as a binder, resulting in a mechanically stable granular adsorbent suitable for handling and reuse, with a maximum adsorption capacity of 24.42 mg/g. The adsorption data for both powdered and granular forms were well described by the Langmuir and Freundlich isotherm models. Furthermore, the granules retained moderate reusability over multiple regeneration cycles, which suggests their potential applicability for ammonia removal in wastewater treatment.Keywords: Water treatment, Ammonia removal, Clinoptilolite, Granules, Ionic gelation methodSubject terms: Chemistry, Engineering, Environmental sciences, Materials science

Introduction

Water is a vital resource for sustaining life on Earth, and its contamination by industrial wastewater poses significant risks to both environmental systems and human health. Large volumes of industrial effluents are discharged annually into surface water bodies, often as a result of insufficient treatment and recycling practices. Such discharges can disrupt photosynthetic activity in aquatic plants, which form the basis of aquatic food chains1.

Ammonia is a common contaminant in refinery and industrial wastewater and is recognized as a major environmental pollutant. Elevated concentrations of ammonia in aquatic environments reduce dissolved oxygen levels, leading to hypoxic conditions that can cause severe stress or mortality in aquatic organisms. In addition, ammonia promotes excessive algal growth, resulting in eutrophication and the disruption of ecosystem balance2. From a regulatory perspective, the acceptable concentration of ammonia in treated wastewater is subject to strict regulatory limits due to its toxicity. Many environmental agencies recommend ammonia–nitrogen concentrations below 10–30 mg/l for industrial effluent discharge into surface waters, while significantly lower levels (typically < 0.5 mg/l as NH3–N) are required for drinking water and sensitive aquatic ecosystems3. As a strong base, ammonia alters the pH of water and soil, increasing toxicity and negatively affecting biological equilibrium and biodiversity. Furthermore, ammonia volatilization contributes to air pollution through the formation of secondary particulate matter, while ammonia accumulation in soils can adversely impact plant growth and soil chemistry. These environmental and regulatory concerns highlight the urgent need for effective ammonia removal strategies, particularly for industrial sources such as refinery wastewater4.

Various methods are employed to reduce ammonia concentrations in industrial effluents. Biological treatment processes, which utilize microorganisms like bacteria and fungi to decompose ammonia, are widely used and have demonstrated effective removal capabilities3. In addition to biological methods, chemical processes using agents such as ferrous sulfate, copper sulfate, and aluminum chloride have also been successful in ammonia removal, primarily by converting ammonia into non-toxic, water-insoluble compounds5. Advanced treatment technologies such as nanoparticle-based applications, membrane processes, and electrocoagulation have proven effective in treating industrial wastewater6. Among these methods, adsorption has been recognized as an effective and widely applied technique for ammonia removal. Adsorbents like activated carbon, zeolite, and silica gel have demonstrated significant capacity to adsorb ammonia from water. This method offers several advantages, including high efficiency, ease of application, relatively low operational cost, and the ability to regenerate adsorbents, which allows for reuse. Moreover, due to their natural origin, biodegradability, and minimal environmental impact, adsorbents such as zeolites are especially favored for sustainable and eco-friendly industrial wastewater treatment applications7.

Granular adsorbents have gained considerable attention in water and wastewater treatment due to their enhanced mechanical stability, improved handling properties, and ease of separation from treated water compared to powdered adsorbents8. The granulation process not only increases the structural integrity of the adsorbent but also facilitates its application in continuous flow systems, which is important for large-scale and industrial operations. Moreover, granular forms reduce issues related to pressure drop and clogging in filtration units, making them more suitable for practical and scalable treatment processes9. Zeolites, as aluminosilicate materials, are particularly well-suited for ammonia adsorption due to their high specific surface area, excellent chemical and thermal stability, and significant ion-exchange capabilities. Their crystalline structure enables zeolites to adsorb various substances, including organic gases, water pollutants, and metal ions, making them ideal candidates for environmental applications10. Among natural zeolites, clinoptilolite (Clin) has attracted considerable attention for its high selectivity towards ammonium ions (NH₄⁺). The ion-exchange process between ammonium and zeolite cations, such as sodium and potassium, allows Clin to effectively remove ammonia from water. Several studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of Clin in ammonia removal, highlighting its potential for environmental applications10,11. For instance, Wasielewski et al. demonstrated that Clin could remove more than 90% of ammonium from landfill leachate, and that its adsorption capacity could be regenerated through microbial activity11. In another study, Mazloomi et al. showed that Iranian natural zeolite could remove over 90% of ammonium from aqueous solutions, although the presence of cationic ions and organic acids reduced the adsorption efficiency12. Similarly, Yang et al. prepared hybrid beads from sodium A )NaA) zeolite and chitosan, which achieved over 95% removal of ammonium under optimal conditions13.

The present study aims to enhance the ammonia adsorption capacity of natural Clin through several chemical modification methods, including NaCl, Fe3O4, NaAlO2, and NaOH. Unlike many previous studies that focus on a single modification strategy, this work provides a systematic comparative evaluation of different chemical treatments under identical experimental conditions to better understand their relative performance. The structural, morphological, and textural properties of the modified materials were characterized using Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), scanning electron microscopy (SEM), and Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) surface area analysis. To address the practical limitations of powdered adsorbents, a simple granulation method using polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) and boric acid was also developed to produce mechanically stable granular materials. The effects of key parameters, including solution pH, crosslinker concentration, and the PVA to Clin ratio, were systematically investigated, and adsorption performance was evaluated under optimized conditions. In addition, adsorption behavior was analyzed using Langmuir and Freundlich isotherm models. Overall, the novelty of this study lies in the combined evaluation of multiple chemical modification routes alongside the development of a simple, scalable granulation strategy to improve both adsorption performance and practical applicability of clinoptilolite for ammonia removal.

Materials and methods

Materials

Clin zeolite sourced from the Miyaneh mines, located in Iran was used as the adsorbent. For the modification process, sodium chloride (NaCl, ≥ 99.5%, Merck, Germany), magnetite (Fe3O4, ≥ 97%, Merck, Germany), sodium aluminate (NaAlO2, ≥ 98%, Merck, Germany), and sodium hydroxide (NaOH, analytical grade, ≥ 98% purity, Merck, Germany) were employed. NaOH and hydrochloric acid (HCl, analytical grade, 37% (w/w), Merck) were used for pH adjustment when required. Ammonium chloride (NH4Cl, ≥ 99.5%, Merck, Germany) was used to prepare the standard ammonia solution, while potassium iodide (KI, ≥ 99%, Merck) and mercuric chloride (HgCl2, ≥ 99.5%, Merck) were utilized in the preparation of Nessler’s reagent. Distilled water was used throughout the experimental procedures to ensure accuracy and prevent contamination.

For the granulation process, polyvinyl alcohol (PVA, MW ~ 89,000–98,000, ≥ 99% hydrolyzed, Merck, Germany) and boric acid (H3BO3, ≥ 99.5%, Merck, Germany) were utilized as the binder and cross-linking agent, respectively. Distilled water was consistently used in all experiments to maintain reproducibility.

Modification of clin

Clin zeolite was carefully ground and sieved through a 200 μm mesh for size classification. To remove impurities, the classified zeolite was continuously stirred in deionized water for 60 min. Afterward, the solid phase was separated through filtration and washed several times with deionized water to ensure substantial removal of impurities13. The processed sample was then dried in an oven at 100 °C for 24 h to eliminate any structural moisture, and this sample was designated as Clin. For the chemical modification, 1 M solutions of NaCl, Fe3O4, NaAlO2, and NaOH were employed. Five grams of Clin were dispersed in 100 mL of each solution and stirred under controlled conditions using a magnetic stirrer at 90 ± 10 °C for 24 h. After the modification, the solid phase was separated by filtration, thoroughly rinsed with deionized water until approximately neutral pH was reached, and then dried at 100 °C for 24 h14. The dried materials were sieved through a 200 μm mesh and labeled as NaCl/Clin, Fe3O4/Clin, NaAlO2/Clin and NaOH/Clin, respectively.

Preparation of granular adsorbent

PVA/NaAlO2/Clin nanocomposite granules were synthesized using the ionic gelation method under controlled conditions. First, a 5% (w/v) solution of PVA was prepared by dissolving the polymer in 50 ml of distilled water. The NaAlO2/Clin adsorbent powder was then added to the PVA solution at different weight ratios and stirred for 2 h at room temperature to form a homogeneous mixture. This mixture was gradually dropped into boric acid solutions at varying concentrations (1, 3, and 5% (w/v)) using a syringe to form granules. The formed granules were left in the boric acid solution for 24 h to allow complete crosslinking. Afterward, the granules were thoroughly washed with distilled water and dried at 65 °C for 24 h.

Critical factors influencing the granule properties, including the pH during synthesis, the concentration of the crosslinking agent, and the weight ratio of inorganic adsorbent to polymer. Variations in pH can alter the ionic state of the components, impacting granule structure and stability. Boric acid, as the main crosslinking agent used in this study, and its concentration play a significant role in determining the crosslink density, mechanical strength, porosity, and surface properties. Additionally, varying the NaAlO2/Clin-to-PVA ratio allows optimization of the structural stability and ensures more uniform distribution of active adsorption sites. All these parameters were systematically examined to understand their impact on the granules’ structural integrity and adsorption efficiency.

Standard ammonium chloride solution

To prepare a 1000 ppm standard solution of NH4Cl, the ammonium chloride salt was first dried in an oven at 100 °C for 1 h. Then, 3.813 g of the dried salt was accurately weighed and transferred into a 1000 mL volumetric flask. The volume was adjusted with distilled water, and the solution was stirred using a magnetic stirrer to ensure complete dissolution15.

Ammonia measurement

Ammonia concentrations (0.1–3 mg/L) were measured using a spectrophotometer and the Nessler method, following the NH3-N-4500 standard. For calibration, standard solutions of 0.2–3 mg/L were prepared from a 1000 ppm stock solution. Fifty milliliters of each sample and blank (distilled water) were treated with a mineral stabilizer (tartrate) and Nessler reagent16,17. Measurements were recorded at 425 nm.

Characterization of the adsorbents

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy was employed to identify the functional groups present on the surface of the adsorbents. The spectra were obtained using an FTIR spectrometer (Tensor 27, Bruker, Germany) within the range of 400–4000 cm⁻¹.

The surface morphology and microstructure of the samples were observed using scanning electron microscopy (MIRA3, TESCAN, Czech Republic).

The specific surface area and pore volume were measured using the BET method with a surface area analyzer (ASAP 2020, Micromeritics, USA). Prior to analysis, all samples were degassed under vacuum at 200 °C for several hours to remove physically adsorbed impurities.

Adsorption and desorption methodology

Batch adsorption experiments were carried out using 100 mL aqueous solutions containing ammonia with initial concentrations ranging from 25 to 200 mg/L. The experiments were performed under different operating conditions including pH values ranging from 2 to 12, adsorbent dosages of 1.0–6.0 g/L, and contact times between 20 and 240 min. The suspensions were agitated in a laboratory shaker at a constant speed of 180 rpm and a temperature of 25 °C. A summary of the experimental parameter ranges is presented in Table 1. After the desired contact time, the solid and liquid phases were separated by centrifugation. The residual ammonia concentration in the supernatant was determined using the Nessler reagent method. The adsorption performance was evaluated by calculating the removal efficiency (R%) and the adsorption capacity at time t (qt) according to Eqs. (1) and (2), respectively.

Results and discussion

Powder-based adsorbents

Characterization

FTIR

FTIR spectroscopy was used to identify functional groups and evaluate the chemical modification of natural Clin. As shown in Fig‌ 0.1, several spectral changes suggested successful modification. Peaks in the 450–550 cm− 1 region corresponded to the vibrational modes of Na–O bonds, indicating the presence of alkali metal ions on the zeolite surface which may enhance the ion-exchange capability of the zeolite and facilitate the adsorption of ammonium ions. Smaller peaks between 600 and 1750 cm− 1 were attributed to the bending vibrations of Si–O and Al–O bonds20. Changes in this region may indicate alterations in the zeolite framework that could influence the interaction between the adsorbent surface and ammonium ions. The sharp peak at 1054 cm− 1 was linked to the asymmetric stretching vibrations of Si–O–Si and Si–O–Al, suggesting the structural integrity of the zeolite framework which is important for maintaining effective ion-exchange capacity during ammonium adsorption. Changes in peak intensity between 1630 and 1650 cm− 1 were associated with the bending vibrations of adsorbed water molecules which may facilitate the mobility and diffusion of ammonium ions toward the active adsorption sites. Moreover, the pronounced increase in intensity between 3400 and 3600 cm− 1, corresponding to the stretching vibrations of hydroxyl (–OH) groups, indicates possible introduction of hydroxyl groups onto the zeolite surface following alkaline modification21. The presence of additional hydroxyl groups may increase the number of active adsorption sites and enhance the interaction between the adsorbent and ammonium ions. Overall, these spectral changes suggest that chemical modification of Clin may alter its structural and surface properties, which could contribute to improved adsorption performance toward ammonium. As observed, specific modifications led to distinct changes. NaCl treatment caused minor shifts in the Si–O vibration region, suggesting ion exchange with sodium ions and potentially increasing the availability of exchangeable cations for ammonium adsorption. Fe3O4 modification introduced new peaks around 580 cm− 1, corresponding to Fe–O stretching vibrations, which indicate the incorporation of magnetic nanoparticles into the zeolite framework which may improve surface heterogeneity and provide additional active sites for ammonium adsorption22. NaAlO2 modification resulted in an increase in the intensity of the 1054 cm⁻¹ peak, reflecting enhanced Si–O–Al interactions due to the incorporation of alumina which may strengthen the zeolite framework and improve its ion-exchange properties for ammonium removal. NaOH modification resulted in a significant increase in peaks related to hydroxyl groups in the 3400–3600 cm− 1 region23. This suggests the addition of hydroxyl groups to the zeolite surface, which may improve its porosity and reactivity and increase the number of adsorption sites available for ammonium ions. Among the modification methods, NaAlO2 modification showed relatively higher performance under the studied conditions, as evidenced by the increase in the 1054 cm− 1 peak, indicating a stronger zeolite framework. Furthermore, the notable rise in hydroxyl group intensity in the 3400–3600 cm− 1 range suggested the creation of additional active sites on the zeolite surface14, which improved its adsorption capacity. Therefore, NaAlO2 modification can be considered a promising approach for modifying Clin in this study.

To investigate the surface characteristics and porosity of the samples, BET analysis was performed, and the results are presented in Table 3. Overall, the BET results suggest that NaAlO2 modification may enhance the surface area and porosity of Clin, while granulation reduces these properties to some extent. However, the combined chemical modification and granulation strategy still provides a balanced material with acceptable adsorption performance under the studied conditions.

Ammonia adsorption results

The performance of five different adsorbents Clin, NaCl/Clin, Fe3O4/Clin, NaAlO2/Clin and NaOH/Clin was systematically evaluated for ammonia removal from aqueous solutions. All modified samples showed an apparent improvement in ammonia adsorption efficiency compared to raw Clin, confirming that each modification strategy may have enhanced the material’s affinity for ammonium ions. This improvement could be partially attributed to the increased surface roughness and porosity observed in SEM images, which revealed that the modified adsorbents particularly those treated with NaOH and NaAlO2, appeared to have more open and porous structures than the unmodified Clin.

As shown in Fig. 3, raw Clin achieved a removal efficiency of approximately 60.3%, while NaCl/Clin, Fe3O4/Clin, d NaOH/Clin reached values of about 71.8, 70.3, and 82.9% respectively. Notably, NaAlO2/Clin demonstrated the highest observed efficiency at around 84.7%, representing a 24.4% increase compared to the raw Clin. These results indicate that all chemical modifications can improve the adsorptive performance, with NaAlO2, showing the most pronounced enhancement under these conditions. The improvements in performance appear to correlate with structural modifications induced by each treatment. For instance, the introduction of NaAlO2 may increase the aluminum content in the zeolitic framework, which could potentially strengthen the negative surface charge and thereby promote ion-exchange interactions with NH4+. The SEM images suggest that NaAlO2/Clin has an expanded pore network and increased porosity, offering additional active sites for adsorption. This is further supported by the observed increase in pore volume, which could enhance mass transfer and facilitate higher ammonia uptake.

Overall, the comparative analysis suggests that NaAlO2/Clin performed better than the other tested materials under the studied conditions, namely 100 mg/L initial NH₄⁺ concentration, 4 g/L adsorbent dosage. Based on its superior adsorption efficiency and improved morphological characteristics, NaAlO2/Clin was considered as the optimal adsorbent for subsequent investigations, especially considering its high potential for real-world applications in large-scale ammonia removal.

Granule-based adsorbents

Granulation parameters

pH effect

The synthesis pH appears to critically influences the structural integrity, morphology, and performance of PVA/NaAlO2/Clin nanocomposite granules. During ionic gelation, pH variations may alter the ionic state of the polymer matrix and inorganic particles, affecting crosslinking density, surface charge, and PVA–NaAlO2/Clin interactions. SEM images presented in Fig 4 suggest that granules formed at low pH tend to exhibit weak and heterogeneous structures, which may be attributed to excessive protonation and reduced electrostatic interactions, potentially leading to lower density and mechanical strength. In contrast, granules synthesized at higher pH show increased porosity; however, they may also present surface irregularities and reduced mechanical stability, which could be associated with stronger repulsive interactions and less uniform particle distribution. Moreover, pH likely influences gelation kinetics and structural development within the boric acid solution. An intermediate pH around 8 appears to provide a balance between ionic interactions and crosslinking, resulting in relatively more uniform granule formation and improved structural stability. Overall, granules prepared at pH 8 show comparatively better morphological uniformity based on SEM observations. These results highlight the importance of controlling pH during granulation to achieve a balance between structural integrity and porosity, thereby supporting improved adsorption performance.

Morphology of PVA/NaAlO2/Clin granules formed in 5% (w/v) boric acid solution at different pH values.

Crosslinking effect

Boric acid at concentrations of 1, 3, and 5% (w/v) was tested as a crosslinking agent in the granulation of PVA/NaAlO2/Clin nanocomposites to evaluate its effect on granule morphology, porosity, mechanical strength, and adsorption performance. SEM observations (Fig. 5) suggest that granules formed with 1% (w/v) boric acid tend to be irregular and non-spherical, with visible cracks, uneven pores, and relatively poor particle distribution, which may result in low structural integrity and high fragility. Granules with 3% (w/v) boric acid show comparatively improved morphology and partial sphericity; however, some surface cracks and structural heterogeneity are still observed, which may indicate incomplete or insufficient crosslinking under these conditions. In contrast, granules synthesized with 5% (w/v) boric acid appeared to be more spherical, with relatively smoother surfaces and more uniform porosity, which may enhance the accessibility of adsorption sites. These observations suggest that increasing boric acid concentration may contribute to the formation of a more developed crosslinking network and improved structural stability of the granules. This structural improvement may be associated with the higher adsorption performance observed for the 5% (w/v) sample (not shown here). Overall, based on SEM observations and adsorption results, 5% (w/v) boric acid appears to provide more favorable conditions for producing mechanically stable and structurally uniform granules suitable for the studied system.

PVA to NaAlO2/Clin ratio

Granules were synthesized using PVA: NaAlO2/Clin ratios of 1:1, 1:2, and 1:3 to determine the composition that may be more suitable for ammonia adsorption. As illustrated in Fig. 6, granules prepared with a 1:1 PVA: NaAlO2/Clin ratio tended to show relatively higher adsorption capacity, which could be related to a balance between the zeolite and polymer phases. This ratio appears to favor relatively uniform dispersion of zeolite particles within the polymer matrix, potentially allowing effective exposure of active sites, and maintaining structural integrity. SEM analysis indicated that these granules were generally spherical, smooth, and porous, with a mostly continuous and crack-free structure. In contrast, granules with a 1:2 PVA: NaAlO2/Clin ratio demonstrated somewhat lower adsorption performance. SEM images suggested somewhat uniform granules, but with decreased porosity and softer textures, which may indicate reduced mechanical stability. Granules with a 1:3 ratio showed the lowest observed performance, with SEM revealing dense, non-spherical, and smooth surfaces, possibly due to excessive encapsulation of zeolite particles and partial pore obstruction, which could limit access to active sites.

Macroscopic and SEM images of PVA/NaAlO2/Clin granules prepared with different PVA: NaAlO2/Clin ratios: (a) 1:1, (b) 1:2, and (c) 1:3.

Overall, the results suggest that a 1:1 PVA: NaAlO2/Clin ratio provides a relatively balanced combination of structural integrity, porosity, and adsorption efficiency. Higher polymer content, while increasing matrix cohesiveness, may compromise granule morphology and slightly reduce accessibility of active sites. Furthermore, as shown in Fig. 7, granules prepared with the 1:1 ratio achieved the highest measured ammonia removal efficiency (79.8%), forming mostly spherical granules with uniform dispersion of zeolite particles, suitable porosity, and good mechanical stability. Granules with the 1:2 ratio showed slightly lower performance (~ 77%) due to less uniformity and moderate structural integrity, while granules with the 1:3 ratio exhibited the lowest efficiency, possibly owing to excessive polymer encapsulation and blocked active sites. Therefore, the 1:1 PVA: NaAlO2/Clin ratio was selected as the most suitable condition within the tested range for subsequent experiments.

Ammonia adsorption results

To evaluate the adsorption performance of the optimized NaAlO2/Clin granules, their efficiency in removing ammonia was investigated under varying operational parameters. The granules, prepared using a 5% (w/v) PVA solution, 5% (w/v) boric acid, and a 1:1 weight ratio of NaAlO2/Clin to binder, had previously demonstrated an ammonia removal efficiency of 79.8% at an initial concentration of 100 mg/L. In this section, the influence of key factors including initial ammonia concentration, solution pH, adsorbent dosage, and contact time on the adsorption capacity of these optimized granules is systematically examined to determine the conditions that generally yield the higher performance.

Effect of initial concentration

The amount of ammonia adsorbed onto the surface of the adsorbent is highly influenced by its initial concentration in the aqueous solution. The effect of this parameter was investigated by varying the initial ammonia concentration between 25 and 200 mg/L. To ensure consistency with other experimental conditions, the pH was maintained at 8, the adsorbent dosage was fixed at 4 g/L, and the contact time was set at 120 min. The results, illustrated in Fig. 8a, demonstrate that increasing the initial ammonia concentration from 25 to 100 mg/L led to an apparent increase in adsorption efficiency for both the NaAlO2/Clin adsorbent and PVA/NaAlO2/Clin nanocomposite granules. This may be attributed to the greater availability of adsorption sites, as the active sites on the Clin structure were likely not saturated. However, further increasing the initial ammonia concentration from 100 to 200 mg/L resulted in a slight decline in adsorption efficiency, which could be explained by the saturation of available active sites. As the concentration of ammonia molecules increases, the adsorbent’s active sites become fully occupied. When ammonium ions undergo ion exchange with sodium (Na+) or potassium (K+) ions, the availability of free active sites decreases, potentially reducing interaction between ammonia molecules and the adsorbent surface and then a decrease in adsorption efficiency. Based on the experimental results, the observed maximum adsorption efficiency for both the powdered and granulated forms of the modified adsorbent was achieved at an initial ammonia concentration of 100 mg/L.

Effect of key parameters on ammonia adsorption efficiency of granular NaAlO2/Clin adsorbent: (a) initial ammonia concentration, (b) pH, (c) adsorbent dosage, and (d) contact time.

Effect of pH

To investigate the influence of pH on ammonia adsorption, experiments were carried out under controlled conditions. The impact of pH on adsorption capacity and efficiency is depicted in Fig. 8b. The process was performed at a constant contact time of 120 min and an initial ammonia concentration of 100 mg/L. The results indicate a clear relationship between pH and adsorption performance. As the pH increased from acidic to near-neutral conditions, both adsorption capacity and removal efficiency increased, reaching maximum values at approximately pH 8. This behavior can be attributed to ion-exchange interactions between ammonium ions (NH4+) and negatively charged sites in the zeolite framework associated with AlO4− groups. In this process, NH₄⁺ ions replace exchangeable cations such as Na+ present in the Clin structure. When the pH exceeded 8, a decrease in adsorption efficiency was observed. This decline can be explained by the equilibrium between ammonium ions (NH4+) and ammonia molecules (NH3). At higher pH values, the equilibrium shifts toward the formation of NH₃, which is not positively charged and therefore cannot participate in ion-exchange reactions with the zeolite framework, leading to reduced adsorption efficiency.

In strongly acidic conditions (pH < 5), lower ammonia removal was also observed. This is mainly due to the higher concentration of hydrogen ions (H+), which compete with NH4+ ions for the available exchange sites on the zeolite surface, thereby decreasing the adsorption capacity. Overall, these results demonstrate that pH is a critical parameter influencing the adsorption process. Both NaAlO2/Clin adsorbent and PVA/NaAlO2/Clin granules exhibited a similar trend, with optimal ammonia removal occurring under slightly alkaline conditions.

Effect of adsorbent dosage

Another critical factor influencing the adsorption performance and capacity of the adsorbents is the dosage used. To evaluate this parameter, both powder- and granule-shaped adsorbents were tested across a dosage range of 1–6 g/L. The ammonia concentration was maintained at 100 mg/L, with a pH of 8 and a contact time of 120 min. The results, presented in Fig. 8c, show that the ammonia removal percentage initially increases with adsorbent dosage. However, beyond a dosage of 4 g/L, the removal efficiency tends to plateau, indicating diminishing returns from adding more adsorbent. This plateau may result from system saturation, reduced driving force, particle aggregation, mass transfer limitations, and approach to equilibrium. As the adsorption sites become saturated, adding more adsorbent does not substantially enhance removal efficiency. Moreover, the adsorption capacity per unit mass of adsorbent (mg/g adsorbent) tends to decrease with increasing adsorbent dosage, likely because overlapping or aggregation of sites reduces the effective surface area.

It is worth noting that, unlike the powdered adsorbent, the granulated form showed a minor increase in removal efficiency beyond the dosage of 4 g/L, possibly due to differences in surface area and diffusion characteristics. However, since the increase in adsorption beyond 4 g/L was marginal for the granules, the same optimal dosage of 4 g/L was selected for both powdered and granulated adsorbents for consistency and practical application considerations.

Effect of contact time

Another critical factor influencing the adsorption process is the contact time between the adsorbent and the pollutant. To evaluate this parameter, contact times ranging from 20 to 240 min were studied, while other variables, including initial ammonium chloride concentrations (100 mg/L), adsorbent dosage (4 g/L for both adsorbents), and pH (8), were maintained constant. The results, as shown in Fig. 8d, indicate that adsorption occurs at a higher rate and intensity during the initial stages, likely due to the availability of vacant active sites. As contact time increases, the available adsorption sites become occupied, reducing the number of sites available for further ammonia interaction. As a result, the adsorption rate gradually decreases as equilibrium is approached. Based on the experimental data, a contact time of 120 min was considered sufficient for achieving effective ammonia adsorption.

Adsorption mechanism

The ammonia adsorption mechanism of sodium aluminate-modified Clin is likely governed by ion exchange, surface adsorption, and structural modifications associated with the incorporation of Al3+ ions. The substitution of Si4+ with Al3+ in the zeolite framework may increase the overall negative charge, thereby increasing the number of active ion exchange sites and potentially improving both the adsorption capacity and selectivity for ammonium ions (NH4+)25,26. In addition, the treatment may promote the incorporation of additional exchangeable sodium ions (Na⁺) within the zeolite structure. These Na⁺ ions can act as active ion-exchange sites and may be replaced by NH₄⁺ ions in aqueous solution, as represented by Eq. (7)26.

In neutral to mildly acidic pH conditions (pH 6–8), ammonia (NH3) predominantly exists as ammonium ions (NH4+), which favors ion exchange. The increased density of negative charges from Al3+ integration likely strengthens the electrostatic attraction between the zeolite surface and NH4+, thus potentially improving the adsorption efficiency. Additionally, the micro and mesoporous structure of Clin may contribute to the diffusion and entrapment of ammonium ions within the framework, supporting the overall adsorption process. Compared to other modification methods used in this study, including NaCl, NaOH, and Fe3O4, the NaAlO2-modified zeolite showed relatively higher adsorption performance under the studied conditions. Alkaline modifications such as NaOH primarily may enhance Na+ availability through cation exchange and surface activation, but do not necessarily induce framework-level substitution of Si4+ with Al3+. Similarly, NaCl modification mainly provides mobile Na+ ions without altering the structure. Fe3O4 addition can improve magnetic separation but is likely to have limited impact on ion exchange capacity.

In contrast, NaAlO2 modification is assumed to introduce Al3+ into the zeolite framework, which may enhance structural charge and increase high-affinity exchange sites for NH4+. This structural integration appears to be an advantage over other treatments. To complement these findings, FTIR analysis of the granulated adsorbents (Fig. 1) suggests significant chemical and structural modifications resulting from the use of PVA and boric acid as binders. Notable peaks at 3673 and 3442 cm-1 indicate the presence of hydroxyl (–OH) groups on the surface. Vibrations observed at 2923 and 2863 cm[-1 are attributed to C–H bonds from alkyl and methyl groups in the granulation agents, while a peak at 1645 cm-1 corresponds to C = O stretching vibrations, which may indicate possible interaction with ammonium ions. Furthermore, peaks at 1051, 603, and 463 cm-1 represent Si–O and Si–O–Al vibrations, which may indicate reflecting the zeolitic framework and structural adjustments that may result from granulation27.

Overall, these results suggest that both NaAlO2 modification and granulation can improve the physicochemical properties of Clin, indicating its potential as an adsorbent for removing ammonia from aqueous environments. While all modification strategies offer certain benefits, NaAlO2 modification appears to induce both structural and compositional changes that may contribute to improved ammonium adsorption. Granulation, despite a minor reduction in capacity due to binder coverage and pore blocking (Fig. 9), can enhance the material’s stability, reusability, and practical application potential. Thus, the synergistic effect of NaAlO2 modification and granulation can be considered a promising approach for ammonia removal from aqueous environments.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates the enhanced ammonia adsorption of natural Clin through chemical modification. Among the modifications tested, NaAlO2 treatment was found to be the most effective, leading to increased surface area, porosity, and active ion exchange sites, as confirmed by FTIR, SEM, and BET analyses. The maximum adsorption capacity of the modified powder reached 39.84 mg/g, indicating a clear improvement compared to unmodified Clin and highlighting the role of aluminum incorporation in strengthening the zeolite framework and enhancing NH4+ affinity. To address practical challenges, the powdered NaAlO2/Clin adsorbent was granulated using PVA and boric acid, producing mechanically stable granules with a maximum adsorption capacity of 24.42 mg/g. This granulation approach balances adsorption performance with ease of handling, mechanical stability, and reusability, which may be beneficial for potential practical applications in wastewater treatment under laboratory conditions. Key operational parameters; pH 8, adsorbent dosage 4 g/L, contact time 120 min, and initial ammonia concentration 100 mg/L, were identified as optimal for ammonia removal. It should be noted that the granulated form shows slightly lower adsorption capacity compared to the powder due to binder coverage and partial pore blockage. Nevertheless, the granules maintained relatively high performance over multiple adsorption–desorption cycles, indicating good reusability and potential operational stability under repeated use in controlled conditions.

Despite these positive outcomes, limitations remain, including the need for further enhancement of adsorption capacity in granulated form, optimization for variable wastewater compositions, and assessment under long-term or industrial conditions. Future research could explore hybrid modifications, hierarchical pore structures, or alternative binders to improve site accessibility and overcome diffusion limitations. Overall, this study presents a cost-effective laboratory-scale strategy for ammonia removal, combining material modification with process optimization. The findings highlight the potential of NaAlO2-modified Clin as a promising adsorbent for further investigation in environmental water treatment applications, although additional studies are required before industrial implementation can be confirmed.
