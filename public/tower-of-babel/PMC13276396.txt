Signal-averaged electrocardiography in horses: characteristics of ventricular late potentials in healthy Thoroughbreds and horses with cardiac abnormalities
Authors: Nozomi SEKI, Ryota TOCHINAI, Shin-ichi SEKIZAWA, Hajime OHMURA, Takanori UENO, Kentaro FUKUDA, Masayoshi KUWAHARA
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSudden death in horses, often attributed to lethal arrhythmias, poses significant welfare and economic risks, yet its prediction remains challenging. This study aimed to characterize ventricular micro-potentials using signal-averaged electrocardiography (SAECG) and evaluate their potential for assessing sudden death risk. High-resolution orthogonal X-, Y-, and Z-lead ECGs were recorded in 24 healthy Thoroughbreds (aged 2–31 years), one horse with bupivacaine-induced myocardial injury, and three horses with persistent pathological murmurs. SAECG parameters, including filtered QRS duration (fQRS), LAS40, RMS40, and RMS50, were analyzed from the vector magnitude waveform. In healthy horses, fQRS showed a significant positive correlation with age, with older horses exhibiting a significantly longer fQRS and LAS40 and lower RMS40 and RMS50 compared with younger horses, likely reflecting age-related myocardial fibrosis. The experimental myocardial injury model, characterized by histopathologically confirmed necrosis, resulted in a prolonged fQRS and LAS40 and decreased RMS40 and RMS50, consistent with the development of ventricular late potentials. Conversely, horses with persistent cardiac murmurs showed SAECG parameters within the range of healthy horses, indicating that valvular abnormalities alone do not significantly affect intraventricular conduction. These findings demonstrate that SAECG can effectively detect ventricular micro-potentials and reflect structural myocardial changes such as necrosis and fibrosis in horses. Consequently, SAECG analysis appears to be a promising non-invasive tool for screening the risk of lethal arrhythmias and sudden death in equine clinical practice, especially when combined with other diagnostic modalities.Keywords: horse, myocardial injury, signal-averaged electrocardiography, ventricular arrhythmia, ventricular late potentials

Sudden death in horses not only compromises animal welfare but also leads to rider injuries and significant economic losses if it occurs during exercise. Regarding the incidence of sudden death, reports indicate 28.7 cases per 100,000 starts among Thoroughbred racehorses in the UK between 2000 and 2007 [17]. In sport horses, 14 cases per 100,000 starts were reported in Fédération Équestre Internationale (FEI) eventing competitions between 2008 and 2014 [23].

While the causes of sudden death include heart failure, respiratory failure, pulmonary hemorrhage, and idiopathic vascular rupture, a substantial proportion of sudden deaths (22–68%) remain classified as “cause unknown” [22]. Under these circumstances, a case was reported where sudden death occurred incidentally in a Thoroughbred racehorse during training while its electrocardiogram (ECG) was being recorded [14]. Analysis of the ECG record revealed that frequent ventricular premature contractions were followed by the R-on-T phenomenon, which progressed to ventricular fibrillation and subsequent cardiac arrest, thereby confirming the existence of arrhythmic death in horses. Since this case, lethal arrhythmias have been increasingly emphasized as a significant factor in equine sudden death of unknown origin. Furthermore, post-mortem findings in horses with arrhythmias and those that died suddenly primarily show myocardial fibrosis. It is hypothesized that fibrotic lesions secondary to myocardial ischemia are involved in the development of ventricular arrhythmias [13, 22]. However, although exercise stress ECG has been attempted for the prediction of sudden death, investigating the cause and establishing preventive measures have been considered difficult due to the rarity of cases and the sudden onset without premonitory signs.

In human medicine, it is known that the prolongation of ventricular late potentials (VLPs)—micro-potentials observed at the end of the QRS complex caused by conduction delays in the diseased myocardium—is associated with the occurrence of ventricular arrhythmias. Analysis of VLPs is thus used for risk assessment of lethal arrhythmias and sudden death [10, 25].

To evaluate VLPs, the mathematical method proposed in 1981 is commonly used [26], which involves signal-averaging ECGs (SAECGs) from three axes (X, Y, and Z) to create a vector magnitude waveform. The evaluative parameters used in human medicine for detecting VLPs include fQRS (filtered QRS duration), LAS40 (duration of low-amplitude signals under 40 µV at the end of the QRS), and RMS40 (root mean square voltage of the terminal 40 msec of the QRS). While studies on VLP have been reported in animals such as dogs and pigs, RMS30 is often used instead of RMS40 in these species to account for their shorter QRS duration compared with humans [4, 28]. However, to date, there have been no reports on the detection of ventricular micro-potentials or the evaluation of VLPs using SAECG in horses.

The analysis of ventricular micro-potentials using SAECG, a method used in humans to assess the risk of lethal arrhythmias and sudden death, is considered potentially useful for investigating the causes of sudden death and predicting lethal arrhythmias in horses as well. Therefore, the objective of this study was to establish baseline SAECG characteristics in healthy horses and validate pathological alterations in SAECG findings using horses with experimentally induced myocardial injury and horses with cardiac murmurs.

Materials and Methods

Animals

Twenty-four clinically healthy Thoroughbred horses (16 males and 8 females; age range 2–31 years) were included. The healthy horses had no history of cardiovascular disease and exhibited normal findings on physical examination and conventional ECG. One additional Thoroughbred mare (4 years old) underwent experimental myocardial injury. Three horses (2 males and 1 female; 4, 18, and 24 years old) with persistent cardiac murmurs detected by auscultation were also enrolled. In all three cases, diastolic murmurs were audible over the aortic valve area, which were clinically suggestive of aortic valve regurgitation.

All procedures were approved by the institutional animal care and use committee. The Ethical Committee of the Equine Research Institute, Japan Racing Association, approved all animal experimental procedures used in this study (Approval Number 17-17).

Electrocardiographic recording

High-resolution electrocardiographic recordings were obtained using a digital Holter ECG system capable of high sampling frequency and low-noise signal acquisition (SER-102, Softron Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). Recordings were performed in unsedated horses at rest to avoid pharmacological effects on cardiac conduction or autonomic tone. All horses were housed individually in familiar stalls and allowed an acclimation period of at least 15 min prior to data acquisition.

Bipolar orthogonal X, Y, and Z leads were configured to approximate three-dimensional cardiac electrical activity. Electrode placement was standardized across horses as follows: the X-axis lead was positioned to record left–right electrical activity across the thorax, the Y-axis lead recorded cranial–caudal activity, and the Z-axis lead recorded dorsal–ventral activity. The electrode placement is shown in Fig. 1. Skin at electrode sites was clipped and cleaned with alcohol, and disposable electrodes (Vitorode G-600, Nihon Kohden Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) were secured using adhesive patches and elastic bandaging to minimize motion artifacts.

Electrode placement for orthogonal bipolar X, Y, and Z leads in horses.

Representative photograph of electrode attachment on a horse. The skin was clipped and cleaned with alcohol to ensure high-resolution signal acquisition.

Electrocardiographic signals were sampled continuously for approximately 90 min with a sampling frequency sufficient for signal-averaged analysis. All recordings were performed between 8:00 AM and 4:00 PM. Signals were stored digitally without real-time filtering to preserve high-frequency components required for subsequent SAECG processing.

Signal-averaged electrocardiography

A segment of the ECG recording exhibiting a stable sinus rhythm with minimal baseline noise was manually selected and analyzed using analysis software (SRV-lp, Softron Co., Ltd.). Ventricular premature complexes, atrial premature complexes, and beats affected by motion artifacts were excluded. QRS complexes were automatically detected and classified based on waveform morphology and area, and only beats with consistent morphology were included in the averaging process. All analyzed QRS complexes were strictly verified to be of sinus origin, and any potential ectopic beats or artifacts were excluded by the analysis software’s template-matching algorithm.

Approximately 1,000 QRS complexes per horse were temporally aligned using the point of maximal slope within the QRS complex as the fiducial reference. Signal averaging was performed to enhance low-amplitude signals by reducing random noise (SA_ECG, Softron Co., Ltd.). A bidirectional finite impulse response Simson filter was applied [26], and signals were band-pass filtered within a high-frequency range appropriate for ventricular late potential detection.

Orthogonal X, Y, and Z leads were combined to construct a vector magnitude waveform calculated as the square root of the sum of squared signals from each axis (√[X2 + Y2 + Z2]). The onset and offset of the filtered QRS complex were defined at an amplitude threshold of 1 µV above baseline noise.

The following SAECG parameters were measured using analysis software (AvgFilter, Softron Co., Ltd.) from the vector magnitude waveform: fQRS, defined as the duration from QRS onset to offset; LAS40; RMS40; and root mean square voltage of the terminal 50 msec (RMS50). The measurement of indicators followed the analysis method used in humans [2, 10, 26]. However, considering that the QRS width in horses is longer than in humans, both RMS40 and RMS50 were measured for RMS.

Conventional ECG

Conventional electrocardiographic analysis was performed using the same high-resolution Holter recordings obtained for SAECG analysis. After exclusion of artifacts and ectopic beats, representative segments of stable sinus rhythm were selected for each horse. Standard ECG parameters, including heart rate, PR interval, QRS duration, QT interval, and corrected QT interval (QTc), were measured using dedicated analysis software (SP2000, Softron Co., Ltd.). QTc was calculated using Bazett’s formula.

Experimental myocardial injury model and histopathology

Administration of bupivacaine into striated muscle induces necrosis of muscle cells [24]. Therefore, a myocardial injury model was created by administering bupivacaine into the myocardium. A 14G catheter was placed in the left jugular vein, and intravenous administration of 0.8 mg/kg xylazine hydrochloride (Elanco Japan, Tokyo, Japan) was performed to achieve adequate sedation. The animal was restrained in a standing position within a swing door. Following collapse induced by intravenous administration of 2.5 mg/kg ketamine (Daiichi Sankyo Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan), endotracheal intubation was performed, and the animal was connected to a large animal inhalation anesthesia machine. Sevoflurane (Maruishi Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Osaka, Japan) was used as the inhalation anesthetic. During surgery, 6 ml/kg·h of lactated Ringer’s solution (Otsuka Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) was administered intravenously. The heart was exposed by making an incision just above the fourth rib and resecting approximately 20 cm of the fourth rib. Using an 18 G injection needle, 40 ml of bupivacaine hydrochloride (Sandoz, Tokyo, Japan) was injected into the left ventricular wall at three separate sites. The rib was left resected, the chest wall was closed, and the animal was then awakened. ECGs were recorded in the stalls for 3 days before and after surgery, and ECG recordings and cumulative average ECG analyses were performed using the aforementioned methods. On postoperative day 4, the horses were euthanized, and the hearts were excised during necropsy. Four cross-sectional slices were prepared, cutting transversely through the injection site where bupivacaine was administered, and fixed in 20% neutral buffered formalin solution. Subsequently, each slice was further cut transversely into half its thickness, resulting in eight specimen preparation sites. Paraffin-embedded specimens of the left ventricular wall, centered around the injection site, were prepared and subjected to HE staining.

Results

SAECG parameters in healthy horses

Figure 2A shows a typical vector magnitude waveform of SAECG obtained from a horse. The mean SAECG parameters of the healthy horses were as follows: fQRS 146.2 ± 18.4 msec, LAS40 81.1 ± 17.0 msec, RMS40 168.1 ± 70.7 µV, and RMS50 248.4 ± 99.4 µV (Table 1).

Age showed a significant positive correlation with fQRS (r=0.48, P<0.05; Fig. 2B). When comparing test horses divided into two groups—12 years old or younger (young group, n=14) and 20 years old or older (old group, n=10)—fQRS and LAS40 were significantly longer in the old group, while RMS40 and RMS50 were significantly smaller in the old group (Fig. 2C). No gender differences were observed in any of the SAECG indices.

Additionally, the values for each standard ECG waveform analysis parameter are shown in Table 1. The correlation between the summed average ECG indices and each ECG waveform parameter is shown in Fig. 3. Positive correlations were observed between fQRS and QRS duration (r=0.42, P<0.05) and between RMS50 and QT interval (r=0.44, P<0.05).

Experimental myocardial injury model

Figure 4B shows the vector magnitude waveforms of the augmented average electrocardiogram before surgery and on postoperative day 3. Furthermore, Fig. 4C shows the changes in values for each indicator of the summed average ECG from pre-injection to postoperative day 1, postoperative day 2, and postoperative day 3. Compared with the young healthy reference range in Table 1, the postoperative fQRS on days 2 and 3 exceeded the upper limit (mean + 2 SD). While LAS40 showed an increasing trend and RMS values showed a decreasing trend relative to the horse’s own preoperative baseline, these parameters remained within the healthy young horse reference range. Throughout the observation period, the horse remained clinically stable with a normal appetite and no evidence of pyrexia. Occasional short runs of ventricular tachycardia (consisting of several consecutive beats) were observed, though these episodes occurred with low frequency and resolved spontaneously.

Horses with persistent cardiac murmurs

In all three of the horses with persistent cardiac murmurs, diastolic murmurs were audible over the aortic valve area and were clinically suggestive of aortic valve regurgitation. SAECG parameters for each horse were compared with the age-matched reference ranges (mean ± 2 SD) of healthy horses (Table 2). In all three horses, every parameter—including fQRS, LAS40, RMS40, and RMS50—remained within the respective healthy reference ranges, and no individual values deviated from these established norms.

Discussion

Analysis of ventricular micro-potentials is also feasible in horses. Similar to findings in humans, a positive correlation was observed between fQRS and QRS duration in terms of SAECG parameters and standard ECG waveforms [11]. In electrophysiological terms, fQRS reflects the total duration of ventricular depolarization, while LAS40 and RMS values reflect the presence and magnitude of fragmented, low-amplitude signals at the end of the QRS complex. In our experimental model, only fQRS exceeded the established reference range on postoperative days 2 and 3. This indicates that fQRS may be the most robust marker for detecting established myocardial injury in horses. On the other hand, while LAS40 and RMS values showed temporal changes relative to the individual’s own preoperative baseline, they did not deviate from the healthy reference range. This lack of deviation is likely due to the large individual variation (wide standard deviation) in these parameters within the healthy equine population. Although it is difficult to conclude the timing of abnormality onset for each parameter from a single case, our findings suggest that total conduction delay, as reflected by fQRS prolongation, is a clearer indicator of pathological alterations than terminal segment fragmentation in this acute necrotic model.

Furthermore, a correlation was found between RMS50 and the QT interval, suggesting that the generation of ventricular micro-potentials may be related to the ventricular repolarization process. Another characteristic of SAECG in horses is that the values of various parameters change with age. In the horses recorded in this study, an analysis of the relationship between SAECG parameters and age revealed that only fQRS showed a positive correlation, while no significant correlations were found for the other parameters. However, the old group exhibited significantly higher fQRS and LAS40 values and significantly lower RMS40 and RMS50 values compared with the young group. Previous reports have indicated that endocardial thickening suggestive of fibrosis is observed in approximately 60% of horses aged 20 years or older [6]. Accordingly, we calculated the proportion of individuals in the old group whose values deviated from the range of the mean ± standard deviation of the young group. The results showed that the proportions were 60% for fQRS, 50% for LAS40, 40% for RMS40, and 60% for RMS50, which were similar to the proportions in the aforementioned report. These findings suggest that in old horses exhibiting SAECG values markedly different from those of young horses, age-related changes such as myocardial fibrosis may have occurred, potentially affecting intraventricular conduction. Hatazoe et al. reported that sudden death in Thoroughbred stallions was associated with myocardial fibrosis and degeneration in the ventricular walls [12]. The age-related changes observed in our study align with these pathological findings, suggesting that SAECG can detect the substrate for lethal arrhythmias before clinical signs appear.

It is also known in humans that SAECG parameter values change with age; specifically, it has been clarified that fQRS and LAS40 are longer and RMS40 is smaller in adults (20–40 years old) than in minors (under 20 years old) [11]. Furthermore, a comparison between healthy individuals aged 50 or younger and those aged 62–102 showed no difference in the incidence of ventricular late potentials; however, fQRS was shorter and LAS40 was longer in the group aged 62–102 [19]. The change in fQRS reported by Mercando et al. was opposite to the results of the present analysis in horses. This discrepancy is likely because women are known to have shorter fQRS durations than men [21], and the shorter fQRS in the older group in the study of Mercando et al. [19] is thought to be the result of a higher proportion of women in the healthy 62–102 age group compared with the group aged 50 or younger. Additionally, differences in left ventricular mass between men and women have been cited as a reason for sex differences in fQRS in humans [29]. In contrast, no sex differences were observed in any of the parameters in horses. Since it has been reported that there are no sex differences in horses regarding left ventricular wall thickness and interventricular septal thickness (in two-dimensional echocardiography) [1] or heart weight [16], it was inferred that there is also no difference in left ventricular mass. However, as age could not be matched between the sexes in this study, further investigation into sex differences in ventricular micro-potentials in horses is considered necessary. It is also important to consider the unique cardiac anatomy of horses. Unlike humans, whose Purkinje fibers are localized to the subendocardium, horses possess a more extensive transmural distribution of these fibers throughout the ventricular wall [18]. This leads to a near-simultaneous burst of ventricular depolarization. Consequently, ventricular late potentials in horses might be shorter in duration or lower in amplitude than those in humans, potentially requiring more sensitive detection criteria.

Injection of bupivacaine into the left ventricular myocardium was confirmed by histopathological examination to have successfully induced clear myocardial necrosis. Postoperatively, fQRS and LAS40 became longer, while RMS40 and RMS50 decreased compared with preoperative values. These results are consistent with those of a porcine myocardial infarction model created by inserting a balloon catheter into the coronary artery under anesthesia [28]. This suggests that the presence of structural abnormalities in the myocardium was reflected in the changes in SAECG parameters as the generation of ventricular late potentials.

The plasma half-life of bupivacaine following intra-articular administration in horses is approximately 12 hr [15]. Bupivacaine is known for its slow binding and dissociation kinetics with myocardial sodium (Na) channels, and it has been reported that excessive inhibition of these channels can induce ventricular arrhythmias [5, 7]. Therefore, the ventricular tachycardia observed from postoperative days 1 to 2 was considered to be a direct effect of this drug. On the other hand, the SAECG parameters showed progressively larger changes from day 1 to days 2 and 3 post-administration compared with pre-administration. This suggests that the changes in ventricular micro-potentials reflect the induced myocardial necrosis and associated inflammation rather than the direct conduction-inhibiting effect of bupivacaine on cardiomyocytes.

Equine cardiac murmurs are classified into pathological and functional murmurs, the latter of which refers to those occurring in the absence of organic cardiac impairment [30]. In this study, we included only individuals with persistent murmurs considered pathological; all of them were identified as diastolic murmurs. Although pulmonary valve regurgitation has been confirmed in horses, it infrequently produces distinct murmurs [18]. Furthermore, since aortic regurgitation is reportedly common, particularly in older horses, it was inferred that the subjects in this study presented with aortic regurgitation.

The values of each SAECG parameter in the horses presenting with persistent pathological murmurs consistently fell within the healthy reference range (mean ± 2 SD), with no individuals exhibiting abnormalities. In humans, the incidence of ventricular late potentials in patients with valvular disease is known to be low. Additionally, a study investigating the relationship between the occurrence of arrhythmias during and after exercise and valvular regurgitation in horses reported no significant association between the two [3]. Although the specific details of the underlying pathology causing the murmurs remain unclear because echocardiography and histopathological examinations could not be performed on the subjects, it is speculated that similar lesions were present, as aortic regurgitation is attributed to valvular thickening and distortion [6]. These findings suggest that valvular abnormalities without severe myocardial remodeling are unlikely to affect intraventricular excitation propagation and do not contribute to the generation of ventricular late potentials.

A significant limitation of this study is the lack of objective diagnostic imaging for the horses with cardiac murmurs. Although the murmurs were clinically identified as diastolic and were highly suggestive of aortic regurgitation, echocardiography and phonocardiography were not performed. Therefore, the definitive diagnosis and the quantitative assessment of valvular severity and cardiac remodeling remain unknown. It cannot be ruled out that more severe valvular disease leading to significant cardiac dysfunction could affect intraventricular conduction. Future studies incorporating comprehensive imaging and electrophysiological evaluations are needed to more precisely clarify the relationship between specific cardiac pathologies and SAECG parameters.

Another consideration is the potential for circadian variation in SAECG parameters. In human medicine, although SAECG indices are generally considered stable, some studies have suggested that circadian fluctuations in autonomic nervous activity may influence ventricular conduction and the detection of late potentials [20]. Since our recordings were limited to the period between 8:00 AM and 4:00 PM, the influence of diurnal rhythms on equine SAECG remains unclear. Future studies investigating 24-hr fluctuations are warranted to establish more standardized recording protocols.

This study suggests that further investigation into the criteria for SAECG positivity could enable its clinical application for risk assessment of lethal arrhythmias and sudden death in horses. In human clinical practice, it has been noted that the detection rate of ventricular late potentials varies depending on the underlying cardiac disease and the location of the lesions [8], and a low positive predictive value has been identified as a limitation. Therefore, a comprehensive evaluation is recommended, integrating left ventricular function assessment and the evaluation of arrhythmias and heart rate variability using Holter ECG [9]. Furthermore, radionuclide ventriculography and cardiac electrophysiological studies are recommended in conjunction for the risk stratification of sudden death [27]. However, since the negative predictive value of SAECG analysis is high [25], the risk of lethal arrhythmias or sudden death is considered low when there are no abnormalities in left ventricular or autonomic nervous system function. In equine medicine, while tests such as radionuclide ventriculography and cardiac electrophysiological studies are not easily performed due to high costs and invasiveness, SAECG analysis is considered a useful tool for risk screening. To improve the signal-to-noise ratio, future studies should consider recording in a shielded room to minimize electromagnetic interference. Additionally, although our recordings were standardized to the morning, potential circadian variations in SAECG parameters influenced by autonomic fluctuations warrant further investigation. It is expected that accumulating further data in combination with other diagnostic methods, such as echocardiography, will lead to more accurate risk assessment in the future.
