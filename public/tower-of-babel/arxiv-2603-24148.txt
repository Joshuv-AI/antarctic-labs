Mpemba effect in a two-dimensional bistable potential
Hisao Hayakawa, Satoshi Takada

Abstract
We present an exactly solvable model of the Mpemba effect in an overdamped Langevin system confined to a two-dimensional, radially symmetric bistable potential. The potential is constructed as a piecewise quadratic-logarithmic function that is continuous and differentiable at the matching radii, enabling an exact mapping of the corresponding Fokker-Planck operator to a Schrödinger-type eigenvalue problem. The relaxation spectrum and eigenmodes are obtained analytically in each region in terms of confluent hypergeometric functions, with eigenvalues determined from matching conditions. Focusing on isotropic equilibrium initial states at inverse temperature β ini \beta_{\rm ini} quenched to a bath at inverse temperature β \beta , we derive explicit expressions for the mode amplitudes governing long-time relaxation. We demonstrate that the coefficient of the slowest mode exhibits a non-monotonic dependence on β ini \beta_{\rm ini} and identify a sufficient crossing condition for the Kullback-Leibler divergence in terms of the two slowest modes, if the global minimum of the potential is located far away from the origin and the second minimum exists near the origin. For corresponding parameters, we demonstrate that the Mpemba effect can be realized. Our results provide a rare example of an analytically tractable two-dimensional model exhibiting anomalous relaxation without any confining walls, extending previous one-dimensional constructions with a hard wall and clarifying the role of radial geometry in nonequilibrium relaxation phenomena.



I
Introduction

The Mpemba effect refers to the anomalous relaxation phenomenon in which an initially hotter system,
quenched into contact with a cold bath, reaches equilibrium faster than the same system prepared at a
lower initial temperature. The effect was first reported by Mpemba and Osborne
Mpemba1969
, who
observed that hotter water can freeze faster than colder water. Although its very existence has been
debated
Burridge2016
;
Katz2017
, theoretical studies soon after the debates already suggested
possible scenarios for faster freezing from higher temperatures
Lu2017
;
Lasanta2017
. In recent
years, numerous experimental and theoretical works have confirmed Mpemba-like processes in which a
“temperature-like” observable exhibits a crossing, such that a system initialized at higher
temperature subsequently relaxes faster than the same system started at lower temperature.

The Mpemba effect has been reported in a broad spectrum of classical systems, including colloidal
particles in optical traps

;

;

, inertial suspensions
Takada2021
;

,
spin glasses
Baity2019
, molecular binary mixtures
Gonzalez2021
, phase transitions
Holtzman2022
;

variety of Markovian model

Lu2017
;

;
Klich2019
;
Busiello2021
;

;
Biswas2023
;

, non-Markovian models

;

. In
addition, quantum analogues of the effect have been demonstrated in open quantum systems

;

, establishing the Mpemba effect as a general nonequilibrium relaxation phenomenon.

Several reviews now provide unified perspectives on both classical and quantum Mpemba effects

Bechhoefer2021
;
Teza2025
;
Ares2025Rev
, emphasizing their connection to nonequilibrium speed limits
and anomalous relaxation mechanisms. Two broad scenarios are typically distinguished

;

;

;

: (i) comparing relaxation from an equilibrium versus a
non-equilibrium initial condition, and (ii) comparing relaxation from two equilibrium states prepared
at different initial temperatures. The latter scenario, which we follow in this paper, has led to
general unifying frameworks

;

A central theoretical picture, proposed by Lu and Raz
Lu2017
, is that the Mpemba effect arises in systems with a non-convex free-energy landscape. Starting from an initial distribution localized near a metastable minimum, the mean relaxation time across the barrier decreases with increasing initial temperature, leading to the observed crossing phenomenon. This interpretation naturally
extends to one-particle dynamics in bistable potentials, as confirmed in experiments on optically trapped colloids
Kumar2020
;
Kumar2022
.
Variants such as the strong Mpemba effect
Klich2019
have also been studied, though their relation to the original freezing anomaly is more tenuous.
If we are interested in relaxations from equilibrium initial conditions, the Mpemba effect arises when the projection onto the slowest mode is smaller for the initially hotter state, even if its total distance from equilibrium is larger.

So far, we have only results for the motion of a particle in a discrete-level model, piece-wise linear potentials, and square box potentials
Lu2017
;
Lasanta2017
;
Kumar2020
;
Walker2021
;
Biswas2023
.
To the best of our knowledge, there are no analytical studies of the motion of a particle confined to a smooth bistable potential, even in one-dimensional (1D) settings.
Experimentally and theoretically, one-dimensional systems differ fundamentally from higher-dimensional systems, such as two-dimensional (2D) systems.
Quite recently, Liu et al.
Liu26
;
Liu26long
have analyzed the motion in 1D asymmetric bistable potentials and found that the metastable picture is not correct for the realization of the Mpemba effect, but a confining wall is necessary to observe the Mpemba effect.
To understand the robustness of the Mpemba effect, it is natural to analyze a 2D system to clarify the differences and common features in 1D and 2D systems, including whether confining walls are necessary to observe the Mpemba effect even in 2D.

In this work, we develop an exactly solvable model of the Mpemba effect in a
smooth 2D asymmetric bistable potential
by using a connection of three potentials taking into account matching conditions at the connection points.
While previous exact or semi-analytical approaches have mostly been restricted to 1D models, our extension to two dimensions provides a natural and richer generalization.
The analysis makes use of the mapping from the Fokker-Planck equation to a Schrödinger-type eigenvalue problem, which enables us to determine the relaxation spectrum and eigenmodes exactly.
We show that the Mpemba effect of the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence
sagawa
, which is the best monotone measure, i.e., decreases monotonically with time, (and its inverse) can be realized in this setting without the introduction of any confining walls, thus broadening the landscape of analytically tractable nonequilibrium phenomena.
Unlike previous criteria based on specific observables, our condition is formulated directly in terms of the full probability distribution via the KL divergence.

II.1
Formulation

Let us consider the motion of a particle at position

We assume that the motion of the particle obeys the dimensionless Langevin equation as

\dot{\bm{r}}=-\frac{\partial V(r)}{\partial\bm{r}}+\bm{\xi}(t),

(1)

where

\langle\xi_{\alpha}(t)\rangle=0,\quad\langle\xi_{\alpha}(t)\xi_{\beta}(t^{\prime})\rangle=2T\delta_{\alpha\beta}\delta(t-t^{\prime})

(2)

The corresponding Fokker-Planck equation can be written as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial P(\bm{r},t)}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{L}P(\bm{r},t),

(3)

\displaystyle\mathbb{L}:

\displaystyle=\bm{\nabla}\cdot\left(\frac{\bm{r}}{r}V^{\prime}(r)\right)+T\nabla^{2},

(4)

where

.
The analysis of the Fokker-Planck equation follows the textbook

Equations (

) and (

) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial P(r,t)}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=-\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{J}(\bm{r},t),\quad\bm{J}(\bm{r},t):=\left\{T\bm{\nabla}+\frac{\bm{r}}{r}V^{\prime}(r)\right\}P(\bm{r},t).

(5)

Note that

should be isotropic if the initial condition is isotropic, i.e.
P(\bm{r},t)=P(r,t)

.

We consider a quench process from
T_{\mathrm{ini}}=\beta_{\mathrm{ini}}^{-1}
to
T=\beta^{-1}
.
We also assume that the initial and final states are at equilibrium as

\displaystyle P_{\mathrm{ini}}(r)

\displaystyle=P_{\mathrm{eq}}(r,\beta_{\mathrm{ini}}):=\frac{e^{-\beta_{\mathrm{ini}}V(r)}}{Z(\beta_{\mathrm{ini}})},

(6)

\displaystyle P(r,t\to\infty)

\displaystyle=P_{\mathrm{eq}}(r,\beta),

(7)

with
Z(\beta):=2\pi\int_{0}^{\infty}drre^{-\beta V(r)}
, and
P_{\mathrm{eq}}(r,\beta)
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{L}P_{\mathrm{eq}}(r,\beta)=0.

(8)

II.2
A choice of solvable potential

In general, it is difficult to obtain the exact solution of the Fokker-Planck equation with a double-well potential.
However, for a suitably chosen form of the potential
V(r)
, we can solve the Fokker-Planck equation.
We therefore construct an exactly solvable radially symmetric double-well potential
V(r)
in two dimensions.
The requirements are:
1)
V(r)
is continuous and differentiable across its domains,
2)
V(r)
has a minimum at
r=0
, a maximum at
r=\xi
, and a second minimum at
r=\alpha>\xi
.

We take
V(r)
the piecewise quadratic-logarithmic form:

\displaystyle V(r)=\begin{cases}V_{\rm in}(r)=\dfrac{k_{\rm in}}{2}r^{2}+C_{\rm in},&0\leq r<r_{-},\\[5.0pt]
V_{\rm mid}(r)=\dfrac{k_{\rm mid}}{2}r^{2}+b_{\rm mid}\ln r+C_{\rm mid},&r_{-}\leq r<r_{+},\\[5.0pt]
V_{\rm out}(r)=\dfrac{k_{\rm out}}{2}r^{2}+b_{\rm out}\ln r+C_{\rm out},&r\geq r_{+},\end{cases}

(9)

where
r_{\pm}
denote matching points, and
C_{\rm in},C_{\rm mid}
, and
C_{\rm out}
are, respectively, constants to ensure continuity.
As will be shown, the conditions
b_{\mathrm{mid}}\propto\xi^{2}
and
b_{\mathrm{out}}\propto\alpha^{2}
should be satisfied.
Notably, this specific form leads to an exactly solvable Fokker-Planck equation.

The derivatives in each region are given by

\displaystyle V^{\prime}_{\rm in}(r)

\displaystyle=k_{\rm in}r,\quad V^{\prime}_{\rm mid}(r)=k_{\rm mid}\left(r-\frac{\xi^{2}}{r}\right),\quad V^{\prime}_{\rm out}(r)=k_{\rm out}\left(r-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{r}\right).

(10)

The second derivative of
V(r)
are given by

\displaystyle V^{\prime\prime}_{\rm in}(r=0)=k_{\rm in},\quad V^{\prime\prime}_{\rm mid}(r=\xi)=2k_{\rm mid},\quad V^{\prime\prime}_{\rm out}(r=\alpha)=2k_{\rm out},

(11)

where
V^{\prime\prime}(r)=d^{2}V(r)/dr^{2}
.
The conditions for bistability are, therefore, given by

\displaystyle k_{\rm in}>0,\qquad k_{\rm mid}<0,\qquad k_{\rm out}>0.

(12)

Continuity of
V^{\prime}(r)
at
r=r_{\pm}
gives explicit formulas for the matching radii:

\displaystyle r_{-}

\displaystyle=\xi\sqrt{\frac{-k_{\rm mid}}{k_{\rm in}-k_{\rm mid}}},\qquad r_{+}=\sqrt{\frac{k_{\rm out}\alpha^{2}-k_{\rm mid}\xi^{2}}{\,k_{\rm out}-k_{\rm mid}\,}}.

(13)

For the sign choice above, these roots are real and positive, satisfying
0<r_{-}<\xi<r_{+}<\alpha
for appropriate parameter sets.

Continuity of
V(r)
at
r_{\pm}
fixes
C_{\rm mid}
and
C_{\rm out}
:

\displaystyle C_{\rm mid}

\displaystyle=\frac{k_{\rm in}-k_{\rm mid}}{2}r_{-}^{2}-b_{\rm mid}\ln r_{-}+C_{\rm in},

(14)

\displaystyle C_{\rm out}

\displaystyle=\frac{k_{\rm mid}-k_{\rm out}}{2}r_{+}^{2}+(b_{\rm mid}-b_{\rm out})\ln r_{+}+C_{\rm mid}.

(15)

One may set
C_{\rm in}=0
without loss of generality.

The potential
V(r)
in Eq. (
10
) has two minima at
r=0
and
r=\alpha
.
Then, the values of
V(r)
at these minima are, respectively, given by
V(0)=0
, and

\displaystyle V(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\frac{k_{\rm in}}{2}r_{-}^{2}+\frac{k_{\rm mid}}{2}\left(r_{+}^{2}-r_{-}^{2}\right)+\frac{k_{\rm out}}{2}\left(\alpha^{2}-r_{+}^{2}\right)+b_{\rm mid}\ln\!\left(\frac{r_{+}}{r_{-}}\right)+b_{\rm out}\ln\!\left(\frac{\alpha}{r_{+}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}k_{\rm out}\alpha^{2}\ln\frac{k_{\rm out}\alpha^{2}-k_{\rm mid}\xi^{2}}{\alpha^{2}(k_{\rm out}-k_{\rm mid})}+\frac{1}{2}k_{\rm mid}\xi^{2}\ln\left(\frac{k_{\rm out}-k_{\rm mid}}{k_{\rm in}-k_{\rm mid}}\frac{-k_{\rm mid}\xi^{2}}{k_{\rm out}\alpha^{2}-k_{\rm mid}\xi^{2}}\right),

(16)

where we have used Eqs. (
9
), (
13
), (
14
), and (
15
).
We distinguish three cases: (i)
V(\alpha)<0
, (ii)
V(\alpha)=0
, and (iii)
V(\alpha)>0
.
For the representative choice
k_{\mathrm{in}}=k_{\mathrm{out}}=\xi=1
, the marginal line for
V(\alpha)=0
is shown in Fig.
2
.

II.3
Map to Schrödinger equation

II.3.1
Framework

We can map from Fokker-Planck equation to Schrödinger equation.
This might help us to analyze the model.
Let us introduce
\mathbb{P}(\bm{r},t)
defined as

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(r,t):=e^{\beta V(r)/2}P(r,t),

(17)

where
\mathbb{P}(\bm{r},t)
satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\mathbb{P}(r,t)

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{H}\mathbb{P}(r,t),

(18)

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}

\displaystyle:=-e^{\beta V(r)/2}\mathbb{L}e^{-\beta V(r)/2}=-T\nabla^{2}+V_{S}(r).

(19)

Recall that the Schrödinger mapping gives

\displaystyle V_{S}(r):=\frac{1}{4T}\,(V^{\prime}(r))^{2}+\frac{1}{2r}\frac{d}{dr}\!\big(rV^{\prime}(r)\big).

(20)

For the potential in Eq. (
9
),
V_{S}(r)
is given by

\displaystyle V_{S}(r)=\begin{cases}k_{\rm in}+\dfrac{k_{\rm in}^{2}}{4T}r^{2},&0\leq r<r_{-},\\[10.00002pt]
k_{\rm mid}+\dfrac{k_{\rm mid}b_{\rm mid}}{2T}+\dfrac{k_{\rm mid}^{2}}{4T}r^{2}+\dfrac{b_{\rm mid}^{2}}{4Tr^{2}},&r_{-}\leq r<r_{+},\\[10.00002pt]
k_{\rm out}+\dfrac{k_{\rm out}b_{\rm out}}{2T}+\dfrac{k_{\rm out}^{2}}{4T}r^{2}+\dfrac{b_{\rm out}^{2}}{4Tr^{2}},&r\geq r_{+}.\end{cases}

(21)

Requiring that
V_{S}(r)
has extrema at
r=0
,
\xi
, and
\alpha
yields

\displaystyle b_{\rm mid}=-k_{\rm mid}\,\xi^{2},\quad b_{\rm out}=-k_{\rm out}\,\alpha^{2}.

(22)

Equation (
21
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle V_{S}(r)=k_{\mathrm{I}}+\frac{k_{\mathrm{I}}b_{\mathrm{I}}}{2T}+\frac{k_{\mathrm{I}}^{2}}{4T}r^{2}+\frac{b_{\mathrm{I}}^{2}}{4Tr^{2}},

(23)

where the suffix I represents the region
\mathrm{I}\in\{\mathrm{in},\mathrm{mid},\mathrm{out}\}
with
b_{\mathrm{in}}=0
.
The advantage of using the map to Schrödinger equation is obvious because of
\mathbb{H}^{\dagger}=\mathbb{H}
.

We note the following:
i) Each segment of
V_{S}(r)
is solvable quadratic plus inverse-square potential.
ii) The solutions of the corresponding radial Schrödinger equation are expressible in terms of confluent hypergeometric functions.
iii) The matching conditions at
r=r_{-}
and
r=r_{+}
enforce continuity of both
V(r)
and
V^{\prime}(r)
, which automatically ensure the continuity of
V_{S}(r)
and the physical wavefunctions.
This construction provides a fully solvable model for a two-dimensional bistable potential with spherical symmetry.

Now, the problem is mapped onto the eigenvalue problem of the operator
\mathbb{H}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}\varphi_{m,n}(r)=\lambda_{m,0}\varphi_{m,n}(r),

(24)

where
\varphi_{m,n}(r)
is related to the left and right eigenfunctions as

\ell_{m,n}(\bm{r})=e^{\beta V(r)/2}\varphi_{m,n}(r),\quad\mathrm{and}\quad r_{m,n}(r)=e^{-\beta V(r)/2}\varphi_{m,n}(r),

(25)

where
\ell_{m,n}(\bm{r})
and
r_{m,n}(\bm{r})
are, respectively, the left and right eigenvectors, which satisfy

\displaystyle\mathbb{L}r_{m,n}(\bm{r})

\displaystyle=-\lambda_{m,0}r_{m,n}(\bm{r}),

(26)

\displaystyle\mathbb{L}^{\dagger}\ell_{m,n}(\bm{r})

\displaystyle=\lambda_{m,0}\ell_{m,n}(\bm{r}),\quad\mathbb{L}^{\dagger}:=-\frac{\bm{r}}{r}V^{\prime}(r)\cdot\bm{\nabla}+T\nabla^{2}.

(27)

Note that the two-dimensional Schrödinger equation has discrete eigenvalues characterized by a set of non-negative integers
(m,n)
, where the eigenvalue for an isotropic potential
V_{S}(r)
should be independent of
n
, i.e.,
\lambda_{m,n}=\lambda_{m,0}
for an arbitrary non-negative integer
n
.

When we begin with an isotropic (equilibrium) condition Eq. (
6
), the solution of Schrödinger equation is independent of the second index
n
.
Thus, the orthonormal relation

\displaystyle\int d\bm{r}\ell_{m,n}(r)r_{m^{\prime},n^{\prime}}(r)=\delta_{mm^{\prime}}\delta_{nn^{\prime}}

(28)

is converted into

\displaystyle\int d\bm{r}\varphi_{m,0}(r)\varphi_{m^{\prime},0}(r)=\delta_{mm^{\prime}}.

(29)

Then, we can use the spectrum decomposition of
P(r,t)
as

\displaystyle P(r,t)

\displaystyle=P_{\mathrm{eq}}(r,\beta)+e^{-\beta V(r)/2}\sum_{m=2}^{\infty}a_{m}\phi_{m}e^{-\tilde{\lambda}_{m}t}

\displaystyle\approx P_{\mathrm{eq}}(r,\beta)+e^{-\beta V(r)/2}\left\{a_{2}\phi_{2}(r)e^{-\tilde{\lambda}_{2}t}+a_{3}\phi_{3}(r)e^{-\tilde{\lambda}_{3}t}\right\},

(30)

where

\displaystyle a_{m}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi\delta_{n0}}{Z(\beta_{\mathrm{ini}})}\int_{0}^{\infty}drr\exp\left[\left(\frac{\beta}{2}-\beta_{\mathrm{ini}}\right)V(r)\right]\phi_{m}(r).

(31)

For such an initial condition, we shall introduce

\displaystyle\phi_{m}(r):=\varphi_{m-1,0}(r),\quad\tilde{\lambda}_{m}:=\lambda_{m-1,0}=\lambda_{m-1,n}.

(32)

Note that
a_{m}
corresponds to a one-dimensional counterpart, where
m=1
and
m=2
represent the zero mode and slowest relaxation mode, respectively.
We also write the equation:

\displaystyle F_{m}(\beta_{\mathrm{ini}}):=\frac{\partial a_{m}}{\partial\beta_{\mathrm{ini}}}=-2\pi\int_{0}^{\infty}drre^{\beta V(r)/2}\phi_{m}(r)[V(r)-\langle V\rangle_{\beta_{\mathrm{ini}}}]P_{\mathrm{ini}}(r)

(33)

with
\langle V\rangle_{\beta_{\mathrm{ini}}}:=2\pi\int_{0}^{\infty}drrV(r)P_{\mathrm{ini}}(r)
,
which is important to detect the necessary condition
F_{2}(\beta_{\mathrm{ini}}^{*})=0
at
\beta_{\mathrm{ini}}=\beta_{\mathrm{ini}}^{*}
.

The approximate expression in Eq. (
II.3.1
) is useful in the long-time limit.
Although the eigenvalues are degenerate for

, the eigenvalues are non-degenerate for modes with

Thus, the eigenvalues satisfy the order
\tilde{\lambda}_{1}=0<\tilde{\lambda}_{2}<\tilde{\lambda}_{3}<\tilde{\lambda}_{4}<\cdots
with finite gaps between
\tilde{\lambda}_{n}
and
\tilde{\lambda}_{n+1}

Therefore, the late-stage dynamics is dominated by the slowest modes associated with

and
\tilde{\lambda}_{3}
,

Using Eq. (

\displaystyle\langle\hat{A}\rangle(t)\approx 2\pi\int_{0}^{\infty}drrP_{\mathrm{eq}}(r,\beta)\hat{A}+2\pi\int_{0}^{\infty}drr\hat{A}\left[e^{-\beta V(r)/2}\left\{a_{2}\phi_{2}(r)e^{-\tilde{\lambda}_{2}t}+a_{3}\phi_{3}(r)e^{-\tilde{\lambda}_{3}t}\right\}\right],

where

Thus, the relaxation of

II.3.2
Condition to observe the Mpemba effect

Let us consider Eqs. (
II.3.1
) and (
34
) in the large
t
regime, where the relaxation term proportional to
a_{2}
is only relevant.
Then, the previous papers
Lu2017
;

;

;

have shown that the condition to observe the Mpemba effect is that

has a maximum or a minimum against
\beta_{\mathrm{ini}}

Indeed, this condition is necessary because the peak of

\beta_{\mathrm{ini}}^{*}
corresponds to the slowest initial temperature to approach the final equilibrium state after the quench.
This suggests that two relaxation processes, one starting from

and the other from
\beta_{\mathrm{ini}}<\beta_{\mathrm{ini}}^{*}

However, this condition may be insufficient, as it does not guarantee that the slowest eigenmode crosses another relaxation mode proportional to

To improve the defect of the previous condition, only

, we will propose a more direct crossing condition for a monotonic measure, such as the KL divergence, to observe the Mpemba effect later.

III.1
Eigenvalue equation

Let us rewrite

introduced in Eq. (

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}

\displaystyle=-\frac{T}{r}\frac{d}{dr}\left(r\frac{d}{dr}\right)+V_{S}(r).

(39)

Accordingly, we can rewrite Eq. (

\displaystyle\phi_{n}^{\prime\prime}(r)+\frac{\phi_{n}^{\prime}(r)}{r}-\left(\beta V_{S}(r)-\beta\tilde{\lambda}_{n}\right)\phi_{n}(r)=0.

(40)

where
\tilde{\lambda}_{n}:=\lambda_{n-1,0}
with a positive integer

Let us introduce the auxiliary parameters

\displaystyle\nu_{\mathrm{I}}:=\frac{|b_{\mathrm{I}}|}{2}\sqrt{\frac{\beta}{T}},\qquad\gamma_{\mathrm{I}}:=\frac{\beta|k_{\mathrm{I}}|}{2}.

(41)

In this paper, we take the positive branch
\nu=|\nu|
(choose the sign consistent with regularity at

) and
\gamma\geq 0
.

Introducing

\displaystyle z_{\mathrm{I}}:=\gamma_{\mathrm{I}}r^{2},\qquad\Phi_{\mathrm{I},n}(z):=\phi_{\mathrm{I},n}(r)r^{-\nu_{\mathrm{I}}}e^{z_{\mathrm{I}}/2},

where

\displaystyle z\Phi^{\prime\prime}_{\mathrm{I},n}(z)+(1+\nu_{\mathrm{I}}-z)\Phi^{\prime}_{\mathrm{I},n}(z)-\mu_{\mathrm{I},n}\Phi_{\mathrm{I},n}(z)=0,

(43)

with the parameter

\displaystyle\mu_{\mathrm{I},n}:=\frac{1+\nu_{\mathrm{I}}}{2}-\frac{\beta\left(\lambda_{n}-k_{\mathrm{I}}-\tfrac{k_{\mathrm{I}}|b_{\mathrm{I}}|}{2T}\right)}{4\gamma_{\mathrm{I}}}.

(44)

Two independent solutions of the confluent-hypergeometric equation are
M(\mu;1+\nu;z):={}_{1}F_{1}(\mu;1+\nu;z)
and

defined in Eq. (

) (see Appendix
A
for the definitions of them and their properties).
Hence, the general solution of Eq. (
40
) in the region can be expressed as

\displaystyle\phi_{\mathrm{I},n}(r)=r^{\nu_{\mathrm{I}}}e^{-\tfrac{\gamma_{\mathrm{I}}r^{2}}{2}}\left[A_{\mathrm{I}}M\left(\mu_{\mathrm{I},n};1+\nu_{\mathrm{I}};\gamma_{\mathrm{I}}r^{2}\right)+B_{\mathrm{I}}U\left(\mu_{\mathrm{I},n};1+\nu_{\mathrm{I}};\gamma_{\mathrm{I}}r^{2}\right)\right],

(45)

with constants
A_{\mathrm{I}}
and
B_{\mathrm{I}}
to be fixed by boundary/matching conditions.

IV.3
Condition of the crossing of the KL divergence

Although we have confirmed the existence of a peak of
a_{2}
as a function of
\beta_{\mathrm{ini}}
, this may not be sufficient to observe the Mpemba effect.
To observe the Mpemba effect, we need a crossing condition for the observable.
In this subsection, we clarify the sufficient condition to observe the Mpemba effect.

Among many possible observables, we focus on the KL divergence defined as

\displaystyle D^{\mathrm{KL}}(P(\beta,\beta_{\mathrm{ini}};t)||P_{\mathrm{eq}}(\beta))

\displaystyle:=\int d\bm{r}P(\bm{r},t)\ln\frac{P(\bm{r},t)}{P_{\mathrm{eq}}(r,\beta)}=2\pi\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{n(n-1)}\int_{0}^{\infty}drr\frac{(\delta P)^{n}}{P_{\mathrm{eq}}^{n-1}},

(59)

which is a typical monotonic measure, where
\delta P:=P(\bm{r},t)-P_{\mathrm{eq}}(\bm{r})
.
If we are interested in the late stage of relaxation dynamics or the deviation from the initial state and the final state is small,
D^{\mathrm{KL}}(P(t)||P_{\mathrm{eq}})
can be approximated as

\displaystyle D^{\mathrm{KL}}(P(\beta,\beta_{\mathrm{ini}};t)||P_{\mathrm{eq}}(\beta))

\displaystyle=2\pi\int_{0}^{\infty}drr\frac{(\delta P)^{2}}{2P_{\mathrm{eq}}}+O(\delta P^{3})\approx\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n\geq 2}C_{n}\left(a_{n}\right)^{2}e^{-2\tilde{\lambda}_{n}t},

(60)

where we have introduced

C_{n}:=\|\phi_{n}\|^{2}_{P_{\rm eq}^{-1}}>0

(61)

with
\|\phi_{n}\|^{2}_{P_{\rm eq}^{-1}}:=2\pi Z(\beta)\int_{0}^{\infty}drr\phi_{n}(r)^{2}
.
Here,
C_{n}
should be independent of
n
because each mode
\phi_{n}(r)
satisfies the normalization.
Thus, we denote
C_{n}
as
C^{*}
.

If we are interested in the late-stage dynamics, the modes associated with the slowest two modes
\tilde{\lambda}_{2}
and
\tilde{\lambda}_{3}
are dominant because
e^{-\tilde{\lambda}_{m}t}\ll 1
for
m\geq 4
is negligible.
Therefore, we adopt the two-mode approximation as

\displaystyle D^{\mathrm{KL}}(P(t)||P_{\mathrm{eq}})\approx\frac{C^{*}}{2}(a_{2}^{2}e^{-2\tilde{\lambda}_{2}t}+a_{3}^{2}e^{-2\tilde{\lambda}_{3}t}).

(62)

Thus, the condition of a crossing of the KL divergence, characterized by Eq. (
62
), is expressed as

\mathcal{F}(\beta_{\mathrm{ini}}^{\sharp},\beta_{\mathrm{ini}}^{*}):=\frac{\Delta_{3}}{\Delta_{2}}<-1,

(63)

where

\Delta_{n}:=\left(a_{n}^{(\mathrm{A})}\right)^{2}-\left(a_{n}^{(\mathrm{B})}\right)^{2}.

(64)

for two different initial conditions A and B.
See Appendix
C
for the derivation of Eq. (
63
).
This quadratic form is valid only when
P(r,t)
is sufficiently close to equilibrium; in particular, it does not reproduce the exact value at
t=0
when the initial condition is another equilibrium distribution.
Therefore, the condition Eq. (
63
) should be regarded as a necessary (but not always sufficient) condition for crossing within the validity of the truncated expansion.

a_{2}
against
\beta_{\mathrm{ini}}

V
Discussion

We now discuss our results, focusing on the difference between 1D and 2D, and thermomajorization.

V.1
Difference between 1D and 2D

Remarkably, the Mpemba effect can be observed even in an unbounded system in a two-dimensional situation, while we need to introduce a wall to confine the particle in a one-dimensional asymmetric potential
Liu26
;
Liu26long
.
The difference may be from the forbidden region for
r<0
in 2D cases, i.e., there is an effective wall at
r=0
in the 2D case.
More explicitly, the equilibrium condition for 2D system is expressed as

\displaystyle P_{\mathrm{eq}}(r)\propto re^{-\beta V(r)}.

(72)

Thus, the effective potential mapping onto one-dimensional systems can be written as

\displaystyle V_{\mathrm{eff}}(r)=V(r)-T\ln r.

(73)

This effective potential prohibits the location of a test particle for
r<0
, which can be regarded as an effective wall at
r=0
.

The necessary condition to observe the Mpemba effect is whether

\displaystyle F_{2}(\beta_{\mathrm{ini}}):=\frac{\partial a_{2}}{\partial\beta_{\mathrm{ini}}}=-2\pi\int_{0}^{\infty}drre^{\beta V(r)/2}\phi_{2}(r)[V(r)-\langle V\rangle_{\beta_{\mathrm{ini}}}]P_{\mathrm{ini}}(r)

(74)

has, at least, one zero for
\beta_{\mathrm{ini}}=\beta_{\mathrm{ini}}^{*}
.
We demonstrate that
F_{2}(\beta_{\mathrm{ini}})
has a different sign in the low-temperature and high-temperature limits for the case (i)
V(\alpha)<V(0)=0
, as shown in Appendix
E.1
.
Thus, we conclude the existence of
F_{2}(\beta_{\mathrm{ini}}^{*})=0
at a
\beta_{\mathrm{ini}}=\beta_{\mathrm{ini}}^{*}
.
Mathematically, the difference in the Jacobian between 2D and 1D leads to different conclusions, while
r=0
can be regarded as an effective potential in 1D problem.
Interestingly, we can prove the absence of positive
\beta_{\mathrm{ini}}^{*}
to satisfy
F_{2}(\beta_{\mathrm{ini}}^{*})
for the case (iii)
V(\alpha)>0
(see Appendix
E.2
).
When we regard
r=0
as an effective potential in 1D system, this result is analogous to that in 1D case with a wall on one side
Liu26
;
Liu26long
.

V.2
Thermomajorization

Vu and Hayakawa
VVu2025
proposed the thermomajorization for a general condition to observe the Mpemba effect in arbitrary monotone measures.
This concept is useful as a general criterion to observe the Mpemba effect, but we may not need the thermomajorization once we know the expansion coefficients
a_{2}
,
a_{3}
, and the others explicitly.
Indeed, the argument in Sec.
IV.3
can be generalized to an arbitrary observable
\hat{A}
by using Eq. (
34
), where the existence of a maximum or a minimum in
a_{2}
is still the necessary condition to observe the Mpemba effect for an arbitrary observable.
If we know
a_{2}
and
a_{3}
explicitly, one can obtain the crossing condition corresponding to Eq. (
63
) for an arbitrary observable
\hat{A}
.
Thus, we do not have to worry about the possibility that some monotone measure may not cross during the relaxation, while the KL divergence exhibits the crossing.

VI
Conclusion

By introducing an exactly solvable model, we have obtained exact eigenmodes of the Fokker-Planck equation in a bistable potential.
From spectral analysis, we identify the necessary and sufficient conditions for observing the Mpemba effect.
Using the slowest and the second slowest eigenmodes of the particle’s motion in a two-dimensional potential, starting from an equilibrium initial condition after a quench into another equilibrium state, we demonstrate the occurrence of a crossing of the KL-divergence, if
V(\alpha)<V(r_{\mathrm{min}})=0
where
r_{\mathrm{min}}
is the location of the minimum of
V(r)
near
r=0
.
In contrast to the 1D case, we can observe the Mpemba effect for a particle constrained in a 2D double potential without the introduction of any confined walls because
r=0
can be regarded as an effective wall.
Interestingly,
a_{2}
has a peak, but neither the Mpemba effect nor the inverse Mpemba effect is observed for
V(\alpha)=0
.
For
V(\alpha)>0
, there is no peak for
a_{2}
and the Mpemba effect cannot be observed.

Appendix A
Properties of Tricomi’s
U
-function

The Tricomi (or Tricomi-Confluent) function
U(a,b,z)
is the second, linearly independent solution of Kummer’s (confluent-hypergeometric) differential equation

zy^{\prime\prime}(z)+(b-z)y^{\prime}(z)-ay(z)=0.

(75)

The other standard solution is Kummer’s
M
-function (also denoted
{}_{1}F_{1}
)
Abramowitz
:

M(a,b,z):={}_{1}F_{1}(a;b;z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(a)_{k}}{(b)_{k}}\frac{z^{k}}{k!},

(76)

where
(q)_{k}:=\Gamma(q+k)/\Gamma(q)
with the Gamma function
\Gamma(x):=\int_{0}^{\infty}dtt^{x-1}e^{-t}
is the Pochhammer symbol.

A convenient and frequently used representation of
U(a,b,z)
is the integral formula (valid for
\Re a>0,\ \Re z>0
):

U(a,b,z):=\frac{1}{\Gamma(a)}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-zt}t^{a-1}(1+t)^{b-a-1}dt.

(77)

By analytic continuation, this defines
U(a,b,z)
for general complex parameters and
z
(with the usual branch cuts).

The connection formula expressing
U
in terms of
M
is

U(a,b,z)=\frac{\Gamma(1-b)}{\Gamma(a-b+1)}M(a,b,z)+\frac{\Gamma(b-1)}{\Gamma(a)}z^{1-b}M(a-b+1,2-b,z),

(78)

which is valid by analytic continuation for non-integer
b
and extends to integer
b
by limit.
Useful identities and asymptotic form are presented as

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dz}M(a,b,z)=\frac{a}{b}M(a+1,b+1,z),

(79)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dz}U(a,b,z)=-a\,U(a+1,b+1,z),

(80)

\displaystyle U(a,b,z)\sim z^{-a}\Big(1+O(1/z)\Big)\qquad(\lvert z\rvert\to\infty,\ \Re z>0).

(81)

In our application (with the notation of the main text), one sets

\displaystyle a=\mu,\qquad b=1+\nu,\qquad z=\gamma r^{2},

(82)

so that
U(\mu;1+\nu;\gamma r^{2})
denotes Tricomi’s function with those parameters.
The identity (
80
) is particularly useful when computing derivatives of the radial solution

\displaystyle\phi(r)=r^{\nu}e^{-\tfrac{\gamma r^{2}}{2}}U(\mu,1+\nu,\gamma r^{2})

(83)

appearing in the matching conditions.

Appendix C
Quantitative Criterion for Crossing of the KL Divergence

In this appendix, we derive a simple quantitative criterion for the
crossing of the KL divergence between two different
initial temperatures.

Consider two initial states
A
and
B
.
Introducing

\Delta D(t):=D_{A}(t)-D_{B}(t),

(89)

with the aid of Eq. (
60

\Delta D(t)=\frac{C^{*}}{2}\sum_{n\geq 1}\Delta_{n}e^{-2\lambda_{n}t}.

(90)

A crossing occurs if there exists a time
t^{\ast}>0
such that

\Delta D(t^{\ast})=0.

(91)

In the minimal two-mode approximation (
n=2,3
), this gives

\Delta_{2}e^{-2\tilde{\lambda}_{2}t^{\ast}}+\Delta_{3}e^{-2\tilde{\lambda}_{3}t^{\ast}}=0.

(92)

Solving for

t^{\ast}=\frac{1}{2(\tilde{\lambda}_{3}-\tilde{\lambda}_{2})}\ln\left(\frac{-\Delta_{3}}{\Delta_{2}}\right).

(93)

A real positive solution exists if and only if Eq. (
63
) is satisfied.

Equation (
63
) shows that a non-monotonic dependence of

on the initial temperature is necessary but not sufficient for the Mpemba effect defined via KL crossing.
The amplitude contrast must be strong enough to reverse the ordering relative to faster modes.

) we have

F_{2}(\beta_{\rm ini})=-\frac{2\pi}{Z(\beta_{\rm ini})}\int_{0}^{\infty}drr\phi_{2}(r)e^{-\beta_{\rm ini}V(r)}\left[V(r)-\langle V\rangle_{\beta_{\rm ini}}\right],

(130)

where

\langle V\rangle_{\beta_{\rm ini}}=\frac{2\pi}{Z(\beta_{\rm ini})}\int_{0}^{\infty}drrV(r)e^{-\beta_{\rm ini}V(r)}.

(131)

Introducing the probability measure

d\mu_{\beta_{\rm ini}}(r)=\frac{2\pi}{Z(\beta_{\rm ini})}re^{-\beta_{\rm ini}V(r)}dr,

(132)

Eq. (
130
) can be rewritten as

F_{2}(\beta_{\rm ini})=-\mathrm{Cov}_{\beta_{\rm ini}}\left(\phi_{2}(r),V(r)\right),

(133)

where the covariance
\mathrm{Cov}_{\beta_{\rm ini}}\left(\phi_{2}(r),V(r)\right):=\phi_{2}(r)\left[V(r)-\langle V\rangle_{\beta_{\rm ini}}\right]
is taken with respect to
d\mu_{\beta_{\rm ini}}
.

Therefore, the sign of
F_{2}
is opposite to the covariance between
\phi_{2}(r)
and
V(r)
under the Gibbs measure.

For
V(\alpha)>0
, the energetic ordering of the two wells is

V(0)<V(\alpha).

(134)

The first nontrivial radial eigenfunction
\phi_{2}(r)
has a single node and opposite signs in the two wells.
Up to an overall normalization, we choose

\phi_{2}(r)\begin{cases}<0,&r\text{ in the inner well near }0,\\
>0,&r\text{ in the outer well near }\alpha.\end{cases}

(135)

Hence, the sign of
\phi_{2}
increases when moving from the lower-energy well to the higher-energy well.
In particular, for any
r_{1},r_{2}
belonging respectively to the inner and outer wells,

V(r_{1})<V(r_{2})\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\phi_{2}(r_{1})<\phi_{2}(r_{2}).

(136)

Thus
\phi_{2}(r)
is an increasing function of
V(r)
in the sense of well-to-well ordering.

Let us decompose the integration domain into the inner-well region
\Omega_{0}
and outer-well region
\Omega_{\alpha}
.
Because of Eq. (
136
),
\phi_{2}(r)
and
V(r)
are positively ordered: larger values of
V
are associated with larger values of
\phi_{2}
.

By Chebyshev’s integral inequality for similarly ordered functions, for any probability measure (see Appendix
E
)

\mathrm{Cov}_{\beta_{\rm ini}}\left(\phi_{2}(r),V(r)\right)\geq 0.

(137)

The inequality is strict because
\phi_{2}
is not a constant function and the two wells have different energies.
Therefore,

\mathrm{Cov}_{\beta_{\rm ini}}\left(\phi_{2}(r),V(r)\right)>0\quad\text{for all }\beta_{\rm ini}>0.

(138)

Importantly, the Gibbs weight

e^{-\beta_{\rm ini}V(r)}

changes continuously with
\beta_{\rm ini}
,
but does not alter the energetic ordering between the two wells.
Hence, the positive ordering between
\phi_{2}
and
V
is preserved for all temperatures.

Combining Eqs. (
133
) and (
138
),
we obtain

F_{2}(\beta_{\rm ini})<0\quad\text{for all }\beta_{\rm ini}>0.

(139)

Therefore
\hat{a}_{2}(\beta_{\rm ini})
is strictly decreasing and has no extremum.

Since an extremum of
\hat{a}_{2}
is a necessary condition for the crossing of the KL divergence,
the Mpemba effect cannot occur in the regime
V(\alpha)>0
.

Chebyshev’s integral inequality for similarly ordered functions

In this subsection, we state and prove Chebyshev’s integral inequality
in a form appropriate for probability measures.
The proof is elementary and relies on a symmetric representation
of the covariance.

Setting.

Let
(X,\mathcal{F},\mu)
be a probability space,
i.e.
\mu(X)=1
.
Let
f,g:X\to\mathbb{R}
be square-integrable functions.

We say that
f
and
g
are
similarly ordered

if for all
x,y\in X
,

(f(x)-f(y))(g(x)-g(y))\geq 0.

(140)

In other words, whenever
f
increases,
g
also increases,
and whenever
f
decreases,
g
also decreases.

Theorem (Chebyshev’s integral inequality).

If
f
and
g
are similarly ordered,
then

\int_{X}f(x)g(x)d\mu(x)\geq\left(\int_{X}f(x)d\mu(x)\right)\left(\int_{X}g(x)d\mu(x)\right).

(141)

Equivalently,

\mathrm{Cov}_{\mu}(f,g)\geq 0.

(142)

If the inequality in (
140
)
is strict on a set of positive
\mu\times\mu
measure,
then the covariance is strictly positive.

Proof.

Recall that the covariance can be written symmetrically as

\mathrm{Cov}_{\mu}(f,g)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{X}\int_{X}(f(x)-f(y))(g(x)-g(y))d\mu(x)d\mu(y).

(143)

To verify (
143
), expand the integrand:

\displaystyle(f(x)-f(y))(g(x)-g(y))

\displaystyle=f(x)g(x)+f(y)g(y)-f(x)g(y)-f(y)g(x).

(144)

Integrating over
X\times X
and using
\mu(X)=1
,
we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{X}\int_{X}f(x)g(x)d\mu(x)d\mu(y)

\displaystyle=\int_{X}f(x)g(x)d\mu(x),

(145)

\displaystyle\int_{X}\int_{X}f(x)g(y)d\mu(x)d\mu(y)

\displaystyle=\left(\int_{X}fd\mu\right)\left(\int_{X}gd\mu\right),

(146)

and similarly for the remaining terms.
Collecting all contributions yields

\int_{X}\int_{X}(f(x)-f(y))(g(x)-g(y))d\mu(x)d\mu(y)=2\mathrm{Cov}_{\mu}(f,g),

(147)

which proves (
143
).

Now assume that
f
and
g
are similarly ordered,
i.e. (
140
) holds for all
x,y
.
Then the integrand in (
143
)
is nonnegative everywhere.
Therefore,

\mathrm{Cov}_{\mu}(f,g)\geq 0.

(148)

If the inequality in (
140
)
is strict on a set of positive
\mu\times\mu
measure,
then the double integral is strictly positive,
and hence

\mathrm{Cov}_{\mu}(f,g)>0.

(149)

This completes the proof.

\square
