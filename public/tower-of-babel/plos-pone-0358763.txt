CfDNA methylation profiling in blood following moderate-to-severe traumatic brain injury using brain cell type–enriched signatures: A pilot study

Shih-Han Kao, Larry N Singh, Jonathan Starr, Firas H Kobeissy, Claudia Robertson, Spyridoula Tsetsou, John B Williamson, Damon G Lamb

ABSTRACT
The human brain consists of diverse cell types that collaboratively perform complex functions and form intricate neuronal networks. When traumatic brain injury (TBI) occurs, multiple molecular pathways involving diverse cell types are activated in response to injury. Understanding how brain cell composition is altered during TBI may help improve patient outcomes. Upon brain trauma, neurovascular disruption leads to the release of brain-derived cell-free DNA (cfDNA) into peripheral blood. The tissue-specific DNA methylation patterns in this cfDNA have the potential to serve as candidate markers for studying brain injury. To characterize brain cell type–enriched methylation features, we analyzed the methylomes of five major brain cell types—neurons, astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, microglia, and vascular endothelial cells (VECs)—using whole-genome bisulfite sequencing (WGBS). Cellular enrichment analysis showed that differentially methylated genes (DMGs) exhibited reference-defined cell type–enriched patterns. Gene Ontology pathway analysis further supported the biological plausibility of these methylation features. To demonstrate the feasibility of applying cfDNA methylation profiling in TBI, we analyzed a limited pilot dataset comprising healthy controls and samples collected at acute (1 day), subacute (1 month), and chronic (6 months) post-injury time points from patients with moderate-to-severe TBI (msTBI). These analyses identified candidate brain cell type–enriched cfDNA methylation features that differed across post-injury sampling groups. Together, our findings highlight that brain cell type-enriched methylation signatures may provide a powerful tool for characterizing distinct cell populations and nominating candidate brain cell-associated cfDNA methylation biomarkers in TBI.

Introduction DNA methylation provides a powerful approach for inferring the cellular composition of tissues during injury. It is a common form of epigenetic modification that predominantly occurs at the cytosine residues preceding guanine (CpG) nucleotides and plays key roles in gene regulation, development and genomic stability [ 1 ]. Importantly, DNA methylation also serves as a signature of cell identity as each cell type exhibits distinct methylation patterns [ 2 ]. Building on this principle, a variety of computational approaches have been developed to identify differentially methylated regions (DMRs) across biological conditions, such as health versus injury or pre- and post-treatment states [ 3 ]. In general, these methods quantify methylation at individual CpG sites, aggregate signals across genomic regions, and then apply statistical models to detect stretches of CpGs that differ significantly between groups [ 4 – 8 ]. Once identified, these DMRs can be leveraged for biomarker discovery by linking methylation patterns to disease states. For example, in oncology, tumor-specific DMRs have been used to detect cancer types and estimate tumor burden [ 9 , 10 ], demonstrating the clinical utility and real-world impact of methylation-based biomarkers. Recently, several computational tools aimed to estimate the cell type composition of bulk DNA samples based on methylation signatures, have been developed for methylation deconvolution [ 2 , 11 – 15 ]. Linear regression, constrained optimization, or Bayesian inference, can be used to resolve bulk methylation signals into estimated proportions of each cell type. One such method, Unmethylated, Mixed, Methylated (UXM) is a reference-based, fragment-level deconvolution algorithm for DNA methylation sequencing data [ 2 ]. It estimates the relative proportions of different cell types in a mixed sample by calculating the percentage of unmethylated fragments per genomic marker using non-negative least squares regression (NNLS) model. The output can be used for tracking cell type-specific injury, inferring cell-free DNA (cfDNA) origins, or characterizing cellular heterogeneity in tissues. Despite these advances, applying such approaches to traumatic brain injury (TBI) remains challenging due to the brain’s cellular heterogeneity. The human brain comprises diverse cell types organized into multi-layered structures [ 16 ], whose interactions regulate functions ranging from basic physiological processes to higher-order cognition [ 17 ]. Following TBI, the disruption of tightly coordinated cellular processes triggers a cascade of molecular events across diverse brain cell types, such as neuronal injury signaling, microglial activation, and astrocytic stress responses, complicating both prognosis and treatment [ 18 , 19 ]. As such, unveiling brain cell type-specific contributions is pivotal to improving outcomes for patients with TBI. Here, we report a pilot study using both a DMR tool and deconvolution algorithm to characterize five brain cell types: neurons, astrocytes, microglia, oligodendrocytes, and vascular endothelial cells (VECs). We also included a cfDNA dataset from 16 individuals with moderate-severe TBI (msTBI) to visualize the methylation changes during acute, subacute, and chronic phases of msTBI. We applied the Azimuth Cell Type database ( https://azimuth.hubmapconsortium.org/ ) and GO enrichment analysis to validate the cell type DMRs. We combined these tools to assess methylation changes in human msTBI cfDNA in order to identify potential brain cell type biomarkers. To our knowledge, this is the first study to systematically interrogate the brain cell-type DNA methylome in the context of human TBI compared with control blood samples. Materials and methods Patients TBI severity was defined according to the U.S. Department of Defense (DoD) criteria and guidelines [ 20 ]. The study cohort included uninjured controls (N = 5) and individuals with msTBI enrolled at acute (1-day post-injury; N = 4), subacute (1-month post-injury; N = 12), and chronic (6-month post-injury; N = 4) time points. All clinical studies were approved by the relevant local ethics committees as follows: the Ethics Committee at University of Pittsburgh (UPitt) (IRB ID: STUDY22100119; date of recruitment: 06/08/2023− 09/03/2025) and Baylor College of Medicine (BCM) Institutional Review Board (protocol H-48245) and Harris Health system (protocol ID 22-12-2966; date of recruitment: 11/01/2022-10/31/2027). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legally authorized representatives prior to enrollment. Additional patient information is provided in S1 File . (Minneapolis, MN) and from iXCells Biotechnologies (San Diego, CA), respectively. Human glial cell line SVG p12 cells (CRL-8621) and peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs, PCS-800–011) were obtained from ATCC (Manassas, VA), and whole blood cells were provided by the Coriell Institute for Medical Research (Camden, NJ). VECs (HEC02) were obtained from Neuromics (Minneapolis, MN), Cell Systems (ACBRI 376, Kirkland, WA), ScienCell (1009, Carlsbad, CA), iXCells (10HU-051, San Diego, CA), and AcceGen (ABC-TC3524, Fairfield, NJ). Primary microglia cells (1909) were purchased from ScienCell (Carlsbad, CA). The HOG oligodendrocyte cell line (SCC163) was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO), and human SH-SY5Y neuroblastoma cells were generously provided by Dr. Kevin Wang (originally from ATCC). All cell lines were authenticated by STR profiling and tested negative for mycoplasma contamination prior to use. One control cfDNA sample was obtained from Biochain (Newark, CA). Raw human neuron and oligodendrocyte primary cell data were downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) GSE107729. Genomic DNA (gDNA) was extracted using DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kits (Qiagen, Germantown, MD) and was subsequently subjected to bisulfite conversion and WGBS (see section below). Blood collection & cfDNA extraction Blood was drawn at UPitt and BCM into the cf-DNA/cf-RNA Preservative Tubes (Norgen Biotek, Ontario, Canada). Plasma was separated by centrifugation at 1,600 x g at each clinical site and stored at −80 °C. The samples were shipped on dry ice to the analytical site (Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia) and stored at −80 °C until use. Prior to extraction, samples were thawed on ice and then centrifuged at 16,000 x g. The cfDNA was extracted using the Chemagic 5k cfDNA kit (Revvity Health Sciences, Hopkinton, MA) and quantified using the Qubit 1x dsDNA high sensitivity assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) on a Qubit 4 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The cfDNA fragment sizes were assessed with the cell-free DNA ScreenTape assay on the Agilent 4200 TapeStation system (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA). Bisulfite conversion, sequencing, and data processing All DNA samples were subjected to bisulfite conversion using the Zymo Bisulfite conversion kit (Zymo Research, Irvine, CA). WGBS libraries were prepared from the bisulfite-converted samples using the xGen Methylation-Sequencing DNA Library Preparation Kit (Integrated DNA Technologies, Coralville, IA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Sequencing was performed on the NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA) to generate 2 × 150 bp paired-end reads at approximately 30 × coverage. Raw reads were demultiplexed, quality assessed using FastQC (v0.12.1), trimmed with Trim Galore (v0.6.10), and aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38/hg38) using Bismark (v0.24.2) [ 21 ]. Bismark was also used for PCR deduplication, post-alignment quality control, and methylation extraction to obtain CpG methylation calls for each sample. Differential methylation region (DMR) analysis To identify the cell type of origin of cfDNA, bisulfite-treated reads were mapped and methylation calls were generated from the sequencing data using Bismark [ 21 ]. Bismark coverage files were imported into SeqMonk (RRID:SCR_001913; https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/seqmonk/ ) using the “Import Data” and “Bismark (Cov)” options, where the methylation quantitation pipeline enables analysis across custom-defined regions [ 8 , 22 , 23 ]. For CpG coverage analysis, probes were generated using the “Read position Probe Generator.” CpGs with a minimum of 1 read were included in the 5x minimum threshold analysis. Methylation values for each probe were quantified using the “Bisulphite methylation over features” pipeline. To create the brain cell type methylation atlas, brain cell type-enriched DMRs were selected based on the following criteria: Significant differences between each brain cell type and blood were assessed using Chi-square tests with false discovery rate (FDR) correction, applying a significance threshold of FDR < 1 × 10 −10 . A DMR was defined as cell type-enriched if it showed a methylation level difference of >25% in the respective cell type compared to others, while the same region exhibited methylation levels within either the unmethylated (0–20%) or methylated (80–100%) range in the other four brain cell types and in blood. Cell type-enriched DMRs were exclusive to their respective brain cell types and were not identified as enriched in the other four cells and blood ( e.g. , neuron-enriched DMRs were not enriched in astrocytes, microglia, oligodendrocytes, VECs, or blood). The cfDNA methylation profiles were compared with the brain cell type methylation atlas described above to infer the contribution of brain-derived signals in acute to chronic msTBI cfDNA samples. A cell type-enriched TBI DMR was defined as a region exhibiting the characteristic methylated or unmethylated pattern of the corresponding cell type, while the same region in control cfDNA displayed the opposite methylation state. Differentially methylated genes (DMGs) were defined as genes whose entire gene bodies were precisely overlapped by the start and end coordinates of a DMR. The brain cell type double-dendrogram heatmap was constructed using methylation values. Only features meeting a false discovery rate (FDR) threshold of < 1 × 10 −10 were included in the visualization. Unsupervised hierarchical clustering was performed using Euclidean distance and complete linkage. Cell type-enriched DMG heatmaps were used to visualize methylation patterns across cell types. Methylation values were z-score normalized across samples prior to visualization. Cell type and functional enrichment analysis We analyzed cell type enrichment of DMGs using Azimuth, a pre-annotated single-cell RNA-seq reference atlas for human tissues, and performed functional enrichment analysis using Gene Ontology in ShinyGO (v0.80) [ 24 ]. Homo sapiens (GRCh38.p13) was selected as the genome assembly. Azimuth cell type terms and Gene Ontology terms from Biological Processes (BP) and Cellular Components (CC) categories with an FDR < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched. Enrichment of DMGs was ranked primarily by fold enrichment, followed by FDR and the number of genes. A reference atlas was rebuilt using WGBS data from the brain cell types listed in Table 1 while retaining the leukocyte cell data included in UXM. The cfDNA methylome data were then imported. The DMR analysis in human brain cell types In this pilot study, we established a human brain cell type methylation atlas using DNA methylomic data from neurons, astrocytes, microglia, oligodendrocytes, and VECs ( Table 1 ). The reference dataset included primary cells, iPSC-derived cells, and immortalized cell lines. Since blood cells are the major contributors to cfDNA in peripheral blood [ 25 – 27 ], WGBS data from blood were included as background. As a small test set, we analyzed cfDNA samples collected at 1 day (N = 4), 1 month (N = 12), and 6 months (N = 4) post-msTBI ( Table 2 ). Control cfDNA was obtained from healthy subjects. We found that the proportion of reads overlapping brain-enriched DMRs was comparable between the TBI (median: 0.248%; IQR: 0.233%–0.255%) and control groups (median: 0.250%; IQR: 0.238%–0.254%), indicating that the overall representation of reads at these loci did not differ substantially between groups ( S2 File ). However, read overlap alone does not establish that a fragment originated from the brain because it does not account for the fragment’s methylation pattern. Therefore, we further evaluated methylation levels within these brain-enriched DMRs to identify fragments exhibiting brain-enriched methylation signatures and assess injury-associated differences between TBI and control samples. To identify genomic regions with significant methylation differences between cell types, we performed probe-level methylation analysis by quantifying methylation at each region and applying chi-square tests with multiple-testing correction (Methods; Fig 1A ). DMRs were subsequently annotated to their associated genes to derive DMGs, which were further classified as cell type–enriched methylated or unmethylated DMGs. 10.1371/journal.pone.0358763.g001 Fig 1 DMR analysis of five human brain cell types. We performed two-dimensional hierarchical clustering analysis to examine how methylation signatures shape relationships among brain cell types. The analysis was restricted to DMRs that were statistically significant when each brain cell type was compared with blood. The analysis revealed that DMRs from oligodendrocytes and microglia clustered closely ( Fig 1B ), consistent with their shared glial identity and roles in neuronal support and synaptic plasticity [ 28 , 29 ]. These shared methylation features were followed by neurons, which clustered more distantly from the glial cells. DMRs from astrocytes and VECs formed a separate cluster, consistent with their roles in vascularization, metabolism, and blood-brain barrier (BBB) formation [ 30 – 32 ]. Next, we applied additional criteria to refine the cell type–enriched DMRs, retaining only those exclusive to their respective brain cell types (Methods). A Venn diagram illustrates the number of cell type–enriched DMRs identified ( Fig 1C ). Most of the cell type-enriched DMRs identified were unmethylated across brain cell types (neurons: 94.2%, astrocytes: 71.7%, microglia: 53.8%, oligodendrocytes: 74.3%, VECs: 95.2%) ( Fig 1D ). These data suggest that unmethylated regions may serve as robust markers for distinguishing cell identities within the brain. Cell type and pathway analysis of cell type-enriched DMGs Neuron-enriched DMGs. To independently verify the cell type enrichment of DMRs, we first annotated each DMR to the human genome (GRCh38/hg38) to derive differentially methylated genes (DMGs). Then, we used Azimuth to determine whether these DMGs aligned with well-curated cell type annotations. Neuron-enriched DMGs mapped predominantly to glutamatergic neurons in the Azimuth database ( Fig 2A ). To further explore the roles of neuron-enriched DMGs, we performed GO enrichment analyses of biological processes and cellular components. This analysis revealed enrichment of pathways related to nervous system development ( Fig 2B ) and multiple neuronal components, including synaptic pathways, dendrite formation, and neuron projection ( Fig 2C ). To determine which VEC-enriched DMGs contribute to TBI cfDNA, we profiled the top 20 genes and found that seven were uniquely methylated and 13 were unmethylated in VECs relative to other brain cell types ( Fig 6D ). As an illustration, Fig 6E shows differential methylation counts for the top-ranked gene, KANK3 (KN Motif And Ankyrin Repeat Domains 3), at the fourth exon. Methylated VEC-enriched DMGs in TBI cfDNA appeared to exhibit higher methylation levels during the acute (1 day) and subacute (1 month) phases relative to controls ( Fig 6F , left). In contrast, unmethylated VEC-enriched DMGs showed a trend toward reduced methylation, with the largest apparent decrease during the chronic phase (6 months), followed by the acute phase, compared to controls ( Fig 6F , right). These findings demonstrate the temporal changes in VEC-enriched methylation signatures in TBI cfDNA. Together, these findings support our DMR analysis and demonstrate the utility of this protocol, which will be further applied and extended in our ongoing study. Cell type deconvolution analysis in TBI cfDNA To quantify overall brain cell type proportions in blood, we also applied the UXM deconvolution algorithm to investigate changes in brain-derived components following msTBI. This analysis included methylome profiles of five brain cell types—neurons, astrocytes, microglia, oligodendrocytes, and VECs—along with blood as a reference. Compared with controls, the overall fraction of brain-derived components was higher at 1-day post-TBI and gradually became lower 1 month and 6 months post-injury ( Fig 7 ), enabling estimation of brain-derived cfDNA contributions following msTBI. Together, these findings reveal the time-associated dynamics of brain-derived cfDNA following TBI, suggesting that cfDNA methylation profiles capture biologically meaningful variation across post-injury phases. Discussion We present a pilot methylomic framework that combines DMR-based analysis with cfDNA deconvolution to characterize candidate brain cell type-enriched cfDNA methylation features across post-injury sampling windows of msTBI. In this framework, DMR analysis was evaluated using independent reference datasets, supporting both the cell type enrichment and functional relevance of cell type–enriched DMGs. Across post-injury sampling windows, several DMGs showed methylation differences in TBI cfDNA that were consistent with reference-defined brain cell type-enriched methylation patterns. Complementing this approach, deconvolution analysis provides a broader view of brain-derived components in blood, supporting the feasibility of using cfDNA methylation profiles to nominate candidate blood-based markers of brain injury for future validation. While corroborating cell-type enrichment using Azimuth, we observed that the enriched cell types for astrocyte- and microglia-enriched DMGs did not align as closely with the designated categories as those for other cell types. This finding is expected given that Azimuth relies on RNA-seq–derived markers [ 41 ], whereas differentially methylated regions (DMRs) do not necessarily correlate one-to-one with transcript abundance. In particular, several exemplar DMGs were located in intronic or exonic regions rather than at transcription start sites (TSSs), where the relationship between methylation and gene expression is less direct [ 42 – 44 ]. We attempted to restrict analyses to DMRs overlapping TSSs; however, the resulting set was too small to support robust cell type enrichment testing. We found that the majority of cell lineage—enriched DMGs in this small msTBI dataset were hypomethylated. Although DNA methylation patterns at individual loci can vary, prior experimental TBI studies have reported global cellular hypomethylation, particularly in microglia and macrophages [ 45 ]. These findings raise the possibility that brain injury may drive broad remodeling of the methylation—demethylation machinery, potentially involving altered expression, enzymatic activity, or subcellular localization of DNA methylation regulators [ 46 – 48 ]. Cell type–enriched DMGs identified in TBI cfDNA may represent candidate biomarkers for future validation and help guide future studies of brain injury biology. While some genes have well-established roles in particular cell types, others are less clearly defined. For instance, we identified MBP among oligodendrocyte-enriched DMGs, consistent with its well-known role as a major component of the myelin sheath in oligodendrocytes and Schwann cells [ 37 ]. In contrast, PLXNB1 , which is primarily implicated in GABAergic synapse development [ 49 ], was also identified as an oligodendrocyte-enriched DMG despite being less commonly associated with this lineage. As reference cell type atlases continue to expand, additional unexpected targets may emerge and help refine our understanding of brain cell type-enriched methylation features in TBI. Our study should be interpreted in the context of several limitations. First, the microglia methylome reference included a limited sample size, which reduces confidence in microglia-enriched interpretations relative to other cell types. In addition, brain injury may alter cellular states, and methylation features may overlap across related glial, immune, and vascular populations. Therefore, the identified features are best interpreted as reference-defined, cell-enriched candidate methylation signatures rather than definitive cell-type-specific markers. Finally, although samples were grouped by post-injury time point, the current dataset is primarily cross-sectional, with only a subset of paired samples. Thus, observations across the 1-day, 1-month, and 6-month groups should be viewed as time point-associated patterns and candidate biomarkers that require confirmation in larger longitudinal cohorts. In sum, these exploratory findings demonstrate the methodology and potential utility of both DMR analysis and deconvolution approaches for interrogating methylomic data. With continued cohort expansion, these complementary tools may help prioritize candidate cfDNA biomarkers and guide future studies of brain cell-associated methylation patterns after TBI.
