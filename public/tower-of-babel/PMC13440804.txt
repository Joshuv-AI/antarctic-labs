Intraventricular rupture of brain abscess: A two-center observational cohort study
Authors: Dimitri T. K. Ndandja, Gennady E. Chmutin, Gerald Musa, Alexander V. Kim, Ignatius N. Esene, Vladimir Nikolenko, Renat Nurmukhametov, Carlos Castillo Rangel, Gervith Reyes Soto, Nicola Montemurro, Manuel De Jesus Encarnacion Ramírez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: Intraventricular rupture of brain abscess (IVROBA) remains one of the most devastating complications of bacterial brain abscess, historically associated with high mortality rates. Early identification of measurable predictors of rupture is essential for timely intervention and improved prognosis.Methods: We analyzed a combined retrospective–prospective cohort of 104 patients treated for brain abscesses at two tertiary Moscow hospitals (2018–2023). Demographic, clinical, and radiological variables were assessed. Microbiological profiles, management strategies, and outcomes were evaluated. Logistic regression and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses determined predictors of IVROBA and unfavorable outcomes.Results: IVROBA occurred in 28 patients (26.9%), representing the strongest independent predictor of mortality (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] = 14.2, p < 0.001) and poor outcome (Glasgow outcome scale 1–4). Multivariate analysis confirmed minimal distance between the abscess capsule and the ventricular wall as the predominant risk factor. Abscesses within 0–2 mm, 2–5 mm, and 5–10 mm of the ventricles had progressively higher adjusted odds of rupture (aOR = 87.2, 48.6, and 8.1, respectively; all p < 0.05) compared with those >10 mm away. Immunodeficiency was an additional independent risk factor (aOR = 9.7, p = 0.049). ROC analysis identified ≤5 mm as the optimal cutoff (area under the curve = 0.872; sensitivity 84.6%, specificity 81.2%).Conclusion: Proximity to the ventricular system is the principal mechanical determinant of intraventricular rupture, surpassing other clinical and radiologic variables. Abscesses within 5 mm of the ventricle should be managed aggressively, favoring early surgical drainage to prevent fatal rupture and ventriculitis.

INTRODUCTION

Brain abscess remains a serious neurosurgical emergency despite advances in diagnostic imaging, antimicrobial therapy, and minimally invasive surgical techniques. While overall prognosis has improved, intraventricular rupture of brain abscess (IVROBA) dramatically worsens outcomes, converting a localized infection into diffuse ventriculitis and meningoencephalitis, often precipitating rapid neurological deterioration and systemic sepsis. Reported mortality rates remain exceptionally high, ranging from 84% to 100%, even with aggressive management.[2,7,18] The pathophysiology of IVROBA is multifactorial. Rupture typically occurs when the abscess capsule, thinner and poorly vascularized on the ventricular side, succumbs to intracavitary pressure or inflammatory necrosis, allowing purulent material to breach the ventricular cavity.[1,12,19] This leads to widespread bacterial dissemination, hydrocephalus, and diffuse cerebral edema. Clinically, patients may deteriorate precipitously, progressing from a relatively stable neurological state to coma within hours. Early signs of impending rupture may include sudden worsening headache, altered mental status, new-onset meningismus, or acute ventricular enlargement on imaging. However, these warning signs are often subtle or absent, making early identification particularly challenging in routine practice. Neuroimaging plays a critical role in diagnosis. Hallmark radiological findings include intraventricular debris, ependymal enhancement, and ventricular septations on computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI).[3,6,15] Despite increasing recognition of clinical and imaging features, predictors of rupture remain incompletely defined. Previous studies suggest contributors, including multiloculated morphology, hematogenous etiology, deep location, and immunocompromised state.[17,19] However, recent literature emphasizes the spatial relationship between the abscess and ventricular wall as a decisive anatomical determinant, yet few studies have quantified this proximity or proposed actionable thresholds for clinical risk stratification.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and setting

We conducted a combined retrospective–prospective cohort study to investigate predictors and outcomes of IVROBA. Patients treated between January 2018 and December 2023 at two tertiary neurosurgical centers in Moscow (City Clinical Hospital No. 68 and Moscow City Children’s Hospital) were included. Our cohort consists of consecutive, confirmed pyogenic brain abscess cases meeting predefined criteria with complete imaging datasets sufficient for distance quantification and with adequate follow-up. The study was performed across two tertiary neurosurgical centers, including a large pediatric referral hospital in the Russian Federation, which explains the pediatric enrichment. This is a two-center observational cohort study; analyses estimate associations and do not support causal inference. Both institutions serve as referral centers for complex intracranial infections and adhere to uniform diagnostic and management protocols. The study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and received ethical approval from the Local Ethics Committee of the Voronovskoye Moscow Medical Clinical Center, Moscow, Russia. Written informed consent was obtained from all adult participants or from legal guardians in pediatric or incapacitated patients.

Patient selection and diagnostic criteria

A total of 104 consecutive patients with radiologically and microbiologically confirmed pyogenic brain abscesses were included. Cases were identified via institutional neurosurgical databases and prospective admissions logs. Inclusion criteria are (1) radiological confirmation of brain abscess through contrast-enhanced CT or MRI, characterized by a ring-enhancing lesion with central necrosis and surrounding edema; (2) availability of complete clinical, imaging, and microbiological data; (3) minimum follow-up of 6 months post-treatment. Exclusion criteria are (1) parasitic, fungal, or tuberculous abscesses; (2) postoperative empyema; (3) incomplete imaging data.

Diagnosis of intraventricular rupture was established based on radiologic evidence of continuity between the abscess cavity and ventricular system, intraventricular debris, or ependymal enhancement on contrast-enhanced CT or MRI.

Data collection

Clinical and imaging records were systematically reviewed using a standardized data collection form. Variables included: (1) demographics and comorbidities; (2) clinical presentation; (3) abscess location and volume; (4) presumed source of infection; (5) microbiological findings; (6) treatment modality.

The level of consciousness at presentation was recorded using the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS). Outcomes were evaluated using the extended Glasgow outcome scale (GOSE) at discharge, 6 months, and 12 months. GOSE scores of 1–4 were classified as unfavorable, and 5–8 as favorable.

Imaging protocol and measurement of ventricular proximity

All patients underwent contrast-enhanced cranial CT on admission, followed by MRI including T1-, T2-, fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR), and diffusion-weighted sequences when clinically feasible.

Ventricular proximity was quantified using two complementary methods: Manual 2D measurement: Axial post-contrast CT slices were used to measure the shortest perpendicular distance between the abscess margin and the ventricular surface.3D volumetric analysis: Semi-automatic surface reconstruction of the abscess and ventricular system was performed using Brainlab Elements™ (Brainlab AG, Munich, Germany. In addition, the minimal Euclidean distance between surfaces was calculated, allowing submillimeter precision and reducing observer bias.

Abscess volume assessment

Abscess volume was calculated via Brainlab 3D segmentation (voxel-based volumetric reconstruction) and expressed in cubic centimeters (cm3). Manual ellipsoid approximation (length × width × height × 0.52) was performed in a random subset of validation, showing excellent correlation (r = 0.94, p < 0.001).

To account for age-related differences in neuroanatomy, all analyses were performed using an age-stratified approach. Patients were categorized into pediatric (≤17 years) and adult (≥18 years) groups. Baseline characteristics, neuroimaging parameters, incidence of IVROBA, and clinical outcomes were analyzed separately for each age group. In addition to stratified analyses, age group was included as a covariate in multivariable logistic regression models assessing predictors of IVROBA and unfavorable outcome. An interaction term between age group and abscess–ventricle distance was tested to evaluate whether the association between ventricular proximity and rupture risk differed between children and adults. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p < 0.05.

RESULTS

Cohort characteristics

The study cohort comprised 104 patients, including 79 pediatric patients (≤17 years) and 25 adults (≥18 years). Pediatric patients had a mean age of 7.6 ± 4.3 years, whereas adult patients had a mean age of 32.7 ± 11.5 years. Given the marked predominance of pediatric cases, all subsequent analyses were stratified by age group to account for developmental differences in brain anatomy and ventricular dimensions. The cohort consisted of 76 males (73.1%) and 28 females (26.9%), yielding a male-to-female ratio of 2.7:1. The mean age for the entire group was 14.9 ± 11.8 years, showing a bimodal distribution. Pediatric patients (n = 79) ranged from infancy to 17 years (mean 7.6 ± 4.3 years), while adults (n = 25) ranged from 18 to 70 years (mean 32.7 ± 11.5 years).

The incidence of IVROBA differed between age groups but followed a similar distribution pattern in relation to abscess– ventricle proximity. In pediatric patients, IVROBA occurred in 20/79 cases (25.3%), whereas in adults, it occurred in 8/25 cases (32.0%). Clinical presentation

The duration of symptoms before admission ranged from 4 to 180 days (median 14 days, SD ± 36.5). The most frequent presenting symptom was fever (86 patients, 82.7%), followed by vomiting (67.3%) and headache (56.7%) [Table 2]. In infants, clinical evaluation relied on indirect indicators, irritability, poor feeding, bulging fontanelle, and suture separation, rather than subjective reporting of pain. The classical triad of fever, headache, and focal neurological deficit was observed in only 22 patients (21.2%), underscoring the nonspecific nature of early presentations. At admission, 60 patients (57.7%) presented with a GCS of 13–15, 11 (10.6%) with 9–12, and 33 (31.7%) were comatose (GCS ≤8). Additional neurological findings included seizures (12.5%), focal motor deficits (11.5%), and meningeal irritation (33.6%).

Neuroimaging characteristics

Neuroimaging revealed 84 supratentorial (80.8%) and 5 infratentorial abscesses (4.8%), while 15 patients (14.4%) harbored multiple lesions. The frontal lobe was the most common site (26.9%), followed by the parietal (19.2%) and temporal lobes (16.3%). A deep-seated location was identified in 44 cases (42.3%), whereas 60 abscesses (57.7%) were cortical. The mean abscess volume measured by 3D segmentation was 17.2 ± 8.6 cm3 (range 3.5–39 cm3). The minimal abscess–ventricle distance varied from 0 to 25 mm (median 6 mm). ROC analysis identified a threshold of ≤5 mm as the optimal predictor of rupture, yielding an area under the curve = 0.872 (95% CI 0.56–1.02; p < 0.001) with 84.6% sensitivity and 81.2% specificity [Figure 1]. Among patients with abscesses located within ≤5 mm of the ventricular system, early surgical intervention was associated with a significantly lower incidence of intraventricular rupture. Of the 26 high-risk patients, 17 underwent surgical drainage within 72 h of admission, whereas 9 received delayed surgery or initial conservative management. Intraventricular rupture occurred in 2 patients (11.8%) in the early surgery group compared with 4 patients (44.4%) in the delayed or conservatively managed group. This difference reached statistical significance (Fisher’s exact test, p = 0.003), indicating a substantially reduced rupture rate with early intervention in this anatomically high-risk subgroup [Table 3].

Microbiological profile

Positive microbiological cultures were obtained in 81 cases (77.9%). Gram-positive organisms predominated, with Staphylococcus aureus (12.5%) representing the most frequently isolated pathogen. Although majority of the cases were classified as community-acquired, this distribution should be interpreted in the context of infection source and pre-referral management. A large proportion of community-acquired abscesses arose from contiguous rhinogenic or otogenic infections, in which S. aureus is a recognized pathogen, particularly in chronic or recurrent disease. In addition, prior antibiotic exposure before referral was common and likely influenced culture yield by suppressing fastidious organisms. Streptococcus intermedius and methicillin-resistant S. aureus accounted for 7.7% and 5.8% of isolates, respectively. Among Gram-negative pathogens, Klebsiella pneumoniae (4.8%) and Pseudomonas aeruginosa (3.9%) were most frequent. Culture-negative results (23 cases, 22.1%) primarily occurred in patients who had received prior antibiotic therapy [Table 4].

Incidence and characteristics of intraventricular rupture

IVROBA, before arrival at the tertiary referral center, occurred in 28 patients (26.9%), 19 at presentation and 9 during hospitalization. Ruptured cases presented with significantly poorer neurological status: 85.7% (24/28) had GCS ≤8, compared with 11.8% in the non-ruptured group (p < 0.001). The clinical triad of rapid consciousness deterioration, meningeal irritation, and severe headache was nearly universal (93%, 96%, and 89%, respectively).

Characteristic imaging findings included intraventricular debris (100%), ependymal enhancement (100%), ventricular septations (64%), and leptomeningeal enhancement (64%). The rupture site was radiologically identifiable in 16 patients (57%).

Follow-up and outcome assessment

Overall 12-month survival was 81.7%, with 73 patients (70.2%) achieving a favorable functional outcome (GOSE 5–8). No recurrence or delayed abscess formation occurred during follow-up. Late complications included hydrocephalus in seven patients (6.7%), managed with ventriculoperitoneal shunting, and seizure recurrence in five patients (4.8%) requiring long-term antiepileptic therapy. Early surgical evacuation (<72 h) in high-risk abscesses within 5 mm of the ventricles significantly reduced rupture rates (11.8% vs. 44.4%; p = 0.003) compared with delayed or conservative management.

We emphasize that the observed association between early surgical intervention and reduced rupture rates does not imply causality.

Given the observational nature of this study, the analysis is inherently vulnerable to confounding by indication, as patients selected for early surgery may have differed systematically from those managed conservatively or with delayed intervention. Reverse causation is also a major consideration, particularly because the majority of IVROBA cases in our cohort were already ruptured at presentation and therefore could not benefit from early prophylactic surgery. In addition, information bias related to imaging timing and clinical instability may have influenced rupture detection. Accordingly, the findings regarding early surgery should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating associations, highlighting a potential protective signal in anatomically high-risk patients rather than evidence of a causal treatment effect.

DISCUSSION

Despite remarkable advances in neurosurgical techniques, antimicrobial therapy, and neuroimaging, IVROBA remains one of the most catastrophic complications of bacterial brain abscess. Reported incidence rates range from 0.3% to 35%, with mortality historically estimated between 84% and 100%.[2,3,7,18] Once rupture occurs, severe ventriculitis often ensues, rapidly progressing to diffuse panmeningoencephalitis if not promptly recognized and treated, typically accompanied by systemic sepsis.[10,18] The predominance of S. aureus in this largely community-acquired cohort warrants specific consideration. While community-acquired brain abscesses are classically associated with streptococcal species and anaerobes, several factors may account for the observed microbiological pattern. First, a substantial proportion of cases originated from rhinogenic and otogenic sources, where S. aureus is increasingly recognized as a relevant pathogen, particularly in chronic or previously treated infections. Second, many patients received empirical antibiotic therapy before referral, which may preferentially suppress anaerobic and fastidious organisms and bias culture results toward more resilient pathogens such as S. aureus. Third, although post-traumatic and iatrogenic abscesses represented a minority of cases, these etiologies disproportionately contribute to S. aureus isolates due to inoculation of skin flora. Finally, referral bias to tertiary neurosurgical centers may enrich severe or refractory infections in which S. aureus is more likely to persist. Taken together, these factors provide a plausible explanation for the microbiological distribution observed in the present study and are consistent with patterns reported in other tertiary-care series.

The predominance of pediatric patients in this cohort warrants careful consideration when interpreting spatial risk thresholds. Developmental differences in brain volume, ventricular size, and cortical thickness may influence absolute distance measurements. To address this, we performed age-stratified analyses and formally tested for interaction between age group and abscess–ventricle distance. The absence of significant interaction suggests that ventricular proximity functions as a patient-specific anatomical risk factor rather than an age-dependent phenomenon. Importantly, abscess– ventricle distance reflects a spatial relationship within the individual intracranial geometry, not a fixed external anatomical reference. This supports its applicability across age groups. Nevertheless, absolute distance thresholds derived from this cohort should be interpreted with caution, particularly when extrapolated to adult-dominant populations. External validation in larger adult cohorts is required before universal application of specific cutoff values.

The 5-mm abscess–ventricle distance represents a statistically derived cut-off rather than a biologically discrete or universally clinically meaningful boundary. This threshold was identified through ROC analysis as the point maximizing sensitivity and specificity for rupture prediction within our dataset. Importantly, the relationship between ventricular proximity and rupture risk is continuous rather than dichotomous, with risk increasing progressively as the abscess approaches the ventricular wall. The 5-mm value should therefore be interpreted as a pragmatic risk inflection point useful for clinical stratification and communication, rather than as an absolute determinant of management. In practice, abscesses marginally above this threshold may still warrant heightened vigilance or early intervention depending on clinical context, while those below it should be considered at particularly high risk.

In the present study, multivariate logistic regression identified IVROBA as the single most powerful independent predictor of poor outcome, with an aOR of 14.2 (95% CI: 4.1–49.1, p < 0.001). This underscores the catastrophic nature of this event and the urgent need for reliable early predictors. The current literature on IVROBA remains limited [Table 8], with no universally accepted management guidelines.[4,12,19]

Early identification and timely intervention remain the cornerstones of achieving favorable outcomes.[17,19] The principal aim of this study was therefore to identify and stratify predictors of intraventricular rupture to guide clinical decision-making. It has been widely hypothesized that abscess capsule formation is more robust on the cortical side than on the ventricular side, primarily due to differences in vascular supply. As a result, ventricular rupture is believed to occur more frequently than rupture into the subarachnoid space.[1,12,18] Our findings strongly support this concept: Univariate analysis revealed that ventricular proximity, deep-seated localization, and immunodeficiency were the main predictors of rupture. However, after multivariate adjustment, only ventricular proximity remained independently significant, outperforming all other variables.

Quantitatively, abscesses situated within 5 mm of the ventricular wall demonstrated a 72.7% rupture risk, compared with only 3.8% among those more than 10 mm away. Immunodeficiency contributed to an additional but secondary risk. These findings are consistent with earlier reports emphasizing the importance of abscess– ventricle distance as a decisive factor in rupture risk.[1,3,15,19] Although deep-seated lesions were significant in univariate analysis (OR = 4.31, p = 0.001), this effect disappeared in the multivariate model (aOR = 1.8, p = 0.297), suggesting that deep localization primarily confers risk through its association with ventricular proximity rather than as an independent variable. Similarly, while some authors have proposed that hematogenous abscesses at the gray–white matter junction, characterized by incomplete capsule formation, have an intrinsic tendency to rupture,[12,19] our dataset did not confirm this association, likely due to small subgroup size and the dominant influence of ventricular adjacency.

Clinically, rapid neurological deterioration remains a hallmark of IVROBA. The progression from headache and fever to coma is typically abrupt, driven by increased intracranial pressure and ventriculitis.[8,9,12,14] Characteristic radiological signs, such as meningeal enhancement and local enhancement of the ventricular wall, often precede rupture.[16] Meningeal irritation is a particularly important clinical clue.[13] Our cohort exhibited a characteristic triad: impaired consciousness (GCS ≤8) in 93% (26/28) of patients, meningeal irritation in 96% (27/28), and photophobia in 57% (16/28). These findings reinforce the diagnostic significance of combining clinical and imaging features for early detection.

From a therapeutic perspective, IVROBA necessitates urgent and aggressive management. Treatment typically involves a combination of broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotics, neurosurgical intervention (such as abscess drainage or excision), and, in many cases, EVD to manage hydrocephalus and facilitate intraventricular antibiotic administration. Despite these measures, outcomes remain poor, underscoring the importance of early recognition and, ideally, prevention of rupture. Consequently, improved risk stratification models, particularly those incorporating anatomical proximity to the ventricular system, may help guide earlier surgical intervention in high-risk patients before catastrophic rupture occurs.

Although abscess size has been reported as a contributor to rupture risk in some series, we did not observe an independent association between volume and IVROBA in our cohort. This discrepancy is likely multifactorial. First, rupture is mechanically determined by the integrity of the ventricular-side capsule and the local pressure gradient at the ventricle–abscess interface; in practice, this appears to be better captured by ventricular proximity than by global size, and proximity remained dominant in multivariable modeling. Second, our management protocol may have attenuated size-related risk: Larger lesions were preferentially triaged to early drainage or excision based on mass effect and clinical status, which can reduce the likelihood of subsequent rupture and thereby bias the crude association between size and rupture toward the null. Third, abscess size and proximity are not independent in many anatomical contexts; when the abscess abuts the ventricle, even a modest-volume lesion may rupture, whereas larger cortical lesions can remain distant from the ventricular wall. Finally, the volume range in our dataset (combined with heterogeneity in age and etiology) may have limited power to detect a small independent size effect once proximity and immunodeficiency were included. Taken together, these findings suggest that distance-to-ventricle is a more actionable, anatomy-based risk marker than size alone for anticipating IVROBA and prioritizing preemptive intervention.

Delayed complications, including intraventricular septations and obstructive hydrocephalus secondary to ependymal scarring, were also observed. These structural changes can hinder cerebrospinal fluid flow and limit antibiotic penetration into infected ventricular spaces.[5,11] Early recognition and aggressive management, including ventricular drainage and intraventricular antibiotic administration when indicated, are therefore essential to prevent secondary neurological decline. To assist clinical decision-making, we propose a novel classification system [Table 7] that stratifies rupture risk according to abscess– ventricle proximity. This proximity-based framework provides a pragmatic approach to identifying patients who may benefit from preemptive surgical intervention before rupture occurs. The management recommendations outlined in Table 7 are directly grounded in the observed, distance-dependent rupture risk within our cohort rather than theoretical assumptions. Ventricular proximity demonstrated a steep, nonlinear relationship with rupture probability, with a marked inflection below 5 mm. This anatomical gradient has provided the rationale for stratifying patients into categories of increasing risk and for proposing increasingly proactive surgical strategies as the abscess approaches the ventricular wall. It is important to emphasize that these recommendations are intended as a risk-driven decision-making framework, rather than prescriptive guidelines, and should be interpreted in the context of individual clinical status and institutional resources.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the cohort was predominantly pediatric, reflecting referral patterns of one participating tertiary center. Although age-stratified analyses demonstrated consistent associations between ventricular proximity and rupture risk in both children and adults, the limited adult sample size restricts the precision of adult-specific estimates. Consequently, the generalizability of absolute distance thresholds to adult populations should be interpreted cautiously and validated in independent cohorts. Future multicenter studies are warranted to validate these findings in larger populations. Second, despite the use of standardized imaging protocols, part of the retrospective dataset relied on historical radiological archives. Variability in CT slice thickness, MRI availability, and scanner resolution may have influenced volumetric measurements and abscess–ventricle distance accuracy, even though Brainlab’s segmentation algorithm was employed for cross-validation. Third, treatment decisions were individualized based on clinical presentation and available resources rather than randomized allocation. While this mirrors real-world neurosurgical practice, it introduces potential treatment bias that may have affected outcome comparisons between surgical and conservative groups. Fourth, microbiological cultures were negative in nearly 20% of cases, due to prior antibiotic exposure. This limited the ability to correlate specific pathogens with rupture risk. Future investigations employing molecular diagnostic tools such as 16S rRNA sequencing could enhance pathogen detection and provide deeper insights into pathogen-specific risks. Finally, the study findings may not be generalizable to all healthcare settings. The proposed proximity thresholds were derived from a tertiary center equipped with advanced imaging and navigation technology. Validation in lower-resource environments and across diverse populations is necessary before universal adoption.

CONCLUSION

This study establishes ventricular proximity as the primary mechanical determinant of intraventricular rupture in brain abscesses. Quantitative modeling demonstrated that abscesses located within 5 mm of the ventricular wall carry exponentially higher rupture risk, independent of microbiological or systemic factors. Immunodeficiency was identified as a secondary but significant modifier of vulnerability. Defining a spatial threshold provides a practical, objective criterion to guide early surgical decision-making. Abscesses within this critical range should be considered for early, image-guided aspiration or excision to preempt rupture and subsequent ventriculitis, an event still associated with mortality rates exceeding 80%.

Future research should aim to validate this proximity-based model through multicenter studies, integrate it into predictive algorithms using machine-learning approaches, and evaluate whether early surgical intervention in high-risk cases translates into measurable survival benefits. By refining risk stratification and promoting timely intervention, IVROBA can evolve from a nearly fatal complication into a predictable and potentially preventable event in modern neurosurgical practice.
