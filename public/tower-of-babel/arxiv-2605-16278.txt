Keeping an Eye on AI: A Framework for Effective Human Oversight of AI Systems
Susanne Gaube University College London Markus Langer University of Freiburg Tim Miller University of Queensland, Kevin Baum, Raimund Dachselt, Anna Maria Feit, Ujwal Gadiraju, Harmanpreet Kaur, Mark T. Keane, Richard Landers, Johann Laux, Q. Vera Liao, Brian Lim, Linda Onnasch, Tim Schrills, Liz Sonenberg, Chenhao Tan, Nava Tintarev, Ziang Xiao, Hanwei Zhang

Abstract
The use of Artificial Intelligence (AI) in high-risk, decision-making scenarios presents technical, safety, and normative challenges; problems that may only be ameliorated by human oversight. However, notions of human oversight lack a common foundational understanding: oversight architectures are not well defined, the roles involved remain unclear, and implementation steps are opaque. Hence, researchers and practitioners struggle to determine how to design, implement, and evaluate systems that enable effective human oversight. This paper advances a practical framework for effective human oversight of AI systems, based on a cross-disciplinary perspective that draws on insights from computer science, human-computer interaction, psychology, philosophy, and law. The core contributions are: (1) a foundational framework, with a working definition, architecture and processes for effective human oversight of AI systems; (2) an initial template for documenting oversight architectures and processes, applied to diverse domains; and (3) a synthesis of open research challenges that need to be considered in the emerging field of effective human oversight of AI systems.

2
Grounding Human Oversight

Human oversight can be viewed as a control and safety layer, ideally one that prevents unfavourable outcomes from AI systems, and critically embedded in a larger risk management architecture
63
given that human oversight cannot address all risks. From this perspective,
Human Oversight of an AI system
can be defined as a:

Deliberate
human activity with the goal of sufficiently mitigating risks in the use of an AI system for a specific task. This oversight, which may be technologically supported, often works within
distributed
organisational structures and processes that have to meet regulatory and economic requirements. It requires one or more people to
monitor
the operation and output of the AI system and to
intervene
when professional judgment, established criteria, or legal provisions indicate that action is required to mitigate risks arising from the system’s processes, outputs, or its context of use. Its
effectiveness
is assessed by the reduced likelihood of occurrence and severity of harms arising from the system operations.

This definition applies to decisions that are
dynamic and time-critical (e.g., overseeing autonomous vehicles) and high-stakes, deliberative (e.g., medical, judicial, safety
16
;
58
;
72
).
Below, we briefly expand on some key considerations in this definition:

Deliberate

Human oversight does not arise incidentally. It requires explicit assignment: individuals must be designated to oversight roles, trained accordingly, and embedded within organisational structures.

Distributed

Human oversight may also be distributed across individuals, hierarchical layers, and organisations, which may often be a key requirement for effective oversight
(
31
)
. An operator may monitor single outputs, a domain expert may review borderline cases, developers may analyse AI system behaviour over time, and a compliance officer may monitor communication between the frontline operators and developers. Tasks with clear goals and responsibilities need to be explicitly allocated to these parties.

Monitor

Monitoring is the ongoing process of observing (potentially aggregated) system behaviour and outputs against relevant benchmarks,
such as pre-defined performance thresholds, legal standards, or professional norms.

Intervene

Intervention is the action taken when monitoring reveals risks: correcting an output, halting a process, flagging a case, escalating a decision, or re-training or re-designing a system. Crucially, the capacity to intervene is predicated on the overseer having both the understanding to recognize when action is needed and the control or causal influence to execute that action
(
76
)
.

Effective

Effective human oversight
“reduces occurrence and severity of harms”
. This can be realised in three increasingly demanding ways:
handover
,
error detection
, and
synergy
.
Handover
involves humans taking over when the AI system fails, or reaches the boundary of its competence. This required sufficient situational awareness to ensure safe transfer of control
(
22
)
.
Error Detection
involves identifying inadequate system behaviour and deciding whether to override the AI or not
(
44
;
65
)
. Its effectiveness depends on the detectability of errors, overseer capability to identify them, and on the efficacy of available interventions.

Synergy
requires
appropriate reliance
—intervening on erroneous outputs while trusting well-functioning AI—, up to the point where human-AI performance exceeds either alone
(
79
;
25
;
20
)
). Here, oversight reduces both AI-related risks and risks introduced by human intervention.

Enabling human oversight effectiveness.

Establishing, ensuring, and evaluating the effectiveness of human oversight is a socio-technical challenge, requiring the integrative design of human, technological, and organisational factors (as argued by e.g.
14
;
39
;
55
;
65
;
76
;
49
). Our framework therefore, adopts a layered approach that takes into account the following factors:

•

Human factors
capture the knowledge, skills, motivation, and behaviours of oversight personnel.
These need to be aligned with the oversight task. Oversight personnel includes individuals trained in taking over control of AI, experts evaluating single AI outputs and edge cases, or people with expertise on technical issues and failures. For example, system developers might monitor technical functions using benchmark information, whereas domain experts might evaluate outputs using domain knowledge and context-specific insights.

•

Technical factors
cover improving model reliability, failure or risk detection, and system interfaces for overseers, including considering human-interaction modes, effective visualisations, and warning modules supporting situational awareness.
In particular, providing transparency into the AI system, including metrics related to the risks being monitored, explainability, and uncertainty communication may be prerequisites for effective oversight.

•

Organisational factors
relate to the work environment, the available time and resources, and establishing
clear responsibilities for and between actors.
For example, oversight organisation requires defining the concrete tasks of oversight personnel, whether oversight personnel works individually or in teams; in a single organisation or across organisations.

3
A Framework for Human Oversight of AI Systems

We propose a framework that casts human oversight as a deliberate, evidence-informed layer of the safety and control infrastructure of AI systems. Thus, human oversight is realised within a complex socio-technical system with layers involving staff, management, organisations, regulators/associations, and government (echoing Rasmussen’s six-layer Risk Management Framework
(
63
)
for accident analysis).

Our framework makes a critical distinction between human oversight architecture and process. The
Oversight Architecture
(Section
3.1
) is a static model that identifies the key components of the framework. The
Oversight Process
(Section
3.2
) is a dynamic model that identifies the flow of information between different components, notably those involving
monitoring
and
intervention
(Section
3.3
).

There are two main layers in the framework, the
task layer
, which covers the task realised with the AI system, and the
oversight layer
, that covers the human oversight of the AI-enabled task layer. Figure
1
shows these two layers with information flows between them for monitoring and intervention; more precisely, the task layer is embedded within the oversight layer (see Figure
3
). Importantly, as we will discuss in Section
3.1.3
, the oversight layer may be modeled as further layers of human oversight to become effective (cf. Figure
2
).

3.1.2
The Oversight Layer

The oversight layer contains the task of overseeing, along with its wider socio-technical aspects (see Figure
1
, upper-left):

•

Oversight Task
: This task contains goals to mitigate risks to safety, health, and fundamental rights and the outcomes of oversight (e.g., effective risk mitigation); optionally, there can be multiple oversight goals (e.g., safety as a primary goal and operational efficiency as a secondary goal).

•

Oversight Technology
(optional): The technology supporting the human oversight task; for instance, an automated warning system to alert overseers when key measures are out of bounds.

•

: Those humans assigned oversight duties, such as domain experts in the object-task domain, or people specifically trained to monitor AI systems.

•

Oversight Organisation
: The organisational constraints, rules, processes, and structures that the human overseers need to consider; for instance, oversight personnel could work as a team in a control centre, specifically designed for oversight duties, where each person has assigned responsibilities.

3.1.3
Unpacking the Oversight Layer

Figure 2:
An Oversight Layer may need to be unpacked into different layers of oversight; there may be a
Real-Time Layer
(i.e., with overseers monitoring/intervening on outputs in real-time), a

The above presentation of the oversight layer may be a simplified view. In order to ensure effective human oversight, often this layer needs to be unpacked into several layers of oversight (see Figure
2
). We argue for, at least, three such layers:

•

Real-time Oversight:
Continuous monitoring of the task layer in real-time, with dynamic control interventions to halt, modify, or override single AI outputs before harm or non-compliance occurs. For instance, real-time oversight could be a radiologist correcting errors in an AI-generated report for a specific patient.

•

Systemic Oversight:
Monitoring the object-level task to perform interventions that recalibrate, redesign, or retrain the AI system; or interventions that modify the attributes of the task, human(s) or organisation, to be better aligned with the oversight goals, to prevent future harm or non-compliance. For instance, systemic oversight could be a system designer monitoring for AI model drift over multiple tasks to eventually retrain a system.

•

Compliance Oversight:
Monitoring the object-level task in real-time to perform interventions that enforce regulatory and contractual obligations. For instance, compliance oversight could be a compliance officer in a hospital using patient reports to mandate model re-training.

These considerations show that oversight is not just restricted to real-time interventions, but can also occur over longer time-periods with monitoring and intervention activities that may require access to information or capabilities that are available in the systemic or compliance layers. For instance, longer-term oversight often acts on aggregate data—over days or weeks—to prevent
future
harm or non-compliance (e.g., monitoring group fairness in AI decisions across many tasks, rather than individual decisions
(
44
)
).

We acknowledge that other ‘layers’ beyond these can affect
human
oversight of AI systems by being e.g. part of a larger risk-mitigation ecosystem. For example, legal aspects can impact the design, organisational policy, and normative judgments made by oversight personnel
(
63
)
. These different layers and the broader risk-mitigation layer highlight the complex inter-dependencies that affect human oversight.

3.3
Monitoring & Intervention Processes

The two fundamental processes underlying human oversight are monitoring and intervention
(
44
;
51
;
74
;
73
)
. Their successful implementation is essential for meeting the requirements of effective human oversight
76
. Specifically, monitoring is related to the epistemic requirements of effective human oversight, encompassing the oversight personnel’s awareness of the operational environment, system states, processes, outputs, associated risks, available interventions, as well as their respective benefits and likely consequences. Intervention, in turn, addresses the requirements concerning human causal influence and control over the system including the opportunity, capabilities, and resources necessary to stop, override, or modify AI processes and outputs. Below, we introduce high-level operational preconditions for successful monitoring and intervention processes

Monitoring

Monitoring AI systems, assessing current risks, and detecting problematic system behaviour can be understood as a
signal detection task

(
44
;
51
)
. Against the backdrop of theories of human perception and judgment such as Signal Detection Theory and Brunswik’s Lens Model
(
30
;
9
;
42
)
, conceptualising monitoring as signal detection highlights that oversight personnel search for information cues in the task layer that provide evidence for risk or problematic behavior. During monitoring, oversight personnel thus accumulate evidence when they attend to, detect, and interpret cues until the available evidence warrants intervention. Whether monitoring is successful depends on the availability and quality of information cues, as well as the ability of the oversight to interpret these
(
69
;
24
)
.

From this perspective, we identify the following three preconditions for successful monitoring:

•

Information availability: Do the oversight personnel have the right information?
The extent to which relevant cues are accessible, observable, and timely for human oversight personnel. Examples of relevant cues include deviations from expected output distributions, violations of domain-specific rules, valid confidence estimates, discrepancies between model outputs and valid external benchmarks, and indicators of mismatches between training and deployment contexts. Monitoring is degraded when relevant cues are hidden, delayed, or fragmented across systems. Availability is shaped by how information signals are captured and surface; e.g., via system logs, telemetry data, uncertainty estimates, explanations, contextual input-output data access, and alerts for anomalous behaviour. Temporal and spatial characteristics also shape availability: latency between cue occurrence and potential failure constrains timely intervention, and cues originating far from the point of failure may require clear mappings. Finally, signals may vary in frequency, modality, complexity, relevance, and completeness; they can be continuous streams or discrete.

•

Information detection: Can the oversight personnel detect the relevant cues?
The ability of human oversight personnel to notice and discriminate relevant cues. Detection can be impeded by poorly-designed interfaces, misaligned presentation of cues, or insufficient presentation time. Human attentional, motivational, and training deficits may further impair cue detection. Detection can be supported through clear interface designs, alerting and prioritisation mechanisms that manage information overload, and targeted training that focuses attention of relevance cues.

•

Information interpretation: Do the oversight personnel interpret the cues correctly?
The process of integrating, weighting, and mapping cues to judge the likelihood and severity of problematic AI behaviour; ultimately informing intervention. Interpretation is shaped by prior beliefs, experience, human judgmental biases, technological support, and perceived costs of errors and interventions. Interpretation can be supported by defining clear standards for problematic behavior, training, documentation and records that support tracing, explanatory tools that clarify why cues are informative, and feedback on past outcomes.

Intervention

Intervention is a process of deliberate planning and action. Particular types of interventions can be pre-determined—e.g. determined by policies, operational guidelines and standards —or deliberated just-in-time— e.g. informed by professional expertise of the oversight personnel. They can vary in scope and impact, and are shaped by the level of control, the nature of the task, and the characteristics of the intervention itself. Building on the work of
76
, we define the following two preconditions for successful intervention:

•

Oversight capability: Is oversight personnel able to act?
The oversight personnel’s capacity to intervene, including the authority (i.e., whether they have been granted access or permissions), relevant skills/expertise (i.e., domain knowledge needed to execute the intervention), their willingness (i.e., tolerance for risks, perceived responsibility), their situation awareness (i.e., correct understanding of the situation, informed by monitoring), and their coordination in distributed oversight situations (e.g., when there are distributed responsibilities among the oversight personnel).

•

Intervention feasibility: Is the intervention available and viable in the current context?
The extent to which potential intervention actions are available, applicable, and address the risk in the current situation. Feasibility varies by layer of oversight (see Figure
2
), and is shaped by characteristics of the interventions, such as frequency (i.e., how often intervention is required), effort (i.e., cognitive load, time, or physical resources needed to intervene), economic/reputational cost (i.e., economic implications of intervention, of delays), priority (i.e., time sensitivity or criticality), risk (i.e., potential harm or disruption of inaction or incorrect intervention), and governing policies and processes.

4
A Template for Human Oversight in Practice

This section introduces a practical template for organising and evaluating human oversight of AI (see Appendix
A
for instructions on how to use the template and Appendices
B
-
E
for use cases). It was developed at the respective Dagstuhl Seminar
(
46
)
, where participants used known requirements of effective oversight (e.g.
76
;
44
) to refine the framework from Section
3
into a set of key attributes required to implement oversight. It was then iteratively improved and verified in several case studies (see Section
4.4
).

The template operationalises our framework by providing a systematic way to document the oversight architectures and processes. It is intended for developers, deployers, auditors, regulators, and researchers, enabling them to think through oversight architectures and processes, document those, and also assess whether they are coherent, effective, and feasible.

The template could be used in participatory workshops, organisational audits, and design reviews, to surface design flaws, responsibilities, limitations, and dependencies. The resulting documentation supports risk management, compliance, and iterative improvement by translating abstract oversight principles into concrete, verifiable, and adaptable procedures. Note that the main aspects of the template are directly related to our oversight architecture and process, whereas the bullet points are examples for key aspects to consider when filling out the template.

Oversight at this level ensures that the AI system functions as intended, including safety, alignment with regulatory requirements, norms, and standards.

: key characteristics such as real-time monitoring capability.

•

functionalities
: what the system does, such as display performance metrics.

•

failure modes
: conditions under which these tools might fail, such as data latency.

•

system interfaces
: how these tools interface with the AI system, such as API interaction.

•

signals
: how they communicate relevant information to the human overseers, such as visual alerts, reports etc.

3.

Oversight Personnel

Describe the personnel responsible for oversight, including:

•

abilities/competencies
: knowledge, skills, abilities required, such as technical expertise.

•

authority
: decision-making power, such as the ability to halt the system.

•

responsibility
: tasks overseers must perform, such as monitoring and intervention duties, decision thresholds, and escalation procedures.

•

intentions
: goals and priorities guiding actions, such as complying with regulations.

4.

Oversight Organisation

Describe the organisational setting in which the oversight takes place, including:

•

composition of oversight
: individual or team oversight; single organisation or distributed oversight.

•

rules and procedural constraints
: standard operating procedures and audit protocols;

•

governance structures
: committees or bodies allocating oversight responsibilities.

•

reporting mechanisms
: incident logs, escalation pathways.

•

coordination
: how oversight activities align with other teams (IT, legal, operations).

•

constraints
: resource limitations or technological dependencies affecting feasibility.

The oversight process describes the relevant activities in the oversight task. For each of the real-time, systemic, and compliance layers, define:

Component

Description

1.

•

signals
: the signals received from the overseen layer.

•

interpretation
: the process for interpreting signals.

•

frequency
: how often monitoring occurs, such as continuous/real-time, scheduled, or conditional on other events.

2.

3.

Decide

•

4.

4.4
Application, Evaluation, and Insights

We applied the template to four domains: hiring, air traffic control, automated insulin delivery, and medical diagnostics, to test feasibility and practical value (Appendices
B
-
E
). In all cases, the template supported a structured reflection on the scope and purpose of oversight, clarified who bears responsibilities, and identified the institutional and technical supports required. For example, in the hiring case, documenting roles and decision thresholds revealed a risk of diffusion of responsibility across two formally designated overseers, motivating a need for clearer role definitions and accountability mechanisms. Completing the template surfaced dependencies and risks that otherwise might have been overlooked.

While the template is generalisable, implementation necessarily reflects contextual factors such as regulatory obligations, technical maturity, and organisational culture. We also saw that concrete and in-depth responses to the template require integrating perspectives from individuals with varied domain expertise (e.g., individuals who work directly with the AI-based system on a task), technical expertise, and AI governance and ethics expertise.

5
Challenges Facing Effective Human Oversight

Although our framework provides a practical starting point for implementing effective human oversight of AI, it also exposes central challenges:
operational challenges
in the core monitoring and intervention activities;
socio-technical challenges
that consider the interactions between the human, the task and the organisational context; and
governance challenges
, concerning the limits and legitimacy of human oversight.

5.1
Operational Challenges

Effective oversight depends on humans being able to
monitor
AI systems and appropriately
intervene
when needed, activities that pose difficulties that go beyond those encountered in traditional human-automation interaction. Moreover, human overseers may require new forms of expertise to meaningfully enact their responsibilities.

Monitoring
AI systems often involves interpreting probabilistic, multi-modal, and dynamically changing information signals rather than deterministic ones
(
57
)
. Signals may drift over time, emerge only at aggregate levels, or be distributed across system components
(
57
;
71
;
62
)
. A challenge, therefore, is determining which information signals should be provided and how they should be represented across different task layers, risk profiles, and oversight roles.

Intervening
in AI systems can require actions at multiple layers, including model modifications, full retraining, changes to data pipelines, or the introduction of human-in-the-loop validation
(
71
;
7
)
. Effective intervention, therefore, requires mapping how AI systems are developed, deployed, and maintained across data sources, supply chains, and model layers
(
68
;
5
)
.

5.2
Socio-technical Challenges

Research in human-automation interaction has identified persistent factors that impede human control
(
39
;
53
;
74
)
. Fundamentally, human oversight is constrained by well-documented limits of human cognition, including reliance on heuristics (i.e., bounded rationality,
(
75
;
53
;
35
)
). These limits are compounded by the restricted effectiveness of explainability
(
41
;
52
;
34
;
64
;
26
;
25
;
12
;
77
)
and transparency
(
50
;
36
)
, which do not guarantee meaningful understanding or reliable intervention.
Oversight tasks also frequently involve prolonged periods of passive monitoring, a mode of engagement for which humans are poorly suited due to limitations in sustaining vigilance
(
43
)
.
These challenges may be further exacerbated by organisational goals and incentive structures that undermine effective oversight, such as productivity targets that reward rapid acceptance of AI outputs rather than critical engagement
(
71
;
59
)
.

Even where human oversight is feasible in principle, sustaining it in practice requires carefully designed socio-technical conditions
(
15
;
32
;
55
;
61
)
. Effective oversight depends on aligning interfaces, workflows, organisational structures, and training with the demands of monitoring and intervention.
Oversight also increasingly requires AI-specific technical competencies, including understanding model behaviour and error modes
(
57
)
. These demands intensify in multi-model and multi-agent systems, where interacting components can give rise to emergent and difficult-to-interpret behaviours, and thus understanding how to exert human control and oversight becomes an even more complex challenge
(
3
)
. For such systems, the kind of real-time oversight that this paper mainly focuses on may be infeasible
(
60
)
. They might additionally require containment architectures that enable anticipatory oversight by providing systems with guardrails before deployment
(
37
;
83
)
.

At the same time, increasing reliance on AI introduces a central dilemma: using AI systems may undermine the expertise needed to oversee them. Cognitive offloading, automation bias, reduced practice, and missing feedback loops contribute to deskilling and erosion of expertise, even in safety-critical settings
(
66
;
10
)
. As AI systems become more capable, humans may have fewer opportunities or incentives to develop and maintain domain expertise
(
22
;
4
)
. Sustaining human oversight, therefore, requires deliberate investment in training and long-term capability maintenance of the oversight personnel – and foundational discussions on where we want to invest time and resources to maintain these capabilities.

5.3
Governance Challenges

When the challenges outlined above are not adequately addressed, human oversight risks becoming symbolic rather than effective or meaningful. The mere presence of oversight personnel or structures does not guarantee risk mitigation and may instead create an illusion of control
(
29
)
. In some cases, responsibility for failures is displaced from designers or organisations onto individual overseers, who become scapegoats
(
21
)
. Finally, scholars have questioned whether meaningful human oversight is ethically defensible in some domains (e.g., in the use of lethal autonomous weapon systems
(
70
)
), and in other contexts, challenges for privacy may arise (e.g., when human oversight is supposed to mitigate risks in user-AI interactions in healthcare). Addressing this challenge requires establishing methods for evaluating and monitoring oversight effectiveness and clear definitions of roles, responsibilities, and evaluation criteria. However, testing whether human oversight actually meets regulatory requirements poses its own challenges, as compliance criteria are difficult to operationalise and standard evaluation methods may be insufficient for the socio-technical complexity of oversight
(
46
;
48
)
. Moreover, oversight architectures themselves can become targets for adversarial exploitation, introducing a further layer of risk
(
18
)
.

5.4
Overall

These challenges prompt key questions:

1.

Operational
: What monitoring and intervention capabilities must be available for oversight to be meaningful and effective?

2.

Socio-technical
: Under what conditions is it reasonable to assign oversight responsibility to humans, and how should feasible oversight goals be set? How can oversight-related expertise, as well as technical and organisational support be designed and sustained over time?

3.

Governance
: How to evaluate and monitor the effectiveness of human oversight and what normative, regulatory, or design responses are warranted in cases where meaningful oversight is not achievable?

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we proposed a framework for effective human oversight of AI systems. Built on cross-disciplinary perspectives from participants at the 2025 Dagstuhl Seminar on human oversight, the key insights reflected in the framework are:
(a)
that human oversight of AI is a multi-layered issue, which goes beyond real-time intervention to include systemic and compliance layers; and
(b)
monitoring and interventions are required at these layers, and require new areas of expertise than we have seen with oversight of ‘traditional automation’. We provided a template for human oversight architectures and processes designed to engage stakeholders when reflecting on the socio-technical conditions for effective human oversight. We have shown this template can be applied in diverse domains, demonstrating the practical applicability of the framework.

We hope that this framework provides a shared foundation for researchers and practitioners working on human oversight of AI systems. However, it cannot resolve all challenges that arise as AI systems, especially systems with agentic capabilities that raise new practical, ethical, and legal demands for human control. We have, therefore, outlined key challenges for future research on human oversight, to focus the community’s research efforts. Many of the issues raised are not completely new, but they are arguably more complex than those seen before. Furthermore, the growing capabilities and widespread deployment of AI systems render these issues as urgent, and a major research program on effective human oversight of AI will be required to resolve them.

Appendix B
Hiring

B.1
Task Layer

This appendix outlines an example of the human oversight framework applied to the domain of hiring where an AI system assesses the fit of applicants against job requirements based on recorded job interviews.

1.

Object-Level Task

Describe the object-level task to be performed:

•

primary goals
: The task is to assess applicant fit against job requirements.

•

risks
: unfair discrimination of individual applicants or groups of applicants, i.e., applicant fit is judged to be lower than the respective applicants’ actual suitability.

•

outcomes
: Time-to-hire; job performance of hired individuals in the future.

2.

AI system

Describe the AI system that supports or automates parts of this task:

•

core properties
: The AI system is an automated job interview evaluation system. Applicants record videos of themselves responding to interview questions. There is a transcription module that transcribes applicant responses and an evaluation module that was trained on 1000 prior applicants and their responses.

•

functionality
: The AI automatically assesses applicant suitability from 0 to 100 percent fit.

•

inputs and outputs
: Input: applicant videos; Output: applicant fit score.

•

level of autonomy
: The AI fully automates the applicant evaluation.

•

known limitations
or
failure modes
: Low audio quality can undermine transcription accuracy; low audio quality paired with applicants whose first language is not English leads to even worse transcription accuracies which can reduce applicant suitability ratings.

3.

Human(s)

Identify the people directly interacting with, using, or affected by the AI system, such as operators or end-users. Describe their:

•

abilities/competencies
: The operator is a hiring manager trained in assessing applicant fit. They have also received some training in the use of the AI system, e.g., on how to interpret the applicant suitability score.

•

degree of discretion or authority
: The operator uses the outputs of the AI system for deciding who to invite to an in-person interview. They can also ignore the AI output for this task.

•

responsibilities
: They are responsible for evaluating applicants, for ensuring that hiring processes follow legal standards, e.g., of non-discrimination. They also communicate with invited and rejected applicants.

4.

Organisation

Describe the organisational setting in which the task takes place, including:

•

hierarchical structures
: The hiring manager is responsible for the successful and quick task completion, and they report to their group leader.

•

relevant rules and procedural constraints
: The organization has a human resources group that defines the hiring processes.

B.2
Oversight Layer

The purpose of the oversight layer is to monitor the operation of the AI system in context, to detect risks or failures, and to intervene when necessary. Oversight at this level ensures that the AI system functions safely, fairly, and as intended.

1.

Oversight Task

Describe the oversight task to be performed:

•

purpose
: The evaluation AI may provide inaccurate results and under- or overestimate the actual applicant suitability especially when audio quality of applicant interviews is low. The evaluation AI may also produce discriminatory outputs if applicants do not have English as their first language.

•

goals
: 1. Assess the adequacy of the AI evaluation module for individual applicants; 2. Assess whether the AI evaluation module is functioning properly on a group level; and 3. Assess whether the AI evaluation module provides discriminatory outputs.

•

risks
: Inadequacy of using the AI evaluation may remain undetected, for instance, due to the large number of applicants applying and due to the limited time provided for real-time oversight.

2.

Oversight Technology (Optional)

Describe the technologies that support oversight, such as monitoring dashboards, warning systems, or audit logs. Describe their:

•

functionalities
: Real-time oversight (i.e., hiring managers) has access to a warning tool that monitors word error rates in transcriptions and audio quality.

Systemic oversight (i.e., technicians) has access to the benchmark dashboard that includes warning modules that highlight benchmarks that are out of bounds and that assess different fairness metrics.

3.

Oversight Personnel

Describe the personnel responsible for oversight, including:

•

composition
: Real-time oversight personnel: a hiring manager overseeing AI use for individual applicants.
Systemic oversight personnel: a technician overseeing the aggregate functioning of the AI system.
Compliance oversight personnel: compliance officers assessing whether AI behavior reflects fairness issues.

•

abilities/competencies
: Real-time oversight personnel is trained in assessing applicant fit and in using the AI system as decision support.
Systemic oversight personnel is trained in assessing technical AI benchmarks.
Compliance oversight personnel is trained in assessing AI fairness challenges.

•

responsibility
: Real-time oversight: Their duty is to assess for each individual applicant whether the AI evaluation system is adequate to use.
Systemic oversight: Their duty is to assess on a group level whether the system is functioning adequately. They monitor whether AI benchmarks are critically out of bounds.
Compliance oversight: Their duty is to assess whether reported challenges reflect fairness issues.

4.

Oversight Organisation

Describe the organisational setting in which the oversight takes place, including:

•

reporting mechanisms
: Real-time oversight reports to the systemic- and compliance oversights if they detect issues for individual applicants.
Systemic oversight reports to the real-time and to compliance oversight personnel if they detect issues on a group level. Compliance oversight reports back to the other layers if critical fairness challenges arise.

B.3
Oversight Process

The oversight process describes all the relevant activities in the oversight task.

1.

Monitor

Specify the types of monitoring activities and their temporal frequency:

•

signals
: Real-time oversight monitoring duties: Examine the word error rate for single individuals, check for audio quality issues in videos, check for accents and dialects that may undermine the quality of the AI evaluation module.
Systemic oversight monitoring duties: monitor benchmarks over time such as shifts in suitability ratings depending on different subgroups of applicants.
Compliance oversight monitoring duties: monitor and assess whether reported issues indeed represent fairness issues.

2.

Assess Risk

Define the risk assessment process to be used by the oversight personnel:

•

The main risk is that the AI evaluation module is inadequate to assess specific applicants due to the transcription module producing higher error rates for certain applicants and certain technical circumstances.

3.

Decide

Define the decision making process that the oversight personnel follow, including:

•

decision protocols
: All oversight personnel is instructed to decide for interventions based on their respective oversight training, duties and based on their own discretion.

•

documentation
: Oversight personnel is instructed to document by reporting to the other oversight layers.

4.

Intervene

Specify intervention types and their temporal frequency (real-time, scheduled, or conditional).

•

Real-time oversight: Ignore AI evaluation module and assess applicant suitability independently.

•

Systemic oversight: Provide a warning signal to real-time and compliance oversight; stop the AI evaluation module.

•

Compliance oversight: Provide a warning signal to the systemic and real-time oversight, stop the AI evaluation module and suggest solutions to fix fairness issues.

Appendix C
Air Traffic Control

This appendix outlines an example of the human oversight framework applied to the domain of air traffic control (ATC) — a part of air traffic management that involves real-time guidance to aircraft from take-off, flying, and landing.

C.1
Task Layer

The task layer consists of
teams
of air-traffic controllers, each of which controls a particular sector of airspace, with managers/supervisors who help to monitor air traffic at a higher level, considering factors such as traffic flow and monitoring for emergencies. Air traffic controllers and managers are assisted by various technological tools, from tasks such as automated aircraft tracking, to collision detection, to decision support, such as which aircraft should change its flight path and how in case of a potential collision.

1.

Object-Level Task

Describe the object-level task to be performed:

•

primary goals
: The goals of object-level task include allocating aircraft to airspace to ensure the safety of people on board and on the ground, while also ensuring a smooth flow of air traffic (e.g. avoiding delays). Individually, controllers allocate paths to aircraft within their own air sector, but are also responsible for the safe handover of aircraft between their sector and that of other controllers. The task is accomplished through a distributed system which spans multiple controllers, interactive artifacts (e.g., flight strips, radar), and organisational structure.

•

risks
: The main risks are: potential accidents, and delays in flights, with the constraint that this is a real-time scheduling problem that must be solved "in flight"—aircraft cannot be parked or paused.

•

outcomes
: The outcomes are whether aircraft land safely and on time, and a successful transformation of the continuous flow of air traffic into a manageable sequence.

2.

AI system

Describe the AI system that supports or automates parts of this task:

•

core properties
: There are several technological components in ATC, but some critical examples using AI include prediction of air traffic 20-30 minutes in advance, prediction of aircraft trajectory, collision prediction, and collision resolution (recommending conflict-free trajectories).

•

inputs and outputs
: inputs such as flight schedules, information on delays, information on aircraft; outputs: prediction of aircraft trajectory.

•

level of autonomy
: The AI can work completely autonomously, such that controllers do not have to rely on mental simulations of flight trajectories.

•

known limitations
or
failure modes
: Failure can result when incorrect information is provided to the AI, when predictions of traffic, trajectories, and collisions are incorrect, or when collision resolution fails; e.g., solves one conflict but introduces another, or does not have access to necessary information that the controller has, including controller preferences. Moreover, automating some tasks can disrupt the working division of labor if it does not account for how different stakeholders coordinate through shared artifacts that they use to make decisions.

3.

Human(s)

Identify the people directly interacting with, using, or affected by the AI system, such as operators or end-users. Describe their:

•

abilities/competencies
: Air traffic controllers, whose decisions are in real time and can have major safety and air traffic flow consequences. These are highly-trained professionals in the core task of air traffic management. They have also received some training on the AI system, for instance regarding its known limitations.

•

degree of discretion or authority
: They have the ability to change flight departure time, arrival time, and flight paths, and the authority to tell pilots to do these.

•

responsibilities
: They are responsible for ensuring safety and air traffic flow.

4.

Organisation

Describe the organisational setting in which the task takes place, including:

•

hierarchical structures
: The organisations involved include airlines, air services, and airports. Air services have authority to dictate—to airlines and airports—flight departure, arrival, paths, etc. Pilots and air traffic controllers report regularly to each other on flight updates.

•

relevant rules and procedural constraints
: Procedurally, aviation is one of the most heavily regulated and compliancy-based sectors.

C.2
Oversight Layer

C.2.1
Real-time layer

The real-time oversight layer helps to ensure safety and air traffic flow by monitoring and intervening on both ATC systems and controllers, in real-time:

1.

Oversight Task

Describe the oversight task to be performed:

•

purpose and goals
: The primary oversight task is to ensure safety by monitoring AI system outputs (trajectory predictions, conflict alerts, resolution recommendations) and intervening when these systems provide inaccurate, incomplete, or unsafe guidance. Secondary goals include maintaining efficient traffic flow and ensuring regulatory compliance. Oversight is not a discrete check but is woven into the ongoing work of controlling: monitoring occurs continuously as controllers interact with AI-generated predictions and recommendations; each decision to accept or override an AI suggestion constitutes human oversight activity.

•

risks
: The oversight may fail due to unclear responsibilities between the ATC and their supervisor; the oversight may fail due to the significant workload for ATCs.

2.

Oversight Technology (Optional)

Describe the technologies that support oversight, such as monitoring dashboards, warning systems, or audit logs. Describe their:

•

functionalities
: The real-time oversight technology include warning systems for potential collisions or congestion, load-balancing dashboards for sector merging for supervisors to monitor sector loads per pilot, intervene in high- or low-load sectors by merging contiguous sectors, or splitting sectors. These oversee the great socio-technical system, not just the software. These tools function as cognitive artifacts that off-load some monitoring burden (e.g., automated scanning for conflicts) while introducing new coordination demands (e.g., understanding why a conflict alert was triggered, distinguishing true alerts from false positives).

•

system interfaces
: This oversight technology interfaces with AI systems by receiving real-time predictions and comparing them against actual aircraft behavior captured by radar and data links.

•

signals
: Warning systems for human overseer(s) may use visual (color-coded alerts, flashing indicators) and auditory signals to communicate urgency.

3.

Oversight Personnel

Describe the personnel responsible for oversight, including:

•

composition
: The two main oversight personnel are the front-line controllers and their supervisors. Oversight responsibility is distributed across multiple roles operating at different timescales and hierarchies.

•

abilities/competencies
: Front-line controllers handle real-time oversight through continuous interaction with the AI task systems. They possess deep expertise in traffic patterns, aircraft performance characteristics, local airspace constraints, and weather impacts. Supervisors maintain shift-level oversight by monitoring system performance at an aggregate level across multiple sectors, tracking both automated tools and controller workload. They have expertise in system behavior patterns, organisational coordination, and resource allocation.

•

authority
: Controllers and supervisors have authority to immediately override or ignore AI recommendations in their sector. They hold authority to disable any malfunctioning systems, reallocate workload, and escalate issues to on-call or offline technical teams.

•

responsibility
: Front-line controllers are responsible for monitoring the air-traffic control systems and intervening in situations that have the potential to cause an incident within their sector or at the boundary between their airspace and a neighbouring sector. Supervisors are expected to identify potential conflict
across
different airspaces and ensuring even spread of traffic at an aggregate level across multiple sectors, tracking both automated tools and controller workload.

4.

Oversight Organisation

As above.

C.2.2
Systemic layer

1.

Oversight Task

Describe the object-level task to be performed:

•

purpose and goals
: The systemic oversight task considers factors beyond the final predictions (e.g., of trajectories, load balancing) to intermediate computational steps that would enable auditing. For example, for trajectory prediction, this may be done by logging the aircraft performance, wind estimates used, waypoint calculations, etc. – such that domain experts can use a model’s explanatory output to trace why a prediction was (in)accurate. This level of oversight must also be responsive to real-time alternatives (e.g., through counterfactual predictions); multiple reports (e.g., when controllers in different sectors encounter similar problems); and added situational factors (e.g., updating the model weights to prioritize weather information). These intermediate computational steps also contextualise the design task for AI system developers who work with the controllers to make updates to the system.

2.

Oversight Technology

Describe the technologies that support oversight, such as monitoring dashboards, warning systems, or audit logs. Describe their:

•

functionalities
: These would typically be immutable logging systems for traceability of runtime decisions, tools for aggregating individual flight information, and replay tools to replay incidents that occur, as well as tools for safety audit, such as root-cause analysis and interpretability tools.

3.

Oversight Personnel

Describe the personnel responsible for oversight, including:

•

composition
: Experienced controllers (e.g., supervisors) continually test AI performance, coordinate across sector controllers to identify common themes of AI errors, and make decisions about systematic AI use in different scenarios. System and safety engineers (e.g., at Air Navigation Service Providers) bridge operations and development. AI developers and data scientists analyze logged data (e.g., accepted/rejected recommendations, computational trace data) to identify potential algorithmic failures and retrain models.

•

abilities/competencies
: For effective oversight, these may be controllers with technical training, which gives them a combination of prior domain expertise and acquired technical skills to make changes. This requires expertise in AI and software development, supported by design technology such as explanatory outputs.

•

responsibility
: Experienced controllers (e.g., supervisors) continually test AI performance, coordinate across sector controllers to identify common themes of AI errors, and make decisions about systematic AI use in different scenarios. System and safety engineers (e.g., at Air Navigation Service Providers) bridge operations and development: they diagnose root causes of controller-reported issues, translating operational problems into technical specifications. AI developers and data scientists analyze logged data (e.g., accepted/rejected recommendations, computational trace data) to identify potential algorithmic failures and retrain models.

4.

Organisation

Describe the organisational setting in which the oversight takes place, including: assessment and support technology.

•

rules and procedural constraints
: Regulatory bodies set formal reporting and performance guidelines (as above). Aviation software certification standards are rigid due to the safety-critical system needs. They require formal verification, hazard analysis, and extensive safety documentation that can extend design timelines. Legacy infrastructure dependencies can be an issue, which require modern AI systems to interface with decades-old radar, communication, and data processing and presentation systems.

•

governance structures

C.4
Cross-layer linkages and evaluation

1.

Information flows

Information flows regularly between controllers, and from controllers to supervisors, during runtime operation. When incidents occur, supervisors communicate these to design, management, safety, and legal teams within the air services organisation, who then, if necessary, report to regulators. Similarly, regulators provide information on risks and new regulations, and the design, management, safety and legal teams communicate to controllers and supervisors about changes in procedure, protocol, etc.

2.

Feedback loops

Incidents that affect safety and traffic flow are reported upwards, and design teams manage incident logs, then are discussed and may become new features with an AI and software system. In most organisations, changes to systems are carefully monitored, and often need to go through simulation testing for several weeks without an incident before being put into practice.

3.

Evaluation metrics

Safety incidents, including the number and severity are the primary measure, with late arrival of aircraft a secondary measure. Errors within the AI system that do not lead to safety incidents are also logged and measured. At the regulatory level, metrics include certification compliance rates, audit findings, incident rates per flight operation, and serious incident investigations involving AI system failures.

4.

Dependencies and feasibility

Infeasible oversight is almost unheard of in ATC, however, in serious situations, such as ATC systems being unusable, many organisations have basic backup systems that provide just enough functionality for all aircraft to land safely as soon as possible. At such times, oversight of controllers is more difficult, and different procedures are put into place. When these infeasibility conditions are identified (e.g., through workload studied, cognitive task analysis, or incident investigations), findings are communicated through multiple channels: air service safety personnel inform ATC controllers and ground staff to implement operational restrictions; inform AI developers and vendors to redesign the algorithm or interfaces; and inform regulators through safety reports that may lead to certification requirement changes.

Appendix D
Automated Insulin Delivery

This appendix outlines an example of the human oversight framework applied to the domain of automated insulin delivery (AID) in Type 1 diabetes (T1D) management — a safety-critical medical application involving real-time glucose monitoring and algorithmic insulin titration.

D.1
Task Layer

The task layer consists primarily of people living with T1D (end-users) who manage their condition daily, supported by healthcare professionals (HCPs) who provide clinical oversight. The system functions through a loop of integrated technological tools including continuous glucose monitors (CGM), insulin pumps, and control algorithms.

1.

Object-Level Task

Describe the object-level task to be performed:

•

primary goals
: To maintain blood glucose levels within a target range (Time-in-Range) to avoid acute hypo- and hyperglycemia, prevent long-term micro- and macrovascular complications, and support normal daily activities as well as exercise.

•

risks
: Severe hypoglycemia (leading to loss of consciousness/seizures) and diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA); risks are exacerbated by inaccurate dosing, delayed trend detection, or hardware/software malfunctions.

•

outcomes
: Short-term safety (avoidance of extremes), long-term metabolic health (in the form of stable HbA1c), and the successful conversion of complex biological regulation into a manageable daily therapy where the cognitive workload remains sustainable for the patient.

2.

AI system

Describe the AI system that supports or automates parts of this task:

•

core properties
: Hybrid closed-loop systems integrate glucose sensing and delivery using control algorithms (e.g., PID or Model Predictive Control) to adjust basal insulin and predict future trajectories.

•

functionality
: The system automatically modulates basal insulin delivery and provides recommendations for bolus dosing based on real-time and predicted glucose levels.

•

inputs and outputs
: Inputs consist of real-time CGM data, carbohydrate estimates, and exercise markers; Outputs consist of automated insulin delivery adjustments and specific bolus dosing recommendations.

•

level of autonomy
: High autonomy for basal control (often functioning while the user sleeps), but relies on user-initiated inputs for meal-time boluses and correction doses.

•

known limitations
or
failure modes
: Failure modes include sensor inaccuracies (e.g., compression lows), communication drops (Bluetooth failure), incorrect trajectory predictions, or inappropriate recommendations that increase hypoglycemia risk. Automation may decrease user vigilance or mask physiological symptoms.

3.

Human(s)

Identify the people directly interacting with, using, or affected by the AI system:

•

abilities/competencies
: End-users with T1D who must interpret trends and symptoms; HCPs with clinical expertise in therapy optimization. In pediatric settings, caretakers without formal medical education may act as the primary operators.

•

degree of discretion or authority
: Users have the final authority to accept or override dosing suggestions and can suspend automation at any time to revert to manual mode.

•

responsibilities
: Ensuring safe device operation, providing sufficiently correct carbohydrate estimation, and responding to system alerts and alarms.

•

intentions
: Optimizing glycemic outcomes while maintaining immediate physical safety and long-term health.

4.

Organisation

Describe the organisational setting in which the task takes place:

•

hierarchical structures
: Clinical care teams prescribe and configure the systems; manufacturers provide the technology, technical support, and initial training.

•

relevant rules and procedural constraints
: Usage is heavily regulated by medical device authorities (e.g., FDA, EMA/MDR); clinical treatment protocols guide system settings.

Appendix E
Diagnostic Decision-Making

This appendix outlines an example of the human oversight framework applied to diagnostic decision-making in radiology, focusing on head CT interpretation supported by an AI-enabled diagnostic decision-support system (e.g., stroke and haemorrhage detection).

E.1
Task Layer

The task layer represents the socio-technical system of the primary activity that involves the AI system. Human oversight focuses on this level’s functioning and its potential risks.

Object-Level Task

Describe the object-level task to be performed:

•

primary goals
: Timely and accurate interpretation of head CT images to support clinical decisions (e.g., identifying intracranial haemorrhage, early ischaemic change, mass effect, hydrocephalus), appropriate escalation of critical findings, and production of a clinically useful radiology report.

•

risks
: Missed findings (false negatives) or incorrect findings (false positives) leading to delayed or inappropriate treatment (e.g., delayed neurosurgical referral, inappropriate stroke pathway activation), communication failures for critical results, and downstream patient harm; risks are exacerbated by time pressure, high caseloads, interruptions, limited clinical information, and image artefacts.

•

outcomes
: High diagnostic accuracy for clinically important findings, good patient outcomes, minimum time-to-report (especially for emergency head CT), and consistency and clarity of reporting (e.g., following structured reporting templates, such as RadReport by RSNA).

AI System

Describe the AI system that supports or automates parts of this task:

•

core properties
: AI-enabled decision-support integrated into PACS/RIS or a vendor platform; typical components include (a) image-processing and pre-processing pipeline (e.g., slice normalisation, artefact handling), (b) one or more trained models for detection/triage (e.g., haemorrhage detection, large vessel occlusion proxy markers, early ischaemic change support), and (c) user interface elements (e.g., overlays, flags, worklist prioritisation).

•

functionality
: Triage and prioritisation of suspected abnormal head CTs, detection and localisation of acute findings (e.g., intracranial haemorrhage subtypes, midline shift, mass effect), and presentation of decision-support outputs to guide review.

•

inputs and outputs
: Inputs include DICOM head CT image series, acquisition parameters, and optionally limited clinical metadata; outputs include worklist flags (e.g., “suspected ICH”), probability or confidence scores, saliency/heatmap overlays or bounding boxes or segmentations, structured prompts (e.g., “consider mass effect”), and pre-worded patient report.

•

level of autonomy
: The AI system operates autonomously in generating alerts and prioritisation recommendations, but is intended as decision support; the radiologist retains authority for interpretation, changing and signing of reports, and escalation.

•

known limitations
or
failure modes
: Underperformance in cases with motion, beam-hardening, post-operative change or or poorly acquired imaging; atypical presentations; confounding anatomy (e.g., calcifications); distribution shift across scanners/protocols/patient populations; miscalibrated confidence leading to overtrust or underuse; false positives contributing to alert fatigue and workflow disruption; false negatives creating unwarranted reassurance if users rely on AI triage.

Human(s)

Identify the people directly interacting with, using, or affected by the AI system, such as operators or end-users. Describe their:

•

abilities/competencies
: Radiologists’ expertise in neuroanatomy, pathology recognition, and clinical reasoning under uncertainty; ability to integrate imaging with clinical context and priors; competence in using PACS/RIS and interpreting AI outputs (including their limits) through formal trainging and/or experience working with decision support systems.

•

degree of discretion or authority
: Radiologists have discretion to accept, ignore, or override AI outputs, determine report content, and initiate escalation (e.g., critical results communication, stroke team notification, neurosurgical referral); referrers act on reports.

•

responsibilities
: Responsibility for accurate diagnosis and appropriate communication of urgent findings; maintaining professional standards in reporting; using AI outputs appropriately as supportive information rather than definitive diagnosis.

Organisation

Describe the organisational setting in which the task takes place, including:

•

hierarchical structures
: radiology teams organised by shift and subspecialty; consultants/attendings, registrars/residents, and reporting radiographers (where applicable); clinical leads and modality leads; interfaces with emergency medicine, stroke services, neurosurgery, and critical care; on-call structures for out-of-hours reporting (can include outsourced teleradiology providers, potentially using different AI systems) and second opinions.

E.3
Oversight Process

The oversight process describes all relevant activities in the oversight task. For each of the
real-time
,
systemic
, and
compliance
layers, define:

E.3.1
Real-time Oversight Process

Monitor

Specify the types of monitoring activities and their temporal frequency:

•

signals
: AI-generated case-level outputs (e.g., predictions, flags, saliency maps, bounding boxes, confidence scores), image features observed during review, discrepancies between AI output and radiologist judgement;

•

interpretation
: radiologists interpret signals through expert pattern recognition, triangulating AI outputs with image evidence, patient information, clinical context, prior imaging, and known AI failure modes;

•

frequency
: continuous and real-time during active clinical practice.

Assess Risk

Define the risk assessment process to be used by the oversight personnel:

•

Risk assessment focuses on the likelihood and severity of immediate diagnostic harm. Radiologists assess whether AI outputs plausibly reflect true pathology, the clinical consequences of acting or not acting on AI suggestions, and their own confidence under current cognitive and workload constraints. Disagreement between AI and human judgement increases perceived risk and triggers heightened scrutiny or verification.

Decide

Define the decision-making process that the oversight personnel follow, including:

•

decision protocols
: primarily rely on human judgement informed by AI as a supportive signal; predefined escalation rules apply for critical findings, but accepting, rejecting, or ignoring AI outputs remains discretionary;

•

documentation
: clinically significant discrepancies, critical result communications, and escalation actions are documented through reporting systems and local discrepancy mechanisms where required.

Intervene

Specify intervention types and their temporal frequency (real-time, scheduled, or conditional):
Interventions are immediate and case-specific. They include overriding or ignoring AI outputs, re-examining image regions highlighted by AI, adjusting the order or priority of case review, seeking a second opinion, and initiating urgent clinical escalation based on clinical judgement.

E.3.2
Systemic Oversight Oversight Process

Monitor

Specify the types of monitoring activities and their temporal frequency:

•

signals
: aggregated AI performance metrics (e.g., alert rates, false-positive/false-negative patterns), clusters of clinician overrides or discrepancies, rates of IT tickes, time taken to process the AI output, incident and near-miss reports, changes in operational context (e.g., software updates, protocol changes), unusal algorithm processing times, performance changes (e.g., changes in turn-around time);

•

interpretation
: senior clinicians and safety teams interpret signals by identifying trends, correlations, and deviations from expected performance, and by distinguishing random variation from systematic bias or drift;

•

frequency
: scheduled (e.g., weekly or monthly review) and conditional, triggered by incidents, sudden performance shifts, or system updates.

Risk Assess

Define the risk assessment process to be used by the oversight personnel:

•

Risk assessment at this layer focuses on the potential for repeated or widespread harm. Oversight personnel assess whether observed issues indicate model drift, data mismatch, interface-induced misuse, training data limitations, or organisational factors such as workload or insufficient training. Risk severity is evaluated in terms of scale, persistence, and clinical impact.

Decide

Define the decision-making process that the oversight personnel follow, including:

•

decision protocols
: decisions are guided by predefined governance processes (e.g., safety review boards) and may include human judgement informed by technical evidence and clinical expertise;

•

documentation
: findings, rationales, and decisions are formally documented through audit reports, incident analyses, and change-management records.

Intervene

Specify intervention types and their temporal frequency (real-time, scheduled, or conditional):

•

Interventions are system-level and preventative. They include recalibrating or retraining models, redesigning interfaces or alerts, modifying deployment scope or use conditions, updating local protocols and training, and, where necessary, suspending AI use pending further investigation.

E.3.3
Compliance Oversight Process

Monitor

Specify the types of monitoring activities and their temporal frequency:

•

signals
: Compliance reports, audit findings, adverse event notifications, evidence of deviation from approved use or certified performance, and outputs from real-time and systemic oversight layers.

•

interpretation
: Regulators and compliance officers interpret signals against formal requirements, approved indications, and risk classifications, rather than local clinical preferences.

•

frequency
: Scheduled (e.g., periodic audits) and conditional (triggered by serious incidents or regulatory notifications).

Risk Assess

Define the risk assessment process to be used by the oversight personnel:

•

Risk assessment focuses on regulatory non-compliance and unacceptable safety risk at scale. This includes assessing whether failures exceed allowable risk thresholds, indicate breaches of approved use, or undermine certification requirements.

Decide

Define the decision-making process that the oversight personnel follow, including:

•

decision protocols
: Decisions are governed by formal regulatory procedures and legal mandates rather than discretionary clinical judgement.

•

documentation
: Compliance decisions, enforcement actions, and rationales are formally recorded through regulatory and contractual documentation.

Intervene

Specify intervention types and their temporal frequency (real-time, scheduled, or conditional):

•

Interventions enforce compliance and may include mandating retraining or re-certification, restricting or withdrawing approval, imposing reporting or monitoring requirements, or requiring organisational changes to ensure ongoing compliance.
