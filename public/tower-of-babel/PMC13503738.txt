Scene-aware contrastive learning with multi-scale domain-invariant feature alignment for robust visual recognition under complex scenes
Authors: Wenjun Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Deep learning models trained on curated datasets often fail when deployed in complex visual scenes involving illumination drift, occlusion, adverse weather, and cross-domain sensor variation. We propose an end-to-end framework that jointly addresses representational robustness and domain-invariant transfer through two integrated components. First, a scene-aware contrastive learning strategy generates positive pairs via physically grounded scene transformations—simulating fog, rain, illumination gradients, and structured occlusion—and employs an adaptive difficulty weighting mechanism that steers the contrastive gradient toward degradation conditions the encoder finds hardest to resolve. Second, a multi-scale domain-invariant feature alignment method applies distributional matching at multiple depths of the encoder hierarchy, coupled with a cross-scale consistency regularizer that prevents semantic drift between layers. The framework requires no target-domain data and operates with minimal or no annotation. Experiments on PACS and a cross-domain scene recognition benchmark (BDD-City) demonstrate that our method outperforms representative supervised, self-supervised, domain generalization, and domain adaptation baselines, with average cross-domain accuracy gains of 3.5 and over 4% points, respectively. Gains are most pronounced under severe degradation conditions, where baseline methods suffer the steepest performance drops. Ablation analysis confirms that each component contributes independently and that their interaction is synergistic.

Introduction

Deep learning has reshaped visual recognition over the past decade, yet a persistent gap remains between benchmark performance and real-world deployment. Models trained on curated datasets often falter when confronted with complex visual scenes characterized by illumination drift, partial occlusion, adverse weather, and distributional shifts across imaging domains1. This fragility stems, in large part, from the tendency of supervised networks to latch onto superficial statistical shortcuts rather than learning semantically robust representations2. When the test environment deviates even moderately from training conditions—fog replacing clear skies, or a surveillance camera replacing a handheld one—accuracy can degrade sharply, sometimes catastrophically.

Two broad research threads have emerged to address this limitation. The first centers on domain generalization and domain adaptation. Domain adaptation methods attempt to align source and target feature distributions through adversarial training or moment matching, and have shown promise when unlabeled target data are accessible during training3. Domain generalization pushes further by demanding that models generalize to entirely unseen domains without any target-domain exposure4. Techniques such as meta-learning across synthetic domain splits5 and invariant risk minimization6 aim to extract domain-agnostic features, but their effectiveness hinges on the diversity and volume of labeled source domains—a requirement that is expensive and, for many practical scenarios, infeasible.

The second thread involves self-supervised learning, which seeks to learn transferable representations from unlabeled data alone. Early pretext tasks—predicting rotations, solving jigsaw puzzles—offered limited downstream benefit7. The arrival of contrastive learning frameworks, however, marked a turning point. SimCLR8 and MoCo9 demonstrated that pulling together augmented views of the same image while pushing apart views of different images could yield representations rivaling those of fully supervised pre-training on ImageNet. BYOL later removed the need for negative pairs altogether, and more recent work has explored multi-crop strategies and asymmetric architectures to further boost representation quality10. These advances suggest that contrastive objectives can capture visual invariances that supervised losses alone miss—an intuition that naturally connects to the domain generalization problem.

Despite these parallel advances, merging contrastive self-supervision with domain-invariant learning for complex scenes remains largely under-explored, and three core difficulties persist. First, standard contrastive augmentation pipelines—random cropping, color jittering, Gaussian blur—were designed for object-centric datasets and do not adequately simulate the structured distortions encountered in real-world scenes, such as spatially non-uniform fog, dynamic cast shadows, or sensor-specific noise patterns. Representations learned under generic augmentations therefore remain brittle to scene-level perturbations11. Second, most high-performing domain generalization methods still assume access to large-scale domain-labeled data, creating a tension between annotation cost and generalization breadth. Self-supervised approaches can, in principle, relieve this dependency, yet naively combining contrastive pre-training with downstream domain generalization often yields suboptimal results because the pre-trained features do not explicitly encode domain-invariance12. Third, aligning feature distributions across domains at a single spatial scale ignores the multi-granular nature of visual recognition: local texture cues and global layout information may shift differently across domains, and a single alignment objective cannot account for this heterogeneity13.

Our work is motivated by the conviction that these three difficulties are interrelated and should be tackled jointly rather than in isolation. Reducing reliance on domain labels calls for self-supervised objectives that are themselves domain-aware; improving robustness to scene-level distortions demands augmentation strategies informed by the physical structure of visual degradation; and achieving reliable cross-domain transfer requires feature alignment at multiple representational scales. We argue that a contrastive learning framework, if carefully redesigned with scene-level priors, can simultaneously address annotation efficiency, representational robustness, and domain-invariant alignment—goals that prior methods have pursued separately at best.

Concretely, this paper makes two principal contributions. We first propose a scene-aware contrastive learning strategy that moves beyond generic data augmentation by incorporating physically grounded scene transformations—simulating illumination gradients, weather artifacts, and occlusion patterns—as positive-pair generators within the contrastive objective. This encourages the encoder to treat such distortions as identity-preserving, directly embedding robustness into the learned representation without requiring any manual annotation. We then introduce a multi-scale domain-invariant feature alignment method that operates across hierarchical feature maps of a convolutional or vision-transformer backbone. Rather than collapsing all spatial information into a single global vector before alignment, our approach applies distribution matching at multiple resolutions, ensuring that both fine-grained texture invariances and coarse-grained structural invariances are simultaneously enforced14. Together, these two components form an end-to-end trainable framework that can be applied to diverse visual tasks—object detection, semantic segmentation, image classification—across domains with minimal or no labeled data.

Each of our two technical components has been designed to address a specific slice of this challenge space. The scene-aware contrastive augmentation simulates illumination drift, occlusion, and weather artifacts through physically grounded transformations, then forces the encoder, via the contrastive objective, to treat these degradations as identity-preserving—precisely the invariance behavior that brittle baselines fail to learn. The multi-scale domain-invariant feature alignment, paired with the cross-scale consistency regularizer, intercepts sensor-induced statistical drift at multiple depths of the encoder hierarchy rather than only at the final embedding, an architectural choice that matters because texture-level statistics (where sensor differences register most strongly) live in early layers while layout-level statistics live deeper. The difficulty-weighted contrastive loss then redirects gradient effort toward whichever degradation the encoder currently handles worst, so the framework does not implicitly trade robustness on one challenge category for robustness on another—a tradeoff we observed in preliminary tests with uniformly weighted contrastive variants.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section II reviews related work on contrastive self-supervised learning, domain generalization, and their intersections. Section III details the proposed scene-aware contrastive objective and multi-scale alignment mechanism. Section IV reports experiments on multiple cross-domain benchmarks under varying scene complexities, and Section V concludes with a discussion of limitations and future directions.

Theoretical foundations and technical background

Fundamentals of self-supervised contrastive learning

Self-supervised learning extracts supervisory signals from the data itself, bypassing the need for human annotation. Two broad paradigms coexist. Generative approaches—autoencoders, masked image modeling—reconstruct input pixels or patches and, in doing so, capture low-level statistical regularities15. Discriminative approaches instead define a proxy classification or matching task over transformed inputs; the model never reconstructs raw data but learns to distinguish among abstract relational properties. Contrastive learning belongs squarely to the discriminative family, and its central premise is disarmingly simple: given an anchor sample, pull its augmented counterpart (the positive) closer in embedding space while pushing all other samples (the negatives) apart16.

Over-parameterized networks can memorize domain-specific textures—a behavior that inflates source accuracy while eroding cross-domain transfer22. Feature representation methods for complex visual scenes

What makes a visual scene “complex” is not a single degradation but the co-occurrence of several. Drastic illumination swings can wash out texture gradients; large-area occlusion removes entire object parts from view; rain, haze, and snow inject spatially correlated noise; and cluttered backgrounds confuse figure-ground separation23. These factors rarely appear in isolation—a nighttime driving scene, for instance, combines low light, glare from oncoming headlights, and rain-streaked windshield artifacts simultaneously. Any representation that is robust to one degradation type but blind to others will inevitably break down in such compound conditions.

Multi-scale feature extraction has been the dominant architectural response to scene complexity. Spatial attention variants extend this idea to selectively amplify informative regions while suppressing background clutter26.

Yet a tension persists between scene diversity and feature robustness. Multi-scale fusion and attention can adapt to within-domain variation, but they lack explicit constraints that enforce invariance across domains. When trained on clear-weather imagery, attention maps learn to weight features that are discriminative under clear conditions—sharp edges, saturated colors—and these same cues become unreliable or absent in degraded scenes. The missing ingredient, from our perspective, is twofold: scene-structural priors that inform the model about how visual content degrades, and domain-invariant regularization that penalizes features drifting across environments27. Our proposed framework addresses precisely this gap by coupling scene-aware contrastive augmentation (to embed structural priors) with multi-scale distribution alignment (to enforce cross-domain invariance), as detailed in Section III.

Recent dense-prediction work has explored a related but distinct angle. Rather than augmenting input pixels, these methods restructure how token-level representations are linked across domains inside vision foundation models. Liao and colleagues investigate this in three complementary directions: multi-granularity token learning, where tokens at different abstraction levels are jointly optimised so the network can capture coarse layout cues and fine semantic details at the same time49; cross-domain token linking, where text-guided alignment exposes invariant tokens hidden inside otherwise frozen backbones50; and concept-guided generalization, where semantic concept tokens are used to calibrate the model’s predictive uncertainty across unseen environments51.

These approaches are formulated for semantic segmentation and operate on transformer token sequences—a different architectural substrate from our convolutional, classification-oriented setting. The shared insight, however, is instructive. Dense-prediction generalization benefits from explicit hierarchical structure in feature space, which aligns with the motivation behind our multi-scale alignment design while suggesting an obvious avenue for cross-architecture extension. We do not claim to subsume these methods; rather, we view them as complementary, and we return briefly to the question of porting our objectives onto token-based backbones in Section V.

Proposed method for generalization enhancement via self-supervised contrastive learning

The first module—scene-aware data augmentation—takes raw images from the source domains and synthesizes degraded views through physically motivated transformations: spatially varying illumination shifts, procedural fog and rain overlays, and structured occlusion masks. Unlike standard augmentation pipelines that apply transformations uniformly and independently, this module chains degradations in a manner that respects their co-occurrence statistics in real scenes29. Each input image produces two augmented views that serve as positive pairs for the subsequent contrastive objective.

These view pairs feed into the contrastive feature encoding module, which follows a dual-branch architecture: an online encoder \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{f}_{\theta\:}$$\end{document} with a projection head \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{g}_{\theta\:}$$\end{document}, and a momentum encoder \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{f}_{\xi\:}$$\end{document} updated via exponential moving average as in Eq. (3). The contrastive loss (Eq. (1)) operates on the projected embeddings, training \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{f}_{\theta\:}$$\end{document} to map scene-degraded variants of the same image to neighboring points in representation space. Because the augmentation module explicitly simulates domain-relevant distortions, the invariances learned here are not generic—they are tuned to the specific degradation types that drive cross-domain divergence8.

The third module—multi-scale domain-invariant feature alignment—operates on intermediate feature maps extracted from multiple layers of the encoder, not just the final embedding. At each selected scale l, a distribution matching loss (MMD or Wasserstein-based, as discussed in Sect. 2.2) penalizes discrepancies between feature distributions originating from different source domains30. This multi-resolution alignment ensures that domain invariance is enforced at both local texture-level and global layout-level representations simultaneously, addressing the single-scale limitation identified in our earlier analysis.

During training, the three losses—contrastive, alignment, and an optional supervised classification term when labels are available—are combined with balancing coefficients and optimized end-to-end through the online encoder. At inference time, only the trained encoder \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{f}_{\theta\:}$$\end{document} is retained; the momentum branch, projection heads, and alignment modules are discarded entirely, so the computational overhead at deployment is zero. This design deliberately separates the representation-learning machinery, which is complex, from the inference path, which remains lightweight—a practical consideration we regard as essential for real-world deployment.

To make the role of the second module fully explicit, the contrastive feature encoding module is the bridge that turns scene-augmented inputs into the feature substrate on which both objectives operate. Scene-aware contrastive learning strategy

Standard contrastive frameworks treat all positive pairs identically—an image and its color-jittered copy receive the same loss weight regardless of how challenging the transformation is for the encoder. This uniform treatment becomes problematic in complex visual scenes, where certain degradation types (dense fog, extreme backlighting) distort features far more aggressively than others (mild brightness shifts). A model trained without awareness of this asymmetry tends to converge toward invariances that are easy to learn while neglecting the hard cases that matter most for cross-domain transfer31. Our scene-aware contrastive learning strategy addresses this imbalance at two levels: the construction of positive and negative pairs, and the weighting of their contributions to the loss.

Figure 3 presents the complete pipeline of the proposed strategy, from scene-conditioned pair generation through difficulty-weighted contrastive optimization.

A difficulty estimator computes per-pair adaptive weights that upweight hard scene transformations in the contrastive loss.

Given an image \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{x}_{i}$$\end{document} with semantic label (or pseudo-label) \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{y}_{i}$$\end{document}, we generate two views \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{x}_{i}^{a}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{x}_{i}^{b}$$\end{document} by applying scene transformations drawn from different degradation categories—for instance, one view under synthetic haze and the other under simulated low-light. The positive set \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathcal{P}\left(i\right)$$\end{document} consists of all pairs sharing the same semantic identity but subjected to distinct scene conditions, while the negative set \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathcal{N}\left(i\right)$$\end{document} gathers embeddings from semantically different instances regardless of their scene attributes32. Not all positive pairs, however, deserve equal attention. We introduce a difficulty-aware weighting mechanism that amplifies the gradient signal from pairs the model currently finds hard to align. For each positive pair \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:({z}_{i},{z}_{j}^{+})$$\end{document}, we define the difficulty score as the inverse of their current similarity (proposed formulation):9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{d}_{ij}=1-\mathrm{s}\mathrm{i}\mathrm{m}({z}_{i},{z}_{j}^{+})$$\end{document}

As we show in Section IV, this adaptive emphasis translates into measurable gains on the hardest cross-domain evaluation splits, where baseline contrastive methods tend to plateau.

Multi-scale feature alignment and domain-invariant representation learning

The scene-aware contrastive loss from Sect. 3.2 encourages the encoder to be invariant to scene degradations, but it operates solely on the final projected embeddings. Domain-specific information, however, can persist in intermediate feature maps—texture statistics at early layers, spatial layout patterns at deeper ones—and if left unconstrained, these residual domain cues propagate to the output and undermine transfer35. We therefore impose explicit distributional alignment at multiple scales within the encoder hierarchy.

Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{F}_{l}^{s}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{F}_{l}^{a}$$\end{document} denote the feature maps at layer l extracted from the original source view and its scene-augmented counterpart, respectively. We treat the augmented views as proxy target domains, since they simulate the distributional shift that would occur under real scene variations. At each of L selected layers, we apply an MMD-based alignment loss that penalizes the discrepancy between these two feature populations. Following the kernel embedding framework of Eq. where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\phi ( \cdot )$$\end{document} maps spatially pooled feature vectors into a reproducing kernel Hilbert space via a Gaussian kernel, and N is the batch size. To prevent this semantic drift across scales, we add a cross-scale consistency regularizer. For each pair of adjacent scales (l,l+1), we project their spatially pooled features to a shared dimensionality through lightweight linear projectors \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{h}_{l}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{h}_{l+1}$$\end{document}, then penalize their cosine disagreement (proposed formulation):14\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{C}\mathrm{S}}=\sum\:_{l=1}^{L-1}\left(1-\frac{1}{N}\sum\:_{i=1}^{N}\mathrm{s}\mathrm{i}\mathrm{m}\left({h}_{l}\left({F}_{l,i}^{s}\right),{h}_{l+1}\left({F}_{l+1,i}^{s}\right)\right)\right)$$\end{document}

This term encourages adjacent layers to encode domain-invariant information that is semantically compatible, without forcing them to be identical—an important distinction, since each scale should retain its own granularity36.

Experiments and result analysis

Experimental setup and benchmark comparison

We evaluate the proposed framework on two complementary benchmarks. The first is PACS, a widely adopted domain generalization dataset containing 9,991 images across four domains—Photo, Art Painting, Cartoon, and Sketch—spanning seven object categories38. The second is a cross-domain scene recognition benchmark assembled from subsets of BDD100K and Cityscapes, which we refer to as BDD-City. BDD100K provides images captured under diverse weather and lighting conditions (clear, rainy, foggy, nighttime), while Cityscapes offers high-resolution urban scenes from a single clear-weather domain39. Together, these two benchmarks test both object-level and scene-level generalization under distributional shift.

For PACS, we follow the standard leave-one-domain-out protocol: three domains serve as source and the held-out domain as target. On BDD-City, BDD-Clear is the primary source domain, with the remaining domains treated as unseen targets. All images are resized to the resolutions listed in Table 1, normalized with ImageNet statistics, and subjected to random horizontal flipping during training. The scene-aware augmentation pipeline (Sect. 3.2) generates additional degraded views on the fly; no offline augmentation is stored.

Our backbone is ResNet-50 pretrained on ImageNet, with multi-scale features extracted from the outputs of residual blocks 2, 3, and 4 for the alignment module. The projection head g( · ) consists of a two-layer MLP with hidden dimension 2048 and output dimension 128. We train for 100 epochs using SGD with momentum 0.9, an initial learning rate of 0.03 decayed by cosine annealing, and batch size 64 per source domain. All experiments run on four NVIDIA A100 GPUs, and each configuration is repeated three times with different random seeds; we report mean accuracy and standard deviation.

We compare against seven baselines spanning five categories: a supervised baseline (ERM), self-supervised pre-training (SimCLR8, MoCo v29), classical domain generalization (CORAL40, Mixstyle41), domain adaptation (DANN3), and a recent 2025 dynamic decision boundary method (DDB-DG52). The inclusion of DDB-DG ensures that the comparison reflects current state-of-the-art on this task rather than relying solely on older baselines. As presented in Table 2, our method achieves the highest accuracy across all target domains on both benchmarks. The average gain over the strongest pre-2025 baseline (Mixstyle) on PACS is 3.5% points, and the margin widens on BDD-City, where the domain shifts are driven by genuine scene degradations rather than artistic style changes. The BDD-Night column is particularly telling: our method outperforms ERM by over 14 points and DANN by nearly 5 points, confirming that the scene-aware contrastive strategy concentrates learning on the hardest conditions.

The comparison set al.so includes a representative 2025 domain generalization method—DDB-DG, a dynamic decision boundary approach with prototype-guided multi-level prediction and an uncertainty-style mixture augmentation52—re-implemented under our backbone and training budget. With this recent baseline present, the gap relative to the older methods naturally narrows. Even so, our framework leads on every column, by roughly 2.4 points on PACS average and 3 to 4 points on each BDD-City target. The pattern suggests that the gain comes from the scene-physics-aware augmentation design rather than from raw architectural complexity: DDB-DG, like the other recent baselines surveyed, does not explicitly model the distributional shift produced by physical degradation, so the residual gap concentrates precisely where our scene-conditioned positives carry the most information. We note also that several other 2025 advances in this space target semantic segmentation rather than classification49–51, and therefore could not be ported into the present table without substantial architectural changes; that extension is discussed briefly in Section V.

The results in Table 4 expose a clear hierarchy. Removing the contrastive module altogether (A) inflicts the largest single drop—3.2 points—confirming that contrastive learning is the backbone of our framework rather than an auxiliary add-on. Swapping scene-aware augmentation for generic transforms (B) costs 2.1 points, which tells us that what the contrastive objective is made invariant to matters almost as much as whether contrastive learning is present at all. An interaction effect is also visible: components (C) and (E) together account for 3.0 points in isolation, yet the actual gap between variant (A) and the full model is 3.2 points for the contrastive branch alone—suggesting that alignment and weighting amplify each other rather than contributing additively45.

Discussion

Why does the proposed framework generalize better than methods that treat contrastive learning and domain alignment as separate concerns? The answer, we believe, lies in how the two objectives reshape the loss landscape in complementary ways. Standard contrastive learning enforces invariance to whatever augmentation pipeline generates the positive pairs—but if those augmentations bear no structural resemblance to the domain shifts encountered at test time, the learned invariances are, in a sense, misdirected. Our scene-aware augmentation bridges this gap by grounding positive-pair generation in the physics of visual degradation: fog gradients, illumination falloff, rain streaks. The contrastive loss then treats these degradations as identity-preserving transformations, which is equivalent to telling the encoder that scene condition is a nuisance variable to be discarded. The difficulty weighting further sharpens this instruction by ensuring that the encoder does not simply ignore the hardest degradations because easier ones dominate the gradient. The net effect, visible in the t-SNE plots of Fig. 8, is a feature space organized by semantic content rather than domain membership—precisely the geometry predicted by the generalization bound in Eq. (4), where minimizing inter-domain divergence while maintaining low source error tightens the bound on target risk.

The multi-scale alignment mechanism adds a second, orthogonal pressure. Domain-specific information does not reside at a single depth in the network; low-level texture statistics (color histograms, edge frequency spectra) concentrate in early layers, while higher-level layout biases emerge deeper47. A single alignment loss applied to the final representation can suppress global domain cues but leave local texture shortcuts intact—a residual that propagates through skip connections or lateral pathways and reappears in the prediction. By imposing MMD penalties at three distinct resolutions, our method intercepts domain-specific signals at their point of origin rather than waiting for them to compound. The cross-scale consistency term (Eq. (14)) then ensures that the invariances enforced at each depth do not contradict one another, preventing the pathological case where one layer discards a feature that another layer retains as discriminative.

Several limitations warrant candid acknowledgment. First, our scene-aware augmentation pipeline is built on a finite library of degradation models—fog, rain, illumination shift, occlusion—and these models, however physically motivated, are approximations. Degradation types not represented in the library (sensor defocus, motion blur from ego-vehicle vibration, or entirely novel imaging artifacts) fall outside the contrastive invariance envelope, and the model has no mechanism to generalize to them without retraining. We observed hints of this boundary in preliminary tests on snow-corrupted images, where performance dropped by roughly 4 points relative to fog at matched severity, suggesting that the augmentation library is a genuine bottleneck rather than a theoretical one. Second, the framework currently relies on a convolutional backbone (ResNet-50), and we have not validated it on vision transformers, where the multi-scale alignment strategy would need rethinking due to the different spatial structure of intermediate representations. Whether the same MMD-based alignment can operate effectively on patch token sequences is an open question that we plan to investigate.

Comparing our approach to existing domain generalization methods such as CORAL and Mixstyle reveals a philosophical difference. Those methods operate on pre-extracted features and assume that style statistics (mean, variance) capture the domain shift. This assumption holds well for artistic style transfer but breaks down when the domain shift is driven by physical scene changes—fog does not merely alter feature statistics; it selectively erases high-frequency content in a depth-dependent manner. Our contrastive formulation sidesteps the need to model how statistics change by instead learning what should remain constant across changes, a more agnostic strategy that makes fewer assumptions about the nature of the shift48.

Looking forward, the framework’s modularity suggests several natural extensions. The scene-aware augmentation module could be replaced with a learned generative model that synthesises novel degradation types from data, removing the reliance on hand-crafted physical models. The multi-scale alignment is backbone-agnostic in principle and could extend to transformer architectures by operating on attention maps at different depths—an extension that connects naturally to recent token-based generalization methods such as multi-granularity token learning49, cross-domain token linking50, and concept-guided generalization51, all of which exploit the explicit hierarchical structure of transformer representations and could plausibly be combined with our scene-physics-aware augmentation policy. Task-wise, nothing in our formulation is specific to classification—object detection and semantic segmentation involve the same encoder features and could benefit from identical contrastive and alignment objectives, provided the positive-pair construction respects spatial semantics. We view the current work as a proof of concept for a broader principle: that contrastive self-supervision, when informed by the structure of real-world visual variation, can serve as a general-purpose robustness mechanism for deep visual models.

Conclusion

This paper addressed the problem of deep model generalization under complex visual scenes—conditions where illumination drift, occlusion, adverse weather, and sensor variation conspire to defeat representations learned from clean source data. We proposed two tightly integrated techniques. The first, a scene-aware contrastive learning strategy, replaces generic augmentation with physically grounded scene transformations and introduces an adaptive difficulty weighting that steers the contrastive gradient toward the hardest degradation conditions. The second, a multi-scale domain-invariant feature alignment method, imposes distributional matching at multiple depths within the encoder and couples these per-scale constraints through a cross-scale consistency regularizer. Together, these components form an end-to-end framework that requires no target-domain data and minimal—or zero—annotation.

Experiments on PACS and BDD-City confirmed that the framework outperforms six representative baselines spanning supervised, self-supervised, domain generalization, and domain adaptation categories. The average cross-domain accuracy gain over the strongest competitor reached 3.5 points on PACS and exceeded 4 points on BDD-City. More telling than the averages, though, was the pattern of improvement: gains concentrated on the most challenging conditions—severe fog, nighttime—where baseline methods suffered the steepest drops. The ablation study verified that each module contributes independently and that their interaction is synergistic rather than merely additive, with the scene-aware contrastive branch and the multi-scale alignment together accounting for the bulk of the performance lift.

The principal novelty of this work, as we see it, is conceptual as much as technical. Prior contrastive learning methods designed augmentation pipelines for representation quality on curated benchmarks; prior domain generalization methods assumed access to domain labels and operated on pre-extracted features. Our framework bridges these two traditions by treating physically structured scene degradation as the augmentation policy itself, so that the invariances learned by contrastive pre-training directly target the distributional shifts responsible for cross-domain failure. The multi-scale alignment adds a second layer of insurance by catching domain-specific signals that the contrastive loss alone cannot suppress. From a theoretical standpoint, this joint design tightens the generalization bound of Eq. (4) along two axes simultaneously—reducing the inter-domain divergence term through alignment and lowering the source risk term through richer, scene-conditioned representations.

On the practical side, the framework’s inference path retains only the trained encoder, incurring no additional computational cost at deployment. This property matters for resource-constrained settings such as autonomous driving and outdoor surveillance, where complex scenes are the rule rather than the exception and latency budgets are tight.

We are candid about what remains unsolved. The current augmentation library covers a finite set of degradation types; truly open-world deployment would require the model to handle scene corruptions it has never encountered during training—a meta-generalization challenge that our fixed library cannot address. Dynamic scenes introduce temporal dependencies (changing weather mid-sequence, moving occlusions) that our frame-level approach ignores entirely. Computational efficiency during training also deserves attention: the alternating optimization and multi-scale MMD computation add overhead that, while manageable on modern hardware, could become prohibitive when scaling to very large datasets or backbone architectures.

Several promising directions follow naturally. Replacing the hand-crafted degradation library with a learned generative model—conditioned, perhaps, on textual scene descriptors from a vision-language foundation model—could expand the diversity of contrastive augmentations far beyond what physics-based simulation permits. Extending the framework to video understanding tasks, where temporal coherence provides an additional supervisory signal for contrastive learning, is another avenue we find compelling. Finally, adapting the multi-scale alignment to operate on patch-token representations within vision transformers would test whether the core principles hold beyond convolutional architectures and open the door to integration with large-scale pre-trained models. We hope that the ideas and evidence presented here encourage further work at the intersection of self-supervised learning and domain-robust visual recognition52.
