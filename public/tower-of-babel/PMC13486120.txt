MZB1 Modulates Inflammatory Severity in Severe Acute Pancreatitis through an IgA-Associated Intestinal Barrier Axis
Authors: Cong Chen, Guohui Xiao, Kaige Yang, Xujie Han, Yaqing Yang, Tong Zhou, Rongli Xie, Dan Xu, Jian Fei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Severe acute pancreatitis (SAP) is characterized by excessive inflammatory responses that critically influence disease severity and systemic complications. Although innate immune activation has been extensively studied in SAP, the contribution of adaptive humoral immunity to inflammatory modulation during disease progression remains incompletely understood. In this study, we performed longitudinal single-cell RNA sequencing of peripheral blood mononuclear cells from patients with mild and severe acute pancreatitis to characterize immune alterations across disease stages. We observed dynamic immune remodeling, including increased engagement of B cell–associated humoral immune programs during later stages of severe disease. Among these changes, expression of the endoplasmic reticulum–resident protein MZB1 was prominently associated with plasma cell differentiation and immunoglobulin-related transcriptional activity. To investigate the functional relevance of MZB1, we employed genetic mouse models of experimental pancreatitis. Loss of MZB1 was associated with exacerbated pancreatic inflammation, impaired IgA production, and compromised intestinal barrier integrity. Together, these findings suggest that MZB1-associated humoral immune responses contribute to modulation of inflammatory severity in severe acute pancreatitis by supporting IgA-dependent intestinal barrier integrity and limiting secondary macrophage-driven inflammation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10753-026-02534-2.

Introduction

Severe acute pancreatitis (SAP) is a life-threatening inflammatory disorder characterized by rapid disease progression, systemic inflammatory response syndrome, and a high risk of multiorgan failure [1, 2]. Despite advances in supportive care, SAP remains associated with substantial morbidity and mortality, underscoring the need for improved therapeutic strategies. Excessive systemic inflammation secondary to pancreatic injury is a major determinant of poor outcomes in SAP [2, 3]. In addition, SAP is frequently complicated by infectious events, including bacterial translocation, infected pancreatic necrosis, and sepsis. Disruption of intestinal mucosal barrier integrity facilitates the translocation of bacteria and microbe-derived products, thereby further amplifying systemic inflammation and organ injury [4, 5]. However, the mechanisms governing inflammatory amplification and resolution remain incompletely understood.

Previous studies have primarily focused on innate immune activation in SAP, particularly the roles of monocytes, macrophages, and pro-inflammatory cytokine cascades [6, 7]. While these responses contribute to early tissue injury, persistent or inadequately resolved inflammation is associated with ongoing pancreatic damage and secondary organ dysfunction. In contrast, the contribution of adaptive immune responses has been less extensively characterized, and the dynamic regulation of B cells, plasma cell differentiation, and immunoglobulin production during the course of SAP remains to be further elucidated.

Emerging evidence suggests that B lymphocytes actively participate in pancreatic inflammation and tissue remodeling. Lee et al. demonstrated that aberrant accumulation of B cells impairs pancreatic tissue regeneration and exacerbates injury in experimental pancreatitis [8]. In addition, Takahashi et al. reported that immune suppression mediated by B cells promotes pancreatic tumorigenesis in disease contexts driven by inflammation [9]. These findings highlight a functional role of B cells in regulating pancreatic inflammation and disease outcomes. However, the mechanisms by which B cells modulate the severity and progression of acute pancreatitis remain largely unknown, particularly in the context of humoral immune regulation and mucosal barrier homeostasis.

MZB1, also known as pERP1, is an endoplasmic reticulum–resident protein co-chaperone predominantly expressed in marginal zone B cells, B1 cells, and plasma cells. It plays an important role in immunoglobulin folding and secretion, particularly for IgM and IgA, and has also been linked to integrin function, calcium homeostasis, and unfolded protein response signaling [10–12]. Recent studies further suggest that MZB1 is involved in mucosal immunity and inflammatory disease, as it promotes dimeric IgA secretion, limits gut inflammation, and is aberrantly expressed in several autoimmune and inflammatory conditions [13–15]. These findings suggest that MZB1 may function not only as a plasma cell–associated secretory factor but also as a broader regulator of immune and inflammatory homeostasis. In our previous work, we identified an expansion of MZB1 positive plasma cells associated with SAP disease stage [16]. However, whether MZB1 mediated humoral immune responses functionally contribute to disease progression or resolution in SAP remains unclear.

In the present study, we performed longitudinal single-cell RNA sequencing of peripheral blood mononuclear cells from patients with mild and severe acute pancreatitis to characterize dynamic immune alterations across disease stages. We observed enhanced activation of B cell associated humoral immune programs during later stages of severe disease, with MZB1 emerging as a key regulator linked to plasma cell differentiation and immunoglobulin production. Using genetic mouse models, we further demonstrate that MZB1 deficiency leads to impaired IgA production, disruption of intestinal barrier integrity, and exacerbated pancreatic inflammation. Together, our findings suggest a mechanism by which MZB1-associated IgA responses regulate intestinal barrier integrity, thereby linking humoral immunity to innate inflammatory amplification in SAP.

Materials and Methods

Sample Collection

Peripheral blood samples were collected from patients with acute pancreatitis (AP) and healthy donors after written informed consent. This study was approved by the Ruijin Hospital Ethics Committee affiliated with the Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine. All participants enrolled in this trial provided informed written consent (2019-90). Patients were classified into mild AP (MAP), moderately severe AP and severe AP (SAP) according to the revised Atlanta classification and modified Marshall scoring system [17]. Exclusion criteria included chronic pancreatitis, malignancy, autoimmune diseases, and recent immunosuppressive therapy. For each participant, 10–20 mL of peripheral blood was drawn into EDTA-coated tubes, processed within 2 h to minimize transcriptomic degradation. Clinical information including age, sex, disease severity, and comorbidities was recorded. All evaluations and histopathological scoring were performed in a blinded manner.

PBMCs Preparation

Peripheral blood samples were collected at multiple time points following hospital admission. For the majority of cases, longitudinal samples were obtained from the same patients at different disease stages. In addition, a subset of samples was derived from different patients at specific time points due to clinical availability constraints.

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated from anticoagulant-treated blood using Ficoll-Paque density gradient centrifugation (17–1440-03; GE Healthcare Pharmacia, Little Chalfont, UK). The blood was diluted with RPMI 1640 medium (22400089; Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA). The diluted blood mixture was carefully layered onto Ficoll‒Paque Plus and centrifuged without braking at 800 × g for 20 min at 25 °C. The middle layer that was acquired was transferred to a sterile centrifuge tube and centrifuged at 600 × g for a duration of 10 min. The PBMCs were collected and kept in liquid nitrogen for further examination after the supernatant was removed.

scRNA-seq Library Preparation and Sequencing

For single-cell sequencing, PBMCs from patients in the M group (6 samples collected on day 1, 5 samples collected on day 3, and 4 samples collected on day 7 postadmission), the S group (6 samples collected on day 1 and day 3, and 5 samples collected on day 7 postadmission) and 2 healthy controls were randomly selected. An additional 10 healthy controls were obtained from the datasets GSE168732 and E-GEAD-635 [18, 19]. Detailed donor information and sampling structure are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Single-cell suspensions were loaded onto the Chromium Controller (10x Genomics, v3 chemistry) targeting ~ 6,000 cells per channel. cDNA libraries were constructed using the Chromium Single Cell 3′ Reagent Kit (v3.1, 10x Genomics) following manufacturer instructions. Library quality was assessed using Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer and Qubit 2.0. Sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (paired-end 150 bp).

scRNA-seq Data Processing

Raw sequencing data were processed using Cell Ranger (v9.0.1, 10x Genomics) against the GRCh38 reference genome (version “2024-A”). Cells with fewer than 500 detected genes, more than 20% mitochondrial gene expression, or more than 10% hemoglobin gene expression were excluded. Doublets were identified and removed using DoubletFinder (v2.0; expected doublet rate 5–7%). Ambient RNA contamination was corrected using SoupX (v1.6).

Filtered count matrices were analyzed using Seurat (v5) [20]. Data were normalized using the SCTransform workflow. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed using RunPCA, and the top 20 principal components were used to construct a shared nearest neighbor graph with FindNeighbors (dims = 1:20). Cell clustering was performed using FindClusters with a resolution of 0.5, and dimensionality reduction was visualized using t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (tSNE).

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test implemented in Seurat, with Benjamini–Hochberg correction applied for multiple testing (|log₂ fold change| ≥ 0.25, adjusted P < 0.05). Cell-type annotation was performed using SingleR (v2.4.1) and validated based on the expression of canonical marker genes [21]. Functional enrichment analyses were conducted using clusterProfiler (v4.10.0) [22].

To analyze dynamic transcriptional programs across disease stages, pseudo-bulk expression profiles were generated for defined cell populations by averaging SCTransform-normalized expression values across cells within each cell population and disease stage. The resulting expression matrices were standardized, and time-associated gene modules were identified using Mfuzz (v2.66.0) based on the defined disease-stage ordering.

Public scRNA-seq datasets of human peripheral blood (GSE168732 and E-GEAD-635) were downloaded and integrated with our cohort. Preprocessing followed the same pipeline described above, and batch effects across datasets were corrected using Harmony prior to downstream analyses [23].

Cell Type Annotation and B Cell Subset Identification

B cell subsets annotation was performed using SingleR (v2.4.1), by comparing single-cell transcriptomes to established immune reference datasets. For peripheral blood mononuclear cells, the Monaco Immune Data reference was used as the primary annotation framework. Predicted labels were assigned to individual cells based on correlation scores, and low-confidence assignments were flagged for further inspection.

Animal Experiments

Mzb1−/− mice were generated by CRISPR/Cas9-mediated deletion of exons 2–4 and maintained on a C57BL/6J background. Heterozygous breeders (Mzb1+/− × Mzb1+/−) were used to generate experimental cohorts. Unless otherwise specified, wild-type littermates (Mzb1+/+) derived from the same litters were used as controls. Mice were 8–10 weeks of age at the time of experiments. Sex was not included as a biological variable in statistical analyses. Animals were randomly assigned to experimental groups, and outcome assessment was performed in a blinded manner when feasible. All mice were acquired from Shanghai GemPharmatech Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). The mice had unrestricted access to food and water, were housed in an environment with regulated humidity (40–60%) and temperature (22–25℃), and were maintained in a 12-hour light-dark cycle. All experimental procedures received approval from the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Shanghai Jiao Tong University.

Mouse Genotyping Strategy

The genotyping of MZB1 knockout mice was performed using PCR-based detection. Four primers were designed to distinguish between the wild-type and knockout alleles of the MZB1 gene, and two primer pairs were used for genotyping, as summarized in Supplementary Table 2. PCR products were analyzed by gel electrophoresis. Using primer pair 1, the wild-type allele produced a 3272 bp product, whereas the knockout allele produced a shorter 363 bp product. Primer pair 2 specifically targeted the deleted region encompassing exons 2–4. In wild-type mice, this primer pair generated a 394 bp band, whereas no product was detected in knockout mice because the intact target region was absent. Mice were genotyped based on the presence or absence of these specific PCR bands, allowing reliable identification of wild-type (WT), heterozygous (HT), and homozygous knockout (KO) mice.

Animal Experimental Pancreatitis Models

Mouse models of experimental pancreatitis were induced by hourly intraperitoneal injections of cerulein (50 µg/kg; CAS 17650-98-5; C9026, Sigma-Aldrich, Germany) for 12 consecutive doses. For survival experiments, mice received seven hourly intraperitoneal injections of cerulein (100 µg/kg), followed by a single intraperitoneal injection of lipopolysaccharide (LPS; 10 mg/kg; CAS 93572-42-0; L2880, Sigma-Aldrich, Germany) 1 h after the final cerulein dose. Control animals received saline matched in volume and schedule to cerulein injections and did not receive LPS. At 24 h after the first injection (cerulein or saline), animals were either euthanized for tissue collection (non-survival cohort) or continued under survival follow-up (survival cohort).

Pancreatic Edema Assessment

Pancreatic edema was quantified on hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-stained pancreatic sections using Fiji software (ImageJ, NIH). For each sample, representative microscopic fields were captured under identical imaging settings. The vacuolated or low-staining area within the pancreatic section, corresponding to edematous spaces, was manually delineated and measured using Fiji. The edema ratio was calculated as the percentage of the vacuolated area relative to the total pancreatic tissue area within the same field. At least three non-overlapping fields were analyzed per section, and the mean value for each sample was used for statistical analysis. Image analysis was performed in a blinded manner.

Plasma Cell Isolation and Adoptive Transfer

Plasma cells were isolated from wild type mouse spleen using the CD138+ Plasma Cell Isolation Kit (130-092-530, Miltenyi Biotec, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, a single-cell suspension was prepared from spleen tissue, and non-plasma cells were first depleted using the Non-Plasma Cell Depletion Cocktail and Anti-Biotin MicroBeads. The enriched fraction was then labeled with CD138 MicroBeads and subjected to positive magnetic selection to obtain highly purified CD138+ plasma cells. For adoptive transfer, 1 × 107 plasma cells in 100 µL PBS were administered to each recipient mouse via tail vein injection 24 h prior to induction of cerulein + LPS-induced severe acute pancreatitis [24, 25].

Antibiotic Treatment

To reduce gut microbial burden, mice were provided with drinking water containing a broad-spectrum antibiotic cocktail for 3 weeks [26]. The antibiotic cocktail consisted of ampicillin (1 g/L, ST007, Beyotime, China), neomycin (1 g/L, ST2533, Beyotime, China), metronidazole (1 g/L, Y175738, Beyotime, China), and vancomycin (0.5 g/L, ST2807, Beyotime, China), prepared in sterile drinking water. Control mice received regular sterile drinking water. The effectiveness of antibiotic treatment was evaluated by measuring total bacterial DNA levels in intestinal contents/fecal samples.

Detailed Method of Oral sIgA Administration

Purified human secretory immunoglobulin A (sIgA; HY-NP0227, MedChemExpress, China) was used for oral supplementation in mice. Prior to each administration, mice were fasted for 6 h to ensure optimal delivery [27]. sIgA was dissolved in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and administered at a dose of 500 µg per mouse using a soft-tipped oral gavage needle. Oral gavage was performed once daily for three consecutive days before induction of pancreatitis.

Bulk RNA-seq Library Preparation and Sequencing

Total RNA was isolated from the pancreata employing the Universal RNA Extraction CZ Kit (RNC643, ONREW). The RNA concentration was determined with a Qubit 4.0 Fluorometer (Invitrogen), while its quality was assessed through electrophoresis conducted on a denaturing agarose gel. RNA libraries were constructed using the VVAHTS® Universal V8 RNA-seq Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NR605-0, Vazyme) and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform utilizing a 150-bp paired-end sequencing approach.

Bulk RNA-seq Data Processing

The raw data were processed using Skewer (version 0.2.2), while FastQC (version 0.11.2) [28] was employed to assess the data quality. The read length measured 2 × 150 bp. The cleaned reads were mapped to the mouse genome (mm10) from Ensembl using STAR (https://github.com/alexdobin/STAR), permitting a single mismatch [29]. Gene expression data were analyzed with StringTie (version 1.3.1c), and the DESeq2 package (version 1.42.1) was utilized for the analysis of differential gene expression. Differential expression was analyzed with DESeq2 with thresholds |log2FC| ≥1.5 and FDR < 0.05. Further investigation of these DEGs was conducted for functional enrichment and signaling pathway analysis with the TopGO package (version 4.4), the KEGG database (https://www.genome.jp/kegg/pathway.html), and the clusterProfiler package (version 4.10.0). A threshold of P < 0.05 was used to identify pathways that were significantly enriched.

Western Blot

Pancreatic tissues and small-intestinal tissues from mice were homogenized in ice-cold RIPA buffer supplemented with protease/phosphatase inhibitor cocktails. Lysates were cleared by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, and protein concentration was determined using a BCA assay. Equal amounts of protein (50 µg per lane) were mixed with 1× Laemmli sample buffer containing 50 mM DTT, denatured at 95 °C for 5 min, separated by SDS–PAGE (10–12% gels), and transferred to PVDF membranes (0.45 μm). Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.6; 150 mM NaCl; 0.1% Tween-20) for 1 h at room temperature, then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies: for pancreas: rabbit anti-MZB1 (12289-1-AP; Proteintech, Wuhan, China; 1:1000), rabbit anti-FPR1 (ab113531; Abcam, Cambridge, UK; 1:1000). For small intestine tissues: rabbit anti-E-cadherin (20874-1-AP; Proteintech; 1:1000), rabbit anti-Occludin (27260-1-AP; Proteintech; 1:1000), rabbit anti-Claudin-1 (28674-1-AP; Proteintech; 1:1000), and anti-rabbit-F4/80 (70076; CST, MA, USA; 1:1000). mouse anti-GAPDH (60004-1-Ig; Proteintech; 1:5000). After washing in TBST, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature: peroxidase-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) (111-035-144; Jackson ImmunoResearch, PA, USA; 1:10,000) and peroxidase-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) (111-035-003; Jackson ImmunoResearch, PA, USA; 1:10000).

Signals were developed using enhanced chemiluminescence reagents (Potent ECL kit; MultiSciences Biotech, Hangzhou, China; P1425) and captured with a G: BOX chemiluminescence imaging system. Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ, with target proteins normalized to GAPDH. At least three independent biological replicates were analyzed.

Immunofluorescence

For immunofluorescence analysis, the tissues sections were deparaffinized using Eco-friendly Deparaffinization Solution (1, 2, and 3) for 10 min each, rehydrated in graded ethanol (absolute, 95%, and 75% ethanol, 5 min each), and rinsed for three minutes each time with distilled water. Antigen retrieval was carried out using a pressure cooker in an EDTA buffer (pH 9.0). Tissue sections were immersed in boiling buffer, heated under pressure for 1.5 min, allowed to cool to room temperature, and rinsed three times with TBST. To inhibit the activity of endogenous peroxidase, the sections were treated with a solution of 3% H2O2 for 10 min, afterward undergoing further TBST washes. The sections were exposed to 10% goat serum for 0.5 h in order to avoid non-specific binding.

For the first primary antibody incubation, F4/80 (70076; CST, MA, USA), IgJ (13688-1-AP; Proteintech, Wuhan, China), Perilipin (9349 S; CST MA, USA), MZB1(12289-1-AP; Proteintech, Wuhan, China), IgA (ab97234, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), FPR1 (ab113531; Abcam, Cambridge, UK) antibody was applied and incubated overnight at 4℃. After washing, secondary antibody (Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG H&L [HRP], 1:4000) was applied at 37℃ for 45 min, followed by iFluor® 488 tyramide staining (1:300) and CY5 tyramide staining (1:400) for 10 min at 25℃. For nuclear staining, DAPI (1:500) was utilized for 5 min in the absence of light. Finally, sections were mounted with fluorescence medium, stored at 4℃, and analyzed under a fluorescence microscope.

Immunohistochemistry

For immunohistochemical analysis, pancreatic and ileal tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight, dehydrated, and embedded in paraffin. Sections were cut at a thickness of 5 μm using a sliding microtome. After dewaxing and rehydration, antigen retrieval was conducted by microwave heating in citrate buffer for 10–15 min. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked by treating sections with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 15 min. Non-specific background staining was minimized by blocking with 10% goat serum for 30 min at room temperature. The sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against F4/80 (70076; CST, MA, USA) diluted in PBS. Following incubation, sections were treated with an anti-rabbit HRP reagent (Abcam, Cambridge, UK; ab205718) at 37 °C for one hour. Visualization was carried out using diaminobenzidine (Maxim Biotechnology, Fuzhou, China; DAB4033). Images were captured using an Olympus BX41 microscope equipped with an Olympus DP50 camera at magnifications of 20x or 40x.

Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) Stains

Sections were first immersed in a clearing agent for 10 min, a process repeated twice with gentle shaking to remove excess liquid between steps. Subsequently, sections were rehydrated through a graded ethanol series: absolute ethanol for 5 min, 95% ethanol for 5 min, 85% ethanol for 5 min, and 75% ethanol for 5 min, followed by rinsing with distilled water for 1 min. Sections were stained for four minutes using Harris hematoxylin solution, and then they were rinsed for two minutes under running water to remove any remaining color. Differentiation was carried out using 0.8% hydrochloric acid alcohol for 2 s, followed by a rinse with tap water. Alternatively, sections could be treated with lithium carbonate solution for bluing, followed by another 2-minute rinse in tap water. Next, sections were treated with a solution of eosin that is soluble in alcohol for a duration of 20 s. Without washing, sections were immediately immersed in 95% ethanol for 5 s to adjust coloration, then dehydrated through two changes of absolute ethanol, each for 2 min. Following xylene clearing, slices were mounted with neutral balsam and examined under a microscope.

Flow Cytometry

Mouse peripheral blood was collected and taken at the indicated time points after model induction. Red blood cells were lysed with ACK buffer, and PBMCs were washed and resuspended in FACS buffer (PBS, 2% FBS, 2 mM EDTA). Cells were stained (30 min, 4 °C, dark) with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies including PE anti-mouse CD19 (E-AB-F0986D, Elabscience, Wuhan, China) and PE-Vio 615 anti-mouse CD138 (130-109-045, Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany). After washing, cells were acquired on a CytoFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter) and analyzed in FlowJo v10.9. Doublets and debris were excluded (FSC-A vs. FSC-H/SSC).

Quantitative Real-Time PCR

TRIzol (G8011; Adams Life, Shanghai, China) was utilized to extract the RNA, followed by the synthesis of complementary DNA (cDNA) with reverse transcriptase (11155ES60; Yeasen, Shanghai, China). To evaluate gene expression, 2×SYBR Green Master Mix was employed (A57115, Thermo Fisher, Shanghai, China). The analysis of RT‒qPCR data was carried out using the 2–ΔΔCT method, using Gapdh as the reference gene for housekeeping. Details of the primers can be found in Supplementary Table 3.

ELISA

The ELISA process was carried out in compliance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, capture antibodies targeting the specific analytes were applied to 96-well plates and subsequently incubated with diluted samples. After washing, biotin-conjugated secondary antibodies and enzyme-linked streptavidin were applied to facilitate colorimetric detection. A microplate reader was used to detect the optical density (OD) at 450 nm, and standard curves made with known analyte concentrations were used to calculate the analyte concentrations. Levels of IgA, IgG, IgM, TNF-α, IL-6, α-amylase, lipase and CCL2 were measured. ELISA kits used are listed in Supplementary Table 4.

Statistical Analysis

The software GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA) was used to perform statistical analyses. An unpaired Student’s t-test and a one-way ANOVA were used to assess statistical significance. Survival data were analyzed using Kaplan–Meier curves and compared by the log-rank (Mantel–Cox) test; when early survival differences were of interest, the Gehan–Breslow–Wilcoxon test was additionally reported. P-values less than 0.05 were regarded as significant, with the subsequent notations applied: ns (not significant), *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, and ****P < 0.0001.

Results

scRNA-seq Reveals Dynamic Immune Response Patterns during Recovery from Severe Acute Pancreatitis

We analyzed peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) collected from 44 individuals, including healthy controls and patients with mild (M group) or moderate severe/severe acute pancreatitis (S group) on days 1, 3, and 7 after hospital admission. The samples are designated as H, M_Day1, M_Day3, M_Day7, S_Day1, S_Day3, S_Day7, and an overview and detailed description of sample collection are provided in Fig. 1A and Supplementary Table 1. In contrast to our earlier work, which focused on a more limited patient cohort, the current analysis incorporates a substantially increased number of clinical samples spanning mild, moderately severe, and severe acute pancreatitis, with longitudinal sampling during the recovery phase. In addition, publicly available scRNA-seq datasets from healthy donors were integrated as external references using standardized batch correction pipelines. Following quality control, batch effects were corrected using Harmony, followed by normalization, dimensionality reduction using t-SNE, clustering, and cell-type annotation with SingleR. This analysis identified five major cell populations, including B cells, bone marrow (BM) cells, monocytes, platelets, and T cells (Fig. 1B–D). A small population annotated as bone marrow (BM) cells was detected in the peripheral blood single-cell datasets. This population is most likely attributable to technical factors or those related to annotation inherent to single cell RNA sequencing, rather than true resident cells of the bone marrow in circulation, and does not affect the main conclusions of this study.

Despite inter-sample variability, overall compositional trends were evident. Compared with healthy controls, the relative proportion of monocytes was higher on day 1 after admission in the M group and further increased in the S group. With ongoing treatment, monocyte representation in the S group declined. Given that monocytes/macrophages amplify inflammatory cascades and drive multiorgan injury through cytokine release, these compositional changes are consistent with a central role for monocyte-associated inflammatory responses in severe AP pathogenesis (Fig. 1E, F). In contrast, the relative proportions of both B cells and T cells were lower in the M and S groups at admission than in controls. During recovery, however, B-cell representation increased at the compositional level, exceeding that observed for T cells by S_Day7 (Fig. 1E, F).

To characterize dynamic gene expression programs across disease severity and time, we calculated weighted average gene expression values for each cell population based on cell numbers and applied Mfuzz soft clustering to group genes with similar temporal expression patterns. This analysis yielded six gene clusters. Clusters 1 displayed genes that were strongly upregulated at S_Day1 and gradually returned toward control levels at S_Day3 and S_Day7, whereas cluster 2 genes were selectively upregulated at S_Day7 (Fig. 1G; Fig. S1A).

GO biological process enrichment analysis revealed that cluster 1 genes were enriched in immune cell chemotaxis and inflammatory responses, which peaked at S_Day1 and subsequently declined, indicating that the systemic immune response was most intense at disease onset and progressively attenuated (Fig. 1H). In contrast, cluster 2 genes were associated with B cell mediated humoral immunity, with immunoglobulin production peaking at S_Day7, highlighting the importance of B cells and antibodies in late-stage recovery from severe AP (Fig. 1I). Genes showing minimal temporal variation were mainly enriched in RNA splicing and cellular energy metabolism pathways (Fig. S1B).

Differential expression and GO analyses at individual time points further revealed the upregulation of “response to unfolded protein” pathways, likely reflecting robust antibody production by plasma cells (Fig. S1C, D). Consistently, genes associated with immunoglobulins including IGHA1, IGHA2, and JCHAIN, were significantly upregulated at S_Day7 (Fig. S2A). Mfuzz analysis restricted to B cell genes also revealed cluster 1 genes were markedly upregulated at S_Day7, with functional enrichment highlighting endoplasmic reticulum–associated pathways (Fig. S2B, C).

During severe acute pancreatitis, intestinal barrier dysfunction not only represents a secondary inflammatory injury but also perpetuates disease progression by promoting bacterial translocation and endotoxin release [32, 33]. Mechanistically, IgA secreted by plasma cells is transported across intestinal epithelial cells via the polymeric immunoglobulin receptor (pIgR) to form secretory IgA (sIgA), which neutralizes bacterial lipopolysaccharides and suppresses macrophage activation, thereby maintaining mucosal integrity (Fig. 5H) [34, 35].

To further validate this mechanism, a cerulein- and LPS- induced SAP model was established (cerulein, 100 µg/kg, hourly ×7; LPS, 10 mg/kg ×1; Fig. 5I). Mzb1−/− mice exhibited significantly higher mortality (83% by day 5) compared with Mzb1+/+ mice (n = 6; Fig. 5J). Histological analysis confirmed exacerbated pancreatic acinar necrosis in Mzb1−/− mice (Fig. 5K).

Moreover, IgA uptake by intestinal epithelial cells was reduced, accompanied by downregulation of the tight junction proteins Claudin-1, Occludin, and E-cadherin, indicating marked disruption of intestinal barrier integrity (Fig. 5L-N). Notably, increased macrophage accumulation was observed in the intestinal mucosa of Mzb1−/− mice, suggesting that impaired IgA-associated immune regulation may promote intestinal macrophage activation and contribute to secondary pancreatic inflammation (Fig. 5O-Q). In the cerulein + LPS-induced SAP model, MZB1 deficiency similarly exacerbated inflammatory responses and pancreatic injury, as evidenced by elevated mRNA expression of inflammatory and macrophage-associated genes, increased serum levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, and higher pancreatic injury markers in Mzb1−/− mice (Fig. S7). Furthermore, plasma cell transplantation, antibiotic treatment, and oral sIgA supplementation alleviated the increased mortality, intestinal barrier damage, and pancreatic injury associated with MZB1 deficiency (Figs. S8 and S9).

MZB1 Deficiency Exacerbates Pancreatic Macrophage-Associated Inflammation

Immunofluorescence staining and qPCR demonstrated markedly increased infiltration of F4/80+ macrophages in pancreatic tissue following cerulein-induced pancreatitis, which was further enhanced in Mzb1−/− mice (Fig. 6C, D). Together with the intestinal barrier defects and mucosal macrophage accumulation observed in the cerulein + LPS model, these findings are consistent with a gut-associated inflammatory amplification process under conditions of impaired IgA secretion. Consistent with these observations, CCL2 levels were elevated (Fig. 6E), and previous studies have shown that injured pancreatic acinar cells are the primary source of CCL2, which recruits macrophages; these infiltrating macrophages subsequently contribute to secondary CCL2 production, amplifying the local inflammatory response [36–38]. This temporal relationship explains the progressive macrophage infiltration observed in our model.

Furthermore, MZB1 deficiency significantly upregulated genes involved in macrophage activation and chemotaxis, including Cxcr2, Ly6G, Fpr1, and Mmp9 (Fig. 6F). Reactome analysis revealed activation of the “Formyl peptide receptors bind formyl peptides and many other ligands” pathway (Fig. 6G). FPR1 is a GPCR expressed on macrophages and has been implicated in pro-inflammatory cytokine responses (Fig. 6H). Immunofluorescence co-staining confirmed robust co-expression of FPR1 and F4/80 in pancreatic macrophages of Mzb1−/− mice (Fig. 6I), which was further validated by western blot analysis showing elevated FPR1 protein levels (Fig. 6J). Consistently, IL-1β and TNF-α levels were significantly elevated in pancreatic tissue homogenates, while IL-6 showed a similar trend without reaching statistical significance (Fig. 6K).

Discussion

This study provides a characterization of immune remodeling at the single-cell level during severe acute pancreatitis. It highlights an underappreciated contribution of humoral immunity associated with B cells to inflammatory modulation during disease progression. By integrating human single-cell transcriptomic data with genetic and pharmacological analyses in mouse models, our findings suggest that later stages of SAP are not solely characterized by attenuation of innate immune activation, but are also associated with increased engagement of plasma cell related immunoglobulin programs.

In particular, we observed that MZB1 expression was closely associated with plasma cell differentiation, IgA production, and inflammatory status in both human and experimental pancreatitis. While our previous work described the immune landscape of acute pancreatitis and reported the expansion of MZB1 positive plasma cells in association with disease stage, the present study extends these observations by examining the functional consequences of MZB1 deficiency in vivo. Using genetic ablation approaches, we found that loss of MZB1 was associated with impaired IgA responses, enhanced macrophage activation, and increased tissue injury, supporting a functional role for MZB1 associated humoral immunity in the regulation of inflammatory severity.

MZB1 has been shown to regulate endoplasmic reticulum homeostasis and antibody secretion during plasma cell differentiation [10, 13, 39–41]. However, its involvement in acute inflammatory conditions has been less well characterized. Our data indicate that MZB1 deficiency is associated with reduced plasma cell maturation and diminished IgA production during experimental acute pancreatitis. Given the established role of IgA in maintaining intestinal mucosal homeostasis, these changes were accompanied by compromised intestinal barrier integrity, including altered tight junction protein levels and reduced epithelial IgA uptake. Previous studies have established that IgA plays a central role in maintaining intestinal mucosal homeostasis and regulating gut immune responses [42, 43]. IgA deficiency has been linked to increased susceptibility to inflammatory bowel diseases, due to impaired neutralization of bacterial antigens and compromised intestinal barrier integrity [44]. Although direct evidence connecting IgA deficiency to SAP in humans is limited, it is plausible that reduced IgA-mediated mucosal protection can exacerbate systemic inflammation in SAP. Disruption of intestinal barrier integrity may facilitate bacterial translocation and exposure to microbial-derived products, thereby exacerbating systemic inflammation, increasing the risk of infected pancreatic necrosis and sepsis, and contributing to poor clinical outcomes [2–5]. Our findings in MZB1-deficient mice, showing decreased IgA levels, impaired intestinal barrier integrity, and aggravated pancreatic inflammation, support a model in which IgA responses associated with MZB1 indirectly modulate SAP severity through maintenance of gut homeostasis. These observations are consistent with our previous clinical data indicating that higher serum IgA levels in SAP patients correlate with more favorable disease outcomes [16]. Furthermore, plasma cell transplantation or antibiotic treatment alleviated pancreatic damage caused by MZB1 deficiency.

Together, these observations are consistent with a model in which MZB1-associated humoral immune responses contribute to limiting systemic inflammation by supporting intestinal barrier function, rather than representing a passive consequence of disease severity.

Consistent with the notion that intestinal barrier dysfunction can amplify secondary inflammatory responses in SAP, we further examined downstream innate immune pathways affected by MZB1 deficiency. We observed increased expression of FPR1 in pancreatic macrophages in the absence of MZB1. One plausible pathway is that MZB1 deficiency impairs plasma cell maturation and IgA secretion, leading to compromised intestinal barrier function. This may increase exposure to microbial stimuli in the gut, which in turn promote intestinal innate immune activation and may contribute to secondary pancreatic macrophage accumulation and inflammatory amplification. In this context, increased FPR1 expression may represent one component of a broader downstream inflammatory response. Although direct evidence for MZB1 regulation of FPR1 signaling is currently limited, our observations are consistent with this model, and ongoing studies are aimed at elucidating the specific molecular interactions between MZB1, FPR1 and macrophage activation.

In summary, our study suggests the existence of a pathway involving MZB1, IgA, and the intestinal barrier that is associated with modulation of inflammatory severity in severe acute pancreatitis. By combining human single-cell analyses with experimental perturbation in mouse models, these findings expand current understanding of immune mechanisms that may influence inflammatory amplification and resolution in SAP. Future studies will be required to further define the cell type–specific contributions of MZB1 and to determine how humoral immune pathways interact with innate immune signaling during acute inflammatory diseases.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. The use of a global Mzb1 knockout model limits precise dissection of cell-specific effects, and lineage-restricted genetic approaches will be important to refine causal relationships. Notably, we did not observe overt differences in baseline inflammatory markers or macrophage accumulation between wild-type and Mzb1-deficient mice under unchallenged conditions. In addition, larger longitudinal human cohorts and targeted rescue experiments will be necessary to further assess the relevance of MZB1-associated humoral immune pathways in SAP. It should be noted that the healthy control samples were obtained from two independent public datasets. Although batch effects were addressed using the Harmony algorithm, the limited number of healthy controls and the integration of multiple datasets may introduce residual confounding between technical and biological variation. In situations where biological conditions are closely associated with different data sources, batch correction methods may not fully disentangle these effects. Therefore, residual batch effects may influence downstream analyses, and the transcriptomic findings derived from these integrated datasets should be interpreted with caution. Despite these limitations, the present work provides mechanistic insight into the relationship between humoral immunity, intestinal barrier integrity, and macrophage-driven inflammation in experimental acute pancreatitis.
