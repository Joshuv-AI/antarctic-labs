Bortezomib-induced gastrointestinal dysfunction, peripheral neuropathy and neuroimmune signalling are influenced by the gut microbiota in mice

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPeripheral neuropathy and gastrointestinal dysfunction are frequent, debilitating side effects of the neurotoxic myeloma drug bortezomib that reduce quality of life and adherence to optimal therapy. Therapeutic strategies to manage these complications are limited. Here, we have developed a mouse model of bortezomib side effects to investigate the role of the gut microbiota in their development.MethodsC57BL/6 specific pathogen-free (SPF), germ-free (GF) and ex-GF mice (colonised with healthy gut microbiota via faecal microbiota transplantation [FMT]) were treated with bortezomib (1 mg/kg) twice-weekly for two weeks. Neurotoxicity and neuroimmune signalling were assessed by serum neurofilament light chain (NfL) quantitation and real-time qPCR analysis of inflammatory markers in sensory and autonomic nerve ganglia, respectively. Faecal microbiota composition was characterised using 16S rRNA gene sequencing. Bortezomib side effects were assessed using behavioural phenotyping, von Frey mechanical sensitivity and rotarod testing, and FITC-dextran gut permeability and Evans Blue dye transit assays.ResultsBortezomib-treated SPF mice displayed altered spontaneous behaviour and developed acute gastric retention, altered gastrointestinal motility and increased intestinal permeability, in the absence of intestinal micro-architecture changes. Concurrently, bortezomib induced sensory loss in, and increased grooming of, paws, and reduced motor performance, indicative of peripheral neuropathy. Bortezomib neurotoxicity was evidenced by elevated serum NfL levels and neuroimmune signalling in sciatic nerve dorsal root and vagal nerve nodose ganglia. In SPF mice, bortezomib altered gut microbiota composition with an acute decrease in microbial diversity and expansion of Lactobacillus gasseri. Notably, GF mice developed a milder symptom profile following bortezomib treatment compared with SPF mice, while FMT mice did not develop overt bortezomib side effects despite displaying evidence of nerve damage.ConclusionsThis is the first study to model gastrointestinal side effects of bortezomib in rodents, implicating neuroimmune dysregulation of the vagus nerve. Our data show that the gut microbiota is not a primary driver of bortezomib side effects. However, the absence of a symptom profile in FMT mice suggests that, in GF mice, the gut microbiota beneficially modulates the host to protect against bortezomib side effects. Further studies are required to determine whether this can be harnessed to mitigate clinical complications of bortezomib.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12974-026-03909-w.

Background

Bortezomib is a first-in-class proteasome inhibitor that is integral to optimal treatment of the malignant plasma cell disorder multiple myeloma; representing a standard-of-care therapy in both transplant-eligible and -ineligible newly diagnosed patients [1]. The drug is also a front-line therapy for light-chain amyloidosis [2]. Bortezomib is a dipeptidyl boronic acid that reversibly inhibits the chymotrypsin-like activity of the 26 S proteasome, a key component of the ubiquitin-proteasome system. This mechanism is central to the success of bortezomib in the treatment of myeloma, which is characterised by high immunoglobulin production, rendering malignant cells particularly vulnerable to proteasome inhibition, subsequent endoplasmic reticulum stress and, ultimately, induction of apoptosis [3].

While considered to be a more “targeted” agent compared with conventional cytotoxic therapies, bortezomib is neurotoxic and causes clinically challenging side effects that reduce quality of life and can necessitate drug dose reductions or treatment cessation, leading to suboptimal clinical outcomes [4–7]. Peripheral neuropathy is the major dose-limiting toxicity of bortezomib that is typically characterised by sensory dysfunction in the extremities, but can also impact motor function. Symptoms include uncomfortable and sometimes painful sensations (pins-and-needles, burning or tingling), numbness and sensory loss in hands and feet, loss of balance and proprioception and, in some cases, muscle weakness [4, 8, 9]. Peripheral neuropathy, of any grade, is reported in approximately 30–50% of people treated with bortezomib, with 8–12% experiencing severe symptoms (grades 3–4), defined by intolerable pain and disabling sensory loss, marked muscle weakness and, rarely, paralysis [10–12]. Previous studies implicate activation of neuroimmune signalling within the peripheral and central nervous system in the pathogenesis of bortezomib-induced peripheral neuropathy [13–15].

While peripheral neuropathy is a well-documented clinical challenge, gastrointestinal side effects are also highly prevalent; being reported in over 50% of people undergoing bortezomib therapy, but are less understood. Gastrointestinal side effects commonly associated with bortezomib use include diarrhoea, constipation, nausea and vomiting [11, 16–20]. A rarer, but more serious, complication of bortezomib therapy is paralytic ileus, characterised by abdominal pain, distention and absence of bowel movements, which can be life-threatening [21–23]. Recent data highlight the impact of bortezomib-associated gastrointestinal symptoms, with a significant decrease in two-year progression-free and overall survival observed in people with severe grade 3 gastrointestinal symptoms in comparison to those with milder grade 1 or 2 symptoms [18]. Both peripheral neuropathy and the gastrointestinal consequences of bortezomib therapy remain without effective intervention. Although current clinical practice guidelines recommend duloxetine for the managememt of painful peripheral neuropathy, the magnitude of benefit is small and, hence, implementation is low [24, 25]. This, combined with the complete lack of specific interventions for gastrointestinal symptoms associated with bortezomib, highlights a major gap in care. Thus, there is a need to better understand the aetiology of these side effects to develop novel, more efficacious supportive care strategies that enhance bortezomib tolerability and adherence.

The ecosystem of micro-organisms in the gut (the gut microbiota) is rapidly emerging as a key modulator of anti-cancer drug toxicity, owing to its ability to modulate drug metabolism, host immune function and cellular repair processes [26–30]. The gut microbiota is increasingly recognised for its ability to influence both central and peripheral nerve function, including autonomic nerves, and, as a result, has been implicated in the development of neuropathic and visceral pain across various clinical contexts [31–35]. Within the oncology setting, the gut microbiota has been causally implicated in the development of peripheral neuropathy and gastrointestinal toxicity caused by some conventional chemotherapeutic agents [36–38]. Of note, mice lacking a gut microbiome (through antibiotic depletion or germ-free status) are resistant to oxaliplatin-induced peripheral neuropathy [36]. Moreover, the transfer of microbiota from mice sensitive to paclitaxel-induced peripheral neuropathy into peripheral neuropathy-resistant mice induces nociceptive hypersensitivity [37]. Beyond these findings, the gut microbiota’s role in mediating anti-cancer drug-induced neuropathy, including those induced by bortezomib, is unclear. Hence, we aimed to develop a mouse model in which to investigate the involvement of the gut microbiota in the development of bortezomib side effects.

Methods

Animals and bortezomib treatment

Male C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old, weight: 16–26 g), were bred and housed in standard, individually ventilated cages (N = 4–5/cage) in the South Australian Health and Medical Research Institute (SAHMRI) specific pathogen-free (SPF) Bioresources Facility (Adelaide, South Australia). Male gnotobiotic (germ-free; GF) C57BL/6 mice (Translational Research Institute, Queensland, Australia) were housed in positively pressurised, high-efficiency particulate air (HEPA)-filtered ISO-P cages (Techniplast, Italy) at the SAHMRI Preclinical, Imaging and Research Laboratories (PIRL). All side effect studies were performed in C57BL/6 mice. All mice were housed at an ambient temperature of 21 °C ± 3.0 °C with a 12:12 h light-dark cycle and had access to gamma-irradiated (SPF mice) or autoclaved (GF) commercially pelleted food and sterilised water ad libitum. Bortezomib (Selleckchem, USA) or vehicle (3.9% DMSO in PBS) alone was given intravenously via tail vein injection twice weekly (days 1, 4, 8 and 11) for two weeks, representing a clinical dosing schedule [11, 12]. Forty eight hours after final treatment (unless otherwise stated), mice were humanely killed via isoflurane anaesthesia, cardiac puncture and cervical dislocation.

Von Frey test

To assess paw sensitivity to mechanical stimulation, the manual von Frey method was performed using filaments ranging from 0.07 to 2 g of force (Stoelting, USA). Unless otherwise stated, mice were acclimatised in the testing room for 15 min and habituated for 45 min on testing grids using modular holding cages 24 h, and then immediately, prior to mechanical sensitivity testing, with testing performed at baseline (24 h prior to first treatment) and at the study end-point (48 h after final treatment) [39]. The ‘ascending stimulus’ method was used with a 50% withdrawal response rate indicative of animals reaching mechanical withdrawal threshold [40]. Baseline mechanical sensitivity assessments in SPF mice informed randomisation into treatment groups.

Behavioural phenotyping

To evaluate non-stimulus-evoked, spontaneous behaviour, automated behavioural analyses were performed 24 h after final treatment using a behavioural spectrometer (Biobserve, Germany) [41]. Animals were acclimatised in the testing room for 15 min prior to being individually placed in the testing arena and recorded for 20 min, as previously described [39, 42]. The time engaged in 23 different behaviours including grooming, rearing and locomotor behaviours were recorded, as well as total track length and zone duration during the testing period.

Rotarod test

The rotarod apparatus (LE8200, Harvard Apparatus) was used to assess animal motor function and coordination. Before each training and testing session, mice were acclimatised in the testing room for 15 min. Mice were trained using an acceleration mode protocol (4–40 rpm) over four consecutive days with increasing time spent on the rod each day, starting from 70 s on day 1, 145 s on day 2, 220 s on day 3 and 300 s on day 4. Rotarod testing (acceleration mode for up to 5 min) was performed 24 h prior to first treatment to randomise mice, and then 24 h after final drug treatment. The mean of 3 tests, performed at least 15 min apart, was recorded for each animal [43]. A normal (time to) fall was defined as being within the range of that observed in vehicle-treated mice.

Intestinal barrier permeability and integrity assessments

Intestinal barrier function was assessed 48 h after final drug treatment by the administration of 4 kDa fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-dextran (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) at a 500 mg/kg dose (75 mg/mL) via oral gavage. Plasma citrulline, a biomarker of enterocyte mass and thus intestinal barrier integrity, was quantified in plasma (collected at the study end-point) by liquid chromatography mass spectrometry, using an API 5500 QpQ mass spectrometer (Sciex, USA) and Acquity UPLC system (Waters Corporation, USA).

Total gastrointestinal transit time assessment

Mice were fasted overnight for 17 h (4pm-9am) before being placed in individual cages and receiving 0.1 mL of a semi-liquid solution containing 5% Evans blue dye, 0.9% NaCl, and 0.5% methylcellulose in PBS, via oral gavage. Mice were monitored for the presence of a blue faecal pellet for up to 6 h after gavage. Total gastrointestinal transit time was calculated as the time from gavage until the time of visualisation of the first blue pellet.

Neurofilament light chain analysis

Neurofilament light chain (NfL), a biomarker of neurotoxicity [44, 45], was quantified in serum (collected at the study end-point) by GeneWorks (Australia) using the Simoa NF-light Advantage v2 Assay following manufacturer’s instructions (Quanterix, USA).

Histopathological analysis of the gastrointestinal tract

Immediately after humane killing, the stomach and entire gastrointestinal tract, from pyloric sphincter to rectum, were dissected. Stomachs were weighed full (i.e. with contents) and empty (after contents were removed). The tissue was then laid flat and one half of the corpus section was excised prior to fixation in 10% neutral buffered formalin (NBF). Intestinal contents were removed by flushing with ice-cold PBS (pH 7.4), before weighing both the small and large intestines. Two samples each (0.5 cm long) of duodenum, jejunum, ileum and colon were collected and fixed in 10% NBF for 24 h, and then processed and embedded in paraffin wax. Sections (5 μm) were stained with haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and subsequently imaged using the NanoZoomer S60 Digital Slide Scanner (Hamamatsu Photonics, Japan). Images were evaluated in a blinded fashion on NDP.view2 software, such that 10 well-oriented crypts and villi were measured per slide, and an average for both was calculated for each animal.

Nerve histopathology

Segments of sciatic nerve from the mid-femoral region and the superior half of the left cervical vagal nerve were excised and fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde (ProSciTech, Australia) for 24 h, before washing in 50 mM sodium cacodylate buffer (pH 7.4). Samples were then stained with 1% osmium tetroxide (ProSciTech) for 1 h at room temperature, dehydrated in graded concentrations of ethanol, embedded into EPON resin (ProSciTech) and incubated at 70 °C overnight to allow polymerisation. Sections (0.5 μm) were cut on an Ultracut E Ultramicrotome (Reichert-Jung, USA) and stained with toluidine blue, before being imaged on the NanoZoomer S60. Fibre and axon diameter were measured in three and five symmetrically-spaced regions of interest from sciatic and vagal nerve sections, respectively, using NDP.view or Image J software (National Institutes of Health, USA, Version 2.14.0). Mean myelin thickness ([fibre diameter] - [axon diameter]) and g ratio ([axon diameter]/[fibre diameter]) were then calculated for each sample.

Inflammatory cytokine gene expression in dorsal root ganglia and nodose ganglia

Dorsal root ganglia (DRG) at levels 3–5 (L3-L5) and nodose ganglia pairs, containing nerve cell bodies of the mouse sciatic nerve and vagus nerve, respectively [46, 47], were resected and snap frozen in liquid nitrogen. RNA was extracted using the Invitrogen PureLink™ RNA Micro Kit following the manufacturer’s instructions (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Briefly, samples were lysed using the TissueLyser II (Qiagen, Germany) and stainless steel beads (Qiagen). RNA was isolated using TRIzol (Thermo Fisher Scientific), including on-column DNase digestion, eluted in 22 µL of RNase-free water and stored at -20 °C. Total RNA yield and purity were quantified using the NanoDrop 8000 Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Next, cDNA was synthesised using Superscript IV reverse transcriptase (Invitrogen), from 650 ng (per three DRG [L3-L5]), or 100 ng (per two nodose ganglia) of RNA template. Real-time quantitative PCR was performed using QuantStudio 3 Real-time PCR system (Applied Biosystems, USA) to determine the mRNA expression of the genes encoding mouse IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-ɑ and F4/80 using RT2 SYBR® Green reagent (Qiagen) and 10 µM primer pair (primer sequences shown in supplementary methods section) in 15 µL reaction volume. Ct values were obtained and analysed using QuantStudio 3 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and changes in gene expression were calculated relative to Gapdh expression, using the 2−ΔCT method [48].

Gut microbiota 16S rRNA gene sequencing

Faecal pellets were collected longitudinally on day 0 (baseline; F0), day 4 (after one treatment; F1), day 7 (after two treatments; F2), day 11 (after three treatments; F3) and day 12 (after four treatments; F4), and stored at -80 °C. Faecal DNA was extracted using the DNeasy PowerSoil Pro Kit (Cat #47014, Qiagen), as per manufacturer’s guidelines. Briefly, samples and the lysis buffer were added to PowerBead Pro Tubes (Qiagen) and incubated at 65 °C for 10 min, before being homogenised using the TissueLyser II at maximum speed for 10 min and DNA was eluted in 50 µL of Tris (10 mM). DNA yield and purity were determined using a NanoDrop 8000 Spectrophotometer. Sequencing of the amplicon library was performed using an Illumina Miseq system (2 × 300 bp run) at the South Australian Genomics Centre (SAHMRI, Adelaide, South Australia). Bioinformatic analysis, including demultiplexing of paired end 16S rRNA gene sequences, error correcting to exact sequence variants and taxonomic assignment was performed, as previously described [49]. Alpha and beta diversity values were determined as previously described [49].

Germ-free mouse studies

Caecal content was collected from five seven-week-old healthy male SPF C57BL/6 mice, pooled and resuspended in PBS with 15% glycerol using a Stomacher® 80 Bag (Thomas Scientific, USA) under anaerobic conditions, as previously reported [50]. Forty GF C57BL/6 mice (four to five weeks old) were divided into two groups (N = 20/group), and one group was colonised with gut microbiota using faecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) via administration of two 100 µL caecal slurries (via oral gavage) [49–51]. At seven weeks of age, GF and FMT mice were treated with vehicle or high-dose bortezomib (HD-BTZ; 1 mg/kg) twice weekly for two weeks, as described earlier (N = 10/group). The GF status of mice was routinely assessed throughout the experiment via amplification of 16S rRNA gene in faecal DNA samples [52]. Following final treatment, GF and FMT mice were transported to SAHMRI Bioresources in a sterile cage, before overnight acclimation. The next day, mice were individually removed from their cages in a biosafety cabinet and behavioural spectrometry was performed (as described above). Mice (six at a time) were then acclimatised in the testing room for 15 min and habituated for 45 min on von Frey testing grids before performing mechanical sensitivity testing (as described above). Sub-cohorts of GF and FMT mice (N = 4/group) were designated for real-time quantitative PCR and stomach weight analyses only to minimise potential contamination from exposure to ambient conditions. Control SPF mice (vehicle and HD-BTZ-treated) were housed at SAHMRI and underwent behavioural phenotyping and von Frey testing 24 h after final treatment in line with GF and FMT mice. Mice were then humanely killed (as described above).

Statistical analysis

Unless otherwise specified, data were analysed using GraphPad Prism 10.4.1. Data were analysed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. For parametric data, multiple-group analyses were performed using a one-way or two-way ANOVA with Dunnett’s or Tukey’s multiple comparisons test or uncorrected Fisher’s LSD test (as appropriate) and two-group analyses were analysed using a t-test. When normality was not confirmed, non-parametric tests were used (Mann-Whitney test to compare two groups or Kruskal-Wallis with Dunn’s multiple comparisons test for multiple-group comparisons). Real-time quantitative PCR data in GF/FMT mice were analysed using individual Mann-Whitney U tests. Tumour burden, behavioural spectrometry ‘time engaged in’ and total gastrointestinal transit time data were log-transformed prior to conducting statistical analyses. For rotarod studies, a Fisher’s exact test was performed. All microbial sequencing data were analysed using R version 3.6.3, as previously described [49]. Correlative analyses between gut microbiota read-outs and behavioural phenotypes were performed using Pearson’s correlation for parametric data and Spearman’s correlations for non-parametric data.

Results

High-dose bortezomib alters weight and spontaneous behaviour in mice indicative of reduced well-being

Side effects of bortezomib therapy adversely affect patient quality of life [53, 54]. To initially explore the effect of bortezomib on animal well-being, C57BL/6 mice were treated with high-dose bortezomib (HD-BTZ; 1 mg/kg), which induces 5TGM1 myeloma tumour regression in the related C57BL/KalwRij mouse strain [55, 56], equivalent to a very good partial or complete response observed clinically, or low-dose bortezomib (LD-BTZ; 0.5 mg/kg) which induces stable disease (Fig. S1A-C). C57BL/6 mice treated with HD-BTZ displayed cyclical, self-limiting weight loss and end-point weight change (-7.00 ± 1.60% relative to baseline weight) was significantly different compared with both vehicle-treated mice (7.30 ± 0.81%, P < 0.01) and LD-BTZ-treated mice (3.22% ± 2.54%, P < 0.001; Fig. 1A, B).

Automated behavioural phenotyping was then performed to evaluate changes in spontaneous animal behaviour related to reduced well-being. Notably, mice treated with HD-BTZ displayed profound changes in behaviour with a 40% reduction in total distance travelled (track length: 3264 ± 297.7 cm), total time engaged in locomotor activity (113.6 ± 9.8 seconds [s]) and time spent in centre zone of testing arena (15.8 ± 2.8 s) compared with vehicle-treated animals (track length: 5476 ± 218.1 cm; P < 0.0001, locomotor time: 192.2 ± 6.4 s; P < 0.001 and centre zone time: 26.6 ± 2.9 s; P < 0.0001; Fig. 1C, D and S2A, B). In addition, HD-BTZ-treated mice spent 52% and 32% less time engaged in total rearing (59.7 ± 6.5 s) and orienting (237.3 ± 17.7 s) behaviour compared with vehicle-treated mice (rearing time: 124.6 ± 4.2 s; P < 0.0001, orienting time: 351.3 ± 6.6 s; P < 0.0001), respectively (Fig. 1D and S2C, D). Consistent with this, HD-BTZ-treated mice spent 139% more time still (437.6 ± 32.4 s) compared with vehicle-treated counterparts (182.8 ± 10.3 s; P < 0.0001; Fig. 1D). Strikingly, while there was no difference in total time spent grooming, HD-BTZ-treated mice demonstrated a 77% increase in time engaged in paw grooming (107.4 ± 23.4 s) compared with vehicle-treated animals (60.6 ± 13.9 s; P < 0.0001) but spent less time engaged in grooming of other body parts including legs, back and genitalia (P < 0.0001) compared with control mice (Fig. 1D, E). In contrast, we found no difference in spontaneous behaviour between LD-BTZ- and vehicle-treated mice (Fig. 1B-D and S2A-D). Taken together, HD-BTZ, but not LD-BTZ, alters weight and spontaneous behaviour of mice that is indicative of reduced well-being.

High-dose bortezomib induces peripheral neuropathy, inflammation in dorsal root ganglia and neuronal damage in mice

Numbness and sensory loss in hands and feet, changes in proprioception and reduced fine motor function are clinical features of bortezomib-induced peripheral neuropathy [4, 8, 9]. To assess whether bortezomib induces a similar neuropathic phenotype in mice, von Frey and rotarod testing was performed. Notably, mice treated with HD-BTZ displayed a significant decrease in paw sensitivity to mechanical stimulus (paw withdrawal threshold median and range: 1 g [0.16–1.4 g]) compared with vehicle-treated mice (0.6 g [0.16–1 g], P < 0.01). In contrast, LD-BTZ-treated mice showed no significant change in mechanical sensitivity (0.6 g [0.16–2 g]) compared with vehicle-treated and HD-BTZ-treated mice (Fig. 2A). In addition, HD-BTZ-treated mice demonstrated a marked reduction in rotarod performance. While time spent on rod was not significantly reduced in HD-BTZ-treated mice compared with vehicle-treated mice (data not shown), 43% (6/13) of HD-BTZ-treated mice fell off the rod more rapidly than the vehicle-treated mice (43% vs. 0%, P < 0.01; Fig. 2B). In contrast, there was no significant difference in rotarod performance in mice treated with LD-BTZ compared with vehicle-treated mice, as assessed by the percentage of mice falling off the rod rapidly (14% [2/12] vs. 0%) (Fig. 2B).

Previous studies have shown that bortezomib induces histological and inflammatory changes in the sciatic nerve of mice [13, 57–59]. While there was a trend towards reduced sciatic nerve myelinated fibre number with HD-BTZ treatment compared with vehicle-treated mice, this did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 2C-E). There were also no differences in sciatic nerve myelinated fibre morphology in HD-BTZ-treated mice compared with vehicle-treated mice (Fig. S3). Dorsal root ganglia (DRG) receive and process sensory information from the periphery and internal organs to the central nervous system (CNS) to control voluntary and involuntary bodily functions [60, 61]. Notably, HD-BTZ induced significant neuroimmune signalling in sciatic nerve dorsal root ganglia (DRG; L3-L5). Specifically, HD-BTZ-treated mice demonstrated a 4-fold increase in Adgre1 (encoding the mouse macrophage marker F4/80) expression in DRG relative to vehicle-treated mice (P < 0.05; Fig. 2F). Pro-inflammatory cytokine gene expression was also significantly increased in DRG from HD-BTZ-treated mice, including a 3.8-fold increase in Tnfa (P < 0.05) and Il6 (P < 0.01) expression, and a 4.8-fold increase in Il1b (P < 0.01) expression, relative to vehicle-treated mice (Fig. 2G-I). In contrast, we found no significant change in DRG expression of Adgre1, Tnfa, Il6 and Il1b between LD-BTZ- and vehicle-treated mice. Expression of Il1b was significantly higher in HD-BTZ-treated mice than LD-BTZ-treated mice (P < 0.05; Fig. 2F-I). These data show that HD-BTZ, but not LD-BTZ, induces peripheral neuropathy in mice that is accompanied by neuroimmune signalling in sciatic nerve DRG. Neurofilament light chain (NfL) is a neuron-specific cytoskeletal protein and serum biomarker of axonal damage associated with neurodegenerative conditions and chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy [44, 45]. Serum NfL is also elevated in MM patients treated with bortezomib, with levels correlating with electrophysiological measures of acute axonal damage in these patients [62]. Notably, we found serum NfL levels to be dramatically elevated in mice treated with HD-BTZ (mean: 791 ± 179 pg/mL) compared with both vehicle-treated mice (76.5 ± 22.8 pg/mL; P < 0.0001) and LD-BTZ-treated mice (51.3 ± 5.6 pg/mL; P < 0.0001) while treatment with LD-BTZ had no significant effect on serum NfL levels (Fig. 2J).

High-dose bortezomib alters gastrointestinal motility and permeability, but does not alter gastrointestinal micro-architecture or induce intestinal inflammation

To explore whether bortezomib induces gastrointestinal dysfunction in mice, we initially assessed wet weights of gastrointestinal tissues at necropsy 48 h after final drug treatment. Notably, HD-BTZ-treated mice displayed a 52% increase in full stomach weight (1.61 ± 0.148% of body weight [%BW]) compared with vehicle-treated mice (1.06 ± 0.665%BW, P < 0.005; Fig. 3A). This increase in full stomach weight was observed as early as 6 h after a single treatment with HD-BTZ (Fig. S4A). Empty stomach weight was also elevated in both HD-BTZ- and LD-BTZ-treated mice compared with control animals (P < 0.05; Fig. 3A). In addition, HD-BTZ-treated mice showed an increase in small intestine (4.42 ± 0.171%BW) and caecum wet weight (1.74 ± 0.093%BW), compared with vehicle-treated mice (3.49 ± 0.120%BW, P < 0.0001 and 1.22 ± 0.057%BW, P < 0.0001, respectively; Fig. 3B). Notably, we found no change in daily food intake in HD-BTZ-treated mice over time (Fig. S4B). Stomach (full), small intestine, caecum and colon wet weights did not differ between LD-BTZ- and vehicle-treated mice (Fig. 3A, B).

Histopathological assessment of gastrointestinal tissues revealed no significant differences in stomach or intestinal architecture (villus height, pit depth or crypt depth) between HD-BTZ-, LD-BTZ- and vehicle-treated mice (Fig. 3C, D). In addition, no change in enterocyte mass was observed, with plasma citrulline levels being comparable across treatment groups (Fig. 3E). Moreover, there were no changes in expression of inflammatory markers Adgre1, Tnfa, Il6 and Il1b in gastrointestinal tissues (Fig. S5) or detectable increases in circulating levels of inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, IFN-γ, IL-1ɑ, IL-6, IL-10, IL-17, CXCL1 and CCL2 (Fig. S6). In contrast, circulating levels FITC-dextran (4 kDa) following oral gavage of the fluorescent probe were significantly elevated in mice treated with HD-BTZ (407 ± 51.0 ng/mL) compared with vehicle-treated mice (191 ± 39.2 ng/mL; P < 0.05; Fig. 3F) indicating increased intestinal permeability [63] which corroborated the observed internalisation of claudin-1, a tight junction protein, in the small intestine of HD-BTZ-treated mice (Fig. 3G). In addition to permeability changes, HD-BTZ caused a decrease in total gastrointestinal transit time (TGITT) (146 ± 26.0 min), indicative of hypermotility, compared with both vehicle-treated (237 ± 22.9 min, P < 0.05) and LD-BTZ-treated (247 ± 14.6 min, P < 0.05) mice. Conversely, LD-BTZ-treated mice did not differ in TGITT in comparison to vehicle-treated mice (Fig. 3H). Collectively, these data suggest bortezomib induces gastrointestinal dysfunction in mice independently of local and systemic inflammation or overt architectural disruption of the gastrointestinal tract.

High-dose dose bortezomib causes inflammation in nodose ganglia of the vagus nerve

While autonomic nerve dysfunction is speculated to play a role in bortezomib-induced gastrointestinal dysfunction [19, 21], the effect of bortezomib on autonomic nerves that control gastrointestinal function is yet to be explored. To investigate this, we examined the micro-architecture of the vagus nerve, a critical communication pathway in the gut-brain axis controlling gastrointestinal function [64]. We found no differences in the number of myelinated or unmyelinated nerve fibres, myelinated fibre size or morphology between treatment groups (Fig. 4A-D and S7). However, HD-BTZ-treated mice demonstrated a significant increase in expression of Adgre1 in nodose ganglia (housing sensory cell bodies of the vagus nerve [47], with a 4.31-fold increase relative to vehicle-treated mice (P < 0.01; Fig. 4E). Nodose ganglia expression of pro-inflammatory markers Tnfa, Il6 and Il1b was not significantly elevated in HD-BTZ-treated mice compared with vehicle-treated animals (Fig. 4F-H). These findings suggest that neuroimmune signalling in the vagal nerve could, at least in part, be associated with gastrointestinal dysfunction associated with bortezomib treatment.

High-dose bortezomib induces a transient alteration in gut microbiota diversity and composition

Previous studies have demonstrated that some anti-cancer treatments can induce changes in gut microbiota composition, with these changes increasingly implicated in the development of drug side effects [65, 66]. However, the effects of bortezomib on gut microbiota composition are yet to be investigated. To address this, we performed 16S rRNA gene sequencing on longitudinally-collected faecal samples from mice with and without HD-BTZ treatment. Analysis of Shannon index identified a transient decrease in alpha diversity four days after initial bortezomib administration compared with vehicle-treated mice (F1; P < 0.05) (F1) and baseline (F0; P < 0.001) and which was not evident at the study end-point (F4; Fig. 5A). Relative abundance analyses identified modest but statistically significant shifts in the microbial composition of HD-BTZ-treated mice compared with vehicle-treated mice at F1 (Fig. 5B), with Lactobacillus gasseri (L.gasseri) expansion observed at this time-point in HD-BTZ-treated mice (HD-BTZ: 27.7 ± 3.72% vs. vehicle: 13.5 ± 3.02%, P < 0.05; Fig. 5C) aligning with the decrease in microbial diversity. Indeed, Shannon index inversely correlated with L.gasseri relative abundance at F1 (r=-0.905; P < 0.0001). The abundance of other taxa were not significantly different between groups. PERMANOVA identified differences in beta diversity between HD-BTZ- and vehicle-treated mice at F1 and F4 (Fig. 5D-F). Correlation analyses revealed that Shannon index inversely correlated with time engaged in orientation behaviour at F1 in HD-BTZ-treated mice (r=-0.601; P < 0.05). In addition, paw withdrawal threshold to mechanical stimulus positively correlated with Shannon index at F1 in HD-BTZ-treated mice (r = 0.598; P < 0.05). Conversely, paw withdrawal threshold inversely correlated with L.gasseri relative abundance (r=-0.613; P < 0.05) at F1 in HD-BTZ-treated mice (Fig. S8).

The gut microbiota modulates the development of bortezomib side effects in germ-free mice

The gut microbiota has previously been implicated in the aetiology of chemotherapy side effects, with gnotobiotic (germ-free, GF) and antibiotic-treated mice reported to be less vulnerable to the toxic effects of numerous chemotherapeutic agents, including oxaliplatin, paclitaxel and irinotecan [36–38, 67]. In light of our data demonstrating that bortezomib alters gut microbiota composition, we next sought to interrogate whether the presence or absence of a gut microbiota influenced bortezomib-induced side effects. To do this, we used GF mice; considered the gold standard for studying gut microbiota contributions to disease [68, 69], and antibiotic (ABX)-treated mice. For GF mice, we compared the development of bortezomib side effects in these mice with conventional SPF mice and GF mice colonised with a healthy gut microbiota from SPF mice (“FMT” mice). 16S rRNA PCR confirmed the germ-free status of GF mice prior to the commencement of bortezomib treatment, and that this status was maintained throughout the course of the study (Fig. S9). FMT mice were successfully colonised with an SPF-derived gut microbiota (Fig. S9). Treatment with HD-BTZ led to significantly decreased end-point weight in SPF mice (end-point change [mean ± SEM]: -10.62 ± 1.36% relative to baseline weight), compared with vehicle-treated couterparts (4.31 ± 1.55%, P < 0.01; Fig. 6A) in line with data presented in Fig. 1B. While this was not observed in GF or FMT mice (Fig. 6A), HD-BTZ significantly decreased end-point weight in both ABX (P < 0.01) and non-ABX (P < 0.01) mice compared with vehicle-treated couterparts (Fig. S10A). Notably, behavioural phenotyping demonstrated that HD-BTZ altered spontaneous behaviour in both GF and SPF mice, although the magnitude of effect was generally milder in GF mice. Specifically, bortezomib-treated GF mice showed a 39.6% reduction in total distance travelled (track length: 3909 ± 694 cm) compared with vehicle-treated GF mice (6468 ± 397 cm, P < 0.01), while SPF mice exhibited a 62.2% reduction with HD-BTZ treatment (vehicle: 6581 ± 333 cm vs. bortezomib: 2489 ± 269 cm, P < 0.0001). Notably, treatment with HD-BTZ did not alter total distance travelled in FMT mice (Fig. 6B) but did in both ABX (P < 0.001) and non-ABX (P < 0.05) mice compared with vehicle-treated mice (Fig. S10B). Bortezomib also caused reductions in time engaged in overall locomotor and orienting behaviours in both GF and SPF mice compared with their vehicle-treated counterparts, with effects less pronounced in GF mice. In contrast, locomotor and orienting behaviour was not affected by HD-BTZ treatment in FMT mice (Fig. 6C). HD-BTZ-treated ABX mice displayed significantly more time spent still and less time engaged in exploratory behaviour (i.e. locomotor, orienting and rearing) relative to vehicle treated mice (P < 0.01), although these effects did not reach statistical significance in non-ABX mice (Fig. S10C). Notably, HD-BTZ caused a 223% increase in time engaged in paw grooming (198 ± 47.5 s) in SPF mice relative to vehicle-treated SPF mice (61.2 ± 5.84 s, P < 0.001), while there was no significant effect in either GF or FMT mice. In fact, time engaged in paw grooming was significantly decreased in both HD-BTZ-treated GF (83.4 ± 25.0 s; P < 0.05) and FMT (56.7 ± 21.0 s; P < 0.001) mice compared with HD-BTZ-treated SPF mice (Fig. 6D). Time engaged in paw grooming was not significantly elevated with HD-BTZ treatment in ABX or non-ABX mice (Fig. S10D).

To assess the contribution of the gut microbiota to the specific feature of sensory loss associated with bortezomib-induced peripheral neuropathy, we performed von Frey testing in GF and FMT mice, and a parallel cohort of SPF mice (N = 6/group). Unlike previous data from a larger cohort of SPF mice (N = 12/group; Fig. 2A), treatment with HD-BTZ did not significantly increased paw withdrawal threshold relative to vehicle treatment in SPF mice (P = 0.067). Elevated paw withdrawal threshold was also not observed in GF and FMT mice treated with HD-BTZ compared with vehicle-treated controls (Fig. 6E). With respect to gastrointestinal dysfunction, HD-BTZ-treated GF mice demonstrated a 2.25-fold increase in full stomach weights (2.83 ± 0.660%BW) compared with vehicle-treated GF mice (1.26 ± 0.220%BW), but this did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.0634) (Fig. 6F). Treatment with HD-BTZ did not affect full stomach weights in FMT mice (Fig. 6F). Collectively, these data indicate that gut microbiota depletion using ABX does not profoundly influence bortezomib toxicity, yet GF mice display a milder symptom profile than SPF mice while bortezomib-induced side effects are not evident in FMT mice.

Faecal microbiota transplantation in GF mice partially restores neuronal sensitivity to bortezomib toxicity

Neuroimmune signalling is central to the aetiology of numerous neurological disorders affecting both the central and peripheral nervous system and is well documented to be influenced by the gut microbiota [70, 71]. We have previously shown that HD-BTZ induces neuroimmune signalling in both DRG and nodose ganglia in SPF mice (Figs. 2F-I and 4E). In contrast, no significant changes in Adgre1, Tnfa, Il6 and Il1b expression in the DRG or nodose ganglia were observed in GF mice treated with HD-BTZ compared with vehicle (Fig. S11A-D). However, Tnfa (P < 0.05) and Il1b (P < 0.05) expression were significantly elevated in the nodose ganglia of FMT mice treated with HD-BTZ compared with vehicle (Fig. S11E-H).

Given that neuroimmune signalling is considered a driver of chemotherapy-induced nerve damage, we then assessed the effect of bortezomib on serum NfL levels in GF and FMT mice. Consistent with previous experiments (Fig. 2J), serum NfL was profoundly elevated in HD-BTZ-treated SPF mice (1506 ± 133 pg/mL), compared with vehicle-treated counterparts (48.6 ± 10.3 pg/mL, P < 0.0001). In contrast, there was no significant increase in serum NfL in GF mice (HD-BTZ: 270 ± 32.1 pg/mL vs. vehicle: 85.4 ± 7.10 pg/mL). Notably, FMT mice had a partially re-established sensitivity to bortezomib-induced nerve damage, with serum NfL levels 7.36-fold higher in HD-BTZ-treated FMT mice (697 ± 174 pg/mL) than in vehicle-treated FMT mice (94.7 ± 16.1 pg/mL, P < 0.0001), and 2.59-fold higher than in HD-BTZ-treated GF mice (P < 0.01). However, serum NfL levels in HD-BTZ-treated FMT mice was 2.16-fold lower than in HD-BTZ-treated SPF mice (Fig. 6G). Collectively, these data provide evidence for a causal role of the gut microbiota in mediating bortezomib-induced neuroimmune signalling and nerve damage. Moreover, these data highlight that despite FMT re-establishing host sensitivity to bortezomib-induced nerve damage, it impedes overt symptomatic presentation of bortezomib side effects.

Discussion

Despite the rapidly evolving landscape in treatment of myeloma over the past two decades, the first-in-class proteasome inhibitor bortezomib remains integral to optimal treatment of the cancer. Peripheral neuropathy and gastrointestinal dysfunction are common side effects of bortezomib that lack effective intervention and thereby impact patient quality of life, treatment adherence and overall survival. Here, for the first time, we have assessed the gastrointestinal side effects induced by bortezomib in rodents and identified that neuroimmune changes in the vagus nerve may be related to their aetiology. Moreover, our studies in GF mice suggest that the gut microbiota is required for the induction of bortezomib-induced nerve damage and can modulate neuropathic symptoms and behavioural deficits associated with the drug, providing a rationale to explore microbiota-directed interventions for their utility in myeloma care.

An important feature of our study is the use of automated behavioural phenotyping to evaluate the effect of bortezomib treatment on spontaneous animal behaviour. Our behavioural phenotyping data demonstrate that HD-BTZ-treated SPF mice were more reluctant, or less able, to explore the testing arena, indicating a significant decline in animal well-being. This is consistent with the clinical situation where bortezomib side effects significantly reduce patient physical and mental functioning, substantively diminishing their quality of life [53, 54]. Self-limiting, cyclic weight loss experienced by mice treated with HD-BTZ is also a novel observation, likely reflecting the impact of individual bortezomib treatments on animal well-being. Another novel finding revealed by behavioural phenotyping was increased paw grooming in mice treated with HD-BTZ. This appears to align with the clinical presentation of peripheral neuropathy, which causes sensory changes and spontaneous pain in the hands and feet [4, 8, 9]. Whilst we cannot specifically attribute this to animal pain in paws, we speculate that this may relate, at least in part, to abnormal sensations or the sensory loss we observed in hind paws of HD-BTZ-treated SPF mice. Unlike current modelling of bortezomib-induced peripheral neuropathy that relies on stimulus-evoked sensory and motor function tests [13, 57–59], this is the first time that a spontaneous behavioural trait of increased paw grooming associated with bortezomib treatment has been reported in an animal model.

In addition to using novel behavioural analyses, our study is the first to model bortezomib-induced gastrointestinal side effects in mice. A striking and robust observation was a significant increase in full stomach weights in SPF mice. Considering that daily food intake did not change in HD-BTZ-treated mice over time and full stomach weights did not significantly differ to baseline weights 48 h after treatment, this suggests acute gastric retention; a phenotype consistent with delayed gastric emptying (gastroparesis) that is a documented clinical consequence of bortezomib treatment [72]. Indeed, enlarged and heavier stomachs in the absence of elevated food intake has previously been associated with gastroparesis in rodents treated with the neurotoxic chemotherapeutics oxaliplatin and cisplatin [73, 74]. Gastroparesis is commonly associated with nerve dysfunction, particularly to the autonomic vagus nerve, which plays a central role in controlling gastrointestinal function including gastric emptying [64, 75–77]. Thus, bortezomib-induced gastric dysfunction supports previous reports of bortezomib-related gastrointestinal autonomic neuropathy in people with myeloma, as well as other symptom profiles indicative of autonomic neuropathy, such as syncope, postural orthostatic hypotension and neurogenic bladder that have been associated with bortezomib use [18, 72, 78]. While our histological analyses did not identify vagus nerve damage, we did observe neuroimmune signalling changes in nodose ganglia, suggesting possible dysfunction of the vagus nerve induced by bortezomib could contribute to the observed gastric dysfunction and faster gastrointestinal transit time. This is further supported by the absence of overt inflammatory or anatomical changes in the gastrointestinal tract induced by bortezomib, which contrasts gastrointestinal damage induced by mucotoxic chemotherapy drugs such as cyclophosphamide, melphalan and irinotecan [63, 79, 80]. While there is currently no specific evidence of neuroimmune signalling in the nodose ganglia contributing to gastrointestinal dysfunction, general dysfunction of autonomic ganglia is well documented to alter autonomic function in the gastrointestinal tract. For example, acute autonomic ganglionopathy, a disorder characterised by the production of ganglionic acetylcholine receptor autoantibodies affecting autonomic ganglia, causes symptoms such as constipation and gastroparesis [81]. Furthermore, there is evidence of ganglionic neuroimmune signalling resulting in symptoms of gastrointestinal dysmotility including gastroparesis, although this specifically relates to changes in enteric ganglia of the myenteric plexus [82]. Thus, it is plausible that bortezomib may affect enteric nerves, or even spinal sensory nerves, to alter gastrointestinal function. Moreover, while we speculate that drug-neuroimmune interactions are a predominant cause of gastrointenstinal dysfunction associated with bortezomib, we cannot exclude the possibility that increased intestinal permeability observed in drug-treated mice may also contribute to the phenotype. Studies are required to investigate this possibility and whether increased permeability may promote the transfer of signals from the gut lumen (e.g. hormones, cytokines or bacterial products) to the circulation to alter nerve function. Ultimately, however, it is likely that gastrointestinal symptoms of bortezomib are multifactorial in nature, with these events co-occurring to dictate the clinical manifestation.

Previous studies suggest that elevated serum neurofilament light chain (NfL) levels are associated with the extent of chemotherapy-induced sensory peripheral nerve damage and electrophysiological dysfunction in rodents [83]. Despite no change reported in serum NfL levels in rats treated with bortezomib [84] and the lack of overt neuropathology observed in either the sciatic and vagus nerves in our studies, serum NfL was elevated in our SPF mice treated with bortezomib; representing the first report of elevated NfL levels in rodents undergoing bortezomib treatment. What remains unclear is the source of NfL, as it is not exclusive to sensory peripheral nerves and may therefore reflect axonal injury in the autonomic nervous system and even the CNS. While the ability of bortezomib to cross the blood-brain-barrier is limited [85], peripheral nerve inflammation and damage can trigger CNS neuroimmune signalling via indirect mechanisms [86]. Thus, NfL could potentially originate from CNS neurons (e.g. in the brain) affected by peripheral inflammatory signals induced by bortezomib. Previous studies have also reported neuroimmune signalling changes at L4-L6 spinal cord dorsal horns in bortezomib-treated rodents [13, 15], suggesting immune signalling in spinal cord dorsal horns (where central terminals of DRG neurons reside) could contribute to elevated serum NfL levels in our bortezomib-treated mice. However, the clinical utility of NfL as a biomarker of drug-induced peripheral neuropathy and association with bortezomib-induced peripheral nerve dysfunction in myeloma patients [45, 62] supports that elevated NfL levels in our bortezomib-treated mice likely reflect, at least in part, sensory peripheral nerve damage. Whilst our inclusion of NfL as an objective measure of nerve damage represents a significant advance in the field of modelling bortezomib side effects in mice, further studies are required to identify its precise source(s) and also its specificity as a biomarker of bortezomib-induced peripheral neuropathy in mice given the neuroimmune changes we observed in the vagus nerve of drug-treated animals.

The gut microbiota is rapidly emerging as a central modulator of cancer drug toxicity. Previous studies in GF mice have causally implicated the gut microbiota in chemotherapy side effects, with FMT restoring oxaliplatin- and paclitaxel-induced peripheral neuropathy and irinotecan-induced gastrointentinal toxicity to that observed in conventional mice [36–38]. Our observation that FMT mice did not recapitulate bortezomib side effect profiles seen in SPF mice suggests that the gut microbiota may not be a major driver of this symptom profile. In support of this, antibiotic-treated SPF mice demonstrated, overall, a similar side effect profile to non-antibiotic-treated mice. However, by performing a battery of assessments (behavioural, performance, molecular and biomarker) in GF mice, complexity in the gut microbiota’s role becomes evident. Our studies in GF mice provide two lines of evidence that the gut microbiota may modulate the development of bortezomib side effects. Firstly, peripheral neuropathy was not as evident in GF mice compared with SPF mice. The gut microbiota is well established to shape host immune system development, with GF mice having defective innate and adaptive immune responses to physiological challenges [87, 88]. Indeed, neuroimmune signalling changes were not observed in DRGs and nodose ganglia of bortezomib-treated GF mice, consistent with low serum NfL levels observed in these mice. As neuroimmune signalling contributes to the pathogenesis of bortezomib-induced peripheral neuropathy [14, 59], it is likely that the absence of a pro-inflammatory response within DRGs of bortezomib-treated GF mice minimised peripheral nerve injury and, subsequently, the development of peripheral neuropathy. Secondly, bortezomib-induced nerve damage and neuroimmune signalling was partially restored in FMT mice relative to GF mice, suggesting the gut microbiota may, at least in part, modulate the neurotoxicity of bortezomib. Despite this, overt behavioural deficits caused by bortezomib were not observed in FMT mice. Whilst this may reflect an insufficient level of nerve damage in FMT mice to induce symptomatic neuropathic effects of bortezomib, it is plausible that colonisation of GF mice with a healthy gut microbiota from SPF mice induced a level of host resilience to the neurotoxic effects of the drug. This begs the question: how can bortezomib-treated FMT mice have clear biochemical evidence of nerve damage without associated functional deficits? Notably, the gut microbiota is well established to influence central and peripheral nervous system function and processing, involving immune, endocrine and metabolic pathways [89, 90], with previous studies showing that healthy-donor FMT restores normal gut function and innate and adaptive immune function in GF mice [91–93]. Indeed, the restorative effects of microbial colonisation on host immune function may account for the partially restored neuroimmune signalling and neurotoxic response to bortezomib in FMT mice. The utility of FMT to protect against chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy has also previously been reported. Specifically, FMT mediated protection against paclitaxel-induced peripheral neuropathy in conventional rats which was associated with maintenance of gut microbiota stability, inhibition of colonic TLR4 signalling and reduced glial cell reactivity in the colon and spinal dorsal horn [94]. As bortezomib activates TLR4 signalling and glial cells in both central and peripheral nerves [95], it is plausible that reduced TLR4 signalling or modulation of microglial cell activity could, at least in part, mediate the protective effects of FMT in the context of bortezomib side effects seen in our study. Moreover, short-chain fatty acids; the major metabolic by-product of commensal gut microbiota, have been shown to decrease microglial cell reactivity and promote anti-inflammatory processes in the CNS [96]. Thus, FMT-mediated short-chain fatty acid production in GF mice could dampen microglial cell reactivity and neuronal signalling in the CNS to limit its response to peripheral nerve injury. In addition, serotonin; a neurotransmitter that is important for mood, behaviour and overall well-being, influences CNS function through diverse mechanisms [97, 98]. Notably, serotonin is significantly lower in GF mice than in SPF counterparts, with the introduction of a gut microbiota via FMT in GF mice restoring serotonin levels [99]. Hence, it is plausible that the restoration of this “feel good” hormone may mask underlying neuropathic events and explain the lack of behavioural deficits observed in FMT mice, despite the presence of nerve injury.

Some chemotherapy drugs, in particular high-dose conditioning agents such as melphalan, are associated with profound gut microbiota changes, including expansion of enteric pathogens [80]. In comparison, the microbial changes we observed with bortezomib in SPF mice appeared mild and transient. Nonetheless, the decrease in alpha diversity and expansion of Lactobacillus gasseri (L. gasseri) at F1 were significant. Moreover, these gut microbiota read-outs were inversely correlated, suggesting L.gasseri expansion is likely responsible for the decrease in alpha diversity. Notably, an inverse correlation between Lactobacillus abundance and alpha diversity, together with Lactobacillus expansion, has previously been reported in mice treated with the neurotoxic drug paclitaxel [100]. However, why microbial changes with bortezomib resolve despite ongoing treatment remains to be determined. Another important question arising from this work is whether gut dysbiosis contributes to the etiology of bortezomib side effects. Given the gut microbiota is a dynamic regulator of the intestinal barrier [101], bortezomib-induced gut microbiota changes could potentially account for the observed increase in intestinal permeability seen in drug-treated SPF mice. Disruption or changes in the composition of the gut microbiota are widely associated with gastrointestinal dysfunction and neurodegenerative conditions including Alzheimer’s and Parkinson’s disease [102, 103]. Moreover, gut dysbiosis is increasingly implicated in chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy through modulation of immune cell function and neuroimmune signalling [71, 104]. Notably, our analyses revealed an inverse correlation between L.gasseri relative abundance and paw withdrawal threshold at F1 in bortezomib-treated mice. Interestingly, L.gasseri has been used as a probiotic, demonstrating beneficial gastrointestinal effects in people with dyspepsia and irritable bowel syndrome [105–107]. Thus, it is plausible that the expansion of L.gasseri at F1 with bortezomib treatment could potentially be a protective response intended to limit or overcome drug side effects in the host.

This study also has some limitations that warrant consideration. Firstly, 16S rRNA sequencing does not provide deep taxonomic or functional insights into the gut microbiota. Given the high degree of functional redundancy within microbial communities, compositional changes may not necessarily reflect altered biological activity [108]. Accordingly, future metagenomic or metabolomic analyses are likely required to determine whether bortezomib induces functionally relevant gut microbiota perturbations. Secondly, our study does not investigate potential mechanisms of FMT-mediated protection against bortezomib side effects in GF mice. Whilst we speculate this could be driven by immune, metabolic and hormonal events, further studies are required to decipher potential effects of FMT on host GF mouse biology that induce resilience to bortezomib side effects. Finally, our side effects studies are limited to male mice. While the use of male mice is consistent with a high proportion of existing bortezomib- and chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy studies performed in rodents, pain responses, neuroimmune signalling and gut microbiota composition can differ between male and female mice [109–111]. Thus, future studies assessing the role of the gut microbiota in bortezomib side effects in female mice are warranted.

Conclusions

We have, for the first time, described the effect of bortezomib on spontaneous animal behaviour indicative of reduced well-being and generated new knowledge on how to model and measure concurrent neuropathologies associated with bortezomib in a clinically-relevant manner. Using this model, we have revealed potential mechanisms underpinning gastrointestinal dysfunction associated with bortezomib, implicating neuroimmune changes in the vagus nerve. Our data indicate that the gut microbiota is not a primary driver of bortezomib side effects. However, the absence of a symptom profile in bortezomib-treated FMT mice, despite clear evidence of nerve damage, suggests that the gut microbiota may shape host biology in GF mice in a manner that protects against the manifestation of overt side effects related to bortezomib neurotoxicity. The paradoxical dissociation between behavioural symptoms and nerve injury in FMT mice is highly relevant, both methodologically and translationally. Firstly, it underscores the importance of using a comprehensive battery of assessments, including behavioural, molecular and biomarker, to fully characterise the phenotype associated with bortezomib toxicity. Secondly, it highlights a potential opportunity to decrease symptomatic bortezomib side effects independently of underlying neuropathology. Further studies are required to unravel the underlying mechanisms of this observation and to investigate whether the protective effects of FMT are recapitulated in a non-GF mouse context or could be harnessed in the clinical setting of myeloma treatment.
