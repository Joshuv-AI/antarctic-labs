Special geodesics and atypical intersections
Matteo Tamiozzo

Abstract
. Let C C be a complex irreducible plane curve that is not the vanishing locus of a modular polynomial. We show that C C contains finitely many real algebraic curves whose projection on each coordinate axis is a union of special geodesics.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Special properties of real quadratic geodesics

In Darmon and Vonk’s approach to explicit class field theory for real quadratic fields
[
DV21
]
, geodesics in the upper half-plane
\mathbf{H}
whose endpoints in
\mathbf{P}^{1}(\mathbf{R})
are real quadratic conjugates play a role analogous to the one of Heegner points in the theory of complex multiplication. In view of this, one is led to wonder which properties making Heegner points “special” are shared by the above geodesics. Let us mention two such properties, of different flavor.

(1)

Formally analogous equidistribution statements hold true for Heegner points and for geodesics with conjugate real quadratic endpoints: see
[
Duk88
]
- and
[
PP25
]
for a recent development in this direction - as well as
[
Mic04
]
,
[
Nor23
]
.

(2)

Heegner points in
\mathbf{H}\subset\mathbf{C}
are the only algebraic points whose image via the
j
-invariant is algebraic. A suitable “geometric” counterpart of this statement holds true for geodesics with conjugate real quadratic endpoints, as well as for those with rational endpoints
[
Tam23
, §3]
.

1.2.
Detecting strongly special curves

Let
Y(1)
be the modular curve with full level, isomorphic to
\mathbf{A}^{1}_{\mathbf{C}}
. For every integer
N\geq 1
, the modular polynomial
\Phi_{N}(T_{1},T_{2})
defines an irreducible complex algebraic curve inside
Y(1)\times Y(1)\simeq\mathrm{Spec}(\mathbf{C}[T_{1},T_{2}])
, image of the Hecke correspondence
Y_{0}(N)\rightarrow Y(1)\times Y(1)
. A curve of this form is called
strongly special
. Another feature of Heegner points is that they can be used to detect strongly special curves in
Y(1)\times Y(1)
. Indeed, André proved the following result - a special case of the André–Oort conjecture.

1.2.1 Theorem
.

(André
[
And98
]
)
An irreducible complex algebraic curve in
Y(1)\times Y(1)
that is not horizontal nor vertical is strongly special if and only if it contains infinitely many points whose coordinates are Heegner points.

1.2.2.

Let us call
real quadratic geodesic
in
\mathbf{H}
a geodesic with conjugate real quadratic endpoints; the image via
j:\mathbf{H}\rightarrow\mathbf{C}=Y(1)(\mathbf{C})
of a real quadratic geodesic in
\mathbf{H}
will be called a real quadratic geodesic in
Y(1)
. In line with the above discussion, one may ask for a characterization of strongly special curves in
Y(1)\times Y(1)
involving real quadratic geodesics rather than Heegner points. Precisely, Darmon raised
1
1

1

After Scanlon’s talk “What is… O-Minimality?” at MSRI, on May 15th, 2023.
the following question.

1.2.3.
Question

Let
C\subset Y(1)\times Y(1)
be an irreducible complex algebraic curve. Assume that
C
contains infinitely many real algebraic curves whose projection to each copy of
Y(1)
is a union of real quadratic geodesics. Is
C
a strongly special curve?

1.2.4.

The aim of this note is to give a positive answer to the above question; this will be achieved in
2.4.6
. In fact, our path to the answer will lead us to a more general result, Theorem
2.3.3
below.

The key idea behind our approach to Question
1.2.3
is to interpret it from an intersection-theoretic viewpoint. In the question, we are implicitly identifying the complex points of
Y(1)
with the real points of
\mathbf{A}^{2}_{\mathbf{R}}
. The curve
C
gives rise via Weil restriction to a real surface
\tilde{C}
in
\mathbf{A}^{2}_{\mathbf{R}}\times\mathbf{A}^{2}_{\mathbf{R}}
. We are interested in real algebraic curves - whose properties will be made more precise in
2.4.6
- in
\mathbf{A}^{2}_{\mathbf{R}}\times\mathbf{A}^{2}_{\mathbf{R}}
, whose real points are contained in sets of the form
\mathcal{S}\cap\tilde{C}(\mathbf{R})
, where
\mathcal{S}\subset\mathbf{R}^{2}\times\mathbf{R}^{2}
is a union of products of real quadratic geodesics in
Y(1)
. Note that the intersection
\mathcal{S}\cap\tilde{C}(\mathbf{R})
is “atypically large”: as
\tilde{C}(\mathbf{R})
and
\mathcal{S}
are two-dimensional subsets of
\mathbf{R}^{4}
, one would expect their intersection to be zero-dimensional.

This viewpoint suggests to relate the above question to the Zilber–Pink conjecture, predicting finiteness of the set of “atypical components” of a complex subvariety of a Shimura variety. We obtain such a relation via the base change strategy already employed for different purposes in
[
Tam23
]
; we then deduce a positive answer to Question
1.2.3
from a known case of a weak version of the Zilber–Pink conjecture for
Y(1)^{4}

[
PT16
]
.

1.2.5 Remark
.

Note that, if
C
is strongly special, then
\tilde{C}
does contain infinitely many real algebraic curves as in Question
1.2.3
, which can be constructed intersecting
\tilde{C}\subset\mathbf{A}^{2}_{\mathbf{R}}\times\mathbf{A}^{2}_{\mathbf{R}}
with suitable real curves
Z_{M}
in the first copy of
\mathbf{A}^{2}_{\mathbf{R}}
, cf.
2.4.1
.

2.
Real atypical intersections in
Y(1)\times Y(1)

2.1.
The Zilber–Pink conjecture for
Y(1)^{n}

Let us quickly recall the terminology and the result from
[
PT16
, §7]
that we will need. Fix an integer
n\geq 1
, and let
Y(1)^{n}=\mathrm{Spec}(\mathbf{C}[T_{1},\ldots,T_{n}])
. By a subvariety of
Y(1)^{n}
we always mean an irreducible closed subset (with its reduced subscheme structure). A special subvariety of
Y(1)^{n}
is an irreducible component of a closed subset defined by finitely many equations either of the form
\Phi_{N}(T_{i},T_{j})=0
, for
1\leq i<j\leq n
and
N\geq 1
, or of the form
T_{i}=c
for
1\leq i\leq n
, where
c\in\mathbf{C}
is a Heegner point.

Let
V\subset Y(1)^{n}
be a subvariety. An atypical subvariety of
V
is an irreducible component
A
of an intersection
V\cap S
, where
S
is a special subvariety of
Y(1)^{n}
, such that

\dim(A)>\dim(V)+\dim(S)-n.

We say that
A
is strongly atypical if it is atypical and no coordinate is constant on
A\subset Y(1)^{n}
.

2.1.1 Remark
.

(1)

Note that the above inequality is equivalent to the inequality
\mathrm{codim}(A)<\mathrm{codim}(V)+\mathrm{codim}(S)
, where codimensions are taken in
Y(1)^{n}
; intuitively, this tells us that
V\cap S
is “atypically large”.

(2)

Observe that
V
is an atypical subvariety of itself if and only if it is contained in a special subvariety
S
different from
Y(1)^{n}
.

2.1.2 Theorem
.

(Pila–Tsimerman,
[
PT16
, Theorem 7.1]
) Every subvariety
V\subset Y(1)^{n}
contains finitely many maximal strongly atypical subvarieties.

2.1.3 Remark
.

(1)

The Zilber–Pink conjecture
[
PT16
, Conjecture 7.1]
predicts that the theorem still holds true if one replaces strongly atypical subvarieties by atypical ones; see
[
Pil22
]
and
[
DO25
]
for background and further results on this conjecture.

(2)

Pila and Tsimerman’s proof of the above theorem relies on
o
-minimality. A different proof, making use of differential algebra, is given in
[
Asl22
, §5]
(cf. Theorem 5.2 and Remark 5.5).

2.2.
Special geodesics in
Y(1)

As in
[
Tam23
, Definition 3.3.5, Remark 3.3.6]
, we call
special geodesic
in
\mathbf{H}
a curve
\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{A}}=\{z\in\mathbf{H}\mid\mathsf{A}z=\bar{z}\}
for some matrix
\mathsf{A}\in\mathrm{M}_{2}(\mathbf{Q})
with trace zero and strictly negative determinant. If
\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{A}}\subset\mathbf{H}
is a special geodesic, we call
j(\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{A}})
a special geodesic in
Y(1)
.

2.2.1 Remark
.

The action on
\mathbf{H}
of a matrix
\mathsf{B}\in\mathrm{GL}_{2}(\mathbf{Q})
with positive determinant sends special geodesics to special geodesics: indeed, we have
\mathsf{B}\cdot\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{A}}=\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{B}\mathsf{A}\mathsf{B}^{-1}}
.

2.2.2.

Special geodesics in
Y(1)
are contained in real algebraic curves
Z_{N}
defined as follows: for an integer
N\geq 1
, let
Z_{N}\subset\mathbf{A}^{2}_{\mathbf{R}}
be the curve with equation
\Phi_{N}(X+iY,X-iY)=0
. These curves enjoy the following properties:

(i)

for every
N\geq 1
, the curve
Z_{N}
is geometrically integral;

(ii)

every special geodesic in
Y(1)
is contained in
Z_{N}
for some
N\geq 1
;

(iii)

for every
N\geq 1
, the set
Z_{N}(\mathbf{R})
is a union of special geodesics.

The first two properties are proved in
[
Tam23
, §3.5.1]
. To prove the third, note that if
(x,y)\in\mathbf{R}^{2}
belongs to
Z_{N}(\mathbf{R})
, then the elliptic curves with
j
-invariants
x+iy
and
x-iy
are related by an isogeny with cyclic kernel of cardinality
N
. Writing
x+iy=j(z)
, we have
x-iy=j(-\bar{z})
, and one sees as in
[
Tam23
, §3.5.3]
that there is a matrix
\mathsf{A}\in\mathrm{M}_{2}(\mathbf{Z})
with determinant
-N
such that
\mathsf{A}z=\bar{z}
.

2.2.3.

We will later make use of the map
f:\mathrm{Spec}(\mathbf{C}[X,Y])\rightarrow\mathrm{Spec}(\mathbf{C}[X,Y])
induced by the morphism of
\mathbf{C}
-algebras sending
(X,Y)
to
(X+iY,X-iY)
. For
N\geq 1
, the base change to
\mathbf{C}
of
Z_{N}
is the preimage via
f
of the strongly special curve with equation
\Phi_{N}(X,Y)=0
.

2.3.
Curves with special geodesic projections

Let
p_{1}:\mathbf{A}^{2}_{\mathbf{R}}\times\mathbf{A}^{2}_{\mathbf{R}}\rightarrow\mathbf{A}^{2}_{\mathbf{R}}
and
p_{2}:\mathbf{A}^{2}_{\mathbf{R}}\times\mathbf{A}^{2}_{\mathbf{R}}\rightarrow\mathbf{A}^{2}_{\mathbf{R}}
denote the two projections. By a real algebraic curve in
\mathbf{A}^{2}_{\mathbf{R}}\times\mathbf{A}^{2}_{\mathbf{R}}
we mean a reduced, closed subscheme of dimension one.

2.3.1 Definition
.

An infinite subset
\mathcal{Z}\subset\mathbf{R}^{2}\times\mathbf{R}^{2}
is a curve with special geodesic projections if it has the following properties:

(1)

there is an irreducible real algebraic curve
Z\subset\mathbf{A}^{2}_{\mathbf{R}}\times\mathbf{A}^{2}_{\mathbf{R}}
such that
Z(\mathbf{R})=\mathcal{Z}
;

(2)

the images
p_{1}(\mathcal{Z})
and
p_{2}(\mathcal{Z})
are not singletons, and are contained in a union of special geodesics.

2.3.2.

Let
\mathcal{Z}\subset\mathbf{R}^{2}\times\mathbf{R}^{2}
be an infinite set such that
p_{1}(\mathcal{Z})
and
p_{2}(\mathcal{Z})
are not singletons. The set
\mathcal{Z}
is a curve with special geodesic projections if and only if there exist integers
N_{1},N_{2}\geq 1
and an irreducible real algebraic curve
Z\subset Z_{N_{1}}\times Z_{N_{2}}
such that
\mathcal{Z}=Z(\mathbf{R})
. Indeed, if
\mathcal{Z}
is of this form, then
p_{i}(\mathcal{Z})\subset Z_{N_{i}}(\mathbf{R})
for
i\in\{1,2\}
, and
Z_{N_{i}}(\mathbf{R})
is a union of special geodesics by
2.2.2
(iii). Conversely, assume that
\mathcal{Z}
has special geodesic projections. Then, by
2.2.2
(ii), the set
\mathcal{Z}=Z(\mathbf{R})
is contained in the union of the sets
Z_{N_{1}}(\mathbf{R})\times Z_{N_{2}}(\mathbf{R})
, as
N_{1}
and
N_{2}
range over all strictly positive integers. For a fixed couple
(N_{1},N_{2})
, the intersection
Z\cap(Z_{N_{1}}\times Z_{N_{2}})
is a Zariski closed subset of
Z
, hence it is either
Z
or a finite set. The set
\mathcal{Z}
, being infinite, contains a smooth point of
Z
, hence an open neighborhood in the Euclidean topology homeomorphic to an interval. In particular,
\mathcal{Z}
is uncountable, hence there exists a couple
(N_{1},N_{2})
such that
Z\subset Z_{N_{1}}\times Z_{N_{2}}
.

The following is our main result, which can be thought of as analogous to Theorem
1.2.1
.

2.3.3 Theorem
.

An irreducible complex algebraic curve
C\subset Y(1)\times Y(1)
which is not horizontal nor vertical is strongly special if and only if
C(\mathbf{C})
contains infinitely many curves with special geodesic projections.

2.3.4 Remark
.

Special geodesics in
Y(1)
contain infinitely many Heegner points (cf. the proof of
[
Tam23
, Proposition 3.3.7]
); however, curves with special geodesic projections may contain only finitely many points
(x_{1},y_{1},x_{2},y_{2})
such that
x_{1}+iy_{1}
and
x_{2}+iy_{2}
are Heegner points. For instance, for any
\lambda\in\mathbf{R}^{\times}
the set
\{(x_{1},y_{1},x_{2},y_{2})\in\mathbf{R}^{4}\mid y_{1}=y_{2}=0,x_{2}=\lambda x_{1}\}
is a curve with special geodesic projections (cf.
[
Tam23
, §3.5.2]
); if
\lambda
is transcendental and
x_{1}\neq 0
, then
x_{1}
and
\lambda x_{1}
cannot be both Heegner points.

Therefore, it is not clear to us if Theorem
2.3.3
can be deduced from Theorem
1.2.1
. Instead, as announced in the introduction we will follow a different path, and deduce (one of the implications of) Theorem
2.3.3
from Theorem
2.1.2
for
n=4
.

2.4.
Proof of the main result

We will now prove Theorem
2.3.3
. The restriction of scalars from
\mathbf{C}
to
\mathbf{R}
of
Y(1)\times Y(1)=\mathrm{Spec}(\mathbf{C}[T_{1},T_{2}])
is
\mathrm{Spec}(\mathbf{R}[X_{1},Y_{1},X_{2},Y_{2}])
, the bijection between
\mathbf{R}
-points of the latter and
\mathbf{C}
-points of the former being the map which sends
(x_{1},y_{1},x_{2},y_{2})
to
(x_{1}+iy_{1},x_{2}+iy_{2})
.

2.4.1.

Let us first show that, if
C\subset Y(1)\times Y(1)
is a strongly special curve, then
C(\mathbf{C})
contains infinitely many curves with special geodesic projections. The curve
C
has equation
\Phi_{N}(T_{1},T_{2})=0
for some
N\geq 1
; let
\tilde{C}=\mathrm{Res}_{\mathbf{C}/\mathbf{R}}C\subset\mathbf{A}^{2}_{\mathbf{R}}\times\mathbf{A}^{2}_{\mathbf{R}}
. Given an integer
M\geq 1
, consider the intersection
\tilde{C}\cap(Z_{M}\times\mathbf{A}^{2}_{\mathbf{R}})\subset\mathbf{A}^{2}_{\mathbf{R}}\times\mathbf{A}^{2}_{\mathbf{R}}
. First of all, we claim that the images via
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
of
\tilde{C}(\mathbf{R})\cap(Z_{M}(\mathbf{R})\times\mathbf{R}^{2})
are contained in a union of special geodesics. For
p_{1}
, the claim follows from
2.2.2
(iii). To prove the claim for
p_{2}
note that, for a point
(x_{1},y_{1},x_{2},y_{2})\in\tilde{C}(\mathbf{R})
, two elliptic curves with
j
-invariants
x_{1}+iy_{1}
and
x_{2}+iy_{2}
are related by an isogeny with cyclic kernel of cardinality
N
. Writing
x_{1}+iy_{1}=j(z_{1})
and
x_{2}+iy_{2}=j(z_{2})
we see that there is a matrix
\mathsf{A}\in\mathrm{M}_{2}(\mathbf{Z})
with positive determinant such that
z_{2}=\mathsf{A}z_{1}
. If
z_{1}
belongs to a special geodesic, then so does
z_{2}
by Remark
2.2.1
.

Let
Z\subset\tilde{C}\cap(Z_{M}\times\mathbf{A}^{2}_{\mathbf{R}})
be an irreducible component such that
\mathcal{Z}=Z(\mathbf{R})
is infinite. The set
\mathcal{Z}
is a curve with special geodesic projections. Indeed, observe that
\tilde{C}
is not contained in
Z_{M}\times\mathbf{A}^{2}_{\mathbf{R}}
(the projection of
C\subset Y(1)\times Y(1)
on the first factor is
Y(1)
). As the variety
Z_{M}\times\mathbf{A}^{2}_{\mathbf{R}}
is cut out by one equation in
\mathbf{A}^{2}_{\mathbf{R}}\times\mathbf{A}^{2}_{\mathbf{R}}
, the irreducible component
Z
has codimension one in
\tilde{C}

[
GW20
, Theorem 5.32]
. Finally, the restrictions of
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
to
\tilde{C}
are finite, hence the maps
\mathcal{Z}\rightarrow\mathbf{R}^{2}
induced by
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
have finite fibers. It follows that the sets
p_{1}(\mathcal{Z})
and
p_{2}(\mathcal{Z})
are not singletons.

Varying
M
, we obtain infinitely many curves with special geodesic projections contained in
\tilde{C}(\mathbf{R})=C(\mathbf{C})
.

2.4.2.
Setup

Now let
C\subset Y(1)\times Y(1)
be an irreducible complex algebraic curve whose complex points contain infinitely many curves with special geodesic projections. We will prove that
C
is strongly special. Let
\tilde{C}=\mathrm{Res}_{\mathbf{C}/\mathbf{R}}C\subset\mathrm{Spec}(\mathbf{R}[X_{1},Y_{1},X_{2},Y_{2}])
. We will use below the fact that, as
C
is not horizontal nor vertical, its projection on each copy of
Y(1)
contains a non-empty Zariski open set
[
GW20
, Theorem 10.19]
.

Let
Z\subset\mathbf{A}^{2}_{\mathbf{R}}\times\mathbf{A}^{2}_{\mathbf{R}}
be an irreducible real algebraic curve such that
\mathcal{Z}=Z(\mathbf{R})
is a curve with special geodesic projections, and
\mathcal{Z}
is contained in
\tilde{C}(\mathbf{R})
. The intersection
Z\cap\tilde{C}
is a closed subset of
Z
containing infinitely many points, hence we have
Z\subset\tilde{C}
. Furthermore, by
2.3.2
, there exist integers
N_{1},N_{2}\geq 1
such that
Z
is contained in
Z_{N_{1},N_{2}}=Z_{N_{1}}\times Z_{N_{2}}
; hence,
Z
is contained in
\tilde{C}\cap Z_{N_{1},N_{2}}
.

2.4.3.
Base change

Let
\tilde{C}_{\mathbf{C}}\subset\mathbf{A}^{2}_{\mathbf{C}}\times\mathbf{A}^{2}_{\mathbf{C}}
be the base change of
\tilde{C}
, and let
Z_{\mathbf{C}}
be the base change of
Z
. Consider the map

f^{2}=f\times f:\mathbf{A}^{2}_{\mathbf{C}}\times\mathbf{A}^{2}_{\mathbf{C}}\rightarrow\mathbf{A}^{2}_{\mathbf{C}}\times\mathbf{A}^{2}_{\mathbf{C}},

where
f
was defined in
2.2.3
. Let us denote by
T_{1},T_{2},T_{3}
and
T_{4}
the coordinates on the target
\mathbf{A}^{4}_{\mathbf{C}}
of
f^{2}
. Let
V=f^{2}(\tilde{C}_{\mathbf{C}})
, let
A=f^{2}(Z_{\mathbf{C}})
, and let
S=S_{N_{1},N_{2}}
be the special subvariety of
\mathbf{A}^{2}_{\mathbf{C}}\times\mathbf{A}^{2}_{\mathbf{C}}
with equations
\Phi_{N_{1}}(T_{1},T_{2})=\Phi_{N_{2}}(T_{3},T_{4})=0
. Note that
S
and
V
are irreducible surfaces, and they are distinct: otherwise, we would have
\tilde{C}_{\mathbf{C}}=(Z_{N_{1},N_{2}})\times_{\mathbf{R}}\mathbf{C}
, therefore
\tilde{C}(\mathbf{R})=Z_{N_{1}}(\mathbf{R})\times Z_{N_{2}}(\mathbf{R})
. But the projection of
\tilde{C}(\mathbf{R})\subset\mathbf{R}^{2}\times\mathbf{R}^{2}
to the first copy of
\mathbf{R}^{2}
contains all but finitely many points.

Let us show that
A
is a strongly atypical subvariety of
V
. First of all, the curve
Z
is irreducible and contains infinitely many real points, hence
Z_{\mathbf{C}}
is irreducible
[
Sta26
,
Tag 0G69
]
. It follows that
A
is irreducible, therefore it is an irreducible component of
S\cap V
(as
S\cap V
has dimension at most one). Finally, no coordinate is constant on
A
. Indeed, the composite of the inclusion
\mathbf{R}^{2}\times\mathbf{R}^{2}\subset\mathbf{C}^{2}\times\mathbf{C}^{2}
and of
f^{2}
sends
(x_{1},y_{1},x_{2},y_{2})
to
(x_{1}+iy_{1},x_{1}-iy_{1},x_{2}+iy_{2},x_{2}-iy_{2})
. If the first or second (resp. third or fourth) coordinate was constant on
A
, then
(x_{1},y_{1})
(resp.
(x_{2},y_{2})
) would be constant on
\mathcal{Z}
, contradicting Definition
2.3.1
.

2.4.4.
Strongly atypical curves in
V

Let us now prove that
V
contains infinitely many strongly atypical curves. As
\tilde{C}\cap Z_{N_{1},N_{2}}
has finitely many irreducible components, there are finitely many curves with special geodesic projections contained in a given
Z_{N_{1},N_{2}}(\mathbf{R})
and in
\tilde{C}(\mathbf{R})
. It follows that there are infinitely many mutually distinct
Z_{N_{1},N_{2}}
whose real points contain curves
\mathcal{Z}\subset\tilde{C}(\mathbf{R})
with special geodesic projections. Therefore, it suffices to show the following statement: take
Z\subset\tilde{C}\cap Z_{N_{1},N_{2}}
and
Z^{\prime}\subset\tilde{C}\cap Z_{N_{1}^{\prime},N_{2}^{\prime}}
such that
Z(\mathbf{R})
and
Z^{\prime}(\mathbf{R})
are curves with special geodesic projections. If
Z_{N_{1},N_{2}}
and
Z_{N_{1}^{\prime},N_{2}^{\prime}}
are distinct surfaces in
\mathbf{A}^{2}_{\mathbf{R}}
then the curves
A=f^{2}(Z_{\mathbf{C}})
and
A^{\prime}=f^{2}(Z^{\prime}_{\mathbf{C}})
in
\mathbf{A}^{4}_{\mathbf{C}}
are distinct. Suppose that
Z_{N_{1}}\neq Z_{N_{1}^{\prime}}
- if
Z_{N_{2}}\neq Z_{N_{2}^{\prime}}
, the following argument applies
mutatis mutandis
. Then, the complex plane curves with equation
\Phi_{N_{1}}(T_{1},T_{2})=0
and
\Phi_{N_{1}^{\prime}}(T_{1},T_{2})=0
are different. It follows that the projection of
S_{N_{1},N_{2}}\cap S_{N_{1}^{\prime},N_{2}^{\prime}}\subset\mathbf{A}^{2}_{\mathbf{C}}\times\mathbf{A}^{2}_{\mathbf{C}}
to the first copy of
\mathbf{A}^{2}_{\mathbf{C}}
has finite image. On the other hand, as no coordinate is constant on
A
, the composite of the inclusion
A\subset\mathbf{A}^{4}_{\mathbf{C}}
and the projection on any coordinate has infinite image. Therefore, the curve
A\subset S_{N_{1},N_{2}}
cannot be contained in
S_{N_{1}^{\prime},N_{2}^{\prime}}
; in particular, we have
A\neq A^{\prime}
.

2.4.5.
Application of Zilber–Pink

By Theorem
2.1.2
the strongly atypical curves in
V
described in the previous paragraph cannot all be maximal strongly atypical subvarieties. Therefore, the surface
V
itself must be strongly atypical. Let us deduce that
C
is a strongly special curve.

By Remark
2.1.1
, the surface
V=f^{2}(\tilde{C}_{\mathbf{C}})
is contained in a strongly special subvariety
S\subsetneq\mathbf{A}^{4}_{\mathbf{C}}
, which is a component of the vanishing locus of finitely many modular polynomials
\Phi_{N}(T_{j},T_{k})
, with
1\leq j<k\leq 4
. To conclude, we make the following observations on the equations defining
S
.

(i)

There cannot be any equation
\Phi_{N}(T_{j},T_{k})=0
with
j=1,k=2
. Indeed, this would imply that the points
(x_{1},y_{1},x_{2},y_{2})\in\tilde{C}(\mathbf{R})
satisfy an equation of the form
\Phi_{N}(x_{1}+iy_{1},x_{1}-iy_{1})=0
. But the projection of
C(\mathbf{C})\subset\mathbf{C}\times\mathbf{C}
on the first component contains all but finitely many points, hence it cannot be contained in
Z_{N}(\mathbf{R})
. For the same reason, there cannot be any equation defining
S
with
j=3,k=4
.

(ii)

If there is an equation defining
S
with
j=1,k=3
then the points
(x_{1},y_{1},x_{2},y_{2})\in\tilde{C}(\mathbf{R})
satisfy an equation of the form
\Phi_{N}(x_{1}+iy_{1},x_{2}+iy_{2})=0
. Therefore, the curve
C\subset Y(1)\times Y(1)
is contained in, hence equal to, the strongly special curve cut out by
\Phi_{N}
. Similarly, if there is an equation defining
S
with
j=2,k=4
then
C
is a strongly special curve.

(iii)

If there is an equation defining
S
with
(j,k)=(2,3)
or
(j,k)=(1,4)
then the points
(x_{1},y_{1},x_{2},y_{2})\in\tilde{C}(\mathbf{R})
satisfy an equation of the form
\Phi_{N}(x_{1}-iy_{1},x_{2}+iy_{2})=0
. As
C(\mathbf{C})\subset\mathbf{C}\times\mathbf{C}
contains infinitely many points with real first coordinate, we see that the intersection between
C
and the complex curve cut out by
\Phi_{N}
is infinite. It follows that
C
is the strongly special curve with equation
\Phi_{N}=0
.

2.4.6.
Answer to Question
1.2.3

Let
\Gamma\subset\mathbf{A}^{2}_{\mathbf{R}}\times\mathbf{A}^{2}_{\mathbf{R}}
be a real algebraic curve of pure dimension one, not necessarily irreducible. Assume that
\mathcal{S}_{1}=p_{1}(\Gamma(\mathbf{R}))
and
\mathcal{S}_{2}=p_{2}(\Gamma(\mathbf{R}))
are a union of special geodesics (for instance, real quadratic geodesics), and that
\Gamma(\mathbf{R})
is contained in
\tilde{C}(\mathbf{R})
. Note that we could add to
\Gamma
irreducible components with finitely many real points, all contained in
(\mathcal{S}_{1}\times\mathcal{S}_{2})\cap\tilde{C}(\mathbf{R})
, leaving
p_{1}(\Gamma(\mathbf{R}))
,
p_{2}(\Gamma(\mathbf{R}))
unchanged and preserving the inclusion
\Gamma(\mathbf{R})\subset\tilde{C}(\mathbf{R})
. One way to avoid this phenomenon is to only consider curves
\Gamma
such that
\Gamma(\mathbf{R})
has no isolated points. Intuitively, this condition tells us that
\Gamma(\mathbf{R})
really “looks like a curve”; precisely, it implies (using
[
BCR87
, Théorème 2.3.6]
) that
\Gamma(\mathbf{R})
is a finite union of sets homeomorphic to (non-degenerate) intervals.

Assume that we have a sequence
(\Gamma_{i})_{i\geq 0}
of real algebraic curves such that
p_{1}(\Gamma_{i}(\mathbf{R}))
and
p_{2}(\Gamma_{i}(\mathbf{R}))
are unions of special geodesics for every
i\geq 0
. Assume that the sets
\Gamma_{i}(\mathbf{R})
are pairwise distinct, are contained in
\tilde{C}(\mathbf{R})
, and contain no isolated points. The last property implies that the union of the real points of the irreducible components of
\Gamma_{i}
with infinitely many real points is
\Gamma_{i}(\mathbf{R})
; hence, we may discard irreducible components with finitely many real points in what follows. We will show that
C(\mathbf{C})
contains infinitely many curves with special geodesic projections. In view of Theorem
2.3.3
, this implies that Question
1.2.3
has a positive answer.

As each
\Gamma_{i}
has finitely many irreducible components, up to passing to a subsequence and choosing suitable irreducible components we may assume that the curves
\Gamma_{i}
are irreducible and pairwise distinct. Hence, the sets
\mathcal{Z}_{i}=\Gamma_{i}(\mathbf{R})
have the following properties: they are pairwise distinct, they are contained in
\tilde{C}(\mathbf{R})
, and the projections
p_{1}(\mathcal{Z}_{i})
and
p_{2}(\mathcal{Z}_{i})
are contained in a union of special geodesics. Finally, the curve
C\subset Y(1)\times Y(1)
is not horizontal nor vertical; therefore the fibers of each projection
C\rightarrow Y(1)
are finite. As
\mathcal{Z}_{i}
is infinite, the projections
p_{1}(\mathcal{Z}_{i})
and
p_{2}(\mathcal{Z}_{i})
cannot be singletons.

2.4.7 Remark
.

Let
W
be the restriction of scalars from
\mathbf{C}
to
\mathbf{R}
of
Y(1)\times Y(1)
. In the above argument, certain real subvarieties of
W
appear naturally: for instance, in
2.4.5
(i) we encountered the varieties
Z_{N}\times\mathbf{A}^{2}_{\mathbf{R}}\subset W
. These do contain infinitely many curves with special geodesic projections, and can be thought of as “special real subvarieties” of
W
; they are ruled out in our argument if we insist that we are only interested in subvarieties of
W
coming from
complex
curves in
Y(1)\times Y(1)
.
