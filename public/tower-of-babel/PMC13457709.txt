Evaluation of the Increase in Dentinal Barrier Thickness Formed by Indirect Pulp Treatment with Two Different Indirect Pulp Capping Agents in Primary Molars: A Randomized Controlled Trial
Authors: Janaki Shaji, R Balagopal Varma, J Suresh Kumar, Parvathy Kumaran, Arun Mamachan Xavier, Malini Venugopal, T Nishna
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAims and backgroundIndirect pulp treatment (IPT) is a conservative approach to preserving pulp vitality in primary molars with deep carious lesions. This study aimed to compare the increase in dentinal barrier thickness following IPT using two different materials, SafeEndo BioStructure MTA Putty and VOCO Calcimol LC, over a 6-month period.Materials and methodsA single-blinded, parallel-group, randomized controlled clinical trial was conducted among children aged 5–8 years requiring IPT. Eligible teeth were randomly allocated into two groups: Group I and group II. Following caries excavation and restoration, dentinal thickness was measured radiographically at baseline, 3 months, and 6 months using CorelDRAW software (version 13) digital analysis. Data were analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test and repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA). The trial was registered in the Clinical Trial Registry of India (CTRI/2024/11/076988).ResultsAt baseline, the mean dentin thickness was 0.62 ± 0.25 mm in group I and 0.48 ± 0.20 mm in group II (p = 0.98). At 3 months, increments were 0.70 ± 0.11 mm (group I) and 0.16 ± 0.14 mm (group II; p = 0.93). From 3 to 6 months, both groups showed an additional mean increase of 0.04 ± 0.01 mm (p = 0.49). Over the entire 6 months, the cumulative increase was 0.11 ± 0.02 mm (group I) and 0.10 ± 0.02 mm (group II; p = 0.45). Intragroup analysis revealed significant increases in dentin thickness over time for both groups (p ≤ 0.02).ConclusionBoth SafeEndo BioStructure MTA and VOCO Calcimol LC effectively promoted dentinal barrier formation in primary molars, with no statistically significant difference between the two materials over 6 months. Most dentin deposition occurred within the first 3 months, followed by a slower maturation phase.Clinical significanceThe results of this trial will contribute to existing knowledge on IPT agents in pediatric dentistry, guide clinical decision-making, and potentially reduce the need for invasive treatments.

Introduction

Indirect pulp treatment (IPT) is a conservative, biologically based approach designed to preserve pulp vitality while managing deep carious lesions that threaten but have not yet produced irreversible pulpal damage. In primary molars, maintaining tooth vitality until natural exfoliation is clinically important to preserve arch length, guide occlusion, and prevent space loss and malocclusion.1 IPT aims to remove infected dentin while leaving a thin layer of affected, mineralizable dentin adjacent to the pulp; a biocompatible capping agent is then placed over this residual dentin to encourage reparative dentinogenesis and to seal the lesion from bacterial ingress. The outcome of IPT is usually assessed radiographically and clinically, but histologic and microcomputed tomography studies have shown the formation of a dentinal barrier as a key correlate of pulpal health and long-term success.

The thickness and quality of the dentinal barrier formed after IPT are influenced by multiple factors: the remaining depth of carious dentin at the time of treatment, the sealing ability and biological activity of the capping material, the degree of bacterial elimination achieved during excavation, and host-related variables such as age and pulpal physiology. In primary teeth, pulpal responses may differ from those in permanent teeth because of anatomical differences and heightened immunologic reactivity; therefore, evidence specific to primary molars is essential.2 Historically, calcium hydroxide has been the most widely used indirect pulp capping agent because of its proven ability to stimulate reparative dentin formation through a high pH and bioactive effects. However, calcium hydroxide is associated with issues such as poor long-term seal and potential for tunnel defects in the reparative dentin. Newer materials such as mineral trioxide aggregate (MTA), Biodentine, and resin-modified glass ionomers claim improved sealing properties, bioactivity, and more favorable tissue responses, potentially promoting a thicker and more homogeneous dentinal barrier.3

Accurate measurement of dentinal barrier thickness after IPT provides an objective endpoint to compare capping agents. Radiographic evaluation gives a practical, clinic-friendly assessment, but modern techniques, such as standardized periapical radiography with calibrated measurements, digital subtraction radiography, or micro-CT in ex vivo studies, offer more precise quantification.4 A greater dentinal barrier thickness is hypothesized to correlate with enhanced protection of the pulp against bacterial toxins and better long-term outcomes, such as absence of symptoms, maintenance of vitality, and avoidance of more invasive procedures like pulpotomy or extraction.5

Given the prevalence of early juvenile caries and the frequent need to manage deep lesions in primary molars, comparative clinical data evaluating how different IPT capping agents influence dentinal barrier formation are clinically relevant.6 This study, therefore, aims to evaluate and compare the increase in dentinal barrier thickness following IPT with two different indirect pulp capping agents in primary molars, providing evidence to inform material selection and optimize conservative pulp therapy protocols in pediatric dentistry.

Materials and Methods

Research Design

This investigation was designed as a single-blinded, parallel-group, randomized controlled clinical trial with 28 samples, conducted in the Department of Pediatric and Preventive Dentistry, Amrita School of Dentistry, Kochi. The trial was registered in the Clinical Trial Registry of India (CTRI/2024/11/076988) over a period of 6 months from 12/2023 to 05/2024.

The study recruited children aged 5–8 years seeking routine dental care. Following an eligibility assessment, the procedure and purpose of the trial were explained to parents or guardians, and written informed consent was obtained. After administration of local anesthesia and placement of rubber dam isolation, the damaged enamel was removed using a no. 245 carbide bur in a high-speed handpiece with water coolant. Caries removal was completed at the cavosurface margins and lateral walls using a spoon excavator and a no. 6 bur at low speed. The cavity was rinsed with saline for 10 seconds and gently dried with cotton pellets. Teeth with accidental pulp exposure were excluded from the trial at this stage. For group I, MTA putty was applied to the prepared dentin surface with a ball-ended condenser under light pressure. Excess material was removed, and after the initial setting, a 1 mm layer of resin-modified glass ionomer cement (RMGIC) was placed as a base, followed by composite restoration after acid etching and bonding. For group II, calcium hydroxide paste was dispensed directly over the dentin, light-cured for 20 seconds, and then covered with a 1 mm RMGIC base, followed by composite restoration. Baseline periapical radiographs were obtained using an XCP holder. Clinical and radiographic follow-up was conducted at 3 and 6 months. Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) software. The Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare the mean dentin deposition between groups at 6 months. Repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to assess within-group changes in dentin thickness from baseline to the follow-up intervals. The significance level was set at p < 0.05.

Results

This clinical trial was conducted to assess the clinical and radiographic effects of SafeEndo BioStructure MTA and VOCO Calcimol LC in IPT in primary teeth at 3 and 6 months. Clinically, the treatment was considered successful when the pulp remained vital at the end of six months without signs of pulpal inflammation, tenderness on percussion, discoloration of teeth, or sinus opening. Radiographically, the treatment was considered successful when tertiary dentin formation occurred and there were no signs of furcation or periapical radiolucency, PDL space widening, internal or external resorption.

A total of 28 samples were included in the trial, of which 7 were excluded: one due to a dislodged restoration and the rest due to loss of follow-up in the sixth month.

Analysis of the dentin thickness measurements revealed a mean increment of about 0.07 mm at 3 months (SD = 0.11 mm), with values ranging from a slight reduction of 0.20 mm to a maximum increase of 0.36 mm. This variation suggests that while most samples established measurable dentin apposition by the 3-month follow-up, a few cases showed minimal change, possibly due to biological variability or measurement limitations. By 6 months, the mean dentin increment increased to 0.11 mm (SD = 0.02 mm), with a narrower range (0.074–0.134 mm), indicating more consistent dentin deposition across the samples. The smaller standard deviation at this time point reflects reduced variability and a more uniform reparative response over the extended observation period.

In group II, the mean dentin thickness increment at 3 months was 0.16 mm (SD = 0.14 mm), with values ranging from 0.051 to 0.45 mm. This indicates a generally positive reparative response, although the relatively high standard deviation suggests considerable variability among samples. Some teeth showed rapid dentin formation, while others demonstrated slower deposition. By the 6-month evaluation, the mean increment was 0.10 mm (SD = 0.02 mm), with a narrower range of 0.079–0.141 mm. This reduced variability implies more consistent dentinogenic activity during the later healing phase. Even though the average gain at 6 months was somewhat lower than at 3 months, the stability and uniformity of the values suggest that dentin bridge maturation continued steadily in this group.

In group I, the mean increase in dentin thickness from baseline to 3 months varied widely, with a notable gain of 0.363 mm. For samples with available 6-month data, the increments from 3 to 6 months were generally smaller, averaging between 0.031 and 0.048 mm, reflecting a slower but continued dentin apposition phase after the initial 3 months. The total gain from baseline to 6 months for these cases ranged from 0.074 to 0.134 mm, indicating steady maturation and consolidation of the dentinal barrier over time.

In group II, the change in dentin thickness from baseline to 3 months showed considerable variation, ranging from 0.051 to 0.450 mm, with several samples showing substantial early gains. This suggests that, for many teeth, the bulk of dentin bridge formation occurred in the initial healing phase. However, other cases showed only minimal increases, indicating possible differences in biological response or lesion severity at baseline. For the subset of samples with data up to 6 months, the additional increment from 3 to 6 months was modest, between 0.028 and 0.055 mm, reflecting a slower but ongoing reparative process after the initial deposition period. The total increase from baseline to 6 months ranged from 0.079 to 0.141 mm, pointing to a stable and consolidated dentin bridge in most cases by the end of follow-up.

At baseline, the mean dentin thickness in group I (0.62 ± 0.25 mm) was slightly higher than in group II (0.48 ± 0.20 mm), but this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.98), indicating that the two groups were comparable before intervention. After 3 months, group I maintained a marginally higher mean dentin thickness (0.68 ± 0.24 mm) compared to group II (0.64 ± 0.16 mm), yet the difference remained statistically insignificant (p = 0.36). This suggests that both materials produced similar early dentinogenic responses. By the 6-month follow-up, the mean dentin thickness increased in both groups, reaching 0.79 ± 0.28 mm in group I and 0.69 ± 0.21 mm in group II. Although group I continued to show slightly greater values, the difference was again not statistically significant (p = 0.34) (Table 1).

From baseline to 3 months, group I demonstrated a mean dentin thickness increase of 0.70 ± 0.11 mm, while group II showed an increase of 0.16 ± 0.14 mm. Discussion

The findings of the present study align with the growing body of literature demonstrating that IPT is a predictable and biologically favorable approach for managing deep carious lesions in primary molars while preserving pulp vitality. The primary result, dentinal barrier thickness, has been extensively used as an indicator of pulpal healing and protection against further bacterial insult.

Marchi et al.1 reported that IPT in primary teeth maintained high clinical and radiographic success over a 4-year follow-up, emphasizing the importance of an adequate coronal seal and biocompatible capping materials. Even though their study did not directly quantify dentinal barrier thickness, the long-term pulp vitality results indirectly suggest effective tertiary dentin formation.7,8

Numerous comparative studies have attempted to quantify dentinal barrier thickness radiographically.8 Batra et al.3 compared MTA, calcium hydroxide, and glass ionomer cement (GIC) in primary molars. They observed that MTA produced a suggestively greater increase in dentinal thickness compared to calcium hydroxide (0.068 mm), with continued deposition over the subsequent months. This was attributed to MTA's superior sealing ability, alkaline pH, and stimulation of odontoblastic differentiation.9

In contrast, Shabzendedar et al.4 used cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) to assess dentinal bridge formation after IPT with calcium hydroxide, GIC, and MTA. All three materials significantly increased dentinal thickness and radiodensity at 6 months, with no statistically significant differences among them. The authors suggested that CBCT's higher resolution may detect subtle barrier formation in all groups, potentially explaining the absence of intergroup differences compared to Batra et al.'s findings.3,10,11

Biodentine, a calcium silicate–based material with favorable handling properties, has emerged as another promising IPT agent. Mittal et al.5 compared Biodentine, calcium hydroxide, and RMGIC with chlorhexidine in primary molars over 12 months.5,11 All groups demonstrated high clinical (94–100%) and radiographic success rates, with Biodentine showing somewhat higher rates but without statistically significant differences. These results suggest that while material properties may influence the rate and quality of dentinogenesis, the complete biological process is robust if a proper seal is achieved and bacterial contamination is minimized.12

Hegde and Naik2 further reinforced MTA's advantages, showing superior clinical and radiographic success compared to calcium hydroxide. They noted more uniform and thicker dentinal bridges with MTA, supporting the idea that bioactive materials with sustained calcium ion release favor more organized tertiary dentin formation.13

The variability in reported results across studies can be attributed to differences in methodology, follow-up period, radiographic methods, patient age, and depth of carious lesions. In addition, measuring dentinal thickness radiographically is subject to limitations such as projection errors, magnification, and difficulty differentiating between tertiary dentin and calcific obliteration. CBCT and micro-CT offer more precise measurements but are less feasible in routine pediatric practice due to radiation concerns and cost.14,15

Mineral trioxide aggregate has many formulations. Among the newer formulations, SafeEndo BioStructure MTA, a putty-based material incorporating calcium fluoride, has shown clinical promise owing to its improved handling characteristics and reduced setting time, making it particularly suitable for use in pediatric dentistry.

VOCO Calcimol LC is a light-cured, resin-modified calcium hydroxide paste used as a pulp capping material and cavity liner in restorative dentistry. It is radiopaque, promoting easy identification on radiographs, and releases calcium ions to stimulate tertiary dentin formation and maintain a high pH for antimicrobial and healing effects. The material is presented in a non-dripping syringe for precise application.

Studies in the past have shown that both MTA and calcium hydroxide work well in terms of biocompatibility, sealing, and encouraging the formation of dental bridges. However, there is no direct evidence comparing SafeEndo BioStructure MTA and VOCO Calcimol LC in the case of primary molars with deep carious lesions. The present study attempts to bridge this gap by systematically evaluating and comparing the effects of SafeEndo BioStructure MTA and VOCO Calcimol LC, a light-curing radiopaque calcium hydroxide paste, on dentin barrier formation over a 6-month period. The literature indicates that IPT with bioactive materials such as MTA and Biodentine, reliably achieves favorable pulp preservation outcomes, often with thicker and more uniform dentinal barriers compared to traditional calcium hydroxide. However, as Shabzendedar et al.4 and Mittal et al.5 suggest, when a well-sealed restoration is placed, even conventional materials can yield comparable success rates. Upcoming studies should standardize measurement methods and assess long-term performance to better clarify the relationship between dentinal barrier thickness and clinical endurance.16

Conclusion

This randomized clinical trial established that both SafeEndo BioStructure MTA Putty and VOCO Calcimol LC are effective indirect pulp capping agents for stimulating dentinal barrier formation in primary molars with deep carious lesions. While group I exhibited slightly higher mean increments at each interval, the differences were not statistically significant, indicating comparable clinical performance of the two materials. Most of the dentin bridge formation occurred within the first 3 months after IPT, with a smaller but steady increase noted between 3 and 6 months, reflecting a transition from active deposition to maturation of the reparative dentin. Clinically, these results support the use of either material in IPT protocols for pediatric patients, provided that adequate isolation, caries removal, and coronal sealing are achieved. Future studies with larger sample sizes and longer follow-up periods are suggested to validate these findings and assess the long-term integrity of the dentinal barrier.
