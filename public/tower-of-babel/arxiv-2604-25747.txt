Quantum Error Correction Exploiting Quantum Spatial Distribution and Gauge Symmetry
Ryo Asaka

Abstract
We explore what the integrated use of quantum spatial distribution (QSD), or more specifically, superposition of both spin and position states of particles, and gauge symmetry (GS) within stabilizer formalism provides for quantum error correction. The exploration employs 3 + 2 3+2 particles on nested squares proposed in the companion letter (arXiv:2504.07941), where three of them encode Shor’s nine-qubit code and the remaining two detect errors in this code through their spin state measurements (unlike the letter’s quantum walk model, each particle evolves by gate operations acting exclusively on either its spin or position state). The first result is that the GS offers resilience against three types of noise acting on a particle: arbitrary decoherence of its spin or position state, and dephasing of both states, which partly or completely destroys its QSD. To show that, we formulate a noise model unifying the above noise and prove the correctability of this unified model under our error-correcting scheme. The second result is that QSD provides architectural flexibility allowing us to stack the error-correcting systems both vertically and horizontally. Indeed, we show implementations of the error detection (stabilizer measurement), logical Hadamard and Toffoli gates, and a quantum adder with the required interactions only between nearest-neighbor and next-nearest-neighbor particles.

Introduction

In quantum mechanics, superposition is a unique property whereby a single particle can simultaneously occupy multiple positions,
reflecting its wave-like nature. Here, we refer to such a phenomenon as quantum spatial distribution (QSD).
The most well-known evidence of the QSD is perhaps the interference pattern projected onto the screen beyond a double-slit wall

Indeed, such a pattern is a consequence of a single particle passing through both slits simultaneously and interfering with itself as if it were two particles

A recent experiment has successfully manipulated the QSD of a single particle (electron, referred to as a flying qubit) over two separate regions,
suggesting that this phenomenon could be directly exploited in quantum computing

In particular, the experiment

on the superposition of two states

The

Two flying qubits entangle through the two-particle scattering (Coulomb interaction)
[
50
,
25
,
47
,
38

.

One advantage of the QSD is the ability to send a single particle to multiple points simultaneously,
whereby the particle can undergo multiple conditional gate operations based on each destination
[
13

For example, quantum random access memory, a prominent theoretical proposal,
exploits this phenomenon to simultaneously query

A particle (photon) simultaneously reaches multiple cells through the QSD, and undergoes gate operations
to encode a qubit state

Here, each cell is attached to a leaf of a full binary tree of depth

and a quantum switch is placed at each node to direct the particle to both branches in a quantum superposition.

The QSD can also offer advantages in reducing the number of particles in quantum computation and eliminating time-dependence in gate operations.
As an example of the former, exploiting the QSD, or more specifically, the particle’s position and spin states, facilitates encoding a multi-qubit state into fewer particles than qubits

Another example is quantum walk models of quantum random access memory

As an example of the latter advantage, researchers have proposed quantum computation in which a particle (a quantum walker) encodes qubit information into its position state

A key nontrivial theoretical challenge for ensuring the practicality and feasibility of the QSD-based computational architectures,
which have been proposed or are yet to be proposed, is incorporating a quantum error-correcting scheme into them.
For this, we would need to develop a novel scheme that protects against noise affecting the position and spin states of a particle, i.e., the multi-qubit state within a particle.
Dephasing noise, which we refer to as partial loss or complete vanishing of the QSD,
would also constitute the dominant source of errors in such architectures.
Moreover, the error-correcting scheme would also need to have architectural flexibility so that the advantages of the QSD are preserved.

Toward addressing this nontrivial challenge,

The first result of the demonstration is that the GS of the present error-correcting model offers resilience against three types of dominant noise in our QSD-based architecture:
(i) unified spin-related noise (ii) unified position-related noise, and (iii) partial loss or complete vanishing of the QSD
(the noise (i) also depends on the particle’s position state).
Specifically, we formulate a noise model that unifies the above three noise types,
and show that our error-correcting scheme eliminates the influence of this unified noise acting on one of the three particles.
From this formulation, we can explicitly verify that the absence of the GS means the unified noise falls outside the correctable class.

The second result is that the QSD provides a form of architectural flexibility, namely stacking flexibility, for scalable quantum computing within quantum error correction.
Specifically, we demonstrate that the logical Hadamard and Toffoli gates, which together can simulate universal quantum computation
[
40
,
1

, are implemented with two nested-squares-systems communicating
through nearest-neighbor (and next-nearest-neighbor) interactions between outermost-to-outermost (and outermost-to-next-outermost) particles.
In addition, we show that a quantum adder is implemented by the stacking of the systems horizontally and vertically,
which reflects the architectural flexibility offered by the QSD.

This paper is organized as follows.
In Sec. 2, we formulate our model of
3+2

In Sec. 3, we discuss our error-correcting mechanism together with the GS.
In Sec. 4, we demonstrate the resilience against the aforementioned three types of noise, which is one of the two main result of this paper.
In Sec. 5, we show that the QSD offers the stacking flexibility for the quantum computation, which is the second main result.
We also briefly estimate the failure probability of the error correction in the Appendix.

-1
randomly,
meaning that
|\bar{0}\rangle
or
|\bar{1}\rangle
emerges,
we can change them arbitrarily by applying
X_{\mathrm{c}}
to all physical particles,
exploiting the following GS:

\displaystyle\bigotimes_{i\in\{0,2,4\}}(X_{\mathrm{c}})_{\mathrm{p}_{i}}=\left(\bar{X}_{g_{0}}\bar{X}_{g_{1}}\right)\cdot\bar{X}\propto\bar{X},

(3.5)

where we use
\bar{X}_{g_{0}}\bar{X}_{g_{1}}\in\mathcal{G}
.

To correct an error arising in the logical QSDs, we also perform the stabilizer measurement and record the measured outcomes.
As will be detailed in Sec.
4
,
the error, i.e., an undesired operation in the logical qubit [cf. Fig.
1
(b)], can be corrected
if the noise source falls within the unified noise model [Eq. (
4.1
)] containing all three types of dominant noise mentioned in the introduction.
The reason why the stabilizer measurement functions against such noise is that, intuitively, each operator element
E^{(n)}
in Eq. (
4.1
)
that acts as a nontrivial error on the logical qubit
collapses to the trivial flipping errors in Table
2
as

\displaystyle E^{(n)}=\sum_{m\in\{0,5\}^{\otimes 6}}\mathfrak{g}_{n,m}\sigma_{m}\rightarrow\mathfrak{g}_{n,m}\sigma_{m}\propto\sigma_{m},

(3.6)

where
\mathfrak{g}_{n,m}\in\mathcal{G}
for
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
Here, the measurement outcome
m\in\{0,5\}^{\otimes 6}
informs us that these flipping errors
\sigma_{m}
remain in the logical qubit.

In practice, rather than applying a recovery operation
\sigma^{\dagger}_{m}
immediately after each stabilizer measurement,
we perform error correction collectively after
N
rounds of stabilizer measurements.
Namely, we can correct accumulated errors in the system by applying the operations
\prod^{N-1}_{i=0}\sigma^{\dagger}_{m^{i}}

determined by the history of the measurement outcomes
\{m^{i}\in\{0,1\}^{6}\mid 0\leq i\leq N\}
.
This recovery strategy is known as a
Pauli frame update

[
33
]
(or
offline error correction

[
23
]
), and its validity in our scheme is explicitly justified by Eq. (
4.8
) in the next section.

Implementation

Our implementation of the stabilizer measurement consists of three stages: eigenvalue measurements of
(s_{0},s_{1})
,
(s_{2},s_{3})

(s_{4},s_{5})

In each stage of the stabilizer measurement,

{\mathrm{p}_{1}}

Namely, as illustrated in Fig.

we cycle the ancillary particle
{\mathrm{p}_{j}}
around its square using the tunneling operations
X_{\mathrm{x}}
and

,
while applying nearest-neighbor
{\mathrm{CNOT}}_{{\mathrm{p}_{j\pm 1}},{\mathrm{p}_{j}}}
or
{\mathrm{CNOT}}_{{\mathrm{p}_{j}},{\mathrm{p}_{j\pm 1}}}

As defined in Eq. (
2.2
),
the pre- and post-operations
X_{\mathrm{c}}^{[10,11]}
,

From the above discussions, we can partly confirm that the QSD offers the stacking flexibility to the stabilizer measurement, as will be fully confirmed in Sec.

Namely, our model implements the circuit in Fig.

4
Analysis of Noise Resilience

We show that the error-correcting system presented in the previous section exhibits resilience
against arbitrary coherent errors or decoherence acting on either spin or position state,
as well as dephasing of both spin and position states (partial loss or complete vanishing of the QSD).
As discussed in the introduction, these would be the dominant noise sources in our architecture, or more generally, in an architecture exploiting QSD.

First, we present a unified noise model containing the above noise sources,
and demonstrate that our QEC scheme functions correctly under this noise model.
The derivation of correctability is inspired by
[
34
,
37
,
36
]
.
Second, we explicitly identify and discuss the above three noise sources within this unified noise model.

4.1
Unified Noise Model

The quantum noise, or more specifically, a nontrivial interaction with the environment (
\mathrm{E}
), that unifies the three types of noise inherent in our architecture, is given by

\displaystyle U_{E}:=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}E^{(n)}\otimes|e_{n+1}\rangle\langle e_{0}|_{\mathrm{E}},\ E^{(n)}=\sum_{m\in\{0,1\}^{6}}\mathfrak{g}_{n,m}\sigma_{m},

(4.1)

where
|e_{i}\rangle_{\mathrm{E}}\in\mathbb{C}^{\infty}
is a state of the environment whose initial state is
|e_{0}\rangle_{\mathrm{E}}
defined via purification
[
36
]
.
In addition,
\mathfrak{g}_{n,m}\in\mathcal{G}
is an arbitrary gauge operation chosen such that
\sum_{n}E^{(n)\dagger}E^{(n)}=I
(the unitarity condition for
U_{E}
).

We first demonstrate that our QEC mechanism provides the logical qubit with resilience against this unified quantum noise.
Subsequently, we explicitly validate the Pauli frame update discussed in the previous section under repeated exposure to the quantum noise given by Eq. (
4.1
).
Here, to facilitate the two analyses,
we express the recovery operation conditioned on the stabilizer measurement in the Kraus-representation (or operator-sum representation
[
36
]
):

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\rho)=\sum_{m=0}^{2^{6}-1}\sigma^{\dagger}_{m}P_{m}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}\rho P_{m}^{\dagger}\sigma_{m}^{\vphantom{\dagger}},\ P_{m}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}:=\frac{1}{2^{5}}\prod_{i=0}^{5}\left(I+(-1)^{m_{i}}s_{i}\right)

(4.2)

where
\rho\in\mathcal{B}((\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes 9})
is a density operator of the three physical particles.
The adjoint action
P_{m}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}\cdot P_{m}^{\dagger}
indicates that the outcome
m\in\{0,1\}^{\otimes 6}
is obtained from the stabilizer measurement [cf. Eq. (
3.3
)],
and
\sigma_{m}\cdot\sigma^{\dagger}_{m}
is the recovery action corresponding to this outcome.

With the Kraus-representation
\mathcal{E}(\rho):=\sum_{n}E^{(n)}\rho E^{(n)\dagger}

(=\tr_{\mathrm{E}}\!\left(U_{\mathrm{E}}\,(\rho\otimes|e_{0}\rangle\langle e_{0}|_{\mathrm{E}})\,U_{\mathrm{E}}^{\dagger}\right))
,
we demonstrate the resilience against the quantum noise
U_{\mathrm{E}}
by deriving the following GS:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}\circ\mathcal{E}\left(|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|\right)\propto|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|,

(4.3)

where
|\psi\rangle:=\alpha|\bar{0}\rangle+\beta|\bar{1}\rangle
(
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{C}
).
This symmetry ensures that the recovery operation removes the influence of the quantum noise on the logical qubit [cf. Fig.
1
(b)].
Indeed, the following derivation establishes Eq. (
4.3
):

\displaystyle|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\mathcal{E}}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\sum_{m\in\{0,1\}^{6}}\mathfrak{g}_{n,m}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}\sigma_{m}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|\sigma_{m}^{\dagger}\mathfrak{g}_{n,m}^{\dagger}

(4.4)

\displaystyle\propto\sum_{m\in\{0,1\}^{6}}\sigma_{m}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|\sigma_{m}^{\dagger}

(4.5)

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\mathcal{R}}\sum_{m,m^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}^{6}}\sigma_{m^{\prime}}^{\dagger}P_{m^{\prime}}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}\sigma_{m}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|\sigma_{m}^{\dagger}P_{m^{\prime}}^{\dagger}\sigma_{m^{\prime}}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}

(4.6)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m\in\{0,1\}^{6}}\sigma_{m}^{\dagger}\sigma_{m}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|\sigma_{m}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}\sigma_{m}^{\dagger}.

(4.7)

The normalization constant is omitted in the second line, and thus the final state reduces to
|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|
.
Here, we used the GS [Eq. (
3.1
)] in the second step,
and two identities in the final step:

P_{m^{\prime}}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}=\sigma_{m^{\prime}}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}P_{0}\sigma^{\dagger}_{m^{\prime}}
and
P_{0}\sigma^{\dagger}_{m^{\prime}}\sigma_{m}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}|\psi\rangle=\delta_{m^{\prime},m}
for all
m,m^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}^{\otimes 6}

(we retain
\sigma_{m}^{\dagger}\sigma_{m}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}(=I)
for later convenience in deriving Eq. (
4.8
)).

We further obtain a symmetry that validates the Pauli-Frame update discussed in Sec.
3.2
,
where the recovery operations are collectively applied according to the outcomes of
N
rounds of stabilizer measurements:

\displaystyle(\mathcal{R}\circ\mathcal{E})^{N}(|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|)\propto\sum^{2^{6N}-1}_{M=0}\left(\bigcirc^{N-1}_{i=0}\mathrm{Ad}(\sigma^{\dagger}_{m^{i}})\right)\left(\bigcirc^{N-1}_{i=0}\mathrm{Ad}(\sigma_{m^{i}}^{\vphantom{\dagger}})\right)(|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|),

(4.8)

where
m^{i}\in\{0,1\}^{6}
and
M=\sum^{N}_{i=0}2^{6i}m^{i}
.
Here, we denote the adjoint action as
\mathrm{Ad}(\sigma_{m^{i}}^{\vphantom{\dagger}})(\rho):=\sigma_{m^{i}}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}\rho\sigma^{\dagger}_{m^{i}}
,
and the composition as
\bigcirc^{N-1}_{i=0}\sigma_{m^{i}}:=\sigma_{m^{N-1}}\circ\sigma_{m^{N-2}}\circ\cdots\circ\sigma_{m^{0}}
.
This follows from repeated application of Eq. (
4.7
). For instance, in the case of
N=2
, we have:

\displaystyle(\mathcal{R}\circ\mathcal{E})^{2}\left(|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|\right)

\displaystyle\propto(\mathcal{R}\circ\mathcal{E})\left(\sum_{m^{0}\in\{0,1\}^{6}}\sigma_{m^{0}}^{\dagger}\sigma_{m^{0}}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|\sigma_{m^{0}}^{\dagger}\sigma_{m^{0}}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}\right)

(4.9)

\displaystyle\propto\sum_{m^{0},m^{1}\in\{0,1\}^{6}}\sigma_{m^{1}}^{\dagger}\sigma_{m^{0}}^{\dagger}\sigma_{m^{1}}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}\sigma_{m^{0}}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|\sigma_{m^{0}}^{\dagger}\sigma_{m^{1}}^{\dagger}\sigma_{m^{0}}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}\sigma_{m^{1}}^{\vphantom{\dagger}},

(4.10)

where we used
\sigma^{\dagger}_{m^{i}}\sigma^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{m^{j}}=\pm\sigma^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{m^{j}}\sigma_{m^{i}}^{\dagger}
(
i,j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
) in the last step.

4.2
Three Types of Dominant Noise

We then explicitly identify and discuss the three types of dominant noise within the unified correctable noise [Eq. (
4.1
)].
The fact that our scheme achieves resilience against these noise sources implies that exploiting the GS
is an essential approach for introducing a QEC mechanism into an architecture that utilizes the QSD, not limited to the present architecture.

Unified Spin-related Noise

We first identify a noise model acting on the spin state of a particle,
which provides a unified description of both arbitrary coherent errors and decoherence.
A key feature of this model is that its action varies depending on the particle’s position, reflecting realistic conditions in our QSD-based architecture.

Given that the projector onto an arbitrary position state can be written as
|n_{1},n_{0}\rangle\langle n_{1},n_{0}|_{\mathrm{xy}}

=(I-(-1)^{n_{1}}Z)_{\mathrm{x}}(I-(-1)^{n_{0}}Z)_{\mathrm{y}}
for
n_{1},n_{0}\in\{0,1\}
,
the unified spin-related noise is explicitly written as follows (letting
n_{0}
and
n_{1}
be 0th and 1st digits of
n
, respectively):

\displaystyle(U_{E,\mathrm{c}})_{\mathrm{p}_{i}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\left(E^{(n)}_{\mathrm{c}}\right)_{\mathrm{p}_{i}}\otimes|e_{n+1}\rangle\langle e_{0}|_{E},

(4.11)

\displaystyle E_{\mathrm{c}}^{(n)}:

\displaystyle=M_{\mathrm{c}}^{(n)}(I_{\mathrm{x}}+(-1)^{n_{1}}Z_{\mathrm{x}})(I_{\mathrm{y}}+(-1)^{n_{0}}Z_{\mathrm{y}}).

(4.12)

where the actions on the spin-state
\{M_{\mathrm{c}}^{(n)}\in\mathrm{End}(\mathbb{C}^{2})\}
are chosen so that
\sum_{n}E_{\mathrm{c}}^{(n)\dagger}E_{\mathrm{c}}^{(n)}=I
,
and we label these actions by
n
.
Given that
M_{\mathrm{c}}^{(n)}
belongs to an algebra generated by
I_{\mathrm{c}}
,
X_{\mathrm{c}}
, and
Z_{\mathrm{c}}
,
the operator element
E^{(n)}_{\mathrm{c}}
is clearly captured by the unified operator
E^{(n)}
in Eq. (
4.1
).

One of the dominant errors or decoherence contained in Eq. (
4.11
) may be amplitude damping (or energy dissipation) of the spin state.
As an extreme case, when
M_{\mathrm{c}}^{(n)}
takes
|0\rangle\langle 0|_{\mathrm{c}}
for
0\leq n\leq 3
and
|1\rangle\langle 1|_{\mathrm{c}}
for
4\leq n\leq 7
, a particle affected by this noise deterministically relaxes its spin state to only
|0\rangle_{\mathrm{c}}
.

Unified Position-related Noise

From Eq. (
4.1
), we subsequently identify a unified noise model acting on the position state of a particle.
As discussed below, this model serves to describe the failure of the gate operation acting on the particle’s position state.

Analogously to the unified spin-related noise in Eq. (
4.11
),
the unified position-related noise is given by

\displaystyle(U_{E,\mathrm{xy}})_{\mathrm{p}_{i}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}\left(\delta_{n_{2},0}E_{\mathrm{x}}^{(n)}+\delta_{n_{2},1}E_{\mathrm{y}}^{(n)}\right)_{\mathrm{p}_{i}}\otimes|e_{n+1}\rangle\langle e_{0}|_{E},

(4.13)

\displaystyle E_{\mathrm{x}}^{(n)}:

\displaystyle=(I_{\mathrm{c}}+(-1)^{n_{1}}Z_{\mathrm{c}})M_{\mathrm{x}}^{(n)}(I_{\mathrm{y}}+(-1)^{n_{0}}Z_{\mathrm{y}}),

(4.14)

\displaystyle E_{\mathrm{y}}^{(n)}:

\displaystyle=(I_{\mathrm{c}}+(-1)^{n_{1}}Z_{\mathrm{c}})(I_{\mathrm{x}}+(-1)^{n_{0}}Z_{\mathrm{x}})M_{\mathrm{y}}^{(n)},

(4.15)

where
n_{i}\in\{0,1\}
is the
i
th digit of
n
for
0\leq i\leq 2
.
In addition,
M_{\mathrm{x}}^{(n)}
(resp.
M_{\mathrm{y}}^{(n)}
) is in an algebra generated by
I_{\mathrm{x}}
,
Z_{\mathrm{x}}
, and
X_{\mathrm{x}}
(resp.
I_{\mathrm{y}}
,
Z_{\mathrm{y}}
, and
X_{\mathrm{y}}
).

In this unified noise model, no operator elements (
E_{\mathrm{x}}^{(n)}
and
E_{\mathrm{y}}^{(n)}
) include the Pauli-X operations acting on both
x
- and
y
-axis position states
(which are uncorrectable within our QEC).
This setting is physically more reasonable than one including an operator element acting on both states.
Namely, under our assumption that a single particle resides at the square vertices with no diagonal connections,
such undesired Pauli
X
operations would occur only through two successive noise events rather than a single one.

One of the dominant noise sources in our architecture, which falls within Eq. (
4.13
),
is the failure of a gate operation acting on the particle’s position state.
As an example, when
E^{(n)}_{\mathrm{x}}
alternately takes
|1\rangle\langle 0|_{\mathrm{x}}
and
|1\rangle\langle 1|_{\mathrm{x}}
while
E_{y}^{(n)}
takes a zero matrix,
the particle affected by this noise deterministically localizes in a region corresponding to the state
|1\rangle_{\mathrm{x}}
.
This situation would physically represent the phenomenon in which the particle succeeds in tunneling (
X_{\mathrm{x}}
or
X_{\mathrm{y}}
) from one region to another
(|0\rangle_{\mathrm{x}}\rightarrow|1\rangle_{\mathrm{x}})

while failing to tunnel in the opposite direction
(|1\rangle_{\mathrm{x}}\rightarrow|0\rangle_{\mathrm{x}})
.

Dephasing noise (loss of QSD)

Finally, we discuss dephasing noise acting on both spin and position states.
Such environmental noise degrades the QSD of a particle, resulting in a mixed state.
As shown below, the unified correctable noise model in Eq. (
4.1
) contains a dephasing under which
a particle reduces to the classical state (fully mixed state), i.e., its QSD completely vanishes.

The most physically natural noise of dephasing contained in Eq. (
4.1
) is the following model:

\displaystyle\left(U_{E,\mathrm{loss}}\right)_{\mathrm{p}_{j}}:=\sum_{n\in\{0,1\}^{3}}\left(E^{(n)}_{\mathrm{loss}}\right)_{\mathrm{p}_{j}}\otimes|e_{n+1}\rangle\langle e_{0}|_{E},

(4.16)

\displaystyle E^{(n)}_{\mathrm{loss}}:=\alpha_{n}\left[I_{\mathrm{c}}I_{\mathrm{x}}I_{\mathrm{y}}-\bigl(I_{\mathrm{c}}+(-1)^{n_{2}}Z_{\mathrm{c}}\bigr)\bigl(I_{\mathrm{x}}+(-1)^{n_{1}}Z_{\mathrm{x}}\bigr)\bigl(I_{\mathrm{y}}+(-1)^{n_{0}}Z_{\mathrm{y}}\bigr)\right].

(4.17)

where
\alpha_{n}\in\mathbb{C}
is chosen so that
\sum_{n}E^{(n)\dagger}_{\mathrm{loss}}E^{(n)}_{\mathrm{loss}}=I
.
Given that the second term in Eq. (
4.17
) can be written as

5
Scalability Enhancement

In this section, we show that the use of QSD offers the stacking flexibility for quantum computation within quantum error correction.
Specifically, we discuss how the three quantum gates (
{\mathcal{CX}}
,
\sqrt{{\mathcal{CX}}}
, and
{\mathcal{CZ}}
) can be implemented in a manner applicable to two systems aligned either horizontally or vertically.
These three gates together realize the logical Hadamard and Toffoli gates in our QEC system and further enable a space-efficient realization of a quantum adder (see Fig.
4
).
Given that the universality of the Hadamard and Toffoli gates has been proven
[
40
,
1
]
, our result indicates that
exploiting the QSD leads to highly scalable fault-tolerant quantum computation.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we have demonstrated that the GS offers resilience against three types of noise inherent in QSD-based architectures,
and the QSD offers a form of architectural flexibility, i.e., stacking flexibility, which enables both vertical and horizontal scaling of the error-correcting system.
The former is ensured by Eq. (
4.5
).
In addition, the latter is ensured by the fact that
the implementation of both the stabilizer measurement and the three primitive logical gates (
{\mathcal{CX}}
,
\sqrt{{\mathcal{CX}}}
and
{\mathcal{CZ}}
) without QSD requires interactions beyond next-nearest-neighbor particles [cf. Fig.
2
].

The present exploration with the companion letter would be a significant step toward unexplored fault-tolerant QSD-based computational architectures.
The noise resilience offered by the GS would be an essential ingredient for achieving fault-tolerance,
and the architectural flexibility offered by the QSD itself would be an essential ingredient
to preserve its advantages, e.g., parallel conditional gate operations, resource-efficiency, and time-independence (autonomous), as discussed in the introduction.
