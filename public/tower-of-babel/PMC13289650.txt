Efficacy of Adjunctive Lumateperone 42 mg on Anhedonia and Across a Broad Range of Depressive Symptoms in Major Depressive Disorder: Post Hoc Analysis of a Phase 3, Randomized, Placebo-Controlled Trial
Authors: Michael E Thase, Willie R Earley, Susan G Kozauer, Changzheng Chen, Hassan Lakkis, Suresh Durgam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeLumateperone is a mechanistically novel Food and Drug Administration-approved antipsychotic to treat schizophrenia, depressive episodes associated with bipolar I and II disorder, and major depressive disorder (MDD). This analysis of the Phase 3, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, multicenter Study 501 (NCT04985942) evaluated the broad efficacy of lumateperone across depression symptoms assessed by Montgomery-Åsberg Depression Rating Scale (MADRS) anhedonia factor and single-item scores.Patients and MethodsEligible adults (18–65 years) met DSM-5 criteria for MDD with inadequate response to 1 or 2 ADTs in the current depressive episode and had MADRS Total score ≥24, Clinical Global Impression–Severity score ≥4, and Quick Inventory of Depressive Symptomatology-Self-Report 16-item score ≥14. Patients were randomized to oral placebo or lumateperone 42 mg adjunctive to ADT for 6 weeks. A post hoc analysis assessed anhedonia symptoms according to MADRS anhedonia factor. MADRS Total score and MADRS anhedonia factor score were analyzed by baseline anhedonia factor score (median value: ≥18). A prospective analysis investigated change in individual MADRS items.ResultsLumateperone+ADT significantly improved anhedonia symptoms versus placebo+ADT at every visit according to MADRS anhedonia factor score (Day 43: least squares mean difference vs adjunctive placebo [LSMD], −2.5; effect size [ES], −0.50; P<0.0001). In patients with baseline anhedonia scores equal to or more than the median, significant improvements in MADRS Total score (LSMD, −5.4; 95% CI, −7.21, −3.59; ES, −0.67; P<0.0001) and anhedonia factor score (LSMD, −2.8; 95% CI, −3.91, −1.65; ES, −0.55; P<0.0001) were observed for lumateperone+ADT versus placebo+ADT at Day 43, with significant between-group differences observed at all study visits. At Day 43, 9 of the 10 MADRS items significantly improved with adjunctive lumateperone compared with adjunctive placebo.ConclusionLumateperone 42mg+ADT significantly improved symptoms of anhedonia and a broad range of depression symptoms compared with placebo+ADT in patients with MDD with inadequate response.

Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a complex and often debilitating mental disorder with a multifactorial etiology and large humanistic and economic burdens of illness.1,2 Although heterogeneous symptoms are characteristic of MDD, anhedonia and depressed mood are the 2 key symptoms required for a diagnosis of MDD.3

According to the fifth edition of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5), anhedonia in the context of MDD is defined as markedly diminished interest or pleasure in all, or almost all, activities for ≥2 weeks.3 An expanded view of the concept better characterizes anhedonia as encompassing deficits in reward processing, which are broadly summarized as anticipatory anhedonia (ie, reduced ability to experience pleasure in anticipation of rewarding events) and consummatory anhedonia (ie, a reduced ability to experience pleasure from activities).4,5 In patients with MDD, anhedonia is reported to occur in varying degrees in up to 70% of individuals6,7 and is associated with a chronic disease course, severe and recurrent symptoms, worse outcomes, greater psychiatric and medical comorbidities, and increased risk for suicidality.8,9

The clinical presentation of anhedonia is characterized by low positive affect, melancholic features, severe depressive symptoms, and social withdrawal.10,11 Anhedonia is challenging to treat; clinical studies show that baseline anhedonia predicts worse response to antidepressant therapy (ADT),12–14 with residual anhedonic symptoms seen in approximately 25% of patients in remission.15 Because patients have goals for MDD treatment that go far beyond symptom improvement, including outcomes such as restoration of positive affect16 and improved functioning and life engagement,17 an effective treatment for anhedonia is a clinical imperative.

Although the specific neurobiological mechanisms of anhedonia are not fully understood,18 many studies have found a relationship between the neural patterns that are related to reward processing deficits and anhedonia in MDD.19–24 The 3 subdomains of reward processing include reward liking (eg, experiencing pleasure from rewards), reward wanting (eg, motivation toward reward), and reward learning (eg, adjusting behavior based on expectations vs reality during prior rewards/punishments).25 These subdomains have neurobiological correlates that involve the ventral and dorsal striatum, lateral habenula, ventral tegmental area, orbitofrontal cortex, anterior cingulate cortex, and ventromedial and dorsolateral prefrontal cortex.8,26 In addition to the dopaminergic mesolimbic pathway, which is believed to play a key role in anhedonia, glutamate and serotonin may also play an important role in some facets of reward impairment.26 Collectively, these findings support the importance of investigating anhedonia as both a clinical component and a neurological mechanism of MDD to help further our understanding of this challenging symptom.

Because approximately half of patients with MDD have inadequate response to ADT,27 clinicians rely on subsequent treatment strategies to improve patient outcomes (eg, optimized ADT dosing, ADT switch, ADT augmentation).28,29 Evidence suggests that first-line ADT may not adequately address motivational and reward-processing deficits in MDD,30 indicating that additional treatment steps may be necessary to adequately treat patients with anhedonia. In cases of inadequate ADT response in MDD, adding an antipsychotic agent to ADT is an evidence-based treatment strategy,31–33 although a higher risk of tolerability issues has been reported for antipsychotics + ADT compared with placebo + ADT in MDD.34 In a real-world case-review study, anhedonia was among the most common reasons clinicians considered or prescribed an adjunctive antipsychotic agent for patients with MDD and inadequate response to ADT,35 which suggests that investigation of adjunctive antipsychotic efficacy in anhedonia could help inform clinical decision-making.

Lumateperone is a mechanistically novel US Food and Drug Administration–approved antipsychotic to treat schizophrenia, depressive episodes associated with bipolar I or bipolar II disorder as monotherapy and as adjunctive therapy with lithium or valproate, and MDD as adjunctive therapy with ADT.36 Lumateperone is a simultaneous modulator of serotonin, dopamine, and glutamate neurotransmission.37,38 Specifically, lumateperone is a potent serotonin (5-HT)2A receptor antagonist, a dopamine D2 receptor presynaptic partial agonist and postsynaptic antagonist, a D1 receptor–dependent indirect modulator of glutamatergic AMPA (α-amino-3-hydroxy-5-methyl-4-isoxazolepropionic acid) and NMDA (N-methyl-d-aspartate) currents, and a serotonin reuptake inhibitor.37,38 Additionally, lumateperone has negligible binding to H1, 5-HT2C, and muscarinic receptors, which may limit cognitive and metabolic effects, and lower striatal D2 receptor occupancy than other antipsychotics, which may reduce extrapyramidal symptom risk.38,39 This unique mechanism of action suggests that lumateperone may have efficacy across broad symptoms, with improved tolerability compared with current treatment options.

The efficacy of adjunctive lumateperone 42 mg + ADT in patients with MDD and inadequate response to ADT has been demonstrated in 2 Phase 3, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled clinical trials (Study 501 [NCT04985942]40 and Study 502 [NCT05061706]41). In each of these studies, the primary and key secondary efficacy endpoints (change from baseline to Day 43 in Montgomery-Åsberg Depression Rating Scale [MADRS] Total score and Clinical Global Impression–Severity [CGI-S] scale, respectively) were met.40,41

This post hoc analysis of Study 501 evaluated the effect of adjunctive lumateperone on symptoms of anhedonia in patients with MDD; changes in MADRS individual items were also investigated, with a focus on the specific symptoms that contribute to anhedonia.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Patients

This post hoc analysis was based on data from Study 501 (NCT04985942; conducted between July 2021 and February 2024),40 a Phase 3, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled study that included patients with MDD and inadequate response to ADT. Detailed methods of the primary study have been published previously.40 Briefly, the study consisted of a 2-week screening period, a 6-week double-blind treatment period, and a 1-week safety follow-up period. Participants were randomized (1:1) to oral lumateperone 42 mg + ADT or placebo + ADT. The study protocol complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice guidelines and was approved by the appropriate institutional review board/independent ethics committee (IRB Protocol # 20213126; for a full list of institutional review boards/independent ethics committees by country, please see the Supplementary material). The protocol was initially approved by the central IRB (WCG, Princeton, NJ, USA) in the United States. In addition, local ethics committees approved it in individual countries. Written informed consent was obtained from primary study participants before entering the study.

Participants were male or female outpatients (18–65 years) who met DSM-5 criteria for MDD confirmed by the Mini-International Neuropsychiatric Interview (MINI)42 and had a current major depressive episode ≥8 weeks and ≤18 months prior to screening. Clinical inclusion criteria were a MADRS Total score ≥24,43 CGI-S score ≥4,44 and a Quick Inventory of Depressive Symptomatology-Self-Report 16-item (QIDS-SR-16)45 score ≥14. Participants were required to have inadequate response to 1 or 2 monotherapy ADTs of at least the minimum effective dose (per package insert) for ≥6 weeks’ duration in the current major depressive episode, with inadequate response (<50% improvement) confirmed by the Antidepressant Treatment Response Questionnaire.46 Patients were excluded if there was a ≥25% decrease in MADRS Total or QIDS-SR-16 Total scores between screening and baseline, a lifetime psychiatric diagnosis other than MDD (eg, schizophrenia, schizoaffective disorder, schizophreniform disorder or other psychotic disorder, or bipolar disorder), or a significant risk for suicidal behavior.

Post Hoc and Prospective Analyses

Outcomes were analyzed in the modified intent-to-treat (mITT) population (all randomized patients who received ≥1 dose of study drug, had a baseline MADRS Total score, and had ≥1 postbaseline MADRS Total score) and in a subgroup of patients with baseline anhedonia (MADRS anhedonia factor score equal to or more than the median baseline value in the mITT population [≥18]). Use of this anhedonia proxy measure is consistent with methodology in prior publications.47,48 Post hoc outcomes of interest were change from baseline in MADRS anhedonia factor score in the mITT population, and in MADRS Total score and anhedonia factor score in the subgroups of patients with baseline anhedonia equal to or more than the median or less than the median.

Additionally, MADRS Total score response (defined as ≥50% improvement from baseline)49 and MADRS Total score remission (defined as MADRS Total score ≤10) were analyzed in the subgroups of patients with baseline anhedonia equal to or more than the median and less than the median.

Change from baseline to Day 43 in MADRS individual items (mITT population) was a prospective analysis of interest that allowed for investigation of efficacy across MDD symptoms, including the items that comprise the anhedonia factor score. Items included in the MADRS Total score are 1 (apparent sadness), 2 (reported sadness), 3 (inner tension), 4 (reduced sleep), 5 (reduced appetite), 6 (concentration difficulties), 7 (lassitude), 8 (inability to feel), 9 (pessimistic thoughts), and 10 (suicidal thoughts).43 The anhedonia factor score is the sum of MADRS items 1, 2, 6, 7, and 8.

Statistical Analysis

Efficacy analyses in the mITT population, including MADRS anhedonia factor score and MADRS individual items, used a mixed-effects model for repeated measures (MMRM) with treatment group, pooled study site, study visit, and treatment group-by-visit interaction as fixed effects and baseline MADRS Total score, and baseline MADRS Total score-by-visit as covariates using the observed data. Dependent variables included change from baseline in MADRS anhedonia factor score for the MADRS anhedonia factor analyses and change from baseline in MADRS item scores for the individual item analyses. An unstructured covariance matrix was used to model the correlation among repeated measurements within patients, and the Kenward-Roger method was used to estimate the denominator degrees of freedom. Effect size (ES) was calculated as the least squares mean difference [LSMD]/pooled within-patient error standard deviation of change from baseline at the visit. For the change from baseline analyses, missing data were left as-is in the MMRM model fitting; the MMRM model assumes that missing data are “missing at random”.

In the subgroup with baseline anhedonia equal to or more than the median and the subgroup with baseline anhedonia less than the median, analyses used an MMRM with treatment group, site (or pooled site), visit, subgroup, treatment group-by-subgroup, subgroup-by-visit, treatment group-by-visit, and treatment-by-subgroup-by-visit interactions as fixed effects, baseline and baseline-by-visit as covariates using the observed data. Dependent variables were the change from baseline in MADRS Total score and the change from baseline in MADRS anhedonia factor score.

Odds ratio estimates and P values for MADRS Total score response and remission were based on a logistic regression model with terms for treatment, subgroup, treatment-by-subgroup, and baseline MADRS Total score. The dependent variables were the responder status (yes vs no) and the remitter status (yes vs no). For these categorical analyses, patients with missing values at the visit were considered as “non-responder” or “non-remitter”.

Results

Patient Population

There were 481 patients in the mITT population (lumateperone + ADT, n=239; placebo + ADT, n=242); most patients completed double-blind treatment (lumateperone + ADT, 95.5%; placebo + ADT, 91.3%).

A total of 320 (66.5%) patients had baseline anhedonia equal to or more than the median (lumateperone + ADT, n=162; placebo + ADT, n=158). Demographic and baseline characteristics were generally similar between treatment groups in the mITT population (Table 1). Mean age was 45 years in the mITT population, with about 65% female patients and three-quarters of patients were White in each treatment group (Table 1). Changes in Depression and Anhedonia in Subgroups

In patients with baseline anhedonia scores equal to or more than the median (lumateperone + ADT, n=162; placebo + ADT, n=158), change from baseline to Day 43 in MADRS Total score was significantly different in favor of lumateperone + ADT over placebo + ADT (LSMD, −5.4; 95% CI, −7.21, −3.59; ES, −0.67; P<0.0001) (Table 2). Significant differences were observed at every visit from Day 8 through Day 43 (Figure 2A and Supplementary Table 2). In patients with baseline anhedonia scores less than the median (lumateperone + ADT, n=77; placebo + ADT, n=84), change was also significantly different in favor of lumateperone + ADT over placebo + ADT at Day 43 (LSMD, −3.9; 95% CI, −6.49, −1.39; ES, −0.49; P<0.01) (Table 2, Figure 2B, and Supplementary Table 2). The change from baseline to Day 43 in MADRS anhedonia factor score was also significantly different for lumateperone + ADT versus placebo + ADT in patients with baseline anhedonia scores equal to or more than the median (LSMD, −2.8; 95% CI, −3.91, −1.65; ES, −0.55; P<0.0001), with significant between-group differences observed at all study visits (Table 2, Figure 3A, and Supplementary Table 2). In patients with baseline anhedonia scores less than the median, change from baseline to Day 43 was also significantly different for lumateperone + ADT versus placebo + ADT (LSMD, −2.0; 95% CI, −3.59, −0.39; ES, −0.40; P<0.05), with significant between-group differences observed from Day 29 through the end of treatment (Table 2, Figure 3B, and Supplementary Table 2). Discussion

In patients with MDD with inadequate ADT response, improvement in overall depression (MADRS Total score from the primary study)40 and anhedonia (post hoc analysis of the anhedonia factor score) were observed with lumateperone + ADT versus placebo + ADT. Significant between-group differences in favor of lumateperone + ADT occurred early in treatment for most individual items and persisted through the end of treatment.

In the subgroup of patients with baseline anhedonia scores equal to or more than the median, significant improvements in MADRS Total score and anhedonia factor score were observed for lumateperone + ADT versus placebo + ADT at every visit during the study, with an effect size for MADRS anhedonia factor score at Day 43 (ES, −0.55) that was similar to the mITT population (ES, −0.50). From Day 29 through the end of treatment, significant changes in MADRS Total score and anhedonia factor score were also observed in the subgroup of patients with baseline anhedonia scores less than the median. Overall, these results indicate significant improvement in symptoms of depression and anhedonia with adjunctive lumateperone in patients with either higher or lower baseline anhedonia. Evidence from analyses assessing the effects of other antipsychotics in patients with MDD based on severity of anhedonia symptoms is limited, with the exception of a post hoc analysis of adjunctive cariprazine,47 which further highlights the importance of the efficacy of adjunctive lumateperone in this patient population.

MADRS Total score response and remission rates at Day 43 were greater with lumateperone + ADT versus placebo + ADT in both subgroups of patients with baseline anhedonia scores equal to or more than the median and less than the median, although the difference was not statistically significant for remission in those with scores less than the median. The patient population in this trial included those with MDD who had experienced inadequate ADT response, and higher levels of anhedonia is an additional factor that predicts poor treatment response.13,14 Thus, the significantly greater response rates with adjunctive lumateperone in this trial among patients with either higher or lower anhedonia are particularly notable.

Significant improvements for lumateperone + ADT over placebo + ADT were also seen on 9 of 10 MADRS individual items, including all of the items that comprise the anhedonia factor. Improvement in inability to feel, which is referred to as the MADRS anhedonia item because of its significant correlation with the Snaith-Hamilton Pleasure Scale,50,51 was seen as early as Day 15 for lumateperone + ADT–treated patients. Greater improvement at Day 43 (with effect sizes ≥0.50) was observed in the reported sadness, apparent sadness, inner tension, and reduced sleep items with adjunctive lumateperone versus adjunctive placebo. Antipsychotics have differing impact on specific symptoms of MDD, as adjunctive aripiprazole treatment for 6 weeks significantly improved 8 of 10 MADRS items but the effect sizes ranged from 0.24 to 0.38 in a pooled analysis; significance was achieved for all items except for inner tension and concentration difficulties.52 In an additional pooled, post hoc analysis, significant improvements (and with effect sizes ≥0.50) were achieved with 6-week adjunctive brexpiprazole for the individual items of reported sadness, apparent sadness, inner tension, reduced appetite, lassitude, inability to feel, and pessimistic thoughts.53

Additionally, significant improvement was observed for lumateperone + ADT over placebo + ADT on the MADRS concentration difficulties item, which is an item included in the anhedonia factor score and may be related to cognitive performance as concentration is a domain of cognition.47,54,55 Although it is not possible to compare results across trials, it is interesting to note that a post hoc analysis of adjunctive cariprazine in patients with MDD and inadequate response to ADT reported improvement of general depression and most anhedonia symptoms in a subgroup of patients with anhedonia, but no significant advantage over placebo was seen on the MADRS concentration item,47 suggesting the possibility that antipsychotic agents may have differential efficacy on specific anhedonia symptoms in MDD. While this is an interesting preliminary finding, additional studies investigating the effect of lumateperone on cognitive symptoms in MDD are warranted.

This investigation had limitations, including the post hoc nature of the analysis. The analyses were performed with observed cases, leading to fewer patients included at the end of study than at baseline. The presence of anhedonia was determined by the MADRS anhedonia factor and not by a scale that was specifically developed and validated for the assessment of anhedonia; however, the MADRS anhedonia factor is commonly used as a measure of anhedonia in clinical trials,47,56–58 and there is high correlation between the MADRS anhedonia factor and the Snaith-Hamilton Pleasure Scale, a validated measure of anhedonia.59 Because participants in the primary study were required to meet inclusion and exclusion criteria, our finding may not be generalizable to the wider population of people with MDD. Because lumateperone was given adjunctively, it is possible that some treatment effect could have been related to ongoing ADT, although this is unlikely because these patients would have been on ADT at stable dose for at least 8 weeks (6 weeks for being eligible for enrollment in this trial, plus 2 weeks of screening period). Of note, limited efficacy has been reported with monotherapy ADT in the treatment of anhedonia.4 Lastly, since good tolerability is an essential component of treatment acceptance and subsequent efficacy, it is important to note that in the primary study, adjunctive lumateperone was generally safe and well tolerated, with minimal risk of weight gain and cardiometabolic abnormalities.40 Notably, the results of the current post hoc analysis demonstrate the efficacy of lumateperone + ADT within a single study; future publications analyzing similar measures are being planned and will include a pooled analysis of the current study with the similarly designed, positive Study 502 (NCT05061706).41

Conclusion

Given that patients emphasize the importance of quality of life, functioning, and positive affect as goals of MDD treatment,16 adequate relief of anhedonia, a core symptom of MDD, is crucial.

In this post hoc analysis, lumateperone + ADT significantly improved symptoms of depression and anhedonia in patients with MDD with inadequate ADT response. Improvement in anhedonia occurred early in the course of treatment, and effect sizes were similar in patients with higher baseline anhedonia and patients in the mITT population, suggesting a possible benefit for adjunctive lumateperone in patients with this difficult-to-treat symptom. Adjunctive lumateperone also demonstrated superior efficacy over placebo in patients with baseline anhedonia less than the median, supporting adjunctive lumateperone in patients with lower levels of anhedonia. Adjunctive lumateperone improved 9 of 10 individual MADRS items, including inability to feel and concentration difficulties, as well as the other items of the anhedonia factor, further demonstrating its efficacy across general depressive symptoms and the key anhedonia symptoms in MDD.

Results of this post hoc analysis in patients with MDD and inadequate response to ADT support that adjunctive lumateperone could be an effective treatment for patients with MDD and concurrent anhedonia.
