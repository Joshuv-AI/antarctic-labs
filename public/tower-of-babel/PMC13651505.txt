Nonmonotonic Charging,
Overscreening, and Ion Competition
in the Electric Double Layer of Platinum Quantified by X‐ray
Spectroscopy

Alfred Larsson, Dogukan Yilmaz, Jan Sebastian Dominic Rodriguez, Tarik Zahzah, de Haij Pieter, Jordy J.J. Eggebeen, Karen van den Akker, Damien Roy, Solenn Reguer, Marc T.M. Koper, Rik Mom

Abstract

The electric double
layer (EDL) is an integral part of
the local
reaction environment in electrocatalysis. Indeed, the strong electric
fields and modified interfacial water structure accompanying ion accumulation
in the EDL have been shown to impact the performance of nearly every
electrocatalytic reaction. However, quantifying interfacial ion accumulation
experimentally is a long-standing challenge, leaving the atomic-level
picture of electrolyte effects in electrocatalysis incomplete. Here,
we address this challenge using an interface-sensitive in
situ X-ray fluorescence spectroscopy approach based on a
mesoporous Pt electrode to track interfacial ion concentration at
the Pt-electrolyte interface in acidic media. We show that the EDL
structure of the Pt electrode has a complex dependence on the applied
potential and electrolyte composition. From a systematic analysis
of ten electrolytes (including ions such as ClO4
–, SO4
2–, K+, and Ca+), we show that adsorbates alter the surface free charge, resulting
in nonmonotonic charging and overscreening. For nonadsorbing anions,
we show that the concentration-dependent surface free charge follows
classical Gouy–Chapman theory. At negatively charged surfaces,
cations and protons compete to screen the surface free charge in the
EDL, with a strong dependence on ion valency.

Introduction

Electrochemical reactions occur at the
interface between an electronic
conductor and an electrolyte, where an electric double layer (EDL)
forms. Such EDL formation includes interfacial ion accumulation, strong
local electric fields, and reorganization of solvent molecules, which
directly influence reaction thermodynamics and kinetics in electrocatalysis.
−

 Resolving the atomic structure of the EDL and its response to electrode
potential and electrolyte composition is therefore a central challenge
in electrochemistry.

Classical descriptions of the EDL, based
on the Gouy–Chapman–Stern
(GCS) framework, assume that the electrochemical
interface is ideally polarizable i.e., free of adsorbates with charge
transfer to the electrode. These assumptions are often not valid,
however, even for well-defined model systems.
,
 On
metal electrodes such as platinum, the surface is rarely free of adsorbates,
even within the so-called double-layer region. Adsorbed species such as hydrogen, hydroxide (OH),
,
 and specifically adsorbing anions modify the interfacial electronic
structure and thus the electrostatic boundary conditions experienced
by the electrolyte. Moreover, electrocatalysis
is typically performed in concentrated electrolytes and in the presence
of adsorbates, where classical GCS theory is no longer applicable.
Since there is no established quantitative EDL theory for these conditions,
it is essential to determine experimentally how system-specific factors,
such as the type and coverage of adsorbates, ion sizes, and interfacial
dielectric behavior, influence EDL structure.

Our current experimental
understanding of the EDL is largely based
on differential capacitance measurements, as the technique is experimentally
straightforward and the capacitance can be directly compared to classical
models.
,
 However, such measurements are restricted
to narrow potential windows free of adsorbates and pseudocapacitive
contributions. For reactive metals such as Pt, this corresponds to
a ∼200 mV window on Pt(111) in which anomalously low electrolyte
concentrations are required to observe the Gouy–Chapman predicted
capacitance minima at the potential of zero free charge (PZFC). Consequently, experimental insight into the adsorbate-free
EDL using capacitance measurements is largely limited to conditions
where classical behavior applies.

Accessing the EDL under catalytically
relevant conditions, therefore,
requires alternative experimental approaches.
,
 Many efforts have been made, including the radioactive tracer method,
,
 scanning probe microscopy, surface
X-ray diffraction,
,
 vibrational spectroscopy,
,
 and X-ray spectroscopy.
,
 However, techniques
that allow direct, straightforward, and quantitative probing of ions
in the EDL are still lacking.

Here, we combine cyclic voltammetry
with in situ resonant X-ray fluorescence spectroscopy
(XRF) on mesoporous Pt
electrodes. The large interface-to-bulk electrolyte ratio within the
porous electrode significantly enhances the spectroscopic signal from
the solid–liquid interface. Moreover, we use one of the most
studied model systems in electrochemistry: platinum in acidic media.
This system is not only highly relevant to catalysis but also provides
a range of adsorbates across the potential window, with regions of
both anion- and cation-dominated charge screening, allowing us to
deliberately study how adsorbates influence the EDL.

The crossover between
cation and anion accumulation around 0.28
VRHE identifies the potential at which the dominant interfacial
ion signal (positive Gibbs excess) switches from cations to anions.
This potential is closely associated with the PZFC, where the sign
of the surface free charge changes from negative to positive. However,
because the XRF measurement probes the interfacial ion signals of
K+ and ClO4
– with different
unknown calibration factors, the exact value of the PZFC cannot be
identified directly from the data. In addition, as can be expected
for the polycrystalline Pt employed here, the transition from cation
to anion dominated EDL is not sharp, and a narrow potential window
exists in which both cations and anions are detected. Such coexistence
occurs even for ideally polarizable, structurally homogeneous electrodes
(e.g., Hg in NaCl electrolyte), but is
strengthened here due to the lateral heterogeneity of surface sites
on polycrystalline Pt, which exhibit slightly different adsorbate
energetics and therefore different local PZFCs.
,

A further complexity in the PZFC behavior is identified when
analyzing
the pH dependence of the onset potential for cation accumulation.
According to classical theory, the PZFC should be constant on the
standard hydrogen electrode (SHE) scale and vary with pH on the RHE
scale, since the surface charging does not involve protons. However,
this is not observed here, as shown in the Supporting Information Section 7. Instead, the potential at which cation
attraction starts remains approximately constant at 0.28 V vs RHE
and does not exhibit the expected 59 mV/pH shift on the RHE scale.
This indicates that the sign change in surface free charge is not
governed by a classical, adsorbate-independent PZFC.

Further
support for the influence of adsorbates on EDL formation
comes from the nonmonotonic trend observed in the interfacial ClO4
– concentration. Upon entering the oxide
formation region, the anion signal decreases despite increasing potential.
Underlining that this is directly related to the presence of surface
oxides, the anion concentration shows a hysteresis between the forward
and backward potential scan, mirroring the irreversibility between
Pt surface oxide formation and reduction.

Since the ClO4
– signal is proportional
to the surface free charge when cations are absent in the EDL, as
is the case in the potential range for surface oxide formation and
reduction, the link between surface oxidation and nonmonotonic ClO4
– accumulation implies an analogous nonmonotonic
trend in the surface free charge. This experimentally observed nonmonotonic
change in surface free charge with potential has previously been theoretically
described by Huang and Eikerling,
,
 following
an original discovery by Frumkin et al., and reflects a coverage-dependent shift in the PZFC, caused by a
shift in the work function associated with surface oxidation.
,

PZFC (E

the applied electrode potential (E), thereby decreasing
the difference between the electrode potential and the PZFC, which

Along similar lines, the nonmonotonic change in
surface free charge
can also be rationalized by considering the generation of metal–oxygen
dipoles during oxidation, which induces an analogous shift in the
PZFC.
,
 Effectively, these two explanations are
two sides of the same coin, but they do provide slightly different
insight into the generalizability of the phenomenon of nonmonotonic
changes in surface free charge. Given that the oxidation-induced work
function/PZFC modification is a general phenomenon,
,,
 one may anticipate that the nonmonotonic
charging observed here is generalizable to systems where OH adsorption
and surface oxidation occur. The dipole framework further extends
this to other systems where strong dipoles are formed (e.g., during
surface chlorination). It would thus be of interest to quantitatively
assess the dipole-surface charge correlation. However, charge redistribution
of the Pt–O bond cannot be assessed within the XRF setup employed
here.

Shifting focus to the shape of the potential-dependent
cation signals,
another indication that adsorbates dictate surface free charge is
evident from the change in slope of the cation signal as a function
of potential near E = 0 VRHE (see also (a) and (b)). Within this potential
range, the adsorbate coverage shifts from a mixed H/OH distribution
on steps to a purely H-covered state. This can again affect the work
function and hence the surface free charge,
causing a change in the slope of the cation charging curve. This is
also observed using EQCM (Supporting Information Section 6, Figure S5) and has been
seen in earlier radioactive tracer experiments for Na on Pt. Overall, our analysis of the Pt-(10 mM HClO4 + 25 mM KClO4) electrolyte interface thus shows
that adsorbates play an important role in EDL formation, affecting
the EDL charging properties of both anion and cation dominated EDLs.

To investigate how EDL formation is altered by specific anion adsorption
(i.e., chemisorption of anions), we also studied EDL formation in
10 mM H2SO4 + 12.5 mM K2SO4. As shown in (b), the overall voltammetric response resembles that in perchloric
acid, yet some notable differences are observed, including a sharpening
of the second HUPD feature (at 0.3 V), and a positive shift
of the oxide formation region to more positive potentials, consistent
with strong anion–surface interactions.

Correspondingly,
we also observe a general resemblance in EDL formation
compared to perchloric acid, but with a few key differences. Most
notably, in contrast to the perchlorate case, the accumulation of
sulfate anions at the surface does not lead to the exclusion of cations.
Instead, K+ remains present in significant concentration
even at potentials where sulfate adsorption is substantial, as schematically
illustrated in (d). The simultaneous accumulation of sulfate and K+ is
consistent with overscreening associated with specific sulfate adsorption.
Related cation–anion coadsorption phenomena have previously
been reported for Pt and other electrode materials,
,−

 which shows the generality of this phenomenon also for more weakly
interacting surfaces such as Au. Underlying the overscreening is the
chemical interaction of the sulfate ions with the electrode surface,
which drives the accumulation of an excess amount of sulfate into
the EDL, i.e., more sulfate ions than needed to screen the positive
Pt surface free charge. As a result, the sulfate anions are only partially
discharged, and the surface, including
adsorbed sulfate ions, is charged net negatively, which is compensated
by the electrostatic attraction of cations. As a consequence, the
interface cannot be described in terms of a simple separation between
cation- and anion-dominated regimes. Instead, the interfacial charge
is continuously modulated by the balance between specific adsorption
and electrostatic compensation.

The correlation between voltammetric
features and the potential
dependence of the SO4
2– and K+ signals further supports the strong coupling between adsorbate structure
and interfacial ion concentration. Increases in the sulfate signal
coincide with the HUPD features, indicating that these
peaks are not solely associated with hydrogen and OH adsorption/desorption
but also involve sulfate adsorption processes. This is particularly
apparent for the voltammetric peak around 0.3–0.35 VRHE, in agreement with radiotracer studies, highlighting the sensitivity of our technique to subtle coadsorption
phenomena.

These observations again demonstrate that adsorbates
have a major
impact on the EDL structure, largely dictating ion accumulation in
the EDL as a function of potential. In the absence of anion chemisorption,
the EDL is dominated by just one type of ion (cation or anion), without
much evidence of cation–anion pairing. In contrast, in the
presence of specific adsorption, cation–anion coexistence does
occur as a result of overscreening by the specifically adsorbed ions.

The fact that the Grahame equation can describe the concentration-dependent

partly holds under these conditions, specifically: a nonadsorbing
anion (perchlorate) interacting with an electrode (platinum), which
is covered by adsorbates (OH and oxide). It also indicates that charge
is stored in the diffuse part of the double layer, without influence
from ion packing or dielectric saturation (despite the significant
bulk ion concentration), as such effects are not considered in GC
theory. However, it is important to note that the sine hyperbolic
potential dependence of the Grahame equation (stemming from the GC
capacitance) is expected to break down at high surface free charges
and high concentrations.

When considering the energy terms for
double layer formation, the
bulk ion concentration dependence on the surface free charge can also
be rationalized in terms of thermodynamics. Increasing the electrolyte
concentration decreases the entropic penalty associated with accumulating
ions at the interface, as the difference in configurational entropy
between ions in the bulk and in the EDL becomes smaller. As a result, the system can sustain a higher
interfacial ion concentrationand thus a higher surface free
chargeat the same applied potential.

In contrast to
the perchlorate electrolyte, the behavior is markedly
different for specifically adsorbing anions such as sulfate, which
suppresses the diffuse double layer. (b) shows the corresponding measurements
in sulfate-containing electrolytes. Here, the interfacial sulfate
signal exhibits little dependence on bulk concentration over the investigated
range. This insensitivity indicates that the interfacial concentration
is not limited by entropic considerations, but instead by the availability
of adsorption sites and the strength of the sulfate–surface
interaction.

In this regime, the free energy of the interface
is dominated by
enthalpic contributions associated with specific adsorption (Helmholtz
capacitance), rather than by the entropy of ion redistribution (diffuse
double layer capacitance). As a consequence, increasing the bulk sulfate
concentration does not significantly alter the interfacial coverage,
which is already close to saturation. This behavior further reinforces
the distinction between nonadsorbing and specifically adsorbing electrolytes:
while the former exhibit concentration-dependent double-layer charging
governed in part by entropy, the latter are controlled primarily by
surface chemistry and adsorption thermodynamics, as schematically
illustrated in (d).

Shifting focus to the negatively charged surface below
∼0.28
VRHE, we also studied the effect of bulk cation concentration
on EDL formation. (a) shows the K XRF intensity as a function
of potential for varying bulk K+ concentrations. Again,
we find that the general shape of the interfacial K+ concentration
vs potential curve is maintained independent of the bulk K+ concentration, indicating a similar charging mechanism in all cases.

Similar to the perchlorate case,
the magnitude
of the signal depends
strongly on the bulk K+ concentration. However, the origin
of this bulk K+ concentration dependence is fundamentally
different from that of perchlorate: it is primarily due to the competition
between K+ and H+ cations. This competition
is quantified in (c), where the interfacial signal at 0.05 V is plotted as a function
of the ratio between K+ and total bulk cation (K+ + H+) concentration (see Supporting Information

indicating that a mixed double layer of K+ and H+ forms, reflecting the bulk ion ratio of K+ and H+. This suggests that the free energy of screening the surface
free charge is similar for H+ and K+. Since
the cation-proton competition is thus not driven by chemically specific
ion-adsorbate interactions, we anticipate that it is generalizable
to other electrochemical systems, including more ideal adsorbate-free
systems.

For
the divalent Ca2+ cation, we find even stronger
deviation from Gouy–Chapman behavior. (b) shows the Ca XRF signal for varying Ca2+ concentrations. In contrast to the K+ case, the

ion competition, and that we are far from Gouy–Chapman behavior.
The observed saturation could point toward local dielectric saturation
or ion crowding, caused by the stronger electrostatic interaction
of divalent ions with the negatively charged surface or by the fact
that the charge is stored in the inner layer and not in the diffuse
part of the double layer.

Conclusion

In this work, we provide
a direct, element-specific
view of EDL
formation at the Pt-electrolyte interface in acidic media by combining
cyclic voltammetry with in situ resonant XRF. Our
results demonstrate that interfacial charging cannot be described
solely in terms of classical electrostatic models, but is shaped by
the coupled interplay of adsorbates, bulk ion concentration, and ion
competition.

Adsorbates define the effective PZFC, inducing
nonmonotonic charging
behavior that strongly deviates from classical theories. For a fixed
adsorbate structure, however, the EDL can still behave close to ideal
depending on the type of ions present. For the perchlorate ion, we
find that the Gouy–Chapman model accurately captures double
layer charging as a function of bulk ion concentration, indicating
that the charge is stored in the diffuse EDL (GC capacitance). In
contrast, cations such as K+ and Ca2+ show Helmholtz
capacitance behavior, with little dependence of the interfacial cation
concentration on the (total) bulk cation concentration. Instead, the
composition of the double layer is determined by the competition between
protons and other cations, with a strong dependence on ion valency.
The understanding of these fundamental differences in the behaviors
commonly used ions provides new handles for moving beyond classical
EDL models. Taking the quantitative interfacial ion accumulation data
provided here as a benchmark, we anticipate that our work will thus
spark new developments in the EDL modeling, accounting for adsorbates
inducing nonmonotonic charging, overscreening, and cation-proton competition
in mixed EDLs.

Methods

The in situ resonant XRF measurements
were carried
out at the LUCIA beamline at the SOLEIL synchrotron. The tender X-ray
beamline provides monochromatized photon beams with energies from
0.8 to 8 keV, allowing measurements of SO4
2–, ClO4
–, K+, and Ca2+ ions at the K-edges of S (∼2472 eV), Cl (∼2822 eV),
K (∼3608 eV), and Ca (∼4038 eV), (for full XAS spectra,
see Supporting Information Section 10, Figures S11–S14). The fluorescence X-rays
were detected with a silicon drift detector (SDD). A peristaltic pump
was used to deliver the electrolyte, which was not recirculated to
avoid the accumulation of beam damage in the electrolyte (e.g., ClO4
– decomposition to form Cl–). Cl K-edge spectra before and after time-resolved measurements
during CVs are shown in the Supporting Information Section 11, Figure S15, indicating
the absence of beam induced damage since the intensity and shape of
the spectrum is preserved. At high X-ray doses this is not the case.

A custom-designed spectro-electrochemical flow cell with a three-electrode
configuration that can accommodate a 1 × 1 cm2 SiN
x
 chip was used. A Pt wire was used as the
counter electrode in combination with a miniature leakless Ag/AgCl
reference electrode (Innovative Instruments). All potentials were
converted to the reversible hydrogen electrode (RHE) scale using E
RHE = E
Ag/AgCl +
0.197 V + 0.059 × pH.

Time resolved XRF signals were measured
using an X-ray energy corresponding
to the white line energy of each respective element, S: 2482 eV, Cl:
2834.5 eV, K: 3616 eV, Ca: 4050.5 eV. The XRF signals were extracted
by integrating the Kα peaks in the XRF spectra recorded by the
SDD. This was done by integrating the signal in the following energy
ranges for each respective element, S: 2262–2411 eV, Cl: 2486–2685
eV, K: 3084–3432 eV, Ca: 3482–3830 eV). The reproducibility
of the time-resolved signals is highlighted in Supporting Information, Section 12.
