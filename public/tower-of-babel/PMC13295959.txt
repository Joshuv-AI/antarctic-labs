From M7824 to SHR-1701: lessons for dual PD-L1/TGF-β targeting
Authors: Ming Yi, Tianye Li, Kongming Wu, Zhijun Dai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRetlirafusp alfa (SHR-1701), a bifunctional programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) antibody fused to a TGF-β trap, was approved in China on January 7, 2026 for first-line treatment of PD-L1-positive advanced gastric or gastroesophageal junction adenocarcinoma with chemotherapy. This first-in-class milestone revisits a key question raised by bintrafusp alfa (M7824): why can dual PD-L1/TGF-β targeting be active in selected settings yet inconsistent across tumors? We highlight that TGF-β-driven immune suppression and immune exclusion are often spatially organized within stromal niches, vary across indications, and are not always the dominant barrier even when PD-L1 is expressed. SHR-1701’s approval provides proof of principle in a defined context and supports mechanism-aligned development using biomarker-driven selection, rational combinations and sequencing, and pharmacodynamic endpoints that directly test these assumptions.

Main text

Retlirafusp alfa (SHR-1701) was approved in China on January 7, 2026 by the National Medical Products Administration.1 It is approved as first-line therapy for locally advanced unresectable, recurrent, or metastatic gastric cancer or gastroesophageal junction (GEJ) adenocarcinoma in patients with programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1)-positive tumors, in combination with fluoropyrimidine-based and platinum-based chemotherapy. SHR-1701 is designed to block PD-L1 while neutralizing TGF-β through a receptor-based trap.2 This approval renews attention to dual PD-L1/TGF-β targeting, a strategy that previously drew both interest and skepticism after clinical experience with bintrafusp alfa (M7824). A practical question follows: what limited M7824, and under what biological and clinical conditions might this drug class provide reproducible benefit?

The biological rationale is straightforward, but translation to consistent clinical benefit is context-dependent. Programmed cell death protein-1 (PD-1)/PD-L1 signaling reduces T-cell activation, cytokine production, and cytotoxic activity, thereby limiting the magnitude and durability of antitumor effector responses. Meanwhile, TGF-β constrains antitumor immunity at multiple levels in the tumor microenvironment, operating across immune, stromal, and tumor compartments. It weakens the killing activity of T cells and natural killer cells, promotes regulatory T-cell differentiation, drives macrophage polarization toward an M2-like state, and increases the abundance and suppressive function of myeloid-derived suppressor cells. In addition, TGF-β activates cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) and increases collagen deposition in the stroma, which can physically restrict lymphocyte infiltration and impede effective immune surveillance.3 Importantly, these pathways act through complementary mechanisms. PD-1/PD-L1 directly dampens effector T-cell signaling, while TGF-β shapes an immunosuppressive and excluded microenvironment by regulating stromal remodeling and suppressive immune cell states.

Consistent with this rationale, multiple studies suggest that combining TGF-β blockade with PD-1/PD-L1 antibodies can enhance antitumor activity and help overcome resistance.4 5 This provided the basis for developing a single agent that targets both pathways. In principle, a bifunctional molecule could block PD-L1 while neutralizing TGF-β in the same tumor setting, including PD-L1-enriched tumor or stromal regions.6 This may avoid a scenario in which checkpoint blockade activates effector cells, but immune cells still cannot effectively enter the tumor or remain functional because of stromal and microenvironmental constraints. From a drug development perspective, a single agent can also simplify dosing and scheduling, compared with coadministering separate PD-L1 antibodies and TGF-β inhibitors.

M7824 highlights a key point: a drug can be mechanistically well designed yet still deliver inconsistent benefit in the clinic. M7824 is a PD-L1 antibody fused to the extracellular domain of TGF-β receptor II, intended to block PD-L1 while trapping TGF-β.7 Early clinical studies suggested activity in certain contexts, but later studies produced mixed outcomes across tumor types and treatment settings. This inconsistency likely reflects the complexity of the tumor microenvironment in real-world settings. In some tumors or disease stages, TGF-β-driven immune suppression may not be the main obstacle, or PD-L1 blockade may not be the critical bottleneck, so simultaneously targeting both pathways does not reliably improve outcomes. Accordingly, dual PD-L1/TGF-β targeting should be evaluated in biomarker-selected patients and appropriate clinical settings.

TGF-β-driven immune suppression is not uniform within tumors. It is often strongest in the tumor stroma, where CAF activity and extracellular matrix remodeling create barriers that limit lymphocyte entry. In many immune-excluded tumors, immune cells are present but remain trapped at the invasive margin or in stromal areas, rather than reaching tumor nests. Checkpoint blockade alone may not overcome this physical and stromal barrier. In addition, TGF-β is frequently present in an inactive (latent) form and becomes activated locally. Therefore, the benefit of a TGF-β trap may depend not only on drug levels in blood, but also on whether it can neutralize active TGF-β in these stromal regions where exclusion is generated.

Notably, immune exclusion is not always driven mainly by TGF-β. In some tumors, other pathways play a larger role, such as VEGF-related angiogenic programs, WNT/β-catenin signaling, or suppressive myeloid mechanisms. In these cases, adding TGF-β blockade to PD-L1 inhibition may provide only limited additional benefit, even when PD-L1 is expressed.8 Moreover, PD-L1 positivity does not necessarily indicate active TGF-β signaling or stromal immune exclusion. PD-L1 expression often reflects an interferon-driven, adaptive response to immune pressure, but immune cells can still be kept out of tumor nests if stromal barriers remain. Conversely, tumors with strong TGF-β-driven stromal remodeling may be poorly inflamed, and therefore may not show strong PD-L1 induction. Together, these points argue that patient selection should not rely on PD-L1 status alone. In gastric/GEJ cancer, biomarkers such as microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H) and tumor mutational burden-high (TMB-H) may help identify more immunogenic tumors, but they do not directly define whether TGF-β-driven stromal immune exclusion is the dominant barrier. For dual PD-L1/TGF-β targeting, more informative biomarkers are likely to include TGF-β-responsive stromal signatures, phospho-SMAD-related pathway activity, CAF-related and extracellular matrix-remodeling features, and spatial evidence of immune exclusion. In practice, the most useful strategy may be a composite biomarker framework that integrates PD-L1 status with stromal biology and spatial immune architecture.

Combination context and treatment sequencing are likely to affect antitumor activity. In immune-excluded tumors, simply reducing TGF-β-driven stromal barriers and myeloid immunosuppression may not be enough if antigen release, innate immune activation, and T-cell priming are suboptimal. Chemotherapy and radiotherapy can increase antigen availability and inflammatory signals in the tumor, and may also reshape suppressive myeloid cells. These upstream changes can complement barrier relief. Innate immune agonists, such as STING or TLR agonists, can further enhance antigen presentation and myeloid activation.9 From this perspective, dual PD-L1 blockade and TGF-β neutralization is best viewed as part of a broader strategy: it works most plausibly when it is paired with treatments that generate antigen and inflammation, while it helps remove stromal and myeloid constraints that prevent immune cells from entering and functioning in tumors. Therefore, dual targeting may perform better in settings that already provide immune stimulation, and may be less effective in profoundly cold tumors or when T-cell priming capacity is limited.

The approved clinical setting for SHR-1701 provides a defined context in which these hypotheses can be evaluated. SHR-1701 is used in a first-line gastric/GEJ setting together with chemotherapy and in a PD-L1-positive population. In this context, chemotherapy can facilitate antigen release and inflammatory signaling and can remodel immunosuppressive myeloid compartments. These processes could complement attenuation of TGF-β-associated immune exclusion. The approval was supported by a randomized phase III study (NCT04950322).

The SHR-1701 experience also underscores the importance of molecular and pharmacologic properties within this class. Although SHR-1701 and M7824 share the same overall design principle of combining PD-L1 blockade with a TGF-β receptor II trap, they are not structurally identical. SHR-1701 has been described as an IgG4 anti-PD-L1 antibody fused to the extracellular domain of TGF-β receptor II, whereas M7824 uses an IgG1 anti-PD-L1 backbone with the same general trapping concept (figure 1). Such differences in molecular architecture and Fc subclass may influence pharmacologic behavior, including the balance between PD-L1 engagement and TGF-β capture, systemic exposure, tissue distribution, and tolerability. However, a similar structure does not guarantee the same pharmacology, and direct head-to-head public data comparing binding kinetics, intratumoral distribution, or tissue penetration remain limited. Therefore, these structural differences should currently be viewed as plausible contributors to differences in clinical behavior rather than as a basis for definitive comparative claims. This issue may be particularly relevant in indications where stromal biology, immune exclusion, and the spatial distribution of active TGF-β vary substantially across tumors.

This issue is relevant not only to pharmacologic behavior, but also to the safety profile of this drug class. From the currently available public data, the overall safety profiles of SHR-1701 and M7824 appear broadly comparable. In the phase 1 study, SHR-1701 showed a manageable safety profile, with approximately 20% of patients experiencing grade 3 or worse treatment-related adverse events, which was reported to be similar to M7824.10 However, adverse-event patterns are not identical. For M7824, reported adverse events have included potentially TGF-β-related skin lesions, whereas similar findings have not been prominently reported for SHR-1701. Because these data come from different studies and treatment settings, direct cross-trial comparison should be interpreted cautiously. Overall, the clinical value of this class likely depends on achieving sufficient pathway modulation for antitumor benefit while maintaining an acceptable safety window in normal tissues.

Taken together, current evidence does not support a one-size-fits-all conclusion for dual PD-L1/TGF-β targeting. Instead, it points to a mechanism-guided approach and several testable questions. Which biomarkers can reliably identify tumors with TGF-β-associated immune exclusion, such as stromal enrichment and specific spatial immune patterns? How can TGF-β activity and immune exclusion be measured with clinically practical assays? Which combinations and sequencing strategies best pair immune activation with relief of stromal and myeloid barriers? What toxicities emerge when immune infiltration increases in tumors, and how should these risks be monitored and managed? Finally, beyond gastric/GEJ cancer, in which indications is immune suppression jointly driven by PD-L1 and TGF-β-related programs, making this strategy most likely to add value?

Conclusion

Overall, experience with M7824 shows that a plausible mechanism does not guarantee consistent benefit across diverse settings. The development and approval of SHR-1701 support the feasibility of this drug class in a defined indication and treatment context. A working hypothesis is that dual PD-L1/TGF-β targeting is most effective when immune activation is already engaged, when tumors show TGF-β-associated exclusion features, and when the degree of pathway modulation remains tolerable. Future progress will depend on biomarker-driven trial designs and pharmacodynamic endpoints that directly test these assumptions. These endpoints should assess not only target engagement (PD-L1 blockade and TGF-β neutralization) but also downstream biological effects, such as changes in stromal programs, myeloid states, immune cell localization, and intratumoral effector function. In this way, SHR-1701’s approval can be viewed as a starting point for more precise, mechanism-guided expansion to settings where dual PD-L1/TGF-β targeting is biologically and clinically justified.
